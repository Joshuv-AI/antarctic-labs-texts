Hits to Higgs: Reconstruction-Free Higgs Classification from Raw LHC Detector Data Using Higgsformers
Sascha Caron, Polina Moskvitina, Roberto Ruiz de Austri, Eugene Shalugin

Abstract
We present a comparative study of Higgs event classification at the Large Hadron Collider that bypasses the traditional reconstruction chain. As a benchmark, we focus on distinguishing t ​ t ¯ ​ H t\bar{t}H from t ​ t ¯ t\bar{t} events with H → b ​ b ¯ H\to b\bar{b} , a particularly challenging task due to their similar final-state topologies. Our pipeline begins with event generation in Pythia8 , fast simulation with ACTS/Fatras, and classification directly from raw detector hits. We show for the first time that a transformer model originally developed for inner tracker hit-to-track assignment can be retrained to classify Higgs events directly from raw hits. For comparison, we reconstruct the same events with Delphes and train object-based classifiers, including multilayer perceptrons and the Particle Transformer. We evaluate both approaches under varying dataset sizes and pileup levels. Despite operating solely on inner tracker hits, Higgsformer achieves strong performance, reaching an AUC of 0.792.

Introduction

The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at CERN produces proton–proton collisions at an unprecedented scale, up to 40 million events per second, generating hundreds of terabytes of online data and tens of petabytes of stored offline data annually. Traditionally, this massive volume is processed through deeply structured reconstruction pipelines. These include particle trajectory fitting, calorimeter clustering, and jet and object identification, which ultimately reduce the raw detector output into a compact set of high-level observables used in physics analyses. While effective, this approach imposes strong inductive biases and may discard low-level information that could be critical for certain tasks.

This raises a natural question:
Can modern machine learning models learn directly from the raw detector data, bypassing intermediate reconstruction stages and high-level features altogether?
In this work, we address this question by directly comparing two pipelines: (i) an end-to-end classifier operating on raw digitized detector hits obtained with ACTS/Fatras, and (ii) a baseline classifier trained on high-level reconstructed objects obtained from a Delphes-simulated detector. Both pipelines share identical physics event generation to ensure a fair comparison.

The classification task we consider is the separation of
t\bar{t}
from
t\bar{t}H
events, with the Higgs boson forced to decay to a
b\bar{b}
pair.
Discriminating
t\bar{t}H
events from the dominant
t\bar{t}
background, especially in the
H\to b\bar{b}
decay channel, is a challenging tasks in Higgs physics at the LHC
ATLAS:2024gth
;
CMS:2024fdo
.

This process pair provides a realistic and challenging benchmark: they share similar final-state topologies, but differ subtly in object multiplicities and kinematics, particularly in the presence of additional
b
-jets from the Higgs decay. These subtle differences make it an ideal case study for assessing whether low-level hit information can rival or surpass traditional high-level approaches.

We build a complete end-to-end event simulation pipeline based on
Pythia8

Sj_strand_2015
event generation, ACTS/Fatras
Spannagel:2019actf
detector simulation, and a FlashAttention-accelerated
dao2023flashattention2
transformer model to classify events directly from hit patterns.
A closely related model was previously developed by us as a
Trackformer
, used for assigning hits to tracks
caron2025
. In this work, we adapt the same basic architecture for Higgs event classification and refer to it as the
Higgsformer
. It is a lightweight, set-based Transformer designed to operate directly on raw detector hits.

For comparison, we construct a parallel baseline where the same generated events are processed through Delphes
deFavereau:2013fsa
to reconstruct standard physics objects (jets, leptons, photons, and missing transverse energy). On this object-level representation, we benchmark against multilayer perceptron (MLP) baselines and the Particle Transformer (ParT)
Qu:2022mxj
. This setup allows us to highlight the novelty of hit-level end-to-end learning with the Higgsformer, while still enabling a comparison to established object-level approaches. However, this comparison should be interpreted with care: in this prototype study, we contrast a full detector setup—including calorimeter, muon system, and inner tracker with models operating only on inner tracker data.

We then evaluate both pipelines under varying detector conditions, including datasets with and without additional proton–proton interactions (pileup), to assess robustness in realistic LHC-like environments.

We find that the hit-level model achieves performance competitive with, and in some cases approaching, that of the reconstructed-object baseline, despite bypassing the entire reconstruction chain. This result supports the potential of hit-level end-to-end learning in high-energy physics.

2
Related Work

The classification of
t\bar{t}H
events particularly in the
H\rightarrow b\bar{b}
decay channel has been the focus of various machine learning (ML) efforts in high-energy physics. Prior work has explored both traditional algorithms and neural approaches, typically relying on handcrafted features derived from reconstructed-level data
Aaboud_2018
;
Sirunyan_2018
.

Våge
vage2018
examined the performance of Boosted Decision Trees (BDTs) and Neural Networks (NNs) in the context of
t\bar{t}H(b\bar{b})
classification at ATLAS. Using standard high-level features such as jet multiplicities, b-tag scores, and invariant masses, the study found comparable results between BDTs and fully connected NNs, both achieving area under the ROC curve (AUC) values in the range of 75–77%. However, it also highlighted that neural networks are more sensitive to class imbalance and training biases, especially in low-statistics regions.

Similarly, Santos et al.
santos2017
conducted a broad benchmarking study of several ML classifiers, ranging from K-Nearest Neighbors and Naive Bayes to XGBoost and neural network variants, on simulated
t\bar{t}H
events with
H\rightarrow b\bar{b}
. Their best models (XGBoost and NeuroBGD) achieved AUCs around 80.0–80.2% and F-scores around 74%, but all required pre-engineered input variables and physics-specific observables.

While these studies demonstrated the power of ML in this classification task, they all operated on reconstructed objects such as jets, leptons, and MET. That paradigm implicitly assumes the availability of full event reconstruction, and carries potential biases from detector modeling, jet clustering algorithms, or b-tagging procedures.

In contrast, our work takes a radically different approach. We eliminate the reconstruction step entirely and operate directly on the raw detector hits using transformer-based models. Inspired by recent work on TrackML
caron2025
, we hypothesize that deep architectures can learn to extract task-relevant structure directly from hit patterns given enough training data and realistic detector simulation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study exploring end-to-end classification of
t\bar{t}
vs.
t\bar{t}H
events from hit-level data alone, without using any handcrafted features or reconstructed quantities.

3
Data Preparation

3.1
Event Generation

We simulate signal and background processes using
Pythia8
integrated within the
ACTS
framework.

For the signal, we consider
t\bar{t}H
events where the Higgs boson is forced to decay into a
b\bar{b}
pair; a dominant decay channel for
m_{H}\approx 125
GeV and of high relevance for experimental analyses. We include both gluon-gluon fusion and quark-antiquark annihilation production channels:

gg\rightarrow t\bar{t}H,\quad q\bar{q}\rightarrow t\bar{t}H\,.

Initial and final state radiation (ISR/FSR) are enabled to simulate realistic parton showering. We apply a parton-level transverse momentum cut of
\hat{p}_{T}>100
GeV to suppress soft events. Hadron-level decays are fully enabled for both the Higgs and top quarks.

The background consists of standard
t\bar{t}
production, including QCD-induced additional jet radiation. This class includes light-flavor jets but lacks the explicit
b\bar{b}
pair from Higgs decay.

Each event is generated with Gaussian vertex smearing to reflect LHC beam spot properties:

\sigma_{x,y}=12.5~\mu\mathrm{m},\quad\sigma_{z}=55.5~\mathrm{mm},\quad\sigma_{t}=5~\mathrm{ns}

A fixed random seed of 42 is used for reproducibility. All datasets are produced in both
ROOT
and
CSV
formats, with 20,000 signal and 20,000 background events per configuration.

3.2
Pileup, postprcessing and reconstruction pipeline

We use the Fatras fast simulation engine provided by ACTS to simulate the interaction of particles with a tracking detector under a 2 T magnetic field. Particle propagation through the detector geometry includes effects such as multiple scattering, energy loss, and material interaction.
The detector geometry is defined using the ACTS
GenericDetector
, mirroring the configuration used in the TrackML challenge
kiehn2019trackml
. Fatras outputs truth-level hits (
SimHits
) for each detector surface where a particle intersects.
Following simulation, we perform digitization to model sensor response. Digitization is configured using ACTS JSON-based configuration files to emulate realistic pixel and strip detector behavior. The result is a set of digitized hits stored in
CSV
format, suitable for downstream processing and machine learning input.

To create TrackML-like datasets, we perform: (i) coordinate transformation from local sensor coordinates to global
(x,y,z)
, (ii) tag each hit with volume, layer and module indices, (iii) compute transverse momentum, count hits per track (
n_{\text{hits}}
), and remove zero-charge particles or malformed hits, and (iv) inspired by TrackML we assign per-hit weights in the same manner.

We generated datasets with pileup (PU) levels of 0, 5 and 20 interactions per bunch crossing, each with 40,000 events.

Detector effects are simulated using
Delphes

deFavereau:2013fsa
with the default ATLAS detector card. The simulation models the calorimeter, tracking, and muon systems, including realistic resolutions, efficiencies, and acceptance cuts. Jets are reconstructed using the anti-
k_{T}
algorithm with
R=0.4
, and standard ATLAS-like
b

•

Electrons and muons with four-momenta and isolation variables.

•

Jets with
p_{T}
,
\eta
,
\phi
, energy, and
b

•

Missing transverse energy and its azimuthal angle.

Objects are ordered by decreasing
p_{T}
and padded to a fixed multiplicity per category to enable batch processing in the machine learning pipeline. Missing entries are zero-padded, and a corresponding mask array is stored for each event.

4
Machine Learning Models

We introduce Higgsformer, a lightweight set-based Transformer operating directly on raw hit coordinates
(x,y,z)
. Each hit is treated as a token, embedded into a
d
-dimensional space, and processed with
L
Transformer encoder layers. Masked mean pooling produces an event-level representation, followed by a linear classifier for binary discrimination.

By default, we use the small configuration:
L=2
layers,
H=2
attention heads,
d=16
hidden size. Larger settings up to
L=4
,
H=4
,
d=64
were also tested. We employ exact FlashAttention
dao2022flashattention
for memory-efficient self-attention and mixed precision training (AMP) for speed. Dropout (0.3) and weight decay are applied for regularization.

Training uses binary cross-entropy with logits. We found out that logits are background biased despite the balanced dataset, so we incorporate a positive-class weight of 1.5 to balance classes. We use AdamW optimizer, learning rate
10^{-3}
, batch size 64, and early stopping with patience of 100 epochs. Models are trained up to 500 epochs.

MLP

As a baseline, we train a multilayer perceptron (MLP)
Rumelhart:1986gxv
on fixed-length feature vectors assembled from Delphes objects.
Up to four jets (
j
), four
b
-jets (
b
), and one particle of each of the remaining types (electron, positron, muon, and anti-muon) are selected in descending
p_{T}
order. Their
(E,p_{T},\eta,\phi)
values are flattened into a one-dimensional array.
We consider two input configurations:
(1) all reconstructed objects together with the missing transverse energy (MET) and its azimuthal angle, which effectively captures the response of the full detector; and
(2) only
b
-jets, which provides information primarily associated with the inner tracker, while excluding light jets, leptons, photons, and MET.

Features are normalized using statistics from the training set:
E
,
p_{T}
, and MET via Z-score, and
\eta
,
\phi
via Min–Max scaling. The MLP has three hidden layers (256–128–64 units, ReLU activations) and a sigmoid output. It is trained using Adam optimizer and binary cross-entropy loss, with early stopping based on validation AUC.

Particle Transformer (ParT)

To benchmark against a state-of-the-art object-level model, we employ the Particle Transformer
Qu:2022mxj
for the
t\bar{t}H
vs.
t\bar{t}
classification task. The model operates on a sequence of reconstructed objects, using self-attention layers to capture correlations between particles. The detailed network configuration and hyperparameters follow our previous work
Builtjes:2022usj
. In this study, we use a simplified version of ParT without interaction matrices or coupling terms.

Time Complexity

Whereas classical CPU based tracking typically takes 1 second per event, Higgsformer-small inference requires below 10 milliseconds on an NVIDIA H100 GPU for pileup=0, i.e. orders of magnitude faster.

6
Conclusions

Our results demonstrate that the Higgsformer model, trained solely on inner tracker hits, can effectively separate
t\bar{t}H
from
t\bar{t}
events, achieving a test ROC–AUC of 0.792 at zero pileup. Performance is likely limited by the size of the training dataset and the degree of model optimization.

The analysis of learned features in Section
5
reveals that: (i) the network does not rely on simple proxies such as the total number of hits; (ii) it systematically assigns higher importance to hits traceable to Higgs decay products; and (iii) with increasing training data, high-importance hits form more symmetric and cylindrical patterns, suggesting that Higgsformer learns both detector geometry and Higgs-specific structure.

While object-level models (based on the full detector) still outperform Higgsformer (which uses only inner tracker inputs) in this study, our results show that modern attention-based architectures can extract meaningful event-discriminative information directly from raw hit-level data. This represents a promising step toward reconstruction-free classification in high energy physics.

Future work will explore scaling to larger datasets, incorporating additional subdetectors, and testing the approach on more realistic detector conditions and high-pileup environments.
