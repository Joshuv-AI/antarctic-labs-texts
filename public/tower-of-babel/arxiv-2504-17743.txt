Abstract
Given an undirected graph G G , the problem of deciding whether G G admits a simple and proper time-labeling that makes it temporally connected is known to be NP-hard (Göbel et al., 1991). In this article, we relax this problem and ask whether a given degree sequence can be realized as a temporally connected graph. Our main results are a complete characterization of the feasible cases, and a recognition algorithm that runs in 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(n) time for graphical degree sequences (realized as simple temporal graphs) and in 𝒪 ⁡ ( n + m ) \mathcal{O}(n+m) time for multigraphical degree sequences (realized as non-simple temporal graphs, where the number of time labels on an edge corresponds to the multiplicity of the edge in the multigraph). In fact, these algorithms can be made constructive at essentially no cost, we present a 𝒪 ⁡ ( n + m ) \mathcal{O}(n+m) time algorithm that outputs, for a given (multi)graphical degree sequence 𝐝 \mathbf{d} , a temporally connected graph whose underlying (multi)graph is a realization of 𝐝 \mathbf{d} , if one exists.

Introduction

The problem of assigning time labels to the edges of a graph in such a way that every vertex can reach every other vertex by a non-decreasing path (temporal path) is known as the gossiping problem.
Problems of this type were extensively studied in the late 70s - early 90s (see, e.g.
[
23
,
24
]
), initially motivated by optimal scheduling of phone calls, where the agents are represented by vertices and the calls by time-labeled edges (e.g.
[
19
,
6
]
).
Most of the research in this line considers interactions that are mutually exclusive (an agent cannot have multiple phone calls at the same time) and non-repetitive (two agents cannot call each other twice).
One of the landmark results in this area was obtained in 1991 by Göbel et al.
[
17
]
, who showed that deciding whether a given graph admits such a labeling is
NP
-complete.

The past decade has seen a renewed interest in this type of problems, motivated by the modeling of various types of dynamic networks, such as wireless networks, social networks, autonomous vehicles, and transportation scheduling.
In today’s terminology, the gossip problem can naturally be rephrased in terms of temporal graphs.
Formally, a temporal graph is a pair
\mathcal{G}=(G,\lambda)
where
G=(V,E)
is the underlying graph (in this work, undirected) and
\lambda
is a mapping that assigns time labels to the edges.
Such a labeling is
simple
if every edge of
G
has a single time label, and it is
proper
if adjacent edges do not share any time label.
The graph
\mathcal{G}
is
temporally connected
(i.e.
in class
TC
) if there exists a path traversing edges with non-decreasing times (temporal path) between each ordered pair of nodes.
In this terminology, the gossip problem corresponds to deciding, given a graph
G
, if there exists a simple and proper labeling
\lambda
such that
\mathcal{G}=(G,\lambda)\in

TC
(and/or to find such a labeling).

A number of similar realizability questions have been considered recently in the temporal graph literature, focusing on labelings that satisfy additional prescribed properties.
For example, several works investigate whether the labeling can ensure that the fastest temporal paths between vertices match (or are upper-bounded by) a given matrix of pairwise durations
[
26
,
14
,
29
,
28
,
30
]
.
Another problem asks that the temporal paths realize a target reachability relation
[
13
]
, under various restrictions of the labeling function (see
[
7
,
10
]
for discussions on the resulting expressivity).
Further studies consider minimizing the number of labels
[
1
]
, minimizing reachability times
[
12
]
, maximizing overall reachability sets
[
5
]
, or avoiding temporal cycles
[
2
]
, to name a few.

Broadly speaking, all these problems, and their non-temporal versions before them, are part of a long series of
realizability questions
in graph theory, having their root in the seminal work of Erdős and Gallai
[
16
]
and Havel and Hakimi
[
22
,
20
]
on whether (and how, respectively) a given degree sequence can be realized as a (static) graph.

In this work, we return to the roots of realizability questions, studying the temporal analog of Erdős and Gallai’s problem, namely, whether (and how) a given degree sequence can be realized as a temporal graph in
TC
, for both the simple and non-simple cases.
This question is partly motivated by the hardness results of Göbel et al.
[
17
]
, a natural question being how to relax the input specification in a way that offers more flexibility (and tractability) for realization.
Another motivation is to investigate whether this format allows for an elegant and concise characterization of all the feasible cases, a situation that is pretty rare in the literature on temporal graphs.

1.1
Main results

Before stating the results, we recall that a
degree sequence

\mathbf{d}
is a non-increasing list of integers. A degree sequence of length
n
is
graphical
if there exist an
n
-vertex (static) graph whose vertices have exactly these values as their degrees. Moreover,
\mathbf{d}
is
multigraphical
if a multigraph with this degree distribution exists.

Our main result is a complete characterization of the graphical sequences that admit a realization as a simple and proper temporal graph in
TC
.
We refer to these sequences as
TC
-realizable graphical sequences
. The characterization, obtained in Section
5
, is the following:

Theorem 1
.

A graphical sequence
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
with
m:=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}
edges is
TC
-realizable if and only if one of the following holds:

•

m=2n-4
,
d_{1}<n-1
, and
d_{n}\geq 2

•

m\geq 2n-3
and either (i)
n\leq 2
or (ii)
n>2
,
d_{n-1}\geq 2
, and
d_{n}\geq 1
.

We also characterize the degree sequences that can be realized as a non-simple and proper temporal graph in
TC
, where
non-simple
means that a same edge can receive multiple time labels.
Conveniently, non-simple temporal graphs are equivalent to simple temporal graphs whose underlying graph is a multigraph and where each edge of the multigraph only receives one label.
Thus, the problem amounts to characterizing
TC
-realizable multigraphical sequences
. The characterization is:

Theorem 2
.

A multigraphical sequence
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
with
m:=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}
edges is
TC
-realizable if and only if one of the following holds:

•

m=2n-4
and
d_{n}\geq 2

•

m\geq 2n-3
and either (i)
n\leq 2

or (ii)
n>2
,
d_{n-1}\geq 2
, and
d_{n}\geq 1
.

As one can see, both characterizations are close to each other, the set of
TC
-realizable multigraphical sequences being naturally more general, as
TC
-realizable graphical sequences are special cases of these. However, the difference is more important than one may think by looking only at these theorems, because multigraphical sequences already differ from graphical sequences. (Both standard characterizations are recalled in the paper.)

On the algorithmic side, we show that
TC
-realizable graphical sequences can be recognized in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time and
TC
-realizable multigraphical sequences in
\mathcal{O}(n+m)
time. We also give a constructive
\mathcal{O}(n+m)
time algorithm that outputs a temporal graph satisfying the desired constraints (if one exists), this algorithm being asymptotically optimal.

The fact that we consider proper labelings is not a limitation. Precisely, if non-proper labelings are considered, then one may either consider strict (i.e. increasing) or non-strict (i.e. non-decreasing) temporal paths. In the case of strict temporal paths, we know that non-proper labelings can always be turned into proper labelings without reducing the reachability relation
[
7
]
. Together with the fact that proper labelings are particular cases of non-proper labelings, this implies that the feasible cases are the same as in the proper setting. If one considers non-strict temporal paths instead, then the problem becomes trivial to solve: a single spanning tree suffices (with the same time label on every edge). Thus, in this case, the problem reduces to testing if the sequence can be realized as a connected graph, which from earlier works
[
27
,
25
,
18
]
is the case if and only if
m\geq n-1
and
d_{n}\geq 1
.

1.2
Technical overview

As already mentioned, the characterization of
TC
-realizable graphs is known to be difficult. Some necessary and sufficient conditions are known from the work of Göbel et al.
[
17
]
(itself based on earlier works in gossip theory
[
21
,
19
,
6
]
), and the remaining cases are NP-hard to decide. A necessary condition for these graphs is to contain two spanning trees that share at most two edges.
If the graphs admit two spanning trees that share at most one edge, then this is sufficient: the graph is
TC
-realizable.
The case with two shared edges is further constrained. If the number of edges is exactly
2n-4
, then it is necessary and sufficient that the two shared edges belong to a central cycle of length
4
.
Göbel et al.
[
17
]
called such graphs “
C_{4}
graphs
” (although they do not consist only of a cycle of length 4). To avoid this confusion, we use the term
C_{4}
-pivotable
graphs instead. A formal definition is given in
Section

2
.
Otherwise, there may exist feasible cases which are difficult to characterize, and these cases are precisely the ones making the problem
NP
-hard.
Interestingly, our results imply that the framework of degree sequences allows one to avoid these hard cases.

Our characterization proceeds as follows: First, we note that the characterization of degree sequences which are realizable as a graph admitting two edge-disjoint spanning trees is already known
[
27
]
. Thus, we focus on characterizing the degree sequences that can be realized as a graph admitting two spanning trees sharing exactly one edge, and, for degree sequences corresponding to exactly
2n-4
edges, we characterize the ones admitting a realization with the above
C_{4}
-pivotable graph property.
Surprisingly, these cases already cover all the
TC
-realizable graphical sequences. Namely, if a graph has strictly more than
2n-4
edges and is
TC
-realizable, then its degree sequence could
also
be realized as a graph with two spanning trees sharing at most one edge, falling back on the previous cases. In other words, our analysis implies that the difficult graphs can safely be ignored in the framework of degree sequences.

In light of these explanations, the feasible cases stated in
Theorem

1
correspond respectively to the following theorems in the paper: A degree sequence is
TC
-realizable if and only if it admits (i) a realization which is a
C_{4}
-pivotable graph (
Theorem

25
) or (ii) a realization with two spanning trees that share at most one edge (
Theorem

17
).
For multigraphical sequences, we prove the characterization similarly.

All of our proofs are constructive and rely on deconstructing recursively the degree sequence to a smaller degree sequence, using gadgets that preserve two edge-disjoint spanning trees in the constructed graph (up to a central component).
These constructive proofs can be implemented efficiently by using suitable data structures that store the degree sequence and the respective graph efficiently.
The labeling is then handled by a dedicated procedure that achieves the claimed time using our data structures and extra features offered by the above algorithms.

Temporal graphs are notoriously intractable objects. Most of the results in this young field are negative and most of the problems turn out to be computationally hard, often due to the fact that the temporal reachability relation is neither symmetric nor transitive. In this respect, the fact the feasible cases for
TC
-realizability admit a characterization that is at the same time compact, purely structural, and easy to recognize is quite significant. This situation is clearly an exception in the landscape of temporal graph theory.

2
Definitions and Important Existing Results

A temporal graph is a pair
\mathcal{G}=(G,\lambda)
, where
G=(V,E)
is a standard (in this work,
undirected
) graph called the
underlying graph
of
\mathcal{G}
, and
\lambda:E\to 2^{\mathbb{N}}
is a labeling function that assigns a non-empty set of time labels to every edge of
E
, interpreted as availability times.
The labeling function
\lambda
can be restricted in various ways. It is called
proper
if adjacent edges cannot share a common time label (
\lambda
is locally injective), and it is called
simple
if every edge has exactly one time label (
\lambda
is single-valued). The typical setting of gossiping, including that of Göbel et al.
[
17
]
, requires that the labeling is both proper and simple.

A pair
(e,t)
such that
t\in\lambda(e)
is a
temporal edge
of
\mathcal{G}
.
A
temporal path
in
\mathcal{G}
is a sequence of temporal edges
\langle(e_{i},t_{i})\rangle
such that
\langle e_{i}\rangle
is a path in the underlying graph and
\langle t_{i}\rangle
is non-decreasing (such a path is
strict
if
\langle t_{i}\rangle
is increasing).
A temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
is
temporally connected
(in class
TC
) if temporal paths exist between all ordered pairs of nodes.
Observe that, beyond modeling mutually exclusive interaction, the proper setting has the technical advantage of removing the distinction between strict and non-strict temporal paths (indeed, all the temporal paths in this case are
de facto
strict), which allows us to rely on a single definition of
TC
throughout the paper.

We can now state the gossiping problem as follows:

Gossiping

Input:
An undirected graph
G=(V,E)
.

Question:
Does there exist a simple and proper labeling
\lambda
such that
(G,\lambda)\in\textsf{TC}
?

A
degree sequence
is a sequence
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
of non-negative integers. By convention, we always require that this sequence is ordered non-increasingly.
The original realizability question of Erdős and Gallai asks the following:

Degree Sequence Realization

Input:
A degree sequence
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
.

Question:
Does there exist a graph
G=(V,E)
with
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
such that
v_{i}
has degree
d_{i}
?

When the answer is yes, the degree sequence
\mathbf{d}
is called
graphical
and
G
is called a
realization
of
\mathbf{d}
.
If multiedges are permitted in
G
and if there is a multigraph that realizes
\mathbf{d}
, then the sequence is instead called
multigraphical

[
11
]
.
In this work, we study the following temporal version of the problem:

Degree Sequence TC Realization

Input:
A degree sequence
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
.

Question:
Does there exist a realization
G=(V,E)
of
\mathbf{d}
and a labeling
\lambda
such that
(G,\lambda)\in\textsf{TC}
?

Next, we state important existing results on (multi)graphical degree sequences and TC-realizable graphs and derive immediate consequences that we use throughout the paper.

2.1
Graphical Degree Sequences

To be
graphical
, a degree sequence must satisfy two properties: (i) the sum of all degrees must be even (standard handshaking lemma), and (ii) to avoid the need for multi-edges, the sum of degrees of
r
vertices cannot exceed what these vertices can actually absorb; namely, at most
r(r-1)
for the edges among themselves (sum of degrees in a complete graph on
r
vertices), plus at most
\min(r,d_{i})
for the edges to each remaining vertex
i
.
This is formalized in the following classical characterization.

Theorem 3

(Graphical sequence
[
16
,
31
]
)
.

A degree sequence
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
is graphical if and only if

(i)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}
is even, and

(ii)

for each
r\in[1,n-1]:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}\leq r\cdot(r-1)+\sum_{i=r+1}^{n}\min(r,d_{i}).

Based on this characterization, we obtain the following sufficient condition under which degree sequences with few edges are graphical.

Corollary 4
.

Let
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
be a degree sequence with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}
being even,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\leq 4(n-1)
,
n>4
,
d_{1}\leq n-1
, and
d_{n}\geq 2
.
Then
\mathbf{d}
is graphical if
d_{4}\geq 3
.

Proof.

According to
Theorem

3
, it suffices to show that for each
r\in[1,n-1]
,
\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}\leq r\cdot(r-1)+\sum_{i=r+1}^{n}\min(r,d_{i})
.
For
r=1
, the statement follows since
d_{1}\leq n-1=(n-1)\cdot 1
.

Consider
r=2
and observe that
d_{1}+d_{2}\leq 2(n-1)
.
Moreover,
\sum_{i=3}^{n}\min(r=2,d_{i})=2(n-2)
since
d_{n}\geq 2
.
Hence,
d_{1}+d_{2}\leq 2(n-1)=2+2(n-2)=2+\sum_{i=3}^{n}\min(r=2,d_{i})
, showing the statement for
r=2
.

Now, consider
r=3
.
Recall that
\sum\mathbf{d}=4(n-1)-c\leq 4(n-1)
,
d_{n}\geq 2
, and
d_{4}\geq 3
.
This implies that
\sum_{i=4}^{n}d_{i}\geq\sum_{i=4}^{n}\min(r=3,d_{i})\geq 3+(n-4)\cdot 2=2(n-1)-3
.
Thus,
d_{1}+d_{2}+d_{3}=\sum\mathbf{d}-\sum_{i=4}^{n}d_{i}\leq 4(n-1)-(2(n-1)-3)=2(n-1)+3
.
Consequently,
d_{1}+d_{2}+d_{3}\leq 2(n-1)+3=6+2(n-1)-3\leq r\cdot(r-1)+\sum_{i=4}^{n}\min(r,d_{i})
, showing the statement for
r=3
.

Finally, consider
r\geq 4
.
Recall that
\sum\mathbf{d}\leq 4(n-1)
and
d_{n}\geq 2
. The latter implies
\sum_{i=r+1}^{n}\min(r,d_{i})\geq 2(n-r)
. Consequently,
\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}=\sum\mathbf{d}-\sum_{i=r+1}^{n}d_{i}\leq 4(n-1)-2(n-r)=2(n-1)+2(r-1)
.
Moreover,
r\cdot(r-1)\geq 4(r-1)
since
r\geq 4
.
This implies that
\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}\leq 2(n-1)+2(r-1)=4(r-1)+2(n-r)\leq r\cdot(r-1)+\sum_{i=r+1}^{n}\min(r,d_{i})\geq 2(n-r)
, showing the statement for
r\geq 4
.
∎

We will make use of this corollary several times in this work to easily argue that specific degree sequences are graphical.
Similarly, we also obtain the following sufficient condition.

Corollary 5
.

Let
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
be a degree sequence with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}=4(n-1)-2
,
n>4
,
d_{1}\leq n-1
,
d_{n-1}\geq 2
, and
d_{n}\geq 1
.
Then
\mathbf{d}
is graphical if
d_{4}\geq 3
.

Sketch.

The statement can be shown analogously to
Corollary

4
.
The only differences are that for each
r\in[2,n-1]
,
\sum_{i=r+1}^{n}\min(r,d_{i})
is smaller by at most one because
d_{n}=1
and
d_{n-1}\geq 2
, and that
\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}
is smaller by at least one since
\sum\mathbf{d}
is reduced by 2 compared to
Corollary

4
.
Hence,
\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}\leq r\cdot(r-1)+\sum_{i=r+1}^{n}\min(r,d_{i})
holds for each
r\in[1,n-1]
.
∎

The following algorithm constructs a graph from a graphical degree sequence.

Definition 6

(Graphical Laying Off Process
[
20
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
be a graphical sequence.
The
laying off
procedure consists of connecting the vertex
v_{i}
to the first
d_{i}
vertices, excluding
v_{i}
. The resulting
residual sequence
is given by:

\displaystyle(d_{1}-1,\dots,d_{d_{i}}-1,d_{d_{i}+1},\dots,d_{i-1},d_{i+1},\dots,d_{n})

\displaystyle\text{if }d_{i}<i,

\displaystyle(d_{1}-1,\dots,d_{i-1}-1,d_{i+1}-1,\dots,d_{d_{i}+1}-1,d_{d_{i}+2},\dots,d_{n})

\displaystyle\text{if }d_{i}\geq i.

The next lemma implies that the laying off process can be used iteratively to build a realization for a graphical degree sequence.

Lemma 7

(
[
15
,
20
]
)
.

If
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
is a graphical sequence, then the residual sequence after laying off any entry is also graphical.

2.2
Multigraphical degree sequences

To be
multigraphical
, a degree sequence should also satisfy that the sum of degrees are even (handshaking lemma again), but since multi-edges are permitted, the other terms are not as restricted as for graphical sequences. The only restriction is that the highest-degree vertex can distribute all its edges among the remaining vertices.
This is formalized as follows.

Theorem 8

(Multigraphical sequence
[
20
]
)
.

A degree sequence
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
is multigraphical if and only if

(i)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}
is even, and

(ii)

\displaystyle d_{1}\leq\sum_{i=2}^{n}d_{i}
.

For multigraphical sequences there also exists a “laying off” process which is similar to
Definition

6
, but more flexible.
Contrary to the graphical case, where a whole degree is laid off, in the multigraphical case, only a single edge is laid off.

Theorem 9

(Multigraphical Laying Off Process
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
be a multigraphical degree sequence with
d_{n}>0
.
Then, for each
j,2\leq j\leq n
, the degree sequence
(d_{1}-1,d_{2},\dots,d_{j-1},d_{j}-1,d_{j+1},\dots,d_{n})
is multigraphical (after reordering).

2.3
(Multi)graphical sequences admitting two edge-disjoint spanning trees

The characterization of (multi)graphical sequences that admit a realization containing
k
edge-disjoint spanning trees is identical for graphical and multigraphical sequences.
For graphical sequences, this result was first established for the special case of
k=2
by Kundu
[
27
]
through a constructive proof, and two years later generalized to arbitrary
k
by Kleitman and Wang
[
25
]
, also constructively. For multigraphical sequences, this statement was proven non-constructively by Gu, Hai and Liang
[
18
]
. We are primarily interested in the case
k=2
. Nevertheless, we state the full characterization for general
k
below.

Theorem 10

(
[
27
,
25
,
18
]
)
.

A (multi)graphical sequence
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
with
n\geq 2
admits a (multigraphical) realization with
k\in\mathbb{N}
edge-disjoint spanning trees if and only if

(i)

d_{n}\geq k
, and

(ii)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\geq 2\cdot k(n-1)
.

2.4
TC-realizable graphs

Finally, we provide an overview on some known necessary and sufficient conditions of TC-realizable (multi)graphs.

Lemma 11

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
G
be a TC-realizable (multi)graph on
n
vertices.
Then
G
has at least
2n-4
edges.

Lemma 12

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
G
be a (multi)graph with two spanning trees that share at most one edge.
Then
G
is TC-realizable.

Definition 13

(Reformulation of Göbel et al.
[
17
]
)
.

A graph
G
on
n
vertices and
2n-4
edges is a
C_{4}
-pivotable graph
if
G
contains an induced cycle
C
of length
4
(called a

) and two spanning trees that share exactly two edges, where both shared edges are from
C
.

This definition is equivalent to the one provided by Göbel et al.
[
17
]
, since no tree can contain more than three edges of the central cycle, and due to the number of edges, each edge of the cycle is contained in at least one of the two spanning trees.

Lemma 14

(
[
17
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph with
n
vertices and
2n-4
edges.
Then
G
is TC-realizable if and only if
G
is a
C_{4}
-pivotable graph.

Even though it is not explicitly stated
[
17
]
, the following generalization of
C_{4}
-pivotable graphs to multigraphs is also TC-realizable by the same labeling procedure used for
C_{4}
-pivotable graphs.

1

1

We will recall this labeling procedure in

Definition 15

Lemma 18

Let
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})

Let
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})

\mathbf{d}

\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}=4(n-1)-4

Lemma 26

Let

C_{4}
-pivotable graph on

has a minimum degree of at least
2
and a maximum degree of at most

.

Proof.

Recall that by definition of a

-pivotable graph,

By definition, each vertex

of the

-pivotable graphs

is (i) part of the central cycle or (ii) a vertex that has at least one incident edge in each of the two spanning trees.
In both cases, vertex

has degree at least 2.
Hence, the minimum degree of

is at least 2.

We now show that
G
also has a maximum degree of at most
n-2
.
We show this statement via induction over

.

For

, there is only a single
C_{4}
-pivotable graph, namely the cycle of length 4 itself, which has a maximum degree of
2=n-2
.
For

, the only (non-isomorphic) graph with minimum degree at least
2
and maximum degree

that has

that contains a universal vertex

and where

is a perfect matching of size
2
.
Hence,
G^{\prime}
contains no induced cycle of length
4
, which implies that
G^{\prime}
is not a
C_{4}
-pivotable graph.

For the inductive step, assume that

and that for

, there is no
C_{4}
-pivotable graph on

vertices with maximum degree

.

Assume towards a contradiction that there is a

-pivotable graph

on

.
Then, the minimum degree of

is exactly

would exceed

.
Let

be a vertex of degree

, which has degree

5
The Characterizations for TC-realizable Sequences

We now prove the complete characterization for graphical and multigraphical TC-realizable sequences.

5.1
Realizations of TC-realizable Graphs

We now prove our characterization of TC-realizable graphical sequences:

Theorem 33
.

A graphical sequence
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
with
s=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}
is TC-realizable if and only if one of the following holds

•

s=4(n-1)-4
,
d_{1}<n-1
, and
d_{n}\geq 2

•

s\geq 4(n-1)-2
and (a)
n\leq 2
or (b)
n>2
,
d_{n-1}\geq 2
, and
d_{n}\geq 1
.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)

If
\sum\mathbf{d}=4(n-1)-4
,
d_{1}<n-1
, and
d_{n}\geq 2
, then due to
Theorem

25
,
\mathbf{d}
has a realization
G
which is a
C_{4}
-pivotable graph.
By Harary and Schwenk
[
21
]
this implies that
G
is TC-realizable and thus that
\mathbf{d}
is TC-realizable.

If
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq 4(n-1)-2
and (i)
n\leq 2
or (ii)
n>2
,
d_{n-1}\geq 2
, and
d_{n}\geq 1
, then
\mathbf{d}
admits a realization
G
which has two spanning trees that share at most one edge (see
Theorem

17
).
By Harary and Schwenk
[
21
]
this implies that
G
is TC-realizable and thus that
\mathbf{d}
is TC-realizable.

(\Rightarrow)

Suppose that
\mathbf{d}
is TC-realizable and let
G
be one of its realizations.
By Hajnal et al.
[
19
]
,
G
has at least
2n-4
edges, which implies
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq 4(n-1)-4
.

First, consider the case that
\sum\mathbf{d}=4(n-1)-4
.
That is,
G
has
2n-4
edges.
Hence, by Göbel et al.
[
17
]
,
G
is a
C_{4}
-pivotable graph.
Thus, by
Theorem

25
,
d_{1}<n-1
, and
d_{n}\geq 2
.

Second, consider the case that
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq 4(n-1)-2
.
Since
G
is TC-realizable,
G
is connected.
For
n\leq 2
,
\mathbf{d}
is from
\{(0),(1,1)\}
as these are the only graphical sequences of length at most 2 fulfilling
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq 4(n-1)-2
.
Thus, consider
n>2
.
Since
G
is connected and
n>2
,
G
has a minimum degree of 1, which implies that
d_{n}\geq 1
.
It remains to show that
d_{n-1}\geq 2
.
To this end, assume towards a contradiction that
d_{n-1}=d_{n}=1
.
Then,
G
contains at least two vertices
u
and
v
of degree 1.
These vertices are not adjacent in
G
, as otherwise,
G
would not be connected.
Hence, the unique edge
e_{u}
incident with
u
and the unique edge
e_{v}
incident with
v
are distinct.
Since
G
is TC-realizable, there is a TC-labeling of the edges of
G
.
To ensure that there is a temporal path from
u
to
v
, this labeling has to assign a label to
e_{u}
which is strictly smaller than the label assigned to
e_{v}
.
But simultaneously, to ensure that there is a temporal path from
v
to
u
, the label assigned to
e_{v}
has to be strictly smaller than the label assigned to
e_{u}
; a contradiction.
Consequently,
G
contains no two vertices of degree

, which implies that
d_{n-1}\geq 2
and
d_{n}\geq 1
in the case that
n>2
.
∎

We now prove our characterization for TC-realizable multigraphical sequences:

Theorem 34

A multigraphical sequence
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
with
s:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}

•

and (a)
n\leq 2
or (b)
n>2

To prove the statement, we again consider both items independently and mostly use the same arguments as for the graphical case.

5.3
Intermediate characterization for spanning trees with one shared edge

First, we provide a characterization for multigraphical sequences that allow for a realization that has two spanning trees with at most one shared edge.

Theorem 35
.

Let
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
be a multigraphical sequence.
Then,
\mathbf{d}
admits a realization with two spanning trees that share at most one edge if and only if

\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\geq 4(n-1)-2
and (a)
n\leq 2
or (b)
n>2
,
d_{n-1}\geq 2
, and
d_{n}\geq 1
.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)

Suppose that
\mathbf{d}
admits a realization
G
with two spanning trees that share at most one edge.
For the (multi)graph
G
to have two spanning trees that share at most one edge,
G
needs at least
2(n-1)-1
edges.
This implies
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\geq 4(n-1)-2
.
Further, by the same arguments used to prove
Lemma

18
, we get that either
n\leq 2
or, if
n>2
,
d_{n-1}\geq 2
and
d_{n}\geq 1
.
This was due to the fact that if any of these conditions would not hold, then each realization of
\mathbf{d}
is disconnected or contains at least two distinct edges incident with degree-1 vertices; hence, no realization with two spanning trees that share at most one edge is possible.
This completes the proof of this direction.

(\Leftarrow)

First consider the case of
n\leq 2
.
If
n=1
, then
(0)
is the only multigraphical sequence, for which the unique realization has two spanning trees that share no edge.
If
n=2
, then each multigraphical sequence
(d_{1},d_{2})
fulfills

. Out of all such sequences,
(0,0)
is the only that does not fulfill
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq 4(n-1)-2
. Every other such sequence has a unique realization and this realization is connected.
Since
n=2

In the remainder, consider

Then, by laying off

.
Recall that laying off

has a realization

and

.
Let

of degree

.
Then, the multigraph

to

.

.

Thus, consider

.
If
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq 4(n-1)
, then

10
implies that
\mathbf{d}
has a realization with two disjoint spanning trees, which shows the statement under this assumptions.
Hence, it remains to consider the case that
\sum\mathbf{d}=4(n-1)-2
.
Note that this implies that

.
We distinguish between the values of

If

It thus remains to consider the case that

Consider the degree sequence
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}
obtained by removing

\mathbf{d}

Then,

5.4
Intermediate characterization for
C_{4}
-pivotable multigraphs

Next, we provide a characterization for multigraphical sequences that allow for a realization which is a
C_{4}
-pivotable multigraph.

Theorem 36
.

Let
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
be a multigraphical sequence.
Then,
\mathbf{d}
admits a realization which is a
C_{4}
-pivotable multigraph if and only if

\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}=4(n-1)-4
and

.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)

By definition, each
C_{4}
-pivotable multigraph on
n
vertices has exactly
2n-4
multiedges.
Hence,
\sum\mathbf{d}=4(n-1)-4
holds.
Next, we show that

also has to hold.
The argument is identical to the one in the proof of
Lemma

26
:
By definition, each vertex
v
of the
C_{4}
-pivotable multigraph

is (i) part of the central cycle or (ii) a vertex that has at least one incident multiedge in each of the two spanning trees that only share edges of the central cycle.
In both cases, vertex
v
has degree at least 2.
Hence, the minimum degree of

is at least 2, which implies that
d_{n}\geq 2
to allow for a realization which is a
C_{4}
-pivotable multigraph.
This completes this direction.

(\Leftarrow)

We have to show that
\mathbf{d}
has a realization which is a

-pivotable multigraph if

and

.
Note that this implies that

.
To show the statement, we distinguish between the possible values of
d_{n-2}
.

If

, then
\sum\mathbf{d}-d_{1}\geq 3(n-3)+d_{n-1}+d_{n}\geq 3(n-3)+4=3(n-1)-2

By
\sum\mathbf{d}=4(n-1)-4
, this implies

Thus, by
Corollary

4
,
\mathbf{d}
is also a graphical sequence, since
d_{4}\geq 3
.
This is due to the fact that
d_{n-2}\geq 3
and

, as otherwise,
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq 3n>4(n-1)-4
.
Hence,
Theorem

25
thus implies that
\mathbf{d}
admits a realization which is a
C_{4}
-pivotable graph (and thus a
C_{4}
-pivotable multigraph).

Hence, we only need to consider the case that
d_{n-2}=2=d_{n-1}=d_{n}
.
First, we consider special cases:
\mathbf{d}\in\{(2,2,2,2),(3,3,2,2,2)\}
.
Note that these are the only two options for
\mathbf{d}
for which
\sum\mathbf{d}=4(n-1)-4
,
d_{1}\leq 4
, and
d_{n-2}=d_{n-1}=d_{n}=2
. Both these degree sequences are graphical and fulfill
d_{1}<n-1
and

.
Hence, by
Theorem

25
,
\mathbf{d}
admits a realization which is a
C_{4}
-pivotable graph (and thus a
C_{4}
-pivotable multigraph).

For all other options of
\mathbf{d}
, we can thus assume that
d_{1}\geq 4
.

We upper-bound the values of
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
.
Since

,
\sum\mathbf{d}-d_{1}\geq 2(n-1)
.
Moreover, by
\sum\mathbf{d}=4(n-1)-4

.
Furthermore,

, as otherwise,

.
With these upper bounds for

and

which is a

-pivotable multigraph.

Let
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}:=(d_{1}^{\prime},\dots,d_{n^{\prime}}^{\prime})
be the degree sequence with

obtained from

by (i) removing

,

by

Hence,

Moreover,
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}
fulfills
\sum\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=\sum\mathbf{d}-8=4(n-1)-4-8=4(n^{\prime}-1)

Thus,

is multigraphical by

8
.
Furthermore,

by the fact that

,

Theorem

35
thus implies that
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}
has a realization

which has two disjoint spanning trees

and

.

We obtain a realization

denote an arbitrary vertex of

with degree

We add three new vertices

, and

Note that

.

With these characterizations, we can prove our characterization for TC-realizable multigraphical sequences, namely

.

Proof of
Theorem

34
.

The proof is very similar to the one of
Theorem

33
.

(\Leftarrow)

If
s=4(n-1)-4
and

, then due to
Theorem

36
,
\mathbf{d}
has a realization

-pivotable multigraph.

\mathbf{d}
is TC-realizable.

If
s\geq 4(n-1)-2
and (a)

or (b)

,

, and

, then
\mathbf{d}
admits a realization

which has two spanning trees that share at most one edge (see

35
).
By
Lemma

12
this implies that
G
is TC-realizable and thus that
\mathbf{d}
is TC-realizable.

(\Rightarrow)

Suppose that
\mathbf{d}
is TC-realizable and let

be one of its realizations.
By Hajnal et al.
[
19
]
,
G
has at least
2n-4
edges, which implies
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq 4(n-1)-4
.

Firstly, consider the case that
\sum\mathbf{d}=4(n-1)-4
.
We need to show that
d_{n}\geq 2
.
Note that
G
has exactly
2n-4
edges.
Assume towards a contradiction that
d_{n}\leq 1
.
Then,
G
contains at least one vertex
v_{n}
of degree at most
1
.
If
v_{n}
has degree
0
,
G
is not connected; a contradiction to the fact that
G
is TC-realizable.
Otherwise, there exists a single edge
e_{n}
incident with

.
Let
\lambda\colon E\to\mathbb{N}
be a TC labeling of

and let

be the unique neighbor of

in

.
Since

is the only edge incident with

, all temporal paths from and towards

traverse edge

at time
\lambda(e_{n})
.
Moreover, since each vertex of

has a temporal path from and to

, this implies that for each vertex

of

there is (i) a temporal path from

that reaches

prior to label

and (ii) a temporal path from

to

that starts later than label

.
This means in particular that

Hence,

This contradicts the fact that

.

Secondly, consider the case that
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq 4(n-1)-2
.
Since

is connected.
For

, all multigraphical sequences have connected realizations apart from
(0,0)
, which must not be considered as it does not fulfill
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq 4(n-1)-2
.
Hence, consider

.
Since

is connected and

.
It remains to show that

.
To this end, assume towards a contradiction that

Then,

6
Linear-Time Algorithm for Realizing TC (Multi)graphs

In the previous section, we presented a characterization of (multi)graphical degree sequences that are TC-realizable (see
Theorems

33
and
34
).
These necessary and sufficient conditions can be checked in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time in the graphical case and in
\mathcal{O}(n+m)
time in the multigraphical case, where
m:=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum\mathbf{d}
denotes the number of edges in each realization.
Hence, in linear time, we can detect whether there is a TC-realizable graph for the given degree sequence.
In this section, we go one step further and show that in both the graphical and multigraphical case, we can construct such a realization (together with a TC labeling) in
\mathcal{O}(n+m)
time.
To this end, we observe that all existential proofs in the previous sections are constructive and allow for linear time implementations with the help of two simple data structures.

6.1
The data structures

In the following, we assume that the graphs are stored via adjacency lists.
Since our arguments rely on spanning trees, we assign two Boolean flags to each edge to indicate whether this edge belongs to the first tree, the second tree, both trees, or neither trees.
This allows us to extract the two spanning trees in
\mathcal{O}(n+m)
time, once the final realization is constructed.

The data structure for degree sequences is as follows.
Instead of storing a degree sequence as a sequence of numbers, we store it as a double-linked list that stores entries of the form
(x,c)
.
Such entries indicate that the respective degree sequence contains exactly
c
entries of value
x
.
The entries of the double-linked list are sorted decreasingly according to their
x
-values.
In this way, for both the graphical and multigraphical cases, we can lay off a degree
d_{i}
in
\mathcal{O}(d_{i})
time, since we only need to remove the
d_{i}
entry and rearrange the
c
-values of at most
d_{i}+1
previous entries of the data structure.
Using this data structure, we can ensure that the degree sequence always stays sorted and no reordering is necessary.

Similarly, the data structure for (multi)graphs
G
is as follows.
It is also a double-linked list and stores entries of the form
(x,S)
.
Such entries indicate that the vertices of
S
are exactly the vertices of
G
that have degree exactly
x
.
These entries are also sorted decreasingly according to their
x
-values.
This way, we can reattach a vertex of degree
d_{n}
to
G
by adding edges properly to vertices of the first
d_{n}
entries of the data structure.
That is, we can obtain a realization for the degree sequence that, after laying off a vertex of degree
d_{n}
, leads to the degree sequence of
G
.
In particular, this can be done in
\mathcal{O}(d_{n})
time.

In case of a realization for a
C_{4}
-pivotable (multi)graph, we also output the central cycle separately.

6.2
Realizations for two edge-disjoint spanning trees

We now turn to the algorithmic aspects of finding a realization with two edge-disjoint spanning trees and show that such a realization can be computed in linear time.

For the graphical case, we used the result by Kundu
[
27
]
. While their proof is constructive in nature, the arguments are brief and incomplete.
Thus, we give a complete construction in
Lemma

37
and argue how it can be implemented in
\mathcal{O}(n+m)
time using our data structure.

For the multigraphical case, we build on the result by Gu et al.
[
18
]
, which was proven non-constructively.
In
Lemma

39
, we give a complete inductive construction for such multigraphical sequences, and argue that this construction, too, can be carried out in
\mathcal{O}(n+m)
time using our data structures.

Lemma 37
.

Let
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
be a graphical sequence with
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq 4(n-1)
and
d_{n}\geq 2
.
We can compute a realization
G
of
\mathbf{d}
and two disjoint spanning trees of
G
in
\mathcal{O}(n+m)
time.

Proof.

To prove the statement, we show that for each
n
and for each graphical sequence
\mathbf{d}
of length
n
with
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq 4(n-1)
and
d_{n}\geq 2
, we can construct a realization with two edge-disjoint spanning trees. We show this statement via induction over

.

For the base case, note that for
n\in\{1,2,3\}
, there is no graphical sequence of length
n
that sums up to
4(n-1)
.
Moreover, for

,
(3,3,3,3)
is the only graphical sequence of length
n
with
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq 4(n-1)
and
d_{n}\geq 2
.
Since the complete graph on four vertices, which obviously contains two edge-disjoint spanning trees, is the only realization of
\mathbf{d}
, the base case holds for

.

For the inductive step, let
n\geq 5
and assume that the statement holds for

.
Let
\mathbf{d}
be an arbitrary graphical sequence of length

fulfilling
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq 4(n-1)
and
d_{n}\geq 2
. We show that
\mathbf{d}
admits a realization with two edge-disjoint spanning trees.
Observe that whenever
\sum\mathbf{d}=4(n-1)
, it holds that

as otherwise
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq 4n
.
We distinguish two cases depending on whether
\sum\mathbf{d}=4(n-1)
and

or not, and begin by addressing the latter.

Case 1:
If
\sum\mathbf{d}=4(n-1)
and
d_{n}=2
, or
\sum\mathbf{d}>4(n-1)
and
d_{n}\geq 2
, consider the residual sequence
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=(d_{1}^{\prime},\dots,d_{n^{\prime}}^{\prime})

according to
Definition

Due to

7
,
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}
is graphical.
To show that
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}
admits a realization containing two edge-disjoint spanning trees, we have to show that

and
\sum\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\geq 4(n-2)
. We distinguish by the value of

.

If

, then
d_{2}>2
as otherwise

. Hence,

since

, and
\sum\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=\sum\mathbf{d}-4\geq 4(n-1)-4=4(n-2)
.
If

, then

. Recall that
\sum\mathbf{d}
is even and larger than
4(n-1)
. Hence
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq 4(n-1)+2
, and it follows

\sum\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=\sum\mathbf{d}-6\geq 4(n-1)+2-6=4(n-2)
.
If
d_{n}\geq 4
, then
d^{\prime}_{n^{\prime}}\geq d_{n-1}-1\geq 4-1=3
, and it follows from
\sum\mathbf{d}\geq n\cdot d_{n}
that
\sum\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=\sum\mathbf{d}-2d_{n}\geq n\cdot d_{n}-2d_{n}=(n-2)\cdot d_{n}\geq 4(n-2)
.

In all three cases,
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}
admits a realization
G^{\prime}
containing two edge-disjoint spanning trees
T_{1}^{\prime}
and
T_{2}^{\prime}
by the induction hypothesis.
Now consider the graph
G
obtained from

by adding a vertex

of degree

and connecting it to
d_{n}
distinct vertices

of

of degree

. Then

is a realization of
\mathbf{d}
. Moreover,

and

.

Case 2:
Otherwise,
\sum\mathbf{d}=4(n-1)
and
d_{n}=3
. In that case, consider the sequence
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}
obtained from
\mathbf{d}
by reducing

by 1 and removing the entry

.

We show that
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}
is graphical and admits a realization with two edge-disjoint spanning trees.
Note that
\sum\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=4(n-1)-1-3=4(n-2)
is even and
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}
has length

. We distinguish by the value of

.

If

, then by

and

\mathbf{d}=(4,3,3,3,3)
and
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=(3,3,3,3)
. We showed in the base case that
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=(3,3,3,3)
is graphical and admits a realization

with two edge-disjoint spanning trees

and
T_{2}^{\prime}
.

If

, observe that

and

(otherwise

\sum\mathbf{d}=2(n-1)+3(n-2)>4(n-1)
). Furthermore,

by

and

.
It now follows from
Corollary

6.4
Computing a TC labeling

Recall that during the construction of the TC-realizable graph
G
, we always stored which edges belong to which of the two spanning trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, allowing us to extract both trees in linear time.
By construction, the trees are edge-disjoint, share exactly one edge
e
, or share exactly two edges, in which case
G
contains a central cycle
C
.
In the latter two cases, the output was
e
or
C
.

We now describe how to efficiently label the edges of
G
using the extracted trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, the potential edge
e
, and the potential central cycle
C
.
The labeling procedures are based on the classical approach by Baker and Shostak
[
3
]
, which we show can be executed in linear time, given
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
,
e
and
C
.
For the sake of completeness, we recall these procedures.

The labelings rely on the general idea that each vertex travels “up” along
T_{1}
to reach a central structure—a central vertex, an overlapping edge
e
, or an overlapping central cycle
C
—and then “down” along
T_{2}
to reach every other vertex. This scheme is referred to as
pivot labeling
in recent literature
[
8
,
9
]
, where the central structure acts as a pivot (turning point).

We begin by assigning a common root
v_{r}
to both
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
.

•

If
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are edge-disjoint, we choose an arbitrary vertex as the root.

•

If the trees share an edge or we return a central cycle, we contract the edge or cycle into a single root vertex.

Next, we label the edges of
T_{1}
in increasing order from the leaves to the root:

•

Initialize a label counter
t=0
, and
X
as the set
L
of leaves of
T_{1}
.

•

Assign the label
t+i
to the edge
\ell_{i}p_{i}
where
\ell_{i}\in X
and
p_{i}
is its parent in
T
. Set
t=t+L
and
X=\{p_{i}:1\leq i\leq L\}
.

•

Assign the label
t+i
to the unlabeled edge
p_{i}q_{i}
where
p_{i}\in X
and
q_{i}
is its parent in
T
.
Set
t=t+\lvert X\rvert
and
X=\{q_{i}:1\leq i\leq\lvert X\rvert\}
.

•

Continue this process until all paths from the leaves have reached the root, i. e.,
X=\{v_{r}\}
.

In this way, the edges in
T_{1}
are labeled in increasing order as we move from the leaves to the root, and every vertex reaches the root by some time
t_{r}
.

Next, we label the central structure. If the trees are edge-disjoint, no additional labeling is required. If they share a single edge
e
, we assign it label
t_{r}+1
to ensure that all vertices can traverse the edge. If there exists a shared central cycle
C=(a,b,c,d)
, we assign label
t_{r}+1
to the opposite edges
ab
and
cd
, and label
t_{r}+2
to the remaining two edges, that is,
\lambda(ab)=\lambda(cd)=t_{r}+1
and
\lambda(bc)=\lambda(da)=t_{r}+2
.

Finally, we label the edges of
T_{2}
in decreasing order from the root to the leaves:

•

Initialize a label counter
t=t_{r}+3
,
X=\{v_{r}\}
, and
S_{v_{r}}
as the set of children of
v_{r}
in
T_{2}
.

•

Assign the label
t+i
to the unlabeled edge
v_{r}s_{i}
where
s_{i}\in X_{v_{r}}
. Set
t=t+\lvert S_{v_{r}}\rvert
,
X=S_{v_{r}}
, and
S_{s_{i}}
as the set of children of each
s_{i}
in
T_{2}
.

•

For each vertex
x\in X
, assign the label
t+i
to the unlabeled edge
xy_{i}
where
y_{i}\in S_{x}
. Set
t=t+\lvert S_{x}\rvert
,
X=X-x\cup S_{x}
, and
S_{y_{i}}
as the set of children of each
y_{i}
in
T_{2}
.

•

Repeat this process until all edges of
T_{2}
have been labeled, i. e.,
X=\emptyset
.

In this way, the edges in
T_{1}
are labeled in increasing order as we move from the root to the leaves, and every vertex is reachable from the root with a temporal path starting at
t_{r}+3
.

Given the trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
and the potential overlapping edge
e
or central cycle
C
, this labeling procedure can be executed in time
\mathcal{O}(n+m)
via BFS algorithms on both spanning trees.

We conclude the following algorithmic main result of our work.

Theorem 41
.

Let
\mathbf{d}
be a (multi)graphical degree sequence.
We can decide in
\mathcal{O}(n+m)
time whether
\mathbf{d}
is TC-realizable.
If this is the case, then in
\mathcal{O}(n+m)
time, we can compute a (multi)graph
G
which is a realization of
\mathbf{d}
together with a TC labeling for
G
.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we presented complete characterizations of graphical and multigraphical degree sequences that admit temporally connected realizations under proper labelings.
All characterizations presented in this paper are constructive and lead to linear-time algorithms for constructing TC realizations (including the corresponding labelings), provided that the input satisfies the corresponding conditions.

As explained in the introduction, the fact that we only considered proper labelings is not a limitation, as the remaining cases involving non-proper labelings are either trivial or covered by our techniques.
Thus, our results cover directly or indirectly all the possible combinations of settings among proper/non-proper, simple/non-simple, and strict/non-strict.

Several natural questions could follow from this work.
For instance, one may ask to enumerate all TC-realizable graphs for a given degree sequence, or ask for realizations with additional constraints such as bounded lifetime, bounded diameter, or the existence of temporal spanners of a certain size.
Another promising avenue is to optimize over these criteria while searching for a realization.
Finally, the realizability question studied in this paper could be generalized to temporal
k
-connectivity or to directed temporal graphs.
