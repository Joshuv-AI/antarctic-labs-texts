The Neuroendocrine-Cardiovascular Axis in Chronic Kidney Disease: The Role of Prolactin and Gonadal Dysfunction
Authors: Vincenzo Barbera, Luca Di Lullo, Antonio Bellasi, Lorenzo D’Elia, Annalisa Villani, Paola Peverini, Aldo Franculli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) is accompanied by endocrine disturbances, including elevated prolactin and disruption of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis. Their reproductive consequences are well recognized, whereas their cardiovascular relevance remains uncertain.SummaryReduced renal metabolism, altered dopaminergic regulation, the uremic milieu, inflammation, and comorbidity may increase prolactin and suppress gonadal function. In men, testosterone deficiency becomes more common as kidney function declines. In women, the dominant phenotype is loss of cyclicity, menstrual disturbance, anovulation, and a shortened reproductive lifespan; a single low estradiol value does not adequately characterize this heterogeneous state. Experimental studies identify candidate endothelial, nitric-oxide, inflammatory, thrombotic, and body-composition pathways. Clinical evidence, however, is predominantly observational, is more extensive in men than women, and is inconsistent for prolactin. Non-dialysis CKD and dialysis cohorts are therefore considered separately. Hormone measurement also requires attention to assay method, biological variability, acute illness, medication exposure, menstrual status, and timing relative to dialysis.Key MessagesProlactin and gonadal dysfunction are best viewed as components of a hypothesis-generating neuroendocrine-cardiovascular framework rather than established causal cardiovascular risk factors. No evidence supports routine dopamine-agonist treatment of asymptomatic CKD-related hyperprolactinemia or hormone therapy for cardiovascular prevention. Symptom-directed endocrine evaluation, rigorous diagnosis, and CKD-specific prospective and interventional studies are needed.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, Hyperprolactinemia, Cardiovascular risk, Hypogonadism, Testosterone, Endocrine dysfunction

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a systemic disorder defined and risk-stratified by cause, glomerular filtration rate (GFR), albuminuria, and, when appropriate, validated kidney-failure risk equations. The 2024 KDIGO guideline places cardiovascular risk reduction within comprehensive CKD care, including blood pressure control, renin-angiotensin system blockade when indicated, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibition, management of diabetes and dyslipidemia, and attention to lifestyle and complications [1]. Even with such measures, CKD remains strongly associated with cardiovascular events, hospitalization, and death [2, 3].

Endocrine disturbances are part of this systemic phenotype. Elevated prolactin (PRL) and hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis dysfunction become more common as kidney function declines and are especially prominent in people receiving dialysis [4, 5]. Their consequences for libido, fertility, menstrual health, bone, erythropoiesis, and body composition are clinically relevant. Whether PRL excess or altered sex hormones independently mediate cardiovascular disease (CVD), rather than reflect kidney dysfunction, inflammation, malnutrition, medication exposure, and comorbidity, is less clear.

The original concept of a neuroendocrine-cardiovascular axis remains useful if it is treated as an organizing model rather than a proven causal chain. In that model, kidney dysfunction perturbs hormone clearance and central regulation; the same uremic and inflammatory environment affects vascular and metabolic biology; and hormonal abnormalities may mark or modify these processes. This distinction matters because a biomarker associated with outcome is not necessarily a therapeutic target.

This narrative review evaluates PRL and gonadal dysfunction across the CKD continuum, separates non-dialysis from dialysis evidence, and distinguishes mechanistic, observational, systematic-review, and interventional data. It also addresses female-specific evidence, practical hormone assessment, and the limits of current treatment data. Search Strategy and Evidence Selection

For this updated narrative review, PubMed/MEDLINE was searched from database inception through May 2026. The core search was ((“chronic kidney disease” OR CKD OR dialysis OR “kidney failure”) AND (prolactin OR hyperprolactinemia OR hypogonad* OR “gonadal dysfunction” OR testosterone OR estradiol OR estrogen) AND (cardiovascular OR endotheli* OR “arterial stiffness” OR inflamm* OR “oxidative stress” OR mortality)). Targeted searches combined each hormone term with measurement, assay, treatment, dopamine agonist, hormone therapy, menstrual health, or fertility. Reference lists of eligible studies and relevant guidelines were hand-searched.

Peer-reviewed English-language human studies were prioritized. Original cohort studies and trials were preferred for clinical claims; systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and guidelines were used to contextualize consistency, diagnostic practice, and treatment. Experimental and ex vivo studies were included only when they addressed a prespecified candidate pathway. Studies were grouped as mechanistic, observational non-dialysis CKD, observational dialysis, systematic review/meta-analysis, or interventional evidence. Differences in CKD stage, dialysis modality, sex, assay, exposure definition, and outcome were considered during interpretation.

Case reports, non-peer-reviewed material, and articles without relevant endocrine or cardiovascular outcomes were not used to support general claims. Because the aim was a structured narrative synthesis rather than an exhaustive systematic review, no protocol was registered, no formal risk-of-bias instrument was applied, and no new meta-analysis was performed. Candidate records and their evidentiary role were reviewed by the author group and resolved by consensus.

Physiology of Prolactin and the Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Gonadal Axis

PRL is secreted predominantly by anterior-pituitary lactotrophs. Tonic dopaminergic signaling through D2 receptors restrains secretion, while stress, sleep, estrogen, thyrotropin-releasing hormone, and several medications can increase measured concentrations [25–27]. PRL receptors are widely distributed, and canonical signaling includes JAK2/STAT pathways; biological effects vary by tissue, concentration, exposure duration, and proteolytic processing of PRL into vasoinhibin isoforms [28].

The HPG axis depends on pulsatile hypothalamic gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), pituitary luteinizing hormone (LH), and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and gonadal steroidogenesis. Sustained pathological hyperprolactinemia can suppress GnRH and contribute to secondary hypogonadism [29]. The relationship is not one-directional – estrogen stimulates lactotroph activity, systemic illness alters gonadotropin pulsatility, and nutrition, adiposity, medications, and age modify sex-steroid concentrations.

Sex steroids have cardiovascular and metabolic actions beyond reproduction. Estrogen-receptor signaling can influence endothelial nitric-oxide synthase (eNOS), vascular smooth-muscle behavior, redox balance, and inflammatory signaling, whereas androgen signaling affects vascular tone, erythropoiesis, muscle protein balance, and body composition [30, 31]. These effects are context-dependent and do not imply that lower concentrations are uniformly harmful or that replacement improves cardiovascular outcomes.

Full-length PRL and proteolytic PRL fragments can have different or even opposing vascular effects. Vasoinhibins inhibit angiogenesis and may alter eNOS signaling, whereas intact PRL can support immune-cell and metabolic signaling. Most clinical assays report immunoreactive total PRL and do not quantify these bioactive fractions. Thus, a higher circulating PRL concentration cannot be translated directly into a single vascular action, especially in CKD, where protease activity and clearance may also be altered [26].

The clinical phenotype also differs by sex and life stage. In men, reduced testicular production and altered sex-hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) may lower total or free testosterone. In premenopausal women, the key abnormality may be failure of cyclic gonadotropin and ovarian responses even when a follicular-phase estradiol value is measurable. After menopause, high gonadotropins and low estradiol represent normal ovarian aging, but CKD-related acyclicity and medication use can obscure the transition. These distinctions are essential when linking a measured hormone to vascular biology.

CKD as a Neuroendocrine Disorder

The kidney metabolizes and clears several peptide hormones and contributes to the internal milieu in which hypothalamic, pituitary, and gonadal signaling operate. With falling GFR, reduced degradation can increase circulating hormone concentrations, while retained uremic solutes, metabolic acidosis, inflammation, autonomic activation, and protein-energy wasting can perturb secretion or target-tissue response [32–34].

This framework should not convert all endocrine abnormalities into independent disease mediators. CKD severity, diabetes, obesity, frailty, inflammation, medications, and acute illness can simultaneously alter hormone values and cardiovascular prognosis. Accordingly, the same biological variable may be a consequence of kidney failure, a marker of illness burden, a partial mediator, or a combination of these roles. Dialysis replaces selected excretory functions but does not reproduce renal endocrine metabolism or fully remove protein-bound and middle-molecule solutes. Residual kidney function, dialysis modality and dose, membrane characteristics, volume status, and treatment vintage may therefore modify hormone concentrations without restoring normal feedback. Successful transplantation often improves PRL and reproductive function, supporting an upstream role for kidney failure, but persistent comorbidity, immunosuppression, and variable graft function prevent uniform recovery [32, 35].

Hyperprolactinemia and Gonadal Dysfunction in CKD and Dialysis

Mechanisms Common to the CKD Continuum

Classic kinetic and stimulation studies indicate that CKD-related PRL elevation reflects both impaired metabolic clearance and altered secretion. Reduced PRL clearance was demonstrated in chronic renal failure, while blunted suppression and stimulation responses supported impaired central regulation [36, 37]. PRL often falls after successful kidney transplantation, although the extent and timing vary [35].

HPG dysfunction is likewise multifactorial. Reduced or disordered GnRH/LH pulsatility, hyperprolactinemia, inflammation, energy deficit, medication effects, and impaired gonadal responsiveness may coexist. In men, this often produces secondary or mixed hypogonadism; in women, loss of cyclicity and anovulation are more accurate descriptors than universal estrogen deficiency [38, 39].

Common CKD medications and conditions can mimic or intensify these patterns. Opioids and glucocorticoids suppress the HPG axis; antipsychotic and antiemetic dopamine antagonists elevate PRL; obesity and insulin resistance lower SHBG; and diabetes, vascular disease, anemia, depression, and neuropathy contribute to sexual dysfunction independently of hormone status. A clinical syndrome should therefore not be attributed to PRL or testosterone without a broader differential diagnosis.

Non-Dialysis CKD

PRL may rise before kidney failure, but cross-sectional prevalence estimates vary because studies use different assays, sex-specific reference intervals, medication exclusions, and CKD-stage distributions. Mild-to-moderate elevation should therefore be interpreted in relation to GFR and competing causes rather than automatically attributed to a pituitary lesion or considered a vascular risk marker.

In men, lower total and free testosterone become more frequent with advancing CKD. Symptoms – reduced libido, erectile dysfunction, fatigue, anemia, reduced muscle mass, and depressed mood – overlap substantially with uremia and comorbidity [40, 41]. A low value obtained during acute illness or without symptom concordance is insufficient to diagnose hypogonadism.

The biochemical pattern is not uniform. Gonadotropins may be low, inappropriately normal, or elevated, reflecting central suppression, testicular resistance, or mixed disease. Accumulation of immunoreactive LH fragments can further separate measured concentration from bioactivity. For this reason, study-specific testosterone thresholds are useful for describing cohorts but should not be imported as CKD-specific diagnostic cutoffs [39].

Dialysis-Dependent Kidney Failure

In dialysis populations, PRL elevation is common and conventional or more frequent hemodialysis does not reliably normalize pre-dialysis concentrations. In a secondary analysis of the Frequent Hemodialysis Network trials, 1 year of more frequent hemodialysis did not materially reduce PRL [21]. Acute pre-/post-dialysis differences may reflect hemoconcentration, distribution, and assay timing and should not be interpreted as sustained endocrine correction.

Male testosterone deficiency is also highly prevalent. In 260 men with dialysis-dependent kidney failure, 44% had total testosterone below 10 nmol/L, 33% had concentrations of 10–14 nmol/L, and only 23% exceeded 14 nmol/L; lower testosterone correlated inversely with C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, and fibrinogen [14]. These associations support biological interaction with inflammation but cannot determine whether inflammation lowers testosterone, low testosterone increases inflammation, or both.

Female-Specific Phenotype

Female reproductive dysfunction in CKD is not adequately summarized as “estrogen deficiency.” Early physiological work in premenopausal women with kidney failure found preserved follicular-phase estradiol and FSH in many participants but loss of cyclicity and impaired positive estradiol feedback, consistent with hypothalamic anovulation [42]. PRL excess may contribute, yet bromocriptine restored ovulation in only one of three treated women in that small study, underscoring multiple mechanisms.

Clinically, menstrual irregularity, amenorrhea, abnormal uterine bleeding, anovulation, infertility, sexual dysfunction, and earlier menopause are reported across advanced CKD and kidney-replacement therapy [43, 44]. A systematic review of 46 studies (35 meta-analyzed) reported menstrual abnormalities in 19–47% of women receiving hemodialysis and 75% of those receiving peritoneal dialysis, with substantial heterogeneity; the estimated reproductive lifespan was 32 years [19]. Kidney transplantation may improve menstrual health, but amenorrhea alone cannot distinguish uremic anovulation from menopause.

This distinction has cardiovascular implications. Cycle phase, age, ovarian reserve, menopause, exogenous hormones, body composition, and diabetes all affect estradiol. Consequently, one concentration, particularly near the lower limits of immunoassays, should not be used to label a woman as estrogen-deficient or to infer cardiovascular risk. Female-specific outcome data remain sparse and, when available, do not support a simple monotonic “lower is worse” model.

Female symptoms also extend beyond amenorrhea. Abnormal uterine bleeding may interact with CKD-related anemia and antithrombotic treatment; vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, and reduced sexual function may be underreported; and fertility can return after intensification of dialysis or transplantation. A careful menstrual and reproductive history is therefore a clinical measurement in its own right, not merely background information for an estradiol assay [19, 43].

Clinical Evaluation and Hormone-Measurement Pitfalls

No CKD guideline currently recommends universal PRL, testosterone, or estradiol testing for cardiovascular risk prediction. Evaluation is most defensible when prompted by galactorrhea, menstrual disturbance, infertility, sexual dysfunction, gynecomastia, otherwise unexplained bone loss, or symptoms and signs compatible with androgen deficiency. The initial question is clinical – what syndrome is being evaluated, rather than whether an isolated hormone value is outside a laboratory interval.

For PRL, venipuncture stress, pregnancy or lactation, sleep, hypothyroidism, liver disease, chest-wall stimulation, and dopamine-antagonist medications (including antipsychotics and antiemetics), verapamil, methyldopa, estrogens, and opioids should be considered. A mildly elevated result obtained under stress should be repeated under standardized, rested conditions. Macroprolactin testing is appropriate in asymptomatic hyperprolactinemia, and a large pituitary lesion with only modest measured PRL should prompt assay dilution to exclude the hook effect [29].

Persistent PRL elevation that is marked, disproportionate to kidney dysfunction, associated with visual symptoms or headache, or accompanied by otherwise unexplained hypogonadism warrants endocrinology assessment and, when appropriate, pituitary magnetic resonance imaging. CKD is a recognized cause of moderate hyperprolactinemia, but it should not prevent investigation of an alternative cause when the phenotype is discordant.

In men, the Endocrine Society recommends diagnosing hypogonadism only when compatible symptoms or signs coexist with unequivocally and consistently low testosterone. Total testosterone should be measured using an accurate assay on two separate fasting mornings; testing during acute or subacute illness should be avoided. If total testosterone is borderline or SHBG is altered, free testosterone should be measured by equilibrium dialysis or calculated from total testosterone, SHBG, and albumin rather than a direct analog immunoassay [45].

LH and FSH help distinguish primary from secondary or mixed hypogonadism, although altered clearance and chronic illness can complicate interpretation. PRL, iron indices, and other pituitary hormones are added when clinically indicated. Before treatment, fertility goals, hematocrit, prostate risk, sleep apnea, heart failure, recent cardiovascular events, and thrombosis risk require assessment [45].

In women, testing should be anchored to menstrual history, pregnancy possibility and intention, age, cycle day when known, menopause status, and exogenous hormonal use. Pregnancy testing, PRL, thyroid-stimulating hormone, LH, FSH, estradiol, and targeted androgen testing may be appropriate depending on the presentation. Amenorrhea during dialysis is not synonymous with menopause, and low-range estradiol immunoassays have limited precision. Repeated or phase-standardized measurements and gynecologic/endocrine input are preferable when results will change management [43, 44].

For people receiving hemodialysis, serial samples should be collected at a consistent clock time and at the same relation to the dialysis session, commonly before a midweek treatment, and the fasting state, recent illness, and medication timing should be recorded. Pre-/post-dialysis comparisons are vulnerable to volume shifts. Peritoneal dialysis status, residual kidney function, nutritional change, and dialysate glucose exposure may also influence interpretation. Absolute results should be interpreted using the laboratory’s sex- and method-specific reference interval. PRL immunoassays differ in recognition of macroforms, testosterone methods differ in accuracy at low concentrations, and estradiol immunoassays have limited analytical sensitivity in postmenopausal ranges. When a result is discordant with the clinical picture, confirmation by a more specific method or discussion with the laboratory is preferable to escalating imaging or treatment. Trends are most informative when the same assay platform and sampling conditions are used.

Questionnaires for sexual function, fatigue, and physical performance may help characterize symptoms, but they are not specific tests of endocrine causality. Conversely, a patient-centered benefit – improved sexual function, menstrual health, fertility, bone protection, or anemia – may be clinically meaningful even if cardiovascular benefit is unproven. The indication and intended outcome should be defined before testing and revisited before treatment.

Prolactin, Sex Hormones, and Cardiovascular Risk outside CKD

Evidence outside CKD helps define plausible pathways but cannot be directly transferred to the uremic setting. Population studies of PRL are inconsistent. One cohort reported positive associations between PRL and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, whereas Framingham analyses found no association with incident cardiovascular risk factors and another prospective study found no prediction of coronary artery disease [46–48]. Differences in PRL range, sex, menopausal status, single baseline measurements, and exclusion of marked hyperprolactinemia limit comparison.

Small studies of prolactinoma or non-CKD hyperprolactinemia report adverse metabolic or vascular markers and improvement after dopamine-agonist treatment [49, 50]. These before-after observations cannot isolate PRL lowering from recovery of gonadal function, weight change, tumor control, or dopaminergic effects, and they were not designed to test cardiovascular events.

Mechanistic observations are also heterogeneous. PRL receptor expression in macrophages within human carotid plaques establishes local biological capacity, not that circulating PRL causes plaque instability [6]. In a transgenic mouse model, PRL/vasoinhibin signaling reduced eNOS activation and nitric-oxide production and increased blood pressure, but the model involved nonphysiological hepatic overexpression and cannot be equated with moderate CKD-related hyperprolactinemia [7]. PRL enhanced platelet aggregation in one in vitro study, again without proving an in vivo thrombotic effect [8].

For sex steroids, experimental vascular actions and observational associations outside CKD are similarly context dependent. Age, adiposity, diabetes, inflammation, menopause, and underlying illness influence both hormone levels and cardiovascular outcome. These data support candidate mechanisms but not a general cardiovascular indication for testosterone or estrogen treatment.

Testosterone concentration in non-CKD cohorts is strongly related to age, adiposity, diabetes, and chronic illness, while randomized treatment data address selected men with confirmed hypogonadism rather than people with an isolated low value. Likewise, physiological estrogen signaling does not imply that menopausal hormone therapy prevents CVD; vascular response varies with age, time since menopause, formulation, route, and thrombotic risk. These examples caution against converting hormone physiology into a therapeutic claim in CKD.

The Neuroendocrine-Cardiovascular Axis in CKD

Mechanistic Interpretation

The CKD-specific axis is best represented as a network with bidirectional and confounded links (Fig. 2). Inflammation, oxidative stress, metabolic acidosis, energy deficit, and retained solutes can suppress hypothalamic signaling and gonadal steroidogenesis while independently impairing endothelial function and muscle anabolism. Low testosterone or loss of ovarian cyclicity may then modify body composition, erythropoiesis, insulin sensitivity, physical activity, and vascular signaling. Because the upstream drivers are shared, association alone does not establish endocrine mediation.

Evidence-informed neuroendocrine-cardiovascular framework in CKD.

The clinical data illustrate this bidirectionality. In dialysis-dependent men, testosterone was inversely related to CRP, interleukin-6, and fibrinogen [14]. This is compatible with cytokine-mediated HPG suppression, with anti-inflammatory effects of androgen signaling, or with low testosterone as part of protein-energy wasting. Experimental eNOS and redox effects provide plausibility, whereas erythrocytosis, fluid retention, and prothrombotic effects of exogenous testosterone demonstrate that biological plausibility is not uniformly protective.

For women, estrogen-receptor signaling can enhance endothelial nitric-oxide bioavailability and influence oxidative and inflammatory pathways [30]. Yet, CKD-related acyclicity, diabetes, menopause, altered body composition, exogenous hormone exposure, and assay limitations complicate serum estradiol as a proxy for tissue signaling. The cardiovascular phenotype should therefore not be reduced to a binary low-estrogen state.

Oxidative stress provides another shared pathway. Uremia reduces nitric-oxide bioavailability and increases reactive oxygen species independently of gonadal status. Experimental estrogen and androgen signaling can modulate antioxidant enzymes, NADPH oxidase, and eNOS coupling, but dose, receptor distribution, and cell type determine direction. Human CKD studies generally measure circulating hormones alongside downstream vascular or inflammatory markers; they do not demonstrate that correcting the hormone reverses the redox defect.

Body composition is a plausible indirect bridge. Low testosterone, acyclicity, inflammation, acidosis, inactivity, and inadequate protein-energy intake can converge on muscle wasting and frailty. Lower muscle mass may reduce physical activity and insulin sensitivity, while frailty predicts hospitalization and mortality. Yet, the same illness burden lowers hormone concentrations, making sarcopenia both a potential mediator and a source of reverse causation. This is why functional outcomes and nutritional covariates are necessary in prospective endocrine studies.

Evidence in Non-Dialysis CKD

The principal PRL outcome study included 457 non-dialysis CKD patients and reported associations between higher PRL, impaired flow-mediated dilation, and incident cardiovascular events. After multivariable adjustment, the hazard ratio for cardiovascular events was 1.27 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.17–1.38) per 10 ng/mL higher PRL [9]. The result is hypothesis-generating – PRL was measured observationally, residual confounding and reverse causation remain possible, and no intervention tested mediation.

A more recent population-based cohort of 2,005 adults followed for a median of 19 years did not find an independent association between PRL and cardiovascular events or a CKD-by-PRL interaction, despite a strong association between CKD and events [10]. Participants generally had milder CKD, marked hyperprolactinemia was excluded, and PRL was measured once. The different populations prevent a direct refutation, but the null result argues against treating PRL as an established independent CKD risk factor.

In 239 men with non-dialysis CKD, lower total and free testosterone correlated with impaired flow-mediated dilation and predicted cardiovascular events during approximately 31 months of follow-up; adjusted hazard ratios per unit increase were 0.83 (95% CI, 0.78–0.88) for total and 0.65 (95% CI, 0.53–0.80) for free testosterone [11]. A larger stages 3–4 CKD cohort found a weaker, borderline association between the lowest testosterone quintile and mortality after adjustment [12]. Both remain vulnerable to illness-related suppression and single-measurement misclassification.

Evidence in Dialysis

In the dialysis cohort of the PRL study (173 hemodialysis patients), higher PRL was associated with pulse-wave velocity and with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality: adjusted hazard ratios per 10 ng/mL were 1.12 (95% CI, 1.06–1.17) and 1.15 (95% CI, 1.08–1.21), respectively [9]. The absence of cardiovascular-outcome trials of PRL lowering prevents inference that this association is modifiable.

Testosterone findings are more numerous but still observational. In 100 nondiabetic men receiving hemodialysis, androgen deficiency was associated with greater carotid intima-media thickness and lower flow- and nitrate-mediated dilation [13]. In 260 men with kidney failure, testosterone deficiency was associated with prevalent CVD and mortality [14]. In a 420-man hemodialysis cohort, however, the association between low testosterone and mortality was substantially attenuated after adjustment for age, comorbidity, inflammation, and nutrition [15].

Systematic reviews pool heterogeneous thresholds, dialysis and non-dialysis settings, and mostly male cohorts. One meta-analysis reported that lower total testosterone was associated with cardiovascular mortality (pooled hazard ratio 4.37; 95% CI, 1.40–13.65) and all-cause mortality (1.96; 95% CI, 1.35–2.83), but estimates were imprecise and female data were insufficient [5]. Another synthesis reached a similar direction of association while emphasizing heterogeneity and the absence of causal trials [20].

Female Cardiovascular Evidence

Female-specific studies challenge a simple deficiency model. In women initiating hemodialysis, higher – not lower – estradiol was associated with all-cause and non-cardiovascular mortality, particularly among women with diabetes, while no adjusted association with cardiovascular mortality was identified [16]. In a separate prospective hemodialysis cohort, the highest estradiol tertile was associated with higher all-cause mortality in sensitivity analysis, without a consistent cardiovascular association [17].

Among 61 reproductive-aged females with CKD, total testosterone was not independently associated with pulse-wave velocity, augmentation index, flow-mediated dilation, or velocity-time integral; the testosterone-to-estradiol ratio showed a positive association with flow-mediated dilation [18]. The small sample and cross-sectional design preclude outcome inference, but the study demonstrates why female hormonal balance, life stage, and cyclicity require direct study rather than extrapolation from men.

Taken together, the evidence supports an integrated, hypothesis-generating axis. PRL and gonadal abnormalities may identify a neuroendocrine phenotype linked to uremia, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and frailty. It does not yet establish that either hormone is an independent causal determinant, that routine measurement improves risk prediction beyond KDIGO-based assessment, or that hormonal normalization reduces cardiovascular events.

Therapeutic Perspectives and Future Directions

Management should begin with evidence-based CKD and cardiovascular care rather than endocrine manipulation. Optimization of volume, blood pressure, diabetes, lipids, anemia, nutrition, physical activity, and kidney-protective therapy has a demonstrated clinical rationale; PRL or sex-hormone treatment has not been shown to add cardiovascular benefit in CKD [1].

Routine treatment of asymptomatic CKD-related hyperprolactinemia is not evidence-based and should not be undertaken solely to normalize a laboratory value or reduce cardiovascular risk. The first steps are confirmation, medication and secondary-cause review, assessment for macroprolactin when appropriate, and evaluation for a pituitary disorder when the clinical pattern is disproportionate. Dopamine agonists remain indicated for established prolactinoma and selected symptomatic hyperprolactinemia according to endocrine guidance, with individualized monitoring [29].

CKD-specific dopamine-agonist evidence is minimal. In a small hemodialysis study, bromocriptine suppressed PRL but did not normalize the broader hypothalamic-pituitary response, and no cardiovascular outcomes were studied [22]. Orthostatic symptoms, interactions with antihypertensive therapy, and in high cumulative-dose contexts, fibrotic adverse effects require attention. More frequent hemodialysis did not lower PRL in the Frequent Hemodialysis Network analysis [21].

Testosterone therapy should likewise follow diagnostic rigor rather than a single low value. In symptomatic men with confirmed hypogonadism, treatment may be considered after evaluation of cause, fertility goals, prostate risk, hematocrit, sleep apnea, fluid status, heart failure, and thrombotic risk. Monitoring should follow the Endocrine Society guideline [45]. CKD-specific trials have not established cardiovascular benefit, and testosterone should not be prescribed for cardiovascular prevention.

The TRAVERSE trial in 5,246 men with symptomatic, confirmed hypogonadism and preexisting or high cardiovascular risk found testosterone gel noninferior to placebo for major adverse cardiovascular events (hazard ratio 0.96; 95% CI, 0.78–1.17) but reported more atrial fibrillation, acute kidney injury, and pulmonary embolism in the testosterone group [24]. This was not a CKD-specific efficacy trial and cannot establish benefit or safety in advanced CKD or dialysis.

Anabolic-steroid studies in dialysis should not be equated with physiological testosterone replacement. In a 79-patient randomized trial, nandrolone increased lean body mass over 12 weeks, while resistance exercise improved training-specific strength; survival and cardiovascular outcomes were not tested [23]. These findings support research on physical function, not routine endocrine treatment.

Kidney transplantation is the intervention most consistently associated with improvement in CKD-related PRL and reproductive abnormalities, but it is not an endocrine treatment and is subject to selection, graft function, and immunosuppressive effects [35, 36]. Dialysis intensification may benefit volume, solute control, and reproductive health in selected patients, but the randomized frequent-hemodialysis analysis did not demonstrate PRL normalization [21]. These observations argue against using PRL change as a surrogate for dialysis adequacy.

For women, neither estradiol measurement nor menopausal hormone therapy has a validated role in CKD cardiovascular prevention. Treatment for menopausal, gynecologic, bone, or reproductive indications should be individualized with nephrology and gynecology/endocrinology input, considering age, route, uterine status, thrombosis, blood pressure, and dialysis-access risk. Female-specific trials are a priority.

Future studies should prespecify CKD stage and dialysis modality; use repeated, standardized hormone measurements and validated assays; enroll adequate numbers of women; account for cycle and menopause; and separate prediction from mediation. Trials should target symptomatic, biochemically confirmed phenotypes and measure patient-reported function, vascular intermediate outcomes, safety, and when feasible, cardiovascular events. Mendelian-randomization or causal-mediation analyses may help prioritize interventions but cannot replace trials.

Limitations of Current Evidence

Most evidence is observational, often single-center, and based on one hormone measurement. Definitions of hyperprolactinemia and hypogonadism, assay methods, sampling time, CKD stage, dialysis modality, covariate adjustment, and outcome ascertainment vary. Confounding by age, diabetes, obesity, inflammation, malnutrition, medication exposure, and illness severity is substantial; reverse causation is plausible. Combining non-dialysis and dialysis populations can obscure important biological and clinical differences.

Women are underrepresented, and menstrual phase, menopause, exogenous hormones, and assay performance are frequently incompletely characterized. Mechanistic studies often use supraphysiological exposure or non-CKD models. Finally, this was a structured narrative review without protocol registration or formal risk-of-bias scoring; the evidence table is an interpretive map, not a graded clinical-practice recommendation.

Conclusion

CKD-related hyperprolactinemia and HPG-axis dysfunction are common and clinically relevant, but their cardiovascular meaning requires restraint. Mechanistic studies identify plausible endothelial, inflammatory, redox, thrombotic, and body-composition pathways; clinical studies show associations that vary by CKD setting, sex, and study design.

The most defensible interpretation is a hypothesis-generating neuroendocrine-cardiovascular network in which endocrine abnormalities may be markers, modifiers, or partial mediators of a shared uremic phenotype. They are not established independent causal risk factors, and current evidence does not support hormone-based cardiovascular screening or treatment.

Clinical care should focus on symptom-directed, methodologically sound endocrine evaluation and on validated CKD and cardiovascular therapies. Routine treatment of asymptomatic CKD-related hyperprolactinemia is not evidence based. Prospective, sex-inclusive, CKD stage-specific studies and appropriately powered interventional trials are required before endocrine correction can be considered a cardiovascular strategy.
