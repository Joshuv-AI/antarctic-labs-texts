Bidirectional associations between parental-child alienation and social anxiety: a longitudinal study on the mediating role of interpersonal sensitivity
Authors: Jingqun Wang, Kai Jing, Baojuan Ye, Guorong Wang, Anliang Wang, Jinxing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven the high prevalence of social anxiety among college students and its multifaceted negative impact on mental health, understanding its underlying mechanisms is critically important.MethodsA three-wave longitudinal study was conducted with 4,846 Chinese college students (Mage = 18.98 years, 51.3% female) over a one-year period. The Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Model (RI-CLPM) was used to examine bidirectional relationships and mediating effects.ResultsAt the within-person level, significant bidirectional positive associations were observed between parent-child alienation and interpersonal sensitivity (β ranging from 0.094 to 0.114, p < 0.001), as well as between interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety (β ranging from 0.093 to 0.143, p < 0.01). Parent-child alienation significantly and positively predicted subsequent social anxiety (β ranging from 0.087 to 0.095, p < 0.01), while the predictive effect of social anxiety on subsequent parent-child alienation was marginally significant (β ranging from 0.049 to 0.051, p = 0.048–0.050). Interpersonal sensitivity acted as a bidirectional longitudinal mediator between parent-child alienation and social anxiety. Multi-group analysis revealed significant gender differences, with females showing higher autoregressive stability in interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety, as well as stronger cross-lagged effects from interpersonal sensitivity to both parent-child alienation and social anxiety.ConclusionThere is a complex bidirectional dynamic among parent-child alienation, interpersonal sensitivity, and social anxiety, with interpersonal sensitivity serving as a key bilateral mediator. These findings clarify the reciprocal influences between the family environment and individual cognitive-affective traits, offering valuable insights and practical implications for the prevention and treatment of social anxiety among college students.Keywords: Parent-Child Alienation, Social Anxiety, Interpersonal Sensitivity, Longitudinal Study, Gender Differences

Introduction

Social anxiety is characterized by negative emotions and behaviors in social interactions, including excessive concern about others’ opinions, fear, and avoidance [1]. It is common among college students, with rates ranging from 10% to 33.4% [2–4]. High social anxiety not only impairs academic and social functioning but is also closely associated with other mental health problems, such as depression and substance use [5–7]. In severe cases, it may even lead to suicidal thoughts and behaviors [8–9]. Given its widespread impact, understanding the factors that contribute to social anxiety during the college years is essential.

The present study focuses on emerging adulthood, a developmental stage spanning approximately ages 18 to 25 [10]. This period is characterized by identity exploration, instability, self-focus, and a sense of being “in‑between” adolescence and full adulthood [10–11]. The family environment, especially the quality of parent-child relationships, plays a crucial role in shaping psychological adjustment during emerging adulthood [12]. Parents remain important attachment figures, and relationship quality consistently influences college students’ psychological well-being [13]. Although perceived parent-child alienation is recognized as particularly harmful during this stage [14], the mechanisms linking it to social anxiety remain underexplored. To address this gap, the present study investigates the longitudinal mediating role of interpersonal sensitivity in this relationship, with the aim of informing prevention and intervention efforts.

The relationship between parent-child alienation and social anxiety

In the present study, parent-child alienation refers to perceived alienation in the parent-child attachment relationship (e.g., emotional distance, poor communication, and feelings of being misunderstood or rejected), as indexed by the alienation dimension of the IPPA [15]. This conceptualization focuses on the child’s subjective sense of disconnection and is distinct from parental alienation in post-separation/high-conflict contexts, which involves behaviors that may undermine the child’s relationship with the other parent [16–17]. According to attachment theory, early parent-child relationships shape individuals’ internal working models of themselves and others, influencing lifelong social and emotional development [18]. Such alienation represents a significant disruption of the parent-child attachment bond [19–20]. When individuals experience chronic parent-child alienation, they may develop negative internal working models, believing they are unworthy of love and that others are untrustworthy [21]. These negative models can serve as distal risk factors for social anxiety [22]. A meta-analysis [23] found that negative parent-child interactions are significantly associated with social anxiety in children, especially in East Asian cultures. Parenting behaviors characterized by low warmth and rejection have been found to positively predict social anxiety symptoms in children [24]. Individuals exposed to parent-child alienation in childhood may exhibit more anxiety symptoms in adulthood [25].

Emotion regulation theory offers a deeper understanding of this relationship [26]. Emotion regulation capacity primarily develops through caregiver interactions; securely attached children learn effective strategies, whereas insecurely attached children struggle to develop healthy regulation skills. A longitudinal study [27] found that parent-child alienation behaviors damage parent-child attachment, which in turn affects adolescents’ emotion regulation and may lead to mental health problems such as anxiety. Negative parenting styles also indirectly contribute to social anxiety by shaping emotion regulation [28].

From a developmental-contextual perspective [29], individual-environment interactions are reciprocal. Social anxiety can result not only from family dysfunction but also affect the quality of parent-child relationships, leading to increased perceived parent-child alienation. This can be explained by “evocative effects,” in which individuals’ psychological traits induce changes in their environment [30]. Longitudinal evidence suggests bidirectional links between adolescent social anxiety symptoms and parenting behaviors; higher social anxiety symptoms prospectively predict changes in parenting, reflecting evocative child effects [31], and a significant association has been observed between social anxiety levels and parent-child attachment quality [32]. Furthermore, research indicates that childhood social avoidance behaviors can lead to maternal overcontrol, creating a vicious cycle [33]. In summary, there may be a bidirectional relationship between parent-child alienation and social anxiety.

The relationship between parent-child alienation and interpersonal sensitivity

Interpersonal sensitivity involves heightened awareness of and concern for others’ behaviors, evaluations, and emotions, along with a tendency to overreact [34]. A closely related construct is rejection sensitivity, defined as anxious expectations and exaggerated reactions to perceived rejection [35]. From an attachment perspective, early parent-child interactions shape internal working models of self and others [18], which may increase later vulnerability to interpersonal sensitivity and rejection-related concerns. Rejection sensitivity theory further posits that early experiences of rejection foster anxious expectations and heightened reactivity to potential rejection cues [35]. Over time, these patterns may generalize to broader social contexts, contributing to heightened interpersonal sensitivity that reflects negative internal working models in social interactions. Research consistently links adverse parenting experiences (e.g., rejection, low warmth, and overcontrol) to greater interpersonal sensitivity across ages and cultures [36–38].

Interpersonal sensitivity may also affect the quality of parent-child relationships. According to Beck’s cognitive theory, schemas formed in early experiences shape how people interpret current information, increasing the likelihood of negative appraisals of ambiguous situations that align with these schemas [39]. Rejection sensitivity has been linked to biased interpretations of ambiguous social information [40]. Accordingly, individuals high in interpersonal and/or rejection sensitivity may interpret relatively neutral parental behaviors (e.g., delayed responses) as indifference or distancing, which may heighten perceived alienation. A 28-day diary study of adolescents further suggested that biased and inflexible interpretations can undermine close relationships by shaping how parents and adolescents perceive each other’s responsiveness and insensitivity in daily interactions [41].

In summary, interpersonal sensitivity may be shaped by multiple factors, including adverse family experiences (e.g., perceived parent-child alienation, parental rejection, or low care) as well as broader dispositional and biological vulnerabilities related to individual sensitivity (e.g., temperament and genetic influences) [42]. Once established, it may serve as a mental filter that biases interpretations of parental behavior, potentially reinforcing perceived alienation and, in turn, undermining the quality of parent-child relationships.

The relationship between interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety

There is a close relationship between interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety, and this association may change over time. According to the cognitive model of social anxiety [43], individuals with negative self-schemas tend to generate threatening expectations about others’ evaluations in social situations, engage in excessive self-focused attention, and underestimate their coping resources. Interpersonal sensitivity—marked by heightened concern about others’ words, behaviors, and evaluations and a tendency to feel easily hurt—may reflect these schema-consistent processing tendencies. Empirical findings support this link. Harb et al. [44] showed that interpersonal sensitivity is elevated and clinically relevant in social anxiety disorder and that the Interpersonal Sensitivity Measure demonstrates good psychometric properties in this population. Preliminary evidence further suggests that fear of negative and positive evaluation may help explain how facets of interpersonal sensitivity relate to social anxiety symptoms [45]. In addition, a four-wave longitudinal study indicated that interpersonal sensitivity can function as a mediator linking interpersonal stressors to subsequent social anxiety, supporting temporal pathways consistent with cognitive-interpersonal accounts [46].

Social anxiety may not only stem from interpersonal sensitivity but may also reinforce it over time. Self-verification theory suggests that people are motivated to seek and accept information consistent with their existing self-views [47]. Given that socially anxious individuals often hold negative self-beliefs, self-verification processes may increase their tendency to attend to and seek rejection-consistent feedback. Consistent with this account, preliminary evidence from a thesis study suggests that highly socially anxious individuals may engage in increased negative feedback seeking even after receiving accepting feedback [48]. More broadly, social anxiety may consume cognitive and affective resources and promote rigid, threat-focused processing, including heightened monitoring of social-evaluative cues and reliance on avoidance or safety strategies [49]. Recent integrative work further highlights that rigid negative interpretation tendencies can co-occur with maladaptive interpersonal strategies (e.g., negative feedback seeking) in social anxiety [50]. In short, social anxiety may increase interpersonal sensitivity over time by fostering rejection-consistent information seeking and more vigilant, less flexible processing of interpersonal cues, potentially contributing to a self-perpetuating cycle.

The mediating role of interpersonal sensitivity

Understanding how external environmental risks influence psychological adaptation requires identifying the underlying mechanisms of transmission. The evidence-based model of the etiology of social anxiety disorder highlights that social anxiety arises from complex interactions between internal vulnerabilities (e.g., biased social-evaluative processing) and external risks (e.g., maladaptive parenting and peer adversity) [51]. This framework provides an integrative rationale for examining interpersonal sensitivity as a mediator linking perceived parent-child alienation and social anxiety.

According to attachment theory [18] and interpersonal rejection sensitivity theory [35], chronic experiences of emotional distance or rejection in the parent-child relationship may contribute to heightened interpersonal sensitivity, including greater concern about evaluation, heightened monitoring for rejection cues, and a tendency toward negative interpretations. Once established, heightened interpersonal sensitivity aligns with cognitive models of social anxiety [43], which emphasize catastrophic expectations about evaluation and excessive self-focused attention as proximal processes that generate and maintain social anxiety.

From a forward-pathway perspective, perceived parent-child alienation is an external relational risk, whereas interpersonal sensitivity is an internal cognitive-affective vulnerability. Consistent with this indirect pathway, prior studies have shown that rejection sensitivity mediates associations between adverse parenting experiences and social anxiety symptoms, including parental rejection [52] and parental psychological control [53]. Together, these findings suggest that interpersonal sensitivity (and closely related rejection sensitivity) may transmit the effect of adverse family experiences on social anxiety.

From a developmental-contextual perspective [29], symptoms may also shape individuals’ relational environments over time. Self-verification theory [47] suggests that individuals are motivated to seek and accept information consistent with their self-views; accordingly, socially anxious individuals with negative self-perceptions may preferentially seek rejection-consistent feedback, with preliminary evidence indicating increased negative feedback seeking even after receiving accepting feedback [48]. In addition, integrative accounts highlight that social anxiety is associated with rigid, threat-focused processing and maladaptive interpersonal strategies, which may reinforce negative interpretations of interpersonal cues [49–50]. Disturbed social information processing in social anxiety [54–55] may further increase the likelihood of construing ambiguous interpersonal signals as rejecting, potentially maintaining heightened interpersonal sensitivity [40]. Through these repeated cycles, heightened interpersonal sensitivity may, in turn, increase perceived parent-child alienation by strengthening negative interpretations of ambiguous parental behaviors [41] and by promoting avoidance or defensive distancing that reduces constructive interaction [56]. Thus, interpersonal sensitivity may play a dual mediating role, transmitting the effect of perceived parent-child alienation on social anxiety while also mediating the reverse influence of social anxiety on perceived parent-child alienation.

Current study

Although existing research has provided preliminary evidence linking parent-child relational disconnection to social anxiety, several gaps remain. First, parent-child alienation—conceptualized as perceived emotional distance and disrupted connection—has received relatively limited attention as a relational experience in its own right [19–20]. Second, much of the evidence comes from child and adolescent samples; for example, adolescent studies have linked parent-related adverse experiences to social anxiety via maladaptive interpersonal cognitions [57]. Fewer studies have focused on emerging adults, a developmentally salient period for college students. During the university transition, changes in proximity to parents may coincide with shifts in attachment-related experiences and adjustment [58]. Moreover, prior work has often emphasized direct associations, with less attention to longitudinal mechanisms, even though interpersonal sensitivity has been implicated as an important cognitive-affective process in the development and maintenance of social anxiety [46].

More critically, common research designs have limited in-depth understanding of the dynamic relations among these constructs. Cross-sectional studies and models assuming only unidirectional influence from the family environment to individual characteristics make it difficult to detect potential bidirectional, within-person processes over time. In addition, whether these longitudinal processes differ by gender remains unclear. Prior studies suggest possible gender-related differences in rejection sensitivity, with some evidence for higher levels in females [59] and gender-related variation in associated outcomes [60], whereas meta-analytic evidence indicates that associations between early adversity and later rejection sensitivity may not depend on the gender composition of samples [61].

Importantly, few studies have tested gender differences in longitudinal structural (i.e., path) relations among these constructs. Therefore, a longitudinal approach that separates stable between-person differences from within-person change is needed. To address these gaps, the present study uses a three-wave longitudinal design and applies the Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Model (RI‑CLPM) [62] to examine within-person bidirectional associations among parent-child alienation, interpersonal sensitivity, and social anxiety, as well as the longitudinal indirect (mediating) effects of interpersonal sensitivity. Potential gender differences in these within-person pathways are also tested. Based on these aims, we propose the following hypotheses:

H1

There is a bidirectional positive predictive relationship between parent-child alienation and social anxiety.

H2

There is a bidirectional positive predictive relationship between parent-child alienation and interpersonal sensitivity.

H3

There is a bidirectional positive predictive relationship between interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety.

H4

Interpersonal sensitivity will exhibit longitudinal mediating effects in both directions, linking parent-child alienation to later social anxiety and linking social anxiety to later perceived parent-child alienation.

H5

Gender moderates the longitudinal associations among parent‑child alienation, interpersonal sensitivity, and social anxiety.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study is part of a nationwide project conducted in Mainland China. Using random cluster sampling, six universities from Guangdong, Jiangsu, Jiangxi, Sichuan, Shaanxi, and Xinjiang were selected as survey sites. Three waves of longitudinal data were collected over one year, in September 2024, March 2025, and September 2025. The initial number of participants in each wave was 5,486, 5,377, and 5,235, respectively. After removing invalid responses due to student absence or patterned answering (e.g., selecting the same option for all items), a final sample of 4,846 participants who completed valid questionnaires across all three waves was included in the longitudinal analysis, resulting in an attrition rate of 11.67%. The valid sample comprised 2,362 males (48.7%) and 2,484 females (51.3%), with a mean age of 18.98 years (SD = 0.98). All participants were Chinese citizens, mainly of Han ethnicity. To assess whether sample attrition introduced systematic bias, attrition analyses were performed. Results showed no significant differences between participants who dropped out (N = 640) and those included in the final analysis with respect to gender (χ² (1) = 0.93, p = 0.34) or age (t = -0.05, p = 0.47). Additionally, the two groups did not differ significantly on baseline (T1) scores of parent-child alienation (t = -1.43, p = 0.15), interpersonal sensitivity (t = -1.12, p = 0.26), or social anxiety (t = -1.31, p = 0.19). These findings suggest there was no systematic attrition bias in the data.

Procedure

Before data collection, this study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the School of Psychology at Jiangxi Normal University (Approval Number: IRB-JXNU-PSY-2024045). Data were gathered online through the Questionnaire Star platform. The assessments were administered by mental health teachers who were uniformly trained during class sessions. The entire process was conducted anonymously, and participants could withdraw at any time. Prior to starting, all participants were required to read an informed consent form and click “Agree” to continue with the survey; those who clicked “Disagree” were automatically directed to exit. All data were stored on encrypted servers and maintained as strictly confidential. Since the electronic questionnaire was set to require responses to all items, there were no missing data at the item level.

Measurements

Parent-child alienation

Parent-child alienation was assessed using the Alienation subscale of the Inventory of Parent and Peer Attachment (IPPA) [15]. The original scale includes six items for paternal alienation and six for maternal alienation. Because the father and mother items are symmetric in content and parents are often perceived as a unit in Chinese culture, as part of a national survey in Mainland China, this study combined the father and mother versions into a single six‑item subscale to limit questionnaire length while maintaining measurement quality. Sample items include “My parents don’t understand what I’m going through these days.” This subscale was rated on a 5‑point Likert scale from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree).

The Chinese version of the Revised Inventory of Parent and Peer Attachment (IPPA‑R) has demonstrated good reliability and validity among Chinese college students [63]. In the current study, the internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α) for the combined six‑item subscale was 0.867. The confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) indicated excellent model fit: χ² (5) = 16.999, CFI = 0.999, TLI = 0.998, RMSEA = 0.017 (90% CI = [0.009, 0.027]), and SRMR = 0.005. All factor loadings were significant, ranging from 0.64 to 0.81. These results suggest that the combined parent-child alienation measure has a unidimensional structure and acceptable construct validity in our sample.

Interpersonal sensitivity

Interpersonal sensitivity was assessed using the Sensitivity subscale of the Chinese College Student Mental Health Screening Scale [64]. This scale is a mental health screening tool specifically designed for Chinese college students. Previous research has supported the utility of this scale among Chinese college students, confirming its satisfactory psychometric properties [65]. The sensitivity dimension measures a person’s excessive concern about others’ words, actions, and judgments, as well as their tendency to be easily hurt. Its conceptual meaning closely aligns with the construct of interpersonal sensitivity proposed by Boyce and Parker [34]. The subscale contains four items, with a typical example being “Others’ words and actions easily make me feel hurt.” All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater interpersonal sensitivity. In this study, the subscale demonstrated good internal consistency reliability, with Cronbach’s α = 0.817. The confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) indicated acceptable fit: χ² (1) = 22.792, CFI = 0.998, TLI = 0.988, RMSEA = 0.053 (90% CI [0.035, 0.072]), and SRMR = 0.008. Given the large‑scale nature of this study, this four‑item subscale was adopted for its brevity and ease of completion, thereby reducing participant burden and enhancing response rates.

Social anxiety

Social anxiety was assessed using the Social Phobia subscale of the Chinese College Student Mental Health Screening Scale [64]. The scale measures an individual’s anxiety experiences and avoidance behaviors in social situations, mainly characterized by fear of interacting with others and frequent feelings of nervousness, anxiety, unease, and fear when required to engage with others. This subscale includes four items, such as “Excessive nervousness when talking with others.” All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater social anxiety. In this study, the internal consistency reliability of this subscale was Cronbach’s α = 0.822, demonstrating good reliability. The confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) of this subscale showed excellent model fit: χ² (1) = 2.748, p = 0.097, CFI = 1.000, TLI = 0.999, RMSEA = 0.015 (90% CI [0.000, 0.037]), and SRMR = 0.002. Similarly, given the large‑scale nature of this study, this four‑item subscale was adopted for its brevity and ease of completion, thereby reducing participant burden and enhancing response rates.

Data analysis

SPSS 27.0 was used for data management, descriptive statistics, scale reliability and validity analyses, and correlation analysis among variables. Before formal analysis, preliminary analyses were performed using Mplus 8.3, including confirmatory factor analyses (CFA), longitudinal measurement invariance tests for the core variables, common method bias tests, and calculation of intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) to evaluate the stability of individual traits.

To accurately distinguish between-person differences from within-person dynamic processes, this study used the Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Model (RI-CLPM) as the main analytical framework, with gender controlled by regressing it onto the random intercepts. The random intercepts for parent-child alienation, interpersonal sensitivity, and social anxiety were specified with means fixed to zero and variances freely estimated. The three waves were spaced approximately 6 months apart, and the time intervals were treated as equal; thus, autoregressive and cross-lagged paths were assumed to be time-invariant. To identify the best model specification, a series of nested models were systematically created and compared: a baseline model (M1) with all paths freely estimated, followed by models with autoregressive paths constrained to be equal across time points (M2), cross-lagged paths constrained to be equal across time points (M3), and both autoregressive and cross-lagged paths constrained to be equal across time points (M4). Model comparisons adhered to the criteria of ΔCFI ≤ 0.01 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015 [66]. All RI-CLPM analyses employed maximum likelihood estimation for model fitting and parameter estimation.

Based on the optimal model, the Bootstrap method (with 5,000 resamples) was used to test the longitudinal mediating effect of interpersonal sensitivity in the relationship between parent-child alienation and social anxiety, and bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Finally, to examine gender differences in the structural pathways, a multi‑group analysis was conducted within the framework of the optimal model (M3), in which cross‑lagged paths were constrained to be equal across time within each gender group while autoregressive paths were freely estimated. Gender was used as the grouping variable, and all parameters were freely estimated between boys and girls. The robust maximum likelihood estimator (MLR) was employed to obtain standard errors robust to non‑normality. A joint Wald test was first performed to evaluate whether the complete set of 12 autoregressive and cross‑lagged coefficients differed overall between genders. Subsequently, individual path‑specific Z‑tests were conducted using the formula for the difference between two independent coefficients to identify which specific paths showed significant gender differences. A two‑tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Common method bias test

The unmeasured latent method factor (ULMC) approach was used to assess common method bias at all time points [67]. Results indicated that after incorporating a common method factor, the changes in model fit indices were as follows: ΔCFI ranged from 0.007 to 0.017, ΔRMSEA was less than 0.008, and ΔSRMR was less than 0.009. According to commonly used criteria for changes in fit indices (e.g., Chen [66]), ΔCFI ≤ 0.01 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015 generally indicate no substantial deterioration in model fit. Although some ΔCFI values slightly exceeded 0.01, the small changes in ΔRMSEA and ΔSRMR imply that common method bias likely had a limited impact on the study’s conclusions and does not seriously threaten the validity of the findings.

Preliminary analyses

Correlation analyses showed that parent-child alienation was significantly positively related to college students’ interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety, suggesting that more severe parent-child alienation is linked to higher levels of both traits. Moreover, interpersonal sensitivity was significantly positively related to social anxiety, indicating that increased interpersonal sensitivity is associated with higher social anxiety. Tests of measurement invariance across the three time points showed that the longitudinal data for each scale had a good fit (Table 2). All scales satisfied the criteria for scalar invariance, establishing the basis for subsequent cross-lagged analyses [68]. The cross-lagged paths showed clear patterns among the three variables. First, an asymmetric predictive relationship was observed between parent-child alienation and social anxiety. Parent-child alienation significantly predicted later social anxiety from T1 to T2 (β = 0.087, p = 0.001) and from T2 to T3 (β = 0.095, p = 0.001). Social anxiety also significantly predicted later parent-child alienation from T1 to T2 (β = 0.051, p = 0.048), and from T2 to T3 (β = 0.049, p = 0.050), but these reverse effects were very weak and only marginally significant, providing limited support for Hypothesis 1.

Second, a reciprocal relationship was found between parent-child alienation and interpersonal sensitivity. Parent-child alienation predicted later interpersonal sensitivity (T1 to T2: β = 0.114; T2 to T3: β = 0.110, p < 0.001), and interpersonal sensitivity predicted later parent-child alienation (T1 to T2: β = 0.106; T2 to T3: β = 0.094, p < 0.001), supporting Hypothesis 2.

Third, interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety exhibited reciprocal effects. Interpersonal sensitivity predicted subsequent social anxiety (T1 to T2: β = 0.093, p = 0.001; T2 to T3: β = 0.097, p = 0.002), and social anxiety predicted later interpersonal sensitivity (T1 to T2: β = 0.143; T2 to T3: β = 0.139, p < 0.001), supporting Hypothesis 3.

Mediation analysis showed that interpersonal sensitivity significantly mediated both pathways. The indirect effect of T1 parent-child alienation on T3 social anxiety, mediated by interpersonal sensitivity, was 0.011 (95% CI = [0.004, 0.021]). Likewise, the indirect effect of T1 social anxiety on T3 parent-child alienation, mediated by interpersonal sensitivity, was 0.013 (95% CI = [0.006, 0.024]). Given the weakness of the reverse path, caution is warranted when interpreting the mediation from social anxiety to parent-child alienation. These findings indicate that interpersonal sensitivity serves as an asymmetric longitudinal mechanism linking these variables over time. Hypothesis 4 was supported.

The multi‑group analysis within the optimal M3 framework revealed significant gender differences in the structural pathways. The joint Wald test indicated that the full set of 12 autoregressive and cross‑lagged coefficients differed significantly between males and females (Wald χ² (12) = 125.22, p < 0.001). Individual Z‑tests (Table 4) identified seven paths with significant gender differences. Specifically, females exhibited markedly higher autoregressive stability in interpersonal sensitivity (T1IS → T2IS: b = 0.572 vs. 0.079, Z = − 8.96, p < 0.001; T2IS → T3IS: b = 0.505 vs. −0.038, Z = − 7.38, p < 0.001) and social anxiety (T1SA→ T2SA: b = 0.413 vs. 0.289, Z = − 2.33, p = 0.020; T2SA → T3SA: b = 0.235 vs. 0.069, Z = − 2.38, p = 0.017) compared to males. Regarding cross‑lagged effects, the path from interpersonal sensitivity to parent‑child alienation (T1IS → T2PA and T2IS → T3PA: b = 0.181 vs. 0.050, Z = − 2.31, p = 0.021) and the path from interpersonal sensitivity to social anxiety (T1IS → T2SA and T2IS → T3SA: b = 0.247 vs. −0.050, Z = − 6.66, p < 0.001) were both significantly stronger in females. The remaining paths, including the cross‑lagged effects from parent‑child alienation to interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety, did not differ significantly between genders (p > 0.05). Overall, these results indicate partially gender-specific within-person dynamics, with interpersonal sensitivity playing a more prominent role among females. Thus, hypothesis 5 was supported.

Discussion

Results indicated significant positive within-person cross-lagged associations in both directions between parent-child alienation and interpersonal sensitivity, as well as between interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety. In addition, parent-child alienation significantly predicted subsequent social anxiety, whereas the reverse pathway (social anxiety → parent-child alienation) was weak and only marginally significant. Interpersonal sensitivity served as an asymmetric longitudinal mediator between parent-child alienation and social anxiety, with stronger indirect effects in the forward direction. Notably, significant gender differences emerged across several structural paths: females showed stronger within-person autoregressive carry-over effects in interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety, and stronger time-invariant cross-lagged effects from interpersonal sensitivity to both parent–child alienation and social anxiety.

Asymmetric predictive pathways between parent-child alienation and social anxiety

The present study found that higher within-person levels of perceived parent–child alienation prospectively predicted higher within-person social anxiety. This pattern aligns with attachment theory [18] and prior evidence linking negative parent-related family interactions to social anxiety [23]. Perceived parent-child alienation—characterized by emotional distance, communication breakdown, and feelings of rejection—may undermine attachment security and contribute to more negative internal working models (e.g., “I am unworthy of love”) [21]. These schema-consistent beliefs may then be reactivated in socially demanding contexts during emerging adulthood, fostering threat appraisals that maintain anxiety [43].

From an emotion-regulation perspective [26], adverse parent-child experiences may hinder the development of adaptive emotion regulation through caregiver interactions [27–28]. Difficulties in emotion regulation may increase reliance on maladaptive strategies, such as expressive suppression, and reduce the use of cognitive reappraisal, both of which have been linked to higher social anxiety [69]. Over time, these processes may contribute to the persistence of anxiety and to maladaptive interpersonal behaviors and strategies that further maintain social-evaluative concerns [50].

The study also provided weak evidence that social anxiety predicted subsequent perceived parent-child alienation (standardized effect approximately 𝛽 ≈ 0.05, with 𝑝 values near 0.05). This reverse pathway is theoretically consistent with transactional accounts and evocative processes [31], in which avoidance and withdrawal associated with social anxiety may shape ongoing parent-child interactions, potentially increasing perceived distance. In addition, cognitive biases in social anxiety may increase the likelihood of construing relatively neutral parental behaviors (e.g., delayed responses) as cold or rejecting [32, 43], thereby intensifying perceived alienation. Given the small magnitude and borderline statistical evidence, this reverse association should be interpreted cautiously. Notably, the alienation measure captures subjective perceptions rather than objective parental behavior; thus, the observed reverse pathway may primarily reflect changes in perceived alienation rather than definitive changes in parents’ actual behaviors.

Bidirectional relationship between parent-child alienation and interpersonal sensitivity

This study found that higher within-person perceived parent–child alienation significantly and positively predicted subsequent interpersonal sensitivity. This pattern aligns with attachment theory [18] and rejection sensitivity theory [35]. These perspectives suggest that repeated experiences of emotional distance or rejection may foster anxious expectations and heightened monitoring for rejection-related cues, increasing the likelihood of negatively interpreting ambiguous interpersonal information. These attentional and interpretive tendencies overlap with key features of interpersonal sensitivity. Extending prior cross-sectional findings [36–38], the present longitudinal results indicate that the predictive effect of parent-child alienation on interpersonal sensitivity was consistent across the two intervals under the model’s time-invariance constraints.

The study also found that interpersonal sensitivity significantly and positively predicted subsequent perceived parent–child alienation. According to Beck’s cognitive theory [39], individuals high in interpersonal sensitivity may rely on schema-consistent interpretations in ambiguous situations. Related evidence indicates that rejection sensitivity is linked to biased negative interpretations of ambiguous social information [40]. Accordingly, highly sensitive individuals may be more likely to interpret relatively neutral parental behaviors (e.g., delayed responses) as distancing or indifference, thereby increasing perceived alienation. A diary study by Gadassi-Polack et al. [41] further suggested that interpretation bias can shape adolescents’ daily perceptions of parental responsiveness, such that perceived alienation may increase even without clear changes in parents’ objective behavior. Behaviorally, heightened rejection-related sensitivity may manifest as hypervigilance to rejection cues and intense emotional reactions [70], as well as defensive distancing [56]. These responses may, in turn, alter ongoing parent-child interactions (e.g., reduced openness or increased tension), in a manner conceptually consistent with evocative processes [30]. Over time, such interaction patterns may further erode perceived relationship quality and reinforce alienation [71].

Taken together, these findings suggest a reciprocal within-person association between interpersonal sensitivity and perceived parent-child alienation. Notably, the multi-group analysis revealed that the cross-lagged effect from interpersonal sensitivity to parent–child alienation was significantly stronger in females than in males, whereas the reverse effect from parent-child alienation to interpersonal sensitivity did not differ by gender.

Bidirectional relationship between interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety

This study found significant reciprocal within-person cross-lagged associations between interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety, providing longitudinal support for the cognitive model of social anxiety [43]. According to this model, individuals with negative self-schemas tend to engage in excessive self-focused attention and threat monitoring in social situations. Interpersonal sensitivity—marked by heightened concern about others’ evaluations and a tendency to feel easily hurt—is conceptually aligned with these schema-consistent processing tendencies and is clinically relevant to social anxiety disorder [44]. In line with the interpersonal sensitivity–to–social anxiety pathway, individuals high in interpersonal sensitivity may interpret ambiguous social cues (e.g., silence or gaze avoidance) as signs of negative evaluation or rejection, which can trigger anxiety [72]. Social anxiety is also maintained by heightened vigilance for social threat [73] and by avoidance or safety behaviors [74]. Preliminary evidence further suggests that fear of negative and positive evaluation may help explain how facets of interpersonal sensitivity relate to social anxiety symptoms [45].

The reverse pathway from social anxiety to interpersonal sensitivity is also theoretically plausible. Self-verification theory proposes that individuals prefer information consistent with their self-views [47]; accordingly, preliminary evidence indicates that highly socially anxious individuals may seek more negative feedback even after receiving accepting feedback [48]. In addition, integrative accounts suggest that social anxiety is associated with rigid, threat-focused processing and reduced flexibility in social information processing [49], which may reinforce negative interpretations of interpersonal cues and maladaptive interpersonal strategies [50]. Disturbances in social information processing observed in social anxiety [54–55] may further heighten sensitivity to interpersonal cues over time. Interpersonally, behaviors associated with social anxiety may elicit fewer positive responses from interaction partners [75], which may in turn reinforce negative expectations and contribute to the maintenance of interpersonal sensitivity. Overall, interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety may form a mutually reinforcing cognitive-affective loop, although the observed cross-lagged effects were moderate. Notably, the multi-group analysis showed that the pathway from interpersonal sensitivity to social anxiety was significantly stronger in females than in males, whereas the pathway from social anxiety to interpersonal sensitivity did not differ by gender.

The bidirectional mediating role of interpersonal sensitivity

The findings indicate that interpersonal sensitivity played an asymmetric longitudinal mediating role between perceived parent-child alienation and social anxiety, which is broadly consistent with the social anxiety risk factor model [51]. Rather than supporting a fully symmetric bidirectional cycle, the results suggest a predominantly forward indirect pathway, with comparatively weaker evidence for reverse processes.

In the forward pathway, perceived parent-child alienation is an external relational risk that may strengthen interpersonal sensitivity, an internal cognitive-affective vulnerability, thereby increasing social anxiety. Longitudinal results were consistent with the temporal order, showing that earlier perceived parent-child alienation predicted later interpersonal sensitivity, which in turn predicted subsequent social anxiety. This pattern aligns with prior evidence that rejection sensitivity mediates associations between adverse parenting experiences (e.g., parental rejection and psychological control) and social anxiety [52–53].

In the reverse pathway, interpersonal sensitivity also showed evidence of mediating the association from social anxiety to perceived parent-child alienation. However, because the reverse cross-lagged effect from social anxiety to perceived parent–child alienation was small (standardized coefficient, 𝛽 ≈ 0.05), statistically close to conventional thresholds, the reverse indirect effect should be interpreted cautiously and requires replication. This reverse pathway may operate through multiple processes. First, social anxiety may help maintain or amplify interpersonal sensitivity through self-verification processes [47], with preliminary evidence suggesting increased negative feedback seeking even after receiving acceptance [48], and through rigid, threat-focused processing that reduces flexibility in social information processing [49]. Disturbances in social information processing associated with social anxiety [54–55] may further heighten sensitivity to interpersonal cues over time.

Second, heightened interpersonal sensitivity may increase perceived parent-child alienation through negative interpretive tendencies (e.g., construing neutral parental behaviors as distancing) [41] and through defensive distancing, which reduces constructive engagement [56]. In this sense, interpersonal sensitivity may function both as a vulnerability that is maintained by social anxiety and as a factor that contributes to greater perceived parent-child alienation.

Overall, the three variables appear to form a dynamic system in which the forward pathway from perceived parent-child alienation to interpersonal sensitivity and then to social anxiety plays a dominant role, whereas reverse processes contribute less strongly. These findings extend the social anxiety risk factor model [51] by incorporating evidence consistent with a reciprocal, but asymmetric, set of longitudinal pathways. The multi-group results further suggest that this dynamic system operates with meaningful gender specificity.

Under the optimal M3 framework, the multi-group analysis indicated that gender moderated several within-person structural pathways. The joint Wald test showed significant overall differences across the 12 autoregressive and cross-lagged coefficients, and follow-up parameter difference tests identified seven coefficients that differed significantly by gender. Females showed stronger within-person autoregressive carry-over effects in interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety, indicating greater persistence of within-person deviations in these vulnerabilities over time. In addition, the time-invariant cross-lagged effects from interpersonal sensitivity to subsequent perceived parent-child alienation and to subsequent social anxiety were stronger in females, whereas the prospective effects from perceived parent-child alienation to interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety did not differ by gender.

These results suggest partially gender-specific dynamics, with interpersonal sensitivity playing a more prominent role among females. This pattern aligns with gender socialization accounts that emphasize greater interpersonal orientation and emotional attunement among females [76–77], which may amplify the downstream impact of interpersonal concerns on perceived family relationships and internal distress. At the same time, under the time-invariance constraint imposed on the cross-lagged paths, the absence of gender differences in the prospective effects of perceived parent-child alienation suggests that this relational risk may confer vulnerability in a broadly similar manner across gender groups, on average across the two lag intervals. Finally, although many prior studies have focused on mean-level gender differences, the present findings underscore that gender may also moderate the structure of longitudinal associations among key constructs [78–79]. Notably, because cross-lagged effects were constrained to be equal across waves, the observed moderation should be interpreted as gender differences in time-invariant (i.e., averaged across lags) dynamic pathways rather than wave-specific effects, highlighting the value of testing moderation at the level of dynamic pathways.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, the three-wave longitudinal design using the Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Model (RI-CLPM) separates stable between-person differences from within-person change, enabling a clearer test of asymmetric within-person dynamics. Compared with traditional cross-lagged panel models, the RI-CLPM more directly targets within-person processes and reduces confounding by stable between-person differences. Second, this study clarifies the predominantly forward-oriented dynamics among perceived parent-child alienation, interpersonal sensitivity, and social anxiety, offering potential implications for prevention and intervention. For students experiencing parent-child alienation, interventions that enhance parent-child communication and emotional connection may be beneficial. For those with social anxiety symptoms, cognitive-behavioral approaches that target interpersonal sensitivity may help reduce negative interpretations of social cues, potentially disrupting maladaptive cycles.

However, several limitations should be noted. First, all variables were assessed using self-report questionnaires, which may be vulnerable to common method bias and social desirability. Although the diagnostics suggested that these concerns were not severe, future work could incorporate multiple data sources (e.g., parent and peer reports, behavioral observations) to improve measurement validity. Second, the prospective effect of social anxiety on subsequent perceived parent-child alienation was small and statistically near conventional thresholds, suggesting that this reverse pathway may be negligible in practice, may require longer time frames to emerge, or may depend on additional moderators (e.g., family structure). Studies with more waves and larger samples are needed to test the robustness of this weak reverse effect. Third, this study examined interpersonal sensitivity as a single mediator; other mechanisms (e.g., emotion regulation difficulties or self-efficacy) may also link perceived parent-child alienation to social anxiety. Future research could test more comprehensive multi-mediator models.

Fourth, several measurement-related limitations warrant consideration. The parent-child alienation subscale originally assessed father- and mother-specific experiences; we combined them into a single six-item measure to reduce questionnaire length. Although the composite scale showed good internal consistency, this approach may have obscured parent-specific information. Future studies should consider retaining separate father- and mother-specific versions. In addition, the interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety subscales each contained only four items. Although these brief scales showed acceptable reliability and have been validated in Chinese college samples, they may not fully capture the breadth of the constructs. Future research could supplement them with more comprehensive measures, such as the Interpersonal Sensitivity Measure [34] or the Social Interaction Anxiety Scale [80].

Fifth, although the present study identified significant gender differences in several structural paths, biological sex is only a proxy for psychosocial mechanisms that may underlie these differences. Future studies could move beyond biological sex by assessing gender-related psychosocial dimensions such as masculinity and femininity orientations [76], which may help explain individual differences in interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety. For example, endorsement of traditional masculine norms may discourage the expression or reporting of interpersonal sensitivity [81], whereas endorsement of feminine norms may be associated with greater interpersonal attunement and concern about evaluation [77]. Incorporating validated measures of gender role orientation (e.g., the Bem Sex Role Inventory [82]) and/or conformity-to-norm measures could provide a more nuanced account of why certain pathways appear stronger in females.

Conclusion

This study suggests that parent‑child alienation, interpersonal sensitivity, and social anxiety are linked in a primarily forward‑directed sequence: parent‑child alienation predicts later social anxiety through increased interpersonal sensitivity. The reverse pathway (from social anxiety to parent‑child alienation) was very weak and only marginally significant, providing only limited support for a self‑reinforcing cycle. While social anxiety may, to a very limited extent, influence perceived parent‑child alienation, the forward pathway dominates the interplay. Importantly, this dynamic system is partially gender‑specific: girls showed higher autoregressive stability in interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety, as well as stronger cross‑lagged effects of interpersonal sensitivity on both parent‑child alienation and social anxiety. These findings extend the social anxiety risk factor model by incorporating a weak reverse pathway and revealing notable gender differences, suggesting that interventions should be gender‑sensitive. For girls, reducing the self‑reinforcing role of interpersonal sensitivity may be particularly beneficial, while for boys, addressing the impact of perceived parent‑child alienation on subsequent maladjustment may be prioritized. Overall, these results highlight the need for interventions that target both parent‑child relationship quality and maladaptive thinking patterns related to interpersonal sensitivity, with priority given to reducing parent‑child alienation and interpersonal sensitivity as key drivers of social anxiety.
