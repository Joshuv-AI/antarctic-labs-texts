Tribonacci-Lucas numbers that are palindromic concatenations of two distinct repdigits
Mahadi Ddamulira

Abstract
The Tribonacci-Lucas sequence { S n } n ≥ 0 \{S_{n}\}_{n\geq 0} is defined by the linear recurrence relation S n + 3 = S n + 2 + S n + 1 + S n S_{n+3}=S_{n+2}+S_{n+1}+S_{n} , for n ≥ 0 n\geq 0 , with the initial conditions S 0 = S 2 = 3 S_{0}=S_{2}=3 and S 1 = 1 S_{1}=1 . A palindromic number is a number that remains the same when its digits are reversed. This paper uses Baker’s theory for nozero lower bounds for linear forms in logarithms of algebraic numbers, and reduction methods involving the theory of continued fraction to determine all Tribonacci-Lucas numbers that are palindromic concatenations of two distinct repdigits.



Keywords and phrases
: Tribonacci-Lucas numbers; palindromes; repdigits; linear forms in logarithms; Baker’s method.

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
: 11B39, 11D61, 11J86.

1
Introduction.

Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the sequence of Tribonacci-Lucas numbers, defined by the linear recurrence relation
S_{n+3}=S_{n+2}+S_{n+1}+S_{n}
, for
n\geq 0
, with the initial conditions
S_{0}=S_{2}=3
and
S_{1}=1
. This sequence A001644 on The On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences (OEIS)
[
10
]
. The first few terms of this sequence are given by

(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}=3,1,3,7,11,21,39,71,131,241,443,815,1499,2757,5071,9327,17155,31553,\ldots.

The sequence
\{S_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is a well–known companion sequence of the classical
Tribonacci sequence
,
\{T_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
. That is, the two sequences satisfy the same linear recurrence relation, but with different initial conditions.
A palindromic number (or
palindrome
) is a number that remains the same when its digits are reversed (such as
17571
). In other words, a palindrome has reflectional symmetry across a vertical axis. A natural followup of the results in
[
5
,
6
]
would be a characterization of
palindromic
Tribonacci-Lucas numbers. That is, we study the Diophantine equation

S_{n}=\overline{\underbrace{d_{1}\cdots d_{1}}_{\text{$\ell$ times}}\underbrace{d_{2}\cdots d_{2}}_{\text{$m$ times}}\underbrace{d_{1}\cdots d_{1}}_{\text{$\ell$ times}}},

(1)

in non-negative integers
(n,d_{1},d_{2},\ell,m)
, where
d_{1},d_{2}\in\{0,\ldots,9\},\quad d_{1}>0
, and
\ell,m\geq 1
.

The main result of this paper is the following.

Theorem 1
.

The only Tribonacci-Lucas number that is a palindromic concatenation of two distinct repdigits is,
S_{8}=131
.

This result gives a continuation of the results presented in
[
4
,
7
]
. The method of proof of our main results makes use of Baker’s theory for nozero lower bounds for linear forms in logarithms of algebraic numbers, and reduction methods involving the theory of continued fractions. All computations are done with the aid of a simple computer program in
SageMath
.

2
Preliminary results.

In this section we recall some facts about the Tribonacci-Lucas numbers and other preliminary lemmas, including Baker’s theory for linear forms in three logs, Baker-Davenport reduction procedure, and the LLL algorithm. These results are crucial to the proof of the main result.

2.1
Some properties of the Tribonacci-Lucas sequence.

Some of the important properties of the Tribonacci-Lucas sequence are stated here. It is well–known that the characteristic equation,

\displaystyle\Psi(x):=x^{3}-x^{2}-x-1=0,

has roots
\alpha,~\beta,~\gamma=\bar{\beta}
, where

\displaystyle\alpha=\dfrac{1+(w_{1}+w_{2})}{3},\qquad\beta=\dfrac{2-(w_{1}+w_{2})+\sqrt{-3}(w_{1}-w_{2})}{6},

(2)

with

\displaystyle w_{1}=\sqrt[3]{19+3\sqrt{33}}\quad\text{and}\quad w_{2}=\sqrt[3]{19-3\sqrt{33}}.

(3)

The Binet-like formula for the general terms of the Tribonacci-Lucas sequence is given by,

\displaystyle S_{n}=\alpha^{n}+\beta^{n}+\gamma^{n}\qquad\text{ for all}\quad n\geq 0.

(4)

Numerically, the following estimates hold:

\displaystyle 1.83

\displaystyle<\alpha<1.84;

(5)

\displaystyle 0.73<|\beta|

\displaystyle=|\gamma|=\alpha^{-\frac{1}{2}}<0.74.

From (
2
), (
3
), and (
5
), it is easy to see that the contribution the complex conjugate roots
\beta
and
\gamma
, to the right-hand side of (
4
), is very small. In particular, we set

\xi(n):=S_{n}-\alpha^{n}=\beta^{n}+\gamma^{n}.\quad\text{Then,}\quad|\xi(n)|\leq\frac{2}{\alpha^{n/2}}\quad\text{for all}\quad n\geq 1.

(6)

The last inequality in (
6
) follows from the fact that
|\beta|=|\gamma|=\alpha^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. That is, for any
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle|\xi(n)|=\left|\beta^{n}+\gamma^{n}\right|\leq|\beta|^{n}+|\gamma|^{n}=\alpha^{-\frac{n}{2}}+\alpha^{-\frac{n}{2}}=2\alpha^{-\frac{n}{2}}=\dfrac{2}{\alpha^{n/2}}.

The following estimate also holds:

Lemma 1
.

Let
m\geq 1
be a positive integer. Then

\alpha^{m-1}\leq S_{m}<\alpha^{m+1}.

Proof.

Lemma
1
follows from a simple inductive argument, with the fact that
\alpha^{3}=\alpha^{2}+\alpha+1
.

Let
\mathbb{K}:=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha,\beta)
be the splitting field of the polynomial
\Psi
over
\mathbb{Q}
. Then,
[\mathbb{K},\mathbb{Q}]=6
. Furthermore,
[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]=3
. The Galois group of
\mathbb{K}
over
\mathbb{Q}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}:=\text{Gal}(\mathbb{K/Q})\cong\{(1),(\alpha\beta),(\alpha\gamma),(\beta\gamma),(\alpha\beta\gamma),(\alpha\gamma\beta)\}\cong S_{3}.

Thus, we identify the automorphisms of
\mathcal{G}
with the permutations of the zeros of the polynomial
\Psi
. For example, the permutation
(\alpha\beta)
corresponds to the automorphism
\sigma:\alpha\to\beta,~\beta\to\alpha,~\gamma\to\gamma
. This will be used later to obtain a contradiction on the size of the absolute values of certain bounds.

2.2
Linear forms in logarithms.

Our approach follows the standard procedure of obtaining nonzero lower bounds for certain linear forms in logarithms of algebraic numbers. The upper bounds are obtained via a manipulation of the associated Binet’s formula for the given sequence. For the lower bounds, we need the celebrated Baker’s theorem on linear forms in logarithms of algebraic numbers. Before stating the result, we need the definition of the (logarithmic) Weil height of an algebraic number.

Let
\eta
be an algebraic number of degree
d
with minimal polynomial

\displaystyle P(x)=a_{0}\prod_{j=1}^{d}(x-\eta_{j}),

where the leading coefficient
a_{0}
is positive and the
\eta_{j}
’s are the conjugates of
\eta
. The logarithmic height of
\eta
is given by

\displaystyle h(\eta):=\frac{1}{d}\left(\log a_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\log\left(\max\{|\eta_{j}|,1\}\right)\right).

Note that, if
\eta=\frac{p}{q}\in\mathbb{Q}
is a reduced rational number with
q>0
, then the above definition reduces to

h(\eta)=\log\max\{|p|,q\}.

We list some well known properties of the height function below, which we shall subsequently use without reference:

\displaystyle h(\eta_{1}\pm\eta_{2})

\displaystyle\leq h(\eta_{1})+h(\eta_{2})+\log 2,

(7)

\displaystyle h(\eta_{1}\eta_{2}^{\pm})

\displaystyle\leq h(\eta_{1})+h(\eta_{2}),

\displaystyle h(\eta^{s})

\displaystyle=|s|h(\eta),\quad(s\in\mathbb{Z}).

Here we present the version of Baker’s theorem proved by Bugeaud, Mignotte and Siksek (
[
1
]
, Theorem 9.4), which is itself due to Matveev.

Theorem 2

(Bugeaud, Mignotte, Siksek,
[
1
]
)
.

Let
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{t}
be positive real algebraic numbers in a real algebraic number field
\mathbb{K}\subset\mathbb{R}
of degree
D
. Let
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}
be nonzero integers such that

\displaystyle\Gamma:=\eta_{1}^{b_{1}}\ldots\eta_{t}^{b_{t}}-1\neq 0.

Then

\displaystyle\log|\Gamma|>-1.4\times 30^{t+3}\times t^{4.5}\times D^{2}(1+\log D)(1+\log B)A_{1}\ldots A_{t},

where

\displaystyle B\geq\max\{|b_{1}|,\ldots,|b_{t}|\},

and

\displaystyle A_{j}\geq\max\{Dh(\eta_{j}),|\log\eta_{j}|,0.16\},\quad\text{for all}\quad j=1,\ldots,t.

2.3
Reduction procedure.

The bounds on the variables obtained via Baker’s theorem are usually too large for any computational purposes. In order to get further refinements, we use the Baker-Davenport reduction procedure. The variant we apply here is the one due to Dujella and Pethő (
[
8
]
, Lemma 5a). For a real number
r
, we denote by
\parallel r\parallel
the quantity
\min\{|r-n|:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, the distance from
r
to the nearest integer.

Lemma 2

(Dujella, Pethő,
[
8
]
)
.

Let
\kappa\neq 0,A,B
and
\mu
be real numbers such that
A>0
and
B>1
. Let
M>1
be a positive integer and suppose that
\frac{p}{q}
is a convergent of the continued fraction expansion of
\kappa
with
q>6M
. Let

\displaystyle\varepsilon:=\parallel\mu q\parallel-M\parallel\kappa q\parallel.

If
\varepsilon>0
, then there is no solution of the inequality

\displaystyle 0<|m\kappa-n+\mu|<AB^{-k}

in positive integers
m,n,k
with

\displaystyle\frac{\log(Aq/\varepsilon)}{\log B}\leq k\quad\text{and}\quad m\leq M.

Lemma
2
cannot be applied when
\mu=0
(since then
\varepsilon<0
). In this case, we use the following criterion due to Legendre, a well–known result from the theory of Diophantine approximation. For further details, we refer the reader to the books of Cohen
[
2
,
3
]
.

Lemma 3

(Legendre,
[
2
,
3
]
)
.

Let
\kappa
be real number and
x,y
integers such that

\displaystyle\left|\kappa-\frac{x}{y}\right|<\frac{1}{2y^{2}}.

Then
x/y=p_{k}/q_{k}
is a convergent of
\kappa
. Furthermore, let
M
and
N
be a nonnegative integers such that
q_{N}>M
. Then putting
a(M):=\max\{a_{i}:i=0,1,2,\ldots,N\}
, the inequality

\displaystyle\left|\kappa-\frac{x}{y}\right|\geq\frac{1}{(a(M)+2)y^{2}},

holds for all pairs
(x,y)
of positive integers with
0<y<M
.

In some cases, it is necessary to apply the LLL-algorithm. This algorithm is used to find an effective lower bound for
|\lambda_{1}x_{1}+\lambda_{2}x_{2}|
, where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
are real numberd, and
x_{2},x_{2}
are rational integers such that
|x_{j}|\leq X_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
for
j=1,2
. We state the following lemma, which is the direct consequence of Cohen (
[
2
]
, Proposition 2.3.20).

Lemma 4
.

Keeping the above assumptions, let
C
be a fixed (large) positive constant. Let
\Lambda
be the lattice generated by the columns of the matrix

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\lfloor C\lambda_{1}\rceil&\lfloor C\lambda_{2}\rceil\end{pmatrix}.

Consider a reduced basis
\{\mathbf{v_{1}},\mathbf{v_{2}}\}
of
\Lambda
together with
\{\mathbf{v_{1}^{*}},\mathbf{v_{2}^{*}}\}
as its Gram-Schmidt associated basis. Put

\displaystyle d_{\Lambda}=\dfrac{\|\mathbf{v_{1}}\|}{\max\{1,\|\mathbf{v_{1}}\|/\|\mathbf{v_{2}^{*}}\|\}}\quad\text{and}\quad T=\dfrac{1+X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2}}{2}.

If
d_{\Lambda}^{2}\geq T^{2}+X_{1}^{2}
, the we have that

\displaystyle|\lambda_{1}x_{1}+\lambda_{2}x_{2}|\geq\dfrac{\sqrt{d_{\Lambda}^{2}-X_{1}^{2}}-T}{C}.

We will also need the following lemma by Gúzman Sánchez and Luca (
[
9
]
, Lemma 7):

Lemma 5

(Gúzman Sánchez, Luca,
[
9
]
)
.

Let
r\geq 1
and
H>0
be such that
H>(4r^{2})^{r}
and
H>L/(\log L)^{r}
. Then

\displaystyle L<2^{r}H(\log H)^{r}.

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{9}\left(d_{1}\times 10^{\ell+m}+(d_{1}-d_{2})\times 10^{m}-(d_{1}-d_{2})\right),

\displaystyle\eta_{2}

\displaystyle:=\alpha,\ \eta_{3}:=10,\ b_{1}:=1,\ b_{2}:=-n,\ b_{3}:=\ell,\ t:=3.

As in the previous cases, we can take
B:=n
and
D:=3
. Using the properties of
h
given in (
7
), we note that

\displaystyle h(\eta_{1})

\displaystyle\leq h(9)+h(d_{1})+(\ell+m)h(10)+h(d_{1}-d_{2})+mh(10)+h(d_{1}-d_{2})+3\log 2

\displaystyle\leq 16\log 3+(2m+\ell)\log 10.

Using equations (
13
) and (
15
), we have that

\displaystyle(2m+\ell)\log 10

\displaystyle<3.12\times 10^{28}(1+\log n)^{2}+7.18\times 10^{13}(1+\log n)<3.13\times 10^{28}(1+\log n)^{2}.

(17)

Thus, we conclude that

h(\eta_{1})<3.14\times 10^{28}(1+\log n)^{2}.

We now find an upper bound for
|\log\eta_{1}|
. We have that

\displaystyle|\log\eta_{1}|

\displaystyle=\left|\log\left(\frac{1}{9}\left(d_{1}\times 10^{\ell+m}+(d_{1}-d_{2})\times 10^{m}-(d_{1}-d_{2})\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\log 9+\left|\log\left(d_{1}\times 10^{\ell+m}+(d_{1}-d_{2})\times 10^{m}-(d_{1}-d_{2})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 3\log 3+\log(d_{1}\times 10^{\ell+m})+\left|\log\left(1-\frac{(d_{1}-d_{2})(10^{m}-1)}{d_{1}\times 10^{\ell+m}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 5\log 3+(\ell+m)\log 10+\left|\log\left(1-\frac{(d_{1}-d_{2})(10^{m}-1)}{d_{1}\times 10^{\ell+m}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 5\log 3+(\ell+m)\log 10+\frac{|(d_{1}-d_{2})(10^{m}-1)|}{d_{1}\times 10^{\ell+m}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{|(d_{1}-d_{2})(10^{m}-1)|}{d_{1}\times 10^{\ell+m}}\right)^{2}+\cdots

\displaystyle\leq 5\log 3+\ell\log 10+m\log 10+\frac{1}{10^{l}}+\frac{1}{2\times 10^{2\ell}}+\cdots

\displaystyle\leq 5\log 3+7.18\times 10^{13}(1+\log n)+1.56\times 10^{28}(1+\log n)^{2}+\frac{1}{10^{\ell}-1}

\displaystyle<1.58\times 10^{28}(1+\log n)^{2},

where, in the last inequality above, we used the bound from (
17
). We note that
D\cdot h(\eta_{1})>|\log\eta_{1}|
. Thus, we let
A_{1}=9.42\times 10^{28}(1+\log n)^{2}
,
A_{2}=\log\alpha
and
3\log 10
. Theorem
2
then implies that

\displaystyle\log|\Gamma_{3}|

\displaystyle>-5.44\times 10^{12}(1+\log n)A_{1}>-5.13\times 10^{42}(1+\log n)^{3}.

Comparing the last inequality with (
16
), we deduce that

n\log\alpha<5.13\times 10^{42}(1+\log n)^{3}+\log 3.

Therefore, we get that

n<8.52\times 10^{42}(\log n)^{3}.

With the notation of Lemma
5
, let
r:=3
,
L:=n
and
H:=8.52\times 10^{42}
and notice that this data meets the conditions of the lemma. Lemma
5
, tells us that

n<2^{3}\times 8.52\times 10^{42}(\log(8.52\times 10^{42}))^{3}.

After a simplification, we obtain the bound

n<6.6\times 10^{50}.

Lemma
6
then implies that

2\ell+m<1.8\times 10^{50}.

The following lemma summarizes what we have proved up to this far:

Lemma 7
.

All solutions to the Diophantine equation (
1
) satisfy the following inequalities

2\ell+m<1.8\times 10^{50}\quad\text{and}\quad n<6.6\times 10^{50}.

3.3
Reduction of the bounds.

The bounds obtained in Lemma
7
are too large to carry out meaningful computations with the computer. Thus, we need to reduce them. To do so, we apply Lemma
2
several times as follows.

First, we return to the inequality (
11
) and put

\displaystyle z_{1}:=(2\ell+m)\log 10-n\log\alpha+\log\left(\frac{d_{1}}{9}\right).

The inequality (
11
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle|\Gamma_{1}|=\left|e^{-z_{1}}-1\right|<\dfrac{28}{10^{\ell}}.

We assume that
\ell\geq 2
, then the right–hand side of the above inequality is at most
28/100<1/2
. The inequality
|e^{z}-1|<x
for real values of
x
and
z
implies that
z<2x
. Thus,

\displaystyle|z_{1}|<\dfrac{56}{10^{\ell}}.

This implies that

\displaystyle\left|(2\ell+m)\log 10-n\log\alpha-\log\left(\frac{9}{d_{1}}\right)\right|<\dfrac{56}{10^{l}}.

Dividing through the above inequality by
\log\alpha
gives

\displaystyle\left|(2\ell+m)\frac{\log 10}{\log\alpha}-n+\frac{\log(d_{1}/9)}{\log\alpha}\right|<\dfrac{56}{10^{\ell}\log\alpha}.

(18)

So, we apply Lemma
2
with the quantities:

\displaystyle\kappa:=\dfrac{\log 10}{\log\alpha},\quad\mu(d_{1}):=\frac{\log(d_{1}/9)}{\log\alpha},\quad 1\leq d_{1}\leq 9,\quad A:=\dfrac{56}{\log\alpha},\quad B:=10.

Let

\kappa=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]=[3;1,3,1,1,14,1,3,3,6,1,13,3,4,2,1,1,2,3,3,2,2,1,2,5,1,1,39,2,1,\ldots],

be the continued fraction expansion of
\kappa
. We set
M:=10^{51}
which is the upper bound on
2\ell+m
. With the help of a simple program in SageMath, we find out that the convergent

\displaystyle\dfrac{p}{p}=\dfrac{p_{98}}{q_{98}}=\dfrac{39444948689252707738489528190760067813905266021850462}{10439083718875559984715310681234336679649552673602845},

is such that
q=q_{98}>6M
. Furthermore, it gives
\varepsilon>0.00227519
, and thus,

\displaystyle\ell\leq\dfrac{\log((56/\log\alpha)q/\varepsilon)}{\log 10}<56.

For the case
d_{1}=9
, we have that
\mu(d_{1})=0
. In this case, it is not possible to reduce the bound via Lemma
2
, so we apply Lemma
3
. The inequality (
18
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\log 10}{\log\alpha}-\frac{n}{2\ell+m}\right|<\frac{56}{10^{\ell}(2\ell+m)\log\alpha}<\dfrac{1}{2(2\ell+m)^{2}},

because
2\ell+m<10^{51}:=M
. It follows from Lemma
3
that
\frac{n}{2\ell+m}
is a convergent of
\kappa:=\frac{\log 10}{\log\alpha}
. So
\frac{n}{2\ell+m}
is of the form
p_{k}/q_{k}
for some
k=0,1,2,\ldots,98
. Thus,

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{(a(M)+2)(2\ell+m)^{2}}\leq\left|\dfrac{\log 10}{\log\alpha}-\frac{n}{2\ell+m}\right|<\frac{56}{10^{\ell}(2\ell+m)\log\alpha}.

(19)

With the aid of a simple computer program in SageMath, we have

a(M)=\max\{a_{k}:k=0,1,2,\ldots,98\}=44.

Thus, using (
19
) we get that

\displaystyle\ell\leq\dfrac{\log\left(\dfrac{46\times 56\times 10^{51}}{\log\alpha}\right)}{\log 10}<55.

So,
\ell\leq 56
in both cases. In the case
\ell<2
, we have that
\ell<2<56
. Therefore,
\ell\leq 56
holds in all cases.

Next, for fixed
d_{1}\neq d_{2}\in\{0,\ldots,9\},~d_{1}>0
and
1\leq\ell\leq 56
, we return to the inequality (
14
) and put

\displaystyle z_{2}:=(\ell+m)\log 10-n\log\alpha+\log\left(\frac{d_{1}\times 10^{\ell}-(d_{1}-d_{2})}{9a}\right).

From the inequality (
14
), we have that

\displaystyle|\Gamma_{2}|=\left|e^{-z_{2}}-1\right|<\dfrac{19}{10^{m}}.

Assume that
m\geq 2
, then the right–hand side of the above inequality is at most
19/100<1/2
. Thus, we have that

\displaystyle|z_{2}|<\dfrac{38}{10^{m}},

which implies that

\displaystyle\left|(\ell+m)\log 10-n\log\alpha+\log\left(\frac{d_{1}\times 10^{\ell}-(d_{1}-d_{2})}{9}\right)\right|<\dfrac{38}{10^{m}}.

(20)

Dividing through by
\log\alpha
gives

\displaystyle\left|(\ell+m)\frac{\log 10}{\log\alpha}-n+\log\left(\frac{(d_{1}\times 10^{\ell}-(d_{1}-d_{2}))/9a}{\log\alpha}\right)\right|<\dfrac{38}{10^{m}\log\alpha}.

Thus, we apply Lemma
2
with the quantities:

\displaystyle\quad\mu(d_{1},d_{2}):=\log\left(\frac{(d_{1}\times 10^{\ell}-(d_{1}-d_{2}))/9}{\log\alpha}\right),\quad A:=\dfrac{38}{\log\alpha},\quad B:=10.

We take the same
\kappa
and its convergent
p/q=p_{98}/q_{98}
as before. Since
\ell+m<2\ell+m
, we set
M:=10^{51}
as the upper bound on
\ell+m
. With the help of a simple computer program in SageMath, for
\ell\in[1,56]
, we get that
\varepsilon>0.0000604124
, and therefore,

\displaystyle m\leq\dfrac{\log((38/\log\alpha)q/\varepsilon)}{\log 10}<58.

For the case
(d_{1},d_{2},\ell)=(1,0,1)
, we have that
\mu(d_{1},d_{2})=0
. In this case, it is also not possible to reduce the bound via Lemma
2
, so we apply Lemma
4
. The inequality (
20
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\log\left|(\ell+m)\log 10-n\log\alpha\right|<\log 38-m\log 10.

(21)

Next we reduce the upper bound for
m
as follows. Let
\lambda_{1}=\log 10
,
\lambda_{2}=\log\alpha
,
x_{1}=\ell+m
,
x_{2}=-n
, and a sufficiently large
C=10^{110}
. The LLL algorithm uses the lattice
\Lambda
generated by the columns of the matrix

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\lfloor 10^{110}\log 10\rceil&\lfloor 10^{110}\log\alpha\rceil\end{pmatrix}.

A simple computer program in SageMath is used to compute the reduced basis
\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2}\}
for
\Lambda
.
Then, the Gram-Schmidt associated basis
\{\mathbf{v}_{1}^{*},\mathbf{v}_{2}^{*}\}
is obtained using the standard procedure:

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{1}^{*}=\mathbf{v}_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{v}_{2}^{*}=\mathbf{v}_{2}-\dfrac{\langle\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{v}_{1}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{1}\rangle}\mathbf{v}_{1}.

Let
X_{1}=1.8\times 10^{50}
and
X_{2}=6.6\times 10^{50}
. Then, it is clear that
|x_{i}|\leq X_{i}
for
i=1,2
. Now, we calculate

\displaystyle d_{\Lambda}=\dfrac{\|\mathbf{v_{1}}\|}{\max\{1,\|\mathbf{v_{1}}\|/\|\mathbf{v_{2}^{*}}\|\}}=5.10\times 10^{109}\quad\text{and}\quad T=\dfrac{1+X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2}}{2}=2.34\times 10^{101}.

Since
d_{\Lambda}^{2}=2.6\times 10^{219}>5.48\times 10^{202}=T^{2}+X_{1}^{2}
,
Lemma
4
guarantees that

\displaystyle\log\left|(\ell+m)\log 10-n\log\alpha\right|>\log(0.0000512).

(22)

Comparing (
21
) with (
22
), we get that
m\leq 6
. Thus, we have that
m\leq 58
in both cases. The case
m<2
holds as well since
m<2<58
.

Lastly, for fixed
d_{1}\neq d_{2}\in\{0,\ldots,9\},~d_{1}>0
,
1\leq\ell\leq 56
and
1\leq m\leq 58
, we return to the inequality (
16
) and put

\displaystyle z_{3}:=\ell\log 10-n\log\alpha+\log\left(\dfrac{d_{1}\times 10^{\ell+m}+(d_{1}-d_{2})\times 10^{m}-(d_{1}-d_{2})}{9}\right).

From the inequality (
16
), we have that

\displaystyle|\Gamma_{3}|=\left|e^{z_{3}}-1\right|<\frac{2}{\alpha^{n}}.

Since
n>500
, the right–hand side of the above inequality is less than
1/2
. Thus, the above inequality implies that

\displaystyle|z_{3}|<\frac{4}{\alpha^{n}},

which leads to

\displaystyle\left|\ell\log 10-n\log\alpha+\log\left(\dfrac{d_{1}\times 10^{\ell+m}+(d_{1}-d_{2})\times 10^{m}-(d_{1}-d_{2})}{9}\right)\right|<\frac{4}{\alpha^{n}}.

Dividing through by
\log\alpha
gives,

\displaystyle\left|\ell\frac{\log 10}{\log\alpha}-n+\log\left(\dfrac{(d_{1}\times 10^{\ell+m}+(d_{1}-d_{2})\times 10^{m}-(d_{1}-d_{2}))/9}{\log\alpha}\right)\right|<\frac{4}{\alpha^{n}\log\alpha}.

Again, we apply Lemma
2
with the following data:

\displaystyle\quad\mu(d_{1},d_{2}):=\log\left(\dfrac{(d_{1}\times 10^{\ell+m}+(d_{1}-d_{2})\times 10^{m}-(d_{1}-d_{2}))/9}{\log\alpha}\right),\quad A:=\dfrac{4}{\log\alpha},\quad B:=\alpha.

We take the same
\kappa
and its convergent
p/q=p_{98}/q_{98}
as before. Since
\ell<2\ell+m
, we choose
M:=10^{51}
as the upper bound for
\ell
. With the help of a simple computer program in SageMath, we get that
\varepsilon>0.000000106965
, and thus,

\displaystyle n\leq\dfrac{\log((4/\log\alpha)q/\varepsilon)}{\log\alpha}<226.

Thus, we have that
n\leq 226
. This contradicts with our initial assumption that
n>500
. Hence, Theorem
1
is completely proved. ∎
