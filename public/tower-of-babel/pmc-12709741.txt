Bilateral temporal skull xanthoma characterized as a Langerhans cell histiocytosis: a case report
Authors: Han Liu, Hailong Tian, Shujun Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundXanthomas are benign lipomatous deposits that can be found systemically in various tissues, including bones, in human beings. Their presence in the skull remains a rare entity. Despite their benign characteristics, imaging modalities are often unable to distinguish them from neoplastic lesions. This leads to a diagnostic dilemma in patients with Langerhans cell histiocytosis. This case report highlights such a case, where clinical history and image findings were concordant with that of Langerhans cell histiocytosis in the temporal skull region.Case descriptionA 32-year-old Han Chinese woman was diagnosed with Langerhans cell histiocytosis. During systemic workup for her tumor, she was found to have bilateral temporal lesions. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain, as well as a computerized tomography scan, were consistent with that of Langerhans cell histiocytosis. As treatment would be drastically affected by the diagnosis, an excision biopsy was performed. The histology was dramatically inconsistent with that of a bone xanthoma.ConclusionXanthomas are benign lesions that can be seen deposited in appendicular and axial skeleton. Lesions are rare, with most case descriptions focusing on their presence in the mandible and temporal bone. They usually have a benign course but may present with symptoms due to localized mass effect. Surgical intervention and histologic diagnosis may still be required in these lesions owing to the lack of imaging characteristics that confirm their diagnosis through noninvasive methods.

Background

Xanthomas are benign lesions of skin and bone, which are usually associated with underlying hyperlipidemia or hypercholesterolemia in human beings [1]. They are formed due to abnormal deposition of cholesterol and lipids into tissues leading to inflammatory cell infiltration. Intraosseous xanthomas are uncommon entities characterized by bone expansion, as well as possible cortical destruction due to such cholesterol deposits, usually in association with previous history of trauma, surgery, or underlying hyperlipidemia states [2, 3]. Several authors have reported the presence of intraosseous xanthomas in the tibia, pelvis, ilium, rib, and, less frequently, the skull. Lesions have been reported in the orbital rim, frontal bone, and unilateral or bilateral temporal bone [4]. To our knowledge, temporal skull involvement for skull xanthomas remains a rare entity. This often poses a diagnostic dilemma in patients who may have underlying neoplasms, thus affecting staging and choice of treatment. We present one case of a patient who was found to have a temporal skull xanthoma initially characterized as likely Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LGH) on the basis of the clinical history and imaging characteristics.

Case history

A 32-year-old Han Chinese woman presented to our department for evaluation of a temporal bone mass for more than 20 days on 13 November 2023. More than 20 days before the admission, the patient inadvertently found a mass in the right external ear canal when she was digging in her ear, and she had decreased hearing in her right ear, but no tinnitus was found. She went to the otolaryngology department of a local hospital for consultation. Mastoid and ear CT showed multiple bone destructions in bilateral temporal bone and soft tissue mass formation in right mastoid area, affecting right tympanum and external ear canal (Fig. 1A). Cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed the irregular soft tissue mass in bilateral temporal bone area with obvious uneven enhancement, which involved the right tympanic cavity, mastoid process, and external ear canal (Fig. 1B). Considering the high possibility of bone-derived tumors, it was recommended to exclude Langerhans cell histiocytosis clinically. For further treatment, the patient was admitted to our hospital. After careful physical examination, a gray mass was seen in the external auditory canal of the patient’s right ear. In addition, the patient had multiple nodules on the knees, elbows, sacrococcygeal joints, and buttocks (but the changes were not obvious in recent years). After the patient was admitted to our department, the hearing examination showed conductive hearing loss in the right ear, and no similar tinnitus was found. Total cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol were slightly elevated. This case was discussed by our multidisciplinary team.

The dermatologist considered that the diagnosis for the patient was lipoma or xanthoma. The patient’s family said that the patient had nodules on the knees, elbows, sacrococcygeal joints, and buttocks since childhood, which had not changed much in the past 10 years, and they refused to undergo a skin biopsy this time. The otorhinolaryngologist was able to assist us in surgery and perform middle ear lesion removal. Hearing loss caused by external auditory canal occlusion and facial paralysis caused by facial nerve injury after surgery should be explained to the patient in the early stage. The radiologist provided the initial diagnosis as Langerhans cell histiocytosis or hyperparathyroidism. They recommended parathyroid hormone (PTH), thyroid function, and thyroid ultrasonography to exclude hyperparathyroidism. The hematologist gave three suggestions. Firstly, relevant examinations should be performed, including anemia series, abnormal peripheral blood cell morphology, thyroid function, pituitary function, abdominal ultrasound, serum immunoelectrophoresis, and serum fixation electrophoresis. Secondly, if a histological examination is performed during the surgery, it is recommended to perform BRAF gene testing. Third, bone puncture could also be performed if necessary. The patient was later considered to have iron-deficiency anemia, and the final postoperative pathological biopsy revealed xanoma. Therefore, no other tests, such as genetic testing, were ultimately performed.

On the basis of the opinions of various departments and preoperative examination, the patient was diagnosed with Langerhans cell histiocytosis. After excluding surgical contraindications, surgical treatment was performed with the assistance of the doctor from the Otolaryngology Department. After general anesthesia, the patient was placed in a supine position with the head frame fixed and the head turned to the right (Fig. 2A). After surgical exposing of the temporal bone, we found that the tumor was yellow and grainy, and the boundary between it and the surrounding tissue was not clear (Fig. 2B). The external auditory canal to the Eustachian canal tumor was first resected, and three auditory ossicles (incus, malleus, and stapes) were removed. Then, the tumor was separated horizontally along the facial nerve and excised to the jugular bulbar, and the residual bone of the mastoid tip was treated. The upper boundary of the tumor was removed to the corner of the sigmoid sinus and the lower boundary to the jugular tubercle. The operation was successful, and the patient returned to the neurosurgical care unit safely.Fig. 2A Intraoperative position. The patient had a perfect recovery after surgery, with only mild nausea and vomiting, hearing loss of the right ear, and no obvious facial paralysis. On the first day after surgery, CT scan showed that the patient recovered well (Fig. 3A), and the drainage tube in the cranial cavity was removed later. Postoperative MRI showed that the resection of the space was satisfactory, and the tumor was totally resected (Fig. 3B). Postoperative pathology revealed the diagnosis of primary xanthoma, and that the lesion had invaded bone tissue. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) photomicrograph of the xanthomatous infiltrate demonstrated the histopathological specimen characterized by foamy macrophages, foreign body giant cells, and cholesterol clefts (Fig. 4). The patient was anemic (decreased hemoglobin: 78.00 g/L) after the operation, and she was given blood transfusion. On the 6th day after surgery, a lumbar puncture was performed, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was colorless and transparent; the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pressure was normal. Discussion

Xanthomas are benign lesions characterized by yellowish cholesterol-rich deposits in various regions, such as skin, tendons, and bones. They are often divided into primary and secondary xanthomas. Secondary xanthomas are frequently associated with underlying metabolic states, such as increased levels of cholesterol, diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, and lipoprotein deficiency [5]. When no other systemic diseases can be found, it is characterized as a primary lesion. Intracranial intra-axial lesions are diagnosed owing to their localized mass effects leading to neurologic symptoms. Conversely, skull involvement with bony destruction may present as seizures or may be due to overlying skin changes [2, 6].

Intracranial xanthomas are extremely rare entities, and their occurrence is usually independent of lipid status. Our patient underwent screening for her blood lipid level, which showed that total cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol were slightly elevated, thus leading to the diagnosis of primary xanthoma after histologic diagnosis was confirmed. In 2000, Kuroiwa et al. [7] reported one of the earliest cases of unilateral temporal bone xanthoma with no evidence of underlying hyperlipidemia. Since then, there have been sporadic case reports of such lesions located in frontal bone [8], the orbitofrontal region [9], and unilateral and bilateral temporal bone with no lipid abnormalities, highlighting the lack of understanding of the nature of origin of such lesions [10]. Clinical examination may be unremarkable for such lesions unless there is presence of cortical bone destruction. Imaging characteristics are also varied, depending on size and location of these lesions. Skull radiographs, if performed, may show localized bone destruction with osteosclerosis. Computed tomography may reveal destructive osteolytic lesions with expansion, as well as surrounding osteosclerosis [11, 12]. Contrast-enhanced MRI will show a heterogeneously enhancing mass on both T1 and T2 sequences due to the presence of cholesterol deposits. Guerrouj et al. [13] showed increased tracer uptake in the diaphysis of humerus and femur in a patient who presented with multiple intraosseous xanthomas and resultant fracture.

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare condition that presents clinically in various ways. The cause and subsequent development of LCH are idiopathic and not fully understood [14]. This disease is mainly seen in childhood [15]. It is rare to have bilateral temporal bone LCH as the initial presentation [16]. LCH isolated to the temporal bone is not only commonly misdiagnosed at initial presentation [17], partly because of its rarity, but also because its initial clinical presentation is nonspecific—otorrhea, otalgia, postauricular skin rashes, hearing loss, and tissue swelling—and mimics other common otologic pathologies, including otitis media, otitis externa, mastoiditis, ear polyps, and temporal abscess [16, 18]. Laboratory values are also nonspecific, although white blood cell count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and C-reactive protein may be elevated [19].

Skull xanthomas may not have any distinguishing features from Langerhans cell histiocytosis on either physical examination or imaging modalities. This case highlights that while xanthomas are benign lesions, we may not be able to distinguish them from other neoplastic occupations, thus requiring surgical resection for diagnosis. As staging of a neoplasm and subsequent treatment will be significantly affected by diagnosis, upfront resection can be offered to allow confirmation despite the known benign course.

Conclusion

Intraosseous xanthomas of the skull are rare entities that may be diagnosed incidentally or due to symptoms from localized mass effects. In the presence of an underlying neoplasm, imaging characteristics may not be able to distinguish them from malignant lesions despite their known benign course. Surgical excision may still be required for histologic diagnosis to allow appropriate treatment and exclude aggressive lesions. Further studies with regard to usage of radionuclide scans and contrast-enhanced imaging may aid in their presumptive diagnosis and management planning.
