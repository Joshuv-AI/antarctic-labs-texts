Evaluation of foreign bodies in the maxillofacial region with cone beam computed tomography and ultrasonography: an ex vivo study
Authors: Kubra Taka, Umut Pamukcu, Gulsun Akay, Yeliz Kilinc, Ilkay Peker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhen foreign bodies in the human body are not diagnosed (detected), it can cause various inflammatory reactions. Therefore, they should be detected and removed immediately. Taking a detailed patient history, performing a clinical examination, and selecting the appropriate imaging method are important for detection. The location, structure, and size of the foreign body affect the choice of radiography technique. This study aims to compare the detectability, measurement accuracy, and acoustic behaviors of various foreign bodies in the maxillofacial region using Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) and Ultrasonography (US) under ex vivo conditions.MethodsForeign bodies (wood, amalgam, glass, tooth, graphite, composite, plastic, and stone) and orthodontic materials (Elgiloy wire, TMA wire, stainless steel wire, stainless steel bracket, and ceramic bracket) with different sizes were placed on the mandibular cortical bone and in the tongue of a sheep’s head. Foreign bodies’ visibility and diameter measurements were made with CBCT and US. Data were statistically analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-order test, the Friedman test, and the Pearson correlation coefficient.ResultsCBCT demonstrated superior inter- and intra-observer agreement, while the US exhibited higher sensitivity (95.4%) in detecting foreign bodies. CBCT failed to visualize plastic and wood, whereas the US successfully detected all materials. Larger foreign bodies could be detected more easily with both imaging methods. There was no statistically significant difference between the diameters of the foreign bodies measured on CBCT and US and their actual diameters (p > 0.05). Posterior acoustic enhancement was detected in glass, amalgam, stainless steel wire, and stainless steel brackets, while posterior acoustic shadowing was observed in teeth, composite, plastic, and stone.ConclusionsCBCT is optimal for radiopaque foreign body detection, whereas the US excels in identifying radiolucent materials. All foreign bodies could be visualized with US, wood and plastic could not be observed with CBCT. As the size of the foreign body increased, it could be observed more clearly on both CBCT and US. The foreign body sensitivity of the US was higher than that of the CBCT. These findings suggest that CBCT is preferable for identifying metalic and dense radiopaque foreign bodies, while the US may be beneficial in detecting radiolucent materials such as wood and plastic.Keywords: CBCT, Ultrasonography, Foreign bodies, Mandible, Tongue

Background

Foreign bodies are objects that enter and become trapped in the body against the person’s will due to traumatic events or with the person’s consent for accessory or treatment purposes [1]. Traumatic events that cause foreign bodies to be trapped in the maxillofacial region can occur as traffic accidents, physical injuries, gunshot wounds, complications of various medical interventions, iatrogenic injuries, during eating, and self-harming behavior [2, 3].

Foreign body injuries constitute 3.8% of pathologies in the head and neck region [4]. Approximately 1/3 of foreign bodies cannot be diagnosed in the clinical and radiological examination of the first application of the patients [5]. When foreign bodies in the human body are not diagnosed (detected) and treated, they can cause various inflammatory reactions such as impaired wound healing, pain, swelling, abscess, and fistula formation [6, 7]. Therefore, foreign bodies should be detected and removed immediately to prevent these potential complications. Especially if the foreign bodies are in a critical location and close to vital structures, and if surgical removal poses a high risk for the patient, localization of the foreign body becomes even more important [8].

To diagnose the type and location of the foreign body, it is important to take a detailed patient history, perform a clinical examination, and select the appropriate imaging method. The location, structure, and size of the foreign body causing the injury affect the choice of radiography technique [1]. Accurate imaging of the location of the foreign body allows the determination of its relationship with the surrounding tissues and the prediction of possible risks that may occur during surgery [7, 8]. In recent studies, different imaging techniques were used to localize and even determine the type of foreign bodies (wood, plastic, fishbone, chicken bone, normal glass, porcelain, barium glass, syringe needle, stone, amalgam, composite, glass ionomer cement, zinc phosphate cement, calcium hydroxide, zinc phosphate, dental floss, gutta-percha, alginate, acrylic orthodontic wires and brackets, metal fragments, graphite, Bakelite, asphalt, tooth, rose thorn, cactus thorn, walnut shell, peanut shell, etc.) trapped in the maxillofacial region [1, 5, 7, 9–16].

This study used 13 foreign bodies of different contents and types, most of which were used separately in previous studies and had a high probability of penetrating and becoming trapped in the maxillofacial region. The detectability and measurement accuracy of various foreign bodies placed in two locations in the maxillofacial region (on the bone [mandible] and in the soft tissue [tongue]) under ex vivo conditions (dead sheep head) were evaluated with two different imaging methods (Cone Beam Computed Tomography; CBCT and Ultrasonography; US). This study aims to compare the detectability, measurement accuracy, and acoustic behaviors of various foreign bodies in the maxillofacial region using CBCT and US under ex vivo conditions.

Methods

This study was approved by Gazi University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee with the date 21.02.2023 and document number 593,091. The study was conducted at Gazi University, Faculty of Dentistry, Department of Oral, Dental and Maxillofacial Radiology, Radiology Clinic in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Preparation of foreign bodies

In this ex vivo study, 13 different foreign bodies (dental treatment materials and other materials) likely to be trapped in the maxillofacial region and found appropriate for use in similar study designs were used [1, 15]. These are; Glass, plastic, stone, graphite, wood, tooth, amalgam (Avalloy, Cavex, Netherlands), composite (Charizma, Kulzer, Germany), and materials used in orthodontic treatment as titanium molybdenum alloy (0.017 × 0.025 in. TMA; Rocky Mountain Orthodontics, Franklin, IN, USA;) wire, stainless steel wire (0.017 × 0.025 in.; American Orthodontics, Sheboygan, WI, USA;), chrome cobalt-nickel alloy wire (0.017 × 0.025 in.; Elgiloy wire, Rocky Mountain Orthodontics, Franklin, IN, USA), stainless steel bracket #34 (American Orthodontics, Sheboygan, WI, USA), and ceramic bracket #34 (American Orthodontics, Sheboygan, WI, USA). Broken glass pieces for glass, Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC) for plastic, small amorphous stone pieces for stone, pencil lead for graphite, dry toothpick for wood, extracted sheep tooth crown pieces for tooth, ready-made amalgam capsules for amalgam, and light-cured dental composite for composite were used. The samples were prepared in three different sizes, approximately 2, 3, and 4 mm in diameter, according to the measurements taken from the widest part, except for the orthodontic materials (Fig. 1). Orthodontic materials were used in one dimension. 1 cm length TMA, stainless steel, and Elgiloy wires, and standard-size stainless steel (5 mm) and ceramic (6 mm) brackets were used as orthodontic materials (Fig. 2). The materials used and their dimensions were selected based on previous literature data and those frequently observed in injuries in the maxillofacial region [9].

Preparation of sample

Foreign bodies were placed in two different areas of the dead sheep’s head: (1) for evaluation on the bone tissue; the mandibular buccal cortical bone, and (2) for evaluation inside the soft tissue; the tongue was selected [12]. For evaluations on bone tissue, an incision line was created with a scalpel from the vestibular sulcus in the bilateral mandible posterior region. Foreign objects were placed by opening the space between the soft tissue and the bone surface. For evaluations inside the soft tissue, a few incisions were made on the dorsal surface of the tongue with a scalpel, and foreign objects were placed in these areas. Although foreign objects had to be placed at different depths depending on the area where they were placed, this depth varied between approximately 3–5 mm.

Acquisition and evaluation of radiographic images and scans

The placement of foreign bodies used in the study into the sheep’s head, acquisition of CBCT images, and US scans were performed on the same day.

CBCT images were obtained with a Planmeca Promax 3D Mid (Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland) device, imaging the maxilla and mandible, with a 14 × 9.2 cm FOV area, 90 kVp, 12 mA, 13.5 s scan time and 0.4 mm voxel size (Fig. 3a). US scans were performed with a SonoSite M-Turbo US (FUJIFILM SonoSite, Inc. 21919 30th Drive SE Bothell, WA 98021 USA) device using a 6–13 Mhz linear probe (Fig. 3b). The ultrasound gel was squeezed into a latex glove, and the probe was placed inside this glove; the US scans were performed in this manner.

a) Position of the sheep’s head in the CBCT device, b) Position of the sheep’s head for US scan

Foreign bodies placed in the sheep’s head were evaluated in two different sessions by two dentomaxillofacial radiologists with at least five years of experience (G.A. and U.P.) and one dentomaxillofacial radiology research assistant with three years of experience (K.T). In both imaging methods, the visibility of foreign bodies was scored between 0 and 4 according to standard evaluation criteria [11].

Score 0: No visibility (Foreign body cannot be viewed).

Score 1: Poor visibility (Foreign body view without details, indistinguishable from the surroundings).

Score 2: Moderate visibility (Foreign body view with insufficient details, indistinguishable from the surroundings).

Score 3: Good visibility (Foreign body view with sufficient details, distinguishable from the surroundings).

Score 4: Excellent visibility (Foreign body view with sufficient clear details, clearly distinguished from the surroundings).

On CBCT images, foreign body dimension measurements were made on cross-sectional sections of orthodontic wires. In contrast, the remaining foreign bodies were measured by their largest diameters on axial sections. Dimension measurements were similarly made with US scans, and visibility scores were evaluated. In addition, acoustic behaviors such as acoustic shadowing or enhancement in the posterior of US scans were recorded [1, 15]. Foreign bodies placed on the buccal cortical bone surface of the mandible were evaluated extraorally via the cheek region, and foreign bodies placed in the tongue region were evaluated intraorally with US.

The images were re-evaluated after 30 days. In the second evaluation of CBCT images, the visibility scores and dimensions of foreign bodies were recorded. The second US evaluations were made on the saved snapshot images of the scans made for the first evaluation. Since the second evaluation of the US was made on the saved snapshot images and a new scan was not possible, the second dimension measurements of the US could not be made.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corporation, USA) program was used for statistical analysis. All statistical analyses evaluated the results at a 95% significance level and p < 0.05. Descriptive data were expressed as mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, median, and frequency. Differences between measurements were analyzed using the Wilcoxon Rank Sign test and the Friedman test. The study evaluated the effectiveness of CBCT and US in determining the visibility of foreign bodies according to their diameters and in measuring their diameters by comparing them with the actual values of the relevant objects. In addition, the sensitivity of CBCT and US in imaging foreign bodies was calculated individually. The visibility scores of foreign bodies visualized with CBCT and US according to their diameters (2, 3, and 4 mm) were statistically compared with the actual diameters of the objects and the diameters measured on the images, and the average of the evaluations made by three observers was calculated. For the visibility scores and diameter measurements on CBCT and US and the posterior acoustic behavior of foreign bodies in the US, since three observers made all evaluations, the Fleiss Kappa Coefficient was calculated for the assessment of inter-observer agreement [17, 18], and the Cohen Kappa Coefficient was calculated for the evaluation of intra-observer agreement. Since the diameter measurements made with the US are a dynamic evaluation and the measurements are not repeated, an intra-observer agreement was not evaluated for the diameter measurements. A kappa coefficient below 0.00 was considered to indicate poor agreement, 0.00-0.20 was considered to indicate very low agreement, 0.21–0.40 was considered to indicate low agreement, 0.41–0.60 was considered to indicate moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 was considered to indicate high agreement and 0.81-1.00 was considered to indicate very high agreement [17, 18]. The relationship between the diameter measurements made on CBCT and US and the real diameters of the foreign bodies was evaluated by Spearman correlation analysis. A correlation coefficient approaching + 1 indicated a perfect relationship, and a correlation approaching 0 indicated no relationship [17]. If the correlation coefficient is less than 0.20, it is considered a very weak relationship, if it is between 0.20 and 0.39, it is considered a weak relationship, if it is between 0.40 and 0.59, it is considered a moderate relationship, if it is between 0.60 and 0.79, it is considered a high level of relationship, and if it is between 0.80 and 1.00, it is considered a very high level of relationship [17].

Results

Agreement of observers

CBCT exhibited significantly higher inter-observer agreement (Fleiss Kappa = 0.674, p < 0.001) for visibility scores compared to the US (Fleiss Kappa = 0.379, p < 0.001). Intra-observer visibility scores were very high both for CBCT (Cohen Kappa Coefficient; 0.865; p < 0.001) and US (Cohen Kappa Coefficient; 0.646; p < 0.001). Inter-observer agreement was very high for the posterior acoustic behavior of foreign bodies with the US (Fleiss Kappa Coefficient; 0.837; p < 0.001). Intra-observer agreement was very high for the posterior acoustic behavior of foreign bodies with the US (Cohen Kappa Coefficient; 0.893; p < 0.001). Intra-observer agreement for diameter measurements on CBCT was high (Spearman Correlation Coefficient; 0.990; p < 0.001).

Statistical analysis results for visibility and diameter measurements of foreign bodies on CBCT

According to the evaluations regarding the visibility scores made by all observers, wood and plastic (for all diameters) could not be observed on CBCT (Score 0). Amalgam and composite (for all diameters) and orthodontic wires (Elgiloy, stainless steel, and TMA) were observed as excellent (score 4) by all observers on CBCT. Therefore, no statistical analysis was performed for the relevant materials.

A statistically significant difference was found between the visibility scores of different diameters of glass, graphite, stone, and orthodontic brackets (stainless steel and ceramic brackets) placed on the mandibular buccal cortical bone on CBCT (p < 0.05) (Table 1). While a statistically significant difference was found between the visibility scores of orthodontic brackets (stainless steel and ceramic brackets) placed in the tongue on CBCT (p < 0.05), there was no statistically significant difference between the visibility scores for the other foreign bodies (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Statistical analysis results for visibility and diameter measurements of foreign bodies with US

However, no statistically significant difference was found for the other foreign bodies (p > 0.05) (Table 2). Additionally, no statistically significant difference was found between the real diameters of foreign bodies placed on the buccal cortical bone of the mandible and in the tongue, and their diameters were measured with US.

Discussion

Foreign bodies are visualized differently in different imaging methods depending on their structure. A foreign body that cannot be visualized in one imaging method can be easily detected in another [1]. When the studies on radiographic localization of foreign bodies are examined, it is seen that their visibility is evaluated chiefly [1, 5, 7, 9–12, 14, 16]. In contrast, few studies have measured the size of the relevant materials with US [15]. This study evaluated the visibility and size measurements of various foreign bodies that may cause injury in the dentomaxillofacial region with CBCT and US.

Abdinian et al. found the inter- and intra-observer agreements excellent for CBCT and US [5]. Demiralp et al. found that inter- and intra-observer agreements were high for CBCT and low for US [1]. In the study of Isman et al., the inter- and intra-observer agreements ranged from 0.78 to 0.91 and 0.83 to 0.97, respectively, for the imaging modalities they used (CBCT, US, MRI, and panoramic radiography) [9]. Javadrashid et al. used CT instead of CBCT and reported excellent intra-observer agreement [12]. In the present study, the inter-observer agreement was found to be high for CBCT and low for US for the visibility scores. The intra-observer agreement was very high for CBCT and US. The inter- and intra-observer agreements were very high for the posterior acoustic behavior. Although mostly consistent with the above studies, the low inter-observer agreement for US for visibility scores may be due to US being an operator-dependent imaging modality.

CBCT is an imaging method designed for the head and neck region to image foreign bodies in three dimensions [19]. Abdinian et al. reported that the detection accuracy of CBCT was significantly affected by the composition of foreign objects and that the detection accuracy of wood was significantly lower than that of other materials [5]. However, for other materials, CBCT was the best detection modality. They found that detection accuracy increased with an increasing foreign body size [5]. Abolvardi et al. stated that plastic particles were of lower quality than those of other materials with CBCT [14]. The 1-mm plastic particles were hardly detected in the nasal cavity and tongue and could not be differentiated in the periosteum (on the bone). They reported that CT could detect smaller foreign bodies better than CBCT [14]. Aras et al. reported that in their examination with CT, metal, glass, stone, and graphite foreign bodies on the bone were clearly detected. However, acrylic and Bakelite could not be clearly observed [11]. They stated that while metal, glass, stone, and graphite foreign bodies were clearly detected in the muscle, wood, acrylic, and Bakelite could be observed with difficulty [11]. Demiralp et al. found that CBCT has high sensitivity in imaging orthodontic wires, amalgam, and stones placed on the bone [1]. In their study, Isman et al. reported that CBCT is the optimal modality for all orthodontic materials in all fields, except for Invisalign in muscle and on bone [9]. Kaviani et al. reported that in the muscle, 1 mm wood and plastic foreign bodies could not be observed with CBCT; objects with a size of 2 mm, except for wood, were also detected [7]. On the bone, metal, stone, glass, tooth, and graphite measuring 1 and 2 mm were detected, but wood and plastic were not [7]. In the present study, plastic and wood foreign bodies (for all diameters) placed on the mandible and in the tongue could not be visualized with CBCT (score 0), consistent with similar findings from previous studies. While there was a statistical difference between the dimensions of glass, graphite, stone, and brackets placed on the mandible and the visibility scores, there was a significant relationship between the dimensions of the brackets and the visibility scores for those placed in the tongue. Generally, as the size of the foreign body increased, the visibility score increased in direct proportion, as in similar studies.

US is a safe, inexpensive, ionizing radiation-free, portable, and easily accessible imaging method for diagnosing foreign bodies [20, 21]. In a systematic review, Davis et al. reported that the US is potentially useful for all types of foreign bodies in the skin and soft tissue and may be an advantageous imaging method for detecting organic/inorganic radiolucent foreign bodies such as wood, plastic, and thorns [22]. Ginsburg et al. reported that they were able to observe wood and plastic only with US in their studies in which they evaluated foreign bodies made of wood, plastic, and glass by placing them in pieces of meat using plain radiography, xeroradiography, CT, and US [23]. In the study by Abdinian et al., the US gave more accurate results for wood than CBCT and panoramic radiography, regardless of size or location [5]. In addition, the detection accuracy of US was greater in soft tissue [5]. Aras et al. stated that with the US, metal, glass, stone, and graphite were detected clearly on the bone, whereas wood, acrylic, and Bakelite were not detected clearly [11]. Metal, glass, wood, stone, graphite, and Bakelite were also detected clearly in the muscle, whereas acrylic was detected with difficulty [11]. Çağlayan et al. reported that all foreign bodies they examined, whether they appeared radiopaque on conventional radiography or not, could be visualized with US [15]. In the Demiralp et al. study, the highest sensitivity was obtained for wood and chicken bone fragments in the muscle imaging with US [1]. On the bone placement, the highest sensitivity was for glass, root, and injector needle [1]. Isman et al. reported that the US detected the TMA, sapphire bracket, and the Invisalign in muscle but only the TMA on bone [9]. Javadrashid et al. reported that wood could only be detected by US and when the pieces were >0.5 mm in size [12]. They did not share the results of the localization differences of the foreign bodies they placed into the muscle and on the bone [12]. In this study, consistent with previous studies, plastic and wood could only be observed with the US. In general, it was found that as the size of foreign bodies increased, their visibility by the US increased.

Other studies report that foreign bodies placed on the bone surface are more difficult to observe than those placed in the muscle [5]. The superiority of the US in detecting foreign bodies in soft tissue over bone surfaces is probably due to the lower penetration of ultrasound waves into tissues. In addition, the ability of the US to detect foreign bodies is affected by the acoustic impedance of the object, its depth, and surrounding tissues [16]. In this study, with the US, wood and plastic were observed more clearly in the tongue than on the bone. There was a statistically significant difference between the visibility scores of amalgam, glass, tooth, composite, and stone placed on the mandible according to their sizes. No statistically significant difference was found between the visibility scores of wood, glass, teeth, composite, brackets, and plastic placed in the tongue according to their dimensions. The study results are similar because Bakelite is a different type of plastic. Additionally, in the present study, although the 2 and 3-mm-diameter plastic placed on the mandible could not be observed, 66% of the 2 and 3-mm plastic in the tongue could be observed.

Çağlayan et al. measured the diameters of foreign bodies with the US. They reported that the measurements did not show any significant difference with the actual diameters of the foreign bodies [15]. This study found no statistical difference between the actual diameters of the foreign bodies and those measured with the US.

In their study examining the posterior acoustic behavior of objects, Çağlayan et al. reported that 62.5% of the objects caused posterior acoustic shadowing on US [15]. Demiralp et al. reported that acoustic shadowing occurred in 46.1% of the foreign bodies examined. It was observed in chicken bone fragments, roots, stones, tooth enamel, amalgam, and orthodontic wires [1]. This study observed that foreign bodies with similar structures caused acoustic shadowing, which aligns with the literature.

Hill et al. placed pieces of plastic and wood under the skin of a previously cut human leg at a depth between 0.5 and 1.5 cm and found that the US was 93% sensitive to wood and 77% sensitive to plastic [24]. In this study, US sensitivity to wood was 100%, and to plastic, 55.5%. It was thought that the differences in the results could be due to the differences in the resolution of US devices used, the depth at which the foreign bodies were placed, and the diameters of the foreign bodies.

Seitz et al. stated that early diagnosis and treatment of penetrating pencil lead injuries, which are common in young children, can prevent many complications. They reported that radiographic imaging is essential in diagnosis and localization, and radiopaque graphite can be detected on conventional radiography, CT, and CBCT [25]. Likewise, in this study, graphite was visualized on both CBCT and with US, but its visibility was higher on CBCT than with US.

A study evaluating the visibility of orthodontic materials (TMA wire, stainless steel brackets, ceramic brackets, clear brackets, Invisalign) reported that these materials were best visualized on CBCT (except Invisalign). In contrast, all orthodontic materials were visualized with US but with lower visibility than CBCT (except Invisalign). In addition, they reported that Invisalign was observed more clearly in muscle tissue with US than on CBCT [9]. In the present study, stainless steel wire, TMA wire, Elgiloy wire, stainless steel bracket, and ceramic bracket were used as orthodontic materials, and their visibility was higher on CBCT than with US, consistent with the previous study [9].

The limitations of this study can be listed as follows: (1) In the study, a sheep head containing muscle, bone, soft tissues, and teeth was used instead of human tissues. This situation should be taken into account when interpreting the results obtained. (2) The study did not evaluate the possibility of false positives. No evaluation was made regarding the images in which the foreign body was not placed. (3) In this study, the use of a single CBCT and a single US device constitutes a limitation for the comparison of possibly different results obtained with different devices. (4) Since this study was conducted under ex vivo conditions, tissue density, fluid dynamics, and soft tissue motion were not considered, which may impact clinical applicability.

Conclusion

CBCT is optimal for radiopaque foreign body detection, whereas US excels in identifying radiolucent materials. The choice of imaging modality should be guided by the foreign body’s composition and anatomical location. It was determined that as the size of the foreign body increased, it was observed more clearly both on CBCT and with US. In cases where a foreign body is suspected in the dentomaxillofacial region, the material’s content and localization should be considered when selecting the imaging method. Clinicians should consider US when evaluating suspected wooden or plastic foreign bodies, while CBCT remains preferable for detecting metallic and dense materials. Although the absence of radiation in the US is a great advantage, based on our experience in this study, its use is complicated and sometimes even impossible, especially in extremely curved (rough) narrow areas such as the maxillofacial region.
