Extracellular vesicles from hypertrophic cardiomyocytes promote breast cancer progression
Authors: Weiwei Chen, Yutong Yao, Benkai Xin, Peipei An, Yonghao Dai, Hanwei Gao, Jingtong Yang, Xiaoyu Wang, Yueru Shi, Zhoulei Zhu, Nan Zhang, Youzhong Wan, Yuquan He, Xin Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Several cardiovascular diseases are associated with tumor development. But the mechanisms underlying how the hypertrophic heart promotes tumor progression remain unknown. Here, we show that extracellular vesicles from hypertrophic cardiomyocytes facilitate cancer progression. Following transverse aortic constriction to induce cardiac hypertrophy, 4T1-Luc cells are orthotopically implanted into mice. Concurrently, AC16 cells are treated with angiotensin II, and extracellular vesicles isolated from these cells are administered to BALB/c nude mice previously implanted with MDA-MB-231 cells in their mammary fat pads. Both transverse aortic constriction-operated mice and mice receiving extracellular vesicles from angiotensin II-treated AC16 cells exhibit accelerated breast cancer progression; however, treatment with GW4869, an extracellular-vesicles-release inhibitor, suppresses this effect. Transcriptome sequencing and mass spectrometry indicate that the expression of miR-362-5p, S100A7, and S100A8 is upregulated in these extracellular vesicles, which significantly boosts breast cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and migration. Importantly, miR-362-5p, S100A7, and S100A8 levels are elevated in plasma extracellular vesicles from patients with myocardial hypertrophy and are positively correlated with inflammatory indices. Our research identifies the specific factors in hypertrophic cardiomyocytes that drive tumor progression, laying a theoretical foundation for future targeted intervention and prevention.

Introduction

Patients with various cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), such as heart failure (HF) and atherosclerosis, are at an increased risk of developing cancer1,2. Several studies in mice have demonstrated that myocardial infarction (MI)-induced HF promotes cancer progression via cardiac cytokines, extracellular vesicles (EVs), and immune responses3–7. Cardiac hypertrophy, a precursor to remodeling caused by pressure overload or myocardial loss, is often maladaptive in advanced cardiac conditions such as ischemic heart disease, hypertension and cardiomyopathy, eventually leading to HF8,9. Epidemiological data demonstrated that hypertension with cardiac hypertrophy correlates with the initiation and progression of breast cancer10–12, endometrial cancer13, and renal and bladder carcinoma14. Notably, Avraham et al.15 showed that inducing cardiac hypertrophy in mice through transverse aortic constriction (TAC) enhanced the proliferation of implanted breast cancer cells, resulting in larger tumor sizes and an increased rate of metastasis compared to controls. In other studies, cardiac hypertrophy was induced in mice through the overexpression of activating transcription factor 3 (ATF3) or low-dose phenylephrine (PE) infusion, resulting in larger tumor volumes and higher metastatic potential of implanted mammary carcinoma cells16,17. However, the mechanisms underlying how the hypertrophic heart promotes tumor progression remain unknown.

EVs derived from cardiomyocytes influence cardiac hypertrophy, survival, apoptosis, fibrosis, and angiogenesis through diverse signaling pathways18. Interestingly, treatment with plasma EVs from mice with MI-induced HF reduces tumor cell sensitivity to the ferroptosis activator erastin5. Nevertheless, the specific cellular source of these EVs in mice with infarcted hearts remains unidentified. Here, we hypothesized that EVs from hypertrophic cardiomyocytes promote the progression of malignancies, such as breast cancer, and sought to identify the intra-vesicular factors that mediate these pro-oncogenic effects. To investigate this, we sought to identify the specific miRNA or protein in EVs from hypertrophic cardiomyocytes responsible for such tumor-promoting effects. Our study underscores the importance of early diagnosis and treatment of cardiac remodeling, given its potential to contribute to cancer progression via S100A7, S100A8, and miR-362-5p in EVs secreted from hypertrophic cardiomyocytes.

Results

Cardiac hypertrophy promoted breast cancer progression via EVs

To validate the establishment of a mouse model of cardiac hypertrophy, we undertook echocardiographic evaluations, hemodynamic assessments, and pathological staining at various intervals post-TAC (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Pulsed-wave Doppler measurements indicated that TAC-operated mice exhibited increased flow velocity at the site of aortic stenosis (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C). Although cardiac hypertrophy was sustained from 2 to 6 weeks post-TAC, a decline in ejection fraction was not observed until week 8 post-surgery (Supplementary Fig. 1D–F). Consistent with these observations, hemodynamic assessments showed that both aortic systolic pressure (AP) and left ventricular end-systolic pressure (LVESP) increased for up to 8 weeks post-TAC (Supplementary Fig. 1G, H). Histological examination of cardiac tissues from TAC-operated mice revealed characteristic manifestations of hypertrophy alongside other indicators of ongoing cardiac remodeling, including extracellular mesenchymal tissue proliferation and expanded fibrotic areas (Supplementary Fig. 1I, J). The expression levels of cardiac hypertrophy biomarkers, including atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), myosin heavy chain 7 (MYH7), and actin alpha 1 (ACTA1), were elevated in TAC-operated animals (Supplementary Fig. 1K, L). Collectively, these findings suggested that TAC-treated mice manifest cardiac remodeling and hypertrophic phenotypes between 2 and 6 weeks post-operation, without overt signs of HF.

To investigate whether EVs were involved in the promotion of breast cancer progression during cardiac hypertrophy, 4T1-Luc cells were orthotopically implanted in mice 3 weeks post-TAC, followed by the intraperitoneal administration of the EV inhibitor GW4869 (Fig. 1A). Tumor volume and weight were significantly greater in the TAC group than in the Sham-operated group; however, GW4869 administration significantly suppressed these tumor-promoting effects in TAC-operated mice (Fig. 1B–D). These findings were corroborated by bioluminescence analysis (Fig. 1E, F). Furthermore, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed that tumor invasion into adjacent tissues was significantly increased in TAC-operated mice. The dashed black line in the H&E staining marks the boundary between tumor and adjacent tissue; this boundary is poorly defined in TAC-treated mice (Fig. 1G). Additionally, Ki67 staining indicated that tumor cell proliferation was enhanced in TAC-operated mice (Fig. 1H, I). Importantly, the elevated invasion and proliferation of tumor cells observed in TAC-operated mice were attenuated by GW4869 treatment (Fig. 1G–I). To exclude any direct effects of GW4869 treatment on myocardial hypertrophy, we undertook echocardiography, hemodynamic monitoring, pathological staining, and quantification of myocardial remodeling markers in both TAC- and Sham-operated mice following 2 weeks of GW4869 administration (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Notably, GW4869 treatment did not affect the aortic stenosis flow rate, the degree of myocardial hypertrophy (Supplementary Fig. 2B–E), cardiac function (Supplementary Fig. 2F), hemodynamics (Supplementary Fig. 2G, H), fibrotic area (Supplementary Fig. 2I, J), or the expression of hypertrophy-related markers (Supplementary Fig. 2K, L). EVs from human hypertrophic cardiomyocytes promoted breast cancer progression

To assess the impact of human hypertrophic cardiomyocyte-derived EVs on human breast cancer progression, AC16 cells were treated with Ang II to induce hypertrophy (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 3A), and EVs were subsequently isolated from both Control and Ang II-treated cells (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 3B, C). Following the implantation of MDA-MB-231 cells into the mammary fat pads of BALB/c nude mice, DiR-labeled Control- and Ang II-EVs were administered via tail vein injection (Fig. 2C). We found that the DiR-labeled EVs achieved whole-body distribution, including in the mammary glands, lungs, and liver, 24 h post-injection (Supplementary Fig. 4A–C). Mice treated with Ang II-EVs displayed significantly greater tumor volumes and mass than those treated with Control-EVs (Fig. 2D–F). Furthermore, histological analysis suggested that Ang II-EVs promoted tumor invasion into adjacent tissues, evidenced by an indistinct boundary between the tumor and adjacent tissue. The dashed black line in the H&E staining marks the boundary between tumor and adjacent tissue; this boundary is poorly defined in Ang II-EVs-treated mice (Fig. 2G). To investigate the effects of EVs derived from hypertrophic AC16 cells on breast cancer cells in vitro, we treated MDA-MB-231 and BT549 cells with PKH67-labeled Ang II- and Control-EVs. Fluorescence staining demonstrated that these EVs were internalized by MDA-MB-231 cells and primarily localized to the cytoplasm (Fig. 3A). Treatment with Ang II-EVs significantly enhanced the proliferation (Fig. 3B), invasion (Fig. 3C, D), and migration (Fig. 3E, F) of MDA-MB-231 cells. Similar results were obtained with BT549 cells (Fig. 3G–L). Moreover, given that epidemiological studies have demonstrated that hypertension is associated with endometrial cancer13 and renal and bladder carcinoma14, we treated renal carcinoma (786-O), bladder carcinoma (5637), and endometrial carcinoma (Ishikawa) cells with the Ang II- and Control-EVs, and found that treatment with Ang II-EVs enhanced the proliferative, invasive, and migratory properties of all three cell types (Supplementary Figs. 5–7, respectively). EVs from hypertrophic cardiomyocytes promoted breast cancer progression via S100A7 and S100A8

To explore the mechanisms by which hypertrophic cardiomyocyte-derived EVs promote breast cancer progression, we performed proteomic profiling to identify proteins showing differential expression between Control-EVs and Ang II-EVs. In total, 72 proteins were found to be differentially expressed between the two sets of EVs, 32 of which were upregulated in the Ang II-EV group (Supplementary Fig. 8A). S100A7, DPP7, DOCK5, S100A8, and CDC73 were found to be the five most significantly upregulated proteins, while TNNT1, FTL, HYPK, HRNR, and MYL3 were the proteins showing the greatest downregulation (Fig. 4A). KEGG enrichment analysis demonstrated that these proteins were predominantly associated with pathways related to transport and catabolism, signal transduction, and infectious and cardiovascular diseases (Fig. 4B). As two of the top five most upregulated proteins—S100A7 and S100A8—belong to the same protein family, we next investigated their specific roles in the tumor-promoting effects of EVs derived from hypertrophic cardiomyocytes. The results indicated that S100A7 and S100A8 expression were significantly higher in Ang II-EVs than in Control-EVs (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. 9A, B). Similarly, S100A7 and S100A8 protein expression were higher in MDA-MB-231 cells treated with Ang II-EVs than in those treated with Control-EVs (Supplementary Fig. 9C, D). S100A7 and S100A8 have been reported to play important roles in cancer development through their effects on the NF-κB signaling pathway19,20. Here, employing a luciferase reporter assay, we confirmed that S100A7 and S100A8 activated NF-κB signaling (Supplementary Fig. 10A). Furthermore, the overexpression of S100A7 and S100A8 significantly increased the proliferation, invasion, and migration of MDA-MB-231 cells; however, treatment with the NF-κB inhibitor BAY-11-7082 suppressed these effects (Supplementary Fig. 10B–F). To further verify the roles of S100A7 and S100A8 in EVs derived from Ang II-treated AC16 cells, we knocked down their expression in AC16 cells and subsequently isolated EVs from these cells after Ang II treatment (Supplementary Fig. 11A–D). Compared with MDA-MB-231 cells treated with Ang II-EVs, those treated with Ang II+siS100A7-EVs, Ang II+siS100A8-EVs, or their combination siS100A7+siS100A8-EVs showed significantly reduced proliferation (Fig. 4D), invasion (Fig. 4E, F), and migration (Fig. 4G, H). Combined, these results demonstrated that EVs from hypertrophic cardiomyocytes promote breast cancer progression via S100A7 and S100A8.

EVs from hypertrophic cardiomyocytes promoted breast cancer progression via miR-362-5p

To comprehensively understand how EVs from hypertrophic cardiomyocytes contribute to breast cancer progression, we employed transcriptome sequencing to identify miRNAs differentially expressed between Control-EVs and Ang II-EVs. In total, 191 miRNAs were found to be differentially expressed, with 51 showing upregulation in the Ang II-EV group (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. 12). Among these, we focused on miR-362-5p, given that it ranked highest in unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts and displayed documented oncogenic effects in multiple cancer types21–23. We confirmed that miR-362-5p expression was elevated in Ang II-EVs relative to that in Control-EVs (Fig. 5B). MiR-362-5p was reported to regulate the NF-κB signaling pathway by targeting CYLD24–27. In MDA-MB-231 cells, the transfection of miR-362-5p mimics decreased CYLD expression, whereas treatment with a miR-362-5p inhibitor exerted the opposite effect (Supplementary Fig. 13A, B). In line with these findings, treatment with Ang II-EVs decreased CYLD levels in MDA-MB-231 cells (Supplementary Fig. 13C, D). Furthermore, the transfection of miR-362-5p mimics increased NF-κB signaling, whereas treatment with the inhibitor BAY-11-7082 suppressed these effects (Supplementary Fig. 14A). Importantly, the transfection of miR-362-5p mimics enhanced the proliferation, invasion, and migration of MDA-MB-231 cells, while BAY-11-7082 administration attenuated these oncogenic effects (Supplementary Fig. 14B–F). These findings suggested that miR-362-5p drives breast cancer progression via the NF-κB pathway. Similar results were observed in BT549 cells (Supplementary Fig. 15A–E). Next, we transfected AC16 cells with a miR-362-5p inhibitor and then isolated EVs from these cells after Ang II treatment (Ang II+inhibitor-362-5p) (Fig. 5C). The inhibition of miR-362-5p suppressed the promotive effects of Ang II-treated AC16-derived EVs on the proliferation (Fig. 5D), invasion (Fig. 5E, F), and migration (Fig. 5G, H) of MDA-MB-231 cells. Conditioned medium from hypertrophic cardiomyocytes derived from induced pluripotent stem cells promoted breast cancer progression

Furthermore, we validated our findings using induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CMs). Myocyte hypertrophy was successfully induced by treating human iPSC-CMs with 150 nM Ang II (Supplementary Fig. 16A–C). S100A7, S100A8, and miR-362-5p expression levels were significantly higher in conditioned medium from Ang II-treated iPSC-CMs than in that from control cells (Supplementary Fig. 16D–F). Subsequently, we treated MDA-MB-231 cells with conditioned medium from iPSC-CMs, and found that CYLD expression was reduced in these cells relative to that in control cells (Supplementary Fig. 17A). Aligning with these observations, MDA-MB-231 cells exposed to the conditioned medium from Ang II-treated iPSC-CMs exhibited significantly enhanced proliferative (Supplementary Fig. 17B), invasive (Supplementary Fig. 17C, D), and migratory (Supplementary Fig. 17E, F) capacities. Collectively, these results suggested that factors secreted from Ang II-stimulated iPSC-CMs, likely EVs, promote the progression of breast cancer cells.

Plasma EVs from patients with cardiac hypertrophy showed increased expression of S100A7, S100A8, miR-362-5p, and promoted breast cancer progression

To determine whether the expression of S100A7, S100A8, and miR-362-5p in EVs is upregulated in the plasma of patients with cardiac hypertrophy, we conducted a case-control study on a cohort comprising 152 patients with cardiac hypertrophy and 117 non-hypertrophic control patients (Fig. 6A). The baseline characteristics of the participants are detailed in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1. Following propensity score matching, liver and kidney functions did not differ between the two groups. However, S100A7, S100A8, and miR-362-5p expression was found to be higher in plasma EVs from patients with cardiac hypertrophy than in those from control patients (Supplementary Fig. 18A–C, Fig. 6B–D). ROC curve analysis suggested that these markers possess diagnostic value, yielding area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.76 (95% CI: 0.71–0.82) for S100A7, 0.67 (95% CI: 0.60–0.73) for S100A8, and 0.82 (95% CI: 0.77–0.87) for miR-362-5p (Fig. 6E). Logistic regression analysis showed that S100A7, miR-362-5p, the neutrophil-percentage-to-albumin ratio (NPAR), the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) were independently associated with cardiac hypertrophy (Table 2). Moreover, Spearman correlation analysis indicated that S100A7 levels were significantly correlated with inflammatory indices derived from laboratory measurements, specifically the SII, NLR, and NPAR (Fig. 6F). In addition, MDA-MB-231 cells treated with plasma EVs from patients with cardiac hypertrophy exhibited significantly enhanced proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 19A), invasion (Supplementary Fig. 19B, C), and migration (Supplementary Fig. 19D, E). Discussion

Our study demonstrates that S100A7, S100A8, and miR-362-5p, present in EVs from hypertrophic cardiomyocytes, facilitate cancer progression. These findings suggest that the early stages of cardiac remodeling warrant increased clinical attention regarding the potential risks of cancer progression.

Cardiac hypertrophy, a central characteristic of cardiac remodeling, is prevalent in patients with CVDs, such as MI and hypertension. While epidemiological research has indicated that hypertension accompanied by cardiac hypertrophy is correlated with the initiation and progression of various cancers, direct evidence linking cardiac hypertrophy to cancer progression has remained insufficient. Here, we found that TAC-induced cardiac hypertrophy enhanced tumor growth, an effect that was suppressed by treatment with the EV inhibitor GW4869. Furthermore, our data indicated that EVs extracted from hypertrophic myocardial cells enhanced the growth, invasion, and migration of cancer cells both in vitro and in vivo. Recent animal-based research demonstrated that MI-induced HF promotes cancer progression through its effects on cardiac cytokines, EVs, and immune responses3–7. Although these investigations suggested that cardiac remodeling facilitates cancer progression through the secretion of cytokines and growth factors, the specific cell types within the diseased heart that contribute to tumor progression have remained unidentified. In this study, we identified hypertrophic cardiomyocytes as a primary source of pro-tumorigenic EVs, thereby establishing a direct link between cardiomyocyte remodeling and breast cancer promotion.

S100A7 is reported to promote breast cancer cell invasion and metastasis by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway28. S100A8, meanwhile, participates in the regulation of several signaling pathways, including the activation of NF-κB and MAPK29, both of which play important roles in tumor occurrence and development. MiRNA dysregulation is frequently observed in cardiac hypertrophy. For instance, plasma miR-1, miR-212/132, and miR-133 are strongly implicated in the regulation of myocardial hypertrophy30–32. Here, we found that miR-362-5p was upregulated in hypertrophic cardiomyocyte-derived EVs. MiR-362-5p was reported to promote tumor growth and metastasis by targeting the CYLD gene, which encodes a deubiquitinating enzyme with tumor-suppressor activity. Elevated miR-362-5p levels lead to the downregulation of CYLD expression, thereby promoting hepatocellular carcinoma development24. Consistent with these studies, we confirmed that miR-362-5p, its target gene CYLD, and the NF-κB pathway are involved in the EV-mediated promotion of tumor development. Our data demonstrated that EVs from hypertrophic cardiomyocytes promote cancer progression through the delivery of S100A7, S100A8, and miR-362-5p, which contribute to tumor progression via their combined effects on the NF-κB signaling pathway.

Accumulating evidence indicates that patients with CVDs are at a heightened risk of developing cancer1,33. Currently, there is a lack of clinical data regarding the correlation between cardiac remodeling and tumor development. Nonetheless, cardiac remodeling is known to occur in hypertension, which has also been associated with tumorigenesis, particularly in breast cancer34. A recent meta-analysis summarizing 13 studies reported that hypertension increases the risk of breast cancer by 7%34. Moreover, each 1-SD increase in systolic blood pressure was associated with a proportional increase in cancer risk35. However, clinicians often overlook the pathophysiological implications of this condition. Our results suggested that enhanced screening for tumor risk in populations with cardiac hypertrophy is of significant clinical relevance. Importantly, our analysis demonstrated that miR-362-5p, S100A7, and S100A8 expression were increased in the plasma of patients with cardiac hypertrophy. Inflammatory indices, such as the NPAR, SII, and NLR, have been shown to correlate with both CVD and tumor development36,37. Our findings suggested that the levels of miR-362-5p, S100A7, and S100A8 are associated with the extent of inflammation in patients with myocardial hypertrophy. This implies that the tumor-promoting effects of these three factors may be mediated through the promotion of systemic inflammation in patients with cardiac hypertrophy. This further suggests that blocking inflammatory responses mediated by factors contained in hypertrophic cardiomyocyte-derived EVs may represent a viable strategy for mitigating the tumor-promoting effects of cardiac remodeling in patients with CVDs

We demonstrated that EVs secreted by hypertrophic cardiomyocytes can promote breast cancer progression and identified specific proteins and microRNAs, including miR-362-5p, S100A7, and S100A8, as the most significant contributors to this process. Importantly, these vesicles may contain additional components that potentially promote tumor growth. However, these components were not investigated in this study. Future research should concentrate on investigating and validating the potential roles of these factors. Another limitation of our study was the relatively small sample size of both the patient and control groups, which limited subgroup analysis based on demographics and ethnicity. While our current data suggest that these molecular signatures are robust, these findings necessitate validation in larger cohorts of patients with cardiac hypertrophy and appropriately matched controls. Prospective studies examining breast cancer incidence in patients with cardiac hypertrophy would also provide support for our conclusions.

In summary, we identified proteins and miRNAs in EVs from human hypertrophic cardiomyocytes that can promote the progression of human breast cancer. S100A7, S100A8, and miRNA-362-5p in EVs may serve as potential molecular markers for early cancer risk assessment for more accurate screening and follow-up.

Methods

Animals

All animal experiments were conducted following the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Changchun Wish Technology Company (IACUC Issue No. 20240116-01). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Female C57BL/6 mice (3 weeks old, 17–20 g) and female BALB/c nude mice (3 weeks old, 16–20 g) were provided by Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., China. The C57BL/6 mice were subjected to either TAC or sham surgery, while the BALB/c mice were used to generate the orthotopic tumor model. All mice were maintained in Changchun Wish Technology Company under specific pathogen-free conditions and a 12-h/12-h light/dark cycle. Sample size was determined based on previous similar studies in the field and practical considerations. Mice were euthanized using deep isoflurane anesthesia (3%) followed by carbon dioxide inhalation and cervical dislocation, and tissue was subsequently collected.

Clinical analysis

Ethics statement

The clinical study strictly adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Review Board of China–Japan Union Hospital (No. 2023081601). All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant following a comprehensive explanation of the study’s purpose and procedures. Demographic data, hemodynamic parameters, and blood samples were collected once informed consent had been obtained.

A single-center, retrospective, case-control clinical investigation was performed to examine the differences in EV-derived miRNAs and proteins between patients with cardiac hypertrophy and non-cardiac hypertrophy. Patients admitted to the Department of Cardiology at the Hospital from June 1, 2017, through December 31, 2020, were enrolled. Inclusion was based on echocardiographic evidence indicating that left ventricular wall thickening and/or interventricular septum measurements were ≥15 mm. Exclusion criteria included hypertrophic cardiomyopathy secondary to genetic metabolic disorders (e.g., familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, cardiac amyloidosis, and Fabry disease); the co-occurrence of diseases such as cancer, lung disease, and liver disease; and missing clinical data. The control group comprised non-hypertensive patients matched to the case group based on age, gender, smoking status, obesity, alcohol consumption, and markers of liver, kidney, and myocardial function, such as BNP.

TAC surgery and echocardiology

Mice were anesthetized by inhalation of 3% isoflurane in oxygen and then subjected to sham surgery or aortic constriction surgery (TAC) to induce cardiac hypertrophy. The skin of the neck was cut to expose the trachea, which was separated along the upper tracheal segment to the mediastinum. After the removal of the thymus, the aortic arch was exposed, and the surrounding tissues were separated. A 27 G needle was placed over the vessel between the first and second branches on the left side of the aortic arch for ligation. The needle was withdrawn after ligation. The same procedure was undertaken in the sham operation group, except that ligation was not performed. After surgery, lidocaine lincomycin ointment was applied for analgesia and to protect against infection. At different times after surgery, mice were anesthetized via the inhalation of 3% isoflurane in oxygen, fixed in the supine position on a flat surgical table, shaven, and subjected to cardiac ultrasonography using an ultrasound probe with a center frequency of 30 MHz [L38-22K3, Kolo Medical (Suzhou) Co., Ltd]. Left ventricle long-axis views and M-mode echocardiograms were recorded, and the ejection fraction (EF), fractional shortening (FS), interventricular septum thickness (IVS), and left ventricular posterior wall thickness (LVPW) values were measured. The M-mode cursor is aligned vertically on the left ventricular short axis at the level of the mitral valve chordae, positioned between the mitral valve leaflet and the papillary muscle, ensuring it is perpendicular to both the interventricular septum and the left ventricular posterior wall. The end-diastolic left ventricular internal diameter (LVIDd) is measured at the peak of the R wave on the electrocardiogram, while the end-systolic left ventricular internal diameter (LVIDs) is measured at the end of the T wave. The Teichholz formula is then utilized to convert these internal diameter measurements into volume, subsequently allowing for the calculation of the ejection fraction. PW calculation: Initially, employ B-mode (two-dimensional mode) and adjust the probe angle to align the ultrasound beam parallel to the longitudinal axis of the aortic arch, thereby facilitating a clear visualization of the aortic arch and its branches, including the brachiocephalic artery, left common carotid artery, and left subclavian artery. Subsequently, activate the Color Doppler mode on the aortic arch section to assess the direction of blood flow and detect any turbulence. It is essential to adjust the Color Doppler velocity scale to an appropriate range to ensure the accurate depiction of blood flow signals. Mice with successful TAC surgery (confirmed by echocardiography) were included. Data from animals that died prior to the endpoint, or from unsuccessful surgeries or tail vein injections, shall be excluded.

Orthotopic tumor model

In total, 5 × 106 mouse breast cancer cells stably expressing luciferase (4T1-Luc) were injected into the right fourth mammary fat pad of C57BL/6 mice in which cardiac hypertrophy had been successfully established. Tumor size was measured with callipers and the tumor volume was calculated with the following formula: Width2 × Length × 0.5. According to the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, the humane endpoint was achieved when the average tumor size reached 1200 mm3. At the humane endpoint, D-luciferin sodium salt (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) was intraperitoneally injected into the mice based on 150 mg/kg body weight. After 30 min, images were captured in vivo using the Tanon 5200 Automatic Chemiluminescence Imaging Analysis System (Tanon Science & Technology, Shanghai, China). Data from animals that died prior to the endpoint, or from unsuccessful tail vein injections, shall be excluded.

Tumor xenograft model

MDA-MB-231 cells (5 × 106) stably expressing luciferase were injected into the right fourth mammary fat pad of BALB/c nude mice. Tumor size was measured with callipers and the tumor volume was calculated with the following formula: Width2 × Length × 0.5. After 3 days of cell implantation, PBS, control EVs (50 μg), or EVs derived from hypertrophic cardiomyocytes (50 μg) were administered to mice via tail vein injection once every 3 days for a total of nine injections. Subsequently, D-luciferin sodium salt (Yeasen) was intraperitoneally injected into the mice based on 150 mg/kg body weight. After 30 min, images were captured in vivo using the Tanon 5200 Automatic Chemiluminescence Imaging Analysis System (Tanon Science & Technology). The mice were subsequently sacrificed and tumor and lung tissues were isolated for immunohistochemical staining.

EV in vivo tracing and administration of the EV inhibitor GW4869 in mice

To explore whether EVs derived from cardiomyocytes could be transported from blood to mammary glands after intramedullary injection, 100 µL of 0.5 µg/µL control EVs (Control-EVs) and EVs from Ang II-treated AC16 cells (Ang II-EVs) were labeled with 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′ tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide (DiR, D127731, Invitrogen, USA). Prior to the experiment, an RNase-free and sterile working environment was prepared, and all solutions were equilibrated to 37 °C prior to use to ensure optimal staining efficiency. First, the stock solution was prepared: DiR powder was dissolved in anhydrous DMSO to a final concentration of 15 mM, aliquoted into light-protected tubes, and unused aliquots were stored at −20 °C to avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles that could cause dye degradation. Prior to staining, the stock solution was diluted with serum-free cell culture medium, HBSS, or PBS to prepare a 10 μM working solution. Adherent cell staining: The culture medium was removed from the culture vessel, and cells were washed twice with pre-warmed PBS to remove residual serum. An appropriate volume of DiR working solution was added to completely cover the cell layer, and the cells were incubated in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator for 30 min. The working solution was then discarded, and cells were washed three times with pre-warmed complete medium to remove unbound dye, followed by subsequent experimental procedures or cell harvesting. In vivo cell tracking applications: After washing, the stained cells were resuspended in sterile PBS or physiological saline, adjusted to the required cell concentration, and injected into animal models according to the experimental protocol. Quality control: Staining efficiency was validated using flow cytometry or fluorescence microscopy (740 nm excitation, 780 nm emission filter) to confirm uniform DiR fluorescence distribution on the cell membrane. For in vivo imaging experiments, a small animal in vivo imaging system equipped with matched near-infrared filter sets was used, with exposure times set to 1–5 s and background subtraction algorithms applied to eliminate non-specific signals. The EVs were then centrifuged to remove unbound dye. Mice were injected with DiR-labeled EVs or PBS via tail vein injection. Live imaging was performed 24 h after injection using the Tanon 5200 Automatic Chemiluminescence Imaging Analysis System (Tanon Science & Technology). After euthanasia, the organs were removed for imaging to analyze EV uptake, while tumor tissue was isolated for immunohistochemical staining. GW4869 (2 mg/kg; SelleckChem, S7609, USA), dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), was administered to TAC-operated mice at a dose of 2 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection once every 2 days. EVs as outlined in the study to ensure alignment with the MISEV2023 guidelines.

Hemodynamic examination

Animals were anesthetized using 3% isoflurane and then submitted to an invasive hemodynamic examination (Optic Pressure Sensor; FISO, Canada) to determine the left ventricular systolic pressure (LVESP), left ventricular diastolic pressure (LVEDP), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and the maximum rates of increase and decrease of left ventricular pressure in an isovolumic diastolic period (dP/dtmax and −dP/dtmax, respectively).

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Left ventricular tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and paraffin-embedded. Left ventricular cross-sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (Sigma-Aldrich), Masson’s stain (Sigma-Aldrich), and wheat germ agglutinin (WGA; Invitrogen). H&E staining was used to observe breast cancer cell invasion in isolated mouse tumor tissues and Ki67 (Servicebio, GB111141, 1:500) immunohistochemical staining was used to detect the proliferative ability of breast cancer cells. Images were obtained with an Olympus IX51 microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and quantified using ImageJ.

Blood sample preprocessing

Blood samples were collected by nurses upon patient admission and immediately centrifuged at 3000×g for 30 min to prevent platelet activation and the release of platelet EVs into the sample. Blood plasma samples were stored at −80 °C for subsequent EV purification and characterization.

ELISA

Samples were pre-lysed in RIPA buffer, and S100A7 and S100A8 were subsequently detected using the respective Human ELISA Kits (SEC035Hu and SEB792Hu; Cloud-Clone Corp., Wuhan, China). Prior to the assay, all kit components, test samples, and standards were equilibrated to room temperature for 30 min. The wash buffer working solution was prepared by diluting the concentrated wash buffer with distilled water at a 1:19 ratio. The microplate was removed from the foil pouch, and unused strips were sealed with desiccant and stored appropriately. Each well was washed three times with 300 μL of wash buffer, residual liquid was aspirated completely, and the plate was blotted against lint-free absorbent paper. Subsequently, 100 μL of serially diluted standards or pre-treated test samples were added to each well (all samples and standards were run in duplicate, with a blank zero-standard well included). The plate was sealed with adhesive film and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Following incubation, the liquid was discarded, and the three-time wash procedure was repeated as described above. Then, 100 μL of detection antibody at working concentration was added to each well, the plate was resealed, and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After another wash step, 200 μL of TMB substrate solution was added to each well, the plate was sealed with a new adhesive film, and incubated for 20 min at room temperature protected from light. Finally, 50 μL of stop solution was added to each well, the plate was gently tapped to ensure uniform mixing if color change was uneven, and the optical density (OD) of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader within 20 min of adding the stop solution. For data analysis, the average OD value of the blank well was subtracted from all readings. A standard curve was constructed by plotting the mean OD of standards against their corresponding concentrations (four-parameter logistic fitting was used as recommended for Cloud-Clone ELISA kits), and the target protein concentrations of unknown samples were extrapolated from the standard curve. Samples with readings exceeding the upper limit of the standard curve were appropriately diluted and re-assayed. The intra-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) were <10%.

Cell lines, cell culture, and transfection

The human MDA-MB-231 and BT549 breast cancer cell lines, the human cardiomyocyte AC16 cell line, hiPSC-cardiomyocytes, and human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells were purchased from ATCC and were cultured in-house. MDA-MB-231, BT549, and HEK-293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, Australia) while AC16 cells and hiPSC-cardiomyocytes were cultured in DMEM/F-12 (Gibco). Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; BI, Israel) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Solarbio, China). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2.

MiR-362-5p mimics and inhibitors were synthesized at GenePharma Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) ([all 5′–3′]; miR-362-5p mimic sense: AAUCCUUGGAACCUAGGUGUGAGU UCACACCUAGGUUCCAAGGAUUUU; miR-362-5p inhibitor: ACUCACACCUAGGUUCCAAGGAUU). Cells were transfected using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (13778150, Invitrogen). Prior to transfection, cells were seeded in 500 µL of antibiotic-free growth medium one day in advance to ensure 30–50% confluency at the time of transfection for standard forward transfection. For each well of a 24-well plate, 6 pmol of siRNA or Stealth™ RNAi duplex was first diluted with 50 µL of serum-free Opti-MEM I reduced serum medium and mixed gently. The Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent was gently inverted to homogenize prior to use, and 1 µL of the reagent was diluted with an additional 50 µL of Opti-MEM I medium and mixed gently. The diluted RNAi duplex and diluted Lipofectamine RNAiMAX were then combined, mixed gently, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min to allow complex formation. The 100 µL RNAi-reagent complex was subsequently added to each well containing pre-seeded cells to achieve a final volume of 600 µL and a final RNA concentration of 10 nM, and the plate was gently rocked back and forth to ensure uniform distribution of the complex. The cells were incubated in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator for 48 h prior to gene knockdown detection, with the option to change the culture medium 6 h after transfection if needed.

EV purification and characterization

Total EVs were isolated using total EVs Isolation Reagent (Invitrogen), which was added to the supernatant at half volume, and the mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 1 h, and the precipitate (isolated EVs) was dissolved in PBS at 10 μg/mL for in vitro experiments. The size, morphology, and number of EVs were determined using a NanoSight nanoparticle analyzer (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK) and transmission electron microscopy (FEI, Tecnai G2 Spirit BioTwin, USA). The expression of the EV marker proteins CD63 and TSG101 was analyzed by Western blot.

The transfer of PKH67-labeled EVs

For PKH67 labeling, EVs derived from AC16 cells were incubated with diluent C and PKH67 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min at room temperature. The reaction was terminated with serum, and the EVs were washed three times with PBS to remove excess dye. MDA-MB-231 and BT549 cells were incubated with PKH67-labeled EVs for 6 h, washed three times with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with Phalloidin-iFluor 555 (Abcam, ab176756) for 60 min, washed with PBS, and counterstained with DAPI. Finally, images were acquired using an Olympus IX51 microscope (Olympus Corporation).

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from whole cells with RIPA lysis buffer. Equal amounts of protein (10 μg) were separated by 8–12% SDS–PAGE and electrically transferred to a 0.22-μm PVDF membrane (Millipore). The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 30 min, incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies targeting ANP (Proteintech, 27426-1-AP, 1:1000), BNP (ABclonal, A2179, 1:500), MYH7 (Proteintech, 22280-1-AP, 1:1000), GAPDH (Bioworld, AP0063, 1:2000), CD63 (Abcam, ab59479, 1:1000), TSG101 (Abcam, ab83, 1:500), CD9 (Proteintech, 20597-1-AP, 1:1000), Calnexin(Proteintech, 10427-2-AP, 1:2000), S100A7 (Invitrogen, PA5-104042, 1:1000), S100A8 (Abmart, T55346, 1:1000), and CYLD (Proteintech, 66858-1-Ig,1:2000), and then with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Images were captured using the Tanon 5200 Chemiluminescent Imaging System.

RNA preparation and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TransZol Up (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) and the RNA concentration was determined by NanoDrop (Allsheng Instruments Co., Ltd). Reverse transcription was performed using EasyScript All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (TransGen Biotech). MiRNAs were extracted from EVs with the EasyPure miRNA Kit (TransGen Biotech). The primers used for miRNA reverse transcription and qPCR were designed and synthesized at GenePharma Co., Ltd. qPCR was performed with TransStart Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech). SnRNA U6 and β-actin were used for normalization of miRNA and mRNA expression levels, respectively. Relative gene expression levels were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method. The sequences of the primers used for PCR were as follows: S100A7-F: ACGTGATGACAAGATTGACAAGC, S100A7-R: GCGAGGTAATTTGTGCCCTTT; S100A8-F: ATGCCGTCTACAGGGATGAC, S100A8-R: ACTGAGGACACTCGGTCTCTA; CYLD-F: TCAGGCTTATGGAGCCAAGAA, CYLD-R: ACTTCCCTTCGGTACTTTAAGGA. The sequence of the primer used to amplify hsa-miR-362-5p was AATCCTTGGAACCTAGGTGTGAGT.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (2 × 103 cells/well) in three repeats. After incubation for the indicated time, 100 µL of a 10% CCK-8 solution was added per well, followed by incubation for 2 h under cell culture conditions. Finally, the OD450 of each well was determined.

Cell invasion and migration assay

For the cell invasion assay, Transwell inserts with 8-μm pore-size membranes (Corning, 3422, USA) were precoated with Matrigel (Corning, 356231) and placed in a 24-well plate (Corning, 3524). A total of 2 × 104 cells were seeded in the upper chamber of each insert in serum-free medium, while the lower chamber was filled with medium containing 20% serum. The cells were then cultured for 36–48 h at 37°C with 5% CO2. Subsequently, the cells in the lower chamber (invading cells) were fixed in tissue fixative, stained overnight, and counted and imaged under an Olympus IX51 microscope. Invading cells were counted in at least five random fields per well.

For the cell migration assay, cells were cultured to 95% confluence, scratched using 200-µL pipette tips, washed with PBS, and cultured in fresh DMEM containing 0.1% fetal bovine serum. Wound healing (cell migration) was assessed at 0, 24, and 48 h. Six fields of view were randomly selected for statistical analysis using ImageJ, and the motility of each cell was separately calculated.

Immunofluorescence assay

AC16 cells, hiPSC-cardiomyocytes, MDA-MB-231 cells, and BT549 cells cultured on climbing films in 24-well plates were washed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, P1110) for 20 min at room temperature, and then incubated with primary antibodies against phalloidin (Abcam, ab176756, 1:1000) diluted in culture medium containing 1% BSA at 4 °C overnight or at room temperature for 50 min. After washing with PBS, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, A-11008, 1:200) or Alexa Fluor 555 secondary antibody (Invitrogen, A-31570, 1:200) at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark, washed again, and counterstained with DAPI (Yeasen, 36308ES11, 1:500) for 5 min. Finally, the cells were observed under an Olympus fluorescence microscope.

EV whole-transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from isolated EVs using the exoRNeasy Maxi Kit (#77164, Qiagen). RNA quality was assessed using Agilent 4200 TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) following standard procedures. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the QIAseq miRNA Library Kit for Illumina (#331505, Qiagen). MiRNAs from EVs were prepared. Prior to initiating the protocol, all kit components were equilibrated to room temperature, and RNase-free pipette tips, plates, and consumables were used throughout the entire procedure to prevent RNA degradation. 3’ Adapter Ligation: The 3’ adapter mix was added to each RNA sample and incubated at 25 °C for 1 h to allow the pre-adenylated 3’ adapter to specifically ligate to the 3’ hydroxyl termini of mature miRNAs and small RNAs. Subsequently, the 5’ adapter mix was added, and the reaction was incubated at 25 °C for 1 h to complete 5’ adapter ligation. Reverse transcription: The reverse transcription primer mix, reaction buffer containing unique molecular identifier (UMI), and reverse transcriptase were added to the ligation products. The mixture was incubated at 42 °C for 1 h to synthesize first-strand cDNA (with UMI incorporation occurring during this reverse transcription stage), followed by heat inactivation of the reverse transcriptase at 70 °C for 15 min. CDNA purification: The synthesized cDNA was purified using the provided magnetic beads: 1.8× volume of bead suspension was added to the cDNA reaction mixture and mixed thoroughly, then incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The plate was placed on a magnetic stand, and the supernatant was discarded once it became clear. The beads were washed twice with freshly prepared 80% ethanol and air-dried for 5 min. The purified cDNA was eluted with nuclease-free water. Library Amplification: PCR master mix, Illumina-compatible index primer mix, and DNA polymerase were added to the purified cDNA. Thermal cycling was performed as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min; followed by 12–18 cycles (optimized based on RNA input: 18 cycles for 1 ng input, 12 cycles for 100 ng input) of 98 °C for 20 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min; with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplified library purification: 1.2× volume of magnetic beads was added to the PCR product, and the bead binding, washing, and elution steps were repeated to remove residual primers, adapter dimers, and short non-specific amplification products. The final library was eluted in 20 μL of elution buffer. Quality Control: The expected fragment size distribution of the library (∼140–160 bp for miRNA libraries) and absence of adapter dimer contamination were confirmed using methods such as Agilent TapeStation High Sensitivity D1000 assay. Library concentration was quantified using fluorometric methods (e.g., Qubit) or qPCR. Library Normalization and Sequencing: Libraries were normalized to 4 nM, indexed libraries were pooled proportionally, and sequencing was performed on an Illumina sequencing platform according to standard Illumina sequencing protocols. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and analyzed at Shanghai Biochip Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Raw data were processed with Cutadapt, and the clean reads were aligned to reference sequences in miRBase22.1, piRNABank, and RfamV14.1 using bowtie-1.3.0. The Unique Molecular Identifier (UMI) counts of small RNAs were calculated using a Perl script. The relative expression levels of small RNAs were normalized to CPM-mapped reads (descriptive indicators of gene expression levels). The target mRNAs of differentially abundant miRNAs were obtained from miRTarBase. The transcriptomics data have been deposited in the GEO database (accession numbers: GSE266430 and GSE266431).

EV proteomic analysis

Proteomics analysis was performed by PTM Biolabs, Inc. Frozen samples were mixed with four times the volume of lysis buffer (1× PBS, 1% protease inhibitor) and lysed by sonication. Following centrifugation at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove debris, the supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube, and the protein concentration was measured using a BCA assay kit. For digestion, trypsin was added at a trypsin-to-protein mass ratio of 1:50 for overnight digestion, followed by a second digestion at a 1:100 ratio for 4 h. HPLC fractionation was not performed. The peptide segments were dissolved in mobile phase A and separated using a NanoElute ultra-high performance liquid chromatography system. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid, 2% acetonitrile in water, and mobile phase B was an acetonitrile/water solution containing 0.1% formic acid. The liquid phase gradient elution program was as follows: 0–14 min, 6–24% B; 14–16 min, 24–35% B; 16–18 min, 35–80% B; 18–20 min, 80% B. The flow rate was maintained at 500 nL/min. After separation, the peptides were injected into a capillary ion source for ionization, followed by timsTOF Pro mass spectrometry for the detection and analysis of peptide parent ions and their secondary fragments. The ion source voltage was set to 1.75 kV. Data were collected in data-independent acquisition parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (diaPASEF) mode, with the primary mass spectrometry scanning range set to 300–1500m/z. Each primary mass spectrometry acquisition was followed by 20 PASEF acquisitions, with the secondary mass spectrometry scanning performed in the 400–850m/z range using 7m/z windows.

The raw mass spectrometry data were imported into the search software, and the analysis parameters were configured according to the experimental design. The data acquired in DIA mode in this experiment were processed using the DIA-NN (v.1.8) search engine with default parameters. The search was conducted against the Homo sapiens protein database (Homousapiens_9606_SP_20231220.fasta, containing 20,429 sequences). Trypsin/P was selected as the digestion enzyme, and the maximum number of missed cleavages was set to 1. The fixed modifications included N-terminal methionine exclusion and carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues. A theoretical spectral library was constructed using deep learning algorithms, and an anti-library was added to calculate the false discovery rate (FDR) due to random matching. The FDR for precursor identification was set to 1%. The proteomics data for our extracellular vesicles have been deposited in the PRIDE database (accession number: PXD062164).

Analysis of mRNA-seq data

For each sample, mRNA-seq clean reads were mapped to the GRCh38.104/GRCm39.104/Rnor_6.0 reference genome using HISAT2 v.2.2.0. Sequencing read counts were calculated using StringTie. The mRNA expression levels in the different samples were normalized by the Trimmed Mean of M-values (TMM) method and then converted to fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped fragments (FPKM). The edgeR package of R was used to analyze the intergroup differences in gene expression. Heatmaps of specific genes were generated using the pheatmap package of R. Principal component analysis (PCA) plots were generated using the scatterplot3d package in R.

Statistics and reproducibility

All values are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM). Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. When comparing two groups with a normal distribution, a standard Student’s t-test was used; otherwise, the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test was applied. For the analysis of data from more than two groups, one-way ANOVA was employed, followed by the Bonferroni post hoc test when the assumptions satisfied a normal distribution. Otherwise, the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test was used, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test to correct for multiple comparisons. Multivariate logistic regression was employed to evaluate risk factors for cardiac hypertrophy. ROC curves were used to assess the diagnostic potential of S100A7, S100A8, and miR-362-5p for cardiac hypertrophy. Correlations between two continuous variables were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation, while Kendall’s tau-b correlation was used for categorical variables. Full details of the methods used for each analysis are described in the figure legends. In our study, all data replicates represented biological replicates from independent samples. Technical replicates were employed solely as a final confirmatory measure to validate the data obtained from these biologically independent replicates. All statistical graphs were generated from data produced by independent replicates. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 and SPSS. p-values < 0.05 were considered significant.
