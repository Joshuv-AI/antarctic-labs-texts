FastReChain: Highly Responsive and Low-Overhead Centralized Route Scheduling in Clos Datacenter Networks
Zihan Zhu, Dongchao Wu, Zhanbang Zhang, Jian Yang

Abstract
Ever since Clos topologies were used in datacenter networks (DCNs), a practical centralized scheduling algorithm that supports dynamic scheduling has been absent. The introduction of optical switches in DCNs as a future-proof solution exacerbates this problem due to several properties of optical switches, such as the fact that they are generally bufferless and therefore rely on centralized scheduling, and that they have long switching times and therefore require the number of rearrangements to be minimized. In this paper, we propose a centralized scheduling algorithm that achieves theoretical maximum throughput even in one-rate bidirectional Clos networks, while producing schemes with near-minimal numbers of rearrangements. It is the only algorithm that directly supports bidirectional Clos networks and has a time efficiency high enough to support dynamic scheduling to date. For static minimal rewiring, its running time ranges from a fraction to a few hundredths of other algorithms, and the number of rearrangements has also been steadily improved, allowing for more frequent adjustments and less impact on ongoing communications. In addition, the algorithm is very flexible and can support various functional requirements in real-world environments. We achieve this result through the replacement chain concept and bitset optimization.

According to different division principles, Clos network scheduling algorithms can be divided into centralized and distributed algorithms, and can also be divided into dynamic algorithms and static algorithms

4

Centralized scheduling algorithms plan the routing scheme in all switches from a global perspective. Static scheduling algorithms generate the corresponding routing scheme based on traffic data within a period of time, while dynamic scheduling algorithms dynamically adjust the routing scheme based on real-time traffic data. Compared to centralized scheduling algorithms, distributed scheduling algorithms do not have centralized control over the network, and therefore cannot fully utilize the network’s link bandwidth resources. Moreover, distributed scheduling algorithms require multiple iterations to converge. On the other hand, centralized scheduling algorithms have the potential to fully utilize link bandwidth resources, and rescheduling can be completed instantly as long as the routing scheme is calculated fast enough. However, centralized scheduling algorithms involve the rearrangement of existing connections, which is rather complex. The time complexity of existing centralized scheduling algorithms (such as algorithms based on matrix decomposition

In contrast, previous works did not consider the number of rearrangements, but focused only on obtaining a feasible routing scheme. Existing minimal rewiring algorithms, as mentioned above, cannot support dynamic scheduling, which is more in line with real-world situations. In this paper, we propose a centralized scheduling algorithm for Clos networks that has the following advantages:

•

Achieves a time complexity in practice far less than linear with the number of network ports and real-time performance in actual network scale through the replacement chain concept and bitset optimization;

•

Maximizes network connectivity (i.e. achieves theoretical maximum throughput) in proportional (see Definition

•

The algorithm itself is very flexible, working on a first-come, first-served basis, and various details of the implementation are adjustable to support diverse functional requirements in real-world environments.

2
Motivation

Existing work uses static scheduling solutions, but this method can only generate routing schemes once a period of time. This failure to respond to traffic changes in real time may cause some links to be congested while other links are idle, resulting in unbalanced resource allocation, especially for burst traffic. It also increases end-to-end latency and therefore significantly affects real-time applications (such as video conferencing and financial transactions). This time interval may also cause the routing scheme to lose timeliness, that is, when the routing solution is generated, the current traffic pattern has changed and is no longer compatible with the routing scheme. The optics community has developed many technologies with milliseconds to nanoseconds of switching time
[
30
]
. If the algorithm can achieve such real-time performance, then we can make full use of the performance of current optical technologies and make it as close to the timeliness of electrical switches as possible. Therefore, dynamic centralized scheduling algorithms are extremely important for the application of optical switches in DCNs.

4.2
Forming a Replacement Chain

We pick a pair of low-level switches
L_{j_{0}}
and
L_{k_{0}}
such that
E(X)_{j_{0},k_{0}}<D_{j_{0},k_{0}}
, and consider how to schedule a connection between
L_{j_{0}}
and
L_{k_{0}}
in scheme
X
. If the links between
L_{j_{0}}
and all top-level switches or the links between
L_{k_{0}}
and all top-level switches are not available, then the connection cannot be added without reducing the number of non-redundant connections. Otherwise, we choose a top-level switch
T_{i_{0}}
and try to add
(T_{i_{0}},L_{j_{0}},L_{k_{0}})
. If the links
(T_{i_{0}},L_{j_{0}})
and
(T_{i_{0}},L_{k_{0}})
are all available, we can comfortably add the connection without reducing the number of non-redundant connections. If one of the links, say
(T_{i_{0}},L_{j_{0}})
is available, and
(T_{i_{0}},L_{k_{0}})
is not, then we have to permute some connection
(T_{i_{0}},L_{j_{1}},L_{k_{0}})
to make
(T_{i_{0}},L_{k_{0}})
available. The problem then reduces to adding back a connection between
L_{j_{1}}
and
L_{k_{0}}
, which is exactly of the same form and can therefore be solved recursively. If both links are not available, then we have to permute two connections and reduce to adding two connections, which is too complex and, therefore, not adopted in our algorithm. The recursion ends when both links are available. We can see that the resulting modification scheme naturally forms a replacement chain. Note that if we need to use an implicitly available link at any point in the process, we need to remove a corresponding redundant connection at the same time.

4.3
Existence of Replacement Chains

Theorem 1
.

In two-level proportional one-rate bidirectional Clos networks, for scheduling a single connection between
L_{j_{0}}
and
L_{k_{0}}
, a valid modification scheme can be found by finding replacement chains, i.e. there exists at least one valid modification scheme that is in the form of a replacement chain and some removal of redundant connections, if and only if there exists at least one link connected to
L_{j_{0}}
and at least one link connected to
L_{k_{0}}
that are available.

Proof.

If all links connected to
L_{j_{0}}
or all links connected to
L_{k_{0}}
are not available, then apparently there exist no valid schemes. Otherwise, we need to transform the network into a three-stage proportional one-rate symmetric Clos network. We can achieve this by constructing a bipartite graph. For each link
(T_{i},L_{j})
, we create
C_{i,j}
vertices, namely
V_{i,j,0},V_{i,j,1},\ldots,V_{i,j,C_{i,j}-1}
. Add two types of edges to the graph: 1) Add edges
(V_{i,j,2t},V_{i,j,2t+1})
for
0\leq t<\frac{C_{i,j}}{2}
; 2) For each connection
(T_{i},L_{j},L_{k})
, allocate one vertex from the vertices of
(T_{i},L_{j})
and one vertex from the vertices of
(T_{i},L_{k})
(each vertex can be allocated at most once), and connect them with an edge. The graph naturally forms a bipartite graph because every vertex is connected to exactly one edge of type 1 and at most one edge of type 2, so that each cycle in the graph alternates between the two types of edges. We then color the bipartite graph with black and white so that every pair of vertices connected by an edge has different colors. We direct all the connections corresponding to type 2 edges from black to white, and divide each link into an up-link and a down-link, where the black vertices represent the up-links and the white vertices represent the down-links. Therefore, each low-level switch is divided into an input switch and an output switch, containing up-links and down-links respectively. Figure
3
shows an example of bipartite coloring. Note that we need to ensure that there is at least one pair of available links connected to different types of switches divided from
L_{j_{0}}
and
L_{k_{0}}
, respectively. This can always be achieved by carefully choosing the color when coloring the graph.

5
The FastReChain Algorithm

5.1
The Algorithm

Replacement chain search algorithm:
We can perform a depth-first search (DFS) to find replacement chains. At each node of the search tree, our goal is to add a connection between
L_{j}
and
L_{k}
. Therefore, we first need to enumerate the top-level switches
T_{i}
through which the connection will pass. In
T_{i}
, if the connection can be added directly, the search ends. Otherwise, one of the links from
L_{j}
and
L_{k}
to
T_{i}
needs to be available. Assuming
(T_{i},L_{j})
is available, we need to replace a connection to
L_{k}
to make
(T_{i},L_{k})
available. Therefore, we need to enumerate
j^{\prime}
, satisfying
X_{i,j^{\prime},k}>0
, then replace
(T_{i},L_{j^{\prime}},L_{k})
, and proceed to the next level of search. To find the shortest replacement chain, we can use iterative deepening search (IDS), that is, repeatedly run a depth-limited version of DFS with increasing depth limits. We can see that at each node of the search tree, two enumerations are required, namely: 1) enumerate the top-level switches
T_{i}
through which the connection between
L_{j}
and
L_{k}
passes; 2) enumerate the lower-level switches
L_{j^{\prime}}
to which the replaced connection
(T_{i},L_{j^{\prime}},L_{k})
is connected (assuming that
(T_{i},L_{j})
is available and we need to make
(T_{i},L_{k})
available). In order to ensure the stability of the algorithm performance and the load balancing of the switches, we need to perform these two enumerations in random orders. For detailed pseudocode, see Appendix
B
.

However, this algorithm does not seem to work in practice. The depth of the search, which is also the length of the replacement chain, can be as large as the number of low-level switches. The search breadth, which is equivalent to the number of selectable switches (i.e. switches that can be selected to allow the search to proceed) at each node of the search tree can also be in the order of hundreds. This yields something similar to
100^{100}
computations per scheduled connection. How could this algorithm possibly work in practice?

Key observation:
When the network scale is small, the shortest replacement chain could be long, but the search breadth would be small since the network scale is small. When the network size is large, the shortest replacement chain is likely to be very short because of the large number of possible replacement chains. We also need to take network load into account: When the network load is low, meaning that many or all of the links are available, it is highly possible that there exist replacement chains of very short length. When the network load is high, the number of selectable top-level switches and replaceable connections is extremely limited. Therefore, the average time complexity is much better than it seems. In our experiment, even when the network load is 100% (i.e. all the links are fully used), in a uniform symmetric Clos network with 155 input/output switches, 128 intermediate switches, and link capacities equal to 4 under the traffic traces from Facebook datacenters, the lengths of the shortest replacement chains did not exceed 6. However, the algorithm still runs pretty slow, especially when the network load is high. Observing and speculating on the algorithm execution process, we found that the bottleneck of the algorithm lies in the enumeration of the switches. When the network load is high, there are very few selectable switches, but we still need to enumerate each switch equally. Especially in the last layer of search, it is common that only one or two switches can complete the construction of the replacement chain, but we need to enumerate all switches, while the search tree is fully expanded exactly at the last layer. Therefore, we need to find a way to filter out selectable switches.

Bitset optimization:
We can use bitset to perform this operation. Bitset supports bitwise operations on 64 bits at a time, or more bits at a time under Single Instruction Multiple Data (SIMD) instructions. Bitset also supports fast iteration through its 1s, with a time complexity associated with division by 64. This is a perfect match for our needs since our network size is several hundred switches. 5.2
Limit to Two Top-Level Switches

To illustrate the low average time complexity of our algorithm, we will show that the time complexity of the algorithm can be linearized when the search is restricted to replacing between two top-level switches and that it imposes a rough upper bound on the length of the shortest replacement chain.

In symmetric Clos networks, the replacement chain limited to two intermediate switches is in the following form:

\begin{gathered}\mathrm{Remove}(A,j_{0},k_{1}),\mathrm{Add}(A,j_{0},k_{0}),\\
\mathrm{Remove}(B,j_{1},k_{1}),\mathrm{Add}(B,j_{0},k_{1}),\\
\mathrm{Remove}(A,j_{1},k_{2}),\mathrm{Add}(A,j_{1},k_{1}),\\
\vdots\\
\end{gathered}

where
\mathrm{Remove}(A/B,j,k)
represents the removal of a connection between
U_{j}
and
V_{k}
that passes through
A/B
, and
\mathrm{Add}(A/B,j,k)
represents the addition of a connection between
U_{j}
and
V_{k}
that passes through
A/B
. It seems that the essence of the replacement chain at this point is a series of existing connections:
(A,U_{j_{0}},V_{k_{1}}),(B,U_{j_{1}},V_{k_{1}}),(A,U_{j_{1}},V_{k_{2}}),(B,U_{j_{2}},V_{k_{2}}),\ldots
, and the construction of the replacement chain is completed when the replacement chain extends to an available link. A valid replacement chain can always be found by extending on the series of existing connections because, if we transform the network into a classical symmetric Clos network as described in Section
4.3
, we can see that in the extension process of the replacement chain, it will never visit a visited link; otherwise, the link would have more than one connection, contradicting the fact that the link capacity is equal to 1. We can draw the connections in
A
and
B
in the same graph, with the connections from
A
as solid lines and the connections from
B
as dashed lines. Then, finding a replacement chain is equivalent to alternating between solid and dashed lines until we reach an available link, as shown in Figure
4
. Therefore, we can perform a breadth-first search (BFS) on this graph. Since each link can be visited at most once, the time complexity is linear in the number of input/output switches. Note that since the link capacity is greater than 1 before transforming the network into a classical one, a link can be visited more than once, but it will not contribute to a shorter replacement chain, because BFS always finds the shortest path to the available links.

5.3
Algorithm Adaptiveness and Flexibility

The algorithm itself is very flexible and can support functional requirements in actual scenarios that other algorithms cannot support. For example, to prevent a single search from taking longer than the average due to excessive search breadth and depth, we can gradually limit the search breadth as the depth limit grows. Specifically, given the maximum number of computations
\mathrm{maxComp}
to achieve and the current depth limit
d
, we limit the breadth at each node of the search tree to:

\mathrm{maxBreadth}=\mathrm{maxComp}^{\frac{1}{d}}

(3)

so that each iteration of depth deepening has approximately same amount of computation equal to
\mathrm{maxComp}
. When the number of tried selectable switches reaches the breadth limit, the algorithm stops expanding on more selectable switches. This operation actually makes the algorithm adaptively trade off running time and the number of rearrangements and adds a layer of protection for the stability of algorithm performance.

Our algorithm also works on a first-come, first-served basis, which means that it naturally supports some priority-related requirements. For example, in heterogeneous networks, it is difficult to determine whether a demand is feasible, so connections that are more important can be scheduled first, so that connections with higher priorities are established with greater probability.

5.4
Refined Version for Static Minimal Rewiring

The original algorithm always finds the shortest possible replacement chain for scheduling a single connection. In static scheduling which involves scheduling multiple connections, we can further improve the algorithm. We consider that there may be some operations in the later scheduling of connections that cancel each other out with some operations in the previous scheduling of connections. Therefore, we can search for multiple replacement chains and select the one with the smallest number of delta rearrangements. This idea is somewhat similar to the Successive Shortest Path algorithm in the minimum-cost flow problem if you consider the replacement chain as a special kind of augmenting path. In our experiments, however, we found that the refined version has some relatively small improvements in the number of rearrangements, but has a larger negative impact on the constant factor of the time complexity. Since this is essentially a trade-off between running time and the number of rearrangements, we leave the choice between these two variants (more specifically, the choice of the number of replacement chains to search) to the actual network designer.

Static Minimal Rewiring Evaluation

6.1
Network Structure Adaptation

There exist multiple static scheduling algorithms for asymmetric Clos networks. However, most of them are based on bipartite graph structure (including the minimal rewiring algorithms based on minimum-cost flow (MCF)) and therefore cannot be applied directly to bidirectional Clos networks. For these algorithms, we can only convert bidirectional Clos networks to symmetric Clos networks and then apply them. Note that this will result in large losses and will not fully utilize the bidirectional Clos networks.

We assume that the bidirectional Clos networks we use have even link capacities. To convert bidirectional network structures and routing schemes to symmetric ones, we use the bipartite coloring method mentioned in Section
4.3
. We also need an algorithm to convert the demands in bidirectional networks to those in symmetric networks. Let
D
be the demand matrix in a bidirectional network and
D^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times m}_{\geq 0}
be the converted demand matrix. Let
C
be the link capacity matrix in the bidirectional network. According to the previous analysis, we know that
D
is feasible if and only if it satisfies the following condition:

\forall j,\sum_{k}D_{j,k}\leq\sum_{i}C_{i,j}

(4)

We also know that
D^{\prime}
is legal and feasible if and only if it satisfies the following conditions:

\displaystyle\forall j,k,D^{\prime}_{j,k}+D^{\prime}_{k,j}=D_{j,k}

(5)

\displaystyle\forall j,\sum_{k}D^{\prime}_{j,k}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}C_{i,j}

(6)

\displaystyle\forall k,\sum_{j}D^{\prime}_{j,k}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}C_{i,k}

(7)

We can tighten the constraints so that
D^{\prime}
satisfies the following conditions:

\displaystyle\forall j,\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}D_{j,k}\right\rfloor\leq\sum_{k}D^{\prime}_{j,k}\leq\left\lceil\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}D_{j,k}\right\rceil

(8)

\displaystyle\forall k,\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}D_{j,k}\right\rfloor\leq\sum_{j}D^{\prime}_{j,k}\leq\left\lceil\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}D_{j,k}\right\rceil

(9)

We now show an algorithm that consistently achieves the above constraints. Let
G_{j,k}
be the remainder of
D_{j,k}
divided by
2
. We can see that
G
can be seen as the adjacent matrix of a simple undirected graph consisting of
m
vertices. First, we repeat the following process: select a vertex with an odd degree in the graph, and arbitrarily select a trail (i.e. a path with repeated nodes but not repeated edges) starting from this vertex until we reach another vertex with an odd degree for the first time. Before reaching another vertex with an odd degree, there always exist unvisited edges to go through, since the vertex degrees are even. Then, orient the edges on the trail in the direction of the traversal and delete them from the undirected graph. It can be seen that in a trail, there is exactly one edge connected to the starting vertex and one edge connected to the ending vertex that are oriented, and for other vertices, there are exactly two edges connected to them that are oriented in opposite directions. Since there must be an even number of vertices with odd degrees in an undirected graph, the above process will turn all vertices with odd degrees into vertices with even degrees. Second, we repeat the same process, but starting from and ending at the same even-degree vertex. After these processes, all edges connected to each vertex in the graph are oriented in either direction, and the difference between the number of edges in the two directions does not exceed 1. Let
G^{\prime}
be the adjacency matrix of the directed graph after all edges are directed, then we let

D^{\prime}_{j,k}=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}D_{j,k}\right\rfloor+G^{\prime}_{j,k}

(10)

It can be found that
D^{\prime}
satisfies the constraints imposed above. For a detailed proof, see Appendix
A
.

Evaluation Setup

We will evaluate the algorithms in both bidirectional and symmetric Clos networks. Therefore, we first need to redefine some notations in the context of symmetric Clos networks. A symmetric Clos network contains
m

m

n
intermediate switches.
D_{j,k}
represents the number of connections required between the
j
-th input switch and the
k
-th output switch.
X_{i,j,k}
represents the number of connections between the
j
-th input switch and the
k
-th output switch that pass through the
i
-th intermediate switch in scheme
X

C_{i,j}
represents the link capacity between the
i
-th intermediate switch and the
j
-th input switch as well as between the
i
-th intermediate switch and the
j

Simulation scenario.
Our simulation considers a DCN where the Top-of-Rack (ToR) switches are interconnected through a layer of spine switches that have uniform links to the ToR switches. The ToR switches correspond to the low-level switches in bidirectional Clos networks or input/output switches in symmetric Clos networks, and the spine switches correspond to the top-level switches in bidirectional Clos networks or intermediate switches in symmetric Clos networks. We assume continuous monitoring of the traffic between ToR switches. Our objective is to generate the demand matrix at a fixed time interval and reconfigure the spine switches accordingly to enable the network configuration to adapt to changes in the traffic pattern.

Task modes.
In each test case, there will be a series of demand phases arranged on a timeline. We will evaluate the algorithms in two task modes. The first is the continuous task mode, in which the original routing scheme is the scheme generated by the algorithm itself last demand phase. For the initial demand phase, the original routing scheme is set to empty (i.e. no connections are established). The second is the discontinuous task mode, in which the original routing scheme is randomly constructed based on last demand. The reason for separating the task modes is that some of the algorithms have the ability to exploit their own scheme structures, so we need to test their performance in different scenarios.

Result measurement.
We measure the quality of our schemes in terms of

(RR). There are
T

Dynamic Scheduling Evaluation

Note that our algorithm is the only centralized scheduling algorithm that supports dynamic scheduling to date, so there are no other baseline algorithms. We evaluated dynamic scheduling only in bidirectional Clos networks.

7.1
Evaluation Setup

Simulation scenario.
The simulation scenario and data source are similar to in Section
6.3
. The difference is that in dynamic scheduling, the demand needs to change dynamically according to changes in network traffic. We define the following two types of atomic demand changes:

•

\textrm{Add}(j,k)
: Add a connection between
L_{j}
and
L_{k}
in the demand, i.e.
D_{j,k}\leftarrow D_{j,k}+1,D_{k,j}\leftarrow D_{k,j}+1
.

•

\textrm{Remove}(j,k)
: Remove a connection between
L_{j}
and
L_{k}
in the demand, i.e.
D_{j,k}\leftarrow D_{j,k}-1,D_{k,j}\leftarrow D_{k,j}-1
.

The scheduling algorithm needs to generate corresponding routing scheme modifications based on each atomic demand change.

Data source.
We still use the traces from Facebook datacenters in 2024, the same as in Section
6.3
. We need an algorithm different from that in Section
6.3
that can generate atomic demand changes dynamically. We still have a traffic matrix
\mathrm{Tr}
aggregated in a time period, which is dynamically updated every second. We assign a priority weight of
\left(\max\left\{\mathrm{Tr}_{j,k},\mathrm{Tr}_{k,j}\right\}+1\right)/r
to the
r
-th connection between
L_{j}
and
L_{k}
in the demand. Each time a new packet between
L_{j}
and
L_{k}
arrives and is added to
\mathrm{Tr}
, we try to add as many connections between
L_{j}
and
L_{k}
to the demand as possible, while removing connections with lower weights that hinder the addition. We choose an aggregation interval length of 600 seconds, and the atomic demand changes are dynamically generated each second.

Parameters.
The parameters are the same as in Section
6.3
, except that the case
l=1.0
is not tested because the algorithm we use to generate demands can only achieve around
l=0.8
in the trace of Facebook cluster A in 2024.

Result measurement.
We measure the performance of our algorithm in terms of running time per operation and number of rearrangements per operation. Running time per operation is the total running time divided by the number of atomic demand changes, in nanoseconds. Number of rearrangements per operation is the total number of rearrangements divided by the number of atomic demand changes.

8
Related Work

In fact, the replacement chain is not a completely new concept. Paull proposed a similar algorithm, but it was limited to replacing between two intermediate switches in traditional Clos networks
[
24
]
. This algorithm has a strict linear time complexity. In this paper, we generalize the algorithm to one-rate Clos networks (in Section
5.2
), and it still has a linear time complexity. We also generalize the algorithm to replacing between multiple intermediate switches to reduce the number of rearrangements, and experimentally show that the efficiency of this algorithm in practice is acceptable, but no strict upper bound on the time complexity is obtained. There have been studies on the upper bound of the number of rearrangements of Paull’s algorithm, such as the work of Satoru Ohta
[
21
,
22
]
and the work of Frank K. Hwang and Wen-Dar Lin
[
18
]
, which may be helpful in proving a strict upper bound on the time complexity.

For the minimum rewiring problem, Google
[
29
,
27
]
and Shuyuan Zhang et al.
[
28
]
have published some algorithms. These algorithms have been briefly described in Section
6.2
. However, the biggest problem with these algorithms is that they all start by modeling the problem as an ILP problem. All subsequent optimizations, including MCF, are only optimizations based on ILP. This idea limits the algorithm to only solving the complete problem, and it is impossible to adjust only the local part of the problem, making it unsuitable for dynamic scheduling. Moreover, this idea does not fully utilize the characteristics of the problem itself. In contrast, our algorithm starts from the actual form of the problem, fully utilizes the characteristics of the problem itself, and divides the solution of the problem into the solution of multiple local atomic problems, making it suitable for dynamic scheduling while also outperforming these algorithms in static scheduling.

9
Conclusion

In this paper, we present a centralized scheduling algorithm that achieves dynamic scheduling for the first time, while outperforming existing algorithms in terms of static minimal rewiring on all metrics. We prove that the algorithm can always achieve the maximum throughput in proportional Clos networks, and demonstrate the efficiency of the algorithm through experiments. With the support of our algorithm, the community can start designing highly responsive optical switching network architectures based on dynamic centralized scheduling, which may bring a promising future for optical networking. Even for static minimal rewiring, our algorithm provides fewer rearrangements and less running time than existing algorithms, thus supporting more frequent network adjustments and less impact on ongoing communications.

The implementation and evaluation code of all the algorithms mentioned above are available in C++ language as open source projects in

. We aim to develop a high-quality open source library of minimal rewiring algorithms for the development of future network architectures.

Proof of the Demand Matrix Conversion Algorithm

Since
G_{j,k}
is the remainder of
C_{j,k}
divided by
2
, it can be represented as

G_{j,k}=D_{j,k}-2\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}D_{j,k}\right\rfloor

(15)

Since all edges connected to each vertex in the graph are oriented in either direction, and the difference between the number of edges in the two directions does not exceed 1, this can be represented as

\displaystyle\forall j,\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}G_{j,k}\right\rfloor\leq\sum_{k}G^{\prime}_{j,k}\leq\left\lceil\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}G_{j,k}\right\rceil

(16)

\displaystyle\forall k,\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}G_{j,k}\right\rfloor\leq\sum_{j}G^{\prime}_{j,k}\leq\left\lceil\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}G_{j,k}\right\rceil

(17)

Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k}D^{\prime}_{j,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}D_{j,k}\right\rfloor+\sum_{k}G^{\prime}_{j,k}

(18)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k}\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}D_{j,k}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}G_{j,k}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}D_{j,k}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}D_{j,k}-\sum_{k}\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}D_{j,k}\right\rfloor\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}D_{j,k}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}D_{j,k}\right\rfloor-\sum_{k}\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}D_{j,k}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}D_{j,k}\right\rfloor

The same goes for other constraints.
