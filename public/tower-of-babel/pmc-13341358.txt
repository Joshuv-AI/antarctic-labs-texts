Association of Physical Activity With All‐Cause and CVD Mortality Across Metabolic and Obesity Phenotypes
Authors: Yunmin Han, Younghwan Choi, Soomin Lee, Yeon Soo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo examine the independent and joint associations of metabolic status, obesity, and physical activity with all‐cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality across metabolic–obesity phenotypes.Materials and MethodsWe analysed 442 666 adults from the Korean National Health Insurance Service health screening cohort (2009–2012). Metabolic status was defined using modified NCEP‐ATP III criteria (elevated triglycerides, low HDL‐C, elevated blood pressure or antihypertensive use, and elevated fasting glucose or antidiabetic use); ≥ 2 abnormalities indicated metabolically unhealthy. Obesity was defined as BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (WHO Asia‐Pacific), yielding four phenotypes: metabolically healthy lean (MHL), metabolically unhealthy lean (MUHL), metabolically healthy obesity (MHO), and metabolically unhealthy obesity (MUHO). Physical activity was assessed by self‐report and categorised by total MET‐min/week (0, 1–499, 500–999, 1000–1499, ≥ 1500). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) for all‐cause and CVD mortality.ResultsIn the primary multivariable model (Model 2a), compared with MHL, MUHL had the highest all‐cause mortality (HR 1.23, 95% CI 1.19–1.26), whereas MUHO was not significantly associated with all‐cause mortality (HR 0.97, 95% CI 0.94–1.01), and MHO showed lower risk (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.81–0.89). Higher physical activity was consistently associated with lower all‐cause and CVD mortality in a dose–response manner, and joint analyses showed progressively lower mortality across all metabolic–obesity phenotypes with increasing activity levels.ConclusionsMetabolic–obesity phenotypes were associated with differential mortality risk, and higher physical activity was associated with lower all‐cause and CVD mortality across phenotypes.

Introduction

Obesity remains a major global public health challenge and is a well‐established risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and premature mortality [1]. However, cardiometabolic risk varies considerably among individuals with obesity, prompting recognition of distinct metabolic–obesity phenotypes, including metabolically healthy obesity (MHO) and metabolically unhealthy obesity (MUHO) [2]. Although MHO has been considered a potentially lower‐risk state, evidence regarding its long‐term CVD and mortality outcomes remains inconsistent and may differ across populations [3].

Physical activity is a key modifiable behaviour that improves insulin sensitivity and cardiometabolic health and is consistently associated with lower risks of all‐cause and CVD mortality, independent of adiposity [4, 5]. Emerging evidence further suggests that higher physical activity may mitigate the adverse prognosis associated with metabolic abnormalities and obesity [6, 7]. However, the extent to which these survival benefits are comparable across metabolic–obesity phenotypes is not well established.

Many prior cohort studies have examined metabolic status, obesity, or physical activity separately, and several have been constrained by limited sample size, heterogeneous phenotype definitions, and relatively short follow‐up [8, 9, 10, 11]. Moreover, evidence from Asian populations remains comparatively limited, despite their distinct body composition and greater metabolic susceptibility at lower body mass index (BMI), alongside a rapidly increasing burden of cardiometabolic disease [12, 13]. Clarifying how physical activity relates to mortality risk across metabolic–obesity phenotypes in this context is therefore important.

Accordingly, using a large nationwide cohort from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS), we examined the independent and joint associations of metabolic status, obesity, and physical activity with all‐cause and CVD mortality, and assessed whether these associations differ across metabolic–obesity phenotypes.

Method

Data Sources and Study Population

This study analysed data from the National Health Insurance Service–National Sample Cohort (NHIS‐NSC), a population‐based cohort established by the Korean National Health Insurance Service. The NHIS‐NSC provides de‐identified nationwide claims and National Health Screening data, including sociodemographic characteristics, ICD‐10‐coded diagnoses, healthcare utilisation and treatment records, prescription information, and health screening results. The National Health Screening dataset includes anthropometric measurements, blood tests, physical examinations, and self‐reported questionnaires on health‐related behaviours, including physical activity [14, 15].

From the NHIS health screening data collected between 2009 and 2012, a total of 484 410 adults were initially included. Among them, individuals were excluded if they were younger than 20 years (n = 1538), had a prior history of CVD (n = 20 784), had missing information on physical activity (n = 5174), or had missing covariates (n = 14 248). After applying these exclusion criteria, 442 666 participants were included in the final analysis. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University (IRB No. E2504/004‐010). This study utilised de‐identified data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS; approval number NHIS‐2025‐07‐2‐028), and the requirement for informed consent was waived in accordance with institutional guidelines. Exposures (Obesity Phenotype)

Metabolic status was defined using a modified ATP III framework. Because obesity status was classified separately using BMI, waist circumference was excluded from the metabolic health definition to avoid conceptual overlap between adiposity and metabolic components. Accordingly, participants with ≥ 2 of the remaining 4 metabolic abnormalities were classified as metabolically unhealthy, consistent with prior Korean population‐based studies [9, 16]. Elevated triglycerides (≥ 150 mg/dL); reduced HDL cholesterol (< 40 mg/dL in men and < 50 mg/dL in women); elevated blood pressure (systolic ≥ 130 mmHg, diastolic ≥ 85 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medication); and elevated fasting glucose based on fasting serum glucose measurements (≥ 100 mg/dL or use of antidiabetic medication), based on the modified ATP III definition, were considered metabolic abnormalities [17, 18]. Participants with ≥ 2 metabolic abnormalities were categorised as metabolically unhealthy, whereas those with < 2 abnormalities were categorised as metabolically healthy.

Obesity was defined as a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 in accordance with Asia‐Pacific guidelines [19]. Based on the combination of metabolic health and obesity status, four phenotypes were constructed: metabolically healthy lean (MHL), metabolically unhealthy lean (MUHL), MHO, and MUHO [7].

Exposures (Physical Activity)

Leisure‐time physical activity was assessed during the national health screening using a standardized, self‐administered questionnaire from the Korean NHIS health screening program. The questionnaire records the frequency (days per week) and duration (minutes per day) of vigorous‐ and moderate‐intensity activity, as well as walking, performed during a typical week based on a 7‐day recall period [20, 21].

Vigorous physical activity (VPA) was defined as exercises causing marked shortness of breath or a rapid heart rate, such as running, high‐speed cycling, or aerobics, performed for at least 20 min per session. Moderate physical activity (MPA) included activities inducing mild shortness of breath, such as brisk walking, cycling at a usual pace, or gardening, performed for at least 30 min per session. Walking was defined as usual‐pace or leisurely walking for at least 10 min at a time. Participants reported the number of days per week they engaged in each activity type.

To quantify total physical activity, each activity intensity was assigned a metabolic equivalent of task (MET) value: 8.0 METs for VPA, 4.0 METs for MPA, and 3.0 METs for walking. For each participant, weekly energy expenditure was calculated by multiplying the assigned MET value by the duration (minutes per session) and frequency (days per week) for each activity intensity and then summing across all activity categories. Total physical activity was expressed as MET‐minutes per week (MET‐min/week).

Participants were classified into five categories by total MET‐min/week (0, 1–499, 500–999, 1000–1499, and ≥ 1500), with cut‐points informed by WHO and ACSM recommendations (≥ 500 MET‐min/week) [22, 23, 24]. Individuals with < 500 MET‐min/week were considered to have insufficient PA, whereas those with ≥ 500 MET‐min/week were categorised as meeting physical activity recommendations.

Outcomes

The primary endpoints for this study were all‐cause and CVD mortality. We ascertained the mortality status of participants by using data from the Korean‐NHIS database, which was linked to the National Death Registry. All‐cause mortality was defined as a death from any reason, while CVD mortality was identified when the underlying cause of death was assigned an International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) code between I00 and I99. Participants were followed until their date of death, the date of loss to follow‐up, or December 31, 2019, whichever occurred first.

Covariates

Baseline covariates were selected based on their established associations with both physical activity and mortality outcomes. The following variables were included in the multivariable models: age (continuous), sex (men or women), smoking status (current smoker or non‐smoker), alcohol consumption (yes or no), and household income (lowest 30% or above). Clinical variables included BMI (kg/m2), total cholesterol (mg/dL), and history of cancer (yes or no). In addition, models were mutually adjusted for adherence to physical activity (≥ 500 MET‐min/week, yes or no) and metabolic or obesity phenotypes to account for potential confounding between these interrelated exposures.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics of participants were summarised as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as numbers (percentages) for categorical variables. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all‐cause and CVD mortality according to physical activity levels and metabolic–obesity phenotypes. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using visual inspection of log–log survival plots, which confirmed that the assumption was satisfied.

Three sequential multivariable models were constructed. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 2a was additionally adjusted for smoking status, alcohol consumption, household income, total cholesterol, and cancer history, with mutual adjustment for physical activity and metabolic–obesity phenotype. Because the phenotype classification incorporated BMI categories, Model 2a was treated as the primary multivariable model. Model 2b additionally included continuous BMI as a supplementary sensitivity analysis to assess whether the observed associations were explained by residual variation in the degree of adiposity within phenotype categories.

Subgroup analyses were performed to examine dose–response associations between physical activity levels and all‐cause and CVD mortality within each metabolic–obesity phenotype category. Additional analyses stratified by sex and age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years) were conducted to explore potential heterogeneity of associations across clinically relevant subgroups. Joint associations of physical activity and metabolic–obesity phenotypes with mortality outcomes were also evaluated is provided in Table S4.

To formally evaluate effect modification, multiplicative interaction between physical activity level and metabolic–obesity phenotype was tested by including an interaction term in the Cox models. To improve clinical interpretability, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were additionally presented to illustrate absolute cumulative risk of all‐cause and CVD mortality across phenotype and physical activity categories. For CVD mortality, competing‐risk analyses were additionally conducted using the Fine–Grey subdistribution hazard model, treating non‐CVD death as a competing event.

Sensitivity analyses were performed by (1) excluding participants who died within the first 3 years of follow‐up to reduce potential reverse causality, (2) excluding underweight individuals (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), and (3) redefining metabolically unhealthy status using an alternative threshold of ≥ 3 metabolic abnormalities. All analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All p‐values were two‐sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Baseline Characteristics

Baseline characteristics by obesity–metabolic phenotype are shown in Table 1. The cohort comprised 442 666 adults (mean age 47.2 years; 50% women) who were followed for a median of 9.3 years, with clear phenotype‐specific differences. MUHO had the least favourable cardiometabolic profile, including the highest BMI, waist circumference, blood pressure, fasting glucose, triglycerides, and the highest prevalence of hypertension (58.9%), type 2 diabetes (29.9%), and dyslipidemia (44.2%). In contrast, MHL individuals showed the most favourable levels of adiposity, glucose, lipids, and comorbidities.

Independent Associations of Physical Activity and Metabolic‐Obesity Phenotypes With All‐Cause and CVD Mortality

Table 2 shows the independent associations of physical activity and metabolic–obesity phenotypes with all‐cause and CVD mortality in the study population. In the primary multivariable model (Model 2a), MUHL showed the highest all‐cause mortality risk compared with MHL (HR 1.23, 95% CI 1.19–1.26), whereas MHO was associated with a significantly lower risk (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.81–0.89), and MUHO was not significantly associated with all‐cause mortality (HR 0.97, 95% CI 0.94–1.01). In the supplementary BMI‐adjusted model (Model 2b), the lower risk observed for MHO was attenuated to null (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.93–1.05), while MUHO showed a modestly increased risk (HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.10–1.22), and MUHL remained at the highest risk (HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.21–1.29). Across physical activity levels, higher activity was consistently protective; ≥ 1500 MET‐min/week conferred the lowest all‐cause mortality (HR 0.75, 0.71–0.79), with benefits evident even at 1–499 MET‐min/week. In the primary multivariable model, compared with MHL, MUHL had higher CVD mortality (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.20–1.42), whereas MHO had lower risk (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.70–0.93), and MUHO showed no significant excess risk (HR 1.06, 95% CI 0.96–1.17). Physical activity also showed a strong inverse association with CVD mortality, with ≥ 1500 MET‐min/week associated with approximately 36% lower risk (HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.54–0.74). For CVD mortality, competing‐risk analyses using the Fine–Grey model yielded results broadly consistent with those of the main Model 2a, with subdistribution hazard ratios of 1.30 (95% CI, 1.20–1.42), 0.81 (0.70–0.93), and 1.06 (0.96–1.17) for MUHL, MHO, and MUHO, respectively. Higher physical activity also remained inversely associated with CVD mortality, with corresponding estimates of 0.83 (0.76–0.91), 0.77 (0.70–0.84), 0.70 (0.61–0.80), and 0.63 (0.54–0.74) across increasing activity categories. Kaplan–Meier survival curves for all‐cause and CVD mortality according to metabolic–obesity phenotype and physical activity level are presented in Figures S1 and S2, respectively. The curves showed clear separation across groups and were consistent with the patterns observed in the multivariable Cox regression analyses (log‐rank p < 0.001 for all comparisons). Sensitivity analyses yielded results broadly consistent with the main findings (Tables S1–S3). Excluding underweight individuals attenuated the favourable association for MHO and made the excess risk for MUHO more apparent, whereas the overall inverse associations for physical activity remained largely unchanged.

Independent Associations of Physical Activity and Metabolic‐Obesity Phenotypes With All‐Cause and CVD Mortality According to Sex and Age Group (< 65 vs. ≥65 Years)

Table 3 presents the independent associations of physical activity and metabolic–obesity phenotypes with all‐cause and CVD mortality according to sex and age group (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years). Sex‐stratified analyses showed broadly similar patterns in men and women, with MUHL associated with higher and MHO with lower all‐cause mortality in both sexes. In contrast, age‐stratified analyses showed clear heterogeneity: among participants aged < 65 years, MUHL and MUHO were associated with higher all‐cause mortality (1.37; 1.30–1.45 and 1.14; 1.08–1.20), whereas among those aged ≥ 65 years, MHO and MUHO were associated with lower all‐cause mortality (0.77; 0.72–0.82 and 0.89; 0.85–0.93) (p for interaction < 0.001). A similar age‐dependent pattern was observed for CVD mortality, with stronger excess risks in the younger group and lower or null risks in the older group.

Association Between All‐Cause or CVD Mortality and Physical Activity Levels, Stratified by Metabolic‐Obesity Phenotypes

Physical activity showed a clear dose–response association with lower all‐cause mortality across all metabolic–obesity phenotypes (Table 4). In the metabolically healthy lean (MHL) group, the greatest reduction was observed at ≥ 1500 MET‐min/week (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.67–0.81), with progressively lower risks at increasing activity levels. Similar graded reductions were evident in the MUHL, MHO, and MUHO groups, indicating consistent survival benefits of higher physical activity irrespective of metabolic–obesity status.

Discussion

In this large population‐based cohort, mortality risk differed markedly according to metabolic–obesity phenotype and physical activity level. In the primary multivariable model, MUHL showed the highest all‐cause mortality, and metabolically unhealthy phenotypes were generally associated with less favourable CVD mortality patterns, although these associations varied across subgroups. Across all phenotypes, higher physical activity was consistently associated with lower all‐cause and CVD mortality in a dose–response pattern. Joint analyses further demonstrated a graded inverse association between physical activity and mortality across metabolic–obesity phenotypes, although the apparent differences in the strength of these associations between phenotype groups should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, age‐stratified analyses suggested heterogeneity in the associations of metabolic–obesity phenotypes with mortality, with more pronounced adverse associations of MUHL and MUHO observed among participants aged < 65 years.

In the present study, MHO initially appeared to be associated with lower all‐cause mortality than MHL, whereas MUHO showed no clear excess risk in the primary model. After additional adjustment for continuous BMI, the lower risk observed for MHO was attenuated and MUHO showed a higher mortality risk. Because the metabolic–obesity phenotypes were partly defined using BMI categories, this BMI‐adjusted model should be interpreted as a supplementary robustness analysis. These findings suggest that the observed associations for MHO and MUHO were not fully independent of absolute BMI differences within phenotype categories [25, 26]. In contrast, excess mortality risk was concentrated among metabolically unhealthy phenotypes, with MUHL showing the highest all‐cause mortality and metabolically unhealthy groups generally exhibiting less favourable CVD mortality patterns [9, 27]. These findings support the value of considering metabolic dysfunction alongside BMI‐based obesity status in mortality risk stratification. This heterogeneity may reflect underlying biological differences between metabolically healthy and unhealthy obesity. Prior studies have suggested that individuals with MHO tend to have lower liver and visceral fat, relatively preserved lower‐body subcutaneous fat distribution, greater cardiorespiratory fitness and physical activity, better insulin sensitivity, lower inflammatory burden, and more preserved adipose tissue function than their metabolically unhealthy counterparts [28].

While metabolically unhealthy phenotypes consistently confer higher risks of mortality and cardiovascular outcomes, evidence regarding metabolically healthy obesity has been less consistent, with reported associations differing across populations and study settings [29, 30]. For example, in a Korean study using metabolically healthy non‐obesity as the reference, metabolically unhealthy non‐obesity was associated with higher all‐cause (HR 1.20, 95% CI 1.15–1.25) and CVD mortality (HR 1.38, 95% CI 1.23–1.55), whereas MUHO showed no clear excess risk for all‐cause mortality (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.91–1.01) despite elevated CVD mortality (HR 1.32, 95% CI 1.16–1.50) [8]. Similar patterns have been reported in a large Chinese cohort, in which both metabolically unhealthy non‐obesity (HR 1.27, 95% CI 1.21–1.34) and MUHO (HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.30–1.44) were associated with increased all‐cause mortality relative to metabolically healthy non‐obesity [31]. UK evidence incorporating Scottish cohorts further supports elevated CVD mortality among metabolically unhealthy phenotypes regardless of obesity status, with comparable hazards for MUHL (HR 1.59, 95% CI 1.30–1.94) and MUHO groups (HR 1.64, 95% CI 1.17–2.30) compared with MHL individuals [6].

Notably, the absence of excess all‐cause mortality in MHO compared with MHL is consistent with the lean high‐risk and obesity paradox patterns reported in some Asian populations [32]. Such observations have been attributed to population‐specific differences in body composition and fat distribution, including relatively greater visceral or ectopic adiposity and lower lean mass at a given BMI. Together, these findings suggest that BMI alone may inadequately capture underlying cardiometabolic risk in this context, highlighting the importance of incorporating metabolic health status when evaluating obesity‐related prognosis.

Age‐stratified analyses further suggested that the prognostic implications of metabolic–obesity phenotypes may differ by age. In particular, the stronger excess risks observed among participants aged < 65 years, together with the attenuated or inverse associations seen in those aged ≥ 65 years, may reflect survivor bias and age‐related heterogeneity in the clinical meaning of BMI‐based obesity categories. Prior literature has described an “obesity paradox” in older adults and has emphasised that BMI is an imperfect marker of adiposity because it does not distinguish fat mass from lean mass or capture body fat distribution [33, 34]. In addition, frailty, sarcopenia, underlying illness, and age‐related weight loss may disproportionately influence mortality risk in older adults, potentially making lean phenotypes appear less favourable and obese phenotypes less harmful in late life. Accordingly, our age‐stratified results should be interpreted cautiously and support the need for future studies incorporating more direct measures of body composition and central adiposity [35, 36].

Importantly, accumulating evidence suggests that physical activity substantially modifies these phenotype‐associated risks. Across population‐based studies, higher levels of physical activity have been associated with lower risks of all‐cause and CVD mortality, and joint analyses indicate that physical activity can attenuate excess risk even within MUHO; for instance, among MUHO, high physical activity versus no MVPA was associated with lower CVD risk (HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.80–0.92) [10, 37]. More broadly, large prospective data suggest that the protective association of physical activity is graded across metabolic–obesity phenotypes, with numerically stronger associations observed in metabolically unhealthy groups, although formal interaction was not statistically significant [26, 37, 38].

Regular physical activity may reduce mortality risk through multiple cardiometabolic pathways, including improved insulin sensitivity, lower chronic low‐grade inflammation, and enhanced endothelial and autonomic function—processes central to the pathogenesis of cardiovascular events and premature death [39]. These benefits are not contingent on substantial weight loss, supporting the clinical relevance of activity‐related improvements in metabolic and vascular health even when adiposity remains unchanged. From a clinical and public health perspective, our findings reinforce physical activity promotion as a core component of prevention across metabolic–obesity phenotypes, with particular emphasis on individuals with adverse metabolic profiles irrespective of BMI. More broadly, these findings also highlight the importance of early lifestyle intervention and obesity prevention. Accumulating evidence indicates that weight reduction, dietary modification, and regular physical activity can improve obesity‐related metabolic abnormalities and metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease, supporting early risk‐factor modification even before overt cardiometabolic disease becomes apparent [40]. Future studies should also investigate biological factors that may contribute to metabolic heterogeneity in obesity, including brown adipose tissue (BAT). Recent evidence suggests that adults with metabolically healthy overweight or obesity may have greater BAT volume and activity, along with higher thermogenesis, than their metabolically unhealthy counterparts, although the relevance of these findings to long‐term mortality risk remains to be established [41].

This study has several notable strengths. It utilised a large, nationwide cohort to examine the joint associations of metabolic–obesity phenotypes and physical activity with all‐cause and CVD mortality. By stratifying risk across these combined strata, our findings provide a clinically interpretable framework for risk assessment at the population level. However, this study has several limitations. First, physical activity was self‐reported and is therefore prone to recall and social desirability bias, which may have introduced non‐differential misclassification and attenuated the observed associations; in addition, non‐leisure domains of physical activity were not captured. Second, metabolic status, obesity phenotypes, and physical activity levels were characterised only at baseline. Consequently, we could not account for longitudinal transitions in metabolic health or behavioural changes in physical activity during the follow‐up period, which may have further diluted the reported associations. Given that MHO may represent a transitional phenotype, the absence of excess risk observed in this study should be interpreted cautiously, particularly within the approximately 9‐year follow‐up period. Third, despite rigorous adjustment for a comprehensive range of cardiometabolic and lifestyle covariates, the possibility of residual confounding remains, particularly because detailed information on dietary quality, specific medication classes, more granular smoking exposure, and multimorbidity burden was not available, in addition to medication adherence and unmeasured disease severity. The analysis was also based on complete‐case data, and multiple imputation or formal missingness diagnostics were not performed. Fourth, our findings are sensitive to the specific operational definitions of metabolic health and BMI cut‐points; the use of alternative criteria or non‐Asian‐specific BMI thresholds might yield different risk gradients. In addition, because obesity status was defined using BMI alone, our findings should be interpreted in the context of BMI‐based phenotypes rather than as direct evidence about adiposity. Finally, as the cohort consisted predominantly of Korean adults, the generalisability of these findings to other ethnic groups or diverse healthcare systems may be limited.

In this large population‐based cohort, mortality risk differed substantially across metabolic–obesity phenotypes, with the highest all‐cause and CVD mortality observed in metabolically unhealthy groups, particularly those who were lean. Higher physical activity was consistently associated with lower all‐cause and CVD mortality across all phenotypes and most strongly attenuated excess risk in metabolically unhealthy individuals. These findings highlight physical activity as a key, modifiable determinant of survival that may mitigate the adverse prognosis associated with metabolic dysfunction, regardless of obesity status.
