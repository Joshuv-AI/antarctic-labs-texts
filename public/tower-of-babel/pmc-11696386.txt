Determination of Key Groups of Coal Spontaneous Combustion and Risk Prediction Based on Ultrasonic Extraction
Authors: Guoju Lu, Guofei Zhao, Liya Yu, Meihong Zhang, Xiaoli Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn order to accurately investigate the key microstructures in the spontaneous combustion exothermic process of coal, an ultrasonic extraction method was employed to extract the coal, and the complex microscopic groups within it were stripped and studied. On this basis, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy and differential scanning calorimetry were employed to assess the content of microscopic groups and the exothermic characteristics of the raw and extracted coal samples. The findings indicated that toluene and methanol demonstrated a notable capacity for extracting aromatic and aliphatic hydrocarbon compounds from coal, whereas N-methyl pyrrolidone (NMP) and ethylenediamine (EDA) exhibited a pronounced effect on oxygen-containing functional groups and hydroxyl groups. The heat flow curves and spontaneous combustion risk indices of the extracted coal samples were reduced to varying degrees, and the coal-oxygen reaction was suppressed. The order of the coal samples’ spontaneous combustion risk indices was E-EDA, E-NMP, E-methanol, and E-toluene, with the latter having the lowest value. The effects of −OH-a and oxygen-containing functional groups on the spontaneous combustion exotherm of the coal samples were greater. The Pearson correlation coefficient method was employed to identify the key groups with the highest correlation with the risk indices of coal. These were found to be −CH2– and −OH-a, respectively. A multivariate linear regression model for predicting the spontaneous combustion risk of coal was subsequently established. The results demonstrated that −CH2– had a more significant effect on the spontaneous combustion risk index than that of −OH-a.

Introduction

China is the world’s largest producer and consumer of coal resources, having produced 4.67 billion tons of coal in 2023, which accounts for over 56% of its primary energy consumption.1,2 However, in comparison to the considerable quantities of coal produced and consumed in China, the country is particularly susceptible to the phenomenon of spontaneous coal combustion. It has been reported that more than 130 large- and medium-sized mines in 25 major coal-producing provinces and regions across China are threatened to varying degrees by spontaneous fires in coal seams.3,4 Of the 657 key coal mines in China, 54.9% exhibit a tendency for coal seam coal spontaneous combustion, which has a significant impact on China’s coal production and results in considerable harm to the lives and wellbeing of local residents and the environment.5,6 The prevention and control of coal spontaneous combustion disasters are urgent.

The cause of spontaneous combustion of coal is the thermal runaway caused by the exothermic agglomeration of various reactive structures in coal reacting with oxygen;7 therefore, accurately identifying the active functional groups that play a key role in coal spontaneous combustion heat release is crucial for disaster prevention and control. However, due to the extremely complex chemical structure of coal, various active groups are mixed together and there are also cross influences during the oxidation heating process. This makes it difficult for existing experimental and research methods to determine the key roles of each component under a unified macromolecular coal structure, thereby limiting the specificity of the prevention and control technologies.

Solvent extraction is one of the commonly used methods for extracting the microscopic functional groups of coal.8 The coal can be placed into a particular solvent, which can be enhanced by additional techniques such as ultrasound, vibration, or heating. When the bonding force of the solvent molecules is greater than the adhesion forces within the coal structure, including hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, and electron supply, the small molecular groups within the coal will separate and dissolve into the solvent.9,10 A comparison of the microstructural and exothermic characteristics of the extracted coals and raw coal can elucidate the dominant role of a key group in the spontaneous combustion process. Based on this, Guo et al.11 summarized the microscopic mechanism of interaction between commonly used organic extraction reagents and the coal structure and described the modification effects of various extraction techniques on the coal structure. Arauzo et al.12 investigated the effects of ultrasound on the extraction of phenolic compounds from coffee grounds using water, methanol, and water–methanol mixtures. Rahman et al.13 extracted ashless coal from low-rank Canadian coal using polar and nonpolar solvents and mixed solutions of both and concluded that there was a significant correlation between the extraction effect and the content of the vitrinite. Saikia et al.14,15 found that sulfur oxides (−S=O and −SO) were formed in coal samples during ultrasonic extraction using hydrogen peroxide solution as the extractant and concluded that the combustion performance of the extracted coal was significantly improved. Tognotti et al.16 found that the oxidation and combustion of coal became easier after pyridine extraction. Zhang et al.17 investigated the extraction effects of seven different extraction solvents on coal functional groups and free radicals, and the results showed that dimethylacetamide (DMAC) had the best extraction effect on free radicals, while acetone had a strong extraction ability on −CH3.

In terms of characterizing and predicting the risk of spontaneous combustion of coal in mines, the concentrations and emergence temperatures of indicator gases, such as CO, C2H4, and alkene ratios, have been widely used in coal mine. Li et al.18 proposed a new method to determine the critical value of CO based on the dependence of CO concentration and temperature. Nie et al.19 determined the correlation between 13 indicator gases and temperature by gray correlation analysis based on the trend of the ratio of various gas products to temperature. Qing20 used a logistic model to fit the volume of indicator gases generated based on coal temperature, established a model equation for the relationship between indicator gases and temperature changes, and classified the risk of coal spontaneous combustion into six levels: safe, low, medium, high, very high, and disaster. Obviously, heat release and accumulation are the direct causes of coal spontaneous combustion, so the exothermic characteristics can reflect the nature of spontaneous combustion more clearly. Based on this, Wang et al.21 established a method to predict the spontaneous combustion period based on specific heat capacity and thermal effect. Zhang et al.22 established a spontaneous combustion risk index based on the exothermic effect, characteristic temperature and exothermic time of coal spontaneous combustion, and at the same time, on the basis of considering the heat transfer efficiency, they established a combustion growth index based on the specific heat capacity, heat diffusion coefficient, and exothermic amount to measure the exothermic and propagation behavior of coal spontaneous combustion.23

Based on the above analysis, this article uses an ultrasonic extraction method to extract from the organic coal matter some molecules with the active functional groups. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) were used to test the microstructure and heat release characteristics of raw coal and extracted coal samples. Based on this, the correlation coefficient method was used to determine the key functional groups with the highest risk of spontaneous combustion heat release from coal, and a prediction model was established using multiple linear regression. The research results of this article have positive significance for the efficient prevention and control of the coal spontaneous combustion mechanism and disasters.

Experimental Equipment and Sample Preparation

Sample Preparation

The coal sample employed in the experiment is a bituminous coal sourced from northern Shaanxi, China, designated as the YM. The coal samples are collected from the mining face and transported to the laboratory in a sealed manner. To avoid the influence of disadvantages such as oil pollution and moisture on the structure of coal, the surface coal should be removed before proceeding with subsequent operations. They are then subjected to crushing in a crusher and passed through a 200 mesh sieve before being sealed and stored under conditions devoid of light. Combined with the experience of previous studies, a total of four organic solvents, toluene, methanol, N-methyl pyrrolidone (NMP), and ethylenediamine (EDA), were selected as the extraction solvents in this paper.24,25 Ultrasonic extraction experiments were carried out on the coal samples using equipment such as an ultrasonic cleaner and rotary evaporator. Each time, 10 g of coal was taken into a round-bottomed flask, and 400 mL of screened organic solvent (Analytical Reagent) was added into the flask, which was then tightly capped and placed in an ultrasonic cleaner for 4 h at 25 °C, and the extraction solution and extracted coals were separated by rotary evaporator at the end of the extraction, and then the residual samples were put into a vacuum oven for drying and then sealed and stored for the subsequent tests. According to the different extraction solvents used in the experiments, the extracted coal samples were named E-toluene (toluene solvent extracted coal sample), E-methanol (methanol solvent extracted coal sample), E-NMP (NMP solvent extracted coal sample), and E-EDA (EDA solvent extracted coal sample).

Experimental Methods

FTIR Experiment

The micro-functional group changes of raw and extracted coal samples were tested using the KBr pellet method. Each experiment, 5 mg of sample and 750 mg of KBr were weighed and combined at a ratio of 1:150 and then transferred to an onyx bowl for grinding. Subsequently, 100 mg of the mixed sample was subjected to a pressure of 10 MPa for a period of 10 min using a tablet press, after which the resulting tablets were placed into the sample compartment of the FTIR (Nicolet iN10 and iZ10, Thermo Fisher). The requisite measurements were duly carried out, with the measurement parameters set as follows: resolution of 4.0 cm–1, detection wavenumber range of 650–4000 cm–1, and 32 scanning acquisitions.

DSC Experiment

The variation characteristics of heat flow curves during the spontaneous combustion process of raw coal and extracted coal samples were tested using a DSC (Setline, SETARAM). The mass of the individual test sample is 10 ± 0.3 mg. The heating range is 30–450 °C, the heating rate is 10 °C/min, the gas flow rate is 50 mL/min, and the atmosphere is dry air.

Results and Discussion

Variations Characteristics of Coal Microscopic Functional Groups before and after Extraction

Figure 1 illustrates the original FTIR curves of the raw coal and the four extracted coal samples without baseline correction and normalization. The results demonstrate that the FTIR curves of the four extracted coals after ultrasonic extraction exhibited notable alterations in comparison with those of the raw coal. For instance, there is a notable distinction exists between the absorption peaks of the E-EDA and the YM within the wavenumber range of 3000–3600 cm–1. This is primarily attributed to the presence of diverse types of hydroxyl groups as well as free and associated hydrogen bonding. The significant changes in the FTIR of coal before and after extraction indicate that the micro-functional groups have undergone changes, which in turn affect the progress of spontaneous combustion. In accordance with the Lambert–Beer law, the contents of microscopic groups present within a given coal is directly proportional to the area of the absorption peaks observed in its infrared curve. Therefore, despite the inability to conduct a comprehensive quantitative analysis, it is still feasible to assess the extraction efficacy by comparing the peak areas of the FTIR curves of the raw coal and the extracted coal samples under identical conditions. Consequently, Peakfit software was employed to deconvolve and integrate the peaks of the infrared curves of the raw coal and the four extracted coal samples, with the objective of determining the specific content of the various functional groups. The determination of peak position is based on the valley values of the second derivative of the FTIR curve.7 The peak fitting process is illustrated in Figure 2. Among them, the wavenumber range of 2600–3000 cm–1 is mainly composed of aliphatic hydrocarbons in coal, the range of 3000–3600 cm–1 is mainly composed of hydrogen bonds and various hydroxyl groups in coal, and the range of 650–1800 cm–1 is widely distributed with functional groups such as aromatic hydrocarbons, aliphatic hydrocarbons, and oxygen-containing functional groups.

From the fitting process illustrated in Figure 2, it can be observed that the infrared spectral curves at varying wavenumber positions exhibited disparate fitted peak shapes and quantities. Consequently, the fitted peak shapes were classified according to the attribution of the characteristic absorption bands of the infrared spectra of the coal samples,7,26−28 as shown in Table 2. The results demonstrated that the extractants had varying effects on the extraction of different groups in coal. These groups were classified into five major categories: aromatic hydrocarbon compounds, aliphatic hydrocarbon compounds, oxygen-containing functional groups, hydroxyls, and inorganic compounds represented by −Si–O–. The percentage of the content for each category is shown in Figure 3. The content of inorganic compounds was relatively stable, ranging from 3.1 to 3.7%. This indicated that solvent extraction was mainly applied to the chemical bonding between the organic compounds in the coal. In comparison to the raw coal, the aromatic hydrocarbon samples of the four extracted coal samples exhibited a notable alteration, characterized by a “three increases and one decrease” pattern. This pattern is evident in the aromatic hydrocarbon content of the E-toluene sample, which decreased from 36.7 to 35.6%. The aromatic hydrocarbon content of the E-toluene sample decreased from 36.7 to 35.6%, while that of the E-methanol, E-NMP, and E-EDA samples increased to 38.0, 38.9, and 38.2%, respectively. It is evident that the reduction in the quantity of a specific type of microgroups indicates that the extractant has a more effective extraction capability, which can disrupt their intermolecular chemical bonds and facilitate their dissolution into the solution. This ultimately results in a decline in the concentration of the corresponding microgroups in the extracted coal samples. Consequently, toluene emerges as the superior solvent for the extraction of aromatic hydrocarbon compounds in coal. Similarly, a significant decrease in aliphatic hydrocarbon compounds was observed in the E-methanol sample, where the aliphatic hydrocarbon compounds decreased from 19.5 to 15.2% of YM, a decrease of more than 22.0%; the largest decrease in oxygen-containing functional groups was observed in the E-NMP sample, which decreased from 25.4 to 24.1%, a decrease of 5.1%; and the largest decrease in hydroxyl group was observed in the E-EDA sample, where the content decreased from 15.2 to 10.9%, a decrease of more than 28.2%. In addition, when comparing the extraction effect of different types of groups, it can be found that the extraction rate of aromatic hydrocarbon compounds is significantly lower than that of other groups. This can be attributed to two reasons. The first reason is because aromatic hydrocarbons are the main core and main body of coal macromolecules, and benzene, naira, anthracene, and other stable chemical bonds are dominant, so it is difficult for the extractant to achieve the destruction of their chemical bonds, resulting in a lower extraction rate. The second reason is that toluene’s dissolution and extraction of aromatic compounds in coal are more targeted at unstable soluble aromatic hydrocarbon structures, which account for a smaller proportion in coal.

On the basis of Figure 3, the four main types of groups obtained were further subdivided to facilitate the classification of aromatic hydrocarbon compounds into three types: substituted benzene, −C=C– and Ar-CH; aliphatic hydrocarbon compounds into −CH3, −CH2– and Al-CH; and oxygen-containing functional groups into −C–O–C–, −C=O, and −COO–. Subsequently, the hydroxyl groups are subdivided into two categories: free hydroxyl (OH-f), which predominantly exists in water, and associated hydroxyl (OH-a), which utilizes intermolecular hydrogen bonding as the primary form of existence. The specific content of each group is illustrated in Figure 4. The results indicate that the extraction of aromatic hydrocarbon structures by toluene is mainly reflected on the C=C– group. Previous studies have shown that the benzene ring contained in toluene will stack with the coal benzene ring in space, forming strong ““π–π”–“π–π”” interactions, leading to the dissolution of unstable and easily soluble aromatic hydrocarbon structures in the solvent.29 In the case of aliphatic compounds, both solvents, namely, methanol and EDA, resulted in a notable reduction in the −CH3 content. However, the extraction effect of methanol was particularly pronounced, which was attributed to the solvent’s strong solubility for polar compounds and unstable chain hydrocarbons. Furthermore, methanol demonstrated a superior extraction effect on Al-CH in coal, with the content decreasing by approximately 27% following extraction. The results presented in Figure 4c demonstrate that NMP has a notable impact on the extraction of −C–O–C–, −C=O, and −COO– in coal, with extraction rates of 2.3, 36.8, and 18.0%, respectively. It is evident that the extraction effect of NMP on the latter two groups is more pronounced. The results of Figure 4d demonstrate that methanol, NMP, and EDA all exhibit superior extraction efficacy with respect to −OH-a in coal. Among these, EDA displays the most pronounced extraction efficacy, with an extraction rate of 31.0%. This is because EDA has a robust electron-donating capacity, which enables it to disrupt the intermolecular hydrogen bond. In contrast, −OH-a is predominantly derived from the intermolecular hydrogen bond within the coal structure, while −OH-f is primarily sourced from the free water present within the coal matrix, so the extraction rate of the former is significantly higher than that of the latter.

Variations Characteristics of Coal Heat Release

As previously discussed, the microscopic composition of the coal samples underwent notable alterations following extraction, which, in turn, impacted the oxidation reaction and the subsequent thermal effect associated with spontaneous combustion. Accordingly, the heat flow curves and the change characteristics of the three key characteristic temperatures of the raw coal and the four extracted coal samples during the thermal reaction process were compared, as illustrated in Table 2. Among them, T1 is the temperature when DSC is 0, which is the boundary point between the endothermic and exothermic stages of coal; T2 and T3 are the two extreme values of the DDSC curve (approximately 250–400 °C), representing the initial and end temperatures of benzene ring preliminary cracking in coal.7 This analysis led to the division of the thermal reaction into four stages, as depicted in Figure 5. It can be observed that the heat flow curves of the extracted coal samples and the raw coal in the thermal reaction process exhibit a comparable trend. As the reaction progresses, the free moisture contained in the coal samples gradually evaporates due to the temperature increase, resulting in an evident heat absorption effect of the coal, as indicated by the negative value of the DSC. Upon exceeding T1, the water in the coal gradually evaporates, facilitating closer coal-oxygen contact and subsequent oxygen physicochemical adsorption on the coal surface. This releases a portion of the absorbed heat, marking the transition of the spontaneous combustion process from the heat-absorbing stage to the exothermic phase. Accordingly, the characteristic temperature T1 represents the demarcation point between the phase of the endothermic and the exothermic processes in the thermal reaction process of coal. This temperature is referred to as the thermal equilibrium temperature. The heat released by physicochemical adsorption accelerated the chemical reaction between the coal and oxygen, which further promoted the spontaneous exothermic combustion, and the DSC curves began to increase gradually, and the coal samples entered the rapid exothermic stage. When the temperature exceeded about 300 °C (T2), an obvious transition of the DDSC curves were observed, and the growth trend of the DSC curves slowed down, which means that the core of the tightly bonded macromolecule aromatic ring in the coal began to be gradually cracked under the action of temperature and part of the heat-absorbing reaction occurred to delay the continuous exothermic reaction of the coal-oxygen reaction, which led to the slowing down of the growth of the DSC curves. When the temperature rises further and exceeds the ignition temperature T3, the benzene ring structure in the coal is cleaved into highly reactive small molecule free radicals. The latter combines with oxygen to undergo a violent combustion reaction and releases a large amount of heat, and spontaneous combustion enters an irreversible combustion stage. Comparing the heat flow curves of the extracted coal samples with those of the original coal, it can be found that the heat flow curves of the extracted coal samples are significantly lower than those of the original coal in the exothermic stage, which means that the exothermic ability of the extracted coal samples to react with oxygen is reduced. Further, the results of the graph indicate that there is the greatest difference between the heat flux curves of the E-EDA and the YM; meanwhile, the previous analysis shows that the content of aliphatic hydrocarbons and hydroxy-OH-a groups in the E-EDA is significantly reduced compared to YM, which means that these two groups have a greater effect on the spontaneous combustion exothermicity of the coal.

Comparison of exothermic curves of raw coal and extracted coal.

Table 3 gives the three characteristic temperatures of the raw coal and extracted coal samples and the differences of the heat flow curves HFD2 and HFD3 at the moments of T2 and T3. It can be seen that the characteristic temperatures of the four extracted coal samples have lagged behind to different degrees compared with YM. Taking the thermal equilibrium temperature T1 as an example, the four extracted coal samples lagged about 6.5, 7.9, 1.0, and 13.8 °C, respectively, compared with the raw coal. This indicates that the reduction of microscopic groups in the coal samples after solvent extraction is unfavorable to the spontaneous combustion reaction of coal. Meanwhile, upon comparison of the difference of heat flow curves HFD2 and HFD3, it was found that the heat flow values of the extracted coal samples at the critical moments were reduced compared to the raw coal, which indicated that the oxidative exotherm between the extracted coal samples and the oxygen was suppressed. This is consistent with the changes in the heat flow curves. In addition, a comprehensive comparison of the lag effects of the characteristic temperatures and heat flow differences shows that the average of the characteristic temperature lag of the four extracted coal samples is E-EDA > E-toluene > E-methanol > E-NMP in descending order from smallest to largest, while the average of the heat flow differences is E-NMP > E-EDA > E-toluene > E-methanol in descending order from smallest to largest. Taken together, E-EDA coal samples had the most significant lag and heat weakening effects, suggesting that the reduction of aliphatic hydrocarbons and associate hydroxyls has a significant negative effect on coal spontaneous combustion.

Determination of Key Functional Groups for Coal Spontaneous Combustion Risk

As previously discussed, the content of microscopic functional groups of coal samples following ultrasonic extraction underwent a notable alteration, which subsequently influenced the exothermicity and risk of spontaneous combustion. Of these changes, the hydroxyl groups exhibited the most pronounced impact. Accordingly, in order to further quantify the intrinsic relationship between the microscopic functional groups and the coal spontaneous combustion risk, Pearson’s correlation coefficient method was employed to identify the specific relationship between the spontaneous combustion risk of coal and the aforementioned microscopic functional groups. The Pearson correlation coefficient method is a commonly employed statistical technique for quantifying the linear relationship between two variables, X and Y. This is achieved by calculating the quotient of the covariance and standard deviation between the two variables, as illustrated in eq 2(30,31)

In this context, n represents the sample size, while X and Y denote the observed values of the two variables. The value of Pearson’s correlation coefficient falls within the range (−1, 1). When the calculated result is close to 1, it indicates a strong positive correlation between the two variables, whereby an increase in the amount of X will result in an increase in Y. Conversely, when the result is close to −1, it indicates a strong negative correlation between the two variables, whereby an increase in X will result in a decrease in Y. The coal spontaneous combustion risk index (Cr) calculated above was designated as the dependent variable (Y), while the contents of the various microscopic functional groups of the raw coal and four extracted coals were designated as the independent variable (X). The results were calculated using eq 2, and the results are presented in Figure 8.

Variations in correlation between spontaneous combustion risk index and micro-functional groups.

It can be observed that there are disparate correlations between disparate groups and the spontaneous combustion risk of coal. The groups with the highest positive correlation coefficients are −CH2– and −OH-a, with positive correlation coefficients of 0.963 and 0.922, respectively. This is followed by two kinds of oxygen-containing functional groups; the correlation coefficients for –C=O and −COO– are 0.848 and 0.734, respectively. The correlation coefficient for substituted benzene is −0.722, while those for the –C=C– and −C–O–C– groups are approximately 0.695. The correlation for other groups, such as −CH3, Al-CH and −OH-f, are not significant. The results of the correlation coefficients demonstrate that the contents of −CH2– and −OH-a exert considerable influence on the propensity of coal to undergo spontaneous combustion. These two groups are identified as the primary determinants of the risk of spontaneous combustion in coal. An increase in the concentration of these two groups is associated with an elevated risk of spontaneous combustion in coal. Indeed, the hydroxyl group is widely regarded as one of the most active in coal in terms of groups. It is highly susceptible to the formation of •OOH radicals with high oxidizing and reactive properties by conjugation with oxygen, which promotes the chain reaction. The −CH2– group is regarded as the principal form of bridging bonds and unsaturated hydrocarbons present in coal. It is the primary source of olefins and alkanes in the process of spontaneous combustion of coal. In contrast, substituted benzene is primarily located within the more stable aromatic hydrocarbon core of coal, which does not undergo significant reactions prior to the ignition temperature. Consequently, an increase in its content indicates a higher degree of coal coalification, which in turn results in a more stable core skeleton and a reduced risk of spontaneous combustion. Among the less relevant groups, −OH-f is primarily present in the free water of coal. This has a negligible impact on the exothermic reaction in the late stage of coal combustion, as the majority of this group is removed during water evaporation.

Conclusions

This article uses the ultrasonic extraction method to perform solvent extraction treatment on coal and uses FTIR and DSC experiments to test the micro-group content and exothermic characteristics of raw coal and extracted coals. The key groups most related to the risk of coal spontaneous combustion are determined, and the specific conclusions are as follows.

(1)Different extractants have different extraction effects on different groups in coal. Toluene and methanol have a certain extraction ability for aromatic and aliphatic hydrocarbon compounds in coal, while NMP and EDA have a significant extraction effect for oxygenated functional groups and hydroxyl groups.

(2)The heat flow curves and spontaneous combustion risk indices of the extracted coal samples were reduced to different degrees, and the coal–oxygen reaction was suppressed. The spontaneous combustion risk indices of extracted coals were E-EDA, E-NMP, E-methanol, and E-toluene in descending order, and the associated hydroxyl groups and oxygen-containing functional groups had a greater effect on the spontaneous exothermic combustion of coal.

(3)Based on the Pearson correlation coefficient method, the key groups of coal spontaneous combustion risk were identified, among which the groups with the highest positive correlation coefficients were −CH2– and −OH-a, and the group with the highest negative correlation coefficients was substituted benzene. Based on the identified key groups, a multivariate linear regression model for predicting the spontaneous combustion risk of coal was established, and it was demonstrated that the effect of −CH2– on the spontaneous combustion risk index was more significant than that of −OH-a.
