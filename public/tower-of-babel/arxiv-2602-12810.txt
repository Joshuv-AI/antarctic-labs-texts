Paid to Look Like Truth: The Prevalence and Dark Patterns of Advertorials in News Outlets
Emmanouil Papadogiannakis Panagiotis Papadopoulos FORTH & University of Crete FORTH-ICS Nicolas Kourtellis Evangelos Markatos Keysight AI Labs FORTH & University of Crete

Abstract
A reader browsing through an online article is highly likely to encounter an advertorial, often without realizing it. Advertorials represent a relatively new marketing strategy where advertisements are deliberately designed to resemble the style and tone of editorial content. Despite their appearance, they are, in fact, paid content intended to promote a product, brand, or service. Studies indicate that advertorials are significantly more effective (81%) and less intrusive than traditional banner ads or pop-ups. Despite ongoing regulatory efforts to ensure clear disclosure of paid content, concerns persist about the deceptive nature of advertorials. Advertorials can mislead readers into believing that they are consuming unbiased editorial content. In doing so, they gain undeserved legitimacy, by draping themselves in the credibility of the publication’s design; not to inform or inspire genuine interest, but to deceive. In this study, we conduct the first large-scale and systematic study of advertorials. We propose a novel automated methodology for detecting advertorials in the wild, and we collect 185K ad URLs over a period of 5 months. We investigate the prevalence of problematic advertorials and explore their structural and linguistic characteristics. We find that advertorials appear in 1 out of 3 news sites, including some of the most popular and credible outlets worldwide (e.g., The Guardian, EuroNews, CNN). We further highlight that legal disclaimers intended to inform users of the promotional nature of the content, are often deliberately obscured or difficult to recognize, raising concerns about user protection.



1
Introduction

In the digital age, advertising has become deeply intertwined with how users consume online content.
Articles, blogs, applications and media are provided free of charge precisely because the underlying content is funded by advertisements rendered by publishers on their platforms.
In recent years, native advertising was introduced to counter the overly disruptive nature of traditional banner and display ads
[
41
]
, and the significant increase in Web ad-blocking (used by a staggering 42.7% of internet users
[
11
]
).
Native advertisements are online ads purposefully designed to blend seamlessly with a website’s content, frequently mimicking its structure and style.
Native advertising yields 53% more views and a
2-3\times
higher Click-Through Rate (CTR) than traditional advertising. Furthermore, 85% of internet users may find native ads more trustworthy than conventional formats
[
55
]
.

Figure 1:
Example of advertorials; paid advertisements designed to look like an article, blending informative content with product promotion. Although they appear to present general information, they ultimately serve to promote a specific product (pillows on the left and cameras on the right).

A category of native advertisements currently gaining traction is
advertorials
: editorial-style content blended with sponsored material explicitly crafted to resemble the tone, format, and language of genuine editorial articles.
Studies indicate advertorials are 81% more effective (and notably less intrusive) than traditional ads
[
26
]
.
Consequently, Web users can sometimes unknowingly encounter content that appears to be a standard article but is, in fact, an advertisement promoting a specific brand, product, or service (as depicted in Figure
1
).

The concept of blending advertising with editorial content is not new; early newspapers and magazines employed this format to bolster revenue without expanding traditional ad spaces.
However, it was the digital revolution that catalyzed the widespread growth of advertorials
[
43
]
.
This proliferation is primarily driven by their higher engagement rates and lower intrusiveness; studies confirm that 70% of users prefer learning about a brand through blog posts and articles rather than traditional advertisements
[
52
]
.

Despite these benefits, the increasing difficulty in distinguishing between actual journalism and paid advertising makes advertorials a particularly problematic form, as they can mislead readers into unknowingly engaging with promotional content.
Users frequently fail to recognize promotional content embedded within editorial material, with only 9% of people identifying such advertorials
[
4
]
.
For instance, in 2013, The Atlantic published a story praising the Church of Scientology’s leader, presented in the same format as its regular editorial content, which was later revealed to be a sponsored post
[
17
]
.
This advertorial’s focus on sensitive topics like religion highlighted the risks of poorly disclosed sponsored content that can mislead or deceive readers.
More recently, various news outlets in Australia promoted gas energy without disclosing that these articles were sponsored by the fossil fuel industry
[
42
]
.
Compounding this issue, studies show advertorials can blend so seamlessly with website content, deceiving older adults
[
5
]
or visually impaired people
[
30
]
.
These issues raise concerns about how lack of transparency in advertorials can influence public opinion and shape perceptions.

Various regulatory bodies, including the Federal Trade Commission (FTC), the European Commission, the Advertising Standards Authority (ASA), and similar organizations worldwide, have sought to curb the unrestrained use of advertorials and ensure that they are clearly distinguishable from editorial content
[
16
,
18
,
1
]
.
These efforts aim to protect users from sponsored content disguised as impartial journalism.
In the academic domain, while substantial research has focused on native advertising and more specifically on problematic ads
[
61
,
2
]
less attention has been paid to the deployment patterns of deceptive or misleading advertorials, as well as the extent to which they comply with existing regulations.

In this work, we conduct the first large-scale and systematic study of advertorials, investigating their prevalence, as well as their typical characteristics.
We set out to explore how frequently online users encounter problematic advertorials, along with the language, style, and structure used by advertorial operators to mislead users.
We examine how advertorials employ common malicious tactics to defraud users, often mimicking financial scams, miracle cures, and fraudulent campaigns, while also using dark patterns to bypass regulations.
Through a systematic analysis of online news articles, we (i) identify common structural and stylistic features of advertorials and (ii) assess the frequency with which users are exposed to such content.
We then examine global regulations and identify systematic non-compliance by advertorial publishers.
Finally, we propose a novel methodology capable of detecting advertorials at scale.
To support further research, enhance transparency, and help prevent user deception, we publicly release the datasets used in this study
[
6
]
.

The contributions of this work include the following:

1.

Large-scale analysis
: We conduct the first large-scale and systematic study of advertorials, investigating their prevalence within the programmatic advertising ecosystem as well as their structural and linguistic features.

2.

Advertorial detection methodology
: We propose a novel automated approach for detecting advertorials in the wild, leveraging both structural and semantic features. We apply this methodology to over 185K ad URLs collected over a five-month period.

3.

Global prevalence:
We find that 1 in 3 news websites worldwide serve ads for advertorials, including some of the most popular and credible outlets, ranked among the top 2,000 websites globally.

4.

Geographical disparities:
Our analysis reveals clear regional differences in advertorial exposure, with users in the USA encountering
2.5\times
more distinct advertorials than users in the EU.

5.

Regional hosting trends:
We find that European news sites are less likely to host advertorials, whereas those based in Asia or North America are 50% more likely to feature such content.

6.

Content focus:
We show that advertorial operators prioritize content over functionality: 44% of identified advertorials address sensitive topics such as health-related products, services, or supplements.

7.

Deceptive design patterns:
We identify dark patterns (including font size, color, and text placement) that obscure legal disclosures, making them difficult to recognize. Advertorials are often written in more sophisticated and complex language than surrounding website content.

2
Background

2.1
Advertorial Definition

An
Advertorial
is a form of advertising that has been purposefully designed to look and feel like editorial content.
That is, the tone, style, and format of an advertorial resembles content written by journalists or writers to inform or educate their audience.
However, the true goal of an advertorial is to promote a specific product, brand or service.
Figure
1
presents representative examples of advertorial content, in which the headlines of the articles imply informative intent, such as offering solutions to specific problems, while the actual content is a paid promotion for a specific product.
The fundamental concept of an advertorial is to provide promotional content within an engaging narrative; a technique that dates back to the early 1900s
[
39
,
53
]
.
However, with the evolution of online advertising, advertorials have become extremely popular
[
19
]
, as they are a perfect candidate for native advertisements because of their less intrusive nature.

More formally, in
[
61
]
, product advertorials are defined as “
Ads for consumer products written in the style of a blog post or news article that do not obviously disclose that they were written by the advertiser, other than in the fine print in the header or footer of the page
”.
A key characteristic of an advertorial is its journalistic tone, which leads readers to perceive it as a standard editorial piece, providing engaging content that retains the user’s attention.
In addition, although various regulatory bodies require that advertisements contain a banner or disclaimer indicating that the content is sponsored, as discussed in
[
61
]
, such disclosures are often in the footer fine print, making them easy to miss.

2.2
Misleading Promotional Tactics

Typically, users encounter advertorials while browsing websites (e.g., news outlets), where they are exposed to native or sponsored ads featuring attention-grabbing imagery and clickbait-style headlines.
Upon interaction, users are redirected to a dedicated
advertorial domain
hosting promotional content and often providing purchase options.
In this work, we focus specifically on news websites, as advertorials are inherently designed to mimic the presentation and tone of news articles or other editorial content.

Figure 2:
Advertorials (highlighted in red) being subtly promoted among the articles of very popular news websites. Ads are seamlessly integrated into the news feed of legitimate news platforms, making them virtually indistinguishable from editorial content. By assimilating into the publication’s design, they illegitimately acquire credibility.

A
problematic advertorial
contains content that appears to be a genuine article but is, in fact, a product advertisement hosted on a website (e.g., health-product.com) without any clear and intelligible indication that the content is
sponsored
or employing common malicious tactics to defraud users.
This advertorial is then promoted through native ads on popular and reputable platforms such as news websites (e.g., credible-news.com).
As illustrated in Figure
2
, these native ads are intentionally designed to blend seamlessly with the website’s editorial content, making it difficult for users to distinguish them from actual journalism.
When users click on the native ad, they are redirected to the advertorial hosted on
health-product.com
.
There, the content typically presents misleading information in a format that appears factual and objective, often supported by fabricated reviews, testimonials, or comments intended to convince consumers of the legitimacy and effectiveness of the promoted products.
A major concern is the authenticity of these testimonials and whether they originate from real consumers
[
21
,
8
]
.

The above strategy constitutes an exploitative tactic that hijacks the trust people have in credible news websites.
Advertorials look like a news article, disrupting the user’s advertising filters and skepticism, and people might assume that since the ad is credible since it appears on a reputable website.
This tactic can also undermine the credibility of the original news website, if users feel they were tricked by the platform that hosted the native ad.

2.3
Regulation on Advertising

Various regulation frameworks regarding advertising aim to protect consumers from practices that may harm them or impair their ability to distinguish advertising from other content.
The European Union introduced the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (UCPD) to protect consumers from deceptive marketing tactics
[
16
]
.
The directive prohibits several misleading actions or omissions that could “limit the consumer’s ability to make an informed decision.”
Importantly, the directive explicitly classifies advertorials as misleading commercial practices, defining them as “using editorial content in the media to promote a product where a trader has paid for the promotion without making that clear in the content or by images or sounds clearly identifiable by the consumer.”
Notably, ads are not permitted to deceive or mislead consumers, even if the information presented is factually correct.

Figure 3:
Overview of methodology for detecting problematic advertorials on news websites.

Similar to the UCPD, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) in the United States has issued guidelines that explicitly prohibit deceptive or unfair practices
[
18
]
.
These guidelines outline how to prevent deception and avoid misleading impressions by ensuring that disclosures are clear and prominently displayed.
In the United Kingdom, the Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) similarly notes that advertorials are particularly difficult for users to recognize as advertisements due to their resemblance to editorial content, and therefore must be explicitly disclosed as ads “upfront, in a prominent and easily noticeable place”
[
1
]
.
Likewise, the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) prohibits advertisements that mislead or deceive consumers, emphasizing that promotional content should include prominently displayed disclaimers
[
9
]
.
The ACCC further specifies that misleading or deceptive conduct cannot be excused by disclaimers buried in fine print, and that the use of fake testimonials to persuade consumers is strictly prohibited.

3
Methodology & Data Collection

In this work, we focus on problematic advertorials that use misleading language, hidden disclaimers, and manipulative visual design to obscure their sponsored nature. Such advertorials often use fake testimonials and images to give the appearance of legitimacy, further complicating detection.

To detect problematic advertorials and differentiate them from other forms of banner or native ads, we propose a novel data-driven automated methodology.
Our methodology relies on structural, linguistic and semantic features of advertorial text to discern it from legitimate editorial content and advertising (i.e., benign ads).
We provide a high-level overview of the overall methodology in Figure
3
.
After data collection and filtering is performed (Sec.
3.1
), the first module of the methodology (Sec.
3.2
) ensures that we study ads that resemble editorial content (i.e., advertorials).
The second module (Sec.
3.3
) evaluates behavioral and reputational features to focus on domains that mislead or deceive users (i.e., problematic advertorials).
A domain is labeled as a problematic advertorial only if both modules concur.
A manual evaluation (Sec.
3.4
) allows us to assess the efficacy of our methodology.

3.1
Data Collection

To study advertorials, we first collect ads from websites in the wild.
Given that advertorials are intentionally designed to resemble journalistic content, we analyze news websites to identify instances of promoted advertorials.
We compile a list of news websites from around the world, focusing on the “News & Media” category provided by SimilarWeb
[
54
]
.
In September 2024, we collect the top five news websites, along with the trending news websites for each country, from SimilarWeb, retaining a list of 450 news websites with available traffic and headquarters information.
We make this list publicly available
[
6
]
.
We provide the distribution of their network traffic in terms of monthly visits in Figure
7
(
x
-axis in logarithmic scale).
We observe that this list is representative of the Web, covering global news sites from highly popular platforms to local outlets with just a few thousand visitors.

Using the open-source scraping tool of
[
49
]
, we visit the list of 450 news websites from three different user locations (EU#1, EU#2 and USA) and collect all ads displayed on the website.
We use a VPN service to set user location and geolocation services to verify the crawler’s location with two EU locations corresponding to different countries.
We visit each website with a clean browser state to keep its behavior and that of the advertising ecosystem independent of previously visited domains.
We also utilize a browser extension
[
46
]
to automatically interact with consent banners and accept all cookies.
Prior work has demonstrated that internal pages may differ from landing pages
[
7
]
, so we visit the landing page of each news website, as well as 5 randomly selected subpages.

Upon visiting a webpage, we allow it to fully load and subsequently wait for an additional 20 seconds to ensure the completion of all ad transactions and the full rendering of ads.
We scroll down each page for 6 secs (if possible) and then scroll to the bottom of the page to ensure that all banner and native ads are displayed.
For each webpage, we collect its HTML content, the generated network traffic, cookie-jar (both first-party and third-party cookies) and detected ads.
To discover ads, we build on top of the methodology presented in
[
50
]
and also extend it to detect ads using cosmetic filters.

We perform a repeated measures study and visit the entire list of news websites five times for each user location, totaling 15 distinct campaigns, from October 2024 to February 2025.
Altogether, we detect and process 186,124 distinct ad URLs, a considerable increase (
\times
34 more processed ads,
\times
3 more collected ads) compared to previous work
[
61
]
.

3.2
Structure-based Detection

Following the collection of 186K advertisement URLs, it is necessary to identify which URLs correspond to advertorial content.
Advertorials have several characteristics that distinguish them from both traditional advertisements and editorial content, including legal disclaimers, user comments, testimonials and customer reviews.
We develop a structure-based method to detect such common advertorial characteristics.
Our methodology relies on both the website’s structure (i.e., HTML structure) and the legal text contained in disclaimers.

To build our system, we first assemble a formal collection of advertorial characteristics.
We manually visit and analyze the landing pages of 850 distinct ads found on news websites.
The ads are randomly selected from the set of 186K distinct ads detected in Section
3.1
.
On each page, we manually identify sections corresponding to testimonials, comments, or reviews, and extract the HTML attributes that define these sections.
Our focus is on HTML class, ID, and other attributes that can serve as selectors to identify these specific sections on other websites.
For instance, we uncover that multiple advertorials have a specific user reviews section that can be singled out using specific HTML attribute.
We also discover some fake comments on advertorial domains that explicitly resemble Facebook comments and can be identified using the attributes
class="comment-holder clearfix"
and
id="fbcomments"
.
We also consider the structural hierarchy of HTML elements, to identify precise instances of webpage sections and prevent misclassifications of unrelated pages sharing similar attribute names.

Figure 4:
Example of a legal disclaimer in an advertorial site.

For disclaimers that explicitly disclose an advertisement as an advertorial, we compile a list of legal vocabulary (i.e., key phrases) that reveal the website’s true nature. An example of such a disclaimer is shown in Figure
4
.
We further refine our system by collecting more detailed advertorial data. Specifically, we emulate different browser dimensions to detect responsive designs that present varied content across devices, and we use a VPN service to access websites from multiple countries, uncovering structural variations or disclaimers translated into different languages.
In total, we compile a list of 103 distinct legal disclaimer key phrases across seven languages (English, Dutch, German, Greek, Hebrew, Spanish, and Portuguese) and make this list publicly available to support further research
[
6
]
.
We acknowledge that this list does not capture all possible disclaimers; therefore, our method provides a conservative estimate and may miss disclaimers that use alternative wording.

We develop a detection module that visits an ad landing page and extract its text content and HTML structure.
This module tests a website against our dataset of disclaimer key-phrases and targeted HTML elements or page structure and classifies the URL based on whether its structure resembles that typically associated with advertorial content.
The module is designed to be deployed on any of the 186K ad URLs to assess whether they are candidate advertorials.

3.3
Content-based Detection

The structure-based detection methodology attempts to discover ads that resemble editorial content (i.e., advertorials).
Next, to explicitly label an ad as a problematic advertorial, we evaluate whether it misleads, deceives or even defrauds users.
Relying on impartial Web engines, we build an automated classification system that identifies domains as suspicious, based on behavioral and reputational indicators.

To build our automated advertorial filter, we first create a ground truth dataset containing 1,000 ads, randomly selected from the initial dataset of ads from actual news websites (Sec.
3.1
).
Then, we conduct a manual (human) annotation procedure to label instances of advertorial content.
The annotation phase involves 2 distinct reviewers that can label an ad as either “Advertorial” or “Benign ad” for legitimate display ads.
One of the reviewers is an author of this work, while the second is a knowledgeable (i.e., computer science background) but independent (i.e., not affiliated with this work) reviewer and is only informed about the definitions of an advertorial (Sec.
2
).
Both reviewers are shown screenshots of ad landing pages and may browse them or use any translation tool they deem necessary.
Reviewers are not informed about where the ad was placed, but are allowed to examine it for as long as needed.
Finally, they perform the manual annotation tasks independently, without interaction or shared decisions.

Following the manual annotation procedure, we analyze inter-rater agreement and find that reviewers classify 820 ads as benign and 157 as advertorials. Reviewers disagree on only 23 ads, resulting in agreement on 97.7% of the evaluated items. The Cohen’s kappa score for the manual annotation is 0.92, indicating almost perfect agreement. In the final dataset, an ad is labeled as advertorial only when both reviewers agree, thereby increasing confidence in the ground truth. The resulting dataset is imbalanced, with more benign ads than advertorials, reflecting real-world conditions in which benign advertisements outnumber advertorials
[
61
]
.

A brief follow-up interview conducted after the annotation task revealed that reviewers’ decisions were primarily influenced by the presence of legal disclaimers and recurring structural patterns on webpages (e.g., page layout, comment sections, and customer review areas). These features served as strong indicators for distinguishing advertorials. Reviewers also noted that the editorial style of advertorials differs substantially from that of conventional advertisements, making them appear less overtly commercial. An illustrative example is provided in Appendix
A
.

Next, to extract behavioral, reputational, and technical data about domains, we build an oracle of 19 independent security, safety, and analytics Web engines.
We select only reputable and trustworthy services, such as VirusTotal, Norton Safe Web, and ScamAdviser.
We report the entire list of Web engines in Appendix
B
and note that the authors have no affiliation with these engines.
We also build a simple crawler that measures the size of the landing page in HTML format.
We make use of 20 signal sources (i.e., engines) and extract 45 features per domain.
To improve model efficiency and reduce overfitting, we apply feature selection using a random forest classifier.
Following common best practices, we encode categorical features as numerical values and impute missing data.
Feature importance is measured using Gini impurity, and 24 features are retained, accounting for 96% of the total importance.
We further discuss feature selection in Appendix
B
.

To build the detection module, we evaluate several classifiers (including K-Neighbors, Multilayer Perceptron, Gaussian Process) and find that a Random Forest Classifier performs best.
We perform hyperparameter tuning and cross validation with 20 iterations to accurately compute the model’s true performance.
In Table
1
, we demonstrate that the classifier achieves accuracy and F1 score of almost 93%.
We also report performance separately for domains classified as suspicious or benign.
Although performance is slightly lower for suspicious domains, this is not concerning, as the classifier is only one component of the overall methodology.
We further discuss this in Section
3.4
.
The proposed filter is applied sequentially after the structure-based detector (Sec.
3.2
), providing increased certainty as to whether the landing page of an ad URL corresponds to a problematic advertorial.

User location:

We examine the advertorials encountered by users located in different locations when visiting news websites.
A news website is considered to serve advertisements leading to advertorials if our detection methodology identifies at least one such instance on either an internal page or the landing page during any visit.
Our findings, shown in Table
3
, reveal that the number of news websites containing advertorial ads is consistent across users from different locations, with only minor fluctuations.
In other words, there appears to be no significant difference in the news sites serving at least one advertorial to users within versus outside the EU.
However, and most importantly, we find that just over 1 in 3 news websites worldwide (between 34.7% and 36.6%) serve ads that may mislead or deceive their visitors (i.e., advertorials).

Furthermore, we find that users visiting from the United States encounter
2.5\times
more advertorials than users from the European Union (last column of Table
3
).
This result highlights a geographical disparity in users’ exposure to advertorial content. EU users tend to encounter the same advertorials across multiple websites, whereas U.S. users are exposed to a substantially larger and more diverse set of advertorials. This increased exposure may hinder users’ ability to distinguish between journalistic and sponsored content, potentially affecting unbiased decision-making.
We also examine the total number of ads served to EU and U.S. users and observe that the median number of ad URLs per news website is 36% lower for U.S. users. This finding indicates that the higher exposure to advertorials among U.S. users is not driven by an overall increase in advertising volume.

The observed differences in advertorial exposure between EU and US users may be influenced by factors such as variations in regulatory pressure, enforcement, and cultural differences in consumer protection.
Although our study does not directly examine these, future work could investigate them to better understand regional differences in advertorial exposure.

Finding 1:

1 out of 3 of news organizations internationally serve ads for advertorial content on their websites.
This is particularly evident for USA users that deal with
\times
2.5 more advertorial domains compared to EU users.

Location of operation:

Next, we investigate whether a website’s location affects the presence of advertorials on news websites.
While many news websites have a global presence and are visited by users from multiple countries, we focus on the location of each website’s headquarters since editorial standards and business practices are often influenced by the hosting country.
Headquarters information is provided by SimilarWeb, as described in Sec.
3.1
.
To better understand the effect of website location, we also group websites by continent using data from the United Nations Statistics Division
[
57
]
.
For each country hosting a news website headquarters, the corresponding continent is identified using this dataset.

Our findings are illustrated in Figure
7
, focusing only on news websites from Europe, Asia, and North America. Websites from other continents were excluded due to small sample sizes that do not ensure statistical representativeness (e.g., only 11 websites from Oceania).
We find that European websites contain the fewest advertorials, while websites hosted in Asia or North America are more prone to serving them.
Approximately 23% of European websites served an advertorial to their visitors, compared to roughly 43% of Asian websites and 42% of North American websites.
Consequently, news websites hosted in Asia or North America are almost twice as likely to serve an advertorial to their users.
The consistency of these results across all user locations suggests that the findings are generalizable.
Moreover, a closer examination of individual countries reveals that websites from India serve the most advertorials, regardless of the user’s location.

Advertorial Prevalence in High-Traffic Sites:

We further investigate the websites that served at least one advertorial to any visitor in our dataset.
We find that advertorial ads appear even on some of the most popular and credible news websites worldwide, including The Guardian, EuroNews, and CNN. Examples are provided in

Using the Tranco list

We observe that 26.9% (i.e., more than 1 in 4) of news websites with advertorials are ranked in the top 2,000 worldwide.
Altogether, these websites receive an aggregated traffic of

Finding 2:

Advertorials are found even in the most popular news websites around the world, with websites hosted in Asia and North America being more prone to serving advertorials.
In Europe, advertorials are more restricted.

Repeated Exposure to Deceptive Advertorials:

Finally, we study the frequency with which certain advertorial domains appear as ads on news websites, identifying domains repeatedly displayed across dozens of sites. For instance, the domain

5.2
Advertorial Content & Website Structure

Next, we perform a deeper investigation into the actual content that advertorial domains serve: the text they use to persuade users to purchase a product or service.
A defining characteristic of advertorials is that they are written to resemble editorial or journalistic content.
To validate this hypothesis, we compare their text length with that of other types of websites.

We construct a corpus of 300 advertorials, 300 benign ads, and 300 news articles based on data collected during the repeated-measures study (Sec.
3.1
).
Benign ads are randomly selected from the manual annotation process (Sec.
3
), while news articles are drawn from the pages visited by our crawler.
We extract each website’s full HTML content and plain text, and in Figure
10
we plot the resulting text-length distributions for advertorials, benign ads, and news articles.

We find that advertorials contain significantly more text than both benign ads and news articles.
Two-sample KS tests across all pairs of distributions show statistically significant differences, with
p
-values close to zero (
<1e^{-6}
).
Notably, advertorial text length is closer to news articles than to benign ads, with a KS statistic of 0.22 versus 0.37.
This supports the hypothesis that advertorials are deliberately crafted to mimic editorial content rather than traditional ads.
The logarithmic
x

Further inspection shows that short-text advertorials primarily rely on embedded videos, whereas lengthy advertorials often promote or review multiple products.

Beyond the textual content presented to users, we also analyze the size of the full HTML pages.
Interestingly, although advertorials contain more on-page text than both benign ads and news articles, they exhibit the smallest HTML footprint.
The median advertorial page contains 106.3K characters:
2.7\times
smaller than the median benign ad page (284.1K) and
4.6\times
smaller than the median news article (484.8K).
This observation suggests that advertorial domains prioritize delivering user-facing text while largely avoiding complex page structures, SEO-oriented markup, or integrations with advertising and tracking services: components that typically inflate HTML size and introduce additional client-side dependencies.

To assess this hypothesis, we quantify the number of third-party entities contacted during page loads.
We visit the corpus of 300 advertorials, 300 benign ads, and 300 news articles, capturing all HTTP(S) traffic generated during the initial landing-page load.
For each website, we wait 10 seconds after page load to capture dynamic requests and automatically accept all consent banners to avoid blocking third parties.
Then, we process all HTTP(S) requests and group their domains on the eTLD+1 level.
For example, requests towards
region1.analytics.google.com
and
accounts.google.com
are all grouped under the
google.com

Figure

We find that advertorial domains interact with fewer third parties than benign ads and news articles: the median advertorial site contacts just 8 distinct third parties, compared with 12 for benign ads and 19 for news articles.
We then evaluate all third-party domains against the Disconnect list of known trackers
[

and find that the median advertorial domain interacts with 5 distinct third-party trackers.
This is still lower than the 7 trackers observed on the median benign ad domain and the 13 trackers present on the median news website.
Altogether, advertorial operators focus primarily on one-time visitors and avoid deploying additional third-party trackers that would yield benefits only for long-term or returning users.

Finding 5:

Advertorial operators prioritize the textual content of their webpages over complex designs or additional functionality.
In many cases, these pages are text-heavy and closely resemble news articles rather than benign ads.

6
Patterns of Malicious Behavior

Our analysis shows many advertorial domains display patterns associated with suspicious, malicious, or illicit websites.

Deceptive Landing Pages:
We observe that some advertorial domains have an empty or completely unrelated landing page.
When users directly type the advertorial domain name and navigate to it (rather than arriving from a third-party site, e.g., a news website), they encounter either a blank page or content that appears entirely unrelated.
For example, we identify cases where the landing page corresponds to an architectural firm, a manufacturing company, a generic privacy policy, etc., yet navigating to specific URLs within the same domain leads users to advertorials promoting health or technology products.
In some instances, advertorial domains even return a 404 error when visiting the landing page.
We attribute this behavior to attempts to conceal advertorial campaigns and avoid detection.
Similar behavior has been documented in prior work, where websites present a benign front to obscure objectionable or illicit operations (e.g.,
[
48
,
63
]
).
The most extreme case is
ushoppyworld.com
, which displays a generic WordPress template as its landing page but serves multiple distinct advertorials depending on the specific URL visited.

Content reuse:

Using perceptual hashing, we discover nearly identical images reused across different advertorials promoting the same product, including weight-loss results, product usage, and customer profile photos.
We also identify “authors” publishing advertorials on multiple seemingly unrelated advertorial domains.
For example, one author (same name and profile picture) has published different advertorials in at least 11 distinct domains.
Similarly, another author has published an identical advertorial (title, content, images, structure) on the same day in 4 different domains, all of which respond with a
404
error when users attempt to navigate to their landing page.
Manual verification shows that the listed authors are fabricated personas.
No verifiable social media or professional accounts could be linked to these “authors”, and reverse image searches revealed that their photos appear on unrelated websites.
This behavior suggests that the domains may be controlled by the same entity or a coordinated network.
A similar pattern emerges in the “user comments” sections.
The same username appears across 7 advertorial websites promoting weight-loss products.
These domains display identical comment threads with the same users in the same order and matching timestamps.
Even the user-submitted photos in the “testimonials” are identical.
These patterns indicate fabricated accounts designed to mimic engagement or approval.

Personal Data Harvesting:
We perform a manual analysis on a set of 10 advertorial domains that receive the lowest scores on the security-engine features of our content-based detection module (Sec.
3.3
).
Several of these domains are low quality and even flagged as scams or potential malware by security engines.
When users attempt to place an order for the promoted product, some domains collect sensitive personal information (name, address, email, phone number, etc.) under the pretense that “a representative will soon get in touch.”
Notably, the domain
ushoppyworld.com
, which exhibits multiple suspicious behaviors (e.g., unrelated landing page, different advertorials per URL), also follows this pattern.
Several other domains present questionnaires that request personal details such as age, weight, height, and address, ostensibly to recommend the most suitable product.
However, regardless of the responses provided, the same product is promoted, revealing a deceptive and non-personalized marketing approach.

Finding 7:

Some advertorial domains exhibit a variety of suspicious patterns, including fake comments, fake authors, cloaked (hidden) pages, image re-purposing, data harvesting, etc., that suggest malicious intent.

6.1
Dark Patterns in Legal Disclaimers

Advertorial operators may appear compliant by disclosing their intent, yet still violate regulations by mimicking news articles.
To assess this, we analyze disclaimer features in light of international regulations, with a particular focus on dark patterns: design choices that subtly steer users toward actions that benefit the service provider
[
20
]
.
We examine the legal wording that reveals the true purpose of the content (to promote a product or service) and differentiate between:
(i) disclaimers, typically written in a formal style, explicitly describing the article’s nature and the publisher’s monetary gain, and
(ii) banners, usually a single word or short phrase, labeling an article as advertorial or sponsored content.
Figure
4
shows an example disclaimer, while the most common banners we encounter are “Advertorial” or “Sponsored Content”.

Disclaimer Content:
We examine the disclaimers published by advertorial domains to disclose that the content is, in fact, an advertisement, even though it resembles editorial material.
These disclaimers are essential for advertorial operators, as they help protect their operations against legal claims.
We discover that the most common disclaimers are “
This website is an advertisement and not a news publication
” and “
the owner has a monetary connection to the product and services advertised on the site
”.
This behavior is consistent for visitors located both within and outside the EU.
The disclaimers appear on 52% and 45% of advertorial domains, respectively, for EU#2 users and 43% and 44% for USA users.
Variations of these disclaimers are also common in legal disclaimers, for instance, the disclaimer “
This is an advertisement and not an actual news article
” is found in 41% of the advertorials the USA user visited.
Interestingly, 30% and 25% of the advertorials the USA and EU#2 users came across, respectively, contain a very important disclaimer that “
This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease
”.

Disclaimer Position:

We examine the locations of banners and disclaimers on advertorial domains.
We extract all text content from advertorial pages and compute the dimensions of the HTML document along with the relative position of each text element on the page.
We intentionally filter out text that is not visible, either because it is contained within hidden elements or because elements are placed outside the visible and scrollable portion of the page.
This is a deliberate choice, as users will never encounter such text and it should therefore be excluded from further analysis.

Our analysis shows that banners are typically placed at the top of the page, while disclaimers appear at the bottom.
The median banner is located at the top 0.06% of the page (standard deviation 1.42), whereas the median disclaimer is at 96.12% from the top, i.e., at the bottom.
This placement highlights a key issue: advertorials do not prominently disclose their promotional nature, deceiving users and hindering their ability to recognize the content as advertising
[
4
]
.

Short top banners cannot convey all essential details, and bottom-page disclaimers are often overlooked, leaving users unaware of critical information, particularly medical content.
FTC guidelines stipulate that “disclosures be placed as close as possible to where users will look first.’
Advertorials frequently fail to meet this standard, placing disclaimers at the bottom of the page (even below the “Purchase” button) so users may act before noticing them.
Similarly, the UCPD prohibits providing information in an “untimely manner”, resulting in users taking action before reading the disclaimer.

Visual Presentation of Disclaimer:

The FTC guidelines also state that “disclosures should stand out, so consumers can easily read or hear them” and that they should be “in a font and color that is easy to read” and “in a shade that stands out against the background.” We study whether these guidelines are properly enforced, focusing first on the visual presentation of text.
For disclaimers, we measure the Contrast Ratio, which quantifies the difference in luminance between text and background colors.
High contrast is necessary for readability
[
58
]
.
The contrast ratio ranges from 1:1, indicating no contrast (e.g., black text on a black background), to 21:1, indicating maximum contrast (e.g., white text on a black background).
The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) by W3C require a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text (AA) and 7:1 for accessibility to users with more severe visual impairments (AAA)
[
58
]
.
Figure
13
shows the distribution of contrast ratios for all disclaimer text we detected.
Unfortunately, we find that the median disclaimer has a contrast ratio of only 4.76:1, and 41.92% of disclaimers have a contrast ratio below the W3C minimum of 4.5.
This indicates that text and background colors are too similar, making the disclaimers difficult to notice and read.
The highest contrast ratios occur in cases of white text on a black background, while the lowest occur for gray text on backgrounds of slightly different shades of gray (Figure
17
in Appendix
C
).

Disclaimer Text Prominence:

Next, we evaluate whether disclaimers or banners are clearly identifiable by users and whether they stand out from other text on the page.
To this end, we extract all text elements from each advertorial page and compute their font sizes.
Analyzing the distribution of font sizes, we find that the majority of disclaimers and banners (69%) have a font size smaller than the page’s median.
As a result, disclaimers are deliberately smaller and harder to notice, effectively functioning as “fine print.”
Surprisingly, only 5.5% of disclaimers have a larger font size, indicating that only a small fraction stand out and can be easily identified.

Finding 8:

Even though advertorials contain legal disclaimers that reveal the true intent of the website, they purposefully employ dark patterns to make these disclaimers hard to identify (e.g., small font size, faint font color, placed at the bottom of the screen).

Finally, we focus on the FTC guideline that disclaimers should be “understandable” and the UCPD requirement that information should not be presented in an “unclear, unintelligible, or ambiguous” manner.
We acknowledge that determining whether a text is understandable is not trivial, as it depends on context and audience.
However, we employ two metrics to estimate how comprehensible and intelligible disclaimers are.

For each advertorial, we split its content into “Main Text”, which contains the text promoting the product and written in journalistic form, and “Disclaimer Text”, which contains the actual legal disclosure.
Any other text element that does not constitute a legal disclosure is categorized as main text (e.g., title, date, etc.).
For each type of text, we compute the Type-Token Ratio (TTR), which measures lexical diversity.
A high TTR indicates greater vocabulary diversity, often found in academic papers or literature, while a low TTR suggests repetitive vocabulary, typically found in informal speech or simple text.
Figure
13
shows the distribution of TTR values for disclaimers compared to main advertorial text.
We find that disclaimers have higher TTR values, with a median score 34% higher than that of the main text, suggesting that disclaimers are in a more sophisticated style than the rest of the article.

To verify this, we also compute the Linsear-Write metric, which measures readability of technical texts.
This metric helps assess how accessible advertorial content is to a general audience.
We find that the median disclaimer text has a Linsear-Write score of 8.57, while the median main text scores 5.6.
These correspond to an 8th or 9th grade reading level for disclaimers, compared to a 5th or 6th grade reading level for the main advertorial content.
Consequently, the main advertorial text is easier to read and more accessible to a general audience than the more complex disclaimer text.

Finding 9:

The legal disclaimer text in advertorials is more sophisticated than the rest of the website text and may not be understandable by a general audience.

Discussion & Conclusion

Discussion & Recommendations:

Problematic advertorials follow a marketing strategy that blurs the lines between journalism and advertising to intentionally deceive users. Concerns exist regarding whether the average consumer (or demographic groups) can recognize they are interacting with a paid promotion rather than an impartial article. This misunderstanding can shape consumer opinions and behavior, reducing their ability to make informed decisions. Our findings indicate that regulations may not be sufficient to protect users from unfair marketing practices. Furthermore, advertorials are often hosted within coordinated networks to evade detection while exhibiting suspicious behavior with malicious intent.

To address the challenges posed by advertorials, a combination of policy, technological, and community-driven measures is required. Clearly defined and better-enforced regulations can have a significant impact, while increased commitment from the advertising ecosystem is needed to prevent ads from appearing on such websites. Search engines, browsers, and social media platforms can contribute by improving filtering, annotation, and warning systems to reduce user exposure. Platforms like VirusTotal (see Appendix
B
) can also support automated flagging and mitigation strategies.
Enhanced ad blockers or ML-based browser plugins that automatically detect problematic advertorials can further increase transparency and reduce exposure to hidden advertising. Together, these measures can create a more transparent and safer digital advertising ecosystem.
Together, these measures can create a more transparent and safer digital advertising ecosystem.

Geographical differences in exposure suggest that regulatory frameworks and cultural factors shape user experiences, underscoring the need for region
-
specific interventions.
Moreover, the structural and linguistic patterns we observe (including deceptive design elements and malicious behavioral cues) indicate that automated detection tools must extend beyond simple layout indicators to effectively identify problematic advertorials.
Collectively, these insights provide guidance for developing technical defenses, standardizing disclosure policies, and informing policymakers on strategies to mitigate advertorial deception at scale.

Conclusion:

Advertorials are ads intentionally designed to resemble editorial articles, subtly promoting products without the user’s awareness. In this work, we perform a large-scale, systematic study of advertorials that draw user traffic from ads served on news websites. We propose a novel detection methodology leveraging structural, semantic, and linguistic features, which accurately distinguishes advertorials from benign ads or other editorial content. We find that advertorials are both widespread and frequently published, with
\sim
35% of news websites worldwide serving at least one ad for an advertorial domain. Additionally, we uncover that some advertorial domains exhibit multiple suspicious behaviors, such as fake comments, fabricated authors, hidden pages, image reuse, and data harvesting, collectively suggesting malicious intent. Finally, our findings highlight inconsistent adherence to international regulations, with legal disclaimers being purposefully difficult to recognize due to their size and placement, while also containing sophisticated text. Overall, our work emphasizes concerns regarding the effectiveness of regulations and underscores the need for stronger consumer protection.

Appendix A
Advertorial Linguistic Tone

Advertorials differ from traditional advertisements in both tone and style.
While regular ads are direct and promotional, advertorials utilize a more informative or editorial voice, often resembling news articles or feature stories.
This approach engages the reader by providing useful content or storytelling, subtly weaving in the product or service rather than overtly selling it.
We demonstrate the distinctive difference of advertorials in Figures
14
and
15
where two similar products (an anti-wrinkle cream and an anti-aging serum) are promoted using different techniques.
In Figure
14
the traditional ad takes a direct persuasive approach.
It focuses on highlighting the benefits of the cream and urges the audience to
buy the product with concise text (i.e., “ADD TO CART”).
On the other hand, the advertorial of Figure
15

Each web engine provides raw data relevant to the target domain, which is then preprocessed to extract meaningful features.
The raw data from these sources are standardized and transformed through a series of steps including cleaning, normalization, and encoding to generate a comprehensive feature set.
To better understand the decision-making process of our model, we compute the feature importance values using Gini impurity and a random forest classifier.
Figure
16
presents the ranking of features according to their contribution to the model’s classification performance.
We observe that features coming from engines focusing on scam detection (i.e., Scam Validator, ScamAdviser) are the most important when detecting misleading websites.
Additionally, the content size of the landing page is a significant feature since it can be associated with malicious intent (Section
6
).
The feature selection process retains only features with meaningful contribution to classification, aiming to reduce noise and redundancy while preserving meaningful information.
The final dataset used for training consists of 24 features, all derived from the Web engines.

Appendix C
Examples of Dark Patterns

Users often rely on visual and textual signals in order to differentiate between editorial and commercial content and that’s why regulation explicitly requires that disclaimers are readily identifiable to indicate the promotional nature of content.
Figure
17
provides a real-world example of the
Low-Contrast
dark pattern.
In this example, the disclaimer text is displayed in a color that closely matches the background, making it harder to detect during casual browsing.
The text and background are both in slightly different shades of gray, increasing the possibility that users will overlook the fact they are reading an advertisement and not editorial content.
Next, Figure
18
illustrates how advertorials often present the disclaimer text in a significantly smaller font than the surrounding editorial text.
Users scanning the page may miss the label entirely due to its reduced size and placement at the bottom of the screen, practically turning disclaimers into the “fine-print”.
Both patterns can be regarded as dark patterns because they deliberately reduce the visibility of legally important information (i.e., disclaimers).
