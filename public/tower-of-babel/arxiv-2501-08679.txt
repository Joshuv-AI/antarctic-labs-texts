Diagonal Over-parameterization in Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces as an Adaptive Feature Model: Generalization and Adaptivity
Yicheng Li liyc22@mails.tsinghua.edu.cn, Qian Lin qianlin@tsinghua.edu.cn

Abstract
This paper introduces a diagonal adaptive kernel model that dynamically learns kernel eigenvalues and output coefficients simultaneously during training. Unlike fixed-kernel methods tied to the neural tangent kernel theory, the diagonal adaptive kernel model adapts to the structure of the truth function, significantly improving generalization over fixed-kernel methods, especially when the initial kernel is misaligned with the target. Moreover, we show that the adaptivity comes from learning the right eigenvalues during training, showing a feature learning behavior. By extending to deeper parameterization, we further show how extra depth enhances adaptability and generalization. This study combines the insights from feature learning and implicit regularization and provides new perspective into the adaptivity and generalization potential of neural networks beyond the kernel regime.

1
Introduction

Understanding the generalization capabilities of neural networks is a central problem in modern machine learning.
The remarkable success of neural networks across diverse domains, such as natural language processing and computer vision, has highlighted their ability to generalize well beyond their training data.
This phenomenon has sparked intense research interest, leading to the development of several theoretical frameworks over the past decade.
Among these, the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory has emerged as a foundational tool for analyzing the training dynamics of wide neural networks in the infinite-width regime
(
Jacot et al., 2018
)
.
NTK theory demonstrates that under specific conditions, neural networks behave like kernel methods during training, offering valuable insights into their convergence and generalization properties.
Parallel to this, an extensive literature on kernel regression (see, e.g.,
Caponnetto and De Vito (2007)
;
Yao et al. (2007)
) has studied its generalization properties, showing its minimax optimality under certain conditions and providing insights into the bias-variance trade-off.
Thus, one can almost fully understand the generalization properties of neural networks in the NTK regime by analyzing the kernel regression method.

However, the application of NTK theory to analyze neural networks, while invaluable, essentially frames the problem within a traditional statistical method by a fixed kernel.
The NTK analysis, by its reliance on the fixed kernel approximation, can not entirely account for the adaptability and flexibility exhibited by neural networks, particularly those of finite width that deviate from the theoretical infinite-width limit
(
Woodworth et al., 2020
)
.
However, empirical evidence and theoretical studies have shown that real-world neural networks exhibit dynamic changes in their feature representations during training.
This evolution, often termed
feature learning
, has been increasingly recognized as a key driver behind the adaptability and success of neural networks beyond the NTK regime
(
Wenger et al., 2023
;
Seleznova and Kutyniok, 2022
)
.

Recent studies on understanding feature learning have particularly focused the context of shallow neural networks as a random feature model.
It has been found that that even a single gradient descent step can induce significant changes in the feature space, enabling the learning of task-relevant representations
(
Ba et al., 2022
)
.
This phenomenon enables neural networks to achieve a level of generalization superior to that of fixed-kernel methods

(
Damian et al., 2022
;
Dandi et al., 2023
;
Moniri et al., 2024
;
Cui et al., 2024
;
Bordelon et al., 2024
)
.
However, these studies are mostly restricted to scenarios where the feature weights are updated with a single gradient descent step, and the output coefficients are subsequently computed through a separate ridge regression.

Another branch of research has focused on the over-parameterization nature of neural networks beyond the NTK regime, exploring how over-parameterized models trained with gradient methods can lead to
implicit regularization
and improve generalization.
Studies in this domain (e.g.,
Hoff (2017)
;
Gunasekar et al. (2017)
;
Arora et al. (2019a)
;
Kolb et al. (2023)
) have revealed that over-parameterized models, particularly those trained with gradient descent and its variants, exhibit biases towards simpler, more generalizable functions, even in the absence of explicit regularization terms.
Additionally, recent works
(
Vaškevičius et al., 2019
;
Zhao et al., 2022
;
Li et al., 2021a
)
have shown that in the setting of high-dimensional linear regression, over-parameterized models with proper initialization and early stopping can achieve minimax optimal recovery under certain conditions.
These findings highlight the advantages of over-parameterized models in transcending traditional statistical paradigms.

Building on these lines of research,
we propose to investigate
adaptive feature model

(
Zhang et al., 2024
)
, wherein the kernel, or equivalently the feature map,
evolves dynamically during training to better align with the underlying data structure.
Particularly, introducing a certain parameterization of the feature map, we can study the gradient descent on both the feature parameters and the output coefficients.
This paradigm has a two-fold interpretation:
in terms of feature learning, the parameterized feature map enables the learning of feature during training,
while in terms of implicit regularization, the extra parameters introduced in the feature map allow the model to implicitly regularize with the gradient descent dynamics.
Moreover, by considering only gradient training dynamics, we can obtain a clearer understanding of the adaptive nature during training.

In this paper, we study a
diagonal adaptive feature
model (see (
7
) and (
8
))
where the eigenvalues of the kernel and the coefficients are simultaneously learned during training,
which serve as a first concrete instantiation of the adaptive feature learning paradigm.
Under the non-parametric regression framework, we show that, by dynamically adjusting the eigenvalues of the kernel, the model can significantly improve its generalization properties compared with the fixed-kernel method.
Moreover, the adaptivity of the model comes from learning the right eigenvalues during the training process, showing a feature learning behavior.
These results provide insights into the adaptivity and generalization properties of neural networks beyond the NTK regime.

1.1
Our Contributions

This paper is an extended version of the conference paper
Li and Lin (2024)
.
In this paper, we study the generalization properties of a diagonal adaptive kernel regression model, where the eigenvalues of the kernel are learned simultaneously with the output coefficients during training.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:

Limitations of the (fixed) kernel regression.

In this work, we first investigate the limitations of the fixed kernel regression method by specific examples,
illustrating that the traditional kernel regression method suffers from the misalignment between the kernel and the truth function.
We show that even when the eigen-basis of the kernel is fixed, the associated eigenvalues, particularly their alignment with the truth function’s coefficients in the eigen-basis, can significantly affect the generalization properties of the method.

Advantages of diagonal adaptive kernel.

Focusing on the alignment between the kernel’s eigenvalues and the truth function’s coefficients,
we introduce a diagonal parameterization of the kernel and study the gradient descent dynamics on both the feature parameters and the output coefficients.
Our theoretical results show the following:

•

We show in Theorem
4
that the diagonal adaptive kernel method, with proper early-stopping, can achieve nearly the oracle convergence rate regardless of the underlying structure of the signal, significantly outperforming the vanilla fixed-kernel method when the misalignment is severe.

•

In Proposition
7
, we show that the adaptive kernel can adapt to the signal’s structure by learning the right eigenvalues during training, showing a feature learning behavior.

•

Considering a deeper parameterization in Theorem
5
, we show that adding depth can further ease the impact of the initial choice of the eigenvalues, thus improving the generalization capability of the model.

•

As the main technical improvement, we consider directly the non-parametric regression framework instead of the Gaussian sequence model approximation in the conference paper
(
Li and Lin, 2024
)
.

1.2
Related Works

Fixed kernel regression.

The regression problem with a fixed kernel has been well studied in the literature
(
Andreas Christmann, 2008
)
.
With proper regularization, kernel methods can achieve the minimax optimal rates under various conditions

(
Caponnetto and De Vito, 2007
;
Lin et al., 2018
;
Fischer and Steinwart, 2020
;
Zhang et al., 2023
)
.
More recently, a sequence of works provided more refined results on the generalization error of kernel methods

(
Bordelon et al., 2020
;
Cui et al., 2021
;
Li et al., 2023b
;
Mallinar et al., 2022
;
Li et al., 2023a
;
Li et al., 2024a
)
.

The NTK regime of neural networks.

Over-parameterized neural networks are connected to kernel methods through the neural tangent kernel (NTK) theory proposed by
Jacot et al. (2018)
,
which shows that the dynamics of the neural network at infinite width limited can be approximated by a kernel method with respect to the corresponding NTK.
The theory was further developed by many follow-up works
(
Arora et al., 2019c
;
Arora et al., 2019b
;
Du et al., 2018
;
Lee et al., 2019
;
Allen-Zhu et al., 2019
)
.
Also, the properties on the corresponding NTK have also been studied
(
Geifman et al., 2020
;
Bietti and Bach, 2020
;
Li et al., 2024b
)
.

Feature learning.

A lot of attention has been paid to the feature learning behavior of neural networks.
Starting from
Ba et al. (2022)
,
most of these works focus on shallow neural networks as a random feature model where the feature weights are firstly trained by one-step gradient descent and then the output weights are computed using ridge regression, showing the generalization properties of the feature learning behavior
(
Moniri et al., 2024
;
Cui et al., 2024
;
Bordelon et al., 2024
;
LeJeune and Alemohammad, 2023
;
Dandi et al., 2024
;
Yang and Hu, 2022
;
Damian et al., 2022
)
.
These studies reveal that feature learning manifests as adaptations in the feature matrix, often leading to rank-one “spikes” in its spectrum, which align with the structure of the target function.
Beside the random feature model, the adaptive kernel perspective has also been investigated in various particular forms
(
Woodworth et al., 2020
;
Gatmiry et al., 2021
)
.

Over-parameterization as implicit regularization.

There has been a surge of interest in understanding the role of over-parameterization in deep learning.
One perspective is that over-parameterized models trained by gradient-based methods can expose certain implicit bias towards simple solutions,
which include linear models
(
Hoff, 2017
)
,
matrix factorization
(
Gunasekar et al., 2017
;
Arora et al., 2019a
;
Li et al., 2021b
;
Razin et al., 2021
)
,
linear networks
(
Yun et al., 2021
;
Nacson et al., 2022
)

and neural networks
(
Kubo et al., 2019
;
Woodworth et al., 2020
)
.
Moreover, variants of gradient descent such as stochastic gradient descent are also shown to have implicit regularization effects
(
Li et al., 2022
;
Vivien et al., 2022
;
Pesme et al., 2021
)
.
While most of these works focus only on the optimization process and the final solution,
only a few works provided generalization guarantees for the over-parameterized models with early stopping.
Being the most relevant to our work,
they include over-parameterization in linear regression
(
Zhao et al., 2022
;
Li et al., 2021a
;
Vaškevičius et al., 2019
)
and single index model
(
Fan et al., 2021
)
.

2
Limitations of Fixed Kernel Regression

Let us consider the non-parametric regression problem given by
y=f^{*}(x)+\varepsilon
,
where
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}
is the input space with
\mu
being a probability measure supported on
\mathcal{X}

and
\varepsilon
is a mean-zero
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian noise.
The function
f^{*}(x)
represents the unknown regression function we aim to learn.
Suppose we are given samples
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, drawn i.i.d. from the model.
We denote
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{\top}
and
Y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})^{\top}
.
For an estimator
\hat{f}
of
f^{*}
, we consider the mean squared error defined as

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\left[\hat{f}(x)-f^{*}(x)\right]^{2}=\norm{\hat{f} - f^*}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\differential\mu)}^{2}
.

Let
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous positive definite kernel and
\mathcal{H}_{k}
be its associated reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS).
The well-known Mercer’s decomposition
(
Steinwart and Scovel, 2012
)
of the kernel function
k
gives

\displaystyle k(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}e_{j}(x)e_{j}(y),

(1)

where
(e_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\differential\mu)
, and
(\lambda_{j})_{j\geq 1}
are the eigenvalues of
k
in descending order.
Moreover, we can introduce the feature map (as a column vector)

\displaystyle\Phi(x)=(\lambda_{j}^{\frac{1}{2}}e_{j}(x))_{j\geq 1}:\mathcal{X}\to\ell^{2}

(2)

such that
k(x,x^{\prime})=\left\langle{\Phi(x),\Phi(x^{\prime})}\right\rangle
.
With the feature map, a function
f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
can be represented as
f(x)=\left\langle{\Phi(x),\beta}\right\rangle_{\ell^{2}}
for some
\beta\in\ell^{2}
.

Defining the empirical loss as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}(f)=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-f(x_{i}))^{2},

(3)

we can consider an estimator
\hat{f}_{t}=\left\langle{\Phi(x),\beta_{t}}\right\rangle_{\ell^{2}}
governed by the following gradient flow on the feature space

\displaystyle\dot{\beta}_{t}=-\nabla_{\beta}\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\left\langle{\Phi(x_{i}),\beta_{t}}\right\rangle_{\ell^{2}})\Phi(x_{i}),\quad\mbox{where}\quad\beta_{0}=\bm{0}.

(4)

This kernel gradient descent (flow) estimator corresponds to neural networks at infinite width limit by the celebrated neural tangent kernel (NTK) theory
(
Jacot et al., 2018
;
Allen-Zhu et al., 2019
)
.

An extensive literature
(
Yao et al., 2007
;
Lin et al., 2018
;
Li et al., 2024a
)

has studied the generalization performance of such kernel gradient descent estimator.
From the Mercer’s decomposition, we can further introduce interpolation spaces for
s\geq 0
as

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{H}_{k}\right]^{s}\coloneqq\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\beta_{j}\lambda_{j}^{\frac{s}{2}}e_{j}\;\big|\;(\beta_{j})_{j\geq 1}\in\ell^{2}\Big\},

(5)

which is equipped with the norm
\norm{f}_{[\mathcal{H}_{k}]^{s}}=\norm{\bm{\beta}}_{\ell^{2}}
for
f=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\beta_{j}\lambda_{j}^{\frac{s}{2}}e_{j}
.
Particularly, the interpolation space
[\mathcal{H}_{k}]^{1}
corresponds to the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{k}
itself.
Then, assuming the eigenvalue decay rate
\lambda_{j}\asymp j^{-\gamma}
,
the classical results (see, e.g.,
Yao et al. (2007)
;
Li et al. (2024a)
) in kernel regression state that
the optimal rate of convergence under the source condition
f^{*}\in[\mathcal{H}_{k}]^{s}
with
\norm{f^*}_{[\mathcal{H}_{k}]^{s}}\leq 1
is

n^{-\frac{s\gamma}{s\gamma+1}}
.
However, since the interpolation space
[\mathcal{H}_{k}]^{s}
is defined via the eigen-decomposition of the kernel,
the generalization performance of kernel regression methods depends substantially on the eigen-decomposition of the kernel and the expansion of the target function under the basis.
Therefore, the choice of the kernel could significantly affect the performance of the kernel method.
To demonstrate this quantitatively, let us consider the following examples.

Example 1

(Eigenfunctions in common order)
.

It is well known that kernels possessing certain symmetries, such as dot-product kernels on the sphere or translation-invariant periodic kernels on the torus,
share the same set of eigenfunctions (such as the spherical harmonics or the Fourier basis).
If we consider a fixed set of eigenfunctions
\{e_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
and a given truth function
f^{*}
,
for two kernels
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
with eigenvalue decay rates
\lambda_{j,1}\asymp j^{-\gamma_{1}}
and
\lambda_{j,2}\asymp j^{-\gamma_{2}}
respectively,
it follows that:

\displaystyle f^{*}\in[\mathcal{H}_{k_{1}}]^{s_{1}}\Longleftrightarrow f^{*}\in[\mathcal{H}_{k_{2}}]^{s_{2}}\quad\mbox{for}\quad\gamma_{1}s_{1}=\gamma_{2}s_{2}.

Given that the convergence rate is dependent solely on the product
s\gamma
, the convergence rates relative to the two kernels will be identical.

Example
1
seems to show that when the eigenfunctions are fixed,
kernel regression methods yield similar performance across different kernels.
However, it’s important to note that this similarity is due to both kernels having
the same eigenvalue decay order
,
which aligns with the predetermined order of the basis.
In fact, if the eigenvalue decay order of a kernel deviates from that of the true function,
even if the eigenfunction basis remain the same, it can lead to significantly different convergence rates.
Let us consider the following example to illustrate this point.

Example 2

(Low-dimensional structure)
.

Consider translation-invariant periodic kernels on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}=[-1,1)^{d}
,
with eigenfunctions forming the Fourier basis
\phi_{\bm{m}}(\bm{x})=\exp(i \pi\ang{\bm{m},\bm{x}})
,
where
\bm{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Within this basis, a target function
f^{*}(\bm{x})
can be represented as

f^{*}=\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f_{\bm{m}}\phi_{\bm{m}}(\bm{x})
.
Assuming
f^{*}
exhibits a low-dimensional structure, specifically
f^{*}(\bm{x})=g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d_{0}})
for some
d_{0}<d
,
and considering
g
belongs to the Sobolev space
H^{t}(\mathbb{T}^{d_{0}})
of order
t
, the coefficients
f_{\bm{m}}
can be shown to simplify to:

\displaystyle f_{\bm{m}}=\begin{cases}g_{\bm{m}_{1}},&\bm{m}=(\bm{m}_{1},\bm{0}),~\bm{m}_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d_{0}},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Let us now consider two translation-invariant periodic kernels
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
given in terms of their eigenvalues:

k_{1}
is given by
\lambda_{\bm{m},1}=(1+\norm{\bm{m}}^{2})^{-r}
for some
r>d/2
,
whose RKHS is the full-dimensional Sobolev space
H^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
;

k_{2}
is given by
\lambda_{\bm{m},2}=(1+\norm{\bm{m}}^{2})^{-r}
for
\bm{m}=(\bm{m}_{1},\bm{0})
and
\lambda_{\bm{m},2}=0
otherwise.
Then, the function
f^{*}
belongs to both
[\mathcal{H}_{k_{1}}]^{s}
and
[\mathcal{H}_{k_{2}}]^{s}
for
s=t/r
.
After reordering the eigenvalues in descending order,
the decay rates for the two kernels are identified as
\gamma_{1}=2r/d
and
\gamma_{2}=2r/d_{0}
.
Thus, the convergence rates with respect to the two kernels are respectively:

\displaystyle\frac{2t}{2t+d}\mbox{\hskip 10.22217ptand\hskip 10.22217pt}\frac{2t}{2t+d_{0}}.

Therefore, we see that when
d
is significantly larger than
d_{0}
, the convergence rate for the second kernel notably surpasses that of the first.

This example illustrates that the eigenvalues can significantly impact the learning rate, even when the eigenfunctions are the same.
In the scenario presented, the second kernel benefits from the low-dimensional structure of the target function by focusing only on the relevant dimensions,
whereas the first one suffers from the curse of dimensionality since it considers all dimensions.
The key point to take away from this example is the
alignment between the kernel and the target function
.
To generalize this example, we can consider the following example where the order of the eigenvalues does not align with the order of the target function’s coefficients.

Example 3

(Misalignment)
.

Let us fix a set of the eigenfunctions
\left(e_{j}\right)_{j\geq 1}

and expand the truth function as
f^{*}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\theta_{j}^{*}e_{j}
.
Note that by giving
\left(e_{j}\right)_{j\geq 1}
, we already defined an order of the basis in
j
,
but coefficients
\theta_{j}^{*}
of the truth function are not necessarily ordered by
j
.
Suppose that an index sequence
\ell(j)
gives the descending order of
\absolutevalue{\theta_{\ell(j)}^*}
,
namely
\absolutevalue{\theta_{\ell(1)}^*}\geq\absolutevalue{\theta_{\ell(2)}^*}\geq\dots

Then we can characterize the misalignment by the difference between
\ell(j)
and
j
.
Specifically, we assume that

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\theta_{\ell(j)}^*}\asymp j^{-(p+1)/2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\ell(j)\asymp j^{q}\quad\mbox{for}\quad p>0,~q\geq 1,

(6)

where larger
q
indicates a more severe misalignment.
In terms of eigenvalues, let us consider
\lambda_{j,1}\asymp j^{-\gamma}
, which is in the order of
j
,
while
\lambda_{\ell(j),2}\asymp j^{-\gamma}
, which is in the order of
\ell(j)
.
Then, the convergence rates with the two sequences of coefficients are respectively

\displaystyle\frac{p}{p+q}\mbox{\hskip 10.22217ptand\hskip 10.22217pt}\frac{p}{p+1}.

Therefore, the convergence rates can differ greatly if the misalignment is significant, namely when
q
is large.

From Example
2
and Example
3
,
we find that it is beneficial that
the eigenvalues of the kernel align with the structure of the target function
.
However, one can hardly choose the proper kernel a priori, especially when the structure of the target function is unknown,
so the fixed kernel regression can be limited by the kernel itself and be unsatisfactory.
Motivated by these examples, we would like to explore the idea of an “adaptive kernel approach,”
where the kernel can be learned from the data.

3
Diagonal Adaptive Kernel Regression

Motivated by the examples in the last section,
as a first step toward the adaptive kernel approach, we consider
adapting the eigenvalues of the kernel with eigenfunctions fixed
.
To this end, we first recall the fixed kernel feature map (
2
) and the kernel gradient flow (
4
).
To learn the eigenvalues, let us consider the feature map parameterized by
\bm{a}=\left(a_{j}\right)_{j\geq 1}
and the corresponding kernel:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\bm{a}}(x)=\left(a_{j}e_{j}(x)\right)_{j\geq 1},\quad k_{\bm{a}}(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{j\geq 1}a_{j}^{2}e_{j}(x)e_{j}(x^{\prime}).

(7)

For the predictor, we consider similarly
f(x)=\left\langle{\Phi_{\bm{a}}(x),\bm{\beta}}\right\rangle_{\ell^{2}}
,
where
\bm{\beta}=\left(\beta_{j}\right)_{j\geq 1}\in\ell^{2}
is the coefficient vector under the feature map.
Then, we consider the gradient flow that trains both
\bm{a}
and
\bm{\beta}
:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{\bm{\beta}}(t)&=-\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f_{t}),\quad\bm{\beta}(0)=\bm{0};\\
\dot{\bm{a}}(t)&=-\nabla_{\bm{a}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f_{t}),\quad a_{j}(0)=\lambda_{j}^{1/2},\end{aligned}\right.

(8)

where
f_{t}=\left\langle{\Phi_{\bm{a}(t)}(x),\bm{\beta}(t)}\right\rangle_{\ell^{2}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{n}
is the empirical loss defined in (
3
).
Here,
(\lambda_{j})_{j\geq 1}
are the initial eigenvalues of the kernel.
We will denote by
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{EigGD}}_{t}=f_{t}
the estimator obtained by the gradient flow (
8
) at time
t
.

First, let us introduce the following assumption that the eigenfunctions are uniformly bounded and the eigenvalues decay polynomially.
This assumption is common in the kernel methods literature
(
Steinwart et al., 2009
;
Zhang et al., 2023
)
,
and is satisfied, for example, the eigenfunctions are the Fourier basis and the kernel is associated with a Sobolev space
Fischer and Steinwart (2020)
.

Assumption 1
.

We assume that
\sup_{j\geq 1}\norm{e_j(x)}_{\infty}\leq C_{\mathrm{eigf}}

and for some
\gamma>1
,

\displaystyle c_{\mathrm{eig}}j^{-\gamma}\leq\lambda_{j}\leq C_{\mathrm{eig}}j^{-\gamma},

(9)

where
C_{\mathrm{eigf}},c_{\mathrm{eig}},C_{\mathrm{eig}}>0
are constants.

For the truth function, since
\left(e_{j}(x)\right)_{j\geq 1}
forms an orthonormal basis,
we can write the truth function as

f^{*}(x)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\theta_{j}^{*}e_{j}(x).

Then, we introduce the following quantities on the truth coefficients
(\theta_{j}^{*})_{j\geq 1}
.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} J_{\mathrm{sig}}(\delta)&\coloneqq\left\{j:\absolutevalue{\theta_{j}^*}\geq\delta\right\},\quad\Phi(\delta)\coloneqq\absolutevalue{J_{\mr{sig}}(\delta)},\\
\Psi(\delta)&=\sum_{j\notin J_{\mathrm{sig}}(\delta)}(\theta_{j}^{*})^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(\theta_{j}^{*})^{2}\bm{1}\{(\theta_{j}^{*})^{2}<\delta^{2}\}.\end{aligned}

(10)

Our assumption on the truth coefficients is as follows.

Assumption 2
.

There exists constant
B_{\infty}
such that
|\theta_{j}^{*}|\leq B_{\infty}
for all
j\geq 1
.
Moreover, there are constants
\kappa\geq 1
and
B_{\mathrm{spn}}>0
such that

\displaystyle\max J_{\mathrm{sig}}(\delta)\leq B_{\mathrm{spn}}\delta^{-\kappa},\quad\forall\delta>0.

(11)

and there is a constant
s_{0},B_{\mathrm{sig}}>0
such that

\displaystyle\Phi(\delta)\leq B_{\mathrm{sig}}\delta^{-(1-s_{0})}.

(12)

In Assumption
2
, condition (
11
) says that the span of the significant components, namely those with
\absolutevalue{\theta_j^*}\geq\delta
,
grows at most polynomially in
1/\delta
.
This assumption is mild and holds for many practical settings, such as cases considered in Example
3
(
\kappa=\frac{2q}{p+1}
for the first kernel).
In other perspective, it imposes a mild condition on the misalignment between the ordering of the truth signal and the ordering of the eigenvalues,
where
\kappa
measures the misalignment between the ordering of
\theta_{j}
and the ordering of
j
itself.
On the other hand, the condition (
12
) requires that the significant components decay fast enough such that
(\theta_{j}^{*})_{j\geq 1}
is summable,
which is also a mild regularity condition.

With these assumptions, we can now present the generalization error of the diagonal adaptive kernel regression.
The proof of the following theorem is given in Section
4
.
We will discuss the implications of this result in Subsection
3.2
.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
hold.
Then, for any
s>0
, by choosing

,
when

we have

\displaystyle\norm{f^* - \hat{f}^{\mr{EigGD}}_t}_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{n}\left[\Phi\left(n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln n}\right)\ln n+n^{\frac{1+s}{2\gamma}}\right]+\Psi\left(n^{-1/2}{\ln n}\right).

(13)

Here, the constant
C
and the hidden constants may depend on the constants in the assumptions and the choice of
s
.

3.1
Deeper Over-parameterization

In addition to the parameterized feature map in (
7
),
we can further consider adding extra parameters to the feature map to form a multilayer model.
Let us now consider the deeper parameterization

\displaystyle\Phi_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}(x)=\left(a_{j}b_{j}^{D}e_{j}(x)\right)_{j\geq 1},\quad k_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{j\geq 1}a_{j}^{2}b_{j}^{2D}e_{j}(x)e_{j}(x^{\prime}),

(14)

where
D>0
is the number of extra layers and
\bm{b}=\left(b_{j}\right)_{j\geq 1}
is the extra parameter.
We consider similarly
f(x)=\left\langle{\Phi_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}(x),\bm{\beta}}\right\rangle_{\ell^{2}}

and the gradient flow on the parameters
\bm{a}
,
\bm{b}
, and
\bm{\beta}
:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{\bm{a}}&=-\nabla_{\bm{a}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f_{t}),\quad a_{j}(0)=\lambda_{j}^{\frac{1}{2}};\\
\dot{\bm{b}}&=-\nabla_{\bm{b}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f_{t}),\quad b_{j}(0)=b_{0};\\
\dot{\bm{\beta}}&=-\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f_{t}),\quad\bm{\beta}(0)=\bm{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

(15)

Here,
b_{0}>0
is the common initialization for all
b_{j}
’s.
We denote by
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{EigGD},D}_{t}
the estimator obtained by this gradient flow (
15

.

We remark here that if one considers the over-parameterization

\Phi(x)=\left(a_{j}b_{j,1}\cdots b_{j,D}e_{j}(x)\right)_{j\geq 1}
with the same initialization

b_{j,k}=b_{0}
,
k=1,\dots,D
,
then
b_{j,k}
’s remain to be the same during the training process by symmetry,
so this is equivalent to our parameterization
\theta_{j}=a_{j}b_{j}^{D}\beta_{j}
modulo a constant factor.

Then, we have the following generalization error bound for the multilayer model.
The proof of the following theorem is deferred to Section
A
.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
hold.
Then, for any
s>0
, by choosing
t\asymp n^{\frac{D+1}{D+2}}
and
b_{0}\asymp n^{-\frac{1}{2(D+2)}}
,
when
n
is sufficiently large, with probability at least
1-C/n^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\norm{f^* - \hat{f}^{\mr{EigGD},D}_t}_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{n}\left[\Phi\left(n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln n}\right)\ln n+n^{\frac{1+s}{(D+2)\gamma}}\right]+\Psi\left(n^{-1/2}{\ln n}\right)

(16)

Here, the constant
C
and the hidden constants may depend on the constants in the assumptions and the choice of

.

3.2
Discussions

Let us discuss the implications of Theorem
4
and Theorem
5
in the following aspects.

3.2.1
Benefits of Over-parameterization

Theorem
4
and Theorem
5
demonstrate the advantage of over-parameterization in the sequence model.
Compared with the vanilla fixed-eigenvalues gradient descent method,
the over-parameterized gradient descent method can significantly improve the generalization performance by adapting the eigenvalues to the truth signal.
For a more concrete example,
if we consider the setting of (
6
), we have

\Phi(\delta)\asymp\delta^{-\frac{2}{p+1}},\Psi(\delta)\asymp\delta^{\frac{2p}{p+1}}
(for a proof, see Lemma F.2 in
Li and Lin (2024)
)
and the following corollary.

Corollary 6
.

Consider eigenvalue adaptive kernel regression in
(
8
)
or
(
15
)
.
Suppose Assumption
1
and
(
6
)
hold and
\gamma>\frac{p+1}{D+2}
.
Then, by choosing
b_{0}\asymp n^{-\frac{1}{2(D+2)}}
(if
D\neq 0
) and
t\asymp n^{\frac{D+1}{D+2}}
,
when
n
is sufficiently large, omitting the logarithmic factor, we have

\displaystyle\norm{f^* - \hat{f}^{\mr{EigGD},D}_t}_{L^{2}}^{2}=\tilde{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-\frac{p}{p+1}}).

(17)

In comparison, the vanilla kernel gradient flow method yields the convergence rate
\Theta(n^{-\frac{p}{p+q}})
.

Corollary
6
shows that the over-parameterized gradient descent method can achieve the optimal rate
n^{-\frac{p}{p+1}}
,
while the vanilla gradient descent method only achieves the rate
n^{-\frac{p}{p+q}}
.
The improvement is significant when
q
is large, which corresponds to the case that the misalignment between the ordering of the truth signal and the ordering of the eigenvalues is severe.
Moreover, if we return to the low-dimensional regression function in Example
2
with the isotropic kernel
k_{1}
,
we can see that while the vanilla gradient descent method suffers from the curse of dimensionality with the rate
\frac{2t}{2t+d}
,
the over-parameterization leads to the dimension-free rate
\frac{2t}{2t+d_{0}}
.
Therefore, the over-parameterization significantly improves the generalization performance.

3.2.2
Learning the Eigenvalues

To further investigate how the eigenvalues are adapted by over-parameterized gradient descent, we present the following proposition.

Proposition 7
.

Given the same conditions as in Theorem
4
or Theorem
5
, for the chosen stopping time
t
,
the term learning the eigenvalues
a_{k}(t)b_{l}^{D}(t)
(taking
D=0
and
b_{j}^{D}=1
for Theorem
4
) satisfies:

•

Signal component: For
\theta_{k}^{*}
such that
|\theta_{k}^{*}|\geq C_{1}n^{-1/2}\ln n
,
we have

\displaystyle a_{k}(t)b^{D}_{k}(t)\geq c_{1}\absolutevalue{\theta_k^*}^{\frac{D+1}{D+2}}.

(18)

•

Noise component: For components where
\absolutevalue{\theta_k^*}\leq n^{-1/2}
and
\lambda_{k}\leq n^{-\frac{1+s}{D+2}}
, we have

\displaystyle a_{k}(t)b^{D}_{k}(t)\leq C_{2}a_{k}(0)b_{k}^{D}(0)\exp(C_3(\sqrt{\ln n} + \sqrt{\ln k})).

(19)

Here,
c_{1},C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}
are all constants not depending on either
n
or
k
.

From this proposition, we can see that for the signal components, the eigenvalues are learned to be at least a constant times a certain power of the truth signal magnitude.
Thus, over-parameterized gradient descent adjusts the eigenvalues to match the truth signal.
In the case of noise components, although the eigenvalues are still increasing due to the training process,
the eigenvalues do not exceed the initial values by some constant factor, provided that
\lambda_{j}
is relatively small.
This finding suggests that over-parameterized gradient descent effectively adapts eigenvalues to the truth signal while mitigating overfitting to noise,
showing a feature learning behavior.
We remark that when
\lambda_{j}
is relatively large, the method still tends to overfit the noise components,
contributing an extra
n^{\frac{1}{D+2}\frac{1}{\gamma}}
term in the generalization error,
but this term becomes negligible when
\gamma
is large.

3.2.3
Adaptive Choice of the Stopping Time

A notable advantage of the adaptive kernel approach is that it does not require the knowledge of the truth parameters to choose the stopping time.
Consider the scenario described by (
6
),
vanilla gradient descent requires the selection of a stopping time
t\asymp n^{(q\gamma)/(p+q)}
to achieve the optimal convergence rate.
However, this choice of stopping time critically depends on the unknown parameters
p
and
q
of the truth parameter.
In contrast, the over-parameterized gradient descent only need to choose the stopping time as
t\asymp n^{\frac{D+1}{D+2}}
,
which does not rely on the unknown truth parameters.

3.2.4
Effect of the Depth

The results in Theorem
5
also show that deeper over-parameterization can further improve the generalization performance.
In the two-layer over-parameterization,
the extra term
n^{\frac{1+s}{2\gamma}}
in Theorem
4
emerges due to the limitation of the adapting large eigenvalues.
With the introduction of depth, namely adding extra
D
layers to the model with proper initialization,
this term can be improved to
n^{\frac{1+s}{(D+2)\gamma}}
in Theorem
5
.
This improvement suggests that the depth can refine the model’s sensitivity to eigenvalue adaptation,
enabling a more nuanced adjustment to the underlying signal structure.
This finding underscores the importance of model depth in enhancing the learning process,
providing also theoretical evidence for the empirical success of deep learning models.

3.2.5
Comparison with Previous Works

Let us compare our results with the existing literature
(
Zhao et al., 2022
;
Li et al., 2021a
;
Vaškevičius et al., 2019
)

on the generalization performance of diagonal over-parameterized gradient descent in the following aspects:

•

Problem settings:
While the existing literature
(
Zhao et al., 2022
;
Li et al., 2021a
;
Vaškevičius et al., 2019
)

investigate the realms of high-dimensional linear regression, focusing on implicit regularization and sparsity,
the present study dives into non-parametric kernel regression,
emphasizing the adaptivity of trainable kernel to the underlying signal’s structure.

•

Over-parameterization setup:

The existing work
Zhao et al. (2022)
considers the over-parameterization setup by the two-layer Hadamard product
\theta=a\odot b

where the initialization is the same for each component that
a(0)=\alpha\bm{1}
and
b(0)=\bm{0}
.
In comparison, our work considers initializing the eigenvalues
a_{j}(0)=\lambda_{j}^{1/2}
differently for each component.
Moreover, we extend the over-parameterization to deeper models by adding extra
D
layers.
Although the subsequent work
Li et al. (2021a)
of
Vaškevičius et al. (2019)
considered deeper over-parameterization,
their over-parameterization is in the form of
\theta=u^{\odot D}-v^{\odot D}
with
u(0)=v(0)=\alpha\bm{1}
.
Though being easy to analysis because of the homogeneous initialization,
this setup could not bring insights into the learning of the eigenvalues, which is a key insight in our results.
Furthermore, the analysis for Theorem
5
involves the interplay between the differently initialized
a_{j}
and
b_{j}
,
so our analysis is more involved than the existing works.
We also remark that although we only consider the gradient flow in the analysis,
the results can be extended to the gradient descent with proper learning rates.

•

Interpretation of the over-parameterization:

The previous works view the over-parameterization mainly as a mechanism for implicit regularization,
while our work provides a novel perspective that over-parameterization adapts to the structure of the truth signal by learning the eigenvalues.
As shown in Proposition
7
, the parameterization of the kernel demonstrates a feature learning behavior,
where the eigenvalues are learned to match the truth signal.

•

The restricted isometry property (RIP) condition:

The existing literature relies on the RIP condition to analyze the gradient dynamics.
In comparison, our work does not require the RIP condition by using concentration of the eigenfunctions and a delicate analysis of the gradient flow dynamics.
Moreover, we remark that as discussed in
Vaškevičius et al. (2019)
,
the RIP condition in
Zhao et al. (2022)
;
Li et al. (2021a)
is in fact too strong to hold for i.i.d. random samples.

4.1
Explicit Form of the Gradient Flow

Let
(e_{j}(x))_{j\geq 1}
be the fixed orthonormal basis.
We denote (as a column vector)
E(x)=\left(e_{j}(x)\right)_{j\geq 1}\in\ell^{2}.

Also, for the adaptive kernel regression in (
8
), we denote

\bm{\theta}(t)=\left(\theta_{j}\right)_{j\geq 1}=\bm{a}(t)\odot\bm{\beta}(t).

Then,

\displaystyle f_{t}(x)=\left\langle{\Phi_{\bm{a}(t)}(x),\bm{\beta}(t)}\right\rangle=\left\langle{E(x),\bm{a}(t)\odot\bm{\beta}(t)}\right\rangle=\left\langle{E(x),\bm{\theta}(t)}\right\rangle=E(x)^{\top}\bm{\theta}(t),

In comparison, the truth function is given similarly by
f^{*}(x)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\theta_{j}^{*}e_{j}(x)=E(x)^{\top}\bm{\theta}^{*}.

Furthermore, we denote

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}E(x_{i})E(x_{i})^{\top},\quad{\bm{r}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}E(x_{i}).

(20)

A direct computation shows that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\beta}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{\bm{\beta}(t)}E(x_{i})\left[E(x_{i})^{\top}\left(\bm{\theta}(t)-\bm{\theta}^{*}\right)-\varepsilon_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=D_{\bm{\beta}(t)}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}E(x_{i})E(x_{i})^{\top}\left(\bm{\theta}(t)-\bm{\theta}^{*}\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}E(x_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=D_{\bm{\beta}(t)}\left[{\hat{\Sigma}}\left(\bm{\theta}(t)-\bm{\theta}^{*}\right)-{\bm{r}}\right].

Similarly,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{a}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f_{t})=D_{\bm{a}(t)}\left[{\hat{\Sigma}}\left(\bm{\theta}(t)-\bm{\theta}^{*}\right)-{\bm{r}}\right].

Therefore, we derive the explicit form of the gradient flow as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{\bm{\beta}}(t)&=-\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\mathcal{L}_{n}=\bm{a}\odot\left[\hat{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta})+{\bm{r}}\right],\quad\bm{\beta}(0)=\bm{0},\\
\dot{\bm{a}}(t)&=-\nabla_{\bm{a}}\mathcal{L}_{n}=\bm{\beta}\odot\left[\hat{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta})+{\bm{r}}\right],\quad a_{j}(0)=\lambda_{j}^{1/2}.\end{aligned}\right.

(21)

4.2
Component Partition

Let us partition the components of
\bm{\theta}
into two sets: the signal components
S\subset\mathbb{N}^{+}
and the noise components
R=S^{\complement}
.
We choose
S
by

\displaystyle S=S_{1}\cup S_{2}=\left\{j\geq 1:\absolutevalue{\theta^*_j}\geq n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln n}\right\}\cup\left\{j\geq 1:\lambda_{j}\geq n^{-1/2}\right\}.

(22)

Then, by Assumption
2
, we have

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{S_1}\leq\Phi(n^{-1/2})\leq Cn^{1/2-s}\qquad\max S_{1}\leq Cn^{\kappa/2}.

Also, by Assumption
1
, we have
\absolutevalue{S_2}\leq Cn^{1/(2\gamma)}.

Noticing that
\gamma>1
, we get

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{S}\leq Cn^{(1-s_{0})/2}\qquad\max S\leq Cn^{\kappa/2}.

(23)

Moreover, Assumption
1
also yields

S_{2}^{\complement}\geq cn^{1/(2\gamma)}
, so

\displaystyle L\coloneqq\min R\geq cn^{1/(2\gamma)}.

(24)

Approximation decomposition.

We can approximate
\hat{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta})+{\bm{r}}
by the diagonalized one
\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta}
by the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta})+\bm{r}=(\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta})+\bm{p}+\bm{q}+\bm{r}=(\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta})+\bm{h}

(25)

where
\bm{h}=\bm{p}+\bm{q}+\bm{r}
,

\displaystyle\bm{p}=(\hat{\Sigma}_{\cdot S}-I_{\cdot S})(\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta})_{S},\quad\bm{q}=(\hat{\Sigma}_{\cdot R}-I_{\cdot R})(\theta^{*}-\theta)_{R},

(26)

and
I
is the infinite-dimensional identity matrix.
In detail, we obtain (
25
) by taking the difference of the following:

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\hat{\Sigma}_{\cdot S}(\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta})_{S}+\hat{\Sigma}_{\cdot R}(\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta})_{R},

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle=I_{\cdot S}(\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta})_{S}+I_{\cdot R}(\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta})_{R}.

4.3
Concentrations

The following lemma give preliminary bounds for the error terms in (
25
).

Lemma 8
.

Under assumption Assumption
1
, there are constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that with probability at least
1-C_{1}/n^{2}
,
for all
k\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{r_k}\leq C_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\ln(nk)}{n}}

(27)

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{(\hat{\Sigma}_{kR} - I_{kR}) \bm{\theta}^*_R}\leq C_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\norm{\bm{\theta}^*_R}_{1}}{n}\ln(nk)},

(28)

\displaystyle\norm{\hat{\Sigma}_{kS} - I_{kS}}_{2}\leq C_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\absolutevalue{S}}{n}\ln(nk)}

(29)

\displaystyle\norm{(\hat{\Sigma}_{kR} - I_{kR})\Lambda_R^{\hf}}_{2}\leq C_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\Tr\Lambda_{R}}{n}\ln(nk)}.

(30)

Proof.

We first establish the inequalities for fixed
k
and finally use the union bound to get the result for all
k
.
For the first inequality, we recall that

\displaystyle r_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}e_{k}(x_{i}).

Since
(x_{i},\varepsilon_{i})
s are i.i.d.,
\absolutevalue{e_k(x_i)}\leq C_{\mathrm{eigf}}
and
\varepsilon_{i}
is sub-Gaussian, we can apply Hoeffding’s inequality Lemma
31
to get
for fixed
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\absolutevalue{r_k}\geq C_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\ln(nk)}{n}}\right\}\leq\frac{C}{k^{2}n^{2}}.

For the second one, we note that

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{kR}\bm{\theta}^{*}_{R}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{k}(x_{i})\sum_{j\in R}e_{j}(x_{i})\theta^{*}_{j}.

Denoting
\xi_{i}=e_{k}(x_{i})\sum_{j\in R}e_{j}(x_{i})\theta^{*}_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\xi_i}\leq C_{\mathrm{eigf}}^{2}\sum_{j\in R}\absolutevalue{\theta^*_j}=\norm{\bm{\theta}^*_R}_{1},

and
\mathbb{E}\xi_{i}=I_{kR}\bm{\theta}^{*}_{R}
.
Therefore, the Hoeffding’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\absolutevalue{(\hat{\Sigma}_{kR} - I_{kR}) \bm{\theta}^*_R}\geq C_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\norm{\bm{\theta}^*_R}_{1}}{n}\ln(nk)}\right\}\leq\frac{C}{k^{2}n^{2}}.

For the third inequality, we note that

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{kS}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(e_{k}(x_{i})e_{j}(x_{i})\right)_{j\in S}.

Defining the random vector
v=\left(e_{k}(x_{i})e_{j}(x_{i})\right)_{j\in S}
, we have

\displaystyle\norm{v}_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j\in S}\absolutevalue{e_k(x_i) e_j(x_i)}^{2}\leq C_{\mathrm{eigf}}^{2}\absolutevalue{S}.

Consequently, using the Hoeffding’s inequality on the Hilbert space, Lemma
32
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\norm{\hat{\Sigma}_{kS} - I_{kS}}_{2}\geq 2\sqrt{\frac{\absolutevalue{S}}{n}\ln(nk)}\right\}\leq\frac{2}{n^{2}k^{2}}.

For the fourth one, similarly, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{kR}\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}_{R}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(e_{k}(x_{i})\lambda_{j}^{\frac{1}{2}}e_{j}(x_{i})\right)_{j\in R}.

Defining the random vector
w=\left(e_{k}(x_{i})\lambda_{j}^{\frac{1}{2}}e_{j}(x_{i})\right)_{j\in R}
, we have

\displaystyle\norm{w}_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j\in R}\absolutevalue{e_k(x_i) \lambda_j^{\hf} e_j(x_i)}^{2}\leq C_{\mathrm{eigf}}^{2}\sum_{j\in R}\lambda_{j}=\Tr\Lambda_{R},

so we also have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\norm{(\hat{\Sigma}_{kR} - I_{kR})\Lambda_R^{\hf}}_{2}\geq 2\sqrt{\frac{\Tr\Lambda_{R}}{n}\ln(nk)}\right\}\leq\frac{2}{n^{2}k^{2}}.

Finally, noticing that the tail probability is summable over
k
, we can apply the union bound to get the result.

∎

Proof of Theorem

\displaystyle T^{\mathrm{err}}=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\absolutevalue{\theta_k(t)}\leq 2\lambda_{k}\exp(E \sqrt{\ln n + \ln k}),\quad\forall k\in R\right\},

For

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{p_k}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\hat{\Sigma}_{kS} - I_{kS}}_{2}\norm{(\bm\theta^* - \bm\theta)_S}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{(\ref{eq:Concen_Sigma_kS})}}}{{\leq}}C\sqrt{\frac{\absolutevalue{S}}{n}\ln(kn)}\cdot\sqrt{\absolutevalue{S}}\norm{(\bm\theta^* - \bm\theta)_S}_{\infty}

On the other hand, for

\displaystyle q_{k}=(\hat{\Sigma}_{kR}-I_{\cdot R})\bm{\theta}^{*}_{R}-(\hat{\Sigma}_{kR}-I_{\cdot R})\bm{\theta}_{R}

For the second term, we use (

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{(\hat{\Sigma}_{kR} - I_{kR}) \bm{\theta}_R}

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{(\hat{\Sigma}_{kR}- I_{k R})\Lambda_R^{\hf} \Lambda_R^{-\hf}\bm{\theta}_R}\leq\norm{(\hat{\Sigma}_{kR}- I_{k R})\Lambda_R^{\hf}}_{2}\norm{\Lambda_R^{-\hf}\bm{\theta}_R}_{2}

Now, when

\displaystyle\norm{\Lambda_R^{-\hf}\bm{\theta}_R(t)}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k\in R}\lambda_{k}\exp(2E \sqrt{\ln n + \ln k})

E
.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{(\hat{\Sigma}_{kR} - I_{kR}) \bm{\theta}_R}\leq C\sqrt{\frac{\Tr\Lambda_{R}}{n}\ln(nk)}\leq C\sqrt{\frac{\ln(nk)}{n}}.

Combining with (

),
we summarize the bounds for
p_{k},q_{k},r_{k}
as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \absolutevalue{p_k}\leq\eta_{k}\norm{(\bm\theta^* - \bm\theta)_S}_{\infty},\quad\absolutevalue{q_k}\leq Cn^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln(nk)},\quad\absolutevalue{r_k}\leq Cn^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln(nk)},\end{aligned}

(32)

where we use (

) to get

\displaystyle\eta_{k}=C\absolutevalue{S}n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln(nk)}\leq Cn^{-s}\sqrt{\ln(nk)}.

4.4.1
Shrinkage Dynamics for Signal Components

For the signals
k\in S
, we use
k\leq\max S\leq Cn^{\kappa/2}
to rewrite (
32
) as

\displaystyle\norm{\bm{p}_S}_{\infty}\leq\eta\norm{(\bm\theta^* - \bm\theta)_S}_{\infty},\qquad\norm{\bm{q}_S + \bm{r}_S}_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon\coloneqq Cn^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln n}.

(33)

where we can choose
\eta=Cn^{-s_{1}}
for some small
s_{1}>0
.
However, the challenge here is that the rough bound on
\norm{\bm{p}_S}_{\infty}
via
\norm{(\bm\theta^* - \bm\theta)_S}_{\infty}\leq C

does not give the desired optimal error bound
n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln n}
.
Nevertheless, if
\norm{(\bm\theta^* - \bm\theta)_S}_{\infty}
decreases,
\norm{\bm{p}_S}_{\infty}
also decreases.
Therefore, we need the following proposition to ensure the decrease of
\norm{(\bm\theta^* - \bm\theta)_S}_{\infty}
,
which shares similar ideas with the proof in
Vaškevičius et al. (2019, Section B.3)
.

Proposition 9

(Shrinkage monotonicity and shrinkage time)
.

Suppose that

\displaystyle\norm{\bm{p}_S}_{\infty}\leq\eta\norm{\bm\theta^*_S - \bm\theta_S}_{\infty},\quad\norm{\bm{q}_S + \bm{r}_S}_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon,

for some constant
\eta\leq 1/8
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Suppose for some
t_{0}\geq 0
that

\displaystyle\norm{\bm\theta^*_S - \bm\theta_S(t_0)}_{\infty}\leq M.

Then,

\displaystyle\norm{\bm\theta^*_S - \bm\theta_S(t)}_{\infty}\leq M\vee 2\varepsilon,\quad\forall t\geq t_{0}.

(34)

Furthermore,

•

If
\eta M\geq\varepsilon
, then

\displaystyle\norm{\bm\theta^*_S - \bm\theta_S(t)}_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2}M,\quad\forall t\geq t_{0}+\overline{T}^{\mathrm{half}}(M),

(35)

where

\displaystyle\overline{T}^{\mathrm{half}}(M)=4M^{-1}\left[2+\frac{1}{2}\max_{k\in S}\left(\ln^{+}\frac{M}{\lambda_{k}}+\ln^{+}\frac{\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k}}{\lambda_{k}}\right)\right].

(36)

•

If
\eta M<\varepsilon
, then for each
k\in S
such that
\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k}\geq 4\varepsilon
, we have

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k - \theta_k(t)}\leq 4\varepsilon,\quad\forall t\geq t_{0}+\overline{T}^{\mathrm{fin}}_{k},

(37)

where

\displaystyle\overline{T}^{\mathrm{fin}}_{k}

\displaystyle\leq 4\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k}^{-1}\left(2+\frac{1}{2}\ln^{+}\frac{M}{\lambda_{k}}+\frac{1}{2}\ln^{+}\frac{\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k}}{4\lambda_{k}}+\ln^{+}\frac{\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k}}{2\varepsilon}\right).

(38)

On the other hand, for all
k\in S
we have

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k - \theta_k(t)}\leq 2\max(\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k},4\varepsilon),\quad\forall t\geq t_{0}+(2\varepsilon)^{-1}.

(39)

Proof.

The proof depends on the analysis of the one-dimensional equation established in Subsection
4.5
.
We define

\displaystyle t=\inf\left\{s\geq t_{0}:\norm{\bm\theta^*_S - \bm\theta_S(s)}_{\infty}>M\vee 2\varepsilon\right\}.

Then, we must have
\norm{\bm\theta^*_S - \bm\theta_S(t)}_{\infty}=M\vee 2\varepsilon
,
which implies that
\absolutevalue{\theta_k^* - \theta_k(t)}=M\vee 2\epsilon
for some index
k
.
The bound then gives

\displaystyle\norm{\bm{h}_S(t)}_{\infty}\leq\kappa\coloneqq\eta(M\vee 2\varepsilon)+\varepsilon<M\vee 2\varepsilon

where we note that
\eta<1/2
.
Similar to the proof of Lemma
10
, we recall (
48
) that

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{k}(t)=(a_{k}^{2}(t)+\beta_{k}^{2}(t))(z-\theta_{k}(t)+h_{k}(t)).

So,
\dot{\theta}_{k}(t)
has the same sign as
z-\theta_{k}(t)
and thus
\absolutevalue{\theta_k^* - \theta_k(t)}
must be decreasing at
t
,
which contradicts the definition of
t
.

Now, we prove the second part.
Using the bound (
34
) that we have obtained, we get

\displaystyle\norm{\bm{h}_S(t)}_{\infty}\leq\kappa,\quad\forall t\geq t_{0}.

If
\varepsilon\leq\eta M
, we have
\kappa<2\eta M\leq M/4
.
Taking
\kappa^{\prime}=M/4
in Lemma
11
and Corollary
15
,
we have
\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k - \theta_k(t)}\leq 2\kappa^{\prime}=M/2
after the maximum of

\displaystyle\left(\kappa^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\left[2+\frac{1}{2}\ln^{+}\frac{M}{\lambda_{k}}+\frac{1}{2}\ln^{+}\frac{\absolutevalue{\theta_k^*}-2\kappa^{\prime}}{\lambda_{k}}\right]\leq 4M^{-1}\left[2+\frac{1}{2}\ln^{+}\frac{M}{\lambda_{k}}+\frac{1}{2}\ln^{+}\frac{\absolutevalue{\theta_k^*}}{\lambda_{k}}\right]

or

\displaystyle\left(\kappa^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\left(1+\ln^{+}\frac{M}{\kappa^{\prime}}\right)\leq 4M^{-1}\ln 4\leq 8M^{-1}.

It shows that we have

\displaystyle\overline{T}^{\mathrm{half}}(M)\leq 4M^{-1}\left[2+\frac{1}{2}\max_{k\in S}\left(\ln^{+}\frac{M}{\lambda_{k}}+\ln^{+}\frac{\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k}}{\lambda_{k}}\right)\right].

If
\varepsilon>\eta M
, we have
\kappa<2\varepsilon
.
Taking
\kappa^{\prime}=2\varepsilon
,
when
\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k}\geq 2\kappa^{\prime}
, the result then follows from Corollary
16
where we take
\delta=2\varepsilon
.
For the last statement, we apply Corollary
18
.
∎

With the previous proposition, we can consider the following iterative shrinkage dynamics.
Let us choose
\nu_{1}=\varepsilon/\eta=Cn^{-1/2+s_{1}}\sqrt{\ln n}
.
Define
M_{i}=2^{-i}B_{\infty}
for
i=0,1,\dots,I+1
with
I=\lfloor\log_{2}(B_{\infty}/\nu_{1})\rfloor
.
Then it is clear that
I\leq\log_{2}\sqrt{n}+C
.
Since
\norm{\bm\theta^*_S - \bm\theta_S(0)}_{\infty}=\norm{\theta^*_S}_{\infty}\leq B_{\infty}
,
we use Proposition
9
iteratively to obtain that

\displaystyle\norm{\bm\theta^*_S - \bm\theta_S(t)}_{\infty}\leq M_{i+1},\quad\forall t\geq\sum_{j=0}^{i}T^{(j)},

where

\displaystyle T^{(i)}=4M_{i}^{-1}\left[2+\frac{1}{2}\max_{k\in S}\left(\ln^{+}\frac{M_{i}}{\lambda_{k}}+\ln^{+}\frac{\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k}}{\lambda_{k}}\right)\right]\leq CM_{i}^{-1}\ln n,

since
\lambda_{k}\asymp k^{-\gamma}
and
\max S\leq Cn^{\kappa/2}
.
Now, we have

\displaystyle\overline{T}\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{I}T^{(i)}\leq C\sum_{i=0}^{I}M_{i}^{-1}\ln n\leq C\nu_{1}^{-1}\ln n\leq Cn^{1/2-s_{1}}\ln n\leq Cn^{1/2}.

(40)

Finally, when
t\geq\overline{T}
, we have
\eta M_{I+1}<\varepsilon
.
Choosing
\nu_{2}=n^{-1/2}\ln n
, we use the second part of Proposition
9
to obtain that
for all
k\in J_{\mathrm{sig}}(\nu_{2})
,

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\theta_k^* - \theta_k(t)}\leq 4\varepsilon,\quad\forall t\geq\overline{T}+T^{\mathrm{fin}},

where

\displaystyle T^{\mathrm{fin}}

\displaystyle\leq C\max_{k\in J_{\mathrm{sig}}(\nu_{2})}4\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k}^{-1}\left[2+\frac{1}{2}\ln^{+}\frac{M_{I+1}}{\lambda_{k}}+\frac{1}{2}\ln^{+}\frac{\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k}}{4\lambda_{k}}+\ln^{+}\frac{\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k}}{2\varepsilon}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\nu_{2}^{-1}\ln n\leq Cn^{1/2}.

On the other hand, for
k\in S\backslash J_{\mathrm{sig}}(\nu_{2})
, we use (
39
) to get

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k - \theta_k(t)}\leq 2\max(\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k},4\varepsilon)

provided
t\geq\overline{T}+Cn^{1/2}\geq\overline{T}+(2\varepsilon)^{-1}
.
Recalling the choice of
S
in (
22
), we find that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in S\backslash J_{\mathrm{sig}}(\nu_{2})}\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k - \theta_k(t)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in S_{1}\backslash J_{\mathrm{sig}}(\nu_{2})}\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k - \theta_k(t)}^{2}+\sum_{k\in S_{2}\backslash J_{\mathrm{sig}}(\nu_{2})}\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k - \theta_k(t)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k\in S_{1}\backslash J_{\mathrm{sig}}(\nu_{2})}\absolutevalue{\theta_k^*}^{2}+C\sum_{k\in S_{2}\backslash(S_{1}\cup J_{\mathrm{sig}}(\nu_{2}))}\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\Psi(\nu_{2})+Cn^{\frac{1}{2\gamma}}\varepsilon^{2}

Consequently, we conclude that after
t\geq\underline{C}n^{1/2}
, we have

\displaystyle\norm{\bm\theta^*_S - \bm\theta_S(t)}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in J_{\mathrm{sig}}(\nu_{2})}\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k - \theta_k(t)}^{2}+\sum_{k\in S\backslash J_{\mathrm{sig}}(\nu_{2})}\absolutevalue{\theta^*_k - \theta_k(t)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\absolutevalue{S}+C\Psi(\nu_{2})+Cn^{\frac{1}{2\gamma}}\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle=C\frac{\ln n}{n}\left(\Phi\left(n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln n}\right)+n^{\frac{1}{2\gamma}}\right)+C\Psi\left(n^{-1/2}\ln n\right).

(41)

Here, we note that the constant
\underline{C}
does not depend on
E
.

4.4.2
Bounding the Noise

In this part, we will use Lemma
19

to show that
T^{\mathrm{err}}\geq\bar{C}\sqrt{n}
for some constant
\bar{C}>0
if
E
is chosen accordingly.
Since
\absolutevalue{\theta_k^*}\leq n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln n}
, it suffices to bound the term

\int_{0}^{\bar{C}\sqrt{n}}\absolutevalue{h_k(t)}\differential t
,
where we recall that
h_{k}=p_{k}+q_{k}+r_{k}
.

Let us denote
\overline{T}^{(i)}=\sum_{j=0}^{i}\overline{T}^{(j)}
.
Now, for
t\in[\overline{T}^{(i)},\overline{T}^{(i+1)}]
, using the results in the previous part, we have

\norm{\bm\theta^*_S - \bm\theta_S(t)}_{\infty}\leq M_{i}
, so the first bound in (
32
) gives

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{p_k(t)}\leq CM_{i}n^{-s}\sqrt{\ln k},\quad t\in[\overline{T}^{(i)},\overline{T}^{(i+1)}].

Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\overline{T}}\absolutevalue{p_k(t)}\differential t

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{i=0}^{I}M_{i}n^{-s}\sqrt{\ln k}\cdot T^{(i)}\leq C\sum_{i=0}^{I}M_{i}n^{-s}\sqrt{\ln k}\cdot M_{i}^{-1}\ln n

\displaystyle\leq\absolutevalue{I}n^{-s}\sqrt{\ln k}\ln n\leq C\sqrt{\ln k}.

Moreover, for
t\geq\overline{T}
, we have

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{p_k(t)}\leq\eta_{k}\norm{(\bm\theta^* - \bm\theta)_S}_{\infty}\leq Cn^{-s}\sqrt{\ln(nk)}\cdot M_{I+1}\leq Cn^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln k},

so

\displaystyle\int_{\overline{T}}^{\bar{C}\sqrt{n}}\absolutevalue{p_k(t)}\differential t\leq C\bar{C}\sqrt{\ln k}.

On the other hand, the other two bounds in (
32
) gives
\absolutevalue{q_k + r_k}\leq Cn^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln n+\ln k}
,
so

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\bar{C}\sqrt{n}}\absolutevalue{q_k(t) + r_k(t)}\differential t\leq C\bar{C}\sqrt{\ln n+\ln k}.

Combining the three bounds, we prove the claim.

4.4.3
Generalization Error

From (
41
) and Subsection

,
we can choose a constant
\bar{C}\geq\underline{C}
and then fix the constant

.
Now, when

(42)

\displaystyle\norm{f^* - \hat{f}_t^{\mr{EigGD}}}_{L^{2}}^{2}=\norm{\bm\theta^* - \bm\theta(t)}_{2}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{n}\left[\Phi\left(n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln n}\right)\ln n+n^{\frac{1+s}{2\gamma}}\right]+\Psi\left(n^{-1/2}\ln n\right).

4.5
One-dimensional Dynamics

In this subsection, we will focus on the one-dimensional dynamics of the gradient flow with perturbation.
Let
h(t):\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous perturbation function.
Let us consider now the following perturbed one-dimensional dynamics:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{\beta}(t)&=a(t)\left[z-\theta(t)+h(t)\right],\quad\beta(0)=0,\\
\dot{a}(t)&=\beta(t)\left[z-\theta(t)+h(t)\right],\quad a(0)=\lambda^{1/2},\end{aligned}\right.

(44)

where
\theta(t)=a(t)\beta(t)
.

Conservation Quantities.

Computing the time derivative of
a^{2}(t)
and
\beta^{2}(t)
, we have

\displaystyle\derivative{t}a^{2}(t)=\derivative{t}\beta^{2}(t)=2a(t)\beta(t)\left[z-\theta(t)+h(t)\right],

so we have the conservation quantity that

\displaystyle a^{2}(t)-\beta^{2}(t)=a^{2}(0)-\beta^{2}(0)=\lambda,

(45)

so
a(t)=(\lambda+\beta^{2}(t))^{1/2}
and thus

\displaystyle\max(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}},\absolutevalue{\beta(t)})\leq a(t)\leq\sqrt{2}\max(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}},\absolutevalue{\beta(t)}),

(46)

\displaystyle\max(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}},\absolutevalue{\beta(t)})\absolutevalue{\beta(t)}\leq\absolutevalue{\theta(t)}\leq\sqrt{2}\max(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}},\absolutevalue{\beta(t)})\absolutevalue{\beta(t)}.

(47)

The evolution of
\theta
.

It is direct to compute that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dot{\theta}&=\dot{a}\beta+a\dot{\beta}=\left[\beta^{2}+a^{2}\right](z-\theta+h)\\
&=\theta^{2}(a^{-2}+\beta^{-2})(z-\theta+h).\end{aligned}

(48)

Lemma 10

(Perturbation bound)
.

Consider
(
44
)
.
Let
t_{0}\geq 0
.
Suppose there exists some
\kappa>0
such that
\absolutevalue{h(t)}\leq\kappa
for all
t\geq t_{0}
.
Then, for
t\geq t_{0}
,

(i)

\absolutevalue{z - \theta(t)}
is decreasing provided that
\absolutevalue{z - \theta(t)}\geq\kappa
.

(ii)

Once
\absolutevalue{z - \theta(t_1)}\leq\kappa
for some
t_{1}\geq t_{0}
,
we have
\absolutevalue{z - \theta(t)}\leq\kappa
for all
t\geq t_{1}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
z\geq 0
.
We recall in (
48
) that

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}=\theta^{2}(a^{-2}+\beta^{-2})(z-\theta+h).

Now, if
\absolutevalue{z - \theta(t)}\geq\kappa
, we have either
\theta(t)\leq z-\kappa
or
\theta(t)\geq z+\kappa
.
For the first case, we have
\dot{\theta}\geq 0
so
\theta(t)
is increasing, which implies that
\absolutevalue{z - \theta(t)}=z-\theta(t)
is decreasing.
Similarly, for the second case, we have
\dot{\theta}\leq 0
and thus
\absolutevalue{z - \theta(t)}
is also decreasing.
Consequently, we have the first part of the proposition.
The second part is just a consequence of the first part.
∎

Lemma 11

(Approaching from below, large
\kappa
)
.

Consider the equation
(

)
with
z\geq 0
(a similar result holds for

Proof.

for all

in (

and thus

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}z(z-\kappa-\theta),\quad\mbox{if}\quad\theta(t_{0})\leq z-\kappa,

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{4}z(\theta-z-\kappa),\quad\mbox{if}\quad\theta(t_{0})\geq z+\kappa.

It shows that for

Appendix A
Proof for Multilayer Model

In this section, we provide the proof for the multilayer model,
which resembles the proof for the two-layer model in Section
4
but is more complicated.

Let us also denote
E(x)=\left(e_{j}(x)\right)_{j\geq 1}
as a column vector.
In this section, we denote

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}(t)=\left(\theta_{j}\right)_{j\geq 1}=\bm{a}(t)\odot\bm{b}^{D}(t),

(53)

where we use
\bm{b}^{D}
to denote the element-wise power of
\bm{b}
.
Then, similar to the single-layer model, we have

\displaystyle f(t)=\left\langle{\Phi_{\bm{a}(t),\bm{b}(t)}(x),\bm{\beta}(t)}\right\rangle=\left\langle{E(x),\bm{a}(t)\odot\bm{b}^{\odot D}\odot\bm{\beta}(t)}\right\rangle=\left\langle{E(x),\bm{\theta}(t)}\right\rangle=E(x)^{\top}\bm{\theta}(t),

Then, following Subsection
4.1
, we can derive the gradient flow of the multilayer model:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{\bm{a}}&=\bm{b}^{D}\odot\bm{\beta}\odot\bm{\Delta},\quad a_{j}(0)=\lambda_{j}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
\dot{\bm{b}}&=D\bm{a}\odot\bm{b}^{D-1}\odot\bm{\beta}\odot\bm{\Delta},\quad b_{j}(0)=b_{0}\\
\dot{\beta}&=\bm{a}\odot\bm{b}^{D}\odot\bm{\Delta},\quad\bm{\beta}(0)=\bm{0},\end{aligned}\right.

(54)

where

\displaystyle\Delta\coloneqq\hat{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta})+\bm{r},\quad\hat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}E(x_{i})E(x_{i})^{\top},\quad\bm{r}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}E(x_{i}).

A.1
Equation Analysis for Multilayer Model

Let us consider the perturbed one-dimensional equation for the multilayer model.
Let
h(t):\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous perturbation function.
Let us consider now the following perturbed one-dimensional dynamics:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{a}&=b^{D}\beta\left(z-\theta+h\right),\quad a(0)=\lambda^{1/2}>0,\\
\dot{b}&=Dab^{D-1}\beta\left(z-\theta+h\right),\quad b(0)=b_{0},\\
\dot{\beta}&=ab^{D}\left(z-\theta+h\right),\quad\beta(0)=0,\end{aligned}\right.

(55)

where
\theta(t)=a(t)b^{D}(t)\beta(t)
.

Conservation quantities.

It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\derivative{t}a^{2}=\frac{1}{D}\derivative{t}b^{2}=\derivative{t}\beta^{2}=2ab^{D}\beta\left(z-\theta+h\right).

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle a^{2}(t)-\beta^{2}(t)\equiv\lambda,\qquad b^{2}(t)-D\beta^{2}(t)\equiv b_{0}^{2}.

(56)

Using this, we see that

\displaystyle a(t)=\left(\beta^{2}(t)+\lambda\right)^{1/2}>0,\qquad b(t)=\left(D\beta^{2}(t)+b_{0}^{2}\right)^{1/2}>0.

(57)

Using these conservation quantities, we can prove the following estimations in terms of
\beta
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\max(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}},\absolutevalue{\beta})\leq a\leq\sqrt{2}\max(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}},\absolutevalue{\beta}),\\
&\max(b_{0},\sqrt{D}\absolutevalue{\beta})\leq b\leq\sqrt{2}\max(b_{0},\sqrt{D}\absolutevalue{\beta}),\end{aligned}

(58)

which also implies that
\absolutevalue{\theta}=\absolutevalue{ab^D\beta}\geq D^{D/2}\absolutevalue{\beta}^{D+2}
.

The evolution of
\theta
.

It is direct to compute that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dot{\theta}&=\dot{a}b^{D}\beta+aDb^{D-1}\dot{b}\beta+ab^{D}\dot{\beta}\\
&=\left[(b^{D}\beta)^{2}+(Dab^{D-1}\beta)^{2}+(ab^{D})^{2}\right](z-\theta+h)\\
&=\theta^{2}(a^{-2}+Db^{-2}+\beta^{-2})(z-\theta+h).\end{aligned}

(59)

The following estimation of the equation will be frequently used in the proof.
Using (
58
), we have

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\theta}=\absolutevalue{ab^D\beta}\geq D^{D/2}\absolutevalue{\beta}^{D+2},\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\absolutevalue{\beta}\leq\left(D^{-D/2}\absolutevalue{\theta}\right)^{1/(D+2)}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\theta^{2}(a^{-2}+Db^{-2}+\beta^{-2})\geq\theta^{2}\beta^{-2}\geq D^{-\frac{D}{D+2}}\absolutevalue{\theta}^{\frac{2D+2}{D+2}}.

(60)

The following is the standard Hoeffding’s inequality for sub-Gaussian random variables, see, e.g.,
Vershynin (2018, Theorem 2.6.2)
.

Lemma 31
.

Let
X,X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be i.i.d.
K
-sub-Gaussian random variables.
Then, for every
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\absolutevalue{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i - \E X}\geq t\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{cnt^{2}}{K^{2}}\right),

where
c>0
is an absolute constant.

The following lemma gives a Hilbert space version of Hoeffding’s inequality, see, e.g.,

Lemma 32

Results on ordinary differential equations

Proposition 33
.

Let
k>0,p>1
.
Consider the ODE
\dot{x}\geq kx^{p}
,
x(0)=x_{0}>0
,

\displaystyle x(t)\geq\left(x_{0}^{-(p-1)}-(p-1)kt\right)^{-\frac{1}{p-1}},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\inf\left\{t\geq 0:x(t)\geq M\right\}\leq\left[(p-1)kx_{0}^{p-1}\right]^{-1},\quad M\geq 0.
