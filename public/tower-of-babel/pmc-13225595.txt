Association between lactate-to-albumin ratio and all-cause mortality in critically ill hepatic failure patients with sepsis: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jinfeng Li, Shifeng Pang, Ximan Ye, Huiya Huang, Jianlin Wu, Diefei Hu, Maowei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatic failure complicated by sepsis is a highly lethal condition with an extremely high fatality rate. The lactate-to-albumin ratio (LAR) has been identified as a predictor of adverse outcomes in various diseases. However, the prognostic value of LAR in critically ill hepatic failure patients with sepsis remains to be defined. Participants were selected from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV database and categorized into tertiles according to the LAR index. The primary outcomes were 30-day and 365-day all-cause mortality. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to visualize survival differences across tertiles. Cox proportional hazards regression and restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were employed to characterize the relationship between the LAR index and all-cause mortality; subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential interactions. A total of 1346 patients were included; all-cause mortality rates at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days were 43.16%, 53.05%, 55.72%, and 58.99%, respectively. Kaplan–Meier curves confirmed that survival at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days decreased significantly across increasing LAR tertiles. Adjusted multivariable Cox regression showed that, compared with the lowest tertile, the highest LAR tertile had an 84% higher 30-day mortality risk (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.84; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.43–2.36; P < .001) and a 63% higher 365-day mortality risk (HR = 1.63; 95% CI: 1.32–2.01; P < .001). RCS analysis verified a positive, nonlinear association between the LAR index and all-cause mortality at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days (all nonlinear P < .001). Subgroup analysis indicated a stronger association between LAR and mortality in non-elderly patients. In critically ill hepatic failure patients with sepsis, elevated LAR is independently associated with increased all-cause mortality. This highlights the potential of LAR as a key prognostic biomarker for risk stratification and management of hepatic failure patients with sepsis in the intensive care unit.

1. Introduction

Hepatic failure is a common and severe hepatic syndrome in clinical practice, with an extremely high fatality rate.[1,2] It results from diverse etiologies, including viral hepatitis, drug-induced liver injury, alcoholic liver disease, and autoimmune hepatitis, and is characterized by massive hepatocyte necrosis or severe functional impairment, leading to abrupt declines in hepatic synthesis, detoxification, metabolism, and biotransformation, followed by clinical manifestations of coagulopathy, jaundice, hepatic encephalopathy, and multiple organ failure.[3,4] Studies report that in-hospital mortality of hepatic failure ranges from 23% to 53%, while 28-day mortality in severe cases reaches 77% to 86%; furthermore, up to 53% of patients die by day 90.[5–8] Infection is a common trigger, and affected patients are highly prone to sepsis, which substantially worsens clinical outcomes and increases mortality.[9,10] Severity and prognosis can be assessed with scoring systems such as the Chronic Liver Failure Consortium (CLIF-C) ACLF score,[11] Child Turcotte Pugh (CTP) score,[12] and the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) and its derivatives.[13] However, these scores appear less effective once infection supervenes, and their requirement for multiple variables may inadvertently delay treatment initiation, increasing the risk of poor outcomes; thus, their predictive value remains limited and performance suboptimal. Consequently, a simple, cost-effective, and highly sensitive marker is urgently needed to quantify disease severity and predict both short- and long-term outcomes.

Lactate is a marker of tissue hypoxia and inflammation, and its level is frequently elevated in both hepatic failure and sepsis.[14] Lactate independently predicts outcome in patients with hepatic failure or sepsis.[15–17] Serum albumin, the most abundant circulating protein, exerts anti-inflammatory, antioxidant effects and serves as a key indicator of nutritional status and inflammatory response.[18,19] Recently, combining these parameters has led to the development of a novel inflammatory biomarker, the LAR, that more comprehensively reflects the patient’s pathophysiological status.[20,21] Multiple studies have identified LAR as a prognostic biomarker across diverse conditions, including sepsis,[22] acute respiratory distress syndrome,[23] cirrhosis,[24] coronary heart disease,[25] and critically ill patients with acute kidney injury (AKI) undergoing CRRT.[26] Nevertheless, its prognostic value in critically ill hepatic failure patients with sepsis has not been investigated. Using data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV) database, this study aimed to examine the association between LAR and all-cause mortality among intensive care unit (ICU) patients with hepatic failure complicated by sepsis. This retrospective cohort analysis expands prognostic options for hepatic failure patients with sepsis and offers a novel perspective for future mechanistic and therapeutic investigations of this critical condition.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Database source

Data were extracted from the MIMIC-IV (version 3.1), a large, publicly available database developed and maintained by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology Laboratory for Computational Physiology (https://physionet.org/content/mimiciv/3.1/). A retrospective analysis of MIMIC-IV (v3.1) was conducted to examine the association between the lactate-to-albumin ratio and outcomes in critically ill hepatic failure patients with sepsis; the database provides comprehensive, high-granularity data on well-defined and fully characterized patients admitted to the ICU of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) between 2008 and 2022, This database has obtained ethical approval from Massachusetts Institute of Technology and BIDMC (2001P-001699/14).[27] To protect patient privacy, all personally identifiable information has been de-identified and replaced with randomly assigned codes. Owing to this anonymization process, no additional ethical approval or individual informed consent was required for the present study. To obtain access, one author (Jinfeng Li) completed the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative program and passed the examinations on “Conflict of Interest” and “Data or Specimens Only Research” (certification number 14347715).

2.2. Study design and population

This study included adult patients (age > 18 years) diagnosed with hepatic failure complicated by sepsis. Hepatic failure was diagnosed based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision-10 codes (K7040, K7041, K7200, K7201, K7210, K7211, K7290, K7291, and K9182). Sepsis was identified according to Sepsis-3 criteria: infection with a Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score ≥ 2.[28] Patient selection consistent with Sepsis-3 was performed as described previously.[29] Exclusion criteria were ICU length of stay <24 hours; multiple ICU admissions for hepatic failure with sepsis, only the first admission was retained; missing lactate or serum albumin within 24 hours of admission. Ultimately, 1346 patients were included and divided into 3 groups based on tertiles of the LAR index (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of patient selection. ICU = intensive care unit, MIMIC-IV = Medical Information Mart in Intensive Care-IV, LAR = lactate-to-albumin ratio.

2.3. Demographic and laboratory variables

We used Structured Query Language via Navicat Premium (version 15.0.12) to extract data from the MIMIC-IV database. Extracted variables comprised demographics (age, gender, race, height, and weight), vital signs (temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, mean arterial pressure [MBP]), initial laboratory values (white blood cell count [WBC], red cell distribution width [RDW], partial thromboplastin time [PTT], alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate transaminase [AST], total bilirubin [TBIL], lactate, albumin, etc), comorbidities (AKI, liver cirrhosis, etc), severity scores (Acute Physiology Score III [APSIII] , SOFA, etc), and supportive therapies (vasoactive agent, invasive mechanical ventilation [IMV], and continuous renal replacement therapy [CRRT]). For variables measured more than once, the first recorded value was used. Missing data were handled by multiple imputation; variables with > 20% missing values were excluded.

2.4. LAR assessment and outcomes

The LAR was defined as follows: LAR = lactate (mmol/L)/ albumin (g/dL). The primary outcomes of this study were all-cause mortality at 30 and 365 days. Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality at 90 and 180 days.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed first; normally distributed data were analyzed using Student t test or 1-way ANOVA and expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables, expressed as absolute numbers and percentages, were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate.

Patients were stratified into 3 groups based on tertiles of the LAR. Kaplan–Meier (K–M) curves were used to estimate the incidence of outcome events across LAR tertiles, with differences assessed using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the association between LAR and outcomes, with adjustments across multiple models. To mitigate multicollinearity-induced overfitting, variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated, and variables with VIF ≥ 5 were excluded. Relevant clinical and prognostic variables were incorporated into multivariable models as follows: Model 1 included LAR alone; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, and race; Model 3 additionally adjusted for cirrhosis, AKI, vasoactive agent, IMV, and CRRT; Model 4 further adjusted for WBC, RDW, PTT, ALT, AST, anion gap, fibrinogen, Urea nitrogen, creatinine, Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS), and Glasgow Coma Scale score. LAR was entered into the models as both a continuous variable and a categorical variable (with the lowest tertile as the reference group). P-values for trend were calculated by modeling LAR tertiles as an ordinal variable. The dose–response relationship between LAR and the risk of primary and secondary outcomes was further examined using RCS analysis, with LAR modeled as a continuous variable.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corporation, USA) and R (version 4.5.0; R Foundation). A 2-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics of study subjects

A total of 1346 critically ill patients with liver failure and sepsis met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. The median (IQR) age was 60.00 (50.00–70.75) years, and 59.14% of the participants were male. Baseline characteristics of the study participants, stratified by admission LAR tertiles (T1: <0.73, T2: 0.73–1.49, T3: >1.49), are summarized in Table 1. From T1 to T3, WBC, glucose, anion gap, lactate, AST, ALT, PT, PTT, INR, respiratory rate and heart rate increased, whereas ALB, total calcium, pH, fibrinogen, urea nitrogen and MBP decreased. Clinical severity, as reflected by SOFA, APS III, and OASIS scores, escalated across increasing LAR tertiles. Moreover, the prevalence of AKI rose progressively from T1 to T3. All-cause mortality was 43.16%, 53.05%, 55.72% and 58.99% at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days, respectively, and was consistently higher in the highest LAR tertile.

3.2. Association between LAR and mortality

Figure 2 presents Kaplan–Meier survival analyses evaluating the association between the LAR and mortality in critically ill hepatic failure patients with sepsis. Kaplan–Meier curves revealed significant differences in survival probability across the 3 LAR tertiles (all log-rank P < .001; Figure 2A–D), with a stepwise decrease in 30-, 90-, 180-, and 365-day survival as the LAR increased.

Kaplan–Meier analyses of critically ill hepatic failure patients with sepsis, stratified by LAR tertiles, for (A) 30-day, (B) 90-day, (C) 180-day, and (D) 365-day mortality. LAR = lactate-to-albumin ratio; tertiles: T1 < 0.73, T2 0.73–1.49, T3 > 1.49.

To assess the independent effect of the LAR on mortality in critically ill hepatic failure patients with sepsis, we constructed 4 Cox regression models (Table 2). When modeled as a continuous variable, the LAR was significantly associated with 30-, 90-, 180-, and 365-day mortality (all P < .001), with corresponding hazard ratios indicating 22%, 21%, 20%, and 19% increases in risk, respectively. After full adjustment in Model 4, the LAR remained independently associated with mortality (all P < .001), conferring an additional 12% increase in risk at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days. Furthermore, when the LAR was analyzed as tertiles in Model 1, patients in the highest tertile exhibited a higher risk of all-cause mortality at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days than those in the lowest tertile. After adjusting for Models 2, 3, and 4, a consistent trend was still observed. Specifically, in Model 4, patients in T3 had 84%, 68%, 70% and 63% higher risks of death at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days, respectively, compared with those in T1. In summary, mortality increased in a stepwise manner across rising LAR levels (P for trend < .001). All covariates exhibited VIF < 5, indicating the absence of meaningful multicollinearity and confirming the robustness of the multivariable models.

3.3. LAR and RCS analysis in patients with hepatic failure complicated by sepsis

Adjusted RCS analysis illustrated in Figure 3 showed a positive association between the LAR and the risk of all-cause mortality at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days (all P < .001), with a monotonic increase in risk as the LAR rose in patients with liver failure and sepsis. Notably, this relationship was nonlinear for each endpoint (all nonlinear P < .001).

Restricted cubic spline analyses of the relationship between the LAR and mortality risk in critically ill patients with liver failure and sepsis. The solid and dashed lines represent the predicted risk and 95% confidence interval, respectively. (A) 30-day mortality, (B) 90-day mortality, (C) 180-day mortality, (D) 365-day mortality. LAR = lactate-to-albumin ratio.

We then employed a 2-piece Cox proportional-hazards model to explore the nonlinear relationship between the LAR and all-cause mortality. Inflection points for 30-, 90-, 180-, and 365-day mortality were 0.89, 0.81, 0.90, and 0.90, respectively (Table 3). Below an LAR of 0.8, each 1-unit increase was associated with a 4.34-, 4.03-, 3.16-, and 3.17-fold increase in the risk of 30-, 90-, 180-, and 365-day mortality, respectively (HR 5.34; 95% CI 2.26–12.63; HR 5.03; 95% CI 1.96–12.92; HR 4.16; 95% CI 2.05–8.42; and HR 4.17; 95% CI 2.12–8.22). Above an LAR of 0.9, the association became nonsignificant (P = .315, .260, .306, and .301 for 30-, 90-, 180-, and 365-day mortality, respectively).

3.4. Subgroup analyses

Adjusted forest plots were used to illustrate the association between the LAR and 30-, 90-, 180-, and 365-day mortality across clinically relevant subgroups of patients with liver failure and sepsis (Fig. 4). A positive association between the LAR and all-cause mortality was observed irrespective of age, sex, race, treatment modalities (IMV, CRRT), or presence of cirrhosis. Interaction tests indicated that the relationship between the LAR and mortality differed significantly across age strata. Compared with patients aged ≥ 60 years, the positive association was more pronounced among those < 60 years at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days. No significant interactions were detected in the remaining subgroups.

4. Discussion

This study investigated the association between the LAR and all-cause mortality in critically ill hepatic failure patients with sepsis. Analysis of the MIMIC-IV database revealed a strong link between elevated LAR and increased mortality risk. Higher LAR values remained consistently associated with greater 30-, 90-, 180-, and 365-day mortality even after adjustment for potential confounders. Kaplan–Meier curves showed significant differences in survival probability across LAR tertiles. RCS analyses indicated a significant nonlinear relationship between rising LAR and increased mortality risk at all time points; threshold analyses further demonstrated that patients with LAR < 0.8 had significantly higher mortality than those with LAR > 0.9. Our findings demonstrate that an elevated LAR is associated with increased short- and long-term mortality, with subgroup analyses indicating that this association is more pronounced in patients aged < 60 years.

In a spectrum of conditions, including sepsis, the LAR predicts survival more accurately than either lactate or albumin alone.[30,31] LAR has been reported to be an independent predictor of 28-day mortality in critically ill patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome, with discriminative power comparable to that of the SAPS II, which incorporates multiple physiologic and clinical variables.[23] Ma et al demonstrated a positive, nonlinear relationship between the LAR and both short- and long-term all-cause mortality in critically ill cirrhotic patients with sepsis.[32] Sun et al found a significant and positive linear relationship between the LAR and 3-month readmission risk in patients with heart failure.[33] Moreover, the LAR has been successfully validated as a prognostic marker across a broad range of disorders, including sepsis,[22] acute pancreatitis,[20] heart failure with chronic kidney disease,[34] cirrhosis,[35] and intracerebral hemorrhage.[36] In sepsis-comorbid conditions, the LAR remains a robust predictor of outcome, serving as a powerful indicator for sepsis-associated liver injury,[37] abdominal sepsis,[38] sepsis-associated encephalopathy,[39] and sepsis-associated acute kidney injury.[40] These findings underscore the broad clinical utility of the LAR in liver failure patients with sepsis. In the present cohort of 1346 critically ill patients, we observed that higher LAR values were significantly associated with both short- and long-term mortality and reflected worse clinical outcomes. Our results support the use of the LAR as a readily available prognostic indicator in this high-risk population.

Lactate, the end-product of anaerobic glycolysis, mirrors the severity of cellular hypoxia and inadequate tissue perfusion. In sepsis, microcirculatory failure and tissue hypoxia drive excessive anaerobic glycolysis, leading to lactate accumulation that, via activation of G-protein-coupled receptor 81 (GPR81) and histone lactylation, promotes M1 macrophage polarization and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, thereby creating a self-amplifying loop that exacerbates systemic inflammation.[41–43] Concurrently, liver failure impairs gluconeogenesis and reduces lactate clearance, further elevating circulating lactate levels. Thus, serum lactate concentrations reflect both the degree of tissue hypoxia and the extent of hepatic injury, a constellation commonly observed in patients with liver failure complicating sepsis. The liver is the principal site of albumin synthesis; hepatic failure severely compromises this function, resulting in marked hypoalbuminemia.[44] When both conditions coexist, lactate rises further while albumin declines, additional hepatic failure-related Kupffer-cell dysfunction limits lactate clearance; sepsis-induced hepatic microcirculatory failure aggravates hepatocellular hypoxia and mitochondrial injury, further curtailing albumin synthesis; and endothelial injury-driven capillary leak exacerbates hypoalbuminemia.[45–47] Although elevated lactate and decreased albumin both signal compromised compensatory capacity and poorer prognosis, either biomarker alone is influenced by multiple confounders and exhibits limited accuracy in predicting mortality. By integrating lactate and serum albumin, the LAR simultaneously captures inflammatory and nutritional pathophysiology. In the present study, rising LAR values were significantly associated with increased mortality risk, suggesting that this composite biomarker may provide superior risk stratification for patients with liver failure complicating sepsis. Consequently, routine monitoring of the LAR should be integrated into clinical practice to refine prognostication in this high-risk population.

In our subgroup analyses, the association between the LAR and mortality was pronounced in patients < 60 years of age, whereas no significant relationship was observed in those ≥ 60 years (P for interaction = .002). These findings indicate that age may be an important modifier of the prognostic value of the LAR. Mechanistically, older adults frequently present with complex metabolic disturbances, immunosenescence, and diminished multi-organ reserve, which may obscure or dilute the signals of tissue hypoperfusion and hepatic dysfunction reflected by an elevated LAR. Conversely, younger patients retain relatively intact compensatory mechanisms, so an elevated LAR is more likely to indicate reversible early shock or hepatic impairment, resulting in a stronger association with mortality. Consistent with previous reports, lactate-based indices exhibit stronger prognostic value in younger individuals.[48] Clinically, these observations suggest that an elevated LAR should trigger heightened vigilance and potentially more aggressive resuscitation or interventions in younger patients with liver failure complicating sepsis, whereas its prognostic utility in older patients is limited and should be interpreted alongside additional measures such as frailty or organ-function scores. We caution that subgroup findings may be influenced by inter-group heterogeneity and require validation in future studies.

Our study highlights the potential of the LAR as a prognostic marker in patients with liver failure complicated by sepsis. Compared with existing prognostic scores such as CLIF-C, ACLF, CTP, and MELD, the LAR is simpler to obtain and less expensive. Unlike traditional biomarkers, the LAR integrates inflammatory and nutritional information, offering a more comprehensive snapshot of a patient’s physiological status. Moreover, the stepwise increase in mortality across LAR tertiles underscores its utility as a rapid risk-stratification tool that can promptly identify high-risk patients. Its ease of acquisition and minimal cost make it particularly valuable in resource-limited settings. The use of the MIMIC-IV database ensured a large and diverse patient cohort, supporting robust statistical analyses. Our findings indicate that the LAR is a powerful adjunct to existing prognostic tools in this population and could be instrumental in clinical decision-making.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. All data were derived from the MIMIC-IV database, which originates from a single U.S. academic medical center (BIDMC); thus, external validity across different regions, hospitals, or ethnic populations remains to be confirmed. Second, the retrospective design precludes the establishment of causality and may introduce inherent observational biases. Larger, multi-center datasets are needed to extend subgroup analyses and enhance the robustness of our findings. In addition, this study only included LAR measurements obtained within 24 hours of patient admission and did not analyze its dynamic change characteristics. Future multicenter prospective cohort studies can be conducted to systematically collect data on the dynamic changes of LAR (e.g., clearance over 48 hours), so as to further verify its predictive value for all-cause mortality in critically ill hepatic failure patients with sepsis. This will help overcome the impact of pre-hospital volume status on initial indicators and provide a more accurate basis for early clinical risk stratification. Nevertheless, our study underscores the clinical relevance of the LAR in the management of critically ill patients with liver failure and sepsis.

5. Conclusion

A large-scale retrospective cohort drawn from the MIMIC-IV database demonstrates that the LAR is an independent and powerful predictor of all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with liver failure complicated by sepsis. Its advantages lie in early availability and ease of calculation. Future prospective multicentre clinical trials should validate the therapeutic implications of the LAR and explore individualized treatment algorithms guided by this metric.
