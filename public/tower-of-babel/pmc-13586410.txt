Impact of Vitamin D Status on Speed, Power, and Sport-Specific Skills in Young Soccer Players Living Above 55° North Latitude
Authors: Eduard Bezuglov, Elvira Usmanova, Ilsiuiar Anishchenko, Viacheslav Kolesnichenko, Anton Emanov, Mikhail Vinogradov, Timur Vakhidov, Elizaveta Kapralova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVitamin D is crucial for calcium metabolism, protein and hormone synthesis, and immune response. Deficiency may impair muscle function and athletic performance, but evidence on sport-specific skills and power remains inconsistent.MethodsWe studied 170 soccer players aged 7–18 from a region above 55°N. In February 2024, serum 25-hydroxy-vitamin D (25[OH]D) was measured by mass spectrometry, and physical performance tests (sprint, jumps, change-of-direction) were conducted. Players were compared by vitamin D status (normal, insufficient, deficient) and by 25th vs. 75th percentiles. Statistical analyses included group comparisons, correlations, and multivariable linear regression adjusted for age, body size, maturation, and supplementation, with false discovery rate correction.ResultsNo significant differences in performance were found between vitamin D groups, except for broad jump in unadjusted comparisons. Crude correlations were weak. In adjusted regression models, 25(OH)D was not significantly associated with any performance outcome; this was consistent across alternative models and after correction for multiple testing.ConclusionsIn young soccer players above 55°N, vitamin D insufficiency and deficiency were common, but adjusted analyses did not provide robust evidence that 25(OH)D independently influences speed, power, or sport-specific skills.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

Vitamin D deficiency is well-documented among various populations.[1] Its importance to human health is well established,[2] with receptors found in almost all organs, suggesting its significant role in numerous functions, including calcium metabolism, gene response signaling, protein and hormone synthesis, and immune response.[3–5] These receptors are also present in muscle tissue, and existing experimental data confirm their potential influence on muscle function through various cellular and genomic mechanisms.[6] Vitamin D influences skeletal muscle through several mechanisms. It regulates calcium flux, which is essential for muscle contraction. It also activates the vitamin D receptor (VDR) on muscle cells, promoting protein synthesis via the Akt/mTOR pathway. In addition, vitamin D supports neuromuscular junction integrity and maintains type II (fast-twitch) muscle fiber size and function, all of which are relevant to athletic performance.[7–9]

This explains the growing interest of sports scientists in studying the influence of vitamin D on parameters primarily related to muscle condition, such as strength and speed. [10–13] Numerous recent studies have investigated the possible effects of vitamin D status on various performance parameters, including speed and strength.[12,14–16] Their results are contradictory. Systematic reviews by Chiang et al. [12] and Tomlinson et al. [17] demonstrated that vitamin D supplementation positively impacted muscle strength. However, a systematic review by Farrokhyar et al. [18] did not find a significant effect of vitamin D supplementation on physical performance indicators.

These discrepancies may be due to methodological differences in the studies. Regarding soccer players, several studies have examined the efficacy of vitamin D supplementation on various aspects of performance,[19,20] showing no significant influence of vitamin D status on strength parameters.

For coaches and sports physicians, understanding the role of vitamin D in sports success, which also involves the development of key physical qualities and sport-specific skills, is important. Most studies to date have not addressed this issue, often involving non-elite athletes, with analyzed parameters not always specific to a particular sport.

There is also a lack of research involving athletes living in regions with low sunlight exposure (above 40 degrees north latitude).[21–24] Studies conducted so far have not provided conclusive evidence on the effect of vitamin D status on analyzed performance indicators, such as sprints and jumps among soccer and rugby players, or sprint speed and grip strength among swimmers.[15,21,23,25] Currently, there is no data on the impact of vitamin D status on non-reactive agility among young elite soccer players.

Therefore, studying the possible association between sport-specific skills, speed and power indicators, and vitamin D status among young elite soccer players living in regions above 55 degrees north latitude may be of practical interest, including optimizing their training programs.

METHODS

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants and their legal guardians signed voluntary informed consent forms and were informed about the risks of participation. The protocol of the study was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of Sechenov First Moscow State University (approval no. 11-19; Jul 25, 2019).

Subjects

The study involved 170 trainees from a leading soccer academy, aged 7 to 18 (mean age 13.42±2.71 years, weight 52.18±16.99 kg, height 163.19±17.08 cm), residing in a city above 55 degrees north latitude (at latitudes above 55 degrees north latitude, cutaneous vitamin D synthesis is negligible during the winter months, as ultraviolet (UV) B (UVB) radiation is insufficient to produce meaningful amounts of vitamin D in human skin [26]), training in the evenings or indoors for at least three consecutive winter months. This was a single-center cross-sectional study, and participants were recruited consecutively during routine pre-season medical examinations in February 2024.

Standardization of diet, training, and environmental conditions

According to questionnaire data, all athletes consumed similar meals in the academy canteen, which helped standardize dietary conditions across the study population. Training volume was consistent among participants: each player performed one training session per day, lasting between 1.5 and 2 hr. All training sessions were conducted under the same environmental conditions. Consequently, any seasonal variation in temperature, sunlight exposure, or training environment affected all athletes equally. Individual sun exposure outside of training was not recorded, which is acknowledged as a limitation. The study was performed in Moscow, central Russia, during the winter season, when the UV index in the region typically ranges from 0 to 3 according to publicly available data.[27]

Laboratory testing

During a routine pre-season medical examination in February 2024, blood samples were collected from all participants in the morning, fasting, between 8:00 and 10:00 am, from the cubital vein by an experienced specialist. All athletes were instructed to rest on the day before blood sampling.

Total 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25[OH]D) blood levels were analyzed using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry on an Agilent 1200 liquid chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA) in combination with an AB Sciex 3200 MD mass detector (Sciex, Foster City, CA, USA). The concentration of 25(OH)D was measured using the total 25(OH)D level, currently considered the most appropriate measure of vitamin D stores in the body.[28,29]

Vitamin D status was classified as deficient (<20 ng/mL), insufficient (20–30 ng/mL), and normal (>30 ng/mL).[30] We used these categories to maintain comparability with prior sports medicine studies,[31–33] while acknowledging that pediatric definitions of vitamin D sufficiency remain debated and that some guidance uses 20 ng/mL as an acceptable threshold primarily in relation to bone health. Participants were grouped by 25(OH)D serum status (normal, insufficient, and deficient) and percentile ranks (25th and 75th percentiles) for serum 25(OH)D concentration. Comparative analyses of the study parameters between these groups were conducted, followed by correlation and adjusted regression analyses.

Performance testing

All tests were conducted indoors on artificial turf familiar to all participants, with air temperatures of 22°C to 24°C and humidity of 60% to 65%. Participants wore their usual training kits and soccer boots, and all had previously performed all testing procedures multiple times. The test battery included speed and sport-specific skills tests: 30 m sprint with 5, 10, and 20 m splits, standing long jump, countermovement jump (CMJ), change of direction (COD) run, t-test, and dribbling (Fig. 1).[34]

After measuring height and weight before physical and sport-specific tests, all participants performed a standard FIFA 11+ warm-up supervised by a coach, lasting 12–15 min and completed no more than three minutes before the tests to minimize acute muscle fatigue. All tests on control and experimental days were conducted in a strictly predetermined sequence.

The rest period between each type of test was 180 sec, with 120 sec rest between attempts. Participants were instructed to perform all tests on the soccer pitch with maximum effort, without verbal encouragement from study coordinators.

Sprint results were measured using the SmartSpeed Plus timing system (Vald Performance, Albion, Australia), with timing gates set at the start line and at 5, 10, 20, and 30 m, 100 cm above the ground. Each participant performed two attempts, with results recorded to the nearest hundredth of a second. All sprint and COD tests began from a two-point start position, with the front foot placed 30 cm behind the start line.

Strength was assessed using a vertical CMJ with hands on the hips, measured by a SmartJump mat (Vald Performance). Participants performed three consecutive attempts, with the best result recorded in centimeters for analysis.

Dribbling was assessed using the SmartSpeed Plus timing system (Vald Performance), six cones, and a soccer ball familiar to the players, inflated to 0.9 atmospheres. Two attempts were made, with 120 sec recovery between each. The best attempt result was used for analysis.

These tests have been shown to have good to excellent reliability in young soccer players when standardized protocols are used.[34,35]

Inclusion criteria

Participants were required to reside above 55 degrees north latitude for the last three months, not miss training due to injury or illness in the month preceding the study, and have informed consent from participants and their legal guardians.

Exclusion criteria

Participants were excluded if they underwent routine pre-season medical examinations separately from the main group of athletes in another clinic or withdrew from the study at any stage by themselves or their legal representatives.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Python (version 3.11.9; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA), with Pandas for data handling, SciPy for statistical tests, and Statsmodels for regression analysis.

Descriptive statistics are presented as means and standard deviations. For categorical comparisons based on serum 25(OH)D status (normal, insufficient, deficient) and percentile ranks (25th and 75th percentiles), normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Depending on distributional assumptions, between-group differences were evaluated using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test.

Because at least one variable in each pair was non-normally distributed, Spearman correlation coefficients were used.

To account for potential confounding by growth and body size, multivariable linear regression models were additionally constructed for each performance outcome. Serum 25(OH)D concentration was included as the main predictor, and age, body mass index (BMI), skeletal age, and vitamin D supplementation status were included as covariates in the primary adjusted models. Because anthropometric and maturation-related variables are biologically related, additional secondary models were run using alternative maturation/body-size covariate sets (e.g., maturity rank, percentage of predicted adult height, or growth phase) to examine the robustness of findings. A sensitivity analysis was also performed after excluding athletes who reported vitamin D supplementation.

For the regression analyses, beta coefficients, standard errors, 95% confidence intervals, and P values were reported. To reduce the risk of false-positive findings due to multiple testing across performance outcomes, false discovery rate correction was applied. Statistical significance was set at a P value of less than 0.05.

RESULTS

Vitamin D status and anthropometric indicators

The average 25(OH)D level among all study participants was 27.43±11.68 ng/mL. Normal 25(OH)D levels were observed in 35.9% (N=61) of the players, while insufficiency and deficiency were detected in 35.9% (N=61) and 28.2% (N=48) of the participants, respectively. Soccer players with normal 25(OH)D levels had significantly higher serum concentrations (39.15±10.48 ng/mL) compared to those classified as insufficient (25.11±3.17 ng/mL) and deficient (15.48±2.55 ng/mL; P<0.001). No statistically significant differences were found between the groups regarding age, height, weight, or BMI in unadjusted comparisons (Table 1). However, because these variables are known to influence physical performance in growing athletes, they were included as potential confounders in the adjusted regression analyses.

When comparing all physical and sport-specific skills among groups with different serum 25(OH)D concentrations, a statistically significant difference was found only in one parameter: the broad jump (Table 2).

When comparing participants in the 75th and 25th percentiles for 25(OH)D levels, serum concentrations were significantly different, with values of 42.26±11.09 ng/mL and 14.95±2.26 ng/mL, respectively (P=0.000). However, while there was a trend toward differences in height (P=0.069), no statistically significant differences were observed in age (P=0.122), weight (P=0.241), or BMI (P=0.612) between these groups (Table 3).

Comparing all physical and sport-specific skills between these groups, a statistically significant difference was again found only in the broad jump (Table 4).

Correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the relationship between serum 25(OH)D concentration and each performance indicator (Table 5). Based on the results of the Shapiro-Wilk normality test, Spearman correlation coefficients were used. Crude analyses suggested weak associations between serum 25(OH)D concentration and some performance metrics. Broad jump showed a weak positive correlation (r=0.212, P=0.013), whereas dribbling and COD showed weak negative correlations (r=−0.117, P=0.129 and r=−0.109, P=0.158, respectively). However, after false discovery rate correction, none of the crude correlations remained statistically significant.

To evaluate the independent association between serum 25(OH)D concentration and physical performance indicators, multivariable linear regression models were constructed (Table 6). In the primary adjusted linear regression models controlling for age, BMI, skeletal age, and vitamin D supplementation, serum 25(OH)D concentration was not significantly associated with sprint performance (5, 10, 20, or 30 m), CMJ, broad jump, dribbling, COD, or Agility t-test performance (all adjusted P>0.05). Findings were consistent across secondary models using alternative maturation/body-size covariates. In the sensitivity analysis restricted to non-supplemented athletes, a nominal association with CMJ was observed before multiple-testing correction, but this did not remain significant after false discovery rate adjustment. Overall, the adjusted analyses did not provide robust evidence that serum 25(OH)D independently predicts physical performance in this cohort.

DISCUSSION

The results showed a high prevalence of vitamin D insufficiency among young soccer players living above 55 degrees north latitude, with 35.9% and 28.2% of participants having insufficient and deficient levels, respectively. However, physical performance and sport-specific skill tests did not show significant differences among young soccer players with varying vitamin D statuses, except for the standing broad jump. Comparisons involved groups with marked and statistically significant differences in 25(OH)D concentration (P<0.00001).

Although some crude associations were observed for selected outcomes, these did not remain robust after adjustment for confounding variables and multiple-testing correction.

Regarding vertical jump indicators, the results differed from previous studies. However, it is important to note that those studies often involved less-trained participants from the general population, rather than regularly intensively trained athletes. For example, the studies by Bezrati et al. [36] and Ward et al. [37] involved amateur teenage soccer players and non-athlete adolescent girls, respectively.

No convincing data have been obtained from studies involving athletes of comparable levels on the impact of vitamin D status on physical performance. For instance, in the study by Wyon et al. [38] involving 84 young elite dancers, no correlation was found between vertical jump height and serum 25(OH)D concentration. Similar results were obtained in the study by Nieman et al. [39], which included NASCAR pit crew athletes. The study by Todd et al. [40] on Gaelic soccer players also did not provide evidence of vitamin D status affecting physical performance.

A senior researcher in this study previously examined the prevalence of vitamin D insufficiency among young athletes at different times of the year and the relationship between its status and various performance aspects.[21, 23,41] A study involving young soccer players from an elite academy (N=131) also located above 55 degrees north latitude showed a significant, though less pronounced, prevalence of vitamin D insufficiency among these athletes: 22.9% had insufficiency, and 19.9% had deficiency. This might be related to the timing of the study: in the 2019 study, blood tests were conducted in December (early winter, one month after starting indoor training), while in the current study, blood was sampled in February (late winter, after at least three months of indoor training).[23]

The method of determining 25(OH)D might also have influenced the results. In 2019, immunoassay analysis was used, while in 2024, mass spectrometry, considered the “gold standard,” was employed.[28]

In both studies, no data indicated an influence of vitamin D status on performance indicators such as sprint and jump results. No evidence of such a relationship was found in the study involving young elite track and field athletes. [21] The prevalence of insufficiency among them was significantly lower than among young soccer players in winter (33.3% had insufficiency, 4.4% had deficiency), possibly due to the timing of the study (summer) and the training characteristics of these athletes: outdoors during the day, in clothing that left significant areas of the body exposed. None of the study’s analyzed parameters correlated with serum 25(OH)D concentration.

The proportion of participants with normal vitamin D levels (35.9%) should be interpreted in the context of the study location (Moscow, approximately 55–56°N) and season (winter). For comparison, a study conducted in Arkhangelsk (64°N), a city at a higher latitude, reported that less than 1% of individuals maintained adequate vitamin D levels during the winter months.[42] The substantially lower rate at 64°N reflects the progressive decline in cutaneous vitamin D synthesis with increasing latitude. Thus, the 35.9% prevalence observed in our cohort is consistent with the expected UVB exposure at approximately 55°N during winter and emphasizes the role of geographic factors in determining vitamin D status.

Although dietary intake was standardized across participants (as confirmed by a questionnaire), training volume was consistent, and environmental conditions were the same for all athletes (so that seasonal variation affected everyone equally), individual sun exposure outside of training sessions was not recorded, which represents a limitation of the present study.

In addition, although we performed adjusted analyses including age, body size, and maturation-related variables available in our dataset, we did not directly measure some potentially relevant biological determinants of performance, such as muscle mass, pubertal hormones, or insulin-like growth factor-1. The broad age range of the cohort (7–18 years) also increased developmental heterogeneity. Furthermore, some adjusted models showed moderate collinearity among maturation- and body size-related covariates, and the sensitivity analysis in non-supplemented athletes was based on a smaller sample. These factors should be considered when interpreting the adjusted estimates.

Future research should likely focus on studying the impact of increasing already normal 25(OH)D levels on various aspects of athletic performance. According to Cannell et al. [43], the benefits associated with serum 25(OH)D concentration levels might be realized at concentrations of 50 ng/mL and higher. Given the multifactorial nature of vitamin D status, further studies should clarify these effects while systematically accounting for sun exposure, dietary intake, and other potentially unknown confounding factors.

The findings indicate a high prevalence of vitamin D insufficiency and deficiency among young soccer players living in regions above 55° north latitude. Although some weak associations were observed in crude analyses, adjusted models accounting for age, body size, maturation-related variables, and vitamin D supplementation did not provide robust evidence that serum 25(OH)D independently influences speed, power, or sport-specific skill performance in this cohort.
