Understanding Social Drivers of Health, Burden, and Impact of Hidradenitis Suppurativa among Different Racial and Ethnic Groups in the USA
Authors: Tarannum Jaleel, Raj Chovatiya, Elizabeth D. Bacci, Evangeline Pierce, Melissa L. Constantine, Julia R. Correll, Beth Mitchell, Dipak R. Patel, Brindley Brooks, Candrice Heath, Vivian Y. Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHidradenitis suppurativa (HS), an inflammatory skin disorder characterized by painful nodules and abscesses, has varying prevalence among different races/ethnicities. This study explored the social drivers of health, burden, and impact of HS among different racial and ethnic groups.MethodsAn online, cross-sectional survey was conducted among adult patients with HS (September 2023–December 2023) in the USA. Patients were recruited through HS Connect (patient advocacy group) and AmeriSpeak (US national sample panel). Descriptive data were collected using patient-reported outcome measures and de novo questions about patients’ disease knowledge and perception, healthcare access and utilization, impact on quality of life (QoL), and social impact. All analyses were descriptive and stratified by racial/ethnic groups.ResultsThe study included 583 patients (mean age, 34.8 years; 95.5% female) representing a range of racial backgrounds: Black or African American (n = 273; 46.8%), white (n = 236; 40.5%), Two or More Races (n = 47; 8.1%), American Indian or Alaska Native (n = 18; 3.1%), Asian (n = 7; 1.2%), and Native Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander (n = 2; 0.3%). Ethnic representation also varied (Hispanic/Latino = n = 76; 13.0%).Patients of all races and ethnicities reported considerable QoL impact (Dermatology Life Quality Index, EQ-5D-5L), with results for smaller subgroups (n < 10) included for descriptive completeness only and not intended for comparison with other groups. During flaring, most patients used over-the-counter products/medications (54.2%) or nonmedical intervention/home remedy (56.9%) Up to 36.5% of patients reported challenges in procuring food, utilities, medicine/healthcare, phone, clothing, or childcare when needed in the past year. Among those who paid out-of-pocket for their HS treatment, 55.6% reported that it stopped them from visiting a healthcare provider for treatment.ConclusionThe findings indicate a high burden and impact of HS across all races and ethnicities. Patients reported social drivers of health and challenges with healthcare utilization, indicating the need for integrating social workers and care management teams in dermatology practice, which could facilitate improved care of patients with HS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01765-3.

Introduction

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), also known as acne inversa, is a chronic, debilitating, inflammatory skin disorder that usually presents after puberty [1]. HS is characterized by painful, deep-seated nodules, abscesses, fistulae, and draining tunnels, most commonly affecting intertriginous areas such as the axillae, groin, gluteal region, and inframammary folds [1]. HS significantly impairs quality of life (QoL), affecting physical, social, and psychological well-being, with patients often experiencing stigma, embarrassment, self-consciousness, unemployment, and relationship issues [2–4]. A retrospective study of over 48 million individuals in the USA identified a 0.1% prevalence of HS, with Black and/or African American individuals experiencing a threefold higher prevalence compared with white individuals (296 per 100,000 versus 95 per 100,000; p < 0.001) [5].

Management of HS is multimodal and varies by disease severity, encompassing a combination of lifestyle modifications, systemic antibiotics, topical agents, biologics, anti-inflammatory agents, laser therapy, phototherapy, and surgical interventions. These treatments can impose a substantial financial burden on patients and payors [6–11].

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of social drivers of health in disease heterogeneity among different races and ethnic groups. In this study, we use the term social drivers of health to describe patient-level social needs and barriers (e.g., access to food, transportation, utilities, and healthcare) reported directly by participants. This term is preferred over social determinants of health, which, while widely used, has faced criticism for implying a passive, fixed relationship between social conditions and health outcomes that may undermine patient and community agency [12, 13]. Social drivers of health reflect a more actionable framing, distinguishing individual-level social needs from the broader structural and systemic factors that influence health at the population level [12, 13]. Higher rates of healthcare utilization, elevated disease burden, lower quality of life (QoL), prolonged diagnostic delays, and more significant barriers to healthcare access have been documented among Hispanic and Black participants relative to white participants [14–17]. However, race- and ethnicity-based evaluation to understand these disparities in HS is sparse [18–20].

Here, we sought to better characterize the overall burden of HS among patients from diverse racial and ethnic groups in the USA from the patient perspective, focusing on disease impact, healthcare access and utilization, QoL, and psychosocial outcomes.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This cross-sectional, non-interventional, anonymous, web-based survey was conducted among US participants with HS from 13 September 2023 to 22 December 2023. Patients were recruited through HS Connect (patient advocacy group) and the AmeriSpeak panel, a national probability sample frame with 97% coverage of the US population. Eligible patients were ≥ 18 years of age (≥ 19 years for Alabama and Nebraska residents, and ≥ 21 years for Mississippi and Puerto Rico residents); able to speak, read, and write US English or US Spanish; able to use a computer or smartphone with access to the internet; living in the USA; willing to provide informed consent to participate in the study; and had a self-reported diagnosis of HS from a medical professional.

Patients were excluded if they had a current or past diagnosis of lupus erythematosus, skin cancer, or if they self-reported three or more of the following conditions: atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, hidradenitis suppurativa, or alopecia areata. This was primarily due to the potential for these diagnoses to impact the patient-reported outcome measures selected for assessing HS in patients’ health, disease burden, quality of life, and social impact.

Survey Content

A comprehensive survey was designed to capture a multifaceted view of participants’ health, disease burden, lifestyle, and social factors. The self-administered electronic survey, consisting of 80–110 items, included screening questions to determine eligibility, population descriptors (Fitzpatrick scale, [21] Monk Skin Tone scale, [22]) and patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures to assess patients’ health and disease burden (EQ-5D-5L [23], Dermatology Life Quality Index [DLQI] [24], and Patient Global Impression of Severity [25], Neuro-QOL Stigma Short Form, PROMIS—Managing Social Interactions Short Form, and the Protocol for Responding to and Assessing Patients’ Assets, Risks, and Experiences (PRAPARE) tool [26] to evaluate social impact. Supplementary Table 1 provides additional information regarding the de novo survey items (e.g., patients’ knowledge about their skin condition, treatment for their skin condition, healthcare access and utilization, condition-related work impairment, and internet access). This study also included six questions derived from the All of Us Research Program healthcare access and utilization survey, which were originally developed for the National Health Interview Survey program [27].

Data Analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the sample and patient responses to survey questions. The analyses were further stratified by racial and ethnic groups, including Black or African American, white, American Indian or Alaskan Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander, two or more races, and Hispanic or Latino or not Hispanic or Latino. The patients’ racial and ethnic identities were separately inquired. This study reported descriptive results only; results for smaller subgroups (Asian and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander patients) are included for descriptive completeness and should not be interpreted comparatively.

Continuous variables were presented as mean, median, standard deviation (SD), and interquartile range. For categorical variables, the frequency and percent distribution by response category were described. Analyses were conducted using SAS Software, Version 9.4. No data imputation was performed, and unanswered questions were coded as missing. All instruments were scored according to the developers’ guidelines, including those for handling missing data.

Data quality checks were used to identify and exclude “speedsters,” who complete surveys in less than one-third of the median time, and “straight-liners,” who consistently select the same response option. Participants were remunerated with US$100 for survey completion by their respective recruiters.

Ethical Approval

The study was approved by the Advarra institutional review board (Pro00070530), conducted in accordance with the ethical principles in the Declaration of Helsinki, and was consistent with Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practices, as well as applicable laws and regulations, as appropriate. Participants provided authorization for the use and disclosure of their personal health information, covering the collection and release of data regarding treatment and its outcomes for the entire period of the study. Confidentiality of participant information was maintained throughout the study. All recruitment procedures complied with current Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act regulations.

Result

Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

Out of 1549 eligible patients who completed the survey, data from 1284 patients passed the quality check, while 265 were excluded due to data quality issues. Among eligible respondents, 583 had HS, with a mean age of 34.8 years, and 95.5% were identified as female. Patients were identified from multiple racial and ethnic groups: Overall, 236 (40.5%) patients identified as white, 273 (46.8%) as Black or African American, 18 (3.1%) as American Indian or Alaskan Native, 7 (1.2%) as Asian, 2 (0.3%) as Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, and 47 (8.1%) belonged to two or more than two races; 76 patients (13.0%) identified as Hispanic or Latino.

All results are descriptive; data for very small subgroups (Asian and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander) are reported for descriptive completeness only and are not intended for comparative interpretation.

The most commonly reported health conditions were anxiety (53.3%) and depression (42.0%), with varying proportions of patients from different racial and ethnic groups experiencing them; these proportions are descriptive. The proportion of patients with household income < US$20,000 was 23.4% among Black or African American, 15.2% among white, and 33.3% among American Indian or Alaskan Native (Table 1). Quality of Life

EQ-5D-5L

The mean (SD) EQ-5D-5L index score for all patients was 0.6 (0.3). Scores were generally consistent among patients from different racial and ethnic groups, with results for smaller subgroups presented descriptively for completeness. The overall mean (SD) EQ-5D-5L Health visual analog scale (VAS) score was 64.3 (19.0) and was similar among all subgroups (Supplementary Table 2). These are low scores on the EQ-5D-5L VAS scale (0–100), indicating a big impact of HS on patients, regardless of race and ethnicity. The EQ-5D-5L domain scores are summarized in Supplementary Table 3.

DLQI

The overall mean (SD) DLQI score was 19.3 (7.2), with most patients (86.3%; n = 503) reporting “very large” to “extremely large” effect on patients’ lives. Mean DLQI scores were generally consistent among patients from different racial and ethnic groups; results for smaller subgroups are included for descriptive completeness only (Supplementary Table 4). The proportion of patients who reported an “extremely large” effect on their lives was 57.1% (n = 4/7) among Asian patients, 53.1% (n = 145/273) among Black or African American patients, and 38.9% (n = 7/18) among American Indian or Alaska Native patients. All results are descriptive and not intended for group-to-group comparison. Descriptive statistics for individual DLQI questions are presented in Supplementary Table 4.

When asked how often doctors or healthcare providers asked patients for their opinions or beliefs about their medical care or treatment, the most common overall response was “some of the time” (32.9%), while 26.9% reported “none of the time.” The proportion of patients who reported “none of the time” was similar for all racial and ethnic groups. No major differences were noted among the ethnic groups (Table 2). The patients were also asked how often they delayed or did not visit doctors or healthcare providers because they were different from them (e.g., in race, religion, or native language), to which 7.2% of patients overall reported “always” and 11.8% reported “most of the time.” However, almost half of the patients reported that this happened “none of the time” (48.9%), and 31.9% reported this only happened “some of the time.” Subgroup patterns are presented descriptively, with small subgroups included for completeness only (Table 2).

Discussion

This study of US participants with HS utilized a comprehensive survey-based analysis to understand the patient perspectives of HS disease burden, social and QoL impacts, and healthcare access and utilization in the overall population, and explored observed patterns among racial and ethnic groups descriptively.

A significant disease burden accompanied by comorbidities such as anxiety and depression has been commonly reported in patients with HS [28, 29]. Consistent with these reports, most (83.9%) patients in the current study reported moderate-to-severe HS accompanied by anxiety among more than half and depression among > 40% of patients. In the general US population, the prevalence of mental illness varies by race, ranging from 34.9% among multiracial individuals to 16.4% among Asian individuals [30]. These differences highlight the need to understand how race and ethnicity may influence the mental health impact of HS.

These findings highlight actionable steps for clinical practice. Clinicians should routinely screen patients with HS for anxiety and depression and consider integration of mental health support into care [31]. Awareness of social drivers such as medication access, financial barriers, and reliance on online resources may facilitate individualized treatment planning [32, 33]. Patient education, referral to clinical specialists, and consideration of social and health system barriers may help mitigate HS burden [8, 33, 34]. Providers should recognize the diverse needs of patients from different racial and ethnic backgrounds and tailor interventions accordingly [35, 36].

HS results in severe QoL impairment, which is often worse than other skin diseases [37, 38], in alignment with the findings of this study. In the current study, patients reported impaired QoL as assessed using PRO measures (EQ-5D-5L and DLQI), wherein patients reported substantial impacts of HS on their career and work life, education, activities of interest, relationships, and parenting. These findings are consistent with reports from patients with atopic dermatitis, indicating that the condition affects multiple areas of their lives. Specifically, many patients experienced notable pain, discomfort, and anxiety or depression, as reflected in the EQ-5D-5 l assessments. Furthermore, patients indicated suffering in terms of symptoms and emotional well-being related to work and education as measured by the DLQI [39, 40].

Patients also reported challenges with healthcare access and utilization. About a fifth of the patients reported not seeing a healthcare provider to treat their HS, while about half consulted a dermatologist for support in managing their HS. Furthermore, during a flare-up, most patients used a nonmedical intervention, home remedy, or an over-the-counter product/medication. A similar lack of consultation with healthcare providers/dermatologists has previously been reported among patients with HS, with multiple possible reasons including stigma among patients, barriers to healthcare access, out-of-pocket costs, and cultural beliefs associated with different races/ethnicities [29]. Another reason could be the increasing internet use as a source of knowledge and support for patients with HS. Notably, more than a third of patients used online communities and online searches as sources of support in this study. However, the inconsistent quality of online content and conflicting information could result in mistrust and limited engagement with dermatologists, compromising patient education and decision-making [41, 42].

This study also investigated the social drivers of health using the PRAPARE tool and other survey questions. More than a third of patients reported being unable to access medicines or any healthcare, food, and clothing when needed, which could increase the delays in treatment/diagnosis, morbidity, and mortality. Cost of care is an important parameter for healthcare access and can be in the form of time required to access care and financial burden [33]. Current findings reveal that patients most commonly had to pay partially out-of-pocket for medications and outpatient visits (with primary care provider/specialist doctor). Of these, more than half of the patients stopped making appointments with their healthcare providers for HS treatment due to out-of-pocket costs. Overall, only a small proportion of patients (< 15%) received financial assistance from a patient assistance program.

Previous reports have revealed disparities in disease burden and healthcare utilization among patients with HS from different races and ethnicities [36]. Similarly, the current study indicated severe HS were observed among 41.4% Black or African American, 29.7% white, 22.2% American Indian and Alaska Native, 14.3% Asian, 50.0% Native Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander, and 36.9% not Hispanic nor Latino patients. Due to very small sample sizes, particularly for Asian (n = 7) and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander (n = 2) patients, these results are descriptive only and should not be compared across groups.

While prior studies have documented HS disease burden, this study provides detailed, patient-reported insights on disease severity, mental health, healthcare access, and social drivers among patients from various racial and ethnic backgrounds in the USA. Perspectives from underrepresented groups provides descriptive insights into real-world challenges, including variations in access to medicine or healthcare, lifestyle, and social factors highlighting the need for enhanced social awareness and community support.

The findings of this study highlight the barriers in care and social drivers of health among patients with HS underscoring the need for tailored treatment approaches and support strategies that address patients’ specific needs. The findings also underscore the need to incorporate social drivers of health in clinical decision-making. Clinicians should use these insights to proactively screen for anxiety and depression, assess access to medications and healthcare, provide patient education, and refer patients to social services when needed. Future research exploring real-world challenges and social drivers of health among different races and ethnicities is needed to optimize HS care.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. In addition, HS diagnosis and disease severity were self-reported and not confirmed by a medical provider owing to the survey’s anonymity, which may introduce misclassification bias. The study population was predominantly female (95.5%), which may limit generalizability to the broader population with patients with HS. Thus, the results should be interpreted primarily in the context of female patients with HS. Small sample sizes for certain racial groups (including Asian and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander) restrict meaningful comparisons across groups and are reported descriptively only. Analyses were descriptive in nature without inferential statistical testing; therefore, findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Generalizability may also be limited due to convenience sampling and online recruitment methods. Participants recruited through HS Connect likely represent a more engaged or digitally connected subset of patients, as only those who received and read one of the advertisements were informed of the study, which may introduce selection bias. Most males were Black or African American (n = 13/26) and not Hispanic or Latino (n = 22/26). Although sex at birth varied by racial and ethnic groups, these variables were not controlled for given the descriptive analytic approach. Future studies with larger, more diverse samples and clinically confirmed diagnoses should incorporate multivariable analyses to control for sex and other potential confounders.

Despite these limitations, the findings highlight persistent disparities in disease burden and social drivers of health among patients with HS and underscore the need for targeted, clinically actionable interventions, including mental health support, addressing social drivers, and tailored treatment strategies.

Conclusions

A high burden and QoL impact of HS were observed across all races/ethnicities included in this study. Social barriers and challenges in accessing healthcare were also noted, highlighting the need for advancing culturally responsive, patient-centered care for people living with HS. Improvements in health outcomes begin with accurate measurement and should include a holistic patient assessment.
