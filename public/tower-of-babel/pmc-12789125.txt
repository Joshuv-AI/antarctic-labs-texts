Reactive oxygen species-sensitive release of cephalexin from hyaluronic acid-g-cephalexin dimer copolymer for treatment of inflammation and infection of oral soft tissue cells
Authors: Chang-Young Kim, Woong Kim, Min-Suk Kook, Ji-Sun Park, Se-Yoon Park, Ho-Joong Kim, Young-IL Jeong, Seok-Jun Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Since inflammation and/or infection against soft tissue cells increased oxidative stress and CD44 receptor expression, graft copolymer was synthesized using hyaluronic acid (HA) and cephalexin (CPX). To produce CPX dimer, to molecules of CPX were conjugated with carboxylic acid group of thioketal dicarboxylic acid (ThdCOOH). CPX dimer was conjugated with HA using thioketal diamine (ThdNH2) to synthesize HA-g-CPX copolymer (Abbreviated as HAgCPX). Nanoparticles of HAgCPX copolymer was fabricated by dialysis method. They were assembled in the aqueous solution and formed spherical nanoparticles with small dimeter less than 300 nm. Since HAgCPX copolymers have ROS-sensitive linkage such as thioketal group, they were disintegrated in the presence of H2O2, particle size was also changed from monomodal to multimodal and then drug release rate was accelerated in the presence of H2O2. These results indicated that HAgCPX nanoparticles have ROS-sensitivity. HAgCPX nanoparticles have anti-inflammatory efficacy and wound healing potential, i.e. they efficiently inhibited inflammatory cytokines and promoted wound healing of LPS-treated HGF-1 cells. LPS treatment against increased CD44 receptor expression on the HGF-1 cells. Fluorescence intensity of HGF-1 cells was significantly increased by treatment of HAgCPX nanoparticles in the absence of free HA pre-treatment while pre-treatment of HA suppressed the intracellular delivery of HAgCPX nanoparticles, indicating that HAgCPX nanoparticles can be delivered via CD44 receptor-mediated pathway. HAgCPX nanoparticles showed antimicrobial activity in vitro against various oral microbials as well as free CPX. We suggested that HAgCPX nanoparticles are promising candidate for ROS-specific and CD44 receptor-mediated delivery of drugs against inflammatory of oral soft tissue cells.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30912-7.

Introduction

Various inflammatory diseases of oral soft tissues, which are commonly caused by bacterial infection, induce sore, swollen, bleeding, loss of bone and, finally, tooth loss1,2. Bacterial infections in the tooth and supporting structures allow them to spread in the surrounding oral soft tissues and pulp3. When they invade the pulp and inner structure of tooth, they can spread to the tooth root and surrounding bone following with formation of abscess4. Chronic inflammation in oral cavity is deeply associated with the progression of systemic diseases such as neurodegenerative disease, arthritis and heart disease5–7. Antibiotic treatment in periodontics can offer synergistical benefit to scaling and root planing but its systemic application in periodontal disease has limitations due to its intrinsic cytotoxicity and adverse effects8. Furthermore, inflammation and/or infection in oral soft tissues is commonly difficult to be cured because antibiotics or anti-inflammatory drug are difficult to be delivered to the site of infection/inflammation in the oral cavity, i.e. complexities of oral cavity affect to the delivery of drugs in the oral cavity and drug efficacy is also affected by physico-chemical circumstances of oral cavity such as saliva and food8–10. Especially, physiology of soft tissues under infection or inflammation is quite different to that of normal tissues or cells, i.e. CD44 receptor expression, oxidative stress and acidity are known to be increased under infection or inflammation condition11–15. Under bacterial infection, expression of CD44 receptor in soft tissue cells is activated12. Lucarini et al. reported that CD44 expression level of patients having peri-implantitis is closely correlated with peri-implant pocket depth and can be used as an inflammation marker13. The oxidative stress is also known to be increased under inflammation condition and systemic disease11,12,14,15. The level of reactive oxygen species is increased under inflammatory environment in the oral cavity such as peri-implantitis. Also, oxidative stress and the production of ROS is increased in the periodontal tissues of patients having diabetes mellitus and then affect to the wound healing/tissue regeneration11. Oxidative stress under periodontitis condition is quite related to the systemic disease15.

Nanoscale-based carriers such as nanoparticles, liposomes, polymer conjugates and polymeric micelles have been extensively investigated in the field of infection and/or inflammatory disease16–18. Nanoscale carriers can be used for targeting inflammatory cells since they have better biocompatibility and targeting ability. They can be designed to be responded to the physiological status of disease. For example, nanoparticles having responsiveness against oxidative stress and reduced pH effectively release anti-inflammatory drug and then reduce the excess oxidants16. Furthermore, drug-encapsulated liposomes or nanoparticles are efficiently regulated the microbial-cellular microenvironment and then prevent biofilm-induced oral diseases18. Since CD44 receptor expression is typically increased under bacterial infection or inflammation condition, hyaluronic acid (HA) has been also applied to target inflammatory cells, i.e. HA can be used to design nanocarriers to target inflammatory cells19. Wang et al. reported that uptake of HA-decorated carriers by M1 macrophages were 3.7 times higher than that of normal counterpart20. HA-decorated nanoparticles having disulfide linkage efficiently targeted abnormal tissues such as solid tumor, which expresses CD44 receptor, and then efficiently suppressed tumor growth21. Therefore, drug targeting based on CD44 receptor and oxidative stress may enable to overcome adverse effects of antibiotics, lower delivery efficiency in oral cavity and then increase treatment efficacy in oral infectious disease.

In this study, we synthesized cephalexin (CPX) dimer using thioketal linkers and then grafted with HA to make HA-g-CPX dimer (HAgCPX) copolymer. Nanoparticles of HAgCPX copolymer were prepared for CD44 specific delivery of antibiotics. CPX were conjugated each other using thioketal linkers to be sensitive to reactive oxygen species and then released from nanoparticles under oxidative stress while HA of HAgCPX nanoparticles can interact with CD44 receptor of surface of inflammatory cells. Physicochemical and biological properties of HAgCPX nanoparticles were investigated in vitro.

Materials and methods

Materials

HA (Sodium hyaluronate) was purchased from Lifecore Biomedical Co. (Chaska, Minesota, USA). Average molecular weight (M.W.) of HA20K (Lot. No. 028662) for synthesis of HAgCPX copolymer was 3.8 × 104 Daltons (size-exclusion chromatography coupled to a multi-angle laser light-scattering detector, MALLS) from manufacturer’s data). Average M.W. of HA5K (Lot. No. 026570) for synthesis of HAPLGA block copolymer was 4659 g/mol from manufacturer’s data. PLGA (PLGA-5005, MW = 5000 g/mol) was purchased from Wako Pure Chemicals Co. (Osaka, Japan). Thioketal diamine (ThdNH2) and thioketal dicarboxylic acid (ThdCOOH) was purchased from RuixiBiotech Co. Ltd. (Xi’an, China). Cephalexin (CPX) monohydrate, N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide HCl (EDAC), N-hydroxy succinimide (NHS), 1-hydroxybenzotriazole hydrate (HOBt), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), pyrene, sodium cyanoborohydride, 3-(4,5-dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)- 2, 5-diphenyl-2H-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) and Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from Escherichia coli (E.coli) O111:B4 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chem. Co. (St. Louis, MI, USA). Rhodamine B amine was purchased from BOC Sciences (NY, USA). Dialysis membranes (Spectra/Por® 7 membranes, molecular weight cutoff size (MWCO): 500 ~ 1000 and 3500 g/mol) were purchased from Spectrum Lab., Inc. (Rancho Dominguez, CA, USA). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), methanol and organic solvents were used as a HPLC grade.

Synthesis of HAgCPX copolymer

CPX dimer

Synthesis of CPX dimer was performed as follows: ThdCOOH (22.4 mg, 100 µM) was dissolved in 5 ml DMSO with 2 equivalents of EDAC (38.4 mg, 200 µM) and NHS (23 mg, 200 µM). This solution was stirred more than 6 h and then 2 equivalent mols of CPX monohydrate (73.1 mg, 200 µM) was added to this solution. Following this, solution was stirred more than 48 h. Resulting solution was poured into 20 ml deionized water and then dialyzed using dialysis membrane (MWCO = 500 ~ 1000 g/mol) against 1L of distilled water for 2 days. MWCO of dialysis membrane was selected based on the expected M.W. of products to remove unreacted molecules and byproducts. To remove unreacted molecules, byproducts and organic solvents, distilled water was exchanged every 3 h intervals. Since the synthesized molecules are considered to have higher M.W. than that of MWCO of dialysis membrane, the synthesized molecules in aqueous solution can be retained in the dialysis membrane and, after that, products can be recovered by lyophilization of aqueous solution. Dialyzed solution was lyophilized more than 2 days to obtain CPX dimer. Yield of resulting solid was higher than 92.1% (w/w). Yield = [(Weight of CPX + weight of ThdCOOH)] × 100.

To the carboxylic end of CPX dimer, ThdNH2 was attached as follows: CPX dimer (88.2 mg, approximately 100 µM as a CPX dimer) was dissolved in 5 ml DMSO with equivalent mol of EDAC (19.2 mg, 100 µM) and NHS (11.5 mg, 100 µM). This solution was stirred more than 6 h and then 3 equivalent mol of ThdNH2 (58.3 mg, 300 µM) was added to this solution following with magnetic stirring more than 48 h. Resulting solution was poured into 20 mL deionized water and then dialyzed using dialysis membrane (MWCO = 500 ~ 1000 g/mol) against 1L of distilled water for 2 days. To remove unreacted byproducts and organic solvents, distilled water was exchanged every 3 h intervals. Dialyzed solution was lyophilized more than 2 days to obtain CPX dimer-ThdNH2 conjugates. Yield of resulting solid was higher than 93.8% (w/w). Yield = [(Weight of CPX dimer + weight of ThdNH2)] × 100.

HAgCPX copolymer

HA (114 mg, 3 µM) was dissolved in 2 ml H2O and then 5 ml DMSO was added. To this solution, EDAC (3.84 mg, 20 µM) and HOBt (2.7 mg, 20 µM) was added with magnetic stirring for 12 h. Following this, CPX dimer-ThdNH2 conjugates (21.2 mg, approximately 20 µM) was dissolved in 5 ml and then poured into HA solution. This solution was stirred for 48 h with magnetic stirrer. After that, resulting solution was dialyzed using dialysis tube (MWCO: 3500 g/mol) against distilled water for two days following with lyophilization for two days to obtain HAgCPX copolymer. Yield of HAgCPX copolymer was higher than 93% (w/w). Yield = [(Weight of CPX dimer-ThdNH2 conjugates + weight of HA)] × 100.

HA-b-PLGA (HAPLGA) copolymer

For comparison, HAPLGA copolymer was also synthesized as described previously21. Briefly, HA (400 mg) dissolved in 10 ml DMSO/H2O (7/3, v/v) solution was mixed with excess amount of sodium cyanoborohydride following with magnetic stirring for 12 h. After that, 10 equivalents cystamine were added and further stirred for 24 h. This solution was put into a dialysis membrane (MWCO, 3500 g/mol) and dialyzed against distilled water for 2 days following with lyophilization for 3 days. NHS-activated PLGA (PLGA-NHS) was synthesized as follows: Five hundred mg PLGA dissolved in 10 ml DMSO with 1.2 equivalents of EDAC and NHS following with magnetic stirring for 12 h. The reactants were precipitated into the excess amount of methanol and then filtered under reduced pressure to obtain white solid. These were dried using vacuum drying for a day.

HAPLGA block copolymer was synthesized as follows: 100 mg HA-cystamine conjugates and 100 mg PLGA-NHS dissolved in DMSO was magnetically stirred for 1 days. After that, reactants were introduced into a dialysis membrane (MWCO = 12,000 g/mol) and then dialyzed against distilled water with exchange of water at 3 h intervals for 2 days. The resulting solution was lyophilized for 3 days and then precipitated into DCM to remove unreacted PLGA for three times. Resulting solids were then filtered and dried under a vacuum for 3 days.

1H nuclear magnetic spectroscopy

The chemical structure was confirmed using 1H NMR spectroscopy (Varian Unity Inova 500 MHz NB High Resolution Fourier transform (FT)-NMR spectrometer; Varian Inc., Santa Clara, CA). For NMR measurements, D2O, DMSO, or D2O/DMSO mixtures were used to dissolve chemicals and synthesized conjugates.

High resolution Time-of-flight (HR-TOF) mass spectrometer

High resolution Time-of-flight (TOF) mass spectrometer (AccuTOF GC-X, JEOL, Ltd., Tokyo, Jaoan) was employed to measure molecular weight of CPX dimer.

Fabrication of nanoparticles

HAgCPX copolymer (20 mg) was distributed into 2 mL deionized water and then 3 ml DMSO was added. This solution was introduced into dialysis membrane (MWCO = 3500 g/mol) and then dialyzed against distilled water with exchange of water every 3 h intervals for 1 day. After that, the volume of dialyzed solution was adjusted to 20 ml of deionized water or phosphate buffered saline (pH 7.4, 0.01 M). This solution was used to measure physicochemical properties and drug release study.

CPX contents in the nanoparticles was evaluated as follows: 5 mg nanoparticles were distributed to 5 mL phosphate buffered saline (pH 7.4, 0.01 M) with 20 mM hydrogen peroxide and then incubated in shaking incubator at 100 rpm more than 1 day. Following this, resulting solution was diluted more than 10 times with DMSO. To evaluate CPX content in the nanoparticles, the absorbance of the solution was measured using an ultraviolet (UV) spectrophotometer (Genesys 10 s UV–VIS spectrophotometer; Thermo Electron Scientific Instruments LLC, Madison, WI) at 260 nm. Particle size distribution and transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM (H-7600, Hitachi Instruments Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was used to observe nanoparticle morphology. The aqueous nanoparticle solution was placed on a carbon film-coated grid and dried at room temperature. Phosphotungstic acid (0.1%, w/w in water) was used for staining, and TEM images were obtained 80 kV. The diameter of the nanoparticles was analyzed using Nano-ZS (Malvern, Worcestershire, UK). Nanoparticles in deionized water (0.1%, w/v based on polymer weight) were used to measure the diameter at room temperature. To evaluate hydrogen peroxide on the physicochemical properties of nanoparticles, HAgCPX nanoparticles were distributed into PBS (pH 7.4, 0.01 M) with H2O2 for 6 h and used to measure particle size or TEM observation.

Drug release study

To measure CPX contents in the HAgCPX nanoparticles, 5 mg of HAgCPX nanoparticles were distributed to the 10 ml PBS (pH 7.4, 0.01 M) containing 20 mM H2O2. This solution was incubated at 100 rpm and 37 °C for 48 h. After that, this solution was diluted with DMSO for 10 times and then measured CPX concentration using UV VIS spectrophotometer (Genesys 10 s UV–VIS spectrophotometer, Thermo Electron Scientific Instruments LLC, Madison, WI USA) at 260 nm. Free HA, ThdCOOH and ThdNH2 were used as a blank to compensate absorption of CPX.

The release rate of CPX from the nanoparticles was evaluated in vitro. The volume of nanoparticles fabricated as described above were adjusted to 20 mL using PBS (pH 7.4, 0.01 M), and then 5 ml of this solution was introduced onto a dialysis membrane (MWCO: 3,500 g/mol), which was then put into a conical tube containing 45 mL PBS (pH 7.4, 0.01 M). For comparison, hydrogen peroxide was added to drug release media. The release study was performed in a shaking incubator at 100 rpm and 37 °C. At specific time intervals, whole media were collected to measure CPX concentration in PBS, which was then replaced with fresh PBS to prevent saturation in the medium. CPX concentration was measured using UV VIS spectrophotometer (Genesys 10 s UV–VIS spectrophotometer, Thermo Electron Scientific Instruments LLC, Madison, WI USA) at 260 nm. The empty nanoparticles were similarly adapted to release study to compensate absorption spectra. Free HA, ThdCOOH and ThdNH2 were used as a blank to compensate absorption of CPX.

Cell culture

Human gingival fibroblast-1 (HGF-1) cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA). L929 mouse fibroblast cells were purchased from Korean Cell Line Bank, Co. (Seoul, Republic of Korea). HGF-1 cells and L929 cells were maintained with Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (RPMI 1640) medium, respectively. DMEM or RPMI1640 was supplemented with 10% heat inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Invitrogen) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin for cell culture study. Cells were cultured in 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C until use. HGF-1 cells (1 × 104 cells/well) or L929 cells (1 × 104 cells/well) seeded in 96 wells were cultured overnight in 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. Following this, CPX, HAgCPX nanoparticles and/or free HA in aqueous solution were sterilized with a 1.2 µm syringe filter. These were diluted with serum free DMEM and then treated to cells following with incubation for 1 day or 2 days. Following this, viability of cells was evaluated with the MTT cell proliferation assay. MTT reagent (5 mg/mL PBS, 30 µL) was added to 96 wells and then further incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Following this, the supernatants were discarded and removed and 100 µl DMSO was added. The viability of cells was measured with an Epoch microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc., Winooski, VT, USA) at 570 nm.

Fluorescence microscopy

HGF-1 cells (3 × 105 cells/well in 6 well plates with cover glass) were treated with nanoparticles. Ninety min later, supernatants were removed and then washed cells with PBS two times. Cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde solution (4%) for 15 min, washed with PBS and then immobilized with Immunomount mounting solution (Thermo Electron Co., Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Cells were observed a fluorescence microscope (Eclipse Ni, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

CD44 expression of HGF-1 cells

To induce CD44 receptor in HGF-1 cells, LPS (100 µg/ml) was added to HGF-1 cell (3 × 105 cells/well in 6 well plates with cover slide) for 6 h and then cells were washed with PBS twice. Then, FITC anti-CD44 antibody (ab27285, Abcam Co., Cambridge, UK) in binding buffer was treated to HGF-1 cells. Cells were washed with PBS and then CD44 receptor expression in HGF-1 cells was immediately observed with fluorescence microscope. For flow cytometric analysis, cells were treated similarly as described above in 6 well plates (3 × 105 cells/well without cover slide) and then washed cells with PBS. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and then analyzed with Invitrogen Attune NxT flow cytometers (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

CD44-receptor mediated targeting of nanoparticles

For CD44-receptor mediated targeting of nanoparticles, HAgCPX nanoparticles were labelled with rhodamine B amine. Briefly, HAgCPX conjugates (70 mg) in 7 ml DMSO/H2O mixtures (DMSO/H2O = 5/2, v/v) were mixed with EDAC (1.9 mg) and NHS (1.15 mg) following with magnetic stirring for 3 h. After that, Rhodamine B amine (4.6 mg) was added to this solution and then reacted 12 h under dark condition. Resulting solution was dialyzed against distilled water to remove unreacted fluorescent dye using dialysis membrane (MWCO = 3500 g/mol) more than 1 day. The experimental labelling contents of rhodamine B was 6.1% (w/w) (Theoretical labelling contents: 6.6% (w/w)). For evaluation of the CD44 receptor interaction of HGF-1 cells, free HA (0 mg/ml, 1 mg/ml or 10 mg/ml) was added to cell culture to block CD44 receptor of LPS-treated HGF-1 cells. One h later, cells were treated with rhodamine B-labelled HAbCPX nanoparticles for 90 min. After that, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with paraformaldehyde solution (4%) for 15 min and washed again with PBS and then immobilized with Immunomount mounting solution. The CD44-mediated delivery capacity of nanoparticles was observed with fluorescence microscope. flow cytometric analysis was conducted in the same manner as the experimental method mentioned above.

Cytokine assay

HGF-1 cells (1 × 106 cells) in 6 cm dish were primarily treated with CPX itself or HAgCPX nanoparticles for 12 h. After that, LPS (10 μg/ml) was treated to cells for 24 h. The media was 2 ml in 6 cm dish. CPX dose were 20 µg/mL. The supernatants were collected and centrifuged for analysis of IL-6 and TNF-α. To measure the concentration of IL-6 and TNF-α in the culture supernatants, commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (eBioscence, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) were used according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The analysis was performed in triplicate. For competition assay against CD44 receptor, LPS (100 µg/ml) was added to HGF-1 cell (1 × 106 cells) in 6 cm dish for 6 h as described above. After that, HGF-1 cells were treated with free HA (250 μg/mL) to block CD44 receptor of HGF-1 cells for 1 h before treatment of CPX or HAgCPX nanoparticles. CPX dose was adjusted to 50 µg/mL. CPX itself or HAgCPX nanoparticles were treated to cells for 4 h and then cells were washed with PBS. Following this, LPS (10 μg/ml) was treated to cells for 24 h. The media was 2 ml in 6 cm dish. CPX dose were 20 µg/mL. The supernatants were collected and centrifuged for analysis of IL-6 and TNF-α as described above.

Wound healing assay

For wound healing assay, HGF-1 cell were confluently cultured in 3.5 cm cell culture dishes and then the effect of HAgCPX nanoparticles on the migration potential of HGF-1 cells was evaluated. Cells were treated with CPX or HAgCPX nanoparticles for 12 h. CPX dose were 20 µg/mL. These were washed with PBS once more and then LPS (10 μg/ml) was treated to cells for 12 h. After that, cells were washed with PBS and then scratched with yellow tip. Cells were washed with PBS, replaced with fresh media and then cultured 48 h in 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. Following this, cells were carefully washed with PBS, and then the field of wound healing and cell migration was observed using light microscopy (Olympus CKX 53, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Apoptosis/necrosis assay

The effect of CPX or HAgCPX nanoparticles on the apoptosis/necrosis behavior of HGF-1 cells was monitored using FITC–annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) (sc-4252 FITC, Santa Cruz Biotech., Inc., Dallas, Texas, USA). Cells were treated with CPX or HAgCPX nanoparticles for 12 h. CPX dose were 20 µg/mL. These were washed with PBS once more and then LPS (10 μg/ml) was treated to cells for 12 h. Cells were washed with PBS, harvested by trypsinization and then collected pellets. These were washed with PBS and resuspended into the binding buffer containing 5 µL FITC–annexin V and 10 µL PI to stain apoptotic cells and necrotic cells, respectively. These were incubated for 10 min in the 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. The apoptotic/necrotic cells were analyzed with a FACScan flow cytometer (Becton Dickenson Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) (Eexcitation: 488 nm for FITC–annexin; 575 nm for PI).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed on data obtained from at least three independent experiments. Results were presented as mean ± SD, data were analyzed using Student’s t-test, and p < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. SigmaPlot® program (Version 11.0, Grafiti LLC Co., Palo Alto, CA, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA) were used for data analysis.

The yield of HAgCPX copolymer was approximately 93% (w/w) as a weight measurement, indicated that HAgCPX copolymer successfully synthesized. Physicochemical properties of HAgCPX nanoparticles

Nanoparticles of HAgCPX copolymers were fabricated by dialysis method and their morphology and particle sizes were measured as shown in Fig. 2. As shown in Fig. 2Aa and Ba, they have spherical shapes in morphological observation using TEM and their average particle sizes were 292 nm with monomodal distribution pattern. These results indicated that HAgCPX copolymers were appropriately aggregated in the aqueous solution and then formed nanoparticles. To study ROS-sensitive behavior of HAgCPX nanoparticles, they were incubated in the presence of H2O2 and then their physicochemical properties were studied as shown in Fig. 2 and Table 2. When HAgCPX nanoparticles were incubated with H2O2 as shown in Fig. 2, they responded to H2O2 and then disintegrated as shown in Fig. 2Ab–d and Bb–d. Morphologies of HAgCPX nanoparticles were disintegrated in the presence of H2O2 and their morphologies became more distorted form according to the H2O2 concentration. Furthermore, particle size distribution was changed from monomodal to multimodal pattern according to the H2O2 concentration. They began to be disintegrated in the presence of H2O2, aggregated with each other and then revealed multi-modal distribution pattern (Fig. 2Bb–d). Then, average particle sizes from multi size distribution were fluctuated by disintegration and aggregation with each other (Table 2). These results indicated that HAgCPX nanoparticles have ROS-sensitivity and then they can respond to oxidative stress in biological system. Figure 2C and D showed the effect of H2O2 on the changes of UV spectra and drug release properties of HAgCPX nanoparticles. As shown in Fig. 2C, HAgCPX nanoparticles in aqueous solution showed intrinsic broad UV spectra between 200 and 400 nm while CPX itself has complex UV spectra between 220 nm and 280 nm. HAgCPX nanoparticles in aqueous solution showed simple UV spectrum below 300 nm and they have relatively little absorption between 240 and 300 nm while CPX itself have intrinsic UV spectrum in these ranges. When H2O2 was added to aqueous solution of HAgCPX nanoparticles, their UV spectrum was significantly changed and then showed different UV spectrum patterns at 220 ~ 280 nm. These results indicated that HAgCPX nanoparticles can be disintegrated in the presence of H2O2 and then CPX can be liberated by stimulus such as oxidative stress. Discussion

Since oral cavity is one of the most complex microenvironments in the human body, infection and/or inflammatory disease of oral cavity is not only associated with the local disease but also associated with the systemic disease such as diabetes, arthritis and heart disease6,23. The most of the infection and/or inflammatory disease in the oral cavity starts from the soft tissues of the periodontium and the pathological changes by inflammation in the periodontium is frequently associated with the bone and tooth loss1,2,6. Furthermore, periodontitis is a one of the major oral infections and is known to affect the host’s susceptibility against systemic disease23. The subgingival biofilms and periodontium act as reservoirs of gram-negative bacteria and as a reservoir of inflammatory mediators, respectively23. Chemotherapeutic option using antimicrobial agents is still one of major treatment regimen for oral and systemic infection/inflammation24. However, antimicrobial agents frequently associated with drug resistant problems and delivery of antibiotics or anti-inflammatory drugs to the therapeutic region of a deep soft tissue cell are problematic25. They argued that continuous drug release formulation of bone graft materials provides useful solution for decontamination of implant surface and healing of soft/hard tissues. However, biocompatibility of these materials should be approved during long durations of drug release because biocompatibility is an essential feature for material longevity in the oral cavity26.

Otherwise, the inflammation of soft tissues also induces the pathophysiological changes, i.e. the increase of various receptors, cytokines, oxidative stress and acidity6,11,13–15,27–31. Especially, inflammation of oral soft tissues increases the expression of inflammatory-related proteins/physiological changes such as VEGF, microvessel density and CD4413. Lucarini et al. reported that the expression of VEGF, CD34, CD44, and peri-implant pocket depth were positively correlated and then the changes of these signals can be used as an inflammation marker of oral disease13. In our study, HGF-1 cells positively expressed CD44 receptor under inflammatory state, i.e. HGF-1 cells strongly expressed CD44 receptor when they were infected with LPS as shown in Fig. 6A. Furthermore, the increase of CD44 receptor in the HGF-1 cells can be used a therapeutic target. the expression of these receptors can be used to target inflammatory cells32. Kosovrasti et al. reported that HA-based nanoparticles have targetability against LPS-activated peritoneal macrophages and then silence TNF-α32. Our study also indicated that LPS-treated inflammatory HGF-1 cells strongly expressed CD44 receptor and then these cells are able to be targeted by HAgCPX nanoparticles as shown in Fig. 6A and B. Since HGF-1 cells strongly expressed CD44 receptor under inflammatory condition, HAgCPX nanoparticles can be specifically delivered via CD44 receptor of inflammatory HGF-1 cells. However, pre-treatment of free HA inhibited intracellular delivery of HAgCPX nanoparticles against LPS-treated HGF-1 cells (Fig. 6B), indicating that HAgCPX nanoparticles can be specifically delivered to the site of inflammation or infection through CD44 receptor specific manner. Furthermore, they can be degraded in the site of inflammation and then liberated antibiotics with ROS-specific manner since inflammation and/or infection induces higher oxidative stress in the cells14,15. The inflammation is always associated with increase of the oxidative stress11,14,15,28,30. As shown in Fig. 2, HAgCPX nanoparticles were disintegrated and/or aggregated with each other under oxidative stress, and then revealed multimodal distribution (Fig. 2Bb–d). The average particle size was fluctuated by treatment of H2O2, indicating that HAgCPX nanoparticles have potential to respond against oxidative stress and then disintegrated with accelerated drug release (Fig. 2). Mijiritsky et al. reported that oxidative stress-related genes influence the commitment of Mesenchymal stem cells and then these changes are related to the loss of tooth14. Furthermore, increased ROS level in the inflammatory or infected cells inhibited wound healing process and then induced the deterioration of wound healing11. ROS accumulation in gingival fibroblast cells significantly decreases migration and proliferation of gingival fibroblasts from rats with diabetes mellitus33. Otherwise, these changes in physiology of oral cavity can be also used as a targeting strategy16,18,31. Pu et al. reported that various kinds of nanoparticle-based drug delivery system efficiently target inflammatory cells because nanomedicines have the advantages in the enhancements of therapeutic effects and the reduction of side effects through the concentration of drug in the site of disease16. The changes of microenvironment in oral cavity can be used to regulate nanoparticle-based drug delivery18. Xiao et al., reported that liposome-encapsulated indocyanine green (ICG) and rapamycin drug-delivery nanoparticle reduced cellular inflammation by modulating microbial-cellular interaction and then upregulate the anti-inflammatory factor TGF-β18. Our results also indicated that HAgCPX nanoparticles efficiently inhibited the secretion of inflammatory cytokines through CD44 receptor-mediated manner (Figs. 4b and 7). HAgCPX nanoparticles responded to the inflammation and/or infection process and then specifically deliver the antibiotics to the inflammatory cells (Fig. 6B and 9). The anti-inflammatory activity of HAgCPX nanoparticles was also inhibited by blocking of CD44 receptor (Fig. 7), i.e. the secretion of inflammatory cytokine was increased by CD44 receptor blocking while CPX itself was not affected by blocking of CD44 receptor, indicating that anti-inflammatory and/or anti-infection activity of HAgCPX nanoparticles can be controlled by CD44 receptor-mediated manner. HAgCPX nanoparticles showed almost similar antibacterial activity compared to CPX itself (Fig. 8), i.e. CPX dimer might also has similar antibacterial activity because CPX dimer was grafted to the backbone of HA and then CPX, CPX dimer or CPX-thioketal debris were released from nanoparticles as shown in Fig. 2C. Compared to CPX itself, HAgCPX nanoparticles formed reasonable antibacterial clear zone and have similar effect on the growth inhibition of oral bacteria even though the clear zone of HAgCPX was slightly smaller than that of CPX itself (Fig. 8A–D). These results indicated that HAgCPX nanoparticles have reasonable antibacterial activity as well as CPX itself and, however, they must be revealed relatively better biological activity since they have superior targetability against inflammatory oral soft tissue cells with CD44/ROS specific manner.
