Current practices in delirium prevention and treatment after acute stroke: results of a multicentre survey
Authors: Markus Kneihsl, Annerose Mengel, Stefan Sumerauer, Natalie Berger, Margherita Cavalieri, Thomas Gattringer, Christian Enzinger, Wilfried Lang, Michael Knoflach, Mira Katan, Else Sandset, Robert Fleischmann, Julia Ferrari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Delirium affects 10–30% of acute stroke patients, is associated with poor outcomes and places a substantial burden on healthcare staff. Evidence-based prevention and treatment strategies are limited, resulting in heterogeneous and poorly standardised delirium management in acute stroke care.Objectives:We aimed to describe real-world delirium management in German and Austrian stroke units (SUs) and to delineate the extent of practice variation in prevention and treatment.Design:We conducted a cross-sectional, web-based survey of stroke neurologists working at SUs in Austria and Germany.Methods:The 30-item questionnaire assessed professional background, delirium prevention and pharmacological treatment approaches. Descriptive statistics and exploratory subgroup analyses were performed.Results:Seventy responses from 63 SUs were analysed (median physician SU experience, 14.5 years). Delirium was estimated to affect 20% of patients and was perceived as highly burdensome for both physicians and nurses (median score, 9/10 each). Delirium prevention measures were established in 42 (67%) SUs but routinely applied to all patients in only 12 (19%), mainly due to staffing shortages (nurses, 48%; physicians, 25%). Pharmacological delirium treatment was reported by all respondents. Benzodiazepines were preferred for alcohol withdrawal delirium (60%) and antipsychotics for hyperactive or mixed delirium (59%). α2-Agonists were the most common escalation therapy across these subtypes (46%–67%).Conclusion:Delirium management in German and Austrian SUs is highly heterogeneous, limited by staffing constraints and strongly relies on non-evidence-based pharmacological strategies. These findings highlight critical gaps in care and call for enhanced staffing and stroke-specific trials to inform evidence-based delirium management.

Introduction and background

Delirium is a common and serious neuropsychiatric complication of acute stroke, defined as a (sub-)acute fluctuating disturbance of attention, awareness and cognition.1,2 It affects approximately 10%–30% of patients admitted to a stroke unit (SU) and is associated with prolonged hospitalisation, increased post-stroke disability, higher readmission rates and mortality.3 –6 Beyond its adverse effects on patient outcomes, delirium imposes a substantial burden on healthcare professionals, as its management requires continuous monitoring and coordinated multiprofessional care. 7 Despite its high clinical relevance, evidence-based strategies for the prevention and treatment of delirium in stroke patients remain limited.8 –10

Therefore, current recommendations for delirium management are largely extrapolated from postoperative and intensive care unit populations. 8 However, these approaches may not be directly applicable to stroke patients owing to distinct pathophysiological mechanisms, 11 clinical presentations and therapeutic constraints. Large, high-quality clinical trials addressing delirium prevention and treatment in stroke patients are conspicuously absent, resulting in considerable clinical uncertainty and heterogeneous practice patterns. 9

To characterise contemporary delirium management in the absence of clear evidence, we conducted a survey of stroke neurologists in Austrian and German SUs. By systematically mapping current practices, perceived challenges and therapeutic preferences, this study aims to quantify practice variation, identify underused prevention measures and treatment approaches and define priority areas for future research and quality improvement in this vulnerable patient population.

Methods

Study design and survey development

We conducted a cross-sectional survey of physicians working in SUs to assess current practices in prevention, diagnosis and treatment of delirium in acute stroke patients. The study was reported in accordance with the Checklist for Reporting Of Survey Studies (CROSS). 12

Survey items were predefined, comprised 30 questions across 4 sections (Professional Background; Screening and Diagnosis; Prevention Strategies; Treatment Approaches) and required approximately 10 min to complete. Item generation was based on a targeted literature review addressing delirium epidemiology, screening instruments and non-pharmacological and pharmacological interventions in neurological and non-neurological populations, from which we derived a set of core, repeatedly reported items representing convergent key components of delirium care.4 –6,9

Validation and survey structure

Content validity was assessed by five board-certified stroke neurologists (MaKn, JuFe, ThGa, AnMe, RoFl) with expertise in delirium management, who reviewed the draft survey for clarity, conceptual relevance and cognitive burden. Items with ambiguous wording were revised. Cognitive interviews with six clinicians from the target population confirmed appropriate interpretation of questions and response options. Branching logic was implemented to ensure that follow-up questions were displayed only when applicable (e.g. medication-specific items shown only to respondents indicating pharmacological delirium management).

Most questions used closed-ended response formats with binary response options, Likert scales or continuous rating scales to enhance consistency and comparability; limited free-text fields were included to capture additional practices not covered by predefined options. Attitudes and perceptions were assessed using numeric rating scales.

Survey administration and distribution

The survey was created and administered using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) and hosted at the Medical University of Graz, Austria. In addition to English language, it was also available in German to facilitate participation among clinicians in Austria and Germany. Invitations were distributed via national professional societies (i.e. the Austrian Stroke Society and the German Stroke Society) to all affiliated SUs.

SUs were instructed to submit a single response by their lead physician or a designated substitute. In cases where more than one response was received from a single centre, responses were handled according to the level of analysis. For analyses of institutional practices (survey items 1a–1e, see Supplemental Material), only the first submitted response was included to avoid overrepresentation. For analyses addressing individual treatment decisions (survey items 2a–2b, see Supplemental Material), all submitted responses were included, as each response was considered to represent a valid and relevant clinical perspective.

Participants accessed the survey through a web-based link; participation was voluntary and anonymous. The open survey was launched on 1 December 2024, and closed on 31 July 2025.

Data handling and statistical analysis

Data were stored securely with restricted access. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistics, Version 30 (IBM Corp, Armonk, USA). Descriptive statistics are reported as numbers (%) for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables. Fisher’s exact test and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test were used as appropriate.

Responses to numeric rating scales (0–10 or 0–100) are presented as medians with 95% confidence intervals. Analyses were based on complete cases only and no imputation of missing data was performed. The survey was designed to require completion of all items prior to submission, therefore, no partial responses were recorded. The study did not include a predefined sample size calculation because it was hypothesis-generating, and effect sizes could not be reliably estimated a priori; instead, all accessible and willing respondents were invited to participate to maximise sample coverage.

Exploratory subgroup analyses compared responses by hospital type (tertiary care centres, defined as centres with access to advanced neurovascular interventions including mechanical thrombectomy and neurosurgical services, vs non-tertiary care centres), physician experience (defined as >5 vs ⩽5 years of SU experience) and country (Austria vs Germany). A two-sided significance level of 0.05 was applied without adjustment for multiple comparisons, given the exploratory design of the current study. To address potential within-centre clustering in individual-level analyses, sensitivity analyses restricted to one respondent per SU (first submitted response) were performed.

Results

Within the study period, 70 responses of stroke neurologists were returned from 63 SUs (Germany: 36/354, 10%; Austria: 27/37, 73%). Responding physicians had a median SU experience of 14.5 years (IQR 8–20 years), and nearly half of the responses were from tertiary care stroke centres (n = 33, 47%).

Estimated delirium prevalence and burden

Respondents reported a subjective estimate of median delirium prevalence of 20% (IQR 15%–30%) among SU admissions. Hyperactive delirium was the most frequently reported subtype (33%, IQR 20%–60%). The perceived burden was rated high for both nurses and physicians (median score, 9/10 each, IQR 8–10). These estimates were independent of physician experience and hospital type (p > 0.1; Table 1).

Delirium screening and prevention

Routine delirium screening was performed in 37 SUs (59%), employing 7 different instruments, most commonly the Nu-DESC (n = 12, 19%). Preventive measures were established in 42 (67%) SUs, but were applied to all patients only in 12 (19%), primarily due to staffing shortages (nurses: 48%; physicians: 25%; Table 1; Supplemental Tables 1 and 2).

Regarding pharmacological delirium prevention, nearly half of respondents (n = 30, 43%) reported substance replacement therapy for patients with known dependence (e.g. alcohol abuse). Fourteen physicians (20%) routinely used melatonin for circadian rhythm support. Independently of these measures, another 14 respondents (20%) reported routine pharmacological delirium prophylaxis, most commonly antipsychotics (20%) and benzodiazepines (10%; Table 2).

SUs led by more experienced physicians (>5 vs ⩽5 years) had more often implemented routine delirium prevention measures (73% vs 36%, p = 0.019), with a trend toward more frequent screening (64% vs 36%, p = 0.097). More experienced stroke neurologists also tended to use prophylactic drugs less often (16% vs 36%, p = 0.100). Of note, routine delirium prevention practices were more frequently implemented in tertiary compared with non-tertiary care centres (83% vs 53%, p = 0.012; Table 1).

Pharmacological delirium treatment

Pharmacological treatment was used by all participating stroke neurologists. The most common indications were risk of harm to self or others (n = 70, 100%), psychotic symptoms (n = 68, 97%) and vegetative dysregulation (n = 68, 94%), whereas restlessness/increased verbal activity (n = 38, 54%) and hypoactive symptoms (n = 44, 63%) led to treatment less often. Pharmacological treatment tended to be initiated less frequently for hypoactive delirium by stroke neurologists working in tertiary care than in non-tertiary care stroke centres (52% vs 73%, p = 0.063), whereas other patterns were independent of hospital type or physician experience (all p > 0.1; Table 2).

Drug selection varied by delirium subtype. At the substance-class level, benzodiazepines were the predominant first-line therapy for alcohol withdrawal delirium (n = 42, 60% of respondents). Antipsychotics were preferred for hyperactive delirium with psychotic features (n = 49, 70%) and psychomotor agitation (n = 33, 47%) and for mixed delirium (n = 41, 59%). Management of hypoactive delirium was inconsistent: 36 respondents (51%) reported to use antipsychotics first-line, whereas 26 (37%) indicated no pharmacological treatment (Figure 1). For escalation therapy, α2-agonists were most commonly selected across subtypes (alcohol withdrawal: 67%; hyperactive with psychotic symptoms: 51%; hyperactive with agitation: 46%; mixed: 51%), while hypoactive delirium was typically not escalated pharmacologically (46%; Figure 2 and Table 2).

Regarding individual agents used for delirium treatment in general, risperidone was most frequently reported for hyperactive delirium with psychotic features (n = 63, 90%), mixed delirium (n = 61, 87%) and hypoactive delirium (n = 38, 54%). In hyperactive delirium with predominant psychomotor agitation, quetiapine and clonidine were equally preferred (n = 48, 69% each). Clonidine was also the leading agent for alcohol withdrawal delirium (n = 60, 86%). Sensitivity analyses restricted to one respondent per SU yielded materially unchanged results. Discussion

This study provides contemporary insights into routine delirium management across a large cohort of German and Austrian SUs. The survey demonstrates a substantial burden associated with delirium care in acute stroke, as perceived by stroke neurologists. Although delirium prevention strategies are widely endorsed by staff, their limited implementation appears primarily driven by staffing constraints rather than lack of acceptance or perceived relevance. Pharmacological management is frequently employed across delirium subtypes, with antipsychotics (predominantly risperidone and quetiapine) being the most commonly used agents for first-line treatment and α2-agonists for escalation therapy.

Evidence guiding delirium management in acute stroke remains scarce, resulting in overall low evidence levels for both preventive and therapeutic strategies.9,12 –14 Nevertheless, the recognised clinical burden has prompted national societies to issue expert-based recommendations despite limited supporting data. 9 Consistent with this, stroke physicians rated the burden of delirium care to be very high (median 9 on a 10-point scale) in the present survey. Respondents also provided subjective estimates of delirium frequency and subtype distribution in routine SU care, which should be interpreted as clinician perceptions rather than objective epidemiological measures. In this context, the higher perceived frequency of hyperactive delirium compared with prior literature may reflect its greater clinical visibility, staff workload and recognition relative to other delirium subtypes.

Despite the high perceived relevance of delirium prevention (also rated 9 of 10), structured screening and preventive measures were implemented infrequently. Although many SUs reported the use of delirium prevention measures, fewer than 20% applied preventive strategies universally to all stroke patients, which contrasts expert-based recommendations. 9 Importantly, our survey gives insights that the omission was not attributed to insufficient stroke-specific evidence but rather to limited personnel resources. This finding is not unexpected, as delirium prevention requires a resource-intensive, multiprofessional approach.9,14 Our survey highlights the necessity of embedding delirium prevention as a shared responsibility across the entire stroke care team, supported by adequate staffing and its systematic integration into daily clinical routines. Evidence from previous studies indicates that such structured approaches are cost-effective and may reduce delirium incidence by up to 30%.15 –19

A particularly noteworthy finding is that one-third of participating SUs reported using pharmacological agents – including benzodiazepines, antipsychotics and α2-agonists – for delirium prevention. Substance replacement therapy, which is guideline-recommended and routinely used, was explicitly excluded from this analysis. Given the lack of supporting evidence and the absence of recommendations for routine pharmacological delirium prophylaxis, this finding deserves critical consideration. Sedative or antipsychotic agents may carry relevant risks, including oversedation, aspiration, falls, cardiovascular adverse effects, and may interfere with neurological assessment. 20 The observed use of preventive pharmacotherapy in routine practice therefore highlights an important discrepancy between current evidence and clinical practice and underscores the need for randomised controlled trials in this field.

In this context, the biological heterogeneity of post-stroke delirium should also be considered, as delirium risk and clinical presentation are likely influenced by patient- and stroke-specific factors such as age, premorbid cognitive impairment, stroke severity and lesion characteristics.8,9 Uniform preventive strategies may therefore be overly simplistic, and future approaches may benefit from risk-adapted prevention strategies.

Drug selection for specific delirium subtypes was largely consistent with international practice recommendations.9,21 Beyond substance classes, our survey provides novel insights into the specific agents most frequently used in routine clinical care. Given the absence of comparative studies within drug classes, these data offer valuable hypotheses for future interventional trials.

Notably, a relatively high proportion of respondents reported pharmacological treatment of hypoactive delirium, despite the absence of supportive evidence or an established pathophysiological rationale for symptomatic drug therapy in this delirium subtype. This observation may reflect diagnostic uncertainty, fluctuating clinical presentations (including mixed delirium) or pragmatic attempts to improve patient engagement in SU care. However, medication use in hypoactive delirium may be particularly problematic, as side effects may either cause psychiatric disturbances that are difficult to monitor (e.g. covert hallucinations) or enhance sedation, impair early mobilisation and rehabilitation and increase the risk of complications such as aspiration. 22 These findings highlight an important evidence–practice gap and support the need for dedicated interventional studies evaluating whether pharmacological strategies in hypoactive delirium improve clinically meaningful outcomes or instead contribute to avoidable harm in stroke patients.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, the survey was conducted exclusively in Austria and Germany, two countries with comparable high-quality healthcare systems; therefore, generalizability to other regions may be limited. Second, response bias cannot be excluded, as SUs with greater interest in delirium management may have been more likely to participate. Response rates differed substantially between countries, with high coverage in Austria (73% of SUs) but substantially lower participation in Germany (approximately 10% of certified SUs), which limits representativeness, particularly for the German cohort. However, the survey included a balanced distribution of tertiary and non-tertiary care centres, demonstrated consistent findings across both countries (for details see Supplemental Tables 4–6) and involved highly experienced stroke neurologists – supporting the exploratory value of the results.

Third, while one response per SU was used for institutional-level analyses, within-unit differences may not be fully captured. Conversely, inclusion of multiple respondents from the same centre in individual-level analyses may introduce clustering effects; however, this concerned only a small proportion of centres (7 of 63 SUs), and sensitivity analyses restricted to one respondent per SU yielded materially unchanged results.

Finally, as no formal sample size calculation was performed for this exploratory survey, subgroup analyses should be interpreted cautiously and not as confirmatory comparisons.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the substantial burden of delirium in acute stroke care and underscores the urgent need for enhanced staffing and structural support to enable effective delirium prevention. Moreover, the marked heterogeneity in pharmacological prevention and treatment practices reinforces the critical need for well-designed randomised controlled trials addressing both preventive and therapeutic delirium strategies in stroke patients.
