Abstract
Blockchain-based smart contracts offer a new take on credible commitment, where players can commit to actions in reaction to actions of others. Such reaction-function games extend on strategic games with players choosing reaction functions instead of strategies. We formalize a solution concept in terms of fixed points for such games, akin to Nash equilibrium, and prove equilibrium existence. Reaction functions can mimic “trigger” strategies from folk theorems on infinitely repeated games—but now in a one-shot setting.We introduce a refinement in terms of safe play . We apply our theoretical framework to symmetric investment games, which includes two prominent classes of games, namely weakest-link and public-good games. In both cases, we identify a safe and optimal reaction function. In this way, our findings highlight how blockchain-based commitment can overcome trust and free-riding barriers.



1
Introduction

Smart contracts are digital tools that allow agents to coordinate in a decentralized environment
(
Gans 2021
;
Bakos and Halaburda 2023
;
Gudmundsson et al. 2024
)
.
In short, a smart contract is a piece of computer code with a unique address on a blockchain.
Agents with shared access to the blockchain can deploy such contracts, deposit funds to them, and hard-code how these funds are used in reaction to choices made by others.
As such, an agent can let a contract act automatically on her behalf, in effect making contract design a strategic choice.
Once deployed, a contract cannot be changed.
In this way, smart contracts ensure commitment as strong as the cryptographic security of the network itself
(
Liang et al. 2024
;
Raskin 2017
, e.g.)
.

We submit that smart contracts can be used as commitment devices when agents play a one-shot game using conditional strategies.
In particular, the contract allows players to program
reaction functions
(i.e., actions chosen by the player contingent on the actions chosen by the other players), which in turn can be submitted to a coordinating contract that determines the outcome that is realized and executed.
Such simple commitment devices are well suited to handle a broad class of symmetric investment games prominent in economics.
To this point, we illustrate how reaction-function play allows players to overcome trust issues in
weakest-link games
and help players battle free-riding in
public-good games
.

As such, we follow up on a line of literature that mixes ideas from game theory and computer science, dating back to von Neumann and further developed through “metagames”
(
Howard 1971
)
, “program equilibrium”
(
Tennenholtz 2004
)
, and “commitment games”
(
Kalai et al. 2010
)
.
In short, the common element is that players’ strategies consists of commitment devices/programs which specify actions in response to the devices chosen by the others.
In
Tennenholtz 2004
, players choose programs that specify a probability distribution over actions (mixed strategy) in response to every profile of programs chosen by the others.
A program equilibrium is thus a Nash equilibrium (in pure strategies) in the game with strategy spaces consisting of programs rather than actions.
The notion of program equilibrium allows a form of “folk theorem” in a one-shot context:
any outcome with at least maxmin payoffs is achievable in program equilibrium
(
Tennenholtz 2004
, Theorem 2 in)
.
1
1

1

Further developments of program equilibrium include
Monderer and Tennenholtz 2009
,
Oesterheld 2019
, and
Cooper et al. 2025
.

An analogous result is established in
Kalai et al. 2010
using their notion of commitment games.
In contrast to
Tennenholtz 2004
, their device specifies an action (pure strategy) in response to the devices chosen by others, and they allow for mixed-strategy equilibria in the commitment game.

Our reaction functions can be viewed as a simpler type of commitment device with the important difference that we condition on the actions (pure strategies) of the other players and not their devices.
Like
Tennenholtz 2004
, we do not consider mixed strategies.
While reaction functions are easier to formulate for the players and thereby more useful for practical implementation, they introduce the problem of determining the outcome of a profile of reaction functions as there can be zero, one, or multiple fixed points for a given profile of reaction functions.
We introduce two assumptions to deal with this problem:
players evaluate a profile by their preferred fixed point of the profile, and players prefer any outcome to a lack of fixed point (in which case we can imagine that players “try again”).
Since, we still may have situations where players expect different fixed-points to be implemented on the basis of the same profile of reaction functions we further focus on
unambiguous
profiles for which there is a Pareto-superior fixed-point.
With these assumptions, we define a reaction-function equilibrium as an unambiguous profile of reaction functions for which there is no beneficial unilateral deviation.
In other words, no player can hope to realize a fixed point with higher payoff given the reaction functions of the other players.

In our first result, Theorem
3.1
, we prove existence of reaction-function equilibrium for any reaction function-game.
For two-player games, Theorem
3.2
characterizes the set of outcomes supported in reaction-function equilibrium as those where players get at least their maxmin payoff.
This mimics the “folk theorems” obtained in
Tennenholtz 2004
and
Kalai et al. 2010
.
Yet, for games with more players, we can support essentially all outcomes in reaction-function equilibrium (Theorem
3.3
).
This is a consequence of that, with many players, reaction functions can be arranged such that fixed points are ruled out for any unilateral deviation.
This calls for further refinements of the equilibrium concept.
Loosely speaking, we then demonstrate that even though “bad” outcomes can be supported in artificial reactions, only “good” ones can be supported in compelling ways.

A natural starting point are reaction functions that guarantee a player at least her maxmin payoff.
In conventional strategic games, a player can always “play safe” by choosing the maxmin action.
However, this may turn out to yield overly conservative outcomes as illustrated by the
Prisoner’s dilemma
game in which safe play coincides with the unique Pareto-dominated Nash equilibrium.
Reaction functions allow us to do better in the sense that, if safe play constitutes a Nash equilibrium in the strategic game, then we can support any Pareto improvement in safe reaction-function equilibrium (Theorem
4.1
).
Yet, there are also games for which there does not exist a safe reaction-function equilibrium (Example
2
).

To illustrate the potential of reaction functions as commitment devices, we zoom in on the class of symmetric investment games that includes the so-called weakest-link and public-good games.
For these games, we first show that if all players’ reaction functions exhibit “conditional collaboration” (i.e., collaborate if the others do, and otherwise undercut the max investment of the others) we are able to support the Pareto-superior “high investment” outcome in reaction-function equilibrium (Theorem
5.2
).

In weakest-link games, each player wants to match the minimum investment of the others, and the higher this is, the better for all
(
Riedl et al. 2016
, e.g.)
.
The Pareto superior outcome where all invest the highest amount is now a Nash equilibrium, as is every coordinated action where players invest the same amount, but the strategy to always play the lowest amount is “safe”. There appears to be an obvious solution—all invest the maximum amount—yet there is overwhelming experimental evidence that players fail to coordinate on the efficient equilibrium
(
Van Huyck et al. 1990
;
Battalio et al. 2001
;
Weber et al. 2001
;
Brandts and Cooper 2006
;
Weber 2006
)
, with some rare exceptions
(
Engelmann and Normann 2010
;
Riedl et al. 2016
;
Riedl et al. 2021
)
.
The problem is that the mere belief or expectation that someone else will play the safe minimum investment action is all it takes to justify playing minimum investment yourself. In general, the more players involved, the harder it gets:even if player
1
can credibly commit to the highest level, it will not sway player
2
if there is doubt on player
3
’s intentions
(
Aumann 1990
, e.g.)
.
To overcome this, we consider the class of reaction functions where players exhibit ”conditional collaboration” and ”payoff consistency” (choose the same reaction across payoff equivalent action profiles) and single out the best-reply reaction function that matches the minimum investment of the others (Theorem
5.3
). This reaction function is safe, and in the specific case of so-called ”high risk” weakest-link games (where investing higher amounts comes with greater risk) it is further singled out as being weakly dominant against monotone play (Theorem
5.4
). This yields a simple, safe, and trustless way to obtain the Pareto-superior outcome and can overcome the inherent curse of mistrust.

In public-good games, each player’s investment benefits everybody, but is a net loss to the player herself
(
Bergstrom et al. 1986
, e.g.)
.
Again, coordination around investing the maximal amount is welfare maximizing, but choosing to invest the minimum amount is safe and dominant.
In Theorem
5.5
we show that ”conditional collaboration” and ”payoff consistency” this time singles out the reaction-function where a player reacts by investing the average (rounded down) of what the others have invested.
In the particular case of high-risk public-good games, this reaction-function is further maximizing welfare subject to safe play (Theorem
5.6
).
This yields a simple and safe way to obtain the Pareto-superior outcome and overcome the inherent curse of free-riding.

In practice, setting up smart contracts in case of a weakest-link game, for example, will be rather simple:
agents submit contracts (e.g. in the form of best-reply reaction functions that matches the lowest investment of the others) to a coordinating contract and deposits an amount of money corresponding to the maximum amount they prefer to invest.
The coordinating contract takes the profile of deposits as the starting point and applies the reaction functions to find a fixed point.
The fixed point (e.g. the profile corresponding to the smallest deposit among the players) is implemented in the sense that the contract withdraws the amount in question from each player’s deposit and returns potential surplus amounts.
In such a setting, players can therefore safely deposit the amount corresponding to the greatest mutual benefit knowing that they will never collectively miscoordinate to their individual disadvantage.

Reaction functions have been used extensively in experimental work since
Selten 1967
, there termed the “strategy method”
(
Selten et al. 1997
, e.g.,)
.
Eliciting reaction functions
(
Fischbacher et al. 2001
;
Thöni and Volk 2018
, “contribution tables” in)
yields a more comprehensive view of the experimental subject’s thought process and allows for instance testing “off-path” behavior (e.g. to compare lab data with subgame-perfection predictions).
As it is demanding to map out a complete contingency plan, subjects are given plenty of time and may make more thoughtful decisions compared to the “direct-response method”
(
Brandts and Charness 2011
)
.
In short, reaction functions are used to derive additional insights on subjects’ behavior in the strategic game being played.
But the goal is merely to
elicit
reaction functions—there is no need to play them out against each other, especially with potential issues of non-existence or multiplicity of fixed points.
For contrast, fixed points of the reaction-function profile are fundamental in our analysis.
To illustrate the methodological difference, in
Fischbacher et al. 2001
all subjects submit a reaction function
and
an unconditional strategy (corresponding to a constant reaction), after which one player is drawn at random and the realized outcome is uniquely determined (compare Remark
1
) by that player’s reaction to the others’ unconditional strategies.
An exception is
Oechssler et al. 2022
, who compute fixed points in a repeated public-good setting but limit to monotone step-function reactions (in at most two steps);
see also
Reischmann and Oechssler 2018
.

2
Games and definitions

In this section, we introduce
reaction-function games
and relate them to conventional
strategic games
.
In strategic games, formalized in Subsection
2.1
, players individually select actions that jointly result in an outcome over which players have preferences.
The classic solution concept is Nash equilibrium.
In reaction-function games, players individually specify their reaction to the actions of other players and are able to commit to these reactions.
Determining the outcome of a reaction-function profile is now more intricate and consequently defining the game and a natural equilibrium concept requires further assumptions.
This is covered in Subsections
2.2
–
2.4
.
We assume players have complete information on the game.

2.1
Strategic games

There is a finite set of
n

players

N
.
Each player
i
has a finite set of at least two
actions

A_{i}
.
An

a\in A\equiv\times_{i}A_{i}
is an action profile.
Each player
i

payoff function

u_{i}\colon A\to\mathbb{R}
, where
i
derives payoff
u_{i}(a)
at outcome

.
The triple
(N,A,(u_{i})_{i})
defines a strategic game.
A
Nash equilibrium
is an action profile
a
such that, for each player
i
and action
a^{\prime}_{i}\in A_{i}
,
u_{i}(a)\geq u_{i}(a^{\prime}_{i},a_{-i})
.

2

As usual, the subscript

projects onto the subspace relative to
N\setminus\{i\}
and
a=(a_{i},a_{-i})
.

Let

.
Note that existence of a Nash equilibrium is not guaranteed as we do not consider mixed strategies.

2.2
Reaction functions

In reaction-function games, players choose
reaction functions
rather than actions.
A
reaction function

R_{i}\colon A_{-i}\to A_{i}
specifies the action
R_{i}(a_{-i})\in A_{i}
that player
i
commits to play in reaction to the actions
a_{-i}\in A_{-i}
of the other players.
That is, we now map out a complete plan of what to do contingent on the actions of the others.
Let
\mathcal{R}_{i}
be player
i
’s reaction functions.
As the number of actions is finite,
\mathcal{R}_{i}
is also finite.
A profile of reaction functions, a
profile
for short, is
R\in\mathcal{R}\equiv\times_{i\in N}\mathcal{R}_{i}
.
For
a\in A
, let
R(a)\in A
be the action profile in which each player
i
plays action
R_{i}(a_{-i})
.

If players are restricted to degenerate,
constant
reaction functions, then we recover strategic games as defined in Subsection
2.1
.
These comprise choosing the same action
a_{i}
irrespective of the choices
a_{-i}
of the others:
set
R_{i}(a_{-i})=a_{i}
for each
a_{-i}\in A_{-i}
.
In economic applications with decision variables such as quantities and prices, it may be natural to assume an order
\leq_{i}
on each action set
A_{i}
.
Constant reactions then belong to the larger class of
monotone
(non-decreasing) reactions for which
a_{-i}\leqq a^{\prime}_{-i}
implies
R_{i}(a_{-i})\leq R_{i}(a^{\prime}_{-i})
, where
a_{-i}\leqq a^{\prime}_{-i}
whenever
a_{j}\leq_{j}a^{\prime}_{j}
for each player
j\neq i
.
For example, in games with strategic complements
(
Topkis 1979
;
Vives 1990
;
Echenique 2004
, e.g.)
, where an increase in one player’s action makes the other players want to increase theirs, the
best-reply
reaction
\textit{BR}_{i}(a_{-i})\in\arg\max_{a_{i}\in A_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},a_{-i})
is monotone.

The strategy space of reaction-function games is orders of magnitude larger than that for strategic games.
With
n
players, each with
m
actions, there are
\left|{A}\right|=m^{n}
outcomes (i.e., action profiles) whereas there are
\left|{\mathcal{R}}\right|=(m^{n})^{n}
reaction-function profiles.

2.3
Executable outcomes in reaction-function games

Reaction functions are commitments to certain actions in reaction to the actions of the others.
In this way, an outcome can be executed if and only if player commitments align:
for each player
i
, the action
a_{i}
must match
i
’s committed reaction to the actions
a_{-i}
of the others.
Put differently, outcome
a
is a fixed point of the profile
R
in the sense that, for each player
i
,
a_{i}=R_{i}(a_{-i})
.
Let
\mathcal{E}(R)=\{a\in A:R(a)=a\}
be the set of fixed points of
R
(which may be empty).
Our approach builds on the following two assumptions:

1.

Player
i
evaluates profile
R
by
i
’s preferred fixed point at
R
.

2.

Any outcome is preferred to the case in which there is no fixed point.

More specifically, we extend the definition of payoffs to reaction-function profiles as follows.
For each player
i
and profile
R
, let

U_{i}(R)=\begin{cases}\max_{a\in\mathcal{E}(R)}u_{i}(a)&\text{if $\mathcal{E}(R)\neq\emptyset$}\\
-\infty&\text{if $\mathcal{E}(R)=\emptyset$.}\end{cases}

The first assumption captures player optimism, familiar for instance from games with externalities
(
Aumann and Peleg 1960
;
Shenoy 1979
, e.g.)
.
The second assumption has to do with that fixed points need not exist:
one can carefully construct profiles in which players “miscoordinate” to eliminate fixed points (e.g., best-reply reactions in the game of
Matching pennies
, see Table
3
later).

For some classes of reaction functions, fixed points always exist.
For instance, if each reaction
R_{i}
is monotone, then
R
is a monotone function on a complete lattice and the set of fixed points
\mathcal{E}(R)
is non-empty
(
Tarski 1955
)
.
Remark
1
takes this one step further and explores fixed-point uniqueness.

Remark 1

(Sufficient condition for unique fixed point)
.

We can associate to each profile
R
a directed graph
(N,E)
on nodes
N
in which there is an edge
ij\in E
whenever
i
’s reaction
R_{i}
depends on (i.e., is not constant in)
j
’s action
a_{j}
.
If the graph is acyclic, then there is a unique fixed point.
This applies for instance if there is some choice sequentiality or player hierarchy that makes a player’s reaction depend only on the actions of those who follow (or precede) the player.
Constant reactions produce empty (so acyclic) graphs;
also the construction leading up to Theorem
3.1
will be acyclic.
For cyclical graphs, there can be any number of fixed points (possibly none);
see for instance the proof of Theorem
3.3
.

\circ

To summarize, a reaction-function game
(N,\mathcal{R},(U_{i})_{i})
comprises players
N
, each player
i
with reaction functions
\mathcal{R}_{i}
and payoff function
U_{i}\colon\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
induced by payoffs
u_{i}
on
A
and determined by
i
’s preferred fixed point when such a point exists.

2.4
Reaction-function equilibrium

As players evaluate profiles by their subjectively preferred fixed point, different players may evaluate the same profile by different outcomes.
To alleviate any concerns to this point, we will focus on
unambiguous

.

Profile
R\in\mathcal{R}
is

a\in\mathcal{E}(R)
such that, for each outcome
a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}(R)

.

For example, the profile in which each player matches the action of the other player is unambiguous in the

(Table

The restriction to unambiguous profiles aligns with the Nash equilibrium requirement that players’ beliefs about the consequences of their actions are correct:
all players associate to an equilibrium profile

Definition 2
.

A

is an unambiguous profile
R\in\mathcal{R}
such that, for each player
i
and reaction function
R^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}

U_{i}(R)\geq U_{i}(R^{\prime}_{i},R_{-i})
.

If players are restricted to constant reaction functions, then fixed points are unique (Remark
1

We record this in Observation
1
.
Applied to the

x
, or both always play
y
.

3

.

A profile of constant reaction functions is a reaction-function equilibrium if and only if its unique fixed point is a Nash equilibrium in the strategic game.

To conclude that a profile

if a player can beneficially deviate through some reaction function, then there is also a constant reaction that the player can deviate through.
If player

3
What outcomes can be supported?

In this section, we first establish existence of reaction-function equilibria (Theorem
3.1
).
We then turn to two-player games, which make out a cornerstone of the game-theoretic literature.
We find a simple characterization of all two-player outcomes that can be supported in reaction-function equilibrium (Theorem
3.2
).
The proof is constructive and illustrates how reaction functions can emulate strategies familiar from infinitely repeated games to support outcomes not attainable in one-shot strategic games.
With yet more players, equilibrium multiplicity becomes an issue as essentially all outcomes can be supported (Theorem
3.3
).

3.1
Equilibrium existence

Our first result records that reaction-function equilibria always exist.
This is in contrast to Nash equilibrium in the corresponding strategic game (recall that players cannot mix).
By Observation
1
, this implies that we will have to go beyond constant reaction functions to prove reaction-function equilibrium existence.
We formalize the argument below, which is instructive and of independent interest as it links to yet another class of games, namely sequential-move games.
These neatly bridge simultaneous-move and reaction-function games.

Suppose players take action in sequence
1,\dots,n
, each observing all prior choices before making theirs (in the conventional sense, not using reaction functions).
This defines a finite extensive-form game of perfect information.
Such games do have Nash equilibria
(
Kuhn 1953
, use backward induction)
.
This equilibrium is a strategy profile
s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})
where each
s_{i}\colon A_{<i}\to A_{i}
maps an action
s_{i}(a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1})\in A_{i}
to all prior moves
(a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1})\in A_{<i}
.
This translates into the reaction function
R_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
with
R_{i}(a_{-i})=s_{i}(a_{<i})
for all
a_{-i}\in A_{-i}
.
As sketched in Remark
1
, there is a unique fixed point at
R
, namely the outcome realized along the equilibrium path of the extensive-form game, and the same holds at
(R^{\prime}_{i},R_{-i})
for all constant reactions
R^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
.
If
i
were able to deviate in the reaction-function game by always taking action
a^{\prime}_{i}
, then
i
would also deviate in the extensive-form game through strategy
s^{\prime}_{i}
with
s^{\prime}_{i}(a_{<i})=a^{\prime}_{i}
for each
a_{<i}\in A_{<i}
.
Therefore, outcomes supported in Nash equilibrium in the extensive-form game---which exist---are also supported in reaction-function equilibrium.

4

is supported in reaction-function equilibrium, can we order the players such that

is realized on the equilibrium path of the corresponding extensive-form game?
This is not true.
The “bad equilibria” of the
Battle of the sexes

3.2
Maxmin payoff bound for two-player games

Our next result mimics the “folk theorems” obtained in
Tennenholtz 2004
and
Kalai et al. 2010
but restricted to two-player games.
Theorem
3.2
asserts that an outcome can be supported precisely when it awards every player at least their
maxmin payoff

(
Maschler et al. 2013
, or “security level”,)
.
Player
i
’s
maxmin payoff

\underaccent{\bar}{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the highest payoff
i
can ensure irrespective of the actions of the others:

\underaccent{\bar}{v}_{i}=\max_{a_{i}\in A_{i}}\min_{a_{-i}\in A_{-i}}u_{i}(a_{i},a_{-i}).

We will let
\underaccent{\bar}{a}_{i}\in A_{i}
denote a corresponding “safe” action, which generically will be unique.

Theorem
3.2
parallels folk theorems of infinitely repeated games but is now obtained in a one-shot setting.
The “trigger strategy” of repeated games here maps into a “promise and threat” reaction function.
To support outcome
a^{*}\in A
(with at least maxmin payoffs), play
R_{i}(a^{*}_{-i})=a^{*}_{i}
and, for
a_{-i}\neq a^{*}_{-i}
, select
a_{i}=R_{i}(a_{-i})
to minimize the other player’s payoff
u_{-i}(a)
.
Compared to the existence proof of Theorem
3.1
, which involved at least one constant reaction, we now go yet further and all players choose more complex reactions.
The proof is in the Appendix.

Theorem 3.2
.

For two-player games, outcome
a\in A
is supported in reaction-function equilibrium if and only if
u_{i}(a)\geq\underaccent{\bar}{v}_{i}
for each player
i
.

To illustrate Theorem
3.2
, consider the

(Table

In the strategic game, the Pareto-efficient cooperative outcome
(C,C)
cannot be sustained as “defecting” (

) is dominant for each player.
However, the cooperative outcome can be supported in the reaction-function game, and in precisely one way:
each player cooperates if and only if the other player does (highlighted in Table

Such reactions lead to two Pareto-ordered fixed points, the inferior Nash equilibrium
(D,D)
and the superior outcome
(C,C)
.
Hence, compared to the unique fixed point in the construction offered in Theorem

, we may now obtain reaction-function equilibria with multiple (but still Pareto-ordered) fixed points.

3.3
More players, more possibilities for support

Once there are more players, we can carefully construct reactions to support an outcome by out-ruling any other outcome from ever becoming a fixed point even following unilateral deviations.
That is, for each outcome
a
, we can find a profile
R\in\mathcal{R}
such that, for each deviation
R^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
, we have
\mathcal{E}(R^{\prime}_{i},R_{-i})\subseteq\mathcal{E}(R)=\{a\}
and thus
U_{i}(R^{\prime}_{i},R_{-i})\leq U_{i}(R)
.
This illustrates how an outcome can be supported simply because there is no other alternative.
The proof is a combinatorial exercise found in the Appendix.
For the case with three players and two actions, we can find examples in which some outcomes cannot be supported in reaction-function equilibrium (see again the Appendix).

Theorem 3.3
.

All outcomes can be supported in reaction-function equilibrium if

•

There are at least four players, or

•

There are at least three players with at least three actions each.

The construction employed in the proof of Theorem
3.3
is highly artificial and provides little in terms of practical insight—for instance, player reactions are set completely independent from their payoffs.
In what follows, we refine the solution concept to simultaneously make equilibria more practically compelling as well as eliminate support for “bad” outcomes.

4
“Playing it safe” with reaction functions

When constructing reaction functions, some reactions appear more unsafe than others.
For instance, Theorem
3.2
builds on “promise and threat” reactions, where an outcome with at least maxmin payoffs is supported on the premise of maximal punishment for any deviation.
Even though these “threats” are not carried out in equilibrium, it seems risky for player
i
to base their reaction solely on player
j
’s payoffs.
Next, we address this by introducing the idea of
safe play
.

“Safe play” in strategic games entails taking a safe action
\underaccent{\bar}{a}_{i}
to ensure at least maxmin payoff
\underaccent{\bar}{v}_{i}
regardless the actions of the others
(
Maschler et al. 2013
, e.g.)
.
This individual risk-minimizing behavior can lead to collectively bad outcomes, for instance, in
Prisoner’s dilemma
(Table
4
).
With reaction functions, there are more options.
We say that reaction
R_{i}
is
safe
if, for all actions
a_{-i}
of the others, the outcome
(R_{i}(a_{-i}),a_{-i})\in A
awards
i
at least payoff
\underaccent{\bar}{v}_{i}
.
5
5

5

A stronger requirement would be
U_{i}(R_{i},R_{-i})\geq\underaccent{\bar}{v}_{i}
for each
R_{-i}\in\mathcal{R}_{-i}
, but this is too demanding.
With more than two players, there is
R_{-i}\in\mathcal{R}_{-i}
such that
\mathcal{E}(R)=\emptyset
and thus
U_{i}(R)=-\infty
for all
R_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
.

A reaction-function equilibrium
R
is
safe
if each player’s reaction
R_{i}
is safe.

Definition 3
.

Reaction function
R_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
is
safe
if
u_{i}(R_{i}(a_{-i}),a_{-i})\geq\underaccent{\bar}{v}_{i}
for each
a_{-i}\in A_{-i}
.

Safe reaction-function play is always possible, for instance by playing best replies
\textit{BR}_{i}
.
Yet simpler is the constant reaction function to always play a safe action
\underaccent{\bar}{a}_{i}
.
If the resulting action profile
\underaccent{\bar}{a}
of such play is a Nash equilibrium in the strategic game, then the corresponding constant reactions form a reaction-function equilibrium (Observation
1
).
In cases like a “race to the bottom”, where players say won’t exert effort unless others do, the low-effort outcome may be a Nash equilibrium and thus supported in safe reaction-function equilibrium.
But importantly, if Pareto improvements are possible, they too can be supported in safe reaction-function equilibrium.
Hence, whenever a safe reaction-function equilibrium exists, a Pareto-efficient one does as well.

Theorem 4.1
.

If a safe outcome
\underaccent{\bar}{a}
of the strategic game is a Nash equilibrium, then there is a safe reaction-function equilibrium.
In general, if a safe reaction-function equilibrium exists, then there exists a Pareto-efficient safe reaction-function equilibrium.

To illustrate Theorem
4.1
and how reaction functions make a difference compared to strategic games, consider the

(tracing back to Rousseau’s

In the strategic game, both taking safe action

)
.
In contrast, matching the action of the other as highlighted is safe and supports the superior payoff-dominant outcome
(x,x)
.

\begin{array}[]{r | cc}&x&y\\
\hline\cr x&\underline{3},\underline{3}&0,2\\
y&2,0&\underline{2},\underline{2}\end{array}

.

Thus, safe reaction-function equilibrium ensures a high-enough lower bound on player payoffs without having to compromise in achieving yet better outcomes.
However, existence is not guaranteed.
Example

5
Symmetric investment games

We now apply our theoretical framework on some games for which implementing reaction functions through blockchain-based smart contracts seems very promising.
These are symmetric
n
-player games with actions
A_{i}=\{0,\dots,H\}
interpreted as investment amounts.
Reaction functions now go from being a theoretical concept to a most practical tool:
conditional investment strategies provide an excellent use case for smart contracts
(
Weber and Staples 2022
, e.g.)
.
Payoffs for our
symmetric investment games
include a non-decreasing common value
v\colon A\to\mathbb{R}
derived symmetrically from costly individual actions.
6
6

6

That is, for each outcome
a\in A
and permutation
\pi\colon N\to N
, we have
v(a)=v(a_{\pi(1)},\dots,a_{\pi(n)})
.

Specifically, for each player
i
, and outcome
a\in A
,

u_{i}(a)=v(a)-a_{i}.

As players are symmetric,
coordinated
outcomes (
a_{i}=a_{j}
for all players
i
and
j
) will be key.
For convenience, we assume that these coordinated outcomes are Pareto ranked and that the higher players coordinate the better:

u_{i}(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)
is increasing in
\alpha\in\{0,\dots,H\}
.
7
7

7

This can be relaxed and we instead obtain a variation on Theorem
5.2
.

Additional structure will be imposed on
v
in Subsections
5.1
and
5.2
, resulting in weakest link and public good games, respectively.

In what follows, we focus on monotone reaction functions (“invest more the more others invest”). Monotone reaction functions make fixed-point existence and computation straightforward.
Let
\mathcal{M}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{i}
denote player
i
’s monotone reaction functions.

Moreover, as action spaces
A_{i}
are identical and contribution to the total value
v
is symmetric across players, it is natural to further constrain to
symmetric
reaction functions.
In essence, the identities of the other players should not matter.
A reaction function
R_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
is
symmetric
if, for each
a_{-i}\in A_{-i}
and permutation
\pi\colon N\to N
with
\pi(i)=i
, we have
R_{i}(a_{-i})=R_{i}(\pi(a)_{-i})
with
\pi(a)\equiv(a_{\pi(1)},\dots,a_{\pi(n)})\in A
.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{i}
denote player
i
’s symmetric and monotone reaction functions.
8
8

8

For instance, in oligopoly models, a firm’s profit typically is unchanged if its competitors’ actions (prices, quantities) are permuted.
In this case, a symmetric reaction function specifies the same reaction to two such permuted action profiles.

With symmetric reaction functions, it becomes meaningful to compare functions across players.
We say that
i
and
j
play
the same
reaction function, denoted
R_{i}\cong R_{j}
, whenever
R_{i}(x)=R_{j}(x)
for each
x\in\{1,\dots,H\}^{n-1}
.
Let
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{R}
be profiles (“social norms”) in which
all
play the same, that is,
R_{i}\cong R_{j}
for all players
i
and
j
.
A reaction function
R^{*}_{i}
is
norm-proof
if, for every norm
R\in\mathcal{N}
, playing
R^{*}_{i}
against the norm
R
yields at least as high payoff as conforming to it.
This is not an equilibrium property, in the sense that we do not require
R^{*}_{i}
to be optimal against a specific
R_{-i}
, but rather an alternative interpretation of safe play.
Even if you do not know which norm
R
the others might settle on, playing
R^{*}_{i}
is a safe and reasonable choice at least as good as conforming to the norm.

Definition 4
.

Reaction function
R^{*}_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{i}
is
norm-proof
if, for each profile
R\in\mathcal{N}
,

U_{i}(R^{*}_{i},R_{-i})\geq U_{i}(R).

Technically, the property bears resemblance to the “evolutionary stable strategy” (ESS) refinement of Nash equilibrium prominent in evolutionary game theory
(
Maynard Smith and Price 1973
)
.
9
9

9

Further evidence to this point is given in the Appendix, where we simulate “evolution” of a pool of reaction functions in a public-good setting.
The simulations indicate that evolutionary selection indeed would push towards a pool of mainly norm-proof reactions.

Norm-proof reaction functions have a certain structure in symmetric investment games.
We say that a reaction function
R_{i}
exhibits
conditional collaboration
if it satisfies the following two criteria:
if the others coordinate, then
i
does as well;
if not, then
i
matches at least the minimum investment but undercuts the maximum investment.
That is,
R_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
exhibits
conditional collaboration
if, for each
\alpha\in\{1,\dots,H\}
,
R_{i}(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)=\alpha
and, for all remaining
a_{-i}\in A_{-i}
,

\textstyle\min_{j\neq i}a_{j}\leq R_{i}(a_{-i})<\max_{j\neq i}a_{j}.

The principle of conditional collaboration is familiar from behavioral economics, where experimental evidence has shown that participants are often willing to contribute more the more others contribute
(
Fischbacher et al. 2001
, e.g.)
.
This has typically been rationalized by preferences reflecting altruism, “warm glow”, or reciprocity
(
Sugden 1984
;
Croson 2007
, e.g.)
.
We offer an alternative perspective where conditional collaboration arises even without such “moral preferences”.
For reaction functions, conditional collaboration can instead be rationalized through norm-proofness.

Theorem 5.1
.

For symmetric investment games, a monotone reaction function is norm-proof if and only if it exhibits conditional collaboration.

There is a unique reaction function exhibiting conditional collaboration if there are only two players (“match the other’s action”) or only two ordered actions (“match the minimum action of the others”), both which are safe.
However, with more players and actions, the class is large (and may contain unsafe reactions).
In common to all of them is that, for symmetric investment games, the desirable high-investment outcome is always supported in reaction-function equilibrium.

Theorem 5.2
.

For symmetric investment games, all profiles consisting only of reaction functions that exhibit conditional collaboration support the high-investment outcome
(H,\dots,H)
in reaction-function equilibrium.

Next, we present a novel consistency property to capture that
i
’s reaction foremost should be determined by
i
’s own payoffs.
The reaction should therefore be consistent across
payoff-equivalent
action profiles.
Formally, if
i
’s payoffs are the same at
a_{-i}
and
a^{\prime}_{-i}
regardless of the action that
i
takes, so
u_{i}(a_{i},a_{-i})=u_{i}(a_{i},a^{\prime}_{-i})
for each action
a_{i}
, then
i
’s reaction should also be the same,
R_{i}(a_{-i})=R_{i}(a^{\prime}_{-i})
.
This is a refinement of the symmetry property.

Definition 5
.

Reaction function
R_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
is
payoff consistent
if, for each
\{a_{-i},a^{\prime}_{-i}\}\subseteq A_{-i}
,

\left(u_{i}(a_{i},a_{-i})=u_{i}(a_{i},a^{\prime}_{-i})\text{ for each }a_{i}\in A_{i}\right)\implies R_{i}(a_{-i})=R_{i}(a^{\prime}_{-i}).

It is immediate that payoff-consistent reaction functions exist (e.g., constant reactions).
The best-reply reaction (with a fixed tie-breaking rule if needed) is also payoff consistent.
In many games, the property even has no bite whatsoever as there might not be any payoff-equivalent action profiles.
However, in the games that we explore next in Subsections
5.1
and
5.2
, payoff consistency combines with norm-proofness to give a unique recommendation.

5.1
Weakest-link games

In weakest-link (or “minimum-effort”) games, the minimum investment determines the outcome;
the higher this is, the better for all
(
Hirshleifer 1983
, e.g.)
.
These games are abundant in practice and arise, for instance, when divisions within a firm contribute independently to a joint project for which the lowest-quality input disproportionally affects the output
(
Knez and Camerer 1994
;
Kunreuther 2009
;
Dietrichson et al. 2022
)
. Specifically, payoffs are given by

u_{i}(a)=\lambda\cdot\textstyle\min_{j}a_{j}-a_{i}

with parameter
\lambda>1
.
Hence, players wish to match the minimum investment of the others and ideally do so at
H
.
Safe play amounts to taking action
a_{i}=0
with
\underaccent{\bar}{v}_{i}=u_{i}(0,\dots,0)=0
.
Here, action profiles
a_{-i}
and
a^{\prime}_{-i}
are payoff equivalent whenever
\min_{j\neq i}a_{j}=\min_{j\neq i}a^{\prime}_{j}
.

The best-reply reaction
\textit{BR}_{i}
is particularly simple for weakest-link games and entails matching the minimum investment,
\textit{BR}_{i}(a_{-i})=\min_{j\neq i}a_{j}
.
Not only is it safe, it is also the only norm-proof and payoff-consistent reaction function.
10
10

10

Combining proofs, one may also show that
\textit{BR}_{i}
is the only norm-proof reaction that is safe for all parameter values
\lambda>1
.

Theorem 5.3
.

For weakest-link games, reaction function
R_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
is norm-proof and payoff consistent if and only if
R_{i}=\textit{BR}_{i}
.

In weakest-link games, there are compelling reasons to use monotone reaction functions.
If we also can expect
others
to use such functions, then we obtain a different argument in favor of
\textit{BR}_{i}
.
For this purpose, we restrict to what we call
high-risk
weakest-link games with parameter
\lambda<H/(H-1)
.
That is, coordination gains are small relative to effort costs to the point that coordinating at
0
is superior to playing
H
against
H-1
.
This does not mean that there is no gain from coordinating at a higher investment level—the payoff is still higher at
(H,\dots,H)
than at
(0,\dots,0)
---rather, it means that higher investments comes with greater risk.
11
11

11

For comparison, when
Van Huyck et al. 1990
found that experiment participants failed to coordinate, the setting corresponded to
H=6
and
\lambda=2
.
This is a much easier environment to coordinate in than high-risk games, where we would have
\lambda<H/(H-1)=1.2
.

We define reaction function
R_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
to be
weakly dominant against monotone play
if, for each
R^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
and
R_{-i}\in\mathcal{M}_{-i}
,
U_{i}(R)\geq U_{i}(R^{\prime}_{i},R_{-i})
.
That is, the player cannot do better than
R_{i}
against monotone reactions
R_{-i}\in\mathcal{M}_{-i}
.
Note that the statement does not restrict
i
’s own reaction (
R_{i}
and
R^{\prime}_{i}
, respectively) to be monotone.
However, the characterization in Theorem
5.4
yields a unique reaction function, namely
\textit{BR}_{i}
, which is monotone.

Theorem 5.4
.

For high-risk weakest-link games, reaction function
R_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
is weakly dominant against monotone play if and only if
R_{i}=\textit{BR}_{i}
.

The high-risk qualification is restrictive, but this is to be expected.
We already know from Theorem
5.2
that many reaction functions “solve” weakest-link games in general.
The purpose of Theorem
5.4
is partly to identify a strong-enough condition under which we can make a unique, precise recommendation.

There are other variations on the same idea such as weak dominance against safe play, where one assumes instead the others to play safe.
In some sense, safe play is more restrictive than monotone play.
Therefore, there are now many weakly dominant reaction functions, for instance all that exhibit conditional collaboration (including
\textit{BR}_{i}
).
One can even define weak dominance in general (so not only against safe or monotone play), but we argue in Remark
2
that this is too demanding.

Remark 2

(Weak dominance in general)
.

Consider the two-player weakest-link game.
Suppose, for contradiction, that reaction function
\hat{R}_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
is weakly dominant.
Suppose first the other player chooses
R_{j}
, which is to always play
H
.
Then we must have
\hat{R}_{i}(H)=H
to ensure that the optimal outcome
(H,H)
is a fixed point.
Consider next the case in which the other player chooses reaction
R^{\prime}_{j}
such that
R^{\prime}_{j}(H-1)=H
and otherwise
R^{\prime}_{j}(a_{i})=0
, which is neither monotone nor safe (in a high-risk setting).
The best attainable fixed point now is
(a_{i},a_{j})=(H-1,H)
, but this would require
\hat{R}_{i}(H)=H-1
, which is a contradiction.
Hence, no reaction function is optimal against both
R_{j}
and
R^{\prime}_{j}
.

\circ

The fundamental message to take from the above is that reaction functions offer a new way to overcome trust issues in weakest-link games.
Reaction-function play does not rely on building trust between the players
(
Chen and Chen 2011
)
, on putting hope to communication routines
(
Blume and Ortmann 2007
;
Dietrichson et al. 2022
)
, or on establishing a central authority to monitor and punish inefficient action
(
Holmstrom 1982
)
.
Neither of this is needed—the solution would in principle allow any large group of strangers to coordinate on their very first attempt.

Once we dig deeper and identify which particular reaction function to play, it is no surprise that the best-reply reaction function stands out (i.e., to match the weakest link).
In a sense, Theorems
5.3
and
5.4
together with the surrounding discussion make for a robustness exercise that verifies that, indeed, the intuitive best-reply reaction is the best option.

5.2
Public-good games

We turn to a different class of games for which, in contrast, best replies are ineffective.
We have already seen that reaction functions introduce new ways for players to coordinate around Pareto-improving outcomes in strictly dominated actions (e.g. in the
Prisoner’s dilemma
).
Now, we will further expand on this in the particular context of public-good games, in which each player’s investment benefits also all others, but the investment is a net loss to the player herself
(
Bergstrom et al. 1986
, e.g.)
.
In public-good games, payoffs are given by

u_{i}(a)=\lambda\cdot\textstyle\sum_{j}a_{j}-a_{i}

with parameter
1/n<\lambda<1
.
12
12

12

Outside this parameter range, high investment (for
\lambda\geq 1
) or zero investment (for
\lambda\leq 1/n
) is both safe, dominant, and efficient.

High investment
(H,\dots,H)
maximizes total payoffs
\sum_{j}u_{j}(a)
but, in the strategic game, free-riding through
a_{i}=0
is dominant.
Hence, the best reply is simply to free-ride and never invest.
Again, safe play yields
\underaccent{\bar}{v}_{i}=u_{i}(0,\dots,0)=0
.
Now, action profiles
a_{-i}
and
a^{\prime}_{-i}
are payoff equivalent if they add up to the same:

\sum_{j\neq i}a_{j}=\sum_{j\neq i}a^{\prime}_{j}
.

Recall that there is only one norm-proof reaction function for the restricted cases of two players or two actions, namely to either “match the other’s action” or to “match the minimum action of the others”.
Although there are many ways to extend on these two principles when there is an arbitrary number of players and actions, there is only one that is payoff consistent in public-good games.
This is to “match the average, rounded down”, which we denote
R^{*}_{i}
.
For instance, with three players and actions
A_{i}=\{0,1,2\}
, we have
R^{*}_{i}(0,2)=R^{*}_{i}(1,2)=1
.
13
13

13

For
z\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\lfloor z\rfloor\in\mathbb{Z}
be the largest integer no larger than
z
.

Theorem 5.5
.

For public-good games, reaction function
R_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
is norm-proof and payoff consistent if and only if, for each
a_{-i}\in A_{-i}
,

R_{i}(a_{-i})=R^{*}_{i}(a_{-i})\equiv\left\lfloor\frac{1}{n-1}\textstyle\sum_{j\neq i}a_{j}\right\rfloor.

One might overlook the subtleties associated to rounding down and propose a different reaction function, such as “match the average, rounded up”.
By our definition, this does not exhibit conditional collaboration.
A profile of such reaction functions is not an equilibrium—for instance, players may be able to deviate even by free-riding (specifically, if
n>H+1
).
In this way, rounding down is important to sustain an equilibrium.

In the experimental literature, some attention has been given social-welfare preferences
(
Charness and Rabin 2002
)
, where a player’s preference depends also on the payoffs of others.
It is possible to interpret the reaction
R^{*}_{i}
in a similar way.
Specifically, we define a reaction function
R_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
to be
welfare-maximizing conditional on safe play
if, for each
a_{-i}\in A_{-i}
,

6
Concluding remarks

We have argued that blockchain-based smart contracts enable credible formulation of reaction functions.
Such an approach is particularly relevant in case of investment games in which players can deposit funds and condition their investment on the investment levels of the others.
The outcome that is implemented is then governed by a self-executable coordinating contract.
As such, reaction functions can help non-cooperative players act out centralized coordination in order to resolve issues of trust (as in weakest-link games) and free-riding (as in public-good games).

Compared to the approaches in
Tennenholtz 2004
and
Kalai et al. 2010
, we submit that the reaction functions bring automated play a step closer to practical application.
For example, to obtain collaboration as a program-equilibrium outcome in the
Prisoner’s dilemma
,
Tennenholtz 2004
suggests a program of the form “if my program is identical to my opponent’s program, then the program collaborates; otherwise, it defects”.
Conditioning your action on the program of the opponent is fragile, as the smallest difference in programs yields defection.
Even though players may be willing to collaborate, programs can be formulated in countless ways and there is a risk that a collaborative outcome would never prevail in practice.
For instance, imagine that the other player is naïve and chooses a program that always collaborates—as the programs differ, this will not lead to collaboration.
In contrast, our reaction functions specify an action for any finite action profile of the others.
This may be more robust as illustrated in the cases of weakest-link and public-good games.
For example, if players exhibit conditional collaboration in a weakest-link game, we will not automatically resort to the worst-case outcome if some players deviate from the best-reply reaction function.

We end on some points for future research.
One is to explore reaction functions in games with incomplete information.
For instance, the idea of “playing safe” seems appealing even if, say, you do not know the preferences of the other players.
A first step in that direction is Theorem
5.4
, which shows that the best-reply reaction is weakly dominant against monotone play in high-risk weakest-link games.
As weak dominance is a statement only on the player’s own payoffs, knowledge of the others’ payoffs is not important.
One can ask more generally, what is required for
\textit{BR}_{i}
to be weakly dominant against monotone play?
Theorem
5.4
then points to some conditions:
(a) payoff
u_{i}(a)
is non-decreasing in
a_{-i}\in A_{-i}
, (b)
\textit{BR}_{i}
is monotone (to ensure fixed points against monotone play), and (c), for each outcome
a\in A
,

a_{i}>\textit{BR}_{i}(a_{-i})\implies u_{i}(a)\leq u_{i}(0,\dots,0).

Under these assumptions,
\textit{BR}_{i}
is weakly dominant against monotone play.

There are many games, different from high-risk weakest-link games, for which this applies.
The common theme is that (a)
i
is not harmed by
j
’s actions, that (b) there are some complementarities between player contributions, and (c) that there is a “target” that the player wants to match but not exceed.
This target could for instance be derived from Kantian fairness reasoning
(
Roemer 2010
;
Alger and Weibull 2013
, e.g.)
combined with a strong disutility from being “exploited” (investing more than what is “right” given the investments of the others to the others’ benefit).
Given the prominent position of the best-reply reaction function, which is simple and intuitive, it would be interesting to map out exactly when it is also optimal.

a^{\prime}_{-i}=(m,\dots,m,m+1,\dots,m+1)
and
\sum_{j\neq i}a^{\prime}_{j}=\sum_{j\neq i}a_{j}
.
By payoff consistency,
R_{i}(a_{-i})=R_{i}(a^{\prime}_{-i})
.
If
a^{\prime}_{-i}=(m,\dots,m)
, then conditional collaboration gives
R_{i}(a^{\prime}_{-i})=m
and thus
R_{i}(a_{-i})=m
, as desired.
If not, so
a^{\prime}_{-i}\neq(m,\dots,m)
, then
a^{\prime}_{-i}
contains at least one
m
and at least one
m+1
.
By monotonicity,
R_{i}(m,\dots,m)\leq R_{i}(a^{\prime}_{-i})\leq R_{i}(m+1,\dots,m+1)
.
By conditional collaboration,
R_{i}(m,\dots,m)=m
,
R_{i}(m+1,\dots,m+1)=m+1
, and
R_{i}(a^{\prime}_{-i})<\max_{j\neq i}a^{\prime}_{j}=m+1
.
Hence,
R_{i}(a_{-i})=m
, as desired.
∎

Theorem 5.6
.

For high-risk public-good games,
R^{*}_{i}
is welfare-maximizing conditional on safe play.

Proof.

As
\lambda>1/n
, welfare is increasing in player
i
’s investment
a_{i}
:

\textstyle\sum_{j}u_{j}(a)=n\lambda\sum_{j}a_{j}-\sum_{j}a_{j}.

Hence, we only need to identify the highest safe investment.
We will show that this is given by
R^{*}_{i}(a_{-i})
.
By definition, the reaction
a_{i}=R_{i}(a_{-i})
to actions
a_{-i}
is safe whenever
u_{i}(a)=\lambda\sum_{j}a_{j}-a_{i}\geq\underaccent{\bar}{v}_{i}=0
.
Equivalently,

\textstyle a_{i}\leq\lambda\sum_{j}a_{j}\iff(1-\lambda)a_{i}\leq\lambda\sum_{j\neq i}a_{j}\iff a_{i}\leq\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\textstyle\sum_{j\neq i}a_{j}.

As
\lambda>1/n
,
\lambda/(1-\lambda)>1/(n-1)
.
Hence,
R^{*}_{i}
is safe:

R^{*}_{i}(a_{-i})=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{n-1}\textstyle\sum_{j\neq i}a_{j}\right\rfloor\leq\displaystyle\frac{1}{n-1}\textstyle\sum_{j\neq i}a_{j}<\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\textstyle\sum_{j\neq i}a_{j}.

Let now
a_{i}>R^{*}_{i}(a_{-i})
, that is,
a_{i}
exceeds the average.
Then
\sum_{j\neq i}a_{j}\leq(n-1)a_{i}-1
.
For high-risk public-good games,
\lambda<H/(nH-1)
.
As the fraction
x/(nx-1)
is decreasing in
x
, we have
H/(nH-1)\leq a_{i}/(na_{i}-1)
.
Furthermore,

\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}<\frac{a_{i}}{(n-1)a_{i}-1}.

Hence,

\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\textstyle\sum_{j\neq i}a_{j}<\displaystyle\frac{a_{i}}{(n-1)a_{i}-1}\cdot\left((n-1)a_{i}-1\right)=a_{i}.

As desired, this shows that
a_{i}>R^{*}_{i}(a_{-i})
is not safe.
Hence,
R^{*}_{i}(a_{-i})
is the highest safe investment.
∎

Appendix: Further remarks on Theorem
3.3

Suppose there are three players with two actions each,
A_{i}=\{0,1\}
.
Suppose that, for each player
i
and outcome
a\neq(1,1,1)
,
u_{i}(a)>u_{i}(1,1,1)
.
By contradiction, suppose that we can support
(1,1,1)
in reaction-function equilibrium.
As
(1,1,1)
is uniquely worst, there cannot be any other fixed point.
Moreover, if there is an outcome
a
for which
R(a)
differs from
a
in only one coordinate, say
R_{i}(a_{-i})\neq a_{i}
, then player
i
beneficially deviates through the constant reaction
R^{\prime}_{i}
associated with action
a_{i}
as
a\in\mathcal{E}(R^{\prime}_{i},R_{-i})
.
Hence, for each
a\neq(1,1,1)
, the reaction
R(a)
must differ from
a
in at least two coordinates.
We use this observation in each step below:

1.

For the reaction
R(0,0,0)
to differ in at least two coordinates, there are at least two players
i
, without loss players
1
and
2
, with
R_{i}(0,0)=1
.

2.

As
R_{1}(0,0)=1
, we must have
R(1,0,0)=(1,1,1)
.
That is,
R_{3}(1,0)=1
.

3.

As
R_{3}(1,0)=1
, we must have
R(1,0,1)=(0,1,1)
.
That is,
R_{1}(0,1)=0
.

In Step 1, we concluded that
R_{1}(0,0)=1
, but also that
R_{2}(0,0)=1
.
Hence, we can take Steps 2 and 3 from player
2
’s perspective as well.
By symmetry, going through outcomes
(0,1,0)
and
(0,1,1)
, we get to
(0,0,1)
and conclude that
R_{2}(0,1)=0
.
But then
R(0,0,1)=(0,0,\cdot)
, which differs in at most one coordinate.
This is a contradiction.

Appendix: Simulation study

We complement the theoretical findings with simulations.
The setup matches
Fischbacher et al. 2001
, namely a four-player public-goods game with actions
A_{i}=\{0,\dots,20\}
and payoffs

\textstyle u_{i}(a)=20-a_{i}+0.4\sum_{j}a_{j}.

We restrict to monotone, payoff-consistent reaction functions.
These can conveniently be represented by non-decreasing functions
R_{i}\colon\{0,\dots,60\}\to\{0,\dots,20\}
, where
i
commits to contribute
R_{i}(\alpha)
conditional on the total contribution of the three other players being
\alpha
.
We maintain a list of
500
such functions, which evolves as worst-performing reactions get replaced by “mutated” variants of the best-performing ones.
Initially, the list consists solely of pure free-riders, but through experimentation, new and more effective functions emerge and gradually displace the original ones.
Once this process converges, the theoretical prediction is that most reaction functions will be alike the norm-proof
R^{*}_{i}
(Theorem
5.5
).

The main take-away from the simulation study is that it gives further support to this prediction.

The flow of the simulation is as follows:

1.

There are
100
independent runs, each starting with a list of pure free-riding.

2.

A run is composed of
15\ 000
batches of games.
Mutation occurs after each batch based on average payoffs within the batch.

3.

A batch is composed of
10\ 000
games.
In each game, four reaction functions are drawn at random from the list (with replacement) and payoffs of the highest fixed point are realized.
As reactions are monotone, this is easy to compute.

In total, the simulations cover
100\times 15\ 000\times 10\ 000=15
billion games.

To capture convergence, mutation frequency and impact decreases exponentially.
That is, the number of replaced worst-performing reaction functions is smaller in later batches.
Moreover, the replacements become more similar to the top-performing reaction functions.
(If
R_{i}
is the top-performing reaction to copy, then the mutated
R^{\prime}_{i}
is such that, for each amount
\alpha\in\{0,\dots,60\}
, we draw
\varepsilon
from
\{-1,0,1\}
and set
R^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha)=R_{i}(\alpha)+\varepsilon
.
By making
\varepsilon=0
more likely,
R^{\prime}_{i}
becomes more similar to
R_{i}
.
If needed, we modify
R^{\prime}_{i}
to make it monotone and within bounds,
0\leq R^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha)\leq 20
.)

Although the functions are defined for all
\alpha\in\{0,\dots,60\}
, mainly higher values of
\alpha
tend to be reached during play in later batches (i.e., at the highest fixed point).
As a result, the replacement step is disproportionately focused on these higher values and the function’s behavior for smaller values, say
\alpha<45
, is likely under-optimized and a bit more “noisy”.
