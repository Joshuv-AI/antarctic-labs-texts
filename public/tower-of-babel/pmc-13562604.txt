From Task‐Technology Fit to Self‐Perceived Performance Impact Among Nurses: Exploring the Statistical Mediation of Self‐Reported Utilization of the Clinical Nursing Information System: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Han Su, Yujiao Zou, Yan Hu, Zheng Yuan, Zhenghong Yu, Chun Yang, Qi Zhang, Wei Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo examine task‐technology fit within a clinical nursing information system and investigate the associations among task characteristics, technology characteristics, task‐technology fit, self‐reported system utilization, and nurses' self‐perceived performance impact.DesignA cross‐sectional study.MethodThis study surveyed 1370 nurses. Introduction

In the global wave of healthcare digitization, technological innovation has become an important driver of service model transformation and improvements in clinical efficiency and patient safety (Mitchell and Kan 2019). The clinical nursing information system (CNIS) has the potential to support the transformation of nursing practice, enhance clinical decision‐making, and improve nursing management. This potential is partly supported by its ability to integrate multiple digital tools, including electronic health records (EHRs), mobile nursing terminals, and real‐time patient monitoring technologies (Paatela et al. 2024). Although CNIS is regarded as having potential for application, its large‐scale development and successful implementation face many practical challenges worldwide, particularly in integrating technology with clinical nursing.

Relevant studies suggest that the functional design of CNIS is often misaligned with the actual needs of clinical nursing, a discrepancy that becomes especially pronounced during nursing practice (Dudding et al. 2018). For instance, the patient assessment undertaken by nurses is a dynamic process. It is expected that real‐time data such as vital signs or symptom changes are collected (collected‐access) with only minimal delays being accepted in interpreting patterns, then generating an appropriate intervention (diagnosed‐matched‐access), compared with available CNIS implementations (Bae et al. 2020; Bakken et al. 2008). Interdisciplinary collaboration with various professionals is an integral aspect of nursing. Nonetheless, the currently available systems are usually inadequate for enabling this promising cooperation. Such deficiencies include limited system compatibility, non‐intuitive user interface design, and suboptimal user experience (Wu et al. 2019). These constraints may increase nurses' cognitive burden and time demands, disrupt task continuity, and contribute to inefficiency, operational errors, and potentially adverse patient outcomes (Nascimento et al. 2021).

Therefore, improving the fit between digital solutions and nursing practices has become an increasingly important issue as healthcare information systems continue to evolve. Task‐technology fit (TTF) theory provides a strong theoretical basis for evaluating this alignment; it emphasizes that technology is more likely to improve performance when it is well matched to the tasks it is intended to support (Cane and McCarthy 2009). Although TTF has been widely used in many areas of information technology (Ammenwerth et al. 2006; Cane and McCarthy 2009; Fuller and Dennis 2009), it is not commonly employed in the nursing field. This study aims to address this gap by examining factors associated with TTF in CNIS and the relationships of TTF with self‐reported system utilization and self‐perceived performance impact among Chinese nurses. This report follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist (von Elm et al. 2008).

The specific aims of this study were as follows:

What is the current level of TTF within the CNIS? Do TTF levels differ among distinct nursing groups?

How are task characteristics and technology characteristics individually and jointly associated with nurses' perceptions of TTF within the CNIS?

To what extent does self‐reported CNIS utilization statistically account for the association between TTF and nurses' self‐perceived performance impact?

Background

CNIS has partially fulfilled its promise to enhance care by integrating a variety of digital tools, including electronic health records, mobile care terminals, and real‐time patient monitoring. However, its implementation has also revealed substantial gaps between system design and clinical practice needs. Dudding et al. (2018) found that changes in information system workflows, interruptions, and electronic health record documentation patterns increased nursing workload, contrary to the original goals of information system implementation. These findings suggest that although preventing nursing errors and improving efficiency are major goals of CNIS implementation, unintended consequences may still emerge during clinical use (Lee and Kang 2021). In 2016, the results of an international survey reported low levels of satisfaction and multilevel concerns among nurse informaticists about EHRs, with more than half of the comments highlighting problems related to user tasks (e.g., systems not meeting nursing‐specific clinical needs) and system‐related issues (Topaz et al. 2016).

According to the TTF theory, initially proposed by Goodhue and Thompson (1995), information technology is more likely to improve performance when it fits the task requirements of users. TTF emphasizes the alignment between task objectives and technological capabilities, rather than focusing on a single aspect, such as technology or user beliefs. This concept has therefore been extensively used to evaluate the effectiveness of information systems across many sectors (Chen et al. 2015; Zigurs and Khazanchi 2008), including healthcare (Hsiao and Chen 2012; Lepanto et al. 2011). Within the domain of CNIS, TTF provides an applied framework for assessing whether system design adequately supports essential nursing activities, including patient monitoring, order implementation, and communication with colleagues. According to the theory, a stronger TTF is associated with greater system use and improved performance outcomes (Zhang et al. 2021). In the present study, we focus on nurses' self‐perceived impact on performance as the key outcome variable. System utilization was also measured through nurses' self‐reports.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This cross‐sectional study was conducted among nurses at a 2000‐bed tertiary general hospital in an eastern Chinese city. Since 2020, the hospital has used its CNIS, a system developed based on the Clinical Care Classification (CCC), a standardized nursing terminology. This system supports nursing documentation, clinical decision‐making, and care management by integrating clinical decision support, standardized nursing language, an evidence‐based nursing knowledge base, and big data analysis technology throughout core nursing processes, including assessment, planning, implementation, and management.

The system can communicate with various mobile electronic monitoring devices and collect real‐time physiological data, including vital signs, intake and output, and blood glucose levels. Based on association rules and big data modelling, the system provides dynamic risk assessment and generates alerts for nurses. Its modular design covers admission management, comprehensive assessment, nursing planning, implementation documentation, and discharge follow‐up, supporting routine processes such as shift handover, patient transfer, and nursing plan revision. In addition, the system incorporates structured nursing documentation and standardized operational guidance to support consistent nursing practice and workflow management.

Participants and Sampling

A convenience sample of nursing staff (N = 1370) from a tertiary general hospital in an eastern Chinese city participated in this study by completing an electronic questionnaire in July 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) registered nurses with more than 1 year of experience using the CNIS; and (2) willingness to be enrolled. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) nurses who worked on rotation or were in training; and (2) nurses with less than 1 year of experience using the CNIS.

Survey Instruments

General Information Questionnaire

This general information survey tool collected demographic and work‐related data, including gender, age, education level, years of work experience, department affiliation, professional title, and whether nurses had received systematic CNIS training.

Job Diagnostic Survey (JDS)

The JDS scale is a validated instrument for assessing task characteristics and comprises five dimensions: autonomy, skill variety, task significance, task identity, and feedback (Oldham and Hackman 1981). This study employed the modified version developed by Idaszak and Drasgow (1987), using a 7‐point Likert scale ranging from “very inaccurate” to “very accurate.” Item 9 was removed from the original 15‐item scale following formal data analysis because its wording in the context of nursing practice exhibited measurement bias and was unreliable. Empirical analysis revealed that this item exhibited a negative corrected item‐total correlation (CITC = −0.031) within the Skill Variety subscale, indicating that it did not cohere with the other items measuring the construct. Moreover, its inclusion suppressed the subscale's Cronbach's alpha to an unacceptably low level (α = 0.248). Upon removal of Item 9, the Cronbach's alpha of the Skill Variety subscale improved substantially to 0.727. This modification did not change the underlying dimensional structure of the scale, and the 14‐item version demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity in the present study. The Cronbach's alpha was 0.959 for the scale used in this study.

Task‐Technology Fit Scale

TTF was measured using the established scale developed by Goodhue (1998). This 24‐item instrument assesses the match between information technology functionality and user task requirements across 12 key dimensions. A 7‐point Likert scale was used, from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree.’ The overall Cronbach's alpha in this study was 0.929.

Nursing Information System Usage and Performance Questionnaire

This 37‐item tool evaluated technology characteristics, self‐reported system utilization, and nurses' self‐perceived performance impact across seven dimensions: system functionality, perceived ease of use, system usage, nursing quality, satisfaction, work efficiency, and professional competence (Chun 2020). The scale was validated in 372 clinical nurses from two first‐class tertiary hospitals in China. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) yielded seven factors, accounting for 73.374% of the total variance (KMO = 0.943; Bartlett's test, p < 0.001), with all factor loadings exceeding 0.7. The scale demonstrated good convergent validity (AVE ranging from 0.606 to 0.753, all > 0.5; CR ranging from 0.851 to 0.937, all > 0.7) and discriminant validity (the square root of AVE for each construct was greater than its correlations with other constructs). The scale exhibited good internal consistency at the dimension level, with Cronbach's alpha values as follows: system functionality (0.936), perceived ease of use (0.924), system usage (0.895), satisfaction (0.935), nursing quality (0.939), work efficiency (0.851), and professional competence (0.872). A 5‐point Likert scale was employed, and the overall Cronbach's alpha in this study was 0.976.

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarise demographic characteristics and study variables. Independent‐samples t‐tests and one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were conducted to examine differences in TTF‐related constructs across demographic and professional characteristics. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to explore associations among variables. Before conducting regression analyses, the assumptions of linear regression were assessed, including linearity, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals, independence of residuals, and multicollinearity. Residual scatterplots and normal P–P plots were examined to evaluate linearity, homoscedasticity, and residual normality. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) and Durbin–Watson statistics (DW) were used to assess multicollinearity and residual independence, respectively. Potential influential observations were assessed using Cook's distance.

Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the relationships among theoretical constructs in the proposed TTF model. The primary regression models included only theoretically specified variables, as this study focused on evaluating the hypothesized relationships among task characteristics, technology characteristics, TTF, self‐reported system utilization, and nurses' self‐perceived performance impact. Demographic and professional characteristics were treated as background variables. Given the significant differences identified in subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses were further conducted using hierarchical regression, adjusting for relevant demographic and professional covariates selected based on significant subgroup differences and their potential relevance to each dependent variable to assess the robustness of the regression findings.

Statistical mediation analysis was conducted using the PROCESS macro version 4.1 for SPSS (Hayes 2022) to examine whether self‐reported system utilization statistically accounted for part of the association between TTF and nurses' self‐perceived performance impact.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Approval No. B2025‐168, dated March 31, 2025). Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before they accessed the survey; participants confirmed their consent by checking a box after reading the study information. All responses were anonymized, and data were stored in a password‐protected database accessible only to the research team. Study data will be securely destroyed two years after the study's completion.

Results

General Characteristics of Participants

A total of 1746 questionnaires were distributed, yielding 1370 valid responses, for a valid response rate of 78.5%. 376 questionnaires were excluded due to incomplete or inaccurate demographic information, unreasonable or contradictory responses, a concentrated selection of extreme values on scale items, and excessively short completion times (< 3 min).

Most participants were female (95.6%), and 96.4% had received CNIS training. The age distribution showed that the majority were aged ≤ 30 years (42.7%). The majority had a bachelor's degree (80.3%). The most common professional titles were senior nurse (47.6%) and nurse‐in‐charge (35.5%). At the departmental level, the distribution was higher for surgery (38.7%) and internal medicine (31.1%). Experience duration was similarly distributed, with the highest percentage (32.8%) in the ≥ 16 year‐work‐experience group. Characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1.

The demographic characteristics of our study sample are generally consistent with the overall profile of nurses in large general hospitals in mainland China. Specifically, the proportion of female participants (95.6%) aligns with the national average (approximately 97%). The age structure is predominantly young and middle‐aged (80% under 40 years old), and over 80% hold a bachelor's degree or higher, reflecting current trends in higher education and younger age among nursing staff in tertiary hospitals (He et al. 2025). However, compared with similar multicentre studies, our sample exhibits two distinctive features: first, an exceptionally high coverage of systematic training (a reference value of 60.4%); second, a relatively high proportion of nurses with longer work experience (≥ 16 years, 32.8%, a reference value of 24.7%).

Common Method Bias

Harman's single‐factor test was conducted to assess potential common method bias. The analysis extracted 10 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, with the first factor accounting for 48.3% of the total variance. Although this value falls below the commonly adopted 50% threshold (Tomita 2024), it is close to that benchmark, indicating that some risk of common method bias cannot be entirely ruled out. To reduce this concern, several procedural measures were implemented during data collection: responses were collected anonymously; participants were reminded that there were no right or wrong answers; items from different scales were presented in separate sections; and invalid questionnaires were removed based on predefined screening criteria. It is important to note that Harman's single‐factor test provides only a preliminary assessment. Future studies could further validate these findings by incorporating objective data (e.g., system background logs).

Group Comparisons on TTF Constructs

Group comparisons revealed differences across the five core constructs. Given that a large sample size can make small differences statistically significant, it is important to use the effect sizes (Cohen's d or η 2) to help distinguish which differences warrant attention. Nurses who received systematic training reported higher scores across all core constructs than those who did not receive training (all p < 0.001; d = 0.629–0.862), indicating medium‐to‐large effects. Furthermore, male nurses reported significantly lower scores on most constructs, including task characteristics, technology characteristics, TTF, and self‐reported system utilization, whereas no significant difference was observed in nurses' self‐perceived performance impact. Regression Analysis

Diagnostic assessments indicated that the assumptions of multiple linear regression were adequately satisfied. Residual scatterplots indicated no evident departures from linearity or homoscedasticity, while normal P–P plots suggested that residuals approximately followed a normal distribution (File S2). The DW statistics were approximately 2, and all VIF values were below the recommended cutoff, indicating satisfactory residual independence and the absence of substantial multicollinearity. Potential influential observations were evaluated using Cook's distance across the three regression models, with maximum values of 0.029, 0.045, and 0.109, respectively. All values were well below 1, suggesting that no single observation exerted a substantial influence on the regression estimates.

Multiple linear regression analysis (Table 6) indicated that both task characteristics (β = 0.427, p < 0.001) and technology characteristics (β = 0.390, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with TTF, together accounting for 56.1% of its variance (R 2 = 0.561). TTF was significantly associated with self‐reported system utilization (β = 0.612, p < 0.001), accounting for 37.5% of its variance (R 2 = 0.375). Furthermore, both TTF (β = 0.269, p < 0.001) and self‐reported system utilization (β = 0.571, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with nurses' self‐perceived performance impact, with a combined variance accounted for of 58.6% (R 2 = 0.586).

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine whether the regression findings remained robust after adjusting for demographic and professional characteristics identified in subgroup analyses (File S3). After adjustment for covariates selected for their significant subgroup differences and relevance to each dependent variable, all primary associations remained statistically significant, with consistent effect directions, supporting the robustness of the regression findings. Specifically, task characteristics (β = 0.420, p < 0.001) and technology characteristics (β = 0.392, p < 0.001) remained significantly associated with TTF; TTF (β = 0.588, p < 0.001) remained significantly associated with self‐reported system utilization; and both TTF (β = 0.421, p < 0.001) and self‐reported system utilization (β = 0.527, p < 0.001) remained significantly associated with nurses' self‐perceived performance impact. These findings indicate that demographic or professional characteristics did not substantially influence the observed associations.

Statistical Mediation Analysis

Before analysis, all variables were standardized. Statistical mediation analysis using bootstrapping (5000 samples) indicated that self‐reported system utilization partially accounted for the statistical association between TTF and nurses' self‐perceived performance impact. The estimated total association between TTF and nurses' self‐perceived performance impact was significant (β = 0.618, p < 0.001). The estimated indirect association through self‐reported system utilization was 0.350 (BootSE = 0.023, 95% BootCI [0.306, 0.395]), accounting for 56.6% of the total statistical association. The direct association between TTF and nurses' self‐perceived performance impact remained significant after statistically controlling for self‐reported system utilization (β = 0.269, p < 0.001). Discussion

This cross‐sectional study, involving 1370 nurses, aimed to examine the current status of TTF within CNIS and its variations across different nursing groups. It further sought to explore the associations between task and technology characteristics and TTF, and to examine whether self‐reported system utilization statistically accounted for part of the association between TTF and nurses' self‐perceived performance impact.

The results indicate that the overall TTF item mean score was 5.241 (SD = 0.868), above the scale's theoretical midpoint, suggesting a relatively favourable perception of system–task alignment among nurses. Among the TTF dimensions, the highest scores were observed for level of detail, presentation format, and accuracy, indicating that nurses perceived relatively strong performance in these areas. This finding is consistent with previous qualitative studies indicating that nurses emphasize the need for integrated information, timely accessibility, and clear information presentation. (Berkhout et al. 2025; Zhai et al. 2022). The high scores on these specific dimensions reflect how the current system partially addresses these needs, particularly regarding the clarity and comprehensiveness of information presentation.

However, nurses reported relatively lower scores for compatibility, reliability, and confusion‐related dimensions. These findings may reflect challenges related to inter‐system compatibility and data sharing, which have been reported as common barriers in healthcare information systems. Such barriers to data exchange may contribute to reduced perceptions of system reliability. A 2025 qualitative study further links these reliability issues directly to such design flaws, noting that conflicts in form logic are key factors in distorting electronic documentation (Wang et al. 2025). Moreover, interoperability challenges extend beyond the technical level to the semantic level. Research indicates that even after adopting standardized terminology, semantic ambiguity may persist in clinical practice due to contextual and interpretive differences (Palojoki et al. 2024), potentially exacerbating confusion and reliability issues.

It is noteworthy that nurses gave the lowest rating to the system's perceived ability to improve work efficiency, which stands in clear contrast to their reported overall satisfaction. Human factors engineering research indicates that systems with low ecological validity may ‘increase additional work and cognitive load’ (Karsh et al. 2011). Further insights from a socio‐technical perspective on barriers to clinical information system implementation reveal that even if a system is recognised for the accuracy and comprehensiveness of its information presentation, deficiencies in infrastructure, human‐computer interaction, or workflow integration can lead users to avoid using it or relegate it to a ‘double‐entry’ tool, failing to genuinely enhance frontline nursing efficiency (Or et al. 2014). This suggests that high user satisfaction may not necessarily correspond to perceived improvements in efficiency; it may instead reflect a compromise between recognition of certain system functions and acceptance of additional procedural burdens. Therefore, future system optimisation should focus not only on the quality of clinical content but also on improving system performance and deepening the integration of technology into nursing workflows.

In this study, nurses who received training outperformed their untrained counterparts across all dimensions, with effect sizes ranging from moderate to large (d = 0.629–0.862). This finding aligns with previous research indicating that training enhances perceived ease of use and usefulness, thereby strengthening intention to use and reducing negative emotions (Lin 2017; Peng et al. 2016). However, a study by Protogiros et al. (2025) across 14 European countries revealed that despite the widespread adoption of digital solutions, 39.6% of healthcare institutions still do not provide formal digital skills training to medical staff. Insufficient nurse informatics competency has been emphasized as a significant influencing factor (Du et al. 2025), further underscoring the necessity of implementing systematic informatics management training. Such training may extend beyond software operation to include clinical scenario‐based simulations, which could support nurses' ability to apply information systems in complex clinical situations. Moving beyond basic operational skills, the focus should be on cultivating comprehensive information literacy.

Furthermore, this study found that male nurses reported lower scores on task characteristics, technology characteristics, TTF, and self‐reported system utilization, with significant differences observed across these constructs (d = 0.633–0.865). In contrast, no significant difference was observed in nurses' self‐perceived performance impact. However, the male nurse sample in this study was highly concentrated in the ICU (73.3% of male samples), a trend consistent with previous reports indicating that male nurses are often concentrated in departments requiring greater physical strength and emergency response capabilities, such as the ICU and emergency department (Ghimire and Qiu 2025; Shen et al. 2022). Concurrently, ICU nurses scored significantly lower in all dimensions compared to nurses in other departments (η 2 = 0.020–0.036, small to medium effects). Therefore, the observed gender differences likely primarily reflect the influence of departmental distribution rather than an independent effect of gender. From a clinical perspective, this suggests that in specialized departments such as the ICU, factors like the complex work environment, high task intensity, and inadequate alignment of existing information systems may contribute to more challenging experiences among nurses. Previous research supports this view: studies from Australia and Finland in ICU settings (Gregory et al. 2022; Jansson et al. 2022) have noted that general clinical information systems often fail to meet the high‐intensity, dynamic needs of the ICU, with issues such as unreasonable interfaces and cumbersome operations negatively impacting nurses' experience. Similarly, Vernic et al. (2025) emphasized that ICU informatization must be tailored to its specific workflow. Future research should further explore the relationship between departmental context and gender factors based on more balanced samples.

In this study, both correlation and regression analyses yielded results consistent with the theoretical framework's initial hypotheses. The task characteristics and technology characteristics of the CNIS together explained 56.1% of the variance in TTF. Unlike previous studies that often focus on the external collaborative aspects of nursing tasks (Hsiao and Chen 2012), this study emphasizes the intrinsic attributes of tasks, and the results similarly indicate that TTF arises from the interaction between task requirements and technological provision. Among nurses' perceptions of the intrinsic attributes of nursing tasks, scores for task significance, skill variety, and feedback were relatively high, while scores for task identity and autonomy were comparatively lower. One possible explanation for the lower scores on task identity and autonomy is that, while clinical nursing is executed under structured protocols, it is still subject to institutional constraints and workflow interruptions that may fragment nurses' sense of task ownership and decision‐making authority. Although preliminary, these findings offer a conceptual foundation that could inform the design of CNIS based on standardized nursing terminology. Standardized nursing terminology clearly links nursing diagnoses, interventions, and patient outcomes (L. Yang et al. 2021), which conceptually aligns with nurses' high recognition of task value. It encompasses various types of actions, including cognitive, operational, educational, and coordinative (Xie et al. 2025), potentially addressing the need for skill variety. Meanwhile, the built‐in outcome evaluation and qualifiers could provide a structured foundation for forming closed‐loop processes, which may be associated with higher perceived feedback (Saba and Whittenburg 2018). Conversely, it should be acknowledged that standardized nursing terminology itself carries strong normative and procedural constraints that can help meet stringent nursing quality management requirements but, if implemented too rigidly, could inadvertently further reduce perceived autonomy.

Regarding nurses' perceptions of system technology implementation, the score for system functionality was slightly higher than that for perceived ease of use. This discrepancy is consistent with the findings of Cho et al. (2016) in their comparison of six electronic nursing record systems: systems with more integrated functionality often come with more complex navigation patterns, increased interface switching, and additional operational layers, which can reduce nurses' evaluation of ease of use. Therefore, when designing CNIS based on standardized nursing terminology, a balance must be struck between functional completeness and operational simplicity. For example, configurable quick‐access panels and task‐oriented navigation structures could be implemented to mitigate ease‐of‐use issues (Lindsay and Lytle 2022). Beyond simplifying existing functions, any new advanced features should be integrated in a way that does not compromise this balance. For instance, the system could embed context‐aware decision support modules to assist nurses in coping with uncertainties, while providing limited autonomous options (such as personalised nursing plan templates) within a compliant framework (Bayor et al. 2025), potentially refining the precision of TTF without adding unnecessary operational complexity.

The statistical mediation analysis results indicate that self‐reported system utilization statistically accounts for a substantial portion of the association between TTF and nurses' self‐perceived performance impact (statistical indirect association = 0.350, accounting for 56.6% of the observed statistical total association). Consistent with the cross‐sectional nature of our data, this should be interpreted as a pattern of statistical associations rather than evidence of a causal pathway. Nonetheless, this observed pattern aligns with a substantial body of research in the information systems field. For instance Thanthrige et al. (2025), found that TTF robustly predicts usage intention, which is a key antecedent of actual usage behaviour. Similarly, the core viewpoint of the Information Systems Success Model is that system use is a significant predictor of application benefits (Ebnehoseini et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2022). Casper et al. (2005), in their design of a technology‐enhanced practice system for home‐based heart failure care, systematically aligned institutional clinical guidelines with a ‘current practice model’ derived from nurses' actual work through comparison and functional mapping. This methodology not only improved the alignment between clinical guidelines and nursing practices but also provided a practical approach for designing technologies that better fit nurses' tasks. Such an approach is consistent with the present study's finding that higher perceived TTF was associated with better nurses' self‐perceived performance impact.

In summary, this study's findings underscore the importance of a holistic approach to CNIS optimisation that extends beyond technical functionality. For the future design and promotion of nursing information systems, in addition to continuously optimising the fit between the system and nursing tasks, greater emphasis should be placed on three interconnected aspects: (1) training programmes aligned with clinical workflows to address the significantly lower scores observed among untrained nurses; (2) interaction designs consistent with nursing cognitive patterns to resolve the discrepancy between high functionality ratings and lower ease‐of‐use scores; and (3) sustained usage support to prevent the system from being relegated to a mere “double‐entry” tool. Crucially, efforts in all these areas should aim to promote not only the initial fit but also the full and correct utilization of the system, given that self‐reported system utilization was identified as a key statistical mediator in the association between TTF and nurses' self‐perceived performance impact in this study. This comprehensive approach may help maximize the value of information technology in supporting the quality and efficiency of nursing care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The use of a cross‐sectional design does not allow for the establishment of causal relationships among variables. Therefore, the mediation findings should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than evidence of causal pathways. Longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to further examine temporal relationships among these variables. Although the sample size was substantial, data were collected from a single tertiary hospital using convenience sampling within a single time period, which may limit generalisability to other nursing populations and healthcare settings. All core variables were measured using nurse self‐report questionnaires, which may introduce social desirability bias or common method bias. In addition, the number of male nurses in this study was relatively small, and most male nurses worked in ICU departments. Therefore, the observed gender differences may be confounded by departmental distribution, making it difficult to distinguish the independent effects of gender and department. One item was removed from the validated JDS scale. Although this modification was empirically justified, it may affect the comparability of skill variety scores with studies using the original instrument. The composite TTF score may also mask meaningful variations across its 12 dimensions. Future studies should include multicentre samples, more balanced demographic and departmental distributions, objective system log data, and multisource performance evaluations to further validate these findings.

Conclusion

Based on TTF theory, this cross‐sectional study of 1370 clinical nurses found that nurses perceived a relatively favourable level of task–technology fit within the CNIS. The findings indicate that both task and technology characteristics are positively associated with TTF. At the same time, self‐reported system utilisation statistically accounted for part of the association between TTF and nurses' self‐perceived performance impact. These findings are consistent with previous research on the use of information technology in nursing and highlight systematic training as a potentially modifiable factor. Hospital administrators and system developers should collaborate to improve system interoperability and operational stability, thereby reducing barriers between technology and clinical workflows. Simultaneously, establishing continuous training programmes integrated into clinical contexts may strengthen nurses' information literacy and support effective use of information technology in nursing practice.
