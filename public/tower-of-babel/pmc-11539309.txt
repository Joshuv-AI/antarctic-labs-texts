Self protective health care in childhood cancer survivors from the perspective of survivors, parents, and professionals: a qualitative study
Authors: Masumeh Akbarbegloo, Mahnaz Sanaeefar, Mehrdad Karimi, Marzieh Hoseini
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionChildhood cancer survivors experience many health problems that they must protect themselves from their destructive physical and psychological effects. Understanding experiences of children about self-protective care will help health care providers manage long-term care plans. Therefore, this study was conducted with the aim of exploring self-protection care in childhood cancer survivors.MethodsThis is a phenomenological study that was conducted in the clinics and cancer care centers of Urmia and Khoy in Iran. 27 participants were selected by purposive sampling until data saturation. The data was collected using a semi-structured and in-depth face to face interview. Content analysis approach used for data analysis and MAXQUDA 10 software used for data management.ResultsData analysis led to the emergence of “self-protection health care” as a main category, two categories “maintaining physical health” and “keeping a strong mindset” and 9 sub-categories including “abstain activities that may cause harm”, “refusing contact with others for possible infection”, “monitoring symptoms and side-effects of cancer coming back”, “avoiding unhealthy diet”, “knowing about cancer from similar others”, “keep away negative thoughts and emotions”, “drawing on spiritual resources”, “creating a bridge to life again” and “being healthy for the sake of one’s family”.ConclusionAccording to the results of this study, self-protection is one of the most important cares for childhood cancer survivors. Over time, the influence of the clinical team and parents on children’s health care diminishes. Therefore, they should promote their health and well-being behaviors and use a variety of protective approaches to self-care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11730-5.

Background

Childhood cancers are one of the most challenging diseases for children and their families [1]. Of course, cancer treatment is very possible nowadays and many children get better during the treatment period [2]. About 17 million cancer survivors live in the United States. Many of them have survived from childhood cancer [3]. Over the past 30 years, treatments and supportive care for cancer survivors have improved. As a result, the average 5-year survival rate for childhood cancers when considered as a whole is 86% [4]. The number of cancer survivors in the United States in 2012 was about 13.7 million people, and this number incresed about 18 million people in 2022 [5]. In Iran with a population of 83 million, 19,973 children were diagnosed with cancer from 2009 to 2019 [6]. A research published in Iran also shows that the 5-year survival rate of Iranian children for leukemia is 65%, Hodgkin’s disease is 74%, and Wilms’ tumor is 86% [7]. But these children are at risk of long-term side effects related to their previous treatments. In a study conducted on the side effects of chemotherapy in adolescents, 59% of them stated that the side effects of anti-cancer treatment are worse than getting cancer [8].

The results of studies confirm that Side effects of chemotherapy and radiation therapy occur immediately or a few weeks or years after the treatment; Also, the process of cancer treatment affects the child’s growth or causes secondary cancer in the future [9]. Because of the possibility of future side effects, childhood cancer survive (CCS) need regular follow-ups throughout their lives. Some studies have apeared that CCS to be more vulnerable to physical and psychosocial difficulties. The increase in suicidal thoughts related to the depression, weaker physical health, pain related to cancer, etc. has caused problems in their normal life [10, 11]. Overall, childhood, adolescent, and young adult cancer survivors were 57% more likely to develop depression, 29% more likely to develop anxiety, and 56% more likely to develop psychotic disorders in the years following treatment compared to their siblings or healthy members [12]. This shows the importance of follow-up and post-treatment care. Therefore, CCS and their families must get information about protecting themselves from possible physical and psychological effects caused by cancer, as well as coping and preventing methods [8]. Since the past two decades, the types of medical care of cancer survivors have shifted from the hospital to the home. Currently, more than 90% of cancer survivors’ care and treatment are done on an outpatient basis at home; One of the things that facilitate the transfer of care from the hospital to home is the protective and preventive measures that are taken by the CCS or relatives [13]. Those who have been dealing with a chronic illness for a long time become proficient in managing their daily affairs in relation to the illness. Therefore, self-protection is the product of an developmental and learning process [14]. One of the most important human characteristics is the ability to self-regulate, pay attention, will and emotions. Therefore, self-protection not only includes the adjustment of behavioral skills in protecting oneself in different environmental and personal conditions, but also a personal sense to apply these skills in the required and relevant conditions [15].

It is essential that CCS maintain physiological balance by adopting protective behaviors and managing symptoms when they occur in order to control and promote their health and safety. Failure to meet self-preservation needs exacerbates negative effects on the quality of life of childhood cancer survivors and may negate any benefit from this increased survival [16].

Given the rapidly increasing CCS population, there is an urgent need to improve our understanding of care and protection challenges faced by young survivors. To do this, we need improved knowledge that looks from the perspective of CCS. Some studies have appeared that CCS to be more vulnerable to physical and psychosocial difficulties. However, in none of the studies conducted on CCS, protective self-care methods from the point of view of children, parents-professionals that prevented these vulnerability have not been mentioned.

Current studies of CCS often focus on family caregivers. Therefore, these studies often present results as analyzes of the perceptions of the entire family, which may obscure the individual experiences of child cancer survivors and professionals [17]. As such, these studies cannot paint a complete picture of the lives of childhood cancer survivors or gain insight into how the childhood cancer experience affects children’s vulnerability. The post-treatment phase is often difficult and requires the child and family to process their experiences and create a new “normalization” [18]. Few qualitative studies specifically focus on the experiences of professionals, parents, and children following treatment [17, 18].

Therefore, this study has identified multiple and diverse protective care strategies used by CCS. Findings may help develop interventions to encourage and facilitate self-protective care designed specifically for this population. Despite the importance of self-protection, research in this field seems limited. Therefore, considering the importance of recognizing self-protection care in CCS, lack of documentation and also existence of cultural differences in this field, the present study was conducted with the aim of exploring self protective health care in childhood cancer survivors from the perspective of survivors, parents, and professionals.

Methods

Study design

This study is a qualitative design that was conducted using the phenomenological method. This approachis a research method attempts to identify core consistencies and meanings through the systematic classification process of coding and identifying themes or categories [19]. Qualitative research is valuable in this area because it provides the ability to explore the insights and understanding of survivors, parents and professionals [20]. An in-depth qualitative examination of post-treatment experiences will enable a more complete understanding of the complex practical and emotional challenges that childhood survivors face during this period. It is anticipated that this will enable a better understanding of the gaps within the system, thus enabling the development of recommendations to better provide psychosocial support to survivors [18].

Sampling procedure and participants

Purposive sampling is a technique widely used in qualitative research to identify and select information-rich cases for more effective use of limited resources. This method includes identifying and selecting people or groups of people who have experience and knowledge about the phenomenon under study. In addition, they are available and willing to participate and have the ability to convey experiences and opinions in an expressive and reflective manner [18]. Therefore, in this research, 27 participants were included in the study using purposefull sampling until data saturation was reached. There are no general rules or direct guidelines for determining the number of participants in qualitative studies, but several factors such as the nature of the topic, data quality, study design and data saturation influence sampling decisions [19]. The participants selected from children’s hospitals in Urmia and Khoy city and also “MAHAK” charity institution in Iran. The inclusion criteria include children who have 12–18 years old, whose treatment was completed at least one year and they have no history of physical and psychological diseases except cancer. Exclusion criteria include recurrence of the disease, lack of information about the disease, and severe progression of the disease or death. In the current study, parents, health professionals and teachers who were in close contact with these children and see their challenges and care behaviors, were also included in the study.

Measure

To collect data, semi-structured face to face interview, which is the most common method in qualitative research, was used to collect comprehensive and complete information. Sampling was done with maximum diversity among childhood cancer survivors, their families, nurse and doctor, counselors of Mahek charity and school teacher. Since CCS have a follow-up treatment plan, and based on that, they refer to doctors’ clinics or hospital, therefore, the interviews were conducted with prior appointment by the children and their families in one of the empty rooms of hospitals in Urmia and Khoy city. Some of the participants were under the help of “MAHAK” Cancer Charity Association, so a number of interviews were conducted with them at the charity. Also, consultant of cancer charity association and school teacher were interviewed at their workplace. Two interviews were conducted with parents of children at their workplace. Interviews with nurse and doctor were also done by appointment in the rest room of nurse and doctor. The interviews continued until data saturation, so that no new category was obtained [19]. In this study, data saturation was achieved up to 25 interviews according to the research group, and in order to ensure that there are no new data and results, 3 more interviews were conducted.

Before starting the interview, the purpose of the study, how to participate in the interview and record the interviews were explained to the participants, and after obtaining informed consent, the interview started. In conducting the interview, the interview guide was used to ensure the expression of all topics. At the beginning of each interview, the demographic information of the participants was asked and then the interview started with a general question. In this way, the children were asked, “What precautions did you take to protect yourself from cancer after treatment?“. Further, according to the interview guide, more in-depth questions were used. For example, “What problems did you experience in protecting yourself after the treatment?“, “What were your needs in self-protection after the treatment of the disease?“. Also, based on the answers and data analysis, explorative interview questions such as “Tell me more about this.“, “What does this mean?” was asked. The questions that were used to interview with professionals and parents were: “What precautions did CCS take to protect themself from cancer after complete the treatment?“. “What problems did CCS experience in protecting themself after complete the treatment?“, “What were CCS needs in self-protection after complete the treatment?“. Probing questions were: “Can you explain it more?”, “What were your feelings at that moment?”, “What did you think about the situation?”, “What is the meaning of caring for children with cancer?”.

The duration of each interview was 40 to 55 min. All interviews were recorded using a Sony ICD-UX560 audio recorder. Data collection lasted for almost 8 months and the entire research was conducted between October 2022 and October 2023.

Data analysis

Simultaneously with the data collection, data analysis was also done with a phenomenological approach using the steps suggested by Dikelman’s 7-step analysis approach [18]. In this way, first the recorded interviews were transcribed and the text of each interview was read several times, and in the second step, an interpretive summary is written to understand the implied concepts. This step required a lot of patience, as the researcher was expected to remain open, accept more global perspectives, and constantly move from the whole to the part and back to the whole. Based on this, meaning units were extracted in a step-by-step manner following immersion in the participants’ transcribed narratives. In the third step, the sub-categories and the general categories were extracted from the meaning units and suitable expressions were made for each one. In the fourth step, all members of the research team discussed the transcribed interviews. All disagreements on interpretations were discussed and a group consensus was reached. In the fifth step, common meanings were identified by comparing and contrasting the texts. After comparing the subject and sub-categories in the sixth step, the relationship between them was determined. In the seventh step, through further discussion and analysis of all data, themes that intersected in all narratives were further explored, disagreements were discussed, consensus was reached, and they were incorporated into the final draft [19]. According to the data analysis method after comparing the interviews and subcategories that gained from children and parent-professionals, the relationship between them was determined and make “categories”. Then common meaning was identified by comparing and contrasting the texts (main category). MAXQDA v10 R150410 software was used for data management (Table 1).

Trustworthiness of the data

In order to achieve the accuracy and trustworthiness of the data, the four criteria suggested by Lincoln and Guba, 1985 were considered [21]. For the credibility of the findings, sufficient time was allocated for data collection, long-term engagement of researchers with the data, and member checking was done. For the dependability of the findings, a review of the colleagues and members of the research group was done. In order to confirmability of the findings, the researchers tried not to interfere with their assumptions as much as possible in data collection and analysis. Finally, in terms of the transferability of the findings, an attempt was made to provide a rich description of data collection stages, coding and data analysis.

Maintaining

physical health

1. Maintaining physical health

One of the categories obtained in the current study was maintaining physical health. After the treatment, participants tried to take care of their physical health, so they performed behaviors that increase health maintenance. This category includes five sub categories, which are explained below.

Abstain activities that may cause harm

One of the common side effects in cancer treatment is fatigue, which is caused by the patient’s feeling of physical weakness. The participants stated that after cancer treatment, they started their normal life activities, but because they got tired quickly, they tried to avoid activities that caused physical harm to them.

Refusing contact with others for possible infection

Participants observed more personal hygiene and tried to avoid contact with people who might transmit the infection to them. Because they believed that their immune system is not sufficiently strengthened yet, they should strengthen it by taking supplements and vitamins, otherwise they may be easily exposed to various diseases and infections.

Monitoring symptoms and side-effects of cancer coming back

Regarding the importance of disease recurrence control, all participants stated that they had medical follow-up after stopping treatment to prevent side effects or disease recurrence. These checkups include physical examinations, blood tests, and imaging methods that the doctor prescribes to check the person’s condition. It was important to have regular checkups after cancer treatment to monitor for signs of recurrence and manage any side effects and symptoms.

Avoiding unhealthy diet

There was a need to prevent nutritional risk factors in different ways in patients. They tried to use less food that is processed and because of the disease they were more inclined to use organic food. Maintaining a healthy diet after cancer treatment was vital for paticipants to speed up the healing process, strengthen the immune system, and reduce the risk of cancer recurrence.

Knowing about cancer from similar other

Participants sought information about cancer from people who have experienced cancer treatment. Talking to others who were in similar situations helped to reduce their loneliness and receive useful ideas from others. In addition, they also received information from their doctor and nurse.

2. Keeping a strong mindset

Another category obtained from the present study was “keeping a strong mindset”. Childhood cancer survivors stated that they used different methods to find peace of mind and wanted to return to their normal and daily lives, even trying to avoid the psychological impact of illness on their parents and family. This category consisted of four sub categories which are explained below.

Keep away negative thoughts and emotions

One of the important needs of cancer survivores was the need to emotionally drain the negative states of the disease. From the perspective of the participants increasing increasing the motivation to live was an effective way to control and improve their disease. Negative internal dialogues were destructive factors in the fight against cancer because the thoughts, feelings and behaviors of cancer survivors affected each other and negative internal dialogues caused negative thoughts followed by negative emotions and behaviors.

Drawing on spiritual resources

The participants considered the development of intense fear and worry to be very influential in the development of the disease and one of the ways to overcome this fear was to trust in God Almighty, so that by being close to God, they can also be friends with their illness and consider it as wisdom from God. Therefore, taking advantage of spiritual forces and faith in God was a way to gain inner peace.

Creating a bridge to life again

The participants stated that after they finished the treatment process and defeated the cancer, they were eager to get their health back and tried to return to their pre-cancer life using different ways and methods.​ Being able to master everyday activities seemed to be an essential priority for the survivors following treatment completion.

Being healthy for sake of one’s family

The participants stated that they did not want cancer to have a negative psychological impact on family members, so they tried to act like a healthy person in the family and talk less about their illness or they avoided the family going to places where cancer patients were being treated.

Discusion

This study was conducted with the aim of exploring the protection needs of chilhood cancer survivores. Based on the findings, “self-protection health care”, was the main category of the present study, which included two categories: maintaining physical health and keeping a strong mindset. Self-protection includes the adjustment of behavioral skills in protecting oneself in different environmental and personal conditions, as well as a personal sense to apply these skills in the required and relevant conditions [22]. Those who have been dealing with a chronic illness for a long time become proficient in managing their daily affairs in relation to the illness. Therefore, protective self-care is the product of an evolutionary and learning process. Protection as a behavior to prevent a potentially harmful outcome such as a disease, which requires an understanding of cognitive, psychological and social factors in order to apply protective behavior to reduce the consequences of the disease [23]. In other words, it can be said that cancer survivores try to prevent the return of disease through taking care and protecting themselves.

“abstain activities that may cause harm” was one of the findings of the present study in the category of “maintaining physical health”. Physical exercises, as they are beneficial for other people, have the same benefits for cancer survivors and cause more muscle strength, fat and weight loss [24]. Excess weight in people with cancer after complete cancer treatment is associated with a shorter life span and a higher chance of cancer [25]. However, it should be noted that cancer causes the body to suffer exhaustion and fatigue, therefore heavy activities that put too much pressure on the person should be avoided and physical activities should be started slowly to prevent physical injuries [26]. In the current study, leukemia survivors felt more tired than other survivors during activities, and osteosarcoma survivors or other cases of cancer that involved their lower limbs refused to do some sports activities.

“Refusing contact with others for possible infection” and “avoiding unhealthy diet” were other findings of the present study. Common infections of children who have survived cancer are infections of the sinuses and respiratory system, digestive system and urinary system [27]. The results of the study by Buro & Stern (2023) in the field of infection in childhood cancer survivors showed that the risk of infection is high and this increases their mortality during the post-treatment period [27]. Therefore, in order for the treatment process to go well and to achieve the expected results in the post-treatment period, people with cancer should avoid contact with patients with viral and bacterial infections [28]. On the other hand, high-risk nutritional behaviors and malnutrition can lead to the breakdown of nutritional reserves, increased risk of disease, reduced response to anticancer treatments, and shorter survival time in cancer patients [29]. The results of the studies show that the awareness of high-risk nutritional behaviors is low and from the point of view of the participants, nutrition education is their main need [30]. Therefore, it is important to determine the patient’s nutritional needs, apply the required nutritional support, appropriate treatment regimen patterns, and monitor nutritional progress throughout the treatment period and after the treatment of cancer. Following a healthy and good diet can help the body recover, strengthen the immune system and improve the energy level of a person [31].

Another finding of the present study is “monitoring symptoms and side-effects of cancer coming back”. One of the most worrisome in cancer survivors is the fear of disease recurrence. Despite the available evidence on cancer treatment, it seems that between 60 and 80% of patients express concern about the return of the disease after recovery [32]. The reason for this return is the resistance of a small number of cancer cells in the treatment process, which ultimately leads to the recurrence of cancer, for this reason, regular follow-up and tests should continue after recovery [33]. The study by Kelada et al. (2019) showed that the child and his family should be educated about the problems and complications of cancer recurrence, the times to visit the doctor. Actions and follow-ups after the treatment are very important in the complete recovery of the patient and his physical strengthening [34]. Therefore, being aware of the situation after the completion of the treatment helps the individual and the family in planning, changing the lifestyle and making important decisions.

“knowing about cancer from similar others” is one of the findings of the present study. In a qualitative study that was conducted in the field of managing and searching for information on cancer survivors and their families, the participants had performed various behaviors to obtain information. The two subscategories included information seeking and information avoidance. In the subcategory of information seeking, participants admitted that they often asked friends and support groups, and sometimes asked questions from doctors and health care providers [35]. Lack of information about the post-treatment period can lead to an increase in physical and mental complications and slow down the healing process. Therefore, disease information literacy enables a person to make an informed decision regarding the care and control of his general condition after treatment [36].

The second category obtained in the current study was “keeping a strong mindset”, that one of the subcategories was “keep away negative thoughts and emotions”. Negative thoughts and feelings such as anger, fear, resentment, despair, and anxiety are mental states that destroy a person’s spirit due to negative energy and create negative motivations in humans [37]. The results of the study by Douki et al. (2019) indicated that turning to positive actions in cancer survivors leads to mental health, health-promoting behaviors, improvement of the immune system, increase hope and better social support networks [38]. Negative emotions in cancer survivors can cause stress, anxiety and fear about the future [39]. In this regard, it is suggested that medical teams including psychiatrists and psychologists be established in the hospital and counseling clinics be established to provide services to childhood cancer survivors.

Another finding of the study was “drawing on spiritual resources”, and most of the patients mentioned religious adherence and trust in God. Prayers, reading books of revelation, or attending religious ceremonies are among the sources that some religious people can use to endure less injuries in the face of stressful life events [40]. The most important spiritual behaviors in children with cancer are talking about God, death, heaven and hell, watching nature and enjoying being with nature, showing love to others, the need for affection and love, hope for the future, worrying about vague things and lack of understanding, happiness and creativity [41]. On the other hand, empirical studies show that there are meaningful relationships between religion, spirituality, and health, and there is a positive and meaningful relationship between mental health, physical health, satisfaction with life, and being alive with spiritual beliefs [40, 42]. In this regard, it is necessary to identify the challenges, problems and spiritual crises of children suffering from cancer; On the other hand, parents should be empowered to take spiritual care of childhood cancer survivors.

Cancer not only affects the children, but also his family, and in a two-way interaction, the psychological symptoms of the child and family affect each other. The study of Ashrafi et al. (2019) also showed that a child with cancer has mental anxiety regarding the burden imposed on the family [43]. Therefore, one of the subcategories obtained in the present study was “being healthy for the sake of one’s family”. In a qualitative study by Rashidi et al., one of the subcategories obtained was caring for others, cancer patients who had the post-traumatic growth pattern not only took care of themselves but also reached the stage where they took care of their family and other people and empathize with others easily. The patient, who until now was surrounded by his needs, problems and goals, changes his direction towards others, this person is no longer someone who only looks after himself, but he takes care of others. At this stage, he loves God and his family. This shows the abandonment of selfishness and all this is actually the height of self-fulfillment [44]. Of course, in the current study, the subcategory of “being healthy for sake of one’s family” appeared more in older children, although they had stopped treatment, still realized the mental burden of disease in their parents, and therefore, they tried to prevent the psychological effects of their illness on the family.

Limitations and strengths

One of the limitations of the present study was that in order to observe accuracy in the study and minimize interfering factors, the study should be carried out in the form of individual interviews with children, which increases the implementation process and takes time.

The greater contact of the researcher with the children and their families in different spaces of the hospital, clinic and charity institutions, as well as the increase in the duration of the interviews as long as the participants wanted to continue, were among the strengths of the present study that these factors played an effective role in clarifying the mental content of children and their families. Due to the breadth and richness of information collected in qualitative research, this method can be used to discover different psychological aspects of children with cancer and find suitable strategies for their long-term survival.

Since this study was conducted on childhood cancer survivors, it is suggested that in future researches, the point of view of hospitalized children and children who survived cancer should be investigated and compared protective care in different aspects of their personal and family life.

Conclusion

Several interventions are being developed to encourage cancer survivors to engage in self-care. To inform such interventions for CCSs, it is essential to understand their perspectives and why they behave the way they do. The results of our research determined that self-protection is one of the most important cares for childhood cancer survivors. Indeed, a significant challenge for childhood cancer survivors is how to ensure effective transition to long-term follow-up. During this transition, survivors typically begin to have less contact with their clinical team. On the other hand, survivors are getting older and the influence of parents on their health care is decreasing. Thus, many of the childhood cancer survivors in this study recognized that they were responsible for maintaining their health and well-being, and they reported engaging in a variety of protective approaches to self-care.
