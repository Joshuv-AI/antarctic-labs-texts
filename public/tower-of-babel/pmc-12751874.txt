Cefazolin-induced coagulopathy: a case report
Authors: Olivier Zerbib, Daniel Kovarsky, Michael Oshrov, Smadja Pierre, lev Shaoul
Published: 2025

Introduction

Cefazolin, a first-generation cephalosporin antibiotic, is commonly used in clinical practice to treat infections caused by gram-positive cocci [1]. Although it is extensively employed in clinical practice, adverse effects associated with its use are uncommon. A multicenter prospective study on patients treated with cefazolin for methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) bacteremia reported that 12.7% (10 out of 69 patients) discontinued treatment due to adverse effects [2]. The most frequently observed adverse effect was drug-induced fever (5 out of 69 cases), followed by skin rash (2 out of 69 cases).

Another study, focusing on cefazolin-treated patients undergoing surgery for bone and joint infections, identified additional rare adverse effects [3]. Among 94 patients analyzed, one developed Clostridium difficile colitis, while another experienced a neurological symptoms.

Hematological adverse effects associated with cefazolin use are extremely rare. Several cases of cefazolin-induced coagulopathy, presenting as hypoprothrombinemia and elevated international normalized ratio (INR), have been documented in the literature [4–6]. These coagulopathies are more frequently observed in patients with underlying renal dysfunction or malnutrition, though they have also been reported in individuals with normal kidney and liver function [7].

The exact mechanism of cefazolin-induced coagulopathy remains unclear. However, it is believed to involve the inhibition of vitamin K epoxide reductase, a hepatic enzyme essential for the synthesis of coagulation factors II, VII, IX, X, protein C, protein S, and protein Z [8]. This hypothesis stems from the chemical structure of cefazolin, which includes a methyl-thiadiazole (MTD) side chain [8]. The MTD side chain is structurally similar to N-methyl-thiotetrazole (NMTT), a side chain present in other cephalosporins that has been shown to disrupt vitamin K metabolism. This proposed mechanism may also explain the increased risk of cefazolin-induced coagulopathy in patients with preexisting vitamin K deficiency.

Chung and Watson identified malnutrition, neoplasia, renal insufficiency, recent surgery, and ileus as high-risk factors for cefazolin-induced coagulopathy [9]. Furthermore, high-dose cefazolin therapy, especially when adjusted for obese patients, has been identified as another potential risk factor [7].

The clinical presentation of this potentially life-threatening condition often includes bleeding symptoms. These may range from mild manifestations, such as bruising or hematuria, to severe complications such as gastrointestinal bleeding or intracranial hemorrhage, typically occurring 5–17 days after the first dose of cefazolin [5, 10, 11]. A significant rise in PT/INR levels is commonly observed within 2–20 days (median of 9.5 days) following the initiation of cefazolin treatment, necessitating immediate discontinuation of the drug and supplementation with vitamin K (20 mg intravenously) [5]. PT/INR levels generally return to baseline within 1–2 days after cefazolin withdrawal; however, bleeding complications may require additional interventions and close monitoring [5].

In this case report, we present an instance of cefazolin-induced coagulopathy, emphasizing the critical need for heightened awareness of this rare but serious adverse effect.

Case presentation

A 78-year-old Caucasian woman was admitted to our emergency department with new-onset shortness of breath, which was attributed to a pleural exudate on the left side. Her medical history includes chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), morbid obesity (BMI > 42), a history of lumpectomy for breast carcinoma diagnosed in 2007, and permanent atrial fibrillation.

Upon admission, lab tests showed mild renal dysfunction with elevated creatinine (1.82 mg/dl, normal range: 0.7-1.4mg/dl), and eGFR 35 (correlates with stage III chronic kidney disease [2021 CKD-EPI Creatinine equation, https://www.mdcalc.com/calc/3939/ckd-epi-equations-glomerular-filtration-rate-gfr]). No hematologic abnormalities, PT/INR 1.3 (normal range 0.4–1.4). Low serum albumin 2.5mg/dl (normal range 3.5–5mg/dl), with normal liver function enzymes levels.

The fluid drained from the pleural cavity was initially interpreted as a transudate, while a subsequent pleural drainage was categorized as an exudate. Given the growth of Staphylococcus lugdunensis (a coagulase-negative cocci) on four separate blood cultures, all showing sensitivity to cephalosporins, the patient was treated with cefazolin intravenously (2 g intravenously three times per day). Considering the serious risk of endocarditis associated with Staphylococcus lugdunensis, we decided to proceed with full-dose cefazolin treatment while awaiting the results of a transesophageal echocardiography (TEE).

Treatment for the iatrogenic pneumothorax, including the placement of a left-side drainage tube, initially led to a temporary improvement in the patient’s respiratory status. However, the patient soon experienced mental deterioration and severe respiratory distress, which required mechanical ventilation in the intensive care unit (ICU). Owing to hemodynamic instability, vasopressors were administered, and a new anterior drainage tube was inserted on the left side (as a new pneumothorax was detected on chest CT). Shortly after, the patient’s hemodynamic condition stabilized.

On day 4 after initiating cefazolin treatment, blood tests revealed elevated INR values (15–20) with normal fibrinogen levels and no clinical or hematologic signs of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) (Fig. 1). A blood smear ruled out hemolysis, with no schizocytes detected. At this point, cefazolin-induced coagulopathy was suspected, leading to the discontinuation of cefazolin treatment. The patient was administered 20 mg of intravenous vitamin K and 2 units of intravenous fresh frozen plasma.

The dynamics of INR values throughout the hospitalization. On day 4 after starting cefazolin, cefazolin treatment was discontinued, and vitamin K (20 mg intravenously) along with 2 units of intravenous fresh frozen plasma were administered

Within 12 h, the INR levels began to decline and returned to the normal range within 24 h. On the basis of the antibiotic sensitivity results from blood cultures, the treatment regimen was switched to levofloxacin and ertapenem, which the patient tolerated well.

However, owing to ongoing deterioration of kidney function, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was initiated. A third anterior thoracic drain was placed, but unfortunately, the patient succumbed to multi-organ failure and septic shock (Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this paper, we present a case of cefazolin-induced coagulopathy treated at our medical center. Several cases of cefazolin-induced coagulopathy have been documented in the literature [4–8, 12]. Among the identified risk factors for this disorder, our patient had several key contributors: chronic kidney disease (since cefazolin is primarily excreted via the renal pathways [13]), deterioration of renal function during hospitalization, hypoalbuminemia (likely linked to malnutrition, vitamin K deficiency, and severe capillary leak due to an inflammatory state), morbid obesity (which necessitated higher doses of cefazolin), and the requirement for ICU care.

In our case, the patient did not exhibit significant signs or symptoms of bleeding disorders, and the diagnosis was primarily based on routine blood tests and a comprehensive clinical history.

This case, along with others previously published, emphasizes the need for a high index of suspicion for this rare adverse effect. As cefazolin-induced coagulopathy is a rare side effect of this commonly used antibiotic, routine coagulation tests—particularly INR monitoring in high-risk patients—may help serve as an effective preventive approach. Since this coagulopathy may manifest with or without overt bleeding, routine coagulation tests should be conducted, potentially preventing life-threatening bleeding associated with this condition.

Conclusion

We presented a case of a 78-year-old patient who developed cefazolin-induced coagulopathy, a rare side effect of this antibiotic. Retrospectively, the patient had several risk factors associated with this condition, including underlying renal dysfunction, high-dose cefazolin treatment, and ICU hospitalization, which could have indicated the potential for this adverse effect. Early diagnosis based on abnormal lab results and prompt discontinuation of cefazolin, along with vitamin K supplementation, could have prevented life-threatening bleeding complications.
