Simultaneous 3D structural-functional assessment of pediatric lung using 0.55-T MRI: a feasibility study
Authors: Xin Miao, Nam G Lee, HaiThuy N Nguyen, Jesus Uribe, Eamon K Doyle, Stanford Chun, Ziwei Zhao, Sophia X Cui, Kamal Singhrao, Gerardo Perez Rojas, Narayan P Iyer, Roberta M Kato, Krishna S Nayak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPediatric pulmonary diseases are commonly evaluated with imaging that either involves ionizing radiation or that lacks regional functional information.PurposeTo evaluate the feasibility of simultaneous structural and functional lung assessment in pediatric participants without radiation, contrast administration, or breath-holding.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed pediatric patients with pulmonary disease and healthy participants, who were recruited from August 2023 to December 2025 via convenience sampling and underwent awake lung MRI on wide-bore 0.55-T scanners. A prototype balanced steady-state free-precession sequence with a half-radial dual-echo readout (bSTAR) was acquired with free breathing. Respiratory-resolved 3-dimensional images were reconstructed for structural evaluation and quantitative ventilation mapping. Two pediatric radiologists independently scored image quality (robustness to motion and streak artifacts, and parenchymal signal) and the anatomic visibility of the airways and vessels using 4-point Likert scales. Interreader agreement of structural findings was evaluated using Cohen’s κ. Ventilation defect percentage measured by bSTAR was compared with 2-dimensional phase-resolved functional lung (PREFUL) MRI using Pearson correlation and Bland–Altman analysis.ResultsFourteen participants (median [range] age, 11 [6-16] years; 8 females) were scanned. Image quality scores ranged from 3.0 to 3.8 on Likert scales. All cases were diagnostically acceptable (artifact robustness scores ≥2), with consistent visualization of segmental airway walls and subsegmental vasculature. Structural findings, including consolidation, mucus plugging, ground-glass opacity, air trapping, bronchiectasis, and mosaic hypointensity, showed substantial interreader agreement (κ = 0.6-0.8). Ventilation defects measured by bSTAR correlated strongly with PREFUL (r = 0.81, P < .001), with 95% limits of agreement of −18% to 23%.ConclusionSimultaneous structural-functional lung imaging in a single free-breathing acquisition was feasible in this cohort of older awake pediatric participants using 0.55-T MRI, with structural images suitable for evaluating major pulmonary findings and quantitative ventilation metrics correlating strongly with PREFUL.

Introduction

Pediatric pulmonary diseases are diagnosed and monitored with limited tools. CT, as the clinical standard for lung structural imaging, is often avoided in routine or repetitive use because of concerns about ionizing radiation.1,2 The standard spirometry-based pulmonary function test provides only global measurements that can be insensitive to early regional changes.3

MRI offers a radiation-free alternative for lung imaging, but at conventional field strengths has been hindered by the intrinsically low parenchymal signal and pronounced susceptibility artifacts at air–tissue interfaces. As a result, structural assessment has largely been limited to detecting hyperintense abnormalities, and achieving meaningful parenchymal signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) requires an ultra-short echo (UTE) approach.4 Recently, 0.55-T MRI emerged as a potential tool to fundamentally improve lung parenchymal imaging.5–7 At this lower field strength, lung T2* increases by about 10-fold (∼10 ms compared to ∼1 ms at 1.5 T),8 and susceptibility artifacts are markedly reduced. Balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) imaging at 0.55 T has shown particularly promising performance, leveraging both the favorable lung-imaging properties of low field strengths and the inherently high SNR efficiency of bSSFP sequence.7,9–11 Submillimeter isotropic resolution with “CT-like” depiction of lung parenchyma was demonstrated in adults.7 Despite this promise, 0.55-T bSSFP lung imaging has not yet been evaluated in pediatric populations.

Combining structural and functional imaging can improve diagnostic confidence when structural findings alone are ambiguous. Dynamic proton MRI has enabled noncontrast-enhanced lung functional imaging.12–15 For example, phase-resolved functional lung (PREFUL) MRI can quantify ventilation and perfusion by analyzing the periodic lung signal variations over respiratory and cardiac cycles.13,15 Two-dimensional (2D) PREFUL has been extensively validated against SPECT12 and hyperpolarized MRI16,17 and investigated in pediatric populations.17,18 Three-dimensional (3D) PREFUL has also been implemented to perform 3D ventilation mapping using UTE sequences at conventional field strengths, although the spatial resolution is substantially reduced to maintain adequate parenchymal SNR.15,16,19

This study aimed to investigate the feasibility of 0.55-T MRI for radiation and contrast-free lung assessment in the pediatric population. A technique combining 0.55-T bSSFP lung imaging with quantitative ventilation function mapping was applied to achieve 3D simultaneous structural and functional imaging within a single free-breathing acquisition.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This was a retrospective analysis of pediatric participants who were prospectively recruited and imaged between August 2023 and December 2025 at an academic pediatric medical center. Patients with pulmonary disease were referred from the pediatric pulmonology clinic; healthy participants were recruited from the public. Inclusion criteria were age 6 to 18 years and the ability to tolerate awake MRI. Exclusion criteria included claustrophobia and MRI-incompatible implants. The study was approved by the institutional review board and is Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act compliant. All participants provided written consent and assent (if age >7 years). Participants were enrolled as a convenience sample, and the numbers of patients approached and of those who declined were not systematically recorded. No formal sample size calculation was performed.

Data acquisition

MRI scans were performed on either of two 0.55-T scanners: a prototype system (“ramped-down” MAGNETOM Aera, Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany) with high gradient specifications (amplitude limit, 45 mT/m; slew rate limit, 200 T/m/s) and a commercial system (MAGNETOM Free.Max, Siemens Healthineers) with different gradient specifications (amplitude limit, 26 mT/m; slew rate limit, 45 T/m/s).

Two MRI sequences were acquired. Balanced steady-state free-precession sequence with half-radial dual-echo readout (bSTAR)7,9 is a 3D radial prototype sequence that uses bSSFP contrast with short repetition time, combining 2-echo readouts to maximize parenchymal SNR. This study used the open-source implementation described by Lee et al.9,20 Imaging parameters on the prototype 0.55-T scanner: Flip angle = 25°, TE1/TE2/TR = 0.2/2.0/2.2 ms, spatial resolution = 0.9 mm isotropic, field of view = 340 mm isotropic, and scan time = 13 minutes. Imaging parameters on the commercial 0.55-T scanner are detailed in Table S1, with major differences being spatial resolution (1.3-mm isotropic) and scan time (8 minutes). As a reference for ventilation function mapping, 2D PREFUL was acquired at 2 to 3 central coronal slice locations using a commercially available bSSFP sequence with an in-plane resolution of 2 mm (interpolated) and a slice thickness of 15 mm.

Chest CT was acquired only on patients as part of clinical care, using a field of view of 270 to 370 mm, in-plane resolution of 0.5 to 0.7 mm, section thickness of 0.6 to 1.2 mm, tube potential of 100 or 120 kVp, and effective tube current of 28 to 55 mAs. The volumetric CT dose index was 2.2 to 5.8 mGy and the dose–length product 75 to 211 mGy·cm.

MRI reconstruction and postprocessing

MRI reconstruction and postprocessing were performed offline in MATLAB R2023a (MathWorks, Natick, MA), using identical processing pipelines across scanners. The correction of the k-space trajectory and the generation of the self-navigation waveform are described in detail in Lee et al.9 Bulk motion was identified manually in the self-navigation waveform, and the corresponding raw data were discarded. Then, the raw data were sorted into 3 motion states based on motion amplitude. Non-Cartesian compressed sensing reconstruction was performed for each motion state, with wavelet regularization enforcing sparsity in the spatial domain.21

A quantitative ventilation map was generated by registering the end-exhalation and end-inhalation frames to the middle-position frame using deedsBCV,22,23 an open-source deformable registration algorithm. Then, fractional ventilation (FV) is calculated for each lung pixel as FV = Sex-SinSex, where Sex and Sin are the signal intensities at the end-exhalation and end-inhalation frames,13 respectively. 2D PREFUL data were processed using a research application software (MR Lung v2.3.0, Siemens Healthineers). Details on image postprocessing can be found in the Supplemental Material.

Structural lung MRI evaluation

Reconstructed 3D volumes at end-exhalation frame were used for structural assessment. The analyses included binary presence versus absence assessment of structural findings, subjective image-quality assessment using Likert scales, and image SNR analysis. Structural findings and image quality were assessed by 2 pediatric radiologists (H.N.N., a pediatric radiology attending with 9 years of postfellowship experience, and J.U., a pediatric radiology fellow), who independently reviewed structural lung MRI scans presented in a randomized order, blinded to CT findings and clinical information.

For the structural finding assessment, each of the following features was recorded as present or absent at the subject level: consolidation, mucus plugging, airway collapse, air trapping, bronchiectasis, ground-glass opacity, and mosaic hypointensity. Interreader agreement was evaluated from the independent reads. The readers then reconciled any structural finding discrepancies by consensus, and the consensus findings were used as the final MRI structural assessment for comparison with the clinical CT reports (described in the following section). For the image-quality assessment, four characteristics, including artifact robustness, parenchymal signal, airway visibility, and vessel visibility, were each scored on a specific Likert scale adapted from Johnson et al.24 Image-quality scores were averaged across the 2 readers. Image SNR was assessed by generating a pixel-wise SNR map for each dataset using the pseudo-replica method.25 Parenchymal SNR was reported as the median SNR within a region of interest spanning the entire 3D lung volume (excluding vessels), and vessel SNR as the median SNR within the vessel regions. Details of the SNR analysis are provided in the Supplemental Material.

Lung MRI and chest CT comparison

When available, chest CT scans performed within 1 year of the lung MRIs were used for comparison. If there were multiple CT scans for a patient, then the one closest in time to the MRI was used. The binary assessment of each structural feature, obtained from MRI consensus reading and clinical CT reports, was compared at the patient level. The original CT images were not independently rereviewed, and no anatomic (lobar or zonal) correlation was attempted. Agreement was summarized as the number of patient-level findings in which the MRI and the CT reports concurred.

Ventilation mapping evaluation

Ventilation function was quantitatively assessed using ventilation defect percentage (VDP), defined as the proportion of defect regions relative to the whole lung area. bSTAR-based 3D FV maps were compared with PREFUL-based 2D FV maps at matched coronal slice locations. Because the 2D PREFUL maps had a 15-mm slice thickness, the bSTAR comparators were generated by averaging slices that spanned the same anatomical range. Ventilation defects were defined as voxels with FV values less than 0.4 × the 90th percentile of the distribution, following the established PREFUL thresholding methods.13 Additionally, the spatial agreement of defects was assessed using an automated defect scoring approach that quantified inter-method differences in 6 subsegments of the lung (Figure S2).

Statistical analysis

Interreader agreement for each structural feature category was assessed using Cohen’s κ statistic. Percent agreement was calculated as the proportion of identical ratings across cases. The agreement of VDP measurement between bSTAR and 2D PREFUL was analyzed using Pearson correlation to evaluate linear association and Bland–Altman analysis to assess bias and limits of agreement. All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.9), and significance was defined as P < .05.

Code availability

The bSTAR sequence, reconstruction, and postprocessing codes used in this study are openly available through the repositories cited in the references.9,23

Results

All 14 recruited pediatric participants (median [range] age, 11 [6-16] years; 8 females) completed the 0.55-T lung MRI and were included in the analysis. No participant withdrew. There were 5 healthy participants and 9 patients with pulmonary disease. Demographics are detailed in Table 1. Nine participants were scanned on the prototype 0.55-T MRI scanner, and 5 on the commercial 0.55-T scanner. Six of the 14 participants had a clinically indicated chest CT within 1 year of the MRI for comparison. In all 6, the CT examination preceded the MRI. The CT-MRI interval was variable and noncontemporaneous (median [range], 2.5 [0.1-10] months).

Discussion

This study demonstrated the feasibility of simultaneous 3D structural–functional lung assessment using a single 0.55-T bSTAR MRI acquisition, without ionizing radiation, contrast administration, or breath-holding. The lung structural images reliably detected both hyperintense and hypointense pathologies with substantial interreader agreement. Image quality was diagnostically acceptable (artifact robustness scores ≥2) in all cases. Segmental airways and pulmonary vasculature were consistently visualized, scored 3.0 (3-point scale) in all cases and 3.9 ± 0.2 (4-point scale), respectively. Ventilation defect percentage derived from the same bSTAR dataset showed a strong correlation with the reference 2D PREFUL method (r = 0.81, P < .001).

The results highlighted the advantages of bSSFP-based lung MRI at low field strength. At 0.55 T, the barriers that complicate bSSFP lung imaging at conventional field strengths, including banding artifacts and specific absorption rate constraints,26,27 are substantially reduced. This allows the strengths of bSSFP to be exploited: T2/T1-weighted contrast for reliable depiction of hyperintense pathologies and pulmonary vasculature, and high SNR efficiency to preserve parenchymal signal and support detection of hypointense abnormalities. This was reflected in the measured SNR, which was approximately 2-fold higher in vessels than in parenchyma, consistent with strong vascular contrast alongside retained parenchymal signal.

Parenchymal signal intensity differences between end-exhalation and end-inhalation frames were shown to serve as quantitative markers of regional ventilation, consistent with prior proton MRI studies.12,13 The correlation between bSTAR- and 2D PREFUL-based VDP measurements was comparable to previously reported correlations between 3D UTE and 2D PREFUL at high field strengths.15,19 Unlike prior approaches that require a separate scan,28 the proposed method enables the natural co-registration of structural and ventilation information from a single scan. The absence of ionizing radiation permits repeated assessment, an advantage over even low-dose and photon-counting CT.

Contemporary low-field MRI systems have seen increasing adoption because of their cost-effectiveness and improved accessibility.29–31 The same bSTAR sequence design was tested on two 0.55-T scanners: 1 prototype system with high-gradient performance and 1 commercial system with lower gradient performance. The commercial system provided lower spatial resolution because of reduced gradient performance. Despite the protocol difference, both systems provided sufficient image details for structural and functional evaluation in our predominantly adolescent cohort, indicating potential for broad clinical translation and increased accessibility. Formal cross-scanner comparisons were not performed because of the limited sample size, although an exploratory retrospective analysis suggested no significant differences in image quality or ventilation measurements between the 0.9- and 1.3-mm acquisitions (Table S2). Both protocol variants can, in principle, be adapted across body sizes. For example, 0.55-T bSTAR in naturally asleep infants has been explored in a pilot study.32 Nevertheless, imaging in infants would require optimization of spatial resolution and motion-management strategies. Scalability is further limited by the current postprocessing pipeline, which requires time-intensive image reconstruction and semiautomatic ventilation mapping with manual input. Broader clinical deployment will depend on accelerated reconstruction and more automated postprocessing.

Most structural MRI findings agreed with prior clinical CT reports at the patient level. The patient case where the 3 MRI–CT discordances occurred (Figure S1) was rated by both readers as having motion artifact that blurred some anatomy. The bronchiectasis discordance (MRI−/CT+) may reflect motion artifacts on MRI, which limit evaluation of subtle bronchiectasis, or interval resolution of traction bronchiectasis, given the concurrent resolution of the left lower lobe consolidation. Of the 2 findings seen on MRI but not reported on CT, the mosaic hypointensity was subtly present on CT on a retrospective review (Figure S1B), indicating report incompleteness, whereas air trapping could not be confirmed on CT. Future studies should pursue contemporaneous MRI–CT comparison with anatomic correlation to characterize structural agreement more rigorously.

There are several limitations of the study. First, the small and heterogeneous cohort precluded correlation with diagnostic performance and clinical outcomes. The CTs used for comparison were obtained for clinical indications rather than for the study, introducing indication bias toward participants with existing pulmonary pathologies, and were noncontemporaneous with the MRI, limiting lesion-level comparison. Larger, more homogeneous cohorts with contemporaneous, protocol-driven experimental and control imaging are needed to estimate diagnostic performance and establish clinical utility. Second, ventilation mapping methods were not fully optimized or validated. Future work will investigate advanced defect detection algorithms13 and include validation against established techniques such as hyperpolarized gas MRI or SPECT. Third, the relatively long acquisition time (8-13 minutes) increases susceptibility to motion and currently restricts application to cooperative participants. Ongoing efforts in sequence acceleration and motion-robust imaging are expected to improve feasibility across broader pediatric populations. Fourth, recruitment was opportunistic and convenience-based, which may have introduced selection bias toward participants able and willing to undergo research imaging.

In conclusion, simultaneous structural–functional lung imaging in a single free-breathing acquisition was feasible in this cohort of older awake pediatric participants using 0.55-T MRI, with structural findings showing majority agreement at the patient level with CT and quantitative ventilation metrics correlating strongly with PREFUL. Future work will be needed to focus on optimizing and validating 3D ventilation mapping, improving motion robustness and acquisition speed, and investigating correlations with clinical findings in larger, more homogeneous cohorts.
