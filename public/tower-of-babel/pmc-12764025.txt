The defensin gene NaDLP1, which confers resistance against Alternaria alternata, is transcriptionally regulated by NaWRKY3/6 in Nicotiana attenuata
Authors: Lan Ma, Guangfen Cui, Wenwen Du, Xiang Li, Wenjie Jia, Qing Duan, Jihua Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlant defensins are ubiquitous across plant species and serve as critical components of the innate immune system against fungal pathogens. In our previous study, we identified NaDLP1, a defensin-like protein 1-encoding gene in wild tobacco (Nicotiana attenuata), which was strongly induced by Alternaria alternata (tobacco pathotype). Virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) of NaDLP1 compromised resistance, highlighting its essential role in pathogen defense. However, the disease resistance mediated by NaDLP1 and its transcriptional regulation required more detailed studies.ResultsHere, we demonstrated that NaDLP1 encoded a typical defensin-like protein and could be secreted into the apoplast space. The recombinant NaDLP1-His protein exhibited potent antifungal activity, inhibiting A. alternata and Botrytis cinerea growth in vitro. Consistent with these findings, NaDLP1-silenced N. attenuata plants (generated via RNA interference (RNAi)) presented increased susceptibility to A. alternata. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and Reverse Transcription Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR) analyses revealed a significant reduction of NaDLP1 transcript levels in NaWRKY3- and NaWRKY6-silenced plants. Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) confirmed that both NaWRKY3 and NaWRKY6 directly bound to the W-box motif within the NaDLP1 promoter. Moreover, transient coexpression assays in Nicotiana benthamiana using firefly luciferase (LUC) as a reporter demonstrated that NaWRKY3 and NaWRKY6 functioned as positive regulators of NaDLP1 expression.ConclusionsCollectively, our findings establish NaDLP1 as a key defensin gene conferring resistance against tobacco brown spot disease, with its transcriptional activation mediated by NaWRKY3 and NaWRKY6.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07711-3.

Background

In their natural environments, plants are continuously exposed to pathogen pressure from various microorganisms, including fungal, bacterial, and viral pathogens. Through evolution, plants have developed complex innate immune systems to detect and resist these biological threats. This defense system comprises both constitutive and inducible components: preexisting physical and chemical barriers provide immediate protection, whereas pathogen recognition triggers the production of antimicrobial compounds such as defensive proteins, phytoalexins, and other secondary metabolites that collectively establish a hostile environment for invading pathogens.

Pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins are crucial components of plant defense systems and are induced in numerous plant species upon pathogen infection. These antimicrobial proteins are classified into 17 distinct families on the basis of their structural and functional characteristics. Among these defensins, plant defensins (PR-12 family), also known as γ-thionins, play a particularly important role in plant innate immunity against fungal pathogens [1, 2]. Structurally, plant defensins are small, cysteine-rich peptides characterized by remarkable stability. They typically contain eight conserved cysteine residues that form a distinctive three-dimensional structure comprising one α-helix and three antiparallel β-strands (βαββ motif) [3]. These peptides exhibit multiple antifungal mechanisms, including (1) inhibition of cell wall formation, (2) membrane disruption, (3) induction of oxidative stress leading to DNA damage, and (4) triggering of apoptotic pathways [4]. The defensive role of these peptides has been demonstrated across various plant species. For example, PR-12/Na-defensins in N. attenuata show activity against Pseudomonas syringae pv tomato-DC3000 [5]. Similarly, the Nicotiana alata defensins NaD1 and NaD2 exhibit broad-spectrum antifungal activity against multiple pathogenic fungi in vitro [6, 7]. More recently, Liu et al. reported that the PaDef gene in Picea asperata is strongly upregulated in response to Lophodermium piceae infection, with the purified protein showing significant antifungal activity against Pestalotiopsis neolitseae [8]. Additional studies have identified functional defensins (PnDEFL1 in Panax notoginseng and LrDef1 in Lilium regale) that are triggered by Fusarium solani infection and exhibit antifungal properties [9, 10]. Despite these advances, our understanding of plant defensins remains incomplete. While numerous defensin genes have been isolated and characterized across plant species, their specific roles in plant-pathogen interactions and the regulatory networks governing their expression require further investigation.

In N. attenuata, we previously identified NaDLP1, a pathogen-responsive gene that is strongly upregulated upon infection by A. alternata, the fungal pathogen responsible for tobacco brown spot disease [11]. Functional analysis via VIGS demonstrates that NaDLP1 plays a critical role in plant defense, as its knockdown significantly increases susceptibility to A. alternata. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms underlying NaDLP1-mediated antifungal activity, as well as the transcriptional regulation of its expression, remain to be elucidated.

WRKY transcription factors (TFs) serve as crucial regulators of plant immune responses, primarily through their ability to bind W-box cis-elements (T/CTGACT/C) in the promoter regions of defense-related genes [12]. These proteins are characterized by a conserved WRKY domain of approximately 60 amino acids, featuring the signature WRKYGQK sequence at the N-terminus and a zinc-finger motif at the C-terminus [13]. Functionally, WRKY TFs can either activate or repress downstream gene expression, thereby orchestrating complex stress response networks [14, 15]. Notably, WRKY TFs play pivotal roles in regulating secondary metabolic pathways associated with plant defense, including the biosynthesis of phytoalexins, phenylpropanoids, and defensins. It has been reported, for instance, in N. attenuata that the insect-responsive WRKY factors NaWRKY3 and NaWRKY6 modulate the biosynthesis of jasmonic acid (JA), subsequently influencing defense metabolism, including that of trypsin proteinase inhibitors (TPIs) and diterpene glycosides (DTGs) [16]. Recent studies have demonstrated that NaWRKY3 regulates phytoalexin biosynthesis and upstream immune signals, including phytohormones such as JA and ethylene, as well as reactive oxygen species (ROS). Additionally, the A. alternata resistance long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) L2 and NaBBL28 are also regulated by NaWRKY3 [17]. NaERF1B-L mediates the regulatory influence of NaWRKY3/6 on the biosynthesis of phytoalexins scopoletin and solavetivone during the infection of N. attenuata by A. alternata [18].

The regulatory function of WRKY TFs extends to defensin genes across various plant species. SlWRKY30 and SlWRKY81 synergistically regulate resistance to Ralstonia solanacearum infection by activating SlPR-STH2a/b/c/d expression in tomato [19]. CaWRKY40 and CaWRKY28 form a heterodimer, which directly binds to the W-box element in the promoter of the defensin gene CaDEF1, thereby activating its expression to enhance resistance against R. solanacearum in pepper [20]. Further studies have revealed that the activity of CaWRKY40 is regulated by the calcium-dependent protein kinase CaCDPK29: CaCDPK29 phosphorylates CaWRKY27b, promoting its translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus and its interaction with CaWRKY40. This forms a CDPK29-WRKY27b-WRKY40 module, which synergistically activates CaDEF1 to achieve the regulation of disease resistance [21]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, WRKY33 directly controls the expression of the well-characterized defensin PDF1.2 [22]. Similarly, in P. notoginseng, PnWRKY9 activates PnDEFL1 expression during Fusarium solani infection, whereas in L. regale, LrWRKY3 positively regulates LrDef1 expression in response to F. oxysporum challenge [9].

In this study, we heterologously expressed the recombinant defensin-like protein NaDLP1 in a bacterial system and evaluated its antifungal activity against two economically important plant pathogenic fungi. To further elucidated the defensive role of NaDLP1 in plant, we generated and characterized NaDLP1-silenced RNAi transformants of N. attenuata. Our investigation revealed that the expression of NaDLP1 during the pathogen response was modulated by the WRKY transcription factors NaWRKY3 and NaWRKY6. Through EMSA and dual-luciferase (Dual-Luc) reporter assay, we demonstrated that these activated transcription factors directly bound to the NaDLP1 promoter and increased its expression.

Methods

Plant and fungal materials

The seeds of the wild-type (WT) N. attenuata used in this study were derived from the 35th generation of an inbred line. The NaWRKY3- and NaWRKY6-silenced transgenic lines (designated ir-wrky3 and ir-wrky6, respectively) were previously generated by Skibbe et al. [16] and were kindly provided by Professor Ian T. Baldwin (Max Planck Institute for Chemical Ecology, Germany). All the seeds were germinated, and the plants were cultivated under controlled conditions according to the established protocol of Krügel et al. [23].

The tobacco pathotype A. alternata strain CN174 was maintained at −80 °C in our laboratory stock collection. For experimental use, fungal cultures were activated by growth on potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates at 28 °C for 5 days. Leaf inoculations were performed following the standardized infection procedure described by Sun et al. [24].

The causal agent of tobacco gray mold, B. cinerea, was isolated from symptomatic tobacco leaves and stored at −80 °C for long-term preservation.

Bioinformatic analysis

The physicochemical properties of the protein were characterized using the ProtParam tool (ExPASy; https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). Conserved domains were identified through SMART analysis (Simple Modular Architecture Research Tool; http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/). Secondary structure prediction was performed using SOPMA (Self-Optimized Prediction Method with Alignment; https://npsa-prabi.ibcp.fr/), while tertiary structure modeling was conducted using SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/), a homology modeling server.

Isolation of RNA and Reverse Transcription Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)

For gene expression analysis, leaf laminae infected with A. alternata were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was isolated via TRIzol reagent (TransGen Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis, and quantification was performed via a NanoDrop spectrophotometer.

First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA via the BeyoRT II cDNA Synthesis Kit (Beyotime). qPCR was performed via iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table 1) were designed via Primer-BLAST. The N. attenuata actin gene (NaActin II, GenBank accession: XM_019370716.1) served as the internal reference for normalization. Each reaction was performed with 3–4 independent biological replicates.

RNA interference (RNAi)

To construct RNAi vectors, we amplified inverted-repeat fragments targeting specific regions of the NaDLP1 gene and cloned them into the pRESC8 plant transformation vector. The recombinant pRESC8-NaDLP1 plasmid was introduced into wild-type N. attenuata plants via Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation (strain GV3101).

Following transformation and regeneration, we identified two independent transgenic lines (ir-dlp1 #1 and ir-dlp1 #2) that were advanced to the T2 generation and confirmed to be homozygous through segregation analysis before being used for subsequent experiments. Molecular characterization by RT-qPCR confirmed significant knockdown (> 75%) of NaDLP1 transcript levels in both transgenic lines compared with the WT controls.

Subcellular localization

The subcellular localization of NaDLP1 was first predicted via the PSORT program (https://www.genscript.com/psort.html), which suggested potential extracellular secretion. For experimental verification, we performed transient expression assays in N. benthamiana leaves via the use of GFP-tagged fusion proteins. The complete NaDLP1 open reading frame (ORF), excluding the stop codon, was amplified using gene-specific primers (referred to Supplementary Table 1) and subsequently cloned and inserted into the pCAMBIA1301 vector, upstream of the eGFP reporter gene, under the regulation of the CaMV 35 S promoter. The empty pCAMBIA1301-35 S::eGFP vector served as a localization control. Both constructs were subsequently transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101, which were subsequently infiltrated into 4-week-old N. benthamiana leaves. Three days after the injection, the fluorescence signal was detected and captured under the confocal microscope. To further demonstrate that whether NaDLP1 was an extracellularly secreted protein, we conducted a plasmolysis experiment, with the specific method as follows: Leaf epidermal peels were soaked in a 20 µM FM4-64 solution to dye the cell membranes for 5 min. Subsequently, they were plasmolyzed by incubation in a 500 mM sucrose solution for 5 min. Finally, microscopic observation was performed.

Prokaryotic expression and antifungal activity

We amplified the mature NaDLP1 coding sequence (CDS) via PCR and cloned it into the pET-28a expression vector to generate a His-tagged fusion protein. The recombinant NaDLP1-His protein was expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) cells induced with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG; Beyotime) at 16 °C for 16 h. The soluble protein was purified via Ni-NTA affinity chromatography (Beyotime) under native conditions, followed by buffer exchange into PBS (pH 7.4). The SDS-PAGE system (Beyotime) was utilized to detect the induction and purification of proteins (2 µg of NaDLP1-His protein). Concurrently, a western blot experiment was conducted on the same samples using a His antibody (Beyotime, 1000-fold dilution) for verification.

The inhibitory effects of NaDLP1 were evaluated against (A) alternata and (B) cinerea via a hyphal growth inhibition assay. Fungal cultures were grown on PDA plates for 3 days at 28 °C, and 100 µL of purified NaDLP1 (0.50 or 0.30 mg/mL in PBS) was applied to fungal colonies. The control treatments received equal volumes of protein storage buffer and His protein obtained through induction and purification of the empty vector pET-28a. The hyphal growth inhibition was documented after 48 h of incubation at 28 °C and the area of fungal growth inhibition was caculated by ImageJ.

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA)

The recombinant NaWRKY3/6-His proteins were acquired as described above. Biotin-labeled DNA probes and mutant probes corresponding to putative W-box elements in the NaDLP1 promoter were synthesized by Sangon Biotech. The final concentration of the purified protein used for EMSA was 300 ng/µL, and 2 µL of the purified protein was added to the 20 µL reaction system. The double-stranded DNA probe and mutant probe were diluted to 33 ng/µL, respectively, while the unlabeled probe was diluted to 1,650 ng/µL. The final volume used for both probes was 1 µL. Additionally, we established a normal reaction group, a cold competition probe binding reaction group, and a mutant probe reaction group, respectively. The specific EMSA binding reaction can be performed according to the Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit instructions (Beyotime). Then the DNA-protein complexes were transferred to nylon membranes and detected via a Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Specificity was confirmed through a competition assay using a 50-fold molar excess of unlabeled probe and the labeled probe with mutations at the W-box site, respectively.

Dual-Luciferase reporter assay (Dual-Luc)

The 1813 bp promoter region of NaDLP1 was PCR-amplified from N. attenuata genomic DNA and cloned upstream of the firefly luciferase (LUC) reporter gene in the pCAMBIA3301 vector to generate the proDLP1::LUC reporter construct. The proNaDLP1-mut fragment, which contained a mutation in the W-box site, was biosynthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai). Subsequently, we obtained the proDLP1-mut::LUC reporter construct. The full-length CDS of NaWRKY3 and NaWRKY6 were independently cloned and inserted into the pCAMBIA3301 vector under the control of the CaMV 35 S promoter to create effector constructs (35 S::NaWRKY3 and 35 S::NaWRKY6). A. tumefaciens strains, each harboring the reporter and effector constructs, were coinfiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. Samples were collected 48 h postinfiltration, and the Dual-Luc Reporter Assay System was used to measure LUC and Renilla luciferase (REN) activity with the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (YEASEN, 11402ES60) on a Multimode Microplate Reader (Tecan, infinite® M200 Pro). The primer sequences for all the constructs were provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical differences in gene expression, lesion diameter, and the LUC/REN ratio between the control and experimental groups were analyzed by Student’s t-test (*, ** and ***, significant difference at p < 0.05, p < 0.01 and p < 0.001, respectively) or one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s test (P < 0.05).

Accession numbers

The nucleotide sequences of NaDLP1 (GenBank accession: XM_019410997.1), NaWRKY3 (XM_019394677.1), and NaWRKY6 (AY456272) are available in the GenBank database.

Results

Identification of the NaDLP1 gene

Through the analysis of our previous RNA-seq data of N. attenuata inoculated with A. alternata [18], we identified four defensin-like protein-encoding genes that respond to A. alternata (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). Among these genes, the defensin gene most highly induced was NaDLP1 (XM_019410997.1). The NaDLP1 encodes a 252-bp ORF corresponding to an 83-amino acid (aa) polypeptide with a predicted molecular weight of 9.64 kDa and a theoretical isoelectric point (pI) of 9.12. The deduced protein exhibited typical plant defensin features (Fig. 1A), including a 28-aa signal peptide followed by a 50-aa mature peptide. SMART analysis classified NaDLP1 as a member of the gamma-thionin family, which contains the characteristic Knot1 domain. Although sequence homology with known plant defensins was relatively low-34.29%, 33.33%, 33.73%, and 37.35% identity to Medicago sativa (MsAFP, AF319468_1), Picea asperata (PaDef, QNR01021), Raphanus sativus (RsAFP1, XP_018469135.1), and A. thaliana (PDF1.2, NP_199255), respectively-multiple sequence alignment revealed eight conserved cysteine residues critical for the formation of four disulfide bridges, a hallmark of defensin structural stability [25]. Furthermore, NaDLP1 adopts a conserved βαββ tertiary structure comprising three β-strands (β1, β2, β3) and one α-helix (α1) (Fig. 1B).

The presence of an N-terminal signal peptide and PSORT II (https://www.genscript.com/tools/psort)-predicted the extracellular, cell wall localization suggested that NaDLP1 could be a secretory protein (Supplementary Fig. 2). To validate this, we constructed a NaDLP1-eGFP fusion vector and transiently expressed it in N. benthamiana via A. tumefaciens-mediated transformation. Before plasmolysis, confocal microscopy confirmed that the green fluorescence (GFP) signal of NaDLP1-eGFP largely overlapped with the red fluorescence signal of the FM4-64 reagent, which specifically stains the cell membrane. Moreover, the GFP signal exhibited uneven aggregation around the membrane, whereas free eGFP (control) was distributed throughout the cell. Following plasmolysis, NaDLP1 was detected in the apoplast space, with intense GFP signal concentrated at retracted membrane regions, suggesting active secretion of NaDLP1 via exocytic vesicles (Fig. 1C). These findings collectively indicated that the secreted extracellular NaDLP1 protein might directly interact with fungal pathogens upon contact or participate in extracellular defense signaling.

NaDLP1 protein inhibits A. alternata growth in vitro

To investigate the antifungal activity of NaDLP1, the NaDLP1-His fusion protein was obtained via a prokaryotic expression system. SDS-PAGE analysis revealed that the recombinant NaDLP1-His protein, predicted to have a molecular weight of approximately 16 kDa (including the His tag), showed high-level expression when induced with 1 mM IPTG at 16 °C. In contrast, no corresponding band was detected in the pET-28a empty vector control under the same induction conditions. The soluble recombinant NaDLP1-His protein was subsequently purified using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography (Beyotime), and a prominent band at the predicted molecular weight appeared on the SDS-PAGE gel, while nonspecific bands were observed in the purified pET-28a empty vector control. Western blot analysis with a His-antibody further confirmed the specificity of the detected band, with signals exclusively present in the lanes containing the NaDLP1-His protein. (Fig. 2A).

To assess the antifungal activity of NaDLP1, 100 µL of purified recombinant protein (either 0.50 or 0.30 mg/mL in PBS) was tested against (A) alternata and (B) cinerea. The results demonstrated that NaDLP1 exhibits broad-spectrum antifungal activity; furthermore, the antifungal activity increased with the increasing mass of the recombinant NaDLP1 protein. NaDLP1 Silencing compromises resistance to A. alternata

In our previous research, we demonstrated that silencing the defensin gene NaDLP1, which was highly induced by A. alternata, via VIGS significantly reduced N. attenuata’s resistance to this pathogen [26]. To further validate its functional role, we generated two independent NaDLP1 RNAi transgenic lines (ir-dlp1 #1/2). Compared with the WT plants, both lines exhibited significant suppression of NaDLP1 expression, with reductions of 79% (line #1) and 92% (line #2) following pathogen invasion (Fig. 3A). Consistent with this molecular phenotype, NaDLP1-silenced plants presented markedly increased susceptibility to A. alternata infection. Three days postinoculation, both transgenic lines presented significantly attenuated disease resistance relative to that of the WT controls, as evidenced by lesion severity (Fig. 3B and C). These findings conclusively establish that NaDLP1 induction is critical for effective defense against A. alternata in N. attenuata.

NaWRKY3/6 is essential for the expression of NaDLP1

From the previous transcriptome data [17], we identified the four defensin-like protein-encoding genes that respond to A. alternata were all downregulated in ir-wrky3 plants compared to the WT (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). Subsequent RT-qPCR experiments demonstrated that the substantial induction of NaDLP1 by A. alternata infection was indeed attenuated in ir-wrky3 plants and its expression was reduced by approximately 68% at 1 day postinoculation (dpi) and approximately 44% at 2 dpi, respectively, relative to that in the WT (Fig. 4A). However, there was no significant difference in the transcript levels of the other three induced defensin genes (XM_019400998.1, XM_019411243.1, and XM_019401036.1) between ir-wrky3/6 plants and the WT (as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1B).

Given that our previous research indicated that NaWRKY6 was a protein similar to NaWRKY3 [18], we verified that NaDLP1 expression was also inhibited in NaWRKY6-silenced plants (ir-wrky6) (Fig. 4A). Here, we revalidated the accuracy of the ir-wrky3 and ir-wrky6 silencing constructs. RT-qPCR analyses demonstrated that NaWRKY3 expression was suppressed by 71% in the ir-wrky3 lines relative to WT plants, whereas the ir-wrky6 constructs achieved a 93% silencing efficiency for the target gene at 1 dpi (Fig. 4B).

To bolster the evidence supporting the regulatory roles of NaWRKY3 and NaWRKY6 in NaDLP1 expression, we transiently overexpressed NaWRKY3 in the leaves of ir-wrky3 and NaWRKY6 in those of ir-wrky6 plants, respectively. Overexpressing NaWRKY3/6 in RNAi constructs could prevent interference from the high induction of NaWRKY3/6 following WT infection with A. alternata. Subsequently, we analyzed NaDLP1 expression levels and disease resistance phenotypes. Compared to the control group of Agrobacterium-mediated plants injected with the empty vector (EV), the ir-wrky3/6 plants, which were introduced with OV-WRKY3/6 vectors to drive high-level expression of NaWRKY3/6 (Supplementary Figs. 3 A, 3 C), experienced a significant upregulation of NaDLP1 at Mock 1 d or 1 dpi (Supplementary Figs. 3B, 3D). Furthermore, the lesion sizes were notably diminished in both OV-WRKY3/ir-wrky3 and OV-WRKY6/ir-wrky6 compared to their corresponding RNAi plants, indicating enhanced disease resistance (Supplementary Figs. 3E, 3 F). These results collectively reinforced the regulatory relationship between NaWRKY3/6 and NaDLP1 in the context of plant defense responses.

NaWRKY3/6 directly promotes NaDLP1 transcription

To explore the underlying molecular mechanism by which NaWRKY3/6 regulated NaDLP1 expression, we analyzed the NaDLP1 promoter sequence (2000 bp). The PlantCARE prediction program identified a W-box cis-acting element with the core sequence TTGACC at position − 1561 bp (Fig. 5A). EMSA was performed to examine the direct binding of NaWRKY3/6-His proteins to the W-box site within the NaDLP1 promoter region. As depicted in Fig. 5B, a distinct signal was present in the EMSA gel when both the biotin-labeled probe and the purified NaWRKY3 or NaWRKY6 protein were included. Notably, this signal was diminished when an unlabeled competitor, 50-fold more abundant than the biotin-labeled probe, was added. However, when the mutant probe was added, the retarded band was not observable.

The Dual-Luc assay was conducted on N. benthamiana leaves to examine the in vivo transactivation activity of the NaDLP1 promoter by NaWRKY3/6. We fused the promoter of NaDLP1/NaDLP1-mut with the LUC gene to generate two reporter constructs. NaWRKY3/6, under the control of the CaMV 35 S promoter, were used as effector constructs (Fig. 5C). When various combinations of reporters and effectors were individually injected into N. benthamiana leaves, RT-qPCR analysis confirmed that NaWRKY3, NaWRKY6, and GUS were all effectively expressed, as depicted in Fig. 5D. The cotransformation of the effector and proDLP1-LUC reporter significantly increased the LUC/REN ratio compared to the control (transformed with the reporter and the empty vector). In contrast, when the effector was cotransformed with proDLP1-mut-LUC, the LUC/REN ratio returned to that of the control. Additionally, compared to their individual injections, the co-injection of NaWRKY3 and NaWRKY6 did not enhance their transcriptional activity on NaDLP1.

Taken together, these results indicated that NaWRKY3/6 both functioned as transcriptional activators of NaDLP1 by binding to the W-box.

Discussion

Plant defensins are induced and isolated from a wide array of plant species during the course of pathogenic assault [27]. Deciphering the induction mechanisms of these defensins holds significant promise for the development and application of these antifungal peptides in agricultural settings. In N. attenuata, our prior research revealed that the NaDLP1 was upregulated following inoculation with A. alternata. Additionally, transient silencing of NaDLP1 in plants, accomplished through the VIGS approach, led to increased susceptibility of the plants to the pathogen [26].

In the present study, through in-depth analysis of RNA-seq and RT-qPCR data, we ascertained that NaDLP1 represented the defensin-like gene with the highest level of expression. Furthermore, we provided a detailed and elaborate account of the physicochemical properties and structural characteristics of the NaDLP1 protein. Prokaryotic expression of the NaDLP1 protein and in vitro antifungal experiments indicated it had a broad-spectrum antifungal property (as depicted in Figs. 1 and 2). Furthermore, stable silencing experiments of NaDLP1 in wild tobacco have once again confirmed that NaDLP1 played a pivotal role in the defense response against A. alternata (Fig. 3). To evaluate the off-target effects mediated by the NaDLP1 RNAi construct, we performed a homology alignment between the NaDLP1 RNAi fragments and two defensin genes (XM_019401036.1 and XM_019411243.1) from A. alternata those were weakly induced under certain conditions. The results indicated that NaDLP1 RNAi sequence had no significant homology with XM_019401036.1, but it exhibited high sequence similarity with XM_019411243.1 (Supplementary Fig. 4 A). Additionally, RT-qPCR analysis showed a non-significant reduction in the expression of XM_019411243.1 in both ir-dlp1 #1/2 lines, while the expression of XM_019401036.1 remained unchanged between WT and ir-dlp1 lines (Supplementary Fig. 4B). This suggests that the increased disease susceptibility phenotype observed in NaDLP1-RNAi plants was likely primarily due to the silencing of NaDLP1, rather than off-target effects.

It has been indicated that the transcription factor NaNAC29 and the JA signaling pathway were involved in the partial regulation of NaDLP1 expression. Additionally, NaNAC29 VIGS plants exhibited a slight reduction in the JA-biosynthetic gene NaLOX3 transcripts. This occurred despite the absence of interaction between NaNAC29 and the NaDLP1 promoter, as demonstrated by yeast one-hybrid assays [26]. In this study, we determined that the transcription factors NaWRKY3/6 were capable of directly regulating the expression of NaDLP1. Previous studies have reported that NaWRKY3/6 transcriptionally activated JA-biosynthetic genes, including LOX3, AOS, AOC, and OPR3, which were essential for JA accumulation following wounding and herbivory. Exogenous application of JA did not affect the transcripts of NaWRKY3/6, but it could complement WRKY-deficient phenotypes [16]. NaWRKY3 directly transactivated NaLOX3, thereby regulating the biosynthesis of JA during infection by A. alternata in N. attenuata [17]. Thus, we hypothesize that the regulation of NaDLP1 by NaWRKY3/6 occurs through two aspects: on one hand, they directly bind to the promoter of NaDLP1 to regulate the expression of this gene; on the other hand, they affect the biosynthesis of JA, thereby regulating the expression of NaDLP1.

Additionally, NaWRKY3 can engage in direct protein-protein interactions with NaWRKY6. Promoter activation assays have demonstrated that co-injection of the two transcription factors resulted in higher transactivation of NaERFB-L promoter compared to the use of either transcription factor alone [18]. However, in contrast to the individual injection of NaWRKY3 or NaWRKY6, the co-injection of these two WRKY transcription factors did not result in increased LUC activity initiated by the NaDLP1 promoter (as illustrated in Fig. 5). We hypothesize that this may be because the binding of NaWRKY3/6 to NaDLP1 is competitive rather than synergistic, or their synergistic regulation of NaDLP1 requires post-translational modifications or other cofactors. However, the further studies are needed to fully clarify the underlying mechanism. This observation implies that the expression regulatory mechanism is highly complex, with different genes manifesting distinct and unique expression patterns. Overall, our study substantially enriches and complements the existing body of knowledge regarding the regulation of NaDLP1 expression.

Conclusions

In this study, we revealed that the most highly induced defensin-like gene, NaDLP1, plays a crucial role in the defense against the tobacco brown spot disease pathogen A. alternata. In addition, the expression of NaDLP1 could be directly promoted by NaWRKY3/NaWRKY6.
