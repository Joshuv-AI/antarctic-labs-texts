Priority-Aware Shapley Value
Kiljae Lee, Ziqi Liu, Weijing Tang, Yuan Zhang

Abstract
Shapley values are widely used for model-agnostic data valuation and feature attribution, yet they implicitly assume contributors are interchangeable. This can be problematic when contributors are dependent (e.g., reused/augmented data or causal feature orderings) or when contributions should be adjusted by factors such as trust or risk. We propose Priority-Aware Shapley Value (PASV) , which incorporates both hard precedence constraints and soft, contributor-specific priority weights. PASV is applicable to general precedence structures, recovers precedence-only and weight-only Shapley variants as special cases, and is uniquely characterized by natural axioms. We develop an efficient adjacent-swap Metropolis–Hastings sampler for scalable Monte Carlo estimation and analyze limiting regimes induced by extreme priority weights. Experiments on data valuation (MNIST/CIFAR10) and feature attribution (Census Income) demonstrate more structure-faithful allocations and a practical sensitivity analysis via our proposed “priority sweeping”.

Recent advances in machine learning have fueled growing interest in

Existing Shapley variants typically address these two aspects in isolation.

To this end, we propose the
Priority-Aware Shapley Value (PASV)
, a single Shapley-type attribution method that simultaneously captures
(i)

(ii)
soft

PASV computes each player’s value by averaging its marginal contribution over precedence-feasible orders, while biasing the order distribution according to the specified priorities.
This unified view is designed to be both expressive (supporting general precedence constraints) and practically feasible (supporting scalable computation for large player sets).

Our main contributions are:

•

Unified formulation.
We introduce PASV, a Shapley-type value that simultaneously incorporates general precedence constraints and soft priority weights within one coherent random-order framework.

•

Theory and interpretability.
We provide an axiomatic characterization that singles out PASV as a canonical precedence-respecting, weight-aware rule, and we analyze limiting regimes that clarify how extreme priorities can modify the underlying precedence structure.

•

Scalable computation.

•

2.3
Soft Priority: Individual (Soft) Priority Weights

While the PSV is a very natural approach to enforce priority, it equally weights all valid linear extensions.
In practice, individual players may vary in their trustworthiness, cost/convenience of data collection, potential legal risk of data use, etc.
We are inclined to include trusted/low-risk data earlier in
\pi
.
Notice that this consideration cannot be captured by the priority relationship (i.e., the DAG).
Therefore,
16
and
26
introduced individual weights
\lambda:=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
,
\lambda_{i}>0,\forall i
and
Weighted Shapley Value (WSV)
.
WSV is defined for a particular class of DAGs, called
ordered partition
.

Ordered Partition.

Let
(B_{1},\dots,B_{m})
be a partition of
[n]
, i.e.,
B_{i}\cap B_{j}=\emptyset,\forall 1\leq i<j\leq m
and
\cup_{i=1}^{m}B_{i}=[n]
.
The DAG consists of edges
i\prec j
if and only if
i\in B_{r}
,
j\in B_{r+1}
for some
r\in[m-1]
.

In WSV, the permutation
\pi\in\Pi^{\preceq}
is sampled in a backward fashion.
To illustrate, suppose
B_{1}=\{1,2\},B_{2}=\{3,4,5\}
.
First sample
\pi_{5}
(the last element of
\pi
) from candidates

with probabilities
(\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4},\lambda_{5})/(\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{4}+\lambda_{5})
.
Suppose
4

\pi_{5}
, then select
\pi_{4}
among the remaining
3
and
5

(\lambda_{3},\lambda_{5})/(\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{5})
, and so on.
After finishing

, select
\pi_{2}
between
1
and
2
, which also decides
\pi_{1}
.

Remark 2.3
.

From this example, we see that
\lambda
acts as a
soft priority
parameter: a larger
\lambda_{i}
increases the probability that
i
appears later in
\pi
.
This is meaningful in real applications because attribution is inherently
context dependent
: the marginal gain of adding
i
can be very different when the coalition already contains other (more trusted, less risky, or more “original”) contributors.
Thus, increasing
\lambda_{i}

does not (imprudently) directly lower
i
’s payoff; instead it prefers to evaluate
i
under richer contexts
, which can reduce spurious early credit for noisy/duplicated/high-risk contributors while still allowing genuinely complementary contributors to remain valuable even when delayed in
\pi

To formalize this procedure, we define two concepts.

Definition 2.4

(Feasible set)
.

A subset
S\subseteq[n]
is

i\in S
and
j\preceq i
imply

.
Denote the collection of feasible sets by
\mathcal{S}^{\preceq}:=\{S\subseteq[n]:S\text{ is feasible}\}

From Definition

Definition 2.5

(Maximal elements)
.

For

, the set of

, denoted by
\max_{\preceq}(S)
, is defined as

\max\nolimits_{\preceq}(S):=\{i\in S:\nexists j\in S\text{ s.t. }i\prec j\}.

Weighted Shapley Value (WSV)

(
16
;
26
)
.
Write
\pi=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n})\in\Pi^{\preceq}
and
S_{t}:=\{\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{t}\}
, define

p_{\mathrm{WSV}}(\pi)=\prod_{t=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{\pi_{t}}}{\sum_{j\in\max_{\preceq}(S_{t})}\lambda_{j}}.

(3)

While WSV addresses the limitation of PSV, it only applies to special DAGs (ordered partitions), limiting its practicality.

Our Method

3.1
Desired Properties

Section
2
reviewed mainstream existing methods.
They either (i) treat all linear extensions uniformly (PSV), capturing hard priority but no individual weighting; or (ii) incorporate weights under the strong structural assumption of ordered partitions (WSV).
Our goal is to design a general approach that combines their strengths while avoiding their limitations.
Specifically, we look for a
p^{(\preceq,\lambda)}
that satisfies the following desiderata:

1.

Hard priority:

p^{(\preceq,\lambda)}
is supported on
\Pi^{\preceq}
.

2.

Soft priority:
when
i,j
are both admissible, their selection probabilities should have ratio
\lambda_{i}:\lambda_{j}
.

3.

If
\lambda_{i}\equiv
constant,
p^{(\preceq,\lambda)}
reduces to PSV.

4.

If DAG is an ordered partition,
p^{(\preceq,\lambda)}
reduces to WSV.

5.

p^{(\preceq,\lambda)}
should be computationally feasible.

3.2
Priority-Aware Shapley Value (PASV)

Now we propose a
p^{(\preceq,\lambda)}
that simultaneously captures hard precedence and player-specific weights.
Our method is called
Priority-Aware Shapley Value (PASV)
.

p^{(\preceq,\lambda)}(\pi)\propto\prod_{t=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{\pi_{t}}|\max_{\preceq}(S_{t})|}{\sum_{k\in\max_{\preceq}(S_{t})}\lambda_{k}},\quad\text{ for }\pi\in\Pi^{\preceq}.

(4)

From (
4
), we can see how PASV combines the hard and soft priorities – while restricting
\pi
to
\Pi^{\preceq}
enforces the hard priority, the soft priorities of admissible candidates for each
\pi_{t}
is adjusted by
\lambda_{i}
’s, thus reflecting the soft priority.
When
\lambda_{i}
’s are all equal, (
4
) becomes
p^{(\preceq,\lambda)}(\pi)\propto 1
, and PASV recovers PSV.
When the DAG is an ordered partition, it can be shown that

p^{(\preceq,\lambda)}(\pi)=\prod_{t=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{\pi_{t}}}{\sum_{k\in\max_{\preceq}(S_{t})}\lambda_{k}}
,
thus recovering WSV.
Formally, we have the following results.

Proposition 3.1

(Reduction to PSV)
.

If
\lambda_{1}=\cdots=\lambda_{n}
, then

p^{(\preceq,\lambda)}(\pi)=p_{\mathrm{PSV}}(\pi),\forall\pi\in\Pi^{\preceq}.

Proposition 3.2

(Reduction to WSV)
.

If the DAG is an ordered partition, then

p^{(\preceq,\lambda)}(\pi)=p_{\mathrm{WSV}}(\pi),\forall\pi\in\Pi^{\preceq}.

Remark 3.3
.

When developing a general
p^{(\preceq,\lambda)}
, one may be tempted to directly apply the formulation of WSV (
3
) to general DAGs, but the resulting distribution does not reduce to PSV for equal weights.
The
|\max_{\preceq}(S_{t})|
factor on the numerator of (
4
) is crucial for ensuring Proposition
3.1
.

PASV can also be efficiently computed.
We relegate the algorithm details to the dedicated Section
4
.

3.3.1
Precedence Random Order Value (PROV)

Recall that the classical Shapley value can be rewritten as a random order value (ROV).
In presence of priority, we should of course constrain our consideration to
p
distributions supported not on all of
\Pi
, but on the subset
\Pi^{\preceq}\subseteq\Pi
that respects priority.

Definition 3.4

(Precedence random order value (PROV))
.

A value
\psi
is called a

precedence random order value (PROV)
if there exists some
p
supported on
\Pi^{\preceq}
, s.t.

\psi_{i}(U)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim p}\big[U(\pi^{i}\cup\{i\})-U(\pi^{i})\big]

for all
U
and

.

While it may be natural to treat PROV as a basic assumption or an axiom, we will show that in fact PROV can be derived from five more basic axioms.
These axioms include three Shapley axioms: Efficiency (E), Linearity (L) and Null player (NP) (see Appendix
A
); plus two more axioms: Monotonicity (M) and Maximal support (MS).

Axiom 3.5

(Monotonicity)
.

If
U
is
monotone
, i.e.,
S\subseteq T\Rightarrow U(S)\leq U(T)
for all
S,T\subseteq[n]
, then every player receives a nonnegative payoff:
\psi_{i}(U)\geq 0
for all
i\in[n]
.

32
showed that axioms E + L + NP + M
\Rightarrow
ROV.
Since PROV is a special ROV, it naturally inherits these axioms.
To present axiom MS, we first define
elementary game
– it has long been used for analyzing Shapley’s values.

Definition 3.6

(Elementary game)
.

For a given subset
T\subseteq[n]
, the
elementary game

u_{T}\in\Gamma
on
T
is defined by

u_{T}(S):=\mathbbm{1}\{T\subseteq S\}
for
S\subseteq[n]
.

We focus on elementary games for feasible
T
, i.e.,
T\in\mathcal{S}^{\preceq}
(recall Definition
2.4
).
The precedence relation singles out the
maximal
elements of

as sole the recipients of credit.
This motivates the following axiom.

Axiom 3.7

(Maximal support)
.

For any elementary game
u_{T}
with
T\in\mathcal{S}^{\preceq}
, if player
i
is not maximal in
T
, i.e.,
i\notin\max_{\preceq}(T)
, then
\psi_{i}(u_{T})=0
.

Theorem 3.8

(Axiomatization of PROV)
.

A value
\psi
is PROV
\Leftrightarrow

3.3.2
State-Choice Factorization (SCF)

The PROV as defined in Definition
3.4
is a huge family, and using the most general PROV directly is computationally challenging.
We therefore restrict attention to a broad and structured family of precedence-respecting order distributions that factorize along the chain of feasible sets induced by a linear extension.
Conceptually, this is the precedence-constrained analogue of stage-wise models for rankings and permutations, such as the Plackett–Luce
(
23
;
27
)
and multistage ranking families
(
6
)
.
To this end, we consider the family of distributions on
\Pi^{\preceq}
of the form

\displaystyle p(\pi)\propto

\displaystyle~\prod_{t=1}^{n}w_{\preceq,\lambda}(\pi_{t};S_{t}),\quad\text{for }\pi\in\Pi^{\preceq}

(5)

for some
w_{\preceq,\lambda}
.
This form (
5
) is the precedence-constrained analogue of Plackett-Luce: the likelihood of some
\pi
depends on
\pi
only through the sequence of feasible candidates
\{S_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}
, and the chosen candidate at this time.
It is often convenient to decompose
w
as a
scale
(“state”):

s_{\preceq}(S_{t}):=\sum_{k\in\max_{\preceq}(S_{t})}w_{\preceq,\lambda}(k;S_{t})
,
where we shall further assume that
s_{\preceq}(S_{t})
does not depend on
\lambda
, which is true for WSV;
and a
normalized priority weighting
(“choice”):

c_{\preceq,\lambda}(i;S_{t}):={w_{\preceq,\lambda}(i;S_{t})}\big/{s_{\preceq}(S_{t})}.

Definition 3.9

(State–Choice Factorization (SCF))
.

For a poset
([n],\preceq)
, define the prefix (remaining) set
S_{t}=\{\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{t}\}
as a chain of feasible sets (
t=n,n-1,\ldots,1
).
A
state-choice factorization (SCF)
assigns an unnormalized weight to
\pi
by multiplying:

(i)

a
state factor

s_{\preceq}(S_{t})
that
captures global bias induced by the precedence structure;

(ii)

a
choice factor

c_{\preceq,\lambda}(\,\cdot\,;S)
that encodes soft priority weighting of admissible candidates
\max_{\preceq}(S)
; it is the entry point for the individual weights
\lambda
.

Formally, an SCF
p
has the following form

p(\pi)\propto\prod_{t=1}^{n}s_{\preceq}(S_{t})\,c_{\preceq,\lambda}(\pi_{t};S_{t}),\quad\text{ for }\pi\in\Pi^{\preceq},

(6)

where
\sum_{i\in\max_{\preceq}(S_{t})}c_{\preceq,\lambda}(i;S_{t})=1
.

The merits of SCF are three-fold:

•

It remains flexible enough to capture a broad range of precedence-respecting random-order models, containing PSV and WSV as special cases.

•

It preserves a clear interpretation of
\lambda
weights as a soft priority parameter;

•

Its factorization form enables efficient MCMC sampling procedures for generating
\pi
.

3.3.3
Boundary case axioms

We desire our method to contain PSV and WSV as special cases.
This is characterized by the following two axioms.

Axiom 3.10

(Weight Proportionality)
.

For every feasible set

and every

,
\frac{c_{\preceq,\lambda}(i;S)}{c_{\preceq,\lambda}(j;S)}=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{j}}
.

Axiom WP requires that at each step the probability of choosing each admissible candidate

\lambda_{i}
.

Axiom 3.11

(Equal-Weight Uniformity)

If
\lambda_{i}\equiv c
for all

, then the induced distribution is uniform on
\Pi^{\preceq}
.

Axiom
3.10

Axiomatization of PASV

Theorem 3.12
.

Suppose

is a PROV as in Definition

satisfying the SCF form as in (
6
).
Then the only

The goal is to clarify how the

\displaystyle c_{\preceq,\lambda}(i;S)=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}+\sum_{k\in\max_{\preceq}(S)\setminus\{i\}}\lambda_{k}}.

6
Conclusion

We introduce Priority-Aware Shapley Value (PASV), a unified Shapley-based attribution method that simultaneously enforces hard precedence constraints and incorporates soft priority weights.
PASV recovers precedence-only and weight-only Shapley variants as special cases, admits scalable MCMC estimation for general DAGs, and has a solid axiomatization basis.
An important tool that PASV provides practitioners is a sensitivity/robustness diagnostic procedure via
priority sweeping
.

Appendix A
Standard Axioms of the Shapley Value

We briefly review the standard axiomatic framework leading to the Shapley value.
Throughout this appendix we fix the player set
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
and assume
U(\emptyset)=0
without loss of generality, since replacing
U
by
U^{\prime}(S):=U(S)-U(\emptyset)
leaves every marginal contribution
U(S\cup\{i\})-U(S)
unchanged.
A
value
is a mapping
\psi
that assigns to each utility function
U
a payoff vector
\psi(U)=(\psi_{i}(U))_{i\in[n]}
.
The Shapley value
\psi^{\text{SV}}
is characterized as the unique value satisfying the following four standard axioms
(
28
)
.

Axiom A.1

(Efficiency; E)
.

For a given utility function
U
,

\sum_{i\in[n]}\psi_{i}(U)=U([n]).

Axiom A.2

(Linearity; L)
.

For given two utility functions
U,V
and all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,

\psi(\alpha U+\beta V)=\alpha\psi(U)+\beta\psi(V).

Axiom A.3

(Null Player; NP)
.

If player
i
satisfies
U(S\cup\{i\})=U(S)
for all
S\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}
,
then

\psi_{i}(U)=0.

Axiom A.4

(Symmetry; S)
.

If two players
i
and
j
satisfy

U(S\cup\{i\})=U(S\cup\{j\})
for all
S\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i,j\}
,
then

\psi_{i}(U)=\psi_{j}(U).

Efficiency requires that the entire value
U([n])
be fully allocated among players.
Linearity ensures that payoffs respect additive changes in utility.
That is, if a game is formed by combining (possibly with weights) two utilities, then the assigned payoffs combine in the same way.
Null player formalizes a fairness requirement in the sense of that a player who never changes the utility receives zero payoff.
Symmetry reflects anonymity by requiring identical treatment of players who are interchangeable in terms of their marginal contributions.

B.2.1
On-Manifold Utility with Conditional
k
-NN Imputation

Feature attribution evaluates how a fixed trained predictor changes as features are revealed, so we define the utility of a feature subset through a masking-based prediction score.
Let
f_{y}(x)
denote the predicted probability assigned to class
y
at input
x
.

For a subset of feature indices
S\subseteq[n]
and a labeled test point
(x,y)
, the utility is computed by keeping the observed coordinates
x_{S}
fixed and imputing the remaining coordinates
x_{S^{c}}
from a conditional reference distribution rather than from the marginal
(
14
;
30
;
7
)
.
This on-manifold construction is used to avoid implausible masked inputs that can arise under marginal imputation when features are dependent.

Concretely, we approximate the conditional distribution of the unobserved coordinates given the observed ones,
p(x_{S^{c}}|x_{S})
, using
k
-NN sampling in the subspace restricted to coordinates in
S
.
Given a test point
(x,y)
and
S
, let
\{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(k)}\}
denote the
k
nearest neighbors of
x
among training inputs when distances are computed using only the coordinates in
S
.
For each neighbor
x^{(j)}
, we form a composite input
x_{S}\oplus x^{(j)}_{S^{c}}
, which keeps the coordinates in
S
from
x
and fills the remaining coordinates from
x^{(j)}
.
The per-point utility is defined as the conditional expectation

u_{S}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}_{S^{c}}\sim p(x^{\prime}_{S^{c}}|x_{S})}\big[f_{y}(x_{S}\oplus x^{\prime}_{S^{c}})\big],

and is estimated using the
k
-NN conditional sampler by

\widehat{u}_{S}(x,y)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}f_{y}(x_{S}\oplus x^{(j)}_{S^{c}}).

Finally, following the global aggregation used in Shapley-based feature attribution, we define the subset utility as

U(S)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim p(x,y)}\big[u_{S}(x,y)\big].

In implementation, we estimate
U(S)
by Monte Carlo using
n_{\text{eval}}
test points
(x^{(t)},y^{(t)})
sampled uniformly from the test split and the
k
-NN estimator
\widehat{u}_{S}(x,y)
, i.e.,

\widehat{U}(S)=\frac{1}{n_{\text{eval}}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{\text{eval}}}\widehat{u}_{S}\big(x^{(t)},y^{(t)}\big).

Implementation details of the utility evaluation procedure are provided in Algorithm
2
.

Algorithm 2

k
NN imputation.

Input:
training set
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}=\{(x^{\prime}_{i},y^{\prime}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n_{\text{train}}}
; test set
\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n_{\text{test}}}
; trained predictor
f
; subset
S\subseteq[n]
; number of neighbors
k
; number of evaluation points
n_{\text{eval}}
.

Initialize

\widehat{U}(S)\leftarrow 0

for

t=1

to

n_{\text{eval}}

do

Sample
(x,y)
uniformly from
\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}

Find
k
nearest neighbors
\{x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(k)}\}
of
x
among
\{x^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{\text{train}}}
restricted to coordinates in
S

Initialize

\widehat{u}\leftarrow 0

for

j=1

to

k

do

Form composite input
\widetilde{x}^{(j)}\leftarrow x_{S}\oplus x^{(j)}_{S^{c}}

\widehat{u}\leftarrow\widehat{u}+f_{y}(\widetilde{x}^{(j)})

end

for

\widehat{U}(S)\leftarrow\widehat{U}(S)+\widehat{u}/k

end

return

.

Additional Results

Sweep Diagostics.

As shown in Figure
12
(and in the right panel of Figure
7
), we notice that varying the priority weights to promote or delay a given feature does not necessarily induce a monotone change in its estimated value.
Motivated by these non-monotone responses, we conduct a diagnostic analysis and focus on a representative case that exhibits a obvious U-shaped pattern under the sweep,
capital-gain
.
First, based on the general DAG in Figure
6(b)
, we can anticipate what happens in each limiting case.
Clearly, since
capital-gain
is maximal in the full player set, in the
\lambda\to\infty
scenario it is placed at the very end of the linear extension with probability one.
On the other hand, in the
\lambda\to 0^{+}
case,
capital-gain
is promoted as much as possible until it becomes the unique element of the maximal set. In particular, in the perspective of the backward sampling scheme, {
age
,
sex
,
native-country
,
race
,
education
,
occupation
} (and only these) always appear before it, so under the limiting distribution
capital-gain
occupies seventh position with probability one (which is also consistent with the rank pattern in Figure
13
).
Meanwhile, we observed the following interesting relationship among the three players
capital-gain
,
capital-loss
, and
marital-status
:

1.

capital-gain
attains a
larger
marginal contribution when
capital-loss
has already been included in the model.

2.

capital-gain
attains a
smaller
marginal contribution when
marital-status
has already been included in the model.

To support this, Table
3
partitions sampled linear extensions by the relative ordering among
capital-gain
,
capital-loss
, and
marital-status
, and reports the corresponding mean marginal contribution of
capital-gain
.
The table shows a dependence among three features.
When
marital-status
precedes
capital-gain
while
capital-loss
remains absent, the marginal contribution of
capital-gain
is the smallest, and this happens frequently.
In contrast, when
capital-loss
precedes
capital-gain
and
marital-status
is still absent,
capital-gain
achieves the largest marginal contribution, although it occurs rarely.
The mixed case, where
capital-gain
appears after both
marital-status
and
capital-loss
(as in
\lambda\to\infty
) or before both (as in
\lambda\to 0^{+}
) yield intermediate marginal contributions.
Noting that the weight average of the marginal contribution in Table
3
is a value of
capital-gain
under
\lambda=1
, this clarifies the U-shape behavior of the sweeping result.
In summary,
capital-loss
acts as an amplifying context for
capital-gain
, whereas
marital-status
acts as a suppressing context, and the priority sweep modulates the value of
capital-gain
value primarily through shifting the frequencies of these contexts.
