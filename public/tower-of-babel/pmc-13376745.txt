UAV-based multispectral imaging and machine learning for detecting and mapping maize leaf diseases in smallholder farms
Authors: Basani Lammy Nkuna, Wonga Masiza, Johannes George Chirima, Solomon W. Newete, Adriaan Johannes Van Der Walt, Adolph Nyamugama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Maize (Zea Mays) is one of the world’s most important staple crops, providing food for humans and feed for livestock. However, its production is threatened by a range of stresses, including crop diseases, which significantly reduce yields, particularly in smallholder farming systems. Traditional disease detection methods, such as visual inspection, are often labour-intensive, subjective, and prone to error, leading to delayed interventions and widespread crop losses. This study uses unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) remote sensing and machine learning (ML) to investigate the feasibility of detecting maize leaf diseases in a smallholder farm located in the Mopani District of Limpopo Province, South Africa. UAV-derived vegetation indices including NDVI, GNDVI, and NDRE were combined with UAV multispectral bands and the three ML algorithms, namely – support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), to first distinguish healthy from diseased plants and then to classify specific maize diseases. The SVM algorithm achieved the highest accuracy in both, distinguishing healthy and diseased crops from other land cover classes (91.73%) and in distinguishing specific diseases (89.41%). Among the diseases identified, Southern Corn Leaf Blight was classified with the highest user’s accuracy, while phosphorus deficiency had the lowest user’s classification accuracy. The results demonstrate the potential of integrating UAV-based multispectral imaging and ML for precision agriculture by providing timely, spatially detailed disease information that enables targeted management practices, reducing crop losses and enhancing food security for smallholder farmers.

Introduction

Maize (Zea Mays) is one of the most widely cultivated crops and serves as a vital source of food for humans and feed for livestock. It ranks among the world’s major cereal crops and plays a particularly important role in food security in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where it contributes substantially to daily caloric intake 1. With continued population growth and rising food demand, improving maize production remains a major agricultural priority 2. However, maize production is constrained by a range of biotic and abiotic stresses, including diseases, pests, and climatic variability, all of which contribute to yield loss and production instability.

Crop diseases are among the leading constraints to maize production, causing substantial economic and yield losses, especially in smallholder farming systems where access to timely monitoring and control is limited. Common maize diseases, such as Northern Corn Leaf Blight (Exserohilum turcicum), Southern Corn Leaf Blight (Bipolaris maydis), maize streak virus can severely reduce productivity, while delayed detection often results in rapid disease spread and costly interventions3,4. In many smallholder systems, disease detection still relies on visual inspection by farmers or extension officers, a process that is labour-intensive, subjective, and prone to error. These limitations create a need for more timely, objective, and spatially explicit approaches to maize disease monitoring.

Advances in remote sensing have created new opportunities for crop disease detection and management. Satellite platforms such as Landsat, Sentinel, and MODIS have been widely used for agricultural monitoring5,6. However, their relatively coarse spatial resolution limits their effectiveness in smallholder farming systems, where fields are often small and heterogeneous7. A study by Ahmad et al.8 shows that models trained under controlled conditions often struggle to generalize to real-world field environments due to variations in illumination, background complexity, occlusion, and plant growth stages. This limitation highlights the need for approaches that are robust under heterogeneous field conditions, particularly in smallholder farming systems.

Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) offer an important alternative because they provide very high-resolution imagery, flexible acquisition timing, and suitability for localized, sub-field scale applications9,10. These characteristics make UAVs particularly attractive for crop health monitoring in fragmented. Interestingly, the altitude used for UAV flight in detecting crop diseases ranges between 20 and 30 m above the ground based on the analysis approach, where 20 m is mostly considered when applying deep learning approach and 30 m in traditional machine learning11.

The integration of UAV-derived imagery with machine-learning (ML) algorithms has shown strong potential for crop health monitoring and disease detection. ML algorithms such as Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) can identify subtle spectral and spatial patterns associated with plant stress and disease symptoms12,13. Previous studies have reported promising results in UAV-based disease detection in crops such as wheat14,15. In maize, Stewart et al.16 demonstrated the usefulness of UAV imagery and deep learning for northern leaf blight phenotyping, Chivasa et al.,17 showed that UAV-based multispectral data can capture maize streak virus responses, and Jia et al.18 used multi-source UAV imagery to monitor maize leaf spot diseases. More recently, Gao et al.19 showed that UAV-based data can support multi-class disease classification in maize. Together, these studies show the growing value of UAV-based approaches for maize disease monitoring, although most have focused on single diseases, experimental conditions, or broader classification settings rather than multiple disease and stress classes in smallholder fields.

Despite these advances, there is still limited research on the UAV-based detection and mapping of multiple maize diseases and stress classes under heterogenous smallholder field conditions. This study addresses this gap by integrating UAV multispectral imagery with ML algorithms to detect and classify maize diseases and stress conditions in Mopani District, Limpopo Province, South Africa. The study used three vegetation indices, namely the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), Green Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (GNDVI), and Normalized Difference Red Edge Index (NDRE), together with UAV multispectral bands to support disease discrimination. The objectives of the study were to (1) classify maize crop health conditions by distinguishing healthy from diseased maize and background classes, and (2) identify and map specific maize diseases and stress classes within a smallholder field. By addressing these objectives, this study contributes to the advancement of precision agriculture by providing a framework for real-time, localized disease monitoring, supporting informed management decisions, and ultimately improving food security for smallholder farmers.

Methods

Study site

The research was conducted in a subsistence maize farm of 0.8 ha situated at 23° 24’ 59.99” S 30° 44’ 59.99” E. The climate of the study area is mainly subtropical with warm temperatures in summer and dry conditions in winter. The Mopani District receives average rainfall of 500 mm over the maize growing period, which spans from October to March . Sometimes, the fields are planted throughout the year with the aid of irrigation water sourced from rivers, dams and boreholes. This study area was chosen due to its widely cultivated maize being a key commodity contributing towards livelihoods and food security. Thus, the farm is a representative of the typical farming conditions in the area (Fig. 1).

Unmanned aerial vehicles data acquisition

The remote sensing data were acquired with an M600 Pro Matrix UAV (SZ DJI Technology Co., Ltd., Guangdong, China, 2016) equipped with Mica-sense red-edge multispectral camera (MicaSense Inc., Seattle, Washington, 2015). The camera consists of five multispectral bands namely 450 nm (blue), 560 nm (Green), 650 nm (red), 730 nm (red edge), to 840 nm (NIR). The data were acquired during the tasselling stage on 03 May 2023 on a sunny day between 11:00 am and 12:00 pm GMT (around solar noon). The flight height was 30 m above the relatively flat study site, maintaining the same altitude and producing pixel size of 2 cm in real-world dimensions.

Image data pre-processing

The acquired images were orthorectified and mosaicked using the drone to map software (D2M). Vegetation indices including NDVI, GNDVI and NDRE were computed using the R programming language These vegetation indices were selected because of their sensitivity to biophysical and biochemical properties of maize20. The image bands and the computed indices were then combined (stacked) and further used as input data in supervised image classification (Table 1).

Field data collection

Field data were collected during tasselling stage of maize growth, which is optimal for disease detection24. Crop conditions were visually inspected by research team members with expertise in crop disease identification. Diseased and healthy crops were identified and marked. Ground truth data used for image classification, including GPS locations, were collected for six land information classes, namely: soil, healthy, diseased, dry maize residues, shadow and weeds (Table 2).

Seventy (70) diseased maize leaves were randomly sampled and categorised into seven different classes based on their similarities, resulting in 10 images collected for each leaf category. The sampled leaves were detached from the plant with petioles removed and placed in a bag, then sent to the ARC’s analytical laboratory for disease identification. A Garmin Montana 600 (© 2011–2014 Garmin Ltd., lathe, Kansas, USA) global positioning system (GPS) was used to record geolocations of the sampled leaves with 1 m accuracy. To match the UAV spatial resolution of 2 cm with the recoded location, 2 m buffer were created. Buffered regions corresponding to the observed field condition were visually verified and used to extract training pixels. The identified leaf conditions and diseases included southern corn leaf blight, northern corn leaf blight, maize streak disease, Phosphorus deficiency and healthy leaves (Table 3).

For both datasets (Tables 2 and 3), data labelling was carried out using ArcMap 10.6 utilizing reference points from both field observation and the laboratory results (Fig. 2). For both training and testing datasets, polygons were created to represent each class based on the homogeneity of the pixel colour and the reference points corresponding to specific classes. This study used balanced and imbalanced data. To address the imbalance in the reference points among the classes, stratified sampling during the train–test split was applied to ensure that each class was proportionally represented in both subsets. A total 725 labelled samples were used. 580 samples were used for training the models (80%), while the remaining 145 samples were reserved for testing (20%).

Image analysis

The applied image analysis procedures included (1) systematic compilation of predictor variables from the multispectral bands and vegetation indices, (2) classification of the UAV image into six land information classes using with RF, SVM and XGBoost, and (3) classification of the UAV leaf image pixels into different conditions and diseases using RF, SVM and XGBoost. The first set of classification experiments was applied to detect diseased crop leaves. Then, the second set was applied to identify specific maize leaf diseases.

Variable importance

To identify important input variables, variable importance measures were applied during model training (RF, SVM and XGBoost). The variable importance rankings were computed with the package CARET and the VarImp function in Posit using the R-Language25. This function is a generic method for calculating the variable importance of the input variable. The function automatically scales the importance scores between 0 and 100.

Image classification algorithms

Image classification models were developed using three algorithms, RF26, SVM27 and XGBoost28 implemented in the R-Language in Posit (R Core Team, 2023). The classification was carried out in two stages, namely: (1) classes of maize health conditions and (2) classes of maize diseases. In the first stage, experiments were conducted to classify the land cover and the crop conditions, and the second part was an attempt to identify different diseases. The performance of these classifiers was evaluated using the user’s accuracy and the producer’s accuracy, overall accuracy and Kappa.

Model parameters

The Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) models were trained using 17,914 samples with 8 predictors. For all three models, the data were pre-processed through centering and scaling, and resampling was performed using 10-fold cross-validation. The RF model was tuned with different mtry values (2, 5, and 8), achieving the highest performance at mtry = 5. The SVM model with a radial basis function kernel was evaluated with different cost values (C = 0.25, 0.50, and 1.00), with the best results obtained at C = 1 and sigma = 0.179. For the XGBoost model, the parameters selected were nrounds = 150, max_depth = 3, eta = 0.4, gamma = 0, colsample_bytree = 0.8, min_child_weight = 1, and subsample = 1, based on maximizing kappa values.

The top five features identified by the RF model were NDVI, Blue, Red Edge, NIR and NDRE. For SVM, the top five features were NDVI, Red, Red Edge, Red and Green, while for XGBoost, they were NDVI, NIR, Red Edge, Red and Blue. The GNDVI was ranked as the least important among the input variables (Fig. 3).

Results of identifying and discriminating different leaf diseases from healthy leaves

XGBoost, while effective, had a marginally lower OA of 88.06% and a Kappa of 0.85, its PA for the healthy was 91.8% and for SCLB classes and 99.21, though the PA for the PD dropped to 70.21%.

Discussion

This study sought to (1) classify maize crop health conditions by distinguishing healthy leaves from diseased maize, and (2) identify and map specific maize diseases in a smallholder field. For the first objective, the three classifiers produced very similar results, with overall accuracies ranging from 91.28% to 91.73%, which indicates that RF, SVM, and XGBoost were comparably effective for separating diseased and healthy maize from dry maize residues, shadows, soil, and weeds. In practical terms, this suggests that UAV multispectral imagery provided enough spatial and spectral detail to distinguish crop health classes from surrounding background and non-crop elements under heterogeneous field conditions, rather than only under simplified experimental settings. The slightly higher performance of SVM suggests a modest advantage in handling class boundaries within this spectrally complex scene, but the narrow differences among the three models show that no algorithm had a decisive advantage for the first-stage classification task. While this study demonstrates the effectiveness of traditional machine learning models (RF, SVM, and XGBoost) for maize disease detection, the results also point toward the potential benefits of integrating lightweight deep learning approaches for real-time applications. Recent work on lightweight CNN models deployed on edge devices suggests that high accuracy can be achieved alongside computational efficiency, enabling near real-time detection and segmentation of crop conditions directly in the field . Incorporating such models into UAV-based systems could reduce processing delays associated with cloud-based workflows and enhance the practical usability of disease detection frameworks for smallholder farmers. The variable-importance results suggest that NDVI and red-edge-sensitive predictors were among the most informative variables, supporting the value of canopy greenness and chlorophyll-sensitive information for maize stress discrimination. NDVI is widely used as an indicator of canopy greenness and vigour, whereas red-edge-based metrics such as NDRE are particularly sensitive to chlorophyll-related stress responses and often remain informative where red-based vegetation indices begin to saturate in denser canopies. Together, these predictors are well suited to capturing spectral responses associated with declining maize health29.

The findings broadly align with earlier UAV-based maize disease studies, although the comparisons must be made carefully. For example, Jia et al.18 used UAV multispectral and thermal imagery to monitor maize leaf spot disease and compared RF, SVM, and XGBoost, likewise showing that these machine-learning approaches can effectively discriminate maize disease conditions from UAV data. A study by Stewart et al.16 also demonstrated the usefulness of UAV imagery for maize northern leaf blight phenotyping, while Chivasa et al.17 showed that UAV-based multispectral data can capture maize streak virus responses in field-grown maize. However, these studies differed from the present one in disease targets, modelling frameworks, and experimental context. The present study additionally required separation of diseased and healthy maize from weeds, soil, residues, and shadow within a smallholder field, making the classification problem more representative of operational field conditions. This is particularly important because plant disease detection models developed under controlled or laboratory conditions often exhibit reduced performance when applied to field environments, where variability in lighting, canopy structure, and background noise is significantly higher8. The high classification accuracies achieved in this study therefore suggest that UAV-based multispectral data combined with machine learning can provide more robust and generalizable solutions for real-world agricultural applications.

For the second objective, the study moved beyond general stress detection and evaluated whether UAV multispectral data could discriminate among several specific maize disease and stress classes, namely Southern Corn Leaf Blight, Northern Corn Leaf Blight, maize streak, phosphorus deficiency, and healthy leaves. This is a more complex classification problem than simply separating healthy from diseased plants, particularly because some of these conditions may produce overlapping spectral responses. Even so, all three classifiers achieved high overall accuracies (88.06–89.41%), with SVM performing slightly better than RF and XGBoost, indicating that UAV multispectral data retained meaningful discriminatory power across multiple maize disease classes. Among the classes, Southern Corn Leaf Blight was identified most consistently, whereas phosphorus deficiency was the most difficult to separate, likely because abiotic nutrient stress and disease can induce partly similar changes in pigmentation and canopy condition30–32. This suggests that not all maize stressors are equally separable using multispectral UAV data alone.

A closely related study by Jia et al.18 used UAV multispectral and thermal imagery to monitor two maize leaf spot diseases and likewise showed that machine-learning classifiers could distinguish disease conditions effectively once symptoms had developed. Similarly, Gao et al.19 demonstrated that UAV-based data can support multi-class disease classification in maize, reporting high accuracies for discriminating Northern Leaf Blight, Common Rust, Gray Leaf Spot, Blight, and healthy maize using deep learning models. However, comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because those studies used different modelling frameworks, larger image datasets, and disease categories that did not include abiotic stress. Other methods such as UAV-based multispectral data with deep learning approaches has demonstrated that incorporating spatial context and pixel-level classification improves vegetation discrimination in heterogeneous landscapes. Singh et al.33 applied deep learning-based segmentation approaches in complex environments such as roadside vegetation, where spectral overlap is common. Such findings can complement traditional machine learning classifiers used in this study, potentially improving the separation of maize disease and stress classes under smallholder field conditions.

While recent studies highlight the growing dominance of deep learning approaches in UAV-based crop disease detection due to their ability to automatically learn hierarchical features, this study demonstrates that traditional machine-learning models remain highly competitive, particularly in data-constrained smallholder farming systems where large labelled datasets required for deep learning are often unavailable . This suggests that ML approaches still provide a practical and computationally efficient alternative for operational deployment in resource-limited environments. By contrast, the present study classified both biotic and abiotic maize stress conditions under smallholder field conditions using UAV multispectral bands and vegetation indices with RF, SVM, and XGBoost. In this respect, the current study extends the literature toward an operational smallholder setting, where multiple stressors may co-occur and are not equally easy to separate. These findings should, however, be interpreted within the scope of a single-field, single-date acquisition, while broader validation across seasons, growth stages, and farming contexts is still needed.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that UAV-based multispectral imagery combined with machine-learning classifiers can effectively detect and map maize disease and stress conditions in a smallholder field in Mopani District, South Africa. RF, SVM, and XGBoost all achieved high accuracies in distinguishing healthy and diseased maize from background classes, while also showing strong potential for separating specific disease classes such as Southern Corn Leaf Blight, Northern Corn Leaf Blight, maize streak, phosphorus deficiency, and healthy maize. The results highlight the value of UAV multispectral data for sub-field crop health monitoring and targeted disease management in heterogeneous smallholder systems. However, because the study was based on a single-field, single-date acquisition, further validation across seasons, growth stages, and farming conditions is needed. Future work should also assess whether multi-temporal, thermal, or hyperspectral data can improve the separation of spectrally similar disease and nutrient-stress classes. Overall, the study shows that UAV-based remote sensing can contribute meaningfully to precision agriculture by supporting earlier detection, localized intervention, and improved crop management decisions.
