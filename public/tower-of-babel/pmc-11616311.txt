Study on the age-period-cohort effects of cognitive abilities among older Chinese adults based on the cognitive reserve hypothesis
Authors: Qian Yang, Tong Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive abilities serves as a critical indicator of healthy aging. As China progresses into a stage of advanced population aging, there has been a significant increase in the number of elderly individuals experiencing age-related cognitive decline. Despite this demographic shift, there is a paucity of longitudinal research examining cognitive abilities among older Chinese adults over extended time periods. This study aims to investigate changes in cognitive abilities and explore group differences among older Chinese adults aged 65 to 110 years, employing a multidimensional temporal approach that encompasses age, period, and birth cohort effects.MethodsThis study utilizes data from eight waves of the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), spanning from 1998 to 2018. The dataset comprises 94,116 observations from 36,157 unique participants. Cognitive abilities are assessed using Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores as a proxy measure. To address the issue of perfect collinearity in the temporal dimension, the study employs the Hierarchical Age-Period-Cohort Cross-Classified Random Effects Model (HAPC-CCREM). This model allows for the examination of age effects, period effects, and cohort effects on cognitive abilities among older Chinese adults. In the model specification, age is treated as a fixed effect, while period and birth cohort are incorporated as random effects. Drawing upon the cognitive reserve hypothesis, the study investigates significant factors influencing cognitive abilities in this population.ResultsAt the fixed-effect level, demographic factors, health behaviors, self-rated health, subjective well-being, and childhood adversity significantly influence cognitive abilities among older Chinese adults. The age effects are significant, with cognitive abilities exhibiting an inverted U-shaped curve across the lifespan. At the random-effect level, period effects are significant, revealing a gradual annual increase in overall cognitive levels among older Chinese adults since 2008. Cohort effects are also significant, demonstrating an increasing trend in overall cognitive levels for the earlier-born cohorts in the first six groups. Conversely, later-born cohorts in the latter five groups show a declining trend in overall cognitive levels. Notably, period effects significantly enhance cohort effects.ConclusionsThe cognitive reserve hypothesis supports the significance of the majority of identified influencing factors. Cognitive abilities demonstrate an accelerating decline with increasing age, following an evolutionary trajectory consistent with physiological principles among older Chinese adults. Since 2008, cognitive abilities have shown a monotonic increasing trend annually, further validating the Flynn effect in this population. The cognitive abilities of the six earlier-born cohorts exhibit an increasing trend, supporting the compression of morbidity hypothesis. Conversely, the cognitive abilities of the five later-born cohorts show a declining trend, supporting the expansion of morbidity hypothesis. These findings collectively contribute to our understanding of cognitive aging patterns and their underlying mechanisms among older Chinese adults.

Introduction

As of 2023, the proportion of the population aged 60 and above in China reached 21.1% [1], and it is projected to increase to 30% by 2035, entering a stage of severe aging [2]. Concurrently, there has been a significant rise in the elderly population experiencing age-relate`d cognitive decline. A nationwide cross-sectional study in 2020 estimated approximately 38.77 million cases of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) among Chinese adults aged 60 and above, with a prevalence rate of approximately 15.5% [3]. MCI, often challenging to accurately identify in its early stages, presents a high risk of progression to dementia [4]. Global projections suggest that by 2050, the population affected by dementia will reach 152 million [5].

Cognitive abilities are essential for elderly individuals' self-care [6] and social participation [7], serving as a crucial indicator of healthy aging [8]. Currently, dementia resulting from cognitive impairment has emerged as a significant risk factor for disability and mortality in elderly individuals [9–11]. This condition not only substantially diminishes the quality of life for affected individuals but also exacerbates the disease burden and care-related costs for families and society at large.

Consequently, dementia poses considerable challenges to healthcare systems, extending its impact beyond the individual to the broader societal and economic spheres [10, 12].

Over the past four decades, China has experienced significant changes in its population age structure, characterized by an accelerating degree of population aging. These demographic shifts potentially influence overall cognitive levels among older Chinese adults. Within this context, the present study addresses the following research questions: First, what are the trajectories of cognitive levels changes across different age groups among older Chinese adults, and what are the distributional characteristics of cognitive abilities among these various age cohorts? Second, to what extent do changes in the external environment influence the overall cognitive ability levels of elderly individuals in China, and do elderly individuals born in the same era exhibit shared patterns of cognitive abilities? These inquiries aim to elucidate the complex interplay among aging, environmental factors, and cognitive abilities among older Chinese adults, thereby contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of cognitive aging within the context of rapid demographic change?

Cognitive abilities

Cognitive abilities, encompassing perception, attention, learning, memory, reasoning, and language, serve as crucial indicators for health assessment and mortality prediction [13]. Baltes (1999) differentiated cognitive abilities into two types: fluid abilities and crystallized abilities [14]. Fluid abilities refer to an individual's capacity to rapidly adapt, learn quickly, and solve novel problems when confronted with new cognitive tasks. These abilities are influenced by factors such as individual health [15, 16], cognitive reserve [17], and cognitive training [18]. Crystallized abilities, in contrast, are stable cognitive capacities developed through long-term learning and knowledge accumulation. Distinct from fluid abilities, crystallized abilities emphasize the knowledge and skills that individuals acquire through learning and experience. These abilities become relatively stable and resistant to change as they are continuously applied and reinforced [19–21].

Age effects on cognitive abilities

The age effects refer to the life course trajectory of individual cognitive abilities accompanying age-related changes, reflecting the distribution and differences of these abilities across various age groups. Most studies support clear and robust age effects on cognitive abilities, with the impact of aging following a multi-directional pattern [22]. Fluid abilities exhibit significant heterogeneity in this regard [23]. Episodic memory, for instance, shows a relatively pronounced decline with age [24–26]. In contrast, semantic memory tends to remain relatively stable, or its decline is less pronounced compared to the other cognitive abilities [27–29]. Executive function also declines with advancing age [30]. However, due to the presence of cohort effects, the trend of age-related decline may be overestimated [31]. Additionally, processing speed plays a more significant role than executive function in explaining age-related memory decline [30]. Crystallized abilities, on the other hand, tend to stabilize after childhood [32], remain highly stable during adulthood, and are less affected by age [33].

Brain structural changes can largely explain the age effects on cognitive abilities in older adults [34]. Specific brain regions, including the hippocampus, frontal and temporal cortices, striatum, thalamus, and nucleus accumbens, gradually undergo atrophy with aging. This atrophy leads to a decline in cognitive abilities such as learning, memory, executive function, and decision-making in elderly individuals. Furthermore, the speed of information processing during individual aging emerges as one of the significant predictors of cognitive abilities in older adults, underlying the age effects on these abilities [35]. Factors such as decreased neural network conductivity and reduced axonal myelination are associated with diminished functional aspects, including slower information processing speed and impaired attention allocation in elderly individuals [36].

Cognitive reserve

During autopsies of older adults exhibiting normal clinical behavior, Katzman observed advanced Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathological features in their brains [37]. In a study comparing cerebral blood flow in the parietal and temporal lobes of AD patients, Stern discovered that an individual's education level may serve as a "reserve" that delays the onset of clinical AD symptoms, allowing individuals to maintain normal cognitive function [38]. Consequently, Stern proposed the cognitive reserve hypothesis based on the brain reserve theory [39, 40]. According to this hypothesis, cognitive reserve is characterized by a dissociation between cognitive performance and brain pathology [41]. It represents a compensatory mechanism that individuals develop based on life experiences, education, and social activities, which can delay the decline and impairment of cognitive abilities [42, 43]. Cognitive reserve is currently regarded as a promising approach for maintaining cognitive health in older adults and delaying the onset of dementia [44]. Although cognitive reserve was initially demonstrated in AD patients, neuroimaging evidence from healthy older adults shows that the correlation between white matter fiber bundle length (FBL) and age is significantly weakened in individuals with high cognitive reserve. This suggests that cognitive reserve also has a protective effect against cognitive decline in healthy older adults [45, 46].

Methods for assessing cognitive reserve include the proxy measures and the functional neuroimaging. The proxy indicator method often uses indicators such as education, occupation, lifestyle, and premorbid IQ [47]. The main measurement tool is the Cognitive Reserve Index questionnaire (CRIq) [48]. Current research primarily focuses on long-term tracking of the cognitive reserve index across multiple time points. The functional imaging method focuses on measuring brain activity levels under different cognitive tasks or at rest. It clarifies the functional organizational changes in the brain during specific cognitive activities, establishing connections between these changes and the proxy indicators of cognitive reserve [49].Presently, multimodal studies, including studies on white matter pathways [50–52], the deposition of amyloid-β [53], connectivity patterns [54], genomics [55], and relevant biomarkers [56], combined with neuropsychological assessments, provide a neurobiological basis for identifying age-related cognitive decline.

The majority of studies indicate a significant positive correlation between cognitive reserve and an individual's cognitive abilities. Individuals with high cognitive reserve exhibit stable and highly organized brain network architectures during the resting state, which is consistent with their superior performance on cognitive tasks [57, 58]. Furthermore, the topological structure of brain networks in these individuals demonstrates greater complexity and robustness, potentially enhancing resilience against cognitive decline [59, 60]. Moreover, individuals display more pronounced neuroplasticity following cognitive training, further supporting the notion that learning serves as an effective intervention strategy for augmenting cognitive reserve [61, 62].

In general, these studies underscore the neurobiological basis of cognitive reserve, providing empirical support for its protective role in cognitive aging and highlighting the potential for targeted interventions to enhance cognitive resilience across the lifespan.

Period effects on cognitive abilities

According to Ryder's definition in his 1965 publication "The Cohort as a Concept in the Study of Social Change," period effects refer to the shared experiences of a specific group due to era-specific changes during a particular timeframe [63]. Over the past two decades, factors such as dietary patterns, climate change, and environmental conditions have influenced overall population cognitive levels. The analysis of period effects aims to identify evolutionary trends in population cognitive levels under changing external environments.

Dietary patterns: In the past 20 years, Chinese dietary habits have shifted significantly from primarily grain-based intake to low-carbohydrate, high-fat consumption patterns, accompanied by malnutrition due to insufficient protein intake in some elderly individuals [64]. This partially explains the recent high incidence of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, which are major risk factors for cognitive impairments such as vascular dementia and AD [65, 66].

Climate change: Over the past 50 years, the intensity and frequency of global warming and extreme high-temperature events have increased significantly, with average annual surface temperatures rising by 1.1 °C. The effect of climate warming on cognitive abilities is gaining increasing attention [67]. Evidence supports a significant negative impact of high-temperature weather on short-term memory and computational ability, with a short-term lag effect on cognitive abilities [68].

Environmental factors: Currently, 40% of global dementia cases are associated with 12 major risk factors, among which air pollution is significant [69–72]. Long-term exposure to air pollution can affect language and mathematical abilities, especially in males and populations with lower educational attainment. The impact of air pollution on language ability tends to become more pronounced with increasing age [73]. Over the past two decades, China has undergone rapid urbanization. Some studies suggest a positive correlation between public health and air pollution, with economic development and social welfare improvements potentially being key driving factors. However, the relationship between air pollution and cognitive abilities remains controversial [74].

These period effects underscore the complex interplay between societal changes, environmental factors, and cognitive abilities, highlighting the need for interdisciplinary approaches in cognitive aging research and the importance of considering broader contextual factors in understanding and addressing cognitive decline in aging populations.

Cohort effects on cognitive abilities

Cohort effects refer to the shared characteristics and group differences that emerge over time among individuals of the same birth cohort, arising from common experiences and similar historical backgrounds [63]. These effects reflect the interactive influences of the era integrated into individual life courses. The cumulative disadvantage theory posits that early-life psychosocial stressors may accumulate and propagate throughout an individual's lifespan [75, 76]. This stress accumulation has been associated with elevated risks of various adverse health outcomes in adulthood, including cardiovascular diseases [77, 78], autoimmune disorders [79], malignant neoplasms [80, 81], and dementia [82].

The biological embedding hypothesis proposes several mechanisms by which early psychosocial stress affects adult health. First, early stress can modify gene expression patterns through DNA methylation and histone modification. These epigenetic alterations may impact individual health in adulthood at cellular and tissue levels [83]. Second, early stress is programmed into M1 macrophages through epigenetic modifications, translational alterations, and tissue remodeling. M1 macrophages, which produce proinflammatory cytokines, exhibit a proinflammatory tendency. This tendency may be exacerbated by behavioral patterns and hormonal imbalances throughout the lifespan. Prolonged inflammatory responses can damage endothelial cells, increasing the risk of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular diseases [84]. Third, early stress may activate stress response systems, such as the hypothalamic‒pituitary‒adrenal (HPA) axis and the autonomic nervous system. This activation can lead to increased production of adrenal cortex hormones and heightened sympathetic nervous system activity. Prolonged exposure to such physiological responses may impact adult health through various pathways [85].

Individuals born around the first half of the twentieth century often experienced adverse external conditions during childhood, such as war, natural disasters, epidemics, and famine. These conditions are known to impact the cognitive abilities of elderly adults across different birth cohorts [86, 87]. Simultaneously, individual factors such as childhood nutrition, educational attainment, family socioeconomic status, and their interaction with the external environment contribute to cognitive reserve during adulthood, potentially influencing cohort effects on cognitive abilities in old age [88].

As China progresses into a stage of advanced population aging, cognitive aging has garnered considerable societal attention. Previous studies have predominantly focused on cross-sectional analyses at single time points. In this study, we utilize large-scale longitudinal tracking data and employ hierarchical Age-Period-Cohort Cross-Classified Random Effects Models (HAPC-CCREM) to explore the following questions: Do the cognitive abilities of older Chinese adults exhibit significant age effects? Have there been significant period effects on cognitive abilities among older Chinese adults over the past 20 years? If so, what trend do they exhibit? Are there significant cohort effects on cognitive abilities among older Chinese adults? If so, do these effects align with the hypotheses of morbidity expansion or morbidity compression?

This study aims to explore and characterize the change trends and population differences in cognitive abilities among older Chinese adults across three temporal dimensions—age, period, and birth cohort. The purpose is to expand the perspectives and research methods used in studying cognitive function among older Chinese adults. This study is beneficial for implementing large-scale cognitive ability screening among older Chinese adults, aiding the government in accurately formulating intervention policies, enhancing overall cognitive levels of the elderly population, and providing theoretical support for actively addressing population aging.

Study design

Data sources and samples

Large-scale public databases focused on aging health research, such as the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), are highly valued by researchers due to their extensive population coverage. However, the CHARLS database, which initiated its first baseline survey in 2011 with the most recent survey conducted in 2020, presents limitations in observing cohort and period effects on cognitive abilities among older Chinese adults due to its relatively short time span.

This study utilizes data from the CLHLS, which conducted survey waves in 1998, 2000, 2002, 2005, 2008/2009, 2011/2012, 2014, and 2017/2018/2019. The dataset spans 20 years, comprising eight waves of survey data. The CLHLS baseline survey in 1998 initially included participants aged 80 and above, and from 2002 onward, gradually incorporated younger elderly individuals aged 60 to 79.

The sample composition of this study is as follows: 12,179 individuals participated in one survey wave, 7,306 in two waves, 5,657 in three waves, 4,610 in four waves, 3,482 in five waves, 2,199 in six waves, 667 in seven waves, and 281 in all eight waves. In total, the study collected 94,116 observations from 36,157 unique individuals. This data structure satisfies the requirements of a multiple-cohort tracking design, facilitating the effective separation of period and cohort effects.

Variable

Key dependent variable

In this study, the key dependent variable is the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score of older Chinese adults, assessed using the Chinese version of the MMSE [89] provided by the CLHLS database. The MMSE scores serve as a proxy measure for cognitive abilities. The MMSE assessment comprises 24 questions across five domains: orientation, registration, calculation and attention, recall, language [67].

The scoring procedure follows the method proposed by Zhang Wenjun [90]. Specifically, the sixth question, which assesses general ability, is scored as follows: participants are asked to name as many food items as possible within one minute, with a maximum score of 7 points. Scores less than 7 reflect the actual number of items named, while naming 7 or more items results in the full score of 7 points. For the remaining 23 questions, correct answers are assigned one point each, while incorrect answers or inability to complete a task are scored as 0 points.

The total MMSE score ranges from 0 to 30 points. A score of 23 or below is defined as indicative of cognitive impairment [91]. The internal consistency of the scale is satisfactory, with a Cronbach's α coefficient of 0.827.

Key independent variables

In this study, the key independent variables are defined as age, period, and birth cohort. The age range of the samples is set to 65–110 years. Based on the birth year, the samples are divided into 11 birth cohorts: those born before 1900 are classified as the first cohort (coded as 0), samples born from 1951 to 1954 are classified as the 11th cohort (coded as 10), and birth cohorts in between are classified every 5 years, with cohort codes ranging from 1 to 9. It is worth noting that the 5th, 6th, and 8th waves of the survey in the CLHLS were conducted over multiple years. Among these, the samples from 1999 and 2017 are smaller and thus are merged for analysis purposes (details of the samples are presented in Table 1). Control variables

The study incorporates several control variables to account for various factors that may influence cognitive abilities. Demographic variables include gender, education level, marital status, pre-retirement occupation, and birthplace. In CLHLS questionnaire, education level is recorded as "years of schooling" and are trimmed at the 99.99th percentile. Marital status is categorized as "married and living with spouse", "married but not living with spouse", "divorced”, “widowed", and "never married". In this study, Marital status is binary variable, with "married and living with spouse" coded as 1 and all other categories coded as 0. Pre-retirement occupation is assessed through the question "What was your main job before age 60?" and includes options such as "professional/technical personnel/doctor/teacher”, “administrative staff”, “general staff/service worker/laborer", "freelancer", "farmer", "housework", "military personnel", "unemployed", and "other". Pre-retirement occupation is also binary variable, with civil servants (including professional/technical personnel, doctors, teachers, administrative staff, and military personnel) coded as 1 due to their generally superior retirement benefits and non-civil servants coded as 0. Birthplace is coded as 1 for urban and 0 for rural.

Health behaviors are represented by proxy variables for smoking and drinking habits. Current smoking and alcohol consumption are each coded as 1 for "Yes" and 0 for "No". Self-rated health and subjective well-being are measured on 5-point Likert scales, with responses ranging from "Very good" (1) to "Very poor" (5).

Given the possibility of individuals exiting due to death or loss to follow-up in the next survey year, which may reflect the individual’s current cognitive level, it is introduced as a control variable. The study also considers various risk factors during an individual's early life that may have significant impacts on health in old age [92]. Childhood nutritional status is assessed using the question "Did you often experience hunger during your childhood?" with "Yes" coded as 1 and "No" coded as 0. Childhood adversity is represented by parental death before the age of 16. The questionnaire asks, "How old were you when your parents passed away?". Responses are coded as 1 if the individual was 16 years old or younger at the time, and 0 if older. These factors are used as control variables to investigate whether there is a cumulative disadvantage effect on cognitive abilities in old age (The descriptive statistics of the main variables are presented in Table 2). Statistical model

The age-period-cohort (APC) model serves as a classic analytical framework for large-scale epidemiological research [93]. In this model, the dependent variable of the model is essentially influenced by three temporal dimensions: age, period, and birth cohort. However, a significant challenge arises due to the perfect collinearity among these three temporal dimensions. Consequently, traditional regression estimation methods struggle to yield unique solutions for the parameters of the APC model, resulting in the "identification problem."

To address this methodological issue, introduced the Hierarchical APC-Cross-Classified Random Effects Model (HAPC-CCREM). This innovative approach leverages extensive multicohort longitudinal data to circumvent the perfect collinearity among age, period, and birth cohort [94]. The HAPC-CCREM conceptualizes these temporal dimensions as nested relationships within a hierarchical structure. In the HAPC-CCREM framework, age is treated as a first-level variable and modeled as a fixed effect, while period and birth cohort are considered second-level variables and are modeled as random effects. This study adopts the assumption that the period and cohort effects involve only random intercept effects, excluding random coefficient effects from the model specification.

Results

Descriptive analysis of MMSE scores among older Chinese adults

When examining changes in MMSE scores by birth cohort, changes in younger age groups are less pronounced, while those in older age groups are substantial. Notably, the cohort born between 1904–1909 (aged 105–110 years during the study) exhibited relatively consistent and elevated MMSE scores in the 2014 and 2018 survey years compared to other survey periods. This observation warrants further investigation, as it deviates from the general trend of cognitive decline with advancing age. It is important to note that the present study may be subject to potential bias due to the survival effect [95]. This phenomenon results in only relatively healthy older adults completing the final survey, thus the MMSE scores obtained reflect solely the cognitive status of surviving participants. This selective attrition may introduce bias into our findings, as the study cannot account for the cognitive trajectories of those who did not survive to participate in the final assessment. The potential impact of this survival selection on our results necessitates consideration in the interpretation of the findings. Figure 1 mixes age effects and cohort effects, making it difficult to separate the period effects and accurately estimate the trend in MMSE scores among older Chinese adults (Figs. 2, 3 and 4).Fig. 1illustrates the trend of MMSE scores among older Chinese adults aged 65 to 110 years, stratified into 10-year age groups. A discernible decline in MMSE scores is observed with increasing age. Conclusions

The data used in this study come from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), which spans from 1998 to 2018 and includes 8 survey waves. This study employed the HAPC-CCREM model to eliminate confounding factors across the temporal dimension, isolated age effects, period effects and cohort effects on MMSE scores among older Chinese adults. The aim is to estimate the trend of MMSE scores in each temporal dimension.

However, the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), while widely used, is recognized as a relatively coarse measurement scale for cognitive abilities, primarily employed in clinical settings as an initial screening instrument for cognitive impairment. Therefore, this paper discusses the findings from the perspective of the cognitive decline among older Chinese adults.

Gender is a significant factor influencing MMSE scores among older Chinese adults. A possible explanation is that, in China’s traditional patriarchal society, females tend to experience greater disparities from males in terms of education level, social interaction networks, and life experiences. As a result, females may have lower cognitive reserves than males, which may explain why females have significantly lower MMSE scores than males in old age [96].

Education level is significantly positively correlated with MMSE scores among older Chinese adults. Education enhances cognitive reserves by stimulating individual cognitive strategies, thereby delaying cognitive decline [97]. Studies have shown a positive correlation between formal education received throughout an individual’s life and cognitive abilities in adulthood, which are effective predictors of the risk of AD [98]. For healthy older adults or those with MCI, lifelong education and cognitive training as a non-pharmacological early intervention strategy has shown positive effects in preventing cognitive decline [99, 100]. Occupation serves as an effective predictor of cognitive reserves. Occupation serves as an effective predictor of cognitive reserves. Due to the differences in cognitive complexity of occupations, the required cognitive intensity can lead to substantial disparities in individuals’ cognitive reserves. A previous study suggested that Occupations with high cognitive complexity have a compensatory effect on cognitive decline [101–103]. Marital status is a significant factor influencing MMSE scores among older Chinese adults. According to the interactive memory theory, spouses in healthy marital relationships tend to show similar developmental trajectories in cognitive abilities [104]. Additionally, individual memory compensatory mechanisms can reduce each other’s cognitive decline [105, 106].

Self-rated health and subjective well-being are both significantly positively correlated with MMSE scores among older Chinese adults. Prospective cohort studies show that self-rated health significantly predicts mild to moderate cognitive impairment among older Chinese adults and serves as an important indication for early intervention in cognitive impairment [107].

Subjective well-being, which inherently comprises emotional and cognitive components, has been identified as a significant predictor of cognitive decline and impairment in older adults [108]. Currently, neuroimaging evidence for elderly is still limited, but studies on younger individuals have shown that subjective well-being is positively correlated with the regional gray matter volume (rGMV) of the right precuneus [76]and the rostral anterior cingulate cortex [109]. These areas are believed to influence subjective well-being by integrating emotional and cognitive components [76].

Nutritional status during childhood exhibits significantly negatively correlation with MMSE scores among older Chinese adults, lending support to the cumulative disadvantage theory. This theory proposes that advantages or disadvantages acquired early in life tend to accumulate over time, resulting in an expanding disparity between individuals as they age [110, 111]. Early life experiences and environmental conditions are crucial determinants of an individual's cognitive reserves, exerting significant influence on physical functions, cognitive abilities, and emotional development in later adulthood. Nutritional deficiencies stemming from childhood poverty may adversely affect cognitive brain regions, including the hippocampus [112], prefrontal cortex [113], and bilateral frontal lobes [114]. This neurological impact potentially diminishes an individual's cognitive reserves, consequently affecting cognitive abilities and elevating the risk of dementia in old age [115, 116]. The death of a father during childhood is positively correlated with MMSE scores among older Chinese adults, whereas the death of a mother is not significant. A possible explanation is that, in traditional Chinese society, males are often the primary decision-makers in family affairs. Compared to the death of a mother, the external environment that an individual faces after his or her father dies during childhood tends to be more uncertain. Therefore, individuals learn a series of life skills and decision-making ability during childhood and adulthood to enhance their psychological resilience [117]. Psychological resilience, in essence, serves as a psychological protective mechanism under stress [118], effectively improving cognitive reserves, delaying cognitive decline, repairing cognitive damage in old age [119]. Urban-born elderly people have significantly greater MMSE scores than their rural-born counterparts. The place of birth reflects an individual’s early living environment. With the typical urban‒rural dual social structure in traditional China, the access to medical care, health awareness, nutritional status, and education level in rural areas lag far behind those in urban areas. Therefore, adverse social environment during childhood may have potentially cumulative disadvantageous effects on cognitive abilities in old age [120, 121] and are also one of the important risk factors for cognitive impairment in old age [122–124].

In this study, no more than 13% of respondents consume more than 50 g of alcohol daily, with the majority being moderate drinkers or nondrinkers. Hence, drinking is significantly positively correlated with MMSE score in older Chinese adults. Most studies suggest that excessive drinking exacerbates atrophy in the frontal lobe, anterior cingulate, and other age-related brain areas, representing one of the risk factors for cognitive impairment [125, 126]. Moderate drinking, however, has a protective effect on cognitive abilities in elderly people [127], enhancing social interaction and connections and thereby improving cognitive reserves, although its specific mechanism of action remains controversial [128, 129].

According to estimates from the HAPC-CCREM model, it is evident that the age effects on MMSE score are significant among older Chinese adults, displaying an inverted U-shaped curve that aligns with the physiological evolution path of cognitive ability in aging.

Traditional cognitive theories typically assume that the cognitive levels in populations remain relatively stable, with changes occurring only on an evolutionary scale. However, Flynn suggested that over time, cognitive abilities such as abstract reasoning and learning capacity have improved as individuals are exposed to advanced technological progress and widespread enhancements in modern education. This improvement in cognitive abilities is attributed to a combination of factors, including environmental, cultural, and nutritional influences [130, 131], education [132], genetics [133], and industrialization [134]. This gradual rise in overall cognitive levels across populations is known as the Flynn Effect [135, 136]. The Flynn effect essentially reflects period effects. For example, education, as a key component of cognitive reserve, it serves as one of the most important forms of cognitive reserve and has a significant impact on the overall cognitive level of society. A study focusing on the population aged 45 and above in China used the 1959–1961 Great Famine as an instrumental variable (IV) and applied two-stage least squares (2SLS) to estimate the causal effect of education on adult cognitive abilities. The results indicate that primary education has a significant causal effect on improving cognitive abilities in adulthood [137]. In a notable study, researchers leveraged China's Compulsory Education Law of 1986 as a quasi-natural experimental policy intervention to examine the intergenerational impact of children's educational attainment on their parents' cognitive aging trajectories. The findings revealed a significant protective effect of children's educational level on their parents' cognitive reserve. Specifically, higher levels of offspring education were associated with a marked reduction in the risk of cognitive impairment among parents [138]. This empirical evidence supports the hypothesis that, at least among older Chinese adults, the overall improvement in educational attainment has a significant causal effect on the Flynn effect. Furthermore, it illuminates the profound impact of social environmental shifts occurring within society during distinct historical epochs, such as the widespread proliferation of educational opportunities, on cognitive abilities. These findings also lend indirect support to the existence of a period effect on cognitive abilities among older Chinese adults.

The period effects observed in this study reveal a complex trajectory of cognitive abilities among older Chinese adults. Following a decline in MMSE scores after 2000 and subsequent minor fluctuations, a consistent monotonic increase emerged post-2008. This trend corroborates the Flynn effect within the context of cognitive.

Several factors may have contributed to this turning point. Societal changes, including the healthcare reforms initiated in 2009, rapid economic development, large-scale popularization of smartphones and the internet, and policies promoting active aging, likely played pivotal roles. The shift might also signify the emergence of cohorts benefiting from the expanded educational opportunities of the 1950s and 1960s, suggesting a cumulative effect of education on cognitive reserve. Furthermore, this trend could reflect an enhanced emphasis on lifelong learning and cognitive engagement in old age.

Potential cohort effects, such as shared experiences psychological resilience and adaptability, along with improvements in nutrition, may also contribute to this phenomenon. The interaction between these cohort effects and broader societal changes is complex and multifaceted. It implies that cognitive abilities in older adults is influenced not only by individual factors such as education but also significantly by environmental and societal conditions.

Future research should aim to examine population and regional differences, and compare studies to assess the universality of this phenomenon. it may lead to a paradigm shift in our understanding of cognitive aging, emphasizing the potential for maintained or even improved cognitive abilities in old age with the appropriate social support.

The compression of morbidity hypothesis, first proposed by Fries (1980), posits that as life expectancy increases, the onset of chronic illness and disability can be delayed relative to the age at death [139]. This theoretical framework suggests that through a combination of lifestyle modifications, preventive healthcare strategies, and advanced medical interventions, individuals can potentially reduce the duration and severity of morbidity in their later years. The hypothesis proposes a more optimistic view of population aging. It implies that the period of life characterized by good health and functional independence can be extended, while the time spent in states of illness or disability before death can be compressed.

The expansion of morbidity hypothesis, proposed as a theoretical counterpoint to the compression of morbidity hypothesis, elucidates another key aspect of global population aging. This theoretical framework, initially articulated by Gruenberg (1977) [140]and Kramer (1980) [141], posits that contemporary advancements in medical technology and ameliorations in living conditions have enabled individuals with chronic diseases to significantly extend their lifespans through efficacious medical interventions. However, this hypothesis also implies a concomitant extension of the period during which individuals survive with chronic illnesses or disabilities. This paradoxical outcome suggests that while life expectancy increases, the absolute duration of morbidity may also be prolonged, potentially leading to an expanded period of compromised health and increased healthcare utilization in later life.

With respect to cohort effects, the determination of morbidity compression versus morbidity expansion hinges critically on the relationship between disease-burdened survival time and healthy life expectancy [142]. After controlling for age effects and period effects in this longitudinal investigation, the results show a discernible upward trajectory in MMSE scores among the six earliest-born cohorts, as individuals with relatively poor health conditions are gradually eliminated with age, and those in better health achieve selective survival, meeting the hypothesis of morbidity compression. This observed pattern aligns with the compression of morbidity hypothesis and can be attributed to two primary mechanisms: First, survival effect: Individuals with comparatively poor health conditions are progressively eliminated from the cohort as age advances. This process results in a survivorship bias towards healthier individuals within each cohort. Second, selective survival: those in better health achieve selective survival, potentially due to genetic factors, lifestyle choices, or better access to healthcare resources.

The MMSE scores show a contrasting trend among the five later-born cohorts, characterized by a discernible decline. This phenomenon can be explained by considering the differential survival selection pressures across cohorts. The earlier-born cohorts, upon entering the survey period, had already undergone more rigorous survival selection compared to their later-born counterparts. This observed pattern aligns with the compression of morbidity hypothesis and can be contribute to several factors contribute to this pattern: First, evolving external conditions: advancements in lifestyle, public health, and treatment levels have altered the survival landscape across later-born cohorts. Second, prolonged period of morbidity: the advancements in healthcare and living conditions have led to an extended duration of life with chronic illnesses for the later-born cohorts. While these improvements have increased overall life expectancy, they have also enabled individuals to survive longer with various health conditions that might have been fatal in earlier-born cohorts. Consequently, members of later-born cohorts tend to experience a longer span of life characterized by the presence of one or more chronic diseases or disabilities. The confluence of these factors suggests that individuals from later-born cohorts tend to exhibit poorer health status in old age, despite overall gains in longevity. At a deeper level, it demonstrates the complexity of interpreting cross-cohort trends in cognitive function and overall health.

In conclusion, this study reveals an inverted U-shaped trend in the cohort effects on MMSE scores among older Chinese adults. Specifically, the earlier-born cohorts demonstrate a higher likelihood of morbidity compression, characterized by a delayed onset of cognitive decline relative to increasing life expectancy. Conversely, the later-born cohorts exhibit an increased risk of cognitive impairment, demonstrating a greater tendency towards morbidity expansion. This phenomenon may be attributed to improved survival rates in the presence of chronic conditions among more recent cohorts.

Cognitive reserve serves as an effective protective mechanism for maintaining cognitive abilities, helping to delay cognitive decline and impairment. Therefore, in the context of China's rapidly aging population, it is essential to strengthen the interventions of vascular diseases such as cerebrovascular disease, coronary heart disease (CHD), and hypertension to effectively reduce the incidence of vascular dementia in the elderly. Such interventions are crucial for effectively mitigating the incidence of vascular dementia among the elderly population. Regular cognitive function screening, especially for the early identification and prevention of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in younger elderly populations, is crucial. Accurate assessment of cognitive reserve levels in older adults, based on multifaceted factors including formal education, cultural experiences, life experiences, and socioeconomic status, is crucial for developing effective, personalized care strategies. Actively promoting lifelong learning and cognitive training can increase cognitive reserve. Furthermore, the modification of living environments coupled with the integration of smart home technologies presents a promising avenue for enhancing the autonomy of older adults and potentially improving their cognitive abilities.

Several limitations of our study should be mentioned, which also represent areas for future in-depth exploration. First, while this study employs the Hierarchical Age-Period-Cohort Cross-Classified Random Effects Model (HAPC-CCREM) to analyze the associations between age, period, and cohort effects on cognitive abilities among older Chinese adults. Despite the statistical sophistication of our study, it is crucial to acknowledge its limitations in establishing causal mechanisms. Furthermore, the cross-sectional nature of some of the data within the longitudinal analysis may introduce bias in estimating longitudinal trajectories, particularly for age effects. This complexity is compounded by the potential presence of death or loss to follow-up in the survey year, which could produce survivor effects for different birth cohorts and periods, thereby influencing the observed cognitive abilities change patterns. Although the HAPC-CCREM provides valuable insights into the relative contributions of age, period, and cohort effects on cognitive abilities among older Chinese adults. these methodological constraints underscore the need for cautious interpretation of the results and point to directions for future studies using additional research methods, such as causal inference or quasi-natural experiment, to further elucidate the causal pathways underlying cognitive impairment among older Chinese adults.

Second, due to data source limitations significant limitation of this study is the omission of key variables that potentially influence cognitive reserve among older Chinese adults. These include variables related to climate change, dietary patterns, and environmental factors. Additionally, this study lacks information on significant external shocks experienced by individuals within the same birth cohort, notably psychological trauma from wars and early-life malnutrition resulting from the Great Chinese Famine of 1959–1961. The absence of these variables may have led to an incomplete understanding of the complex interplay between individual experiences, historical contexts, and cognitive levels. Future research should integrate a life course perspective with an environment analysis framework, incorporating measures of early-life adversities, nutritional status, and environmental factors. This comprehensive approach will facilitate a more nuanced understanding of the complex relationships between these multifaceted determinants and cognitive decline among older Chinese adults.

Third, a limitation of this study lies in its reliance on the MMSE scores as a proxy measure for cognitive ability among older Chinese adults. However, the primary purpose of MMSE scores is not to assess cognitive abilities but to assist to identify dementia or MCI in clinical practice, which may lead to an incomplete or potentially biased representation of cognitive function in the study population. The sensitivity and accuracy of this assessment for individuals with different educational levels remain controversial, a factor relevant in the diverse educational landscape of China. This educational bias may result in: a) Underestimation of cognitive abilities in individuals with limited formal education. b) Reduced sensitivity in detecting subtle cognitive changes in highly educated individuals. For instance, among highly educated populations, the MMSE scores are prone to a ceiling effect, resulting in near-perfect scores. This may lead to underdiagnosis of MCI. These factors were not sufficiently considered in the study.

Fourth, this study focuses on the cognitive abilities among older Chinese adults. The cross-cultural applicability of the results remains a concern. China's unique societal structure, characterized by distinctive socioeconomic conditions, demographic structures, healthcare systems, and cultural norms, may limit the generalizability of these results to other populations or countries. Therefore, the generalizability of these findings to other populations or countries should be approached with caution. Future research should focus on cross-cultural comparative studies to identify universal and culture-specific aspects of cognitive aging, as well as develop culturally sensitive assessment tools for more accurate international comparisons. This approach will enhance our understanding of cognitive aging across diverse populations and improve the global applicability of research findings in this field.
