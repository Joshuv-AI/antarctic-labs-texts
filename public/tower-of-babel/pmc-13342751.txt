Effect of low-level laser therapy on postoperative swelling after surgical management of zygomaticomaxillary complex fracture
Authors: Vidit Jain, Prashant Punde, Nilesh Mishra, Vinay Kumar Gupta, Deepshikha Bhave
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:The face is highly vulnerable to traumatic injuries, with the zygomaticomaxillary complex (ZMC) frequently fractured due to its prominent position. Postoperative swelling after ZMC surgery can lead to discomfort, limited mouth opening, and aesthetic concerns. Reduction methods include medications such as corticosteroids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), enzyme preparations, and non-pharmacological approaches such as manual lymph drainage, cryotherapy, and head elevation. Low-level laser therapy (LLLT), also known as cold laser therapy, has shown promise in reducing postoperative swelling. LLLT uses low-energy lasers (10–500 mW) in the red to near-infrared spectrum (660–905 nm) to penetrate tissues, promoting lymphatic drainage and tissue repair. It modulates inflammatory responses, increases ATP production, and enhances microcirculation. LLLT’s non-invasive nature and its ability to reduce swelling and promote healing make it a valuable adjunctive therapy. In this study, LLLT was used for managing postoperative swelling after ZMC fractures.Objective:The objective of our study was to determine the effect of LLLT on postoperative swelling after surgical management of a ZMC fracture.Materials and Methods:The study was performed at Krishna Hospital and School of Dental Sciences and included 46 patients with ZMC fractures, diagnosed via 3D CT facial scans. Patients, aged 20–50 years, were randomly assigned to two groups: one received LLLT postoperatively, while the other did not. All patients underwent open reduction and internal fixation surgery under general anesthesia with standardized postoperative medications. Swelling was measured using tape measurements between specific facial landmarks. Group one received LLLT of 810 nm at 500 mW for 17 minutes twice daily for 4 days, with evaluations on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and after 1 month. LLLT was administered in an isolated room, with both patients and operators wearing protective eyewear. Measurements were taken by a different operator to minimize bias.Results:The study evaluated LLLT on postoperative swelling in patients with ZMC fractures. We observed significant reductions in ala-tragus and outer canthus to mandible measurements in the LLLT group compared to the control. The LLLT group showed a consistent and significant decrease in swelling over 30 days, especially notable in the ala-tragus measurements, indicating LLLT’s effectiveness in reducing postoperative swelling.Conclusion:This study showed that LLLT significantly reduces immediate postoperative swelling in ZMC fractures compared to a control group. Although long-term swelling reduction was similar, LLLT proved effective in the early postoperative period, highlighting its potential for broader maxillofacial applications.

INTRODUCTION

The face is a key area of the human body that is often vulnerable to traumatic injuries. Accidents, falls, and physical altercations can all result in these injuries. Due to its delicate nature, the facial structure is particularly susceptible to fractures, lacerations, and soft-tissue damage. Among the facial bones, the zygomaticomaxillary complex (ZMC) is especially prone to fractures because of its prominent position.[1] This bone is the second most frequently affected mid-facial bone and accounts for approximately 25% of all facial fractures.[2] Postoperative swelling is a common concern following surgical procedures, particularly in the ZMC region.[3] Postoperative swelling in this region can be significant, leading to patient discomfort, limitation of mouth opening, delayed recovery, and aesthetic concerns. Methods for postoperative swelling reduction may vary depending on the type of surgery and individual patient factors.

Various modalities for reducing postoperative edema include the use of medications such as corticosteroids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, a combination of corticosteroids and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, or enzyme preparations such as serrapeptase.[4] In addition to medication, non-pharmacological approaches to address postoperative edema include manual lymph drainage, soft laser therapy,[5] cryotherapy, head elevation, compression, and limited physical activity. Advancements in medical technology and surgical techniques for patients with facial trauma have significantly improved outcomes. Medical professionals dedicate their expertise to ensuring individuals regain their standard of living and appearance. In the past, postoperative swelling has been reduced using low-level laser therapy (LLLT). Light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation (LASER) is a device that generates light through a process of optical amplification based on the stimulated emission of electromagnetic radiation.[6] LLLT, also known as cold laser therapy or photobiomodulation, is a non-invasive medical procedure that uses low-energy lasers or light-emitting diodes to stimulate and promote tissue healing and reduce inflammation.[7] Research has demonstrated that LLLT enhances edema reduction, pain management, and tissue repair.[8] This technology has also gained popularity as a successful treatment for a number of conditions such as musculoskeletal pain, dermatological issues, orthopedic conditions, aphthous ulcers, herpes simplex infections, oral lichen planus, xerostomia, mucositis, dentinal hypersensitivity, cavity preparations, enamel and dentine conditioning, myofascial pain dysfunction syndrome, root development stimulation, pulpotomy, endodontic surgeries, periodontal therapy, gingivectomy, frenectomies, oral lesion removal, and reducing discomfort during orthodontic treatment.[910] The superficial location of the zygoma and maxilla allows for direct access to the affected tissue areas, enabling targeted delivery of lasers. By utilizing specific wavelength parameters and irradiation techniques, LLLT can be applied to the surgical sites to modulate the inflammatory response, promote lymphatic drainage, and accelerate tissue healing processes.[11] Furthermore, the non-invasive nature of LLLT makes it an attractive adjunctive therapy in the postoperative care of individuals undergoing ZMC procedures. Various studies have been conducted using LLLT to reduce postoperative swelling. However, these studies have not specifically focused on the ZMC. Despite this, the findings of these studies are relevant to the broader field of oral and maxillofacial surgery. Thus, LLLT may offer benefits in managing postoperative swelling in this specific ZMC anatomical region as well. The aim of the present study is to compare the effect of LLLT on postoperative swelling after management of ZMC fractures.

Each patient underwent a 3D CT facial scan for diagnosis, which was based on fracture lines at the frontozygomatic suture, infraorbital region, maxillary buttress, and zygomatic arch. The inclusion criteria were patients aged 20–50 years with ZMC fractures, classified as ASA I or ASA II, who were willing to participate and follow up. The exclusion criteria were medically compromised patients, fracture sites with infection, immunocompromised patients, ASA III or ASA IV patients, and non-compliant individuals. All patients signed informed consent and consented to participate in English or Marathi. Patients were randomly divided into two groups using the envelope method: Group 1 received LLLT postoperatively, while group 2 did not. All patients underwent open reduction and internal fixation under general anesthesia. Intraoperative and postoperative medications were standardized, including amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, metronidazole, diclofenac sodium, and omeprazole. Other swelling management methods, such as ice packs, steroids, and pressure dressings, were avoided. Swelling measurements were taken using the tape measurement method between the ala of the nose and the tragus, as shown in Figure 1, and between the outer canthus of the eye and the angle of the mandible, as shown in Figure 2. Measurements were recorded on day 1 just before administering LLLT. Patients received LLLT in an isolated room. For safety, patients wore black eye protection, as shown in Figure 3, and the operator used black Polaroid goggles, as shown in Figure 4. LLLT was administered with a probe kept 5 mm away from the skin, as shown in Figure 5, starting at the most prominent part of the swelling and moving in a spiral motion outward, as shown in Figure 6. The therapy, set at 810 nm and 500 mW, was given for 17 minutes twice a day. Afterward, swelling was evaluated using the tape measurement method after each session. LLLT was administered for 4 consecutive days, after which swelling measurements were taken on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and after 1 month. To avoid bias, postoperative swelling was measured by a different operator, and all data were systematically recorded on a prepared data collection sheet.

This study sought to ascertain the impact of LLLT on postoperative swelling following the surgical management of patients with ZMC fractures. A total of 46 patients with ZMC fractures who had undergone surgical management were included in the research. Patients were split into two equal groups based on the treatment they received: group 1 (test group; n = 23) received LLLT postoperatively, while group 2 (control group; n = 23) did not receive LLLT postoperatively.

Table 1 and Graph 1 represent the demographic details of the study participants. The mean age of the subjects in the test group and control group was 33.26 ± 9.27 years and 38.57 ± 13.44 years, respectively. There were 22 male subjects and one female subject in each group. Left ZMC fractures were most common in the test group (60.9%), whereas right ZMC fractures were most common in the control group (52.2%).

DISCUSSION

The ZMC bone is the second most commonly affected mid-facial bone, accounting for approximately 25% of all facial fractures.[2] The goal of managing ZMC fractures is 3D alignment and fixation, along with addressing concomitant infraorbital rim and orbital floor fractures.[12] Generally, fractures of the ZMC result in cosmetic deformities due to edema in the midface region and functional deficits such as altered vision, restricted mouth opening, and paraesthesia. The more dramatic edema in ZMC fractures is due to the loose connective tissue in the periorbital region.[13] ZMC fractures can lead to a loss of facial prominence due to the flattening of the malar prominence. Additionally, postoperative swelling in this region is a common concern, as it can be significant, leading to patient discomfort, limitation of mouth opening, delayed recovery, and aesthetic concerns. These issues can cause psychological, aesthetic, and functional disturbances, potentially affecting the patient’s quality of life. Therefore, in addition to prompt diagnosis and treatment of these fractures, it is essential to reduce postoperative swelling and inflammation early to enhance the patient’s well-being. These factors underscore the importance of researching ZMC fractures for the betterment of patients.

To minimize postoperative edema, various measures have been tried. Drugs such as corticosteroids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), a combination of corticosteroids and NSAIDs, or enzyme preparations such as serrapeptase have been used. Other non-medication methods such as manual lymph drainage and cryotherapy have also been tried.[14] Although various methods for reducing postoperative swelling have been used, the choice largely depends on the operator’s preference. There is a lack of scientific evidence in the literature to justify which method is most effective. Therefore, the present study aimed to provide solid evidence on a newly implemented treatment modality for reducing postoperative edema in ZMD fractures.

There is growing evidence of the effectiveness of LLLT across a wide range of specialties. LLLT uses light with wavelengths ranging from 660 nm to 905 nm. At the tissue level, LLLT induces vasodilation, raising the stimulus threshold of pain receptors and improving lymphatic drainage and blood circulation. It also increases neutrophil and fibroblast activity. According to Tadakuma et al.,[15] LLLT is beneficial during each of the three stages of inflammation: exudation, alteration, and proliferation. Pain, inflammation, redness, swelling, and heat are all reduced by laser therapy. Recently, the anti-inflammatory properties of LLLT have been utilized to decrease swelling following surgery.

Previous studies such as those by Shamloo et al.,[16] de Souza et al.,[17] and Silva JA Jr.[18] have evaluated the efficacy of LLLT in experimental animal models and have supported its effectiveness in anti-inflammatory and anti-edematous actions. Additionally, various human studies have demonstrated the positive effects of these wavelengths on pain, inflammation, and tissue repair, especially in reducing postoperative swelling. However, these studies have not specifically focused on postoperative swelling in ZMC fracture cases. There is insufficient evidence in the literature to substantiate whether LLLT can be used as an adjunctive treatment modality for managing postoperative swelling after ZMC fracture. The present study focused on these gaps in the literature and aimed to compare the effect of LLLT on postoperative swelling after the management of ZMC fractures.

Despite the growing evidence supporting the anti-inflammatory and anti-edematous actions of LLLT, significant gaps remain in the current literature. Most studies on LLLT have primarily focused on its general effects on tissue repair, inflammation reduction, and pain management, without specifically addressing its role in managing postoperative swelling in ZMC fractures. Moreover, there is a notable lack of comparative data evaluating LLLT alongside other standard modalities, such as corticosteroids, cold therapy, or compression, which are commonly used to manage postoperative swelling. Additionally, the precise mechanisms by which LLLT exerts its therapeutic effects in the context of ZMC fractures remain insufficiently explored. LLLT is thought to modulate cellular activities by enhancing mitochondrial function and ATP production, reducing oxidative stress, and promoting tissue healing. It also provides localized anti-inflammatory effects without systemic side effects, unlike pharmacological treatments. Furthermore, LLLT improves microvascular circulation, reducing edema by facilitating lymphatic drainage and minimizing capillary leakage. Its non-invasive and painless nature makes it a promising adjunctive treatment modality, potentially offering superior patient compliance compared to other interventions. Addressing these gaps, this study aims to explore the efficacy of LLLT in reducing postoperative swelling after ZMC fracture management, providing insights into its comparative effectiveness and underlying mechanisms.

There are various types of lasers used in health sciences, classified based on the lasing medium, wavelength range, power, and tissue applicability. The diode laser is a solid-state semiconductor that produces a range of laser wavelengths from 810 nm to 980 nm.[19] Various authors have used different laser types depending on the clinical scenarios. Dakhil et al.[20] used a carbon dioxide laser for the treatment of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. Alan H et al.[21] and Asutay et al.[22] utilized gallium-aluminum-arsenide (GaAlAs) diode laser devices with a continuous wavelength of 810 nm, which were used in non-contact mode. Several studies, such as those by Khadra M et al.,[23] support the use of diode lasers in promoting faster healing of bone defects or fractures with a significant increase in calcium, phosphorus, and protein. These reasons led us to choose a diode laser, which may also hasten bone healing in the context of our study.

In the present study, a Novogreen Laser 810-nm diode laser machine (3rd generation) with a multi-attachment handpiece and photobiomodulation probe was used. The literature reveals various techniques for facial swelling measurements: the tape measurement method, face bow, ultrasound, and portable stereophotogrammetry such as the 3dMD Photogrammetric System. In the present study, the swelling measurements were performed based on a study by Chukwuneke et al.[24] Using a horizontal and vertical guide with a tape on four reference points, namely the tragus, ala of the nose, outer canthus of the eye, and angle of the mandible, the measurements were taken. The horizontal measure corresponds to the distance between the ala of the nose and the tragus, while the vertical measure corresponds to the distance between the outer canthus of the eye and the angle of the mandible. With the development of modern technologies, several investigators have used the 3dMD Photogrammetric System or other analytical photogrammetry systems. However, the literature presents conflicting opinions on the use of 3dMD.[25] These techniques are not always reliable as there could be variations in posing for which no correction could be applied. Hence, the tape measurement method was chosen for the present study.

In the study group, there was a significant reduction in the ala-tragus measurement from day 1 to day 5 compared to the control group. Additionally, there was a significant difference between the ala-tragus measurements on days 7 and 30 in the test group. However, there was no significant difference between the ala-tragus measurements on days 7 and 30 in the control group. Both groups showed a gradual reduction in the ala-tragus measurement from day 1 to day 30, and this reduction was statistically significant. The ala-tragus measurement up until day 5 was significantly lower in the test group compared to the control group. Moreover, the percentage reduction in the ala-tragus measurement was significantly greater in the test group compared to the control group by the end of day 5.

The outer canthus of the eye-to-angle of mandible measurement in our study showed a gradual reduction from day 1 to day 30 in both groups, and this reduction was statistically significant. In both groups, the measurements on days 1 and 3 were significantly greater than those on days 5, 7, and 30. However, the measurements on days 5, 7, and 30 in both groups were almost similar and showed no significant difference. There was a non-significant difference in the outer canthus of the eye-to-angle of mandible measurement and the percentage reduction in this measurement between the two groups at each interval studied.

In a similar study performed by Baek et al.,[26] there was a statistically significant greater reduction in edema in the LLLT group than in the control group. The study reported a 16.5% reduction in edema in the LLLT group and a 7.3% reduction in the control group. These results align with those of the present study. In Baek et al.’s study, various facial fractures, including nasal bone, orbital wall, zygomaticomaxillary bone, mandible, and frontal sinus fractures, were included, while the present study focused exclusively on zygomaticomaxillary fractures. The variety of fracture sites and the severity of the fractures may affect the speed of post-traumatic edema reduction, leading to discrepancies in the variables.

In contrast to our present study, A.K. Roynesdal et al.[5] noted no statistically significant differences in the comparison between the experimental side and the placebo side. The authors also stated that soft-laser treatment had no beneficial effect on swelling, trismus, and pain after third molar surgery. However, in the present study, a diode laser with an 810-nm wavelength was utilized, whereas A.K. Roynesdal et al.[5] used an 830-nm biophoton laser. The differences in methodology and laser type could explain the variation in results.

Although LLLT is easy to use, a few technical difficulties were encountered in the study. The patient was required to be taken into an isolated room for LLLT. Transferring a patient to immediate postop care was difficult and required additional personnel. The patient had to wear black-colored eye-protective gear, and the operator had to wear protective black-colored Polaroid goggles for safety. These measures made the patient anxious, necessitating verbal counseling before the procedure.

In the future, studies with larger samples should be conducted to overcome the limitations of the present study. Comparative studies using lasers with different wavelengths or types should be undertaken to provide concrete evidence on LLLT and fully utilize its potential in healthcare.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrated that LLLT is effective in significantly reducing immediate postoperative swelling in patients with ZMC fractures. The LLLT group showed a notable decrease in swelling measurements compared to the control group, particularly within the first 5 days post-surgery. Although the long-term differences in swelling were minimal, the initial reduction provided by LLLT can improve patient comfort, facilitate quicker recovery, and enhance aesthetic outcomes. Given the non-invasive nature and potential benefits of LLLT, it presents a promising adjunctive treatment for managing postoperative edema in maxillofacial surgery. Future research with larger sample sizes and comparisons of different laser types and wavelengths is recommended to further validate these findings and optimize the use of LLLT in clinical practice.
