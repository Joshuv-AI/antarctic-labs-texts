Introduction

Moutan Cortex, the root bark of Paeonia suffruticosa Andrews, has been used clinically in China for nearly 2000 years [1]. Peony is native to parts of Asia, Europe, and North America and is widely cultivated in East Asia, especially in China and Japan, where it is valued both as an ornamental plant and as a medicinal resource [2]. As the principal medicinal part of peony, Moutan Cortex has a long history of use in traditional Chinese medicine and was recorded as early as Shennong’s Herbal Classic of Materia Medica [3]. Modern pharmacological studies have further linked Moutan Cortex and its phenolic rich constituents to multiple bioactivities, including anti-inflammatory [4], antioxidant [5], antibacterial [6], and antidiabetic [7] effects. These properties, together with the growing interest in plant derived health ingredients, make Moutan Cortex an attractive raw material for developing polyphenol-based extracts and related functional products.

In many laboratories and industrial settings, polyphenols are still commonly extracted with traditional organic solvents or ethanol–water mixtures. These approaches are easy to implement, but they often require relatively large amounts of solvent and extended extraction times to achieve satisfactory yields [8]. Moreover, because polyphenols vary widely in polarity and structure, a single conventional solvent system may not extract different phenolic compounds equally well, which can result in suboptimal selectivity and inconsistent extract composition [9]. The use of volatile organic solvents also introduces practical concerns, including flammability and operator exposure risks, as well as environmental burdens associated with solvent emissions and energy intensive recovery steps. Together, these issues make conventional solvent-based extraction less aligned with green chemistry principles and the increasing demand for safer and more sustainable processing technologies. Natural deep eutectic solvents (NADES) have emerged as promising “green” alternatives for extracting bioactive compounds from plants. NADES are typically formed by mixing a hydrogen bond acceptor (HBA) and a hydrogen bond donor (HBD), creating a stable liquid through extensive hydrogen-bond networks [10]. NADES are DES systems formulated from natural primary metabolites. Compared with traditional solvents, NADES have several advantages, including rapid preparation, high extraction efficiency, biodegradability, cost-effectiveness and minimal environmental impact [11].

Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) is a widely applied, environmentally friendly technique for recovering bioactive compounds from natural sources. The enhancement effect of UAE is mainly attributed to acoustic cavitation, in which ultrasonic waves induce the formation, growth, and collapse of microbubbles in the extraction medium [33]. The collapse of cavitation bubbles generates localized mechanical effects, including microjets, shock waves, and shear forces, which can disrupt plant cell walls, improve solvent penetration into the plant matrix, reduce mass-transfer resistance, and accelerate the diffusion of target compounds into the solvent [35]. These effects collectively contribute to higher extraction efficiency, shorter extraction time, and reduced solvent consumption. However, NADES-assisted UAE of plant polyphenols is a typical multi-factor process governed by several coupled variables, including solvent viscosity and polarity, liquid-to-solid ratio, ultrasonic power, and extraction time. These variables can interact in a highly nonlinear manner by jointly affecting solvent penetration, plant matrix disruption, solute diffusion, and polyphenol stabilization. Therefore, stepwise single-factor optimization is inefficient and may fail to locate the true optimum. Although response surface methodology (RSM) remains one of the most commonly applied optimization tools for extraction processes due to its simplicity and interpretability, its predictive performance is generally based on predefined polynomial relationships. Therefore, the quadratic models used in RSM may become insufficient when dealing with highly nonlinear systems involving multiple interacting factors. This limitation is particularly relevant for NADES-assisted ultrasound extraction, where extraction efficiency is simultaneously affected by solvent viscosity, polarity, hydrogen-bond interactions, acoustic cavitation effects, and mass transfer behavior. These coupled physicochemical phenomena may generate complex nonlinear relationships that are difficult to accurately describe using conventional regression-based approaches. Recent studies have demonstrated that machine learning-based models can provide improved predictive capability for complex extraction processes by capturing nonlinear relationships between processing variables and extraction responses [12]. Therefore, we hypothesized that integrating a rationally designed NADES system with ultrasound-assisted extraction and intelligent optimization could enhance polyphenol recovery while overcoming the limitations of conventional optimization strategies. To test this hypothesis, this study established a Gaussian-strategy-enhanced whale optimization algorithm (GSWOA)- support vector regression (SVR) model to optimize extraction parameters and predict extraction performance, employed COSMO-RS to elucidate the molecular interactions between Bet-LevA and representative polyphenols.

Beyond experimental optimization, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were integrated to provide a microscopic explanation for the superior extraction capacity of Bet-LevA. By modeling the behavior of five representative polyphenols, the simulations aimed to characterize the diffusion dynamics and intermolecular interactions, including hydrogen bonding and interaction energy, that govern solute mobility and solubility within the NADES network. Furthermore, the functional relevance of the resulting extract was evaluated using an AML-12 hepatocyte model of lipid accumulation. Oil Red O staining was employed to assess the attenuation of free fatty acid-induced intracellular lipid deposition, providing preliminary evidence for the in vitro lipid-lowering potential of MCP. Compared with previous studies that mainly focused on conventional solvent extraction, solvent screening, or empirical optimization of plant polyphenols, the novelty of this study lies in the integration of Bet-LevA-assisted green extraction, MD-based solvent-polyphenol interaction analysis, GSWOA-SVR-assisted ultrasonic extraction optimization, and in vitro lipid accumulation evaluation. This comprehensive framework provides a systematic strategy for the sustainable valorization of MCP and supports its potential application in functional foods and nutraceuticals with in vitro lipid-lowering potential, pending further in vivo validation.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Moutan Cortex was purchased from Nanjing Tongrentang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China). Choline chloride, betaine, L-proline, ethylene glycol, levulinic acid, glycerol and citric acid used for synthesizing NADES were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The purity of all reagents is higher than 99.0%. Forty kinds of standard compounds such as rutin, gallic acid, quercetin 3-β-D-glucoside and 3,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (USA).

Preparation and physicochemical characterization of NADESs

For the synthesis of NADESs, heating and stirring were used as described previously [13], [14]. The extraction solvent was an aqueous NADES solution. Following a previously reported protocol, eight NADES systems were prepared by combining the HBA and HBD at predefined molar ratios. The mixtures were heated to 80 °C and agitated at 500 rpm for 15 min, or until a clear and homogeneous liquid was obtained. Subsequently, deionized water was added to each neat NADES to prepare aqueous NADES solutions containing 30% water by weight. The addition of 30% water was designed to reduce the relatively high viscosity of neat NADES, improve solvent penetration into the plant matrix, enhance mass transfer, and adjust solvent polarity, thereby facilitating the release and dissolution of plant polyphenols. Meanwhile, the water content was controlled at 30% to prevent excessive weakening of the intrinsic hydrogen-bonding network of NADES, which is essential for maintaining their characteristic solute–solvent interactions. Physicochemical properties, including pH, viscosity and polarity, were analyzed according to the methods of Li et al. [15] and Liu et al. [16], as shown in Table 1.

Ultrasound-assisted NADES extraction and TPC determination

For the preliminary screening of NADES formulations, ultrasound-assisted extraction was performed under fixed conditions (420 W ultrasonic power, 30 min extraction time, 45 ± 0.5 °C, and a liquid-to-solid ratio of 30:1 mL/g). These parameters were selected based on previous reports on NADES-assisted ultrasound extraction and our previous experience with plant polyphenol extraction systems. Moderate ultrasonic intensity was applied to provide sufficient cavitation effects for cell wall disruption and mass transfer enhancement while avoiding excessive thermal effects that may accelerate phenolic degradation. An extraction time of 30 min was selected to ensure adequate solvent–matrix interaction while preventing unnecessary prolonged sonication. The temperature was controlled at 45 °C because mild heating can decrease NADES viscosity and improve diffusion without causing substantial degradation of phenolic compounds. The liquid-to-solid ratio of 30:1 mL/g was selected to achieve sufficient solvent accessibility and maintain practical solvent consumption. For comparison, 70% ethanol was used as the conventional extraction solvent and was extracted under the same ultrasound-assisted conditions as the NADES groups. After extraction, the mixtures were centrifuged at 4000 r/min for 10 min, and the supernatants were collected and clarified by vacuum filtration. The filtrates were then purified using an AB-8 macroporous resin column to enrich MCP and remove water-soluble impurities. The detailed operating parameters and purification efficiency evaluation, including polyphenol recovery, enrichment factor, and sugar removal rate, are provided in the Supplementary Material. The collected polyphenol-rich eluates were concentrated and used for subsequent TPC determination and other analyses of MCP.

The TPC of MCP was measured by Folin-Ciocalteu method as described in our previous work [17]. Gallic acid was used as the standard, and the results were expressed as gallic acid equivalent per gram dry weight of MCP (mg GAE/g DW). In brief, an aliquot of appropriately diluted sample solution (5.0 mL) was mixed with 0.5 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and allowed to react for 5 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 1.0 mL of 20% (w/v) sodium carbonate solution was added, and the mixture was incubated in the dark for 60 min at room temperature. Absorbance was recorded at 760 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. A series of gallic acid standard solutions within the range of 0–100 μg/mL were prepared to construct the calibration curve. The linear regression equation for the standard curve was y = 0.0069x + 0.0846, with a coefficient of determination R2 = 0.9923. All measurements were carried out in triplicate.

UPLC-Q Exactive/MS analysis

Phenolic constituents in MCP obtained by ultrasound-assisted extraction using a betaine–levulinic acid (UABL) deep eutectic solvent and by 70% ethanol were characterized by UPLC–HRMS using a Vanquish UPLC system coupled to a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) (Table S1). Prior to analysis, all samples were passed through 0.22 μm membrane filters, and 2 μL was injected. Separation was carried out on a Waters ACQUITY HSS T3 column (50 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) maintained at 40 °C, with a flow rate of 0.30 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% (v/v) acetic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B). Elution was performed with the following gradient: 10% B from 0 to 2 min, ramped to 60% B and held from 6 to 9 min, then returned to 10% B at 9.1 min and equilibrated until 12 min. The concentrations of the detected phenolic compounds were calculated using external calibration curves prepared with authentic standards and expressed as ng/100 mg sample. No internal standard was used for matrix-effect correction. High-resolution MS data were collected using an electrospray ionization (ESI) source, and raw files were processed in Xcalibur 4.1 and TraceFinder 4.1 Clinical (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

FT-IR analysis

FT-IR spectroscopy was used to investigate intermolecular interactions in the Bet-LevA system. Betaine, levulinic acid, and the synthesized Bet‑LevA were analyzed. The FT-IR spectral analysis was performed using a Cary 610/670 (Microscopic Infrared Spectrometer from Varian, USA.) over the wavenumber range of 4000–500 cm−1 [18]. The spectra of Bet, LevA, and Bet‑LevA were compared to identify characteristic band shifts and intensity changes associated with NADES formation.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

The residues obtained after UABL extraction and 70% ethanol extraction, along with the raw Moutan Cortex powder (untreated), were collected and pre-treated for observation. The samples were thoroughly washed with deionized water to remove residual solvents and subsequently dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C until a constant weight was achieved. The surface topography and cellular structure of the samples were observed using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (S-4800 II, Hitachi Limited, Japan) at an acceleration voltage of 10 kV. Micrographs were captured at various magnifications to compare the degree of surface erosion, pore formation, and structural collapse induced by the different extraction treatments. For quantitative evaluation of matrix disruption, SEM micrographs obtained under identical magnification and imaging conditions were further analyzed using ImageJ software. Images were converted to grayscale and subjected to identical thresholding and binary segmentation procedures to identify apparent pore and crack regions. The apparent pore/crack area fraction was calculated as the segmented pore/crack area divided by the total analyzed image area and expressed as a percentage. Three SEM fields were analyzed for each group (n = 3), and the resulting data were statistically analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. The quantitative results are provided in Supplementary Fig. S4.

COSMO-RS modeling

COSMO‑RS modeling was used to interpret the solubility differences of MCP in various NADES and to quantify the key intermolecular interactions. Representative polyphenols and all NADES constituents were first geometry‑optimized by DFT in turbomole, followed by single‑molecule COSMO calculations to generate COSMO files and obtain the surface screening charge density distribution (σ) [19]. The resulting σ‑surfaces were then processed in COSMOtherm (COSMOlogic), where mixture behavior is described by statistical thermodynamics and the interaction energy is resolved into electrostatic misfit, hydrogen‑bonding, and dispersion contributions. Solubility tendencies were predicted for each NADES, and σ‑profiles and σ‑potentials were analyzed to compare H‑bond donor/acceptor features and rationalize the observed differences among solvent systems. Optimization of extraction conditions using GSWOA-SVR model

Preliminary single-factor experiments were conducted to determine the suitable ranges of key ultrasound-assisted extraction parameters. Ultrasonic power (140, 280, 420, 560, 700, and 840 W), extraction time (10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 min), and liquid–solid ratio (10:1, 20:1, 30:1, 40:1, 50:1, and 60:1 mL/g) were selected as optimization variables based on preliminary screening and previous studies on ultrasound-assisted extraction of plant bioactive compounds. A hybrid GSWOA-SVR framework was established to optimize ultrasound-assisted extraction of polyphenols from Moutan Cortex powder. SVR was used to capture the nonlinear relationship between the extraction variables (liquid–solid ratio, ultrasonic time, and ultrasonic power) and the response variable TPC [21]. In this procedure, the whale optimization strategy performs global search in the hyperparameter space, while Gaussian mutation is introduced to maintain population diversity and reduce the likelihood of premature convergence, thereby helping the algorithm escape local optima [22]. A total of 25 experimental datasets were used for model development and were randomly divided into a training set (n = 17) and an independent testing set (n = 8). The training set was used for model construction and hyperparameter optimization, whereas the testing set was retained for independent evaluation of the finalized model. To reduce the risk of overfitting, five-fold cross-validation was performed within the training set during model optimization, with each subset serving once as the validation subset while the remaining subsets were used for model fitting. The predictive ability and robustness of the optimized model were assessed using the coefficient of determination (R2), root-mean-square error (RMSE), residual predictive deviation (RPD), and five-fold cross-validation Q2. For five-fold cross-validation, the 25 data points were divided into five subsets, and each subset was used once as the validation set following Nadimi‑Shahraki et al. [23]. The finalized model was then used to examine interaction effects among variables, and three-dimensional response surfaces were generated in MATLAB R2021b to visualize how combined factor changes influenced TPC and to identify conditions leading to higher extraction performance.

MD simulations

To elucidate the microscopic extraction mechanism and directly compare the solvation behavior of polyphenols in a NADES versus a conventional solvent, MD simulations were performed for a Bet‑LevA and a 70% ethanol system. Five representative polyphenols, i.e., gallic acid, benzoic acid, catechin, 4‑hydroxybenzoic acid, and vanillic acid, were selected as model solutes. The Bet‑LevA solvent box was constructed by randomly packing betaine and levulinic acid molecules at the target molar ratio in a cubic box using PACKMOL [24], followed by short pre-equilibration to relax unfavorable contacts. The 70% ethanol control was modeled as an ethanol–water binary mixture according to the experimental solvent composition. For each solvent environment, a solvation system was generated by inserting one polyphenol molecule into the center of the equilibrated solvent box while avoiding steric clashes; this procedure was repeated independently for each of the five polyphenols to ensure consistent comparison between Bet‑LevA and 70% ethanol.

All simulations were carried out using GROMACS. Topologies of Bet‑LevA components and polyphenols were generated using the General Amber Force Field (GAFF), and partial atomic charges were assigned using the AM1‑BCC scheme [25]. Water molecules in the 70% ethanol–water mixture were modeled using the TIP3P water model. Ethanol, betaine, levulinic acid, and the five representative polyphenols were parameterized using the GAFF force field, and their partial atomic charges were assigned using the AM1-BCC scheme, whereas water was described by the fixed-charge TIP3P model. After system construction, both the Bet-LevA and 70% ethanol–water systems were placed in cubic simulation boxes with dimensions of approximately 8 × 8 × 8 nm3, and periodic boundary conditions were applied in all three dimensions. Each system was first energy-minimized using the steepest-descent algorithm, followed by 100 ps NVT equilibration at 300 K and 100 ps NPT equilibration at 300 K and 1.0 atm. Temperature and pressure were controlled using the V-rescale thermostat and Parrinello-Rahman barostat, respectively. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the particle-mesh Ewald (PME) method with a real-space cutoff of 1.4 nm, while the van der Waals cutoff was also set to 1.4 nm. After equilibration, a 100 ns production simulation was performed under the NPT ensemble using a 2 fs integration time step. All covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm. Long-range electrostatics were treated with the particle-mesh Ewald (PME) method, and consistent real-space cutoffs were applied for both Coulombic and van der Waals interactions. Trajectory frames were saved at regular intervals for post-simulation analysis.

Trajectory analyses were performed to quantify solvent-dependent interaction patterns relevant to extraction efficiency. Radial distribution functions were calculated between key heteroatoms of each polyphenol and solvent species to characterize preferential solvation and local structuring. Hydrogen bonding between polyphenols and solvent components was quantified using geometric criteria. Hydrogen bonds were defined using a donor–acceptor distance of less than 0.35 nm and a donor–hydrogen–acceptor angle greater than 120°. The average number of hydrogen bonds was then calculated from the equilibrated trajectory segments to evaluate the solvent–solute interaction strength. In addition, non-bonded interaction energies between each polyphenol and the surrounding solvent environment were computed to compare the relative contributions of electrostatic and van der Waals interactions in Bet‑LevA versus 70% ethanol. Representative snapshots extracted from the equilibrated segments of trajectories were visualized with VMD and PyMOL to illustrate typical solvation configurations. Together, these MD descriptors were used to rationalize the enhanced affinity of Bet‑LevA toward polyphenols relative to 70% ethanol, thereby supporting the experimentally observed differences in extraction performance.

Establishment of lipid accumulation model in AML‑12 cells and Oil Red O staining

MCP refers to the Moutan Cortex polyphenol fraction obtained by Bet-LevA based ultrasound-assisted extraction followed by AB-8 macroporous resin purification. After purification, the polyphenol-rich eluate was concentrated under reduced pressure to remove ethanol and residual solvent, and the obtained MCP fraction was dried and stored at −20 °C before use. The chemical composition of MCP was characterized by UPLC-Q Exactive/MS, and the main representative phenolics included gallic acid, benzoic acid, catechin, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, and vanillic acid.

Before cell treatment, MCP was freshly dissolved in DMEM/F-12 medium and diluted to the required concentrations. A vehicle control was included to exclude the possible effect of the solvent used for MCP dissolution, and the final solvent content was kept consistent among the corresponding groups. AML‑12 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 12-well plates and cultured until reaching approximately 70–80% confluence. Before Oil Red O staining, the cytotoxicity of MCP toward AML-12 cells was evaluated using a CCK-8 assay to confirm that the selected treatment concentrations did not affect cell viability. MCP was tested at 10–1280 μg/mL, and the detailed procedure and results are provided in the Supplementary Material. The cells were divided into the control group, free‑fatty‑acid (FFA) model group, and MCP treatment groups (50, 100, and 200 μg/mL). Each group contained three replicate wells. To induce intracellular lipid accumulation, cells were exposed to a FFA mixture consisting of palmitic acid (PA, 0.25 mmol/L) and oleic acid (OA, 0.5 mmol/L), with or without MCP treatment. After 24 h of incubation, cells were subjected to subsequent analyses [26].

Intracellular lipid deposition was assessed by Oil Red O staining. An Oil Red O working solution was freshly prepared by diluting the saturated Oil Red O stock solution with distilled water at a ratio of 6:4 (v/v) and filtered before use. After treatments, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 8–10 min at room temperature, and rinsed twice with PBS. Cells were briefly pretreated with 60% isopropanol for 15–20 s and then stained with Oil Red O for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After staining, cells were differentiated with 60% isopropanol for 3–5 s, washed with distilled water, and counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were captured under a light microscope, and lipid accumulation was quantified using ImageJ software as the percentage of Oil Red O-positive staining area [27]. Oil Red O-positive regions were segmented with the Color Threshold tool based on the red staining signal, and the same threshold settings were applied to all images. The percentage of positive area was calculated as the thresholded Oil Red O-positive area divided by the total image area. All Oil Red O images in this study were analyzed using the same workflow.

Results and discussion

Screening of DESs based on solvent state and polyphenol yield

Fig. 1A outlines the integrated extraction and purification workflow used in this study. The plant material was first pulverized and sieved, followed by thermal mixing and ultrasound-assisted extraction to accelerate mass transfer. After centrifugation to remove insoluble residues, the crude extract was further processed by macroporous resin chromatography, which is a practical step for reducing matrix interferences and facilitating downstream compositional analysis. This process design is consistent with the general strategy reported for NADES ultrasound synergistic extraction systems, where ultrasound enhances cell disruption and diffusion, while solvent choice governs solute–solvent affinity and overall recovery.

Fig. 1C presents the macroscopic state of the prepared solvent systems. Differences in appearance and phase uniformity were observed among the NADESs, reflecting composition-dependent solvent structuring and likely variations in polarity and viscosity [28]. From an extraction perspective, a homogeneous and stable liquid phase is generally advantageous because it supports consistent solute–solvent contact and more reproducible mass transfer under ultrasound, whereas increased turbidity or heterogeneity may indicate less favorable microenvironments for dissolving phenolic compounds [29]. Therefore, visual inspection of solvent state was used as an initial practicality check prior to performance-based screening.

The extraction performance of the candidate solvents was subsequently evaluated using the yield of total polyphenols (Fig. 1B). The results demonstrated a pronounced dependence of MCP recovery on solvent composition. Among the tested systems, Bet-LevA achieved the highest mean MCP yield (25.87 ± 1.59 mg GAE/g DW), while ChCl-EG also exhibited a comparably high extraction efficiency (24.18 ± 1.09 mg GAE/g DW), with no significant difference between these two groups. Both NADES systems were significantly superior to the conventional extraction solvent, as 70% ethanol produced only 13.99 ± 0.68 mg GAE/g DW (p < 0.05). Quantitatively, the MCP content obtained using Bet-LevA was approximately 1.85-fold that obtained using 70% ethanol, while ChCl-EG reached approximately 1.73-fold of the 70% ethanol extract, further highlighting the advantage of NADES-assisted extraction for this matrix. This improvement is plausibly attributable to the hydrogen-bond network and tunable polarity of NADESs, which can strengthen solute–solvent interactions and facilitate the desorption and diffusion of polyphenols from the plant material, particularly under ultrasound-enhanced mass transfer [30]. Because Bet-LevA was not significantly different from ChCl-EG, its selection was based on a comprehensive consideration rather than TPC alone. Bet-LevA showed the highest mean yield, maintained a suitable liquid state, and was compatible with subsequent AB-8 resin purification and UPLC-Q Exactive/MS analysis. In addition, the betaine–levulinic acid system may provide a suitable hydrogen-bonding environment and mild acidity for the release and stabilization of phenolic compounds from Moutan Cortex. Therefore, Bet-LevA was selected for subsequent experiments.

Based on the quantitative screening outcome, Bet-LevA, as the best performing NADES, was selected for subsequent experiments. Meanwhile, 70% ethanol extraction was retained as the conventional control to enable a direct comparison and to clearly demonstrate the advantages and effectiveness of NADES-assisted extraction throughout the subsequent optimization and mechanistic evaluations.

UPLC-Q Exactive/MS fingerprinting and quantitative profiling of individual phenolics

To clarify which phenolic compounds were enhanced by the NADES extraction, the extract was characterized by UPLC-Q Exactive/MS. The representative fingerprint chromatogram (Fig. 1D) shows a rich and well resolved phenolic profile within 0–10 min. The numbered peaks in Fig. 1D correspond to the compounds identified in Table S1, indicating that the Moutan Cortex extract contains a diverse set of phenolics, mainly including phenolic acids (e.g., gallic acid, vanillic acid, caffeic acid, ferulic acid) and flavonoids (e.g., rutin, quercetin derivatives, luteolin, apigenin, kaempferol derivatives). The presence of multiple intense peaks and peak clusters (notably in the mid-retention region) suggests that the extraction system must accommodate a broad polarity range, making it suitable for evaluating solvent selectivity and overall extraction capacity.

The overlaid chromatograms (Fig. 1E) provide a direct comparison between UABL and the conventional 70% ethanol extract. Across most of the chromatographic window, UABL exhibits higher signal intensities and more pronounced responses for many peaks, implying that the NADES-ultrasound combination enhances extraction broadly rather than improving only a single marker compound. This fingerprint level enhancement is consistent with a stronger overall solubilization of phenolics and improved mass transfer under ultrasound in the Bet-LevA system. Importantly, the trend observed in the fingerprints is supported by the compound-level quantification in Table S1. For example, gallic acid (Peak 1) increased from 55,155.33 ± 432.26 ng/100 mg in the 70% ethanol extract to 73,164.23 ± 342.98 ng/100 mg in the UABL extract, indicating a substantial improvement in extracting highly polar phenolic acids. Similarly, benzoic acid (Peak 24) increased from 9,606.30 ± 425.68 to 14,499.12 ± 352.68 ng/100 mg. For flavonoids, UABL also promoted higher extraction levels: quercetin (Peak 30) increased from 19.83 ± 0.62 to 866.17 ± 24.94 ng/100 mg. Notably, some compounds showed particularly marked enrichment under UABL, such as kaempferol (Peak 37), which increased from 10.68 (70% ethanol) to 348.48 ng/100 mg (UABL), highlighting that Bet-LevA may provide a more favorable microenvironment for extracting certain less polar flavonoids.

It should be noted that 70% ethanol was extracted under the same ultrasound-assisted conditions as the NADES groups. Therefore, the comparison between 70% ethanol and Bet-LevA mainly reflected the effect of solvent type rather than differences in extraction method. Mechanistically, these consistent increases at both the fingerprint and quantitative levels can be attributed to the tailorable hydrogen-bond network and polarity of NADES [31], which can strengthen solute–solvent interactions with phenolics and facilitate desorption from the plant matrix. Under the same ultrasound-enhanced mass transfer conditions, these solvent advantages allowed Bet-LevA to achieve higher extraction contents across multiple phenolic classes [32]. Collectively, Fig. 1D and E together with Table S1 demonstrate that UABL not only improves the overall phenolic level but also enhances the extraction of a wide spectrum of individual phenolics compared with 70% ethanol.

FT-IR analysis

The molecular electrostatic potential surfaces shown in Fig. 2A provide a qualitative visualization of the charge distributions of betaine, levulinic acid, and the five representative polyphenols. The coexistence of electron-rich and electron-deficient regions suggests spatial complementarity between potential hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor sites, providing possible interaction sites between Bet-LevA and the phenolic molecules. These electrostatic potential maps therefore provide structural context for the intermolecular interactions further examined by FT-IR, COSMO-RS, and MD simulations. The formation of the Bet-LevA system was analyzed using FT-IR spectra to understand the interactions between its components as shown in Fig. 2B. The spectra were processed with routine background subtraction and baseline correction before comparison, while no peak deconvolution was applied. Therefore, the broad O-H stretching region between 3000 and 3600 cm−1 was interpreted qualitatively based on band broadening and peak shifts rather than quantitative resolution of individual hydrogen-bonding species. For betaine, the spectrum is marked by three sharp peaks between 3486 and 3208 cm−1 that represent the hydrogen bonding within its carboxyl groups. It also shows key signals such as the C=O vibration at 1621 cm−1 and the carboxylate group at 1394 cm−1. In contrast, levulinic acid is characterized by a free O-H peak at 3583 cm−1 and a strong carbonyl C=O signal at 1737 cm−1. These peaks serve as the fingerprints for the individual raw materials before they combine.

When betaine and levulinic acid are mixed to form the Bet-LevA, several significant changes occur that indicate the creation of a new substance. The sharp triple peaks of betaine disappear completely, and the O-H signals of levulinic acid shift to new positions at 3580 and 2535 cm−1. This transformation suggests that the original bonds within the individual molecules were broken and replaced by a more stable and uniform hydrogen-bond network between the two components. This new network causes the O–H vibrations to move to lower frequencies and become broader.

Further evidence of this interaction is seen in the carbonyl group of levulinic acid, which moves from 1737 to 1720 cm−1 in the Bet-LevA mixture. This shift happens because the carbonyl group starts acting as a hydrogen bond acceptor, which reduces its vibration frequency and intensity. Similarly, the carboxylate peaks of betaine shift to 1617 and 1400 cm−1 due to strong interactions with the acid. We also observed that the C-O signal of levulinic acid splits into two peaks at 1214 and 1164 cm−1, meaning the movement of these bonds is now more restricted within the new solvent environment. Importantly, no new peaks representing covalent bonds appeared in the spectrum. This confirms that betaine and levulinic acid are held together solely by non-covalent hydrogen bonds, creating a stable and green extraction medium.

Morphological analysis of Moutan Cortex powders

SEM was used to compare the microstructural features of Moutan Cortex powders subjected to different extraction treatments (Fig. 2C–E), in order to provide physical evidence for the enhanced polyphenol extraction achieved by the Bet-LevA system. The untreated powder (Fig. 2C) exhibited relatively compact and aggregated particles with smoother and more intact surfaces at both magnifications, indicating that the plant matrix remained largely preserved. Such a dense morphology generally limits solvent accessibility, as fewer fissures and channels are available for solvent infiltration, and phenolics embedded within cell-wall networks or retained in internal compartments are less readily released [33].

After ultrasound-assisted extraction with 70% ethanol (Fig. 2D), the residue showed rougher surfaces, partial erosion, peeling, and fragmentation. These structural changes can be partly attributed to acoustic cavitation generated during sonication. The formation and collapse of cavitation bubbles produce localized microjets, shock waves, and shear forces, which can disrupt the plant matrix, increase the accessible surface area, and facilitate solvent penetration and solute diffusion [34]. Nevertheless, large areas still appeared relatively cohesive and compact, implying that mass transfer resistance within the solid matrix was not fully eliminated. This incomplete disruption is consistent with an extraction process in which solvent penetration and diffusion pathways are improved only to a certain extent, thereby constraining the release of polyphenols.

In contrast, the powder residue after Bet-LevA-assisted ultrasound extraction (Fig. 2E) displayed the most pronounced morphological disruption, characterized by clear cracking, layer collapse, and a more porous and loosened texture. The highly irregular and fractured surface indicates that the structural integrity of the plant matrix was substantially weakened, which would further increase the effective contact area between the solvent and solid phase and provide more diffusion routes for solute migration [35]. Such microstructural opening can facilitate deeper solvent penetration and promote the desorption and dissolution of polyphenols, thereby accelerating their transport from the interior of the particles into the surrounding solvent [36]. Compared with 70% ethanol, the more extensive structural disruption observed after Bet-LevA extraction suggests that the favorable properties of Bet-LevA further enhanced solvent-matrix interactions under the same ultrasound conditions. Quantitative SEM image analysis further supported these morphological observations (Fig. S4). The apparent pore/crack area fraction increased significantly after extraction compared with the untreated material and was highest in the Bet-LevA-treated group. This result confirms that Bet-LevA caused more extensive matrix opening and structural disruption than 70% ethanol.

Therefore, the enhanced extraction efficiency of the Bet-LevA system can be attributed to the complementary effects of ultrasound and the NADES solvent. Ultrasound primarily promotes physical matrix disruption and solid–liquid mass transfer through acoustic cavitation, mechanical erosion, and enhanced solvent circulation, whereas Bet-LevA provides a favorable solvent environment for the dissolution and stabilization of the released polyphenols through strong intermolecular interactions. The SEM observations therefore support a synergistic mechanism in which ultrasound facilitates the release and transport of polyphenols from the Moutan Cortex matrix, while Bet-LevA enhances their subsequent solubilization, ultimately contributing to the higher TPC obtained with the Bet-LevA-assisted ultrasound extraction system.

COSMO-RS model and exploration of extraction mechanism

To further interpret the experimental solvent screening results, COSMO-RS analysis was conducted using the same eight NADES systems evaluated in Fig. 1B. Bet-LevA had already been identified as the best-performing NADES based on the actual MCP yield. Therefore, the COSMO-RS calculation was not used as the sole criterion for solvent selection, but as a complementary molecular-level analysis to explain why Bet-LevA showed superior extraction performance. The remaining seven NADESs were included as comparative systems to evaluate their relative compatibility with representative phenolic compounds.

COSMO-RS model for verifying the solubility of polyphenols

To rationalize why Bet-LevA outperformed the other solvent systems in extracting phenolics, a COSMO-RS model was applied to predict the relative solubility of representative polyphenols in the candidate NADES formulations. COSMO-RS combines quantum chemistry derived surface polarization information with statistical thermodynamics to estimate solute–solvent affinity [37]; therefore, higher predicted solubility can be interpreted as stronger molecular compatibility between the solvent microenvironment and the target polyphenols. In this work, based on the UPLC-Q Exactive/MS profiling, five major phenolic constituents (gallic acid, catechin, benzoic acid, 4‑hydroxybenzoic acid, and vanillic acid) were identified as the dominant phenolics in Moutan Cortex; collectively, these compounds accounted for more than 90% of the total quantified phenolics. Therefore, they were selected as representative probe molecules for COSMO‑RS prediction, and their solubilities were compared across different NADES formulations (Fig. 3A–B).

COSMO-RS based solubility prediction and molecular interaction analysis. (A) Predicted solubility of five representative polyphenols in various NADES systems. (B) Heatmap showing the solubility distribution of target solutes across different solvent environments. (C–E) σ-profiles of NADES and five representative polyphenols. (F–H) σ-potential of NADES and five representative polyphenols.

As shown in Fig. 3A, the predicted solubility of the five phenolics varied substantially with NADES composition, indicating clear solvent selectivity. Among the tested systems, Bet-LevA consistently provided high solubility values for most compounds, and it showed the most pronounced advantage particularly for the more hydrophilic phenolics (e.g., gallic acid and catechin). By contrast, several other NADES combinations exhibited lower predicted solubilities for the same solutes, implying weaker solute–solvent interactions and a reduced driving force for dissolution [38]. Fig. 3B was presented as a heatmap to provide a more intuitive comparison of which phenolic compound shows higher predicted solubility in which NADES, and to further reveal the solvent selectivity governed by different HBA or HBD combinations. As visualized by the color intensity, catechin and gallic acid generally exhibited relatively high solubility in several NADES systems, whereas benzoic acid and vanillic acid showed a clearer dependence on solvent composition.

σ-surface and σ-profile analysis

To further elucidate the molecular origin of the different solubilization capacities among the tested NADES, COSMO-RS σ-profile and σ-potential analyses were performed for both solvents and major phenolics (Fig. 3C–H). The σ-profile describes how polar (hydrogen-bonding) and nonpolar surface segments are distributed distributed on a molecule: the negative σ region mainly reflects HBD type surface (σ < −0.0084 e/Å2), the positive σ region corresponds to HBA type surface (σ > 0.0084 e/Å2), while the near zero σ region represents nonpolar segments (−0.0084 < σ < 0.0084 e/Å2) [39]. As shown in Fig. 3C–D, changing either the HBA or the HBD markedly reshaped the σ-profiles of the solvents, indicating that the hydrogen-bonding microenvironment of NADES is highly composition-dependent. In particular, Bet-LevA exhibited a relatively stronger presence in both negative and positive σ regions compared with most other formulations, suggesting a more balanced ability to provide and accept hydrogen bonds, which is beneficial for accommodating polyphenols bearing multiple hydroxyl and carboxyl functionalities [40]. The σ-profiles of the five dominant phenolics are presented in Fig. 3E. Gallic acid and catechin display broader and more intense polar distributions, consistent with their multiple hydroxyl groups and strong hydrogen bonding demand, whereas benzoic acid and vanillic acid show comparatively weaker or narrower polar features, implying greater sensitivity to solvent composition. This helps explain why the same NADES can perform well for one phenolic but less effectively for another: the match between the solute’s polar surface pattern and the solvent’s available hydrogen bonding segments is critical [41]. The σ-potential (Fig. 3F–H) provide the energetic perspective of these contacts. More favorable (more negative) values in the hydrogen-bonding regions indicate that forming contacts with polar surface segments is energetically preferred, translating into stronger solute–solvent affinity [42]. Consistent with the solubility trends, Bet-LevA shows a more favorable interaction tendency across the polar σ regions, meaning it can stabilize both donor and acceptor type sites on phenolics more effectively. Overall, the σ-profile and σ-potential analyses jointly suggest that the superior solubilization of the major MCP is mainly driven by composition tunable hydrogen bond complementarity, and Bet-LevA provides a particularly compatible polarity and hydrogen-bonding environment for these compounds.

Taken together, the COSMO-RS results were in agreement with the experimental extraction data. Compared with the other seven NADESs, Bet-LevA showed more favorable molecular matching with the representative phenolic compounds, as reflected by its σ-surface, σ-profile, σ-potential, and predicted solubility tendency. These results provide mechanistic support for the high MCP yield obtained with Bet-LevA in Fig. 1B.

MD analysis

To gain molecular-level insights into the superior extraction performance of Bet-LevA, 100 ns MD simulations were conducted. The dynamic evolution of the 70% EtOH and Bet-LevA systems, along with their interactions with five representative polyphenols, was systematically evaluated (Fig. 4).

MD simulation and interaction energy analysis. (A) MD simulation of the 70% EtOH system: (a–c) representative snapshots at 0, 50, and 100 ns; (d) evolution of the number of hydrogen bonds between the solvent and each representative polyphenol; (e) evolution of the total number of hydrogen bonds between the solvent and all polyphenols (All); (f) Van der Waals interaction energy. (B) MD simulation of the Bet-LevA system: (g–i) representative snapshots at 0, 50, and 100 ns; (j) evolution of the number of hydrogen bonds between the solvent and each representative polyphenol; (k) evolution of the total number of hydrogen bonds between the solvent and all polyphenols (All); (l) Van der Waals interaction energy. (C) Comparison of average total interaction energies between 70% EtOH and Bet-LevA for five representative polyphenols, *** indicate statistical significance (p < 0.001).

The MD snapshots (Fig. 4a–c, g–i) demonstrated that all polyphenol molecules remained stably dispersed within the solvent matrices throughout the simulation. Quantitative analysis of direct polyphenol-solvent hydrogen bonding (Fig. 4d, e, j, k) showed that the 70% EtOH system maintained a higher number of polyphenol-solvent H-bonds than the Bet-LevA system. Importantly, the hydrogen-bond numbers reported here include only contacts between the representative polyphenols and solvent molecules; solvent–solvent hydrogen bonds were excluded from the analysis. The higher number observed in 70% EtOH may reflect the greater availability and rapid exchange of hydroxyl-containing ethanol and water molecules around the hydrogen-bonding sites of the polyphenols. Nevertheless, hydrogen-bond number is a geometric descriptor and does not directly represent the total strength of solvent–solute interactions. Therefore, the hydrogen-bond results were interpreted together with the interaction-energy analysis [43].

The decisive advantage of Bet-LevA was elucidated through the analysis of interaction energies. As shown in Fig. 4f and l, Bet-LevA exhibited significantly more negative van der Waals (vdW) interaction energies across all time scales compared to 70% EtOH. To further assess the statistical uncertainty of these interaction energies, block averaging was performed on the equilibrated 20–100 ns trajectories, and the corresponding mean ± SD values are provided in Fig. S3. This indicates that the structural components of the NADES (Betaine and Levulinic acid) provide a more compatible hydrophobic environment and better spatial fit for the aromatic rings of the polyphenols [33].

The average total interaction energy further confirmed the thermodynamic superiority of the NADES system (Fig. 4C). Despite having fewer H-bonds, Bet-LevA showed substantially stronger binding affinities for all five polyphenols, with energy increases ranging from −183.62 kJ/mol (Vanillic acid) to a remarkable −751.16 kJ/mol (Catechin). These results indicate that the enhanced vdW forces and overall interaction energy in the Bet-LevA system effectively compensate for the lower H-bond density. This robust thermodynamic affinity facilitates more efficient mass transfer and stabilizes the polyphenols within the solvent, providing a rigorous molecular explanation for the experimental results showing that the extraction rate of Bet-LevA is higher than 70% ethanol. Notably, the 70% ethanol system exhibited a relatively high hydrogen-bond number in the MD simulation, mainly due to the presence of water molecules in the ethanol–water mixed solvent. This result supports that water participated in the simulated solvent network and contributed to hydrogen-bond formation. However, the MD simulation remains a simplified molecular model focusing on solvent–solute interactions. It does not fully reproduce plant matrix effects, ultrasound-induced cavitation, cell wall disruption, or diffusion during practical extraction. Therefore, the MD results should be interpreted as molecular-level support for the experimental extraction trend rather than as a complete simulation of the entire extraction process.

Optimization of UABL extraction conditions using the GSWOA‑SVR model

Effects of key extraction variables on total polyphenol content

Fig. 5A–C illustrates how ultrasonic power, ultrasonic time, and the liquid–solid ratio shaped the TPC content. For ultrasonic power (Fig. 5A), TPC increased strongly when power was raised from 140 to 560 W, reaching about 25.28 ± 0.54 mg GAE/g DW. This increase can be attributed to the enhanced acoustic cavitation generated by higher ultrasonic power. Stronger cavitation promotes the disruption of plant cell walls, improves solvent penetration into the plant matrix, and accelerates the diffusion of polyphenols into the NADES phase. However, when the ultrasonic power was further increased to 700–840 W, TPC showed a slight decrease. Excessively high ultrasonic intensity may cause localized heating, promote degradation or oxidation of heat-sensitive phenolic compounds, and increase the co-extraction of non-target components, thereby reducing the measurable TPC. Therefore, moderate ultrasonic power is more favorable for balancing extraction enhancement and polyphenol stability [44].

GSWOA-SVR model development and predictive performance. (A–C) Effects of single factors on TPC: (A) ultrasonic power, (B) ultrasonic time, and (C) liquid–solid ratio. (D) Regression plot showing the correlation between target and output values. (E) Fitting performance for the training set. (F) Fitting performance for all samples. (G) Comparison between actual experimental values and GSWOA-SVR predicted values across forecast samples. (H) Distribution of absolute errors for the training set. (I) Convergence curve of the GSWOA algorithm.

Ultrasonic time showed a similar practical optimum (Fig. 5B). Extending the treatment from 10 to 40 min steadily improved TPC, with the maximum close to 25.94 ± 0.81 mg GAE/g DW at about 40 min. This is expected because a longer treatment simply gives the solvent more opportunity to wash polyphenols out of the plant matrix. However, increasing the time beyond 40 min did not improve the outcome and even led to a small drop at 50–60 min. A reasonable explanation is that most readily extractable polyphenols had already been released by then, so extra time brings little new material, while prolonged sonication increases the chance of chemical changes that can lower the measurable TPC [45]. From a processing viewpoint, 40 min is therefore a good balance between extraction efficiency and unnecessary time input.

The liquid–solid ratio also had a clear optimum (Fig. 5C). Raising the ratio from 10:1 to 40:1 increased TPC from roughly 16.91 ± 13.3 to about 25.62 ± 0.71 mg GAE/g DW. Beyond 40:1, however, the benefit disappeared and TPC slightly declined at 50:1–60:1. A sufficient amount of solvent can reduce mass-transfer resistance and promote the dissolution and diffusion of polyphenols. However, when the liquid-to-solid ratio exceeded 40:1, TPC no longer increased and slightly declined at higher ratios. Once the solvent amount is sufficient, additional solvent may mainly dilute the extraction system and reduce the effective ultrasonic energy density per unit volume. Taken together, these trends indicate that TPC is governed by non-linear responses with clear optimal regions rather than simple “the more, the better” which supports the need for a data-driven optimization approach in the subsequent GSWOA-SVR modeling.

Development and performance of the GSWOA-SVR model

Because the three key factors (ultrasonic power, ultrasonic time, and liquid–solid ratio) affect TPC in a clearly non-linear way, we built a SVR model to learn the relationship between extraction conditions and TPC. SVR is well suited for this type of problem because it can fit curved, non-linear trends without forcing a simple straight-line relationship. To make the SVR model perform as well as possible, its main hyperparameters were tuned using an improved Whale Optimization Algorithm, namely GSWOA, where a Gaussian strategy was introduced to enhance the search behavior. In simple terms, this hybrid approach lets the algorithm explore the parameter space more efficiently and reduces the risk of stopping at a sub‑optimal setting.

The agreement between the measured and predicted values is shown in Fig. 5D–F. The regression plot (Fig. 5D) indicates a strong linear consistency between model outputs and experimental targets, with a high correlation coefficient (R = 0.99378) and a fitted relationship close to the ideal one. This means the model tracks changes in TPC very well across the studied range, while showing only a small overall bias. In the parity plots, the training set (Fig. 5E) points lie almost on the 1:1 line, giving R2 = 0.99891 and a very small calibration error (RMSEC = 0.069257), which indicates that the model can describe the experimental data with high precision. More importantly, the model also performed well when considering all samples (Fig. 5F). The overall fitting remained strong, with R2 = 0.98305 and a low prediction error (RMSEP = 0.22394). In practical terms, the model’s typical deviation from the measured TPC is small compared with the response range in this study (around 20–27 mg GAE/g DW), meaning it can be used with confidence for condition screening and optimization rather than only for describing the existing data.

Although RSM is widely used for extraction optimization because of its simplicity and interpretability, its quadratic polynomial structure may limit its ability to describe complex nonlinear extraction processes. Recent studies have shown that intelligent optimization models, such as GA-ANN, GA-ACO-BP, and SVR-based algorithms, can provide improved prediction performance for the extraction of bioactive compounds [46], [47], [48], [49]. To further evaluate the predictive advantage of the proposed model, a conventional RSM model was additionally constructed using the same extraction variables and TPC response. As summarized in Table S3, GSWOA-SVR exhibited a higher R2 and RPD together with lower RMSE and MAE values than RSM. Moreover, the experimentally verified TPC obtained under the GSWOA-SVR-predicted optimum showed closer agreement with the corresponding predicted value. These results suggest that GSWOA-SVR provided improved predictive capability for the nonlinear extraction behavior within the investigated experimental domain, while RSM remained a useful conventional and interpretable benchmark. Therefore, GSWOA-SVR was applied here as a nonlinear prediction and optimization tool to better capture the relationship between extraction variables and TPC yield. In this study, the response surface plots were mainly used to visualize the effects of extraction variables, whereas the GSWOA-SVR model was used for quantitative prediction and optimization. Fig. 5G–H provides a more intuitive view of model reliability. The predicted curve follows the experimental curve closely across samples (Fig. 5G), and the quantitative indicators confirm this agreement (R2 = 0.99884, RMSE = 0.071103). The absolute errors shown in Fig. 5H fluctuate narrowly around zero with no obvious drift, suggesting that errors are mostly random rather than systematic [48]. This is a useful sign because systematic errors usually imply that the model consistently overestimates or underestimates under certain conditions, which would be risky for optimization.

Finally, the optimization process itself converged quickly (Fig. 5I). The fitness value decreased rapidly in the early iterations and then stabilized after roughly 30–40 iterations, indicating that GSWOA was able to locate a stable near‑optimal parameter region without excessive computation. Taken together, these results show that the GSWOA‑SVR model provides both accurate fitting and reliable prediction, supporting its use as a quantitative tool to guide the optimization of ultrasonic extraction conditions for maximizing TPC.

Response surface analysis and experimental validation of the optimum conditions

Fig. 6A–C shows the response surface plots built from the optimized SVR model described in Section 3.7. These plots help visualize how the extraction factors work together, rather than looking at each factor one at a time. In Fig. 6A, TPC increases when both power and time move from low to moderate levels, and the surface peak appears around the power range and 40 min. The key interaction message is that increasing power is more helpful at a suitable extraction time, and extending time is more effective when power is not too low; however, pushing both too high leads to a slight decline, suggesting diminishing returns under overly intense conditions[50]. Fig. 6B shows that a higher liquid–solid ratio improves TPC more clearly when the time is around the middle range. When time is too short, increasing solvent ratio cannot fully compensate for insufficient extraction time; when time is too long, the benefit of extra solvent becomes limited. This indicates that solvent amount and time need to be matched to reach the peak region. In Fig. 6C, TPC increases when power and liquid–solid ratio rise together toward moderate values, but the surface does not keep rising at the highest settings. This means extra power works best when there is enough solvent to carry released polyphenols into solution, while excessive power or excessive solvent does not lead to proportional gains [51]. It should be noted that the response surfaces in Fig. 6A-C were generated primarily to visualize pairwise interaction trends and the approximate high-response region. Each surface represents a two-variable slice of the three-variable SVR response space, with the remaining variable fixed at the corresponding reference level. Therefore, the visually estimated peak of an individual response surface should not be interpreted as the exact global optimum obtained when all three variables are optimized simultaneously.

(D) Evaluation of lipid-lowering effects in AML-12 hepatocytes: (a) Control group; (b) FFA group (cells treated with free-fatty-acids to induce lipid accumulation); (c, d, e) cells co-treated with FFA and varying concentrations of MCP (50, 100, and 200 μg/mL); (f) Quantitative analysis of Oil Red O staining area percentage. Based on the numerical optimization of the finalized GSWOA-SVR model described in Section 3.7, the exact predicted optimum was 557 W ultrasonic power, 41.4 min extraction time, and a liquid-to-solid ratio of 42:1 mL/g, corresponding to a predicted TPC of 27.23 mg GAE/g DW. These conditions fall within the broad high-response region visualized in Fig. 6A–C. Considering equipment resolution and practical operation, the validation experiment was performed at the closest feasible settings of 560 W, 41 min, and 42:1 mL/g, yielding an experimental TPC of 27.03 ± 0.41 mg GAE/g DW. The close agreement between the predicted and measured values supports the reliability of the optimized operating conditions.

UABL extracted MCP alleviates fatty acid-induced steatosis in AML‑12 cells

The CCK-8 assay showed that MCP did not obviously reduce AML-12 cell viability within the concentration range covering the Oil Red O experiment. Cell viability remained stable at 40–320 μg/mL MCP, while a significant effect was observed only at much higher concentrations (Fig. S2). Therefore, the reduction in Oil Red O-positive staining area in MCP-treated cells was not caused by cytotoxicity. And Fig. 6D is designed to show, in a very direct way, whether the MCP obtained from the Bet‑LevA extraction process can reduce fat build up inside liver cells. In this experiment, AML‑12 hepatocytes were first induced a lipid accumulation state by free fatty acids. This state mimics a fatty liver like condition at the cellular level, where excess fatty acids enter the cells and are stored as lipid droplets. Oil Red O staining was then used to visualize and quantify these lipid droplets, because the dye specifically stains neutral lipids and makes intracellular fat appear as red deposits under the microscope [52], [53].

As shown in Fig. 6D (a-f), the control cells exhibited minimal Oil Red O staining, indicating low baseline lipid storage under normal culture conditions. When free fatty acid treatment without MCP, the cells displayed a strong increase in red staining, confirming that the lipid accumulation model was successfully established. In contrast, when cells were treated with MCP (50, 100, and 200 μg/mL), the intensity and coverage of Oil Red O‑positive areas visibly decreased. Importantly, this reduction followed a clear dose dependent trend: higher MCP concentrations produced fewer and smaller lipid droplets, suggesting that MCP can effectively counteract fatty acid induced lipid deposition in hepatocytes. The quantitative analysis in Fig. 6D(f) supports the visual observations. The percentage of Oil Red O‑positive staining area was highest in the FFA model group and progressively decreased with MCP at 50, 100, and 200 μg/mL. Different letters above the bars indicate statistically significant differences among groups, showing that the lipid lowering effect is not only visible but also measurable and reliable across replicates. Together, these results indicate that MCP treatment can significantly reduce intracellular lipid accumulation in AML‑12 cells under fatty acid challenge.

From a practical perspective, this finding is meaningful because excessive lipid storage in hepatocytes is a key early event in fatty liver development and metabolic disorders. The ability of Bet-LevA-extracted MCP to reduce lipid droplet deposition suggests potential value for further development as a functional polyphenol-rich ingredient. However, the present AML-12 model represents an initial in vitro functional assessment and does not establish comparative superiority over extracts obtained using other extraction methods. Further studies involving mechanistic biomarkers, animal models, and extraction-method-matched bioactivity comparisons will be necessary to evaluate its broader physiological relevance.

Conclusions

This study developed a green and efficient ultrasound-assisted extraction strategy for recovering polyphenols from Moutan Cortex using a Bet-LevA natural deep eutectic solvent. Through systematic NADES screening and GSWOA-SVR-assisted optimization, the nonlinear relationships among liquid-to-solid ratio, ultrasonic power, extraction time, and extraction efficiency were successfully characterized, and the optimized conditions (560 W, 41 min, and 42:1 mL/g) were experimentally validated, achieving a total polyphenol content of 27.03 ± 0.41 mg GAE/g DW, which was 1.93-fold higher than that obtained using 70% ethanol extraction. UPLC-Q Exactive/MS analysis further demonstrated that Bet-LevA-assisted UAE enhanced the recovery of diverse phenolic acids and flavonoids rather than selectively enriching individual compounds.

Beyond process optimization, this study provided molecular-level insights into the superior extraction performance of Bet-LevA through integrated FT-IR characterization, COSMO-RS prediction, and molecular dynamics simulations. These analyses revealed that the formation of a stable hydrogen-bonding network and stronger solvent–polyphenol interactions, including favorable hydrogen-bonding and van der Waals interactions, contributed to improved solubilization, mass transfer, and stabilization of extracted polyphenols during ultrasound-assisted extraction. Furthermore, functional validation using an AML-12 hepatocyte lipid accumulation model indicated that the obtained Moutan Cortex polyphenol extract retained biological activity and effectively alleviated fatty acid-induced lipid accumulation.

Collectively, this study establishes a comprehensive framework integrating green solvent design, intelligent process optimization, molecular mechanism elucidation, and functional evaluation, providing a sustainable strategy for the high-value utilization of Moutan Cortex in functional food and nutraceutical development. Nevertheless, further studies are still required to evaluate the in vivo bioavailability and physiological effects of the extracted polyphenols, investigate the recycling and scale-up feasibility of the NADES extraction system, and verify the transferability of the GSWOA-SVR model to other plant matrices and bioactive compound extraction processes.
