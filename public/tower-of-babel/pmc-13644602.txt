At-home subcutaneous atezolizumab for non-small cell lung cancer: a prospective, single-arm phase II feasibility study
Authors: Lily Yang, Sana Iman Mutalib, Elizabeth Qi, Andrew Zeng, Jacob Thomas, Robert Hsu, Peter Kuhn, Luciano Nocera, Jorge Nieva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeFrequent in-clinic cancer treatment can be burdensome, particularly for older adults and patients with limited mobility. This study aimed to assess the safety, feasibility, and patient experience of at-home subcutaneous (SC) atezolizumab injection administration in patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).Patients and MethodsThis feasibility study included five patients with NSCLC treated with atezolizumab SC injections (1,875 mg every 21 days). The first three treatment cycles were administered in clinic, followed by nurse-administered home treatment. Patients underwent routine laboratory testing, telehealth visits, and standard disease monitoring. Safety, injection timeliness, treatment compliance, and patient satisfaction were evaluated over a total of 70 treatment cycles.ResultsOf 70 total SC injections, 65 (93%) were delivered on time. No delays were attributable to atezolizumab SC administration. Two of five patients (40%) experienced an adverse event. Home administration required a shorter measured administration duration than in-clinic treatment (42.3 vs. 86.7 minutes). Digital monitoring engagement was limited. Mean Likert scores showed high overall satisfaction with drug administration (3.3/4) and preference for subcutaneous over intravenous administration (3.2/4).ConclusionAt-home atezolizumab SC administration was feasible in this small feasibility study and was associated with high patient satisfaction and shorter measured administration time.Clinical Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier is NCT05340309.Keywords: Subcutaneous atezolizumab, non-small cell lung cancer, immunotherapy, home administration, digital patient monitoring

Introduction

The delivery of systemic cancer therapy at home is an increasingly explored alternative to traditional in-clinic administration, with the potential to reduce healthcare costs, alleviate time burdens on patients and healthcare providers and facilities, and enhance quality of life. This model has been shown to be safe, effective, and widely preferred by patients across various cancer types and treatment modalities [1]. In a systematic review of 24 studies, home-based parenteral chemotherapy was associated with high patient satisfaction (92%) and preference (88%), without compromising safety or increasing adverse events1. More recently, subcutaneous immunotherapy has emerged as a strategy that may facilitate home-based oncology care, with studies of subcutaneous atezolizumab demonstrating comparable efficacy and safety to intravenous administration and greater patient preference for the subcutaneous route [12,13].

In the United States, lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related death, with an estimated 229,410 new cases and 124,990 deaths projected in 2026 [2]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) comprises nearly 85% of these cases and is often treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors like atezolizumab, a programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitor [3,4]. The burden of frequent infusion center-based administrations, including travel time, waiting between lab draws, clinic visit, infusion center registration, and chair time in the infusion center, can be significant, particularly for older adults and those with limited mobility or access to care [5]. This “time toxicity” of traditional treatment models has led to a growing interest in home-based delivery of systemic therapies, particularly in the context of SC immunotherapies, which offer shorter administration times and easier logistics compared to intravenous regimens [1,6].

Atezolizumab SC injections have demonstrated efficacy as both monotherapy and in combination regimens for NSCLC, including in patients ineligible for platinum-based chemotherapy [7]. Recent developments have also explored the integration of digital patient monitoring (DPM) systems to support remote administration, allowing for real-time symptom tracking, adherence monitoring, and early detection of adverse events [8]. More broadly, advances in precision oncology and targeted therapeutic strategies continue to reshape the delivery of systemic cancer treatment, including approaches that improve patient convenience while maintaining treatment quality [9–11].

This prospective, single-center Phase II feasibility study evaluated home administration of atezolizumab SC using a decentralized clinical trial model in patients with NSCLC. This report presents five patients with NSCLC who received atezolizumab SC monotherapy at home. We discuss the clinical course, safety outcomes, and logistical considerations associated with home administration, providing insight into the feasibility of this evolving treatment approach.

Methods

This study enrolled five patients (labeled 101, 102, 103, 104, 105) with NSCLC with indications for atezolizumab SC injection treatment as monotherapy. Patients were prospectively enrolled between November 2022 and April 2024 at USC Norris Comprehensive Cancer Center under an IRB-approved protocol (Protocol No. HS-22-00073). Major eligibility criteria included adults with NSCLC who had tumors with high PD-L1 expression (TPS ≥ 50% and IHC cutoff 50%) or did not respond well to first-line platinum-based chemotherapy, ECOG performance status 0–2, adequate organ function, and the ability to participate in home monitoring. Key exclusion criteria included untreated brain metastases, active autoimmune disease requiring immunosuppression, uncontrolled infection, or prior severe hypersensitivity to atezolizumab. The co-primary endpoints were safety and feasibility of home administration. The prespecified safety endpoint was the incidence of grade 3 or higher nonhematologic adverse events through 30 days after the last dose. Feasibility was assessed by the successful completion of home administration visits within the study-defined timing window, defined as within 1 day of the planned 21-day interval. Secondary endpoints included patient and nurse satisfaction. Exploratory endpoints included digital monitoring, physical activity, and administration time assessments.

Atezolizumab SC injections were administered in fixed doses of 1875 mg every 21 days by trained mobile nurses via a subcutaneous catheter placed in the anterior thigh using the Koru Freedom Edge Pump. The pump delivered the 15-mL dose over approximately 3–8 minutes. The first three doses were administered in clinic to establish tolerability before transition to home administration. Starting at cycle 4, home SC injections were performed by trained mobile nurses who received protocol-specific instruction on use of the Koru Freedom Edge pump, with telephone support from USC study nurses during business hours and the principal investigator after hours. Patients had labs done 2 days before each home administration at a convenient site. Vital signs were obtained within 60 minutes before each administration, and patients were instructed to report any delayed post- administration symptoms to the study physician. Telemedicine visits with the treating oncologist were conducted on the day of or day before each at-home administration. In the event of suspected anaphylaxis during home administration, nurses followed protocol-defined emergency procedures including immediate discontinuation of the administration, intramuscular epinephrine administration if indicated, and activation of emergency medical services. Tumor assessments were performed every 12 weeks, which included CT scans, MRI scans, and bone scans if indicated.

To assess timeliness of administration, each scheduled dose was recorded as either “on time” or “delayed.” A dose was considered “on time” if it was administered within 1 day of the planned 21-day interval. Delays were categorized by cause based on nurse documentation.

Adverse events (AEs) were assessed throughout the treatment and follow-up period using patient reports, nurse observations, laboratory assessment, and telehealth physician assessments. AEs were coded and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. Grades ranged from 1 (mild) to 5 (death related to AE). Clinically relevant treatment-emergent AEs were recorded and summarized according to CTCAE grade, clinical management, and outcome.

Administration time was assessed by comparing the duration of in-clinic visits vs time spent during administration at home visits. In-clinic time was measured from patient check-in at the infusion center to completion of treatment. Home administration time was defined as the time the infusion nurse spent in the patient’s home administering treatment. Because these measures capture different components of the treatment process, they were analyzed descriptively and were not intended to represent equivalent measures of total treatment time. Travel time, laboratory testing, telemedicine visits, drug preparation and delivery, and nurse travel time were not included in either measure.

Patients were given wearable Fitbit fitness trackers and instructed to wear the device for 16 hours daily and to charge it for 8 hours while sleeping. Fitbit usage data were automatically uploaded to secure Fitbit servers. Fitbit compliance was quantified as a percentage per cycle by dividing the number of compliant days in the cycle by the total number of days in the cycle. A day was considered compliant if the patient wore it for at least 16 hours.

Patients were instructed to measure their vital signs including blood pressure, temperature, and oxygen saturation at least once per day. Vitals compliance was quantified as a percentage per cycle by dividing the number of compliant days in the cycle by the total number of days in the cycle. A day was considered compliant if they uploaded their vitals at least once.

Weekly surveys were supposed to be filled out by the patients to assess any adverse events they were experiencing from treatment. Survey compliance was quantified as a percentage per cycle by dividing the number of compliant weeks in the cycle by the total number of weeks in the cycle. A week was considered compliant if the patient completed the survey at least once that week.

Satisfaction surveys were administered electronically to patients at predefined time points. Patients were asked to rate the following statements via a Likert scale score:

“I was satisfied with the drug administration process today.”

“I was satisfied with the drug administration process today.”

“I prefer subcutaneous infusions to intravenous infusions.”

“I prefer subcutaneous infusions to intravenous infusions.”

“My drug administration today was comfortable.”

“My drug administration today was comfortable.”

“My drug administration today was convenient.”

“My drug administration today was convenient.”

Satisfaction surveys were collected at multiple study time points. Because the planned cycle 3 and cycle 6 schedule was not consistently implemented, the 17 available responses were analyzed descriptively as repeated measurements rather than as cycle-specific assessments. Healthcare-provider and mobile-nurse satisfaction was a prespecified secondary endpoint but was not collected; therefore, this outcome could not be analyzed.

Timeliness of SC injection

Among the 70 administrations, 15 were administered in the clinic and 55 were administered at home. Of the 55 administrations, 50 (90.9%) occurred within the study-defined timing window and five (9.1%) were delayed. At the patient level, three of five patients (60%) completed all home administrations without delay. Of the five late administrations of atezolizumab SC injections, two were attributable to medical complications unrelated to atezolizumab: an upper respiratory infection and an elevated serum creatinine level. One was due to a courier failure where the atezolizumab was not delivered on time, and two were due to the patient being out of state and unable to receive the atezolizumab (Table 2). The delay due to courier failure resulted in drug wastage and rescheduling of the treatment. No patient doses were missed because of drug preparation or administration errors.

Safety

Two of five patients (40%) experienced an adverse event, corresponding to 2 of the 70 total administrations (2.9%). One patient developed pruritus during one cycle of their treatment (CTCAE Grade 1), but it resolved with loratadine 10 mg. They were able to continue with the rest of their treatment following this event without dose delays (Table 3). Another patient developed severe neutropenia and likely immune related adverse event secondary to atezolizumab (CTCAE Grade 4), prompting a hold on treatment (Table 3). They subsequently underwent endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) with biopsy, which identified a new left lower lobe lung nodule consistent with mucinous adenocarcinoma with enteric features that was histologically distinct from the original tumor and was therefore classified as a new primary lung cancer rather than progression of the original malignancy. The patient subsequently discontinued the clinical trial. No patient experienced a grade 3 or higher nonhematologic adverse event through 30 days after the last dose (0/5 patients; 0%), meeting the definition of the prespecified primary safety endpoint. The grade 4 neutropenia was hematologic and therefore did not meet this endpoint.

Comparison of clinic and home administration times

For the first 3 cycles of administrations in the cancer center, the patients spent a mean of 86.7 minutes (range 35.0–158.0 minutes; median 90.0 minutes) in the office. For the subsequent home administrations, the nurses spent a mean of 42.3 minutes (range 17.0–80.0 minutes; median 42.0 minutes) performing the administrations per home visit (Figure 1). These measurements captured different components of care and therefore were not directly comparable. Of note, the administration time of the Koru Freedom pump is fixed and consistent. This means that the time savings shown here are mostly attributable to efficiencies in the operational and administrative procedures.

Fitbit compliance

Overall engagement with digital monitoring was limited. Fitbit compliance was 0% in 46 of 70 treatment cycles (65.7%), meaning that the patients did not have a single day in the cycle where they wore the Fitbit for at least 16 hours. Accordingly, median compliance across all cycles was 0%. Among cycles with any device use (24/70), the median compliance was 71.4% (Figure 2).

Fitbit compliance by treatment cycle. Bars show Fitbit compliance for individual treatment cycles, ordered from highest to lowest compliance. The dashed line indicates the median compliance among cycles with recorded Fitbit use (71.4%).

Vitals measurement compliance

Overall engagement with vitals measurement was limited. 36 of the cycles had 0% vitals measurement compliance, meaning that the patients did not have a single day in the cycle where they measured their vital signs once a day. Among all cycles, the median vitals measurement compliance was 0%. Excluding the cycles with 0% compliance, the remaining 34 cycles had a median compliance of 95.2% (Figure 3).

Vital signs monitoring compliance by treatment cycle. Bars show vital signs monitoring compliance for individual treatment cycles, ordered from highest to lowest compliance. The dashed line indicates the median compliance among cycles with recorded vital signs monitoring (95.2%).

Weekly survey compliance

Overall engagement with survey use was limited. 45 of the cycles had 0% compliance, indicating that the patients did not fill out a weekly survey once that cycle. Among all cycles, the median weekly survey compliance was 0%. Excluding the cycles with 0% compliance, the remaining 25 cycles had a median compliance of 66.7% (Figure 4).

Fitbit, vital sign, and weekly survey compliance by patient

The overall compliance level with Fitbit wearing, vital sign measuring, and weekly survey completion varied between patients. Using Fitbit wearing as a metric, Patient 101 was the most compliant with a mean compliance of 45.5%, followed by Patient 104 at 36.2%, Patient 103 with 0.8%, and Patients 102 and 105 both at 0.0% (Figure 5). Using vital sign compliance as a metric, Patient 101 was the most compliant with a mean compliance of 53.1%, followed by Patient 104 at 48.0%, Patient 103 at 31.2%, Patient 102 with 2.4%, and Patient 105 with 0.0% (Supplementary Figure 1). With survey compliance as a metric, Patient 101 was the most compliant at 35.7%, followed by Patient 104 at 33.3%, Patient 103 at 23.0%, and Patients 102 and 105 at 0.0% (Supplementary Figure 2).

Likert scale scores of satisfaction

On a five-category Likert scale (0 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree), the mean score for overall satisfaction with the drug administration process was 3.3 (SD = 0.85; n = 17), with a median of 4. Preference for subcutaneous over intravenous administration was also high (mean = 3.2; SD = 0.97; median = 4; n = 17). When asked about comfort, the mean score was 3.2 (SD = 0.92; n = 10) and a median of 3.5. Regarding convenience, the mean was 2.8 (SD = 0.92; n = 10) with a median of 2.5. Results are summarized in Table 4. The 17 satisfaction responses were contributed by five patients, with Patients 101–105 contributing 2, 6, 5, 2, and 2 responses, respectively (Table 5). Mean patient-level satisfaction scores ranged from 2.8 to 3.5 (Table 5). Satisfaction surveys were administered electronically at predefined study time points. Because of the small sample size and repeated measurements within patients, the satisfaction results are presented descriptively. The questionnaire was developed for this study and was not formally validated.

Discussion

This prospective, single-center phase II feasibility study demonstrates the feasibility of atezolizumab SC injections in the home setting for patients with NSCLC. All five patients successfully transitioned to at-home administrations following initial in-clinic administration, and 93% of the total 70 administrations were delivered on time (Figure 1). The delays that did occur were primarily related to non-treatment-related logistical or personal factors, rather than systemic issues with the at-home administration process itself and only led to one case of drug wastage due to courier failure.

These findings support previous research suggesting that home-based immunotherapy is a viable alternative to traditional clinic-based models, particularly when supported by appropriate digital infrastructure and care coordination. Variability among subject compliance for data collection using the remote tools was high and future efforts to increase education and patient selection will be required for large scale implementation. In our cohort, compliance differences appeared to be patient-specific: two patients (101 and 104) demonstrated consistently higher Fitbit, vital sign, and survey compliance than the others. This pattern suggests that variability was driven more by individual engagement than by limitations of the digital tools themselves.

Given the small cohort and the absence of systematically collected information regarding event timing, method of detection, and administration setting, no conclusions regarding comparative tolerability in the clinic and home settings can be drawn. Two adverse events were observed, both of which were promptly recognized and managed. In one case, severe neutropenia with suspected immune-related toxicity prompted a temporary treatment hold, with further evaluation showing disease progression and subsequent discontinuation from the trial. Importantly, both events were promptly managed and highlight the importance of continued monitoring even in decentralized care models. While it is possible that earlier recognition or intervention in a clinic might have slightly accelerated the detection of these events, existing evidence suggests that for therapies with low acute toxicity, such as SC immunotherapy, the clinical course and outcomes are generally comparable between home and hospital settings [12,13]. This is especially true when patients are supported by structured monitoring and telehealth access1. In this cohort, digital monitoring tools did not appear to directly contribute to the detection or management of adverse events or other clinical decisions. Two patients had no recorded compliance with any of the digital monitoring tools but nevertheless received home-administered treatment. Thus, while telemedicine and remote monitoring may be feasible components of a home-based treatment model, our findings do not establish that Fitbit, vital-sign, or weekly survey monitoring was necessary for safe home administration.

Successful implementation of home-based atezolizumab SC requires careful patient selection and standardized care processes. In our study, patients completed their first three treatment cycles in the infusion center before transitioning to home administration to confirm treatment tolerance. Home administrations were administered by trained nurses with routine laboratory monitoring and telemedicine follow-up. Although no administration -related emergencies occurred in this feasibility study, established protocols for communication with the oncology team and escalation of care are important for broader implementation.

Patient-reported outcomes indicated high satisfaction with atezolizumab SC injections. On a five-category Likert scale scored from 0 to 4, the mean satisfaction score was 3.3, and the median was 4.0, suggesting that most patients agreed or strongly agreed that they were satisfied with the administration process (Table 4). Similarly, the mean score for preferring SC over intravenous (IV) infusion was 3.2, highlighting a general preference for SC delivery (Table 4). Patients also rated the comfort of the SC injection favorably (mean 3.2, median 3.5), although the perceived convenience scored slightly lower (mean 2.8, median 2.5), potentially reflecting logistical or scheduling challenges (Table 4). Overall, these findings support the acceptability and patient-centered benefits of atezolizumab SC injections, with opportunities to further improve convenience through enhanced care coordination.

Home administration was associated with a shorter measured administration time than in-clinic visits (42.3 vs. 86.7 minutes). However, these measures were not directly comparable because clinic time included check-in through treatment completion, whereas home time reflected only the nurse’s time in the home. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive rather than as estimates of total treatment time. Although the clinic and home administration time measures were not directly comparable, the shorter measured duration of home administration suggests the potential to reduce patient time burden. Combined with the reported patient satisfaction, this approach may improve convenience and quality of life, particularly for patients with limited mobility or transportation challenges. Home administration may also reduce demands on infusion center resources, although these operational benefits were not formally evaluated in the present study.

Although this study did not formally evaluate costs, home-based atezolizumab SC administration has the potential to reduce healthcare resource utilization by decreasing infusion chair time and administrative burden while lowering patient travel and time costs. Prior literature reviews have estimated 9%-53% cost savings with home-based oncology administrations [1,5]. From a market perspective, the switch from IV formulation anticancer therapies to SC formulations has the potential for economic impact, though health systems have varied economic structures that favor in clinic or home administration. Agents such as nivolumab (Opdivo), pembrolizumab (Keytruda), and trastuzumab–pertuzumab combinations (Phesgo) have been developed for SC delivery to reduce administration time and support home-based treatment models [1]. Even partial transition of these high-volume IV therapies to home-based SC administration could lead to substantial reductions in infusion chair time, personnel and healthcare center burden, and healthcare delivery costs.

This study is limited by its small sample size. Moreover, the relatively high-functioning nature of the patients enrolled who could engage with digital monitoring may limit generalizability. Because this report includes a small cohort from a prospective feasibility study, detailed information regarding the timing of adverse events relative to individual doses, the setting of administration (clinic versus home), and the method of event detection was not systematically collected. Satisfaction responses were obtained longitudinally from a small number of patients and therefore should not be interpreted as independent observations. Additionally, health care provider travel time was not formally captured, so the time savings reported only reflect reductions in patient time burden and infusion center utilization. Future studies should investigate the applicability of this model in more diverse populations across multiple tumor types and assess long-term clinical outcomes, cost-effectiveness, caregiver burden, and feasibility from a home health delivery perspective.

Conclusion

This prospective, single-center phase II feasibility study supports the feasibility and logistical practicality of administering atezolizumab SC at home for selected patients with NSCLC. In this small cohort, home administration was associated with few observed adverse events, high on-time treatment delivery, positive patient satisfaction, and reductions in administration time. While these findings suggest that home administration may represent a patient-centered and efficient model of care, larger prospective studies are needed to more fully evaluate its safety, effectiveness, and generalizability.
