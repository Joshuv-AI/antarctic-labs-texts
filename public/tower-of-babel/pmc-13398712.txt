Fostering Social Connection: Progress and Opportunities in VA and Beyond
Authors: Jay A. Gorman, Susan Strickland, Alan R. Teo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Social isolation and loneliness threaten both mental and physical health. This commentary highlights innovative programs that prioritize treating social connection as a social need and driver of health, drawing from four major initiatives within the Department of Veterans Affairs: Veterans Socials, Caring Contacts, the Expiration Term of Service Sponsorship Program, and Department of Veterans Affairs S.A.V.E. Key features of these four programs and suggestions for how healthcare systems can act as meaningful catalysts of social connection are provided, including community engagement, volunteer involvement, commitment to research and evaluation, and scaling evidence‐based models and education.

Decades of research link mortality, chronic disease, and suicide to loneliness and social isolation (1, 2). Recently, these twin dangers have been declared a public health epidemic in the U.S. (3) but healthcare providers have historically been left to independently navigate how to support their patients' social connection needs.

In this commentary, we highlight four distinct programs that address these challenges. Two are explicitly designed to enhance social connection, while the other two integrate social connection‐focused strategies within broader suicide prevention efforts; all are being implemented by the Veterans Health Administration (VHA), the country's largest integrated healthcare system serving over 9 million enrolled veterans. These social connection solutions sit within a broader portfolio of initiatives that demonstrate VHA's multifaceted approach to addressing diverse veteran needs and priorities. In sharing these examples, we believe VHA's efforts toward veterans may inform similar approaches within other healthcare systems and contexts.

VETERANS SOCIALS: BUILDING COMMUNITY, ONE EVENT AT A TIME

Veterans Socials are a community‐building intervention, that recognize regular social contact is fundamental to friendship formation (4). Veterans, alongside peer specialists employed by VA, co‐host community meetups on a weekly to monthly basis. Meetups are designed to foster a sense of belonging and a microculture enabling relationships to grow and develop. By leveraging VA's extensive community partnerships, these Socials create accessible welcoming spaces where veterans can share experiences and make friends. Cross‐sectional surveys indicate that over half of attendees meet someone new and maintain those connections at least quarterly outside the scheduled gatherings, demonstrating potential to meaningfully expand veterans' social networks and reduce isolation (4). Furthermore, empowering veterans as co‐hosts encourages participant‐driven engagement and leadership, strengthening the durability of Veterans Socials after initial Department of Veterans Affairs (VA), support. Currently, over one‐third of the 172 Veterans Socials across 29 states operate without the need for a VA peer specialist.

ETS‐SP: A GUIDING HAND THROUGH A COMPLEX TRANSITION

The transition from military to civilian life can be one of the most isolating and risky periods for a veteran (5). The Expiration Term of Service Sponsorship Program (ETS‐SP) directly addresses this vulnerability by providing one‐on‐one certified sponsor support for transitioning service members (6). Sponsors, who are veterans themselves, connect their mentees to crucial VA and community resources and provide early intervention when needed. ETS‐SP is a multidimensional initiative targeting multiple socioecological levels: individual (guidance), interpersonal (supportive relationships), community (local resources), and societal (structured reintegration pathways). A randomized control trial found that veterans who engaged in a community‐based intervention connecting them to local physical and social activities and worked with an ETS sponsor had fewer reintegration difficulties and had more social support over 12 months, suggesting that the addition of a sponsor augmented and outperformed the community‐based intervention alone (6).

CARING CONTACTS: AN OFFER OF SUPPORT, NO STRINGS ATTACHED

Caring Contacts is an evidence‐based suicide prevention strategy dating back to the 1970s that, in its most basic form, delivers a series of brief, caring messages over the course of many months to vulnerable patients. Such outreach, which often occurs in the form of postcards, is thought to remind the recipient they are not forgotten. These simple, unprompted caring messages help bridge patients through crises. In one of the largest suicide prevention experiments to date, VA conducted a comparative effectiveness trial with over 100,000 veterans who had called the Veterans Crisis Line to receive one of two different versions of Caring Contacts (7). Researchers found that both groups receiving Caring Contacts had increased healthcare utilization but there was no effect on incidence of suicide attempts or all‐cause mortality. While these results were seemingly mixed, an ongoing pragmatic trial is testing Caring Contacts that includes the availability of direct support from a peer specialist located at a VA near to the veteran. This newer trial 1 aims to determine whether the increased support from peers might translate into reduced loneliness, suicidal ideation, and even all‐cause mortality.

VA S.A.V.E.: TRAINING A NATION OF FIRST RESPONDERS

Distinct from previously mentioned programs, VA S.A.V.E provides a foundational infrastructure to support workforce capacities by teaching interpersonal skills essential to cultivating an ecosystem of social connection among veterans. It is a suicide prevention skills training designed to teach individuals, including community members, frontline VA staff, and caregivers, how to recognize and respond to veterans at risk for suicide. In addition to providing information and awareness, VA S.A.V.E equips trainees with tangible skills to listen empathetically and to engage in difficult conversations with veterans. VA's partnership with community‐based organizations such as PsychArmor further help broaden the reach of VA S.A.V.E. with free, video‐based courses and helps build the community fabric around all veterans. Following a pilot where PsychArmor's video version of VA S.A.V.E. significantly increased self‐reported suicide prevention skills compared to control (8), VA is currently conducting a larger and longer randomized controlled trial that will objectively score participants on their suicide prevention skills and follow participants for 12 months.

Lessons for Other Healthcare Systems

Although healthcare systems alone are not responsible for the promotion of social connection, effective efforts demand collaboration across multiple sectors (e.g., housing, transportation, legal, employment). These four programs are not isolated efforts but are part of an array of other efforts that underscore a prioritization of social connection as a social determinant of health (9). While VHA is unique, with its reduced vulnerability to the fee‐for‐service payment model, we believe other healthcare systems can also act as meaningful catalysts for health promotion through interventions that foster social connection. Our key suggestions include the following:

Enhance Community Engagement

Engaging with the community requires individuals who are embedded in, and respected members of, the community. VHA uses Community Engagement Partnership Coordinators, staff stationed in VA facilities around the country, who are dedicated to local outreach and coalition‐building. They represent a strategic investment to support upstream suicide prevention initiatives beyond VA's physical footprint.

Engage Volunteers

VA has long partnered with Veteran Service Organizations for key volunteer support. More recently, VA has expanded volunteer infrastructure through the Compassionate Contact Corps (10), a nationwide program that enrolls and trains volunteers to provide consistent phone outreach to veterans experiencing loneliness or social isolation.

Commit to Research and Evaluation

Rigorous research and systematic program evaluation are key elements in the refinement and expansion of social connection interventions, adding to the body of evidence‐informed practices. Further, evaluating the sustainability of program users' social connections is a critical research need. More remains to be learned, evaluating the sustainability of program users' social connections or whether and how online forums such as gaming and virtual support communities can be leveraged to foster and optimize social connection (11, 12, 13).

Scale Up Evidence‐based Models

Elevating social connection as a key health priority among policymakers, providers, and the public, can facilitate scaling and replication of services. Scale‐up in large systems such as VA are most effective when there is a combination of top‐down authority (e.g., clear policy directives with resource allocation), bottom‐up championship (e.g., respected facility‐level leaders), and local adaptation (e.g., for resource‐constrained rural facilities). Resource allocation, standardized training, customizable guidance tools, and alignment of policy and leadership structures—present in the initiatives highlighted here—are vital to success. Synergies in concurrently implemented programs, such as shared volunteer and referral pathways, can further optimize resources while strengthening impact.

Taken together, social connection efforts within healthcare systems are feasible, and holds promise for addressing a pressing public health need at scale.
