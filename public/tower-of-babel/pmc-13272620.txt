Unconventional atomic-level mechanism induced by a submerged saddle in the O(1D) + CH3OCH3 reaction
Authors: Xingyu Yang, Jianwei Cao, Fengyi Li, Jintao Xie, Wensheng Bian, Kevin M Hickson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElucidating the mechanisms of excited-state atom reactions with hydrocarbons and their derivatives is important for the study of interstellar and combustion chemistry. For these reactions, atom insertion into chemical bonds is the acknowledged pathway, however, this concept could be invalid. Here, through a combined experimental and theoretical study of the O(1D) + CH3OCH3 reaction, we demonstrate that the accepted insertion mechanisms, including O-insertion into C−O bonds, do not occur, and reveal an unconventional atomic-level mechanism, designated as O+H−C collision activation stereocyclization, that supersedes O-insertion into C−H bonds. Dynamics calculations, performed on a high-level ab initio potential energy surface, yield rate coefficients in very good agreement with experiment, exhibiting an unexpected increase with temperature. This finding is attributed to an unusual submerged saddle as part of the unconventional mechanism. As this mechanism probably exists in other excited-state atom reactions, the dynamical effects uncovered here are expected to have general significance.Subject terms: Reaction kinetics and dynamics, Chemical physics, Reaction mechanisms

Introduction

Recent advances in combined theoretical and experimental studies have greatly enhanced our understanding of reaction kinetics and mechanisms at low-to-moderate temperatures1–6. However, in contrast to certain well-studied elementary reactions7–13, our understanding of mechanisms and dynamics for polyatomic and multiple channel reactions1,14–17 is still very limited. In particular, there is a rich history of research into the reactions between hydrocarbons or their derivatives and excited-state atoms, with conventional wisdom accepting that the insertion of excited-state atoms into chemical bonds such as C−H and C−C is ubiquitous18–22, however, this concept could be misleading, since convincing mechanistic evidence is often lacking. This can be contrasted with the mechanisms of excited-state atom insertions into H–H bonds in three-atom reactions, which are thoroughly understood23–26. Here, we find that the acknowledged insertion mechanisms, in particular O-insertion into C−O and C−H bonds, do not occur in the O(1D) + CH3OCH3 reaction, and we further reveal an unconventional one, designated as O+H−C collision activation stereocyclization, which replaces the previously accepted mechanism of O-insertion into the C−H bond18,19,27,28. We find that this mechanism proceeds by activating the C–H bond, forming a C–O bond through a cyclic structure. This provokes an unusual increase of the rate coefficients with temperature, which deviates from the well-known capture type behavior29,30.

These findings are achieved through combined experimental and theoretical studies on the title reaction, including low temperature experiments and dynamical calculations on a high-level ab initio potential energy surface (PES) constructed by us based upon large-scale DLPNO-CCSD(T)/aug-cc-pVTZ calculations at over twenty-eight thousand symmetry-unique geometries, yielding excellent agreement between the measured and calculated rate coefficients. We further reveal the underlying mechanisms by performing extensive analysis on the PES. The O(1D) + CH3OCH3 system is a typical example of reactions between excited state atoms and a hydrocarbon derivative, and thus we expect that the mechanism and dynamical effects uncovered here also exist in other similar reactive systems. These findings are important for acquiring a deeper understanding of polyatomic complex-forming reactions.

Several interesting studies have been performed on the O(1D) + CH3OCH3 (dimethyl ether, or DME) reaction in recent years. In 1998, Schalley et al.31 reported the reaction of O(1D) addition to DME to produce dimethyl ether oxide, and indicated that a methyl shift in dimethyl ether oxide to produce dimethyl peroxide requires an energy of more than 40 kcal mol−1 and can proceed by retention or inversion of the configuration at the migrating carbon. In 1999, Wrobel et al.32 studied the title reaction using matrix isolation spectroscopy and quantum chemical calculations, and discussed that O(1D) preferentially inserts directly into the out-of-plane C−H bonds of DME, without formation of intermediate radicals to yield methoxymethanol conformers. In 2013, Hays and Widicus Weaver27 performed theoretical calculations that identified direct, barrierless insertion into the C−H bond as the most favorable pathway for reaction, although they did not provide any mechanistic information. More recently, Zhong et al.22 employed mid-infrared Faraday rotation spectroscopy and direct absorption spectroscopy, in conjunction with master equation simulations, to determine the product branching ratios of the O(1D) + CH3OCH3 reaction. They identified and quantified four products (OH, H2O, HO2, and CH2O), observing a high yield of CH2O. Based on simulations, they fitted the temporal profiles of the four products and determined a branching ratio of 76% for the CH3O + CH3O pathway. Identifying this as the dominant channel, they proposed that the high yield of CH2O originates from secondary reactions of CH3O. They also estimated the overall rate coefficient based upon approximate theoretical calculations.

Despite this, the experimental rate coefficient has never been measured for the O(1D) + DME reaction, whose determination is challenging, due to the fast and highly exothermic nature of this process. Meanwhile, little information is available about its dynamics based on a PES. In this work, we construct an accurate high-dimensional ab initio PES with the fundamental invariant neural network (NN) fitting method, and based upon this PES, conduct a combined experimental and theoretical study on the kinetics and dynamics of the O(1D) + CH3OCH3 reaction.

Results

Reaction rate coefficients

Our theoretical calculations are supported by measurements of the temperature dependent rate coefficients for the title reaction using a supersonic flow reactor over the 50-296 K temperature range. Descriptions of the experimental and theoretical methodologies are provided in the Methods, and Supplementary Methods sections. Figure 1 shows the experimental and theoretical rate coefficients as a function of temperature, where the variable reaction coordinate–variational transition state theory (VRC-VTST) method is applied to perform dynamical calculations on the PES for the O(1D) + CH3OCH3 reaction.

Comparison of experimental and theoretical total rate coefficients as a function of temperature for the O(1D) + CH3OCH3 reaction.

The theoretical result is obtained by summing the contributions from the O+H−C channel (dashed line) and the O− O addition channel (dash-dotted line). Dynamics calculations are performed on the potential energy surface constructed in this work.

The calculated total rate coefficients are in good general agreement with the measured values and both results present an apparent positive temperature dependence in the temperature range 50−300 K. As a result of the uncertainty on the experimentally derived rate coefficients, a temperature independent value for the rate coefficient could also be possible as the error bars for all measurements overlap although this is a much less probable outcome. Indeed, the mean values of the low (50, 75, and 127 K) and high (296 K) temperature measurements of 3.4 × 10−10 cm3 molecule−1 s−1 and 3.9 × 10−10 cm3 molecule−1 s−1, respectively, support this conclusion. The calculated rate coefficients for the O+H−C collision activation channel (referred to as O+H−C channel hereafter), represented by the dashed line in Fig. 1, are much higher at all temperatures than those for O−O addition channel, represented by the dashed-dotted line, and thus this channel clearly represents the major one.

More importantly, the total rate coefficient plot and the one for the O+H−C channel, show an appreciable increase with temperature, in contrast to a typical capture type behavior29,30 which is governed by long-range forces and characteristically exhibits a negative temperature dependence for rate coefficients. This is remarkable, and we attribute it to the O+H−C collision activation mechanism, revealed here and discussed below. In addition, the rate coefficient plot for the minor channel of O–O addition shows a slight decrease with temperature in the low-temperature regime, consistent with the usual capture behavior. The O(1D) + CH3OCH3 reaction is a barrierless complex-forming reaction (see Fig. 2), for which a negative temperature-dependence for the total rate coefficient is normally predicted according to the capture model. This discrepancy suggests that the underlying atomic-level mechanisms may not be the expected ones. Further PES and dynamics investigations to elucidate these differences are described in the following sections.

Reaction profile and pathways

High-level ab initio calculations are performed for the title system at the DLPNO-CCSD(T)/aug-cc-pVTZ level of theory. The potential energy profile and the reaction paths for possible reaction channels are shown in Fig. 2. It can be seen that there are numerous intermediates and transition states (TSs) along these multiple competing reaction pathways. Some intermediates can yield products through various unimolecular decomposition or isomerization steps, often facilitated by low-lying TSs. The structures of these intermediates and TSs are shown in Fig. 1, while more details are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1.

It can be seen from Fig. 2 that there are two main branches of reaction channel: the O–O addition channel in blue and the O+H−C collision activation channel in red. Through the O–O addition channel, the reactants first form IM1 via a barrierless process, which then transforms into IM2 via TS1. The most energetically favorable pathway through this channel is a barrierless dissociation yielding CH3O + CH3O. As for the other channel (the O+H–C one), the reactants first form IM3 via a barrierless process, followed by an optimal reaction pathway that proceeds through TS9 to yield CH2O and CH3OH, which is the lowest energy pathway as indicated by the heavy red line in Fig. 2. The energetically preferred pathway, found for the first time in this work to the best of our knowledge, is entirely consistent with the high yield of CH2O observed in the most recent experiment22. Consequently, it is no longer necessary to invoke the O–O addition channel leading to CH3O + CH3O22 to explain the high yield of CH2O. It is clear from Fig. 2 that the pathway to produce CH3O + CH3O is energetically unfavorable compared with the one indicated by the heavy red line. The possibility of reaction occurring by O(1D) insertion into the C−O bond or by the unconventional mechanism will be discussed further in section “Do the generally accepted insertion mechanisms actually occur?”.

It should be noted that intersystem crossing (ISC) may influence reactions of O(3P) with small organic molecules, as demonstrated by Casavecchia and coworkers1,33. However, ISCs are found nearby or below the O(3P) asymptote, which are much lower in energy than the O(1D) asymptote, and the present study focuses primarily on the dynamical evolution in regions from the entrance channels of O(1D) + CH3OCH3 down to the neighboring wells (our experiments also measure reactant consumption rather than product formation). We estimate that ISC has a negligible impact on the rate coefficients and reaction mechanism reported in this work.

In a general sense, the two kinds of reaction channels described here are roughly consistent with those reported in most previous work. However, the O+H−C channel was not identified previously, instead a channel occurring by O(1D) insertion into the C–H bond was assumed, while O(1D) insertion into the C−O bond channel22 was suggested recently. These will be discussed in detail below.

Potential energy surface and submerged saddle

To gain further atomic-level insights into the mechanism and dynamics occurring through the reaction channels identified above, we construct a high-dimensional ab initio PES based upon large-scale ab initio calculations using the DLPNO-CCSD(T)/aug-cc-pVTZ method at over twenty-eight thousand symmetry-unique geometries (see “Methods” and Supplementary Methods) covering various related PES regions and mainly concentrated in regions from the entrance channels to deep wells. The obtained NN PES has a root-mean-square fitting error of 0.22 kcal mol−1 for the whole data set. In particular, the fitting error in regions from the entrance channels to wells is around 0.11 kcal mol−1. These indicate the high quality of the PES fits (see “Methods” for more details).

A general definition of a van der Waals saddle in a chemical reaction is given in our previous work23, and here we extend this concept to the interaction regions of a PES, and introduce the submerged saddle where the saddle point appears in the well region. The submerged saddle refers to the PES region surrounding the local saddle point up to the asymptote, and its key property is its depth, which is defined as the energy difference between the saddle point and the corresponding asymptote. The configuration and key geometrical parameters for the saddle point obtained from the present PES in the O+H−C channel are shown in Fig. 3b. We see that the geometry of the saddle point is linear for O+H−C, while the C−H bond length of 1.35 Å is much longer than its value of 1.10 Å at the DME equilibrium geometry, indicating that the C−H bond is activated. Our normal-mode analyses on the PES indicate that the involved saddle point is a secondary one with two imaginary frequencies, with the two imaginary frequencies corresponding to the C−H bending towards the parallel and vertical directions, respectively. The coordinates describing the relative positions for the attack of O at the H−C bond are shown in Fig. 3a. It can be seen that there is a bending angle α in the X-Y plane, which is used to describe H movement after the saddle point, corresponding to the C−H parallel bending motion. In addition, there is a bending angle β in the Y-(-Z) plane, which is similar to α except going downwards, called the C−H vertical bending motion.

Two imaginary frequencies are clearly identified with values of 1390i and 1231i cm−1, suggesting two kinds of strong bending motion orthogonal to each other.

It is clear that all the contour lines are smooth and without any artificial oscillations, indicating that the constructed PES is physically reasonable in all regions. So the energy value of −48 kcal mol−1 of the saddle point structure in the figure corresponds to the depth of the saddle, referred to as the submerged saddle hereafter, which plays a crucial role in the reaction mechanism.

Do the generally accepted insertion mechanisms actually occur?

Studies of excited atom insertions into chemical bonds such as H–H, C–H and C–C have been performed for many years18,34. For example, the insertion of an excited singlet O atom into the C–H bond of a hydrocarbon compound was proposed as early as 196418. Convincing evidence regarding this kind of insertion mechanism is often lacking however, except for the case of insertions into the H–H bond23–26. In particular, as mentioned above, Zhong et al.22 recently proposed that O(1D) insertion into the C−O bond dominates the O(1D) + CH3OCH3 reaction, leading to the formation of two CH3O radicals via dimethyl peroxide dissociation. However, we did not find any evidence for the reaction pathway corresponding to O(1D) insertion into the C−O bond.

Figure 4a, b, c reveal all possible reaction channels in the entrance region when O(1D) attacks DME. As can be seen, there are five potential wells: the one at the top middle corresponds to the O−O addition channel, while the other four wells correspond to the four O+H−C channels, in which the O(1D) atom attacks the terminal H atom of different H−C bonds. Due to the longer distance from the C–O–C plane, Fig. 4d displays only three attractive wells: one corresponding to the O–O addition channel and two associated with the O-attack on terminal H channels. We can see that no potential energy minimum appears in the region along the direction of O(1D) attack on the C−O bond; instead, the potential energy is relatively high, indicating that this attack pathway is unfavorable from an energetic perspective.

We further investigate the possibility of direct O(1D) insertion into the C–O bond of DME using multireference electronic structure calculations. Some representative calculations are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, where contour plots are shown for cases where O(1D) approaches the C–O bond from two typical directions. We see from Supplementary Fig. 3, that the potential energy surface along the X–O coordinate is strongly repulsive, featuring a barrier of ~43–67 kcal mol−1. Consequently, direct insertion of O(1D) into the C–O bond must overcome at least a 40 kcal mol−1 energy barrier. Considering that the formation of IM2 from IM1 via TS1 (see Fig. 2) presents a much more favorable energy pathway, the probability of direct O(1D) insertion into the C–O bond is negligible. In summary, although earlier work by Zhong et al.22 suggested a reaction pathway directly yielding a peroxy-type intermediate, CH3OOCH3 (IM2), our calculations indicate that this pathway is energetically prohibited.

The conclusion drawn from Fig. 4 is also supported by a frontier orbital analysis. In DME, the HOMO is mainly localized on the C–H bonds and oxygen lone pairs, leaving little density on the C–O σ framework for overlap with the 2p orbital of O(1D). So insertion of O(1D) into the C–O bond of DME is hindered by poor orbital overlap between DME and O(1D), as well as significant steric hindrance from the methyl groups, which will result in a high-energy and structurally distorted TS. In contrast, insertion of O(1D) into C–H is an essentially barrierless process due to efficient orbital interaction and minimal geometric distortion.

Taken together, these results indicate that, despite the greater thermodynamic stability of CH3OOCH3 (IM2) compared to IM1, the direct insertion of O(1D) into the C−O bond is kinetically inaccessible. This conclusion is supported by the absence of a barrierless insertion pathway, coupled with prohibitive steric hindrance and stringent orbital symmetry constraints. Consequently, IM2 is far more likely to arise from secondary rearrangements of oxygen-centered intermediates such as IM1, rather than being formed as a direct product of O(1D) attacking the C−O bond. This interpretation is consistent with our PES analysis and orbital interaction data.

In conclusion, as for the title reaction, O(1D) insertion into C–O bonds does not occur, while the previously accepted mechanism for O(1D) insertion into C–H bonds should be revised. This latter process actually occurs by a O+H–C collision activation cyclization mechanism (see Fig. 7), which has the effect of activating the C–H bond to yield the main products. This unconventional mechanism is discussed in detail below.

Schematic profile of the O+H − C collision activation cyclization mechanism.

Geometries and their energies (E) along the minimum energy reaction path is indicated.

O+H−C collision activation cyclization mechanism

Figure 5a, b present contour plots of the present surface as a function of inter-atomic distances for the O+H–C channel and O–O addition channel, respectively. We see from Fig. 5a that, the minimum appears around RC−H = 1.35, RO−H = 1.01Å and ∠OHC = 180 degrees, indicating an elongated C–H bond (compared to 1.10 Å in the DME equilibrium geometry) and shortened O–H distance, which starts from 4.0 Å. No barrier is found along the minimum energy path (MEP). This minimum corresponds to the saddle point (indicated by ×) in Fig. 6b or d, which is suggested to be a secondary one since there are two downhill directions related to it. It has two imaginary frequencies, leading to two orthogonal cyclization directions. This suggests that multiple C–H bending (followed by cyclization) directions in three-dimensional space are possible, and thus we call it stereocyclization. It can be seen that this minimum has an energy value of −48 kcal mol−1 which corresponds to the depth of the submerged saddle. Figure 5b displays the O–O addition channel, for which the C–O bond is not elongated.

As discussed above, the channel where O(1D) attacks the H–C bond is found to be the major one, and the underlying mechanism is revealed as a O+H–C collision activation stereocyclization mechanism, as illustrated in Fig. 7. It should be noted that only the parallel cyclization direction is shown here. The key geometrical parameters and energies of the displayed configurations are derived from the MEP, and the configuration at the saddle point (activation complex) is included as a critical point, which has the effect of activating the C–H bond, and transferring to the deep-well direction. It is interesting to notice that following the structure at the saddle point, a cyclic structure appears with the C–O bond formed, and then the product is finally formed through C–H bond fission. It should be mentioned that, in their study of the O + HF reaction, Jambrina et al.35 found that the O atom does not insert into the H–F bond but interacts with F to form an intermediate via H migration. However, the mechanism revealed in that reaction is distinctly different from the one presented in our work: first, in their study, O attacks the F atom first, not the H atom as in our work; second, due to high barriers for linear approaches in O + HF, O attacks HF from a bent direction, whereas in our system, O approaches H in the direction of the H–C bond axis. As far as we know, this is the first report of this kind of mechanism, which is usually assumed to be a mechanism involving excited atom insertion into a C–H bond in multiple-channel reaction systems.

As mentioned above, the rate coefficient plot shows an appreciable increase with temperature. This may result from the increase of collision energy with temperature which is helpful for the O+H−C collision activation channel, having the effect of promoting reaction. However, this kind of effect is small, since the linear O+H−C complex lies at the bottom of a submerged saddle, quite different from the normal collision complex (TS) located above the entrance valley. This is supported by our preliminary trajectory calculations, since we did not observe an insertion mechanism which would lead to a temperature independent rate coefficient2. In addition, as discussed above, the barrierless O–O addition channel follows the capture model. So the O+H−C collision activation mechanism should be the underlying cause for the temperature dependence of the rate coefficient revealed in Fig. 1.

Discussion

In this work, through a combined experimental and theoretical study, we find that the conventionally accepted insertion mechanisms for the O(1D) + CH3OCH3 reaction do not occur, while a unconventional reaction mechanism, O+H−C collision activation stereocyclization, is revealed. It is interesting to examine whether the conclusions are system specific. Although it is presently prohibitive for us to conduct similar comprehensive and time-consuming studies for other systems besides O(1D) + CH3OCH3, we can provide some reasonable analyses. There have been numerous studies15,36–40 on the reactions of excited-state atoms with hydrocarbons and their derivatives, and similar kinetic behaviors and mechanisms have been revealed for these reactions. The title reaction is a typical example of this class of reactions, and thus the previously mentioned insertion mechanisms, including O-insertion into C−O and C−H bonds, may not occur either for other similar reactions. We also performed some preliminary studies on other systems such as O(1D) + CH2CH2 and O(1D) + CH2CCH2, and qualitatively similar conclusions are obtained. Of course, in some cases, the O+H−C collision activation mechanism revealed here may display subtly different features, and it may not be the dominant one when the studied hydrocarbon (or its derivative) has fewer C−H bonds and more active reaction sites.

A high-level ab initio NN PES is constructed based upon over twenty-eight thousand symmetry-unique energy points calculated using the DLPNO-CCSD(T)/aug-cc-pVTZ method, and further dynamics calculations are performed to obtain rate coefficients which are in excellent agreement with our low-temperature experimental measurements. A previously unidentified lowest energy pathway is revealed, forming IM3 through a barrierless process, followed by the formation of CH2O and CH3OH over TS9. This provides a better explanation for the high yield of CH2O observed by Zhong et al.22 recently in their spectroscopic experiments.

Our work demonstrates the importance of the O+H−C collision activation stereocyclization mechanism in controlling the reaction rate at the PES level, which leads to a positive temperature dependence for the rate coefficient and differs from a typical capture type behavior. Experiment validates theory in this respect. The conclusions drawn from this work should be applicable for other similar reactive systems. It is our hope that the present discussion on insertion mechanisms will help to improve our understanding of the reactions of electronically excited atoms with hydrocarbons and their derivatives. For future work, it will be interesting to investigate whether similar mechanisms and phenomena exist in other complex-forming reactions such as O(1D) + CH2CCH2 and C(1D) + CH2CH2.

Methods

Ab initio calculations

High-level ab initio calculations for the system are performed at 28182 symmetry-unique geometries covering various PES regions using the domain-based local pair natural orbital (DLPNO) coupled-cluster method with singles, doubles, and perturbative triples excitations [CCSD(T)]41 and the augmented correlation-consistent basis set aug-cc-pVnZ42,43 (n = T). The reliability of the single-reference description for these points is confirmed by checking the T1 diagnostic values44. To ensure high accuracy in both the DLPNO calculations and self-consistent-field (SCF) convergence, the TightPNO45 and TightSCF settings are applied. The computations are accelerated by employing the aug-cc-pVTZ/C auxiliary basis set for correlation fitting, and by using the RI-JK approximation with the aug-cc-pVTZ/JK auxiliary basis set for coulomb and exchange fitting46. The geometries of the reactants, products, intermediates, and TS structures are optimized using density functional theory (DFT) with the ωB97X functional including D3 dispersion corrections, combined with the aug-cc-pVTZ basis set (See Supplementary Table 1). All local minima (reactants, products, and intermediates) and TSs are confirmed by a harmonic frequency analysis. Intrinsic reaction coordinate calculations47,48 are performed to verify the connectivity of reactants, products and the corresponding TSs. To systematically explore all possible reaction channels, multireference calculations are also carried out. Electronic energies involving the channel of direct insertion of O(1D) into the C–O bond are computed using a second-order perturbation theory (CASPT2)49, with reference wavefunctions obtained from the five-state-averaged complete active space self-consistent field (SA-CASSCF)50 calculations employing the aug-cc-pVTZ basis set. A (6e,5o) active space is adopted, comprising the bonding and antibonding C–O orbitals (2e,2o) together with the three 2p orbitals of the O(1D) atom (4e,3o). The DLPNO-CCSD(T) and ωB97X-D3 calculations are performed using the ORCA program package (version 5.0.1)51, and the multireference calculations are carried out with the Molpro 2015.1 program52.

Potential energy surface construction and dynamics/kinetic calculations

In this work, a full-dimensional ab initio PES for the O(1D) + CH3OCH3 system is constructed using the fundamental invariant (FI)-NN fitting approach53, mainly concentrated in regions from the entrance channels to associated wells and based on the aforementioned geometries obtained at the DLPNO-CCSD(T)/aug-cc-pVTZ level. These geometries are selected from grid scanning around the MEP, normal-mode vibrational grid points, and our trajectory calculations, covering O(1D)-DME distances up to 10 Å and energies below 55 kcal mol−1 relative to the reactant asymptote. The fitting is a crucial step in the PES construction54–58. Here, the feedforward NN with two hidden layers is used, with the number of neurons in hidden layers chosen as 7 and 15, respectively. The input layer contains 1263 FIs. To prevent over fitting, the “early stopping” method59 is applied, with 70% of the data points randomly assigned to the training set, with the remaining 30% as the validating set. The final PES is generated by averaging over five best fittings. The distribution of fitting errors as a function of the energy is displayed in Supplementary Fig. 4a, and the number of geometries for each interval of the fitting error is shown in Supplementary Fig. 4b. As can be seen, most fitting errors are within 0.02 kcal mol−1, indicating that the current fitting reaches a high accuracy. Further details about the PES construction are given inSupplementary Methods and Supplementary References. Generally speaking, this is a local PES focused on the entrance channels and associated regions, but it is well suitable for rate coefficient calculations in this work owing to the barrierless and exothermic characteristics of the O(1D) + DME reaction.

The rate coefficients of the O(1D) + DME reaction are calculated using the VRC-VTST approach as implemented in the Polyrate 2023 program60, and the present PES is used. Within the framework of VRC-VTST approach, the TS dividing surfaces are defined by constraining the distance between pivot points located on the two reacting fragments, and the variational criterion of TST is applied by minimizing the predicted rate coefficient with respect to their separation. To investigate the contributions of different channels to the reaction, we use the approach61,62 in which the different binding sites are separated by infinite potentials. The association rate coefficient to each binding site can then be evaluated separately, with the total rate coefficient obtained as the sum of the individual contributions. In addition, in each case, a set of radical orbital-based pivot points is considered; as for the O(1D) atom, the pivot point is taken to lie on its nucleus. Numerical details are given in the Supplementary Methods, and many test calculations were performed to check the convergence.

Experimental measurement

The gas-phase O(1D) + CH3OCH3 reaction was studied experimentally using a continuous flow Laval nozzle reactor63,64. Three Laval nozzles employing argon as the carrier gas allowed low temperature flows of 127 K, 75 K and 50 K to be generated while experiments at 296 K were performed without a Laval nozzle. The flow characteristics for the nozzles used here can be found in previous work65. O(1D) atoms were formed in situ by the pulsed laser photolysis of ozone, O3, by aligning the 266 nm photolysis laser along the axis of the supersonic flow. Previous work66 has shown that O(1D) atoms represent the major O3 photolysis product at this wavelength (90 %), with O(3P) as the minor product. O3 was created continuously upstream of the reactor by the ultraviolet photolysis of O2 in a quartz cell at pressures around 700 Torr using the 184.9 nm output of a mercury lamp. The use of high pressures here facilitated the three-body recombination of O(3P) atoms with O2. CH3OCH3 gas was introduced through a calibrated mass flow controller into the main argon flow upstream of the Laval nozzle reservoir along with O3.

For the kinetic experiments described here, O(1D) atoms reacted in the presence of an excess of CH3OCH3 allowing the pseudo-first-order approximation to be applied (see the Supplementary Methods for details). To derive temperature dependent rate coefficients for the title reaction, the kinetics of O(1D) atom decay was followed in the presence of a range of CH3OCH3 concentrations.
