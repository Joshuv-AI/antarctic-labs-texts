INDRA-CMIP6: Indian subcontinent high resolution Downscaled pRecipitation and Air temperature data from CMIP6
Authors: Joyjit Mandal, Divya Sardana, Akash Singh Raghuvanshi, Ankit Agarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate regional climate information is vital for hydrological applications, impact studies, and adaptation planning. However, Global Climate Model (GCM) outputs are constrained by coarse resolution and systematic biases, limiting their utility over the climatically diverse and topographically complex Indian subcontinent. To address this, we present INDRA-CMIP6, a high-resolution (0.1° × 0.1°) daily dataset of precipitation, maximum temperature (Tmax), and minimum temperature (Tmin) for the historical period (1951–2014) and future projections (2015–2100) under four emission scenarios (SSP126, SSP245, SSP370, SSP585). The dataset, derived from 14 CMIP6 GCMs and their multi-model ensemble (MME) mean, was developed using the Double Bias-Corrected Constructed Analogue (DBCCA) approach with MSWEP and MSWX as reference datasets. Evaluation against reference data shows reduced warm and cold biases and improved representation of regional climate. INDRA-CMIP6 enables assessments of climate extremes and supports water resource planning.Subject terms: Hydrology, Atmospheric dynamics, Climate sciences

Background & Summary

The Indian subcontinent is among the most densely populated and climate-sensitive regions of the world, with millions of people relying on agriculture for their livelihoods. Observations indicate that the surface air temperature over India has increased by approximately 0.7 °C during the period 1901–20181. Such long-term warming, consistent with global trends, provides clear evidence of human-induced climate change in recent decades, supported by reanalysis datasets and climate model simulations. These changes are primarily attributed to anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions, aerosol forcings, and modifications in land use and land cover2,3. Projections of future climate under different scenarios suggest substantial and robust shifts in the mean state, variability, and extremes of key climatic parameters across the Indian subcontinent and adjoining regions1. In particular, changes in surface air temperature4 and precipitation5–7 are projected to have profound impacts on the climate and societies of the Indian subcontinent.

Projections from General Circulation Models (GCMs) are indispensable for understanding and assessing future climate changes. However, their coarse spatial resolution limits their reliability for regional and local applications, especially in regions with complex topography and climate variability8,9. GCMs have minimum skillful score because of their inherent resolution and parameterization10. High-resolution and accurate projections of precipitation and temperature are particularly critical for impact assessments in sectors such as water resources and agriculture11. Despite their utility, GCM outputs often exhibit systematic biases due to coarse resolution and model parameterizations12,13. Bias is referred as the systematic difference between model output and reference dataset. To address these issues, bias correction and downscaling techniques have been widely adopted14–16. Dynamical downscaling through regional climate models can enhance spatial detail but remains computationally demanding and may still retain biases17,18.

In contrast, statistical approaches based on empirical relationships between observations and model outputs are computationally efficient and widely applied for generating bias-corrected, high-resolution climate projections19,20. Over the past few decades, a variety of statistical downscaling approaches have been introduced, including the Statistical Downscaling Model (SDSM)19, Quantile Delta Mapping (QDM)21, and Empirical Quantile Mapping (EQM)22, all of which have been widely applied in climate impact studies. Recently, bias-corrected daily projections of precipitation, maximum, and minimum temperatures at 0.25° resolution were developed for South Asia using EQM applied on bilinearly interpolated model output23. However, applying EQM independently to each grid cell can distort spatial covariance structures, limiting its effectiveness in preserving spatial coherence24,25. Additionally, most of the global downscaled bias corrected datasets26,27 were developed at coarser resolution of 0.25°. Recently efforts have been made to achieve high resolution dataset for Asian region28. However, it is available as an ensemble-mean product, thereby highlighting the need for a finer resolution daily downscaled dataset for Indian subcontinent.

To overcome this limitation, several advanced gridded statistical downscaling methods have been developed, including Bias Correction Spatial Disaggregation (BCSD)29, Climate Imprint Delta Method (CI)30, Bias-Corrected Climate Imprint Delta Method (BCCI)30, Bias Correction Constructed Analogues (BCCA)31, Double Bias Correction Constructed Analogues (DBCCA)31, and Bias Correction Constructed Analogues with Quantile Mapping Reordering (BCCAQ)21,31. These methods correct biases across multiple sites or grid cells while preserving spatial covariance structures. Among these approaches, Constructed Analogue (CA)-based techniques have shown distinct advantages over BCSD. While BCSD largely preserves only monthly variability, CA methods better capture day-to-day variability in weather events and produce sharper spatial gradients of precipitation and temperature at scales finer than the parent GCMs11. Within this family, the DBCCA method has demonstrated superior performance in reproducing 27 core climate extreme indices, as defined by the Expert Team on Climate Change Detection and Indices (ETCCDI; https://www.climdex.org/), based on daily downscaled precipitation and temperature data31.

Given the pressing challenges of climate change impacts over the Indian subcontinent, this study employs the Double Bias-Corrected Constructed Analogue (DBCCA) approach to develop INDRA-CMIP6, a very high-resolution (0.1° × 0.1°) bias-corrected daily dataset of precipitation, maximum temperature (Tmax), and minimum temperature (Tmin). Derived from 14 CMIP6 (Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6) GCMs and their multi-model ensemble (MME), the dataset provides both historical (1950–2014) and future (2015–2100) projections under four Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP126, SSP245, SSP370, SSP585), with the GCMs selected based on the availability of daily precipitation and temperature variables across all scenarios. At this fine spatial resolution, INDRA-CMIP6 enables applications not feasible with coarse-scale projections, including improved representation of rainfall over the Himalayas and Western Ghats and basin-scale hydrological processes, thereby supporting water resource management, agricultural impact assessments, and drought and flood risk evaluation at actionable scales. Furthermore, by resolving sub-regional climate gradients, the dataset provides a robust basis for studying compound extremes and offers critical inputs for climate-resilient infrastructure planning and localized adaptation strategies across the Indian subcontinent Fig. 1.

Methods

Acquisition of observational and climate model data

High-resolution bias-corrected historical and future projections were developed for the Indian subcontinent and adjoining regions, including Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, and parts of Tibet and Myanmar. Reference daily gridded precipitation and temperature data at 0.1° × 0.1° resolution were obtained from GloH2O (https://www.gloh2o.org/). The dataset is calibrated for 1979–2000 and validated across 2001–2014 time period. Daily precipitation was retrieved from the Multi-Source Weighted-Ensemble Precipitation (MSWEP) dataset (https://www.gloh2o.org/mswep), which merges gauge observations, satellite-based estimates, and reanalysis products32. MSWEP has been shown to effectively capture the spatiotemporal variability of the Indian Summer Monsoon Rainfall (ISMR) across hydro-climatically diverse regions of the subcontinent33. Daily maximum and minimum temperatures were obtained from the Multi-Source Weather (MSWX) dataset34, which integrates multiple observational sources with downscaling and bias-correction techniques. MSWX is designed to be compatible with MSWEP and has demonstrated improved agreement with observed meteorological conditions across Indian river basins35. Additionally, MSWX datasets has been previously utilized for producing statistically downscaled dataset over East Asia28.

For historical (1979–2014) and future (2015–2100) periods, we obtained daily precipitation and temperature (maximum and minimum) projections from 14 CMIP6 GCMs (Table 1) through the Earth System Grid Federation archive (https://pcmdi.llnl.gov/CMIP6/). Projections were available under four Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs): SSP126, SSP245, SSP370, and SSP585, used in the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report36,37. These scenarios combine socio-economic narratives with radiative forcing targets by 2100, ranging from a strong mitigation pathway (SSP126: 2.6 Wm−2) to a high-emission pathway (SSP585: 8.5 Wm−2). For consistency, all three variables across all SSPs were retrieved only for these 14 GCMs (details in Table 1), using the r1i1p1f1 realization at daily resolution.

Bias correction and downscaling framework

Outputs of daily precipitation, maximum, and minimum temperature from GCMs typically exhibit systematic biases, necessitating bias correction to obtain reliable estimates at regional and local scales for climate impact assessments. To address this, we employed the Double Bias Correction Constructed Analogues (DBCCA31) statistical downscaling model to generate high-resolution climate data. DBCCA extends the Bias Correction Constructed Analogues (BCCA11) approach by introducing an additional round of bias correction to mitigate known BCCA limitations such as drizzle effects and residual biases.

DBCCA involves four main steps: steps 1–2 are conducted on coarse-resolution GCM grids, while steps 3–4 are applied on finer reference/observation grids (0.1° × 0.1°). In step 1, reference data are aggregated to the GCM grid, followed by bias correction of daily GCM outputs using quantile mapping11. In step 2, for each GCM day, a 91-day (±45 days) moving window is used to construct a historical library of reference data across all years. A total of 30 analogs from the historical library that best match the spatial pattern of the target GCM day are then identified, and their weights are estimated using statistical ridge regression11. This weighted combination represents the coarse-resolution constructed analogue (CA). In step 3, the downscaled version (0.1° × 0.1°) is obtained by applying the same analogue maps and weights corresponding to observed days at finer resolution. This procedure preserves the daily sequencing of weather events, thereby capturing day-to-day variability beyond large-scale forcing, while producing sharper spatial gradients of precipitation and temperature changes11. Finally, in step 4, a second daily-scale Quantile mapping (QM) bias-correction is applied to adjust residual biases introduced during the CA construction. For future projections, bias correction employed Detrended QM, with month-wise trends estimated separately38

In this study, the DBCCA method was calibrated for each GCM using reference datasets (MSWEP for precipitation and MSWX for maximum and minimum temperature) over the historical period 1979 to 2000 (Fig. 2). Leap year days were excluded from both reference and model data prior to calibration. Model validation was performed for 2001 to 2014 using mean absolute error (MAE) and bias as performance metrics. Finally, the calibrations were then applied to produce bias corrected, downscaled outputs for historical (1950 to 2014) and future (2015 to 2100) simulations under SSP126, SSP245, SSP370, and SSP585 scenarios, for precipitation, maximum, and minimum temperature across all GCMs. Further, an MME mean dataset was also generated for each variable and scenario (Fig. 2). Note that following standard practice, a threshold of 0.1 mm is applied to the precipitation time series, whereby values below 0.1 mm are set to 0 after downscaling39.

Data Records

Downscaled and bias-corrected multi-model ensemble (MME) mean daily precipitation40 (pr_ensemble.zip; 10.5281/zenodo.18789030), maximum temperature41 (tmax_ensemble.zip; 10.5281/zenodo.18789439), and minimum temperature42 (tmin_ensemble.zip; 10.5281/zenodo.18789422) over the Indian subcontinent at high spatial resolution (0.1° × 0.1°) for the historical period (1979–2014) and future projections (2015–2100) under four Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP126, SSP245, SSP370, and SSP585) are compressed into a single.zip file for each variable and made available via Zenodo. Each variable is provided in NetCDF (.nc) format at daily temporal resolution, structured as three-dimensional arrays (longitude: 65.5°E–100°E, latitude: 5°N–40°N, and time beginning 1 January 1979). The ensemble historical file size is approximately 8.5 GB per variable, and the ensemble future projection file is approximately 11.3 GB per variable, resulting in ~53.7 GB per variable and a total Zenodo archive size of approximately 161.1 GB. In addition to the archived MME datasets, the downscaled and bias-corrected outputs for the individual 14 CMIP6 GCMs are available via Google Drive (https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1kW-B8ME17LIB1YB1ZZP2yoFPDarQMnUH?usp=sharing), organized under the directory structure /{variable}/{model_name}/{scenarios}, where {variable} represents pr, tmax, or tmin, {model_name} refers to each individual GCM, and {scenarios} includes the historical and four SSP scenarios; these individual model files are approximately 8.5 GB (historical) or 11.3 GB (future) each, amounting to ~806 GB per variable and an overall dataset size of approximately 2.4 TB. The data usage information is provided in Supplementary Text S2 and Supplementary Fig. S2, and a sample monthly precipitation dataset (ACCESS-CM2, historical) is included to facilitate data exploration.

Technical Validation

Seasonality in Raw and DBCCA Downscaled Multi-Model Ensembles

Figure 3 presents the seasonal cycles of precipitation, Tmax, and Tmin based on MME derived from raw and downscaled GCM outputs, alongside the corresponding reference datasets. The shaded regions represent uncertainties, defined as ± one standard deviation from the ensemble mean, across 14 CMIP6 GCMs. In the raw MME, precipitation is systematically underestimated during the Indian Summer Monsoon Rainfall season (June–September; JJAS), with a substantially large inter-model spread (MAE: 0.38 mm/day), reflecting uncertainties from internal variability and model biases. Tmax is overestimated (MAE: 0.76 °C) during Indian Summer Monsoon Rainfall, whereas Tmin is consistently underestimated (MAE: 1.47 °C) throughout the year (Fig. 3a,c,e). Following bias correction and downscaling, the seasonal cycles show marked improvement, with model estimates aligning more closely with the reference data for all three variables (Fig. 3b,d,f). Notably, the reduction in errors during the Indian Summer Monsoon Rainfall months for precipitation and the improved representation of summer Tmax and Tmin are evident. Overall, the downscaled MME reduces the MAE to 0.24 mm/day, 0.20 °C, and 0.16 °C for precipitation, Tmax, and Tmin, respectively, while also narrowing uncertainties, thereby enhancing both the accuracy and consistency of the simulations.

Seasonal cycle of precipitation, maximum temperature, and minimum temperature during the validation period (2001-2014) for raw and downscaled GCMs. Red line represents MSWEP (for precipitation) and MSWX (for Tasmax and Tasmin) while blue line depicts the multi-model ensemble mean of 14 CMIP6-GCMs. The shaded area represents uncertainties (± one standard deviation) of all 14 CMIP6-GCMs.

Performance evaluation of raw and DBCCA downscaled datasets

To evaluate the effectiveness of the DBCCA approach in correcting systematic biases, we assessed the mean bias and MAE of precipitation, Tmax, and Tmin from 14 CMIP6 GCMs during the validation period 2001–2014, relative to the reference dataset. Raw GCM outputs were first bilinearly interpolated to the reference grid. For each dataset (raw, downscaled, and reference), daily climatological means were computed for 2001–2014 across all 365 Julian days. Bias and MAE were then calculated for each daily climatological mean and subsequently averaged across the year to obtain the overall mean bias and MAE.

Figure 4 shows the spatial distribution of mean bias from the MME of raw and downscaled simulations. The raw MME exhibits a pronounced dry bias (>3 mm/day) in mean annual precipitation over the Bay of Bengal and most of India, except for the southern peninsula, consistent with earlier studies43–45. This dry bias is primarily linked to a ~10° southward displacement of the Intertropical Convergence Zone in CMIP6 GCMs relative to its observed position (~22°N) during the Indian summer monsoon46. In contrast, positive precipitation biases appear over the Tibetan Plateau, southern Myanmar (excluding coastal regions), and southern Peninsular India (Fig. 4a). For temperature, the raw MME shows a strong cold bias in both Tmax and Tmin over the Himalayan region (Fig. 4c, e). Moreover, across large parts of the Indian subcontinent and northern Pakistan, a warm bias is evident in Tmax, whereas Tmin exhibits a cold bias. Application of the DBCCA approach at the daily scale substantially reduces these systematic errors. The corrected and downscaled outputs show marked reductions in both mean bias and MAE across all three variables (Fig. 4b,d,f), demonstrating the effectiveness of the DBCCA-based downscaling framework. Furthermore, Spatial Stratified Heterogeneity (SSH) in precipitation was evaluated and is presented in Supplementary Text S1 and Supplementary Fig. S1.

Annual mean bias from the multi-model ensemble (MME) of 14 CMIP6 GCMs for the validation period (2001–2014). Panels show mean bias in (a,b) precipitation (mm/day), (c,d) maximum temperature (°C), and (e,f) minimum temperature (°C) for raw (left) and downscaled (right) simulations.

The MAE of precipitation in raw MME (Fig. 5a,b) is largest along the Western Ghats, the Himalayan ranges, the Myanmar coastline, and the northern Bay of Bengal, whereas relatively smaller errors are found over the Tibetan Plateau and Pakistan. For temperature, both Tmax and Tmin show high MAE (>4 °C) over the Tibetan Plateau and the Himalayas, with additional Tmin hotspots across Rajasthan and adjoining northwestern India. In contrast, comparatively low errors are noted over the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal. Application of the DBCCA-based downscaling framework substantially reduces MAE across all three variables, particularly in regions of complex topography such as the Western Ghats, the Himalayan foothills, and the Tibetan Plateau. The improvements are especially notable for Tmax and Tmin (Fig. 5d,f), where large warm and cold biases in the raw MME (Fig. 4c,e) translate into elevated MAE that are considerably diminished after DBCCA-based downscaling.

Annual mean MAE from the multi-model ensemble (MME) of 14 CMIP6 GCMs for the validation period (2001–2014). Panels show mean MAE in (a,b) precipitation (mm/day), (c,d) maximum temperature (°C), and (e,f) minimum temperature (°C) for raw (left) and downscaled (right) simulations.

Further insights are provided by the boxplots of annual mean bias and MAE for precipitation, Tmax, and Tmin across the 14 GCMs and their MME (Fig. 6). The whiskers denote the 10th and 90th percentiles of bias and MAE for each model. For precipitation, raw GCMs exhibit a broad bias range (–3 to +3 mm/day; Fig. 6a) and high MAE (1.5–4.5 mm/day; Fig. 6b). After DBCCA approach, precipitation biases are centered near zero (interquartile range ~ –0.5 to 0.5 mm/day), and MAE is reduced to ~1–2 mm/day with markedly lower inter-model spread. For Tmax, raw models generally show a warm bias and elevated MAE, whereas the downscaled outputs reduce both metrics to ~0–1 °C for most models (Fig. 6c,d). Tmin errors are the largest in raw GCMs, with biases and MAE of 2–6 °C (whiskers extending to 10–12 °C; Fig. 6e,f); after downscaling, these are constrained to <1–2 °C with substantially reduced spread. Overall, the significant reduction in both bias and MAE across raw and downscaled simulations demonstrates the robustness of the DBCCA approach in improving the spatial representation and magnitude of precipitation and temperature over the region.

Biases in the frequency of extreme events

Figure 7 shows the biases in the frequency of extreme precipitation, Tmax, and Tmin events in raw and downscaled simulations relative to the reference dataset for the validation period 2001-2014. Extreme events were defined as the total number of days exceeding the 95th percentile threshold derived from the reference data over the same period. Across all three variables, the raw GCMs exhibit substantial biases and wide inter-model spreads, diverging markedly from the reference data and highlighting significant challenges in capturing the observed frequency of extremes. The downscaled data, however, demonstrate clear improvements, with a consistent reduction in both the mean bias and the interquartile range. This indicates not only enhanced accuracy in simulating the frequency of extreme events but also improved consistency across the 14 CMIP6 GCMs. These results underscore the effectiveness of the bias-correction and downscaling framework in reducing systematic errors, thereby providing a more robust representation of extremes in the Indian subcontinent.

Extremes are defined as the number of days exceeding the 95th percentile threshold from the reference data.
