Synchronous Bilateral Carotid Body and Bilateral Vagal Paragangliomas: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Nahar Alanezi, Yazeed Alofisan, Mohammed Dahman, Hassan Gado, Majid Althobaiti, Omar Tawfiq Abualnasr, Tariq Alanezi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Head and neck paragangliomas (HNPGLs) are rare neuroendocrine neoplasms that may be sporadic or hereditary and may occur as isolated, multifocal, or syndromic disease within the pheochromocytoma-paraganglioma spectrum. Synchronous bilateral carotid body tumors (CBTs) and bilateral vagal paragangliomas (VPGLs) are exceptional, particularly when biochemical activity and metastatic nodal involvement coexist. Case Presentation: We report a 33-year-old man with a 2-year history of enlarging bilateral neck masses, positive family history, and markedly elevated noradrenaline (6430 pg/mL; reference range <750 pg/mL). Initial CT angiography suggested bilateral CBTs, and Metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) scintigraphy did not demonstrate abnormal adrenal or distant uptake. After alpha- and beta-blockade, staged surgery was performed. Right CBT excision demonstrated metastatic PGL, with two of four lymph nodes positive. Subsequent MRI and operative reassessment revealed synchronous bilateral CBTs and bilateral VPGLs. Left-sided surgery required partial debulking of a vagal-adherent mass to preserve nerve continuity; pathology confirmed PGL with Ki-67 index approximately 2% and left neck nodes were negative. Postoperatively, profound bradycardia required temporary then permanent pacing, together with bilateral vocal cord paralysis. During follow-up, swallowing and voice improved, the pacemaker was removed, and late imaging showed stable residual cervical disease without visceral metastasis on chest/abdominal CT. Conclusions: This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic complexity of multicentric, biochemically active HNPGLs and supports individualized multidisciplinary management, genetic counseling, biochemical surveillance, and long-term follow-up.

1. Introduction

Paragangliomas (PGLs) are rare, highly vascular neuroendocrine tumors arising from extra-adrenal paraganglia derived from the embryonic neural crest [1]. They belong to the broader pheochromocytoma-paraganglioma (PPGL) spectrum, which includes adrenal pheochromocytomas and sympathetic or parasympathetic extra-adrenal PGLs. Head and neck PGLs (HNPGLs) account for approximately 3% of all PGLs and predominantly originate from the parasympathetic paraganglia, most frequently at the carotid body, followed by the jugular bulb, tympanic plexus, and the ganglion nodosum of the vagus nerve [1,2]. Clinically, carotid body tumors (CBTs) are often indolent and present as slow-growing, painless lateral neck masses; however, proximity to major vessels and lower cranial nerves may create substantial diagnostic and therapeutic complexity even when lesions are histologically low grade [3,4].

Modern PPGL management increasingly emphasizes systematic endocrine and genetic assessment because HNPGLs may be hereditary, multicentric, metachronous, associated with pheochromocytoma or extra-head-and-neck PGLs across the lifespan, and occasionally catecholamine-secreting [5,6,7,8]. Plasma-free or urinary fractionated metanephrines are recommended as initial biochemical tests in suspected PPGL, and functional tumors require alpha-adrenergic blockade before surgery [9]. Although many parasympathetic HNPGLs are clinically nonsecretory, recent data indicate that clinically meaningful catecholamine secretion may be present in approximately 7.7% to 9.2% of HNPGLs, particularly among CBTs, cervical sympathetic PGLs, and SDHB-associated disease [6]. Therefore, characterization of multifocal HNPGL should include anatomical imaging, biochemical assessment, hereditary-risk evaluation, and consideration of whole-body functional imaging when multifocal or metastatic disease is suspected [3,10,11].

Synchronous involvement of CBTs and vagal PGLs (VPGLs) in the same patient remains rare, and reports combining bilateral CBTs with bilateral VPGLs are exceptional [12,13]. The objective of this report was to describe a rare case of synchronous bilateral CBTs and bilateral VPGLs with biochemical activity, histologically confirmed regional nodal metastasis, severe postoperative bradyarrhythmia, and delayed functional recovery, and to contextualize this case against the available literature on multicentric cervical PGLs.

2. Case Presentation

2.1. Initial Presentation and Diagnosis

A 33-year-old man with no history of chronic medical illness presented with bilateral neck masses that had gradually increased in size over 2 years. There was no history of preceding hypertension, sustained arrhythmia, flushing, carcinoid-like symptoms, collapse/syncope, or hyperadrenergic crises before the initial imaging assessment. A positive family history was recorded, with relatives reported to have had neck masses treated by different methods, although no definite diagnosis had been established from the available family records. On 8 March 2020, initial diagnostic evaluation with computed tomography (CT) angiography, before catecholamine testing was completed, demonstrated bilateral enhancing lesions/tumors arising at the bifurcation of both carotid arteries, encasing the internal carotid arteries and splaying the external carotid arteries bilaterally (Figure 1). No contrast-associated adrenergic crisis occurred during or after CT angiography. The right-sided lesion measured approximately 6.5 × 4 cm and showed some necrotic change, whereas the left-sided lesion measured approximately 6.4 × 3.7 cm. At the time of the initial assessment, these lesions were considered CBTs. Given the potential for a functional PGL, biochemical screening was conducted. Plasma catecholamine levels were markedly elevated, with a noradrenaline level of 6430 pg/mL (reference range <750 pg/mL). Plasma free or urinary fractionated metanephrines, normetanephrines, methoxytyramine, dopamine, serotonin, and 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid were not measured. A subsequent Metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) scan was performed to screen for adrenal, extra-adrenal, or distant metastatic disease, which showed no evidence of distant metastases at that time.

2.2. Pre-Operative Management and First Surgery

The patient was evaluated by the endocrinology team and underwent preoperative medical preparation. Prazosin was administered for alpha-adrenergic blockade before atenolol was added for rate control after alpha blockade had been initiated. This sequencing was used to reduce the risk of intraoperative hypertensive crisis in the setting of biochemical catecholamine excess. After an adequate period of medical preparation, a staged surgical approach was planned, beginning with the slightly larger right-sided tumor. Intraoperatively, the circumscribed tumor was identified at the carotid bifurcation. Multiple enlarged regional lymph nodes were also noted and excised. The procedure was completed without immediate neurovascular complications. Histopathological examination of the resected specimen confirmed PGL, with clear surgical margins and no capsular invasion. Lymph node assessment demonstrated metastatic disease, with two of four excised regional lymph nodes positive for PGL. Nodal stations, Ki-67 index, or the complete immunohistochemical panel were not specified in the first surgical pathology report.

2.3. Post-Operative Evaluation and Planning for Second Surgery

Following confirmation of histologically confirmed nodal metastatic disease, a staging CT scan of the neck, chest, and abdomen, including the adrenal regions, was performed and it did not identify adrenal lesions or distant visceral metastases in these regions. Retrospective review of the CT imaging, including coronal reconstructions, clarified the right-sided anatomy as two adjacent right parapharyngeal/cervical masses separated by a visible cleavage plane, rather than a single confluent lesion. In contrast, on the left side, a single mass was identified without a separating cleavage plane. Interval reassessment with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the neck on 7 October 2020, demonstrated no substantial interval change in the cervical disease burden compared with prior imaging. The right-sided lesion measured 3.8 × 3.2 × 3.1 cm, and the left-sided lesion measured approximately 5 × 3.3 × 2.7 cm (Figure 2).

The right-sided tumor indented the right hypopharynx and caused slight narrowing of the lumen at the level of the oropharynx. Multiple signal-void areas were present within the tumor, attributed in the report to prominent vessels. Diffusion imaging demonstrated multiple slightly prominent lymph nodes in the posterior triangle with post-contrast enhancement. The MRI also described a small oval T2-hyperintense focus in the right side of the T2 vertebral body, measuring approximately 4 × 34 mm, which did not suppress on fat-saturated images and enhanced after intravenous contrast (Figure 3). The overall impression was bilateral CBTs and bilateral VPGLs with slightly prominent bilateral cervical lymphadenopathy in the deep posterior triangles and a small focal T2 vertebral body lesion that was radiologically suspicious for possible metastasis but was not histologically confirmed.

Initial cross-sectional imaging was interpreted as demonstrating a single large parapharyngeal mass; however, intraoperative findings and subsequent detailed radiologic review established the presence of two anatomically distinct lesions, consistent with synchronous bilateral CBTs and bilateral VPGLs, biochemical activity, and regional lymph node metastasis. On retrospective review, subtle splaying of the carotid bifurcation and a separate cranial extension along the expected course of the vagus nerve were appreciated, although these findings had not been clearly distinguishable preoperatively because of mass effect. The coexistence of bilateral CBTs and bilateral VPGLs was therefore definitively recognized only after integration of operative findings with detailed radiological re-review and MRI. The case was discussed in a multidisciplinary team meeting involving vascular surgery, head and neck surgery, and endocrinology. A decision was made to proceed with surgery for left-sided neck mass excision with modified neck dissection and possible right-sided completion at a later stage. The patient was again prepared for surgery and was given the same antihypertensive medications.

2.4. Second Surgery and Post-Operative Course

On 12 October 2020, the patient underwent left-sided carotid mass excision with neck dissection. Intraoperatively, the tumor was found to be densely adherent to the left vagus nerve. To preserve the continuity and potential function of the nerve, complete resection was not feasible, and a partial debulking of the mass was performed. A full left neck dissection was completed on the left side. Histopathological examination of the specimen confirmed a PGL with a Ki-67 index of approximately 2%. All examined left-sided lymph nodes were negative for tumor involvement (jugular, 0/3; level IIa, 0/1; levels III and VI, 0/12). Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for monitoring and was initially extubated successfully.

Upon a trial of oral water intake, severe coughing occurred, with no reported aspiration, followed by profound bradycardia and a near-arrest episode that necessitated re-intubation. In the ICU, he experienced multiple episodes of severe bradycardia, with heart rates decreasing to 30 beats per minute. Cardiology consultation was obtained, and a temporary pacemaker was inserted. Bilateral vocal cord paralysis was also documented, likely reflecting vagal-region involvement and surgical manipulation; the left-sided deficit occurred postoperatively, whereas the right-sided deficit was attributed to tumor-related mass effect or prior right-sided surgical manipulation that had not been clinically apparent. His condition gradually improved, and no fever or other apparent signs of infection were documented. Continued cardiology follow-up led to a recommendation for permanent pacemaker insertion, which was subsequently performed. Thereafter, his overall condition improved. He reported some dizziness but was able to walk without severe bradycardia, and his heart rate was documented as 70 beats per minute; he was then transferred to the ward. He was maintained on procyclidine 2.5 mg orally twice daily, later increased to 5 mg orally twice daily. The wound healed without reported complications.

2.5. Follow-Up

Follow-up CT of the neck on 19 November 2020 showed that the residual left-sided mass had regressed in size to 3.0 × 1.6 cm after debulking, while the right-sided lesion showed no interval change in size. The study also suggested left vocal cord paralysis, based on medialization of the left vocal cord and an enlarged laryngeal ventricle. Serial postoperative catecholamine or metanephrine testing was not performed after each operation. No postoperative somatostatin receptor imaging with OctreoScan or 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT was undertaken, and adjuvant octreotide therapy was not initiated. By approximately 1 year postoperatively, the permanent pacemaker had been removed, and the patient reported no further hoarseness or choking episodes. Excision of the right vagal tumor was offered by the head and neck surgeon; however, the patient declined after counseling regarding potential complications. At subsequent follow-up, Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA) of the neck on 15 January 2026 reportedly demonstrated no change in size compared with the most recent prior imaging (2023) (Figure 4), and CT of the chest and abdomen showed no evidence of visceral metastasis, as shown in Table 1.

3. Discussion

Although many HNPGLs follow an indolent course, their clinical importance lies in their hypervascularity, proximity to major vessels and lower cranial nerves, potential hereditary basis, occasional catecholamine secretion, and the functional consequences of treatment [3,14]. Multicentricity has been reported in 10% to 20% of sporadic cases and may approach 80% in familial PGL syndromes [15,16,17]. Within this spectrum, synchronous CBTs and vagal involvement in the same patient is distinctly uncommon, and the literature remains dominated by isolated case reports rather than robust comparative series (Table 2) [12,13,18]. Therefore, the present case is notable because it combined multicentric bilateral cervical distribution, biochemical activity, histologically confirmed regional nodal metastasis, complex operative anatomy, and severe postoperative cardiolaryngeal morbidity.

Current evidence supports a hereditary framework for a substantial proportion of HNPGLs, and guideline-oriented studies recommend genetic counseling and consideration of germline testing for all patients with PGL, particularly when the disease is bilateral, multifocal, metastatic, or presents at a relatively young age [3,28]. Up to 40% of HNPGLs are associated with genetic mutations, predominantly in the succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) complex genes (SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD) [3,29,30]. SDHD mutations are classically associated with multifocal parasympathetic HNPGL and demonstrate a parent-of-origin effect, with disease manifesting predominantly after paternal inheritance. In contrast, SDHB mutations are more often associated with extra-adrenal sympathetic PGLs and a higher risk of metastatic disease than SDHD-associated tumors [31,32,33]. In the large Papaspyrou series of 175 patients, multifocality and mutation-positive disease were important recurring themes in clinical evaluation [17]. More recently, Bellamkonda et al. analyzed 49 patients with bilateral HNPGLs comprising 116 tumors, including 90 CBTs and 15 VPGLs, further highlighting that bilateral disease is a distinct management subset [34]. These observations are highly relevant to the present patient because he presented at 33 years of age with bilateral cervical masses and a positive family history of relatives with neck masses. Although formal molecular testing had not been performed, this phenotype strongly supports referral for genetic counseling, germline testing, family evaluation, and long-term surveillance [35].

HNPGLs typically present as slow-growing, painless, pulsatile lateral neck masses. Symptoms often arise from the mass effect on adjacent lower cranial nerves (CN IX-XII), leading to hoarseness, dysphagia, or Horner’s syndrome [2]. The present patient had a 2-year history of progressively enlarging bilateral neck masses, consistent with the slow growth pattern reported for these tumors (mean growth rate of 1–2 mm/year) [36]. Most HNPGLs have historically been considered non-secretory; however, contemporary data indicate that clinically meaningful catecholamine excess is not negligible [6,28,37]. Smith et al. reported that clinically significant catecholamine secretion was present in 9.2% of patients and 7.7% of tumors, with enrichment among CBTs and SDHB-associated disease [6]. This finding is clinically important because biochemical activity influences perioperative preparation, cardiovascular risk, and interpretation of symptoms [38]. In the current patient, the markedly elevated noradrenaline level justified preoperative alpha- then beta-blockade and distinguishes this case from many published HNPGL reports in which biochemical activity was absent or not reported.

The anatomical distribution and size of HNPGLs dictate the surgical approach and associated morbidity. This case featured bilateral lesions initially thought to be isolated CBTs. However, intraoperative findings and retrospective imaging review revealed a complex combination of bilateral CBTs and bilateral VPGLs. CBTs characteristically arise at the carotid bifurcation, splay the internal and external carotid arteries (Lyre sign), and are graded by the Shamblin classification according to carotid vessel encasement [39]. VPGLs typically originate more cephalad from the nodose ganglion and displace the internal carotid artery anteriorly [12]. The synchronous presence of four anatomically distinct parapharyngeal and cervical tumors suggests marked multifocality, a phenotype reported in SDHx-associated disease [4].

Standard anatomical evaluation relies on contrast-enhanced CT and MRI, which define carotid bifurcation splaying, cranial extension, skull-base proximity, and vascular encasement [3,37]. However, in multifocal or closely adjacent lesions, anatomical distinction may be difficult when one tumor distorts the tissue planes of another. In the present case, the diagnosis of synchronous bilateral CBTs and bilateral VPGLs was established only after integration of operative findings, MRI, and detailed radiological re-review. The modern diagnostic pathway has also evolved beyond anatomic imaging alone. Lin et al. recommended 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT as first-line functional imaging when multifocal or metastatic disease is suspected [3]. Janssen et al. demonstrated that 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT localized more HNPGL lesions than conventional anatomical imaging and other functional modalities in parasympathetic HNPGLs [10]. Although MIBG scintigraphy did not demonstrate adrenal or distant uptake at initial work-up, current evidence supports 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT as the preferred functional imaging modality when multifocal or metastatic HNPGL is suspected [11].

Multifocality should not be equated with metastatic behavior. In PPGL, metastatic disease is defined by tumor presence in sites lacking normal paraganglionic tissue rather than by local invasion or histological features alone [3,37]. Clinically evident metastatic behavior occurs in approximately 5% of CBTs, whereas VPGLs appear to carry a higher metastatic propensity, with reviews citing metastatic rates of roughly 10% to 19% [37,40]. In the present case, PGL involvement in two of four regional lymph nodes after the first operation established histologically confirmed regional metastatic disease. By contrast, the T2 vertebral focus was radiologically suspicious but was not biopsy-confirmed; therefore, it should be regarded as a possible, rather than definitive, distant metastasis. This distinction differentiates the current patient from several comparator reports describing combined CBTs/VPGLs without documented nodal metastatic spread [12,13,41]. Long-term survival for metastatic HNPGLs is variable; lymph node metastases may follow an indolent course, whereas distant metastases involving bone or lung may be associated with worse outcomes and may require consideration of systemic or radionuclide-based therapies in selected patients [42].

Management of HNPGLs has shifted from a uniformly aggressive surgical approach toward individualized decision-making that balances tumor control against long-term function [14]. Factors influencing treatment choice include age, comorbidity, tumor size and site, bilaterality, growth, secretory status, baseline cranial nerve function, metastatic behavior, and patient preference [14,28]. CBTs and VPGLs do not carry equivalent operative risk. In the bilateral HNPGL series by Bellamkonda et al., CBTs had the lowest risk of postoperative cranial nerve deficits after surgery compared with vagal and jugular lesions [34]. Del Guercio et al. reported preoperative cranial nerve palsy in 36% of patients, new postoperative nerve dysfunction in 16%, and persistent deficits in 12% in a mixed cervical series managed at an experienced center [43]. For catecholamine-secreting tumors, preoperative alpha-adrenergic blockade before beta-blockade is recommended to reduce perioperative adrenergic risk [9]. For bilateral tumors, concurrent bilateral resection is generally avoided because of the risk of bilateral lower cranial nerve palsy and baroreflex failure [44]. The staged, single-center multidisciplinary strategy used in the present case, with right-sided resection performed first and left-sided surgery undertaken subsequently after recognition of dense vagal adherence, is consistent with a function-conscious approach in bilateral or multicentric HNPGLs [45]. Complete resection was not pursued at the cost of definite nerve sacrifice; instead, partial debulking was undertaken to preserve nerve continuity. This approach aligns with literature supporting selective resection, radiotherapy, or surveillance in anatomically high-risk lesions rather than routine radical excision [28,40]. Functional morbidity remains one of the most important determinants of outcome in HNPGL management, especially when the vagus nerve is involved. Lower cranial nerve dysfunction may manifest as hoarseness, dysphagia, aspiration risk, shoulder dysfunction, or the need for voice and swallowing interventions. Neskey et al. emphasized that postoperative outcomes after HNPGL resection should be judged not only by tumor control, but also by functionally meaningful endpoints such as feeding-tube or tracheostomy dependence, need for vocal fold medialization, and cerebrovascular complications [46]. In the current case, the postoperative bilateral vocal cord paralysis and transient swallowing difficulty reflected the recognized functional burden of surgery for vagal-region disease. The most distinctive complication was the severe postoperative bradyarrhythmia requiring temporary and then permanent pacing, followed by later pacemaker removal after recovery. Major bradycardic events of this severity are uncommon in the HNPGL literature but biologically plausible through carotid sinus hypersensitivity, vagal reflex activation, or manipulation of autonomic structures during surgery [47]. Bauer et al. described carotid sinus hypersensitivity after preoperative embolization of a CBT that required temporary pacemaker placement before definitive resection [48], and Duan et al. reported intraoperative cardiac arrest during CBT resection attributable to carotid sinus hypersensitivity [25]. The present case indicates that profound postoperative bradycardia may occur in parallel with vagal-related laryngeal dysfunction after complex HNPGLs surgery and that both complications may improve over prolonged multidisciplinary follow-up.

Limitations of the Current Case Report

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, endocrine characterization was incomplete; biochemical evaluation showed markedly elevated noradrenaline, but plasma or urinary metanephrines, normetanephrine, dopamine, methoxytyramine, serotonin/5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid, and serial postoperative biochemical values were not measured. Second, germline testing of the patient and endocrine/genetic evaluation of relatives were not performed despite young age, family history, bilateral disease, multifocality, and metastatic nodal involvement. Third, radiological and pathological characterization relied on formal reports because source MIBG images, pathological slides, detailed immunohistochemistry, and pathological micrographs were not provided for publication. Additionally, recognized predictors of difficult resections, such as the distance of the tumor to the base of the skull [49], were not assessed. Fourth, the T2 vertebral lesion was radiologically suspicious but not histologically confirmed. These limitations reinforce the need for comprehensive biochemical testing, genetic counseling, functional imaging, family assessment, and structured long-term surveillance in similar cases.

4. Conclusions

This real-world case demonstrates a rare presentation of synchronous bilateral CBTs with bilateral VPGLs in a young patient with positive family history, biochemical activity, histologically confirmed regional nodal metastasis, staged surgical management, severe postoperative bradyarrhythmia, bilateral vocal cord dysfunction, and delayed functional recovery. Several practical messages can be drawn. First, bilateral or multifocal HNPGLs should prompt consideration of hereditary disease, genetic counseling, family evaluation, and long-term surveillance. Second, careful re-review of imaging and, where available, contemporary functional imaging may reveal multicentric disease not fully appreciated on initial assessment. Third, treatment should be individualized, particularly when vagal involvement creates a high risk of permanent lower cranial nerve morbidity. Finally, rare cardiovascular complications such as carotid sinus hypersensitivity or vagally mediated bradyarrhythmia should remain within the perioperative differential in complex HNPGL surgery. Despite the severe presentation, the patient’s subsequent functional improvement, pacemaker removal, stable residual cervical disease, and absence of visceral metastasis on late chest/abdominal imaging suggest a favorable clinical course to date, while continuing to justify prolonged multidisciplinary surveillance.
