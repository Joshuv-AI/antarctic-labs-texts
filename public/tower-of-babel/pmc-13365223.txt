Alkali metal N-heteroarenes as a new family of luminescent materials
Authors: Yuting Wang, Khai Chen Tan, Hong Wen, Panwang Zhou, Yang Yu, Jiaquan Guo, Jinglu Sun, Chong Fang, Xin Guo, Jingwei Guo, Teng He, Ping Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organic-based luminescent materials have broad applications across illumination, display, and imaging. However, intramolecular vibrations and intermolecular interactions, such as π-π stacking, can induce non-radiative transition and lead to low efficiency and fluorescence quenching for solid-state materials, especially for small conjugated molecules. In the present study, a facile yet versatile conceptual approach to fabricate a new type of luminescent materials, i.e., alkali metal N-heteroarene salts, was developed via a metal substitution strategy. Over 30 solid-state ionic compounds have been designed and synthesized accordingly. These compounds possess rigid crystal structures through forming an ionic bonding network of metal‒N bonds and cation-π interactions, which can minimize the vibrations of individual molecules, thereby suppressing non-radiative transition. Consequently, some small conjugated molecules with negligible fluorescence, such as imidazole, indole, and azaindole, become strong emitters upon alkali metal substitution. The alkali metal substitution forms an N-heterocyclic anion owing to the Lewis acidity of the alkali metal cation, thus increasing the electron density on the conjugated system. Concurrently, the formation of N-heterocyclic anions tailors band gaps of these organic salts, enabling diverse fluorescent emissions across the visible spectrum. The applications of these organic salts in information anti-counterfeiting are also demonstrated successfully.

Introduction

Light is one of the most indispensable but mysterious entities to human beings1. For instance, artificial lighting accounts for one-fifth of global electricity consumption. Nowadays, tremendous research efforts have been given to organic-based luminescent materials thanks to their broad applications in illumination2, display3, detection4, cellular imaging5, fluorescence probes6, information security7, and so on. A wide range of visible-spectrum emission is needed for these emitters, especially for the display applications8. To manipulate the color emitted by organic chromophores, different approaches, including metal coordination9, solid-state aggregation10, supramolecular assembly11, nanosizing12 etc., have been developed. Those approaches, however, are not viable for small molecules such as pyrrole, indole, carbazole, and their derivatives that are abundant and inexpensive.

Theoretically, small molecules could potentially emit light of different wavelengths after excitation because of their simple yet variable conjugated structures. However, intra/inter-molecular vibrations and intermolecular interactions, such as π‒π stacking, induce non-radiative transition in their molecular crystals, leading to low luminescence efficiency or bathochromic shifts13. Therefore, it is highly challenging to obtain efficient and diverse visible-spectrum emission with organic small molecules. Particularly, these small molecules are difficult to emit long-wavelength light due to their large HOMO-LUMO energy gaps. To realize diverse visible-spectrum emission of such molecules, efforts should be devoted to: (1) minimizing the non-radiative transition, e.g., intermolecular vibration-induced thermal relaxation; and, more importantly14, (2) modulating the electronic structure of the chromophore to adjust its emission color15. For the former, construction of a rigid structure through aggregation16,17, crystallization18, the formation of metal-organic frameworks19,20, or the confinement of isolated chromophores21,22, have been well demonstrated to effectively minimize the non-radiative transition and enhance the luminescence efficiency. For example, Tang and coworkers discovered an aggregation-induced emission (AIE) effect in organic fluorescent molecules and proposed a restriction of intramolecular motions (RIM) mechanism16. An et al. confined isolated chromophores within ionic crystals, which could inhibit the non-radiative transition induced by molecular vibrations and facilitate highly efficient blue phosphorescent emission21. However, based on the existing methods, the emission color of a chromophore cannot be regulated across a wide range since its electronic structure can only be modulated to a limited extent.

Herein, we developed a new class of fluorophores, i.e., alkali metal N-heteroarene salts, via a metal substitution strategy that transforms N-heteroarene molecular compounds into organic salt-based fluorophores, enabling a wide range of emission (Fig. 1a). Our structural analyses show that deprotonation of the N–H group induced by alkali metal substitution results in the formation of an N-heterocyclic anion because of the Lewis acidity of the alkali metal cation. This process enhances the electron density on the conjugated ring, hence modifying the HOMO-LUMO energy gap and tuning the emission property. Ionic compounds with rigid crystal structures can be effectively formed via this metal substitution strategy through the cation–N ionic bonding and cation–π interactions. These bonding modalities and interactions could effectively minimize the vibrations of individual molecules, therefore suppressing their thermal relaxation. Based on this strategy, over 30 compounds have been designed and synthesized, some of which, with negligible light emissions, can show enhanced photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQY) upon the metal substitution. Most importantly, these alkali metal N-heteroarene salts exhibit broad emissive regions covering the visible spectrum owing to the modulation of the electronic structures of N-heteroarenes by alkali metal and various types of N-heteroarene molecules. Besides, these materials also exhibit potential applications in information security and anticounterfeiting. Results

Materials design and syntheses

As mentioned above, small N-heteroarenes with sp2 hybridized conjugated systems usually exhibit negligible luminescence properties or low PLQY in solid form, although many of them in fact contain chromophores. This is mainly ascribed to the presence of intra/inter-molecular vibrations and intermolecular interactions (such as π-π interactions), which lead to fluorescence quenching23. Recently, we have developed a class of alkali metal organic salts for hydrogen storage and ion conduction24,25. These compounds contain cation‒N/O bonds and cation-π interactions, forming rigid ionic crystal structures that may effectively suppress fluorescence quenching. Meanwhile, the electron density of the organic conjugated system upon alkali metal substitution changes substantially as a result of the Lewis acidity of the alkali metal cations, thus forming N-heterocyclic anion. We believe that these organic salts, with characteristics of rigid ionic structures and varied electronic structures (Fig. 1a), may exhibit significantly different luminescent properties compared to their parent organic small molecules.

To validate our hypothesis, we synthesized more than 30 small N-heteroarene-based salts by replacing the protic H in various small N-heteroarene molecules with alkali metals through reacting alkali metal hydrides with these molecules (Fig. 1b). Here, we selected potassium 5-azaindolide (K-5-AID) as an example to demonstrate the preparation (Fig. S1 for K-5-AID and Figs. S2-S11 for other alkali metal N-heteroarene salts) and characterization of these alkali metal N-heteroarene salts. As shown in Fig. S1, ca. one equivalent of H2 was generated from the reaction between 5-azaindole (5-AID) and potassium hydride (KH). X-ray diffraction (XRD) characterization shows that K-5-AID exhibits a completely new set of diffraction peaks, which are distinct from those of the pristine 5-AID and KH precursors (Fig. S12). The disappearance of the N‒H signal in the 1H NMR indicates the successful removal of H(N) (Fig. S14). Meanwhile, compared to the parent molecule, the as-prepared material exhibits significant chemical shift changes in both ¹H and ¹³C NMR spectra (Figs. S13 and S14). In addition, energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and organic elemental analysis (EA) tests illustrated a uniform distribution of carbon (C), hydrogen (H), nitrogen (N), and potassium (K) throughout the K-5-AID sample (Fig. S15 and Table S1), confirming the formation of a new substance. Other protic H-containing N-heteroarenes, such as imidazole (IMD), indole (ID), carbazole (CZ), 5,11-dihydroindolo[3,2-b]carbazole (5,11-ICZ), and their derivatives, were also employed to react with alkali metal hydrides (LiH, NaH, and KH) to generate various alkali metal N-heteroarene salts (Fig. 1b). The XRD, NMR, and organic elemental analysis (EA) characterizations for these new organic salts are shown in Figs. S12–S47 and Table S1, and the abbreviations for these compounds are listed in Table S2.

Diverse emission color

The ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectra of the as-prepared organic salts were collected and compared with their corresponding parent compounds in the solid state (Figs. S48–S58). All the PLQYs of the synthesized organic salts were determined using the excitation wavelength at the maximum intensity in their excitation spectra (Figs. S59–66). Excitingly, the photoluminescence properties in terms of quantum yield and emission color are significantly changed upon forming organic salts (Fig. 2). Some of the pristine small organic molecules, such as imidazole (IMD), indole (ID), 5-azaindole (5-AID), 6-azaindole (6-AID), 7-azaindole (7-AID), and δ-carboline (δ-CB), exhibit negligible luminescence. In contrast, the PLQYs of K-IMD, Li-ID, Li-5-AID, K-5-AID, Na-6-AID, K-7-AID, and K-δ-CB are 35%, 47%, 43%, 54%, 26%, 45%, and 31% (Figs. 2a, S67–S74), respectively. Although CZ could exhibit a PLQY of 18% at solid-state, its sodium organic salt (Na-CZ) possesses a PLQY as high as 47%, ca. 2.5 times higher than that of pristine CZ (Figs. 2a, S71). It is worth mentioning that the five-membered ring salt, i.e., potassium imidazolide (K-IMD), could emit sky-blue light, representing the smallest N-heteroarene fluorophore ever reported (Figs. Equally important, the wavelength of emission is significantly changed upon forming alkali metal N-heteroarene salts, leading to various color emissions. For instance, although carbazole (CZ) can emit fluorescence with a peak at 415 nm in the solid state, substituting the protic H(N) with Na results in a strong photoluminescence peak for Na-CZ at around 503 nm, a ca. 88 nm shift from the pristine CZ (Fig. 2b). Similarly, disodium 5,11-dihydroindolo[3,2-b]carbazolide (2Na-5,11-ICZ) and dipotassium 5,11-dihydroindolo[3,2-b]carbazolide (2K-5,11-ICZ) exhibit significant redshifts of 110 nm and 140 nm, respectively, relative to the parent compound 5,11-ICZ (Fig. 2b), suggesting that stronger Lewis acidity of K substitution can lead to greater redshifts. The reason for these shifts will be further discussed in the following section. Accordingly, the CIE chromaticity coordinates of the CZ and 5,11-ICZ chromophores show significant changes in their fluorescence color upon forming the corresponding alkali metal organic salts (Fig. S77). It is deduced that, compared with the pristine N-heteroarenes that exhibit negligible or weak emission, their organic salts could achieve a wide range of fluorescence emission.

Therefore, Fig. 2c summarizes the photoluminescence (PL) spectra of 11 selected powders of these organic salts. Delightfully, their PL spectra cover almost the entire visible light region, ranging from 300 nm to 700 nm. A general trend is as follows: smaller compounds, exemplified by indole-based salts, emit bluer light, while larger compounds, such as ICZ-based salts, lie in the redder region. Accordingly, the emission colors, spanning from near-UV blue to green-yellow and extending to light-red, can be observed in the chromaticity coordinates established by the CIE shown in Fig. 2d and S78–S88. The inset photos in Fig. 1c depict the luminescence of these 11 organic salts under a UV lamp, demonstrating that all three primary colors (blue, green, and red) can be obtained. For instance, the high-frequency luminescence (near-UV or deep blue) has long been documented as a prime challenge for full-color display technologies in organic optoelectronics. Specifically, in the present study, near-UV luminescence can be obtained in K-5-AID, with an emission peak at 368 nm (3.37 eV) and a narrow full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 52 nm. In addition, Na-6-AID emits deep blue light with an emission peak at 400 nm (3.10 eV) and a CIE coordinate of (0.164, 0.075), which is very close to the standard blue chromaticity values (0.131, 0.046)27. On the other end of the spectrum, 2K-5,11-ICZ powder emits long-wavelength orange-red fluorescence. Upon adding dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) into 2K-5,11-ICZ (abbreviated as 2K-5,11-ICZ-DMSO in Fig. 2c), in which DMSO may act as a ligand and/or lattice solvent, we observed a redshift in the emission peak, yielding a chromaticity coordinate value close to the standard red light, probably because of the increased dielectric constant and stabilization of the excited state by the polar DMSO environment28. This redshifted emission was further validated by replacing solvents with different polarities, such as DMF and toluene. Accordingly, the results show a consistent redshift with increasing solvent dielectric constant (Fig. S89).

Notably, the problem of self-absorption, which is often observed in chromophores, can be avoided in these organic salts owing to their large Stokes shifts. For instance, K-5-AID and Na-CZ, with narrow FWHMs of 0.43 eV and 0.44 eV, exhibit Stokes shifts of 0.44 eV and 0.73 eV (Fig. 2e), respectively. The excitation wavelength-dependent PL for K-5-AID shows that the position and shape of PL peaks are independent of excitation wavelength (Fig. S90). The same conclusion applies to other alkali metal N-heteroarene salts (Figs. S91–S94). In addition, the time-resolved PL (TRPL) measurements of the organic salts demonstrated that the luminescence lifetimes of these powders last for nanoseconds, indicating their fluorescent nature (Figs. S95–S106). Table S3 summarizes the basic optical properties of the 11 selected organic salts.

Rigid ionic structure

To understand the enhanced luminescence of the newly developed organic salts, we investigated their structures and solved several well-crystallized materials. As an example, the structures of ionic K-5-AID and its parent molecule 5-AID were solved using powder XRD (Figs. S107–S108 and Table S4). The pristine 5-AID crystallizes in an orthorhombic structure, where the adjacent 5-AID molecules along the c-axis are aligned and linked continuously from head to toe through hydrogen bonding (pyrrole) N‒H···N (pyridine), forming the H-bond-connected AID chains with the shortest distance of 1.73 Å (Figs. 3a, 3b). The adjacent AID molecules between these AID chains interact through the parallel-displaced π-stacking (staggered stacking), arranged in a running bond pattern viewed in both the a and b directions (only the (100)-view is shown in Fig. 3a). It is worth mentioning that the significant dihedral angles between adjacent faces (49.25° and 36.84°), and the minimal overlap of conjugated π planes of adjacent 5-AID molecules would result in a weak π‒π interaction. Such weak intermolecular interactions could lead to the loosely packed 5-AID molecules, which may be evidenced by its low lattice formation energy (refer to Section 1.6 in Supplementary Information) and low melting temperature (Fig. S109). Upon deprotonation and substitution by K+, the resultant K-5-AID ionic compound also possesses an orthorhombic structure with a similar cell dimension (Table S4) but exhibits distinct cation-anion interactions, i.e., C7H5N2– anions and K cations are not only zigzagly linked through K‒N (pyrrole) bonds along a direction, but also connected through alternating K‒N (pyrrole) bonds and K‒N (pyridine) bonds along the c direction (Fig. 3c), establishing an ionic bonding network. In addition, K+ also interacts with two additional C7H5N2– anions via cation-π interactions, with the shortest distances of 2.87 Å and 3.12 Å recorded. The local coordination environments of the K+ and C7H5N2– anion are shown in Fig. 3d. Each K+ is surrounded by five C7H5N2– anions through three K‒N bonds (two from pyrrole N and one from pyridine N) and two cation-π interactions (Fig. 3d). Likewise, each C7H5N2– anion is tethered by five K+ via three K‒N bonds and two cation-π interactions (Fig. 3d). Apparently, the chromophore C7H5N2– group is strongly constrained within this rigid structure, and the strong cation-anion interactions will stabilize the crystal structure compared to the pristine 5-AID molecular compound. The strong interionic interactions and structural stabilization by metal substitution of K-5-AID may be partially supported by its higher melting point (ca. >107 °C, Figs. S109, S110) and exothermic formation energy (0.94 eV, experimental section 1.6 in Supplementary Information) compared to those of pristine 5-AID. Edge-to-face T-shaped π‒π stacking (i.e., pi-teeing interaction) is observed in the K-5-AID crystal (Fig. 3e), characterized by short distances (2.43–2.61 Å), and a near-perpendicular angle of 89.31°, forming a stable and rigid structure that reduces the vibrational degrees of freedom of molecular rings in the K-5-AID crystal framework. Other as-synthesized alkali organic salts also form rigid ionic crystal structures compared to their parent molecular compounds. For instance, carbazole molecules in its crystal structure stack in a herringbone manner, which leads to a weak π-π interaction, while Na-CZ constrains its chromophore (CZ anion) with strong Na‒N bonds and cation-π interaction (Fig. 3g, 3h).

Furthermore, to verify our hypothesis that rigid crystal structures can suppress nonradiative transition, we constructed crystalline models of 5-AID and K-5-AID and performed periodic Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations. Periodic time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT)29 calculations for 5-AID and K-5-AID were carried out using the mixed Gaussian and plane wave (GPW) scheme as implemented in CP2K30. First, we performed the ground-state lattice relaxation, and then calculated the excited-state energy and geometric structure (Fig. S111, Table S5) (please refer to Section 1.6 in Supplementary Information). The geometric distortion of the crystal upon excitation was quantified using the root-mean-square displacement (RMSD) of atoms between the S1 and S0 states. Additionally, the extent of excited-state geometric reorganization in 5-AID and K-5-AID was evaluated by calculating the difference between the vertical excitation energy (VEE) and fluorescence emission energy using both PBE and PBE0 functionals, as summarized in Table S5. The reorganization energies (λ) and RMSD values for 5-AID and K-5-AID are comprehensively presented in Fig. 3f. K-5-AID exhibits a significantly attenuated geometrical deformation during the S1 → S0 transition, the atomic RMSD (i.e., 0.0038 Å) of K-5-AID is reduced by 83.9% compared to that of 5-AID (0.0237 Å). While the excited-state reorganization energy (λ = 0.04 eV, PBE functional) of K-5-AID is decreased by 73.3% relative to that of 5-AID (λ = 0.15 eV, PBE functional). This indicates that the S1 state of K-5-AID undergoes a substantially smaller magnitude of structural relaxation in comparison to its S0 state. Such results demonstrate that the enhanced rigidity of the ionic crystal framework could efficiently suppress intermolecular vibration, thereby reducing thermal relaxation and enhancing fluorescence emission.

We further performed PLQY and time-resolved photoluminescence for CZ and Na-CZ (Figs. S112, S113), and calculated the radiative decay rate (kᵣ) and nonradiative decay rate (knr) for both compounds (Table S6). It is shown that the non-radiative transition rate knr of Na-CZ (0.20*108 s−1) is significantly reduced compared to that of CZ (1.29*108 s−1), confirming that the enhanced PLQY is primarily because of the suppressed non-radiative decay by the rigidity of ionic structure.

Tuning electronic structure via metal

The introduction of alkali metals can not only induce rigid crystal structures of the resultant organic salts but also effectively tune the electronic structures of the conjugated fluorophores therein. As shown in Fig. 2b, the emission spectra of the organic salts significantly redshift relative to their parent molecules. This reflects that alkali metal substitution, driven by the cation Lewis acidity, forms a N-heterocyclic anion that enhances the electron density on the conjugated ring, thus modifying the HOMO-LUMO energy gap and tuning emission properties. To decipher the structure-property relationship, density functional theoretical (DFT) calculations based on single-molecule and periodic condensed phase crystals were performed on 5-AID and K-5-AID crystal models to obtain their energy levels and electronic structural information in Fig. 4. The results show that the HOMO-LUMO energy gap of K-5-AID (2.79 eV) based on single-molecule is smaller than that of its parent molecule 5-AID (5.77 eV) (Fig. 4a). Calculations based on the condensed phase revealed that the electron density of the conjugated π system of the C7H5N2– chromophore ring increases significantly from 0.53|e| to 0.86|e| upon replacing H(N) by K (Fig. 4b). The density of states (DOS) analysis for K-5-AID demonstrates that both the conduction band minimum (CBM) and the valence band maximum (VBM) mainly consist of C-p and N-p orbitals, similar to that of unsubstituted species, showing that the band edges of these compounds are predominantly determined by the conjugated π system in the organic anion. This explains that the calculated band gap of K-5-AID (3.17 eV) is smaller than that of its parent molecule (3.33 eV) (Fig. 4c). Similarly, we also calculated the HOMO-LUMO energy gap, electron density, and energy band structure of Na-CZ and CZ (Fig. 4d–4f), which yielded results analogous to those of K-5-AID and 5-AID. In sum, the obvious electron density change on the conjugated ring is exemplified by the large redshifts of PL peaks of Na-CZ, 2Na-5,11-ICZ, and 2K-5,11-ICZ relative to their pristine CZ and 5,11-ICZ (Fig. 2b), respectively. The alkali metal substitution approach, can thus be utilized as a highly effective strategy to modulate the emission color of a fluorophore.

Luminescence for Information Anti-Counterfeiting

The as-prepared alkali metal N-heteroarene salts exhibit high thermal-, photo-, mechanical-, and air-stability, as exemplified by K-5-AID. The alkali metal substituted ionic compounds usually exhibit higher melting points than their parent molecular counterparts (Figs. S109, S110). When the as-prepared K-5-AID was treated under different conditions, such as irradiation under UV, visible, and IR; heat treatment at 200 °C; energetic ball milling; and exposure to the air, the sample could retain its fluorescence emission and structural integrity (Fig. 5a, S114–S117). Other alkali metal N-heteroarene salts investigated in this study, such as K-7-AID, K-δ-CB, and Na-CZ can also maintain their fluorescence intensity and structural integrity after air exposure (Figs. S118-S123). The multicolored emissions of the alkali metal N-heteroarene salts developed in the present study hold great potential for full-color displays. For example, when K-5-AID, Na-6-AID, and Na-CZ were mixed and exposed to the light of different wavelengths, distinct emission colors of blue, turquoise, and green could be observed (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, since different materials can be excited under different irradiations, providing different information, these organic salts may be effectively utilized in information security or anti-counterfeiting. We designed an information anti-counterfeiting model using four samples: K-5-AID, Na-6-AID, K-7-AID, and Na-CZ, to represent “D”, “I”, “C”, and “P”, respectively. Figure 5c illustrates that, upon exposure to 254 nm ultraviolet light, four samples exhibit fluorescence in different colors: purple (Em = 368 nm), blue (Em = 400 nm), light blue (Em = 440 nm), and green (Em = 503 nm), corresponding to the code “DICP”. Under 365 nm UV light, “ICP” is displayed; under 390 nm light, only the letter “P” is generated; and under natural light, the color of the sample itself is displayed. Similarly, the numeral “8” and the letter “W” composed of three materials (K-5-AID, Na-6-AID, and Na-CZ) in different parts could result in varied codes when exposed under different lighting conditions (Fig. 5d), i.e., “W”, “N”, and “V” or “8”, “9”, and “3” are displayed under light irradiation of 254 nm, 365 nm, and 390 nm, respectively. In short, our system operates on the principle of optical authentication, where a hidden piece of information (e.g., a pattern, number, or logo) is revealed only upon stimulation with a specific physical key, for instance, in this case, a particular wavelength of light. This is a hallmark application of luminescent anticounterfeiting technologies, which are designed to be difficult to replicate and easy to authenticate with a specific stimulus, demonstrating potential applications in protecting documents, labels and high-value goods.

Discussion

In summary, a new type of fluorophores, i.e., alkali metal N-heteroarene salts, was developed via a metal substitution strategy. Upon substitution, the fluorescence of some negligibly luminescent small conjugated N-heteroarenes can be enhanced, such as K-5-AID, Li-ID, and K-7-AID, presenting enhanced PLQYs. Moreover, these organic salts exhibit diverse emissions across the visible spectrum. The crystal structures of the exemplary 5-AID and K-5-AID systems were determined through XRD, and DFT calculations were performed on the HOMO-LUMO energy gaps, electron density, PDOS, and band structure. The crystal structures determined experimentally and the modified electronic structures revealed by calculations confirmed that the significantly improved luminescence properties of these organic salts compared to their parent molecules can be ascribed to the rigid ionic crystal structures and the optimized electronic structures. Finally, we demonstrated that these organic salts exhibit superior thermal-, photo-, mechanical-, and air-stability, possessing great promise for display and information anti-counterfeiting applications. Our study provides a facile yet versatile conceptual strategy for the development of highly tunable small organic-based luminescent materials.

Methods

Materials

Commercial indole (99%, Alfa Aesar), imidazole (99%, Alfa Aesar), 5-azaindole (97%, ACMEC), 6-azaindole (98%, ACMEC), 7-azaindole (98%, Sigma-Aldrich), carbazole (95%, Alfa Aesar), δ-carboline (95%, Bidepharm), 11,12-dihydroindolo[2,3-a]carbazole (96%, ACMEC), 5,11-dihydroindolo[3,2-b]carbazole (97%, Aladdin), lithium hydride (99%, Aldrich), sodium hydride (90%, Aldrich), and potassium hydride (90%, Aldrich) were used as precursors in the syntheses of alkali metal organic salts. All the reagents were stored in a glove box filled with high-purity argon. Sodium hydride and potassium hydride were rinsed with cyclohexane (HPLC grade, Macklin) to remove the mineral oil before use. Materials characterizations

Powder X-ray Diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected on a PANalytical X’pert diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 40 mA) for phase identification. The samples were loaded in a homemade sample cell covered with KAPTON film to avoid air and moisture contamination.

Liquid-state Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy was recorded on a JEOL JNM-ECZL400S 400 MHz NMR spectrometer (11.7 T) at ambient temperature. Liquid-state proton (1H) and carbon (13C) NMR spectroscopy were conducted using DMSO-d6 or THF-d8 as the deuterated solvent.

Thermal stability was investigated using a thermogravimetric coupled with a differential scanning calorimeter (TG-DSC, Netzsch STA 449 F5), and purified argon was used as a carrier gas. 5 mg of the samples were used for the analysis. Firstly, the K-5-AID sample was raised to a temperature of 250 °C, followed by cooling to 35 °C at a rate of 2 °C min−1. Nevertheless, pristine 5-AID was only heated to a temperature of 120 °C and cooled to 35 °C at a rate of 2 °C min−1. The TG-DSC data were obtained using the STA 449 F5 Jupiter software, and the data were processed using Netzsch Proteus Thermal Analysis 8.0.3 software.

Luminescence performance tests

The absorption spectra for all the samples were measured by an Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectrophotometer (Jasco V-750). The data was collected in absorption mode over a wavelength range of 200 nm to 900 nm at a medium scanning rate, utilizing BaSO4 as the reference.

All the relevant photoluminescence tests were recorded on an Edinburgh FS5 fluorescence spectrometer with a Xenon arc lamp (Xe900) and a microsecond flash-lamp (uF900).

The PL lifetimes (τ) of the samples were obtained by fitting the decay curve with a multiexponential decay function of I(t) = A1exp(−t/τ1) + A2exp(−t/τ2) +…+ Aiexp(−t/τi), and were recorded on Edinburgh FS1000 fluorescence spectrometer, where Ai and τi represent the amplitudes and lifetimes of the individual components for multiexponential decay profiles, respectively.

Photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) measurements were collected using the FS5 Spectrofluorometer (Edinburgh Instruments) in conjunction with the SC-30 integrating sphere. The sample powder was densely packed onto the sample holder, and the surface was subsequently flattened for measurement. A 150 W continuous-wave ozone-free xenon lamp was focused onto the sample.

Colorimetric measurements of all samples were taken from the CIE 1931 colorimetric diagram using the Fluoracle (version 2.15.2) operating software of the FS5 spectrometer from the corresponding PL spectrum.

Stability tests

In order to study the thermal-, photo-, mechanical-, and air-stability of organic salts, taking K-5-AID as an example, we collected the photophysical properties of K-5-AID powder after 3 days of exposure to air. We found that K-5-AID retained the purple fluorescence of the main peak at 368 nm, the PL curve and XRD basically did not change, and the PLQY recorded was 42%. Similarly, for other organic salts such as K-7-AID, K-δ-CB, and Na-CZ, after exposure to air for 2 days, 5 days, and 1 day, respectively, their PL curves and XRD showed negligible change. When K-5-AID was continuously exposed to UV light (wavelength at 300 nm) for 4 hours under ambient conditions, minimal variation in fluorescence intensity at 368 nm emission was detected. When K-5-AID was subjected to continuous irradiation with 400 nm–780 nm visible light and 800 nm–1100 nm infrared light for 4 hours under the same conditions, the intensity and peak profile of the PL curve remained mostly unchanged. In addition, we treated K-5-AID to a temperature of 200 °C at a heating rate of 1 °C min−1; the intensity and peak pattern profile of the PL curve and XRD exhibited minimal changes. In addition, K-5-AID was subjected to ball-milling for 2 hours at a speed of 100 r min−1. The PL curve and XRD also exhibited no significant changes, and the purple fluorescence was still maintained.

Crystal structure solving and DFT calculations

The crystal structures of 5-azaindole (5-C7H6N2) and potassium-5-azaindolide (K-5-C7H5N2) were determined using combined direct space methods and first-principles molecular dynamics simulated annealing based on lab powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) data of both 5-C7H6N2 and K-5-C7H5N2. Rietveld structural refinement on the optimal structural candidate was performed using the GSAS package31 on the lab powders' XRD data of 5-C7H6N2 and K-5-C7H5N2. The C7H6N2 and C7H5N2- were kept as rigid bodies with common bond lengths and bond angles constrained as reasonable values due to the inadequate number of observations and insensitivity of X-ray to H atoms. For 5- C7H6N2, C7H6N2 molecule positions and lattice parameters were refined, yielding the agreement factors of Rwp = 0.0582, Rp = 0.0602, and χ2 = 3.36. For K-5-C7H5N2, C7H5N2- group and the K+ positions together with lattice parameters were refined, yielding the agreement factors of Rwp = 0.0493, Rp = 0.0354, and χ2 = 1.79. The Rietveld fits to the lab XRD patterns of K-5-C7H5N2 and 5-C7H6N2 are shown in Figures S105 and S106, respectively. The refined lattice parameters of these two crystal structures are listed in Table S4. The CheckCif file for 5-AID did not show any A- or B-level alerts. In contrast, a B-level alert (PLAT340) was present in the CheckCif report for the K-5-AID structure. This alert arises from the inherent limitations in data resolution associated with powder X-ray diffraction relative to single-crystal diffraction, leading to slightly elevated standard uncertainties for C–C bond lengths. Nevertheless, this limitation does not affect the overall accuracy of the structural model nor the reliability of the key geometric parameters.

For the structure optimization, spin polarization density generalization calculations were simulated by the Vienna ab initio simulation package32,33. The electronic exchange-correlation energy was calculated within the generalized-gradient approximation with the Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof (PBE) functional34. The projector augmented wave method was used to describe the electron-ion interactions35,36, and the one-electron wave function was expanded on the basis of plane waves with the kinetic cutoff energy of 500 eV. Convergence criteria of energy and force for all geometry structures were fixed to 10−5 eV and 0.02 eV Å−1. For the bulk 5-azaindole, K-5-azaindolide, carbazole, and Na-carbazolide models, 4 × 3 × 2, 3 × 4 × 2, 3 × 1 × 4, and 1 × 3 × 4 Monkhorst-Pack k-points were used in the calculations, respectively.

The formation energy of K-5-AID from 5-AID is estimated with the reaction of ‘5-AID + K → K-5-AID + 1/2H2’. The reaction energy is calculated by equation of Ef = E (K-5-AID)/n – E (5-AID)/n – E (K)/n + 1/2 E (H2), where E (K-5-AD), E (5-AID), and E (K) represent the energies of each crystal, respectively, n is the number of atoms in the crystal, and E (H2) is the energy of the individual H2 in gas phase. This reaction is exothermic by 0.94 eV, indicating that the ionic crystal K-5-AID is thermodynamically more stable than the neutral 5-AID crystal, resulting in 5-AID having a higher lattice formation energy. The weak π-π interaction between the loosely packed 5-AID molecules, along with the high lattice formation energy and low melting temperature, may partially reflect the unstable lattice structure of 5-AID, the large vibrational degrees of freedom in 5-AID molecular rings, which is conducive to the non-radiative transition of thermal relaxation.

Periodic time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT)29 calculations for 5-AID and K-5-AID were carried out using the mixed Gaussian and plane wave (GPW) scheme implemented in CP2K30. The PBE exchange–correlation functional with DZVP-MOLOPT-SR-GTH basis sets and GTH pseudopotentials was employed, together with DFT-D3(BJ)37 dispersion corrections. The plane-wave cutoff for the finest multigrid level was set to 500 Ry, with a 70 Ry cutoff for mapping product Gaussians onto the multigrid levels. Ground-state lattice relaxation was first performed, with Monkhorst–Pack 2×2×1 k-point sampling for both systems. Excited-state energies and geometries were then computed using TDDFT with a 2×2×1 supercell, where the k-point mesh was restricted to the Γ point. Excitation energies were also calculated using the PBE0 hybrid functional combined with the auxiliary density matrix method (ADMM)29.
