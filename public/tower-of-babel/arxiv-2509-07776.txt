In our previous paper

Now, we prove a similar homeomorphism theorem for the real axis. As for the segment, this result provides the uniqueness of the minimax point on the real axis, independently of the specific reduction technique.

Results of this kind are inspired by the problem of optimizing the Lagrange interpolation of a continuous function. Let us give an overview of the results known to us.

Let
f
be a function continuous on
[0,1]
and
y_{0}=0<y_{1}<\ldots<y_{n}<1=y_{n+1}
be interpolation nodes. Denote by
\pi_{n+1}
the space of polynomials of degree at most
n+1
and by
P_{\mathbf{y}}:C_{[0,1]}\to\pi_{n+1}
the Lagrange interpolation operator

P_{\mathbf{y}}f(t):=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n+1}f(y_{j})\ell_{j}(t),\quad\ell_{j}(t):=\prod\limits_{i\neq j}\dfrac{t-y_{j}}{y_{i}-y_{j}}.

It is easy to show that

\|P_{\mathbf{y}}f-f\|_{C_{[0,1]}}\leqslant\dist(f,\pi_{n+1})(1+\|P_{\mathbf{y}}\|),

where
\|P_{\mathbf{y}}\|
is the operator norm. Therefore, it is natural to minimize
\|P_{\mathbf{y}}\|
by
\mathbf{y}
to optimize the interpolation. It is known that
\|P_{\mathbf{y}}\|=\|\Lambda_{\mathbf{y}}\|_{C_{[0,1]}},
where

\Lambda_{\mathbf{y}}(t):=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n+1}|\ell_{j}(t)|
is the Lebesgue function.
Thus, this optimization problem is exactly a minimax problem for the functions
\Lambda_{\mathbf{y}}.
Denote
\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{y}):=\max\limits_{t\in[y_{j},y_{j+1}]}\Lambda_{\mathbf{y}}(t),\ j=0,\ldots,n.

In 1931, S. N. Bernstein
[
10

conjectured that the minimum of
\|\Lambda_{\mathbf{y}}\|_{C_{[0,1]}}
is attained when
\mathbf{y}
is an
equioscillation point
, i. e.,

\lambda_{0}(\mathbf{y})=\ldots=\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{y}).

In 1977, T. A. Kilgore
[
11

proved Bernstein’s conjecture. Moreover, he showed the uniqueness of the equioscillation point. More precisely, Kilgore’s note
[
12
]
describing the proof of the statement “the minimax point is an equioscillation point” was first published, and a few months later, the complete proof was presented in
[
11
]
. Subsequently, C. R. de Boor and A. Pinkus
[
13
]
also proved Bernstein’s conjecture. In fact, they obtained the following general result.

Theorem A
.

Let

S^{[0,1]}:=\{\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ 0<y_{1}<\ldots<y_{n}<1\}.

Then the difference function

D_{\lambda}:S^{[0,1]}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\mathbf{y}\mapsto(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{y})-\lambda_{0}(\mathbf{y}),\ldots,\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{y})-\lambda_{n-1}(\mathbf{y}))

is a homeomorphism between
S^{[0,1]}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

It immediately implies that there is exactly one equioscillation point. De Boor and Pinkus then refer to Kilgore’s earlier note with the proof that the minimax point is an equioscillation point, and together all this proves Bernstein’s conjecture.

In 1996, Y. G. Shi
[
14

considered more general functions
\varphi_{j}(\cdot),\ j=0,\ldots,n,
instead of
\lambda_{j}(\cdot).
Shi supposed that all the functions

and satisfy conditions

\min\limits_{0\leqslant j\leqslant n}\lim\limits_{y_{j+1}-y_{j}\to 0}\ \max\limits_{0\leqslant i\leqslant n-1}|\varphi_{i+1}(\mathbf{y})-\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{y})|=\infty

and

\Phi_{k}(\mathbf{y}):=\det\left(\dfrac{\partial\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{y})}{\partial y_{j}}\right)^{n\ n}_{j=1,\ i=0,\ i\neq k}\neq 0,\quad\mathbf{y}\in S^{[0,1]},\ k=0,\ldots,n.

Shi dealt with the following minimax problem: find a vector
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}),\ 0<y_{1}<\ldots<y_{n}<1,
that minimizes

\max\limits_{j=0,\ldots,n}\varphi_{j}(\cdot).

Under these assumptions, Shi proved that there exists a unique extremal point and it has the equioscillation property. Moreover, Shi obtained the homeomorphism theorem for the difference function

D_{\varphi}:S^{[0,1]}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\mathbf{y}\mapsto(\varphi_{1}(\mathbf{y})-\varphi_{0}(\mathbf{y}),\ldots,\varphi_{n}(\mathbf{y})-\varphi_{n-1}(\mathbf{y})).

In particular, this theorem implies the uniqueness of the minimax point.

\displaystyle K(t_{2})-K(t_{1})\leqslant K(t_{2}+h)-K(t_{1}+h).

In particular, for almost all

such that

\displaystyle K^{\prime}(t_{1})\leqslant K^{\prime}(t_{2}).

Proof.

(1)

Inequality (

[

.

To prove the statement about

, it is sufficient to assume that

is defined at

and
t_{2}+h,
divide (

) by
t_{2}-t_{1}
and pass to the limit.

(2)

Sufficiently using (

), point 1 and (

G
M

\displaystyle(K(t_{2}+h)-K(t_{2}))-(K(t_{1}+h)-K(t_{1}))=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{[t_{2},t_{2}+h]}K^{\prime}(t)-\int\limits_{[t_{1},t_{1}+h]}K^{\prime}(t)\geqslant h\left(\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}K^{\prime}(t)-\lim\limits_{t\to-\infty}K^{\prime}(t)\right)\geqslant 0.

∎

Let us show that admissibility of a field is equivalent to the following more general property.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
K_{j}\colon(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},\ j=1,\ldots,n,
be kernels.
If a field

is admissible for
K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}
, then for any
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\lim\limits_{|t|\to\infty}\left(J(t)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}K_{j}(t-y_{j})\right)=-\infty.

Proof.

Let us prove the lemma for
t\to\infty
. If
t\to-\infty
, then we can consider
\widetilde{J}(t):=J(-t)
and
\widetilde{K}_{j}(t):=K_{j}(-t),\ j=1,\ldots,n,
and apply what is proved for
t\to\infty
.

1. Suppose that all
K^{\prime}_{j}
be bounded below for large arguments.
By our assumption and point 1 of Lemma
2.1
, there are

such that

(11)

\displaystyle|K^{\prime}_{j}(t)|\leqslant C,\quad t\geqslant L,\ j=1,\ldots,n.

Without loss of generality, assume that
\min\{t,t-y_{n}\}\geqslant L
.
Using (
7
) and (

), we get

K_{j}(t-y_{j})-K_{j}(t)\leqslant C|y_{j}|,\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

Therefore, by the admissibility of

, we obtain

2. Now assume that
K^{\prime}_{i}
is not bounded below for some

and for large

Denote

Note that

is concave on

. Take arbitrary point

where

exists. For any

, we have
[
17
, p. 12, Th. D]

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{y},t)\leqslant f^{\prime}_{t}(\mathbf{y},t_{0})\cdot(t-t_{0})+f(\mathbf{y},t_{0}).

By point 1 of Lemma
2.1
for

and our assumption regarding

, we conclude that

for large

as a sum of non-increasing functions and a function not bounded below.
Due to (

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}f(\mathbf{y},t)=-\infty.

Since

∎

3.
Behavior of sums of translates for large arguments

In this section, we prove a key lemma which allows us to reduce our problem on the axis to the case of the segment. Another form of this statement was proven for sums of translates with positive multiples of a single kernel in
[
6
, Lemma 4.3]
. Now we are dealing with several kernels and we need a slightly modified estimate, so we will provide a proof.

The following statement is well-known, see, e.g.,
[
6
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Lemma A
.

Suppose that a function
g
is concave, nondecreasing on the semiaxis
[M,\infty)
and is continuous at
M.
Then
g
is uniformly continuous on
[M,\infty)
.

We also need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
K_{j}\colon(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},\ j=1,\ldots,n,
are kernels and
J\colon\mathbb{R}\to\underline{\mathbb{R}}
is an admissible field, then for any
\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{\infty}}

we have

\lim\limits_{\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{y},\ |t|\to\infty}F(\mathbf{x},t)=-\infty.

Proof.

Let us prove the lemma for
t\to\infty.
For
t\to-\infty
the proof is carried out by considering the reflection
\widetilde{K}(t):=K(-t),\ \widetilde{J}(t)=J(-t).

Denote
I_{1}:=\{j:\ K_{j}\text{ is non-decreasing on }(0,\infty)\}.
Let
j\in I_{1}.

By Lemma
A
, the function
K_{j}
is uniformly continuous on
[1,\infty)
. Hence if
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|
is sufficiently small, then there exists
C_{j}>0
such that for large
t
we have
K_{j}(t-x_{j})-K_{j}(t-y_{j})\leqslant C_{j}
.

Now consider
I_{2}:=\{j:\ K_{j}\text{ is not non-decreasing on }(0,\infty)\}
and
j\in I_{2}.
By the concavity,
K_{j}
decreases for large
t
. Since
\mathbf{x}
converges, there is
c
such that
x_{j}\leqslant c
for all
j.
So, for large
t
we have that
K_{j}(t-x_{j})\leqslant K_{j}(t-c).

Using the inequalities above, we get for large
t
and for
\mathbf{x}
sufficiently close to
\mathbf{y}

F(\mathbf{x},t)=J(t)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}K_{j}(t-x_{j})\leqslant J(t)+\sum\limits_{j\in I_{1}}(K_{j}(t-y_{j})+C_{j})+\sum\limits_{j\in I_{2}}K_{j}(t-c).

Applying Lemma
2.2
, we obtain
\lim\limits_{\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{y},\ t\to\infty}F(\mathbf{x},t)=-\infty.

∎

Now, let us prove the main lemma of this section.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
K_{j}\colon(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},\ j=1,\ldots,n,
be kernels and
J:\mathbb{R}\to\underline{\mathbb{R}}
be an admissible field.
Then for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
there is a number
\tau_{N}>N
such that for each
\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{N}}

\left(t<-\tau_{N}\implies F(\mathbf{y},t)\leqslant m_{0}(\mathbf{y})-1\right)\ \text{and}\ \left(t>\tau_{N}\implies F(\mathbf{y},t)\leqslant m_{n}(\mathbf{y})-1\right).

In particular, this implies that

(13)

\displaystyle m^{\tau_{N}}_{j}(\mathbf{y})=m_{j}(\mathbf{y}),\quad\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{N}},\quad j=0,\ldots,n.

Proof.

Let us prove the statement for
t<-\tau_{N}
. The proof of the second part is similar.

Assume for a contradiction that for some
N\in\mathbb{N}

\forall M\in\mathbb{N},\ M>N\quad\exists\,\mathbf{y}_{M}\in\overline{S^{N}}\quad\exists\,t_{M}\in\mathbb{R}\quad\left(t_{M}<-M\quad\&\quad F(\mathbf{y}_{M},t_{M})>m_{0}(\mathbf{y}_{M})-1\right).

Take
x\in(-\infty,-N)
. By our assumption, we have for all
k
with
-M_{k}<x<-N

J(x)=F(\mathbf{y}_{M_{k}},x)-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}K_{j}(x-y^{M_{k}}_{j})\leqslant F(\mathbf{y}_{M_{k}},t_{M_{k}})+1-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}K_{j}(x-y^{M_{k}}_{j}).

Using continuity of
K
at
x-y^{*}_{j}<0
and Lemma
3.1
, we get

\displaystyle J(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\left(F(\mathbf{y}_{M_{k}},t_{M_{k}})+1-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}K_{j}(x-y^{M_{k}}_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}F(\mathbf{y}_{M_{k}},t_{M_{k}})+1-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}K_{j}(x-y^{*}_{j})=-\infty.

So,
J(x)\equiv-\infty
for
x<-N.
We have a contradiction with our assumption, since then also
F(\mathbf{y}_{M},t_{M})=-\infty.

∎

4.
Local homeomorphism

In this section, we prove a statement about local homeomorphism by reducing the problem to a segment. For
N\in\mathbb{N}
take

from Lemma

Lemma B

5.
Properness of the difference function

The idea of the proof of the following lemma was communicated to us by Szilárd Gy. Révész. We already used this idea in
[
6
, Th. 3.1]
with his permission. However, we now prove a statement of a different nature, and therefore we need a slightly different estimate in the proof. Moreover, we are dealing with several kernels satisfying (

⁢
G
M

) instead of just one monotone kernel.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume that the kernels
K_{j}\colon(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},\ j=1,\ldots,n,
satisfy (

⁢
G
M

), and
J
is an admissible field. Let
\{\mathbf{y}^{N}\}\subset R
be an unbounded sequence convergent in the extended sense. Then there exists
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that

|m_{i}(\mathbf{y}^{N})-m_{i-1}(\mathbf{y}^{N})|\to\infty,\quad N\to\infty.

Proof.

1. Denote
\mathbf{y}^{N}:=(y^{N}_{1},\ldots,y^{N}_{n}),\ y^{N}_{0}:=-\infty,\ y^{N}_{n+1}:=\infty.
If
y^{N}_{n}\to\infty,
take
i:=\min\{j:y^{N}_{j}\to\infty\}.
Otherwise, we have
y^{N}_{1}\to-\infty
and take
i:=\max\{j:y^{N}_{j}\to-\infty\}.
Without loss of generality, consider the first case. The proof for the second case is similar.

2. Let us show that there exists
z>0
such that
J(z)\neq-\infty
and
z\in(y^{N}_{i-1},y^{N}_{i})
for
N
large enough.

By definition of
i
, we have either
y^{N}_{i-1}\to-\infty
or
y^{N}_{i-1}\to y_{i-1}\in\mathbb{R}.
In both cases, there exist
A>0,\ N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
y^{N}_{i-1}<A-1
for all
N>N_{0}.
Since
\{\mathbf{y}^{N}\}\subset R
for all
N
, there are arbitrarily large points where
J
is finite. Fix one such point
z>A.
Then, for all
N>N_{0}
, we have
y^{N}_{i-1}<z-1
. Moreover, there exists
N_{1}>N_{0}
such that
y^{N}_{i}>z+1
for all
N>N_{1}
. For
N>N_{1},
we conclude that
z\in(y^{N}_{i-1},y^{N}_{i})
and
|z-y^{N}_{j}|>1
for all
j.
Next we consider these
N
.

3. Let us estimate

F(\mathbf{y}^{N},t)=J(t)+\sum\limits_{j<i}K_{j}(t-y^{N}_{j})+\sum\limits_{j\geqslant i}K_{j}(t-y^{N}_{j})

for
t\in(y^{N}_{i},y^{N}_{i+1})
.

If
j<i,
apply (
8
) with
t_{1}=1,\ t_{2}=t-z+1,\ h=z-1-y^{N}_{j}
.
If
j>i
, use (
9
) with

In both cases, we obtain

K_{j}(t-y^{N}_{j})-K_{j}(z-y^{N}_{j})\leqslant K_{j}(t-z+1)-K_{j}(1),\quad j\neq i.

Since
K_{i}
satisfies (

⁢
G
M

), by (
9
) with
t_{1}=z-y^{N}_{i},\ t_{2}=t-y^{N}_{i},\ h=y^{N}_{i}-z-1
, we get

(17)

K_{i}(t-y^{N}_{i})-K_{i}(z-y^{N}_{i})\leqslant K_{i}(t-z-1)-K_{i}(-1).

By (
16
) and (
17
),
we obtain

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{y}^{N},t)

\displaystyle\leqslant J(t)+\sum\limits_{j\neq i}(K_{j}(z-y^{N}_{j})+K_{j}(t-z+1)-K_{j}(1))

\displaystyle+K_{i}(z-y^{N}_{i})+K_{i}(t-z-1)-K_{i}(-1)

\displaystyle=F(\mathbf{y}^{N},z)+J(t)-J(z)+\sum\limits_{j\neq i}(K_{j}(t-z+1)-K_{j}(1))

\displaystyle+K_{i}(t-z-1)-K_{i}(-1).

Let
\displaystyle C:=-J(z)-\sum\limits_{j\neq i}K_{j}(1)-K_{i}(-1).
By the above estimate, we have

(18)

F(\mathbf{y}^{N},t)\leqslant F(\mathbf{y}^{N},z)+J(t)+\sum\limits_{j\neq i}K_{j}(t-z+1)+K_{i}(t-z-1)+C.

Since

is admissible, by Lemma

, we have that

J(t)+\sum\limits_{j\neq i}K_{j}(t-z+1)+K_{i}(t-z-1)\to-\infty,\quad t\to\infty.

Take arbitrary

. Since

for large

we obtain that (

.
Hence, by (

, we get that for

Therefore, taking into account the choice of

m_{i}(\mathbf{y}^{N})\leqslant F(\mathbf{y}^{N},z)-L\leqslant m_{i-1}(\mathbf{y}^{N})-L.

Thus

m_{i}(\mathbf{y}^{N})-m_{i-1}(\mathbf{y}^{N})\to-\infty,\quad N\to\infty.

∎

Definition 5.1
.

A function
g:A\to B
between two Hausdorff topological spaces is called
proper
if for any compact set
Q\subset B
we have that
g^{-1}(Q)
is also a compact set
[
19
, p. 20]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Assume that the kernel functions
K_{j}\colon(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},\ j=1,\ldots,n,
are singular and satisfy (

⁢
G
M

). Let a field function
J
be admissible. Then
the difference function
D:R\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is proper.

Proof.

Let
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a compact set. We need to show that
D^{-1}(Q)
is also a compact set, i. e.,
D^{-1}(Q)
is closed and bounded.

Let us prove that
D^{-1}(Q)
is closed.
By Lemma
4.2
,
D
is continuous. Hence, since
Q
is closed,
D^{-1}(Q)
is relatively closed in
R
, i. e.,

D^{-1}(Q)=A\cap R,\quad\text{where $A$ is closed in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$}.

Consider
\{\mathbf{y}^{N}\}\subset D^{-1}(Q)
and let
\mathbf{y}^{N}\to\mathbf{y},\ N\to\infty.

We have that
\mathbf{y}\in A,
since
\{\mathbf{y}^{N}\}\subset A
and
A
is closed in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
If we prove that
\mathbf{y}\in R
, it immediately follows that
D^{-1}(Q)
is closed.

The field
J
is finite at least at
n+1

there is
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
such that
m_{i}(\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{R}.
Moreover, since
\{\mathbf{y}^{N}\}\subset D^{-1}(Q),
there exists
C>0
such that for all
N\in\mathbb{N}

|m_{j}(\mathbf{y}^{N})-m_{j-1}(\mathbf{y}^{N})|\leqslant C,\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

By Corollary
4.2
, the functions
m_{j}
are continuous.
Therefore, the differences
m_{j}(\mathbf{y})-m_{j-1}(\mathbf{y})
also satisfy

for all
j=1,\ldots,n
.
Taking into account the finiteness of

, this implies that
\mathbf{y}\in R.

It remains to show that
D^{-1}(Q)
is bounded. Suppose, contrary to our claim, that there exists an unbounded sequence
\{\mathbf{y}^{N}\}\subset D^{-1}(Q)
. Without loss of generality, let
\mathbf{y}^{N}
converges in the extended sense. By Lemma
5.1
,
there is
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that

|m_{i}(\mathbf{y}^{N})-m_{i-1}(\mathbf{y}^{N})|\to\infty,\quad N\to\infty.

Hence
Q\supset D(\{\mathbf{y}^{N}\})
is not bounded. This contradicts the assumption that

is a compact set, and this finishes the proof.
∎

8.
Homeomorphism theorem for weighted generalized

polynomials on the real axis

Let
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}>0
be arbitrary. Denote
\mathbf{r}:=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})
. Consider the following set of monic generalized nonnegative polynomials
[
21
, p. 392]
of degree
r\penalty\ :=\penalty\ \sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbb{R}):=\left\{p(\mathbf{y},t)=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}|t-y_{j}|^{r_{j}}:\ -\infty<y_{1}<\ldots<y_{n}<\infty\right\}.

Let
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
denote the subset of vectors from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with positive coordinates.

Corollary 8.1
.

Let
w:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
be a bounded above function assuming non-zero values at more than
n
points. Assume that

\lim\limits_{|t|\to\infty}w(t)\cdot t^{r}=0.

Let

S^{\infty}:=\{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ -\infty<y_{1}<\ldots<y_{n}<\infty\},\quad

and

X:=\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\ w(t)=0\}.

For convenience, let
y_{0}:=-\infty,\ y_{n+1}:=\infty.

Denote

R:=\{\mathbf{y}\in S^{\infty}:\ (y_{j},y_{j+1})\not\subseteq X\quad\text{for}\quad j=0,\ldots,n\}.

Then the mapping

R\ni\mathbf{y}\mapsto\left(\dfrac{\sup\limits_{t\in(y_{1},y_{2})}w(t)p(\mathbf{y},t)}{\sup\limits_{t\in(-\infty,y_{1})}w(t)p(\mathbf{y},t)},\ldots,\dfrac{\sup\limits_{t\in(y_{n},\infty)}w(t)p(\mathbf{y},t)}{\sup\limits_{t\in(y_{n-1},y_{n})}w(t)p(\mathbf{y},t)}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}

is a homeomorphism between
R
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. Moreover, it is locally bi-Lipschitz.

Proof.

Let
K_{j}:=r_{j}\log|\cdot|,\ j=1,\ldots,n,\ J:=\log w.
Obviously, these kernels and field satisfy the conditions of Theorem
1.1
. Therefore, the difference function

D:R\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\mathbf{y}\mapsto\left(\sup\limits_{t\in(y_{j},y_{j+1})}\log(w(t)p(\mathbf{y},t))-\sup\limits_{t\in(y_{j-1},y_{j})}\log(w(t)p(\mathbf{y},t))\right)_{j=1}^{n}

is a homeomorphism between
R
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and it is locally bi-Lipschitz.
Since the logarithm strictly increases on
(0,\infty),

\sup\limits_{t\in(y_{j},y_{j+1})}\log(w(t)p(\mathbf{y},t))=\log\left(\sup\limits_{t\in(y_{j},y_{j+1})}w(t)p(\mathbf{y},t)\right),\quad\mathbf{y}\in R.

Let us write the difference function
D
as

\mathbf{y}\mapsto\left(\log\dfrac{\sup\limits_{t\in(y_{j},y_{j+1})}w(t)p(\mathbf{y},t)}{\sup\limits_{t\in(y_{j-1},y_{j})}w(t)p(\mathbf{y},t)}\right)_{j=1}^{n}

Note that the function

E:\ \mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\quad\mathbf{x}\mapsto(\exp(x_{1}),\ldots,\exp(x_{n}))

is a homeomorphism, and it is locally Lipschitz. It remains to see that the mapping in our statement is equal to
E\circ D.

∎

9.
Interpolation by products of log-concave functions

with weight

The homeomorphism theorem allows one to obtain results on interpolation by weighted products of log-concave functions. This is discussed in detail in
[
9
, Sect. 9]
for the homeomorphism theorem on the segment. The authors proved general results
[
9
, Th. 9.2, 9.6]
on the existence and uniqueness of an interpolation function. They also studied applications of these results to trigonometric interpolation
[
9
, Th. 9.7]
. Moreover, in
[
9
, Subsect. 9.3, 9.4]
the authors investigated the applications to moving node Hermite–Fejér interpolation.
Below we prove some analogues of the most general of these results on the real axis.

9.1.
Abstract log-concave interpolation on the real axis

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
L_{1},\ldots,L_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
be log-concave
functions vanishing at
0
and satisfying

\lim\limits_{t\to-\infty}(\log L_{j}(t))^{\prime}\leqslant\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}(\log L_{j}(t))^{\prime},\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

Let
w:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
be a bounded above function assuming non-zero values at more than
n
points. Assume that

\lim\limits_{|t|\to\infty}w(t)\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}(t)=0.

For any
-\infty<x_{0}<\ldots<x_{n}<\infty
with
w(x_{j})>0
and
\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{n}>0
there are a unique

C>0
and points
y_{1}<y_{2}<\ldots<y_{n}
with
x_{j}<y_{j+1}<x_{j+1}
for
each
j\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
such that for the function

G(t):=Cw(t)\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}(t-y_{j})

we have

G(x_{j})=\alpha_{j},\quad j=0,\ldots,n.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle K_{j}(t)

\displaystyle:=\log L_{j}(t),\quad j=1,\ldots,n,

\displaystyle J(t)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\log w(x_{j}),\quad t=x_{j},\ j=0,\ldots,n,\\
-\infty,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\}.\end{cases}

It is easy to see that
K_{j}
and
J
satisfy the conditions of Theorem
1.1
.

By construction of
J
, the regularity set has the following form

R:=\{\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{\infty}}:\ x_{j}<y_{j+1}<x_{j+1}\text{ for }j=0,\ldots,n-1\}.

Take arbitrary
\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{n}>0.
By Theorem
1.1
, there is a unique
\mathbf{y}\in R

such that

(20)

\displaystyle m_{j}(\mathbf{y})-m_{j-1}(\mathbf{y})=\log(\alpha_{j}/\alpha_{j-1}),\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

We have

(21)

\displaystyle m_{j}(\mathbf{y})=F(\mathbf{y},x_{j})=\log w(x_{j})+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\log L_{k}(x_{j}-y_{k}),\quad j=0,\ldots,n.

Therefore, using (
20
), after exponentiating we get

\dfrac{\exp m_{j}(\mathbf{y})}{\exp m_{j-1}(\mathbf{y})}=\dfrac{w(x_{j})\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}L_{k}(x_{j}-y_{k})}{w(x_{j-1})\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}L_{k}(x_{j-1}-y_{k})}=\dfrac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{j-1}},\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

Hence

\dfrac{w(x_{j})\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}L_{k}(x_{j}-y_{k})}{w(x_{0})\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}L_{k}(x_{0}-y_{k})}=\dfrac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{0}},\quad j=1,\ldots,n,

and for the function

G(t):=Cw(t)\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}L_{k}(t-y_{k})\quad\text{with}\quad C:=\dfrac{\alpha_{0}}{w(x_{0})\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}L_{k}(x_{0}-y_{k})},

we obtain

G(x_{j})=\alpha_{j}\quad j=0,\ldots,n.

Let us show the uniqueness. Assume that for some
\widetilde{C}>0
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}=(\widetilde{y}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{y}_{n})\in R
we have

\widetilde{C}w(x_{j})\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}L_{k}(x_{j}-\widetilde{y}_{k})=\alpha_{j}\quad j=0,\ldots,n.

Using (
21
), we have

\log\widetilde{C}+m_{j}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}})=\log\widetilde{C}+\log w(x_{j})+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\log L_{k}(x_{j}-y_{k})=\log\alpha_{j}\quad j=0,\ldots,n.

Hence

m_{j}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}})-m_{j-1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}})=\log(\alpha_{j}/\alpha_{j-1}),\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

By Theorem
1.1
, we conclude that
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{y},
therefore,
\widetilde{C}=C,
too.
∎
