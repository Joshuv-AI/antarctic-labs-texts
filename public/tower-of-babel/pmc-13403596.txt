Aberrant expression of Gremlin1 induces trophoblast senescence in the placenta of advanced maternal age pregnancy
Authors: Haojun Tian, Yingying Wei, Lu Zhang, Xuancheng Wei, Mengtian Wei, Ruixue Wang, Qizhi He, Tao Duan, Xiaojie Huang, Kai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global incidence of advanced maternal age (AMA) has risen substantially over the past two decades, accompanied by elevated risks of adverse perinatal outcomes. This phenomenon has been linked to premature placental aging, yet the precise molecular pathways have yet to be fully elucidated. Our study aims to delineate the impact of Gremlin1 (GREM1) aberrant expression in AMA pregnancies and elucidate its underlying molecular mechanisms.MethodsThis study employed a multi-level approach to investigate GREM1’s role in AMA-associated placental aging. Analysis of 42 human placental samples quantified GREM1 expression patterns. In vitro functional validation was performed using the HTR-8/SVneo trophoblast cell through GREM1 overexpression/knockdown interventions, assessing proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities, as well as senescence through the expression of key markers and senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining. Mechanistic studies examined GREM1-BMP4 interactions and SMAD1/5/9 phosphorylation via Western blotting, supplemented by rescue experiments with exogenous BMP4 and BMP-SMAD pathway activator sb4. In vivo validation utilized AAV9-mediated GREM1 overexpression in pregnant mice, with placental aging phenotypes evaluated following sb4 intervention.ResultsFirst-time identification revealed significant GREM1 upregulation in human AMA placentas. Cellular experiments demonstrated GREM1 overexpression suppressed trophoblast proliferation, migration, and invasion while inducing senescence, effects that were ameliorated by GREM1 knockdown. Mechanistically, GREM1 promoted senescence by antagonizing BMP4 and inhibiting SMAD1/5/9 phosphorylation. Both BMP4 and sb4 partially rescued senescence phenotypes in vitro. Animal studies confirmed AAV9-GREM1 induced premature placental aging and reduced placental efficiency in mice, phenotypes significantly alleviated by sb4 treatment. In naturally aged AMA mice, sb4 treatment mitigated molecular senescence markers but did not significantly improve macroscopic pregnancy outcomes.ConclusionThis work elucidates GREM1/BMP4 imbalance-driven trophoblast senescence as one of the mechanisms underlying AMA-related placental dysfunction, mediated through impaired BMP-SMAD signaling. While targeting this axis effectively mitigates molecular aging markers, the multifaceted nature of clinical AMA suggests that fully reversing macroscopic outcomes may require integrated, multi-pathway interventions. Nevertheless, targeted modulation of the BMP-SMAD pathway or anti-GREM1 strategies holds significant clinical intervention potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08311-x.Keywords: Advanced maternal age (AMA), Placenta, GREM1, Aging

Introduction

Advanced maternal age (AMA), clinically characterized as maternal age ≥ 35 years at delivery [1], has undergone a global epidemiological shift in recent decades. Driven by sociocultural evolution, advancements in assisted reproductive technology (ART) [2], and changing fertility preferences, the mean age of first-time mothers at parturition has exhibited a sustained upward trajectory. Chinese national health statistics reveal a 68.8% surge in AMA prevalence between 2011 and 2019, with an annualized growth rate of 7.6% [3].Notably, accumulating epidemiological evidence demonstrates AMA significantly elevates risks for obstetric complications, including fetal growth restriction (FGR) [4], stillbirth, preterm birth, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) [5], and preeclampsia (PE), with adverse pregnancy outcomes occurring 1.5- to 3-fold more frequently than in younger cohorts. Consequently, deciphering the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying AMA-associated risks constitutes a critical priority in perinatal medicine.

The placenta is a transient organ specific to pregnancy, which develops from the trophectoderm and the extraembryonic mesoderm [6]. It undergoes rapid growth during early- to mid-pregnancy and evolves into a pivotal structure for maintaining gestation [7]. As a critical functional unit at the maternal-fetal interface, the placenta not only facilitates bidirectional transport of oxygen [8], nutrients, and metabolic waste but also critically maintains pregnancy through the secretion of hormones and signaling molecules such as human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), progesterone, and placental growth factor (PlGF) into the maternal circulation [9]. The placenta comprises a heterogeneous cellular population, including functionally dominant trophoblasts, stromal fibroblasts, maternal decidual cells, immune-regulatory cells, and vascular endothelial cells [10]. Among these, trophoblasts orchestrate placental morphogenesis and functional homeostasis through spatiotemporally regulated proliferation, differentiation, migration, and invasion. Substantial clinical evidence demonstrates that trophoblast dysfunction disrupts placental equilibrium, directly triggering pregnancy-associated complications such as PE [11], FGR [12], and spontaneous preterm birth [13], thereby establishing trophoblasts as a central research focus in placental biology.

Cellular senescence represents a progressive functional decline governed by genetic programs or environmental stressors [14], featuring cell-cycle arrest, metabolic dysregulation [15], and the secretion of molecules associated with the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [16], which correlates with organ dysfunction and disease pathogenesis [17]. During normal pregnancy, the placenta undergoes programmed senescence [18], a timely activated mechanism essential for placental detachment during parturition [19]. However, in AMA pregnancies, placental tissues frequently exhibit pathological premature senescence as early as the first trimester [20, 21]. Notably, accelerated senescence of extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs) disrupts placental vascular network formation, leading to insufficient placental perfusion and adverse perinatal outcomes [20, 22]. Currently, the molecular regulatory network governing placental senescence remains incompletely elucidated, and its interplay with trophoblast dysfunction warrants further investigation.

Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), a family of secreted extracellular matrix-associated proteins, regulate fundamental developmental processes, including limb patterning and organogenesis [23]. Their activity is tightly controlled by secreted antagonists such as GREM1, which inhibits BMP signaling through ligand sequestration [24]. Our analysis of 42 human placental specimens revealed an inverse regulatory relationship between GREM1 and BMP4 in AMA placentas. Specifically, BMP4 demonstrated tripartite protective effects in trophoblasts by attenuating cellular senescence, fibrosis, and inflammation, whereas GREM1 overexpression potently antagonized these beneficial functions. The disruption of this delicate balance may directly contribute to diminished placental invasiveness and accelerated senescence in AMA pregnancies, positioning the GREM1-BMP4 axis as a potential therapeutic target for placental dysfunction.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Human placental tissues across different gestational stages were collected between October 2021 and June 2022 at Shanghai First Maternity and Infant Hospital. A consecutive recruitment strategy was utilized to enroll eligible participants into the designated study cohorts. For the term placenta cohorts, we recruited a normal control group (maternal age 20–30 years at delivery, n = 20) and an AMA group (maternal age 35–45 years at delivery, n = 22). Parturients aged 31 to 34 years were intentionally excluded to establish a physiological buffer zone, minimizing intermediate transitional phenotypes and maximizing the detection of distinct age-related molecular divergence. Inclusion criteria strictly required all subjects in these term cohorts to be singleton, full-term pregnancies (gestational age ≥ 37 and < 42 weeks) delivered via elective cesarean section. Concurrently, an early-to-mid pregnancy cohort was enrolled, comprising first-trimester (chorionic villi, 6–12 weeks of gestation, n = 4) and second-trimester (placental tissues, at 14, 16, 19, and 21 weeks of gestation, n = 4) samples obtained from healthy women undergoing elective, voluntary termination of pregnancy for non-medical reasons. All fetuses in this cohort were confirmed to have normal karyotypes, and the mothers met the same stringent health criteria (excluding maternal systemic diseases and adverse obstetric histories) as the normal term control group.

To ensure the observed placental phenotypes were primarily attributable to maternal age, rigorous exclusion criteria were applied: [1] pregnancy complications (e.g., GDM, PE, FGR, and premature rupture of membranes); [2] maternal pre-existing conditions (e.g., chronic hypertension, overt diabetes, renal diseases, autoimmune disorders, and severe acute or chronic infections); [3] adverse obstetric history, including recurrent spontaneous abortion, prior ectopic gestations, or stillbirth; [4] pregnancies conceived via assisted reproductive technology (in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer, IVF-ET); and [5] history of smoking, alcohol consumption, or substance abuse.

Potential participants who met all the aforementioned criteria were approached. Final inclusion was dependent on the provision of written informed consent and the immediate availability of research personnel to process the placental tissues to ensure sample integrity. This study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shanghai First Maternity and Infant Hospital (No. KS21288) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

For each placenta, biopsies were obtained from the central region, approximately 3–5 cm adjacent to the umbilical cord insertion site, strictly avoiding the placental margins and any macroscopically visible areas of infarction or calcification. Full-thickness tissue samples, encompassing the entire cross-section from the fetal surface (chorionic plate) to the maternal surface (basal decidua), were excised.

Following sterile PBS washing, placental specimens were processed through two protocols: Fresh tissues (100–200 mg per Eppendorf tube) underwent immediate snap-freezing in liquid nitrogen prior to storage at -80 °C for later analysis. Parallel samples (30–50 mg per Eppendorf tube) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C overnight and embedded in paraffin for histological preparations.

Transcriptome sequencing

OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) performed the transcriptomic profiling via high-throughput sequencing. Briefly, sequencing libraries were constructed with the TruSeq® RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) following the vendor’s instructions, and 150-bp paired-end reads were generated on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform. Alignment to the human reference genome (GRCh38) was performed using HISAT2, followed by FPKM-based gene expression quantification. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package (v1.36.0). To account for multiple hypothesis testing, P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to calculate the False Discovery Rate (FDR). Genes meeting the criteria of an adjusted P-value (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 2 were defined as significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Hierarchical clustering of the DEGs was performed and visualized utilizing the pheatmap package (v1.0.12). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses of the DEGs were executed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.4.4) based on hypergeometric distribution tests. Overall data visualization was implemented using the R software (v4.2.1) within the RStudio environment (v2022.07.1) and the ggplot2 package (v3.3.6).

Cell culture and treatment

HTR-8/SVneo cell line (an SV40-immortalized extravillous trophoblast cell line) was acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Rockville, MD, USA) and maintained at 37 °C under 5% CO2 atmosphere. The culture system employed DMEM/F-12 (1:1) basal medium (Gibco, NY, USA) with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) supplementation. To investigate the biological effects of recombinant human GREM1 (rhGREM1; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and rhBMP4 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), cells were exposed to rhGREM1 (100 ng/mL or 300 ng/mL) or rhBMP4 (50 ng/mL or 200 ng/mL) in DMEM/F-12 medium for 48 h, with medium replenishment every 24 h. For senescence induction, cells were treated with various concentrations of doxorubicin (DOX; 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 µM) to establish the model, and 1.0 µM was utilized for all subsequent functional assays.

Cell proliferation assay

To evaluate proliferative capacity under different treatment conditions, HTR-8/SVneo cells were plated in a 96-well plate at 2,000 cells/well in 150 µL complete DMEM/F12 medium. For the experiments specific to Fig. 5, empty vector control and GREM1-overexpressing stable cell lines were pretreated with BMP signaling agonist sb4 (MedChemExpress, HY-124697, USA, 10 µM) for 48 h prior to plating. After 48-h and 72-h incubation periods, cell proliferation was assessed using the CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution MTS Assay kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) per manufacturer instructions.

Invasion and migration assays

Cell migratory and invasive capacities were evaluated using Corning FluoroBlok 24-well insert systems (8 μm pore size; Costar, Corning, NY, USA). For invasion assays, inserts were pre-coated with 100 µL Matrigel matrix (1:20 dilution in 1% FBS DMEM/F12; BD Biosciences, USA) and polymerized at 37 °C for 1 h. HTR-8/SVneo cells were detached using trypsin and reconstituted in serum-depleted medium. For the experiments in Fig. 5, empty vector control and GREM1-overexpressing stable cell lines were pretreated with sb4 (10 µM) for 48 h prior to this step.

Cell suspensions containing 5.5 × 10⁴ cells in 300 µL serum-free medium were added to the upper chamber (duplicate wells per condition). The lower chamber contained 800 µL 1% FBS DMEM/F12 as chemoattractant. Following 16 h incubation (37 °C, 5% CO₂), stationary cells on the upper membrane were cleared using cotton swabs. Migrated/invaded cells in the lower chamber were fluorescently labeled with 4 µM Calcein AM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in PBS for 30 min at 37 °C. Fluorescence signals were captured using a Nikon Eclipse Ti inverted fluorescence microscope (Tokyo, Japan) with NIS-Elements imaging software. Quantitative analysis was performed by measuring integrated fluorescence density using ImageJ.

RNA isolation and reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

Total RNA extraction was performed with TRIzol™ Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to established procedures. RNA purity (A260/A280 ratio) and concentration were determined and quantified via NanoDrop™ 2000c UV-Vis spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed on 1 µg total RNA inputs using the PrimeScript™ Reverse Transcription system (TaKaRa Bio, Japan). qPCR reactions were performed in triplicate with TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (TaKaRa Bio) on a QuantStudio™ 5 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Gene-specific primers (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China; sequences listed in Table S1) were validated by melting curve analysis with the following protocol: 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and 95 °C for 15 s. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with normalization to GAPDH as the endogenous control.

Western blot analysis

Total protein content was quantified via BCA methodology (Pierce™ Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) adhering strictly to manufacturer’s instructions. Equal protein aliquots were resolved by SDS-PAGE and electrophoretically transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA) via wet transfer at 100 V for 90 min. Membranes underwent 1 h blocking at room temperature with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) prepared in TBST (10 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20, pH 7.6), followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Antibody specifications are summarized in Table S1). After three 10-min TBST washes, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated AffiniPure goat anti-mouse/rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (1:10,000 dilution; Proteintech, Cat# SA00001-1, SA00001-2) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using Immobilon™ Western HRP Substrate (MilliporeSigma) with a Tanon imaging system. Densitometric quantification of the protein bands was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.53k, NIH, USA). The relative expression level of each target protein was determined by normalizing its integrated optical density to that of GAPDH within the same sample.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of secreted IL-1β, IL-6, and MMP9 in the cell culture supernatants were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) adhering strictly to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to remove residual particulates and cellular debris. Prior to assay execution, the cleared supernatants were appropriately diluted to ensure the target analyte concentrations fell within the linear detection range of the respective standard curves. Equal aliquots of the cleared supernatants or standard solutions were dispensed into 96-well microplates pre-coated with target-specific capture antibodies. Following the specified incubation periods and thorough washing procedures to eliminate unbound substances, HRP-conjugated detection antibodies were added to each well. The colorimetric reaction was developed using a tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate solution and subsequently terminated with a stop solution. The optical density (OD) of each well was immediately determined at a primary wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader. To correct for optical imperfections in the microplate, readings at 540 nm were subtracted from the 450 nm readings. The absolute concentrations of the target analytes were interpolated from standard curves generated via a four-parameter logistic (4PL) curve fit.

Gene modulation experiments

Three human GREM1-targeting siRNAs (siGREM1) and a scrambled control siRNA (siNC) were synthesized by Genomeditech (Shanghai, China). We transfected HTR-8/SVneo cells with 50 nM siRNA complexes employing Lipofectamine™ 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) with transfection reagent-only groups serving as vehicle controls.

For stable overexpression, cells were transduced with human GREM1-encoding lentiviral particles (Genomeditech, Shanghai, China) which carry a puromycin resistance gene for selection. 48 h post-transduction, cells underwent 48-h selection with 2 µg/mL puromycin (Solarbio, Beijing, China) to establish stable polyclonal populations. GREM1 modulation efficiency was validated by immunoblotting at 72 h (siRNA) and post-selection (lentivirus) timepoints.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Tissues underwent 48 h fixation in 4% PFA followed by paraffin embedding. For immunohistochemistry, sections underwent antigen retrieval (98 °C citrate buffer, 20 min), peroxidase blocking (3% H₂O₂), and 5% BSA blocking, followed by primary antibody incubation at 4 °C overnight. DAB chromogen (GTVision kit, GeneTech) and hematoxylin counterstaining were applied. Five random fields per section were imaged (Nikon microscope) and quantified using ImageJ. To minimize subjective bias, all image acquisitions and quantitative analyses were performed by two independent investigators blinded to the experimental groupings. For immunofluorescence, sections followed the same pretreatment and primary antibody incubation, but were then incubated with fluorescent-conjugated secondary antibodies and counterstained with DAPI. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

SA-β-gal staining

SA-β-gal activity, a senescence marker, was detected using Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (MedChemExpress, USA). Cells were fixed with provided fixative (15 min, at room temperature) followed by 16 h incubation in staining solution at 37 °C within a CO₂-depleted environment. SA-β-gal-positive cells (blue staining) were imaged using a Nikon bright-field microscope. Quantitative analysis was performed by calculating the percentage of positively stained areas in three randomly selected fields per sample using ImageJ 1.50i (threshold method), with triplicate biological replicates.

Animal experiments and model establishment

Female C57BL/6J mice were stratified into two age groups: young (2–3 months old, n = 15) and aged (10–12 months old, n = 15), obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Nulliparous mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with strictly regulated ambient temperature (23 ± 1 °C), humidity (50 ± 5%), and 12-h light/dark cycle. For pregnancy induction, females were paired with 8-week-old male breeders at a 2:1 female-to-male ratio. Females were introduced to males at 8:00 PM and separated at 8:00 AM the following morning. Vaginal plug detection was performed daily at 8:00 AM, the day of vaginal plug detection was designated embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5). Pregnant mice were humanely euthanized at E18.5 (or E17.5 for Fig. 6-associated experiments) via CO2 asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation. After collection, for each dam, two non-consecutive fresh placental tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent molecular analysis, while another two non-consecutive placental tissues were fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight and embedded in paraffin for histological sectioning.

Female C57BL/6J mice (2 months old, n = 24) (Vital River, Beijing) were randomly divided into four experimental groups: control, sb4 intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection, AAV-Grem1(adeno-associated virus overexpressing mouse Grem1) tail vein (i.v.) injection, and AAV-Grem1 plus sb4 combination therapy. Following mating with 8-week-old male breeders, vaginal plug detection was performed daily at 8:00 AM, the day of vaginal plug detection was designated E0.5. Interventions commenced at E6.5 with AAV-NC (100 µL, 5 × 10¹¹ vector genomes/mL; Genomeditech, Shanghai) or Grem1-expressing AAV9 particles (equivalent dose in PBS) administered via tail vein. The BMP-SMAD pathway activator sb4 was intraperitoneally administered at 5 mg/kg every 48 h from E6.5 onward. The combination group received concurrent AAV-Grem1 and sb4 treatments. To be specific, pregnant mice were intravenously injected with either an empty vector or AAV-Grem1 via the tail vein at E6.5. Half of the mice in each group were randomly assigned to receive intraperitoneal injections of sb4 every 48 h until tissue collection at E17.5. All mice were maintained under SPF conditions (23 ± 1 °C, 12 h light/dark cycle) until humane euthanasia at E17.5 using CO2 asphyxiation. Placental tissues were either snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen or fixed in 4% PFA for subsequent analysis. Procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Tongji University Experimental Animal Center (No. TJBG11325103).

Statistical analyses

All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and R software (v4.2.1) within the RStudio environment (v2022.07.1). Prior to comparative analyses, the normality of continuous variables was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed via Levene’s test. For data meeting the assumptions of normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for two-group comparisons, and a one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was employed for multi-group analyses. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann-Whitney U test was utilized for comparisons between two groups, and the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test was applied for multi-group analyses. For the analysis of clinical characteristics, binary and categorical variables (such as fetal sex and parity) were assessed using Fisher’s exact test. To account for the nested structure of the murine data (multiple placentas per litter) and to minimize maternal effects, linear mixed-effects (LME) models were constructed using the lme4 (v1.1.35.3) and lmerTest packages (v3.1.3). In these models, the experimental group was treated as a fixed effect, while the individual dam was included as a random effect. For RNA sequencing differential expression analysis, P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the FDR. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Placentas from women of AMA exhibit senescent phenotypes

Compared to normal controls, human term placentas from AMA pregnancies exhibited significant upregulation of established senescence biomarkers p53 and p21. (Fig. 1A-B). Consistent with this, placental tissues from aged mice (10 months old) exhibited significantly increased expression levels of p21 and p53 relative to young controls (Fig. S1A-B). In addition, we performed immunohistochemistry to detect the expression of biomarkers of senescence in placentas. It was observed that the staining intensity levels of p16, p21, α-SMA, and BCL2 were higher from both human and murine AMA placentas than those from younger control pregnancies. (Fig. 1C-D, Fig. S1C-D). Furthermore, phenotypic characterization uncovered maternal-fetal disparities in aged dams, manifesting as diminished placental efficiency and abnormal developmental parameters in offspring, including reduced body length and weight (Fig. S1E-F).

GREM1 is aberrantly expressed in AMA placenta

To investigate the mechanisms underlying placental senescence, we performed RNA sequencing on 42 full-term placental samples encompassing maternal ages from 23 to 45 years (Table 1). Hierarchical clustering analysis revealed distinct transcriptional divergence between AMA and control placentas. Among 1566 detected transcripts, 198 exhibited differential expression (55 upregulated, 143 downregulated) in AMA placentas versus controls. (Fig. 2A). GO enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed significant enrichment in a broad spectrum of biological processes. While the most significantly altered pathways included terms such as cardiac muscle cell differentiation and regulation of angiogenesis, our study focused on those relevant to placental function and senescence, including extracellular space, cell morphogenesis, and cell-cell signaling (Fig. 2B). Among the upregulated genes, GREM1 was selected for further investigation as it exhibited the highest log FDR value. A volcano plot further highlights GREM1 as a core regulatory candidate (Fig. 2C). Simultaneously, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that DEGs were significantly enriched in metabolism-related and inflammation-related pathways (Fig. S2A-B). Our findings demonstrate that AMA placentas exhibit senescence with multiple complex aging phenotypes.

Then we analyzed mRNA transcription and protein expression of GREM1 in human placentas during pregnancy. The results demonstrated that the mRNA (Fig. 2D) and protein (Fig. 2E-F) levels of GREM1 were significantly elevated in AMA placentas compared to controls. Furthermore, GREM1 protein expression increased progressively with advancing gestational age (Fig. 2G-H), which was consistent with the immunofluorescence results. To further characterize the spatial distribution of GREM1, we performed immunofluorescence staining on placental tissues across different gestational stages. GREM1 (red) was found to be predominantly expressed in EVTs, as evidenced by co-localization with the EVT-specific marker HLA-G (green). Notably, increased GREM1 expression was observed in the placental tissues of the AMA group compared to the normal term group (Fig. 2I). Consistent with these clinical findings, parallel analyses in our aged murine model yielded similar results. Both mRNA and protein levels of Grem1 were found to be upregulated in the placental tissues of aged mice compared to young controls (Fig. S2C-E). These collective results demonstrate spatiotemporal co-patterning between GREM1 overexpression and senescence markers in AMA placentas.

GREM1 regulates cellular functions in HTR-8/SVneo cells

To investigate the functional impact of GREM1 dysregulation in trophoblasts, we established stable GREM1-overexpressing HTR-8/SVneo cell lines via lentiviral transduction. Our data showed that GREM1 protein levels were significantly increased in HTR-8/SVneo cells compared with empty vector (Fig. 3A). Then, we performed MTS assays to assess the effect of GREM1 on trophoblast cell proliferation. The results showed that upregulated GREM1 decreased the cell proliferation (Fig. 3B). In addition, we carried out transwell migration and invasion assays. Elevated GREM1 expression significantly impaired cellular migration and invasion capacities, reducing both functions by 50–60% (Fig. 3C-D). Further validating GREM1’s trophoblast functions, we used two siRNAs to reduce GREM1 (Fig. 3E). As shown, GREM1 knockdown significantly promoted trophoblast cell proliferation (Fig. 3F). While it induced a partial restoration of migration (Fig. 3G), a significant enhancement of invasion was specifically observed with siRNA #2 (Fig. 3H). Collectively, these data demonstrate that GREM1 is essential for regulating trophoblast migration and invasion capabilities.

Effect of BMP4 and GREM1 on doxorubicin-induced senescence in HTR-8/SVneo cells

To further explore whether GREM1 directly contributes to the senescence phenotype observed in clinical samples, we utilized DOX to establish an in vitro premature senescence model. Cells underwent 2 h pulsed exposure to DOX to induce DNA damage without immediate cytotoxicity, followed by 72 h recovery in DOX-free medium to permit senescence phenotype development (Fig. 4A). Western blot revealed that protein levels of senescence-associated markers (P16, p21, p53, BCL2, and γ-H2AX) with increasing concentrations of DOX (Fig. 4B, Fig. S3A).

To dissect GREM1’s regulatory role in senescence, we investigated effects of BMP4 and GREM1 in DOX-treated cells. Results demonstrated that rhGREM1 synergistically enhanced DOX-induced senescence in a dose-dependent manner, markedly elevating p16 and p53 expression (Fig. 4C, Fig. S3B). To further clarify GREM1-BMP4 dynamics in senescence regulation, we supplemented normal and senescent cells with rhGREM1 and quantified senescence markers. rhGREM1 treatment increased the expression of senescence markers, whereas rhBMP4 supplementation attenuated these effects (Fig. S3C-D). rhGREM1 significantly increased the staining of SA-β-gal in contrast to the effects of BMP4 (Fig. 4D). Analysis of the SASP components via RT-qPCR showed elevated expression of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, MMP9) and fibrosis marker α-SMA in rhGREM1-treated senescent cells, though some changes lacked statistical significance (Fig. 4E). To validate these findings at the protein level, ELISA was performed on the cell culture supernatants. Consistent with the transcriptional changes, rhGREM1 treatment amplified the secretion of IL-1β, IL-6, and MMP9, whereas rhBMP4 supplementation suppressed the secretion of these pro-inflammatory and proteolytic factors (Fig. 4F).

Dysregulation of the BMP-SMAD signaling axis contributes to cellular senescence and functional impairment in HTR-8/SVneo trophoblasts

Based on the opposing effects of GREM1 and BMP4 on trophoblast senescence, we proceeded to evaluate the activation status of their canonical downstream target, the BMP-SMAD signaling pathway. On the basis of the above observations, we speculated that the overexpression of GREM1 in AMA placentas disrupted the BMP-SMAD signaling pathway. To validate this hypothesis, we assessed the role of BMP-SMAD signaling pathway in GREM1-overexpressed trophoblast. Integrated analysis of BMP-SMAD pathway proteins and phosphorylation levels of SMAD1/5/9 confirmed that GREM1 overexpression potently suppressed pathway activation in HTR-8/SVneo cells. GREM1 overexpression significantly reduced protein levels of BMP4, and the ratio of p-SMAD1/5/9 to SMAD was also decreased (Fig. 5A, Fig. S4A). Treatment with sb4, a specific BMP-SMAD agonist, counteracted the effects of GREM1 overexpression by dose-dependently restoring the levels of BMP4 and p-SMAD1/5/9, while reducing the elevated expression of p21 and p53 (Fig. 5B, Fig. S4B). In line with these observations, SA-β-gal-positive staining was more than 2.5-fold lower in sb4 treated GREM1-overexpressed cells (Fig. 5H).

Furthermore, to assess the effects of BMP4-SMAD signaling pathway on GREM1-overexpressed trophoblast functions, the cells were subjected to cell proliferation, migration and invasion assays. The results of cell proliferation assay showed that sb4 increased the proliferation of GREM1-overexpressed HTR-8/SVneo cells (Fig. 5C). Meanwhile, sb4 significantly reversed the inhibitory effects of GREM1 overexpression on trophoblast cell migration and invasion (Fig. 5D-G).

Consistent with the effects of BMP4, sb4 also increased the mRNA level of ID1, a downstream transcription factor in the BMP-SMAD pathway; this induction was conversely suppressed by GREM1 (Fig. 5I).

To further investigate whether GREM1 exerts its pro-senescent effects by antagonizing the BMP4-SMAD pathway, we established a BMP4-knockdown (BMP4-KD) model in HTR-8/SVneo cells using siRNA and evaluated the expression of BMP-SMAD signaling components and canonical senescence markers (Fig. S5A). Western blot analysis and subsequent densitometric quantification revealed that both BMP4 depletion and treatment with rhGREM1 led to a significant reduction in p-SMAD1/5/9 levels, accompanied by the upregulation of senescence-associated markers (Fig. S5B). Furthermore, functional assays demonstrated that BMP4 knockdown phenocopied the effects of rhGREM1, leading to a comparable suppression of cell proliferation (Fig. S5C), migration, and invasion (Fig. S5D-E).

Grem1-overexpressing mice exhibit placental senescence phenotypes

To determine whether these in vitro mechanistic findings translate to an in vivo physiological context, we established a Grem1-overexpressing mouse model using AAV9 vectors (Fig. 6A). Normal-aged pregnant mice were intravenously injected with the viral vector at E6.5, followed by intraperitoneal administration of sb4, with placental and fetal tissues collected at E17.5. Western blot confirmed successful GREM1 overexpression in placentas (Fig. 6B, Fig. S6A). Western blotting indicated that compared with those in the placentas of control group, in addition to decrease of p-SMAD1/5/9, the levels of GREM1 and senescence markers (p16, p21, p53) proteins were upregulated in Grem1-overexpressed mice, which was rescued by sb4 treatment (Fig. S6A).

Fetal phenotyping demonstrated that the Grem1-overexpression group exhibited increased rates of fetal resorption and hemorrhage at E17.5 compared to the empty vector control and sb4-treated groups, while sb4 treatment showed a trend toward reducing these abnormal outcomes (Fig. 6C). Histopathological examination (H&E staining) showed that empty vector control placentas maintained an intact labyrinth layer with dense vascular networks and well-defined maternal-fetal boundaries. In contrast, Grem1-overexpressing placentas, despite normal size, displayed labyrinthine hypoplasia characterized by reduced vascular density, structural disorganization, blurred boundaries, and extensive maternal erythrocyte infiltration, indicative of vascular rupture and hemorrhage (Fig. 6D). This result may have contributed to the impairment of placental efficiency (Fig. 6E). Consequently, while absolute placental weights remained unchanged across groups, significantly lower fetal birth weights were observed in the AAV-Grem1 group. Notably, although sb4 effectively rescued placental structural architecture, it did not significantly reverse the fetal weight reduction at E17.5 (Fig. 6F-H).

Similarly, we performed rescue experiments in AMA pregnant mice by administering sb4, via tail vein injection (Fig. S6B), with the goal of alleviating the advanced maternal age phenotype by restoring the BMP4-SMAD signaling suppressed by GREM1. Our results demonstrated that sb4 treatment significantly enhanced the phosphorylation levels of SMAD1/5/9 and concurrently reduced the expression of canonical senescence markers, although the reductions in p16 and p53 did not reach statistical significance in the placental tissues of AMA mice in the placental tissues of AMA mice (Fig. S6B-C). However, despite a subtle trend toward improvement, we did not observe statistically significant changes in fetal weight, placental weight, or placental efficiency following sb4 treatment (Fig. S6D-F).

Given the systemic tropism of the AAV9 vector, we also evaluated off-target GREM1 expression in maternal organs. While immunohistochemistry revealed distinct off-target GREM1 expression in the maternal liver and kidneys of the AAV-Grem1 group, histopathological and senescence marker evaluations showed no evidence of severe acute organ damage or massive inflammatory infiltration (Fig. S7A-B).

Discussion

In recent years, the global rise in pregnancies among women of AMA has been accompanied by a concurrent increase in maternal and neonatal complications such as gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders, chromosomal abnormalities, and elevated cesarean section rates [25, 26]. Although extensive epidemiological studies have confirmed the association between AMA and adverse outcomes [27], the underlying mechanisms are still undefined.

In our study, sequencing analysis of 42 human placental samples revealed for the first time a significant upregulation of GREM1 expression in AMA placentas. GREM1 was widely distributed in placental tissues, particularly enriched in trophoblast cells.

It is well elucidated that dysregulated proliferation and apoptosis of trophoblast cells contribute to placental dysfunction, which underlies the pathological mechanisms of gestational complications [28, 29]. To investigate the role of elevated GREM1 in aging trophoblasts, we modulated GREM1 expression in HTR-8/SVneo cells. Overexpression of GREM1 suppressed cell functions, including proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas knockdown of GREM1 rescued these alterations.

By manipulating the senescence status of HTR-8/SVneo cells [30], we found that exogenous GREM1 recombinant protein significantly promoted cellular senescence. As a classical antagonist of BMP4 [31], GREM1 likely exerts its pro-senescent effects in AMA placentas via the BMP-SMAD pathway. This was supported by the partial rescue of senescence phenotypes in aged HTR-8/SVneo cells upon supplementation with recombinant BMP4 protein.

As secreted extracellular matrix (ECM)-associated proteins, BMPs regulate diverse developmental processes, including limb and kidney formation. Their activity is modulated by antagonists such as GREM1 in a context-dependent manner. Recent studies in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) models suggest that BMP4 possesses anti-senescent properties, while GREM1, primarily a BMP4 antagonist, appears to directly amplify senescence induction [33], which is consistent with our present data.

Our results demonstrate that GREM1 promotes senescence in HTR-8/SVneo cells by antagonizing BMP4 and impairing downstream SMAD1/5/9 phosphorylation. This impairment of SMAD signaling results in the loss of ID1, a known negative regulator of senescence markers; consequently, this loss releases the brake on p16 and p21 expression, locking the trophoblasts into a senescent state. Using sb4, a BMP-SMAD pathway activator [32], we validated these findings in animal models, where sb4 administration partially rescued placental premature aging, reduced placental efficiency, and GREM1 overexpression induced by AAV9-Grem1 in young mice.

In summary, we identified GREM1 acted as a pro-senescent driver, targeting the BMP-SMAD signaling axis in trophoblasts. Our study highlights BMP4 supplementation and anti-GREM1 therapies as potential novel strategies to mitigate placental aging in AMA. Future research should focus on mapping the dynamic landscape of placental senescence and developing cell-type-specific interventions tailored to the placental microenvironment.

Several limitations of the current study warrant consideration. Our differential expression analysis is relatively underpowered, and these transcriptomic findings should explicitly be interpreted as an exploratory analysis. Furthermore, while the HTR-8/SVneo cell line is widely utilized, this immortalized model may not perfectly recapitulate the complex physiological characteristics of primary extravillous trophoblasts in vivo. From a genetic standpoint, the absence of a conditional Grem1 knockdown mouse model restricts our ability to provide the most direct test of whether GREM1 causally drives the AMA phenotype. This methodological constraint is compounded by the broad systemic tropism of the AAV9 vector used for in vivo overexpression. Although supplementary evaluations did not indicate severe acute organ damage, off-target GREM1 overexpression in maternal organs, such as the liver or kidney, could still potentially confound the observed fetal and placental phenotypes. Future investigations employing trophoblast-specific promoters will be essential to isolate local placental effects from maternal systemic confounders completely. Ultimately, the inability of the BMP signaling agonist to fully reverse macroscopic adverse pregnancy outcomes suggests that AMA-associated complications are driven by multifaceted systemic impairments that extend beyond a single signaling axis.

Notably, recent studies have reported that other senescence-associated pathways (e.g., mTOR-autophagy dysregulation) are involved in placental aging [33]. However, aberrant activation of the mTOR pathway is generally linked to nutrient stress-induced autophagy inhibition [34], suggesting that GREM1-BMP4 imbalance may act independently of canonical nutrient-sensing mechanisms. These findings indicate that, compared to pan-senescence-related pathways, GREM1-BMP4 imbalance may represent an early molecular event in AMA placental senescence, which directly disrupts trophoblast cell function rather than causing global metabolic collapse to drive pathological phenotypes, thereby providing a more precise temporal and spatial window for targeted interventions.

Although experimental data indicate that BMP4 supplementation partially rescues senescence phenotypes, this study did not account for factors such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation or telomere attrition. Notably, mitochondrial dysfunction during senescence is often concomitant with ROS overproduction and reduced Adenosine Triphosphate synthesis [35], which should be quantified in follow-up investigations. Additionally, senescence-associated telomerase (TERT) deficiency may synergistically exacerbate BMP-SMAD signaling attenuation [36]. These observations suggest that GREM1/BMP4 imbalance likely represents a single node within the placental senescence cascade rather than an isolated mechanism. Future studies should employ multi-omics integration to dissect the spatiotemporal dynamics of multi-factor crosstalk, thereby comprehensively elucidating the hierarchical regulatory architecture underlying AMA-associated placental dysfunction.

Conclusion

This study elucidates that aberrant GREM1 expression in AMA placentas drives trophoblast senescence and dysfunction by antagonizing BMP4 and impairing SMAD1/5/9 phosphorylation, ultimately leading to placental aging and adverse pregnancy outcomes. In vitro, GREM1 overexpression suppressed trophoblast proliferation, migration, and invasion while inducing senescence, whereas activating the BMP-SMAD pathway (via sb4 agonist or exogenous BMP4) rescued these defects. In vivo, AAV9-mediated Grem1 overexpression triggered premature placental aging and fetal abnormalities in mice, phenotypes significantly ameliorated by sb4 treatment. Notably, although sb4 did not significantly reverse macroscopic outcomes in naturally aged AMA mice, it mitigated molecular senescence markers, suggesting that AMA complications involve multifaceted mechanisms beyond the GREM1/BMP4 axis. These findings identify GREM1-BMP4 imbalance as a core mechanism underlying AMA placental pathology and propose targeted modulation of the BMP-SMAD axis or anti-GREM1 strategies as promising therapeutic interventions.
