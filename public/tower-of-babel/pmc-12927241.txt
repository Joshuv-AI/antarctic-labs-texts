Golden hour(s) quality improvement protocols and their effect on outcomes in preterm infants ≤ 32 weeks of gestation: a scoping review
Authors: Leonore M C Wever, Vilya Nous, Marije Hogeveen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePrematurity is the leading cause of under-five mortality worldwide. The first hours after birth (Golden Hours, GH), are critical for preterm infants, as early interventions impact morbidity and mortality. While GH-protocols aim to optimize stabilization and NICU admission, their components and outcomes vary. This study systematically reviews GH-protocols to identify common elements, implementation differences, and clinical outcomes, informing best practices and highlighting knowledge gaps.Study designJBI and PRISMA-ScR guidelines were followed to map GH-protocol content and outcomes for preterm infants. Three-step search strategy included database searches and reference screening. Two reviewers extracted data on study characteristics, GH-protocol components, and outcomes. Study quality was assessed using the QI-MQCS checklist.ResultsOf 2,656 records, 34 studies were included, mostly from the USA (n=17). Infants had gestational ages from 24.4-30.2 weeks, and birth weights from 670-1,418 grams. GH-protocol content varied, with respiratory support (31/34 studies), cardiovascular support (26/34), thermoregulation (24/34), and team-based interventions (24/34) being most common. Improved protocol adherence was reported, yielding decreased mechanical ventilation, and quicker GH completion. Clinical outcomes showed improvements in mortality, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, intraventricular hemorrhage, necrotizing enterocolitis, and sepsis. Additionally, 7/9 studies observed significant increase in delayed cord clamping.ConclusionGH-protocols for preterm infants demonstrate beneficial effects on morbidity and mortality, yet exhibit variability. Differences in populations, hospital resources, and methodologies hinder cross-study comparisons. Standardized guidelines and outcome measures are needed to refine GH-protocols and identify effective interventions. Future research should evaluate short- and long-term effects of GH-protocols across diverse healthcare settings to enhance neonatal outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06464-0.Keywords: Golden hour protocol, Quality improvement, Adherence, Neonatology, Preterm

Introduction

Prematurity is the leading cause of death among children under five worldwide [1]. In the Netherlands, 48,4% of neonatal mortality in 2020 occurred in infants born between 24 weeks and 32 weeks of gestation [2]. The transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life is critical, particularly for preterm infants, who are highly vulnerable. Early postnatal care significantly influences both short- and long-term outcomes [3]. Consequently, the first hour(s) after birth are often referred to as the Golden Hour(s) (GH).

Originating in traumatology, the GH concept emphasizes early interventions in the first hour(s) after trauma to optimize patient outcomes [4]. In neonatology, it refers to the first hours after birth, during which initial stabilization, assessment and admission to the neonatal intensive care (NICU) occur. Several studies have described quality improvement initiatives targeting this critical period, although the specific components and reported outcomes of these GH-protocols vary [5–7]. Previous reviews have primarily focused on individual interventions [8–10]. However, the implementation of a standardized, evidence-based GH-protocol tailored to specific clinical settings may improve neonatal outcomes in this high-risk population.

Two recent reviews have examined GH-protocols in more detail [11, 12]. Lamary et al. highlighted the potential benefits of standardized GH-protocols but did not apply a systematic methodology or assess implementation quality [11]. Sheng et al. focused on selected GH quality improvement bundles and performed a meta-analysis of three predefined outcomes; however, their review did not systematically compare protocol content or implementation strategies, and included only a limited number of studies [12]. In contrast, our scoping review applies a comprehensive search strategy, includes a broader and more diverse set of studies, and uses the QI-MQCS tool to evaluate methodological and implementation quality. This approach allows for a structured synthesis of both GH protocol components and real-world application, offering practical insights to guide future quality improvement and research efforts.

Our scoping review addresses these gaps by systematically mapping published GH-protocols for preterm infants born before 32 weeks’ gestation. In contrast to previous work, we analyse both the clinical content and real-world implementation of these protocols, using a structured quality appraisal tool (QI-MQCS) to assess methodological rigour. By identifying common elements, implementation strategies, and reported outcomes, we provide a broader and more detailed synthesis. This approach highlights critical gaps in the literature and offers practical insights for future research and local quality improvement initiatives.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the latest JBI guidance for scoping reviews [13] and reported according to the PRISMA-ScR checklist (Document 1, additional material) [14]. An a priori protocol was developed for the scientific internship of VN (available upon request).

Inclusion criteria

Participants

Very preterm infants born (32 weeks of gestation), very low birth weight (<1500 grams) and extremely low birth weight infants (< 1000 grams).

Concept

Description of content and related outcomes of GH-protocols for very preterm infants concerning the immediate perinatal period. A GH-protocol was defined as a bundle of multiple interventions or actions, within multiple domains, and related to multiple outcomes. Interventions in these protocols should primarily focus on actions taken shortly before or within the first hours after birth.

Context

Perinatal hospital care of very preterm infants, covering the period from imminent preterm delivery up to the first hours after NICU admission.

Types of sources of evidence

Research articles published in English, German, French, Spanish or Dutch between inception and April 1, 2023. The following study designs were excluded: reviews, case reports or series, editorials, comments, and abstracts. If applicable, systematic reviews were screened for relevant articles. Given the specific nature of this topic, no grey literature was searched.

Search strategy

A three-step search strategy as recommended by JBI was employed [13]. First, pre-existing literature was searched to identify key articles. An analysis of their index terms was conducted to develop a search strategy in cooperation with an experienced literature specialist and according to the PRESS checklist (Document 2, additional material) [15]. Second, MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, Embase, and CINAHL were searched from inception until April 1, 2023. Third, reference lists of included articles and relevant reviews were hand-searched.

Sources of evidence selection

Results were imported and deduplicated in Endnote version X9. Thereafter, the remaining results were imported into Rayyan QCRI [16]. After pilot testing a random sample of 25 results with the team, we discussed and clarified the inclusion criteria, and the specific factors used to assess eligibility. Subsequently, one reviewer (VN) screened all titles and abstracts, classifying them as either “clearly not eligible” or “maybe eligible” based on these criteria. After this initial selection, a second title and abstract screening was conducted by two independent reviewers on the latter category (LW, VN). Publications were categorized into “not eligible” and “possibly eligible”. Full text analysis was then performed independently by the same two on the latter category. Discrepancies between the two reviewers were discussed with a third reviewer (MH) until consensus was reached.

Data charting

Extracted data were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and presented as tables focusing on content and outcomes.

Critical appraisal

To obtain insight into the quality of our results, the Quality Improvement Minimum Quality Criteria Set (QI-MQCS) checklist was used after inclusion [19]. Individual studies were scored on sixteen domains, each domain received a score of 1 if minimum criteria were met, and a score of 0 if not. To visually summarize the direction of evidence across studies reporting on outcomes, Harvest plots were created on the three most frequently reported outcomes. Each study is represented by a single bar, stratified by the reported direction of effect (positive, negative or no effect). The height of the bar corresponds to the study’s QI-MQCS score. The plots were generated using R (version 4.3.0) with ggplot2 and dplyr packages.

Characteristics of included studies

The majority of studies were published after 2010, and originated from high-income countries, primarily the USA (n = 17), Canada (n = 9), both (n = 2), and Europe (n = 3). Mean/median gestational age (GA) in the individual studies varied from 24.4 to 30.2 weeks, and birth weight (BW) from 670 to 1,418 grams. Cohort sizes ranged from 7 to 12,407 patients. Inclusion criteria were based on GA alone (25 studies), GA, and BW combined (6 studies), or BW (3 studies). GA thresholds varied from < 26 weeks to < 34 weeks, while BW thresholds ranged from < 1,000 to < 1,500 grams. Approximately one third (11/34) provided a checklist of their GH-protocol. Six studies reported on protocol adherence only, five studies on clinical outcomes only, and 23 studies reported both on adherence and clinical outcomes.

The majority of the studies included one or more items on ventilatory support and/or oxygen/saturation management in their GH-protocol (31/34 studies, 91%), with surfactant administration as the most commonly reported item (23/31).

Intravenous access, either peripheral or central, was included in 23/26 studies. The registration of vital signs was mentioned in 10/26 studies, and hypotension management was reported in 8/26 studies.

Most studies reporting on thermoregulation described the use of a polyethylene bag (23/24), and monitoring the temperature of the newborn (11/24). Preventive measures were mentioned in 22 studies including increase of delivery room (DR) temperature (15/22), use of a heated mattress (15/22), a pre-warmed incubator (12/22), a radiant warmer (11/22), and heated blankets (7/22).

(De)briefing (16/24), role assignment (13/24), and training (12/24) were most commonly reported in the team domain.

Nutritional focus was on early parenteral nutrition (20/23), early expressed breast milk (EBM) or donor milk use (8/23), and colostrum use (6/23).

Various studies included elements of further investigations, such as x-rays or pharmacology management (16/21). Lab orders (standardized times, ordersets, cord blood) were discussed in 13/21 studies, and 10/21 studies included the (rapid) use of radiology for example after line placement.

DCC was incorporated in 22/34 studies.

For infection prevention, 14/18 studies structured antibiotics use (timing of administration, empiric use, early termination), and 7/18 studies implemented hygiene practices.

Timely communication with family was part of the GH-protocol in 13 studies.

Regarding neuroprotection, 9/12 studies reported on specific positioning of the preterm infant, 8/12 studies addressed minimal handling, 7/12 studies discussed bed elevation (headside), 6/12 studies included reducing environmental stimuli such as noise and light control or two-person care, and 5/12 studies encouraged skin-to-skin care.

In the environment-based domain, 6/10 studies examined specialized rooms for very preterm infants (either DR or in NICU), and 5/10 studies addressed managing light and noise levels.

GH protocol adherence

At least one protocol adherence measure was reported in 29 out of 34 studies. Protocol adherence outcomes included improved GH completion time, increased DCC (7/9), reduced mechanical ventilation (5/29) decreased time to iv access, and/or fluid administration (8/18), decreased use of opioids and sedation, increased early caffeine prescription, increased prescription of vitamin K and of prophylactic indomethacin, improved compliance with laboratory orders, and reduced time to blood gas results and x-ray acquisition.

Surfactant administration was reported in 8/27 studies, with significant reductions in three studies, and increased use in one. Higher admission temperature or less hypothermia was reported in 6/19 studies, with increased hyperthermia incidence reported in one.

Both decreased and increased hypoglycemia rates were reported (3/12 studies).

Table 4 presents GH-protocol effects on morbidity and mortality (29/34 studies). Most reported outcomes are mortality/survival (27/34), respiratory (26/34), and neurological outcomes (26/34). Significant changes in mortality/survival were reported in 9/27 studies. BPD was studied in 25/34 studies, of which 7/25 observed a significant decrease. Of the 22 studies reporting on incidence of NEC, seven reported significant decreases in NEC and/or FIP, either overall or in subgroups. Decreased incidence of ROP was described in 5/19 studies. Most studies reporting composite scores used IVH and mortality (9/34) of which 89% studies showed a significant decrease. One study reported a significant reduction in outcome “death and PHVD”, and one study described a significant increase in survival without any neurodevelopmental injury (NDI). Lapcharoensap et al. showed an increased IVH rate in hypothermic newborns (< 36 °C). While GH protocol implementation was generally associated with improved outcomes, heterogeneity in study design, populations, and protocol adherence limits direct comparisons. Further standardization and research are required to determine the most effective GH interventions.

Critical appraisal

A total of 34 studies were assessed using the QI-MQCS domains (Table 5, additional material) with varying emphasis across categories. Key domains such as organizational motivation, intervention rationale, and implementation were reported in all studies. Organizational characteristics (31 studies) and adherence to the intervention (28 studies) were commonly documented, while penetration/reach (5 studies) and sustainability (14 studies) were less frequently addressed (Fig. 2, additional material). These findings highlight strengths in reporting intervention details but also reveal gaps in sustainability and comparator care processes. Studies are arranged according to QI-MQCS scores in Datafile 1, additional material (Tab E). To visualize outcome differences across studies according to their QI-MQCS scores, separate Harvest plots were created for mortality, BPD, and IVH (Figs. 3a-c, additional material).

Discussion

The “Golden Hour(s)” in neonatology refer to structured stabilization protocols aimed at improving survival and reducing complications in very preterm infants. Given increasing evidence supporting their efficacy, we systematically reviewed the literature to map existing GH-protocols and their components, to assess their impact and to identify areas for standardization and future research.

Our review highlights considerable variation in GH-protocols, including differences in bundle components, adherence, and reported outcomes. Heterogeneity in study methodologies, definitions, population, and adherence monitoring complicates comparisons. Variability in hospital resources, staff training, and concurrent quality improvement initiatives further contributes to differences in effectiveness.

Despite variability, respiratory support was the most commonly addressed domain, with surfactant administration frequently included. However, the timing of surfactant remains contentious, underscoring the need for further research to determine optimal strategies based on risk profiles. Other commonly included components were team-based interventions, thermoregulation, intravenous access, early nutrition, and infection prevention. In contrast, communication with families, neuroprotection, and environmental considerations were less frequently addressed.

Although GH-protocols were generally associated with improved outcomes, including mortality and morbidity, findings were inconsistent. Some studies reported decreased mechanical ventilation and better respiratory outcomes, while others found no clear benefit. Inotropes were inconsistently addressed, perhaps reflecting diverse cardiovascular management strategies. Nutritional practices vary, highlighting the need for standardized guidelines to optimize growth and reduce complications such as necrotizing enterocolitis.

Effective teamwork and communication are pivotal during GH. Studies emphasize the importance of well-coordinated multidisciplinary teams to execute GH-protocols efficiently [52]. Simulation training and checklists were commonly used to enhance team performance and adherence [53, 54].

However, long-term outcomes, particularly neurodevelopmental measures, remain largely unexplored. Only two studies investigated long-term effects, showing no significant impact at two years corrected age [41, 42]. Future research should assess the broader quality of life implications of GH-protocols, including chronic health conditions, functional abilities, and social integration.

A major limitation of GH research is methodological heterogeneity, with studies differing in design, adherence measures, and outcome reporting. While this variability hampers direct comparisons and prevents definitive conclusions about causality, it also reflects the nature of quality improvement work: interventions are inherently adapted to local context, resources, and resources. Rather than viewing this heterogeneity solely as a limitation, it should also be recognized as a strength of QI science: rooted in practice. For example, one NICU may prioritize thermoregulation in response to high rates of admission hypothermia, while another may focus on early respiratory support. This tailored approach limits generalizability but enhances local success. Our review, therefore, does not seek to prescribe a single GH-protocol, but instead identifies shared elements, enablers of success, and gaps in implementation reporting. To facilitate cross-site learning, we recommend consistent use of QI reporting frameworks, context description, and core outcome sets. Tools such as the QUICK checklist may also help clinicians apply findings across diverse healthcare settings [55]. Additionally, most studies originate from high-income settings, limiting generalizability to low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where resource constraints may affect protocol feasibility and effectiveness.

Cost-effectiveness analyses are also lacking. While GH interventions may reduce complications and length of stay, the long-term economic impact remains unclear. Future studies should evaluate both direct and indirect costs to assess feasibility across diverse healthcare settings.

A critical observation from our review is the inconsistent or absent reporting of protocol adherence. Fewer than half of the included studies described adherence in a structured or quantifiable way, and where this was done, it often applied only to selected components of the bundle. This lack of process measurement limits interpretability of findings: without knowing whether a protocol was delivered as intended, it is not possible to determine whether observed improvements—or lack thereof—are attributable to the intervention itself. As the goal of QI is not only to implement change but to understand its effects, future GH studies must define and measure process indicators alongside clinical outcomes. Consistent use of implementation frameworks and adherence metrics will strengthen both internal validity and the ability to generalize findings. Strengths and limitations

A key strength of our review is the incorporation of implementation quality. We used the QI-MQCS tool to appraise implementation domains across studies, revealing wide variation in adherence reporting, training strategies, sustainability planning, and reach. While the QI-MQCS tool was valuable in assessing the methodological quality and reporting completeness of the included QI studies, it does not provide detailed guidance on how to extract actionable elements for practice change. The recently proposed QUICK tool (Quality Improvement Checklist for Knowledge Translation [55] offers a complementary perspective by helping clinicians and QI teams apply learnings from published studies to their own settings. The integration of tools such as QUICK could enhance future scoping and systematic reviews by bridging the gap between evidence appraisal and implementation planning. Two recent reviews have also examined GH quality improvement protocols in neonatology [11, 12]. Sheng et al. focused on clinical outcomes in extremely preterm or extremely low birth weight infants, reporting improvements in admission temperature, glycaemia, and—less consistently—BPD [12]. Lamary et al. described variability in bundle composition and implementation barriers but did not use a structured framework [11]. Our review builds on these by providing a more detailed analysis of protocol components, assessing process and implementation outcomes, and applying the QI-MQCS framework. While our findings partly align with Sheng et al., we include a broader, more global sample, and focus on implementation quality. Collectively, these comparisons emphasize that although GH-protocols may improve outcomes, substantial variability in content and adherence persists, underscoring the need for improved reporting and implementation-informed research. Compared to these reviews, our study adds value through a rigorous search strategy and structured PRISMA-ScR reporting. This global scope highlights underrepresentation of LMIC’s, where resources may influence GH protocol adoption.

Conclusions and future directions

GH-protocols offer a promising, structured approach to optimizing care for very preterm infants, integrating evidence-based and consensus-driven practices. However, significant variability in protocol content, implementation, and outcome reporting hinders generalizability and limits causal inference. Our review shows that reporting on adherence, as other implementation domains, is often lacking. To optimize GH care, future studies should use standardized study methodologies, adherence measures, and core outcome reporting. Future research should focus on refining GH-protocols, tailoring them to diverse healthcare settings, and assessing their long-term impact on neonatal outcomes.

This review provides a foundation for developing new GH-protocols and highlights the need for continuous quality improvement to ensure the best possible care for vulnerable neonates.

Take home messages

Although Golden Hour protocols are widely implemented and associated with improved outcomes for preterm infants, substantial variability exists in their content, execution, and reporting. Our review highlights that many studies fail to report key implementation outcomes—particularly adherence—limiting the ability to draw causal inferences. To truly understand and optimize GH care, future studies must not only report clinical outcomes, but also adopt standardized frameworks for quality improvement reporting and explicitly evaluate fidelity and sustainability.
