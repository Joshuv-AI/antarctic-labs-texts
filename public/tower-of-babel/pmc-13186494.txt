Efficacy and safety of daily drug regimens containing high-dose versus standard-dose rifampicin for treating pulmonary tuberculosis: Systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ashutosh N. Aggarwal, Ritesh Agarwal, Sahajal Dhooria, Perumal K. Bhavani, Kuruswamy T. Prasad, Inderpaul S. Sehgal, Valliappan Muthu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Standard rifampicin doses used to treat pulmonary tuberculosis lie at the lower end of drug exposure-response curve. We evaluated if higher daily rifampicin doses result in greater efficacy or harm. We searched literature databases for randomized controlled trials comparing efficacy and/or safety of standard and high rifampicin doses (>=15 mg/kg/day) for treating pulmonary tuberculosis. Our co-primary efficacy endpoints were proportion of patients with positive mycobacterial culture at end of intensive phase and end of treatment. The primary safety endpoint was incidence of severe hepatotoxicity. Secondary efficacy endpoints were proportion of patients with culture positivity at 1, 3, and 4 months, death, treatment failure, recurrence after treatment, and loss to follow-up. Secondary safety endpoints were incidence of adverse event (any, serious, and drug-related serious), hepatotoxicity, and treatment interruption/discontinuation. We generated summary risk ratios (RR) using random effects models. We identified 5668 publications and selected 12 studies with 3230 participants. Nine studies had low risk of bias. Those on high-dose rifampicin had significantly lower culture positivity at end of intensive phase (summary RR 0.74, 95%CI 0.62-0.89), but not at end of treatment (summary RR 0.52, 95%CI 0.23-1.15), as well as significantly higher incidence of severe hepatotoxicity (summary RR 2.06, 95% CI 1.15-3.68) and treatment interruption/discontinuation (summary RR 1.89, 95%CI 1.09-3.28). Other secondary efficacy and safety outcomes were similar between the two treatment strategies. We conclude that higher daily rifampicin doses accelerate sputum conversion by the end of intensive phase of ATT, but severe hepatotoxicity remains a potential concern.

INTRODUCTION

Tuberculosis continues to be an important public health problem globally, with 10.8 million incident cases and 1.25 million deaths estimated worldwide in 2023.[1] Advances in pharmacotherapy have shortened the anti-tubercular treatment (ATT) duration to 6 months. However, the treatment success rates are still around 88% globally, with wide geographic variations, indicating a significant scope for improvement.[1]

Rifampicin is a critical component of the current multi-drug short-course ATT because of its good bactericidal and sterilising activity, both intracellular and extracellular, and the ability to prevent recurrence after treatment. Unfortunately, the optimal rifampicin dose was never established rigorously, but largely chosen based on economic and safety considerations.[2] Indeed, the currently recommended daily rifampicin dose (10 mg/kg, range 8–12) is actually placed at the lower end of the drug exposure-response curve.[2] Subtherapeutic drug levels are not uncommon at these doses, which might lead to worse outcomes or acquisition of rifampicin resistance.[345] Hence, increasing rifampicin dose can further improve treatment outcomes, and potentially reduce the ATT duration.[6]

Animal experiments indicate that greater rifampicin exposure accelerates disease clearance and reduces subsequent recurrence in murine tuberculosis models.[789] Other studies suggest that escalating rifampicin dose up to 35 mg/kg or even more could lead to an increase in peak drug concentration, enhancing its bactericidal activity.[10111213] However, increased risk of adverse effects, especially hepatotoxicity, remains a potential concern at such high doses.[14] Clinically, higher rifampicin doses have been safely and successfully used for patients exhibiting low plasma rifampicin levels while on ATT, and in patients with severe disease (including meningeal tuberculosis).[15] High-dose rifampicin has shown equivalent safety profile in patients with tuberculous meningitis, but does not reduce mortality.[1617] A recent study found that treatment with higher rifampicin doses may also augment host immunological response against pulmonary tuberculosis.[18]

Therefore, consolidating the evidence base on this topic with reference to current standards of treating pulmonary tuberculosis is an unmet need.[19] We conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate if higher daily oral rifampicin doses (≥15 mg/kg/day) administered to patients with pulmonary tuberculosis were associated with different therapeutic efficacies or incidence of adverse effects, when compared to standard-dose rifampicin. We also explore if progressively increasing daily rifampicin doses show any trend towards greater efficacy or harm.

METHODS

The approach for this systematic review and meta-analysis is outlined in a protocol registered with the PROSPERO International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (ID CRD42024510530). Approval from our Institutional Ethics Committee was not required as we extracted and summarised already published information. We report our findings in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.[20]

Search strategy

We initially searched PubMed and Embase databases and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) for publications indexed up to 15 February 2024 using the search terms (rifampicin OR rifampin) AND (dosage OR dose) AND (high OR higher OR increased OR greater) AND (tuberculosis OR TB OR mycobacterium). No geographic, linguistic, or date restrictions were applied. We further updated our search on 1 October 2024. Additionally, relevant publications were identified from the bibliographies of research articles and reviews, and from our personal files.

Study selection

We included parallel-group randomised controlled trials (RCTs) that compared the efficacy or safety of various rifampicin doses for treating rifampicin-susceptible active pulmonary tuberculosis in adult human subjects. We ensured that each included study had at least one group receiving oral rifampicin in higher doses from treatment initiation for at least the intensive phase of therapy, and at least another group receiving oral rifampicin in a standard dose throughout treatment. Tuberculosis was microbiologically confirmed for study participants based on sputum smear, molecular testing, or culture analysis. Both high-dose and standard-dose treatment regimens contained at least isoniazid and rifampicin, administered for a minimum of 4 months (or 16 weeks), with at least ethambutol or pyrazinamide in the initial intensive phase. We targeted publications reporting on daily ATT at least in the intensive phase as intermittent regimens throughout do not reflect current standards of tuberculosis care. Daily ATT regimens were considered as those in which patients received drugs for at least 5 days in a week. We defined standard-dose rifampicin as a daily dose of rifampicin of 10 mg/kg based on current guidance for tuberculosis treatment, and high-dose rifampicin as a daily rifampicin dosage of 15 mg/kg or greater. For fixed rifampicin dosing regimens, we considered daily rifampicin doses of up to 600 mg, and 900 mg or greater, as standard and high doses, respectively, and converted it to a per-unit-weight dose for a 60 kg person for our analysis.

Identification of studies fulfilling our search criteria was done independently by two reviewers (ANA and RA). Any disagreements were resolved through mutual discussion or by consulting a third adjudicator (SD) in the absence of consensus. After removing duplicate citations, we screened all study titles and abstracts identified from our literature search. Apart from studies not fulfilling our inclusion criteria, we also excluded conference presentations, review articles, editorials, case reports, letters to editor not describing original research, and clinical trial protocols. We then evaluated full-text versions of the remaining potentially relevant publications against our eligibility criteria. Where multiple publications described any outcome of interest among the overlapping or same study population, we chose the one with a larger sample size or one reporting greater details on that outcome as our primary dataset. We finally selected studies meeting all our inclusion criteria and describing one or more outcomes of interest (see below).

Study endpoints

Our co-primary efficacy endpoints were the proportion of patients with positive mycobacterial culture at end of intensive phase (2 months or 8 weeks) and at end of treatment (6 months or 24–26 weeks). The primary safety endpoint was incidence of severe hepatotoxicity during treatment. Secondary efficacy endpoints were time to mycobacterial culture conversion (assessed at the end of intensive phase and at end of treatment), proportion of patients with mycobacterial culture positivity at 1, 3, and 4 months (or equivalent weeks), death during treatment, treatment failure, recurrence after treatment completion, and loss to follow-up. Secondary safety endpoints were incidence of any adverse event, serious adverse event, drug-related serious adverse event, any hepatotoxicity, and treatment interruption/discontinuation due to adverse effects.

Data extraction

We extracted the following information from the included studies: Location and period of study, trial design, participant inclusion and exclusion criteria, demographic and clinical characteristics of study subjects, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status, method of tuberculosis diagnosis, drugs and their dosing and duration in each treatment arm, any co-interventions (if applicable), number of subjects randomised and analysed in each rifampicin regimen, supervision of drug administration, and outcomes of interest in each treatment arm.

Wherever available, we used results from Mycobacterial Growth Indicator Tube (MGIT) platform over Lowenstein–Jenson slopes for analysing mycobacterial culture data. Wherever studies stratified adverse events using tools like Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), or Division of AIDS (DAIDS) grading, we considered serious adverse events as those that were severe or potentially life-threatening or resulted in death (i.e., grade 3 or higher).[2122] If studies reported adverse effects as number of events, rather than number of participants experiencing events, we extracted data only for outcomes that were likely to occur a single time (e.g., treatment interruption, mortality).

Study quality and publication bias

Risk of bias appraisal was done independently for each included study using the Cochrane’s Risk of Bias Tool version 2.0.[23] This tool checks if adequate measures were undertaken to reduce the risk of bias arising from five specific domains: (a) randomisation process, (b) deviations from intended interventions, (c) missing outcome data, (d) outcome measurement, and (e) selective reporting. For each domain, risk of bias was graded as being ‘low risk’, ‘some concerns’, or ‘high risk’. A study was considered as having an overall high risk of bias if any domain was rated as high risk.

Risk of publication bias was assessed by visual appraisal of funnel plot asymmetry, and Begg and Mazumdar’s rank correlation test, only when at least ten studies were available for meta-analysis of any pre-specified outcome.[2425]

Statistical analysis

We calculated risk ratios (RRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for each study describing the number of subjects experiencing binary outcomes in the high-dose and standard-dose treatment arms. We extracted hazard ratio (HR) and corresponding 95% CI for time-to-event data. Wherever feasible, we assessed drug safety from patients administered any drug dose in the intention-to-treat (ITT) study population and evaluated drug efficacy from the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) study population. If results were available only for the per-protocol analysis, we assigned a corresponding favourable outcome to subjects not being counted (e.g., those unable to submit sputum specimens for culture at follow-up) before analysis. For studies reporting zero events in any one group, we applied a continuity correction by adding 0.5 to all values in 2 × 2 tables containing an empty cell.

We performed meta-analyses using the statistical package R (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). When two or more clinical trials assessed the same outcome, their data were summarised using random effects inverse variance models according to the DerSimonian–Laird method.[26] If data from multiple treatment regimens were reported in any study, we attempted to pool information from all high-dose and all standard-dose regimens prior to our initial comparisons. We expressed between-study heterogeneity quantitatively through Higgins’ inconsistency index (I2) and considered it high for values >75%.[27] Wherever I2 value exceeded 50%, heterogeneity was further explored through a separate subgroup analysis if at least ten valid studies were available for the primary analysis. For subgroup analysis, we stratified studies based on the following: (a) risk of bias assessment (low, some concerns, or high), (b) number of participants receiving high-dose rifampicin (<100 or more), (c) studies using high-dose rifampicin throughout or only during intensive phase of ATT, and (d) studies including people living with HIV (PLHIV) or not. As a sensitivity analysis, we explored the robustness of our estimates for the primary outcomes by performing meta-analysis by another frequentist technique (Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman method) and through Bayesian approaches with vague priors (assuming log of RR to have a normal distribution with a mean of zero and a standard deviation of 10) and semi-informative priors (assuming log of RR to have a normal distribution with a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one, with half-normal distribution of heterogeneity parameter).[282930] We also calculated the summary estimates after excluding studies with some concerns or high risk of bias. Since different high-dose rifampicin regimens could have resulted in different outcomes, the analyses were also stratified according to the daily rifampicin dose administered to the high rifampicin dose groups (15, 20–25, and ≥30 mg/kg). Forest plots were constructed to graphically display various meta-analyses results. Two-tailed statistical significance was based on individual 95% CIs excluding the null value. We assumed a largely similar baseline risk across studies for each of the outcome measures being assessed, and calculated the number needed to treat for an additional beneficial outcome (NNTB), or for an additional harmful outcome (NNTH), as the reciprocal of corresponding summary risk difference (RD) values.[3132] We also performed a meta-regression analysis to evaluate the impact of various high rifampicin doses on our primary outcomes. We also summarised the overall certainty of evidence for our three primary outcomes using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) approach.[33]

RESULTS

We identified 5668 citations from our literature search and finally selected 12 RCTs for data synthesis [Figure 1].[343536373839404142434445] Reasons for excluding other publications after full-text review are summarised in Table S1 of online supplement. All studies selected for this review were published during the last 9 years and include datasets from the RIFATOX,[3436] HIGHRIF,[35] MAMS-TB,[37] HIRIF,[38] RIFAVIRENZ,[39] OneRIF,[40] RIFASHORT,[41] SAEFRIF,[42] TRIDORE,[43] HICON-R,[44] and Hi-DoRi-3[45] clinical trials. Overall, 3230 subjects were randomised to receive ATT in these studies, 1310 with standard-dose rifampicin regimen, and 1920 with high rifampicin doses ranging between 15 and 35 mg/kg/day.

Study characteristics and interventions

All included studies were phase 2 or 3 RCTs conducted across Africa, Asia, and South America [Table 1], with three (25.0%) operating concurrently in multiple countries.[343741] Most study participants were men, ranging between 63% and 90% across the 12 datasets [Table 1]. One study enrolled only subjects with prior history of tuberculosis treatment,[43] while another reported 7.0% such study participants.[40] Two (16.7%) studies were conducted exclusively on PLHIV,[3942] while six (50.0%) studies excluded all such individuals [Table 1].[343640414445] Tuberculosis was diagnosed using smear microscopy, culture, and/or molecular testing on respiratory specimens, except for one (9.1%) study on PLHIV that additionally included urinary lipoarabinomannan as a diagnostic criterion.[42] Sputum smear grading was reported in six (50.0%) publications, and most subjects in these studies showed 2+ smear positivity or higher [Table 2].[353638414445] Chest radiographic severity was assessed in only four (33.3%) studies [Table 2]. Cavitary disease was present in <50% subjects in three studies,[383944] and in 86.8% subjects in another study.[41] In two studies, most subjects had advanced lung disease or involvement of more than two lung zones.[4144] Two (16.7%) studies were placebo-controlled,[3538] while the remaining were open-label.

The included studies investigated several different treatment schedules and rifampicin dosages [Table 3]. All except three (25.0%) studies were considered as showing low risk of bias across all domains [Figure S1 of online supplement]. These three studies had some concerns over selection bias in the randomisation process domain as they did not report on concealment of allocation of their treatment arms.[354345]

Primary outcomes

Eight RCTs provided data for sputum culture positivity rates after 2 months (8 weeks) of ATT from 1334 and 795 subjects receiving high-dose and standard-dose rifampicin, respectively, in their mITT study population.[3435373839414244] Those on high-dose rifampicin had significantly lower culture positivity than those receiving standard-dose rifampicin (summary RR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.62–0.89), with little heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 15.5%). Summary risk ratio estimates were largely similar for frequentist and Bayesian meta-analysis [Figure 2]. Results from all RCTs favoured high-dose rifampicin, except for one study, although such differences were significant only in two recent studies [Figure 3].[4144] Summary estimates remained similar even after excluding a single study with some concern of bias [Table S2 of online supplement].[35] Based on summary RD values, 18 patients need to be treated with high-dose rifampicin to reduce one positive sputum culture [Table 4]. Based on the GRADE criteria, the overall quality of evidence was rated as high [Table 5], providing high confidence that high-dose rifampicin improved sputum conversion at 2 months of ATT in this population.

After 6 months of ATT, there was no significant difference in residual sputum culture positivity between the two groups (summary RR: 0.52, 95% CI: 0.23–1.15, I2 = 16.5%), based on data from four studies reporting on 645 and 443 patients administered high-dose and standard-dose rifampicin, respectively.[3637384244] A Bayesian approach with vague prior resulted in slighter lower summary risk ratio estimate with wider 95% credible intervals (95% CrI) [Figure 2]. Individually, all studies favoured high-dose rifampicin, although results were significant only for a single study [Figure 3].[44] We calculated that 33 patients will need to receive high-dose rifampicin-based ATT to reduce one positive sputum culture at end of treatment [Table 4]. The overall quality of evidence was rated as moderate. The evidence was downgraded for imprecision due to few events in most studies, and wide and variable CIs/CrIs [Table 5].

Seven publications reported on severe hepatotoxicity during treatment with high-dose (1160 subjects) or standard-dose rifampicin (648 subjects).[34353941424344] Most studies (five, 71.4%) used the DAIDS grading to assign severity status.[3439414244] All studies, except one, documented a higher RR in the high-dose group, although none of these differences were statistically significant [Figure 4]. Overall, there was a significantly higher incidence of severe hepatotoxicity among those receiving high-dose rifampicin (summary RR: 2.06, 95% CI: 1.15–3.68) with negligible heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 0.0%). This translates to an additional severe hepatotoxicity episode for every 45 patients receiving high-dose rifampicin-based ATT [Table 4]. However, the summary risk estimates were not significant using either the Bayesian or Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman procedures for meta-analysis [Figure 2]. Summary estimates remained similar even after excluding two studies with some concern of bias [Table S2 of online supplement].[3543] The overall quality of evidence was rated as moderate, suggesting a fair degree of confidence in the likely negative impact of high-dose rifampicin. The evidence was downgraded for imprecision due to few events in most studies, and wide and variable CIs/CrIs [Table 5].

Adverse events during treatment among study subjects receiving high-dose or standard-dose rifampicin

Secondary efficacy outcomes

Three studies each reported on residual sputum culture positivity at 1, 3, and 4 months of ATT [Figure 3]. At all three time-points, summary RR estimates were not statistically significant [Table 4]. One recent study significantly favoured high-dose rifampicin at 1 and 3 months of ATT,[44] while another significantly favoured high-dose rifampicin at 1 month of ATT [Figure 3].[35] There was also significant heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 89.2%) included for analysis at the end of 1 month treatment. Studies evaluating culture after 3 months ATT showed moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 51.6%), and the summary RD estimates suggested that 20 patients treated with high-dose rifampicin could lead to one less sputum culture positivity [Table 4]. Summary estimates remained similar even after excluding a single study with some concern of bias, with no improvement in heterogeneity [Table S2 of online supplement].[35]

Three studies described the time to sputum culture conversion at end of 2 or 6 months of ATT.[353744] However, all compared two different doses of high-dose rifampicin against the standard dose, and it was not possible to pool this information to a single high-dose category for analysis. Another study mentioned no significant difference in overall time to culture conversion among subjects receiving 30 mg/kg/day or standard-dose rifampicin, but did not provide additional details.[45]

Ten studies reported data on death during treatment with high-dose (1599 subjects) or standard-dose rifampicin (1143 subjects).[35363738404142434445] Six clinical trials studied tuberculosis recurrence during post-treatment follow-up among 901 and 717 subjects receiving high-dose and standard-dose rifampicin, respectively.[373840414445] Five publications assessed treatment failure in 616 and 535 study participants receiving high-dose and standard-dose rifampicin, respectively.[3638404245] Six studies provided information on loss to follow-up among 1040 and 743 subjects receiving high-dose and standard-dose rifampicin, respectively.[383940414245] On meta-analysis, none of these outcomes were significantly different between high-dose and standard-dose rifampicin groups [Figure 5]. The analysis on disease recurrence indicated moderate heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 52.9%); other comparisons showed low between-study heterogeneity [Table 4]. The summary risk of death during treatment was somewhat higher from two studies with some concern of bias [Table S2 of online supplement].[3543]

Miscellaneous efficacy outcomes among study subjects receiving high-dose or standard-dose rifampicin

Secondary safety outcomes

Eight studies compared incidence of any adverse event during ATT among 1201 and 768 subjects receiving high-dose and standard-dose rifampicin, respectively.[3738394142434445] However, the pooled differences were not significant (summary RR: 1.06, 95% CI: 0.94–1.20) [Figure 4]. Summary estimates remained similar even after excluding two studies with some concern of bias [Table S2 of online supplement].[4345]

Ten studies reported data on serious adverse events during treatment with high-dose (1789 subjects) or standard-dose rifampicin (1233 subjects), which was similar between the two groups [Figure 4].[34353738404142434445] There was low between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 9.0%). Summary estimates remained similar even after excluding the three studies with some concern of bias, with further improvement in heterogeneity [Table S2 of online supplement].[354345] There was no indication of potential publication bias for this analysis, as supported by a largely symmetric funnel plot (t = 1.54, P = 0.16) and non-significant result on rank correlation test (Kendall’s tau = 0.24, P = 0.38).

Five studies (total 526 and 424 participants on high-dose and standard-dose rifampicin, respectively) also provided information on drug-related serious adverse events during treatment.[3537384243] There was negligible between-study heterogeneity and no significant difference between the two rifampicin doses [Figure 4]. The summary risk difference suggested that treatment of 37 patients with high-dose rifampicin would cause an additional drug-related serious adverse event [Table 4]. Hepatotoxicity (any grade) was also similar between the two doses based on data from 735 and 573 subjects receiving high-dose and standard-dose rifampicin, respectively, in four clinical trials [Figure 4].[34384043] Summary estimates remained similar for both analyses even after excluding studies with some concern of bias [Table S2 of online supplement].[3543]

Treatment with high-dose rifampicin was associated with a significantly higher rate of ATT interruption or discontinuation among 969 and 571 study participants getting high-dose and standard-dose rifampicin, respectively, in four studies (summary RR: 1.89, 95% CI: 1.09–3.28).[37384144] This implies an additional such occurrence for every 50 patients getting ATT with high rifampicin dose [Table 4].

Effect of rifampicin dose

The studies included in this review compared multiple higher rifampicin doses with standard-dose ATT across a range of outcomes. We arbitrarily categorised data from these RCTs into three daily rifampicin dose subsets: 15 mg/kg (N = 4),[34353638] 20–25 mg/kg (N = 9),[343536373839404144] and 30 mg/kg or greater (N = 6),[374142434445] for a subgroup analysis. While most earlier studies evaluated the lower end of this dosage spectrum, more recent work has focussed on relatively higher doses [Table 3]. Results from individual studies for each dose subset are detailed in Tables S3 and S4 of online supplement.

Comparison of adverse effects with various high-dose rifampicin regimens

In general, the culture positivity rates at 1–4 months of treatment were largely similar between standard-dose and 15 mg/kg/day rifampicin [Figure S2 of online supplement]. Culture positivity at 2 months was significantly lower with both 20–25 mg/kg/day (RR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.58–0.95; NNTB 18, 95% CI: 10–72) and ≥ 30 mg/kg/day (RR: 0.64, 95% CI: 0.50–0.83; NNTB 12, 95% CI: 7–37) rifampicin doses. There appeared to a dose-dependent gradient for greater culture conversion rates at 1–3 months of ATT with progressively higher rifampicin doses [Figure S2 of online supplement]. Other efficacy outcomes (mortality, recurrence after ATT, treatment failure, and loss to follow-up) were broadly similar for all three high-dose rifampicin subsets, with no discernible gradient across dose categories [Figure S3 of online supplement].

The risk for drug-related serious adverse events, severe hepatotoxicity, and treatment interruption appeared to progressively worsen with increasing rifampicin doses [Figure S4 of online supplement]. Severe hepatoxicity was significantly higher only at ≥30 mg/kg/day rifampicin doses (RR: 2.58, 95% CI: 1.28–5.22; NNTH 31, 95% CI: 17–136). Similarly, treatment interruption was significantly greater with daily rifampicin doses ≥30 mg/kg (RR: 3.66, 95% CI: 1.56–8.57; NNTH 31, 95% CI: 19–89), but not with other high-dose rifampicin subsets.

Meta-regression analysis did not show any statistically significant change in the primary outcomes with progressively increasing rifampicin doses [Figure S5 of online supplement].

DISCUSSION

We identified 12 RCTs published since 2016 that evaluated several high-dose rifampicin-based ATT regimens given daily for pulmonary tuberculosis. Overall, administration of high-dose rifampicin resulted in significantly lower residual culture positivity after 2 months of ATT (RR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.62–0.89) but was associated with a significantly higher incidence of severe hepatotoxicity (RR: 2.06, 95% CI: 1.15–3.68) and treatment interruption/discontinuation (RR: 1.89, 95% CI: 1.09–3.28). On subgroup analysis, these outcomes were particularly evident with rifampicin doses of ≥30 mg/kg/day.

A major strength of our systematic review is the inclusion of recent good-quality RCTs that reflect the current global standards of tuberculosis care, including daily administration of ATT, use of both rifampicin and isoniazid throughout treatment duration, and additional administration of ethambutol and/or pyrazinamide during the initial intensive phase. We also included clinical trials with clearly higher daily rifampicin doses (≥15 mg/kg/day) and excluded studies with intermittent dosing regimens or those with borderline rifampicin doses (e.g. 12 mg/kg/day). This improves the relevance and applicability of our observations in the currently prevalent clinical context. In general, our subgroup analyses found that outcomes after administration of 15 mg/kg/day of rifampicin were not significantly different across any efficacy parameter (even though the number of studies is rather small). We therefore believe that further research should be concentrated on higher rifampicin dosing.

We used DerSimonian–Laird random effects modelling as our primary meta-analytic procedure. We also checked the robustness of summary risk-ratio estimates for our primary outcomes through alternative frequentist and Bayesian meta-analytic techniques. The Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman method, based on a t-distribution rather than normal distribution, may perform better when there is heterogeneity and the number of studies in the meta-analysis is small. The significant reduction in sputum culture positivity rates at the end of intensive phase of ATT was observed across all these models and offers promising clinical implications for treating pulmonary tuberculosis. Quicker bacillary clearance during ATT has an enormous epidemiological significance in reducing disease transmission. Much of this improvement was significant only for the higher rifampicin doses (≥20 mg/kg/day). Previous data also suggest that a far greater proportion of patients receiving higher rifampicin doses achieve serum drug concentrations in the therapeutic range.[1146] Although increasing rifampicin dose can drive quicker and more effective mycobacterial clearance, potentially shortening ATT duration, it may also increase the risk and severity of adverse effects. While there was some evidence (of moderate certainty) for greater incidence of severe hepatoxicity with higher rifampicin doses, this was probably important only for daily doses of 30 mg/kg or higher. Overall, the benefit from early sputum conversion tended to outweigh the potential harm from severe hepatotoxicity (NNTB 18 versus NNTH 45, Table 4). Such data can aid policymakers and clinicians once they further explore the feasibility, utility, and risk of higher rifampicin doses during implementation in programmatic settings. Perhaps it might be prudent to gather additional experience with 20–25 mg/kg/day dosing before moving on to still higher doses.

Whether the higher sputum conversion rates during intensive phase of high-dose rifampicin-based ATT will permit shortening of treatment duration from the standard 6-month regimen is still not clear.[6] Two recent studies included in our analysis administered ATT for a shorter duration to their experimental groups; both demonstrated comparable safety but lack of clear non-inferiority for their primary composite efficacy outcomes.[4145] However, in one study, culture-confirmed recurrence rates were 4.5 times higher with 4 months of high-dose rifampicin as compared to the standard regimen [Figure 4].[41] This is an area that requires further exploration.

Our findings are at a slight variance with previous systematic reviews evaluating the safety and efficacy of high-dose rifampicin for treating tuberculosis.[474849] One old systematic review reported better sputum culture conversion rates with higher rifampicin doses in pulmonary tuberculosis.[47] However, all included studies used ATT regimens with intermittent rifampicin administration without pyrazinamide. Another meta-analysis also showed a higher sputum culture conversion rate at end of intensive phase of therapy but no difference in treatment failure, severe hepatotoxicity, or other adverse effects.[48] However, this analysis also included studies offering higher rifampicin doses for only 2 weeks, and those with a marginally higher dose of 12 mg/kg/day, which currently is the upper limit of standard daily rifampicin dosing in adults.[50] A more recent meta-analysis looked at high-dose rifamycins (rifampicin, rifabutin, or rifapentine) for treating pulmonary and extrapulmonary tuberculosis.[49] It found no significant difference in bacteriological conversion rates or adverse events between pulmonary or meningeal tuberculosis patients receiving high-dose versus standard-dose rifamycin-based ATT.[49] However, a separate detailed analysis of rifampicin-based ATT for pulmonary tuberculosis was not available, and studies with ATT regimens not providing rifampicin throughout treatment were also evaluated. None of these meta-analyses systematically evaluated the impact of progressively increasing rifampicin dose on treatment efficacy or incidence of adverse effects.

Our analysis is not without limitations. The 12 included studies used very different ATT regimens, in both the standard-dose and high-dose rifampicin treatment arms. Although there was no substantial statistical heterogeneity for several outcomes studied [Table 4], the number and nature of drugs used, daily rifampicin doses, period of high-dose rifampicin administration, and total treatment duration were all highly variable [Table 3]. The various RCTs also looked at different sets of outcomes, which were sometimes measured or expressed using somewhat different tools. The number of studies in each dose subgroup was also limited. These variations preclude generalisability of any outcome to any higher rifampicin dose or regimen. The sample size of the included studies ranged from below 100[3945] to more than 600.[4041] We could not study publication bias for most outcomes due to a small number of studies. However, since all our included studies were reported in English language, we may have missed some small clinical trials published in foreign languages.

In conclusion, we found a higher rate of microbiological clearance at 2 months with the use of high-dose rifampicin regimens, with some increase in severe hepatoxicity events, particularly at rifampicin doses of 30 mg/kg/day or higher. There is need to further assess high-dose rifampicin regimens in routine programmatic settings, particularly with daily rifampicin doses of 20–25 mg/kg/day, and to explore specific patient populations that might benefit more from such treatment schedules.
