Lucien K. L. Ng, Pedro Moreno-Sanchez, Mohsen Minaei, Panagiotis Chatzigiannis, Adithya Bhat, Duc V. Le

Abstract
Zero-Knowledge Succinct Non-Interactive Argument of Knowledge (zk-SNARK) schemes have gained significant adoption in privacy-preserving applications, decentralized systems (e.g., blockchain), and verifiable computation due to their efficiency. However, the most efficient zk-SNARKs often rely on a one-time trusted setup to generate a public parameter, often known as the “Powers of Tau” (PoT) string. The leakage of the secret parameter, τ \tau , in the string would allow attackers to generate false proofs, compromising the soundness of all zk-SNARK systems built on it. Prior proposals for decentralized setup ceremonies have utilized blockchain-based smart contracts to allow any party to contribute randomness to τ \tau while also preventing censorship of contributions. For a PoT string of d d -degree generated by the randomness of m m contributors, these solutions required a total of O ⁡ ( m ​ d ) O(md) on-chain operations (i.e., in terms of both storage and cryptographic operations). These operations primarily consisted of costly group operations, particularly scalar multiplication on pairing curves, which discouraged participation and limited the impact of decentralization. In this work, we present 𝙻𝚒𝚝𝚎 ​ - ​ 𝙿𝚘𝚃 \mathtt{Lite\text{-}PoT} , which includes two key protocols designed to reduce participation costs: (i) a fraud-proof protocol to reduce the number of expensive on-chain cryptographic group operations to O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) per contributor. Our experimental results show that (with one transaction per update) our protocol enables decentralized ceremonies for PoT strings up to a 2 15 2^{15} -degree, an ≈ 16 × {\approx}16\times improvement over existing on-chain solutions; (ii) a proof aggregation technique that batches m m randomness contributions into one on-chain update with only O ⁡ ( d ) O(d) on-chain operations, independent of m m . This significantly reduces the monetary cost of on-chain updates by m m -fold via amortization.



I
Introduction

Many cryptographic protocols depend on an initial setup to generate
public parameters
. This is a critical step in preserving their security properties.
This setup also creates an unwanted
trapdoor
(also called a backdoor or toxic waste in the literature). If anyone learns this trapdoor, it would undermine the security properties of the system using those parameters.
The
powers-of-tau
(PoT) is a prominent example of such a setup. It is used in KZG polynomial commitments
[
19
]
, Verkle trees
[
21
]
, fast proofs of multi-scalar multiplication (MSM)
[
10
]
, the Danksharding proposal
[
1
]
and zk-SNARKs
[
22
,
17
]
.
In fact, the increasing use of zk-SNARKS in the blockchain domain make powers-of-tau setup an indispensable tool within a variety of blockchain applications, including zk-rollups
[
23
]
, zkBridges
[
29
]
and privacy-preserving payment systems such as ZeroCash
[
2
]
.

In PoT, the public parameters (
\mathsf{pp}
) are defined as:

\displaystyle\mathsf{pp}=(

\displaystyle\tau I_{\mathbb{G}_{1}},\tau^{2}I_{\mathbb{G}_{1}},\ldots,\tau^{n}I_{\mathbb{G}_{1}};\tau I_{\mathbb{G}_{2}},\tau^{2}I_{\mathbb{G}_{2}},\ldots,\tau^{k}I_{\mathbb{G}_{2}})\in\mathbb{G}_{1}^{n}\times\mathbb{G}_{2}^{k}

Here,
I_{\mathbb{G}_{1}}
and
I_{\mathbb{G}_{2}}
are the generators of elliptic curve groups
\mathbb{G}_{1}
and
\mathbb{G}_{2}
, respectively.
We hereby denote the value
d=n+k
as the
number of powers-of-tau
, where
the value
\tau
is the trapdoor.
Supporting a large number of powers-of-tau is crucial for enhancing the expressiveness of an application using
\mathsf{pp}
. For example, in zk-SNARKs, the number of powers-of-tau dictates how complex the statement of the proof can be.
To ensure the security of an application using
\mathsf{pp}
, it is crucial that
\tau
is generated randomly and remains unknown to anyone.
As a straightforward solution, one could rely on a trusted party to create
\mathsf{pp}
in a so-called
trusted setup
.
This party selects
\tau
at random, generates
\mathsf{pp}
, and deletes
\tau
(and the randomness used to create it) as soon as the generation of
\mathsf{pp}
is completed.
However, trusted setups are not desirable in practice for decentralized applications as finding a universally accepted trusted party can be challenging.

Given this problem, there is a clear motivation for a distributed protocol for efficiently generating powers-of-tau parameters, which allows multiple users to participate.
To realize such a protocol, a key observation about the powers-of-tau
\mathsf{pp}
is that it is
re-randomizable
.
Given
\mathsf{pp}
and without knowing
\tau
, one can produce a new string
\mathsf{pp}^{\prime}
by choosing a new random value
r

and multiplying each component of
\mathsf{pp}
by an appropriate power of

r
, more concretely:

\displaystyle\mathsf{pp}^{\prime}=(

\displaystyle r\cdot\tau I_{\mathbb{G}_{1}},\ldots,r^{n}\cdot\tau^{n}I_{\mathbb{G}_{1}};r\cdot\tau I_{\mathbb{G}_{2}},\ldots,r^{k}\cdot\tau^{k}I_{\mathbb{G}_{2}})

Here, the new trapdoor becomes
r\cdot\tau
, which is secure as long as
either

\tau
or
r
are unknown and
neither
is zero.
This re-randomizability property of powers-of-tau permits a relatively simple serial MPC protocol (also called
ceremony
): the
i
-th user in turn randomizes the public parameters with their own chosen value
r_{i}
.
As long as each participant correctly re-randomizes the
\mathsf{pp}
from the previous user and at least one of the participants destroys their value
r_{i}
(and the randomness used to draw it), the cumulatively constructed public parameter will be secure.

However, realizing such a ceremony in practice presents several challenges:

(i)
all participants should agree on the final value of
\mathsf{pp}
(
consensus
);

(ii)
each participant should only be able to re-randomize the current
\mathsf{pp}
and not simply replace it with one for which the trapdoor is known to them (
validity
);

(iii)
the final
\mathsf{pp}
should be available for all participants, as well as the history of prior versions for auditability (
data availability
);

(iv)
it should not be possible to prevent any participant from contributing to the ceremony (
censorship resistance
).

I-A

State-of-the-art and Goals

Nikolaenko et al.
[
24
]
recently proposed the
first truly decentralized and permissionless setup ceremony
for powers-of-tau.
They observed that one can leverage a smart contract in a blockchain with expressive smart contracts (like Ethereum) to coordinate the contributions of different participants of the ceremony. In a nutshell, upon receiving a contribution from a participant, the smart contract
first checks its validity and
then stores it as the last valid
\mathsf{pp}
.
In doing so, this approach seamlessly leverages existing blockchain platforms to achieve part of the desired properties.
In particular:

(i)
consensus is inherently provided by the blockchain;

(ii-iii)
the validity of each contribution as well as data availability are ensured by the smart contract itself;

(iv)
the permissionless nature of existing blockchains makes them naturally resistant to censorship.

However, the validity check required by the smart contract has a cost in terms of gas (i.e., the fee that the user needs to pay in Ethereum to execute the contract) that is linear in
d
.
More significantly, this validity check involves costly elliptic curve group operations, making it expensive for users to contribute.
For instance, even for contributing a
\mathsf{pp}
with a small number of
d
, e.g.
2^{10}
elements, a user’s contribution will require
11,500,000
gas (i.e., $315 as reported in
[
24
]
).
Note that limiting the number of powers-of-tau in
\mathsf{pp}
restricts the applications for which the generated
\mathsf{pp}
can be used.
For instance, in zk-SNARKS, the size of the statement that can be proven depends on the number of elements within powers-of-tau (i.e., fewer values of
d
can only work with small statements).

The goal of this work is to answer the question:

Can we devise a decentralized setup ceremony that permits a larger number of powers-of-tau while maintaining consensus, validity, data availability, and censorship resistance?

I-B

Our Contributions

In this work, we positively answer this question. For this, our contributions are as follows.

First, we introduce
\mathtt{Lite\text{-}PoT}
, a fully decentralized and permissionless setup ceremony.
Unlike the approach in
[
24
]
, where each
\mathsf{pp}
update is proactively checked for two conditions before acceptance –
(i)
the contributor knows the value
r
used in the contribution, and
(ii)
the proposed
\mathsf{pp}
is well-formed –
\mathtt{Lite\text{-}PoT}
’s key innovation is a fraud-proof protocol that shifts the paradigm for creating
\mathsf{pp}
. In
\mathtt{Lite\text{-}PoT}
, each proposed
\mathsf{pp}
update is only checked for condition
(i)
before acceptance, while condition
(ii)
is not proactively verified. If a user
a posteriori
identifies a
\mathsf{pp}
update as invalid under condition (ii), they can submit a succinct fraud-proof. If the contract verifies this fraud-proof, it rejects the invalid
\mathsf{pp}
update and reverts to the previous valid
\mathsf{pp}
update.

This paradigm shift allows us to design the fraud-proof protocol in a way that significantly reduces gas consumption compared to
[
24
]
. This reduction enables setup ceremonies with a larger number of powers-of-tau while maintaining key properties such as consensus, validity, data availability, and censorship resistance.

Second, to further enhance the efficiency of
\mathtt{Lite\text{-}PoT}
, we introduce an aggregation technique for combining multiple contributions while keeping the on-chain cost constant. In this setting, users do not submit their
\mathsf{pp}
and proof for fact

(i)
directly to the contract. Instead, they send their contributions to an
untrusted
operator, who collects and forwards them to the contract. A key innovation here is our proof aggregation protocol, which allows the operator to batch
m
tuples of the form (
\mathsf{pp}_{j}
,
\pi_{j}
)—where
\mathsf{pp}_{j}
is the contributed
\mathsf{pp}
and
\pi_{j}
is the proof for fact
(i)
—into a single
\mathsf{pp}^{*}
and a constant-size proof
\pi^{*}

consisting of only
3
group elements.
When the operator submits
(\mathsf{pp}^{*},\pi^{*})
to the contract, the contract verifies
\pi^{*}
and accepts
\mathsf{pp}^{*}
as the latest contribution. As with
\mathtt{Lite\text{-}PoT}
,
\mathsf{pp}^{*}
is not proactively checked for validity under fact
(ii)
, so users can still challenge its validity later using the fraud-proof designed in
\mathtt{Lite\text{-}PoT}
.
This aggregatable contribution reduces the on-chain cost from
O(m\cdot d)
– which would be required to store and process
m
individual contributions – to
O(d)
. This makes the on-chain gas cost
independent to
the number of contributions
batched off-chain.
Looking ahead, our construction employs bilinear pairing following the BLS signature scheme
[
5
]
, which is known to be vulnerable to rogue-key attacks if used in a multi-party setting. To address this vulnerability, we implement multiple countermeasures in our proof aggregation protocol and prove the security of our scheme in the algebraic group model (AGM).

Third, we implemented both the on-chain fraud-proof mechanism and the off-chain aggregation techniques in
\mathtt{Lite\text{-}PoT}
. Our performance evaluation shows that, even without aggregation,
\mathtt{Lite\text{-}PoT}
can reduce on-chain gas consumption by
16
-fold compared to the state-of-the-art solution
[
24
]
. More concretely, the efficiency boost of
\mathtt{Lite\text{-}PoT}
allows creating a
\mathsf{pp}
with a number of powers-of-tau of
2^{15}
, thereby enabling for the first time its usage in applications like ProtoDanksharding
[
8
]
, that requires
\mathsf{pp}
with a number of powers-of-tau between
2^{12}
and
2^{15}
.
As a side benefit of our off-chain collaboration paradigm,
our evaluation demonstrates an order-of-magnitude speedup because of the optimized
\mathsf{pp}
verification.
With our aggregation technique, the contributors can move most of the computation off-chain without worrying about increasing the on-chain cost.
The
\mathsf{pp}
verification after each sequential update, which is one of the bottlenecks in the ceremony,
can be shifted off-chain.
Contributors can now adopt optimization,
e.g.
, parallelization, to accelerate the off-chain computation.
It greatly reduces the verification time compared to the prior art
[
24
]
, which entirely relies on the Ethereum VM
–
an environment that lacks support for parallelism and is constrained in its ability to perform high-performance computations.

Our protocols involve three types of entities:

\mathcal{C}
,

,

Algebraic Group Model (AGM)
[
16
]
.
AGM is an idealized model of group operations for constructing security proofs. It white-boxes the group operations of a PPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
.
Whenever
\mathcal{A}
outputs a group element
Y

\mathcal{A}

Basically, it restricts
\mathcal{A}

AGM is used to prove the tight security reduction for the (original) BLS signature scheme
[
16

and will serve as an important tool to prove the security of our proof aggregation scheme.

Fraud-Proof Mechanism

Our first key observation for reducing

Unlike

If anyone successfully challenges an ill-formed
\mathsf{pp}
using a fraud-proof, the contract reverts the state to the last valid
\mathsf{pp}

Optimistic Paradigm.

In this paradigm, a user contributes to the power-of-tau generation with a tuple
(\mathsf{pp}_{t},\pi_{t})

\mathsf{pp}_{t}
is the user’s contribution and
\pi_{t}
is the zero-knowledge proof required for

The contract accepts the update

Unlike

If any user detects an invalid

III-B

Aggregateable Contribution

Despite the improvement above, the on-chain asymptotic costs remain

O(m\cdot d)
(in terms of calldata) for
m

\mathsf{pp}
with proofs individually.
We aim to reduce the on-chain cost to
O(d)
, independent of
m
.
We introduce an aggregation technique that compresses multiple updates into a single one, forming our second key contribution to this work.

Our second key observation to reduce cost of the proof-of-tau ceremony (c.f.
2
in
Figure

1
) is as follows.
Instead of having each contributor upload their own update on-chain,
we propose a (trustless) operator that aggregates contributors’ updates and proofs
off-chain
. This operator only needs to upload a pair consisting of the final
\mathsf{pp}
and an aggregated proof.
Upon submission, the contract performs
\mathtt{KnowledgeCheck}
and
\mathtt{NonDegenCheck}
but omits
\mathtt{WellformCheck}
(as described in
Section

III-A
).

In our aggregation protocol, contributors first obtain the public parameters
\mathsf{pp}
from the operator. After computing their proofs, they submit updates to the operator to incorporate into
\mathsf{pp}
. Once the operator publishes the updated
\mathsf{pp}
on-chain, contributors can verify that their contributions were properly included. Only after this verification do contributors proceed with additional operator interactions.

Aggregation Process.

In more detail, the aggregation process occurs sequentially as follows: the first contributor queries the latest state
\mathsf{pp}_{t}
from the operator. The contributor then updates
\mathsf{pp}_{t}
to
\mathsf{pp}_{t+1}
and submits the proof
\pi_{t+1}
for
\mathtt{KnowledgeCheck}
to the operator. After the operator verifies the update and the proof, the process continues with the next contributor.
Once the operator has received
m
contributions, it will execute a proof aggregation algorithm to generate a single proof from all individual proofs received from the contributors. Both the final aggregated contribution and the aggregated proof are then submitted to the contract.

Key Challenge in Proof Aggregation.
The main challenge in this process is how to design such an aggregated proof. Each contributor must prove their knowledge of a random value
r
used to update
\mathsf{pp}=(\tau\cdot I_{\mathbb{G}_{1}},\ldots)
to
\mathsf{pp}^{\prime}=(r\cdot\tau\cdot I_{\mathbb{G}_{1}},\ldots)
, ensuring that they cannot “reset”
\tau
.
The contributor can use Schnorr’s
\Sigma
-protocol
[
26
]
and BLS-style proof of possession
[
25
]
to prove its knowledge of
r
.

However, when aggregating updates, the final
\mathsf{pp}^{\prime}
becomes
(r_{1}\ldots r_{m}\cdot\tau I_{\mathbb{G}_{1}},\ldots)
, and no party – including the operator or contributors – knows the accumulated random value
r_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot r_{m}
.
Furthermore, for security reasons, no individual should learn the entire accumulated randomness.

Alternatives and Limitations.
One alternative is to use off-the-self MPC protocols or collaborative zk-SNARKS. These techniques, however, require the contributors to collaborate and stay online, executing the protocol synchronously. This introduces inefficiencies, as re-execution of the entire protocol may be needed if a party aborts.
Instead, we aim for a
fire-and-forget
aggregation, where contributors can complete their tasks in a single message to the operator, avoiding complex interactions with either the operator or other contributors.

Efficiency and Practicality.

Looking ahead, the succinctness of our aggregated proof and the efficiency of verification are crucial for practicality.
Our conservative estimation on the on-chain cost of the prior Schnorr’s
\Sigma
-based-protocol
[
24
]
is
{>}10,000
gas per contributor.
In Ethereum’s KZG Ceremony for PoT, with over
140,000
contributors,
this would lead to a gas consumption exceeding
1.4
billion, more than
46\times
the gas limit per Ethereum block.

In contrast, our scheme’s verification is significantly more efficient.
The smart contract only needs to verify
a proof consisting of a three group elements using three pairing operations, making this the first practical approach for Ethereum’s KZG ceremony for PoT.

(Intra-Operator) Rogue Key Attack.

Despite the inclusion check protocol, there remains a vulnerability known as the
Rogue Key Attack
, a well-known issue in BLS-style schemes.
In our context, a rogue key attack allows a malicious operator to deceive a contributor into believing that its randomness is included in the latest
\mathsf{pp}
.

Here is how the attack works in our context:
Suppose a contributor,
\mathsf{U}
, with public key
\mathsf{pk}
, submits their key to the operator
\mathsf{S}
, who then maliciously discards all other information from
\mathsf{U}
.

\mathsf{S}
then proceeds to work with a compromised contributor
\mathsf{U}^{\prime}
, with
(\mathsf{sk}^{\prime}
,
\mathsf{pk}^{\prime})
, and uploads the final
\mathsf{pp}
containing only
\mathsf{U}^{\prime}

The goal is to make
\mathsf{U}
believe that its contribution was included.

\mathsf{S}
publishes a list of public keys,
\mathsf{st}_{\mathsf{pk}}=(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{pk}^{\prime}-\mathsf{pk})
and claims the verification key
\mathsf{vk}=\mathsf{pk}+(\mathsf{pk}^{\prime}-\mathsf{pk})=\mathsf{pk}^{\prime}
.
Notice how
\mathsf{U}

\mathsf{pk}
is effectively “cancelled” out.
Using
\mathsf{sk}^{\prime}
,
\mathsf{S}
generates a valid signature

, which passes the verification, thus falsely convincing
\mathsf{U}
that their contribution is present simply because
\mathsf{pk}
appears in
\mathsf{st}_{\mathsf{pk}}
.

Preventing Rogue Key attack with Proof-of-Possession (PoP).

To defend against this attack, we adopt the proof-of-possession approach
[
25
]
. This requires each contributor to prove ownership of their public key by signing a hash of the key using their secret key.
Specifically, each contributor
\mathsf{U}
must provide
\mathsf{PoP}=\mathsf{sk}\cdot H_{\mathbb{G}_{2}}(\mathsf{pk})
to
\mathsf{S}
, where the operator can verify the proof using the pairing equation
e(\mathsf{pk},H_{\mathbb{G}_{2}}(\mathsf{pk}))=_{?}e(I_{\mathbb{G}_{1}},\mathsf{PoP})
.
The key insight is that an adversary generating fake public keys (like
\mathsf{pk}^{\prime}-\mathsf{pk}
) cannot sign on the corresponding hash without knowing the respective secret keys (
\mathsf{sk}^{\prime}-\mathsf{sk}
).

Our Final Inclusion Check Protocol.

We enhance the inclusion check protocol by incorporating a PoP mechanism.

\mathsf{S}
now publishes the list of key and proof pairs,
\mathsf{st}_{\mathsf{pk}}=\{(\mathsf{pk}_{i},\mathsf{PoP}_{i})\}_{i}
on its public server.
During the verification process, a contributor
\mathsf{U}_{j}
with public key
\mathsf{pk}_{j}
confirms:

(i)

;

(ii)

\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{cur}}=\sum_{i}\mathsf{pk}_{i}
;
and
(iii)
the validity of all
\mathsf{PoP}

With these checks in place, the protocol secures the inclusion of each contributor’s randomness, and we formally prove its security in

from the next verification key.

\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{cur}}=-\mathsf{vk}_{1}+\mathsf{pk}_{2}
.
Notice, the new verification key to check
\mathsf{S}_{2}
’s update becomes

\displaystyle\mathsf{vk}=\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{pre}}+\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{cur}}=\mathsf{vk}_{1}+(-\mathsf{vk}_{1}+\mathsf{pk}_{2})=\mathsf{pk}_{2}

Now,
\mathsf{S}_{2}
can use its
\mathsf{sk}_{2}
to produce a valid
\sigma
on any
\mathsf{pp}
.

Unhelpful PoP.

Unfortunately, the PoP approach, which defends against the intra-operator rogue key attack, is ineffective here.
Verifying all
\mathsf{pk}
s and
\mathsf{PoP}
s would be too costly for
\mathcal{C}
, as their total size scales linearly with the number of contributions, thus defeating the purpose of a constant-size aggregate proof.

Random Coefficients for
\mathsf{vk}
s and
\sigma
s.

To defend this attack, we resort to a “random coefficient” approach
[
15
,
11
,
4
]
,
which is a recurring technique for batched signature verification.
Instead of computing
\mathsf{vk}=\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{pre}}+\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{cur}}
,

\mathcal{C}
now samples two random coefficients
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\leftarrow_{\$}\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{*}

and computes
\mathsf{vk}=\rho_{1}\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{pre}}+\rho_{2}\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{cur}}
.
Now, the key cancellation trick cannot help a malicious
\mathsf{S}
to get rid of the prior
\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{pre}}

because with overwhelming probability

\displaystyle\mathsf{vk}

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{pre}}+\rho_{2}\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{cur}}=\rho_{1}\mathsf{vk}_{1}+\rho_{2}(-\mathsf{vk}_{1}+\mathsf{pk}_{2})

\displaystyle=(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})\mathsf{vk}_{1}+\ldots\text{ and }(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})\neq 0

Obviously,
\sigma
computed by our prior approach cannot pass
Equation

3

as
\mathsf{vk}
is no longer a simple sum of all
\mathsf{pk}
s.
To cater for the randomly combined
\mathsf{vk}
s, the operator and contributors need to instead update two
\sigma
s:
\sigma_{\mathsf{prv}}
and
\sigma_{\mathsf{cur}}
.
In our protocol,
\mathsf{S}
fetches
\sigma_{\mathsf{prv}}
from
\mathcal{C}
and then asks
\mathsf{U}
with a random value
r
, new
\mathsf{pp}
, and key pair
(\mathsf{sk},\mathsf{pk})
to update as following

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathsf{prv}}\leftarrow r\cdot\sigma_{\mathsf{prv}}\quad\sigma_{\mathsf{cur}}\leftarrow\mathsf{sk}\cdot\mathsf{pp}[Q_{1}]+r\cdot\sigma_{\mathsf{cur}}

Note that
\sigma_{\mathsf{prv}}
and
\sigma_{\mathsf{cur}}
are supposed to contain the
\mathsf{sk}
s in
\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{pre}}
and
\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{cur}}
, respectively.

Then,
\mathsf{U}
sends
\mathsf{pp},\pi=(\sigma_{\mathsf{prv}},\sigma_{\mathsf{cur}},\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{PoP})
to
\mathsf{S}
.
After gathering all the updates from contributors,

\mathsf{S}
uploads to
\mathcal{C}
the final
\mathsf{pp}
and

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathsf{S}}=\mathsf{agg}(\{\pi_{i}\}_{i\in[m]})\triangleq(\pi_{m}[\sigma_{\mathsf{prv}}],\pi_{m}[\sigma_{\mathsf{cur}}],\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{cur}}=\sum_{i\in[m]}\mathsf{pk}_{i})

Finally,

\mathcal{C}
computes
\mathsf{vk}=\rho_{1}\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{pre}}+\rho_{2}\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{cur}}
and
\sigma=\rho_{1}\sigma_{\mathsf{prv}}+\rho_{2}\sigma_{\mathsf{cur}}
and
uses
Equation

3
to verify them.
The correctness and soundness hold because the above verification is (almost) equivalent to verifying

\displaystyle e(\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{pre}},\mathsf{pp}[Q_{1}])=_{?}e(I_{\mathbb{G}_{1}},\sigma_{\mathsf{prv}})\wedge e(\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{cur}},\mathsf{pp}[Q_{1}])=_{?}e(I_{\mathbb{G}_{1}},\sigma_{\mathsf{cur}})

and for each of these equality checks, we can apply our prior conclusion about the security properties.

Progressive Key Aggregation.

The random combination on
\sigma
and
\mathsf{vk}
is
not
only for this one-time verification.
After passing the verification,
\mathcal{C}
will update in its state:

\displaystyle\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{pre}}\leftarrow\rho_{1}\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{pre}}+\rho_{2}\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{cur}};\quad\sigma_{\mathsf{prv}}\leftarrow\rho_{1}\sigma_{\mathsf{prv}}+\rho_{2}\sigma_{\mathsf{cur}}

Without these random coefficients in the update
\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{pre}}
, an inter-operator rogue key attack is still possible.

Another issue for
\mathcal{C}
is how to sample
\rho_{1,2}
.
In practice,
\mathcal{C}
is implemented on a blockchain and
\mathcal{C}
’s randomness can be biased to favor the adversary.
Here, we propose
\mathcal{C}
samples
\rho_{\{1,2\}}
via the following hashing

\displaystyle\rho_{j}=H(\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{pre}}||\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{cur}}||\sigma_{\mathsf{prv}}||\sigma_{\mathsf{cur}}||\mathsf{pp}[Q_{1}]||j)\text{ for }j\in\{1,2\}

We provide a formal security analysis and proofs
against inter-operator rogue key attacks in Theorem
3
(Lemma
2
and Lemma
5
).

We consider an adversary
\mathcal{A}
that attempts to convince a contributor
\mathsf{U}
that
\mathsf{U}
’s

is included in the final
\mathsf{pp}
,
while
\mathcal{A}
has excluded

from that
\mathsf{pp}
.
We define inclusion soundness by stating the above situation is computationally infeasible:
A PPT
\mathcal{A}
cannot correctly guess the trapdoor in the final
\mathsf{pp}
with non-negligible probability as long as
\mathsf{U}
has confirmed its randomness inclusion via

We allow
\mathcal{A}
to
compromise all other contributors except
\mathsf{U}
and all operators
and
invoke any function except
\mathtt{ContractSetup}
.
Nevertheless, we assume
\mathcal{A}
cannot alter the execution on
\mathcal{C}
, and all invalid
\mathsf{pp}

\mathsf{pp}
is decided.

VI
Discussion

Participation of Operators and Contributors.

The providers of the applications relying on a PoT string are inherently incentivized to act as operators,
as the security of the PoT is fundamental to the security of the application.
To increase the confidence of users in the security of their application,
it is in the providers’ interest to collect contributions from future users and provide inclusion proofs to them.
The providers can also set up incentive schemes,
e.g.
, lottery schemes, to attract independent operators and contributors.
Hence, further incentive analysis can be an interesting future direction and orthogonal to our work.

Handling Temporary Invalid Powers-of-Tau Parameters.

Our protocol allows potentially invalid parameters to remain on-chain for a short period of time in the absence of challenges. While honest users might use these ill-formed parameters for their application, this poses minimal risk for two reasons. First, the local verification of parameter well-formedness is computationally inexpensive, allowing users to efficiently validate parameters before use. Second, we could enhance security by adopting an approach similar to optimistic rollups, implementing a challenge period during which participants can submit fraud proofs to identify and reject invalid parameters.

Concurrency and Front-running.
When multiple operators simultaneously attempt to update
\mathsf{pp}
on
\mathcal{C}
,
only one update will be accepted, every update else becomes useless due to the nature of sequential updates.
Nevertheless, one can deploy an orthogonal mechanism to resolve the concurrency issue.
For example, operators can register for the contract to distribute the update time slot.
A security concern is sybil attacks, where the attackers create many operators to occupy all time slots for a DoS attack.
Still, many solutions are available, including:

(i)
asking the operator to put collaterals on the contract;

(ii)
selecting the operators using public randomness;

(iii)
prioritizing operators with real-world identities; and many more.
We leave further study on concurrency control as a future work.

Disincentive of Submitting Ill-formed
\mathsf{pp}
.

Attackers might submit ill-formed
\mathsf{pp}
to thwart the ceremony.
However, such attacks are economically irrational because anyone can defeat them
using
10\sim 78
-fold less financial cost, as shown in
Table

IV
.
In the face of irrational attacks, our fraud-proof cost is also cheap enough (
{\approx}
US$ 25) for the providers and future users to disprove without financial concerns.

Round-Robin-Based Setup.

Ben-Sasson
et al.

[
3
]
introduced one of the first setup ceremony protocols that can tolerate
m-1
malicious parties (thus is a “more-the-merrier” protocol) with the use of multiplicative shares.
Following a round-robin, each party sequentially introduces their private randomness into the
\mathsf{pp}
as a multiplicative share and broadcasts the updated
\mathsf{pp}
. The protocol concludes when all parties have made their contributions. As long as at least one honest participant has updated the parameters, the final output is secure, thus ensuring that the trapdoor
\tau
remains hidden from the adversary.
As the protocol is executed in a round-by-round manner, only one party can act on the
\mathsf{pp}
and broadcast the message to other parties via a broadcast channel like a blockchain.
However, this protocol limits the number of participants to a pre-selected set as its security proof requires a pre-commitment phase.

Bowe
et al.

[
7
]
avoids the pre-commitment phase by using a random beacon model, which is an abstraction of delayed randomness such as the hash value from a hash-chain or blockchain.
Skipping the pre-commitment phase enables dynamic participation for new contributors and thus perpetual powers-of-tau ceremony, where the ceremony is ever-going.
Kohlweiss
et al.

[
20
]
optimizes Boew
et al.
’s protocol by eliminating the use of a random beacon model and proves its security in the algebraic group model.

Decentralized Setup.

The above works cannot prevent censorship because these
centralized
parties can censor contribution to the
\mathsf{pp}
, allowing an adversary to have full knowledge of the randomness (
i.e.
,
r
s)
and thus learn
\tau
from
\mathsf{pp}
.
Nikolaenko
et al.

[
24
]
proposes running a decentralized ceremony on a smart contract on Ethereum. The contract stores
\mathsf{pp}
and allows anyone to join the ceremony as the blockchain is permissionless. Yet, the gas cost per update remains high, which results in a high monetary cost that discourages participation and limits the total degree of
\mathsf{pp}
.

Asynchronous PoT Setup.

Das
et al.

[
12
]
revisited the PoT setup within the context of an asynchronous network model. Their protocol demonstrates greater efficiency in terms of (off-chain) computation and communication costs compared to previous methods.
However, due to the inherent limitation of the asynchronous network setting, it can only tolerate up to
m/3
Byzantine parties.
Thus, this protocol does not have the more-the-merrier property and requires the users of the final
\mathsf{pp}
to have trust in the majority of the contributors.

Optimistic Paradigm and Fraud Proofs.

Optimistic rollups
[
14
]
is a layer-2 solution for Ethereum to scale the transaction throughput.
In a nutshell, the (level-1) blockchain accepts the off-chain transaction result without verifying it,
unless a valid fraud proof is received.
Arbitrum
[
18
]
is a concrete scheme of optimistic rollups.
It proposes a fraud-proof mechanism that a challenger can run a bisection game with the transaction submitter
to pinpoint and prove the invalid operation in the transaction.
However, it incurs expensive logarithmic rounds of on-chain interactions.

Rogue-Key Attack Mitigation.

BLS signature scheme
[
5
]
uses bilinear pairing for short signatures and aggregation of signatures. However, the rogue-key attack is a well-known issue of aggregation over BLS signatures.
Ristenpart
et al.

[
25
]
proposes proof-of-possession (POP) to protect BLS signatures against rogue-key attacks. It requires every secret key holder to register their keys by signing on the hash value of its public key to show its knowledge of its secret key.
However, it is not succinct enough for our case as we discussed in
Section

III-B3
.
Boneh
et al.

[
4
]
proposes another way to defend against rogue-key attacks by combining public keys and signatures using random coefficients. These coefficients are derived via hashing all the involved public keys.
It has two drawbacks:
(i)
a verifier (
e.g.
, the contract or a contributor) needs access to all public keys and

(ii)
it requires a pre-selected set of public keys
which restricts the participation of late-join contributors.
A generic approach to sample random coefficients is Fiat-Shamir heuristic,
where the verifier hashes the entire transcript to output random values.
In the context of a smart contract, it means
\mathcal{C}
needs to hash all the prior
\mathsf{pp}
s,
\mathsf{vk}
s, and
\sigma
s,
which is too expensive because
\mathcal{C}
’s computation cost would become
O(t^{2})
for
t
on-chain updates, while our approach’s is
O(t)
.

VIII
Conclusion

We have presented an efficient and fully decentralized PoT setup ceremony scheme
\mathtt{Lite\text{-}PoT}
,
which significantly reduces the participation cost and can support larger PoT strings for broader applications. Our key contributions include

(i)
introducing an efficient fraud-proof mechanism to reduce the on-chain cost for each update;

(ii)
proposing a proof aggregation protocol to enable batching the contribution;

(iii)
defining the security notion of aggregated contribution and rigorously proving our
\mathtt{Lite\text{-}PoT}
’s security under AGM;
and
(iv)
evaluating
\mathtt{Lite\text{-}PoT}
’s concrete cost and performance.

Lemma 2

Suppose

\displaystyle 0=\rho_{j,1}D_{j,1}+\rho_{j,1}D_{j,2}

Proof.

We expand

(\rho_{k,1}D_{k,1}+\rho_{k,2}D_{k,2})

and
\rho_{k,2}
with total degree of
2
.
Since the value of
\rho_{k,1}
and
\rho_{k,2}
are sampled after
D_{k,1}
and
D_{k,2}
, and thus the polynomial is evaluated at random points.
Then by the Schwartz-Zippel lemma,

with
1-2/p
probability.
Given that
D_{k,1}=0
,
D_{k+1,1}=D_{k+1,1}=0
with
1-2/p
probability for the same reason.
By induction, we know

with probability

\geq(1-2/p)^{k-i+1}

if
we do not apply the Fait-Shamir Heuristic.
However, in our protocol,
\rho_{k,*}
are sampled through a random oracle, so the above step takes

2(j-i+1)
queries to the random oracle.
Notice that an adversary can also change the values of
D_{k,*}
to resample
\rho_{k,*}
, and each sampling takes
2
hash queries.
Hence, the probability of violating all
D_{k,*}
being zeros is further increased to
\leq 1-(1-2/p)^{q_{H}/2}\leq q_{H}/p
.
∎

\sum_{k=i}^{j}(\prod_{\nu=i+1}^{j}\rho_{\nu,1})(\rho_{k,1}D_{k,1}+\rho_{k,2}D_{k,2})
.
