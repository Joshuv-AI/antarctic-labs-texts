A complete solution to the directed Oberwolfach problem of order 2 ( mod 4 ) 2\pmod{4} with cycles of even lengths
A. C. Burgess, P. H. Danziger, A. Lacaze-Masmonteil

Abstract
The Oberwolfach problem asks for a 2 2 -factorization of the complete graph in which each 2 2 -factor is isomorphic to a specific factor F F . Recently, this problem has been extended to directed graphs. In this case, the directed Oberwolfach problem asks for a directed 2-factorization of the complete symmetric digraph in which each directed 2 2 -factor is isomorphic to a specific directed factor F F . In this paper, we consider the directed Oberwolfach problem with directed 2-factors comprised of cycles of even lengths. Specifically, we provide a complete solution to this particular case when the order of the complete symmetric digraph is congruent to 2 modulo 4.



Keywords:
Directed graphs, decompositions, cycles, Oberwolfach problem, Mendelsohn designs.

Mathematics Subject Classifications:
05B30, 05C20, 05C51.

1
Introduction

The Oberwolfach problem was first posed by Ringel in 1967
[
24
]
, who asked the following:

In a venue with round tables of sizes
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{t}
, where
m_{1}+m_{2}+\cdots+m_{t}=n
, is it possible to find a seating plan for
n
people over
(n-1)/2
successive meals such that each person sits next to each other person exactly once?

The Oberwolfach problem can be modeled as a graph factorization problem; to describe how, we first introduce the requisite terminology.

Let
G
be a graph. We say that
G
is
decomposed
into subgraphs
H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{s}
if the
H_{i}
are pairwise edge-disjoint and their edge sets partition
E(G)
. If
H_{1}\cong H_{2}\cong\cdots\cong H_{s}\cong H
, then we speak of an
H
-decomposition of
G
. A
k
-factor
of
G
is a spanning
k
-regular subgraph of
G
; we will be particularly interested in the case
k=2
. In this case, a
2
-factor may be thought of as a collection of disjoint cycles whose lengths sum to the order of
G
. A
2
-factorization
of
G
is a decomposition of
G
into
2
-factors. For a given
2
-factor
F
of
G
, we will refer to an
F
-decomposition of
G
as an
F
-factorization of
G
. We denote the 2-regular graph consisting cycles of length
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{t}
(directed or undirected) by
[m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{t}]
. We use the standard exponential notation
[m_{1}^{\alpha_{1}},m_{2}^{\alpha_{2}},\ldots,m_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}]
to denote a
2
-regular graph containing
\alpha_{i}
cycles of length
m_{i}
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
.

Ringel’s original formulation of the Oberwolfach problem asks whether
K_{n}
admits an
F
-factorization, where
F
is a given
2
-factor of order
n
. As a
2
-factorization of
K_{n}
is only possible when
n
is odd, for even
n
it is common to instead seek a factorization of the cocktail party graph
K_{n}-I
, formed by removing the edges of a
1
-factor
I
from a complete graph of order
n
. We thus consider the Oberwolfach problem, denoted
\op(F)
, to ask whether
K_{n}
(if
n
is odd) or
K_{n}-I
(if
n
is even) admits an
F
-factorization. Various instances of
\op(F)
have been solved, notably when
F
is uniform (i.e. all cycles have the same length)
[
4
,
5
,
18
]
, bipartite (i.e. all cycles have even length)
[
8
,
17
]
, or has two components
[
27
]
. The Oberwolfach problem has been completely solved for orders
n\leq 100

[
14
,
22
,
25
]
; in this range, there are exactly four
2
-factors for which no solution exists, specifically
[3^{2}]
,
[3^{4}]
,
[4,5]
, and
[3^{2},5]
.

Bryant and Scharaschkin
[
9
]
were the first to give a complete solution to the Oberwolfach problem for infinitely many orders, namely an infinite family of primes congruent to
1
(mod
16
) as well as orders of the form
2p
where
p\equiv 5
(mod
24
) is prime; this result was extended to orders of the form
2p
where
p
is a prime congruent to
5
(mod
8
) in
[
3
]
. It has recently been shown by non-constructive methods that a solution to
\op(F)
exists for sufficiently large orders
[
16
]
. However, despite the fact that this result stipulates that the set of unsolved cases is finite, no bound on the size of this set is known; thus the Oberwolfach problem remains wide open in general. For further background on the Oberwolfach and related problems, we refer the reader to the survey
[
10
]
.

In this paper, we will consider a variant of the Oberwolfach problem known as the
directed Oberwolfach problem
with tables of length
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{t}
, denoted
\op^{*}([m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{t}])
. Introduced in
[
12
]
, this problem considers a similar scenario as the Oberwolfach problem with the modified constraint that each guest be seated to the right of every other guest precisely once. The directed Oberwolfach problem is part of a recent surge of interest in directed variants of well-known cycle decomposition problems. For example, researchers have began to undertake the study of the question of existence of solutions to the directed Hamilton-Waterloo problem
[
28
,
29
]
as well as the directed Oberwolfach problem for the complete symmetric equipartite directed graph
[
15
]
.

To formulate the directed Oberwolfach problem in graph-theoretic terms, we will require the following definition. A
directed 2-factor of directed graph
G
with directed cycles of length
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{t}
, denoted
F=[m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{t}]
, is a spanning subdigraph of
G
comprised of the vertex-disjoint union of directed cycles of lengths
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{t}
. In
[
12
]
, Burgess and Šajna posed the directed Oberwolfach problem as the question of the existence of a decomposition of the complete symmetric directed graph
K_{n}^{*}
into copies of
F
. Although Burgess and Šajna were the first to formulate the directed Oberwolfach problem as a cycle decomposition problem, they remarked that the directed Oberwolfach problem could also be viewed as a design theoretic problem by constructing a design known as a resolvable Mendelsohn design
[
13
]
. This particular approach is one taken by the authors of
[
6
,
7
,
30
]
.

When
n
is odd, any solution to
\op([m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}])
yields a solution of
\op^{*}([m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}])
by simply directing each 2-factor in the 2-factorization of
K_{n}
in both orientations. Therefore, emphasis is placed on the case
n
is even for
\op^{*}([m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{t}])
. The directed Oberwolfach problem has been completely resolved for tables of uniform lengths. The complete solution to this particular case is given in Theorem
1.1
below.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
1
,
2
,
6
,
7
,
11
,
12
,
21
,
26
]
)
.

Let
m\geq 2
be an integer. There exists a solution to
\op^{*}([m^{\alpha}])
if and only if
(m,\alpha)\notin\{(4,1),(6,1),(3,2)\}
.

Zhang and Du
[
30
]
provide a complete solution to
\op^{*}
when the seating arrangements are comprised of
\alpha
tables of length three and one table of length four or five.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
30
]
)
.

Let
\ell\in\{4,5\}
. For every
\alpha\geq 1
, there exists a solution to
\op^{*}([3^{\alpha},\ell])
.

The directed Oberwolfach problem was also completely resolved for the case in which we have two tables, as given in Theorem
1.3
.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
19
,
20
]
)
.

Let
2\leq m_{1}\leq m_{2}
be integers. There exists a solution to
\op^{*}([m_{1},m_{2}])
if and only if
[m_{1},m_{2}]\neq[3,3]
.

Kadri and Šajna
[
20
]
derived a recursive method that gave rise to several infinite families of solutions to the directed Oberwolfach problem. In addition, their results in conjunction with those of Quirion
[
23
]
, completely settle the directed Oberwolfach problem for all
n\leqslant 17
.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
20
,
23
]
)
.

Let
m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}
be integers such that each
m_{i}\geq 2
and
m_{1}+\cdots+m_{t}\leq 17
. There exists a solution to
\op^{*}([m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}])
if and only if
[m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}]\notin\{[4],[6],[3,3]\}
.

In this paper, we will consider the case of
\op^{*}([m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{t}])
in which all directed cycles have even length, and solve this case when
n\equiv 2\pmod{4}
. In particular, our main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
F
be a bipartite directed
2
-factor of order
n\equiv 2\pmod{4}
. There exists a solution to
\op^{*}(F)
if and only if
F\neq[6]
.

We structure this paper as follows. In Section
2
, we provide the necessary notation and definitions required to describe our solutions. Then, in Section
3
, we show that it suffices to construct the desired decompositions for two particular classes of directed graphs that require four and nine directed
2
-factors, respectively, in their decomposition. In the case of the first class of directed graphs, a complete solution is also given in Section
3
. The purpose of Section
4
is to complete the proof of Theorem
1.5
by further reducing the question of existence of a 2-factorization of the second class of directed graphs to the existence of a particular set of directed paths and cycles, which we then give.

Notation and preliminaries

First, we introduce pertinent terminology and notation. Given a directed graph (digraph for short), we denote its vertex set as
V(D)
and its arc set by
A(D)
. An arc from
x
to

is written as

cycles of length
m_{i}
for each

. We call a digraph of the form

2
-regular digraph
. Note that, in a
2
-regular digraph, every vertex either has in- and out-degree
1
or in- and out-degree 0. While we use the same notation
[m_{1}^{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,m_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}]
in the undirected and directed cases, it should be clear from context whether we are referring to a directed or undirected 2-regular graph. Lastly, a
2
-factor
of a graph

directed

-factor
of digraph

The directed cycle

contains arcs

the directed cycle obtained by traversing the arcs of

in the opposite direction, i.e.

is the

of

of

of a dipath

. Given paths

, we define the

of

as the walk

. Note that

Setup and approach

Our approach is inspired by the solution of the original undirected Oberwolfach problem for bipartite factors given in
[
8
,
17
]
. A similar approach was also recently successfully employed in
[
19
]
to complete the solution of the two-table directed Oberwolfach problem.

Note that
\op^{*}(F)
has been completely solved when
n<18

[
20
,
23
]
, so we may assume that
n\geq 17
, i.e.
m\geq 9
. In fact, our methods apply to all
m\geq 7
. The basic idea to solve
\op^{*}(F)
in the case that
F
is a directed
2
-factor of order
n=2m
, where
2m\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, is as follows. First, we apply the following theorem to decompose
K_{n}^{*}
into spanning subdigraphs isomorphic to
H_{n}^{*}
and
W_{n}^{*}
. As a consequence of the proofs of Lemmas 10 and 13 of
[
19
]
, Theorem
3.1
reduces the problem to finding
F
-factorizations of
H_{n}^{*}
and
W_{n}^{*}
, which is the focus of this paper.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
m\geq 7
be and odd integer. There exists a decomposition of
K_{2m}^{*}
into one copy of
W_{2m}^{*}
and
\frac{m-5}{2}
copies of
H_{2m}^{*}
.

To factor
H_{2m}^{*}
, we generalize a result of Häggkvist
[
17
]
to the directed case. In particular, Häggkvist proved the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
17
]
)
.

Let
m>1
be an integer. For every bipartite
2
-factor
F
of order
2m
, there exists an
F
-factorization of
H_{2m}
.

Lemma
3.2
immediately shows that, for any bipartite directed
2
-factor

not containing a directed
2
-cycles, the digraph
H_{2m}^{*}
admits an
F
-factorization. Namely, one simply directs the cycles in an
[m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}]
-factorization of
H_{2m}
in both direction. The following lemma settles the case in which
F
contains directed
2
-cycles.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
F=[m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{t}]
be a directed 2-factor such that
m_{1}\leq m_{2}\leq\ldots\leq m_{t}
, and suppose that

has

F
-factorization of

.

Proof.

From the hypothesis, it follows that
m_{i}=2
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
for some
s\geq 1
.

If
s\geq 2
, by Lemma
3.2
,
H_{2m}
admits a
[{2s},{m_{t-s}},{m_{t-s+1}},\ldots,{m_{t}}]
-factorization,
\cal H
. For each 2-factor in
\cal H
, we direct the cycles of size
m_{i}
,
i\in\{t-s,\ldots,t\}
, each way, giving two directed partial factors
\{F_{1},F_{2}\}
. Now, take a 1-factorization,
\{I_{1},I_{2}\}
, of the cycle of length
2s
. For each edge in

,

, we replace it with the corresponding directed 2-cycle. This gives two sets of 2-cycles

and

that are both vertex-disjoint from

and
F_{2}
. Now, for each
i\in\{1,2\}

is a factor of
H_{2m}^{*}
with the prescribed cycle structure. Repeating this for each 2-factor in
\cal H

.

\displaystyle C_{m_{i}}^{0}=(x_{a},x_{a+1},y_{a+2},x_{a+3},y_{a+4},x_{a+5},\ldots,y_{a+k_{i}-1},y_{a+k_{i}},x_{a+k_{i}-1},y_{a+k_{i}-2},x_{a+k_{i}-3},\ldots,y_{a+1});

\displaystyle C_{m_{i}}^{2}=(y_{a},x_{a+1},x_{a+2},x_{a+3},x_{a+4},\ldots,x_{a+k_{i}},y_{a+k_{i}-1},y_{a+k_{i}-2},y_{a+k_{i}-3},\ldots,y_{a+1});

\displaystyle C_{m_{i}}^{1}=\overleftarrow{C_{m_{i}}^{0}};\;C_{m_{i}}^{3}=\overleftarrow{C_{m_{i}}^{2}}.

Finally, for each

we form the following subdigraph

It is tedious but straightforward to verify that directed cycles in

span the vertex set meaning that

is an
F
-factor. Furthermore,

is the desired

-factorization. ∎

-factorizations of

-factorization of

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{L}}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{9})
.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{9})
and
\mathcal{R}=(R_{1},\ldots,R_{9})
be complementary left and right caps, respectively, such that
\mathcal{L}
is an
(\mathscr{E},\ell)
-left cap and
\mathcal{R}
is an
(\mathscr{E},r,t,M)
-right cap, where
M
is a multiset
[m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}]
of directed cycle lengths. For
i\in\{1,\ldots,9\}
, let
P_{i}
be the directed path in
R_{i}
. If
\textrm{len}(P_{i})+\textrm{len}(R_{i})=m_{0}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,9\}
, then there exists an admissible
[m_{0},m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}]
-decomposition of
J^{*}_{2(\ell+r)}
with external pattern
\mathscr{E}
.

Proof.

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,9\}
, let
D_{i}
be the digraph formed by the taking the union of
L_{i}
and
\sigma^{\ell}(R_{i})
. We claim that the digraphs
D_{1},\ldots,D_{9}
form the required decomposition. We will first verify that, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,9\}
,
D_{i}\cong[m_{0},m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}]
. To do this, it suffices to check that
L_{i}+\sigma^{\ell}(P_{i})
is a directed cycle of length
m_{0}
that is vertex-disjoint from any directed cycle in
\sigma^{\ell}(R_{i})
.

Let
(v_{1},v_{2},S_{i})
be the internal pattern of
L_{i}
and
R_{i}
where
S_{i}
is the set of vertices in
\{x_{0},x_{1},y_{0},y_{1}\}
that are also internal vertices of
\sigma^{-\ell}(L_{i})
. By item 2 of Definition
4.11
,
S_{i}
is also the set of vertices in
\{x_{0},x_{1},y_{0},y_{1}\}
that do not appear in
R_{i}
. Therefore, the internal vertices of
L_{i}
are not contained in
\sigma^{\ell}(R_{i})
. Since
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{L}}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{R}}
, items 1 and 2 of Definition
4.11
imply that
\sigma^{\ell}(v_{1})=s(L_{i})=t(\sigma^{\ell}(P_{i}))
and
\sigma^{\ell}(v_{2})=t(L_{i})=s(\sigma^{\ell}(P_{i}))
. As a result, the concatenation
L_{i}+\sigma^{\ell}(P_{i})
is indeed possible. In addition, since the internal vertices of
L_{i}
do not appear in
\sigma^{\ell}(P_{i})
, the digraph
L_{i}+\sigma^{\ell}(P_{i})
is a directed cycle, which has length
m_{0}
because
\textrm{len}(P_{i})+\textrm{len}(R_{i})=m_{0}
. Lastly, since the internal vertices of
L_{i}
do not appear in digraphs of
\sigma^{\ell}(R_{i})
, this directed cycle is also vertex-disjoint from the remaining directed cycles of
R_{i}
and thus,
D_{i}\cong[m_{0},m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}]
, as claimed.

We now show that
\{D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{9}\}
is a decomposition of
J_{2(\ell+r)}^{*}
. It is easy to see that the union of their arcs contains all arcs of
J_{2(\ell+r)}^{*}
. Recall that digraphs in
\cal L
are pairwise arc-disjoint, as are the digraphs in
(\sigma^{\ell}(R_{1}),\sigma^{\ell}(R_{2}),\ldots,\sigma^{\ell}(R_{9}))
, by Definition
4.10
. Moreover, by item 2 of Definition
4.10
, the union of the arc sets of
\sigma^{\ell}(R_{1}),\ldots,\sigma^{\ell}(R_{9})
is
A(\sigma^{\ell}(J_{2r}^{*}))\cup\{x_{\ell}y_{\ell}\}
. Therefore, if
L_{i}
and
\sigma^{\ell}(R_{j})
(where
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,9\}
) are not arc-disjoint, then
L_{i}
contains the arc
x_{\ell}y_{\ell}
, thereby contradicting the definition of left cap.

Lastly, we show that the decomposition
\{D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{9}\}
is admissible with external pattern
\mathscr{E}
. Since
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
are complementary, then for each
w\in\{x_{0},x_{1},y_{0},y_{1}\}
, items 1(b) and 2(b) of Definition
4.10
jointly imply that
w
occurs in
L_{i}
if and only if
\sigma^{r}(w)
does not occur in
R_{i}
. Hence, for each
w\in\{x_{0},x_{1},y_{0},y_{1}\}
, exactly one of
w
and
\sigma^{\ell+r}(w)
occurs in
D_{i}
. Thus, the decomposition is admissible; it is easy to see that its external pattern is
\mathscr{E}
.
∎

We will construct left and right caps in order to create cycles of length
m\in\{8,10,12,14\}
in

, as well as some other
2
-regular digraphs involving
2
- and
4
-cycles. In order to extend our constructions to directed cycles of arbitrary even length, we now introduce the concept of a

. Informally, a centre piece is a pair of directed paths that can be placed between left and right caps to create longer cycles. These dipaths must satisfy a particular set of conditions, given in Definition
4.15
.

Definition 4.15
.

Let
c>0
be an integer. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,9\}
, let
C_{i}=(Q_{i},U_{i})
denote the digraph on vertex set
\{x_{i},y_{i}\mid i=0,\ldots,c+1\}
comprised of two vertex-disjoint directed paths
Q_{i}
and
U_{i}
. The list
(C_{1},\ldots,C_{9})
is called a
centre piece of length
c
if:

1.

The union of the arc sets of the
C_{i}
is

\Big(A(J_{2c}^{*})\cup\{x_{0}y_{0}\}\Big)\setminus\{y_{c}x_{c}\}
, and

2.

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,9\}
:

(a)

\textrm{len}(Q_{i})+\textrm{len}(U_{i})=2c
.

(b)

s(Q_{i})\in\{x_{0},y_{0},x_{1},y_{1}\}
,
t(Q_{i})=s(\sigma^{c}(Q_{i}))
,
s(U_{i})\in\{x_{c},y_{c},x_{c+1},y_{c+1}\}
, and
t(U_{i})=s(\sigma^{-c}(U_{i}))
.

(c)

For each vertex
u\in\{x_{0},x_{1},y_{0},y_{1}\}\setminus\{s(Q_{i}),t(U_{i})\}
, exactly one of
u
and

\sigma^{c}(u)
is in
Q_{i}
or
U_{i}
.

(d)

Each vertex in
\{x_{2},y_{2},x_{3},y_{3},\ldots,x_{c-1},y_{c-1}\}
is in precisely one of
Q_{i}
or
U_{i}
.

We note that Conditions
2b
and
2c
of Definition
4.15
imply that copies of a component of a centre piece can be concatenated to form a longer centre piece. That is, given a centre piece
(C_{1},\ldots,C_{9})
of length
c
, where
C_{i}=(Q_{i},U_{i})
, the list
(C_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,C_{9}^{\prime})
, where
C_{i}^{\prime}=(Q_{i}+\sigma^{c}(Q_{i}),\sigma^{c}(U_{i})+U_{i})
, is a centre piece of length
2c
. In the following, we define centre pieces of length

, which can be concatenated to obtain centre pieces of all lengths congruent to
0\pmod{4}
. We formalize this idea in Lemma
4.16
.

Lemma 4.16
.

If there exists a centre piece of length
4
, then there exists a centre piece of length
4k
for all
k\geqslant 1
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{9})
be a centre piece of length 4 such that
C_{i}=(Q_{i},U_{i})
. For each
q\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
, let
Q^{q}_{i}=\sigma^{4q}(Q_{i})
and
U^{q}_{i}=\sigma^{4q}(U_{i})
, and let
C^{\prime}_{i}=(Q_{i}^{\prime},U_{i}^{\prime})
, where

Q_{i}^{\prime}=Q_{i}^{0}+Q_{i}^{1}+\cdots+Q_{i}^{k-1}\ \textrm{and}\ U_{i}^{\prime}=U_{i}^{k}+U_{i}^{k-1}+\cdots+U_{i}^{0}.

We claim that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=(C^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,C^{\prime}_{9})
is a centre piece of length
4k
. To that end, we verify that all conditions of Definition
4.15
are met.

First, we must show that
Q^{\prime}_{i}
and
U_{i}^{\prime}
are vertex-disjoint directed paths. By virtue of Condition
2b
of Definition
4.15
, we have that
t(Q_{i}^{q})=s(Q_{i}^{q+1})
for all
q\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
, and
t(U_{i}^{q})=s(U_{i}^{q-1})
for all
q\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Therefore the above concatenation is in fact possible. It remains to be shown that
Q_{i}^{\prime}
and
U_{i}^{\prime}
are vertex-disjoint. Note that, if
q_{1}-q_{2}\geqslant 2
, then
Q_{i}^{q_{1}}
and
Q_{i}^{q_{2}}
are clearly vertex-disjoint. In addition, Conditions
2b
and
2d
of Definition
4.15
jointly imply that the only common vertices of
Q_{i}^{q}
and
Q_{i}^{q+1}
are their terminal and source, respectively. By virtue of conditions
2b
and
2c
,
Q^{\prime}_{i}
and
U^{\prime}_{i}
are vertex-disjoint directed paths, and by Condition
2a
on the constituent paths in the concatenation, the sum of their lengths is
8k
. This means that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
satisfies Condition
2a
.

Observe that, by Condition
2b
,
s(\sigma^{4}(Q_{i}))=t(Q_{i})
. Since
Q_{i}^{0}=Q_{i}
,
s(\sigma^{4k}(Q_{i}^{0}))=t(Q_{i}^{4k})
. Therefore,
s(\sigma^{4k}(Q^{\prime}_{i}))=t(Q_{i}^{\prime})
A similar reasoning applies to
U_{i}^{\prime}
and thus, Condition
2b
holds for
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. Again, by a similar reasoning, it can be shown that Condition
2c
also holds for
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.

As for Condition
2d
, consider
x_{r+4\ell}\in\{x_{2},\ldots,x_{4k-1}\}
, where
0\leqslant r<4
and
0\leqslant\ell<k
(a similar reasoning applies to
y_{4+4\ell}
). Because
Q_{i}^{\prime}
and
U_{i}^{\prime}
are vertex-disjoint, it suffices to show that
x_{r+4\ell}
must be in at least one of
Q_{i}^{\prime}
or
U_{i}^{\prime}
. Since
\sigma^{-4\ell}(x_{r+4\ell})=x_{r}
is in exactly one of
Q_{i}
or
U_{i}
,
\cal C^{\prime}
satisfies Condition
2d
.

In order to show that Condition
1
holds for
\cal C^{\prime}
, it suffices to show that directed paths in
\{U^{\prime}_{i},U^{\prime}_{j},Q^{\prime}_{i},Q^{\prime}_{j}\}
are pairwise arc-disjoint when
i\neq j
.
Clearly,
U_{i}^{\prime}
and
Q^{\prime}_{i}
are arc-disjoint; likewise for
Q^{\prime}_{j}
and
U_{j}^{\prime}
. Suppose that there exists an arc
a\in A(U^{\prime}_{i})\cap A(U^{\prime}_{j})
. Without loss of generality, suppose that
a=(x_{r+4\ell},x_{s+4\ell})
where
0\leqslant\ell\leqslant k
and
0\leqslant r,s<4
.

Consider the arc
(\sigma^{-4\ell}(x_{r+4\ell}),\sigma^{-4\ell}(x_{s+4\ell}))=(x_{r},x_{s})
. By definition, this arc lies in
A(U_{i})
and
A(U_{j})
, thereby contradicting the fact that these dipaths are arc-disjoint. A similar reasoning applies to the remaining pairs of dipaths, namely
Q_{i}^{\prime}
and
Q_{j}^{\prime}
,
U_{i}^{\prime}
and
Q_{j}^{\prime}
, and
U_{j}^{\prime}
and
Q_{i}^{\prime}
. ∎

Next, we give precise conditions that a centre piece of length

when paired with a complementary pair of left and right caps.

Definition 4.17
.

Given a centre piece
\mathcal{C}=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{9})
, the

of
C_{i}=(Q_{i},U_{i})
is the list
I^{\prime\prime}_{i}=(t(U_{i}),s(Q_{i}),\bar{S})
, where
\bar{S}
is the set of vertices in
\{x_{0},x_{1},y_{0},y_{1}\}
that are not in
V(Q_{i})\cup V(U_{i})
. The
internal pattern of
\mathcal{C}
is the list
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}=(I_{1}^{\prime\prime},\ldots,I_{9}^{\prime\prime})
.

We say that a left (resp. right) cap
\mathcal{L}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}
) and a centre piece

are

if they have the same internal patterns, that is,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{L}}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}
(resp.
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}

\mathcal{L}
and

are complementary left and right caps, and a centre piece

Lemma 4.18
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{9})
and
\mathcal{R}=(R_{1},\ldots,R_{9})

\mathcal{C}

\mathscr{E}
for each integer

.

Proof.

The case

4.14

. By Lemma

, there is a centre piece
\mathcal{C}_{4k}=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{9})

.

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,9\}
, we have

. Furthermore, recall that

is

.

Consider the subdigraph

. Furthermore, since

,
\sigma^{\ell}(Q_{i})
and
\sigma^{\ell}(U_{i})

, the dipaths

. That

.

\mathscr{E}
. To verify that
\mathcal{F}

, where

. To see that they are arc-disjoint, first note that

and
L_{j}

and

, and
\sigma^{\ell+4k}(R_{i})

\sigma^{\ell+4k}(R_{j})
. Further, it is clear that since

,

and

. Since the arc set of

, which is

. ∎

As a consequence of Lemma

, in order to construct an admissible decomposition of

Recall that each of our caps will have external pattern

\mathcal{X}=(\{y_{1}\},\{x_{0},x_{1},y_{1}\},\{x_{1},y_{0},y_{1}\},\{x_{0},x_{1},y_{0},y_{1}\},\{y_{1}\},\{x_{0},x_{1},y_{0}\},\{x_{1},y_{1}\},\\
\{x_{1}\},\{x_{0},x_{1},y_{0},y_{1}\}).

Lemma 4.19
.

Now that we have established the existence of admissible
F
-decompositions of
J_{2m}^{*}
for cases where
F
has one component or a small number of components with small cycle lengths, we may employ these results with Lemma
4.7
to produce decompositions for more general
2
-regular graphs
F
. This approach will then allow us to construct directed
2
-factorizations of
W_{2m}^{*}
.

Theorem 4.21
.

Let
m\geq 4
and
F\neq[2^{m}]
be a bipartite
2
-regular of order
2m
. There exists an admissible
F
-decomposition of
J_{2m}^{*}
.

Proof.

Write
F=[m_{1}^{\alpha_{1}},m_{2}^{\alpha_{2}},\ldots,m_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}]
where
2\leq m_{1}\leq m_{2}\leq\cdots\leq m_{t}
and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{t}
are positive integers. We consider three cases depending on the value of
m_{1}
.

CASE 1:
m_{1}\geq 6
. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
, there exists an admissible
[m_{i}]
-decomposition of
J_{2m_{i}}^{*}
with external pattern
\mathcal{X}
, by Lemma
4.20
if
m_{i}=6
and by Lemma
4.19
otherwise. Thus, by Lemma
4.7
, there exists an admissible
F
-decomposition of
J_{2m}^{*}
with external pattern
\mathcal{X}
.

CASE 2:
m_{1}=4
. We consider cases depending on the value of
\alpha_{1}
.

Sub-case 2.1:

\alpha_{1}\geq 2
. Write
\alpha_{1}=2\beta+3\gamma
, where
\beta
and
\gamma
are non-negative integers. By Lemma
4.20
, there exist an admissible
[4^{2}]
-decomposition of
J_{8}^{*}
and an admissible
[4^{3}]
-decompositions of
J_{12}^{*}
, each with external pattern
\mathcal{X}
. The existence of an admissible
[4^{\alpha_{1}}]
-decomposition of
J_{4\alpha_{1}}^{*}
follows by Lemma
4.7
. Using Case 1, there exists an admissible
[m_{2}^{\alpha_{2}},\ldots,m_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}]
-decomposition of
J_{2m-4\alpha_{1}}^{*}
. These two latter decompositions are compatible, each having external pattern
\mathcal{X}
, and hence, by Lemma
4.7
, there is an admissible
[m_{1}^{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,m_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}]
-decomposition of
J_{2m}^{*}
with external pattern
\mathcal{X}
.

Sub-case 2.2:

\alpha_{1}=1
. Note that there is an admissible
[4,m_{2}]
-decomposition of
J_{4+m_{2}}^{*}
by Lemma
4.19
in the case
m_{2}>6
and Lemma
4.20
if
m_{2}=6
. By Case 1, there is an admissible
[m_{2}^{\alpha_{2}-1},m_{3}^{\alpha_{3}},\ldots,m_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}]
-decomposition of
J_{2m-(4+m_{2})}^{*}
. As both of these decompositions have external pattern
\mathcal{X}
, Lemma
4.7
implies the existence of an admissible
[m_{1}^{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,m_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}]
-decomposition of
J_{2m}^{*}
with external pattern
\mathcal{X}
.

CASE 3:
m_{1}=2
. Since
\mathcal{F}\neq[2^{m}]
, we have that
t\geq 2
. We proceed depending on the congruence class of
\alpha_{1}
modulo
3
.

Sub-case 3.1:

\alpha_{1}\equiv 0\pmod{3}
. The result follows by Lemma
4.7
, using the
[2^{3}]
-decomposition from Lemma
4.20
and an admissible
[m_{2}^{\alpha_{2}},\ldots,m_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}]
-decomposition of
J_{2m-2\alpha_{1}}^{*}
from Case 1 or 2.

Sub-case 3.2:

\alpha_{1}\equiv 1\pmod{3}
. We again use Lemma
4.7
, this time with an admissible
[2,m_{2}]
-decomposition of
J_{2+m_{2}}^{*}
from Lemma
4.19
(if
m_{2}\geq 8
) or Lemma
4.20
(if
m_{2}\in\{4,6\}
), as well as an admissible
[2^{3}]
-decomposition from Lemma
4.20
(if
\alpha_{1}>1
) and an
[m_{2}^{\alpha_{2}-1},m_{3}^{\alpha_{3}},\cdots m_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}]
-decomposition of
J_{2m-2\alpha_{1}-m_{2}}^{*}
from Case 1 or 2.

Sub-case 3.3:

\alpha_{1}\equiv 2\pmod{3}
. This case is similar to Subcase 3.2, except that we use a
[2^{2},m_{2}]
-decomposition of
J_{4+m_{2}}^{*}
from Lemma
4.19
or
4.20
in place of a
[2,m_{2}]
-decomposition. ∎

Corollary 4.22
.

Let
m\geq 4
be an integer and
F\neq[2^{m}]
is a bipartite directed
2
-factor of order
2m
. There exists an
F
-factorization of
W_{2m}^{*}
.

Proof.

The result follows directly from Theorem
4.21
by applying Lemma
4.3
.
∎

We now prove the main result of this paper.

Theorem
1.5
.

Let
F
be a bipartite directed
2
-factor of order
n\equiv 2\pmod{4}
. There exists a solution to
\op^{*}(F)
if and only if
F\notin\{[4],[6]\}
.

Proof.

If
F
is uniform, then the result follows by Theorem
1.1
; in particular, this means that we may assume that
F\neq[2^{n/2}]
. Note that
[
20
, Theorem 9.1]
solves the problem for orders
n<14
. For
n\geq 14
, the result follows from Theorem
3.5
and Corollary
4.22
.
∎

Theorem
1.5
completely resolves the case of the directed Oberwolfach problem in which all directed cycles are of even length and the number of vertices is congruent to
2
modulo
4
.
The natural next step is to consider the case where the number of vertices is congruent to
0
modulo
4
. We have embarked on this study; although similar methods may apply, this case appears to be more complicated.
