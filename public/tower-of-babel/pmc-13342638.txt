Comparative effects of diazepam administration and chronic sleep deprivation on cognitive function and biochemical markers in rats
Authors: Ghasem Forutan, Yaghoob Farbood, Alireza Sarkaki, Seyed Esmaeil Khoshnam, Sadegh Moradi Vastegani, Zohreh Lamoochi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSleep deprivation (SD) is a prevalent issue in modern society, with profound implications for cognitive function and overall health. Although benzodiazepines are frequently employed to manage sleep disorders, their use is complicated by adverse side effects, notably cognitive impairment. This study investigates the comparative effects of chronic sleep deprivation and diazepam administration on memory, learning, and biochemical markers in male Wistar rats. Eighteen rats were randomly assigned to three groups: a sham group receiving DMSO (diazepam solvent) as a vehicle control, a Diaz group administered diazepam, and a chronic sleep restriction (CSR) group subjected to continuous sleep deprivation and DMSO, each for a duration of three weeks. Cognitive performance was assessed using established behavioral paradigms, specifically the passive avoidance memory test and the novel object recognition test, which evaluate aversive and recognition memory, respectively. Biochemical analyses measured key markers, including total antioxidant capacity, reactive oxygen species (ROS), TNF-α, and BDNF levels in brain tissue, to elucidate underlying molecular mechanisms. Results revealed that both chronic SD and diazepam administration negatively affect learning and memory. However, impairments caused by SD were consistently more severe, affecting both behavioral and molecular parameters. These findings highlight the critical role of adequate sleep in sustaining cognitive health, indicating that while diazepam poses risks to cognitive function, chronic SD constitutes a more substantial threat to brain integrity and performance. This study underscores the urgent need for effective sleep restoration strategies and cautions against the risks of prolonged sleep loss and the unsupervised use of sedative medications.Keywords: Chronic Sleep Restriction, Diazepam, Learning and Memory, TNF-α, BDNFSubject terms: Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Despite significant advances in living standards, modern societies face growing psychological and occupational pressures that disrupt sleep quality and quantity. Insufficient sleep, particularly due to work-related stress and lifestyle demands, has become a global health concern. Studies indicate that sleep deprivation affects approximately 10 to 30% of the population, with some communities reporting rates as high as 50 to 60%1. To cope with these sleep disturbances, many individuals turn to pharmacological interventions such as benzodiazepines. Research reveals that approximately 12.5% of adults in the United States use these medications2. Evidence indicates that sleep deprivation impairs cognitive functions, especially learning and memory3, although the underlying pathological mechanisms remain insufficiently understood4. Earlier studies have demonstrated that sufficient sleep bolsters memory, whereas sleep deficiency leads to memory deficits5. Memory is generally defined as the capacity to store and retrieve information through neural plasticity6, a dynamic process influenced by factors such as medications7, sleep deprivation8 and stress9, all of which can disrupt memory retention and recall.

Studies suggest that both rapid eye movement (REM) sleep and slow-wave sleep (non-REM sleep, or SWS) contribute to memory consolidation10. This process strengthens new memory traces, making them persistent and interconnecting them with existing knowledge11. Sleep deprivation is believed to negatively affect long-term potentiation (LTP) in the hippocampus, a key structure involved in encoding and retaining new information12. Memory encoding refers to the cognitive process through which new information is actively processed and integrated into pre-existing knowledge networks13. During wakefulness, newly learned information is initially processed in the hippocampus, and its repeated activation, primarily during sleep, facilitates memory consolidation and integration into the neocortex14. Recent studies in animal15 and human16 models have shown that the hippocampus may also play a role in consolidating sleep-dependent memories, even when it is not directly involved during the encoding phase. Experimental models consistently show that post-learning sleep deprivation impairs memory retention compared to normal sleep conditions17.

The prescription and use of benzodiazepines as a class of psychotropic drugs with better safety profiles than central nervous system depressants began in the 1960s18. Benzodiazepines are categorized based on their duration of action into short-acting, intermediate-acting, and long-acting types. For managing insomnia, short-acting and intermediate-acting benzodiazepines are typically chosen, whereas long-acting benzodiazepines are more suitable for addressing anxiety19. Long-term treatment with benzodiazepines has been described as causing cognitive impairment20.

Benzodiazepine receptor antagonists have been shown to block or reverse learning and memory impairment21. The use of benzodiazepines can negatively affect the ability to learn new information and retain it over extended periods. These drugs impair memory through multiple mechanisms, although it remains unclear whether the associated central nervous system disorders diminish or resolve over time22. Benzodiazepines exhibit significant pharmacological variability, including differences in onset, intensity, and duration of action, which are influenced by factors such as lipid solubility, receptor affinity, and metabolic pathways. Among these factors, lipid solubility and receptor affinity appear to have the most pronounced impact on memory23. Diazepam, a member of the benzodiazepine family, is available in various formulations, including injectable solutions, oral tablets, and rectal gels24.

Diazepam is widely prescribed for the treatment of numerous conditions, including anxiety disorders, seizures, alcohol withdrawal syndrome, muscle spasms, and insomnia25. However, numerous studies have demonstrated that its use is associated with memory impairment as a notable side effect26–28, further underscoring the broader cognitive effects of benzodiazepines. The cognitive effects of diazepam have been reported to be influenced by dose, duration of use, age, and concomitant exposure to sedative medications; hence careful clinical management of these factors may change memory impairment29.

Direct and long-term human studies on this subject are limited due to ethical considerations. However, evidence from existing research suggests that the adverse effects of acute and chronic sleep deprivation on cognition and overall health often surpass the side effects associated with short-term, controlled use of benzodiazepines30. Given the parallel negative effects of both chronic sleep deprivation and benzodiazepine use on memory and learning, an important question arises: Is it less harmful to endure sleep deprivation or to pharmacologically induce sleep using agents like diazepam? This study aims to address this question by comparing cognitive and biochemical outcomes in male rats subjected to either chronic sleep deprivation or diazepam treatment, relative to a control group.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The present research utilized a controlled experimental design to investigate the effects of chronic sleep deprivation and diazepam treatment in adult male Wistar rats. This study was conducted in strict accordance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines31. Furthermore, all procedures were conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines for animal research and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences, Ahvaz, Iran.

Animals

Eighteen adult male Wistar rats, aged eight weeks and weighing 200–220 g, were obtained from the Animal Center of Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences, Ahvaz, Iran. Rats were acclimatized for one week prior to the experiment.

Group allocation

The animals were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6):

Received intraperitoneal (IP) injections of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; vehicle) at the onset of the light phase and were housed in sleep deprivation apparatus without sleep deprivation induction.

Received intraperitoneal (IP) injections of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; vehicle) at the onset of the light phase and were housed in sleep deprivation apparatus without sleep deprivation induction.

diazepam group: Received IP injections of diazepam (1 mg/kg body weight) at the onset of the light phase and were housed in sleep deprivation apparatus without sleep deprivation induction.

diazepam group: Received IP injections of diazepam (1 mg/kg body weight) at the onset of the light phase and were housed in sleep deprivation apparatus without sleep deprivation induction.

CSR group: Subjected to 18 h/day of sleep deprivation for 21 consecutive days and received IP injections of DMSO at the onset of the light phase.

CSR group: Subjected to 18 h/day of sleep deprivation for 21 consecutive days and received IP injections of DMSO at the onset of the light phase.

Chronic sleep restriction (CSR) protocol

A modified multiple platform method (MMPM) was used to induce chronic sleep deprivation. The apparatus consisted of a circular water tank (90 cm diameter, 50 cm height) containing 12 small platforms (7 cm diameter, 10 cm height), each protruding 2 cm above the water surface. The number of platforms was approximately twice the group size to allow adequate movement and position changes. For the sham and diazepam groups, larger platforms (15 cm diameter) were placed in a dry chamber of the same apparatus, providing a stable surface for rest and sleep without the risk of falling.

The CSR group was placed on small platforms from 2:00 p.m. to 8:00 a.m. daily, ensuring 18 h of continuous wakefulness per cycle. The water temperature was maintained at 22 ± 1 °C, and the tank water was replaced daily to ensure hygiene. Food and water were suspended above the tank for continuous access. The light/dark cycle and humidity were kept constant throughout the 21-day experimental period. Only one group was housed in the apparatus at a time to avoid interference32,33.

Diazepam preparation and administration

Diazepam (CAS No. 439-14-5, Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a stock solution. The drug was administered IP at a dose of 1 mg/kg body weight (0.5 mL per animal) daily for 21 days34,35. This dose was administered during the light phase to coincide with the rats’ inactive period, based on its established use as a moderate, behaviorally relevant dose in experimental studies36. The sham and CSR groups received equivalent volumes of DMSO as a vehicle control.

The diazepam solution was prepared fresh daily at a standard concentration. Immediately prior to each daily injection, every animal in the Diazepam group was individually weighed. The injection volume for each rat was then calculated mathematically based on its exact weight of that day, ensuring a consistent 1 mg/kg dose. This process was efficiently managed by a team of three researchers working in coordination, allowing for rapid weighing, calculation, and administration for all animals without significant delay, thus minimizing procedural stress.

Experimental timeline and assessments

After 21 days, animals were transferred to testing rooms for subsequent behavioral and physiological assessments.

Cognitive tests

Passive avoidance task (PAT)

The Passive Avoidance Task (PAT) was employed to evaluate associative learning and memory in rodents, particularly their ability to retain aversive experiences. The apparatus consisted of a two-compartment shuttle box, with one compartment illuminated and the other dark. The dark compartment was equipped with a grid floor capable of delivering a mild electric foot shock. During the training session, each rat was positioned in the lit section of the apparatus, and the time taken to move into the dark section was noted. Once the rat entered the dark area, the door was shut, and a gentle electric shock (0.5 mA for 2 s) was applied. Following this, the rat was taken out of the device and placed back in its cage. A day later, the memory retention test was carried out. The rat was once more placed in the lit section, and the delay before entering the dark section was measured. No electric shock was given during this test. A longer delay in entering the dark section was seen as a sign of better memory retention37.

Novel object recognition (NOR)

The Novel Object Recognition (NOR) test was used to assess non-spatial working memory and recognition memory in rodents. The NOR task was conducted in an open-field arena (50 cm × 50 cm × 40 cm) under dim lighting conditions. The test consisted of three phases: habituation and training (day one) along with testing phase (day two).

Habituation Phase: Rats were allowed to freely explore the empty arena for 10 min on to familiarize them with the environment.

Habituation Phase: Rats were allowed to freely explore the empty arena for 10 min on to familiarize them with the environment.

Training Phase: Two identical objects (Object A1 and Object A2) were placed in the arena, and each rat was allowed to explore the objects for 5 min. The time spent exploring each object was recorded. Exploration was defined as the rat directing its nose toward the object at a distance of ≤ 2 cm or touching the object with its nose.

Training Phase: Two identical objects (Object A1 and Object A2) were placed in the arena, and each rat was allowed to explore the objects for 5 min. The time spent exploring each object was recorded. Exploration was defined as the rat directing its nose toward the object at a distance of ≤ 2 cm or touching the object with its nose.

Testing Phase: Twenty-four hours after the training phase, one of the familiar objects (Object A1) was replaced with a novel object (Object B). The rat was placed back in the arena and allowed to explore the objects for 5 min. The time spent exploring the novel object (Object B) and the familiar object (Object A2) was recorded.

Testing Phase: Twenty-four hours after the training phase, one of the familiar objects (Object A1) was replaced with a novel object (Object B). The rat was placed back in the arena and allowed to explore the objects for 5 min. The time spent exploring the novel object (Object B) and the familiar object (Object A2) was recorded.

Behavioral sessions were recorded using a video camera connected to a computer and monitored remotely to minimize human interference and external distraction. This procedure ensured that exploration behavior was not influenced by experimenter presence or movement during testing.

Recognition memory was quantified using the Discrimination Index (DI), calculated as follows:

DI = Time spent exploring the novel object - Time spent exploring the familiar object / Total exploration time.

A higher DI value indicated better recognition memory38.

Behavioral testing was completed over three consecutive days. On day 1, animals underwent habituation and training for the Novel Object Recognition (NOR) task. The NOR test was performed 24 h later. To limit the impact of aversive shock-related stress on subsequent behavioral performance, NOR testing preceded the Passive Avoidance Test (PAT). Accordingly, PAT training was conducted on the evening of the NOR test day, followed by the PAT test session 24 h later. This schedule minimized stress-related interference and reduced potential fading effects of sleep restriction and diazepam on behavioral outcomes.

Biochemical parameters

Following the completion of cognitive behavioral tests, the rats were deeply anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of sodium thiopental (100 mg/kg)39. Subsequently, euthanasia was performed by decapitation, and the brains were rapidly excised from the skull and the hippocampus was carefully dissected from the surrounding tissue using a coronal incision on ice. Hippocampi were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. On the day of measurement, tissue homogenization was performed using a homogenizer. Specifically, 500 mg of brain tissue was mixed with 2 mL of homogenization buffer (pH 7.2) containing 50 mM Tris, 0.5% Triton X-100, 150 mM NaCl, and a protease inhibitor cocktail. The resulting homogenate was centrifuged at 4,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Then the supernatant was collected for biochemical analysis40.

Measurement of oxidative stress (ROS, TAC)

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in rat brain tissue were quantified using 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) via flow cytometry (Kiazist Life Sciences Kit, Iran). Protein concentration in the supernatant was determined using the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The supernatant was incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in PBS at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. DCFH-DA is deacetylated by endogenous esterases to form DCFH, which is subsequently oxidized by ROS to produce the fluorescent compound 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescein (DCF). Fluorescence intensity was determined using a flow cytometer set to an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm. At least 10,000 events were captured for each sample. The collected data were processed with FlowJo software (Tree Star, Ashland, OR, USA) and presented in terms of relative fluorescence units (RFU). To normalize ROS levels to protein content, the RFU was divided by the protein concentration (mg/mL) of the sample, yielding ROS levels in relative fluorescence units per milligram of protein (RFU/mg protein)41. Total antioxidant capacity (TAC) was determined based on the sample’s ability to reduce ferric ions to ferrous ions, forming a detectable blue complex. This reaction was measured photometrically, with absorbance recorded at the specified wavelength (maximum absorbance at 700 nm) according to the kit protocol42.

Measurement of TNF-α and BDNF

Following deep and irreversible anesthesia with a high dose of sodium thiopental, rat brains were rapidly dissected, and hippocampal tissues were isolated and immediately placed on ice. The tissue was rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. The concentrations of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) in the hippocampal tissue samples were measured using specific ELISA kit (Sun Long Biotec Co. LTD), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Briefly, samples were added to microplate wells pre-coated with primary antibodies against TNF-α or BDNF. After a 30-minute incubation, a stop solution was added, and the chromogenic color change was quantified at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer43,44. Biochemical concentrations were expressed as ng/mg protein to normalize for tissue heterogeneity and enable accurate cross-sample comparisons.

Statistical analysis

In this study, data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism version 8. Initially, the Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to check if the data followed a normal distribution. For normally distributed data, one-way ANOVA was applied to compare group means. When a statistically significant difference between groups was observed (P < 0.05), Tukey’s post hoc test was used for pairwise comparisons.

Discussion

The present study investigated the effects of diazepam administration and chronic sleep restriction (CSR) on cognitive performance and biochemical parameters over a three-week period in rats. Behavioral and biochemical analyses demonstrated significant impairments in both treated groups, with CSR exerting more profound adverse effects compared to diazepam.

The Passive Avoidance Test (PAT) and Novel Object Recognition (NOR) test were employed to assess memory performance. PAT is a fear-based instrumental learning task in which animals learn to suppress a specific behavioral response that has previously been associated with an aversive stimulus. In contrast, the novel object recognition (NOR) test exploits rodents’ innate tendency to explore unfamiliar objects, offering a reliable and non-invasive approach for assessing recognition memory without the use of aversive stimuli or deprivation protocols36. NOR is a low-stress, non-spatial task that primarily engages the hippocampus and perirhinal cortex37, whereas PAT is an associative, emotionally driven test involving moderate stress and the activation of both the hippocampus and amygdala38. Both paradigms serve as valid and complementary tools and provides a more comprehensive assessment of cognitive performance and hippocampal function in rodent models.

Previous studies investigating the effects of benzodiazepine administration and CSR in rodents have consistently reported detrimental outcomes, particularly manifesting as memory impairments and amnesic effects36,37. Sleep plays a critical restorative role and is essential for the consolidation of memory in humans36. Sleep deprivation has also been shown to impair long-term potentiation (LTP) and reduce synaptic plasticity within the hippocampus, both of which are fundamental mechanisms underlying memory formation and cognitive function45. In a study by Lu et al., rats subjected to 21 consecutive days of CSR for 18 h per day were evaluated using the Morris Water Maze test, which revealed significant impairments in cognitive behavior46. Similarly, Saygin et al. demonstrated that various forms of sleep deprivation negatively affect hippocampal-dependent learning and memory processes in rats47. In case of benzodiazepines Unrug-Neervoort et al. reported that diazepam administration led to impairments in long-term memory, suggesting that its cognitive effects may be primarily linked to reductions in alertness and vigilance, rather than its anxiolytic properties48. In a study utilizing the six-arm radial maze, White et al. demonstrated that diazepam produced a dose-dependent increase in working memory errors during testing49. Similarly, Arolfo et al., employing the Morris Water Maze paradigm, found that diazepam disrupted the retention phase of spatial memory without significantly affecting the acquisition process50. These findings align with the results of the present study and others employing PAT NOR tasks51,52, further support the idea that diazepam negatively affects memory performance.

In this study in the PAT, animals exposed to CSR showed a marked reduction in step-through latency, suggesting considerable deficits in aversive memory retention. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted by our colleagues53. Research by Louise Carton et al. showed that administering diazepam at 15 mg/kg/day over an 8-week period led to deficits in working memory in mice54. These findings align with the outcomes of our study. Although the diazepam-treated group also exhibited impairments, the extent was notably less severe than in the CSR group. These findings imply that while diazepam has some detrimental effects on memory consolidation, prolonged sleep loss may have a more destructive impact on hippocampus-dependent learning and memory. Similar trends were observed in the NOR task, where the CSR group demonstrated a negative discrimination index, indicating a failure to distinguish between novel and familiar objects, a clear sign of recognition memory impairment. While diazepam-treated rats also performed worse than the sham group, their cognitive performance remained above chance level. These results collectively indicate that while both treatments impair memory, chronic sleep deprivation has a more deleterious effect.

Total antioxidant capacity (TAC) offers valuable insight into the dynamic in vivo balance between pro-oxidant and antioxidant systems and may serve as a useful marker for evaluating physiological influences on redox homeostasis55. Several studies have shown that diazepam and sleep deprivation induce oxidative stress35,56. In the present study TAC was significantly reduced in both experimental groups, indicating elevated oxidative stress. The comparable reduction in TAC across both groups suggests that oxidative stress is a common feature of both benzodiazepine use and sleep deprivation. On the other hand, ROS levels were significantly elevated in the Diaz group and even more so in the CSR group, highlighting a heightened oxidative stress state that likely exacerbates neuronal damage and cognitive impairment. A significant reduction in TAC as an index of antioxidant defense, and increased ROS production also suggests that both sleep deprivation and diazepam administration contribute to increased free radicals or decreased activity of enzymes involved in reducing oxidative reactions with more pronounced effects in CSR. These results highlight the degree of harm inflicted by free radicals, suggesting that following a 21-day course of diazepam treatment and chronic sleep restriction in particular, the antioxidant system was unable to counteract the accumulated oxidative damage.

Exposure to chronic stress can result in a sustained elevation of cytokine levels by amplifying the immune response57. Hence prolonged CSR as a form of chronic stress, can elicit a pro-inflammatory response by increasing several cytokines, including TNF-α. Previous studies suggest that sleep deprivation elevates pro-inflammatory cytokines58–61. This is in agreement with the present study, which demonstrated a significant increase in TNF-α levels following chronic sleep restriction. Biochemical analysis in present study also revealed that diazepam did not significantly elevate TNF-α levels compared to the Sham group. However, the CSR group as mentioned earlier, exhibited significantly higher TNF-α levels, with a notable difference between Diaz and CSR (Fig. 2), indicating a more pronounced inflammatory response following sleep deprivation for 21 consecutive days. An anti-inflammatory role for diazepam has been reported in the scientific literature. Gomes et al. observed that diazepam administration reversed the elevation of hippocampal IL-1β and IL-6 levels62. Moreover, earlier studies have demonstrated that diazepam can suppress the release of cytokines such as TNF-α by glial cells following traumatic brain injury in rats63. These findings align with the results of the present study, where TNF-α levels did not significantly differ from those in the sham group. One possible explanation is that the absence of elevated pro-inflammatory markers in the diazepam-treated group may be related to its anxiolytic mechanism, which enhances GABAergic activity in the brain64.

Furthermore, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels were significantly decreased, with the CSR group showing a more prominent reduction. BDNF is closely associated with the mechanisms underlying synaptic plasticity, neurogenesis, and neuronal survival, playing a pivotal role in hippocampal-dependent spatial learning and memory formation65,66. Previous research has shown that chronic sleep deprivation leads to a significant reduction in hippocampal BDNF levels67. In line with SD effects, it has been said that diazepam reduces BDNF levels68. These findings suggest that both CSR and diazepam may contribute to the downregulation of BDNF, potentially affecting neuroplasticity and cognitive performance. The more severe depletion in the CSR group in present study supports the hypothesis that sleep is vital for maintaining neurotrophic support and neuronal resilience. However, although BDNF decline appears to be a contributing factor, it may also serve as a biomarker reflecting broader neural dysfunction induced by these interventions. Further mechanistic studies are needed to dissect its specific role.

Growing evidence suggests that oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and reduced BDNF levels are not independent events but rather mutually reinforcing processes. Oxidative stress can activate redox-sensitive transcription factors such as nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), leading to increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β and TNF-α. These inflammatory mediators, in turn, further enhance ROS generation, thereby exacerbating oxidative damage69. Both oxidative stress and neuroinflammation have been shown to suppress BDNF expression. Importantly, reduced BDNF levels diminish neuronal resilience and synaptic plasticity70. Thus, these processes synergistically contribute to cognitive impairment observed under conditions such as chronic sleep deprivation or pharmacological sedation.

The combined impact of elevated inflammation, oxidative stress, and reduced neurotrophic support appears to underlie the cognitive impairments observed. However, an important distinction lies in the reversibility of diazepam’s effects. Previous findings, including those by Carton et al., suggest that cognitive impairments induced by diazepam are not permanent and may resolve following treatment discontinuation. This implies that the structural integrity of memory-related brain regions remains intact under chronic benzodiazepine use, whereas sleep deprivation may induce more enduring damage54. Despite the fact that diazepam may increase total sleep time and reduce sleep latency, it alters sleep architecture by suppressing slow-wave sleep, a stage critical for memory consolidation71,72. Furthermore, benzodiazepines carry a significant risk of dependence and may induce rebound insomnia upon discontinuation73. These considerations underscore the importance of prioritizing nonpharmacological strategies, such as cognitive behavioral therapy and sleep hygiene interventions, for the long-term management of anxiety-related sleep disturbances.

Although the present study was conducted in an animal model, the observed alterations in oxidative stress markers, inflammatory cytokines, and BDNF levels may have relevant implications for human neurological health. These changes have been implicated in increased vulnerability to neurodegenerative processes, particularly in aging populations74. While direct extrapolation from animal models to humans should be approached with caution, the convergence of experimental and clinical findings suggests that prolonged sleep disturbances or unsupervised benzodiazepine consumption may contribute to inflammation-related neural dysfunction through shared molecular pathways involving oxidative stress, inflammation, and impaired BDNF signaling. Further longitudinal human studies are warranted to clarify causality and long-term neurological outcomes.

A primary limitation of this study is the exclusive use of male subjects, which constrains the generalizability of the findings. This design choice was necessitated by the need to control for the significant confounding effects of the female estrous cycle, which is known to modulate sleep architecture, stress reactivity, and cognitive function. While this approach enabled a controlled analysis of the core variables, it precludes insight into potential sex differences. Consequently, future investigations should examine these effects in females, with careful consideration of estrous cycle phase.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that while both sleep deprivation and pharmacologically induce sleep using diazepam adversely affect cognitive function and brain biochemistry, chronic sleep deprivation exerts a more profound and potentially irreversible impact on memory, learning, oxidative balance, inflammatory status, and neurotrophic support. In contrast, diazepam’s effects, although significant, appear milder and may be reversible upon treatment discontinuation. Therefore, based on behavioral and molecular outcomes, diazepam may pose a comparatively lower risk to cognitive health than prolonged sleep loss. Nonetheless, caution must be exercised when considering long-term benzodiazepine use due to its potential for dependency and other side effects. Future studies should investigate safer alternatives for managing sleep disturbances, particularly in populations at risk for cognitive decline.
