High‐Order Multipolar Excitations Governing Circular Dichroism in an All‐Dielectric Chiral Metasurface
Authors: Chien‐Feng Chien, Hsiu‐Ping Su, Yu‐Tsung Lin, Shu‐Fei Ho, Pin Chieh Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChiral metasurfaces provide a versatile platform for tailoring polarization‐dependent light–matter interactions with strong field confinement. Here, we report an all‐dielectric asymmetric gammadion metasurface that exhibits pronounced circular dichroism (CD) in the visible spectral range. By breaking the in‐plane mirror symmetry of the unit cell, strong polarization‐selective resonances are induced under circularly polarized illumination. Numerical simulations and experiments reveal two distinct CD resonances near 652 and 735 nm with maximum absolute CD values exceeding 50% and 40%, respectively. Electromagnetic multipole decomposition shows that the strong CD at 652 nm originates from selective excitation of high‐order electric and magnetic modes under right‐handed circularly polarized illumination leading to a large imbalance in multipolar scattering and strongly localized chiral near fields. In contrast, the weaker CD at 735 nm arises from a more symmetric and reduced multipolar excitation landscape. Angle‐resolved analyses further demonstrate that the quadrupole‐dominated resonance maintains robust CD under oblique incidence, whereas the simpler multipolar excitation at 735 nm yields a more angle‐insensitive but moderate CD response. These results establish a physical link between structural asymmetry, multipolar excitation, near‐field optical chirality, and angle‐resolved chiroptical behavior, providing practical design guidelines for low‐loss dielectric metasurfaces with robust chiroptical responses.Keywords: circular dichroism, dielectric metasurfaces, multipolar resonances, optical chirality

Introduction

Circular dichroism (CD), defined as the differential optical response of a system to left‐ and right‐handed circularly polarized light, constitutes a fundamental manifestation of chiral light–matter interactions [1, 2]. It has long served as a critical tool in molecular spectroscopy, enantiomeric discrimination, and polarization‐sensitive photonic technologies [3, 4]. However, in natural chiral materials, CD signals are typically weak due to limited intrinsic chirality and the inefficient coupling between light and matter at subwavelength scales [5]. Over the past decade, metasurfaces (planar arrays of artificially engineered nanostructures) [6, 7, 8] have emerged as a powerful platform for overcoming these limitations by enabling precise control over electromagnetic fields through structural symmetry breaking and resonant enhancement [9, 10, 11].

Among various metasurface platforms, all‐dielectric chiral metasurfaces [12, 13, 14] have attracted increasing attention owing to their low optical loss, high refractive index contrast, and ability to sustain sharp resonances with strong field confinement. Unlike plasmonic systems, dielectric metasurfaces support a rich hierarchy of electromagnetic multipolar modes, including not only electric and magnetic dipoles but also higher‐order excitations such as electric and magnetic quadrupoles and toroidal modes [15, 16, 17]. These high‐order multipolar resonances play a crucial role in shaping both near‐field and far‐field optical responses, offering new degrees of freedom for tailoring chiroptical effects. In particular, symmetry‐broken dielectric structures can enable polarization‐selective excitation of specific multipolar channels, giving rise to enhanced CD and localized chiral near‐fields.

Recent studies have demonstrated that the excitation and interference of multipolar modes can be engineered to amplify chiroptical responses [18, 19, 20], generate high‐quality‐factor resonances [12, 21, 22] and even approach quasi‐bound states in the continuum [23, 24]. Rigorous theoretical tools [25], including electromagnetic multipole decomposition, Mie theory, and quasinormal mode analysis, have provided valuable insights into the modal origins of chiral optical phenomena. Nevertheless, a comprehensive physical picture linking high‐order multipolar excitations to optical chirality enhancement and robust CD responses remains incomplete, particularly for all‐dielectric metasurfaces operating in the visible regime and under oblique illumination. In many reported systems, CD enhancement is often attributed qualitatively to structural chirality, whereas the specific modal contributions and their polarization‐dependent excitation pathways are not fully resolved.

In this work, we investigate the chiroptical response of an all‐dielectric asymmetric gammadion metasurface composed of amorphous silicon (a‐Si), with a particular focus on the role of high‐order multipolar resonances in governing CD and optical chirality. By performing a systematic electromagnetic multipole decomposition [15, 26], we show that the pronounced CD observed at visible wavelengths originates from polarization‐selective excitation of electric and magnetic high order modes enabled by broken in‐plane mirror symmetry. Through detailed analysis of near‐field electric and magnetic distributions, we further demonstrate that these high‐order modes generate strongly localized chiral near‐fields leading to a substantial enhancement of optical chirality. Moreover, we examine the angular dependence of the chiroptical response and reveal that the metasurface maintains a robust CD over a wide range of incident angles, reflecting the combined influence of intrinsic and extrinsic chirality supported by the multipolar resonances. Although planar dielectric gammadion metasurfaces with strong chiro‐optical responses have previously been reported, the present work addresses a different physical regime and question. For example, in the TiO2 gammadion/waveguide platform [14], the main resonance was associated with dominant higher‐order radiation, especially toroidal‐quadrupole and magnetic‐octupole contributions, in an optically thick diffraction‐assisted configuration. By contrast, our asymmetric a‐Si gammadion metasurface is used here to elucidate how broken in‐plane mirror symmetry redistributes the helicity‐dependent excitation among dipolar, quadrupolar, and toroidal channels across two visible CD resonances. Therefore, this work lies not simply in invoking higher‐order multipoles, but in establishing an explicit link between structural asymmetry, multipolar redistribution, and localized optical chirality enhancement of the chiroptical response. Our results elucidate the fundamental multipolar mechanisms underlying chiroptical phenomena in dielectric metasurfaces and provide clear physical guidelines for designing polarization‐selective nanophotonic devices with enhanced and resilient chiral responses.

Results and Discussion

Figure 1 summarizes the structural design, fabrication quality, and basic chiroptical response of the proposed all‐dielectric asymmetric gammadion metasurface. As shown in Figure 1A, the metasurface consists of a‐Si nanostructures arranged in a periodic square lattice on a SiO2 substrate. The gammadion geometry is intentionally designed with unequal arm lengths and asymmetric air gaps, thereby breaking the in‐plane mirror symmetry while preserving a compact subwavelength footprint. This structural asymmetry is essential for enabling polarization‐dependent light–matter interactions and generating intrinsic optical chirality under normal incidence. The use of high‐refractive‐index a‐Si allows the metasurface to support strong electromagnetic resonances in the visible spectral range, providing a suitable platform for multipolar mode engineering.

The fabrication quality of the metasurface is verified by the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images shown in Figure 1B. The tilted‐view SEM image confirms the uniformity of the nanostructure array and the well‐defined vertical sidewalls of the a‐Si elements, whereas the top‐view image reveals high fidelity of the asymmetric gammadion geometry across the entire patterned area. Subsequently, we investigated the transmittance and CD properties of the all‐dielectric asymmetric gammadion metasurface under normal incidence of right‐handed circularly polarized (RCP) and left‐handed circularly polarized (LCP) light.

Figure 1C,D present the simulated transmission spectra under RCP and LCP illumination, respectively. The discrepancies between simulation and experiment are mainly attributed to fabrication induced dimensional deviations, such as linewidth and corner rounding. In addition, because the optical measurements were performed using a 10 × objective lens with a numerical aperture of 0.26, the incident beam contains finite oblique angle components rather than an ideal normally incident plane wave. Additional simulations under 15° oblique incidence show better agreement with the experimental spectra (see Supporting Information S1: Figure S5), indicating that both geometric deviations and finite angle illumination contribute to the observed differences. It is notable that the transmittance under LCP illumination is significantly higher than that under RCP, indicating stronger resonant excitation for RCP incidence, which will be further analyzed in subsequent sections. Pronounced differences between the two polarization states are observed at specific wavelengths, indicating a strong polarization‐selective resonant response induced by the asymmetric geometry. The simulations predict pronounced CDabs peaks at 652 and 735 nm, reaching values of 52.1% and 40%, respectively. These results are in good agreement with experimental observations, where CDabs peaks of 44.9% at 649 nm and 33.65% at 719 nm are recorded. Furthermore, comparative analysis with symmetric structures reveals that, under C2 symmetry, a single CDabs peak emerges near 652 nm (as shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S2). Upon breaking the symmetry to C4 symmetry [28], an additional peak appears at 735 nm, demonstrating the tunability of optical chirality through structural asymmetry. These results demonstrate that the asymmetric dielectric gammadion structure effectively converts structural chirality into measurable far‐field optical chirality. Additional polarization‐resolved measurements confirm that the cross‐polarized transmission components are much weaker than the copolarized ones in the present design (see Supporting Information S1: Figure S3). Therefore, including them in the CDabs calculation [29] leads mainly to a quantitative change in amplitude, whereas the main resonance positions and physical interpretation remain unchanged. In the following sections, we elucidate the physical origin of these CDabs resonances by analyzing the underlying multipolar excitations and their associated near‐field chiral characteristics.

To elucidate the physical origin of the pronounced CDabs observed in the asymmetric gammadion metasurface, we perform an electromagnetic multipole decomposition of the scattered fields under circularly polarized illumination. This method is adopted here because it directly links the full‐wave optical response to the underlying electric, magnetic, and toroidal multipolar scattering channels, allowing us to identify the helicity‐dependent modal contributions responsible for the two observed CDabs resonances. Figure 2A shows the wavelength‐dependent multipolar contributions under RCP excitation. At the CDabs resonance wavelength of 652 nm, the scattering response is dominated by high‐order multipolar modes, most notably the electric quadrupole (Q e) and magnetic quadrupole (Q m), whereas the contributions from conventional electric and magnetic dipole modes (P x , P y , M x , and M y ) remain comparatively weak. This clear dominance of quadrupolar modes indicates that the resonance at this wavelength is governed primarily by higher order multipolar scattering rather than dipolar resonances. Such high order modes are known to support stronger electromagnetic energy confinement within the nanostructure, which is consistent with the reduced transmittance and enhanced light–matter interaction observed at this resonance. In particular, the strong Q e contribution suggests a highly localized electric field distribution inside the asymmetric gammadion geometry, a feature that will be directly visualized through near‐field analysis in Figure 3. To examine the possible interaction between adjacent meta‐units, we further analyzed the electric‐field distributions in a metasurface array at the two representative resonance wavelengths (refer to Supporting Information S1: Figure S8). In both cases, the electric fields remain predominantly confined within each individual gammadion element, whereas the field intensity in the regions between neighboring units is weak and shows no significant overlap. These results indicate that the resonant response is mainly localized to each meta‐unit and is governed by effective Mie‐type resonances of the high‐index dielectric structure. Therefore, although the lattice period is not deeply subwavelength over the entire spectral range, the observed chiroptical response is still dominated primarily by the polarization‐selective resonances of the individual meta‐units, whereas inter‐element coupling appears to be minor.

The polarization‐dependent nature of the multipolar excitations is further clarified by comparing the scattering intensities under RCP and LCP illumination. As summarized in Figure 2B, at 652 nm, the metasurface exhibits a pronounced asymmetry in modal excitation between the two polarization states. Under RCP illumination, the scattering response is dominated by electric and magnetic quadrupole modes (Q e and Q m) accompanied by moderate contributions from the magnetic dipole along the x direction (M x ) and the magnetic octupole (O m). In contrast, LCP illumination preferentially excites different modal channels, including electric dipole and toroidal dipole components, reflecting a strong polarization‐selective coupling enabled by the broken in‐plane mirror symmetry of the metasurface. This substantial contrast in modal composition directly leads to a large differential scattering response and gives rise to the pronounced CD observed at this wavelength. In contrast, at the longer wavelength resonance around 735 nm, where the CDabs amplitude is comparatively weaker, the multipolar excitation profiles under RCP and LCP illumination become much more similar. As shown in the lower panel of Figure 2B, both polarization states excite a comparable set of modes, including electric dipoles, the magnetic dipole along the x direction, and toroidal octupole components. The absence of a dominant polarization‐selective high‐order mode at this wavelength reduces the imbalance between RCP and LCP scattering resulting in a weaker CDabs response.

I m denotes the scattering intensity of a given multipolar component under incident polarization m, where m = RCP or LCP. S r denotes the normalized relative difference of a given multipolar scattering contribution under opposite circularly polarized excitations. The symbol r denotes the corresponding multipolar radiation channel considered in the decomposition, such as the electric dipole, magnetic dipole, toroidal dipole, electric quadrupole, or magnetic quadrupole contribution. At the CDabs resonance wavelength of 652 nm, the radar chart reveals a highly asymmetric distribution of multipolar scattering strengths between the two circular polarizations. In particular, the M x , Q e, and Q m components exhibit large positive contrasts, whereas the P x , P y , TD x , and TD y components show pronounced negative contrasts, indicating a strong redistribution of scattering strength between RCP and LCP excitation. This pronounced imbalance in the multipolar scattering landscape directly reflects the strong polarization selectivity of the metasurface and constitutes the primary physical origin of the large CDabs observed at this wavelength. Notably, this asymmetry is not confined to a single resonance but extends across multiple multipolar channels, underscoring that the observed CDabs arises from a collective redistribution of scattering contributions among chiral‐sensitive modes rather than from an isolated multipolar excitation.

By contrast, at 735 nm, the relative differences across most multipolar components remain comparatively small as evidenced by the more symmetric radar pattern. The scattering strengths of the dominant modes under RCP and LCP illumination are of similar magnitude indicating that the metasurface responds to both helicities in a nearly equivalent manner at this wavelength. Nevertheless, certain modes, such as Q e, TD y , and Q T, still exhibit non‐negligible differences in Sr, giving rise to an observable but weaker CDabs signal in the spectrum. This comparison between the two resonances highlights that the magnitude of CD is directly governed by the degree of asymmetry in the multipolar scattering distribution between opposite circular polarizations. It should be emphasized that the presence of high order multipolar excitations does not by itself guarantee a maximal CDabs response. In the present metasurface, the role of the high order modes is to define the modal origin and helicity selectivity of the resonance. The absolute CD magnitude is instead determined by the degree of imbalance among the radiative multipolar channels excited by opposite helicities. At 652 nm, RCP selectively excites strong electric and magnetic quadrupole contributions, whereas LCP still couples to non‐negligible dipolar and toroidal channels, so the unfavored helicity is not completely suppressed. As a result, the differential transmission remains moderate rather than approaching unity. At 735 nm, the multipolar compositions under RCP and LCP become more similar, leading to a smaller helicity‐dependent imbalance and hence a weaker CDabs response. In addition, the achievable CDabs in the present platform is also influenced by the intrinsic material loss of a‐Si in the visible range. Although a‐Si provides a high refractive index favorable for supporting strong visible resonances, its extinction coefficient remains non‐negligible at these wavelengths. This material absorption reduces the resonance quality and weakens the buildup and interference of helicity‐selective multipolar channels, thereby limiting the peak CDabs. To verify this effect, we additionally performed simulations with a reduced imaginary part of the refractive index and found that the CDabs increases when the extinction coefficient is lowered (refer to Supporting Information S1: Figure S6). This result confirms that the moderate CDabs values in the present structure are governed not only by the multipolar excitation landscape but also by the optical loss of the dielectric material platform.

Figure 3 provides a direct visualization of the near‐field electromagnetic responses and optical chirality distributions that originate from the polarization‐dependent multipolar excitations identified in Figure 2. Figure 3A–D show the simulated z‐components of the induced electric and magnetic fields at the CDabs resonance wavelength of 652 nm under RCP and LCP illumination. Consistent with the multipole decomposition results in Figure 2, RCP illumination gives rise to pronounced high‐order multipolar field patterns. In particular, the electric field distribution in Figure 3A exhibits two vertically aligned lobes with opposite phases across the structure, a hallmark of a dominant electric quadrupole Q e resonance oriented along the z direction. Correspondingly, the magnetic field in Figure 3C displays multiple nodal features along the y direction, which is characteristic of a magnetic quadrupole Qm–type mode. In contrast, under LCP illumination (Figure 3B,D), the induced electric and magnetic fields are relatively weaker and exhibit distinctly different spatial distributions. The absence of well‐defined quadrupolar field patterns reflects the suppression of Q e and Q m modes under LCP excitation, in agreement with the multipolar analysis in Figure 2, where LCP preferentially couples to electric dipole and toroidal dipole channels. This pronounced asymmetry in near‐field intensity and modal composition between RCP and LCP excitation directly mirrors the far‐field multipolar imbalance and confirms the polarization‐selective nature of the asymmetric metasurface.

The chiral near fields are strongly localized in regions associated with the quadrupolar field lobes, particularly in the upper and lower parts of the unit cell. Under RCP illumination, these regions exhibit significantly stronger and more confined optical chirality compared to the LCP case, leading to a pronounced differential enhancement. This clear correspondence between multipolar field distributions and localized optical chirality confirms that the large CD observed in this system arises from the selective excitation of high‐order multipolar resonances that generate strongly chiral near fields.

To further elucidate the robustness of the chiroptical response under realistic illumination conditions, we analyze the transmittance and absolute circular dichroism CDabs of the asymmetric gammadion metasurface under oblique incidence. Figure 4A,B show the angle‐resolved transmittance maps under RCP and LCP illumination in the spectral region around the primary resonance near 652 nm, with the incident angle θ varied from 0° to 20°. Under normal incidence (θ = 0°), the metasurface exhibits strong attenuation of RCP light while remaining largely transmissive for LCP light resulting in a pronounced polarization contrast. This behavior originates from the intrinsic chirality of the asymmetric gammadion geometry, which enables selective excitation of high‐order multipolar resonances at this wavelength. As the incident angle increases, the evolution of the transmittance differs for the two helicities indicating that the underlying multipolar resonances respond differently to oblique excitation depending on the polarization state. This helicity‐dependent angular dispersion reflects the fact that the dominant resonant modes at 652 nm (primarily the Qe‐ and Qm‐dominated modes identified in Figure 2) retain strong polarization selectivity even when the illumination direction deviates from normal incidence. The corresponding angle‐resolved CDabs map in Figure 4C further highlights this behavior. The CDabs reaches a peak value exceeding 52% near normal incidence and remains above 40% over a broad angular range up to ∼13°. Such sustained CDabs under oblique illumination demonstrates that the chiroptical response is not limited to a narrow angular condition but is instead intrinsically robust. This robustness can be attributed to the combined effects of intrinsic chirality, arising from the broken in‐plane mirror symmetry of the metasurface, and extrinsic chirality introduced by the tilted incident wave vector. Figure 4D,E present the corresponding angle‐resolved transmittance maps for the secondary resonance near 735 nm. In contrast to the behavior observed at 652 nm, the transmittance responses under RCP and LCP illumination in this spectral region become much more symmetric across the entire angular range up to 20°. Consequently, the CDabs map in Figure 4F exhibits a comparatively weaker CDabs response with a maximum value of ∼42%. This reduced CDabs magnitude under normal incidence can be attributed to the involvement of fewer and less polarization‐selective multipolar modes at this wavelength. As shown in Figure 2, both RCP and LCP illumination at 735 nm excite largely overlapping sets of multipolar resonances resulting in a smaller imbalance in modal excitation. Notably, the reduced complexity of multipolar coupling at 735 nm also leads to a CDabs response that is less sensitive to variations in the incident angle. Because fewer multipolar channels contribute to the chiroptical response, the interference conditions governing CDabs are less perturbed under oblique illumination. As a result, the CDabs remains relatively stable across a wide angular range despite its smaller magnitude compared to the primary resonance. This comparison between the two resonances highlights a trade‐off between CDabs strength and angular sensitivity: while strong CDabs at 652 nm arises from highly polarization‐selective excitation of multiple high‐order modes, the more moderate yet angle‐insensitive CDabs at 735 nm originates from a simpler and more symmetric multipolar excitation landscape.

Conclusion

In summary, we have demonstrated an all‐dielectric asymmetric gammadion metasurface that exhibits pronounced and angle‐robust CDabs in the visible spectral range. By deliberately breaking the in‐plane mirror symmetry of a high‐index a‐Si metasurface, strong polarization‐dependent light–matter interactions are enabled, allowing the conversion of structural chirality into measurable far‐field optical chirality. Numerical simulations and experimental results consistently reveal two prominent CDabs resonances near 652 and 735 nm, whose amplitudes and spectral positions can be tuned through structural asymmetry. Through a comprehensive electromagnetic multipole decomposition, we have elucidated the physical origins of these chiroptical responses. At the primary resonance near 652 nm, the CDabs is governed by the polarization‐selective excitation of high‐order multipolar modes, particularly electric and magnetic quadrupoles, which are strongly excited under RCP illumination but suppressed under LCP illumination. This pronounced asymmetry in the multipolar scattering landscape gives rise to a large differential far‐field response and is directly responsible for the observed high CDabs. In contrast, the secondary resonance near 735 nm involves fewer and more symmetric multipolar contributions under opposite circular polarizations leading to a reduced but still observable CDabs response. Near‐field analysis further reveals that the selective excitation of high‐order multipolar modes generates strongly localized chiral near fields. The electric and magnetic quadrupole dominated resonances produce spatially confined regions of enhanced optical chirality, resulting in a large differential total optical chirality enhancement between RCP and LCP illumination. The strong correspondence between multipolar field distributions, localized optical chirality, and far‐field CDabs establishes a clear physical link between modal excitation and chiroptical response. A route suggested by our additional loss reduction test for further CD enhancement is to employ a lower‐loss dielectric platform or reduce the material extinction coefficient, which improves the resonance quality and strengthens the helicity‐selective interference. In addition, more complex geometries with additional degrees of freedom, such as coupled or multilayer resonators, could further suppress the undesired‐helicity transmission channel and improve the overall CDabs response. In this sense, our present design was intended primarily to elucidate the distinct multipolar origins of the two visible CDabs resonances in a simple asymmetric a‐Si metasurface rather than to maximize the absolute peak CDabs. We note that although the metasurface is not deeply subwavelength over the entire spectral range, additional diffraction efficiency calculations show that the first higher diffraction orders are weak (< 2%) and only appear below ∼500 nm (see Supporting Information S1: Figure S7). Therefore, the CDabs resonances discussed here are dominated by the zeroth‐order transmitted fields. Angle‐resolved studies demonstrate that the metasurface maintains robust chiroptical performance under oblique incidence. The strong CDabs at 652 nm persists over a wide angular range due to the resilience of polarization‐selective quadrupolar modes supported by the combined effects of intrinsic and extrinsic chirality. Meanwhile, the weaker CDabs at 735 nm, arising from a simpler multipolar excitation landscape, exhibits reduced sensitivity to the incident angle highlighting a trade‐off between CDabs magnitude and angular stability. These findings underscore the critical role of multipolar mode engineering in tailoring both the strength and robustness of chiroptical responses. The insights presented here offer clear design guidelines for developing low‐loss polarization‐selective nanophotonic devices and pave the way toward advanced applications in chiral sensing, polarization optics, and integrated photonic systems.

Methods

Simulation

The computational analyses were performed using the COMSOL Multiphysics commercial software suite, which enables precise simulation of field distributions, multipole decomposition, and reflection behavior. The design of the asymmetric gammadion metasurface was numerically approached using the wave optics module. For the calculation of the single meta‐atom design, a unit cell boundary condition was applied along the x‐direction and y‐direction to simulate transmission and phase shift in an array structure. Floquet periodic boundary conditions were set along the x and y axes for normal and oblique incidence. Similarly, the distributions of various electromagnetic fields on each structural cross‐section can also be revealed through the simulation results. To determine the scattering cross‐section, a perfectly matched layer (PML) was utilized in the simulations. The refractive indices of a‐Si and SiO2 were determined through ellipsometry measurements.
