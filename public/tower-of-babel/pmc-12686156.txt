Combining anti-metabolic treatments with the repurposing of eribulin for glioblastoma: a clinical opportunity?
Authors: Étienne Audet-Walsh, Lucas Galey, Samuele Renzi, Nadine Morin, Samer M I Hussein, Maxime Richer, Marc-Étienne Huot
Published: 2025

Glioblastoma (GBM) can either develop de novo or through progression from clinically silent low-grade gliomas or diffuse infiltrating astrocytomas, which usually develop throughout childhood and slowly expand through young adulthood. In most cases, this devastating disease has a high risk of recurrence and morbidity, which mostly affects young adults (1,2). Aggressive treatment typically involves initial maximal surgical resection, followed by radiotherapy with concomitant and maintenance temozolomide (TMZ), in combination with bevacizumab (3). However, once developed, the infiltrative and recurrent nature of GBM accounts for post-chemo/radiotherapy failures and a short median survival (less than 15 months) (1-3). Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop new therapies to improve the survival and the quality of life of GBM patients.

In their recent study, Alcântara et al. studied the impact of eribulin on glioma and GBM cells (4). Eribulin is a drug commonly used for breast cancer chemotherapy (5). This drug is known to efficiently inhibit microtubule polymerization in numerous cancer cells, and the authors tested if it could be repurposed for GBM treatment. To this end, they used several cell lines in vitro, including HOG, T98G, U87MG, U251MG, and U138MG. While low nM concentrations of eribulin were highly efficient in some cell lines, reducing, for example, cell viability <95% in HOG cells, heterogeneity was observed with some cell lines being much less sensitive (e.g., U251MG). These less responsive cell lines are PTEN-mutated or null cell lines, suggesting that activated mTOR/Akt signalling could hinder eribulin’s effect. Still, in most GBM cell lines, eribulin caused a blockade of cell cycle progression with an increase in apoptotic markers, such as cleaved-PARP, and a decrease in migratory capacities. This work highlights the potential therapeutic opportunity of eribulin in specific preclinical models, which is in line with recent in vitro and in vivo studies (6,7). As stated by the authors, their work was motivated by recent evidence possibly linking the efficacy of eribulin in combination with radiotherapy in GBM mice models (8). Given that radiotherapy is part of the standard of care in humans affected by GBM (3), increasing its efficiency is indeed urgently needed.

Interestingly, the authors also discovered that eribulin induces mitochondrial dysfunction, notably generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. These results suggest that one molecular consequence of eribulin is the alteration of mitochondrial biology, consistent with recent findings in breast cancer cells showing that eribulin enhances interactions between mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (9). However, what is the global impact of eribulin on mitochondria is mostly uncharacterized.

This is highly relevant in glioma, as one of its critical features is the expression of mutated isocitrate dehydrogenases (IDHs) that disrupt metabolism and produce the oncometabolite R-2-hydroxyglutarate (2HG) (10-13). IDH wildtype enzymes catalyze the formation of α-ketoglutarate (αKG) from isocitrate, a metabolite involved in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (14). IDH mutations occur specifically in amino acid residues involved in substrate binding (15,16), which confer a neomorphic enzymatic activity leading to the conversion of αKG to the oncogenic enantiomer of 2HG (10-13). Given the importance of the TCA cycle, significant consequences of IDH mutations in cancer cells can be observed, including an intracellular decrease in αKG and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) levels, which limit their metabolic potential and decrease the mitochondrial flexibility of these cancer cells (17,18). This is the consequence of the uncontrolled mutated enzyme, which continuously produces 2HG using αKG as substrate and the NADPH cofactor (12), creating an important mitochondrial metabolic stress in IDH-mutated cells (19). This metabolic stress also has effects beyond the TCA cycle: because of the structural similarity between the two metabolites, 2HG can inhibit αKG-dependent enzymes (20,21). More than 60 enzymes are categorized as αKG-dependent dioxygenases, which include several subfamilies, such as the Jumonji family of (histone) lysine demethylases and the TET enzymes, known to regulate histone and DNA methylation and demethylation (22-24). Hence, IDH mutations and the ensuing production of 2HG directly cause alterations in metabolism and epigenetics (25). Interestingly, PTEN mutations occur predominantly during the later stages of glioma progression (grades 3 and 4). While it has been shown that 2HG induces constitutive activation of mTOR signalling, the level of mTOR signalling activities remains lower than that observed in cells with PTEN or PI3K mutations (12). This suggests that eribulin could be a promising treatment option for low-grade gliomas (grades 1 and 2), as resistance to eribulin has only been associated with activation of the mTOR/AKT pathway.

While eribulin might sensitize GBM cells to radiotherapy, it remains to be determined if co-targeting cell metabolism, in both IDH wildtype and mutated diseases, is a viable therapeutic approach. As eribulin is already approved by health agencies in other cancer settings, this could reveal a new therapeutic approach for patients with GBM, for which new treatments are urgently needed.
