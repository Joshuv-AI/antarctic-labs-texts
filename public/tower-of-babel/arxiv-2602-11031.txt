1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a group, and
\varphi\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
be an automorphism of
G
. Two elements
u,v\in G
are said to be
\varphi
-twisted conjugated, if there exists
x\in G
such that
u=(x\varphi)^{-1}ux
, and we denote it by
u\sim_{\varphi}v
. This twisted conjugacy was first introduced by Reidemeister
[
17
]
from topological view point, and plays a pivotal role in modern Nielsen fixed point theory. One can observe that
\sim_{\varphi}
is an equivalence relation on
G
, and it coincides with the usual conjugation, when we replace
\varphi
by the identity homomorphism.

In a similar manner, one can define endo-twisted conjugacy — for a given endomorphism
\psi\in\operatorname{End}(G)
and two elements
u,v\in G
, we say that
u,v
are
\psi
-twisted conjugated, denoted by
u\sim_{\psi}v
, if there exists
x\in G
such that
u=(x\psi)^{-1}ux
. The algorithmic approach of endo-twisted conjugacy is the following:

Endo-Twisted Conjugacy Problem,
E-TCP(G)
:
For a finite presentation
G=\left\langle X\mid R\right\rangle
,
given an endomorphism
\psi\colon G\to G
(given by the images of the generators), and two words on the generators,
u,v\in F(X)
, decide whether
u
and
v
represent
\psi
-twisted conjugate elements in
G
, i.e.,
u\sim_{\psi}v
.

The central topic of this article is the endo-twisted conjugacy problem for solvable Baumslag–Solitar groups,
BS(1,n)
.

Instead of an endomorphism, if we execute the algorithm with an automorphism as a part of input, then it is known as auto-twisted conjugacy problem or simply by twisted conjugacy problem of
G
, in short TCP(G). The study of Algorithmic Group Theory was initiated with three Dehn Problems (see
[
9
]
), back in 1911, namely the word problem, the conjugacy problem and the isomorphism problem.
E-TCP(G)
or
TCP(G)
generalizes the so-called conjugacy problem,
CP(G)
as discussed above. A positive solution to the twisted conjugacy problem instinctively provides a solution to the conjugacy problem, and hence a solution to the word problem. On the contrary, there exist finitely presented groups with solvable word problem but unsolvable conjugacy problem (see
[
15
]
). Similarly, there exists a finitely presented group with solvable conjugacy problem, but unsolvable twisted conjugacy problem (see
[
3
, Corollary 4.9]
).

It is well-known that the celebrated three Dehn problems are unsolvable in their full generality, but there are substantial intriguing results do exist in the literature solving these three problems in certain families of groups. Let,
1\rightarrow F\rightarrow G\rightarrow H\rightarrow 1
be a given short exact sequence of groups with certain conditions, specifically
F
has solvable auto-twisted conjugacy problem. Bogoploski–Martino–Ventura
[
3
]
proved that
G
has solvable conjugacy problem if and only if the corresponding action subgroup
A\leqslant\operatorname{Aut}(F)
is orbit decidable. As applications, it was shown that the auto-twisted conjugacy problem is solvable for virtually surface groups and for virtually polycyclic groups in
[
3
]
.
There has been an extensive study based on the auto-twisted conjugacy problem. The auto-twisted conjugacy problem for free groups is decidable
[
2
]
and also studied in the other families of groups
[
1
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
14
]
.
But there is only sparse literature on the endo-twisted conjugacy problem. The
endo-twisted conjugacy problem for free groups is known to be decidable for certain non-surjective maps
[
12
]
, in general it is an open problem.

In this article, we study the endo-twisted conjugated problem for
BS(1,n)
and later connect
E-TCP
with the outer fixed points of the endomorphisms of
BS(1,n)
. This connection provides another approach to decide whether two given elements
u,v
of
BS(1,n)
are endo-twisted conjugated, i.e.,
u\sim_{\varphi}v
, where
u,v
and
\varphi\in\operatorname{End}(BS(1,n))
are determined by a suitably constructed endomorphism
\psi\in\operatorname{End}(BS(1,n))
. The later part of this work was motivated by
[
5
]
, where Ciobanu–Logan computed the outer fixed points (not being a proper power) in order to find the primitive element which generates the non-trivial fixed subgroup of monomorphisms of free groups of rank
2
.

The article is organised as follows. We briefly review some preliminaries regarding Baumslag–Solitar groups, specifically solvable Baumslag–Solitar groups,
BS(1,n)=\langle a,t\mid a=t^{-1}a^{n}t\rangle
in Section
2
. This brief survey mainly includes a normal form of its elements, and its semi-direct product structure based on the notations developed in
[
14
]
. Afterwards in Subsection
2.2
, we discuss the construction of two types of endomorphisms, following the results from Collins–Levin
[
6
]
and O’Neil
[
16
]
.
In Section
3
, we prove that the endo-twisted conjugacy problem
E-TCP
(see Theorem
3.2
) is decidable in
BS(1,n)
. In this aspect, we would like to mention that in
[
14
]
Mitra–Roy–Ventura solved the auto-twisted conjugacy problem in
BS(1,n)
.
Finally, in Section

we construct an endomorphism
\psi
in a way so that the intersection
[a]\cap\operatorname{Fix}\psi
is non-empty if and only if certain
u
and
v
are
\varphi
-twisted conjugated, where
u
and
v
,
\varphi\in\operatorname{End}(BS(1,n))
are determined by that
\psi
(see Proposition
4.2
). Later, we define the weakly outer fixed points and prove (see Proposition
4.9
) that the existence of weakly outer fixed points of an endomorphism

\psi
.

General notation and conventions

For a group
G
,
\operatorname{End}(G)
(resp.,
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
) denotes the group of endomorphisms (resp., automorphisms) of
G
. We write them all with the argument on the left, that is, we denote by
(x)\varphi
(or simply
x\varphi
) the image of the element
x
by the homomorphism
\varphi
; accordingly, we denote by
\varphi\psi
the composition
A\xrightarrow{\varphi}B\xrightarrow{\psi}C
. Specifically, we will reserve the letter
\gamma
for right conjugations,
\gamma_{g}\colon G\to G,x\mapsto g^{-1}xg
. Following the same convention, when thinking of a matrix
A
as a map, it will always act on the right of horizontal vectors,
\textbf{v}\mapsto\textbf{v}A
. We denote by
\operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
the linear group over the integers. We use the function
|\cdot|_{a}
to count the number of
a
-occurrences in a word
w
.

2.
Preliminaries on Solvable Baumslag–Solitar groups

The so-called Baumslag–Solitar groups
BS(m,n)
are the class of two-generated one-related groups presented by

BS(m,n)=\left\langle a,t\mid a^{m}=t^{-1}a^{n}t\right\rangle,

for
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
. They all are HNN-extensions of
\mathbb{Z}
(with associated subgroups
m\mathbb{Z}
and
n\mathbb{Z}
) sitting in the middle of the well known short exact sequence

(1)

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}1&\to&\ll\!a\!\gg&\,\to&BS(m,n)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi}}{{\to}}&\mathbb{Z}=\left\langle t\right\rangle&\to&1.\\
&&&&a&\mapsto&1&&\\
&&&&t&\mapsto&t&&\end{array}

The homomorphism
\pi\colon BS(m,n)\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
,
w\mapsto t^{|w|_{t}}
, is well defined because the defining relation is
t
-balanced (not being the case for
a
, unless
m=n
). We note that
\ker\pi=\ll\!a\!\gg\,=\{w(a,t)\mid|w|_{t}=0\}\unlhd BS(m,n)
. The elementary examples include
BS(0,1)\simeq\mathbb{Z}
,
BS(1,1)\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, and
BS(1,-1)=\left\langle a,t\mid a=t^{-1}a^{-1}t\right\rangle
, which is the fundamental group of the Klein bottle.

From now on, we concentrate in the solvable groups within this family, and that is the case when
m=1
. Also, for the rest of the paper, we assume
n\neq\pm 1
.

2.1.
Normal Form

In this Section we recall the normal form of an element in
BS(1,n)
developed in
[
14
, Section 2.1]
and we fix some notations which will be used throughout the paper. The defining relation of
BS(1,n)
is
a=t^{-1}a^{n}t
, which can also be thought as of the forms
ta=a^{n}t
and
ta^{-1}=a^{-n}t
— allows us to accumulate each positive occurrence of
t
to right. Repetitively using these relations, one can easily obtain:

(2)

t^{p}a^{k}=a^{kn^{p}}t^{p},

for every
p,k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
p\geq 0
. Similarly, rewriting the defining relation as
at^{-1}=t^{-1}a^{n}
and
a^{-1}t^{-1}=t^{-1}a^{-n}
tells us that each negative occurrence of
t
’s can always be moved to the left, and so

(3)

a^{k}t^{-p}=t^{-p}a^{kn^{p}},

for every
p,k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
p\geq 0
.

Hence, any element
w(a,t)\in BS(1,n)
can be expressed in the form
g=t^{-p}a^{k}t^{q}
, where
p,k,q\in\mathbb{Z}
,
p,q\geq 0
. Since,
BS(1,n)
is torsion-free, we note that in the group
BS(1,n)
,
t^{-p_{1}}a^{k_{1}}t^{p_{1}+c_{1}}=t^{-p_{2}}a^{k_{2}}t^{p_{2}+c_{2}}
if and only if
c_{1}=c_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
k_{1}/n^{p_{1}}=k_{2}/n^{p_{2}}\in\mathbb{Z}[1/n]
, where
p_{i},k_{i},c_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
p_{i}\geq 0
, for
i=1,2
. It is clear that
c_{1}=c_{2}
is a necessary condition by applying
\pi
from (
1
).
Thus, any element of
BS(1,n)
can be written in the form
g=t^{-p}a^{k}t^{q}=t^{-p}a^{k}t^{p}t^{c}
, where
p,k,c\in\mathbb{Z}
,
p\geq 0
— close to being a normal form.

From now on, we set the following notation: for
\alpha=k/n^{p}\in\mathbb{Z}[1/n]
(here,
k,p\in\mathbb{Z}
,
p\geq 0
) write

a^{\alpha}:=t^{-p}a^{k}t^{p}\in BS(1,n).

The key ideas for setting up this notation are the following: (1) the equality among rational numbers
kn/n^{p+1}=k/n^{p}
corresponds to the equality
t^{-p-1}a^{kn}t^{p+1}=t^{-p}(t^{-1}a^{n}t)^{k}t^{p}=t^{-p}a^{k}t^{p}
among the corresponding elements in
BS(1,n)
; (2) this notation unifies the above two equations (
2
) and (
3
) —
t^{c}
, for
c\in\mathbb{Z}
can be shifted to the right of rational powers of
a
at the price of multiplying its exponent by
n^{c}
.

We record Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
2.2
from
[
14
]
without giving the proofs. Lemma
2.1
proves linearity for this family of groups:

Lemma 2.1
.

The map

(4)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi\colon BS(1,n)&\to&\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}[1/n])\leqslant\operatorname{GL}_{2}({\mathbb{Q}})\\[3.0pt]
a&\mapsto&A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\\[3.0pt]
t&\mapsto&T=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}n&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\end{array}

defines a monomorphism of groups. In particular,
BS(1,n)
is a linear group.

Lemma
2.2
states that the new exponential notation is compatible with the standard rules of computation:

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
\alpha=k/n^{p},\,\beta=\ell/n^{q}\in\mathbb{Z}[1/n]
(
k,\ell,p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
,
p,q\geq 0
) and
c,r\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

(i)

t^{c}a^{\alpha}=a^{\alpha n^{c}}t^{c}
(equivalently,
a^{\alpha}t^{c}=t^{c}a^{\alpha n^{-c}}
);

(ii)

a^{\alpha}a^{\beta}=a^{\alpha+\beta}
;

(iii)

(a^{\alpha})^{r}=a^{r\alpha}
;

(iv)

t^{-c}a^{\alpha}t^{c}=a^{\alpha/n^{c}}
;

(v)

\big(a^{\alpha}t^{c}\big)^{-1}=a^{-\alpha n^{-c}}t^{-c}
;

(vi)

\big(a^{\alpha}t^{c}\big)^{r}=a^{\frac{n^{rc}-1}{n^{c}-1}\alpha}t^{rc}
.

The reader is referred to
[
14
]
for explicit proofs of both the aforementioned Lemmas.

Using this notation and the above arguments, every element
g\in BS(1,n)
can be written, in a unique way, as
g=a^{\alpha}t^{c}
, for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[1/n]
and some
c\in\mathbb{Z}
. Moreover,
\big(a^{\alpha_{1}}t^{c_{1}}\big)\big(a^{\alpha_{2}}t^{c_{2}}\big)=a^{\alpha_{1}+n^{c_{1}}\alpha_{2}}t^{c_{1}+c_{2}}
.

Finally, we conclude this section by discussing the semi-direct product structure of
BS(1,n)
. Clearly,

\ker\pi=\ll\!a\!\gg=\{a^{\alpha}t^{c}\in BS(1,n)\mid c=0\}=\{a^{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[1/n]\}\simeq\mathbb{Z}[1/n].

And so, the short exact sequence (
1
) for
m=1
has the form

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}1&\to&\mathbb{Z}[1/n]&\,\to&BS(1,n)&\to&\mathbb{Z}&\to&1,\end{array}

and it splits because
\mathbb{Z}
is free. In particular,
BS(1,n)\simeq\mathbb{Z}[1/n]\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
, where
\mathbb{Z}=\left\langle t\right\rangle
acts on
\mathbb{Z}[1/n]
by multiplying by
n
. We also observe that
BS(1,n)^{\rm ab}=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/|n-1|\mathbb{Z}
, with
\ll\!a\!\gg
being, for
n\neq 1
, the only normal subgroup whose quotient is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
.

2.2.
Endomorphisms of
BS(1,n)

Following a previous work by Collins–Levin
[
6
]
, J. O’Neill
[
16
]
explicitly described two types of endomorphisms, namely
type-I
and
type-II
, of
BS(1,n)
, where the first type,
type-I
are monomorphisms and the second type,
type-II
contains the subclass of proper retracts. We state here the result without giving its proof.

Proposition 2.3

(J. O’Neill,
[
16
, Proposition 2.1]
)
.

For
n\neq\pm 1
,

\operatorname{End}(BS(1,n))=\{\psi^{I}_{\alpha,\beta,1},\,\psi^{II}_{0,\beta,c}\mid\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}[1/n],\,c\in\mathbb{Z}\}

where, for
\alpha=\ell/n^{k}
,
\beta=q/n^{p}
and
c=r-p
with
k,\ell,p,q,r\in\mathbb{Z}
,
k,p,r\geq 0
,
\psi^{I}_{\alpha,\beta,1}
and
\psi^{II}_{0,\beta,c}
are defined as follows:

(5)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\psi^{I}_{\alpha,\beta,1}\colon BS(1,n)&\to&BS(1,n)\\
a&\mapsto&a^{\alpha}=t^{-k}a^{\ell}t^{k}\\
t&\mapsto&a^{\beta}t=t^{-p}a^{q}t^{p+1};\end{array}

(6)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\psi^{II}_{0,\beta,c}\colon BS(1,n)&\to&BS(1,n)\\
a&\mapsto&a^{0}=1\\
t&\mapsto&a^{\beta}t^{c}=t^{-p}a^{q}t^{r}=t^{-p}a^{q}t^{p}t^{r-p},\end{array}

for
\psi^{II}_{0,\beta,c}
, if
q
is a multiple of
n
, then
p=0
or
r=0
.

Observation 2.4
.

Let
\psi^{II}_{0,\beta,c}
be a type-II endomorphism, where
\beta=q/n^{p}
and
c=r-p
with
p,q,r\in\mathbb{Z}
,
p,r\geq 0
. If
q
is a multiple of
n
, i.e.,
q=kn
, for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then
p=0
or
r=0
. Further, if
p=0
, then
\psi^{II}_{0,\beta,c}=\psi^{II}_{0,\beta^{\prime},c^{\prime}}
, where
\beta^{\prime}=kn
and
c^{\prime}=r
—
\psi^{II}_{0,\beta^{\prime},c^{\prime}}\,\colon a\mapsto 1,\,t\mapsto a^{kn}t^{r}
. And if
r=0
, then
\psi^{II}_{0,\beta,c}=\psi^{II}_{0,\beta^{\prime\prime},c^{\prime\prime}}
, where
\beta^{\prime\prime}=q/n^{p}
and
c^{\prime\prime}=-p
—
\psi^{II}_{0,\beta^{\prime\prime},c^{\prime\prime}}\,\colon a\mapsto 1,\,t\mapsto a^{q/n^{p}}t^{-p}
. Thus, independent of the fact whether
q
is a multiple of
n
or not, any type-II endomorphism of
BS(1,n)
is of the form
\psi^{II}_{0,\beta,c}
, where
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}[1/n]
and
c\in\mathbb{Z}
.

The following Proposition captures how any two endomorphisms in
BS(1,n)
compose in the our notation of ‘normal form’.

Lemma 2.5
.

The composition rules of type-I and type-II endomorphisms are the following:

(i)

\psi^{I}_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},1}\circ\psi^{I}_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{2},1}=\psi^{I}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2},\beta_{1}\alpha_{2}+\beta_{2},1}
;

(ii)

\psi^{II}_{0,\beta_{1},c_{1}}\circ\psi^{II}_{0,\beta_{2},c_{2}}=\psi^{II}_{0,\mu\beta_{2},c_{1}c_{2}}
, where
\mu=\frac{n^{c_{1}c_{2}}-1}{n^{c_{2}}-1}
;

(iii)

\psi^{II}_{0,\beta_{1},c_{1}}\circ\psi^{I}_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{2},1}=\psi^{II}_{0,\beta_{1}\alpha_{2}+\mu_{1}\beta_{2},c_{1}}
, where
\mu_{1}=\frac{n^{c_{1}}-1}{n-1}
;

(iv)

\psi^{I}_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},1}\circ\psi^{II}_{0,\beta_{2},c_{2}}=\psi^{II}_{0,\beta_{2},c_{2}}
.

Proof.

First we check that both the maps type-I,
\psi^{I}_{\alpha,\beta,1}
and type-II,
\psi^{II}_{0,\beta,c}
are well defined by proving that they preserve the defining relation:

\displaystyle a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\psi^{I}_{\alpha,\beta,1}}}{{\quad\mapsto\quad}}

\displaystyle a^{\alpha},

\displaystyle t^{-1}a^{n}t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\psi^{I}_{\alpha,\beta,1}}}{{\quad\mapsto\quad}}

\displaystyle\big(a^{\beta}t\big)^{-1}\big(a^{\alpha}\big)^{n}\big(a^{\beta}t\big)=t^{-1}a^{-\beta}a^{n\alpha}a^{\beta}t=t^{-1}a^{n\alpha}t=a^{\alpha}.

It is very straightforward for the type-II:

\displaystyle a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\psi^{II}_{0,\beta,c}}}{{\quad\mapsto\quad}}

\displaystyle 1,

\displaystyle t^{-1}a^{n}t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\psi^{II}_{0,\beta,c}}}{{\quad\mapsto\quad}}

\displaystyle\big(a^{\beta}t^{c}\big)^{-1}\,1\,\big(a^{\beta}t^{c}\big)=1.

Now let us check that the composition
\psi^{I}_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},1}\circ\psi^{I}_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{2},1}
equals
\psi^{I}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2},\beta_{1}\alpha_{2}+\beta_{2},1}
. In fact, for
\alpha_{1}={\ell}_{1}/n^{k_{1}}
,
\beta_{1}=q_{1}/n^{p_{1}}
and
\alpha_{2}={\ell}_{2}/n^{k_{2}}
,
\beta_{2}=q_{2}/n^{p_{2}}
, with
k_{i},\ell_{i},p_{i},q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
k_{i},p_{i}\geq 0
,
i=1,2
, we have

\displaystyle a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\psi^{I}_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},1}}}{{\qquad\mapsto\qquad}}t^{-k_{1}}a^{\ell_{1}}t^{k_{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\psi^{I}_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{2},1}}}{{\qquad\mapsto\qquad}}

\displaystyle\big(a^{\beta_{2}}t\big)^{-k_{1}}\big(a^{\alpha_{2}}\big)^{\ell_{1}}\big(a^{\beta_{2}}t\big)^{k_{1}}=

\displaystyle=a^{\frac{n^{-k_{1}}-1}{n-1}\beta_{2}}t^{-k_{1}}a^{\ell_{1}\alpha_{2}}a^{\frac{n^{k_{1}}-1}{n-1}\beta_{2}}t^{k_{1}}

\displaystyle=a^{\frac{n^{-k_{1}}-1}{n-1}\beta_{2}}a^{n^{-k_{1}}\ell_{1}\alpha_{2}}a^{n^{-k_{1}}\frac{n^{k_{1}}-1}{n-1}\beta_{2}}

\displaystyle=a^{\frac{n^{-k_{1}}-1}{n-1}\beta_{2}+\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}+\frac{1-n^{-k_{1}}}{n-1}\beta_{2}}

\displaystyle=a^{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}},

and

\displaystyle t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\psi^{I}_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},1}}}{{\qquad\mapsto\qquad}}t^{-p_{1}}a^{q_{1}}t^{p_{1}+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\psi^{I}_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{2},1}}}{{\qquad\mapsto\qquad}}

\displaystyle\big(a^{\beta_{2}}t\big)^{-p_{1}}\big(a^{\alpha_{2}}\big)^{q_{1}}\big(a^{\beta_{2}}t\big)^{p_{1}+1}=

\displaystyle=a^{\frac{n^{-p_{1}}-1}{n-1}\beta_{2}}t^{-p_{1}}a^{q_{1}\alpha_{2}}a^{\frac{n^{p_{1}+1}-1}{n-1}\beta_{2}}t^{p_{1}+1}

\displaystyle=a^{\frac{n^{-p_{1}}-1}{n-1}\beta_{2}}a^{n^{-p_{1}}q_{1}\alpha_{2}}a^{n^{-p_{1}}\frac{n^{p_{1}+1}-1}{n-1}\beta_{2}}t

\displaystyle=a^{\frac{n^{-p_{1}}-1}{n-1}\beta_{2}+\beta_{1}\alpha_{2}+\frac{n-n^{-p_{1}}}{n-1}\beta_{2}}t

\displaystyle=a^{\beta_{1}\alpha_{2}+\beta_{2}}t.

Therefore,
\psi^{I}_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},1}\circ\psi^{I}_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{2},1}=\psi^{I}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2},\beta_{1}\alpha_{2}+\beta_{2},1}
, as we wanted to see in (i).

The following part of the proof contains the composition rule when both the endomorphisms are of type-II:

\displaystyle a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\psi^{II}_{0,\beta_{1},c_{1}}}}{{\qquad\mapsto\qquad}}1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\psi^{II}_{0,\beta_{2},c_{2}}}}{{\qquad\mapsto\qquad}}

\displaystyle 1,

and

\displaystyle t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\psi^{II}_{0,\beta_{1},c_{1}}}}{{\qquad\mapsto\qquad}}a^{\beta_{1}}t^{c_{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\psi^{II}_{0,\beta_{2},c_{2}}}}{{\qquad\mapsto\qquad}}

\displaystyle\big(a^{\beta_{2}}t^{c_{2}}\big)^{c_{1}}\,\,=\,\,a^{\frac{n^{c_{1}c_{2}}-1}{n^{c_{2}}-1}\beta_{2}}t^{c_{1}c_{2}}\,\,=\,\,a^{\mu\beta_{2}}t^{c_{1}c_{2}}.

Hence,
\psi^{II}_{0,\beta_{1},c_{1}}\circ\psi^{II}_{0,\beta_{2},c_{2}}=\psi^{II}_{0,\mu\beta_{2},c_{1}c_{2}}
, as announced in (ii).

).

Let
x\in[a]\cap\operatorname{Fix}\psi^{I}
, also let
w\in BS(1,n)
such that
x=w^{-1}aw
. Then we have the following

w^{-1}aw\,=\,(w^{-1}aw)\psi^{I}\,=\,u(w\varphi_{z})^{-1}\,a\,(w\varphi_{z})u^{-1},

where the first equality holds as
w^{-1}aw\in\operatorname{Fix}\psi^{I}
, and the second equality comes from the previous calculation. This in turn implies that

(w\varphi_{z})u^{-1}w^{-1}a\,w\,u(w\varphi_{z})^{-1}\,=\,a.

Thus, we get
(w\varphi_{z})u^{-1}w^{-1}
centralises the generator
a
. As
BS(1,n)\simeq\mathbb{Z}[1/n]\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
, there exist some
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[1/n]
such that
(w\varphi_{z})u^{-1}w^{-1}=a^{\alpha}
, and so
u^{-1}=(w\varphi_{z})^{-1}a^{\alpha}w
. Hence, the announced result follows.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[1/n]
be given and
\psi^{I}\in\operatorname{End}(BS(1,n))
be defined as in
4.2
. Then it is algorithmically decidable whether
[a]\cap\operatorname{Fix}\psi^{I}
is non-empty.

Proof.

We note that from the construction of
\psi^{I}
,
u\in BS(1,n)
and
\varphi_{z}\in\operatorname{End}(BS(1,n))
are given; also from the hypothesis
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[1/n]
is given. Applying Theorem
3.2
, it is algorithmically decidable whether
u^{-1}\sim_{\varphi_{z}}a^{\alpha}
for given
u,\alpha,\varphi_{z}
. Now
u^{-1}\sim_{\varphi_{z}}a^{\alpha}
if and only if there exists
w\in BS(1,n)
such that
u^{-1}=(w\varphi_{z})^{-1}a^{\alpha}w
. Then,
w^{-1}aw\in[a]\cap\operatorname{Fix}\psi^{I}
. As we already computed
(w^{-1}aw)\psi^{I}\,=\,u(w\varphi_{z})^{-1}\,a\,(w\varphi_{z})u^{-1}
, and from Proposition
4.2
,
(w^{-1}aw)\psi^{I}=w^{-1}aw
, i.e.,
w^{-1}aw\in[a]\cap\operatorname{Fix}\psi^{I}
, if and only if
u^{-1}\sim_{\varphi_{z}}a^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[1/n]
. This completes the proof.
∎

We note that as
a
is mapped to
a
by
\varphi_{z}
and
a
is mapped to
uau^{-1}
by the newly constructed homomorphism
\psi^{I}
. So, we are only focusing on type-I endomorphisms in this section as we mentioned before.

Now, we introduce the notion of weakly (outer) fixed points to study how the existence of weakly (outer) fixed points guarantees the endo-twisted conjugacy.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
G
be a group and
\varphi\in\operatorname{End}(G)
. A
weakly fixed point
of
\varphi
is an element
g\in G
such that
g\varphi\sim a
, where
a
is a fixed point of
\varphi
.

Construction 4.5
.

Let
\widetilde{\psi}^{I}
be an arbitrary type-I endomorphism of
BS(1,n)
such that
a\widetilde{\psi}^{I}=uau^{-1}
and
t\widetilde{\psi}^{I}=v
. Let
x=u^{-1}vu
and
\varphi_{x}\in\operatorname{End}(BS(1,n))\colon a\mapsto a,t\mapsto x
. Note that the constructions of
\widetilde{\psi}^{I}
and
\varphi_{x}
(a type-I endomorphism) are same as of
\psi^{I}
and
\varphi_{z}
respectively (see Proposition
4.2
), except that
|u|_{t}
is not necessarily equal to
0
. Also, note that if we consider
v=a^{\beta}t
for some
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}[1/n]
, then
x=a^{\beta^{\prime}}t
for some
\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}[1/n]
; and so,
t\widetilde{\psi}^{I}=v=uxu^{-1}=ua^{\beta^{\prime}}tu^{-1}
. Set,
\mu=\frac{n^{d}-1}{n-1}
.

The image of an arbitrary element
a^{\gamma}t^{d}
by
\widetilde{\psi}^{I}
is the following:

\begin{array}[]{rl}(a^{\gamma}t^{d})\widetilde{\psi}^{I}\,=&u\,a^{\gamma}\,u^{-1}(uxu^{-1})^{d}\\
=&u\,a^{\gamma}\,(a^{\beta^{\prime}}t)^{d}\,u^{-1}\\
=&u\,a^{\gamma}\,a^{\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\,u^{-1}\\
=&u\,a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\,u^{-1}\end{array}

Observation 4.6
.

Let us consider
\widetilde{\psi}^{I}\in\operatorname{End}(BS(1,n))
defined as above. Then, all the weakly fixed points of
\widetilde{\psi}^{I}
are of the form
a^{\gamma}t^{d}
where
a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\sim a^{\alpha}t^{d}
and
a^{\alpha}t^{d}
is a fixed point
\widetilde{\psi}^{I}
.

Definition 4.7
.

Let
G
be a group and
\varphi\in\operatorname{End}(G)
. Fix
a\in\operatorname{Fix}\varphi
. An element
x\in G
is a
weakly outer fixed point related to
a
of
\varphi
if there exists
g\in G
such that
(g^{-1}xg)\varphi=g^{-1}ag
.
In general,
x
is a
weakly outer fixed point
of
\varphi
if there exists
g\in G
such that
(g^{-1}xg)\varphi=g^{-1}ag
for some fixed point
a
of
\varphi
.

Observation 4.8
.

If
x
is a weakly outer fixed point of an endomorphism
\varphi
of
G
, then
x
is a weakly fixed point of
\varphi
.

The following Proposition connects the weakly fixed points of
\widetilde{\psi}^{I}
and the twisted conjugacy problem in
BS(1,n)
.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
\widetilde{\psi}^{I}\in\operatorname{End}(BS(1,n))
be defined as in
4.5
and
a^{\alpha}t^{d}\in\operatorname{Fix}\widetilde{\psi}^{I}
. For arbitrary elements
g_{1},g_{2}\in BS(1,n)
if

(g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}g_{1})\psi^{I}=g_{1}^{-1}a^{\alpha}t^{d}g_{1}

holds then
u^{-1}=(g_{1}\varphi_{x})^{-1}g^{\prime}g_{1}
, where
g^{\prime}\in BS(1,n)
is determined by
\psi^{I}
.

Proof.

Let
g_{2}=a^{\gamma}t^{d}
, where
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}[1/n]
,
d\in\mathbb{Z}
. We assume that

(17)

(g_{1}^{-1}a^{\gamma}t^{d}g_{1})\psi^{I}=g_{1}^{-1}a^{\alpha}t^{d}g_{1}

holds, and so
a^{\gamma}t^{d}
is a weakly fixed point of
\psi^{I}
. From Observation
4.6

a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\sim a^{\alpha}t^{d}
, and let
g^{\prime}=a^{\delta}t^{r}
be the right conjugator, i.e.,

(18)

a^{\alpha}t^{d}=(a^{\delta}t^{r})^{-1}a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\,(a^{\delta}t^{r}).

From the construction of
\widetilde{\psi}^{I}
,

\begin{array}[]{rl}(g_{1}^{-1}a^{\gamma}t^{d}g_{1})\widetilde{\psi}^{I}\,=&g_{1}^{-1}(a\widetilde{\psi}^{I},t\widetilde{\psi}^{I})\,(a^{\gamma}t^{d})\widetilde{\psi}^{I}\,g_{1}(a\widetilde{\psi}^{I},t\widetilde{\psi}^{I})\\
=&g_{1}^{-1}(uau^{-1},uxu^{-1})\,u\,a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\,u^{-1}\,g_{1}(uau^{-1},uxu^{-1})\\
=&u\,g_{1}^{-1}(a,x)\,u^{-1}\,u\,a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\,u^{-1}\,u\,g_{1}(a,x)\,u^{-1}\\
=&u(g_{1}\varphi_{x})^{-1}\,a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\,(g_{1}\varphi_{x})u^{-1}\end{array}

Thus using (
17
) and (
18
) in one hand, we have

(19)

(g_{1}^{-1}a^{\gamma}t^{d}g_{1})\widetilde{\psi}^{I}\,=g_{1}^{-1}(a^{\delta}t^{r})^{-1}a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\,(a^{\delta}t^{r})g_{1}.

And, on the other hand,

(20)

(g_{1}^{-1}a^{\gamma}t^{d}g_{1})\widetilde{\psi}^{I}\,=u(g_{1}\varphi_{x})^{-1}\,a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\,(g_{1}\varphi_{x})u^{-1}.

Altogether from (
19
) and (
20
) we deduce the following:

(g_{1}\varphi_{x})\,u^{-1}\,g_{1}^{-1}\,(a^{\delta}t^{r})^{-1}\,a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}=a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\,(g_{1}\varphi_{x})\,u^{-1}\,g_{1}^{-1}\,(a^{\delta}t^{r})^{-1}

Hence,
(g_{1}\varphi_{x})\,u^{-1}\,g_{1}^{-1}\,(a^{\delta}t^{r})^{-1}=1
, which in turn implies that
u^{-1}=(g_{1}\varphi_{x})^{-1}(a^{\delta}t^{r})g_{1}
. This completes the proof.
∎

The following Corollary connects the weakly outer fixed points of
\widetilde{\psi}^{I}
and the endo-twisted conjugacy in
BS(1,n)
.

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
\widetilde{\psi}^{I}\in\operatorname{End}(BS(1,n))
be defined as in
4.5
and
a^{\alpha}t^{d}\in\operatorname{Fix}\widetilde{\psi}^{I}
. Let
w
be the weakly outer fixed point relative to
a^{\alpha}t^{d}
of
\widetilde{\psi}^{I}
. Then
u^{-1}=(g\varphi_{x})^{-1}g^{\prime}g
, where
g,g^{\prime}\in BS(1,n)
determined by
\widetilde{\psi}^{I}
and
a^{\alpha}t^{d}
.

Proof.

Since
x
is the weakly outer fixed point relative to
a^{\alpha}t^{d}
of
\widetilde{\psi}^{I}
, there exists
g\in BS(1,n)
such that
(g^{-1}xg)\widetilde{\psi}^{I}=g^{-1}a^{\alpha}t^{d}g
. Let
w=a^{\gamma}t^{d}\in BS(1,n)
, where
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}[1/n],\,d\in\mathbb{Z}
. We have already showed that
(a^{\gamma}t^{d})\widetilde{\psi}^{I}=ua^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}u^{-1}
. Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{rl}(g^{-1}a^{\gamma}t^{d}g)\widetilde{\psi}^{I}\,=&g^{-1}(a\widetilde{\psi}^{I},t\widetilde{\psi}^{I})\,(a^{\gamma}t^{d})\widetilde{\psi}^{I}\,g(a\widetilde{\psi}^{I},t\widetilde{\psi}^{I})\\
=&g^{-1}(uau^{-1},uxu^{-1})\,u\,a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\,u^{-1}\,g(uau^{-1},uxu^{-1})\\
=&u\,g^{-1}(a,x)\,u^{-1}\,u\,a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\,u^{-1}\,u\,g(a,x)\,u^{-1}\\
=&u(g\varphi_{x})^{-1}\,a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\,(g\varphi_{x})u^{-1}\end{array}

From the hypothesis and Observation
4.8

a^{\gamma}t^{d}
is a weakly fixed point of
\widetilde{\psi}^{I}
and so by Observation
4.6
,
a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\sim a^{\alpha}t^{d}
with right conjugator
g^{\prime}
. In other words,

a^{\alpha}t^{d}=(g^{\prime})^{-1}a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}g^{\prime}.

Replacing
a^{\alpha}t^{d}
by above expression, in one hand we have,

(21)

(g^{-1}a^{\gamma}t^{d}g)\widetilde{\psi}^{I}=g^{-1}a^{\alpha}t^{d}g=g^{-1}(g^{\prime})^{-1}a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}g^{\prime}g.

On the other hand,

(22)

(g^{-1}a^{\gamma}t^{d}g)\widetilde{\psi}^{I}=u(g\varphi_{x})^{-1}\,a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\,(g\varphi_{x})u^{-1}.

Combining (
21
) and (
22
) we have the following:

\begin{array}[]{ccl}g^{-1}(g^{\prime})^{-1}a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}g^{\prime}g&=&u(g\varphi_{x})^{-1}\,a^{\gamma+\mu\beta^{\prime}}t^{d}\,(g\varphi_{x})u^{-1}\\
\Rightarrow(g\varphi_{x})u^{-1}g^{-1}(g^{\prime})^{-1}&=&1\end{array}

Hence,
u^{-1}=(g\varphi_{x})^{-1}g^{\prime}g
and we get the announced result.
∎
