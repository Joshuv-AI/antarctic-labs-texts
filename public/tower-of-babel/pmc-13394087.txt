Neurochemical Endpoints to Inform Early‐Stage Trials of Spinocerebellar Ataxia 2 and 3 in a Multisite Setting
Authors: James M. Joers, Yaqiao Wei, Dinesh K. Deelchand, Adam Berrington, Young Woo Park, Guita Banan, Michal Povazan, Jens T. Rosenberg, André J. W. van der Kouwe, Tetsuo Ashizawa, Khalaf O. Bushara, Chiadi U. Onyike, Sub H. Subramony, Jeremy D. Schmahmann, Brian J. Soher, Peter B. Barker, Thomas H. Mareci, Eva‐Maria Ratai, Lynn E. Eberly, Gülin Öz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveNeurochemical levels measured by brain MR spectroscopy (MRS) have been proposed as endpoints for clinical trials in early‐stage spinocerebellar ataxia (SCA) trials. We tested their trial‐readiness by quantifying neurochemicals in three affected brain regions in early‐stage cohorts of SCA2 and SCA3, examining their reproducibility in both patients and controls in a multisite/multivendor setting, and determining if the same neurochemical outcomes can be used as endpoints for different SCA genotypes.MethodsSeventy‐two participants (18 SCA2, mean Scale for the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia (SARA) = 9 +/− 5; 25 SCA3, mean SARA = 10 +/− 6; 29 controls) underwent 3 T scans at four sites using scanners from two vendors and a harmonized MRS protocol. Scans were performed twice with a maximum 2‐week interval. We measured neurochemical concentrations in the cerebellum, pons, and putamen, and assessed their within‐ and between‐site reproducibility and associations with clinician‐ and patient‐reported outcomes.ResultsTest–retest coefficients of variance were comparable across sites and vendors, but concentration estimates had to be adjusted for sites to account for small vendor‐based biases. Total N‐acetylaspartate (tNAA) and glutamate were lower, and myo‐inositol (mI) and total creatine (tCr) were higher in the cerebellum and pons in both SCAs vs. controls. Pontine tNAA and mI, and cerebellar tNAA, mI, and glutamate levels were significantly different between SCA2 and SCA3, with strong effect sizes (|Cohen's d| = 1.1–2.1). The tNAA/tCr ratio had the strongest associations with clinical outcomes (|r| ≥ 0.5), with stronger associations in the cerebellum for SCA2 and in the pons for SCA3.InterpretationThese data support the use of tNAA and its ratios in a genotype‐specific manner in early‐stage SCA2 and SCA3 trials in the multisite/multivendor setting.

Introduction

Spinocerebellar ataxias (SCAs) are rare inherited neurodegenerative diseases that degrade cerebellar control of coordination, balance, gait, and speech, eye movements, and non‐ataxic features that include neuropathy, dystonia, Parkinsonism, and impairments in cognitive and emotional processing [1, 2, 3, 4]. Several common SCAs, including SCA2 and SCA3, are caused by CAG repeat expansion mutations. The pathological expansion of the CAG repeat in the ATXN2 gene results in misfolding of ataxin 2, an RNA‐binding protein, in SCA2, and CAG expansion in ATXN3 causes misfolding of ataxin 3, a deubiquitinating enzyme, in SCA3. Despite widespread expression of ATXN2 and ATXN3, both SCA2 and SCA3 exhibit selective regional neurodegeneration [5, 6], with the pons, brainstem, and cerebellum undergoing substantial atrophy, as shown by structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at mid‐to‐late disease stages [7, 8, 9, 10]. These volumetric MRI outcomes have been evaluated in multisite settings as candidate trial endpoints [11].

The benefits of early interventions prior to substantial tissue loss have been demonstrated in SCA mouse models [12]. Morphological changes in preataxic or early‐stage SCAs can be subtle, however, motivating investigations of additional imaging approaches to quantify disease processes that precede atrophy. Recent work demonstrated that neurochemical and microstructural differences measured by magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) and diffusion MRI can be detected at an early stage [13, 14, 15] and may be more sensitive than volumetric measures for the detection of early cerebral and cerebellar pathology in SCAs [15, 16].

Multiple strategies are in the therapeutic pipeline for common SCAs [17, 18, 19], including SCA2 and SCA3. These two ataxia genotypes have overlapping but different phenotypic and pathological characteristics [6, 17]. They share the cerebellar motor syndrome of ataxic gait, dysarthric speech, and limb dysmetria, but slowed eye movements are an early feature of SCA2, and dystonia may be prominent in young‐onset SCA3 patients. In these genotypes, the cerebellum and pons exhibit morphologic alterations before ataxia manifests [11, 15]. In this MRS study, we focused on both regions in early‐stage cohorts. We also obtained single‐voxel MRS measurements from the putamen based on a previous imaging and autopsy report describing degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the striatum in SCA2 and SCA3 [20].

Primary neurochemical targets for the pons and cerebellum in this study have demonstrated differences in early‐stage disease [13, 15], and are candidate endpoints for upcoming interventional trials of SCAs: myo ‐Inositol (mI) is a purported glial osmolyte, with increased concentrations associated with inflammation under cell stress. mI is elevated in both SCA2 and SCA3, with a distinct elevation in the cerebellum in SCA2 [13, 21, 22]. Glutamate (Glu) is the brain's most abundant excitatory neurotransmitter, but a larger pool of brain Glu (detected by MRS) is involved in cellular metabolism, including amino acid synthesis (e.g., GABA), nitrogen transport, and participation in the TCA cycle. Glu concentration has been shown to be decreased in SCA2 and SCA3 [13] and decreased with disease severity in SCA mouse models [23]. The sum of creatine and phosphocreatine (total creatine, tCr) may indicate energy stores available in the pool to regenerate cellular ATP. An increase in tCr has also been associated with an increase in gliosis [24]. Increases in tCr have been reported in SCA2 and SCA3 [13, 21, 22]. The sum of N‐acetylaspartate and N‐acetylaspartyl‐glutamate (total NAA, tNAA) is a marker of neuronal integrity. Reductions in tNAA and Glu and increases in mI and tCr were shown in both SCAs, but to a greater extent in SCA2 than SCA3 at the same clinical stage [13, 21, 22]. Secondary target neurochemicals are reliably measured on 3T scanners and included N‐acetylaspartate (NAA), choline‐containing compounds (tCho), the sum of glutamate and glutamine (Glx) and glucose plus taurine (Glc+Tau). Importantly, prior work [15, 23, 25, 26] has demonstrated that measures of atrophy and metabolite levels provide independent information at different SCA disease stages, that is, the abnormal metabolite levels are not merely a reflection of tissue loss.

To achieve enrollment targets, rare disease trials are conducted in the multisite setting. Multisite studies facilitate the attainment of targeted enrollment and enable the study of geographically unique cohorts that enhance the generalizability of findings. Additionally, cross‐site comparisons reveal site‐specific biases that may influence data and its interpretation. These benefits are crucial to the development of imaging biomarkers for diseases such as SCA. The Clinical Research Consortium for the Study of Cerebellar Ataxia (CRC‐SCA) [27, 28] was formed to provide infrastructure for cerebellar ataxia trials and to facilitate the development of disease‐modifying therapies. We deployed a harmonized MRS protocol across 4 sites of the CRC‐SCA that utilize scanners from two different MR manufacturers to cross‐sectionally study individuals with SCA2 or SCA3 and healthy controls. To inform trial design by determining the most promising 3 T MRS outcomes, we enrolled early‐stage SCA2 and SCA3 participants and evaluated the reproducibility of neurochemical measurements within and between sites and MR vendors.

Participants/Materials and Methods

Participants

Participants were enrolled from May 2017 to May 2022 at 4 CRC‐SCA sites: Massachusetts General Hospital, Johns Hopkins University, the University of Florida, and the University of Minnesota. Inclusion criteria were genetic confirmation of SCA2 or SCA3 and early‐to‐moderate disease stage for patients (Scale for the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia, [29] (SARA) score ≤ 15) and no neurological conditions for control participants. In addition to MR contraindications (e.g., claustrophobia, presence of metal implants), exclusion criteria were seizures, diabetes, alcoholism, cigarette smoking, brain tumor, or restless leg syndrome.

After protocol approval from each site's Institutional Review Board, participants were enrolled after providing informed consent. Individuals with SCA2 and SCA3 were asked to hold ataxia and anti‐Parkinsonian medications (when the latter was used for non‐seizure conditions) for 24 h and 12 h, respectively, prior to study activities. All participants were asked to hold antioxidant supplements (e.g., vitamins C and E, coenzyme Q) for three weeks prior to testing. Sample size was ultimately determined by feasibility during the COVID‐19 pandemic, with a minimum requirement of 5 participants with SCA and 5 controls at each site.

Clinician‐ and Patient‐Reported Outcome Assessments

Disease burden was estimated using two validated scales that were administered on the same day as the first MR session. The SARA [16] (version 5) is an observational outcome measure involving several aspects of coordination and speech disturbances and was administered by certified study personnel. The FARS‐ADL [30], Friedreich Ataxia Rating Scale—Activities of Daily Living survey, is a self‐reported outcome measure for the assessment of the effects of ataxia on performing daily tasks. Higher scores in both scales indicate higher disease burden, with SARA ranging from 0 to 40 and FARS‐ADL ranging from 0 to 36. Disease duration was computed based on self‐reported onset of gait impairment.

MR Data Acquisition

Enrolling sites used 3 T MR systems manufactured by Philips Medical Systems (Ingenia Elition X; Best, The Netherlands) and Siemens Healthineers (Prisma Fit; Erlangen, Germany). The Philips systems operated under software versions 5.6.1 (Florida) and 5.5.2, 5.6.1, and 5.7.1 (Johns Hopkins), and the Siemens systems under VE11C. Data acquisition for both vendors used a body coil transmit and a vendor‐specific 32‐channel receive coil. Study participation involved two neuroimaging sessions (test/retest), with a single session being completed within 60 min.

Vendor‐specific alignment protocols (Philips, SmartScan; Siemens, AutoAlign) enabled images of the head to be reproducibly aligned and acquired across the two time points. An aligned sagittal 1 mm isotropic 3D T1 image set was acquired using vendor‐supplied sequences: Philips: Fast field echo, TR/TE = 10.1/3.4 ms; Siemens: MPRAGE, TR/TE/TI = 2430/3.65/1100 ms.

Voxel placement was achieved using AutoVOI [31], a pipeline that registers a scanner‐created NIfTI 3D T1 image to MNI [32] space and uses a library of predefined voxels to calculate the coordinates of the requested MRS voxel. AutoVOI ran remotely on a server located at the University of Minnesota. Specific MRS voxel locations were: Right hemispheric cerebellar white matter (CBWM) (17 × 17 × 17 mm3), the pons (16 × 16 × 16 mm3), and the right putamen (25 × 11 × 10 mm3). If needed, MR operators could override AutoVOI to translate and/or rotate AutoVOI‐calculated voxels to best capture the desired volume of interest (VOI), but were instructed not to resize the MRS voxel. Representative voxel positions from a randomly chosen control participant are shown in Figure S1.

A vendor‐harmonized sLASER sequence compatible with AutoVOI, as previously demonstrated [33, 34] was implemented across sites, using TR/TE = 5000/30 ms to collect 64 individually‐saved water suppressed transients and four unsuppressed water transients per VOI, resulting in an acquisition time of under 6:00 per MRS voxel.

MR Data Handling

Each site uploaded MR data to a Flywheel instance (flywheel.io, Minneapolis, MN) to provide centralized access for data analysis. See Supplementary Methods for additional details regarding vendor differences in data handling.

MRS Data Processing and Quantification

Structural 3D T1 images were segmented into gray matter, white matter, and CSF using SPM12 [35]. Binary voxel masks were generated for each dataset using custom MATLAB (Natick, MA) scripts. CSF voxel fraction was estimated by applying the binary mask to the tissue probability maps.

MRS data were preprocessed using MRspa (Dinesh Deelchand, MRspa: Magnetic Resonance signal processing and analysis, available at: https://www.cmrr.umn.edu/downloads/mrspa/ (accessed March 17, 2026)). Philips 32‐channel single‐shot spectra were weighted to match the customized coil‐combination approach used in the Siemens online reconstruction. Single‐shot spectra were eddy current, phase, and frequency corrected before summation. The resulting spectrum, along with the corresponding unsuppressed water reference, CSF voxel fraction, and tissue water T2 estimates (pons, 101 ms; CBWM, 99 ms; putamen, 100 ms), were included as input to LCModel to adjust metabolite concentrations [36]. Additional details regarding vendor‐specific aspects of data preprocessing are included in Supplementary Methods.

Spectra were batch‐processed by VOI using LCModel (Version 6.3‐1 N) with water‐scaling. A basis set of 19 simulated metabolites (Table S1) and experimentally‐measured macromolecules was used to fit spectra over the spectral range of 0.5–4.2 parts‐per‐million (ppm) to estimate metabolite concentrations and their associated lower limits of variance (Cramér‐Rao lower bounds, CRLB). In addition to single metabolite concentrations, five neurochemical sums were also estimated (tCho, tCr, tNAA, Glx, and Glc + Tau).

To avoid quantification biases, individual spectra were included in the statistical analysis if the unsuppressed water peak linewidth was less than 13Hz [37] and a visual inspection of the binary mask over the 3D T1 image confirmed correct voxel placement. Metabolites with an LCModel mean CRLB under 20% for a single group in each VOI for both the test and retest sessions were deemed reliably quantifiable in that VOI and were included in statistical analysis across all groups, even if the mean CRLB for other groups did not satisfy this criterion. The metabolites meeting these requirements were: Glu, mI, NAA, tNAA, tCho, tCr, Glx, and Glc + Tau. Of these, Glc + Tau was not reliably quantified in the putamen.

Measurement reproducibility of estimated concentrations was determined by test–retest coefficient of variation (CV, the standard deviation of a region‐specific metabolite concentration divided by the mean value of the same region‐specific metabolite concentration for a given group) for each metabolite across sites.

Differences in neurochemical concentrations for each VOI were analyzed using a linear mixed model (LMM) that used subject group, sex, age, and site (which included an MR vendor component) for fixed effects, with the random effect being the individual. Differences among group coefficients in each model were then compared, and their p‐values calculated. Cohen's d was calculated for pairwise group comparisons of regional neurochemical concentrations. Correlations for each of the three clinical measures (SARA, FARS‐ADL, and disease duration) with regional neurometabolite concentrations were estimated separately for each VOI and SCA group using bootstrapping. VOI‐group‐specific data containing both visits were resampled and replaced 25 times (by study ID), resulting in 25 estimated correlations and 25 corresponding p‐values. These correlation coefficients were then averaged to estimate correlations for each of the regional neurochemicals, along with their corresponding p‐values. Individuals with missing clinical measures were omitted from data resampling for that clinical measure, resulting in different resampling pools across clinical measures (Table S2).

Results

Cohort Characteristics and Data Availability

The SCA groups were at an early disease stage (SCA2 group mean SARA = 9.4; range: 0–18.5; SCA3 group mean SARA = 9.7; range: 2–21.5). In aggregate, SCA2 and SCA3 groups were well matched with respect to age at enrollment, age of disease onset, manifest disease duration, as well as SARA and FARS‐ADL scores (Table 1, Table S2). However, the mean age of both SCA groups was roughly 10 years older than that of the control group, resulting in a need to adjust for age in statistical comparisons across groups.

Each site enrolled at least 7 individuals with SCA and 5 control participants (Table S2, Figure S2). Test–retest data were available from 15 (pons), 16 (CBWM), and 16 (putamen) participants with SCA2, 21 (pons), 20 (CBWM), and 24 (putamen) participants with SCA3, and 24 (pons), 25 (CBWM), and 25 (putamen) individuals from the control group (Table S3, Figure S2). SARA scores were missing for 3 participants, one from each group. The FARS‐ADL scale was not collected at one site, and one score each was missing from the control and SCA2 groups (Table S1).

Data Quality and Neurochemical Concentrations Across Sites

The reproducibility of MR data quality and neurochemical concentrations was first assessed using healthy control data. The MRS protocol yielded high‐quality spectra with reproducible region‐specific patterns across sites (Figure S1). Table S3 shows regional data quality measures of water linewidth and signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) for each site. Sporadic site differences in water linewidth were noted in the pons and the putamen; however, mean linewidth values were below 11 Hz for all regions and sites, indicating good data quality at all sites. Regional site differences in SNR in CBWM and the putamen were generally delineated by vendor (Table S3). Despite differences in SNR across sites, neurochemical levels in control spectra were comparable between sites, with small region‐dependent differences in select neurochemicals (e.g., Glu and Glx in the pons and putamen, Figure 1, Control column).

Neurochemical concentrations of the control group across all VOI were more tightly grouped than the neurochemical concentrations in the SCA2 or SCA3 groups (Figure 1), reflecting neurochemical differences due to disease state across individuals within the ataxia groups.

Test–Retest Reproducibility of Neurochemical Concentrations

Site‐specific CVs of selected neurochemical concentrations were comparable in controls across sites and vendors, with most median CV values less than 10% (Figure S3). One site had larger variability in the 3–4 ppm spectral region (Figure S1), which was also reflected in larger metabolite concentration ranges for that site (Figure S3), particularly in the pons.

Test–retest reproducibility (Figure S4A, B) and CRLB (Figure S5) of metabolite concentrations were examined for CBWM and the pons because control‐SCA differences were apparent in subject‐level data for these VOI (see tNAA, mI, and tCr values in the top two rows of Figure 1).

Metabolites tCho, tCr, mI, NAA, and tNAA had nominal mean CV and CRLB of ≤ 5% with low dispersion, whereas Glu, Glx, and Glc + Tau had elevated mean CV and CRLB with more pronounced ranges in both CBWM and in the pons, across all groups (Figures S4A,B and S5). Metabolite‐specific group differences were noted for mean CVs of Glu, Glc + Tau, and tNAA (Figures S4A, D). The CVs of these three neurochemicals were further plotted on a vendor basis (Figure S6) for each group. Comparable CVs between vendors for each of the control and SCA groups confirmed that group differences in test–retest CVs for these metabolites were not driven by vendor differences. Rather, higher tNAA and Glu CVs in SCAs vs. controls and lower Glc + Tau CV in SCAs vs. controls were likely due to concentration differences between groups.

Due to scheduling challenges during the COVID‐19 pandemic, not all retest data sessions were performed at the same retest time, apart from data acquired at Massachusetts General Hospital, where all test–retest sessions were run on the same day. Individual neurochemical test–retest CVs for the pons and CBWM for different retest time gaps are shown in Figure S7 and Figure S8, respectively. There were no systematic biases in test–retest CVs of neurochemical concentrations for different retest time gaps.

Group Differences of Regional Neurochemicals

Based on comparable data quality (Table S3), similar neurochemical concentrations (Figure 1), and similar test–retest reproducibility (Figure S3 and Figure S4) among sites, we pooled data across sites and test–retest visits (henceforth, ‘pooled data’).

Visual inspection of mean spectra for each VOI and group revealed spectral differences across groups in the CBWM and the pons, but not in the putamen (Figure 2). Lower tNAA and higher tCr and mI peak intensities in the CBWM and pons spectra from SCA groups compared to controls were visually apparent. Higher mI and lower tNAA were more visually prominent in SCA2 compared to SCA3 at the same disease stage (Table 1). Furthermore, subject‐level concentrations of tNAA, NAA, tCr, mI, and Glu were different in the pons (Figure 3) and CBWM (Figure 4), but not in the putamen (Figure S9).

Neurochemical Correlations With Clinician and Patient‐Reported Outcomes

Correlations between clinical measures and the primary neurochemicals and commonly‐used neurochemical ratios [38, 39] were individually assessed for each VOI and each SCA group using a bootstrapping approach. The resulting heat map is shown in Figure 6. Metabolite correlations varied by SCA group and region. In SCA2, tNAA concentrations and ratios were negatively correlated, and Glc + Tau concentrations were positively correlated with SARA scores in CBWM and the pons. In SCA3, statistically significant correlations were observed between SARA and FARS‐ADL scores vs. tNAA and its ratios in the pons. Correlations of tNAA/tCr with clinical outcomes were slightly to moderately stronger than those of tNAA alone (Figure 6). Overall, tNAA/tCr displayed the strongest correlations with SARA and FARS‐ADL in both SCAs, with |r| values in the range of 0.5–0.7. Associations between MRS and clinical outcomes were stronger in the cerebellum for SCA2 and in the pons for SCA3.

Discussion

This study assessed the trial‐readiness of neurochemical concentrations for early‐stage interventions in two common SCAs. This is the first demonstration of test–retest reproducibility of neurochemical concentrations in a multisite/vendor setting in the SCAs. We show comparable test–retest reproducibility in patients with SCAs and healthy controls across four sites and across two scanner platforms. We identify the early neurometabolite differences between these SCAs vs. controls that are reliably detectable in a multisite trial setting, and those that are associated with clinician‐ and patient‐reported outcomes. We show that mean group neurometabolite levels differ between SCA2 and SCA3 genotypes at the same clinical stage, a finding of practical importance for trial designs with multiple SCA genotypes.

Prior single site work at higher fields has shown SCA‐specific group differences in multiple metabolites, including neurotransmitters and antioxidants [13, 40]. Studies conducted at 3 T have demonstrated that the most reliably quantified metabolites, specifically tNAA, tCr and mI, are top candidates as clinical trial endpoints in SCAs [21, 41]. While not disease‐specific, these metabolites are highly sensitive to the early impact of the disease in the brain [15], reflect underlying pathology based on validation studies in animal models [23, 42], correlate with clinical outcomes [13, 15] and are sensitive to longitudinal change [26, 41]. The current findings addressed a critical remaining question before these MRS outcomes can be utilized in upcoming trials as endpoints, namely, multi‐site, multi‐vendor reproducibility.

Test–retest reproducibility of MR measures is routinely reported in healthy volunteers; however, such data typically are not obtained from patient populations due to practical concerns, even though the technical reproducibility in clinical cohorts is most relevant for robust, well‐powered trial designs. Here, we first investigated test–retest reproducibility in healthy volunteers and showed that reproducibility of the metabolites‐of‐interest for SCAs, namely tNAA, mI, and tCr [13], was high, with CVs < 5% in most participants. A harmonized and automated MRS protocol, including automation of the VOI placement to mitigate operator bias, enabled this level of reproducibility in the multisite setting. Test–retest CVs in some participants were as high as 20% for these reliably quantified metabolites. The reproducibility of metabolite concentration estimates is affected by factors that contribute to spectral quality, and ultimately, to the goodness of individual LCModel spectral fits, including signal‐to‐noise ratio, variable water residuals, and broad spectral lines due to motion and/or less than optimal B0 shimming. Therefore, when designing clinical trials with MRS outcomes, it is critical to consider potential differences in reproducibility in patient vs. control groups. For instance, we observed higher tNAA CVs in SCA groups due to lower tNAA concentration in the CBWM VOI. Neglecting such group differences in reproducibility would result in biased sample size estimations for trials. Finally, we were able to conclude that test–retest variability was driven by technical factors associated with removing the participants from the scanner and repeating the scan, and did not reflect short‐term physiological variability, because CVs were not impacted by the duration between test–retest acquisitions.

In interpreting group differences in metabolite levels, potential quantification biases need to be considered, such as the impact of assumed relaxation parameters and age differences between groups. The acquisition protocol used in this study, with a long repetition time and short echo time, minimizes T1 saturation and signal loss due to T2 effects. Since SCA groups had higher levels of CSF contribution in the MRS voxels (Figure S11) and were roughly 10 years older than controls (Table 1), we estimated these effects on metabolite concentrations. The potential impact of an age‐related relaxation was estimated using a relaxation model with tabulated T2 (ms) and T2 slope (ms/year) values [43]. These corrections were small (< 1%, Supplementary Discussion). The larger CSF voxel fraction in the SCA groups (Figure S11) resulted in a roughly 5% change in metabolite concentrations after correction for the T2 of the CSF voxel fraction (Figure S12). This correction did not change the significant results and enhanced most of the findings (Figure S12, Figure 5). In later disease stages with more severe tissue loss, metabolite ratios may be preferred because the primary metabolites of interest are intracellular, removing the need for CSF correction. Figure S13 shows that metabolite ratios further improved group separations, particularly in SCA2, where all individual measures are fully resolved from individual controls. Disease‐related alterations in water T2 estimates are expected to be subtle due to a lack of lesion appearance in T2‐weighted images in SCAs. Furthermore, time constraints rendered the collection of relaxation data from this clinical cohort impractical; therefore, literature values were used for both control and patient groups.

Despite different targets of polyQ mutations and their cellular location (ATXN2, cytosolic; ATXN3, cytosolic and nuclear), MRS measurements of well‐documented regions of (early stage) neurodegeneration in SCA2 and SCA3 reveal similar metabolite alterations compared to healthy participants in both the pons and cerebellum. These are associated with neuronal dysfunction (tNAA, Glu), glial activation (mI and possibly tCr), and impaired energetics (Glu and possibly tCr) in both SCAs, with more prominent abnormalities in SCA2 than in SCA3, indicating that both CBWM and pons are more impacted in SCA2 at the same clinical stage. Glc + Tau was also significantly higher in SCA2 than in SCA3 in the CBWM, where the overlap between individual SCA3 and control data points was substantial. However, it is not possible to determine if this difference in Glc + Tau is associated with processes that involve taurine [44] or glucose metabolism [45, 46]. Correlations between neurochemicals and clinical scales were also more notable in SCA2, possibly due to a larger dynamic range of neurochemical abnormalities. Significant correlations of tNAA, tCr, and Glc + Tau with SARA and FARS‐ADL indicate that neuronal and energetic dysfunction are associated with ataxia severity and disease impacts on daily function.

The putamen was not studied by MRS in SCAs previously. Degeneration of striatal dopamine (DA) neurons was shown by PET in individuals with SCA2 or SCA3, similar to that in Parkinson disease (PD) [20, 47]. The lack of putaminal neurochemical differences between either SCA group and healthy controls could be due to partial volume effects due to an MRS voxel size that averages bulk signal over a large part of the putamen, thereby missing local neurodegeneration within the structure. Alternatively, it could be due to a lack of sensitivity to specific metabolites. The putamen is heavily composed of GABAergic neurons, and DA degenerative patterns similar to those in PD [20] may affect signaling metabolites (e.g., GABA/Glu) rather than quantifiable changes in mI, tNAA, tCr, and tCho. A 7T MRS study of putamen in PD [48] reported a difference only in GABA levels between healthy individuals and those with early‐moderate PD, indicating a similar trend in the putamen: DA system degeneration, but no readily visible MR neurochemical differences in the putamen. Therefore, our negative finding in the putamen is consistent with prior observations of the putamen under DA system degeneration.

Strengths and Limitations

A strength of this study is that it provides test–retest reproducibility data in SCA populations in a multisite and multivendor setting at sites of the CRC‐SCA consortium that has built trial‐ready SCA cohorts to accelerate the development of interventions for common SCAs. Despite age differences between healthy control and SCA groups, the SCA groups were well age‐matched, allowing us to compare regional neurochemistry between genotypes at the same clinical stage. We show that spectral quality (signal‐to‐noise ratio and linewidth) in these early‐stage SCA groups was comparable to that in controls and was not impacted by disease‐related factors (Table S3, Figure S2) such as higher levels of motion in patients. However, in longitudinal studies where motion may become more common with disease progression, robust prospective motion correction strategies will be important for data quality.

Enrollment in this study was interrupted by the COVID‐19 pandemic, which resulted in limited sample sizes at some sites, particularly for SCA2, lowering statistical power. Despite this limitation, the MRS data were of high quality and had comparable reproducibility across sites such that group data were amenable to pooling across sites.

Because of the cross‐sectional nature of this study, our results are limited with respect to the interpretation of neurochemical changes during disease progression as would be observed in a longitudinal clinical trial. We enrolled patients at an early stage because potential therapeutics are more likely to be effective prior to substantial neurodegeneration. Of note, test–retest reproducibility of neurochemicals related to neuronal viability may become poorer at later stages.

We observed mildly elevated mean [Glc + Tau] in SCA vs. control groups, with clinical measures correlating weakly in SCA2. We defer interpretation of these differences to future study because of the limited SCA2 sample size, age differences between the SCA and control groups, and the lack of glucose control or monitoring at the time of the MR sessions.

Conclusions

Neurochemical data collected with a harmonized MRS protocol using different scanners have comparable test–retest reproducibility (CV) across platforms, with site adjustments necessary for concentration estimates.

Cerebellar and pontine tNAA, mI, and tCr concentrations are measured with high reproducibility in SCA2 and SCA3 and are candidate trial endpoints in these SCAs. Of these, tNAA and its tCr ratio were strongly associated with clinician‐reported (SARA) and patient‐reported (FARS‐ADL) outcomes. If an MRI protocol can accommodate only one VOI for MRS, we recommend the CBWM for SCA2 and the pons for SCA3 because regional metabolite levels in these regions were associated with clinical outcomes. We conclude that the putamen metabolite levels are not candidate endpoints for early‐stage trials in these SCAs, but may be targets for future mechanistic studies investigating GABA.

The present data indicate that neurochemical endpoints should be analyzed separately for each SCA in studies of mixed ataxia groups due to highly significant differences between different SCA genotypes at similar stages of disease.
