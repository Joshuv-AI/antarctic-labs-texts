Breaking down costs: rehabilitation robotics vs. usual care therapy in diverse healthcare models
Authors: Valerio Gower, Taya Hamilton, Federico Spinelli, Mattia Randazzo, Irene Giovanna Aprile, Francesca Falchini, Marco Germanotta, Furio Gramatica, Todd Wagner, Hermano Igo Krebs
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
One of the significant barriers to the adoption of rehabilitation robotics into clinical care over the last 30 years has been the high investment costs of the technology. There have been limited efforts to understand the healthcare economics of implementing rehabilitation robotics compared to usual care. From the few studies that have investigated the cost, the findings have had limited generalizability to other clinical settings or contexts due to diverse healthcare models and the complexity of implementation factors. This study compares three organizational models in rehabilitation centers across the USA, UK, and Italy. We developed a common formula to compute cost differences between robotics and usual care for the upper limb and conducted a deterministic sensitivity analysis to identify key cost-influencing parameters. Results indicate that models where a single therapist supervises multiple patients can lead to significant economic savings: the Veteran Affairs in the US could achieve 18% savings with full utilization of robotic treatment slots (4300 h/year) and Italy’s Fondazione don Gnocchi rehabilitation model could realize 30% savings (6187 h/year). Conversely, the organizational model adopted by the RATULS study in UK generates 105% additional costs with 1820 h/year of robotics usage, mainly due to the 1:1 therapist-patient ratio adopted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24656-7.

Introduction

Rehabilitation robotics is an exercise-based therapy using robotic devices that enable the implementation of highly repetitive, intensive, adaptive, and quantifiable physical training1. It was developed over 30-years ago with the aim of revolutionizing both therapeutic efficacy and the objective assessment of motor impairments following neurological injury or disease. The design of the technology harnessed emerging knowledge at the time about optimizing neuroplasticity by increasing the intensity and number of repetitions in a therapy session2. Robotic therapy can also include other elements shown to induce greater plasticity such as challenging and varied tasks, timely feedback on movement performance, and an engaging, goal orientated, and motivating training environment3,4.

Conventional neurorehabilitation or usual care for individuals with motor impairments typically involves performing repetitive exercises or functional tasks supported directly or indirectly by therapists. This practice has obvious limits in terms of time and human resources, which can substantially impact an individual’s therapy outcome5. Rehabilitation robotics has been proposed as a possible solution to alleviate the laborious manual handling required for therapists retraining repetitive tasks such as walking or reaching with individuals with neurological conditions6,7.

Over the last two decades, a large body of scientific literature has been published to evaluate the efficacy and, in a few pragmatic trials, the effectiveness of robotic treatment as compared to usual care. The most significant amount of scientific evidence on effectiveness has been obtained on individuals with stroke, particularly in the robotic rehabilitation of the upper limb8–10. Those clinical trials on the efficacy of robotic rehabilitation were conducted with the goal of evaluating the same therapy duration for robotic rehabilitation and usual care8–10. To account for the potential of a neutral trial showing improvements in both groups, some studies included a positive control group. This third group matched the same therapy duration and high intensity as the robotic therapy group. In fact, when administered at the same duration and high intensity, robotic rehabilitation seems to have the same efficacy as therapist delivered high intensity rehabilitation. Summarizing the results of the Veteran Affairs pragmatic trial: “we found that in a chronic stroke population intensive therapy had significant benefit after 12 weeks of therapy and although the benefits persisted, the differences were slightly attenuated after 36 weeks compared with usual care”8,11. However, this statement should be taken with appropriate caveats, as the numerous studies now present in the literature are very heterogeneous in methodological terms (duration of treatment, frequency of sessions, intensity within therapy sessions, and specific treatment modalities)12–15.

Despite the substantial body of literature on efficacy, the clinical adoption of robotics technology has been limited by several factors including, among others, cultural barriers leading to reduced acceptance levels by physiotherapists. Moreover, there have been limited efforts to understand the healthcare economics of implementing rehabilitation robotics compared to usual care. As a result, the high cost of the technology has been recognized as one of the most significant barriers to the adoption of rehabilitation robotics into clinical care since its inception16. Yet, robotic devices, if used within appropriate organizational models, have the potential to reduce the costs sustained by hospitals to deliver upper limb rehabilitation compared to usual care17–20. Robotic devices allow for the implementation of organizational models in which a single therapist can supervise a group of patients, each interacting individually with a single device to deliver personalized treatments21,22, therefore allowing to increase the amount of treatments that can be delivered with the same number of therapists. This can contribute to optimizing resource allocation and potentially improve care access, especially in countries that face a shortage of healthcare workers. It is therefore crucial to determine the factors that influence the cost differences between robotic and usual care.

In light of these considerations, the objective here is to determine the conditions that make the costs of robotics rehabilitation (RT) more enticing, compared to those of usual care (UC), from the perspective of a healthcare provider, within different reimbursement scenarios. This has been done by comparing three different organizational models of robotic upper limb rehabilitation adopted in three different countries: the United States (US), the United Kingdom (UK) and Italy. Specifically, the three organizational models have been implemented in 4 hospitals of the Department of Veteran Affairs (within the VA-Robotics study) in the US, 4 hospitals of the National Health System in the UK (within the Robot Assisted Training for the Upper Limb after Stroke (RATULS) study), and one hospital of Fondazione don Gnocchi (FDG) in Italy. Our study provides a set of tools to support rehabilitation hospitals in making data-driven decisions about investing in technology-based rehabilitation.

An important aspect to consider when performing an economic evaluation of healthcare programs, as highlighted in the guidelines of the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS)23, is to define the perspective (or viewpoint) adopted by the study. Possible perspectives include those of the patient and their informal caregivers, the healthcare provider (e.g. the rehabilitation center delivering the treatments), the funder (e.g. the National Healthcare Service or private insurance), and other impacted governmental bodies (e.g. the Ministry of Health budget or the Ministry of Welfare). A “healthcare sector” and a “societal perspective”, resulting from the aggregation of different stakeholders’ viewpoint and cost items, can also be considered to evaluate the wider economic impact24,25.

The choice of perspective ultimately depends on which type of healthcare decision-makers the economic evaluation is intended to inform26–28. The final objective of our study is to provide a set of tools to support decision makers within healthcare providers (in particular, a rehabilitation hospital or clinic) in making data-driven decisions about investing in technology-based rehabilitation.

Recent reviews on economic studies of digital motor rehabilitation technologies29,30 highlighted the lack of sufficient data to draw conclusions about the cost minimization, cost-effectiveness, cost utility, and cost benefit of using robotics for the rehabilitation of individuals with neurological injury or disease. Consequently, it is essential to equip healthcare administrators and other stakeholders involved in procuring robotic technology with a deeper understanding of the factors that influence the healthcare economics of robotic rehabilitation.

Materials and methods

This analysis compares the three largest Randomized Control Trial (RCT) studies in the field of upper limb robotic rehabilitation of stroke patients, that also include an economic evaluation. The authors, who were involved in varying degrees and capacities with the original studies, evaluated the efficacy and costs of the different organizational models, described in the original studies, compared to usual care treatments8–10,17,20,31. The original studies were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, as described in details in the respective papers9,10,32. All the experimental protocols of the original studies were approved by relevant institutional bodies: the Veteran Affairs (VA) Robotics study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards at each participating site and the human rights committee at the coordinating center (VA Cooperative Studies Program Human Rights Committee, West Haven, CT); approval for the RATULS study protocol was granted by the National Research Ethics Committee Sunderland (reference 12/NE/0274); finally the institutional Ethics and Experimental Research Committee of the Fondazione Don Carlo Gnocchi (FDG) approved the study protocol of the FDG multicenter clinical trial on April 6th, 2016 (FDG_6.4.2016). Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects involved in the respective clinical trials.

Description of the three organizational models

VA-Robotics

Four Department of VA medical centers were equipped with three modules of the MIT-Manus robot, designed to train movements of the shoulder, elbow, wrist, and hand21,33. The clinical protocol involved sessions of one hour over a period of 12 weeks delivered to outpatients32. In usual care there was a 1:1 therapist-to-patient ratio where a single therapist treated a single patient for 60 min. In the upper limb rehabilitation robotic program, a therapist supervised the patient for 15 min out of the 60-min treatment session. The other 45 min were devoted to other productive robotic assisted tasks or activities. This is equivalent to a single therapist supervising 4 patients in a treatment slot of 1 h (1:4 therapist-patients ratio). The “base case” described in the paper20 employs 2 patients per slot and foresees 43 slots of robotic therapy per week and 50 weeks of robotic gym usage per year. The resulting maximum number of robotic sessions that can be delivered per year is 4,300.

RATULS

In the RATULS study the robotic gyms were equipped with 2 MIT-Manus (InMotion commercial version) robotic devices. Treatments were predominantly delivered to outpatients in 1-h slots with a therapist supervising a single patient for the entire duration of treatment (1:1 therapist-patient ratio) both in robotic and usual care. The advantage of robotics, compared with conventional treatments, was expected in terms of better clinical outcomes. The base case described in the paper31 foresees 35 slots of rehabilitation robotic treatment per week, 1 patient per slot, and 52 weeks of robotic gym usage per year. The resulting maximum number of robotic sessions that can be delivered per year is 1,820.

FDG

The FDG rehabilitation center “Santa Maria della Provvidenza” (SMP) – based in Rome, Italy – is equipped with 4 devices: the Motore (Humanware – Italy) and 3 Tyromotion devices (Amadeo, Diego and Pablo, Tyromotion – Austria). The suite of 4 devices, which were selected through a structured Health Technology Assessment procedure34, can provide a comprehensive rehabilitation of the upper limb. The clinical protocol adopted by FDG foresees treatment sessions of 45 min. In the SMP rehabilitation center of FDG, treatments are delivered to patients in three different care settings: inpatient, day hospital and outpatient, accounting for an average of 56.4%, 34.3% and 9.3% of the total rehabilitation services provided respectively17. Patients treated in the inpatient setting require an assistant to transport them from their hospital bed to the robotic gym. In addition, approximately half of the day hospital patients require an assistant for transportation to their robotic therapy session. Conventional therapy for inpatients is delivered at their bed and therefore an assistant for transportation is not needed.

Within a FDG session of robotic rehabilitation, a single therapist supervises 3 patients concurrently, each interacting individually with a single device (1:3 therapist-patient ratio). This is equivalent to a therapist supervising a patient for 15 min out of the 45-min session duration. In usual care, a single therapist treats a single patient for 45 min (1:1 ratio).

The maximum number of robotic therapy sessions that can be delivered per year using the FDG model is 8,250, determined as follows: 55 slots of robotic rehab treatment per week (10 per day from Monday to Friday and 5 on a Saturday), 3 patients per slot, and 50 weeks of robotic gym usage per year. Considering the treatment duration (45 min) this corresponds to a total of 6,187.5 h of annual robotic device availability.

VA-Robotics

Considering all the patients recruited during the 2-yearlong study, at 12 and 36 weeks, the mean FMA score for patients receiving robot-assisted therapy was better than that for patients receiving usual care (difference at 12-weeks, 4.2 points; [95% CI 1.3 to 7.1] and difference at 36-weeks, 4.6 points; [95% CI 0.6 to 8.6]) and the differences were significant and MCID (5.25) is within the 95% CI. The results on the SIS were significantly better for patients receiving robot-assisted therapy than for those receiving usual care at 12-weeks but not at 36-weeks (difference at 12-weeks, 8.3 points; [95% CI 2.7 to 14.0] and difference at 36-weeks, 3.2 points; [95% CI –2.9 to 9.4]). The results on the Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT) were significantly better for patients receiving robot-assisted therapy than for those receiving usual care at 12-weeks but not at 36-weeks (difference at 12-weeks, 7.6 s; 95% CI, 0.1 to 15.1 and difference at 36-weeks, 7.8 s; 95% CI, − 0.05 to 15.7)11.

RATULS

The primary outcome was the Action Research Arm Test (ARAT) success at 3 months. The definition of success differed depending on baseline severity: baseline ARAT score 0–7 required an improvement of 3 points or more; baseline ARAT 8–13 required an improvement of 4 points or more; baseline ARAT 14–19 required an improvement of 5 points or more; baseline ARAT 20–39 required an improvement of 6 points or more. The results of the ARAT demonstrated that, at 3 months, the robot-assisted training group did not show significant differences compared to Enhanced Upper Limb Therapy and usual care. Nonetheless the results for the FMA motor subscale indicated a reduced upper limb impairment for the patients in the robot-assisted group compared with the usual care group both at 3 months (adjusted mean difference 2.79 [98.3% CI 0.66 to 5.01]) and 6 months (adjusted mean difference 2.54 [98.3% CI 0.07 to 5.06])10. Some of the secondary outcome comparisons indicated differences that are likely to be clinically important, since the MCID is within the 98.3% CI.

FDG multicenter clinical trial

The results indicated there was no significant difference in the change of FMA score between the robotic (mean improving: 8.50 points) and usual care groups (mean improving: 8.57 points) although both groups have exceeded the MCID (5.25). The Motricity Index (MI) showed a greater improvement for the robotic group when compared with the usual care group (mean difference 4.42 [95% CI 0.27 to 8.57]) at the completion of the rehabilitation program9.

As demonstrated by the clinical studies that have implemented the organizational models described in this paper, outcomes obtained with robotics rehabilitation are comparable with those of conventional treatments even for those models in which a single therapist supervises multiple patients.

Methods for cost comparison

Given the difference in session duration (45 min for FDG and 60 min for VA and RATULS) we have normalized the costs for an hour of treatment. Moreover, all costs have been expressed in US dollars using the following currency exchange rates based on the averages of the Purchasing Power Parity values, indicated by the World Bank Group37, in the period between the first study (VA-Robotics, 2009) and the last study (FDG study, 2018): 1€ = 1.3627 $ and 1£ = 1.4353 $.

Initially the cost difference between robotic and usual care has been calculated by assigning to the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${h}_{treats}$$\end{document} the maximum total hours of robotic devices availability (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${h}_{treat{s}_{Max}}$$\end{document}). This cost difference represents the maximum theorical difference that can be achieved if all the available slots of robotic treatments are filled. However, the actual number of robotic treatments delivered by the hospital will be lower than the available slots for several reasons, including the “availability” of patients (e.g., the number of patients referred to the clinic for rehabilitation may not be sufficient to fill all the slots), cultural barriers leading to a lower number of robotic treatments prescribed by clinicians, organizational inefficiencies, etc38. To take these constraints into consideration, we have introduced a new parameter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$fil{l}_{\%}$$\end{document}) that represents the percentage of available slots of robotic treatments that are actually filled (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${h}_{treats}={h}_{treat{s}_{Max}}\cdot fil{l}_{\%}$$\end{document}). We have therefore represented the cost difference as a function of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$fil{l}_{\%}$$\end{document}, and we have identified what is the minimum percentage of slots that must be filled to obtain savings with robotic treatments (i.e., to have \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta Cost<0$$\end{document}).

In this scenario where the reimbursement for robotic treatment is identical to usual care treatment, the differential profit margin is equal to the differential cost (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta Cost$$\end{document}) evaluated in the hypothesis of filling all available robotic therapy slots.

The orange dots represent the fourth scenario (i.e. no additional tariff for the use of robotic devices and reimbursement for the therapist time only). In this scenario, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{device}=0$$\end{document}, the differential profit margin depends only on the reimbursement tariff for usual care treatment. For the VA-Robotics and FDG models, because only a fraction of therapy time is reimbursed, the differential profit margin is always in favor of usual care treatments. For the RATULS case, the orange dot coincides with the red dot, again due to the 1:1 therapist patient ratio.

Discussion

As shown in the results of this study, organizational models where a single therapist supervises multiple patients during a therapy session can achieve economic savings with robotic devices compared to usual care, while still providing individualized therapy. These models allow to increase the rehab center capacity (i.e. the number of annual treatments that can be delivered with the same number of therapists), potentially reducing waiting lists and decreasing the cost of technology per treatment. Conversely, if technology-based treatments are delivered with a 1:1 therapist-to-patient ratio, the cost of robotic devices adds to personnel costs, making savings unattainable. This highlights the importance of maximizing the potential of robotic devices to deliver personalized and tailored therapy to multiple patients under the supervision of a single therapist.

The savings (or additional costs) presented in Table 2 assume that the hospital can fill all available robotic therapy slots. However, in reality, the actual number of robotic therapy sessions delivered annually by the hospital will be lower due to various factors. These include the number of patients seeking or referred for rehabilitation (which may not be sufficient to fill all available therapy slots), cultural barriers among clinicians (leading to fewer robotic treatments prescribed), and organizational inefficiencies (e.g. difficulties in rescheduling when patients miss appointments), among others. These constraints are accounted for in our formula by introducing the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$fil{l}_{\%}$$\end{document} which represents the percentage of available robotic therapy slots that are actually filled. As shown in Fig. 2, in organizational models where one therapist supervises multiple patients, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$fil{l}_{\%}$$\end{document} must exceed a certain threshold to achieve savings with robotic rehabilitation (47.7% in the FDG model and 76.6% in the VA-Robotics model). It is worth noting that these are conservative figures, as inefficiencies, leading to underutilization of therapists’ capacity, may exist also for conventional rehabilitation.

Fig. 3 presents the curves of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta Cost=0$$\end{document} (i.e., the costs of robotic and usual care treatments are equivalent) as a function of two parameters potentially under the healthcare provider’s control: the time a therapist spends supervising each patient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}_{supervising}$$\end{document}) and the percentage of robotic therapy slots filling (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$fil{l}_{\%}$$\end{document}). The values of those two parameters falling in the area under each curve are those that allow to achieve savings with robotic rehabilitation. The farther the point of coordinates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({d}_{supervising}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$fil{l}_{\%}$$\end{document}) is the higher the savings/additional cost achievable with robotic rehabilitation. Fig. 3 is therefore a visual tool that can be used by the healthcare administrators and decision makers to assess their hospital’s position in terms of savings or additional costs within a given organizational model and level of robotic therapy slot saturation. The grey area in Fig. 3 represents the region that cannot be reached due to clinical (minimum patient supervision time) constraints.

The results of the local sensitivity analysis (Table 4) show that parameters with the largest influence on differential costs can vary across the three different organizational models. The most influential parameters for each model are the hourly cost of the therapist (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{t\text{h}}$$\end{document}), the total hours of robotic gym usage (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{h}}_{treats}$$\end{document}), and the time spent by therapists supervising each patient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}_{supervising}$$\end{document}). Given the hourly cost of therapists is largely beyond the control of the individual healthcare provider (and instead influenced by factors such as industry standards and market demand), this indicates that technology use may be more beneficial in high labor cost settings. The other two parameters are the key drivers for optimizing the costs of robotic rehabilitation and highlight the importance of increasing the utilization of robotic devices within the clinic (i.e. increase the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{h}}_{treats}$$\end{document}) and carefully selecting robotic technology where the usability and the design of the device facilitates safe, patient-centered, and effective therapy so one therapist can supervise multiple patients (i.e. reduce the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}_{supervising}$$\end{document}).

The method used to assess investment costs changes the effect that a variation of the discount rate parameter has on the calculation of the investment itself. An increase in the discount rate has opposite effects depending on whether the investment is calculated using the Net Present Value or the Equivalent Annual Cost. However, the discount rate is not among the most influential parameters in the VA-Robotics and RATULS models. Table 4 shows that the methodology for evaluating investment costs does not significantly alter the differential cost results. Savings for one hour of robotic therapy move from 18 to 25% for VA-Robotics, and from 26 to 30% for FDG, while additional costs for RATULS robotic therapy range between 92 and 113%.

We identified 4 possible reimbursement scenarios and calculated the additional (or reduced) profit margin (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta M$$\end{document}) that a healthcare provider might achieve with robotic devices. Figure 4 shows how \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta M$$\end{document} varies across scenarios based on the potential additional reimbursement for robotic technology and usual care reimbursement levels. In models where a therapist supervises multiple patients, robotic treatments can achieve higher profit margins under certain conditions. Additional margins can be achieved if the entire therapy time is reimbursed or if the additional reimbursement tariff for robotics exceeds a threshold dependent on the organizational model and the usual care reimbursement tariff. These results can help healthcare providers negotiate appropriate reimbursement models with policymakers.

The formula for computing cost and the analysis methods defined in this study provide a valuable tool for the neurorehabilitation field by offering a comprehensive framework that both healthcare administrators and technology manufacturers can use to make informed and data-driven decisions about the investment and implementation of robotic rehabilitation, that is not based on intuition or anecdotal evidence. This analysis highlights the importance of prioritizing the usability and utilization of robotic devices during procurement and implementation of technology, to leverage both the investment value and the impact on patient outcomes. Selecting robotic technology that enables patients to independently participate in meaningful, appropriately challenging therapy, with tailored and timely feedback will reduce the reliance on 1:1 therapist-to-patient therapy sessions41 and facilitate a more economic investment. Similarly, identifying patient, clinic-specific, therapist, and device training factors that optimize a device’s utilization (such as maximizing the number of therapists trained on the device, implementing a coordinated system for scheduling robotic sessions, and positioning the device to allow access for a diverse range of patients), will also improve the economic feasibility of the investment.

Overall, this study serves as a critical tool for all stakeholders considering adopting rehabilitation robotic technology to make well informed decisions that optimize both financial and clinical outcomes for neurorehabilitation.

Limitations

The results of the present study are based on the analysis of robotics rehabilitation organizational models implemented within three different studies conducted in US, UK and Italy. Although such studies are among the most relevant clinical trials, in terms of sample size, on upper limb robotics rehabilitation, the generalizability of results for other countries must be taken with caution as contextual elements (e.g. different prevalence of the target population) may affect the results.
