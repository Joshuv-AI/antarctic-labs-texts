The relationship between 24-h movement behaviors and internalizing and externalizing problems in children: a systematic review
Authors: Shunding Hu, Ziyun Zhang, Xiaosu Feng, Peng Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo review the relationship between 24-h movement behaviors and internalizing and externalizing problems in children, with the aim of providing recommendations for the development of public health policies.MethodsAfter searching the databases of CNKI, Web of Science, PubMed, SPORTDiscus, and Cochrane Library by computer (searched up to January 2024), two researchers performed literature screening, data extraction, and quality evaluation. Frequency analysis was used to statistically analyze studies related to potential positive benefits.ResultsA total of 5 documents (6 studies) were included, involving 32,713 children aged 3 to 14 years. This study found that adherence to screen time (ST) guidelines, sleep guidelines, and combined ST and sleep guidelines were positively associated with improvements in externalizing problems, with the most significant effects observed for adherence to combined ST and sleep guidelines. However, adherence to any guidelines did not show a clear association with improvements in internalizing problems.ConclusionAdherence to 24-h movement behavior guidelines is associated with improvements in externalizing problems, but further studies are needed to explore the relationship between adherence to these guidelines and internalizing problems. These findings suggest that public health policies should recommend healthy behavior practices for children and adolescents to prevent and control potential mental health issues.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-05930-z.

Introduction

Mental health in childhood is crucial for individual development [17]. At present, the mental health issues of children are becoming increasingly serious [16]. A global burden of disease study [28] found that 8.8% of children and adolescents worldwide have been diagnosed with various mental health issues. From 1990 to 2019, the age-standardized rate of disability- adjusted life years (DALYs) for children and adolescents diagnosed with mental health problems increased from 803.8 per 100,000 people to 833.2 per 100,000 people [28]. In the field of children’ mental health, internalizing and externalizing problems are prominent and prevalent major mental health issues [9]. Among them, internalizing problems manifest as inward emotional and cognitive abnormalities such as anxiety, depression, and social withdrawal; externalizing problems are characterized by explicit behaviors such as aggressive behavior, hyperactivity and discipline problems, and substance abuse [1]. Both types of problems can hinder children’s cognitive development, social competence building, and emotional maturity, and have a developmental cascade effect [10]. That is, unresolved psychological troubles in childhood may continuously affect adult occupational adaptation, intimate relationships, and even social functions through pathways such as neuroplasticity changes and environmental interaction patterns [3, 26]. Tick et al. [36] found that emotional and behavioral problems in Dutch children had increased year by year over a 20-year period; Askari et al. [4] also found that externalizing problems in American adolescents had gradually decreased. Therefore, researchers have focused on children's internalizing and externalizing problems and are committed to exploring strategies to improve these mental health issues.

Children’s internalizing and externalizing problems are closely related to their lifestyles and habits [37]. With the advancement of science and technology and the development of the social economy, children worldwide generally lack physical activity (PA), excessive sedentary behavior (SB), and insufficient sleep duration (SD) [18, 20, 22], so PA, SB, and SD have gradually become lifestyle issues that public health fields pay close attention to. In early research on internalizing and externalizing problems in young children, researchers focused on exploring the independent effects of PA, SB, and SD on internalizing and externalizing problems. In earlier research on promoting the mental health of children, researchers focused on exploring the independent effects of PA, SB, and SD on mental health benefits. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have accumulated evidence in this field, indicating that PA has a small to moderate potential impact on reducing internalizing and externalizing problems [33],excessive SB is associated with an increased risk of internalizing and externalizing problems [39]. In addition, on the basis of independent effects, researchers have gradually paid attention to the combined effects of any two variables of PA, SB, and SD on internalizing, and externalizing problems. For instance, Wu et al. [39] found that students with less PA and more time spent on computers and electronic games had a higher prevalence of internalizing and externalizing disorders.

From the perspective of activity behavior, PA, SB, and SD together construct an individual’s complete 24-h movement behavior system. These three behaviors do not exist in isolation but are closely linked and interdependent, showing a significant collinear characteristic [40]. In this complex system, any change in one behavior will inevitably lead to changes in other behaviors, thus affecting the individual’s overall health status [40]. In view of this, Carson et al. [8] proposed that in order to maximize and comprehensively achieve individual health benefits, one should not merely focus on the optimization of a single behavior but needs to find a delicate balance among the three key dimensions of maintaining an appropriate amount of PA, high-quality SB, and sufficient SD. Based on this, some studies [8, 12, 13] have proposed that compared with the traditional guidelines that only focus on PA or SB, integrating and considering all activity behaviors within 24 h and formulating a comprehensive activity behavior guideline can more accurately meet the individual’s health needs and provide a more scientific and comprehensive guiding framework for promoting health. Subsequently, countries such as Canada and Australia have formulated 24-h movement behavior guidelines for children, recommending that children aged 5–13 engage in at least 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous PA, have screen time (ST) less than 2 h, and sleep for 9–11 h, and that adolescents aged 14–17 sleep for 8–10 h [12, 24, 29]. These guidelines not only provide clear guidance on healthy lifestyles for children, adolescents, and their parents but also have sparked extensive and in-depth attention and discussion in the fields of public health and physical education.

Currently, researchers are increasingly focusing on the relationship between the 24-h movement behaviors guidelines for children and mental health issues such as internalizing and externalizing problems, and have accumulated numerous empirical studies. In addition, some systematic reviews [14, 19] has summarized the evidence, suggesting that maximizing PA, minimizing SB, and ensuring adequate SD can enhance mental health. However, there are still the following limitations. Firstly, the aforementioned studies include the entire life-stage population in the review scope, not specifically targeting children, which reduce the applicability of the research results. Second, the above-mentioned studies lack specific discussions on common mental health problems such as internalization and externalization in particular. Finally, research findings regarding children are also inconsistent. For example, some studies [12, 29] have found that meeting the 24-h movement behavior guidelines is associated with lower internalizing and externalizing problems in children, but other studies [8, 40] have not found such an association. This inconsistency has hindered government decision-making.

Based on the limitations of the above-mentioned studies, this research aims to explore the relationship between children's 24-h movement behaviors and internalizing and externalizing problems through a systematic review. It is expected that this study will enrich the research field of children’s 24-h movement behaviors, provide support for improving children’s internalizing and externalizing problems, and offer a basis for the formulation of relevant policies.

Methods

This study is a systematic review. This study strictly adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) for literature search, screening, assessment, data extraction, and result synthesis steps (Rethlefsen et al., 2021). This review was registered (CRD420251031891) in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO).

Search strategy

A single researcher carried out this study. They adopted a search strategy involving Chinese and English keywords to look through the databases of CNKI, WOS, PubMed, SPORT Discus, and Cochrane Library. The search topic is made up of three components as follows: (1) Children OR Preschool OR Pupil OR Primary; (2) 24-h OR 24-h movement OR 24-h movement behaviors; (3) Mental Health OR Internalizing and externalizing problems OR Internalizing problems OR Externalizing problems. The three types of search terms were combined using the Boolean logic connector “AND”. The search period was set from the time when each database was founded up to January 2024. Moreover, to avoid any possible oversights, this study also reviews the references included in the study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The research question of this study is the correlation between adherence to 24-h movement behavior guidelines and internalizing and externalizing problems in children. Based on this, the study developed the following inclusion and exclusion criteria according to the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes and Study (PICOS) criteria [23]. Inclusion criteria: (1) Participants are healthy children without specific diseases; (2) The intervention (i.e., independent variable) is 24-h movement behaviors; (3) The outcome variables (i.e., dependent variables) are internalizing and externalizing problems; (4) The original study is a cross-sectional study to explore the correlation between the two. Exclusion criteria: (1) Participants are atypical children; (2) Participants are athletes in the children stage; (3) Studies that focus on a single variable of PA, SB, and SD or combinations of two; (4) Reviews, abstracts, comments, editorials, etc.; (5) Studies that cannot obtain original data; (6) For duplicate publications, only the literature with relatively higher research quality is included. Two researchers will independently conduct the screening, and the two researchers will check the screened literature, and if there are controversial documents, a group discussion will be held to make a joint decision.

Data coding

The information extracted from the literature includes (1) bibliographic information (first author and publication year); (2) participant characteristics (sample size, age, proportion of female students, and nationality); (3) exposure factors (PA, SB, and sleep) and measurement tools; (4) outcome variables (internalizing, and externalizing problems) and measurement tools, and (5) confounding factors. Two researchers will independently extract the literature data, and the two researchers will check the extracted data, and if there are controversial documents, a group discussion will be held to make a joint decision.

Quality assessment

In this study, the JBI scale developed by the Joanna Briggs Institute [25] was used to evaluate the quality of the included cross-sectional studies. The tool consists of 10 items, and evaluators are required to score each item as 0, 1, or 2. A score of 0 indicates that the requirement is not met,a score of 1 means the item is mentioned but not described in detail; and a score of 2 signifies a detailed, comprehensive, and accurate description. Two researchers independently conducted the literature quality assessment, and their results were cross-checked. In case of disputes over any literature, decisions were made through group discussion. In this study, the Kappa coefficient was used to assess the degree of consistency between evaluators. It was found that the Kappa coefficient among evaluators was 0.779, indicating a high level of consistency.

Statistical analysis

This study uses frequency analysis to perform descriptive statistics on results with statistical significance. Referring to the study by Shi et al. [32],"00"is used to indicate that the results with statistical significance account for 0% ~ 33%, that is, the association is not strong,"??"indicates that the results with statistical significance account for"34% ~ 59%", that is, the association is not clear;"+ +"indicates that the results with statistical significance account for"60% ~ 100%", that is, the association is very clear.

Results

Literature screening results

This study initially retrieved 4,198 documents, and after eliminating duplicates, 246 documents were obtained. Following the literature screening process, 5 documents were selected. Among them, Fung et al. [12] explored the associations between adherence to 24-h movement behavior guidelines and internalizing and externalizing problems in children of different age groups. Therefore, this study split their research into two separate studies for analysis. In conclusion, a total of six studies were analyzed in this study. Basic information of included studies

In this study, the included studies were summarized and organized according to the established data coding rules, and described from four aspects: bibliographic information, participant characteristics, measurement tools, and outcome variables with confounding factors.

Bibliographic information

The publication years of the 6 studies included range from 2019 to 2024. Among them, 1 study [8] was published in 2019; 1 study [24] was published in 2020,1 study [29] was published in 2021; 2 studies [12a,b] were published in 2023; 1 study [40] was published in 2024.

Participant characteristics

The 6 studies included a total of 32,713 participants, with an average of 5,452 participants per study. The study with the most participants had 11,875 participants [29], while the study with the fewest participants had 205 participants [40]. The ages of the participants ranged from 3 to 14 years old. Except that the proportion of female children and adolescents in McNeill et al. [24] is unclear, the proportion of female participants is reported in the other studies, ranging from 42.9% [40] to 48.0% [12a]. The participants included in the studies were from the United States [12a,b] [29], China [40], Canada [8], and Australia [24], covering four countries.

Measurement tools for PA, SB and sleep

Among the 6 included studies, 3 studies [8, 24, 40] used accelerometers to collect data on participants’ PA and ST. Also, 2 studies [8, 24] used accelerometers to collect data on participants’ sleep duration. In addition, the remaining studies used measurement tools with high validity and reliability, self-reports, or parent reports to collect data on participants’ 24-h movement behaviors. Discussion

This study summarizes the relationship between children's adherence to 24-h movement behavior guidelines and their internalizing/externalizing problems. It finds that adhering to ST and sleep guidelines can effectively improve children’s externalizing problems, while the relationship between adhering to 24-h movement behavior guidelines and internalizing problems requires further exploration. Based on these findings, the following discussion is conducted.

Adherence to ST and sleep guidelines can effectively improve externalizing problems

The results of this study show that among the six studies, five found that meeting the ST guidelines and sleep guidelines can effectively improve externalizing problems in children. In addition, the study results also show that both meeting the ST guidelines and meeting the sleep guidelines can effectively improve externalizing problems in children. These findings are similar to previous studies [11, 15, 38], which also suggest that restricting ST and improving sleep quality can effectively improve externalizing problems in children.

Externalizing problems refer to issues manifested through outward behaviors, typically characterized by aggressive or destructive actions toward the external environment, or violations of rules [1, 21]). Such behaviors are extroverted, impulsive, and aggressive, including fighting, verbally abusing others, destroying objects, truancy, and defying authority, etc. [1, 21]. Such issues may have adverse effects on the growth and future development of children. On online platforms, children may be exposed to violence, pornography, and negative values. Prolonged exposure to such harmful content may lead them to imitate these behaviors and attitudes, resulting in externalizing problems such as aggressive behavior and vulgar language. Restricting ST can reduce their exposure to such harmful information and lower the likelihood of imitating negative behaviors. Moreover, limiting ST can encourage children to step out of the virtual world and increase their social interaction, which helps them, acquire normative social skills and emotional expression skills [31]. This, in turn, can improve externalizing problems such as being reclusive, uncooperative, and prone to conflicts with others. Insufficient sleep not only leaves children in a state of physical and mental fatigue but also weakens their psychological resilience [2]. This makes them more likely to exhibit negative emotions and behavioral responses when facing stress and setbacks. Good sleep helps to eliminate fatigue and allows children to face various situations in life in a more peaceful and stable state. It enhances their psychological stress resistance and adjustment abilities, enabling them to approach problems with a more positive mindset instead of escaping or venting through externalizing behaviors [6].

However, this study found that the association between meeting the PA, ST, and sleep guidelines and internalizing problems is unclear. Further exploration revealed that meeting the PA guidelines alone, meeting both the PA and ST guidelines, or meeting both the PA and sleep guidelines were all weakly associated with externalizing problems. This suggests that, whether meeting the PA guidelines acts independently or in combination with other health behavior guidelines, there is no strong link with externalizing problems. Therefore, it is speculated that meeting the PA guidelines may have a limited effect on improving externalizing problems in children. Nevertheless, some studies [34, 41] have found a negative correlation between PA and externalizing problems in children. However, other study [13] has suggested that the development of children’s behaviors/emotions is weakly associated with MVPA. Thus, the relationship between PA and externalizing problems in children warrants further investigation.

The association between 24-h movement behavior and internalizing problems needs to be further explored

Internalizing problems refer to issues primarily characterized by internal emotional or cognitive disorders, which usually do not directly impact external behaviors but cause inner distress to individuals [1, 21]. Their core features include emotional repression, anxiety, depression, social withdrawal, low self-esteem, etc. [1, 21]. If left unresolved over the long term, internalizing problems may lead to more severe psychological disorders, significantly affecting an individual’s mental health and quality of life. The results of this study found that the relationship between meeting the guidelines for PA, ST, and sleep and internalizing problems in children is unclear. In addition, the relationships between meeting the guidelines for ST and sleep, and between meeting the guidelines for ST and meeting the guidelines for sleep and internalizing problems in children are also unclear. A series of studies [5, 7, 27, 41] have found that increasing PA, restricting ST, and improving sleep quality can effectively improve internalizing problems in children. However, other studies have found no significant correlation between lifestyle and internalizing problems. For example, Tang et al. [35] found that the evidence linking ST to internalizing problems is relatively limited.

The unclear relationship between meeting the guidelines for PA, ST, and sleep and internalizing problems in children may be due to the complexity of these factors and the limitations of research methods. For PA, its type, intensity, and frequency all have different impacts on children. For ST, it includes various screen-related activities such as educational, entertainment, and social interactions, and the impact of different content on children can be vastly different. For example, educational programs may have a positive effect on the healthy development of children, while violent, or negative social information may trigger internalizing problems. Therefore, it is difficult to simply determine a unified standard for PA and ST in relation to internalizing problems. Moreover, many studies use questionnaires to assess PA, ST, and sleep duration. However, this self-report method may have problems with inaccurate information, which affects the consistency and accuracy of research results. At the same time, in real life, children are influenced by multiple factors from family, school, and society. Some studies exploring the relationship between 24-h movement behaviors and internalizing problems have not yet considered more control variables.

Implications of the results of this study

This study systematically analyzed the relationship between 24-h movement behaviors and internalizing and externalizing problems in children, providing important evidence for the development of public health policies. In other words, when governments formulate public health policies to promote the mental health of children, they should incorporate comprehensive 24-h movement behavior guidelines. This study suggests designing and implementing comprehensive intervention programs at the school and community levels to encourage children to develop healthy lifestyle habits. For example, schools can increase physical education classes, organize outdoor activities, club activities, and sports and art events; communities can build sports facilities and host parent–child sports activities to enhance PA levels. At the same time, campaigns should be launched to guide children and adolescents to reduce ST and improve sleep quality, thereby promoting mental health. Moreover, based on the varying degrees of association between different movement behaviors and mental health indicators identified in this study, policymakers can develop more targeted policies. For instance, given that reducing ST and improving sleep quality have significant effects on alleviating externalizing problems, policies that specifically restrict ST and ensure adequate sleep should be prioritized for children with severe externalizing problems.

The limitations of this study

This study is of great significance in exploring the relationships between 24-h movement behaviors and internalizing and externalizing problems in children. However, it still has limitations. These limitations affect the universality, accuracy, and in-depth nature of the research conclusions, and provide certain implications for the direction of subsequent studies. Firstly, while many existing studies have used cross-sectional designs to explore the association between adherence to 24-h movement behavior guidelines and internalizing-externalizing problems in children, such designs can only reveal current status rather than clarify causal relationships or dynamic change mechanisms. It is recommended that future research should: 1) strengthen longitudinal follow-up studies to clarify the causal sequence by long-term monitoring of the developmental trajectories of children’s 24-h movement behaviors and internalizing-externalizing problems; 2) incorporate experimental intervention studies by designing randomized controlled trials to verify the effectiveness of intervention programs based on movement behavior guidelines in improving children’s mental health. Secondly, there is a diversity of measurement tools in the included studies. For example, when measuring 24-h movement behaviors, there are accelerometers, self-reports, or parent-reports, etc. There are also various tools for measuring internalizing and externalizing problems. Different tools have different levels of accuracy and reliability. Self-reports are vulnerable to issues such as recall bias and social desirability bias, which affect the accuracy and reliability of the research results. It is suggested that future studies should establish a standardized measurement system to reduce the result heterogeneity caused by tool differences. Forthly, this study uses frequency analysis to describe the results. Although it can describe the homogeneity of the current research results to a certain extent, it cannot precisely quantify the degree of association. Therefore, it is recommended to introduce more refined quantitative analysis methods such as meta-analysis to accurately measure the association strength between children's 24-h movement behaviors and internalizing and externalizing problems, so as to provide a more solid basis for policy-making. Finally, due to the limited number of original studies included in this research, potential heterogeneity factors have not been explored. It is recommended that follow-up studies include more original research to further explore the association between 24-h movement behaviors and internalizing-externalizing problems, and on this basis, delve into the potential heterogeneity factors underlying this association, so as to provide more targeted policy recommendations for different groups.

Conclusion

This study conducts a systematic review on the relationship between 24-h movement behaviors of children and internalizing and externalizing problems. The results indicate that adherence to the ST and sleep guidelines is more significantly associated with externalizing problems. However, the relationship between 24-h movement behavior guidelines and internalizing problems still needs to be further explored in future studies. In addition, the results of frequency analysis need to be interpreted with caution, and it is expected that follow-up studies will use meta- analysis for further exploration. This study indicates that comprehensive consideration of 24-h movement behaviors is crucial for promoting the mental health of children. The findings of this study emphasize the importance of a healthy lifestyle for mental health and provide evidence to support comprehensive intervention measures targeting multiple health behaviors. The results may be instructive for public health policymakers and individuals in developing health promotion plans.
