Combinatorial Targeting of Common Docking and ATP Binding Sites on Mps1 MAPK for Management of Pathogenic Fungi
Authors: Zhiwei Kong, Saijie Li, Juxian Li, Yitong Chen, Meiqing Chen, Xin Zhang, Dongli Wang, Junfeng Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractResistance in pathogenic fungi necessitates the development of fungicides with new mechanisms of action. The Mps1 MAPK of Magnaporthe oryzae, the pathogen of rice blast disease, has been shown to be a molecular target for fungicide research. Here, we present compound TAK-733 that interacts with the common docking (CD) site of Mps1 and can be used in combination with ATP-competitive inhibitors. We initially identified compounds PLX-4720 and TAK-733 that interact with Mps1. Subsequent assays show that PLX-4720 is an ATP-competitive inhibitor, whereas TAK-733 binds to the CD site of Mps1—an interaction site for its MAPKK—but not to the ATP-binding pocket as it does in the kinase MEK1. In vivo assays demonstrated that TAK-733 exhibits combinational effects with ATP-competitive inhibitors PLX-4720 and A378–0. Collectively, we present TAK-733 as having a new mechanism of action suitable for combinational application with ATP-competitive inhibitors in the management of pathogenic fungi.Keywords: Magnaporthe oryzae, MAPK, inhibitor, CD site, combination

Introduction

The whole life of crops, from seeds to plants and fruits, is threatened by a variety of fungal pathogens.1 Chemical control is an essential strategy for plant disease management, but this method is challenged by the development of resistance, making it imperative to develop novel fungicides.2,3 Rice (Oryza sativa) is one of the main food crops worldwide, and its yield is threatened by blast disease, the pathogen of which is the fungus Magnaporthe oryzae (syn. Pyricularia oryzae).4 Besides rice, M. oryzae also infects many other Poaceae family plants, including wheat and barley, and serious outbreaks of wheat blast have already been reported.5 The infection process of M. oryzae in plants starts when the conidium of M. oryzae attaches to the hydrophobic surface of plant, germinates, and develops into a specialized cell called appressorium. The appressorium then accumulates enough turgor pressure to penetrate the host cell wall.6 Therefore, proteins that play important roles in appressorium development and penetration of M. oryzae might serve as targets for fungicides to control this pathogen.

Kinases control signal transduction in eukaryotic cells by phosphorylating substrates, i.e., by transferring the γ-phosphate group from the cofactor ATP to Ser/Thr/Tyr residues.7 There are common structural features in kinases, including an N-terminal domain (N-lobe), a C-terminal domain (C-lobe), and an active site for ATP binding and catalytic reaction located between these domains.8 The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade consists of sequentially activated kinases: MAPK kinase kinase (MAPKKK), MAPK kinase (MAPKK), and MAPK.9 In M. oryzae, there are three MAPK cascades: cell wall integrity (CWI), invasive growth, and high osmolarity growth.10 MAPK 1 (Mps1) is the MAPK of the CWI cascade of M. oryzae. Mps1 not only plays a role in cell-wall stability, but it is also indispensable for appressorium penetration and fungal virulence.11 Recently, we reported the crystal structure of Mps1 in complex with a chemical inhibitor named A378-0 and demonstrated that A378-0 could suppress the virulence of M. oryzae by targeting Mps1.11 This work, which has been cited by a review regarding targets of M. oryzae, not only indicates that Mps1 is a potential target for fungicide development but also provides an experimentally determined structure of Mps1 for structure-based evaluation of new inhibitors.12 Meanwhile, the natural compound melatonin has been found to target Mps1, and optimizations have been conducted that have yielded compounds with better properties.13−15

Kinase inhibitors have been widely used in the treatment of many human diseases, especially cancers.16,17 On the other hand, only seven fungicides that target kinases have been reported (https://www.frac.info/). Of these seven compounds, fenpiclonil and fludioxonil target HOG1 MAPK, chlozolinate, and other four compounds target Daf1 MAPK, while quinoxyfen and proquinazid have no target identified. The comparison of the quantity of human drugs and fungicides highlights the underdevelopment of fungicide targeting kinases. In this work, we present a compound named TAK-733 that interacts with the common docking (CD) site of Mps1, but not with the ATP-binding pocket as it does in the kinase MEK1, and exhibits combinational effects when used together with ATP-competitive inhibitors, providing a new strategy for managing plant diseases.

Materials and Methods

Protein Expression and Purification

The full-length Mps1 (GenBank ID: XP_003712437.1) recombinant protein was overexpressed and purified in Escherichia coli.18 The condomized Mps1 sequence was subcloned into the pHAT2 vector, which includes an N-terminal 6× His tag. Recombinant protein was overexpressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3). Purification was performed by using affinity chromatography with NTA resin (GenScript) and size-exclusion chromatography with a Superdex 200 10/300 column (Cytiva). The constructs of Mps1 mutants were prepared with PCR. The Mps1 mutant proteins as well as the full-length CgSlt2 (GenBank ID: XP_031889253.1) and FoMpk1 (GenBank ID: XP_018240084.1) recombinant proteins were expressed and purified in the same manner as the wild-type (WT) Mps1 protein.

Screening and Affinity Assays with Surface Plasmon Resonance

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) was conducted with a Biacore 8K+ instrument (Cytiva) at 25 °C. The proteins were coupled onto a Series S CM5 sensor chip by a standard amino-coupling method to a level of 8000–10,000 response units (RU). The compounds were purchased from Shelleckchem (TX, USA). Compounds were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), diluted to the desired concentrations with PBST buffer (PBS supplemented with 0.005% Tween-20 (v/v)), resulting in a final DMSO concentration of 5% (v/v), and sequentially injected onto the chip surface. Data were analyzed with Biacore Insight Evaluation software. The dissociation equilibrium constant (KD) was calculated by fitting the data to a 1:1 steady-state affinity model.

Crystallization, Data Collection, and Structure Determination

Recombinant Mps1 protein was prepared in HBS buffer (20 mM HEPES (pH 7.3), 150 mM NaCl, and 2 mM DTT), concentrated to 9.0 mg/mL, supplemented with 2 mM ATP and 2 mM MgCl2, and incubated on ice for 30 min before crystal screening. Crystals were grown at 18 °C using the sitting-drop diffusion method with the Oryx4 instrument (Douglas). Crystals for data collection were obtained in a reservoir solution containing 20%–30% Tacsimate, 0.05 M sodium cacodylate trihydrate (pH 6.5–7.5), and 1 mM spermine. For X-ray diffraction data collection, crystals were transferred into a reservoir solution supplemented with 20% glycerol (v/v) and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. Diffraction data were collected at Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility Beamline 18U1 (SSRF BL18U1). Data were indexed, integrated, and scaled with autoPX.19 Phase was obtained by molecular replacement using Phaser with the apo Mps1 structure (PDB ID: 5Z33) as the initial search model.20 Ligand building was performed with LigandFit.21 Model correction and refinement was performed with Phenix.Refine22 and COOT.23 Data processing and refinement results are listed in Table S2. All structural figures were generated with PyMOL.24

Enzyme Assay

Mps1 activity was measured using the ADP-Glo kinase assay kit (V6930, Promega).18 The Swi6–1 peptide (sequence: EGRYRHLVALAT; synthesized by Solarbio) was used as the substrate. Each sample contained 40 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 0.2 mg/mL Mps1 protein, 0.4 mM substrate, 0.1 mM ATP, 20 mM MgCl2, compounds at desired concentrations (0–100 μM), 2% (v/v) DMSO, and 0.1 mg/mL bovine serum albumin. The samples were handled according to the kit manual and were finally analyzed with a plate reader (Infinite F200, Tecan). Enzyme activities of CsSlt2 and FoMpk1 were determined in the same manner as for Mps1.

Bioactivity Assays

M. oryzae strain P131 was cultured on oatmeal tomato agar plates at 28 °C.25 Rice cultivar Nipponbare and barley cultivar E9 were grown at 28 and 25 °C, respectively. The bioactivity assays, including vegetative growth, appressorium formation, cell-wall stability, appressorium penetration, and virulence, were performed as described.18 All assays were repeated at least three times. (1) For the vegetative growth assay, a fresh mycelial plug was placed on agar medium supplemented with or without a compound. The colony diameter of M. oryzae was measured after incubation at 28 °C in the dark for 6 days. (2) For the appressorium formation assay, a 5 μL conidia suspension (2 × 105 to 3 × 105 conidia/mL), with or without compound, was placed onto a borosilicate glass coverslip. After incubation at 28 °C in the dark for 12 h, appressorium formation was observed with a Nikon microscope. (3) For the cell-wall stability assay, M. oryzae mycelium was incubated with compounds at 28 °C for 6 h in complete medium. Lysing enzyme was added to a final concentration of 7 mg/mL and incubated for an additional 1 h. Cell wall homeostasis was observed with a Nikon microscope.26 (4) For the appressorium penetration assay, the middle sections of one-week-old barley leaves were cut and placed backside-up in 9 cm Petri dishes. Conidia suspension (as described in the appressorium formation assay) was dropped onto the leaf surface. The leaves were incubated at 28 °C in the dark for 36 h, and appressorium penetration was observed under a Nikon microscope. (5) For the virulence assay, leaves from 8 day-old barley or 4 week-old rice were used. Conidia suspension (3 × 104 to 5 × 104 conidia/mL in 0.025% Tween-20), with or without compound, was inoculated onto the leaves. Inoculated leaves were incubated at 26 °C for 36 h and then exposed to a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle for another 3–4 days. ImageJ was used to measure disease lesions on the leaves.

Results

Screening of Inhibitors Targeting Mps1

The MAPK inhibitor library (Shelleckchem, TX, USA; catalogue no. L3400–01) contains 41 compounds. According to their working mechanisms, we classified these compounds into two groups: 28 compounds are ATP-competitive and are numbered C-1 to C-28, while 13 compounds are non-ATP competitive and are listed as N-1 to N-13 (Table S1). Molecular targets of these compounds include Raf MAPKKK, MEK1/2 MAPKK, p38 MAPK, JNK MAPK, and ERK MAPK. We then screened these compounds (at a concentration of 50 μM) with Mps1 protein using the SPR method, which is widely used for discovering and characterizing new drugs due to its efficiency and sensitivity (Figure 1A).27 A378–0 and AMP-PNP were used as controls: A378–0 interacts with Mps1 with an affinity (represented by the dissociation equilibrium constant, KD) of 69.7 μM, whereas AMP-PNP exhibits no response at a 50 μM concentration.18 After the experiment, compounds with a response of less than 25 RU (suggesting weak binding) or greater than 60 RU (exceeding theoretical maximum response), or showed abnormal sensorgrams (caused by aggregation or nonspecific binding), were excluded from the next round of analysis. Thus, eight compounds were chosen for the next round of analysis, specifically, a binding affinity assay using SPR. In this assay, each compound was diluted in a 2-fold serial dilution with a duplicate at 12.5 μM to verify repeatability. Among the tested chemicals, C-5 (PLX-4720) and N-11 (TAK-733) exhibited detectable binding affinities to Mps1, with KD values of 16.6 and 31.9 μM, respectively, whereas the other six compounds showed little or no affinity to Mps1 and were excluded from further analysis (Figures 1B,C and S1).

Both PLX-4720 (c log P = 2.304, log S = −6.155) and TAK-733 (c log P = 1.660, log S = −3.505) contain one fused heterocycle and one substituted benzene ring (Figure 1D). PLX-4720 is a derivative of pyrrolopyridine, which resembles the purine group of ATP and is found in many kinase inhibitors.28,29 TAK-733 is a derivative of pyridopyrimidine, which is found in many kinase inhibitors that target active or allosteric sites of kinases.30,31 We then investigated the working mechanisms of PLX-4720 and TAK-733 on Mps1.

Crystal Structure or Structural Models of Mps1 with AMP/PLX-4720/TAK-733

To elucidate the working mechanisms of PLX-4720 and TAK-733, we tried to determine the complex structures of the two compounds and ATP (substrate of kinase) with Mps1. However, only crystals of Mps1 supplemented with ATP were obtained. The structure was determined to a resolution of 2.26 Å, and residues Gln5-Thr360 and Gln394-Ala409 of Mps1 were included in the model (Table S2). The Fo–Fc map between the N-lobe and C-lobe of Mps1 suggests the presence of an AMP molecule, although it was ATP used in cocrystallization (Figure 2A). This is probably due to that the β- and γ-phosphate groups of ATP were too flexible to contribute to detectable X-ray diffraction or that the hydrolysis reaction occurred.

Complex structures of PLX-4720 and TAK-733 with their respective targets have been reported (PDB ID: 3C4C for B-Raf-PLX-4720, 3PP1 for MEK1-TAK-733), which could help infer the binding positions of these two compounds on Mps1.28,30 By performing a structural superimposition of Mps1-AMP with B-Raf-PLX-4720, we found that PLX-4720 is located at the same position as AMP in the ATP-binding pocket, indicating that PLX-4720 is an ATP-competitive inhibitor of Mps1 (Figure 2B, Table S1).28 On the other hand, structural superimposition of Mps1-AMP with MEK1-TAK-733 showed that there is no overlap between AMP and TAK-733, indicating that TAK-733 is a non-ATP competitive inhibitor (Figure 2C, Table S1).30 However, clashes were observed between TAK-733 and Mps1, suggesting that TAK-733 might not interact with Mps1 as it does with MEK1 (Figure 2C). A kinase might be in either active or inactive states, and there are structural indicators that reflect its state, including the αC helix and side chain of the αC-Glu residue (the glutamate residue of αC helix).8,32 In the Mps1-AMP and MEK1-TAK-733 structures, αC helices of the two proteins do not fully overlap, and the side chains of their αC-Glu residues point in opposite directions, indicating that Mps1 and MEK1 are in different states: Mps1 is in the active state, while MEK1 is in the inactive state (Figure 2C). To sum up, the analysis above suggests that PLX-4720 is an ATP-competitive inhibitor of Mps1, whereas TAK-733 might not bind to the ATP-binding pocket of Mps1 as it does in MEK1.

In Vitro Evaluation of the Interactions between PLX-4720/TAK-733 and Mps1

We used enzyme and binding assays to evaluate the binding sites of PLX-4720 and TAK-733 on Mps1.18,33 We found that PLX-4720 inhibits the activity of Mps1 in a dose-dependent manner, while TAK-733 does not significantly influence the activity of Mps1 (Figure 3A). This supports that PLX-4720 binds to the ATP-binding pocket of Mps1 to inhibit enzyme activity, whereas TAK-733 might not (Figure 2B,C). To evaluate whether the binding sites of PLX-4720 and TAK-733 on Mps1 overlap, we prepared a mixture of the two compounds and examined its performance on Mps1 interaction using SPR. We found that the mixture showed response levels similar to the sum of its components, suggesting that the binding of the two compounds to Mps1 is independent, in accordance with the enzymatic assay (Figure 3B). Therefore, TAK-733 probably binds to a different site on Mps1 rather than the ATP-binding pocket.

We then attempted to identify the binding site of TAK-733 on Mps1. Activation of a MAPK requires interaction with and phosphorylation (at the TEY site) by a specific MAPKK. Since TAK-733 is not ATP-competitive, it might influence the interaction between Mps1 and its MAPKK, Mkk2.10 Recently, the complex structure of p38α (MAPK) and MKK6 (MAPKK) has been reported.34 The structures of Mps1 MAPK and p38α MAPK are similar, with an RMSD of 2.78 Å (Figure 3C). In the p38α–MKK6 complex structure, MKK6 interacts with three sites on p38α: the CD site, the lipid-binding site, and the N-lobe site. Accordingly, we performed molecular docking of TAK-733 to the three sites on Mps1 with AutoDock Vina (Figure 3D).35,36 After analyzing the docking results, we inferred the residues of Mps1 that might be involved in the interaction with TAK-733 (Figure S2). We then performed multisite mutations on Mps1, including Mut-1a (R81A/H125A/D160A) and Mut-1b (R81A/H125A/D160A/W317A) for the CD site, Mut-2a (V235A/L238A/Y264A) and Mut-2b (V235A/L238A/Y264A/R195A) for the lipid-binding site, and Mut-3 (Q15A/K53A) for the N-lobe site, and evaluated these mutants with SPR (Figures 3E and S3). The results show that the binding affinity between TAK-733 and Mps1 with mutations at the CD site was substantially reduced, while the affinity between TAK-733 and the other Mps1 mutants was not significantly affected (Figures 1C and 3E and S3). On the other hand, the binding affinity between PLX-4720 and the Mps1 mutants was not significantly changed, indicating that PLX-4720 does not interact with the CD site, the lipid-binding site, or the N-lobe site of Mps1 (Figures 1C and 3E and S3). In summary, TAK-733 interacts with the CD site of Mps1 but not with the other two sites, whereas PLX-4720 does not interact with any of the three sites mentioned above. Therefore, we constructed a working model for PLX-4720 and TAK-733 on Mps1, in which the two compounds bind to different sites on the protein (Figure 3F).

In Vivo Evaluation of PLX-4720 and TAK-733 on M. oryzae

Mps1 is important for appressorium penetration, virulence, and cell-wall stability but does not influence vegetative growth or appressorium formation in M. oryzae.11 Accordingly, we examined whether PLX-4720 and TAK-733 affect these phenotypes of M. oryzae.

In the appressorium penetration assay, an appressorium that successfully penetrates the plant cell wall generates an infection site on the surface of the plant. Both PLX-4720 and TAK-733 inhibit the appressorium penetration of M. oryzae, but TAK-733 shows a better effect (Figure 4A,B). Specifically, TAK-733 at 100 μg/mL could completely inhibit the formation of infection sites, whereas PLX-4720 at the same concentration still allowed approximately one-third appressoria to penetrate successfully. In the inoculation assays for evaluating the virulence of M. oryzae, both PLX-4720 and TAK-733 inhibit the formation of lesions on rice and barley leaves, but TAK-733 performs better (Figures 4C,D and S4). Specifically, TAK-733 at 100 μg/mL completely abrogates the virulence of M. oryzae, which is comparable to the commercial fungicide isoprothiolane (Iso, mainly targets the phosphatidylcholine biosynthesis pathway in fungi and is a fungicide primarily used to control rice blast disease), whereas PLX-4720 at the same concentration still allows lesion formation, albeit with reduced size. In the cell-wall stability assay, after incubation with 100 μg/mL PLX-4720 or TAK-733, the hyphae of M. oryzae were hypersensitive to the cell-wall-digesting enzyme and dissolved (Figure S5A). In the vegetative growth assay, neither PLX-4720 nor TAK-733 inhibited the radial growth of M. oryzae hyphae (Figure S5B,C). In the appressorium formation assay, both PLX-4720 and TAK-733 showed no significant influence (Figure S5D,E). In summary, these assays show that PLX-4720 and TAK-733 target Mps1 in vivo and exhibit biological activity.

Since PLX-4720 and TAK-733 bind to different sites on Mps1, we examined whether they have a combinational effect in vivo using appressorium penetration and virulence assays. In the appressorium penetration assay, we found that “PLX + TAK”, in which the two compounds are in a 1:1 mass ratio, exhibited better activity than its components, especially at low concentrations (25 and 50 μg/mL) (Figure 4A,B). In the virulence assay, PLX + TAK also showed a better inhibitory effect than PLX-4720 or TAK-733 alone (Figures 4C,D, and S4). Specifically, even at a concentration of 25 μg/mL, PLX + TAK inhibits the lesion area to less than 50% of that in the control group. Therefore, these assays show that PLX-4720 and TAK-733 have a combination effect in vivo. In addition, we examined whether TAK-733 could be used in combination with other ATP-competitive inhibitors of Mps1, exemplified by the compound A378–0, which we have demonstrated to be an ATP-competitive inhibitor of Mps1 through crystallography.18 The binding of TAK-733 and A378–0 to Mps1 is independent, as evaluated with SPR (Figure S6A). In vivo assays show that the combination of TAK-733 and A378–0 performs better than using them separately (Figure S6). Collectively, TAK-733, which binds to the CD site of Mps1, could be used in combination with other ATP-competitive inhibitors of Mps1 for more effective control of blast disease.

Besides, we evaluated the toxicity of PLX-4720 and TAK-733. Inoculation of the two compounds on barley or rice leaves does not result in black lesions, a typical phenotype of phytotoxicity, suggesting that they are safe to plants (Figure S7A). We also predicted acute oral and contact toxicities of the two compounds to bees with the BeeToxAI Web server. The results show that the overall performance of TAK-733 is better than that of PLX-4720 (Figure S7B).

Broad-Spectrum Potential

The Mps1 MAPK cascade is conserved in plant pathogenic fungi and functions as a key signal-transduction component in virulence on hosts (Figures 5A and S8).9 Specifically, the four residues of the Mps1 CD site involved in TAK-733 interactions are strictly conserved among Mps1 and its orthologues and exhibit subtle variations in Pmk1 and Osm1, the other two MAPKs of M. oryzae (Figure S8). Colletotrichum spp. infect nearly all crops, causing anthracnose spots and blights on living plants as well as postharvest rot of fruits.37Fusarium oxysporum is a soil-borne pathogen that causes symptoms such as vascular wilt, stunting, and death in more than 100 plants species.38 Thus, we chose Mps1 orthologues CgSlt2 from Colletotrichum gloeosporioides and FoMpk1 from F. oxysporum to evaluate the broad-spectrum potential of TAK-733. SPR assays showed that PLX-4720 and TAK-733 interact with CgSlt2 and FgMpk1 (Figure S9). Enzyme and SPR assays with PLX-4720, TAK-733, and their mixture suggest that PLX-4720 and TAK-733 interact with different sites on the two proteins, similar to their interaction with Mps1 (Figure 5B,C). These assay results suggest that the combinational use of TAK-733 with an ATP-competitive inhibitor is also promising in the management of pathogenic fungi in addition to M. oryzae.

Discussion

In this work, we screened a MAPK inhibitor library and identified two compounds, PLX-4720 and TAK-733, that interact with Mps1 (Figure 1). Both PLX-4720 and TAK-733 have been reported to bind to the ATP-binding pocket of the kinases B-Raf and MEK1, respectively.24,26 However, in our assays, we found that PLX-4720 is also an ATP-competitive inhibitor of Mps1, whereas TAK-733 may not be since the position of TAK-733 in the structural model clashes with Mps1 and TAK-733 does not influence the enzyme activity of Mps1 (Figures 2 and 3A). The recently reported complex structure of MAPKK-MAPK has prompted us to explore whether TAK-733 has an effect on prohibiting the recognition of Mps1 by its MAPKK, Mkk2 (Figure 3C). Molecular docking and mutational assays show that TAK-733 binds to the CD site of Mps1, which is different from its position in MEK1 (Figure 3D,E). In vivo assays demonstrated that both PLX-4720 and TAK-733 exhibit biological activities against M. oryzae, with TAK-733 performing better. More importantly, since TAK-733 binds to a different site on Mps1 compared to ATP-competitive inhibitors and thus regulates Mps1 in a different way, it can be used in combination with ATP-competitive inhibitors, exemplified by PLX-4720 and A378–0, for the management of rice blast disease (Figures 4 and S4 and S6).

Of the reported fungicides, only seven target kinases, including HOG1 MAPK and Daf1 MAPK, while the biochemical details of how these compounds interact with HOG1 or Daf1 remain unclear (https://www.frac.info/). In this work, we demonstrated that PLX-4720 and TAK-733 interact with different sites on Mps1, laying the foundation for further research and application. Point mutations in targets are the main reason for the development of fungicide resistance.39,40 Since TAK-733 binds to the CD site of Mps1, rather than competing with ATP as most kinase inhibitors do, combining TAK-733 with ATP-competitive inhibitors, such as PLX-4720 and A378–0, can slow down the development of resistance, providing a new strategy for more efficient management of pathogenic fungi.18

The KD values of PLX-4720 and TAK-733 to Mps1 are 33.5 μM (13.8 μg/mL) and 15.1 μM (7.61 μg/mL), respectively, higher than the concentrations used in the inoculation assays. This is because in the inoculation assay, the compounds need to enter the cytosol of fungi to perform their functions, which could reduce the efficiency to some extent. Besides, there are other compounds of PLX and TAK series (Table S1 and Figure S10). PLX-4032 (numbered C-6 in our screen) is similar to PLX-4720, but its response to Mps1 is low (Figure 1A). On the other hand, TAK-715 (C-20) and TAK-632 (C-26) are rather different from TAK-733 (Figure S10). In addition, the other two MAPKs of M. oryzae are Pmk1 and Osm1: Pmk1 is required for appressorium development, and Osm1 does not contribute to fungal phenotypes except for growth on culture medium with high osmotic pressure.41,42 We have shown that both PLX-4720 and TAK-733 do not affect appressorium development, indicating they do not target Pmk1 (Figure S5D,E). Meanwhile, we found TAK-733 does not influence growth of M. oryzae on the culture medium with high osmotic pressure, demonstrating the compound does not target Osm1 (Figure S11).

The limitation of this work is that crystals of the Mps1–PLX-4720 and Mps1–TAK-733 complexes have not been obtained, rendering it impossible to visualize the interactions between Mps1 and the two compounds at the atomic level. Further studies might focus on optimizing TAK-733 to improve its affinity for Mps1 and testing the combined effect of TAK-733 with other ATP-competitive inhibitors on a range of plants, not restricted to rice and barley, cultured in pots or in the field. In summary, our research presents the collaboration of inhibitors that regulate one kinase in different ways, providing a new perspective for the development of agrochemicals and drugs.
