Integrated network pharmacology and experimental validation to elucidate the mechanism of Lysimachia paridiformis var. stenophylla Franch. in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Zhongxiu Wu, Yuan Zhang, Pingping Luo, Xudong Tian, Qiuyun Luo, Hongshi Liu, Lei Cheng, Yong Chen, Hong Liu, Ting Liu, Zixue Xuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the potential targets and mechanism of action of Chinese Miao Medicine Lysimachia paridiformis var. stenophylla Franch (ZhuiFengSan, LPSF) against rheumatoid arthritis (RA) using an integrated approach combining network pharmacology, molecular docking, and experimental validation. Components of LPSF were systematically collected from multiple databases, and RA-related targets were retrieved from disease databases. Cross-referencing identified 99 potential targets for LPSF against RA. A protein-protein interaction network revealed 5 core targets (TNF, IL6, AKT1, IL1B, and PTGS2), with TNF exhibiting the highest Degree value. Functional enrichment analysis indicated these targets were significantly associated with inflammatory responses and were notably enriched in the TNF signaling pathway. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation confirmed strong binding affinities between TNF-α and several primary active components of LPSF, namely alternariol, galangin-3-methoxy, and alpinetin. Experimental validation demonstrated that LPSF significantly inhibited TNF-α-induced migration in MH7A cells and downregulated the mRNA and protein expression levels of key inflammatory factors (TNF, IL-6, IL-1β, and COX-2). Furthermore, LPSF inhibited TNF-α activity, increasing viability in L929 cells and reducing luciferase expression in an NlucP-NF-κB reporter gene system. In conclusion, this multi-dimensional study demonstrates that LPSF exerts its therapeutic effects against RA primarily by targeting TNF-α.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53163-6.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis, Lysimachia paridiformis var. stenophylla Franch, TNF-α, Network pharmacology, Experimental validationSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Drug discovery, Immunology, Rheumatology

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by synovial inflammation, cartilage destruction, and progressive joint deformity, leading to significant disability and reduced quality of life1. Epidemiological studies indicate that the global prevalence of RA ranges between 0.5% and 1%, while in mainland China has a prevalence of 0.42%. Notably, the disease shows marked gender disparity, with female predominance evidenced by a 4:1 female-to-male ratio2. Current therapeutic strategies for RA focus on modulating inflammatory cascades and alleviating arthralgia. As first-line therapeutics, disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) exert therapeutic effects by suppressing inflammatory mediators and decelerating joint degeneration3. The advent of biologic agents targeting pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukin (IL)−1β, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α)) has revolutionized therapeutic paradigms, demonstrating significant improvements in disease management and clinical outcomes4. However, Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) herbal therapy for RA is historically entrenched, demonstrating unique therapeutic advantages through its characteristic multi-component and multi-target mechanisms, which enable holistic regulation of disease pathology5.

Chinese Miao Medicine ZhuiFengSan, derived from the dried whole plant of Lysimachia paridiformis var. stenophylla Franch (LPSF) and endemic to Southwest China (such as Guizhou, Yunnan and Sichuan), has been traditionally valued for dispelling wind and activating collaterals, as well as promoting blood circulation and relieving pain6,7. Modern pharmacological studies confirm its anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects, which have been clinically applied in managing joint disorders for decades8. Qi et al. observed that the total flavonoids of LPSF significantly alleviated xylene-induced ear edema in mice and Freund’s complete adjuvant-induced adjuvant arthritis in rats, demonstrating potent analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects9. Liu et al. reported that the standard decoction of LPSF demonstrates protective effects in collagen-induced arthritis rat models10. Multiple ethnomedicinal preparations containing LPSF have been commercially available for managing arthritis and rheumatoid arthritis, including Hei-Gu-Teng Zhuifenghuoluo capsule, Fenghe Chubi Tincture, and Bike granules11. However, the molecular mechanism underlying LPSF’s anti-RA effects remains unclear, which has limited its further development and clinical application.

Network pharmacology, an emerging paradigm integrating systems biology, bioinformatics, and pharmacology, offers a powerful tool to decode the complexity of TCM12. Additionally, molecular docking is a theoretical method used to study the interaction and recognition between protein receptors and small-molecule ligands, as it can predict binding modes and affinities13. The complementary integration of network pharmacology and molecular docking can be applied to the study of TCM, providing new insights for screening active compounds and exploring mechanisms. By constructing compound-target networks, this approach enables the prediction of synergistic interactions between bioactive molecules and disease-related proteins, bridging the gap between traditional medicine and modern mechanistic research14. In this study, we combined network pharmacology with experimental validation to systematically investigate the therapeutic mechanism of LPSF in RA. Using public databases, we identified RA-associated targets and LPSF-bioactive components, constructed LPSF-component-target and protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks, and prioritized core component and targets. Subsequent validation experiments in TNF-α-induced human rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblast-like synoviocyte cell line (MH7A), mouse fibroblast cell line (L929), and NlucP-NF-κB reporter cells, confirmed the regulatory effects of LPSF on pivotal inflammatory cascades and joint pathology. Our findings not only elucidate the mechanisms of LPSF in RA but also provide a methodological framework for accelerating the development of botanically derived therapies for autoimmune diseases.

Methods and materials

Identification of components and targets of LPSF

The chemical compounds of LPSF were retrieved from the PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), CNKI (https://www.cnki.net/), VIP (https://qikan.cqvip.com/), and Wanfang (https://www.wanfangdata.com.cn/) databases using “Lysimachia paridiformis var. stenophylla Franch” and “ZhuiFengsan” as search terms. The components and their references are listed in Table S1. Subsequently, the targets of LPSF’s components were predicted using SwissTargetPrediction (http://swisstargetprediction.ch/).

Screening for RA targets

Targets associated with RA were retrieved using the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), OMIM (https://www.omim.org/), and TTD (https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/) databases. The retrieved targets were merged, duplicate entries were removed, and the resulting unique set constituted the final RA targets.

Construction of PPI network and screening of core targets

A Venn diagram of RA targets and LPSF targets was constructed to obtain their common targets using Venny 2.1 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html). The common targets were then imported into STRING (https://cn.string-db.org/) to construct PPI networks. Finally, the networks were visualized by Cytoscape 3.10.0 software, with the “Analyze Network” tool used to calculate Degree values. The screening criterion for core targets was set as Degree value > twice the median15–17.

Analysis of kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) and gene ontology (GO)

The common targets of RA and LPSF were analyzed using the DAVID bioinformatics platform (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/) for GO functional annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment, with the species set to “Homo sapiens”. The top 15 enriched KEGG pathways and top 5 significant GO terms (biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF)) were subsequently visualized using Hiplot (https://hiplot.com.cn/).

Construction of LPSF-component-target network

A LPSF-component-target interaction network was constructed with Cytoscape software (version 3.10.0) using LPSF, its constituent compounds, and their associated targets. Based on node Degree ranking, the top 15 components were identified as key elements for subsequent analysis.

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation

The AMDock software was used to perform molecular docking. The crystallographic structure of the TNF-α target (PDB ID: 7JRA) was downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). The structure of 7JRA was determined by X-ray diffraction with a resolution of 2.10 Å. According to the PDB validation report, the Ramachandran outlier for this structure is 0.7%. SDF files of key components (quercetin, quercetin-3,3’-dimethyl ether, alpinetin, apigenin, alternariol monomethyl ether, kaempferol, luteolin, alternariol, (E)-cardamonin, galangin-3-methoxy, methyl 2,5-dihydroxybenzoate, L-malic acid, acacetin, galangin, and estragole) were acquired from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). These structures were converted to mol2 format using ChemDraw3D software. Finally, TNF-α and the key components were imported into AMDock software using the AutoDock4 module for molecular docking. The results were visualized with PyMOL and Discovery Studio 4.5 software. Lower docking scores indicate stronger binding affinity of the small molecule to the ligand. To validate the reliability of the docking protocol, the co-crystal ligand was re-docked using the same method as described above.

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS v2020.6. Sodium and chloride ions were added to each system to maintain electrical neutrality. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent method, followed by NVT and NPT ensemble equilibration (300 K, 1 bar), and finally a 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation was conducted. All hydrogen-containing bonds were constrained using the LINCS algorithm, and periodic boundary conditions were applied. Temperature and pressure were maintained using the V-rescale and Parrinello-Rahman algorithms, respectively. Long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the PME method. The output files were analyzed using GROMACS analysis tools, and data visualization was performed using Qtgrace software.

Preparation of LPSF extract

Lysimachia paridiformis var. stenophylla Franch (LPSF, lot: 20240530) was purchased from the Bird and Flower Market in Guiyang and authenticated by Chinese Medicine Pharmacist Youmei Peng from Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital Bijie Hospital. The certificate specimen was stored in Bijie City Key Laboratory of Clinical Pharmacy and Drug Research, Guizhou Province. The raw material was combined with 50% ethanol at a ratio of 1:15, followed by continuous reflux extraction for 2 h. This extraction process was repeated three times. The resulting solutions from the three extractions were combined and concentrated using a rotary evaporator at 65℃ to obtain the dried LPSF extract.

Cytotoxicity testing of LPSF in L929, MH7A, and NlucP-NF-κB reporter cells

L929 (Jennio biological technology, China) and MH7A cells (Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank, China) were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 10⁵ cells/mL, respectively. After 24 h, MH7A cells were treated with LPSF at different concentrations for 24 h and L929 cells were treated for 12 h. Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8, lot: 25002k1018, APExBIO) was then added to each well, incubated for 1 h, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Cell viability was calculated relative to the control group.

Human embryonic kidney (HEK293T) cells (Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank, China) were seeded at the same density for 24 h. HEK293T cells were transfected for 24 h using FuGENE® 6 transfection reagent (lot: 0000407389, Promega) and the pNL3.2.NF-κB-RE[NlucP/NF-κB-RE/Hygro] plasmid (lot: N111A, Promega) at a 3:1 ratio to construct NlucP-NF-κB reporter cells. After transfection, cells were treated with LPSF for 5 h, and cell viability was assessed by CCK-8 assay.

Wound healing assay

MH7A cells were seeded in 6-well plates (1.5 × 10⁵ cells/mL) for 24 h. Cells were then treated with TNF-α (30 ng/mL, lot: #21825, PeproTech) alone, or with LPSF (0.1, 0.25, 0.5 µg/mL) or UCB-9260 (10 µmol/L, lot: HY-133122, MCE) in combination with TNF-α. A uniform wound was created in each well using a 200 µL pipette tip, and baseline images were captured using an inverted microscope. After 24 h of incubation, the wound images were reacquired. The cell migration rates were calculated using ImageJ software.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

MH7A cells were treated with LPSF as described in the wound healing assay section. Total RNA cells were extracted with the Eastep® Super total RNA extraction kit (lot: 0000484334, Promega). Reverse transcription was performed to synthesize cDNA using the 5 × PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Perfect Real Time) kit (lot: AL61674A, Takara). qRT-PCR was performed using 2 × Green qPCR MasterMix (lot: 776571AH, Biomed). Finally, the relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method. The primers used are listed in the Table 1.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

After treating MH7A cells with LPSF, cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 min at 4℃. The levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β were measured using ELISA kits (Human TNF-α (cat: E-EL-H0109), IL-6 (cat: E-EL-H6156), and IL-1β (cat: E-EL-H0149), Elabscience) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, 100 µL of standards or samples were added to each well and incubated at 37℃ for 1 h, followed by incubation with biotin-labeled detection antibody for 30 min and HRP-conjugated streptavidin for 30 min. After adding TMB substrate for 15 min, the reaction was stopped and absorbance was read at 450 nm. Cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves.

Western blotting

MH7A cells were lysed in radio immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (cat: R0010, Solarbio) containing 1% phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4℃. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (cat: PC0020, Solarbio). A total of 20 µg of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE using SDS-PAGE Gel Kit (cat: P1200, Solarbio) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Subsequently, the membranes were blocked with 5% BSA (cat: IA0910, Solarbio) for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4℃ with primary antibodies against COX-2 (1:1000, cat: BSM-52502R, Biosynthesis) and GAPDH (1:5000, BSM-33033 M, Biosynthesis). After washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10000) for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection reagent (cat: WBKLS0500, Millipore) and quantified using Gel Image system ver.4.00 (Tanon, China).

TNF-α inhibitory activity assay

L929 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a cell density of 1.5 × 105 cells/mL and incubated in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37℃. The cells were treated with LPSF of different concentrations (100, 150, and 200 µg/mL, each co-treated with 30 ng/mL TNF-α) for 12 h. UCB-9260 (10 µmol/L) was used as a positive control. Cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 assay.

HEK293T cells were seeded and transfected as described above. Cells were then treated with TNF-α (20 ng/mL) alone or in combination with LPSF (15, 25, 50 ng/mL) or UCB-9260 (10 µmol/L) for 5 h. Luciferase expression was detected using the Nano-Glo® Luciferase Assay System (lot: 0000495402, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Results were expressed as fold change in relative luciferase units (RLU) relative to the control group.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS 26 software and presented as mean ± standard deviation. Differences between two groups were evaluated by independent samples t-test, whereas one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for comparisons among multiple groups. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Network pharmacology analysis identifies TNF as a key target of LPSF in RA

Following merging and deduplication of targets obtained from the databases, 469 LPSF targets and 667 RA targets were identified. The 99 potential targets associated with LPSF treatment for RA were identified using Venny 2.1.0 (Fig. 1A). To further investigate the core targets of LPSF in RA, these 99 common targets (their Degree values were list in Supplementary Table S2) were used to construct a PPI network, comprising 99 nodes and 1840 edges (Fig. 1B). Targets with a Degree value greater than twice the median were screened as core targets (Degree > 74). As shown in Fig. 1C, the core targets identified were TNF, IL-6, AKT1, IL-1β, and PTGS2. Notably, TNF exhibited the highest Degree value within the network. To elucidate the mechanism of LPSF in treating RA, KEGG and GO enrichment analyses were performed on the 99 common targets and visualized. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the TNF signaling pathway was among the most significantly enriched pathways associated with LPSF treatment for RA (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Table S3). Furthermore, GO analysis showed that LPSF primarily regulates inflammatory response (BP), which is critical in RA pathogenesis. CC analysis indicated enrichment in extracellular space and region, consistent with the secretion of inflammatory factors. MF analysis highlighted protein binding, suggesting LPSF may interfere with protein-protein interactions involved in inflammation (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Table S4-S6). Collectively, these findings suggest that LPSF exerts its anti-RA effects primarily through TNF target.

Identification of core components based on LPSF-component-target network

To further identify the key components of LPSF responsible for its anti-RA effects, we constructed the LPSF-component-target network (Fig. 2A). This network included 522 nodes and 1219 interacting edges, which were then analyzed to obtain the Degree values of the compounds in the LPSF-component-target network. The top 15 components with Degree values were quercetin, quercetin-3,3’-dimethyl ether, alpinetin, apigenin, alternariol monomethyl ether, kaempferol, luteolin, alternariol, (E)-cardamonin, galangin-3-methoxy, methyl 2,5-dihydroxybenzoate, L-malic acid, acacetin, galangin, and estragole (Fig. 2B). These compounds with higher Degree values may be involved in LPSF treatment of RA.

Verification of results by molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation

First, the co-crystal ligand of TNF-α (PDB ID: 7JRA) was re-docked with an RMSD of 2.32 Å (< 2.5 Å), and the docked conformation highly overlapped the original ligand (Supplementary Figure S1), confirming the reliability of the docking method18. Subsequently, molecular docking was performed between the key components and the TNF-α target, and a scatter plot was generated based on the results (Fig. 3). The binding energies lower than − 5.0 kcal/mol were considered to possess significant binding affinity, a widely accepted threshold in molecular docking studies19. All key components of LPSF exhibited strong binding affinity to the TNF-α target. In addition, UCB-9260, a known small-molecule TNF-α inhibitor, showed a binding energy of −10.97 kcal/mol. Notably, the binding affinities of the top 4 LPSF components (alternariol, galangin-3-methoxy, alternariol monomethyl ether, and alpinetin) were comparable to that of UCB-9260. These results indicate that TNF-α is a key target for LPSF in treating RA, and these components mediate its binding to TNF-α. The corresponding binding energies are presented in Table 2.

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed to evaluate the binding stability of the top 4 compounds (alternariol, galangin-3-methoxy, alternariol monomethyl ether, and alpinetin) with TNF-α. The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent accessible surface area (SASA), and principal component analysis-free energy landscape (PCA) were used to evaluate the stability of the trajectories. As shown in Fig. 4A, the RMSD values of alternariol, galangin-3-methoxy, and alpinetin remained below 0.3 nm throughout the simulation, indicating stable binding of the ligands. The RMSD curve of the TNF-α-alternariol monomethyl ether complex exhibited noticeable fluctuations. RMSF analysis showed that the residue fluctuations of TNF-α were generally low for most regions across all four complexes, confirming structural stability upon compound binding (Fig. 4B). The lower volatility observed in the Rg and SASA analyses also indicates that these complexes exhibit overall structural stability (Fig. 4C-D). As shown in Fig. 4E, PCA free energy landscape revealed well-defined low-energy conformational states for all four complexes. These results indicate that three components (alternariol, galangin-3-methoxy, alpinetin) of LPSF exhibited stable binding during the simulation.

Cytotoxicity testing of LPSF

The cytotoxicity of LPSF was evaluated in MH7A, L929, and NlucP-NF-κB reporter cells using the CCK-8 assay. Treatment with LPSF at concentrations (0.5–3.0 µg/mL) in MH7A cells (Fig. 5A), 100–2000 µg/mL in L929 cells (Fig. 5B), and 20–300 ng/mL in NlucP-NF-κB reporter cells (Fig. 5C) did not significantly affect cell viability. These results indicate that LPSF exhibits no cytotoxicity at the concentrations used in subsequent experiments.

LPSF inhibits TNF-α-induced migration of MH7A cells

Fibroblast migration is a major pathological mechanism in RA20. In this study, we investigated the effect of LPSF on the migration of MH7A cells. As shown in the wound healing assay (Fig. 6), TNF-α treatment significantly increased the cell migration rate compared to the Control group (P < 0.001). When cells were co-incubated with various concentrations of LPSF and TNF-α, the cell migration rate was significantly decreased after LPSF treatment (0.1, 0.25, and 0.5 µg/mL) compared to the TNF-α group (P < 0.05).

LPSF decreases TNF-α-induced expression of pro-inflammatory factors in MH7A cells

Based on the network pharmacology results, we found that LPSF treatment of RA was associated with inflammatory responses and pro-inflammatory factors. Therefore, we further investigated the anti-inflammatory effects of LPSF in vitro. As shown in Fig. 7A-D, the pro-inflammatory factors TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and COX-2 were significantly increased in MH7A cells by stimulation with TNF-α compared with the control group (P < 0.001). Interestingly, LPSF (0.1, 0.25, and 0.5 µg/mL) significantly reduced the mRNA expression levels of these pro-inflammatory factors compared to the TNF-α group (P < 0.05).

LPSF significantly inhibits TNF-α activity

L929 cells are a classical model for studying TNF-α inhibitory activity, while the NlucP-NF-κB reporter gene system reflects the activation of downstream signaling pathways by TNF-α21. Therefore, we established a TNF-α-induced L929 cell model and treated the cells with LPSF (100, 150, and 200 µg/mL). As shown in Fig. 9A, compared to the control group, cell viability was significantly decreased in the TNF-α-treated group (P < 0.001). Treatment with LPSF significantly increased cell viability compared to the TNF-α group (P < 0.001). Furthermore, we employed an NlucP-NF-κB reporter gene system. Stimulation with TNF-α significantly increased luciferase expression (Fig. 9B, P < 0.001). In contrast, LPSF treatment (15, 25, 50 ng/mL) significantly inhibited TNF-α-induced luciferase expression, reducing the RLU values (Fig. 9B, P < 0.001). These results were consistent with the TNF-α inhibitor UCB-9260, further demonstrating that TNF-α is a key target for LPSF to exert anti-RA activity.

Discussions

In this study, we employed network pharmacology to explore the mechanism by which LPSF treats RA, revealing a strong association with the TNF-α target. Molecular docking analysis and molecular dynamics simulations were then performed to evaluate the binding affinity and stability of the key components of LPSF to TNF-α. Finally, we experimentally validated that TNF-α is a key target of LPSF in the treatment of RA.

Firstly, we constructed a PPI network using common targets between LPSF and RA. Five core targets were screened based on Degree values, among which TNF-α displayed the highest Degree centrality. TNF-α, a potent pro-inflammatory cytokine, exerts pleiotropic effects on diverse cell types and critically contributes to the pathogenesis of chronic inflammatory diseases including RA22. Presently, multiple TNF-α biologic inhibitors (such as Adalimumab, Golimumab, and Infliximab) treat RA by blocking TNF-α activity23. In addition, we also found that the TNF signaling pathway and inflammatory responses were significantly enriched by KEGG pathway enrichment and GO analysis. TNF-α is the most important member of the TNF signaling pathway, which is activated and generates a series of inflammatory responses when TNF-α binds to its receptor24. In RA, TNF-α is produced by macrophages and fibroblasts under inflammatory conditions25, and the TNF-α produced further activates its downstream signaling pathways to generate inflammatory responses26,27. These results suggest that TNF-α plays a pivotal role in LPSF’s therapeutic mechanism for RA. Therefore, we next performed molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations to validate the binding between TNF-α and the components of LPSF.

Next, our study also employed molecular docking to predict the binding affinity of the top 15 components with TNF-α to identify the anti-RA components of LPSF. Relevant studies report that a molecular docking binding energy lower than − 5 kcal/mol indicates good affinity, with lower binding energy signifying stronger affinity28. The results predicted that 15 components exhibit favorable binding affinity with TNF-α (affinity < −5 kcal/mol). UCB-9260 is a well-characterized, orally active small-molecule TNF-α inhibitor that was reported in Nature Communications29,30. The binding energy of UCB-9260 to TNF-α is −10.97 kcal/mol. Notably, the binding affinities of top 4 components were comparable to those of the reference compounds, including alternariol (−10.0 kcal/mol), galangin-3-methoxy (−9.52 kcal/mol), alternariol monomethyl ether (−9.4 kcal/mol), and alpinetin (−9.38 kcal/mol). However, molecular dynamics simulations further revealed that the three compounds (alternariol, galangin-3-methoxy, and alpinetin) of LPSF can bind stably to TNF-α, suggesting their potential as active components contributing to the observed anti-TNF-α activity. These computational prediction results show that LPSF effectively inhibits TNF-α activity.

Furthermore, relevant studies indicate that the pathogenesis of RA involves immune-inflammatory responses, where cytokine dysregulation (characterized by altered secretion of various pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) plays a pivotal role in its pathophysiology, with TNF-α acting as a central regulator31,32. The MH7A cell line is an established immortalized rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblast model that closely recapitulates the pathological features of primary RA-fibroblast-like synoviocytes (RA-FLSs). It is widely utilized as an in vitro research model for RA33. Therefore, this study employed TNF-α-induced MH7A cells as an inflammatory model to mimic the pathological process of rheumatoid arthritis. It has been reported that TNF-α is detected as significantly elevated in the synovial fluid and serum of RA patients and promotes the proliferation and migration of RA-FLSs34. Consistent with this, our study also found a significant increase in MH7A cell migration induced by TNF-α, and LPSF inhibited this process. In addition, TNF-α activates and recruits FLSs, inducing the release of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α35. Activation of FLSs further releases pro-inflammatory mediators, such as COX-2, which play an important role in joint matrix degradation and pain transmission36,37. IL-1β induces proliferation of cells from FLSs leading to synovial membrane hyperplasia, bone resorption, and cartilage damage38. IL-6, a pro-inflammatory cytokine highly expressed in rheumatoid synovium, causes inflammation and cartilage destruction39. Our study demonstrates that TNF-α induction significantly increases mRNA expression and protein expression levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and COX-2 in MH7A cells, whereas LPSF treatment markedly reduces their expression levels. These findings provide preliminary evidence that LPSF exerts anti-RA effects at both the transcriptional and protein levels in vitro.

Finally, to further clarify that TNF-α is the target of LPSF against RA, we utilized a TNF-α-induced L929 cell model and the NlucP-NF-κB reporter gene system for validation. TNF-α, as a pleiotropic cytokine, exhibits cytotoxic effects on cells, with L929 cells being particularly sensitive to it40. The TNF-α-induced L929 cell injury model is commonly used for screening TNF-α inhibitors41. Additionally, the luciferase reporter gene assay is used for high-throughput drug screening and related drug activity detection due to its advantages of high sensitivity, rapid response, non-radioactivity, and long half-life42. In the pNL3.2.NF-κB-RE[NlucP/NF-κB-RE/Hygro] plasmid, the NF-κB response element (NF-κB-RE) is stimulated by TNF-α, subsequently driving the rapid transcriptional expression of the downstream NanoLuc®-PEST (NlucP) luciferase. Convincingly, our results demonstrate that LPSF significantly increased the TNF-α-induced L929 cell viability and reduced luciferase expression. These findings further underscore the critical role of the TNF-α target in the therapeutic mechanism of LPSF for RA.

In summary, this study integrated network pharmacology and experimental validation to demonstrate that TNF-α is the key target mediating LPSF’s anti-RA effects. Network analysis identified TNF-α as a core target, supported by functional enrichment of the TNF pathway. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation further predicted strong binding affinity between LPSF’s core anti-RA components and TNF-α. Experimentally, LPSF inhibited TNF-α-induced migration and pro-inflammatory mediator expression in MH7A cells. The targeting of TNF-α by LPSF was confirmed using TNF-α-sensitive L929 cytotoxicity and NF-κB reporter assays. These results establish TNF-α as the key target mediating LPSF’s anti-RA effects, thereby positioning LPSF as a promising multi-component TNF-α inhibitor. However, our study has the following limitations: Firstly, our study did not compare the key compounds identified in the results with any standard compounds in vivo or in vitro. Secondly, the network pharmacology analysis was based on reported compounds rather than the actual compounds of the LPSF extract determined by HPLC-MS. Finally, further in vivo studies are required to confirm the anti-RA efficacy of LPSF. Despite these limitations, our work establishes a foundation for understanding LPSF’s mechanism and thus supports its further investigation as a potential multi-component anti-TNF-α agent. In future research, our work will continue to prioritize further exploring the mechanisms and active components of LPSF in RA treatment.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides compelling evidence that inhibition of the TNF-α signaling pathway is a key mechanism underpinning LPSF’s efficacy against RA. By integrating network pharmacology, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and functional cellular assays, we demonstrated that LPSF attenuates TNF-α-induced MH7A migration, decreases the expression of key pro-inflammatory factors, and suppresses TNF-α-induced cytotoxicity and luciferase expression. Our findings provide preliminary evidence that LPSF exhibits significant anti-TNF-α activity, offering a basis for its modern clinical application in RA. These findings position LPSF as a promising multi-component TNF-α inhibitor. Going forward, we will conduct HPLC-MS and animal-level studies to further validate the active components, targets, and mechanisms identified in this study, aiming to provide additional scientific evidence for the clinical application of LPSF in RA treatment.
