The multifactor chain mediation model of anxiety in the MSM population: the pathways of perceived social support, self-esteem, and psychological resilience
Authors: Yiwei Zhou, Zejie Zhang, Fanglv Xiang, Minlu Xu, Zumu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates how perceived social support is associated with anxiety among men who have sex with men (MSM). A cross-sectional online survey was conducted from March to June, 2024, recruiting 1,070 MSM participants via internet platforms. Measures included the Perceived Social Support Scale-12 (PSSS-12), Self-Esteem Scale-10 (SES), Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale-25 (CD-RISC-25), and Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7). Using PROCESS Model 6 and the Bootstrap method, a significant negative correlation was found between perceived social support and anxiety (r = − 0.187, P < 0.01). The total effect of perceived social support on anxiety was − 0.0970 (95% CI: − 0.1181 to − 0.0759), with mediation accounting for 53.92% of the total effect. Specifically, self-esteem (effect value = − 0.0444) and psychological resilience (effect value = − 0.0054) significantly mediated the relationship, accounting for 45.77% and 5.57% of the total mediation effect, respectively. The joint indirect effect through self-esteem and psychological resilience accounted for an additional 2.58% of the total effect. The study highlights that social support is linked to lower anxiety in MSM by enhancing self-esteem and resilience, emphasizing their importance for MSM mental health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46302-6.Keywords: MSM, Perceived social support, Psychological resilience, Anxiety, Self-esteem, Chain mediation modelSubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Anxiety is a prevalent and complex mental health challenge among men who have sex with men (MSM), influenced by a range of interrelated psychosocial and health-related factors. Data from China underscore the severity of this issue, with reported anxiety prevalence rates as high as 21.2% in this population1. This heightened vulnerability among Chinese MSM cannot be fully understood without considering the pronounced socio-cultural pressures they face, most notably the deep-seated norms of filial piety (xiao). While family-oriented pressures related to marriage and lineage are common in many cultures, and are particularly salient in other East Asian contexts such as Japan and Korea, the specific manifestation of filial piety within Chinese society creates a powerful and culturally specific source of minority stress. These norms impose intense expectations regarding marriage and parenthood, often placing MSM in direct conflict with family obligations and societal acceptance, thereby constituting a profound and culturally specific source of minority stress1. Crucially, the impact of these pressures is not uniform across the diverse MSM population. The relevance of filial piety may be particularly acute for those who are not “out” to their families or who face pressure to enter heterosexual marriages to fulfill family expectations. Conversely, for MSM who are in accepting families or who have navigated these expectations through alternative life paths, the impact may be more indirect. It is worth noting that MSM who identify as heterosexual experience filial piety pressures in ways that are also distinctive and should not be generalized.

In addition to these socio-cultural pressures, MSM face considerable health threats, including a notably elevated risk of Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection2–4, along with other sexually transmitted or chronic conditions. Beyond physical health implications, these risks function as persistent psychological stressors, contributing to growing scholarly concern regarding the mental well-being of this group5,6, In particular, depression and anxiety have emerged as key focuses within this research landscape7–9. Among the identified risk factors, inadequate social support is widely acknowledged as a significant correlate of anxiety; however, the precise pathways and mechanisms through which it operates require further elucidation10.

In recent years, studies have gradually revealed the important roles of social support, self-esteem, and psychological resilience in alleviating anxiety11. Social support refers to the emotional, material, and informational assistance that individuals receive from their social networks12,13. For the MSM population, a lack of social support is often closely associated with increased anxiety9. Research indicates that MSM individuals frequently face social pressures related to their sexual orientation, often without adequate social support, which exacerbates their anxiety. For example, family and social rejection, fear of HIV infection, and the need to conceal their same-sex sexual behaviour—conceptually distinct from concealing one’s sexual orientation, particularly for those who identify as heterosexual despite engaging in such behaviour—can all contribute to heightened psychological stress9. Therefore, social support plays a crucial protective role in alleviating anxiety among the MSM population.

Self-esteem refers to the degree of positive evaluation and self-acceptance an individual holds14. Social support is significantly associated with self-esteem levels13,15. When individuals perceive more social support, their self-esteem tends to increase accordingly. Higher self-esteem, in turn, is significantly associated with lower anxiety levels16. Specifically, individuals with higher self-esteem are often more confident in coping with stress and challenges, less likely to be overwhelmed by negative emotions, and thus exhibit lower levels of anxiety12,16. However, the exact mechanisms remain unclear16.

Psychological resilience refers to an individual’s ability to adapt and recover in the face of stress and adversity9,12. Research indicates that individuals with higher psychological resilience are better able to regulate their emotional states when experiencing anxiety, thereby reducing anxiety levels9,17. For example, MSM individuals with higher psychological resilience can employ positive psychological adjustments and coping strategies to alleviate anxiety when facing social discrimination and sexual orientation-related pressures18.

Social support, self-esteem, and psychological resilience exhibit a mutually reinforcing relationship12,19–21. Social support is associated with higher levels of self-esteem and psychological resilience9,13. Specifically, social support helps individuals build a positive self-perception by providing emotional comfort and practical assistance, thereby boosting self-esteem. At the same time, social support equips individuals with resources to cope with stress, thereby enhancing psychological resilience. Both self-esteem and psychological resilience play crucial mediating roles in the relationship between social support and anxiety12. Social support can indirectly alleviate anxiety symptoms by improving self-esteem and psychological resilience21.

Building on this established interplay among social support, self-esteem, and resilience, we further hypothesize a specific sequential mechanism for the MSM population: perceived social support reduces anxiety through a chain mediation pathway in which self-esteem precedes psychological resilience (“Perceived Social Support → Self-esteem → Psychological Resilience → Anxiety”). This postulated order is grounded in the conceptualization that self-esteem, as a core and relatively stable evaluation of self-worth, constitutes a fundamental psychological resource that is directly fostered by supportive social environments16,22. An enhanced sense of self-worth, in turn, provides a critical internal foundation for developing and mobilizing broader adaptive capacities, such as psychological resilience, when confronting stressors12,20. This “self-evaluation to adaptive capacity” pathway has been proposed in recent theoretical and cross-sectional research involving stressed populations12,20,23. While an alternative sequence is theoretically conceivable and longitudinal evidence specifically testing the temporal order between self-esteem and resilience remains limited, the proposed order aligns with the theoretical conceptualization of self-esteem as a more proximal and stable self-construct that may facilitate the development of situation-specific adaptive capacities24. To address the aforementioned gap in research on MSM, the present study aims to test this specific chain mediation model within the context of Chinese MSM.

Although existing studies have indicated that self-esteem and psychological resilience mediate the relationship between social support and anxiety25,26, most current research is limited to simple mediation models27,28, failing to fully reveal the complex and dynamic interactions among multiple psychological factors13. Given the numerous and intertwined factors influencing anxiety, relying solely on simple mediation models is insufficient to comprehensively dissect its underlying mechanisms12. Additionally, although the chain mediation model is widely used in psychological research, to our knowledge, there are no studies that have used it to explore the mediating role of self-esteem and psychological resilience in the relationship between social support and anxiety among MSM populations18.

Furthermore, existing research still has several limitations. On the one hand, the mechanisms of action remain unclear10. While some studies have found that social support can alleviate anxiety by enhancing self-esteem and psychological resilience, the specific pathways and influencing factors require further exploration. On the other hand, other potential mediating variables (e.g., social identity, cultural background) may also influence the relationship between social support and anxiety29.

Therefore, investigating the psychosocial mechanisms of anxiety among Chinese MSM necessitates a framework that explicitly accounts for these culturally embedded stressors. To this end, this study employs a chain mediation effect model to analyze the relationships among social support, self-esteem, psychological resilience, and anxiety in the MSM population. The chain mediation effect model is a statistical approach used to examine the interactions among multiple mediating variables. In recent years, it has been widely applied in mental health research to uncover the complex relationships between different psychological factors. After searching multiple databases, such as PubMed/Medline, EMBASE, Web of Science, SinoMed/CBM, CNKI, no studies have been found that utilize the chain mediation effect model to analyze the relationships among social support, self-esteem, psychological resilience, and anxiety in the MSM population. This study aims to apply the chain mediation effect model to the MSM population, assessing the impact of perceived social support on anxiety and the chain mediating roles of self-esteem and psychological resilience in the relationship between perceived social support and anxiety, and to provide a scientific basis for targeted mental health interventions in the MSM population by exploring the mediating mechanisms of social support, self-esteem, and psychological resilience on anxiety.

Participants and methods

Participants

We carried out a web-based cross-sectional survey conducted between March and June 2024. The study utilized the MSM population’s internet-based comprehensive HIV prevention and control service platform (“Sunshine Test”) to distribute the questionnaire. The questionnaire was disseminated through social platforms commonly used within the MSM community, such as WeChat groups and QQ groups. To protect personal privacy, the completion of the survey was conducted anonymously. The survey data were collected using the Jinshuju website (www.jinshuju.com). This study employed a convenience sampling method to recruit MSM participants through online platforms. The sample was predominantly drawn from Zhejiang Province, China, with supplementary participants from other provinces, as verified through IP address geolocation. To enhance participation rates among the MSM population, we implemented an incentive policy to encourage active and timely completion of the questionnaire. Upon successful completion of the survey, participants received a 10 CNY (approximately 1.5 USD) monetary compensation, primarily distributed as mobile phone credit vouchers. To maintain data integrity, we restricted submissions to one response per IP address, thereby preventing duplicate entries.

Given that recruitment was conducted through a service platform focused on HIV prevention and control—an area closely tied to sexual behavior—inclusion criteria were defined based on behavioral indicators: men who self-reported oral or anal sex with men in the past year; age ≥ 18 years; basic reading and questionnaire completion skills; and ability to operate a mobile phone correctly. Neither gender identity nor sexual orientation identity was assessed in this study.

Data were collected from 1113 eligible participants. After excluding incomplete or invalid questionnaires, the final analytical sample comprised 1,070 participants. This sample size exceeds the minimum requirement of 200 for adequate power in mediation analysis using the bootstrap method30, and post-hoc power analysis (G*Power 3.1)31, confirmed achieved power > 0.99 for detecting small-to-medium effects (f2 = 0.05).

Participant exclusion and data cleaning

Participants were excluded using three criteria prior to data analysis: (1) Questionnaire incompleteness: Records were excluded if they contained missing responses for more than 5% of the items on any core psychological scale (i.e., the Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS-12), the Self-Esteem Scale (SES-10), and the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-25)32 or for any key sociodemographic or health-related variable (e.g., age, HIV status). (2) Logical inconsistencies: Responses exhibiting clear contradictory patterns (e.g., straight-lining or providing antithetical answers within a validated scale) were removed. (3) Insufficient response time: Questionnaires completed in less than 2 min (120 s) were excluded—an empirical threshold based on the guideline of 2 s per item for adequate responding33, questionnaire length, pre-test data, and prior methodological research34.

We evaluated the 2-min exclusion threshold through three sensitivity analyses. Relaxing the cutoff to 180 s retained only two additional questionnaires, both of which were subsequently excluded for logical inconsistencies. Among the five time-excluded questionnaires, four also failed other quality checks (logical inconsistencies or missing data) and one showed straight-lining, indicating substantial overlap with other exclusion criteria. Comparisons between time-excluded and retained samples revealed no systematic differences in observable variables (all p > 0.05).

Handling of remaining missing data

After applying the above exclusion criteria, the remaining dataset contained minimal, random item-level missing values within scales. These values were handled using multiple imputation in SPSS (e.g., 20 imputed datasets). Each imputed dataset was analyzed separately using the PROCESS macro, and the results were pooled to obtain final parameter estimates and inferences.

Contents of survey

The questionnaire was specifically designed for MSM populations using a closed-ended format. It began with an introductory section outlining the study’s objectives and significance. The content of the survey consists of three parts: socio-demographic basic information of the respondents, laboratory testing information, and psychological scales. The basic socio-demographic information includes age, occupation, education level, marital status, personal income, religious belief, employment status, and place of residence. Laboratory testing information includes syphilis testing status, hepatitis C testing status, and HIV testing status. See Supplementary File S1 for details.

Control variables

To account for potential confounding effects, several control variables were selected based on theoretical frameworks and prior evidence. Socio-demographic controls included age, education level, marital status, personal monthly income, employment status, urban/rural residence, and religious belief, all of which are established determinants of mental health and resource access in minority populations1,8,9,43. Given the salient health concerns within the MSM population, we also controlled for HIV, syphilis, and hepatitis C testing status, as awareness or avoidance of these conditions represents a significant source of anxiety5,6. Controlling for these factors aligns with the Minority Stress Model1 and helps isolate the unique relationships between perceived social support, self-esteem, psychological resilience, and anxiety.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. Bivariate correlations among variables were examined using Kendall’s tau-b, and multicollinearity was assessed by calculating variance inflation factors (VIFs) for all predictors; VIF values exceeding 10 were considered indicative of potential multicollinearity44. To test our primary hypothesis regarding the sequential mediation pathway, we employed Hayes’s PROCESS macro (Model 6) for SPSS45, a regression-based path analytic approach. This pre-specified chain mediation model specified perceived social support as the independent variable (X), anxiety as the dependent variable (Y), self-esteem as the first mediator (M1), and psychological resilience as the second mediator (M2), corresponding to the hypothesized path X → M1 → M2 → Y. Participants’ socio-demographic and laboratory variables were included as covariates. We acknowledge that this method is conceptually and procedurally distinct from structural equation modeling (SEM). While SEM evaluates overall model fit to observed covariance structures using global fit indices (e.g., CFI, TLI, RMSEA), the regression-based framework of PROCESS emphasizes precise estimation and significance testing of specific direct and indirect effects through bootstrapping. Accordingly, model adequacy in this framework is evaluated based on the explanatory variance (R2) accounted for by each constituent regression and the statistical precision of effect estimates—as indicated by bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals—rather than global model fit statistics. The significance of mediation effects was evaluated using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals generated from 5,000 resamples; effects were considered statistically significant if 95% confidence intervals excluded zero. Total, direct, and specific indirect effects, along with their 95% confidence intervals and the proportion of the total effect mediated, were obtained from this analysis. The significance level (α) was set at 0.05 for all tests.

Robustness checks were conducted by re-estimating the chain mediation model with alternative sets of control variables to assess the robustness of the indirect effects.

Since the core variables of this study (social support, self-esteem, psychological resilience, and anxiety) and selected demographic variables (age, income, and education level) are either continuous or ordinal in nature, we employed Kendall’s tau-b correlation coefficient to examine the bivariate associations among these seven variables. Other nominal categorical variables (e.g., occupation, marital status, employment status, religious belief, place of residence, and HIV/syphilis/hepatitis C testing status) were not included in the correlation matrix. In the subsequent mediation analysis, these nominal variables were converted into dummy variables and entered into the regression model as covariates to control for their potential confounding effects. Regarding anxiety, although a GAD-7 score of ≥ 10 was used as the clinical threshold for anxiety symptoms in descriptive analyses, the continuous raw scores were retained for the correlational and mediation analyses to preserve greater informational richness and statistical power.

Ethics review

This study was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Kangning Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Approval No.: 2023014). We conducted research in line with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice. Participants were explicitly assured of strict anonymity and comprehensive data protection measures. Prior to the survey, informed consent was obtained from each participant. Participants who did not provide informed consent were excluded from the survey.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the MSM population

A total of 1,113 MSM individuals were recruited for this survey. After excluding 43 invalid questionnaires—33 due to incomplete responses, 7 for logical inconsistencies, and 3 for insufficient completion time—1,070 valid responses were retained, corresponding to an effective response rate of 96.1%. Among the respondents, 210 respondents (19.6%) exhibited moderate to severe anxiety symptoms. The average age of participants was 28.5 ± 8.5 years, with 54.1% (579/1,070) aged 20–29 years. Unmarried individuals accounted for 79.3% (848/1,070), and those with a high school education or below accounted for 30.5% (326/1,070). Participants with a monthly income of ≥ 6,000 CNY accounted for 37.4% (400/1,070), while 23.9% (256/1,070) identified as Buddhist. Those with stable employment accounted for 28.7% (307/1,070), and 33.7% (361/1,070) resided in urban areas. Additionally, 24.9% (267/1,070) had not been tested for hepatitis C, 23.0% (246/1,070) for syphilis, and 12.2% (131/1,070) for HIV. See Table 3.

Prior to regression analysis, collinearity among independent variables, control variables, and mediating variables was examined. Using perceived social support, self-esteem, psychological resilience, and control variables as independent variables and anxiety as the dependent variable, collinearity diagnostics revealed that all variance inflation factor (VIF) values were < 10 and tolerance values were > 0.1, indicating no multicollinearity issues among the independent variables.

Estimated values of different indirect effect pathways

In the SPSS mediation effect model, C1, C2 and C3 typically represent the estimated values of different indirect effect pathways in chain mediation analysis. Specifically: C1 refers to the indirect effect of the first mediator (M1) alone on the dependent variable (Y); C2 refers to the indirect effect of the second mediator (M2) alone on the dependent variable (Y); C3 refers to the indirect effect of both mediators (M1 and M2) jointly on the dependent variable (Y).

These values help understand the magnitude of the roles played by different mediators in the effect of the independent variable (X) on the dependent variable (Y). The effect value of self-esteem on anxiety was − 0.0390, the effect value of psychological resilience on anxiety was − 0.0419, and the combined effect value of both self-esteem and psychological resilience on anxiety was − 0.0029, as shown in Table 6.

Assessment of selection bias

To assess potential selection bias from the questionnaire exclusion process, we conducted the following sensitivity analyses: (1) Demographic comparison: No statistically significant differences were observed between the excluded group (n = 43) and the included group (n = 1,070) in terms of age, education level, marital status, or other available sociodemographic variables (all p > 0.05). (2) Pattern of missing data: Most invalid questionnaires exhibited a “drop-out” pattern, in which respondents discontinued participation partway through the survey, rather than selectively omitting sensitive items. This suggests that exclusions were likely due to non‑substantive factors—such as declining engagement or limited time—rather than being systematically linked to specific psychological traits. In light of the low exclusion rate (3.9%) and the absence of systematic differences between groups, we conclude that any resulting selection bias is unlikely to substantially affect the overall study findings.

Discussions

Theoretical background and research gap

Anxiety constitutes a major public health concern within MSM communities due to its high prevalence and multifaceted etiology8,43,46. In China, the severity of this issue is particularly pronounced, with a pooled anxiety detection rate of 32.2% among MSM—significantly higher than in the general population8. This elevated risk is corroborated by longitudinal data, such as a two-year cohort study in western China reporting an anxiety prevalence of 26.03% among 1,771 MSM47. While these findings consistently highlight the commonality of anxiety in this population, its manifestation is influenced by a complex interplay of methodological, social, and cultural factors48,49. Crucially, the underlying psychosocial mechanisms—specifically, how perceived social support may exert its protective effect by fostering key internal resources such as self-esteem and psychological resilience within the Chinese cultural context—remain inadequately elucidated. The present study aims to address this gap by constructing and testing a sequential mediation model to unravel these intricate pathways.

Key findings: the chain mediation pathway and its cultural significance

This study identified a significant chain mediation pathway in which perceived social support is associated with anxiety levels through self-esteem and psychological resilience. This pathway holds particular significance within the Chinese cultural context. Traditional Chinese filial piety culture, which emphasizes family responsibility and procreation, is linked to unique psychological pressures on the MSM population50. In this context, a perceived lack of family support or experiences of social rejection show a connection to lower self-esteem, which in turn is related to diminished psychological resilience in coping with stress, and finally co-occurs with higher levels of anxiety51. Thus, our model provides evidence for the protective association of social support and underscores the crucial role of self-esteem as the first-stage mediator in the Chinese context—suggesting it may be a key psychological factor for sustaining resilience when facing combined familial and societal pressures.

Direct effect and the predominance of indirect pathways

This study investigated the mechanisms through which perceived social support relates to anxiety among the MSM population by constructing a chain mediation model. The results revealed a significant direct negative association between perceived social support and anxiety (effect = − 0.0447), consistent with prior findings20. Crucially, however, the indirect effects mediated by self-esteem and psychological resilience accounted for the majority (53.92%) of the total effect. This pattern, where indirect pathways outweigh the direct effect, suggests that for Chinese MSM, the protective function of social support operates primarily not merely as a stress buffer, but more profoundly through the cultivation of internal psychological resources. This interpretation aligns with the “Gain Spiral” model of resource conservation and acquisition52, positing that external resources (e.g., social support) can be transformed and amplified into internal resources (e.g., enhanced self-esteem and resilience), thereby providing a more sustainable foundation for coping with chronic minority stress.

Distinctive role of psychological resilience: conduit versus buffer

The non-significant direct effect of psychological resilience on anxiety, contrasted with its significant indirect effects, warrants further theoretical consideration. This pattern may reflect the nature of chronic, identity-based stigma faced by MSM populations. Drawing on the updated minority stress theory, we posit that for sexual and gender minorities, resilience often operates less as a direct psychological “shield” and more as a capacity to harness protective resources—functioning primarily as a mediator or moderator in the stress-health pathway53. In our model, resilience significantly transmitted the benefits of perceived social support and was itself bolstered by higher self-esteem. This synergistic process suggests that, for Chinese MSM facing filial and social pressures, resilience is closely tied to positive self-worth and supportive networks, acting as a “conveyor” that amplifies the effects of upstream resources. Examining such specific mechanisms is crucial for clarifying the precise role of resilience54. Future research should test this hypothesis by comparing resilience’s function in MSM samples versus general populations facing different stressor types.

Protective function of social support: beyond stress buffering

Perceived social support is significantly and negatively associated with anxiety levels among the MSM population, reaffirming its role as a critical protective factor24,55,56. This association holds particular salience for MSM, who frequently face sexual orientation-based discrimination and social rejection—core components of minority stress51. Within this context of marginalization, social support transcends general stress buffering. “Alternative” or “chosen” support from peers, partners, or accepting family members not only provides emotional sustenance but also validates identity and directly counters internalized stigma45. Thus, for many MSM, robust social support networks function as an indispensable lifeline for mental health preservation. This underscores that interventions aimed at reducing anxiety must prioritize the cultivation and strengthening of these affirming support systems.

Differential mediating roles of self-esteem and psychological resilience

An examination of the specific mediation pathways reveals a notable disparity: the indirect effect via self-esteem accounted for 45.77% of the total effect, substantially larger than the 5.57% contributed by the path through psychological resilience alone. This suggests that the enhancement of self-esteem may constitute a more central and proximal mechanism linking perceived social support to reduced anxiety among Chinese MSM. This aligns with the theoretical conception of self-esteem as a core self-evaluation that serves as a primary psychological buffer against social appraisal and stress57. In the context of minority stress, perceived social support likely functions first by affirming an individual’s self-worth and mitigating internalized stigma. This fortified self-esteem, in turn, provides a crucial foundation for the development of broader adaptive capacities, such as psychological resilience. Thus, our hypothesized chain (perceived social support → self-esteem → psychological resilience → anxiety) delineates a sequential process wherein external resources are internalized into core self-regard before being translated into more dynamic coping capabilities.

Theoretical integration: self-esteem as a core resource and resilience as a dynamic process

Notably, the statistical support for this role distinction stems from the finding that while the direct path from psychological resilience to anxiety was non-significant, the corresponding serial indirect effect tested via bootstrapping was significant. This indicates that psychological resilience does not function as an independent, direct protective factor, but rather as a secondary, synergistic adaptive capacity whose effect unfolds dynamically within its sequential linkage with self-esteem. In contrast, the significant direct effect of self-esteem on anxiety underscores its role as a core and proximal protective factor—a positive self-evaluation that directly buffers distress. This pattern aligns with the view that resilience operates less as a static trait and more as a dynamic process of resource mobilization58. Within our chain model, perceived social support first enhances self-esteem, which in turn provides the essential psychological foundation enabling the effective deployment of resilient coping strategies to manage stressors and ultimately contribute to the alleviation of anxiety. Thus, the model delineates a sequential process in which external support is internalized into core self-regard, subsequently activating broader adaptive capacities.

Translating findings into practice: a stepped care framework following the mediation chain

Guided by the identified chain-mediation mechanism, we propose a tiered intervention framework that corresponds to the sequential pathway in our model: (1) Foundation Tier: Building Secure Support Networks. Initiatives should prioritize creating safe, affirming spaces, such as peer-led online communities. These are particularly crucial for geographically dispersed or less visible MSM to counteract isolation and provide identity-affirming connections59, (2) Core Tier: Strengthening Self-Esteem. Interventions should directly target the enhancement of self-worth by addressing internalized stigma. Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) group interventions can be effective in challenging negative self-schemas and fostering a positive self-evaluation18, (3) Enhancement Tier: Cultivating Resilience Skills. Once a stronger sense of self-esteem is established, skills-based training in stress management, problem-solving, and emotional regulation can be introduced. This “resilience training” equips individuals to actively deploy adaptive strategies when facing stressors, leveraging the foundation of secure self-regard.

Implications and future directions

This study yields significant implications for both clinical practice and public health. From a clinical perspective, interventions should adopt a multi-tiered approach informed by the identified chain-mediation pathway. First, strengthening perceived social support could be achieved through facilitating peer-led online support groups and community-building activities, capitalizing on platforms akin to those used in this study. Second, structured group therapies focusing on cognitive restructuring and skills training may effectively enhance self-esteem and psychological resilience, thereby interrupting the progression toward anxiety. It is crucial that these interventions be culturally adapted to address context-specific stressors, such as the familial pressures rooted in filial piety norms within Chinese society.

From a public health standpoint, integrating routine mental health screening—using tools such as the GAD-7—into existing sexual health and HIV prevention services for MSM is essential to enable early identification and referral of anxiety symptoms. Concurrently, anti-stigma campaigns and sensitivity training for healthcare providers are needed to foster a more supportive social environment, which in turn lays the groundwork for sustainable enhancement of social support.

Future research should extend this work in several directions. First, longitudinal or experimental designs that actively manipulate perceived social support are warranted to establish causal relationships within the proposed model. Second, to improve generalizability, studies should recruit more diverse and representative samples, allowing for subgroup analyses—such as comparing transgender and cisgender MSM—to test the model’s applicability across populations. Third, investigating additional potential mediators (e.g., internalized homophobia) and moderators (e.g., degree of community connectedness) would provide a more nuanced understanding of anxiety development in this population and help inform more personalized intervention strategies.

Study limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged to appropriately contextualize its findings. First, One key limitation stems from the cross‑sectional design, which precludes causal inference. This constraint is particularly relevant to the proposed chain mediation model (X → M₁ → M2 → Y), because not only is causality unverified, but the assumed temporal order of the mediators themselves remains empirically unsupported. Specifically, while we hypothesized that self‑esteem precedes psychological resilience based on theoretical grounds, alternative sequences (e.g., resilience → self‑esteem) or bidirectional dynamics are plausible. Thus, the model represents an exploratory rather than a confirmed causal pathway, and findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Second, the representativeness of the sample is constrained. Despite a relatively large sample size (n = 1070), online recruitment may have introduced selection bias, favoring individuals with stronger community ties, greater willingness to disclose their sexual orientation, and higher health awareness—potentially differing systematically from more concealed or less connected peers. Moreover, the geographic concentration of participants in Zhejiang Province limits the examination of contextual factors such as urban–rural disparities or regional variations in social support, which may affect generalizability. Third, although the scales used show good reliability and validity, subtle inaccuracies could have arisen due to the localization process. Finally, several potentially influential confounders—including internalized homophobia and psychiatric comorbidities—were not assessed and may have influenced the observed associations.

Additional limitations pertain to sample definition and composition. The operational definition of MSM was based on behavioral and biological sex criteria (“any biological male assigned male at birth who has sex with men”), which aligns with conventional public health frameworks but diverges from contemporary emphasis on gender identity diversity. Notably, this definition excludes transgender men (assigned female at birth but identifying as male) and does not encompass non-binary or other gender-diverse individuals who have sex with men, thus limiting the generalizability of findings to these populations. Furthermore, although the sample contained diversity in sexual identity, the study did not report subgroup distributions or conduct stratified or moderation analyses. Existing literature suggests that meaningful psychosocial differences may exist between cisgender, transgender, and other gender-diverse MSM, and merging these groups could obscure distinctive mechanisms60,61. The definition also did not explicitly incorporate emerging sexual identity categories (e.g., pansexual, demisexual), which may further affect interpretability across identity groups. Future studies should therefore employ more inclusive recruitment and classification strategies, clearly report sample composition across gender and sexual identity dimensions, and conduct subgroup-specific analyses to better capture population heterogeneity in psychosocial pathways.

Conclusions

In summary, this study is the first to empirically test and support a chain mediation model linking perceived social support to anxiety through self-esteem and psychological resilience within a Chinese MSM population. This finding not only delineates a specific psychosocial pathway through which social support influences mental health but also underscores the pivotal role of self-esteem as a critical hub under culture-specific pressures, such as those rooted in filial piety. By demonstrating this sequential mechanism, our work bridges minority stress theory with a resource-gain perspective, offering a refined, process-oriented understanding of resilience. Consequently, it provides precise targets for developing culturally adapted interventions that focus on fortifying these internal psychological processes to mitigate anxiety among Chinese MSM.
