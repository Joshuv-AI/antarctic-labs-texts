The use of the raw pulse/breathing rate ratio (PBR) as a predictor of mortality and criticality in the emergency department: a retrospective study
Authors: Tongyan Zhang, Xin Yang, Sha Luo, Yanyan Ren, Yan LI, Yazhu Hou, Yajun Du, Yue Xi, Zheyi Dong, Xiumei Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore the relationships between the pulse/breathing rate ratio (PBR) and the risk of death and critical illness.DesignThis was a retrospective observational study.SettingThis was a single-centre study from a tertiary hospital in Tianjin, China.ParticipantsThe study population consisted of patients aged ≥16 years who underwent consultation and were monitored for medical purposes at the clinic.InterventionsBetween April 2021 and December 2021. Before the patients received any medical intervention, vital signs were measured, and the PBR and the National Early Warning Score (NEWS) were calculated on the basis of the above measured indicators.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe associations of PBRs with death and critical illness were evaluated.ResultsA total of 1048 outpatients with fever were included. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) bars were used to explore potential nonlinear associations between PBR and mortality and critical illness. The abilities of the PBR and NEWS to predict the risk of death and critical illness were compared through decision curve analysis (DCA). The RCS showed a U-shaped nonlinear distribution of associations between PBRs and death and critical illness (nonlinear p values of p=0.036 and p=0.005, respectively). The risk of mortality was lowest between 4.6 and 6.2 for PBR, with the risk of mortality increasing progressively with decreasing PBR for PBR<4.6, and the risk of mortality increasing progressively with increasing PBR for PBR>6.2. The risk of critical illness was lowest when the PBR was between 4.6 and 5.5, and the risk increased gradually with decreasing PBR for PBR<4.6 and with increasing PBR for PBR>5.5. The DCA results revealed that the value of the PBR in predicting death was similar to that of the NEWS. In the DCA, the net benefit achieved by the NEWS ranged from 3% to 10% threshold probability, and the net benefit achieved by the PBR ranged from 4% to 10% threshold probability, with similar net benefits for the NEWS and PBR and 1% for both the PBR and the NEWS when the threshold probability was 7% (equivalent to correctly identifying 10 mortalities per 100 patients).ConclusionsThe PBR helps to predict the risk of mortality and critical illness in acutely ill patients, and its value in predicting mortality is similar to that of the NEWS.

Background

Early identification of patients at risk of death or critical illness is an important task for departments treating acutely ill patients.1 Owing to the presence of physiological reserves and compensatory mechanisms in the human body,2 the typical symptoms of tachycardia, tachypnoea, peripheral wetness and coldness, hypotension, etc, may not be present in the early stages of acute and critical illnesses such as septic shock.3 For example, some young patients usually do not show symptoms until late in life because of their compensatory capacity, whereas some immunosuppressed patients often do not have obvious clinical manifestations because of a poor stress response. In resource-limited medical conditions, there is no equipment to evaluate criticality, such as what is usually done in the intensive care unit (ICU) and resuscitation room. Therefore, favourable risk assessment tools for the early identification of patients at risk of death or critical illness, with no equipment, are needed.

Various risk assessment tools, such as the National Early Warning Score (NEWS),4 the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II score5 and the quick Sequential Organ Function Assessment score,6 have been developed, among which the NEWS has been shown to outperform other risk assessment tools in terms of accuracy, sensitivity and specificity in predicting death and ICU admission in emergency triage.4711 However, there are some drawbacks to the NEWS; for example, it may be less sensitive when used in the context of long-term chronic diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and acute brain injury.12 13 The NEWS is not sufficient for early risk stratification of all patients, as a high proportion of these patients have long-term chronic diseases14 15 or are at risk of acute brain injury.16 17 The use of the NEWS in the Chinese healthcare system is low, and there is no common language among healthcare professionals; on the other hand, it is difficult to obtain all the parameter values of the NEWS in a short period of time under restricted conditions,18 which hinders early assessment of patients’ disease status. Therefore, risk assessment tools with wider application scopes that are easier to use are needed.

The pulse/breathing rate (BR) ratio (PBR) is the result of dividing the pulse rate (PR) by the BR. Previous studies have shown that the most sensitive indicators of change in clinical practice in emergency medicine are usually not changes in consciousness or blood pressure but changes in PR and respiration.19 Although the PBR is derived from the PR and BR, it not only reflects the PR and respiratory status but also emphasises the coordination of the core organs of the body and reflects the function of the major organs. Scholkmann and Wolf,20 and others, believe that changes in PBR are influenced by the cardiopulmonary system,21 22 cardiovascular system23 and nervous system24 and can simultaneously reflect cardiopulmonary function, blood perfusion and nervous system function, which is a powerful parameter for responding to the complex regulatory state of the heart, lung and brain. There are also many records about PBR prediction of death in ancient China, such as ‘Pulse Classic’,25 which says, “One breath accompanied by 4 pulses is normal, 5 is not a major problem, 3 is a slow pulse, 1–2 signals critical illness, two breaths accompanied by 1 pulse signals death, 6 is a rapid pulse, 7 is the maximum in the normal state, 8 is critical, 9 is severe and 10 is an even more critical state.” These records suggest that even ancient physicians realised that a normal PBR is usually between 4 and 5. Modern research also suggests that the number of normal PBRs is between approximately 4 and 5.26 27 However, current research on PBR has focused mainly on human physiological development,28 cancer29 and other non-acute and critical conditions,28 neglecting the study of PBR as an important predictor of death and critical illness.

The PBR measures PR and BR. The PR is an important risk indicator for mortality,30 31 and studies have shown that both high and low PR fluctuations in patients in the ICU are associated with increased mortality and that PR fluctuations are associated with a U-shaped distribution of mortality risk.32 The BR is an extremely sensitive indicator in the early stages of critical illnesses.33 When the body is deprived of oxygen, the BR is usually the first sign of change.34 Studies have shown that the BR is an important predictor of serious events such as cardiac arrest and admission to the ICU.35 36 Both indicators have the same U-shaped distribution, but their distribution patterns are not necessarily synchronised, giving rise to outliers in PBRs. There is still a lack of research on the predictive value of outliers in PBR for critical illness and death.

However, PBRs respond to changes in PR and respiratory rate and play important roles in regulating the function of vital organs. However, there is still a lack of relevant studies on the use of PBR for predicting the risk of death and critical illness. Therefore, the aim of our study was to elucidate the relationship between PBR and the risk of death or critical illness and to compare the predictive effects of PBR and the NEWS on the risk of early detection of death and critical illness.

Methods

Design and setting of the study

This was a single-centre retrospective observational study. The study was conducted in the fever clinic of a tertiary care hospital in Tianjin, China. The fever clinic is an emergency clinic and is open 24 hours a day to treat patients with fever symptoms. Cross-sectional data from April 2021 to December 2021 were collected for the study.

Characteristics of participants

We included participants aged 16 years and older who presented to the clinic with the main complaint of ‘fever’, had complete vital sign data measured before medical intervention and were assessed as needing further medical observation for treatment in the fever clinic after an initial consultation with a physician. All patients had complete records of vital signs, consciousness and the need for oxygen therapy, and their NEWS was evaluated.

Major outcomes

This study analysed the associations between PBR and mortality, critical illness and mortality and critical illness combined.

The items observed in the study included PBR, the NEWS, death (in-hospital mortality) and critical illness (including the need for admission to the resuscitation room or to the ICU).

The primary outcome of the study was the value of the PBR in predicting the risk of mortality. The secondary outcome was the value of the PBR in predicting the risk of critical illness, mortality and critical illness and mortality combined. The detailed flow of the study is shown in figure 1.

Measurement of vital signs

The following vital signs were recorded: PR, BR, systolic blood pressure, peripheral oxygen saturation, axillary temperature, state of consciousness (assessed via the AVPU scale, A: awake, V: verbal response, P: painful response, U: unresponsive) and the need for oxygen therapy. Vital signs were measured when the patient entered the fever clinic area without any medical intervention. The PR and oxygen saturation were measured by a finger pulse oximeter (Philips DB12), and the BR was recorded while the patient sat up in a chair or in bed by counting the number of times the patient’s chest or abdomen rose over the course of 1 min. Systolic blood pressure was measured using an electronic sphygmomanometer (Yuwell YE690F), axillary temperature was measured with a mercury thermometer and the patient’s state of consciousness and need for oxygen were measured. The PBR and NEWS were calculated from the above measurements.

Data analyses

Microsoft Excel was used to create a relevant database and manage data, such as demographic data, vital signs, laboratory tests, diagnostic information and in-hospital mortality rates.

Descriptive statistics were performed on participants’ baseline characteristics. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to test the normal distribution of the data. Continuous variables, with data conforming to a normal distribution expressed as the mean±SD and data not conforming to a normal distribution expressed as M (P25, P75), were compared between groups via Student’s t-test and analysis of variance for the former and non-parametric tests for the latter. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to perform non-parametric tests on two independent samples, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to perform non-parametric tests on multiple independent samples, the Wilcoxon test was used to perform non-parametric tests on two paired samples and the Friedman test was used to perform non-parametric tests on multiple paired samples. Categorical variables are expressed as n (%), and comparisons between groups were made via the χ2 test, Fisher’s exact test or the rank sum test.

A restricted cubic spline (RCS) was used to explore potential nonlinear associations between PBR and mortality, critical illness and mortality and between PBR and critical illness. To eliminate the effects of confounders, we adjusted for age and sex. The number of nodes for RCS is generally selected as 3–5. When conducting data analysis, we used 3, 4 and 5 nodes, respectively, for the RCS analysis. The results showed that when there were 3 nodes, the best fit was achieved. The goodness of fit detection was evaluated via the Akaike information criterion.

To investigate the ability of the PBR and NEWS to predict the risk of mortality and critical illness, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed via the rmda package. Briefly, DCA can be used to determine the net benefit of diagnostic approaches in comparison with interventions for all or no participants.37

The data were statistically analysed via SPSS V.26.0 and R (V.4.3.2). P<0.05 indicates that the difference is statistically significant, and p<0.01 indicates that the difference is highly statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined using the Events per Variable (EPV) criterion, a widely adopted method for ensuring statistical reliability in regression analyses. This study specified one covariate and two confounding variables, resulting in three parameters for estimation. With an EPV value set to 10, the minimum number of death events required was 30 (10 events×3 parameters). The total sample size was then calculated by dividing this number of events by the expected mortality rate. Based on previous literature indicating an approximate mortality rate of 4% among emergency patients, the minimum total sample size required was 750 (30 events/0.04). Consequently, a sample size of 750 or more is considered methodologically sound for this investigation.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This retrospective study included 1048 patients with fever, and there were no missing covariate data. The median age of all included patients was 75 years (IQR 65 to 84), and there were 601 (57.3%) males and 447 (42.7%) females. A total of 424 (40.5%) of all patients with fever had lower respiratory tract infections. The median PBR was 4.6 (IQR 3.9 to 5.6), and the median NEWS was 6 (IQR 4 to 9).

Among the non-survivors, 35 (59.3%) had lower respiratory tract infections than the survivors was 389 (39.3%); the median BR of the non-survivors was 22 (20 to 31) breaths/min, whereas that of the survivors was 20 (19 to 25) breaths/min and the median NEWS was 8 (5 to 10) in the non-survivors and 6 (4 to 9) in the survivors.

Critically ill patients were those who needed to be transferred to the resuscitation room or ICUs for further treatment. The median age was 76 (68 to 84) years versus 75 (65 to 83) years, the percentage of patients with lower respiratory infections was 108 (49.1%) versus 316 (38.2%), the median PR was 105 (91 to 122) beats/min versus 100 (89 to 114) beats/min, the median BR was 23 (20 to 30) versus 20 (19 to 25), the percentage of patients in an unconscious state was 83 (37.7%) versus 233 (28.1%) and the median NEWS was 7 (5 to 10) versus 6 (4 to 9) in critically ill and non-critically admitted patients, respectively (online supplemental material 1).

The baseline features do not include race or ethnicity, but the vast majority of patients in our study were Chinese.

Relationships between PBR and the risk of mortality and critical illness

The PBR was associated with the risk of mortality, critical illness and mortality combined with critical illness. RCS bars revealed a U-shaped nonlinear distribution of risk between PBR and mortality, critical illness and mortality and critical illness combined (nonlinear p values of 0.036, 0.005 and 0.002, respectively).

The risk of mortality was lowest between 4.6 and 6.2 for PBR, with the risk of mortality increasing progressively with decreasing PBR for PBR<4.6, and the risk of mortality increasing progressively with increasing PBR for PBR>6.2. The risk of critical illness was lowest when the PBR was between 4.6 and 5.5, and the risk increased gradually with decreasing PBR for PBR<4.6 and with increasing PBR for PBR>5.5. The risk of mortality and critical illness was lowest between 4.6 and 5.6 for PBR, gradually increased with decreasing PBR for PBR<4.6, and increased with increasing PBR for PBR>5.6 (figure 2A–C).

Comparison of PBR versus the NEWS

In the DCA, the net benefit achieved by the NEWS ranged from 3% to 10% threshold probability, and the net benefit achieved by the PBR ranged from 4% to 10% threshold probability, with similar net benefits for the NEWS and PBR and 1% for both the PBR and the NEWS when the threshold probability was 7% (equivalent to correctly identifying 10 mortalities per 100 patients) (figure 3).

Discussion

This retrospective study included 1048 patients with fever who were monitored for treatment in the fever clinic, of whom 59 (5.6% of all patients) died, and 220 (21% of all patients) became seriously ill. The mortality and criticality rates reported in this study were comparable to those reported in previous studies in acute care settings.38 39 Our results revealed a typical U-shaped curve between PBR and mortality or critical illness, with PBR between 4.6 and 5.5 indicating a lower risk of both mortality and critical illness. Our study demonstrated the importance of the PBR for the prediction of the risk of mortality and critical illness.

A previous study of the heart/BR (HBR) for predicting the risk of mortality or ICU admission in patients with acute heart failure in the emergency setting showed that40 the combination of HBR>4 and the respiratory efficacy index has high accuracy, sensitivity and specificity for the risk of mortality and ICU admission in patients with acute heart failure in the emergency setting. However, this study does not conduct more detailed research because a U-shaped curve was not found in this study. There is still a lack of studies related to the use of HBR or PBR to predict the risk of mortality and critical illness. Similar results were observed when PR or BR alone was used to predict mortality, with a U-shaped nonlinear correlation between PR or 24-hour PR fluctuations and BR and mortality.324145 On the basis of these results, we hypothesised that there should also be a corresponding U-shaped nonlinear correlation between PBR and mortality or critical illness. Our findings support our hypothesis and are consistent with our recent findings.46 The U-shaped distribution of PBR is also in line with the biopathological characteristics of the human body, which are always in a state of equilibrium, and an excessive increase or decrease in body indices can disrupt equilibrium and cause damage to the human body.47 Changes in PBR are affected by the cardiopulmonary, cardiovascular and autonomic nervous systems and can reflect cardiopulmonary, blood perfusion and neurological functions at the same time.20 The PBR is an external indicator of the inner balance of the core of an organism. The PBR can be used as an early predictor of the internal dynamics of the body.

Our study also revealed that the net benefit of the PBR in predicting mortality was comparable to that of the NEWS, which demonstrates the high predictive value of the PBR for mortality and its suitability for clinical use in mortality risk assessment. However, the predictability of the PBR was slightly lower than that of the NEWS for predicting critical illness, mortality and mortality and critical illness combined. The NEWS covers all vital signs, but the PBR covers only the PR and BR, so it is understandable that it is slightly less effective than the NEWS. PBR is much more convenient than NEWS in a timely and equipmentless manner. Taken together, these results suggest that the value of the PBR for predicting mortality and critical illness is in an acute care setting.

Our study data also suggest that the raw PBR can accurately predict the risk of mortality and critical illness. Even though the PR and BR may return to normal ranges after medical intervention, they still indicate an increased risk of worsened clinical outcomes. Therefore, the application of PBR for mortality and critical illness risk assessment is crucial for both the present and future development of these conditions.

Our study used PBR as a predictor instead of the HBR ratio; compared with heart rate, PR is an effective heart rate that can reach the end of the limb. The PR is more important to the body than the heart rate is because the distal part of the body is effectively perfused. The PR can exclude factors such as arrhythmia48 and atherosclerosis,49 where the heartbeat is partially ineffective in revealing the irregular status of the criticality.

In the analyses of PR and BR with respect to the risk of death and critical illness, we observed the lowest risk of death between 72–102 for PR and 19–20 for BR, which differed from previous findings of a normal value of 60–10050 for PR and 12–2051 for BR. Our study population included untreated patients with fever whose PR and BR increased in the febrile state,52 which may account for the inconsistency of our results with those of healthy people.

Our results revealed a U-shaped curve between PBR and mortality or critical illness, and PBR for the prediction of the risk of mortality and critical illness is similar to NEWS. However, this study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study based on real-world data, and there may be some errors in the measurement of PR and BR due to differences in the method of admission; for example, if patients are transferred by ambulance, bedside measurements of vital signs are taken, and the PR recorded in medical history is actually the heart rate measured by electrocardiographic monitoring. Second, our study was conducted in a fever clinic. Although the fever clinic is also an emergency department, due to the special characteristics of fever clinic patients, it is not representative of all emergency patients. Third, our sample size is not large enough, and a study with a larger sample size is needed in the next step. Fourth, our data are from 2021, of which the majority of records were handwritten, and some recording errors may have occurred. Fifth, our study did not consider the patient’s race, ethnicity, lifestyle or other characteristics. Therefore, there is currently no way to further discuss demographic characteristics. Sixth, for some simple variables, our model can make adjustments to accommodate more potential confounding variables. However, as we have not recorded any lifestyle differences among patients, we are unable to use them as confounding variables. Moreover, owing to the complexity of comorbidities, we do not yet have a better way to evaluate them as confounding variables. In future research, we will seek more suitable methods to evaluate comorbidities as confounding factors.

Conclusions

Our study revealed a U-shaped correlation between raw PBR and mortality and critical illness. The PBR is an easily accessible and reliable predictor of the risk of mortality and critical illness in patients who are acutely ill, and the predictive value of the raw PBR is comparable to that of the NEWS.
