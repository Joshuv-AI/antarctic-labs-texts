Finitary coding and Gaussian concentration for random fields
J.-R. Chazottes, S. Gallo, D. Takahashi

Abstract
We study Gaussian concentration inequalities for random fields obtained as finitary codings of i.i.d. processes, thereby establishing a new and natural bridge between finitary codings and concentration inequalities. A finitary coding formalizes the fact that a random field with nontrivial dependence, like the Ising model, can be generated from a simple i.i.d. source by a shift-equivariant map that determines each output spin using only a finite, configuration-dependent window of the input. Gaussian concentration refers to uniform sub-Gaussian fluctuation bounds for all local observables. Our main abstract result shows that Gaussian concentration is preserved under finitary codings of i.i.d. fields provided the associated coding volume has a finite second moment. The proof relies on a refinement of the classical worst-case bounded-differences inequality, due to Talagrand and Marton, which allows us to exploit configuration-dependent bounded-differences. Under a further structural condition that we call short-range factorization property , and which is satisfied in particular by codings constructed via coupling-from-the-past algorithms, we show that finite first moment of the coding volume is enough. We show that these moment conditions are sharp. Applications are given to Gibbs measures and Markov random fields on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} , probabilistic cellular automata, and one-dimensional stochastic processes. For classical lattice models such as the Ising, Potts, and random-cluster models, we obtain sharp necessary and sufficient criteria for Gaussian concentration, coinciding with uniqueness regimes. For countable-state Markov chains, we derive several equivalent characterizations of Gaussian concentration, including geometric ergodicity, exponential return-time tails, and the existence of finitary i.i.d. codings with exponential tails. Finally, we conclude the paper with a discussion of several natural open questions. Keywords: finitary factor, Bernoulli property, coupling-from-the-past algoritm, probabilistic cellular automata, Gibbs random field, Ising model, Potts model, random-cluster model, Markov chains.



1
Introduction

Gaussian concentration inequalities provide strong, uniform control on the fluctuations of local observables of a random field. They assert that every local function with bounded single-site oscillations exhibits sub-Gaussian deviations, with constants independent of the size of its dependence set. Such bounds play a central role in probability theory, with applications ranging from statistics and information theory to statistical mechanics and ergodic theory. A natural question is how Gaussian concentration behaves under the introduction of dependencies. In particular, one may ask under which conditions Gaussian concentration for an i.i.d. random field is preserved when the field is transformed by a local, translation-equivariant map. The purpose of this paper is to address this question in the framework of
finitary codings
.

The modern theory of finitary codings has its roots in Ornstein’s work on the isomorphism problem for stochastic processes. A central achievement of Ornstein’s theory was the classification of Bernoulli shifts up to measure-theoretic isomorphism by entropy, showing in particular that many highly dependent stochastic processes are isomorphic to i.i.d. processes. This led to the notion of a factor of an i.i.d. process, which captures when a process can be obtained from an i.i.d. source via a shift-equivariant measurable map. While extremely powerful, this notion is intrinsically nonconstructive: the factor map may depend on the entire configuration and need not exhibit any form of locality. This theory was subsequently and considerably generalized to countable amenable groups by Ornstein and Weiss; we refer to
[
62
]
for an overview.

A substantial strengthening of this theory came with the introduction of finitary factors, or finitary codings, in which the value at each site is determined by inspecting only finitely many input variables, in a configuration-dependent way. Such codings play a central role in the study of random fields for exactly this reason. Informally, a finitary coding shows that a potentially complex random field can be generated from a simple i.i.d. source by a translation-equivariant map that determines each output spin using only a finite, configuration-dependent window of the input. Unlike block codes, the size of this window is allowed to be random and unbounded, but it must be finite almost surely. This framework is particularly well suited to lattice systems arising in statistical mechanics, where dependencies are spatially structured and often strongly correlated.

The distinction between being a factor of an i.i.d. process and being a finitary factor turns out to be crucial in this context. Ornstein and Weiss (unpublished, see
[
1
]
) showed that the plus state of the Ising model on
\mathds{Z}^{d}
at any positive temperature is a factor of an i.i.d. process, or equivalently Bernoulli in the ergodic-theoretic sense. This result shows that the non-finitary factor-of-i.i.d. notion is too coarse to detect phase transitions, as it does not distinguish between uniqueness and non-uniqueness regimes.

By contrast, van den Berg and Steif
[
60
]
obtained a striking refinement. They showed that the plus state of the Ising model is a finitary factor of an i.i.d. field if and only if the Gibbs measure is unique at that temperature. Thus, for the Ising model, the existence of a finitary coding of an i.i.d. field aligns perfectly with the presence or absence of a phase transition. Subsequently, these results were refined and substantially extended by Spinka et al.
[
57
,
58
,
35
]
, who developed a systematic framework to construct finitary codings with quantitative control on their tails. Taken together, these works point to a strong connection between finitary codings and phase transitions in lattice systems.

The first part of the present paper is devoted to understanding Gaussian concentration as an intrinsic property of random fields. For shift-invariant finite-valued random fields, we recall that Gaussian concentration implies the blowing-up property and positivity of the relative entropy
[
13
,
15
]
. We further show that Gaussian concentration implies Bernoullicity. This new implication is the conceptual starting point for our study of the relationship between Gaussian concentration and finitary codings, which we develop in this work for more general random fields.

The core of the paper develops general principles for obtaining Gaussian concentration through finitary codings, providing a new and natural bridge between finitary codings and concentration inequalities. To the best of our knowledge,
[
32
]
were the first to point out such a relation in the specific context of stochastic chains with unbounded memory.

Our main abstract result shows that if a random field taking values in a standard Borel space is obtained as a finitary coding of an i.i.d. random field (also taking values in a standard Borel space), and if the associated coding volume has a finite second moment, then Gaussian concentration is preserved.
The proof relies on a substantial refinement of classical concentration methods for independent random variables, originating in the work of Talagrand and developed by Marton. Uniform bounded-differences inequalities are ineffective in our setting, since the influence of a single input variable is random and configuration-dependent. We therefore exploit what we call Marton’s Gaussian concentration bound, also known as the bounded-differences inequality in quadratic mean, which extends the classical bounded-differences method to this nonuniform framework and provides a further illustration of its power.
We refer to
[
4
, Chapter 8]
for a clear and comprehensive account of these inequalities.
The key technical step is to control the expected squared influence of each input site via overlaps of random coding windows, which naturally leads to a second-moment condition on the coding volume. This viewpoint illustrates how correlations arising from finitary codings weaken the concentration. We show that, in the absence of further assumptions, no weaker condition can be expected.

Motivated by this basic obstruction, we identify an additional structural assumption, satisfied in particular by codings constructed via coupling-from-the-past algorithms, under which finiteness of the first moment of the coding volume already suffices. This moment condition is sharp: we demonstrate this both by exhibiting a concrete counterexample, namely the Ising model at critical temperature, and by providing a complementary abstract argument.

Coming back to the Ising model, in the uniqueness regime, van den Berg and Steif construct finitary codings by building a bridge from dynamics to coding
[
64
]
. They consider monotone probabilistic cellular automata that are exponentially ergodic, meaning that their law converges rapidly to a unique invariant measure regardless of the initial configuration. Using Propp-Wilson’s coupling-from-the-past framework in a monotone context, they couple two realizations of the dynamics, one started from the all-plus configuration and the other from the all-minus configuration, and run both processes backward in time, allowing their initial times to vary. For each site, the first time at which the two evolutions agree determines the value at time zero. This agreement time is almost surely finite with good tail behavior and yields a finitary coding from a finite-valued i.i.d. field to the invariant measure. Their abstract result formalizes this mechanism: the limit of a monotone, exponentially ergodic probabilistic cellular automaton is a finitary coding of an i.i.d. process.

Spinka subsequently built on and significantly refined this approach. In
[
57
,
58
]
, he developed a systematized framework for finitary codings based on probabilistic cellular automata and coupling-from-the-past constructions, substantially weakening the underlying assumptions and achieving sharper quantitative control on the coding radius.
In
[
35
]
, the framework is extended to include models such as the random-cluster model, which is of infinite range in contrast to Markov random fields.

In the second part of the paper, we apply these abstract results to a broad class of models. We begin with probabilistic cellular automata, whose invariant measures admit finitary codings constructed via coupling-from-the-past arguments. The results for Gibbs measures and Markov random fields on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
then follow as consequences of this representation, together with Spinka’s and Harel-Spinka results, as they provide finitary codings with exponential or stretched-exponential tails.

At a conceptual level, the emerging picture is simple: in uniqueness regimes, both finitary codings of i.i.d. fields and Gaussian concentration hold, whereas in coexistence regimes, neither does. Beyond providing a unifying framework for Gaussian concentration in Gibbs random fields and establishing a conceptually natural bridge between finitary codings and Gaussian concentration, our approach improves upon existing results in terms of the range of parameters for which Gaussian concentration holds (as compared, for instance, with Dobrushin’s uniqueness regime), and yields genuinely new results that seems inaccessible to previous methods.

In all the examples in dimension
d\geq 2
that we consider, the assumptions of our main theorems (finite first or second moment of the coding volume) are satisfied with substantial room to spare. Indeed, in these settings the coding radius typically exhibits exponential or stretched-exponential tails. It is therefore reasonable to expect that our abstract theorems will also apply to other lattice models for which the coding radius exhibits only polynomial tails, provided the corresponding moment conditions are satisfied.

We also treat one-dimensional stochastic processes, such as stochastic chains with unbounded memory. In particular, even in the classical setting of irreducible and aperiodic countable-state Markov chains, our results shed new light on Gaussian concentration, by providing several equivalent characterizations, including geometric ergodicity, exponential return-time tails, and being the finitary coding of an i.i.d. process.

Finally, we discuss sharpness, stability under composition of finitary codings, and a number of open problems. Among them is the question of whether Gaussian concentration itself entails the existence of a finitary i.i.d. coding under an appropriate moment condition on the coding volume. This question is known to have an affirmative answer in several one-dimensional settings, such as Markov chains and renewal processes, but remains open in higher-dimensional and more general contexts.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we introduce configuration spaces, random fields, and finitary codings, and fix notation.
In Section
3
, we define Gaussian concentration and study its structural consequences for finite-valued random fields, including the blowing-up property, Bernoullicity, and positive relative entropy.
Section
4
contains our main results. We show that finitary codings of i.i.d. fields with finite second-moment coding volume satisfy Gaussian concentration, and that under an additional abstract assumption, a finite first moment of the coding volume already suffices. This latter condition is shown to be sharp in general and is naturally satisfied by codings constructed via coupling-from-the-past algorithms.
In Section
5
, we apply our results to Gibbs measures and Markov random fields, including the Ising, Potts, and random-cluster models, as well as to probabilistic cellular automata, yielding sharp Gaussian concentration criteria in several cases.
Finally, Section
6
discusses sharpness, stability under composition of finitary codings, extensions beyond
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and a number of open problems.

2
Configuration spaces, random fields and finitary codings

As the concepts in this section lie at the intersection of ergodic theory, information theory, and stochastic processes, we will freely use the terminology and notation of all three fields.

Fix
d\geq 1
. Let
(A,\mathcal{F})
,
(B,\mathcal{G})
be standard Borel spaces (finite alphabets with the discrete topology are a special case) and consider
the configuration spaces

A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}=\{x=(x_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}:x_{i}\in A\},\qquad B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}=\{y=(y_{j})_{j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}:y_{j}\in B\},

with the product
\sigma
-algebras.
For
j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
, we denote by

T^{j}:A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}\to A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}},\qquad S^{j}:B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}\to B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}

the shift operators acting by translation of coordinates,

(T^{j}x)_{i}=x_{i+j},\qquad(S^{j}y)_{i}=y_{i+j},\qquad i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}.

We use the
\ell^{\infty}
norm
\|i\|_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq k\leq d}|i^{(k)}|
and the closed
\ell^{\infty}
-balls

B_{\infty}(j,r)=\{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}:\|i-j\|_{\infty}\leq r\}\,.

We will denote its cardinality by
|B_{\infty}(j,r)|=(2r+1)^{d}
.
We use
\Lambda\subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
for a generic subset, and write
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
to indicate that
\Lambda
is finite.

Definition 2.1

(Coding map and coding radius)
.

A measurable
\varphi:A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}\to B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
such that
\varphi\circ T^{j}=S^{j}\circ\varphi
, for all
j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
, is called a coding map.

For
x\in A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
, define the (pointwise)
coding radius at the origin

r\!_{\varphi}(x)\;:=\;\inf\Big\{r\in\mathds{N}_{0}:\ \forall x^{\prime}\in A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}},\ x^{\prime}_{B_{\infty}(0,r)}=x_{B_{\infty}(0,r)}\ \Rightarrow\ \varphi(x^{\prime})_{0}=\varphi(x)_{0}\Big\}\ \in\ \mathds{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}.

By shift-equivariance, the radius at site
j
is
r\!_{\varphi}(T^{j}x)
and
\varphi(x)_{j}
depends only on

x|_{B_{\infty}(j,r\!_{\varphi}(T^{j}x))}
. When
d=1
, the coding radius is also usually called the coding length.

Let
\mu
be a
T
-invariant probability measure on
A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
.
In ergodic-theoretic terminology, the triple

\big(A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}},(T^{j})_{j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}},\mu\big)

is called a (measure-theoretic) shift dynamical system.
If
\varphi:A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}\to B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
is a coding map, then the pushforward measure

\nu:=\varphi_{*}\mu
is
S
-invariant on
B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
.
This defines another shift dynamical system
\big(B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}},(S^{j})_{j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}},\nu\big)
,
which is called a
factor
of
\big(A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}},(T^{j})_{j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}},\mu\big)
.

An equivalent formulation is in terms of canonical random fields.
Given
\mu
as above, let
X=(X_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
be the canonical
A
-valued random field
on
(A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}},\mu)
, defined by
X_{i}(x)=x_{i}
.
We use the same notation for the natural action of the shift on random fields:
for
j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
,

(T^{j}X)_{i}:=X_{i+j}.

With this convention,
X
is shift-invariant in law,

T^{j}X\eqlaw X,\qquad j\in\mathds{Z}^{d},

and we will simply say that
X
is shift-invariant.
If
\varphi:A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}\to B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
is a coding map, then

Y:=\varphi(X)
is the canonical
B
-valued random field under
\nu=\varphi_{*}\mu
,
and
Y
is shift-invariant under
S
.
In this case, one says that
Y
is a
coding
of
X
.
The pointwise coding radius
r\!_{\varphi}(x)
becomes the random variable

r\!_{\varphi}(X)
on
(A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}},\mu)
.

We will be particularly interested in coding maps that are finitary.

Definition 2.2

(Finitary coding / finitary factor)
.

With the notation introduced above, a coding map
\varphi
is said to be

if
r\!_{\varphi}(x)<\infty
for
\mu
-almost every
x
, or equivalently,

r\!_{\varphi}(X)<\infty

In this case,

is called a
finitary coding
of
X
,
and equivalently the shift dynamical system

\big(B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}},(S^{j})_{j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}},\nu\big)
is called a

of

\big(A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}},(T^{j})_{j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}},\mu\big)
.

Remark 2.1
.

A
block code
is the special case in which the coding radius
r\!_{\varphi}

is bounded deterministically.
A classical example is provided by hidden Markov chains, obtained as functions of
finite-state Markov chains.
Finitary codings allow for unbounded coding radii, but require

Our primary focus is on the situation where

is obtained as a finitary coding of an i.i.d. random field. In other words, we study dynamical systems
\big(B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}},(S^{j})_{j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}},\nu\big)

d

-dimensional Bernoulli shift.

Definition 2.3

(i.i.d. random field and Bernoulli shift)
.

Let
(A,\mathcal{F})
be a standard Borel space with probability law
\varrho
.
An
i.i.d. random field
is a family
X=(X_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
of
A
-valued random variables
that are independent and identically distributed with law
\varrho
.
Equivalently, the joint law of
X
on
A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
is the product measure

\varrho^{\otimes\mathds{Z}^{d}}
.
The associated
d
-dimensional Bernoulli shift
is the shift dynamical system

\big(A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}},(T^{j})_{j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}},\varrho^{\otimes\mathds{Z}^{d}}\big)
.

In concrete applications, it is natural to seek quantitative control of the coding radius,
for instance tail bounds for
r\!_{\varphi}
, or equivalently moment bounds for the coding volume

|B_{\infty}(0,r\!_{\varphi})|
.
We say that
\varphi
has an
integrable coding volume
if

\int|B_{\infty}(0,r\!_{\varphi}(x))|\,\dd\mu(x)<\infty
,
which can be compactly written as
\mathds{E}\big[\,|B_{\infty}(0,r\!_{\varphi}(X))|\,\big]<\infty
.
In many examples, one can even obtain exponential or stretched-exponential tail estimates, which implies that all moments of
|B_{\infty}(0,r\!_{\varphi}(X))|
are finite.

Remark 2.2
.

More generally, one could work with random fields
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
defined on an
arbitrary probability space.
All notions (coding radius, finitary coding, integrable radius/volume) extend verbatim to that
setting.
However, since our applications involve only invariant measures on the configuration spaces

A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
and
B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
, we formulate everything using the canonical representations for the sake of simplicity.

We conclude this section with a brief caution regarding terminology and the distinction between ergodic-theoretic and dynamical notions of ergodicity.
When we speak of ergodicity of a shift-invariant probability measure (or random field), we always mean ergodicity in the sense of ergodic theory: a shift-invariant measure
\mu
on
B^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is ergodic if every shift-invariant measurable set has
\mu
-measure
0
or
1
, or equivalently if
\mu
is an extreme point of the convex set of shift-invariant measures. This notion should not be confused with the use of
ergodicity
for Markov chains or probabilistic cellular automata, where it typically refers to irreducibility and convergence to a unique invariant measure of the dynamics, possibly with quantitative rates.

3
Gaussian concentration and resulting structural properties

3.1
Gaussian concentration bounds

For
j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
and a measurable function
f:B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}\to\mathds{R}
, define the (per-site) oscillation

\delta_{j}f\;:=\;\sup\bigl\{|f(y)-f(y^{\prime})|:\ y_{\ell}=y^{\prime}_{\ell},\ \forall\,\ell\neq j\bigr\}\in[0,\infty].

The
dependence set
of
f
is

\dep(f)\;:=\;\{\,i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}:\ \delta_{i}f>0\,\}.

We say that
f
is
local
if
\dep(f)
is finite (written
\dep(f)\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
).
Note that, by definition,
\dep(f)=\{i:\delta_{i}f>0\}
is the smallest subset
\Lambda\subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
such that
f
depends
only on the coordinates in
\Lambda
.

For an integer
p\geq 1
, let
\|\delta f\|_{p}:=\Big(\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}(\delta_{i}f)^{p}\Big)^{1/p}
.

A local function
f
has the bounded-difference property, or is said to be separately bounded, if

\delta_{j}f<+\infty,\;\forall j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}.

Of course, for
j\notin\dep(f)
we have
\delta_{j}f=0
. Bounded local functions obviously have the bounded-difference property.
Quasilocal functions are defined as uniform limits of local functions.

Remark 3.1
.

If
B
is finite, then
B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
is compact in the product topology, and quasilocal functions are exactly the continuous functions on
B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
(which are bounded).

We define the Gaussian concentration property for a random field.

Definition 3.1

(Gaussian concentration)
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
be a
B
-valued random field where
B
is a standard Borel space. Then
Y
satisfies a Gaussian concentration bound if there exists a constant
C>0
such
that, for any local function
f:B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}\to\mathds{R}
with the bounded-difference property, and for any
\lambda>0
, one has

\log\mathds{E}\big(\operatorname{e}^{\lambda(f(Y)-\mathds{E}[f(Y)])}\big)\leq\frac{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{452438}{393216}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle C$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{452438}{393216}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle C$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{511808}{393216}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle C$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{612288}{393216}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle C$}}}}}}{2}\lambda^{2}\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}\,.

(1)

If we are given the law
\nu
of the random field
Y
that satisfies (
1
), we will simply say that
\nu
satisfies Gaussian concentration.

Thus, a Gaussian concentration bound provides a specific type of upper bound on the cumulant moment generating function of the random variable
f(Y)-\mathds{E}[f(Y)]
.
Note that since
\lambda f(Y)=(-\lambda)(-f(Y))
, it follows immediately that (
1
) also holds for any
\lambda<0
.

A key feature of (
1
) is that the constant
C
depends only on the underlying random field, not on the observable
f
; in particular, it is independent of
|\dep(f)|
, the size of the dependence set
(the sole
f
-dependence enters through
\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i\in\dep(f)}(\delta_{i}f)^{2}
).

By a standard argument (see
e.g.

[
9
, Proposition 3.1]
), (
1
) implies the tail bounds

\mathds{P}(|f(Y)-\mathds{E}[f(Y)]|>u)\leq 2\exp\bigg(-\frac{u^{2}}{2C\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}}\bigg),\quad\forall u>0.

(2)

Remark 3.2
.

Conversely, if we assume that a random field
Y
satisfies (
2
) (for all local functions
f
with the bounded-difference property and for
C>0
independent of
f
), the reader can verify that (
1
) also holds, with a modified constant replacing
C
. We omit the details here.
Therefore, the Gaussian concentration bounds can equivalently be characterized by (
1
) or (
2
).

Observe that shift invariance is not required in the definition of Gaussian concentration. However, in the sequel we will be interested only in shift-invariant measures. If a shift-invariant measure satisfies Gaussian concentration, one can show that it must be ergodic and, in fact, mixing in the ergodic-theoretic sense. We will see later that Gaussian concentration in fact forces an even stronger property, namely Bernoullicity.

Remark 3.3
.

An alternative terminology for (
1

Y
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy
C\,\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}
, see, e.g.,
[
4
,
65
,
66
]
.

Remark 3.4

(McDiarmid’s inequality / i.i.d random variables))
.

When

is an i.i.d. random field, one can take

in (

It is usually stated for functions of

viewing a local

\dep(f)

3.2
Finite-valued random fields

We henceforth restrict to
finite-valued
random fields, i.e.,
B
-valued fields with
B
finite.
This class is already quite rich. It includes, in particular, many classical Gibbs random fields such as the Ising model.
We describe the interconnections among the “positive relative entropy property”, the “blowing-up property”, Gaussian concentration, and coding (finitary or otherwise).
In particular, we will show that any shift-invariant random field satisfying Gaussian concentration is isomorphic to a Bernoulli shift.

, the
\varepsilon
-blowup of
E
is
[E]_{\varepsilon}\;=\;\big\{x\in B^{\Lambda}:\ \bar{\mathrm{d}}_{\Lambda}(x,E)\;<\ \varepsilon\,|\Lambda|\big\}
.

We now introduce the blowing-up property for ergodic measures.

Definition 3.2

(Blowing-up property)
.

An ergodic probability measure
\nu
on
B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
has the blowing-up property if, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
\delta>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}

such that for all
n\geq N
and all
E\subseteq B^{\Lambda_{n}}
,

\nu(E)\,\geq\,\operatorname{e}^{-(2n+1)^{d}\delta}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\nu([E]_{\varepsilon})\,\geq\,1-\varepsilon.

(By
\nu(E)
we mean
\nu(\{x\in B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}:x_{\Lambda}\in E\})
, and
x_{\Lambda}:=(x_{i})_{i\in\Lambda}
is the restriction of
x
to
\Lambda
.)

By convention, if
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
is a
B
-valued random field with joint law
\nu
that has the blowing-up property, we simply say that
Y
has the blowing-up property.

The next result was proved by Marton and Shields for
d=1

[
42
]
and extends to all
d\geq 1
with no essential changes.

Theorem 3.1

(Blowing-up is preserved by finitary coding)
.

Let
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
be a finitary coding of
X=(X_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
, and assume that
X
is ergodic.
If
X
has the blowing-up property, then
Y
has the blowing-up property as well.
In particular, if
X
is an i.i.d. random field, then
Y
has the blowing-up property.

Remark 3.5
.

If
Y
is a (not necessarily finitary) coding of
X
, then
X
having the blowing-up property does not, in general, imply that
Y
inherits it.
We shall see an example illustrating this below (Ising model for
d\geq 2
below the critical temperature).

For shift-invariant random fields, Gaussian concentration implies the blowing-up property, and indeed yields a stronger and more explicit form of it.

Proposition 3.1

(Gaussian concentration implies quantitative blowing-up,
[
13
]
)
.

Let
Y
be an ergodic random field with law
\nu
, and assume
Y
satisfies the Gaussian concentration bound (
1
) with constant
C
. Then
Y
has the blowing-up property. More precisely, for any

\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
and any
E\subseteq B^{\Lambda}
with
\nu(E)>0
,

\nu([E]_{\varepsilon})\ \geq\ 1-\exp\!\left[-\,\frac{|\Lambda|}{2C}\left(\,\varepsilon-\sqrt{\frac{2C}{|\Lambda|}\,\log\frac{1}{\nu(E)}}\,\right)^{\!2}\,\right],

(4)

whenever
\displaystyle\varepsilon>\sqrt{\frac{2C}{|\Lambda|}\,\log\frac{1}{\nu(E)}}
.

Remark 3.6
.

The blowing-up property for a shift-invariant measure implies ergodicity and, in fact, mixing in the ergodic-theoretic sense, we therefore restrict attention to ergodic measures.

Remark 3.7
.

We will give an example of a system that has the blowing-up property but does not satisfy Gaussian concentration.

3.2.2
Gaussian concentration implies Bernoullicity/finite determination

Gaussian concentration is strong enough to imply
Bernoullicity
.

Definition 3.3

(Bernoullicity)
.

Let
(B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}},(S^{j})_{j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}},\nu)
be a measure-preserving shift dynamical system.
We say it is
Bernoulli
if it is measure-theoretically isomorphic to a
d
-dimensional
Bernoulli shift.

A measure-theoretic isomorphism is a coding map that is invertible modulo null sets: after removing sets of measure zero in the source and target, it restricts to a bijection with a measurable inverse.
Since
B
is finite in this section, the target
d
-dimensional Bernoulli shift can also be taken to be
B
-valued.

Theorem 3.2

(Gaussian concentration implies Bernoullicity)
.

Let
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
be a
B
-valued random field whose law
\nu
is ergodic for the shifts
S^{j}:B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}\to B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
,
j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
, and assume that
\nu
satisfies Gaussian concentration. Then
\big(B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}},(S^{j})_{j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}},\nu\big)
is Bernoulli.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.1
,
Y
has the blowing-up property and therefore also the almost blowing-up property, which we do not define here.
The latter is equivalent to
Y
being a coding of an i.i.d. random field; see
[
56
, Chs. III-IV]
for
d=1
, and note that the same argument applies for all
d\geq 1
. Finally, by Ornstein’s isomorphism theory for amenable group actions (valid for all
d\geq 1
for the action of
\mathds{Z}^{d}
), any factor of a
d
-dimensional Bernoulli shift is Bernoulli; see
[
46
]
. This completes the proof.
∎

It is worth noting that Bernoullicity admits several equivalent characterizations, one of which we find particularly appealing: finite determination. Unlike Bernoullicity, which is formulated in abstract ergodic-theoretic terms, finite determination has a more concrete and constructive flavor, as it concerns the approximation of the process by its finite-dimensional marginals. This perspective aligns naturally with Gaussian concentration, which is concerned with controlling the fluctuations of local observables.
To state it, we recall three notions.
Let
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
be distinct shift-invariant probability measures on
B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
.

First, for each
k\in\mathds{N}
, the total variation distance between their marginals
\nu_{k}
and
\nu^{\prime}_{k}
on
B^{\llbracket-k,k\rrbracket^{d}}
is

\big\|\nu_{k}-\nu^{\prime}_{k}\big\|_{\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle TV}}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b_{k}\in B^{\llbracket-k,k\rrbracket^{d}}}\big|\nu_{k}(b_{k})-\nu^{\prime}_{k}(b_{k})\big|\,,

where
\llbracket-k,k\rrbracket^{d}=[-k,k]^{d}\cap\mathds{Z}^{d}
.

Second, the entropy of, say,
\nu
is

\ent(\nu)=-\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{d}}\sum_{b_{k}\in B^{\llbracket-k,k\rrbracket^{d}}}\nu_{k}(b_{k})\,\log\nu_{k}(b_{k})\,.

Third, and finally, the
\bar{d}
-distance between
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
is

\bar{\mathrm{d}}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{d}}\,\inf_{P_{k}\in\mathcal{J}_{k}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})}\iint\bar{\mathrm{d}}_{\llbracket-k,k\rrbracket^{d}}(x,x^{\prime})\dd P_{k}(x,x^{\prime})\,,

where
\bar{\mathrm{d}}_{\llbracket-k,k\rrbracket^{d}}(x,x^{\prime})
is defined in (
3
), and where
\mathcal{J}_{k}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})
denotes the set of couplings of
\nu_{k}
and
\nu^{\prime}_{k}

on
B^{\llbracket-k,k\rrbracket^{d}}\times B^{\llbracket-k,k\rrbracket^{d}}
, that is,
\iint\mathds{1}\!_{E}(x)\dd P_{k}(x,x^{\prime})=\nu(E)
and
\iint\mathds{1}\!_{E}(x^{\prime})\dd P_{k}(x,x^{\prime})=\nu^{\prime}(E)
, for all

E\subseteq B^{\llbracket-k,k\rrbracket^{d}}
.

Notice that the two above limits always exist for shift-invariant probability measures.

Definition 3.4

(Finitely determined random field)
.

Let
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
be a
B
-valued random field with ergodic law
\nu
. We say that
Y
is
finitely determined
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exist
k\in\mathds{N}
and
\delta>0
such that, for any ergodic
\nu^{\prime}
on
B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
,

\big\|\nu_{k}-\nu^{\prime}_{k}\big\|_{\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle TV}}}\leq\delta\quad\text{and}\quad\big|\ent(\nu)-\ent(\nu^{\prime})\big|<\delta\ \Longrightarrow\ \bar{\mathrm{d}}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})<\varepsilon.

Since Bernoullicity is equivalent to finite determination
[
46
]
, we obtain the following reformulation of Theorem
3.2
.

Theorem 3.3

(Gaussian concentration implies finite determination)
.

Let
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
be a
B
-valued random field with ergodic law
\nu
, and assume that
\nu
satisfies Gaussian concentration. Then
Y
is finitely determined.

It is shown in
[
42
]
that the blowing-up property is
equivalent
to being finitely determined together with an exponential-rate convergence property for both frequencies and entropy.
The proofs are given for
d=1
, and extend to all
d\geq 1
.
Likewise, it is easy to show that Gaussian concentration implies an exponential-rate convergence property for frequencies.
We do not reproduce the formal definitions of these rate properties. Roughly speaking, they quantify the speed of convergence in probability in the ergodic theorem and in the Shannon-McMillan-Breiman theorem, and this description should allow the reader to easily infer the corresponding formal definitions.

3.2.3
The positive relative entropy property

Another consequence of Gaussian concentration is the positive relative entropy property.

Given two shift-invariant probability measures
\nu,\nu^{\prime}
on
B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
, the lower relative entropy of
\nu^{\prime}
with respect to
\nu
is

\ent_{*}(\nu^{\prime}|\nu)=\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{d}}\sum_{b_{k}\in B^{\llbracket-k,k\rrbracket^{d}}}\nu^{\prime}_{k}(b_{k})\log\frac{\nu^{\prime}_{k}(b_{k})}{\nu_{k}(b_{k})}\,.

One can likewise define the upper relative entropy
\ent^{*}(\nu^{\prime}|\nu)
by taking a limit superior. In general the lower and upper relative entropies need not coincide (pathologies can occur; see, for example,
[
55
]
for
d=1
). However, in the context of Gibbs random fields, this is a well-behaved object.

Definition 3.5

(Positive relative entropy property)
.

Let
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
be a
B
-valued random field with ergodic law
\nu
. We say that
Y
has the
positive relative entropy property
if

\ent_{*}(\nu^{\prime}|\nu)>0\quad\text{for every ergodic }\nu^{\prime}\neq\nu.

We have the following theorems.

Theorem 3.4

(
[
42
]
)
.

Let
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
be a
B
-valued random field with ergodic law
\nu
, and assume that it has the blowing-up property. Then
Y
has the positive relative entropy property.

Assuming Gaussian concentration (which, by Proposition
3.1
, entails the blowing-up property), we obtain the positive relative entropy property.

Theorem 3.5

(
[
15
]
)
.

Let
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
be a
B
-valued random field with ergodic law
\nu
, and assume that
\nu
satisfies Gaussian concentration.
Then
Y
has the positive relative entropy property.

(A different, much less direct proof is given in
[
13
]
.)

We can use the previous result to show that being a coding of an i.i.d. process does not force Gaussian concentration.

Proposition 3.2
.

There exists a finite-alphabet process with law
\mu
that is a coding of an i.i.d. process on a finite alphabet and that does not satisfy Gaussian concentration.

Proof.

In
[
55
]
there is a construction of a coding of an i.i.d. process yielding a law
\nu
such that

\ent_{*}(\nu^{\prime}|\nu)=0,

where
\nu^{\prime}
denotes the law of the binary symmetric i.i.d. coin-tossing process, yet
\nu\neq\nu^{\prime}
. If
\nu
satisfied Gaussian concentration, Theorem
3.5
would imply the positive relative entropy property,
hence
\ent_{*}(\nu^{\prime}|\nu)>0
for every
\nu\neq\nu^{\prime}
, a contradiction. Therefore
\nu
does not satisfy Gaussian concentration.
∎

We shall see other examples later on, in the context of phase transitions in Gibbs random fields.

For finite-valued random fields, Gaussian concentration sits at the top of a hierarchy of regularity properties.
It implies the blowing-up property, positive relative entropy, and is strong enough to force Bernoullicity.
At the same time, none of these implications can be reversed in general.
Thus Gaussian concentration is not merely an ergodic-theoretic property of factors of i.i.d., but a genuinely stronger quantitative constraint.
This naturally raises the question of how Gaussian concentration behaves under
finitary
coding, which is the focus of the next section and the main theme of this work.

4
Gaussian concentration for finitary codings of i.i.d. random fields

We establish two main abstract theorems.
The first, Theorem
4.1
, applies to general finitary codings and unavoidably requires finiteness of the second moment of the coding volume. This reflects the correlations inherently introduced by such codings. The necessity of this second-moment condition already emerges from the proof itself, and in Section
6.1
we further explain why the result is sharp at this level of generality, in the absence of any additional structural assumptions.

Our second main result, Theorem
4.3
, shows that under an additional abstract assumption, finiteness of the first moment of the coding volume is sufficient to obtain Gaussian concentration. This assumption is satisfied by random fields that can be simulated via a coupling-from-the-past algorithm, which so far constitutes the main general method for constructing finitary codings.

This first-moment condition is also sharp: there exist examples in which Gaussian concentration fails whenever the expected coding volume is infinite for every finitary coding. In particular, the finiteness of the first moment cannot be relaxed. In Section
6.1
we also provide a more conceptual explanation of this obstruction.

4.1
Finite second-moment coding volume implies Gaussian concentration

We begin with a fully abstract result showing that Gaussian concentration is preserved under arbitrary finitary codings of i.i.d. random fields, provided the coding volume has a finite second moment.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
d\geq 1
.
Let
X=(X_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
be an i.i.d.
A
-valued random field, where
A
is a standard Borel space,
and let
Y=\varphi(X)
for some finitary coding
\varphi:A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}\to B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
.
Assume the coding volume has a finite second moment, i.e.

\mathds{E}\left[\,|B_{\infty}(0,r\!_{\varphi}(X))|^{2}\,\right]<\infty\,.

(5)

Then, for every local and continuous
f:B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}\to\mathds{R}
with the bounded-difference property,

\log\mathds{E}\big[\exp\{\lambda(f(Y)-\mathds{E}f(Y))\}\big]\;\leq\;2^{d}\lambda^{2}\,\mathds{E}\big[(2r\!_{\varphi}(X)+1)^{2d}\,\big]\,\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}\,,\qquad\forall\,\lambda>0.

Remark 4.1
.

When
B
comes with the discrete topology, for instance when
B
is finite, local functions are automatically continuous
(and the bounded-difference property is automatic as well).

The factor
\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}
depends on the
output

coordinates, while
\mathds{E}\big[\,|B_{\infty}(0,r\!_{\varphi}(X))|^{2}\,\big]
captures the “algorithmic locality
cost” of expressing
f(\varphi(X))
in terms of independent inputs.

To prove Theorem
4.1
, we appeal to the following inequality, originating with Talagrand.
Marton subsequently rederived and sharpened it via a conditional transportation inequality; see

[
4
, Th. 8.5, p. 245]
.
We use the version stated in
[
4
, Th. 8.6, p. 245]
, where it is referred to as the bounded-differences inequality in quadratic mean, and we recast it below in a form tailored to our setting.
Note that we work with a two-sided control on the functions, while retaining the key feature:

nonuniform
, configuration-dependent bounded differences.

Theorem 4.2

(Marton’s Gaussian concentration bound)
.

Let
X=(X_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
be an i.i.d. random field where the
X_{i}
take values in a standard Borel space
A
.
Let
g:A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
be a local function.
Assume there are measurable functions
c_{i}:A^{\dep(g)}\to[0,+\infty)
,
i\in\dep(g)
, such that for all
x,x^{\prime}\in A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}

\big|\,g(x)-g(x^{\prime})\big|\leq\sum_{i\,\in\dep(g)}c_{i}(x)\,\mathds{1}_{\{x_{i}\neq x^{\prime}_{i}\}}.

(6)

Then

\log\mathds{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda(g(X)-\mathds{E}[g(X)])}\right]\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\sum_{i\in\dep(g)}\mathds{E}\big(c_{i}^{2}(X)\big),\quad\forall\lambda>0.

(7)

The usefulness of (
7
) hinges on the finiteness of the expectation on the right-hand side.
Compared to McDiarmid’s inequality, Theorem
4.2
is a decisive refinement: instead of requiring deterministic worst-case Lipschitz constants, it allows the single-site sensitivity to depend on the configuration.
This flexibility is precisely what is needed in our setting, where the coding radius is random and configuration dependent.

Our goal is to apply Theorem
4.2
to observables of the form

g=f\circ\varphi,

where
\varphi
is a finitary coding and
f
is local.
However, the map
\varphi
has a random coding radius, and therefore the influence of a single input site on
g
is itself random and a priori unbounded.
To bring the situation within the scope of (
6
), we first truncate the coding map so as to obtain deterministic locality.

Definition 4.1

(Truncation of the coding map)
.

Let
\varphi:A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}\to B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
be a coding map, and
T^{j}
the shift on
A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
.
Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and a reference symbol
b_{0}\in B
.
Define the truncated radius at site
j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
by

r^{(n)}_{\varphi}(T^{j}x):=\min\{r_{\varphi}(T^{j}x),\,n\}.

Define
\varphi^{(n)}:A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}\to B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
coordinatewise by

\varphi^{(n)}(x)_{j}\;:=\;\begin{cases}\varphi(x)_{j},&\text{if \;}r_{\varphi}(T^{j}x)\leq n,\\[2.84526pt]
b_{0},&\text{if \;}r_{\varphi}(T^{j}x)>n.\end{cases}

Lemma 4.1
.

\varphi^{(n)}
is measurable, shift-commuting, and is a block code of deterministic
\ell^{\infty}
radius
\leq n
, i.e. each coordinate
\varphi^{(n)}(x)_{j}
depends only on
x
restricted to
B_{\infty}(j,n)
.
Moreover, if
r\!_{\varphi}(T^{j}x)\leq n
then
\varphi^{(n)}(x)_{j}=\varphi(x)_{j}
.

Proof.

If
r\!_{\varphi}(T^{j}x)\leq n
, then
\varphi(x)_{j}
is determined by
x
on
B_{\infty}(j,n)
. If
r\!_{\varphi}(T^{j}x)>n
, then
\varphi^{(n)}(x)_{j}=b_{0}
, which is constant.
Hence each coordinate is a function of
x|_{B_{\infty}(j,n)}
. Shift-commutation follows from the definition; the last claim is by construction.
∎

Before giving the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we prove a lemma. The key point is that we first telescope in the
output coordinates
, and only then estimate the resulting indicators in terms of input disagreements. More precisely, writing
y=\varphi^{(n)}(x)
and
y^{\prime}=\varphi^{(n)}(x^{\prime})
, locality of
f
gives

|f(y)-f(y^{\prime})|\leq\sum_{j\in\dep(f)}\delta_{j}f\,\mathds{1}_{\{y_{j}\neq y^{\prime}_{j}\}}.

We then control each indicator
\mathds{1}_{\{y_{j}\neq y^{\prime}_{j}\}}
using the deterministic block-code property of
\varphi^{(n)}
, which localizes the dependence of the
j
-th output coordinate to a fixed
\ell^{\infty}
-ball determined by the truncated coding radius at
x
. This yields a bound in which all influence coefficients depend only on the base configuration
x
, while the dependence on
x^{\prime}
appears solely through the indicators
\mathds{1}_{\{x_{i}\neq x^{\prime}_{i}\}}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
f:B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
be local. Let
n\in\mathds{N}
, and let
\varphi^{(n)}
be the truncated coding map (Definition
4.1
). Then, for any
x,x^{\prime}\in A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
,

\big|f\circ\varphi^{(n)}(x)-f\circ\varphi^{(n)}(x^{\prime})\big|\leq\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}\Bigg(\sum_{j\in\dep(f)}\delta_{j}f\;\mathds{1}\!\left\{\|j-i\|_{\infty}\leq r^{(n)}_{\varphi}(T^{j}x)\right\}\Bigg)\mathds{1}_{\{x_{i}\neq x^{\prime}_{i}\}}.

Proof.

Set
g^{(n)}:=f\circ\varphi^{(n)}
.
Write
y:=\varphi^{(n)}(x)
and
y^{\prime}:=\varphi^{(n)}(x^{\prime})
.
By telescoping over the output coordinates in
\dep(f)
,

\big|g^{(n)}(x)-g^{(n)}(x^{\prime})\big|=|f(y)-f(y^{\prime})|\leq\sum_{j\in\dep(f)}\delta_{j}f\;\mathds{1}_{\{y_{j}\neq y^{\prime}_{j}\}}.

(8)

Fix
j\in\dep(f)
.
Since
\varphi^{(n)}
is a block code of deterministic
\ell^{\infty}
-radius
\leq n
(Lemma
4.1
),
each coordinate
\varphi^{(n)}(x)_{j}
depends only on the restriction of
x
to
B_{\infty}(j,n)
.
Hence, if
x
and
x^{\prime}
agree on

B_{\infty}\big(j,r_{\varphi}^{(n)}(T^{j}x)\big)\subset B_{\infty}(j,n),

then necessarily

\varphi^{(n)}(x)_{j}=\varphi^{(n)}(x^{\prime})_{j}.

Equivalently,

\mathds{1}_{\{\varphi^{(n)}(x)_{j}\neq\varphi^{(n)}(x^{\prime})_{j}\}}\leq\sum_{i\in B_{\infty}(j,r_{\varphi}^{(n)}(T^{j}x))}\mathds{1}_{\{x_{i}\neq x^{\prime}_{i}\}}.

Injecting this bound into (
8
) yields

\displaystyle\big|g^{(n)}(x)-g^{(n)}(x^{\prime})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in\dep(f)}\delta_{j}f\sum_{i\in B_{\infty}(j,r^{(n)}_{\varphi}(T^{j}x))}\mathds{1}_{\{x_{i}\neq x^{\prime}_{i}\}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}\mathds{1}_{\{x_{i}\neq x^{\prime}_{i}\}}\Bigg(\sum_{j\in\dep(f)}\delta_{j}f\;\mathds{1}\!\left\{\|j-i\|_{\infty}\leq r^{(n)}_{\varphi}(T^{j}x)\right\}\Bigg),

which is the desired bound.
∎

We introduce shorthand notation.

Notation
.

Since the coding map
\varphi
is fixed throughout, we simplify the notation by writing
r^{(n)}_{j}(x)
for
r^{(n)}_{\varphi}(T^{j}x)
, where
n\in\mathds{N}
and
j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
.

In view of Theorem
4.2
, the structural bound of Lemma
4.2
reduces Gaussian concentration for
g^{(n)}
to the control of the quadratic influence term

\sum_{i\in\dep(g^{(n)})}\big(c_{i}^{(n)}(X)\big)^{2}.

Indeed, by (
7
), concentration is governed precisely by the expectation of this quantity. The next proposition shows that it can be estimated in terms of the squared oscillations of
f
and a purely coding-dependent convolution term involving the truncated radii.

Proposition 4.1
.

Under the same condition as in Theorem
4.1
, we have

\mathds{E}\sum_{i\in\dep(g^{(n)})}\big(c_{i}^{(n)}(X)\big)^{2}\leq\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}\,\|b\|_{1},

where for all
j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
,

b_{j}=\mathds{E}\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}\mathds{1}\!\left\{\|i\|_{\infty}\leq r_{0}^{(n)}(X)\right\}\mathds{1}\!\left\{\|j-i\|_{\infty}\leq r_{j}^{(n)}(X)\right\}\,.

(9)

Proof.

Let
f
be local with the bounded-difference property and fix
n\in\mathds{N}
.
By Lemma
4.2
,

c_{i}^{(n)}(x)=\sum_{j\in\dep(f)}\delta_{j}f\;\mathds{1}\!\left\{\|j-i\|_{\infty}\leq r^{(n)}_{\varphi}(T^{j}x)\right\}.

Then

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\sum_{i\in\dep(g^{(n)})}\big(c_{i}^{(n)}(X)\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{k,\ell\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}\delta_{k}f\,\delta_{\ell}f\;\mathds{E}\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}\mathds{1}\!\left\{\|k-i\|_{\infty}\leq r_{k}^{(n)}(X)\right\}\mathds{1}\!\left\{\|\ell-i\|_{\infty}\leq r_{\ell}^{(n)}(X)\right\}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{k,\ell\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}\delta_{k}f\,\delta_{\ell}f\;b_{\ell,k}\,,

where

b_{\ell,k}:=\mathds{E}\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}\mathds{1}\!\left\{\|k-i\|_{\infty}\leq r_{k}^{(n)}(X)\right\}\mathds{1}\!\left\{\|\ell-i\|_{\infty}\leq r_{\ell}^{(n)}(X)\right\}\,.

We claim that, since
T^{k}X\eqlaw X
,
b_{\ell,k}=b_{\ell-k,0}=b_{\ell-k}
, where
b_{\ell-k}
is defined in (
9
).
Indeed, perform the change of variables
j=i-k
. Then

\|k-i\|_{\infty}=\|j\|_{\infty},\quad\|\ell-i\|_{\infty}=\|(\ell-k)-j\|_{\infty},

and therefore

b_{\ell,k}=\mathbb{E}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathds{1}\!\left\{\|j\|_{\infty}\leq r_{k}^{(n)}(X)\right\}\mathds{1}\!\left\{\|(\ell-k)-j\|_{\infty}\leq r_{\ell}^{(n)}(X)\right\}.

By shift-covariance of the coding radii, we have for all
u\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
r_{u}^{(n)}(X)=r_{0}^{(n)}(T^{-u}X)
.
In particular,

r_{k}^{(n)}(X)=r_{0}^{(n)}(T^{-k}X),\qquad r_{\ell}^{(n)}(X)=r_{\ell-k}^{(n)}(T^{-k}X).

Substituting these identities yields

\displaystyle b_{\ell,k}

Definition 4.2

(Short-range factorization property)

We say that a coding satisfies the short-range factorization property with constant

5
Applications and examples

In this section we illustrate the scope of the abstract results of Section 4 by applying them to a variety of models from statistical mechanics, interacting particle systems, and stochastic processes. The purpose is primarily illustrative: we show how existing finitary-coding constructions, when combined with our general concentration-transfer results, yield Gaussian concentration inequalities in a unified and transparent way.

A recurring theme is that Gaussian concentration holds precisely in regimes where the model admits a finitary coding of an i.i.d. field with sufficient quantitative locality, and fails when such locality breaks down, for instance due to phase coexistence, diverging susceptibilities, or heavy-tailed coding radii. In several classical models, this leads to sharp necessary and sufficient criteria.

We begin with limiting distributions of probabilistic cellular automata, where coupling-from-the-past constructions naturally produce finitary codings satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
4.3
, and therefore require only a finite first moment of the coding volume. We then turn to Gibbs measures and Markov random fields on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where it is sufficient to rely on Theorem
4.1
, which requires a finite second moment of the coding volume. In most of these examples, the coding radius in fact has exponential or stretched-exponential tails.
We refer to Section
6
for an open problem highlighting the disparity between the strength of available finitary-coding constructions, which typically yield exponential or stretched-exponential tails for the coding radius, and the much weaker moment assumptions required in Theorem
4.1
.

Our approach sharpens known hypotheses, yields necessary and sufficient conditions in several cases (notably for the Ising and Potts models), and produces new results that were inaccessible to previous methods. Further examples include non-equilibrium systems such as the parking process, as well as one-dimensional processes, where Gaussian concentration can be characterized in terms of return-time or regeneration properties. In particular, for irreducible and aperiodic countable-state Markov chains, we obtain several equivalent characterizations of Gaussian concentration.

Altogether, these applications show that Gaussian concentration is robust under finitary codings with controlled coding volume, yet sensitive enough to detect qualitative changes in the underlying dependence structure.

The ferromagnetic Ising model

Take

H_{\Lambda}(y_{\Lambda}y^{\prime}_{\Lambda^{\!\mathrm{c}}})\;:=\;-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{i,j\}\in E\\

Theorem 5.2
.

For the ferromagnetic Ising model in dimension
d\geq 2
, Gaussian concentration holds if and only if
\beta<\beta_{c}(d)

\beta<\beta_{c}(d)

, Gaussian concentration holds in the high-temperature regime
\beta<\beta_{c}
, while for
\beta>\beta_{c}
it fails for both extremal Gibbs states
\nu_{\beta}^{-}
and
\nu_{\beta}^{+}

Proof.

For
\beta<\beta_{c}(d)
, this is a direct consequence of Theorem
4.1
and Theorem 1.1
[
57

by an i.i.d. random field with exponential tails (or by a finite-valued i.i.d. random field with stretched-exponential tails).

For
\beta>\beta_{c}(d)

\ent_{*}(\nu^{-}_{\beta}|\nu^{+}_{\beta})=\ent^{*}(\nu^{-}_{\beta}|\nu^{+}_{\beta})=0

[

Assume

2
]

At criticality, the ferromagnetic Ising model on

admits a unique infinite-volume Gibbs measure; let

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that

satisfies Gaussian concentration.
Then there exists a constant
C<\infty
such that for every local function

{\mathrm{Var}}(f(Y))=\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(Y)^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(Y)\right]^{2}\leq C\,\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}.

(18)

(The implication from Gaussian concentration to (

Let
\Lambda_{n}=B_{\infty}(0,n)
and consider the partial sums

At criticality, the Ising Gibbs state is centered and ferromagnetic, so that

\mathbb{E}(Y_{0})=0
and

{\mathrm{Cov}}(Y_{0},Y_{j})=\mathbb{E}(Y_{0}Y_{j})\geq 0

for all

Moreover, it is known that the susceptibility diverges, namely

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathbb{E}(Y_{0}Y_{k})=+\infty.

(19)

As a consequence,

\frac{1}{|\Lambda_{n}|}\,{\mathrm{Var}}(S_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}+\infty.

(20)

Indeed, by shift-invariance, for any fixed

,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Lambda_{n}|}{\mathrm{Var}}(S_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\frac{|\Lambda_{n}\cap(\Lambda_{n}-k)|}{|\Lambda_{n}|}\,\mathbb{E}(Y_{0}Y_{k})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\|k\|_{\infty}\leq R}\frac{|\Lambda_{n}\cap(\Lambda_{n}-k)|}{|\Lambda_{n}|}\,\mathbb{E}(Y_{0}Y_{k}).

As

, the weights converge uniformly to
1
on

, so that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Lambda_{n}|}{\mathrm{Var}}(S_{n})\geq\sum_{\|k\|_{\infty}\leq R}\mathbb{E}(Y_{0}Y_{k}).

Letting

20

On the other hand, applying (

, we obtain

{\mathrm{Var}}(S_{n})\leq C\,\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}\leq 4C\,|\Lambda_{n}|,

and therefore

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Lambda_{n}|}{\mathrm{Var}}(S_{n})\leq 4C,

which contradicts (

When

and
\beta>\beta_{c}

\nu_{\beta}^{-}
and
\nu_{\beta}^{+}

\nu_{\beta}^{-}
nor
\nu_{\beta}^{+}
satisfies Gaussian concentration, by the argument above, which applies in any dimension

This concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

This is no longer the case in dimension
d=3
, where at sufficiently low temperature one encounters the so-called Dobrushin states, which are not shift-invariant and hence do not correspond to equilibrium states. We refer to
[
33
]
for these results.

Gaussian concentration itself does not require shift invariance a priori. Nevertheless, the statement of the theorem is restricted to shift-invariant Gibbs measures, since our proof of the failure of Gaussian concentration in the presence of multiple Gibbs states relies crucially on shift invariance. It therefore remains open whether non-shift-invariant Gibbs measures may satisfy Gaussian concentration. Nevertheless, we do not expect Gaussian concentration to hold for Dobrushin states, due to the presence of macroscopic interface fluctuations.

The next proposition provides a canonical example of a random field for which Gaussian concentration fails while the blowing-up property holds. The latter follows from the mere existence of a finitary coding by an i.i.d. random field, whereas Gaussian concentration fails intrinsically for the field itself (see Theorem
5.2
).

More importantly, the proposition shows that the critical Ising model realizes the optimal obstruction to Theorem
4.3
: although finitary codings from an i.i.d. random field do exist at criticality, every such coding must have infinite expected coding volume. The universal nature of this obstruction rules out the possibility of improving Theorem
4.3
by weakening its moment assumptions.

Both the failure of Gaussian concentration and the impossibility of finite expected coding volume have the same underlying cause, namely the divergence of the susceptibility at criticality.

Proposition 5.1

(Ising model at criticality)
.

Let
d\geq 2
. For the ferromagnetic Ising model at
\beta=\beta_{c}(d)
, the unique Gibbs measure does not satisfy Gaussian concentration.
Nevertheless, it satisfies the blowing-up property, since it admits a finitary coding from an i.i.d. random field. Moreover, any finitary coding of this random field by an i.i.d. random field necessarily has infinite expected coding volume.

Proof.

The failure of Gaussian concentration at criticality is established in Theorem
5.2
. On the other hand, the Ising model at
\beta=\beta_{c}(d)
does admit finitary codings from an i.i.d. random field; in particular,
[
63
]
constructs such a coding from a finite-valued i.i.d. source. Consequently, the unique Gibbs measure satisfies the blowing-up property by Theorem
3.1
.

Finally, Theorem 4.3 of
[
60
]
shows that at
\beta=\beta_{c}(d)
, the existence of a finitary coding already forces the expected coding volume to be infinite, that is,

\mathds{E}\big[\,|B_{\infty}(0,r\!_{\varphi}(Y))|\,\big]=\infty
. Here is the proof for completeness.
Assume, towards a contradiction, that such a finitary coding
\varphi
exists. Write
Y=\varphi(X)
, and
let
r\!_{\varphi}=r\!_{\varphi}(X)
be the coding radius at the origin and let
r\!_{\varphi}(j)
be the coding radius at site
j
.
We will show that if we assume a finite expected coding volume we get

\sum_{j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}\mathds{E}[Y_{0}Y_{j}]\;<\;\infty,

contradicting (
19
).

Fix
j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
and set
r_{j}:=\lfloor\|j\|_{\infty}/2\rfloor-1
. Decompose

\mathds{E}[Y_{0}Y_{j}]\;=\;\sum_{k,\ell\geq 0}\mathds{E}\big[Y_{0}\,\mathds{1}_{\{r\!_{\varphi}=k\}}\;Y_{j}\,\mathds{1}_{\{r\!_{\varphi}(j)=\ell\}}\big]\;=\;S_{1}(j)+S_{2}(j),

where
S_{1}(j)
sums over
\max\{k,\ell\}\geq r_{j}
and
S_{2}(j)
over
\max\{k,\ell\}<r_{j}
.

For
S_{2}(j)
the two input regions used to determine

and
Y_{j}
are disjoint (since

B_{\infty}(0,k)
and
B_{\infty}(j,\ell)
are disjoint when

), hence by independence of the i.i.d. input

we have

S_{2}(j)\;=\;\sum_{k,\ell<r_{j}}\mathds{E}\big[Y_{0}\,\mathds{1}_{\{r\!_{\varphi}=k\}}\big]\;\mathds{E}\big[Y_{j}\,\mathds{1}_{\{r\!_{\varphi}(j)=\ell\}}\big]\;=\;\mathds{E}[Y_{0}]\,\mathds{E}[Y_{j}]\;=\;0.

We bound
S_{1}(j)
as follows. Then

\displaystyle S_{1}(j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\max\{k,\ell\}\geq r_{j}}\mathds{E}\big[Y_{0}\,\mathds{1}_{\{r\!_{\varphi}=k\}}\;Y_{j}\,\mathds{1}_{\{r\!_{\varphi}(j)=\ell\}}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq r_{j}}\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathds{E}\big[Y_{0}\,\mathds{1}_{\{r\!_{\varphi}=k\}}\;Y_{j}\,\mathds{1}_{\{r\!_{\varphi}(j)=\ell\}}\big]\;+\;\sum_{k\geq r_{j}}\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\mathds{E}\big[Y_{0}\,\mathds{1}_{\{r\!_{\varphi}=k\}}\;Y_{j}\,\mathds{1}_{\{r\!_{\varphi}(j)=\ell\}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\geq r_{j}}\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathds{E}\big[\mathds{1}_{\{r\!_{\varphi}(j)=\ell\}}\big]\;+\;\sum_{k\geq r_{j}}\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\mathds{E}\big[\mathds{1}_{\{r\!_{\varphi}=k\}}\big]\qquad\text{since }|Y_{0}Y_{j}|\leq 1

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq r_{j}}\mathds{P}\big(r\!_{\varphi}(j)=\ell\big)\;+\;\sum_{k\geq r_{j}}\mathds{P}\big(r\!_{\varphi}=k\big)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k\geq r_{j}}\mathds{P}\big(r\!_{\varphi}=k\big)\,,

where the last equality uses shift-invariance:
r\!_{\varphi}(j)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}r\!_{\varphi}
.
Sum this bound over
j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
. Group sites by
m:=\lfloor\|j\|_{\infty}/2\rfloor-1
and note that the number of

is
\asymp(2m+3)^{d}-(2m-1)^{d}\lesssim(m+1)^{d-1}
. Thus,

\sum_{j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}\mathds{E}[Y_{0}Y_{j}]\;\leq\;C_{d}\sum_{m\geq 0}\,(m+1)^{d-1}\;\sum_{k\geq m}\mathds{P}(r\!_{\varphi}=k)\;\leq\;C_{d}^{\prime}\,\mathds{E}\big[(r\!_{\varphi}+1)^{d}\big],

where we used the elementary inequality

This contradicts the critical Ising divergence stated above. Therefore no such finitary coding with integrable coding volume can exist.

Remark 5.2
.

In dimension d=1, the Ising model admits a unique infinite-volume Gibbs measure for every
\beta\geq 0
. This measure coincides with the stationary distribution of a two-state irreducible and aperiodic Markov chain.
This process is a finitary coding of an i.i.d. process, with a coding window whose tail decays exponentially; see
[
3
]
.
Moreover, it follows for instance from
[
20
]
that it also satisfies Gaussian concentration.
In fact, one can go beyond finite-state Markov chains. Any countable-state irreducible and aperiodic Markov chain with exponential return-time tails is a finitary coding of an i.i.d. process with exponential coding-length tails
and satisfies Gaussian concentration. Conversely, exponential return-time tails are not merely sufficient but in fact necessary for these two properties to hold.
We will discuss Markov chains and these phenomena in detail in Section
5.5
.

5.2.2
The random-cluster model

As before, let
E
be the set of nearest-neighbor edges of the lattice
\mathds{Z}^{d}
. A configuration is
y\in\{0,1\}^{E}
where
y(e)=1
means that
e
is open and
y(e)=0
closed.

Fix a box
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
with edge set
E(\Lambda)
(all edges having at least one endpoint in
\Lambda
). For parameters
p\in[0,1]
and
q>0
, and a boundary condition
z\in\{0,1\}^{E\setminus E(\Lambda)}
, the finite-volume random-cluster measure on
\{0,1\}^{E(\Lambda)}
is

\phi^{z}_{\Lambda,p,q}(y)\;=\;\frac{1}{Z^{z}_{\Lambda}(p,q)}\;p^{\,|y|}\,(1-p)^{\,|E(\Lambda)|-|y|}\;q^{\,k_{\Lambda}^{z}(y)},\quad y\in\{0,1\}^{E(\Lambda)},

where
|y|=\sum_{e\in E(\Lambda)}y(e)
is the number of open edges, and
k_{\Lambda}^{z}(y)
is the number of connected components (clusters) of the graph formed by the open edges in
E(\Lambda)
together with the wiring prescribed by
z
on
E\setminus E(\Lambda)
. Two standard boundary conditions are:

\text{free: }z\equiv 0\;\text{(no wiring across the boundary)},\text{wired: }z\equiv 1\;\text{(all boundary vertices identified)}.

The infinite-volume random-cluster measures
\phi^{\mathrm{free}}_{p,q}
and
\phi^{\mathrm{wired}}_{p,q}
are weak limits of

\phi^{0}_{\Lambda,p,q}
and
\phi^{1}_{\Lambda,p,q}
as
\Lambda\uparrow\mathds{Z}^{d}
, respectively. They are shift-invariant and ergodic.
When they coincide, we may omit the superscript for notational clarity and write
\phi_{p,q}
for the common measure.
It is known that there exists a critical parameter
p_{c}(q)\in[0,1]
such that

\phi^{\,i}_{p,q}\big\{\exists\ \text{an infinite cluster}\big\}=\begin{cases}0,&p<p_{c}(q),\\[4.0pt]
1,&p>p_{c}(q).\end{cases}

Notice that
q=1
corresponds to Bernoulli bond percolation,
hence the infinite-volume measure is unique for all
p
(the phase transition concerns only the appearance of an infinite open cluster).

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
q>1
.

If
p<p_{c}(q)
, then
\phi_{\Lambda,p,q}
satisfies Gaussian concentration.

If
p>p_{c}(q)
, then neither
\phi^{\mathrm{free}}_{p,q}
nor
\phi^{\mathrm{wired}}_{p,q}
satisfies Gaussian concentration.

Proof.

If
p<p_{c}(q)
, by Theorem 1.3 in
[
35
]
, this model is a finitary coding of finite-valued i.i.d. random field whose coding radius has stretched-exponential tails, so the
result follows at once from Theorem
4.1
.
If
p>p_{c}(q)
, there is phase coexistence: at least two distinct infinite-volume Gibbs measures, namely
\phi^{\mathrm{free}}_{p,q}
and
\phi^{\mathrm{wired}}_{p,q}
have zero relative entropy with respect to one another, hence by Theorem
3.5
, Gaussian concentration cannot hold.
∎

In dimension
d=2
, every infinite-volume Gibbs measure is a convex combination of the free and wired measures. In particular, the only translation-invariant ergodic Gibbs measures are

\phi^{\mathrm{free}}_{p,q}
and
\phi^{\mathrm{wired}}_{p,q}
(when they are distinct). Hence, in dimension
d=2
, the previous theorem provides a complete picture for
p\neq p_{c}(q)
.

Fix an integer

.

Let

If
\beta<\beta(q)

[

5.2.4
Weak and strong spatial mixing

Let
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
be a
B
-valued Markov random field with law
\nu
supported on the feasible set of configurations
\mathsf{Y}\subset B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
.
Recall that for a finite set
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
, the external (nearest-neighbor) boundary is

\partial\Lambda\;:=\;\{\,i\in\Lambda^{\mathrm{c}}:\ \mathrm{dist}(i,\Lambda)=1\,\}
.

Write
\mathsf{Y}_{\partial\Lambda}
for the feasible boundary configurations on
\partial\Lambda
. For
\Lambda^{\prime}\subset\Lambda
finite and
z\in\mathsf{Y}_{\partial\Lambda}
, denote by

\nu_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}}^{\,z}(\,\cdot\,)\;:=\;\mathrm{Law}_{\nu}\big(Y_{\Lambda^{\prime}}\in\cdot\ \big|\ Y_{\partial\Lambda}=z\big)

for
\nu
-a.e. feasible
z\in\mathsf{Y}_{\partial\Lambda}
, the conditional
\Lambda^{\prime}
-marginal inside
\Lambda
under boundary condition
z
.

We recall two classical notions of spatial mixing. Weak spatial mixing will only play a role when
d=2
, but we will come to that property in the section on open problems.

We say that
\nu
satisfies
weak spatial mixing with rate
\varrho:\mathds{N}\to[0,\infty)
(nonincreasing with
\varrho(n)\to 0
) if for every finite
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
, every
\Lambda^{\prime}\subset\Lambda
, and all feasible
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Y}_{\partial\Lambda}
,

\big\|\,\nu_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}}^{\,z}-\nu_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}}^{\,z^{\prime}}\,\big\|_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TV}}}\;\leq\;|\Lambda^{\prime}|\;\varrho\big(\mathrm{dist}(\Lambda^{\prime},\partial\Lambda)\big).

If there exist constants
c,C>0
such that
\varrho(n)\leq C\operatorname{e}^{-cn}
for all
n\geq 1
, we say that
\nu
satisfies
exponential weak spatial mixing
.

We say that
\nu
satisfies
strong spatial mixing with rate
\varrho:\mathds{N}\to[0,\infty)
(nonincreasing with
\varrho(n)\to 0
) if for every finite
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
, every
\Lambda^{\prime}\subset\Lambda
, and all feasible
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Y}_{\partial\Lambda}
,

\big\|\,\nu_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}}^{\,z}-\nu_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}}^{\,z^{\prime}}\,\big\|_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TV}}}\;\leq\;|\Lambda^{\prime}|\;\varrho\big(\mathrm{dist}\big(\Lambda^{\prime},\ \{i\in\partial\Lambda:\ z_{i}\neq z^{\prime}_{i}\}\big)\big).

In words: the influence of boundary conditions decays with the
\ell^{1}
-distance from
\Lambda^{\prime}
to the disagreement set on the boundary.
If there exist constants
c,C>0
such that
\varrho(n)\leq C\operatorname{e}^{-cn}
for all
n\geq 1
, we say that
\nu
satisfies
exponential strong spatial mixing
.

As a direct consequence of Theorem 1.1 in
[
58
]
and Theorem
4.1
, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
d\geq 1
and let
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
be a random field taking values in a finite set
B
.

If
Y
satisfies exponential strong spatial mixing, then
Y
satisfies Gaussian concentration.

If
d=2
and
Y
satisfies exponential weak spatial mixing for squares and has no hard constraints (that is, the topological support of its
law if
B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
), then
Y
also satisfies Gaussian concentration.

We illustrate this theorem with one example borrowed from
[
58
]
. More details and references about this examples, as well as other examples, such as the hard-core model, the Widom-Rowlinson model or the beach model, can be found therein.
In regimes that either coincide with the uniqueness regime or are contained in it, we can deduce Gaussian concentration.
Notice that, for the beach model, neither Dobrushin’s uniqueness condition nor disagreement percolation can be directly applied. For
d\geq 2

and
\lambda<2/(2d^{2}+d-1)
(where the activity
\lambda
is the parameter governing the model), we can deduce Gaussian concentration.

Proper colorings.

Let
q\geq 3
be an integer. A proper
q
-coloring is a configuration
x\in\{1,\dots,q\}^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
satisfying
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
for any adjacent vertices
i
and
j
. The set of all proper

-colorings define a subshift of finite type of
\{1,\dots,q\}^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
on which the Gibbs
measures will be supported.
For
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
and a boundary condition
z
on
\Lambda^{\mathrm{c}}
, the finite-volume Gibbs measure is the uniform law on proper
q
-colorings of
\Lambda
that match
z
on
\partial\Lambda
.
It is well known (e.g., by Dobrushin’s uniqueness condition) that there is a unique Gibbs measure for proper
q
-colorings when

, and that this measure satisfies exponential strong spatial mixing. This condition can slightly broadened to

q>2\alpha d-\gamma
where

\alpha^{\alpha}=\operatorname{e}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\gamma:=\frac{4\alpha^{3}-6\alpha^{2}-3\alpha+4}{2(\alpha^{2}-1)}.

(21)

Numerically,

and

.

Theorem 5.6
.

For
d\geq 2
and
q>2\alpha d-\gamma
, with

and

5.3
The thermodynamic jamming limit of the car parking process

Here we consider the simple parking process, which is a particular example of the general class of random sequential adsorption (RSA) models
[
49
,
16
]
. These models are defined by an irreversible deposition mechanism (here on
\mathds{Z}^{d}
) and therefore do not belong to the class of equilibrium models, such as Gibbs distributions
[
24
,
48
]
, which typically arise as the stationary measures of reversible dynamics.

As before, let
\Lambda_{n}:=[-n,\ldots,n]^{d}\cap\mathds{Z}^{d}
, which we view as an initially empty box (
Y_{i}=0
for all
i\in\Lambda_{n}
) on which cars are parked sequentially according to the following rule. At each step, a site
i\in\Lambda_{n}
is sampled uniformly at random among those not previously selected. If all
2d
nearest neighbors of
i
are empty, the site is occupied (that is, we set
Y_{i}=1
); otherwise, it remains empty. Once all sites have been examined, the procedure stops, and the resulting configuration
Y_{\Lambda_{n}}\in\{0,1\}^{\Lambda_{n}}
is called the jamming limit of
\Lambda_{n}
.

Penrose
[
49
]
proved a weak law of large numbers and a central limit theorem for the proportion of occupied sites as
n\to\infty
. Subsequently, Ritchie
[
51
]
established a strong law of large numbers by introducing the thermodynamic jamming limit of the parking process, namely a random field
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
. He constructed this field as a finitary coding of i.i.d. random variables
X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
,
i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
, with a coding radius having an exponential tail.

As a consequence, Gaussian concentration bounds for this random field
Y
follow from Theorem
4.1
. This result is substantially stronger than Proposition 2.4 in
[
16
]
, which is restricted to the proportion of occupied sites, since our theorem applies to arbitrary functions with the bounded-difference property.

5.4
Left finitary processes

Stochastic processes are commonly modeled as probability measures on
B^{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
\mathbb{Z}
is interpreted as the time axis. In this section, we introduce a general class of processes, which we call
left finitary processes
. Closely related notions already appear in the literature under the name of
unilateral codings

[
21
,
45
]
.

Let
A
and
B
be standard Borel spaces. Suppose that the
B
-valued process

Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is obtained as a (stationary) coding of an
A
-valued process

X=(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Let
\varphi
denote the coding map. For
x\in A^{\mathbb{Z}}
, we define the
left coding radius at the origin
by

r_{\varphi}^{-}(x)\;:=\;\inf\Big\{r\in\mathds{N}_{0}:\ \forall y\in A^{\mathbb{Z}},\ y_{-r}^{0}=x_{-r}^{0}\ \Rightarrow\ \varphi(y)_{0}=\varphi(x)_{0}\Big\}\ \in\ \mathds{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}.

We say that a stochastic process
Y
is a
left finitary coding
of
X
if

r_{\varphi}^{-}
is almost surely finite.

The following result is immediate from the definition.

Theorem 5.7
.

If
Y
is a left finitary coding of an i.i.d. process
X
as above, then for every local function

f:B^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathds{R}
satisfying the bounded-difference property,

\log\mathds{E}\big[\exp\{\lambda(f(Y)-\mathds{E}f(Y))\}\big]\;\leq\;3\,\lambda^{2}\,\left(\mathds{E}\big[2r_{\varphi}^{-}(X)+1\big]\right)^{2}\,\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\forall\,\lambda>0.

(22)

Proof.

A left finitary coding satisfies the short-range factorization property with
\alpha=1

(see Definition
4.2
). Indeed, since the coding is one-sided, whenever

\max\{\|\ell-i\|,\|k-i\|,\|\ell-k\|\}=\|\ell-i\|,

the random variables

\mathds{1}\!\left\{\|k-i\|_{\infty}\leq r^{-}_{k}(X)\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathds{1}\!\left\{\|\ell-k\|_{\infty}\leq r^{-}_{\ell}(X)\right\}

depend on disjoint sets of input variables and are therefore independent. The claim then follows by applying Theorem
4.3
.
∎

If
Y=\varphi(X)
is a left finitary coding, then there exists a measurable function

\psi:\bigcup_{k\geq 1}A^{k}\to B
and a stopping time
\tau
such that

Y_{0}=\psi(X_{0},X_{-1},\ldots,X_{-\tau}).

Thus, left finitary processes - that is, left finitary codings of i.i.d. processes - can be viewed as natural generalizations of moving average processes of finite order
m

[
6
]
, which admit representations of the form

Y_{0}=\psi(X_{0},X_{-1},\ldots,X_{-m}).

Coupling from the past algorithms

Let
X=(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with values on a standard Borel space
A
. A stochastic recursive sequence
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a stochastic sequence of random variables on a standard Borel space
B
, defined through
Y_{i}=f_{i}(Y_{-\infty}^{i-1};X_{i}),i\in\mathbb{Z}
where
f=(f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a family of deterministic functions
f_{i}:B^{[-\infty,i-1]}\times A\rightarrow B
. We will assume that the functions are equal up to translation.
For any
i\leq j
define also the composition
f^{[i,j]}:B^{[-\infty,i-1]}\times A\rightarrow B
, by
f^{[i,i]}:=f_{i}
and recursively for any
j>i

f^{[i,j]}(y_{-\infty}^{i-1};x_{i}^{j}):=f_{j}(y_{-\infty}^{i-1}f^{[i,i]}(y_{-\infty}^{i-1};x_{i})\ldots f^{[i,j-1]}(y_{-\infty}^{i-1};x_{i}^{j-1});x_{j}).

Define the
regeneration time
for the recursive sequence
Y

\theta=\theta(X):=\inf\left\{k\geq 0:Y^{[y_{-\infty}^{-k-1}]}_{0}\,\,\text{doesn't depend on }\,y_{-\infty}^{-k-1}\right\}.

If
\theta<\infty
a.s. with respect to the law of
X
, there exists a unique stationary process
Y
defined using
f
and
X
and that the state
Y^{[y_{-\infty}^{-\theta-1}]}_{0}\in B
is distributed according to its stationary law. When the stochastic process admits a representation as a stochastic recursive sequence and the regeneration time is almost surely finite, we have an explicit algorithm that can construct the process, which is a particular instance of perfect simulation algorithms
[
26
]
(see Section 4 therein and the concept of weak verifiability).

One of the first examples of perfect simulation using a stochastic recursive sequence was obtained for finite state Markov chains and called the coupling-from-the-past (CFTP) algorithm
[
50
]
. Later on, several works developed CFTP algorithms for Markov chains on a general alphabet
[
27
,
44
]
or non-Markov chains on a countable alphabet
[
17
,
25
]
.

From the definitions, it is clear that a process that can be obtained by a CFTP algorithm with almost surely finite regeneration time is a left-finitary process. Hence, we have the following consequence of Theorem
5.7
.

Corollary 5.1
.

If
Y
is obtained by a CFTP algorithm using
X
and
\mathbb{E}\theta<\infty
, then
Y
has Gaussian concentration bounds of the form of (
22
) with
\theta
in place of
r_{\varphi}^{-}
.

Some consequences of this result are given below.

5.5
Markov chains

Gaussian concentration for Markov chains has been established in several works
[
40
,
54
,
14
,
47
,
11
]
under various sets of assumptions. All these results, however, require at least
uniform ergodicity
that is,

\sup_{b\in B}\big\|P^{n}(a,\cdot)-\pi(\cdot)\big\|_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TV}}}\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\;0,

where
\pi
denotes the stationary distribution of the Markov chain.
More recently,
[
20
]
showed that a Markov chain satisfies Gaussian concentration if and only if it is
geometrically ergodic
,
i.e.
, there exists
\rho\in(0,1)
such that for every
b\in B
there is a constant
C_{b}
satisfying

\big\|P^{n}(b,\cdot)-\pi(\cdot)\big\|_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TV}}}\;\leq\;C_{b}\,\rho^{n}.

This assumption is strictly weaker than uniform ergodicity (see the Toboggan Markov chain discussed below as an example).
Subsequently,
[
36
]
derived Gaussian concentration under geometric ergodicity, with an explicit but often difficult to calculate concentration constant.

In this section, we explore the consequences of our results in the specific context of Markov chains.

5.5.1
Geometrically ergodic Markov chains

When we specialize to the case where
Y
is a Markov chain, Theorem
4.1
yields the following striking consequence.
We will say that
Y
has exponential return times if for every
b\in B
, there exist constants
c,C>0
such that
w

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{b}>k|X_{0}=b)\leq C\operatorname{e}^{-ck}\,,\forall k\in\mathds{N},

where
\tau_{b}:=\inf\{k\geq 1:Y_{k}=b\}
.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
Y=(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a stationary, irreducible, and aperiodic Markov chain with countable state space
B
, transition matrix
P
, and unique stationary distribution
\pi
.
Then the following properties are pairwise equivalent:

(1)

Y
is geometrically ergodic;

(2)

Y
satisfies Gaussian concentration;

(3)

Y
has exponential return times;

(4)

Y
is a finitary process with exponentially decaying coding radius;

(5)

Y
is a coding of an i.i.d. process;

(6)

Y
is finitarily isomorphic to an i.i.d. process.

Remark 5.4
.

Several additional equivalences could be included in Theorem
5.8
. For instance,
[
29
]
lists 27 equivalent characterizations of geometric ergodicity. Moreover,
[
5
]
(see Theorem 3.7 therein) shows that geometric ergodicity is equivalent to exponential
\beta
-mixing. We refer the reader to
[
5
]
for a formal definition of
\beta
-mixing and note that this property is also known in the literature as absolute regularity or weak Bernoullicity; see, for example,
[
56
]
.

Remark 5.5
.

It was established by
[
27
]
that, for Markov chains on general state spaces, the existence of a coupling-from-the-past simulation algorithm is equivalent to uniform ergodicity. A notable implication of Theorem

is thus that geometrically ergodic Markov chains which fail to be uniformly ergodic provide concrete examples of finitary processes that cannot arise from any coupling-from-the-past construction.

Proof.

(1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2).
This equivalence was established on general state spaces by
[
20
]
.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3).
Fix a state

. If the Markov chain satisfies Gaussian concentration,
using (

) with
u=\pi(b)/2
gives

\mathds{P}(\tau_{b}>n)=\mathds{P}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\{Y_{i}=b\}}=0\bigg)\leq\mathds{P}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\{Y_{i}=b\}}-\pi(b)\leq-\tfrac{\pi(b)}{2}\bigg)\leq\operatorname{e}^{-c_{b}n},

where
c_{b}:=\pi(b)^{2}/(8C)
. Moreover, by stationarity,

\mathds{P}(\tau_{b}=n)=\pi(b)\,\mathds{P}(\tau_{b}\geq n-1|Y_{0}=b),

which shows that

has an exponential tail.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(4).
This implication was proved in
[

(see Theorem 1 therein) for stationary, irreducible, and aperiodic Markov chains on a countable state space.

(4)
\Rightarrow

This implication is immediate from the definitions.

(5)

This implication is essentially due to Smorodinsky and appears in

This follows directly from Theorem

(6)

5.5.2
Further remarks about Markov chains

Explicit bounds under uniform ergodicity

For Markov chains in general state spaces,
[
27
]
showed that a Markov chain can be obtained by a CFTP algorithm if, and only if, it is uniformly ergodic. Thus, uniformly ergodic Markov chains satisfy Corollary
5.1
and have the Gaussian concentration (
22
) holding with
\theta
in place of
r_{\varphi}^{-}
, where we recall that
\theta
is the coupling time of the CFTP.
Consequently, the derivation of an explicit Gaussian concentration bound boils down to computing
\mathds{E}[\theta]
for a specific CFTP construction.

Under uniform ergodicity, the chain satisfies a Doeblin condition: there exist an integer
m\geq 1
, a probability measure
\nu
on the state space
B
, and a constant
\beta\in(0,1)
such that

\inf_{x\in B}P^{m}(x,E)\;\geq\;\beta\,\nu(E),\quad\forall E\subset B.

In this context,
[
27
]
propose the
multigamma coupling
of
[
44
]
as a CFTP scheme (see the proof of Theorem 4.2 therein). For this coupling,
\theta/m
is geometrically distributed with parameter
\beta
, that is,

\frac{\theta}{m}\eqlaw\mathrm{Geo}(\beta).

In particular,
\mathds{E}[\theta]=m/\beta<+\infty
, and according to the above discussion we get the explicit Gaussian concentration bound

\log\mathds{E}\big[\exp\{\lambda(f(Y)-\mathds{E}f(Y))\}\big]\;\leq\;3\,\lambda^{2}\,(2m/\beta+1)^{2}\,\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\forall\,\lambda>0.

in terms of the Doeblin parameters
(m,\beta)
, which can be read off directly from the transition kernel
P
.

Explicit bounds under geometric ergodicity

We mentioned that
[
36
]
obtained explicit Gaussian concentration results under the weaker assumption of geometric ergodicity. In the present framework, Theorem
5.8
, together with Theorem
4.1
, yields the bound

\log\mathds{E}\big[\exp\{\lambda(f(Y)-\mathds{E}f(Y))\}\big]\;\leq\;2^{d}\lambda^{2}\,\mathds{E}\big[(2r\!_{\varphi}(X)+1)^{2}\,\big]\,\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}\,,\qquad\forall\,\lambda>0.

(23)

where
r\!_{\varphi}
denotes the coding radius associated with a finitary coding of the Markov chain; see Section
2
.

In particular,
[
3
]
constructed a finitary coding for geometrically ergodic Markov chains, showing that such chains are finitary codings of i.i.d. processes. In principle, their construction yields explicit (though involved) bounds on the tail distribution of
\theta
from which we could extract bounds on
\mathbb{E}[\theta^{2}]
and
\mathbb{E}[\theta]
to insert into (
23
). However, these bounds are far less transparent than in the uniformly ergodic case, where the concentration inequality can be expressed directly in terms of the Doeblin parameters
(m,\beta)
. Nevertheless, we explain below how this approach can be carried out explicitly in a non-uniformly ergodic example.

A toy example: the Toboggan Markov chain

Consider the Markov chain on
B=\mathbb{N}
with transition matrix
P
defined by

P(0,i)=p_{i},\quad i\geq 0,\qquad P(i,i-1)=1,\quad i\geq 1,

where
(p_{i})_{i\geq 0}
is a probability distribution on
\mathbb{N}
. Assume that
p_{i}>0
for all
i\geq 0
, so that the chain is irreducible and aperiodic. State
0
is necessarily recurrent.

This chain is classical in the Markov chain literature and is sometimes referred to as the
forward recurrence time
or
residual lifetime
process; see
[
43
, Section 10.3]
. It is positive recurrent if and only if
\mu:=\sum_{i\geq 0}i\,p_{i}<\infty
, in which case there exists a unique stationary distribution given by

\pi(j)=\frac{1}{\mu}\sum_{k\geq j}p_{k},\qquad j\geq 0.

Moreover, the chain satisfies any (and hence all) of the equivalent properties listed in Theorem
5.8
if and only if there exists
r>1
such that

\mathds{E}_{0}\!\left[r^{\tau_{0}}\right]<\infty,

where
\tau_{0}
denotes the return time to
0
; see
[
43
, Theorem 15.1.4]
. However, unless the distribution
(p_{i})
has finite support, the chain is not uniformly ergodic. Indeed, for any
n
,

P^{n}(k,0)=0\quad\text{for all }k>n,

and therefore

\sup_{b\in B}\,\bigl\|P^{n}(b,\cdot)-\pi(\cdot)\bigr\|_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TV}}}\;\geq\;\bigl|P^{n}(n+1,0)-\pi(0)\bigr|\;\geq\;\pi(0).

We now compute
\mathds{E}[\theta^{2}]
for the finitary coding constructed in
[
3
]
in this example. Following their approach, consider the binary process

Y_{i}:=\mathds{1}_{\{X_{i}=0\}},\quad i\in\mathbb{Z},

which is a renewal process with renewal state
1
and inter-arrival times distributed as
\tau_{0}
. A finitary coding from an i.i.d. process for
Y
automatically yields a finitary coding for
X
, since for every
i\in\mathbb{Z}
,

X_{i}=r_{i}-i,\qquad r_{i}:=\inf\{k\geq i:Y_{k}=1\}.

Proposition 4 of
[
3
]
describes a finitary coding of
Y
from an i.i.d. process under the assumption that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathds{P}(\tau_{0}=n|\tau_{0}\geq n)>0.

To obtain an explicit illustration, consider the geometric distribution
p_{i}=2^{-i-1}
for
i\geq 0
. In this case,

\mathds{P}(\tau_{0}=n|\tau_{0}\geq n)=\tfrac{1}{2},\qquad\forall n\geq 0,

and the simplest instance of the construction in
[
3
]
applies. A direct inspection of their proof (case of full support) shows that

\mathds{P}(\theta=i)=2^{-(i+1)},\qquad i\geq 0,

so that
\mathds{E}[\theta]=1
and
\mathds{E}[\theta^{2}]=3
. Plugging these values into (
23
) yields a Gaussian concentration bound with an explicit constant, despite the absence of uniform ergodicity.

5.5.3
The case of renewal processes

A discrete-time renewal process is a binary-valued stochastic process in which the distances between consecutive 1’s are independent and identically distributed random variables.
Let us denote by
(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}
their common distribution. The common distribution of these distances is called the inter-arrival (or waiting-time) distribution. Notice that this process
is a binary Markov chain if and only if
(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is the geometric distribution, so in this case, we already know that gaussian concentration will be satisfied by preceding
discussion. Although not Markovian in general, the renewal process shares several similarities with the case of Markov chain since, as is well known, the indicator process of the s
uccessive visits to any fixed symbol in a Markov chain is a renewal process.
Using this relation between renewal and Markov processes, we obtain the following.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
Y=(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathds{Z}}
be a renewal process with
\gcd\{k\geq 1:f_{k}>0\}=1
(non-lattice inter-arrival distribution). The following are pairwise equivalent:

(1)

Y
satisfies a Gaussian concentration bound;

(2)

\sum_{k\geq 1}s^{k}f_{k}<\infty
for some
s>1
;

(3)

Y
is a finitary process with exponential tail coding radius;

(4)

Y
is a finitary coding of an i.i.d. process.

Proof.

(1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2). This is proved in
[
11
]
, see Theorem 3.4 therein.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3). This is Theorem 2 of
[
3
]
.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(4). Follows by definition.

(4)
\Rightarrow
(2). This is Proposition 3 of
[
3
]
.
∎

5.6
Stochastic chains with unbounded memory

A stochastic chain with unbounded memory (SCUM) is a discrete-time stochastic process whose conditional distribution at time
n
given the past may depend on an unbounded part of the past rather than on a fixed finite window; this class encompasses Markov chains and renewal processes as a special case but also includes genuinely non-Markovian processes.
More precisely, a SCUM is defined via conditional expectations with respect to the past. For each
n\in\mathds{Z}
, a probability kernel is a function

g_{n}:\mathcal{B}\times B^{[-\infty,n-1]}\to[0,1]

such that, for every
x\in B^{[-\infty,n-1]}
, the map
S\mapsto g_{n}(S|x)
defines a probability measure on
\mathcal{B}
, and for every
S\in\mathcal{B}
,
the map
x\mapsto g_{n}(S|x)
is measurable. Since we only consider shift-invariant kernels, we drop the index
n
and simply write
g
.

If
g(S|x)
depends only on the most recent symbol
x_{-1}
, then
g
reduces to a usual transition matrix on
B
. More generally, a stationary process
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathds{Z}}
, with corresponding translation-invariant probability measure
\mu
on
B^{\mathds{Z}}
, is said to be compatible with the kernel
g
if

\mathds{E}_{\mu}\big[\mathds{1}_{S}(X_{n})|X^{n-1}_{-\infty}=x\big]=g(S|x)

for all
n\in\mathds{Z}
, all
S\in\mathcal{B}
, and for
\mu
-almost every
x\in B^{[-\infty,n-1]}
, where
\mathds{1}_{S}
denotes the indicator function of the set S. Since we allow for general kernels g, not necessarily of Markovian type, such processes are referred to as stochastic chains with unbounded memory (SCUMs) in
[
11
]
. They also appear in the literature under various other names.

Gaussian concentration for SCUMs is established in
[
11
]
under suitable regularity assumptions on the kernel
g
. In the present paper, we instead rely on Corollary
5.1
, showing that if the SCUM can be obtained from a CFTP algorithm with finite expected regeneration time,
i.e.
,
\mathds{E}[\theta]<\infty
, then it satisfies a Gaussian concentration with a constant proportional to
(\mathds{E}[\theta])^{2}
.

Gaussian concentration for SCUMs was established in
[
11
]
under suitable regularity assumptions on the kernel
g
. In the present paper, instead of imposing direct regularity conditions on
g
, we invoke Corollary
5.1
. This shows that whenever the SCUM is generated by a CFTP algorithm with finite expected regeneration time,
i.e.
,
\mathds{E}[\theta]<\infty
, the process satisfies a Gaussian concentration inequality with a constant proportional to
(\mathds{E}[\theta])^{2}
.

The first CFTP construction for SCUMs was introduced in
[
17
]
. Assuming that
B
is countable, they defined the following quantities associated with the kernel
g
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{b\in B}\inf_{x\in B^{[-\infty,-1]}}g(b|x);

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle:=\inf_{a_{-k}^{-1}\in B^{[-k,-1]}}\sum_{b\in B}\inf_{x\in B^{[-\infty,-k-1]}}g(b|xa_{-k}^{-1})\,\,,k\geq 1.

They proved that if
\prod_{k\geq 0}\alpha_{k}>0
, then their CFTP satisfies
\mathds{E}[\theta]<\infty
,
so the following result follows directly from Corollary
5.1
.

Proposition 5.3
.

If the process
Y
has countable alphabet
B
and kernel
g
satisfying
\prod_{k\geq 0}\alpha_{k}>0
, then it has Gaussian concentration.

The existence of a CFTP with finite expected coalescence time has since been extended well beyond the framework of
[
17
]
(see, e.g.,
[
30
,
19
,
31
,
32
,
18
]
). Consequently, the corresponding SCUMs also satisfy Gaussian concentration under these broader sets of assumptions.

Finally, we note that although Corollary
5.1
applies to general alphabets, CFTP constructions for SCUMs are, to the best of our knowledge, currently available only for countable alphabets.

6
Final remarks and open problems

6.1
About the sharpness of Theorems
4.1
and
4.3

Here we examine the sharpness of our two main abstract results and show that, in terms of their dependence on the coding volume, they are essentially optimal.

Both Theorems
4.1
and
4.3
rely on Marton’s inequality (Theorem
4.2
) and on Lemma
4.2
. It is conceivable that there exists an alternative approach that avoids the use of Marton’s inequality; however, we do not believe this to be the case. Lemma
4.2
appears to be unavoidable, and in fact optimal, if one wants the argument to apply to arbitrary finitary codings and to arbitrary local observables
f
.

The only step where a nontrivial upper bound is used occurs in Proposition
4.1
, which is an application of Young’s inequality for discrete convolutions.
To assess the sharpness of this step, we isolate the underlying analytic mechanism in the following abstract result, which we then apply directly to our setting.

Proposition 6.1

(Saturation of the Toeplitz quadratic form)
.

Let
b=(b_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
be a nonnegative, absolutely summable kernel on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, with

\|b\|_{1}:=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}b_{m}<\infty.

For any finitely supported vector
\delta=(\delta_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, define the quadratic form

Q(\delta):=\sum_{k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\delta_{k}\,\delta_{\ell}\,b_{\ell-k}.

Then

\sup_{\delta\neq 0}\frac{Q(\delta)}{\|\delta\|_{2}^{2}}=\|b\|_{1}.

Moreover, this supremum is asymptotically attained by block indicators:
if
\delta^{(L)}_{k}:=\mathds{1}_{\{k\in\Lambda_{L}\}}
with
\Lambda_{L}:=[-L,L]^{d}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, then

\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{Q(\delta^{(L)})}{\|\delta^{(L)}\|_{2}^{2}}=\|b\|_{1}.

Proof.

Young’s inequality with exponents
p=q=2
and
r=1
yields the upper bound

Q(\delta)\leq\|\delta\|_{2}^{2}\,\|b\|_{1}.

For the block indicators
\delta^{(L)}
, we compute

\frac{Q(\delta^{(L)})}{\|\delta^{(L)}\|_{2}^{2}}=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}b_{m}\,\frac{|\Lambda_{L}\cap(\Lambda_{L}-m)|}{|\Lambda_{L}|}.

For each fixed
m
, the overlap ratio converges to
1
as
L\to\infty
, and it is uniformly bounded by
1
.
Since
b\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
, dominated convergence yields the claim.
∎

We now apply this proposition to our setting.

Corollary 6.1
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the kernel

b_{m}:=\mathbb{E}\Big[\,\big|B_{\infty}(0,r^{(n)}_{0})\cap B_{\infty}(m,r^{(n)}_{m})\big|\,\Big],\qquad m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Let
f^{(L)}
be a local function such that
\delta_{k}f^{(L)}=\delta>0
for
k\in\Lambda_{L}
and
\delta_{k}f^{(L)}=0
otherwise.
Then

\liminf_{L\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\big(c_{i}^{(n)}(X)\big)^{2}}{\|\delta f^{(L)}\|_{2}^{2}}\;=\;\|b\|_{1}.

Consequently, the universal bound

\mathbb{E}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\big(c_{i}^{(n)}(X)\big)^{2}\;\leq\;\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}\,\|b\|_{1}

is sharp: neither the prefactor nor the functional
\|b\|_{1}
can be replaced by a strictly smaller universal quantity.

Intuitively, the functions
f^{(L)}
maximize overlap by spreading oscillations evenly over large regions, causing the Toeplitz quadratic form to asymptotically attain its
\ell^{1}
norm.

In the setting of Theorem
4.1
, the quantity
\|b\|_{1}
is controlled by a second moment of the coding volume.
The corollary therefore shows that, in the absence of additional structure, the second-moment scale appearing in
Theorem
4.1
cannot be improved by analytic refinements alone.

To pass from a finite second moment to a finite first moment of the coding volume, we rely on an additional abstract structure, namely the short-range factorization property introduced in Definition
4.2
, which allows us to derive Theorem
4.3
. This naturally raises the following question: can one do better than assuming finiteness of the first moment of the coding volume?

More precisely, suppose one attempts to replace the first moment of the coding volume by a strictly smaller functional in a bound that is meant to hold
universally
, that is, simultaneously for every finitary coding, every i.i.d. input, and every local observable. We show that no such universal squared-influence estimate can improve upon the scale of the first moment of the coding volume.

Proposition 6.2

(No universal bound below the first moment)
.

Let
d\geq 1
. Suppose there exist a constant
K>0
and a nondecreasing functional

\Psi:\ \{\text{nonnegative integer-valued random variables}\}\longrightarrow[0,\infty]

with the following
universality property
:

For every finitary coding
\varphi
, every i.i.d. input
X
, every local observable
f
, and every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathds{E}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\big(c_{i}^{(n)}(X)\big)^{2}\ \leq\ K\,\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}\,\Psi\big(r_{\varphi}^{(n)}(X)\big),

where
r_{\varphi}^{(n)}
denotes the truncated coding radius at the origin.

Then, for all
n
,

\mathds{E}\,\big|B_{\infty}\!\big(0,r^{(n)}_{\varphi}(X)\big)\big|\ \leq\ K\,\Psi\big(r_{\varphi}^{(n)}(X)\big),

and letting
n\to\infty
,

\mathds{E}\,\big|B_{\infty}\!\big(0,r_{\varphi}(X)\big)\big|\ \leq\ K\,\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Psi\big(r_{\varphi}^{(n)}(X)\big).

In particular, any universal squared-influence bound depending only on the random variable
r_{\varphi}

must control at least the first moment of the coding volume; no strictly smaller such functional
can replace it.

Sketch of proof.

Test the bound with a single-site observable
f(y)=h(y_{0})
, scaled so that
\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}=1
.
In this case,

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\big(c_{i}^{(n)}(X)\big)^{2}=\big|B_{\infty}(0,r_{\varphi}^{(n)}(X))\big|\quad\text{pointwise}.

Taking expectations yields the claim.
∎

The proposition is formulated at the level of the marginal law of the coding radius
r_{\varphi}(X)
.
Only monotonicity with respect to stochastic domination is relevant.
Accordingly,
\Psi
may be viewed as a nondecreasing functional of the random variable
r_{0}
itself.

Typical examples include

\Psi(r)=\mathds{E}\,|B_{\infty}(0,r)|,\quad\text{or more generally}\quad\Psi(r)=\mathds{E}\,\phi(r),

where
\phi:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to[0,\infty]
is any nondecreasing function.
The proposition asserts that any universal squared-influence bound depending only on the law of
r_{\varphi}(X)

must, in the worst case, control at least the first moment
\mathds{E}|B_{\infty}(0,r_{\varphi}(X))|
of the coding volume.

In addition to this general obstruction, which is tied to our use of Marton’s inequality, we recall that in the case of the Ising model at the critical temperature (and for any

d\geq 2
), we saw that any finitary coding of the Gibbs measure from an i.i.d. random field has a coding volume with infinite first moment. In particular, Gaussian concentration fails for this measure.
By the way, there do exist a finitary coding by a (finite-valued) i.i.d. random field for the Ising model at the critical temperature; see the proof of Proposition
5.1
.

6.2
Composing finitary codings

A natural question is to look at what happens if we compose, say, two finitary codings
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
. One expects to still have Gaussian concentration for
\varphi_{2}(\varphi_{1}(X))

when
X
is i.i.d., under appropriate moment conditions on the coding volumes of
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
. We state a general result without proof (we only provide the main idea) and roughly explain
why at this level of generality the fourth moments of the coding volumes pop up.

Proposition 6.3

(Composition bound)
.

Let
d\geq 1
. Let
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
be finitary codings with coding radii

r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, and let
Y_{2}=\varphi_{2}(\varphi_{1}(X))
, where
X
is i.i.d.
Assume

\mathds{E}\big((2r_{1}+1)^{4d}\big)<\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathds{E}\big((2r_{2}+1)^{4d}\big)<\infty.

Then
Y_{2}
satisfies a Gaussian concentration bound with constant

K_{d}\,\big(\mathds{E}\big((2r_{2}+1)^{4d}\big)\big)^{1/2}\,\big(\mathds{E}\big((2r_{1}+1)^{4d}\big)\big)^{1/2},

where
K_{d}
depends only on the dimension.

The proof follows the above strategy based on Marton’s Gaussian concentration bound, reducing Gaussian concentration to a bound on the
expected squared influences of the composite coding.
The key geometric step is that the dependence set of the composition at the origin is contained in a union of random
balls

\bigcup_{k\in B_{\infty}(0,M)}B_{\infty}\big(k,\,r_{1}(T^{k}X)\big),\qquad M:=r_{2}(Y_{1}),

where
Y_{1}=\varphi_{1}(X)
, so that its size is bounded by a sum of random volumes over a random index set.

Crucially, the random radius
M
and the random volumes inside this union are both functions of the same input field
X
.
Without additional structure, the second coding may select a large inspection window precisely when the first coding exhibits unusually large local dependence volumes.
This intrinsic “selection bias” prevents any universal decoupling leading to a product of second moments. To obtain the above bound, we thus apply Cauchy–Schwarz, which
results in a fourth-moment condition on the coding volumes

6.3
Beyond
\mathds{Z}^{d}
and shifts

In this paper we work with the following setup:
X=(X_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
is an
A
-valued random field and

Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
is a
B
-valued random field, and
\varphi:A^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}\to B^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
is a measurable,
shift-equivariant map, that is,
\varphi\circ T^{j}=S^{j}\circ\varphi
for all
j\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
. It is assumed to be a coding, that is,

Y\eqlaw\varphi(X)
.

On the geometric side, we take the graph
G=(\mathds{V},E)
with vertex set
\mathds{V}=\mathds{Z}^{d}
and nearest-neighbor edges

E=\bigl\{\{x,y\}\subset\mathds{Z}^{d}:\ \|x-y\|_{1}=1\bigr\}.

The group
\Gamma=\mathds{Z}^{d}
acts on
\mathds{V}
by shifts
x\mapsto x+j
, every shift is a graph automorphism, and the action is transitive
(hence quasi-transitive).

More generally, one may take any countably infinite vertex set
\mathds{V}
and a group
\Gamma
acting on
\mathds{V}
, and define a coding

\varphi:A^{\mathds{V}}\to B^{\mathds{V}}
by requiring
\Gamma
-equivariance (and
Y\eqlaw\varphi(X)
).
If
G
is a locally finite graph on
\mathds{V}
and
\Gamma
acts
quasi-transitively on
\mathds{V}
by graph automorphisms, then
G
is a quasi-transitive graph, meaning that there are only finitely many
symmetry types of vertices.

Many results of Spinka and coauthors
[
58
,
35
]
extend to this setting, in particular to quasi-transitive amenable graphs.
Amenability is an isoperimetric property that expresses small boundary relative to volume at large scales.
Gaussian concentration can be formulated in this general context, so several of our statements could have been written
for quasi-transitive graphs. Natural lattice examples beyond
\mathds{Z}^{d}
include the triangular and hexagonal graphs.

6.4
Does Gaussian concentration imply finitary coding by an i.i.d. field?

Theorem
4.1
raises a natural question. Does Gaussian concentration implies that a shift-invariant random field has to be a finitary coding of an i.i.d. process, under a suitable moment condition on the coding volume?
The guiding intuition is that Gaussian concentration imposes strong structural constraints on the dependence structure of the field. More precisely, we ask the following.

Question 1
.

Let
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
be a shift-invariant random field satisfying Gaussian concentration.
Does Gaussian concentration entail the existence of a finitary i.i.d. coding, under an appropriate moment condition on the coding volume?

When
Y
takes values in a finite alphabet, one may further ask whether the i.i.d. random field used for the coding can also be chosen to be finite valued.

As discussed above, this conjecture holds for countable-state Markov chains and for renewal processes.

6.5
Heavy-tailed coding volumes

As all the examples in dimension
d\geq 2
discussed above illustrate, the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
(finite second moment of the coding volume) or of Theorem
4.3
(finite first moment of the coding volume) are very broadly satisfied. Indeed, in these examples the coding radius typically has exponential or stretched exponential tails.
This naturally leads to the following question.

Question 2
.

Can one find examples of Gibbs measures in dimension
d\geq 2
that are finitary codings of i.i.d. random fields, with polynomially decaying coding radii, but such that the coding volume still has a finite first or second moment?

A natural candidate to explore in this direction is processes or fields with a correlation decay that is lower-bounded by a polynomial function, as an exponential decay of the coding radius implies an exponential decay of the correlation. The long-range Ising model is an interesting class to explore in this direction.

On the other hand, we may revisit the question below raised by Spinka
[
58
]
, and relate it to Gaussian concentration.

Question 3
.

Does exponential weak spatial mixing imply the existence of a finitary coding by an i.i.d. random field with finite expected coding radius?

If the answer is affirmative and the coding is obtained via a coupling-from-the-past algorithm, then Gaussian concentration follows as a consequence of Theorem
4.3
.

6.6
Coding volume with infinite first moment

We have seen an example in which any finitary coding necessarily has a coding volume with infinite first moment, and for which not only Gaussian concentration fails, but even a moment concentration bound of order
2
is impossible, namely the Ising model in dimension
d\geq 2
at the critical temperature.

Gaussian concentration is a particularly strong form of concentration, and its complete failure for the Ising model at the critical temperature highlights the need to consider weaker notions. It is therefore natural to ask, in a more general setting, whether some form of concentration may still persist, such as suitable control of moments up to a critical order, or even control of all moments with constants whose growth as a function of the moment order is too fast to yield exponential moment bounds and hence Gaussian concentration.

Examples illustrating such intermediate behaviors can be found, for instance, in
[
14
]
and in
[
8
]
for the Ising model in dimension
d\geq 2
at sufficiently low temperature, where concentration is of stretched exponential type.

More precisely, we say that a random field
Y
satisfies a moment concentration bound of order
2p
, with
p\in\mathds{N}
, if there exists a constant
C_{p}>0
such that for every local function
f
with the bounded-differences property,

\mathds{E}\big[(f(Y)-\mathds{E}f(Y))^{2p}\big]\leq C_{p}\,\|\delta f\|_{p}^{2p}.

This naturally leads to the following question in view of the two abstract theorems of the present paper.

Question 4
.

Let
Y=\varphi(X)
, where
X
is an i.i.d. random field and
\varphi
is a finitary coding with infinite mean coding volume, that is,
\mathds{E}(|B_{\infty}(0,X)|)=\infty
.
Does the moment concentration bound of order
2
necessarily fail to hold?

In addition to the Ising model at the critical temperature, the voter model in dimension
d=3
provides another example suggesting that the answer to this question is likely affirmative.

6.7
Weak Bernoulli property and Gaussian concentration

A classical mixing property for stochastic processes (in dimension
d=1
) is the weak Bernoulli property, also known as
\beta
-mixing; see
[
5
]
. This property is known to be sufficient to imply Bernoullicity.
A natural question is whether this notion can be extended to random fields in higher dimensions
d\geq 2
. This turns out to be rather subtle and was addressed in
[
7
]
. In order not to lengthen the present article, we do not recall the definition here and instead refer the interested reader to
[
7
]
.
It was subsequently shown in
[
61
, Theorem 1.6]
that if
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
is a finitary coding of an i.i.d. process
X
(not necessarily finite-valued), and if

\mathds{E}\bigl(|B_{\infty}(0,X)|\bigr)<\infty
, then
Y
has the weak Bernoulli property.

On the other hand, we have shown that Gaussian concentration forces Bernoullicity (Theorem
3.2
). This naturally leads to the following question.

Question 5
.

Does Gaussian concentration imply the weak Bernoulli property?

In particular, all Gibbs measures encountered above which admit a finitary coding from an i.i.d. random field are weak Bernoulli and satisfy Gaussian concentration (the first assertion follows from Theorem 1.6 in
[
61
]
).
This is, for instance, the case of the Ising model in dimension
d\geq 2
when
\beta<\beta_{c}
. These examples therefore support an affirmative answer to the question above.

Every irreducible and aperiodic countable-state Markov chain has the weak Bernoulli property; see
[
5
, Theorem 3.2]
. On the other hand, for such chains, Gaussian concentration is equivalent to having exponential tails for return times to any given state. Hence, the answer to the above question is affirmative within the class of countable-state Markov chains, and there exist plenty of Markov chains that are weak Bernoulli but do not satisfy Gaussian concentration.

Let us also note that the
T,T^{-1}
-process associated with simple symmetric random walk in dimension
d\geq 5
provides an example of a system which is weak Bernoulli but not a finitary factor of an i.i.d. process, and for which Gaussian concentration fails. See the remark following Theorem 1.1 in
[
61
]
. The failure of Gaussian concentration follows from the proof of that theorem together with the fact that Gaussian concentration forces standard large deviation behavior; we omit the details. Thus, one may have the weak Bernoulli property without Gaussian concentration.
