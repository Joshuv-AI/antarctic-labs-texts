Factors Affecting Recollapse of the Augmented Vertebrae After Percutaneous Vertebral Augmentation: A Prospective Study
Authors: Yawen Zhang, Junchuan Liu, Maoyu Zhao, Yudian Qiu, Quan Ji, Nan Min, Qiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo investigate the relevant factors of augmented vertebrae recollapse after percutaneous vertebral augmentation (PVA) for the treatment of osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs).MethodA prospective study was performed on 116 patients who were diagnosed with osteoporotic compression fractures in which the anterior height loss of the vertebral body was greater than 1/3. All patients underwent percutaneous vertebral augmentation (PKP or PVP). Possible risk factors for augmented vertebral height decrease, such as age, sex, body mass index, surgical approach, fracture location, cement leakage, patterns of vertebral fractures, Vertebral height restoration (VHR), and intervertebral cleft (IVC), were analyzed.ResultsThe radiography follow‐up period was 1 year. Ninety‐five patients completed the trial. Twenty‐four patients suffered postoperative recollapse. Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging showed that the incidence of IVC in the recollapse group was significantly higher than that in the no‐recollapse group (p < 0.050). The OR value (157.891) indicates that the presence of IVC before surgery significantly exacerbates the incidence of postoperative recollapse. The surgical approaches of the recollapse group and the no‐recollapse group were statistically significant (p < 0.050), and the risk of recollapse in the PKP group was lower compared to the PVP group (OR = 0.094).ConclusionIntervertebral cleft (IVC) and those with PVP intervention are at higher risk of recollapse. Patients with osteoporotic compression fractures with anterior vertebral height loss greater than 1/3, especially those with IVC, should be concerned and taken seriously, and PVP should be carefully selected to prevent their clinical course from worsening.Keywords: augmented vertebrae, percutaneous vertebral augmentation, recollapse, risk factors

Introduction

Osteoporotic Vertebral Compression Fractures (OVCFs) are the main cause of acute and chronic pain in the elderly, and can cause disability in severe cases, impacting patients' quality of life [1]. Percutaneous vertebral augmentation (PVA) includes percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) and percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP). PVA can effectively alleviate pain symptoms in patients, with short surgical time and minimal trauma, making it the preferred method for treating osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures [2, 3, 4]. With the popularization of PVA, complications of PVA presented in increasing studies include pulmonary embolism, transient fever, infection, hemorrhage, neurologic deficit or nerve root irritation, extravasations of cement, fracture of the rib or posterior elements of the vertebrae, unremitting pain, and pneumothorax [5, 6]. With the expansion of long‐term follow‐up studies, late complications such as adjacent vertebrae refracture and enhanced vertebrae recollapse have gradually been focused on. However, to date, there are few reports of further recollapse of previously treated vertebrae. Even the definition and naming of recollapse are still controversial. Lavelle and Cheney named ‘recurrent fracture’ of previously treated vertebrae [7]. Kim suggests that the term ‘recompression’ should be used to describe this condition [8]. Heo and wang recommend that the term ‘recollapse’ should be applied [9, 10]. In this study, we prospectively enrolled 116 patients to identify the risk factors related to recollapse.

Methods

Patient Population

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Hospital (2019BJYYEC‐010‐06) and registered at the China Clinical Trial Registration Center with registration number ChiCTR1900021960. All patients signed informed consent forms before enrollment. According to literature reports, the significant clinical difference between PKP and PVP in the treatment of osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures is the recovery of vertebral height, calculated by PASS11.0, α = 0.050, 1‐β = 0.800. Taking the recovery of vertebral height as the effect value and considering the 20% dropout rate, the sample size was calculated to be 116 cases. From June 2019 to November 2020, patients underwent vertebral reinforcement treatment for osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures in the orthopedic department of Beijing Hospital. Inclusion criteria: (1) Elderly osteoporosis patients over 60 years old, with vertebral compression fractures caused by minor trauma (flat fall) or no clear history of trauma; (2) Imaging examination confirmed a fresh vertebral compression fracture with a loss of more than one‐third of the anterior edge height of the vertebral body. Exclusion criteria: (1) Patients with spinal compression fractures and signs of nerve compression who require open surgery for spinal canal decompression and internal fixation treatment; (2) Pathological compression fractures caused by spinal metastatic tumors; (3) Individuals with severe coagulation disorders; (4) Local skin infection or systemic infectious diseases at the puncture site; (5) Those who cannot be followed up on schedule after treatment. Randomized Grouping and Surgical Methods

The random number table was generated by SAS software in the epidemic ward of Beijing Institute of Geriatrics, Ministry of Health, Beijing Hospital, and put into an opaque envelope. The patients were randomly divided into the PKP group and the PVP group. All operations were performed by doctors with the title of deputy chief physician or above in Beijing Hospital. In the PKP group, the Kyphon Xpander system was used. The PVP group was treated with Spineplex. Both groups were operated under local anesthesia, and the operation method of bilateral pedicle puncture in the prone position was adopted. Two hours after the operation, patients wore an apron and went down to the ground. Regular anti‐osteoporosis treatment was provided after the operation. This study is a single‐center, randomized, single blind (subject blind), parallel‐controlled clinical study. All patients included in the study underwent radiography follow‐up at 1 day, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months after the operation. All patients received systematic anti‐osteoporosis treatment [11]. Recollapse was defined as a 4‐mm decrease in height or the presence of morphological changes on follow‐up radiographs. Anterior vertebral body height was digitally measured using a Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). Three repeated measurements were obtained, and the mean value was used for analysis. All measurements were performed by the same doctor who was blind to patient information and surgical groups [12].

Factors Evaluated

Age, sex, body mass index, surgical approach (PVP or PKP), fracture location (grouped by whether it occurred in the thoracolumbar junction (from T11 to L2)), cement leakage, patterns of vertebral fractures (whether the filling pattern is a solid lump or not), Vertebral height restoration (VHR), and intervertebral cleft (IVC). All patients underwent preoperative lateral plain films and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to define the fresh vertebral fracture and to clarify the existence of IVC (Figure 2A). One day after surgery, a lateral X‐ray was taken to reveal the recovery height of the anterior edge of the vertebral body and the leakage situation (Figure 2B). During the last follow‐up, lateral radiographs were taken to determine if there was any recurrence of vertebral collapse (Figure 2C).

Statistical Analysis

SPSS 25.0 statistical software was used for analysis. The continuous variables of normal distribution were expressed as mean ± SD. The two groups were compared by the t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test; the categorical variables were expressed by the number of cases or percentage, using the Chi‐square test; p < 0.100 was determined to be statistically significant and then included in multivariate regression analysis. Multivariate regression analysis and forward selection was used to analyze the related factors of vertebral recollapse. In this study, p < 0.050 was identified as statistically significant.

Results

As Figure 1 shows, after a year of follow‐up, 18 men and 77 women with an average age of 73.263 ± 5.560 years were included in this study. All patients completed the trial. In the end, 24 patients experienced postoperative recollapse. The characteristics of the 95 patients with or without the presence of recollapse are summarized in Table 1. There was no statistically significant difference in age between the recollapse group and the no‐recollapse group (p > 0.100). Similarly, there was no significant statistical difference in the proportion of female patients between the two groups (75% vs. 83%). We compared the BMI of the two groups and did not find any significant differences [13]. What surprised us was that there was a certain statistical significance in the recollapse rate of different surgical methods (p < 0.100), so we further included this factor into multivariate regression analysis. Previous literature has shown that whether it is the thoracolumbar stage is closely related to the incidence of recollapse, but our study does not support this point (p > 0.100). The distribution of the recollapse vertebrae was T11 (3/24), T12 (5/24), L1 (13/24), L2 (1/24), L3 (1/24) and L5 (1/24), cement leakage, patterns of vertebral fractures, and Vertebral height restoration (VHR) presented no statistically significant differences between the two groups (p > 0.100). There is a significant difference in the presence rate of IVC between the two groups (p < 0.100). In the recollapse group, the presence rate of IVC is as high as 62.5%, far exceeding the rate of the no‐recollapse group (2.8%).

Discussion

OVCF is one of the main complications of osteoporosis, and PVP and PKP surgical treatment methods are often used in clinical practice. However, many patients still experience augmented vertebral recollapse after surgery, with a rate of 25.3% in this prospective study of recollapse after PVA study. Previous studies have shown that the incidence of recollapse of the augmented vertebrae ranged from 3.21% to 63% [6, 8, 10, 12, 14]. Compared to the study of adjacent vertebral refracture after surgery, there are few studies related to the augmented vertebral recollapse, and there is even controversy over its naming and definition. Kim et al., defined recollapse as a 1‐mm decrease in height or the presence of morphological changes on follow‐up radiographs [8]. Some scholars suggest setting the recollapse standard at 4 mm to consider compensating for changes in enhanced vertebral mechanical properties [12, 15, 16]. There are inconsistent reports on the risk factors for vertebral recollapse after PVA surgery. Therefore, it is important to identify the risk factors for enhanced vertebral recurrence after PVA treatment for OVCF.

From our research, it can be found that women account for the majority of vertebral fractures, indicating that the risk of osteoporosis fractures in women is significantly higher than that in men. However, gender differences did not have a significant impact on whether further collapse occurred. Similarly, our study did not show significant differences in age between the two groups. The possible reason for considering this is that our inclusion criteria define the population as the elderly (over 60), eliminating the proportion of young people. Two studies reported that BMI has a significant difference between the two groups and a positive correlation with cemented vertebrae recollapse [17, 18]. But our study did not yield similar results. Similarly, we hold the opposite view to previous studies regarding the impact of surgical methods on recollapse. We found that compared to PVP, the choice of PKP will reduce the occurrence of recollapse. Percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) uses balloon expansion to create a centralized cavity, which facilitates controlled cement injection and more effective load redistribution. Bio‐mechanical studies have confirmed that PKP restores vertebral height and stiffness more effectively than percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP), thus reducing the risk of recollapse [17].

In our study, the location of vertebral fractures spans T5 to L5, and the thoracolumbar region is usually defined as T11–L2. Considering the extensive activity and huge load in this region, the enhanced vertebrae bear higher loads than the non‐thoracolumbar region [19]. In addition, the anatomical study conducted by Ratcliffe et al., showed that the ventral region of the thoracolumbar spine is only provided by the anterior peripheral artery and the sealing artery at the metaphyseal end. Therefore, when OVCF occurs, these weak arteries are easily damaged. In addition, ischemic necrosis and the formation of IVC may occur [20]. Our research results indicate that the affected vertebrae in the thoracolumbar region are not more likely to cause collapse of the enhanced vertebrae. The proportion of bone cement leakage in our study is 27.4%, far less than the 34 to 64% reported by Hollensteiner et al. [21]. There is no statistical difference in the leakage rate of bone cement between the two groups.

Solid lump cement can concentrate stress on the surrounding fragile bones and intercept mechanical interlocking with the surrounding trabecular bone, as there is no or very few speculation into the trabecular bone [22], but our study did not indicate that the distribution of solid lumps is more likely to cause recollapse. The same result also occurred in the factor of VHR, which may be because our inclusion criterion is that the anterior edge of the vertebral body collapses by more than 1/3, which, to some extent, changed the difference in deformation ability of the vertebral body after augmentation.

Our study results exhibited that preoperative IVC could easily induce recollapse of the augmented vertebrae (OR = 157.891; p < 0.001). IVC was diagnosed on MRI as a linear or irregular cystic lesion with abnormal signal intensity on T1‐weighted images and T2‐weighted images. This is consistent with most previous studies; intravertebral cleft (IVC) usually indicates localized osteonecrosis and structural disintegration within the vertebral body, which weakens intrinsic mechanical stability. The presence of IVC suggests dynamic instability and may impede uniform cement dispersion. Uneven cement distribution caused by IVC alters load transmission after augmentation, increasing the risk of recollapse [8, 23, 24]. The vertebrae of IVC fractures have the lowest connection density, the number of trabeculae, and the highest trabecular separation [25]. Therefore, the injected cement is difficult to fill the cancellous bone area of vertebral fractures with IVC; therefore, this distribution pattern is not suitable for exposing stress on the vertebrae. In addition, in the absence of IVC, the injected cement is dispersed in a more even manner throughout the entire space. Therefore, the incidence of recollapse of enhanced vertebrae with IVC before surgery is significantly higher.

Although our study has strict exclusion and inclusion criteria and was carefully screened for risk factors, it still has some limitations. The sample size of the overall experiment is not sufficient, and prospective, multicenter studies with larger sample sizes need to be further developed. Most cases of recollapse occur during the follow‐up period of 3 months after surgery, and more detailed follow‐up time settings can be further studied. Some chronic diseases can adversely affect bone quality, thereby increasing the risk of fracture [26]. This study did not conduct subgroup analyses for this association. We acknowledge that diabetes, chronic kidney disease, medications, and adherence to anti‐osteoporosis treatment may influence bone quality and the risk of recollapse. We also note that these variables will be included in further multivariate analyses in future extended studies. The setting of a simple elderly population has, to some extent, increased the bias caused by age variation. In addition, the setting of a fresh vertebral compression fracture with a loss of more than one‐third of the anterior edge height of the vertebral body to some extent affects the deformation ability of the reinforced vertebral body.

Conclusion

The results of this study suggest that IVC and PVP may be important factors related to inducing recollapse of previously treated vertebrae after PVA. Patients with osteoporotic compression fractures with anterior vertebral height loss greater than 1/3, especially those with IVC, should be concerned and taken seriously, and PVP should be carefully selected to prevent their clinical course from worsening.
