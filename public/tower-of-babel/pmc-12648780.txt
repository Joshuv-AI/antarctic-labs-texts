The spiritual support perceptions and attitudes towards death among intensive care nurses in Turkiye
Authors: Turkan Karaca, Suzan Havlioğlu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis study was conducted to determine the perceptions of spiritual support and attitudes towards death among intensive care nurses, who are among the health care professionals who frequently encounter death.MethodsThe study was conducted with nurses working in the intensive care units of a university hospital. Introduction

Intensive care units (ICU) are specialised units that provide the care and treatment necessary for critical groups of patients to maintain their vital functions. Recent advances in treatment methods and technology are extending human life expectancy and increasing the number of patients requiring intensive care [1]. Intensive care nurses are the group that encounters people at the end of life more frequently in intensive care units, where interventional treatments are used more frequently than in other clinics and high mortality and morbidity rates are observed [2]. Thoughts about death due to the threat to the lives of individuals hospitalised in these units, the presence of unfamiliar people and environment, bed dependency, disruptions in family processes, frequent repetition of painful interventions, and the desire to pray increase the need for spiritual support for both patients and intensive care nurses who care for them [3, 4].

Nurses who focus on the concept of care are more involved with patients and participate more in end-of-life care than other members of the healthcare team [5]. Caring for the dying is one of the most important and challenging aspects of the nursing profession [6]. While death is an event that most people do not want to talk about, think about or be close to, nurses feel death intimately, face their own realities of death and experience complex emotions while caring for the dying patient [7, 8]. Nurses caring for dying patients may experience feelings of guilt, fear, helplessness, failure, anxiety and inadequacy when they lose their patients [9]. When the literature is examined; it is stated that the characteristics of nurses such as marital status, religious belief, gender, education level, age, professional experience and loss of life may have both positive and negative effects on their attitudes towards death [2, 10].

ICU patients experience more biological, physiological and psychosocial health problems and need more spiritual care. The fact that nurses have negative attitudes towards death may lead them to distance themselves from the patient, negatively affect their communication with the patient, or ignore their psychosocial needs by focusing only on the physical care of the patient [11, 12]. Nurses with a positive perception of death have been found to have more positive attitudes towards patient care [6]. Therefore, it is important and necessary for nurses to be able to recognise and control their own perceptions and attitudes towards death, to understand the feelings of patients and their families, and to have the knowledge and skills to provide effective end-of-life care in order to help dying patients cope with death and provide better quality care.

As ICU nurses care for critically ill patients on a daily basis, it is important to explore their perceptions of spiritual support and their attitudes towards caring for dying patients. In studies conducted with ICU nurses, there are studies that examine attitudes to death and care of the dying patient separately, but there are no studies that address perceptions of spiritual support together [13–15]. With this in mind, this study was conducted to determine the perceptions of spiritual support and attitudes towards death among intensive care nurses, who are among the health care professionals who frequently encounter death. This study is both valuable and unique because it is a study that assesses attitudes towards spiritual care and care of the dying, which are thought to be related, together and examines their relationships.

With the global rise in life expectancy and the increasing demand for critical care services, understanding ICU nurses’ perceptions of spiritual support and their attitudes toward end-of-life care has become increasingly relevant on an international scale. Cultural, spiritual, and professional factors that shape end-of-life care practices differ widely across countries; therefore, the findings of this study not only contribute to the national body of knowledge but also offer meaningful insights for the global nursing and healthcare community.

It is believed that this research will be useful in understanding the impact of intensive care nurses’ attitudes towards death on the care of dying patients and may provide guidance, particularly in the field of intensive care nursing. In addition, this study will be able to identify the factors that influence the perception of spiritual support and attitudes towards the care of dying patients among ICU nurses working in our country, and will shed light on more comprehensive studies.

Materials and methods

Sample and participants

The study was conducted with nurses working in the intensive care units of a university hospital. No sampling method was used in the study and the aim was to reach all 126 nurses working in the hospital during the data collection process, 123 nurses participated in the study voluntarily. The study was conducted at University Hospital between 01 and 28 February 2025.

Data collection instruments

The Descriptive Characteristics Form, the Spiritual Support Perception Scale and the Attitudes Towards Death Scale were used to collect data.

Descriptive characteristics form

The information form, which we developed after reviewing the literature, consisted of 15 questions, including age, gender, educational status, marital status, working hours, working hours in ICU, working hours in the institution, working hours per week, satisfaction with ICU, knowledge of spirituality and spiritual care, having received spiritual care training before or after graduation, believing in the need to provide spiritual care to patients, and having lost a patient while working in ICU.

Spiritual support perception scale

The one-dimensional scale of perception of spiritual support developed by Kavas and Kavas within the sample of health professionals working as doctors, nurses and midwives is a Likert-type scale scored from ‘0 strongly disagree’ to ‘4 strongly agree’ [16]. The scale is designed to measure the perception of spiritual support among health care workers. As the total mean score of the Perception of Spiritual Support scale increases, the level of perception of spiritual support/spiritual care concepts increases positively. The highest score that can be obtained from the total scale is 60. The perception of spiritual support is scored as 0 (low), 20–40 (medium) and 41–60 (high). There is no cut-off point in scale scoring. The Cronbach Alpha value of the scale developed by Kavas and Kavas is 0.94 [16]. The scale used in this study Cronbach Alpha value was found as 0.98.

Attitude towards death scale

It was developed by Wong et al. (1994) to determine individuals’ attitudes towards death, and the adaptation study in our country was conducted by Çelik and Kav (2013) [17, 18]. Each subscale has a 7-point Likert scale and consists of a total of 26 items. The main advantage of the scale is that it can directly assess the nature and extent of acceptance of death. It consists of three sub-dimensions: acceptance of neutrality and approach, acceptance of escape and fear of death and death avoidance. Neutral acceptance and approach acceptance are explained as the belief that death is an inevitable part of life and a transition to the afterlife. Escape acceptance is the belief that death will save one from the physical or psychological harms of life; the Fear of Death and Death Avoidance sub-dimension is defined as the fear that the individual feels when approaching death and the avoidance of thinking or talking about death in order to reduce the feeling of anxiety caused by death. Higher mean scores obtained from the total and subscales indicate a positive attitude towards death. The Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale used in this study is 0.84; neutral acceptance-approach acceptance is 0.85; escape acceptance is 0.77; fear of death and death avoidance is 0.81 [17, 18].

Data collection

The research data were collected using the face-to-face interview technique, after explaining the purpose of the study to the nurses and obtaining their verbal and written consent. Data collection took an average of 15–20 min.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS 25.0). The Shapiro-Wilk test, kurtosis and skewness values were used to assess whether the data conformed to a normal distribution. The fact that the kurtosis and skewness values are within the range ‘±1.5’ is accepted as fulfilling the normal distribution conditions of the data [19]. Descriptive statistics (numbers, percentages, means), t-test in independent groups for those that fit the normal distribution, Mann-Whitney U-test and Kruskal-Wallis analysis for those that did not fit the normal distribution were used to evaluate the study data. In statistical decisions, p < 0.05 was accepted as an indicator of significant difference.

Ethical considerations

Approval for the study was obtained from the University’s Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee (2025/20). Written consent was obtained from the institution where the study was conducted. Verbal and written consent was obtained from the individuals who participated in the study. Permission to use the scale was obtained from the researchers who performed the Turkish reliability validation of the measurement tools used. In addition, after the nurses were informed about the research, their written and verbal consent was obtained voluntarily who agreed to participate in the study. It was specified that students should not write their names on the questionnaire, ensuring the anonymity of the participants. The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and publication ethics were followed in our study.

Discussion

This study’s results identified the factors that influence the perception of spiritual support and attitudes towards the care of dying patients among ICU nurses working in Türkiye.

The mean total score of the spiritual support perception scale of the nurses participating in the study was found to be 47.89 ± 9.03, in other words, at a high level. When reviewing the literature, in a study conducted by Kara and Çolak (2020) with nurses, the mean score of nurses from the spiritual support perception scale was found to be 47.89 ± 8.03 [20]. In another study conducted by Pür (2023) with nurses, the average perception of spiritual support of nurses was found to be 50.6 ± 7.4 [21]. In another study that investigated the perception of spiritual support of nurses, the average perception of spiritual support was found to be 49.5 ± 8.2 [22]. Similar to our study, although nurses’ perceptions of spiritual support were generally found to be at a high level, these results show that nurses generally feel that spiritual support is sufficient, but they feel that this perception can be improved.

The mean total score of the attitude towards death scale was found to be 99.26 ± 10.56. In the study conducted by Kaya and Özdelikara (2022) with nurses, the mean of the nurses’ attitude towards death was found to be 115.1 ± 20.2 [23]. In another study conducted by Çelik (2019) with nurses, the mean of nurses’ attitudes towards death was found to be 97.1 ± 9.3 [24]. Scores on the nurses’ attitude towards death scale generally range between 90 and 115 points in the studies. This shows that nurses generally have a moderate attitude towards death.

Nurses who participated in the study and have knowledge of spirituality and spiritual care, those who are satisfied with their working unit, and those who believe in the necessity of providing spiritual care to patients, were found to have higher scores in spiritual support perception. In a study conducted by Tambağ et al. (2018) with nurses working in intensive care units, similar to our study, a statistically significant difference was found between nurses’ satisfaction with the intensive care units they worked in and the total mean scores of perceived spiritual support [25]. In the literature, there is a wide range of variables in studies investigating the factors associated with nurses’ perceptions of spiritual support. Yılmaz et al. (2019) found that female nurses’ perceptions of spiritual support were higher than those of male nurses. In addition, age and marital status were also reported to be among the factors affecting this perception [26]. Another study found that the perception of spiritual support was stronger among nurses with master’s and advanced education [22]. Perceptions of spiritual support among nurses working in intensive care units were found to be associated with stress in working conditions. Nurses’ perceptions of spiritual support are influenced by many internal and external factors. It can be said that working conditions, level of education, social support and gender are the main factors associated with this perception.

A statistically significant difference was found between the age of nurses and the mean scores of the fear of death and death avoidance sub-dimensions. Similar to our study, Mermer and Özçelik (2022) also found a statistically significant difference between nurses’ age and the sub-dimensions of fear of death and death avoidance [27]. In some further analyses, contrary to our study, fear of death and death avoidance attitudes were found to decrease with increasing age (Karahan & Tan, 2021; Çelik & Doğan, 2020) [10, 24]. Based on these findings, it can be assumed that nurses encounter death more frequently with increasing age and professional experience, and that older nurses generally have a more accepting understanding of death and are better able to cope with death as a result of their professional experience.

No relationship was found between nurses’ scores on the spiritual support perception scale and their scores on the attitude towards death scale. When the literature was reviewed, the results of the studies conducted were similar to our research [28–30]. This can be explained by the fact that the perception of spiritual support is more related to emotional and psychological coping, empathy and emotional healing, whereas attitudes towards death are more related to personal experience, age and religious beliefs. This means that both scales address different psychological processes and therefore may develop independently.

A positive, weakly significant relationship was found between the score on the Spiritual Support Perception Scale and the score on the Neutral Acceptance sub-dimension. In Aras and Erkoç’s (2024) study, which examined nurses’ perceptions of spiritual support, a weak positive relationship was found between the perception of spiritual support and the neutral acceptance sub-dimension, similar to our study [15]. The finding of a positive, weakly significant relationship between the perception of spiritual support and the neutral acceptance sub-dimension may suggest that nurses see the psychological relief provided by spiritual support as a step towards accepting death as a natural process. This can help carers to cope with death in a healthier way. Spiritual support can improve the emotional and psychological process of dying, leading to a more accepting, neutral attitude.

Study limitations

The data in this study were obtained from nurses only in one university hospital in Turkey, which limits the generalization of the findings. Future studies need to be conducted with larger samples selected randomly from several institutions and locations to ensure representativeness.

Conclusion

Our study shows that there is no direct relationship between nurses’ perceptions of spiritual support and their attitudes towards death. In other words, nurses’ perceptions of spiritual support do not have a significant effect on their general attitudes towards death. However, on closer analysis, there was a positive but weakly significant relationship between the perception of spiritual support and the sub-dimension of ‘neutral acceptance’ of death. This shows that nurses’ perception of spiritual support has a limited relationship with their attitude of neutral acceptance of death, and the strength of this relationship is low.

In light of these findings, it is important to emphasise that nurses provide spiritual support in the care they provide to their patients. In addition, programmes can be created in which nurses can receive psychological support or benefit from spiritual support, taking into account their own spiritual needs. Environments where nurses can find spiritual and psychological support in the workplace can help them both to reduce work stress and to develop healthier attitudes towards death and other challenging situations.
