Geospatial disparities in emergency medical utilization and neighborhood-level socioeconomic status in Yamato City, Japan
Authors: Hiroaki Iwasaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveNeighborhood-level socioeconomic disparities are becoming more apparent in Japan; however, their relationship with emergency medical utilization remains unclear.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted in Yamato City, a suburban municipality near Tokyo. The city was divided into nine daily living districts based on the jurisdictions of Regional Comprehensive Support Centers. Demographic and socioeconomic indicators were obtained from municipal statistics for June 2025 and the 2020 national census. Data on 1199 ambulance transports, including 953 city residents, during secondary emergency duty from April 2024 to March 2026, were extracted from our hospital's medical records.ResultsThe proportions of residents aged ≥65 and ≥75 years, nursing care certification rate, welfare recipient rate, ambulance transport rate, emergency hospitalization rate, and areal deprivation index (ADI) showed a consistent north–south geographic gradient, with higher values in the southern area. Both ambulance transport and emergency hospitalization rates were positively correlated with ADI across the nine districts. The southern area had the highest ambulance transport rate for fall-related injuries, particularly among women.ConclusionsNeighborhood-level socioeconomic disadvantage was associated with ambulance transport and emergency hospitalization in a Japanese suburban city. Tailored public health strategies may help optimize the emergency medical care system in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Ambulance transport, Emergency hospitalization, Social determinants of health, Areal deprivation index, Neighborhood, Health disparities

Introduction

Social determinants of health (SDoH) can be broadly categorized into individual- and community-level factors (Marmot et al., 2008). Individual-level factors include personal socioeconomic characteristics such as sex, ethnicity, education, income, and housing status, as well as social relationships with family, friends, and the broader community (Diez Roux and Mair, 2010). Community-level factors include neighborhood characteristics such as safety, cleanliness, walkability, access to public transportation, green spaces, recreational and commercial facilities, and local unemployment and poverty rates (Diez Roux and Mair, 2010; Chamberlain et al., 2020). The areal deprivation index (ADI), a composite measure of neighborhood socioeconomic disadvantage based on multiple SDoH indicators (Nakaya, 2011), is independently associated with health behaviors (Hanibuchi and Nakaya, 2020), disease incidence, clinical outcomes (Honjo et al., 2015; Koohsari et al., 2020), and mortality in Japan (Nakaya et al., 2014; Kurotani et al., 2019).

In Japan, emergency medical care is broadly organized into three levels (Kato et al., 2019). Primary emergency care generally provides initial assessment and treatment for patients with relatively mild conditions who do not require hospitalization or surgery. Secondary emergency care provides emergency evaluation and treatment, including hospitalization and surgery when required, for patients with more severe conditions. Tertiary emergency care is provided for critically ill or injured patients who require advanced and specialized care beyond the capacity of secondary emergency care. In the Japanese healthcare system, designated secondary emergency hospitals commonly provide secondary emergency care. Because individual hospitals, particularly small- and medium-sized hospitals, cannot provide secondary emergency care continuously 24 h a day, 365 days a year due to staffing and other resource limitations, many communities use a rotating-duty system involving multiple secondary emergency hospitals. Under this system, one of these hospitals assumes responsibility for secondary emergency care, including receiving ambulance-transported patients, during designated night-time and holiday periods.

Ambulance services in Japan are publicly funded and provided continuously by local governments, and can generally be accessed free of charge by dialing at any time. According to national statistics, ambulance transports increased by 13.2% between 2019 and 2024, reaching a record high of 6,764,838 in 2024 (Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, 2025). Older adults aged ≥65 years accounted for 63.3% of all ambulance transports in 2024. As demand for ambulance transport continues to rise, sustaining the already strained emergency medical care system has become an urgent public health challenge. Previous studies have demonstrated that a neighborhood's socioeconomic status is associated with patterns of healthcare utilization, including emergency department visits, even in countries with universal healthcare systems (Zhang et al., 2020; Strum et al., 2024).

In Japan, socioeconomic polarization among urban neighborhoods has intensified since the 1980s (Toyoda, 2007), potentially leading to widening health disparities within metropolitan areas. Although municipalities plan and deliver the regional emergency medical care systems, it remains largely unknown whether neighborhood-level socioeconomic disadvantage is associated with geographic variation in emergency medical utilization within a single municipality. This single-center observational study examined ambulance-transported patients to one secondary emergency hospital during its designated night-time duty and aimed to investigate the association between neighborhood-level socioeconomic characteristics and emergency medical utilization in a suburban city within the Tokyo metropolitan area. A better understanding of these relationships may facilitate more effective neighborhood-level public health interventions and healthcare resource planning tailored to local community characteristics, thereby helping reduce avoidable emergency care demand and support a sustainable emergency medical care system.

Methods

Study design and population

The study was conducted in Yamato City, which covers 27.09 km2 and is geographically elongated, extending approximately 9.79 km from north to south and 3.22 km from east to west. Yamato City is located near the center of Kanagawa Prefecture, approximately 40 km from central Tokyo (Fig. 1A and B). Although Yamato City is the fifth-smallest municipality by area among cities in Kanagawa Prefecture, it has one of the highest population densities in the prefecture, ranking second. A total of nine daily living districts were established in Yamato City based on the administrative jurisdictions of the Regional Comprehensive Support Centers: Shimotsuruma/Tukimino, Chuo-Rinkan, Minami-Rinkan, Tsuruma, Fukami/Yamato, Kamisoyagi/Chuo, Sakuragaoka/Wada, Fukuda-Kita, and Fukuda-Minami (Fig. 1C and D). The city was further categorized into northern, central, and southern areas according to the distribution of these nine districts (Fig. 1D).

Demographic and socioeconomic data on neighborhoods as of June 1, 2025 were derived from the Yamato City Statistics Bureau (Yamato City Office, 2025). Variables included total population, average household size, the proportions of residents aged ≥20, ≥65, and ≥75 years, the nursing care certification rate, and the welfare recipient rate for each district. ADI was calculated using data from the 2020 national census obtained from the official Japanese Government Statistics site, e-Stat (Japanese Government Statistics, 2024). Yamato City had a total population of 245,901. The populations of the nine districts, in order of district number, were 41,374, 31,179, 29,254, 22,407, 29,581, 33,175, 22,200, 13,989, and 22,742 residents (Fig. 1E). The southern area has limited resident turnover; therefore, the decline in total population has become more pronounced recently (Yamato City Office, 2026).

Yamato City has five secondary emergency hospitals that provide secondary emergency care on a rotational basis during designated night-time periods (17:00–08:00) and continuously 24 h a day on holidays (Fig. 1C). On designated secondary emergency duty days, the duty hospital functions as the receiving facility for secondary emergency patients from across the city, rather than exclusively for residents of the immediate local area. During the period when our hospital was responsible for secondary emergency duty for patients aged ≥18 years, from April 1, 2024, to March 31, 2026, 1199 patients were transported to our emergency department by ambulance. The analysis included 953 patients residing in Yamato City, whereas 246 patients residing outside the city were excluded. Ambulance crews may provide on-site assessment and treatment, and some ambulance calls may not result in hospital transport. This study excluded ambulance calls that resulted in non-transport or transport to other hospitals. Hospitalization was defined as emergency admission to our hospital or transfer to a higher-level medical facility. Non-hospitalization was defined as completion of emergency department evaluation and treatment followed by discharge home or return to a residential or care facility without hospital admission or transfer. Individual-level variables extracted from electronic medical records included age, sex, residential address, nationality, household size, housing type, health insurance status and details, welfare recipient status, and the presence of physical or psychiatric disabilities.

Measures

The proportions of residents aged ≥65 years and ≥75 years, and the welfare recipient rate were calculated using the total population as the denominator. The nursing care certification rate was calculated using the population aged ≥65 years as the denominator, whereas the ambulance transport rate and emergency hospitalization rate were calculated using the population aged ≥20 years as the denominator because of the age-group structure of the available municipal population data. During the study period, 9 of the 953 Yamato City residents included in the ambulance-transported study population were aged 18–19 years, indicating that the proportion of residents aged 18–19 years was small in the study population. The variables were represented using six color-coded categories and spatially visualized using a descriptive mapping approach to show relative differences in each indicator's magnitude and to assess regional trends and relationships among variables within the city.

Statistical analysis

The relationships between neighborhood socioeconomic characteristics and emergency medical utilization were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (rs). Statistical analyses were conducted using Microsoft R Open version 3.5.3 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and EZR software (Kanda, 2013). A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Calculation of gravity centroids

The population centroid was calculated using the population of each district as the weighting factor and therefore represents the population-weighted geographic center of Yamato City. Gravity centroids for other variables, including socioeconomic indicators and emergency medical utilization, were calculated by weighting the geographic coordinates of each district by the corresponding value of each variable. Polygon data for Yamato City was obtained from the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan's open data on the Geographic Information System (GIS) (https://www.gsi.go.jp/gis.html). The geographic data were linked to the nine Regional Comprehensive Support Centers located throughout the city (https://www.city.yamato.lg.jp/gyosei/shiseijoho/tokeijoho/index.html). Each district's attribute data were integrated into polygon shapefiles. Spatial analyses were conducted using QGIS version 3.40 LTR (https://www.qgis.org). The spatial displacement between the population centroid and the gravity centroid of each indicator was used as a descriptive measure of the spatial distribution of that indicator relative to the underlying population distribution. This approach summarizes the direction and magnitude of spatial concentration or displacement that may not be apparent from comparisons of district-level rates alone.

Disease definitions

A review of electronic medical records was conducted to identify major symptoms, diseases, and injuries that warrant ambulance transport, emergency hospitalization, and transfer to tertiary care centers. The diagnoses were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes (https://www.byomei.org/Scripts/search/index_search.asp).

Results

Socioeconomic characteristics of the nine districts in Yamato City

The citywide average household size, proportions of residents aged ≥65 and ≥75 years, nursing care certification rate, and welfare recipient rate were 2.06, 24.6%, 14.7%, 19.5%, and 12.2‰, respectively (Figs. 2A–E). Household size did not show a consistent geographic trend across the three areas (Fig. 2A). In contrast, the proportions of residents aged ≥65 and ≥75 years, nursing care certification rate, and welfare recipient rate showed similar geographic distributions, with generally higher values in the southern area, lower values in the northern area, and intermediate values in the central area (Figs. 2B–E). Relative to the population centroid, the gravity centroids of the proportions of residents aged ≥65 and ≥75 years, nursing care certification rate, and welfare recipient rate were displaced southward by 0.94 km, 1.12 km, 1.10 km, and 1.50 km, respectively (Fig. 2F).

Emergency medical utilization and socioeconomic disadvantage across the nine districts in Yamato City

During the two-year study period, our hospital provided secondary emergency care for Yamato City on 104 designated duty days. The citywide average ambulance transport rate and emergency hospitalization rate were 19.5 and 5.91 cases per 10,000 residents aged ≥20 years, respectively (Fig. 3A and B). Both rates were higher in the southern area than in the central and northern areas. The ADI was positively correlated with the welfare recipient rate across the nine districts (rs = 0.766, P = 0.021) (Fig. 3C) and showed a geographic distribution similar to those of the ambulance transport rate and emergency hospitalization rate (Fig. 3D). Similarly, the gravity centroids for the ambulance transport rate, emergency hospitalization rate, and ADI were displaced southward from the population centroid by 0.59 km, 0.59 km, and 0.85 km, respectively (Fig. 3E).

Associations between socioeconomic characteristics and emergency medical utilization across the nine districts in Yamato City

No statistically significant correlation was observed between the proportion of residents aged ≥65 years and the ambulance transport rate (rs = 0.677, P = 0.059) (Fig. 4A). In contrast, the ADI was positively correlated with the ambulance transport rate (rs = 0.733, P = 0.031) (Fig. 4B). The proportion of residents aged ≥65 years was positively correlated with the emergency hospitalization rate (rs = 0.833, P = 0.003) (Fig. 4C), whereas the ADI showed a stronger positive correlation with the emergency hospitalization rate (rs = 0.911, P = 0.001) (Fig. 4D).

Socioeconomic characteristics, living arrangements, and residence types of patients requiring emergency medical care in Yamato City

Among ambulance-transported patients in the three areas of Yamato City, the proportion requiring emergency hospitalization was 37.4% in the southern area, compared with 25.2% in the northern area and 26.4% in the central area (Fig. 6A). Among patients requiring emergency hospitalization, the proportion living in single-person households was 19.7% in the southern area, compared with 12.6% in the northern area and 17.3% in the central area (Fig. 6B). Among ambulance-transported patients in the southern area, 18.3% were residents of public housing (Fig. 6C). Among patients requiring emergency hospitalization in the southern area, 16.8% were residents of public housing. Furthermore, among ambulance-transported patients residing in the southern area, public housing residents accounted for 52.2% of welfare recipients, 23.5% of those with physical or mental disabilities, and 55.6% of foreign nationals (Fig. 6D).

Discussion

In this study, neighborhood-level socioeconomic disadvantage, as measured by ADI, showed stronger associations with ambulance transport and emergency hospitalization rates than the proportion of residents aged ≥65 years alone. The gravity centroids of the ambulance transport, emergency hospitalization, and ADI were displaced approximately 0.6–0.9 km southward from the population centroid. Within Yamato City, a relatively small, densely populated municipality, these spatial displacements provide a concise and intuitive description of geographic differences in emergency medical utilization and socioeconomic disadvantage.

Several mechanisms may explain the association between socioeconomic disadvantage and increased emergency medical utilization. First, economic barriers and time constraints may reduce access to preventive healthcare and regular medical follow-up (McMaughan et al., 2020; Chapman et al., 2022). The southern area of Yamato City had a higher proportion of blue-collar workers (30.6%) than the northern and central areas (19.9% and 25.8%, respectively), potentially reflecting financial constraints and inflexible working conditions. Second, physical and geographic barriers to healthcare access may delay medical consultation. Japan's universal healthcare and support systems for older adults reduce out-of-pocket healthcare costs, regardless of income. Nevertheless, older residents who live at a considerable distance from medical facilities may experience transportation difficulties when seeking routine outpatient care. Third, social isolation and reduced social capital may also contribute to increased emergency medical utilization. Older adults living in socioeconomically disadvantaged neighborhoods are more likely to suffer from social isolation, loneliness, and have poorer subjective health (Nakaya and Hanibuchi, 2013). These psychosocial vulnerabilities may affect healthcare-seeking behavior during the early stages or acute exacerbation of illness, leading to greater reliance on emergency care (Zhang et al., 2020; Strum et al., 2024; Asano et al., 2024).

Socioeconomic disadvantage is associated with a distinct pattern of disease susceptibility, with women having a high prevalence of nonfatal but disabling diseases, while men are more likely to suffer from fatal and chronic diseases related to mortality (Pongiglione et al., 2015; Luy and Minagawa, 2014). The incidence of musculoskeletal disorders and fractures tends to be higher among women (Santosa et al., 2016; Moreno et al., 2018). Consistent with these reports, the disease-specific analyses highlighted a higher frequency of emergency medical utilization due to fall-related injuries, particularly among women living in the southern area. Consequently, they may benefit from targeted interventions such as fall-prevention programs, balance and lower extremity strength training, osteoporosis prevention, and environmental modifications. The southern area also had higher emergency medical utilization for circulatory and respiratory diseases among both men and women. Neighborhood socioeconomic disadvantage has been linked to poor food environments, physical inactivity, smoking, and excessive alcohol consumption, thereby increasing the burden of cardiovascular and pulmonary risks (Diez Roux and Mair, 2010; Chamberlain et al., 2020). Promoting healthy lifestyles, expanding access to regular health checkups, and ensuring the early detection and management of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia may reduce the incidence of circulatory and respiratory diseases in these communities.

Although non-hospitalization does not necessarily imply inappropriate ambulance use, approximately two-thirds of patients transported by ambulance did not require emergency hospitalization. A previous study from Japan indicates that more than half of ambulance-transported patients present with mild symptoms, raising concerns that this may delay care delivery to less than 10% of patients with severe or very serious conditions (Nakao et al., 2020). Older age, living alone, prior ambulance use, and limited access to transportation may be associated with increased non-urgent ambulance requests (Kawakami et al., 2007). In this study, single-person households did not constitute a greater proportion of ambulance-transported patients in the southern area. Furthermore, the southern area also had a substantially higher rate of emergency hospitalization, indicating that increased emergency medical utilization in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas cannot be attributed solely to non-urgent use.

The findings regarding public housing in the southern area are particularly noteworthy. In Japan's metropolitan suburban areas, many large public rental housing complexes were built during Japan's rapid economic growth in the 1960s and 1970s. Public housing allocation policies prioritize populations, such as older adults, low-income individuals, welfare recipients, and persons with physical or mental disabilities (Kanagawa Prefectural Government, 2024), potentially creating a “housing trap” in which socioeconomic disadvantage and health vulnerability become spatially concentrated (Nakaya and Hanibuchi, 2013). Recent statistics show that foreign residents account for approximately 20% of Yamato City's prefectural housing population (Kanagawa Prefectural Government, 2024). Public health interventions targeting specific features of the environments surrounding public housing complexes may be an important strategy for reducing emergency medical utilization.

Our findings suggest that emergency healthcare planning should integrate neighborhood-level socioeconomic data alongside conventional demographic indicators. Local governments organize primary, secondary, and tertiary healthcare services (Kato et al., 2019). Free access is a feature of the Japanese healthcare system, allowing patients to choose any healthcare facility regardless of disease severity or insurance status. A majority of emergency care referrals are to hospital-based outpatient and inpatient care, and physicians in these hospitals are required to provide both primary and secondary care, including emergency care (Kato et al., 2019). Recently, concerns have been raised about the fragility of the secondary emergency medical care system due to shortages of human and material resources, particularly in small and medium-sized hospitals that provide secondary emergency care (Ii and Watanabe, 2022; Sera et al., 2023). As of 2025, Yamato City's five secondary emergency hospitals have 109, 403, 207, 45, and 100 beds, respectively; four of these correspond to this scale category. Patients are less likely to request an ambulance when they are aware of their local primary care provider (Kawakami et al., 2007). Establishing an integrated network that encompasses hospitals, clinics, preventive healthcare providers, and community support systems—tailored to distinct local socioeconomic characteristics—is essential to curbing emergency care and safeguarding the sustainability of the healthcare system (Kato et al., 2019).

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the association between neighborhood socioeconomic disadvantage and emergency medical utilization. The survey did not account for district-level demographics, including changes in the population's age structure or people moving in and out. Second, electronic medical records lacked information on individual-level socioeconomic variables, such as educational attainment, income, and employment status. Residual confounding by unmeasured individual socioeconomic characteristics cannot be excluded. Third, a hospital in the city specializes in treating cardiovascular disorders, such as acute coronary syndromes and arrhythmias, which may be affecting emergency medical utilization related to circulatory diseases. Finally, this was a single-center study, and our hospital's location in the southern part of the city may have influenced the findings. The study focused only on ambulance-transported patients during the nighttime hours (17:00–08:00) on the city's designated secondary emergency duty days, when emergency patients were concentrated across the city. Future multicenter longitudinal studies incorporating both individual- and neighborhood-level socioeconomic data are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, neighborhood-level socioeconomic disadvantage was associated with emergency medical utilization within a Japanese suburban municipality. Districts with greater socioeconomic deprivation experienced disproportionately higher rates of ambulance transport and emergency hospitalization, particularly for circulatory and respiratory diseases, as well as fall-related injuries. Integrating neighborhood-level socioeconomic indicators into emergency healthcare planning could facilitate tailored preventive interventions, optimize resource allocation, and enhance the sustainability of the emergency medical care system.
