Current advancements and future research directions in the green synthesis and applications of nanoparticles
Authors: Riyadh Abdulmalek Hassan, Waheeba Ahmed Al-Amrani, Abduelwhab B. Alwany, Jamil M. A. S. Obaid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid expansion of research on green-synthesized nanoparticles (GSNPs), particularly those produced through biological processes, has generated a highly fragmented literature that obscures structural trends and translational gaps. This study conducts a comprehensive bibliometric analysis of publications indexed in Scopus between 2020 and 2025 to map the intellectual landscape of GSNPs and identify priority directions for future research. The findings demonstrate that silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) dominate the field, driven by their established antibacterial effectiveness and broad biomedical and environmental utility. At the same time, plant extracts remain the primary biosynthetic resources, while alternative biological sources, including bacteria and fungi, are still inadequately investigated. The analysis also highlights the limited use of high-resolution characterization tools, including field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), atomic force microscopy (AFM), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), compared to the prevalent conventional techniques, such as X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), revealing a persistent characterization gap at the nano–bio interface. The geographic analysis highlights Asia, especially India and China, as leading contributors, supported by dense international collaboration networks. Conceptually, the study clarifies green synthesis within the 12 principles of green chemistry, integrating biological, physical, and chemical methods and proposing a taxonomy that resolves the frequent conflation of green synthesis with biosynthesis alone. Finally, by linking bibliometric patterns to toxicity and regulatory keywords, the analysis exposes key barriers to commercialization. It outlines research strategies to advance GSNPs toward scalable and regulation-ready nanotechnology platforms.Graphical abstract

Introduction

The growing demand for the production of nontoxic nanoparticles (NPs) has led to the development of more environmentally friendly synthesis methods. Traditional chemical and physical techniques often produce toxic waste, require extreme reaction conditions, and incur high reagent costs, making them ecologically hazardous. In contrast, biosynthesis leverages the capabilities of plants, bacteria, fungi, and algae to reduce and stabilize NPs at room temperature, employing fewer harmful compounds with low ecotoxicity [1].

Moreover, researchers have broadened the definition of green synthetic strategies to encompass low-toxicity physical and chemical methods, such as ion sputtering and microwave-assisted synthesis [2, 3]. Another key principle of green chemistry involves utilizing recycled electronic waste materials as raw inputs, which promotes sustainable, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly advancements [4]. This shift towards sustainability has catalyzed a notable increase in research output, a trend quantitatively demonstrated by our bibliometric analysis of Scopus data (Fig. 1). NPs are versatile materials with a wide range of applications in medicine, agriculture, and environmental science, primarily due to their unique physicochemical properties. Their high surface area-to-mass ratios enhance both reactivity and functionality. In the healthcare sector, NPs are used for wound healing [5], dental treatment [6], and the management of periodontal disease [7], primarily due to their antimicrobial activity against resistant pathogens [8]. Beyond medical applications, they play roles in biosensing [9], water purification [10], and the degradation of photochemical waste [11], harnessing their antioxidant and phyto-reductive capabilities [12, 13].

Key characteristics, including size, shape, surface chemistry, and morphology, influence the effectiveness of NPs [14]. For instance, metallic nanoparticles (MNPs), such as Ag NPs, gold (Au NPs), and copper (Cu NPs), exhibit surface plasmon resonance (SPR), enabling tunable optical properties. Magnetic NPs, including iron oxide (Fe3O4) and cobalt, as well as their alloys (e.g., Fe-Pt, Co-Pt3), display superparamagnetic behavior, making them advantageous for imaging, sensing, and targeted therapy applications [15–21]. Furthermore, noble MNPs have attracted significant research interest due to their SPR and surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) effects, making them highly promising across various research fields [22].

Despite the notable advantages, several limitations complicate the scaling up of green synthesis methods. Foremost among these are the challenges associated with large-scale production and the long-term stability of NPs. Furthermore, concerns regarding the safety of NPs for both human health and the environment remain. Addressing these issues necessitates thorough safety evaluations and the development of standardized regulatory frameworks [23].

In recent years, literature on GSNPs, particularly those produced by biosynthesis, has grown substantially, with numerous reviews summarizing advances in GSNP routes and specific application domains [24–26]. Existing work has typically focused on either a particular biological agent (e.g., microorganisms, bacteriophages, algae, or plant extracts) [27–32] or individual NP types, such as Ag, Au, or ZnO NPs [33–36]. On the other hand, bibliometric reviews have focused on specific types of NPs, such as Ag, Au, and magnetite [37–39], as well as on their use in particular applications, including nanocatalyst, water purification, cancer treatment, musculoskeletal diseases, and sustainable agriculture [40–44]. While these reviews provide valuable descriptive overviews, they remain fragmented across separate NP classes and narrow application areas and lack a quantitative, network-based view of how themes, methods, and applications co-evolve.

To date, no study has systematically mapped the GSNP field using bibliometric network analysis to capture, in a single framework, the diversity of synthesis strategies, the maturity of application domains, and the translational bottlenecks that limit commercialization. This work addresses that gap by applying VOSviewer-based co-occurrence and co-authorship analysis to GSNP literature from 2020 to 2025, thereby revealing structural patterns that are not accessible through narrative reviews alone.

Bibliometric analysis framework

The bibliometric data were retrieved from the Scopus database in April 2025. Scopus was selected as the primary data source due to its extensive multidisciplinary coverage, rigorous indexing standards, and strong representation of peer-reviewed literature across nanotechnology, materials science, environmental science, and biotechnology. It provides comprehensive citation metadata and analytical tools that are well-suited for mapping research trends and scientific networks in emerging interdisciplinary fields such as GSNPs.

Nevertheless, reliance on a single database may introduce limitations, including the potential exclusion of relevant publications indexed in other sources, such as Web of Science or PubMed. Despite this limitation, Scopus is widely recognized as a reliable and comprehensive platform for bibliometric analysis, offering robust, representative coverage of the global research landscape.

An initial search using the keyword string (green synthesis OR biological synthesis OR biosynthesis) AND nanoparticle yielded 30,676 documents. To focus on application-oriented studies, the search was refined to include the keyword application, yielding 11,124 records.

A systematic screening process was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) framework (Fig. 2). All retrieved records were first exported, and duplicate entries were removed. A two-stage screening procedure was then applied: (i) title and abstract screening to exclude irrelevant studies, and (ii) full-text assessment to confirm eligibility based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies not directly related to green synthesis approaches, non-English publications, and conference papers were excluded. This process ensured the inclusion of high-quality and relevant studies, thereby enhancing the reliability and validity of the bibliometric analysis.Fig. 2PRISMA 2020 flow diagram illustrating the identification, screening, and selection of research articles on GSNPs. The process yielded a final dataset of 8,853 documents for bibliometric visualization

Co-occurrence maps of author keywords, co-authorship networks, and country collaboration networks were generated to visualize relationships among key entities, including topics, authors, institutions, and countries. In these maps, item size represents occurrence frequency (e.g., keyword frequency or publication count), while link strength reflects the intensity of relationships (e.g., co-occurrence or co-authorship). Total link strength (TLS) was used as a centrality metric to indicate the importance of items within the network structure [45]. This approach enables the identification of major research themes, emerging clusters, influential contributors, and global collaboration patterns in GSNP research.

Mapping and intellectual structure

Bibliometric network visualization provides a robust approach for examining the structural organization and developmental trajectory of a research field. In this study, co-occurrence analysis of author keywords was performed to map the intellectual landscape of GSNP research.

In this context, the link attribute represents the number of unique connections a keyword shares with other terms, reflecting the breadth of its conceptual associations. In contrast, TLS quantifies the cumulative strength of these connections and serves as an indicator of thematic centrality and influence. Keywords with high TLS values typically function as conceptual hubs, linking multiple research domains and shaping scientific discourse.

This bibliometric framework enables a systematic understanding of how research themes interact, evolve, and cluster within the GSNP domain. Visualization maps derived from keyword co-occurrence analysis, therefore, provide insight into both dominant research areas and emerging scientific directions.

The keyword clusters generated using the VOSviewer algorithm represent groups of frequently co-occurring terms corresponding to closely related research themes. These clusters were further interpreted at a higher conceptual level to provide a clearer understanding of the field’s major research directions. By analyzing dominant keywords and their semantic relationships, the clusters were synthesized into broader thematic domains, including synthesis methodologies, biomedical applications, environmental remediation, and agricultural applications. Conceptual clarification of green synthesis

The concept of green synthesis of NPs is articulated through two predominant perspectives in the scientific literature. The first perspective restricts green synthesis to biological methods, in which organisms such as plants and microorganisms act as reducing and stabilizing agents [51–53].

Conversely, the second, broader perspective encompasses not only biological approaches but also chemical, physical, and hybrid synthetic routes that align with the 12 principles of green chemistry proposed by Anastas and Warner [54–57]. This study adopts this broader perspective to move beyond simple biosynthesis and establish a more rigorous conceptual framework. The taxonomy presented in Fig. 5 is anchored in the selective application of the 12 principles of green chemistry to specific synthetic trajectories. In this framework, the greenness of a method is evaluated based on its adherence to key principles. Waste prevention (P1) and atom economy (P2) are primarily associated with bottom-up molecular assembly, whereas safer solvents (P5) and energy efficiency (P6) define the sustainability of top-down physical routes. Central to this framework are the principles of design for safer chemicals (P4) and inherently safer chemistry (P12), which are enabled by renewable feedstocks (P7) and the formation of biogenic surface coronas. By directly mapping these principles (P1–P12) onto different synthesis pathways, the framework provides a standardized basis for evaluating the environmental and safety performance of NP synthesis strategies.

Top-Down Green Routes: Techniques such as pulsed laser ablation in liquids (PLAL) exemplify this approach by producing Au and Ag NPs without the need for stabilizing agents. This ligand-free process is inherently environmentally friendly [58–60] and has been extended to TiO2, Pd, and silica-coated Au NPs [61–64]. These methods primarily align with Principle 5 (safer solvents) and Principle 6 (energy efficiency).

Bottom-Up Green Routes: This approach utilizes green chemical reducers or biological nanofactories (e.g., plants and microorganisms) to initiate nucleation [60]. For example, ascorbic acid can act as a dual reducing and stabilizing agent, enabling one-step synthesis at room temperature within seconds [65]. Similarly, elemental hydrogen gas represents a zero-waste chemical alternative [66]. These routes emphasize Principle 2 (atom economy) and Principle 7 (renewable feedstocks).

In plant-mediated bottom-up synthesis of MNPs (Fig. 6), phytochemicals derived from plant extracts serve as both reducing agents and stabilizing ligands, enabling a one-pot, environmentally benign process. Two complementary mechanisms govern this synthesis: (i) redox-mediated electron transfer and (ii) coordination-driven ligand-assisted reduction.

In the redox pathway, bioactive compounds such as polyphenols and flavonoids undergo oxidation, donating electrons that reduce metal ions (Mn+) to their zero-valent state (M⁰), thereby initiating nucleation. In parallel, functional groups such as hydroxyl (–OH) and carboxyl (–COOH) moieties coordinate to metal ions, forming transient metal–biomolecule complexes. This interaction facilitates localized electron transfer and provides spatial control over nucleation and crystal growth [51, 67, 68].

Comparison between green and chemical np synthesis

A critical comparison between green and conventional NP synthesis highlights a fundamental trade-off between process control and environmental sustainability. Conventional methods provide superior control over particle size, morphology, and crystallinity due to well-defined reaction conditions [69]. This level of control ensures high reproducibility and scalability, which are essential for industrial production [70].

However, these methods often rely on hazardous reducing agents, require significant energy input, and involve multi-step processing. These factors raise concerns regarding environmental impact, operational cost, and residual toxicity.

In contrast, green synthesis employs plant-derived phytochemicals as both reducing and stabilizing agents, enabling a one-pot, low-energy, and environmentally benign process. This approach improves biocompatibility and eliminates the need for toxic reagents [71]. Consequently, green synthesis is strongly associated with biomedical and sustainability-oriented applications within bibliometric networks [72].

Nevertheless, these advantages are offset by challenges in reproducibility, batch-to-batch variability, and NP uniformity. These limitations primarily arise from the complex and variable composition of biological extracts [73, 74]. Bibliometric evidence further indicates that, although biosynthesis dominates early-stage experimental studies, it shows limited association with large-scale production and industrial applications, reflecting lower Technology Readiness Levels (TRLs).

Synergistic modalities: hybrid green methods

Hybrid green methods represent an emerging and promising direction in sustainable nanotechnology, integrating biological extracts with defined green chemical precursors or physical energy inputs.

This hybridization introduces a dual-action mechanism for NP stabilization. In such systems, biological agents (e.g., polyphenols) act as primary capping agents, while green chemical additives (e.g., glucose, citric acid) function as kinetic accelerators. This synergy, often referred to as the biogenic–chemical interface, enables directed nucleation (P3) and improves monodispersity and batch-to-batch reproducibility, which are typically limited in purely biosynthetic approaches.

Furthermore, bio-physical hybrid systems employ microwave-assisted or sonochemical techniques to facilitate reduction under mild conditions. These approaches can significantly reduce the overall environmental impact of the process (P9). In practice, combining plant extracts with microwave or ultrasound energy has been shown to shorten reaction times, narrow particle size distributions, and improve reproducibility compared to purely biological synthesis routes.

Within the bibliometric network, keywords such as microwave synthesis, ultrasound-assisted synthesis, and laser ablation exhibit moderate occurrence and TLS values. This indicates that hybrid strategies are gaining attention but have not yet reached mainstream adoption in GSNP research.

Terminology and occurrence of GSNPs

Bibliometric analyses reveal that green synthesis remains the dominant term in the literature, with 2317 occurrences (TLS 4702), reflecting its broad conceptual scope. In comparison, biosynthesis (453 occurrences, TLS: 930) highlights the continued emphasis on biologically driven approaches. Broader terms such as green chemistry (173 occurrences, TLS: 282) appear less frequently but remain essential for framing environmentally responsible synthesis strategies. Additional terms, including green process and sustainable process, are also reported, although at lower frequencies. Collectively, these terms fall under the overarching concept of green synthesis [78].

These terminologies are grounded in the 12 principles of green chemistry, which promote safer, more efficient, and environmentally responsible pathways for NP synthesis [79, 80].

More specialized terms, such as biogenic synthesis (37 occurrences, TLS: 81), biological synthesis (23 occurrences, TLS: 47), and biomimetic synthesis (5 occurrences, TLS: 4), represent niche subdomains focused on nature-inspired fabrication strategies. The emerging concept of sustainable synthesis (7 occurrences, TLS: 14) further extends this framework by emphasizing the reduction of hazardous materials and waste generation, thereby reinforcing alignment with green chemistry principles [81].

The co-occurrence network visualization (Fig. 3) also highlights the most actively explored green physical and chemical synthesis platforms. Among these, microwave-assisted synthesis (22 occurrences, TLS: 63) is increasingly utilized due to its rapid, uniform heating, which facilitates efficient NP formation with reduced energy consumption [82, 83]. Laser ablation (17 occurrences, TLS: 34) is another promising technique for generating NPs via high-intensity laser pulses in liquid media. Ultrasound-assisted synthesis (14 occurrences, TLS: 29) has similarly gained attention as a green approach for NP production.

In addition, synthesis methods such as hydrothermal (20 occurrences, TLS: 25), coprecipitation (17 occurrences, TLS: 31), and sol–gel (14 occurrences, TLS: 26) are frequently integrated with biological systems to enhance sustainability [84, 85]. Electrochemical synthesis (7 occurrences, TLS: 11) appears less frequently in the GSNP literature. However, electrochemical synthesis in ionic liquids is widely recognized as a promising green chemistry approach [86–88]. Overall, these methods, as reflected by their bibliometric indicators, demonstrate the expanding role of green synthesis across both physical and chemical domains.

Nanoparticle biosynthesis

The biosynthesis of NPs involves the use of reducing and stabilizing agents derived from biological sources. This method has several advantages, including being non-toxic, using low-cost raw materials, relying on renewable resources, being easy to implement, and adhering to environmentally sustainable practices. Consequently, the biosynthetic approach has garnered significant scholarly interest, as demonstrated by numerous studies and experimental investigations in the literature.

Plant-mediated NP

Plant-mediated NP synthesis, commonly referred to as phytosynthesis (23 occurrences, TLS: 53), represents the most extensively investigated biological route for NP fabrication. Within the bibliometric network, plant-based phytosynthesis dominates the GSNP landscape, as evidenced by its central position. This strategy exploits secondary metabolites, such as polyphenols (19 occurrences, TLS: 43) and flavonoids (14 occurrences, TLS: 28), as multifunctional reducing and stabilizing agents, thereby eliminating the need for toxic chemical reagents. These biomolecules contain functional groups, including hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl moieties, which facilitate the reduction of metal ions to their elemental state. Simultaneously, many of these metabolites adsorb onto NP surfaces, acting as natural capping agents that stabilize the resulting nanostructures.

This intrinsic dual functionality significantly simplifies NP synthesis by removing the requirement for separate reducing and stabilizing reagents. Consequently, phytosynthesis can often be conducted as a single-step process under mild reaction conditions, thereby reducing both environmental impact and operational complexity [89, 90]. From a sustainability perspective, these characteristics explain the rapid expansion of plant-mediated NP research over the past decade.

Bibliometric analysis further highlights a strong preference for medicinal plant species (15 occurrences, TLS: 31) as NP precursors. In particular, leaf extracts (25 occurrences, TLS: 63) dominate the co-occurrence network, with higher occurrence and TLS values than those of other plant organs, such as roots, seeds, fruits, or flowers. This predominance reflects several biochemical and practical advantages. Leaves typically contain higher concentrations of redox-active metabolites, especially phenolic compounds and flavonoids, which play essential roles in plant defense and oxidative metabolism [91, 92]. These compounds provide efficient reducing capacity while simultaneously stabilizing the synthesized NPs.

From a sustainability standpoint, leaves represent an abundant and renewable biomass resource that can be harvested without damaging the plant. In many cases, leaf material is derived from agricultural by-products or pruning residues, enabling the conversion of low-value biomass into high-value nanomaterials within a circular bioeconomy framework.

The formation of NPs via plant extracts generally follows a bottom-up mechanism involving three interrelated stages: reduction, nucleation, and growth [95, 96]. During the reduction stage, biomolecules in plant extracts transfer electrons to metal ions such as Ag+, Au3+, or Zn2+, reducing them to neutral metal atoms. Polyphenolic compounds and flavonoids are particularly effective in this process due to their conjugated structures, which facilitate electron donation and metal ion chelation. These reduced atoms subsequently aggregate into nanoscale clusters that serve as nucleation centers. The number of nucleation events strongly influences NP size distribution, with higher nucleation rates typically producing smaller particles due to rapid precursor consumption.

During the growth stage, additional metal atoms deposit onto these nuclei, forming stable NPs. Concurrently, phytochemicals adsorb onto the NP surface, forming an organic capping layer that stabilizes the colloidal system through electrostatic and steric repulsion. This phytochemical corona plays a crucial role in determining NP stability, morphology, and surface functionality.

The physicochemical properties of phytosynthesized NPs are highly sensitive to reaction parameters, including pH, temperature, precursor concentration, and extract composition. For example, alkaline conditions often enhance reduction kinetics by promoting the deprotonation of phenolic groups, while moderate temperatures accelerate nucleation and growth. Adjusting the extract-to-metal precursor ratio further enables control over NP size, morphology, and dispersity [95, 97–99].

Despite these advantages, plant-mediated synthesis presents several challenges for industrial implementation. Variability in phytochemical composition among plant species, seasonal fluctuations in metabolite concentrations, and differences in extraction protocols can lead to inconsistencies in NP properties. Furthermore, the complex chemical composition of plant extracts complicates mechanistic interpretation and limits the standardization of synthesis processes.

Microbial-mediated biosynthesis

Microorganisms constitute another important biological platform for NP production. Bacteria, fungi, and algae possess enzymatic systems capable of reducing metal ions and generating NPs with distinct physicochemical characteristics. However, bibliometric analysis indicates that microbial synthesis is less frequently reported than plant-mediated approaches, as reflected by the relatively low occurrence of keywords such as microbial synthesis (7 occurrences, TLS: 12) and microorganisms (5 occurrences, TLS: 10).

Several bacterial genera, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa (19 occurrences, TLS: 43), Bacillus subtilis (11 occurrences, TLS: 27), and Bacillus cereus (8 occurrences, TLS: 14), have demonstrated the ability to synthesize NPs through intracellular or extracellular pathways. In these systems, enzymatic processes involving reductases and electron-transfer proteins mediate the reduction of metal ions [100]. Non-pathogenic microorganisms, such as cyanobacteria (13 occurrences, TLS: 31), marine actinobacteria (5 occurrences, TLS: 20), and magnetotactic bacteria (5 occurrences, TLS: 8), have also been investigated for their potential to produce metal and bimetallic NPs [101–103].

Microbial-derived NPs have attracted increasing attention in agricultural applications, where they contribute to pathogen suppression, enhanced nutrient availability, and improved crop resilience [100]. Additionally, probiotic microorganisms (5 occurrences, TLS: 15) have been explored as biocompatible platforms for NP synthesis, particularly in biomedical contexts [104]. Algae represent another promising biological source for NP biosynthesis. Both microalgae and macroalgae produce a wide range of metabolites, including polysaccharides, proteins, pigments, and polyphenols, that facilitate metal ion reduction and NP stabilization. These organisms are widely distributed across terrestrial, marine, and freshwater environments [105, 106]. Frequently reported examples include microalgae (27 occurrences, TLS: 48), Chlorella vulgaris (8 occurrences, TLS: 18), marine algae (5 occurrences, TLS: 12), and brown seaweed (5 occurrences, TLS: 9), all of which demonstrate effective NP synthesis under environmentally benign conditions.

Fungi also offer significant advantages for NP production due to their ability to secrete large quantities of extracellular enzymes and metabolites that facilitate metal ion reduction [107, 108]. Fungal synthesis, often referred to as mycosynthesis (13 occurrences, TLS: 27), can occur intracellularly or extracellularly, with extracellular synthesis offering advantages in downstream recovery. Commonly studied species include Candida albicans (12 occurrences, TLS: 29), Aspergillus flavus (10 occurrences, TLS: 22), Aspergillus niger (9 occurrences, TLS: 16), and Fusarium oxysporum (8 occurrences, TLS: 16). Although symbiotic systems such as lichens have also been explored, their representation in the bibliometric network remains limited, indicating that this area is still in an early stage of development [109].

Despite their advantages, microbial synthesis routes face several practical challenges that limit their large-scale application. These systems are highly sensitive to environmental conditions such as pH and temperature, which complicates process control and reproducibility. Additionally, biosafety considerations and operational costs represent significant barriers, as the use of pathogenic or genetically modified organisms requires strict containment measures and specialized facilities. Extended production times further hinder scalability and industrial implementation [110]. Consequently, for many research settings, plant-based synthesis offers a more accessible and cost-effective alternative.

Beyond plant and microbial systems, other natural matrices, such as honey (12 occurrences, TLS: 26) and propolis (6 occurrences, TLS: 13), have emerged as effective precursors for GSNP synthesis. These apicultural products possess intrinsic bioactive properties, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antitumor activities. Moreover, they provide functional groups that simultaneously reduce and stabilize NPs in a single green-processing step [111, 112].

Taken together, the biosynthetic routes discussed above demonstrate that the choice of biological system not only governs the reduction mechanism and physicochemical properties of the resulting NPs, but also shapes their positioning within the broader GSNP research network. These variations are directly reflected in the keyword co-occurrence structure (Fig. 3), where different NP types exhibit distinct occurrence frequencies and connectivity patterns. Therefore, to systematically interpret how material classes emerge and interact within the GSNP landscape, NP types are further classified based on their bibliometric signatures, specifically occurrence and TLS, as summarized in Table 1 and discussed in the following section.

Types of NPs based on bibliometric network

The classification of GSNPs presented in this study is derived directly from the keyword co-occurrence network (Fig. 3), specifically based on occurrence frequency and TLS. Occurrence reflects research intensity, whereas TLS represents the degree of connectivity within the network.

The bibliometric mapping reveals that GSNP research is structured around a set of highly recurrent and strongly interconnected NP types, alongside emerging materials with lower occurrence but increasing network relevance. Based on these patterns, GSNPs are systematically categorized into two principal classes: (i) metallic and metallic oxide NPs and (ii) non-metallic and hybrid NPs.

Metallic and metallic oxide NPs: highest occurrence and network centrality

MNPs constitute the most dominant category in the GSNP bibliometric network, exhibiting the highest values of both occurrence and TLS in Table 1. Among them, Ag NPs represent the most prominent node, with 1048 occurrences and a TLS of 2231, confirming their central role within the network (Fig. 3). Their strong connectivity with keywords such as antibacterial activity, cytotoxicity, and drug delivery indicates that their dominance is closely associated with biomedical applications. The high TLS further demonstrates that Ag NPs function as hub nodes that link the synthesis, characterization, and application domains.

Au NPs also exhibit a significant presence (298 occurrences, TLS: 577), particularly within biomedical and sensing-related clusters. In contrast, other metallic systems, including copper (84 occurrences, TLS: 166), palladium (33 occurrences, TLS: 52), and platinum (15 occurrences, TLS: 29), show moderate occurrence while maintaining relevance in biomedical, environmental, and corrosion-resistant applications. Collectively, these high occurrences frequencies and TLS values confirm that MNPs form the structural backbone of GSNP research.

Metallic oxide NPs represent another key class within the GSNP framework, offering high stability, photocatalytic activity, and environmental compatibility. ZnO NPs are the most prominent metal oxide system in the network (150 occurrences, TLS: 353), widely associated with antibacterial activity, UV-blocking, and photocatalysis. Other oxide systems, including copper oxide, iron oxide, titanium dioxide, cerium oxide, magnesium oxide, and nickel oxide, also appear within the network. Among these, iron oxide NPs are strongly linked to magnetic separation, targeted drug delivery, and imaging applications, while titanium dioxide NPs are primarily associated with photocatalysis and environmental remediation.

Bimetallic NPs (21 occurrences, TLS: 49) are included in this category due to their close bibliometric association with MNP clusters, similar synthesis pathways, and shared application domains. Their positioning within the network reflects strong conceptual proximity to metallic systems. By integrating two metals, bimetallic NPs exhibit synergistic physicochemical properties that extend beyond single-metal systems. The strong link strength between Ag and Au nodes further indicates the growing relevance of Ag–Au systems, while other combinations, such as zinc ferrite and silver–iron NPs, are associated with catalytic and biomedical applications.

The link strength (33) between Ag and Au NPs underscores the rising prominence of Ag–Au bimetallic NPs, widely recognized for their enhanced catalytic efficiency, tunable plasmonic responses, and potent antimicrobial activity [113]. Similarly, biosynthetic approaches using plant extracts have enabled the fabrication of bimetallic NPs, such as zinc ferrite and silver–iron NPs, that exhibit superior catalytic water treatment, as well as antioxidant, antidiabetic, and antibacterial activities [114–116].

Non-Metallic and hybrid NPs: functionally specialized and emerging clusters

In contrast to metallic systems, non-metallic and hybrid NPs occupy more specialized and emerging positions within the bibliometric network. Although these materials generally exhibit lower occurrence values, their TLS patterns indicate increasing integration and growing relevance in advanced applications.

Carbon-based nanomaterials

Carbon-based nanomaterials, including graphene oxide (GO), reduced graphene oxide (rGO), and carbon dots, represent an emerging yet strategically important class of GSNPs. In the bibliometric network, GO (58 occurrences, TLS: 133) and rGO (43 occurrences, TLS: 85) occur less frequently than MNPs but exhibit strong connectivity with keywords related to nanocomposites, energy applications, and advanced materials.

Their relatively lower occurrence indicates that this area is still developing; however, their strong association with emerging keywords such as supercapacitors, electrochemical systems, and nanocomposites underscores their growing importance. From a materials perspective, GO provides a functionalized surface rich in oxygen-containing groups, facilitating interaction with biomolecules during green synthesis. Its reduction to rGO restores electrical conductivity while maintaining structural integrity, making these materials highly suitable for hybrid nanocomposite systems.

These bibliometric patterns indicate a gradual transition from single-component metallic systems toward multifunctional carbon-based and hybrid nanomaterials.

Silicon and Sulfur-based NPs

Silicon- and sulfur-based NPs appear as low-occurrence nodes within the bibliometric network, reflecting a more specialized research focus. These materials are primarily associated with niche applications, including sensing, agriculture, and environmental monitoring [117]. Their limited occurrence suggests that they remain at an early stage of development within GSNP research, although their presence in the network indicates potential for future expansion.

Polymer-based NPs

Polymer-based NPs, such as chitosan-based nanostructures, exhibit a lower incidence and lower TLS values than metallic systems. Their position in the network is more peripheral, reflecting a domain-specific orientation. These materials are primarily used in biomedical applications, including drug delivery, biocompatibility, and controlled-release systems. Their role is largely supportive, providing functional matrices that enhance stability and targeting performance.

Hybrid Nanocomposites

Hybrid nanocomposites represent a key emerging direction in GSNP research. Although individual systems may not show the highest occurrence values, the keyword nanocomposite shows strong TLS in Fig. 3, indicating extensive interconnections across multiple clusters.

These materials combine different classes of nanostructures, including metal–carbon, polymer–metal, and metal oxide–carbon systems, thereby enhancing catalytic, antimicrobial, and electronic properties. Importantly, hybrid nanocomposites differ from bimetallic NPs: bimetallic systems consist of alloyed metals within the metallic cluster, whereas hybrid nanocomposites integrate structurally distinct material classes and occupy bridging positions within the network.

Their elevated TLS indicates that they serve as connectors between otherwise distinct research domains, underscoring their importance in advancing GSNP research toward multifunctional, application-driven materials.

Integrated bibliometric perspective on NPs types

The combined analysis of occurrence and TLS reveals a hierarchical and evolving structure within GSNP research. Metallic and metal oxide NPs dominate due to their high prevalence and centrality, forming the core of current research. In contrast, non-metallic and hybrid NPs, particularly carbon-based systems and nanocomposites, show lower occurrence but increasing TLS, indicating their emergence as high-impact materials.

Polymer-based and other non-metallic systems maintain specialized roles within specific application domains. Overall, the bibliometric network (Fig. 3) and quantitative indicators (Table 1) demonstrate a clear transition from reliance on MNPs toward more complex, hybrid, and multifunctional systems designed for advanced applications in biomedicine, environmental sustainability, and materials science.

Application of GSNPs

Building on the types of NPs, the dominance of plant-based synthesis, and the structural and physicochemical characteristics discussed in previous sections, this section examines how these intrinsic properties govern the functional performance and application potential of GSNPs.

Biomedical and pharmaceutical applications

The bibliometric results reveal that biomedical and pharmaceutical applications are among the most mature and densely connected clusters in the GSNP research landscape. This cluster connectivity is dominated by high-occurrence frequency, high-TLS keywords such as antibacterial, antioxidant, anticancer, antidiabetic, and drug delivery, indicating that the field has converged around a limited set of application axes rather than being diffusely spread across all possible medical uses. The following subsections explicitly interpret these themes using underlying keyword co-occurrence and network link strengths, as well as translational relevance.

Antimicrobial research

Antimicrobial applications constitute the core hub within the biomedical cluster and represent the most established and interconnected topic in the GSNP network. This dominance is quantitatively supported by the high occurrence and TLS of the terms antibacterial (553 occurrences, TLS: 1389) and antibacterial activity (334 occurrences, TLS: 736), which collectively form the primary bridge between GSNPs and clinical translation. The central position and strong connectivity of these nodes demonstrate that antibacterial functions are the main driver of biomedical GSNP research.

Within this hub, the network highlights Ag NPs as the leading metallic agents, with strong co-occurrence links to antibiofilm (53 occurrences, TLS: 143), antibiotic resistance (10 occurrences, TLS: 33), and key pathogens such as Escherichia coli (20 occurrences, TLS: 48) and Staphylococcus aureus (22 occurrences, TLS: 46) [124]. This pattern suggests that the community relies on a relatively small set of model organisms to benchmark GSNP-based interventions against multidrug-resistant infections. Methodologically, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay (19 occurrences, TLS: 49) exhibits a higher research density than the zone of inhibition method (6 occurrences, TLS: 14), reflecting a shift toward quantitatively robust, clinically relevant metrics.

The antimicrobial cluster connectivity is also expanding beyond bacteria. The terms antifungal (95 occurrences, TLS: 272) and antifungal activity (51 occurrences, TLS: 108) form a secondary, but clearly connected, sub-cluster, indicating that GSNPs are increasingly investigated against fungal pathogens. More recently, the emergence of the antiviral (16 occurrences, TLS: 45) node and its specific links to COVID-19 (8 occurrences, TLS: 16) demonstrate that viral inhibition has rapidly become a critical extension of the antimicrobial network. In these studies, Ag NPs are repeatedly associated with mechanisms such as inhibition of viral entry, interference with replication, and modulation of host immune responses [125–128].

Antioxidant activity

Antioxidant applications constitute the second major application hub in the biomedical network. The keyword antioxidant (405 occurrences, TLS: 1137) and its closely related term antioxidant activity (193 occurrences, TLS: 453) demonstrate strong connectivity to medicinal plants (15 occurrences, TLS: 31), phenolic compounds (10 occurrences, TLS: 23), and flavonoids (14 occurrences, TLS: 28), underscoring the central role of phytochemicals in enabling antioxidant functions in GSNPs. This pattern indicates that antioxidant behavior is not an isolated property, but is structurally embedded in the network through green synthesis and surface-bound bioactive compounds.

Mechanistically, the co-occurrence of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (24 occurrences, TLS: 59) with antioxidant-related terms supports the consensus that GSNPs primarily exert their effects through ROS scavenging and redox modulation [129, 130]. The prominence of specific assays, especially the DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) assay (16 occurrences, TLS: 38), suggests a methodological convergence on a small number of standardized tools for quantifying radical scavenging capacity [131]. Within this sub-cluster, GSNPs derived from plant sources such as Azadirachta indica (27 occurrences, TLS: 75), Moringa oleifera (30 occurrences, TLS: 68), and Aloe vera (26 occurrences, TLS: 56), are frequently observed, highlighting them as benchmark systems for achieving high antioxidant efficiency and supporting their positioning as safer, sustainable alternatives to chemically produced NPs [47, 132–134].

Anticancer applications

Anticancer applications form another high-impact node in the biomedical landscape and are closely intertwined with both antioxidant and drug delivery subclusters. The terms anticancer (219 occurrences, TLS: 612) and anticancer activity (106 occurrences, TLS: 259) anchor a network that spans multiple oncology targets, indicating that GSNPs are being tested across a broad range of tumor types rather than in a single, highly specialized indication. Within this structure, breast cancer (MCF 7, 82 links) and cervical cancer (HeLa, 22 links) represent the most intensively studied models, followed by colorectal (29), lung (28), colon (20), ovarian (12), liver (11), and skin cancers (11).

The term apoptosis (64 occurrences, TLS: 162) has a link strength of 16 to green synthesis and anticancer; link strength to Ag NPs is 11; to Au NPs and anticancer activity is 6; and to ROS is 5, indicating that ROS-mediated apoptosis is the dominant mechanistic pathway underpinning GSNP-based cancer therapy. The presence of drug resistance (5 occurrences, TLS: 16) and cancer treatment (5 occurrences, TLS: 16) nodes within this cluster connectivity suggests a targeted effort to use GSNPs to overcome the limitations of conventional chemotherapy, including systemic toxicity and resistance [48, 135]. ZnO NPs, for example, are frequently reported to exhibit dose-dependent cytotoxicity comparable to that of cisplatin but with a more favorable toxicity profile toward normal cells, reinforcing the potential of GSNPs as alternative or adjunct anticancer agents [136–138].

The relatively moderate occurrence weight and TLS for drug delivery applications indicate a promising yet somewhat less extensively explored area compared to anticancer studies. In contrast, the increasing number of publications in this field signals that green NPs are gaining traction as a versatile platform for drug delivery, especially in personalized medicine. Their role in optimizing the release profiles and bioavailability of various drugs suggests their potential to revolutionize treatment paradigms across numerous medical fields.

Anti-inflammatory applications

The anti-inflammatory theme represents a smaller but strategically critical sub-cluster within the biomedical network. The term anti-inflammatory (77 occurrences, TLS: 225) has a notably high TLS relative to its occurrence frequency, indicating that it is strongly interconnected with other biomedical keywords. Its closest links are to antioxidants, antimicrobials, and anticancer agents, suggesting that anti-inflammatory effects are often co-designed with other therapeutic functions in multifunctional GSNP formulations.

This connectivity pattern also highlights specific botanical sources and nanomaterials that drive the cluster. For instance, Azadirachta indica (27 occurrences, TLS: 75) is frequently associated with Ag NPs that exhibit both antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties, particularly in wound healing contexts [46]. In parallel, curcumin-coated Fe3O4 NPs form a distinct sub-branch that combines magnetic targeting with curcumin’s antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, pointing to growing interest in neuroprotective and CNS-oriented applications [139].

Antidiabetic potential

The antidiabetic theme is an emerging node, as indicated by the term antidiabetic (44 occurrences, TLS: 134), suggesting an active yet consolidating research direction. Its network links to green synthesis and diabetes mellitus demonstrate that GSNPs are primarily being investigated as functional agents that mitigate oxidative stress and restore pancreatic β-cell function, rather than as inert carriers.

Preclinical studies in this cluster report GSNP-mediated reductions in blood glucose levels and attenuation of complications, such as nephropathy and neuropathy [140]. Fe2O3 NPs synthesized using Securidaca longipedunculata, for example, are frequently cited in this context [141]. The pattern of connections suggests that the antidiabetic activity of GSNPs is closely tied to their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, supporting a multi-mechanical approach to diabetes management.

GSNPs as advanced drug delivery nanocarriers

Drug delivery applications form a sophisticated but numerically smaller cluster connectivity relative to antibacterial and anticancer themes. The terms drug delivery (47 occurrences, TLS: 109), drug release (6 occurrences, TLS: 14), and nanocarriers (5 occurrences, TLS: 9) demonstrate strong interconnections with high-impact disease nodes, rather than existing as an isolated methodological topic. This pattern underscores that GSNP-based delivery systems are being developed primarily for specific, challenging indications.

Within this cluster, curcumin delivery emerges as a central motif. Curcumin-loaded Ag NPs are repeatedly reported to display superior in vitro cytotoxicity compared to free curcumin or bare NPs, reflecting the network’s focus on overcoming poor solubility and bioavailability [142]. A distinct branch of this cluster connectivity is focused on neurodegenerative diseases, where GSNPs are designed to target amyloid β and neuroinflammation in Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease models [143–145]. In addition, the rise of polymeric nanomaterials such as liposomes, micelles, and dendrimers suggests an emerging convergence between green synthesis and established nanocarrier platforms [143, 146].

The integration of anticancer drugs, such as doxorubicin (15 occurrences, TLS: 49), further underscores the translational potential of these systems. In acute myeloid leukemia (6 occurrences, TLS: 20), for example, doxorubicin-loaded NPs smaller than 50 nm show enhanced uptake by leukemia stem cells and higher apoptosis rates than the free drug, consistent with the network’s emphasis on overcoming multidrug resistance through targeted delivery [147, 148].

Environmental remediation

Environmental remediation constitutes the second major application axis of GSNPs after biomedical uses, but is organized into a partially distinct cluster within the bibliometric network. This cluster connectivity is anchored by the terms environmental remediation (28 occurrences, TLS: 67) and bioremediation (19 occurrences, TLS: 49), reflecting the field’s response to rapid industrialization, population growth, and the resulting burden of water and soil pollution [149, 150]. The network structure demonstrates that most environmental studies focus on aquatic systems, with comparatively weaker development of soil-related themes.

The bibliometric analysis of author keywords identifies wastewater treatment (68 occurrences, TLS: 144) and water treatment (24 occurrences, TLS: 57) as the dominant nodes, confirming that the majority of environmental GSNP research targets aqueous pollution. In contrast, soil remediation (6 occurrences, TLS: 9) appears as a small, weakly connected node, quantitatively indicating that terrestrial applications remain underexplored despite growing concerns about heavy metal contamination in soils.

Core of pollutants and network benchmarks

Within the environmental cluster, the network clearly distinguishes between organic and inorganic pollutants. Organic pollutants (10 occurrences, TLS: 25) are the primary focus of the research, largely driven by effluents from textile, petrochemical, and pharmaceutical industries. Among these, organic dyes (23 occurrences, TLS: 52) and phenolic compounds (10 occurrences, TLS: 23) are the most densely connected targets, reflecting their widespread use as model contaminants in GSNP-based removal studies.

The dye-related sub-cluster is dominated by methylene blue (MB) (95 occurrences, TLS: 257), which serves as the principal benchmark pollutant owing to its recalcitrance and well-documented toxicological impacts [151, 152]. Other key dye nodes include malachite green (39 occurrences, TLS: 81), methyl orange (23 occurrences, TLS: 68), rhodamine B (15 occurrences, TLS: 38), Congo red (17 occurrences, TLS: 34), and crystal violet (11 occurrences, TLS: 28). The high TLS values associated with these dyes indicate a strong consensus on their use as standard analytes to evaluate catalytic, photocatalytic, and adsorptive performance of GSNPs.

Beyond dyes, the network extends to a broader range of phenolic and pharmaceutical pollutants. Phenolic compounds such as 4-nitrophenol (30 occurrences, TLS: 66) and bisphenol A (9 occurrences, TLS: 21) are emerging nodes, signaling increased attention to endocrine-disrupting and persistent organic contaminants [153, 154]. In parallel, heavy metals (26 occurrences, TLS: 53) form a robust inorganic sub cluster encompassing arsenic (9 occurrences, TLS: 17), cadmium (13 occurrences, TLS: 27), chromium (9 occurrences, TLS: 23), copper (25 occurrences, TLS: 54), lead (7 occurrences, TLS: 20), and nickel (7 occurrences, TLS: 20). The network position of these metals confirms their status as priority targets in GSNP based remediation owing to their toxicity and prevalence in industrial effluents.

The methodological structure of the environmental remediation cluster connectivity is dominated by two interlinked hubs: adsorption and photocatalysis. Taken together, these patterns demonstrate that the environmental GSNP network is evolving from purely sorption-based approaches toward more sophisticated hybrid systems that couple adsorption with photocatalytic and catalytic degradation, while still relying on a small set of standard dyes and metals as performance benchmarks.

Agricultural and food applications

The increasing interest in sustainable solutions related to the field of agriculture is manifested by the high co-occurrence of the keyword’s germination (26 occurrences, TLS: 58), plant growth (6 occurrences, TLS: 16), and larvicidal activity (17 occurrences, TLS: 41). Furthermore, terms such as nanofertilizers (11occurrences, TLS: 27), plant pathogens (5 occurrences, TLS: 15), and pesticides (5 occurrences, TLS: 8) are associated with increasing requirements for ecofriendly practices in agriculture to reduce the environmental impact and decrease the cost of traditional fertilizers.

GSNPs are also useful as sorbent materials for abating noxious greenhouse gases (including CO2, N2O, and CH4) [24, 157]. In addition to their environmental advantages, NPs increase agricultural productivity through enhanced nutrient absorption and increased crop tolerance, especially for major crops, such as rice (20 occurrences, TLS: 29), wheat (15 occurrences, TLS: 29), and maize (5 occurrences, TLS: 13). In addition, GSNPs further reduce health hazards to farmers who have come into contact with conventional agrochemicals while they are working.

The increasing complexity of food systems, from cultivation to distribution, has driven the trend of combined requirements to assure food safety and quality from production to processing [158]. GSNPs are potential replacements for foodborne pathogens (9 occurrences, TLS: 21), a global primary health concern. These pathogens are frequently present in foods contaminated by microorganisms (including bacteria, fungi, and viruses). They can cause mild symptoms, such as diarrhea, or life-threatening disorders, such as meningitis [159]. According to the WHO, unsafe food results in 600 million foodborne disease cases and 420,000 deaths annually, with children under the age of 5 years carrying 30% of the burden. Food can be contaminated with pathogens at all stages of the food chain, from pre-harvest and post-harvest to processing, transportation, handling, and preparation for consumption. The nature of what we eat makes minimally processed foods vulnerable.

GSNPs are essential for inactivating pathogens, enhancing food safety, and preventing foodborne diseases in food applications (e.g., food processing), while also modifying food parameters such as texture, appearance, and nutritive value. Encapsulation (13 occurrences, TLS: 27), a technique commonly used to protect labile plant pigments such as anthocyanins, notably enhances their stability and activity in food products.

Bioactive compounds such as vitamins, proteins, lipids, and carbohydrates, which are prone to degradation in acidic environments, benefit from encapsulation. This protects them from harm, preserves their integrity, and allows them to be readily incorporated into foods. Recent progress has also led to the invention of intelligent NP based capsules, which are designed to enhance the administration of essential micronutrients, vitamins, and drugs via daily food consumption [160].

Moreover, GSNPs in food-type emulsions has been able to facilitate the production of new green emulsifiers and stabilizers such as Pickering emulsions (8 occurrences, TLS: 6), essential oils (17 occurrences, TLS: 29), nanocellulose (8 occurrences, TLS: 15), and cellulose nanocrystals (11 occurrences, TLS: 10). These substances have shown promise because of their redeeming value, such as their renewable nature, low toxicity, amphiphilic nature, compatibility with the BioPhase, and high aspect ratio, which might be innovative additives in food emulsions [161]. For instance, Wu et al. [162] presented cellulose nanofibril films as promising packaging materials, utilizing an oregano essential oil Pickering emulsion stabilized by ZnO NPs to fabricate them, which demonstrate antimicrobial and antioxidative properties.

Packaging is an essential means to prolong shelf life, improve quality and safety, and retard food spoilage. The occurrence weight and TLS of active packaging (7 occurrences, TLS: 22) and food packaging (9 occurrences, TLS: 21) exemplify a paradigm shift from static containment to dynamic interaction with food. Such devices deliver active compounds or adsorb detrimental units to alter the package’s optimal mix, thereby suppressing its oxidative, microbial, or enzymatic breakdown.

In addition to gas scavengers (e.g., O2, CO2, and CH4), these systems include moisture absorbers and antibacterial components [163]. Moreover, sustainable or green packaging is rapidly emerging due to increasing environmental concerns, which has stimulated the development of new packaging technologies [164]. AP systems can be divided into migrating and nonmigrating systems, in which migrating systems allow the active compound to penetrate deeper into the food, while nonmigrating systems target the food surface [165, 166].

In food preservation, NPs have been used in packaging to extend the shelf life of perishable foods and reduce microbial spoilage, especially in minimally processed foods. The GSNPs with adjustable distinctiveness features, such as Ag NPs, ZnO NPs, and TiO2 NPs, can be added to packaging materials to improve food safety and prolong the storage life of food products, and are becoming increasingly common [167, 168]. Nanocomposites based on traditional materials with NPs are also attracting interest. For example, nanocomposite films prepared from chitosan and gelatin, blended with green-synthesized ZnO NPs, provide an eco-friendly solution for postharvest storage of fresh produce [169, 170].

Energy and sensor applications

The applications of GSNPs extend to green energy, particularly energy storage. The growing attention to supercapacitors (50 occurrences, TLS: 85) demonstrates the feasibility of green nanomaterials for energy applications. Green-scale MNPs, derived from plant extracts, have been investigated for their potential to significantly enhance energy storage capacity. For example, NiO NPs synthesized from aloe vera are excellent for electrochemical applications and are candidates for energy storage [171]. Furthermore, ZnO and Ag-doped ZnO-based nanocomposites have demonstrated supercapacitor potential with high capacitance retention for several thousand cycles [172].

Additionally, the integration of GSNPs has led to advancements in photocatalytic water splitting and hydrogen production in renewable energy. Unmodified ZnInS (gZIS) NPs have also achieved self-assisted photocatalytic water splitting, with a solar-to-hydrogen conversion efficiency matching that of noble-metal-decorated materials [173]. Sulfur vacancies and hierarchical morphologies make these systems more favorable for charge transfer and interaction with water molecules. Hybrid platforms that integrate biological systems with photocatalysts are an emerging field; some have even demonstrated impressive solar-to-hydrogen efficiencies, while also fixing CO [174].

In sensor applications, GSNPs play a crucial role in sensor design and in improving the detection and monitoring of various analytes. The terms sensor (15 occurrences, TLS: 23), biosensor (15 occurrences, TLS: 37), colorimetric sensor (8 occurrences, TLS: 17), electrochemical sensor (26 occurrences, TLS: 41), gas sensor (9 occurrences, TLS: 19), and humidity sensor (5 occurrences, TLS: 8) demonstrate the increasing importance of GNPs in the field of sensors. Green nanosensors, which utilize environmentally friendly nanotechnology, are being rapidly developed in various industries, including food safety and efficiency upgrades [175]. For example, Seekaew et al. [176] constructed a humidity sensor based on p-type Co3O4/p-type AgO NPs, which showed good response, repeatability, and stability toward relatively high humidity levels over a wide range.

Together, these patterns show that application development has outpaced systematic evaluation of long-term safety, environmental fate, and regulatory compliance, raising critical questions about the toxicological robustness of current GSNP research. However, this application’s momentum outpaces systematic toxicological evaluation, creating a critical translational bottleneck, as analyzed in Sect. 9.

Toxicology and biosafety: the translational bottleneck

Despite the rapid expansion of GSNP applications, toxicological assessment and biosafety remain relatively underdeveloped compared with synthesis and application testing. The bibliometric analysis reveals that keywords related to nanotoxicology, in vivo studies, risk assessment, and regulatory frameworks occur far less frequently than those associated with antimicrobial activity, photocatalysis, or environmental remediation, highlighting a critical translational bottleneck.

The successful commercialization of GSNPs needs a proven functional efficacy and a rigorous, regulatory-compliant safety profile assessment. Bibliometric analysis for nanotoxicology revealed a variety of occurrences for toxicity terms. High occurrence for cytotoxicity (291 occurrences, TLS: 762) as a general term, in contrast to the low occurrence of specific toxicology mechanisms and devastating effects terms as listed below. A lack of focus on specific toxicology terms may threaten the GSNP translational and commercialization pathway, as it limits understanding of the risks associated with nanomaterials and hinders the development of targeted safety regulations. Moreover, this may also indicate that most GSNP researchers remain in the basic phase; very few are translated into the applied phase. This is really driven by the limited funds and high costs of these analyses in developing countries.

Regarding the earlier-established mechanisms of GSNP toxicity. The principal GSNP-induced toxicity is driven by the production of ROS and oxidative stress, culminating in numerous destructive processes [177]. The occurrence of ROS was 24 (TLS 59), and that of oxidative stress was 39 (TLS 80). Fewer studies analyzed the toxic effects of GSNP. The toxic NPs produce ROS, leading to genotoxicity or DNA damage (20 occurrences, TLS: 45) and cell-cycle arrest (5 occurrences, TLS: 10). Scant studies specify their aim of safety testing as nanotoxicity (6 occurrences, TLS: 15).

Toxicity testing, described as in vivo studies (5 occurrences, TLS: 15) and in vitro studies (7 occurrences, TLS: 21), remains lower than expected. Notably, some studies did not use these terms but instead used cytotoxic activity (24 occurrences, TLS: 60), which is measured in tissue cultures. For instance, some used hepatoma cell line HepG2 (8 occurrences, TLS: 21). The cytotoxicity test primarily aimed to assess cell viability after exposure to NPs. The tests used were a cell viability test (15 occurrences, TLS: 36) and an MTT assay (23 occurrences, TLS: 52). Generally speaking, this pattern suggests that the current safety paradigm still meets the needs. Additionally, it is heavily weighted toward high-throughput, short-term screening, providing toxicity information on the experimental cell lines, but few studies translate this insight into toxic effects on all body systems under the term toxicity (49 occurrences, TLS: 120).

Nevertheless, bibliometric patterns highlight a safety gap between the demonstrations of toxicity testing and regulatory expectations. Toxic effects on cells, as indicated by the cytotoxicity term, were covered by more studies than general descriptors like toxicity. This disparity suggests that while GSNP researchers are proficient at short-term safety testing, they have not matured to include systemic nanotoxicity assessment to meet regulatory agencies’ requirements for industrial scaling and long-term use.

Toxicology and regulatory frameworks

Regulatory-related terms occupy only peripheral positions in the network, further confirming that formal approval pathways remain underrepresented in current GSNP discourse. References to major regulatory bodies and standards, such as the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the European Medicines Agency (EMA), and ISO 10993, appear sporadically and are seldom tightly linked to the main clusters of synthesis and application. This peripheral placement is consistent with the basic phase or preclinical phase studies mentioned above. It is worth noting that regulatory agencies, such as the FDA, typically require multilayered in vivo toxicokinetic and toxicodynamic data for any nanomaterial intended for clinical, food, or environmental applications.

At the same time, the emerging presence of associated keywords leads to a possible path forward. Initiatives funded by the European Union, such as NANOTEST and NANoREG, along with increasing references to in silico modeling and predictive toxicology in the next-generation risk assessment (NGRA), indicate a great interest in establishing standardized, reproducible safety evaluation. In silico modeling aims to anticipate hazardous nanoforms and toxicity pathways before full-scale production, thereby shortening the otherwise lengthy regulatory trajectory for nano-enabled products [178, 179].

In summary, the bibliometric evidence makes clear that toxicology, nanotoxicology, and regulatory considerations are not marginal add-ons but central determinants of GSNP commercialization. The dominance of simple cytotoxicity metrics, the underrepresentation of systemic nanotoxicology, and the rarity of regulatory concepts collectively delineate the main bottlenecks that must be addressed. Aligning future GSNP research with NGRA principles will explicitly link experimental design to regulatory standards and convert green-synthesized NPs from laboratory curiosities into safe, market-ready technologies.

The global collaboration strength on GSNPs is characterized by high-intensity research hubs where collaboration strength, evidenced by thicker pairwise links and elevated TLS values, identifies key network leaders. India (TLS 1,176,967) occupies the preeminent position, followed by Saudi Arabia (736,015), China (429,181), Pakistan (420,683), and Egypt (369,286). The architectural density of these networks facilitates rapid method diffusion and standards adoption through two distinct collaborative pathways: robust South–South ties between nations such as India and Saudi Arabia enable efficient bioresource and expertise sharing, while North–South links between regions like the US and India provide essential analytical depth to biosynthetic research.

In addition, research on scientific productivity indicates that citation impact per paper is a distinct dimension from total volume, separating countries that produce a high volume of research from those that generate highly cited work. The volume-driven nations often excel in output, while smaller, high-income nations tend to deliver high-impact work in specialized niches. Citation impact per paper decouples leadership dimensions: volume leaders (India, China) excel in output, but smaller nations like Singapore (34.84 citations/document), Qatar (31.29), and Ireland (31.03) deliver high-impact work in niches such as advanced characterization and translation, often tied to strong GDP per capita and consortia participation.

Institutionally, high-output countries (India, China, Saudi Arabia, Pakistan) rely on large universities/national centers leveraging local flora and agendas (e.g., Saudi Vision 2030), generating broad portfolios [181–183]. High-impact systems (Singapore, Qatar) focus on fewer, more highly cited studies within international networks and provide methodological expertise. This hybrid model, volume-driven innovation from the Global South plus depth from impact hubs, positions GSNP research for scalable, regulatory-compliant advances.

In conclusion, India serves as an example of how national policy strategically aligns with scientific innovation to foster research leadership. The strong presence of the country in GSNP synthesis is attributed to (I) prioritization of science and technology, (II) rich biodiversity for biogenic NP synthesis, (III) institutional support, (IV) robust international collaboration, and (V) advancements in NP applications in nanotechnology [184]. Furthermore, the extensive postharvest food waste loss of fresh food of up to 40% in India makes it a rich, low-cost source for agricultural residues and plant extracts for GSNPs, which makes the process economic and environmentally attractive [185].

The bibliometric evidence illustrates a dynamic global research system, with developing countries driving the system through local solutions and international collaboration. The geographic concentration of clusters in a region facilitates local problem-solving, while cross-cluster links provide opportunities for knowledge exchange and collaboration. Future strategies should prioritize equitable partnerships, biodiversity preservation, and scaling up plant-based processes for industry. This will help more evenly spread the returns from development and capitalize on the comparative advantages of both developed and developing countries, benefiting sustainable nanotechnology.

While these patterns clarify where GSNP research is produced and how countries collaborate, they do not yet reveal which publications and authors exert the greatest intellectual influence within this global network.

Across the landmark set, highly cited contributions are dominated by works on ZnO and Ag NPs produced via plant-mediated routes, particularly using extracts from fruits, leaves, and medicinal plants. This pattern suggests a consolidation of plant-based synthesis as a central paradigm in GSNPs, where ease of preparation, perceived biocompatibility, and alignment with green chemistry principles have collectively driven citation impact. The frequent use of waste-derived biomaterials, such as fruit peels and other agro-residues, further underscores the growing integration of circular-economy concepts into NP design.

Functionally, the landmark papers consistently couple green synthesis with clearly defined applications, rather than treating synthesis as an end in itself. Antibacterial and antioxidant activities, photocatalytic degradation of organic pollutants and dyes, and broader environmental remediation applications recur among the most cited works. This convergence suggests that citation impact in the GSNP domain is increasingly associated with studies that demonstrate technological relevance and performance in realistic environmental or biomedical settings.

The temporal clustering of these highly cited articles also signals a shift from exploratory demonstrations of green routes toward more sophisticated systems, including doped metal oxides, nanocomposites, and multifunctional hybrids tailored for specific target pollutants or pathogens. In particular, publications that integrate structural tuning (such as doping or composite formation) with green synthesis strategies tend to attract higher citations, reflecting community interest in performance-optimized, yet environmentally benign, nanomaterials. Overall, the citation evolution of landmark GSNP papers reveals a trajectory toward application-driven, materials-engineered designs grounded in renewable, plant-based resources.

Analysis of influential authors and network centrality

Beyond individual publications, bibliometric indicators of author productivity and network connectivity provide further insight into the intellectual leadership within the GSNP research domain. Table 6 evaluates author influence using three complementary metrics: number of documents, total citations, and TLS, which reflects the degree of collaboration and knowledge exchange within the co-authorship network.

Within the analyzed dataset, the most influential authors exhibit different combinations of productivity, citation impact, and centrality. Some authors, such as those with high total citation counts, function as core intellectual references, whereas others contribute primarily through extensive publication output or through highly connected collaborative roles. This differentiation suggests that intellectual leadership in GSNPs is not solely determined by the number of publications but also by how authors are positioned within the broader collaboration network.

High TLS values highlight authors who act as hubs linking multiple research groups and thematic clusters. These central authors often engage in interdisciplinary collaborations that span environmental nanotechnology, materials engineering, and plant-based nanobiotechnology, thereby facilitating cross-fertilization of ideas between methodological and application-oriented research lines. Their central positions in the network suggest that collaboration-driven influence is a key mechanism for accelerating the dissemination of new green synthesis strategies and functional nanomaterials.

In addition, several authors demonstrate rapidly growing citation profiles over a relatively short period, reflecting citation bursts associated with novel synthesis approaches, doped or hybrid nanostructures, and high-efficiency photocatalytic or antimicrobial systems. Such dynamics imply that the GSNP field is responsive to technical innovations that demonstrably improve performance while maintaining strong environmental credentials. Collectively, these author-level indicators underscore that the GSNP research landscape is shaped by a combination of highly cited leaders, productive contributors, and collaboration-intensive bridging researchers.

Challenges and future prospects

The combined bibliometric evidence on synthesis routes, application domains, toxicological assessment, and global collaboration highlights a clear disconnect between the rapid academic expansion of GSNP research and its limited translational maturity. Although green synthesis has become a central organizing concept, multiple technical, regulatory, and commercialization barriers still constrain GSNPs to early-stage development.

The NP market, projected at USD 40.6 billion by 2027, demands industrial-scale NPs [186]. Nevertheless, the GSNP shift is not without challenges, as depicted in Fig. 8 [187–190]. These challenges encompass (i) dispersity and inconsistent morphology from variable biological extracts (species, geography, growth conditions); (ii) mechanistic opacity in biosynthesis influenced by phytochemicals/enzymes, lacking real-time monitoring [49, 50, 191, 192]; (iii) labor-intensive bottom-up steps (harvesting, centrifugation) causing agglomeration/impurities, plus top-down barriers [188, 193, 194]; (iv) low yields are insufficient for throughput; (v) instability (aggregation, oxidation) requires costly stabilization while preserving biocompatibility [195, 196]; and (vi) regulatory/toxicity gaps, with rapid GSNP deployment outpacing data on chronic exposure/bioaccumulation (e.g., Ag NPs disrupting aquatic environments may damage ecosystems, alter nitrogen cycling, and accumulate within the food web, posing potential health and ecological risks) [197–201].Fig. 8Challenges and limitations in the commercialization of GSNPs

Addressing these requires standardized protocols for reproducibility, interdisciplinary efforts (chemists, biologists, toxicologists, regulators), and comprehensive LCAs from cradle to grave to pinpoint hotspots and optimize trade-offs [202–205]. Hybrid innovations (e.g., extracts produced by flow chemistry) and simulations can enhance scalability, while Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) aligned toxicology ensures safety.

Bibliometric evidence confirms GSNPs’ pre-commercial status, with patent, technology readiness level, industry collaboration, and funding as marginal nodes (1 occurrence; TLS < 70), indicating scant focus on IP, partnerships, or resources. Patent activity is limited and academic-dominated (e.g., Ag NPs; US12133863B1: lipid-encapsulated, Ag NPs; US11806363B1: Euphorbia extract, and Ag NPs; WO2024123268A1: hazelnut husk extract), showing few industry assignees or scale-up claims in 2020–2025 WO/US/EP filings.

From a translational perspective, the development of GSNPs remains largely concentrated at low TRLs, primarily within TRL 1–4 (lab validation), with absent co-occurrences for TRL 5–9 (e.g., pilot plant, techno-economic analysis), per keyword networks. Bridging requires TRL roadmaps: process standardization, regulatory toxicology (OECD), and LCAs quantifying barriers, as shown in Fig. 8, to enable the academia–industry transition. These findings collectively inform the broader implications and future directions of GSNP research.

Collectively, these challenges make it clear that future progress in GSNPs will depend less on further increasing publication volume and more on strategically aligning synthesis design, toxicology, process engineering, and policy frameworks to support industrial-scale, regulation-ready implementation. These interconnected challenges demand a strategic research pivot from publication volume toward advancing TRL, regulatory alignment, and industrial collaboration, as synthesized in the concluding analysis.

Conclusions

This bibliometric analysis demonstrates that biosynthesis is the dominant paradigm in GSNP research, with plant-mediated routes representing the most prevalent approach due to their phytochemical richness and operational simplicity. In contrast, microbial, algal, and fungal systems remain underexplored despite their scalability potential, indicating that practical and biosafety constraints continue to shape methodological preferences.

Despite rapid expansion, the field remains materially and conceptually concentrated around Ag, Au, and ZnO NPs, with a strong emphasis on in vitro biomedical applications that often lack sufficient toxicological depth. This imbalance highlights a broader limitation in GSNP research, where publication volume continues to grow without a corresponding increase in translational readiness.

A clear gap persists between scientific output and technological advancement, with most studies confined to early development stages (TRL 1–4). This limitation is reinforced by the predominance of conventional characterization techniques, limited integration of advanced surface-sensitive analyses, and the minimal presence of commercialization indicators, such as patents and industry collaboration.

A clear disconnect also exists between scientific output and technological advancement, with most studies remaining at early development stages (TRL 1–4). This is further reinforced by the predominance of conventional characterization techniques and the limited integration of advanced surface analyses, patent activity, and industry collaboration.

Addressing these challenges requires a strategic shift toward integrative research frameworks. Future efforts should prioritize standardized nanotoxicological evaluation (e.g., OECD-aligned protocols), LCA, to validate sustainability claims, and the development of scalable synthesis platforms. In particular, hybrid approaches that combine green chemistry with controlled processing technologies, such as flow systems and bioreactors, offer a promising pathway toward improved reproducibility and industrial scalability.

Collectively, this work establishes a quantitative and conceptual framework that not only maps the GSNP research landscape but also identifies the critical scientific and translational pathways required to advance green synthesis from an academic paradigm toward industrial implementation and regulatory maturity.

Although this study is based on a single database (Scopus), the observed patterns provide a robust foundation for understanding the structural dynamics of GSNP research. Future bibliometric investigations integrating multiple databases, such as Web of Science and PubMed, along with complementary analytical tools, are recommended to validate further and extend these findings.
