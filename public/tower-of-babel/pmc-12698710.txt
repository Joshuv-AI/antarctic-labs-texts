A hot-humid tolerant and antibacterial MXene-based hydrogel sensor for real-time cardiorespiratory monitoring in endurance sports
Authors: Xiaoyan Wang, Hongcheng Xu, Chuanyu Zhang, Eng G Lim, Yinchao Zhao, Kai Hoettges, Xueyong Wei, Qifeng Lu, Fuzhou Niu, Pengfei Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWearable sensors for continuous physiological monitoring during intense exercise face significant challenges, including motion artifacts and skin discomfort. Conductive hydrogels offer a promising solution due to their skin-like flexibility and excellent electrical conductivity, yet their application in extreme conditions like marathon running remains challenges. Here, we develop a MXene-based dual-network hydrogel composed of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and tempo-oxidized cellulose nanofibers (TOCNF) crosslinked with MXene nanosheets and borax. This hydrogel exhibits exceptional environmental stability (35 days at 4 °C and 30% relative humidity) and strain sensitivity (gauge factor of 7.79 at 800% strain), while MXene integration provides outstanding antibacterial properties (>99% inhibition). As a proof of concept, under simulated marathon conditions (38°C, 52% relative humidity), the sensor maintains stable performance for 6 h, demonstrating reliable heart rate and respiration monitoring. These capabilities are crucial for identifying early signs of cardiorespiratory abnormalities during endurance sports. Our work presents a robust strategy for developing wearable hydrogel sensors with long-term reliability in extreme environments, offering significant potential for sports medicine, exercise physiology, and continuous health monitoring applications.Subject terms: Sensors, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

The advent of wearable sensor technology has dramatically revolutionized healthcare, allowing for real-time, non-invasive tracking of physiological parameters, which is particularly valuable in sports, especially intense events such as marathons. Marathon-related fatalities often result from undetected cardiovascular or respiratory abnormalities1,2, highlighting the critical need for continuous monitoring of these physiological signals in endurance athletes. However, current available commercial wearable devices like smartwatches and fitness bands offer basic heart rate and respiration monitoring functionalities but suffer motion artifacts due to limb movement and skin slippage, tightness induced discomfort, and bacterial proliferation in humid environments.

In contrast, wearable sensors based on conductive hydrogels hold great potential to solve these existing challenges3,4. This promising performance is attributed to the unique material properties of conductive hydrogels, especially their combination of mechanical flexibility and electrical conductivity. In particular, their three-dimensional cross-linked polymer networks, enriched with high water content, closely mimic human skin. This similarity allows them to reduce motion artifacts by adhering closely to the skin and enhance wearing comfort, even during intense physical activity. As a result, they can provide more precise and real-time heart rate and respiration data during exercise, which is crucial for the timely detection of potential health risk5,6.

Conductive hydrogel-based wearable sensors have demonstrated significant potential for physiological signal monitoring, with recent advances enabling high-fidelity detection of pulse and respiratory patterns7–9. For example, Liu et al. developed a temperature-responsive conductive hydrogel, which can adhere to skin and detect electrocardiogram (ECG) for health monitoring9. In particular, during physical activity such as running or cycling, human skin experiences dynamic changes in temperature, humidity, and mechanical strain, posing significant challenges for sensor stability and accuracy. To address these challenges, researchers have explored strategies to enhance hydrogel the environmental adaptability for monitoring physiological signals. These strategies involve incorporating moisturizing agents, such as organic solvents10,11 and inorganic salts12 materials into the hydrogel system. However, most previous research has validated monitoring physiological under static or room-temperature humidity, and few studies have rigorously tested hydrogel sensors under the coupled thermo-mechanical-humidity conditions that mimic exercising human skin, where sustained temperatures (∼38°C) and elevated humidity (>50% relative humidity (RH))13.

MXenes, a class of two-dimensional transition metal carbides and nitrides, are attractive candidates for hydrogel engineering owing to their high electrical conductivity, abundant surface terminations, and inherent antibacterial activity14. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), widely used in hydrogel systems, provides a flexible polymeric backbone with abundant hydroxyl groups that enable dynamic crosslinking and impart stretchability15. TEMPO-oxidized cellulose nanofibers (TOCNF), in turn, form a mechanically robust nanofibrous framework that further strengthens the hydrogel and improves water retention16. When integrated, MXenes, PVA, and TOCNF are expected to act synergistically. In brief, MXenes enhance conductivity and antibacterial performance, PVA contributes flexibility and processability, while TOCNF reinforces the polymer matrix and stabilizes the network14–16. Recent studies on MXene-nanocellulose composites have demonstrated advances in flexible electronics, energy devices, and conductive films17–20. In particular, demonstrating their multifunctional properties, bio-interfacing capability, and long-term operational stability17. Beyond MXene-specific efforts, the broader field of two-dimensional (2D) materials for sensor design has been extensively reviewed by Poushali and Ganguly18, which emphasized the importance of rational material integration strategies for achieving multifunctional performance. Despite these advances, the use of MXene-nanocellulose hybrids for wearable sensors under hot-humid and mechanically dynamic conditions remain largely unexplored.

Here, we report a MXene-based dual-network hydrogel composed of PVA and TOCNF crosslinked with MXene nanosheets and borax. The dual-network architecture, established through hydrogen bonding and dynamic borate–diol linkages, provides high mechanical resilience, long-term environmental stability, and reliable signal transduction. Leveraging the synergistic contributions of MXene, PVA, and TOCNF, the hydrogel exhibits stable performance under conditions simulating marathon running, while simultaneously offering antibacterial protection. As a proof of concept, the hydrogel sensor is applied for continuous heart rate and respiration monitoring, demonstrating its potential for durable wearable electronics in extreme environments.

Results and discussion

Structure design, composition characterization and Mechanical properties of the PBTM hydrogel

The synthesis procedures of PVA-Borax-TOCNF-MXene hydrogel and its potential applications for long-term biomedical and wearable sensors are illustrated in Fig. S1 and Fig. 1. To enhance the conductivity, MXene nanosheets are chosen as fundamental conductive components21,22. The hydrogel network is constructed from multiple nanomaterials, each contributing complementary functionalities that collectively define the mechanical, electrical, and biological properties of the system. Ti₃C₂Tₓ MXene, derived from the Ti₃AlC₂ MAX phase, was selected as the conductive nanofiller owing to its combination of high intrinsic conductivity and abundant surface terminations (-O, -OH, -F). These terminations enable strong hydrogen bonding and dynamic borate–diol coordination with PVA and TOCNF, thereby reinforcing the dual-network structure and establishing continuous electron pathways. Compared with other MXene compositions, Ti₃C₂Tₓ offers well-documented synthesis protocols, reproducibility, and scalability, making it a practical choice for large-scale hydrogel sensor fabrication. Such structural compatibility and electrochemical stability support the sensor’s reliable performance under hot–humid and mechanically dynamic conditions typical of endurance exercise. The colloidal stability of the Ti₃C₂Tₓ MXene suspension was further evaluated by zeta-potential analysis. The measured zeta-potential was –20.7 ± 0.5 mV (n = 3), which is sufficiently negative to provide electrostatic repulsion between nanosheets, thereby preventing aggregation and ensuring a homogeneous dispersion. Early studies demonstrated that surface terminations of MXene nanosheets impart intrinsic hydrophilicity and dispersion stability23, while more recent reports established that suspensions with zeta-potentials in the range of –15 to –35 mV are typically considered colloidally stable24. These results, together with the observed Tyndall scattering effect, confirm the stable aqueous dispersion of our prepared MXene suspension. Additional discussion and the zeta-potential measurement results are provided in Supporting Information (Table S1). PVA and TOCNF were used to construct the hydrogel network system, serving distinct yet complementary roles. Highly crystalline and tightly networked PVA elastomers, in conjunction with MXene nanosheets, provide structural support through hydrogen bonding to create an elastic layer that mimics the parallel arrangement of “aragonite plates” in natural stratified structures. However, the weak interactions between neighboring layers via relatively feeble hydrogen bonding or Van der Waals forces are unable to create a reliable interfacial connection25. To address the shortcoming, a simple yet universal complexation strategy was developed, where robust coordination bonds were employed between carboxyl groups and metal ions, to effectively achieve stable interlayer linkages15,26–28. Specifically, the plant-originated TOCNF16,29, exhibits a multitude of advantages: uniform microscale size (0.8–2.0 μm), exceptional mechanical properties, ease of modification, and abundant carboxyl groups resulting from the selective oxidation of C6 primary hydroxyl groups on cellulose. Therefore, the TOCNFs were selected as the multifunctional agents to intertwine MXene nanosheets, while acting as interlamellar cross-linkers. This role is enabled by the abundant carboxyl and hydroxyl groups of TOCNF, which establish hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with the surface terminations of MXene (-O, -OH, -F). Meanwhile, the nanofibrous morphology of TOCNF physically interpenetrates between the nanosheets, preventing their restacking and enhancing the integrity of the dual-network hydrogel. Additionally, borax was incorporated to establish dynamic borate bonds with hydroxyl groups in the system30 to form a dual network conductive hydrogel. The boric acid groups in borax dynamically and reversibly cross-link PVA and TOCNF via borate ester bonds, which enables the hydrogel with outstanding self-healing capabilities3,16.

Schematic illustration of the dual-network MXene hydrogel sensor with multifunctional properties for wearable cardiorespiratory monitoring under hot and humid conditions

Ultimately, the designed hydrogel was obtained by sequentially adding TOCNF, Borax, and MXene into the dissolved PVA solution under continuous stirring, yielding the final product named PBTM hydrogel. The concentration of MXene in the hydrogel is denoted as PBTM-X, such as PBTM-1 corresponding to 1 mL of MXene solution added. A collection of PBTM-X was prepared with detailed raw materials’ contents found in Table S2. This holistic design highlights the complementary and synergistic roles of all initial nanomaterials—PVA, TOCNF, MXene, and borax—in establishing a mechanically robust, conductive, self-healing hydrogel suitable for wearable physiological sensors, directly addressing the need for a systematic analysis of the material system.

The chemical structure of the hydrogel was characterized by FTIR, XRD, XPS and SEM to verify the integration of key components and the structure of the hydrogel. The FTIR spectra of PVA-TOCNF, PBTM-0, and PBTM-3 hydrogels are shown in Fig. 2a. PBTM-3 exhibited characteristic peaks at 3272 cm⁻¹ (O-H stretching from water in PVA), 2942 cm⁻¹ (C-H stretching from PVA’s methyl groups), 1640 cm⁻¹ (C = O stretching from PVA), 1413 cm⁻¹ (C-H bending from PVA’s methyl groups)31, 1331 cm⁻¹ (C-N stretching from TOCNF)32, and 1055 cm⁻¹ (C-O stretching from PVA). In addition, the peaks at 915 cm⁻¹ (B-O stretching from Borax)33 and 828 cm⁻¹ (a characteristic peak of MXene)34 confirmed the incorporation of borax and MXene in PBTM-3. In contrast, PBTM-0 exhibited the similar peaks except the characteristic peak of MXene at 828 cm⁻¹, while PVA-TOCNF showed no peaks at 915 cm⁻¹ and 828 cm⁻¹, confirming the absence of borax or MXene in PVA-TOCNF. Furthermore, the evidence of MXene in PBTM-3 hydrogel was also provided by XRD and XPS analyses indicate successful incorporation of MXene into the hydrogel matrix, with preserved borate crosslinking and minor surface chemical modifications. XRD patterns (Fig. 2S) reveal clear differences between PBTM-0 and PBTM-3. The peak centered at ~10° is attributed to the layered MXene, which confirmed its successful incorporation into the hydrogel matrix. Additionally, the slight shift and increased intensity around 30° suggest enhanced crystallinity, indicating that MXene promotes a more ordered polymer network35. The XPS analysis in Fig. 2d further confirms the incorporation of MXene in the hygrogel. In detail, the survey spectra of PBTM-3 reveal the presence of Ti (~455.2 eV) and F ( ~ 684.4 eV) peaks, which were absent in the pristine PVA-Borax-TOCNF hydrogel36,37. The C 1 s spectra in Fig. 2e, f show that the C-C/C-H ( ~ 284.2 eV) and C-O ( ~ 286.2 eV) peaks are largely preserved, with a slight reduction in the C-O peak intensity. The result suggests partial interaction between the hydroxyl groups of the polymer matrix and the MXene or borate species. The O 1 s spectra predominantly show the O–C bond at ~532.5 eV, with a minor reduction observed in PBTM-3 as shown in Fig. 2g, h, which is consistent with interfacial interactions between MXene and the polymer network. All the result is consistent with the XRD results, which indicated enhanced structural order in PBTM-3 upon the incorporation of MXene into the hydrogel. Furthermore, the B 1 s spectra in Fig. 2i, j demonstrate the presence of B-O-C ( ~ 191.2 eV) and a slight increase in B(OH)₃ (~193.0 eV). The result indicates the preservation of the borate crosslinking network and surface B enrichment enrichment38, which is able to contributes to the stability and mechanical strength of the hydrogel. Therefore, both XRD and XPS results confirm that the MXene has been successfully incorporated into the PBTM-3 hydrogel, which maintains the borate crosslinking network while induces minor surface chemical modifications. As a result, the overall structural order is improved. The SEM images (Fig. 2b) indicated that the PBTM-3 hydrogel has a porous structure, which is attributed to the introduction of MXene skeleton during synthesis. These results confirm the successful formation of a dual-crosslinked porous hydrogel network, which enhances mechanical integrity and promotes moisture diffusion, a critical factor for wearable strain sensing in dynamic and humid environments (Fig. 2k).

The incorporation of MXene nanosheets considerably influenced the mechanical performance of the dual-network hydrogel. As shown in Fig. 2c, Fig. S3, Fig. S4 and Table S3 increasing MXene loading enhanced tensile strength, Young’s modulus, and maximum force (Fmax), reflecting efficient stress transfer through hydrogen bonding and interfacial interactions between MXene and the polymer matrix. However, excessive MXene accumulation reduced stretchability, as dense nanosheet domains restricted chain mobility and created stress concentration sites. The optimized PBTM-3 composition achieved a favorable balance, exhibiting both robust tensile strength and high elongation (up to 800%), which is essential for electronic skin applications.

Beyond mechanical robustness, the hydrogel also demonstrated excellent self-healing ability. Cut PBTM-3 samples autonomously rejoined within 10 min under ambient conditions (Fig. S5a) and restored nearly full mechanical performance after 12 h (Fig. S5b and Table S4). The observed self-healing is driven by dynamic and reversible borate-diol coordination bonds and hydrogen bonding among hydroxyl-rich PVA/TOCNF chains, allowing spontaneous reorganization across damaged interfaces. Importantly, the interplay between MXene reinforcement and dynamic crosslinking governs the overall trade-off: moderate MXene loading enhances stress transfer while leaving sufficient reversible bonds for energy dissipation and reconfiguration, thereby maintaining self-healing efficiency. In contrast, excessive MXene disrupts this dynamic bond reorganization, diminishing the hydrogel’s ability to recover. Together, these mechanisms dictate the balance of strength, stretchability, and healing capacity, highlighting the importance of finely tuning MXene content to achieve multifunctional performance.

The PBTM hydrogel also presented strong self-adhesion, which is essential for skin-mounted strain sensors by eliminating the need for external adhesives. As shown in Fig. 2l, m, the hydrogel adhered firmly to various substrates, including through abundant hydrogen bonding interactions. Lap-shear tests (Fig. S6) revealed adhesive strengths of 3.68 kPa (Cu), 4.08 kPa (acrylic plates), 15.52 kPa (wood), and 14.30 kPa (non-woven fabrics) (Table S5). These values indicate sufficient adhesion for stable skin contact and reliable long-term physiological signal acquisition, providing a qualitative benchmark relative to other reported hydrogels. This suggests that the PBTM-3 hydrogel demonstrates comparable or superior adhesion, particularly on rough or porous substrates. Such strong interfacial interactions ensure stable contact with skin (Fig. 2n), maintain accurate signal acquisition during continuous deformation, and together with the hydrogel’s intrinsic biocompatibility, support reliable and long-term epidermal compatibility for cardiopulmonary monitoring.

The antibacterial properties of PBTM hydrogel

Since hydrogel sensors often provide a suitable environment for bacteria when sweating during exercise, directly adhering hydrogels to the skin is equivalent to introducing bacteria onto the skin surface, thereby posing a potential risk of bacterial infection39. Thus, the study of antimicrobial properties in hydrogel materials is essential. Antimicrobial materials can effectively prevent bacterial infections or inflammation on both normal skin and wounds. The MXene nanosheets have demonstrated antibacterial properties40. MXene nanosheets have been demonstrated to exhibit broad-spectrum antibacterial activity through multiple mechanisms, including physical disruption of bacterial membranes by their sharp nanosheet edges, generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that oxidize membrane lipids, proteins, and DNA, and electrostatic interactions between the negatively charged MXene surface and positively charged bacterial membranes41. In line with these findings, Papi et al. also explored the antibacterial effects of Ti₃C₂Tx MXenes42, demonstrating that both few- and multi-layer MXenes exhibit significant antibacterial properties, particularly under laser-induced conditions. The study highlighted how these MXenes enhanced antibacterial effects through a combination of physical disruption and ROS generation, mechanisms similar to those observed in our PBTM-3 hydrogel system. The incorporation of MXene into our hydrogel formulation similarly results in a substantial increase in antibacterial activity, as evidenced by the 100% antibacterial rate against both E. coli and B. subtilis. These findings are consistent with the strong antibacterial performance observed by Papi et al., further reinforcing the role of MXene in bacterial growth inhibition. In the PBTM-3 hydrogel, the uniform dispersion of MXene nanosheets within the polymer network maximizes bacterial contact and enhances these synergistic effects. To evaluate the bacteriostatic ability, MXene-based hydrogels were tested with Gram-negative bacteria E. coli and Gram-positive bacteria B. subtilis. Here, the hydrogels’ antibacterial efficacy was assessed by the flat colony counting method, and the antibacterial rate was calculated based on number of colonies43. The colony number is the colony count result of each group under the corresponding dilution ratio, following the national standard GB4789.2-2016, with the selected colony number between 30 CFU and 300 CFU for colony counting. Under the minimum dilution, samples showing no colony growth prove the strong antibacterial effect. The PBTM-3 hydrogel with MXene showed an antibacterial effect against both E. coli (Fig. 3b) and B. subtilis (Fig. 3e) bacteria, with a 100% antibacterial rate (Fig. 3c and f and Table S6). In contrast, the hydrogels without MXene showed poor antibacterial activity against Gram-negative bacteria E. coli (Fig. 3a) and Gram-positive bacteria B. subtilis (Fig. 3d). Beyond antibacterial activity (Fig. 3), the hydrogels exhibited moderate adhesion to diverse substrate, which ensures secure yet non-irritating skin contact. This combination of antibacterial functionality and suitable adhesion provides indirect evidence for potential long-term epidermal compatibility during continuous cardiopulmonary monitoring. Compared with literature-reported conductive hydrogels, the PBTM-3 hydrogel exhibits comparable or superior antibacterial efficacy40, demonstrating that MXene incorporation effectively mitigates bacterial proliferation risk without compromising skin comfort. Future biological studies could further consolidate this conclusion, particularly under prolonged exercise conditions.

Electrical properties, sensing performance and environment stability of PBTM hydrogel

Besides the mechanical properties, the hydrogel should exhibit high electrical conductivity to ensure that the hydrogel-based strain sensor can promptly respond to electrical signals generated from external stimuli. Figure 4a and Figure S7 shows the resistance measurements of the hydrogels with different MXene contents and different strains. It is observed that an initial decline in resistance with increasing MXene content and the PBPM-3 displays the minimum resistance of about 3 k Ω at the initial phase. However, further MXene addition (PBPM-4 and PBPM-5) increases the resistance of the hydrogels, due to the aggregation of MXene. Meanwhile, the resistance also rose proportionally with applied strain. Figure 4b and Table S7 show the GF analysis of the hydrogels with differing MXene contents and various strains. The increased MXene content enhanced the sensitivity of the hydrogels and the PBTM-3 reached the highest peak at GF = 7.79 (800% strain). Beyond the threshold, PBTM-4 and PBTM-5 show reversed changes in GF. MXene aggregation tends to impede electron transport, consequently lowering the electrical conductivity of the hydrogels in PBTM-4 and PBTM-55,14. As a result, PBTM-3 hydrogel was selected for further study in strain sensors. Furthermore, the PBTM-3 hydrogel was affixed to a finger (Fig. 4c) for movement detection. The relative resistance change ∆R/R0 exhibits distinct peaks and troughs corresponding to the bending angles. ∆R/R0 increases as the finger bends from 0° to 90° demonstrating the ability of hydrogel to accurately detect different levels of finger flexion. The clear and reproducible signals also are obtained from the finger’s flexion and extension movements further attested to the sensor’s reliable sensing performance. The results highlight the significant potential of hydrogel in monitoring joint flexion at injured sites, providing crucial clinical insights for guiding post-surgical rehabilitation and athletic recovery programmes.

Hydrogels with low water retention capabilities are prone to dehydration under various environments, resulting in unstable mechanical performance and thereby restricting the dependability and lifespan in practical applications44. Therefore, it is important for hydrogels to adapt to hot-humid environments to meet practical application requirements. Here, the water retention capacity of the PBTM hydrogels was evaluated through the gravimetric analysis. The stability of PBTM-3 hydrogel under high temperature and high humidity was investigated in a temperature and humidity regulation chamber, where the environment was heated using a blower and humidified through a humidifier, as shown in Fig. 4d. Simultaneously, temperature and humidity regulations were managed by a temperature sensor and a humidity sensor, respectively. At a condition of 38°C and 52% RH mimicking human exercise13, hydrogels were observed to retain over 94% of water content after exposure for 6 h (Fig. 4e), which surpasses both conventional synthetic hydrogels45,46 and bio-inspired hydrogel with moisturizing factors47. In addition, the hydrogel is still able to maintain the structural integrity after being 38°C and 52% RH for 6 h. Moreover, the resistance and GF change of PBTM-3 were investigated under 38°C and 52% RH, in response to different levels of strain (ΔL/L) over 6 h. The resistance and GF keep stable at different levels of strain due to excellent water retention (Fig. 4f, g). For the sensing performance under high temperature and humidity, the tests on finger bending movements are inside the temperature and humidity regulation chamber (Fig. 4h and Video 1, Supplemental Information), while the electrical response is exceptionally stable and consistent under 38 °C, 52% RH mimicking human exercise (Fig. 4j). We also investigate the performance of PBTM hydrogel under low temperatures, the hydrogels show excellent water retention capability (Figs. S8 and S9) and outstanding sensing capability (Fig. S10 and S11, Table S8) under low temperature. Owing to the environmental stability at various environments, the PBTM hydrogel could be reused several times when it maintains sufficient water highlights its applicability in living environments. Under hot–humid (38 °C, 52% RH) condition, the hydrogel maintained stable ΔR/R₀ signals during repeated cycling (Fig. 4j), confirming reliable charge transport under thermohygrometric stress. Together with tensile test results after self-healing (Fig. S5), these findings indicate that the reversible borate ester bonds and hydrogen bonding facilitate structural recovery after deformation, thereby supporting signal fidelity and mechanical adaptability. Further mechanistic studies could provide deeper insights into energy dissipation processes, but the present results already highlight the crucial role of dynamic crosslinking in sustaining sensing durability.

The environmental tolerance of the PBTM hydrogel was systematically investigated. SEM observations (Fig. 2b) confirmed that MXene nanosheets were uniformly distributed within the dual-network matrix, forming interconnected conductive pathways. This microstructural organization, combined with hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with TOCNFs and PVA, prevented nanosheet aggregation and supported stable charge transport. To further assess hydration stability, water-retention measurements were conducted under hot-humid condition (38 °C, 52% RH), revealing that the PBTM hydrogels maintained a high water content over extended periods (Fig. 4e). This hydration environment is critical for preserving ionic conductivity and mechanical compliance. Electrochemical stability tests showed that the hydrogel maintained consistent impedance and gauge factor values under hot-humid condition cycling (Fig. 4f, g). Moreover, the ΔR/R₀ responses remained stable across repeated stretching–relaxation cycles in hot-humid environment (Fig. 4j). These results demonstrate that the synergy between MXene microstructural organization and the intrinsic hydration capacity of the hydrogel preserves charge transport pathways and sensing fidelity under thermohygrometric stress. Such robustness provides a solid foundation for reliable long-term physiological monitoring, which is further validated in the following section.

To further illustrate the advantages of this hydrogel, its overall performance was compared with previously reported materials (Table S9 and S10) and evaluated by 6 key indicators (Fig. 4i). Comparative analysis results highlighted the dual-network hydrogel’s superior sensitivity, environmental adaptability, adhesion, water retention, antibacterial ability and application for physiological signal, positioning it as a versatile solution for biomedical and wearable applications. This comprehensive performance under different conditions also supports its suitability for challenging real-world applications.

As summarized in Table 1, all the experimental results demonstrate that the PBTM hydrogel is insensitive to different environments, because the dual network structure ensures the stable water retention of hydrogel for consistent electrical signal output. The environmental stability of hydrogel positions the sensor as a promising candidate for Arctic exploration, cryogenic medical monitoring, industrial robotics, and real-time health tracking in athletes.

Demonstration as sensors for physiological signal monitoring

Building on the demonstrated environmental tolerance, the PBTM hydrogel sensor—characterized by excellent mechanical robustness, reliable sensing performance, and sustained stability under hot and humid conditions—was further evaluated for real-world physiological monitoring. Such robustness ensures consistent performance during physical activity, where temperature fluctuations and perspiration can otherwise compromise sensor reliability. Moreover, the pulse is considered a vital physiological indicator, capable of providing essential medical insights into heart rate and the state of the vascular system48,49. To assess the sensor’s real-time monitoring capability, we conducted a simultaneous measurement of carotid artery pulse and respiratory motion during exercise using a hydrogel-based sensor. As shown in Fig. 5a, the hydrogel-based sensor was affixed to the carotid artery for pulse monitoring, while a smartwatch was used on the wrist for comparison. Figure 5b shows the simultaneously recorded resistance response to the carotid artery at rest and during elliptical exercise at three intensity levels, namely slow, medium, and fast, the corresponding HR (heart rate) was calculated as 82, 96, 118, and 125 beats per minute (bpm) by counting the number of R peaks in 1 min. The HR signal obtained by the PBTM hydrogel sensor is comparable to that obtained from the smartwatch (Table S11). To monitor respiratory activity, the sensor was placed on a volunteer’s chest. By analyzing the peaks of relative resistance response, RR (respiratory rate) of about 22, 26, 33 and 39 times are recorded at rest and during elliptical exercise at slow, medium, and fast speed respectively (Fig. 5c). Both HR and RR exhibited a consistent upward trend as exercise intensity increased, demonstrating a clear cardiorespiratory coupling that reflects the body’s coordinated response to higher metabolic demands.

Wearable cardiorespiratory monitoring using PBTM-3 hydrogel sensors.

a Illustration of PBTM-3 hydrogel sensor on carotid artery during elliptical exercise. PBTM-3 hydrogel sensor for right carotid artery (b), respiration (c) for monitoring the sensing performance

This observed trend aligns well with established physiological principles. According to Hoogeveen et al., HR exhibits a near-linear relationship with oxygen uptake and correlates strongly with endurance performance, particularly at the ventilatory threshold (VT) (r = 0.93) s50. In contrast, RR displays a nonlinear response, remaining relatively stable at lower intensities but increasing sharply near metabolic thresholds such as VT1 and VT2. Nabetani et al. reported that the inflection point in RR closely coincides with VT (r = 0.88–0.96), supporting RR as a sensitive marker of ventilatory transitions51. This characteristic is reflected in our data, the heart rate to respiratory rate ratio (HR/RR) decreased slightly from ~3.69 at rest to ~3.21 at fast speed (Table S12), indicating RR increases at a relatively greater rate than HR under higher workloads. According to established clinical guidelines, heart rate of 120-150 bpm and respiratory rates of 35–45 respirations per minute (rpm) are typical during vigorous exercise in healthy adults52,53. However, if the respiratory rate becomes disproportionately elevated with >40 rpm at moderate heart rates (~118 bpm), this may signal respiratory inefficiency, poor cardiorespiratory coordination, or early metabolic acidosis54. Furthermore, Contreras-Briceño et al. confirmed that both HR and RR can be used to accurately predict VT1 and VT2 using wearable sensors, with correlation values exceeding r = 0.88 for RR and r = 0.75–0.88 for HR across different thresholds55. Importantly, RR has been proposed as a sensitive marker of real-time psychophysiological strain. Nicolò et al. emphasized that RR not only reflects metabolic transitions but also rapidly responds to acute changes in workload, fatigue, thermal load, and perceived exertion53.

These findings highlight the critical value of the PBTM hydrogel sensor capturing both linear cardiovascular response (via HR) and the nonlinear ventilatory compensation (via RR). The observed HR/RR dynamics align well with theoretical models, confirming the sensor’s effectiveness in real-world conditions. This integrated monitoring capability offer an early warning system to help detect potential cardiovascular or respiratory risks during endurance sports such as marathons, where undetected abnormalities can lead to serious events56. Therefore, the PBTM hydrogel sensor presents a robust and efficient platform for real-time physiological monitoring in sports. In addition to exercise-related applications, this sensor also demonstrates promising capabilities in a wide range of human motion monitoring scenarios, including facial expression detection, speech recognition, and handwriting tracking (Fig. S12).

Conclusion

A high-performance MXene-based PVA/Borax/TOCNF hydrogel with excellent mechanical properties, environmental stability, and sensing capability, was developed based on a dual-network structure design. The dual-network design effectively minimizes water evaporation under harsh conditions (water retention reached 94% at 38 °C & 52% RH for 6 h), ensuring stable operation in hot and humid environments. Also, the hydrogel exhibits exceptional elasticity and a low Young’s modulus, making it an ideal candidate for electronic skin applications. Moreover, the introduction of MXene endows the hydrogel with high conductivity and 100% antibacterial activity against E. coli and B. subtilis. Benefiting from reliable self-healing ability and harsh condition tolerance, the hydrogel can be functioned as a sensitive epidermal sensor capable of detecting subtle physiological signals, such as pulse and respiration. As a demonstration, the hydrogel is used for real-time monitoring of cardiovascular or respiratory during physical activity, where continuous assessment of cardiorespiratory indicators can help prevent exercise-induced risks. This work presents a promising strategy for the development of advanced hydrogel-based on-skin sensors, offering great potential for real-time healthcare monitoring in athletic and wearable applications.

Material

Commercial Ti3AlC2 MAX phase (98% purity, 400 mesh) powders (molecular weight: 194.80) were purchased from Jilin 11 Technology Co., Ltd. (2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidin-1-yl) oxyl (TEMPO)-oxidized cellulose nanofibrils (TOCNF) (coniferous wood, colloid concentration: 1.2%, and length: 0.5-1.0 μm) were purchased from Tianjin University of Science and Technology. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA AR 1799) and di-Sodium tetraborate (Borax) (99.5% purity, 100 mesh) were purchased from Rhawn Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd. All other chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Preparation of MXene

2 g of LiF was dissolved in 40 ml 9 M HCl in a Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) container and the mixture was stirred for 30 min. Subsequently, 2 g of Ti3AlC2 powder was gradually added into the solution and allowed to react for 24 h at 35°C under continuous stirring. The obtained slurry was repeatedly washed with deionized (DI) water and centrifuged at 3500 revolutions per minute until the pH reached 5. At this pH, the MXene precipitate was redissolved in DI water, with ultrasonication for 10 min, and then collected by centrifugation at 3500 revolutions per minute for 5 min. The supernatant from the final centrifugation step was utilized in all subsequent procedures to ensure the full exfoliation of MXene. The concentration of MXene solution is 10 mg/mL.

Preparation of hydrogel

Firstly, an amount of 0.5 g of PVA particles was added to 5 mL of DI water and stirred continuously with a magnetic stirrer. The mixture was then vigorously stirred at 90°C until the PVA was completely dissolved. After cooling the above solution to room temperature, 4 g of TOCNF was added and stirred for 60 min to ensure homogeneous mixing. Then, 1 mL MXene solution and 4 mL DI water were added to the mixture, followed by 30 min of stirring and 30 min of ultrasonication, respectively. Subsequently, 0.5 mL borax solution (50 mg/mL) was introduced into the PVA-TOCNF and MXene mixture to obtain a stretchable and self-healing hydrogel. The hydrogel was finally transferred into a mold for further processing.

Characterization and mechanical performance testing

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed using a ZEISS GeminiSEM 300 to analyze the microstructural morphology of the samples. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometry was conducted using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iS20 with the samples mixed with KBr from 4000 to 50 cm−1. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained with a Rigaku SmartLab SE using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54056 Å). Measurements were conducted over a 2θ range of 5–90° with a step size of 2° and a scan duration of 1 min per step. The zeta-potential was measured three times under identical conditions (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90, 25 °C, aqueous suspension, ~0.5 mg/mL). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed by Thermo Scientific K-Alpha system with monochromatic Al Kα radiation. The binding energy was referenced to the C 1 s line at 284.6 eV from adventitious carbon. The spot size was 400 μm, the operating voltage was 12 kV, and the filament current was 6 mA. For the survey scan, the pass energy was 150 eV with a step size of 1 eV. For the narrow scan, the pass energy was 50 eV with a step size of 0.1 eV. Shirley background subtraction was applied for background correction, and Lorentzian-Gaussian mixed fitting was used for peak analysis.
