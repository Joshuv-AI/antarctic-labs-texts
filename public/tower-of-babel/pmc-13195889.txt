Proteomic analysis in Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias: a focus on sex-specific differences
Authors: Aina Comas-Albertí, Albert Lladó, Diana Esteller-Gauxax, Sergi Borrego-Écija, Neus Falgàs, Farida Dakterzada, Agnès Pérez-Millan, Roger Puey, Tània Collet-Romà, Núria Guillén, Miquel Massons, Adrià Tort-Merino, Josep Maria Augé, Guadalupe Fernandez-Villullas, Bea Bosch, Raquel Ruiz-García, Laura Naranjo, Mircea Balasa, Gerard Piñol-Ripoll, Anna Antonell, Raquel Sánchez-Valle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFluid protein studies in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and plasma have provided important insights into neurodegenerative dementias; however, there is a limited investigation of sex-related differences and cross-biofluid relationships. In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Lewy body dementia (LBD), and frontotemporal dementia (FTD), large-scale, sex-stratified analyses of paired CSF and plasma samples remain scarce. Using the multiplex and ultrasensitive capabilities of NULISAseq™ technology, this study aims to characterize sex- and disease-specific proteomic alterations associated with Central Nervous System (CNS) pathology to explore underlying mechanisms.MethodsCSF and plasma samples from 359 individuals with AD, LBD, FTD, and cognitively healthy controls were analyzed using the NULISAseq™ CNS Disease Panel 120. Differential protein expression analyses were conducted across diagnoses and stratified by sex, adjusting for relevant covariates. Spearman’s correlation analyses were performed to assess concordance between CSF and plasma protein levels. All statistical analyses were conducted in R v4.4.3.ResultsDifferential protein expression analyses across diagnoses revealed two potential transdiagnostic biomarkers: ICAM1 in CSF and ANXA5 in plasma, showing consistent increases across AD, LBD, and FTD. Sex-stratified analyses in CSF showed modest changes, including higher CCL26, ANXA5, and IL10 in females with AD, and higher IL9, PRDX6, and CX3CL1 in males with AD. In LBD, females exhibited upregulation of ACHE, SFRP1, POSTN in both CSF and plasma. NPTX1 was identified as a potential CSF biomarker for FTD, showing downregulation particularly in males. In contrast, analyses stratified by sex in plasma displayed a larger number of proteins across all dementias, with females showing a higher number of upregulated inflammation-related proteins predominantly involved in cytokine signaling. Overall cross-fluid correlations were restricted to a small subset of proteins, indicating compartment-specific regulation.ConclusionsThis study represents a large-scale, sex-stratified proteomic analysis of CSF and plasma across major neurodegenerative dementias using NULISAseq™ technology. The findings highlight sex-dependent biomarker patterns, particularly in plasma, and underscore the importance of incorporating sex as a biological variable in dementia research. Future studies should validate candidate proteins in independent cohorts, investigate their functional and mechanistic roles, and assess their utility for biomarker development and sex-tailored therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-026-02068-7.

Background

Neurodegenerative dementias represent a major global health burden, with prevalence expected to rise dramatically as the population ages [1]. Among these, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Lewy body dementia (LBD), and frontotemporal dementia (FTD) together account for the majority of cases [2], yet the biological mechanisms underlying sex differences in disease prevalence remain not fully understood. Increasing evidence indicates that sex is a critical biological variable influencing disease susceptibility, progression, and clinical phenotypes, as well as response to therapeutic interventions [3]. Recently, a review integrated imaging and single-biofluid biomarker evidence to summarize sex differences in neurodegenerative dementias [4]. Proteomic analyses can offer a promising approach to identify sex-specific molecular signatures that may contribute to the prevalence differences.

AD is the most common form of dementia, accounting for 60–70% of cases worldwide. Its prevalence is consistently higher in females, even after adjusting for longer life expectancy and other potential confounders, suggesting that biological factors beyond longevity contribute to disease vulnerability [5, 6]. Sex differences have been observed in cognitive trajectories, structural and functional neuroimaging measures [7], and in inflammatory and synaptic protein profiles [8, 9]. This growing evidence collectively highlight that sex-related mechanisms are integral to AD heterogeneity and should be incorporated into disease modeling, biomarker discovery, and therapy development.

LBD, the second most common degenerative dementia [10], shows a male predominance [11, 12], and its clinical presentation differs between sexes: women are less likely to exhibit hallmark motor and behavioral symptoms, contributing to higher underdiagnosis rates [12, 13]. Men, in turn, tend to show more parkinsonism and REM sleep behavior disorder, whereas women often experience more severe cognitive impairment [14]. However, sex-specific fluid biomarker data in LBD are scarce, and their implications for pathogenesis and disease heterogeneity remain largely unexplored.

FTD comprises a heterogeneous spectrum of clinical and pathological entities, typically presenting at younger ages and imposing substantial social and economic burden [15]. Epidemiological studies suggest a higher risk of certain subtypes – particularly the behavioral variant (bvFTD) – in men [16, 17]. Moreover, genetic forms involving MAPT, GRN, and C9orf72 mutations, may exhibit sex-related modulation in penetrance and clinical expression [18]. Nevertheless, findings remain inconsistent, and systematic studies of sex-dependent proteomic profiles in FTD are lacking.

Despite growing recognition of sex as a determinant of neurodegenerative disease biology, few studies have assessed sex-specific proteomic alterations across major dementias. Most available work has focused on AD brain tissue [19–21], while LBD and FTD remain underrepresented in works with sex-stratified analyses. Moreover, comparisons between cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and plasma proteomes in relation to sex are largely absent, leaving a critical gap in understanding interactions between peripheral and central nervous system (CNS) processes interact across neurodegenerative diseases.

Recent advances in ultrasensitive proteomic technologies now enable comprehensive molecular profiling of CNS pathology. The Nucleic acid Linked Immuno-Sandwich Assay with next-generation sequencing (NULISAseq™) platform provides high-sensitivity multiplex quantification of low-abundance proteins in biofluids, with superior analytical performance and dynamic range compared to conventional immunoassays [22]. Previous studies have primarily benchmarked NULISAseq™ in AD, demonstrating robust quantification of established biomarkers —including phosphorylated tau protein at residue 217 (p-Tau217), phosphorylated tau protein at residue 181 (p-Tau181), total tau, amyloid-beta 42 (Aβ42), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) [23]— showing strong agreement with measurements obtained using standard assays, as well as with longitudinal tau- and Aβ-PET changes [24–26]. Other studies have included additional dementias such as FTD or LBD; however, most have focused on a single biofluid [27–29].

We simultaneously profiled CNS-related proteins in both CSF and plasma from participants with AD, LBD, FTD, and healthy controls (HC). Our goal was to characterize sex-specific proteomic alterations across these three diseases, with particular emphasis on AD, where sex prevalence differences are most evident and yet incompletely understood. To our knowledge, this represents one of the firsts large-scale, sex-stratified NULISAseq™ analyses across three neurodegenerative dementias in paired CSF and plasma samples, providing novel insight into how sex shapes molecular mechanisms in each disease.

Methods

Participants

The study cohort comprised 359 participants classified into AD, LBD, FTD and HC groups, recruited from the Alzheimer’s Disease and other Cognitive Disorders Unit, at Hospital Clínic de Barcelona (N = 313) and the Biomedical Research Institute of Lleida (IRBLleida) (N = 46, including 26 HC and 20 AD participants).

Only participants aged over 50 years were included to homogenize the cohorts. Patients with sporadic amnestic AD were included if they met clinical diagnostic criteria for mild cognitive impairment due to AD or mild AD dementia [30], supported by an AD-consistent CSF biomarker profile [31]. Individuals with atypical clinical presentations (e.g., primary progressive aphasia) or autosomal dominant AD were excluded to ensure etiological homogeneity. The LBD group fulfilled established clinical diagnostic criteria for dementia with Lewy bodies [32] and was required to have a CSF positive alpha-synuclein RT-QuIC assay and a negative p-Tau181 biomarker for AD (< 65 pg/mL). The latter criterion was applied to minimize potential co-pathology with AD. The FTD group comprised exclusively patients meeting clinical criteria for the sporadic bvFTD [33], selected to reduce heterogeneity within the cohort. The HC group included cognitively unimpaired individuals aged over 50 years with normal performance on a comprehensive neuropsychological battery and normal CSF AD biomarker levels.

Sample collection

CSF samples were obtained by lumbar puncture and collected into polypropylene tubes, while plasma samples were obtained by venipuncture into EDTA tubes. All samples were centrifuged within two hours after collection at 4 °C for 10 min at 2000×g, except for those from IRB Lleida, which were processed at room temperature for 10 min at 1000×g for CSF and 1500×g for plasma. Samples were stored at − 80 °C in polypropylene tubes until further use, with a maximum storage time of 10 years after collection. All samples underwent a single freeze–thaw cycle.

After storage, all samples were processed and analyzed using the same standardized workflow, minimizing center-related variability.

NULISAseq™ assay

Plasma and CSF samples were analyzed using the NULISAseq™ CNS Disease Panel 120 (Alamar Biosciences, Fremont, CA, USA) [22], which targets ~ 120 proteins related to neurodegeneration, inflammation, and immune response. From each sample, 10–20 µL of CSF or plasma was plated in 96-well plates. Samples were randomized within each fluid type and distributed in 4 CSF plates and 4 plasma plates. Each plate included assay controls: internal sample controls (2 replicates/plate), inter-plate controls (IPC; 3 replicates/plate), and negative controls (2–3 replicates/plate).

NULISAseq™ is a highly sensitive proximity ligation assay employing DNA-barcoded antibodies and a dual capture-release mechanism to enhance sensitivity and reduce background. Performed on Alamar’s automated ARGO™ system, immunocomplexes were captured, released, and recaptured on magnetic beads before ligation to generate DNA reporters with sample- and target-specific barcodes. These reporters were pooled, PCR-amplified, and sequenced on an instrument Biosciences AVITI.

Normalization was conducted in two steps. First, intraplate normalization was performed by dividing each sample’s target counts by its internal control counts. Second, interplate normalization was applied by dividing the normalized values by the target-specific median of the IPC wells on each plate. The normalized data were then rescaled, a pseudocount of 1 was added, and values were log₂-transformed to yield NULISA Protein Quantification (NPQ) units, used in all downstream statistical analyses.

Fold changes between groups were calculated as 2^(ΔNPQ). Assay reproducibility was evaluated using intra- and inter-plate coefficients of variation based on the internal sample controls. Limits of detection (LOD) were defined for each target and plate as the mean plus three standard deviations of the unlogged normalized counts from the negative controls, followed by log₂ transformation. A target was considered detectable if more than 50% of the samples showed a signal above the LOD. It is generally recommended to apply a 50% detectability threshold within each plate as a criterion for inclusion in downstream analyses. For each biofluid, four plates were analyzed, and some targets were excluded completely (if LOD < 50% in 3 or 4 plates) or partially (if LOD < 50% in 1 or 2 plates).

Statistical analysis

Differential protein expression was evaluated with multivariant linear regression models implemented in the limma package (Bioconductor), which apply an empirical Bayes-moderated t-test. All analyses were adjusted by age. In addition, comparisons involving AD were further adjusted by APOEε4 allele presence. The comparison between LBD and HC was additionally adjusted for sex because the sex distribution between the two groups was significantly unbalanced. To adjust for multiple comparisons, false discovery rate (FDR) corrections were applied to each group-wise comparison. Proteins were considered differentially expressed when the absolute log2 fold change (log2 FC) was ≥ 0.5 and the adjusted p-value < 0.05. All p-values reported in this study are FDR-adjusted unless otherwise stated.

Sex-stratified analyses were subsequently conducted following the same modelling strategy and adjustment approach. Comparisons were conducted separately for females (patients vs. HC) and males (patients vs. HC). In these analyses, where adjustment for sex is not applicable, the use of limma inherently stabilizes variance estimates and improves statistical reliability in the comparisons with a small sample size. Then Venn Diagrams were performed to assess the similarities and differences between females and males for each disease.

We subsequently examined selected significantly altered proteins to assess differences between diagnostic groups using a multivariant linear regression model, followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons with the emmeans package in R. These models were adjusted for age, sex, and APOEε4 allele presence, and FDR correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons. Individual NULISAseq™ markers were visualized using boxplots to independently assess their diagnostic performance.

To evaluate the concordance between NULISAseq™ and established immunoassay platforms, we compared the concentrations of two established neurodegeneration biomarkers —light-chain neurofilaments (NEFL), and p-Tau217— measured using NULISAseq™ (NPQ values), Lumipulse (for plasma p-Tau217 and NEFL, pg/ml), and/or SIMOA (for CSF NEFL; pg/ml). Spearman correlation analyses were performed to assess agreement between NULISAseq™ and the conventional platforms. Correlation models were fitted across all diagnostic groups combined to demonstrate the overall analytical concordance and global biomarker performance.

To assess the correlation between NPQ values and CSF and plasma biomarkers, Spearman correlation analyses were performed adjusting for age, sex and APOEε4 allele presence, and using FDR for multiple testing correction.

All analyses were performed in R (https://www.r-project.org) statistical software v4.4.3.

Pathway classification of proteins showing sex‑specific differential expression

To biologically contextualize the proteins that were uniquely differentially expressed in one sex in the sex-stratified comparisons, we classified them according to the predefined pathway categories of the NULISAseq CNS Disease 120 Panel (Alamar Biosciences). The panel groups approximately 120 proteins into eight major biological pathways relevant to central nervous system diseases: Aberrant proteostasis, Altered Energy Homeostasis, Cytoskeletal Abnormalities, DNA and/or RNA defects, Inflammation, Pathological Protein Aggregation, Synaptic and Neuronal Network Defects and Neuronal Cell Death, as described by the manufacturer (https://alamarbio.com/nulisaseq-cns-disease-panel/) and based in literature [34].

The plasma cohort included 335 participants: 191 patients with AD, 34 with LBD, 46 with bvFTD, and 64 HC. Paired CSF-plasma samples were available for 97.6% of AD, 84.2% of LBD, 88.2% of FTD, and 64.1% of HC participants.

Quality control

Rigorous quality control procedures were applied to ensure the reliability of the proteomic data. Five CSF and four plasma samples were excluded based on poor detectability and suboptimal quality metrics.

Protein targets were excluded when their detectability or reproducibility fell below predefined thresholds. Specifically, NEFH and Ab38 were removed in both fluids because of high inter-plate coefficient of variation, and APOE4 was excluded since it served solely for genotyping purposes.

In CSF, 19 additional targets showed insufficient detectability across most plates and were therefore excluded (BDNF, GDNF, HBA1, IL17A, IL1B, IL2, IL4, MME, Oligo-SNCA, PDLIM5, pTDP43-409, RUVBL2, S100A12, UBB, YWHAZ, SAA1, VGF, BASP1, and IL33), with a detectability of 83.5%. A few targets failing QC on one or two of the four plates were excluded only from those plates (ARSA, CCL22, pSNCA-129, TNF, NGF, REST, CCL11, CCL26, pSNCA-129, VEGFD, FGF2, UCHL1, and YWHAG). The final number of samples analyzed for these targets is reported in Table S1 (Additional File 1).

In plasma, UCHL1, GDI1, and SNCB were excluded because they showed low detectability across at least three of the four plates, giving a detectability of 97.6%. Additional targets that failed QC on a single plate (UBB, YWHAG, BASP1 and YWHAG) were excluded only from the affected one. The final number of samples analyzed for these targets is reported in Table S1 (Additional File 1).

Differential protein levels analysis

Single full-disease cohort vs. healthy controls comparisons

In CSF, differential expression analyses identified proteins significantly dysregulated relative to HC: 45 in AD, 41 in LBD, and 7 in FTD. Most proteins were upregulated compared with HC, except for NPTX1, NPTX2 and NPTXR (logFC = -0.589, p = 1.29 × 10⁻³; logFC = -0.926, p = 7.92 × 10⁻⁷; logFC = -0.651, p = 7.92 × 10⁻⁷), which were downregulated in FTD. GFAP, ICAM1, and NEFL were consistently elevated across all groups, while AD and LBD showed extensive overlap in their CSF proteomic profiles (Fig. 1A). In plasma, differentially expressed proteins compared with HC were 11 in AD, 13 in LBD, and 16 in FTD. Like in CSF, most proteins were upregulated, except for Hemoglobin subunit alpha 1 (HBA1), which was decreased in AD (logFC = -1.303, p = 4.22 × 10⁻⁴) and FTD (logFC = -0.997, p = 4.25 × 10⁻²). All p-values have been FDR-corrected. The overlap between dysregulated plasma protein signatures is shown in Fig. 1B. Annexin A5 (ANXA5) and NEFL were commonly upregulated across all three dementia groups.

Sex-stratified analyses in Alzheimer’s disease

In CSF, the comparison between female AD (fAD) and female HC (fHC) identified 47 differentially expressed proteins (Fig. 2A), whereas the male comparison revealed 46 (Fig. 2B), with 42 dysregulated proteins shared between sexes (Fig. 2C). Female-specific proteins showing the largest effects included CCL26 (logFC = 0.977, p = 1.10 × 10⁻²), ANXA5 (logFC = 0.700, p = 5.00 × 10⁻³), CXCL8 (logFC = 0.609, p = 1.46 × 10⁻⁵) and IL10 (logFC = 0.607, p = 1.32 × 10⁻3). In male AD (mAD) versus male HC (mHC) comparison, the strongest specific differences were observed for IL9 (logFC = 0.781, p = 6.98 × 10⁻⁵), PRDX6 (logFC = 0.575, p = 1.01 × 10⁻³), and CX3CL1 (logFC = 0.546, p = 1.01 × 10⁻³).

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the levels of central nervous system disease related proteins from 359 individuals, including patients with AD, LBD, and FTD, as well as HC, using the NULISAseq™ platform, to explore at disease-wide and sex-stratified levels the molecular signatures within each diagnostic group and in two biofluids. Our analysis revealed differentially expressed proteins in the full-disease cohort and the sex-stratified analyses, in both CSF and plasma, with females generally showing in plasma a higher number of inflammation-related proteins overexpressed compared to HC.

In the CSF analysis of differential protein levels within the unstratified full-disease cohort, ICAM1 emerged as a promising candidate biomarker for neurodegeneration, alongside well-established markers, as it was significantly upregulated across all three diseases compared to HC. ICAM1 is an adhesion protein expressed on endothelial cells that becomes upregulated during neurovascular inflammation. Elevated ICAM1 facilitates leukocyte adhesion and migration across the blood–brain barrier, thereby amplifying local inflammatory responses and astrogliosis [35]. Consistently, another study using the NULISAseq™ platform also reported increased ICAM1 levels in neurodegeneration, further supporting our findings [25].

In addition, the CSF differential protein analysis at the disease-wide level revealed a substantial overlap in proteins dysregulated in both AD and LBD compared with HC. Notably, several immune-related proteins were consistently upregulated across both conditions, supporting shared pathophysiological mechanisms involving the immune system [36], particularly chemokine signaling and cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, which have previously been implicated in neurodegenerative disease progression [37, 38]. Regarding FTD patients, the overall number of differentially expressed proteins was smaller, with NPTX1, NPTX2, and NPTXR distinguishing FTD from AD and LBD. Previous studies have reported reduced levels of NPTX1, NPTX2, and NPTXR in the CSF of individuals with different FTD subtypes [39, 40], in line with our findings, and decreases in NPTX1 and NPTX2 have also been described in AD [41]. These two proteins modulate neuroimmune interactions implicated in tau-mediated neurodegeneration and are considered biomarkers of excitatory synaptic pathology [41].

In the unstratified full-disease cohort plasma analysis, we identified ANXA5 as an upregulated protein across all three diseases relative to controls, pointing to its potential as a transdiagnostic peripheral biomarker. ANXA5 is a protein involved in neuronal cell death, and elevated ANXA5 levels are characteristic of microvesicles exposing phosphatidylserine on their surface [42], a process typically associated with cellular stress, apoptosis and inflammatory activation [43–45]. It has previously been reported to be overexpressed in extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from AD brain tissue [46], in EVs from the CSF of amyloid-β-treated rats [43], and in plasma from patients with AD and LBD [47], underscoring its involvement in disease-related mechanisms and supporting our results.

Analyses stratified by sex identified subtle differences in protein expression profiles across AD, LBD, and FTD.

CSF stratified by sex analyses in AD revealed that many proteins were similarly increased in both females and males compared to HC, suggesting that most pathological processes are shared across sexes. Yet, some proteins were specific to each sex-stratified comparison: (a) in mAD vs. mHC comparison, we found upregulation of PRDX6, FLT1, CX3CL1, and IL9. PRDX6 contributes to antioxidant defense and protection against oxidative stress [48]; FLT1 (VEGFR1) mediates vascular remodeling and endothelial activation [49]; CX3CL1 (fractalkine) regulates neuron–microglia communication and microglial homeostasis [50]; and IL9 participates in Th9-type immune regulation, modulating the balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory responses in the CNS [51]. Together, these changes may reflect compensatory and homeostatic neuroimmune responses in males. (b) in fAD vs. fHC comparison, we found upregulation of IL10, ANXA5, CCL26, PGF, and CXCL8. IL10 is a key anti-inflammatory cytokine that limits excessive immune activation [52, 53]. Notably, a recent study reported enhanced IL10 signaling in microglia and astrocytes in female AD brains compared with female controls [54], supporting the presence of a sex-specific immune biomarker in AD. CCL26 (eotaxin-3) and CXCL8 (IL8) are proinflammatory chemokines promoting leukocyte recruitment and endothelial activation [55], and their levels have been reported to be increased in AD CSF and serum [56], and in plasma, respectively [24]. Finally, PGF enhances angiogenesis and vascular inflammation through FLT1 signaling and has been proposed as a candidate marker of cerebrovascular injury in AD [57].

Collectively, these findings suggest that males may predominantly activate neuroprotective and redox-regulating pathways, whereas females show stronger immune and vascular signaling, reflecting distinct sex-dependent mechanisms in the early clinical stages of AD pathophysiology. These observations are consistent with a recently published proteomics study reporting marked sex-specific pathway engagement in AD [58]. They described a predominant involvement of male-specific proteins in innate immune activation in CSF, supporting the concept of an early immune-driven response aimed at restoring homeostasis in the face of pathological stimuli. Conversely, female-specific proteins in this study were mainly associated with dysregulation of protein metabolic processes.

Sex-stratified plasma analyses in AD revealed a markedly higher number of upregulated proteins in females than in males’ comparison, mainly involved in inflammation, pathological protein aggregation and synaptic/neuronal network defects pathways. A point worth highlighting is that ANXA5 protein arised in both CSF and plasma fAD vs. fHC comparisons, indicating that apart from their potential as transdiagnostic biomarker is also a protein that may present subtle differences in protein levels between sexes.

Another finding was for HBA1 protein, which was uniquely downregulated in mAD vs. mHC comparison. Hemoglobin-related proteins such as HBA1 have been reported to be altered in the brains of patients with AD, showing a reduced expression in neurons from affected cortical regions [59]. Consistent with these observations, alterations in hemoglobin-related pathways may reflect disruptions in oxygen metabolism associated with neurodegenerative processes [60].

Analyses stratified by sex in LBD showed a greater number of differentially expressed proteins in the females (fLBD) comparison than in the males’ one, in both CSF and plasma, many of which being involved in cytokine and interleukin signaling pathways of the immune system. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory, given the limited sample size of the fLBD subgroup (N = 7 in plasma, N = 8 in CSF), which reduces statistical power and precision.

In the CSF fLBD vs. fHC comparison, we identified three inflammation-related proteins (CXCL8, IL10, and PGF), that overlapped with the fAD vs. fHC comparison, supporting the possibility that some biological pathways may be shared across both conditions. In the plasma comparisons, we found higher GFAP levels in the fLBD vs. fHC comparison, but not in the mLBD vs. mHC one. Interestingly, this observation is consistent with a recent study reporting significantly higher plasma GFAP levels in women than in men in both LBD and AD, suggesting enhanced astroglial activation in females in these dementias [61]. In our study, however, we observed increased plasma GFAP levels relative to healthy controls in both males and females from AD cohort, suggesting a disease-specific dysregulation in AD.

In LBD, we identified upregulation of several differentially expressed proteins, in both CSF and plasma, in the fLBD vs. fHC sex-stratified comparison: POSTN, ACHE and SFRP1. While POSTN was also detected in the unstratified full-disease cohort analysis, its identification in the sex-stratified context may suggest potential sex-related differences that require further validation. POSTN protein is involved in extracellular matrix remodeling and neuroinflammatory responses, and previous studies showed that elevated POSTN levels in plasma are associated with cognitive decline in aging populations [62].

Furthermore, SFRP1 protein has been shown to cause hippocampal synaptic dysfunction and memory impairment [63]. Mechanistically, SFRP1 acts as a mediator that amplifies glial signaling, contributing to the detrimental effects of chronic neuroinflammation in neurodegenerative disorders [64], and its levels are significantly elevated in the CSF and brain tissue of AD patients [65], and preclinical models suggest a more pronounced upregulation in females [66]. Although SFRP1 has not been explored in LBD yet, these observations raise the possibility of a sex-dependent contribution that warrants further investigation, while acknowledging the exploratory nature of the current findings.

In addition, ACHE, a protein expressed by microglia and functions hydrolyzing acetylcholine, has been implicated in the cholinergic deficit characteristic of LBD. Its upregulation may reflect inflammatory activation [67]. Given that ACHE inhibition is being explored as a therapeutic strategy for LBD [68], and that our preliminary results showed increased ACHE levels in fLBD in both fluids, sex differences should be considered in future treatment approaches. However, due to the limited sample size, these results should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger cohorts.

In FTD, the sex-stratified analyses identified fewer differentially expressed proteins than in the AD and LBD comparisons. But it’s worth noting that in CSF, NPTX1 was significantly downregulated in the males’ comparison, whereas NPTX2 and NPTXR proteins were downregulated in both sexes’ comparisons (in line with findings of the full-disease cohort analysis).

Although in CSF no proteins were identified in the females’ comparison, in plasma we observed a clear upregulation pattern. For example, increased TIMP3 levels distinguished FTD from the other diseases and from HC in the unstratified analysis but also emerged as significantly overexpressed in females’ sex-stratified analysis, suggesting the levels of this protein may be different between the two sexes. TIMP3 regulates extracellular matrix remodeling and neuroinflammatory processes through inhibition of matrix metalloproteinases. Dysregulation of these pathways has been implicated in neurodegenerative diseases, contributing to disease progression [69]. Elevated TIMP3 levels have also been reported in the plasma of individuals who are neurodegeneration-positive (N+) and show mild associations with reduced cortical thickness [24]. Our results, together with these findings, support the relevance of TIMP3 as a potential marker of neurodegeneration, but also as a protein with sex differences in FTD.

Correlation analyses across biofluids showed that p-Tau217, p-Tau231, NEFL, GFAP, and several vascular proteins exhibited strong CSF–plasma correlations, whereas most other proteins showed only modest associations, likely reflecting compartment-specific effects. Moreover, both p-Tau217 and NEFL demonstrated robust cross-platform concordance, underscoring the reliability of NULISAseq™ technology and reinforcing its validity as a tool for measuring fluid dementia biomarkers, even in accessible tissues such as blood.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the proteomic analysis was restricted to a pre-selected panel of 120 CNS-related proteins, chosen based on prior evidence of involvement in neurodegenerative processes. While this targeted approach increases analytical detectability, it limits the exploration of broader or novel biological pathways that may also be relevant to disease mechanisms. Second, the small sample size of the female LBD subgroup (n = 7–8, except for a few targets excluded in 1 or 2 plates) limited statistical power and increased the risk of type I error. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require validation in larger, independent cohorts. Finally, as this is a cross-sectional study, causal relationships between protein alterations and disease progression cannot be established at this moment.

Conclusions

This study provides a large-scale, disease-wide and sex-stratified proteomic analysis of paired CSF and plasma across AD, LBD, and FTD using ultrasensitive NULISAseq™ technology. ICAM1 in CSF and ANXA5 in plasma emerged as transdiagnostic biomarkers, indicating shared inflammatory and vascular-related processes across neurodegenerative dementias. Sex-dependent differences were most pronounced in plasma, where females consistently exhibited a higher number of upregulated inflammation-related proteins across all diseases, whereas CSF showed fewer and more modest sex-specific effects. The limited concordance between CSF and plasma underscores compartment-specific regulation of protein alterations. Overall, these findings highlight sex-dependent proteomic patterns and emphasize the importance of incorporating sex as a biological variable in dementia research, with implications for biomarker development and future sex-tailored therapeutic strategies.
