Courcelle’s Theorem for Lipschitz Continuity
Tatsuya Gima, Soh Kumabe, Yuichi Yoshida

Abstract
Lipschitz continuity of algorithms, introduced by Kumabe and Yoshida (FOCS’23), measures the stability of an algorithm against small input perturbations. Algorithms with small Lipschitz continuity are desirable, as they ensure reliable decision-making and reproducible scientific research. Several studies have proposed Lipschitz continuous algorithms for various combinatorial optimization problems, but these algorithms are problem-specific, requiring a separate design for each problem. To address this issue, we provide the first algorithmic meta-theorem in the field of Lipschitz continuous algorithms. Our result can be seen as a Lipschitz continuous analogue of Courcelle’s theorem, which offers Lipschitz continuous algorithms for problems on bounded-treewidth graphs. Specifically, we consider the problem of finding a vertex set in a graph that maximizes or minimizes the total weight, subject to constraints expressed in monadic second-order logic ( MSO 2 \textsf{MSO}_{2} ). We show that for any ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , there exists a ( 1 ± ε ) (1\pm\varepsilon) -approximation algorithm for the problem with a polylogarithmic Lipschitz constant on bounded treewidth graphs. On such graphs, our result outperforms most existing Lipschitz continuous algorithms in terms of approximability and/or Lipschitz continuity. Further, we provide similar results for problems on bounded-clique-width graphs subject to constraints expressed in MSO 1 \textsf{MSO}_{1} . Additionally, we construct a Lipschitz continuous version of Baker’s decomposition using our meta-theorem as a subroutine.

Lipschitz continuity of algorithms, introduced by Kumabe and Yoshida
[

[
15
,
17
]

To address this limitation, we present an algorithmic meta-theorem for Lipschitz continuous algorithms, which can be seen as Lipschitz continuity analogue of celebrated Courcelle’s theorem

1.1
Technical Overview

Now we provide a technical overview of our framework.
Since the arguments for clique-width are similar, we focus here on the treewidth results and omit the clique-width case.

For simplicity, here we consider the maximum weight independent set problem on a full binary tree (a rooted tree in which every vertex has exactly 0 or 2 children).
This corresponds to the case where
\varphi(X)=\forall x\forall y((x\in X\land y\in X)\rightarrow\lnot\mathsf{adj}(x,y))
.
Let
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{V}
be the weight vector.

If we do not care about Lipschitzness, this problem can exactly be solved by the following algorithm.
For each vertex
v\in V
, define
\mathsf{DP}[v][0]
to be an independent set in the subtree rooted at
v
that does not include
v
with the maximum weight.
Similarly, define
\mathsf{DP}[v][1]
to be an independent set in the subtree rooted at
v
with the maximum weight.
If
v
is a leaf, then
\mathsf{DP}[v][0]=\emptyset
and
\mathsf{DP}[v][1]=\{v\}
.
Otherwise, let
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
be the two children of
v
.
We have
\mathsf{DP}[v][0]=\mathsf{DP}[u_{1}][1]\cup\mathsf{DP}[u_{2}][1]
, and
\mathsf{DP}[v][1]
is the one with the larger weight of
X_{v,0}:=\mathsf{DP}[u_{1}][0]\cup\mathsf{DP}[u_{2}][0]\cup\{v\}
and
X_{v,1}:=\mathsf{DP}[u_{1}][1]\cup\mathsf{DP}[u_{2}][1]
.
By performing this dynamic programming in a bottom-up manner, the problem can be solved exactly.

However, this algorithm is not Lipschitz continuous.
This is because when we compute
\mathsf{DP}[v][1]
, which of
X_{v,0}
or
X_{v,1}
has a larger weight is affected by small changes in the weights.
To address this issue, we use the exponential mechanism
[
8
]
.
Specifically, for some constant
c>0
, we select
X_{v,i}
with probability proportional to
\exp(c\cdot w(X_{v,i}))
.
This approach makes the algorithm Lipschitz continuous, with only a slight sacrifice in the approximation ratio.
Specifically, we can prove that by appropriately choosing
c
for a given
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,1]
, the increase in the Lipschitz constant can be bounded by
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{\prime-1})
by reducing the approximation guarantee by a factor of
1-\varepsilon^{\prime}
at each vertex where the exponential mechanism is applied.

While this approach makes the algorithm Lipschitz continuous, the Lipschitz constant is still too large when the height
h
of the tree is large.
Specifically, to achieve an approximation guarantee of
1-\varepsilon
, we need to set
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\varepsilon}{h}
, leading to a Lipschitz constant of
h\cdot\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{\prime-1})=\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}h^{2})
.
This is larger than the trivial bound
n
when
h=O(n)
.
We resolve this issue by using the fact that any tree has a tree decomposition of width 5 and height
O(\log n)

[
2
]
.
By performing dynamic programming with the exponential mechanism on this tree decomposition, we can obtain
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm with Lipschitz constant
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
.
This argument can be naturally extended to the case where
G
is a bounded treewidth graph.
By following the proof of Courcelle’s Theorem
[
3
,
6
]
, we further extend this argument to the case where
\varphi
is a general
\textsf{MSO}_{2}
formula with a free vertex variable.

We prove
Theorem

5
by leveraging a lower bound on a related notion of sensitivity for the maximum cut problem
[
9
]
, where sensitivity measures the change in the output with respect to the Hamming distance under edge deletions
[
24
]
.
Specifically, we reduce the maximum cut problem, where stability is defined with respect to edge deletions, to the max ones problem, where stability is measured with respect to weight changes.

1.2
Related Work

Lipschitz continuity of discrete algorithms is defined by Kumabe and Yoshida
[
15
]
, and they provided Lipschitz continuous approximation algorithms for the minimum spanning tree, shortest path, and maximum weight matching problems.
In a subsequent work
[
17
]
, they further provided such algorithms for the minimum weight vertex cover, minimum weight set cover, and minimum weight feedback vertex set problems.
In another work
[
16
]
, they defined Lipschitz continuity for allocations in cooperative games and provided Lipschitz continuous allocation schemes for the matching game and the minimum spanning tree game.

A variant known as
pointwise Lipschitz continuity
has also been studied, which is defined using the unweighted Hamming distance instead of the weighted Hamming distance.
Kumabe and Yoshida
[
15
]
defined pointwise Lipschitz continuity and provided pointwise Lipschitz continuous algorithms for the minimum spanning tree and maximum weight bipartite matching problems.
Liu et al.
[
19
]
proposed the
proximal gradient method
as a general technique for solving LP relaxations stably. Using this, they provided pointwise Lipschitz continuous approximation algorithms for the minimum vertex
(S,T)
-cut, densest subgraph, maximum weight (
b
-)matching, and packing integer programming problems.

(Average) sensitivity, introduced by Varma and Yoshida
[
24
]
with preliminary ideas by Murai and Yoshida
[
20
]
, is a notion similar to Lipschitz continuity. While Lipschitz continuity evaluates an algorithm’s stability against unit changes in the input weights, (average) sensitivity evaluates an algorithm’s stability against (random) deletions of elements from the input.
Algorithms with small (average) sensitivity have been studied for several problems, such as maximum matching
[
24
,
26
]
, minimum cut
[
24
]
, knapsack problem
[
14
]
, Euclidean
k
-means
[
25
]
, spectral clustering
[
22
]
, and dynamic programming problems
[
13
]
.
Recently, Fleming and Yoshida
[
9
]
constructed a PCP framework to prove the sensitivity lower bound for the constraint satisfaction problem.

2.4
Lipschitz Continuity

We formally define Lipschitz continuity of algorithms.
As this is sufficient for this work, we only consider algorithms for vertex-weighted graph problems.
The definition can be naturally extended to other settings, such as edge-weighted problems.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph.
For vertex sets
X,X^{\prime}\subseteq V
and weight vectors
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{V}
, we define the
weighted Hamming distance
between
(X,w)
and
(X^{\prime},w^{\prime})
by

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{w}}((X,w),(X^{\prime},w^{\prime})):=\left\|\sum_{v\in X}\bm{1}_{v}w_{v}-\sum_{v\in X^{\prime}}\bm{1}_{v}w^{\prime}_{v}\right\|_{1}=\sum_{v\in X\cap X^{\prime}}|w_{v}-w^{\prime}_{v}|+\sum_{v\in X\setminus X^{\prime}}w_{v}+\sum_{v\in X^{\prime}\setminus X}w^{\prime}_{v},

where
\bm{1}_{v}\in\{0,1\}^{V}
denotes the
characteristic vector
of
v
, that is, the vector
\bm{1}_{v,u}=1
holds if and only if
u=v
.
For two probability distributions
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
over subsets of
V
, we define

\displaystyle\mathsf{EM}\left((\mathcal{X},w),(\mathcal{X}^{\prime},w^{\prime})\right):=\inf_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{(X,X^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[d_{\mathrm{w}}((X,w),(X^{\prime},w^{\prime}))\right],

where the minimum is taken over
couplings
of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
, that is, distributions over pairs of sets such that its marginal distributions on the first and second coordinates are equal to
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
, respectively.
Consider an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, that takes a graph
G=(V,E)
and a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{V}
as an input and outputs a vertex subset
X\subseteq V
.
We denote the output distribution of
\mathcal{A}
for weight
w
as
\mathcal{A}(w)
.
The
Lipschitz constant
of
\mathcal{A}
is defined by

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\\
w\neq w^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathsf{EM}\left((\mathcal{A}(w),w),(\mathcal{A}(w^{\prime}),w^{\prime})\right)}{\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{1}}.

For two random variables
\bm{X}
and
\bm{X}^{\prime}
, the
total variation distance
between them is given as:

\displaystyle\mathsf{TV}\left(\bm{X},\bm{X}^{\prime}\right):=\inf_{\mathcal{D}}\Pr_{(X,X^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[X\neq X^{\prime}\right],

where the minimum is taken over couplings between
\bm{X}
and
\bm{X}^{\prime}
, that is, distributions over pairs such that its marginal distributions on the first and the second coordinates are equal to
\bm{X}
and
\bm{X}^{\prime}
, respectively.
For an element
u\in V
, we use
\bm{1}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{V}
to denote the
characteristic vector
of
u
, that is,
\bm{1}_{u}(u)=1
and
\bm{1}_{u}(v)=0
for
v\in V\setminus\{u\}
.
The following lemma indicates that, to bound the Lipschitz constant, it suffices to consider pairs of weight vectors that differ by one coordinate.

Lemma 15

(
[
15
]
)
.

Suppose that there exist some
c>0
and
L>0
such that

\mathsf{EM}\left((\mathcal{A}(G,w),w),(\mathcal{A}(G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{u}),w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{u})\right)\leq\delta L

holds for all
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{V}
,
u\in V
and
0<\delta\leq c
. Then,
\mathcal{A}
is
L
-Lipschitz.

In our algorithm, we use the following procedures
softmax
and
softmin
.
These procedures are derived from the
exponential mechanism
in the literature of

[
8

and frequently appear in the literature on Lipschitz continuity
[
15
,
17

.
Here, we organize them into a more convenient form for our use.
Let

The

of

In this section, we prove
Theorems

,

Appendix

In
Section

3.1

Section

In

3.1
Definitions

For a graph
G
, a weight vector
w
, and an
\textsf{MSO}_{2}
formula
\varphi
, we define

\displaystyle\mathsf{opt}_{w}[G,\varphi]=\max_{S\subseteq V(G),G\models\varphi(S)}w(S).

(1)

for maximization problems and

\displaystyle\mathsf{opt}_{w}[G,\varphi]=\min_{S\subseteq V(G),G\models\varphi(S)}w(S).

(2)

for minimization problems.
If
\mathsf{sat}(G,\varphi)=\emptyset
, we define
\mathsf{opt}_{w}[G,\varphi]=\mathtt{nil}
.
For graphs
G
and
\textsf{MSO}_{2}
formulas
\varphi
with
\mathsf{sat}(G,\varphi)\neq\emptyset
, our algorithm recursively computes the vertex set
\mathsf{DP}_{w}[G,\varphi]
that approximately achieves the maximum in Equation (
1
) or minimum in Equation (
2
).
When it is clear from the context, we omit the subscript
w
and simply write
\mathsf{opt}[G,\varphi]
and
\mathsf{DP}[G,\varphi]
.
We perform the dynamic programming algorithm using the formulas defined in
Corollary

14
over the parse tree of a term obtained from
Lemma

8
and
Proposition

11
. Specifically, at every stage of the algorithm, it is guaranteed that each
X\in\mathsf{sat}(G,\varphi)
satisfies
X\subseteq V(G)\setminus\mathsf{src}(G)
.
Furthermore, our algorithm is randomized. Thus,
\mathsf{DP}[G,\varphi]
can be considered as a probability distribution over vertex sets in
\mathsf{sat}(G,\varphi)
.

To bound the approximation ratio, for a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{V}
, we bound the ratio between
\mathbb{E}\left[w(\mathsf{DP}[G,\varphi])\right]
and
\mathsf{opt}[G,\varphi]
.
To bound the Lipschitz constant, for a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{V}
,
u\in V
, and
\delta>0
, we bound
\mathsf{EM}\left(\mathsf{DP}_{w}[G,\varphi],\mathsf{DP}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}[G,\varphi]\right)
.
From now on, we will concentrate on maximizing problems.
The algorithm for minimization problems is similar to that for maximization problems, while the detail of the analysis is slightly differ.
We discuss the minimization version in Appendix
C
.

3.2
Base Case

Here we consider the base case.
Let
G
be a graph with a single vertex
v
and no edges, where
v
is a source of
G
, and
\varphi
be an
\textsf{MSO}_{2}
formula such that
\mathsf{sat}(G,\varphi)
is nonempty and contains no set containing
v
.
In particular, we have
\mathsf{sat}(G,\varphi)=\{\emptyset\}
.
We set
\mathsf{DP}[G,\varphi]:=\emptyset
.
Since
w(\emptyset)=0
, it is clear that
w\left(\mathsf{DP}[G,\varphi]\right)=\mathsf{opt}[G,\varphi]=0
.
Furthermore, it is obvious that

\displaystyle\mathsf{EM}\left(\mathsf{DP}_{w}[G,\varphi],\mathsf{DP}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}[G,\varphi]\right)=\mathsf{EM}(\emptyset,\emptyset)=0.

3.3
Parallel Composition

Next, we consider the parallel composition.
Let

\displaystyle\mathsf{sat}(G\mathbin{/\hskip-2.84526pt/}H,\varphi)=\biguplus_{i\in[p]}\mathsf{sat}(G,\theta_{i})\boxtimes\mathsf{sat}(H,\psi_{i}).

(3)

Assume we have already computed
\mathsf{DP}[G,\theta_{i}]
and
\mathsf{DP}[H,\psi_{i}]
for each
i\in[p]
.
We compute
\mathsf{DP}[G\mathbin{/\hskip-2.84526pt/}H,\varphi]
.
For each
i\in[p]
, we denote
\mathsf{opt}_{i}:=\mathsf{opt}[G,\theta_{i}]+\mathsf{opt}[H,\psi_{i}]
.
By definition, we have
\max_{i\in[p]}\mathsf{opt}_{i}=\mathsf{opt}[G\mathbin{/\hskip-2.84526pt/}H,\varphi]
.
Then, we take
\bm{i}^{*}=\mathsf{softargmax}^{\varepsilon}_{i\in[p]}\mathsf{opt}_{i}
and define

\mathsf{DP}[G\mathbin{/\hskip-2.84526pt/}H,\varphi]:=\mathsf{DP}[G,\theta_{\bm{i}^{*}}]\cup\mathsf{DP}[H,\psi_{\bm{i}^{*}}]
.
First we analyze the approximation ratio.

Lemma 20
.

Let
0<\alpha\leq 1
and suppose
\mathbb{E}\left[w(\mathsf{DP}[G,\theta_{i}])\right]\geq\alpha\mathsf{opt}[G,\theta_{i}]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[w(\mathsf{DP}[H,\psi_{i}])\right]\geq\alpha\mathsf{opt}[H,\psi_{i}]

hold for all
i\in[p]
.
Then, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[w\left(\mathsf{DP}[G\mathbin{/\hskip-2.84526pt/}H,\varphi]\right)\right]\geq(1-\varepsilon)\alpha\mathsf{opt}[G\mathbin{/\hskip-2.84526pt/}H,\varphi]
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[w(\mathsf{DP}[G\mathbin{/\hskip-2.84526pt/}H,\varphi])\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[w(\mathsf{DP}[G,\theta_{\bm{i}^{*}}]\cup\mathsf{DP}[H,\psi_{\bm{i}^{*}}])\right]\geq\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{opt}[G,\theta_{\bm{i}^{*}}]+\mathsf{opt}[H,\psi_{\bm{i}^{*}}]\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{softmax}^{\varepsilon}_{i\in[p]}\mathsf{opt}_{i}\right]\geq(1-\varepsilon)\alpha\max_{i\in[p]}\mathsf{opt}_{i}=(1-\varepsilon)\alpha\mathsf{opt}[G\mathbin{/\hskip-2.84526pt/}H,\varphi].\qed

Now we analyze the Lipschitz constant.
We denote the variable
\bm{i}^{*}
corresponding to the weight
w
and
w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}
by
\bm{i}^{*}_{w}
and
\bm{i}^{*}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}
, respectively.
We note that
\mathsf{DP}_{w}[G,\theta_{i}]
and
\mathsf{DP}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}[G,\theta_{i}]
are the same as a distribution unless
u\in V(G)\setminus\mathsf{src}_{G}
.
The same also holds for
H
.
Since
V(G)\setminus\mathsf{src}_{G}
and
V(H)\setminus\mathsf{src}_{H}
are disjoint, without loss of generality, we can assume the
\mathsf{DP}_{w}[H,\psi_{i}]
and
\mathsf{DP}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}[H,\psi_{i}]
are the same as a distribution.
We have the following.

Lemma 21
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and suppose
\mathsf{EM}\left(\mathsf{DP}_{w}[G,\theta_{i}],\mathsf{DP}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}[G,\theta_{i}]\right)\leq\beta

holds for all
i\in[p]
.
Then, we have
\mathsf{EM}\left(\mathsf{DP}_{w}[G\mathbin{/\hskip-2.84526pt/}H,\varphi],\mathsf{DP}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}[G\mathbin{/\hskip-2.84526pt/}H,\varphi]\right)\leq 30\varepsilon^{-1}\log(2p\varepsilon^{-1})\delta+\beta
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathsf{EM}\left(\mathsf{DP}_{w}[G\mathbin{/\hskip-2.84526pt/}H,\varphi],\mathsf{DP}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}[G\mathbin{/\hskip-2.84526pt/}H,\varphi]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{TV}\left(\bm{i}^{*}_{w},\bm{i}^{*}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}\right)\left(\mathsf{opt}_{w}[G\mathbin{/\hskip-2.84526pt/}H,\varphi]+\mathsf{opt}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}[G\mathbin{/\hskip-2.84526pt/}H,\varphi]\right)+\max_{i\in[p]}\left(\mathsf{EM}\left(\mathsf{DP}_{w}[G,\theta_{i}],\mathsf{DP}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}[G,\theta_{i}]\right)\right)

5
Baker’s Technique

In this section, we provide a technique for Lipschitz continuous algorithms for optimization problems on planar graphs.
Our technique is a modification of Baker’s technique
[
1
]
, which provides a PTAS for many optimization problems on planar graphs.
For simplicity, in this section, we focus on the maximum weight independent set problem on planar graphs.
Similar techniques can be applied to obtain Lipschitz continuous algorithms for other problems, such as the minimum weight vertex cover problem on planar graphs.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a planar graph and
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{V}
be a weight vector.
A vertex subset
X
is an independent set of
G
if no two vertices in
X
are adjacent.
The
maximum weight planar independent set
problem asks to maximize the weight
w(X):=\sum_{v\in X}w_{v}
among all independent sets of
G
.
We denote this maximum value by
\mathsf{opt}
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
.
We provide a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the maximum weight planar independent set problem with Lipschitz constant
O((\varepsilon^{-1}+\log\log n)\varepsilon^{-1}\log^{2}n)
.

Let
r\in V
be an arbitrary vertex. For
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, let
R_{i}
be the set of vertices at exact distance
i
from
r
, where the distance between
r
and
v
is measured as the minimum number of edges in an
r
-
v
path.
Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
.
For
j\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
, let
G_{j}
be the subgraph of
G
induced by
V\setminus\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\colon i\equiv j\pmod{m}}R_{i}
.
We denote the maximum weight of an independent set of
G_{j}
by
\mathsf{opt}_{j}
.
The following lemmas are known.

Lemma 25

(
[
23
]
)
.

For each
k\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
, the treewidth of
G_{i}
is at most
3m-2
.

Lemma 26

(
[
1
]
)
.

We have
\max_{j\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}}\mathsf{opt}_{j}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\mathsf{opt}
.

In the original Baker’s technique, a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the maximum weight planar independent set problem is obtained by finding the maximum weight independent set for each
G_{j}
and then outputting the one with the maximum weight among them.
We make this algorithm Lipschitz continuous by replacing the max operation with a softmax operation.
We set
m=\lceil 2\varepsilon^{-1}\rceil
.
Then, we take
\bm{j}^{*}=\mathsf{softargmax}^{m^{-1}}_{j\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}}\mathsf{opt}_{j}
and compute an approximate maximum weight independent set of
G_{\bm{j}^{*}}
using the algorithm in Section
3
, where
\varepsilon
is set as
m^{-1}
.

Lemma 27
.

Our algorithm has approximation ratio
1-\varepsilon
.

Proof.

From Lemma
16
, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{opt}_{\bm{j}^{*}}\right]\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\max_{j\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}}\mathsf{opt}_{j}
.
For each
j\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
, our framework outputs
\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)
-approximate solution for the maximum weight independent set problem on
G_{j}
.
Therefore, the overall approximation ratio is
\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)^{2}\geq 1-\frac{2}{m}\geq 1-\varepsilon
.
∎

Lemma 28
.

Our algorithm has a Lipschitz constant
O((\varepsilon^{-1}+\log\log n)\varepsilon^{-1}\log^{2}n)
.

Proof.

Let
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{V}
,
u\in V
, and
\delta>0
.
We bound
\mathsf{EM}\left(\bm{X}_{w},\bm{X}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}\right)
, where
\bm{X}_{w}
and
\bm{X}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}
are the output of our algorithm on weight vector
w
and
w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}
, respectively.
We denote the variable
\bm{j}^{*}
corresponding to the weight vector
w
and
w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}
by
\bm{j}^{*}
and
\bm{j}^{*}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}
, respectively.
From Lemma
17
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{TV}\left(\bm{j}^{*}_{w},\bm{j}^{*}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}\right)\leq\frac{10m\log(2m\varepsilon^{-1})\delta}{\max_{j\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}}\mathsf{opt}_{j}}\leq\frac{10m\log(2p\varepsilon^{-1})\delta}{\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\mathsf{opt}}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{EM}\left(\bm{X}_{w},\bm{X}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{TV}\left(\bm{j}^{*}_{w},\bm{j}^{*}_{w+\delta\bm{1}_{u}}\right)\mathsf{opt}+O\left(m+\log m+\log\log n\right)m\log^{2}n\delta

\displaystyle\leq\left(O(m\log m)+O\left(\left(m+\log\log n\right)m\log^{2}n\right)\right)\delta

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\left(m+\log\log n\right)m\log^{2}n\right)\delta

\displaystyle=O\left(\left(\varepsilon^{-1}+\log\log n\right)\varepsilon^{-1}\log^{2}n\right)\delta,\qed

where the first inequality is from Theorem
3
.

Lower Bounds for Max Ones

In this section, we prove
Theorem

5
.
To this end, we use a known sensitivity lower bound for the maximum cut problem.
In the maximum cut problem, given a graph
G=(V,E)
, the goal is to find a bipartition of
V
that maximizes the number of edges between the two parts.
Then, the
sensitivity
of a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for the maximum cut problem on a graph
G=(V,E)
is defined to be

\max_{e\in E}\mathsf{EM}(\mathcal{A}(G),\mathcal{A}(G-e)),

where
G-e
denotes the graph obtained from
G
by deleting
e
and the distance
d
underlying the earth mover’s distance is the Hamming distance, i.e.,
d(S,S^{\prime})=|S\triangle S^{\prime}|
.
We use the following lower bound:

Lemma 29

(
[
9
]
)
.

There exist
\varepsilon,\delta>0
such that any
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the maximum cut problem sensitivity
\Omega(n^{\delta})
, even on constant-degree graphs.

Proof of
Theorem

5
.

Let
\varepsilon,\delta>0
be as in
Lemma

29
.
Assume that there exists a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\rm MO}
for the max ones problem with Lipschitz constant
s(n)
, where
n
is the number of variables in the input 3CNF formula.
Below, we design a low sensitivity algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\rm MC}
for the maximum cut problem using
\mathcal{A}_{\rm MO}
.

Given an unweighted graph
G=(V,E)
, we construct a 3CNF formula
\varphi
over
X:=\{x_{v}:v\in V\}\cup\{x_{uv}:\{u,v\}\in\binom{V}{2}\}
as follows.
For each pair
\{u,v\}\in\binom{V}{2}
, we add constraints over
x_{u}
,
x_{v}
, and
x_{uv}
that are satisfied if and only if (i)
x_{uv}=1
and
x_{u}\neq x_{v}
or (ii)
x_{uv}=0
.
More specifically, we add constraints
(\bar{x}_{uv}\vee x_{u}\vee x_{v})
and
(\bar{x}_{uv}\vee\bar{x}_{u}\vee\bar{x}_{v})
.
Note that
\varphi
does not depend on
E
.
Next, we define a weight function
w:X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
w(x_{u})=0
for every

and
w(x_{uv})=1
if and only if
\{u,v\}\in E
.
Then, we run the algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\rm MO}
on the instance
(\varphi,w)
, and let
\sigma:X\to\{0,1\}
be the obtained solution.
Finally, let
S:=\sigma^{-1}(1)\cap V
be the solution for
G
, and outputs
S
with probability half and
V\setminus S
with the remaining probability of half.

Note that the total weight of edges cut by

is equal to the total weight of

Hence,
\mathcal{A}_{\rm MC}
is a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the maximum cut problem.

Now, we analyze the sensitivity of
\mathcal{A}_{\rm MC}
.
Let
G=(V,E)
and
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime})
be two graphs with
|E\triangle E^{\prime}|=1
.
Let
w
and
w^{\prime}
be the weight functions constructed from
G
and
G^{\prime}
, respectively.
Note that
\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{1}=1
.
Let
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
be the outputs of
\mathcal{A}_{\rm MO}
on
(\varphi,w)
and
(\varphi,w^{\prime})
, respectively.
Let
F=\{\{u,v\}\in E\mid\sigma(x_{uv})=1\}
and
F^{\prime}=\{\{u,v\}\in E^{\prime}\mid\sigma^{\prime}(x_{uv})=1\}
.
Let
S
and
S^{\prime}
be the outputs of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{M}C}
for

, respectively.
Then, we have

\mathsf{EM}(S,S^{\prime})\leq\Delta\cdot\mathsf{EM}(F,F^{\prime})=O(\mathsf{EM}(F,F^{\prime})),

where

is the degree bound of the graphs
G
and
G^{\prime}
.
Because
\mathcal{A}_{\rm MO}
has Lipschitz constant
s(n)
and the number of variables in

is

, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{EM}(S,S^{\prime})\leq O(\mathsf{EM}(F,F^{\prime}))=O(\mathsf{EM}((\sigma^{-1}(1),w),((\sigma^{\prime})^{-1}(1),w^{\prime})))

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{\mathsf{EM}((\sigma,w),(\sigma^{\prime},w^{\prime}))}{\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{1}}\right)=O(s(n^{3})).

This implies that
\mathcal{A}_{\rm MC}
has sensitivity at most

By
Lemma

29
, we must have

.
∎

.
For each parameter
c\in I
, we consider transporting probability mass for
\bm{c}=c
to that for
\bm{c}^{\prime}=c
as far as possible. The remaining mass is transported arbitrarily. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{TV}\left(\mathsf{softargmin}^{\epsilon}_{i\in[p]}x_{i},\mathsf{softargmin}^{\epsilon}_{i\in[p]}x_{i}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{TV}\left(\bm{c},\bm{c}^{\prime}\right)+\sup_{c\in I}\mathsf{TV}\left(\left(\mathsf{softargmin}^{\epsilon}_{i\in[p]}x_{i}\mid\bm{c}=c\right),\left(\mathsf{softargmin}^{\epsilon}_{i\in[p]}x_{i}^{\prime}\mid\bm{c^{\prime}}=c\right)\right).

Now, for
c\in I
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{TV}\left(\left(\mathsf{softargmin}^{\epsilon}_{i\in[p]}x_{i}\mid\bm{c}=c\right),\left(\mathsf{softargmin}^{\epsilon}_{i\in[p]}x_{i}^{\prime}\mid\bm{c^{\prime}}=c\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[p]}\max\left(0,\Pr\left[\mathsf{softargmin}^{\epsilon}_{i\in[p]}x_{i}=i\mid\bm{c}=c\right]-\Pr\left[\mathsf{softargmin}^{\epsilon}_{i\in[p]}x_{i}^{\prime}=i\mid\bm{c}^{\prime}=c\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[p]}\max\left(0,\frac{\exp(-cx_{i})}{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[p]}\exp(-cx_{i^{\prime}})}-\frac{\exp(-cx_{i}^{\prime})}{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[p]}\exp(-cx_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[p]}\frac{\exp(-cx_{i})-\exp(-cx_{i}^{\prime})}{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[p]}\exp(-cx_{i^{\prime}})}=\sum_{i\in[p]}\frac{(1-\exp(-c(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i})))\exp(-cx_{i})}{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[p]}\exp(-cx_{i^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[p]}\frac{(1-\exp(-c\delta))\exp(-cx_{i})}{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[p]}\exp(-cx_{i^{\prime}})}=1-\exp(-c\delta)\leq 2c\delta\leq\frac{8\epsilon^{-1}\log(2p\epsilon^{-1})\delta}{\min_{i\in[p]}x_{i}},

where the fourth inequality is from the fact that
1-\exp(-x)\leq x\leq 2x
holds for
x\leq 1
.
Therefore, we have

\mathsf{TV}\left(\mathsf{softargmin}^{\epsilon}_{i\in[p]}x_{i},\mathsf{softargmin}^{\epsilon}_{i\in[p]}x_{i}^{\prime}\right)\leq\frac{2\delta}{\min_{i\in[p]}x_{i}}+\frac{8\epsilon^{-1}\log(2p\epsilon^{-1})\delta}{\min_{i\in[p]}x_{i}}\leq\frac{10\epsilon^{-1}\log(2p\epsilon^{-1})\delta}{\min_{i\in[p]}x_{i}}.\qed
