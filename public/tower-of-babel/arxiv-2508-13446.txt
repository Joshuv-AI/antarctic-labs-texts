CAST: Counterfactual Labels Improve Instruction Following in Vision-Language-Action Models
Catherine Glossop, William Chen, Arjun Bhorkar, Dhruv Shah, Sergey Levine

Abstract
Generalist robots should be able to understand and follow user instructions, but current vision-language-action (VLA) models struggle with following fine-grained commands despite providing a powerful architecture for mapping open-vocabulary natural language instructions to robot actions. One cause for this is a lack of semantic diversity and language grounding in existing robot datasets and, specifically, a lack of fine-grained task diversity for similar observations. To address this, we present a novel method to augment existing robot datasets by leveraging vision language models to create counterfactual labels. Our method improves the language-following capabilities of VLAs by increasing the diversity and granularity of language grounding for robot datasets by generating counterfactual language and actions. We evaluate the resulting model’s ability to follow language instructions, ranging from simple object-centric commands to complex referential tasks, by conducting visual language navigation experiments in 3 different indoor and outdoor environments. Our experiments demonstrate that counterfactual relabeling, without any additional data collection, significantly improves instruction-following in VLA policies, making them competitive with state-of-the-art methods and increasing success rate by 27% on navigation tasks.



I
Introduction

Large vision-language models (VLMs) are powerful not only because of their diverse capabilities but also because they can be steered with fine-grained instructions to produce specific outputs. Ideally, powerful generalist robot policies should exhibit the same level of controllability on embodied tasks.
Vision-language-action (VLA) policies take advantage of strong VLM priors to train robot policies end-to-end that map open-vocabulary language instructions to robot actions. However, in practice, these policies often struggle with following language instructions. Even with high-quality and diverse data, the future action distribution typically collapses given any single observation (e.g., given an observation of a chest of drawers, the only probable task for a robot is “open the drawer”). Thus, even powerful models have little incentive to pay attention to the language command, suffering from
posterior collapse

[
1
,
2
]
. While this problem can be partially mitigated with curated data collection, this seems contradictory to the promise of training generalist models on all available data, including data collected without the intention of promoting language following.

In this paper, we address the problem of training steerable language-conditioned robot policies. To this end, we present an approach to augment existing datasets with language-labeled
counterfactual
trajectories in hindsight by leveraging VLM priors, without requiring existing language labels. We leverage a VLM to generate hypothetical instructions that the robot could perform given a situation encountered in the dataset, exploring variations in behavior and language. Given a hypothetical instruction, we then synthesize plausible actions that
would have been
taken in these observed states. This effectively expands the training label distribution beyond what is present in the original dataset. For example, given a trajectory of a robot navigating straight down a hallway, our method can generate counterfactual labels such as “navigate along the left wall" or “drive to the right of the pedestrian," even if these specific situations, and therefore labels, were not present in the original data. To enable this sort of counterfactual data generation, our key observation is that it is much easier to train a policy to produce good actions for simple commands like “turn left” or “turn right.” This policy can then generate action labels for more complex commands, like “follow the left wall,” by breaking them up into these simple commands using large VLMs. This counterfactual data can then be used to train VLA policies that understand and respond to a broad range of user instructions at test time.

The main contribution of our work is

We fine-tune an open-source VLM
[
3

with our augmented dataset and evaluate performance in 3 environments on 27 language prompts. We also compare CAST to alternate schemes proposed in prior work, as well as state-of-the-art vision-language navigation baselines, and find that our policy achieves a 53% success rate on challenging, diverse language prompts, outperforming a VLA trained without CAST by 27%.
In the interest of reproducibility, we have open-sourced the CAST augmentation code, CounterfactualVLA policy training code, the CAST dataset, as well as trained checkpoints.

III
Preliminaries and Problem Statement

Our goal is to train a policy that can control an autonomous mobile robot to follow complex language commands in the real world based on image observations. We can formalize this as the task of recovering a policy
\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{a}_{t}|\ell_{t},\mathbf{o}_{t})
with parameters
\theta
, where
\mathbf{a}_{t}
denotes the action at time
t
,
\mathbf{o}_{t}
denotes the observation (e.g., an image from the robot’s camera), and
\ell_{t}
denotes a language instruction, such as “move along the white wall.” We consider the setting of imitation learning, where the policy is typically trained by approximately maximizing the log-likelihood of a dataset according to

\theta\leftarrow\arg\max_{\theta}\sum_{(\mathbf{o}_{t,i},\ell_{t,i},\mathbf{a}_{t,i})\in\mathcal{D}_{l}}\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{a}_{t,i}|\ell_{t,i},\mathbf{o}_{t,i}),

(1)

and
\mathcal{D}_{l}=\{\mathbf{o}_{t,i},\ell_{t,i},\mathbf{a}_{t,i}\}
is a
labeled
dataset, with subscript
i
denoting the
i^{\text{th}}
trajectory, and
t
denoting the time step in that trajectory. In this paper, we do not focus on the specific algorithm or model, but rather on how the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{l}
is obtained. If the language labels
\ell_{t,i}
in the dataset are not necessary for the policy model to predict the correct action, then the model will ignore it at test-time — a phenomenon known as
posterior collapse

[
1
,
2
]
. The goal of our method is to take an
unlabeled
dataset
\mathcal{D}_{u}=\{\mathbf{o}_{t,i},\mathbf{a}_{t,i}\}
and construct a new
labeled
dataset
\mathcal{D}_{l}=\{\mathbf{o}_{t,i},\ell_{t,i},\mathbf{a}_{t,i}\}
that results in an effective instruction-following policy when we train according to
Eq.

1
. In constructing
\mathcal{D}_{l}
from
\mathcal{D}_{u}
, we will not only construct labels for existing trajectories,
\bar{\ell}_{t,i}
, if language labels do not exist, but also new counterfactual language labels
\ell^{cf}_{t,i}

and
actions
\mathbf{a}^{cf}_{t,i}
for observations
\mathbf{o}_{t,i}\in\mathcal{D}_{u}
.

IV
Counterfactual Label Augmentation

In order for policies to be
steerable
they must be trained to pay attention to user-specified language instructions, avoiding posterior collapse. To this end, it is not enough for policies to be trained on diverse environments and instructions. We also need different instructions to lead to different actions, even when the observations are otherwise similar. That is, the language must correlate with the action
even when conditioning on the observation
, otherwise the model may simply ignore the instruction and still achieve high training accuracy. This property is difficult to ensure, because in practice the correlation between the observation and the instruction is very high – e.g., the robot might only be asked to navigate to a doorway when a doorway is visible.

Our goal with CAST is to generate multiple action-language tuples for any observation in
\mathcal{D}_{u}
, such that the policy must attend to the language instruction in order to produce the right action. To do this, we synthetically generate potential language commands
\ell^{cf}_{t,i}
that are feasible from the observations
\mathbf{o}_{t,i}\in\mathcal{D}_{u}
, but are counterfactual to the existing trajectories.

Our key insight is that these counterfactual trajectories can be obtained synthetically from a simpler
atomic
policy, which is much easier to train than a general instruction-following policy and can be reliable over short horizons. This atomic policy follows simple
atomic
language commands
\ell^{a}_{t,i}
, such as “turn left” or “turn right”, and is given by
\pi_{a}(\mathbf{a}_{t,i}|\ell^{a}_{t,i},\mathbf{o}_{t,i})
. To connect the synthetic high-level instruction
\ell^{cf}_{t,i}
to this atomic policy, we prompt the VLM to generate both the corresponding atomic command
\ell^{a,cf}_{t,i}
alongside the counterfactual high-level command
\ell^{cf}_{t,i}
given the observation
\mathbf{o}_{t,i}
, and then use the atomic policy
\pi_{a}(\mathbf{a}_{t,i}|\ell^{a}_{t,i},\mathbf{o}_{t,i})
to generate a suitable action
\mathbf{a}^{cf}_{t,i}
. This allows us to construct an expanded dataset where, for every trajectory, several counterfactual trajectories are generated that branch off from the states covered in the original data, as shown in the right side of
Fig.

2
. When training on this expanded dataset, the policy will encounter several instructions associated with a given observation that produce distinct actions.

IV-A

How counterfactuals enable language following

We motivate our approach by presenting a simple conceptual model for how well a particular language label induces language following. For simplicity of exposition, we will drop time step and sample subscripts
t
and
i
in this derivation, such that the counterfactual language label is simply denoted as
\ell
, the atomic language label as
\ell^{a}
, the action as
\mathbf{a}
, and the observation as
\mathbf{o}
.

We expect a model to attend to language input when it adds additional information about the action
\mathbf{a}
that cannot be deduced from the observation
\mathbf{o}
. This can be captured by the
conditional
mutual information between the label and the action,
conditioned
on the observation
I(\mathbf{a};\ell|\mathbf{o})
. In this section, we will assume that language following can be improved by maximizing this mutual information quantity, which we argue is a reasonable, though simple, conceptual model of language following. We will show that by increasing the number of different
\ell^{a}
executed at a given
\mathbf{o}
, while ensuring that each atomic command corresponds to a unique
\ell
, we effectively maximize a lower bound on
I(\mathbf{a};\ell|\mathbf{o})
. This will motivate our method as a principled way to improve language following, albeit under this mutual information assumption. See
Section

A2
for details.

Lemma IV.1

Given a language label
\ell
, an atomic command
\ell^{a}
, and an observation
\mathbf{o}
, the mutual information
I(\mathbf{a};\ell|\mathbf{o})
is lower bounded by
H(\ell^{a}|\mathbf{o})-H(\ell^{a}|\ell,\mathbf{o})
.

From Lemma
IV.1
, maximizing
H(\ell^{a}|\mathbf{o})-H(\ell^{a}|\ell,\mathbf{o})
optimizes a lower bound on the MI between language and action; thereby we would expect improved language following.
Thus, an effective way to improve language following is to produce a high-entropy, diverse distribution over atomic labels
\ell^{a}
, while ensuring that the atomic labels are easy to predict given the full language command
\ell
. CAST approximates just this: we generate a diverse set of counterfactual language instructions that correspond to distinct atomic commands. This results in a high atomic label entropy but a low conditional entropy given the full command, and this bound pushes up on the mutual information between language and actions, thus improving language following.

IV-B

Generating the CAST dataset

The first step of CAST is to obtain a dataset of atomic trajectory segments with associated atomic labels
\mathcal{D}_{a}=\{\mathbf{o}_{t,i},\ell^{a}_{t,i},\mathbf{a}_{t,i}\}
. We also obtain the instructions that describe the trajectories that were
actually
executed by the robot,
\bar{\ell}_{j,i}
, which must be generated if they do not exist in
\mathcal{D}_{u}
. Using
\mathcal{D}_{a}
and
\bar{\ell}_{j,i}
, we can describe in language the existing trajectories in the dataset, which is the starting point for generating the novel counterfactual trajectories with CAST. The definition of atomic labels and the generation process for
\bar{\ell}_{j,i}
are domain-specific and are described in
Section

V
.

Generating counterfactual instructions.
At a given observation in
\mathcal{D}_{u}
, we have
\bar{\ell}_{t,i}
and
\ell^{a}_{t,i}
, which describe in language the overall instruction the robot was following and the short horizon behavior of the robot in the original trajectory. With this information, we leverage a state-of-the-art vision-language model (VLM)
[
30
,
31
]
to generate plausible alternative language commands
\ell^{cf}_{j,i}
that the robot
could
execute from the images
\mathbf{o}_{t,i}\in\mathcal{D}_{u}
, but that differ semantically and geometrically from the original trajectory. Our method for generating plausible counterfactual instructions
\ell^{cf}_{j,i}
is to select decision points – that is, the boundaries of the atomic segments in
\mathcal{D}_{a}
– and query the VLM for alternative instructions via a
relabeling prompt
. Details and specific prompts are described in
Section

H
. As shown in
Fig.

2
, the
relabeling prompt
is constructed using image and atomic label tuples
(\mathbf{o}_{t_{i},i},\ell^{a}_{t_{i},i})
corresponding to each decision point, as well as the instruction labels
\{\bar{\ell}_{j,i}\}
described in
Section

V
. This prompt instructs the VLM to select a decision point, describe the counterfactual behavior that could be executed, and also indicate the
atomic
instruction that would correspond to this behavior. For example, at a point where the robot previously turned left, it may be possible for it to instead turn right. This results in both an alternative instruction
\ell^{cf}_{t,i}
and a corresponding atomic command
\ell^{a,cf}_{t,i}
associated with the observation
\mathbf{o}_{t,i}
. Multiple such alternatives (or
counterfactuals
) are generated, potentially for the same observation, resulting in our relabeled dataset
\mathcal{D}_{c}=\{\mathbf{o}_{t,i},\ell^{cf}_{t,i},\ell^{a,cf}_{t,i}\}
.

Generating counterfactual trajectories.
With access to counterfactual instructions
\ell^{cf}_{i}
and their atomic commands
\ell^{a,cf}_{i}
in
\mathcal{D}_{c}=\{\mathbf{o}_{t,i},\ell^{cf}_{t,i},\ell^{a,cf}_{t,i}\}
, we now discuss our methodology for generating counterfactual trajectories. Using
\mathcal{D}_{a}
, we can train an atomic policy
\pi_{a}(\mathbf{a}|\ell^{a}_{t,i},\mathbf{o}_{t,i})
. Now, we can sample the action label
\mathbf{a}^{cf}_{t,i}\sim\pi_{a}(\mathbf{a}|\ell^{a}_{t,i},\mathbf{o}_{t,i})
for each
(\mathbf{o}_{t,i},\ell^{a,cf}_{t,i})\in\mathcal{D}_{c}
. This action label acts as the counterfactual
ending
that branches from the original trajectory in the
\mathcal{D}_{u}
and can be associated with the
high-level
command
\ell^{cf}_{t,i}
, resulting in a training tuple
(\mathbf{o}_{t,i},\ell^{cf}_{t,i},\mathbf{a}^{cf}_{t,i})
that we can add to the labeled training set
\mathcal{D}_{l}
, where t spans from the beginning of the original trajectory to counterfactual ending. We also include the non-counterfactual instruction labels
\bar{\ell}_{j,i}
associated with every image and true action in the trajectory to provide dense labels. This labeled training set can then be used to train a vision-language navigation policy as discussed in
Section

III
.

V
Implementation

We instantiate CAST for the task of natural language instruction following in visual navigation. The first step of CAST is designing a domain-specific decomposition of atomic behaviors and accessing a dataset with language instruction labels. However, there are no existing large-scale real-world datasets for the visual language navigation domain. Therefore, we design two components to implement CAST for navigation: 1) the atomic policy and 2) a hindsight relabeling pipeline to obtain language labels.

Atomic labeling and policy.
For each observation
(\mathbf{o}_{t,i},\mathbf{a}_{t,i})\in\mathcal{D}_{u}
, in this case the GNM data mixture, we first compute an atomic label
\ell^{a}_{t,i}
in the set {
turn right
,
turn left
,
adjust right
,
adjust left
,
go forward
,
stop
}, to create the intermediate dataset
\mathcal{D}_{a}=\{\mathbf{o}_{t,i},\ell^{a}_{t,i},\mathbf{a}_{t,i}\}
. Further details on how this discretization is performed are provided in the
Section

B
. This provides us with the training set for the atomic policy
\pi_{a}(\mathbf{a}_{t,i}|\ell^{a}_{t,i},\mathbf{o}_{t,i})
via supervised learning. Because the atomic labels
\ell^{a}_{t,i}
are computed directly from the actions
\mathbf{a}_{t,i}
, they correlate strongly with the action, and therefore
\pi_{a}(\mathbf{a}_{t,i}|\ell^{a}_{t,i},\mathbf{o}_{t,i})
follows these atomic language labels very well. We predict an action chunk, following recent work on robotic imitation learning
[
37
,
38
,
5
]
, which can equivalently be thought of as short “trajectory snippets.” The model representing
\pi_{a}
encodes each observation with an EfficientNet-b2
[
39
]
ConvNet, and incorporates the commands
\ell^{a}
by conditioning on a T5 language embedding
[
40
]
. The image and language embeddings are concatenated and passed through a Transformer to generate a context vector, which is then used to train an action-chunked diffusion model to predict the actions
[
5
]
, resulting in the atomic policy
\pi_{a}
.

Hindsight relabeling pipeline.
We start with an existing diverse collection of visual navigation datasets
[
41
]
. We generate multiple possible language instructions
\bar{\ell}_{j,i}
per robot trajectory (indexed by
j
), each describing the entire trajectory. We first prompt a VLM (see
Section

H
) with a subsampled sequence of observations from the trajectory,
\mathbf{o}_{1:K:T,i}
, and query it to describe the objects, structures, and the relative positions of those objects to each other and the robot. We then query the VLM to summarize these descriptions and format the result as
new descriptive instructions
for the robot to execute the trajectory. We encourage the VLM to generate instructions following a structured format to simplify the learning problem. These labels are generated without access to the raw robot actions and capture the inferred
intent
of the robot’s interaction with the environment.

Additionally, we found it useful to further filter and augment this set of labels in a second labeling phase, where we prompt the VLM a second time with the images, previously-generated labels, and the sequence of atomic labels along the trajectory. This prompt instructs the VLM to select the appropriate labels (i.e., consistent with the sequence of atomic labels), filtering out instructions that clearly do not align with the trajectory (e.g. "Move down the hallway on the right" would be filtered out if the atomic action sequence indicates the robot turns left). We additionally query the VLM for any additional labels based on the provided information. This results in a set of labels
\{\bar{\ell}_{j,i}\}
for each trajectory.

Training a policy.

We use CAST to generate synthetic data for training a navigation vision-language-action (VLA) model. We run our synthetic annotation pipeline on the GNM dataset
[
41
]
, which contains indoor and outdoor robot trajectories from several different robot embodiments, including small wheeled robots, quadrupeds, and ATV-like robots. We follow
[
4
]
and normalize the action spaces across our datasets, transforming the actions into Cartesian deltas that predict how much the robot should move in xy-coordinates. We train the policy to predict 16 action tokens, with an action dimension of 2 corresponding to the xy deltas and an action horizon of 8 steps. At test time, a separate controller converts these deltas into low-level velocities. See
Section

E
for more details on data processing.

With these data normalization and post-processing steps in place, we train a high-capacity language-conditioned policy on this augmented data. We start with the 3 billion parameter PaliGemma VLM
[
3
]
, and fine-tune it on the CAST dataset. The model consists of a SentencePiece
[
42
]
language tokenizer, which we add 128 tokens to represent the binned action values, a SigLIP 400M
[
43
]
vision model that encodes the input observations, and a Gemma 2B
[
44
]
language model, all of which are kept unfrozen during training. We call this model
CounterfactualVLA
, and use it for all subsequent experiments in this paper. For details on the model architecture and training, see
Section

F
.

VI-A

Does CAST enable more effective language-conditioned policies for navigation?

Our main hypothesis is that introducing counterfactual labels promotes more effective language following than the standard labels associated with the actions that were seen in the original dataset. The baseline that allows us to evaluate this hypothesis, which we call “hindsight labels” and the corresponding “standard VLA”, uses the same hindsight labeling scheme as our method, but without the counterfactual labels. We use the same navigation data mixture as the base dataset and follow the same fine-tuning procedure as for CounterfactualVLA with the hindsight labels.

Fig.

3
compares the performance of CounterfactualVLA against the standard VLA, along with the prior methods discussed in
Section

VI-B
. CounterfactualVLA demonstrates an average success rate of
53%
across several challenging vision-language tasks, demonstrating a
27%
improvement over the standard VLA. Although both policies demonstrate strong navigation capabilities, the standard VLA tends toward more generic navigation behaviors, largely ignoring language. The standard VLA succeeded most often in tasks where collision avoidance was required, such as “move between the rows of chairs”, but especially struggled in object navigation, only succeeding for objects that were well represented in the dataset (e.g. “person”, “garbage can”). The performance of CounterfactualVLA demonstrates that including the counterfactual labels produced by CAST significantly improves a model’s ability to follow complex language instructions, as compared to hindsight labels alone.

VII
Discussion

We presented CAST, a novel synthetic labeling method that uses VLMs to generate diverse counterfactual labels for robot trajectories. CAST addresses the challenge of training visual navigation policies to pay attention to language instructions by ensuring that the synthetic labels provide a variety of different language commands and action labels for the same set of observations, forcing policies to learn the relationship between language and actions. We use CAST to augment existing visual navigation datasets and then train a steerable navigation policy, CounterfactualVLA, using the augmented dataset and no additional human annotations. Our experiments demonstrate that label diversity is the most significant factor in enabling instruction following abilities in VLA models, improving over policies trained without counterfactual labels by 27%. CounterfactualVLA also outperforms several existing baselines for language-conditioned navigation by over 19%. We also show that using a VLM base model rather than a smaller ResNet + FiLM architecture is necessary for understanding and following complex language instructions and co-training on the CAST dataset and a dataset of atomic trajectories can improve low-level referential behavior. CAST shows a promising recipe for utilizing
existing
unlabeled robot datasets to train steerable robot policies, and in future work, we hope to explore how similar ideas can be used to improve instruction following capabilities in applications beyond navigation.

Limitations:
CAST requires a few design decisions that are specific to navigation, and might require additional effort to generalize to other domains, such as robotic manipulation. The atomic labels are generated with a heuristic segmentation method that would require more care to extend to other domains, and leverages the fact that atomic commands for navigation depend only on the robot’s motion, and not on images. The prompts for labeling the data also contain some generic domain knowledge that relates to robotic navigation. Lastly, while CAST focuses on augmenting the dataset by counterfactual action labels, we are still limited by the visual diversity of observations in the dataset. Combining CAST with large-scale simulation
[
46
]
, generative augmentations
[
47
]
, or cross-embodiment data
[
4
]
could be an interesting direction of future work. Additionally, automatically annotating with VLMs remains slow, imperfect, and the quality of annotations varies with the specific models and prompts (see
Section

H
). However, we remain optimistic that VLMs will continue to get faster, cheaper (eventually, open-source), and better grounded in the physical world in the future, making our method increasingly effective.

B
Atomic Discretization of Navigation Dataset

We use Cartesian waypoint representations for our robotic navigation task, and since these labels are purely functions of the robot’s Cartesian-space trajectory, they can be computed with simple automated heuristics that discretize the trajectory into segments. Each segment is determined by chopping the trajectory at points where the change in yaw exceeds a manually selected threshold, indicating a turn has occurred and labeling it appropriately. If a significant change in yaw does not occur over a set number of steps, in this case 10, then the chunk is labeled as
Go forward
or
Stop
based on the distance traveled. This yields the atomic labels
\ell^{a}_{t,i}
, along with lists of time steps
\{t^{a}_{i,j}\}
indicating the start of each atomic segment (which we will use later in our method). These labels are generated independently of
\mathbf{o}_{t,i}
, meaning they are strongly correlated with
\mathbf{a}_{t,i}
.

D
Description on Tasks

Object navigation:
The policy is instructed to go to a specific object in the environment. In each of the environments, the objects are placed on opposite sides of the robot’s starting position in front of the robot. The rollout is marked as successful if it reaches within 50cm of the target object.

Referential navigation:
The policy is instructed to interact with an object or structure in the environment in a referential way that requires spatial understanding. For example, “move to the right of the chair.” or “move to the table next to the pillar”. The rollout is marked as successful if the policy moves in the correct way without collision or to the correct object.

Continuous navigation:
The policy is instructed to interact with an object or structure in the environment that requires a continuous behavior. For example, “move along the wall". The trial is considered successful if the policy moves the robot to within 1m of the reference structure, for behaviors such as “move along …”, and/or continues the behavior for 2m or more.

E
Data Processing

We provide an overview of the data processing and tokenization decisions for training the VLA policy.

We bin the actions into 128 tokens, normalized range -1 to 1, using a normalization factor that corresponds to the average distance in meters between steps in the trajectory for a particular dataset. The observations are resized to 224 by 224 square images, without maintaining the aspect ratio. The trajectories are chunked with an action horizon of 8 with action dimension 2 corresponding to the cartesian coordinate deltas in x and y. Therefore, the model is trained to predict 16 action tokens.

F
Model Architecture and Training

We train the policy for 100,000 steps on a v4-8 TPU VM, taking approximately 50 hours, which can be accelerated to 20 hours with a v4-32 TPU pod with batch size 768. We use a batch size of 192 and a learning rate of 1e-4.
