Implementation of infection prevention and control strategies in Italian intensive care units: results from the SPIN-UTI network
Authors: Martina Barchitta, Andrea Maugeri, Elisabetta Campisi, Erminia Di Liberto, Giuliana Favara, Roberta Magnano San Lio, Fortunato D’Ancona, Antonella Agodi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare-associated infections (HAIs) are a persistent challenge in intensive care units (ICUs), further aggravated by Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR). Although the World Health Organization (WHO) Infection Prevention and Control Assessment Framework (IPCAF) provides a standardized method to evaluate the Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) capacity, limited data are available on its application in Italy and its association with HAI incidence in ICUs. This study aimed to assess IPC implementation within the Italian Nosocomial Infections Surveillance in Intensive Care Units’ network (Sorveglianza Prospettica delle Infezioni Nosocomiali nelle Unità di Terapia Intensiva, SPIN-UTI) using the WHO IPCAF tool, analyze its relationship with HAI and process indicators, and identify key barriers to effective IPC practices.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted in 43 adult ICUs participating in the 2023 SPIN-UTI surveillance programme. Data on HAIs and IPC-related process indicators were collected alongside IPCAF assessments and a targeted survey on implementation barriers. Descriptive statistics, Spearman’s correlation, and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were used for analysis.ResultsIPCAF total scores, calculated for each ICU, ranged from 350.0 to 782.5 (median: 610.0), with 51.2% of ICUs achieving an advanced IPC level. The lowest scores were observed in education, monitoring, and multimodal strategies domains. Common barriers included inadequate human and financial resources. A significant inverse correlation was found between IPCAF scores and the number of reported barriers (ρ = – 0.583; p < 0.001). No significant associations were found between IPCAF scores and HAI incidence, except for a positive correlation between HAI surveillance domain scores and central line–associated bloodstream infections rates (CLABSIs) (ρ = 0.424; p = 0.016). Strong positive correlations emerged between IPCAF domains and key process indicators, such as antimicrobial stewardship, oral decontamination, and catheter care.ConclusionsThe study shows substantial variability in IPC implementation across Italian ICUs and identifies key areas for improvement. While higher IPCAF scores correlate with better adherence to preventive practices, they do not directly predict HAI rates, possibly due to differences in surveillance sensitivity, lack of correlation with actual incidence, or the subjective nature of IPCAF responses. Strengthening education, staffing, monitoring and feedback systems is essential to enhance IPC effectiveness and patient safety.

Background

Over the past decade, the global healthcare community has faced increasing challenges linked to the prevention and control of Healthcare-Associated Infections (HAIs), particularly in the context of the rising threat of Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) [1, 2]. Notably, around 70% of infections caused by AMR pathogens are HAIs, emphasizing the critical link between Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) and efforts to combat AMR. This significant overlap highlights the essential role of effective IPC programmes in healthcare settings, not only to reduce the burden of HAIs, but also as a central strategy to prevent the emergence and spread of resistant organisms [3].

Numerous outbreaks have demonstrated how rapidly epidemic-causing pathogens can spread within hospitals and healthcare facilities, especially in Intensive Care Units (ICUs) [1, 4]. These high-risk environments are characterized by the frequent use of invasive procedures, immunocompromised patients, and a high prevalence of Multidrug-Resistant (MDR) organisms, making them particularly vulnerable to HAIs [1, 5]. HAIs remain among the most frequent complications associated with healthcare, with significant impacts on patient outcomes, length of stay, mortality, and healthcare costs [6–9].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the prevalence of HAIs affects approximately 7% of hospitalized patients in high-income countries and up to 15% in low-income settings [10]. Despite strong evidence supporting the effectiveness of core IPC strategies - such as hand hygiene, aseptic techniques, environmental cleaning, and antimicrobial stewardship - their practical implementation is often fragmented and influenced by local context, available resources, and institutional culture and the type of healthcare [5]. To effectively combat HAIs and AMR, healthcare systems require not only technical guidance but also robust tools to assess and strengthen their IPC programmes at the facility level [10].

In this context, the Infection Prevention and Control Assessment Framework (IPCAF) tool, developed by WHO in 2018, represents a critical milestone [11]. The IPCAF is a structured closed-formatted, standardized questionnaire/tool designed to support healthcare facilities in evaluating the implementation of IPC programmes based on the eight core components defined in the WHO Guidelines on Core Components of IPC Programmes.

Our study specifically focuses on the application of the WHO IPCAF tool within the Italian Nosocomial Infections Surveillance in Intensive Care Units’ network (Sorveglianza Prospettica delle Infezioni Nosocomiali nelle Unità di Terapia Intensiva, SPIN-UTI). The primary aim is to assess the level of IPC implementation in these high-risk settings and to explore potential associations between IPCAF scores and selected HAI and prevention indicators. In addition, the study includes a targeted survey to identify the main barriers to effective IPC implementation as perceived by ICU professionals. By adopting this targeted approach, the study aims to generate evidence to guide and strengthen IPC strategies within ICUs, thereby contributing to the broader improvement of patient safety and infection control practices across the healthcare system.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted within the framework of the SPIN-UTI project, a national surveillance programme on HAIs in Italian ICUs. Established in 2006 by the Italian Study Group of Hospital Hygiene (GISIO) of the Italian Society of Hygiene, Preventive Medicine and Public Health (SItI), the SPIN-UTI project is implemented in accordance with the European surveillance protocol of the “Healthcare-associated Infections Surveillance Network in Intensive Care Units” (HAI-Net ICU) [12]. Until 2022, the project was organised in biennial surveillance cycles covering the period from October 1st to March 31st of the following year. Starting in 2023, surveillance has been extended to cover, on voluntary basis, the entire calendar year. The current analysis was based on data from 43 adult ICUs located across Italy, all of which had participated in the 2023 SPIN-UTI surveillance edition. Only patients with an ICU length of stay greater than 48 h were eligible for inclusion. Participation was voluntary, and all collected data were anonymised at the facility level. No patient-identifiable information was included. The SPIN-UTI protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee “Catania 1” (Catania, Italy), under protocol numbers 111/2018/PO, 295/2019/EMPO, 277/2020/EMPO and 292/2022/EMPO. Further details on the surveillance protocol and study design have been reported in previous publications [13–17].

Surveillance of HAIs and prevention indicators

Data collection was conducted through a secure, web-based platform developed to collect detailed data. These included demographic characteristics, admission details, exposure to invasive devices, and infection-related variables.

HAI incidence was calculated using standard indicators: (i) patient-based incidence was defined as the proportion of patients with at least one HAI among all eligible patients; (ii) cumulative incidence was calculated as the number of HAIs per 100 patients; (iii) incidence density was expressed as the number of HAIs per 1000 patient-days. In addition to overall HAI metrics, incidence indicators were also stratified by infection type. Specifically, incidence density was calculated for the three main device-associated infections under surveillance: Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP), Central Line–Associated Bloodstream infections (CLABSIs), and Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infections (CAUTIs). These were expressed as the number of infections per 1000 device-days, using mechanical ventilation days, central line days, and urinary catheter days as denominators, respectively [12].

In addition to infection data, participating ICUs were required to report selected structural and process indicators related to IPC, in accordance with both the SPIN-UTI and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) surveillance frameworks. Reported indicators included compliance with oral decontamination protocols, implementation of non-supine patient positioning, adherence to management bundles, and the existence of formal antimicrobial stewardship initiatives [12]. These data were self-reported by local ICU teams using standardised definitions provided in the surveillance protocol.

Assessment of implementation of IPC strategies

To evaluate the implementation of IPC strategies, each ICU completed the WHO IPCAF, a structured closed-formatted, standardized self-administered questionnaire (tool) composed of eight core components: (1) existence of a formal IPC program; (2) availability and implementation of IPC guidelines; (3) staffing levels and bed occupancy; (4) IPC education and training; (5) surveillance of HAIs; (6) monitoring and auditing of IPC practices with feedback mechanisms; (7) implementation of multimodal strategies; and (8) adequacy of the built environment and availability of IPC-related materials and equipment. Each component is scored from 0 to 100, producing a total score ranging from 0 to 800. Based on the WHO classification, ICUs were stratified into four levels of IPC implementation: Inadequate (0–200), Basic (201–400), Intermediate (401–600), and Advanced (601–800) [5, 11]. In this study, all participating units fell into the Basic, Intermediate, or Advanced categories. The IPCAF questionnaires, translated in Italian, were completed by the local IPC teams or designated professionals at each facility.

Survey on IPC implementation barriers

To explore the contextual challenges in implementing IPC programmes, each ICU completed a structured self-administered questionnaire focusing on perceived barriers, with the aim of collecting information on the most common obstacles encountered and identifying areas where action is needed to improve existing strategies. To develop the questionnaire used in this study, we conducted a preliminary literature review aimed at identifying the main barriers commonly encountered by healthcare facilities in implementing IPC strategies. Based on this review, a list of relevant and recurring barriers was compiled [18–21]. These informed the design of a structured, ad hoc questionnaire specifically created for the purposes of this study, rather than relying on an existing validated tool. The questionnaire was uniform across all participating healthcare facilities and divided into thematic sections, allowing professionals to report challenges in a comprehensive and standardized manner. Response options covered a wide range of factors, including the clarity of the IPC programme, adequacy of staff training, support for adherence to procedures, clarity in the definition of responsibilities and objectives, availability of financial and human resources, communication effectiveness among staff, timeliness in updating protocols, and the perceived impact of IPC programme implementation on workload. Additionally, factors related to the use of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE), the adequacy of isolation facilities, and the organization of pathways for patients, visitors, and providers are considered. Facilities could indicate multiple barriers and there is a dedicated section to report additional difficulties not listed, as well as to suggest improvements for the application of IPC programmes within the facility. The total number of barriers reported by each ICU was recorded, and descriptive statistics were used to summarize the responses.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise IPCAF scores, HAI incidence indicators, perceived barriers, and compliance with prevention indicators collected from the involved facilities. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with ranges or means with standard deviations, based on the distribution. Correlations between IPCAF scores (total and by component) and HAI indicators, as well as between IPCAF scores and process/structural measures, were analysed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. The association between the total IPCAF score and the number of reported barriers was similarly assessed. Comparisons of HAI incidence indicators across IPC implementation levels were performed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, appropriate for non-parametric distributions. Statistical significance was set at a p-value of < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Assessment of IPC implementation

This study evaluated 43 ICUs using the IPCAF comprising eight core components of IPC. Each ICU obtained a numerical score for each component, and the sum of these values constituted the total IPC score for each facility (see Fig. 1). The total scores ranged from 350.0 to 782.5, with a median of 610.0, indicating a marked heterogeneity in IPC implementation and infrastructure across the evaluated ICUs. Based on total IPC scores, facilities were stratified into three performance tiers: Advanced (n = 22, 51.2%), Intermediate (n = 15, 34.9%), and Basic (n = 6, 13.9%). ICUs classified as Advanced demonstrated comprehensive IPC programs, clear operational guidelines, and continuous practice monitoring, suggesting a correlation between systemic investment and IPC performance. Conversely, ICUs in the Intermediate and Basic categories exhibited critical deficiencies, particularly in the domains of education and training, and staffing and workload management.

At the component level, the highest median scores were observed for IPC guidelines (median: 95.0; range: 37.5–100.0), adequacy of the built environment and equipment (median: 90.0; range: 75.0–100.0), HAI surveillance (median: 80.0; range: 32.5–100.0), and workload, staffing and bed occupancy (median: 80.0 range: 15.0–100.0). In contrast, the lowest-performing components included IPC education and training (median: 60.0; range: 3.0–100.0), monitoring and auditing with feedback (median: 67.5; range: 30.0–100.0), and multimodal strategies (median: 75.0; range: 0.0–100.0), identifying these as priority areas for targeted improvement initiatives, and IPC programmes (median: 75.0; range: 15.0–100.0).

Analysis of reported barriers to IPC implementation

In the context of the survey, multiple barriers to the effective implementation of IPC programs were identified across participating facilities. The most frequently cited obstacles were insufficient human resources (73.8%) and inadequate financial support (66.7%). These were followed by limitations in isolation infrastructure, reported by 38.1% of facilities. A secondary cluster of challenges, each reported by 33.3% of respondents, included the lack of clearly defined IPC objectives, inadequate staff training, ambiguous delineation of responsibilities, excessive workload, and limited physical space within healthcare environments. Less commonly reported barriers included reluctance to use PPE, cited by 11.9% of respondents due to discomfort, and by 14.3% due to perceived interference with patient communication. Additional challenges comprised delays in updating operational protocols (16.7%), limited multidisciplinary involvement in IPC program development (21.4%), and lack of clear, concise, and implementable IPC documentation (26.2%). Further issues included poorly defined pathways for patients, visitors, and suppliers (26.2%), communication breakdowns between staff and management (31.0%), and inadequate institutional support for adherence to IPC practices (31.0%). Notably, one potential barrier - “The control measures regarding the use of PPE and safety precautions are not timely, adequate, or sufficient” - was not reported by any facility. Additionally, 2.4% of institutions reported no experience of the proposed barriers, while 7.1% indicated an absence of any barriers in implementing IPC measures (see Fig. 2).

Quantitatively, the median number of reported barriers per ICU was 5 (range: 0–10). Importantly, a significant negative correlation was observed between the overall IPCAF score and the number of reported barriers (Spearman’s ρ = -0.583; p < 0.001), suggesting that higher-performing facilities encountered fewer impediments to IPC implementation.

Surveillance data on HAIs in participating ICUs

Among the 43 ICUs included in the study, 40 took part in the 2023 edition of SPIN-UTI project. These units surveyed a total of 1,989 patients who had ICU stays exceeding 48 h, accumulating 21,390 ICU patient-days. During the surveillance period, 513 HAIs were identified, involving 327 patients with at least one infection. This corresponded to a patient-based HAI incidence of 16.4%, an overall HAI incidence of 25.8 per 100 patients, and an incidence density of 24.0 infections per 1,000 patient-days. At the unit level, considerable variability in HAI metrics was observed. The incidence of patients with at least one HAI ranged from 0 to 85.0%, with a mean value of 20.6%. Similarly, the total HAI incidence varied from 0 to 160 per 100 patients, with a mean of 31.6, while incidence density values ranged from 0 to 106.3 per 1,000 patient-days, with a mean of 25.0.

When analyzed by infection type, VAP showed the widest range of incidence density, spanning from 0 to 434.8 per 1000 ventilator-days, with a mean of 25.2. CAUTIs had a more contained distribution, ranging from 0 to 24.7 per 1000 catheter-days, with a mean of 4.6. CAUTIs ranged from 0 to 125.0 per 1000 central line-days, with a mean of 6.2.

Comparison of HAI incidence indicators across ICUs classified according to the WHO IPCAF score (Basic, Intermediate, Advanced) did not reveal statistically significant differences, as determined by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (p > 0.05). Furthermore, correlation analyses between the IPCAF total score, as well as its individual components, and the HAI indicators did not demonstrate significant associations. The only exception was a statistically significant positive correlation between the HAI surveillance component score and the rate of CLABSIs (Spearman’s correlation coefficient = 0.424; p = 0.016).

The analysis revealed several statistically significant associations. Notably, compliance with antimicrobial stewardship protocols showed a positive correlation with higher scores in the Monitoring and Audit of IPC Practices with Feedback component (Spearman’s ρ = 0.407; p = 0.020). Similarly, adherence to oral decontamination procedures was strongly associated with higher scores in multiple domains, including the IPC Programme component (Spearman’s ρ = 0.600; p < 0.001), the Multimodal Strategies component (Spearman’s ρ = 0.682; p < 0.001), and again the Monitoring and Audit of IPC Practices with Feedback component (Spearman’s ρ = 0.734; p < 0.001). Furthermore, a significant positive correlation was observed between oral decontamination compliance and the overall IPCAF score (Spearman’s ρ = 0.631; p < 0.001), reinforcing the link between comprehensive IPC infrastructure and adherence to key preventive measures. The practice of non-supine patient positioning, another key preventive intervention, showed improved compliance in correlation with higher scores in the HAI Surveillance component (Spearman’s ρ = 0.689; p < 0.001), indicating that robust surveillance systems may foster better adherence to evidence-based practices. Additionally, compliance with Central Venous Catheter (CVC) management protocols exhibited positive correlations with several IPCAF domains. These included the IPC Programme (Spearman’s ρ = 0.537; p = 0.012), IPC Guidelines (Spearman’s ρ = 0.538; p = 0.014), IPC Education and Training (Spearman’s ρ = 0.514; p < 0.020), and HAI Surveillance (Spearman’s ρ = 0.636; p < 0.001), underscoring the multifactorial nature of effective catheter care and its dependence on institutional policies, staff training, and surveillance capacity. No significant correlation was evident between process and HAI incidence indicator.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of IPC programme implementation across a representative sample of Italian ICUs, using the standardized WHO IPCAF. The evaluation covered all core components of IPC: programme structure, guidelines, education and training, HAI surveillance, multimodal strategies, monitoring and feedback, staffing and workload, and the built environment. By providing both quantitative scores and qualitative insights, the IPCAF enables healthcare facilities to identify existing strengths, prioritize areas for improvement, and track progress over time, ultimately fostering a culture of continuous improvement and institutional accountability [5, 22].

Originally developed by the WHO to support standardized evaluation of IPC implementation, the IPCAF was first piloted in 46 countries and 181 healthcare facilities, involving 324 participants. The pilot phase demonstrated strong internal consistency and high interobserver reliability, with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.92, highlighting the tool’s robustness and suitability for global use [5]. Since its introduction, the IPCAF has been widely adopted across different geographic and income settings. A 2019 global WHO survey involving over 4,400 healthcare facilities in 81 countries reported a median IPCAF score of 605, reflecting an advanced IPC implementation level. However, only 15% of facilities met al.l minimum requirements across the eight core components, with notable disparities between high-income and low-income countries [22]. These findings underscore both the utility of the IPCAF in highlighting gaps and its potential as a driver of system-level improvement. In 2023–2024, WHO undertook a similar global survey, but based on the assessment tool of the WHO minimum requirements for IPC programmes [23] at the facility level, which represents a simplified version of the IPCAF tool used for the 2019 global survey [10]. In Europe, several countries - including Germany, Austria, and Turkey [24–26] - have applied the IPCAF at national scale, revealing persistent challenges in areas such as staffing, resource allocation, and adherence to multimodal strategies. Similarly, in low-resource settings like Malawi, the IPCAF has been instrumental in uncovering structural deficiencies, thereby informing targeted investments and policy initiatives [27]. In Italy, the application of IPCAF is relatively recent but growing. Previous research – such as a study conducted in the Piedmont region - has begun to investigate the relationship between the short version of the IPCAF questionnaire and HAI prevalence, suggesting that higher levels of IPC implementation are associated with lower infection prevalence [28].

Our analysis within the Italian ICU context shows substantial heterogeneity in IPC performance, infrastructure, and adherence to recommended practices. The wide range of total IPCAF scores and the stratification of facilities into Basic, Intermediate, and Advanced categories underscore a variable degree of IPC maturity among ICUs. While more than half of the ICUs achieved an Advanced level of IPC implementation, the presence - albeit limited - of units classified at the Basic level highlights the persistence of structural and organizational shortcomings that require targeted intervention.

The lowest-performing components - namely IPC education and training, monitoring and feedback, and multimodal strategies - highlight critical areas that should be prioritized in national and local quality improvement initiatives. These findings are consistent with international data, which frequently point to deficits in workforce development and system-wide accountability mechanisms as barriers to sustained IPC improvement [26, 27].

The survey on perceived barriers corroborates the IPCAF-based evaluation by illustrating the complex interplay between structural limitations and implementation challenges. Resource constraints - particularly in terms of human capital and financial support - emerged as the most frequently cited obstacles. Importantly, a statistically significant inverse correlation between the number of reported barriers and total IPCAF scores suggests that facilities with better IPC infrastructure and governance experience fewer hindrances to implementation. This relationship reinforces the notion that robust IPC systems are both a product of and a prerequisite for institutional support, adequate staffing, and a culture conducive to infection prevention [28].

Despite the observed variability in IPC performance, no statistically significant associations were identified between IPCAF scores and HAI incidence indicators. This finding aligns with previous literature suggesting that the relationship between IPC structure and infection outcomes may be attenuated by differences in case mix, ICU workload, and surveillance sensitivity [5, 22, 27]. Notably, a positive correlation between the HAI surveillance component and CLABSIs rates may reflect enhanced detection capacity in units with stronger surveillance systems, rather than higher true incidence. This phenomenon underscores the complexity of interpreting HAI rates in isolation and highlights the importance of accounting for surveillance intensity when benchmarking infection outcomes [29]. However other possible explanations could be reporting bias in filling the questionnaire or a low correlation between IPCAF score and HAI due to the complexity of the healthcare in ICU.

However, the analysis of process indicators yielded more robust associations. Strong positive correlations were found between specific IPCAF domains - particularly Monitoring and Feedback, IPC Programme, and Multimodal Strategies - and adherence to key preventive interventions such as oral decontamination, antimicrobial stewardship, and catheter care. These associations suggest that the presence of structured programmes and continuous performance monitoring could be linked to improved guideline adherence, thereby providing a pathway from institutional capacity to bedside practice [30–32]. Furthermore, the association between surveillance scores and compliance with non-supine positioning suggests and reinforces the central role of active monitoring in promoting evidence-based interventions.

Several limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference. While associations between IPC capacity and process indicators were observed, the temporal directionality of these relationships cannot be established. Longitudinal assessments would be necessary to evaluate whether improvements in IPC infrastructure translate into sustained changes in clinical practice and HAI outcomes over time. Second, although the IPCAF is a validated and widely adopted tool, it primarily relies on self-assessment, which may introduce reporting bias. Facilities with greater IPC awareness or institutional support may be more likely to accurately report strengths and weaknesses, while underperforming units may underreport deficiencies, particularly in the absence of external auditing. Third, the lack of a statistically significant association between the IPCAF score and HAI incidence likely reflects a combination of heterogeneity in surveillance sensitivity and case‑definition accuracy [33], the inherent subjectivity of self‑reported IPCAF data [34], and the fact that IPCAF - by design - captures only selected structural and process elements, omitting several context‑specific determinants of infection risk [25]. Moreover, the positive association observed between surveillance scores and reported bloodstream infection rates may reflect a detection bias, whereby institutions with more robust surveillance systems identify a greater number of cases. This phenomenon, previously described in the context of surgical site infection surveillance, suggests that higher reported rates may not necessarily indicate a true increase in incidence, but rather enhanced case ascertainment [35]. A key limitation of our analysis is that we did not explore associations between specific barriers and individual IPCAF components. While such analyses could provide valuable insights, especially given that certain barriers may have greater relevance depending on the ICU level or the specific IPC issue, they were not conducted due to statistical and methodological constraints. In particular, the relatively small sample size, the binary nature of barrier reporting, and the risk of inflated Type I error due to multiple comparisons limited the feasibility and reliability of such detailed subgroup analyses. Finally, although the study included a high number of ICUs with varying levels of IPC maturity, participation was voluntary and limited to units within the SPIN-UTI network. This may introduce a selection bias, as these ICUs are likely more engaged in infection prevention efforts than the national average. Consequently, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all Italian ICUs or to healthcare settings outside the network.

Conclusions

This study suggests that structured IPC programmes may play an important role in high-risk settings such as ICUs. The WHO IPCAF tool appeared useful in identifying structural and organizational differences across facilities. However, the absence of a clear correlation with HAI outcomes indicates that IPC capacity alone might not be sufficient to improve clinical results without consistent implementation and effective surveillance. The observed link between IPCAF scores and process indicators points to the framework’s potential value in supporting implementation efforts. Nonetheless, ongoing investment in training, staffing, and feedback systems is likely needed. Strengthening IPC programmes in Italian ICUs may benefit from policy actions that take into account both quantitative data and the perspectives of healthcare professionals on the ground.
