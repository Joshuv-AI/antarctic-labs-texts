Echoes of resilience: spatio-temporal analysis of five-decade battle against malaria in Rajasthan, India
Authors: Himmat Singh, Sangeeta Singh, Poonam Saroha, Kuldeep Singh, R S Sharma, Ravi Prakash Sharma, Jyoti Gupta, Sanjeev Kumar Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRajasthan state of India is exhibiting diverse malaria transmission patterns shaped by its varied ecological landscapes. This study explores five decades (1975–2023) of malaria patterns in different ecogeographical regions of Rajasthan.MethodsThe four ecogeographical regions of Rajasthan, namely Desert, Aravalli, Gangetic plain, and Hadoti Plateau, were taken into consideration to identify spatio-temporal malaria trend, Anopheles species diversity, and insecticide susceptibility status of malaria vectors with public health insecticides. Additionally, correlation analysis between malaria incidence (API) and average annual rainfall was conducted to understand the climatic influence on transmission dynamics.ResultsOver five decades, Malaria peaked in 1976 and 1996, followed by a sharp decline post-2000. High-risk areas like Aravalli and Hadoti Plateau require focused interventions combining community awareness, improved healthcare access, and climate-responsive strategies. Integrating ecological insights with innovative tools such as GIS mapping and interactive dashboards can enhance monitoring, guide targeted actions, and reduce vector density. Background

Malaria has long been a significant public health challenge in India, posing persistent difficulties for the country’s healthcare system. In 2023, 34 states and union territories along with 697 districts, achieved an Annual Parasite Incidences (API) of less than 1, attaining category-1 (Elimination phase; having API < 1 per 1000 population) status in line with India’s Malaria elimination goals for 2030 as outlined by the National Center for Vector Borne Diseases Control [1]. Globally, Malaria elimination is driven by the WHO Global Technical Strategy for Malaria 2016–2030 [2], which emphasizes reducing case incidence and mortality rates, preventing re-establishment, and strengthening surveillance. In India, these principles are reflected in the National Framework for Malaria Elimination 2016–2030 [3] and the National Strategic Plan 2023–2027 [4], which outline phased targets to achieve zero indigenous transmission nationwide by 2030.

Before 1953, Malaria was a major public health problem in India, with high incidence and mortality rates. At the time of independence in 1947, an estimated 75 million cases and 0.8 million deaths were reported [5]. Between 1953 and 1958, the National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP), later expanded into the National Malaria Eradication Programme (NMEP) [5], implemented interventions such as DDT spraying and other interventions, resulting in a dramatic decline in malaria cases and deaths, with near elimination by the mid-1960s. Zero deaths were reported in 1961, and annual cases fell to below 50,000. However, a resurgence occurred between 1965 and 1976, with cases increasing to 6.45 million by 1976.

Rajasthan state of India has a diverse geography, ranging from the arid desert terrain with its dry climatic conditions, the subtropical forests in the hilly Aravalli region, the fertile Gangetic plain with its alluvial soil, and the sub-humid Hadoti plateau. In the arid desert regions like western Rajasthan, despite low rainfall and extreme temperatures, urban malaria persists due to Anopheles stephensi breeding in household water storage [6, 7]. The subtropical forests of the Aravalli hills provide perennial water sources and dense vegetation, favouring stable transmission by Anopheles culicifacies [8]. In the fertile Gangetic plains, abundant irrigation and stagnant waters in agricultural fields support seasonal outbreaks driven by the same vector [9]. The sub-humid Hadoti plateau provides ideal mosquito breeding grounds as well as increases humidity of the atmosphere for survival of the mosquitoes, leading to frequent localized malaria cases [10].

Historically, the state has been recognized as a zone for unstable malaria transmission, this is mainly due to post-monsoon conditions, where water gets stagnant in shallow wetlands with inadequate drainage systems are created Because of the calcareous soil, creating the perfect mosquito breeding grounds and leads to sporadic outbreaks [7, 11]. Multiple studies have identified various factors that may have influenced malaria that may have contributed as the presence of irrigation canals, frequent population movements, specific ecological conditions, and underlying sociological aspects [12–15]. Anopheles stephensi and An. culicifacies have been identified as the primary malaria vectors across both urban and rural areas of Rajasthan. These species are responsible for transmitting Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium falciparum, the two parasites that significantly contribute to malaria cases in the state [16]. However, in 2023, all 33 districts in the state achieved an API less than 1 [1, 6]. Despite the efforts of both central and state governments through the National Framework for Malaria Elimination (2016–2030), along with initiatives such as indoor residual spraying, fogging, public awareness campaigns, and LLIN distribution, malaria remains a persistent challenge [15].

Rajasthan’s efforts to control Malaria demonstrate the challenges of controlling diseases in a region with diverse and complex conditions. Looking back at 50 years of malaria monitoring and prevention efforts is important to understand how the disease has changed over time in this region. This review helps to identify how factors like rainfall (flood and drought), insecticide susceptibility, and faunal composition have influenced malaria transmission. It also looks at the impact of key measures, such as controlling mosquitoes, early case detection, and raising public awareness. Rajasthan has made progress in lowering malaria cases, proving that these efforts work. However, challenges like drug resistance in parasites, Insecticide resistance in mosquitoes, and inequitable access to healthcare. This review provides valuable lessons for improving future strategies. It will also help Rajasthan play a key role in India’s goal of eliminating Malaria by 2030. The insights gained from this study can serve as a valuable reference for other regions facing comparable challenges, highlighting the importance of context-specific strategies in effectively combating disease transmission.

Methods

Study site

Rajasthan, the largest state in India, is located in the northwest of the country, between latitudes 23°03' and 30°12' North and longitudes 69°30' and 78°17' East. Situated in the extreme western part of the Indian subcontinent, it shares an international border with Pakistan and comprises 33 districts. Spanning an area of 342,239 km2, Rajasthan accounts for approximately 10.4% of India's total landmass [17]. The state boasts diverse physiographic features. To the west lies the arid expanse of the Great Indian Thar Desert, an extension of the Sahara Desert [18]. The Aravalli Range runs diagonally across Rajasthan, hosting deciduous forests and serving as a natural divider. The northeast region comprises the fertile Gangetic plains, while the southeast is marked by the humid conditions of the Hadoti plateau [13, 19] (Fig. 1). Each of these regions has unique geographic, climatic, and environmental characteristics [20]. Koppen classification also includes similar categories, classified under BWhw, BShw, Cwg, and Aw climate types [21].

Desert Region: This region covers around 60% of the state's area (175,000 km2), and constitutes the arid Thar Desert. Stretching across both India and Pakistan in the west, it is bounded by the Aravalli range to the east. This region has a fragile ecosystem and an extreme arid climatic condition. Annual rainfall ranges between 200 and 350 mm, while the average annual temperature is 25 °C, with extremes from 0 to 50 °C [22]. According to the Köppen classification, this region falls predominantly under the BWhw, with certain districts classified as BShw. It includes 11 Districts i.e., Barmer, Bikaner, Churu, Sri-Ganagnagar, Hanumangarh, Jaisalmer, Jalore, Jhunjhunu, Jodhpur, Nagaur, Pali [23].

Gangetic Plain Region: Located in eastern Rajasthan, this fertile region borders Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, and Madhya Pradesh. It is bordered by the Aravalli Range to the west and the Hadoti plateau to the south. The region experiences annual rainfall ranging from 259.3 mm, with an average temperature of 24.5 °C (varying between 5 and 44 °C). The terrain is characterized by alluvial plains formed by sediments from the Ganga and Yamuna rivers, and classified as Cwg according to the Köppen classification. The region comprises 7 Districts, i.e. Alwar, Bharatpur, Dhaulpur, Dausa, Karauli, Sawai-Madhopur, and Tonk.

Aravalli Region: Aravalli Range, one of the oldest mountain ranges in the world, dating back to the Proterozoic era [24]. Spanning approximately 692 km from Gujarat to Delhi, it passes through Rajasthan and serves as a natural barrier between the Thar Desert and the eastern plains. The region receives an average annual rainfall of 625 mm (ranging from 400 to 850 mm) and has an average temperature of 24.5 °C, with variations between 4 and 45 °C, and classified under the BShw in the Köppen classification It includes 10 Districts: Ajmer, Banswara, Bhilwara, Chittaurgarh, Dungarpur, Jaipur, Pratapgarh, Rajsamand, Sirohi, and Udaipur.

Hadoti Plateau Region: Located in southeastern Rajasthan, the Hadoti plateau encompasses the districts of Kota, Bundi, Baran, and Jhalawar. It is characterized by a humid climate and is bordered by the Malwa plateau to the northeast, the Aravalli Hills to the west, and Madhya Pradesh to the south. The Hadoti plateau's elevation varies from 300 to 900 m. Known for its fertile black soil, the region is irrigated by the Chambal River. The annual rainfall averages 900 mm (ranging from 700 to 1100 mm), and the temperature averages 36.3 °C, with a range of 3–46 °C [25], and falls under the Aw category in the Köppen classification. It comprises 4 districts: Kota, Jhalawar, Baran, and Bundi.

Data source

This review includes studies conducted in Rajasthan that focused on various aspects related to malaria, including disease prevalence, malaria vector insecticide susceptibility, the distribution and diversity of anopheline species, and the status of antimalarial drugs. Research on environmental factors such as floods and droughts in state transmission was also considered.

National, state and district-level Malaria data from 1961 to 1995, which includes malaria cases and API, were retrieved from the books Malaria and its Control in India [26], and Epidemiology and Control of Malaria in India 1996 [27]; 1996 to 2023 were obtained from the public domain records of the NCVBDC [1]. District-level Malaria data were available from 1975 onwards, except for 1985, for which data were unavailable.

Average annual rainfall of Rajasthan from 1975 to 2023 was obtained from the Ministry of Earth and Science, India Meteorological Department [28] and the India WRIS portal [29]. Population statistics were obtained from the Census of India [30]. Information on Malaria vectors, and other Anopheline species, Insecticide susceptibility status, and antimalarial drugs was gathered from published reports and peer-reviewed studies in the year between 1933 and 2024, through searches on PubMed and Google Scholar. In addition, records of malaria outbreaks, developmental projects, irrigation canal installation, droughts, and floods in Rajasthan were also obtained from the same search portals.

Data analysis

The annually collected total Malaria cases, blood slides examined, population, and rainfall from 1975 to 2023 were entered in MS Excel worksheet, categorized by district or ecogeographical regions for further analysis. The analysis included calculating API, the percentage of P. falciparum cases (Pf%), and the Annual Blood Slide Examination Rate (ABER) for Districts and ecogeographical regions using the following formulas:

API = (Confirmed malaria cases during the year ÷ Population under surveillance) × 1000

API = (Confirmed malaria cases during the year ÷ Population under surveillance) × 1000

Pf% = (Number of positive P. falciparum cases ÷ Total positive malaria cases) × 100

Pf% = (Number of positive P. falciparum cases ÷ Total positive malaria cases) × 100

ABER = (Number of blood slides examined during the year ÷ Population under surveillance) × 100

ABER = (Number of blood slides examined during the year ÷ Population under surveillance) × 100

The statistical data analysis, including t-tests and correlation, was carried out using IBM SPSS software version 26 [31] and Mann Kendell trend Analysis for rainfall conducted using R Studio Version R-4.4.3 [32]. The map was prepared using QGIS version 3.34.13, and the canals are traced from Google Earth Pro [33, 34]by directly visualizing KML files. Time series analysis was conducted from 1975, as granular district-wise data was available after 1974. A time series map was prepared by first averaging API data over the five-year interval and then creating the map using QGIS.

The data of Anopheles species composition and insecticide susceptibility were systematically recorded in Excel and analysed based on district or ecogeographical region.

Results

Spatio-temporal malaria trend, policies, and control strategies (State and Districts)

In 1947, India had a population of about 330 million, with an alarming 75 million Malaria cases. To combat this public health crisis, the Government of India launched the National Malaria Control Programme in 1953. This initiative was followed by the National Malaria Eradication Programme (NMEP) launched in 1953, which achieved success through a comprehensive approach. Key strategies included spraying dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT), early diagnosis, prompt treatment, vector control, and community engagement. As a result, Malaria cases dropped to less than 50,000 nationwide, with Rajasthan achieving an API of less than one by 1961. However, NMEP primarily focused on rural malaria, neglecting the urban region. Over time, the programme faced challenges such as increasing resistance to insecticides and drugs, rapid urbanization, and growing financial and administrative constraints, which hindered its continued effectiveness.

In 1976, India experienced a significant Malaria outbreak, with 6.4 million reported cases, of which Rajasthan contributed 6.38%, resulting in the state’s average API reached to 14.49 (Fig. 2). To address this, the Modified Plan of Operations (MPO) was launched in 1977, emphasizing integrated strategies and community participation to reduce Rajasthan’s API to below 5.

In 1982, the first National drug policy on Malaria was introduced, recommending the use of pyrimethamine in regions with chloroquine resistance [35]. These measures contributed to a rapid decline in API from 1980 to 1984. By the 1990s, synthetic pyrethroids such as Deltamethrin were introduced in public health programme to combat vector resistance to earlier insecticides like organochloride and organophosphate [36]. Between 1994 and 1997, almost all regions of India experienced malaria outbreaks, with Rajasthan’s API reaching up to 6.8 in 1996 due to floods and heavy rainfall. In response, the Government launched the Enhanced Malaria Control Project (EMCP), funded by the World Bank [37].

Another outbreak was observed in Rajasthan during 2001–2005 due to heavy rainfall in the western desert region. To mitigate this, the government introduced the Intensified Malaria Control Project (IMCP) in 2005 under the Urban Malaria Scheme [1, 38]. In 2010, the National Drug Policy on Malaria was revised to recommend artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) for P. falciparum, using Artemether-Lumefantrine (AL) or Artesunate + Sulfadoxine-Pyrimethamine (AS + SP). For P. vivax, chloroquine was advised for the blood-stage infection, with primaquine for relapse prevention. To further combat malaria, the Government launched National Framework for Malaria Elimination 2016–30 (NFME) in 2016 [3] and National Strategic Plan for Malaria Elimination (NSP) 2017–2022 in 2017 [4, 35]. These initiatives provided a detailed action plan to achieve malaria-free status through targeted interventions and enhanced monitoring. As a result, Rajasthan successfully reduced its API to below one across all districts [35] (Fig. 2).

On the district level, between 1975 and 1979, 22 districts reported an API greater than 5 (Fig. 3). However, with the consistent implementation of state and central government policies, such as the MPO, significant progress was achieved. By 1980–1984, 26 districts reported an API below 5, although districts in southeastern Rajasthan continued to experience high API levels. Between 1986 and 1990, the number of districts in southern Rajasthan with an average API greater than 5 dropped to nine. Between 1991 and 2000, this malaria hotspot (API > 5) shifted from eastern to western Rajasthan, with 9 districts. Enhanced Malaria control efforts from 2000 to 2004 led to further progress, with 31 districts reported API below 5. The western districts of Jaisalmer and Barmer remained exceptions. After 2005, almost all the districts successfully reduced their API to below 5. By 2016, all districts in Rajasthan reported an API less than 1, marking a significant milestone in malaria control efforts (Fig. 3). In 2021, Karauli, Dholpur, and Jhalawar Districts reported API zero, Sawai Madhopur districts reported zero API in 2022, and in 2023, Dholpur, Bundi, and Sawai Madhopur reported zero API, indicating efforts towards successful malaria elimination.

Malaria pattern (Ecogeographical regions)

The ecogeographical regions of Rajasthan—Aravalli, Desert, Gangetic plain, and Hadoti plateau—were analysed to study the variation in malaria distribution across these four distinct regions.

In the Aravalli region, a consistently high API was reported until 2001, followed by a sharp decline. The highest API of 24.43 was recorded in Ajmer in 1996. The desert region showed high variability in Malaria distribution until 2010, after which cases began to decline, with the highest API of 55.5 in 1995 in Jaisalmer. In the Gangetic plains, a high API pattern persisted till 1998, after which cases declined, with the highest API observed in 39.51 in 1996 at Bharatpur. In the Hadoti plateau region, Malaria incidence remained consistently low, with a peak API of 9.9 in 1984 at Bundi. After 2008, there was a rapid decline, and maintaining a low API and the ABER in almost all regions was above 10% except in 2020–21 due to the COVID-19 pandemic (Fig. 4).

Climatic correlation (Rainfall)

Correlation analysis between annual malaria incidences (API) and annual rainfall from 1975 to 2023 revealed a weak but statistically significant positive correlation with rainfall in the desert (r = 0.39; p = 0.006) and Gangetic plain (r = 0.35; p = 0.014), indicating that an increase in rainfall may contribute to malaria cases. The Aravalli ecogeographical region shows statistically non-significant and weak positive correlation (r = 0.07; p = 0.610), suggesting rainfall has minimal influence on malaria incidences. Whereas in Hadoti region, despite having comparatively higher annual rainfall than other ecogeographical regions, it revealed a weak negative and nonsignificant correlation with malaria incidences (r = −0.08; p = 0.58). This contrasting trend in Hadoti plateau highlights the complex relationship with malaria dynamics in relation to rainfall across western India. Furthermore, the Mann–Kendall test results also show that the trend in average annual rainfall across the state from 1975 to 2023 was not found statistically not significant (p = 0.181) despite exhibiting positive s and tau values.

Discussion

As compared to the Indian scenario of malaria transmission trend of India, Rajasthan has exhibited intermittent patterns, with certain years experiencing high transmission rates and others showing lower incidences. This variability suggests that climatic factors, along with anthropogenic factors, influenced the transmission dynamics of malaria in the state.

To overcome the water scarcity in the western arid deserts region of Rajasthan, in the early 1970s government of India initiated the construction of India’s largest irrigation canal, “Indira Gandhi Nahar Project (IGNP)”, and completed in 1983. The project aimed to supply water for drinking and agricultural purposes. It channeled water from Firozpur in Punjab to Pugal in Bikaner, eventually extending to Jaisalmer in the far west, covering approximately 9,245 km. While the canal significantly contributed to agricultural and economic growth by irrigating seven desert districts—Barmer, Bikaner, Churu, Hanumangarh, Jaisalmer, Jodhpur, and Sri Ganganagar—it also inadvertently facilitated the spread of malaria in the regions [23, 63].

The influx of malaria infected labourers from the endemic region [64] during the construction of IGNP and water seepage along the canal created breeding grounds for mosquito breeding, particularly An. culicifacies, the primary malaria vector in India [39]. Consequently, Jaisalmer and Barmer districts became the highest malaria contributors in the command area. Villages located near the canal, such as Chinu, Mohangarh Sadana, Raimala, and Ramgarh of Jaisalmer, reported a significant increase in malaria incidences. This underscores the inadvertent role of the canal in fostering mosquito proliferation and contributing to malaria outbreaks [11, 16, 65–68]. Similar observations have been reported during the construction of the Narmada canal at Jalore district of Rajasthan in 2008, and globally with large-scale canal irrigation projects, which have increased malaria cases [69]. Examples include the construction of dams in Ethiopia and Africa, as well as during the construction of canals in countries such as Egypt (Suez Canal) and Panama (Panama Canal) [70–76]. In Rajasthan, the distribution of malaria has not followed a uniform pattern. Data collected at five-year intervals reveal a shifting pattern of high-incidence districts, indicating localized variations in malaria transmission influenced by environmental and anthropogenic factors.

Rajasthan's diverse ecology is characterized by varying environmental conditions, including distinct soil types, undulating plains, hills, and desert landscapes. These different conditions might change the rainfall pattern of Rajasthan, as it receives the southwest monsoon June–September with large areas of western and central India receiving more than 90% of their total annual precipitation during the period, which remains less than 500 mm annually for the whole of Rajasthan and less than 250 mm in the desert region. And higher than 500 mm in South-eastern Rajasthan, which also receives monsoon rains arising from the Bay of Bengal [28].

ABER reflects the operational efficacy of the Malaria control programme and surveillance efforts. A higher ABER leads the better detection of malaria cases, which leads to a more accurate calculation of API. According to the NCVBDC guideline, the ABER for malaria should be at least 10% or higher. In 2021, Rajasthan and other parts of India experienced a decline in ABER due to the COVID-19 pandemic. However, by 2023, the ABER in Rajasthan showed a slight recovery, exceeding 10%, indicating effective monitoring of malaria endemicity in the region (Table 1) [1].

In India, the average Pf% increased significantly from 14% in 1975 to 60% in 2023, largely attributed to antimalarial drug resistance [77]. In contrast, Rajasthan's Pf% remained relatively stable, ranging from 9 to 10.11%. Among Rajasthan's regions, the Aravalli region exhibited a comparatively higher Pf%, influenced by its unique geography with hilly rocks, socioeconomic conditions such as poverty & limited healthcare access, and poor housing infrastructure, which delays diagnosis and treatment additionally, the region’s substantial tribal population, living in remote areas [78].

The Aravalli, Desert, or Plain regions have a lower API scatter plot median but exhibit greater variability due to more frequent or severe outbreaks that elevate their average API during specific periods. The Hadoti plateau maintains a consistent and stable level of malaria transmission, with fewer fluctuations or extreme outbreaks. This region has uniform environmental conditions that support steady transmission rates. This steadiness contrasts with the unpredictable and harder-to-manage malaria patterns observed in other regions (Fig. 6).

The present study also shows that, among six primary malaria vectors identified in India [79], Rajasthan has documented An. stephensi, An. culicifacies and An. fluvitalis, with An. annularis and An. subpictus as secondary vectors. Across all ecogeographical regions of Rajasthan, An. stephensi and An. culicifacies remain the most prevalent dominant and primary malaria vector [79] (Table 2). The heterogeneity observed in Anopheles species composition across different regions of Rajasthan may be due to the limited number of entomological studies conducted in the Hadoti Plateau, Gangetic Plain, and Aravalli region of Rajasthan. Addressing this limitation is critical because region-specific vector composition directly affects transmission dynamics and the efficacy of focused malaria control strategies. Without reliable, localized data, control strategies risk being inadequate or misaligned with the real vector ecology of these understudied areas.

Mostly existing anopheles faunal data based on previous study by Christopher 1933 [54] Several previously reported species, such as Anopheles jeyporensis, Anopheles aconitus, Anopheles moghulensis, Anopheles karwari, Anopheles tessellatus, Anopheles theobaldi, and Anopheles jamesii have not been recorded in the collection after 1933, with this species like Anopheles multicolor, Anopheles turkudii and Anopheles maculatus recorded in a few recent studies of Rajasthan [39, 54] (Table 2). Further research is required to confirm the extinction status of various species, particularly in the western part (Desert) and southern (Hadoti plateau) region of the state. This change may be concurrently with the construction of India's largest canal IGNP, and the development of extensive manmade irrigation canals from the Chambal River [10]. These water canals created stagnant water pockets due to seepage, which serves as an ideal breeding habitat for mosquitoes; such ecological transformation may lead to change in faunal species composition and distribution [16, 80]. Additionally, there are gaps in our knowledge of species diversity and distribution because of the inadequate faunal monitoring across the state. Therefore, it is crucial to conduct routine mosquito faunal surveys to track dynamics and facilitate efficient vector management strategies.

Both An. culicifacies and An. stephensi developed resistance to organochlorides and organophosphates in all regions, and susceptibility to Synthetic pyrethroids means it remains an effective public health insecticide. In State, the emergence of double resistance in primary vectors poses a significant challenge to vector control efforts of management programmes by narrowing the range of chemical interventions and highlighted the need for regular resistance monitoring and the adoption of alternative or integrated vector control strategies [56, 58, 59] (Table 2).

Factors affecting the dynamics of malaria incidence in Rajasthan

Rainfall and malaria correlation: The positive correlation between Rainfall and malaria incidences in the Gangetic Plain, Aravalli and Desert region can be attributed to increased rainfall creating favorable conditions for mosquitoes breeding [7], while the negative correlation observed in the Hadoti plateau region depicts that, despite receiving relatively high rainfall than other regions, its rocky terrain, part of Vindhanchal Mountain Range [20], has minimal surface soil and slopes, these factors might help in flushing away rainwater or larvae faster than other areas this might reduce the availability of outdoor breeding sites for mosquitoes be one of the reasons for the negative correlation with respect to rainfall [81] (Fig. 7).

The regression analysis plot between Annual Parasite Incidence (API) and annual rainfall across different ecogeographical regions of Rajasthan (Desert, Aravalli, Hadoti plateau & plain) from 1975 to 2023, reveals a statistically significant weak positive correlation between malaria incidence and rainfall in the Desert (r = 0.39; p = 0.006) and Gangetic Plain (r = 0.35; p = 0.014) regions. In contrast Aravalli region shows a non-significant and weak positive correlation(r = 0.07; p = 0.610), while the Hadoti Plateau region demonstrates a non-significant weak negative correlation (r = −0.08; p = 0.580)

Flood induced outbreaks: Outbreaks of malaria have been frequently reported in Rajasthan, particularly in the Gangetic plain and Desert regions, often triggered by heavy rainfall or flooding [82]. In the Gangetic plain region, significant outbreaks were observed in Bharatpur and Alwar [83] following intense rainfall during periods such as 1975–76, 1983–84, and 1996–97, among others (Fig. 2). The Desert region, encompassing areas such as Barmer, Bikaner, Pokhran, Kawas, Jaisalmer, and Jodhpur, experienced Malaria outbreaks in 1975–76, 1979, 1985, 1994, 2001, 2004, and 2006 [83–86].

Drought and malaria incidences in Rajasthan: India experienced several droughts from the pre-independence era to the present, with notable occurrences in 1877,1901, 1918, and 1987 being the most widespread, affecting more than 60% population with a high death during between 1965 and 1967 [87]. Rajasthan has similarly faced major droughts in recent decades, particularly in 1972, 1979, 1987, 2000, and 2002. While droughts are common in the desert area of Rajasthan, prolonged droughts initiate out-migration of inhabitants along with the livestock. The migrating population comes back as favorable conditions are evident mostly during post-rains with a potential malaria risk [46]. Although Malaria records before 1961 were unavailable. Historical drought was documented as far back as 1362 [87]. Malaria reports from 1975 indicate that drought reduced the malaria burden (Fig. 2), likely due to out-migrating populations and decreased outdoor mosquito breeding sites.

Tribal and forest malaria: Tribal communities make up 13.4% of Rajasthan's total population, with several groups closely linked to forest ecosystems, including the Bhil, Garsiya, Damor, Mina, Meo, Banjara, and Kathodi [88]. These tribes are primarily concentrated in the southern Aravalli region districts of Udaipur, Banswara, and Dungarpur. Additionally, tribes such as the Mirasiya, Kanjar, Rabari, and Sansi are also found in Rajasthan, particularly in districts like Sawai Madhopur and Sirohi, although they are not associated with forest malaria. Malaria remains a significant public health issue among tribal populations, who face challenges such as high population growth rates, limited health-seeking behaviour, inadequate healthcare infrastructure, and poor transportation access to health centers [59, 78, 89–93].

Desert malaria: Desert malaria occurred in arid and semi-arid regions where malaria transmission takes place in non-conducive conditions due to migration, water storage sources, i.e., underground tanks “Tanka & Beri” nomadic population, and vector adaptation to man-made containers [82]. In desert regions, "desert malaria" is often considered seasonal, with the mosquito species An. stephensi playing a key role in maintaining transmission throughout the year, but in certain situations, An. culicifacies" can become the dominant vector, particularly when conditions favours its breeding and survival, effectively "taking over" as the primary malaria carrier in the area; this shift can be influenced by factors like changing water availability and environmental condition [6].

Migrant malaria: In Rajasthan, transmission of malaria from endemic areas to non-endemic areas of the state is observed due to the movement of people, which leads to the outbreak and reintroduction of parasites from eliminated areas [13]. Migration occurs for various reasons in a state, i.e., migration of workers for economic opportunities [53, 94, 95], urbanization [96], tourism and travel [97], and cultural and social reasons [13]. Moreover, migration among desert communities like Raika and Rewari, who move with their livestock to neighboring states like Madhya Pradesh and Gujarat, contributes to parasite transfer from endemic areas to Rajasthan also observed [46, 98, 99].

G6PD deficiency: Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, a genetic condition, has been observed among Rajasthan's tribal populations, including the Bhil, Damor, Garasia, and Mina communities [89]. This condition offers partial protection against P. falciparum by making infected cells more susceptible to early destruction. While G6PD deficiency provides some defense against malaria, it also presents challenges in treatment, particularly with certain antimalarial drugs. In Rajasthan's mixed population, the prevalence of G6PD deficiency is approximately 1.8%, with notable cases among the Bhil community in Udaipur [89–91, 100].

Duffy gene: Duffy glycoprotein acts as a receptor for P. vivax. In the Indian population, three different alleles were characterized at the genetic level: FY*A, FY*B, and FY*O type. As there were no Duffy-negative individuals could be found in the Rajasthan population, it means the northwestern population of India seems to be susceptible to P. vivax infection [101, 102].

Awareness and compliance: In Rajasthan state, Awareness and prevention about malaria transmission exists, the population aware of the symptoms (fever, chills) and transmission through mosquito bites. However, knowledge about the role of the Plasmodium parasite, preventive measures like the use of LLINs, and the importance of seeking timely medical intervention remains limited. Compliance with prevention measures is often low due to several socio-economic and environmental challenges. The use of LLINs and participation in vector control measures like indoor spraying are hindered by accessibility issues, especially in tribal areas. Additionally, cultural beliefs and traditional practices play a significant role in undermining malaria control efforts. For instance, people may not fully adhere to prescribed malaria treatments or might rely on traditional remedies [15, 103].

Drug resistance in the community: In response to the growing resistance to chloroquine [104], ACT was introduced as the first-line treatment for malaria in Rajasthan. More studies should be conducted to monitor the emergence of resistance to ACT, particularly to artemisinin components of the combination.

Severe malaria: Severe Malaria is a challenge and reported in nearly 25 countries across the world mostly due to P. falciparum [105]. In India cases of severe malaria has been reported from Rajasthan and several other states including Gujarat, Odisha, Maharashtra, West Bengal, Jharkhand, and Karnataka [106–111], Severe anaemia, cerebral malaria, severe thrombocytopenia, Acute renal failure, and hypoglycaemia are commonly diagnosed in severe malaria. In Rajasthan, P. falciparum has been reported with high severity and fatal outcomes, particularly in the western part of the state. Factors like the construction of the Indira Gandhi canal, afforestation, and migration of labour linked to the increasing malaria incidences in this region. Although P. falciparum is generally associated with non-severe, relapsing malaria, there are reports of fatal and multidrug-resistant cases emerging from areas like Bikaner and Barmer, this poses may challenge to efforts towards malaria elimination [85].

In Rajasthan, cases of Malaria in Pregnancy (MiP) recorded the highest rates of P. falciparum-associated abortions/miscarriage and stillbirths among MiP women presenting with severe malaria. Moreover, clinical P. vivax infection in Rajasthan was associated with a fivefold increased risk of maternal anaemia [112], hypoglycaemia (4%), and severe thrombocytopenia (56%) [113]. Moreover, in other studies, cerebral malaria (76%), acute renal failure, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and pulmonary oedema (13.3%) are also diagnosed in MiP women of Rajasthan [85, 114, 115]. The drug regime in Rajasthan for such complicated malaria are according to the national drug policy, but there is a lack of studies on sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine and artemisinin resistance in MiP so further research, particularly longitudinal studies, are essential to monitor drug resistance patterns and their impact on maternal and fetal health outcomes [116].

Future research: This review highlights that the majority of the state’s research on Anopheles diversity and vector biology has been concentrated in the Desert region. However, for a more comprehensive understanding of vector behaviour, breeding patterns, and disease transmission dynamics, similar efforts should be extended in the Aravalli, Hadoti Plateau, and Gangetic plain region. Future research should be focused on the identification of gaps in the comprehensive approach, mainly focused on malaria treatment, resistance status in both the malaria vectors and the Plasmodium parasite. Such efforts support malaria elimination and its sustainability, along with this longitudinal research on antimalarial drug resistance, MIP, status of G6PD deficiency, are crucial for guiding the refinement and upgradation of treatment regimens at the state level.

Although malaria cases are declining in Rajasthan, the state continues to face outbreaks of other vector-borne diseases such as Dengue [117], Zika [118, 119], and Scrub typhus [120]. In addition, sporadic cases of Japanese encephalitis [121, 122], Filariasis [123], Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever (CCHF) [124], Chandipura virus [125, 126] have also been reported. To strengthen surveillance and response mechanisms, the development of a real-time digital dashboard [127], integrated with Geographic Information Systems (GIS), is essential. This integration enables spatial analysis and visualization of disease patterns, helping to identify hotspots, track vector distribution, and support timely decision-making [128]. Such a system would significantly enhance the monitoring, management, and targeted control of vector-borne diseases across diverse ecological zones of the state.

Conclusion

This study provides important insights into Rajasthan’s malaria trends, highlighting the state’s complex ecological diversity and the evolution of disease transmission over five decades. The analysis of surveillance data, rainfall patterns, and vector distribution reveals distinct regional differences in Malaria burden. While Rajasthan has made notable progress, evidenced by API values dropping below one in all districts since 2016, certain ecogeographical zones, particularly the Aravalli and Desert regions, continue to report higher malaria risk. The Hadoti plateau, in contrast, shows low and stable transmission, indicating the influence of topography and hydrology on vector ecology.

Rainfall remains a key determinant of malaria dynamics in the state. Most regions show a positive correlation between rainfall and malaria incidence, except the Hadoti plateau, where transmission appears independent of precipitation patterns. Despite a general decline in P. falciparum prevalence across Rajasthan, the Aravalli region consistently reports higher Pf%, underscoring the need for targeted interventions in this area. These findings call for more nuanced, region-specific malaria control strategies rather than a one-size-fits-all approach.

To eliminate Malaria in Rajasthan by 2030, several critical actions must be prioritized. Strengthening disease surveillance using real-time digital tools such as GIS and Dashboard will help in identifying early detection and timely response, especially in high-risk and remote areas. Regional vector control programmes should be adapted to address evolving insecticide resistance, including the use of effective alternatives and integrated vector management. Primary healthcare services must be empowered through expanded diagnostic capacity and continuous training of health workers, particularly for managing severe malaria and malaria in pregnancy.

In addition, addressing drug resistance requires regular monitoring of treatment efficacy and rational use of antimalarial drugs. Climate-adaptive strategies are also essential, considering the increasing frequency of extreme weather events that disrupt transmission patterns. Promoting behavioural change and ensuring the use of personal protection methods like insecticide-treated nets, especially among tribal and mobile populations, will improve community-level compliance and outcomes.

Finally, inter-sectoral collaboration among government agencies, research institutions, and global health partners is vital for sustaining progress. Investment in innovation, operational research, and public engagement will support Rajasthan’s journey toward malaria elimination. With focused planning, ecological sensitivity, and political commitment, the state can serve as a model for other regions striving to eliminate malaria and maintain it in the post-elimination phase.
