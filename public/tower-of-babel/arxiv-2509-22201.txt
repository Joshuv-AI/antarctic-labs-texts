Tubular partitions and representations of the quantum automorphism group of a homogeneous rooted tree Thanks: Brownlowe was supported by the Australian Research Council grant DP200100155.
Nathan Brownlowe, David Robertson

Abstract
. We introduce the rigid tensor category of tubular partitions, and use it to provide a combinatorial model for the representation category of the quantum automorphism group of a homogeneous rooted tree.



1.
Introduction

Pontryagin duality says that a compact abelian group
G
can be reconstructed from its dual group
\hat{G}
. Classical Tannaka-Krein duality extends this idea to non-abelian compact groups
G
, where the dual group is replaced by the category
\operatorname{Rep}(G)
of finite-dimensional unitary representations of
G
. While
\operatorname{Rep}(G)
is not in general a group, the structure of
\operatorname{Rep}(G)
can be used to reconstruct
G
.

Classical Tannaka-Krein duality has been extended in various directions, including to the setting of compact quantum groups. In
[
15
]
Woronowicz introduced compact quantum groups as unital
C^{*}
-algebras equipped with a coassociative comultiplication and satisfying certain density conditions. The definition is such that a
commutative
compact quantum group is just the
C^{*}
-algebra of continuous functions on a compact group. Tannaka-Krein duality was developed by Woronowicz in
[
16
]
for compact
matrix
quantum groups, showing that any compact matrix quantum group can be reconstructed from the category of its finite-dimensional unitary representations, which is now a rigid
C^{*}
-tensor category that is non-symmetric. The non-symmetry of this category reflects the non-commutativity of the underlying
C^{*}
-algebra. In
[
12
]
Wang showed that Woronowicz’s results extend naturally to cover all compact quantum groups.

An important class of compact quantum groups are the quantum symmetric groups
S_{n}^{+}
introduced by Wang in
[
13
]
in his quest to find the correct notion of a quantum permutation of
n
points. The underlying
C^{*}
-algebra of
S_{n}^{+}
is the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by the entries of a magic unitary matrix—an
n\times n
matrix of orthogonal projections where each row and column sum to
1
(meaning
S_{n}^{+}
is a compact matrix quantum group). When
n\leq 3
,
S_{n}^{+}
is commutative and agrees with the classical symmetric group
S_{n}
; for
n\geq 4
,
S_{n}^{+}
is non-classical and is infinite-dimensional as a
C^{*}
-algebra.

The representation category of
S_{n}^{+}
is very well understood, and forms part of the study of easy quantum groups. In
[
1
]
Banica and Speicher introduced a class of compact quantum groups whose representation category can be described by a category of partitions; these quantum groups are now commonly known as easy quantum groups. Symmetry of this representation category corresponds to the category of partitions containing a crossing partition, and the corresponding
C^{*}
-algebras are commutative. Any non-crossing category of partitons therefore corresponds to a quantum group, and the category of
all
non-crossing partitions corresponds to the quantum symmetric groups of Wang. The wider class of easy quantum groups have given rise to a number of new examples of compact quantum groups, and their classification was completed in
[
11
]
after work in
[
1
,
2
,
14
,
9
,
10
]
.

The notion of a quantum permutation of
n
points leads naturally to questions of quantum analogues of automorphisms of more general objects. For
G
a finite graph, the quantum automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}^{+}(G)
of
G
was introduced by Bichon in
[
3
]
, and is the universal compact quantum group coacting on the vertex set of
G
in an adjacency preserving way. The underlying
C^{*}
-algebras can again be described as a universal
C^{*}
-algebra by imposing additional relations on the magic unitary defining the quantum symmetric group of the vertex set. The problem of whether a given finite graph has quantum symmetry or not, or in other words, whether the underlying
C^{*}
-algebra is commutative, is still open in full generality. It is known that almost all graphs have no quantum symmetry
[
7
]
, which is a quantum analogue of the classical result that in a probabalistic sense almost all graphs have no classical symmetry.

While it is uncommon for a graph to admit quantum symmetry, it was shown in
[
5
]
that almost all trees admit quantum symmetry. A special class of trees was considered by the authors in
[
4
]
, where we examined the quantum automorphisms of infinite rooted homogeneous trees. In particular, for a finite set
X
we introduced the quantum automorphism group
\mathbb{A}_{X}
of the infinite rooted homogeneous tree
X^{*}
, and we used
\mathbb{A}_{X}
to introduce the notion of self-similarity for compact quantum groups.

This paper is devoted to studying the representation category of
\mathbb{A}_{X}
. In
[
4
]
,
\mathbb{A}_{X}
is shown to be an infinitely iterated free wreath product of the quantum symmetric group
S_{X}^{+}
, giving a quantum analogue of the description of the classical automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(X^{*})
as an infinitely iterated wreath product of the symmetric group
S_{X}
. This suggests that the structure of the representation category of
\mathbb{A}_{X}
should be governed by combinatorial data involving categories of partitions, as is the case for quantum symmetric groups. Indeed, in
[
6
]
Lemeux and Tarrago describe the representation category of a free wreath product of a compact quantum group and a quantum symmetric group as a category of decorated partitions. In this paper we define a higher-dimensional partition we call a
tubular partition
, and we show that the category of all tubular partitions determines the representation category of
\mathbb{A}_{X}
.

In section
2
we recall the necessary definitions and results about compact quantum groups and their representation categories, including Woronowicz’s (and Wang’s) notion of Tannaka-Krein duality. In section
3
we define the category of tubular partitions and we look at its structure. In section
4
we state our main result that the rigid
C^{*}
-tensor category determined by the cateogry of tubular partitions is the representation category of
\mathbb{A}_{X}
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we state the required definitions and results concerning representations of compact quantum groups, as well as the definition of the quantum automorphism group
\mathbb{A}_{X}
of of a homogeneous rooted tree.

2.1.
Compact quantum groups and their representations

The following notion of a compact quantum group was introduced in
[
17
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

[
17
, Definition 1]

A
compact quantum group
is a pair
(A,\Delta)
where
A
is a unital
C^{*}
-algebra and
\Delta:A\to A\otimes A
is a unital
*
-homomorphism such that

(1)

(\Delta\otimes\operatorname{id})\Delta=(\operatorname{id}\otimes\Delta)\Delta

(2)

\overline{(A\otimes 1)\Delta(A)}=A\otimes A=\overline{(1\otimes A)\Delta(A)}
.

We call
\Delta
the
comultiplication
and (
1
) is called
coassociativity
.

We include the following definitions for completeness; more details about the representation theory of compact quantum groups can be found in
[
8
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
(A,\Delta)
be a compact quantum group. A
finite-dimensional unitary representation

u
of
A
is a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and a unitary
u\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\otimes A
satisfying (using the standard leg-numbering notation)

(2.1)

(\operatorname{id}\otimes\Delta)(u)=u_{12}u_{13}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\otimes A\otimes A.

If we choose a basis
\{e_{i}:1\leq i\leq d_{u}:=\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{H})\}
and identify
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\otimes A
with
M_{d_{u}}(A)
then
u=(u_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{d_{u}}
and (
2.1
) becomes the identity

\Delta(u_{i,j})=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{u}}u_{i,k}\otimes u_{k,j}.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
u
and
v
be finite-dimensional unitary representations of a compact quantum group
(A,\Delta)
on Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
. An
intertwiner
from
u
to
v
is a linear map
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
satisfying

(T\otimes 1_{A})u=v(T\otimes 1_{A}).

2.2.
The category of finite-dimensional unitary representations
\operatorname{Rep}(A)

The objects of
\operatorname{Rep}(A)
are finite-dimensional unitary representations
u
of
A
, and a morphism from
u
to
v
is an intertwiner
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
. The identity morphism
\operatorname{id}_{u}
is the identity intertwiner
I\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and composition of morphisms is the usual composition of linear maps. Given
u,v\in\operatorname{Rep}(A)
, the tensor product is

u\otimes v=u_{13}v_{23}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{K})\otimes A.

This turns
\operatorname{Rep}(A)
into a
C^{*}
-tensor category. In fact,
\operatorname{Rep}(A)
is a rigid
C^{*}
-tensor category, where the dual of
u
is the contragredient representation
u^{c}
.

2.3.
Tannaka-Krein duality for compact quantum groups

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a complete concrete rigid
C^{*}
-tensor category. A pair
M=(B,\{v^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}})
is a
model
of
\mathcal{R}
if

(i)

B
is a unital
C^{*}
-algebra

(ii)

for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}
,
v^{\alpha}
is a unitary element of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\alpha})\otimes B
where
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
is a finite-dimensional Hilbert space

(iii)

v^{\alpha\otimes\beta}=v^{\alpha}\otimes v^{\beta}
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{R}

(iv)

(T\otimes 1_{B})v^{\alpha}=v^{\beta}(T\otimes 1_{B})
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{R}
and
T\in\operatorname{Hom}(\alpha,\beta)
.

Definition 2.5
.

Suppose
\mathcal{R}
is generated by a set of objects
N\subset\mathcal{R}
. A pair
(B,\{u^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in N})
is called
\mathcal{R}
-admissible
if there is a model
M=(B,\{v^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}})
with
u^{\alpha}=v^{\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in N
. An
\mathcal{R}
-admissible pair
(B,\{u^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in N})
is called
universal
if, for any other
\mathcal{R}
-admissible pair
(C,\{w^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in N})
there exists a
C^{*}
-homomorphism
\varphi:B\to C
such that

(\operatorname{id}\otimes\varphi)u^{\alpha}=w^{\alpha}

for all
\alpha\in N
.

The following result was first proved in
[
16
]
for compact matrix quantum groups, and then extended to compact quantum groups in
[
12
, A.3. Theorem]
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a complete concrete rigid
C^{*}
-tensor category generated by
N\subset\mathcal{R}
. Then there is a unique universal
\mathcal{R}
-admissible pair
(A,\{u^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in N})
with uniquely determined model
(A,\{u^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}})
such that
A
is a compact quantum group with comultiplication
\Delta
, and for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}
,
u^{\alpha}
is a finite-dimensional unitary representation of
(A,\Delta)
on
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
.

2.4.
Quantum automorphisms of a rooted homogenous tree

Let
X
be a finite set, and consider the infinite homogeneous rooted tree
X^{*}
. Recall from
[
4
, Definition 3.2]
and
[
4
, Theorem 3.4]
that the quantum automorphism group
X^{*}
is the compact quantum group
(\mathbb{A}_{X},\Delta)
, where
\mathbb{A}_{X}
is the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by elements
\{a_{u,v}:u,v\in X^{n},n\geq 0\}
subject to the relations

(1)

a_{\varnothing,\varnothing}=1
,

(2)

for any
n\geq 0,u,v\in X^{n}
,
a_{u,v}^{*}=a_{u,v}^{2}=a_{u,v}
, and

(3)

for any
n\geq 0,u,v\in X^{n}
and
x\in X
,

a_{u,v}=\sum_{y\in X}a_{ux,vy}=\sum_{z\in X}a_{uz,vx}.

The comultiplication
\Delta:\mathbb{A}_{X}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{A}_{X}
satisfies

\Delta(a_{u,v})=\sum_{w\in X^{n}}a_{u,w}\otimes a_{w,v},

for all
u,v\in X^{n}
and
n\geq 1

Remark 2.7
.

Note the following consequences of the defining relations of
\mathbb{A}_{X}
:

(i)

As is pointed out in
[
4
, Remarks 3.3]
, we have
a_{x,z}a_{y,z}=\delta_{x,y}a_{x,z}
for all
x,y,z\in X
.

(ii)

If we take
u,v=\emptyset
in relation (3) above, then we get
\sum_{y\in X}a_{x,y}=\sum_{x\in X}a_{x,y}=1_{\mathbb{A}_{X}}
for all
x,y\in X
.

Notation 2.8
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we write
a^{(n)}:=(a_{u,v})_{u,v\in X^{n}}
. Note that
a^{(0)}=(1_{\mathbb{A}_{X}})
. It follows from the defining relations of
\mathbb{A}_{X}
that each
a^{(n)}\in\operatorname{Rep}(\mathbb{A}_{X})
with
(a^{(n)})^{*}=(a_{v,u})_{u,v\in X^{n}}
.

3.
The category of tubular partitions

In this section we define a category
\mathcal{T}
, the morphisms of which we will call
tubular partitions.
This category will be an increasing union of small categories
\mathcal{C}_{k}
for
k\geq 1
, identified via embedding functors. Defining the categories
\mathcal{C}_{k}
is done through induction.

3.1.
The base category

3.1.1.
Category structure

The base category
\mathcal{C}_{1}
is the category of
non-crossing partitions
, typically denoted as
\operatorname{NC}
. We will now recap the key definitions we need about this category, but further details can be found in
[
1
]
.

The objects in
\operatorname{NC}
are positive integers
n\geq 0
and a morphism
p\in\operatorname{NC}(m,n)
is a partition of
m+n
points
\{1,\dots,m+n\}
into blocks, such that there are no points
x_{1}<x_{2}<x_{3}<x_{4}
with
\{x_{1},x_{3}\}
and
\{x_{2},x_{4}\}
contained in distinct blocks. We write
p=\{B_{1},\dots,B_{l}\}
where each
B_{i}\subseteq\{1,\dots,m+n\}
. The upper and lower points in a block
B_{i}
are denoted by
U_{B_{i}}=B_{i}\cap\{1,\dots,m\}
and
L_{B_{i}}=B_{i}\cap\{m+1,\dots,m+n\}
, respectively.

For example, consider
p=\{\{1,5\},\{2\},\{3,4\}\}\in\operatorname{NC}(2,3)
. We can depict
p
graphically by drawing
2
upper points and
3
lower points, then joining points in the same block with a line:
\bullet
1
\bullet
2
\bullet
5
\bullet
4
\bullet
3

Note that the upper points are labelled left-to-right while the lower points are labelled right-to-left. This ensures that the lines joining points in a non-crossing partition are visually non-crossing. With this convention set, we will not continue to label points in partitions.

Composition in
\operatorname{NC}
is best described graphically: given
p\in\operatorname{NC}(m,n)
and
q\in\operatorname{NC}(n,r)
the composition
q\circ p\in\operatorname{NC}(m,r)
is the partition obtained by first stacking the diagram representing
p
on top of the diagram representing
q
, and then identifying the middle
n
points and deleting any block which has no connection to the
m
upper or
r
lower points. For example, given
p
above and
q=\{\{1,4,5,7\},\{2,3\},\{6\}\}\in\operatorname{NC}(3,4)
we can depict the composition as follows:
p=\
\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
q=\
\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
\mapsto
\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
=q\circ p

We see that
q\circ p=\{\{1,3,4,6\},\{2\},\{5\}\}\in\operatorname{NC}(2,4)
.

\operatorname{NC}

by

. For example, in the composition illustrated in the example above, there are four removed blocks—three from the inner partition and one from the outer partition—and so we have

.

Finally, as in
\operatorname{NC}
, any morphism
\rho\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathcal{C}_{k})
has an adjoint
\rho^{*}
obtained by vertical reflection. The visual representation of
\rho^{*}
is of course then just the visual representation of
\rho
upside down.

3.2.2.
Monoidal structure

Given objects
\gamma=(m;\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
and
\delta=(n;\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})
in
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, the tensor product is given by

\gamma\otimes\delta=(m+n;\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}).

If
\rho=(p;\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{l})
and
\pi=(q;\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{l^{\prime}})
are morphisms in
\mathcal{C}_{k}
the tensor product is given by

\rho\otimes\pi=(p\otimes q;\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{l},\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{l^{\prime}}).

Graphically, the tensor product on both objects and morphisms is just horizontal juxtaposition, analogous to the case for
\operatorname{NC}
.

3.3.
The category of tubular partitions

Definition 3.3
.

For each
k
we inductively define functors
I_{k}:\mathcal{C}_{k}\to\mathcal{C}_{k+1}
. Given
m\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
we define
I_{1}(m):=(m;0,\dots,0)\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
, and given a morphism
p\in\mathcal{C}_{1}(m,n)
we define
I_{1}(p):=(p;\operatorname{id}_{0},\dots,\operatorname{id}_{0})\in\mathcal{C}_{2}(I_{1}(m),I_{1}(n))
. For
k\geq 2
and
\gamma=(m;\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})\in\mathcal{C}_{k}
we define

I_{k}(\gamma):=(m;I_{k-1}(\alpha_{1}),\dots,I_{k-1}(\alpha_{m}))\in\mathcal{C}_{k+1},

and given a morphism
\rho=(p;\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{l})\in\mathcal{C}_{k}(\gamma,\delta)
we define

I_{k}(\rho):=(p;I_{k-1}(\phi_{1}),\dots,I_{k-1}(\phi_{l}))\in\mathcal{C}_{k+1}(I_{k}(\gamma),I_{k}(\delta)).

Definition 3.4
.

The category of tubular partitions
\mathcal{T}
is the union

\mathcal{T}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{C}_{k},

where we identify each
\mathcal{C}_{k}
with its image
I_{k}(\mathcal{C}_{k})\subset\mathcal{C}_{k+1}
. The monoidal structure is inherited from each subcategory
\mathcal{C}_{k}
and the unit object is given by the image of
0\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
.

A key property of
\mathcal{T}
is the existence of an endofunctor
\Psi
, which can be interpreted visually as taking a morphism and putting it inside a cylindrical tube. This visual interpretation is the inspiration for the name
tubular partitions
given to morphisms in
\mathcal{T}
. Mathematically, the existence of this endofunctor is the manifestation of the self-similarity of the compact quantum group
\mathbb{A}_{X}
obtained after applying Tannaka–Krein duality (see also the discussion before Proposition
4.2
).

3.7.
C^{*}
-tensor category

To apply Tannaka-Krein duality we must first build a concrete rigid
C^{*}
-tensor category by associating Hilbert spaces and linear maps to the objects and morphisms of
\mathcal{T}
. This construction is analogous to the definition of
easy quantum groups
from partition categories, see
[
1
]
.

We begin by associating a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
to each object
\alpha\in\mathcal{T}
. We do this by defining an index set consisting of generalised words for an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
.

3.7.1.
Generalised words and Hilbert spaces

Fix a finite set
X
. Given an object
\alpha\in\mathcal{T}
we want to define a set
X^{\alpha}
, the elements of which we will refer to as
generalised words of shape
\alpha
. We do this inductively. If
m=0\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
, then
X^{m}:=\{\varnothing\}
. If
m\geq 1\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
then

X^{m}:=\{\mathbf{x}=x_{1}\cdots x_{m}:x_{i}\in X,\,1\leq i\leq m\}

is the usual set of words in
X
of length
m
. For
u\in X^{m}
we write
|u|:=m
. Then given an object
\gamma=(m;\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})\in\mathcal{C}_{k+1}\subset\mathcal{T}
, inductively define

X^{\gamma}:=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(X\times X^{\alpha_{i}})=\{v=(x_{1},u_{1})\cdots(x_{m},u_{m}):x_{i}\in X,\,u_{i}\in X^{\alpha_{i}},\,1\leq i\leq m\}.

We refer to an element of
X^{\gamma}
as a
generalised word of shape
\gamma
, and for
v\in X^{\gamma}
we write
|v|:=\gamma
. Notice that there is a canonical bijection between
X^{\gamma\otimes\delta}
and
X^{\gamma}\times X^{\delta}
. We now use these generalised words to associate Hilbert spaces to each object in
\mathcal{T}
.

Definition 3.12
.

If
\alpha\in\mathcal{C}_{k}
define the finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}:=\mathbb{C}^{X^{\alpha}}
with canonical orthonormal spanning set

. Notice that
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\mathbb{C}
, and this assignment of Hilbert space respects the monoidal structure of the categories
\mathcal{C}_{k}
in the sense that
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha\otimes\beta}
is canonically isomorphic to
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\beta}
.

We now want to associate a linear map

Fix a morphism

4.
Tannaka-Krein duality

We can now state our main result, which says that the compact quantum group naturally associated to the category of tubular partitions
\mathcal{T}
via Tannaka–Krein duality is the quantum automorphism group
(\mathbb{A}_{X},\Delta)
. Recall from Definitions
3.12
and
3.14
the Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
and linear maps
T_{\rho}
we associate to the elements of
\mathcal{T}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X
be a finite set, and
\mathcal{T}
the category of tubular partitions. Let
\mathcal{R}_{X}=(R,\{\mathcal{H}_{r}\}_{r\in R},\operatorname{Hom}(r,s)_{r,s\in R})
be the smallest complete concrete rigid
C^{*}
-tensor category containing the Banach spaces

\mathcal{R}_{X}(\alpha,\beta):=\operatorname{span}\{T_{\rho}:\rho\in\mathcal{T}(\alpha,\beta)\}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\alpha},\mathcal{H}_{\beta}),

where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{T}
. Then there is a family
\{u^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{T}}\subseteq\operatorname{Rep}(\mathbb{A}_{X})
such that
(\mathbb{A}_{X},\{u^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{T}})
is the unique universal
\mathcal{R}_{X}
-admissible pair associated to
(\mathcal{R}_{X},\mathcal{T})
.

It is proved in
[
4
, Theorem 4.5]
that the map
\operatorname{Aut}(X^{*})\times X\to X\times\operatorname{Aut}(X^{*})
given by
(g,x)\mapsto(g(x),g|_{x})
has a quantum analogue in the form of a homomorphism
\psi:\mathbb{C}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{A}_{X}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{X}
satisfying
(\Delta\otimes\operatorname{id})\psi=(\operatorname{id}\otimes\psi)(\psi\otimes\operatorname{id})(\operatorname{id}\otimes\Delta)
. We begin the proof of Theorem
4.1
by showing that
\psi
induces a map on
\operatorname{Rep}(\mathbb{A}_{X})
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
u=(u_{i,j})
be a finite-dimensional unitary representation of
\mathbb{A}_{X}
on
\mathcal{H}
. Then there is a unitary representation
\psi(u)=(\psi(u)_{(x,i),(y,j)})\in\operatorname{Rep}(\mathbb{A}_{X})
on the Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{X}\otimes\mathcal{H}

such that the matrix elements satisfy the relation

\psi(e_{x}\otimes u_{i,j})=\sum_{y\in X}\psi(u)_{(x,i),(y,j)}\otimes e_{y}.

Moreover, if
v\in\operatorname{Rep}(\mathbb{A}_{X})
is a representation on
\mathcal{K}
, and
T\in\operatorname{Rep}(\mathbb{A}_{X})(u,v)
is an intertwiner between

\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{C}^{X}}\otimes T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{X}\otimes\mathcal{H},\mathbb{C}^{X}\otimes\mathcal{K})
is an intertwiner between
\psi(u)
and
\psi(v)
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in X}\Delta(\psi(u)_{(x,i),(y,j)})\otimes e_{y}

\displaystyle=(\Delta\otimes\operatorname{id})\psi(e_{x}\otimes u_{i,j})

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{id}\otimes\psi)(\psi\otimes\operatorname{id})(\operatorname{id}\otimes\Delta)(e_{x}\otimes u_{i,j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq k\leq d_{u}}(\operatorname{id}\otimes\psi)(\psi\otimes\operatorname{id})(e_{x}\otimes u_{i,k}\otimes u_{k,j}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in X\\
1\leq k\leq d_{u}\end{subarray}}(\operatorname{id}\otimes\psi)(\psi(u)_{(x,i),(z,k)}\otimes e_{z}\otimes u_{k,j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y,z\in X\\
1\leq k\leq d_{u}\end{subarray}}\psi(u)_{(x,i),(z,k)}\otimes\psi(u)_{(z,k),(y,j)}\otimes e_{y},

and comparing tensor factors gives

\Delta(\psi(u)_{(x,i),(y,j)})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in X\\
1\leq k\leq d_{u}\end{subarray}}\psi(u)_{(x,i),(z,k)}\otimes\psi(u)_{(z,k),(y,j)}.

To see that
\psi(u)
is a representation, it remains to show that
\psi(u)
is unitary. First note that

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\psi(u^{*})_{(y,i),(x,j)}\otimes e_{x}

\displaystyle=\psi(e_{y}\otimes(u^{*})_{i,j})

\displaystyle=\psi(e_{y}\otimes u_{j,i})^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in X}\psi(u)_{(y,j),(x,i)}^{*}\otimes e_{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in X}(\psi(u)^{*})_{(x,i),(y,j)}\otimes e_{x},

and hence we have

(\psi(u)^{*})_{(x,i),(y,j)}=\psi(u^{*})_{(y,i),(x,j)}.

Now it follows that for each
x,y\in X
and
1\leq i,j\leq d_{u}
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in X\\
1\leq k\leq d_{u}\end{subarray}}\psi(u)_{(x,i),(z,k)}\psi(u)_{(z,k),(y,j)}^{*}\otimes e_{z}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in X\\
1\leq k\leq d_{u}\end{subarray}}\psi(u)_{(x,i),(z,k)}\psi(u^{*})_{(y,k),(z,j)}\otimes e_{z}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt=\sum_{1\leq k\leq d_{u}}\Big(\sum_{z\in X}\psi(u)_{(x,i),(z,k)}\otimes e_{z}\Big)\Big(\sum_{z^{\prime}\in X}\psi(u^{*})_{(y,k),(z^{\prime},j)}\otimes e_{z^{\prime}}\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt=\sum_{1\leq k\leq d_{u}}\psi(e_{x}\otimes u_{i,k})\psi(e_{y}\otimes u_{k,j}^{*})

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt=\psi\Big(e_{x}e_{y}\otimes\sum_{1\leq k\leq d_{u}}u_{i,k}u_{k,j}^{*}\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt=\delta_{x,y}\delta_{i,j}\psi(e_{x}\otimes 1_{\mathbb{A}_{X}})

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt=\delta_{x,y}\delta_{i,j}\sum_{z\in X}a_{x,z}\otimes e_{z},

and hence for each
z\in X
we have

\sum_{1\leq k\leq d_{u}}\psi(u)_{(x,i),(z,k)}\psi(u)_{(z,k),(y,j)}^{*}=\delta_{x,y}\delta_{i,j}a_{x,z}.

Hence

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in X\\
1\leq k\leq d_{u}\end{subarray}}\psi(u)_{(x,i),(z,k)}\psi(u)_{(z,k),(y,j)}^{*}=\delta_{x,y}\delta_{i,j}\sum_{z\in X}a_{x,z}=\delta_{x,y}\delta_{i,j}1_{\mathbb{A}_{X}}

showing that
\psi(u)
is a unitary representation.

For the second claim, we view
T=(T_{i,j})
and
(\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{C}^{X}}\otimes T)_{(x,i),(y,j)}
as matrices; we need to show that for any
x,y\in X
and
1\leq i\leq d_{v}
,
1\leq j\leq d_{u}

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in X\\
1\leq k\leq d_{u}\end{subarray}}(\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{C}^{X}}\otimes T)_{(x,i),(z,k)}\psi(u)_{(z,k),(y,j)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in X\\
1\leq k\leq d_{u}\end{subarray}}\psi(v)_{(x,i),(z,k)}(\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{C}^{X}}\otimes T)_{(z,k),(y,j)},

which is equivalent to

(4.1)

\sum_{1\leq k\leq d_{u}}T_{i,k}\psi(u)_{(x,k),(y,j)}=\sum_{1\leq k\leq d_{u}}\psi(v)_{(x,i),(y,k)}T_{k,j}.

Equation
4.1
follows from the calculation:

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in X}\Big(\sum_{1\leq k\leq d_{u}}T_{i,k}\psi(u)_{(x,k),(y,j)}\Big)\otimes e_{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq k\leq d_{u}}T_{i,k}\Big(\sum_{y\in X}\psi(u)_{(x,k),(y,j)}\otimes e_{y}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq k\leq d_{u}}T_{i,k}\psi(e_{x}\otimes u_{k,j})

\displaystyle=\psi\Big(e_{x}\otimes\Big(\sum_{1\leq k\leq d_{u}}T_{i,k}u_{k,j}\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\psi\Big(e_{x}\otimes\Big(\sum_{1\leq k\leq d_{u}}v_{i,k}T_{k,j}\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in X}\Big(\sum_{1\leq k\leq d_{u}}\psi(v)_{(x,i),(y,k)}T_{k,j}\Big)\otimes e_{y}.

∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Fix finite-dimensional unitary representations
u,v
of
\mathbb{A}_{X}
on
\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}
respectively. The linear mapping

T:\mathbb{C}^{X}\otimes\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{K}\to\mathbb{C}^{X}\otimes\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{X}\otimes\mathcal{K}

satisfying

T(e_{x}\otimes h\otimes k)=e_{x}\otimes h\otimes e_{x}\otimes k

for all
x\in X,h\in\mathcal{H},k\in\mathcal{K}
, is an intertwiner between
\psi(u\otimes v)
and
\psi(u)\otimes\psi(v)
.

Before proving Proposition
4.3
we need a lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Fix
u,v\in\operatorname{Rep}(\mathbb{A}_{X})
and
x_{1}\neq x_{2}\in X
. Then

\psi(u)_{(x_{1},i),(y,j)}\psi(v)_{(x_{2},i^{\prime}),(y,j^{\prime})}=0

for any
1\leq i,j\leq d_{u},1\leq i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\leq d_{v}
and
y\in X
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in X}\psi(u)_{(x,i),(y,j)}\otimes e_{y}

\displaystyle=\psi(e_{x}\otimes u_{i,j})

\displaystyle=\psi(e_{x}\otimes u_{i,j})\psi(e_{x}\otimes 1_{\mathbb{A}_{X}})

\displaystyle=\psi(e_{x}\otimes u_{i,j})\sum_{y^{\prime}\in X}a_{x,y^{\prime}}\otimes e_{y^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in X}\psi(u)_{(x,i),(y,j)}a_{x,y}\otimes e_{y},

and it follows that
\psi(u)_{(x,i),(y,j)}=\psi(u)_{(x,i),(y,j)}a_{x,y}
. Similarly, we have
a_{x,y}\psi(u)_{(x,i),(y,j)}=\psi(u)_{(x,i),(y,j)}
. Hence we have

\psi(u)_{(x_{1},i),(y,j)}\psi(v)_{(x_{2},i^{\prime}),(y,j^{\prime})}=\psi(u)_{(x_{1},i),(y,j)}a_{x_{1},y}a_{x_{2},y}\psi(v)_{(x_{2},i^{\prime}),(y,j^{\prime})}=0.

∎

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

If we write
T
as a matrix
T=(T_{(x,i,y,j),(z,k,l)})
, where
x,y,z\in X
,
1\leq i,k\leq d_{u}
,
1\leq j,l\leq d_{v}
, we have

(T\psi(u\otimes v))_{(x,i,y,j),(z,k,l)}=((\psi(u)\otimes\psi(v))T)_{(x,i,y,j),(z,k,l)},

(4.2)

\delta_{x,y}\psi(u\otimes v)_{(x,i,j),(z,k,l)}=\psi(u)_{(x,i),(z,k)}\psi(v)_{(y,j),(z,l)}.

By Lemma

we know the right-hand side of (
4.2
) is

\delta_{x,y}\psi(u)_{(x,i),(z,k)}\psi(v)_{(x,j),(z,l)}.

We also have

\displaystyle\psi(e_{x}\otimes(u\otimes v)_{(i,j),(k,l)})

\displaystyle=\psi(e_{x}\otimes u_{i,k})\psi(e_{x}\otimes v_{j,l})

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in X}\psi(u)_{(x,i),(z,k)}\psi(v)_{(x,j),(z,l)}\otimes e_{z},

and it follows that

\psi(u\otimes v)_{(x,i,j),(z,k,l)}=\psi(u)_{(x,i),(z,k)}\psi(v)_{(x,j),(z,l)}.

Hence (
4.2
) holds.
∎
