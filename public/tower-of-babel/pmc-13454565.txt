CD40.HIVEnv, an antibody mediated vaccine, induces long-term and recall immunogenicity in non-HIV-1 infected volunteers

ABSTRACT
The ANRS VRI06 trial investigated CD40.HIVRI.Env, an Antibody Mediated Vaccine composed by a CD40 monoclonal antibody fused with gp140 Env, designed to target antigen to dendritic cells. Initial results in 72 HIV-uninfected volunteers showed that administration of 0.3-3.0 mg of CD40.HIVRI.Env, given alone or with DNA-HIV-PT123, was safe and immunogenic up to 48 weeks. After week 48 in the trial, 45 volunteers were randomized to receive a late boost (LB) of 0.3 mg CD40.HIVRI.Env, either with or without adjuvant, to assess continued safety and immunogenicity at 2 and 24 weeks post-LB. The median LB interval from VRI06 baseline was 80 and 79 weeks in unadjuvanted (n=23) and adjuvanted (n=22) groups, respectively. Env-specific IgG and functional CD4+ T-cells persisted at the time of the LB, a median of 55 weeks after the last injection at W24. The LB was well tolerated and markedly boosted immune responses. In particular: i) IgG response rates against gp140 and 9 gp120 antigens reached 100% (vaccine-matched) and 82-100% (heterologous) by 24 weeks post-LB; ii) response rates to gp70 V1V2 antigens increased substantially (e.g., from 0-5% to 5-35%) against autologous 96ZM651 and heterologous V1V2 antigens; iii) neutralizing titers against Tier 1 MW965.26 rose from 14-22% to 35-59%; iv) the frequency of polyfunctional Env-specific CD4+ T-cells increased and persisted 24 weeks post-LB. No marked differences were observed between adjuvanted and unadjuvanted LB groups. CD40.HIVRI.Env vaccination is safe and induced broad, potent and long-lasting anti-HIV immune responses recalled by a single late boost, even without adjuvant. Introduction

The durability of vaccine-elicited immune responses is critical for sustained protection against pathogens. Among the challenges in developing a preventive HIV vaccine, the induction of broad and durable immune responses would be critical for an efficient control of the epidemic at the general population level. Although a preventive vaccine against HIV is still needed, the phase III RV144 trial testing a 4-dose pox (ALVAC-HIV)-gp120 protein heterologous prime–boost regimen administered over 6 months, showed vaccine efficacy up to 60.5% at 1 year but waned to 31.2% at 3.5 years1. This waning protection was associated with a decay in the magnitude and quality of vaccine-elicited immune responses, including binding antibody responses (bAbs) to HIV envelope, IgG3 bAbs to the V1V2 region, higher levels of which were correlated with a decreased risk of HIV-acquisition2–5.

Late boost strategies aimed to improve the durability and magnitude of responses have been tested in RV144 participants and other smaller trials in HIV-uninfected volunteers. Several boosters (i.e. ALVAC-HIV and/or gp120 protein combined or not with different adjuvants, at different doses) were administered at different time points from 12, 18, 30 months to 7-8 years after the last boost in the RV144 and other trials in HIV-uninfected volunteers. Moreover additional boosting were included in the HVTN 100 and 702 clinical trials6,8. Overall these studies showed a booster effect on humoral and cellular responses, regardless of the regimen tested6–11, but with a limited durability. These results indicate that, despite these significant efforts, induction of sustainable immune responses against HIV remains a challenge.

Exploration of new strategies to extend the longevity of the magnitude and quality of immune response are warranted. Several parameters may influence the boosting strategies including the administration of a heterologous or homologous boost, the optimal dosing and timing, and the possible need of an adjuvant. The ANRS VRI06 phase I trial tested an innovative mode of delivery of the HIV antigenic determinant. The CD40.HIVRI.Env vaccine composed of a gp140 Env designed against the Clade C (96ZM651) fused to a humanized IgG anti-CD40 mAb and designed to target and deliver the antigen to CD40-expressing dendritic cells was administered to HIV-uninfected volunteers. In this dose-escalation study, CD40.HIVRI.Env was adjuvanted with the TLR3 agonist Poly-ICLC (Hiltonol®) and combined or not with the DNA-PT123 vaccine that encodes 96ZM651 gp140/Gag and 97CN54 Pol/Nef. Results showed that, regardless of the dose and the combination with the DNA-PT123, the CD40.HIVRI.Env was safe and elicited robust humoral and cellular immune responses against HIV that persisted until W48, i.e., 6 months after the last injection12. The long-term safety and durability of the responses induced by a DC-targeting vaccine remained to be evaluated. Similarly, the potential effects of a late booster of this vaccine with or without adjuvant were unknown. Therefore, based on the W48 results, the ANRS VRI06 was amended to extend the follow-up and to propose volunteers from the initial active vaccine arms to receive an additional late boost of the CD40.HIVRI.Env combined or not with the TLR3-Hiltonol® adjuvant. All objectives of the extended follow-up were secondary objectives relative to the initial trial protocol and aimed to explore the safety, tolerability and immunogenicity of CD40.HIVRI.Env when given as a late boost in participants previously vaccinated with Poly-ICLC (Hiltonol®) adjuvanted CD40.HIVRI.Env alone or combined with DNA.PT123.

Results

The initial trial (ANRS VRI06 study) included 72 healthy adult participants who received vaccine injection at week 0, 4 and 24 and were monitored from week 0 to week 48 (part I of the study). Among them, 60 were enrolled in active vaccine arms and 45 agreed to participate to the extended follow-up after 48 weeks and late boost (LB) administration: 23 volunteers received 0.3 mg CD40.HIVRI.Env s.c. alone (V-Adj), and 22 volunteers received 0.3 mg CD40.HIVRI.Env s.c. adjuvanted with 1 mg Poly-ICLC (Hiltonol®) (V+Adj) in a single blind parallel group design (Suppl Figure 1). The characteristics of the 45 active vaccine recipients enrolled in Part II and the 15 not enrolled in this part did not suggest any major differences in their characteristics (Suppl Table 1).

For these 45 participants, median (IQR) age was 31 years (26.0; 45.0), and 25 (55.6%) were assigned the male sex at birth (Table 1). Twenty-one had received Hiltonol® adjuvanted CD40.HIVRI.Env alone in the Part 1 of the study, and 24 in combination with DNA-HIV-PT123. Safety

At the time of administration of the LB, no volunteers reported any serious adverse events since the last previous injection. Overall, the LB was well tolerated (Suppl Table 2). Most of the vaccine related adverse events (AE) were associated with systemic and local reactogenicity (Fig. 1). They were mainly mild to moderate in severity and resolved within around three days.Fig. 1Adverse events related to vaccination by system organ class, group, and grade.

Adverse events related to vaccination by system organ class, group, and grade.

Local reactogenicity consisted of injection site reactions. The most frequently reported systemic reactogenicity symptoms were fatigue, headache, and myalgia. Only two grade 3 adverse events considered possibly related to vaccination (one fever occurring on the day of LB vaccination; and an increase of lipase, without any clinical symptoms, occurring nearly 6 months after the LB vaccination) were reported in two volunteers from the V+Adj group, both of one-day duration. No grade 4 events were observed and no serious adverse events (SAE) related to the vaccines were reported (Suppl Table 3).

Cellular immunogenicity

We have previously reported that adjuvanted CD40.HIVRI.Env elicited functional HIV-Env specific CD4+ T-cell responses and high response rates and magnitudes of IgG responses against autologous and heterologous Env antigens regardless of the dose or the combination with DNA-HIV-PT123 at W26 (i.e., two weeks after the third immunization of the primary series), which persisted 6 months later (i.e., W48). At the time of LB (i.e. a median of 55 weeks post-W24 injection), the median (IQR) frequency of Env-specific CD4+ T-cell responses (cells producing up to three cytokines (IFN-γ ± IL-2 ± TNF)) measured by ex-vivo intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) was 0.09 (0.05; 0.18) % compared to 0.15 (0.10; 0.20) % at W48.

The observed frequency increased to 0.26 (0.15; 0.36) % (n=45) two weeks after the LB. When analyzed separately in the V-Adj and V+Adj groups, the frequency of Env-specific CD4+ T-cell responses was 0.17 (0.12; 0.29) % and 0.31 (0.21; 0.37) %, respectively (Fig. 2; Suppl Table 4). At W24 post LB, responses remained numerically higher than at the time of the LB and were similar to values observed at W48 (Fig. 2), without substantial differences between groups: 0.13 (0.07; 0.17) % and 0.17 (0.14; 0.22) % in the V-Adj and V+Adj groups, respectively. Discussion

Induction of durable immune responses is a challenge in HIV vaccine development. We report here the long term follow-up of HIV-uninfected volunteers recipients of CD40.HIVRI.Env vaccine, adjuvanted with TLR3 agonist (Hiltonol®), combined or not with a DNA vaccine12, in an early phase exploratory clinical trial. We show the good safety profile and persistent detection of humoral and cellular immunity of the experimental vaccines more than one year in median after three injections of the initial immunization series. At that time point, we proposed to volunteers a single later low-dose boost of the CD40.HIVRI.Env vaccine, adjuvanted or not. Clinically, one interesting lesson from the current study is the booster effect of CD40.HIVRI.Env, even unadjuvanted, raising both anti-Env IgG binding and neutralizing antibodies and specific functional CD4+ T-cells. These responses were detectable for six additional months following this late boost.

These results may have several implications for future vaccine development. First, this long-term evaluation of a first-in-human administration of a new route of delivery of HIV Env antigens targeting dendritic cells was safe and did not reveal long-term local or systemic adverse events. An additional late boost of CD40.HIVRI.Env showed an acceptable reactogenicity as observed in the first part of the ANRS VRI06 trial12. Second, we show that, despite a decrease from the peak response after the initial immunization series, specific IgG antibody responses against autologous HIV Env gp140 persisted prior to the late boost, a median of 55 weeks after the last injection of CD40.HIVRI.Env alone or combined with DNA at W24. The additional boost of CD40.HIVRI.Env increased these responses, which then again decreased by six months thereafter. Evaluation of the correlates of protection in the modestly protective RV144 study indicates a potential role for non-neutralizing antibodies toward the variable loops 1–2 (V1/V2), likely via antibody-dependent effector functions, and Fc-mediated recruitment of effector cells2,3,13,14. We show that the additional late boost of CD40.HIVRI.Env restored IgG specific level responses against homologous V1V2 antigens over six months to levels similar to those observed at the latest time point of the first part of the ANRS VRI06 trial12. We observed a decline in IgG3 responses at 24 weeks after the LB that is consistent with the known biology of this subclass, which is typically induced early following antigen exposure and characterized by a relatively short half-life. This reduction may reflect the natural contraction of the inflammatory phase of the immune response and could also indicate a shift in the cytokine environment toward a less inflammatory state during the establishment of immune memory.

Although vaccine regimens tested in the ANRS VRI06 trial induced Tier 1A nAbs12, these responses waned in the long-term follow-up. However, these responses were recalled two weeks, and up to 6 months, following the additional late boost. Results from the RV144 study showed that the vaccine regimen induced only antibodies able to weakly neutralize Tier 1 viruses. Globally, these results confirm that these antibodies are very difficult to elicit by vaccination15. Therefore, a main challenge in HIV vaccinology is to design adapted immunogens and vaccine regimens able to induce broadly neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs)16,17. One approach is to use information about Env epitopes and to combine relevant conformations in new immunogens, with the aim of selectively inducing a desired bnAb response. However, despite a long-term effort to design optimized Env antigens mimicking the native prefusion Env trimer or targeting germline IgG repertoire of nAbs, only limited Tier 2 neutralization breadth has been accomplished to date18–22. Globally, lessons learned from the RV144 trial suggest that vaccine protection could include both cross reactive and functional non-neutralizing Abs and nAbs responses. The Clade C 96ZM651 Env protein fused to the CD40 monoclonal antibody used in the CD40.HIVRI.Env is not suitable for the induction of nAbs. However, the durability of the responses and the good safety profile of the CD40 targeting platform represent an innovative way to deliver antigens for inducing immune responses. Moreover, this technology already allowed the plug-in of various virus or tumor antigens as demonstrated by the current candidates in clinical development (EuCT number: 2023-504594-20-00/NCT06255626, EuCT number: 2024-516767-92-01/NCT07113106, EuCT number: 2022-502930-25-00/NCT06007092). Leveraging on the versatility of this technology, various Env conformational immunogens fused to the anti-CD40 mAb are under testing in preclinical models.

While CD40-targeting strategies may enhance recall of pre-existing Env-specific responses, this raises considerations regarding immune imprinting and potential competition with germline-targeting approaches. However, such imprinting may also contribute to ongoing affinity maturation in vivo, promoting the accumulation of somatic hypermutations and potentially enhancing antibody affinity. In addition, although anti-drug or anti-idiotype responses against the administered CD40 monoclonal antibody cannot be excluded, the absence of reduced antibody responses upon repeated administrations in our study suggests a limited functional impact within the timeframe analyzed.

In the Part 1 of the ANRS VRI06 trial we showed the strong and persistent induction of polyfunctional specific CD4+ T-cells, regardless of the dose or the combination with DNA vaccine12. Our current follow-up study extends these findings, indicating that these responses are sustained and required only a single, low, unadjuvanted dose of CD40.HIVRI.Env to be boosted. Globally, these cellular responses were observed over a period of more than 2-3 years, considering the follow-up length from study entry to the final endpoint (i.e. 6 months post-late boost). However, due to limitations in blood sampling, we did not evaluate in the LB part of the study the presence of vaccine elicited Env specific CD8+ T cell responses previously shown after in vitro restimulation.

We explore in the current study the potency of CD40 targeting to boost memory T and B cell responses. CD40.HIVRI.Env induces an Env-specific humoral and CD4⁺ T response with prolonged durability before recall, suggesting a properly established memory compartment, and a marked recall effect after a single late boost, even in the absence of adjuvant. These results are supported by several preclinical studies and specially the capacity of CD40-targeting constructs to elicit stem cell memory T-cells23 and durable memory humoral responses24. Collectively these observations may provide insights about the advantage of the anti-CD40 platform to recall and to improve in vivo pre-existing responses. Another potential advantage is that CD40-DC targeting might be suitable in the case of sequential injections to induce B cell maturation with different Env proteins. This hypothesis will be tested in the ongoing HVTN318 study including volunteers from the previous Mosaico trial (HPX3002/HVTN706), which did not demonstrate protection25. Volunteers will receive boosts with CD40.HIVRI.Env vaccine aiming to mature HIV-specific B cell responses. The effect of HIV Env delivery through CD40 targeting on B-cell maturation will be analyzed by sorting of Env-specific B cell in the HVTN 318 study.

This study has some limitations. We did not compare the immunogenicity of a boost versus a non-boosted control group. However, the design allows for a causal interpretation given that the trial is performed in healthy volunteers at very low risk of HIV exposure. Thus, any natural fluctuation (i.e. increase) in HIV-specific immune responses not related to vaccination is very unlikely. Regarding the evaluation of the immunogenicity, we did not perform Fc function activities and molecular analysis of Env-specific B cell to formally evaluate the maturation of B cell using the CD40 platform. These tests will be performed in an ongoing study (HVTN 318; NCT06665646), in which volunteers from the previous MOSAICO trial will received the CD40.HIVRI.Env boost strategy. Finally, elicitation of Env specific CD8+ T cell responses after in vitro restimulation of PBMC was observed in the first part of the trial. We were not able to follow these responses in the second part of the trial because of a limitation of blood sampling.

In conclusion, the current study showed that a single booster low dose of unadjuvanted CD40.HIVRI.Env, after one year of a vaccine-free-interval, is safe and can maintain and/or restore preexisting responses. Clinically, the capacity to induce immunity without requiring an adjuvant would help to accelerate the development of a vaccine with improved tolerability. These observations could be useful for preventive vaccines such as HIV, which require broad and durable immune responses. In addition, the anti-CD40 platform may therefore represents an excellent booster of pre-existing immunity, either induced by natural infection of by previous priming with vector or mRNA-based vaccines. This hypothesis is currently being tested in a phase 1/2a clinical trial testing the boost effect of a CD40.Covid vaccine, without adjuvant, in individuals already vaccinated with a primary series of mRNA vaccine (EuCT number: 2023-504594-20-00/NCT06255626).

Methods

Study design and participants

The ANRS VRI06 trial (N° EudraCT: 2020-001814-40; NCT04842682) has been previously described12. In brief, it was a phase I placebo-controlled, multicentre dose-escalation trial of the CD40.HIVRI.Env vaccine (0·3, 1·0, or 3·0 mg) adjuvanted with the TLR3 agonist Poly-ICLC (Hiltonol®, 1·0 mg) administered either alone or co-administered with the DNA-HIV-PT123 vaccine (4.0 mg) at weeks (W) 0, 4, and 24 (Fig. 6A). Cohorts of twelve volunteers, including both men and women, enrolled in France and Switzerland were randomized (5:1) to active vaccination or placebo.Fig. 6Overview of the ANRS VRI06 trial design from the first (A) and second part of the trial (B).

Overview of the ANRS VRI06 trial design from the first (A) and second part of the trial (B).

The first part of the trial enrolled 72 HIV-negative volunteers, aged 18-65 years and at low risk of infection, with follow-up for vaccine safety and immunogenicity until W48 (Part 1). Recipients of active vaccine doses in Part 1 (n=60) who had neither met vaccine discontinuation criteria nor criteria of study termination and consented to the receipt of a late boost (LB) injection (n=45) were again randomized (1:1) to receive one LB of CD40.HIVRI.Env (subcutaneously, s.c.; 0.3mg) alone (V-Adj) or with 1.0 mg of Hiltonol® (V+Adj) in a single-blind parallel-group design (n=44). The 45th participant received V-Adj in a non-randomized allocation due to shelf life expiry of the adjuvant during his screening period. Participants were followed 6 months post-LB (Fig. 6B). The rationale for selecting the lowest dose (0.3 mg) as a booster was based on converging evidence from preclinical studies and ongoing clinical evaluations of CD40-targeting vaccines, suggesting that in the context of pre-existing immunity, low antigen doses can be sufficient to effectively recall memory immune responses. This approach is consistent with a dose-sparing strategy aimed at minimizing antigen exposure while maintaining immunogenicity. For this reason, the 0.3 mg dose was also evaluated without adjuvant to assess the intrinsic immunostimulatory capacity of CD40 targeting. Full details of the protocol are provided in the Appendix 3.

Randomization and masking

Randomization sequences for the LB allocation were generated using stratification on the intervention group in Part I and alternating blocks of size two and four. Block sizes were concealed during the trial. Randomization was computer-generated using SAS software (version 9.4) and implemented in a validated online randomization tool (Ennov Clinical software). Participants were randomized at the time of the LB. The participants and the centralized immunology labs were blinded to LB allocation, while the investigators were not.

Vaccine candidates

The CD40.HIVRI.Env vaccine manufacturing and characteristics have been previously described12 as well as the adjuvant Poly-ICLC (Hiltonol®), manufactured by Dalton Pharma Services (Toronto, Canada) on behalf of and supplied by Oncovir Inc. (Washington DC, USA). For the LB injection, CD40.HIVRI.Env was administered as a single injection of 0.3 mg s.c., either alone (V-Adj) or adjuvanted with 1.0 mg Hiltonol® (V+Adj) .

Immune response assays

Assays performed during this extended follow-up and LB phase were identical to those performed during the first phase of the trial allowing the monitoring of the evolution of immunological responses. All assays were performed in the same centralized laboratories as those involved in the first part of the trial, on cryopreserved peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and serum collected at the time of the LB, and 2 and 24 weeks thereafter. Antigen-specific T-cell responses were assessed by intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) assay on cryopreserved PBMCs as described12. Peptide pools covered sequences from the Env gp140 Clade C 96ZM651 protein and were organized into three pools (Env1, Env2, Env3). The flow cytometry panel included a viability marker, staining for CD3, CD4, and CD8 for T-cell lineage determination of and antibodies against IFN-γ, IL-2, and TNF. Total Env-specific responses were determined by adding the Env1, Env2, and Env3 responses.

The frequency, magnitude and breadth of IgG antibody responses were measured by the HIV-1 binding antibody multiplex assay (BAMA) in serum specimens as previously described11. Recombinant envelopes, V1V2 peptides and the providers are described in the appendix (Suppl Table 9). Serum samples were diluted at 1/20 and 1/80 for IgG and at 1/20 for IgG3 and incubated with carboxylated fluorescent bead sets (Bio-Rad) covalently coupled to Env proteins. The BAMA assay and the definitions of responses have been previously described12. Briefly, IgG responders were defined by a post-vaccination background-subtracted mean fluorescence intensity (MFI-blank) higher or equal to the antigen/isotype specific MFI-blank threshold, which was based on the 95th percentile of the MFI-blank at W0 or MFI-blank > 3 × MFI-blank at W0.

HIV neutralizing antibodies (nAbs) were assessed using the TZM-Bl neutralization assay as previously described26. Information on the pseudoviruses and primary isolates used and the definitions of responses are available in the appendix (Suppl Table 9 and Appendix 1).

Outcomes

Safety outcomes including the frequency of severe local and systemic reactogenicity signs and symptoms were assessed in real time during the LB and 6 months period. The immune responses to previous vaccinations were assessed at the time of the LB as well as 2 and 24 weeks post-LB. Immunogenicity endpoints included cellular, binding and neutralizing antibody responses against HIV.

Statistical analysis

The extended follow-up and LB phase of trial (Part II) was exploratory and not powered for direct comparisons between groups. All endpoints in this Part are secondary of exploratory as the primary endpoint of the trial was based on the safety in Part I.

Sample size has been defined for Part I with calculations based on the precision of the primary safety endpoint. Given that Part II was an exploratory extension of Part II, added via a protocol amendment, no specific additional sample size calculation has been performed for Part II.

Proportions of participants with safety endpoints are described and detailed listings of relevant endpoints provided. The distribution of antibody titers (median with their interquartile range (IQR)), as well as the proportion of responders with their two-sided 95% confidence interval, are described. The total cytokine response among CD4+ T-cells assessed by ICS was described after background subtraction. Additional post-hoc analyses of the V+Adj versus V-Adj effects on the immune response secondary endpoints two weeks after the late boost were performed by a Wilcoxon test for two independent groups with FDR adjustment for multiple testing. These comparisons should be interpreted with caution given their post-hoc and exploratory nature, prone to type I and type II statistical error.

All analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4) and R (version 4.2.2 and higher).
