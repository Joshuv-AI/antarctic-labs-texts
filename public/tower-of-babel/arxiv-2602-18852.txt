Three-slab model for the dielectric permittivity of a lipid bilayer
M.M.B. Sheraj † , \,{}^{{\dagger},}, Amaresh Sahu ‡ , \,{}^{{\ddagger},}

Abstract
. A model for the tensorial dielectric permittivity of phospholipid membranes is presented here. The four-nanometer-thick membrane is treated as a composite made up of three dielectric slabs: one for each of the two phospholipid head-group regions, and one for the entire domain spanned by the lipid tails. Equal and opposite bound surface charge densities surround each head-group slab, and account for the membrane dipole potential. Three-slab model parameters are obtained from molecular dynamics simulations, and capture both the zero-field electric potential and the membrane response to applied electric fields. The tail region is well-approximated as having vacuum permittivity, while the head-group region is highly anisotropic due to the configurations of molecular dipoles. For the bilayers studied, the out-of-plane permittivity of the head-group region is 10–15 times that of the vacuum, while the in-plane permittivity is an order of magnitude larger. Membrane responses to applied electric fields up to 30 millivolts per nanometer are found to be in the linear regime. The model overcomes a fundamental limitation of microscopic theories—where the out-of-plane permittivity is ill-posed in the head-group region due to large gradients in the local electric field—by averaging over slab widths, thereby introducing new length scales. Our approach can be extended to characterize general interfacial systems with similarly ill-defined permittivities.

Introduction.
—Cells and their internal organelles are surrounded by biological membranes: two-dimensional structures that partition the various electrolytic intracellular and extracellular compartments.
Phospholipid molecules, which consist of a hydrophilic head group bound to two hydrophobic tails, are a major component of these membranes and lead to their bilayer structure.
Importantly, the oily interior of the bilayer prevents the passage of charged species, and so electric potential differences are readily established across the membrane.
A wide array of biological phenomena rely on such potential differences
[

Prior experimental

\epsilon_{0}

Such an idealization, however, fails to account for three features of the bilayer structure.
First, the head group is significantly more polarizable than the lipid tails
[

Second, the dipole moment of zwitterionic lipids is predominantly tangent to the membrane surface
[

Finally, even when no external potential is applied, the out-of-plane polarization density is nonzero (see Fig.

As these observations have not yet been incorporated into membrane thoeries, their effect on biological phenomena remains unexplored.

In this Letter, we develop a model for the permittivity of a zwitterionic phospholipid bilayer surrounded by water.
Microscopic features of membrane electrostatics are obtained from all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations via established statistical mechanical methods
[
19
]
.
The local, position-dependent permittivity tensor is calculated in this manner.
However, the notion of a local permittivity is ill-defined when there are gradients in the intrinsic electric field over atomic length scales, as is the case here.
This issue is resolved by coarse-graining fine-scale details and modeling the membrane as a composite material made up of three slabs: one for the entire hydrophobic interior, and one each for the head-group region in the top and bottom leaflets (see Fig.
2
(a)
).
The intrinsic polarization density from Fig.
1
(c)
is accounted for with surface bound charge densities at slab boundaries, and the head-group regions are characterized by a tensorial dielectric permittivity.
The three-slab model captures the overall membrane response to in-plane and out-of-plane electric fields, and we additionally determine the field beyond which the membrane response is nonlinear.
We validate the model against MD simulations of dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC) bilayers in the fluid phase, with similar results for dioleoylphosphatidylcholine (DOPC) membranes presented in the Supplemental Material (SM)
[
21
]
.

Statistical mechanics and electrostatics.
—Following the seminal work of
H.A. Stern
and
S.E. Feller

[
19
]
, let
\bm{\Gamma}
denote the microstate of the membrane and water system—consisting of all atomic positions and momenta.
With the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{0}(\bm{\Gamma})
and polarization density
\bm{P}(\bm{r},\bm{\Gamma})
at the position

\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
the canonical-ensemble–averaged polarization density
\langle\bm{P}(\bm{r})\rangle_{0}
is given by

\big\langle\bm{P}(\bm{r})\big\rangle_{0}\,=\,\int\!{\text{d}}\bm{\Gamma}\Big(e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}_{0}}\,\bm{P}(\bm{r},\bm{\Gamma})\Big)\bigg/\int\!{\text{d}}\bm{\Gamma}\,e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}_{0}}~.

(1)

Here and from now on, a subscript ‘0’ indicates no external electric field is applied, and the inverse temperature

\beta:=(k_{\text{B}}\vartheta)^{-1}

for Boltzmann constant
k_{\text{B}}
and absolute temperature
\vartheta
.
We take the membrane to be fluctuating about the
x
–
y
plane, along which the system is translationally invariant; ensemble-averaged quantities then depend only on the distance
z
from the bilayer midplane.
When a constant electric field
\bm{\hat{E}}
is applied, the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{\hat{E}}):=\mathcal{H}_{0}(\bm{\Gamma})-\bm{M}(\bm{\Gamma})\bm{\cdot}\bm{\hat{E}}
,
where

\bm{M}(\bm{\Gamma}):=\int\!{\text{d}}\bm{r}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{P}(\bm{r},\bm{\Gamma})

is the total dipole moment of the system.
If the field
\bm{\hat{E}}
is small, the change in average polarization density

\langle\Delta\bm{P}(z)\rangle_{\mkern-1.0mu\bm{\hat{E}}}:=\langle\bm{P}(z)\rangle_{\mkern-1.0mu\bm{\hat{E}}}-\langle\bm{P}(z)\rangle_{0}

can be expressed as

\big\langle\Delta\bm{P}(z)\big\rangle_{\mkern-1.0mu\bm{\hat{E}}}\,=\ \bm{\psi}(z)\,\bm{\hat{E}}~,

(2)

with

\bm{\psi}(z):=\beta\mkern 1.0mu(\langle\bm{P}(z)\otimes\bm{M}\rangle_{0}-\langle\bm{P}(z)\rangle_{0}\otimes\langle\bm{M}\rangle_{0})

[
19
,
22
]
.
Since lipid membranes in the fluid phase are additionally isotropic in-plane,

\langle P_{\mkern-1.0mu\mkern-1.0mux}\rangle_{0}=\langle P_{\mkern-1.0mu\mkern-1.0muy}\rangle_{0}=0

and

\bm{\psi}
is diagonal with two independent components—which we denote

\psi^{\mkern 3.0mu\vphantom{\perp}\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 2.0mu\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 3.0mu}(z):=\psi_{xx}(z)=\psi_{yy}(z)

and

\psi^{\perp}(z):=\psi_{zz}(z)
.
From the definition of
\bm{\psi}
below Eq. (
2
),
\psi^{\mkern 3.0mu\vphantom{\perp}\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 2.0mu\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 3.0mu}
and
\psi^{\perp}
can be determined from MD simulations.
Our task now is to connect
\psi^{\mkern 3.0mu\vphantom{\perp}\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 2.0mu\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 3.0mu}
and
\psi^{\perp}
to the membrane dielectric permittivity tensor
\bm{\epsilon}
—which is also diagonal, with the two independent components
\epsilon^{\mkern 3.0mu\vphantom{\perp}\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 2.0mu\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 3.0mu}(z)
and
\epsilon^{\perp}(z)
.

By definition, for systems with an intrinsic polarization density, the permittivity relates the changes in average polarization density and average electric field as

\big\langle\Delta\bm{P}(z)\big\rangle_{\mkern-1.0mu\bm{\hat{E}}}\,=\,\big(\bm{\epsilon}(z)\mkern 1.0mu-\mkern 1.0mu\epsilon_{0}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{I}\big)\mkern 1.0mu\big\langle\Delta\bm{E}(z)\big\rangle_{\mkern-1.0mu\bm{\hat{E}}}~,

(3)

where
\bm{I}
is the identity tensor.
In addition, for MD simulations of a system with volume
V
, a constant electric field
\bm{\hat{E}}
entering the Hamiltonian causes a change in the average field
\langle\Delta\bm{E}\rangle_{\mkern-1.0mu\bm{\hat{E}}}
according to
[
19
]

\big\langle\Delta\bm{E}(z)\big\rangle_{\mkern-1.0mu\bm{\hat{E}}}\,=\,\bm{\hat{E}}\,+\,\dfrac{\bm{e}_{z}}{\epsilon_{0}}\bigg(\dfrac{\langle\Delta M_{z}\rangle_{\mkern-1.0mu\bm{\hat{E}}}}{V}\,-\,\big\langle\Delta P_{\mkern-1.0mu\mkern-1.0muz}(z)\big\rangle_{\mkern-1.0mu\bm{\hat{E}}}\bigg)~,

(4)

where
\bm{e}_{z}
is the unit vector along the
z
-axis and
\langle\Delta M_{z}\rangle_{\mkern-1.0mu\bm{\hat{E}}}
is the change in the
z
-component of the total dipole moment of the system
[
23

.
Combining Eqs. (

\bm{\psi}
and

We now separately consider the membrane response to in-plane and out-of-plane electric fields to determine
\epsilon^{\mkern 3.0mu\vphantom{\perp}\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 2.0mu\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 3.0mu}(z)

To calculate

be parallel to the membrane; we choose

According to Eqs. (

Our simulations thus indicate
\epsilon^{\perp}(z)
is ill-defined in the head-group region.
To understand why, recall that the dielectric constant is fundamentally a macroscopic material property.
A spatially-dependent permittivity then relates, at each material point, the mean polarization density and electric fields—averaged over a coarse-graining volume.
This construction implicitly assumes the fields do not vary appreciably within the coarse-graining volume.
In the present scenario, however, there is no such separation in length scales because
\langle E_{z}(z)\rangle
varies considerably over atomic distances
[
21
]
.
Consequently,
\epsilon^{\perp}(z)
is not physically meaningful in the head-group region, and does not describe the overall membrane response to an out-of-plane electric field.

Three-slab model.
—While lipid membranes cannot be characterized by a local permittivity tensor
\bm{\epsilon}(z)
for the aforementioned reasons, Figs.
1
–
3
show bilayer properties are also far from uniform across the membrane thickness.
A fundamental question thus remains:
How can the response of a phospholipid bilayer to external electric fields be quantified?
We address this question by recognizing membrane features are qualitatively different within the head-group and tail regions, and thus modeling the membrane as a composite material made up of three uniform dielectric slabs (see Fig.
2
(a)
).
There is one slab of thickness
\delta_{{\text{h}}}
for each of the two head-group regions, while a single slab of thickness
2\mkern 1.0mu\delta_{{\text{t}}}
represents the entire tail region.
With our three-slab construction, microscopic features are averaged over lengths larger than the atomic distances over which
\langle E_{z}(z)\rangle
varies, and smaller than the bilayer thickness—such that the out-of-plane heterogeneity of the membrane is retained.

In the absence of an external electric field, a material comprised of three dielectric slabs is unpolarized and at a constant electric potential throughout.
Both features are inconsistent with the MD data in Figs.
1
(c)
and
2
(b)
.
We thus introduce an intrinsic, zero-field polarization density into the three-slab model.
For simplicity, the polarization density is constant and limited to the head-group slab.
It is captured by equal and opposite bound surface charge densities of magnitude
\sigma_{\mkern-1.0mu{\text{h}}}
on the faces of the head-group slabs, as shown in Fig.
2
(a)
.
We take the permittivity of the tail slab to be
\epsilon_{0}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{I}
, as the MD results in Fig.
3
show
\epsilon^{\mkern 3.0mu\vphantom{\perp}\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 2.0mu\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 3.0mu}(z)/\epsilon_{0}
and
\epsilon^{\perp}(z)/\epsilon_{0}
are just above unity in the tail region.
In contrast, the head-group slabs in our model have an anisotropic permittivity, with in-plane and out-of-plane components respectively denoted
\epsilon^{\mkern 3.0mu\vphantom{\perp}\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 2.0mu\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 3.0mu}_{{\text{h}}}
and
\epsilon^{\perp}_{{\text{h}}}
.
The three-slab model accordingly captures key electrostatic features of the membrane.

Conclusions.
—In this Letter, we developed a model for the dielectric permittivity of a lipid membrane, in which a bilayer is treated as three dielectric slabs.
The central slab represents the oily membrane interior, and has vacuum permittivity.
Outer slabs model the phospholipid heads, and have anisotropic permittivities and intrinsic polarization densities.
We overcame the ill-posedness of
\epsilon^{\perp}(z)
in the head-group regions, and calculated a physically meaningful
\epsilon^{\perp}_{{\text{h}}}
, by averaging microscopic features over slab thicknesses.
With all-atom MD simulations, we verified that the three-slab model captures zero-field membrane properties and the average bilayer response to electric fields.

Since our model involves only a few parameters, it can be incorporated into continuum theories of membrane electromechanics
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
.
Doing so could shed light on bilayer flexoelectricity
[

,

,

,

]
, as well as electric field-induced vesicle deformations
[

34

and phase transition modifications
[

.
Before investigating such complex phenomena, it would be natural to first extend the three-slab model to biologically-relevant systems with ions in the surrounding fluid and multiple phospholipid species, some of which carry a net charge.
One could also examine whether the unphysical local permittivity of water near solid surfaces

can be resolved with a slab-like approach.
