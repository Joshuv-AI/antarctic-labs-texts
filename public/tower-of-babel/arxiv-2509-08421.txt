Sparse BEV Fusion with Self-View Consistency for Multi-View Detection and Tracking
Keisuke Toida, Taigo Sakai, Naoki Kato, Kazutoyo Yokota, Takeshi Nakamura, Kazuhiro Hotta

Abstract
Multi-View Multi-Object Tracking (MVMOT) is essential for applications such as surveillance, autonomous driving, and sports analytics. However, maintaining consistent object identities across multiple cameras remains challenging due to viewpoint changes, lighting variations, and occlusions, which often lead to tracking errors. Recent methods project features from multiple cameras into a unified Bird’s-Eye-View (BEV) space to improve robustness against occlusion. However, this projection introduces feature distortion and non-uniform density caused by variations in object scale with distance. These issues degrade the quality of the fused representation and reduce detection and tracking accuracy. To address these problems, we propose SCFusion, a framework that combines three techniques to improve multi-view feature integration. First, it applies a sparse transformation to avoid unnatural interpolation during projection. Next, it performs density-aware weighting to adaptively fuse features based on spatial confidence and camera distance. Finally, it introduces a multi-view consistency loss that encourages each camera to learn discriminative features independently before fusion. Experiments show that SCFusion achieves state-of-the-art performance, reaching an IDF1 score of 95.9% on WildTrack and a MODP of 89.2% on MultiviewX, outperforming the baseline method TrackTacular. These results demonstrate that SCFusion effectively mitigates the limitations of conventional BEV projection and provides a robust and accurate solution for multi-view object detection and tracking.



1
Introduction

Multi-object tracking (MOT) is a task that aims to identify and track multiple objects, such as pedestrians and vehicles, across frames in video sequences
[
1
,
2
]
. It has gained significant importance due to its broad applicability in areas such as surveillance, robotic vision, autonomous driving, and sports analytics.

In multi-view multi-object tracking (MVMOT), the goal is to associate the same object observed from different viewpoints and track its movement across a larger area. However, challenges unique to the multi-view setting arise, including variations in appearance due to changes in camera angle and lighting conditions, occlusions caused by blind spots, and increased computational overhead from simultaneous multi-camera processing.

MVMOT which utilizes multiple viewpoints can enhance the robustness against occlusion. However, the process of projecting features into a common Bird’s-Eye-View (BEV)
[
3
]
space introduces new challenges. Specifically, the non-uniform density of projected features, a problem caused by variations in object scale with distance has been largely unaddressed. This density distortion degrades the detection accuracy, particularly for distant objects. This paper tackles this problem of feature non-uniformity in BEV projection.

We propose three solutions. (1) a sparse transformation method to avoid unnatural pixel interpolation, (2) density-aware weighting that adjusts feature fusion based on spatial confidence and distance to cameras, and (3) a multi-view consistency loss that encourages each camera’s features to be individually informative for BEV detection.

SCFusion’s robust performance is validated on standard benchmarks. It achieved a new state-of-the-art 95.9% IDF1 tracking score on WildTrack and a significantly improved 85.4% MOTP for detection on MultiviewX.

In Section 2, we review related works on MVMOT, categorizing existing methods into late and early fusion approaches. Section 3 describes our SCFusion in detail, addressing the feature integration problem in multi-view scenarios. Section 4 presents experimental validation on the MultiviewX dataset. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper and outlines future directions.

2.1
Multi-View Multi-Object Tracking (MVMOT)

MVMOT is a technique that tracks multiple objects from multiple viewpoints. Compared to single-view methods, MVMOT mitigates issues such as occlusion and blind spots by leveraging information from multiple perspectives. As illustrated in Figure
1
, MVMOT approaches can be categorized into two types based on the stage at which multi-view information is fused: Late Multi-View and Early Multi-View.
The Late Multi-View approach first performs detection independently on each camera and then fuses the results. Examples of this method include PolarMOT
[
4
]
, which projects 2D detection results into a BEV polar coordinate system, and MTMCT
[
5
]
, which uses re-identification based on features from single-view tracking. However, this method is limited because information from each camera is kept separate during detection. A detection error in one view due to occlusion cannot be corrected by another view, which limits accuracy.

In contrast, the Early Multi-View approach aggregates feature maps from all cameras into a unified BEV before performing detection. By sharing the information at the feature level, this approach is more robust to occlusion and allows for more accurate object localization. In fact, methods like MVDet
[
6
]
, MUTR3D
[
7
]
, and BEVFormer
[
8
]
have demonstrated that this Early approach can achieve more accurate localization. EarlyBird
[
9
]
extends the MVDet architecture by projecting camera features to the BEV and performing joint detection and tracking end-to-end using a unified network. TrackTacular
[
10
]
shown in Figure
2
also follows the Early Multi-View paradigm by fusing camera features in BEV space to achieve high performance in both detection and tracking.
After aggregating the BEV projection and the features from all cameras, Tracktacular stores the BEV feature map before the detection decoder (denoted as History BEV(t–1)) and reuses it in the next frame t, effectively addressing occlusion.

However, this advanced approach still faces its own challenge: the projection from 2D to BEV creates feature distortion and non-uniform density. This problem is particularly noticeable as the scale of objects changes with distance, which degrades the final detection quality.
Our proposed SCFusion directly solves this projection problem to further enhance MVMOT performance.

Proposed Method

3.1
Overview of the Proposed Method: SCFusion

Figure 3:
Overview of the SCFusion. Each camera view is projected sparsely into BEV space, followed by confidence-based fusion and consistency supervision.

The overall architecture of our proposed SCFusion is illustrated in Figure
3
.
Our approach transforms features extracted from multiple camera through three key components.

1.

Sparse Perspective Transform (SPT):
To avoid the information distortion caused by conventional dense transformation methods, we sparsely project features into the BEV space as shown in Figure
4
.

2.

Density-Aware Weighted Aggregation:
We aggregate the BEV features from each camera by weighting them according to their reliability (i.e., density). This process yields a feature representation that better reflects physical confidence, as visualized in Figure
5
.

3.

Multi-View Consistency Loss:
We compute a detection loss (
L_{\mbox{single}}
) on the single-view BEV features before fusion and combine it with the loss (
L_{\mbox{multi}}
) from the final fused features. This encourages each view’s features to be independently discriminative, thereby improving overall robustness.

The following subsections detail these components.

3.2
Proposed Solution for Multi-View Feature Integration

We propose two techniques to address the challenges of multi-view feature integration. The overall architecture is illustrated in Figure
3
.
First, we introduce a
sparse transformation method
that accounts for the non-uniform density of information during the projection of camera features into the BEV space. This approach properly handles the variation in feature density caused by differences in object distance within the image, leading to more accurate feature representations.

Second, we propose a
multi-view consistency loss
, which performs independent detection learning on the BEV features of each camera. This encourages each camera’s features to become individually effective for BEV-based detection before the fusion stage, resulting in improved overall feature quality during multi-view integration.

3.3
Sparse Projection and Density-Aware Fusion

We focus on the problem of
information density distortion
in the projection from camera images to the BEV space, which has not been properly addressed by conventional bilinear interpolation-based methods. In perspective images, near objects are represented with higher pixel density than distant ones.
When these are projected into the BEV space, sparse regions representing nearby objects may be unnaturally stretched.
This stretching causes spatial misalignment and distorts the feature representation, harming the model’s ability to accurately localize objects.

To address this, we introduce a
sparse projection method
that selectively projects only valid feature points, preserving the natural density distribution of the scene. Figure
4
compares our sparse warping method against conventional bilinear warping. As shown in Figure
4
(a), bilinear warping unnaturally stretches features and introduces distortion. In contrast, our method shown in Figure
4
(b) selectively projects only valid feature points, preserving the scene’s structure more faithfully. To further support learning from sparse representations, we modify the network architecture immediately after feature fusion. Specifically, we use convolutional layers with larger kernels (from 3
\times
3 to 5
\times
5), and
dilated convolutions

[
11
]
are used to expand the receptive field without sacrificing resolution.

Additionally, we incorporate
density-aware weighting
during multi-view feature fusion. Specifically, we assign higher confidence to features from nearby objects and lower confidence to those from distant regions, based on their depth in camera space. The effectiveness of this weighted aggregation is visualized in Figure
5
. Unlike simple fusion (TrackTacular), which can result in information gaps in the feature map (circled in red), our SCFusion utilizes density maps as weights to generate a richer and more uniform feature representation. This yields a fused representation that better reflects the physical reliability of features.

3.4

4
Experiments

4.1
Experimental Setup

In experiments, we evaluate our SCFusion on the WildTrack dataset
[
13
]
, and MultiviewX dataset
[
14
]
.
WildTrack is a large-scale multi-camera pedestrian detection and tracking dataset captured in a real-world outdoor environment. As shown in Figure
6
(a), it includes seven static HD cameras with overlapping fields of view, each with a resolution of
1920\times 1080
pixels and a synchronized frame rate of 2 FPS. The dataset contains 400 annotated frames, total 2800 images across all views. The average number of pedestrians per frame is approximately 24.
MultiviewX is a synthetic multi-view pedestrian detection and tracking dataset rendered using a game engine. As shown in Figure
6
(b), it includes six virtual cameras with overlapping fields of view.
The average number of pedestrians per frame is around 40. Ground-truth annotations are provided for both detection and tracking in the BEV space (
640\times 1000
pixels).
Ground-truth annotations are provided both in the image plane and on the BEV
ground plane, allowing precise evaluation of detection and tracking in world coordinates.

Compared to MultiviewX which is rendered using a game engine, WildTrack provides a more realistic evaluation environment. It features real-world imagery from seven static HD cameras (1920x1080 resolution at 2 FPS) observing an average of 24 pedestrians per frame. Furthermore, WildTrack offers higher camera calibration accuracy, making it particularly suitable for evaluating geometry-aware multi-view detection algorithms under realistic occlusion and lighting conditions.

For the Gaussian filter used in density estimation, we set the standard deviation to
\sigma=1
and the kernel size to
5\times 5
. The parameters for the Focal Loss are set as
\alpha=2
and
\gamma=4
, following the same configuration as the baseline Tracktacular. The balancing coefficient
\beta
for the multi-view consistency loss is set to 0.1, as it showed the best validation accuracy among tested values
{0.001,\ 0.1,\ 1.0,\ 10,\ 100}
.
Experiments were run on a machine with RTX 4080 super GPU, Intel i7-14700K CPU.
We conducted quantitative evaluation on the BEV detection map using standard 2D detection and tracking metrics. MODA measures detection accuracy considering false positives and false negatives. MODP evaluates the average localization precision of correct detections. MOTA reflects overall tracking accuracy, accounting for false positives, false negatives, and ID switches. MOTP indicates the average localization error of matched objects. IDF1 is the F1 score based on correctly identified detections.

To further evaluate the contribution of each component, we conduct an ablation study shown in Table
3
. We evaluated three components individually and in combination: Sparse Perspective Transform (SPT), Weighted Aggregation, and Multi-View Consistency Loss (MCLoss).
Adding SPT (+ SPT) improved all metrics, notably boosting MODP from 76.2% to 79.5%. This indicated that avoiding unnatural interpolation by projecting only valid features (as explained in Section 3.3) leads to more precise object localization, which is directly reflected in the MODP score.
Adding Density-Aware Weighting (+ Weight Aggregation) further improved performance, especially on tracking metrics like IDF1 and MOTA. By assigning higher confidence to features from nearby objects, the model effectively mitigates distortions from distant, low-resolution regions. This results in a more reliable fused representation and, consequently, more stable tracking with fewer identity mismatches.
Finally, including the Multi-View Consistency loss (+ MC loss) provides the largest boost to IDF1 (95.9%). This loss encourages each camera’s features to be more effective for detection before fusion. This pre-training of individual views makes the final representation more robust to occlusions and challenging scenarios.

In addition to the quantitative metrics, Figure

provides a qualitative comparison of tracking trajectories on the WildTrack dataset, visually confirming our method’s effectiveness.
The baseline method, TrackTacular shown in Figure
7
(a), exhibits several noticeable tracking errors. Some trajectories are fragmented, indicating that the tracker frequently loses objects. Other paths show abrupt color changes, which correspond to identity switches where the tracker confuses one person for another.
In contrast, the trajectories generated by our SCFusion shown in Figure

This visual improvement attributes to our robust feature fusion mechanism. By mitigating feature distortions through sparse projection and density-aware weighting, SCFusion maintains a more reliable representation of each object. This qualitative analysis indicates that SCFusion achieves more robust multi-object tracking.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we proposed SCFusion, a framework to address the feature distortion and non-uniform density that arise when multi-view features are projected into a single BEV space. Our approach integrates three key components: a sparse projection method to prevent interpolation artifacts, density-aware weighting to prioritize reliable features, and a multi-view consistency loss to ensure that each view learns a robust representation before fusion.
Experimental results on standard benchmarks validated the effectiveness of our method. Notably, SCFusion set a new state-of-the-art in tracking on the WildTrack dataset, achieving a 95.9% IDF1 score. This demonstrated that our approach successfully integrates multi-view information to achieve robust and accurate scene understanding, mitigating the tracking inconsistencies caused by projection distortions.

Despite these improvements, promising directions for future work remain. Enhancing computational efficiency for applications that run in real time and developing methods that can operate without pre-calibrated camera parameters are key steps toward practical deployment. Our future research will focus on these areas to bring robust multi-view tracking closer to real situations.
