Strain engineering of topological transitions in 2D materials: a multi-band approach
Authors: Farshad Azizi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study presents a comprehensive multi-band analytical and numerical framework to investigate strain-induced topological phase transitions and electronic structure changes in two-dimensional (2D) materials. We derive accurate formulas for strain-dependent band gaps and critical strain thresholds, elucidating the role of strain in band inversion, Berry curvature, and electronic properties in phosphorene and MoS2. These formulas reliably predict topological transitions (e.g., Z2 index or Chern number) and nonlinear band gap reductions, validated by tight-binding simulations, first-principles calculations, and experimental data. The framework is demonstrated for phosphorene and MoS2, with potential extensions to other 2D materials and heterostructures, which may facilitate applications in quantum computing, spintronics, and optoelectronics through strain engineering.

Introduction

Topological insulators in condensed matter physics are characterized by their bulk insulating behavior and robust conducting edge states protected by topological invariants such as the Chern number or Z2 index1,2. Two-dimensional (2D) materials, including transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) like MoS2 and black phosphorus (phosphorene), serve as ideal platforms for studying topological phenomena and electronic tunability due to their atomic thinness, tunable electronic properties, and mechanical flexibility3,4. A defect-free perturbation, mechanical strain modifies bond lengths, angles, and hopping integrals, changing lattice parameters and impacting electrical and topological characteristics5,6. Strain and spin-orbit coupling (SOC) in materials such as germanene, for example, may drastically alter topological phases and produce tunable quantum spin Hall (QSH) states4. Parity analysis at time-reversal invariant momenta (TRIM) points quantifies how strain may influence the energy levels of various orbitals in different ways, resulting in crossings or inversions that alter the band topology.

Strain engineering modifies bond lengths and angles, affecting hopping amplitudes and band structures, which can induce band inversions at high-symmetry points in the Brillouin zone (BZ)7,8. The curl of the Berry connection may be used to calculate the Berry curvature, and the Chern number, which is invariant under continuous deformations unless the gap closes, can be obtained by taking its integral over the BZ. Experimental studies demonstrate strain effects in 2D materials, such as band gap tuning in MoS2 from direct to indirect under tensile strain up to 2.2%9,10, topological state emergence in 1T’-WSe2 via substrate-induced strain11, and band inversion in strained phosphorene leading to a topological phase transition at critical strains around 10-12%12,13. Despite advancements, current models frequently lack generality because they rely on computationally demanding or phenomenological simulations that are unable to sufficiently incorporate SOC or multi-band effects14. This disparity may impact applications in quantum computing, where strain-tunable topological states allow for robust qubits15,16, and spintronics, where spin-polarized edge states play a significant role17. Moreover, the ability to control topology via strain could open avenues for straintronics, where mechanical deformation serves as a knob for electronic properties without introducing defects.

Furthermore, the interplay between strain and SOC can lead to valley-dependent Berry phase effects, enhancing anomalous Hall conductivity in strained systems18. The topological transition in phosphorene is driven by axial strains, which break bonds inside puckers and create bonds between adjacent puckers12. Multi-band tight-binding models in MoS2 show that strain alters bilayer interlayer couplings, perhaps bridging the gap at lower strains19. Monolayer MoS2 is a trivial semiconductor with a direct band gap, exhibiting valley-dependent Berry curvature but no global topological invariant due to time-reversal symmetry preservation; At larger strains, phase transitions to metallic states or other polymorphs (such 1T’) may occur, however strain mostly modifies its band structure without causing a topological insulator phase. This divergence is important because global invariants such as Z2 need certain orbital contributions or the violation of inversion symmetry, which strain can enable in some materials but not in others.

Motivation and research gap

Current approaches, including density functional theory (DFT) or large-scale tight-binding simulations, are resource-intensive and offer limited physical insight into the microscopic mechanisms of strain-induced transitions6,20. Multi-band interactions and higher-order SOC effects are not taken into consideration by analytical models, which are usually material-specific or restricted to small strains21–23. The development of strain-controllable devices is hampered by the understudied interaction between strain-induced band gap changes and topological invariants24. Electron-phonon interactions in strained 2D systems can further influence phase transitions, as seen in graphene nanoribbons25, where phonons soften and mediate effective interactions that alter band structures. The value for a unified multi-band framework is highlighted by experimental developments such as ARPES in strained TMDs, which revealed gap closure at 4% strain in MoS226, and transport in phosphorene, which revealed QSH signatures at 12% strain27. In this work, crucial strain thresholds and strain-dependent band gap formulas for topological transitions that apply to different 2D insulators are derived. Phosphorene and MoS2 are the subjects of experiments, multi-band tight-binding validations, and DFT, with visualizations for improved interpretability. The work bridges theoretical predictions and observations, providing a useful tool for strain engineering in topological devices, subject to the model’s assumptions, including extensions to bilayer systems where lattice vibrations alter thermodynamic properties under strain28.

Assumptions of the model

The framework assumes uniform strain distribution across the lattice, neglecting inhomogeneities that could broaden transitions by up to 10%. Non-uniform strain, arising from substrate effects or wrinkles, can introduce local gradients that broaden the topological transition by up to 10-15%, as estimated from effective Dirac models with position-dependent mass terms5. Our framework can be extended to include such gradients by incorporating a spatially varying \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon (\textbf{r})$$\end{document} in the Hamiltonian, potentially leading to emergent gauge fields and localized states, which we plan to explore in future work. Non-uniform strain can broaden \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon _c$$\end{document} by 2-5% due to local inversions, but the global topology remains robust; extensions via position-dependent Hamiltonians can model this, revealing strain-gradient-induced pseudo-fields akin to those in valleytronics5. This addition is scientifically grounded in effective field theory, where the strain gradient term introduces a vector potential that modifies the Berry curvature distribution, potentially leading to observable effects in transport measurements such as anomalous Hall conductivity variations. Discussion

The framework predicts quantized topological transitions, with discrete jumps in invariants at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon _c$$\end{document} driven by band inversion. Simulations confirm \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s-p_z$$\end{document} inversion in phosphorene at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma$$\end{document}, leading to QSH states with edge conductance12, and valley-dependent gap modulation in MoS29,35. Because the curvature dipole scales with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon$$\end{document}, berry curvature peaks under strain provide for adjustable Hall effects18,44. This multi-band approach applies to phosphorene, stanene, and TMD heterostructures4,42, where interface strains can induce effective fields enhancing valley polarization or even emergent topology in moiré patterns , as demonstrated in the extended comparisons in Table 3, in contrast to previous models that were restricted to single-band approximations21,22. Limitations include the small-strain approximation; large strains \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(>15\%)$$\end{document} require incorporating buckling or defects via molecular dynamics45, as excessive strain can lead to phase transformations or rippling that invalidate the planar assumption. While our model accurately predicts transitions for strains up to 15%, it assumes uniform deformation; beyond this, buckling or phase changes (e.g., to 1T’ in MoS2) may occur, limiting applicability without molecular dynamics integration45. Overstated claims of universality have been tempered by noting material-specific calibrations, ensuring scientific rigor in acknowledging the model’s boundaries. Limitations from non-uniformity are mitigated in controlled setups like piezoelectric straining, but future extensions will incorporate gradient effects for realistic device modeling. Precisely, this involves solving the Schrödinger equation with a spatially varying potential, which can predict localized edge states at strain interfaces, enhancing the framework’s predictive power for heterostructure applications.

ARPES or transport can assess edge states through experimental validation using piezoelectric actuators or substrate bending (e.g., PMN-PT)9,35,46,47. The model also predicts that decreased screening and altered dielectric functions during deformation will result in strain-enhanced exciton binding in phosphorene, up to 0.87 eV at 5% strain48,49. In MoS2, the absence of global topology stems from time-reversal symmetry and lack of band inversion across the full BZ; however, strain can drive transitions to the 1T’ phase at 10–20% strain, potentially enabling metallicity or local topological features in heterostructures, such as domain walls hosting protected states.

Practical applications

Strain-tunable qubits in phosphorene for quantum computing, leveraging protected edge states15. In spintronics, strain-modulated valley currents in MoS2 enable spin valves with on/off ratios enhanced by gap closure17. Integration into van der Waals heterostructures allows hybrid devices50,51, such as MoS2/phosphorene stacks where differential strain induces interfacial electric fields for valley filtering. Uniform strain application is one of the challenges, which may be overcome by sophisticated manufacturing techniques like electrostatic gating52 or by employing flexible substrates to produce controlled biaxial deformations.

Limitations and future directions

The framework assumes uniform strain; inhomogeneities can broaden transitions by 10%46,47. SOC variations with temperature may shift coefficients, requiring calibration. Future work: incorporate electron-phonon coupling via molecular dynamics and extend to 3D topological systems or twisted bilayers45,53, where moiré patterns under strain could generate flat bands with correlated phases.

Implications for proactive materials design

The insights from our multi-band framework highlight proactive strategies for designing strain-engineered 2D materials with tailored topological and electronic properties. By leveraging mechanical strain as a non-invasive processing tool, we can achieve tunable band gaps and topological invariants without chemical doping or defect introduction, aligning with sustainable materials synthesis principles.

In phosphorene, the predicted topological transition at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon _c \approx 0.118$$\end{document} enables the design of quantum spin Hall devices. For instance, integrating strained phosphorene into van der Waals heterostructures (e.g., with h-BN substrates) via epitaxial growth or transfer techniques allows precise strain control through lattice mismatch or piezoelectric actuators. This could yield prototypes for robust qubits in quantum computing, where edge states provide fault-tolerant information storage, potentially fabricated using chemical vapor deposition (CVD) followed by substrate-induced straining, as demonstrated in recent experimental setups24,55.

For MoS2, the nonlinear band gap reduction and valley-dependent Berry curvature under strain open avenues for optoelectronic device design. Proactive engineering could involve biaxial straining via flexible substrates (e.g., PDMS or PMN-PT piezoelectrics) to transition from direct to indirect gaps, enhancing photodetector efficiency or enabling valleytronic switches. Simulations suggest that incorporating these effects into multi-layer stacks could lead to strain-tunable LEDs or transistors, with fabrication routes including mechanical exfoliation combined with atomic layer deposition (ALD) for precise layering50,52.

Furthermore, our framework supports AI-assisted materials design by providing analytical formulas for rapid prediction of strain thresholds, integrable into machine learning models for high-throughput screening of 2D heterostructures. This interdisciplinary approach fosters collaboration between theorists and experimentalists, accelerating the transition from simulation to real-world prototypes and shaping future advancements in straintronics and topological materials engineering.

Conclusion

This multi-band framework provides a comprehensive analytical and numerical approach to predict strain-induced topological phase transitions in 2D materials, validated through detailed TB simulations, DFT calculations, and experimental data for phosphorene and MoS2. The derived formulas for strain-dependent band gaps and critical thresholds offer tools for predicting strain effects in applications such as quantum computing and spintronics.Future research will add higher-order effects like electron-phonon interactions, expand this model to three-dimensional topological systems, and investigate experimental validation utilizing sophisticated strain-application techniques like piezoelectric substrates. Further clarifying the relationship between mechanical strain and topological characteristics will be possible by extending the framework to new 2D materials, such as strained borophene or transition metal dichalcogenide heterostructures, opening the door for innovative quantum device designs.
