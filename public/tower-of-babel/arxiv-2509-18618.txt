Isoperimetric problems and lower bounds on curvature
Gioacchino Antonelli

Abstract
. This note surveys some classical results and recent developments on the interplay between lower curvature bounds and the isoperimetric problem. It is based on mini-courses given at the European Doctorate School of Differential Geometry (Granada, July 2024) and the summer school Optimal transport, heat flow and synthetic Ricci bounds (Chicago, June 2025).



1.
Introduction

This note is intended as a gentle introduction to some aspects of the
interplay between lower curvature bounds and isoperimetry
. Many arguments are only sketched, simplified, or treated in particular cases. This is therefore not a paper with complete proofs, but rather a motivating guide for further study. The reader is encouraged to attempt the
Exercises
collected at the end, and to reflect on the
Open Questions
posed throughout the lectures, some of which touch on open problems in the field. For an overview of the material, we refer to the table of contents above and the four lectures into which this note is organized.

The material is based on my collaborations and papers with Elia Bruè (B), Mattia Fogagnolo (F), Federico Glaudo (G), Stefano Nardulli (N), Enrico Pasqualetto (Pa), Marco Pozzetta (Po), Daniele Semola (S), Ivan Violo (V), Kai Xu (X). 2.1.
Preliminaries

A general reference for results about sets of finite perimeter is Maggi’s book
[
66
]
. In this note
(M^{n},g)
will be a complete smooth Riemannian manifold without boundary (if not otherwise stated). The Riemannian distance associated to
g
will be denoted
\mathrm{d}
. The open (resp., closed) ball of center
p\in M
and radius
r>0
will be denoted
B_{r}(p)
(resp.,
\overline{B}_{r}(p)
).

Let
k\geqslant 0
,
\delta>0
, and
E\subset M
. We denote the Hausdorff measure

\mathcal{H}^{k}(E):=\sup_{\delta>0}\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\delta}(E):=\sup_{\delta>0}\inf\left\{2^{-k}\omega_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\diam(A_{i})^{k}:E\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i},\diam(A_{i})\leqslant\delta\right\},

where
\omega_{k}:=\pi^{k/2}\Gamma((k+2)/2)^{-1}
. When
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\omega_{k}
is the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
\mathcal{H}^{k}(\cdot)
is a Borel regular measure on
M^{n}
.

Let
E\subset M^{n}
be a Borel set. The
volume
of
E\subset M^{n}
is
\mathcal{H}^{n}(E)
. It will also be denoted with
|E|
. Let
\alpha=0
(resp.,
\alpha=1
), and let
E\subset M
be a Borel set. We denote
E^{(\alpha)}
the
essential exterior
(resp., the
essential interior
) of
E
:

E^{(\alpha)}:=\left\{x\in M:\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{|E\cap B_{r}(x)|}{|B_{r}(x)|}=\alpha\right\}.

The
essential boundary
of
E
is
\partial^{*}E:=E\setminus(E^{(0)}\cup E^{(1)})
.

The
perimeter of
E
inside an open set
\Omega\subset M
is

(1)

P(E,\Omega):=\sup\left\{\int_{E}\mathrm{div}(X)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n}:X\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega;TM),g(X,X)\leqslant 1\right\}.

The
perimeter
of
E
is
P(E):=P(E,M)
. We say that
E
has
finite perimeter
if
P(E)<\infty
.

2.1.1.
Properties and comments

(1)

(Density
1/2
and essential boundary) (De Giorgi–Federer) When
E
has finite perimeter, it can be shown that
P(E,\Omega)=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}E\cap\Omega)
for every open set
\Omega
. Moreover, for
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-almost every
x\in\partial^{*}E
we have:

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{|E\cap B_{r}(x)|}{|B_{r}(x)|}=\frac{1}{2}.

In addition, at
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-almost every
x\in\partial^{*}E

the
(unique) blow-up of
\partial^{*}E
at
x
is a codimension-one Euclidean hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
: a generalized unit normal
\nu(x)
is defined at
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-almost every point
x\in\partial^{*}E
.

(2)

(A general notion of perimeter) The notion of perimeter can be defined in a metric measure space
(X,\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})
, where
(X,\mathrm{d})
is a complete separable metric space, and
\mathfrak{m}
is a Radon measure. See Ambrosio
[
3
]
, and Miranda
[
72
]
. In such a setting, given
f\in L^{1}(X,\mathfrak{m})
, we say that
f
is of
bounded variation
, or
f\in\mathrm{BV}(X,\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})
, if

|Df|(X):=\inf\liminf_{i\to\infty}\left\{\int_{X}|\nabla f_{i}|\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}:f_{i}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X,\mathrm{d}),f_{i}\rightarrow f\,\text{in $L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X,\mathfrak{m})$}\right\}<+\infty.

Here
|\nabla f|(x):=\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{|f(y)-f(x)|}{\mathrm{d}(x,y)}
. An
\mathfrak{m}
-finite set
E\subset M
has
finite perimeter
when
\chi_{E}\in\mathrm{BV}(X,\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})
. This notion is consistent with the one given above (
1
).

The notions of set of finite perimeter and function of bounded variation have been recently thoroughly studied in abstract metric measure spaces. In particular, the basic structure results known in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and Riemannian manifolds that we are discussing here in
Section

2.1.1
have been extended, as of today, to the setting of
N
-dimensional
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
-spaces with
K\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\in[1,\infty)
(see
Section

2.3
), see, e.g.,
[
6
,
34
,
33
,
10
]
.

(3)

(Precompactness and lower semicontinuity) If
(E_{i})\subset M
is a sequence of sets of finite perimeter with

\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}P(E_{i})<\infty,

then up to subsequences

\chi_{E_{i}}\longrightarrow\chi_{E},\qquad\text{in $L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}$},

and

P(E)\leqslant\liminf_{i\to\infty}P(E_{i}).

(4)

(Approximation) Let
f\in\mathrm{BV}(M,\mathrm{d},\mathcal{H}^{n})
. Then there exists
f_{i}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(M)
such that
f_{i}\to f
in
L^{1}
and
\int_{M}|\nabla f_{i}|\to|Df|(M)
.

(5)

(Coarea formula) For every
f\in\mathrm{Lip}(M)
we have

\int_{M}|\nabla f|\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}P(\{f\geqslant t\})\mathrm{d}t.

A general version of the co-area formula valid in arbitrary metric measure spaces can be found in
[
72
, Section 4]
.

2.2.
Isoperimetric problem

Let
(M^{n},g)
be a Riemannian manifold. Let
I_{M}:(0,|M|)\to[0,+\infty]
be the
isoperimetric profile
defined as:

I_{M}(v):=\inf\left\{P(E):|E|=v\right\}.

If a Borel set
E\subset M^{n}
satisfies
I_{M}(|E|)=P(E)
, then
E
is called an
isoperimetric set
. For discussions about the regularity of the function
I
see the end of Lecture 1 and Lecture 2. When there is no risk of confusion we will write
I
instead of
I_{M}
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
(M^{n},g)
be a compact Riemannian manifold. Then for every
v\in(0,|M|)
there exists an isoperimetric set with volume
v
.

Proof.

Take
(E_{i})\subset M
such that
|E_{i}|=v
and
P(E_{i})\to I(v)
and apply
Item

3
of the list of properties above.
∎

Remark 1

(About existence in the non-compact case)
.

Lemma

1
holds also when
M
is possibly non-compact but has finite volume, due to Ritoré–Rosales
[
85
]
. When
M
has infinite volume, one can come up with simple examples for which there are volumes
v
such that no isoperimetric sets of volume
v
exist (see
1
).
Constructing examples without isoperimetric sets keeping the curvature bounded below is more challenging, see Lecture 4.

Theorem 1

(Almgren, Bombieri, Morgan)
.

Let
\widetilde{E}\subset M^{n}
be an isoperimetric set. Then there exists an open set
E\subset M^{n}
with
|E\Delta\widetilde{E}|=0
such that
\partial E=\partial^{*}E
and we can decompose:

\partial E=\partial^{\mathrm{sing}}E\sqcup\partial^{\mathrm{reg}}E,

where:

(1)

\partial^{\mathrm{sing}}E
is closed, and for every
d>n-8
,
\mathcal{H}^{d}(\partial^{\mathrm{sing}}E)=0
;

(2)

\partial^{\mathrm{reg}}E
is an open smooth hypersurface with constant mean curvature.

2.2.1.
Isoperimetric sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

We give a proof of the well-known isoperimetric inequality on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a set of finite perimeter with
|E|=|B_{1}(0)|=\omega_{n}
. Then

(2)

P(E)\geqslant P(B_{1}(0)).

If equality holds, then there is
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
|E\Delta B_{1}(p)|=0
.

Proof.

We only prove the inequality. For
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}
, let
A+B:=\{a+b:a\in A,b\in B\}
. Define the
lower Minkowski content
of
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
as:

\mathcal{M}(E):=\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{|E+B_{r}|-|E|}{r}.

It is known in general that the lower semicontinuous relaxation of
\mathcal{M}(\cdot)
with respect to the convergence in measure (on finite measure sets) is the perimeter, see Ambrosio–Di Marino–Gigli
[
7
]
. So it is enough to show that if
|E|=|B_{1}(0)|
, then

\mathcal{M}(E)\geqslant P(B_{1}(0)).

By the Brunn–Minkowski inequality (
3
) we have

\mathcal{M}(E)\geqslant\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\left(|E|^{1/n}+|B_{r}|^{1/n}\right)^{n}-|E|}{r}=n\omega_{n}=P(B_{1}(0)).

The proof by Steiner symmetrization is better suited to show the equality case: see
[
66
]
.
∎

Moreover, in

(2)

There are several approaches to the proof of the isoperimetric inequality. The reader might consult, e.g., Lecture 4, the recent survey

2.3.
Brunn–Minkowski inequality and Curvature–Dimension condition

Let us give a proof of the Brunn–Minkowski inequality that is different from the one hinted at in
3
. This proof, which leverages a result of
[
68
]
, unveils a connection with the theory of optimal transport and curvature-dimension conditions in the weak sense.

Let us recall that
e_{i}:X\times X\to X
denotes the projection on the
i
-th factor of the product, and
\mathbb{P}(X)
denotes the set of probabilities on
X
.
For more details on what follows the interested reader can consult, e.g., Chapters 8, 10, 15, and 19 of
[
5
]
.
The reader can consult
[
5
, Chapter 10]
for other properties (e.g., being non-branching, or positively curved) of
(X,\mathrm{d})
that are transmitted to
(\mathscr{P}_{2}(X),W_{2})
.

Definition 1

(Wasserstein space)
.

Let
(X,\mathrm{d})
be a complete and separable metric space, and define

\mathscr{P}_{2}(X):=\left\{\mu\in\mathbb{P}(X):\exists x_{0}\in X\,\text{s.t}\,\int_{X}\mathrm{d}(x,x_{0})^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<+\infty\right\}.

For
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(X)
, define

W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu):=\min\left\{\int_{X\times X}\mathrm{d}^{2}(x,y)\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y):\pi\in\mathbb{P}(X\times X):(e_{1})_{\#}\pi=\mu,(e_{2})_{\#}\pi=\nu\right\}.

Then
(\mathscr{P}_{2}(X),W_{2})
is a complete and separable metric space. Moreover, if
(X,d)
is compact (resp., geodesic),
(\mathscr{P}_{2}(X),W_{2})
is also compact (resp., geodesic).

Definition 2

(Rény entropy)
.

Let
(X,\mathrm{d})
be a complete and separable metric space. Let
\mathfrak{m}
be a Borel measure which is finite on bounded sets. Let
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(X)
and
N\geqslant 1
. Then, if we decompose
\mu=\rho\mathfrak{m}+\mu^{s}
, where
\rho\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathfrak{m})
and
\mu^{s}\perp\mathfrak{m}
, the
N
-Rény entropy
of
\mu
is

\mathcal{E}_{N}(\mu):=-\int_{X}\rho^{1-\frac{1}{N}}\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}.

An important feature of the
n
-Rény entropy is that it is convex along geodesics of
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The next proposition, which implies Brunn–Minkowski inequality as we will see below, is due to McCann. We sketch its proof referring the reader to
[
5
]
for the missing details.

Theorem 3

(
[
68
, Proposition 1.2]
)
.

The
n
-Rény entropy is convex along geodesics connecting two probability measures in
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that are absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue
\mathscr{L}^{n}
.

Sketch.

Let
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\ni\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\ll\mathscr{L}^{n}
. Let
\nabla\phi
, where
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(\infty,\infty]
is convex and lower semicontinuous, be the transport map from
\mu_{0}
to
\mu_{1}
. Then the geodesic connecting
\mu_{0}
to
\mu_{1}
is

\mu_{t}=((1-t)\mathrm{id}+t\nabla\phi)_{\#}\mu_{0}=:(T_{t})_{\#}\mu_{0}.

Let us call
U(s):=-s^{1-\frac{1}{n}}
, and
\rho
the density of
\mu_{0}
with respect to
\mathscr{L}^{n}
. It can be shown that, for every
t\in[0,1]
,

\mathcal{E}_{n}(\mu_{t})=\int_{D_{0}\setminus\Sigma}U\left(\frac{\rho(x)}{\det\nabla T_{t}(x)}\right)\det\nabla T_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}\mathscr{L}^{n},

where
D_{0}
is the
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-full-measure set of points
x
where both
\phi
and
\nabla\phi
are differentiable, and
\Sigma:=\{x:\det\nabla T(x)=0\}
is
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-negligible. Now observe that for every
x\in D_{0}\setminus\Sigma

a(t):=(\det\nabla T_{t}(x))^{1/n}
is concave, and for every
z>0
, and
x\in D_{0}\setminus\Sigma
, we have that
b(t):=z^{n}U(\rho(x)z^{-n})
is convex and non-increasing. Then
[0,1]\ni t\mapsto\mathcal{E}_{n}(\mu_{t})
is convex.
∎

As one can see inspecting the proof, the key property that we are using is that the function
U(s)
defining the Rény entropy
\mathcal{E}_{n}
has the following property:
(0,\infty)\ni s\mapsto s^{n}U(s^{-n})
is convex and non-increasing.

The previous
Theorem

3
can also be understood as one of the starting point of the abstract theory of spaces with curvature lower bounded in the weak sense.

Definition 3

(Sturm
[
88
,
89
]
, Lott–Villani
[
65
]
)
.

Let
(X,\mathrm{d})
be a complete and separable metric space. Let
\mathfrak{m}
be a Borel measure which is finite on bounded sets. Let
N\geqslant 1
. We say that
(X,\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})

satisfies the
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
condition
if the following holds.

For any
\mathscr{P}_{2}(X)\ni\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\ll\mathfrak{m}
with bounded support, there exists a geodesic
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
in
(\mathscr{P}_{2}(X),W_{2})
such that
\mu_{t}\ll\mathfrak{m}
for every
t\in[0,1]
and
[0,1]\ni t\mapsto\mathcal{E}_{N^{\prime}}(\mu_{t})
is convex, for all
N^{\prime}\geqslant N
.

Proof of Brunn–Minkowksi inequality using
Theorem

3
.

Let
A,B
be Borel sets with
\mathscr{L}^{n}(A)>0
and
\mathscr{L}^{n}(B)>0
. Let
\mu_{t}
be the
W_{2}
-geodesic connecting
\mu_{A}:=\frac{\chi_{A}}{\mathscr{L}^{n}(A)}\mathscr{L}^{n}
and
\mu_{B}:=\frac{\chi_{B}}{\mathscr{L}^{n}(A)}\mathscr{L}^{n}
. Thus, by
Theorem

3
we have, for every
t\in[0,1]
,

-\mathcal{E}_{n}(\mu_{t})\geqslant-(1-t)\mathcal{E}_{n}(\mu_{A})-t\mathcal{E}_{n}(\mu_{B})=(1-t)\mathscr{L}^{n}(A)^{1/n}+t\mathscr{L}^{n}(B)^{1/n}.

Notice that by Jensen inequality, whenever a probability measure
\mu=\rho\mathscr{L}^{n}
is supported on a closed set
C
, then
-\mathcal{E}_{n}(\mu)\leqslant\mathscr{L}^{n}(C)^{1/n}
. Thus, since
\mu_{t}
is supported on the set
(1-t)A+tB
(the optimal transport takes place along geodesics, see
[
5
]
for more details), we finally get

\mathscr{L}^{n}((1-t)A+tB)^{1/n}\geqslant(1-t)\mathscr{L}^{n}(A)^{1/n}+t\mathscr{L}^{n}(B)^{1/n},

as desired.
∎

Verbatim as in the proof of Brunn–Minkowski inequality above we have the following.
Theorem

4
will be useful in Lecture 4.

Theorem 4

(Brunn–Minkowski inequality on
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
spaces)
.

Let
N\geqslant 1
, and
(X,\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})
be a
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
metric measure space. Let
A_{0},A_{1}
be Borel sets with positive
\mathfrak{m}
measure. For every
t\in[0,1]
let us define

A_{t}:=\{y\in X:\exists(x_{0},x_{1})\in A_{0}\times A_{1},\,\,\mathrm{d}(x_{0},y)=t\mathrm{d}(x_{0},x_{1}),\,\,\mathrm{d}(y,x_{1})=(1-t)\mathrm{d}(x_{0},x_{1})\}.

Then, for every
t\in[0,1]
and
N^{\prime}\geqslant N
we have

\mathfrak{m}(A_{t})^{1/N^{\prime}}\geqslant(1-t)\mathfrak{m}(A_{0})^{1/N^{\prime}}+t\mathfrak{m}(A_{1})^{1/N^{\prime}}.

We do not delve into giving the precise definition of
\mathrm{CD}(K,N)
and
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
spaces when
K
might be non-zero, or
N
might be infinite. We refer the reader to the seminal papers
[
65
,
88
,
89
,
8
,
9
,
57
]
. We only remark that on a complete
N
-dimensional Riemannian manifold the
(\mathrm{R})\mathrm{CD}(K,N)
-condition is equivalent to
\mathrm{Ric}\geqslant K
, see
[
89
, Theorem 1.7]
.

2.4.
Statement of the sharp concavity inequality

In Lecture 2 we are going to prove the following result. We start by stating it. We focus on the case where
M
is a Riemannian manifold, but the proof works for arbitrary
n
-dimensional
\mathrm{RCD}(k,n)
-spaces
(X,\mathrm{d},\mathcal{H}^{n})
, see
[
17
]
.

Theorem 5

(Bavard–Pansu
[
26
]
, Bray
[
30
]
, Bayle
[
27
]
, A–Pa–Po–S
[
17
]
)
.

Let
(M^{n},g)
be a complete Riemannian manifold with
\inf_{x\in M}|B_{1}(x)|>0
. Assume that
\mathrm{Ric}\geqslant k\cdot g
as quadratic forms, for some
k\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

(3)

-I_{M}^{\prime\prime}I_{M}\geqslant k+\frac{(I_{M}^{\prime})^{2}}{n-1},\qquad\textit{holds in the viscosity sense on $(0,|M|)$}.

Remark 3

(About
I_{M}>0
)
.

Notice that if
M
is compact, the hypothesis
\inf_{x\in M}|B_{1}(x)|>0
is redundant.

Also, if
M
is compact, isoperimetric sets with volume
v
exist for every
v\in(0,|M|)
, and thus (since they have positive perimeter by the local isoperimetric inequality)
I(v)>0
for every
v\in(0,|M|)
. When
M
is non-compact, one can still prove that
\inf_{x\in M}|B_{1}(x)|>0
and
\mathrm{Ric}\geqslant k\cdot g
imply that
I(v)>0
for every
v\in(0,|M|)
: see, e.g.,
[
12
]
.

Remark 4

(Regularity of
I_{M}
)
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

5
one can directly prove
2
2

2

The classical argument is due to Gallot
[
56
]
, see also Munoz Flores–Nardulli
[
75
]
.
that
I_{M}
is locally
\frac{n-1}{n}
-Hölder continuous on
(0,|M|)
, thus continuous.

Inequality (
3
) means that for every
v\in(0,|M|)
and every smooth real-valued function
\varphi
such that
\varphi(v)=I_{M}(v)
and
\varphi(x)\leqslant I_{M}(x)
in a neighborhood of
v
, we have

-\varphi^{\prime\prime}(v)\varphi(v)\geqslant k+\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(v)^{2}}{n-1}.

The inequality (
3
) holds also in the appropriate distributional sense, after rewriting it as a differential inequality on
I_{M}^{\frac{n}{n-1}}
. Classical analysis (see
5
) tells us that (
3
), joined with the fact that
I_{M}
is continuous, implies that it is locally semi-concave. Hence

\mathrm{Ch}(f):=\inf\bigg\{\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}\int|\nabla f_{n}|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}\;\bigg|\;(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq{\rm LIP}_{\mathrm{bs}}(X),\,f_{n}\to f\text{ in }L^{2}(X)\bigg\}\,.

.

(2)

For an open set

4.3.
Isoperimetric inequality on non-negatively curved manifolds with Euclidean volume growth

On a non-compact manifold
(M^{n},g)
with
\mathrm{Ric}\geqslant 0
one can define, for
p\in M
,

\mathrm{AVR}(M):=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{|B_{r}(p)|}{\omega_{n}r^{n}}.

The definition does not depend on
p
and it is well-posed by the Bishop–Gromov monotonicity formula (
21
). Notice that, by Bishop–Gromov monotonicity,
\mathrm{AVR}>0
implies
\inf_{x\in M}|B_{1}(x)|\geqslant\omega_{n}\mathrm{AVR}>0
.

Manifolds with
\mathrm{Ric}\geqslant 0
and
\mathrm{AVR}>0
are asymptotically conical in the GH sense, see
[
45
]
. More precisely, given
r_{i}\to\infty
, and
p\in M
, the rescaled metric space
(M,r_{i}^{-1}\mathrm{d},p)
pGH convergence, up to subsequences, to a space
(C(X),\mathrm{d}_{\infty},o)
that is a
metric cone
on a metric space with
\mathrm{diam}(X)\leqslant\pi
. Any such a space is called
asymptotic cone
of
M
.

Recall that, given a metric space
(X,\mathrm{d})
with
\mathrm{diam}(X)\leqslant\pi
, the
metric cone over
X
, denoted by
C(X)
, is the the space
[0,+\infty)\times X
, where
\{0\}\times X
is collapsed to a point, endowed with the distance

\mathrm{d}_{C}((a,x_{1}),(b,x_{2})):=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}-2ab\cos(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},x_{2}))}.

The asymptotic cone might depend on the specific sequence
r_{i}\to\infty
when
\mathrm{Ric}\geqslant 0
, see Perelman
[
78
]
, or Colding–Naber
[
51
]
. However, if
\mathrm{Sect}\geqslant 0
, the cone is independent on the sequence, see Ballman–Gromov–Schroeder
[
22
]
.

By Bishop–Gromov monotonicity formula
\mathrm{Ric}\geqslant 0
implies
|B_{r}(p)|\leqslant\omega_{n}r^{n}
for every
p\in M
and
r>0
. Hence, manifolds with
\mathrm{Ric}\geqslant 0
, and
\mathrm{AVR}>0
have maximal volume growth at infinity. On the other side of the spectrum, since
\mathrm{Ric}\geqslant 0
always implies
|B_{r}(p)|\geqslant C(p)r
(by a result of Calabi–Yau) for every
r>1
, we have manifolds such that
|B_{r}(p)|\sim r
as
r\to\infty
. Those manifolds are, in a sense that can be made precise,
asymptotically cylindrical
. In this note we will not give more details on the structure of the latter class of spaces, on which it is also interesting to study the isoperimetric problem. Such a study has been carried over in A–Po
[
19
]
, and Zhu
[
92
]
.

Now, we are ready to state a sharp isoperimetric inequality on manifolds with
\mathrm{Ric}\geqslant 0
and
\mathrm{AVR}>0
. We postpone the proof to next lecture.

(Agostiniani–Fogagnolo–Mazzieri
[

, Brendle
[

]
, Fogagnolo–Mazzieri

Let
(M^{n},g)

We give a sketch and a picture. Let us describe the idea of the construction. The main idea is to first obtain the same non-existence result for the (relative) isoperimetric problem in unbounded convex bodies.
