Transition metal oxide nanoparticle–graphene oxide composites as potential sensors for commercially formulated pesticides
Authors: Zia Ul Hassan, Shehryar Ahmad, Zameer Abbas, Khush Bakht, Wajid Rehman, Abdullah Y. A. Alzahrani, Ahson Jabbar Shaikh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The binding interactions of organic molecules with carbon-based materials or nanoparticles have already been studied; however, understanding the preferred binding affinity towards any of these components is difficult, involving time-consuming and expensive procedures. In this work, we investigated a new and simple method to identify the preferred interactions of commercial formulations of three pesticides, i.e., λ-cyhalothrin, imidacloprid, and metsulfuron-methyl, with transition metal oxide nanoparticles (t-MONPs, Fe3O4, CoO, NiO, CuO, and ZnO) or graphene oxide (GO) by using their composites. Fluorescence spectroscopy was performed to study the sensing potential of these composites through Stern–Volmer plots, and dynamic light scattering/zeta potential measurements were carried out to investigate the preferred binding affinity of the pesticides towards either graphene oxide or transition metal oxide nanoparticles. The linear regression analysis of Stern–Volmer plots revealed that the detection limit is in the micromolar range for these composites, indicating their satisfactory sensing performance. The binding constants, limit of detection, and limit of quantification were calculated for these composites.

Introduction

Although pesticides have potential benefits, they cause toxicity in humans and other species,1 and their numerous simultaneous adverse effects on human health cannot be ignored. The residues of some pesticides can be found in daily foods, which can be metabolized, expelled, or bioaccumulated in the body fat,2 and can be associated with numerous health effects including vision (eye pain, miosis, and blurred vision), ingestion (tenesmus, nausea, and abdominal cramps), and inhalation (cough, chest tightness, and pulmonary oedema) issues1 in humans and animals. Among these pesticides, λ-cyhalothrin, belonging to a pyrethroid group of insecticides, is used against the insects of cotton crops, household agriculture, and human lice. It has some major toxic effects on human health, including headache, eye irritation, vomiting, paresthesia, and sedative effects.3 Another widely used insecticide, imidacloprid, is used for crop protection against piercing and sucking pests and insect control on cats and dogs. Its toxicity is hazardous to the liver, which increases the serum transaminase and alkaline phosphatase levels, causing health issues.4 Further, metsulfuron-methyl, an important compound of the sulfonylurea family, is used to control nearly 50–60 harmful species for promoting plant growth;5 however, it also causes many chronic and sub-chronic toxicity issues including reproductive risks, cancers, and skin and eye irritation, affecting the health.6 Further, the widespread use of pesticides, their harmful effects, environmental protection, and concern for public health require the development of detection methods that are sensitive, selective, generally applicable, and practical. Graphene-based composite materials have great potential for environmental remediation due to their unique exceptional properties, especially in removing organic pollutants. Studies have shown that GO and rGO can adsorb a wide range of pesticides, including carbendazim, glyphosate, and atrazine, due to the existence of organic bases that provide π–π stacking interactions.7 There is another study on GO-Fe3O4 that shows that this nanocomposite has higher interactions with endrin pesticides due to the existence of the endo-position of the oxygen atom in the molecule.8 The detection of pesticides at very low concentrations, especially in the presence of binders in the commercial formulations, is urgently needed.9 Various methods for the detection of pesticides are based on gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy,10 ion mobility spectroscopy (IMS),11 nanomaterial-based electrochemical biosensors,12 assay strip biosensors,13 surface plasmon resonance (SPR)-based sensors,14 HPLC- and GC-based sensors, etc.15 These sensing methodologies are not only complicated but also very costly, time-consuming, and specific to substrates, with advanced instrumentation requirement for analysis, unreliable sensitivities, and less selectivity. Among the most important types of chemical and biological sensors are fluorescence-based sensors, which come under the category of optical sensors. Other examples of optical sensors are colorimetric sensors, SERS sensors, SPR-based sensors, and chemiluminescent sensors. The sensing methods are classified according to the type of recognition, e.g., enzyme–substrate, antigen–antibody, molecularly imprinted polymers, aptamers and host–guest reactions. These fluorescent sensors are used for cell sorting, DNA sequencing, high-throughput screening, clinical diagnostics, etc.16 Fluorescence-based sensing systems have a significant advantage as they have very high sensitivity and provide sensing capabilities even at a single-molecule level. Other advantages include rapid response time, high signal-to-noise ratio, and wide applicability in the real-world environment.17 There are hundreds of sensing applications using fluorescence spectroscopy. Currently, the level of detection for the quantum dots is in the ppm level.18 Microfluidic-based fluorescent sensor arrays have also been developed, which comparatively sense at the ppb level.19 Many of the pesticides that are detected using this technique are organophosphate-based pesticides.20

The binding interaction of organic molecules with carbon-based nanomaterials,21 polymeric materials,22 or metal oxide nanoparticles,23etc., is a known fact. Such interactions are of vital importance for various applications and functionalities.24 There are numerous reports that show comparative binding to each other, using different techniques.25 The binding affinity could be estimated through various means26 using complex instrumentation,27 which could be time-consuming28 and even costly.29 Interestingly, when two moieties are needed to be compared simultaneously, separate experiments are needed for each of these, e.g., porphyrins were studied separately to be bound to gold nanoparticles and PbSe quantum dots.30,31 In a similar manner, separate studies will be required for pesticides to obtain preferential binding of pesticides towards either carbon-based moiety or any other nanomaterial, potentially for the detection purposes.32 Further, in most cases, the reported binding of such moieties, e.g., pesticides are reported as a pure component.

Although carbon-based fluorescent materials have been extensively investigated for pesticide detection, most reported studies have focused on individual nanomaterials and analytical-grade pesticide standards. As shown in Table 1, studies comparing multiple GO-transition metal oxide composites using commercial pesticide formulations are still limited. Moreover, only a few reports combine fluorescence measurements with binding affinity, hydrodynamic size and zeta potential analyses to better understand the sensing mechanism. Graphene oxide was selected because its abundant oxygen-containing functional groups provide excellent aqueous dispersibility and facilitate stable composite formation with transition metal oxide nanoparticles. These functional groups also promote interactions with organic molecules through hydrogen bonding, electrostatic and π–π interactions, making GO well suited for investigating preferential binding behavior. Furthermore, our previous study has demonstrated that the selected transition metal oxide nanoparticles exhibit distinct interaction strengths with GO,33 providing the scientific basis for employing these GO-t-MONP composites in the present work.

Various types of materials that can be used as fluorescent sensors include macrocycles, polymers, metallo-organics, and nanomaterials.38 As far as nanomaterials are concerned, these can be fabricated of metals,39 non-metals, or semi-metals, which are vastly used in the sensing field, particularly fluorescence-based detection as they quickly respond to the presence of pesticides through colorimetric, induced fluorescence, or electrochemical changes.38 For the electrochemical detection of pesticides, graphene and transition metal di-chalcogenides, both of which are two-dimensional layers, have attracted significant research interest because graphene's unique electrolytic properties and high surface area noticeably enhance the bio-sensing performance.40 Different types of pesticides, e.g., imidacloprid,41 methyl parathion, and carbofuran, are detected using an AChE biosensor with modified nanocomposites of graphene.42 Similarly, cyano-functional group-containing pesticides are detected through fluorescence resonance energy transfer between gold nanoparticles and up-conversion nanoparticles.43 Carbon-based materials as composites with other nanomaterials have several applications as chemical sensors,44 sensors for the detection of biological moieties,45 functional groups,33 proton exchange membranes,46 photocatalysis,47 drug delivery,48 electrochemical applications,49 and few others.50

Graphene oxide (GO) was chosen because its abundant oxygen-containing functional groups provide excellent dispersibility in water and promote stable interactions with transition metal oxide nanoparticles. These surface functionalities also facilitate hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction and π–π interactions with organic molecules, making GO well suited for investigating preferential binding behavior. The ability of GO-based materials to adsorb a wide range of organic pollutants has been well documented, further supporting their use in fluorescence sensing applications.51,52

In our previous work, we investigated the interactions between graphene oxide and five transition metal oxide nanoparticles (Fe3O4, CoO, NiO, CuO and ZnO) and found that each metal oxide exhibited a distinct binding behaviour towards GO, reflecting differences in their surface chemistry and physicochemical properties. Based on these findings, the same GO-t-MONP composites were selected in the present study to examine how these intrinsic interactions influence the preferential binding and fluorescence response of commercially formulated pesticides. This approach provides a direct link between GO-t-MONP interactions and the sensing performance of the resulting hybrid composites.33

Although fluorescence spectroscopy has been widely applied for pesticide sensing, the interaction of commercial pesticide formulations with GO-transition metal oxide hybrid composites has received comparatively little attention. In this study, fluorescence measurements were interpreted alongside Stern–Volmer analysis, DLS and zeta potential data to obtain a broader understanding of the interaction process. The combined analytical approach makes it possible to relate changes in fluorescence to variations in the physicochemical characteristics of the hybrid composites. Fluorescence spectroscopy is widely used to evaluate the response of sensing materials; however, changes in fluorescence intensity alone provide limited information about the underlying interaction mechanism. To gain a more complete understanding of the sensing process, complementary techniques are required. In the present study, fluorescence measurements were combined with dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential analysis to monitor changes in particle size and surface charge during pesticide binding. The combined use of these techniques makes it possible to relate the fluorescence response to changes in the physicochemical properties of the GO-t-MONP composites, providing additional insights into the preferential interaction of commercial pesticide formulations with the individual components of the hybrid materials.

In this work, a new method based on graphene oxide-t-MONPs composites is developed for the preferred binding and detection of λ-cyhalothrin, imidacloprid, and metsulfuron-methyl pesticides, as three different classes of pesticides, present in the commercial formulation. Five graphene oxide composites of transition metal oxides (Fe3O4, CoO, NiO, CuO, and ZnO) as five new potential sensors (1, 2, 3, 4, and 5) are used for the investigation of their sensing potential by using fluorescence spectroscopy, UV-vis spectroscopy, dynamic light scattering and zeta potential analysis,53 and the best among them is selected on the basis of LOD and LOQ. The preferential binding interaction mechanism is revealed by DLS and zeta potential studies. The structures of the selected pesticides are provided in Fig. 1. In one example, the detection of organophosphate pesticides is determined using carbon quantum dots and gold nanoparticle composites.34 The method is indirect, complicated and relatively inefficient, as the detection is performed under very controlled conditions. We believe that the proposed method described in this manuscript is simple, efficient, and cost-effective, as compared to the prior literature.

Materials and methods

The chemicals and pesticides used for the synthesis and sensing purpose were obtained commercially and were used as received, without any further purification steps. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), absolute alcohol (ethanol, C2H5OH), methanol (CH3OH), and sodium nitrate (NaNO3) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Copper acetate monohydrate (Cu(CO2CH3)2·H2O), cobalt chloride (COCl3·3H2O), zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 47%), nickel nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O), sodium borohydride (NaBH4), ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O), graphite powder (C), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), potassium permanganate (KMnO4) and hydrochloric acid (HCl) were purchased from Daejung, Korea. Commercial formulation of λ-cyhalothrin (25 g L−1) was purchased from Agri-Force, Pakistan; imidacloprid (250 g kg−1) was purchased from Agri TAS, Pakistan; and metsulfuron-methyl was purchased from MATRIX, China.

Synthesis of graphene oxide and transition metal oxide nanoparticles

Graphene oxide sheets were synthesized via the modified Hummers' method.54 Fe3O4 NPs were synthesized by a wet chemical reduction technique;55 thermal decomposition method was used for the preparation of cobalt oxide nanoparticles (CoO NPs).56 The co-precipitation method was followed for the chemical synthesis of nickel oxide nanoparticles (NiO NPs).57 The copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO NPs) were prepared by the co-precipitation method;58 and finally, zinc oxide (ZnO NPs) nanoparticles were synthesized by a sol–gel method.59 All the synthesized nanoparticles and graphene oxide were characterized and confirmed by UV-vis spectroscopy, FT-IR spectroscopy, SEM analysis, X-ray diffraction, dynamic light scattering, and zeta potential analysis, which are provided in the SI of this article. The synthetic protocols in detail are also provided in the SI of this article.59,60 All the synthesized nanoparticles and graphene oxide were characterized and confirmed by UV-vis spectroscopy, FT-IR spectroscopy, SEM analysis, X-ray diffraction, dynamic light scattering, and zeta potential analysis, which are provided in the SI of this article. The synthetic protocols in detail are also provided in the SI of this article.

Preparation of graphene oxide-based composites as potential sensors

First, a 250 ppm solution of graphene oxide (GO) in water and another solution of each of the transition metal oxide nanoparticles (t-MONPs) (2 mg in 20 mL of water) were prepared. All these solutions were sonicated for 60 minutes to ensure the homogenous dispersion of GO and t-MONPs. All GO-t-MONP suspensions were freshly prepared prior to each experiment and sonicated under identical conditions to minimize aggregation and obtain homogeneous dispersions. To ensure a meaningful comparison, the GO concentration, nanoparticle loading, solvent, sonication time and temperature were kept identical for all the five composites.

The interaction is purely between GO and NPs, as all the materials were synthesized through methods, where no capping agents were surrounded by the NPs. Both materials have a common solvent such as water, which enables them to interact with each other easily. GO has many bonding sites including oxygen-rich edges of carboxylic acid functional groups, where metal oxide NPs can potentially bind.61 For composite 1 (GO-Fe3O4), mixed iron oxide NPs with GO; for composite 2 (GO-CoO), mixed cobalt oxide NPs with GO; for composite 3 (GO-NiO), mixed nickel oxide NPs with GO; for composite 4 (GO-CuO), mixed copper oxide NPs with GO; and finally, for composite 5 (GO-ZnO), graphene oxide mixed with zinc oxide were prepared. All the solutions were sonicated for up to 90 minutes to provide a suitable environment for the best interaction. The bond interaction, stability, and sensing ability were analyzed by UV-vis spectroscopy, fluorescence emission spectroscopy, hydrodynamic radius, and zeta potential. A table for the formulation of titration experiments of composites with pesticides for UV-vis spectroscopy, fluorescence spectroscopy, hydrodynamic size and zeta potential studies is provided in the SI of this article. The composites have also been previously synthesized and characterized in detail in our earlier published results.33

Characterization

The synthesized transition metal oxide nanoparticles, i.e., Fe3O4, CoO, NiO, CuO, and ZnO, and graphene oxide were characterized through UV-vis spectroscopy, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction pattern, SEM analysis, dynamic light scattering, and zeta potential analysis, which are provided in the SI of this article.

A UV-visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1700) was used for the UV-vis analysis. The wavelength range selected was 200–800 nm with a fixed slit width of 1 mm. The sampling interval was 0.5 nm. Quartz cuvettes were used with a fixed path length of 10 mm. Fig. 2 and SI 7 (SI) show the UV-vis analysis of composites 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 with pesticides (λ-cyhalothrin, imidacloprid, and metsulfuron-methyl). UV-vis absorption titration experiments were conducted with the increasing concentration of each pesticide (stock solutions: λ-cyhalothrin (2 mM), imidacloprid (0.5 mM), and metsulfuron-methyl (0.9 mM)) in water with increasing volumes (0.3 mL, 0.6 mL, and 1.2 mL) for each transition metal oxide–GO hybrid to understand the composite's ground-state binding ability towards these pesticides. It is important to mention here that the concentrations of pesticides mentioned here are based on the active component concentration.

UV-vis absorption spectra of composite 1 (Fe3O4-GO composite) with the pesticides: (a) λ-cyhalothrin, (b) imidacloprid, and (c) metsulfuron-methyl.

The fluorescence emission spectroscopy measurements were made using a Horiba Fluoromax-4 spectrophotometer at room temperature. A quartz cuvette (1.3 mL) transparent from all sides with dimensions of 10 mm × 10 mm was used. The slit width was fixed at 2 mm with a bandpass of 8.5 nm and the excitation wavelength was kept fixed at 265 nm for λ-cyhalothrin, whereas the excitation wavelength was kept fixed for imidacloprid and at 305 nm for metsulfuron-methyl.

The dynamic light scattering (DLS for size) and zeta potential (for stability) measurements were analyzed at room temperature (25 °C) using a Zetasizer Nano ZSP from Malvern Instruments, UK; using the Malvern Zeta-sizer software (v7.13). All the solutions including individual t-MONPs, GO, composites, and titration experiments of pesticides were prepared in distilled water and sonicated for 5–10 minutes before analysis. Measurements were taken in triplicate. The size was measured using 173° non-invasive backscatter mode and M3 phase light scattering mode analyses. The accuracy of the instrument was within ±2% on NIST traceable latex standards and was equipped with a 10 mW 633 nm He–Ne laser. Stability studies were also carried out using the same instrument at the same temperature and same concentration as those used for DLS analysis. The samples were filled in disposable folded capillary cells (DTS1070) by using a disposable 5 mL syringe, to avoid the formation of bubbles inside the cell.

X-ray diffraction studies were carried out using a Bruker D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation at a wavelength (λ) of 1.5406 Å. The instrument scan range was set at 2θ = 5°–80° with the increment in the step size of 0.025°. The generator settings were fixed at 30 mA and 40 kV. The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy measurements to observe the functional groups were carried out using a Shimadzu FTIR-8400S Spectrometer. Its resolution was 0.85 cm−1 with S/N 20 000 : 1; the ATR mode was used to record the FT-IR spectra in the mid-region of 400–4000 cm−1. The surface morphology and size of GO and t-MONPs were observed using a TESCAN MIRA 3 SEM. Its resolution power is good up to 10 nm.

Development of the detection mechanism through UV-vis absorption spectroscopy

The above-discussed techniques confirmed the formation of t-MONPs and GO. Further UV-vis absorption spectra, fluorescence emission spectra, change in size and zeta potential were studied to understand the interacting behavior of metal oxide composites with graphene oxide towards the commercial formulation of three different classes of pesticides, namely, λ-cyhalothrin, imidacloprid and metsulfuron-methyl. For the UV-vis absorbance spectroscopy, we observed the absorbance spectra of GO, transition metal oxide nanoparticles, t-MONP-GO composites, pesticides, and the composites with increasing concentrations of pesticides in water. Due to the relatively high concentration of pesticides along with their additive materials, the absorbance peak was higher than the detection limit of the instrument, except for metsulfuron-methyl. However, the lowest concentration of the pesticides used for our titration studies was observable within the detection limits. The detailed UV-vis titration experiments for all these combinations of pesticides and composites of Fe3O4, CoO, NiO, CuO and ZnO are provided in Fig. SI 7 in the SI of this article. For clarity purposes, we provide below an example of Fe3O4-GO composite with these three pesticides, as shown in Fig. 2, which excludes the spectra of GO only, t-MONPs only, and composite-pesticide spectra with higher concentrations of pesticides. The experimental details are provided in the materials and methods section of this manuscript, and additional details are provided in the SI of this article. Increasing the concentration of pesticides increases the overall absorption. This hyper-chromic shift is possibly due to the auxochrome groups present in the pesticides. For the Fe3O4 composites with metsulfuron-methyl as shown in Fig. 2c, the absorbance of the pesticides decreases when titrated with these composites. The base line of the pesticides only is generally at the lowest absorbance as compared to the pesticides along with composites, which is a good indication that the interaction of pesticides is occurring, either with the nanoparticles or with the GO of these composites, but overall, we did not have any clear indication of the processes occurring with these UV-vis titration studies.

The composite used for the detection of pesticides shown in Fig. 2 is Fe3O4-GO composite. After the addition of λ-cyhalothrin in composite, the intense absorption is noted as shown in Fig. 2a. All the UV-vis spectroscopy results show a change in the complex structure, which revealed the binding of composites with pesticides.

Development of the detection mechanism through fluorescence emission spectroscopy and zeta-sizer analysis

Unlike fluorescence measurements, which primarily reflect changes in emission intensity, the accompanying DLS and zeta potential analyses provide complementary evidence of particle-size and surface-charge variations during pesticide interaction, allowing the fluorescence response to be interpreted in terms of the underlying binding mechanism. The sensing potential of all these composites of graphene oxide and metal oxide nanoparticles, towards selected pesticides, was investigated using fluorescence spectroscopy. Stern–Volmer plots were obtained with non-linear and linear fits, from which estimated binding constants were obtained.30,31 Similarly, a zeta-sizer was used to investigate the binding mechanisms of these pesticides towards these composites. A zeta-sizer is an important tool for studying the stability of nanocomposites and charge on the surface of the medium.76 It also gives information about the interaction between two moieties, and the effect on the hydrodynamic diameter and the stability of suspension can be observed.77 The interaction of all these five composites (1, 2, 3, 4, and 5) with λ-cyhalothrin, imidacloprid, and metsulfuron-methyl was analyzed by studying changes in the hydrodynamic size through dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements and zeta potential measurements. The DLS study of these composites with pesticides discloses interesting and varying results with each pesticide, which enables us to observe the preferential interaction of pesticides towards graphene oxide or nanoparticles present in these composites, based on their molecular structure. These results show either observation of free nanoparticles or change in stability after titration experiments. Generally, the change in size represents the binding of pesticides with the composite; however, a decrease in average hydrodynamic diameter shows that pesticides attach to the graphene oxide part of composite (e.g., GO-Fe3O4). Graphene oxide is a 2D macromolecule that contains very reactive surface due to the presence of multiple functionalities and provides many sites to preferably react with the selected functional groups of these pesticides, essentially releasing the nanoparticles already attached to it. This results in an increase in the concentration of the nanoparticles in the solution as compared to the new composite (GO-pesticide), and the instrument detects the presence of free nanoparticles based on their intensity (concentration), hence lowering of the size is observed. The strong interaction of pesticides with the oxygen-rich edge of GO makes a relatively stable suspension, which is also observed and discussed later in this section. In other cases, the interaction of pesticides with nanoparticles specifically releases GO in the medium. The large size of the GO results in a gradual increase in the average diameter of suspension, as observed for other cases, and a potential decrease in the stability of the solution as larger aggregates tend to coagulate in the suspension. A higher size in the medium is directly associated with the lower stability of the solution. This mechanism is also observed for few cases discussed later accordingly.

Composite 1 (GO-Fe3O4)

The sensing potential of composite 1 (GO-Fe3O4 composite) towards these pesticides was investigated using fluorescence spectroscopy, as shown in Fig. 3a–f. The titration experiments for composite 1 were carried out with the increasing concentration of these pesticides in water (λ-cyhalothrin (2 mM), imidacloprid (0.5 mM), and metsulfuron-methyl (0.9 mM)). The excitation wavelength was fixed at 265 nm for λ-cyhalothrin and 305 nm for imidacloprid and metsulfuron-methyl. As shown in Fig. 3a, the gradual addition of the pesticide λ-cyhalothrin from 0 mL to 1.8 mL causes the emission intensity of composite 1 to significantly quench; however, the addition of imidacloprid and metsulfuron-methyl causes the emission intensity to be significantly enhanced (Fig. 3b and c). Due to the quenching mechanism, we observe an upward corresponding Stern–Volmer plot for λ-cyhalothrin (Fig. 3d), whereas the enhancement in the fluorescence causes a downward curvature for imidacloprid and metsulfuron-methyl (Fig. 3e and f). The significant fluorescence quenching of composite 1 with λ-cyhalothrin is due to the interactive binding of λ-cyhalothrin with graphene oxide,78 which is also validated by the dynamic light scattering titration studies, discussed later in this section. At the same time, the nanoparticles are released into the solution, as the sites occupied by nanoparticles have been replaced by pesticide. The enhancement of the fluorescence intensity of composite 1 when titrated with imidacloprid is because imidacloprid is fluorescent itself;79 when added to the composites, it increases the overall fluorescence emission. Further, the linear Stern–Volmer plot for this titration experiment also describes that its binding with the hybrid is lower than that of λ-cyhalothrin, but higher than that of metsulfuron-methyl. Similarly, when metsulfuron-methyl is titrated with composite 1, the fluorescence is enhanced. As we know, metsulfuron-methyl is not fluorescent, therefore the only explanation that is applicable here is that it binds with the nanoparticles and releases graphene oxide, which in turn enhances the fluorescence and a downward curve is obtained for the corresponding Stern–Volmer plot. While comparing the Stern–Volmer plots, we observe that the binding is lowest in the case of metsulfuron-methyl and highest with λ-cyhalothrin. The preferable binding of λ-cyhalothrin towards graphene oxide lies towards its structure with extended phenyl groups, which have preferable interaction towards the ring structures of graphene oxide, whereas the imidacloprid with its hetero structure and the presence of a nitro group does not have preferable interaction towards graphene oxide or the metal oxide nanoparticles, hence its binding is lower than that of λ-cyhalothrin. However, the structure of metsulfuron-methyl has an urea moiety present in the structure, which makes it more interactive towards the Fe3O4 nanoparticles.80 The Stern–Volmer plots for λ-cyhalothrin and metsulfuron-methyl were nearly linear, and little curve was observed for imidacloprid. A very good R2 value of composites with all three pesticides is obtained, which is 0.9917, 0.9887, and 0.9936, respectively. The linear quenching behavior of the Stern–Volmer plot shows selective static or dynamic quenching. In case of curved plots, a combination of these two mechanisms occurs. The complex formation of the donor with the acceptor moieties at ground state is referred to as static quenching,81–83 whereas the transfer of electron and energy of the composite with the interacting analyte in the excited state is referred to as dynamic quenching.84

Corresponding Stern–Volmer plots shown in figures (d–f). Corresponding size (g–i) and zeta potential (j–l) analyses of composite 1 with the pesticides.

The sensitivity of composite 1 with all three pesticides was also evaluated by calculating their limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ). The LOD was calculated by using 3.3 × standard deviation of intercept/slope, and for the LOQ, 10 × standard deviation of intercept/slope was used; with the help of linear regression analysis. The corresponding LOD of composite 1 for λ-cyhalothrin, imidacloprid, and metsulfuron-methyl was calculated to be 77.5 µM, 40 µM, and 28.98 µM, respectively. Similarly, the LOQ obtained for these three pesticides was 255.8 µM, 120 µM, and 86.9 µM, respectively.80

A different trend was observed for the Fe3O4-GO composite, which exhibited the lowest detection limit for metsulfuron-methyl instead of imidacloprid.85 This behavior may be related to the higher affinity of Fe3O4-based materials for sulfonylurea herbicides, consistent with previous reports describing the efficient extraction and preconcentration of these compounds using Fe3O4-supported magnetic sorbents.

The analysis of the hydrodynamic diameter of composite 1 with three pesticides is presented in Fig. 3g–i. The composite 1 revealed an average diameter of ∼400 nm, and the addition of λ-cyhalothrin causes the overall size to be decreased, as shown in Fig. 3g. This is due to the interaction of pesticides with graphene oxide, and consequently, the nanoparticles are free, which due to their higher concentration shows lower size in DLS studies. Similarly, the zeta potential of these studies, as shown in Fig. 3j–l, also tend to confirm our hypothesis that binding is biased towards graphene oxide as the zeta potential of composites was decreased when composites were formed; upon addition of pesticides, the average zeta potential is regained to become similar to that of free nanoparticles. However, further addition of pesticides causes a decrease in the zeta potential, as more GO-pesticide composites are being formed.86 The zeta potential of composite 1 with λ-cyhalothrin shows a gradual rise in the stability of the suspension with a gradual increase in the concentration of pesticides. Similar size and zeta potential analyses were performed with imidacloprid, as shown in Fig. 3h and k, respectively, which shows a gradual increase in the hydrodynamic diameter of suspension, which means that the interaction between 1 and imidacloprid is weak compared to λ-cyhalothrin, as no significant changes are observed. The zeta potential also agrees with this result because the stability gradually decreases, as shown in Fig. 3k. The selectivity of composite 1 was also analyzed for metsulfuron-methyl, which confirmed the increase in size upon addition of metsulfuron-methyl, and upon further increase, the size slightly decreases representing minor attraction towards the composite. The stability also decreased with the increasing concentration of pesticides. These results reveal that composite 1 is more selective toward λ-cyhalothrin and less selective toward imidacloprid and metsulfuron-methyl.

The sensing potential of composite 2 (GO-CoO composite) towards these pesticides was also investigated using fluorescence spectroscopy. The titration experiments for composite 2 were conducted with the increasing concentration of these pesticides in water (λ-cyhalothrin (2 mM), imidacloprid (0.5 mM), and metsulfuron-methyl (0.9 mM)). The excitation wavelengths were fixed as discussed in the previous example. As shown in Fig. 4a, the gradual addition of the pesticide λ-cyhalothrin from 0 mL to 1.8 mL causes the emission intensity of composite 2 to significantly quench; however, the addition of imidacloprid and metsulfuron-methyl causes the emission intensity to be enhanced, where a significant effect is observed for imidacloprid (Fig. 4b and c). Due to the quenching mechanism, we observe an upward corresponding Stern–Volmer plot for λ-cyhalothrin (Fig. 4d), whereas the enhancement in the fluorescence causes a downward curvature for imidacloprid and metsulfuron-methyl (Fig. 4e and f). The Stern–Volmer plots for λ-cyhalothrin and metsulfuron-methyl were nearly linear, and a little curve was observed for imidacloprid. A very good to reasonable R2 value of composites with these three pesticides is obtained, which is 0.9829, 0.9658, and 0.927, respectively.

The zeta potential in this case also confirms that the instability increases with the increasing concentration of pesticides, possibly because metsulfuron-methyl has interaction with both the moieties or the composite itself. Similar fluorescence quenching has been reported for pyrethroid pesticides using carbon-based fluorescent nanosensors, achieving detection limits as low as 0.87 nM.87 In comparison, the GO-t-MONP composites developed in the present study achieved a detection limit of 77.5 µM for λ-cyhalothrin. Although the sensitivity is lower than that of highly engineered fluorescent nanosensors, the present work employs a simple, label-free platform and evaluates commercially formulated pesticides, providing a more realistic assessment of practical sensing performance.

The five composites (1, 2, 3, 4, and 5) were used for the sensing of three pesticides λ-cyhalothrin, imidacloprid, and metsulfuron-methyl. The analysis results show that composite 1 (Fe3O4-GO) has the overall best results followed by composite 2 (CoO-GO), composite 4 (CuO-GO), composite 3 (NiO-GO), and composite 5 (ZnO-GO), respectively, however it more depends on the pesticide used. For example, considering pesticides, imidacloprid was best sensed with all five composites, followed by metsulfuron-methyl and λ-cyhalothrin, respectively. The highest LOD was obtained for λ-cyhalothrin. Overall, the best sensing was achieved for metsulfuron-methyl using composite 1 with the detection limit of 86.9 µM.

Conclusions

In this work, five graphene oxide–transition metal oxide nanoparticle (GO-t-MONP) composites (Fe3O4, CoO, NiO, CuO and ZnO) were investigated as fluorescence sensing platforms for three commercially formulated pesticides: λ-cyhalothrin, imidacloprid and metsulfuron-methyl. Among the composites studied, Fe3O4-GO exhibited the best overall performance, while λ-cyhalothrin consistently produced the strongest fluorescence quenching together with the highest Stern–Volmer and binding constants, indicating stronger interactions with the composite surface. The fluorescence, DLS and zeta potential results collectively showed that GO-t-MONP composites can effectively differentiate the preferential interactions of commercial pesticide formulations with the graphene oxide and transition metal oxide components.

Although the proposed platform provides detection limits in the micromolar range, it offers a simple, label-free approach for studying the preferential binding behavior of commercial pesticide formulations under conditions that better reflect practical agricultural applications. Further improvements in material design and surface functionalization, together with validation using real environmental and agricultural samples, will help enhance its analytical performance and broaden its practical applicability.
