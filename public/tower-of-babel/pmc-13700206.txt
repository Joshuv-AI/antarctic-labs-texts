The shared burden of frailty in rheumatoid arthritis – a qualitative study of informal caregiving roles and support needs from the perspectives of patients and their informal caregivers
Authors: Signe Marie Abild, Julie Midtgaard, Charlotte Werdal Hansen, Luise Holberg Lindgren, Katrine Storm Piper, Bente Glintborg, Tanja Thomsen, Mathilde Glud Christensen, Bente Appel Esbensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty is increasingly recognized among older patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and is associated with functional decline and growing dependence on others. As frailty progresses, significant others often assume extensive practical, emotional, and coordinative responsibilities, becoming informal caregivers. Given that RA is commonly managed across healthcare sectors, these responsibilities may also involve navigating and coordinating care across settings. Yet, their experiences and support needs remain underexplored in rheumatology care. This study aimed to explore how frailty in older patients with RA affects their informal caregivers, including the support they provide and their perceived needs, as understood by both older patients and informal caregivers themselves.MethodIndividual, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 17 older patients with RA and frailty (11 women, mean age 74) and thirteen informal caregivers (5 women, mean age 69). Older adults were recruited from four rheumatology outpatient clinics affiliated with Copenhagen University Hospital, Denmark. Data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsThree interrelated themes were developed: (1) Gradual consolidation of caregiving in everyday life, (2) Emotional landscape of caregiving, and (3) Persistently navigating the healthcare system. Caregiving was identified as a progressively expanding role that reshaped daily routines, introduced emotional strain, and required navigation of fragmented healthcare services. Both patients and informal caregivers highlighted a need for clearer information about RA symptoms and disease trajectories, as well as care pathways and available support services, alongside improved coordination in the healthcare system and greater recognition of the roles of informal caregivers.ConclusionThe findings suggest that frailty in RA affects not only patients but also those closest to them. Recognizing informal caregivers as key partners in care and strengthening communication between health professionals across healthcare sectors may help mitigate caregiver burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41927-026-00675-3.

Background

In 2020, an estimated 17 million people worldwide were living with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [1–3]. RA is a chronic inflammatory disease, manifesting symptoms such as pain, morning stiffness, impaired physical functioning, and fatigue [4, 5]. Notably, people with RA encounter fluctuating disease activity, which can impede the management of daily activities [4]. Moreover, people with RA exhibit heightened rates of anxiety, depression, and comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes compared with the general population [4, 6]. Despite advances in pharmacological treatment, living with RA continues to exert physical, mental, and social impacts on quality of life [6]. Management of RA often involves care across healthcare sectors, requiring coordination between hospital-based and primary care services [7].

More recently, the risk of developing frailty among people with RA has become an increasing research focus [5]. Frailty refers to a state of increased vulnerability and diminished physiological reserve, leading to a reduced capacity to cope with health-related stressors [8]. It is associated with reduced strength and endurance, increased risk of falls, and fatigue, which can impair daily functioning and increase reliance on others, affecting roles and relationships. Frailty is also linked to depression, further influencing coping and independence [8]. Although evidence on the prevalence of frailty in people with RA remains limited, a 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis reported a pooled frailty prevalence of 33.5% among adults with RA. Although the review was not restricted to older adults, several included studies focused on populations aged ≥ 55 or ≥ 65 years, and the mean age across studies was 63 years [9]. Current literature consistently underscores an elevated risk of frailty among people with RA, attributed to chronic systemic inflammation promoting multisystem dysregulation and to potential adverse effects of treatment [5, 10, 11]. In a recent qualitative interview study of 17 patients aged ≥ 65 years with RA and classified as frail, frailty was described as a gradual and multifaceted process shaped by long-term RA symptoms, ageing, and comorbidities, affecting everyday life, identity, and autonomy [12].

Importantly, the impact of frailty often involves support from informal caregivers in the management of RA and related health challenges [5, 9, 11]. Over recent decades, structural changes in healthcare systems, including a shift from hospital admission to ambulatory monitoring, changes in treatment strategies, and reduced institutionalized care, have increased the emphasis on patient self-management [13–15]. However, these expectations may be particularly challenging for older people with RA who are frail and have reduced physiological and psychological reserve. In such situations, significant others, defined as individuals who are important to a person’s wellbeing, often become actively involved in supporting disease management and everyday functioning, thereby taking on the role of informal caregivers [12, 16].

A substantial body of research has shown that providing care for older patients with chronic illness may have negative consequences for informal caregivers, including increased psychological distress, physical health problems, and even elevated mortality risk [17–20]. However, most existing research has focused on caregiving in the context of ageing and chronic illness more broadly, with limited attention to the specific challenges associated with inflammatory diseases such as RA. This gap may be particularly important in the context of frailty in RA, where declining physical and functional capacity may increase the reliance on informal caregivers in everyday life and disease management. Despite this, little is known about how informal caregivers are affected when supporting older patients with RA and frailty, including the type of support they provide and the needs that may arise in this context. This study constitutes a sub-study within the INSPIRE study, a broader research program aimed at developing an intervention to reduce caregiver burden among informal caregivers of older people with rheumatoid arthritis.

This study therefore aimed to explore how frailty in older patients with RA affects their informal caregivers, including the support they provide and their perceived needs, as understood by both older patients and informal caregivers themselves.

Method

Study design and epistemological stance

The study was designed as an explorative qualitative interview study informed by a social constructivist epistemological perspective [21]. Data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis described by Braun and Clarke [22, 23]. The analysis was informed by a relativist and constructionist position, acknowledging that participants' experiences and researchers’ interpretation are inevitably shaped by social, cultural and linguistic context.

Sampling

Sampling was undertaken within the larger INSPIRE study, which included older people with RA and frailty and also comprised a parallel qualitative study reported elsewhere [12]. For the present study, older patients were sampled using a purposive maximum variation strategy to achieve an information-rich sample with variation in sex, age, and frailty severity [24]. Informal caregivers were sampled through participating patients with RA using a snowball sampling approach, as patients were considered best positioned to identify individuals closely involved in their everyday lives and disease management. Together, these sampling strategies supported the inclusion of participants with relevant and diverse experiences [24, 25].

Recruitment of patients with RA

Participants were recruited from four rheumatology outpatient clinics across geographically diverse sites affiliated with the Department of Rheumatology and Spine Diseases at Copenhagen University Hospital, Denmark. Patients with RA attending routine outpatient visits were invited to participate. Eligible participants were older patients (≥ 65 years) with a confirmed RA diagnosis and indications of frailty.

Screening and recruitment were facilitated through the Danish nationwide clinical rheumatology registry DANBIO, where patients routinely complete patient-reported outcome measures during outpatient visits [26]. Potential participants were identified using predefined Multi-Dimensional Health Assessment Questionnaire (MD-HAQ) criteria previously applied to indicate frailty in people with RA, as described in detail in our previous study [12]. Between May and June 2024, patients aged ≥ 65 years meeting these criteria received a pop-up invitation on the DANBIO screen while completing the questionnaire, asking whether they were interested in receiving further information about the study.

Patients who indicated interest were subsequently contacted by phone and informed verbally about the study. See Fig. 1 for recruitment flow. After obtaining oral consent, screening for frailty was conducted using the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) to determine eligibility [27, 28]. CFS evaluates frailty based on factors such as functional ability, resulting in a score from 1 (very fit) to 9 (terminally ill) [28]. A CFS range of 4 (very mild frailty) to 7 (severe frailty) was chosen to capture a broad spectrum of frailty. Inclusion criteria therefore comprised a CFS score between 4 and 7, age ≥ 65 years, being helped by an informal caregiver with any form of everyday tasks, and a confirmed diagnosis of RA. All included participants were deemed to have adequate cognitive capacity to give informed consent and engage in an interview, assessed during the initial telephone contact.

To further characterize the participants, additional clinical data were obtained from the DANBIO registry. These data included patient-reported visual analogue scale (VAS) scores for pain and fatigue ranging from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity [29]. Disease activity was assessed using the Disease Activity Score for Rheumatoid Arthritis based on 28 joints and C-reactive protein (DAS28-CRP), a validated composite measure of RA disease activity [30]. DAS28-CRP scores range from 0 to 9.4, with scores below 2.6 indicating remission, 2.6–3.2 low disease activity, > 3.2–5.1 moderate disease activity, and > 5.1 high disease activity. Participant characteristics and clinical measures are presented in Table 2.

Recruitment of informal caregivers

During initial contact, older patients were asked whether they had an informal caregiver, who regularly supported them (e.g., family member, neighbor, or friend), and if so, whether this person could be invited to participate in a separate interview. Study information was emailed to the older patient, who confirmed being able to forward the email to their informal caregiver. If the informal caregiver agreed to participate, contact details were exchanged and oral consent was obtained by the research team. In the remainder of the article, ‘older patients’ are referred to as ‘patients’ and ‘informal caregivers’ as ‘caregivers’.

Interviews

Data collection consisted of individual semi-structured interviews with patients and individual semi-structured interviews with caregivers, independent of each other. As part of the INSPIRE study, interviews with patients contributed to two study aims, including a parallel qualitative study reported elsewhere [12]. Questions related to the different aims were addressed in separate parts of the interview, and data relevant to each study aim were extracted and analyzed separately. All interviews were conducted by the first author (SMA) during May and June 2024. All participants could choose to be interviewed either in their own home or at the hospital. When possible, hospital-based interviews were scheduled in connection with existing appointments to ease logistics for participants.

Patients with RA were given the option to have a caregiver present during the interview to provide support. Separate semi-structured interview guides were developed for patients and caregivers, respectively. The interview guides were developed collaboratively by SMA, BAE, and JM and were informed by the study aim and previous research on frailty in other chronic disease populations and caregiver-targeted interventions. They included main questions and follow-up prompts on the impact of RA and frailty on daily life and the role of caregivers (See Supplementary File 1). Following the initial interviews conducted by SMA, SMA and BAE assessed the relevance and comprehensibility of the interview guides and determined that no modifications were required.

To avoid stigmatizing language, the concept of frailty was introduced for participants as “a need for assistance,” in line with the terminology used in the CFS. All interviews were digitally recorded, transcribed using the secure AI-based service Viceron Transcription (Viceron APS), and subsequently reviewed and edited by SMA. In addition, field notes were made during the interviews to capture contextual observations and reflections, which informed the subsequent analysis.

Researcher positionality

SMA is a female physiotherapist with experience in rehabilitation and community-based mental health and had no role in the participants’ treatment or care apart from conducting the interviews. To ensure reflexivity, a preliminary interview with the first author (SMA) was conducted by a co-author (CWH) to explore her preconceptions, experiences, and perspectives related to the topic. An audit trail was kept, documenting methodological decisions and analytic reflections. Reflexivity was actively maintained throughout the study through continuous dialogue and reflection with co-authors with backgrounds in nursing and psychology.

Analysis

A reflexive thematic analysis was conducted by SMA to enable an in-depth, interpretive exploration of patterns within the data using NVivo 15 [31]. The analytic process followed Braun and Clarke’s six-stage approach and was guided by an inductive orientation [32]. Although interviews with patients and caregivers were conducted separately, they were initially coded separately to preserve perspectives specific to each participant group and subsequently analyzed within a shared dataset to explore experiences across both groups. Throughout the analytical process, codes, subthemes, and themes were regularly discussed with BAE and JM to challenge interpretations, enhance reflexivity, and support the refinement of the final themes.

Stage 1 involved familiarization with the data through listening to recordings, reading transcripts, and reviewing interviewer notes. This provided a comprehensive understanding of the material and formed the basis for reflective notetaking, which captured preliminary analytical reflections on recurring topics, contrasts in experiences, and the described role of caregivers. In stage 2, codes were generated inductively through iterative engagement with the data, moving back and forth between transcripts while maintaining focus on the aim of the study (i.e. experiences of support needs and the impact of RA and co-existing frailty). Patient and caregiver interviews were initially coded separately to preserve perspectives specific to each participant group, including differences in roles, experiences, and support needs, before patterns were explored across the dataset. Both semantic and latent content were captured. In stage 3, the codes were organized into meaningful patterns and grouped into subthemes, which were subsequently condensed into overarching themes. Stage 4 involved refining these themes, ensuring internal coherence and consistency with the coded material. Stage 5 focused on defining and naming the final themes, supported by analytically derived subthemes. Stage 6 entailed producing a coherent and concise report, supported by selected participant quotations. Patient and public involvement

In alignment with recommendations from the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR), this study involved two patient research partners (PRPs) [33]: one person living with RA and one caregiver supporting a person with RA in daily activities. The aim of involving PRPs was to incorporate patient and caregiver perspectives into the interpretation and dissemination of the study findings. Reporting of involvement followed the Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public short form (GRIPP2) [34].

The PRPs were not involved in recruitment or data collection and had no access to personal or sensitive participant information. Their involvement focused on providing feedback on the interpretation of results and on drafts of the discussion and conclusion sections of the manuscript. Their feedback contributed to refining the interpretation of findings and ensuring that the results resonated with patient and caregiver perspectives. In particular, the PRPs emphasized that caregiving responsibilities often develop gradually and may remain unrecognized by both patients and caregivers, which strengthened the interpretation of caregiving as an evolving and often implicit role. PRP involvement was therefore primarily related to interpretation and manuscript development and helped strengthen the relevance and clarity of the findings. However, involvement was limited to later stages of the research process to ensure participant confidentiality. The PRPs were offered co-authorship but chose not to take on this role.

Results

Participants

In total, 125 potentially frail patients were identified through DANBIO and invited to express interest in participation. Of these, 58 agreed to be contacted, and 17 were subsequently included based on eligibility criteria and the applied sampling strategy. All individuals who met the inclusion criteria agreed to participate, except for one who stated that they did not perceive themselves to be in the target population of the study. Among the 17 patients, 13 chose to be interviewed in their own homes, while four preferred interviews at the hospital. Only one of the patients participating in the study had a significant other present during the interview. The interviews had a mean duration of 40 min (range: 20–100 min).

A total of 20 caregivers were invited to participate, of whom 13 agreed. The main reason for declining was lack of time. Among the 13 who participated, two were interviewed in the home of the patient, although not their own residence; six were interviewed in their own home, which they shared with the patient; two interviews were conducted by telephone to accommodate practical constraints at the participants' request; one interview took place at the caregiver’s workplace; and two were conducted at the hospital in a private, undisturbed room. The interviews lasted an average of 36 min (range: 16–55 min). Available demographic and clinical characteristics of both caregivers and patients are presented in Tables 2 and 3.

The gradual consolidation of caregiving in everyday life

The participants described how, with increasing age, frailty, and RA-related limitations, the need for support from caregivers intensified. Caregiving became embedded in everyday life through a gradual and often unnoticed process, where occasional support developed into a stable pattern of assistance shaping daily routines, responsibilities, and availability for both patients and caregivers.

Caregivers described how isolated acts of assistance, such as carrying groceries, adjusting medication routines, or assisting during symptom flare-ups, expanded into a broader set of responsibilities. This development rarely involved explicit conversations or deliberate decisions; instead, caregiving evolved quietly and became an assumed part of daily life, often perceived as inevitable and without alternative. As tasks accumulated, responsibility increasingly settled with the caregiver and became integrated into the everyday organization of the household.

I handle everything at home now. My husband really can’t do much anymore, so most of the daily tasks fall to me, but I’ve gotten used to it. It’s just the way things are. (Significant other 7)

I handle everything at home now. My husband really can’t do much anymore, so most of the daily tasks fall to me, but I’ve gotten used to it. It’s just the way things are. (Significant other 7)

From the patient’s perspective, this gradual shift could create gaps between emerging needs and what caregivers recognized. Subtle declines in physical function, fatigue, or fluctuating symptoms were not always visible, particularly when caregivers did not live in the same household. Patients therefore described how their limitations could go unnoticed in everyday interactions, where they often sought to maintain independence.

It is difficult with family. They do not always really understand how you feel. They think you are still doing well if you look like you are doing well. (Patient 2)

It is difficult with family. They do not always really understand how you feel. They think you are still doing well if you look like you are doing well. (Patient 2)

While caregiving was often described as a natural part of the relationship, it increasingly shaped how everyday life could be organized. Caregivers described how responsibilities affected their use of time, reduced flexibility, and limited the range of activities they could maintain alongside work, family obligations, and household tasks.

It has become a different life. You can clearly see that it is a sign that we are getting old. And the life we have lived until now cannot continue in the same way. (Caregiver 5)

It has become a different life. You can clearly see that it is a sign that we are getting old. And the life we have lived until now cannot continue in the same way. (Caregiver 5)

These changes reflected not only the patients’ limitations but also the caregivers’ own ageing and health challenges. Several patients expressed concern that the person supporting them was approaching the limits of what they could manage physically.

I think my brother would like to help, but I don’t think he has the energy for it. I suspect he is not very well himself. (Patient 4)

I think my brother would like to help, but I don’t think he has the energy for it. I suspect he is not very well himself. (Patient 4)

As caregiving demands increased, everyday life became organized around the need for ongoing availability rather than planned activities. Tasks were postponed, delegated, or abandoned as routines were continuously adjusted to accommodate the patient’s fluctuating capacities, with caregivers carrying the primary responsibility for these adaptations.

The emotional landscape of caregiving

Alongside the gradual practical and logistical reorganizations, caregiving involved continuous emotional work within the relationship. Patients and caregivers described how feelings, expectations, and communication were actively managed as part of giving and receiving support. This emotional landscape was characterized by attentiveness, restraint, and ongoing interpretation of one another’s needs.

Both patients and caregivers described caregiving as involving a sustained effort to protect one another from emotional strain. However, caregivers often managed their own emotional burden with limited opportunities for support. Their concerns were shaped by uncertainty about symptoms, disease progression, and how the patient was coping. While caregiving was grounded in love and relational commitment rather than obligation, this also contributed to emotional restraint, as caregivers described holding back worries, frustrations, or tiredness to avoid increasing emotional pressure on the patient.

It is not something I have really expressed to my wife, that I have been feeling sad. I tend to keep those feelings to myself, as I do not want to add to the burden she is already carrying. (Caregiver 12)

It is not something I have really expressed to my wife, that I have been feeling sad. I tend to keep those feelings to myself, as I do not want to add to the burden she is already carrying. (Caregiver 12)

Patients recognized this protective stance and frequently mirrored it. Many described holding back symptoms, fears, or requests for help to avoid burdening the caregiver emotionally, including downplaying concerns or refraining from sharing distressing thoughts in everyday interactions. These mutual adjustments reflected attentiveness but also reinforced patterns of silence and self-restraint within the relationship.

It is mostly about not wanting to worry him unnecessarily. But also, about not wanting to be the reason he goes around feeling concerned. (Patient 3)

It is mostly about not wanting to worry him unnecessarily. But also, about not wanting to be the reason he goes around feeling concerned. (Patient 3)

This reciprocal restraint sometimes obscured the communication of needs. Caregivers described how patients’ tendency to downplay or withhold their needs required them to take a more active role in encouraging the patient to articulate practical concerns. While these protective behaviors were well-intentioned, they could delay or complicate the provision of appropriate support.

I often have to push her to say what she needs. She just does not ask for help. I say, ‘Come on, just tell me. I am going to the shops anyway. What should I bring?’ I almost have to drag it out of her. (Caregiver 3)

I often have to push her to say what she needs. She just does not ask for help. I say, ‘Come on, just tell me. I am going to the shops anyway. What should I bring?’ I almost have to drag it out of her. (Caregiver 3)

Caregivers also described how patients’ pain evoked worry and sadness, often accompanied by a sense of inadequacy when they were unable to alleviate the suffering. This emotional response could extend into concerns about future caregiving demands, further reinforcing restraint on both sides.

Limited disease-specific knowledge sometimes intensified these emotional dynamics. Caregivers described uncertainty in interpreting symptoms and worried about overlooking important signs, particularly when patients had multiple health conditions. Patients were often aware of this uncertainty, which made emotional interactions more demanding and highlighted gaps in shared understanding.

Sometimes I feel I do not know enough about the arthritis. My wife just says that it hurts, but I do not understand what hurts or how it hurts. (Caregiver 4)

Sometimes I feel I do not know enough about the arthritis. My wife just says that it hurts, but I do not understand what hurts or how it hurts. (Caregiver 4)

Despite a shared intention to support one another, tensions arose when emotional boundaries, expectations, or capacities did not align. Caregivers described feeling torn between adapting to caregiving demands and maintaining space for their own needs. Although caregiving was framed as an expression of love, several described an underlying sense of constraint when personal priorities had to be set aside and attempts to assert their own needs were often accompanied by guilt.

There are also moments where I have to catch myself becoming irritated with her, thinking ‘come on, you can do it’. I do not say it directly, but it is there. (Caregiver 12)

There are also moments where I have to catch myself becoming irritated with her, thinking ‘come on, you can do it’. I do not say it directly, but it is there. (Caregiver 12)

Patients were keenly aware of this ambivalence and described moderating their requests for help accordingly, not only to avoid emotional burden but also to protect caregivers’ opportunities for rest and independence. However, this reciprocal consideration could intensify tension when needs remained unspoken or unmet.

Caregivers described moments of frustration, particularly when caregiving demands coincided with their own fatigue or emotional limits. These reactions were often followed by self-criticism, as irritation conflicted with their self-image as supportive and caring. Patients, in turn, described difficulties in asking for help, often due to discomfort and guilt, which meant that support was sometimes only requested when situations became unmanageable.

They come and help when I need it, but I am not very good at asking for help. That is probably the biggest problem. (Patient 1)

They come and help when I need it, but I am not very good at asking for help. That is probably the biggest problem. (Patient 1)

These misalignments generated emotional strain on both sides and highlighted caregivers’ need for space to express their own concerns, fatigue, and boundaries related to their time, energy, and caregiving responsibilities within the relationship. Caregivers experienced frustration when needs required immediate action despite their own constraints, while patients struggled to judge when and how to ask for help without adding to the emotional burden.

It frustrates me when everything has to be on my wife’s terms. I have hard days too, and sometimes I need to postpone things just once. (Caregiver 8)

It frustrates me when everything has to be on my wife’s terms. I have hard days too, and sometimes I need to postpone things just once. (Caregiver 8)

Persistently navigating the healthcare system

Experiences with the healthcare and welfare system shaped how caregivers and patients coordinated responsibilities, understood treatment plans and navigated available services. Caregiving extended beyond the home and involved continuous, largely invisible work to interpret information, manage communication and compensate for a fragmented system. This required caregivers to take on responsibility without formal roles, guidance, or clear boundaries, highlighting their need for clearer information and professional support.

Involvement in healthcare encounters varied widely and was shaped by relational boundaries, preferences and emotional dynamics between patients and caregivers. Some caregivers deliberately held back during consultations, viewing discussions regarding RA as belonging primarily to the patient, even when they possessed detailed knowledge of daily challenges. Others were consistently actively engaged in consultations and regarded their involvement as necessary to understand RA and support treatment decisions.

She was a bit unsettled when the medication stopped working. That is why she has to switch to the new one, and we have not started it yet. I say we because we are in this together. (Caregiver 1)

Patients differed in how much involvement they welcomed. For some, joint attendance reduced misunderstandings and relieved them of the burden of relaying information afterwards. Others hesitated to involve caregivers, worrying that questions might feel intrusive or disrupt established relational roles.

My daughter asked if she could come with me to a doctor’s appointment. I told her she shouldn’t. Then she sits there asking a lot of questions, which I find embarrassing. (Patient 5)

Navigating the wider healthcare and welfare system was described as demanding and often unclear. Patients frequently received care across multiple settings, including both primary and secondary care, often for several conditions in addition to RA. In this context, caregivers assumed responsibility for maintaining an overview of appointments, medication, home care, and available services, often with limited professional guidance. This coordinative work required sustained attention to ensure continuity of care. Caregivers described how different specialties tended to focus narrowly on their own areas, leaving patients and caregivers to identify how treatments and conditions interacted. In response, caregivers often acted as informal coordinators, attempting to bridge gaps between services.

It often felt like each department focused on their own part, which meant that I had to ask how his different medications were being considered and whether anyone was coordinating across departments. (Caregiver 7)

This complexity contributed to uncertainty in supporting ageing patients with changing needs, particularly when information was unclear or fragmented. Caregivers described difficulty in interpreting symptoms and determining how best to provide appropriate support, while also feeling responsible for ensuring that nothing was overlooked. Patients were aware of this pressure. In contrast, clear communication and timely guidance provided relief and enabled both parties to focus on managing everyday life with RA. These experiences highlighted caregivers’ needs for clearer knowledge about RA symptoms and disease trajectories, guidance in navigating complex systems, and support in managing caregiving responsibilities.

Discussion

Taken together, the findings of this study illustrate that caregiving for older adults living with RA and frailty involves interconnected practical, emotional, and organizational demands that develop gradually over time. Caregiving was experienced as a natural extension of existing relationships rather than as a distinct role, which may explain why increasing responsibilities often remain unrecognized, unarticulated, and unnegotiated. These findings provide insight into how the evolving nature of caregiving can obscure support needs and delay recognition of the caregiver role.

Our results show how caregiving develops through small adjustments that accumulate over time, such as gradually taking on responsibility for coordinating appointments, monitoring symptoms, assisting with daily tasks, and navigating services, with no clear point at which these caregiving responsibilities shift. This trajectory corresponds to care-ethical accounts describing family care as something people grow into rather than as a clearly defined or consciously chosen role [35]. Similar observations are reported in studies of chronic illness management, where caregiving evolves from everyday routines and established relationship patterns rather than explicit decision-making, and where informal caregivers gradually assume responsibility as primary system navigators, filling coordination gaps within fragmented healthcare and welfare systems [36, 37]. Such understandings help understand why the patients and informal caregivers in the present study may not have recognized when expectations changed or when support needs exceeded earlier patterns, as these shifts often occur gradually and without conscious awareness.

As described by the participants in our study, fluctuating RA symptoms and gradual changes in everyday functioning and support needs further contributed to the progressive and often unnoticed expansion of caregiving responsibilities. Qualitative research on episodic disability and age-related vulnerability in older patients with multiple chronic conditions shows that variations in pain, energy and functional capacity create unstable care needs that are difficult for others to understand, making it challenging for caregivers to interpret health changes and determine when increased support is required [38]. Studies across diverse designs, including both chronic physical and mental diseases, further emphasize that caregivers require adequate knowledge about symptoms and disease trajectories to support timely help-seeking [39, 40]. In line with this research, our findings show that limited disease-specific knowledge increased uncertainty and placed greater interpretive responsibility on caregivers. Previous research among people living with RA has shown that it can be difficult to distinguish whether symptoms and functional changes are related to RA, comorbidities, or age-related changes [12]. While previously described from the patient perspective, our findings suggest that similar uncertainties may also affect caregivers when interpreting symptoms and care needs. Scoping reviews of informal caregivers navigating healthcare systems further suggest that caregivers are often required to be proactive in seeking out, integrating and interpreting information across services [36, 41]. In line with this, we believe that such information-seeking and sense-making work may place additional demands on caregivers’ cognitive, time-related, and emotional resources. At the same time, engaging in information-seeking may also provide a sense of clarity and reassurance, potentially alleviating uncertainty and enabling caregivers to feel more confident in their supportive role [42, 43].

Our findings show how caregiving was also shaped by continuous relational negotiation, as both patients and caregivers managed their own limitations. This dynamic was particularly evident as some caregivers were themselves older and often spouses, meaning that both partners navigated ageing, health concerns and everyday limitations simultaneously. Patients sought to maintain autonomy and preserve dignity, while caregivers aimed to shield them from worry or emotional strain. These dynamics resemble patterns described in relational caregiving research as protective buffering, where concerns, needs or frustrations are deliberately withheld to maintain relational stability and reduce emotional strain [44]. In our study, protective buffering functioned not only as an emotional coping strategy but also as a form of relational regulation, shaping what concerns, needs or limitations were openly discussed between the patient and the caregiver, and which issues remained unspoken to protect one another from worry. This relational work contributed to sustained uncertainty and placed ongoing responsibility on caregivers to interpret symptoms, needs and changes in health in the absence of explicit communication. While protective buffering may help preserve relational stability and prevent immediate distress, previous research suggests that it can also have unintended consequences [45, 46]. By limiting open communication, protective buffering may delay the expression of needs, increase uncertainty, leaving partners to rely on interpretation rather than explicit communication when responding to illness-related changes [45, 46]. Research on dyadic coping in chronic illness similarly shows how partners often engage in mutual efforts to regulate stress and protect each other from distress, but that such strategies may also obscure needs and complicate shared coping processes [47, 48]. Building on this understanding, our findings draw attention to the emotional demands embedded in caregiving relationships.

From the perspective of patients, efforts to protect their caregivers were often linked to a desire to maintain autonomy and avoid becoming a burden. Mutual protection required ongoing emotional regulation within the relationship. While caregivers described carrying responsibility for interpreting needs and managing concern, patients simultaneously described holding back worries or requests for help in order to reduce the perceived strain on their partners. Similar patterns have been reported in caregiving for older patients with frailty across multiple chronic diseases, where emotional restraint increases uncertainty and places an ongoing interpretive burden on caregivers [40, 49]. In our study, fluctuating symptoms and limited disease-specific knowledge intensified this emotional work, and misalignments between expectations and capacities gave rise to frustration and tension.

Findings from our study suggest that caregiving for older patients with RA and frailty involves not only emotional and relational work but also substantial effort to coordinate care across healthcare and welfare systems. In line with previous research, responsibility for contact with healthcare services increasingly shifted to informal caregivers as care needs and health-related challenges accumulated over time [39]. For the participants in the present study, participation in healthcare encounters was selective and negotiated, shaped by preferences for autonomy, relational boundaries and concerns about emotional burden. Consistent with existing literature on caregiving in the context of frailty and chronic illness, coordination and navigation of the healthcare system emerged as demanding tasks that often remained largely invisible [36]. Other studies report that caregivers often lack support in managing appointments, medication and fragmented services. In our study, unclear communication and limited guidance intensified this burden, whereas access to timely and relevant information reduced uncertainty and strain as caregiving responsibilities accumulated over time. A scoping review of caregiving among older patients with diverse and complex conditions further suggests that support from care navigators or similar coordination roles may help caregivers manage system navigation and reduce uncertainty when navigating complex healthcare systems [36]. Our findings add nuance to this body of knowledge by demonstrating that such coordinative work is frequently undertaken without a formal mandate, clear guidance, or explicit recognition, thereby placing caregivers in an ambiguous position of responsibility across multiple systems.

As shown in previous studies, informal caregivers require knowledge to interpret symptoms and support timely care, assistance in coordinating fragmented services, and support in managing emotional strain and relational tensions. In our study, most caregivers were spouses and therefore often older patients themselves, which may further shape the capacity and sustainability of the support provided. These findings demonstrate how these needs are shaped by concerns about autonomy and burden, and how caregiving work often unfolds quietly in the background of daily life, influencing both sustainability of support and well-being.

Methodological considerations

This study has several methodological strengths and limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. A key strength is the inclusion of both caregivers and patients, allowing insight into how caregiving is experienced from both sides of the relationship. Although caregivers and patients were interviewed separately, this enabled exploration of how caregiving responsibilities, expectations, and needs are understood within the relationship. The combined focus on RA and frailty further contributes to knowledge about caregiving in a context where inflammatory disease interacts with age-related vulnerability, which is less frequently addressed in caregiving research that has predominantly focused on conditions such as dementia, cancer or advanced multimorbidity [50–53].

The study was informed by a social constructivist epistemological perspective, which guided the use of semi-structured interviews and reflexive thematic analysis to explore how participants understood and made sense of caregiving in the context of RA and frailty. This approach supported an in-depth exploration of subjective experiences while acknowledging that the findings represent context-dependent interpretations co-constructed between participants and the researcher and may therefore not be universally transferable [21].

The qualitative, exploratory design supported an in-depth exploration of caregiving experiences but also entails certain limitations. Participants were individuals who were able and willing to take part in interviews and reflect on relational and emotional aspects of caregiving. Recruitment through the DANBIO system required participants to engage with a digital patient platform and identify and invite an informal caregiver to participate. Consequently, individuals with more severe frailty, greater fatigue, cognitive impairment, limited digital literacy, or fewer social resources may be underrepresented. This may be reflected in the relatively low number of participants classified with higher levels of frailty. The findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting caregiving experiences among older adults with RA and frailty, and their caregivers, who were able to navigate the recruitment procedures and participate in interviews.

The characteristics of the participating caregivers should also be considered when interpreting the findings. Some caregivers were spouses and therefore often older patients themselves, in several cases managing their own health challenges alongside the caregiving role. This reflects a common caregiving pattern in later life but may also shape how caregiving responsibilities, limitations and support needs are experienced and articulated. When caregiving occurs between ageing partners, support is negotiated within the context of shared ageing and potentially declining capacities on both sides. Although other types of relationships were also represented among the caregivers, the presence of several spouse caregivers means that some of the experiences described reflect caregiving within long-term partner relationships where both individuals may face age-related vulnerabilities.

Interviews with patients originally contributed data to two study aims. Consequently, it cannot be ruled out that questions addressing experiences of frailty in the initial part of the interview, which were reported in a previously published article [12], may have influenced responses in the later part of the interviews that informed the aim of the present article. However, we consider this sequential structure a potential strength rather than a limitation. The initial focus on experiences of frailty appeared to facilitate reflection and enabled participants to articulate more nuanced and grounded accounts of the role and burden of caregivers. Many participants spontaneously reflected on the role of caregivers already in the early part of the interview, which underscores the importance of caregivers in everyday life with frailty and RA and supports the credibility of the findings presented in this article.

The relational and emotionally sensitive nature of the topic also has methodological implications. The interviews relied on participants’ willingness to articulate experiences that were often characterized by hesitation, restraint or protective buffering [45, 46]. Protective buffering may therefore have shaped not only caregiving practices but also the interview situation itself, influencing what participants felt able or willing to express. Rather than viewing this solely as a limitation, such silences can be understood as analytically meaningful, reflecting the relational dynamics that were central to the study’s findings.

While the study is situated within the context of RA and frailty, the findings may also be transferable to other chronic conditions characterized by changing care needs and increasing dependency, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cancer or cardiovascular disease.

Conclusion

This qualitative study underscores how caregiving in the context of RA and frailty becomes integrated into everyday life and gradually reshapes roles, responsibilities, and relationships between patients and caregivers. Caregiving involved a complex interplay of practical, emotional, and relational challenges, often carried out by spouses who were themselves ageing and sometimes managing their own health conditions. These experiences were shaped by how frailty manifested in everyday life, including physical limitations, fluctuating symptoms, and changing support needs. Caregivers expressed needs for clearer information about RA and coexisting conditions, support in communication and collaboration with the patient, recognition of their caregiving role, and guidance in navigating healthcare and welfare services. Although participants did not explicitly refer to frailty, these needs were closely related to the manifestations and consequences of frailty in everyday life. Clinically, this highlights the need for health professionals to more actively involve caregivers and address their informational, emotional, and coordinative support needs. These findings have informed the development of a targeted intervention to be tested in a feasibility study.
