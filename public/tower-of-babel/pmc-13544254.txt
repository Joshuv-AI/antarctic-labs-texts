AMPK phosphorylation proceeds through hierarchical proteoform cascades revealed by integrated mass spectrometry
Authors: Boris Krichel, Hsin-Ju Chan, Liam Bandura, Zhan Gao, Man-Di Wang, Holden T. Rogers, Sean J. Mcilwain, Charlotte Uetrecht, Ying Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Protein kinases integrate cellular signals through complex phosphorylation cascades, yet resolving how chemical perturbations trigger and modulate these cascades in therapeutic targets remains a major challenge. Here, we dissect adenosine 5′-monophosphate–activated protein kinase (AMPK) proteoforms during activation through controlled biochemical reactions with a hybrid mass spectrometry (MS) approach integrating bottom-up MS for site-specific kinetics with top-down proteoform characterization. We reveal that AMPK phosphorylation proceeds through hierarchical cascades rather than binary switching, with dual entry points: canonical calcium- and calmodulin-dependent protein kinase kinase 2 (CaMKK2)–mediated phosphorylation or allosteric activator PF-739 both triggering extensive autophosphorylation with α1-Ser496 (S496) showing the highest kinetic priority. Proteoform-resolved analysis uncovers channeled β1-Ser24/25 (S24/25) + Ser108 (S108) cophosphorylation linking subcellular localization with allosteric responsiveness. Site-directed mutagenesis demonstrates CaMKK2 targets only α1-Thr183 (T183), with all other modifications arising through autophosphorylation. Phosphatase competition reveals asymmetric control where PP1A selectively removes activation-loop phosphorylation while autophosphorylation sites remain protected, establishing persistent regulatory states. Resolving AMPK’s temporal kinetics and proteoform architecture during activation enables a proteoform-centric understanding on kinase regulation.

INTRODUCTION

Posttranslational modifications (PTMs) expand the molecular diversity of the proteome far beyond its genetic blueprint, empowering proteins with the ability to adapt and regulate their functions dynamically (1, 2). Proteoforms, molecular variants arising from PTMs, alternative splicing, and genetic variation, underpin biological complexity (3, 4). Among the PTMs, phosphorylation creates functionally distinct proteoforms that regulate cellular signaling and metabolic homeostasis (5, 6). Elucidating how multiple phosphorylation sites collectively modulate protein function is essential for developing therapeutics targeting metabolic kinases, which control cellular energy homeostasis and represent promising drug targets for metabolic disorders including diabetes and obesity (7, 8). However, capturing the temporal dynamics and combinatorial patterns of these phosphorylations with analytical precision remains challenging (9–11).

Mass spectrometry (MS)–based proteomics has transformed phosphorylation research by enabling multiplexed and quantitative analysis with sequence-level resolution (12–16). Bottom-up proteomics excels at deep proteome coverage and site identification through peptide level analysis, yet it disrupts the proteoforms and loses information on combinatorial modification patterns (17, 18). In contrast, top-down proteomics (19) preserves intact proteoform architectures and combinatorial modification patterns but with limited throughput and difficulty in mapping phosphorylation sites especially for large proteins (20, 21). Resolving phosphorylation cascades demands tracking of how individual sites change over time, because phosphorylation occurs in hierarchical cascades where initial modifications gate subsequent events (22, 23). For kinases with multiple phosphorylation sites and complex regulatory patterns, comprehensive proteoform-centric analysis requires integrating both approaches to capture the temporal kinetics of individual sites and the combinatorial architecture of intact proteoforms. This integration enables a proteoform-centric perspective on how modification patterns evolve and coordinate regulatory functions, providing insight inaccessible to a single method. A prior study by Lössl et al. integrated complementary MS-based approaches to characterize Polo-like kinase 1 and its coactivators, Aurora kinase A and Bora, revealing multisite phosphorylation together with interaction dynamics (24).

Adenosine 5′-monophosphate (AMP)–activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a central regulator of cellular energy metabolism with broad therapeutic relevance across metabolic, cardiovascular, and aging-related diseases (25–29). This heterotrimeric complex (α-β-γ) is canonically known to require AMP binding and activation loop phosphorylation at α1-T183 by upstream kinases such as calcium- and calmodulin-dependent protein kinase kinase 2 (CaMKK2) to achieve catalytic activation and downstream substrate phosphorylation (30–32). However, AMPK also undergoes extensive autophosphorylation, creating diverse proteoform landscapes whose regulatory roles and temporal relationships remain poorly defined (33, 34), exemplifying the challenge of resolving dynamic, multisite phosphorylation (35, 36).

Here, we dissect AMPK’s temporal kinetics and proteoform architecture using an integrated hybrid MS strategy combining quantitative intact mass measurements, bottom-up site-specific kinetics, and top-down proteoform sequencing. By combining kinase phosphorylation, dephosphorylation, and allosteric activation with proteoform-resolved MS, we reveal how autophosphorylation creates diverse modification patterns at sites linked to distinct regulatory functions. These findings extend beyond the traditional activation model to define a dynamic and multimechanistic proteoform-centric activation model for this major therapeutic target.

RESULTS

A hybrid MS strategy resolves proteoform-level phosphorylation dynamics

Our hybrid MS strategy unifies three complementary MS approaches to comprehensively characterize phosphorylation dynamics (Fig. 1, A to C): Intact protein reverse phase liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (RPLC-MS) quantifies proteoform changes over time (Fig. 1A), bottom-up MS resolves site-specific phosphorylation kinetics (Fig. 1B), and top-down fragmentation using ultrahigh-resolution MS definitively identifies proteoforms through sequencing (Fig. 1C). This integrated approach captures temporal kinetics and proteoform architecture by combining complementary MS measurements (Fig. 1D).

We applied this strategy to AMPK, which exists as 12 possible heterotrimeric combinations through different isoforms (α1-2, β1-2, and γ1-3) (37). We produced full-length recombinant α1β1γ1 isoforms in Escherichia coli (38), the ubiquitously expressed isoform combination in human tissues, and purified the complex to homogeneity (Fig. 1, E and F, and table S1). We activated AMPK using AMP- and CaMKK2-mediated phosphorylation (Fig. 1G). AMPK functionality was validated through activity assays using synthetic SAMS peptide substrate which confirmed that the enzyme was functional with a median effective concentration (EC50) = 12 nM, demonstrating quality comparable to commercial preparations (Fig. 1H) (39). Phosphorylation reactions were quenched at defined intervals by addition of formic acid to capture temporal dynamics (Fig. 1I).

AMPK subunits exhibit distinct phosphorylation profiles

AMPK subunits undergo differential phosphorylation with distinct temporal patterns. To resolve these dynamics, we performed in vitro phosphorylation of the AMPK complex using CaMKK2/calmodulin. CaMKK2 represents a primary upstream kinase of AMPK, phosphorylating α1-T183 in the activation loop, resulting in about 100-fold activity enhancement (30). To analyze phosphorylation status, we applied an intact protein RPLC-MS approach, providing highly reproducible chromatographic separation (Fig. 2A). Intact protein MS at the initial time point showed well-resolved charge state distributions (Fig. 2B and fig. S1A). Deconvoluted mass analysis confirmed proteoform identification with high mass accuracy (Fig. 2C). Although we detected initial phosphorylation and mass heterogeneity through nonspecific modifications known from E. coli, including gluconoylation and phosphogluconoylation (40) (Fig. 2D), the unphosphorylated species (0P) was by far the dominant form in all three subunits prior to CaMKK2 treatment, after which phosphorylation levels increased dramatically (Fig. 2E). Analysis of total phosphorylation (Ptotal) over 200 min revealed the sequential mass increase across AMPK subunits, culminating in the highest levels for α1 (5.33 ± 0.04), intermediate for β1 (2.73 ± 0.02), and minimal for γ1 (0.61 ± 0.01) (Fig. 2F; extended validation in fig. S1, B to D). β1 and γ1 spectra showed full isotopic resolution, while the larger α1 subunit exhibited an elevated spectral baseline due to its large size, high charge states, and coeluting species, necessitating smoothing and background subtraction prior to peak-picking (Fig. 2D).

Representative deconvoluted mass spectra across the time course demonstrate the sequential phosphorylation events (Fig. 2E). The maximum phosphorylation states observed at 200 min (α1: 7P, β1: 4P, and γ1: 1P) represent the minimum number of phosphorylatable sites on each subunit, since not all sites reach full stoichiometric phosphorylation. Distribution of AMPK proteoforms throughout the spectra revealed distinct phosphorylation behaviors: α1 exhibited a broad, distributed envelope indicating multiple competing modification pathways, whereas β1 showed preferential accumulation at the P2 state, suggesting a more channeled phosphorylation pattern with one predominant proteoform.

While numerous phosphorylation sites have been reported for AMPK subunits (34, 41–45) and prior intact MS studies have observed activation-dependent proteoform changes in α2β2γ3 complexes (46), our results reveal richer phosphorylation patterns than expected from direct CaMKK2 targeting alone, indicating triggered autophosphorylation cascades following initial α1-T183 activation. MS-based and biochemical studies report variable numbers of potential autophosphorylation sites (six for α1, seven for β1, and none for γ1 based on PhosphoSitePlus compilation), although the specific sites and stoichiometry depend heavily on experimental conditions (47). These quantitative profiles revealed subunit-specific hierarchies, indicating complex regulatory mechanisms beyond canonical T183 phosphorylation.

Phosphatase competition reveals asymmetric control of AMPK activation

AMPK regulation involves a dynamic equilibrium between kinase-mediated phosphorylation and phosphatase-mediated dephosphorylation, with PP1A preventing constitutive activation (48). Understanding which sites are preferentially targeted by phosphatases provides insight into AMPK’s regulatory architecture.

We investigated the interplay through competitive phosphorylation-dephosphorylation reactions using increased PP1A concentrations (Fig. 3A). In the absence of PP1A, total phosphorylation after 100 min reached levels (α1: 4.44 ± 0.03P, β1: 2.34 ± 0.01P, and γ1: 0.46 ± 0.01P) (fig. S2, A to C) comparable to previous experiments. However, even low PP1A (4.52 μM) concentrations selectively reduced α1 phosphorylation by ∼1P (∼0.93P decrease), while β1 (∼0.18P decrease) and γ1 levels (decrease within error) remained relatively stable (Fig. 3B). Even at high PP1A concentrations, substantial phosphorylation persisted, indicating that PP1A selectively removes the activating phosphorylation at T183 on the α1 subunit, whereas autophosphorylation sites appear protected from dephosphorylation. This asymmetric control demonstrates that autophosphorylation sites are kinetically stable once established, potentially persisting beyond acute signaling events (Fig. 3C).

Chemical-genetic dissection defines CaMKK2 specificity

To distinguish CaMKK2-driven phosphorylation from AMPK autophosphorylation, we generated two α1-subunit mutants that selectively disrupt different aspects of AMPK activation (Fig. 3D and fig. S3A). The α1-Thr183→Ala (T183A) mutant prevents activation loop phosphorylation, blocking canonical activation. The α1-Lys56→Asn (K56N) mutant disrupts ATP binding, abolishing all kinase activity; thus, any phosphorylation detected must originate from an external kinase. This dual-mutant strategy definitively separates upstream kinase targets from downstream autophosphorylation.

RPLC-MS analysis revealed notable differences in CaMKK2-mediated modification patterns (Fig. 3E). Wild-type (WT) AMPK exhibited phosphorylation levels (α1: 4.31 ± 0.05P, β1: 2.25 ± 0.01P, and γ1: 0.48 ± 0.02P) consistent with previous experiments. The α1-T183A mutant showed virtually no detectable phosphorylation on any subunit, indicating that without T183 phosphorylation, the activation loop cannot open and no autophosphorylation cascade can proceed. Most revealing was the catalytically inactive K56N mutant, which acquired exactly one phosphorylation on α1 and none on β1 (fig. S3B). This single modification identifies T183 as the sole direct CaMKK2 target. All other phosphorylation sites across AMPK subunits are generated exclusively through autophosphorylation triggered by this initial priming event.

Allosteric activation bypasses canonical phosphorylation requirements

AMPK harbors an allosteric drug and metabolite (ADaM) binding site at the α-kinase domain and β-subunit interface, activated by small molecules up to 10-fold (49). We used PF-739, a chemical probe ADaM site activator, demonstrating dose-dependent AMPK activation with 3.8-fold enhancement over AMP alone (Fig. 3, G to I) (50).

We next assessed whether allosteric stimulation alone could initiate the phosphorylation cascade in the absence of the canonical upstream trigger. While AMP alone produced minimal phosphorylation in both WT and T183A AMPK, addition of PF-739 resulted in substantial and nearly identical phosphorylation levels in both variants (WT: α1: 4.80 ± 0.09P, β1: 1.86 ± 0.01P; T183A: α1: 4.64 ± 0.10P, β1: 1.79 ± 0.01P) (Fig. 3J and fig. S3, C and D). These phosphorylation levels are comparable to those achieved through CaMKK2 activation, demonstrating that allosteric activation enables robust autophosphorylation even when T183 cannot be phosphorylated. This establishes that activation loop phosphorylation is not strictly required for AMPK to execute its regulatory phosphorylation program. These results support a model where PF-739 binding promotes a catalytically competent conformation that bypasses the canonical requirement for activation loop phosphorylation, establishing dual entry points into the hierarchical autophosphorylation cascade (Fig. 3K and fig. S4A) (51). While intact MS quantifies total phosphorylation and proteoform distributions, it is limited in resolving which specific sites contribute to each phosphorylation state. Hence, to map the specific phosphorylation sites underlying these intact-mass changes, we next performed site-specific bottom-up analysis.

Site-specific phosphorylation kinetics reveal mechanistic hierarchy

To map the kinetic hierarchy underlying AMPK proteoform dynamics, we used bottom-up MS using identical aliquots from the CaMKK2-AMPK reaction samples (Fig. 4A and fig. S5, B to D). Phosphorylation kinetics were quantified by monitoring extracted ion chromatograms (EICs) for peptide pairs showing inversely correlating behavior (Fig. 4, B and C, tables S3 to S5, and fig. S5A). Monitoring unphosphorylated peptide disappearance for 16 peptide pairs provided the most reliable measure of modification progression (Fig. 4D).

Kinetic profiling revealed a distinct hierarchy of phosphorylation rates across AMPK sites (Fig. 4, E and F). The most rapid autophosphorylation occurred at α1-S496, an inhibitory site (52), exhibiting exceptionally high kinetic rate (0.16 ± 0.05 min−1). This reflects efficient trans-autophosphorylation where CaMKK2-activated AMPK molecules phosphorylate S496 on neighboring complexes faster than CaMKK2 phosphorylates α1-T183 (0.077 ± 0.005 min−1).

The well-characterized β1-S108 showed relatively rapid kinetics, reaching complete modification by 50 min, with the kinetic curve showing high goodness-of-fit (R2 = 0.99) (51). β1-Thr148 (T148), crucial for glycogen binding (53), exhibited higher rate constant (0.077 ± 0.083 min−1) but moderate extent (0.45) and quantification challenges from complex peptide sequence, making kinetic parameter interpretation less reliable (53). These kinetic profiles established a temporal hierarchy distinguishing rapid regulatory modifications from slower secondary events.

Several sites displayed moderate kinetics, including α1-Ser176 (S176) (0.053 ± 0.014 min−1, extent = 0.62) and γ1-Ser9 (S9) (0.055 ± 0.027 min−1, extent = 0.80), while sites like α1-Thr388 (T388) (0.007 ± 0.004 min−1, extent = 0.35) showed very slow kinetics likely representing secondary regulatory mechanisms.

Among the analytical challenges was quantification of convoluted phosphorylations at the α1 C-terminal regulatory domain. However, a shorter 23-residue peptide from semi-specific trypsin cleavage (SSEVSLTSSVTSLDSSPVDLTPR/P) separated into three distinct chromatographic peaks corresponding to phosphorylation at α1-Ser516 (S516), α1-Ser524 (S524), and α1-Thr526 (T526) (fig. S5E). The unphosphorylated peptide decreased slowly (k = 0.008 ± 0.008 min−1) but achieved high phosphorylation extent (0.81). The total phosphorylation is distributed across three sites with chromatographic peak area ratios (S516:S524:T526 = 1.0:1.9:0.7) indicating preferential S524 modification.

The α1 and β1 subunit difference between peptide-level and intact protein quantification (4.6P versus 5.3P and 2.0P versus 2.7P, respectively) suggests the presence of modifications not captured by bottom-up analysis. Site-specific kinetic profiling revealed AMPK autophosphorylation proceeds through a hierarchical cascade with distinct temporal classes. While this identified individual site kinetics, the specific combinations of co-occurring modifications within intact proteoforms remained unresolved, motivating direct proteoform characterization.

Proteoform-resolved characterization identifies channeled modification patterns

To identify co-occurring modifications and resolve proteoform architectures, we used ultrahigh resolution [>400,000 at 400 mass/charge ratio (m/z)] MS top-down sequencing analysis. Direct nano–electrospray ionization (ESI) of denatured AMPK subunits yielded high-quality spectra with isotopic resolution. However, the α1 subunit showed strong signal suppression due to size-related effects and was only detectable in the unphosphorylated control sample. In contrast, the smaller β1 and γ1 subunits dominated the signal response under both conditions (Fig. 5A and fig. S6, A and B).

Examination of β1 intact mass spectra from the CaMKK2-treated sample revealed heterogeneous phosphorylated species (1P-7P), with doubly phosphorylated (2P) as the major component (Fig. 5B). Top-down measurements showed total β1 phosphorylation of 3.1P, slightly higher than our earlier intact protein LC-MS analysis (2.7P), a difference we attribute to the distinct detection principles, ionization conditions, and independent protein preparations used across the two measurements. Summing phosphorylation extents from all identified peptides in bottom-up analysis yielded only 2.0P, revealing a 0.7 to 1.1P discrepancy compared to intact protein measurements. This discrepancy made the predominant 2P β1 proteoform an ideal target for identifying the missing modifications through direct sequencing. Quadrupole isolation of the 2P β1 23+ precursor (1323 m/z) achieved mass species selectivity with high mass accuracy (1.9 parts per million) (Fig. 5, C and D).

Comprehensive structural characterization was performed using complementary fragmentation approaches combining collision-induced dissociation (CID) and electron capture dissociation (ECD) at optimized parameters (Fig. 5E and fig. S7). Data processing used in-house MASH software with manual curation (54). The combined fragmentation experiments yielded 179 high-confidence fragment ions (43 b-, 54 c-, 34 y-, and 48 z-type) across the 269-residue β1 sequence, achieving 54% backbone coverage and high mass precision (Fig. 5, F and G). Systematic fragment analysis revealed that the major β1 bis-phosphorylated proteoform (2P) carries phosphorylation at S24/25 and S108. As is inherent to top-down sequencing of multiply phosphorylated proteins, this level of coverage is sufficient to localize the major phosphorylation sites with confidence, although minor phosphorylation states in uncharacterized regions cannot be fully excluded.

The first phosphorylation was localized to the S24/25 double serine motif based on c22, c23, and c24 fragment ions, with predominant modification at S24 (fig. S8), consistent with the mutually exclusive phosphorylation pattern described for this site (44). The identification of S24/25 phosphorylation directly addresses the quantitative discrepancy between intact protein and peptide-level measurements. This site was absent from bottom-up analysis due to poor N-terminal coverage; the six-residue tryptic peptide DSSGTK containing S24/25 was not detected.

The second phosphorylation site, S108, was definitively assigned based on 27 high-confidence fragment ions spanning this region. This S24/25 + S108 combination represents a notable finding, as S24/25 phosphorylation has been linked to extranuclear distribution, while S108 phosphorylation facilitates allosteric activation by stabilizing the ADaM site binding pocket (42, 55). The strong predominance of the 2P species in intact mass spectra, combined with the consistent identification of this specific S24/25 + S108 combination, suggests a channeled proteoform formation pathway rather than stochastic modification of available sites. In contrast, β1-Ser101 (S101) and β1-T148 phosphorylation could not be detected in our top-down analysis, despite identification in bottom-up experiments, indicating that these represent minor proteoforms under these experimental conditions. Proteoform-resolved characterization thus revealed selective modification patterns that coordinate distinct regulatory mechanisms within AMPK.

Integrated analysis reveals mechanistic model of AMPK activation

Integrating kinetic, proteoform, and chemical perturbation data reveals a model for AMPK proteoform regulation based on four key mechanisms (Fig. 6A): (i) dual entry into the activation cascade through CaMKK2-dependent α1-T183 phosphorylation or allosteric activation; (ii) hierarchical phosphorylation kinetics with defined priorities, where α1-S496 shows highest modification efficiency; (iii) channeled proteoforms with selective co-occurrence, exemplified by the predominant β1-S24/25 + S108 combination coupling localization and allosteric sensitivity; and (iv) asymmetric dephosphorylation control where PP1A reverses α1-T183 but not autophosphorylation sites, enabling proteoform persistence beyond acute signaling.

Phosphorylation sites cluster into four structural hotspots with distinct regulatory functions (Fig. 6, B and C). The activation sites and catalytic center (Fig. 6D) cluster α1-T183, α1-S176, and β1-S108, where α1-T183 and β1-S108 drive main and allosteric activation. The α1 ST-loop (Fig. 6E) encompasses S496, S524, S516, and T526, orchestrating negative feedback through electrostatic interactions (52, 56). The α1 linker region C-terminal to the autoinhibitory domain (AID) (57) (Fig. 6F) contains phosphorylations at Thr355 (T355), Ser356 (S356), Ser360 (S360), Thr388 (T388), and Ser397 (S397), with T355, S356, and S360 additionally falling within the broader autoinhibitory sequence (AIS) as annotated by UniProt (residues 302 to 381). This phosphorylation cluster may constitute a feedback mechanism modulating AMP sensitivity and allosteric regulation. The β1 carbohydrate-binding module (Fig. 6G) features T148 mediating glycogen sequestration (53), alongside S96 and S101. This spatial organization reveals how AMPK integrates multiple regulatory inputs through autophosphorylation cascades that create proteoform diversity, extending beyond a binary activation model to enable fine-tuned kinase activity and function.

DISCUSSION

Protein kinases regulate cellular processes through dynamic phosphorylation cascades, yet understanding how chemical perturbations trigger and control these cascades requires resolving both temporal kinetics and proteoform architecture. Applied to AMPK, our hybrid MS approach combining intact protein profiling, site-specific kinetics, and proteoform characterization reveals when sites are modified, how modifications propagate hierarchically, and which combinations form co-occurring proteoforms (30, 35).

We found that AMPK activation proceeds through hierarchical autophosphorylation cascades triggered by a single priming event. Kinetic profiling resolved distinct temporal classes: rapid modification of α1-S496, moderate rates at allosteric control sites (34), and slow modifications clustered in regulatory domains. These findings reveal for the first time the hierarchical ordering during activation. Phosphatase experiments revealed asymmetric regulation where PP1A selectively removes activation-loop phosphorylation while leaving autophosphorylation sites intact. Prior studies observed one autophosphorylated site corresponding to α1-S496 being resistant to protein phosphatases using Western blot (44), yet, our intact protein measurements demonstrate that specific deactivation does not roll back the proteoform diversity generated through activation. Scott et al. (58) previously demonstrated that pS108 is more resistant to dephosphorylation than pThr172 (T172) by PP2Ca, a phosphatase analogous to the PP1A used in this study, and proposed that this kinetic decoupling creates a memory state in which pS108-carrying AMPK retains sensitivity to ADaM site ligands following canonical deactivation. Our measurements extend this concept to the proteoform level by showing that the broader autophosphorylation-derived proteoform diversity is preserved following selective removal of activation-loop phosphorylation.

Allosteric activation revealed a second activation route. PF-739 initiated robust autophosphorylation without T183 phosphorylation. Although allosteric activation without T183 phosphorylation may have limited physiological relevance for canonical AMPK function (59), the substantial proteoform remodeling suggests that diverse modification states can be generated through alternative pathways with implications for selective pharmacological modulation. Whether this T183-independent autophosphorylation has physiological relevance remains debated. Scott et al. and Dite et al. (58, 60) demonstrated that ADaM site ligands combined with AMP can activate AMPK independently of activation loop phosphorylation in vitro and in cells, with Dite et al. further identifying ULK1 as a cellular S108 kinase operating independently of upstream kinase activity. Willows et al. (59), however, showed that in cells lacking both LKB1 and CaMKK2, downstream substrate phosphorylation becomes undetectable, indicating that activation loop phosphorylation is essential in a cellular context. The proteoforms generated through T183-independent autophosphorylation may therefore be most relevant to pathological settings with compromised upstream kinase activity, where S108-phosphorylated proteoforms could show enhanced ADaM site ligand sensitivity and ST-loop phosphorylated species may be desensitized to canonical AMP-mediated regulation. The direct observation of these proteoform species following allosteric activation represents a previously unexplored layer of AMPK regulation, complementing existing biochemical evidence for T183-independent signaling.

Proteoform characterization identified channeled β1-S24/25 + S108 cophosphorylation as the predominant modified state, supporting a channeled phosphorylation pathway that couples subcellular localization with allosteric responsiveness through selective site co-occurrence (42, 58). The mechanistic basis of this channeling remains unresolved. Whether it reflects ordered sequential modification or structural coupling between sites could be addressed by future mutagenesis studies using nonphosphorylatable or phosphomimetic substitutions.

Integrating complementary measurements revealed modifications missed by individual approaches. Sites missed in bottom-up workflows were recovered by intact and top-down analysis, while discrepancies between methods revealed modifications not captured by any single approach. Our phosphatase competition experiments illustrate that the balance between kinase and phosphatase activity directly shapes the proteoform landscape. In vivo, this balance shifts continuously across cell types, subcellular compartments, metabolic states, and signaling inputs, meaning that multiple distinct AMPK proteoform populations likely coexist simultaneously, with their relative abundances reflecting the integrated history of local kinase and phosphatase activity (36). Resolving such distributions in physiological settings will ultimately require endogenous protein purification, single-cell approaches, and continued technological advancement in proteoform-resolved MS (61–63).

Several limitations of this study should be noted. All experiments were performed using recombinantly expressed proteins from E. coli, which lacks the posttranslational processing machinery of mammalian cells. β1-Gly2 myristoylation, a membrane-anchoring modification in mammalian AMPK, is absent from our bacterial expression system. Nonmyristoylated AMPK has been reported to display comparable or enhanced autophosphorylation capacity in vitro, and our solution-based assay conditions lack the membrane environment where myristoylation-dependent effects are most relevant (64, 65). The His-tag on the α1 subunit represents an additional nonnative feature, although no evidence of tag-mediated artifacts was observed in our data. In addition, top-down MS sequencing of multiply phosphorylated proteoforms presents inherent challenges for site localization when multiple sites are simultaneously modified. These challenges become even greater for larger proteins, for which achieving sufficient fragmentation depth and sequence coverage remains difficult in top-down proteomics (19, 66). Thus, although our 54% backbone coverage for β1 substantially improves assignment confidence, it does not fully eliminate ambiguity. Bottom-up quantification of phosphorylation kinetics is inherently limited by differential ionization efficiencies between modified and unmodified peptides, incomplete detection of multiply phosphorylated species, and potential protease accessibility changes induced by phosphorylation (14). The use of unphosphorylated peptide disappearance as the primary measure, validated by inverse correlation with the corresponding phosphopeptide signal, provides a controlled readout of modification progression. Last, the discrepancy between peptide-level and intact protein phosphorylation quantification for α1 indicates modification sites not captured by our bottom-up workflow, likely due to incomplete sequence coverage.

In summary, our findings establish that AMPK operates through hierarchical proteoform cascades that enable fine-tuned regulation beyond binary switching. Combining controlled biochemical reactions with hybrid MS to capture temporal kinetics, proteoform architecture, and responses to chemical and genetic perturbations revealed regulatory mechanisms inaccessible to any single method. Dual activation pathways, hierarchical autophosphorylation kinetics, and selective proteoform co-occurrence identify nodes for pharmacological intervention. These findings demonstrate that resolving the kinetics and architecture of proteoform cascades is essential for dissecting complex regulatory mechanisms in AMPK and other therapeutic kinase targets in metabolic disease.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Genes and protein constructs

Recombinant protein sequences were based on UniProt entries human AMPK α1 (Q13131), β1 (Q9Y478), and γ1 (P54619). The expression plasmid pET28-H6-AMPKγ1β1α1 for AMPK full-length, with His tag (MSYYHHHHHHEGVRTM-) N-terminally fused to the α subunit, and pET24 H6GST-hPP1Aα (amino acids 1–314, PP1A domain) were generated by Karsten Melcher Lab and provided by the Van Andel Institute. Site-directed mutations on AMPK α K56N and T183A were introduced using the Q5 site-directed mutagenesis kit (New England Biolabs) and confirmed using long-read DNA sequencing (UW-Madison Biotechnology Center).

Expression and purification

AMPK and PP1Aα were produced as recombinant proteins in One Shot BL21 Star (DE3) E. coli (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were grown in 1-liter baffled flasks to optical density at 600 nm (OD600) = 0.8 to 1.0, induced with 0.1 M isopropyl-β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside and continued at 18°C for 20 hours. Cell pellets were lysed in His equilibration buffer (20 mM imidazole, 40 mM phosphate, and 300 mM NaCl, pH 8) by sonication. His-tagged proteins were purified using HisPur Ni-NTA resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with sequential washes (50 mM imidazole) and elution (300 mM imidazole). Proteins were buffer-exchanged into storage buffer [20 mM phosphate, 150 mM NaCl, 300 mM imidazole, 15% glycerol, and 2 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)], concentrated to 5 to 10 mg/ml, and stored at −80°C. Concentrations were determined by Bradford assay (BioRad) or NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific). CaMKK2 [CaMKK β isoform 2 (1–541)] was obtained through the MRC PPU Reagents and Services facility (MRC PPU, College of Life Sciences, University of Dundee, Scotland).

Activity assay

AMPK kinase activity was assessed using the ADP-Glo luminescence assay (Promega) in white-bottom 384-well plates. For the dose-response curve (Fig. 1H), AMPK was titrated against fixed substrate conditions [500 μM ATP, SAMS peptide (0.2 μg/μl), 200 μM AMP, and 5 mM MgCl2). For the allosteric activation assay (Fig. 3I), AMPK was held at 10 ng/μl with 200 μM AMP as baseline and PF-739 titrated at the indicated concentrations. Reactions (5 μl total) were incubated 60 min at room temperature, followed by ADP-Glo reagent (40 min) and kinase detection reagent (30 min) per the manufacturer’s protocol. Fold activation was calculated relative to the AMP-only condition.

AMPK phosphorylation reactions

All reactions were performed in assay buffer (50 mM Hepes, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, and 0.01% Brij 35). CaMKK2 was premixed with calmodulin (Enzo Life Sciences) at a 1:2 molar ratio. For phosphorylation, 7.5 μM AMPK was incubated with 0.155 μM CaMKK2 and 0.31 μM calmodulin in the presence of 500 μM ATP, 200 μM AMP, 5 mM (CH3COO)2Mg, and 1 mM CaCl2 at 20°C for up to 200 min in [240 μl total volume]. Aliquots (∼15 μg of protein) were quenched at defined intervals by addition of formic acid to a final concentration of 0.1% and immediately placed on ice. For concentration-dependent PP1A phosphatase experiments, PP1A was added at varying concentrations to CaMKK2 AMPK mixtures, and samples were quenched after 200 min.

Quenched reactions were desalted using chloroform-methanol-water precipitation. AMPK was diluted into 400 μl of water, precipitated with 400 μl of methanol, phase-separated with 100 μl of chloroform, and centrifuged at 15,000g for 10 min. Following supernatant removal, 400 μl of methanol was added and mixed by rocking. After centrifugation, proteins were further desalted by two consecutive methanol washes. Air-dried pellets were stored at −80°C until analysis.

Intact LC-MS analysis

Desalted reaction samples were reconstituted in 5% FA/20% acetonitrile-isopropanol and adjusted to 200 ng/μl. LC-MS was performed using nanoAcquity UPLC (Waters) coupled to Impact II Quadrupole Time-of-Flight (QTOF) (Bruker Daltonics). Samples (300 to 500 ng) were separated on a home-packed C4 column (Halo, 2.7-μm, 250-μm ID, 10 cm, 1000 Å) using the following gradient: 0 to 3 min, 10% B; 3 to 20 min, 10 to 80% B; 20 to 24 min, 80 to 95% B; and 24 to 30 min, 95 to 10% B (A: 0.1% FA in H2O; B: 0.1% FA in acetonitrile) at 4 μl/min.

Mass spectra were acquired at 0.5 Hz over 300 to 3000 m/z. Source parameters were as follows: capillary voltage 4500 V, nebulizer pressure 50 kPa (0.5 bar), and dry gas 4.0 liter/min at 200°C. The ion transfer settings were as follows: funnel RF 300 V, quadrupole energy 5 V, low mass cutoff 650 m/z, isCID 10 V, and collision RF 2600 Vpp.

Data processing used Compass Data Analysis (Bruker). Summed mass spectra were generated from fixed time windows. Deconvolution used MaxEnt with different parameters for isotopic resolved (β/γ: resolution 40,000) versus nonresolved (α: resolution 10,000) charge envelopes. For the α1 subunit, spectra were additionally smoothed (Savitzky-Golay 0.2) and background-subtracted (factor 0.55) prior to peak-picking, to account for the elevated spectral baseline. Peak lists were manually curated for phosphorylation masses and exported for weighted average calculations. The total weighted phosphorylation average was calculated by the following formula(P0×0+P1×1+…×Pi×i)(P0+P1+…+Pi)=ptotal

The complete dataset of extracted peak parameters and weighted average phosphorylation calculations for all intact MS quantifications is provided as supplementary data (data S1).

Bottom-up MS analysis

Desalted reaction samples were reconstituted and neutralized with ammonium bicarbonate (pH 8), before being reduced with 10 mM TCEP (37°C, 30 min), alkylated with 500 mM chloroacetamide (room temperature, 30 min), and digested with trypsin (37°C, overnight). Peptides were desalted using C18 stage tips and analyzed by LC-MS/MS.

Samples (200 ng) were separated on Ion Optics columns (25 cm × 75 μm, C18 1.6 μm) at 55°C using 50-min gradients (2 to 37% B, then 37 to 85% B over 5 min; A: 0.2% FA in water, B: 0.2% FA in ACN) at 400 nl/min. Analysis used a Trapped Ion Mobility Spectrometry (tims)-TOF Pro (Bruker) in PASEF mode with ion mobility 0.60 to 1.60 Vs/cm2 and standard fragmentation parameters (10 MS/MS scans, 1.17-s cycle time, 20 to 59 eV collision energy).

Data were processed in Skyline (v23.1.0.380) to extract peak areas (area under the curve, AUC) from corresponding phosphorylated/unphosphorylated peptide pairs. Peak areas were normalized to the maximum value within each peptide series and analyzed in GraphPad Prism. Unphosphorylated peptides were fit to one-phase decay models to obtain rate constants (k, min−1), while phosphorylated peptides were fit to one-phase association models for validation. Phosphorylation extent was calculated as (AUCmax − AUCmin)/AUCmax from the normalized peak areas, representing the fraction of peptide phosphorylated. Only peptide pairs showing inverse correlation were included in the final analysis.

Phosphorylation kinetics were determined from unphosphorylated peptide disappearance (normalized to initial values), as this provides the most reliable measure of modification progression independent of differential ionization efficiencies between modified and unmodified forms.

Top-down MS

Phosphorylated AMPK samples were analyzed using solariX XR 12T Fourier-Transform Ion Cyclotron Resonance (FTICR) mass spectrometer (Bruker) coupled to TriVersa Nanomate (Advion). The nano-ESI conditions were as follows: 3.1 kPa (0.45 psi) desolvating gas pressure, 1.5 to 1.55 kV voltage, and 3 liter/min dry gas at 180°C. Source optics were optimized: capillary exit 240 V, detector plate 220 V, funnel 1 at 150 V, skimmer 50 V, funnel RF 300 Vpp, octopole frequency 5 MHz with 600 Vpp RF, and collision cell at 2 MHz with 2000 Vpp RF.

Mass spectra were acquired with 2M data points over 200 to 4000 m/z range. Precursor isolation used ≥4 m/z windows. Collision-activated dissociation (CAD) used 12 to 35 V collision energy. ECD parameters were as follows: pulse length 0.01 to 0.03 s, bias 0.5 to 1.5 V, and lens voltage 5 to 15 V. Fragment ion assignments were performed using standard top-down proteomics approaches for proteoform characterization. MS2 data were analyzed using MASH Native for proteoform sequencing and PTM localization (54). Fragments were identified using eTHRASH algorithm. All fragments were manually validated.

Data integration and analysis

Intact protein phosphorylation levels were quantified from deconvoluted mass spectra using weighted averages. Site-specific extent from bottom-up analysis were correlated with total phosphorylation measurements. FTICR MS2 data provided definitive proteoform identification and phosphosite localization and were used for complementing the results.
