The caregiving experiences of male spouses of women diagnosed with breast cancer: a qualitative study
Authors: Jui-Chih Chin, Yin-Ying Chen, Po-Sheng Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMale spousal caregiving is generally shaped by the gender norms embedded in the society. Rooted in a Confucian cultural heritage that emphasizes men’s roles as leaders and protectors of the family, Taiwanese male spouses may experience challenges and adaptations as they take on caregiving responsibilities. The objective of this study was to examine the caregiving experiences of male spouses of women diagnosed with breast cancer within the context of Taiwanese cultural beliefs and values.MethodsThis study had an inductive qualitative design. We interviewed 18 male spouses individually between January and June of 2022. Data were subjected to qualitative content analysis following the phenomenological approach.ResultsThe results yielded five themes: (1) Commitment in one’s own terms; (2) Navigating through wives’ ups and downs; (3) Guiding children to move on toward normalcy; (4) The challenges of emotional management; and (5) Compromise in sexual relationship. Findings revealed that male spouses struggled to persist in shouldering caring duties. The appraisal of their marital role shaped male spouses’ commitment and their approach to providing care. The findings indicate that most male spouses demonstrated a caring masculine style of caregiving, providing stress and protective buffering while undergoing emotional labor. In contrast, two spouses exhibited a traditional masculine caregiving style, emphasizing their role as providers and their problem-solving capabilities.ConclusionThis study supports the notion of plural masculinities by revealing two distinct masculine caregiving styles coexisting within a given culture. The findings illuminate how cultural values shape male spousal caregiving experiences and inform healthcare professionals in tailoring interventions to better support male caregivers.Keywords: Breast cancer, Caregiving experiences, Confucian culture, Male spouses, Plural masculinity

Background

Cancer has been characterized as a “we-disease” [1]. A diagnosis of cancer is life-changing not only for the patient but also for their family and caregivers. Spouses have been reported to be the predominant primary informal caregivers for cancer patients in Taiwan [2, 3]. Spousal caregivers of breast cancer patients often experience high levels of psychological distress, reduced quality of life, and a substantial risk of burnout [4]. Especially in families with young or school-aged children, female patients frequently struggle with an impaired mothering role, leading to feelings of guilt and further adaptative difficulties [5]. These challenges can negatively affect the patient’s marital relationship and place additional strain on male spouses as they assume caregiving responsibilities. Because caregiving involves ongoing interpersonal exchanges, it can further burden relationships, particularly among couples with dependent children [6].

Studies examining the experiences of male caregivers of breast cancer diagnosed (BCD) patients have revealed that they make sacrifices in many aspects of their lives. Specifically, they often prioritize their wives’ and children’s needs, suffer from disruptions to their schedules, and are left with insufficient time for work [6, 7]. Sometimes they are perplexed by their children’s reactions and are unsure how to respond to children’s emotionally charged questions [8, 9]. The sudden requirement of needing to be always immediately available is noted as a particularly significant emotional burden [9]. The new dynamic can lead to either increased couple intimacy [10] or marital discord [6]. The tendency of caregivers to de-prioritize their personal life needs while managing additional tasks can reduce emotional well-being.

The aforementioned studies focused mainly on male spouses’ responses to patients’ needs and changes in relationships and lifestyle, with little attention being paid to contextualizing factors. According to the conceptual model of cancer family caregiving experience proposed by Fletcher et al. [11], three interacting components—contextual factors, the stress process, and the cancer trajectory—can significantly impact caregivers’ health and well-being. Contextual factors include personal characteristics (e.g., age, gender, health conditions), sociocultural aspects (e.g., ethnicity, education, socioeconomic status), and relational variables (e.g., relationship to the patient). The stress process involves primary stressors (e.g., patient pain), secondary stressors (e.g. spillover effects), cognitive appraisal (e.g. perceived caregiving burden), and cognitive-behavioral responses. The cancer trajectory encompasses all stages of the illness, from diagnosis to survivorship or end-of-life care. According to Fletcher’s model, these three components interact to shape family caregivers’ experiences and influence their wellbeing.

Research guided by this model has shown that certain contextual factors negatively impact caregivers’ mental and physical health. For instance, younger age [12–14] and being a spousal caregiver [13] have been identified as risk factors for poorer mental health, while caregivers with multiple health comorbidities are at greater risk for physical health decline [12]. However, contextual factors in Fletcher’s model differ from stressors and the illness trajectory in that they serve as the background conditions that shape how caregiving is experienced and interpreted. While the aforementioned quantitative studies have identified statistically significant associations between contextual factors and caregiver outcomes, they offer limited insight into how these factors affect the caregiving experiences of spouses.

There is a growing recognition of the need to examine deeper sociocultural influences–such as cultural beliefs, values, and gender norms–in shaping male caregiving experiences [15–17]. The cultural script of masculinity has been identified as a particularly influential factor. The theory of plural masculinities, proposed by Connell [15], suggests that there are various definitions and enactments of masculinity across multicultural societies. Masculinity is culturally specific and intrinsically tied to societal structures and norms. In some Western societies, where masculinity is often associated with independence, strength, and stoicism [18], male caregivers of cancer patients tend to focus on practical tasks, prioritize minimizing disruption, and suppress their own stress [7, 18]. These caregiving efforts are often framed as fulfilling roles of protector or provider [19]. On the other hand, in certain Western societies where gender equality is more strongly emphasized and male dominance has declined, male caregivers are more likely to exhibit nurturing attitudes and value relational interdependence [20, 21].

In contrast, studies from non-Western societies highlight caregiving as being deeply associated with familial obligation [16]. In ethnic groups and Asian societies influenced by Confucian cultural heritage, male caregiving is shaped less by gender roles but by norms of interpersonal ethics. Confucian teachings place significant emphasis on the ethics embedded in the Five Cardinal Relationships (i.e., ruler-subject, father-son, husband-wife, elder brother-younger brother, and friend-friend) [22]. The behavioral norms prescribed in these relationships—particularly within family bonds—serve as the foundation for achieving social harmony and fostering moral conduct. Consequently, filial piety in the parent–child relationship, along with a cultural commitment to respecting elders, underpins caregivers’ sense of duty to care for parents and family members [16, 23]. Involvement in caregiving is thus perceived by male caregivers as a commitment rather than a burden [23].

In the context of marriage, Confucian ethics—characterized by a hierarchical dynamic of superiors and subordinates—emphasize righteousness on the part of the husband and submission on the part of the wife [22]. The husband is the head of the household, responsible for leadership and protection, while the wife is expected to support and comply with her husband. Each spouse is expected to follow and uphold these relational ethics, a process of conquering selfishness and restoring propriety [23].

In contemporary Taiwanese families, the organizational structure still largely follows this tradition. Even in dual-income households, women often remain primarily responsible for housework and childcare. These enduring cultural expectations may shape how men perceive and perform caregiving roles. They may feel compelled to shift from an external role of economic provision and leadership to an internal role of caregiving and emotional support. This role transition can create tension with traditional masculine ideals among Taiwanese male spouses. Conversely, Confucian relational ethics regard the ultimate expression of manhood as a commitment to caring for one’s family. Influenced by such beliefs, male spouses may strive to embody the culturally expected role of a responsible husband and fulfill family expectations. This raises the question of whether Taiwanese male spouses’ caregiving experiences differ depending on the values they hold regarding masculinity and manhood.

Guided by Connell’s framework of multicultural masculinity [15] and the influence of Confucian cultural heritage, the objective of the present study was to examine the caregiving experiences of Taiwanese male spouses of BCD women, within the broader context of Taiwanese cultural beliefs and values. Despite growing attention to gender and caregiving, existing studies on male caregivers are predominantly based on Western conceptualizations of masculinity that offer limited insight into how Confucian values shape men’s caregiving identities in East Asian contexts. Moreover, few scholars have sought to integrate Confucian relational ethics with Connell’s framework of multicultural masculinities to understand how men navigate caregiving for wives with breast cancer. To address these research gaps, the study questions are how Taiwanese male caregivers perceive, feel, and react to life changes consequent to their partners’ diagnoses of breast cancer, and how contextual factors, such as cultural beliefs and values, shape such experiences.

Methods

Participants

The current study was part of a larger research project on BCD patient-spouse dyadic coping. The two-year project aimed to investigate the dyadic coping experiences of BCD couples with young or school-aged children (aged 4–12 years) during 2021–2022, with the goal of integrating key findings to develop and implement a family-oriented supportive program for BCD families in 2022–2023. This program is grounded in the premise that cancer is a family affair and seeks to promote family adjustment as a cohesive unit. The project was conducted at the principal investigator’s (PI’s) university in Taipei City. Interview data collected from Taiwanese spouses of BCD women during the first year was utilized in the current study.

Convenient sampling was conducted from two sources. Firstly, breast cancer foundations and patient networks were contacted for permission to post recruiting notices on their Facebook or LINE platforms. Interested mothers completed an online Google form that was hyperlinked to the recruiting notice. Secondly, BCD mothers were approached by a registered nurse and an assistant of the third author (YP), who is a medical doctor at a hospital. Patients were given only the research recruitment information and the corresponding link. They were clearly informed that participation was entirely voluntary and assured that choosing not to participate would in no way affect their medical rights, which they fully acknowledged. The dyad inclusion criteria were: (i) a woman who was diagnosed with primary or recurrent breast cancer in the last 2 years; (ii) a cohabitating spouse/partner; and (iii) having at least one child who was 4–12 years old at the time of diagnosis. The exclusion criteria were: (i) the male partner declined to participate; and (ii) the patient’s physical condition prevented an interview. The research assistant contacted each interested mother by phone, explained the study’s purpose, and asked the patient to ensure her husband’s participation.

Through convenience sampling, we recruited 18 Taiwanese BCD patient-spouse dyads; including 1 unmarried dyad. For simplicity, the dyad pairs are referred to as spouse-wife dyads from here forward. Their characteristics are described in Table 1. The mean age of BCD participants was 42.8 years and that of their spouses was 45.0 years. The patient sample included 9 women who had a stage II cancer diagnosis and 3 women who were being treated for a cancer recurrence (stage IV). The time since diagnosis for the 18 patients ranged from 3 months to 23 months. No dyads dropped out.

Data collection

BCD women and their spouses were invited to the PI’s university in Taipei city. A semi-structured interview was conducted with each participant in a private meeting room, woman and man separately. Informed consent from each participant was taken before the start of the interview. BCD women were interviewed by the PI, who is a tenured professor, experienced in breast cancer research, and also a female breast cancer survivor. Spouses were interviewed by the second author (CY), a female associate professor with expertise in family studies. Only data collected from spouse interviews were used in this study.

The spouse interview questions were based on Montford et al.’s work [24]. The interview started with a query about the general history of the diagnosis, which was followed by queries regarding caregiving experiences, including (a) Please tell me about what you have experienced and about life changes after your wife’s diagnosis. (b) How did you feel and why? (c) How did you deal with these changes? (d) What about your child(ren)? What and how did you respond to their reactions? (e) What do you think and how do you feel about your role in the family after your wife’s diagnosis? The interview guide was reviewed by two BCD wives and subsequently used to interview their husbands, who had participated in the PI’s previous study. Their feedback indicated that the questions were appropriate and effective in eliciting the interviewees’ reflections on their coping experiences. However, they suggested including a straightforward question about couples’ sex life, as Taiwanese individuals are less likely to discuss sexuality unless explicitly asked. Consequently, a direct question regarding sexual intimacy was added as the sixth question: (f) How has your sex life with your wife been? No repeat interviews were conducted.

Each interview was audiotaped with prior consent, conducted in Mandarin or Taiwanese, and transcribed verbatim by a research assistant. During each interview (mean duration, 87 min; range, 60–126 min), CY took field notes on critical memories and emotional expressions to aid in clarifying and probing responses. These field notes were then cross-checked against interview transcriptions to ensure accuracy and to capture nuances that might not have been fully reflected in the recordings. The recordings and transcriptions were stored in password-protected personal computers in PI’s and CY’s homes.

The interviewers (PI and CY) had no relationships with the dyads prior to the study. Although four breast cancer patients were under YP’s care, both the patients and their spouses were informed prior to the interviews that YP would not be made aware of their participation. They were further assured that their identities would be protected through anonymization and that YP would not have access to any non-anonymized data at any stage of the study. Before the interview, the dyads were informed of the PI’s cancer survivorship, CY’s expertise, YP’s collaboration, and the background of the current study.

Data analysis

Inductive data analysis was conducted following the phenomenological approach [25]. The PI and CY repeatedly reviewed both the interview transcripts and field notes, allowing for deep reflection on participants’ experiences from spousal, male, fatherly, and familial perspectives. This collaborative reflective process helped the researchers identify critical episodes and the associated insights regarding caregiving experiences. According to Priest [25], such reflection sensitizes researchers to the phenomenon, thereby facilitating the logical and systematic synthesis required to achieve essential descriptions of experience. Four target areas of care provisions were identified: ill wife, children, self, and couple intimacy. For example, caring for an ill wife experiences included physical labor, accompaniment to appointments, and negative thoughts and emotions. To divide the text into units, experiences were subdivided in response to the identification of further issues. For example, the experience of coping with the BCD woman’s negative thoughts and emotions was subdivided into mortality/appearance- and child-associated issues. After identifying caregiving issues, the researchers transformed units into meanings expressed as phenomenological concepts by extracting and labeling caregiving concepts based on the meanings reflected in caregivers’ behaviors. The resulting codes were compared and refined through constant comparison, then grouped into categories based on similarity and relevance. These categories were then consolidated and integrated into overarching themes according to their relatedness. Five themes emerged and were named to reflect a holistic interpretation of the data.

Data collection was concluded based on the following considerations: (1) the cohort demonstrated sample diversity, as it included BCD women diagnosed across stages 1 to 4; and (2) the interviews exhibited strong dialogical quality. Since the purpose of this study was to explore male spousal caregiving experiences, it was essential to capture participants’ genuine thoughts and emotions. Both the PI and CY observed that male spouses generally shared positive accounts and reflected on challenging times as they recounted their caregiving experiences during the interviews. Such self-disclosure provided important evidence of the data’s information power. As Malterud et al. [26] describe, information power suggests that the more relevant and substantial the data are for addressing the research question, the fewer participants are needed. In this study, the depth and openness of participants’ self-disclosures yielded rich, nuanced insights into their caregiving experiences, thereby enhancing the information power of the data and supporting the adequacy of our sample size. No follow-up interviews were conducted.

Ethical consideration

The study protocol, along with the informed written consent, was approved by MacKay Memorial Hospital IRB (21MMHIS135e) on August 12, 2021. All participants signed written consent forms before being interviewed. After completing the interview, each participant received a subsidy of one thousand NT dollars for transportation. Although this information was not included in the recruitment materials, it was clearly stated in the informed consent form and verbally explained prior to the interview. The PI and CY reminded participants that they had the right to decline any question or withdraw from the study at any time, emphasizing that the subsidy would be provided regardless of their level of participation.

Rigor

Peer debriefing and member checking were used to enhance credibility. Peer debriefing involved a series of discussions between PI and CY. YP was consulted to resolve disagreements about codes, categories, and themes. Refinement was conducted through consensus. The synthesized data were sent to interviewed spouses for critical evaluation of whether the findings aligned with their experiences and thoughts. Some participants provided positive feedback such as feeling of being understood and feeling empowered to move on. To promote transferability, the characteristics of the participants, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and the interview guide were described to enable readers determine the applicability of the results in other contexts.

Results

The findings revealed that spouses’ caregiving experiences were contextualized by cultural values. Five themes emerged: (1) Commitment in one’s own terms; (2) Navigating through wives’ ups and downs; (3) Guiding children to move on toward normalcy; (4) The challenges of emotion management; and (5) Compromise in sexual relationship. Quotes are presented by participant number (e.g., F2 is participating father #2).

Theme 1: commitment in one’s own terms

Commitment in one’s own terms, which was defined as the attitude or mindset that motivated a spouse to be involved in caregiving, varied with individuals’ appraisals of their role as a husband. Four attitude subthemes emerged: (a) share and collaborate with ill wife; (b) shoulder a life-long responsibility for a loved one; (c) stand by you, but following my leadership; and (d) act on leftover needs.

Share and collaborate with ill wife

This theme reflects the belief among some spouses that household maintenance and childcare are primarily the wife’s responsibilities. However, when the wife becomes ill, the husband is expected to assume or share these roles. Several participants in our sample adhered to this view.

F12 relayed, when she was hospitalized, I would bring food for her. It wasn’t possible for me to do that before…I know that now that she is ill, we have to do more…this is basic.

F4 expressed, …before illness, my wife did more, then she complained that she got ill because she did too much…I cannot deny…[after sharing duties] sometimes I think that I’d rather do more…if we divide [household work] so distinctly, she is not happy… complaining goes nowhere, you still have to do…

In families maintaining a traditional organization structure, husbands willingly took on more duties. Even for those, like F4, who were not ready for the new responsibilities, acceptance was seen as the best way to avoid upsetting their BCD wives. Several spouses referred to taking up the gap in family maintenance resulting from their wives’ illnesses as a fundamental obligation.

Most described their additional workload as doable, describing the situation with statements like “just taking days off, not a big deal” (F4) and “just move yourself a little more to help” (F12). Those who had support from parents or parents-in-law reported experiencing no significant change in workload. However, one spouse (F14) who lived in America while his wife received her diagnosis and treatment expressed feeling tired. His caregiving demands impaired his job performance, leading to his being laid off and his subsequent return to Taiwan.

Shoulder a life-long responsibility for a loved one

This theme illustrates the perspective that assuming caregiving responsibilities was perceived not as a matter of fairness, but as a moral and emotional duty of a husband toward his ill wife. Among these spouses, caregiving was primarily motivated by the desire to ensure their wives’ comfort and relaxation.

F2 shared, I would take over household work as much as possible…I think that…she can let go at she wishes and leave it to me…I hope that she can become worry-free by my taking-over.

F10 shared, I’m simply thinking that I’ll do all I can offer [repeat twice]…even though there are some things [fulfilling wife’s requests] that I would rather not to do, I could accept and do it….

The spouses indicated that they were motivated to take on duties whenever their wives felt it necessary. Primarily, they hoped to help their wives feel at ease. As to why these husbands were devoted to caregiving tasks, they shared that they perceived having an inherent obligation to their union.

F2 expressed, I think she has given her whole life to me, I certainly should take up responsibility, that’s it…

F8 relayed, when you marry her, she is the one whose hands you should hold for the rest of life, you should hold them tightly…She is my family…

The belief that marriage is a life-long agreement is still upheld in Taiwanese culture. In the context of valuing a strong marriage and family ties, spouses express commitment to caregiving and are willing to take on the full responsibility of looking after their wives.

Stand by you, but following my leadership

This theme represents the belief that husbands should assume full responsibility for caring for their ill wives, grounded in the notion that wives should respect their husbands’ authority and submit to their leadership. While these spouses shared with the previous group a strong determination to undertake caregiving tasks, they differed from the previous group in that they tended to emphasize their grace in the provision of care. They considered their dedication as meritorious and deserving of their wives’ respect.

F6 said, I always tell her that ‘were you not married with me, you would be divorced already…I am not like those useless men, who let wife work for money and get divorced when something bad happens…

F13, whose partner had a recurrence, expressed, I give her my promise that I won’t leave her. I let her have an expectation that I will accompany her to the last minute…I stay by her side. I can act like a husband, not just a boyfriend…

In both of the above excerpts, the spouses expressed satisfaction with their determination to not abandon their wives. Their tone and expressions communicated pride in their manhood. However, when probed about their caregiving involvement, they did not articulate their efforts but rather described their expectations for their wives.

F6 continued, I support whatever she wants…But, I told her not to show a sullen face to me… spilled bitterness irritates me…about the child stuff, she decides and I don’t want to be bothered by those tedious things…I would go nuts when she started complaining…

F13 relayed, I’ve been through various lessons in my life…I often take the lead [in discussing issues]…Others may think that I am a chauvinist, but my shoulder is hard, it’s OK to be a chauvinist… Sometimes she wanted me to pamper her and understand her feelings…I said that’s impossible…‘your heart is made of flesh, so is mine’.

It appeared that both men were willing to take on some extent of caregiving responsibility. However, there was a line that the BCD wife should not transgress. Such a boundary may lead to a relational barrier between a BCD patient and her spouse.

Act on leftover needs

This theme reflects the perspective that, although husbands attempt to care for their ill wives, their ability to meet their wives’ true needs is limited by strained marital relationships. As a result, caregiving is limited to what the spouses themselves can infer or what is explicitly requested. Because the patients’ true needs are often out of reach—the wife may not wish to reveal or communicate them—the care provided by spouses addresses only these residual, “leftover” needs, rather than the wife’s primary needs. There were two couples whose marriages were on the verge of divorce before the breast cancer diagnosis. The two spouses (F5 and F17) expressed limited capacities for offering help to their ill wives. One of these husbands (F17) acted only upon his wife’s requests, while the other (F5) tried very hard to create and negotiate caregiving needs with his wife.

F17 explained, …when it cost time and energy, I would go with her[to hospital]…[when asked about his wife’s negative thinking] frankly speaking, I did not notice it…but when she expressed her needs, I would respond to her requests…

F5 shared, When I learned about her diagnosis, I was asking myself ‘what else could I do’, our relationship was very weak…I cooked vegetable soup every day, but she didn’t care. There’s nothing left that I could do…I was thinking that ‘had you been needing me more, would our relationship get better?’…we are now like roommates only working for caring our child’

Although both spouses engaged in caregiving tasks, only one (F5) was very active in determining his wife’s needs, despite perceiving that she did not appreciate his efforts much. It appeared that his caregiving could not save his marriage, but only maintained a collaboration in childcare. It should be noted that F5 had previously already taken on most of the household and childcaring duties because he earned less than his wife. Quality of marriage seems to have a substantial impact on the extent of caregiving that spouses engaged in.

Based on the different ways of perceiving the husband’s role, the nature of caregiving experiences varied. In our cohort, except for F6, F13, and F17, the majority of the 15 male spouses expressed that they struggled with their wives’ emotional turbulence (Theme 2) and strived to maintain a sense of normalcy for their children (Theme 3). Moreover, they had to manage their own emotional challenges (Theme 4) and make compromises in their sexual relationships (Theme 5). The following caregiving themes therefore depict four domains of lived experiences among male spouses who perceived caregiving as a shared household responsibility with their wives and as a lifelong commitment within the marital relationship.

In contrast, F6 and F13, who assumed a leadership role in deciding what and how to care for their wives according to their own preferences, focused more on their expectations of how their wives should cope with the illness rather than on responding to their wives’ needs. F17, who provided care only when requested, showed limited awareness of his wife’s needs unless they were explicitly communicated by her. Hence, the following four themes do not represent the caregiving experiences of these three participants.

Theme 2: navigating through wives’ ups and downs

BCD mothers have experienced an array of negative emotions, which have spill-over effects on caregiving loads and the mother-child relationship. Most of the spouses were able to provide positive reasoning and engage in reassuring actions when their wives had negative thoughts. They tried to help their BCD wives re-frame negativity circumstances in a positive light.

F16 shared, when she just got diagnosed, she was very negative and said, ‘let’s divorce, you can find someone to live with’. I told her ‘Life was not about picking up food, I chose you…, I should accept what happens to you…I can’t leave you when life becomes bitter…life is not about picking up my wished dishes’…

F15 said, she expressed her worries about changes in her appearance. I told her not to worry and engaged in silly behaviors intentionally, like kissing her bald head and saying ‘it’s lovely [giggling]…you can become a soldier’…I hoped that she took it as not a big deal

However, some spouses became less emotionally responsive, including becoming a passive listener as in, “I just listen while she complains…I don’t take initiative” (F4) or becoming an interlocutor repeating similar messages “responding with similar comments will do…I think women just want to reiterate the same stuff” (F16).

The reason why spouses reacted passively may be lack of dexterity in comforting, as one spouse explicated,

F2 recounted, my wife sat on the bed crying one night [hurt by the 4-year-old child’s comment about no breast]. I know she’s hurt. I put myself in her shoes and imagine how she may be feeling…but I don’t know what to say but only patting her…I am afraid my expression may drive her to despair, make it even worse… I can’t say a word in turn

The spouse was eager to empathize with his wife but found himself at a loss for comforting words. His lack of confidence in conveying comforting messages verbally may reflect the common inexperience of Taiwanese men with providing emotional support to their wives.

Spouses also shared that their wives were affected by their children’s naïve comments and inconsiderate behaviors. Settling of mother-child disputes was described as very challenging.

F2 continued the episode of wife becoming upset about the child’s comment about her having no breast: I told my wife that young children say what they see… Later [when he talked about her bald head]… I told him not to say that to mom. But he asked why…

F18 relayed, …sometimes when my wife gets irritated by him [their son]…they would argue badly…I heard them fighting, but I wouldn’t dare pick a side. I let them go about it on their own… if I stand by mom, my son would say that I’m biased… The next day, I would talk with my boy [rather than] doing it on the spot…

The spouses thus shared that they often played the role of a peacemaker. On one hand, they helped their wives to reappraise their children’s responses in light of their developmental stage. On the other hand, they educated their children about their mothers’ illnesses with compassionate reasoning. This go-between role was difficult because it required an understanding of child development, awareness of psychological needs of the mother-child dyad, and good judgement of timing for reconciliation.

Theme 3: guiding children to move on toward normalcy

The fathers in our sample emphasized the value of maintaining normalcy in children’s daily lives. They did not feel a need to disclose too much information about the illness, because elementary-school aged children were too young to grasp it. Providing young children with detailed information was deemed unnecessary in that it may cause fear and anxiety (F7). It was presumed that older children (adolescents) would understand without parental explanation.

Most of the spouses felt that their younger children behaved normally without exhibiting specific reactions, and thus they assumed that the children were not affected. One father indicated that he did not know how his daughter perceived the situation because she behaved normally and continued her routine activities (i.e., finishing schoolwork as usual). “I did not ask, not because I was unwilling to but because … I did not notice such a need” (F7). It appeared that continuity in children’s daily activities may have constrained fathers’ abilities to recognize children’s needs.

Keeping normalcy in life included fathers’ efforts to guide their children in assuming household responsibilities and reacting considerately to their mothers. However, when children misbehaved, they showed an understanding of children’s behaviors.

F12 shared, Children are strange. They know mother is sick…they don’t show their caring verbally… no, they express in a different way…I expect them to be considerate and express their love for mother, and they did not… but I think they care…

We found that fathers may help their children buffer stress via family conversation in daily life. For example, one participant talked about chatting with children about illness-related experiences in an intentionally humorous way.

F9 said, we would try to talk about serious stuff in a fun way…like we told the children that the nurse was shocked by a book-like pile of mom’s medical records…we shared it in a funny way…more healthy [to children’s psychology]….

Illness-related information and experiences was shared selectively and delivered in an upbeat way when conversing with children.

Another father (F10) shared that he would teach his child a problem-solving mindset in confronting adversity through family conversations:

I often tell my wife and my girl ‘don’t get trapped by things that happen, it’s doesn’t work at all unless you can change them. If there’s no way to change it, your recurrent thinking does not help you at all… you should change what you are able to change’.

The father intended to provide guidance on coping with adversity, especially cultivating a problem-focused attitude, through daily conversation with his children.

When dealing with the ultimate stressor of mortality, only two fathers reflected on how they comprehended the issue and addressed it differently with children.

F11, whose wife had a recurrence, shared, …I had told [my daughter], ‘if mom’s illness was not cured, mom would be gone’ at the time of the first cancer diagnosis…this time I did not mention it…because I think the situation is more serious this time…and if I speak it out, it may come true. I intend not to talk about it….

F14 relayed, …she just knew that mom was sick, a very bad illness…I think I would like to share the stories of my parents [F14’s parents lost their parents at very young age]…the child can learn from family stories…some vigilance or awareness…to enable her to manage it [death] in a calm demeanor…like vaccination

F11 was reluctant to address this issue due to the high probability of mortality in cases of recurrence. Fear of misfortune, akin to a prophecy coming true once spoken aloud, prevented the father from discussing it with his children and led him to downplay its impact. Meanwhile, F14 used a family story of a similar experience to prepare his children for potential adversity.

Theme 4: the challenges of emotion management

The interviewed spouses expressed unanimously that confronting a problem was the best way to deal with a stressor. Despite this problem-focused mindset, they reported experiencing negative emotions such as shock, sadness, distress, a sense of ill-boding, and guilt. One husband expressed feeling anger toward his wife, though he was unable to recognize his anger until seeing a counselor. He (F14) commented, “I don’t know how to take care of my emotions”. Some husbands shared how they controlled their emotions and engaged in venting strategies. Two subthemes emerged from such conversations: (a) engaging in emotional labor; and (b) finding leeway.

Engaging in emotional labor

Despite their emotions, spouses associated with this subtheme tended to focus on persisting in their caregiving duties. Some spouses stressed their mentality as “just carry and continue carrying until one day you can no longer do it” (F10). Many used cognitive strategies to control their emotions, such as “making your life enriched and thus forgetting about it” (F18) and “not thinking that she’s got a serious illness” (F15). However, emotional reactions may emerge despite the use of cognitive strategies. For example, F8 recalled:

I couldn’t bear it the next day [after diagnosis]…I couldn’t fall asleep and went to the kitchen with the door shut. I started to wash dishes left in the basin, and burst into a flood of tears…I don’t want them to see [me cry]…[so] I wipe my tears before I walk out, then turn into a good father and a good husband, switching my faces…one face inside [kitchen] and back to my normal face when the door opens…

F8 intended to not show weakness in front of his wife and children. He had full awareness of his fatherly role as well as that of a supportive husband. Such expressions characterized the emotional labor that spouses of BCD women perform in daily life.

Finding leeway

Many shared having engaged in activities for relaxation when having been faced with long-term hardship:

I watch YouTube when I do housework…to distract my attention…so as not to think that I am doing housework or feeling sick of it… (F2)

I would go for a drink with my coworkers for relaxation, but I would not mention family matters. I don’t want to pass negativity on to others… (F4)

…I went to a wine-tasting gathering after putting my child to sleep…‘me time’ is important, like Friday is my free time, I enjoy such a recess… I enjoy messaging or spacing-out by myself at home… (F5)

These spouses valued finding joy in times of adversity. When occupied by household work or childcare duties, outlets such as making housework cheerful, allocating time for oneself, and engaging in social activities served to alleviate stress. Notwithstanding, the spouses did not think it was appropriate for them to release illness-related issues to people outside the family.

Theme 5: compromise in sexual relationship

Most of the spouses shared that they had experienced compromise in their sexual relationship. Some couples tried to continue their sexual lives as before, but faced difficulties due to surgeries and medication side effects. Some who had infrequent sexual interactions prior to the illness did not experience a notable change. The former group shared their thoughts about sex openly.

F14 said, after the second chemo, we had it once, but she was bleeding… Frankly speaking, I was frightened…because she wanted to, we tried a second time, but I had developed a mental barrier…I feared she would be gone because of what I’d done…no, no…

F4 relayed, she was still not feeling good, she would tell me…then we could do less…it’s like one have to eat…I could only try to settle for second best…to see if it works out

They were generally well aware of the difficulties experienced by their wives and were willing to prioritize their wives’ conditions and needs. Faced with psychological concerns about sex, spouses were willing to reduce the frequency of, or eliminate, sex.

Spouses who were accustomed to rarely having sex noted their wives’ low sex drives, childcare demands, and family routines.

F15 said, because of being occupied by childcare, we did not do it, nothing has changed, just like before

F16 relayed, because we four people slept together [with two children], there was no sex of course…she did not want it, and I did not want to burden her…we didn’t do it often, so it does not bother me a great deal

This subset of spouses had become accustomed to infrequent sex before their wives became ill. They were considerate of their wives’ conditions, like the husbands who did experience a loss of sex, but they tended to minimize the importance of sexuality in their relationships.

Discussion

Main findings

The current study indicates that contextual factors play a central role in determining the extent of caregivers’ commitment. To our knowledge, this is one of few studies illustrating an impact of marriage-related constructs as an a priori factor on, rather than an outcome of, spousal caregiving. The present findings regarding the caregiving approaches of male spouses of Taiwanese BCD women were anchored in cultural values of marriage and gender role constructs. This study supports the notion that caregiving is culturally constructed experience.

The interviewed men perceived their commitment to caregiving as being consequent to their roles as husbands. Their construal of the husband’s role is rooted in Confucian thought. The hierarchical structure of marriage prescribes a division of roles within the family, associating men with earning a living, while women are expected to stay at home to manage household duties and caregiving [27]. Although a clear distribution of gender roles was evident in traditional Confucianism, modern Confucian scholars have advocated for the importance of equality and mutuality in marriage to foster each other’s moral development [28].

Interestingly, the three subgroups of spouses who had an intact marriage endorsed different components of Confucian teachings on couple relationships. The first subgroup, who observed social expectations of husbands and wives in a family, tended to perceive caregiving as assuming their wives’ duties while their wives were ill. These husbands were acting with a team mindset, filling a gap to maintain a normalcy in the family. The second subgroup, who viewed marriage as a life-long commitment to care for a loved one, underscored the virtuous nature of responsibility. This subgroup aligned with Confucian thought in viewing the family as the fundamental agent for cultivating virtues but deviated from its emphasis on hierarchical structure and designated roles. They regarded caregiving for a beloved wife as an essential duty of the husband, emphasizing lifelong commitment as the essence of the marital union. Consistent with the modern Confucian view [28], these spouses appeared to value social and behavioral norms that reflect relational equality and passionate love in marriage.

The two men in the third subgroup, who endorsed a patriarchal structure, appeared to perceive their responsibility for caring and their not abandoning their wives as benevolent deeds from superiors given to subordinate wife. Their caregiving was viewed in masculine terms wherein they are fulfilling a protector/provider role [19]. They exerted control over their wives’ reactions and had a limited tolerance for attending to their wives’ needs, instead emphasizing the expectation that their ill wives remain submissive—a duty traditionally assigned to a subordinate wife in traditional Confucian thought. This type of caregiving would weaken the reliability of their support.

A fourth subgroup was constituted by two men who were in weak marriages. They tended to engage in activities that they observed were left undone. Past research has documented a negative relationship between relational quality and a caregivers’ subjective burden, even when physical labor was involved [29, 30]. Our results extend past results by illustrating that the quality of a marital relationship prior to diagnosis affects the extent of caregiving that husbands engage in. One husband reported that he actively sought to identify the needs he could fulfill for his wife, even though his caregiving was not met with gratitude. We speculate that the lack of acknowledgment from the ill wife in this case was a message of rejecting the husband’s care. Accordingly, caregiving may be the product of a process of negotiation between the caregiver and the care recipient. In this way, women may prevent their husbands from being involved in ways the husbands would have wanted [24]. Further investigation is needed to clarify how care needs are negotiated and the associated effects on the adjustments of both the caregiver and patient.

Our data suggest that spouses served a stress-buffering function for their ill wives during periods of emotional turbulence and despair caused by their children. When their BCD wives succumbed to negative thinking, husbands reported engaging in positive reasoning, reassuring, and cheerful behaviors to comfort their wives. Similar to the findings of Ussher and Sandoval’s study [31], the men in this study indicated that they would put a positive spin on things and use humor to improve their wives’ moods.

Conversely, some husbands in our sample showed passivity and lacked confidence in responding to their wives’ emotions. Spouses adopting a passive attitude came across as being out of touch with their wives’ emotional needs and being inexperienced in providing comfort to their wives during emotional moments. Such findings aligned with the unmet needs and challenges faced by spouses caring for BCD wives [32]. The findings suggest that Taiwanese husbands may benefit from guidance that would enhance their sensitivity to their wives’ emotional needs and enable them to provide responsive emotional support to their BCD wives.

We found strengths and constraints in Taiwanese fathers’ guidance for children. Typically, they helped to ameliorate the stress of maternal illness on children through daily conversations. They used conversations about illness-related experiences to reframe the illness as a less pressing life event and to encourage a solution-oriented attitude in the face of adversity. When addressing maternal mortality, one father used a family story of similar experiences in hopes that the story would help the children in case their mother died of cancer. Fathers’ efforts to maintain normalcy in their children’s daily lives manifested the educative functions of the social environment advocated by guided participation [33]. Through participation in daily activities and family conversations structured by adults, children appropriate the meaning of the situation that is intended.

Spouses also reported taking on a peacemaker role in mother-child disputes. When ill wives were distressed by children’s responses, male spouses encouraged patients to view children’s naïve behaviors in light of developmental limitations. And they encouraged their children to be compassionate. The fact that the fathers in our study did not punish their children for such transgressions or demand compliance likely reflects a recent generational shift in Taiwan from an absolute-submission view of filial piety to one of mutual affection in the parent-child relationship [34]. Furthermore, this observation exemplifies a shift from the traditional disciplinary role of fathers in Confucian thought [35] to a mentoring role, where fathers strive to consider the child’s perspective, take their development into account, and respond with compassion.

On the other hand, Taiwanese fathers were constrained by a cultural belief that their young children were not mature enough to absorb sensitive information [36]. Such beliefs may prevent fathers from noticing their children’s psychological needs. Fathers’ observations of continuity in their children’s daily activities appeared to have led to a false belief that their children were not affected. Hence, the fathers in our sample did not report the same difficulties with informing children about issues of maternal illness that have been observed in Western literature [8, 9].

Our data demonstrated Taiwanese spouses’ engagement in protective buffering [37] (i.e., hiding cancer-related concerns and emotions from one’s spouse) by controlling their negative emotions and downplaying sexual concerns. Their efforts to suppress their feelings and allow lapses in emotional control only during private moments illustrate the extent of the emotional labor they have exerted, consistent with the elevated emotional stress observed in male caregivers in the United States [38].

Despite experiencing sexual difficulties due to their wives’ conditions [6], the spouses in our study prioritized their wives’ physical well-being and accepted a compromised situation of little to no sexual activity. Those who did not frequently have sex before the illness tended to minimize the importance of sexual intimacy in family life. Commonly, the husbands had a resigned or fearful mentality towards sex. Although such protective buffering is often considered costly and detrimental to the psychosocial adjustment of patient-caregiver dyads [37], it can help maintain patients’ confidence in coping with cancer [39] and is therefore seen as fulfilling husbands’ obligations to protect their wives within the Chinese cultural context [29].

Regarding emotional issues, our cohort tended to focus on tasks while keeping their stress to themselves, aligning with the masculine approach to problem-solving described in Western literature [18]. However, in contrast to the emotional stoicism often emphasized in traditional masculinity, the caregivers in our sample did not report feeling ashamed of their emotional reactions. They proactively engaged in emotional regulation strategies. Unlike male spouses in the United States, who may turn to friends or respite care for decompression [38], Taiwanese men preferred to keep caregiving a private family matter, rather than disclosing it to friends or seeking outside support [22, 23]. Their methods of emotional release included staying busy, reframing the illness as less serious, distracting themselves from unfavorable household tasks, and setting aside personal time after completing routine care duties. These strategies, which involve emotional detachment and cognitive reappraisal, have also been observed among Israeli family caregivers [40]. The findings reinforce a broader consensus on the benefits of emotional outlets in reducing caregivers’ stress and frustration [6, 38].

Revisiting masculinity in male caregiving experiences

The traditional approach to masculine caregiving in many Western societies is characterized by a problem-centered focus, a tendency to control situations, an emphasis on practical tasks, and a reluctance to seek social support [18]. Male caregivers often reframe their caregiving role in terms of being a provider or protector, aligning it with traditionally hegemonic masculine traits [19].

However, our findings on Taiwanese male caregiving reveal a pluralistic configuration of masculinity. Their approach to caregiving reflects how they perceive a husband’s role and obligations, which are influenced by different aspects of Confucian teachings on marriage. Only two spouses in our cohort endorsed a traditional masculine approach by stressing their provider role and requesting wives’ compliance and submissiveness in return. These two spouses overemphasized the superior status of a husband and the obedient duty of wife in Confucian teachings on the hierarchy structure in a marriage.

On the contrary, the majority of Taiwanese spouses in this cohort were more willing to incorporate caregiving into a husband’s obligations, though based on different appraisals of caregiving. While some spouses viewed caregiving as a way of sharing their wives’ duties (i.e., adhering to the traditional division of husband and wife roles in Confucian thought) and others as an inherent marital obligation (i.e., endorsing the fundamental value of marriage and emphasizing relational commitment and mutuality from a modern Confucian perspective), all invested time and effort in comforting their wives’ emotions, fulfilling a fatherly mentoring role, and providing protective buffering for their wives. Although their caregiving reflected a form of masculinity in their coping style, such as being task-oriented and withholding emotions, a strong sense of family ties and relational commitment was embedded throughout their caregiving experiences. They walk gently with their wives and children through challenging issues, demonstrating an understanding of their wives’ and children’s developmental needs. These spouses appeared to relinquish the superior status of a husband and set aside traditional masculinity. This approach supports the notion of caring masculinities, which refers to a masculine identity that excludes domination and adopts relational and interdependent qualities of care [20]. Caring masculinities suggest that male gender norms can be multidimensional, incorporating nurturing attitudes and a connection to the emotional aspects of family life [20, 21]. The caring masculinities observed in our cohort may reflect a shift from a hierarchical structure of marriage and parent-child relationships to a more egalitarian one in contemporary Taiwanese society.

In our cohort, both traditional masculine and caring masculinity styles of caregiving were identified, depending on the aspects of cultural norms and values that male spouses endorse. The traditional masculine style of caregiving is often observed in societies where male dominance and power are highly valued, as noted in Western literature [18]. On the other hand, the caring masculinities style of caregiving is more likely to be found in societies that advocate for gender equality such as those in Europe [20], and in cultures that emphasize familial duty and lifelong reciprocal obligations such as those in Asian countries or related ethnic groups [16, 41]. For the application of these findings to different cultural settings, we suggest two key points: (a) male spousal caregiving experiences are shaped by both their masculine identity and cultural norms regarding the husband’s role; and (b) since male caregivers may adopt specific aspects of cultural norms within a given culture, gaining a deeper understanding of why and how they perceive their caregiving can offer a broader cross-cultural perspective or a more nuanced subcultural view of male caregiving experiences.

The current study has several limitations. First, the male participants were recruited upon their wives’ requests rather than being approached independently. Thus, their participation may itself represent an act of support aligned with their wives’ wishes. Moreover, all except two of the couples reported being in good-quality relationships. As a result, this convenience sample may overrepresent BCD women with supportive spouses and underrepresent those experiencing marital strain. This recruitment bias toward intact, cooperative marriages likely affects the interpretation of findings, particularly the predominance of caring masculinity approaches in this cohort. The emphasis on family cohesion, relational commitment, and willingness to share caregiving duties may reflect the dynamics of supportive relationships, rather than broader patterns of male caregiving. In less harmonious relationships, such expressions of caring masculinity may be less common. Caution is warranted when applying these results to spouses in strained relationships or individuals in different family structures. For future studies, direct strategies for recruiting male spouses are recommended to ensure the inclusion of varying qualities of marital relationships and diverse family structures.

Second, the male participants were interviewed by a woman. Asian men are often socialized to be strong-willed and to suppress their inner feelings. As a result, this cohort of spouses may be reluctant to express emotions or show vulnerability, particularly when interacting with a female interviewer. They might downplay their levels of distress or the significance of the stressful events they have experienced. To address these challenges, the female interviewer in our study referenced her husband’s reactions within family dynamics, as well as stories from other men encountered in her professional work. These examples encouraged participants to reflect on and compare their own experiences. However, to better navigate potential communicative barriers related to gender—especially in the context of emotional vulnerability and culturally shaped expectations of masculinity—future studies may consider using male interviewers when interviewing male spouses. Gender-matched interviewers may be more effective in establishing rapport, fostering empathy, and achieving intersubjectivity, particularly in Asian contexts where cultural norms may inhibit open emotional expression.

Third, the recruited patients had been diagnosed within the past 3 to 23 months. Given that the caregiving burden is generally considered most intense during the first six months following a cancer diagnosis, the most demanding period for caregivers had passed for two-thirds of the participants. The timing of the interviews may have influenced the caregiving episodes recalled by the male spouses. Because most interviews took place after the intensive treatment phase, male participants may have filtered out earlier experiences related to acute medical demands, the sudden transition into caregiving roles, and the pressure of balancing work and family responsibilities.

Rather than focusing on the stressful and labor-intensive aspects of caregiving during the early stages of their wives’ illness, many spouses instead recalled significant moments that carried symbolic meaning—such as F8’s account of crying in the kitchen or F2’s reflection on not knowing how to comfort his wife. These emotionally charged memories may have felt more relevant or meaningful in hindsight, especially as their wives were in remission at the time of the interview. These recalled episodes were interpreted through the lens of meanings constructed in the current context, where caregiving was defined less by immediate stress or crisis and more by its emotional impact and the personal transformation it brought. As a result, this temporal distance may have contributed to the relative absence of caregiver burden themes in the data. For future studies, male spousal caregiving experiences should be investigated at clearly defined timepoints corresponding to the cancer trajectory, such as the intensive treatment phase (0–6 months post-diagnosis) and the survivorship phase (6–24 months). This phase-based approach can better capture the evolving nature of male spouses’ caregiving experiences across different stages of the illness.

Fourth, it is worth noting that three male spouses (F6, F13, and F17) had caregiving experiences that were not reflected in Themes 2 to 5. The main reason was that their accounts of caregiving diverged from the enactment of caregiving observed among other participants. Specifically, F6 and F13 tended to describe their involvement or companionship as an expression of a husband’s pride, whereas F17 acknowledged his limited ability to understand his wife’s needs. The essence of their experiences therefore differed from the common themes that focused on how spouses attentively responded to the needs of patients, children, and themselves. We thus incorporated the caregiving mindsets of F6 and F13 into Theme 1 and discussed them as representations of a traditional masculine approach to caregiving. Their enactment of care was consequently underrepresented in this study. To address this gap, future research could begin with a quantitative survey to assess spouses’ perceptions of husbands as caregivers, thereby identifying individuals who hold more traditional masculine beliefs. Focus group interviews could then provide an in-depth exploration of how such spouses enact caregiving in practice and how they negotiate the relationship between caregiving enactment and gender identity.

Relevance to clinical practice

Our findings indicate that male spousal caregiving experiences vary according to how men perceive and internalize their roles as husbands. In Asian cultural contexts, men often have limited capacities to manage both their wives’ and their own emotional distress. Furthermore, they are less likely to disclose family difficulties to outsiders; consequently, caregiving challenges tend to become private matters, with limited access to external support or resources. Therefore, we suggest that government health and welfare departments allocate funding and resources to establish supportive services for women with breast cancer and their caregiving spouses. These services should be initiated through referrals made by healthcare professionals after the start of breast cancer treatment. A multidisciplinary clinical team—comprising healthcare providers, counselors, social workers, and child psychologists—can work collaboratively to enhance the couple’s medical and psychosocial adjustment. Such services should not only offer caregiving guidance to male spouses to improve their caregiving skills, but also provide opportunities for couples to address marital relationships, men’s perceptions of the caregiving role, dyadic communication regarding caregiving needs, and strategies for managing emotional challenges. Because these services are introduced through trusted medical institutions and healthcare professionals, they are more likely to gain the couple’s confidence and increase their motivation to participate.

Clinical interventions can incorporate reflective practices to help these men examine the meaning of being a husband within the context of their cultural beliefs. Health professionals can support male spouses in need by connecting them with other men who share similar views on the husband’s role in marriage and caregiving. Providing opportunities for these men to share their experiences can be valuable, as peer support from those on similar paths is particularly beneficial for men who are less inclined to discuss family issues openly.

Conclusions

The caregiving of BCD women by their spouses is influenced by contextual factors, including cultural values and masculine ideology. This study identified two distinct masculine approaches to caregiving. For men who adhered to a hierarchical view of marriage, caregiving reflected a traditional masculine style, emphasizing the husband’s role as a provider and the wife’s submissiveness. In contrast, a caring masculine approach was observed in men who perceived caregiving as a partnership and who valued relational compassion in marriage. Spouses who embodied a caring masculinity tended to reduce stress for their BCD wives and children, maintained family normalcy, and provided protective buffering while managing their own negative emotions. This study supports Connell’s [15] concept of plural masculinities, demonstrating that multiple forms of masculinity can coexist within a given culture. The two approaches identified in this study differed in the cultural norms that male caregivers adopted regarding the husband’s role, which in turn influenced their caregiving motives and actions, ultimately shaping their overall caregiving experiences.
