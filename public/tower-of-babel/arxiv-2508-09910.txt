Joint moments of characteristic polynomials from the orthogonal and unitary symplectic groups
THEODOROS ASSIOTIS, MUSTAFA ALPER GUNES, JONATHAN P. KEATING and FEI WEI

Abstract
We establish asymptotic formulae for general joint moments of characteristic polynomials and their higher-order derivatives associated with matrices drawn randomly from the groups USp ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) \mathrm{USp}(2N) and SO ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) \mathrm{SO}(2N) in the limit as N → ∞ N\to\infty . This relates the leading-order asymptotic contribution in each case to averages over the Laguerre ensemble of random matrices. We uncover an exact connection between these joint moments and a solution of the σ \sigma -Painlevé V equation, valid for finite matrix size, as well as a connection between the leading-order asymptotic term and a solution of the σ \sigma -Painlevé III ′ equation in the limit as N → ∞ N\rightarrow\infty . These connections enable us to derive exact formulae for joint moments for finite matrix size and for the joint moments of certain random variables arising from the Bessel point process in a recursive way. As an application, we provide a positive answer to a question proposed by Altuğ et al. [Quarterly Journal of Mathematics, 65 (2014), 1111–1125].

1.1

Background and motivation

In this paper we address the problem of calculating joint moments of the characteristic polynomials, along with their higher-order derivatives, of random matrices drawn from the unitary symplectic
\mathrm{USp}(2N)

Problem 1.1

This ensures that, after evaluation, the contribution from the aforementioned extra terms is zero. A more precise sketch of these strategies is provided in Section

Characteristic polynomials and Painlevé equations

The work of most relevance to the setting herein is

and denoted therein by

given by:

solves the

\left(t\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\tau_{a,b}(t)\right)^{2}+4\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\tau_{a,b}(t)\right)^{2}\left(t\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\tau_{a,b}(t)-\tau_{a,b}(t)\right)-\left(a\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\tau_{a,b}(t)+1\right)^{2}=0,

(5)

for any

, along with the initial conditions:

\begin{cases}\tau_{a,b}(0)=0,&\text{for}\ a>0,\\
&\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\tau_{a,b}(t)\Bigr\rvert_{t=0}=-\frac{1}{a},&\text{for}\ a>1.\end{cases}

Here, we note that for the derivatives to exist at

) does not depend on

whose coefficients are polynomials of

In the proof of the above theorem, we provide a recursive method to obtain explicit formulae for the polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{m}^{(a)}(n_{2},\ldots,n_{k};t_{1})
. Furthermore, as mentioned earlier, the function
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-t_{1}\mathfrak{e}_{1}(a)}\right]
can be written in terms of a solution to the

-Painlevé III
′

, which, when combined with Theorem

, also allowing one to express them efficiently as rational functions of

.
We give a few examples of these formulae in the corollary below. These expressions, in spite of their brevity, appear to be new, and at least to our knowledge, are not mere consequences of the probabilistic representations the random variables
\{\mathfrak{p}_{m}(a)\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
admit.

Corollary 1.7
.

Let
t\geq 0
. For

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathfrak{e}_{1}(a)}\mathfrak{p}_{2}(a)]=\frac{a}{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathfrak{e}_{1}(a)}]+\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathfrak{e}_{1}(a)}]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathfrak{e}_{1}(a)}\mathfrak{p}_{2}(a)^{2}]=\frac{a^{2}+2}{t^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathfrak{e}_{1}(a)}]+\frac{-3a^{2}+2at}{t^{3}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathfrak{e}_{1}(a)}]+\frac{t-3a}{t^{3}}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathfrak{e}_{1}(a)}].

The formulae above may appear singular at

the moments of
\mathfrak{e}_{1}(a)=\mathfrak{p}_{1}(a)

and
\mathfrak{p}_{2}(a)

satisfy relations that ensure cancellations of the singular terms in the denominator.

As an application of Corollary

1.3.2
A question of Altuğ, Bettin, Petrow, Rishikesh, and Whitehead

Finally, as an application of the above results concerning the connections between the joint moments of the random variables
\mathfrak{e}_{k}(a)
and integrable systems, we provide an affirmative answer to a question of Altuğ, Bettin, Petrow, Rishikesh, and Whitehead, as addressed in the paragraph below
[

In the following, we give the necessary background and explicitly formulate the question, which one can also ask for various other compact matrix groups
(G(N))_{N=1}^{\infty}
. In
[
1
]
, the authors investigated the asymptotic formulae for the moments of the derivatives of characteristic polynomials over the groups
\mathrm{USp}(2N)
,
\mathrm{SO}(2N)
and
\mathrm{O}^{-}(2N)
. To be more precise, they showed that for any

, the leading-order coefficients of

\displaystyle M_{s}(G(N),k)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\int_{G(N)}\left(\phi_{\mathbf{A}}^{(k)}(1)\right)^{s}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(\mathbf{A}),

(9)

admit expressions in terms of the derivatives of

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{s,l}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\det_{1\leq i,j\leq s}\Big(f_{2i-j+l}(t)\Big)

(10)

with respect to
t
at zero for the following cases: for

and

Here, for

\displaystyle f_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi{\textnormal{i}}}\oint_{|w|=1}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{w+\frac{t}{w^{2}}}}{w^{n+1}}\mathrm{d}w=\frac{1}{\Gamma(n+1)}{}_{0}F_{2}\left(;\frac{n}{2}+1,\frac{n+1}{2};\frac{t}{4}\right)

is the hypergeometric function. These results were generalized by Andrade and Best in
[

to arbitrary

(or, more generally, for joint moments of
k_{1}
-th and
k_{2}
-th derivatives), in terms of the derivatives (with respect to
t
at zero) of

\displaystyle\det_{1\leq i,j\leq s}\Big(f_{2i-j+2l_{i}}(t)\Big)

(11)

for certain non-negative integers

. The approaches used in
[
1
]
and

are based on the shifted moment formulae of Conrey, Farmer, Keating, Rubinstein, and Snaith in
[

[

by Conrey, Rubinstein, and Snaith. As explained in
[
1
]

in terms of the derivatives (with respect to
t

\displaystyle\det_{1\leq i,j\leq s}\Big(I_{i+j-1}(2\sqrt{t})\Big)

in the case where

is the modified Bessel function of the first kind. In

. The Hankel determinant (

.

(Altuğ et al.

Does there exist a differential equation that plays the analogous role for symplectic and orthogonal types as the

As a direct consequence of our results we provide a positive answer to this question by confirming that a suitable choice of differential equation is the

Corollary 1.12

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{M_{s}(\mathrm{USp}(2N),k)}{N^{\frac{s^{2}+s}{2}+ks}}=\frac{2^{\frac{s^{2}}{2}}G(1+s)\sqrt{\Gamma(1+s)}}{\sqrt{G(1+2s)\Gamma(1+2s)}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu_{1}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mu_{s}}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}^{(2)}}\frac{(k!)^{s}2^{-2\sum_{j=1}^{s}\theta(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j})}}{\prod_{j=1}^{s}(l(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j})-\theta(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}))!}\prod_{j=1}^{s}b_{\theta(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j})}\left(s+\frac{1}{2}\right)

[

Convergence of joint moments of power sums

We present a quick argument that takes as input convergence of points along with some uniform integrability and gives joint convergence in distribution and of appropriate joint moments for an arbitrary number of power sums. We only apply this to the hard-edge rescaled Jacobi ensemble in Section
2.1
but it could be of independent interest.

The general setting is as follows. Suppose we are given sequences of random variables
(\lambda_{j,N})_{1\leq j\leq N}
, for
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and
(\gamma_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
such that:

•

For every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we have, almost surely,

0<\lambda_{1,N}<\lambda_{2,N}<\cdots<\lambda_{N,N},

and almost surely
0<\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}<\gamma_{3}<\cdots
.

•

For every fixed
l\in\mathbb{N}
, as
N\to\infty
, we have,

\left(\lambda_{1,N},\ldots,\lambda_{l,N}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{l}).

(13)

Abusing notation we will always write
\mathbb{P}
for the underlying laws of these random variables, even if not necessarily defined on the same probability space, and
\mathbb{E}
for the corresponding expectations. Finaly define, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the negative power sums:

\displaystyle M_{N}^{(k)}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j,N}^{k}},\ \ M^{(k)}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma_{j}^{k}}.

Observe that, the random variables
M^{(k)}
are well-defined but without further assumptions, in principle, could be infinite.

Proposition 2.1
.

In the setting described above, suppose

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[M_{N}^{(1)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[M^{(1)}\right]<\infty.

(14)

Then, for a fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
, as
N\to\infty
, we have:

\left(M_{N}^{(1)},\ldots,M_{N}^{(m)}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\left(M^{(1)},\ldots,M^{(m)}\right).

Moreover, if we further have that

\sup_{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\big(M_{N}^{(1)}\big)^{r}\right]<\infty,

(15)

for some
r\geq 1
, and
h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}\in[0,\infty)
are such that
\sum_{k=1}^{m}kh_{k}<r
, then we also have convergence of joint moments:

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{m}\left(M_{N}^{(k)}\right)^{h_{k}}\right]\longrightarrow\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{m}\left(M^{(k)}\right)^{h_{k}}\right]<\infty.

(16)

The strategy to show convergence of joint moments will be to split each
M_{N}^{(k)}
into separate contributions, with the first, and also dominating term having the desired convergence properties, so that the final result is obtained by a limiting procedure. With this goal in mind, for fixed
l\in\mathbb{N}
, we define:

\mathsf{Z}_{N,l}^{(k)}=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j,N}^{k}},\quad\mathsf{Y}_{N,l}^{(k)}=\sum_{j=l+1}^{N}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j,N}^{k}},

and their infinite counterparts:

\mathsf{Z}_{l}^{(k)}=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\frac{1}{\gamma_{j}^{k}},\quad\mathsf{Y}_{l}^{(k)}=\sum_{j=l+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma_{j}^{k}},

so that
M_{N}^{(k)}=\mathsf{Z}_{N,l}^{(k)}+\mathsf{Y}_{N,l}^{(k)}
and
M^{(k)}=\mathsf{Z}_{l}^{(k)}+\mathsf{Y}_{l}^{(k)}
for each
l,N\in\mathbb{N}
. Throughout this section we also denote
Q=\sum_{k}kh_{k}
whenever the choice of
h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}\in[0,\infty)
is clear. We begin with the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
2.1
, we have that for each fixed
l\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathsf{Y}_{N,l}^{(1)}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Y}_{l}^{(1)}

in distribution as
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

First observe that for each
l\in\mathbb{N}
, the sequence
\{\mathsf{Y}_{N,l}^{(1)}\}_{N\geq 1}
is tight due to the assumption in (
14
). In particular, we have that every subsequence has a further subsequence
\{\mathsf{Y}_{N_{j},l}^{(1)}\}_{j\geq 1}
that converges in distribution to a random variable
\mathsf{T}_{l}
.
Hence, by virtue of the Portmanteau lemma, we have that for each
l,m\in\mathbb{N}
with
l\leq m
and
t\in(0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{T}_{l}\geq t\right)\geq\limsup_{j\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{Y}_{N_{j},l}^{(1)}\geq t\right)\geq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=l+1}^{m}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i,N_{j}}}>t\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=l+1}^{m}\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}}>t\right).

Next, taking
m\to\infty
by means of monotone convergence theorem, we see that

\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{T}_{l}\geq t)\geq\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{Y}_{l}^{(1)}>t).

(17)

Now, observe that immediately from the assumptions, we have
\mathsf{Z}_{N,l}^{(1)}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Z}_{l}^{(1)}
in distribution as
N\to\infty
. Hence, using (
14
) and Fatou’s lemma, we obtain

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{Y}_{l,N}^{(1)}\right]=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\bigg\{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{N}^{(1)}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{Z}_{l,N}^{(1)}\right]\bigg\}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[M^{(1)}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{Z}_{l}^{(1)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{Y}_{l}^{(1)}\right].

In particular, using the above gives, upon integrating both sides of (
17
) over
t\in(0,\infty)
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{T}_{l}\geq t)\mathrm{d}t=\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{T}_{l}]\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{Y}_{l,N_{j}}^{(1)}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{Y}_{l}^{(1)}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{Y}_{l}^{(1)}>t)\mathrm{d}t,

where for the first inequality we again use Fatou’s lemma. Therefore, we therefore must have that

\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{T}_{l}\geq t)=\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{Y}_{l}^{(1)}>t)

(18)

for almost every
t\in(0,\infty)
. But then the function
t\mapsto\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{T}_{l}\geq t)
can have countably many discontinuities, so that
\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{T}_{l}>t)=\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{Y}_{l}^{(1)}>t)
for almost every
t\in(0,\infty)
, hence showing that
\mathsf{T}_{l}
must have the same law as
\mathsf{Y}_{l}^{(1)}
. In particular, we conclude that every subsequence of
\{\mathsf{Y}_{l,N}^{(1)}\}_{N\geq 1}
has a further subsequence that converges in law to
\mathsf{Y}_{l}^{(1)}
, which then implies the desired convergence for the full sequence. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Observe that immediately from the lemma above with
l=1
, we have the desired distributional convergence for
M_{N}^{(1)}
. To deal with
M_{N}^{(k)}
with
k\geq 2
, we proceed with the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

With
M_{N}^{(k)}
,
\mathsf{Z}_{N,l}^{(k)}
, and
\mathsf{Y}_{N,l}^{(k)}
defined as above, we have

\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{m}\left(M_{N}^{(k)}\right)^{h_{k}}\right]\right)^{1/S}\leq\sum_{J\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k\in J}\left(\mathsf{Z}_{N,l}^{(k)}\right)^{h_{k}}\prod_{k\notin J}\left(\mathsf{Y}_{N,l}^{(k)}\right)^{h_{k}}\right]\right)^{1/S},

(19)

where
S\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}h_{k}
, and the sum is over all subsets of
\{1,\ldots,m\}
including
\emptyset
.

Proof.

Define, for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
,
p_{k}=\frac{h_{k}}{S}
. It is easy to see that

\left(M_{N}^{(k)}\right)^{h_{k}}=\Bigl[\bigl(\mathsf{Z}_{N,l}^{(k)}+\mathsf{Y}_{N,l}^{(k)}\bigr)^{p_{k}}\Bigr]^{S}\leq\Bigl[\bigl(\mathsf{Z}_{N,l}^{(k)}\bigr)^{p_{k}}+\bigl(\mathsf{Y}_{N,l}^{(k)}\bigr)^{p_{k}}\Bigr]^{S}.

The result now follows by taking products over
k=1,\dots,m
, expanding the product, and using the triangle inequality for
\mathrm{L}^{S}\big(\mu_{N}^{(a,b)}\big)
.
∎

In what follows, for ease of notation, we define, for each
J\subset\{1,\ldots,m\}
and
l\in\mathbb{N}
, the averages

T_{N,J}^{(l)}=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k\in J}\left(\mathsf{Z}_{N,l}^{(k)}\right)^{h_{k}}\prod_{k\notin J}\left(\mathsf{Y}_{N,l}^{(k)}\right)^{h_{k}}\right].

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

We start by proving the convergence of joint moments. That is, we fix
r\geq 1
as in the statement of the result (note that the first moments are always uniformly bounded under the assumption (
14
)), and show that whenever
h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}\in[0,\infty)
are such that
Q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}kh_{k}<r
, we have the convergence given in (
16
). We will then show that this implies distributional convergence, again heavily exploiting positivity. To begin the proof, we first claim that

\mathsf{T}_{N,\{1,\ldots,m\}}^{(l)}\longrightarrow\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{l}\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}^{k}}\right)^{h_{k}}\right]

(20)

as
N\to\infty
. Indeed, by the assumption in (
13
), we have

\prod_{k=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{l}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i,N}^{k}}\right)^{h_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\prod_{k=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{l}\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}^{k}}\right)^{h_{k}}.

Combining this with the uniform integrability given by applying concavity of
t\mapsto t^{1/k}
to each multiplicand, and making use of the assumption (
15
), we get the convergence of averages. Moving on, note once again utilizing positivity, we have

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{m}(M_{N}^{(k)})^{h_{k}}\right]\geq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{l}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i,N}^{k}}\right)^{h_{k}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{l}\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}^{k}}\right)^{h_{k}}\right].

(21)

Now, taking
l\to\infty
on the right hand side gives that

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{m}(M_{N}^{(k)})^{h_{k}}\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{m}(M^{(k)})^{h_{k}}\right].

For the upper bound, we first claim that whenever
J\neq\{1,\ldots,m\}
,

\limsup_{l\to\infty}\limsup_{N\to\infty}T_{N,J}^{(l)}=0.

Indeed, applying Holder’s inequality with exponents
\frac{Q}{kh_{k}}
on the term corresponding to
k\in J
, we see that

T_{N,J}^{(l)}\leq\prod_{k\in J}\mathbb{E}\bigg[(\mathsf{Z}_{N,l}^{(k)})^{Q/k}\bigg]^{\frac{kh_{k}}{Q}}\prod_{k\notin J}\mathbb{E}\bigg[(\mathsf{Y}_{N,l}^{(k)})^{Q/k}\bigg]^{\frac{kh_{k}}{Q}}.

The claim now follows by observing that for each
k
, we have
(\mathsf{Z}_{N,l}^{(k)})^{Q/k}\leq(\mathsf{Z}_{N,l}^{(1)})^{Q}
and
(\mathsf{Y}_{N,l}^{(k)})^{Q/k}\leq(\mathsf{Y}_{N,l}^{(1)})^{Q}
, and also that by means of Lemma
2.2
along with assumption (
15
),

\limsup_{l\to\infty}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[(\mathsf{Y}_{N,l}^{(k)})^{Q/k}\bigg]\leq\limsup_{l\to\infty}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[(\mathsf{Y}_{N,l}^{(1)})^{Q}]=\limsup_{l\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathsf{Y}_{l}^{(1)})^{Q}\right]=0.

Hence, combining this with (
20
) and (
21
) we get the convergence of joint moments. For the joint convergence in law, pick, under the given assumptions,
h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
Q=\sum_{k}kh_{k}<r
.
First, note that by a combination of Markov’s inequality and Prokhorov’s theorem, the random vectors

\mathsf{X}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left((M_{N}^{(1)})^{h_{1}},\ldots,(M_{N}^{(m)})^{h_{m}}\right)

form a tight family as
N
ranges over
\mathbb{N}
. In particular, every subsequence of
\{\mathsf{X}_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
has a further subsequence which converges in distribution. Next, we claim that all of these subsequential limits are identical. Indeed, consider an arbitrary subsequence
\{\mathsf{X}_{N_{j}}\}_{j\geq 1}
, and let
(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m})
be the limiting random vector. Then, for each
l\in\mathbb{N}
, by the Portmanteau lemma, one has:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{1}\geq t_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}\geq t_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{j\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left((M_{N_{j}}^{(1)})^{h_{1}}\geq t_{1},\ldots,(M_{N_{j}}^{(m)})^{h_{m}}\geq t_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left((\mathsf{Z}_{{N_{j}},l}^{(1)})^{h_{1}}>t_{1},\ldots,(\mathsf{Z}_{{N_{j}},l}^{(m)})^{h_{m}}>t_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left((\mathsf{Z}_{l}^{(1)})^{h_{1}}>t_{1},\ldots,(\mathsf{Z}_{l}^{(m)})^{h_{m}}>t_{m}\right).

Next, noting that each
\mathsf{Z}_{l}^{(k)}
is monotonically increasing in
l
, we see that by the monotone convergence theorem,

\lim_{l\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left((\mathsf{Z}_{l}^{(1)})^{h_{1}}>t_{1},\ldots,(\mathsf{Z}_{l}^{(m)})^{h_{m}}>t_{m}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left((M^{(1)})^{h_{1}}>t_{1},\ldots,(M^{(m)})^{h_{m}}>t_{m}\right).

Hence, we have shown that

\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\geq t_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}\geq t_{m})\geq\mathbb{P}\left((M^{(1)})^{h_{1}}>t_{1},\ldots,(M^{(m)})^{h_{m}}>t_{m}\right).

(22)

Next, we show that integrating both sides of this inequality leads to the same value. Indeed, using the first part of the proof, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}}\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\geq t_{1},\ldots,

\displaystyle Y_{m}\geq t_{m})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}=\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{1}\cdots Y_{m}\right]=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[(M_{N}^{(1)})^{h_{1}}\cdots(M_{N}^{(m)})^{h_{m}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(M^{(1)})^{h_{1}}\cdots(M^{(m)})^{h_{m}}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}}\mathbb{P}\left((M^{(1)})^{h_{1}}>t_{1},\ldots,(M^{(m)})^{h_{m}}>t_{m}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}.

In particular, it must be the case that equality is achieved in (
22
) for almost every

\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
. Hence, arguing as in the final paragraph of the proof of Lemma
2.2
, we arrive at the distributional equality

(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}((M^{(1)})^{h_{1}},\ldots,(M^{(m)})^{h_{m}}).

Thus, we have shown that every subsequence of
\{\mathsf{X}_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
has a further subsequence that converges in law to
((M^{(1)})^{h_{1}},\ldots,(M^{(m)})^{h_{m}})
, and hence the whole sequence must converge in distribution to the same vector, which completes the proof.
∎

2.1

Application to Jacobi ensemble

We apply Proposition
2.1
to the hard-edge rescaled Jacobi ensemble. The Jacobi ensemble is defined as follows
[
34
]
.

Definition 2.4
.

The Jacobi ensemble
\mu_{N}^{(a,b)}
with parameters
a,b>-1
is the probability measure on
\mathbb{W}_{N}
given by

\mu_{N}^{(a,b)}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{a,b}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{a}(1-x_{j})^{b}\mathbf{1}_{x_{j}\in[0,1]}\prod_{i<j}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

where the normalisation constant is given by the following formula,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{a,b}=\frac{1}{N!}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\Gamma(a+j)\Gamma(b+j)\Gamma(1+j)}{\Gamma(a+b+N+j)\Gamma(2)}.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define the negative power sums

\mathsf{M}_{N}^{(k)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\big(\lambda_{j,N}\big)^{k}},

where
\lambda_{j,N}=N^{2}\mathsf{x}_{j,N}
, and
\big(\mathsf{x}_{1,N},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N,N}\big)
are distributed according to
\mu_{N}^{(a,b)}
. The
N^{2}
-scaling here is the so-called hard-edge scaling for the Jacobi ensemble. Note that
\mathsf{M}_{N}^{(k)}
depends on
a
and
b
but we suppress it from the notation. We also define their Bessel analogues:

\mathsf{M}^{(k)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\big(\mathfrak{B}_{a}^{j}\big)^{k}}.

Note that
\mathsf{M}^{(k)}
depends on
a
(there is no parameter
b
here) but we suppress it from the notation. We then have the following result, where here, and throughout the rest of the paper, for any positive real number
x
, we define

\lfloor x\rfloor^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}\lfloor x\rfloor,&x\notin\mathbb{Z},\\
x-1,&x\in\mathbb{Z}.\end{cases}

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
a>0
,
b>-1
, and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, we have

\big(\mathsf{M}_{N}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathsf{M}_{N}^{(m)}\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\big(\mathsf{M}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathsf{M}^{(m)}\big),

as
N\to\infty
, where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{\longrightarrow}}
denotes convergence in distribution. Moreover, for
h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, with
\sum_{k=1}^{m}kh_{k}<\lfloor a+1\rfloor^{\prime}
we have convergence of moments:

\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(a,b)}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{m}(\mathsf{M}_{N}^{(k)})^{h_{k}}\right]\longrightarrow\mathbb{E}^{(a)}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{m}(\mathsf{M}^{(k)})^{h_{k}}\right]<\infty,\ \ \mathrm{as}\ N\to\infty.

Proof.

The result will follow as a special case of Proposition
2.1
, once the conditions therein are verified. Firstly, the fact that convergence in (
13
) is guaranteed for
(\lambda_{1,N},\ldots,\lambda_{l,N})=(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{l}^{(N)})
is well-known, even for the Jacobi
\beta
-ensemble, see
[
42
,
51
]
. As for the moment assumptions, we first note that under the given conditions on the parameters, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(a,b)}\Big[\mathsf{M}_{N}^{(1)}\Big]=\frac{1}{a}=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{M}^{(1)}\big],

where the first equality follows from Aomoto’s integral formula (see, for instance,
[
32
]
), and the second equality is seen by first writing the average as the one-dimensional integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-1}\mathsf{K}_{a}^{\mathrm{Bes}}(x,x)\mathrm{d}x
, thanks to the determinantal property of the process
\{\mathfrak{B}_{a}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
, and computing this integral explicitly using formulae for Bessel functions (a roundabout way to obtain the desired formula would be to combine results of
[
9
,
5
]
). Finally, as a consequence of
[
35
, Proposition 4.1]
, we get that whenever
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
with
\alpha<a+1
, we have

\sup_{N}\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(a,b)}\Big[\big(\mathsf{M}_{N}^{(1)}\big)^{\alpha}\Big]<\infty,

so that the result now follows from an application of Proposition
2.1
with
r
being the largest such
\alpha
.
∎

Proofs of Theorem
1.3
and Proposition
1.4

The main goal of this section will be to prove Theorem
1.3
. Towards this end recall that, for a Haar-distributed random matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathrm{USp}(2N)
with eigenvalues
\mathrm{e}^{\pm{\textnormal{i}}\theta_{1}},\ldots,\mathrm{e}^{\pm{\textnormal{i}}\theta_{N}}
, the density of the eigenangles
(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{N})\in[0,\pi)
is given by

\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{\mathrm{USp}}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\left(\cos(\theta_{k})-\cos(\theta_{j})\right)^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\sin^{2}(\theta_{j})\mathrm{d}\theta_{j},

for an explicit normalisation constant
\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{\mathrm{USp}}
, see
[
34
]
. Similarly, the density of the eigenangles
(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{N})\in[0,\pi)
for
\mathbf{A}\in\mathrm{SO}(2N)
is given by:

\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{\mathrm{SO}}}\prod\limits_{1\leq k<j\leq N}\left(\cos\left(\theta_{k}\right)-\cos\left(\theta_{j}\right)\right)^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{d}{\theta}_{j},

for an explicit normalisation constant
\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{\mathrm{SO}}
, see
[
34
]
. Our starting point will be the following formula.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose
h_{0},\ldots,h_{m}\in[0,\infty)
. Let
s=\sum_{i=0}^{m}h_{i}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}^{\mathrm{USp}}_{N}(h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{m})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{J}^{\mathrm{USp}}_{N}(s,0,\ldots,0)\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(s+\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{m}(\mathfrak{R}_{N,k}(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)}))^{h_{k}}\right],

\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}^{\mathrm{SO}}_{N}(h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{m})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{J}^{\mathrm{SO}}_{N}(s,0,\ldots,0)\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(s-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2})}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{m}(\mathfrak{R}_{N,k}(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)}))^{h_{k}}\right],

where for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathfrak{R}_{N,k}
is defined by

\mathfrak{R}_{N,k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\dots,n_{N}\geq 0\\
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{N}=k\end{subarray}}\binom{k}{n_{1},\dots,n_{N}}\,\prod_{j:\,n_{j}\geq 2}\left(1+\frac{2^{n_{j}}-2}{4x_{j}}\right).\quad

Proof.

We first claim that for any
k\geq 1
, we have

\frac{\varphi_{\mathbf{A}}^{(k)}(0)}{\varphi_{\mathbf{A}}(0)}=(-\mathrm{i})^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\dots,n_{N}\geq 0\\
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{N}=k\end{subarray}}\binom{k}{n_{1},\dots,n_{N}}\,\prod_{j:\,n_{j}\geq 2}\left(1+\frac{2^{n_{j}}-2}{4x_{j}}\right),\quad\textrm{with }x_{j}=\frac{1}{2}(1-\cos\theta_{j}).

Indeed, writing

\varphi_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\varphi_{j}(\theta),

where we define

\varphi_{j}(\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\big(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\theta-\theta_{j})}\big)\big(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\theta+\theta_{j})}\big)=1-2\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}\cos\theta_{j}+\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\theta},

we easily see that

\varphi_{j}^{(n)}(0)=\begin{cases}2\,(1-\cos\theta_{j}),&n=0,\\[2.84526pt]
(-\mathrm{i})^{n}\Bigl[2^{n}-2\cos\theta_{j}\Bigr],&n\geq 1.\end{cases}

Hence, using the change of variables
x_{j}=(1-\cos(\theta_{j}))/2
, we see that for any
n\geq 1
,

\frac{\varphi_{j}^{(n)}(0)}{\varphi_{j}(0)}=(-\mathrm{i})^{n}\,\frac{2^{n}-2+4x_{j}}{4x_{j}}.

Next, one may differentiate repeatedly to compute:

\frac{\varphi^{(k)}_{\mathbf{A}}(0)}{\varphi_{\mathbf{A}}(0)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\dots,n_{N}\geq 0\\
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{N}=k\end{subarray}}\binom{k}{n_{1},\dots,n_{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\varphi_{j}^{(n_{j})}(0)}{\varphi_{j}(0)}.

From this the result follows by making the change of variables
x_{j}=\frac{1}{2}(1-\cos\theta_{j})
in the corresponding matrix integral.
∎

The main point in the formula above is that the highest contribution will come from sums of terms involving elementary symmetric polynomials in inverse points of
x_{j}
sampled according to
\mu_{N}^{(a,b)}
. To establish this, we first need the following proposition. Here we make use of the determinantal property of
\mu_{N}^{(a,b)}
. One could obtain some uniform boundedness for elementary symmetric polynomials by writing them in terms of power sums and using our previous arguments but this restricts the parameters for which such bounds exist and it is quite suboptimal.

Throughout the rest of this section we write
[N]=\{1,\ldots,N\}
for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
[N]^{(l)}
denote, for each
l,N\in\mathbb{N}
, the set of subsets of
[N]
of size
l
.

Proposition 3.2
.

For each
a>0
,
b\geq-\frac{1}{2}
we have

\sup_{N}\frac{1}{N^{2l\alpha}}\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(a,b)}\left[\mathrm{e}_{l}\left(\frac{1}{\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)}},\ldots,\frac{1}{\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]<\infty,

where
\alpha=\lfloor a+1\rfloor^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We first recall the determinantal property we need. Consider the so-called
l
-point density function:

\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N;l}^{(a,b)}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{l})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{a,b}}\int_{(x_{l+1},\ldots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N-l}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}(1-x_{j})^{a}x_{j}^{b}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}(x_{j}-x_{k})^{2}\mathrm{d}x_{l+1}\ldots\mathrm{d}x_{N}.

Using standard arguments, see, for instance,
[
34
, Proposition 5.1.2]
, this function can be written as:

\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N;l}^{(a,b)}\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{l}\right)=\frac{(N-l)!}{N!}\det\left[K_{N}^{(a,b)}(x_{i},x_{j})\right]_{i,j=1}^{l}.

(23)

The so-called correlation kernel,
K_{N}^{(a,b)}(x,y)
, appearing in the determinant is given by:

K_{N}^{(a,b)}(x,y)=\sqrt{w^{(a,b)}(x)w^{(a,b)}(y)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{P}_{j}^{(a,b)}(x)\mathcal{P}_{j}^{(a,b)}(y),

(24)

where the weight
w^{(a,b)}
is given by

w^{(a,b)}(x)=x^{a}(1-x)^{b},\ \ x\in[0,1],

and the
\big\{\mathcal{P}_{j}^{(a,b)}\big\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
are polynomials indexed by their degree defined by the orthonormality condition:

\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{P}_{j}^{(a,b)}(x)\mathcal{P}_{k}^{(a,b)}(x)w^{(a,b)}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\mathbf{1}_{j=k}.

Now, since
\alpha
is an integer, expanding, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(a,b)}\left[\mathrm{e}_{l}\left(\frac{1}{\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)}},\ldots,\frac{1}{\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]\leq C(\alpha)\sum_{\sum_{J\in[N]^{(l)}}m_{J}=\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(a,b)}

\displaystyle\left[\prod_{J\in[N]^{(l)}}\prod_{j\in J}\frac{1}{{\big(\mathsf{x}_{j}^{(N)}\big)}^{m_{J}}}\right]

\displaystyle=C(\alpha)\sum_{\sum_{J\in[N]^{(l)}}m_{J}=\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(a,b)}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\big(\mathsf{x}_{j}^{(N)}\big)^{\sum_{J:j\in J}m_{J}}}\right],

where
C(\alpha)
is a constant only depending on
\alpha
.
Next, define, for a fixed
(m_{J})_{J\in[N]^{(l)}}
, the sets

\displaystyle A=

\displaystyle\,\{j\in[N]\mid j\in J\textrm{ for some }J\textrm{ with }m_{J}\neq 0\},

\displaystyle A_{j}=

\displaystyle\,\{J\in[N]^{(l)}\,\mid j\in J,\,m_{J}\neq 0\},

and let
\kappa=|A|
. Then, observe that by the determinantal property (
23
), we have that

\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(a,b)}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\big(\mathsf{x}_{j}^{(N)}\big)^{\sum_{J:j\in J}m_{J}}}\right]=\frac{(N-\kappa)!}{N!}\int_{(y_{j})_{j\in A}\in\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}}\prod_{j\in A}\frac{1}{y_{j}^{\sum_{J\in A_{j}}m_{J}}}\det_{l,k\in A}\left[K_{N}^{(a,b)}(y_{l},y_{k})\right]\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

(25)

Next, note that applying Cauchy-Schwarz to (
24
) immediately gives

K_{N}^{(a,b)}(z,w)\leq\sqrt{K_{N}^{(a,b)}(z,z)}\sqrt{K_{N}^{(a,b)}(w,w)}.

Hence, expanding the determinant as a sum over the symmetric group and applying this inequality
we obtain that the average in (
25
) is bounded above by

\frac{C(\alpha)}{N^{\kappa}}\prod_{j\in A}\int\frac{1}{y^{\sum_{J\in A_{j}}m_{J}}}K_{N}^{(a,b)}(y,y)\mathrm{d}y\leq\frac{C(\alpha)}{N^{\kappa}}\prod_{j\in A}N^{2\sum_{j\in J}m_{J}}=C(\alpha)N^{2\alpha l-\kappa}

where for the inequality we used the bound

\int_{0}^{1}x^{-\theta}K_{N}^{(a,b)}(x,x)\mathrm{d}x\leq C(a,b,\theta)N^{2\theta}

that holds for all
1\leq\theta<a+1
(see the proof of Proposition 2.2 in
[
40
]
). Combining these observations, one then has

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}_{l}\bigg(\frac{1}{\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)}},\ldots,\frac{1}{\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)}}\bigg)^{\alpha}\right]\leq C(\alpha)\sum_{\sum_{J\in[N]^{(l)}}m_{J}=\alpha}N^{2\alpha l-\kappa((m_{J})_{J_{\in}[N]^{(l)}})}.

Note, then that for each fixed
\kappa=l,\ldots,\alpha l
, there are at most

C(\alpha,l)\,\binom{N}{\kappa}

choices of
(m_{J})_{J\in[N]^{(l)}}
that gives
\kappa=\kappa\big((m_{J})_{J_{\in}[N]^{(l)}}\big)
. This then gives the desired bound.
∎

Let

In this section, we establish a connection between the joint moments of

We begin by representing the left-hand side of formula (

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
N\geq 1

\displaystyle g_{m}(t_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\exp(-t_{1})U(b+1,-a-2N+2+m;t_{1}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(a,b)}\Bigg[\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{t_{1}}{\mathsf{x}_{j}^{(N)}}}\prod_{q=2}^{k}

Proof.

First, observe that by the definition of
\mathbb{E}_{N,2}^{(a,b)}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(a,b)}\Bigg[\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{t_{1}}{\mathsf{x}_{j}^{(N)}}}\prod_{q=2}^{k}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}

\displaystyle(\mathsf{x}_{j}^{(N)})^{-q}\Big)^{n_{q}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N!\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{a,b}}\int_{[0,1]^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{t_{1}}{x_{j}}}\prod_{q=2}^{k}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}(x_{j})^{-q}\Big)^{n_{q}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{a}(1-x_{j})^{b}\Delta^{2}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

We now replace the Vandermonde determinant above by the Vandemonde determinant of differential operators. To be more precise, defining

\displaystyle\Delta\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}w_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}w_{N}}\bigg)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}w_{i}}-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}w_{j}}\bigg)=\det_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{d}^{j-1}}{\mathrm{d}w_{i}^{j-1}}\bigg),

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38092pt\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(a,b)}\Bigg[\mathrm{e}^{-t_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\mathsf{x}_{j}^{(N)}}}\prod_{q=2}^{k}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\mathsf{x}_{j}^{(N)})^{-q}\Big)^{n_{q}}\Bigg]

(32)

\displaystyle\hskip-17.07182pt=

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\frac{1}{N!\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{(a,b)}}\Delta^{2}\big(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}w_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}w_{N}}\big)\underbrace{\int_{[0,1]^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{w_{j}}{x_{j}}}\prod_{q=2}^{k}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{-q}\Big)^{n_{q}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{a+2N-2}(1-x_{j})^{b}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}h(w_{1},\ldots,w_{N})}\Big|_{w_{1}=\cdots=w_{N}=t_{1}}

m=2,\ldots,k\end{subarray}}\prod_{m=2}^{k}\binom{n_{m}}{l_{m,1},\ldots,l_{m,N}}\int_{[0,1]^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{w_{j}}{x_{j}}}x_{j}^{-\sum_{m=2}^{k}ml_{m,j}}x_{j}^{a+2N-2}(1-x_{j})^{b}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}w_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}w_{N}}\right)h(w_{1},\ldots,w_{N})\Big|_{w_{1}=\ldots=w_{N}=t_{1}}

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N!(-1)^{\sum_{m=2}^{k}mn_{m}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sum_{j=1}^{N}l_{m,j}=n_{m}\\

where

\displaystyle f(w)=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{w}{x}}x^{a+2N-2}(1-x)^{b}\mathrm{d}x.

Note that for

\displaystyle f(w)=\mathrm{e}^{-w}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-wz}z^{b}(z+1)^{-a-2N-b}\mathrm{d}z=\Gamma(b+1)\mathrm{e}^{-w}U(b+1,-a-2N+2;w).

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}w^{m}}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{-w}U(b+1,-a-2N+2;w)\Big)=(-1)^{m}\mathrm{e}^{-w}U(b+1,-a-2N+2+m;w).

(34)

Combining these, the desired expression follows.

4.1

Ideas behind the proofs of Theorem
1.9
and Theorem
1.6

By Proposition
4.1
, to prove Theorem
1.9
, it suffices to show that (
4.1
), the linear combinations of determinants of the form
\det_{0\leq i,j\leq N-1}\Big(g_{i+j+m_{j}}(t_{1})\Big)
with the associated combinatorial coefficients, possess the required structure,
namely, that they can be expressed as linear combinations (with polynomial coefficients in
t_{1}

of suitable-order derivatives) of the Hankel determinant
\det_{0\leq i,j\leq N-1}\Big(g_{i+j}(t_{1})\Big)
.

Inspired by the idea used in the authors’ previous work
[
8
, Section 4]
, we tried lifting formula (
4.1
) to a setting involving partial derivatives of a ”large” Hankel determinant, whose entries are constructed as generating functions of
g_{n}(t_{1})
with respect to
t_{2},\ldots,t_{k}
, evaluated at
(0,\ldots,0)
. That is,

\displaystyle(\ref{combinatotrialexpression})=C_{N}^{(a,b)}\prod_{q=2}^{k}\frac{\partial^{n_{q}}}{\partial t_{q}^{n_{q}}}\Psi_{N}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\Bigg|_{t_{2}=\cdots=t_{k}=0},

where

\displaystyle\Psi_{N}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})=\det_{0\leq i,j\leq N-1}\left(\sum_{m_{2}=0}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{m_{k}=0}^{\infty}\frac{t_{2}^{m_{2}}\ldots t_{k}^{m_{k}}}{m_{2}!\ldots m_{k}!}g_{i+j+\sum_{q=2}^{k}qm_{q}}(t_{1})\right).

(40)

Unfortunately, the above construction fails due to the non-convergence of the infinite series defining the matrix entries. This divergence arises because, for fixed
t_{1}
,
g_{m}(t_{1})
grows as fast as
m!
as
m\rightarrow\infty
, according to (
30
). So we need to change the strategy.

To overcome this difficulty, we begin by studying a truncated version of the Hankel determinant appearing on the right-hand side of equation (
4.1
). Specifically, we consider

\displaystyle\Phi_{N}(R_{2},\ldots,R_{k};t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\det_{0\leq i,j\leq N-1}\left(\sum_{m_{2}=0}^{R_{2}}\cdots\sum_{m_{k}=0}^{R_{k}}\frac{t_{2}^{m_{2}}\ldots t_{k}^{m_{k}}}{m_{2}!\ldots m_{k}!}g_{i+j+\sum_{q=2}^{k}qm_{q}}(t_{1}),\right)

(41)

where the key difference is that the summation in each matrix entry is finite rather than infinite, and therefore, there is no issue of convergence.

The motivation behind this truncation approach is from the observation that, with appropriately chosen parameters
R_{2},\ldots,R_{k}
, the basic relation (
4.1
) is preserved. Specifically, for any
R_{q}\geq n_{q}

q=2,\ldots,k
,

\displaystyle(\ref{combinatotrialexpression})=C_{N}^{(a,b)}\prod_{q=2}^{k}\frac{\partial^{n_{q}}}{\partial t_{q}^{n_{q}}}\Phi_{N}(R_{2},\ldots,R_{k};t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\Big|_{t_{2}=\cdots=t_{k}=0}.

(42)

The price we pay for performing the truncation is that we lose the neat structure observed in the representation of the partial derivatives of the Hankel determinant (
41
) as shifted Hankel determinants, as was the case in our previous work
[
8
, Section 4.3]
. Here, ”neat” refers to the fact that in the aforementioned reference, the partial derivatives of (
40
) with respect to
t_{2},\ldots,t_{k}
could be fully expressed in terms of shifted Hankel determinants. In contrast, in our setting, the representation of the partial derivatives of (
41
) involves not only shifted Hankel determinants but also additional terms, which we refer to as
error terms
.

One of the main differences between this paper and
[
8
, Section 4.3]
is that we explicitly compute these error terms. This is carried out in Subsection
4.2
, where we express them as multivariate polynomials in
t_{2},\ldots,t_{k}
, with exponents determined by the parameters
R_{2},\ldots,R_{k}
, and coefficients given in terms of traces of certain matrices.

Another key difference between this section and
[
8
, Section 4.3]
lies in the evaluation of the representations obtained in Subsection
4.2
for the partial derivatives of the shifted version of (
41
) at
t_{2}=\cdots=t_{k}=0
. In our case, we must carefully choose appropriate values for
R_{2},\ldots,R_{k}
, depending on the order of the partial derivatives. On one hand, to ensure that the error terms vanish upon evaluation at this point; on the other hand, to ensure that the remaining terms in the representation have the analogue form as (
4.1
) after evaluation. These resulting quantities are the ones we shall process the induction. We provide the details of the above arguments in Subsection
4.3
.

Since the recursive formulas obtained in Subsection
4.3
share the same structure as those in
[
8
, Propositions 4.13 and 4.15]
, we can apply the inductive argument developed therein to prove Theorem
1.9
in Subsection
4.4
, as well as the limiting case (as
N\to\infty
) stated in Theorem
1.6
. It is worth noting that, although the expression for the coefficients in Theorem
1.9
(the finite
N
case) depends on both
a
and
b
, in the limiting case, as one would expect, the coefficients depend only on
a
. This is because the terms involving
b
do not contribute to the leading order as
N\to\infty
, details for which are provided in the proof of Theorem
1.6
.

\frac{1}{t^{l}}

for
l=1,\dots,\sum_{k=1}^{m}kn_{k}
vanish (which follows by again considering the asymptotic expansion at
t\sim 0
and noting that it has to be bounded due to Theorem
1.9
), we see that right-hand side of equation (
4.4
) must be a rational function in
a

of the form described above; and therefore, so does the left-hand side of equation (
4.4
). Hence, by Newton’s formula, we readily conclude that
\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(a,-\frac{1}{2}{})}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{m}\mathfrak{e}_{N,k}^{h_{k}}\right]
has the rational function form
\frac{Q_{2}(N;a)}{Q_{1}(a)}
,
for certain polynomials
Q_{1}(a)
and
Q_{2}(N;a)
in
a
,
whose coefficients of the powers of
a
in
Q_{2}(N;a)
are polynomials in
N
whenever
\Re(a)>\sum_{k=1}^{m}kh_{k}-1
. On the other hand, the map
a\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(a,-\frac{1}{2})}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{m}\mathfrak{e}_{N,k}^{h_{k}}\right]
, which is initially defined on a part of the real line, extends trivially to an analytic function on the set
\{a\in\mathbb{C}\mid\Re(a)>\sum_{k=1}^{m}h_{k}-1\}

Thus, by the identity theorem, it must equal

\frac{Q_{2}(N;a)}{Q_{1}(a)}
whenever
\Re(a)>\sum_{k=1}^{m}h_{k}-1
. Since
\sum_{k=0}^{m}h_{k}-\frac{1}{2}>\sum_{k=1}^{m}h_{k}-1
when
h_{0}\geq 0
,
\frac{Q_{2}(N;a)}{Q_{1}(a)}
is a rational function of
h_{0}
on all of
h_{0}\geq 0
. Note that, when
h_{0}>\frac{1}{2}
,
N^{-2\sum_{k=1}^{m}{kh_{k}}}\frac{Q_{2}(N;a)}{Q_{1}(a)}
converges as
N\rightarrow\infty
. Since this is a rational function, this convergence holds for any
h_{0}\geq 0
and the limit is a rational function of
h_{0}
, which is the desired claim.

To complete the proof, we observe that thanks to Proposition
1.4
, the function

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varphi}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R_{+}}

\displaystyle h_{0}

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{m}\mathfrak{e}_{k}\big(h_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}h_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\big)^{h_{k}}\right]

is well-defined, and rational in
h_{0}
on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, since all finite-dimensional averages taken with respect to the Laguerre ensembles therein can be reduced to finite linear combinations of ratios of
\Gamma
-functions, which simplify to rational functions. Combining this with the earlier claim, we see that under the given assumptions, both sides of (
2
) (modulo a common factor) define a rational function on
h_{0}\in[0,\infty)
. Moreover, by Theorem
1.3
, these two functions agree on
h_{0}>\frac{1}{2}
, and hence, they must agree everywhere. This completes the proof of the desired result.
∎

1.6
.

First, we claim that whenever

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2\sum_{m=2}^{k}mn_{m}}}\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(a,b)}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{t_{1}}{N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\mathsf{x}^{(N)}_{j}}}\prod_{m=2}^{k}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\mathsf{x}^{(N)}_{j})^{-m}\right)^{n_{m}}\right]
