Stevens–Johnson Syndrome and Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis: A Five-Year Experience at a Tertiary Referral Hospital in Somalia
Authors: Abdisalam Ibrahim Aden, Mohamed Mukhtar Mohamed, Ahmed Isse Ali, Ömer Metin, Mohamed Adan Odowaa, Hassan Ali Mumin, Mohamud Mohamed Abdulkadir, Yasir Khalif Ali, Fatima Mohamud Ahmed

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo describe the clinical characteristics, suspected drug triggers, management, and in-hospital outcomes of patients with Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS), SJS/toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) overlap, or TEN at a tertiary referral hospital in Somalia.Patients and MethodsThis retrospective descriptive study included 37 patients admitted from January 2020 through December 2025. Age was categorized as 1–18, 19–49, and ≥50 years. Final clinician-documented diagnoses, demographic and clinical characteristics, suspected drugs, first-admission laboratory classifications, documented treatments, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, complications, length of stay, and survival were summarized using counts and percentages. Standardized SCORTEN and ALDEN assessments were unavailable. Mortality modeling was not performed because only seven deaths occurred and the extracted data were insufficient for a stable multivariable model.ResultsOf 37 patients, five (13.5%) were aged 1–18 years, 18 (48.6%) were aged 19–49 years, and 14 (37.8%) were aged ≥50 years; 23 (62.2%) were female. Final clinician-documented diagnoses were TEN in 27 (73.0%), SJS/TEN overlap in six (16.2%), and SJS in four (10.8%). Laboratory findings indicated significant systemic involvement, including elevated inflammatory markers and liver enzymes. Prednisolone was widely used (94.6%), and more than half of patients received cyclosporine (56.8%), while IVIG use was minimal (2.7%). Nine patients (24.3%) required ICU admission. Because the study was small and retrospective and lacked standardized severity and drug-causality assessments, it cannot establish treatment effects or mortality predictors. Prospective multicenter studies using SCORTEN, ALDEN, standardized treatment documentation, and long-term follow-up are needed.Keywords: antibacterial agents, cyclosporine, drug causality, SCORTEN, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis

Introduction

Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) are rare but severe, life-threatening mucocutaneous reactions, most commonly triggered by medications. These conditions are characterized by widespread epidermal necrosis, mucosal involvement, and potentially serious systemic complications requiring urgent medical intervention. Based on the extent of detached or detachable epidermis, SJS involves less than 10% of body surface area, SJS/TEN overlap involves 10–30%, and TEN involves more than 30%.1,2

Although the incidence of SJS and TEN is low, the associated morbidity and mortality remain substantial, particularly in patients with extensive skin detachment and multiorgan involvement.3 Mortality is generally higher in TEN than in SJS, and survivors may experience long-term complications affecting multiple organ systems.3,4 Several drug classes have been implicated, including antibiotics, antiepileptic agents, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and allopurinol.3 Prompt recognition, immediate withdrawal of possible culprit drugs, and meticulous supportive care are central to management.2,4

Despite advances in supportive care, the optimal systemic treatment of SJS/TEN remains unsettled. Systemic corticosteroids, cyclosporine, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and other immunomodulatory therapies have been used, but no single regimen is universally accepted because treatment decisions are influenced by disease severity, timing, contraindications, patient characteristics, clinician expertise, and resource availability.3,5 SCORTEN is the most widely validated tool for estimating mortality risk.6 The ALDEN algorithm provides a structured method for assessing drug causality.7

Most available evidence on SJS/TEN originates from high- and middle-income countries, while data from low-resource settings remain limited.8,9 Differences in drug utilization patterns, healthcare access, referral pathways, and clinical management may significantly influence disease presentation and outcomes.9,10 Published African data remain limited, and evidence from Somalia is particularly scarce. Understanding these context-specific factors is important for improving patient care, pharmacovigilance, referral planning, and clinical decision-making.

Therefore, this study aimed to describe the demographic, clinical, laboratory, treatment, and in-hospital outcome characteristics of patients diagnosed with SJS, SJS/TEN overlap, or TEN at a tertiary referral hospital in Somalia. Because the cohort was small and only seven deaths occurred, the analysis was descriptive and no reliable mortality prediction model was attempted.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

A retrospective descriptive study was conducted at Mogadishu Somali Turkiye Training and Research Hospital, Mogadishu, Somalia, a tertiary teaching and national referral hospital. Patients were identified from the dermatology inpatient service and other inpatient units. The study records covered January 2020 through December 2025.

Study Population and Case Definition

All admitted patients with a final clinician-documented diagnosis of SJS, SJS/TEN overlap, or TEN during the study period were eligible, irrespective of age. Internationally accepted spectrum definitions use less than 10%, 10–30%, and more than 30% detached or detachable body surface area for SJS, overlap, and TEN, respectively. In this retrospective study, final diagnoses were those recorded by treating clinicians; the original charts did not permit standardized retrospective verification of body surface area for every patient.1,2

Records with an unclear final diagnosis or insufficient information to confirm eligibility were excluded. The retained analytic file contained 37 eligible patients. The number of records screened before exclusion was not preserved in the available extraction, so a reliable screening flow diagram could not be reconstructed.

Data Collection and Variables

Data were abstracted from the hospital electronic medical record system (FONET) using a structured form. The final analysis included age category, sex, final clinician-documented diagnosis, mucosal involvement, suspected drug, hospital length-of-stay category, ICU admission, documented treatments, recorded major complications, and in-hospital survival. Age was categorized as 1–18, 19–49, and ≥50 years. Because only five patients were aged 1–18 years and subgroup-specific clinical outcomes were not retained, pediatric outcome comparisons were not performed.

Laboratory measurements represented the first test obtained at admission for each patient. For the present analysis, results were retained as normal or high according to the hospital laboratory information system. The categorical classifications were based on the following local reference intervals: platelets 100–430 × 103/mm3; white blood cell count 4–10 × 103/mm3; C-reactive protein 0–10 mg/L; aspartate aminotransferase 0–31 U/L; alanine aminotransferase 0–45 U/L; urea 10–45 mg/dL; creatinine 0.5–1.35 mg/dL; sodium 135–150 mEq/L; potassium 3.5–5.5 mEq/L; calcium 8.3–10.6 mg/dL; albumin 3.5–5.5 g/dL; and bicarbonate 22–28 mEq/L. The extracted analytic file available for this study did not contain a complete patient-level numerical matrix; therefore, cohort medians, interquartile ranges, and numerical survivor-versus-non-survivor comparisons could not be calculated without re-extracting the source records. No missing-value imputation was performed.

A second body-surface-area classification field was inconsistent with the final diagnosis field and could not be reconciled from the retained extraction. To avoid duplicate and contradictory severity reporting, the analysis uses the final clinician-documented diagnosis only.

Drug Causality and Severity Assessment

Suspected causative drugs were those documented by the treating team. A formal ALDEN or Naranjo causality assessment was not performed in the source records, and exact exposure dates were not consistently available. Drug attribution is therefore reported as suspected rather than confirmed.7 SCORTEN could not be reconstructed because all seven required variables at the specified assessment time were unavailable.6

The source dataset separately listed ceftriaxone and a broader beta-lactam category. To avoid double counting, ceftriaxone is reported separately and the remaining category is labeled other beta-lactams.

Treatment Documentation

Management was clinician directed and the study did not audit adherence to a single institutional protocol. For contextual interpretation, current guidance emphasizes immediate withdrawal of suspected medications, multidisciplinary supportive care, atraumatic wound and mucosal management, and escalation to intensive care when clinically required.4,11

The records identified whether prednisolone, cyclosporine, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), topical antibiotics, topical corticosteroids, silver sulfadiazine, ophthalmic treatment, and oral/mucosal care were documented. Silver sulfadiazine was used only in patients whose suspected culprit drug was not a sulfonamide. Dose, timing, duration, tapering, treatment sequence, combination timing, treated body site, and contraindications were not consistently retained and therefore could not be compared. Treatment frequencies are descriptive and must not be interpreted as evidence of efficacy or endorsement of a specific regimen.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Additional outcomes were ICU admission, hospital length-of-stay category, and recorded major complications. Long-term ocular, oral, genital, respiratory, psychological, disability, and quality-of-life outcomes were not systematically available.

Sample Size and Sampling

A total population sampling approach was used. All eligible patients diagnosed with SJS, SJS/TEN overlap, or TEN during the study period were included in the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed in SPSS version 24 using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables, including the classifications derived from the first laboratory test obtained at admission, are reported as counts and percentages. Inferential mortality modeling was not undertaken because only seven deaths occurred and the available patient-level data were too sparse for stable estimation, creating a high risk of overfitting and imprecise or misleading effect estimates. The extracted analytic file did not contain a complete patient-level numerical laboratory matrix or valid survivor-specific distributions; consequently, median/interquartile-range summaries and survivor-versus-non-survivor laboratory comparisons could not be calculated from the available data. The study therefore does not claim independent predictors of mortality.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Mogadishu Somali Turkiye Training and Research Hospital, Mogadishu, Somalia (Approval No. MSTH/23478). Patient confidentiality was strictly maintained, and all data were anonymized prior to analysis. As this was a retrospective study using existing medical records, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

The cohort included 37 patients. Five patients (13.5%) were aged 1–18 years, 18 (48.6%) were aged 19–49 years, and 14 (37.8%) were aged ≥50 years. Twenty-three (62.2%) were female and 14 (37.8%) were male. Final clinician-documented diagnoses were TEN in 27 patients (73.0%), SJS/TEN overlap in six (16.2%), and SJS in four (10.8%). Mucosal involvement was documented in all 37 patients. Nine patients (24.3%) required ICU admission. Twenty-two (59.5%) remained in hospital for seven days or fewer, ten (27.0%) for 8–14 days, and five (13.5%) for more than 14 days (Table 1).

Suspected Drug Triggers

Antibacterial agents were the most frequent suspected exposures, accounting for 26 of 37 patients (70.3%): other beta-lactams in 12 (32.4%), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole in nine (24.3%), ceftriaxone in four (10.8%), and amikacin in one (2.7%). Other suspected exposures were nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs in five patients (13.5%), antiepileptic drugs in three (8.1%), antimalarial drugs in two (5.4%), and allopurinol in one (2.7%) (Table 1). These assignments reflect clinician documentation and were not confirmed using ALDEN.

Laboratory Findings

Categorical findings from the first laboratory test obtained at admission are summarized in Table 2. High platelet, AST, and sodium flags were recorded in all 37 patients. High urea was recorded in 36 (97.3%), bicarbonate in 35 (94.6%), ALT in 34 (91.9%), C-reactive protein in 33 (89.2%), and white blood cell count in 12 (32.4%). No patient was flagged as having high creatinine, potassium, or calcium, while one patient (2.7%) had high albumin. Because the extracted analytic file contained categorical status rather than a complete patient-level numerical matrix, these unusually uniform patterns should be interpreted cautiously and not as quantitative estimates of biochemical severity.

Treatments and Outcomes

Prednisolone was documented in 35 patients (94.6%), cyclosporine in 21 (56.8%), and IVIG in one (2.7%). Topical antibiotics were recorded in 28 patients (75.7%), ophthalmic treatment in 13 (35.1%), topical corticosteroids in 11 (29.7%), oral/mucosal care in nine (24.3%), and silver sulfadiazine in five (13.5%) (Table 3). None of the five patients treated with silver sulfadiazine had a sulfonamide recorded as the suspected culprit drug. Because dose, timing, sequence, treated site, and indication were not consistently available, combination treatment and comparative effectiveness could not be assessed.

Thirty patients (81.1%) had no recorded major complication, five (13.5%) had multiorgan failure, and two (5.4%) had a cardiac event. Thirty patients survived and seven died, giving an in-hospital mortality of 18.9% (Table 3). No mortality-prediction analysis was undertaken.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the clinical characteristics, management, and outcomes of patients with Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) at a tertiary referral hospital in Somalia. Our findings demonstrated a female predominance, a high proportion of clinician-documented TEN, antibacterial medicines as the leading suspected drug group, frequent use of prednisolone and cyclosporine, and an in-hospital mortality rate of 18.9%. Because only seven deaths occurred and patient-level data were limited, no inferential mortality model was retained.

The observed female predominance is consistent with previous studies reporting a slightly higher occurrence of SJS/TEN among females.12,13 Although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, potential explanations include sex-related differences in immune response and drug metabolism. The age distribution was predominantly adult: 18 patients were aged 19–49 years and 14 were aged ≥50 years, while five were aged 1–18 years. The 19–49-year category was the largest group, but no age category represented a majority.

Antibacterial medicines, particularly other beta-lactams and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, were the most commonly suspected agents, consistent with global evidence identifying antibiotics as important triggers of SJS/TEN.8 The lower contribution of antiepileptic drugs compared with some African series may reflect regional prescribing patterns and drug availability.9,10 However, formal ALDEN assessment and complete medication timelines were unavailable; the agents should therefore be interpreted as suspected rather than confirmed causes.7 These findings highlight the importance of context-specific pharmacovigilance, particularly where antibacterial use is widespread.

A key finding was the high proportion of patients with a final clinician-documented diagnosis of TEN. This may reflect delayed presentation, limited access to specialized care, referral of more severe cases to a national tertiary center, or variation in clinical documentation.9,10 A duplicate body-surface-area field was inconsistent with the final diagnosis field and was therefore excluded. Consequently, the diagnostic categories could not be independently reclassified from source body-surface-area measurements.

Despite the high proportion of clinician-documented TEN, most patients survived, and the observed mortality of 18.9% was within the broad range reported in previous series.9,11 However, this retrospective descriptive study cannot establish that supportive care or any immunomodulatory treatment caused the observed survival. Multiorgan failure and cardiac events were recorded, but survivor-versus-non-survivor patient-level comparisons were unavailable and these complications should not be presented as independent mortality predictors. SCORTEN would provide a more standardized mortality-risk assessment in future cohorts.6

Laboratory findings demonstrated a pattern consistent with systemic inflammation and physiological stress, including elevated inflammatory markers and liver enzymes. Elevated urea with preserved creatinine may be compatible with dehydration or catabolic stress rather than intrinsic renal impairment.14 Nevertheless, the very high frequencies of several categorical flags, including platelet, AST, sodium, urea, and bicarbonate elevations, are unusual. The local white blood cell reference interval was 4–10 × 103/mm3 and the bicarbonate interval was 22–28 mEq/L. Each result represented the first test obtained at admission. Because the extracted analytic file did not contain a complete patient-level numerical matrix, the magnitude, distribution, and prognostic importance of these abnormalities could not be assessed, and the findings should be interpreted cautiously.

With regard to treatment, prednisolone was widely used, more than half of patients received cyclosporine, and IVIG use was minimal. This pattern reflects local practice and the continuing absence of a universally accepted immunomodulatory regimen.3,5 Dose, timing, duration, tapering, treatment sequence, and combination timing were not consistently available, so comparative effectiveness cannot be inferred. A randomized trial suggested potential benefit from etanercept compared with corticosteroids.15 Evidence for JAK inhibitors remains limited and investigational, requiring further study before these agents can be regarded as standard care.16

Silver sulfadiazine and topical corticosteroids were documented in a minority of patients. Contemporary guidance does not recommend routine silver sulfadiazine for SJS/TEN and generally favors atraumatic nonadherent wound care.4,11 However, the use of topical silver sulfadiazine in our study reflects local clinical practice at our institution during the study period rather than an endorsement of its routine use. All five recipients had a non-sulfonamide suspected culprit drug; this observation does not establish the safety or effectiveness of silver sulfadiazine. Topical corticosteroids may have selected site-specific roles under specialist supervision, particularly for mucosal disease, but general cutaneous use is not standardized.4,11 The limited documentation of ophthalmic and oral/mucosal care is also important because survivors may experience chronic ocular, oral, genital, cutaneous, respiratory, and psychological sequelae with substantial long-term effects on quality of life.17 Standardized early specialist assessment and structured follow-up should be incorporated into future care protocols and data collection.

The findings also have practical implications for primary and district-level facilities. Clinicians should suspect SJS/TEN when painful erythema, blistering, epidermal detachment, or mucosal erosions develop after a new medication. Potential culprit drugs should be stopped immediately; airway, circulation, temperature, fluid balance, pain, nutrition, and infection risk should be addressed; the eyes and other mucosal surfaces should be assessed early; and urgent referral should be arranged to a center capable of multidisciplinary supportive care.2,4 The suspected culprit and related drugs should be clearly documented to reduce the risk of re-exposure.

Strengths and Limitations

This study contributes otherwise scarce clinical information from Somalia and describes the complete cohort retained in the available hospital extraction for the specified period. Its principal limitations are the retrospective single-center design, small sample, only seven deaths, and probable tertiary-referral selection bias. The number of screened records was unavailable. Only five patients were aged 1–18 years, preventing reliable pediatric outcome comparisons. Comorbidities, detailed organ involvement, complete drug exposure timelines, ALDEN, SCORTEN, treatment dose and timing, combination sequence, and long-term sequelae were not consistently available. Laboratory results represented the first test obtained at admission, but the extracted analytic file retained categorical normal/high classifications rather than a complete patient-level numerical matrix. Therefore, cohort medians, interquartile ranges, and numerical survivor comparisons could not be calculated from the available dataset. The categorical findings used documented local reference intervals, including white blood cell count 4–10 × 103/mm3 and bicarbonate 22–28 mEq/L, and the unusually uniform patterns require cautious interpretation. Mortality modeling was not undertaken. These limitations restrict generalizability and preclude conclusions about treatment efficacy or independent mortality predictors.

Conclusion

In this tertiary hospital cohort, SJS/TEN was most often associated with suspected antibacterial drug exposure, most patients had a final clinician-documented diagnosis of TEN, and in-hospital mortality was 18.9%. These descriptive data do not establish causal drug attribution, comparative treatment benefit, or mortality predictors. For frontline clinicians, the central messages are rapid recognition, immediate withdrawal of possible culprit medicines, early stabilization and mucosal assessment, clear documentation to prevent re-exposure, and urgent referral for multidisciplinary supportive care. Larger prospective multicenter studies should use standardized diagnostic definitions, ALDEN, SCORTEN, quantitative laboratory data, prespecified treatment variables, and long-term outcome assessment.
