Abstract
. Let f f be a conformal (analytic and univalent) map defined on the open unit disk 𝔻 {\mathbb{D}} of the complex plane ℂ {\mathbb{C}} that is continuous on the semi-circle ∂ 𝔻 + = { z ∈ ℂ : | z | = 1 , Im z > 0 } \partial{\mathbb{D}}^{+}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z|=1,{\rm{Im}}\,z>0\} . The existence of a uniform upper bound for the ratio of the length of the image of the horizontal diameter ( − 1 , 1 ) (-1,1) to the length of the image of ∂ 𝔻 + \partial{\mathbb{D}}^{+} under f f was proved by Gehring and Hayman. In this article, at first, we generalize this result by introducing a simple pole for f f in 𝔻 {\mathbb{D}} and considering the ratio of the length of the image of the vertical diameter I = { z : Re z = 0 ; | Im z | < 1 } I=\{z:{\rm{Re}}\,z=0;~|{\rm{Im}}\,z|<1\} to the length of the image of the semi-circle C ′ = { z : | z | = 1 ; Re z < 0 } C^{\prime}=\{z:|z|=1;~{\rm{Re}}\,z<0\} under such f f . Finally, we further generalize this result by replacing the vertical diameter I I with a hyperbolic geodesic symmetric with respect to the real line, and by replacing C ′ C^{\prime} with the corresponding arc of the unit circle passing through the point − 1 -1 .

1.
Introduction

We will use the following notations throughout the article:

{\mathbb{C}}:\text{The finite complex plane}

\hat{{\mathbb{C}}}:{\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}

{\mathbb{D}}:\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z|<1\}

\partial{\mathbb{D}}:\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z|=1\}

\partial{\mathbb{D}}^{+}:\{z\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}:~{\rm{Im}}\,z>0\}

\mathbb{H}:\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:{\rm{Im}}\,z>0\}

{{\mathbb{R}}}^{+}:\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:{\rm{Im}}\,z=0;~{\rm{Re}}\,z>0\}

\mathbb{I}^{+}:\{z\in\mathbb{H}:{\rm{Re}}\,z=0\}

I:\{z\in{\mathbb{D}}:{\rm{Re}}\,z=0;~|{\rm{Im}}\,z|<1\}

C^{\prime}:\{z\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}:~{\rm{Re}}\,z<0\}
.

In this article, by a conformal mapping, we mean an analytic and univalent mapping. Let
f
be a conformal mapping of
{\mathbb{D}}
that remains continuous on
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
. In
[
4
]
, Fejér and Riesz proved that, if
f
maps
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
onto a curve of length
L
, then the diameter
(-1,1)
will be mapped onto a curve whose length does not exceed
{L}/{2}
. Later, Piranian conjectured (see
[
5
]
) the semi-circle version of the Fejér-Riesz theorem as stated below.

Piranian’s Conjecture.

Suppose
f
is a conformal mapping of
\mathbb{D}
that remains continuous on the semi-circle
\partial{\mathbb{D}}^{+}
and maps this semi-circle onto a curve of length
L
. Then
f
maps the diameter
(-1,1)
onto a curve whose length does not exceed
AL
, where
A
is an absolute constant.

The Fejér-Riesz theorem is a consequence of the following inequality which is valid for any function
f
analytic in
{\mathbb{D}}\cup\partial{\mathbb{D}}
:

\int_{-1}^{1}|f(x)|dx\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|f(e^{i\theta})|d\theta.

Unfortunately, this inequality is not true when considering its semi-circle version. However, Piranian’s conjecture was ultimately validated. In
[
5
]
, Gehring and Hayman replaced
\mathbb{D}
,
\partial{{\mathbb{D}}}^{+}
and the horizontal diameter
(-1,1)
with
\mathbb{H}
,
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{+}
and
\mathbb{I}^{+}
, respectively, and proved the upper half-plane version of the Piranian’s conjecture, which we state below (see
[
5
, Theorem 1]
):

Theorem A.

If
f
is a conformal mapping of
\mathbb{H}
which remains continuous on the positive x-axis and maps this half-axis onto a curve of length
L
, then
f
maps the positive y-axis onto a curve of length
L^{\prime}
, where

L^{\prime}\leq AL.

Here,
A
is an absolute constant with
\pi\leq A<74
.

In order to present the generalized Gehring-Hayman theorem, we first introduce the notion of the hyperbolic metric in
{\mathbb{D}}
. The hyperbolic metric in the unit disk
{\mathbb{D}}
is defined by

\lambda_{{\mathbb{D}}}(z_{1},z_{2})=\underset{C}{\min}\int_{C}\frac{|dz|}{1-|z|^{2}}~~\mbox{for}~~z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathbb{D}},

where the minimum is taken over all curves
C
in
{\mathbb{D}}
from
z_{1}
to
z_{2}
; the curve that minimizes the length is called the hyperbolic geodesic joining
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
(see
[
10
]
, p.6). It is well known that, for
z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathbb{D}}
, the hyperbolic geodesic joining
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
is the arc of the circle through
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
, orthogonal to
\partial{{\mathbb{D}}}
. The generalized Gehring-Hayman theorem states that, if
f
is a conformal mapping of
{\mathbb{D}}
and
J\subset{\mathbb{D}}
is any Jordan arc from
z_{1}
to
z_{2}
, then

\int_{S}|f^{\prime}(z)||dz|\leq A\int_{J}|f^{\prime}(z)||dz|,

where
A
is an absolute constant and
S
is the hyperbolic geodesic joining
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
. We refer to
[
10
, Theorem 4.20]
and
[
11
]
for more information on the generalized Gehring-Hayman theorem. Numerous significant results and conjectures have been derived from the above-mentioned Gehring-Hayman inequality, among which, one is the famous Hall’s conjecture.
We add here that, despite remaining unsolved for an extended period, Hästö and Ponnusamy (see
[
6
]
) recently proved it. In 1968, Jaenisch
[
7
]
improved the second part of Theorem A by showing
A\geq 4.56
with numerical computations and reducing the range of
A
to
4.56\leq A\leq 17.45
. However, the disc version of the Theorem A is not true if we merely take
f
to be analytic in
{\mathbb{D}}
and continuous on
\partial{\mathbb{D}}^{+}
. For example, the mapping

f_{0}(z)=e^{i\left(\frac{1+z}{1-z}\right)},~~z\in{\mathbb{D}},

is analytic in
{\mathbb{D}}
and continuous on
{\partial{\mathbb{D}}}^{+}
. Here,
f_{0}
maps
{\partial{\mathbb{D}}}^{+}
onto a curve of length 1, but the length of
f_{0}((-1,1))
is infinite. Also, Theorem A cannot be extended to quasi-conformal mappings (see
[
5
, Theorem 4]
). Thus, a natural question arises: Does Theorem A hold in the case of meromorphic univalent functions?

In this article, we give an answer to this question by proving a result similar to Theorem A for meromorphic univalent functions with a simple pole in
{\mathbb{D}}
. For this purpose, we have chosen our
f
to be a meromorphic and univalent function in
{\mathbb{D}}
with a simple pole at
z=p\;(0<p<1)
that remains continuous on
C^{\prime}
. Let
\ell=\|f(C^{\prime})\|
,
\ell^{\prime}=\|f(I)\|
, where
\|\gamma\|
denotes the length of
\gamma
. In Theorem
1
, we will prove the existence of a constant
A_{p}
satisfying
\ell^{\prime}\leq A_{p}\ell
and provide a range for
A_{p}
. For the lower bound of
A_{p}
, consider the function

k_{p}(z)=\frac{pz}{(1-pz)(p-z)},~~z\in{\mathbb{D}},

which is meromorphic and univalent in
\mathbb{D}
with a simple pole at
z=p\,(0<p<1)
. It can be shown that (see
[
2
]
)

k_{p}(\mathbb{D})=\hat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\left[\frac{-p}{(1-p)^{2}},\frac{-p}{(1+p)^{2}}\right].

Also,
k_{p}
is continuous on
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
and

k_{p}(-1)=\frac{-p}{(1+p)^{2}};~~k_{p}(\pm i)=\frac{-p}{1+p^{2}}.

Since
k_{p}
maps
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
onto the interval
[-{p}{(1-p)^{-2}},-{p}{(1+p)^{-2}}]
, we conclude that
k_{p}
maps
C^{\prime}
onto the interval
(-{p}(1+p^{2})^{-1},-{p}{(1+p)^{-2}}]
twice. Thus,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\|k_{p}(C^{\prime})\|=2\bigg(\frac{p}{1+p^{2}}-\frac{p}{(1+p)^{2}}\bigg)=\frac{4p^{2}}{(1+p^{2})(1+p)^{2}}.

Also, for
r\in(-1,1)
,

k_{p}(ir)=\frac{ir}{(1-irp)(1-\frac{ir}{p})}=\frac{-r^{2}(p+\frac{1}{p})+ir(1-r^{2})}{(1-r^{2})^{2}+r^{2}(p+\frac{1}{p})^{2}}.

So,
k_{p}
maps
I
conformally onto the circle centered at
(-{p}/{2(1+{p^{2}})},0)
with radius
{p}/{2(1+{p^{2}})}
excluding the point
(-{p}(1+p^{2})^{-1},0)
. Consequently,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\|k_{p}(I)\|=\frac{2\pi}{2(p+\frac{1}{p})}=\frac{p\pi}{1+p^{2}}.

Combining equations (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), we obtain

\frac{\|k_{p}(I)\|}{\|k_{p}(C^{\prime})\|}=\frac{(1+p)^{2}\pi}{4p}.

Therefore, we deduce that

(1.3)

A_{p}\geq\frac{(1+p)^{2}\pi}{4p},~p\in(0,1).

Following this, several lemmas will be demonstrated, which will be used to establish the existence of a constant
A_{p}
in Theorem
1
. Furthermore, we will identify a specific region in
{\mathbb{D}}
for the pole where the conclusion of Theorem
1
remains valid. Finally, we will give an extension of Theorem
1
by replacing vertical diameter
I
with any hyperbolic geodesic in
{\mathbb{D}}
that is symmetric about the real line and
C^{\prime}
with the corresponding arc of
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
that passes through
-1
and joins the endpoints of the aforementioned hyperbolic geodesic. This is the content of Theorem
2
.

2.
Preliminary Results

Since we aim to determine

\ell^{\prime}=\|f(I)\|=\int_{I}|f^{\prime}(z)||dz|,

we need an upper bound for the distortion
|f^{\prime}(z)|
at the points
z
of
I
. Let
0<\alpha<1
and
\gamma
be the hyperbolic geodesic in
{\mathbb{D}}
which is symmetric about the real axis and passes through the point
{\alpha}/{(1+\sqrt{1-{\alpha}^{2}})}
. Since hyperbolic geodesics of
{\mathbb{D}}
are arcs of circles in
{\mathbb{D}}
that are perpendicular to
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, we obtain

(2.1)

\gamma=\{(x,y):x^{2}+y^{2}-({2}/{\alpha})x+1=0,x^{2}+y^{2}<1\}.

Now,
\gamma
meets
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
at the points
\alpha\pm i\sqrt{1-{\alpha}^{2}}
and determines two hyperbolic half-planes; let
\Omega
be the one that contains the origin. Then, the following function (see
[
9
, p. 85]
)

(2.2)

\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha}(z)=\frac{z(1-\alpha z)}{z-\alpha},\;z\in\Omega,

maps
\Omega
conformally onto
{\mathbb{D}}
with
\phi_{\alpha}(0)=0
and
\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(0)=-{1}/{\alpha}
.

Lemma 1
.

If
f
maps
\Omega
conformally onto a domain
D
, then

\displaystyle|f^{\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{4|{\phi_{\alpha}}^{\prime}(z)|}{1-|{\phi_{\alpha}(z)}|^{2}}\rho(f(z)),~~~~z\in\Omega,

where
\phi_{\alpha}
is the function in
(\ref{Eq1})
that maps
\Omega
conformally onto
{\mathbb{D}}
and
\rho(f(z))
denotes the distance from
f(z)
to the boundary of
D
.

Proof.
We note that
f\circ{\phi_{\alpha}}^{-1}
maps
{\mathbb{D}}
conformally onto
D
. Applying the Koebe distortion theorem (see
[
10
, p.9]
) to
f\circ{\phi_{\alpha}}^{-1}
, we get

(2.3)

\displaystyle|(f\circ{\phi_{\alpha}}^{-1})^{\prime}(\zeta)|\leq\frac{4\rho(f\circ{\phi_{\alpha}}^{-1}(\zeta))}{1-|\zeta|^{2}},\;\zeta\in{\mathbb{D}}.

Now
|(f\circ{\phi_{\alpha}}^{-1})^{\prime}(\zeta)|=|f^{\prime}({\phi_{\alpha}}^{-1}(\zeta))|\;|({\phi_{\alpha}}^{-1})^{\prime}(\zeta)|
.
Replacing
\zeta
by
\phi_{\alpha}(z)
, we have

|(f\circ{\phi_{\alpha}}^{-1})^{\prime}(\zeta)|=\frac{|f^{\prime}(z)|}{|\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(z)|}

and

\rho(f\circ{\phi_{\alpha}}^{-1}(\zeta))={\operatorname{dist}}({f\circ{\phi_{\alpha}}^{-1}}(\zeta),\partial({f\circ{\phi_{\alpha}}^{-1}}({\mathbb{D}})))={\operatorname{dist}}(f(z),\partial f(\Omega))=\rho(f(z)).

Thus, letting
\zeta=\phi_{\alpha}(z)
in (
2.3
), we get the desired inequality.
∎

The above lemma provides a local measure of distortion of a conformal mapping defined on
\Omega
. Now, we will proceed toward our main goal, i.e. proving Theorem
1
using a similar method of proof which was adopted by Gehring and Hayman in
[
5
]
. Let

(2.4)

\displaystyle g(z)=\frac{-1-iz}{z+i},~z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

It is easy to see that,
g
maps
{\mathbb{D}}
conformally onto
\mathbb{H}
with

(2.5)

g(0)=i~~~\text{ and }~~~g\left(\frac{\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-{\alpha}^{2}}}\right)=-\alpha+i\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}.

Also,
g(I)=\mathbb{I}^{+}
and
g(C^{\prime})={{\mathbb{R}}}^{+}
. Since
g
is a Möbius map, it will map geodesics onto geodesics, and geodesics of
\mathbb{H}
are semi-circles orthogonal to the real axis and vertical half-lines. Now,
g
maps
\gamma
which is defined in (
2.1
) conformally onto the semi-circle

(2.6)

\sigma=\{(x,y):x^{2}+y^{2}+({2}/{\alpha})x+1=0,y>0\}.

It is clear that
\sigma
is perpendicular to the real line. Thus,
\sigma
is a hyperbolic geodesic in
\mathbb{H}
. Let
\Omega_{1}
be the hyperbolic half-plane of
\mathbb{H}
determined by
\sigma
that contains the point
i
. Therefore,
g
maps
\Omega
, which is defined in Lemma
1
, conformally onto
\Omega_{1}
. The following lemma is the upper half-plane version of Lemma
1
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
w
map
\Omega_{1}
conformally onto a domain
D
. Then

|w^{\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{8|{\phi_{\alpha}}^{\prime}(-\frac{iz+1}{z+i})|}{|z+i|^{2}(1-|{\phi_{\alpha}}(-\frac{iz+1}{z+i})|^{2})}\rho(w(z)),~~~~~z\in\Omega_{1},

where
\rho(w(z))
denotes the distance from
w(z)
to the boundary of
D
and
\phi_{\alpha}
is as in
(\ref{Eq1})
.

Proof.
Since
w\circ g
maps
\Omega
conformally onto
D
, from Lemma
1
we get

(2.7)

|(w\circ g)^{\prime}(\zeta)|\leq\frac{4|{\phi_{\alpha}}^{\prime}(\zeta)|}{1-|\phi_{\alpha}(\zeta)|^{2}}\rho({w\circ g}(\zeta)).

Now, letting
\zeta=g^{-1}(z)=-{(iz+1)}/{(z+i)}
, we have

\rho({w\circ g(\zeta)})={\operatorname{dist}}(w\circ g(\zeta),\partial(w\circ g(\Omega)))={\operatorname{dist}}(w(z),\partial w(\Omega_{1}))=\rho(w(z))

and

\frac{2|w^{\prime}(z)|}{|\frac{-iz-1}{z+i}+i|^{2}}\leq\frac{4|{\phi_{\alpha}}^{\prime}(\frac{-iz-1}{z+i})|}{1-|\phi_{\alpha}(\frac{-iz-1}{z+i})|^{2}}\rho(w(z))\\

from (
2.7
). The desired inequality follows from the above estimate.
∎

The lemma below provides an upper bound for
\rho(w)
(see
[
5
, Lemma 2]
).

Lemma A.

Suppose that
M
is a simply connected domain and
\beta
is a set of boundary points of
M
which lie within distance
d
of a point
w_{0}
not in
M
. Then if
u(w)
is harmonic in
M
, satisfies
0\leq u(w)\leq 1
there, and approaches
0
as
w
approaches any boundary points of
M
not in
\beta
, we have

\rho(w)\leq d\,\cot^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}u(w)\right).

We now introduce the notion of harmonic measure, which will be used to prove Lemma
3
below. Given a domain
D
, a point
z\in D
, and a Borel set
E
of
\overline{D}
, where
\overline{D}
denotes the closure of
D
,
\omega(z,E,D)
denotes the harmonic measure at
z
of
\overline{E}
with respect to the component of
D\setminus\overline{E}
containing
z
. The function
\omega(.,E,D)
precisely represents the solution to the generalized Dirichlet problem with boundary data
\phi=1_{E}
(see
[
1
, ch.3]
,
[
8
, p.297]
). Clearly,
\omega(z,E,D)
is a harmonic function satisfying
0\leq\omega(z,E,D)\leq 1
. The next theorem provides a relation between the harmonic measures defined on a domain and on its subdomain (see
[
3
, p.282]
and
[
8
, p.302]
).

Theorem B.

Let
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
be two domains in
{\mathbb{C}}
. Assume that
D_{1}\subset D_{2}
and let
F\subset\partial D_{2}
be a closed set. Let
\eta=\partial D_{1}\setminus\partial D_{2}
. Then for
z\in D_{1}
,

\displaystyle\omega(z,F,D_{2})=\omega(z,F,D_{1})+\int_{\eta}\omega(z,ds,D_{1})\omega(s,F,D_{2}).

After all these preparations, we prove the following lemma which will be used to establish Theorem
1
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\alpha\in(1/\sqrt{2},1)
and
\Omega_{1}
be the same as in Lemma
2
. Suppose that
0<a<b
and that
\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}
are the segments joining
a
to
b
and
ia
to
ib
, respectively. If
w
is a conformal mapping of
\Omega_{1}
, which remains continuous on
\beta
and maps
\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}
onto curves of length
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
, then we have

\ell^{\prime}<B_{\alpha}\ell,

where
B_{\alpha}
is a constant which depends on
\alpha
and
{b}/{a}
.

Proof.
Let
\omega(z,\beta,\mathbb{H})
and
\omega(z,\beta,\Omega_{1})
denote the harmonic measures at the point
z
of
\beta
with respect to
\mathbb{H}
and
\Omega_{1}
, respectively. From Theorem B, we get

(2.8)

\displaystyle\omega(z,\beta,\Omega_{1})=\omega(z,\beta,\mathbb{H})-\int_{\sigma}\omega(z,ds,\Omega_{1})\omega(s,\beta,\mathbb{H}),

where
\sigma
is given in (
2.6
).
Note that
\omega(z,\beta,\mathbb{H})
is
{1}/{\pi}
times the angle which
\beta
subtends at
z
. Hence, for
z\in\beta^{\prime}
(see
[
5
, Lemma 3]
),

(2.9)

\omega(z,\beta,\mathbb{H})\geq\frac{1}{\pi}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{b-a}{b+a}\right).

Next, we will calculate an upper bound of
\omega(s,\beta,\mathbb{H})
on
\sigma
. Let
s=(x,\sqrt{r^{2}-(x+m)^{2}})
be an arbitrary point on
\sigma
, where

m=\frac{1}{\alpha},~~r=\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}{\alpha}~~\text{ and}~~\frac{-1-\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}{\alpha}\leq x\leq\frac{-\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}.

Then,

\displaystyle\omega(s,\beta,\mathbb{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\left[\tan^{-1}\frac{b-x}{\sqrt{r^{2}-(x+m)^{2}}}-\tan^{-1}\frac{a-x}{\sqrt{r^{2}-(x+m)^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\tan^{-1}\frac{(b-a)\sqrt{r^{2}-(x+m)^{2}}}{ab-1-2mx-(b+a)x}.

Since
ab-1-2mx>0
and
-(a+b)x>0
for all
x\in[-({1+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}})/{\alpha},-{\alpha}/({1+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}})]
and
\tan^{-1}(x)
is an increasing function, we obtain from the equation above

(2.10)

\displaystyle\omega(s,\beta,\mathbb{H})<\frac{1}{\pi}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{b-a}{b+a}\psi(x)\right),

where

\psi(x)=\frac{-\sqrt{r^{2}-(x+m)^{2}}}{x}.

Here,
\psi
is continuous in
[-({1+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}})/{\alpha},-{\alpha}/({1+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}})]
and attains its maximum at the point
x=-\alpha
. Then from (
2.10
), we get

\omega(s,\beta,\mathbb{H})<\frac{1}{\pi}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{b-a}{b+a}\psi(-\alpha)\right)=\frac{1}{\pi}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{b-a}{b+a}\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}{\alpha}\right)

for
s\in\sigma
.
Therefore,

(2.11)

\displaystyle\int_{\sigma}\omega(z,ds,\Omega_{1})\omega(s,\beta,\mathbb{H})

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{b-a}{b+a}\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}{\alpha}\right)\int_{\sigma}\omega(z,ds,\Omega_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{b-a}{b+a}\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}{\alpha}\right)\omega(z,\sigma,\Omega_{1})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{b-a}{b+a}\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}{\alpha}\right).

Thus, from (
2.8
), using (
2.9
) and (
2.11
), for
z\in\beta^{\prime}
, we have

(2.12)

\displaystyle\omega(z,\beta,\Omega_{1})>\frac{\xi}{\pi},

where

\xi=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{b-a}{b+a}\right)-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{b-a}{b+a}\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}{\alpha}\right).

Since
\alpha\in(1/\sqrt{2},1)
, consequently
0<{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}/{\alpha}<1
. Thus,
0<\xi<\pi/4
.
Suppose
\beta_{1}=w(\beta)
and
\beta^{\prime}_{1}=w(\beta^{\prime})
. Let
w_{0}
denote the point on
\beta_{1}
that divides its arclength in half. Define

u(w)=\omega(z(w),\beta,\Omega_{1}),

where
z(w)
is the inverse of
w
. Therefore,
\beta_{1}
lies in the disk
|w-w_{0}|\leq d=\ell/2
and
u(w)
and
\beta_{1}
satisfy the hypothesis of Lemma A. Now, (
2.12
) implies

u(w)>\frac{\xi}{\pi}

at each point
w\in\beta^{\prime}_{1}
and we conclude from Lemma A and Lemma
2
that

|w^{\prime}(z)|<\frac{4\ell|{\phi_{\alpha}}^{\prime}(-\frac{iz+1}{z+i})|}{|z+i|^{2}(1-|\phi_{\alpha}(-\frac{iz+1}{z+i})|^{2})}\cot^{2}\left(\frac{\xi}{4}\right)

at each point
z\in\beta^{\prime}
. By integrating the above inequality along
\beta^{\prime}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\ell^{\prime}=\int_{\beta^{\prime}}|w^{\prime}(z)||dz|

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 4\ell\;\cot^{2}\left(\frac{\xi}{4}\right)\int_{[ia,ib]}\frac{|{\phi_{\alpha}}^{\prime}(-\frac{iz+1}{z+i})|}{|z+i|^{2}(1-|\phi_{\alpha}(-\frac{iz+1}{z+i})|^{2})}|dz|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\ell\;\cot^{2}\left(\frac{\xi}{4}\right)\int_{a}^{b}\frac{|{\phi_{\alpha}}^{\prime}(i(\frac{1-y}{1+y}))|}{(1+y)^{2}(1-|{\phi_{\alpha}}(i(\frac{1-y}{1+y}))|^{2})}dy.

By letting
r=(1-y)/(1+y)
and using (
2.2
), from the above inequality we get

\displaystyle\ell^{\prime}

\displaystyle 2\ell\;\cot^{2}\left(\frac{\xi}{4}\right)\int_{\frac{1-b}{1+b}}^{\frac{1-a}{1+a}}\frac{|{\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(ir)}|}{1-|\phi_{\alpha}(ir)|^{2}}dr

Main Results

We now state and prove the main result of this article.

Theorem 1
.

Let
p\in(\sqrt{2}-1,1)
. Suppose
f
is a meromorphic univalent function in
{\mathbb{D}}
with a simple pole at
z=p
that remains continuous on the semi-circle
C^{\prime}
. If
\ell=\|f(C^{\prime})\|
and
\ell^{\prime}=\|f(I)\|
, then there exists a constant
A_{p}
such that

(3.1)

\ell^{\prime}\leq A_{p}\;\ell

with

(3.2)

\frac{(1+p)^{2}\pi}{4p}\leq A_{p}\leq\displaystyle{\min_{q\in(1,\infty)}M_{p}(q)},

where

(3.3)

M_{p}(q)=\frac{(1+p^{2})}{2p}\cot^{2}\left(\frac{\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{q-1}{q+1}\right)-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{(1-p^{2})(q-1)}{2p(q+1)}\right)}{4}\right)\log q.

Let
g
be the mapping defined in
(\ref{Eq5})

(3.4)

It is easy to see that
p\in(\sqrt{2}-1,1)
if and only if
\alpha\in({1}/{\sqrt{2}},1)
. Then
w=f\circ g^{-1}
is meromorphic and univalent in
\mathbb{H}
with a simple pole at
\alpha^{\prime}:=g(p)=-\alpha+i\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}
(see (
2.5

{{\mathbb{R}}}^{+}
. Let

, where
\Omega_{1}
is the same as in Lemma
2
. Since
\alpha^{\prime}
lies on
\sigma
, which is defined in (
2.6
), then
w_{1}
is conformal on
\Omega_{1}
and remains continuous on
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{+}
. Choose
1<q<\infty
. For
n=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,...
, let
\beta_{n}
and
\beta^{\prime}_{n}
denote the segments joining
q^{n}
to
q^{n+1}
and
iq^{n}
to
iq^{n+1}
, respectively. Let
\ell_{n}
and
\ell^{\prime}_{n}
denote the lengths of these segments under
w
(or
w_{1}
). Then from Lemma
3
, we get

(3.5)

\ell^{\prime}_{n}<B_{\alpha}(q)\ell_{n},

where

(3.6)

B_{\alpha}(q)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\cot^{2}\left(\frac{\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{q-1}{q+1}\right)-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}(q-1)}{\alpha(q+1)}\right)}{4}\right)\log q.

\ell=\|f(C^{\prime})\|=\|(f\circ g^{-1})({{\mathbb{R}}}^{+})\|=\|w_{1}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{+})\|

\ell^{\prime}=\|f(I)\|=\|(f\circ g^{-1})(\mathbb{I}^{+})\|=\|w_{1}(\mathbb{I}^{+})\|.

Thus, from (
3.5
), we get

(3.7)

\ell^{\prime}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\ell^{\prime}_{n}\leq B_{\alpha}(q)\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\ell_{n}=B_{\alpha}(q)\ell.

Now in (

, we obtain
