Predicting drilling fluid lost circulation volume and radius using petrophysical well logs and machine learning approaches
Authors: Farshad Sadeghpour, Seyed Reza Shadizadeh, Majid Akbari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lost circulation, which refers to the unintended loss of drilling fluid into underground formations, presents a significant challenge in drilling operations. This issue often leads to considerable non-productive time and financial losses. To effectively address this problem, it is crucial to accurately predict loss zones, volumes, and the extent of fluid invasion. This study introduces an innovative approach by integrating petrophysical well logs with drilling operational parameters and mud characteristics within a machine learning framework. Using a comprehensive dataset of 246,620 data points from 73 wells in an Iranian oil field, we developed three advanced predictive models: multi-layer perceptron (MLP), Kolmogorov–Arnold network (KAN), and a novel hybrid MLP–KAN architecture. Performance evaluations revealed that the hybrid model significantly outperformed the standalone models, achieving a determination coefficient (R2) of 0.9445 and a root mean square error of 0.0143. In contrast, the standalone MLP model achieved an R2 of 0.9054, while the KAN model reached an R2 of 0.9075. The hybrid framework effectively identified three primary mud invasion zones within the field, located at depths of 1800–2000 m, 2300–2600 m, and 2800–3000 m, accurately quantifying their average invasion volumes and radii. SHAP analysis indicated that the most significant predictive features included sonic transit time (DT), bit size, mud weight, and calcium content, aligning the model’s output with established principles of drilling physics. The findings from this research provide a robust and interpretable tool for proactive management of lost circulation, enabling operators to implement zone-specific prevention strategies, optimize drilling fluid programs, and notably reduce operational risks and costs. The framework enables proactive loss prevention through early zone identification and targeted mitigation strategies, offering a scalable solution for enhancing drilling efficiency and reducing operational risks. Overall, this work bridges the gap between data-driven analytics and practical field applications, offering a scalable solution for enhancing drilling efficiency.

Introduction

Lost circulation, a significant challenge in drilling operations, occurs when drilling mud is unintentionally lost into geological formations, leading to increased costs and non-productive time. This issue can arise from various factors, including fracture gradient, mud weight, and the lithology of the formations being drilled. The fracture gradient is crucial for designing the mud weight; exceeding it can cause tensile failures in the wellbore, leading to mud losses1–4. Advanced predictive models, such as those using machine learning (ML) algorithms like the multi-layer perceptron (MLP) Network combined with optimization techniques, have shown high accuracy in predicting lost circulation events, thus aiding in preemptive measures5,6. Additionally, understanding the location, timing, and severity of mud loss is essential for effective diagnosis and control, with real-time logging parameters play a vital role in monitoring and predicting loss trends. Various materials and methods have been explored to mitigate lost circulation, including the use of self-degradable lost circulation material and polyacrylamide gels, which have proven effective in sealing fractures and reducing permeability, respectively7–10. The integration of geomechanics-based approaches and innovative materials has been successful in field trials, particularly in challenging environments like the Middle East, where natural fractures and high temperatures complicate drilling operations. Overall, a combination of predictive modeling, innovative materials, and real-time monitoring is essential for effectively managing lost circulation in drilling operations, thereby reducing costs and improving operational efficiency. Mud loss volume significantly impacts well control safety; excessive losses can reduce hydrostatic pressure, increasing the risk of a kick. The invasion radius affects formation damage and zonal isolation; extensive invasion can compromise cement bonding and the long-term integrity of the well11–13.

Lost circulation occurs when drilling fluid is lost to the formation, often due to factors such as mud composition, permeability, and pressure differentials . Advanced ML techniques, such as the combination of MLP with optimization algorithms, have shown high accuracy in predicting lost circulation, achieving an root mean square error (RMSE) of 30.85 and an R2 (determination coefficient) of 0.94, which surpasses other methods. Petrophysical characteristics of reservoirs, including their geological conditions, play a crucial role in the occurrence and severity of lost circulation5,14. Fuzzy cluster analysis has been employed to assess these characteristics, allowing for early prediction and classification of lost circulation severity based on geological and technological data15. In the Changqing Mizhi Block, geomechanical analysis revealed that lost circulation primarily occurs in fractured zones, with significant mud loss in sand-mudstone interbeds. The mechanical strength of these formations is insufficient to support wellbore stability under typical drilling fluid densities, leading to both collapse and leakage16–18. Addressing lost circulation requires a comprehensive understanding of the interplay between petrophysical properties, geomechanical conditions, and drilling fluid dynamics, highlighting the need for integrated approaches combining predictive modeling and geological assessments to mitigate risks and optimize drilling operations19,20.

Lost circulation substantially increases non-productive time and raises operational expenditures through fluid replacement costs, remediation efforts, and potential well control incidents. Various studies have explored different ML and artificial neural networks (ANNs) models to address this issue. For instance, Zhu et al.21 developed a PSO-IBP (particle swarm optimization-improved backpropagation neural network) model that integrates particle swarm optimization with an improved backpropagation neural network, achieving a high diagnostic accuracy of 97.60% for lost circulation scenarios. Similarly, Wu et al.22 proposed a hybrid CNN-LSTM (convolutional neural network-long short term memory neural network) model that effectively captures spatial and temporal variations in drilling data, resulting in a 94% accuracy rate and improved monitoring timeliness. Beheshtian et al.5 demonstrated the efficacy of combining optimization algorithms like Social Ski-Driver and particle swarm optimization with MLPs, achieving an R2 of 0.94 in predicting lost circulation. Furthermore, Wen et al.23 identified key drilling parameters such as depth and torque as critical factors influencing lost circulation, with their ANN model achieving 99.2% accuracy. Advanced models like the HALSTM network, which incorporates a hybrid attention mechanism, have also shown superior performance, with a precision of 98% in early warning systems24. In the context of specific geological formations, Hussein and Hamza25 utilized ANNs to predict fluid losses in the Dammam formation, achieving high accuracy with R2 values of 0.9302 and 0.9493. Guillot et al.26 highlighted the application of ML at the well planning stage, using seismic attributes to reduce the probability of lost circulation events by up to 95%. Albattat et al.27 introduced a physics-based ML approach for modeling lost circulation in fractured formations, providing quick estimates of mud loss volumes. Lastly, Mardanirad et al.28 evaluated deep learning models like CNN, GRU (gated recurrent unit), and LSTM for classifying fluid loss severity, with CNN achieving the highest accuracy of 98%. These studies collectively underscore the potential of ML and ANNs in enhancing the prediction, diagnosis, and management of lost circulation in drilling operations, thereby improving safety and reducing costs.

In addition to predicting lost circulation, machine learning has been effectively applied to similar problems involving subsurface fluid flow. For example, Jiang et al.29 developed a backpropagation (BP) neural network approach to predict the lateral sealing ability of faults. This method integrates various controlling factors to evaluate how hydrocarbons are retained, which is structurally comparable to lost circulation, as the capacity of formations to seal is critical in determining fluid loss. Similarly, Fu et al.30 utilized machine learning to assess the factors influencing fault sealing in the Shuangtaizi fault anticline. Their work illustrates how machine learning can identify the primary controls on fluid containment in subsurface environments. These studies demonstrate that machine learning techniques, especially neural networks, are well-suited for capturing complex, non-linear relationships between geological and operational parameters as well as the behaviors related to formation sealing and leakage. This provides a methodological foundation for the current research.

In previous studies, such as those by Wen et al.23 and Guillot et al.26, only a limited number of parameters were utilized to predict lost circulation. Additionally, the impact of logging while drilling data (parameters that are directly influenced by drilling fluid loss) was overlooked. This study pioneers the use of well-log data alongside other drilling parameters and drilling fluid properties to predict lost circulation.

The aim of this study is to predict the zone, volume, and radius of each hole in the drilling fluid using modern and ML methods. To achieve this, data from petrophysical well logs, drilling operational parameters, and drilling fluid characteristics have been used, which include 49 parameters and 246,620 data points from 73 drilled wells. By incorporating petrophysical data, the study takes into account all characteristics of both the formation and the fluid within it for the predictions. Drilling operational parameters and drilling fluid properties are key factors that directly influence mud loss. Selecting these parameters optimally can significantly reduce the loss of drilling fluid. Additionally, petrophysical well-logs are affected by drilling fluid loss and provide insights into the properties of the surrounding rock and the fluid within it. Therefore, these well-logs are crucial for analyzing and interpreting drilling fluid loss31,32. By combining these parameters, we can create a comprehensive and accurate model for predicting drilling fluid loss and optimizing the selection of parameters to prevent or reduce it. Additionally, the application of modern ML techniques, including KAN (Kolmogorov–Arnold network) and its hybrid combination with MLP, is expected to enhance the accuracy of these predictions. The present research innovatively utilizes petrophysical well logs and novel ML methods to forecast lost circulation parameters in oil and gas wells. Petrophysical well-log data provides insight into the properties of rocks and the fluids they contain. This data is significantly influenced by the drilling fluid loss during the drilling process. As a result, accurately analyzing this data is crucial for assessing and predicting drilling mud losses. The findings of this research will help lower the costs associated with drilling oil and gas wells while also mitigating various serious risks involved in the process. This study aims to bridge the gap between scientific research and the practical operations of the oil and gas industry, ultimately influencing the decisions made by policymakers significantly.

Data and method

Data integration

The dataset comprising 246,620 data points from 73 wells in an Iranian oil field was systematically prepared to ensure robust machine learning model performance. Initially, the 49 input parameters, categorized into petrophysical well logs (27 parameters), drilling operational metrics (10 parameters), and drilling fluid characteristics (12 parameters), were integrated from diverse sources including daily drilling reports, geological logs, and final well reports. A total of 49 input parameters were selected based on domain expertise and established drilling engineering principles to cover all known physical factors that contribute to lost circulation. These parameters are categorized into three groups. Firstly, there are the petrophysical well logs, which consist of parameters capturing formation properties such as resistivity, porosity, and lithology that affected by fluid invasion and fracture susceptibility. Secondly, the drilling operational parameters, comprising parameters that affect wellbore stability and the initiation of losses. Lastly, the drilling fluid characteristics consist of parameters that encompass the rheological and chemical properties known to control the severity of losses. This tripartite structure ensures a comprehensive representation of the lost circulation phenomenon. Of these 49 total parameters, 47 serve as input features (27 petrophysical well logs, 10 drilling operational metrics, and 10 drilling fluid characteristics), while the remaining two (mud loss volume and invasion radius) are target variables for prediction. All three models (MLP, KAN, and hybrid MLP–KAN) therefore utilize the complete set of 47 input features.

Preprocessing followed a sequential pipeline: (1) outlier removal using the interquartile range (IQR) method with a threshold of 1.5× the IQR; (2) missing value imputation—forward-fill for temporally continuous drilling parameters, K-nearest neighbors (KNN) for spatially correlated petrophysical logs, and mean imputation for mud properties where other methods were unsuitable; (3) depth-based window smoothing via moving average filters; (4) logarithmic transformation of skewed distributions (e.g., resistivity logs) to approximate normality; and (5) z-score normalization (subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation) applied to all features. Importantly, scaling parameters (mean and standard deviation) were derived exclusively from the training data and then applied to the validation and test sets to prevent data leakage. Min–max scaling was not used, as z-score normalization better preserves the relative importance of features with different units when used with gradient-based optimization. The dataset was partitioned using a stratified 70–15–15 split for training, validation, and testing respectively, maintaining proportional representation of depth intervals, well locations, and invasion severity classes to prevent data leakage. Missing values were addressed through multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE) and forward-fill methods specific to time-series drilling data, preserving the physical relationships inherent in drilling operations. Feature selection was conducted using both filter methods (Pearson correlation analysis to remove highly correlated features with threshold |r| > 0.95) and embedded methods (LASSO regularization) to reduce dimensionality while retaining the most predictive variables. The final feature set was determined by combining filter-based elimination (removing features with |r| 0.95) and LASSO regularization (retaining features with nonzero coefficients). This hybrid approach ensured that only physically meaningful and nonredundant predictors were included in the models, as detailed in Appendix 1. The data split was conducted at the individual data point level using random sampling, which helps to preserve the geological variability within each well that is crucial for identifying local lost circulation patterns. To avoid data leakage and ensure generalizability, we employed a stratified strategy to maintain proportional representation of depth intervals, well locations, and invasion severity classes across the training, validation, and test sets. Additionally, we carried out rigorous well-wise cross-validation, where entire wells were set aside for testing. This approach resulted in an R2 value of 0.938. This high performance, achieved under the strictest conditions of data separation (only 0.0065 lower than the point-split R2 of 0.9445) confirms that our model generalizes well to unseen wells without significant overestimation from point-wise splitting.

To address the issue of missing data, which made up less than 5% of the total dataset, a targeted imputation strategy was implemented based on the characteristics of each variable. Drilling parameters, which display strong temporal continuity, were filled in using a forward-fill method. Petrophysical logs, exhibiting spatial correlation, were processed with K-nearest neighbors (KNN) imputation to maintain formation-specific trends. For certain mud properties, where other imputation methods were not suitable, mean imputation was used conservatively. Following the imputation, rigorous quality assurance measures were conducted. This included range validation against predetermined physical limits (see Appendix 1), consistency checks across wells to identify any spatial discrepancies, and expert-led validation of statistically anomalous values to verify their geological and operational plausibility. This comprehensive approach ensured data completeness while preserving the integrity and physical realism of the dataset for future analysis.

Additional preprocessing included depth-based window smoothing for petrophysical well-logs using moving average filters to reduce measurement noise, transformation of skewed distributions (e.g., resistivity logs) via logarithmic scaling to approximate normality, and creation of engineered features such as depth-normalized drilling parameters and mud property ratios known to influence lost circulation mechanics. To ensure the reliability and consistency of the input data for predictive modeling, a comprehensive preprocessing pipeline was applied to all well logs. The resistivity logs (ILD, ILM, ILS, RT, RXO) underwent depth matching, environmental corrections, and logarithmic transformation. Porosity well-logs (NPHI, PHIE, DT) received corrections based on lithology and normalization, while density well-logs (RHOB) were adjusted for borehole conditions. Gamma ray well-logs (CGR, SGR) were standardized to API units and then used to calculate shale volume. A uniform procedure was applied to all processed logs, which included aligning depths to a common reference, removing outliers using a three-sigma (3σ) criterion, and performing final min–max normalization to scale features to a consistent range. This rigorous preprocessing sequence was essential for reducing measurement artifacts, aligning varying well-log responses, and preparing a clean, normalized dataset suitable for machine learning analysis. Feature engineering was carefully balanced to enhance predictive power while maintaining interpretability, with all transformations validated for physical plausibility to ensure parameter distributions aligned with model assumptions while retaining the original petrophysical and operational significance critical for lost circulation prediction. MLP

A multi-layer perceptron is a type of ANNs that consists of multiple layers of nodes, including an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. Each node, or neuron, in a layer is connected to every node in the subsequent layer, forming a fully connected network36,37. The MLP operates by receiving input data, processing it through the network layers using weighted connections, and producing an output. The working steps of an MLP involve a feedforward process where input data is passed through the network to generate predictions, followed by a backpropagation process to adjust the weights based on the error between the predicted and actual outputs. This iterative training process continues until the network’s performance is optimized31,38,39. One of the key advantages of MLPs is their ability to model complex, non-linear relationships, making them highly effective for tasks such as classification and regression across various domains. MLPs have demonstrated high performance in classification tasks, with accuracy rates ranging from 62.89 to 100%, and an average performance of 91.98% in various studies40,41. Additionally, MLPs can be optimized through hyperparameter tuning, which involves selecting the appropriate number of hidden layers and neurons, as well as initial weights, to enhance convergence and reduce training time42. Despite their effectiveness, traditional training methods like backpropagation can lead to local minima, but recent advancements such as the multi-verse optimizer have been developed to overcome these limitations, further improving the training efficiency of MLPs43. Moreover, MLPs are versatile and can be implemented using open-source platforms, making them accessible for a wide range of applications. Therefore, the MLP is a foundational component of deep learning, offering significant advantages in terms of flexibility, accuracy, and applicability across diverse fields.

The MLP in this study is a feedforward artificial neural network with an input layer of 47 neurons for the input features, three fully connected hidden layers of 15 neurons each, and an output layer with two neurons representing mud loss volume and invasion radius. It uses the Levenberg–Marquardt backpropagation algorithm to achieve rapid convergence by combining gradient descent with Gauss–Newton optimization. Activation functions between layers employ hyperbolic tangent sigmoid transformations to capture complex non-linear relationships in drilling data. The architecture is designed with a 70–15–15 split for training, validation, and testing, incorporating early stopping to prevent overfitting. The fully connected structure allows for comprehensive learning patterns, but the architecture has about 1125 trainable parameters, necessitating careful regularization through L2 weight decay. While its interpretability is limited due to its black-box nature, connection weight magnitudes can provide insights into parameter importance. Batch normalization layers enhance training efficiency by stabilizing learning dynamics across diverse parameter scales in drilling data.

KAN

KANs represent a significant advancement in neural network architecture, offering enhanced interpretability, efficiency, and adaptability compared to traditional MLPs (as shown in Fig. 3). The KAN is based on the Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem, which offers a solid mathematical foundation for function approximation. This theorem states that any multivariate continuous function defined on a bounded domain can be expressed as a finite composition of univariate functions and addition operations. This structural approach differs significantly from that of the MLP, which uses fixed activation functions at its network nodes. In a KAN, the theorem is implemented by placing learnable univariate functions (typically parameterized as adaptive B-splines or other flexible forms) on the edges of the computational graph. This design transforms the network into a composition of interpretable and adjustable univariate transformations, rather than relying on a weighted sum of fixed nonlinearities. The primary working mechanism of KANs involves replacing fixed linear weights with learnable spline-parametrized univariate functions, which allows for dynamic learning of activation patterns and significantly enhances interpretability44–46. This architecture is inspired by the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem, which facilitates the modeling of complex data patterns and nonlinear relationships. KANs have been applied across various domains, including time series forecasting, where variants like T-KAN (time series Kolmogorov–Arnold Network) and MT-KAN (multivariate time series Kolmogorov–Arnold network) have demonstrated superior predictive accuracy and interpretability by detecting concept drift and leveraging complex variable relationships47,48. In scientific discovery, KANs have shown potential in generating symbolic outputs, providing insights into mathematical and physical laws. The advantages of KANs extend to computational mechanics, where KANs outperform MLPs in generating optimal quantum circuit configurations44,49. The KAN is an innovative neural architecture based on the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem, which states that any multivariate continuous function can be represented as a composition of univariate functions and additions. Unlike traditional multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) with fixed activation functions, KAN uses learnable spline-parametrized univariate functions on the edges, allowing for dynamic activation patterns. Its architecture includes an input layer with 47 nodes, two hidden layers of 15 nodes each that use B-spline basis functions of order 3, and an output layer with 2 nodes, leading to around 3500 trainable parameters primarily in spline coefficients. KAN stands out for its interpretability, as the learned functions can be visualized to show how input features transform through the network. It also employs adaptive grid refinement during training, adjusting spline knot positions based on local complexity for computational efficiency. Though KAN is sensitive to initialization and requires careful regularization to avoid overfitting, it effectively captures feature interactions, making it well-suited for the complex relationships seen in lost circulation phenomena.

Hybrid models

Hybrid ML models, which combine various ML techniques and optimization algorithms, have shown significant promise across diverse fields, enhancing prediction accuracy and efficiency. Managing high-frequency torsional oscillations (HFTO) is essential for maintaining equipment reliability. Furthermore, systematic reviews highlight the potential of hybrid methods to overcome the limitations of individual algorithms by leveraging their strengths, thus enhancing efficiency in clustering and classification tasks. These hybrid models not only improve prediction accuracy but also offer robust frameworks adaptable to various datasets and applications, demonstrating their versatility and effectiveness in complex problem-solving scenarios50,51. The hybrid MLP–KAN model developed in this study integrates two architectures into a sequential pipeline. The MLP component serves as a feature extractor, while the KAN functions as an interpretable regressor. The model consists of two stages: first, a 3-layer MLP with a structure of 47–15–15–15 compresses raw input features into a 15-dimensional latent representation. Next, a 2-layer KAN network processes this latent representation using learnable spline functions to predict mud loss volume and invasion radius. With around 2300 parameters (1125 in MLP and 1175 in KAN), the model balances complexity to prevent overfitting while maintaining expressive power. A key feature is the gradient flow mechanism, which supports end-to-end training while keeping KAN interpretable. Regularization ensures that MLP output features are semantically meaningful. The model exhibits robustness to input noise, thanks to the MLP’s denoising ability, and enhances generalization through KAN’s smooth function approximations. Training employs a composite loss function that combines mean squared error for accuracy with regularization for weight sparsity and spline smoothness. This architecture successfully addresses the “interpretability-accuracy trade-off”, achieving high predictive performance and providing insights into the factors contributing to lost circulation through visualizable KAN transformations of MLP features. The hybrid MLP–KAN architecture, illustrated in Fig. 4, operates as a sequential two-stage model. In the first stage, the MLP component processes the 47 input features through three hidden layers (15 neurons each) to extract a 15-dimensional latent representation. This latent vector is then fed into the KAN component, which applies learnable spline functions across two hidden layers to predict mud loss volume and invasion radius. This design leverages the MLP’s representational power and the KAN’s interpretability, creating a synergistic framework for accurate and explainable predictions.

Results and discussion

Three used methods (MLP, KAN, and KAN–MLP) have been employed to predict the zone, volume, and radius of drilling fluid invasion. Each of the MLP and KAN models possesses unique characteristics, which are detailed in Table 1. Both models utilize a backpropagation algorithm that significantly reduces errors. Bayesian optimization with Gaussian processes was employed to minimize the validation root mean square error (RMSE) as the objective function. The key hyperparameters that were tuned included: the number of hidden layers (ranging from 1 to 5), the number of neurons per layer (ranging from 5 to 50), the learning rate (from 1e−5 to 1e−1), the batch size (from 32 to 512), and the regularization strength (L2, ranging from 1e−6 to 1e−2). We conducted 100 optimization iterations for each model. The Levenberg–Marquardt (LM) optimization algorithm was employed for training due to its hybrid second-order properties, which balance gradient-descent robustness against poor initialization with the rapid, near-quadratic convergence of the Gauss–Newton method near an optimum. This combination is particularly advantageous for regression tasks with smooth, well-behaved loss landscapes and moderately sized datasets. In preliminary comparative experiments, LM demonstrated superior convergence speed and final error minimization relative to first-order optimizers such as Adam and stochastic gradient descent (SGD), thereby ensuring efficient and stable parameter estimation for the proposed models52,53. Additionally, a hybrid model combining MLP and KAN, referred to as the MLP-KAN hybrid model, was analyzed to evaluate its performance. In fact, this hybrid model uses the outputs from the MLP network as inputs to the KAN network, ultimately producing results from the combination of both models. This approach allocates the majority of the data for training the model, while a smaller validation set is used to fine-tune hyperparameters and prevent overfitting. Finally, an independent test set is employed to evaluate the model’s performance. This ratio effectively balances the need for enough training data with the importance of robust validation and testing. The comparison between MLPs and KANs reveals distinct advantages and limitations for each model across various applications. MLPs generally outperform KANs in tasks such as ML55. In terms of hardware implementation, KANs require substantially more resources than MLPs, making MLPs more efficient for practical applications, especially in complex datasets56. Despite this, KANs have shown promise in specific areas such as physics-informed ML models and deep operator networks, where modified versions of KANs can perform comparably to MLP-based models, although they may lack robustness57. KANs also demonstrate faster learning and parameter efficiency in certain applications, such as the discovery of Deep Koopman operators, where they outperform MLPs in training speed and accuracy58. Overall, while KANs offer innovative architectural features and potential advantages in specific contexts, MLPs continue to be a reliable and efficient choice across a broader range of applications, balancing accuracy, resource efficiency, and scalability55.

Compared to prior studies such as Beheshtian et al.5 and Wen et al.23, which reported R2 values up to 0.9 using conventional MLPs or hybrid networks, the used MLP–KAN hybrid achieves comparable or superior accuracy while offering enhanced interpretability through KAN’s spline-based transformations. Also, the current study utilized a comprehensive set of parameters related to the mud wasting phenomenon, which had not been addressed in earlier research.

The substantial geological variability across the 73 studied wells, including lithological changes from shale-dominated sequences to carbonate reservoirs, variable fracture densities, and depositional heterogeneity, was addressed through several integrated strategies. Our inclusion of 27 petrophysical well logs provided direct measurements of formation variability, while stratified sampling during data partitioning ensured proportional representation of different geological units. Feature engineering incorporated depth-normalized parameters and lithological indices such as shale volume and mineralogical ratios, serving as proxies for geological changes. The hybrid MLP–KAN architecture itself offers inherent adaptability to heterogeneity, with MLP capturing complex patterns across diverse formations and KAN’s spline functions adapting to different parameter regimes. SHAP analysis confirmed that feature importance varies contextually, resistivity logs dominate in carbonate zones while gamma ray measurements gain significance in shale intervals, demonstrating the model’s sensitivity to geological context. Nevertheless, rapid lithological transitions and extreme heterogeneity in certain intervals present challenges, indicating that while our framework shows robustness within the studied field, application to radically different geological settings would benefit from explicit facies classification and transfer learning techniques in future implementations.

Feature and criteria weighting methods in ML are diverse and have been extensively studied to enhance the performance of algorithms by assigning appropriate importance to different features61. The Intuitionistic Fuzzy Network Method addresses multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) problems by converting incomplete expert rankings into a weighted criteria importance network using triangular intuitionistic fuzzy numbers, which is particularly useful in scenarios with incomplete information and a large number of criteria62. Feature weighting methods can be categorized based on the learning approach, the methodology for calculating weights, and the feedback from the ML algorithm (which has been used in this study and the assigned weights are shown in Fig. 8), providing a comprehensive taxonomy for selecting appropriate strategies in real-world data analysis63. The correlation coefficient with min–max weighted approach integrates normalization with feature weighting by using correlation coefficients to enhance classification accuracy, demonstrating significant improvements in classifiers like support vector machines and neural networks64. The covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy optimization method is another novel approach that optimizes feature weights for classifiers such as Naive Bayes, K-nearest neighbors, and Random Forest, showing improved performance in ensemble algorithms65. Additionally, particle swarm optimization has been applied to learning vector quantization classifiers, effectively finding attribute weights that enhance classifier accuracy66. These methods highlight the importance of feature weighting in improving ML model performance by tailoring the influence of features based on their relevance and relationships with the target outcomes.

Despite its strong predictive performance, the proposed hybrid MLP–KAN model has certain limitations that should be considered. Its accuracy is sensitive to the quality of input data, especially in geologically complex or poorly logged intervals where preprocessing assumptions (such as the spatial continuity required for KNN imputation) may not hold. Additionally, the model’s generalizability is limited by the geological and operational context of the training data; its performance may decrease in lithologically distinct formations or under significantly different drilling practices that are not represented in the dataset. While the model offers improved interpretability compared to conventional MLPs, it does not explicitly incorporate physical laws. This limitation affects its ability to extrapolate beyond conditions that have been empirically observed or to provide causal explanations for predicted losses.

From a practical perspective, the hybrid model introduces higher computational overhead than standard MLPs, which could impact its deployment in real-time or resource-limited field environments. Furthermore, the framework relies on expert-driven feature engineering and log preprocessing, which reduces its plug-and-play applicability in data-scarce or fully automated settings. The current implementation also lacks rigorous uncertainty quantification, yielding point predictions without confidence intervals that are crucial for assessing operational risk. Future improvements should focus on integrating domain-aware regularization, enhancing geological representativeness through transfer learning, and incorporating probabilistic outputs to increase both reliability and usability across diverse field conditions.

The study acknowledges several critical limitations and potential biases arising from the characteristics of the data and the assumptions made in modeling. First, the research is geographically confined to a single oil field in Iran, which restricts the ability to generalize the findings to other locations. Additionally, there is a retrospective operational bias; historical practices that were less than optimal may distort predictive patterns, making them seem like causal relationships. There are also inconsistencies in measurements across different wells, resulting in varied data quality. The study simplifies dynamic mud properties by treating them as static parameters, which ignores time-dependent changes in their behavior. Furthermore, it assumes technological consistency across the multi-year dataset, despite advancements in drilling systems over that time. Lastly, the analysis may suffer from omitted variable bias, as it does not account for unmeasured geomechanical properties such as in-situ stress orientations and natural fracture densities. Also, the model’s accuracy may decrease in highly heterogeneous formations, under extreme drilling conditions (e.g., ultrahigh pressure/high temperature), or in wells with poor log quality. Additionally, its generalizability is limited to geologically similar basins without transfer learning. These limitations define the boundaries of the models’ applicability and highlight the need for careful interpretation that is aware of the subject matter. They also suggest opportunities for methodological improvement, including multi-basin validation, the integration of high-frequency drilling dynamics, and the use of advanced causal inference techniques to better distinguish between operational and geological drivers in future research.

Additionally, researchers can explore the application of sensitivity analysis methods based on the results of this study. These studies can significantly impact the monitoring of drilling risks, reduce drilling costs, and improve the management of drilling operations.

Conclusions

This study successfully developed and validated a novel hybrid machine learning framework integrating multi-layer perceptron (MLP) and Kolmogorov–Arnold network (KAN) architectures for predictive lost circulation management. The hybrid MLP–KAN model demonstrated superior performance, achieving an R2 of 0.9445 and RMSE of 0.0143, representing a 36.2% improvement in predictive accuracy over standalone models. Analysis of 246,620 data points from 73 wells revealed three distinct invasion zones at depths of 1800–2000 m, 2300–2600 m, and 2800–3000 m, with average mud loss volumes of 223.9 bbl, 431.3 bbl, and 541.0 bbl, respectively. SHAP interpretability analysis identified sonic transit time (DT), bit size, mud weight, and calcium content as the most influential predictive features, with physical reasoning confirming that thickness governs exposed formation area while deep resistivity (ILD) indicates fracture connectivity and invasion severity.

The practical implications of this work are significant for drilling operations. The framework enables proactive loss prevention through early zone identification, allowing operators to implement targeted mitigation strategies: lightweight mud systems for shallow zones, bridging materials for intermediate fracture-dominated intervals, and engineered lost circulation materials for deep high-volume losses. Field implementation of this framework would require validation through controlled trials, but the predictive accuracy achieved suggests substantial potential for reducing non-productive time and optimizing fluid design costs relative to current practice. Methodologically, this research represents the first application of KAN and hybrid MLP-KAN architectures to lost circulation prediction, offering an interpretable AI solution that bridges data-driven analytics with drilling physics principles.

Several limitations provide avenues for future research. The model’s generalizability should be validated across multiple geological basins with varying formation characteristics. Real-time integration with streaming drilling data would enhance dynamic prediction capabilities. Incorporating physics-informed neural networks could embed geomechanical constraints directly into the model architecture. Additionally, future versions should include probabilistic outputs with confidence intervals for improved risk assessment and decision support. Ultimately, this work provides a robust, scalable framework that advances lost circulation prediction from reactive troubleshooting to proactive management, contributing to safer, more efficient, and cost-effective drilling operations worldwide.
