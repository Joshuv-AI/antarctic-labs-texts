Study on the Performance of Ti-MOF-Modified Bi2O2CO3 Photocatalyst for the Removal of Organic Matter
Authors: Xingwang Wang, Changchao Hu, Rui Zhang, Wenjie Tan, Zhiwei Tang, Pengyuan Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this work, a MIL-125­(Ti)/Bi2O2CO3 (MIL/BOC) composite photocatalyst was fabricated via a facile solvothermal strategy to construct a Z-scheme heterojunction. Comprehensive characterization, including SEM, XRD, XPS, UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy, PL, EIS, and BET analysis, confirmed the successful integration of MIL-125­(Ti) with BOC and revealed enhanced physicochemical properties of the composite. The photocatalytic performance toward tetracycline hydrochloride (TCH) degradation was systematically evaluated by optimizing catalyst dosage and initial pollutant concentration. The MIL/BOC composite exhibited significantly improved activity compared with pristine BOC, achieving a degradation efficiency of up to 95% for 20 mg·L–1 TCH. This enhancement is attributed to the synergistic effects of increased specific surface area, improved light absorption, and efficient charge carrier separation. Radical trapping experiments demonstrated that •O2 – and h+ played dominant roles in the degradation process. With the aid of the GC-MS analysis method, the degradation path of OTC was analyzed. Overall, this study may guide the development of new photocatalysts that respond to visible light and can efficiently remove various organic matter.

Introduction

Antibiotics are a widespread class of medication that is used extensively throughout the world to treat illnesses in both humans and animals, yet they are frequently disposed of incorrectly into wastewater after usage. It has been observed that antibiotic concentrations in untreated home wastewater range from 100 ng/L to 6 μg/L, whereas antibiotic concentrations in wastewater from hospitals and the pharmaceutical industry are between 100 and 500 mg/L. , The majority of antibiotics are structurally stable and cannot be completely digested by either people or animals. Ecology and public health are seriously threatened by the growing concentration of antibiotics in aquatic environments. − As a result, there is an urgent need to create an effective and cost-efficient antibiotic treatment method. Physical, chemical, and biological treatments are now used to treat wastewater containing antibiotics. Among these, photocatalytic oxidation technologywhich has recently emerged as a wastewater treatment research hotspotcan quickly and efficiently break down trace organic contaminants in a mild environment without causing secondary pollution.

It is essential to research photocatalysts for photocatalytic oxidation technology. Further investigation into more effective photocatalysts is still required, though, since conventional photocatalysts are constrained by low solar energy utilization (3–5%). , The mineral Bi is abundant and inexpensive, and Bibased photocatalysts have good application prospects in photocatalysis due to their low toxicity and unique photoelectric properties and energy band structure (e.g., Bi2S3, BiVO4, , Bi2WO6, BiOX (X = Cl, Br, I), Bi2O2CO3 and Bi4Ti3O12 ). Among them, because of its low toxicity and other features, bismuth subcarbonate (Bi2O2CO3) has emerged as a promising option in the hunt for novel high-efficiency photocatalysts. The layered structure of Bi2O2CO3, an oxide of the Aurivillius type, is made up of alternating [Bi2O2]2+ and CO3 2– layers. − This structure promotes the formation of plate-like nanostructures and speeds up the transfer of photoelectrons to the material’s surface. Furthermore, the photocatalytic potential of Bi2O2CO3 is enhanced by its strong electron conductivity and unusual structure. However, because Bi2O2CO3 has a broadband gap (2.87–3.55 eV) and can only absorb a small part of sunlight, its practical application in visible light is severely limited. − Some recent efforts have been made to increase the photocatalytic activity of Bi2O2CO3, such as the doping of elements (N and Cl) and the deposition of metal (Ag), as well as the development of heterostructures (Ag6Si2O7/Bi2O2CO3, Bi2O2CO3/ZnFe2O4, Ag2O/Bi2O2CO3, Ag2S@CdZnS, etc.). In recent years, heterojunction has been demonstrated in photocatalysts as one of the most inventive approaches to boost visible light responsiveness and facilitate the separation of photogenerated electron–hole pairs. −

The porous organic–inorganic hybrid materials known as metal–organic skeletons (MOFs) are self-assembled from metal ions or metal clusters and organic ligands. They offer several benefits, including a large specific surface area, high porosity, adjustable pore structure, and numerous active sites. − MOFs are widely used in adsorption, storage, chemical separation, and photocatalytic applications. Numerous MOFs possessing photocatalytic activity have been found, including MIL-101­(Fe), ZIF-8, and NH2–UiO-66­(Zr). The greater bandgap of MIL-125­(Ti), a traditional MOFs material with cheap preparation costs and easy accessibility, may restrict its use in the photocatalytic field. , Fortunately, because compounding with standard inorganic semiconductors offers significant prospects, we investigated the construction and activity of the resulting nanocomposites.

In this paper, we used a solvothermal method to create hierarchical tandem heterojunctions by compositing Bi2O2CO3 and porous MIL-125­(Ti) to overcome the problem of high combination rate of photogenerated carriers, as well as to solve the wide bandgap defects of BOC and improve the absorption of visible light in the materials. Subsequently, the physicochemical properties of the MIL-125­(Ti)/BOC composites were characterized and the degradation performance of the composites against organic matter was studied in depth.

Experimental Section

Material and Reagents

Bismuth nitrate pentahydrate (Bi­(NO3)3·5H2O), sodium Carbonate (Na2CO3), tetrabutyl titanate (TBT), 1,4-benzenedicarboxylic acid (H2DBC), N′N-dimetylformanide (DMF), methanol (CH3OH), isopropanol (IPA), p-benzoquinone (BQ), ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid disodium (EDTA-2Na) and sodium sulfate (Na2SO4) were purchased from Kelong Chemical Reagent Factory­(China). All chemicals were of analytical grade and were used without further purification.

Synthesis of MIL-125­(Ti)

MIL-125­(Ti) was synthesized via a solvothermal method. First, TBTwas dissolved in DMF and stirred at room temperature for 1 h. Separately, H2DBC was dispersed in CH3OH and stirred for 1 h under the same conditions. To ensure successful synthesis, the two precursor solutions were then combined in a single beaker and further stirred for 1 h until a uniform white mixed solution was obtained. Subsequently, the resulting suspension was transferred into a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 160 °C for 24 h. After naturally cooling to room temperature, the product was collected, washed several times with DMF and CH3OH, and then separated by centrifugation. Finally, the obtained solid was dried at 60 °C to yield the target product.

Synthesis of MIL-125­(Ti)/Bi2O2CO3 Composite Photocatalyst

First, a particular amount of MIL-125­(Ti) was dissolved in 20 mL glycol and constantly magnetically stirred. After ultrasound for 20 min, 1.455 g Bi­(NO)3·5H2O was added to the aforesaid solution, and the resulting solution was denoted as solution A. Under the same conditions, 0.159 g of Na2CO3 was dissolved in 10 mL of glycol at the same time, and the resulting solution was designated solution B. Solution B was then gradually poured into solution A, and the mixture was magnetically spun for half an hour. Finally, the resulting mixture was added into a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated up to 160 °C for 18 h. After cooling, the required photocatalyst MIL-125­(Ti)/Bi2O2CO3 was obtained by repeated centrifugal washing with pure water and anhydrous ethanol and drying at 60 °C. The produced composites were given the designation MIL/BOC-x (where x = 15, 20, and 25) according to the mass percentage of MIL-125 (Ti) that was added.

Under the same circumstances, with the same other parameters, pure Bi2O2CO3(BOC) was produced.

Characterization of Prepared Materials

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Carl Zeiss AG, ZEISS EV0MA15) equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy to identify element composition and distribution was used to examine the morphology of all samples. The crystal structure of the materials was determined using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, PANalytical B.V., X Pert PRO MPD) with Cu Ka in the 2θ range of 5 to 70°. The valence and composition of the produced materials were examined using X-ray electron spectroscopy (XPS, Nexsa, Thermo Scientific). UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV–vis DRS, PerkinEImerLambda850, Platinum Elmer Instrument Co., Ltd., American) was used to determine the optical absorption property of samples. An electrochemical workstation was used to measure the flat-band potentials and electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) of several materials.

Photocatalytic Activity Measurement

The degradation of tetracycline under visible light irradiation was used to determine the photocatalytic activity of the produced catalysts. To achieve an adsorption–desorption equilibrium, 50 mg of the photocatalyst was typically disseminated in a solution of tetracycline (20 mg/L, 100 mL), and the suspension was constantly agitated in the dark for 60 min before light irradiation. Under an incandescent light, 3 mL of the sample suspension was sampled at 10 min intervals, then centrifuged, and measured at 200–800 nm by a Hitachi UV1800 UV–vis spectrophotometer.

Traps were also added to identify the specific active free radicals created during photocatalysis. As a scavenger for hydroxyl radicals (•OH–), superoxide radicals (•O2 –), and photogenerated holes (h+), respectively, the reaction system was supplemented with 10 mmol/L isopropanol (IPA), 1 mmol/L p-benzoquinone (BQ), and 1 mmol/L ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid disodium (EDTA-2Na).

Results and Discussion

The composite, as depicted in Figure c, has a microsphere structure resembling that of pure BOC, and MIL-125­(Ti) undergoes a morphological change from a uniformly smooth block to an irregular block. Furthermore, the presence and uniform distribution of elements Bi, C, O, and Ti in the composites were confirmed by elemental mapping pictures of MIL/BOC-20 (Figure d), suggesting that MIL-125­(Ti) was nearly evenly distributed on the BOC surface. The growth of MIL-125­(Ti) nanoblocks on BOC flower-like microspheres was demonstrated by the results, suggesting that MIL-125­(Ti) and BOC produced the heterogeneous structure of the composite. The uniform spatial distribution of O, Bi, C, and Ti elements observed in the EDS mapping images indicates the homogeneous dispersion of CoS/BOC and MIL-125­(Ti), as well as the effective interfacial integration between these components.

Band Structures and Optical Property

The UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectra (DRS) of BOC, MIL-125­(Ti), and MIL/BOC are displayed in Figure a, while the corresponding band gap energies are presented in Figure b. BOC primarily absorbs in the ultraviolet region, whereas MIL/BOC exhibits a slightly red-shifted absorption edge relative to BOC, suggesting that the incorporation of MIL-125­(Ti) enhances light absorption and extends the response into the visible region. The change of optical absorption properties of MIL after coupling with BOC implies the modulation of the band gap in the MIL/BOC composite. The estimated band gap energies (E g) values of BOC (n = 2), and MIL-125­(Ti) (n = 2) were 3.38 and 3.82 eV, respectively (Figure b). The potential for the conductive band (CB) and valence band (VB) of MIL-125­(Ti) and BOC were calculated using electrochemical Mott–Schottky to demonstrate the impact of the matched energy band between the two on the enhancement of the photoactivity. The Mott–Schottky curves for MIL-125­(Ti) and BOC, as shown in Figure d,e, had positive slopes, indicating that they were both n-type semiconductors. The flat band potentials (E fb) were measured to be +0.63 V for BOC and −0.69 V for MIL-125­(Ti) versus SCE, corresponding to +0.87 V and −0.45 V versus NHE, respectively. Assuming the conduction band edge (E CB) of n-type semiconductors lies approximately 0.1–0.3 eV more negative than E fb, the ECB values were estimated as ca. +0.67 V (BOC) and −0.65 V (MIL-125­(Ti)) vs NHE.

To confirm the charge transfer and separation, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy was used to analyze the BOC, MIL-125­(Ti), and MIL/BOC samples, as illustrated in Figure a. Since the semicircular radius of the Nyquist plot generally exhibits a positive correlation with the electrode/electrolyte interface resistance, it can serve as an indirect indicator of the effectiveness of charge transfer and separation at the photocatalyst interface. At high frequencies, the semicircle of MIL/BOC-20 was significantly smaller than that of BOC, showing that the interfacial electron transfer resistance of MIL/BOC-20 was very low, which can be attributed to the establishment of heterojunction. The PL spectrum was analyzed to provide more insight into the charge separation and recombination behavior of the photocatalysts as-prepared. Higher recombination rates will result in an increased fluorescence signal. The strength of the PL intensity indicates the rate of electron and hole recombination in the material. Figure b shows the steady-state PL spectra of the as-prepared MIL-125­(Ti), BOC, and MIL/BOC-20, respectively. Among all the samples, the MIL/BOC sample had the smallest emission peak, indicating the lowest efficiency of photogenerated carrier recombination.

Photocatalytic Degradation of TCH

Effect of MIL-125­(Ti) Weight Ratios on the Degradation of TCH

The photocatalytic degradation performance of several materials was investigated by degrading TCH at concentrations of 20 and 40 mg/L. The samples were disseminated in TCH solution and physically mixed in the dark for 60 min before photocatalytic degradation to achieve adsorption equilibrium. As shown in Figure a,b, MIL/BOC composites significantly improved TCH (20 mg/L) removal efficiency compared to pure BOC, with the highest removal performances of MIL/BOC-20 and MIL/BOC-25, which both showed more than 94% TCH removal rate within 60 min and their corresponding reaction rate constants were categorized as 0.02873 and 0.0291 min–1, both of which were 6 times higher than pure BOC (0.00469 min–1). To better verify the photocatalytic performance of the composites, we degraded a higher concentration of TCH solution as shown in Figure c,d. From the figure, it can be seen that MIL/BOC-20 has the highest degradation rate for TCH solution with a concentration of 40 mg/L. The removal rate still reaches 91.9% after 60 min of light, and the reaction rate constant is 0.02539 min–1, which is 6 times higher than that of pure BOC (0.00426 min–1). Therefore, the MIL/BOC-20 composite has the best photocatalytic performance.

Effect of TCH Concentration

It is necessary to comprehend how the initial concentration of TCH affects the degradation efficiency to investigate the photodegradation capacity of MIL/BOC composite photocatalysts. When the starting substrate concentration of TCH solution was increased from 20 to 70 mg/L, as shown in Figure , the degradation rate gradually reduced. When the concentration is low, the removal rate of the TCH solution can reach more than 90% in 60 min. When the concentration continued to increase to 70 mg/L, the composite material removed only 62.5% of the TCH solution in 60 min. The above phenomenon is mainly since a certain amount of photocatalyst can only produce a certain amount of active substances to promote the photocatalytic degradation reaction.

Reusability of Composites

The reusability of photocatalysts is a crucial factor to consider while researching materials. Considering the maximum and high efficiency of the application, 30 mg/L TCH solution was chosen as the degradation object for the cycling experiment. After identifying the optimal experimental conditions, 50 mg of MIL/BOC-20 photocatalyst was introduced and its stability was assessed by repeatedly degrading a 30 mg/L TCH solution. Figure a shows the results. After recycling the catalyst four times, the removal rate of TCH decreased from 91.9 to 81.2%. The MIL/BOC-20 composite material has been demonstrated to be a stable photocatalyst since the degradation rate did not significantly drop during the procedure. In addition, the crystal structure of MIL/BOC-20 remained essentially unchanged after four cycles (Figure b), which is favorable for practical applications.

Photocatalytic Mechanism

As presented in Figure , radical scavenging tests were performed to identify the key reactive species involved in TCH degradation over MIL/BOC-20. Upon the addition of IPA, EDTA-2Na, and BQ, the removal efficiency decreased from 91.9 to 85.5%, 72.4%, and 67.9%, respectively. This illustrates that h+ participates in the degradation process even though •O2 – radicals are the most active species.

Trapping experiment of TCH (30 mg/L) degradation with different scavengers.

Based on the above results, a possible photocatalytic mechanism for tetracycline hydrochloride (TCH) degradation over the MIL/BOC composite is proposed, as illustrated in Figure . The MIL/BOC composite exhibits a higher specific surface area than pure BOC, which enhances pollutant adsorption. The formation of a Z-scheme heterojunction between MIL-125­(Ti) and Bi2O2CO3 extends visible-light absorption and facilitates efficient charge separation while maintaining strong redox ability.

Schematic of photocatalytic reaction mechanism over MIL/BOC nanocomposite under visible light irradiation.

Upon visible-light irradiation, both MIL-125­(Ti) and BOC are photoexcited to generate electron–hole pairs. In the Z-scheme system, the photogenerated electrons in the conduction band (CB) of BOC recombine with the holes in the valence band (VB) of MIL-125­(Ti) at the interface. This recombination pathway promotes spatial separation of the remaining charge carriers.

As a result, the electrons accumulated in the CB of MIL-125­(Ti) (E CB ≈ −0.65 eV vs NHE) possess sufficient reduction ability to convert adsorbed O2 into superoxide radicals (•O2 –), as this potential is more negative than the O2/•O2 – redox potential (−0.33 eV). Meanwhile, the holes retained in the VB of BOC (E VB ≈ 4.05 eV vs NHE) exhibit strong oxidation ability and can oxidize H2O or OH– to generate hydroxyl radicals (•OH).

These reactive species (•O2 – and •OH) synergistically attack TCH molecules, ultimately leading to their degradation and mineralization into harmless products. Therefore, the enhanced photocatalytic performance of MIL/BOC can be attributed to improved light absorption, efficient charge separation via the Z-scheme heterojunction, and the preservation of strong redox potentials.

Analysis of Degradation Pathways and Product Ecotoxicity

Identification of intermediates is an important step in photocatalysis to understand the extent and pathways of the degradation process. During the photocatalytic degradation of OTC (20 mg/L) by MIL/BOC composites, many intermediates are derived from the original OTC molecule. As the structure of OTC is disrupted, the reaction products become more complex. Therefore, there will be some small molecule intermediates. To identify more intermediates, the GC-MS technique was used to find the intermediate compounds and the possible degradation pathways of OTC were inferred as shown in Figure . It can be inferred that in the presence of free radicals, the photocatalytic degradation of OTC produces several intermediate products with larger molecular weights (P1, P2, P3), which are then further decomposed into intermediate products with smaller molecular weights (P4, P5, P6, P7). These intermediate products are produced in two main ways: one is a ring-opening reaction and the other is the cleavage of the central carbon. Finally, these intermediates are oxidized to form CO2, H2O, and NH4 +, among others. In addition, some intermediates cannot be detected by GC-MS because of their short lifetimes.

Possible OTC photocatalytic degradation pathways.

In addition, the acute toxicity, developmental toxicity, and mutagenicity of OTC and its seven intermediates were evaluated and studied based on the quantitative structure-effect relationship (QSAR) method using toxicity evaluation software (T.E.S.T.) as shown in Figure . As can be seen in Figure a, the lethal concentration of OTC for 50% fathead minnow after 96 h (LC50, 96h) was 1.12 mg/L, which was significantly lower than most of the degradation intermediates except P1 and P3, and the lethal concentration of the intermediates for 50% Daphnia magna after 48 h (LC50, 48 h) was higher than that of OTC, except for P1, P2, P3, and P7. For the 50% rat lethal dose after oral administration (LD50), all intermediates except P2 and P3 had LD50 higher than that of OTC (Figure b). The above results demonstrate the decreased acute toxicity of most of the degradation products. In addition, it can be seen from Figure c that the MIL/BOC photocatalytic reaction system also reduced the developmental toxicity of all the products compared to OTC (0.83), although all the intermediates except P3, P5, and P6 had higher mutagenicity than OTC. As can be seen from the above data, although some of the intermediates produced by the photocatalytic degradation of OTC are still toxic, it is believed that further photocatalytic technology can realize the total nontoxicity of the products.

Conclusions

In this study, MIL-125/BOC composite photocatalysts were successfully fabricated via a solvothermal method, and their structural characteristics and photocatalytic performance were systematically evaluated. The results demonstrated that MIL-125­(Ti) was uniformly distributed on the surface of BOC nanoflowers, and the specific surface area of the composite significantly increased from 21.54 to 72.44 m2·g–1, providing more active sites and enhancing pollutant adsorption.

Photocatalytic experiments revealed that under visible light irradiation, MIL/BOC achieved a removal efficiency exceeding 95% for 20 mg/L tetracycline, with an apparent rate constant of 0.02873 min–1, which is approximately 6 times higher than that of pure BOC. Even at a higher concentration of 40 mg/L, a removal efficiency of 91.9% was maintained, demonstrating excellent performance under high pollutant concentrations. Additionally, the composite exhibited effective degradation of oxytetracycline (up to 93.4%) and retained 81.2% removal efficiency after four cycles, indicating good stability and reusability.

Mechanistic investigations indicated that •O2– and h+ were the dominant reactive species, with •O2– playing a major role. Based on band structure analysis and XPS results, an Z-scheme heterojunction with an internal electric field was established in the MIL/BOC system, which promoted efficient charge separation and preserved strong redox capability. This synergistic effect significantly enhanced the photocatalytic activity. Overall, this work provides a promising strategy for designing efficient visible-light-driven photocatalysts.
