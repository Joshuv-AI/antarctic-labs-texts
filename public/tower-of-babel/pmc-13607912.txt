Direct spinal cord injury during cervical epidural and nerve root blocks: A case series and pathophysiological reevaluation
Authors: Sung Jin Kim, Young Jin Kim, In Ho Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCervical epidural and nerve root blocks effectively manage radicular pain but pose risks of severe neurologic complications, including acute myelopathy. Although vascular embolization of particulate steroids is often implicated in these events, direct mechanical spinal cord injury remains an underrecognized etiology. Identifying the true pathophysiologic mechanism is essential to prevent permanent neurological deficits. We retrospectively reviewed four patients who developed acute spinal cord injuries immediately following cervical nerve blocks or targeted acupuncture. We analyzed clinical presentations, intraprocedural events, and longitudinal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings to elucidate the underlying mechanism of injury. All patients reported an instantaneous, excruciating “electric shock-like” sensation during needle insertion, followed immediately by severe motor weakness and paresthesia. MRI consistently revealed focal, central T2-weighted high signal intensities strictly localized within the spinal cord parenchyma. Unlike typical vascular infarctions following specific arterial territories, these findings strongly correlate with the physical trajectory of a direct needle puncture. The pathognomonic intraprocedural electric shock sensation and the central localization of MRI lesions strongly suggest direct mechanical needle injury as a primary cause of these catastrophic complications. Immediate cessation of needle advancement upon patient report of a shock-like sensation is critical to prevent irreversible spinal cord damage.

INTRODUCTION

Cervical transforaminal and interlaminar epidural blocks are widely used to manage radicular pain.[1] Despite their utility, severe complications, including acute myelopathy and quadriparesis, occasionally occur.[23] Conventionally, inadvertent intraarterial embolization of particulate steroids was considered the predominant pathophysiological cause. Consequently, clinical practice has shifted toward using nonparticulate steroids to mitigate ischemic risks.[45] However, this pharmacological focus may obscure another critical etiology: unrecognized direct spinal cord penetration by the procedural needle.[67] Radiologic and clinical evidence frequently deviates from typical vascular ischemic patterns, regardless of the injectate used. Here, we present four cases of severe spinal cord injury occurring during cervical nerve blocks. By analyzing their clinical presentations, MRI findings, and anatomical constraints, we reevaluate the etiology of postprocedural myelopathy and highlight the substantial risk of direct mechanical cord injury.

CASE REPORTS

Case 1

A 53-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with right upper extremity motor weakness (Medical Research Council [MRC] Grade 2) and radiating pain. Initial cervical MRI indicated C4–5 foraminal stenosis. She had previously undergone a selective nerve root block at a local clinic. During that procedure, she reported an excruciating, “electric shock-like” pain in all limbs, immediately followed by left upper extremity weakness (MRC Grade 2). Postprocedural MRI revealed a focal T2-weighted high signal intensity within the C4–5 spinal cord [Figure 1a and b]. 1 month later, follow-up MRI demonstrated more pronounced central signal changes [Figure 1c and d]. Her motor strength improved to MRC Grade 4 by 6 months.

(c and d) Follow-up T2-weighted sagittal and axial images 1 month later demonstrating more pronounced central signal changes

Case 2

A 42-year-old woman presented with quadriplegia and paresthesia following an epidural block for bilateral shoulder pain. During the procedure, she experienced an instantaneous “electric shock-like” sensation throughout her limbs, accompanied by sudden motor weakness (MRC Grade 4). Postprocedural MRI revealed T2-weighted signal changes extending across the C3–6 spinal cord levels [Figure 2a and b]. One month later, she achieved complete neurological recovery (MRC Grade 5), and corresponding imaging showed slightly reduced cord signal hyperintensity [Figure 2c and d].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of Case 2. (a and b) Initial postprocedural T2-weighted sagittal and axial images revealing signal changes in the spinal cord across the C3–6 levels. (c and d) 1-month follow-up MRI showing a slight reduction in the spinal cord signal change, consistent with the patient’s neurologic recovery

Case 3

A 60-year-old woman presented with right upper extremity weakness and paresthesia following a lancinating, “electric-like” pain during a cervical block. She exhibited impaired fine motor skills and weakness in the first through third digits (MRC Grade 4), while peripheral nerve studies suggested C8 or T1 radiculopathy. Initial MRI did not display definitive signal changes at C5–6 [Figure 3a and b]. However, a 1-year follow-up MRI revealed a delayed, focal increase in T2 signal intensity at C5–6 [Figure 3c and d], consistent with chronic myelopathic progression.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of Case 3. (a and b) Initial postprocedural T2-weighted sagittal and axial images lacking definitive signal changes at the C5–6 level. (c and d) 1-year follow-up MRI revealing a delayed, focal increase in signal intensity centrally within the cord at the C5–6 level

Case 4

A 51-year-old female violinist presented with right upper extremity motor weakness (MRC Grade 4) after experiencing an “electric shock-like” sensation during cervical acupuncture. Initial T2-weighted MRI showed a focal high signal at the C2 level, indicative of a penetrating injury tract [Figure 4a and b]. Over the subsequent year, she developed right thenar muscle atrophy and persistent lower extremity radiating pain. Although the 1-year follow-up MRI demonstrated decreased signal intensity, her clinical motor weakness persisted [Figure 4c and d].

(c and d) 1-year follow-up MRI showing a slight decrease in signal intensity, though clinical symptoms persisted

DISCUSSION

The pathophysiology of spinal cord injury during cervical injections remains highly debated. While intraarterial embolization is widely accepted, our analysis of these four cases suggests that direct mechanical needle injury is a highly probable alternative etiology, supported by three primary considerations.

First, anatomically, the microscopic dimensions of the target vessels make successful arterial cannulation extremely difficult. Terminal spinal branches entering the foramina typically measure 0.5–1.2 mm in diameter, and the artery of cervical enlargement averages 0.49 mm.[8] In contrast, the cervical spinal cord is a much larger target (10–13 mm in diameter). Consequently, a procedural needle is physically more likely to penetrate the cord parenchyma than to cannulate a mobile, microscopic blood vessel.

Second, intraprocedural clinical events provide a pathognomonic sign of mechanical trauma. All four patients reported an instantaneous, excruciating “electric shock-like” pain, which is markedly distinct from routine radicular irritation. We contend this sensation directly correlates with the physical disruption of spinal cord tracts by the needle tip.

Third, MRI lesion localization challenges the vascular infarction theory. Embolic infarctions typically follow specific vascular distributions, often presenting as diffuse or peripheral ischemic patterns. Conversely, our cases demonstrated focal T2 hyperintensities located centrally within the cord parenchyma [Figures 1-4].[9] This circumscribed localization is far more characteristic of mechanical needle penetration than a broad vascular stroke.

These findings emphasize that direct mechanical injury poses a substantial and preventable risk. If a patient experiences an electric shock sensation during needle advancement, the clinician must immediately halt the procedure and carefully withdraw the needle. Relying solely on the absence of vascular spread under fluoroscopy is insufficient, as needles can penetrate the spinal cord without yielding blood or cerebrospinal fluid aspiration.

CONCLUSION

This case series highlights that direct mechanical spinal cord injury may be a more frequent etiology of postprocedural myelopathy than previously recognized. The anatomical improbability of cannulating microscopic arteries, the presence of pathognomonic intraprocedural electric shocks, and the central localization of MRI findings collectively support a mechanical mechanism over an exclusively embolic one. Properly responding to intraprocedural warning signs remains critical to optimizing patient safety.
