Body Posture‐Related Differences in Pain Perception and Their Mediation by Cardiovascular Factors
Authors: Gustavo A. Reyes del Paso, Dolores Santiago, Belén Moreno, Casandra I. Montoro, Stefan Duschek, Dmitry M. Davydov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPain modulation arising from the cardiovascular system has repeatedly been described. It is mediated by the baroreceptor system, where the activity of baroreceptor afferents inhibits nociception. Body posture changes cause blood volume redistribution that might modulate baroreceptor activity and antinociceptive effects. This study investigated posture‐related differences in pain perception and their causation by cardiovascular factors. Pressure pain threshold and tolerance and pain intensity ratings were obtained in 83 healthy individuals (61 women and 22 men) while they were sitting, lying down and standing. Electrocardiography, impedance cardiography and continuous blood pressure were recorded in each posture. Pain threshold was higher while lying down than standing, and pain tolerance was higher while lying down than standing and sitting. Pain intensity ratings for suprathreshold stimulation (4 kg/cm2) were higher while standing than sitting and lying down. Blood pressure, stroke volume, cardiac output, total peripheral resistance and baroreflex sensitivity decreased while standing and increased while lying down. Blood pressure was positively associated with pain threshold in men, but not in women. Total peripheral resistance was positively associated with pain threshold and tolerance and blood pressure was negatively related to pain intensity ratings in the total sample. Lying down is associated with increased venous return, central blood volume and higher baroreceptor load, which may result in stronger antinociceptive effects. The opposite changes along with baroreceptor unloading occur while standing. Assuming a supine position is a functional human response to alleviate acute pain. However, in terms of negative reinforcement, pain reduction while lying down may contribute to passive coping behaviors in chronic pain.

Introduction

The cardiovascular system modulates pain processing and constitutes a powerful source of antinociceptive influences (Bruehl and Chung 2004). This is reflected by the inverse association between blood pressure (BP) and pain perception (Bruehl and Chung 2004; Ditto et al. 2007). In comparison with individuals with BP from the normotensive range, patients with arterial hypertension exhibit lower pain sensitivity (i.e., hypertension‐related hypoalgesia) (France 1999; France and Katz 1999; Ghione 1996), whereas chronically low BP is associated with increased pain sensitivity (Duschek, Dietel, et al. 2009; Duschek et al. 2008). Experimentally induced BP rise leads to lower experimental pain responses (Duschek, Heiss, et al. 2009). Moreover, some epidemiological studies suggested that higher BP levels protect against headaches (Hagen et al. 2002) and chronic musculoskeletal complaints (Hagen et al. 2005). However, a recent large study did not find a direct association between BP and the development and spreading of chronic pain across body sites (Tanguay‐Sabourin et al. 2023).

The inhibitory influence of baroreceptor stimulation on the central nervous system (CNS) is regarded as the main mechanism in the effects of BP on pain (Bruehl and Chung 2004; Duschek, Werner, and Reyes del Paso 2013; Rau and Elbert 2001; Reyes del Paso et al. 2015). Mechanical, electrical or pharmacological stimulation of arterial baroreceptors, and experimental activation of low‐pressure volume cardiopulmonary baroreceptors, precipitate anti‐nociceptive effects (D'Antono et al. 2000; Dworkin et al. 1994; Rau et al. 1994; Reyes del Paso et al. 2014; Venezia et al. 2024). Moreover, the pain‐dampening effect of baroreceptor activity is reflected by the inverse association between the sensitivity of the cardiac baroreflex (baroreflex sensitivity, BRS) and clinical and experimentally induced pain (Duschek et al. 2007; Reyes del Paso et al. 2010, 2011, 2021).

Changes in body posture (i.e., gravitational stress) alter the distribution of blood volume in the body, affecting BP regulation and baroreceptor activity. When standing up, blood accumulates in the lower extremities, reducing central blood volume, venous return to the heart and BP. The reduction of venous return decreases myocardial preload and, due to the Frank‐Starling mechanism (Hall and Hall 2020), reduces stroke volume (SV) and cardiac output (CO). In contrast, lying down increases central blood volume, associated with higher SV and CO (Contreras‐Merino et al. 2022; Hall and Hall 2020).

Posture‐related cardiovascular and hemodynamic changes modulate the activity of the arterial and low‐pressure volume baroreceptors, which in turn might affect pain perception. While standing upright, reduced central blood volume, BP, SV and CO cause lower baroceptor load and activity in cardiovascular afferents, which may diminish antinociceptive influences. In contrast, while lying down, increased central blood volume, BP, SV and CO may be accompanied by greater load and activity of baroreceptors, causing stronger pain inhibition. These hypotheses are in accordance with observations that mild hypohydration, which is associated with lower blood volume and baroreceptor load, increases pain sensitivity (Bear et al. 2016; Perry et al. 2016).

This study analyzed the effects of body posture, as a natural modulator of central blood volume, on pain perception. It was hypothesized that postures that increase baroreceptor load (such as lying down) would be associated with lower pain sensitivity, and that postures that decrease baroreceptor load (such as standing) would be associated with higher pain sensitivity. Experimental pressure pain was evoked in healthy individuals while sitting, lying down, and standing; pain measures included pain threshold, pain tolerance, and pain intensity ratings for stimulation with different pressure intensities.

The hypothesized larger baroreflex antinociceptive effect during lying down may be a mechanism explaining the typical sedentarism and low physical activity in chronic pain patients, in which bed rest is considered counterproductive (Munneke et al. 2025), as this inhibitory effect can negatively reinforce inactivity by reducing pain. To investigate the role of cardiovascular variables in posture‐related alterations of pain perception, BP, BP pulse amplitude, interbeat interval (IBI), SV, CO, total peripheral resistance (TPR), and BRS were recorded during each posture. Considering the pain‐dampening effects arising from the cardiovascular system, inverse associations between these variables and pain sensitivity were expected.

Evoked pain studies suggest greater sensitivity to pressure pain in women, with men typically exhibiting higher pain thresholds and tolerance than women (e.g., Vesal et al. 2024). However, evidence is less clear concerning pain intensity ratings, where some studies have revealed sex differences (e.g., Kröner‐Herwig et al. 2012) whereas others have not (Petrini et al. 2015). Regarding cardiovascular measures, young men usually display higher BP and larger BP reactivity to stress than women (e.g., Drury et al. 2023), while women display lower IBI and larger IBI reactivity (e.g., Whited and Larkin 2009). Given these sex differences, sex was also included in the analysis.

Method

Participants

In total, 83 university students (61 women, 22 men) with a mean age of 20.32 ± 4.57 years and a mean body mass index (BMI) of 23.90 ± 4.29 kg/m2 participated in the study. None of the participants suffered from current physical (including hypertension) or mental diseases, or were taking medications affecting the CNS, cardiovascular system, or pain perception. Women using oral contraceptives were excluded from participation to ensure sample homogeneity and to control for the well‐established confounding effects of exogenous gonadal hormones on both cardiovascular regulation and pain processing. Previous research has shown that hormonal contraceptives alter BP, plasma volume, and baroreflex sensitivity (e.g., Jacob et al. 2022), which could impact the hemodynamic responses to orthostatic stress. Furthermore, exogenous hormones are known to modulate experimental pain sensitivity and endogenous pain inhibition (e.g., Bohm‐Starke et al. 2004). Given that our study specifically investigates the interaction between hemodynamic adjustments and pain perception during an orthostatic challenge, including hormonal contraceptive users would introduce significant variance in both systems, thereby masking the targeted physiological mechanisms.

The sample size was determined based on previous studies investigating the connection between BP and pain (Ditto et al. 2007; France 1999; Duschek, Dietel, et al. 2009; Duschek et al. 2008), which showed associations in the moderate range. Taking an alpha level of 5%, a test power of 90% and a sample size of 80 as a basis, Pearson correlations ≥ 0.32 would reach significance (Faul et al. 2009). In the ANOVAs computed for pain threshold and tolerance (alpha = 5%, power = 90%, N = 80, correlations between repeated measures = 0.50; c.f. Section 2.5), effect sizes ≥ 0.16 (Cohen's f) would be significant.

Pain Assessment

Pain was evoked using a wireless Tracker Freedom algometer (JTECH Medical, Midvale, UT, USA) with a stimulation area of 1 cm2. The algometer is inserted into a screw piston designed to stimulate fingernails. Pressure on the algometer is guided by visual feedback from a computer. To assess pain intensity, a 10‐cm visual analog scale (VAS) was used (“How intense was the pain?”). The extremes of the VAS were marked as “painless” (value = 0) and “extremely painful” (value = 10). Before testing, participants were familiarized with the concepts of pain threshold (“the point at which you begin to feel pain”) and tolerance (“the maximal pressure you can tolerate”) and pain assessment using the VAS. During this practice phase performed in a sitting posture, participants were presented with seven stimuli (5 s each) on the second fingernail of the right hand with intensities in the following random order: 1.5, 3.5, 1, 2.5, 2, and 4 kg/cm2. Following this, and a 5‐min rest period, pain threshold and tolerance measurements were obtained at the third fingernail of the left hand with a pressure rise rate of 1 kg/s. After another 5‐min rest period, six stimuli (in ascending pressure order 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, and 4 kg/cm2) were delivered to the third fingernail of the left hand (stimulus duration 5 s, interstimulus interval 10 s). Participants were asked to rate their pain intensity on the VAS after each stimulus.

Cardiovascular Recording and Analysis

A Task Force Monitor (CNSystems, Graz, Austria) was used for cardiovascular recordings. Four electrodes were applied to the chest, two close to the shoulders, and two at the lower rib cage to record two bipolar ECGs (Einthoven I and II). Four further electrodes, two at the lateral chest (xiphoidal level), one at the lower nape, and a ground electrode at the right ankle, were used to record impedance cardiography (ICG). Continuous BP was taken at the first phalange of the second and third fingers of the right hand, and oscillometric BP was taken from the left brachial artery (with the right hand positioned at the level of the heart). The device recalibrated continuous finger BP according to brachial artery BP every 60 s without interrupting the recording. Recordings of ECG and ICG were performed at 1000 Hz and that of continuous BP at 200 Hz.

IBI (ms) was derived from the interval between R waves from the ECG. Systolic BP (SBP), diastolic BP (DBP) and mean arterial pressure (MAP) (mmHg) during the cardiac cycle were obtained from the continuous finger BP recording. SV (mL) was obtained from ICG by application of the Kubicek equation (Berntson et al. 2016). CO (l/min) was calculated as SV × heart rate. TPR (dyne × s/cm5) was computed using the formula: (mean arterial pressure − central venous pressure)/CO × 80 (Berntson et al. 2016). The ICG data were processed by means of the algorithms of Wang et al. (Wang et al. 1995), which are based on the time–frequency distribution of the nonstationary ICG signal and use a spectrogram method to detect the B, C, and X points in the dZ/dt signal waveform. Beat‐to‐beat data for all variables were screened for artifacts (deviations of > 2.5 SD from the moving average of the previous 10 beats). When detected, they were corrected by linear interpolation.

BRS was quantified using the spontaneous sequence method (Reyes del Paso et al. 2017). Sequences of 3–6 consecutive cardiac cycles were selected in which SBP increases were accompanied by IBI increases or in which SBP decreases were accompanied by IBI decreases. When such a baroreflex sequence was detected, the regression line was computed between SBP and IBI values within the sequence. BRS is expressed as the change in IBI (ms) per mmHg change in SBP (slope of the regression line). BP pulse amplitude (SBP—DBP) was taken as an index of the pulsatile stimulation input to the arterial baroreceptors (Reyes del Paso et al. 2025). Due to recording problems in some postures, resulting in periods of signal loss, complete IBI data were unavailable for two participants, SBP for two participants, DBP for three participants and ICG‐derived variables for five participants. Therefore, the final sample sizes for cardiovascular variables were 81 for IBI (21 men and 60 women), 81 for SBP (22 men and 59 women), 80 for DBP (21 men and 59 women), 80 for MAP (21 men and 59 women), 78 for SV and CO (19 men and 59 women), 77 for TPR (19 men and 58 women), and 80 for BRS (21 men and 59 women).

Experimental Protocol

The experimental procedure involved a modified version of the Chronic Pain Autonomic Stress Test (Davydov and Perlo 2015). The protocol included a 4‐min baseline in a sitting position with eyes open, 4 min of standing upright (first orthostatic phase) with the eyes open, 4 min of lying down with the eyes closed, and 4 min of standing upright again with the eyes open (second orthostatic phase). In this autonomic test, each posture serves as the physiological context for the subsequent posture, hereby influencing the ensuing hemodynamic responses (Davydov and Perlo 2015). For this reason, the postures are performed in a fixed order rather than being counterbalanced. To allow stabilization of the cardiovascular measures, the cardiovascular recordings were interrupted during the posture changes and started again 1 min after the new posture was assumed (Davydov and Perlo 2015). Participants were instructed not to speak or make sudden movements to avoid interference with the recordings. Experimental pain testing was conducted during sitting, lying down, and the second standing period (second orthostatic phase) after the cardiovascular recordings had finished. Pain threshold and tolerance were assessed twice while sitting and once while lying down and standing upright. The assessment during the sitting posture was repeated to increase reliability, as pain threshold and tolerance measurements tend to show high variability when taken for the first time. Pain ratings (VAS) for the six pressure stimuli were taken once during each of the postures. Figure 1 illustrates the experimental protocol of the study.

Procedure

The study was carried out from February to May 2025. Health status and demographic information of the participants were assessed via phone calls prior to the study and a semi‐structured interview at the beginning of the study session. During the phone call, participants were asked to abstain from alcohol and vigorous exercise for 3 h before the experimental session and from analgesics for 24 h. The study was approved by the ethical committee of the University of Jaén, and all participants provided their written informed consent. The protocol of the study was registered prior to conducting the research and it is publicly accessible at DOI: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/TXYSJ.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed by mixed ANOVAs using the Wilks λ multivariate test statistic that does not require compliance with the sphericity assumption. Mean values of the cardiovascular variables were computed for each of the 4‐min body posture. For pain threshold and tolerance, Posture (sitting, lying down, and standing upright) was applied as a within‐subjects factor. The average of the two‐pain threshold and tolerance values obtained while sitting was used in the analysis. For pain intensity ratings (VAS), Posture (sitting, lying down, and standing upright) and Pressure intensity (1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, and 4 kg) were used as within‐subjects factors. Cardiovascular variables were analyzed by repeated‐measures ANOVAs (Wilks λ multivariate test) with the within‐subjects factor Posture (sitting, standing 1, lying down, and standing 2). Sex was included in all these ANOVAs as a between‐subjects factor. t‐tests for dependent samples were used for post hoc comparisons. To indicate effect sizes in these analyses, partial eta squared (ηp2) was used. Values of ηp2 < 0.01 were interpreted as trivial, values of 0.01–< 0.06 as small, values of 0.06–< 0.14 as moderate, and values of ≥ 0.14 as large (Lakens 2013). Associations between cardiovascular and pain variables were quantified by multiple regression analysis using as predictor each cardiovascular variable, Sex and the interaction term between the cardiovascular variable and Sex. Variables were mean‐centered for computing the interaction term. In the case of a significant Sex interaction, Pearson correlations were reported separately for men and women. Standardized beta coefficients are presented. Cardiovascular variables display higher stability during the sitting posture than in the other postures. During lying down and standing cardiovascular activity is subject to dynamic changes to adapt hemodynamics to gravitational stress (Contreras‐Merino et al. 2022; Davydov, Gálvez‐Sánchez, and Reyes del Paso 2024). As cardiovascular variables were averaged across the 4‐min periods (which may differ from the specific cardiovascular state during pain evaluation), regression and correlational analyses were performed specifically for the sitting posture. Given that the three BP variables yielded similar results, MAP (as a more global indicator of BP) was reported in the regression analysis to simplify the presentation of results. Alpha was set at 0.05. Because all analyses were conducted to test a priori specified hypotheses (not alternative ones), no adjustments for repeated statistical testing were made, as recommended (Rothman 1990).

Pain Measures

Figure 2 displays the pain threshold values during the three body postures. The ANOVA for pain threshold revealed a main effect of Posture (F(2, 80) = 3.61, p = 0.032, ηp2 = 0.193). Pain threshold was higher while lying down than standing (t(82) = −2.35, p = 0.021 ,ηp2 = 0.063), with no other significant differences between postures (t(82) = −1.07, p = 0.286 ,ηp2 = 0.014 for the comparison between lying down and siting and t(82) = 0.33, p = 0.745 for the comparison between siting and standing, ηp2 = 0.001). The effect of Posture was also significant for pain tolerance (F(2, 80) = 10.96, p < 0.0001, ηp2 = 0.215), which was higher while lying down than sitting (t(82) = −3.20, p = 0.002 ,ηp2 = 0.111) and standing (t(82) = 4.62, p < 0.0001, ηp2 = 0.207) (Figure 3). The difference between sitting and standing upright was not significant (t(82) = 1.00, p = 0.319, ηp2 = 0.012). Finally, BRS decreased from sitting to standing 1 (t(80) = 7.80, p < 0.0001, ηp2 = 0.432), increased from standing 1 to lying down (t(81) = −10.95, p < 0.0001, ηp2 = 0.597) and decreased again from lying down to standing 2 (t(81) = 11.31, p < 0.0001, ηp2 = 0.612). BRS was also greater while lying than sitting (t(80) = −7.11, p < 0.0001, ηp2 = 0.387).

Associations Between Cardiovascular and Pain Variables

For pain threshold results showed significant effects of MAP (β = 0.349, t = 3.12, p = 0.003) and the interaction term MAP × Sex (β = −0.227, t = −2.11, p = 0.038). The effect of sex was not significant (β = −0.126, t = −1.15, p = 0.255). BP variables correlated positively with pain threshold in men (r = 0.431, p = 0.045; r = 0.447, p = 0.037 and r = 0.466, p = 0.029; for SBP, DBP and MAP, respectively) but not in women (r = 0.156, p = 0.235; r = 0.078, p = 0.552 and r = 0.133, p = 0.310; for SBP, DBP and MAP, respectively). For pain tolerance only the effect of Sex was significant (β = −0.243, t = −2.17, p = 0.033); the effect of MAP (β = 0.206, t = 1.82, p = 0.073) and the interaction term MAP × Sex (β = −0.196, t = −1.79, p = 0.077) did not reach significance.

TPR positively predicted, regardless of interaction with sex, both pain threshold (β = 0.272, t = 2.44, p = 0.017; β = −0.242, t = −2.29, p = 0.025 for the effect of Sex and β = 0.034, t = 0.31, p = 0.760 for the interaction term TPR × Sex) and tolerance (β = 0.249, t = 2.32, p = 0.023; β = −0.306, t = −3.00, p = 0.004 for the effect of Sex and β = −0.095, t = −0.89, p = 0.378 for the interaction TPR × Sex).

Regarding pain ratings during the ascending pressure stimulation (1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, and 4 kg/cm2), regression analysis showed a significant inverse effect for MAP during the pressure conditions of 2.5 kg (β = −0.277, t = −2.34, p = 0.022; β = −0.145, t = −1.24, p = 0.218 for the effect of Sex and β = −0.072, t = −0.64, p = 0.526 for the interaction term MAP × Sex), 3.5 k (β = −0.285, t = −2.39, p = 0.019; β = −0.197, t = −1.68, p = 0.097 for the effect of Sex and β = 0.045, t = 0.39, p = 0.696 for the interaction term MAP × Sex) and 4 k (β = −0.241, t = −2.00, p = 0.049; β = −0.056, t = −0.47, p = 0.639 for the effect of Sex and β = −0.029, t = −0.25, p = 0.806 for the interaction term MAP × Sex) (results of the 3 kg condition: β = −0.227, t = −1.90, p = 0.062 for the effect of MAP; β = −0.187, t = −1.58, p = 0.117 for the effect of Sex and β = −0.033, t = −0.29, p = 0.773 for the interaction term MAP × Sex).

We also analyzed associations for pain intensity ratings during the final test of the pain familiarization phase to pressure stimulation in random order, with pain intensity ratings (VAS) of 0.32 ± 0.61 for 1 kg, 0.53 ± 1.45 for 1.5 kg, 0.82 ± 1.22 for 2 kg, 1.07 ± 1.55 for 2.5 kg, 1.38 ± 1.50 for 3 kg, 1.52 ± 1.83 for 3.5 kg and 2.44 ± 2.24 for 4 kg. Regression analysis showed significant effects of MAP for all pressure conditions: 1 k (β = −0.241, t = −2.00, p = 0.049; β = −0.056, t = −0.47, p = 0.639 for Sex and β = −0.029, t = −0.25, p = 0.806 for the interaction term MAP × Sex), 1.5 k (β = −0.252, t = −2.12, p = 0.037; β = −0.258, t = −2.20, p = 0.031 for Sex and β = 0.06, t = 0.50, p = 0.616 for the interaction term MAP × Sex), 2 k (β = −0.289, t = −2.42, p = 0.018; β = −0.05, t = −0.42, p = 0.675 for Sex and β = 0.03, t = 0.24, p = 0.808 for the interaction term MAP × Sex), 2.5 k (β = −0.266, t = −2.22, p = 0.029; β = −0.01, t = −0.12, p = 0.903 for Sex and β = −0.01, t = −0.07, p = 0.943 for the interaction term MAP × Sex), 3 k (β = −0.299, t = −2.51, p = 0.014; β = −0.154, t = −1.31, p = 0.194 for Sex and β = 0.12, t = 1.08, p = 0.282 for the interaction term MAP × Sex), 3.5 kg (β = −0.274, t = −2.28, p = 0.025; β = −0.08, t = −0.65, p = 0.517 for Sex and β = 0.07, t = 0.61, p = 0.547 for the interaction term MAP × Sex), and 4 kg (β = −0.283, t = −2.36, p = 0.021; β = −0.09, t = −0.78, p = 0.436 for Sex and β = 0.15, t = 1.28, p = 0.204 for the interaction term MAP × Sex). The effect of TPR on pain ratings was not significant.

No significant results of regression analysis arose for BP peak amplitude, SV, CO, IBI, and BRS, in any of the pain measures.

Discussion

This study investigated the effects of body posture on pain perception based on experimental pressure pain testing. Findings for pain threshold and tolerance supported the hypothesis of greater pain sensitivity during an upright than a supine position. Differences in cardiovascular variables between postures and associations of BP and TPR with pain variables confirmed the involvement of cardiovascular activity in posture‐related differences in pain perception.

Pressure pain threshold was higher while participants were lying down than in an upright position, and pain tolerance was higher while lying down than while standing and sitting. It is commonly assumed that pain threshold predominantly reflects the sensory dimension of pain, whereas pain tolerance is also affected by emotional‐motivational and cognitive factors like fear of pain, pain catastrophizing, perceived control over pain, or self‐efficacy (Baker and Kirsch 1991; Feldner and Hekmat 2001; Litt 1988). Accordingly, posture effects are not restricted to the sensory pain component but also involve the affective, cognitive, and motivational pain dimensions.

Regarding pain intensity ratings, for stimulation with greater pressures (3 to 4 kg), VAS ratings were higher during the standing than the sitting posture; moreover, for 4 kg pressure, pain reported while standing also exceeded that while lying down. A different picture arose for the smaller pressures. For 2 kg pressure, pain ratings were higher while standing than sitting; however, contrary to the study hypothesis, for 1.5 to 2.5 kg pressures, lying down was associated with greater pain reports than sitting. Regarding this discrepancy, it must be noted that the average pain thresholds of the sample ranged between 3.4 and 3.6 kg for the different postures. As such, the lower pressures may not have been perceived as painful by most participants and, by definition, the VAS was not suitable to evaluate non‐painful tactile perceptions.

Sex differences were observed in the behavioral pain measures, but not in pain intensity ratings. Women exhibited lower pain thresholds and tolerance than men, which is consistent with previous reports (Vesal et al. 2024). Factors proposed to explain these differences include gonadal hormones (with testosterone exerting antinociceptive effects), variations in endogenous pain inhibitory systems (men generally exhibit more efficient conditioned pain modulation and greater opioid receptor density and availability), neuroimmune factors, differences in tissue composition (e.g., adipose tissue distribution, muscle mass, and skin thickness) and a lower density of peripheral nociceptive nerve fibers in men. Additionally, women have been reported to exhibit greater pain catastrophizing, higher anticipatory anxiety, and increased hypervigilance to pain, among other factors (Fillingim et al. 2009).

Our study did not find sex differences in pain ratings, which also are in accordance with evidence of other studies showing sex differences in pain thresholds and tolerance despite their absence in pain ratings (Petrini et al. 2015). Dissociations between behavioral pain measures and intensity ratings have repeatedly been described in the literature (Lorenz et al. 2003; Uddin et al. 2014; Nielsen et al. 2009). This supports the hypothesis that different pain measures tap into specific physiological and psychological mechanisms of pain processing (Montoro et al. 2025; Nielsen et al. 2009).

As expected, BP variables decreased from the sitting to the standing posture (first orthostatic phase) and increased while lying down. However, BP did not decrease again during the subsequent standing phase (second orthostatic phase). This discrepancy can be explained by the magnitude of the gravitational challenge: transitioning from a supine to a standing position represents a much stronger orthostatic stress than standing up from a sitting position. The larger and faster reduction in venous return during the supine‐to‐stand transition likely provokes a stronger compensatory sympathetic and baroreflex response, which effectively mobilizes unstressed blood volume reserves and prevents the BP drop seen in the milder sitting‐to‐stand transition. Because the second standing posture lacked this transient hypotensive shift, the baroreceptor unloading might have been blunted. Consequently, one might speculate that if pain had been assessed during the first standing period—when relevant BP drop occurred—the posture‐induced changes in pain perception would have been more pronounced.

BP pulse amplitude and IBI were greater while lying down than during the other postures. Differences in venous return due to posture changes strongly affect SV (Hall and Hall 2020), which decreased while standing upright and increased while lying down. The changes in SV and IBI led to CO increase while lying down and CO decrease while standing upright; however, these CO changes were only significant in women, but not men. TPR was lower during standing than sitting. Moreover, BRS was higher while lying down than sitting and standing upright. Cardiac BRS is a widely recognized index of parasympathetic influences on heart rate (Reyes del Paso et al. 2017, 2025); therefore, the results reflect higher vagal cardiac tone while lying down than the other postures.

Regarding sex differences in cardiovascular variables, men exhibited overall greater SBP, DBP, MAP, pulse pressure, and TPR than women. Consistent with our findings, the literature has extensively documented that men exhibit higher BP and TPR than premenopausal women. Several underlying mechanisms have been proposed to explain these sex differences. First, sex hormones play a pivotal role; estrogens exert vasoprotective effects by enhancing nitric oxide bioavailability and downregulating the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system, whereas testosterone has been associated with increased oxidative stress and elevated endothelin‐1 production. Second, differences in autonomic regulation contribute to these variations. In young men, there is a positive correlation between muscle sympathetic nerve activity and TPR. In contrast, in women, β‐adrenergic vasodilatory pathways partially offset α‐adrenergic vasoconstriction. Furthermore, hemodynamic modeling suggests that BP in men is predominantly driven by vascular resistance, whereas in women it is more closely linked to stroke volume dynamics (Hart et al. 2009; Reckelhoff 2001). However, we did not find sex differences in IBI or IBI reactivity, contrary to what was observed in previous studies (e.g., Whited and Larkin 2009). The higher BP observed in men compared to women is consistent with the higher pain threshold and tolerance we found in males.

It may be hypothesized that the effects of posture on pain relate to modulations of cardiovascular activity triggered by gravitational stress and the impact of these modulations on CNS nociceptive processing. As compared to an upright position, a supine position is associated with greater central blood volume, venous return to the heart, SV, CO, and BP (Contreras‐Merino et al. 2022; Davydov, de la Coba, et al. 2024). Previous research suggested that modulations of central blood volume via changes of fluid intake affect pain perception, where hypohydration is accompanied by increased, and hyperhydration by decreased, pain sensitivity (Arca and Singh 2021; Bear et al. 2016; Perry et al. 2016; Tan et al. 2022). The arterial baroreceptors may play a crucial role in transmitting the effects of posture. Reductions of BP, SV, and CO while standing reduce baroreceptor load and lower discharge of baroreceptor afferents. In turn, this is associated with lower activity in pain inhibitory pathways and stronger pain experience. In contrast, the rise of BP, SV, and CO during lying down, and the associated greater central blood volume, increase baroreceptor load and stimulate the afferent pathway‐induced antinociceptive effects (Bruehl and Chung 2004; Duschek, Werner, and Reyes del Paso 2013; Rau and Elbert 2001; Reyes del Paso et al. 2014).

In the present study, this mechanism is illustrated by the inverse relationship between BP, TPR, and pain sensitivity. The relation between BP and pain threshold was modulated by sex, as SBP, DBP, and MAP were associated with higher pain thresholds in men, but not in women. In contrast, TPR was positively related to both pain threshold and tolerance regardless of sex. For pain intensity ratings, the associations were also independent of sex. During ascending pressure stimulation, inverse associations between MAP and pain were observed at the 2.5, 3.5, and 4 kg intensities. However, when stimuli were presented in random order, associations emerged across all seven intensities. Thus, the associations between BP and pain intensity ratings may have been more broadly captured under random presentation than during predictable ascending intensity. One might speculate that predictions regarding the increasing pressure generated expectations that influenced pain ratings, potentially masking the underlying influence of BP.

These results are consistent with previous observations of hypoalgesia in chronic hypertension (France 1999; Ghione 1996; Hagen et al. 2005), hyperalgesia in chronically low BP (Duschek, Dietel, et al. 2009; Duschek et al. 2008) and negative correlations between BP and pain sensitivity in the normotensive range (Bruehl and Chung 2004; Ditto et al. 2007; Montoro et al. 2025). Antinociceptive effects of baroreceptor activity are also reflected by pain dampening due to experimental stimulation of the arterial baroreceptors (Dworkin et al. 1994; Rau et al. 1994; Reyes del Paso et al. 2014; Venezia et al. 2024) and increased BP following alpha‐adrenergic medication and respiratory maneuvers (Duschek, Heiss, et al. 2009; Reyes del Paso et al. 2015).

It is plausible that, in addition to the arterial baroreceptor system, the low‐pressure baroreceptors located in the lungs, atria and large vessels contribute to the effects of posture on pain (Hall and Hall 2020; Suarez‐Roca et al. 2022). These receptors detect changes in blood volume rather than BP and are activated by factors increasing venous return, for example when lying down or elevating the legs (Suarez‐Roca et al. 2022). However, evidence concerning experimental manipulation of low‐pressure baroreceptors on pain remains controversial, where positive and negative results have been reported (D'Antono et al. 2000; McIntyre et al. 2008).

In contrast to the BP variables, IBI, SV, and CO were unrelated to the pain measures. Pulse pressure reflects the magnitude of the pulsatile input to arterial baroreceptors (Reyes del Paso et al. 2025); however, this measure did not correlate with the pain variables. Conversely, TPR was associated with pain threshold and tolerance. This suggests that vasomotor tone—likely due to its substantial effect on BP (Hall and Hall 2020; Reyes del Paso et al. 2025)—also contributes to the cardiovascular modulation of pain. Furthermore, these findings indicate that vascular factors may play a more prominent role than cardiac factors in mediating these antinociceptive effects.

BRS did not correlate with the pain measures, which is inconsistent with previous reports of inverse associations of BRS with experimental and clinical pain (Duschek et al. 2007; Reyes del Paso et al. 2010, 2011). These associations were interpreted as indicative of a functional equivalence of the cardiovascular and CNS branches of the baroreceptor system, in the sense that greater sensitivity of the baroreflex is associated with stronger CNS inhibition (Duschek et al. 2007; Duschek, Wörsching, and Reyes del Paso 2013). However, the BRS–pain association was only demonstrated in individuals with BP in the high normotensive range and did not arise in those with low BP (Duschek et al. 2007). Therefore, the lack of correlations in this study may be ascribed to the relatively low BP levels of the participants (average SBP 105–111 mmHg and average DBP 64–71 for the different postures).

The present study has several limitations. First, since our original aim was not focused on sex differences, the sample was not balanced by sex, with a lower number of men in comparison to women. This may have affected the statistical power of the study, especially regarding the male subsample. Second, the young age and good health of the participants, including their low BP, could have limited the results and the associations between cardiovascular and pain variables. Studies that have identified the BP‐hypoalgesia phenomenon have usually observed this association in samples with relatively high BP or BP in the upper normotensive range (France 1999; France and Katz 1999; Ghione 1996; Hagen et al. 2002, 2005). Thus, the relatively low BP of our sample may have limited the magnitude of the observed antinociceptive BP effect. Furthermore, the good health of our sample limits the generalization of the results to chronic pain conditions. Third, as a methodological limitation of the study, it must be acknowledged that the order of the postures was not counterbalanced in the testing procedure (Davydov and Perlo 2015). Therefore, possible pain sensitization due to repeated pressure stimulation may have influenced the measurements during the different postures. However, previous studies did not report pain sensitization on repeated pressure stimulation in healthy individuals (de la Coba et al. 2017, 2021), and the relatively long intervals between measurements (5 min) may have prevented such effects. Another restriction pertains to the low intensity of the pressure stimuli used for subjective pain evaluation, as four of the six stimuli were below the average pain threshold in the sample. Finally, the relatively small magnitude of the observed antinociceptive effects raises questions regarding their clinical relevance. Future studies employing active postural changes in older populations and patients with chronic pain conditions are needed to further elucidate this issue.

To conclude, this study revealed evidence of posture‐related differences in pain perception, where a supine position is associated with lower pain sensitivity than an upright position. Differences in baroreceptor activity due to changes in the blood volume distribution in the body may be crucial to mediating these effects. Further research should explore the clinical relevance of the findings. While lying down constitutes an intuitive and functional human response to alleviate acute pain, it may be less adequate in chronic pain conditions. Maintenance of physical activity is generally recommended in the rehabilitation of chronic pain, and bed rest is regarded as counterproductive (Colleary et al. 2017; Munneke et al. 2025). It may be considered that posture effects on pain are implicated in maladaptive learning mechanisms fostering passive coping with chronic pain (Flor and Turk 2006). In terms of negative reinforcement, pain reduction during a passive supine position may support avoidance behaviors and the appraisal of physical activity as harmful. In turn, this may contribute to the vicious cycle between passive coping behaviors and long‐term pain amplification.
