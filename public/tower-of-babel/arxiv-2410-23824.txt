Abstract
Federated learning enables edge devices to collaboratively train a global model while maintaining data privacy by keeping data localized. However, the Non-IID nature of data distribution across devices often hinders model convergence and reduces performance. In this paper, we propose a novel plugin for federated optimization techniques that approximates Non-IID data distributions to IID through generative AI-enhanced data augmentation and balanced sampling strategy. Key idea is to synthesize additional data for underrepresented classes on each edge device, leveraging generative AI to create a more balanced dataset across the FL network. Additionally, a balanced sampling approach at the central server selectively includes only the most IID-like devices, accelerating convergence while maximizing the global model’s performance. Experimental results validate that our approach significantly improves convergence speed and robustness against data imbalance, establishing a flexible, privacy-preserving FL plugin that is applicable even in data-scarce environments.

Introduction

Deep learning

However, achieving such performance often requires extensive data, which is impractical in many real-world scenarios due to privacy concerns and the prohibitive costs associated with data sharing

FL, therefore, provides a crucial solution for privacy-preserving learning, especially in sensitive sectors such as healthcare.

While FL achieves comparable performance to centralized learning by aggregating models from local devices, it often requires numerous global epochs between the central server and the edge devices to achieve satisfactory accuracy

This challenge intensifies when data at each edge device is non-independent and identically distributed (Non-IID)

]
.
Such statistical and computational heterogeneity among edge devices often results in local models drifting towards distinct minima, thus degrading the performance of the aggregated model
[

and increasing the communication overhead required for convergence
[

]
,
[

.
Addressing these issues is essential for deploying FL effectively in real-world applications, particularly in medical tasks where data distributions are often highly imbalanced
[

]
.

Several methods have been proposed to address this client-drift phenomenon by modifying the local training process.
For instance, proximal regularization
[
12
]
and restricted-softmax function
[
13
]
help alleviate client-drift but increase edge-side complexity.
In this paper, we propose a novel plugin to mitigate client drift in heterogeneous FL environments.
Our approach involves paraphrasing data using generative AI and selecting an optimal set of edge devices to participate in the updates, thereby approximating the Non-IID data distributions to IID as shown in Fig.
1
.
Our proposed plugin provides a flexible solution that can be adapted to a wide range of FL frameworks and practical use cases, especially relevant in medical text classification.

Key idea is to utilize generative AI to synthesize data, balancing the distribution of data across edge devices to approximate IID conditions.
By addressing data imbalance and utilizing balanced sampling at the central server, we reduce the need for prolonged communication rounds and improve convergence speed.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first approach to apply generative AI for balancing Non-IID distributions within FL, specifically targeting heterogeneous environments with computational and data distribution challenges.
Our method thus represents a significant advance in overcoming core limitations in FL.

•

We propose a generative AI-based method for alleviating Non-IID data distributions, referred to as

•

We use generative AI at the edge device level to synthesize data, filling gaps in underrepresented classes to achieve a more balanced distribution without additional real data.

•

We design a balanced sampling approach at the central server to replace random sampling, selecting the most statistically balanced edge device set for aggregation to reduce communication rounds and accelerate model convergence.

•

We demonstrate the effectiveness of this approach by applying it to representative FL methods showing improved training time and accuracy on medical text classification tasks under heterogeneous setting.

II
Preliminaries

In this section, we summarize the commonly used FL methods used in distributed training, namely FedAvg, FedProx, and FedRS.
These methods address challenges in model aggregation and client variability in FL. We denote
N
as the number of participating edge devices and
g_{e}
as the global epoch for aggregation.

FedAvg
: FedAvg is the foundational FL method where each edge device independently trains a model on its local data and then sends the updated parameters to the central server. Then, the central server averages these locally updated models to create a new global model, which is then redistributed to the edge devices for further training.
FedAvg approach is particularly effective in environments where data distributions across devices are homogeneous. However, FedAvg may experience challenges, such as client drift, in Non-IID settings where data distributions vary significantly among edge devices, leading to slower convergence and potential instability.

FedProx
: FedProx extends FedAvg by introducing a proximal term at edge device local learning process that helps alleviate client-drift in heterogeneous environments.
By constraining each device’s update to be close to the current global model, the proximal term reduces the impact of divergent local models.
Specifically, each edge device
n
minimizes the following objective during local training:

f_{n}(\theta_{g_{e}}^{n})=f_{n}(\theta_{g_{e}}^{n})+\frac{\mu}{2}||\theta_{g_{e}}^{n}-\theta_{g_{e}}||^{2},

(1)

where
f_{n}(\cdot)
is the loss function for edge device
n
,
\theta^{g_{e}}
is the global model, and
\mu
is a tunable parameter controlling the strength of the proximal term.
After local training, the central server aggregates the updated models as in FedAvg.
FedProx maintains stability across diverse client data distributions with the help of the proximal term.

FedRS
: FedRS tackles data heterogeneity problem by scaling down updates for classes missing in an edge device’s local dataset.
During local training, FedRS adjusts the predicted probability
\pi_{i,y}
for class
y
of sample
i
by applying a scaling factor to the weights of missing classes as follows:

\pi_{i,y}=\frac{\exp(\alpha_{y}\,w_{y}^{T}\,h_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{C}}\exp(\alpha_{j}\,w_{j}^{T}\,h_{i})},

(2)

where
w_{y}
is the weight vector associated with class
y
, and
h_{i}
represents the feature vector for sample
i
. Here,
\alpha_{y}=1
for observed classes and
\alpha_{y}=\alpha\in[0,1]
for missing classes, effectively downscaling the influence of those classes. By limiting the effect of missing classes on the model update, this adjustment improves convergence in Non-IID settings when the central server aggregates the models as in FedAvg.

III
Problem and Model

III-A

Federated Heterogeneous Setting

In this section, we describe the mathematical objective of the FL setting and introduce our proposed plugin to handle heterogeneous data distributions across edge devices.
We consider a federated network composed of
N
edge devices, each communicating with a central server.
Each edge device
n=1,\ldots,N
holds a private dataset
\mathcal{D}^{n}
, with each dataset varying in both size and class distribution.
Furthermore, edge devices possess different computational capabilities, affecting the number of local epochs
1\leq l_{e}\leq L
within maximum value
L
, they can perform during each update round.
FL aims to optimize a shared global model
\theta_{g_{e}}\in\Theta
within the parameter space
\Theta
without transferring the local datasets
\mathcal{D}=\cup_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{D}^{n}
to the central server, thus preserving data privacy.

To achieve this, the FL objective aims to minimize the global loss function
F(\theta)
, defined as:

\min_{\theta_{g_{e}}\in\Theta}F(\theta)\triangleq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(\theta_{g_{e}}^{n}),

(3)

where
F(\theta_{g_{e}})
represents the global objective at the central server, and each local objective function
f_{n}(\theta)
for edge device
n
is defined as:

f_{n}(\theta_{g_{e}}^{n})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}^{n}|}\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}^{n}}f_{n}(\theta_{g_{e}};x;y).

(4)

Here,
f_{n}(\theta;x)
represents the loss function for data sample
x
within the local dataset
\mathcal{D}^{n}
.
Due to the Non-IID nature, the distribution of data on edge devices
i
and
j
may differ significantly for
i\neq j
. Such statistical heterogeneity can lead to divergent local models, motivating the need for techniques to balance the data distribution, as proposed in this work.

III-B
1
Phase 1-Data Augmentation with Generative AI

As shown in Fig.
3
, we utilize generative AI to alleviate computational and data imbalance on edge devices, which are representative challenges in FL.
By generating synthetic data, especially for underrepresented classes, the technique augments the datasets, enabling a transformation from Non-IID distributions to approximate IID conditions. Providing a practical solution for edge devices with limited or skewed datasets, this data synthesis method helps to achieve a more balanced learning environment.

Fig. 3:
Illustration of data augmentation at edge device using generative AI to approximate Non-IID data to IID distributions. Original data is supplemented with synthetic data generated by generative AI to balance class distributions.

Let
\mathcal{D}^{n}=\{(x_{i}^{n},y_{i}^{n})\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{D}^{n}|}
represent the local dataset at edge device
n
, where
x_{i}^{n}
is the input data, and
y_{i}^{n}
is the corresponding label.
When certain classes are underrepresented in
\mathcal{D}^{n}
, a generative AI
\mathcal{G}
is used to synthesize additional samples to balance the dataset:

\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\text{aug}}=\mathcal{D}^{n}\cup\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{D}^{n}),

(5)

where
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{D}^{n})
generates synthetic samples to balance the distribution. Specifically, the generative AI model produces samples for each class
y
where data is insufficient, ensuring that class imbalance is minimized. To avoid introducing unseen data, the process only generates data for classes that are present in
\mathcal{D}^{n}
:

\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{D}^{n})=\bigcup_{y\in\mathcal{C},|\mathcal{D}^{n}_{y}|>0}\mathcal{G}_{y}(\mathcal{D}^{n}),

(6)

where
\mathcal{G}_{y}(\mathcal{D}^{n})
denotes the synthetic data generated for class
y
, and
\mathcal{C}
represents all classes.

To determine the quantity of synthetic data, we calculate the deficiency ratio for each class
y
by comparing its size
|\mathcal{D}^{n}_{y}|
with the maximum class size
|\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\text{max}}|
:

\Delta_{y}=\frac{|\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\text{max}}|-|\mathcal{D}^{n}_{y}|}{|\mathcal{D}^{n}|},\quad\text{if }|\mathcal{D}^{n}_{y}|>0.

(7)

If
\Delta_{y}
is positive, indicating class imbalance, the generative model synthesizes additional samples as:

\mathcal{G}_{y}(\mathcal{D}^{n})=\begin{cases}r(1,\Delta_{y}\cdot|\mathcal{D}^{n}|)&\text{if }|\mathcal{D}^{n}_{y}|>0\\
0&\text{if }|\mathcal{D}^{n}_{y}|=0\end{cases}

(8)

where
r(1,\Delta_{y}\cdot|\mathcal{D}^{n}|)
is a random integer between 1 and
\Delta_{y}\cdot|\mathcal{D}^{n}|
. The final augmented dataset for each edge device
n
is thus:

\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\text{aug}}=\mathcal{D}^{n}\cup\bigcup_{y\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{G}_{y}(\mathcal{D}^{n}).

(9)

Data augmentation ensures that the local model trained on
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\text{aug}}
benefits from a more balanced class distribution, improving the global model’s performance by mitigating the effects of Non-IID data. Consequently, this approach allows edge devices to contribute more effectively to the federated learning process, enhancing convergence speed and model robustness.

Phase 2-Balanced Sampling

Following data augmentation phase, the central server implements a balanced sampling strategy as shown in Fig
4
.
This strategy optimizes model aggregation at the central server by focusing on edge devices whose set of data distributions most closely approximate an IID target.
By prioritizing balanced data across edge devices, the strategy improves convergence, ensuring that model updates are derived from a representative subset of
\mathcal{D}
.

Fig. 4:
Illustration of the proposed balanced sampling at the central server, selecting set of
K=2
edge devices with statistically representative data distributions for aggregation to achieve a optimized global model.

For each edge device
n
with augmented dataset
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\text{aug}}
, the proportion of data for each class
y
is calculated as:

p_{n}(y)=\frac{|\{x\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\text{aug}}:y=y\}|}{|\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\text{aug}}|},

(10)

where
p_{n}(y)
denotes the proportion of class
y
in edge device
n
’s dataset. By collecting these class proportions from each device, the central server calculates a weighted global distribution
p_{\text{global}}(y)
as:

p_{\text{global}}(y)=\frac{\sum_{i}p_{i}(y)\cdot|\mathcal{D}^{i}_{\text{aug}}|}{\sum_{i}|\mathcal{D}^{i}_{\text{aug}}|}.

(11)

Aiming for uniform class representation, the target IID distribution is:

p_{\text{IID}}(y)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|},

(12)

where
|\mathcal{C}|
is the total number of classes. By measuring the distance between each edge device’s class distribution
p_{i}(y)
and the target IID distribution
p_{\text{IID}}(y)
using a distance function
d(\cdot)
, such as Euclidean or Manhattan distance, the central server assesses similarity to the target distribution:

d(p_{i}(y),p_{\text{IID}}(y))=\|p_{i}(y)-p_{\text{IID}}(y)\|_{2}.

(13)

Next, the central server then forms a subset by selecting K edge devices that show the smallest distances to the target IID distribution. Only these selected edge devices participate in the local learning stage, ensuring that the aggregation process draws from a well-balanced subset of data distributions. Each selected edge device performs local training on its augmented dataset
\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\text{aug}}
using the AdamW
[
14
]
optimizer.

The local objective function for each selected edge device is defined as:

f_{k}(\theta_{g_{e}}^{k})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\text{aug}}|}\sum_{(x_{i}^{k},y_{i}^{k})\in\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\text{aug}}}f_{k}(\theta_{g_{e}};x_{i}^{k},y_{i}^{k}),

(14)

where
f_{k}(\theta_{g_{e}};x_{i}^{k},y_{i}^{k})
represents the loss function (e.g., cross-entropy loss) for the sample
(x_{i}^{k},y_{i}^{k})
.
By incorporating both adaptive moment estimation and weight decay, the AdamW optimizer updates the local model
\theta_{g_{e}}^{k}
, enhancing convergence and generalization.

AdamW updates the parameters as follows. First, compute the gradient of the local objective function:

\textsl{g}_{l_{e}}=\nabla_{\theta_{g_{e}}}f_{k}(\theta_{g_{e},l_{e}}),

(15)

where
l_{e}
denotes the local epoch step.
Update the first moment estimate
m_{l_{e}}
and the second moment estimate
v_{l_{e}}
using exponential moving averages:

\displaystyle m_{l_{e}}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}m_{l_{e}-1}+(1-\beta_{1})\textsl{g}_{l_{e}},

(16)

\displaystyle v_{l_{e}}

\displaystyle=\beta_{2}v_{l_{e}-1}+(1-\beta_{2})\textsl{g}_{l_{e}}^{2},

(17)

where
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
are hyperparameters controlling the decay rates (e.g.,
\beta_{1}=0.9
,
\beta_{2}=0.999
). Bias-corrected estimates are computed to adjust for the initialization bias:

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{l_{e}}

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{l_{e}}}{1-\beta_{1}^{l_{e}}},

(18)

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{l_{e}}

\displaystyle=\frac{v_{l_{e}}}{1-\beta_{2}^{l_{e}}}.

(19)

Update the model parameters with weight decay
\lambda
as:

\theta_{g_{e},l_{e}+1}=\theta_{g_{e},l_{e}}-\eta_{l}\left(\frac{\hat{m}_{l_{e}}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_{l_{e}}}+\epsilon}+\lambda\theta_{g_{e},l_{e}}\right),

(20)

where
\eta_{l}
is the local learning rate,
\epsilon
is a small constant to prevent division by zero, and
\lambda
is the weight decay coefficient.
Each edge device performs
1\leq l_{e}\leq L
local epochs of training using these update rules, resulting in an updated local model
\theta^{k}_{g_{e}}
. By benefiting from the adaptive learning rates of AdamW and regularization through weight decay, the local optimization process leads to improved performance on heterogeneous data.

After local training, the central server aggregates the locally updated models
\vartheta=\{\theta^{k}_{g_{e}}\}_{k=1}^{K}
to update the next global model
\theta_{g_{e}+1}
. By aggregating the local models using weighted averaging based on dataset sizes, the global model is then updated:

\theta_{g_{e}+1}=\frac{\eta_{g}}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{g_{e}}^{k},

(21)

where
\eta_{g}
is the global learning rate.
This process is repeated for each global epoch
g_{e}
, iteratively refining the global model until the desired performance is achieved or the maximum number of global epochs
G
is reached.

In our experiments, we set the number of selected edge devices participating in each global round to

We applied the proposed method to enhance the performance of commonly used federated learning algorithms, including FedProx and FedRS, which are designed to handle heterogeneous environments in FL.

For data augmentation, we utilized the Ollama API to leverage representative Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate synthetic data from original data.
We used multiple LLMs for data generation, including Gemma 1.0 (7B parameters)

The experimental outcomes demonstrate that our synthetic data augmentation and balanced sampling strategies not only improve accuracy but also significantly enhance training efficiency across different FL frameworks.

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a novel plugin for FL that addresses data heterogeneity challenges through generative AI-enhanced data augmentation and balanced sampling strategy.
Our experiments demonstrated the plugin’s effectiveness across various text classification models and FL algorithms while reducing required training epochs.
The plugin showed strong performance improvements in medical domain-specialized models, with BioBERT achieving 53.4% accuracy from a 42.1% baseline.
Our approach maintained robust performance under extreme Non-IID conditions, showing consistent improvements across different LLM configurations.
These results establish our plugin as a flexible solution for enhancing FL performance in real-world heterogeneous environments.
