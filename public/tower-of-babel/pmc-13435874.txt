Transesophageal echocardiography and pulmonary artery catheter use and short-term outcomes in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass surgery: A multi-center retrospective study
Authors: Kiyan Heybati, Olivia Haldenby, Jiming Fang, Jodie Chiu, Jiawen Deng, Jacobo Moreno Garijo, Pablo Perez, Dennis T. Ko, Stephen Fremes, Maral Ouzounian, Angela Jerath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile the use of transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) or pulmonary artery catheters (PAC) has been studied in coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), outcomes associated with their concurrent use remain understudied. Therefore, we sought to investigate the association between intraoperative TEE, with or without PAC, and patient outcomes following isolated CABG.MethodsThis population-based study included adults (≥ 18 years old) undergoing isolated CABG from 2009 to 2019 across 11 Ontario centers using administrative databases. The primary outcome was a composite of 30-day mortality and major complications. Multivariable regression with general estimating equations was performed, adjusting for patient, surgical, and hospital factors. Subgroup analyses by surgical urgency and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and a weighted propensity score sensitivity analysis were done.ResultsAmong 70,065 patients undergoing CABG, 45.5% received TEE and 70.7% PAC. PAC alone (45.2%) was the most common, followed by TEE + PAC (25.5%). Adjusted analyses revealed a higher risk of the primary outcome for TEE + PAC (aRR 1.18; 95% CI, 1.11–1.25) and PAC alone (aRR 1.05; 95% CI, 1.02–1.09), while TEE alone was not significant (aRR 1.06; 95% CI, 0.99–1.14) versus neither modality. PAC alone was associated with lower 30-day mortality (aRR 0.67; 95% CI, 0.49–0.93) versus neither modality. Sensitivity analyses were consistent. TEE + PAC was associated with a higher risk of the primary outcome in elective and emergent surgeries versus neither modality. For LVEF ≥ 35%, both TEE + PAC and PAC alone were linked to higher risk; no differences were observed in LVEF < 35%. When compared to TEE alone, TEE + PAC was associated with a lower risk of 30-day mortality (aRR 0.59; 95% CI, 0.36–0.96).ConclusionsCombined TEE + PAC use in CABG was associated with higher adverse outcomes versus neither modality. PAC use was associated with mixed findings depending on the comparator. Prospective studies are needed to identify subgroups benefiting from specific modalities and evaluate longer-term outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05927-1.

Introduction

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) surgery remains the most commonly performed cardiac surgical procedure globally, with an estimated incidence of approximately 36.7 cases per 100,000 population annually [1]. Advanced intraoperative monitoring strategies frequently used to support patient care include transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and pulmonary artery catheters (PAC). Both monitors provide complementary hemodynamic assessment, with TEE offering additional information on cardiac structure while PAC provides continuous assessment of cardiac pressures and flow [1, 2].

The ACC/AHA/SCAI Guidelines for Coronary Artery Revascularization recognized TEE as a tool for the real-time assessment of cardiac function [3]. TEE is also strongly recommended by the American Society of Anesthesiology (ASA) to guide clinical decision-making in cardiac surgical patients with valvular or aortic disease, infective endocarditis, or to manage hemodynamic instability unresponsive to conventional therapies [4]. However, routine TEE use during isolated CABG surgery remains debated, with limited data and conflicting study findings on patient outcomes such as death, complications, and length of hospital stay [5, 6]. Pulmonary artery catheters continue to be used widely in cardiac surgery (i.e. up to 73% of patients) despite limited high-quality evidence supporting their routine use, with only weak recommendations from international societies endorsing PAC use in CABG [7]. Pulmonary artery catheters are particularly advantageous for providing real-time continuous hemodynamic measurement and supporting cardiac therapeutic decision-making during surgery and postoperative intensive care unit (ICU) settings, particularly for patients with ventricular dysfunction and pulmonary hypertension. Unlike TEE, PAC placement requires less formal training and certification and can be used for monitoring beyond the intraoperative period, including in those who are no longer sedated.

Although several observational studies have independently evaluated the effect of TEE or PAC on surgical outcomes in CABG and mixed cardiac surgical cohorts with varied findings, the effect of TEE in the context of concurrent PAC monitoring among patients undergoing isolated CABG surgery has not been studied [5–7]. Given the widespread use of both modalities, additional evidence is needed to further clarify any potential benefits of these monitoring techniques and better inform clinical decision-making. Hence, this population-based study of adult patients undergoing isolated CABG surgery sought to evaluate the association between intraoperative TEE, with or without use of PAC, and short-term postoperative patient outcomes across Canadian cardiac surgical centers.

Methods

Study design

This multi-center retrospective cohort study was conducted using population-based administrative healthcare databases in Ontario, Canada. The use of data in this project was authorized under Sect. 45 of Ontario’s Personal Health Information Protection Act [8], and research ethics board review was not required [9, 10]., This study was completed following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) framework (Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM] Table 1) [11].

Study cohort

We included adults (≥ 18 years of age) who underwent isolated CABG between 2009 and 2019 across all 11 Ontario hospitals performing cardiac operations. ​ For those with multiple procedures during the study dates, only the index procedure was included to avoid interdependence of observations. We excluded patients who were not Ontario residents, those who died intraoperatively, cases with procedure dates and institutions that could not be verified in CorHealth Ontario [12], and patients with missing outcomes data. Accordingly, a total of 70,065 patients undergoing CABG were included in the main analysis (Fig. 1).

Data sources and covariates

As outlined in prior studies [9, 10], cardiac surgeries and specific cardiac health information were identified through CorHealth Ontario. Demographics (i.e. age, sex), hospital setting (i.e. rural versus urban), and socioeconomic status were obtained from the the Registered Persons Database (RPDB) [13], Vital Statistics [13] and Ontario census data (description of datasets is included in ESM Table 2). Socioeconomic status was based on neighborhood income quintiles (1 being the lowest, 5 being the highest).

As previously described [9, 10], the Canadian Institute of Health Information Discharge Abstract Database (CIHI-DAD) and National Ambulatory Care Reporting System (NACRS) consist of all acute care hospital admissions and provide information during primary hospitalization, as well as readmissions [14]. Physician service claim characteristics were obtained from the Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP) database. Surgical variables included duration, urgency (elective versus emergent), and date of procedure. Hospital variables included teaching status, surgical volume, and bed number [15]. Comorbid conditions were identified through several databases (i.e. Ontario Diabetes Database, Asthma Database, Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease Database, Ontario Hypertension Database, and Ontario Myocardial Infarction Database), as well as using ICD-10 codes from hospital admissions within 3 years of the index surgery [13]. Patient factors of interest included prior myocardial infarction, hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, stroke, chronic liver disease, anemia, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), year of surgery, and components of the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score. Renal function was captured using the most recent glomerular filtration rate obtained from the Ontario Laboratory Information System (OLIS) before surgery [16, 17]. We also captured whether patients had a preoperative transthoracic echocardiogram up to 6 months before the index surgery date.

Major complications within 30 days after surgery (e.g. ICU re-admission, re-operation, re-hospitalization, or another advanced intervention) were extracted from CorHealth, CIHI-DAD, Continuing Care Reporting System (CCRS), Ontario Laboratory Information System (OLIS), and Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP) [16, 17]. Mortality was identified through the Registered Persons Database (RPDB) [13]. ICU and hospital length of stay (LOS) were determined through CIHI-DAD [14]. Data were linked through unique anonymized patient identifier numbers. The cardiac surgery procedure codes are summarized in ESM Tables 3, and in-depth database details have been previously described [13, 14, 18].

Exposure variable

Use of TEE and PAC was captured from physician billing codes using OHIP on the day of CABG surgery for PAC use and the day of CABG up to 3 days postoperatively for TEE use (ESM Table 3) [15]. These codes are separate from the fee codes used for CABG surgery and anesthesia, which are submitted by the primary surgeon and anesthesiologist providing patient care. The code for TEE is distinct, allowing this imaging modality to be differentiated from transthoracic and other forms of echocardiography. The TEE fee codes permit reimbursement for multiple assessments during a surgical case (i.e. comprehensive imaging before and after cardiopulmonary bypass). However, for this analysis, only one TEE code per patient was used to represent intraoperative imaging. Use of TEE and PAC was operationalized as a four-level categorical variable: both TEE and PAC used, TEE used without PAC, PAC used without TEE, neither TEE nor PAC used.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was a composite of 30-day postoperative mortality and major complications. Major complications have been previously defined [9, 18] and include patients requiring ICU readmission, reoperation, rehospitalization, or another advanced intervention (e.g., pacemaker, angioplasty, dialysis, tracheostomy, prolonged ventilation, intra-aortic balloon pump, endoscopic procedure, extra-corporeal life support). Secondary outcomes included assessment of 30-day postoperative mortality and major complications separately, as well as postoperative ICU and hospital LOS.

Statistical analysis

The data were summarized using descriptive statistics with frequency (percentage) for categorical variables and median (interquartile range, IQR) for continuous variables. Patient, surgical, and outcome variables were compared across the four groups of patients receiving TEE and PAC, as described above. The unadjusted proportion of patients managed with the use of TEE and PAC was described over the study period by calendar year and by hospital institution.

The main analysis used a multivariable regression model with general estimating equations to evaluate the association between TEE and PAC use with study outcomes after adjusting for the above patient, surgical, and hospital characteristics determined a priori with the primary 30-day composite outcome. Surgical CABG volume was operationalized as tertiles, age was assessed for every 10 years, and surgical duration used a 3-knot restricted cubic spline. Variables with data assumed to be missing at random included rural setting (n = 90, 0.1%), socioeconomic status (n = 266, 0.4%), left ventricular function (n = 2,608, 3.7%), preoperative eGFR (n = 9,994, 14.3%), and duration of surgery (n = 82, 0.1%). Multiple imputation using a predictive mean matching approach was used for variables with missing data. The model clustered patients within hospitals and used a Poisson distribution with a log link to estimate relative effect size and presented as adjusted relative risk (aRR, 95% confidence interval). The adjusted event rates were estimated using Lsmeans methods in PROC GENMOD [19]. The secondary outcomes of 30-day mortality and morbidity were assessed using a similar model. ICU and hospital LOS were modelled using a negative binomial distribution with a log link. The unit analysis for all models was at the patient level. Sensitivity analyses included restricting the primary outcome to patients with complete data and applying a weighted propensity score (PS) to further explore the effect of TEE with and without PAC, as well as PAC with and without TEE on study outcomes. The propensity score model compared the exposure of TEE with no PAC to no TEE or PAC monitoring, combined TEE and PAC monitoring to PAC alone, PAC alone to neither modality, as well as PAC with TEE to TEE only. Propensity scores were estimated using a hierarchical logistic regression model with hospitals as a random effect, adjusting for all covariates listed above. The average treatment effect was estimated using stabilized PS weights. Covariate balance after weighting was assessed using standardized differences, with values generally below 0.1 indicating acceptable balance. Relative risks (RR) for the outcomes were calculated using generalized estimating equations models with a robust variance estimator.

Several additional analyses were performed to evaluate the effect of TEE and PAC use on the primary outcome. To explore the a priori hypothesis that use of TEE is beneficial and may be associated with a lower incidence of the primary composite outcome in patients with poor left ventricular function or needing emergency surgery, subgroup analyses were performed by stratifying patients on the urgency of surgery (elective or emergent) and LVEF (< 35% and ≥ 35%, as predefined by the Cardiac Care Network [CCN] [20]).

All analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel (v.2010, Redmond, WA), and SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, US) [9]. Two-sided p-values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. No statistical power calculation was performed before conducting this study, and the sample size was based on the available data meeting the above eligibility requirements. No evidence of multicollinearity was observed with variance inflation factors (VIFs) of < 5.

Results

During the study period, 70,065 patients undergoing CABG were included (Fig. 1) across 11 institutions. The overall utilizations of TEE (45.5%, 31,892) and PAC (70.7%, 49,556) were common across study sites. From 2009 to 2019, use of TEE steadily rose from 34.8% to 54.7% while use of PAC fell from 88.4% to 58.3% (Fig. 2A). Intraoperative use of TEE (8.3% to 89.6%) and PAC (1.1% to 98.4%) varied between hospitals, with higher utilization of PAC generally seen among institutions with lower use of TEE (Fig. 2B).

Use of PAC without TEE was most common (45.2%, 31,677), followed by use of both TEE and PAC (25.5%, 17,879), TEE without PAC (20.0%, 14,013) and neither PAC nor TEE (9.3%, 6,496; Table 1; ESM Table 4). Patients were clinically similar in age (median [IQR] 66 [59–73] years) and sex (20.0%, 14,019 female) across all four groups, with most undergoing elective CABG surgery (54.4%, 38,144). Overall, the burden of common chronic health diseases such as diabetes, stroke, and prior myocardial infarction was numerically higher among patients receiving both TEE and PAC or PAC alone and lower among patients managed by neither PAC nor TEE (Table 1; ESM Table 4). Use of PAC and TEE was clinically similar across patients of different socioeconomic status, but there was a numerically higher use of TEE alone in rural settings. Patients with advanced left ventricular dysfunction (LVEF < 35%) were most commonly managed by both TEE and PAC or TEE alone (Table 1; ESM Table 4). Within 30 days after CABG surgery, 13.4% (9,400) of patients had experienced the primary outcome, with 12.8% (8,966) having had a major complication and 0.6% (434) having died. The rates were numerically higher among patients receiving both TEE and PAC, followed by PAC alone, neither TEE nor PAC, and lowest among those receiving TEE alone, with a significant difference between the 4 groups (ESM Table 5). The median postoperative ICU and hospital length of stay were 27 (22–52) hours and 6 (5–8) days, respectively. Specifically, ICU LOS varied between a median of 26 h in patients with no TEE or PAC and PAC alone to 30 h in patients with TEE alone or a combination of both TEE and PAC (ESM Table 5).

Following adjustment for patient, surgical and hospital characteristics, we identified significantly higher adjusted relative risk for the 30-day composite outcome in patients receiving both TEE and PAC (aRR 1.18, 95% CI 1.11–1.25; event rate 24.0%, 95% CI 15.2–37.8) and PAC alone (aRR 1.05, 95% CI 1.02–1.09; event rate 21.4%, 95% CI 13.7–33.7), with no difference among patients receiving TEE alone (aRR 1.06, 95% CI 0.99–1.14; event rate 21.6%, 95% CI 13.4–35.0%), versus patients receiving neither TEE or PAC. Similar associations were seen across groups on the secondary outcome of 30-day morbidity, but the use of PAC alone was associated with a lower risk of 30-day mortality versus neither modality (aRR 0.67, 95% CI 0.49–0.93). Post-operative ICU LOS was the longest among patients with both TEE and PAC or TEE alone, whereas no association was observed with PAC alone versus patients with neither modality (Table 2). Post-operative hospital LOS was significantly higher across all patient groups that received TEE and/or PAC monitoring versus those with neither (Table 2). Sensitivity analysis after removing patients with any missing data points (i.e. remaining sample of 57,639) revealed similar findings for the primary outcome (ESM Table 6).

Discussion

This multi-center observational study assessed the outcomes of TEE and PAC monitoring in patients undergoing isolated CABG across 11 Canadian cardiac surgery centers. Over a 10-year study period, TEE use steadily rose with overall rates similar to those observed in other large multi-center studies using the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Database (53.9 to 62%) [6, 21]. With rising TEE use, we observed a steady decline in PAC use with overall rates remaining high and consistent within the wide range (9–73%) described by other mixed cardiac surgery cohort studies [7, 22–24].

Our study findings add to the literature that has typically evaluated TEE or PAC in isolation and produced mixed results. We identified that patients monitored with a PAC either alone or in combination with TEE had a higher short‑term risk of the composite morbidity‑mortality outcome. This excess risk may in part reflect confounding by indication, as sicker or more medically and surgically complex patients are more likely to receive invasive and additional hemodynamic monitors. Although our datasets are unable to capture hospital medications, the related use of vasoactive, anti‑arrhythmic or inotropic agents guided by these monitors to optimize cardiac function in this patient subgroup is likely associated with longer ICU and hospital stays and worse patient outcomes, as seen in this and other studies [25].

Our findings revealed that PAC use alone was associated with a higher risk of the primary composite outcome. This was mainly driven by higher morbidity (i.e. complications, particularly in patients with LVEF ≥ 35%) but may have short-term mortality benefits with no change in ICU LOS. The evidence surrounding PAC in cardiac surgery patients has been heavily debated, with mixed findings in the literature. The largest randomized clinical trial conducted around 20 years ago, involving a mixed medical and surgical ICU study population in the United Kingdom, found that PAC use did not improve survival or reduce ICU or hospital LOS [26]. Research specifically focused on adult cardiac surgery remains limited and is largely observational. These studies have reported mixed findings, differing from lower ICU stay with less cardiopulmonary morbidity, fewer transfusions, to no significant survival or health system benefit [27–29]. A more recent nationwide observational study of over 60,000 mixed cardiac surgical patients in Korea demonstrated lower 30-day (aOR 0.76, 95% CI 0.69–0.83) and 1-year (aOR 0.85, 95% CI 0.79–0.90) mortality in patients managed using a PAC [7]. Overall, these studies are limited by their nonrandomized nature, mixed cardiac surgery cohorts with varied perioperative risk, and sample size; however, they highlight the continuing interest and use of PAC in cardiac surgery.

Use of TEE alone was not associated with more favorable postoperative morbidity and mortality outcomes, but was linked to longer ICU and hospital LOS. This contrasts with prior studies that have reported mortality benefits from intraoperative TEE use in patients undergoing CABG [5, 6]. Specifically, observational studies using Medicare or STS datasets in 114,871 to 1.2 million patients observed that TEE use was associated with lower short-term mortality (aOR 0.95, 95% CI 0.91–0.99), particularly among higher-risk patients, but was also associated with increased odds of unplanned valve surgeries [5, 6]. However, these studies did not examine the concomitant use of PAC monitoring on outcomes. Our study did not replicate these findings using either regression or weighted propensity score models, which may potentially be due to its smaller size. It also did not identify any clear benefit in higher-risk patients (i.e. poor ventricular function [LVEF < 35%], emergency surgery). Consistent with prior observational studies, data on additional clinical and medication factors that may identify higher-risk patients receiving TEE or PAC, such as ongoing chest pain, need for intravenous heparin, nitroglycerin, and heart failure drugs at the time of surgery, are unavailable.

The use of TEE, PAC, or both is influenced by many factors, including local institution protocols, provider training and preferences, local echocardiography expertise and availability, teaching opportunities for trainees, and the patients’ surgical risk and need for continuous cardiac assessment [30]. High-quality contemporary randomized data specific to cardiac surgery are lacking to determine which patients would benefit from PAC, TEE, or both modalities. Although we were unable to specifically examine in our datasets, prior studies have shown that serious adverse events from TEE (i.e. esophageal perforation) and PAC (i.e. knots, pulmonary infarction) remain relatively uncommon [5, 7, 25].

This study has several notable strengths, including the analysis of a large, population-level sample of more homogenous patients (i.e. isolated CABG), over 10 years, across all 11 hospitals performing cardiac surgeries in the province of Ontario. We also collected data on an extensive number of covariates, as determined a priori, which allowed us to adjust for patient, surgical, and hospital characteristics through multivariable regression modelling. We also used a weighted propensity score to improve the balance between study groups to evaluate the relationship of TEE on study outcomes, which confirmed similar findings. Although the use of TEE in isolated CABG surgery has not received strong recommendations, this study adds to the growing body of literature by uniquely understanding its role when a PAC is also used to monitor patients during and after surgery.

Our study also has several limitations, primarily related to the data source. Given the retrospective nature of the study, there is a potential for misclassification. As outlined by prior studies, variations in billing, physician documentation, and practices could impact the assignment of ICD-10 codes [31–33]. Nonetheless, this coding system has been shown to provide relatively reliable population-based estimates of hospitalization rates [34]. Additionally, there is a risk of residual confounding as data on physiological variables, illness severity scores, and variables used in risk scoring systems that may identify sicker patients were not available in administrative databases. Important patient symptoms and clinical care factors, including ongoing chest pain, anticoagulation, nitroglycerin infusions, as well as suspicion for valvular disease and/or tamponade and/or aortic calcifications (i.e. favoring TEE), along with concern for pulmonary hypertension and/or a shock state (i.e. favoring PAC) can influence patient risk assessment and the choice of intraoperative monitoring is not recorded in administrative databases. Notably, the current study also did not look at the outcomes of those receiving TEE who were then found to have valvular heart disease requiring repair in addition to CABG and further investigation is therefore warranted. However, we tried to capture more complex patients based on surgical acuity, left ventricular function, and preoperative glomerular filtration rate. Administrative data may also underestimate the true incidence and severity of complications after surgery. Therefore, this could have impacted the unadjusted estimates for the composite outcomes and complications. Due to data limitations, we also could not capture complications that may have been as a direct result of TEE and/or PAC use, such as esophageal injury (i.e. from TEE) and/or new arrhythmias/entrapment from PAC placement. Additionally, data on individual hospital protocols were unavailable; however, the management strategy of patients undergoing CABG in Ontario is relatively consistent regarding antibiotic prophylaxis, senior physician-led care, routine monitoring, and postoperative ICU care. Due to the failure of databases to capture key variables predictive of cardiac risk (i.e. race, preoperative medications, arrhythmia), we were also unable to adjust for the Society of Thoracic Surgery risk scores. We captured the use of TEE within 3 days of surgery to manage misclassification of the date of surgery and acknowledged that some echocardiography imaging may have been performed after surgery. Moreover, while the 10-year study period (i.e. 2009 to 2019) enabled us to describe trends in the use of TEE and PAC over time, due to the evolving practice patterns, additional studies are needed to provide comparative data on more recent years.

Conclusions

Over a 10-year study period, TEE use grew steadily, with a gradual decline in PAC use but overall rates remained high. Combined TEE + PAC use in patients undergoing isolated CABG was associated with higher rates of combined morbidity and mortality versus neither modality. The use of PAC was associated with mixed findings depending on the comparator. Given the retrospective design, further prospective data are needed to confirm these findings and clarify which patients are likely to experience the greatest benefit from these monitoring modalities.
