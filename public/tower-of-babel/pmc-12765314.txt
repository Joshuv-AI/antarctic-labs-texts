Unveiling the role of root functional traits in enhancing nitrogen use efficiency in wheat under nitrogen deficiency
Authors: Jian Zeng, Xigui Hu, Kehao Zhang, Yiran Cheng, Yi Wang, Houyang Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenetic variation in plant functional traits has been recognized as a potential avenue for enhancing nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) in wheat, which is a cost-effective strategy for reducing environmental burden of excessive N fertilizer application. In this study, a total of 126 diverse wheat genotypes originating from 16 countries were cultivated in pots supplied with a low N level at 10 mg kg− 1 to explore the functional traits determining NUE and their responses to N limitation.ResultsUnder low N condition, significant genetic variations were observed in root morphological traits, photosynthesis capability and NUE among the wheat genotypes. The ranges of NUE and physiological NUE across 126 genotypes were 0.39–1.41 g mg− 1 and 0.39–1.26 g mg− 1, respectively. Multivariate analysis revealed the examined root morphological traits were closely associated with plant N content, NUE and biomass accumulation under low N condition. Specially, roots with high root mass density (RMD) were beneficial to improve NUE, while high specific root length (SRL) and specific root surface area (SRA) were positively correlated with plant N concentration in response to N limitation. In contrast, photosynthesis traits exerted little effect on NUE in wheat at early growth stage under low N condition.ConclusionRoot morphological traits were superior to photosynthesis capacity in mediating wheat response to low N condition. Notably, RMD holds promise for application in breeding programs aimed at improving NUE in wheat.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07828-5.Keywords: Root traits, Nitrogen deficiency, Wheat genotype, Photosynthetic performance, Nitrogen use efficiency

Background

In contemporary agriculture, a crucial issue is to enhance crop yield while minimizing the cultivation costs. Nitrogen (N), a fundamental element regulating plant growth and development, is widely regarded as the primary limiting nutrient for agricultural production [1]. Soil N concentration and distribution vary considerably due to several environmental factors such as soil physical properties, leaching, and microbial activity [2]. Although N fertilizers are essential for increasing cereal crop yields, only 30%–50% of applied N fertilizers is absorbed by crops [3]. The remaining N is either immobilized in soil organic matter or lost from the soil, ultimately resulting in substantial economic losses and environmental pollution [4]. Thus, developing novel strategies to decrease the dependence on N fertilizers and minimize N loss is of great urgency [5, 6]. An effective approach would involve coordinated management of soil water and N status, enhancement of root N acquisition efficiency from the soil, and optimization of the conversion of acquired N into vegetative biomass or edible organs. Therefore, the development of N-efficient crops represents an ideal solution for minimizing N fertilizer loss while improving agricultural productivity, profitability, and sustainability [7].

Natural plant genotypes exhibit significant genetic variations in functional traits that contribute to N use efficiency (NUE) [8]. The NUE is a complex trait involving gene interaction networks that regulate N uptake, assimilation, translocation, and storage, which refers to the economic yield per unit N supplied to plants in most cases. Plant responses to N availability are determined by both genotype and the interaction between genotype and N supply level. Changes in N supply levels frequently altered plant NUE, and a negative correlation between NUE and the amount of N taken up by plants has been reported [9]. Efficient N utilization is important for plants growing in N-poor environments. Studies have shown that plants can modify root development and growth, adjusting the root-to-shoot ratio and lateral to primary root ratio in order to forage for soil nutrients [10]. The root system exhibits high plastic in response to soil nutrients availability, and coordinated changes in root traits directly influence plant N acquisition [11, 12]. Under low N condition, plants typically allocate more assimilates to root morphogenesis, which is crucial for promoting N acquisition [13]. Mild N limitation stimulates primary and lateral root elongation, whereas severe N starvation inhibits primary root growth and lateral root primordium development [14, 15]. Therefore, low N condition triggers adaptive modifications of root traits, which directly regulate N uptake, transport, and assimilation processes, thereby mediating NUE at the whole-plant level.

In addition, enhancing photosynthesis has been regarded as a critical strategy for increasing crop production. Previous studies have established a link between improved photosynthesis and higher NUE, and attempts have been made to enhance NUE in rice by optimizing photosynthetic capacity and sink strength [16]. At the leaf level, photosynthetic N use efficiency (PNUE) is defined as the rate of carbon assimilation per unit leaf nitrogen, which reflected the physiological relationship between leaf N content and photosynthetic capacity. PNUE increased towards higher mass-based maximum photosynthetic carbon assimilation rates and leaf N concentrations [17]. Under low N condition, most plants tend to allocate more N to leaves to maintain sufficient leaf area for high photosynthetic productivity [18]. In modern wheat cultivars, appropriate N application rates can improve NUE and achieve high productivity by optimizing N distribution and increasing photosynthetic capacity [19]. However, it remains unclear whether improving photosynthesis can still enhance NUE in wheat under low N condition.

Wheat is a staple food crop for 35% of the global population and consumes large amounts of N. A previous study has demonstrated that total root length and distribution determine water and nutrients uptake as well as overall crop performance [20]. Increased root length density and root depth are closely related to high N uptake and assimilation in wheat [21]. Moreover, N uptake and utilization in wheat varied greatly among cultivars with high root plasticity [22]. Therefore, identifying genotypes with favorable root functional traits for integration into elite germplasm has become a feasible approach for improving NUE in wheat following varying N levels. It is thus necessary to simultaneously examine root functional traits and photosynthetic capacity across a large number of wheat genotypes.

In the present study, 126 wheat genotypes from 16 countries were used to investigate the root functional traits and photosynthetic ability associated with N acquisition and utilization under low N condition. We focused on variations in root functional properties, including total root length, average root diameter, root volume, root surface area, root mass density, specific root length and specific root surface area. In addition, gas exchange parameters, chlorophyll fluorescence indices, dry mass accumulation, N uptake and NUE were integrated to assess the relationships between root traits, photosynthesis and NUE under low N level. Therefore, the study integrated 7 root morphological traits, 10 plant economic traits and 9 leaf photosynthetic traits with aim of (1) revealing the genetic variation in plant functional traits under low N condition; (2) exploring the associations between root functional traits and NUE in response to low N condition; (3) clarifying the correlations between genetic variation in photosynthetic ability and NUE traits under low N condition. This knowledge will provide valuable insights into how root traits interact with each other or with photosynthesis to enhance N acquisition and utilization in wheat.

Materials and methods

Plant cultivation

A total of 126 genetically diverse wheat genotypes, collected from 16 countries between 30° and 45° north latitude were used in this study. These genotypes were kindly provided by the United States Department of Agriculture–Agricultural Research Service and the Xinjiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences of China (Table S1). Among the 126 genotypes, 60 were from European countries, 32 from Middle East countries, and 34 from Asian countries. Seeds were surface-sterilized in 10% (v/v) H2O2 for 5 min, thoroughly rinsed with distilled water for 10 min, and germinated on moist filter paper in the dark at room temperature. About two weeks later, uniformly developed seedlings were transplanted into black cylindrical plastic pots (10 cm diameter × 15 cm height) filled with 1.5 kg of mixed substrate (sterilized quartz sand: vermiculite: perlite = 1:2:2, w/w) at the two-leaf stage. Each pot contained three seedlings, and the N source was supplied as ammonium nitrate at a rate of 10 mg kg− 1 growth substrate. To ensure adequate supply of other nutrients, the mixed substrate was supplemented with the following basal nutrients: 50 mg kg− 1 KH2PO4, 200 mg kg− 1 KCl, 50 mg kg− 1 MgSO4, 5 mg kg− 1 EDTA·Fe·Na, 0.72 mg kg− 1 H3BO3, 5.8 mg kg− 1 MnCl2·4H2O, 8 mg kg− 1 ZnSO4·7H2O, 2.0 mg kg− 1 CuSO4·5H2O and 0.25 mg kg− 1 Na2MoO4. All Seedlings were grown in a greenhouse from October 6th to November 10th in 2020, with an average day/night temperature of 25/15°C, and a mean relative humidity of 65%. Each genotype had three replicate pots, and pots were placed in greenhouse completely at random and watered daily to maintain a moisture content of 65%.

Biomass accumulation assay

Plants were harvested at the tillering stage. One wheat plant from each replication were collected and then dried in an oven at 105 °C for 30 min followed by 70 °C to a constant weight. Root dry weight (RDW) and shoot dry weight (SDW) of each plant were measured using analytical balance, and total dry weight (TDW) was calculated as the sum of RDW and SDW. The root to shoot ratio was calculated as RDW divided by SDW.

Root morphological traits analysis

At the end of the experiment, root samples from each replicate were carefully washed with tap water and homogeneously spread in a plastic tray filled with water. Root scanning and analysis were performed using Win-RHIZO software (Regent Instruments Inc., Québec, QC, Canada) connected to a scanner. The following root morphological parameters were determined, total root length (TRL), average root diameter (ARD), root volume (RV), and root surface area (RSA). Additionally, specific root length (SRL) and specific root surface area (SRA) were calculated as TRL/RDW and RSA/RDW, respectively, representing TRL and RSA per unit root dry mass. Root mass density (RMD) was calculated as RDW divided by RV [23].

N content and N use efficiency determination

Dried plant samples were ground into a fine powder and approximately 0.3 g of ground samples was digested with a mixed solution consisting of H2SO4 and H2O2 (5:1, v/v) at 180–200 °C for pre-carbonization followed by heating at 250–300 °C until the solution turned clear. Shoot and root N contents were determined using a Kjeltec 8400 analyzer (FOSS Manufacturing Company, Hilleroed, Denmark). NUE was calculated as the ratio of shoot dry weight to shoot N content, while physiological NUE as the ratio of total dry weight to total plant N content [24, 25].

Gas exchange and photochemistry determination

During the tillering stage before harvesting, gas exchange of young, fully expanded leaves at the top of plants were measured using a LI-6400 portable photosynthesis system (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA) between 9:00 am and 12:00 pm. During measurements, leaf temperature and relative humidity were maintained at 25 ± 2 °C and 70%, respectively. The illumination intensity was controlled at 400 ± 5 µmol m− 2 s− 1 and ambient CO2 concentration was set to 380 ± 5 µmol mol− 1. The following photosynthetic parameters were measured for three plants per replicate: net photosynthetic rate (Pn), intercellular carbon dioxide concentration (Ci), stomatal conductance (gs), and transpiration rate (Tr). Relative chlorophyll content was measured using a chlorophyll meter of SPAD-502 (Konica, Minolta INC, Tokyo, Japan) and leaf area was determined with a LI-3000 C portable leaf area meter (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA). Photosynthetic N use efficiency (PNUE) was calculated as the ratio of Pn to leaf N content per unit leaf area.

Chlorophyll fluorescence traits of the same leaves used for gas exchange measurement were analyzed using an IMAGING-PAM chlorophyll fluorescence spectrometer (WALZ, Germany). Leaves were dark-adapted for 30 min before measurements to determine minimum fluorescence (Fo) and maximum fluorescence (Fm). The parameters for PSII efficiencies and photochemical quenching, such as non-photochemical quenching (NPQ), photochemical quenching coefficient (qP), maximum photochemical efficiency (Fv/Fm), and actual photosynthetic efficiency (Yield) were calculated according to Sperdouli and Moustakas [26].

Statistical analysis

All plant trait data were expressed as mean values ± standard deviation (SD) across 126 wheat genotypes in response to low N availability. Coefficients of variation (CV) were calculated to assess trait variability. The comparisons of different parameters between groups were performed by independent samples t-test. * and ** indicated p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the correlation matrix was performed with the prcomp function in R (Version 4.2.2) to determine the multivariate ordination of the functional traits for 126 wheat genotypes and PCA plots were generated using the Factoextra package in R. Linear regression analysis was conducted in OriginPro 2018 (OriginLab corporation, Massachusetts, USA).

Results

Plant growth variations

Substantial variation in dry matter accumulation was observed among the 126 wheat genotypes under low-N supply (Fig. 1). SDW ranged from 0.14 g plant− 1 (genotype PI57145) to 1.23 g plant− 1 (genotype CITR14345), with a mean of 0.48 g plant− 1 and a CV of 45.3% (Fig. 1A). RDW varied from 0.01 g plant− 1 (PI57145) to 0.22 g plant− 1 (Shumai969), with a mean of 0.08 g plant− 1 and a CV of 56.9% (Fig. 1B). TDW differed by a nearly 9-fold, ranging from 0.15 g plant− 1 (PI57145) to 1.37 g plant− 1 (CITR14345), with a mean of 0.56 g plant− 1 and a CV of 44.8% (Fig. 1C). The R/S ratio also exhibited large variation, ranging from 0.07 (PI45234) to 0.35 (Aikang58), with a mean of 0.21 and a CV of 39.8% (Fig. 1D). Among the 13 genotypes with the top 10% of TDW, six genotypes (Shumai969, PI87115, PI157571, PI117606, CITR15025 and CITR14345) also ranked in the top 10% for both SDW and RDW. Most of these high biomass genotypes were from Asian countries, except PI117606, which originated from Greece. No significant difference in SDW, RDW, TDW and R/S ratio were observed among genotypes from the three geographic regions of origin (Fig. 1).

Correlation analysis and multivariate ordination

Principal component analysis (PCA) of 26 functional traits across the 126 wheat genotypes under low N condition identified four principal components that explained about 63.1% of the total variation (Fig. 5). The first component (PC1) represented 25.3% of the variance and was primarily associated with RDW, root N content, RV, SDW, SRL and SRA. The second component (PC2) represented 18.8% of the variance and was dominated by shoot N concentration, physiological NUE, NUE, shoot N content, RMD, RSA and TRL; and third component (PC3) (9.8% of variation) was associated with PNUE, Yield and Pn, while PC4 (9.2% of variation) was linked to qP (Fig. 5, Table S2). In the biplots of PC1 versus PC2, genotypes mainly from Asia (e.g. CITR15021, CITR15032, PI61347, CITR15030, Aikang58 and CITR15033), two genotypes from Middle East (PI585032 and PI57144) and one genotype (CITR11362) from Europe were scattered in the direction of NUE, physiological NUE and RMD. In contrast, some European genotypes were scattered in the direction of shoot and root N concentration, SRA and SRL. Similar patterns were observed in other bioplots, where Asian genotypes consistently clustered with NUE and physiological NUE (Fig. 5).

Correlation analysis revealed that most root morphological traits co-varied with dry matter weight and N content under low N condition (Table S3). Shoot and root N contents were positively correlated with root morphological traits. Shoot and root N concentrations were positively correlated with SRL (r = 0.36 and 0.48, p < 0.01) and SRA (r = 0.38 and 0.47, p < 0.01), but negatively correlated with RMD (r = −0.60 and 0.54, p < 0.01). SRL was negatively correlated with NUE (r = −0.26, p < 0.01) and physiological NUE (r = −0.27, p < 0.01, Table S3), while SRA was negatively correlated with NUE (r = −0.26, p < 0.01, Table S3) and physiological NUE (r = −0.27, p < 0.01, Table S3). However, RMD was positively correlated with NUE (r = 0.58, p < 0.01, Table S3) and physiological NUE (r = 0.57, p < 0.01, Table S3). With the exception of Ci, gas exchange traits were positively and significantly correlated with RDW and root N concent, whereas, genetic variation in PNUE at the leaf level was not associated with physiological NUE or NUE at the whole plant level (Table S3).

Discussion

The relationship between root traits and NUE

The development of N-efficient crops is a top priority, as N deficiency frequently limits crop productivity. The cultivation of wheat with high NUE, particularly under low N input, is an effective strategy to reduce N pollution and ensure food security [27]. Generally, plants adapt to low soil nutrient availability by increasing root absorptive surface and directing growth toward nutrient-rich soil patches [28]. Modifications in root morphology play a key role in enhancing N acquisition efficiency [29]. In this study, there were large variations in root morphological traits among the 126 wheat genotypes under low N condition, and most of these traits were strongly correlated with SDW, RDW, shoot and root N contents. These results indicated that root morphological traits have a strong influence on N content and biomass production in wheat at early growth stage under low N condition. In particular, SRL and SRA were positively correlated with shoot and root N concentrations (Table S3). Roots with higher SRL and lower ARD typically have higher root turnover rates and shorter lifespan, which is a manifestation of lower production cost [30]. Importantly, SRL is positively correlated with root-nutrient uptake efficiency and negatively correlated with soil fertility [3, 31]. Therefore, low SRL is commonly observed in infertile soil. Additionally, RMD was highly correlated with NUE or physiological NUE at whole plant level, highlighting the importance of RMD for improving NUE in wheat under low-N supply. Previous studies have revealed that coarse roots and roots with high RMD have higher construction costs per unit root length, but exhibit enhanced stress resistance [32, 33]. Our results confirmed that high RMD is associated with improved NUE in wheat under low N condition, which is consistent with earlier findings on root traits changes related to nutrition acquisition in maize and chickpea [34, 35]. High RMD is associated with denser root tissue and have a longer lifespan, which increases the surface area of nutrient-binding sites (e.g., cation exchange capacity via cell wall polysaccharides) and reduces N leaching from root apoplasts [23, 32]. Since substantial variation in root phenotypes exists within crop germplasm for array of root phenotypes that improve nitrogen capture, combining these observations with the characterization of major quantitative trait loci for N acquisition-related root traits will increase the feasibility of improving NUE through the genetic improvement of root system proliferation [36].

N is an important nutrient for promoting plant growth, and biomass accumulation directly reflects N utilization efficiency, making it useful indicator of crop nutrient use. Malik et al. [37] provided an insight into how the NUE of wheat can be enhanced through various responses to N supply levels. In the present study, substantial variability in SDW, RDW, and TDW was found among the 126 wheat genotypes, and RDW was correlated with NUE and physiological NUE under low N condition (Table S2). Previous studies have demonstrated that shoot and root growth rates are associated with increased N use capacity in wheat during the early developmental stage [38, 39]. However, shoot biomass accumulation drives root N acquisition rather than vice versa in wheat under low N condition [40]. Our results showed that N accumulation was tightly correlated with both above-ground and below-ground dry mass accumulation, suggesting that shoot and root biomass accumulation play equally important and potentially synergistic roles in N uptake and utilization in wheat grown in N-deficient soil during early growth stage. Further field works are required to confirm this conclusion.

Association of photosynthesis traits with NUE

In woody plants, NUE can be improved by increasing PNUE [41], but this relationship remained to be verified in crop plants. N regulates photosynthetic efficiency primarily through its effects on leaf area index, leaf N content, chlorophyll synthesis, and stomatal conductance [42]. However, PNUE tends to gradually decrease as plant N uptake increases [43]. Increasing photosynthetic rate for a given leaf N concentration may enhance PNUE and decreasing the N content per unit leaf area within a certain range can improve NUE [44]. In our study, large genetic variations in photosynthetic functional traits and PNUE were identified among the 126 wheat genotypes under low N condition. However, no correlation was found between PNUE and physiological NUE or NUE, suggesting that leaf-level PNUE has no effect on improving whole-plant NUE in wheat at early growth stage under low N stress. This is consistent with the findings of Pang et al. [45], who reported that leaf-level photosynthetic phosphorus use efficiency (PPUE) is not associated with whole-plant PPUE or P-utilization efficiency in chickpea under low P conditions. N and P acquisition and utilization interact at multiple levels to influence plant physiological and metabolic processes [46]. However, PNUE of flag leaf is closely associated with NUE in wheat plants with high N accumulation, but only during the jointing-to-anthesis period [47]. Therefore, the effect of leaf-level PNUE on whole-plant NUE depended on the growth and development stage of wheat, and improving PNUE as a potential breeding target for reducing N input can be ignored during the early growth stage. Furthermore, no correlation was observed between photosynthetic functional characteristics and NUE or physiological NUE. This may be because N deficiency limited the ability of enhanced photosynthesis to improve NUE in wheat at early growth stage. Under N-deficient conditions, maize maintains electron transport by prioritizing N uptake and restricting N allocation to chlorophyll light-capturing proteins, leading to reduced photosynthesis [48]. In this case, N allocation in the photosynthetic apparatus directly affected photosynthetic capacity. Reynolds et al. [49] also suggested that improved photosynthesis during the post-anthesis period resulting from increased sink strength can positively affect NUE. Thus, the effect of photosynthesis on NUE depends on the plant growth and developmental stage. Multiple factors influence whole-plant NUE and biomass production, including N availability, environmental conditions, and N remobilization [50–52].

With regard to the geographical origin of genotypes, there were three main groups, with 34 genotypes from Asia, 60 genotypes from the Middle East and 32 genotypes from Europe. Our results showed that Asian genotypes had significantly higher Pn, Tr, and PNUE than those from the Middle East and Europe, while European genotypes had significantly higher qP and Yield than those from Asia or the Middle East. Asian genotypes also had significantly higher Fv/Fm than Middle East genotypes under low N condition. However, the effect of geographic origin was only significant for PNUE (p = 0.049) and Yield (p = 0.025), indicating that genetic variation in photosynthesis is less affected by geographic origin.

Pot experiment limitations and field applicability

While our pot experiment controlled for environmental variability, it is important to acknowledge limitations in extrapolating results to field conditions. Pot size may have restricted root expansion, particularly for high-RMD genotypes with dense root systems. Previous studies show that pot-bound roots can overestimate RMD-NUE correlations [53], as limited space enhances root packing. In fact, field conditions involve additional stressors (e.g., drought, soil compaction) that may interact with RMD. For instance, high-RMD roots have greater mechanical strength [33], which could enhance NUE under soil compaction by maintaining root integrity. Integrating RMD with stress tolerance traits in field trials will further strengthen its value as a breeding target. Such validation of pot based findings in field trials is essential for agricultural production.

Conclusion

Our results revealed substantial genetic variations in root and photosynthetic functional traits among the 126 wheat genotypes under low N condition. Root morphological traits, including TRL, RSA, ARD, and RV, were positively correlated with N content, whereas SRL and SRA were negatively correlated with N concentration. Importantly, RMD was positively correlated with NUE and physiological NUE in wheat at early growth stage under low N condition, making it a promising trait for inclusion in wheat breeding programs for improving NUE. In contrast, photosynthetic traits showed little correlation with NUE in wheat during the early growth stage under low N condition. Genotypes from Asia exhibited higher Pn, Tr, PNUE, NUE and physiological NUE than those from the Middle East or Europe, indicating their potential for breeding N-efficient wheat varieties with reduced dependence on N fertilizers. In conclusion, root functional traits, especially RMD should be prioritized in breeding programs for N-efficient wheat varieties. Future research should integrate phenotypic and genotypic data for genome-wide association mapping to identify key genetic loci and further elucidate the physiological and molecular mechanisms underlying low-N tolerance in wheat.
