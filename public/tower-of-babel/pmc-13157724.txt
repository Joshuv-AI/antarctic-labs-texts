Socioeconomic disparities and mortality among acute patients with sepsis: a retrospective Danish cohort study
Authors: Camilla Schade Skov, Mikkel Brabrand, Bas de Groot, Flemming Schønning Rosenvinge, Helene Skjøt-Arkil, Christian Backer Mogensen, Isik Somuncu Johansen, Annmarie Touborg Lassen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesSocioeconomic inequalities exist in infectious diseases and sepsis in high-income countries. We investigated the association between income and mortality among patients with sepsis, overall and among those treated in the intensive care unit (ICU) versus general wards.DesignA retrospective register-based cohort study.SettingThe Region of Southern Denmark (RSD).ParticipantsAll adult patients with an unplanned contact with a hospital in the RSD from 1 January 2016 to 20 March 2018. Patients with sepsis were identified based on the following criteria: (1) blood culture(s) performed within 48 hours of arrival, (2) antibiotic(s) administered within 48 hours of arrival, (3) a discharge diagnosis of infection and (4) a SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment) score of ≥2. The cohort was divided into quartiles according to household income.Primary and secondary outcome measuresCox proportional hazards models were used to estimate the association between income groups and mortality. The primary outcome was 90-day mortality with 7-day and 365-day mortality as secondary outcomes. All outcomes were calculated overall and stratified by general ward treatment only and ICU admission.ResultsWe identified 7813 first-time visits with community-acquired sepsis, including 886 ICU admissions (11.3%). Among patients in the lowest income group, sepsis was associated with a HR of 1.16 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.34) for 90-day mortality compared with the highest income group. This association was particularly pronounced at 365-day follow-up: HR=1.24 (95% CI 1.10 to 1.39). No difference was observed in 7-day all-cause mortality, HR=1.13 (95% CI 0.89 to 1.45). The association was not observed among patients admitted to the ICU.ConclusionLow income was associated with increased mortality in patients with sepsis, particularly during long-term follow-up. The impact of income disparities was not observed among patient admitted to the ICU.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition defined as organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection and may account for up to 15% of all deaths in Denmark.1 2 Previous research has indicated that socioeconomic inequalities persist in infectious diseases and sepsis in high-income countries.3 4 A low socioeconomic status (SES) is associated with increased incidence, mortality and risk of readmission in patients with sepsis.46 This inverse association has also been observed in Danish patients with bacteraemia7 8 and in patients with sepsis admitted to a Danish intensive care unit (ICU).9

Similar disparities have been reported in a population-based cohort study from Norway, which examined the relationship between individual-level SES and the risk of sepsis and sepsis-related death. The study demonstrated an increased risk of mortality among patients with sepsis and low SES and suggested that this increased risk was primarily mediated by potentially modifiable lifestyle and health-related factors (eg, smoking, alcohol consumption, cardiovascular disease and chronic diseases).10

No studies have assessed SES in community-acquired sepsis identified by combining clinical measures on arrival with blood test results and discharge diagnoses. Furthermore, comprehensive research investigating the impact of individual SES on mortality in patients with community-acquired sepsis treated in general wards versus those admitted to the ICU is lacking. Knowledge of how SES influences sepsis-related mortality across different levels of care is important because it might indicate if hospital factors contribute to disparities.

In this study, we aimed to (1) estimate the incidence of community-acquired sepsis among all acute patients in the Region of Southern Denmark (RSD), based on clinical measures on arrival combined with blood test results and discharge diagnoses; (2) investigate whether income disparities are associated with increased mortality among sepsis patients overall, and among those treated in the ICU versus general wards and (3) estimate the absolute risk of mortality stratified by age group, sex, comorbidity and income.

Methods

Study design and setting

In this register-based cohort study, we included all adult patients (≥18 years) arriving at an emergency department (ED) or at an acute medical unit in the RSD between 1 January 2016 and 20 March 2018. The RSD has a population of approximately 1.2 million residents, of whom 968 761 were aged ≥18 years as of 1 January 2017 (see online supplemental appendix C.1 for a more detailed description of the organisation of healthcare and acute care in the RSD).

Selection of study population

Among all acute adult patients, we identified those who presented with sepsis on arrival at the hospital. Patients with repeated contacts with sepsis were included at their first contact within the study period. The date of hospital admission was defined as the index date. To avoid potential nosocomial sepsis, we excluded patients with any admission 7 days prior to the index date.

Sepsis was defined in accordance with the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock.1 Infection was considered present if the patient had blood cultures performed within 48 hours of arrival, antibiotics initiated within 48 hours of arrival and a discharge diagnosis indicating infection—all three criteria had to be fulfilled (infection diagnosis codes are available in online supplemental appendix table A.1). Organ failure was classified according to the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score,11 modified for the ED (online supplemental appendix table A.2). The chosen cut-off values for organ failures corresponded to an increase of 2 points in the SOFA score. To be classified as a patient with sepsis, the patient had to meet the criteria for infection and have at least one organ failure.

Data sources

All Danish residents have a Civil Personal Register number, which is used in all contacts with the healthcare system and allows for linkage of registers at an individual level.12 In this study, we combined data from local clinical databases and patient medical records with information from Danish nationwide registers.

We extracted the cohort of all adult acute contacts to a hospital in the RSD within the defined period from medical records along with information regarding antibiotic administration and vital signs at arrival (systolic blood pressure, peripheral oxygenation and Glasgow Coma Scale). The hospital logistics system provided data on time of arrival and discharge. Blood test results were extracted from the laboratory databases. Data on blood cultures were retrieved from the microbiological databases.

The cohort was enriched with data from several nationwide Danish registers. We retrieved data on age, sex, civil status, emigration and vital status from the Danish Civil Registry12; discharge diagnoses and comorbidity from the National Patient Registry13; equivalent disposable household income and employment status from the Income Statistics Register14 and the highest completed education from the Danish Education Registers.15

Exposure and outcomes

We selected equivalent disposable household income as the exposure variable, serving as a marker of SES. Equivalent disposable household income is calculated annually by Statistics Denmark and accounts for both total household income (after taxes and transfers) and household composition. An equivalence scale is used to adjust for the number of adults and children in the household.16 Statistics Denmark use the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development modified scale17 to weight income according to household size and composition.

The cohort of patients with first-time community-acquired sepsis within the inclusion period was divided into four groups based on income quartiles: (1) lowest, (2) low, (3) high and (4) highest. Household income was calculated as a mean of the income over the past 5 years prior to the index date. This approach was chosen to counteract the effect of potential outliers, reduce the effect of yearly income variation and minimise potential effect of disease on income. If an individual had a negative equivalent disposable household income, it was recoded to zero, as recommended by Statistics Denmark.

The primary outcome was all-cause 90-day mortality. Secondary outcomes were all-cause 7-day and 365-day mortality. All patients were followed from the index date until death, emigration or the end of the follow-up period.

Analyses and statistics

Baseline and clinical characteristics were presented as proportions or medians, as appropriate, and described according to income quartile. The International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes used for identification of baseline variables are outlined in online supplemental appendix table A.3. The overall incidence of sepsis per 100 000 person-years for the RSD was calculated. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed for each income group to illustrate 90-day sepsis mortality.

We used the Cox proportional hazards models to examine the association between income level and 90-day all-cause mortality. Estimates were calculated for the four income quartiles and presented as hazard ratios (HRs), with the highest income quartile as the reference group. HRs were adjusted sequentially. The sequential adjustment consisted of three analyses: a crude model, a model adjusted for age group and sex and a fully adjusted model adjusted for age group, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI)18 and number of organ failures at arrival. In the main analysis, the absolute risk of 90-day mortality was illustrated graphically by heatmaps.

The analyses were performed overall and repeated in a subgroup analysis, in which all patients with sepsis were stratified according to treatment at the general ward or the ICU. We repeated the analyses using 7-day and 365-day all-cause mortality as outcomes. We performed a sensitivity analysis in which we only included those aged 30–65 years, as individuals in this age group typically represent the working-age population. We expect most individuals above 30 years to have completed their education and thus not to receive the Danish State Educational Grant. Furthermore, we performed a sensitivity analysis with educational level as the exposure variable. Both sensitivity analyses used sequential adjustment similar to the main analyses and included the same mortality outcomes (7-day, 90-day and 365-day mortality). The proportional hazards assumption was tested by log-minus-log plots and Schoenfeld residuals to ensure there were no time-varying covariates. Stata V.18 (StataCorp LLC, Texas) was used for calculations.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study did not involve direct patient participation. Permission to collect and use the data for scientific purposes was given by the Danish Patient Safety Authority by no. 3–3013-2272/1. Data storage was permitted by the RSD (17/24904, 20/24502). All data were stored and handled in accordance with Danish law. Data available to the authors were pseudonymised.

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Patient characteristics

We identified 443 953 acute contacts and 7824 cases of incident community-acquired sepsis during the study period (figure 1). The incidence of sepsis was 364/100 000 person-years in the RSD (95% CI 356 to 373). Income information was unavailable for 11 patients; the final sepsis cohort thus consisted of 7813 patients. Most patients with sepsis were treated in general wards, 6927 (88.7%), while 886 patients (11.3%) were admitted to the ICU during hospitalisation.

Flowchart of study population. ED, emergency department; ICU, intensive care unit; RSD, Region of Southern Denmark; SOFA, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment.

Overall, patients in the lowest and low-income quartiles were older and had a higher comorbidity score (table 1). They had the highest frequency of low educational level. Furthermore, 6.9% of patients in the lowest income quartile were from non-Western countries, while this number was 0.5% in the highest income quartile. The proportion of patients admitted to the ICU was approximately equal across income groups (table 1).

Mortality

A total of 1749 patients (22.4%) with sepsis died within 90 days. The mortality rate was highest among patients in the lowest income quartile (25.6%) compared with those in the highest quartile (17.0%), corresponding to an absolute risk difference of 8.6%. The survival curve stratified by income groups illustrates an early difference in survival between the groups (figure 2). The mortality within the four income groups distributed over the three time periods is illustrated in online supplemental appendix figure B.1.

Crude Kaplan-Meier survival curves of the four income quartiles.

Being a patient with sepsis in the lowest income quartile was associated with a crude HR of 1.59 (95% CI 1.38 to 1.82) for 90-day all-cause mortality compared with the highest income quartile. The effect of income on mortality was gradually attenuated with sequential adjustment for confounders (table 2). Thus, the adjusted model found a 19% increased HR (HR=1.19, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.37), while the fully adjusted model revealed a 16% increase in HR (HR=1.16, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.34) in the lowest income group compared with the highest income group. The effect was approximately the same for patients treated in general wards, whereas no association was found between income group and 90-day mortality among patients admitted to the ICU.

The crude HR for all-cause 7-day mortality was 1.57 (95% CI 1.23 to 1.99) in the lowest income group compared with the highest. This association was attenuated after full adjustment (HR=1.13, 95% CI 0.89 to 1.45), which was also observed across the low and high-income quartiles (table 2). For the 365-day follow-up, the crude HR was 1.67 (95% CI 1.49 to 1.87) comparing patients in the lowest quartile to those in the highest quartile. Although attenuated after adjustment, the HR remained increased (fully adjusted HR=1.24, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.39).

The effect of age and comorbidity on the absolute risk of 90-day all-cause mortality in patients with sepsis is clearly illustrated in the heatmap (figure 3). Increasing age and the CCI strongly contributed to an increased risk of 90-day all-cause mortality, while income group seemed to contribute less.

Sensitivity analyses

When restricting the analysis to those aged 30–65 years, the crude HR for 90-day mortality for patients in the lowest income quartile compared with the highest was 1.63 (95% CI 1.06 to 2.51), with a fully adjusted HR of 1.48 (95% CI 0.96 to 2.28), corresponding to 48% higher mortality; see online supplemental appendix table B.1. Low education was associated with a crude HR of 1.19 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.40) and a fully adjusted HR of 1.08 (95% CI 0.92 to 1.27) for 90-day mortality, compared with high education (online supplemental appendix table B.2).

Discussion

In this study, we found an association between low income and increased mortality. This inverse association was continuously present despite confounder control with a 16% increase in 90-day mortality among individuals in the lowest income quartile compared with those in the highest. The association was persistent for 365-day mortality but was not observed at the 7-day follow-up.

The overall mortality rates in this study were 7.7%, 22.4% and 34.3% for 7-day, 90-day and 365-day mortality, respectively. Similar mortality rates have been reported in other Scandinavian studies, including a Norwegian study with a 365-day mortality of 34.3% and another study reporting comparable 7-day and 90-day mortality rates.19 20 The heatmap (figure 3) revealed low absolute risks of mortality among younger patients with a CCI of zero. For comparison, other researchers have estimated an in-hospital mortality of 4.6% among ICU patients with sepsis aged ≤44 years and without comorbidities.21 However, as illustrated by our heatmap, few events occurred among younger patients, limiting the ability to estimate robust absolute risks in these groups.

A previous Danish study reported a crude HR of 1.58 (95% CI 1.39 to 1.80) when comparing patients with bacteraemia in the low-income tertile to those in the highest. This unadjusted HR aligns with our estimate (HR=1.59, 95% CI 1.38 to 1.82), although their fully adjusted HR was higher than ours (HR=1.30, 95% CI 1.13 to 1.49 vs HR=1.16, 1.01–1.34).7 Stensrud et al found that lower educational attainment was associated with an increased sepsis-related mortality (HR=1.32, 95% CI 0.98 to 1.79). However, no data on income were reported. Instead, low SES, measured by occupation, receipt of social benefits or financial difficulties, was associated with an increased risk of mortality in patients with sepsis.10

As suggested by previous studies evaluating SES and mortality risk in patients with sepsis or bacteraemia, the disparities are probably explained by different factors that are difficult to measure. These may include unregistered comorbidities, lifestyle-related factors and poor social support, all of which we assume were part of residual confounding.7 10 A study from Norway found that smoking and alcohol consumption explained 57% of the effect between educational level and risk of sepsis, which increased to 82% when the researchers included risk factors of cardiovascular disease and chronic diseases in their mediation analysis.10 Furthermore, it is possible that low SES is associated with a different perception of illness, which might influence healthcare seeking behaviour.

Interestingly, the effect of income on mortality was absent in patients with sepsis admitted to the ICU, whereas it was evident among those treated in general wards. This could indicate that once the patients are admitted to the ICU, it is their physiological condition that determines the outcome and not their more permanent risk factors. However, the result could also reflect limited statistical power in the subgroup. Another important aspect is the selection of patients admitted to the ICU, which is typically reserved for patients requiring advanced life support and who are expected to benefit from intensive care with potential to recover. The proportion of ICU admissions within the different income quartiles was almost equal. This seems reasonable, as a previous Danish study found no association between income and risk of ICU admission among patients with sepsis.22

The association of income and mortality seemed most prominent for the 365-day follow-up. This supports the hypothesis that long-term mortality is influenced more by the patient’s permanent risk factors, including SES, while short-term mortality is primarily influenced by the acute disease. Moreover, we found no significant differences in HRs between the income groups in 7-day mortality after confounder adjustment, which indicates that there is no discrimination in acute hospital care among patients with sepsis regardless of SES. The increased risk of mortality associated with low income is probably mediated by several complex out-of-hospital factors. Besides lifestyle-related factors and healthcare-seeking behaviour, these factors could include appropriate transitions in the healthcare system, assignment of relevant rehabilitation programmes and adherence to such rehabilitation programmes. Overall, the time-related increase in HRs likely reflects a delayed impact of SES on sepsis-related mortality, which may be mediated by disparities in postacute care and lack of social support.

Income and educational level are often used interchangeably when studying SES and related outcomes, but despite being related, these indicators are measuring different phenomena. While education is a good indicator of SES early in life, household disposable income may be a better indicator later in life.23 24 Income is a strong indicator of material living standards, and disposable income is the most ideal measure because it represents how much a person—(or in our case) the household—can spend. However, there is a risk of a reverse causation, as health status may influence income. In this study, this implies that CCI and organ failure could act as mediators in the pathway between income and mortality in patients with sepsis. The CCI and number of organ failures were added in the fully adjusted model with a limited change in the HRs compared with the model adjusted for age groups and sex solely. Another important disadvantage is that income is a parameter that can change remarkably within a short time.23 Although we calculated the mean of the income over the 5 years prior to the index date to reduce the impact of short-term illness-related income changes, the potential impact of persistent illness on income cannot be excluded. Furthermore, income is expected to decrease after a certain age due to retirement. In Denmark, all citizens are entitled to a public pension when they reach the state pension age, but many receive pensions from multiple sources (eg, employer-paid pensions or individual pension scheme).25

Strengths and limitations

The completeness of the Danish registers, with detailed demographics on a personal level, is a major strength and eliminates potential selection bias. A persistent challenge in epidemiological sepsis research is to identify patients with sepsis in registers, which often relies on ICD codes. This reliance can lead to substantial variation in reported sepsis incidence depending on the diagnostic codes applied.26 To address this issue, the use of electronic health record data for identifying sepsis cases has been suggested by Rhee et al.27 In this study, we combined clinical measures on arrival to hospital and blood test results with discharge diagnoses of infection to minimise the risk of misclassification bias, which is a major strength. The use of a mean equivalent disposable income for the household during the past 5 years prior to the index date provided a robust indicator of the patient’s SES.

This study has limitations that should be highlighted. There is no gold standard in identification of patients with sepsis in data-driven studies, and the incidence of sepsis highly varies depending on the applied discharge diagnoses.26 It is unknown if the applied method in this study tends to overestimate or underestimate the true incidence of sepsis. The definition of organ failure included some limitations, such as using oxygen saturation <92% in the absence of PaO2 to identify respiratory failure. The extent to which another cut-off might have influenced the results are unknown, but the cut-off was inspired by previous studies and chosen as a pragmatic solution.28 29

We lack individual-level income information for all citizens in the RSD, which limits our ability to further clarify the association between income levels and mortality risk in patients with sepsis. Additionally, the variable nature of income and its potential for sudden changes pose an inherent limitation. The potential for CCI and organ failure to act as mediators rather than pure confounders represents a limitation. Despite using a 5-year mean equivalent disposable household income, residual social drift cannot be fully excluded. Furthermore, the causes of death directly related to the sepsis episode among deceased patients are unknown. This factor should be carefully considered when interpreting the all-cause 365-day mortality. A measure that would have been interesting for this study is frailty—eg, measured by the Clinical Frailty Scale commonly used in elderly patients.30 In a recent retrospective chart review study of all deceased patients in a Norwegian hospital, researchers estimated that 28% of all in-hospital deaths were sepsis-related. The deceased patients with sepsis were characterised by advanced age, frailty and substantial comorbidities.31 If a higher proportion of frail patients were found among those in the lowest income quartile, this could have contributed to an increased risk of mortality among those with low income. Unfortunately, we do not have information on frailty in this cohort.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that socioeconomic disparities influence mortality outcomes related to sepsis. We identified an association between low income and increased mortality in patients with sepsis, particularly pronounced at the 365-day follow-up. Notably, this impact of income disparities was not observed among patients with sepsis admitted to the ICU, nor was it observed in 7-day mortality. The results indicate that the increased mortality among patients with sepsis and low SES is mediated by a complex combination of out-of-hospital factors. Identifying and understanding these factors, including insight into healthcare-seeking behaviour among patients with sepsis and low SES, could contribute to prevention of sepsis or sepsis-related death.
