Convergence of a continuous Galerkin method for the Biot–Allard poroelasticity system
Jakob Seierstad Stokke, Markus Bause, Florin Adrian Radu

Abstract
We study a space-time finite element method for a system of poromechanics with memory effects that are modeled by a convolution integral. In the literature, the system is referred to as the Biot–Allard model. We recast the model as a first-order system in time, where the memory effects are transformed into an auxiliary differential equation. This allows for a computationally efficient numerical scheme. The system is discretized by continuous Galerkin methods in time and equal-order finite element methods in space. An optimal order error estimate is proved for the norm of the first-order energy of the unknowns of the system. The estimate is confirmed by numerical experiments.



keywords
Biot-Allard equations, Poroelasticity, Space-time finite element methods, Dynamic permeability

Introduction

We consider a poroelastic system, which models the coupling of flow and deformation in a fully saturated porous medium and includes memory effects, referred to as the Biot–Allard model. There is a wide variety of applications of the poroelastic theory today, ranging from bio-mechanics and bio-medicine to subsurface engineering. For example, in bone remodeling, the activation of bone cells is closely related to memory effects due to fluid flow along cell bodies
Robling et al. (2006)
. Another example is induced seismicity due to fluid injection. In this case, the delay effects in the Biot–Allard model can be viewed as a consequence of drag forces in the pores, which in large-scale experiments can lead to different onset times for the seismic event. It is worth noting that small-scale experimental results in

;

Computational efficiency has been a major aspect of solving the Biot system. In this respect, splitting schemes, i.e., schemes that sequentially solve the different subproblems, have become very popular. Two commonly used iterative splitting schemes are the fixed-stress and the undrained split. The idea behind them is to add a stabilization term to either the flow or the mechanics subproblem

Mikelić and Wheeler (2013)
;
Both et al. (2017)
;
Bause et al. (2017)
;
Storvik et al. (2019)

, using the gradient flow technique
Both et al. (2019)

Both et al. (2022)
. Furthermore, the fixed-stress approach has been extended to a simplified Biot–Allard model with convolution integrals

In this paper, we seek to solve and rigorously show the convergence of a variational space-time finite element method for the Biot-Allard system. The main challenge is how to deal with the convolution integral. Our solution is to use a result from
Stokke et al. (2024)

N

Variational discretizations in space and time offer appreciable advantages for the discretization of systems of coupled partial differential equations. (3.1c)

\displaystyle c_{1}\rho_{f}F\partial_{t}\Psi+\rho_{f}F\Psi+d_{1}\eta_{k}\nabla p+\rho_{f}d_{1}\eta_{k}\partial_{t}\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\rho_{f}d_{1}\eta_{k}\bm{f},\quad

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega\times(0,T],

(3.1d)

\displaystyle\bm{u}(0)=\bm{u}_{0},\quad\bm{v}(0)=\bm{v}_{0},\quad p(0)=p_{0},\quad\Psi(0)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{0},\quad

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega,

(3.1e)

\displaystyle\bm{u}=\bm{0},\quad\bm{v}=\bm{0},\quad p=0,\quad\Psi

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T].

(3.1f)

The existence of a sufficiently smooth solution to (
3.1
) such that all of the below-given estimates are well-defined is assumed here. For this, the data are supposed to be sufficiently smooth as well. The corresponding weak formulation reads as follows: Find
(\bm{u},\bm{v},p,\Psi)\in\left(L^{2}(I;\bm{H}^{1}_{0})\times L^{2}(I;\bm{H}^{1}_{0})\times L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{0})\times L^{2}(I;\bm{H}^{1}_{0})\right)
such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}\bm{u},\bm{\Phi}^{U}\rangle dt-\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bm{v},\bm{\Phi}^{U}\rangle\,dt=0,\end{split}

(3.2a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\rho\langle\partial_{t}\bm{v},\bm{\Phi}^{V}\rangle dt+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bm{C}\varepsilon(\bm{u}),\varepsilon(\bm{\Phi}^{V})\rangle dt+\int_{0}^{T}\alpha\langle\nabla p,\bm{\Phi}^{V}\rangle dt\\
+\int_{0}^{T}\rho_{f}\langle\partial_{t}\Psi,\bm{\Phi}^{V}\rangle\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}\rho\langle\bm{f},\bm{\Phi}^{V}\rangle\,dt,\end{split}

(3.2b)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}c_{0}\langle\partial_{t}p,\Phi^{P}\rangle dt+\int_{0}^{T}\alpha\langle\nabla\cdot\bm{v},\Phi^{P}\rangle dt+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\nabla\cdot\Psi,\Phi^{P}\rangle\,dt=0,\end{split}

(3.2c)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}c_{1}\rho_{f}F\langle\partial_{t}\Psi,\bm{\Phi}^{\Psi}\rangle dt+\int_{0}^{T}\rho_{f}F\langle\Psi,\bm{\Phi}^{\Psi}\rangle dt+\int_{0}^{T}d_{1}\eta_{k}\langle\nabla p,\bm{\Phi}^{\Psi}\rangle dt\\
+\int_{0}^{T}\rho_{f}d_{1}\eta_{k}\langle\partial_{t}\bm{v},\bm{\Phi}^{\Psi}\rangle\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}\rho_{f}d_{1}\eta_{k}\langle\bm{f},\bm{\Phi}^{\Psi}\rangle\,dt,\end{split}

(3.2d)

for
(\bm{\Phi}^{U},\bm{\Phi}^{V},\Phi^{P},\bm{\Phi}^{\Psi})\in\left(L^{2}(I;\bm{H}^{1}_{0})\times L^{2}(I;\bm{H}^{1}_{0})\times L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{0})\times L^{2}(I;\bm{H}^{1}_{0})\right)
.

Next, we still derive a stability estimate for the solution of (
3.2
). From this result, we identify the first-order energy of the system’s unknowns
(\bm{u},\bm{v},p,\Psi)
. The stability result will then induce the norm for our error control that is based on a corresponding estimation of the discrete error between an interpolation of the continuous solution and its fully discrete approximation.

For simplicity, we derive the stability estimate assuming that the source terms
\bm{f}\equiv 0
. In (
3.2
), we choose the test functions
\bm{\Phi}^{U}=\partial_{t}\bm{u}
,
\bm{\Phi}^{V}=\bm{v}
,
\Phi^{P}=p
and
\bm{\Phi}^{\Psi}=\frac{1}{d_{1}\eta_{k}}\Psi
based on seeking to cancel out the coupled terms. First note that (
3.2a
) disappears, and when we add the equations in (
3.2
) together, the coupling terms arising from
\alpha\nabla p
and
\alpha\nabla\cdot\bm{v}
cancel each other out. The same happens for the coupling terms between the auxiliary and pressure equations. Then, we are left with two more coupling terms

\int_{0}^{T}\rho_{f}\langle\partial_{t}\bm{v},\Psi\rangle\,dt\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\int_{0}^{T}\rho_{f}\langle\partial_{t}\Psi,\bm{v}\rangle\,dt.

To control these terms, we derive an estimate for the velocity and the memory variable simultaneously. Because of our chosen test functions, we define the matrix

\displaystyle\bm{N}:=\begin{pmatrix}\rho&\rho_{f}\\
\rho_{f}&\rho_{f}c_{1}F\eta_{k}^{-1}d_{1}^{-1}\ \end{pmatrix},

(3.3)

and write the combined weak form as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\mathopen{\hbox{${\bigg\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\bigg\langle}$}}\bm{N}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{t}\bm{v}\\
\partial_{t}\Psi\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\bm{v}\\
\Psi\end{pmatrix}\mathclose{\hbox{${\bigg\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\bigg\rangle}$}}+\langle\bm{C}\varepsilon(\bm{u}),\varepsilon(\bm{v})\rangle+c_{0}\langle\partial_{t}p,p\rangle+\frac{\rho_{f}F}{d_{1}\eta_{k}}\|\Psi\|^{2}_{\bm{L}^{2}}\,dt=0.

This then results in the stability estimate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{\hbox{${\bigg\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\bigg\langle}$}}\bm{N}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{v}(T)\\
\Psi(T)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\bm{v}(T)\\
\Psi(T)\end{pmatrix}\mathclose{\hbox{${\bigg\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\bigg\rangle}$}}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\textbf{C}\varepsilon(\bm{u}(T)),\varepsilon(\bm{u}(T))\rangle+\frac{c_{0}}{2}\langle p(T),p(T)\rangle

(3.4)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{\hbox{${\bigg\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\bigg\langle}$}}\bm{N}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{v}_{0}\\
\Psi_{0}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\bm{v}_{0}\\
\Psi_{0}\end{pmatrix}\mathclose{\hbox{${\bigg\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\bigg\rangle}$}}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\textbf{C}\varepsilon(\bm{u}_{0}),\varepsilon(\bm{u}_{0})\rangle+\frac{c_{0}}{2}\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle.

Since the solution of the weak form at a final time
T
is bounded by the initial conditions in this combined energy quantity, we have chosen to analyze the convergence of the numerical scheme with respect to this quantity.

Remark 1
.

The matrix
\bm{N}
defined in (
3.3
) is symmetric positive definite if
\frac{\rho c_{1}F}{\eta_{k}d_{1}}-\rho_{f}>0
. Using the definition of the kinetic viscosity, this simplifies to
\frac{\rho c_{1}F}{\eta d_{1}}>1
. First, note that for any poroelastic medium, the formation factor satisfies
F\geq 1
and becomes large for materials with low porosity. Also, for realistic parameter values, the total mass density
\rho>1
, and viscosity
\eta<1
. Further, we note that
c_{1}>d_{1}
for a variety of different poroelastic materials, including sandstone saturated with water, epoxy-glass, and materials with higher porosity, cf.
(
Stokke et al., 2024
, Remark 1)
. Further examples where
c_{1}>d_{1}
can be found in Appendix
B
. In essence, this means that
\bm{N}
is SPD when considering realistic parameter values.

\displaystyle C(\bm{v},\psi):=-\alpha\langle\nabla\cdot\bm{v},\psi\rangle,

\displaystyle\quad G_{1}(\bm{\phi}):=

\displaystyle\langle\rho\bm{f},\bm{\phi}\rangle,\quad

\displaystyle G_{2}(\bm{\phi}):=\langle\rho_{f}d_{1}\eta_{k}\bm{f},\bm{\phi}\rangle.

We denote the
\bm{L}^{2}
-orthogonal projection onto
\bm{V}^{r}_{h}
by

\displaystyle\langle\textbf{C}\varepsilon(\bm{R}_{h}\bm{w})-\textbf{C}\varepsilon(\bm{w}),\varepsilon(\bm{\phi}_{h})\rangle=0,\quad\mbox{for }\bm{w}\in H^{1}_{0},\,\bm{\phi}_{h}\in\bm{V}^{r}_{h}.

Let the relation
\langle\bm{A}_{h}\bm{w},\bm{\phi}_{h}\rangle=A(\bm{w},\bm{\phi})

\displaystyle\langle\bm{A}_{h}\bm{w},\bm{\phi}_{h}\rangle=\langle\textbf{C}\varepsilon(\bm{w}),\varepsilon(\bm{\phi}_{h})\rangle=\langle\bm{A}\bm{w},\bm{\phi}_{h}\rangle,\,\mbox{for }\bm{\phi}\in\bm{V}^{r}_{h},\,

Remark 3
.

We note that the error estimate (
4.55
) is of optimal order with respect to the approximation in space and time. Here, the error is measured in terms of the first-order energy of the variables
\{\bm{u},\bm{v},p,\Psi\}
of the system (
3.1
). In particular, an error bound for the displacement field
\bm{u}
in the
\bm{H}^{1}
seminorm is part of the estimate. The error norm occurs naturally in our energy-type error analysis and is due to a stability argument similarly to the one of (
4.24
). The error constant
c
depends on norms of the continuous solutions that are introduced in
Lemma

5
and
Lemma

7
to
Lemma

12
. These norms, as well as the weighted energy norm used in the error estimate, may depend on the model parameters. However, the constant
c
does not depend on the discretization parameters
h
and
\tau
.

Remark 4

(Non-equal order approximation)
.

•

The discrete schemes of Problem
1
and
2
are based on an equal-order approximation of the variables. For vanishing coefficients
c_{0}\rightarrow 0
and
|\mathcal{A}|\rightarrow 0
, a Stokes-type system structure is obtained in (
3.1
) for the variables
\partial_{t}\bm{u}
and
p
such that the well-known stability issues of mixed approximations of the Stokes system emerge; cf.
Volker (2016)
. In such a case, equal-order spatial discretizations do not become feasible without additional stabilization of the discretization. Also, structure-preserving first-order in space and time approaches might be applied; cf.
Hong and Kraus (2018)
;
Kanschat and Riviere (2018)
. For a more detailed discussion of stability properties for the quasi-static Biot system, we also refer to, e.g.,
Hong and Kraus (2018)
;
Murad and Loula (1992)
;
Murad and Loula (1994)
;
Murad et al. (1996)
;
Rodrigo et al. (2018)
.

•

Using a non-equal order approximation in space for
(\bm{u},\bm{v},p,\Psi)\in(\bm{V}_{h}^{r+1}\times\bm{V}_{h}^{r+1}\times V_{h}^{r}\times\bm{V}_{h}^{r+1})
built upon the Taylor-Hood pair of elements satisfies the inf-sup stability condition. In
Section

5
, we have included numerical experiments with inf-sup stable elements for the sake of completeness.

•

Based on the numerical results in
Table

3
and
Table

6
, we conjecture that the error estimate (
1.4
) holds for non-equal order inf-sup stable elements. By comparing the estimates in
Lemma

7
with the conjecture (
1.4
), it is apparent that the gradient of the interpolation error of the pressure, i.e.
\langle\nabla\omega,\bm{\Phi}_{\tau,h}^{3}\rangle
, causes a loss of one spatial order in the analysis. We expect that proving the conjecture can be done by building upon
Theorem

14
. The proof of the conjecture remains an open problem and is left as work for the future.

Since we see that the ADE formulation converges towards a manufactured solution of the full convolution integral formulation, we now consider how accurate the reconstruction of the dynamic permeability in the frequency domain compares to the JKD dynamic permeability
(
Johnson et al., 1987
, Eq. 3.4b)
. Under the time harmonic convention
e^{-i\omega t}
, the JKD dynamic permeability for an isotropic medium is expressed as

\hat{\mathcal{A}}(\omega)=\frac{k}{\sqrt{1-\frac{4ia_{\infty}^{2}k^{2}\rho_{f}\omega}{\Lambda^{2}\eta\phi^{2}}}-\frac{i\alpha_{\infty}k\rho_{f}\omega}{\eta\phi}},

(B.1)

where
k
is the permeability,
\eta
is the viscosity,
\phi
is the porosity,
\rho_{f}
is the fluid density,
a_{\infty}
is the infinite-frequency tortuosity and
\Lambda
a tunable geometric parameter. In this sign convention, the approximation of the dynamic permeability in the frequency domain is

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{A}}(\omega)\approx\frac{\eta_{k}}{F}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{d_{i}}{1-i\omega c_{i}},

(B.2)

where
\eta_{k}=\eta/\rho_{f}
and
F=a_{\infty}/\phi
. The constants
c_{i},d_{i}
are obtained by a numerical procedure from
Yvonne Ou (2014)
. We consider three examples: a sandstone formation saturated with water where the application of interest is seismic wave propagation in the frequency range 1Hz-1000Hz, a different sandstone formation saturated with water where the application of interest is acoustic borehole logging in the frequency range 250kHz-350kHz, and a constructed sound absorber saturated with air where the application of interest is noise protection. For the last example, we consider a broader frequency range than the audible range. The associated parameters and constants
c_{i},d_{i}
for each example can be found in
Table

9
. In the first example, the comparison is shown in
Figure

2
; the approximation is highly accurate when
N=1
. Similarly, for the second example, the results are displayed in
Figure

3
;
N=1
appears to be sufficient in the frequency range of interest, despite not being as good a fit as in the previous example. In the last example, see
Figure

4
,
N=1
does not appear to be sufficient for higher frequencies, and a larger
N
results in a better approximation. However, we note that at audible frequencies (20Hz-20000Hz),
N=1
is still accurate. The examples indicate that
N=1
is a sufficient approximation for applications with a narrow frequency bandwidth or at smaller frequencies. For broader ranges,
N
needs to be larger to accurately depict the convolution integral. To assess this fully, a comparison with different numerical approaches for the convolution integral form is required and is left as work for the future.
