Generalization Bounds for Markov Algorithms through Entropy Flow Computations
Benjamin Dupuis* benjamin.dupuis@inria.fr, Maxime Haddouche maxime.haddouche@inria.fr, George Deligiannidis george.deligiannidis@stats.ox.ac.uk, Umut Simsekli umut.simsekli@inria.fr

Abstract
Many learning algorithms can be represented as Markov processes, and understanding their generalization error is a central topic in learning theory. For specific continuous-time noisy algorithms, a prominent analysis technique relies on information-theoretic tools and the so-called “entropy flow” method. This technique is compatible with a broad range of assumptions and leverages the convergence properties of learning dynamics to produce meaningful generalization bounds, which can also be informative or extend to discrete-time settings. Despite their success, existing entropy flow formulations are limited to specific noise and algorithm structures (e.g., Langevin dynamics). In this work, we exploit new technical tools to extend its applicability to all learning algorithms whose iterative dynamics is governed by a time-homogeneous Markov process. Our approach builds on a principled continuous-time approximation of Markov algorithms and introduces a new, exact entropy flow formula for such processes. Within this unified framework, we establish novel connections to a well-studied family of modified logarithmic Sobolev inequalities, which we use to connect the generalization error to the ergodic properties of Markov processes. Finally, we provide a detailed analysis of all the terms appearing in our theory and demonstrate its effectiveness by deriving new generalization bounds for several concrete algorithms.

Introduction

Understanding the generalization error of machine learning algorithms remains a crucial challenge.
We model such learning problems by a tuple
(\ell,\mathcal{Z},\mu_{z},\mathcal{H})
where
\mathcal{H}
is a parameter space (
\mathcal{H}={\mathds{R}^{d}}
in our study),
\mathcal{Z}
is a data space endowed with a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}
,
\mu_{z}
is a data distribution, and
\ell:\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathds{R}_{+}
is a loss function.
We aim to minimize the
population risk
,

\mathcal{R}(w):=\int\ell(w,z)\text{d}\mu_{z}(z)=\mathds{E}_{Z\sim\mu_{z}}\left[\ell(w,Z)\right],\quad w\in{\mathds{R}^{d}},

over the parameter space
\mathcal{H}={\mathds{R}^{d}}
. Unfortunately, since
\mu_{z}
is unknown, practitioners resort to the minimization of the
empirical risk
,

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(w)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(w,z_{i}),\quad w\in{\mathds{R}^{d}},

where
S:=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\sim\mu_{z}^{\otimes n}
is a dataset sampled from
\mu_{z}
.

Modern machine learning systems achieve this minimization through the use of
stochastic optimization algorithms
. In our study, we focus on iterative algorithms with a Markov chain structure:
X_{k+1}^{S}=F(X_{k}^{S},U_{k},S)
where
S\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
and
U_{k}
denotes the internal randomness of the algorithm at step
k
, independent of
S
. This encompasses many popular algorithms, including stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with constant step size
(
Dieuleveut et al., 2018
)
and stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD).
To assess the quality of learning beyond
S
, it is classical to provide
generalization bounds
on the learned parameter
X_{k}^{S}
,
i.e.
, upper bounds on the quantity
G_{S}(X_{k}^{S})
where
G_{S}(w):=\mathcal{R}(w)-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(w)
.
To provide computable guarantees, a popular approach is to derive high probability bounds of the form
1

\lesssim
for informal statements omitting absolute constants or weakly relevant terms.
,

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{S\sim\mu_{z}^{\otimes n}}\left(\mathds{E}\left[G_{S}(X_{k}^{S})|S\right]\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\texttt{Complexity}+\log(1/\zeta)}{n}}\right)\geq 1-\zeta,

(1)

where
Complexity
translates a certain facet of the problem, for instance, the Rademacher complexity
(
Bartlett and Mendelson, 2002
)
or the VC dimension
(
Vapnik, 2000
)
.

Generalization bounds for iterative algorithms.

As Rademacher complexity or VC dimension are algorithm-independent, they are not tailored to exploit the properties of the learning algorithm.
Hence, alternative approaches such as algorithmic stability
(
Bousquet, 2002
)
emerged, which yielded generalization bounds for SGD (among others)
(
Hardt et al., 2016
;
Feldman and Vondrak, 2019
)
exploiting, in particular, its Markovian properties
(
Zhu et al., 2023
)
. Unfortunately, these bounds often rely on relatively strong assumptions (convexity, Lipschitz, and/or Lipschitz gradient) and might not be time-uniform in non-convex settings
(
Bassily et al., 2020
)
.

Another prospect is that of information-theoretic bounds, which provided
expected
bounds for noisy algorithms (e.g., SGLD)
(
Xu and Raginsky, 2017
;
Negrea et al., 2019
;
Haghifam et al., 2020
)
. These methods have also been extended to SGD by
Neu et al. (2021)
at the expense of potential time- and dimension-dependence, and by
Clerico et al. (2023)
in the case of gradient-Lipschitz losses.
Of particular interest to us are the PAC-Bayesian bounds
(
McAllester, 1999
;
Catoni, 2007
)
where the term
Complexity
in
Equation

1
is typically expressed as
\text{{{KL}}}\left({\mathrm{Law}}(X_{k}^{S})||\pi\right)
, where
\pi
is a data-free ‘prior’ distribution,
{\mathrm{Law}}(X_{k}^{S})
is the ‘posterior’ distribution, and
\text{{{KL}}}\left(\cdot||\cdot\right)
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence (KL).
More recently,
Harel et al. (2025)
obtained time-uniform PAC-Bayesian bounds for any Markov algorithm that involves the Gibbs potential of the invariant distribution of the algorithm. However, the existence of this invariant distribution is unclear in many applications, and the Gibbs potential might be intractable in practice.
The Markov structure of SGD was also exploited by
Camuto et al. (2021)
;
Hodgkinson et al. (2022)
through geometric properties, at the cost of non-explicit mutual information terms.

The particular case of continuous-time algorithms.

In parallel to these developments, many studies have focused on ‘continuous-time algorithms’
(Y_{t}^{S})_{t\geq 0}
, typically represented by stochastic differential equations (SDE), for their structure is often easier to understand. Such procedures are often seen as an approximation of discrete-time methods.
A fundamental example is SGD, approximated by Langevin processes
(
Cheng et al., 2020
;
Mandt et al., 2016
;
Anastasiou et al., 2019
;
Xie et al., 2021
)
and heavy-tailed SDEs
(
Simsekli et al., 2019
;
Gürbüzbalaban et al., 2021
;
Raj et al., 2023
)
.
Continuous-time analysis can also be used as an intermediary to reach bounds on discrete-time methods by using interpolation techniques
(
Mou et al., 2017
)
, which unfortunately only hold for specific noise structures.
Moreover, the approximation of discrete-time optimizers by continuous-time dynamics remains largely disputed
(
Li et al., 2021
;
Wojtowytsch, 2021
)
and restricted to specific settings, such as small learning rates
(
Li et al., 2018
)
or high-dimensional limits
(
Ben Arous et al., 2022
)
.

A crucial toolbox: the entropy flow method.

Among continuous-time algorithms, the generalization error of continuous Langevin dynamics (CLD) (and its discrete-time counterpart SGLD) has been studied through many of the techniques mentioned above
(
Raginsky et al., 2017
;
Farghly and Rebeschini, 2021
;
Dupuis et al., 2024
)
, notably involving PAC-Bayesian theory and information-theoretic tools
(
Mou et al., 2017
;
Li et al., 2020
;
Futami and Fujisawa, 2023
)
where the goal is to upper bound the KL divergence
\text{{{KL}}}\left({\mathrm{Law}}(Y_{T}^{S})||\pi_{t}\right)
, with
\pi_{t}
a (possibly time-dependent) prior.
These techniques often rely on the so-called
entropy flow
, which informally consists in the following derivations, up to absolute constants,

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\text{{{KL}}}\left({\mathrm{Law}}(Y_{t}^{S})||\pi_{t}\right)=\mathrm{A}-\mathscr{I}\left(\rho_{t}^{S}|\pi\right)\leq\mathrm{B}-\gamma\text{{{KL}}}\left({\mathrm{Law}}(Y_{t}^{S})||\pi_{t}\right),

(2)

where
\mathscr{I}\left(\rho_{t}^{S}|\pi\right)
is a relative Fisher information term
(
Hyvärinen, 2005
)
, and
\mathrm{A}
and
\mathrm{B}
are quantities that are usually dependent on the stochastic gradient,
{\mathrm{Law}}(Y_{t}^{S})
, and
\pi_{t}
(see
(
Mou et al., 2017
)
). The above inequality is a consequence of the celebrated logarithmic Sobolev inequality (LSI)
(
Gross, 1975
)
, which must be satisfied by
\pi_{t}
.
In most cases, the prior is associated with a data-independent ergodic Markov process (e.g., its invariant measure), which is the source of the inequality in
Equation

2
.
These approaches reveal a fundamental trade-off on the prior between having ergodic properties and being ‘close enough’ to the posterior distribution
{\mathrm{Law}}(Y_{t}^{S})
to make the term
\mathrm{B}
small, see
(
Mou et al., 2017
)
for more details.
Once
Equation

2
is stated, a combination of information-theoretic bounds and Grönwall’s lemma
(
Gronwall, 1919
)
leads to informative generalization bounds, which can be shown to be time uniform under additional assumptions (e.g., Lipschitz losses).

Therefore, this technique has three main advantages compared to the stability-based and other information-theoretic approaches mentioned above:
(i)
it has the ability to produce time-uniform generalization bounds,
(ii)
it rely on a flexible set of assumptions, and
(iii)
it explicitly exploits intrinsic convergence properties of the algorithm through the use of LSIs.
Despite recent extensions to differential privacy
(
Chourasia et al., 2022
)
and
\alpha
-stable noise
(
Dupuis et al., 2025
;
Dupuis and Simsekli, 2024
)
, the entropy flow method remains limited to specific noise structures (
i.e.
, Gaussian or
\alpha
-stable), as it requires a precise description of the time evolution of the density of the Markov process (e.g., a Fokker-Planck equation), which is a core element of all the aforementioned works.

1.1
Contributions

In this work, we propose alleviating the issues mentioned above by extending the scope of the entropy flow method to all Markov algorithms.
At the heart of our approach lies a new class of continuous-time approximations of general (discrete-time) Markov algorithms with formal guarantees.
For a given Markov algorithm
X_{k+1}^{S}=F(X_{k}^{S},U_{k},S)
, we define the
Poissonization
of
(X_{k}^{S})_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}
as the continuous-time process
Y_{t}^{S}:=X^{S}_{N_{t}}
, where
N_{t}
is a Poisson process
(
Lasota and Mackey, 1994
)
(see
Definition

1
).
This technique has been classically used in the analysis of the convergence of Markov chains
(
Diaconis and Saloff-Coste, 1996
;
Chen et al., 2008
;
Caputo et al., 2024
;
Del Moral et al., 2003
;
Wang and Wu, 2020
)
and has recently emerged in optimization theory in
Even et al. (2021)
to study Nesterov acceleration.

To obtain generalization bounds, we derive an exact and compact entropy flow formula for Poissonized Markov algorithms.
Rather than a simple extension, this formula relies on new technical tools. In particular, the role of the Fokker-Planck equation is now replaced by a general “Boltzmann equation”.
We connect our theory to a class of ‘modified’ LSIs
(
Diaconis and Saloff-Coste, 1996
)
and show that this can lead to generalization bounds with improved time dependence compared to classical methods.
This effectively connects the generalization error of Markov algorithms to ergodic theory.
Finally, we provide a rigorous and easy-to-use analysis of all the terms appearing in our bounds and apply our theory to obtain generalization bounds for several practical learning algorithms.
This shows that, similar to the classical entropy flow method, our framework is compatible with a wide range of assumptions, but is applicable to a broader class of algorithms.

Our detailed contributions are listed below.

1.

We analyze the generalization error of Markov algorithms through their Poissonization. We first show that Poissonization is a sound continuous-time proxy and then, with the notation above, prove high-probability bounds on the
Poissonized
generalization error,

\mathds{E}\left[G_{S}(Y_{t}^{S})|S\right]=e^{-t}\sum_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\mathds{E}\left[G_{S}(X_{k}^{S})|S\right].

2.

To achieve this goal, we derive a closed-form expression for the entropy flow of Poissonized algorithms. For a prior distribution
\pi
, our formula is informally given by

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\text{{{KL}}}\left({\mathrm{Law}}(Y_{t}^{S})||\pi\right)=\Delta_{S}(t)-\mathcal{E}_{\pi}(t),

where
\Delta_{S}(t)
is a ‘distance’ between two Markov kernels:
(i)
the kernel of the process
(X_{k}^{S})_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}
and
(ii)
a kernel
P
whose
\pi
is the invariant measure; the term
\mathcal{E}_{\pi}(t)
is called the
Dirichlet form
and characterizes the convergence properties of
P
and
\pi
.

3.

By making the connection with PAC-Bayesian bounds and a class of modified LSIs, we derive generic generalization bounds of the form,

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{S\sim\mu_{z}^{\otimes n}}\left(\mathds{E}\left[G_{S}(Y_{T}^{S})|S\right]\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\gamma(T-t)}\Delta_{S}(t)\text{d}t+\log\frac{3}{\zeta}}\right)\geq 1-\zeta,

(3)

where
\gamma
is a modified LSI constant associated with the prior distribution.

4.

We present various techniques to analyze the term
\Delta_{S}(t)
in several settings for noisy and non-noisy algorithms.

5.

Finally, we apply our framework in several examples. First, we recover Poissonized counterparts of classical generalization bounds for SGLD. Then, we use our entropy flow method to obtain new generalization bounds for both SGD and a recently proposed gradient descent method with noise injection
(
Orvieto et al., 2023a
)
.

Technical Background

In this section, we recall some notions related to Markov kernels and define the
Poissonization

2.1
Markov kernels

Given a time-homogeneous Markov process
(X_{k})_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}
in
{\mathds{R}^{d}}
, the
Markov kernel

P(x,A)
describes the probability of observing
X_{k+1}
in
A
, given that
X_{k}=x
.
More precisely, it is a map
P:{\mathds{R}^{d}}\times\mathcal{B}({\mathds{R}^{d}})\to\mathds{R}_{+}
such that
P(x,\cdot)\in\mathcal{P}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
for all
x\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}
and the map
x\mapsto P(x,A)
is measurable for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathds{R}^{d})
.
Classically
P
induces maps
P:\mathcal{P}({\mathds{R}^{d}})\to\mathcal{P}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
and
P:\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\mathds{R}^{d}})\to\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
defined for
\mu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathds{R}^{d}}),~A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathds{R}^{d})
and
f\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
by

\displaystyle\mu P(A):=\mathds{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[P(X,A)\right],\quad Pf(x):=\mathds{E}_{Y\sim P(x,\cdot)}\left[f(Y)\right].

These operations are dual from each other, in the sense that
\int f\text{d}(\mu P)=\int Pf\text{d}\mu
,
whenever one of the integrals exists
(
Rudolf, 2012
)
.
Note that the operator
P:\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\mathds{R}^{d}})\to\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
may be extended outside of
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
, when it is well-defined.

A probability measure
\pi
is
invariant
under
P
if
\pi P=\pi
, and
reversible
if

\displaystyle\int fPg\text{d}\pi=\int gPf\text{d}\pi,\quad f,g\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\mathds{R}^{d}}).

Dual operator.
Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
and assume that
P
satisfies the following
continuity preservation
condition: for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
, if
\mu\ll\nu
, then
\mu P\ll\nu
.
Let
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\nu)
with
f\geq 0
and
\int f\text{d}\nu=1
. This allows us to define

\displaystyle P^{\star}f:=\frac{\text{d}\mu P}{\text{d}\nu},\quad\text{d}\mu:=f\text{d}\nu.

(4)

We can easily extend this formula for all
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\nu)
by linearity and by decomposing
f
into positive and negative parts. This gives an operator
P^{\star}:\mathrm{L}^{1}(\nu)\longrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{1}(\nu)
.

2.2
Poissonization

Let us first recall the definition of a Poisson process.

Definition 1

(Poisson process)
.

A Poisson process
(N_{t})_{t\geq 0}

{\mathds{N}}

and
\mathds{P}\left(N_{t}=k\right)=e^{-\lambda t}{(\lambda t)^{k}}/{k!}

In all the following, we fix a Poisson process
(N_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with intensity
1
, independent of all other random variables.
We refer to
(

)
for the definition of a Lévy process.
Given a Markov process
(X_{k})_{k\in\mathds{N}}

(

)
is

\displaystyle\boxed{Y_{t}:=X_{N_{t}}.}

(5)

Equivalent definitions of Poissonization exist. For example,

used a stochastic integral formulation in their study of Nesterov acceleration. Poissonization can also be seen as a

,

(

;

;

;
Wang and Wu, 2020
)
.

.

3.1
Poissonized Markov algorithms

In what follows, we fix a data-dependent time-homogeneous Markov process
(X_{k}^{S})_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}
in
{\mathds{R}^{d}}
, where
S\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
.
Several examples (such as SGD and SGLD) will be discussed in

We denote by

the Markov kernel of
(X_{k}^{S})_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}
, for
S\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}

We assume that the dynamics is initialized from a Borel probability measure

,

.

With these notations, our theory aims to obtain generalization bounds for the Poissonized process
(Y_{t}^{S}:=X_{N_{t}}^{S})_{t\geq 0}
, as defined in
Section

2.2
. We will denote by
\rho_{t}^{S}:={\mathrm{Law}}(Y_{t}^{S})
the probability distribution of

, with

and
S\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
.
Our goal is to apply the PAC-Bayesian bounds of
Theorem

2
using
\rho_{t}^{S}
as a posterior distribution. To better exploit the Markovian structure of the problem, we define the prior distribution as follows.

Definition 3

(Prior distribution)
.

We call a prior distribution the invariant measure
\pi\in\mathcal{P}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
of a data-independent Markov kernel
P
, called the prior Markov kernel.

In the following, we systematically assume that the initial distribution

is absolutely continuous with respect to the chosen prior

,

.

Depoissonization.

The generalization error of the Poissonized process is expressed as

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[G_{S}(Y_{t}^{S})|S\right]=e^{-t}\sum_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\mathds{E}\left[G_{S}(X_{k}^{S})|S\right]\quad\text{($\mu_{z}^{\otimes n}$-almost surely)}.

(7)

We show in
Lemma

26
that this quantity is well-defined under our assumptions.
Whether this provides pertinent information about the non-Poissonized iterates is a legitimate concern.
A discrete-time Markov chain and its Poissonized version are known to have comparable properties
(
Jacquet and Szpankowski, 1998
;
Levin and Peres, 2017
;
Caputo et al., 2024
)
. That being said, reconstructing the depoissonized distribution of
X_{k}
from
Y_{t}
is a long-standing technical problem
(
Teugels, 1972
;
Vallée, 2018
;
Jacquet and Szpankowski, 1998
)
.

Beyond these classical depoissonization results, we show below that Poissonization provides a sound approximation of the generalization error of convergent Markov algorithms.

Theorem 4
.

Assume that
|\ell|\leq B<\infty
and that
\mathrm{TV}(\mu_{0}P_{S}^{k},\mu^{S})\to 0
for some
\mu^{S}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
, a.s. for
S
.
Then, a.s.,
\left|\mathds{E}\left[G_{S}(X_{k}^{S})-G_{S}(Y_{k}^{S})|S\right]\right|\to 0
.
If moreover there exists
C_{S}>0
and
a_{S}\in(0,1)
such that, a.s.,
\mathrm{TV}(\mu_{0}P_{S}^{k},\mu^{S})\leq C_{S}a_{S}^{k}
, then, a.s.,

\left|\mathds{E}\left[G_{S}(X_{k}^{S})-G_{S}(Y_{k}^{S})|S\right]\right|\leq 2BC_{S}e^{-(1-a_{S})k}.

If
\ell
is
L
-Lipschitz, then we can replace
\mathrm{TV}
by the Wasserstein distance
\mathrm{W}_{1}
(and
B
by
L
).

The conditions
\mathrm{TV}(\mu_{k}^{S},\mu^{S})\leq C_{S}a_{S}^{k}
(resp.
\mathrm{W}_{1}(\mu_{k}^{S},\mu^{S})\leq C_{S}a_{S}^{k}
) used above are related to
geometric
(resp.
Wasserstein
) ergodicity
(
Meyn and Tweedie, 1993
;
Gallegos-Herrada et al., 2023
)
that has been widely studied in the context of convergence of Markov chains
(
Rudolf and Schweizer, 2017
)
.
Note that our condition is weaker than geometric ergodicity: we do not assume the uniqueness of the invariant distribution.
These concepts have received increasing attention in learning theory due to their connections with SGD
(
Zhu et al., 2023
)
and differential privacy
(
Şimşekli et al., 2024
)
. Although our study is not specific to this exact setting,
Theorem

4
provides a
sufficient
condition to ensure that Poissonization is a relevant continuous-time approximation of discrete dynamics.

3.2
Main assumptions and notations

Whenever it makes sense, we denote the Radon-Nikodym derivative between the posterior distribution and the prior distribution as

\displaystyle v_{t}:=\frac{\text{d}\rho_{t}^{S}}{\text{d}\pi},\quad t\geq 0,~S\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}.

(8)

Our theory relies on a simple condition, which simply ensures that the posterior and prior Markov kernels (
P_{S}
and
P
) are of similar nature, in the sense that
v_{t}
cannot explode too fast. This is made precise by the following assumption.

Assumption 1

(Compatibility condition)
.

Let
\mu_{0}
be an initial distribution such that
\text{{{KL}}}\left(\mu_{0}||\pi\right)<+\infty
and
v_{0}:=\text{d}\mu_{0}/\text{d}\pi
is bounded away from
0
.
We assume that the map
S\mapsto\text{{{KL}}}\left(\rho_{t}^{S}||\pi\right)
is
\mathcal{F}^{\otimes n}
-measurable.
Moreover, we assume the following, for all
S\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}

(H.1)

(continuity preservation)
For all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
, if
\mu\ll\pi
, then
\mu P_{S}\ll\pi
.

(H.2)

For all
t\geq 0
, we have
\Phi(P_{S}^{\star}v_{t})\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\pi)
, where
\Phi(x):=x\log(x)
.

The assumption that
\mu_{0}\ll\pi
and the condition
(H.1)
are natural in our context, since it ensures that
\rho_{t}^{S}\ll\pi
.
We expect it to be mild in practice and, in particular, it holds in the following examples: (i) if
\pi
is fully supported on a discrete space, (ii) if
P_{S}
corresponds to SGD with Gaussian noise addition (
i.e.
, SGLD), (iii) if
\pi\ll\mathrm{Leb}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
and
P_{S}
corresponds to SGD on a gradient-Lipschitz loss with small enough learning rate (see
(
Clerico et al., 2023
, Theorem 3)
).
Moreover, by
Item

(H.1)
, we can define the adjoint
P_{S}^{\star}:\mathrm{L}^{1}(\pi)\to\mathrm{L}^{1}(\pi)
of
P_{S}
with respect to
\pi
, in the sense of
Section

2.1
.

It can be seen from the proof of
Theorem

6
that
\text{{{KL}}}\left(\rho_{t}^{S}||\pi\right)
is finite under the condition
Item

(H.2)

(note that this does not imply that
\text{{{KL}}}\left(\rho_{t}^{S}||\pi\right)
is small, which is the purpose of the presented theory).
The measurability assumption on
S\mapsto\text{{{KL}}}\left(\rho_{t}^{S}||\pi\right)
is mild, as it holds as soon as
\mathcal{Z}
is countable, for instance.
We also expect
(H.2)
to be mild.
In particular, we prove that it holds for all the examples discussed in
Sections

5.1
,
5.2
and
5.3
.
To further illustrate this, we provide a functional characterization of this assumption in
Lemma

27
, showing that this condition is satisfied as soon as
P_{S}
defines a continuous operator
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\pi)\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\pi)
.

3.3
The entropy flow for Markov algorithms

In this section, we present our entropy flow formula for Poissonized Markov algorithms, which is the backbone of our contributions.
We denote
\Phi(x):=x\log(x)
(with
\Phi(0):=0
).

To compute the derivative of the map
t\mapsto\text{{{KL}}}\left(\rho_{t}^{S}||\pi\right)
, we first need to understand the time-derivative of
v_{t}
, defined in
Equation

8
. The evolution of the probability density of a Poissonized process is well-understood and satisfies a so-called Boltzmann equation
(
Lasota and Mackey, 1994
)
. The following lemma is an adaptation of
(
Lasota and Mackey, 1994
, Equation 8.3.7)
to our setup and notation (the main difference being the use of
\pi
as a reference measure). We provide a rigorous proof of this lemma in
Section

A.1
.

Lemma 5

(Boltzmann equation)
.

Assume that the continuity preservation condition (
Item

(H.1)
) holds, then
v_{t}
is solution of the following Boltzmann equation, for all
t>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v_{t}}{\partial t}=\left(P_{S}^{\star}-I\right)v_{t}.

In our paper, the Boltzmann equation plays an analogous role to the Fokker-Planck equations in the analysis of the Langevin algorithm
(
Mou et al., 2017
)
.
Equipped with this lemma, we compute in the next theorem the entropy flow for Poissonized algorithms.

Theorem 6

(Entropy flow)
.

Assume that
Assumption

1
holds. We have that

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\text{{{KL}}}\left(\rho_{t}^{S}||\pi\right)=\int(P_{S}-P)(\Phi^{\prime}\circ v_{t})v_{t}\text{d}\pi-\mathcal{E}_{\pi,P}(\Phi^{\prime}\circ v_{t},v_{t}),\quad t>0.

where
\mathcal{E}_{\pi,P}(f,g):=-\int g(P-I)f\text{d}\pi
is the
Dirichlet form
associated with the prior process.

In the sequel, we use the following notation for the first term of the entropy flow.

Definition 7

(Expansion term)
.

We call
expansion term
the first term on the right-hand side of
Theorem

6
. Whenever it is defined, we will denote it as (note that
\Phi^{\prime}=1+\log
),

\Delta_{P,P_{S}}(f):=\int(P_{S}-P)(\log f)f\text{d}\pi.

Theorem

6
expresses the entropy flow as the difference between the
expansion
term and the
Dirichlet form
.
The expansion term represents the discrepancy between the posterior dynamics
P_{S}
and the prior one
P
, and
Section

4
is dedicated to its analysis.
The Dirichlet form is a classical object in the study of Markov processes
(
Bakry et al., 2014
;
Böttcher et al., 2013
)
, where it plays a crucial role in the construction of continuous-time semigroups and in the formulation of functional inequalities satisfied by their invariant distribution.

By the invariance
\pi
under
P
, the Dirichlet term
\mathcal{E}(\log v_{t},v_{t})
can be represented as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\pi,P}(\log v_{t},v_{t})

\displaystyle=\iint\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(v_{t}(x),v_{t}(y))\text{d}(\pi\otimes P)(x,y)\geq 0,

(9)

where
\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(a,b):=\Phi(a)-\Phi(b)-\Phi^{\prime}(b)(a-b)
denotes the Bregman divergence
(
Amari, 2016
)
.
By the convexity of
\Phi
, we see that
\mathcal{E}_{\pi,P}(\log v_{t},v_{t})\geq 0
. This representation as an integrated Bregman divergence is analogous to the “Bregman integral” appearing in
(
Dupuis and Simsekli, 2024
)
in their study of heavy-tailed SDEs.

Remark 8
.

In
Theorem

6
, the prior
\pi
is the invariant measure of a Markov kernel
P
. Alternatively, one can use time-dependent priors
\pi_{t}
by defining
\pi_{t}
as the law of a Poissonized data-independent Markov process
(X_{k})_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}
. Then
Equation

9
still holds, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\text{{{KL}}}\left(\rho_{t}^{S}||\pi_{t}\right)=\Delta_{P,P_{S}}(v_{t})-\iint\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(v_{t}(x),v_{t}(y))\text{d}(\pi_{t}\otimes P)(x,y),\quad v_{t}:=\frac{\text{d}\rho_{t}^{S}}{\text{d}\pi_{t}}.

Remark 9
.

By adapting the regularity assumptions, our theory holds formally for more general convex functions
\Phi
, hence, yielding
\Phi
-entropy flows, which we leave for future work.

We conclude this subsection with the following theorem, which is the first generic generalization bound that we can deduce from our theory.

Theorem 10
.

Assume that
Assumption

1
holds and that the loss is
\Sigma^{2}
-subgaussian. Then we have, with probability at least
1-\zeta
over
S\sim\mu_{z}^{\otimes n}
that for all
T>0
,

\mathds{E}\left[G_{S}(Y_{T}^{S})|S\right]\leq\frac{2\Sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\left\{\int_{0}^{T}\Delta_{P,P_{S}}(v_{t})\text{d}t+\text{{{KL}}}\left(\mu_{0}||\pi\right)+\log(3/\zeta)\right\}^{1/2}.

Proof.

By
Theorem

2
, with probability at least
1-\zeta
over
S\sim\mu_{z}^{\otimes n}
for all
T>0
,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[G_{S}(Y_{T}^{S})|S\right]\leq 2\Sigma\sqrt{\frac{\text{{{KL}}}\left(\rho_{T}^{S}||\pi\right)+\log(3/\zeta)}{n}}.

(10)

We report in
Section

A.5
a justification of
Equation

10
, taking care of measure-theoretic considerations, which we omit here for simplicity. In particular, under
Assumption

1
,
\text{{{KL}}}\left(\rho_{T}^{S}||\pi\right)<+\infty
.
Fix
S\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
. By
Theorem

6
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\text{{{KL}}}\left(\rho_{t}^{S}||\pi\right)=\Delta_{P,P_{S}}(v_{t})-\mathcal{E}_{\pi,P}(\log v_{t},v_{t}),\quad t>0.

(11)

By
Equation

9
and
Lemma

28
(proven in the appendix), we deduce that

\displaystyle\text{{{KL}}}\left(\rho_{T}^{S}||\pi\right)\leq\text{{{KL}}}\left(\mu_{0}||\pi\right)+\int_{0}^{T}\Delta_{P,P_{S}}(v_{t})\text{d}t.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem

10
shows that obtaining generalization bounds for the Poissonized process reduces to an upper bound on the expansion term
\Delta_{P,P_{S}}(v_{t})
. In
Section

4
, we present several methods to control this term. Before presenting these aspects, we observe that the generalization bound above grows linearly with time even if
\Delta_{P,P_{S}}(v_{t})
is uniformly bounded.
This suggests that the inequality
\mathcal{E}_{\pi,P}(\log v_{t},v_{t})\geq 0
can be improved, as explained in the next subsection.

3.4
Generalization bounds under modified log-Sobolev inequalities

We first recall the notion of (classical) LSI (see
Bakry et al., 2014
;
Chafai and Lehec, 2017
for modern introductions). Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
, we associate to
\nu
the
entropy
functional,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu}\left(f\right)=\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu}^{\Phi}\left(f\right):=\mathds{E}_{\nu}\left[\Phi(f)\right]-\Phi(\mathds{E}_{\nu}\left[f\right]),\text{ with }\Phi(x)=x\log(x).

(12)

The entropy functional generalizes the KL divergence, in the sense that
\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu}\left(\text{d}\mu/\text{d}\nu\right)=\text{{{KL}}}\left(\mu||\nu\right)
as soon as
\mu\ll\nu
.
A probability measure
\nu
is said to satisfy the
\beta
-LSI if for all positive
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\nu)\cap\mathcal{C}^{1}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
we have the inequality,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu}\left(f\right)\leq\frac{1}{2\beta}\int\frac{\left\|\nabla f\right\|^{2}}{f}\text{d}\nu=\frac{1}{2\beta}\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{diff}}(\log f,f),\quad\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{diff}}:=\int\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle\text{d}\nu.

For example,
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
satisfies a
1/\sigma^{2}
-LSI
(
Gross, 1975
)
.
Such inequalities have been extensively studied for their links with the convergence of Markov processes
(
Bakry et al., 2014
)
. In learning theory, they have been used for the generalization analysis of noisy algorithms
(
Mou et al., 2017
)
and differential privacy
(
Chourasia et al., 2022
;
Dupuis et al., 2025
)
.
In the above inequality,
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{diff}}
is called the Dirichlet form associated with the Langevin process whose invariant distribution is
\nu
.
This reveals a more general structure and, in fact, variants of LSIs are naturally associated with more general Dirichlet forms
(
Bakry et al., 2014
)
.
In our study, we use the following notion of
modified
LSI.

Definition 11

(Modified LSI)
.

We say that
(\pi,P)
satisfies a modified
\gamma
-LSI if for any positive
f
such that
f\log f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\pi)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\pi,P}(\log(f),f)\geq\gamma\mathrm{Ent}_{\pi}\left(f\right).

(13)

To make this definition perfectly rigorous, we show in
Lemma

29
that
\mathcal{E}_{\pi,P}(\log(f),f)
always has meaning in
\mathds{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
under our assumption and that
\mathcal{E}_{\pi,P}(\log(f),f)\geq 0
.

Such inequalities were introduced by
Diaconis and Saloff-Coste (1996)
and extensively studied to analyze the convergence rate of Markov chains
(
Bobkov and Tetali, 2006
;
Bobkov and Ledoux, 1998
;
Goel, 2004
;
Wu, 2000
;
Ané and Ledoux, 2000
)
.
The term ”modified” LSI refers to the use of the term
\mathcal{E}(\log(f),f)
instead of the more classically used
\mathcal{E}(\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f})
and this terminology has been adopted by many of the aforementioned authors. Both definitions are equivalent in the Gaussian case, and in general, modified LSI is weaker than LSI.

In the next theorem, we exploit modified LSIs to reach a novel generalization bound.

Theorem 12

(Generalization bound under LSI)
.

In the setting of
Theorem

10
, assume that
(\pi,P)
satisfies a modified
\gamma
-LSI. Then, with probability at least
1-\zeta
, for all
T>0
,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[G_{S}(Y_{t}^{S})|S\right]\leq\frac{2\Sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\left\{\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\gamma(T-t)}\Delta_{P,P_{S}}(v_{t})\text{d}t+e^{-\gamma T}\text{{{KL}}}\left(\mu_{0}||\pi\right)+\log\left(\frac{3}{\zeta}\right)\right\}^{1/2}.

Proof.

The proof follows the same lines as the proof of
Theorem

10
until
Equation

11
(included). Then, by the modified logarithmic Sobolev inequality, we have that for all
t>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\text{{{KL}}}\left(\rho_{t}^{S}||\pi\right)\leq\Delta_{P,P_{S}}(v_{t})-\gamma\text{{{KL}}}\left(\rho_{t}^{S}||\pi\right).

By
Lemma

28
in the appendix and Grönwall’s lemma, we conclude that

\displaystyle\text{{{KL}}}\left(\rho_{T}^{S}||\pi\right)\leq e^{-\gamma T}\text{{{KL}}}\left(\mu_{0}||\pi\right)+\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\gamma(T-t)}\Delta_{P,P_{S}}(v_{t})\text{d}t.

The result follows.
∎

This shows that priors satisfying modified LSIs induce an exponential decay
e^{-\gamma(T-t)}
in the bound, improving over
Theorem

10
. This is analogous to the role of LSIs for Gaussian
(
Mou et al., 2017
)
or heavy-tailed
(
Dupuis and Simsekli, 2024
)
SDEs.

Theorem

12
reduces the problem of controlling time-uniformly
\mathds{E}\left[G_{S}(Y_{t}^{S})|S\right]
to upper-bounding
\Delta_{P,P_{S}}(v_{t})
.

To further characterize the modified LSI constant, we define below the entropy contraction coefficient, as suggested by
Raginsky (2014)
(and references therein).

Definition 13

(Contraction coefficient)
.

Let
(\pi,P)
be as in
Definition

3
.
We define
\mathscr{F}:=\left\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathds{R}^{d}}),~\text{{{KL}}}\left(\nu||\pi\right)<+\infty\right\}
. We define the entropy contraction coefficient as

\displaystyle\delta(\pi,P):=\inf_{\nu\in\mathscr{F}}\frac{\text{{{KL}}}\left(\nu||\pi\right)-\text{{{KL}}}\left(\nu P||\pi\right)}{\text{{{KL}}}\left(\nu||\pi\right)}.

By invariance of
\pi
under
P
and the data processing inequality, we have
\delta(\pi,P)\geq 0
.

There exist many coefficients to measure the contraction properties of Markov chains, such as Dobrushin’s coefficient
(
Dobrushin, 1956
)
. We invite the reader to consult
(
Caputo et al., 2024
)
for a recent exposition of these notions.
Many authors noted that, under structural assumptions on
P
,
\delta(\pi,P)
is related to the optimal modified LSI constant appearing in
Definition

11

(
Raginsky, 2014
;
Blanca et al., 2022
;
Del Moral et al., 2003
)
.
In our case, we will make use of the following lemma, for which we provide a rigorous proof in
Section

A.6
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
(\pi,P)
be as in
Definition

3
and assume that
\pi
is reversible for
P
.
Then
\mathcal{E}_{\pi,P}
satisfies the following modified LSI: for all non-negative
f
such that
f\log(f)\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\pi)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\pi,P}(\Phi^{\prime}(f),f)\geq\mathrm{Ent}_{\pi}\left(f\right)-\mathrm{Ent}_{\pi}\left(Pf\right)\geq\delta(\pi,P)\mathrm{Ent}_{\pi}\left(f\right).

The following example is an application of
Lemma

14
, showing that discrete Ornstein Uhlenbeck processes satisfy LSIs. See
Section

A.6
for a proof.

Example 15
.

Consider the discrete Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process defined by
X_{k+1}=(1-\gamma)X_{k}+\sigma\xi_{k}
, with
\sigma>0
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
, and
(\xi_{k})_{k\geq 0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})^{\otimes\infty}
. Then, the invariant distribution of this process is
\pi:=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\pi}^{2})
with
\sigma_{\pi}:=\sigma/\sqrt{1-(1-\gamma)^{2}}
. Then
\pi
is reversible, and we have
\delta(\pi,P)\geq 2\gamma-\gamma^{2}\geq\gamma
. Thus, by
Lemma

14
,
(\pi,P)
satisfies a modified LSI with constant
\gamma
.

It is clear from the proof in
Section

A.6
that this lemma can be generalized to obtain modified LSIs for Markov kernels associated with any diffusion process whose reversible measure satisfies a (classical) LSI. Hence, this
Lemma

14
may be seen as a transfer result between classical and modified LSIs.

4
Controlling the Discrepancy Between Markov Kernels

In
Section

3.4
, we have controlled the Dirichlet form that appears in
Theorem

6
through modified LSIs and proved that such inequalities were satisfied by diffusive priors. To apply our theory to practical algorithms, it remains to analyze the expansion term
\Delta_{P,P_{S}}(v_{t})
.
We present two methods to achieve this, depending on the structure of the algorithm. First, we consider noisy algorithms in
Section

4.1
and then turn to non-noisy algorithms in
Section

4.2
.

4.1
Generalization bounds for noisy algorithms

The generalization error of noisy iterative algorithms has been extensively studied
(
Haghifam et al., 2020
;
Xu and Raginsky, 2017
;
Negrea et al., 2019
;
Bu et al., 2020
)
.
A fundamental example is noisy SGD, for which we introduce some notation in the following.

Example 16

(Noisy SGD)
.

We define noisy SGD by the recursion

X_{k+1}^{S}=(1-\lambda\eta)X_{k}^{S}-\eta\widehat{g}_{S}(X_{k}^{S},U_{k})+\xi_{k},

with learning rate
\eta>0
, regularization coefficient
\lambda\geq 0
(potentially
0
), stochastic gradient
\widehat{g}_{S}(x,U_{k})
, and added noise
\xi_{k}
, where
(\xi_{k})_{k\geq 0}
are independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.).

(U_{k})_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}
represent the randomness of the batch indices and are i.i.d. and independent of
(\xi_{k})_{k\geq 0}
. We denote
\mu_{U}
the law of
U_{k}
. SGLD corresponds to
\xi_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
.

With our notation, we characterize a noisy algorithm by the following assumption.

Assumption 2

(Noisy algorithms)
.

For all
x\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}
and
S\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
, we have
\delta_{x}P_{S}\ll\delta_{x}P
.

Many of our applications use prior kernels such that
\delta_{x}P\ll\mathrm{Leb}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
. Therefore,
Assumption

2
describes algorithms that have densities with respect to the Lebesgue measure.
Thus, a typical example of noisy algorithm is
Example

16
with additional noise
(\xi_{k})
that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

The next proposition presents a generic upper bound on the expansion term for noisy algorithms. The proof can be found in
Section

B.1
.

Proposition 17
.

Assume that
Assumptions

1
and
2
hold. Then, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{P,P_{S}}(v_{t})\leq\int{\inf_{q>0}\left(\frac{1}{q}\text{{{KL}}}\left(\delta_{x}P_{S}||\delta_{x}P\right)+q\mathrm{I}(q,x,t)\right)}\text{d}\rho_{t}^{S}(x),

(14)

where, using the normalized entropy notation
\overline{\mathrm{Ent}}_{\nu}(f):=\nu(f)^{-1}\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu}(f)
, we define

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}(q,x,t):=\frac{1}{q}\int_{0}^{q}\overline{\mathrm{Ent}}_{\delta_{x}P}(v_{t}^{\epsilon})\frac{\text{d}\epsilon}{\epsilon^{2}}\underset{q\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Var}_{\delta_{x}P}(\log v_{t}).

(15)

Proposition

17
can be seen as a global-to-local bound, as it transfers the estimation of the “global” divergence
\text{{{KL}}}\left(\rho_{t}^{S}||\pi\right)
to the “local” divergence
\text{{{KL}}}\left(\delta_{x}P_{S}||\delta_{x}P\right)
, which in general has a simpler structure. This term quantifies the impact of an individual iteration on the generalization error.

Equation

14
contains a free parameter
q>0
. This is analogous to the use of Young’s inequality in entropy flow derivations along Langevin dynamics
(
Mou et al., 2017
, eq. (23))
or Lévy-driven SDEs
(
Dupuis and Simsekli, 2024
, Cor. 4.2)
.
Proposition

17
is a formal generalization of this proof technique for general Markov processes.

We see in
Equation

15
that the term
\mathrm{I}(q,x,t)
is of order
1
in
q
as
q\to 0^{+}
(if
\log(v_{t})\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\delta_{x}P)
). Therefore, the local KL divergence dominates the bound as
q\to 0^{+}
. In
Section

5
we show in specific examples how the term
\mathrm{I}(q,x,t)
can be explicitly bounded or even avoided.
In the next example, we express the local KL divergence for SGLD.

Example 18

(SGLD)
.

Consider SGLD as in
Example

16
, with
\lambda>0
. Let the prior Markov kernel be
P(x,\cdot)=\mathcal{N}((1-\lambda\eta)x,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
. Then, by the joint convexity of the KL divergence
(
van Erven and Harremoës, 2014
)
, we have, for
x\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}
,
\text{{{KL}}}\left(\delta_{x}P_{S}||\delta_{x}P\right)\leq\frac{\eta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|\widehat{g}_{S}(x,U)\|^{2}|S]
.

This example will be used in
Section

5
to obtain Poissonized generalization bounds for SGLD.
The following example gives an intuitive interpretation of the variance term appearing in
Equation

15
, in the case of Gibbs distributions.

Example 19

(Gibbs distributions)
.

Assume that
\text{d}\rho_{t}^{S}\propto e^{-V^{S}_{t}(x)}\text{d}x
and
\text{d}\pi\propto e^{-V}\text{d}x
, where
V^{S}_{t},V:{\mathds{R}^{d}}\to\mathds{R}
are Gibbs potentials. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\delta_{x}P}(\log v_{t})=\mathrm{Var}_{\delta_{x}P}\left(V^{S}_{t}-V\right).

Consider the SGLD example of
Example

16
with
\lambda>0
and choose
P(x,\cdot)=\mathcal{N}(\gamma x,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
with
\gamma=1-\lambda\eta
, then we expect the above quantity to be close to
\mathrm{Var}_{\delta_{x}P}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S})
as
t\to\infty

(
Raginsky et al., 2017
)
. In this case, we have
\mathrm{Var}_{\delta_{x}P}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S})=\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(\gamma x+\xi)-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(\gamma x+\xi)])^{2}]
with
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
.
This term is similar in nature to the “expected sharpness” appearing in
(
Neyshabur et al., 2017
, Eq. (8))
and the “local value sensitivity” used by
Neu et al. (2021)
. Therefore, we can interpret this variance term as a measure of the sharpness of the local minimum to which the algorithm has converged.

4.2
Analysis of non-noisy algorithms

Unfortunately, various popular procedures, starting with SGD, cannot satisfy
Assumption

2
.
In this subsection, we extend our framework beyond noisy algorithms.

To avoid the condition that
\delta_{x}P_{S}\ll\delta_{x}P
, we perform an expansion of
P_{S}
“around
P
”. This is made precise by the following proposition, which is inspired by
(
Polyanskiy and Wu, 2016
, Proposition
1
)
and
(
Raginsky et al., 2017
, Lemma 3.5)
.

Proposition 20
.

Assume that, for all
t\in[0,T]
, there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that
v_{t}
satisfies the linear growth condition:
\forall x\in{\mathds{R}^{d}},~\left\|\nabla\log v_{t}(x)\right\|\leq c_{1}\left\|x\right\|+c_{2}
, then we have.

\displaystyle\Delta_{P,P_{S}}(v_{t})\leq\mathds{E}_{Y\sim\rho_{t}^{S}}\left[\mathrm{W}_{2}(\delta_{Y}P,\delta_{Y}P_{S})^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{c_{1}}{2}\left\|P\right\|_{t}+\frac{c_{1}}{2}\left\|P_{S}\right\|_{t}+c_{2}\right),

with
\left\|P\right\|_{t}^{2}:=\mathds{E}_{X\sim\rho_{t}^{S}P}\left[\left\|X\right\|^{2}\right]
(resp.
P_{S}
) and
\mathrm{W}_{2}
the Wasserstein distance (
Section

B.2
).

The main feature of
Proposition

20
, proven in
Section

B.2
, is to relate the expansion term to the Wasserstein distance
\mathrm{W}_{2}(\delta_{Y}P_{S},\delta_{Y}P)
. Note that this term is local,
i.e.
, is computed for every point
Y\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}
and is expected over the posterior distribution. In the case of SGD, with a relevant choice of
P
, we usually have
\mathrm{W}_{2}(\delta_{Y}P,\delta_{Y}P_{S})^{2}\leq\eta^{2}\mathds{E}_{U}\left[\left\|\widehat{g}_{S}(Y,U)\right\|^{2}\right]
, with the notation of
Example

16
. Similar Wasserstein distances between Markov kernels have been extensively studied in the context of convergence and geometry of Markov chains
(
Rudolf and Schweizer, 2017
;
Ollivier, 2007
)
and have been used by
(
Zhu et al., 2023
)
to obtain stability-based bounds for SGD.
Therefore,
Proposition

20
connects our framework and the prior art through these Wasserstein terms.

Proposition

20
is based on a condition of linear growth of
\nabla\log v_{t}=\nabla\log u_{t}^{S}-\nabla\log\pi
.
This assumption can be seen as an assumption on the tail of the posterior density
u_{t}^{S}
and can hint towards good choices of prior,
i.e.
, with tails similar to the posterior density.
Finally, note that a related condition was used by
Li et al. (2020, Section A.4)
.

Applications

In this section, we apply the results of
Sections

3
and
4
to obtain generalization bounds in concrete examples. We start by showing in
Section

5.1
that our techniques recover known rates in the case of SGLD, and then show their ability to reach novel generalization bounds.

5.1
Sanity check: Stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics

In this subsection, we apply our framework to the SGLD algorithm, using the notations of
Example

16
. According to
Section

3.1
, we denote the Poissonized SGLD process as
Y_{t}^{S}:=X_{N_{t}}^{S}
. To apply our theory to SGLD, we rely on
Theorem

6
and bound the expansion term using the same proof technique as in the proof of
Proposition

17
. In the particular case of SGLD, we can exploit the specific structure of diffusion processes to simplify
Equation

14
even further. This leads to the following bound, whose proof is postponed to
Section

C.1
.

Theorem 21

(Poissonized SGLD)
.

Suppose that
\eta\lambda<1
and take
(P,\pi)
as in
Example

15
.
Let
\pi
be the invariant distribution of this process.
Assume that
Assumption

1
hold and the loss is
\Sigma^{2}
-subgaussian.
Then, with probability at least
1-\zeta
over
S\sim\mu_{z}^{\otimes n}
, for all
T>0
,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[G_{S}(Y_{t}^{S})|S\right]\leq\frac{2\Sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\frac{\eta^{2}(2-\lambda\eta)}{2\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\lambda\eta(T-t)}\mathds{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{g}_{S}(Y_{t}^{S},U)\right\|^{2}|S\right]\text{d}t+K_{T}+\log\frac{3}{\zeta}\right)^{1/2},

with
K_{T}:=e^{-\lambda\eta T}\text{{{KL}}}\left(\mu_{0}||\pi\right)
, for some
U\sim\mu_{U}
independent of
Y_{t}^{S}
.

Theorem

21
is proven in
Section

C.1
. We also prove in
Lemma

33
that
Assumption

1
is satisfied in the particular case where
\nabla\ell(\cdot,z)
is Lipschitz and that
\text{d}\mu_{0}/\text{d}\pi
is bounded away from zero.
Although Poissonization might not be the preferred framework to prove generalization bounds for SGLD, we observe that the orders of magnitude of the different terms in
Theorem

21
are the same as the results of
Mou et al. (2017)
obtained under similar assumptions (it should be noted that our result does not require a Lipschitz loss).
Indeed, since the Poisson process
(N_{t})_{t\geq 0}
has intensity
1
, the integral
\int_{0}^{K}
for
K\in{\mathds{N}}
should be intuitively thought of as the Poissonized counterpart of a sum
\sum_{k=1}^{K}
, where
K
is the number of iterations.
Therefore, our framework is general enough to recover Poissonized counterparts of classical results, hence unifying the proof techniques of several generalization bounds.

Remark 22

(Dissipativity assumption)
.

Our proof technique also applies to SGLD without regularization, at the cost of assuming the dissipativity of the loss, i.e.,
\langle w,\nabla\ell(w,z)\rangle\geq m\left\|w\right\|^{2}-b
, for some

and

. Such dissipativity assumptions are common in this literature
(
Futami and Fujisawa, 2023
;
Raginsky et al., 2017
;
Farghly and Rebeschini, 2021
)
.

5.2
SGD with perturbed final iterate

We now apply our framework to obtain novel generalization bounds for SGD. We consider the regularized SGD recursion with the notation of
Example

16
,
i.e.
,
X_{k+1}^{S}=(1-\lambda\eta)X_{k}^{S}-\eta\widehat{g}_{S}(X_{k}^{S},U_{k})
. We adopt a technique similar to
(
Neu et al., 2021
;
Neyshabur et al., 2017
)
to obtain information-theoretic bounds for SGD, consisting in perturbing the
last
SGD iterate with Gaussian noise. Hence, instead of observing
X_{k}^{S}
directly, we consider the random variables,
\overline{X}_{k}^{S}:=X_{k}^{S}+\sigma\xi_{k}
, with
(\xi_{k})_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})^{\otimes\infty}
independent of
(X_{k}^{S})_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}
and
\sigma>0
.
According to
Section

3.1
, we actually consider a Poissonized version of this stochastic process,

\displaystyle{\overline{Y}}_{t}^{S}:={\overline{X}}_{N_{t}}^{S}=Y_{t}^{S}+\sigma\xi_{N_{t}},

Our goal is to obtain a bound on
\mathds{E}\big[{G_{S}({\overline{Y}}_{T}^{S})|S}\big]
for all time horizon
T>0
, with high probability over
S\sim\mu_{z}^{\otimes n}
.
We show in
Lemma

34
that the operations of Poissonization and addition of Gaussian noise
(\xi_{k})_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}
commute,
i.e.
,
{\overline{Y}}_{t}^{S}=Y_{t}^{S}+\sigma\Xi
, with
\Xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
independent of
Y_{t}^{S}
.
Thus, the (Poissonized) generalization error is written as

\displaystyle G_{S}(Y_{t}^{S})=G_{S}({\overline{Y}}_{t}^{S})+\delta_{t}^{S},\quad\delta_{t}^{S}:=G_{S}({\overline{Y}}_{t}^{S})-G_{S}(Y_{t}^{S}),

where
\delta_{t}^{S}
is formally similar to the difference of
local value sensitivities
appearing in
(
Neu et al., 2021
, Theorem 1)
. It is also related to the
expected sharpness
of
Neyshabur et al. (2017)
(to be precise, this term is linked to
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}({\overline{Y}}_{t}^{S})-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(Y_{t}^{S})
).
We refer to these works for further analysis of these terms and focus our analysis on the perturbed Poissonized process.

The main difficulty arising is that the process
({\overline{X}}_{k}^{S})_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}
defined above is not Markovian in general. This means that the posterior density cannot satisfy the same Boltzmann equation as
Lemma

5
. Nevertheless, we adopt a proof technique that relies on noting that the posterior density actually satisfies a
perturbed
Boltzmann equation. This observation, which is explained in
Section

C.2
, allows us to exploit our framework to obtain a new generalization bound for SGD.
We make the following assumptions on the loss function.

Assumption 3
.

The stochastic gradient norms are bounded by a fixed constant
L>0
, i.e.,
\left\|\widehat{g}_{k}\right\|<L
.
The initialization
v_{0}:=\text{d}\mu_{0}/\text{d}\pi
has compact support included in
B(0,L/\lambda)
and there exists
r>0
such that
v_{0}
is bounded away from
0
on
B(0,r)
.

This assumption justifies the regularity conditions necessary for our entropy flow derivations.
The following theorem is a generalization bound for perturbed SGD.

Theorem 23

(Generalization bound for perturbed SGD)
.

Take
\pi:=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
.
Assume that
Assumption

3
holds, that the loss
\ell(w,z)
is
\Sigma^{2}
-subgaussian with respect to
z\sim\mu_{z}
, and that the initial distribution
\mu_{0}
has its support included in the ball
B(0,L/\lambda)
. Then, we have with probability at least
1-\zeta
over
S\sim\mu_{z}^{\otimes n}
that, for all
T>0
,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[G_{S}(\bar{Y}_{T}^{S})|S\right]\leq\frac{2\Sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\left\{\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\eta Le^{-\lambda\eta(T-t)}}{\lambda\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{\mathds{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{g}_{S}(Y_{t}^{S},U)\right\|^{2}\big|S\right]}\text{d}t+K_{T}+\log\frac{3}{\zeta}\right\}^{1/2},

with
K_{T}:=e^{-\lambda\eta T}\text{{{KL}}}\left(\mu_{0}||\pi\right)
and
U\sim\mu_{U}
is independent of
Y_{t}^{S}
.

Theorem

23
shows that the generalization of the perturbed last iterate of SGD is upper bounded by a weighted integral of the gradient norms encountered during training. Due to the presence of exponential decay,
e^{-\lambda\eta(T-t)}
, in the integral, this bound puts more weight on the stochastic gradient norms toward the end of training. This observation is consistent with the popular idea that generalization is improved when the algorithm converges to flat minima
(
Jastrzebski et al., 2018
;
Keskar et al., 2017
)
.

Theorem

23
should be compared to
(
Neu et al., 2021
, Theorem 1)
, which can be informally summarized with our notation as

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{S,U}\left[G_{S}({\overline{X}}_{K}^{S})\right]\lesssim\frac{\Sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\left\{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\eta^{2}\mathds{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{g}_{S}(X_{k}^{S})-\nabla\mathcal{R}(X_{k}^{S})\right\|^{2}\right]\right\}^{1/2},

where we omitted the sensitivity terms.
Our bound directly involves the gradient norm
\left\|\widehat{g}_{S}\right\|^{2}
instead of its distance from the population gradient. Although the latter might be smaller in some cases, it cannot be estimated from the training dataset.
Moreover, our framework naturally provides high probability bounds, while
Neu et al. (2021)
only prove expected bounds.
Most importantly,
Theorem

23
shows that, in the presence of regularization, the sum over all iterations can be replaced by an integral with an exponential decay term, which significantly improves the time dependence of the bound.
Note, however, that our theory only provides a generalization bound for the Poissonized iterates
{\overline{Y}}^{S}_{t}
, see
Section

3.1
.

5.3
SGD with noise injection for strongly convex losses

It was suggested by several authors that noise injection inside the evaluation of the stochastic gradient can lead to improved generalization
(
Orvieto et al., 2023a
;
Liu et al., 2021
;
Nesterov, 2005
)
. To the best of our knowledge, no explicit generalization bound has been derived for this algorithm. In this subsection, we show that our framework naturally leads to a generalization bound in this setting.
More precisely, we study the following regularized gradient descent algorithm with noise injection,

\displaystyle X_{k+1}^{S}=X_{k}^{S}-\eta\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(X_{k}^{S}+\sigma\xi_{k}),\quad(\xi_{k})_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})^{\otimes n},

where
\xi_{k}
is independent of
X_{k}^{S}
. As before, we denote the Poissonized process by
Y_{t}^{S}:=X_{N_{t}}^{S}
. Its probability distribution is denoted
\rho_{t}^{S}
and we write
{\overline{\rho}}_{t}^{S}:=\rho_{t}^{S}\ast\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
.

According to
Orvieto et al. (2023b)
, this recursion is implicitly regularized toward flat minima. Indeed, by Itô’s lemma, we see that if
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}
is twice differentiable, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(x+\sigma\xi_{k})|S\right]=\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\sigma^{2}}\mathds{E}\left[\Delta\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(x+\sqrt{u}\xi_{k})|S\right]\text{d}u\approx\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(x)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(x).

Therefore, the above recursion is analogous to using the trace of the Hessian matrix of
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}
as a regularizer, hence pushing the dynamics toward flatter regions, which are commonly associated with better generalization error
(
Jastrzebski et al., 2018
;
Keskar et al., 2017
)
.

Thanks to our framework, we easily obtain a generalization bound for this algorithm in the case of a strongly convex loss function.

Theorem 24
.

Assume that
\ell(\cdot,z)\in\mathcal{C}^{2}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
and is
\gamma
-strongly convex (with
\gamma\eta<1
) and gradient-Lipschitz for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
and that
\ell(w,z)
is
\Sigma^{2}
-subgaussian with respect to
z\sim\mu_{z}
. Then, we have with probability at least
1-\zeta
over
S\sim\mu_{z}^{\otimes n}
that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[G_{S}(Y_{t}^{S})|S\right]\leq\frac{2\Sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\left\{\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\eta\gamma(T-t)}\mathds{E}_{{\overline{\rho}}_{t}^{S}}\left[2\Delta\widehat{L}_{S}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left\|\nabla\widehat{L}_{S}\right\|^{2}\right]\text{d}t+K_{T}+\log\frac{3}{\zeta}\right\}^{1/2},

where
\widehat{L}_{S}(y)=\gamma^{-1}\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(y)-\left\|y\right\|^{2}/2
,
K_{T}=e^{-\gamma\eta T}\text{{{KL}}}\left(\mu_{0}||\pi\right)
, and
\pi=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\gamma\eta/(2-\gamma\eta)I_{d})
.

To our knowledge,
Theorem

24
is the first generalization bound for this algorithm, which confirms the regularization effect of noise injection.
The assumption that
\nabla\ell(\cdot,z)
is Lipschitz-continuous is only meant to ensure that
Assumption

1
holds in this case. In particular, the value of this smoothness constant does not appear in the final statement.
We note that our generalization bound is expressed in terms of two quantities, the gradient norms and the Laplacian of the empirical loss, expected over the perturbed Poissonized process
Y_{t}^{S}+\sigma\Xi
, with
\Xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
. Compared to the results of
Section

5.2
, this shows that noise injection connects the generalization error to the curvature of the loss landscape.

5.4
Toward generalization bounds for SGD under linear growth assumption

We consider the regularized SGD recursion as defined in
Section

5.2
(with the notation of
Example

16
),
X_{k+1}^{S}:=(1-\lambda\eta)X_{k}^{S}-\eta\widehat{g}_{S}(X_{k}^{S},U_{k})
.
We denote
\rho_{t}^{S}
the distribution of the Poissonized process
Y_{t}^{S}
and, when defined, its density with respect to the Lebesgue measure is denoted
u_{t}^{S}
.
The following result is a consequence of
Proposition

20
and
Theorem

6
.

Theorem 25
.

Assume that
\ell(w,z)
is
\Sigma^{2}
-subgaussian and that
Assumption

1
is satisfied with
\pi:=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
, for some
\sigma>0
. For simplicity, set
\mu_{0}=\pi
and assume that there exist constants
a,b>0
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
, we have
\left\|\nabla\log u_{t}^{S}(x)+x/\sigma^{2}\right\|\leq a\left\|x\right\|+b
.
Then, we have, with probability at least
1-\zeta
over
S\sim\mu_{z}^{\otimes n}
, that for all
T>0
,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[G_{S}(Y_{T}^{S})|S\right]\leq\frac{2\Sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\left\{\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\lambda\eta(T-t)}\mathrm{N}_{S}(t)\left(a\mathrm{N}_{S}(t)+a\mathrm{M}_{S}(t)+b\right)\text{d}t+\log\frac{3}{\zeta}\right\}^{1/2},

with
\mathrm{N}_{S}(t)^{2}:=\eta^{2}{\mathbb{E}[\|\widehat{g}_{S}(Y_{t}^{S},U)\|^{2}|S]}+\sigma^{2}{\lambda\eta d}
and
\mathrm{M}_{S}(t)^{2}:=2{\mathbb{E}[\|Y_{t}^{S}\|^{2}|S]}
.

This theorem illustrates the flexibility of our framework, which allows to obtain many generalization bounds for both noisy algorithms (
Sections

5.1
and
5.2
) and non-noisy algorithms such as SGD under specific assumptions, while relying on the same unified proof technique.
We see that
Theorem

25
relates the generalization error of SGD to the stochastic gradient norms, averaged over the posterior distribution. Similar quantities appear classically in the study of noisy SGD
(
Mou et al., 2017
;
Negrea et al., 2019
;
Haghifam et al., 2020
;
Dupuis and Simsekli, 2024
)
and were already involved for non-noisy SGD
(
Neu et al., 2021
)
. Compared to
(
Neu et al., 2021
)
, the main advantage of
Theorem

25
is the presence of the exponential decay term,
e^{-\lambda\eta(T-t)}
.
The assumption that
\left\|\nabla\log u_{t}^{S}(x)+x/\sigma^{2}\right\|\leq a\left\|x\right\|+b
is meant to ensure the linear growth condition of
Proposition

20
, which is the main ingredient in the proof of
Theorem

25
.
Intuitively, this assumption implies that the SGD iterations do not converge too fast to singular distributions, which we believe might be related to the edge of stability phenomenon

(
Cohen et al., 2021
)
.
This shows that the regularity of the distributions that are induced by the algorithm impact its generalization properties.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we introduced a new framework to extend the entropy flow method to general time-homogeneous Markov algorithms.
Drawing inspiration from the Poissonization operation, we derive an exact entropy flow formula for Poissonized Markov algorithms which we connected to modified logarithmic Sobolev inequalities, hence, relating the generalization error of general Markov algorithms to ergodic theory.
We provided generic tools to upper bound the expansion term that appears in our theory, both for noisy and non-noisy algorithms.
Thanks to this new framework, we easily recovered (Poissonized versions of) classical generalization bounds for SGLD and obtained new generalization bounds for several algorithms, such as SGD and variants of SGD with noise injection.

Future work.

Several directions remain to be explored with our new entropy flow method.
As mentioned in the introduction, the entropy flow is a popular method to analyze the differential privacy of Langevin Monte Carlo algorithms or differentially-private SGD with Gaussian noise.
Therefore, it would be an interesting direction for future work to apply our framework to obtain new differential privacy guarantees.
Moreover, modified LSIs are a popular tool to analyze the convergence properties of discrete Markov chains.
Thus, one potential direction for future research is to apply our work to understand the generalization error of Markov algorithms in discrete parameter spaces.

Before presenting the proofs of

4

We observe that
(P^{\star})^{k}=(P^{k})^{\star}
, which we will denote
P^{\star k}
, without ampiguity.
Note that the operator
P^{\star}
, which we will call the dual operator, is neither Markovian nor sub-Markovian in general, and it differs from the dual operators constructed by
Del Moral et al. (2003)
. In particular, it does not satisfy Jensen’s inequality. Nevertheless, the operator
P^{\star}
satisfies the following duality formula,

\displaystyle\int fPg\text{d}\nu=\int gP^{\star}f\text{d}\nu,

(16)

which is true as soon as
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\nu)
and
gP^{\star}f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\nu)
, by basic properties of Markov kernels.

A.1
Proof of
Lemma

5
and additional technical lemmas

Proof.

(of
Lemma

5
)

Let
v_{0}:=\text{d}\mu_{0}/\text{d}\pi
.
For any Borel set
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathds{R}^{d})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(X^{S}_{N_{t}}\in A\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\left(\bigcup_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}\left\{X_{k}^{S}\in A,N_{t}=k\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}e^{-t}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\mu P_{S}^{k}(A)\quad\text{(disjoint union and independence)}.

By definition of the dual operator and Tonelli’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(X^{S}_{N_{t}}\in A\right)=e^{-t}\sum_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\int_{A}P_{S}^{\star k}v_{0}\text{d}\pi=\int_{A}\bigg(e^{-t}\sum_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}P_{S}^{\star k}v_{0}\bigg)\text{d}\pi.

Therefore, we have, by definition of the Radon-Nikodym derivative,

\displaystyle\boxed{v_{t}(x):=\frac{\text{d}\rho_{t}^{S}}{\text{d}\pi}(x)=e^{-t}\sum_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}P_{S}^{\star k}v_{0}(x)=e^{-t}\sum_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\frac{\text{d}{\mathrm{Law}}(X_{k}^{S})}{\text{d}\pi}(x).}

(17)

By the same reasoning and using
Equation

16
, we show that
P_{S}^{\star}v_{t}(x)=e^{-t}\sum_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}P_{S}^{\star(k+1)}v_{0}(x).

Let
b>a>0
and
t\in[a,b]
, for
k\in{\mathds{N}}^{\star}
we have
|\partial_{t}(t^{k}P_{S}^{\star k}v_{0}/k!)|\leq b^{k-1}P_{S}^{\star k}v_{0}/(k-1)!
. Therefore, as
P_{S}^{\star}kv_{b}\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\pi)
, we can differentiate under the sum to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v_{t}}{\partial t}=-v_{t}+e^{-t}\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{t^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}P_{S}^{\star k}v_{0}(x)=-v_{t}+P_{S}^{\star}v_{t}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Poissonized semigroup

A formally similar computation provides the expression of the Poissonized Markov semigroup
(P_{S})_{t}
of
Y_{t}^{S}
, for any bounded Borel-measurable function

\displaystyle(P_{S})_{t}f(x):=\mathds{E}\left[f(Y_{t}^{S})|Y_{0}^{S}=x\right]=e^{-t}\sum_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}P_{S}^{k}(x).

A.4
Proof of the entropy flow formula

Recall that
\Phi(x):=x\log(x)

Proof.

(of
Theorem

6
)

Let

and

.
Note that
\Phi
is lower
bounded and

\displaystyle v_{t}=e^{-t}\sum_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}P_{S}^{\star k}v_{0}\leq e^{-a}v_{0}+e^{b-a}e^{-b}\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{b^{k}}{k!}P_{S}^{\star k}v_{0}\leq e^{-a}v_{0}+be^{b}P_{S}^{\star}v_{b}.

\displaystyle v_{t}(x)\Phi^{\prime}(e^{-b}v_{0}(y))\leq v_{t}(x)\Phi^{\prime}(v_{t}(y))\leq\Phi(v_{t}(x))+v_{t}(y),

,
where
\rho_{t}^{S}:={\mathrm{Law}}(Y_{t}^{S})
and
Qf(x):=\mathds{E}\left[f(x+\sigma\Xi)\right]
for
\Xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
is the heat kernel with variance
\sigma^{2}
.
Indeed, by
Lemma

34
, we have
{\overline{\rho}}_{t}^{S}=\rho_{t}^{S}\ast\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
, then the result follows from the self-adjointness of
Q
.

In particular, we have that
{\overline{\rho}}_{t}^{S}=\rho_{t}^{S}Q
.
Now it is clear that
Q
satisfies the continuity presrvation condition with respect to
\pi
, therefore, we can define its adjoint
Q^{\star}
with respect to
\pi
in
Equation

4
.
By definition, we have

\displaystyle{\overline{v}}_{t}(x)=Q^{\star}v_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi(x)}\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\pi(x-y)v_{t}(y)\pi(y)\text{d}y.

This leads to the following computation.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\overline{v}}_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi(x)}\partial_{t}\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\pi(x-y)v_{t}(y)\pi(y)\text{d}y.

We note that, for
t\in[a,b]
with
a<b
, we have
|\partial_{t}v_{t}|=|(P_{S}^{\star}-I)v_{t}|\leq e^{b-a}\left(P_{S}^{\star}v_{b}+v_{b}\right)\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\pi)
.
As
\pi
is bounded, we can differentiate under the integral to get that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\overline{v}}_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi(x)}\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\pi(x-y)(P_{S}^{\star}-I)v_{t}(y)\pi(y)\text{d}y.

Therefore, we obtain the perturbed Boltzmann equation for
{\overline{v}}_{t}
,
\partial{\overline{v}}_{t}=(Q^{\star}P_{S}^{\star}-Q^{\star})v_{t}
.
∎

This leads to the following perturbed entropy flow formula.

Corollary 37

(Perturbed entropy flow)
.

Assume that
Assumption

3
holds and that
Q^{\star}P_{S}^{\star}
satisfies condition
(H.2)
in
Assumption

1
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\text{{{KL}}}\left({\overline{\rho}}_{t}^{S}||\pi\right)=\int(P_{S}Q-P)\log({\overline{v}}_{t})v_{t}\text{d}\pi-\mathcal{E}_{\pi,{\overline{P}}}(\log({\overline{v}}_{t}),{\overline{v}}_{t}).

Proof.

As in the proof of
Theorem

6
, we first verify that we can differentiate under the integral. Under our assumptions, by
Lemma

39
,
v_{t}
has compact support included in
\overline{B}(0,L/\lambda)
. This observation extends to
P_{S}^{\star}v_{t}
, as its support is that of the distribution
\rho_{t}^{S}P_{S}
.

Let
a<b
and
t\in[a,b]
. Observing that
{\overline{v}}_{t}=Q^{\star}v_{t}
(by
Lemma

34
) and
{\overline{v}}_{t}\geq e^{-b}{\overline{v}}_{0}
, we apply
Lemma

38
below to show a bound of the form
|\log({\overline{v}}_{t})(Q^{\star}P_{S}^{\star}-Q^{\star})v_{t}|(x)\lesssim\mathrm{Pol}(\left\|x\right\|)e^{C\left\|x\right\|}
,
with
C>0
and
\mathrm{Pol}
constant and polynomial depending only on
(a,b,d,\sigma,\lambda,\eta,L)
. This ensures the uniform integrability of the above quantity with respect to
\pi
on
[a,b]
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\text{{{KL}}}\left({\overline{\rho}}_{t}^{S}||\pi\right)

\displaystyle=\int\log({\overline{v}}_{t})(Q^{\star}P_{S}^{\star}-Q^{\star})v_{t}\text{d}\pi

\displaystyle=\int(P_{S}Q-P)\log({\overline{v}}_{t})v_{t}\text{d}\pi-\int{\overline{v}}_{t}\log({\overline{v}}_{t})\text{d}\pi+\int v_{t}Q{\overline{P}}\log({\overline{v}}_{t})\text{d}\pi

\displaystyle=\int(P_{S}Q-P)\log({\overline{v}}_{t})v_{t}\text{d}\pi-\mathcal{E}_{\pi,{\overline{P}}}(\log({\overline{v}}_{t}),{\overline{v}}_{t}).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 38
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

23
, let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
be a Borel probability measure with compact support
K\subset{\mathds{R}^{d}}
. Let
Q(dx|y):=\mathcal{N}(y,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
and
\pi:=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
. Then, there exist
a_{1},a_{2}>0
, depending only on
(K,\sigma,d)
such that
\text{d}(\mu Q)/\text{d}\pi(x)\leq a_{1}e^{a_{2}\left\|x\right\|}.

If, moreover,
\mu\ll\pi
and there exist
\mathcal{O}:=B(0,r)\subset K
with
r>0
, and
\inf_{\mathcal{O}}(\text{d}\mu/\text{d}\pi)>0
, then there exist
b_{1},b_{2}>0
, depending only on
(r,\sigma,d,\mu)
such that
{\overline{v}}(x)\geq b_{1}e^{-b_{2}\left\|x\right\|}
.

Proof.

First part:
Let
{\overline{v}}:=\text{d}(\mu Q)/\text{d}\pi
. For
x\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}
, we have

\displaystyle{\overline{v}}(x)=\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\frac{\pi(x-y)}{\pi(x)}\text{d}\mu(y)=\int_{K}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\left\|y\right\|^{2}-2\langle x,y\rangle\right)\right\}\text{d}\mu(y).

Let
R>0
such that
K\subset B(0,R)
, then we have
{\overline{v}}(x)\leq e^{R\left\|x\right\|/\sigma^{2}}
.

Second part:
Let
x\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}
and
v:=\text{d}\mu/\text{d}\pi
, by a similar computation, we have

\displaystyle{\overline{v}}(x)

\displaystyle\geq(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-d/2}\inf_{\mathcal{O}}(v)\int_{\mathcal{O}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\left\|y\right\|^{2}-\langle x,y\rangle\right)\right\}\text{d}y\geq Ce^{-r\left\|x\right\|^{2}/\sigma^{2}},

where
C>0
depends only on
(d,\sigma,r)
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We now show that, under the assumptions of
Theorem

23
,
v_{t}^{S}
has compact support.

Lemma 39
.

Consider the SGD recursion as above and assume that
Assumption

3
hold. Further assume that
\left\|X_{0}^{S}\right\|\leq L/\lambda
.
Then, for all
S\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
and all
k\in{\mathds{N}}
, we have almost surely (on the noise of the stochastic gradient) that
\left\|X_{k}^{S}\right\|\leq L/\lambda
.

Proof.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz and Young inequalities, we have

\displaystyle\left\|X_{k+1}^{S}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda\eta)^{2}\left\|X_{k}^{S}\right\|^{2}+\eta^{2}\left\|\widehat{g}_{S}(X_{k}^{S},U_{k})\right\|^{2}-2\eta(1-\lambda\eta)\langle X_{k}^{S},\widehat{g}_{S}(X_{k}^{S},U_{k})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left((1-\lambda\eta)^{2}+\eta\lambda(1-\eta\lambda)\right)\left\|X_{k}^{S}\right\|^{2}+\big((\eta^{2}+\frac{\eta(1-\eta\lambda)}{\lambda}\big)\left\|\widehat{g}_{S}(X_{k}^{S},U_{k})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda\eta)\left\|X_{k}^{S}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\eta L^{2}}{\lambda}.

As we have
\left\|X_{0}^{S}\right\|^{2}\leq L^{2}/\lambda^{2}
, we immediately deduce the result by recursion.
∎

C.3
Proof of
Theorem

Proof.

(of
Theorem

)

Let
S\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
.
By
Corollary

37
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathrm{Ent}_{\pi}\left({\overline{v}}_{t}\right)=\Delta_{t}-\mathcal{E}_{\pi,{\overline{P}}}(\log({\overline{v}}_{t}),{\overline{v}}_{t}),\quad\Delta_{t}:=\int(P_{S}Q-P)\log({\overline{v}}_{t})v_{t}\text{d}\pi.

For the second term, we note that
\pi
has been constructed as the reversible measure of the operator
{\overline{P}}
. Therefore, we can use the modified log-Sobolev inequality for
\mathcal{E}_{\pi,{\overline{P}}}
, which gives
\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathrm{Ent}_{\pi}\left({\overline{v}}_{t}\right)\leq\Delta_{t}-\lambda\eta\mathrm{Ent}_{\pi}\left({\overline{v}}_{t}\right)
.
For the term

, we use the proof of
Proposition

17
to get

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}\leq\int{\inf_{q>0}\left(\frac{1}{q}\text{{{KL}}}\left(\delta_{x}(P_{S}Q)||\delta_{x}P\right)+\int_{0}^{q}\overline{\mathrm{Ent}}_{\delta_{x}P}({\overline{v}}_{t}^{\epsilon})\frac{\text{d}\epsilon}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)}\text{d}\rho_{t}^{S}(x).

(21)

Observe that
P_{S}Q
is the Markov operator of
X_{k+1}^{S}=(1-\lambda\eta)X_{k}^{S}-\eta\widehat{g}_{S}(X_{k},U_{k})+\sigma\xi_{k}
.
with the notations of
Example

.
By the joint convexity of the KL divergence, we obtain

\displaystyle\text{{{KL}}}\left(\delta_{x}(P_{S}Q)||\delta_{x}P\right)\leq\frac{\eta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\left\|\widehat{g}_{S}(x,U)\right\|^{2}|S],

where

is the internal randomness of
\widehat{g}_{S}
and is independent of

.
For the integral term, we use the (classical) logarithmic Sobolev inequality satisfied by
\delta_{x}P=\mathcal{N}((1-\lambda\eta)x,\sigma^{2}I_{d})

(note that, by convolution,
{\overline{v}}_{t}
is continuously differentiable). This gives

\displaystyle\overline{\mathrm{Ent}}_{P}({\overline{v}}_{t}^{\epsilon})\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2P({\overline{v}}_{t}^{\epsilon})}P\big({\left\|{\overline{v}}_{t}^{\epsilon}\right\|^{2}}/{{\overline{v}}_{t}^{\epsilon}}\big)=\frac{\sigma^{2}\epsilon^{2}}{2P({\overline{v}}_{t}^{\epsilon})}P\left({\overline{v}}_{t}^{\epsilon}\left\|\nabla\log{\overline{v}}_{t}\right\|^{2}\right),\quad\epsilon>0.

It remains to estimate the score of the relative density
{\overline{v}}_{t}
, defined for all
y\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}
by

\displaystyle\nabla\log{\overline{v}}_{t}(y)=\nabla\log\frac{\text{d}{\overline{\rho}}_{t}^{S}}{\text{d}x}(y)-\nabla\log\pi(y)=\nabla\log\frac{\text{d}{\overline{\rho}}_{t}^{S}}{\text{d}x}(y)+\frac{y}{\sigma^{2}}.

Recall that
{\overline{\rho}}_{t}^{S}

(

)
, we have that, for all

\displaystyle\nabla\log\frac{\text{d}{\overline{\rho}}_{t}^{S}}{\text{d}x}(y)=\nabla\log p_{{\overline{Y}}_{t}^{S}}(y)=\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log p_{{\overline{Y}}_{t}^{S}|Y_{t}^{S}}(y|Y_{t}^{S})\big|{\overline{Y}}_{t}^{S}=y].

The conditional density of
{\overline{Y}}_{t}^{S}
given

is given by
Lemma

34
. Thus,

\displaystyle\nabla\log{\overline{v}}_{t}(y)=-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}[{y-Y_{t}^{S}}\big|{\overline{Y}}_{t}^{S}=y]+\frac{y}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}^{S}\big|{\overline{Y}}_{t}^{S}=y].

By

39
below, we have that, almost surely,

for all

. We deduce that
\|Y_{t}^{S}\|\leq L/\lambda
. Therefore, by Jensen’s inequality, we have

.
Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{q}\overline{\mathrm{Ent}}_{\delta_{x}P}({\overline{v}}_{t}^{\epsilon})\frac{\text{d}\epsilon}{\epsilon^{2}}\leq\frac{qL^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}}.

We conclude by optimizing over

and applying Grönwall’s lemma like in
Theorem

12
.
∎
