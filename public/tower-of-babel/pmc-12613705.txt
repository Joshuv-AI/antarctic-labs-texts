Preoperative prediction and localization of synchronous metastases in colon cancer: the role of CECT combined with clinical characteristics
Authors: Yinfei Fan, Ruidan Yang, Yue Shu, Wenjun Xie, Xiaopeng He, Jiong Liu, Jian Shu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to assess the utility of preoperative contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) and clinical characteristics in predicting the presence and location of synchronous metastases in colon cancer (CC) patients.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on pathologically confirmed CC patients who underwent CECT within two weeks prior to radical resection and lymphadenectomy. Synchronous metastases were confirmed via biopsy or characteristic imaging findings. Two independent radiologists reviewed CECT features, including enhancement patterns, tumor characteristics, and lymph node status. Both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were employed to evaluate the distribution of CECT features.ResultsThe final cohort included 212 patients (95 males and 117 females, with a mean age of 64 years). Distant metastases were categorized as synchronous liver metastasis (65 patients), synchronous peritoneal metastasis (19 patients), and metastases to other sites (22 patients). Elevated carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA19-9) levels, increased tumor length, higher tumor length-to-thickness ratio, presence of tumor necrosis, lymph node necrosis, and arterial-venous phase CT value differences emerged as independent predictors of synchronous metastases in CC. A predictive model, incorporating these parameters, exhibited strong predictive capability (the area under the curve (AUC) = 0.852, sensitivity: 0.802; specificity: 0.736, accuracy: 0.769). Additionally, tumor necrosis and ascites were significantly associated with synchronous peritoneal metastases, with the model demonstrating high predictive efficacy (AUC = 0.869, sensitivity: 0.895; specificity: 0.782, accuracy:0.802).ConclusionsIntegrating clinical characteristics with CECT imaging features provides an effective means of predicting synchronous distant metastases and their locations in CC patients, aiding in the development of personalized treatment plans that may enhance patient survival and quality of life.Keywords: Colon cancer, Peritoneal metastasis, Synchronous neoplasms, Computed tomography, Clinical laboratory diagnoses

Introduction

Colon cancer (CC) is one of the most prevalent and lethal cancers globally, driven by the gradual accumulation of genetic alterations in tumor suppressor genes or oncogenes, resulting in tumor progression and metastasis [1, 2]. CC accounts for approximately 7% of all newly diagnosed cancers and 11% of cancer-related deaths [2]. By 2035, the global incidence of CC is projected to rise to 2.5 million new cases [3]. Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, metastatic CC remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality [4], as metastases are often fatal due to mass effects and disruption of homeostasis [5, 6]. Approximately 20%−25% of CC patients present with synchronous metastases at diagnosis, involving organs such as the liver, peritoneum, lungs, bones, and brain [7, 8]. These metastases serve as independent prognostic factors for poor survival in patients who have not undergone chemotherapy [9]. Surgical resection of the primary and metastatic sites remains a key therapeutic strategy, though only about 25% of patients with liver metastases are eligible for surgery [9, 10]. For synchronous lung metastases, surgical intervention still shows better outcomes compared to chemotherapy alone, with 5-year survival rates ranging from 35%−70%, and 10-year survival rates between 20%−30%, compared to around 20% for those treated solely with drugs [11]. Patients with peritoneal metastases have a significantly worse prognosis than those with other metastatic sites [12]. Therefore, early detection and effective management of synchronous metastases are critical for improving CC patient outcomes.

Imaging studies, including contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography CT/MRI (PET-CT/MRI), are fundamental for assessing colon cancer (CC) and its distant metastases. MRI excels in soft tissue contrast, crucial for assessing tumor invasion and local lymph node involvement, particularly in rectal cancer, but requires longer examination times and greater patient cooperation. PET-CT/MRI integrates metabolic and anatomical imaging, useful for detecting systemic metastases and recurrences, though it is costly, involves high radiation, and may yield false positives. CECT, with its high spatial resolution, provides detailed assessments of local tumor extension, lymph node status, and distant metastases, and is recommended by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines as the preferred preoperative imaging modality for CC [13]. However, accurately determining distant metastasis preoperatively and identifying occult metastases remains challenging. Despite advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) and the integration of big data, the complexity and limited interpretability of most models hinder their routine clinical application [14–16]. Additionally, clinical characteristics such as age, gender, body mass index (BMI), and laboratory markers like CEA and CA19-9 are crucial for predicting CC metastasis risk, providing significant insights for personalized treatment and clinical decision-making [17].

In summary, this study investigates the non-invasive assessment of synchronous metastases and their locations in colon cancer patients by analyzing preoperative CECT imaging features of the primary tumor alongside clinical parameters. The goal is to enhance clinical decision-making and facilitate timely interventions, ultimately improving patient survival and quality of life.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective single-centre study was approved by the ethics committee of our hospital (Approval No.KY2024052), which waived the need for written informed consent. All procedures were conducted in strict adherence to established guidelines and regulations. We retrospectively identified patients (≥ 18 years old) with histopathologically confirmed colon cancer who underwent surgery between September 2018 and October 2023 (N = 907). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Underwent CECT within two weeks prior to radical resection and lymphadenectomy; (2) Diagnosed with either synchronous distant metastasis or no synchronous metastasis, as confirmed by biopsy or characteristic findings on CECT imaging. For hepatic lesions with indeterminate characteristics on CECT, further evaluation using MRI was performed when clinically indicated, consistent with standard diagnostic protocols. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Received any form of preoperative treatment, including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or chemoradiotherapy; (2) Diagnosed with metastatic cancer or malignancies originating from other anatomical sites; (3) Had incomplete medical records or insufficient diagnostic data; (4) Had suboptimal image quality (such as the presence of motion artifacts). A total of 106 patients with synchronous distant metastases were identified as the synchronous metastasis group. To maintain comparability, 106 patients without synchronous metastasis were randomly selected from 637 non-synchronous metastasis cases. Figure 1 outlines the patient selection workflow.

Flow diagram of patient selection

Comprehensive clinical data were retrieved from the electronic medical records, encompassing variables such as age, gender, BMI, tumor location, CA19-9, and CEA levels.

Image acquisition

Two 128-slice CT scanner (Philips Brilliance iCT 256 and Philips IQon Spectral CT, Royal Dutch Philips Electronics Ltd) was utilized in this study. The CT protocol included both non-contrast and contrast-enhanced imaging (arterial and portal venous phases) to provide a comprehensive assessment for colon cancer staging. This multi-phase approach allows for optimal characterization of the primary tumor's vascularity, detailed evaluation of regional lymph nodes, and highly sensitive detection of potential distant metastases. The scanning parameters were as follows: tube current of 129 mAs, tube voltage of 120 kV, collimation of 128.0 × 0.625 mm, increment of 0.5–3 mm, and slice thickness ranging from 1 to 5 mm. The contrast agent used was VISIPAQUE (320 mg/ml, Jiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd.), administered at a flow rate of 3.0 ml/s, with a total dosage of 75–150 ml. Arterial and venous phase images were acquired at 25–35 s and 55–75 s post-injection, respectively. The scans were performed from the diaphragm's apex to the distal rectum. Initially, non-contrast CT scans were conducted to obtain baseline images, followed by contrast administration to assess the contrast medium concentration within the abdominal aorta. Subsequent arterial and venous phase scans were initiated once the predetermined threshold was reached. The acquired images were then archived in the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS).

Image analysis

The CECT images were independently evaluated by two radiologists, one with over ten years of experience in abdominal imaging diagnosis and the other with five years of experience. Both radiologists adjusted the window width and level to 350 and 50, respectively, and performed a comparative analysis of the non-contrast and CECT images of patients with colon cancer. The radiologists were blinded to the clinicopathological data. Multiplanar reformation (MPR) images were utilized to assess and quantify imaging features. The assessment included the primary tumor and lymph node characteristics on CECT, such as the location of the primary lesion, tumor morphology (including tumor length, maximum thickness, and length-to-thickness ratio), enhancement pattern, tumor margins, presence of tumor necrosis, and any associated complications (such as intestinal obstruction or perforation). Additionally, arterial and venous phase CT values (mean, minimum, maximum, and standard deviation) of the solid tumor components were recorded(Fig. 2a-c). The evaluation also included counting suspicious lymph nodes, defined as those with a short-axis diameter greater than 0.5 cm, in the peritumoral and retroperitoneal regions, determining the total number of suspicious lymph nodes, assessing the enhancement pattern of the largest lymph node, and noting the presence of necrosis and irregular morphology in suspicious lymph nodes (Fig. 2d). Finally, the arterial and venous phase CT values (mean) of the largest lymph node and the difference in mean CT values between the arterial and venous phases were analyzed.

Statistical analysis

To assess the reproducibility of the imaging features, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated. The ICC values were interpreted as follows: an ICC of less than 0.40 was considered to indicate poor consistency, an ICC between 0.40 and 0.75 indicated moderate consistency, and an ICC greater than 0.75 indicated good consistency. Features with an ICC below 0.75 were excluded from further analysis.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.2.2, in conjunction with MSTATA software https://www.mstata.com/. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages, and their relationships were assessed using Pearson's chi-square test, continuity-corrected chi-square test, or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. For continuous variables, those with a normal distribution and equal variance were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the t-test; otherwise, they were reported as median (range or interquartile range [IQR]) and compared using non-parametric tests, such as the Mann–Whitney U test. Binary logistic regression was employed to develop two predictive models aimed at identifying patients with synchronous metastasis and peritoneal metastasis in CC. In constructing the predictive model for synchronous metastasis in CC, all features were initially subjected to univariate analysis to identify differences, and those with a p-value < 0.05 were selected for further analysis. The final models were developed and validated using multivariate logistic regression, with feature selection based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC) through a backward stepwise method, optimizing for the combination of features with the lowest AIC. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess the predictive performance of clinical parameters and CECT imaging features in identifying synchronous metastasis and specific metastatic sites in CC.

Results

Patient demographics

We collected pathological data from 212 consecutive patients between September 2018 and October 2023, all of whom met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The cohort consisted of 95 males and 117 females, with a mean age of 64 years (range: 56–71 years). The primary tumor was located in the right colon in 121 patients (57%) and in the left colon in 91 patients (43%). The average tumor length was 5.0 cm (range: 3.9–6.1 cm). Distant metastases were classified as synchronous liver metastasis (65 patients, 61%), synchronous peritoneal metastasis (19 patients, 18%), and metastasis to other sites (22 patients, 21%). Interobserver agreement

The interobserver consistency was assessed by reviewing the images again two weeks later. With the exception of moderate interobserver consistency in the tumor arterial phase CT values (mean, maximum, minimum, and standard deviation), the tumor venous phase CT standard deviation, and the tumor arterial-venous phase CT mean difference, all other qualitative features and quantitative imaging variables demonstrated good intraobserver and interobserver consistency (Fig. 3).

Comparison of measurement consistency between two observers

Clinical information

In laboratory tests, the synchronous metastasis group exhibited significantly higher levels of CEA (25 [5, 67], p < 0.001) and CA19-9 (30 [11, 81], p < 0.001) compared to the non-synchronous metastasis group. Additionally, the incidence of intestinal obstruction was significantly higher in the synchronous metastasis group (82.1%, p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in other clinical parameters between the synchronous and non-synchronous metastasis groups (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

CECT imaging features

Regarding tumor CECT imaging morphology, the synchronous metastasis group exhibited significantly larger tumor lengths (5.29 [4.46, 6.69], P = 0.004) and a greater length-to-Thickness ratio (3.32 [2.86, 4.32], P = 0.006) compared to the non-synchronous metastasis group. In enhanced scans, tumor necrosis was more frequently observed in the synchronous metastasis group (30.2%, P = 0.015). With respect to lymph nodes, statistically significant differences were noted between the synchronous and non-synchronous metastasis groups in terms of maximum lymph node short-axis diameter (P = 0.014), short-to-long diameter (SD/LD) ratio (P = 0.018), maximum lymph node venous phase CT mean value (P = 0.030), arterial-venous phase CT mean difference (P = 0.011), and the presence of lymph node necrosis (P < 0.001) (Table 1). No significant differences were observed in other imaging characteristics between the two groups.

Multivariate logistic regression analyses

In the multivariate logistic regression (MLR) analysis, synchronous metastasis was significantly associated with elevated CEA levels (Odds Ratio[OR]: 1.016; 95% Confidence Interval[CI]: 1.007–1.026; P = 0.001), higher CA19-9 levels (OR: 1.013; 95% CI: 1.004–1.024; P = 0.015), a greater length-to-maximum thickness ratio (OR: 1.556; 95% CI: 1.140–2.201; P = 0.022), the presence of intestinal obstruction (Negative vs. Positive; OR: 6.159; 95% CI: 2.929–13.829; P < 0.001), necrosis within lymph nodes (Negative vs. Positive; OR: 2.419; 95% CI: 1.118–5.384; P = 0.027), and the arterial-venous phase CT mean difference for the largest lymph node (OR: 0.972; 95% CI: 0.947–0.997; P = 0.029). A predictive model based on the MLR coefficients achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.852 (95% CI: 0.801–0.903), sensitivity: 0.802; specificity: 0.736, accuracy: 0.769 (Fig. 4A). The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit (GOF) test confirmed that the logistic regression model fits the data well, with a P-value of 0.176, indicating no significant deviation from the observed data. Discussion

This study aims to integrate clinical characteristics with CECT imaging features for accurate preoperative identification of synchronous metastases and their locations in colon cancer patients. Colon cancer, one of the most common gastrointestinal malignancies, presents synchronous distant metastases in 20–25% of patients at diagnosis [7]. These metastases, spread via hematogenous, lymphatic, direct invasion, and implantation pathways [18], are associated with poorer prognoses and shorter survival times compared to localized disease. The location of metastases is a critical factor influencing prognosis. For resectable metastatic lesions, simultaneous or staged surgical intervention offers potential curative options. Thus, precise preoperative identification of synchronous metastases is essential for optimizing treatment strategies and improving patient outcomes, as illustrated in Fig. 5.

Sixty-one-year-old man with sigmoid colon cancer. The portal venous phase reveals a 7.6 cm mass with heterogeneous enhancement and intralesional necrosis, accompanied by the presence of ascites(arrow). Postoperative pathology confirmed sigmoid colon cancer with peritoneal metastasis

The definition of synchronous and metachronous metastases in colon cancer remains debated, with the timing of primary tumor diagnosis or surgery often used as a reliable cutoff [11, 19]. Serum tumor markers, CEA and CA19-9, are widely used for auxiliary diagnosis and monitoring disease progression. This study identified CEA and CA19-9 as independent risk factors for synchronous metastases, showing strong correlations with metastatic potential. Elevated CEA levels, as demonstrated by Prager et al., promote angiogenesis by enhancing endothelial cell adhesion, proliferation, and migration [20]. Similarly, CA19-9-positive phenotypes exhibit strong metastatic potential, as shown by Nakayama et al. [21, 22]. These findings emphasize the need for vigilance in patients with elevated CEA and CA19-9 levels.

Imaging parameters, including tumor length and the length-to-thickness ratio, were significantly correlated with tumor invasiveness and metastatic risk. Prior studies using multi-detector CT found that larger tumor dimensions are associated with regional lymph node metastases [23]. Additionally, intestinal obstruction was linked to synchronous metastases, reflecting the complex biology of advanced tumors. Mohd Azri Mohd Suan et al. reported that intestinal obstruction, often indicative of late-stage disease, is associated with poor nutritional status and reduced survival [24].

While tumor necrosis correlated with metastases in univariate analysis, it lacked statistical significance in multivariate models. Lymph node necrosis and differences in CT values between arterial and venous phases suggested higher malignancy potential. Previous studies have shown that changes in CT values predict tumor aggressiveness and metastatic risk [25]. Peritoneal metastases, often accompanied by ascites, indicate advanced disease and poor prognosis. Tumor necrosis and ascites signify rapid growth, hypoxia, and immune evasion, which drive tumor invasiveness and metastasis [26–29].

Our predictive model integrates CECT imaging and clinical parameters to provide actionable insights for personalized patient management. High-risk indicators (e.g., elevated CEA/CA19-9, high tumor length/thickness, bowel obstruction, necrotic lymph nodes on CECT) for overall synchronous metastasis guide enhanced preoperative staging (MRI, PET-CT) and MDT treatment sequencing. Low-risk predictions streamline workflows. Specifically, for peritoneal metastasis, CECT findings of tumor necrosis and ascites prompt diagnostic laparoscopy before definitive surgery to confirm extent (surgical Peritoneal Cancer Index).

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective single-center study, selection bias and a small sample size are unavoidable. Prospective, multi-center studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate these findings. Second, the analysis focused solely on peritoneal metastases among synchronous metastasis sites, and future research should explore other types of synchronous metastases in colon cancer. Third, while our study provides a predictive model for peritoneal metastasis, it did not systematically assess radiological or surgical Peritoneal Cancer Index, nor did it include routine exploratory laparoscopy. Future prospective studies incorporating these detailed assessments would further enhance the clinical utility of preoperative prediction models.

In conclusion, this study evaluates the risk of synchronous metastasis and metastatic sites in colon cancer patients based on preoperative CECT imaging morphological features, which can assist clinicians in making personalized and precise decisions regarding imaging examinations, thereby being crucial to treatment decision-making and long-term prognosis for patients.
