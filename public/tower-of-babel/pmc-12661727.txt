Elevated expression and secretion of TGF-α contribute to Temozolomide resistance in human glioblastoma cells
Authors: Jen-Tsung Yang, I-Neng Lee, Ming-Shan Chen, Cheng Huang, Hsiu-Chen Huang, Yu-Ping Wu, Jui-Chieh Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is a highly aggressive brain tumor often treated with Temozolomide (TMZ). Research reveals that secretory substances and receptor-activated signaling may contribute to TMZ resistance in GBM cells. RNA-Seq and bioinformatics analyses reveal that TMZ treatment downregulates most genes, particularly those involved in cell structure and metabolism, while activating genes linked to secretory substances like cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors. Antibody array analysis identified a significant increase in TGF-α secretion after TMZ treatment, which also triggered its associated pathways. Moreover, the remarkable secretion of TGF-α also triggered the activation of its associated pathways. Notably, a marked increase in TGF-α expression was observed in TMZ-resistant cells. TGF-α knockdown restored TMZ sensitivity in a mouse xenograft model. Tissue analysis revealed significantly higher TGF-α levels in GBM, suggesting its potential as a drug resistance biomarker and target for new therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04050-z.Keywords: Glioblastoma (GBM), Temozolomide (TMZ), Drug resistance, Transforming growth factor-alpha (TGF-α), Protein secretion

Background

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), also known as grade IV astrocytoma, is the most common and aggressive brain tumor [1]. Despite progress in surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy, the average survival for GBM patients remains below 15 months, highlighting the urgent need for new treatments [2, 3].

Temozolomide (TMZ), a DNA-alkylating agent that easily permeates the blood-brain barrier, is the first-line treatment for GBM. Radiotherapy combined with TMZ therapy can significantly prolong the median survival, 2-year survival, progression-free survival, and improve the quality of life of patients [2]. TMZ exerts its effects by methylating DNA during replication, inducing strand breaks that result in cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase and subsequent apoptosis [4]. However, its efficacy declines as cancer cells acquire resistance [5–7]. Thus, studying the underlying molecular mechanisms of TMZ resistance is crucial for developing more effective chemotherapy.

With the rise of high-throughput sequencing, RNA-Seq has become a prominent tool for studying cancer transcriptomes. It uncovers mechanisms driving cancer progression, aiding in novel diagnostics and improving treatment efficacy and patient prognosis [8]. The tumor microenvironment, composed of various interacting cells, not only nourishes tumor cells but also secretes factors that promote treatment resistance [9]. RNA-Seq identifies RNA expression profiles within individual cells, enhancing our understanding of single-cell functions in the tumor microenvironment. This technique has been used to study gliomas, revealing cell interactions that help predict survival and treatment responses in GBM patients [10].

In the present study, we integrated RNA-sequencing (RNA-Seq) with bioinformatics analysis and employed antibody microarray technology to investigate differential secretion profiles, identifying a link between transforming growth factor-alpha (TGF-α) and TMZ resistance. TGF-α, a ligand that activates EGFR, is synthesized as a transmembrane precursor and requires proteolytic cleavage by a disintegrin and metalloproteinase 17 (ADAM17) for its extracellular release [11, 12]. Notably, ADAM17 has been shown to confer TMZ resistance in GBM cells [13]. TGF-α is a 50-amino-acid polypeptide that has only about 30% structural homology to EGF, and it also has binding ability to EGFR [14]. It has been found that TGF-α is able to promote cell growth and oncogene expression through autocrine and paracrine secretion, influencing tumorigenesis, progression, migration, drug sensitivity, and patient outcomes [15–23]. Elevated TGF-α levels have been observed in high-grade gliomas [24, 25]; however, its role in TMZ resistance in GBM remains largely unexplored.

Materials and methods

Cell lines, reagents, and chemicals

U-87 MG (ATCC HTB-14, RRID: CVCL_0022), DBTRG-05MG (ATCC CRL-2020, RRID: CVCL_1169), and M059K (ATCC CRL-2365, RRID: CVCL_0401) cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), Manassas, VA, USA. All cell lines were authenticated by ATCC using STR profiling and routinely tested negative for mycoplasma contamination. The Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Cat# 11965092) and fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cat# 26140079) required for the experiment were sourced from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA. Antibodies for Western blot analysis included TGF-α (Santa Cruz Cat# 374433, RRID:AB_10989073), p-EGFR (Cell Signaling, Cat# 6963S, RRID:AB_10839407), cleaved caspase-3 (Cell Signaling, Cat# 9661S, RRID:AB_2341188); ADAM17 (Cell Signaling, Cat# 3976S, RRID:AB_2242380); EGFR (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat# sc-03, RRID: AB_631420), p53 (Santa Cruz, Cat# sc-126, RRID: AB_628082); cleaved PARP (Abcam, Cat# ab32064, RRID: AB_777102); MGMT (LifeSpan BioSciences, Cat# LS-C116198, RRID: AB_10914789); and α-tubulin (Millipore, Cat# 05-829). TMZ (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# T2577) and dimethyl sulfoxide (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# D2650) were used in drug treatments.

Cell culture

GBM cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 units/ml penicillin G, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin in an incubator at 37 °C with 95% air and 5% CO2.

Methylation-specific PCR assay

DNA extraction and bisulfite conversion were performed using the EZ DNA Methylation-Direct Kit (Zymo Research, Cat# D5020) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The primers used are as follows:

MGMT unmethylated (U): F 5′-TTTGTGTTTTGATGTTTGTAGGTTTTTGT-3′, R 5′- AACTCCACACTCTTCCAAAAACAAAACA-3′.

MGMT methylated (M): F 5′-TTTCGACGTTCGTAGGTTTTCGC-3′, R 5′-GCACTCTTCCGAAAACGAAACG-3′. The amplification reaction was conducted in a thermal cycler (Bio-Rad) with the following program: 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 50 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplified PCR products were separated by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis and visualized with ethidium bromide.

RNA-Seq and iDEP analysis

For RNA-seq analysis, U-87 MG cells were treated with 62.5 µM TMZ for 48 h. Under these conditions, cell viability remained above 80% compared to the untreated control group. Control cells were treated with an equivalent concentration of DMSO, which was used as the vehicle to dissolve TMZ. These treatment conditions were selected to ensure sufficient RNA yield for sequencing while preserving cell viability, allowing us to capture TMZ-induced transcriptional responses without the confounding effects of excessive cell death. RNA was extracted using REzol reagent (Protech Technology, Taipei, Taiwan; cat. no. PT-KP200CT). RNA purity and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and Agilent Bioanalyzer, respectively, ensuring RNA integrity number (RIN) > 8.0. Library construction and sequencing were performed by Genomics Co., Ltd. (New Taipei City, Taiwan) on the Illumina HiSeq/NovaSeq platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. Transcript-level quantification was performed, and TPM values were used for downstream analysis. Differential expression and pathway enrichment analyses were performed using iDEP (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/idep/) [26], a web-based tool for integrative RNA-seq data analysis. We set a threshold of adjusted p (padj) value < 0.05, and all filtered genes were uploaded to iDEP for analysis. The iDEP settings were set to 2000 for the “most variable genes” and 2 for the “number of clusters.”

Preparation of conditioned media (CM)

Cells were cultured to 70% confluence, washed three times with PBS to remove residual FBS, and then incubated in serum-free DMEM with or without 62.5 µM TMZ (low-toxicity concentration) for 48 h. CM was collected and subjected to a centrifugation process at 500 × g for 15 min at RT. The supernatant was then meticulously aspirated and further concentrated by centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 3 h at 4 °C using an Amicon Ultra (Merck KGaA) with a 3 kDa cutoff.

Human growth factor antibody array

CM were analyzed using a human growth factor antibody array (Abcam, cat. no. ab134002) targeting 41 factors, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein samples (200 µg) were mixed with an array buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C with a pre-blocked membrane. After washing, the membrane was incubated with the primary antibody cocktail for two hours, washed again, and then incubated with the secondary antibody for 30 min. The bound antibodies were visualized using ECL reagents and autoradiography. Signals from duplicate spots were quantified and normalized to the negative controls using UN-SCAN-IT Gel Software (Silk Scientific), and changes in treated cells were compared to the control array.

Western blot

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher, cat. no. 89900) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Roche, cat. no. 04693159001), and protein concentration was measured using a BCA assay (Pierce, Thermo Fisher, cat. no. 23225). A total of 30 µg protein per sample was separated by SDS-PAGE, and then transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, cat. no. IPVH00010). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) and incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Following multiple washes with TBST, the membrane was exposed to a peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody and then washed to remove unbound secondary antibody. Visualization was performed using ECL reagents (Thermo Fisher, cat. no. 32106).

Human EGFR phosphorylation antibody array

U-87 MG cells were starved overnight in serum-free DMEM and exposed to 50 ng/ml TGF-α (Sino Biological, cat. no. 11252-HNAE) for 15 min or left unstimulated. The cell lysate (200 µg/sample) was hybridized to a pre-blocked array membrane containing 17 different phosphorylated human EGFR (ab134005; Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. After decanting the sample, the membrane was washed with buffer, and biotin-conjugated anti-EGFR was added. Following wash, the membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated streptavidin for 2 h, washed again, and the bound antibodies were visualized using detection buffer and autoradiography.

Establishment of TMZ resistant cells

The TMZ-resistant cell lines, U-87 MG-R and DBTRG-05MG-R, were established by stepwise exposure of parental cells to increasing concentrations of TMZ, starting from 15.625 µM and gradually increasing to 250 µM over a period of more than 6 months. The concentration was increased every 2–3 weeks after cells showed stable growth.

Identification of cell apoptosis through DAPI dye labeling and quantification

A total of 2.5 × 105 GBM cells per well were placed into 6-well culture plates and incubated overnight. The cells were then exposed to specified concentrations of TMZ for designated periods of time. Subsequently, the cells were preserved using 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, before being stained with DAPI dye (50 µg/ml) for 10 min. Apoptosis was determined by evaluating changes in nuclear morphology, specifically DNA condensation and fragmentation, using a fluorescence microscope (Lecia, Bensheim, Germany). The number of apoptotic nuclei was quantified from at least three randomly selected microscopic fields per sample using ImageJ software. Data were expressed as the percentage of apoptotic cells relative to total nuclei, and statistical analyses were performed to compare experimental groups.

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (qPCR)

RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol reagent (EBL Biotechnology, MRE-3200) and reverse transcribed into cDNA with the PrimeScript RT Reagent kit (Takara Bio). Quantitative PCR was conducted using KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR Master Mix (Takara Bio) and the MyGo PCR Detection System (IT-IS Life Science). Thermal cycling included an initiation at 95 °C for 30 s, denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s, repeated for 40 cycles. The primer sequence used was as follows: TGF-α: 5′-TTGCTGCCACTCAGAAACAGTG-3′ (forward) and 5′-TTGATCTGCCACAGTCCACCTG-3′ (reverse); GAPDH: 5′-CACCCATGGCAAATTCCATGGCA-3′ (forward) and 5′-TCTAGACGGCAGGTCAGGTCCACC-3′ (reverse). Relative gene expression was calculated using the comparative Ct (2−∆∆CT) method, normalizing to GAPDH.

MTT

Cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 103 in a 96-well plate with 100 µl of medium, then incubated overnight for attachment. Prior to the end of the experiment, a 0.5 mg/ml MTT solution was added and incubated for 4 h. After removing the medium, 100 µl of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals, and absorbance was measured at 550 nm, with background values at 750 nm subtracted.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay

Cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well in a 96-well plate containing 100 µl of medium and incubated overnight to allow for attachment. Following treatment with the indicated concentrations of TMZ for 48 h, cell-free supernatants were collected. LDH release was then measured using a Cytotoxicity Detection Kit (cat. no. 11644793001, Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm, and the percentage of LDH release was calculated using the formula: LDH release (%) = [LDH activity in the medium/total LDH activity] × 100, where total LDH activity was defined as the sum of LDH activity in the medium and in the cells.

Antibody neutralization assay

Initially, U-87 MGR cells were starved in serum-free DMEM for 4 h. The cells were then treated with a neutralizing antibody against TGF-α (Santa Cruz Cat# 374433, RRID:AB_10989073) for 2 h, followed by exposure to TMZ.

TGF-α knockdown

Lentiviral vectors, including pLKO.1-shControl, TRCN0000369237 (shTGF-α#1), and TRCN0000369238 (shTGF-α#2), were obtained from the National RNAi Core Facility, Academia Sinica, Taiwan. Recombinant lentiviruses were produced in 293 T cells and used to infect target cells with 8 µg/ml polybrene for 24 h. After a 48 h incubation in fresh medium, stable cell lines were selected with 5 µg/ml puromycin.

TGF-α overexpression

M059K cells were transfected using TurboFect transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) with either 1 µg of the TGF-α encoding plasmid (pCMV3-TGF-α) or its equivalent control vector (pCMV3), both obtained from Sino Biological Inc.

Xenograft mouse model

Female BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old) were obtained from BioLASCO (Taipei, Taiwan) and housed in aseptic cages at the Chang Gung Memorial Hospital Animal Center (Chiayi, Taiwan). Mice were injected subcutaneously with 5 × 106 U-87 MGR cells. When tumors reached ~ 60 mm³, TMZ (10 mg/kg) was administered 2–3 times weekly for 15 days. Tumor volume was calculated as length × width² × 0.5. Mice were euthanized with CO₂, and tumors were excised and fixed in formaldehyde for IHC analysis.

Tissue array

To analyze TGF-α in brain tumors, we used a tissue microarray (cat. No. GL208, US Biomax, Inc.) with 27 astrocytomas, 33 GBMs, and 9 normal tissues. Paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene, treated with 5% hydrogen peroxide for 20 min, and blocked with 3% BSA, Tris-buffered saline, and 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 h. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TGF-α (1:500 dilution; Santa Cruz Biotechnology cat. no. sc-374433 for tissue array, Abcam cat. no. ab208156 for mouse tumor tissue) or cleaved caspase 3 (1:200). After washing, slides were treated with biotinylated anti-rabbit secondary antibody and peroxidase-labeled streptavidin, followed by DAB substrate chromogen and hematoxylin counterstaining.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent biological replicates. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed paired Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 6.0.

TMZ treatment induced TGF-α secretion and activated its associated pathways

We used an antibody array to analyze secreted proteins in CM from TMZ-treated and untreated U-87 MG cells, providing insights into potential TMZ-related mechanisms. As shown in Figs. 2A and 10 secreted proteins changed over 1.5-fold, with 8 decreasing and 2 increasing. Notably, TMZ treatment caused a nearly 6000-fold increase in TGF-α secretion. Western blot confirmed dose-dependent TGF-α secretion (Fig. 2B), and a decrease in TGF-α levels in cell lysates after TMZ treatment (Fig. 2C). ADAM17, responsible for pre-TGF-α cleavage, was also upregulated after TMZ treatment (Fig. 2D). We also retrospectively re-analyzed the sequencing results from RNA-Seq, revealing a 2-fold upregulation of TGF-α mRNA and a 1.6-fold increase in ADAM17 expression under TMZ stimulation (Fig. 2E and F). With the rapidly increasing abundance of gene expression datasets in public databases, we also analyzed RNA-Seq data of TMZ-resistant GBM cell lines (A172R) and their parental cells (A172) obtained from the NCBI GEO database. The results demonstrated that TGF-α expression was notably upregulated in TMZ-resistant cells, showing an increase of 2.76-fold compared to the parental cells (Fig. 2G). To clarify that TGF-α mediates EGFR activation in U-87 MG cells, we analyzed phosphorylation levels of 17 EGF receptors using an antibody array. A notable increase in phospho-EGFR (Tyr845) was observed following recombinant TGF-α treatment (Fig. 2H), which was validated by Western blot (Fig. 2I). These findings suggest that TMZ promotes TGF-α secretion and activates associated pathways.

High expression and secretion of TGF-α induced TMZ resistance in U-87 MG cells

We generated a TMZ-resistant cell line, U-87 MGR, to determine the impact of TGF-α on TMZ resistance. To verify that U-87 MGR cells are more resistant to TMZ than the parental cells, both were treated with increasing concentrations of TMZ for 48 h, and cell apoptosis was analyzed via DAPI staining followed by quantitative analysis. As shown in Fig. 3A, the proportion of apoptotic cells was significantly higher in U-87 MG than in U-87 MGR cells. We collected the culture supernatant, quantified equal total protein for antibody arrays, and identified four proteins with secretion levels altered by more than 2.5-fold. TGF-α secretion was elevated in U-87 MGR cells, suggesting its role in TMZ resistance. GCSF, GM-CSF, and TGF-β were also highly secreted in U-87 MGR cells (Fig. 3B). TGF-α expression was validated by real-time quantitative and Western blot, confirming higher expression in U-87 MGR cells (Fig. 3C-D). To confirm secretion levels, Ponceau S staining verified equal protein loading (Fig. 3E, right), showing elevated TGF-α secretion in U-87 MGR cells compared to U-87 MG cells (Fig. 3E, left). Neutralizing TGF-α with an anti-TGF-α antibody increased TMZ sensitivity in U-87 MGR cells (Fig. 3F). U-87 MGR cells were subjected to TGF-α knockdown using an shRNA lentiviral system, as verified by Western blot (Fig. 3G). MTT assay showed that TGF-α knockdown significantly increased TMZ sensitivity in U-87 MGR cells compared to shControl (Fig. 3H). Consistently, LDH release assay further confirmed that silencing TGF-α significantly enhanced TMZ-induced cytotoxicity in U-87 MGR cells (Fig. 3I). In addition, TGF-α silencing further elevated cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP levels induced by TMZ (Fig. 3J). We next examined the impact of TGF-α knockdown on p-EGFR, ADAM17, and TGF-α expression following TMZ treatment. Both shControl and shTGFa groups showed decreased p-EGFR expression after TMZ exposure, with a more pronounced reduction in the shTGFa group. Knockdown of TGF-α did not significantly affect ADAM17 levels under TMZ treatment, but TGF-α expression was markedly downregulated in the shTGFa group (Fig. 3K). These results clearly indicated that TGF-α levels are positively correlated with TMZ resistance.

High levels of TGF-α conferred the increased resistance to TMZ in GBM cells

Subsequently, we examined the correlation between TGF-α expression and TMZ sensitivity across human GBM cell lines. Quantitative analysis of DAPI-stained apoptotic nuclei (Fig. 4A) revealed that TMZ sensitivity, from lowest to highest, was ranked as follows: DBTRG-05MG, U-87 MG, and M059K, with statistically significant differences observed between each pair of cell lines. The cleavage of PARP and caspase-3 was further investigated through Western blot analysis, revealing that the lowest levels of the cleaved forms were observed in DBTRG-05MG cells, followed by U-87 MG cells, with the highest levels seen in M059K cells (Fig. 4B). The expression levels of TGF-α in the three GBM cells were then further verified by qPCR and Western blot. The results revealed that TGF-α was positively correlated with TMZ resistance (4 C-4D). Next, we evaluated the impact of neutralizing TGF-α with an anti-human TGF-α antibody on TMZ sensitivity. As depicted in Fig. 4E, pretreatment with the TGF-α antibody effectively enhanced the sensitivity of DBTRG-05MG cells to TMZ. Since DBTRG-05MG cells have higher TGF-α expression and TMZ resistance, we further suppressed the expression of TGF-α in DBTRG-05MG cells and analyzed whether the cells were more sensitive to TMZ. As shown in Fig. 4F, shTGF-α lentivirus resulted in an obviously reduced expression of TGF-α as compared to shControl. According to the MTT assay results, TGF-α knockdown slightly increased the sensitivity of DBTRG-05MG cells to TMZ compared to the shControl group (Fig. 4G). Consistently, LDH release assay further confirmed that silencing TGF-α significantly enhanced TMZ-induced cytotoxicity in DBTRG-05MG cells (Fig. 4H). After TMZ treatment, TGF-α-knockdowned DBTRG-05MG cells markedly increased the expression of cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP compared with the shControl group (Fig. 4I). Conversely, M059K had lower TGF-α expression and higher TMZ sensitivity; therefore, we overexpressed TGF-α in M059K and analyzed its effect on TMZ sensitivity. As shown in Fig. 4J, TGF-α was overexpressed in M059K cells after transfection with the TGF-α expression vector, as evaluated by Western blot. The overexpression of TGF-α in M059K cells significantly increased resistance to TMZ compared with the empty vector, as assessed using an MTT assay (Fig. 4K). Furthermore, we also established a TMZ-resistant DBTRG-05MG subline called DBTRG-05MGR to investigate the relationship between TGF-α levels and TMZ resistance. As shown in Fig. 4L, DBTRG-05MGR cells were more resistant to TMZ than their parental DBTRG-05MG cells. In addition, DBTRG-05MGR cells had a high level of expression and secretion of TGF-α compared to DBTRG-05MG cells (Fig. 4M and N). The results clearly indicated that the amount of TGF-α had a positive relationship with TMZ resistance in GBM cells.

To assess the impact of TGF-α on tumor growth in vivo, we implanted equal numbers of U-87 MGR/shTGF-α and U-87 MGR/shControl cells into mice. Tumor sizes were measured from day 5 and harvested on day 20. By day 15, the TGF-α knockdown group showed significantly reduced tumor growth compared to controls, though no difference in fold change was observed (Fig. 5A-C). Additionally, we utilized a subcutaneous tumor cell implantation model to further validate the link between TGF-α expression and TMZ sensitivity. From day 12 of TMZ treatment, the TGF-α knockdown group exhibited a significant reduction in tumor volume compared to the control group (Fig. 5D). At the end of the experiment, photographs were taken of the mice with subcutaneous tumors, and the results revealed that the tumor size in the U-87 MGR/shTGF-α group was notably smaller in comparison to the control group (Fig. 5E). Tumor tissues were then taken from the mice and it was also observed that U-87 MGR/shTGF-α group had a smaller tumor size (Fig. 5F). IHC staining confirmed a marked reduction in TGF-α levels in cells transduced with shTGF-α lentiviruses (Fig. 5G). As shown in Fig. 5K, GBM tissues exhibited significantly higher TGF-α protein levels compared to astrocytoma and normal tissues, suggesting a correlation between elevated TGF-α expression and tumor progression. Discussion

Recently, RNA-Seq and antibody arrays have been widely used in cancer research to explore molecular mechanisms and identify potential diagnostic, prognostic, therapeutic, and drug development targets [26, 28–30]. In the present study, we combined RNA-Seq and antibody arrays to identify elevated TGF-α expression and secretion under TMZ treatment, potentially linked to TMZ resistance. Exogenous TGF-α increased phosphorylated EGFR (Tyr 845), suggesting that TGF-α-activated pathways are involved in this resistance. In GBM cells, TGF-α levels correlated with TMZ resistance, where TGF-α knockdown or neutralizing antibody enhanced TMZ sensitivity, while overexpression promoted resistance.

MGMT expression is a key resistance mechanism in TMZ therapy, as this repair enzyme removes the methyl group from the O6 position of guanine, counteracting the anti-tumor effects of TMZ. MGMT expression can also be silenced via promoter methylation [31]. p53 status is also linked to TMZ sensitivity, with recent studies showing that TMZ-resistant GBM cells exhibit upregulated p53 expression and phosphorylation, driving E2F7 expression and promoting chemotherapy resistance [32, 33]. While MGMT and p53 are promising prognostic and predictive biomarkers, their role in guiding GBM treatment, particularly MGMT, remains controversial due to inconsistencies between MGMT promoter methylation and treatment response. Further exploration of drug resistance mechanisms in GBM is needed [34].

In this study, U-87 MG cells with low MGMT and p53 expression were treated with TMZ, and RNA sequencing revealed activation of secretory protein pathways. Antibody arrays confirmed that TMZ significantly induces and secretes TGF-α, an EGF family member involved in neural progenitor proliferation, cell fate determination, neuronal survival, differentiation, astroglial metabolism regulation, neuroprotection, and astroglial neoplasia [35]. Notably, RNA-seq analysis of U-87 MG cells in our dataset revealed no detectable expression of MGMT indicating that the resistance observed in our system is likely MGMT-independent and may be driven instead by EGFR-associated signaling.

TGF-α is involved in astrocyte genesis and function, with defects in TGF-α or ErbB1 expression leading to reduced astrocyte maturation [36]. Disruption of the TGF-α/ErbB1 signaling pathway is common in gliomas, suggesting its role in early tumor formation by influencing astrocyte proliferation and apoptosis [35, 37]. These findings indicate that TGF-α may be involved in the early stages of tumor formation by influencing the proliferation and apoptosis of astrocytes. A recent study demonstrated that TGF-α exposure can transform mature astrocytes into neural progenitor and stem cells, potentially contributing to the cancerous neural stem cell-like properties of glioma and impacting treatment outcomes [38].

TGF-α expression positively correlates with EGFR expression and proliferative activity in human astrocytic gliomas, increasing with tissue malignancy grade [39]. In our experiments, TGF-α knockdown enhanced the inhibition of EGFR activation by TMZ. Recent studies indicate that TGF-α plays a key role in sEGFR dimerization, potentially contributing to drug resistance [40, 41]. Thus, high TGF-α expression and secretion may promote sEGFR dimerization, leading to cellular resistance to therapy.

ADAM17, also known as TGF-α-converting enzyme (TACE), is responsible for shedding pro-TGF-α [12, 42, 43]. Ectodomain shedding is a proteolytic mechanism that cleaves cell surface proteins into secreted forms and modifies the surrounding microenvironment [44–46]. In GBM tumors, cancer cells manipulate neurons, glial cells, and endothelial cells to create a tumor-promoting microenvironment [47]. A recent study highlights cancer stem cells as therapeutic targets due to their role in reconstituting a recurrence-associated microenvironment [48]. Therefore, targeting ADAM17 may offer a promising strategy for cancer treatment [49, 50].

We observed a TGF-α band at approximately 17 kDa in the conditioned medium, which is notably higher than its theoretical molecular weight (~ 5.5 kDa). This discrepancy may be attributed to post-translational modifications (e.g., glycosylation), dimerization, or membrane-bound precursor forms. Although we performed centrifugation steps to remove extracellular vesicles, the possibility of vesicle-associated TGF-α cannot be fully excluded. While this study did not specifically investigate the molecular forms of TGF-α, future work is warranted to elucidate its precise forms and release mechanisms under TMZ treatment.

Due to the limited availability of GBM tissue, the role of TGF-α in GBM patients needs further validation. However, many literatures have linked TGF-α expression to tumor recurrence and poor prognosis. High TGF-α levels in primary head and neck squamous cell carcinomas are associated with poorer survival, identifying patients at higher risk for recurrence [21]. In breast cancer, elevated TGF-α detected by IHC predicts a poor prognosis [51]. Similarly, TGF-α expression correlates with lower survival rates in esophageal carcinoma [52].

Previous studies have shown that overexpression of TGF-α in transgenic mice induces epithelial hyperplasia, pancreatic metaplasia, and tumor formation in the mammary gland and liver [53–55]. TGF-α activates EGFR signaling through both autocrine and paracrine mechanisms, thereby promoting tumor cell survival, proliferation, and angiogenesis [25, 56, 57]. In GBM specifically, elevated levels of TGF-α have been associated with increased cell motility, invasiveness, and a higher risk of tumor recurrence [24, 58–60]. However, these early reports did not investigate whether chemotherapy directly induces TGF-α expression and secretion, nor did they explore its role in TMZ resistance. In this study, we demonstrate for the first time that TMZ treatment induces TGF-α expression and significantly enhances its secretion—by up to 6000-fold in GBM cells. This leads to EGFR pathway activation and contributes to chemotherapy resistance. Beyond its potential role as a biomarker of tumor malignancy, we also show that TGF-α represents a promising therapeutic target, as evidenced by both in vitro and in vivo models. These findings provide novel mechanistic insights into TGF-α–mediated resistance in GBM and suggest new opportunities for diagnosis and therapeutic intervention.

Although our study focuses on the role of TGF-α in mediating TMZ resistance via EGFR signaling, accumulating evidence from other malignancies suggests that TGF-α may also influence the tumor microenvironment (TME). In colon cancer cells, overexpression of TGF-α has been shown to promote a pro-metastatic microenvironment by upregulating VEGF-A, IL-8, and matrix metalloproteinases, as well as recruiting macrophages that secrete VEGF-C, thereby enhancing lymphangiogenesis and metastatic potential [61]. In triple-negative breast cancer cells, TGF-α induces the expression of pro-inflammatory chemokines such as CXCL2, further contributing to a TME favorable for tumor progression [62]. These findings raise the possibility that TGF-α may regulate the microenvironment in GBM through similar mechanisms, although direct evidence in GBM is currently lacking. Future studies are warranted to determine whether TGF-α in GBM can also trigger cytokine release, immune cell recruitment, or extracellular matrix remodeling, thereby promoting therapeutic resistance.

The molecular mechanisms underlying TGF-α-mediated TMZ resistance involve complex signaling cascades. TGF-α is a member of the EGF family that regulates diverse cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, migration, and survival [63]. TGF-α exerts its biological functions through binding to EGFR, leading to receptor dimerization and autophosphorylation, which initiates downstream signaling. Key pathways activated by TGF-α include the Ras/MAPK pathway, which promotes cell proliferation and survival, and the PI3K/Akt pathway, which supports cell survival and inhibits apoptosis [64]. In addition, TGF-α has been reported to modulate the tumor microenvironment and enhance angiogenesis and metastasis [65]. In our study, treatment with recombinant TGF-α resulted in increased EGFR phosphorylation at Tyr845, indicating receptor activation. Furthermore, cells overexpressing TGF-α exhibited enhanced resistance to TMZ-induced apoptosis, consistent with the anti-apoptotic effects of activated EGFR signaling. These findings suggest that the TGF-α–EGFR axis plays a key role in mediating TMZ resistance in GBM.

Conclusions

We investigated the effects of TMZ on U-87 MG cells using RNA-Seq and bioinformatics, revealing that GBM cells activate secretion pathways in response to TMZ, potentially promoting drug resistance. Antibody array analysis revealed a remarkable increase in TGF-α secretion upon TMZ stimulation, subsequently initiating its associated pathways. We used genetic manipulation to modulate TGF-α expression and validated its role in TMZ resistance via in vitro and in vivo approaches. Tissue arrays of clinical samples revealed a strong correlation between high TGF-α expression and tumor progression. These findings suggest TGF-α as a biomarker for TMZ resistance, offering insights into GBM treatment strategies.
