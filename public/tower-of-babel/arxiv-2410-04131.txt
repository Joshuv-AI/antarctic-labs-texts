A several variables Kowalski-Słodkowski theorem for topological spaces
Jaikishan, Sneh Lata, Dinesh Singh

Abstract
. In this paper, we provide a version of the classical result of Kowalski and Słodkowski that generalizes the famous Gleason-Kahane- Z ˙ \dot{\rm Z} elazko (GKZ) theorem by characterizing multiplicative linear functionals amongst all complex-valued functions on a Banach algebra. We first characterize maps on 𝒜 \mathcal{A} -valued polynomials of several variables that satisfy some conditions, motivated by the result of Kowalski and Słodkowski, as a composition of a multiplicative linear functional on 𝒜 \mathcal{A} and a point evaluation on the polynomials, where 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is a complex Banach algebra with identity. We then apply it to prove an analogue of Kowalski and Słodkowski’s result on topological spaces of vector-valued functions of several variables. These results extend our previous work from [ 3 ] ; however, the techniques used differ from those used in [ 3 ] . Furthermore, we characterize weighted composition operators between Hardy spaces over the polydisc amongst the continuous functions between them. Additionally, we register a partial but noteworthy success toward a multiplicative GKZ theorem for Hardy spaces.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{A}
denote a complex Banach algebra with identity and
G(\mathcal{A})
denote the set of invertible elements in
\mathcal{A}
. A non-zero linear functional
F
defined on
\mathcal{A}
is called multiplicative or a character if
F(xy)=F(x)F(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
. It is easy to see that a multiplicative linear functional on
\mathcal{A}
can never vanish on
G(\mathcal{A})
. Interestingly, the converse is also true, and it is the well-known Gleason-Kahane-
\dot{\rm Z}
elazko (GKZ) theorem, which characterizes all multiplicative linear functionals on a complex unital Banach algebra.

Theorem 1.1

(GKZ theorem)
.

Let
F
be a linear functional on a complex unital Banach algebra
\mathcal{A}
with identity
e
such that
F(e)=1
and
F(x)\neq 0
for every
x\in G(\mathcal{A})
, then
F(xy)=F(x)F(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
.

The GKZ theorem was first proved by Gleason
[
2
]
and, independently, by Kahane and
\dot{\rm Z}
elazko
[
4
]
for commutative Banach algebras. Afterward,
\dot{\rm Z}
elazko
[
16
]
extended it to the non-commutative case. An elementary proof of Theorem
1.1
, due to Roitman and Sternfeld, can be found in
[
10
]
.

Around a decade later, Kowalski and Słodkowski gave an interesting generalization of the GKZ theorem in
[
6
]
. They proved the following characterization of multiplicative linear functionals amongst all complex-valued functions on complex unital Banach algebras without using the linearity condition.

Theorem 1.2

(KS theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a complex unital Banach algebra with identity
e
and let
F:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{C}
be a map satisfying
F(0)=0
and

(1)

\big(F(x)-F(y)\big)e-(x-y)\notin G(\mathcal{A})

for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
. Then
F
is multiplicative and linear.

Note that for
y=0
, Condition (
1
) yields
F(x)e-x\notin G(\mathcal{A})
for every
x
in
\mathcal{A}
, which for a linear
F
, is equivalent to
F
being non-zero on
invertible elements in
\mathcal{A}
. Thus, the KS theorem generalizes the GKZ theorem. Interestingly, the above characterizations have analogues for function spaces that are not necessarily algebras.

To lend perspective to the discussion at hand, which has set the tone for our current work, we shall first discuss analogues of the GKZ theorem as proved in
[
3
,
8
,
11
]
. Unless specified otherwise, we consider all vector spaces over the field of complex numbers and assume all the functions to be complex-valued.

Recently, Mashreghi and Ransford
[
8
]
extended the GKZ theorem to certain Banach spaces of analytic functions on the open unit disc
\mathbb{D}
. The following is their version for the Hardy spaces.

Theorem 1.3
.

[
8
, Theorem 2.1]
For
0<p\leq\infty
, let
F:H^{p}(\mathbb{D})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a linear functional such that
F(1)=1
and
F(g)\neq 0
for all
outer
functions
g
in
H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
. Then there exists
w\in\mathbb{D}
such that

F(f)=f(w)\ \ for\ all\ f\in H^{p}(\mathbb{D}).

This is a genuine counterpart of the GKZ theorem for Hardy spaces, as any function
f
in the Hardy space with an
g
in the same space such that
fg=1
must be outer. Moreover, a point evaluation
is multiplicative whenever the multiplication is well-defined. In the same paper, they also gave the following analogous result for a much broader class of Banach spaces.

Let
Hol(\mathbb{D})
denote the set of all holomorphic functions on the open unit disc
\mathbb{D}
, and
let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq Hol(\mathbb{D})
be a Banach space with the following properties:

(1)

for each
w\in\mathbb{D}
, the map
f\mapsto f(w)
is continuous;

(2)

\mathcal{X}
contains all polynomials, and they are dense in
\mathcal{X}
;

(3)

multiplication with the function
z
is well-defined on
\mathcal{X}
.

Further, suppose
\mathcal{W}
is a subset of
\mathcal{X}
which satisfies the following properties:

(1)

if
f\in\mathcal{X}
is bounded and
|f|
is bounded away from zero on
\mathbb{D}
, then
f\in\mathcal{W}
;

(2)

\mathcal{W}
contains the linear polynomials
g(z)=z-\lambda,\ |\lambda|=1
.

Theorem 1.4
.

[
8
, Theorem 3.1]

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq Hol(\mathbb{D})
be a Banach space and let
\mathcal{W}
be a subset of
\mathcal{X}
. Suppose they satisfy the above-stated properties
(\mathcal{X}1)-(\mathcal{X}3)
and
(\mathcal{W}1)-(\mathcal{W}2)
, respectively. If
F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{C}
is a continuous linear functional such that
F(1)=1
and it never vanishes on the set

w\in\mathbb{D}
such that
F(f)=f(w)
for all

.

Inspired by the work mentioned above of Mashreghi and Ransford, the authors of this article proved a new analogue of the GKZ theorem in
[
3
]
. This analogue is interesting because we dropped the condition that requires the vector space to be a Banach space. In fact, we assumed the vector space has a topology that is not necessarily related to its algebraic structure. More precisely, we proved the following:

Let
\mathcal{F}(r,\mathbb{C})
denote the family of complex-valued functions defined on the open disc

in

with center at

Theorem 1.5
.

[
3

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathcal{F}(r,\mathbb{C})

(1)

for each
w\in D(0,r)
, the map
f\mapsto f(w)
is continuous;

(2)

\mathcal{X}
contains the set of complex polynomials as a dense subset.

Let
\mathcal{Y}=\{(z-\lambda)^{n}:n\geq 0,\ \lambda\in\mathbb{C},\ |\lambda|\geq r\}

is, in a spirit, similar to Theorems
1.3

. The point to be also noted about Theorem

1.5
gave a version of Theorem
1.3
, but with a restriction of continuity on the linear functional, which has been justified in
[
3
]
with an example. It is also worth noting that unlike the proofs of Theorems

and
1.4
, as given in
[

]
, the proof of Theorem
1.5
in
[

In
[
11
]
, Sampat obtained a version of the GKZ theorem for function spaces in the several variables setting. This result has its roots in
[
5
]
and
[
8
]
. In
[

]
, Kou and Liu characterized the bounded linear operators on

gave yet another proof of Theorem

in which they worked with a specific subclass of outer functions, namely,

. Furthermore,
their proof does not rely on the GKZ theorem, and the result assumes the continuity of the linear functional. Sampat extended Kou and Liu’s ideas to the several variables setting in
[
11
]
. The following is his analogue of the GKZ theorem. For an open set
U\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, let
\mathcal{X}\subset Hol(U)
be a Banach space satisfying the following properties:

(1)

The set of polynomials is dense in
\mathcal{X}
;

(2)

For each
z\in U,
the point evaluation map
f\mapsto f(z)
is bounded on
\mathcal{X}
. Furthermore,
U
contains each
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
for which the map
p\mapsto p(z)
on the set of polynomials extends to a bounded linear functional on all of
\mathcal{X}
;

(3)

The
i^{th}
- shift operator
S_{i}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
, defined as
S_{i}f(z):=z_{i}f(z)
for every
(z_{k})_{k=1}^{n}=z\in U
and
f\in\mathcal{X}
, is a bounded linear operator for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

Theorem 1.6
.

[
11
, Theorem 1.4]

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Banach space that satisfies the above properties
(JS1)-(JS3)
over an open set
U\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Let
\Lambda:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{C}
be a continuous linear functional such that
\Lambda(e^{w\cdot z})\neq 0
for every
w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Then there exist a non-zero
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
and
w\in U
such that

\Lambda(f)=\alpha f(w)\quad\text{for all }f\in\mathcal{X}.

We now focus on an analogue of the KS theorem for function spaces. By following the ideas of Mashreghi and Ransford from
[
8
]
, Sebastian and Daniel extended the KS theorem to modules in
[
12
, Theorem 2.3]
; then they used it to obtain the following generalization of Theorem
1.3
.

Theorem 1.7
.

[
12
, Theorem 3.1]

Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
F:H^{p}(\mathbb{D})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a function such that
F(0)=0,\ F(1)\neq 0,
and

(2)

\big(F(f_{1})-F(f_{2})\big)1-F(1)(f_{1}-f_{2})\notin S

for all
f_{1},\ f_{2}\in H^{p}(\mathbb{D}),
where
S
is the set of all outer functions in
H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
. Then there exists a unique character
\chi
on
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
such that

F(fg)=\chi(f)F(g)

for all
f\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
and
g\in H^{p}(\mathbb{D}).
Further, there exist a non-zero constant
c
and
w\in\mathbb{D}
such that
F(g)=cg(w)
for all
g\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}).

Apart from being captivating in their own right, these various analogues of the GKZ theorem and its generalization, the KS theorem, are attracting attention as they lead to, in their respective settings, characterizations of linear maps that preserve outer functions. Mashreghi and Ransford used
Theorem
1.3
to show that linear operators on Hardy spaces
H^{p}(\mathbb{D}),\ 0<p\leq\infty
preserving outer functions are necessarily weighted composition operators (
[
8
, Theorems 2.2 & 2.3]
). Sebastian and Daniel in
[
12
]
, Kou and Liu in
[
5
]
, and Sampat in
[
11
]
have also used their results
mentioned above to obtain similar results for their respective settings.

In this paper, we extend the KS theorem to a much larger class of topological spaces (Theorem
3.2
)—as opposed to Banach spaces—consisting of functions defined on a bounded subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and taking values in a fixed complex Banach algebra with identity. To be precise, given a topological space
\mathcal{X}
of functions defined on certain bounded subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
taking values in a complex Banach algebra
\mathcal{A}
with identity, we characterize non-zero continuous functions on
\mathcal{X}
that satisfy some conditions similar to those imposed in the KS theorem as a composition of a point evaluation on
\mathcal{X}
and a multiplicative linear functional on
\mathcal{A}
. We note that Theorem
3.2
extends our earlier work Theorem
1.5
to functions of several variables; nevertheless, the techniques used in this paper differ from those employed for the proof of Theorem
1.5
in
[
3
]
. In addition, it is worth mentioning that many interesting and well-known function spaces such as the
\mathbb{C}^{m}
-valued Hardy spaces on
\mathbb{D}^{n}
, the Drury-Arveson space, the Dirichlet-type spaces on
\mathbb{D}^{n}
, and the polydisc algebra are covered by our result. A more elaborated list is given in Section
3
.

Furthermore, we use Theorem
3.2
to characterize weighted composition operators between Hardy spaces (Theorem
3.8
) amongst the family of continuous functions between them. Lastly, in Section
4
, we give a partial but noteworthy result towards a multiplicative GKZ theorem for Hardy spaces.

has extended the KS theorem to Hardy spaces

2.
Kowalski-Słodkowski’s Theorem for Hardy spaces

In this section, we shall refine Theorem
1.7
. We shall show that the hypotheses of Theorem
1.7
are indeed enough to establish that the map, as desired, is a point evaluation (up to a constant). We want
to note that we fail to understand why the authors have restricted the result to only
p\geq 1
when their
arguments are valid for
0<p\leq\infty.
The following is our rectification of Theorem
1.7
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
0<p\leq\infty
and let
F:H^{p}(\mathbb{D})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a non-zero function such that
F(0)=0
and

(3)

\big(F(f_{1})-F(f_{2})\big)1-F(1)(f_{1}-f_{2})\notin S

for all
f_{1},\ f_{2}\in H^{p}(\mathbb{D}),
where
S
is the set of all outer functions in
H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
. Then there exist a non-zero constant
c
and
w\in\mathbb{D}
such that

F(f)=cf(w)

for every
f\in H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
.

Proof.

The proof of Theorem
1.7
as given in
[
12
]
uses
[
12
, Theorem 2.3]
. We note that even for
0<p<1,
the triple,
\mathcal{A}=H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}),\ \mathcal{M}=H^{p}(\mathbb{D}),\ S
equals the set of all outer functions in
H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem 2.3 from
[
12
]
, and so just as shown in
[
12
]
, there exists a character
\chi:H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})\to\mathbb{C}
such that

F(fg)=\chi(f)F(g)
for all
f\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
and
g\in H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
, where
0<p\leq\infty
. Further, by using arguments as were used in Theorem
1.7
, there exists a point
w\in\mathbb{D}
such that

\chi(f)=f(w)

for all
f\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}).

This implies that for every
f\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, we obtain
F(f)=\chi(f)F(1)=F(1)f(w).

Let
O
be an outer function in
H^{p}(\mathbb{D}).
Then there exist outer functions
O_{1}
and
O_{2}
in
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
such that
O=O_{1}/O_{2}.
This means
O_{1}=O_{2}O
, and so
F(O_{1})=\chi(O_{2})F(O).
Therefore,
F(O)=F(1)O_{1}(w)/O_{2}(w)=F(1)O(w).
This, along with the inner-outer factorization of functions in
H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
, implies that
F(f)=F(1)f(w)
for every
f\in H^{p}(\mathbb{D}).

∎

Now, using Theorem
2.1
and employing arguments similar to the ones used in
[
8
, Theorem 2.2]
, we can improve Theorem 3.2 from
[
12
]
by extending it from
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
to
H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
as follows.

Theorem 2.2
.

For
0<p\leq\infty
, let
T:H^{p}(\mathbb{D})\to Hol(\mathbb{D})
be a continuous map satisfying
T(0)=0,\ T(1)(z)\neq 0
for every
z\in\mathbb{D},
and

(Tf(z)-Tg(z))1-T1(z)(f-g)\notin S

for every
f,g\in H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
and
z\in\mathbb{D},
where
S
is the set of outer functions in
H^{p}(\mathbb{D}).

Then there exist holomorphic functions
\phi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
and
\psi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
such that

Tf=\psi\cdot(f\circ\phi).

for every
f\in H^{p}(\mathbb{D}).

Proof.

For each fixed
w\in\mathbb{D}
, the map
T_{w}:H^{p}(\mathbb{D})\to\mathbb{C}
given by
T_{w}(f)=(Tf)(w)
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
2.1
; therefore, there exist a point
a_{w}\in\mathbb{D}
and a constant
c_{w}
such that
(Tf)(w)=c_{w}f(a_{w})
for every
f\in H^{p}(\mathbb{D}).
Then by taking
f
to be the constant function
1
, we see that
c_{w}=T1(w)\neq 0.
Further, by taking
f(z)=z
, we see

a_{w}=(Tz)(w)/T1(w).
We now define
\psi=T1
and
\phi=(Tz)/\psi
. Then
\psi
and
\phi
are holomorphic function on
\mathbb{D}
such that
\psi
doesn’t vanish at any point in
\mathbb{D}
,

\phi(\mathbb{D})\subseteq\mathbb{D}
, and
Tf=\psi\cdot(f\circ\phi)
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.
Kowalski-Słodkowski’s theorem for topological spaces

In this section, we shall prove an analogue of the KS theorem (Theorem
1.2
) for topological spaces of functions of several variables.

Let
\mathbb{Z}^{+}(n)
denote the set of
n
-tuples
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
of
non-negative integers. For
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}(n)
and
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n},
define
z^{\alpha}:=z_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}z_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\dotsm z_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
. For a Banach algebra
\mathcal{A}
with identity,
let
\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{A}}_{n}

denote the set of
all
\mathcal{A}
-valued polynomials in
n
variables, that is,

\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{A}}_{n}=\left\{p(z)=\sum\limits_{|\alpha|\leq k}a_{\alpha}z^{\alpha}:a_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A},\ z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\text{ and $k$ is a non-negative integer}\right\},

where
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\dotsm+\alpha_{n}
.
For convenience, we shall write
\mathcal{P}_{n}
instead of
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}
if
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{C}.
Clearly, given any compact subset
K
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
can be viewed as a subalgebra of the Banach algebra
C(K,\mathcal{A})
, the set of all continuous
\mathcal{A}
-valued functions with the
sup norm. We say
z_{0}
is zero of a polynomial
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}
if
p(z_{0})=0
. For the rest of this section, let

\mathcal{A}
represents a fixed complex Banach algebra with identity
e
.

The following result is integral to our version of the KS theorem for topological spaces (Theorem
3.2
). We present it as an independent result as, apart from being interesting in its own right, it brings out the crux of the method used to prove Theorem
3.2
even more prominently. It characterizes multiplicative linear functionals on
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}
as non-zero complex-valued functions on
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}
that satisfy a condition very similar to the one assumed in the KS theorem.
First, we recall the notion of a polynomial convex hull of a set in
\mathbb{C}^{n}.
The polynomial convex hull
\widehat{D}
of a bounded subset
D
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is consisting of all
w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that

|p(w)|\leq\sup_{z\in D}|p(z)|

for all polynomials
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Note that
\widehat{D}
is a closed set and
D\subseteq\widehat{D}.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Lambda:\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathbb{C}
be a non-zero function with
\Lambda(0)=0
. Suppose
D
is a bounded subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that for every
p
and
q
in
\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
the polynomial

\big(\Lambda(p)-\Lambda(q)\big)e-(p-q)

has a zero in
D
. Then there exist
w\in\widehat{D}
and a multiplicative linear functional
\phi
on
\mathcal{A}
such that

\Lambda(p)=\phi(p(w))\quad\text{for all }p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}.

Moreover, we assert
w\in D
whenever

(1)

n=1
;

(2)

D=D_{1}\times\cdots\times D_{n}
for some bounded sets
D_{1},\dots,D_{n}
in
\mathbb{C};

(3)

D
is an open or a closed Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Proof.

For every
p,\ q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}
there exists
w_{p,q}\in D
such that
(\Lambda(p)-\Lambda(q))e=p(w_{p,q})-q(w_{p,q})
.
In particular, for each polynomial
p
, there is a point
w_{p}
in
D
such that
\Lambda(p)e=p(w_{p})
. This gives us

(4)

\left\|(\Lambda(p)-\Lambda(q))e\right\|=\left\|p(w_{p,q})-q(w_{p,q})\right\|\leq\sup_{z\in\overline{D}}\left\|p(z)-q(z)\right\|.

Let
\overline{\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}}
denote the closure of
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}

with respect to the sup norm on
C(\overline{D},\mathcal{A})
, and let
f
be an element of
\overline{\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}}
.
Then there is a sequence
\{p_{n}\}_{n}
of polynomials that
converge to
f
, which, using Inequality (
4
), yields the existence of

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\Lambda(p_{n})
. This allows us to define a function
\Lambda_{1}:\overline{\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{A}}_{n}}\to\mathbb{C}
as
\Lambda_{1}(f):=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\Lambda(p_{n})
,
where
p_{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}
such that
p_{n}\longrightarrow f
.
It is easy to verify that
\Lambda_{1}
is a well-defined function.
Indeed, if there is another sequence
\{q_{n}\}_{n}
converging to
f
, then from Inequality (
4
), we get

\left\|\big(\Lambda(p_{n})-\Lambda(q_{n})\big)e\right\|\leq\left\|p_{n}-q_{n}\right\|_{\infty}
, from where it follows that
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\Lambda(p_{n})=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\Lambda(q_{n})
.

Now we want to show that
\big(\Lambda_{1}(f)-\Lambda_{1}(g)\big)e-(f-g)\notin G(\overline{\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}})
(the set of invertible elements of
\overline{\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}}
) for all
f,g\in\overline{\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}}
.
Suppose there are
f
and
g
in
\overline{\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}}
such that
\big(\Lambda_{1}(f)-\Lambda_{1}(g)\big)e-(f-g)\in G(\overline{\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}})
. Let
\{p_{n}\}_{n}
and

\{q_{n}\}_{n}
be sequences of polynomials in
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}
such that
p_{n}\longrightarrow f
and
q_{n}\longrightarrow g
in
\overline{\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}}
. Then

\big(\Lambda_{1}(p_{n})-\Lambda_{1}(q_{n})\big)e-(p_{n}-q_{n})\longrightarrow\big(\Lambda_{1}(f)-\Lambda_{1}(g)\big)e-(f-g)

in
\overline{\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}}
as
n\longrightarrow\infty
. Since
G(\overline{\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}})
is open, there
exists a positive integer
N
such that

\big(\Lambda_{1}(p_{n})-\Lambda_{1}(q_{n})\big)e-(p_{n}-q_{n})\in G(\overline{\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}})
for all
n\geq N
. In particular,
\big(\Lambda_{1}(p_{N})-\Lambda_{1}(q_{N})\big)\mathbf{e}-(p_{N}-q_{N})
can’t vanish on
D
. But this is a contradiction to the hypothesis. So,
(\Lambda_{1}(f)-\Lambda_{1}(g))e-(f-g)\notin G(\overline{\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}})
for all
f,g
in
\overline{\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}}
.

Then, by Theorem
1.2
,
\Lambda_{1}
is multiplicative and linear. In particular,
\Lambda
is multiplicative and linear on
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}
.

For each
1\leq i\leq n,
define
r_{i}(z):=z_{i}e
. Let
w=(\Lambda(r_{1}),\Lambda(r_{2}),\dots,\Lambda(r_{n}))
. Since
\Lambda
is multiplicative, we obtain
\Lambda(z^{\alpha})=\Lambda(ez_{1}^{\alpha_{1}})\Lambda(ez_{2}^{\alpha_{2}})\cdots\Lambda(ez_{n}^{\alpha_{n}})=w^{\alpha}
. Now consider the map
\phi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{C}
given by
\phi(a)=\Lambda(a)
. Then for any polynomial
p(z)=\sum\limits_{|\alpha|\leq k}a_{\alpha}z^{\alpha}
in
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}
, we have

\displaystyle\Lambda(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{|\alpha|\leq k}\Lambda(a_{\alpha}z^{\alpha})=\sum\limits_{|\alpha|\leq k}\Lambda(a_{\alpha})\Lambda(ez^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{|\alpha|\leq k}\phi(a_{\alpha})\Lambda(ez^{\alpha})=\sum\limits_{|\alpha|\leq k}\phi(a_{\alpha})w^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi\left(\sum\limits_{|\alpha|\leq k}a_{\alpha}w^{\alpha}\right)=\phi(p(w))

This establishes
\Lambda(p)=\phi(p(w))
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}
.

To prove
w\in\widehat{D},
let
p
be a polynomial in
\mathcal{P}_{n}.
Then
p_{1}(z)=p(z)e
is a polynomial in
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}};
thus,

\Lambda(p_{1})=\phi(p_{1}(w))=p(w).
This, using Inequality (
4
), implies that

|p(w)|=|\Lambda(p_{1})|\leq\sup_{z\in\overline{D}}|p(z)|=\sup_{z\in D}|p(z)|.

Hence,
w\in\widehat{D}.

Finally, we will establish the assertions made in the moreover part. First, suppose
n=1,
that is, we are in the one-variable situation. Take
p(z)=ez,
then
\Lambda(p)=w.
Also, as we noted at the beginning of the proof, there exists a point
a\in D
such that

\Lambda(p)e=p(a)=ea
, which implies that
w=a\in D.

We now turn to the general
n
case. Let
D=D_{1}\times\cdots\times D_{n}
for some subsets
D_{1},\dots,D_{n}
of
\mathbb{C}.
The idea here is very similar to case
n=1
. Note that
\Lambda(r_{i})=\phi(r_{i}(w))=\phi(w_{i}e)=w_{i}
. Additionally, as mentioned earlier, some
a
exists in
D
such that
\Lambda(r_{i})e=r_{i}(a)=ea_{i}.
Thus
w_{i}=a_{i}\in D_{i}
; hence,
w\in D_{1}\times\cdots\times D_{n}=D.

Lastly, let
D=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:||z-b||_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}<r\}
for some
b\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and positive real number
r.
Let
p(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\overline{w_{i}-b_{i}})e(z_{i}-b_{i}e)
. Then

\Lambda(p)=\phi(p(w))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|w_{i}-b_{i}|^{2}=||w-b||^{2}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}.

Also, there exists some
a\in D
such that
\Lambda(p)e=p(a)
, which implies that

||w-b||^{2}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\overline{w_{i}-b_{i}})(a_{i}-b_{i})=\langle{w-b,a-b}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}
. Thus, using the
Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we infer
||w-b||_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\leq||a-b||_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}<r.
Thus,
w\in D
. A similar set of arguments will work when
D
is a closed Euclidean ball in
C^{n}
. This completes the proof.
∎

We are ready to prove our version of the KS theorem for topological spaces of functions of several variables that take values in a fixed complex Banach algebra with identity. Before we state our result, we first fix some notations for it. Recall that we have fixed
\mathcal{A}
for Banach algebra with identity
e
. Let
D
be a bounded subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\mathcal{F}(D,\mathcal{A})
denotes the vector space of all
\mathcal{A}
-valued functions defined on
D
. We assume that when
n\geq 2
,
D
is either a Cartesian product of
n
bounded subsets of
\mathbb{C}
or an Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a subset of
\mathcal{F}(D,\mathcal{A})
equipped with a topology that satisfies the following properties:

(1)

for each
z\in D
, the evaluation map
f\mapsto f(z)
is continuous;

(2)

\mathcal{X}
contains the polynomial set
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}
as a dense subset.

Let
\Lambda:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{C}
be a non-zero continuous function such that
\Lambda({0})=0
and for every
p
and
q
in
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}

the polynomial

\big(\Lambda(p)-\Lambda(q)\big)e-(p-q)

has a zero in
D
. Then there exist
w\in D
and a multiplicative linear functional
\phi
on
\mathcal{A}
such that

\Lambda(f)=\phi(f(w))\quad\text{for every }f\in\mathcal{X}.

Proof.

Clearly, the restriction
\Lambda:\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
3.1
. Then, using the moreover part of Theorem
3.1
, there exist
w\in D
and a multiplicative linear functional
\phi
on
\mathcal{A}
such that
\Lambda(p)=\phi(p(w))
for every

p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}
. Now the continuity of
\Lambda
and the denseness of
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}
in
\mathcal{X}
yield

\Lambda(f)=\phi(f(w))\quad\text{for all }f\in\mathcal{X}.

∎

Theorem 3.8
.

For
0<p,q\leq\infty,
let

be a continuous function, where we consider weak-start topology on

Suppose
T(0)=0,\ T(1)(w)\neq 0
for each
w\in\mathbb{D}^{m}
. If given any pair

of complex-valued polynomials in
n
-variables and

the polynomial

\big(Tp(w)-Tq(w)\big)1-\big(T1(w)\big)(p-q)

has a zero in
\mathbb{D}^{n}
, then there exist
\psi\in H^{q}(\mathbb{D}^{m})
and an analytic function
\phi:\mathbb{D}^{m}\to\mathbb{D}^{n}
such that

T(f)=\psi\cdot(f\circ\phi)

for every
f\in H^{p}(\mathbb{D}^{n}).

Proof.

For each fixed
w\in\mathbb{D}^{m},
define the map
T_{w}:H^{p}(\mathbb{D}^{n})\to\mathbb{C}
given by

. It is easy to see that
T_{w}/(T1)(w)
satisfies all the conditions in Corollary
3.6
, so there exists
\zeta\in\mathbb{D}^{n}
such that
(Tf)(w)=(T1)(w)f(\zeta)
for all

. By taking

, the

coordinate function, we get
\zeta=(Tz_{1}(w),\dots,Tz_{n}(w))/(T1)(w)

4.
A Multiplicative GKZ Theorem for Hardy spaces

In this section, we shall investigate a multiplicative analogue of the GKZ theorem within the context of Hardy spaces. By a multiplicative analogue of the GKZ theorem, we mean a result that asserts the linearity of a complex-valued multiplicative function on an unital Banach algebra that assumes a value in the spectrum. The first positive result concerning multiplicative maps taking values in the spectrum was obtained by Maouche
[
7
]
.

Theorem 4.1

(Maouche)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a unital Banach algebra and
\phi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{C}
be a map satisfying

(i)

\phi(x)\in\sigma(x)
,

(ii)

\phi(xy)=\phi(x)\phi(y)

for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
. Then there exists a unique character
\psi_{\phi}
on
\mathcal{A}
such that
\phi(x)=\psi_{\phi}(x)
for all
x\in G_{1}(\mathcal{A})
, where
G_{1}(\mathcal{A})
is the connected component of
G(\mathcal{A})
containing the identity.

In the same paper
[
7
]
, Maouche gave a concrete example to establish that even on the nicest Banach algebra, namely
C[0,1]
, one can’t guarantee the linearity of
\phi
on the entire Banach algebra. It is noteworthy that Maouche’s function was not continuous; hence, any hope of proving that
\phi
itself is linear, therefore, a character would have to include the continuity of
\phi
as an assumption.

Recently, Tou
\acute{\rm r}
e and Brits, along with their other collaborators, have extensively studied multiplicative versions of the GKZ and its generalization–the KS theorem–on
C^{*}
-algebras; for their specific results refer to
[
1
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. In
[
1
]
, they showed that a multiplicative continuous complex-valued function
F
on a
C^{*}
-algebra
A
satisfying
F(a)\in\sigma(a)
must be linear. However, the result for a general Banach algebra stays where Maouche left it.

We study an analogous result for the Hardy spaces. Before we give the statement, we would like to note that, in light of Theorem
1.3
, a non-zero continuous multiplicative linear functional on a Hardy space must be a point evaluation.

Theorem 4.2
.

For
0<p<\infty
, let
F:H^{p}(\mathbb{D})\to\mathbb{C}
be a continuous function such that
F(f)\in Im(f)
and
F(fg)=F(f)F(g)
, whenever
f,g\in H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
with
fg\in H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
. Then there exists a point
w\in\mathbb{D}
such that
F(f)=f(w)
for every nowhere-vanishing
f\in H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
.

Proof.

We consider the restriction of the map
F
to the disc algebra
A(\mathbb{D})
(a subset of
H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
). The restriction of the map
F
to
A(\mathbb{D})
(equipped with the sup-norm) satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
4.1
. As a result, there exists a character
\psi_{F}
on
A(\mathbb{D})
such that
F(f)=\psi_{F}(f)
for every
f\in G_{1}(A(\mathbb{D})),
the connected component of the set of invertible functions in
A(\mathbb{D})
containing the constant function
1
. But,
G_{1}(A(\mathbb{D}))
equals the set of all invertible functions in
A(\mathbb{D})
. Indeed, a simple adaptation of the arguments used in
[
9
, Lemma 3.5.14]
can be used to establish this fact. Therefore, we conclude that
F(f)=\psi_{F}(f)
for each invertible function in
A(\mathbb{D}).

Furthermore, the characters on the disc algebra are point evaluations, which implies that there exists a point
w\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
such that

F(f)=\psi_{F}(f)=f(w)
for every invertible
f\in A(\mathbb{D}).
Now,
g(z)=z+2
is an invertible function in
A(\mathbb{D});
therefore,
F(g)=\psi_{F}(g)=w+2.
On the other hand, by the hypotheses,
F(g)=a+2
for some
a\in\mathbb{D}
. Thus, we conclude that
w=a\in\mathbb{D}.

Finally, let
f\in H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
with no zero in
\mathbb{D}
. For
0<r<1
, let us define
f_{r}(z)=f(rz),\ z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. Then each
f_{r}
is in
H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
and
f_{r}\to f
in
H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
as
r\to 1.

Since
f
is analytic on
\mathbb{D}
and has no zero in
\mathbb{D}
, each
f_{r}
is an invertible function in
A(\mathbb{D})
. Hence,

F(f_{r})=\psi_{F}(f_{r})=f_{r}(w)
for every
r.
Lastly, the continuity of
F
yields
F(f)=\lim\limits_{r\to{1^{-}}}F(f_{r})=\lim\limits_{r\to{1^{-}}}f_{r}(w)=f(w).
This completes the proof.
∎
