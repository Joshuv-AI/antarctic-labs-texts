Clinical and laboratory predictors for bacteremia in critically ill calves
Authors: Mathilde L Pas, Jade Bokma, Filip Boyen, Bart Pardon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis is a main contributor to calf mortality, but diagnosis is difficult.ObjectivesDevelop and validate a predictive model for bacteremia in critically ill calves (CIC).AnimalsA total of 334 CIC, sampled for blood culture.MethodsCross‐sectional study. Multivariable logistic regression and classification tree analysis on clinical, ultrasonographic, and laboratory variables were performed on a dataset including all animals. Model validation was done on 30% of the dataset. Similar statistics (except validation) were performed on a subset of the database (n = 143), in which presumed contaminants were excluded.ResultsThe best performing model to predict bacteremia, taking all detected bacteria into account, included tachypnea, tachycardia, acidemia, hypoglycemia, venous hypoxemia, and hypoproteinemia. Sensitivity and specificity of this model were 70.6% and 98.0%, respectively, but decreased to 61.5% and 91.7% during model validation. The best‐performing model, excluding presumed contaminants, included abnormal temperature, heart rate, absence of enteritis, hypocalcemia, and hyperlactatemia as risk factors for bacteremia. Sensitivity and specificity of this model were 71.4% and 93.9%, respectively. Both classification trees performed less well in comparison to logistic regression. The classification tree excluding presumed contaminants, featured hypoglycemia, absence of diarrhea, and hyperlactatemia as risk factors for bacteremia. Sensitivity and specificity were 39.4% and 92.7%, respectively.Conclusions and Clinical ImportanceHypoglycemia, hyperlactatemia, and hypoproteinemia seem relevant in assessing bacteremia in CIC. The performance of these models based on basic clinical and blood variables remains insufficient to predict bacteremia.Keywords: antimicrobial stewardship, biomarkers, blood culture, prediction model, sepsis

INTRODUCTION

Sepsis is a life‐threatening condition, with an estimated prevalence of 30% in both diarrheic and critically ill calves (CIC). 1 , 2 , 3 The 2 key therapeutic measures recommended for treating sepsis in both humans and cattle involve rapid IV administered broad‐spectrum antimicrobials and fluid therapy. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Taking this into consideration, critically important antimicrobials still play an important role in the treatment of septicemic calves in many European countries, although under legally regulated conditions. In contrast, the United States completely prohibits the use of fluoroquinolones and permits the use of ceftiofur only under strict conditions. 7 , 8 In compliance with antimicrobial stewardship, and considering pressure from the government and public opinion to decrease the use of critically important antimicrobials, unnecessary treatment should be avoided in animals. 7 In human medicine, blood culture (BC) is considered a reference method for the diagnosis of sepsis, pathogen identification, and susceptibility testing. 9 In calves, a positive BC significantly increases mortality risk. 3 Although BC and subsequent susceptibility testing are helpful in optimizing antimicrobial treatment, it has important disadvantages such as imperfect sensitivity and specificity, as well as a long turnover time (>24 hours) until results become available. 9 , 10 Because it is known that delayed initiation of antimicrobial treatment increases the mortality risk in humans and similar concerns are raised in cattle, refraining from antimicrobial administration in calves with genuine sepsis until diagnosis could compromise animal welfare. 11 , 12 , 13

To bridge the gap between initial presentation and sepsis confirmation by BC, there is a need for practical calf‐side tests or decision support tools, which can provide a fast and reliable diagnosis, aiding in the decision to use or not use critically important antimicrobial products. Sepsis‐scoring models can be used as such supporting tools. Although previous sepsis‐scoring models have provided interesting results, they lack validation on external populations. 1 , 2 Furthermore, these studies mainly focus on diarrheic calves, which might, according to recent research, not be the most clinically relevant risk group. 1 , 2 , 14 Certain blood variables and biomarkers could be used as such rapid calf‐side tools. Studies have emphasized the importance of impaired cellular metabolism and circulatory dysfunction in calves with septicemia, which is typically associated with hyperlactatemia and hypoglycemia, although such blood variables tend to have low specificity. 4 , 15 Nevertheless, it is uncertain to what extent these blood variables can be used in prediction models for bacteremia in CIC. In addition, research on more specific biomarkers is ongoing to identify sepsis (eg, procalcitonin), but these biomarkers currently are not accessible to practitioners in field conditions. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20

Therefore, our objective was to develop and validate a predictive model for bacteremia in CIC based on clinical signs and calf‐side laboratory variables.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and study sample

A cross‐sectional study was conducted using the medical records of CIC routinely sampled for BC, and presented at the Large Animal Internal Medicine clinic. Data were collected retrospectively for the period February 2017 to December 2021. Between January 2021 and December 2023, data were prospectively collected. Inclusion criteria for CIC were age <130 days, severe respiratory signs (tachypnea or dyspnea), cardiovascular abnormalities (tachy‐ or bradycardia, prolonged capillary refill time, or prolonged filling of jugular vein), neurological signs (abnormal posture or mental state) or some combination of these. As a referral clinic, the study sample included a substantial number of animals that were previously unsuccessfully treated by an ambulatory veterinarian and in a clinically advanced state.

Examination

Information was gathered on demographic, historical, clinical, ultrasonographic, and laboratory examinations conducted on the day of BC sampling. Historical information regarding previous treatment with antimicrobials, corticosteroids, and non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) was collected during history taking. The studied demographic and clinical variables were breed, sex, behavior, color of mucosal membranes, capillary refill time, skin turgor, presence of swollen or painful joints, swollen or warm jugular veins, diarrhea and umbilical inflammation. Ultrasonography was performed as previously described, and information concerning the umbilicus, the presence of enteritis or pneumonia, and other internal infections (hepatitis, abomasitis, peritonitis, phlebitis, splenitis, nephritis) was included. 3 Pneumonia was defined as the presence of a consolidation regardless of depth and width. However, to be considered a focal infection, only pneumonia with consolidation ≥1 cm was included (bronchopneumonia pattern). Other infections included in focal infection were omphalitis, arthritis, hepatitis, abomasitis, peritonitis, phlebitis, splenitis, or otitis. Blood was sampled out of the jugular vein using a partially evacuated blood collection tube system (Venoject, Terumo, Leuven, Belgium) with a 4 mL heparin tube for blood gas analysis and analyzed within 5 minutes (RAPIDPoint 405 and RAPIDPoint 500e, Siemens Healthcare, Beersel, Belgium), with EDTA (4 mL tube) for hematology (IDEXX ProCyte Dx Hematology Analyzer, IDEXX Europe B.V., Hoofddorp, The Netherlands), whereas serum (8.5 mL) was used for biochemical analysis (IDEXX Catalyst One Chemistry Analyzer, IDEXX Europe B.V., Hoofddorp, The Netherlands). Standard biochemical and hematologic analyses were performed within 30 minutes of sampling, and samples were stored at ambient temperature in the meantime. Laboratory information included venous blood variables; pH, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO2), partial pressure of oxygen (pO2), bicarbonate (HCO3), packed cell volume (PCV), base excess (BE), glucose, sodium, potassium, ionized calcium, chloride, L‐lactate, urea, creatinine, total protein, gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT), aspartate transaminase (AST), bilirubin, leukocytes, neutrophils, lymphocytes and thrombocytes. Lastly, the diagnosed disease, the presence of multiple morbidities, survival or mortality during hospitalization (and information regarding natural death or euthanasia, and whether death occurred within 5 days of hospitalization), were included in the database.

Blood culture sampling

Blood for cultures was obtained from the opposite jugular vein from which the heparin, serum, and EDTA samples were collected. The jugular vein was aseptically prepared as previously described. 10 Sterile gloves and a 2‐needle technique (21G) were used for aseptic sampling, in combination with disinfecting the lid of the culture flask. Aerobic enriched media flasks were filled to their maximum recommended volume (BACTEC Plus Aerobic medium with 10 mL of blood or BD BACTEC Peds Plus medium with 3 mL of blood; BD, Erembodegem, Belgium). Subsequently, the bottles were processed as previously described. 10 Blood culture media were considered positive if signaled by an automated BACTEC FX system.

Statistical analysis

Data were divided into 2 datasets, for which the inclusion and exclusion criteria are outlined in Figure 1. The first dataset, named “all bacteria” included all calves sampled for BC and bacteremia was defined as a positive BC regardless of the type of isolated bacterium. The second dataset, named “excluding contaminants” included only calves sampled for 2 BC media. In this dataset, the bacteria isolated from the BC were interpreted according to previously described guidelines, 10 and subsequently assumed contaminants were classified as negative BC. The gathered information was entered into Excel and then transferred to SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0. Armonk, New York: IBM Corp) for statistical analysis.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria of critically ill calves sampled for blood cultures in both databases “all bacteria” (N = 334) and “excluding contaminants” (N = 143). BC, blood culture; CIC, critically ill calves.

The dataset “all bacteria” (n = 334) was randomly divided into a training (70%, n = 234) and validation (30%, n = 100) set. Binary logistic regression was performed to determine predictive factors for bacteremia on the training dataset. The association between bacteremia and the presence of multiple morbidities, as well as prior treatment, also were examined for informative reasons by means of logistic regression. The model‐building procedure was as follows: First, variables were tested univariably for their association with bacteremia. Variables were tested continuously (when possible) or categorically (cut‐offs for continuous variables determined with receiver operator characteristic analyses). All variables with P < .20 and with >25 measurements in the composed subcategories were retained for the multivariable procedure. Subsequently, both a clinical and combination (clinical + laboratory) multivariable logistic regression (MLR) model was built, using stepwise backward selection (gradually excluding non‐significant variables). Using the Pearson correlation test, continuous variables were assessed, and when >0.6, the most significant variable was retained for further modeling. For categorical variables, logistic regression was used to determine association. Missing values were excluded from the analyses. Significance was set at P < .05. Afterwards, validation of the models formed with MLR was performed on the remaining 30% of the dataset. Model fit was evaluated using Hosmer‐Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit. For calves ≤7 days of age, an additional univariate logistic analysis was performed to check for the association between bacteremia and total protein concentration.

In a second approach to analyze the database “all bacteria” (n = 334), a classification tree was constructed. Maximum tree depth was determined automatically. Growth limits were set as 20 in the parent node and 10 in the child node. Impurity was measured using the Gini index (minimum change in improvement 0.0001), maximizing the homogeneity of child nodes. No pruning was applied. Missing values were excluded, as surrogates were set at 0. For this classification tree, split‐sample validation was performed (the same 70%‐30% partitioning as in MLR).

For the second dataset “excluding contaminants” (n = 143), the same univariate and MLR analyses were repeated as described above. For this database, no split‐sample validation was possible because of an insufficient number of observations. The previous classification tree analyses were repeated on the database “excluding contaminants,” with as the sole difference being the use of cross validation instead of split‐sample validation (because of too few observations).

Performance of the different models was assessed using sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV; calculated using Winepi), 21 positive likelihood ratio (LR+, calculated as sensitivity/[1 − specificity]), negative likelihood ratio (LR−, calculated as [1 − sensitivity]/specificity) and the Youden Index (YI, calculated as [sensitivity + specificity] − 1).

Information on total bilirubin, GGT, AST, and blood count was scarce, and thus these values were only included in univariable analysis, and not in the multivariable models.

Blood culture

In model “all bacteria” 33.2% (111/334) of the calves had positive BC (Figure 1). In the “excluding contaminants” model, 23.1% (33/143) had positive BC. In model “all bacteria,” 50.5% of the positive BC samples contained gram‐negative bacteria, 27.2% contained gram‐positive bacteria, 7.2% contained polybacterial results, and in 14.4% no pathogen identification was available. In the model “excluding contaminants,” 87.9% of the bacteria were gram‐negative and 12.1% was gram‐positive.

The isolated bacteria included in the “excluding contaminants” model (n = 33) were Escherichia coli (18), Salmonella enterica (5), Raoultella ornithinolytica (2), Enterobacter cloacae (1), Klebsiella oxytoca (1), Campylobacter jejuni (1), Trueperella pyogenes (1), Streptococcus uberis (1), Staphylococcus xylosus (1), Staphylococcus sciuri (1) and 1 BC included both S. enterica and E. coli (1).

Diseases in CIC

The diseases found in all CIC, combined with the average age in which they occurred, can be found in Table 3. The most frequent disease was enteritis/diarrhea (53%), but only in 16.8% of the cases it was the sole morbidity. Of the calves with enteritis/diarrhea, 28.6% had positive BC. Pneumonia was the second most frequent condition (42%) in which 10.2% of cases it was the sole morbidity. Of the animals with only pneumonia, 44.1% had positive BC. Calves with impaired lung maturation (6.3%; neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, premature), although critically ill, had BC positivity of only 9.5%.

Predictive factors associated with bacteremia in CIC

Logistic regression

The univariable clinical, ultrasonographic, and laboratory variables significantly associated with BC positivity (database “all bacteria”) or with presence of likely true pathogens (database “excluding contaminants”) are shown in Table 4. Sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of potentially interesting univariate biomarkers were: 44%, 79%, and 68% for hyperlactatemia (>9.2 mmol/L) and 0%, 100%, and 68% for hypoglycemia (<74 mg/dL) for the database “all bacteria.” For the database “excluding contaminants,” sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy for hyperlactatemia (>7.7 mmol/L) were 0%, 100%, and 77% and for hypoglycemia (<59 mg/dL) were 0%, 100%, and 77%, respectively. Decision tree analysis

The decision tree for risk factors associated with BC positivity built on the training set (N = 234) of the dataset “all bacteria” resulted in 8 different usable variables with an increased risk for bacteremia (Figure 2). An increased bacteremia risk was observed when the glucose concentration was ≤66.5 mg/dL, abnormal behavior was present, respiratory rate was >26 breaths per minute, potassium concentration was ≤4.52 mEq/L, and an abnormal mucosal membrane color (pale/hyperemic) was observed. When glucose concentration was >67 mg/dL, age >21 days, L‐lactate >7.3 mmol/L, and bicarbonate ≤19.3 mmol/L, a higher risk for BC positivity was observed. This tree had a sensitivity of 59.0%, a specificity of 83.3%, and an accuracy of 75.2%. The YI was 0.42.

Classification tree for risk factors associated with blood culture positivity built on the training set (N = 234) of the database including all bacteria (N = 334) with a sensitivity of 59%, specificity of 83.3%, and accuracy of 75.2%. The positive and negative likelihood ratio for this tree were 3.53 and 0.49, respectively. BC, blood culture.

Validation of the decision tree containing all bacteria with the use of split sampling (70%‐30%) resulted in a sensitivity of 30.3%, a specificity of 77.7%, an accuracy of 62.0%, and a YI of 0.08.

The decision tree for risk factors associated with bacteremia when excluding contaminants (N = 143) resulted in 3 different usable variables to predict bacteremia (Figure 3). When the glucose concentration was ≤59 mg/dL, the risk of bacteremia was higher. In this subgroup (with glucose concentration ≤58.50 mg/dL), the calves presented with diarrhea had decreased risk for bacteremia compared with those without diarrhea. When glucose concentration was >59 mg/dL, calves with L‐lactate >7.3 mmol/L had higher risk of bacteremia. This tree had a sensitivity of 39.4%, a specificity of 92.7%, and an accuracy of 80.4%. The YI was 0.32.

DISCUSSION

Our objective was to identify factors associated with bacteremia for a rapid and reliable diagnosis of bacteremia in CIC, and subsequently make and validate a prediction model. Before discussing the main findings, some limitations of the study design should be discussed, to allow correct interpretation of the study results. A first limitation is the retrospective nature of the majority of the study. This design resulted in certain promising variables (eg, total protein, lactate, WBC count, kidney and liver function test results) being measured in a smaller number of cases. Lactate was only routinely measured from October 2019 onwards, but still was present in 2 final models. Unfortunately, this resulted in exclusion of WBC count, as well as kidney and liver function test results from all multivariable models, causing potentially important variables to be lost. Creatinine, GGT, AST, and leukocytes appeared promising on univariable analysis, but further research including more data is required to incorporate these variables in multivariable models. Another limitation is that because of these missing data, the exact effect of certain factors (eg, venous pO2 tension) is uncertain, because wide confidence intervals (CI) were observed in the final models. In addition, for both venous pO2 and pCO2, the adverse effect of a polypropylene vacutainer system on the stability of the measurements is well‐known. 22 Furthermore, a 4 mL heparin tube was used in the study, which is less stable for pCO2 than tubes with a small air‐to‐blood volume ratio (2‐3 mL). 23 However, we suspect that because the blood gas analyses were performed immediately calf‐side, the discrepancies between the measurements likely were minimal.

Another limitation is that guidelines used in humans recommend obtaining at least 2 BC sets (1 set includes 2 BC flasks; 1 aerobic and 1 anaerobic), 6 and because of economic constraints, doing so was not feasible in our study, nor in food producing animals in general. Thus, throughout the study period, 2 BC samples were not always obtained, which limited the “true pathogen” database in which likely contaminants were excluded, resulting in our not being able to validate these final models. When calves were double sampled, the decision was made to collect 2 separate aerobic flasks, instead of 1 aerobic and 1 anaerobic, to facilitate exclusion of contaminants. Anaerobic flasks were omitted from this study because there is still limited information available on strictly anaerobic pathogens that cause sepsis in calves. We cannot exclude potentially having missed anaerobes, viruses, and other pathogens that might cause sepsis. A last limitation is the presence of many Belgian Blue calves in the database, which may decrease the relevance of our observations to other breeds. Despite these limitations, to our knowledge, our study is the largest available to predict bacteremia in CIC. Furthermore, our study offers several novel features such as the inclusion of ultrasonography in the evaluation of the animals, as well as determining relevant variables after the exclusion of contaminants by obtaining 2 BC, and attempting to validate the established models.

Promising factors to predict sepsis were identified, especially those appearing in multiple final models, such as hypoglycemia and hyperlactatemia. Both are laboratory variables that can be measured rapidly calf‐side using handheld devices (although validation is required). Because both glucose and lactate previously were suggested to be associated with sepsis, 4 , 14 these likely have high external validity. The cut‐offs used in our study were determined using ROC or classification tree analysis, and thus were tailored to the database, and they might differ somewhat using a different study sample.

Hypoglycemia also previously was associated with bacteremia and sepsis in foals, and with endotoxemia in calves. 4 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 A previous study evaluated glucose as a calf‐side test in comparison to BC, which showed poor performance as a single test. 10 We also found a very poor sensitivity (0%) for both databases if hypoglycemia was used alone to predict bacteremia. Nonetheless, in combination with other variables in the MLR models or the classification tree, it has potential. The main reason for low blood glucose concentration in septicemic animals is suggested to be because of impaired cellular function. 14 Thus, its use as an early sepsis predictor is debatable, because cellular impairment is associated with a shock‐like state. Also, hypoglycemia previously was associated with increased mortality in CIC, which suggests that hypoglycemia might be manifested in advanced stages of disease. 3 , 29 Important information missing in our dataset was the time from last nursing, which could affect blood glucose concentration. Given the critical state of the animals, it was assumed that few calves nursed shortly before presentation, and the resulting impact on our dataset was considered negligible.

To the our knowledge, ours is the first study establishing an association between hyperlactatemia and bacteremia in CIC, despite this association being well‐known in human medicine. 30 , 31 Similar to hypoglycemia, hyperlactatemia likely can be attributed to insufficient nutrients at the cellular level. 4 This deficiency is presumably also the cause of its association with mortality in sick calves. 3 , 29 , 32 Guidelines in human medicine recommend measuring lactate concentration in patients with suspected sepsis, both for screening purposes as well as for the well‐established association of hyperlactatemia with mortality. 33 As a stand‐alone univariate biomarker, the performance (sensitivity, 44%; specificity, 79%) again was weak. Neither lactate nor glucose appear to be useful early stage sepsis predictors. However, they might be useful to determine whether treatment is still worthwhile in septicemic animals, keeping in mind economic and welfare considerations.

Diarrhea/enteritis was a leading cause of critical illness in our study sample. However, presence of diarrhea statistically decreases the odds of bacteremia in some multivariable models or trees, meaning that in our study sample of CIC, the calves with diarrhea did not appear to have the highest sepsis risk. Earlier studies on sepsis developed specific score models for diarrheic calves and diarrheic calves were always assumed to be the at‐risk population. 1 , 2 Nevertheless, other recent literature also suggests a lower rate of bacteremia in diarrhetic calves (not CIC) compared to these previous studies. 1 , 2 , 14 Another frequent disease and important risk factor for bacteremia in the models including all bacteria was pneumonia. Nevertheless, this association disappeared when using the dataset “excluding contaminants.” However, we suspect that pneumonia can be a relevant risk factor for bacteremia, and more research is encouraged using a larger dataset excluding contaminants, because ours is only the first study to assess pneumonia with ultrasonography in relation to bacteremia.

The sepsis risk in CIC appears to be higher in older calves (>21 days), which is in accordance with a previous sepsis prediction model, although a lower cut‐off was established in that study (>7 days). 1 We do not attribute this finding to a decreased risk for sepsis at a younger age, but to other reasons for critical illness in susceptible neonates (eg, NRDS in Belgian Blue calves), and fewer potential differential diagnoses for their critically ill condition in older animals.

Heart rate was included in both the model “all bacteria” as well as the model “excluding contaminants.” The cut‐offs were determined using ROC analyses. Unexpectedly, this analysis resulted in a higher risk for bacteremia in animals that did not have tachy‐ or bradycardia. This finding suggests that the systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) criterion heart rate (>120 bpm) be reconsidered as a risk factor for sepsis. 29 Body temperature in our study (38.5°C‐40.1°C), had a similar tendency as the suggested temperature (38.5°C‐39.5°C) for SIRS. 29 Tachypnea also was a risk factor for bacteremia in our study. Compared to previously suggested cut‐offs for SIRS (>36 breaths per minute), in our study, a higher cut‐off was established for logistic regression (>50 breaths per minute), but lower in the decision tree (>26 breaths per minute). 29 As in human medicine, SIRS does not seem to be a good criterion to assess the sepsis risk in CIC, but further research is warranted. 33 Behavior, a last clinical variable included in the decision tree including all bacteria, seems to be a relevant variable to assess sepsis risk, and it also holds importance in human medicine in the diagnosis of sepsis. 31 In cattle, however, interpreting mental state could be more challenging, because a main cause of neurological signs in diarrhetic calves is D‐lactatemia. 29 , 34 Other research showed that lethargic diarrheic calves have a higher risk of bacteremia compared with those with normal mental state. Thus, even in diarrheic calves evaluating mental status could be valuable when determining bacteremia risk. 14

Total protein concentration had a significant univariable association with bacteremia in both databases. It was solely excluded from the MLR “excluding contaminants” model because of too few measurements. Total protein concentration previously was associated with sepsis directly, as well as incorporated in failure of transfer of passive immunity, as a good indirect measurement for estimating immunoglobulins G in calves <1 week of age. 2 , 25 , 35 We specifically evaluated the association between bacteremia and hypoproteinemia in calves ≤7 days of age. Interestingly, the significant association between total protein concentration and bacteremia disappeared, and thus in our dataset the link with lack of passive immunity could not be demonstrated.

With respect to blood gas analysis, acidemia previously was linked with endotoxemia in calves, partly caused by L‐lactatemia secondary to sepsis. 22 However, in calves with diarrhea, D‐lactatemia also should be considered. 24 , 29 Both metabolic and respiratory acidosis previously have been associated with increased mortality, with septicemic calves included in both study populations. 3 , 29 In our study, very low venous pO2 was associated with bacteremia, likely caused by cellular impairment, peripheral hypoxemia, and organ damage, because the effect of sepsis on oxygen delivery and consumption is well known. 36 , 37 , 38 Another hypothesis could be that venous hypoxemia can originate from severe pneumonia, possibly resulting in bacteremia, because tissue inflammation and damage in the lungs might increase the chance of bacteria entering the bloodstream.

Another variable that should be considered in calves suspected of sepsis would be serum ionized calcium concentration because it was included in the model “excluding contaminants.” The main portion of calcium is bound to albumin, and low serum total calcium concentration often is seen in critically ill patients because of loss of albumin, but decreased serum ionized calcium concentration also has been associated with bacteremia in critically ill human patients. 39 In humans, this finding is suggested to be caused by parathyroid gland suppression, failure to synthesize active vitamin D, calcium chelation or sequestration, or magnesium imbalance. 39 However, in our study, a more likely cause for low serum ionized calcium concentration is the anorexia seen in many CIC, which is associated with a decreased suckle reflex, previously suggested as a sepsis predictor. 2 Additionally, earlier research demonstrated that serum ionized calcium concentration <2.34 mEq/L decreased the odds of survival, suggesting that low serum ionized calcium concentration might predict both sepsis and poor prognosis in calves. 3 However, neither of these studies adjusted the ionized calcium concentration for pH and chloride, as previously recommended. 40 Although the clinical relevance of in vivo changes in pH and chloride on the in vivo serum ionized calcium concentration is small according to above‐mentioned study, not adjusting these variables should be considered a limitation in accurately assessing serum ionized calcium concentration.

Previously, an association was found between endotoxemia and leukopenia, as well as endotoxemia and thrombocytopenia. 24 In our univariable results, the same association was seen for bacteremia and leukopenia and thrombocytopenia. Because hematology was not performed in a sufficient number of calves in our study, these variables could not be withheld in the final multivariable model. Leukopenia and neutropenia are assumed to be associated with sepsis and bacteremia in calves. 2 , 25 , 29 Regardless, these variables were not included in previous scoring models in the veterinary literature, contrary to the presence of toxic changes in neutrophils or the presence of immature neutrophils. 2 , 25 , 29 Although both neutropenia and leukopenia significantly increased the odds of bacteremia in our study, the sensitivity of these variables was insufficient as stand‐alone tests.

Despite highlighting interesting variables in our study, the performance of all sculpted models remains limited. The low sensitivity means that the recognition of bacteremic calves is insufficient, which is unfavorable for animal welfare, given that septicemic animals are missed and would not be treated. However, the value of the present models lies in their high specificity, which implies that non‐septicemic calves rarely have false‐positive results. High specificity is beneficial in the context of rational antimicrobial use because unnecessary treatment can be avoided. The LR+ of combination model 2, 3, and 4 was >10, which indicates a large increase in post‐test probability of the disease. In contrast, both clinical models and combination model 1 (MLR), as well as both decision trees, had LR+ between 5 and 10, reflecting only a moderate increase in post‐test probability. 41

We believe that the prevalence of bacteremia (±30%) in sick calves is too high to delay initiation of antimicrobials until susceptibility results are available. 1 , 2 , 3 , 10 This is particularly true when there is a strong suspicion of sepsis or evidence of other inflammatory processes (eg, pneumonia). Despite the low sensitivity of our established prediction models, the incorporated variables should be given careful attention during examination of CIC. In cases with strong suspicion of sepsis, we recommend starting empirical IV bactericidal broad‐spectrum antimicrobial treatment, and de‐escalating treatment based on microbial culture results and susceptibility tests, as suggested in human medicine. 5 , 6

In conclusion, taking the limitations of our study into account, we conclude that the development of a sepsis score solely on clinical signs is not accurate enough. The use of models combining clinical signs and laboratory variables (such as hypoglycemia, hyperlactatemia and hypoproteinemia) seems promising, but sensitivity remains too low. Hence, as in human medicine, improvement in sepsis diagnosis may need to arise from other or novel biomarkers. Despite imperfect performance, the decision tree in particular can be informative and aid in the shared treatment decision‐making process between client and veterinarian when confronted with CIC.
