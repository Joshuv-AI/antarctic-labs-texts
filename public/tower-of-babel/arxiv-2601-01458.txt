Abstract
. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra (FTA) asserts that every complex polynomial has as many complex roots, counted with multiplicities, as its degree. A probabilistic analogue of this theorem for real roots of real polynomials—sometimes referred to as the Kac theorem—was found between 1938 and 1943 by J. Littlewood, A. Offord, and M. Kac. In this paper, we present several more versions of FTA: Kac type FTA for Laurent polynomials in one and many variables, Kac type FTA for polynomials on complex reductive groups arising in the context of compact group representations (similar to Laurent polynomials arising in torus representation theory), and FTA for exponential sums in one and many variables. In the case of Laurent polynomials, the result, even in the one-dimensional case, is unexpected: most of the zeros of a real Laurent polynomial are real. This text is a supplemented and more detailed version of [ 33 ] .



1.
Introduction

A probabilistic refinement of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra (FTA),
concerning the real roots of random real polynomials and sometimes referred to as

Kac theorem
,
was found between 1938 and 1943 by J. Littlewood, A. Offord, and M. Kac;
see
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
The underlying observation is elementary:
for any integer
m
, the probability that a root of a real polynomial of degree
m

is real is strictly positive.
In particular, for
m=1
this probability equals
1
.

Theorem 1.1

(Kac)
.

Let
P
be a random real polynomial of degree
m
in one variable whose coefficients
are independent Gaussian random variables with zero mean and unit variance.
Then, as
m\to\infty
, the expected number of real roots of
P
is asymptotic to

\frac{2}{\pi}\log m
.
Equivalently,

\mathcal{P}(m)\asymp\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\log m}{m},

where
\mathcal{P}(m)
denotes the probability that a root of
P
is real.

Remark 1.1
.

For refined results on the distribution of the number of real roots of random real polynomials,
we refer to
[
5
]
and the references therein.

Remark 1.2
.

If the expected value of the
k
th coefficient is taken to be
\binom{m}{k}
,
then in Kac’s theorem the logarithmic term
\log m
is replaced by
\sqrt{m}
.

In the present paper we establish Kac-type results for Laurent polynomials in one and several variables,
as well as for polynomials on complex reductive groups arising naturally in the representation theory
of compact groups.
The latter class may be viewed as an analogue of Laurent polynomials
associated with torus representations.
A notable feature of this setting is that,
in contrast with the classical polynomial case,
the probability that a root is real converges to a nonzero limit as the degree tends to infinity.
For example, in the one-variable Laurent case this limit equals
1/\sqrt{3}
;
see Corollary
2.1
(c).

In §
4
we further formulate and prove an analogue of the FTA for systems of equations
defined by exponential sums in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Since such systems have infinitely many solutions,
the corresponding statement takes the form of an explicit density formula
for the zero set.
This result is analogous to the Bernstein–Kushnirenko–Khovanskii formula
for polynomial systems.
The material of §
4
is independent of §
2
and §
3
.

2.
Kac type theorem for Laurent polynomials

2.1.
Laurent polynomials of one variable

Recall that a Laurent polynomial is a function on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0

of the form

P(z)=\sum_{m\in\Lambda}a_{m}z^{m},

where
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{Z}}
is a finite set, called the
spectrum
of
P
.

Definiton 2.1
.

(1)

A Laurent polynomial
P
is called
real
if its restriction to the unit circle

S\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is real-valued.

(2)

A root of
P
lying on
S
is called a
real root
of the Laurent polynomial.

The following assertions are immediate consequences of Definition
2.1
.

Lemma 2.1
.

(1)

A Laurent polynomial
P(z)=\sum a_{k}z^{k}
is real if and only if

a_{k}=\overline{a_{-k}}\qquad\text{for all }k\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

In particular, the spectrum of a real Laurent polynomial is centrally symmetric.

(2)

The set of roots of a real Laurent polynomial is invariant under the involution

z\mapsto\bar{z}^{-1}
.

(3)

Restriction to
S
defines an isomorphism between the space of real Laurent polynomials
with spectrum
\Lambda
and the space of trigonometric polynomials with the same spectrum,
namely functions of the form

(2.1)

\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\bigl(\alpha_{k}\cos(k\theta)+\beta_{k}\sin(k\theta)\bigr),\qquad\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Definiton 2.2

(Random real Laurent polynomials)
.

Let
{\rm Trig}(\Lambda)
denote the space of trigonometric polynomials of the form (
2.1
).
We regard
{\rm Trig}(\Lambda)
as a finite-dimensional subspace of
L_{2}(S)
,
endowed with the inner product

(f,g)=\int_{S}f(\theta)g(\theta)\,d\theta.

Random real Laurent polynomials with spectrum
\Lambda

are defined by equipping
{\rm Trig}(\Lambda)
with the Gaussian probability measure
associated with this
L_{2}
-metric.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f_{\Lambda}
be a random real Laurent polynomial with spectrum
\Lambda
.
Then:

(a)

The expected number of real roots of
f_{\Lambda}
equals

2\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{\#\Lambda}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\lambda^{2}},

where
\#\Lambda
denotes the cardinality of
\Lambda
.

(b)

The probability
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
that a root of
f_{\Lambda}
is real is given by

\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)=\frac{1}{\deg(f_{\Lambda})}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\#\Lambda}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\lambda^{2}},

where
\deg(f_{\Lambda})=\max_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|\lambda|
.

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
\Lambda=\{-m,\ldots,0,\ldots,m\}
, and let
f_{m}
be a random real Laurent polynomial
with spectrum
\Lambda
, i.e. a random real Laurent polynomial of degree
m
.
Then:

(a)

The expected number of real roots of
f_{m}
equals

2\sqrt{\frac{m(m+1)}{3}}.

(b)

The probability
\mathcal{P}(f_{m})
that a root of
f_{m}
is real equals

\sqrt{\frac{m+1}{3m}}.

(c)

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{P}(f_{m})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}.

Proof.

This follows from the identity

\sum_{k=1}^{m}k^{2}=\frac{m(m+1)(2m+1)}{6}.

∎

Remark 2.1
.

Since
1/\sqrt{3}>1/2
, Corollary
2.1
(c) implies that
most zeros of a random real Laurent polynomial are real.

Remark 2.2
.

The statements of Corollary
2.1
are equivalent to computing
the expected number of zeros of trigonometric polynomials of fixed degree on the circle;
see
[
6
]
.

The proof of Theorem
2.1
relies on a classical Crofton-type formula;
see, for example,
[
7
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mu
be the standard Gaussian measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, given by

\mu(U)=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\int_{U}e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\,dx.

For
0\neq\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, set

Z(\xi)=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid(x,\xi)=0\}.

Let
K
be a compact curve in the unit sphere
S^{n-1}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Then

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\#(K\cap Z(\xi))\,d\mu(\xi)=\frac{1}{\pi}\,\mathrm{length}(K).

To apply Proposition
2.1
, let
S\subset{\mathbb{C}}
denote the unit circle.

Definiton 2.3
.

For
s\in S
, consider the linear functional
\tilde{s}
on

{\rm Trig}(\Lambda)
defined by
\tilde{s}(f)=f(s)
.
Using the
L_{2}(S)
inner product

(f,g)=\int_{S}f(s)g(s)\,ds,

we define a mapping
\Theta\colon S\to{\rm Trig}(\Lambda)
by the condition

(f,\Theta(s))=\tilde{s}(f)\qquad\text{for all }f\in{\rm Trig}(\Lambda).

The expected number of real roots of a random real Laurent polynomial
with spectrum
\Lambda
equals to

(2.2)

\int_{{\rm Trig}(\Lambda)}\#\bigl(\Theta(S)\cap Z(\xi)\bigr)\,d\mu(\xi),

where
Z(\xi)=\{x\in{\rm Trig}(\Lambda)\mid(x,\xi)=0\}
.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
K_{\Lambda}
denote the image of
S
under the map

s\mapsto\Theta(s)/|\Theta(s)|
.
Then the expected number of real roots equals

\frac{1}{\pi}\,\mathrm{length}(K_{\Lambda}).

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Proposition
2.1
.
∎

Consequently, Theorem
2.1
(a) is equivalent to the identity

(2.3)

\mathrm{length}(K_{\Lambda})=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{1}{\#\Lambda}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\lambda^{2}}.

To compute the length of the curve
K_{\Lambda}
we require the following auxiliary statement,
which will also be used in §§
2.4.1
,
3.3
in the proof of multidimensional analogues of Theorem
2.1
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let

•

X
be a homogeneous space of a compact Lie group
K
, equipped with a
K
-invariant Riemannian metric
g
normalized by
\int_{X}dg=1
;

•

V\subset C^{\infty}(X)
be a finite-dimensional
K
-invariant subspace endowed with a
K
-invariant inner product

(\phi,\psi)=\int_{X}\phi\psi\>dg

For
x\in X
define
F_{x}\in V
by

(2.4)

F_{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(x)\,f_{i},

where
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{N}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
V
.
Then:

(1)

For all
x\in X
and
f\in V
one has
f(x)=(f,F_{x})
.

(2)

The map
\kappa\colon X\to V
,
\kappa(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}F_{x}
, is independent of the choice of the orthonormal basis
\{f_{i}\}
.

(3)

The image
\kappa(X)
lies on the unit sphere in
V
, i.e.
(F_{x},F_{x})=N
for all
x\in X
.

Proof.

(1) For each
k\leq N
we have

(f_{k},F_{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(x)(f_{k},f_{i})=f_{k}(x),

which implies the claim by linearity.

(2) Assertion (1) shows that
F_{x}
is characterized by the identities
(f,F_{x})=f(x)
for all
f\in V
, and hence does not depend on the choice of the orthonormal basis.

(3) Since the action of
K
on
V
is orthogonal, the function
x\mapsto|F_{x}|^{2}
is
K
-invariant and therefore constant on
X
.
Let us denote this constant by
r^{2}
.
Integrating
r^{2}
over
X
using (
2.4
) we obtain

r^{2}=\int_{X}(F_{x},F_{x})\,dg=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{X}f_{i}(x)^{2}\,dg=N,

where we used the orthonormality of
\{f_{i}\}
.
Thus
r=\sqrt{N}
, proving (3).
∎

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\Theta\colon S\to\mathrm{Trig}(\Lambda)
be the map from Definition
2.3
.
Choose the orthonormal basis
\{f_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
of
\mathrm{Trig}(\Lambda)
defined by

f_{\lambda}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cos(\lambda\theta),&\lambda>0,\\[6.00006pt]
\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sin(\lambda\theta),&\lambda<0,\\[6.00006pt]
\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}},&\lambda=0\in\Lambda.\end{cases}

Then for all
s\in S
:

(1)

\Theta(s)=f_{0}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{0<\lambda\in\Lambda}\bigl(\cos(\lambda s)\,f_{\lambda}+\sin(\lambda s)\,f_{-\lambda}\bigr).

(2)

|\Theta(s)|^{2}=\#\Lambda
.

(3)

\Theta^{\prime}(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{0<\lambda\in\Lambda}\bigl(-\lambda\sin(\lambda s)\,f_{\lambda}+\lambda\cos(\lambda s)\,f_{-\lambda}\bigr).

(4)

|\Theta^{\prime}(s)|^{2}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\lambda^{2}
.

Proof.

We regard
S
as a homogeneous space under its own action and set
V=\mathrm{Trig}(\Lambda)
.
By definition one has
\Theta(s)=F_{s}
, so assertions (1) and (2) follow directly from Lemma
2.2
.
Assertions (3) and (4) are obtained by differentiation of (1) and the identity
\cos^{2}+\sin^{2}=1
.
∎

Since
|\Theta^{\prime}(s)|^{2}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\lambda^{2}
and
|\Theta(s)|^{2}=\#\Lambda
, we obtain

\mathrm{length}(K_{\Lambda})=\int_{S}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\#\Lambda}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\lambda^{2}}\,ds=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{1}{\#\Lambda}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\lambda^{2}}.

This proves equality (
2.3
) and hence completes the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

2.2.
The Bernstein–Kushnirenko–Khovanskii theorem

We now consider systems of multivariate Laurent polynomials.
Let
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
be a finite set.
A Laurent polynomial in
n
variables with spectrum
\Lambda
is a function

f(z)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}a_{\lambda}z^{\lambda}

on the complex algebraic torus
({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{n}
,
where for
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
we write

z^{\lambda}=z_{1}^{\lambda_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{\lambda_{n}}
.
The set
\Lambda
is called the
spectrum
of
f
,
and its convex hull

\Delta={\rm conv}(\Lambda)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

is the
Newton polyhedron
of
f
.

The following result, due to Bernstein, Kushnirenko, and Khovanskii,
may be regarded as a multidimensional analogue of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra;
see, for example,
[
8
]
.

Theorem 2.2

(BKK)
.

Let
F=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
be a system of Laurent polynomials in
n
variables,
with spectra
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n}
and Newton polyhedra

\Delta_{i}={\rm conv}(\Lambda_{i})
.
Let
N(F)
denote the number of isolated roots of the system

f_{1}=\cdots=f_{n}=0
in
({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{n}
.
Then, for a generic choice of the coefficients,
all roots are isolated and

(2.5)

N(F)=n!\,{\rm vol}(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n}),

where
{\rm vol}(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n})
denotes the mixed volume of the polyhedra

\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n}
.

More precisely, in the space of all systems
F=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})

there exists a proper algebraic hypersurface
D

such that equality (
2.5
) holds for all
F\notin D
.

In the special case where
\Delta_{1}=\cdots=\Delta_{n}=\Delta
,
the theorem was first established by A. Kushnirenko,
and the mixed volume reduces to the ordinary volume:

{\rm vol}(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n})={\rm vol}(\Delta).

2.3.
A multidimensional Kac theorem for Laurent polynomials

We now formulate three results that may be viewed as multivariate analogue
of the Kac-type Theorem
2.1
for Laurent polynomials.
We begin by introducing the multidimensional counterparts of the
one-dimensional notions discussed in §
2.1
.

(1)

A Laurent polynomial in
n
variables is called
real

if it takes real values on the compact subtorus

S^{n}=\{z\in({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{n}:\ z=(\>{\rm e}^{i\theta_{1}},\ldots,\>{\rm e}^{i\theta_{n}})\}.

(2)

A zero of a Laurent polynomial lying in
S^{n}
is called a
real zero
.
More generally, a solution of a system of Laurent polynomials
contained in
S^{n}
is called a
real root
of the system.

(3)

A Laurent polynomial
P
is real if and only if

a_{k}=\overline{a_{-k}}
for all
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.

(4)

The zero set of a real Laurent polynomial is invariant under the involution

z\mapsto\overline{z}^{-1}
, whose fixed point set is
S^{n}
.

(5)

Let
{\rm Trig}(\Lambda)
denote the space of trigonometric polynomials of the form

\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\bigl(\alpha_{k}\cos(k,\theta)+\beta_{k}\sin(k,\theta)\bigr),\qquad\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Restriction to
S^{n}
defines a linear isomorphism between the space of real
Laurent polynomials with spectrum
\Lambda
and the space
{\rm Trig}(\Lambda)
.

(6)

Since every vector in
2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
is a period of any trigonometric polynomial,
the space
{\rm Trig}(\Lambda)
may be regarded as a finite-dimensional subspace of

L^{2}(S^{n})
, equipped with the inner product

(f,g)=\int_{S^{n}}f(\theta)g(\theta)\,d\theta.

(7)

Random vectors in
{\rm Trig}(\Lambda)
, and hence random real Laurent polynomials
with spectrum
\Lambda
, are assumed to be Gaussian with respect to the
standard Gaussian measure
G_{\Lambda}
induced by this inner product.

(8)

Given real Laurent polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
with spectra

\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n}
, let
N(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
denote the number of
isolated real roots of the system
f_{1}=\cdots=f_{n}=0
.
The
expected number of real roots
of the random system

f_{1}=\cdots=f_{n}=0
is defined by

(2.6)

\int_{{\rm Trig}(\Lambda_{1})\times\cdots\times{\rm Trig}(\Lambda_{n})}N(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})\,dG_{\Lambda_{1}}\cdots dG_{\Lambda_{n}}.

It turns out that the expected number of real roots of a random system of

n
real Laurent polynomials in
n
variables with prescribed spectra
can be expressed in terms of mixed volumes of certain
n
-dimensional
ellipsoids, called
Newton ellipsoids
; see Theorem
2.3
below.

Definiton 2.4
.

The
Newton ellipsoid

{\rm Ell}(\Lambda)
associated with a finite set

\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
is the ellipsoid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
whose support function is given by

h_{\Lambda}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\#\Lambda}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\lambda(x)^{2}},\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

where each
\lambda\in\Lambda
is regarded as a linear functional on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Recall that the support function of a compact convex set
A\subset({\mathbb{R}}^{n})^{\ast}

is defined by

h_{A}(x)=\max_{\xi\in A}\xi(x).

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n}
be centrally symmetric finite subsets of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
Then the expected number of real roots of a random system of real Laurent
polynomials with spectra
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n}
equals

n!\,{\rm vol}\bigl({\rm Ell}(\Lambda_{1}),\ldots,{\rm Ell}(\Lambda_{n})\bigr),

where
{\rm vol}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
denotes the mixed volume of the convex bodies

A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
.

Remark 2.3
.

In the one-dimensional case
n=1
, the Newton ellipsoid
{\rm Ell}(\Lambda)

reduces to the interval
[-h_{\Lambda}(1),h_{\Lambda}(1)]
,
and Theorem
2.1
(a) is recovered.

Remark 2.4
.

If
\Lambda
is centrally symmetric, then

{\rm Ell}(\Lambda)\subset{\rm conv}(\Lambda).

Indeed, for compact convex sets
A,B
,
the inclusion
A\subseteq B
is equivalent to the inequality

h_{A}\leq h_{B}
of their support functions.
For
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the support function of
{\rm conv}(\Lambda)
is

h_{{\rm conv}(\Lambda)}(\xi)=\max_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\lambda(\xi)=\max_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|\lambda(\xi)|,

where the second equality uses the symmetry of
\Lambda
.
By Definition
2.4
,
h_{\Lambda}(\xi)
is the root mean square of the
numbers
\{|\lambda(\xi)|:\lambda\in\Lambda\}
, which does not exceed their
maximum. This implies the claim.

Remark 2.5
.

Mixed volumes of ellipsoids in connection with the distribution of zeros of system
of random equations were first studied in
[
13
]
.

Applying the Bernstein–Kushnirenko–Khovanskii theorem yields the following
consequence; for details see §
2.4.2
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n})
be the probability that a root
of a random system of
n
real Laurent polynomials with spectra

\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n}
is real. Then

(2.7)

\mathcal{P}(\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n})=\frac{\text{{\rm vol}}\bigl({\rm Ell}(\Lambda_{1}),\ldots,{\rm Ell}(\Lambda_{n})\bigr)}{\text{{\rm vol}}\bigl({\rm conv}(\Lambda_{1}),\ldots,{\rm conv}(\Lambda_{n})\bigr)},

where
{\rm conv}(\Lambda_{i})
is the Newton polyhedron of the
i
-th polynomial.

Building upon the asymptotic analysis in
[

for expanding spectra, which provides a multidimensional generalization of Corollary

Remark 2.6
.

The integrand
x^{2}(1-x^{2})^{(n-1)/2}\,dx
belongs to the class of
Chebyshev differential binomials
.
We recall a classical result of Chebyshev
[
14
]
,
extending earlier observations of Euler,
which states that the binomial differential
x^{m}(a+bx^{n})^{p}dx
admits an antiderivative in elementary functions
if and only if at least one of the quantities
p
,
\frac{m+1}{n}
, or
\frac{m+1}{n}+p
is an integer.
In the present case, these conditions are satisfied for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
: specifically,
for odd
n
we recover the first case, while for even
n
the third case applies.

A further characterization of the asymptotic behavior of
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n})

for general sequences of expanding spectra is established in Theorem
2.6
within §

2.4.1.
Theorem
2.3

The computation of the expected number of roots relies on the integral geometric formula (
2.12
) established in
[
10
]

(cf.
[
12
,
11
]
for related applications).

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional subspace of
C^{\infty}(X)
, where
X
is a compact differentiable manifold. We assume throughout that the evaluation functionals are non-vanishing; that is,

(2.9)

\text{for each }x\in X,\text{ there exists }f\in V\text{ such that }f(x)\neq 0.

Let
(\cdot,\cdot)
be a scalar product on
V
, and denote by
G
the associated Gaussian measure.

Definiton 2.5
.

The
evaluation mapping

\Theta\colon X\to V^{*}
is defined by
\Theta(x)(f)=f(x)
for
f\in V
. Condition (
2.9
) ensures that
\Theta
takes values in
V^{*}\setminus\{0\}
. We further define the normalized mapping
\bar{\Theta}\colon X\to V^{*}
by

(2.10)

\bar{\Theta}(x)=\frac{\Theta(x)}{\|\Theta(x)\|_{*}},

where
\|\cdot\|_{*}
denotes the norm induced by the dual scalar product on
V^{*}
. Let
D_{x}\colon T_{x}X\to V^{*}
be the differential of
\bar{\Theta}
at
x
,
and let
D_{x}^{*}\colon V\to T_{x}^{*}X
be its adjoint operator.

Definiton 2.6
.

Let
B\subset V
be the unit ball.
The
V
-

4.

4.1.
Exponential sums of one variable

An
exponential sum
is a complex-valued function of the form

f(z)=\sum\nolimits_{\lambda\in\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{C}},\,c_{\lambda}\in{\mathbb{C}}}c_{\lambda}\>{\rm e}^{\bar{\lambda}z}.

The set
\Lambda
is called the
spectrum
of

, and the convex hull
\Delta={\rm conv}(\Lambda)
is referred to as the

of

.
If

, the zero set of an exponential sum is infinite.
For example, the function
f(z)=\>{\rm e}^{2\pi iz}-1
vanishes precisely on
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

The following result may be regarded as an analogue of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra for exponential sums.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let

denote the number of zeros of an exponential sum
f
inside the disk
B_{r}
of radius
r
centered at the origin.
Then

(4.1)

N(f,r)=\frac{r}{2\pi}\,l(f)+O(1),

where
l(f)
denotes the semiperimeter of the Newton polygon
\Delta
of
f
.

Remark 4.1
.

If the convex polygon
\Delta
degenerates to a line segment, its perimeter tends to twice the length of that segment.
Hence, when the Newton polygon of
f
is a segment, the quantity
l(f)
in (
4.1
) equals the length of this segment.

Remark 4.2
.

Under the substitution
z\mapsto\>{\rm e}^{z}
, a Laurent polynomial becomes an exponential sum.
Therefore, Theorem
4.1
implies the classical Fundamental Theorem of Algebra for polynomials.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Let
r_{1},\ldots,r_{N}
be the rays generated by the outward normals to the sides

\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{N}
of the Newton polygon
\Delta
associated with
f
.
For each
j
, let
U_{j,R}
denote the set of points in
{\mathbb{C}}
whose distance from
r_{j}
does not exceed
R
.
If

is sufficiently large, then all zeros of

are contained in

\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq N}U_{j,R}
.
Denote by

the number of zeros of

lying in
U_{j,R}\cap B_{r}
.
Using the argument principle, it is not difficult to obtain

(4.2)

N_{j}(f,r)=\frac{r}{2\pi}\,l_{j}(f)+o(r),

where

is half the length of the side

.
Summing over all

yields the leading term of the asymptotic relation (

).
It remains to show that the

term in (

term.
This follows from the next lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

be an exponential sum, and let

be a compact set.
Then there exists a constant

such that,

Proof.

Consider the space of exponential sums with a fixed spectrum
\Lambda
,
and let

denote its projectivization.
We identify points of
{\mathbb{P}}
with exponential sums defined up to multiplication by a nonzero scalar.
Under this identification, the shift action of

on exponential sums,
given by

induces a projective representation

of the additive group
{\mathbb{C}}
in the projective space

.

To obtain a contradiction, suppose that such a constant

does not exist.
Then there would exist a sequence of points

such that the number of zeros of the function

which is impossible.
∎

Let
A
be a rectangle in
{\mathbb{C}}
whose one side has length exceeding

and is perpendicular to the ray

Let

denote the number of zeros of

in the domain

It follows from the argument principle that for any

Then, applying Lemma

Let
\Lambda
be a finite set in the space of linear functionals on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
.
Then, as
t\to+\infty
,
the function

\frac{1}{t}\log\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\>{\rm e}^{2t\lambda(x)}

locally uniformly converges to
twice the support function of the convex polyhedron
{\rm conv}(\Lambda)
.

4.2.4.
Concluding remarks

We conclude by the description of some aspects of the geometry
connected to the formula (
4.4
).

(1)

In the setting of Example
4.1
,
the support of the measure
\Xi_{n,\Upsilon}
coincides with the space

{\rm Im\>}({\mathbb{C}}^{n})
, and one has

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}\phi\,d\Xi_{n,\Upsilon}=\frac{n!}{(2\pi)^{n}}{\rm vol}_{n}\bigl({\rm conv}(\Delta_{1}),\ldots,{\rm conv}(\Delta_{n})\bigr)\int_{{\rm Im\>}({\mathbb{C}}^{n})}\phi\,dy,

where
dy
denotes the standard
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure on

{\rm Im\>}({\mathbb{C}}^{n})
, and

{\rm vol}_{n}({\rm conv}(\Delta_{1}),\ldots,{\rm conv}(\Delta_{n}))

is the mixed volume of the corresponding Newton polytopes.

(2)

In the one-dimensional case, the support of the measure
\Xi_{1,\Delta}

is the union of the rays of outward normals to the sides of the Newton
polygon; see §
4.1
.
On a ray
r
dual to a side
\Delta
,
the measure is given by integration over
r
with weight

\frac{1}{4\pi}\,{\rm length}(\Delta)
.

(3)

The support of the current
\Xi_{k,\Upsilon}
is the
(2n-k)
-dimensional
fan
\mathcal{K}_{\Sigma}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
consisting of cones dual to the

k
-dimensional faces of the Minkowski sum

\Sigma={\rm conv}(\Lambda_{1})+\cdots+{\rm conv}(\Lambda_{k})

of Newton polyhedra.
Recall that the cone dual to a face
\Gamma
of
\Sigma
consists of all

z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such that the linear functional

\psi(\delta)={\rm Re\>}(z,\delta)
attains its maximum on
\Sigma

simultaneously at all points of
\Gamma
.

(4)

Let
\Upsilon=(\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n})
,
where
\Lambda_{1}=\cdots=\Lambda_{n}=\Lambda
and
\Delta={\rm conv}(\Lambda)
.
We now describe the measure
\Xi_{n,\Upsilon}
in this special case.
As observed in (3), the support of this measure is the union of cones
dual to the
n
-dimensional faces of the polyhedron

\Delta={\rm conv}(\Lambda)
.

Let
K_{\Gamma}
be the cone dual to an
n
-dimensional face
\Gamma
of

\Delta
.
On
K_{\Gamma}
, the measure
\Xi_{n,\Upsilon}
coincides with the

n
-dimensional Euclidean measure multiplied by the factor

\frac{n!}{(2\pi)^{n}}\>c(\Gamma)\,{\rm vol}_{n}(\Gamma),

where
{\rm vol}_{n}(\Gamma)
denotes the Euclidean
n
-volume of
\Gamma
, and

c(\Gamma)=\cos\bigl(T_{\Gamma}^{\perp},\sqrt{-1}\,T_{\Gamma}\bigr)

is the cosine of the angle between the subspaces

T_{\Gamma}^{\perp}
and
\sqrt{-1}\,T_{\Gamma}
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n*}
.
Here
T_{\Gamma}
is the tangent space to the face
\Gamma
and

T_{\Gamma}^{\perp}
is its orthogonal complement.
By definition, the cosine of the angle between two
n
-dimensional
subspaces is the absolute value of the Jacobian of the orthogonal
projection from one onto the other with respect to the measure

{\rm vol}_{n}
.

(5)

Under the assumptions of (4), let
{\rm pvol}(\Delta)
denote the value

\Xi_{n,\Upsilon}(B_{1})
, where
B_{1}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is the unit ball centered
at the origin.
We call
{\rm pvol}(\Delta)
the
pseudovolume
of the polyhedron

\Delta
.
For example:

(a)

If
\Lambda\subset{\rm Re\>}{\mathbb{C}}^{n*}
, then

{\rm pvol}(\Delta)={\rm vol}_{n}(\Delta)
;

(b)

If
n=1
, then
{\rm pvol}(\Delta)
coincides with the semiperimeter of

\Delta
.

The pseudovolume of a convex polyhedron in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
may be regarded as a
complex analogue of the usual volume in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
In particular, the map
\Delta\mapsto{\rm pvol}(\Delta)
is a homogeneous
polynomial of degree
n
on the space of convex polyhedra in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

(6)

It follows from Theorem
4.3
that the pseudovolume admits a
continuous extension, with respect to the Hausdorff topology, to a
polynomial on the space of all compact convex sets.
We now present an equivalent description of this extension in the case
of smooth convex bodies.

Let
K\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be a smooth convex body, let
x\in\partial K
, and
denote by
\eta(x)
the outward unit normal vector at
x
.
Define a differential
1
-form
\alpha
on
\partial K
by

\alpha(x_{t})={\rm Im\>}(x_{t},\eta(x)),

where
x_{t}
is a tangent vector to
\partial K
at
x
, and

(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the standard Hermitian inner product on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Then

(4.6)

{\rm pvol}(K)=\int_{\partial K}\alpha\wedge(d\alpha)^{n-1}.

Indeed, consider the Gauss map

G\colon\partial K\to\partial B_{1}
defined by
G(x)=\eta(x)
.
A direct computation shows that the pullback of the form
d^{c}h
,
where
h
is a support function of
K
,
differs from
\alpha
by the differential of the function

{\rm Im\>}(x,G(x))
.
Since exact terms do not contribute to the integral in
(
4.6
), the formula follows.

(7)

It is shown in
[
31
]
that the pseudovolume defines a translation-
and unitary-invariant valuation on convex bodies in
{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}
.
Note that, in contrast to the usual volume, the pseudovolume is not
monotone with respect to inclusion.
In general, the relation
\Delta\subset\Gamma
does not imply

{\rm pvol}(\Delta)\leq{\rm pvol}(\Gamma)
.
Further results on pseudovolumes of convex bodies may be found
in
[
28
,
29
]
.

(8)

In a related setting, mixed pseudovolumes arise in estimates of the
asymptotic density of common zeros of entire functions of exponential
growth; see
[
32
]
.
