Inexact DC Algorithms in Hilbert Spaces with Applications to PDE-Constrained Optimization
P. D. Khanh, V. V. H. Khoa, B. S. Mordukhovich, D. B. Tran, N. V. Vo

Abstract




Abstract.
In this paper, we design and apply novel inexact adaptive algorithms to deal with minimizing difference-of-convex (DC) functions in Hilbert spaces. We first introduce I-ADCA, an inexact adaptive counterpart of the well-recognized DCA (difference-of-convex algorithm), that allows inexact subgradient evaluations and inexact solutions to convex subproblems while still guarantees global convergence to stationary points. Under a Polyak-Łojasiewicz type property for DC objectives, we obtain explicit convergence rates for the proposed algorithm. Our main application addresses elliptic optimal control problems with control constraints and nonconvex
L^{1-2}
sparsity-enhanced regularizers admitting a DC decomposition. Employing I-ADCA and appropriate versions of finite element discretization leads us to an efficient procedure for solving such problems with establishing its well-posedness and error bound estimates confirmed by numerical experiments.

Keywords.
inexact difference-of-convex-functions algorithm, Polyak-Łojasiewicz conditions, PDE-constrained optimization, sparse controls, finite element discretization

Introduction

DC (difference-of-convex) programming has emerged as a powerful framework for tackling a broad class of nonconvex optimization problems that retain a rich variational structure. Since the seminal work by Pham Dinh Tao and Le Thi Hoai An
[
30
]
, the
difference-of-convex algorithm
(DCA) and its variants have been successfully applied to numerous models in statistics, machine learning, and signal processing including feature selection, robust regression, support vector machines, and binary logistic regression; see, e.g., the surveys in
[
22
,
23
]
for a comprehensive account of developments. In many of these applications, the DC structure arises naturally from modeling considerations such as robustness, sparsity, and nonconvex regularization while DCA provides a simple yet effective first-order scheme that alternates between convex subproblems and exhibits good practical performance even in large-scale settings. More recently, Nesterov-type inertial techniques initiated in
[
29
]
have been incorporated into DCA to further accelerate its convergence. In particular, the accelerated DCA (ADCA) introduced in
[
32
]
(see also
[
33
]
) combines DCA with an extrapolation step and has demonstrated state-of-the-art performance for sparse binary logistic regression and other finite-dimensional DC models.

Despite this progress, various existing DC algorithms and their accelerated and/or inexact variants
[
2
,
3
,
13
,
22
,
23
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
are formulated and analyzed in finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces, with a few exceptions as in
[
5
,
18
,
24
,
43
]
. However, many modern applications in inverse problems, optimal control, and PDE-constrained optimization are inherently infinite-dimensional being posed naturally in Hilbert (sometimes in more general) spaces. Typical examples include distributed optimal control of semilinear elliptic and parabolic equations
[
1
,
16
,
36
,
38
,
39
]
, and variational problems with function-space sparsity constraints
[
37
,
40
]
. In such settings, a direct “discretize then optimize” approach may obscure the underlying DC structure at the continuous level and may lead to algorithms whose behavior depends in subtle ways on the discretization. This motivates the development of DC algorithms
directly in Hilbert spaces
, together with convergence theory and rate estimates that are robust with respect to discretization. To the best of our knowledge, a systematic development of
inexact DCA-type methods
to Hilbert spaces involving inexact computations in both subgradient evaluations and subproblem solving, has not yet been carried out.

A second major motivation for this work comes from
sparse optimal control of elliptic PDEs
. Consider, for instance, the classical distributed optimal control problem for a linear elliptic equation with control constraints (see, e.g.,
[
38
, page 4]
):

\displaystyle\underset{y\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),\,u\in L^{2}(\Omega)}{\operatorname{minimize}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|y-y_{d}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|u-u_{d}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

subject to

\displaystyle-\Delta y=u\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\beta_{1}\leq u\leq\beta_{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ a.e. in }\Omega,

where the control
u
enters as a source term, and where the aim is to track a desired state
y_{d}
. When a standard quadratic control cost is used, optimal controls are typically non-sparse and spread over the entire domain. To promote
sparsity
(and hence to reduce computation costs), Stadler
[
37
]
augments the objective with a
nonsmooth term

\beta\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}
, which drives the optimal control to zero on subregions where further reduction of the cost is negligible; the threshold for what counts as negligible is set by
\beta>0
. In those areas, no actuation is applied. This mechanism is central to optimal placement of control devices, e.g., on piezoelectric plates associated with linear material laws. The reader is referred to
[
9
,
11
,
40
]
and the monographs
[
17
,
39
,
36
]
for further developments and error bound analysis. The resulting
L^{1}
-regularized control problems have been extensively studied over the last decade, both analytically and numerically, and form a well-established benchmark in PDE-constrained optimization.

At the same time, there exists mounting evidence from finite-dimensional compressed sensing and imaging that the difference of the
\ell^{1}
and
\ell^{2}
norms,

\ell^{1-2}(x):=\|x\|_{1}-\|x\|_{2},\hskip 10.00002ptx\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},

can promote sparsity more effectively than the plain
\ell^{1}
norm in highly coherent settings. In particular, it is shown in
[
41
]
that
\ell^{1-2}
-based models yield sparser solutions and improved recovery performance in several compressed-sensing and image-denoising tasks. This suggests replacing the
L^{1}
control cost in PDE-constrained optimization by an
L^{1-2}
-type functional as

L^{1-2}(u):=\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}-\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\hskip 10.00002ptu\in L^{2}(\Omega),

with the goal of enhancing sparsity at the level of distributed controls. The resulting sparsity-enhancing regularizer is nonsmooth and nonconvex, but it admits a DC decomposition and hence fits into the DC programming framework. Quite recently, a certain inexact DCA with a sieving strategy (iDCA) has been proposed in
[
43
]
for discretized PDE-constrained optimal control problems with an
L^{1-2}
control cost.

The present paper aims to bridge these developments by formulating and analyzing inexact adaptive DC algorithms directly in Hilbert spaces and by applying them to
L^{1-2}
–regularized elliptic optimal control
problems. We consider general DC programs of the form

\min_{u\in\mathcal{H}}f(u):=g(u)-h(u)

defined on an arbitrary Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, where
g
and
h
are proper, lower semicontinuous, and convex. Our first main contribution is to develop the
inexact adaptive DC algorithm
(I-ADCA) for solving such problems in Hilbert spaces. The proposed scheme incorporates a general form of Nesterov-type extrapolation step, and it allows for inexactness in two places: (i)
\varepsilon
–subgradients of
h
computed at “extrapolated” points, and (ii)
\varepsilon
–approximate minimizers of the associated convex subproblems involving
g
. At each iteration, the inexactness levels are controlled online by a backtracking-type (adaptive) procedure. More precisely, I-ADCA adjusts the accuracy parameter at each iteration to ensure that the inexactness is commensurate with the actual progress of the algorithm that leads us to a family of estimates in the form

\varepsilon_{k}\lesssim\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\|^{2}

and hence to a genuine descent of the objective values. Building upon advanced tools of convex and variational analysis in Hilbert spaces
[
26
,
28
,
27
,
42
]
, we prove that I-ADCA is well-defined and preserves fundamental convergence features of the exact accelerated DCA
[
32
]
developed in finite dimensions. Under additional assumptions, most notably, a
Polyak-Łojasiewicz type
property for the objective
f
and local Lipschitz continuity of either
\nabla g
or
\nabla h
, we establish explicit convergence rates for the objective values generated by I-ADCA. To the best of our knowledge, this is the
first convergence-rate
result for a DC algorithm that simultaneously incorporates
acceleration, inexactness, and infinite dimensionality
of the space in question.

Yet another major contribution of this paper is applying the proposed scheme to
elliptic optimal control
problems with
L^{1-2}
sparsity-enhanced regularization
. Consider the problem

\min_{y\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),\,u\in L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-y_{d}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|u-u_{d}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\beta\left(\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}-\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\right\}

subject to a linear elliptic PDE and pointwise control bounds; see (
5.1
) for the precise formulation. We show that, in the Hilbert space setting, the associated cost functional can be written in the form
f=g-h
with
g
being strongly convex, and so the problem fits into our abstract I-ADCA framework. This allows us to derive the specialized inexact adaptive DC algorithm, denoted by I-ADCA-PDECO, for the PDE-constrained optimization problem (
5.1
). Our analysis ensures well-posedness of the scheme, solvability and uniqueness of the exact subproblems, and finite-element error estimates that relate continuous and discrete controls.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we recall some preliminary notions, which are broadly used in the subsequent material. Section
3
introduces the inexact adaptive DC algorithm I-ADCA and discusses some of its properties. In Section
4
, we establish global convergence and convergence rates of the proposed algorithm under a version of the Polyak-Łojasiewicz condition in Hilbert spaces. Section
5
is devoted to optimal control of elliptic equations with
L^{1-2}
sparsity-enhanced regularization and presents an algorithm for its solution based on applications of the inexact adaptive DCA developed above. In Section
6
, we justify a finite element scheme for the numerical solution of the optimal control problem and present the results of numerical experiments. The concluding Section
7
summarizes the main achievements of the paper and discusses some directions of our future research.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall for the reader’s convenience several basic notions with the standard notation broadly used below.
In the framework of Hilbert spaces, the symbols ‘
\rightarrow
’ and ‘
\rightharpoonup
’ signify the
strong and weak convergence
, respectively. Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{*},\mathcal{H}}
denote the
duality pairing
between a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and its continuous dual
\mathcal{H}^{*}
, i.e.,

\langle u^{*},u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{*},\mathcal{H}}:=u^{*}(u)\text{ for every }u^{*}\in\mathcal{H}^{*}\text{ and }u\in\mathcal{H}.

The
diameter
of a set
\Omega\subset\mathcal{H}
is defined as

\mathrm{diam}(\Omega):=\sup_{u,v\in\Omega}\|u-v\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

Letting
u\in\mathcal{H}
and
\Omega\subset\mathcal{H}
, denote

\mathrm{dist}(u;\Omega):=\inf_{v\in\Omega}\|u-v\|

the
distance
from
u
to
\Omega
. In
\mathcal{H}
,
B(u,r)
and
{\rm I\!B}(u,r)
stand for the
open
and
closed ball
centered at
u
with radius
r>0
, respectively, while
B
and
{\rm I\!B}
signify in this way the open and closed unit ball in
\mathcal{H}
. Unless otherwise stated, all functions
f:\mathcal{H}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}:=(-\infty,\infty]
under consideration are
proper
, i.e., their
effective domain

\mbox{\rm dom}\,f:=\{u\in\mathcal{H}\mid f(u)<\infty\}

is nonempty. An extended-real-valued function
f:\mathcal{H}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
is called
lower semicontinuous
(l.s.c.)
at

\overline{u}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,f
if for any sequence
u_{k}\to\overline{u}
, we have

\liminf_{k\to\infty}f(u_{k})\geq f(\overline{u}).

The function
f
is said to be lower semicontinuous if it is l.s.c. at any point in its domain. Further,
f
is
convex
on
{\cal H}
if

f\big(\lambda u+(1-\lambda)v\big)\leq\lambda f(u)+(1-\lambda)f(v)\;\mbox{ for all }\;u,v\in\mathcal{H}\;\mbox{ and }\;\lambda\in[0,1].

The
modulus of strong convexity
of
f
(in the sense of Polyak
[
34
]
), denoted as
\sigma_{f}
, is defined as the supremum of all
\sigma\geq 0
for which the inequality

f\big(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\big)\leq\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y)-\dfrac{\sigma}{2}\lambda(1-\lambda)\|x-y\|^{2}

(2.1)

holds whenever
x,y\in\mathcal{H}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. It is said that
f
is
strongly convex
if
\sigma_{f}>0
.

Given two Hilbert spaces
(\mathcal{H},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})
and
(\mathcal{K},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{K}})
, we endow the product space
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{K}
with the
inner product
defined by

\langle(u_{1},v_{1}),(u_{2},v_{2})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{K}}:=\langle u_{1},u_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{K}},

which induces the
product norm

\|(u,v)\|_{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{K}}:=\sqrt{\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\|v\|_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}}.

By
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
, we understand the normed space of linear bounded operators from
\mathcal{H}
into
\mathcal{K}
.

3
Inexact Adaptive DC Algorithm in Hilbert Spaces

Given a Hilbert space
\big(\mathcal{H},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\big)
, a
DC minimization problem
on
\mathcal{H}
is written as

\inf_{u\in\mathcal{H}}\big[f(u):=g(u)-h(u)\big],

(3.1)

where
g
and
h
belong to the class
\Gamma(\mathcal{H})
of proper l.s.c. convex extended-real-valued functions on
\mathcal{H}
. In this case,
f
is called a
DC function
while
g,h
are called its
DC components
.

A point
u\in\mathcal{H}
is called a
critical point
of (
3.1
) if

\partial g(u)\cap\partial h(u)\neq\varnothing,

(3.2)

where the
subdifferential of convex analysis
is defined by

\partial g(u):=\big\{v\in\mathcal{H}\mid\langle v,u^{\prime}-u\rangle\leq g(u^{\prime})-g(u)\,\text{ for all }\,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}\big\},\hskip 10.00002ptu\in\mathcal{H}.

(3.3)

As is well known, DCA is a
descent method
without line search designed to find critical points of DC problems. Owing to its simplicity, efficiency, and robustness, DCA has proven to be highly effective for addressing large-scale nonsmooth nonconvex programs; see Section
1
. For problem (
3.1
), it computes iteratively

\begin{cases}v^{k}\in\partial h(u^{k}),\\
u^{k+1}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\limits_{u\in\mathcal{H}}\Big[g(u)-\langle v^{k},u\rangle\Big],\end{cases}

(3.4)

where the
subproblem
of finding
u^{k+1}\in\mathrm{argmin}_{u\in\mathcal{H}}\Big[g(u)-\langle v^{k},u\rangle\Big]
is a convex program obtained from (
3.1
) by replacing the convex component
h
with its affine minorization via taking the subgradient
v^{k}\in\partial h(u^{k})
.

The following
accelerated DCA
(ADCA), introduced in
[
32
]
in finite dimensions, incorporates
Nesterov’s acceleration scheme
into its construction to boost the performance of DCA.

Algorithm 1

ADCA: Accelerated DCA for solving (
3.1
)

Initialization:

z^{0}=u^{0}=u^{-1}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,f
,
q\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
,
t_{0}=1
,
k=0
.

Repeat

1:

Compute
t_{k+1}=\frac{1+\sqrt{1+4t_{k}^{2}}}{2}
.

2:

Compute
z^{k}=u^{k}+\dfrac{t_{k}-1}{t_{k+1}}\big(u^{k}-u^{k-1}\big)
.

3:

If
f(z^{k})\leq\displaystyle\max_{t=\max\{0,\,k-q\},\ldots,k}f(u^{t})
, then set
w^{k}=z^{k}
. Otherwise, set
w^{k}=u^{k}
.

4:

Choose
v^{k}\in\partial h(w^{k})
.

5:

Compute

u^{k+1}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\limits_{u\in\mathcal{H}}\big[\,g(u)-\langle v^{k},u\rangle\,\big].

6:

Set
k\leftarrow k+1
.

Until
Stopping criteria.

To address
inexactness
in subgradient computations, we proceed—similarly to
[
31
]
in finite dimensions—by replacing the exact subdifferential (
3.3
) of a convex function
f:\mathcal{H}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
at
u\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,f
by its
\varepsilon
-
subdifferential
counterpart defined by

\partial_{\varepsilon}f(u):=\big\{v\in\mathcal{H}\mid\langle v,u^{\prime}-u\rangle\leq f(u^{\prime})-f(u)+\varepsilon\,\text{ for all }\,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}\big\},

(3.5)

with
\partial_{0}f(u)
reducing to (
3.3
); see
[
28
,
42
]
for more details on (
3.5
). We say that

u_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{H}
is an
\varepsilon
-solution
to the problem
\inf_{u\in\mathcal{H}}f(u)
and denote it as
u_{\varepsilon}\in\varepsilon\text{-}\mathrm{argmin}_{u\in\mathcal{H}}f(u)
if

f(u_{\varepsilon})\leq\inf_{u\in\mathcal{H}}f(u)+\varepsilon.

In what follows, our standing assumptions on the data of (
3.1
) are:

(H1)

\mbox{\rm dom}\,h=\mathcal{H}
and
h
is bounded on bounded subsets of
\mathcal{H}
.

(H2)

\inf_{u\in\mathcal{H}}f(u)>-\infty
.

Now we are ready to design our underlying
inexact adaptive DCA
(abbr. I-ADCA) in which the main
adaptive step
(Step 4) is inspired by the inexact gradient step from
[
19
, Algorithm 1]
.

Algorithm 2

I-ADCA: Inexact Adaptive DCA for Solving (
3.1
)

Initialization:

u^{0}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,f
, reduction factor
\gamma\in(0,1)
, and
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1]
. Set
k=0.

Repeat

1:

Choose
w^{k}
such that
f(w^{k})\leq f(u^{k})
.

2:

Compute
v^{k}\in\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}h(w^{k})
.

3:

Compute an
\varepsilon_{k}
-solution
u^{k+1}
of

\inf_{u\in\mathcal{H}}\big[\,g(u)-\langle v^{k},u\rangle\,\big].

4:

While

\varepsilon_{k}>\frac{\sigma_{g}+\sigma_{h}}{32}\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|^{2},

set
\varepsilon_{k}\leftarrow\gamma\,\varepsilon_{k}
and
restart
from Step 2 with the new
\varepsilon_{k}
.

5:

Choose
\varepsilon_{k+1}\in(0,\varepsilon_{k}]
and set
k\leftarrow k+1
.

Until
Stopping criteria.

Remark 3.1
.

Multiple selections of
w^{k}
can satisfy Step 1 in Algorithm
2
. A particularly effective option to improve performance, illustrated numerically in Section
6
, is to combine
Nesterov-type acceleration
with the “look-back” step as in Algorithm
1
(when
q=0
), or to use other momentum schemes such as, e.g.,
Polyak’s heavy-ball method

[
35
]
.

Next we show that the steps of Algorithm
2
are
well-defined
. To proceed, recall the classical notion of
Fenchel conjugate
for
f\in\Gamma(\mathcal{H})
given by

f^{*}(v):=\sup\big\{\langle u,v\rangle-f(u)\mid u\in\mathcal{H}\big\},\hskip 10.00002ptv\in\mathcal{H}.

It follows from
[
42
, Theorem 2.3.3]
that
f^{*}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{H})
. Considering further a set-valued mapping/multifunction
S\colon{\cal H}\rightrightarrows{\cal H}
, denote by

\mbox{\rm dom}\,S:=\{u\in\mathcal{H}\mid S(u)\neq\varnothing\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{rge}\,S:=\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{H}}S(u)

its
domain
and
range
, respectively. First we check the well-definiteness of Steps 2 and 3 of Algorithm
2
in the following remark.

Remark 3.2
.

(i)
At each iteration, the nonemptiness of
\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}h(w^{k})
is guaranteed by assumption (H1) and
[
42
, Theorem 2.4.4(iii)]
. Thus, Step 2 of Algorithm
2
can always be carried out.

(ii)
Regarding Step 3, observe that (H2) implies that

\inf_{v\in\mathcal{H}}\big[h^{*}(v)-g^{*}(v)\big]=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{H}}\big[g(u)-h(u)\big]=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{H}}f(u)>-\infty,

and therefore
\mbox{\rm dom}\,h^{*}\subset\mbox{\rm dom}\,g^{*}
. Then it follows from Step 2 in Algorithm
2
and
[
42
, Theorem 2.4.4(iii)-(iv)]
that

v^{k}\in\mathrm{rge}\,\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}h=\mbox{\rm dom}\,\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}h^{*}=\mbox{\rm dom}\,h^{*}\subset\mbox{\rm dom}\,g^{*}=\mbox{\rm dom}\,\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}g^{*}.

This ensures that Step 3 is well-defined.

Now we show that the adaptive procedure in Step 4
stops after finitely many iterations
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let the function
h
in (
3.1
) be continuous. At each iteration in Algorithm
2
, if
w^{k}
is not a critical point (
3.2
) of (
3.1
), then the adaptive process terminates after a finite number of steps. Consequently, we have the estimate

\varepsilon_{k}\leq\frac{\sigma_{g}+\sigma_{h}}{32}\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|^{2}.

(3.6)

[
Proof.]
Fix
k\in{\rm I\!N}\cup\{0\}
and suppose on the contrary that there exist sequences
\{v^{k,l}\}_{l\in{\rm I\!N}}\subset\mathcal{H}
,
\{u^{k+1,l}\}_{l\in{\rm I\!N}}\subset\mathcal{H}
, and
\{\varepsilon_{k,l}\}_{l\in{\rm I\!N}}\subset{\rm I\!R}
decreasing to
0
such that for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
we have

\begin{cases}v^{k,l}\in\partial_{\varepsilon_{k,l}}h(w^{k}),\\
u^{k+1,l}\in\varepsilon_{k,l}\text{-}\mathrm{argmin}\big[g(u)-\langle v^{k,l},u\rangle\big],\\
\varepsilon_{k,l}>\dfrac{\sigma_{g}+\sigma_{h}}{32}\big\|u^{k+1,l}-w^{k}\big\|^{2}.\end{cases}

(3.7)

The first relationship in (
3.7
) ensures the boundedness of
\{v^{k,l}\}
due to the boundedness of
\partial_{\varepsilon_{k,1}}h(w^{k})
, which follows from
[
42
, Theorem 2.4.9]
and the continuity of
h:\mathcal{H}\to{\rm I\!R}
. Without loss of generality, assume that
v^{k,l}\rightharpoonup\overline{v}
as
l\to\infty
, which together with the first inclusion in (
3.7
) yields
\overline{v}\in\partial h(w^{k})
.
The last condition in (
3.7
) clearly gives us
u^{k+1,l}\to w^{k}
as
l\to\infty
. Moreover, from (
3.7
) we have the inequalities

\langle v^{k,l},u-u^{k+1,l}\rangle\leq g(u)-g\big(u^{k+1,l}\big)+\varepsilon_{k,l}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }u\in\mathcal{H}\text{ and }\;l\in{\rm I\!N}.

(3.8)

Taking further into account the lower semicontinuity of
g
and the weak convergence
v^{k,l}\rightharpoonup\overline{v}
as well as the strong one
\big(u^{k+1,l},\varepsilon_{k,l}\big)\to(w^{k},0)
, we pass to the limits as
l\to\infty
in (
3.8
) and arrive at the inequality

\langle\overline{v},u-w^{k}\rangle\leq g(u)-g(w^{k})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }u\in\mathcal{H},

i.e.,
\overline{v}\in\partial g(w^{k})
. This shows that
w^{k}
is a critical point of problem (
3.1
), a contradiction that completes the proof of the lemma.

In the finite-dimensional setting, it is proved in
[
30
, Theorem 3(i)]
that the DCA update rule (
3.4
) generates a monotonically decreasing sequence
\{f(u^{k})\}
of objective values for problem (
3.1
). Leveraging the
adaptive mechanism
(Step 4 in Algorithm
2
), we now establish a similar property in the presence of
inexactness
in the Hilbert space framework. The property of strong convexity (
2.1
) is significantly utilized below.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
g,h\in\Gamma(\mathcal{H})
, and let the function
h
be continuous. Suppose that Algorithm
2
generates sequences
\{\varepsilon_{k}\}
,
\{u^{k}\}
,
\{v^{k}\}
, and
\{w^{k}\}
, where
w^{k}
is not a critical point of (
3.1
) for each
k\in{\rm I\!N}\cup\{0\}
. Then the following assertions hold.

(i)
For any
k\in{\rm I\!N}\cup\{0\}
, we have the estimates

\dfrac{\sigma_{g}+\sigma_{h}}{8}\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|^{2}\leq f(w^{k})-f(u^{k+1})\leq f(u^{k})-f(u^{k+1}).

(3.9)

(ii)
If either
g
or
h
is strongly convex, then

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|^{2}<\infty,

(3.10)

which implies that
\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|=0
and
\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\varepsilon_{k}=0
.

[
Proof.]
We begin with verifying (
3.9
) for a fixed number
k\in{\rm I\!N}\cup\{0\}
. Algorithm
2
asserts that
v^{k}\in\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}h(w^{k})
, which together with (
3.5
) and (

) yields

\displaystyle\Big\langle v^{k},\dfrac{1}{2}(u^{k+1}-w^{k})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\Big\langle v^{k},\Big(\dfrac{1}{2}u^{k+1}+\dfrac{1}{2}w^{k}\Big)-w^{k}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle\leq h\Big(\dfrac{1}{2}u^{k+1}+\dfrac{1}{2}w^{k}\Big)-h(w^{k})+\varepsilon_{k}

\displaystyle\leq-\dfrac{\sigma_{h}}{8}\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\|^{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}h(u^{k+1})+\dfrac{1}{2}h(w^{k})-h(w^{k})+\varepsilon_{k}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\sigma_{h}}{8}\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\|^{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}h(u^{k+1})-\dfrac{1}{2}h(w^{k})+\varepsilon_{k}.

It follows therefore that

\langle v^{k},u^{k+1}-w^{k}\rangle+\dfrac{\sigma_{h}}{4}\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|^{2}\leq h(u^{k+1})-h(w^{k})+2\varepsilon_{k}.

(3.11)

Furthermore, Algorithm
2
tells us that
g(u^{k+1})-\langle v^{k},u^{k+1}\rangle\leq g(u)-\langle v^{k},u\rangle+\varepsilon_{k}
for all

, i.e.,
v^{k}\in\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}g(u^{k+1})
. Similarly to (

which being added to (
3.11

\dfrac{\sigma_{g}+\sigma_{h}}{4}\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|^{2}\leq f(w^{k})-f(u^{k+1})+4\varepsilon_{k}.

(3.12)

Employing (
3.6
), we deduce from (

) that

\displaystyle\dfrac{\sigma_{g}+\sigma_{h}}{4}\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(w^{k})-f(u^{k+1})+4\varepsilon_{k}

\displaystyle\leq f(w^{k})-f(u^{k+1})+\dfrac{\sigma_{g}+\sigma_{h}}{8}\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\|^{2}.

Consequently, Algorithm
2
ensures that

\dfrac{\sigma_{g}+\sigma_{h}}{8}\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|^{2}\leq f(w^{k})-f(u^{k+1})\leq f(u^{k})-f(u^{k+1}),

which thus justifies assertion (i) .

To verify (ii), assume that either
g
or
h
is strongly convex, i.e.,
\sigma_{g}+\sigma_{h}>0
. Summing up (

) over
k=0,\ldots,N
with

\displaystyle\dfrac{\sigma_{g}+\sigma_{h}}{8}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N}\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N}\left[f(u^{k})-f(u^{k+1})\right]

\displaystyle=f(u^{0})-f(u^{N+1})

\displaystyle\leq f(u^{0})-\inf_{u\in\mathcal{H}}f(u).

Since

, we get in addition that

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N}\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|^{2}\leq\dfrac{f(u^{0})-\inf_{u\in\mathcal{H}}f(u)}{(\sigma_{g}+\sigma_{h})/8}.

Passing to the limit as

leads us to

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|^{2}<\infty,

which readily implies that

as

. Then it follows from (

) that

thus completing the proof of the lemma.

[

If

. Then it follows from Step 2 in Algorithm
2

4
Global Convergence and Convergence Rates of Inexact Adaptive DCA

In this section, we establish global convergence with explicit convergence rates for Algorithm
2
under some additional assumptions. First, we recall the needed notions from infinite-dimensional variational analysis; see, e.g.,
[
26
,
27
]
and the references therein. For a proper function
f:\mathcal{H}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
, the (Fréchet)
regular subdifferential
of
f
at
\overline{u}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,f
is

\partial_{F}f(\overline{u}):=\left\{v\in\mathcal{H}\;\Big|\;\liminf\limits_{u\to\overline{u}}\frac{\varphi(u)-\varphi(\overline{u})-\langle v,u-\overline{u}\rangle}{\|u-\overline{u}\|}\geq 0\right\},

(4.1)

while the (Mordukhovich)
limiting subdifferential
of
f
at
\overline{u}
is defined by

\partial_{M}f(\overline{u}):=\big\{\overline{v}\in\mathcal{H}\;\big|\;\exists\text{ sequences }v_{k}\rightharpoonup\overline{v},\;u_{k}\xrightarrow{f}\overline{u}\text{ with }v_{k}\in\partial_{F}f(u_{k}),\;k\in{\rm I\!N}\big\},

(4.2)

where
u_{k}\xrightarrow{f}\overline{u}
signifies that
u_{k}\rightarrow\overline{u}
and
f(u_{k})\rightarrow f(\overline{u})
as
k\to\infty
. Both constructions
\partial_{F}f(\overline{u})
and
\partial_{M}f(\overline{u})
reduce to the classical gradient
\{\nabla f(\overline{u})\}
when
f
is of class
{\cal C}^{1}
around
\overline{u}
. They also agree with the convex subdifferential (
3.3
) for convex functions
f
. A vector
\overline{u}\in\mathcal{H}
is called a
limiting/(M)ordukhovich-stationary point
of
f
if

0\in\partial_{M}f(\overline{u}).

(4.3)

A central role in our global convergence and convergence rate results is played by the following version of the (generalized)
Polyak-Łojasiewicz property
.

Definition 4.1
.

A proper function
f:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
has the
Polyak-Łojasiewicz property
if:

(i)

f|_{\mathrm{dom}f}
is continuous on
\mbox{\rm dom}\,f
.

(ii)
For each
M
-stationary point
\overline{u}
of
f
, there exist
C>0
,
\varepsilon>0
, and
\theta\in[0,1)
such that

\left|f(u)-f(\overline{u})\right|^{\theta}\leq C\|v\|\;\mbox{ for all }\;u\in B(\overline{u},\varepsilon)\;\mbox{ and }\;v\in\partial_{M}f(u),

(4.4)

with the convention that
0^{0}:=0
.

When
\theta=1/2
, property (
4.4
) was introduced by Polyak
[
34
]
for functions of class
{\cal C}^{1,1}
(i.e., continuously differentiable with Lipschitzian gradients) in Hilbert spaces. Polyak’s motivation and main result
[
34
, Theorem 4]
were to establish linear convergence of the gradient descent method for
{\cal C}^{1,1}
functions in the Hilbert space setting. Independently, Łojasiewicz
[
25
]
defined a version of (
4.4
) for analytic functions in finite-dimensions for arbitrary
\theta\in(0,1)
by using tools of semialgebraic geometry without any applications to optimization. Much later, Kurdyka
[
21
]
extended the semialgebraic approach by Łojasiewicz to the general class of o-minimal structures. Extensions of (
4.4
) to nonsmooth functions via various subgradient mappings were proposed under different names and applied to optimization problems in, e.g.,
[
3
,
2
,
4
,
7
,
19
,
27
]
and many other publications in finite-dimensional spaces. Hilbert space extensions were given in
[
8
,
15
]
and the references therein. In the aforementioned publications, the reader can find broad classes of functions for which the Polyak-Łojasiewicz property and its generalizations hold.

Recall further that
\overline{u}\in\mathcal{H}
is a
strong cluster point
of a sequence
\{u_{k}\}\subset\mathcal{H}
if there exists a subsequence of
\{u_{k}\}
that strongly converges to
\overline{u}
. Before deriving the main result of this section, we present two lemmas of their own interest.

Lemma 4.2
.

In the setting of Theorem
3.5
, let
\{u^{k}\}
,
\{v^{k}\}
, and
\{w^{k}\}
be generated by Algorithm

is l.s.c.,
\{u^{k}\}
is bounded, and the set
\Omega
of strong cluster points of

\bar{f}

(ii)

\left|f(u)-\bar{f}\right|^{\theta}\leq C\|v\|\;\text{ whenever }\;v\in\partial_{M}f(u).

(iii)
If

is differentiable and
\Omega
is compact, then there exist
C,\varepsilon>0
and
\theta\in[0,1)
such that when

\left|f(u)-\bar{f}\right|^{\theta}\leq C\|v\|\;\text{ whenever }\;v\in\partial_{M}(-f)(u).

[
Proof.]
To verify (i), we get from Lemma

\{f(u^{k})\}
is a decreasing sequence. Then it follows from assumption (H2) that
\{f(u^{k})\}
converges. Pick further some

and show that

. By the definition of

. Since

Then the lower semicontinuity of

ensures that

\begin{split}\limsup\limits_{j\rightarrow\infty}f(u^{k_{j}})&=\limsup\limits_{j\rightarrow\infty}\big[g(u^{k_{j}})-h(u^{k_{j}})\big]\\

and thus we have

To verify (ii) under the additional assumptions imposed, first we show that

Employing further the Polyak-Łojasiewicz property of

Furthermore, it follows from the compactness of

(4.8)

\{u^{k+1},w^{k}\}\subset B\big(x_{i(k)},3r_{i(k)}/8\big).

Employing Lemma
4.3
, we deduce from
v^{k}\in\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}g(u^{k+1})\cap\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}^{1/\theta}}h(w^{k})
the existence of vectors
x^{k+1}\in{\rm I\!B}\big(u^{k+1},\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}\big)
and
y^{k}\in{\rm I\!B}(w^{k},\varepsilon^{1/2\theta}_{k}\big)\subset{\rm I\!B}(w^{k},\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}\big)
for which

\begin{array}[]{ll}v^{k}&\in\big[\nabla g(x^{k+1})+\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}{\rm I\!B}\big]\cap\big[\partial h(y^{k})+\varepsilon^{1/2\theta}_{k}{\rm I\!B}\big]\\
&\subset\big[\nabla g(x^{k+1})+\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}{\rm I\!B}\big]\cap\big[\partial h(y^{k})+\varepsilon^{1/2}_{k}{\rm I\!B}\big].\end{array}

(4.14)

By
\big\{u^{k+1},w^{k}\big\}\subset B\big(x_{i(k)},3r_{i(k)}/8\big)
, it follows from (
4.11
) and (
4.12

\begin{cases}\big\|x^{k+1}-x_{i(k)}\big\|\leq\|x^{k+1}-u^{k+1}\|+\big\|u^{k+1}-x_{i(k)}\big\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}+3r_{i(k)}/8<r_{i(k)}/2,\\
\big\|y^{k}-x_{i(k)}\big\|\leq\|y^{k}-w^{k}\|+\big\|w^{k}-x_{i(k)}\big\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}+3r_{i(k)}/8<r_{i(k)}/2.\end{cases}

(4.15)

We also have the estimates

\begin{split}\big\|x^{k+1}-y^{k}\big\|&\leq\big\|x^{k+1}-u^{k+1}\big\|+\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|+\big\|w^{k}-y^{k}\big\|\leq 2\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}+\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|.\end{split}

(4.16)

The inclusions in (
4.14
) imply by the Lipschitzian conditions in (
4.10
) and the relationships in (
4.15
) and (
4.16
) that

v^{k}\in\left[\nabla g(y^{k})+\left(\big(2\ell+1\big)\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}+\ell\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|\right){\rm I\!B}\right]\cap\left[\partial h(y^{k})+\varepsilon^{1/2}_{k}{\rm I\!B}\right]\ \ \text{ for all large }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

This gives us a subgradient
b^{k}\in\partial h(y^{k})
satisfying

\begin{split}\big\|b^{k}-v^{k}\big\|&\leq\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2},\\
\big\|b^{k}-\nabla g(y^{k})\big\|&\leq\|b^{k}-v^{k}\|+\|v^{k}-\nabla g(y^{k})\|\leq 2\big(\ell+1\big)\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}+\ell\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|,\\
b^{k}-\nabla g(y^{k})&\in\partial_{F}h(y^{k})-\nabla g(y^{k})=\partial_{F}(h-g)(y^{k})=\partial_{F}(-f)(y^{k})\subset\partial_{M}(-f)(y^{k}).\end{split}

(4.17)

Deduce further from (
4.11
), (
4.12
), and the nonexpansive property of
\mathrm{dist}(\cdot;\Omega)
that

\mathrm{dist}(y^{k};\Omega)\leq\mathrm{dist}(w^{k};\Omega)+\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}\leq\varepsilon.

Taking (
4.17
) into account and applying the result of
Lemma
4.2
(iii) leads us to the inequalities

\big|f(y^{k})-\bar{f}\big|^{\theta}\leq C\big\|b^{k}-\nabla g(y^{k})\big\|\leq C\left[2\big(\ell+1\big)\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}+\ell\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|\right].

(4.18)

In order to achieve the claimed convergence rates of the algorithm, we need to pass from the information of
f
at
y^{k}
to the iterate
u^{k+1}
in (
4.18
). To this end, combining (
3.6
), (
3.9
), (
4.10
), (
4.18
), and the decrease of
\{f(u^{k})\}
to
\bar{f}
from Lemma
4.2
tells us that

\begin{split}\left[f(u^{k+1})-\bar{f}\right]^{\theta}&=\left[f(u^{k+1})-f(w^{k})+f(w^{k})-f(y^{k})+f(y^{k})-\bar{f}\right]^{\theta}\\
&\leq\left[0+\big|f(w^{k})-f(y^{k})\big|+\big|f(y^{k})-\bar{f}\big|\right]^{\theta}\\
&\leq\big|f(w^{k})-f(y^{k})\big|^{\theta}+\big|f(y^{k})-\bar{f}\big|^{\theta}\\
&\leq\big(\ell\|w^{k}-y^{k}\|\big)^{\theta}+C\left[2\big(\ell+1\big)\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}+\ell\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|\right]\\
&\leq\ell\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}+C\left[2\big(\ell+1\big)\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2}+\ell\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|\right]\\
&\leq\widehat{C}\left[f(u^{k})-f(u^{k+1})\right]^{1/2},\end{split}

(4.19)

where the third inequality is due to
0^{\theta}=0
and the concavity of the function
t\mapsto t^{\theta}
as
t\geq 0
. Observe that
\widehat{C}
depends only on
\sigma_{g},\sigma_{h},\ell
, and
C
. Letting
\rho_{k}:=f(u^{k})-\bar{f}
as
k\in{\rm I\!N}
, it follows from the relationships in (
4.19
) that

\rho_{k+1}^{2\theta}\leq\widehat{C}^{2}\big(\rho_{k}-\rho_{k+1}\big)\;\text{ for all large }k\in{\rm I\!N}.

Case 2:
Assumption
(A3)
holds
. In this setting, a similar line of reasoning as presented in Case 1 can be applied. However,
the role of
y^{k}\in{\rm I\!B}(w^{k},\varepsilon^{1/2\theta}_{k}\big)
in (
4.17
) and (
4.18
) is now replaced by selecting
x^{k+1}\in{\rm I\!B}\big(u^{k+1},\varepsilon_{k}^{1/2\theta}\big)
.
In both cases, we eventually get the estimate

\rho^{2\theta}_{k+1}\leq\widetilde{C}(\rho_{k}-\rho_{k+1})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all large }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}

(4.20)

with some
\widetilde{C}>0
. Applying finally the standard device (see, e.g.,
[
3
, Lemma 4.8]
and the references therein) allows us to deduce from (
4.20
) the convergence rates claimed in assertions (i) and (ii) and thus to complete the proof of the theorem.

Observe that when
\mathcal{H}
is a finite-dimensional Euclidean space, the requirement that

\text{``every subsequence of $\{u^{k}\}$ has a convergent subsequence''},

(4.21)

imposed in (A4), is clearly equivalent to the boundedness of the iterates
\{u^{k}\}
. The latter assumption is frequently used in the studies examining convergence rates of DCA in finite dimensions; see
[
32
, Theorem 2]
for the exact case and
[
31
, Theorem 5]
in the presence of some inexactness. Below we discuss the availability of (
4.21
) in the general framework of infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.

Remark 4.5
.

If there exists a Banach space
X
with
compact embedding

X\subset\subset\mathcal{H}
(see, e.g.,
[
12
, page 286, Section 5.7]
) and
\{u^{k}\}
is bounded in
X
, then (
4.21
) is fulfilled. Indeed, any subsequence
\{u^{k_{n}}\}
of
\{u^{k}\}
is bounded in
X
. Then observe by the definition of compact embedding
X\subset\subset\mathcal{H}
that
\{u^{k_{n}}\}
has a subsequence that strongly converges to some limit in
\mathcal{H}
. The condition (
4.21
) is thus verified.

Consequently, a natural way to verify (
4.21
) is to establish an a priori bound for the iterates

\{u^{k}\}
in a more regular space

X
(for instance,
the (Sobolev)
H^{1}
or (bounded variation) BV-type space) although the convergence analysis is formulated in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
(e.g.,
L^{2}
), where the iterates are generated. In such situations, compactness results such as Rellich–Kondrachov
[
12
, Section 5.7]
ensure the compact embedding
X\subset\subset\mathcal{H}
and hence yield (
4.21
) automatically. In particular, (
4.21
) becomes a verifiable consequence of a standard
boundedness
estimate in
X
, rather than an abstract sequential
compactness
assumption on

\{u^{k}\}
itself.

Example 4.6
.

We aim to construct an example of a DC problem (
3.1
) on an
infinite-dimensional
Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, and a sequence of iterates
\{u^{k}\}
generated from Algorithm
2
that satisfy all the assumptions of Theorem
4.4
including (H1)–(H2) and (A1)–(A4).

Let
\mathcal{H}:=L^{2}(0,1)
be the Lebesgue space with the usual inner product

\langle u,v\rangle:=\int_{0}^{1}u(x)v(x)\,dx,\hskip 20.00003pt\|u\|:=\sqrt{\langle u,u\rangle}.

Define the function
e\in\mathcal{H}
by

e(x)\equiv 1\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }\hskip 10.00002ptx\in(0,1).

Then
\|e\|^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}1\,dx=1
. Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and define convex functions
g,h:\mathcal{H}\to{\rm I\!R}
by

g(u):=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2},\hskip 20.00003pth(u):=\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\langle u,e\rangle^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt(u\in\mathcal{H}).

In particular,
h
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
on
\mathcal{H}
and hence satisfies (H1).

The objective function

f(u):=g(u)-h(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\langle u,e\rangle^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt(u\in\mathcal{H})

is of the DC type on
\mathcal{H}
. Observe that

f(u)\geq\dfrac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}-\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\|u\|^{2}=\left(\dfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\right)\|u\|^{2}\geq 0=f(0)\;\mbox{ for all }\;u\in\mathcal{H},

(4.22)

and therefore condition (H2) is satisfied.

Next we verify that
f
possesses the Polyak-Łojasiewicz property. Since

\nabla f(u)=u-\alpha\,\langle u,e\rangle\,e,\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla f(0)=0\;\mbox{ as }\;u\in{\cal H},

we readily have the estimate

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f(u)-\nabla f(v),u-v\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle u-v-\alpha\,\langle u-v,e\rangle\,e\,,\,u-v\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\|u-v\|^{2}-\alpha\langle u-v,e\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\alpha)\|u-v\|^{2}\;\mbox{ for all }\;u,v\in\mathcal{H}

which comes from
|\langle u-v,e\rangle|\leq\|u-v\|
. In particular,
\nabla f(u)=0
yields
u=0
, so the
M
-stationary point is unique and equals
\overline{u}:=0
. Moreover, it follows from
[
28
, Theorems 5.40 and 6.10]
that
f
is strongly convex with modulus
1-\alpha
. Consequently, we arrive at the inequality

f(v)\geq f(u)+\langle\nabla f(u),v-u\rangle+\left(\dfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\right)\|v-u\|^{2},\hskip 10.00002ptu,v\in\mathcal{H}.

(4.23)

At
v=\overline{u}=0
, (
4.22
) and (
4.23
)
imply that

\displaystyle 0\leq f(u)-f(\overline{u})=f(u)-f(0)

\displaystyle\leq\langle\nabla f(u),u\rangle-\left(\dfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\right)\|u\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\left(\dfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\right)\left\|u-\dfrac{\nabla f(u)}{1-\alpha}\right\|^{2}+\dfrac{\big\|\nabla f(u)\big\|^{2}}{2(1-\alpha)}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\big\|\nabla f(u)\big\|^{2}}{2(1-\alpha)},

which brings us to the estimate

|f(u)-f(\overline{u})|^{1/2}\leq\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-\alpha)}}\|\nabla f(u)\|,\hskip 10.00002ptu\in\mathcal{H}.

Therefore, the Polyak-Łojasiewicz property of
f
(and thus assumption (A1)) holds with
C:=1/\sqrt{2(1-\alpha)}
,
\varepsilon>0
arbitrary, and
\theta:=1/2
. Furthermore, we deduce from

\nabla g(u)=u,\hskip 20.00003pt\nabla h(u)=\alpha\,\langle u,e\rangle\,e

that both
g
and
h
are
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
on
\mathcal{H}
with Lipschitz moduli
1
and
\alpha
, respectively. This tells us that the assumptions in (A2) and (A3) are fulfilled.

Finally, we specify a sequence
\{u^{k}\}
generated by Algorithm
2
such that (A4) holds for it. Let us start with
u^{0}:=e
. At each iteration, choose in Step 1
w^{k}:=u^{k}
. In Step 2, take

v^{k}:=\nabla h(w^{k})=\alpha\,\langle u^{k},e\rangle\,e.

In Step 3, compute
u^{k+1}
as the (exact) minimizer of

g(u)-\langle v^{k},u\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}-\langle v^{k},u\rangle=\dfrac{1}{2}\big\|u-v^{k}\big\|^{2}-\dfrac{1}{2}\big\|v^{k}\big\|^{2},

which clearly ensures that

u^{k+1}=v^{k}=\alpha\,\langle u^{k},e\rangle\,e,\hskip 10.00002ptk\geq 0.

Proceeding by induction leads us to

u^{k}=\alpha^{k}\,\langle u^{0},e\rangle\,e=\alpha^{k}e,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and hence}\hskip 10.00002pt\|u^{k}\|=\alpha^{k}\to 0\text{ as }k\to\infty.

In particular,
\{u^{k}\}
converges strongly to
0
in
L^{2}(0,1)
, which verifies (A4).

5
Optimal Control of Elliptic Equations with
L^{1-2}
Sparsity-Enhanced Regularization via Inexact Adaptive DCA

In this section, we use our main Algorithm
2
for infinite-dimensional DC optimization to design a constructive procedure for solving an optimal control problem governed by elliptic equations with sparsity regularization. The needed preliminaries on functional analysis, measure theory, and partial differential equations can be found in the standard references; see, e.g.,
[
12
,
38
]
.

Consider a nonempty open bounded set
\Omega\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}

(n=2,3)
whose boundary
\Gamma
is Lipschitz continuous and whose Lebesgue measure is denoted by
|\Omega|
. This framework clearly covers the case when
\Gamma
is
polygonal
, i.e.,
\Gamma
consists of a finite number of straight-line segments.

We are interested in the following
elliptic optimal control
problem
associated with an
L^{1-2}
regularization
and
hard control constraints
(see, e.g.,
[
17
,
37
,
38
]
):

\displaystyle\underset{y\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),\,u\in L^{2}(\Omega)}{\operatorname{minimize}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|y-y_{d}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|u-u_{d}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\beta\big(\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}-\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big)

subject to

\displaystyle Ay=\phi+u\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ in }\Omega,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\beta_{1}\leq u\leq\beta_{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ a.e. in }\Omega.

When
\beta=0
, the model corresponds to the well-known
optimal heat-source
problem (as in Tröltzsch
[
38
, Section 1.2]
) with
u(\cdot)
modeling a heat source in
\Omega
generated by, e.g., electromagnetic induction or microwaves. Stadler
[
37
]
encourages sparsity by augmenting the objective with the nonsmooth penalty
\beta\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}
. The
L^{1-2}
-regularized model, treated in
[
43
]
by using a completely different inexact DCA variant, is motivated by the computational studies in Yin et al.
[
41
]
, which show that
\ell^{1-2}:=\|\cdot\|_{1}-\|\cdot\|_{2}
persistently outperforms
\ell^{1}
in highly coherent settings (including image denoising) while yielding stronger sparsity and better recovery.

Note that the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition
y=0
on
\Gamma
is expressed by
y\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. We suppose that
y_{d}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
u_{d}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
\phi\in L^{3}(\Omega)\subset H^{-1}(\Omega)
, and the pointwise control bounds
\beta_{1}<0,\beta_{2}>0
are finite. The term with
\alpha>0
representing the cost of control, produces a smoother solution to problem (
5.1
). For this reason,
\alpha
is called a
regularization parameter
. The
sparsity-enhanced parameter

\beta>0
leads to an optimal control with a sparse structure so that its support is small. In simple terms, as
\beta
increases, the support of the optimal control becomes smaller. In the state equation,
A\in\mathscr{L}\big(H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),H^{-1}(\Omega)\big)
denotes the
elliptic
linear bounded operator from
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
to
H^{-1}(\Omega)
, the continuous dual of
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
defined by

Ay:=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\big[a_{ij}(x)y_{x_{i}}\big]_{x_{j}}+a_{0}(x)y\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }y\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

(5.2)

where
y_{x_{i}}
denotes the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
-partial derivative of
y
, the coefficients
\{a_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
are symmetric and Lipschitz continuous on the closure of
\Omega
, and where
a_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
is such that
a_{0}\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
. Furthermore,
A
satisfies the
H_{0}^{1}
-elliptic property
meaning that there exists
\theta>0
such that

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\geq\theta\|\xi\|^{2}\ \text{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega\text{ and for all }\xi\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

We first observe in the following remark that under our standing assumptions, the state equation in (
5.1
) possesses a unique solution
y=y(u)
.

Remark 5.1
.

(i)

For every
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, the elliptic state equation
Ay=\phi+u
possesses a
unique solution

y=y(u)\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. This is a direct consequence of
[
39
, Lemmas 9.2 and 9.3]
. For a more comprehensive result applicable to broader settings of the state equation, see Casas et al.
[
11
, Theorem 1.1.2]
.

(ii)

From (i), the relation
u\mapsto y(u)-y_{d}
is an
affine mapping
from
L^{2}(\Omega)
to
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, i.e., on
L^{2}(\Omega)
we have
y(u)=L(u)+w_{0}
for some linear mapping
L:L^{2}(\Omega)\to H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and for some
w_{0}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. In particular, the function
u\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\|y(u)-y_{d}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
is
convex
.

Now let us introduce the equivalent
reduced problem
associated with (
5.1
):

\begin{split}\underset{u\in L^{2}(\Omega)}{\operatorname{minimize}}\quad&\frac{1}{2}\big\|y(u)-y_{d}\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|u-u_{d}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\beta\big(\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}-\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big)\\
\text{subject to}\quad&\beta_{1}\leq u\leq\beta_{2}\quad\text{ a.e. in }\Omega.\end{split}

(5.3)

We set
U:=L^{2}(\Omega)
, where
L^{2}(\Omega)
is identified with its dual by the Riesz representation theorem, and set
Y:=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. Denote the set of
admissible controls
as

U_{{\rm ad}}:=\{u\in U\mid\beta_{1}\leq u\leq\beta_{2}\ \text{ a.e. in }\ \Omega\}.

(5.4)

Since the strong convergence in
L^{2}(\Omega)
yields almost everywhere convergence up to a subsequence,
U_{{\rm ad}}
is a nonempty closed convex subset of
U
. Define the functions
g,h\in\Gamma(U)
by

\displaystyle g(u):=\frac{1}{2}\|y(u)-y_{d}\|_{U}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|u-u_{d}\|^{2}_{U}+\beta\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\delta_{{U}_{{\rm ad}}}(u)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }\;u\in U,

(5.5)

\displaystyle h(u):=\beta\|u\|_{U}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }\;u\in U

(5.6)

via the
indicator function

\delta_{{U}_{{\rm ad}}}
of
{U}_{{\rm ad}}
given by
\delta_{{U}_{{\rm ad}}}(u)=0
when
u\in{U}_{{\rm ad}}
, and
\infty
otherwise. Then we represent the reduced problem (
5.3
) as a
DC optimization problem
of the form

\underset{u\in U}{\operatorname{minimize}}\;\;\;f(u):=g(u)-h(u),

(5.7)

in which
g
is strongly convex with
\sigma_{g}=\alpha
while
\sigma_{h}=0
.

To be able to apply our inexact adaptive DC algorithm to problem (
5.7
) with the above data, observe first that the function
h(u)=\beta\|u\|_{U}
is differentiable with the gradient
\nabla h(u)=\beta u/\|u\|_{U}
at each
u\neq 0
and that
0
is a subgradient of
h
at
u=0
. Regarding Step 2 of Algorithm
2
, we choose in each iteration the vector

v^{k}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }w^{k}=0,\\
\beta\dfrac{w^{k}}{\|w^{k}\|_{U}}&\text{if }w^{k}\neq 0.\end{cases}

This results in solving the subproblem

u^{k+1}\in\begin{cases}\varepsilon_{k}\text{-}\mathrm{argmin}_{u\in U}g(u)&\text{if }w^{k}=0,\\
\varepsilon_{k}\text{-}\mathrm{argmin}_{u\in U}\Bigg[g(u)-\Bigg\langle\beta\dfrac{w^{k}}{\|w^{k}\|_{U}},u\Bigg\rangle_{U}\Bigg]&\text{if }w^{k}\neq 0,\end{cases}

(5.8)

and leads us to the following elaboration of Algorithm
2
to
solve (
5.7
).

Algorithm 3

I-ADCA-PDECO: Inexact Adaptive DCA for PDE Control

Initialization:

u^{0}\in{U}_{{\rm ad}}
,
reduction factor
\gamma\in(0,1),\;\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1]
.

Set
k=0
.

1:

Choose
w^{k}
satisfying
f(w^{k})\leq f(u^{k})
.

2:

Find
u^{k+1}\in U
such that

u^{k+1}\in\begin{cases}\varepsilon_{k}\text{-}\mathrm{argmin}_{u\in U}g(u)&\text{if }w^{k}=0,\\[5.0pt]
\varepsilon_{k}\text{-}\mathrm{argmin}_{u\in U}\Bigg[g(u)-\Bigg\langle\beta\dfrac{w^{k}}{\|w^{k}\|_{U}},u\Bigg\rangle_{U}\Bigg]&\text{if }w^{k}\neq 0.\end{cases}

3:

While

\varepsilon_{k}>\frac{\sigma_{g}+\sigma_{h}}{32}\big\|u^{k+1}-w^{k}\big\|^{2},

set
\varepsilon_{k}\leftarrow\gamma\,\varepsilon_{k}
and restart from Step 2 with the new
\varepsilon_{k}
.

4:

Choose
\varepsilon_{k+1}\in(0,\varepsilon_{k}]
and set
k\leftarrow k+1
.

Until
Stopping criteria.

It follows from Section
3
that Algorithm
3
is
well-defined
since the assumptions in (H1) and (H2) are fulfilled as discussed below.

Remark 5.2
.

(i)

The function
h:=\beta\|\cdot\|_{U}
is clearly continuous and satisfies (H1).

(ii)

Assumption (H2) holds for the DC problem (
5.7
). Indeed, for
u\in{U}_{{\rm ad}}
we get

\|u\|_{U}\leq|\Omega|^{1/2}\cdot\max\{|\beta_{1}|,|\beta_{2}|\}<\infty.

Thus it easily follows from (
5.5
) and (
5.6
) that

\inf_{u\in U}f(u)=\inf_{u\in U}\big[g(u)-h(u)\big]\geq\inf_{u\in U}\big[-\beta\|u\|_{U}\big]>-\infty.

Therefore, the sequences
\{u^{k}\}
,
\{w^{k}\}
, and
\{v^{k}\}
generated by Algorithm
3
from any starting point
u^{0}\in{U}_{{\rm ad}}
, are well-defined.

(iii)

Subproblem (
5.8
) admits a unique solution
u^{k+1}
when
\varepsilon_{k}=0
. Indeed, since the objective functions in (
5.8
) are convex, continuous, and bounded from below, the proof easily follows from that of either Ulbrich
[
39
, Lemma 9.4]
, or Casas et al.
[
11
, Theorem 1.2.6]
. The strongly convex term
\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\|u-u_{d}\|^{2}_{U}
in
g(\cdot)
from (
5.5
) clearly yields the solution uniqueness.

6
Finite Element Discretization and Numerical Implementation

For solving subproblem (
5.8
) in Algorithm
3
numerically, we make use of the so-called “first discretize then optimize” approach beginning with a suitable discretization of the problem based on a
finite element approximation
. The ideal scenario is described as follows.

6.1
Finite Element Scheme

Suppose that
\Omega\subset{\rm I\!R}^{2}
is an open bounded convex set whose boundary is
polygonal
. Following Glowinski
[
14
, Chapter IV, Section 2.5]
, we consider a family of regular triangulations
\{\mathcal{T}^{h}\}_{0<h\leq 1}
of
\Omega
with the mesh size
h>0
possessing the properties:

•

Each triangulation

\mathcal{T}^{h}
is defined by

\mathcal{T}^{h}:=\left\{T_{i}^{h}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{2}\;\Big|\;T_{i}^{h}\text{ is a closed triangle, }i=1,\ldots,t^{h}\right\}\text{ for some }t^{h}\in{\rm I\!N}.

•

\bigcup_{i=1}^{t^{h}}T_{i}^{h}=\overline{\Omega}
, the closure of
\Omega
.

•

For all
i\neq j
, we have that
\operatorname{int}T_{i}^{h}\cap\operatorname{int}T_{j}^{h}=\varnothing
and that
T_{i}^{h}\cap T_{j}^{h}
must either be a common edge, or a common vertex, or the empty set.

•

The mesh size
h
stands for the length of the longest edge among all triangles within the triangulation
\mathcal{T}^{h}
, and
h_{T}
denotes the length of the longest edge in a particular triangle
T\in\mathcal{T}^{h}
. For each
T\in\mathcal{T}^{h}
, let
R_{T}
be the diameter of the largest ball contained in
T
. Moving on, suppose that there exist two
constants
\rho,R>0
satisfying

\dfrac{h_{T}}{R_{T}}\leq R,\hskip 10.00002pt\dfrac{h}{h_{T}}\leq\rho\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }\;T\in\mathcal{T}^{h},\;\text{ for all }\;0<h\leq 1.

Then we define the sets

\displaystyle Y^{h}

\displaystyle:=\left\{y^{h}\in C(\overline{\Omega})\;\Big|\;y^{h}|_{T}\text{ is (affine) linear for all }T\in\mathcal{T}^{h}\text{ and }y^{h}|_{\Gamma}=0\right\}\subset Y,\text{ and}

\displaystyle U^{h}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{u^{h}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\;\Big|\;u^{h}|_{T}\text{ is constant for all }T\in\mathcal{T}^{h}\Big\}\subset U,

as finite-dimensional counterparts (cf. Ciarlet
[
10
]
) to
Y=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and
U=L^{2}(\Omega)
, respectively.

Let
\Sigma^{h}
be the set of all vertices in the triangulation
\mathcal{T}^{h}
while
\Sigma_{0}^{h}:=\{P_{1}^{h},\ldots,P_{v^{h}}^{h}\}

(v^{h}\in{\rm I\!N})
is the collection of all interior vertices. By
[
20
, Lemma 2.10]
, for each inner vertex
P_{i}^{h}

(i=1,\ldots,v^{h})
there exists a unique function
\psi^{h}_{i}\in Y^{h}
satisfying
\psi^{h}_{i}(P_{i}^{h})=1
and
\psi^{h}_{i}(Q)=0
for all
Q\in\Sigma^{h}\setminus\{P^{h}_{i}\}
. Obviously, the collection
\{\psi^{h}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{v^{h}}
forms a basis for
Y^{h}
and each
y^{h}\in Y^{h}
can be represented as the sum

y^{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{v^{h}}y^{h}(P_{i}^{h})\psi_{i}^{h}.

On the other hand, each element
u^{h}\in U^{h}
admits the unique representation

u^{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{t^{h}}u^{h}_{i}\chi_{i}^{h}

via the characteristic function
\chi_{i}^{h}
of the triangle
T_{i}^{h}\in\mathcal{T}^{h}
and the constant
u_{i}^{h}:=u^{h}|_{T_{i}^{h}}
. In this case,
\{\chi_{i}^{h}\}_{i=1}^{t^{h}}
is a basis for
U^{h}
satisfying
[
40
, Assumption 4.2]
. Then for each
u^{h}\in U^{h}
, we define in the spirit of (
5.1
)-(
5.2
) the
associated discrete state
as the unique solution
y^{h}(u^{h})\in Y^{h}
to

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}a_{ij}y^{h}_{x_{i}}\varphi^{h}_{x_{j}}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}a_{0}y^{h}\varphi^{h}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}(\phi+u^{h})\varphi^{h}\,dx\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }\varphi^{h}\in Y^{h},

(6.1)

the existence of which is a direct application of Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem (see
[
11
, page 135]
). Further, the
discrete control-to-state mapping

u^{h}\in U^{h}\mapsto y^{h}(u^{h})\in Y^{h}
is of class
{\cal C}^{2}
; see
[
39
, page 212]
. The discrete counterpart set
{U}_{{\rm ad}}^{h}
to
{U}_{{\rm ad}}
in (
5.4
) is given as

{U}_{{\rm ad}}^{h}:=\big\{u^{h}\in U^{h}\;\big|\;\beta_{1}\leq u^{h}_{i}\leq\beta_{2}\,\text{ for all }\,i=1,\ldots,t^{h}\big\}.

Now we replace the exact version of the subproblem in (
5.8
) associated with some
w^{k}\in U\setminus\{0\}
by its finite-dimensional
discretized form
:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{u^{h}\in{U}_{{\rm ad}}^{h}}{\operatorname{minimize}}&\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\big[y^{h}(u^{h})-y_{d}\big]^{2}\,dx+\displaystyle\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega}\big(u^{h}-u_{d}\big)^{2}\,dx\\
&+\,\beta\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{t^{h}}|u^{h}_{i}|\cdot|T_{i}^{h}|-\displaystyle\dfrac{\beta}{\|w^{k}\|_{U}}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w^{k}u^{h}\,dx.\end{array}

(6.2)

The
existence
and
uniqueness
of the optimal solution to the discrete problem (
6.2
) are ensured by a slight modification of the proofs in
[
39
, Lemma 9.3]
and
[
11
, Theorem 1.4.7]
by taking into account that the objective function in (
6.2
) is continuous and strongly convex and that the discrete admissible set
{U}^{h}_{\text{ad}}
is closed and convex being a weakly sequentially closed subset of
U
. The following
first-order necessary optimality conditions
for (
6.2
) can be deduced from
[
11
, Equations (1.4.11)–(1.4.13), (1.4.15)–(1.4.17)]
, where the formula for subdifferentials of the
L^{1}
-norm function is utilized
[
11
, page 137]
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\overline{u}^{h}\in U^{h}
be the optimal solution to problem (
6.2
). Then there exist
\overline{y}^{h},\overline{z}^{h}\in Y^{h}
, and
\overline{\lambda}^{h}\in\partial\big(\|\cdot\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\big)(\overline{u}^{h})
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}a_{ij}\overline{y}^{h}_{x_{i}}\varphi^{h}_{x_{j}}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}a_{0}\overline{y}^{h}\varphi^{h}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\big(\phi+\overline{u}^{h}\big)\varphi^{h}\,dx\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }\varphi^{h}\in Y^{h},

(6.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}a_{ij}\varphi^{h}_{x_{i}}\overline{z}^{h}_{x_{j}}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}a_{0}\varphi^{h}\overline{z}^{h}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\big(\overline{y}^{h}-y_{d}\big)\varphi^{h}\,dx\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }\varphi^{h}\in Y^{h},

(6.4)

\displaystyle\overline{u}^{h}_{l}=\mathrm{proj}_{[\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]}\left[-\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\left(q^{h}_{l}-\mathrm{proj}_{[\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]}q^{h}_{l}\right)\right]\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }l=1,\ldots,t^{h},

(6.5)

where each
q^{h}_{l}
is an average integral given by

q^{h}_{l}:=\dfrac{1}{|T_{l}^{h}|}\int_{T^{h}_{l}}\left(\overline{z}^{h}-\alpha u_{d}-\dfrac{\beta}{\|w^{k}\|_{U}}w^{k}\right)dx.

(6.6)

Building on this, a concrete numerical implementation of the discretized problem (
6.2
) associated with the distributed optimal control problem for elliptic equation is presented in Subsection

via solving the system of first-order optimality conditions
(
6.3
)–(
6.5
) by using the
semismooth Newton method
; see, e.g., Hintermüller et al.
[
16
]
, Stadler
[
37
]
, and Ulbrich
[
39
]
.

The next step is to demonstrate the convergence of the discretized solutions
\overline{u}_{h}
to the continuous one
\overline{u}
as
h\to 0
and
to establish
error estimates/rates of convergence
for the difference between
\overline{u}_{h}
and
\overline{u}
by means of the finite element discretization under consideration.

Theorem 6.2
.

Denote by
\overline{u}
and
\overline{u}^{h}
, respectively, the optimal solution to the second exact subproblem in (
5.8
) and to the discrete version (
6.2
) with respect to a discretization parameter
h\in(0,1]
. Then there exists
a constant
C>0
independent of
h
for which the
error estimate

\big\|\overline{u}_{h}-\overline{u}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq Ch

(6.7)

holds for all
h
sufficiently small. Consequently,
\big\|\overline{u}_{h}-\overline{u}\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\widehat{C}h
for some
\widehat{C}>0
.

[
Proof.]
By
[
11

and the construction of (
5.8

[
11
, Theorem 1.3.3]
is justified with
\delta:=\alpha

[

gives us a constant

ensuring the claimed error estimate (

Numerical Experiments

This part demonstrates numerical performance of the proposed I-ADCA-PDECO algorithm for sparse PDE-constrained optimization problem. All of our results are implemented in MATLAB R2024b running on a PC with Ryzen 5900X CPU and 32GB of memory. Our codes are built on top of the PDE-constrained optimization framework developed by Antil
[
1
]
.

We begin by introducing the algorithmic configuration settings, which are applied to all the examples below. The discretization was implemented by using the standard piecewise linear finite element approach, i.e., 5-point stencil. In all the numerical experiments, we evaluate the accuracy of an approximate optimal solution via the corresponding residual error by choosing zeros as the initial point. The stopping criterion for Algorithm
3
is given by

\frac{\|u^{k+1}-u^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{\max\{\|u^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,,1\}}\leq\varepsilon.

We terminate the algorithm when either
\varepsilon<10^{-12}
, or the maximum number of iterations equal to 20 is reached. Consider the problem:

\displaystyle\underset{y\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),\,u\in L^{2}(\Omega)}{\operatorname{minimize}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|y-y_{d}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|u-u_{d}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\beta\big(\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}-\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big)

subject to

\displaystyle Ay=\phi+u\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ in }\Omega,

(6.8)

\displaystyle\beta_{1}\leq u\leq\beta_{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ a.e. in }\Omega

in which
\Omega:=[0,1]\times[0,1]
and
A:=-\Delta
is the
Laplacian operator
, i.e., in (
5.2
) we let
a_{0}\equiv 0
and

Kronecker delta

(

(6.9)

(6.10)

Concluding Remarks and Future Research

In this work, we designed and justified an inexact adaptive DC algorithm in Hilbert spaces and applied it to sparse elliptic optimal control problems with an
L^{1-2}
-type regularization. The proposed scheme called I-ADCA (Algorithm
2
) allows both inexact subgradients and inexact solutions of the convex subproblems while preserving global convergence to stationary points and, under a Polyak-Łojasiewicz-type property and mild regularity assumptions, guaranteeing explicit convergence rates. Specializing the designed algorithm to PDE-constrained optimization led us to develop the I-ADCA-PDECO method (Algorithm
3
), for which we established well-posedness, uniqueness of the subproblems, and finite element error estimates. The conducted numerical experiments illustrated the ability of I-ADCA-PDECO to compute sparse controls with good performance in a benchmark distributed control problem.

Among major directions of our future research, we mention further investigations of the convergence rates for the inexact adaptive DC algorithm I-ADCA under appropriate versions of the Polyak-Łojasiewicz property, especially in the case of lower exponents
\theta\in(0,1/2)
. We also plan to develop our approach to design and justify an appropriate inexact adaptive counterpart of the
boosted DCA
(BDCA) in Hilbert spaces; cf.
[
3
,
7
]
for exact versions in finite dimensions. Our future interests in this direction include the design and complexity analysis of acceleration versions of the inexact infinite-dimensional BDCA with both nonsmooth components, which exhibit significant new features in comparison with partially smooth counterparts; see
[
13
]
for the plain framework in finite dimensions. The proposed algorithmic developments will be tested in applications to new classes of regularized problems of PDE-constrained optimization to promote their sparsity and reduce computational costs.
