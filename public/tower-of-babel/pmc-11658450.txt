Impaired liver function: effect on paclitaxel toxicity, dose modifications and overall survival
Authors: Marieke Schmidt, Robin Vernooij, Merel van Nuland, Erin Smeijsters, Lot Devriese, Nadia Haj Mohammad, Thom Hermens, Julian Stammers, Christina Swart, Toine Egberts, Saskia Haitjema, Laureen Lammers
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe anticancer drug paclitaxel is primarily metabolized in the liver. Previous studies have indicated a correlation between impaired liver function and paclitaxel toxicity, which may indicate dose reduction. Since the evidence is limited, the aim of this study was to investigate the effect of impaired liver function on the hematological toxicity of paclitaxel, dose modifications and overall survival (OS).MethodsFor this single-center retrospective observational study, patients treated with paclitaxel for breast, esophageal and ovarian cancer at the University Medical Centre Utrecht between 2011 and 2022 were identified from the Utrecht Patient Oriented Database (UPOD). Based on regression analysis, the risk of developing Grade 3/4 hematological toxicity was compared between patients with normal and impaired (based on the NCI criteria for bilirubin and ASAT (aspartate aminotransferase) concentrations) liver function. Additionally, differences in the occurrence of toxicity-related dose modifications and OS were evaluated between the two groups.ResultsA total of 569 patients were included. Breast cancer patients who were receiving advanced treatment and had mildly impaired liver function (ASAT ≤ 2x ULN, bilirubin ≤ ULN) had an increased risk of developing grade 3/4 neutropenia (HR = 4.39, 95% CI 1.20-16.02; p = 0.03). In addition, patients with impaired liver function treated according to the advanced ovarian cancer regimen had an increased risk of developing grade 3/4 leukopenia (HR = 12.64, 95% CI 2.12–75.22, p = 0.01) and dose modification (treatment discontinuation) (HR = 3.91, 95% CI 1.74–8.79, p < 0.01). Impaired liver function was also associated with decreased OS in inoperable esophageal and advanced ovarian cancer patients (HR = 7.65, 95% CI 2.54–23.1, p < 0.01 and HR = 2.98, 95% CI 1.36–6.54, p < 0.01, respectively). The risk of developing grade 3/4 hematological toxicity during lower-dose paclitaxel treatment protocols was not significantly different in patients with impaired liver function.ConclusionsThis study revealed that patients with impaired liver function treated with paclitaxel for breast and ovarian cancer in an advanced setting are at greater risk of developing hematological toxicity than patients with normal liver function at the start of therapy. Furthermore, in patients with ovarian (advanced) or inoperable esophageal cancer, impaired liver function is associated with decreased OS. Within these groups of patients, it is important to weigh the risk of upfront paclitaxel dose modifications versus an adaptive strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13330-2.

Background

Many drugs, including oncological medicines, are substantially metabolized by the liver. In patients with impaired liver function, the metabolic capacity of the liver can be reduced, potentially increasing the risk of toxicity [1, 2]. To prevent toxicity within this group of patients, dose adjustments may be necessary [3].

Adequate dosing is particularly important in oncology patients [4, 5]. For example, the cytotoxic agent paclitaxel (Taxol®) is a highly active cytotoxic antineoplastic agent widely used in solid tumors with broad antitumour activity. Paclitaxel is primarily metabolized in the liver by CYP2C8 and CYP3A4, after which metabolites are predominantly eliminated via biliary excretion [6].

Although paclitaxel is a frequently used cytotoxic agent, research on the impact of impaired liver function on paclitaxel dosing is limited. The Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC), as part of the marketing authorization of paclitaxel, states that inadequate data are available to recommend dosage alterations in patients with mild to moderate hepatic impairments [7, 8]. A few small studies have indicated a correlation between hepatic impairment, determined by elevated transaminase levels and/or total bilirubin concentration, and the risk of hematological toxicity, an exposure-dependent side effect of paclitaxel [9–11]. Hence, in a recently published review by Krens et al., upfront paclitaxel dose reduction was recommended for patients with impaired liver function based on these parameters [4, 5]. This advice is mainly based on two pharmacokinetic studies by Joerger et al. (n = 35) and Briasoulis et al. (n = 9) [9, 11]. Both have shown a relationship between liver impairment and paclitaxel exposure.

Apart from the limited amount of evidence, the definition of impaired liver function in the literature in relation to dosing recommendations varies. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) recommend using the Child‒Pugh classification [12–14]. However, since underlying diseases, such as cancer metastasis or cachexia, can cause alterations in Child‒Pugh classification components (e.g., albumin level, encephalopathy and ascites), the National Cancer Institute (NCIc) classification, based on total bilirubin or ASAT (aspartate aminotransferase) concentrations, is commonly used in oncology clinical practice [15]. In addition, studies of patients with impaired liver function based on transaminase levels and/or total bilirubin concentrations have used different cutoff values for the definition of impaired liver function. Importantly, transaminase levels are not always associated with liver disease but are correlated with an advanced clinical stage of cancer, which also influences treatment outcome [16–18]. This complicates a comprehensive assessment of all study results.

To achieve more evidence-based paclitaxel dose recommendations in patients with clearly defined impaired liver function, additional research is warranted. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the effect of impaired liver function (defined by the NCI criteria) on paclitaxel hematological toxicity, dose modifications and overall survival at different dosing regimens in patients with breast, esophageal and ovarian cancer.

Methods

Study setting

This single-center retrospective observational study was conducted at the University Medical Centre Utrecht (UMC Utrecht). The UMC Utrecht is an academic hospital located in the Netherlands. For this study, data from the Utrecht Patient Oriented Database (UPOD), which comprises data on patient characteristics, medication orders, and laboratory tests for all patients treated at the UMC Utrecht, were used [19]. Data were prospectively documented (collected with a forward-looking approach) in order to provide a more accurate and systematic analysis of the historical data.

Furthermore, details regarding paclitaxel treatment were collected from the hospitals’ software program for prescribing, preparing and administering chemotherapies (BD Cato™) [20]. In total, 5 different paclitaxel treatment regimens for 3 different clinical curative and advanced indications were selected according to the paclitaxel dose (ranging from 50 mg/m2 to 175 mg/m2 per administration), frequency and total number of administrations within one treatment course (Table 1).

Study population

All patients treated with paclitaxel for breast, esophageal, or ovarian cancer at the UMC Utrecht between January 2011 and January 2022 were eligible for inclusion in this study. Patients (≥ 18 years) were included if they were treated according to a specific treatment regimen (Table 1) or if hematological laboratory values (neutrophil count, white blood cell count and platelet count) were available at baseline (with a maximum of 14 days prior to the start of paclitaxel therapy) and throughout the course of treatment.

Patients were excluded if they had hematological toxicity (≥ grade 1 neutropenia, leukopenia or thrombocytopenia) at the start of paclitaxel treatment according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, version 5.0) [21]. In addition, patients who received an adjusted starting dose of paclitaxel compared to the treatment protocol (Table 1) were excluded. Males were excluded from the breast cancer protocol.

Patients were divided into two groups based on liver function at the start of paclitaxel therapy: those with impaired or normal liver function. Liver function was classified by ASAT and total bilirubin concentrations according to the National Cancer Institute (NCI) criteria (Table 2) [15]. In the absence of baseline liver parameters, patients were assigned to the normal liver function group based on the assumption that there was no clinical indication to determine these parameters and thus to expect liver impairment.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measures of the study were differences in the occurrence of hematological toxicity (neutropenia, leukopenia and thrombocytopenia) between patients with normal liver function and patients with impaired liver function at the start of paclitaxel therapy within the different treatment regimens for breast, esophageal or ovarian cancer (Table 1). Hematological toxicity was defined as a grade 3, 4 or 5 adverse event according to the Common Terminology Board (CTCAE) criteria of the National Cancer Institute (Table 3) [21].

The secondary outcomes were differences in OS and the occurrence of paclitaxel-related toxicity dose modifications, including dose reduction, dose delay and early treatment discontinuation, between the two groups. Dose reductions were taken into account when the dose was reduced by ≥ 20% compared to the previous paclitaxel dose. Dose delay was defined as a delay in paclitaxel administration of ≥ 5 days compared to the treatment protocol (Table 1). Early treatment discontinuation was defined as the number of administrations specified in the treatment protocol that had not been achieved. Treatment discontinuation was not assessed in patients treated according to the advanced breast cancer regimen since this protocol does not consist of a fixed number of paclitaxel doses per treatment course.

Statistical analysis

Because of differences in paclitaxel dose between the treatment regimens studied (Table 1), analyses were performed per treatment. Patient characteristics at baseline were analyzed using descriptive statistics and independent sample t tests (normally distributed data) and Mann–Whitney U tests (not normally distributed data). The Shapiro‒Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data distribution. Per paclitaxel treatment protocol (Table 1), Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to evaluate (time-to-event) differences in (1) development of grade 3/4 hematological toxicity which was assessed by differences in time to nadir of neutrophil, platelet and white blood cell count. The nadir is defined as the point at which the count is lowest after the first paclitaxel treatment cycle. Furthermore, Cox regression analyses were performed to evaluate (time-to-event) differences in (2) overall survival (OS) and (3) differences in the risk of dose modifications (dose reduction, dose delay, and treatment discontinuation) between patients with normal and impaired liver function at the start of therapy. Age and sex were found to be significantly associated with the outcome parameters and were therefore included as covariates in the multivariable time-to-event regression analyses. A p value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All the statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 [22].

Results

Patient characteristics

In total, 631 patients were treated with paclitaxel for breast, esophageal, or ovarian cancer between 2011 and 2022. Among those patients, 29 were excluded due to hematological toxicity (CTCAE grade ≥ 1) at the start of paclitaxel therapy, and 26 were excluded due to an adjusted paclitaxel starting dose compared to the protocol. Furthermore, seven males were excluded from the breast cancer protocol (Fig. 1). Overall, 569 patients were included: 65 patients in the advanced breast cancer group (29 patients with normal liver function and 36 patients with impaired liver function at the start of paclitaxel therapy), 220 patients in the curative/advanced ovarian cancer group (178 patients with normal liver function and 42 patients with impaired liver function), 55 patients in the advanced ovarian cancer group (46 patients with normal liver function and 9 patients with impaired liver function), 172 patients in the curative (5x weekly) esophageal cancer group (151 patients with normal liver function and 21 patients with impaired liver function) and 57 patients in the curative (6x weekly) esophageal cancer group (52 patients with normal liver function and 5 patients with impaired liver function) (Table 4). In all protocols, hematological laboratory values at baseline were comparable between patients with normal and impaired liver function at the start of paclitaxel treatment (Table 4). Only in the advanced breast cancer group were patients with impaired liver function slightly younger than patients with normal liver function at the start of therapy (56.5 vs. 61.0 years; p = 0.04). As expected according to the inclusion criteria for both groups, the median ASAT concentration was significantly greater in the groups with impaired liver function. However, the total bilirubin concentration was not significantly different between the groups, as there were only three patients with an increased bilirubin concentration (> 21 µmol/L) at the start of the study. Therefore, the classification of liver function was mainly based on ASAT concentrations. Additionally, patients with severely impaired liver function (ASAT > 2x ULN and bilirubin > ULN) were absent. For the distribution of patients among the NCI grades for total bilirubin and ASAT per paclitaxel treatment protocol, see Table 5. Furthermore, liver parameters at baseline were absent in 27% (total bilirubin) and 8.4% (ASAT) of the patients on average (Table 5). Based on the assumption that there was no clinical indication to determine these parameters and thus to expect liver impairment, these patients were assigned to the normal liver function group. Supplemental Tables 1 and Supplemental Table 2 show the results of the corresponding sensitivity analysis, which support this assumption. Furthermore, Supplemental Tables 3 and 4 show the results of the univariate analyses.

Primary outcomes

In the advanced breast cancer group, grade 3/4 neutropenia occurred in 33.3% of patients with impaired liver function at the start of paclitaxel therapy compared to 10.3% of patients with normal liver function (HR = 4.39, 95% CI 1.20-16.02; p = 0.03; Table 6; Fig. 2). Furthermore, 33.3% of the ovarian cancer patients with impaired liver function treated conformed to the advanced regimen experienced grade 3/4 leukopenia, whereas 6.5% of the patients with normal liver function at the start of therapy experienced grade 3/4 leukopenia (HR = 12.64, 95% CI 2.12–75.22; p = 0.01; Table 6; Fig. 2). No other significant differences in the occurrence of Grade 3/4 hematological toxicity were observed between patients with normal and impaired liver function at the start of paclitaxel therapy. No grade 5 toxicity was observed.

Discussion

In this retrospective observational study, the effect of hepatic impairment (based on the NCI criteria) on paclitaxel hematological toxicity and overall survival was investigated in patients with breast, esophageal and ovarian cancer receiving different dosing regimens. The results showed that patients with impaired liver function treated with paclitaxel for breast cancer (90 mg/m2 paclitaxel three times every four weeks) had an increased risk of developing grade 3 or 4 neutropenia compared to patients with normal liver function at the start of paclitaxel therapy. Toxicity-based dose reductions in paclitaxel also tended to occur more often in this group of patients than in breast cancer patients with normal liver function (44.4% versus 31%, p = 0.14). However, this difference was not statistically significant and may be caused by the use of supportive care treatment, such as granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF). Information about the use of G-CSF was not available at the time of analysis. Apart from adjusting the paclitaxel dosage during subsequent administrations within the treatment protocol, G-CSF is often used as a supportive treatment in patients with paclitaxel-induced neutropenia [23].

Furthermore, our results showed that patients with impaired liver function treated for ovarian cancer (175 mg/m2 paclitaxel in combination with carboplatin AUC 5 every three weeks, advanced treatment) had an increased risk of developing grade 3 or 4 leukopenia and discontinued treatment earlier. Interestingly, these results were observed only in patients treated according to the advanced ovarian protocol, while the paclitaxel dosage and frequency in both the curative/advanced and advanced protocols were the same (175 mg/m2 every three weeks), and the concurrent, nonadjusted dose of carboplatin, which also causes myelosuppression, was lower in the advanced regimen. The advanced protocol is typically applied in cases of disease progression after a clinically relevant response to 1st -line carboplatin-based therapy. Patients treated in accordance with the advanced protocol are likely to have a lower bone marrow reserve due to previous therapies, possibly increasing susceptibility to hematological toxicity. The impact of an advanced clinical stage of cancer can also be seen in our findings of impaired liver function on OS. Although the number of patients with impaired liver function was relatively small, patients with impaired liver function and inoperable esophageal cancer and advanced ovarian cancer had a worse OS than patients with normal liver function at the start of treatment. Nevertheless, our results should be interpreted with caution given the relatively small event rate, which made confounding adjustment impossible. A similar finding was shown by Lu et al. [16]. They showed that a higher pretreatment ASAT is associated with poorer OS in patients with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. Based on the NCI criteria, the classification of impaired liver function in our study was based mainly on ASAT concentrations. Although the underlying relationship between ASAT and cancer activity has not been fully elucidated, other studies have also demonstrated that ASAT levels are significantly associated with an unfavorable prognosis in other cancers, such as hepatocellular and renal cell carcinoma, pancreatic cancer, NSCLC, and breast cancer [17, 18, 24–26].

Regarding the relatively high-dose paclitaxel protocols for breast and advanced ovarian cancer, the results of this study are in line with previous pharmacokinetic studies in which a relationship between liver impairment and susceptibility to hematologic toxicity was shown [9, 11]. However, our results also showed that hepatic impairment did not affect the risk of developing grade 3 or 4 hematological toxicity in patients treated with the two lower-dose paclitaxel regimens for esophageal cancer (50 mg/m2 paclitaxel weekly). The disparity in the results of the different treatment protocols shows that the effects of hepatic impairment on paclitaxel toxicity depend not only on biomarkers for liver function but also on paclitaxel dose and dosing schedule. For treatment regimens in which paclitaxel is administered at a relatively low dose and over a short period of time (50 mg/m2 weekly for a maximum of 5 or 6 weeks), dose reductions in patients with mildly impaired liver function at the start of paclitaxel therefore seem unnecessary to prevent toxicity. Given the absence of patients with severely impaired liver function in this study, adequate monitoring for paclitaxel-related toxicity in these patients is recommended.

Despite our observations in breast cancer and (advanced) ovarian cancer patients with impaired liver function, one may question the necessity of adjusting the paclitaxel dosage at the start of therapy, as advised by Krens et al. [4, 5]. Although dose reduction can prevent paclitaxel toxicity, this may also affect treatment effectiveness, which is at least as important in oncology patients. Although our study period was more than ten years, patients with severely impaired liver function were absent, and only three patients with increased bilirubin concentrations (> 21 µmol/L) at the start of paclitaxel treatment were included. Therefore, the classification of liver function in our study was mainly based on ASAT concentrations, with median values in the impaired liver function groups of ≤ 2x the ULN. Instead of proactively reducing the dosage at the start of paclitaxel treatment in patients with mildly impaired liver function (ASAT concentrations ≤ 2x ULN and normal bilirubin concentrations), paclitaxel treatment may be started with the full treatment protocol dosage followed by close monitoring of paclitaxel toxicity together with corresponding supportive care and dose modifications during treatment, if necessary.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study showed that patients with mildly impaired liver function treated with paclitaxel for breast and ovarian cancer are at greater risk of developing hematological toxicity than patients with normal liver function at the start of therapy. This could suggest that these patients should be treated with a lower paclitaxel dosage. However, within this group of patients, it is important to weigh the risk of treatment ineffectiveness by immediately reducing the paclitaxel dose at the start of treatment against the occurrence of adverse events when starting with the full dosage followed by appropriate follow-up of the treatment as an adaptive strategy.

Our results also showed that hepatic impairment in patients with ovarian (advanced) or inoperable esophageal cancer is associated with decreased OS. Furthermore, impaired liver function did not affect the risk of developing Grade 3 or 4 hematological toxicity in patients treated with lower-dose paclitaxel regimens for esophageal cancer. Given the absence of patients with severely impaired liver function in this study, additional research and adequate monitoring of paclitaxel-related toxicity in these patients are recommended.
