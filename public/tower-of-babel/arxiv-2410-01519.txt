Abstract
. In an earlier publication, the last two authors showed that a finite-dimensional module for a quantum affine algebra of type A A whose q q -factorization graph is totally ordered is prime. In this paper, we continue the investigation of the role of totally ordered pseudo q q -factorization graphs in the study of the monoidal structure of the underlying abelian category. We introduce the notions of modules with (prime) snake support and of maximal totally ordered subgraphs decompositions. Our main result shows that modules with snake support have unique such decomposition and that it determines the corresponding prime factorization. Along the way, we also prove that prime snake modules (for type A A ) can be characterized as the modules for which every pseudo q q -factorization graph is totally ordered.



1.
Introduction

A major problem in the realm of finite-dimensional representations of quantum affine algebras is that of describing the factorizations of a given simple module as a tensor product of prime ones. Certainly, the most successful theoretical approach so far is the connection with cluster algebras. Still, given a specific example, it is not such a simple task to find the appropriate answer using this approach. Also, it might be that, for certain classes of modules, other approaches may lead to more efficient algorithms for finding such factorizations. This is the spirit of our recent publications, which is further developed here.

The concept of pseudo
q
-factorization graphs was recently introduced in
[
3
]
as a combinatorial language which is suited for capturing certain properties of Drinfeld polynomials. Among all pseudo
q
-factorization graphs afforded by a given Drinfeld polynomial, there is a maximal (the fundamental factorization graph) and a minimal (the
q
-factorization graph). Using certain known representation theoretic facts about tensor products of Kirillov Reshetikhin modules and qcharacters, combined with special topological/combinatorial properties of the underlying
q
-factorization graphs, it was shown in
[
3
]
that, for algebras of type
A
, modules associated to totally ordered
q
-factorization graphs are prime. This provided a first glimpse into the role of totally ordered pseudo
q
-factorization graphs in the study of prime factorizations. We have also used certain totally ordered pseudo
q
-factorization graphs to construct strongly real modules in
[
2
]
.

A connected pseudo
q
-factorization graph with at most two vertices is totally ordered and the underlying module is certainly prime. The prime factorization of a module whose
q
-factorization graph has 3 vertices was described in
[
4
]
and we recall it in
2.6.1
below. In particular, there is a complete characterization of prime modules whose
q
-factorization graphs have 3 vertices, but are not totally ordered. The guiding line of the present paper is the following question: Can we describe classes of modules for which the prime factorizations can be found from the (purely combinatorial) study of chains of maximal totally ordered subgraphs (mtos for short) of a given pseudo
q
-factorization graph? Our main result,
2.5.1
, describes such a family: that of modules with snake support. We also show that
2.5.1
includes, as a particular case, the main result of
[
1
]
on the prime factorization of modules supported on a single node of the underlying finite type Dynking diagram. In other words, the study of mtos describes the prime factorizations of modules with snake support and, in particular, of any module afforded by monochromatic pseudo
q
-factorization graphs.

The concepts of snakes and prime snakes were introduced in
[
5
]
for algebras of types
A
and
B
. We review the formal definition for type
A
in
Section

2.4
. It is immediate that the original definition of prime snakes is equivalent to requesting that the associated fundamental factorization graph is totally ordered. We provide another characterization in
2.4.1
: a simple module is a prime snake module if and only if all of its pseudo
q
-factorization graphs are totally ordered. This characterization could serve as a type independent conceptual definition of prime snakes. We give a few first steps on the discussion about switching between choices of pseudo
q
-factorization graphs in
Section

3.2
. We keep finding examples where intermediate pseudo
q
-factorization graphs are more suited for answering different questions than the two extremal graphs. It is not clear to us how to answer the question: what is the most convenient graph to work with? We leave further studies in this direction for the future. The initial discussion we make here provides a proof for
2.4.1
.

Let us explain the statement of
2.5.1
. Given a Drinfeld polynomial
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, let
\boldsymbol{\bar{\pi}}
be the Drinfeld polynomial having exactly one copy of each fundamental factor of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
. We say
\boldsymbol{\pi}
has snake support if
\boldsymbol{\bar{\pi}}
arises from a prime snake. If that is the case,
2.5.1
says the fundamental factorization graph
G_{f}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
admits a unique (up to isomorphism) mtos-quochain and, moreover, if
G_{1},\dots,G_{l}
is the multicut of
G_{f}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
associated with such a quochain, then

V(\boldsymbol{\pi})\cong V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{l})

is the unique prime factorization of the simple module
V(\boldsymbol{\pi})
, where
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{j}
is the Drinfeld polynomial associated to
G_{j}
. The notion of quochains was introduced in
[
2
]
and reviewed in
Section

2.3
below, where we also introduce the notion of isomorphic quochains. In summary, the aforementioned multicut is obtained as follows:
G_{1}
is an mtos of
G_{f}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
,
G_{2}
is an mtos in the graph obtained from
G_{f}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
by deleting the vertices and arrows related to
G_{1}
, and so on. For any such choice of multicut, the corresponding factors
V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{j})
are isomorphic.

The basic background and notation for the main statements are collected in Sections
2.1
to
2.3
. The main results are stated in Sections
2.4
and
2.5
. As mentioned above,
2.5.1
can be seen as a generalization of the main result of
[
1
]
. Since the terminology used in
[
1
]
is different than the one used here, we also provide a more explicit comparison in
Section

2.5
. In
Section

2.6
, we make some comments about modules for which the prime factorizations do not arise from mtos-quochains. Further background needed for the proofs and the main proofs are given in
Section

3
.

2.1.
Quantum Algebras and Their Finite-Dimensional Modules

Although we use the basic notation as in
[
3
,
4
,
2
]
, for the reader’s convenience, we review it here.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a simple Lie algebra of type
A_{n}
over
\mathbb{C}
and let
I
be the set of nodes of its Dynkin diagram. We let
x_{i}^{\pm},h_{i},i\in I
, denote generators as in Serre’s Theorem and let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{n}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{+}
be the corresponding triangular decomposition. The symbols
R,R^{+},Q,Q^{+},P,P^{+}
will stand for, respectively, the sets of roots, positive roots, root lattice, the monoid generated by the positive roots, the weight lattice, and the set of integral dominant weights. The fundamental weights and simple roots will be denoted by
\omega_{i},\alpha_{i},i\in I
. For
i\in I
, let
i^{*}=w_{0}(i)
, where
w_{0}
is the Dynkin diagram automorphism induced by the longest element of the Weyl group. For
i,j\in I
, we let
[i,j]
denote the connected subgraph having
i,j
as boundary nodes, while the set of boundary nodes of
J\subseteq I
is denoted by
\partial J
. We also let
d(i,j)=\#[i,j]-1
and
d(J,K)=\min\{d(j,k):j\in J,k\in K\}
for
J,K\subseteq I
.

We let
U_{q}(\tilde{\mathfrak{g}})
be the quantum affine (in fact loop) algebra over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero
\mathbb{F}
, where
q\in\mathbb{F}^{\times}
is not a root of unity. The generators are denoted by
x_{i,r}^{\pm},k_{i}^{\pm 1},h_{i,s},i\in I,r,s\in\mathbb{Z},s\neq 0
. The subalgebra generated by
x_{i}:=x_{i,0}^{\pm},k_{i}^{\pm 1},i\in I
is a Hopf subalgebra of
U_{q}(\tilde{\mathfrak{g}})
isomorphic to the Drinfeld-Jimbo quantum group
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
.

For
i\in I
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
, we let
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a}
denote the corresponding fundamental
\ell
-weight, which is the Drinfeld polynomial whose unique non-constant entry is equal to
1-q^{a}u\in\mathbb{F}[u]
.
We let
\mathcal{P}^{+}
denote the multiplicative monoid generated by such elements, with identity element denoted by
\boldsymbol{1}
, while
\mathcal{P}
denotes the corresponding abelian group. We shall say
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a}
occurs in
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
if it appears in a reduced expression for
\boldsymbol{\pi}
as a product of fundamental
\ell
-weights and set

\sup(\boldsymbol{\pi})=\{i\in I:\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a}\text{ occurs in }\boldsymbol{\pi}\text{ for some }a\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{i}^{+}=\{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}:\sup(\boldsymbol{\pi})=\{i\}\}
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the full subcategory of that of fintie-dimensional
U_{q}(\tilde{\mathfrak{g}})
-modules whose simple factors have highest
\ell
-weights in
\mathcal{P}^{+}
and, hence,
\ell
-weights in
\mathcal{P}
. Thus, a finite-dimensional
U_{q}(\tilde{\mathfrak{g}})
-module
V
is in
\mathcal{C}
iff

V=\bigoplus_{\boldsymbol{\varpi}\in\mathcal{P}}V_{\boldsymbol{\varpi}}

where
V_{\boldsymbol{\varpi}}
is the
\ell
-weight space of
V
associated to
\boldsymbol{\varpi}\in\mathcal{P}
. Set

\wt_{\ell}(V)=\{\boldsymbol{\varpi}\in\mathcal{P}:V_{\boldsymbol{\varpi}}\neq 0\}.

For
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
,
V(\boldsymbol{\pi})
will denote a simple
U_{q}(\tilde{\mathfrak{g}})
-module whose highest
\ell
-weight is
\boldsymbol{\pi}
. Since
\mathcal{C}
is a monoidal category, the notion of prime objects is defined. Moreover, since
V(\boldsymbol{1})
, the one-dimensional trivial representation, is the unique invertible object in
\mathcal{C}
, it follows that
V\in\mathcal{C}
is prime iff

V\cong V_{1}\otimes V_{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad V_{j}\cong V(\boldsymbol{1})\quad\text{for some}\quad j\in\{1,2\}.

Every simple object in
\mathcal{C}
admits a decomposition as a tensor product of simple prime modules and a simple object
V
is said to be real if
V\otimes V
is simple.

For an object
V\in\mathcal{C}
, let
V^{*}
and
{}^{*}V
be the dual modules to
V
such that the usual evaluation maps

V^{*}\otimes V\to\mathbb{F}\quad\text{and}\quad V\otimes{}^{*}V\to\mathbb{F}

are module homomorphisms (cf.
[
3
, Section 2.6]
). Then
({}^{*}V)^{*}\cong V\cong{}^{*}(V^{*})
and
(V_{1}\otimes V_{2})^{*}\cong V_{2}^{*}\otimes V_{1}^{*}
.
The Hopf algebra structure on
U_{q}(\tilde{\mathfrak{g}})
is chosen so that, if
V=V(\boldsymbol{\pi})
, then
V^{*}\cong V(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})
, where
\boldsymbol{\pi}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
is the group automorphism of
\mathcal{P}
determined by

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a}^{*}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i^{*},a-\check{h}}.

Similarly, considering the automorphism determined by
{}^{*}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i^{*},a+\check{h}}
, it follows that
{}^{*}V(\boldsymbol{\pi})\cong V({}^{*}\boldsymbol{\pi})
.

Given
i\in I,a\in\mathbb{Z},r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, define

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a,r}=\prod_{p=0}^{r-1}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a+{r-1-2p}}.

These are the Drinfeld polynomials of the Kirillov-Reshetikhin modules and, hence, we refer to them as Drinfeld polynomials of KR. The set of all such polynomials will be denoted by
\mathcal{KR}
.
Every
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
can be written uniquely as a product of KR type polynomials such that, for every two factors supported at
i
, say
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a,r}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,b,s}
, the following holds

(2.1.1)

a-b\notin\mathscr{R}_{i}^{r,s}:=\{r+s-2p:0\leq p<\min\{r,s\}\}.

Such factorization is said to be the
q
-factorization of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
and the corresponding factors are called the
q
-factors of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
.
By abuse of language, whenever we mention the set of
q
-factors of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
we actually mean the associated multiset of
q
-factors counted with multiplicities in the
q
-factorization. It is often convenient to work with factorizations in KR type polynomials which not necessarily satisfy (
2.1.1
). Such a factorization will be referred to as a pseudo
q
-factorization and the associated factors as the corresponding
q
-factors of the factorization.

Given
(i,r),(j,s)\in I\times\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
, set

(2.1.2)

\mathscr{R}_{i,j}^{r,s}=\{r+s+d(i,j)-2p:-d([i,j],\partial I)\leq p<\min\{r,s\}\}.

Note

(2.1.3)

\mathscr{R}_{i}^{r,s}\subseteq\mathscr{R}_{i,i}^{r,s}.

It is well-known that

(2.1.4)

V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a,r})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j,b,s})\text{ is reducible}\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad|a-b|\in\mathscr{R}_{i,j}^{r,s}.

Moreover, in that case,

(2.1.5)

V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a,r})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j,b,s})\text{ is highest-$\ell$-weight}\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad a>b.

If
r=s=1
, we simplify notation and write
\mathscr{R}_{i,j}
for
\mathscr{R}_{i,j}^{1,1}
.

2.2.
Pseudo q-Factorization Graphs

The notion of a pseudo
q
-factorization graph was introduced in
[
3
]
. We now review the rephrased definition given in
[
2
]
.

Let
G=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A})
be a digraph with vertex set
\mathcal{V}
and arrow set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}
. If
a=(v,w)\in\mathcal{A}
, we set
h(a)=w
and
t(a)=v
(the head and the tail of
a
). A pseudo q-factorization map over
G
is a map
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{KR}
such that

(2.2.1)

\mathcal{F}(v)=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a,r}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \mathcal{F}(w)=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j,b,s}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\Big[(v,w)\in\mathcal{A}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad a-b\in\mathscr{R}_{i,j}^{r,s}\Big].

In particular, if such a map exists,
G
does not contain loops nor oriented cycles. A pseudo q-factorization graph is a digraph equipped with a pseudo q-factorization map. We shall say that a pseudo
q
-factorization map
\mathcal{F}
over
G
is fundamental if, for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
,
\mathcal{F}(v)
is a fundamental
\ell
-weight. The corresponding pseudo
q
-factorization graph will then be referred to as a fundamental factorization graph. Recall (
2.1.1
) and (
2.1.3
). We shall say
\mathcal{F}
is a
q
-factorization map if
\mathcal{F}(v)
is a
q
-factor of

(2.2.2)

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathcal{F}}:=\prod_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{F}(v)\in\mathcal{P}^{+}.

In that case, the corresponding pseudo
q
-factorization graph will be referred to as a
q
-factorization graph.
By abuse of notation, we shall identify
v\in\mathcal{V}
with
\mathcal{F}(v)
, so we can shorten the above to
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathcal{F}}=\prod_{v\in\mathcal{V}}v
. Moreover, we shall abuse of language and simply say “
G
is a pseudo
q
-factorization graph” with no mention to the structure data
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F})
and then write
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{G}
instead of
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathcal{F}}
. Despite the lack of accuracy, this should not cause contextual confusion and should be most often beneficial for the conciseness of the text. We shall also say
G
is a pseudo
q
-factorization graph over
\boldsymbol{\pi}
if
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{G}=\boldsymbol{\pi}
.
If
H\triangleleft G
. i.e., if
H=(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{A}^{\prime})
is a subgraph of
G
, we set

(2.2.3)

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{H}=\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathcal{F}|_{\mathcal{U}}}.

Conversely, given any map
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{KR}
defined on a nonempty finite set
\mathcal{V}
, we can construct a pseudo
q
-factorization graph having
\mathcal{V}
as vertex set and
\mathcal{F}
as its
q
-factorization map by defining
\mathcal{A}
by the requirement:

(v,w)\in\mathcal{A}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad V(\mathcal{F}(v))\otimes V(\mathcal{F}(w))\ \ \text{is reducible and highest-$\ell$-weight.}

We denote this graph by
G(\mathcal{F})
. If
\mathcal{F}
is an actual
q
-factorization map over
G(\mathcal{F})
and
\boldsymbol{\pi}=\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathcal{F}}
, we also use the notation
G(\boldsymbol{\pi})
and call it the
q
-factorization graph of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
. If
G(\mathcal{F})
is a fundamental factorization graph, we also use the notation
G_{f}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
and call it the fundamental (factorization) graph of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
.

Given pseudo
q
-factorization graphs
G
and
G^{\prime}
over
\boldsymbol{\pi}
and
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}
, respectively, we denote by
G\otimes G^{\prime}
the unique pseudo
q
-factorization graph over
\boldsymbol{\pi}\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}
whose vertex set is
\mathcal{V}_{G}\,\dot{\cup}\,\mathcal{V}_{G^{\prime}}
. Here,
\mathcal{V}_{G}
denotes the set of vertex of the graph
G
, and so on.

As commented above, if
G
affords the structure of a pseudo
q
-factorization graph, it contains no loops nor oriented cycles and, hence, the set
\mathcal{A}
induces a partial order on
\mathcal{V}
by transitive extension of the relation
h(a)<t(a)
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
. We say
G
is totally ordered if this order is linear.

Let
P
be a representation theoretic property, i.e., a property assignable to modules such as being prime or real. We shall interpret
P
as a (extrinsic) graphical property as follows. Suppose
H
is a subgraph of a pseudo
q
-factorization graph
G
. We shall say
H
satisfies
P
if
V(\pi_{H})
satisfies
P
. For instance, we shall say
H
is real if
V(\pi_{H})
is real.
Suppose
\mathcal{G}=G_{1},\dots,G_{l}
is a multicut of
G
, i.e., the family
G_{k},1\leq k\leq l
, is a family of subgraphs of
G
with disjoint vertex sets and whose union is
\mathcal{V}_{G}
. Given a graphical property
P
, we shall say
\mathcal{G}
has the property
P
if
G_{k}
has property
P
for all
k
(when regarded as subgraphs of
G
).

By a morphism from a graph
G=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A})
to a graph
G^{\prime}=(\mathcal{V}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime})
we mean a map
f:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
such that

\big(f(t(a)),f(h(a))\big)\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\quad\text{for all}\quad a\in\mathcal{A}.

If
G
and
G^{\prime}
are pseudo
q
-factorization graphs, we further require that

(2.2.4)

\mathcal{F}(v)=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(f(v))\quad\text{for all}\quad v\in V.

We use the notation
f:G\to G^{\prime}
to indicate that
f
is a morphism from
G
to
G^{\prime}
. If
f
is a morphism, it induces a map
\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{\prime},a\mapsto\big(f(t(a)),f(h(a))\big)
, which we also denote by
f
.
We say
f
is a full morphism if

f(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{G^{\prime}_{f(\mathcal{V})}}.

If
f
, as well as the induced map on arrows, are bijective, we say
f
is an isomorphism. If an ismorphism exists, we write
G\cong G^{\prime}
.

2.3.
Quochain Decompositions

Let us recall the notion of quochains in the sense of
[
2
]
. Let
\mathcal{G}=G_{1},\dots,G_{l}
be a multicut of a graph
G
and set

(2.3.1)

\bar{G}_{k}=G_{k+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes G_{l}\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq k\leq l.

Note

(2.3.2)

G_{k}\triangleleft\bar{G}_{k-1},\quad\bar{G}_{k}=\bar{G}_{k-1}\setminus G_{k}\quad\text{for all}\quad 0<k\leq l,

and the sequence
\bar{G}_{0},\dots,\bar{G}_{l}
is a proper descending chain of subgraphs:

(2.3.3)

\emptyset=\bar{G}_{l}\triangleleft\bar{G}_{l-1}\triangleleft\cdots\triangleleft\bar{G}_{1}\triangleleft\bar{G}_{0}=G.

We shall refer to this chain as the quochain associated to
\mathcal{G}
. By abuse of language, we shall often refer to
\mathcal{G}
as a quochain as well.

Suppose
P
is a “graphical property” (including extrinsic properties), i.e., a property assignable to subgraphs of a graph, such as being connected, totally ordered, real, prime, etc.. We shall say the multicut
\mathcal{G}
determines a
P
-quochain (or that
\mathcal{G}
is a
P
-quochain by abuse of language) if
G_{k}
has the property
P
when regarded as a subgraph of
\bar{G}_{k-1}
for all
1\leq k\leq l
.

We now introduce two notions which will play a prominent role in this paper, starting with that of isomorphic quochains.
Two quochains
G_{1},\dots,G_{l}
and
G_{1}^{\prime},\dots,G^{\prime}_{l^{\prime}}
will be said to be isomorphic if
l^{\prime}=l
and there exists
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
such that

G^{\prime}_{k}\cong G_{\sigma(k)}\quad\text{for all}\quad 1\leq k\leq l.

We shall say
G
has a unique
P
-decomposition if
G
admits a
P
-quochain and every two
P
-quochains in
G
are isomorphic.
For instance, if
P
is “being a maximal connected subgraph of
G
”, then
G
admits a
P
-quochain and any
P
-quochain corresponds to an enumeration of the connected components of
G
. Thus,
G
has a unique
P
-decomposition.

We shall be concerned with the case that
P
is “being a maximal totally ordered subgraph”. For short, we shall refer to quochains with such property as mtos-quochains and to the associated decompositions as mtos-decompositions. Evidently, every pseudo
q
-factorization graph
G
admits an mtos-quochain.

2.4.
Snake Modules and Totally Ordered Pseudo
q
-Factorization Graphs

Let us recall the definition of snake modules for type
A
. Given
(i_{k},a_{k})\in I\times\mathbb{Z},k\in\{1,2\}
, it is said that the ordered pair
((i_{1},a_{1}),(i_{2},a_{2}))
is in snake position if

a_{2}-a_{1}\in\min\mathscr{R}_{i_{1},i_{2}}+2\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}=d(i_{1},i_{2})+2\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

If
a_{2}-a_{1}\in\mathscr{R}_{i_{1},i_{2}}
, then it is said the pair is in prime snake position. More generally, if
\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{l})\in I^{l}
and
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{1},\dots a_{l})\in\mathbb{Z}^{l}
, it is said that
(\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{a})
is a (prime) snake if every pair
((i_{k},a_{k}),(i_{k+1},a_{k+1})),1\leq k<l
, is in (prime) snake position. Given
(\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{a})\in I^{l}\times\mathbb{Z}^{l}
, set

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{a}}=\prod_{k=1}^{l}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i_{k},a_{k}}.

If
(\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{a})
is a (prime) snake, the module
V(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{a}})
is called a (prime) snake module. The first of our main results gives an alternate perspective for the definition of prime snake modules.

Theorem 2.4.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
. Then,
V(\boldsymbol{\pi})
is a prime snake module if and only if every pseudo
q
-factorization graph over
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is totally ordered.
\diamond

2.5.
Snake Support and Prime Factorization

Given
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
, let
\mathcal{F}
be the pseudo
q
-factorization map associated to
G:=G_{f}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
and consider

(2.5.1)

\boldsymbol{\bar{\pi}}=\prod_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{V})}\boldsymbol{\omega}.

By definition,
\boldsymbol{\bar{\pi}}
has exactly one copy of each fundamental factor of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
. We shall say
\boldsymbol{\pi}
has snake support if
V(\boldsymbol{\bar{\pi}})
is a prime snake module. As usual, by abuse of language, we shall also say
G
has snake support. We are ready to state the second of our main results.

Theorem 2.5.1
.

If
\boldsymbol{\pi}
has snake support, then
G=G_{f}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
has a unique mtos-decomposition. Moreover, if
\mathcal{G}=G_{1},\dots,G_{l}
is an mtos-quochain, then

V(\boldsymbol{\pi})\cong V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{G_{1}})\otimes\cdots\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{G_{l}})

is the unique prime factorization of
V(\boldsymbol{\pi})
.
\diamond

The following lemma will be proved in
Section

3.4
.

Lemma 2.5.2
.

If
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}_{i}
for some
i\in I
, the connected components of
G_{f}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\pi}})
are totally ordered.
\diamond

As a consequence, if
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}_{i}
for some
i\in I
, the prime factorization of
V(\boldsymbol{\pi})
is given by
2.5.1
. The prime factorization of such modules was described in
[
1
, Theorem 2]
in terms of the concept of
(i,n)
-segments. Thus,
2.5.1
can be seen as a generalization of
[
1
, Theorem 2]
.
We now establish the dictionary between the two languages.

Given
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{l})\in\mathbb{Z}^{l}
, set

\boldsymbol{k}^{\pm}=(k_{1}^{\pm},\dots,k_{l-1}^{\pm})\quad\text{with}\quad k_{s}^{\pm}=k_{s+1}\pm k_{s}.

An
(i,n)
-segment of length
l
was defined in
[
1
]
as a sequence
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{l})\in\mathbb{Z}^{l}
such that

We shall denote by
S_{i,n,l}
the set of
(i,n)
-segments of length
l
and set

Given
\boldsymbol{k}\in S_{i,n}

denote its length, i.e.,
\ell(\boldsymbol{k})=l
if and only if
\boldsymbol{k}\in S_{i,n,l}
.

Given
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{l},i\in I,
and

, set

\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{i,\boldsymbol{k},a}=\prod_{s=1}^{l}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a+k_{s}}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}^{+}.

Note that, if
\boldsymbol{k}\in S_{i,n}

(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a+k_{s+1}},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a+k_{s}})\in\mathcal{A}_{G}\quad\text{for all}\quad 1\leq s<\ell(\boldsymbol{k}).

In other words, the concept of
(i,n)
-segments is equivalent to that of prime snakes supported at a single node of the Dynkin diagram. In particular, it follows from
2.4.1
that any pseudo
q
-factorization graph over

-module is prime and strongly real in the sense of
[
2
]
. Continuing along the lines of the proof of
2.4.1
, we shall prove the following in
Section

3.4
.

Theorem 2.5.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}_{i}
for some
i\in I
. The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

\boldsymbol{\pi}=\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{i,\boldsymbol{k},a}
for some
\boldsymbol{k}\in S_{i,n},a\in\mathbb{Z}

(ii)

is a prime snake module.

(iii)

is totally ordered.

(iv)

G(\boldsymbol{\pi})
is totally ordered.

(v)

is prime.
\diamond

It follows from the results of this
Section

2.5
that, for monochromatic fundamental factorization graphs, the concept of mtos-quochain is equivalent to that of maximal prime snake quochains or, equivalently, quochains of maximal
(i,n)
-segments.

G^{\prime}=(\mathcal{V}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
. Recall that, once we have defined
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
and any map
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}:\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{KR}
, there exists unique
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
turning
G^{\prime}
into a pseudo
q
-factorization graph.
If the fusion is not pure, we let
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}=\mathcal{V}
, and set

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(v)=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,c,k},\quad\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(w)=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,d,l},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(u)=\mathcal{F}(u)\text{ for all }u\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{v,w\}.

The roles of
v
and
w
are clearly interchangeable above, i.e., the obtained graphs are isomorphic. In the case the fusion is pure, we let
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}=\mathcal{V}\setminus\{w\}
and set

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(v)=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a,r}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,b,s}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(u)=\mathcal{F}(u)\text{ for all }u\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\setminus\{v\}.

Again, the roles of
v
and
w
are interchangeable. Evidently,
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{G^{\prime}}=\boldsymbol{\pi}
. We shall say
G^{\prime}
is obtained from
G
by fusing two vertices and we indicate that by the notation
G^{\prime}\prec G
. If the fusion is pure, we write
G^{\prime}<G
. Both
\prec
and
<
can be extended by reflexivity and transitivity to equip the set
{\rm Graph}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
of pseudo
q
-factorization graphs over
\boldsymbol{\pi}
with two distinct partial orders. Evidently,

(3.2.1)

G^{\prime}\leq G\quad\Rightarrow\quad G^{\prime}\preceq G,

but the converse may not be true. Also

(3.2.2)

G(\boldsymbol{\pi})\preceq G\ \ \text{and}\ \ G\leq G_{f}(\boldsymbol{\pi})\quad\text{for every}\quad G\in{\rm Graph}(\boldsymbol{\pi}).

In particular,
G_{f}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
is the maximum for both partial orders.

We now address the following question. Suppose
G^{\prime}\leq G
and that
G
is totally ordered. Under which additional assumptions can we conclude
G^{\prime}
is also totally ordered? To shorten the writing, we go back to abusing of terminology by saying that
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{K}R
is a vertex of a given graph, instead of saying
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\mathcal{F}(v)
for some vertex
v
.

It suffices to address the question in the case that
G^{\prime}
is obtained from
G
by fusing only one pair of vertices. Let
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and
\boldsymbol{\varpi}
be the fused vertices and assume
(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\varpi})\in\mathcal{A}
. In particular,

(3.2.3)

\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{\varpi}\in\mathcal{KR}

is a vertex in
G^{\prime}
. All the remaining vertices of
G
remain vertices in
G^{\prime}
.
Thus, given
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\varpi}\}
, we need to understand under which conditions
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is comparable to
\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{\varpi}
with respect to the partial order of
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is adjacent to either
\boldsymbol{\omega}
or
\boldsymbol{\varpi}
.

Write
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a,r}
and
\boldsymbol{\varpi}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j,b,s}
. Then, the assumption
(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\varpi})\in\mathcal{A}
together with (
3.2.3
) implies

(3.2.4)

a=b+r+s\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{\varpi}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a-s,r+s}.

Let also
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j,c,t}
.
Let us check

(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\omega})\in\mathcal{A}\quad\Rightarrow\quad(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{\varpi})\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}.

Indeed, the assumption
(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\omega})\in\mathcal{A}
means

c-a=r+t+d(i,j)-2p\quad\text{with}\quad-d([i,j],\partial I)\leq p<\min\{r,t\},

and, therefore,

c-(a-s)=(r+s)+t+d(i,j)-2p\quad\text{and}\quad-d([i,j],\partial I)\leq p<\min\{r+s,t\}.

Similarly, one checks

(\boldsymbol{\varpi},\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathcal{A}\quad\Rightarrow\quad(\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{\varpi},\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}.

Thus,
G^{\prime}
may fail to be totally ordered only if

(3.2.5)

\exists\ \boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{V}\quad\text{such that}\quad\boldsymbol{\varpi}<\boldsymbol{\mu}<\boldsymbol{\omega}.

Here,
<
is the partial order on
\mathcal{V}
induced by
\mathcal{A}
. Up to arrow duality, we may assume
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is adjacent to
\boldsymbol{\omega}
, so
(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathcal{A}
and, hence,

(3.2.6)

a-c=r+t+d(i,j)-2p\quad\text{with}\quad-d([i,j],\partial I)\leq p<\min\{r,t\}.

On the other hand,
3.1.1
(b) implies

(3.2.7)

c-b=s+t+d(i,j)-2k\quad\text{for some}\quad k<\min\{s,t\}.

Using (
3.2.4
) and (
3.2.6
), we also have

c-b=c-(a-r-s)=(c-a)+r+s=s-t-d(i,j)+2p,

and, hence,

(3.2.8)

t+d(i,j)=k+p.

The upper bounds on (
3.2.6
) and (
3.2.7
) then imply

t+d(i,j)-k<t\quad\text{and}\quad t+d(i,j)-p<t,

or, equivalently,

(3.2.9)

d(i,j)<\min\{p,k\}.

If
\min\{r,s\}=1
, it follows that
\min\{p,k\}\leq 0
and, hence, (
3.2.9
) is not satisfied, i.e., there is no
\boldsymbol{\mu}
as in (
3.2.5
). This proves:

Proposition 3.2.1
.

Let
G
be a totally ordered pseudo
q
-factorization graph. If
G^{\prime}
is obtained from
G
by a sequence of pure fusions such that in every step at least one of the vertices being fused is fundamental, then
G^{\prime}
is also totally ordered. ∎

Corollary 3.2.2
.

Let
G
be a fundamental factorization graph. If
G
is totally ordered, then any pseudo
q
-factorization graph over
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{G}
is totally ordered.∎

The following is easily checked directly from the definitions of snakes and factorization graphs.

Lemma 3.2.3

If

(i)

(\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{a})
is a prime snake.

(ii)

(iii)

G_{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{a}})

.

Let

∎

(\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime})\in I^{l^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{Z}^{l^{\prime}}
be such that

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}=\prod_{s=s_{1}}^{s_{2}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i_{s},a_{s}}.

In particular, in the notation of (
3.1.7
), there exists
j,j^{\prime}
such that
\{j,j^{\prime}\}=\{1,2\}
,

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i^{\prime},a^{\prime},1,l^{\prime}-1},\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\pi}_{j^{\prime}}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i^{\prime},a^{\prime},2,l^{\prime}},

and, hence,
V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2})
is reducible. Moreover, it follows from (
3.1.8
) that there exists
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}\in\wt_{\ell}V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{j})
such that

(3.3.8)

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{1}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{2}\notin\wt_{\ell}V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}).

Note (
3.1.6
), applied to
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{G_{j}}
in place of
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{a}}
, implies

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{j}^{<}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}({}^{*}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{j}^{>})^{-1}\in V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{G_{j}})

and, therefore,

\boldsymbol{\omega}:=\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}^{<}\,\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}^{<}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_{2}\,({}^{*}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}^{>}\,{}^{*}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}^{>})^{-1}\in\wt_{\ell}V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{G_{1}})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{G_{2}}).

Thus, in order to complete the proof, it suffices to check

\boldsymbol{\omega}\notin\wt_{\ell}V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{G_{1}}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{G_{2}}).

Indeed, by definition, we have
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}^{<}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}^{<}\lessdot\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}\lessdot\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}^{>}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}^{>}
and, therefore, an application of (
3.1.5
) implies we have an epimorphism

V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}^{>}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}^{>})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}^{<}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}^{<})\to V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{G_{1}}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{G_{2}}).

Hence, it suffices to show

(3.3.9)

\boldsymbol{\omega}\notin\wt_{\ell}V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}^{>}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}^{>})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2})\otimes V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}^{<}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}^{<}).

Note (
3.1.3
) implies
(^{*}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}^{>}\,{}^{*}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}^{>})^{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}^{<}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}^{<}
occur in
\boldsymbol{\omega}
. Then, if (
3.3.9
) were false, an application of
3.1.8
would imply
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{1}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{2}\in\wt_{\ell}V(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2})
, yielding a contradiction with (
3.3.8
).
∎

3.4.
The Case of Monochromatic Graphs

We now prove
2.5.3
, starting with:

Proof of
2.5.2
.

Assume
G=G_{f}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\pi}})
is connected and let
S=\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k}:1\leq k\leq l\}
be the set of fundamental factors of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
. Then,
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a_{k}}
for some
a_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
. We assume the enumeration is chosen so that
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{l}
. In particular

r<s\quad\Rightarrow\quad(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a_{r}},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a_{s}})\notin\mathcal{A}_{G}.

Then, the connectedness of
G
implies that, for each
1\leq r<l
, there exists
r<s\leq l
such that
(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a_{s}},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a_{r}})\in\mathcal{A}_{G}
. It follows from
3.1.2
(a) that

(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a_{k}},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a_{m}})\in\mathcal{A}_{G}\quad\text{for all}\quad r\leq m\leq k\leq s,

which completes the proof.
∎

The comments before the statement of
2.5.3
shows we have already proved (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii), while
3.2.2
shows (iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv) and the main result of
[
3
]
(Theorem 3.5.5) shows (iv)
\Rightarrow
(v). On the other hand, (v) implies
G_{f}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
is connected and then,
2.5.2
implies it has snake support which, in light of
2.5.1
, shows that (v)
\Rightarrow
(ii). Thus, it suffices to show that, for monochromatic graphs, (iv)
\Rightarrow
(iii) (this is easily seen to be false in general).

Let
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
and assume
G(\boldsymbol{\pi})
is connected and
\sup(\boldsymbol{\pi})=\{i\}
. In particular, there exists
a\in\mathbb{Z},l\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
k_{j}\in\mathbb{Z},m_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},1\leq j\leq l
, such that

\boldsymbol{\pi}=\prod_{j=1}^{l}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a+k_{j}})^{m_{j}}\quad\text{and}\quad k_{j}<k_{j^{\prime}}\ \text{ if }\ j<j^{\prime}.

Moreover, any other such expression differs by replacing
a
by
a-k
for some
k
, which replaces
k_{j}
by
k_{j}+k
and does not change
l
or
m_{j}
. Thus, fix
a
and let
\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
be the sequence
(k_{1},\dots,k_{l})
. The proof of (iv)
\Rightarrow
(iii) is clearly completed with the following lemma.

Lemma 3.4.1
.

If
G(\boldsymbol{\pi})
is totally ordered, then
m_{j}=1
for all
1\leq j\leq l
and
\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{\pi})\in S_{i,n}
.
\diamond

We need some preparation to prove this lemma. Given
\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\varpi}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
, we will write
\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\varpi}
to mean that
\boldsymbol{\omega}
divides
\boldsymbol{\varpi}
. If
\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\varpi}\in\mathcal{KR}
, and
\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\varpi}
, there exist
i\in I,a,m\in\mathbb{Z},r,s\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},s\leq r
, such that

\boldsymbol{\varpi}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a,r},\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a+m,s}

and,

(3.4.1)

m=r+s-2p\quad\text{for some}\quad s\leq p\leq r.

Given
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
, recall that we denote by
\preccurlyeq
the partial order on the vertex set of
G(\boldsymbol{\pi})
induced by the arrow structure.

Lemma 3.4.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
. If there exist
q
-factors of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, say
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and
\boldsymbol{\varpi}
, such that
\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\varpi}
, then
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and
\boldsymbol{\varpi}
are not related by
\preccurlyeq
.

Proof.

Fix the notation leading to (
3.4.1
). If it were
\boldsymbol{\omega}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{\varpi}
, there would exist vertices

v_{1}=\boldsymbol{\omega},v_{2},\dots,v_{k}=\boldsymbol{\varpi}
such that
(v_{j+1},v_{j})
are arrows for
1\leq j<k
. In other words, we would have

v_{j}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a_{j},r_{j}}\quad\text{with}\quad a_{j+1}-a_{j}=m_{j},\ \ m_{j}\in\mathscr{R}_{i,i}^{r_{j},r_{j+1}}.

In particular,

m=-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}m_{j}.

Writing
m_{j}=r_{j}+r_{j+1}-2p_{j}
and
d=d(i,\partial I)
, the condition
m_{j}\in\mathscr{R}_{i,i}^{r_{j},r_{j+1}}
is equivalent to

-d\leq p_{j}<\min\{r_{j},r_{j+1}\}.

Hence,

m=s+r-2p\quad\text{with}\quad p=r+s-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}p_{j}+\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}r_{j}.

Thus,

p=r+(s-p_{1})+\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}(r_{j}-p_{j})>p_{1}>r

yielding a contradiction with (
3.4.1
).

Similarly, if it were
\boldsymbol{\varpi}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{\omega}
, by repeating the above argument with
v_{1}=\boldsymbol{\varpi}
and
v_{k}=\boldsymbol{\omega}
, we would conclude

m=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}m_{j}=s+r-2p\quad\text{with}\quad p=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}p_{j}-\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}r_{j}.

Thus,

p=p_{1}+\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}(p_{j}-r_{j})<p_{1}<s,

yielding a contradiction with (
3.4.1
) again.
∎

We will also need:

Lemma 3.4.3
.

Let
i\in I,r,s\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and assume
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a,r}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a+m,s}
are the
q
-factors of
\boldsymbol{\pi}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a,r}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a+m,s}
. If
m\in\mathscr{R}_{i,i}^{r,s}
, then
m-s+1-(r-1)\in\mathscr{R}_{i,i}
or, equivalently,

(1-r,3-r,\dots,r-1,m-s+1,m-s+3,\dots,m+s-1)\in S_{i,n,r+s}.

Proof.

Write
m=r+s-2p
with
-d(i,\partial I)\leq p<\min\{r,s\}
. Therefore,

m-s+1-(r-1)=-2(p-1)\in\{2j:1-\min\{r,s\}\leq j\leq d(i,\partial I)+1\}

and we need to show we must have
j\geq 1
. If it were
j<1
or, equivalently,
p>0
, the union of the
q
-strings
(1-r,3-r,\dots,r-1)
and
(m-s+1,m-s+3,\dots,m+s-1)
would form a longer
q
-string, contradicting the assumption that
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a,r}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i,a+m,s}
are the
q
-factors of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
.
∎

Finally, we can give the:

Proof of
3.4.1
.

If it could be
m_{j}>1
for some
j
, it would follow that
\boldsymbol{\pi}
has two
q
-factors with a common root. The fact that they are
q
-factors implies the assumptions of
3.4.2
are satisfied, yielding a contradiction with the assumption that
G(\boldsymbol{\pi})
is totally ordered. Thus, it remains to show
\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{\pi})\in S_{i,n}
, which follows from
3.4.3
.
∎
