What pediatric nursing must prepare for now and in the future
Authors: Dong Hee Kim
Published: 2026

Over the first quarter of the 21st century, major gains in child survival and pediatric technology have changed the case mix of child health. The pediatric agenda is no longer defined only by whether children survive acute illness, but increasingly by whether they can develop, function, and thrive across home, school, digital, and community settings. Mental health problems now shape morbidity on a broad scale; digital routines influence sleep, mood, and learning; chronic health risks are appearing earlier in life; more children with disabilities and medical complexity are living longer at home; and environmental instability is becoming a pediatric care issue rather than a distant policy concern. Accordingly, pediatric nursing must prepare not only for disease management but also for longitudinal risk, family caregiving demands, and equitable access to both in-person and digital care [1-4].

1. Child Mental Health as a Core Outcome

Mental health problems among children and adolescents are no longer peripheral concerns but have become major drivers of both current and future morbidity. During the COVID-19 (coronavirus disease 2019) pandemic, symptoms of depression and anxiety increased substantially among children and adolescents worldwide, highlighting the vulnerability of youth mental health to large-scale social disruption [3]. For pediatric nursing, this shift underscores the need to recognize emotional distress as a core pediatric outcome because it affects treatment adherence, sleep, school participation, family functioning, and long-term developmental trajectories.

The recent worsening of child and adolescent mental health is best understood as the cumulative result of interacting factors that have intensified over the past decade rather than as the consequence of a single, time-limited disruption. Pandemic-related school closures served as an acute trigger by removing daily structure, peer interaction, and access to school-based support while increasing loneliness and social isolation. However, these patterns largely reflect the acceleration of pre-existing trends in social disconnection and family stress. Loneliness has emerged as a particularly important contributor as childhood is increasingly characterized by reduced face-to-face interaction and weakened community networks. Evidence suggests that loneliness predicts both current and future depression and anxiety, with longer durations of loneliness associated with a greater risk of mental health problems. Family conflict, parental distress, and reduced emotional availability may also act as chronic stressors that contribute to children’s emotional difficulties. In addition, disruptions in school connectedness may weaken a critical source of social support, belonging, and emotional stability for children and adolescents. Collectively, these findings suggest that contemporary child mental health problems are cumulative and socially embedded, arising from the interaction of family, school, and community environments [3,5].

The practical implication for pediatric nursing is a shift from reactive, referral-based care to structured mental health pathways integrated into routine pediatric encounters. Pediatric nurses should incorporate age-appropriate mental health screening into well-child, chronic care, school-linked, and transition visits; ensure prompt escalation of care for suicide risk; and assess sleep, social connectedness, bullying, family stress, and digital behaviors as interconnected determinants of well-being. Nursing outcomes should also extend beyond screening rates to include timely follow-up, completion of safety planning, symptom improvement, and successful re-engagement in daily activities.

2. Digital Exposure as a Clinical Determinant

The digital issue is no longer limited to device ownership but increasingly concerns how, when, and why digital technologies are used. Evidence suggests that problematic smartphone use is associated with depression, anxiety, stress, poor sleep, and poorer educational outcomes, while greater screen exposure is consistently linked to shorter sleep duration and an increased risk of sleep-related problems [2,6]. As digital technologies become increasingly integrated into children’s daily lives, the assessment of digital habits should become a routine component of pediatric nursing care.

For pediatric nurses, the goal is not moral alarm but clinically meaningful assessment. Attention should focus on patterns of use, timing, content, and function, including whether digital engagement primarily serves learning, social connection, coping, or avoidance. Counseling can then emphasize practical and achievable strategies, such as maintaining healthy presleep routines, limiting device use in bed, promoting positive caregiver modeling, and distinguishing beneficial digital engagement from patterns that disrupt sleep, well-being, or daily functioning.

3. Early Chronicity and Home-Based Complexity

Chronic conditions are emerging earlier in life, and advances in pediatric care are enabling more children with disabilities and medical complexity to survive and live longer at home. Consequently, an increasing proportion of pediatric care is devoted to the long-term management of chronic conditions and complex health needs beyond the hospital setting. Recent evidence emphasizes that pediatric home health care is medically necessary not only for physiologic stability but also for supporting children’s participation and functioning across home, school, and community environments [1]. Collectively, these shifts are moving the focus of pediatric care from episodic, hospital-based treatment toward ongoing management integrated into daily life [7].

For pediatric nursing, success can no longer be defined solely by the avoidance of readmission. Transitional and home-based care must evaluate whether families can safely provide care, recognize signs of deterioration, maintain daily routines, and sustain quality of life over time. Evidence suggests that limitations in home nursing support can impose substantial financial and caregiving burdens on families of children with medical complexity, underscoring the importance of family capacity as a measurable outcome rather than an implicit assumption [7].

Pediatric nursing should therefore focus on supporting children and families as they adapt to long-term care in the home setting. This includes building caregiver confidence and competence, facilitating care coordination across healthcare, school, and community settings, supporting equipment and medication management, coaching families to recognize early warning signs, and continually assessing caregiver burden and practical challenges. By supporting both clinical management and family capacity, pediatric nursing can promote safer and more sustainable care for children living with chronic conditions and medical complexity at home [1,7].

4. Climate and Environmental Risk

Climate and environmental instability have become significant determinants of child health rather than distant public health concerns. The World Health Organization identifies climate change, air pollution, unsafe water and sanitation, chemicals, radiation, and e-waste as major environmental threats to children, who are particularly vulnerable because of their developing organ systems and higher exposure levels relative to their body size [8]. Recent evidence further demonstrates that climate-related exposures contribute to respiratory illness, heat-related morbidity, and other adverse health outcomes among children [9].

For pediatric nursing, the priority is to make environmental risk clinically visible. Assessment should extend beyond traditional household exposures to include heat, wildfire smoke, dampness and mold, traffic-related pollution, unsafe housing conditions, flooding, and access to safe outdoor environments. Anticipatory guidance provided in asthma, newborn, school, and home-care settings should address heat illness prevention, air-quality alerts, hydration, disaster preparedness, and the continuity of essential medications and medical equipment. Because environmental exposures and adaptive resources are unevenly distributed, environmental health should also be recognized as a health equity issue that requires targeted support for vulnerable children and families [8,9].

5. Equity as a Clinical Requirement

Equity has emerged as a central issue in pediatric nursing because contemporary child health risks and access to healthcare are increasingly shaped by social and structural conditions rather than by clinical factors alone. Mental health vulnerability, chronic disease burden, environmental exposures, and barriers to healthcare access are disproportionately concentrated among children living in socioeconomically disadvantaged communities. At the same time, the rapid digitalization of pediatric healthcare is transforming how children and families access assessment, treatment, and ongoing support. Telehealth, digital therapeutics, remote monitoring, and other technology-enabled services are increasingly integrated into routine care. Consequently, inequities in digital access are no longer peripheral concerns but have become potential barriers to essential healthcare, increasing the risk that existing disparities may be perpetuated or amplified [4,10].

Recent evidence suggests that access itself is rarely operationalized in evaluations of pediatric digital health despite the growing reliance on digital care systems. These findings have prompted increased emphasis on equity-focused implementation frameworks that incorporate measurable indicators, such as digital access, language support, service availability, and follow-up completion, into pediatric care systems [4,10]. Equity, therefore, should be understood not as an aspirational goal but as a clinical design requirement that is embedded within referral pathways, telehealth delivery, communication systems, and outcome monitoring.

For pediatric nursing, this means systematically assessing barriers that may limit families’ ability to engage in care, including digital access, language needs, caregiving constraints, transportation challenges, disability accommodations, and previous healthcare experiences. Nursing workflows should incorporate flexible and accessible care pathways that respond to diverse family circumstances. Ultimately, programs should be evaluated not only by service uptake but also by their ability to reduce barriers to care and strengthen families’ capacity to participate in ongoing health management [4,10].

6. Conclusion

Pediatric nursing is entering an era in which its effectiveness will be judged not only by the management of illness but also by its ability to anticipate risk, strengthen family capacity, and support children across the settings in which they live, learn, and grow. Future pediatric nursing must move beyond episodic encounters toward continuous, coordinated, and context-sensitive care that reflects the realities of contemporary childhood. This will require nurses to integrate mental health, digital health, chronic care management, environmental health, and equity into routine practice rather than addressing them as separate domains. By leading this transformation, pediatric nursing can help build resilient systems that promote not only survival but also the long-term health and well-being of children and families.
