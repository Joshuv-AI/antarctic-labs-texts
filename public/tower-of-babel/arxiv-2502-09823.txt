Compression properties for large Toeplitz-like matrices
Bernhard Beckermann, Daniel Kressner, Heather Wilber

Abstract
Toeplitz matrices are abundant in computational mathematics, and there is a rich literature on the development of fast and superfast algorithms for solving linear systems involving such matrices. Any Toeplitz matrix can be transformed into a matrix with off-diagonal blocks that are of low numerical rank. This compressibility is relied upon in practice in a number of superfast Toeplitz solvers. In this paper, we show that the compression properties of these matrices can be thoroughly explained using their displacement structure and Zolotarev numbers. We provide explicit bounds on the numerical ranks of important submatrices that arise when applying HSS, HODLR and other approximations with hierarchical low-rank structure to transformed Toeplitz and Toeplitz-like matrices. Our results lead to very efficient displacement-based compression strategies that can be used to formulate adaptive superfast rank-structured solvers.

low-rank approximation, Toeplitz, numerical rank, displacement structure, superfast solver, hierarchical matrices, HSS matrices

Introduction

A matrix that is constant along the diagonals is called a Toeplitz matrix:

T=\begin{bmatrix}t_{0}&t_{-1}&\cdots&t_{-n+1}\\
t_{1}&t_{0}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&t_{-1}\\
t_{n-1}&\cdots&t_{1}&t_{0}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}.

This and similar matrix structures appear frequently in computational mathematics, for instance in the context of discretized differential and integral equations, orthogonal polynomials, time series analysis, as well as image and signal processing
[
3
,
24
,
23
,
29
,
31
,
45
]
. Although the development of fast (and superfast) solvers for linear systems with Toeplitz matrices is a classical topic that is more than
60
years old, there have still been significant advances in recent years. In particular, a novel and versatile class of solvers is based on the observation that any Toeplitz (or Toeplitz-like) matrix can be transformed into a Cauchy-like matrix
C
with compressible off-diagonal blocks
[
13
,
38
,
55
]
. In turn, this allows one to leverage existing algorithms for matrices with hierarchical low-rank structure, not only for linear systems but also for, e.g., eigenvalue problems
[
44
]
, matrix functions, and matrix equations
[
32
]
.
The goal of this work is to perform an analysis of the compressibility of
C
.
Our analysis not only completes and improves upon existing analyses, but we will demonstrate that it is also the basis of new, deterministic algorithms for compressing
C
.

Most fast direct solvers for a matrix
T
with Toeplitz and related structures are based on the observation that the displacement rank of
T
is small. More specifically, in this work we consider the Sylvester displacement equation

(1)

ZT-TZ=GH^{*},\quad Z=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
I_{n-1}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad{\rm rank}({G}{H}^{*})=\rho\ll n.

For a Toeplitz matrix
T
, one can choose the displacement rank
\rho=2
.
Our developments also apply to Toeplitz-like matrices
[
31
,
45
]
, that is, matrices
T
that satisfy (
1
) for some
\rho
that is still small but possibly larger than
2
. Let us stress that the choice of displacement equation is by no means unique
[
31
]
.
Our choice (
1
) is simple but not suitable for all purposes because the linear operator
T\mapsto ZT-TZ
is singular and, in turn,
T
cannot be fully reconstructed from the
generator matrices

G
and
H
. Other choices have been made in the literature, using coefficient matrices different from
Z
or a Stein equation instead of a Sylvester equation.

Classical fast solvers of complexity
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
, such as the generalized Schur algorithm, exploit the observation that Schur complements and inverses preserve the displacement rank, which in turn allows one to perform certain operations on
T
, such as the LU factorization, entirely in terms of the generator matrices; see
[
31
]
for a survey. Classical superfast solvers of complexity
\mathcal{O}(n\log^{2}n)
are obtained by combining this idea with a divide-and-conquer approach; see
[
30
]
and the references therein.

The matrix
Z
in (
1
) is circulant and thus easily diagonalized by a unitary discrete Fourier transform
F
. In turn, the transformed matrix
C=FTF^{*}
satisfies a displacement equation with diagonal coefficients. This observation has been used in
[
20
]
to derive fast solvers with enhanced numerical stability; pivoting does not destroy the Cauchy-like structure of
C
. More recently, several works
[
13
,
38
,
55
]
have derived new superfast solvers from the fact that off-diagonal submatrices of
C
are well-approximated by low-rank matrices. Algorithm
1
describes the general framework of these solvers applied to a linear system

(2)

Tx=b,\quad T\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n},

with a Toeplitz-like matrix
T
satisfying (
1
).

Algorithm 1

Superfast solvers for a Toeplitz-like linear system (
2
).

1:

Compute
\widetilde{b}=Fb
,
\widetilde{G}=FG
,
\widetilde{H}=FH
.

2:

Determine
\widetilde{C}
, a hierarchical low-rank approximation of
C=FTF^{*}
from its generators
\widetilde{G},\widetilde{H}
.

3:

Solve
\widetilde{C}\widetilde{x}=\widetilde{b}
.

4:

Compute
x=F^{*}\widetilde{x}.

Steps 1 and 4 of Algorithm
1
can be easily accomplished in
\mathcal{O}(n\rho\log n)
operations using fast Fourier transforms (FFTs).
For Step 2, several hierarchical approximations of
C
are possible. This includes the relatively simple HODLR (Hierarchical Off-Diagonal Low-Rank) format, as well as the more involved SSS (sequentially semi-separable) and HSS (hierarchical semi-separable) formats used in
[
13
,
37
,
55
]
.

In all these formats, the approximation
\widetilde{C}
is constructed by recursively partitioning
C
into successively smaller submatrices and replacing each submatrix by a low-rank approximation. HSS format features additional structure; the low-rank factors between different levels of the recursion tree are nested; see Section
4
.
Its use leads to particularly efficient
\mathcal{O}(np^{2})
direct solvers for Step 3
[
12
,
55
]
,
assuming that the rank of each low-rank approximant in
\widetilde{C}
is bounded by
p
.
The efficiency of Steps 2 and 3 depends on (i) the hierarchical low-rank format used, (ii) the compressibility of
C
, (iii) the method of approximation used to find
\widetilde{C}
, and (iv) the type of solver used in Step 3.
In this work, we focus on analysis related to (ii) and (iii).

1.1
Main results

The primary novelty in our paper is the introduction of new bounds that more thoroughly explain the low-rank properties of the Cauchy-like matrix
C
. This mproves upon some results that currently exist in the literature, and it provides explicit bounds on the singular values of the relevant submatrices of
C
. We show that the alternating implicit direction (ADI) method can be used to construct low rank approximations with errors controlled by our bounds. This leads to an efficient and adaptive algorithm for constructing HODLR and HSS approximations to
C
in Step 2 of Algorithm
1
.
The current state-of-the-art for solving (
2
) given an arbitrary Toeplitz matrix is based on randomized linear algebra
[
55
]
. Our analysis more thoroughly explains the effectiveness of this method, and it also serves as the basis of competitive deterministic analogue methods.

To describe the compressibility of
C
, we use the notion of numerical rank, also called
\epsilon
-rank:

Definition 1
.

Let
\sigma_{1}(X)\geq\sigma_{2}(X)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{\min\{m,n\}}(X)\geq 0
denote the singular values of a matrix
X\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
. Given
0<\epsilon<1
, the
\epsilon
-rank of
X
is
{\rm rank}_{\epsilon}(X)=k
for the smallest integer
k
such that
\sigma_{k+1}(X)\leq\epsilon\|X\|_{2}
,
where
\|\cdot\|_{2}=\sigma_{1}(X)
denotes the spectral norm.

If
{\rm rank}_{\epsilon}(X)\leq k
then
X
is well-approximated by a rank
k
matrix
X^{(k)}
in the sense that
\|X-X^{(k)}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\|X\|_{2}
.

While some theoretical justification on the rank structure of
C
is given in
[
13
,
37
]
, these arguments are incomplete (see section
2.3
). For example, they do not duly justify the use of HODLR, HSS, or other weakly-admissible
[
27
]
hierarchical approximations to
C
. These arguments also do not supply a priori estimates on the numerical ranks of submatrices of
C
. In contrast, we explicitly bound the numerical ranks of the off-diagonal submatrices of
C
, including those arising in weakly admissible hierarchical formats. This leads to adaptive hierarchical low-rank approximation schemes that automatically select appropriate approximation parameters based on a relative tolerance parameter
0<\epsilon<1
.
Our bounds show that generally, an off-diagonal submatrix
X
of
C
has
\epsilon
-rank of order
\mathcal{O}(\rho\log n\log(1/\epsilon))
. The compressibility of submatrices sufficiently separated from the main diagonal of the matrix is even better; we prove that in this case, the bound is
\mathcal{O}(\rho\log\tfrac{1}{\epsilon})
, completely independent of
n
(see Theorem
3
).

2
Compressibility of Cauchy-like matrix
C

In
[
6
]
, the
\epsilon
-rank for certain matrices with displacement structure is bounded via solutions to certain rational approximation problems. We derive bounds on the off-diagonal blocks of the Cauchy-like matrix
C
using a similar strategy.

2.1
Displacement structure of
C

Starting with the displacement equation satisfied by
T
in (
1
), we first diagonalize
Z
with a unitary discrete Fourier transform. Letting
\omega=\exp(\mathrm{i}\pi/n)
, we have that

(3)

FZF^{*}=D,\quad F=\left(\frac{\omega^{2jk}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)_{j,k=0,\ldots,n-1},

where
D={\rm diag}(\omega^{0},\omega^{2},\ldots,\omega^{2n-2})
. Here and in the following, we adopt the convention of indexing matrix entries starting from 0. Inserting (
3
) into (
1
), it follows that the transformed matrix
C=FTF^{*}
satisfies the displacement equation

(4)

DC-CD=\widetilde{G}\widetilde{H}^{*},\quad\widetilde{G}=FG,\quad\widetilde{H}=FH,

with the same displacement rank
\rho
.

Given the the right-hand side of (
4
), each
off-diagonal
entry
c_{jk}
,
j\not=k
, of
C
can be recovered by multiplying the corresponding off-diagonal entry of
\widetilde{G}\widetilde{H}^{*}
with

1/(\omega^{2j}-\omega^{2k})
. The latter can be viewed as the discretization of the function
f(x,y)=1/(x-y)
with
x,y
both on the unit circle. When
|x-y|
is large enough, a truncated Taylor expansion of
f
could be used to construct good low-rank approximations to
submatrices of
C
only containing indices
(j,k)
for which
|\omega^{2j}-\omega^{2k}|
is not small; see, e.g.,
[
8
]
.
Intuitively, this suggests that submatrices of
C
far from the main diagonal as well as from the top-right and bottom-left corners (due to the periodicity of
\omega^{2j}
) should be compressible. To get sharper bounds, especially for regions close to the singularity of
f
, we appeal to arguments based on displacement structure instead of Taylor expansions.

Due to the singularity of the linear operator
C\mapsto DC-CD
, the diagonal entries of
C
cannot be recovered from the right-hand side of (
4
). These missing entries of
C
can be cheaply computed from
T
by exploiting the original displacement equation (
1
). To see this, consider the operator
\mathcal{S}:\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
defined by
\mathcal{S}(T):=ZT-TZ
. By the definition of
Z
, the null space of
\mathcal{S}
is the subspace of all cyclic
n\times n
matrices. Letting

\Pi(T)
denote the orthogonal projection of
T
onto this null space, it follows that
F\Pi(T)F^{*}
is diagonal. Its diagonal entries coincide with the diagonal entries of
C
, which can thus be computed by
applying an FFT to the first column of
\Pi(T)
. This first column is cheap to compute because the projection

\Pi(T)
is just the average along the cyclic diagonals of
T
. In particular, when
T
is a Toeplitz matrix,
the
k
th entry of this vector is given by

\left((n-k)t_{k}+kt_{-n+k}\right)/n.

In summary, only
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
operations are needed to compute the diagonal of
C
. Throughout the rest of the paper, we will therefore focus on computing the off-diagonal entries of
C
.

2.2
Singular value decay for matrices with displacement structure

Let us consider a general displacement equation of the form
AX-XB=Y
, where
A
and
B
are normal matrices with disjoint spectra
\Lambda(A)
and
\Lambda(B)
. It is shown in
[
6
]
that

(5)

\sigma_{k\rho+1}(X)\leq Z_{k}(E_{1},E_{2})\|X\|_{2},

where
\rho={\rm rank}(Y)
and
E_{1},E_{2}
are disjoint subsets of the complex plane with
\Lambda(A)\subset E_{1}
,
\Lambda(B)\subset E_{2}
.
The
k
th
Zolotarev number

Z_{k}(E_{1},E_{2})
is defined by

(6)

Z_{k}(E_{1},E_{2}):=\inf_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{k,k}}\frac{\sup_{z\in E_{1}}|r(z)|}{\inf_{z\in E_{2}}|r(z)|},

where
\mathcal{R}_{k,k}
contains all rational functions with numerator and denominator of degree at most
k
.

When
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are well-separated,
one expects that
Z_{k}(E_{1},E_{2})
decays rapidly as
k
increases and, in turn, the singular values of
X
also decay rapidly, provided that
\rho
is small.
1
1

1

Note that one can still establish rapid singular value decay for large
\rho
when the singular values of
Y
decay sufficiently fast
[
50
]
.

To gain meaningful insights, simple tight and non-asymptotic bounds for
Z_{k}(E_{1},E_{2})
are needed, which are only explicitly available for a few special cases of
E_{1},E_{2}
; see
[
6
]
. In
[
48
]
, a bound for the Zolotarev number is given for rather general
E_{1}
,
E_{2}
, but this bound depends on an invariant parameter associated with the conformal modulus of
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
and on the behavior of Faber rational functions, which can be difficult to tightly control for finite
k
. In general, this parameter can only be computed numerically. As we will see below, the sets
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
relevant in this work can always be taken as subarcs on the unit circle, which will allow us to
formulate and analyze explicit bounds on
Z_{k}(E_{1},E_{2})
.

2.3
Bounding the

Clearly, the technique from Section
2.2
cannot be applied directly to the Sylvester equation (

On the other hand, due to the diagonal structure of

By (

D_{J}C_{JK}-C_{JK}D_{K}=\widetilde{G}_{J}\widetilde{H}_{K}^{*},

where the diagonal matrix

contains the diagonal elements
\omega^{2j}
for

,

is defined analogously, and

\widetilde{G}_{J}

\widetilde{H}_{K}

,
\widetilde{H}

As long as

are disjoint and the equation (

are contained in disjoint arcs and we need to measure the relative gap between these arcs, taking the periodicity of

Given index sets

\displaystyle\max\{|k\!-\!k^{\prime}|:k,k^{\prime}\in K\}\leq m\!-\!1,

\displaystyle\min\{|j\!-\!k|:j\in J,k\in K\}\geq\mathsf{sep},\quad\min\{|j\!-\!k\!-\!n|:j\in J,k\in K\}\geq\mathsf{sep}.

Remark 4

Related results

Most of the existing bounds in the literature on the numerical rank of Cauchy matrices are derived from the separable approximation of the Cauchy kernel

3.2.1
A faster HODLR approximation

It turns out that the structure of

can be exploited further such that only one fADI computation per tree level is required (related to ideas mentioned in

). For this purpose, we introduce the “base” matrix

with entries

\mathscr{C}_{jk}=\frac{\omega^{j}\omega^{k}}{\omega^{2j}-\omega^{2k}}=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}\sin(\pi(j-k)/n)},\quad j\neq k,

and
\mathscr{C}_{jj}=0
, where
0\leq j,k\leq n\!-\!1
.
Note that
\mathscr{C}
satisfies a displacement equation of the form (
4

(22)

D\mathscr{C}-\mathscr{C}D=ff^{\top}-{\rm diag}(f^{2}),\quad f=\big[1,\omega^{1},\ldots,\omega^{n-1}\big]^{\top}.

In turn,

3
,

5
, and

6
can all be applied to the off-diagonal HODLR blocks of
\mathscr{C}
. However, it is easy to check that
\mathscr{C}
is also a Hermitian Toeplitz matrix.
This implies that the
2^{\ell+1}
off-diagonal HODLR blocks on a fixed level
\ell
are nearly identical: Considering the first off-diagonal block
\mathscr{C}_{v}
on level
\ell
, the other level-

blocks in the upper and lower triangular part are identical to
\mathscr{C}_{v}
and
{\mathscr{C}_{v}^{*}}
, respectively. Hence, it suffices
to construct a low-rank approximation for the block
\mathscr{C}_{v}
and use this approximation (or its Hermitian transpose) for all other blocks on the same level. This reduces the
\log^{2}\!n
factor to
\log n
in the complexity estimate (
21
).

To relate
\mathscr{C}
to the Cauchy-like matrix
C
, let us adjust the right-hand side of the displacement equation (
4
) by setting
\widehat{G}:=\mathrm{diag}(f)^{-1}\widetilde{G}
,

\widehat{H}^{*}:=\widetilde{H}^{*}\mathrm{diag}(f)^{-1}
. It follows that the off-diagonal parts of
C

and
\mathscr{C}\circ\widehat{G}\widehat{H}^{*}
are identical. In particular, arbitrary off-diagonal blocks
X=C_{JK}
,

\mathscr{X}=\mathscr{C}_{JK}
satisfy the relation

(23)

X=\mathscr{X}\circ\widehat{G}(J,:)\widehat{H}(K,:)^{*}=\sum_{j=0}^{\rho-1}{\rm diag}(\widehat{G}(J,j))\,\mathscr{X}\,{\rm diag}(\widehat{H}(K,j))^{*},

where we used Matlab notation to indicate submatrices of
\widehat{G}
,
\widehat{H}
. Using the fact that diagonal matrices permute, one easily verifies (see, e.g., (
18
)) that the
k
th iterates
X^{(k)}
and
\mathscr{X}^{(k)}
of fADI for
X
and
\mathscr{X}
, respectively, are related through

(24)

X^{(k)}=\mathscr{X}^{(k)}\circ\widehat{G}(J,:)\widehat{H}(K,:)^{*}=\sum_{j=0}^{\rho-1}{\rm diag}(\widehat{G}(J,j))\,\mathscr{X}^{(k)}\,{\rm diag}(\widehat{H}(K,j))^{*}.

This shows that the error estimate of Corollary
6
is maintained.

Because the computations of
\widehat{G},\widehat{H}
, and the diagonal do not add significantly to the cost, we still gain the complexity factor
\log n
mentioned above, both in terms of memory and cost.
This assumes that
X^{(k)}
is represented in the factored form (
24
), with the low-rank matrix
\mathscr{X}^{(k)}
obtained by carrying out one fADI computation per level.
This implicit representation is well suited when, e.g., carrying out matrix-vector products with the corresponding approximations
\widetilde{C}
and
\widetilde{T}
.
If each low-rank off-diagonal HODLR block

is actually evaluated, by multiplying the factors of
\mathscr{X}^{(k)}
with the diagonal matrices, this complexity advantage is lost. However, one can still expect this to perform
better than applying ADI separately for every off-diagonal block.

4
HSS approximation of Cauchy-like matrix
C

While the HODLR format has the advantage of being relatively simple to implement, significant savings in Steps 2 and 3 of Algorithm
1
are possible if more complicated hierarchical low-rank formats, such as the HSS format, are used.

Compared to HODLR, HSS features additional recursive relations in the off-diagonal low-rank factors across partition levels
[
5
,
26
,
37
]
.
Specifically, let
\widetilde{C}
be a HODLR matrix, and let every off-diagonal HODLR block
\widetilde{C}_{v}

be expressed as
\widetilde{C}_{v}=U_{v}B_{v}V_{\tilde{v}^{*}}
, where
\tilde{v}
is the sibling vertex of
v
in the tree
\mathcal{T}
determining the HODLR partitioning. For simplicity, we assume that each such block is of equal rank
p
and, hence,
B_{v,\tilde{v}}
is
p\times p
.
For an HSS matrix
\widetilde{C}
, the left factor
U_{v}
for a parent vertex
v
admits the expression

(25)

U_{v}=\begin{bmatrix}U_{2v+1}&0\\
0&U_{2v+2}\end{bmatrix}R_{v},\quad R_{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{2p\times p}.

In other words, the columns of
U_{v}
are contained in the subspace spanned by the direct sum of the left factors at the children vertices.
The transfer of information from the children to the parent vertex is encoded in the transfer matrix
R_{v}
. If the sibling
\tilde{v}
is a parent vertex, the right factor
V_{\tilde{v}}
also satisfies a similar relation:

(26)

V_{\tilde{v}}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{2\tilde{v}+1}&0\\
0&V_{2\tilde{v}+2}\end{bmatrix}W_{\tilde{v}},\quad W_{\tilde{v}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2p\times p}.

Expanding the recursions (
25
)–(
26
),
the factorization
\widetilde{C}_{1}=U_{1}B_{1,2}V_{2}^{*}
for the example from Figure

takes the form

\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}U_{7}&&&\\
&U_{8}&&\\
&&U_{9}&\\
&&&U_{10}\end{bmatrix}}_{4n_{\min}\times 4p}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}R_{3}&\\
&R_{4}\end{bmatrix}}_{4p\times 2p}\underbrace{R_{1}}_{2p\times p}\underbrace{B_{1,2}}_{p\times p}W_{2}^{*}\begin{bmatrix}W_{5}^{*}&\\
&W_{6}^{*}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}V_{11}^{*}&&&\\

where

In general, the recursion explained above allows one to store only the small matrices
R_{v}
,
W_{v}
, and
B_{v,\tilde{v}}
for every parent vertex
v\in\mathcal{T}
. On the lowest level
\widehat{\ell}
of the tree
\mathcal{T}
,
one explicitly stores the
n_{\min}\times n_{\min}
diagonal blocks as well as
U_{v},B_{v,\tilde{v}},
and
V_{v}
for each of the
2^{\widehat{\ell}}
leaf vertices
v
. We refer to the representation in terms of these small matrices as the
HSS format
of
\widetilde{C}
. Storing
\widetilde{C}
in the HSS format requires only
\mathcal{O}(np)
memory, which is about a factor
\log_{2}n
smaller compared to the HODLR format with HODLR rank
p
.

4.1
Superfast HSS-based solvers

The HSS format not only reduces the cost for storing but also the cost for operating with a matrix
\widetilde{C}
. For example, a matrix-vector product
\widetilde{C}y
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(np)
operations
[
37
, Alg. 1]
.
Crucially, the HSS format also makes the solution of a linear system
\widetilde{C}\widetilde{x}=\widetilde{b}
especially efficient to compute. In the following, we only sketch how such fast solvers work and refer to
[
12
,
55
]
for more details. After computing a so-called ULV factorization
[
12
]
– a recursive unitary transformation to triangular form that takes advantage of the nested hierarchical structure – a fast merge-and-backward-substitution routine, followed by a forward-substitution scheme, can be used to solve the linear system. This ULV factorization and the subsequent solution only require
\mathcal{O}(p^{2}n)
and
\mathcal{O}(pn)
operations, respectively. In
[
55
]
, a related ULV-like solver is introduced that relaxes the requirement of using unitary transformations, saving about a factor of 2 over the standard ULV solver
[
55
, Sec. 3.6]
. For this approach to work, every
U_{v},V_{v},R_{v}
, and
W_{v}
in the HSS format need to originate from interpolative decompositions.
2
2

2

In our experiments in
section

5
, we use the interpolative decompositions described in Section
4.3
, but then orthogonalize and apply the standard ULV solver.

Our focus in this work is not the reproduction of the HSS solvers already developed in
[
13
,
37
,
55
]
. Instead, we develop an fADI-based interpolative decomposition for appropriate HSS blocks. This HSS approximation is cheap and deterministic, and it allows for any HSS solver to be used, even the specialized one in
[
55
]
.

As discussed in

For a perfectly balanced binary

, the integer

4.3
Approximation by interpolative decompositions

By
[
32
, Thm. 4.7]
, there is an HSS matrix

\widetilde{C}
of HSS rank
p
, with the value of
p
stated in Lemma
8
, such that

\|C-\widetilde{C}\|_{2}\lesssim\sqrt{n}\epsilon
. Standard algorithms
[
54
]
for constructing such a compression
\widetilde{C}
proceed by recursively compressing the HSS blocks and rows in a bottom-up fashion. The compression on the upper levels by orthogonal projections is expensive, requiring already
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
operations for just evaluating the entries of the corresponding HSS block columns/rows. Interpolative decompositions
[
14
,
37
,
55
]
are a common approach to mitigate this problem. In this section, we explain how interpolative decompositions can be combined with fADI to cheaply construct HSS approximations to
C
.

4.3.1
Interpolative decompositions on the leaf level

Consider a leaf
v\in\mathcal{T}
with sibling
\tilde{v}
. Let
\mathbb{J}_{v}\subset J_{v}
and
\mathbb{K}_{\tilde{v}}\subset J_{\tilde{v}}
denote subsets, each of cardinality
p\ll m
. We aim at constructing
a two-sided interpolative decomposition
[
14
]
for the corresponding off-diagonal block
C_{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
,
m=n_{\min}
, of the form

(27)

C_{v}\approx\widetilde{C}_{v}=U_{v}B_{v,\tilde{v}}V_{\tilde{v}}^{*},\quad B_{v,\tilde{v}}=C(\mathbb{J}_{v},\mathbb{K}_{\tilde{v}}),

This decomposition is obtained from constructing one-sided
interpolative decompositions for the corresponding HSS block row/column:

(28)

C_{v}^{\mathrm{row}}\approx\widetilde{C}_{v}^{\mathrm{row}}=U_{v}C_{v}^{\mathrm{row}}(\mathbb{J}_{v},\,:\,),\quad C_{\tilde{v}}^{\mathrm{col}}\approx\widetilde{C}_{\tilde{v}}^{\mathrm{col}}=C_{\tilde{v}}^{\mathrm{col}}(\,:\,,\mathbb{K}_{\tilde{v}})V_{\tilde{v}}^{*}.

This requires that the selected subset
\mathbb{J}_{v}
of rows represents a good approximation
to the row space of
C_{v}^{\mathrm{row}}

and, analogously, that the
selected subset
\mathbb{K}_{\tilde{v}}
of columns represents a good approximation
to column space of
C_{\tilde{v}}^{\mathrm{col}}
. Both are instances of the column subset selection problem
[
10
]
, for which numerous algorithms have been developed, including
the column-pivoted QR (CPQR)
[
21
]
, (strong) rank-revealing QR decomposition
[
25
]
or Osinsky’s algorithm
[
43
]
.
However, applying such algorithms directly to HSS block rows/columns is expensive. For example,
C_{v}^{\mathrm{col}}
is of size
(n\!-\!m)\times m
, so the cost depends at least linearly on the “long” dimension
n
. One can do better with carefully implemented randomized methods
[
37
,
55
]
, but even this approach requires an initial precomputation that multiplies the uncompressed matrix
C
with an
n\times\Theta(p)
Gaussian random matrix.
3
3

3

In
[
55
]
, this step is greatly improved with a fast matrix-vector multiplication routine for
C
based on its relationship to the Toeplitz matrix
T
.

In the following, we show how the interpolative decompositions (
28
)
can be found deterministically using fADI with a cost that depends only on
m
and
p
.

We focus on the selection of
\mathbb{J}_{v}
; the process for finding
\mathbb{K}_{\tilde{v}}
is entirely analogous.
Set
X:=C_{v}^{\mathrm{row}}
.
We know from (
4
) that
X
satisfies

D_{J}X-XD_{K}=\widetilde{G}_{J}\widetilde{H}_{K}^{*},\quad J=J_{v},\quad K=J_{v}^{c}.

After
k
iterations of fADI, the approximation

(29)

X\approx X^{(k)}=ZW^{*},\quad Z\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times p},\quad p=k\rho,

is constructed. To find an approximate one-sided row interpolative decomposition, the (large) matrix
W
is actually not needed. A key advantage of fADI is that it constructs
Z
independently from
W
using only the
m\times\rho
matrix
\widetilde{G}_{J}
and the
m\times m
diagonal matrix
D_{J}
(see
algorithm

2
). In turn, the construction of
Z
only requires
\mathcal{O}(m\rho)
operations.

One continues from (
29
) by selecting a good subset of rows in the (smaller) factor
Z
. For convenience,
our numerical experiments apply CPQR to
Z^{*}
for this purpose. This has a computational cost of only
\mathcal{O}(mp^{2})
operations and is usually a safe choice.
4
4

4

Despite famous adversarial examples where CPQR fails, it is considered highly reliable in practice
[
25
]
.
Better theoretical bounds are obtained, at the same asymptotic cost, by using more sophisticated methods, such as Osinsky’s algorithm
[
43
]
; see also
[
15
]
. For this purpose, we first compute a QR decomposition
Z=QR
with
Q\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times p}
. Applying Algorithm 2 from
[
43
]
to
Q^{*}
returns an index set

\mathbb{J}_{v}
of cardinality
p
such that

(30)

\|Z\cdot Z(\mathbb{J}_{v},:)^{-1}\|_{2}=\|Q\cdot Q(\mathbb{J}_{v},:)^{-1}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{1+p(m-p)};

see
[
43
, Thm. 5]
. Setting
U_{v}=Z\cdot Z(\mathbb{J}_{v},:)^{-1}
concludes the construction of the desired
approximation
C_{v}^{\mathrm{row}}\approx U_{v}C_{v}^{\mathrm{row}}(\mathbb{J}_{v},\,:\,)
.

Lemma 9
.

Set
X=C_{v}^{\mathrm{row}}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times(n-m)}
.
The approximation
X\approx U_{v}X(\mathbb{J}_{v},\,:\,)
described above satisfies the error bound

(31)

\|X-U_{v}X(\mathbb{J}_{v},\,:\,)\|_{2}\leq 4\xi^{-k}\big(1+\sqrt{1+p(m-p)}\big)\|X\|_{2},

with
\xi=\exp(\pi^{2}/(2\log(4m))
and
p=k\rho
.

Proof.

Observe that

\|X-U_{v}X(\mathbb{J}_{v},\,:\,)\|_{2}\leq\|X-ZW^{*}\|_{2}+\|ZW^{*}-U_{v}X(\mathbb{J}_{v},\,:\,)\|_{2}.

The second term is bounded by

\|ZW^{*}-U_{v}X(\mathbb{J}_{v},\,:\,)\|_{2}=\big\|U_{v}\big(Z(\mathbb{J}_{v},:)W^{*}-X(\mathbb{J}_{v},\,:\,)\big)\big\|_{2}\leq\|U_{v}\|_{2}\|ZW^{*}-X\|_{2}.

The proof is concluded by using (
30
) for bounding
\|U_{v}\|_{2}
, and
\|X-ZW^{*}\|_{2}\leq 4\xi^{-k}\|X\|_{2}
,
which follows from a straightforward extension of
corollary

6
.

We view (
31
) as the deterministic analogue to the error bounds supplied in
[
55
, Eq. 3.6]
for interpolative decompositions based on randomized linear algebra. There, one finds
\Theta_{v}X(\mathbb{J}_{v},\;:\;)
, which is a rank
p+\mu
approximate row interpolative decomposition for
X
, with
\mu
selected as a small oversampling parameter
[
28
]
. Properties of Gaussian random matrices can be used to show that with probability
1-6\mu^{-\mu}
, the error is bounded as
[
55
, Eq. (3.6)]

(32)

\|X-\Theta_{v}X(\mathbb{J}_{v},\;:\;)\|_{2}\leq\big(1+11\sqrt{m(p+\mu)}\big)\sigma_{p+1}(X).

Using (
13
) and (
5
), our bounds show that
\sigma_{p+1}(X)\leq 4\xi^{-\lfloor p/\rho\rfloor}\|X\|_{2}
, where
\xi
is given in (
13
). This makes (
32
) explicit so that if one prefers to use a solver based on randomized linear algebra,
p
can be chosen a priori in order to ensure (w.h.p.) that the relative error is bounded by
\epsilon
.

Once the interpolative decomposition (
27
) is known for each block on the finest level
\widehat{\ell}
of
\mathcal{T}
, the remaining factors associated with the non-leaf vertices of
\mathcal{T}
are determined
in a bottom-up fashion. We briefly describe the process of merging from the leaf level up to the next coarsest level, but refer to
[
53
]
for substantial details on such processes, which are admittedly technical but also standard and well-established.

Let
v
be a vertex on level
\ell=\widehat{\ell}\!-\!1
. Then, as before, we aim at finding an approximation
C_{v}\approx\widetilde{C}_{v}=U_{v}B_{v,\tilde{v}}V_{\tilde{v}}^{*}
. It is assumed that the outer factor
U_{v}
and

satisfy the relations (
25
) and (
26
), respectively, and it thus only remains to determine

,
W_{\tilde{v}}

\mathbb{J}_{v}
,
\mathbb{K}_{\tilde{v}}
defining

C_{v}^{\mathrm{row}}

C_{2v+1}^{\mathrm{row}}
,
C_{2v+2}^{\mathrm{row}}

:

C_{2v+j}^{\mathrm{row}}(\,:\,,\,J_{v}^{c})\approx U_{2v+j}C_{2v+j}^{\mathrm{row}}(\mathbb{J}_{2v+j},\,J_{v}^{c}\,).

(33)

C_{v}^{\mathrm{row}}\approx\begin{bmatrix}U_{2v+1}&0\\
0&U_{2v+2}\end{bmatrix}C_{v}(\widehat{\mathbb{J}},\,:\,),

where

4.5
A faster HSS factorization scheme

As with the HODLR approximation to
C
, the identity (
22
) can be used to improve the overall cost of the HSS factorization. We take advantage of this when performing fADI on the lowest level
\hat{\ell}
of the partitioning determined by
\mathcal{T}
. It suffices to find fADI approximants only for the first HSS row and column of
\mathscr{C}
. Then, we use these approximations and (
23
) to cheaply compute the left factors of the low-rank approximations for every level-
\hat{\ell}
HSS row and column of
C
. Once this is done, CPQR or Osinsky’s algorithm are used find interpolative decompositions from each of these low rank approximations.

In principle, one could go further with this idea, and cheaply compute interpolative decompositions
\mathscr{C}_{v}=\mathcal{U}_{v}\mathscr{C}(\mathbb{J}_{v},\mathbb{K}_{\tilde{v}})\mathcal{V}_{\tilde{v}}^{*}
on every partition level. When the structure of
\mathscr{C}
is fully exploited and approximations to the submatrices of
C
are stored in the factored form shown as in (
24
), the cost for finding an approximate HSS factorization only requires
\mathcal{O}(\rho^{3}\log^{3}n\log^{3}1/\epsilon)
operations, that is, this step only depends poly-logarithmically (and not even linearly) on
n
. This is an effective format for some operations (e.g., matrix-vector products). For solving linear systems, (
23
) must be used to assemble low rank approximations to each
C_{v}
. A similar idea is discussed in
[
13
,
55
]
. We note that applying (
23
) destroys the interpolative structure of the HSS factors, ruling out the use of the accelerated ULV-like solver from
[
55
]
in Step 3 of Algorithm
1
.

5
A fast HSS-based solver

Without exploiting (
24
), the computational complexity of constructing an HSS approximation to
C
with our fADI-based interpolative decomposition scheme requires
\mathcal{O}(n(\rho\log n\log 1/\epsilon)^{2})
operations; a more detailed complexity
analysis can be found in
[
53
, Ch. 4]
. Putting this together with existing HSS-based solvers for linear systems, the overall complexity for solving
Tx=b
via
algorithm

1
is also
\mathcal{O}(n(\rho\log n\log 1/\epsilon)^{2})
.

We remark that the bounds in
theorem

3
indicate that a solver for
Tx=b
with linear asymptotic complexity in
n
appears to be possible. This is because submatrices of
C
sufficiently separated from the main diagonal have
\epsilon
-ranks that are constant with respect to
n
. An appropriate hierarchical matrix approximation with strong admissibility could exploit this fact. Linear inversion routines for such matrices are available
[
2
,
40
]
, but the analysis and implementation of these methods are beyond the scope of this paper.

6
Conclusions

We have developed new ADI-based strategies for cheaply and reliably approximating Toeplitz-like matrices with hierarchical low-rank matrices, specifically HODLR and HSS matrices. We have demonstrated how such an approximation can be used to develop a competitive solver for linear systems with Toeplitz structure. Other potential applications include
eigenvalue problems
[
44
]
, matrix functions
[
11
]
, and matrix equations
[
32
]
involving Toeplitz and Toeplitz-like matrices.
