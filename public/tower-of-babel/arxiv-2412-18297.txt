Learning to Play Against Unknown Opponents
Eshwar Ram Arunachaleswaran, Natalie Collina, Jon Schneider

Abstract
We consider the problem of a learning agent who has to repeatedly play a general sum game against a strategic opponent who acts to maximize their own payoff by optimally responding against the learner’s algorithm. The learning agent knows their own payoff function, but is uncertain about the payoff of their opponent (knowing only that it is drawn from some distribution 𝒟 \mathcal{D} ). What learning algorithm should the agent run in order to maximize their own total utility, either in expectation or in the worst-case over 𝒟 \mathcal{D} ? When the learning algorithm is constrained to be a no-regret algorithm, we demonstrate how to efficiently construct an optimal learning algorithm (asymptotically achieving the optimal utility) in polynomial time for both the in-expectation and worst-case problems, independent of any other assumptions. When the learning algorithm is not constrained to no-regret, we show how to construct an ε \varepsilon -optimal learning algorithm (obtaining average utility within ε \varepsilon of the optimal utility) for both the in-expectation and worst-case problems in time polynomial in the size of the input and 1 / ε 1/\varepsilon , when either the size of the game or the support of 𝒟 \mathcal{D} is constant. Finally, for the special case of the maximin objective, where the learner wishes to maximize their minimum payoff over all possible optimizer types, we construct a learner algorithm that runs in polynomial time in each step and guarantees convergence to the optimal learner payoff. All of these results make use of recently developed machinery that converts the analysis of learning algorithms to the study of the class of corresponding geometric objects known as menus.



1
Introduction

How should we design learning algorithms that act on our behalf in repeated strategic interactions (e.g., repeatedly bidding into an auction, playing a repeated game against an opponent, repeatedly negotiating a contract, etc.)?

One approach (arguably, the standard approach in the literature) is to design learning algorithms that satisfy strong worst-case counterfactual guarantees. For example, it is possible to design learning algorithms that guarantee
low (external) regret
: that after
T
rounds, the gap between the utility achieved by the algorithm and the utility achieved by the best fixed action in hindsight grows sublinearly in
T
. This approach has the strength that it assumes essentially nothing about the behavior of the opponent (that is, it works for any adversarially chosen sequence of opponent actions). But this strength is also, in some ways, a weakness: in most strategic environments, the opponent will be a rational agent who acts not completely adversarially, but to maximize their own payoff. Paradoxically, this can lead to situations where a learning algorithm performs quite poorly despite satisfying these guarantees; for example,
[
6
]
show that if a bidder uses certain low regret algorithms to decide how to bid in a repeated auction, the auctioneer can take advantage of this to extract the full welfare of the buyer. Moreover, it is common to have at least some information about our opponent’s incentives, even if we do not understand them completely, and it is wasteful to not take advantage of this information.

A second approach (the one we take in this paper) is to take inspiration from Bayesian mechanism design and consider the problem of designing a learning algorithm to play against a
prior distribution
of possible opponents. Each opponent in the support of this distribution has their own utility function, is aware of the learning algorithm the learner is using, and (non-myopically) chooses their sequence of actions to maximize their own utility. We would like to design a learning algorithm that maximizes the utility of the learner over the course of this process. In other words, we want to solve a Stackelberg equilibrium problem in
algorithm space
, where the learner leads by committing to a specific learning algorithm, and their opponent follows by playing the sequence of actions that optimally responds to this learning algorithm.

In contrast to the first approach (where efficient algorithms achieving a variety of different regret guarantees have been designed), fairly little is currently understood about this second approach. When the distribution
\mathcal{D}
is a singleton distribution (i.e., the learner knows the optimizer’s payoff exactly), recent work
[
33
,
8
]
has resolved the algorithmic question of efficiently finding this optimal commitment. However in the more general setting where the learner possesses some uncertainty about the optimizer’s payoff (the setting where one would most naturally want to employ a learning algorithm), this question remains open.

1.1
Our Results

In this paper, we address this question by providing efficient algorithms for this problem in a variety of different contexts. More formally, we consider a setting where two players – a learner and an optimizer – repeatedly play a general-sum normal-form game
G
, where the learner has
m
pure strategies and the optimizer has
n
pure strategies. In this game, the payoff function
u_{L}
of the learner is fixed and known to both parties, but the optimizer’s payoff function
u_{O}
is sampled (once, before the game) from a known prior distribution
\mathcal{D}
with support size
k
.

At the beginning of the game, the learner commits to playing a specific learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, which describes their action at time
t
as a function of the actions taken by the optimizer from rounds
1
through
t-1
. The optimizer (of a specific type, sampled from
\mathcal{D}
) best responds to this commitment by playing the sequence of actions which maximizes their own utility. We are now faced with the following computational question: can we efficiently construct a learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that maximizes the utility of the learner?

In this paper we study this computational question in a handful of different settings. We prove the following main results:

1.

We first study this question under the constraint that our learning-algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is
no-regret
(obtaining
o(T)
regret after
T

m

k

\mathcal{A}
that achieves and computes the optimal utility for the learner (Theorem
3.2
).

2.

We then turn our attention to the problem of constructing the optimal learning algorithm under no additional constraints. In this setting, we provide a method to construct an
{\varepsilon}
-approximately optimal learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
in time

\left(\frac{mnk}{{\varepsilon}}\right)^{O(\min(mn,k))}.

This results in an efficient construction when
either
the size
mn
of the game or the size
k
of the support is constant-sized (Theorem
4.1
).

3.

We then provide some evidence to show that the unconstrained problem above is hard when both
mn
and
k
are large. In particular, we prove that it is NP-hard to take as input a
k

(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{k})

\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{A}

4.

Finally, complementing this hardness result, we demonstrate an explicit efficient optimal learning algorithm under no additional constraints for the
maximin

V

V
(a problem we conjecture is NP-hard).

Naively, the above computational problems are quite difficult, as they involve optimizing over the space of
learning algorithms
: complex, infinite-dimensional objects defined via sequences of multivariate functions (e.g., the function which maps the history at time
t
to the action played at time
t
). We handle this by applying a sequence of reductions from this general optimization problem to more tractable optimization problems.

First, we employ a framework introduced by
[
1
]
that allows us to translate utility-theoretic statements about learning algorithms to equivalent statements about convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{mn}
called
menus
. At a high level, given any learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, the menu
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})\subset\mathbb{R}^{mn}
contains all distributions over pairs of pure strategies (“correlated strategy profiles”) that the optimizer can asymptotically induce by playing against this learning algorithm. Importantly, these correlated strategy profiles contain all the information necessary to compute quantities such as the utilities of both the learner and optimizer along with various forms of regret incurred by the learner. The menu
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
of a learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
therefore forms a sufficient summary of all relevant properties of
\mathcal{A}
; instead of thinking of an opponent as playing an arbitrary sequence of
T
actions, we can think of this opponent as selecting a specific correlated strategy profile outcome from the menu
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
and inducing it by playing the corresponding sequence of actions.

This reduces an optimization problem over the set of all algorithms to an optimization problem over a collection of convex sets (possible menus corresponding to a learning algorithm). Although convex sets are much “nicer” mathematical objects than learning algorithms, this optimization problem is still quite difficult – the space of convex sets is still infinite-dimensional, even when they are subsets of a finite-dimensional Euclidean space. Here we reduce the problem further by applying a variant of the revelation principle and considering the resulting matching from the
k
different optimizer types to the correlated strategy profiles they choose from the menu, which we call a
CSP assignment
. This CSP assignment is
kmn
-dimensional (specifying one correlated strategy profile in
\mathbb{R}^{mn}
for each optimizer type), and so if we can provide an effective description of the set of valid CSP assignments (e.g., via an efficient separation oracle), we can efficiently compute the CSP assignment which optimizes the learner’s utility (and with a little more work, the optimal learning algorithm that induces this CSP assignment).

We are now left with the problem of characterizing the set of valid CSP assignments, which in turn involves understanding the space of valid menus. In the setting where we restrict our algorithm to also be no-regret, we show how to write this set as an explicit
kmn
-dimensional polytope, thus allowing us to compute the optimal algorithm in polynomial time. Our main tool here is a characterization by
[
1
]

1.2
Related Work

Our paper most directly builds off of the work of
[
1
]
, who originally introduced the framework of considering asymptotic menus of learning algorithms. This framework was introduced by
[
1
]
with the goal of characterizing the set of
Pareto-optimal
learning algorithms. In the context of this paper, these are exactly the learning algorithms that are optimal for the learner to commit to against some distribution
\mathcal{D}
over optimizer payoffs. Surprisingly,
[
1
]
show that in some games
G
(i.e., for some payoff functions
u_{L}
for the learner), standard no-regret algorithms such as multiplicative weights and follow-the-regularized-leader are Pareto-dominated, implying that in these games they will never appear as solutions to our optimization problem.

Single-shot (i.e., not repeated) instances of Stackelberg games have been somewhat well studied in the economics and computational game theory literature
[
21
]
. In these games, a leader commits to an optimal strategy against a rational follower. The specific variant of the problem that we study is one where the leader has some uncertainty over the follower’s type, in the form of a prior distribution over types of the follower. This problem was introduced by
[
11
]
, who called it the Bayesian Stackelberg Problem. Our problem can be phrased as a Bayesian Stackelberg problem where the learner’s action set is the set of all learning algorithms and the optimizer’s action set is the set of all action sequences (although framing it directly in this way is not particularly helpful from a computational point of view, since both these action spaces are incredibly large). Interestingly,
[
11
]
show that this problem is NP-hard even for explicit bimatrix games, making it somewhat striking that we can efficiently compute the optimal no-regret algorithm to commit to. Other papers, such as
[
30
]
have attempted to come up with good heuristics and algorithms to solve this problem for various special cases corresponding to applications of interest, including airport security, cybersecurity, etc.

There is also a rich line of work focused on the problem of learning the (single-shot) Stackelberg equilibrium of an unknown game via repeated interaction with an agent of some specified behavior. For example, one can try to estimate this Stackelberg equilibrium by repeatedly interacting with an agent that myopically best-responds every round
[
26
,
3
,
34
]
, a no-regret agent
[
7
,
20
]
, a no-swap-regret agent / an agent that best-responds to calibrated action forecasts
[
7
,
23
]
, or an agent belonging to a parametrized class of behaviors
[
32
]
. It is worth emphasizing that this is a fairly different computational problem than the problem we study in this work – although the algorithms we commit to may “try” to learn the payoffs of their opponent in a similar way to these algorithms, we 1. do not make any behavioral assumptions about the opponents (allowing them to react fully rationally and non-myopically), and 2. attempt to find the Bayesian Stackelberg equilibrium of the full repeated game, not the single-shot Stackelberg equilibrium. Indeed, the recent work of
[
2
]
shows that (in some sense) there can be a gap between these two quantities; there are cases where a learner provably cannot (i.e., in no Nash equilibrium of the game) achieve their expected Stackelberg value against a distribution of opponents.

Our paper also contributes to a growing body of research focused on the strategic manipulability of learning algorithms within game-theoretic contexts. One of the foundational contributions to this area is by
[
6
]
, who examined these issues within the framework of non-truthful auctions involving a single buyer. Since then, similar dynamics have been explored across various economic models, such as alternative auction settings
[
15
,
12
,
25
,
24
]
, contract theory
[
19
]
, Bayesian persuasion
[
10
]
, general games
[
16
,
7
]
, and Bayesian games
[
28
]
. Both
[
16
]
and
[
28
]
establish that no-swap-regret is a necessary criterion to prevent optimizers from gaining by manipulating learners.

We crucially use the existence of efficient no-swap regret algorithms in constructing our optimal no-regret algorithm in Section
3
. The first no-swap-regret algorithms were introduced by
[
18
]
, who also demonstrated that their dynamics converge to correlated equilibria. Since then, numerous researchers have developed learning algorithms aimed at minimizing swap regret in games
[
22
,
5
,
31
,
13
]
. Our polynomial-time algorithm for designing general menus for the maximin objective is similar to (and partially motivated by) the technique of
semi-separation
recently introduced by
[
14
]
, who use this technique to design learning algorithms that minimize a variant of swap regret (linear swap regret) despite this variant being computationally hard to compute.

Model and Preliminaries

Notation

Unless otherwise specified, all norms
\|x\|
of a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
refer to the Euclidean norm. Given any convex set
K
, we write
K^{{\varepsilon}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\exists\,y\in K\text{ s.t. }\|x-y\|\leq{\varepsilon}\}
to denote the
{\varepsilon}
-expansion of
K
. We write
\Delta_{d}
to denote the
d
-simplex
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid x_{i}\geq 0,\sum x_{i}=1\}

2.1
Online Learning

We consider a setting where two players, a
learner

L
and an
optimizer

O
, repeatedly play a two-player bimatrix game
G
for
T
rounds. The game
G
has
m
pure actions for the learner and
n
pure actions for the optimizer. The learner has a fixed payoff function
u_{L}:[m]\times[n]\rightarrow[-1,1]
, known to both parties. The optimizer has one of
k
finitely many possible payoff functions
u_{O,i}:[m]\times[n]\rightarrow[-1,1]
, where their type
i\in[k]
is drawn from a known distribution
\mathcal{D}
once at the beginning of the game. We will let
\alpha_{i}\in[0,1]
denote the probability associated with type
i
.

During each round
t
, the learner picks a mixed strategy
x_{t}\in\Delta_{m}
while the optimizer simultaneously picks a mixed strategy
y_{t}\in\Delta_{n}
; the learner then receives reward
u_{L}(x_{t},y_{t})
, and the optimizer with private type
i\in[k]
receives reward
u_{O,i}(x_{t},y_{t})
(where here we have linearly extended
u_{L}
and
u_{O,i}
to bilinear functions over the domain
\Delta_{m}\times\Delta_{n}
). Both the learner and optimizer observe the full mixed strategy of the other player (the “full-information” setting). For any mixed strategy
y\in\Delta_{n}
for the optimizer, we define
\BR_{L}(y)=\arg\max_{x\in\Delta_{m}}u_{L}(x,y)
to be the set of best responses for the learner.

The learner will employ a
learning algorithm

\mathcal{A}
to decide how to play. For our purposes, a learning algorithm is a family of horizon-dependent algorithms
\{A^{T}\}_{T\in\mathbb{N}}
. Each
A^{T}
describes the algorithm the learner follows for a fixed time horizon
T
. Each horizon-dependent algorithm is a mapping from the history of play to the next round’s action, denoted by a collection of
T
functions
A^{T}_{1},A^{T}_{2}\cdots A^{T}_{T}
, each of which deterministically map the transcript of play (up to the corresponding round) to a mixed strategy to be used in the next round, i.e.,
x_{t}=A^{T}_{t}(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{t-1})
.

We will be interested in settings where a learner (not knowing
u_{O}
, but knowing
u_{L}
and
\mathcal{D}
) commits to a specific learning algorithm at the beginning of the game. This algorithm is then revealed to the optimizer, who will approximately best-respond by selecting a sequence of actions that approximately (up to sublinear
o(T)
factors, breaking ties in the learner’s favor) maximizes their payoff.

2.2
Menus

This faces the learner with the very complex task of
optimization over all learning algorithms
– complex mathematical objects described by families of functions. One main contribution of this paper is to demonstrate that this optimization task is (in many cases) feasible, by following a framework introduced in
[
1
]
that allows us to translate statements about learning algorithms to statements about convex sets called
menus
.

Note that every transcript of play of two players induces a distribution over pairs of actions they played. In particular, each transcript
\{(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2}),\dots,(x_{T},y_{T})\}
corresponds to an
mn
-dimensional
correlated strategy profile (CSP)

\phi\in\Delta_{mn}
, that is defined as

\phi=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}\otimes y_{t}.

A CSP
\phi
contains enough information to answer many questions about the outcome of this game: for example, with the CSP
\phi
we can compute the average utility
u_{L}(\phi)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}u_{L}(x_{t},y_{t})
of the learner (and likewise, of any of the types of optimizers).

For this reason, instead of understanding the full complexities of a learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, it is sufficient to understand which CSPs it is possible for an optimizer to implement against this learning algorithm. Informally, this is exactly what a menu is: the
asymptotic menu
(or simply,
menu
)
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\Delta_{mn}
is a closed, convex subset of CSPs with the property that any
\phi\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
is close to some CSP that the optimizer can induce by playing some sequence of actions
y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{T}
against this learning algorithm, for sufficiently large
T
.

For completeness, we spell out the exact correspondence between learning algorithms and menus (following
[
1
]
) in Appendix
B
. For the remainder of the paper, however, instead of thinking about the setting of algorithmic commitment described previously, we will instead consider the following equivalent setting of
menu commitment
:

1.

The learner commits to a menu
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\Delta_{mn}
which is a valid asymptotic menu of some learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
.

2.

The optimizer (of type
i
) then chooses the CSP
\phi\in\mathcal{M}
that maximizes their utility
u_{O,i}(\phi)
, breaking ties in favor of the learner.

3.

The learner then receives utility
u_{L}(\phi)
.

For any asymptotic menu
\mathcal{M}
, define
V_{L}(\mathcal{M},u_{O})
to be the utility the learner ends up with under this process. Specifically, define
V_{L}(\mathcal{M},u_{O},\varepsilon)=\max\{u_{L}(\varphi)\mid\varphi\in\mathcal{M},u_{O}(\varphi)\geq\max_{\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}}u_{O}(\varphi^{\prime})-\varepsilon\}
and
V_{L}(\mathcal{M},u_{O})=V_{L}(\mathcal{M},u_{O},0)
.
We refer to any CSP in the set
\argmax_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}}\{u_{L}(\varphi)\mid u_{O}(\varphi)\geq\max_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}}u_{O}(\varphi)\}
as the point picked or selected by the optimizer
u_{O}
in the menu
\mathcal{M}
.

The menu commitment problem above is complicated by the fact that learner must commit to a menu
\mathcal{M}
which is the asymptotic menu of some learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
(we will often refer to such sets as
valid menus
); without this constraint, the learner could simply pick their favorite CSP in all of
\Delta_{mn}
and force the optimizer to play it. Luckily, it is possible to provide an extremely precise characterization of all possible valid menus.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
1
]
)
.

A closed, convex subset
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\Delta_{mn}
is a valid menu iff for every
y\in\Delta_{n}
, there exists a
x\in\Delta_{m}
such that
x\otimes y\in\mathcal{M}
.

An immediate corollary of this property is that menus are upwards closed under inclusion.

Corollary 2.2
.

If
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}\subseteq\Delta_{mn}
and
\mathcal{A}
is a valid menu, then
\mathcal{B}
is also a valid menu.

In fact, an artifact of the proof of Theorem
2.1
is that any menu with an efficient representation can be implemented via an efficient learning algorithm that asymptotically realizes the original menu. The main idea of this algorithm is to run an algorithm for Blackwell approachability
[
4
]
to converge to the appropriate set of CSPs, combining this with a “padding” argument to guarantee that every point in this set is indeed approachable. For the sake of completeness, we outline the same sequence of ideas in Appendix
B.3
.

2.3
Menus and Regret

Many learning algorithms are designed to possess specific counterfactual regret guarantees. Two of these properties – no-regret and no-swap-regret – are of particular importance in this paper.

A learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is a
no-regret algorithm
if it is the case that, regardless of the sequence of actions
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{T})
taken by the optimizer, the learner’s utility satisfies:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}u_{L}(x_{t},y_{t})\geq\left(\max_{x^{*}\in[m]}\sum_{t=1}^{T}u_{L}(x^{*},y_{t})\right)-o(T).

A learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is a
no-swap-regret algorithm
if it is the case that, regardless of the sequence of actions
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{T})
taken by the optimizer, the learner’s utility satisfies:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}u_{L}(x_{t},y_{t})\geq\max_{\pi:[m]\rightarrow[m]}\sum_{t=1}^{T}u_{L}(\pi(x_{t}),y_{t})-o(T).

Here the maximum is over all swap functions
\pi:[m]\rightarrow[m]
(extended linearly to act on elements
x_{t}
of
\Delta_{m}
). It is a fundamental result in the theory of online learning that both no-swap-regret algorithms and no-regret algorithms exist (see
[
9
]
).

The above properties on algorithms directly translate into properties of the menus that they induce. We say that a given CSP
\varphi
is
no-regret
if it satisfies the no-regret constraint

\sum_{i\in[m]}\sum_{j\in[n]}\varphi_{ij}u_{L}(i,j)\geq\max_{i^{*}\in[m]}\sum_{i\in[m]}\sum_{j\in[n]}\varphi_{ij}u_{L}(i^{*},j).

(1)

Similarly, we say that the CSP
\varphi
is
no-swap-regret
if, for each
j\in[n]
, it satisfies

\sum_{i\in[m]}\varphi_{ij}u_{L}(i,j)\geq\sum_{j\in[n]}\max_{i_{j}^{*}\in[m]}\sum_{i\in[m]}\varphi_{ij}u_{L}(i_{j}^{*},j).

(2)

For a fixed
u_{L}
, we will define the
no-regret menu

\mathcal{M}_{NR}
to be the convex hull of all no-regret CSPs, and the
no-swap-regret menu

\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
to be the convex hull of all no-swap-regret CSPs. The following result states that the asymptotic menu of any no-(swap-)regret algorithm is contained in the no-(swap-)regret menu.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
1
]
)

If a learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is no-regret, then
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{NR}
. If
\mathcal{A}
is no-swap-regret, then
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
.

Note that both
\mathcal{M}_{NR}
and
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}

x\otimes y

(thus satisfying the characterization in Theorem

[
1
]
) of all no-swap-regret algorithms and the set
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
.

First, we see that all no-swap-regret algorithms have the same asymptotic menu, which is exactly the set
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
1
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a no-swap-regret algorithm, then
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
.

To complement this property, we see that the menu of every no-regret algorithm weakly contains the set
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
1
]
)
.

For any no-regret algorithm
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
.

Theorem
2.4
is significant, since there exist several known efficient no-swap-regret algorithms (see
[
5
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
31
]
), and so we obtain an explicit algorithm which has the menu
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
. Taken together with Lemma
2.5
, this means that we can use any no-swap-regret algorithm as a “fallback subroutine” when constructing an optimal no-regret algorithm against some distribution of optimizers – we make use of this idea in the proof of Theorem
3.2
.

2.4
Stackelberg Equilibria

Finally, we present an alternative view of the polytope
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
in terms of its extreme points rather than its defining hyperplanes.

Lemma 2.6

(
[
1
]
)
.

The no-swap-regret menu
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
is the convex hull of all CSPs of the form
x\otimes y
, with
x\in\Delta_{m}
and
y\in\BR_{L}(x)
.

Correlated strategy profiles of the form
x\otimes y
, with
x\in\Delta_{m}
and
y\in\BR_{L}(x)
have a special significance in games. They are the candidate solutions for the Stackelberg Equilibrium problem
[
21
]
, which we define below.

Two players with action sets
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
and payoff functions
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
(respectively) play a Stackelberg game in the following manner. Player 1, the “leader”, commits to an action
a^{*}
in
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and player 2, the “follower”, plays a best-response
\BR(a^{*})\in\mathcal{B}_{2}
that maximizes their payoff, tie-breaking in favor of the leader. The leader must pick an action
a^{*}
to maximize their own resulting payoff. Formally

a^{*}\in\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}u_{1}(a,b^{*})

where
b^{*}\in\argmax_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}u_{2}(a^{*},b)
and
u_{1}(a^{*},b^{*})\geq u_{1}(a^{*},b^{\prime})
for all
b^{\prime}\in\argmax_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}u_{2}(a^{*},b)
. The resulting action pair
(a^{*},b^{*})
is referred to as a
Stackelberg equilibrium
of the game and the payoff of the leader is referred to as the
Stackelberg leader value
.

In the normal form game version of this problem (where
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
are both simplices and the payoffs are bilinear functions), Stackelberg equilibria are guaranteed to exist and can be computed efficiently.

Theorem 2.7

(
[
11
]
)
.

There exists an efficient (polynomial time in the size of the game) algorithm to compute Stackelberg equilibria in normal form games.

This definition of Stackelberg equilibria allows us to reinterpret Lemma
2.6
in the following manner – the extreme points of
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
are exactly the set of candidate Stackelberg equilibria in the Stackelberg variant of our game where the optimizer is the leader and the learner is the follower. In other words, an asymptotic best-response for an optimizer playing against a learner running a no-swap-regret algorithm is to play their Stackelberg leader strategy on every single round.

2.5
Main Results

We conclude this section by restating our main problem in the language of menus. Note that the expected payoff of a menu
\mathcal{M}
chosen by the learner against a distribution
\mathcal{D}
of optimizers is given by

V_{L}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{D})=\mathbb{E}_{u_{O}\sim\mathcal{D}}[V_{L}(\mathcal{M},u_{O})]=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}V_{L}(\mathcal{M},u_{O,i}).

(3)

We can similarly define
V_{L}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{D},\varepsilon)
in terms of the
V_{L}(\mathcal{M},u_{O,i},\varepsilon)
. We primarily consider the following two computational questions:

1.

(General commitment)
. Given
u_{L}
, a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
u_{O,i}
, and a parameter
\varepsilon
, compute a menu
\mathcal{M}
that is
\varepsilon
-approximately optimal; i.e., such that

V_{L}(\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{D},\varepsilon)\geq\max_{\text{$\mathcal{M}$ is a valid menu}}V_{L}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{D})-\varepsilon

2.

(No-regret commitment)
. The same question, but with the added constraint that
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{NR}
; that is, what is the best menu to commit to if you also want to guarantee low regret against any possible opponent (not necessarily in the support of
\mathcal{D}
)?

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In Section
3
, we prove that we can efficiently (in polynomial time in
n
,
m
, and
k
) solve the problem of no-regret commitment even for
\varepsilon=0
. In Section
4
, we study the problem of general commitment. We prove that if either
k
(the support of
\mathcal{D}
) or
mn
(the size of the game) is constant, it is possible to solve the problem of general commitment in time polynomial in
1/\varepsilon
and the other parameters. On the other hand, we show that it is in general hard to even decide whether a given convex set is a menu, even if it is specified concisely as an intersection of a small number of half-spaces. In particular, we show that this problem is hard even when the convex sets are restricted to be of a certain simple class associated with optimal commitments. Finally, in Appendix
B.3
, we show how to “invert” these optimal menus to derive optimal algorithms.

Our results also hold for some generalizations of these problems with minimal modification. We discuss two orthogonal generalizations below.

•

The optimizer’s type also affects the learner’s payoff. In particular, the learner now has
k
payoff functions
\{u_{L,i}\}_{i\in[k]}
and receives payoff
u_{L,i}(a,b)
when playing action
a
against the optimizer type
i
’s action
b
.

•

The objective function of interest is not the expected payoff of the learner (over draws of the optimizer), but the minimum payoff of the learner against any possible optimizer type. More generally, any objective function that is a concave function of the CSP can be maximized.

Both generalizations only entail a small change in the objective functions used optimization subroutines in our algorithms for the no-regret and general menu commitment problem. We discuss the precise modifications in the appropriate sections.

3
Optimal No-Regret Commitment

We begin by discussing the problem of
no-regret commitment
: the problem of finding the optimal no-regret menu
\mathcal{M}
(and hence the optimal no-regret algorithm) for the learner to commit to that maximizes the expected utility of the learner. Interestingly, we will show that it is possible to solve this problem exactly and efficiently.

Recall that we assume that the optimizer is one of
k
different types, drawn from a known distribution
\mathcal{D}
where type
i
has probability
\alpha_{i}
of occurring and corresponds to the payoff function
u_{O,i}
. Under the optimal menu
\mathcal{M}
, each of these different optimizers will pick some CSP in
\mathcal{M}
. We call the
k
-tuple of CSPs
\Phi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k})\in\Delta_{mn}^{k}
a valid
CSP assignment
of this menu
\mathcal{M}
if
\varphi_{i}\in\arg\max_{\varphi}u_{O,i}(\varphi)
for all
i
. A given menu may have multiple valid CSP assignments due to ties. To resolve this, we assume that the optimizer breaks ties in favor of the learner ( any further tie-breaking rules does not affect the learner’s utility). However, we do not explicitly enforce this constraint; instead, by searching over all valid assignments, we inherently identify the optimal one from the perspective of the learner’s utility.

Note that the eventual expected utility of a learner who has committed to the menu
\mathcal{M}
depends only on the CSP assignment of
\mathcal{M}
; in fact, this expected utility is simply
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}u_{L}(\varphi_{i})
. This motivates the following observation: instead of directly optimizing over all valid no-regret menus (which while simpler than the underlying learning algorithms, are still an infinite dimensional family of convex sets), we can optimize over the set of viable CSP assignments (which are
kmn
dimensional objects) that correspond to valid no-regret menus. This has a similar flavor to the standard revelation principle in mechanism design, where instead of optimizing directly over the set of all mechanisms, it is often easier to optimize over the space of assignments of outcomes to players (with the caveat that such outcomes must satisfy some incentive compatibility guarantees).

To make use of this observation, we need a way to characterize which CSP assignments are in fact viable. Luckily, we can make use of the structure of no-regret menus – specifically, the fact that all no-regret menus must contain the no-swap-regret menu – to establish the following concrete characterization of such CSP assignments.

Lemma 3.1
.

The CSP assignment
\Phi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k})
corresponds to a valid no-regret menu
\mathcal{M}
iff the following conditions hold:

1.

For all
i\in[k]
,
\varphi_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{NR}
(every optimizer is assigned a no-regret CSP).

2.

For all
i,i^{\prime}\in[k]
,
u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i})\geq u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i^{\prime}})
(the optimizer of type
i
prefers their CSP to the CSP assigned to any other type
i^{\prime}
).

3.

For all
i\in[k]
and no-swap-regret CSPs
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
,
u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i})\geq u_{O,i}(\varphi)
(the optimizer of type
i
prefers their CSP to every no-swap-regret CSP).

Proof.

Note that if
\mathcal{M}
is a no-regret menu, then by Lemma
2.5
,
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}\subseteq\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{NR}
. If
(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k})
is the CSP assignment corresponding to this menu, then since
\varphi_{i}\in\arg\max_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}}u_{O,i}(\varphi)
, this immediately implies the conditions above (in particular, both
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
and
\varphi_{i^{\prime}}
belong to
\mathcal{M}
).

To prove the other direction, consider fix a CSP assignment
\Phi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k})
satisfying the above conditions, and consider the menu
\mathcal{M}=\conv(\mathcal{M}_{NSR}\cup\{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k}\})
. Since menus are upwards closed under inclusion, this is a valid menu, and since each
\varphi_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{NR}
, this is a no-regret menu (
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{NR}
). The two latter constraints then imply that
\varphi_{i}\in\arg\max_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}}u_{O,i}(\varphi)
, and thus
\Phi
is the corresponding CSP assignment to
\mathcal{M}
.
∎

With a little more work, Lemma
3.1
allows us to write the set of valid no-regret CSP assignments as an explicit polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{kmn}
. For each type
i
, let
v_{i}=\max_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}_{NSR}}u_{O,i}(\varphi)
be the utility of the optimizer of type
i
under their favorite no-swap-regret CSP. Note that since the extreme points of the no-swap-regret menu are Stackelberg CSPs (Lemma
2.6
), this is equivalently the Stackelberg leader value of the optimizer of type
i
and therefore can be computed efficiently. We can now write down the following linear program in the variables
\varphi_{i}
to compute the optimal CSP assignment.

\displaystyle\max\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}u_{L}(\varphi_{i})

s.t.

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{NR},\quad\forall i

\displaystyle u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i})\geq u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i^{\prime}}),\quad\forall i,i^{\prime}\in[k]

\displaystyle u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i})\geq v_{i},\quad\forall i\in[k]

The constraints in the LP (
) correspond exactly to the three constraints in Lemma
3.1
, and therefore also characterizes the set of all valid CSP assignments and computes the optimal no-regret CSP assignment. We summarize this process in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

1:

Input

i\in[k]
, compute the Stackelberg leader value

(this can be done in time

per type by Theorem

).

.

Algorithm

\mathcal{M}
to commit to for a learner with payoff
u_{L}
against a distribution
\mathcal{D}
of optimizers. This algorithm runs in time polynomial in the size of the game and the support of the distribution
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

Since
v_{i}=\max_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}_{NSR}}u_{O,i}(\varphi)
, the three constraints in the LP (

, any solution
\Phi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k})
to the LP (

\mathcal{M}
(in particular, the
\mathcal{M}
output by Algorithm

). Since the expected utility of a learner using this menu is precisely
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}u_{L}(\varphi_{i})
, the CSP assignment returned by the LP (
) is the CSP assignment of the no-regret menu that optimizes this expected utility. Finally, the algorithm runs in polynomial time – each computation of the Stackelberg value
v_{i}
takes polynomial time, and the final LP has
mnk
variables and
O(k^{2}+kn)
constraints (verifying that
\varphi_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{NR}
can be done by checking that the regret with respect to each of the
n

∎

We conclude this section with several remarks on how Theorem
3.2
can be generalized to other settings. First, note that we can replace the expected utility objective in the LP (
) with any concave function of the variables
\varphi_{i}
and still efficiently solve the resulting program. This lets us capture the generalizations mentioned at the end of Section
2.5
. Namely, if we want to e.g. maximize the worst-case utility against any of the optimizers, we can replace the current objective with the concave function
\min_{i\in[k]}u_{L}(\varphi_{i})
. Similarly, if the type
i
affects the learner’s utility, we can instead consider the linear objective
\sum_{i}u_{L,i}(\varphi_{i})
.

Secondly, the crucial structural property we used about no-regret menus is that every such menu
\mathcal{M}
both contains the valid menu
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
and is contained in the valid menu
\mathcal{M}_{NR}
. The procedure in Algorithm
1
can be adjusted to optimize over any “sandwich set” of menus; i.e., all menus
\mathcal{M}
satisfying
\mathcal{M}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{2}
for given valid menus
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
. One main difficulty in solving the general menu commitment problem is that the set of all valid menus is
not
of this form.

Finally, it is natural to wonder if this procedure is necessary at all, or whether the no-swap-regret menu
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
(the most restrictive of all no-regret menus) always leads to the best outcome for the learner. The following theorem (whose proof is deferred to Appendix
A
) shows that there are cases where no-swap-regret algorithms are strictly sub-optimal.

Theorem 3.3
.

There exists learner utility
u_{L}
and a distribution over optimizer types
\mathcal{D}
(with support
k=1
) such that the menu output by Algorithm
1
is strictly better than the no-swap regret menu
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
.

4
Optimal General Commitment

We now lift the constraint that the menus we consider must correspond to no-regret algorithms, and study the problem of general menu commitment. This will turn out to be a considerably harder problem than the no-regret commitment problem studied in the previous section, and we will no longer be able to establish an efficient algorithm that exactly optimizes over the space of all possible menus. Our ultimate goal in this section is to establish the following theorem, which shows that whenever
either
the size
mn
of the game or the size
k
of the support of the optimizer distribution
\mathcal{D}
is constant, then there exists a polynomial-time algorithm for computing an
{\varepsilon}
-approximate optimal menu.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
d=\min(mn,k)
. There exists an algorithm that runs in time

\displaystyle\left(\frac{mnk}{{\varepsilon}}\right)^{O(d)}

and constructs an
{\varepsilon}
-approximate optimal menu
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}
. Moreover, this menu has an efficient membership oracle (running in time
\poly(m,n,k)
) and therefore corresponds to a learning algorithm with a polynomial per-round time complexity.

Our high-level strategy will be similar to the one employed previously for the problem of no-regret commitment. As in that case, instead of thinking directly about the problem of menu design, we will attempt to design the optimal CSP assignment
\Phi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k})
that corresponds to a valid menu. The main difference between these two settings is that we will lose the structural property present for no-regret menus that any valid no-regret menu must contain
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
as a sub-menu. In particular, we will not be able to always construct our optimal menu by taking the convex hull of our “fallback” menu
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
with the CSPs in our assignment
\Phi
. Instead, for any CSP assignment
\Phi
, we will have to determine whether there exists a viable fallback menu
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
that can take the place of
\mathcal{M}_{NSR}
in this construction.

To formalize this, given any CSP assignment
\Phi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k})
, we define its
candidate menu

\mathcal{C}(\Phi):=\{\varphi\in\Delta^{mn}\,\mid\,u_{O,i}(\varphi)\leq u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i})\,\forall i\in[k]\}
to be the set of CSPs
\varphi
where the optimizer of type
i
prefers their assigned CSP
\varphi_{i}
to
\varphi
for all types
i
. Intuitively, this is the largest possible “fallback menu” we could offer where each optimizer would select their assigned CSP over any CSP in this set (with the important caveat that this set may not in fact be a valid menu). In particular, we can characterize the set of valid CSP assignments
\Phi
in the following manner.

Lemma 4.2
.

The CSP assignment
\Phi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k})
corresponds to a valid menu
\mathcal{M}
iff the following conditions hold:

1.

For each
i\in[k]
,
\varphi_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
.

2.

The set
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
is a valid menu.

If these two conditions hold, then
\Phi
is the CSP assignment for the menu
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
.

Proof.

First, note that if both conditions hold, then by the definition of
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
the optimizer of type
i
will prefer
\varphi_{i}
to any other CSP in the menu
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
, and therefore
\Phi
is the CSP assignment for the valid menu
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
.

On the other hand, if
\Phi
is the CSP assignment for some valid menu
\mathcal{M}
, note that we must have
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
(any
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
would be better for one of the optimizers than their assigned CSP, by the construction of
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
). It follows that
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
is a valid menu, since menus are upwards closed under inclusion (Corollary
2.2
). Moreover, in any CSP assignment we must have
u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i})\geq u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i^{\prime}})
for any
i,i^{\prime}\in[k]
, and therefore each
\varphi_{i}
is guaranteed to belong to
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
.
∎

We again wish to use this characterization to optimize over the space of valid CSP assignments. To this end, let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\Delta_{mn}^{k}
be the set of all CSP assignments that correspond to a valid menu. We will use the characterization in Lemma
4.2
to write
\mathcal{P}
as the intersection of the following two convex sets. First, we have the
incentive compatible set

\mathcal{R}
containing all CSP assignments satisfying the first condition of Lemma
4.2
, namely,

\mathcal{R}:=\{\Phi\in\Delta^{mnk}\mid u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i})\geq u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i^{\prime}})\,\forall i,i^{\prime}\in[k]\}.

Second, we have the
valid candidate set

\mathcal{S}
containing all CSP assignments satisfying the second condition of Lemma
4.2
, namely,

\mathcal{S}:=\{\Phi\in\Delta_{mn}^{k}\mid\mathcal{C}(\Phi)\text{ is a valid menu}\}

Note that the description of
\mathcal{R}
above implies that it is a convex polytope. Although less obvious, it can also be shown that the set
\mathcal{S}
is convex.

Lemma 4.3
.

The set
\mathcal{S}
is a convex subset of
\Delta_{mn}^{k}
.

Proof.

Recall (by Theorem
2.1
) that
\mathcal{M}
is a valid menu iff for every
y\in\Delta_{n}
, there exists an
x\in\Delta_{m}
such that
x\otimes y\in\mathcal{M}
. Let
\Phi_{1}=(\varphi_{1,1},\varphi_{1,2},\dots,\varphi_{1,k})
and
\Phi_{2}=(\varphi_{2,1},\varphi_{2,2},\dots,\varphi_{2,k})
be two elements of
\mathcal{S}
. We will show for any
\Phi^{\prime}=\lambda\Phi_{1}+(1-\lambda)\Phi_{2}
(where
\lambda\in[0,1]
) that
\mathcal{C}(\Phi^{\prime})
is a valid menu and thus that
\Phi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
.

Fix any
y\in\Delta_{n}
. It suffices to show that there exists an
x\in\Delta_{m}
such that
x\otimes y\in\mathcal{C}(\Phi^{\prime})
. Since
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
belong to
\mathcal{S}
, there exist elements
x_{1},x_{2}\in\Delta_{m}
such that
x_{1}\otimes y\in\mathcal{C}(\Phi_{1})
and
x_{2}\otimes y\in\mathcal{C}(\Phi_{2})
. In particular, we have that
u_{O,i}(x_{1},y)\leq u_{O,i}(\varphi_{1,i})
and
u_{O,i}(x_{2},y)\leq u_{O,i}(\varphi_{2,i})
for all
i\in[k]
. But since
u_{O,i}
is bilinear, this implies that
u_{O,i}(\lambda x_{1}+(1-\lambda)x_{2},y)\leq\lambda u_{O,i}(\varphi_{1,i})+(1-\lambda)u_{O,i}(\varphi_{2,i})=u_{O,i}(\lambda\varphi_{1,i}+(1-\lambda)\varphi_{2,i})=u_{O,i}(\varphi^{\prime}_{i})
. Therefore
x^{\prime}=\lambda x_{1}+(1-\lambda)x_{2}
has the property that
x^{\prime}\otimes y\in\mathcal{C}(\Phi^{\prime})
, and therefore
\Phi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
.
∎

From Lemma
4.2
, it immediately follows that
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{S}
. Note furthermore that we have an explicit (and efficiently checkable) description of
\mathcal{R}
as a convex polytope with a polynomial number of facets. If we had a similar description for
\mathcal{S}
, we would be able to efficiently optimize over all of
\mathcal{P}
and solve the general menu commitment problem. Unfortunately, no such succinct description exists for
\mathcal{S}
in general (as we will later show, it is NP-hard to even decide membership in
\mathcal{S}
). Instead, we will show how to construct an approximate separation oracle for
\mathcal{S}
that runs in time exponential in
\min(mn,k)
, but polynomial in all other parameters. This oracle will eventually allow us to algorithmically construct an
\epsilon
-approximate optimal menu.

To formally establish this, we introduce the following relaxations of the above definitions. For a CSP assignment
\Phi
, let
\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
denote the
{\varepsilon}
-approximate candidate menu defined via
\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon}):=\{\varphi\in\Delta^{mn}\,\mid\,u_{O,i}(\varphi)\leq u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i})+{\varepsilon}\,\forall i\in[k]\}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
is a valid menu with the property that
\varphi_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
for all
i\in[k]
, then

V_{L}(\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon}),\mathcal{D},{\varepsilon})\geq V_{L}(\Phi).

Proof.

Recall that
V_{L}(\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon}),\mathcal{D},{\varepsilon})=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}V_{L}(\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon}),u_{O,i},{\varepsilon})
. It therefore suffices to show that
V_{L}(\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon}),u_{O,i},{\varepsilon})\geq u_{L}(\varphi_{i})
. But this directly follows from the definitions of
\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
and
V_{L}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{D},{\varepsilon})
; in particular,
\varphi_{i}
has the property that
u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i})\geq\max_{\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})}u_{O,i}(\varphi^{\prime})-{\varepsilon}
and so it is a candidate CSP in the computation of
V_{L}(\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon}),u_{O,i},{\varepsilon})
.
∎

We likewise define the relaxed candidate set
\mathcal{S}({\varepsilon})
via

\mathcal{S}({\varepsilon}):=\{\Phi\in\Delta_{mn}^{k}\mid\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})\text{ is a valid menu}\}

We will work with
approximate separation oracles
for
\mathcal{S}
of the following form: given as input a CSP assignment
\Phi\in\Delta_{mn}^{k}
and a precision parameter
\delta
, the oracle either returns that
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}(\delta)
or returns a hyperplane separating
\Phi
from
\mathcal{S}
. This can be thought of as analogous to the standard definition of a weak separation oracle for the set
\mathcal{S}
(which either returns that
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}^{\delta}
or a separating hyperplane).

Below, we show that such an oracle for
\mathcal{S}
allows us to compute an
{\varepsilon}
-approximate optimal menu (thus letting us eventually establish Theorem
4.1
). Here we make one additional technical assumption (common in convex optimization, and necessary for execution of cutting-plane methods such as the ellipsoid algorithm): we assume that
\mathcal{P}
contains a small ball of radius
r_{\min}
with
\log(1/r_{\min})=\poly(m,n,\log(1/{\varepsilon}))
. Note that since we are only concerned with finding approximately optimal menus, this assumption is essentially true without loss of generality, since we can expand
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{S}
by
{\varepsilon}/100
(thus guaranteeing
r_{\min}=\Omega({\varepsilon})
) without significantly changing the approximation guarantee; nonetheless, for ease of exposition we will proceed under the assumption that this ball exists.

Lemma 4.5
.

Assume we have access to an approximate separation oracle for the set
\mathcal{S}
(as defined above). Then there exists an algorithm that constructs an
{\varepsilon}
-approximate optimal menu
\mathcal{M}^{{\varepsilon}}
that makes at most
\poly(m,n,\log(1/{\varepsilon}))
oracle calls with precision
{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

Note that by additionally checking the constraints associated with the set
\mathcal{R}
, we can construct an approximate separation oracle that either returns that
\Phi\in\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{S}({\varepsilon})
or a hyperplane separating
\Phi
from
\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{P}
. We can use this primitive in combination with standard cutting-plane methods (see
[
27
]
) to find a CSP assignment
\Phi^{*}\in\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{S}({\varepsilon})
with the property that
V_{L}(\Phi^{*})\geq\max_{\Phi\in\mathcal{P}}V_{L}(\Phi)-{\varepsilon}
. This procedure makes at most
\poly(m,n,\log(1/{\varepsilon}),\log(1/r_{\min}))=\poly(m,n,\log(1/{\varepsilon}))
calls to this oracle.

We claim that the menu
\mathcal{M}^{{\varepsilon}}=\mathcal{C}(\Phi^{*},{\varepsilon})
is an
{\varepsilon}
-approximate menu. To see this, note that by Lemma
4.4
we have that
V_{L}(\mathcal{M}^{{\varepsilon}},\mathcal{D},{\varepsilon})\geq V_{L}(\Phi^{*})
. By the previous guarantee, this is at least
\max_{\Phi\in\mathcal{P}}V_{L}(\Phi)-{\varepsilon}=\max_{\text{$\mathcal{M}$ is a valid menu}}V_{L}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{D})-{\varepsilon}
, and therefore
\mathcal{M}^{{\varepsilon}}
is
{\varepsilon}
-optimal.
∎

4.1
Approximate oracles for the valid candidate set

We now turn our attention to constructing explicit approximate separation oracles for the valid candidate set
\mathcal{S}
. In combination with Lemma
4.5
, this will let us construct explicit algorithms for producing approximately optimal menus for the general menu commitment problem.

In this section we will demonstrate two different constructions of such an oracle: one that runs in polynomial time when the size
mn
of the game is constant, and one that runs in polynomial time when the size
k
of the support of the distribution
\mathcal{D}
is constant. Both constructions will reduce the problem of determining whether a given CSP assignment corresponds to a valid menu to the problem of deciding whether a set is approachable in a specific instance of Blackwell approachability. We therefore begin by reviewing the relevant tools we need from the theory of Blackwell approachability.

The core object of study in Blackwell approachability is a
vector-valued game
: a game where two players take actions in some convex set and receive a “payoff” in the form of a bilinear vector-valued function of their two actions. In all of our applications of Blackwell approachability, we will continue to consider games between a learner and an optimizer where the learner plays actions
x\in\Delta_{m}
and the optimizer plays actions
y\in\Delta_{n}
. However, instead of considering the specific (single-dimensional) payoff functions
u_{L}
and
u_{O,i}
, we will consider an arbitrary bilinear
d
-dimensional vector-valued
payoff function
u:\Delta_{m}\times\Delta_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The goal of Blackwell approachability is to characterize which
d
-dimensional sets
\mathcal{M}
the learner can force the time-averaged value of
u(x_{t},y_{t})
to approach. The main theorem of Blackwell approachability shows that these sets are precisely the
response-satisfiable
sets.

Definition 4.6

(Response-Satisfiability)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a closed convex set, and let
u:\Delta_{m}\times\Delta_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a vector-valued payoff function. The set
\mathcal{M}
is said to be
response-satisfiable
with respect to
u
if for every mixed strategy
y\in\Delta_{m}
of the optimizer, there exists a mixed strategy
x\in\Delta_{n}
of the learner such that

u(x,y)\in\mathcal{M}.

Theorem 4.7

(Blackwell Approachability Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a closed convex set, and let
u:\Delta_{m}\times\Delta_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bilinear vector-valued payoff function. If
\mathcal{M}
is response-satisfiable with respect to
u
, then there exists a learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for the learner such that, for any sequence of strategies
y_{1},y_{2},\dotsc
chosen by the optimizer, the sequence of the learner’s strategies
x_{1},x_{2},\dotsc
produced by
\mathcal{A}
satisfies

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}d\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}u(x_{t},y_{t}),\mathcal{M}\right)=0,

where
d\left(\mathbf{v},\mathcal{M}\right)=\inf_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}}\|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{m}\|
denotes the Euclidean distance from vector
\mathbf{v}
to the set
\mathcal{M}
.

Blackwell approachability is closely related to the validity of menus in the following sense. Consider the instance of Blackwell approachability where
d=mn
and
u(x,y)=x\otimes y
(i.e., the single round CSP where the learner plays
x
and the optimizer plays
y
). Then a set
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\Delta_{mn}
is a valid asymptotic menu iff it is approachable in this instance of Blackwell approachability (indeed, this is just a restatement of Theorems
2.1
and
4.7
). In particular, to decide whether a CSP assignment
\Phi
belongs to
\mathcal{S}
, it suffices to decide whether the set
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
is approachable.

Our main tool to decide whether a given set is approachable is the following theorem of
[
29
]
, which runs in time exponential in
d
(but polynomial in all other parameters) and approximately decides whether a set is approachable.

Theorem 4.8

(
[
29
]
)
.

There is an algorithm which, given a candidate menu
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\Delta_{mn}
(as an explicit polytope with a polynomial number of defining faces) and a precision parameter
\delta
, runs in time
(mnk/\delta)^{O(d)}
and correctly either:

•

Outputs that the set
\mathcal{M}^{\delta}
is approachable

•

Outputs that
\mathcal{M}
is not approachable, along with a
y\in\Delta_{n}
certifying that
\mathcal{M}
is not response satisfiable (i.e., a
y
s.t.
u(x,y)\not\in\mathcal{M}
for all
x\in\Delta_{n}
).

To use Theorem
4.8
to construct our approximate separation oracle, we will need two auxiliary lemmas. First, we will need to relate the property of (some expansion of)
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
being approachable to the property of
\Phi
belonging to
\mathcal{S}({\varepsilon})
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\Phi
be a CSP assignment. If
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)^{{\varepsilon}}
is a valid menu, then
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}({\varepsilon}\sqrt{mn})
.

Proof.

Note that
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)^{{\varepsilon}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon}\sqrt{mn})
, since if
\|\varphi-\varphi^{\prime}\|\leq{\varepsilon}
, it must be the case that
|u_{O,i}(\varphi)-u_{O,i}(\varphi^{\prime})|\leq\sqrt{mn}
(since all coefficients of
u_{O,i}
are bounded in
[-1,1]
). Since menus are upwards closed under inclusion, this means that
\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon}\sqrt{mn})
is a valid menu, and therefore
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}({\varepsilon}\sqrt{mn})
.
∎

Secondly, we will show that a certificate of non-response-satisfiability can be used to construct a separating hyperplane between
\Phi
and
\mathcal{S}
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Assume we are given a CSP assignment
\Phi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k})
and a
y\in\Delta_{n}
such that
x\otimes y\not\in\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
for any
x\in\Delta_{m}
. Then it is possible (in
\poly(m,n,k)
time) to construct a
kmn
-dimensional hyperplane separating
\Phi
from
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\text{sep}}
denote the convex set
\Delta_{m}\otimes y
. By assumption, we have that
\mathcal{C}_{\text{sep}}
and
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
are disjoint convex sets. By the theory of LP duality, there must be a hyperplane separating
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
from
\mathcal{C}_{\text{sep}}
that can be written as a linear combination of the defining halfspaces of
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
. Since
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
is defined by halfspaces of the form
u_{O,i}(\varphi)\leq u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i})
, this means that there is a vector
h\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{k}
such that the inequality

\sum_{i=1}^{k}h_{i}u_{O,i}(\varphi)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}h_{i}u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i})

(5)

holds for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
and is violated for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{sep}}
.

Now consider an arbitrary CSP assignment
\Phi^{\prime}=(\varphi^{\prime}_{1},\varphi^{\prime}_{2},\dots,\varphi^{\prime}_{k})\in\Delta_{mn}^{k}
. Consider the half-space constraint (in
kmn
-dimensional space) implied by the inequality

\sum_{i=1}^{k}h_{i}u_{O,i}(\varphi^{\prime}_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}h_{i}u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i})

(6)

Note that
\Phi
clearly satisfies this constraint. On the other hand, every
\Phi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
must have the property that
\mathcal{C}(\Phi^{\prime})
contains an element of the form
x\otimes y
(for some
x
), and thus must have non-empty intersection with
\mathcal{C}_{\text{sep}}
. But any CSP
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}(\Phi^{\prime})
must satisfy

\sum_{i=1}^{k}h_{i}u_{O,i}(\varphi)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}h_{i}u_{O,i}(\varphi^{\prime}_{i})

(7)

and therefore (by (
6
)) also satisfies inequality (
5
), and thus cannot lie in
\mathcal{C}_{\text{sep}}
. It follows that the hyperplane given by (
6
) is a hyperplane separating
\Phi
from
\mathcal{S}
.
∎

We are now ready to construct our first separation oracle (that is efficient for small values of
mn
).

Theorem 4.11
.

There exists an algorithm that takes as input a CSP assignment
\Phi
and a precision parameter
\delta
, runs in time
(mnk/\delta)^{O(mn)}
and either outputs that
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}(\delta)
or returns a hyperplane separating
\Phi
from
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

We run the algorithm of
[
29
]
(Theorem
4.8
) on the set
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
with precision
\delta^{\prime}=\delta/\sqrt{mn}
. If this algorithm returns that
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)^{\delta^{\prime}}
is approachable, then by Lemma
4.9
, we are guaranteed that
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}(\delta)
, so we return that. On the other hand, if this algorithm returns a
y\in\Delta_{n}
certifying that
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
is not response-satisfiable, we follow the procedure in Lemma
4.10
to construct a hyperplane separating
\Phi
from
\mathcal{S}
and return that. The time of this procedure is dominated by the
(mnk/\delta)^{O(mn)}
time it takes to run the procedure of
[
29
]
.
∎

4.1.1
Small support

To handle the case when
k
is small, we consider a slightly different reduction to the problem of Blackwell approachability with the
k
-dimensional bilinear payoff function
u:\Delta_{m}\times\Delta_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{k}
defined via
u(x,y)=(u_{O,1}(x,y),u_{O,2}(x,y),\dots,u_{O,k}(x,y))
. The key observation is that for any CSP
\varphi\in\Delta_{mn}
the
k
values in
u(\varphi)
are sufficient for determining whether
\varphi
belongs to the candidate set
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
for a given CSP assignment
\Phi
. In particular, for any CSP assignment
\Phi
, define the
candidate utility set

\mathcal{U}(\Phi)
via

\mathcal{U}(\Phi):=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\mid u_{i}\leq u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i})\,\forall i\in[k]\}.

Likewise, define the relaxed candidate utility set
\mathcal{U}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
via

\mathcal{U}(\Phi):=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\mid u_{i}\leq u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i})+{\varepsilon}\,\forall i\in[k]\}.

The following lemma formally establishes the above analogy between the sets
\mathcal{U}
and the menus
\mathcal{C}
.

Lemma 4.12
.

Fix a CSP assignment
\Phi
, a CSP
\varphi
, and any
{\varepsilon}\geq 0
. Then
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
iff
u(\varphi)\in\mathcal{U}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
. Moreover,
\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
is a valid menu iff
\mathcal{U}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
is approachable.

Proof.

If
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
, then
u(\varphi)_{i}=u_{O,i}(\varphi)\leq u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i})+{\varepsilon}
, and therefore
u(\varphi)
satisfies all the constraints of
\mathcal{U}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
. Likewise, if
u(\varphi)\in\mathcal{U}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
, then
\varphi
satisfies all the constraints of
\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
.

Recall that
\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
is a valid menu iff it is response-satisfiable: for any
y\in\Delta_{n}
there exists an
x\in\Delta_{m}
such that
x\otimes y\in\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
. But
x\otimes y\in\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
iff
u(x,y)\in\mathcal{U}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
, and so it follows that
\mathcal{U}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
is response-satisfiable (and thus approachable) iff
\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
is.
∎

We additionally have the following analogue of Lemma
4.9
for expansions of
\mathcal{U}(\Phi)
.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
\Phi
be a CSP assignment. If
\mathcal{U}(\Phi)^{{\varepsilon}}
is an approachable set, then
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}({\varepsilon})
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\mathcal{U}(\Phi)^{{\varepsilon}}\subseteq\mathcal{U}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
, since if
\mathcal{U}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
is approachable then
\mathcal{C}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
is a valid menu, and therefore
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}({\varepsilon})
. Let
u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}(\Phi)^{{\varepsilon}}
and
u\in\mathcal{U}(\Phi)
be so that
\|u-u^{\prime}\|\leq{\varepsilon}
. But then
|u_{i}-u^{\prime}_{i}|\leq{\varepsilon}
for any coordinate
i\in[k]
, and it follows that
u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}(\Phi,{\varepsilon})
.
∎

We can now construct our second separation oracle.

Theorem 4.14
.

There exists an algorithm that takes as input a CSP assignment
\Phi
and a precision parameter
\delta
, runs in time
(mnk/\delta)^{O(k)}
and either outputs that
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}(\delta)
or returns a hyperplane separating
\Phi
from
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

Similar to the proof of Theorem
4.11
, we run the algorithm of
[
29
]
(Theorem
4.8
) on the set
\mathcal{U}(\Phi)
with precision
\delta
. If this algorithm returns that
\mathcal{U}(\Phi)^{\delta}
is approachable, then by Lemma
4.13
, we are guaranteed that
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}(\delta)
, so we return that. On the other hand, if this algorithm returns a
y\in\Delta_{n}
certifying that
\mathcal{U}(\Phi)
is not response-satisfiable, then
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
is likewise not response-satisfiable (by Lemma
4.12
), and we can again follow the procedure in Lemma
4.10
to construct a hyperplane separating
\Phi
from
\mathcal{S}
and return that. The time of this procedure is dominated by the
(mnk/\delta)^{O(k)}
time it takes to run the procedure of
[
29
]
.
∎

Finally, by combining Lemma
4.5
with the better of these two separation oracles, we can prove Theorem
4.1
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

If
mn<d
, run the reduction of Lemma
4.5
with the separation oracle defined in Theorem
4.11
. Otherwise, run the same reduction but with the separation oracle defined in Theorem
4.14
. The total time complexity is at most
(mnk/{\varepsilon})^{O(\min(mn,k))}
, as desired.
∎

4.2
Hardness of testing the validity of menus

While our algorithm finds an optimal commitment efficiently when either
k
or
mn
is small, our procedure does not run in time polynomial in the number of actions and the number of types simultaneously. In this section we show that our algorithmic approach would require significant alterations to do so by demonstrating that it is in general hard to decide whether a given CSP assignment can correspond to a valid menu (i.e., decide membership in
\mathcal{S}
).

Theorem 4.15
.

The problem of deciding whether
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
is a valid menu (given as input a CSP assignment
\Phi\in\Delta_{mn}^{k}
and a collection of optimizer payoffs
u_{O,i}
) is NP-hard.

The proof follows from a similar hardness result in
[
29
]
about the hardness of testing approachability of the non-negative orthant. We state this result below.

Theorem 4.16

(
[
29
]
)
.

The problem of taking as input a vector-valued function
u:\Delta_{m}\times\Delta_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and deciding whether the non-negative orthant
[0,\infty)^{d}
is approachable is NP-hard.

To prove Theorem
4.15
, we will demonstrate how to reduce the approachability problem in Theorem
4.16
to an instance of our problem of determining whether a menu is valid. The proof is deferred to Appendix
A
.

4.3
A Polynomial Time Algorithm for the Maximin Objective

In the previous section, we showed that it is computationally hard to determine whether a given menu is valid. If even checking whether a single menu is hard, this may suggest that it is hard to design a learning algorithm that implements the optimal menu.

Somewhat counterintuitively, in this section we will show that this is possible, at least in certain settings. In particular, we will design learning algorithms (without any additional constraints) that are nearly optimal for maximizing the learner’s
maximin utility
; the worst-case utility they might receive against any type of opponent.

For any
u_{L}
and set of
k
optimizers
\{u_{O,i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, let
\textsf{OPT}=\sup_{\mathcal{M}}\min_{i\in[k]}V_{L}(\mathcal{M},u_{O,i})
denote the best asymptotic per-round maximin utility a learner can achieve against this set of optimizers (where the supremum is over all valid menus
\mathcal{M}
). We have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.17
.

For any fixed
{\varepsilon}>0
, there exists a learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{{\varepsilon}}
(Algorithm
2
) that guarantees a per-round maximin utility of

\min_{i\in[k]}V_{L}(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}_{{\varepsilon}}),u_{O,i})\geq\textsf{OPT}-{\varepsilon}

and runs in time
\poly(n,m,k)
per iteration.

The primary insight behind the design of the algorithm in Theorem
4.17
is that even if we do not know whether a specific menu is approachable (in the sense of Blackwell approachability,
4.7
), we can still attempt to run an algorithmic instantiation of the Blackwell approachability theorem to attempt to approach this set.

What happens when we do try to approach an unapproachable set
\mathcal{M}
? Most algorithms for Blackwell approachability do not immediately fail when run on such sets (indeed, if they did, that would contradict the hardness result of
[
29
]
). Instead, they will approach
\mathcal{M}
for some time until at some round (depending on the behavior of the adversary) they eventually fail to play an action, certifying that the set
\mathcal{M}
is unapproachable.

We demonstrate this for the specific application of Blackwell Approachability we need in Theorem
4.17
. Consider the approachability game defined earlier where
u:\Delta_{m}\times\Delta_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{mn}
is defined via
u(x,y)=x\otimes y
, and let
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
be the candidate menu for an arbitrary incentive-compatible CSP assignment
\Phi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k})\in\mathcal{R}
. We will try to force
u
to approach
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
. Note that while we assume
\Phi
is incentive-compatible, it is not necessarily the case that
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}
, and so
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
is not necessarily response satisfiable with respect to
u
.

We say that a learning algorithm is
abortable
if the learning algorithm has the option to terminate its own execution after a finite number of rounds (i.e., outputting an
Abort
signal instead of playing an action)
3
3

3

We include a formal definition of what this means (and how this affects the definition of menus) in Appendix
B.4
.
. The following theorem provides guarantees on an abortable learning algorithm for approaching the set
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
for any incentive-compatible
\Phi
(in particular, note that the quantity on the LHS of (
8
) can be thought of as the maximum extent to which the CSP
\varphi=(1/T)\cdot(\sum_{t}x_{t}\otimes y_{t})
violates the constraints of
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
).

Theorem 4.18
.

For any incentive-compatible CSP assignment
\Phi\in\mathcal{R}
, there is an efficient abortable learning algorithm
\textsc{BlackwellAbort}(\Phi)
with the following properties.

•

If at round
\tau
,
\textsc{BlackwellAbort}(\Phi)
has not stopped, then its transcript of play up to this round satisfies:

\max_{i\in[k]}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}(u_{O,i}(x_{t},y_{t})-u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i}))\right)=O(\sqrt{\tau\log k}).

(8)

In particular, the menu
\mathcal{M}(\textsc{BlackwellAbort}(\Phi))
is contained within the set
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
.

•

If the learning algorithm
\textsc{BlackwellAbort}(\Phi)
ever aborts, then the set
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
is not response-satisfiable with respect to
u
.

•

Each
\varphi_{i}\in\Phi
is contained in the menu
\mathcal{M}(\textsc{BlackwellAbort}(\Phi))
; i.e., there is a sequence of opponent actions against which this algorithm will never abort and where the CSP will converge to
\varphi_{i}
.

Proof.

Consider the following learning algorithm:

•

Initialize an instance of Anytime Hedge
[
17
]
with
k
actions (this algorithm has the guarantee that the external regret at round
t
is at most
O(\sqrt{t\log k})
for all
t\geq 1
).

•

For each round
t
(for
t\geq 1
), do the following:

–

Receive the action
p^{t}\in\Delta_{k}
from the instance of Anytime Hedge.

–

Choose an
x_{t}\in\Delta_{m}
s.t.

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p^{t}_{i}(u_{O,i}(x_{t},y)-u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i}))\leq 0

for all
y\in\Delta_{n}
. We can find such an
x_{t}
by solving a linear program; if no such
x_{t}
exists, abort.

–

Play this
x_{t}
and observe the opponents action
y_{t}\in\Delta_{n}

–

Feed Hedge the reward vector
r^{t}\in[-1,1]^{k}
defined via
r^{t}_{i}=u_{O,i}(x_{t},y_{t})-u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i})
.

We claim that this learning algorithm has the desired guarantees. To see this, first note that if the above algorithm ever aborts, by von Neumann’s minimax theorem, it must be the case that there exists a
y^{*}\in\Delta_{n}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p^{t}_{i}(u_{O,i}(x,y^{*})-u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i}))>0

for all
x\in\Delta_{m}
. But now,
y^{*}
is a certificate that
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
is not response-satisfiable with respect to
u
(i.e., there is no
x
such that
u(x,y^{*})=x\otimes y^{*}
satisfies all the constraints of
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)
).

On the other hand, note that if the algorithm has successfully run for
\tau
rounds, then

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[k]}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}(u_{O,i}(x_{t},y_{t})-u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i}))\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[k]}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}(u_{O,i}(x_{t},y_{t})-u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i}))\right)-\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\sum_{i=1}^{k}p^{t}_{i}(u_{O,i}(x,y^{*})-u_{O,i}(\varphi_{i}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[k]}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}r_{t,i}-\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\langle p^{t},r_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Reg(\mathrm{AnytimeHedge})=O(\sqrt{\tau\log k}).

Finally, this algorithm, as written, does not guarantee that
\varphi_{i}
belongs to its menu. However, we can easily modify this algorithm in the same way as Lemma 3.5 of
[
1
]
by adding a cheap talk phase (or implementing one through the details of the protocol) where we cooperate with the opponent to play a sequence of actions converging to the
\varphi_{i}
of their choice. See also the discussion in Section
B.3
for more details.
∎

Algorithm 2

Polynomial Time Learning Algorithm for Maximin Value

Optimal No-Regret Commitment

The optimal menu returned by our algorithm for optimal no-regret commitment always has the form

The optimal menu returned by our algorithm for optimal general commitment always has the form

(where here the norm is the
\ell_{2}
norm). In particular, letting
T_{\mathrm{long}}=\sum_{k\in S_{\mathrm{long}}}T_{k}
(and noting that
|1/T_{\mathrm{long}}-1/T|\leq T_{\mathrm{short}}/(TT_{\mathrm{long}})\leq KT^{-0.1}/T_{\mathrm{long}}
), this implies that

\left|\varphi-\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{long}}}\sum_{k\in S_{\mathrm{long}}}T_{k}\varphi_{k}\right|\leq 2KT^{-0.1}.

The CSP
\varphi
is therefore within distance
o(T)
of the CSP
\varphi^{\prime}=\sum_{k\in S_{\mathrm{long}}}(T_{k}/T_{\mathrm{long}})\varphi_{k}
. This itself is a convex combination of CSPs
\varphi_{k}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{B}_{K}^{T})
and therefore has the property that

\varphi^{\prime}\in\conv\left(\bigcup_{k\in S_{\mathrm{long}}}\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{B}_{k}^{T_{k}})\right),

where each
T_{k}\geq T^{0.9}
. As we take
T\rightarrow\infty
, the above set must therefore converge in Hausdorff distance to the convex hull of the sets
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{B}_{k})
for
k\in S_{\mathrm{long}}
. It follows that for sufficiently large
T
, any CSP
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}(A^{T})
must be arbitrarily close to some CSP in
\conv(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{B}_{1})\cup\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{B}_{2})\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{B}_{K}))
, establishing the theorem.
∎
