On the q q -factorization of power series
Robert Schneider

Abstract
Any power series with unit constant term can be factored into an infinite product of the form ∏ n ≥ 1 ( 1 − q n ) − a n \prod_{n\geq 1}(1-q^{n})^{-a_{n}} . We give direct formulas for the exponents a n a_{n} in terms of the coefficients of the power series, and vice versa, as sums over partitions. As examples, we prove identities for certain partition enumeration functions. Finally, we note q q -analogues of our enumeration formulas.



Keywords: Power series; infinite product; integer partitions

MSC codes: 30B10, 11P81, 40A20

In honor of George Andrews and Bruce Berndt for their 85th birthdays

1
Introduction and main results

Many major results in number theory, analysis, and combinatorics take the form of “a series equals a product”; in particular, the use of product–sum generating functions is a prevalent method in partition theory.

In
[
1
, p. 98, Ex. 2]
, George Andrews considers the factorization of an ordinary power series
with unit constant term into a
q
-product:

1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}r(n)q^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-q^{n})^{a_{n}}},

(1.1)

where the
r(n)
and
a_{n}
are real numbers.

Andrews suggests a recursion by which the
r(n)
can be calculated from a given sequence
a_{n}
,
namely

nr(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}r(n-j)\sum_{d\mid j}da_{d}.

(1.2)

The reverse problem, where the
r(n)
are given and the
a_{n}
are calculated recursively,
is given by David Bressoud
[
3
, p. 61, Ex. 2.3.10]
:

na_{n}=D_{n}-\underset{d<n}{\sum_{d\mid n}}da_{d},

(1.3)

where

D_{m}=mr(m)-\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}D_{j}r(m-j).

Bressoud
[
3
, Exs. 2.3.8 and 2.3.10 resp.]
provided
Mathematica
code for both (
1.2
) and (
1.3
).
A Maple implementation of (
1.3
) is provided by Frank Garvan
[
7
]
in his Maple
qseries.m
package as the

prodmake
procedure.
As Garvan’s
prodmake
is extremely useful in searching for Rogers–Ramanujan
type identities, Shashank Kanade and Matthew Russell used it extensively in their
own work
[
8
]
.

A natural question to ask, then, is:
can explicit (non-recursive) formulas be given, to express the sequences
a_{n}
and
r(n)
in terms of one another?
Below we present such explicit formulas in Proposition
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
.

First, let us recall basic definitions and notations associated with partitions.
A
partition

\lambda
of an integer
n
is a weakly decreasing finite
sequence of positive integers
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{\ell})
, where

\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{\ell}>0
, that sum to
n
.
Each
\lambda_{i}
is called a
part
of the partition
\lambda
. The

length

\ell=\ell(\lambda)
of a partition
\lambda
is the number of parts in
\lambda
.
The multiplicity
m_{i}=m_{i}(\lambda)
of
i
in
\lambda
is the number of times that
i

appears as a part in
\lambda
. The notation
\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}
means a sum is being taken over all partitions
\lambda
of

n
.

In
[
9
]
, G. Meinardus gave an asymptotic formula for the
r(n)
when the

a_{n}
are all nonnegative real numbers.
Meinardus’ result was subsequently extended by H. Todt
[
14
]

to all real numbers
a_{n}
provided
the
r(n)
are increasing.
For recent work on these asymptotics, see Bridges et al.
[
4
]
.

We now give direct formulas for
r(n)
as a partition sum
in terms of the exponents
a_{k}
, and
a_{n}
as a divisor sum containing an inner sum over partitions in terms
of the coefficients
r(k)
.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
r(n)
and
a_{n}
be defined as in (
1.1
). Then

r(n)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\frac{(a_{1})_{m_{1}}(a_{2})_{m_{2}}\cdots}{m_{1}!\ m_{2}!\cdots},

(1.4)

where

(a)_{k}=(a)(a+1)(a+2)\cdots(a+k-1)
is the usual rising factorial.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
r(n)
and
a_{n}
be defined as in (
1.1
). Then

a_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}\mu\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)d\sum_{\lambda\vdash d}\frac{(-1)^{\ell-1}(\ell-1)!\ r(1)^{m_{1}}r(2)^{m_{2}}\cdots}{m_{1}!\ m_{2}!\cdots}.

(1.5)

2
Proof of Proposition
1.1

Our Proposition
1.1
follows as an application of the following theorem
of N. J. Fine
[
6
, §22, Theorem 1]
, rewritten to suit our current notation.

Theorem 2.1

(Fine)
.

Let
\psi_{j}(q)=\sum_{k\geq 0}C_{j}(k)q^{k},\ (j=1,2,\dots).
Then

\prod_{j\geq 1}\psi_{j}(q^{j})=\sum_{n\geq 0}q^{n}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}C_{1}(m_{1}(\lambda))C_{2}(m_{2}(\lambda))\cdots.

All we need to do is set Fine’s
\psi_{j}(q):=(1-q)^{-a_{j}}
, then
we must have

C_{j}(k)=\frac{(a_{j})_{k}}{k!},

by the Maclaurin series
expansion of the the binomial series. Proposition
1.1
follows immediately. ∎

3
Some applications of Proposition
1.1

In this section, we present applications of Proposition
1.1
to give identities for certain partition enumeration functions.

Example 3.1
.

A canonical special case of (
1.1
) is where all
a_{n}=1
, and thus
r(n)=p(n)
, the
number of partitions of
n
. Then (
1.4
) reduces to the trivial statement

p(n)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}1,

as
(1)_{m}=m!
. In other words,
p(n)
is obtained by
literally counting all partitions of
n
, one by one.

Example 3.2
.

Let
p_{S}(n)
denote the number of partitions of
n
in which all parts are in some subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{+}
of
the positive integers.
Observe that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{S}(n)q^{n}=\prod_{n\in S}\frac{1}{1-q^{n}}.

(3.1)

Upon noting
(0)_{0}=1
(the empty product) and
(0)_{m}=1
when
m
is a positive integer,
we see that

p_{S}(n)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\chi(\mbox{ all parts of $\lambda$ are in $S$ }),

(3.2)

where the characteristic function
\chi(A)=1
if
A
is true and
0
if
A
is false.
So (
3.2
) is just a brute force count of allowable partitions of size
n
as one runs through the
unrestricted partitions of
n
.

Example 3.3
.

Recall that if
\overline{p}(n)
denotes the number of overpartitions
[
5
]
of
n
, then

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\overline{p}(n)q^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1+q^{n}}{1-q^{n}}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-q^{n})^{a_{n}}}

(3.3)

where

a_{n}=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $n$ is even}\\
2&\mbox{if $n$ is odd}\end{cases}.

Thus we conclude that

\overline{p}(n)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}(m_{1}+1)(m_{3}+1)(m_{5}+1)\cdots.

(3.4)

Example 3.4
.

Fix a positive integer
k
.
If
p_{k}(n)
denotes the number of
k
-color partitions of
n
, then

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{k}(n)q^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-q^{n})^{k}}.

(3.5)

Thus we conclude that

p_{k}(n)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\frac{(k)_{m_{1}}(k)_{m_{2}}\cdots}{m_{1}!\ m_{2}!\cdots}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\binom{k+m_{1}-1}{k-1}\binom{k+m_{2}-1}{k-1}\binom{k+m_{3}-1}{k-1}\cdots.

(3.6)

Example 3.5
.

If
PL(n)
denotes the number of plane partitions of
n
, then

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PL(n)q^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-q^{n})^{n}}.

(3.7)

Thus we conclude that

PL(n)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\frac{(1)_{m_{1}}(2)_{m_{2}}(3)_{m_{3}}\cdots}{m_{1}!\ m_{2}!\ m_{3}!\cdots}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\binom{m_{2}+1}{1}\binom{m_{3}+2}{2}\binom{m_{4}+3}{3}\cdots.

(3.8)

Example 3.6
.

The
k
-broken diamond partitions
are defined by Andrews and P. Paule in
[
2
]
. If
r_{k}(n)
denotes the number of

k
-broken diamond partitions of
n
, then

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r_{k}(n)q^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-q^{n})^{a_{n}}},

where

a_{n}=\begin{cases}2&\mbox{if $n$ is even or $n\equiv(2k+1)\pmod{4k+2}$}\\
3&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Thus

r_{k}(n)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2+m_{2j+1})(1+m_{2j+2})(1+m_{(4k+2)j+(2k+1)})}{{(2+m_{(4k+2)j+(2k+1)})}}.

(3.9)

Remark
.

Further results along similar lines are noted in Appendix D of
[
12
]
.

4
Proof of Theorem
1.2

As before, let
r(n)
and
a_{n}
be defined by (
1.1
) and let
b(n)
be defined by

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b(n)q^{n}=\log\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-q^{n})^{a_{n}}}.

(4.1)

If we differentiate (
4.1
) with respect to
q
and equate coefficients of

q
, we observe that

nb(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}da_{d},

(4.2)

and thus by Möbius inversion, we have

na_{n}=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)d\,b(d).

(4.3)

Next, we need to prove a lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

We have that

n\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\frac{(-1)^{\ell(\lambda)-1}(\ell(\lambda)-1)!\ r(1)^{m_{1}(\lambda)}r(2)^{m_{2}(\lambda)}\cdots}{m_{1}(\lambda)!m_{2}(\lambda)!\cdots}\\
=\sum_{j=1}^{n}jr(j)\sum_{\mu\vdash n-j}\frac{(-1)^{\ell(\mu)}\ell(\mu)!\ r(1)^{m_{1}(\mu)}r(2)^{m_{2}(\mu)}\cdots}{m_{1}(\mu)!m_{2}(\mu)!\cdots}.

(4.4)

Proof.

Let
\lambda
be an arbitrary partition of size
n
.
Thus we have

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}jm_{j}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}j\frac{m_{j}(\lambda)!}{(m_{j}(\lambda)-1)!}\mbox{ (where we follow the convention that $\frac{0!}{(-1)!}:=0$)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}j\frac{m_{1}(\lambda)!\cdots m_{j-1}(\lambda)!m_{j}(\lambda)!m_{j+1}(\lambda)!\cdots m_{n}(\lambda)!}{m_{1}(\lambda)!\cdots m_{j-1}(\lambda)!(m_{j}(\lambda)-1)!m_{j+1}(\lambda)!\cdots m_{n}(\lambda)!},

which implies

\frac{n(-1)^{\ell(\lambda)-1}(\ell(\lambda)-1)!}{m_{1}(\lambda)!\cdots m_{n}(\lambda)!}\\
=\sum_{j=1}^{n}j\frac{(-1)^{m_{1}(\lambda)+\cdots m_{n}(\lambda)-1}(m_{1}(\lambda)+\cdots m_{n}(\lambda)-1)!}{m_{1}(\lambda)!\cdots m_{j-1}(\lambda)!(m_{j}(\lambda)-1)!m_{j+1}(\lambda)!\cdots m_{n}(\lambda)!}.

Upon multiplying both sides by
r(1)^{m_{1}(\lambda)}r(2)^{m_{2}(\lambda)}\cdots

and then summing over all partitions of
n
, the result follows.
∎

Now, we have the ingredients in place to prove Theorem
1.2
. Letting
f(q):=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}r(n)q^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n})^{-a_{n}}
,
and performing logarithmic differentiation, we obtain

\frac{d}{dq}\log f(q)=\frac{df}{dq}\cdot\frac{1}{f(q)}.

Writing each expression as a power series (see
[
13
, p. 3, Lemma 1]
for
the power series representation of
1/f(q)
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nb(n)q^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}jr(j)q^{j-1}\right)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{\lambda\vdash k}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}\ell!\ r(1)^{m_{1}}\cdots r(k)^{m_{k}}}{m_{1}!\cdots m_{k}!}\right]q^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}jr(j)\sum_{\mu\vdash n-j}\frac{(-1)^{\ell(\mu)}\ell(\mu)!\ r(1)^{m_{1}(\mu)}\cdots r(n-j)^{m_{n-j}(\mu)}}{m_{1}(\mu)!\cdots m_{n-j}(\mu)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q^{n-1}n\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\frac{(-1)^{\ell(\lambda)-1}(\ell(\lambda)-1)!\ r(1)^{m_{1}(\lambda)}\cdots r(n)^{m_{n}(\lambda)}}{m_{1}(\lambda)!\cdots m_{n}(\lambda)!},

where the last equality was proved as Lemma
4.1
.

By equating powers of
q
in the extremes, we now have
b(n)
in terms of

r(1),r(2),\dots,r(n)
. Finally, apply (
4.3
), to obtain Theorem
1.2
.

Some Applications of Theorem
1.2

Example 5.1
.

Let
c(n)
denote the number of compositions of
n
. It is well known that

c(n)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $n=0$}\\
2^{n-1}&\mbox{if $n>0$}\end{cases},

and that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c(n)q^{n}=\frac{1-q}{1-2q},

but as a consequence of Theorem
1.2
, we further conclude that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c(n)q^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-q^{n})^{a_{n}}},

where

a_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}\mu\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)(2^{d}-1),

(5.1)

i.e., the sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}=(1,1,2,3,6,9,18,30,56,99,186,335,630,\dots)
, which is OEIS sequence
A059966
[
11
]
.
OEIS indicates that the
a_{n}
are the Lie analog of the sequence of partition numbers,
which gives the dimensions of the homogeneous polynomials with one generator in
each degree, and several other interpretations.

To see that (
5.1
) holds, apply Theorem
1.2
with
r(n)=c(n)=2^{n-1}
if

n>0
and
r(0)=c(0)=1
. Thus we need to show that

d\sum_{\lambda\vdash d}\frac{(-1)^{\ell-1}(\ell-1)!(2^{0})^{m_{1}}(2^{1})^{m_{2}}(2^{2})^{m_{3}}\cdots}{m_{1}!\ m_{2}!\ m_{3}!\cdots}=2^{d}-1.

(5.2)

Apply Lemma
4.1
with
n=d
to the left side of (
5.2
):

\displaystyle\phantom{=}d\sum_{\lambda\vdash d}\frac{(-1)^{\ell-1}(\ell-1)!(2^{0})^{m_{1}}(2^{1})^{m_{2}}(2^{2})^{m_{3}}\cdots}{m_{1}!\ m_{2}!\ m_{3}!\cdots}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}j\ 2^{j-1}\sum_{\mu\vdash d-j}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}\ \ell!\ (2^{0})^{m_{1}}(2^{1})^{m_{2}}(2^{2})^{m_{3}}\cdots}{m_{1}!\ m_{2}!\ m_{3}!\cdots}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}j\ 2^{j-1}\cdot\mbox{ coeff of }q^{d-j}\mbox{ in }\frac{1}{1+2q+4q^{2}+8q^{3}+\cdots}

(5.3)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}j\ 2^{j-1}\cdot\mbox{ coeff of }q^{d-j}\mbox{ in }\frac{1-2q}{1-q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}j\ 2^{j-1}\cdot\mbox{ coeff of }q^{d-j}\mbox{ in }1-q-q^{2}-q^{3}-q^{4}-\cdots

\displaystyle=d\ 2^{d-1}-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}j\ 2^{j-1}

\displaystyle=2^{d}-1,

where (
5.3
) follows from the preceding line by
[
13
, p. 3, Lemma 1]
, and
the last equality follows by induction on
d
.

Example 5.2
.

Let
F_{n}
denote the
n
th Fibonacci number, where

F_{1}=F_{2}=1,\mbox{ and }F_{n}=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}\mbox{ for $n>2$ }.

It is well known that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}F_{n+1}q^{n}=\frac{1}{1-q-q^{2}},

but as a consequence of Theorem
1.2
, we also see that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}F_{n+1}q^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-q^{n})^{a_{n}}},

where

a_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}\mu\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)L_{d},

(5.4)

where
L_{1}=1,L_{2}=3,L_{n}=L_{n-1}+L_{n-2}
for
n>2
(the Lucas numbers).
The sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}=(1,1,1,1,2,2,4,5,8,11,18,25,40,58,90,135,

210,\dots)
is
OEIS sequence
A006206
[
10
]
.

To see that (
5.4
) holds, note that the
d=1
and
d=2
cases can be
easily verified by direct computation. For
d>2
, we start by applying Theorem
1.2
with

r(n)=F_{n+1}
, and observe that we need to show that

d\sum_{\lambda\vdash d}\frac{(-1)^{\ell-1}(\ell-1)!\ F_{2}^{m_{1}}F_{3}^{m_{2}}F_{4}^{m_{3}}\cdots}{m_{1}!\ m_{2}!\ m_{3}!\cdots}=L_{d}.

(5.5)

Apply Lemma
4.1
with
n=d
to the left side of (
5.5
):

\displaystyle\phantom{=}d\sum_{\lambda\vdash d}\frac{(-1)^{\ell-1}(\ell-1)!F_{2}^{m_{1}}F_{3}^{m_{2}}F_{4}^{m_{3}}\cdots}{m_{1}!\ m_{2}!\ m_{3}!\cdots}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}j\ F_{j+1}\sum_{\mu\vdash d-j}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}\ \ell!\ F_{2}^{m_{1}}F_{3}^{m_{2}}F_{4}^{m_{3}}\cdots}{m_{1}!\ m_{2}!\ m_{3}!\cdots}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}j\ F_{j+1}\cdot\mbox{ coeff of }q^{d-j}\mbox{ in }\frac{1}{F_{1}+F_{2}q+F_{3}q^{2}+F_{4}q^{3}+\cdots}

(5.6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}j\ F_{j+1}\cdot\mbox{ coeff of }q^{d-j}\mbox{ in }(1-q-q^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}j\ F_{j+1}\cdot\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $j=d$ },\\
-1&\mbox{if $j=d-1$ or $d-2$, }\\
0&\mbox{otherwise; }\end{cases}

\displaystyle=-(d-2)F_{d-1}-(d-1)F_{d}+dF_{d+1}

\displaystyle=-F_{d}-(d-2)(F_{d-1}+F_{d})+2F_{d+1}+(d-2)F_{d+1}

\displaystyle=-F_{d}-(d-2)F_{d+1}+2F_{d+1}+(d-2)F_{d+1}

\displaystyle=-F_{d}+F_{d+1}+F_{d+1}

\displaystyle=F_{d-1}+F_{d+1}

\displaystyle=L_{d},

where the equality at line (
5.6
) follows by
[
13
, p. 3, Lemma 1]
.

6
A
q
-analogue of
r(n)

As a final observation, we note that the expressions
(a_{i})_{m_{i}}/{m_{i}!}
appearing in Proposition
1.1
have a natural
q
-analogue

\lim_{q\to 1}\left[\begin{matrix}a_{i}-1+m_{i}\\
m_{i}\end{matrix}\right]_{\displaystyle q}=\frac{(a_{i})_{m_{i}}}{m_{i}!},

so long as
a_{i}\geq 0
, where

\left[\begin{matrix}n\\
k\end{matrix}\right]_{\displaystyle q}=\frac{(q;q)_{n}}{(q;q)_{k}(q;q)_{n-k}}

is the Gaussian binomial coefficient,
0\leq k\leq n
. Then for
|q|<1
, we may define
r_{q}(0):=1
and, for
n\geq 1
,

r_{q}(n):=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\left[\begin{matrix}a_{1}-1+m_{1}\\
m_{1}\end{matrix}\right]_{\displaystyle q}\left[\begin{matrix}a_{2}-1+m_{2}\\
m_{2}\end{matrix}\right]_{\displaystyle q}\cdots

to yield a
q
-analogue of
r(n)
, satisfying
\lim_{q\to 1}r_{q}(n)=r(n)
.

The generating function of
r_{q}(n)

may be obtained by attaching
z^{n}
to each side of this equation, and summing over all
n\geq 0
. This yields
a sum now over all the partitions on the right-hand side:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r_{q}(n)z^{n}=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}z^{\displaystyle\lvert\lambda\rvert}\left[\begin{matrix}a_{1}-1+m_{1}\\
m_{1}\end{matrix}\right]_{\displaystyle q}\left[\begin{matrix}a_{2}-1+m_{2}\\
m_{2}\end{matrix}\right]_{\displaystyle q}\cdots.

(6.1)

This identity leads us to a product generating formula for
r_{q}(n)
..

Theorem 6.1
.

If
r(n)
and
a_{n}
are defined as in equation (
1.1
) such that
a_{n}\geq 0
is satisfied for all
n\geq 0
, then we have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r_{q}(n)z^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(z^{n};q)_{a_{n}}}.

Proof.

Expanding the product

\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\left[\begin{matrix}a_{n}-1+1\\
1\end{matrix}\right]_{\displaystyle q}z^{n}+\left[\begin{matrix}a_{n}-1+2\\
2\end{matrix}\right]_{\displaystyle q}z^{2n}+\left[\begin{matrix}a_{n}-1+3\\
3\end{matrix}\right]_{\displaystyle q}z^{3n}+\cdots\right),

(6.2)

we obtain the right hand side of (
6.1
). Now, using the well-known
q
-series identity

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(qz)^{k}\left[\begin{matrix}a-1+k\\
k\end{matrix}\right]_{\displaystyle q}=\frac{1}{(qz;q)_{a}}

with
a\geq 0
, we make the substitutions
z\mapsto z^{a_{n}}q^{-1}
and
a\mapsto a_{n}\geq 0
, take the product over
n\geq 1
, then compare with (
6.2
), to arrive at the result.
∎

7
Open questions

We close with some open questions and suggestions for future related work.

1.

Can one impose conditions on the
r(n)
that would guarantee the
a_{n}
are nonnegative, so that the
r(n)
might be explicitly counting something?

2.

Can one impose conditions on the
r(n)
so that the
a_{n}
are periodic with respect to a fixed modulus, as in the product side of Rogers–Ramanujan type identities?

3.

Can one impose conditions on the
a_{n}
that guarantee the
r(n)
exponentially, subexponentially, or at specified rates?

4.

Is there a natural combinatorial interpretation for the
q
-analogue
r_{q}(n)
?

5.

Does Theorem
6.1
(or a generalization) hold for
a_{n}<0
under the definition of the
q
-Pochhammer symbol for negative indices, viz.
(a;q)_{-n}:=(aq^{-n};q)_{n}^{-1}
?
