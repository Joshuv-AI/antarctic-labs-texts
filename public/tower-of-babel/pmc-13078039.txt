A safer airway strategy during induction and emergence: laryngeal mask airway versus face mask in pediatric rigid bronchoscopy
Authors: Zunyuan Liu, Limin Wei, Yue Xie, Liqiang Yu, Yanchao Liu, Jing Zhao
Published: 2026

To the editor

Anesthetic management for pediatric rigid bronchoscopy during foreign body removal is particularly challenging, with the highest risk for adverse events occurring during induction and emergence [1, 2]. Conventional face mask (FM) ventilation is associated with substantial airway complications, as pediatric anatomy predisposes to obstruction, a risk exacerbated by translaryngeal anesthesia triggering laryngospasm and desaturation. The laryngeal mask airway (LMA) may overcome these limitations by securing the pharyngeal airway and providing more efficient ventilation and anesthetic delivery. We compared LMA with FM during induction and emergence in children undergoing rigid bronchoscopy.

Children aged 1–8 years (ASA I–IV) scheduled for elective or emergency rigid bronchoscopy for foreign body removal were eligible, excluding those with a full stomach. Patients were randomly assigned to LMA or FM during both induction and emergence using computer-generated randomization with sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. The attending anesthesiologist was not blinded, while a separate blinded assistant recorded the information.

Following intravenous induction with fentanyl (1.5 µg · kg− 1) and propofol (2.5 mg · kg− 1), the allocated airway strategy was applied. In the LMA group, sevoflurane inhalation was continued via the inserted laryngeal mask airway, whereas in the FM group, it was maintained via the FM. Topical anesthesia with 2% lidocaine (5 mg · kg− 1) was then administered: via the small opening at the top of the LMA’s “L”-shaped connector (maintaining a closed circuit) in the LMA group, or by direct laryngeal spray under laryngoscopy (requiring circuit disconnection) in the FM group. Once spontaneous respiration and adequate anaesthetic depth were stabilised, sevoflurane was discontinued. The rigid bronchoscope was then inserted for the surgical procedure. Anesthesia was maintained with a continuous intravenous infusion of propofol at 12 mg · kg− 1 · h− 1. Upon bronchoscope removal, the allocated airway device (reinserted LMA or lateral-position FM) was reapplied during emergence until protective reflexes returned.

Laryngospasm (acute upper airway obstruction, absent ETCO₂, increased resistance) and hypoxemia (SpO₂ < 90%) during induction or emergence were recorded. Durations of high-dose sevoflurane inhalation (> 5%) and high fresh gas flow (> 3 L·min− 1) during induction, along with lowest SpO₂ values, were also noted.

Between April 2021 and December 2022, 46 children were randomized; four withdrew consent (all from the LMA group), leaving 23 in the LMA group and 19 in the FM group (Supplementary Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics were comparable between groups (Table 1).

The LMA group had lower rates of laryngospasm (4.3% vs. 36.8%; RR 0.12; 95% CI 0.02–0.88; P = 0.015) and hypoxemia (8.7% vs. 42.1%; RR 0.21; 95% CI 0.05–0.86; P = 0.026). Induction was more efficient with the LMA, with shorter durations of sevoflurane > 5% (median 1.0 vs. 3.0 min; P < 0.001) and high fresh gas flow > 3 L·min− 1 (median 1.3 vs. 4.0 min; P < 0.001) (Table 2). Lowest SpO₂, emergence time, PACU stay, and hemodynamics did not differ significantly (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Using an LMA during induction and emergence for pediatric rigid bronchoscopy was associated with lower rates of laryngospasm and hypoxemia compared with an FM, addressing the substantial risk inherent to conventional management [3].

The reduction in airway complications with LMA use stems from two mechanisms. First, it creates a “closed-circuit” system for induction and topical anesthesia. Unlike FM ventilation, requiring circuit disconnection and direct laryngeal spraying (a potent laryngospasm trigger), the LMA allows topical anesthesia while maintaining uninterrupted ventilation and anesthetic delivery [4]. This decouples topical anesthesia from the risk of provoking laryngospasm. Second, the LMA enables a “sandwich” strategy that secures the airway both before bronchoscope insertion and immediately after removal, bridging the two most vulnerable periods.

This LMA-based approach shifts from reactive management to proactive prevention. Connecting the LMA directly to the anesthesia circuit allows real-time monitoring of respiratory mechanics and end-tidal sevoflurane concentration, moving beyond the “black box” of traditional mask ventilation and providing objective guidance for anesthesia during induction and emergence.

The LMA also offered practical advantages. Shorter durations of high-dose sevoflurane and high fresh gas flow reflected more rapid and controllable induction. The sealed airway may also reduce occupational exposure to volatile anesthetics and optimize human resource use by eliminating the need for dedicated mask-holding during critical periods [5].

In conclusion, using an LMA during induction and emergence for pediatric rigid bronchoscopy was associated with lower rates of laryngospasm and hypoxemia compared with an FM, suggesting a safer strategy for managing these critical phases.
