1.
Introduction

Real-time communication (RTC) applications centered around audio and video have become central to remote work and education. As reported in
(
businessresearchinsights2025webrtc
)
, over
1.7
billion individuals utilized WebRTC-based applications in 2023 alone, with continued growth anticipated due to advancements in generative AI for audio and video. WebRTC has emerged as the de facto standard for browser-based RTC, with congestion control mechanisms such as Google’s Congestion Control (GCC) playing a key role in managing delay-sensitive traffic.

Simultaneously, Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellite constellations have gained prominence as a means to extend high-speed connectivity to underserved regions lacking terrestrial infrastructure. Notably, direct-to-device (D2D) LEO constellations– such as the
110
node Blue Walker constellation proposed by AST SpaceMobile for global coverage
(
ASTSpaceMobile
)
– present a unique opportunity for RTC. These constellations are designed to offer longer duration satellite links with fewer handovers. Specifically, these networks leverage fewer satellites (e.g., a few hundred) with larger antennas to achieve global coverage as opposed to dense LEO systems such as Starlink which comprise thousands of satellites. We refer to constellations such as Blue Walker as sparse constellations and draw a distinction with dense constellations such as Starlink. These properties make sparse constellations uniquely well-suited for RTC, which is notoriously sensitive to disruptions caused by handovers.

Despite their promise, RTC systems over LEO networks remain fragile. The high altitude and orbital dynamics of LEO satellites introduce burst latency and buffer bloating
(
tao2023transmitting
)
, degrading quality of experience (QoE) for end users. In particular, we find that WebRTC’s default sender-side pacing queue management– originally designed for relatively low-latency terrestrial links– exacerbates performance issues in the presence of routine LEO delays. Note that while the sending queue holds encoded media frames waiting to be processed, the pacing queue serves a different purpose: it takes the resulting packets and carefully times their release onto the network to prevent congestion. In our work, we focus specifically on the management of the pacing queue. For the remainder of the paper, we refer to managing the pacing queue as queue management.

To illustrate these challenges, we conduct approximately
1500
WebRTC videoconferencing calls across emulated terrestrial mobile links (5G and 4G) and sparse LEO constellations. We leverage Mahimahi and real mobile traces to emulate terrestrial mobile links, and utilize StarryNet
(
lai2023starrynet
)
– a high-fidelity integrated satellite-terrestrial network (ISTN) emulator– for LEO conditions. The results of our initial exploration (see Figure
1
) indicate that the default sender-side queue policy leads to substantially lower video bitrates and significantly higher freeze rates in sparse LEO settings. Unlike challenging dynamic scenarios, the substantial regression under ideal conditions with infrequent handovers suggests under utilization of network resources.

This paper argues that a “one-size-fits-all” queue policy is fundamentally misaligned with the time-varying structure of LEO networks. To address this, we propose a novel
handover-aware
queue management mechanism that dynamically adjusts the sender queue capacity based on predicted handover activity. During stable phases with no expected handovers, the queue size is reduced to minimize latency. Conversely, during periods of anticipated handover, the queue is proactively enlarged to absorb the resulting jitter and throughput dips.

Our approach formulates this decision-making task as an offline contextual bandit problem. Rather than explicitly modeling reward functions or estimating value functions– as is common in full offline reinforcement learning– we adopt an imitation learning strategy. Specifically, we construct an expert policy by clustering videoconferencing segments from LEO environments and labeling optimal queue sizes based on empirical QoE. Our policy network, a Transformer-based encoder, is trained to imitate this heuristic using supervised learning, sidestepping the complexities of counterfactual reasoning and reward design.

This near real-time imitation-driven approach has several key benefits: (1) it avoids the need for high-variance value estimation; (2) it naturally restricts learning to expert-demonstrated behavior, improving interpretability and reliability; (3) by considering only sparse constellations, we can model the future states of the network in near real-time using public tools such as the SGP4 orbital propagation model and Two-line element set (TLE) data to inform our queuing decisions. The near real-time execution of our queue policy has little to no impact on the system’s real-time performance.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

We identify a key failure mode of GCC in sparse LEO networks stemming from "one-size-fits-all" sender-side queue limits.

•

We formulate queue management as an offline imitation task and propose a policy that dynamically adapts WebRTC’s maximum send-side queue limit based on predicted handover patterns.

•

We demonstrate that our approach yields up to
3\times
improvements in video bitrate and a
62\%
reduction in freeze rate compared to WebRTC’s default configuration.

3.
Methods

Figure
2
describes our system’s workflow. First, an initial queue policy is deployed on client devices. Second, we collect telemetry logs generated from these user videoconferencing calls. Client logs are then transferred to a central server for processing where state-action pairs are extracted. Third, we cluster the resulting state-action pairs based on the number and frequency of observed handovers per videoconferencing segment. The target management policy is then constructed by labeling each cluster with the queue limit that produced the highest call quality. This process generates state-action pairs that reflect the experiences of a dynamic queue management policy (
3.2
). Third, our system is then trained to imitate this target policy (
3.3
). Lastly, the learned policy is deployed on client devices. We describe our implementation in Section

3.1.
Problem Formulation

Given a fixed set of offline telemetry logs, we seek to learn a
queue management policy that selects the most suitable queue size to utilize for a given videoconferencing segment.

Imitation Learning.
We formulate our learning problem as an offline contextual bandit. Contextual bandit problems are formulated as
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P}(X),r(X,A))
, where
\mathcal{X}
is the context space (e.g., network states),
\mathcal{A}
is the action space,
\mathcal{P}(X)
is the distribution from which contexts are observed at each step, and
r(X,A)
is the expected reward for taking action
A
given context
X
. In the offline setting, we are provided with a fixed dataset of experiences,
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},a_{i},r_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and cannot interact with or query the environment for new information. This contrasts with online bandit algorithms that can directly query the target environment for exploration and exploitation. Our goal is to learn a policy
\hat{\pi}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
from this static offline dataset.

Rather than directly estimating the reward function
r(X,A)
, our approach in this work leverages imitation learning. We seek to imitate a non-linear queue management heuristic, which acts as our expert. Specifically, given a static dataset of collected demonstrations,
\Xi=\{(s_{j},\pi_{\text{exp}}(s_{j}))\}_{j=1}^{N}
, where
s_{j}\in\mathcal{X}
represents the observed network state and
\pi_{\text{exp}}(s_{j})
is the action taken by the expert in that state, our objective is to learn an imitation policy
\hat{\pi}
. This is achieved by framing the problem as a supervised learning task:

(3)

\hat{\pi}^{*}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\hat{\pi}}\sum_{s_{j}\in S}L(\hat{\pi}(s_{j}),\pi_{\text{exp}}(s_{j}))

where
L
is a suitable loss function (e.g., cross-entropy for discrete actions) that measures the discrepancy between the imitator policy’s action
\hat{\pi}(s_{j})
and the expert heuristic’s action
\pi_{\text{exp}}(s_{j})
for a given context
s_{j}
.
\hat{\pi}^{*}
is the learned policy that best mimics the expert based on the provided demonstrations.

Contextual Bandit.
At the beginning of videoconferencing segment
j
, we construct state
s_{j}=(\vec{h_{j}},\vec{t_{j}})
to capture anticipated handover conditions for the duration of the segment.
\vec{h_{j}}
refers to a binary predictive handover feature; specifically,
\vec{h_{j}}
contains a
1
in
\vec{h_{j,t}}
if a handover is likely to occur at time
t+1
. In contrast,
\vec{t_{j}}
refers to the normalized total number of expected handovers. These state variables consist of multiple measurements that provide a coarse, per-second view of the LEO environment throughout the videoconferencing segment. Note we select these features as they can be directly inferred from LEO satellite movement, scraped from tools like starlink-grpc, or estimated via a combination of orbital propagation models such as SGP4 and publicly available TLE data
(
ahangarpour2024trajectory
)
. Additionally, capturing the full duration of the segment enables our bandit to delineate between various LEO settings by implicitly modeling time-varying features such as the distribution of handover times (see Figure
3
). We consider
2
minute videoconferencing segments as handovers occur less frequently– on the order of minutes– in sparse constellations
(
yu2023ondemand
)
; hence, each feature contains
120
sample measurements. We input the
240
dimensional state
s_{j}
into our policy network.

Our parameterized policy
\pi_{\theta}
is represented with a six-layer Transformer encoder. Given
s_{j}
, our policy network selects the maximum queue size in ms to run for the duration of the videoconferencing segment with output
a_{j}
where
a_{j}\in\{500,600,900,2000\}
. This action space was selected through A/B testing with an initial set of
15
queue limits ranging from
100
ms to
2000
ms. During training, we measure queue selection performance in terms of QoE
r_{j}=\alpha R_{j}-\beta F_{j}
where
R_{j}
and
F_{j}
correspond to the normalized average video bitrate and video freeze rate observed during videoconferencing segment
j
. This QoE formulation is chosen for its simplicity and its effectiveness in capturing the trade-off between throughput
R_{j}
and disruptions
F_{j}
. While not all encompassing, this balance is critical for assessing how queue selection strategies impact overall user experience in videoconferencing.

3.2.
Data Collection & Processing

To emulate a sparse D2D LEO constellation, we utilize StarryNet
(
lai2023starrynet
)
, a high-fidelity container-based ISTN emulator. StarryNet integrates crowd-sourced satellite TLEs and the SGP4 orbital propagation model to capture time-varying dynamics such as satellite positions, propagation delays, visibility, and connectivity. Previous studies have demonstrated that StarryNet’s fidelity ensures spatial and temporal consistency with real constellations
(
lai2023starrynet
;
li2024stable
)
. We emulate sparse LEO constellations ranging from
80
to
100
satellites. Additionally, we consider a grid+ topology where each satellite has inter-satellite links (ISL) to neighboring satellites and adjacent orbital planes. We consider ISL speeds of
1
Gbps and packet loss of
1\%
for satellite and ground station links. We leverage OSPF for ISL routing and Linux traffic control to attenuate satellite links based on visibility and orbital movement. We perturb satellite movement by modifying orbital parameters such as altitude, Right Ascension of the Ascending Node (RAAN), and mean anomaly to capture altitude, plane, and phasing maneuvers. To improve data diversity, we sample pairs of locations from popular cities across the globe to emulate different user terminal (UT) locations.

Offline learning is bound by the best combination of poli-
cies present in the fixed offline dataset. To ensure adequate
coverage over the space of queue policies, we collected logs
from
2000
videoconferencing calls using GCC and a random queue policy from LEO environments. Prior to each videoconferencing segment, the random policy uniformly
selects among
15
distinct queue limits– ranging from
100
ms to
2000
ms– to run for the duration of the segment. Our telemetry system logs the relevant trajectory information: the estimated handover features, the
queue decision actions, and the QoE video metrics. StarryNet employs a delay-based handover policy, initiating a handover when a candidate satellite offers a lower delay than the serving satellite. We identify handovers by monitoring for changes in the serving satellite ID, which is provided by StarryNet. Experience was collected once prior to training.

To construct the target queue policy, we first apply K-means clustering to the collected samples based on two features: the total number and frequency of handovers, where a handover corresponds to a change in the serving satellite. This initial step groups calls that exhibited similar handover dynamics (see Figure
3
) under the assumption that these distinct handover profiles may benefit from different queue limit strategies. We label each cluster based on the best performing queue size in terms of QoE
r_{j}=2*R_{j}-F_{j}
where
R_{j}
and
F_{j}
correspond to the normalized average video bitrate and video freeze rate observed during videoconferencing segment
j
. We empirically find
k=4
with queue sizes
\{500,600,900,2000\}
to yield the best results.

3.3.
Architecture & Training

The policy network was implemented as a six-layer transformer encoder and trained using offline heuristic demonstrations. Each time step was tokenized into a
64
dimensional embedding using a multi-layer perceptron (MLP). We set the batch size to
128
and minimized the cross entropy loss between our policy’s actions and heuristic actions over the course of
100
training epochs. We employed Adam optimization with a cosine annealing learning rate schedule with the following parameters: initial learning rate of
0.001
, a weight decay of
0.01
, and a dropout rate of
0.1
for regularization. Hyperparameter selection was validated using five-fold cross-validation.

3.4.
Policy Deployment

We implement our queue selection policy in Pytorch and integrate it directly on top of WebRTC
(
webrtc
)
, an open-source framework for real-time videoconferencing. Specifically, our queue selection policy runs on end-devices and directly modifies the maximum queue limit of WebRTC’s send-side packet pacer through the pacer’s API (e.g.,
setQueueLimit(TimeDelta limit)
). Additionally, given that our policy executes in near real-time (on the order of seconds), we are not limited to real-time constraints (e.g., sub
50
ms). As a result, we leverage SGP4 and TLE data to estimate high-fidelity LEO features for the duration of the videoconferencing call and adopt a transformer-based architecture to select the queue limit. Our approach provides the sender with knowledge of upcoming handovers by estimating their frequency over a short horizon (e.g.,
2
minutes). This is made possible by the predictable orbital motion of satellite constellations, which can be tracked and modeled via publicly available TLE data and SGP4, and results in handovers at regular intervals (e.g., every 15 seconds for Starlink
(
tanveer2023making
)
). This periodicity allows for coarse-grain handover prediction. Our method is complemented by
(
ahangarpour2024trajectory
)
, which can infer fine-grain features of the next serving satellite by combining TLE data with the sender’s local antenna obstruction maps. Our focus on a sparse LEO deployment enables the near real-time estimation of future constellation states, without compromising the real-time performance of WebRTC.

4.1.
Testbed Setup

Our queue policy was evaluated through peer-to-peer videoconferencing calls conducted within a controlled testbed environment. Our testbed environment leveraged Cloudlab
(
duplyakin2019design
)
for the underlying compute infrastructure, AlphaRTC
(
eo2022opennetlab
)
– an open-source WebRTC fork– as the videoconferencing client, and the StarryNet network emulation tool
(
lai2023starrynet
)
. StarryNet was configured using publicly available TLE data to emulate dynamic satellite network conditions. We evaluated our approach against WebRTC’s default queue limit policy, where GCC served as the common rate control algorithm for both setups. We report the following key performance metrics: video bitrate (Mbps), freeze rate (freezes/minute), packet loss (%), and end-to-end delay (ms). We perform A/B testing across hundreds of calls and report our results below.

4.2.
Ideal LEO Dynamics

We baseline our queue policy against GCC’s default queue policy. We utilized publicly available TLE data from SpaceTrack to emulate sparse LEO constellations containing
80
satellites. In this sparse configuration, handovers occurred infrequently, at a rate of approximately
0
-
1
per call. For each evaluation call, we randomly sampled pairs of locations from major cities around the globe to represent geographically separated peers. We then conducted videoconferencing calls with WebRTC’s default queue policy and our handover-aware queue method. Our evaluation consisted of over
500
calls per queue method. Our key metrics are summarized in Figure
4
.

The results demonstrate that our method generalizes from offline training to an online emulated environment. Additionally, we observe no significant difference in packet loss between WebRTC’s default queue policy and ours; however, our approach leads to a marginal increase in the end-to-end delay. In contrast, our handover-aware queue policy delivers up to a
3
x increase in video bitrate, while reducing the video freeze rate by up to
62\%
compared to WebRTC’s default queue policy. We observe that the default queuing policy, which allows packets to buffer for up to
2
seconds, performs poorly in stable LEO conditions. This excessive buffering causes bloated sender-side queues; draining them introduces delay spikes and burst traffic that leads GCC to conservatively lower its target bitrate. This prevents GCC from capitalizing on available network capacity resulting from the low handover rate. In contrast, our proposed policy limits the maximum queue size to
500
ms-
600
ms. This aggressive queue policy prevents buildup while ensuring smooth packet flow, and provides GCC with a more accurate view of the network’s delay characteristics. As a result, GCC can increase its bandwidth estimates, enabling the video encoder to opportunistically encode frames at a higher quality and bitrate.

Our results suggest that GCC’s default configuration is too conservative for LEO networks. The large propagation delays inherent to LEO networks are more conducive to buffer bloat and introduce self-inflicted delay-spikes; hence, even on a stable connection with few handovers, GCC’s delay-based algorithm, which was designed for terrestrial networks, maintains an overly-conservative sending rate.

100
satellites. It is important to note that StarryNet leverages a delay-based handover policy. By increasing the constellation size, handovers are more likely to occur since our UT has a higher probability of perceiving a candidate satellite with a lower propagation delay (see Figure

). We empirically observe that the handover rate increases from

-

to
0
-

per call. Additionally, we introduce altitude, plane, and phasing maneuvers. All other details are consistent with our previous setup. We report our results in Figure

.

Consistent with our previous evaluation, these results demonstrate that our method successfully generalizes from offline training to online emulated environments. However, in these more dynamic LEO scenarios, the observed performance gains were more muted. Specifically, our handover-aware queue policy yielded up to a
25\%
increase in video bitrate and a
25\%
reduction in the video freeze rate when compared to GCC’s default queue policy.

We observed that our policy preemptively expands its queue capacity to
900

2000
ms under these dynamic conditions, which enables the send-side queue to better absorb these disruptions at the expense of more rapid transmissions. Additionally, we observed no significant difference in packet loss between WebRTC’s default queue policy and ours. However, mirroring our earlier results, we observed a marginal increase in the end-to-end packet delay.

5.
Future Work

We identified the send-side queue limit as a critical bottleneck for RTC in sparse LEO constellations. However, in RTC, audio and video packets are generated and consumed in real-time, making playback buffers– like those found in other streaming applications– unavailable. This inherent characteristic of RTC presents a unique challenge to generalize to LEO. Maintaining high video quality during rapid handovers is still significant challenge due RTC’s high susceptibility to network disruptions.
Our research specifically addresses sparse LEO constellations, such as those proposed by AST SpaceMobile
(
ASTSpaceMobile
)
. However, these systems are not yet widely deployed and have primarily been validated through small-scale tests. Sparse constellations tend to perform handovers every few minutes, a frequency considerably more favorable for RTC. In stark contrast, dense LEO networks like Starlink, which already operate thousands of satellites across multiple orbital shells with inter-satellite links, experience handovers approximately every
15
seconds
(
tanveer2023making
)
. These dense deployments may necessitate substantially larger send-side queues or more sophisticated dynamic queue management strategies to effectively mitigate the performance impact of rapid handovers.

Additionally, we focus on managing the send-side queue limit, whereas prior research has investigated modifying FEC
(
chen2022rl
;
lee2022r
)
and bandwidth estimation
(
gottipati2023offline
;
gottipati2024balancing
;
fang2019reinforcement
)
modules. A key direction for future work is the joint optimization of various WebRTC components to more effectively navigate the challenging dynamics of LEO networks. For instance, the per-timestep embeddings generated by our transformer model could be leveraged for downstream tasks like FEC and bandwidth estimation. This approach could involve utilizing queue optimization as a pre-training task for these interconnected components.

6.
Conclusion

Our work tackles key challenges in adapting existing RTC stacks to LEO networks. We propose a handover-aware queue management policy that dynamically adapts the maximum pacing queue limit based on anticipated handover dynamics. Through a series of experiments, we
illustrate that our offline approach generalizes from offline telemetry logs to an online emulated setting. We then provide initial results showing that in sparse LEO settings, our dynamic queue policy yields up to a
3
x improvement in video bitrates and reduces video freeze
rates by up to
62\%
when compared to WebRTC’s default queue policy. Adapting existing RTC stacks to widely deployed dense LEO constellations like Starlink presents a compelling direction for future investigation.
