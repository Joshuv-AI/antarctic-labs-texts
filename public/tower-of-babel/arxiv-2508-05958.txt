Hierarchical Tucker Low-Rank Matrices: Construction and Matrix-Vector Multiplication Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Yingzhou Li, Jingyu Liu

Abstract
In this paper, a hierarchical Tucker low-rank (HTLR) matrix is proposed to approximate non-oscillatory kernel functions in linear complexity. The HTLR matrix is based on the hierarchical matrix, with the low-rank blocks replaced by Tucker low-rank blocks. Using high-dimensional interpolation as well as tensor contractions, algorithms for the construction and matrix-vector multiplication of HTLR matrices are proposed admitting linear and quasi-linear complexities respectively. Numerical experiments demonstrate that the HTLR matrix performs well in both memory and runtime. Furthermore, the HTLR matrix can also be applied on quasi-uniform grids in addition to uniform grids, enhancing its versatility.

Introduction

This paper considers the integral equation (IE) taking the form

a(\boldsymbol{x})u(\boldsymbol{x})+\int_{\Omega}k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})u(\boldsymbol{y})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}=f(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad x\in\Omega,

(1.1)

where
\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
is the unit box in
d
.
Here the
kernel function

k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
as well as the function
a(\boldsymbol{x})
in (
1.1
) are given.
In some cases, the function
u(\boldsymbol{x})
is known, and we would like to evaluate the integral equation (
1.1
) to obtain
f(\boldsymbol{x})
.
This is known as the forward evaluation or the application.
In other cases, the function
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is known and
u(\boldsymbol{x})
is unknown.
We would like to solve the integral equation (
1.1
) to obtain
u(\boldsymbol{x})
.
This is known as the backward evaluation or the inversion.
Both forward and backward evaluations are of great importance in practice.

Discretizing (
1.1
) using typical approaches leads to a dense
linear system

\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{f},

(1.2)

where
\boldsymbol{A}\in\real^{N\times N}
is a dense matrix and
N
is the number of discretization points.
For instance, the Nyström scheme of (
1.1
) on a uniform grid with
n
points in each direction yields the following linear system of size
N=n^{d}
:

a(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})u_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}K_{i,j}h^{d}u_{j}=f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),\quad i=1,\dotsc,N,

(1.3)

in which
u_{i}\approx u(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
approximates the value on the grid points.
The matrix
\boldsymbol{K}
can be viewed as a kernel matrix, with the exception of a modification on the diagonal.
Its entries are defined as follows:

K_{i,j}\coloneq\begin{cases}k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}),&j\neq i,\\
\int_{\Omega_{i}}k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}/h^{d},&j=i,\end{cases}

(1.4)

where the integral is computed numerically.
The product
K_{i,j}h^{d}
is the approximated integral of
k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\cdot)
on a square domain
\Omega_{j}
with width
h=1/n
and center
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
.
The discretization (
1.3
) can also be regarded as a collocation method using piecewise-constant basis functions whose supports are
\{\Omega_{i}\}
, except that the off-diagonal entries use an approximation of the integral.

1.1
Related Work

Typically, dense direct methods for solving (
1.2
) such as the LU factorization
take the
\mathscr{O}(N^{3})
time complexity and
\mathscr{O}(N^{2})
storage complexity, which becomes prohibitive as
N
increases to moderately large values.

Fast algorithms have been designed to reduce both the time and storage complexity in addressing (
1.2
).
The key insight is the observation that off-diagonal submatrices of
\boldsymbol{A}
in (
1.3
) are numerically low-rank if the kernel function
k
is smooth and not highly oscillatory away from the diagonal.
Such kernel functions are commonly encountered in practice, including the Green’s function for elliptic PDEs and low frequency wave equations, as well as Gaussian kernels.
This
low-rank property
brings the possibility to enhance both storage efficiency and computational performance for the matrix.
This idea can be traced back to the
Barnes–Hut algorithm

[
3
]
(also known as the tree-code) and
fast multipole methods
(FMM)
[
8
,
15
,
21
,
49
]
, which accelerate the matrix-vector multiplication for kernel matrices.
Besides, Hackbusch and his collaborators have introduced
hierarchical matrices

[
22
,
25
]
(also known as
\mathcal{H}
-matrices and
\mathcal{H}^{2}
-matrices).
These matrices achieve quasi-linear or linear complexity for most matrix algebraic operations including matrix-vector multiplication, matrix-matrix multiplication, matrix LU factorization, etc
[
6
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
35
]
.
Notably,
\mathcal{H}
-matrices and
\mathcal{H}^{2}
-matrices can be viewed as the algebraic version of the Barnes–Hut algorithm and FMM respectively.

Hierarchical block-separable
(HBS) matrices (also referred to as
hierarchical semi-separable
, HSS, matrices)
[
40
]
represent another class of fast algorithms that accelerate kernel matrix operations.
These matrices are closely related to
\mathcal{H}^{2}
-matrix under weak admissibility condition.
Various HBS matrix factorization algorithms are proposed to achieve quasi-linear or linear complexity for matrix-vector multiplication and solving linear systems
[
9
,
12
,
17
,
19
,
48
]
.
Another family of fast algorithms factorizes the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
as a product of block sparse lower and upper triangular matrices, where each block could be applied or inverted in
\mathscr{O}(1)
complexity.
Algorithms in this category include
recursive skeletonization factorization

[
31
,
41
]
and
hierarchical interpolative factorization

[
32
]
.
Two key techniques employed for algorithms in this family are the
interpolative decomposition

[
10
]
and the
proxy surface

[
39
]
.
When the kernel function is smooth without singularity, such as in the case of Gaussian kernels, the low-rank approximation can be applied more aggressively, extending to those diagonal blocks as well
[
46
,
47
]
.

Though aforementioned fast algorithms achieve low complexity with respect to the matrix size, they still face the challgenge of the
curse of dimensionality
(CoD).
When the problem dimension
d
increases, the prefactor in the complexity with respect to the size of each direction scales exponentially with
d
.
To address this issue, tensor low-rank decompositions are commonly used techniques.
In
[
34
]
, the authors provide a comprehensive introduction to tensor decompositions such as the
CANDECOMP/PARAFAC
(CP) decomposition and the
Tucker decomposition
, both of which can be viewed as a generalization of the matrix singular value decomposition (SVD).
Tensor decompositions could be computed by various numerical algorithms, including
high-order SVD
(HOSVD)
[
13
]
,
higher-order orthogonal iteration
(HOOI)
[
14
]
,
sequentially truncated higher order SVD
(STHOSVD)
[
45
]
, and their randomized versions
[
30
,
42
]
.
Besides,
tensor train
,
tensor ring
, and
tensor network
are another family of tensor decompositions widely used in practice, especially in computational physics and chemistry
[
11
,
43
]
.

Blending the structure of hierarchical decomposition and tensor decomposition has the potential to yield linear or quasi-linear fast algorithms with improved prefactors.

Hierarchical Kronecker tensor-product
(HKT) approximation
[
16
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
combines these two techniques, resulting om a quasi-linear representation of some high-dimensional integral and elliptic operators.
Recently,
tensor butterfly algorithm

[
33
]
has been proposed to represent
high-dimensional oscillatory integral operators, which combines the
butterfly factorization

[
36
,
37
,
38
]
and Tucker decomposition, further enhancing computational efficiency in high-dimensional contexts.

1.2
Contributions

In this paper, a
hierarchical Tucker low-rank
(HTLR) matrices is defined by replacing the low-rank blocks in an
\mathcal{H}
-matrix with
Tucker low-rank
(TLR) matrices.
The Tucker decomposition enables TLR matrices to mitigate the CoD compared to conventional low-rank matrices, resulting in lower memory requirements and faster computational runtime.
Our analysis demonstrates that only
\mathscr{O}(N)
storage is needed to store an HTLR matrix of size
N
.
Linear construction and quasi-linear application algorithms are proposed for HTLR matrices.
In the construction algorithm, the TLR matrices are generated via multidimensional interpolations.
A theoretical error bound is established for a specific class of kernel functions.
The application algorithm uses tensor contractions for matrix-vector multiplication, achieving an improvement in the prefactor of the dominant complexity compared to
\mathcal{H}
-matrices.
While the HTLR matrix is first introduced and discussed in the context of problems with a uniform grid discretization, we also present its application on a quasi-uniform grid discretization.
Numerical experiments are conducted across various settings.
The results not only support our complexity analysis but also offer compelling evidence for the efficiency of HTLR matrices.

2.1
Notations

For a positive integer
N
, the index set
\{1,\dotsc,N\}
is denoted by
[N]
.
The notation
|\cdot|
denotes either the number of elements in a set or the area of a domain.
Vectors are denoted by boldface lowercase letters, e.g.,
\boldsymbol{a}
, matrices are denoted by boldface capital letters, e.g.,
\boldsymbol{A}
, and tensors (with order greater than
2
) are denoted by boldface Euler script letters, e.g.,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
.
We use MATLAB notations throughout the paper.
For example, the
i
-th entry of a vector is denoted by
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
or
\boldsymbol{a}(i)
.
The submatrix of a matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
corresponding to row and column index sets
\tau
and
\sigma
are denoted by
\boldsymbol{A}_{\tau,\sigma}
or
\boldsymbol{A}(\tau,\sigma)
.
A colon is used to indicate all entries of a dimension.
For example, the
j
-th column of a matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
is denoted by
\boldsymbol{A}_{:,j}
or
\boldsymbol{A}(:,j)
.
The conjugate transpose of a matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
is denoted by
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
.
A matrix
\boldsymbol{U}
is
orthonormal
if its columns form an orthonormal set, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{U}^{*}\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{I}
.

We make use of multi-indices on tensor structures. For instance, when
dealing with a tensor grid
\{(x_{1;i_{1}},x_{2;i_{2}})\}_{i_{1},i_{2}=1}^{n}
, we use
\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},i_{2})
as the index for each point and
represent it by
\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{i}}=(x_{1;i_{1}},x_{2;i_{2}})
.
The one-to-one mapping between a multi-index and its linear order is given
by
\boldsymbol{i}\leftrightarrow i_{1}+(i_{2}-1)n
, and the grid is also
denoted as
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\}_{\boldsymbol{i}=1}^{n^{2}}
.
Additionally, we denote
|\boldsymbol{i}|\coloneq i_{1}+i_{2}
and

\boldsymbol{i}!\coloneq i_{1}i_{2}
. These notations and their corresponding
meanings can be extended analogously to
d
-dimensional cases.

Our notations and terminologies of tensors are consistent with
[
34
]
.
The mode-
\ell
product (contraction) of a tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\in\real^{n_{1}\times\dotsb\times n_{d}}
and a matrix
\boldsymbol{U}\in\real^{m\times n_{\ell}}
is denoted as
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{\ell}\boldsymbol{U}\in\real^{n_{1}\times\dotsb\times n_{\ell-1}\times m\times n_{\ell+1}\times\dotsb\times n_{d}}
.
The contraction of two tensors
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
is represented by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
, where
\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
are vectors specifying the dimensions in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
to be contracted.

The
Tucker decomposition
of a tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
with
core tensor

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}\in\real^{p_{1}\times\dotsb\times p_{d}}
and
factored matrices

\{\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}\in\real^{n_{\ell}\times p_{\ell}}\}_{\ell=1}^{d}
is defined as follows:

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\coloneq\operatorname{Tucker}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}},\{\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{d})\coloneq\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}\times_{1}\boldsymbol{U}_{1}\times_{2}\dotsb\times_{d}\boldsymbol{U}_{d}.

(2.1)

The vector
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},\dotsc,p_{d})
is referred to as the
Tucker rank
or simply, the
rank
of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
.
When
p_{1}=\dotsb=p_{d}=p
, we also say
p
is the rank of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
.
In this article, we often consider
2d
-order tensors where the first and last
d
dimensions correspond to points in two different tensor grids.
In such cases, the notation

\operatorname{Tucker}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}},\{\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{d},\{\boldsymbol{V}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{d}\bigr)\coloneq\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}\times_{1}\boldsymbol{U}_{1}\times_{2}\dotsb\times_{d}\boldsymbol{U}_{d}\times_{d+1}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\times_{d+2}\dotsb\times_{2d}\boldsymbol{V}_{d}

is used to represent the Tucker decomposition.

It is often advantageous to enforce the factored matrices to be orthonormal, which can be satisfied by most Tucker decomposition algorithms, such as those presented in
[
13
,
45
]
.
In particular, given a Tucker computation (
2.1
), this can be achieved through a series QR factorizations on each factored matrix, followed by a modification to the core tensor.
When QR factorization with column pivoting (QRCP) is adapted, we can further compress the Tucker rank.
Assuming that all
p_{\ell}=p
and
n_{\ell}=n
, then the total complexity of the orthogonalization is
\mathscr{O}(dp^{2}n+dp^{d+1})
.

2.2
Hierarchical Matrices

The definition of
\mathcal{H}
-matrices is based on the concept of
cluster tree, a tree encoding the partition information of the grid
points.

Definition 1
(Cluster tree).

Let
I
be the index set associated with the grid points from the
discretization of IE (
1.1
). A tree
\mathsf{T}_{I}
is said to be a
cluster tree corresponding to
I
if the following conditions
hold:

1.

Every node in
\mathsf{T}_{I}
is a subset of
I
.

2.

The root of
\mathsf{T}_{I}
is
I
.

3.

Each node
\tau\in\mathsf{T}_{I}
of
\mathsf{T}_{I}
is not empty.

4.

For every
\tau\in\mathsf{T}_{I}
, let
\operatorname{children}(\tau)
be the set consisting of its children.
Then, either
\operatorname{children}(\tau)=\emptyset
(
\tau
is a leaf node) or it forms a partition of
\tau
, i.e.,

\tau=\bigsqcup_{\tau^{\prime}\in\operatorname{children}(\tau)}\tau^{\prime}.

Here we use
\sqcup
to denote the union of pairwise disjoint sets.

Typically, each point
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is associated with a computational domain
\Omega_{i}
, which can be viewed as the support of its corresponding piecewise-constant function and satisfies the non-overlapping condition
|\Omega_{i}\cap\Omega_{j}|=0
for
j\neq i
.
It can be obtained from the Voronoi cells
[
2
]
associated with the grid points.
In particular, when consdiering a uniform grid,
\Omega_{i}
is a square domain centered at
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
.
For a indexset
\tau
associated with the points
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\tau}
, we define
\Omega_{\tau}\coloneq\cup_{i\in\tau}\Omega_{i}
.

When
\Omega
is a unit box
[0,1]^{d}
and the points are obtained from a uniform grid, the cluster tree can be constructed using a recursive
2^{d}
uniform partition.
In the construction of a cluster tree, a stopping criterion is typically imposed to prevent the successive partition, ensuring that the number of indices in a leaf node is neither too small nor exceeds a user-specified value, denoted as
N_{0}
.
Specifically, when
|\tau|\leq N_{0}
, we assign
\tau
be the leaf node and halt further partition on it.

Example 2.1
(A cluster tree on 2D uniform grid).

Suppose
\Omega=[0,1]^{2}
and
I
is associated with discretization points given by

\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{i}}=\biggl((i_{1}-1)h+\frac{h}{2},(i_{2}-1)h+\frac{h}{2}\biggr),\ \boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},i_{2}),\ 1\leq i_{1},i_{2}\leq n,

where
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
h=1/n
.
Consequently, the corresponding computational domain for each point is defined as
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{i}}=[(i_{1}-1)h,i_{1}h]\times[(i_{2}-1)h,i_{2}h]
.
The cluster tree
\mathsf{T}_{I}
is a quadtree and its leaf nodes are shown in Figure
2.1
.

.

n=16
,
N=n^{2}=256
and

.

The structure of
\mathcal{H}
-matrices is determined by its admissibility condition, a criterion for whether the interaction matrix of two domains can be regarded as low-rank.
There are typically two types of admissibility conditions: weak admissibility condition and strong admissibility condition.
Figure
2.2
demonstrates them respectively.

Definition 2
(Weak admissibility).

Two nodes
\tau
and
\sigma
in
\mathsf{T}_{I}
are weakly admissible if
\Omega_{\tau}
and
\Omega_{\sigma}
are non-overlapping.

Definition 3
(Strong admissibility).

Two nodes
\tau
and
\sigma
in
\mathsf{T}_{I}
are strongly
admissible if

\max\{\operatorname{diam}(\Omega_{\tau}),\operatorname{diam}(\Omega_{\sigma})\}\leq\eta\operatorname{dist}(\Omega_{\tau},\Omega_{\sigma}),

where
\operatorname{diam}(\Omega_{\tau})\coloneq\max_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\Omega_{\tau}}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|
is the diameter of

\Omega_{\tau}
and
\operatorname{dist}(\Omega_{\tau},\Omega_{\sigma})\coloneq\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega_{\tau},\boldsymbol{y}\in\Omega_{\sigma}}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|
is the distance between

\Omega_{\tau}
and
\Omega_{\sigma}
. Here
\eta>0
is a
predefined hyperparameter. In this case
\Omega_{\tau}
and

\Omega_{\sigma}
are said to be well-separated.

Figure 2.2
:

parameter
\eta=\sqrt{2}
(right) in
Example
2.1
. The grid points we consider is

\Omega_{\tau}
for each

.

When the kernel function in IE (
1.1
) is smooth except the diagonal and not highly oscillatory, such as the Green’s function of elliptic PDEs, the main distinction between weak and strong admissibility lies in whether the rank of the interaction matrix associated with two admissible nodes can be bounded by a constant independent of the matrix size.
Weak admissibility provides a straightforward hierarchical structure, sometimes referred to as
hierarchical off-diagonal low-rank
(HODLR) matrices
[
1
]
since all off-diagonal blocks of the matrices are regarded as low-rank.
However, the rank of these low-rank matrices may increase mildly with the matrix size
[
19
,
31
]
.
In contrast, strong admissibility ensures a numerically constant rank but results in a more complex
\mathcal{H}
-matrix structure
[
6
,
25
]
.

Given a cluster tree
\mathsf{T}_{I}
and an admissibility condition, the block cluster tree, defined as follows, provides a partition of
I\times I
and specifies which block possesses a low-rank representation.
The
\mathcal{H}
-matrix is then defined using the block cluster tree by compressing the submatrices of admissible leafs into low-rank matrices.

Definition 4
(Block cluster tree).

Suppose
\mathsf{T}_{I}
is a cluster tree associated with the index set
I
.
The block cluster tree
\mathsf{T}_{I\times I}
, associated with
\mathsf{T}_{I}
and an admissibility condition, is a tree whose nodes are subsets of
I\times I
, which is constructed using the following procedure starting from the root node
(\tau,\sigma)=(I,I)
:

•

If
\tau
and
\sigma
are admissible, then
(\tau,\sigma)
is an admissible leaf node.

•

If
\operatorname{children}(\tau)=\emptyset
or
\operatorname{children}(\sigma)=\emptyset
, then
(\tau,\sigma)
is an inadmissible leaf node.

•

If
\operatorname{children}(\tau)\neq\emptyset
and
\operatorname{children}(\sigma)\neq\emptyset
, then
(\tau,\sigma)
is a non-leaf node with children nodes given by
(\tau^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})\in\operatorname{children}(\tau)\times\operatorname{children}(\sigma)
.

Particularly, it can be directly verified that a block cluster tree is also a cluster tree.

Definition 5
(Hierarchical
matrix,
\mathcal{H}
-matrix,
[
5
]
).

Let
I
be an index set of the grid points from the discretization
of the IE (
1.1
) and
\mathsf{T}_{I\times I}
be a block cluster
tree. An
\mathcal{H}
-matrix of rank
r
, denoted by

\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathcal{H}}
, is a matrix on
\mathsf{T}_{I\times I}

such that for every leaf
(\tau,\sigma)\in\mathsf{T}_{I\times I}
,
the following conditions hold:

•

If
(\tau,\sigma)
is an admissible leaf node, then

\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathcal{H}}_{\tau,\sigma}
is a low-rank matrix
with rank at most
r
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathcal{H}}_{\tau,\sigma}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}
for some

\boldsymbol{U}\in\real^{|\tau|\times r}
,
\boldsymbol{V}\in\real^{|\sigma|\times r}
and
\boldsymbol{G}\in\real^{r\times r}
.

•

If
(\tau,\sigma)
is an inadmissible leaf node, then

\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathcal{H}}_{\tau,\sigma}\in\real^{|\tau|\times|\sigma|}
is a dense matrix.

•

If
(\tau,\sigma)
is a non-leaf node, then

\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathcal{H}}_{\tau,\sigma}
is a block matrix
with blocks
\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathcal{H}}_{\tau^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}
, where
(\tau^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})\in\operatorname{children}(\tau,\sigma)
.

\mathcal{H}
-matrices under weak and

\eta=\sqrt{2}

Compared to the typical

storage complexity of a complete dense matrix, the storage cost of an
\mathcal{H}
-matrix is significantly lower, specifically
\mathscr{O}(rN\log N)

[

.
However, as we will demonstrate in Section

, for a given accuracy, the upper bound of the desired rank

, taking the form

.

3
Hierarchical Tucker Low-Rank Matrices

When considering interaction matrices between two different tensor grids, Tucker decomposition is more appropriate for the low-rank compression.
By combing this idea with the
\mathcal{H}
-matrix framework, in this section we propose the HTLR matrix, which achieves linear storage complexity.
Additionally, we illustrate how the HTLR matrix can be effectively applied to a quasi-uniform grid.

3.1
Tucker Decomposition from High-Dimensional Interpolation

Interpolation is a widely-used technique for obtaining low-rank representations of kernel matrices
[
7
,
15
]
.
We first review the high-dimensional interpolation on a regular domain and then apply this method to construct the Tucker decomposition of interaction matrices between two tensor grids.

We begin with the one-dimensional case.
Suppose
f\colon[a,b]\to\real
is a function to be interpolated on
p
Chebyshev points given by

\xi_{t}=\frac{b-a}{2}\cos\biggl(\frac{(2t-1)\pi}{2p}\biggr)+\frac{b+a}{2},\quad 1\leq t\leq p.

The interpolant of
f
is defined as
\mathscr{J}_{[a,b]}[f](x)\coloneq\sum_{t=1}^{p}f(\xi_{t})L_{[a,b];t}(x)
where

L_{[a,b];t}(x)\coloneq\prod_{j=1,\ j\neq t}^{p}\frac{x-\xi_{t}}{\xi_{j}-\xi_{t}}

is the Lagrange interpolation function on the interval
[a,b]
.

In the
d
-dimensional case, suppose
f
is a function defined on the
box
B=\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}[a_{\ell},b_{\ell}]
, the interpolant of
f

using Chebyshev tensor points is given by

\mathscr{J}_{B}[f](\boldsymbol{x})\coloneq\sum_{\boldsymbol{t}\in[p]^{d}}f(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{t}})L_{B;\boldsymbol{t}}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(3.1)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{t}}=(\xi_{1;t_{1}},\dotsc,\xi_{d;t_{d}})
is the interpolation point in
B
indexed by the multi-index
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},\dotsc,t_{d})
and
p
is the number of interpolation points along each dimension.
The Lagrange interpolation function
L_{B;\boldsymbol{t}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is defined as the product of Lagrange interpolation functions associated the interval on each dimension, i.e.,
L_{B;\boldsymbol{t}}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneq\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}L_{[a_{\ell},b_{\ell}];t_{\ell}}(x_{\ell})
.
The upper bound of the approximation error is given by the following lemma.

Lemma 6
(Approximate error of the
d
-dimensional interpolation,
[
23
]
Lemma B.7).

Suppose
f
is a function on
B=\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}[a_{\ell},b_{\ell}]
which has
(p+1)
-th continuous derivatives, then for the Chebyshev interpolation (
3.1
), we have

\|\mathscr{J}_{B}[f]-f\|_{\infty,B}\leq 2\Lambda_{p}^{d-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\biggl(\frac{b_{\ell}-a_{\ell}}{4}\biggr)^{p+1}\frac{\|\partial_{\ell}^{p+1}f\|_{\infty,B}}{(p+1)!},

(3.2)

where
\Lambda_{p}
is the Lebesgue constant and satisfies the
estimate
\Lambda_{p}\sim 2\log(p)/\pi
(See
[
44
]
, Theorem 15.2).

Let
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
be the kernel function and
B_{\tau}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}[a_{\ell},b_{\ell}]
and
B_{\sigma}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}[c_{\ell},d_{\ell}]
be two disjoint boxes in
d
. By
applying (
3.1
) twice on
\boldsymbol{x}
and

\boldsymbol{y}
, we obtain

k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\approx\sum_{\boldsymbol{t}\in[p]^{d}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}\in[p]^{d}}k(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{t}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{s}})L_{B_{\tau};\boldsymbol{t}}(\boldsymbol{x})L_{B_{\sigma};\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{y}).

(3.3)

Suppose
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{i}}=(x_{1;i_{1}},\dotsc,x_{d;i_{d}})\}\subset B_{\tau}
and
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{j}}=(y_{1;j_{1}},\dotsc,y_{d;j_{d}})\}\subset B_{\sigma}
are two set of points, each containing
n^{d}
points.
The interaction matrix
\boldsymbol{K}\in\real^{n^{d}\times n^{d}}
of them is defined by
\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j})=k(\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{i}},\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{j}})
.
Utilizing the interpolation scheme (
3.3
), each entry
\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j})
can be approximated by

\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j})\approx\sum_{\boldsymbol{t}\in[p]^{d}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}\in[p]^{d}}k(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{t}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{s}})L_{B_{\tau};\boldsymbol{t}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{i}})L_{B_{\sigma};\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{j}}).

(3.4)

By exploiting the tensor form of the points as well as the Lagrange interpolation functions, we denote

\begin{gathered}\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}(i_{\ell},\mu_{\ell})=L_{[a_{\ell},b_{\ell}];\mu_{\ell}}(x_{\ell;i_{\ell}}),\quad\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}\in\real^{n\times p},\quad 1\leq\ell\leq d,\\
\boldsymbol{V}_{\ell}(j_{\ell},\nu_{\ell})=L_{[c_{\ell},d_{\ell}];\nu_{k}}(y_{\ell;j_{\ell}}),\quad\boldsymbol{V}_{\ell}\in\real^{n\times p},\quad 1\leq\ell\leq d,\\
\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{s})=k(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{t}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{s}}),\quad\boldsymbol{G}\in\real^{p^{d}\times p^{d}}.\end{gathered}

(3.5)

Substituting these into (
3.4
), we can reformulate it as

\boldsymbol{K}\approx\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{V}^{*},

(3.6)

where
\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{U}_{d}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\boldsymbol{U}_{1}\in\real^{n^{d}\times p^{d}}
and
\boldsymbol{V}=\boldsymbol{V}_{d}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\in\real^{n^{d}\times p^{d}}
.
The decomposition (
3.6
) serves as the low-rank representation of admissible leaf nodes in
\mathcal{H}
-matrices
[
22
,
23
]
, requiring a storage of
2n^{d}p^{d}+p^{2d}
.
Typically, the matrices
\boldsymbol{U}
and
\boldsymbol{V}
are required to be orthonormal, which can be achieved through QR factorizations on them, along with a modification of
\boldsymbol{G}
.
The overall complexity of this process is
\mathscr{O}(p^{2d}n^{d}+p^{3d})
.
The matrices
\boldsymbol{U}
and
\boldsymbol{V}
are called
basis matrices
and
\boldsymbol{G}
is called the
core matrix
of the low-rank decomposition.

However, because of the inherent tensor structure of points, it is more advantageous to represent (
3.4
) using a Tucker low-rank decomposition, rather than constructing
\boldsymbol{U}
and
\boldsymbol{V}
explicitly.
Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}
are
2d
-order tensors defined by

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}(i_{1},\dotsc,i_{d},j_{1},\dotsc,j_{d})=\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}),\quad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}(t_{1},\dotsc,t_{d},s_{1},\dotsc,s_{d})=\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{s}).

Using (
3.5
), we can express (
3.4
) as the following Tucker decomposition:

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}\approx\operatorname{Tucker}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}},\{\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{d},\{\boldsymbol{V}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{d}\bigr).

(3.7)

Compared with (
3.6
), the Tucker low-rank structure (
3.7
) maintains the same accuracy while revealing the essense of interpolation on tensor grids.
It is also more data-efficient, requiring only
2dnp+p^{2d}
storage.
If we ignore the common cost
p^{2d}
on the core part (
\boldsymbol{G}
or
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}
), the Tucker low-rank matrix (
3.7
) exhibits greater effectiveness, as the rest complexity,
2dnp
, scales linearly with the dimension
d
.
To meet the requirement of orthonormal factored matrices, we perform an additional orthogonalization step after the interpolation, as discussed in the end of Section
2.1
.
A detailed algorithm is postponed till Section
4.1
.
The overall complexity is
\mathscr{O}(dp^{2}n+dp^{2d+1})
.
Notably, the dominant term,
dp^{2}n
, exhibits linear dependence on
d
.

Remark 7
.

This paper frequently treats a matrix as a tensor, or vice versa.
To maintain conciseness, we adopt the agreement that when dealing with two tensor grids, the interaction can be represented as either a matrix or a
2d
-order tensor.
The relationship between the two is given by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}(i_{1},\dotsc,i_{d},j_{1},\dotsc,j_{d})=\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j})
.
At times, we may use matrix notation and tensor notation interchangeably to simplify our illustrations, and this usage should be correctly inferred from the context.

We end this section with an error estimate for the interpolation (
3.3
), which provides an estimate of the numerical rank of the Tucker decomposition for a specific class of kernel functions.
One important property of them is the
asymptotical smoothness
.

Definition 8
(Asympotically smoothness,
[
5
]
).

A kernel function
k\colon\real^{d}\times\real^{d}\to\real
is
said to be asymptotically smooth if there exists constants
C_{\mathrm{as}},\gamma>0
depending only on
k
satisfying

|\partial_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\partial_{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})|\leq C_{\mathrm{as}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\beta}\bigr)!\gamma^{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\frac{|k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})|}{\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|^{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|+|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{y},\ \boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

(3.8)

where
\partial_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\coloneq\partial_{x_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\dotsm\partial_{x_{d}}^{\alpha_{d}}
and

\partial_{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\coloneq\partial_{y_{1}}^{\beta_{1}}\dotsm\partial_{y_{d}}^{\beta_{d}}
denote the
multi-dimensional partial differential operators.

For example, it could be verified that kernels
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|^{-a}
and
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\log(\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|)
are asymptotically
smooth
[
23
]
.

Theorem 9
(Interpolation error for asymptotically smooth kernels).

Suppose
k
is an asymptotically smooth kernel defined on two boxes
B_{\tau}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}[a_{\ell},b_{\ell}]
and
B_{\sigma}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}[c_{\ell},d_{\ell}]
in
d
.
If
\operatorname{dist}(B_{\tau},B_{\sigma})\geq\eta\max\{\operatorname{diam}(B_{\tau}),\operatorname{diam}(B_{\sigma})\}
,
then the approximation error
r(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
of the Chebyshev interpolation (
3.3
), defined by

r(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{s}\in[p]^{d}}k(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{t}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{s}})L_{B_{\tau};\boldsymbol{t}}(\boldsymbol{x})L_{B_{\sigma};\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{y})-k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}),

has the following estimate

\|r(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\|_{\infty,B_{\tau}\times B_{\sigma}}\leq\frac{4C_{\mathrm{as}}\gamma^{p+1}\Lambda_{p}^{2d-1}d}{(4\eta)^{p+1}}\|k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\|_{\infty,B_{\tau}\times B_{\sigma}},

(3.9)

where
C_{\mathrm{as}}
and
\gamma
are the constants
in (
3.8
) and
\Lambda_{p}
is
the Lebesgue constant
in (
3.2
).

Proof.

The desired estimate follows directly from Lemma
6
and the property outlined in (
3.8
).

From the estimate of the Lebesgue constant
\Lambda_{p}
, for a fixed dimension
d
, the growth rate of the numerator in (
3.9
) with respect to
p
can be considered negligible compared to the denominator when
4\eta>\gamma
(a condition that is usually satisfied).
Consequently, Theorem
9
demonstrates that for an asymptotically smooth kernel, if
B_{\tau}
and
B_{\sigma}
are well-separated, the relative approximate error of Chebyshev interpolation decreases nearly exponentially as the interpolation order
p
increases.
Additionally, we deduce from (
3.9
) that in this case, the numerical rank of the interaction matrix remains independent of its size.

3.2
Hierarchical Tucker Low-Rank Matrices

Motived by the Tucker low-rank structure discussed in Section
3.1
, we propose the definition for hierarchical Tucker low-rank matrices.
Since our previous discussion relies on the tensor structure of the grid, we focus on the discretization (
1.3
) and require the cluster tree and block cluster tree to inherit the “tensor property” as well.

Definition 10
(Tensor node cluster tree).

Let
I=\bigl\{\boldsymbol{i}\colon\boldsymbol{i}\in[n]^{d}\bigr\}
be the index set associated with tensor grid points from the discretization (
1.3
).
A cluster tree
\mathsf{T}_{I}
is called a tensor node cluster tree if every node
\tau\in\mathsf{T}_{I}
can be expressed in the form
\tau=\tau_{1}\times\dotsb\times\tau_{d}
, where each
\tau_{\ell}
is the index set corresponding to the
\ell
-th dimension.

Definition 11
(Tensor node block cluster tree).

Suppose
\mathsf{T}_{I}
is a tensor node cluster tree with root index

I
. The tensor node block cluster tree is defined as the
block cluster tree
\mathsf{T}_{I\times I}
corresponding to

\mathsf{T}_{I}
and an admissibility condition.

One main property of the tensor node cluster tree is that
for every node, the corresponding points exhibit a tensor structure and its computational domain is a
d
-dimensional box.
Therefore, the submatrices can be approximated using Tucker low-rank decomposition.

Definition 12
(Hierarchical Tucker low-rank matrices, HTLR matrices).

Suppose
\mathsf{T}_{I\times I}
is the tensor node block cluster tree
with root
I
. We define a matrix
\boldsymbol{A}^{\operatorname{HTLR}}
as an HTLR
matrix (on
\mathsf{T}_{I\times I}
) of rank
p
if for every leaf

(\tau,\sigma)\in\mathsf{T}_{I\times I}
, the following conditions
hold:

•

If
(\tau,\sigma)
is an admissible leaf, then
\boldsymbol{A}^{\operatorname{HTLR}}_{\tau,\sigma}
is a Tucker low-rank (TLR) matrix of rank at most
p
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{A}^{\operatorname{HTLR}}_{\tau,\sigma}=\operatorname{Tucker}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}},\{\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{d},\{\boldsymbol{V}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{d}\bigr)
for some
\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}\in\real^{|\tau_{\ell}|\times p}
,
\boldsymbol{V}_{\ell}\in\real^{|\sigma_{\ell}|\times p}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}\in\real^{p\times\dotsb\times p}
.

•

If
(\tau,\sigma)
is an inadmissible leaf, then
\boldsymbol{A}^{\operatorname{HTLR}}_{\tau,\sigma}
is a dense matrix.

•

If
(\tau,\sigma)
is a non-leaf node, then
\boldsymbol{A}^{\operatorname{HTLR}}_{\tau,\sigma}
is a block matrix with blocks
\boldsymbol{A}^{\operatorname{HTLR}}_{\tau^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}
, where
(\tau^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})\in\operatorname{children}(\tau,\sigma)
.

From the relationship (
3.6
) and (
3.7
), the conventional low-rank representation of rank
p^{d}
achieves the same accuracy as the TLR representation of rank
p
when both are derived from high-dimensional interpolation.
Using the storage cost of TLR matrices discussed in Section
3.1
, the storage complexity for an HTLR matrix is linear, which is stated in the following proposition.

Proposition 13
(Storage of HTLR matrices under weak admissibility).

For a fixed dimension
d\geq 2
and
N_{0}=n_{0}^{d}
. Let
I=\{\boldsymbol{i}\colon\boldsymbol{i}\in[n]^{d}\}
be the index set
corresponding to the tensor grid points from the
discretization (
1.3
) and
N=n^{d}
. Suppose the
tensor node cluster tree
\mathsf{T}_{I}
is constructed by a
2^{d}

partition whose every leaf node contains fewer than
N_{0}
points and

\mathsf{T}_{I\times I}
is the corresponding tensor node block cluster
tree under weak admissibility condition. If
\boldsymbol{A}^{\operatorname{HTLR}}
is
an HTLR matrix of rank
p
and
p\leq n_{0}\leq 2p
, then the
storage required for
\boldsymbol{A}^{\operatorname{HTLR}}
is
\mathscr{O}(p^{d}N)
, where
the prefactor depends only on
d
.

1

1

From now on, for every
complexity estimate, the prefactor is assumed to depend only on

d
.

Proof.

Withou loss of generality, assume
N=n^{d}=2^{dL}n_{0}^{d}
, where

L
is the maximum level in
\mathsf{T}_{I}
. For each level
0\leq\ell\leq L
, note that the matrix size corresponding to each node,
the number of nodes, inadmissible and admissible blocks of a node are
bounded by
N/2^{d\ell}

2^{d\ell}
and
1

2^{d}-1

N

denoted by
S^{\operatorname{HTLR}}(N)

\displaystyle S^{\operatorname{HTLR}}(N)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}2^{d\ell}(2^{d}-1)\bigl(2dn_{0}2^{L-\ell}p+p^{2d}\bigr)+2^{dL}(N/2^{dL})^{2}

(3.10)

\displaystyle\leq 8dpn_{0}\frac{N}{n_{0}^{d}}+p^{2d}\frac{N}{n_{0}^{d}}+n_{0}^{d}N\leq\biggl(16dp^{2-d}+p^{d}+2^{d}p^{d}\biggr)N=\mathscr{O}\bigl(p^{d}N\bigr),

where the prefactor depends only on

.

Since the only difference between an
\mathcal{H}
-matrix and an HTLR matrix is the representation of low-rank submatrices, by a discussion analogous to that in the proof of Proposition

\mathcal{H}
-matrix of size

is

\displaystyle S^{\mathcal{H}}(N)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{O}\bigl(p^{d}N\log_{2}(N)+p^{d}N\bigr).

Comparing (

\mathcal{H}
-matrices.
Furthermore, examining the distinct components of the exact storage complexity expression reveals that the first term for HTLR matrices has a smaller coefficient that depends only on the dimension
d
.
In contrast, the coefficient for
\mathcal{H}
-matrices grows exponentially with
d
and logarithmically with

.
Therefore, HTLR matrix also exhibits better asymptotic performance.
Finally, it is remarkable that for the HTLR matrix, the main cost is the storage of Tucker core tensors, while that of the
\mathcal{H}

Similar results and dissussions can be obtained under strong admissibility.

3.3
HTLR Matrices on the Quasi-Uniform Grid

The HTLR matrix proposed in Section
3.2
requires a (uniform) tensor grid.
However, in certain scenarios, functions are sampled from a quasi-uniform grid instead.
In this section we show how HTLR matrices can be appied to the IE (
1.1
) using a discretization on the quasi-uniform grid.
As with the non-uniform fast Fourier transform
[
4
,
20
]
, the key tool is the interpolation between the tensor grid and the quasi-uniform grid.

Suppose quasi-uniform grid points are given by
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with non-overlapping computational domains
\{\Omega_{i}^{\mathrm{qu}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
,satisfying
\Omega=\cup_{i=1}^{N}\Omega_{i}^{\mathrm{qu}}
.
An example of this can be seen in the triangulation of
\Omega
from the finite element method, where each
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is the the center of the triangular domain, as illustrated in Figure
3.1
.
The discretization in this case is expressed as follows:

a(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})u_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}K_{i,j}\bigl|\Omega_{j}^{\mathrm{qu}}\bigr|u_{j}=f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),\quad i=1,\dotsc,N,

where

K_{i,j}\coloneq\begin{cases}k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}),&j\neq i,\\
\int_{\Omega_{j}}k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}\big/\bigl|\Omega_{j}^{\mathrm{qu}}\bigr|,&j=i.\end{cases}

This formulation can be viewed as applying the collection method using piecewise-constant basis functions
\bigl\{\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{i}^{\mathrm{qu}}}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr\}_{i}
.

Figure 3.1
:
Triangulation of
[0,1]^{2}
. The edges of each triangle are
colored blue, and the centers are colored orange and marked with star
“
*
”.

Throughout this section, we assume the vector
\boldsymbol{u}
in the forward computation is sampled from a smooth function
u(\boldsymbol{x})
, i.e.,
u_{i}=u(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
.
To utilize the HTLR matrix, a finer uniform tensor grid is prepared.
Let
m
denote the number of points in each direction, resulting in the uniform grid denoted by
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{m^{d}}
with computational domains
\{\Omega_{t}^{\mathrm{uni}}\}_{t=1}^{m^{d}}
.
Define
M=m^{d}
as the number of uniform grid points and let
\boldsymbol{\hat{A}}
represent the matrix defined by (
1.3
) on the uniform grid.
We introduce two interpolation matrices
\boldsymbol{T}:\real^{M}\to\real^{N}
and
\boldsymbol{S}:\real^{N}\to\real^{M}
, that facilitate the relationship between the quasi-uniform grid and the uniform grid, such that the following approximation holds:

\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{u}\approx\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{\hat{A}}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{u},

(3.12)

Thus, the forward evaluation
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{u}
can be approximately computed as
\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{\hat{A}}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{u}
.
The equation (
3.12
) can be interpreted as the following three steps:
We first interpolate a function on the quasi-uniform grid to the uniform tensor grid by multiplying the interpolation matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
.
Next, we perform the matrix-vector multiplication on the uniform tensor grid using
\boldsymbol{\hat{A}}
.
Finally, we interpolate the result from the uniform grid to the quasi-uniform grid by acting
\boldsymbol{T}
on it.
Since
\boldsymbol{\hat{A}}
can be compressed into an HTLR matrix, its matrix-vector multiplication can be computed efficiently (see Section
4.2
).
On the other hand, as will be shown later, the matrices
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{S}
are local interpolation matrix and, therefore, sparse.
By combing these observations, we conclude that the matrix-vector multiplication can be performed efficiently on the quasi-uniform grid.
If we replace
\boldsymbol{\hat{A}}
in (
3.12
) with its HTLR approximation
\boldsymbol{\hat{A}}^{\operatorname{HTLR}}
, this can also be interpreted as a data-sparse representation of
\boldsymbol{A}
.

We discuss the choice of
M
and the construction of
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{S}
.
The selection of
M
is a trade-off between accuracy and efficiency:
A large
M
improves the accuracy but may reduce the efficiency.
Typically,
M\approx\rho^{d}N
is sufficient where
\rho\geq 1
is a small constant.
Relevant numerical results are presented in Section
5.4
.
To construct the interpolation matrices, we adopt the perspective of the Garlarkin method.
Every vector
\boldsymbol{u}\in\real^{N}
on the quasi-uniform grid
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is interpreted as a piecewise-constant function defined by

u^{\mathrm{qu}}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneq\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{i}^{\mathrm{qu}}}(\boldsymbol{x}).

For a given uniform tensor grid
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{M}
, the task is to determine the coefficients
\{\hat{u}_{t}\}
such that

\displaystyle u^{\mathrm{qu}}(\boldsymbol{x})\approx u^{\mathrm{uni}}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneq\sum_{t=1}^{M}\hat{u}_{t}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{t}^{\mathrm{uni}}}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(3.13)

By taking the inner product with
\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{t}^{\mathrm{uni}}}(\boldsymbol{x})
on both sides, we obtain

\hat{u}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{|\Omega_{t}^{\mathrm{uni}}\cap\Omega_{i}^{\mathrm{qu}}|}{|\Omega_{t}^{\mathrm{uni}}|}u_{i},

which means that the interpolation matrix from quasi-uniform grid to
uniform grid is given by
\boldsymbol{S}_{t,i}=|\Omega_{t}^{\mathrm{uni}}\cap\Omega_{i}^{\mathrm{qu}}|/|\Omega_{t}^{\mathrm{uni}}|
. Similarly, the the
interpolation matrix
\boldsymbol{T}
from uniform grid to quasi-uniform
grid is given by
\boldsymbol{T}_{i,t}=|\Omega_{i}^{\mathrm{qu}}\cap\Omega_{t}^{\mathrm{uni}}|/|\Omega_{i}^{\mathrm{qu}}|
. Since each region only
intersects with few neighboring regions, both
\boldsymbol{T}
and

\boldsymbol{S}
are sparse, with the number of nonzeros approximately

\mathscr{O}(\max\{M,N\})=\mathscr{O}(M)
.

4
Construction and Application of HTLR Matrices

This section focuses on the construction and application (matrix-vector multiplication) of HTLR matrices.
Generally speaking, operations adhere follow a similar framework to those of
\mathcal{H}
-matrices, with the exception of a distinct step related to admissible leaf nodes.
Throughout this section, we assume that
\mathsf{T}_{I\times I}
is a given tensor node block cluster tree associated with a uniform grid.

4.1
Construction of HTLR Matrices

The construction of the HTLR matrix
\boldsymbol{A}^{\operatorname{HTLR}}
directly follows the process outlined in Definition
12
.
More specifically, let
(\tau,\sigma)
be the node we are currently working on.
This can be categorized into three cases:

•

(\tau,\sigma)
is an admissible leaf node:
In this case, computational domains
\Omega_{\tau}
and
\Omega_{\sigma}
are non-overlapping and
\boldsymbol{A}_{\tau,\sigma}
is a TLR matrix constructed from the kernel function
k
.
Specifically, we first construct the TLR matrix

\boldsymbol{A}_{\tau,\sigma}=\operatorname{Tucker}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}},\{\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{d},\{\boldsymbol{V}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{d}\bigr)
by the
d
-dimensional interpolation of the kernel matrix as (
3.5
), followed by a sequential QR to have each
\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{\ell}
orthonormal, then multiply the core tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}
by
h^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{R}
factors from the QR factorization.

•

4.2
Application of HTLR Matrices

Using the hierarchical structure,
the application, or matrix-vector multiplication, of HTLR matrices can also be computed efficiently.
Starting at
(I,I)
, let

\boldsymbol{f}_{\tau}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{f}_{\tau}+\boldsymbol{A}^{\operatorname{HTLR}}_{\tau,\sigma}\boldsymbol{u}_{\sigma}.

There are three cases depending on the type of the node

•

is an admissible leaf:
In this case, assume the TLR representation of

\boldsymbol{f}_{\tau}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{f}_{\tau}+(\boldsymbol{U}_{d}\otimes\dotsb\otimes\boldsymbol{U}_{1})\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{V}_{d}\otimes\dotsb\otimes\boldsymbol{V}_{1})^{*}\boldsymbol{u}_{\sigma},

\boldsymbol{G}

7
).
The product of Kronecker product of a matrix series and vector is computed as follows.
Consider the calculation of

Proposition 15
(Complexity of HTLR application).

For both weak and strong admissible conditions, the matrix-vector
multiplication complexity for a rank-
p
HTLR matrix is
\mathscr{O}(pN\log N+p^{d}N)
.

For a rank-
p^{d}

\mathcal{H}
-matrix of size
N
, the complexity of matrix-vector multiplication of is
\mathscr{O}(p^{d}N\log N+p^{d}N)
.
Specifically, for both HTLR matrices and
\mathcal{H}
-matrices, the computations involving dense matrices in inadmissible leaves incur the same cost,
\mathscr{O}(p^{d}N)
.
Similarly, the complexity for computations associated with core matrices or tensors in admissible leaves is also
\mathscr{O}(p^{d}N)
.

\mathcal{H}
-matrices is
\mathscr{O}(p^{d}N\log N)
.
From the analysis, we conclude that HTLR matrices have a significantly smaller prefactor in the leading complexity, and are more efficient than
\mathcal{H}
-matrices when applied to a vector.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we introduce hierarchical Tucker low-rank matrices and present the corresponding algorithms for construction and matrix-vector multiplication.
When the underlying discretization exhibits a tensor structure, HTLR matrices are generally more efficient than
\mathcal{H}
-matrices.
We establish that the complexity for storage and construction is
\mathscr{O}(N)
, while the complexity for application is
\mathscr{O}(N\log N)
.
Compared to
\mathcal{H}
-matrices, the prefactors of the dominant terms are smaller and usually exhibit a linear dependence on the dimension rather than an exponential dependence.
Furthermore, we demonstrate the application of HTLR matrices on quasi-uniform grids, enhancing their applicability. We also discuss the relevance of Tucker low-rank structures.
Numerical results show that HTLR matrices usually save
3
to
10
times in memory and provide speedups of
2
to
6
times compared to
\mathcal{H}
-matrices.

There are several future directions to consider.
One potential avenue is to develop additional algebraic operations for HTLR matrices, including matrix addition, multiplication, and LU decomposition.
Furthermore, exploring the parallelization of these algorithms could enhance their efficiency and scalability, enabling them to accommodate larger problem sizes effectively.
