Closure of chronic oroantral fistula: Comparison between pedicled buccal pad fat flap and free buccal pad fat graft
Authors: Shehab A. Hamad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and Objective:Oroantral communication and fistula are not uncommon complications of maxillary molar extraction. The objective of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of buccal fat flaps and grafts in the closure of oroantral fistulae.Materials and Methods:This prospective comparative clinical study involved patients with chronic oroantral fistulae. In 13 patients, a pedicled buccal fat pad flap was used for closure (group 1), while a free buccal fat graft was used in 14 patients (group 2). Parameters assessed included the duration of surgery, extent of facial swelling, loss of vestibular depth, and wound breakdown and failure of closure.Results:The mean duration of surgery was 32.4 (10.5) minutes in group 1 and 29.8 (8.6) minutes in group 2. The amount of facial swelling on the second postoperative day was 2.5 (±0.6) cm in group 1 and 1.3 (±0.8) cm in group 2. At two weeks postoperatively, the reduction in vestibular depth was 5.2 (±1.7) mm in group 1 and 3.7 (±0.9) mm in group 2. Significantly greater facial swelling and reduction in vestibular depth were observed in group 1 compared to group 2 (P = 0.00). While one case in group 1 and two cases in group 2 experienced failure with fistula reestablishment, this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.58).Conclusion:When employed as a free graft or pedicled flap, the buccal fat pad has demonstrated success in closing oroantral fistulas, even when dealing with large defects. This technique can be utilized either independently or in conjunction with a buccal vestibular flap to achieve a double-layer closure, enhancing the effectiveness of the procedure.

INTRODUCTION

Oroantral communication (OAC) is the term used to describe an abnormal connection between the oral and antral cavities. The epithelialization of a communication results in the establishment of an oroantral fistula (OAF).[1] Due to the close anatomical relationship between the sinus floor and the root apices of posterior maxillary teeth, these communications usually occur after the extraction of maxillary premolars and molars. A fistula may also occur as a result of trauma, benign or malignant tumor excision, or treatment of maxillary cysts.[2]

The primary clinical manifestations after oroantral communication (OAC) forms involve the passage of air and fluids into the nose and mouth. Clinically, the clinician may observe blood bubbles within the defect, or the patient may detect air leakage when blowing with closed nostrils. Common complaints from patients include an unpleasant salty discharge into the mouth from the opening, the presence of odor, and the reflux of fluids and foods into the nose from the mouth.[3] Additionally, the leakage of air can sometimes hinder activities such as smoking. Larger defects may result in patients experiencing changes in sound resonance and speech difficulties,[4] popping out of the antral polyp into the oral cavity, and sinusitis.[5]

The primary objective in managing OAC or fistulas is to achieve tight closure of the defect, aiming to prevent the regurgitation of oral bacteria and food debris into the sinus. The introduction of these oral contaminants into the sinus can lead to infection or inflammation, potentially impacting ciliary function, sinus isolation, causing congestion, and even resulting in sinusitis.[3]

The closure of the oroantral fistula (OAF) is a technique-sensitive and challenging surgery. Different techniques are available for closure, but regardless of the chosen method, the success of the surgical procedure hinges on the thorough removal of the fistulous tract and the complete elimination of any sinus pathology or infection. The key determinants for selecting the appropriate surgical approach for OAF closure include the size and location of the defect.[6]

Typically, techniques of OAF repair use adjacent autogenous soft tissue flaps derived from buccal or palatal tissues, buccal fat pads, and tongue flaps. Additional techniques include the use of bone grafts and synthetic materials like hydroxyapatite, soft polymethylmethacrylate, resorbable collagen membranes, gold plate, and golden foil.[78]

A pedicled buccal fat pad (BFP) flap was first popularized by Egyedi.[9] This fat mass, when used as a pedicled flap, offers a consistent and reliable blood supply. An individual’s BFP size is unaffected by changes in body weight and fat distribution. It is positioned close to the surgical site, which minimizes donor-site morbidity, shortens the operating time, and makes it easy to harvest and mobilize. It also has an excellent blood supply. It usually takes two to three weeks for complete epithelialization to occur. Furthermore, the depth of the buccal sulcus remains unaffected by this method, negating the possible disadvantage of decreased vestibular depth linked to buccal advancement flap restoration.[10]

The aim of this study was to evaluate the outcomes of closing oroantral communications and fistulas with BFP flaps.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This observational prospective clinical study was conducted at a governmental hospital, during a period of 10 years, from April 2013 to February 2024 on patients presented with chronic OAF. The study was strictly adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki (Revised 2013) developed by the World Medical Association. The study was also revised and accepted by our institutional board. Ethical clearance was obtained from KHCMS ethical committee Ref no. 234 dated 23.10.2024. The patients were thoroughly briefed on the utilization of BFP for closure, along with its associated complications. They were also informed about alternative management options, inclusive of their respective advantages and disadvantages. A patient who agreed to participate has signed an informed consent form. The patients were randomly assigned into two groups:

Group 1: Pedicled buccal fat pad flap.

Group 2: Free buccal pad fat graft.

Criteria for inclusion included chronic OAF persisting over an extended period, defect size exceeding 6 mm, positive nose-blowing test, and history of tooth extraction, regardless of age and gender.

Criteria for exclusion were patients with medical conditions that compromise health (above ASA 2), preexisting sinus conditions, history of previous sinus surgery, and patients currently experiencing sinus infection.

Surgical technique

The majority of cases, barring two exceptions, underwent surgery using local anesthesia containing 2% lignocaine with 1:100,000 adrenaline. Two patients underwent surgery under general anesthesia. One of them required the Caldwell-Luc procedure for the removal of root from the sinus, while another patient opted for general anesthesia due to personal preference.

After excising the granulation tissue from within the fistulous tract with the No. 11 scalpel blade, the wound margins were excised and refreshed, and then, a buccal mucoperiosteal flap with four corners was raised. At the depth of the flap, a 1 cm incision in the periosteum was made in the region of the second molar, just behind the zygomatic buttress. The buccal fat pad (BFP) was carefully exposed by blunt dissection by inserting a small mosquito forceps into the periosteal incision.

In group 1, the pedicled fat pad graft was carefully moved to cover the alveolar defect, and a 4/0 vicryl suture was used to create a tension-free closure [Figure 1].

Closure of oroantral fistula with pedicled buccal fat pad flap

In group 2, the buccal fat graft was severed at its base to obtain a free graft, which was packed into the fistula and secured with sutures [Figure 2].

Closure of oroantral fistula with free buccal pad fat graft

The initially elevated buccal advancement flap was then retracted to guarantee a tension-free closure and create a second lining on top of the defect.

Immediate postoperative instructions were provided, advising patients to adhere to a soft diet and to refrain from vigorous oral rinsing, sneezing with a closed mouth, or blowing through their nose for two weeks. Patients were also instructed to avoid smoking for at least 3 months. Systemic antibiotics, analgesics, and nasal decongestants were prescribed for 1 week postoperatively.

Facial swelling was estimated on the second postoperative day by subtracting preoperative from postoperative measurement of tragus-pogonium and tragus-subnasale, by a tape measure laid on the skin. Using a ruler, the reduction of vestibular depth was measured on the second postoperative week by subtracting the postoperative from the preoperative measurement.

Patients were clinically evaluated using the nose blow test at four weeks following surgery to assess the effectiveness of the procedure. A negative nose blow test, along with complete epithelialization and the absence of any continuity defects, indicated a successful outcome of the flap treatment. Because detection of small perforations is not always possible, fogging of a mouth mirror placed at the orifice has also been used to confirm the wound dehiscence and reestablishment of the fistula.

Data was analyzed using IBM SPSS software package version 20.0. (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Chi-square test and Student’s “t” test were used for statistical comparison. The significance of the obtained results was judged at the 5% level.

DISCUSSION

The main objectives of treating OAFs are to completely close the defect and prevent food particles and oral bacteria from entering the sinus cavity. OAF increases the chance of nasal contamination, leading to chronic sinusitis and impaired healing. Fistulae exceeding 5 mm in diameter or persisting beyond a three-week period seldom close on their own and typically necessitate surgical intervention.[11]

Two fundamental principles must be taken into account when performing operations for OAFs/OACs. The initial principle involves ensuring the sinus is devoid of any infection with sufficient nasal drainage. The second principle emphasizes tension-free closure, entailing a broad-based, well-vascularized soft tissue flap covering the defect. Achieving successful closure of the OAFs necessitates prior elimination of all sinus pathology, including the fistulous tract, sinus infection, degenerated mucosa, and diseased bone.[12] In our study, patients were treated conservatively before surgery to eliminate sinus infection with antibiotics, nasal decongestants, excision of herniated mucosa, and thorough daily irrigation with warm normal saline.

Introduced by Rehrman in 1936,[13] the buccal advancement flap is regarded as one of the oldest and commonly used techniques for closing OAC/OAF. High success rates have been reported in the literature, attributed to the ample vascularization provided by the wide base of the flap.[14] Although the Rehrman method boasts high success rates, von Wowern[15] has demonstrated that in half of the cases, there is a permanent reduction in sulcus depth following the procedure. While this flap technique proves effective for treating small to medium-sized communications, it is contraindicated for defects located in the palatal region that necessitate extensive flap sliding, leading to even more obliteration of the buccal sulcus.

The use of a pedicled palatal flap offers an alternative approach to addressing significant communications without compromising the vestibular depth. However, this procedure entails notable morbidity due to the denuded area it creates in the palate.[16]

In the present study, 13 patients with OAF were treated with pedicled BPF flap and 14 other patients were treated with free BPF graft. The success rate of the two flaps was nearly identical; however, the reduction in sulcus depth was significantly higher in the cases treated with pedicled BPF flap. Hanazawa et al.[17] treated 14 patients with OAF using the BPF flap, achieving successful closure of the defect in 13 patients. Similarly, Park et al.[18] treated 25 OAF patients with the BFP flap, achieving closure in 23 patients (92%). Esen and Akkulah[19] treated seven patients with OAF induced by bisphosphonate-related maxillary osteonecrosis by performing sequestrectomy, platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), and BFP flap and observed that the fistula was successfully closed in all patients. Jain et al.[20] treated 15 patients with OAF using a BFP flap with the closure of all fistulas obtained; however, they needed closure of large defects by covering the BFP with a buccal advancement flap to achieve a two-layer closure. Also, Scott et al.[21] observed that even when the BFP was used alone in the treatment of OAC, the healing was uneventful with a 100% success rate in the 50 cases in which the procedure was conducted. Daif[22] treated 25 patients with OAF with BFP flap and noted a 100% success rate with no postoperative breakdown or liquefaction necrosis and no complications during the 10-year follow-up.

Autogenous free fat grafting is gaining increasing utility in cosmetic surgery, with ample documentation indicating that the primary healing mechanism of free buccal pad fat graft involves fibrosis of the adipose tissue.[23] Kablan and Laster were the first to document the utilization of free buccal fat grafts with bone augmentation. In their report, they highlighted the predominant healing process of free buccal pad fat graft, which involves fibrosis of the graft.[24] Free buccal fat graft was also successfully used in the regenerative treatment of periimplantitis[25] and in the closure of soft-tissue defects and dehiscence in the hard palate.[26]

The free buccal pad fat graft has a number of benefits over the buccal pad fat flap as a soft-tissue graft in the oral cavity, including. Since there is no loss of fat tissue for the pedicle, less fat volume should be removed and following the procedure, mouth opening is unrestricted. In addition, there is no change in the vestibular depths and there is no concern about injury to the transplant in dentate patients. The primary benefit of the free graft is that it may be used in any area of the oral cavity.[26]

The limitations of this study are the small sample size and the short duration of follow-up period as well as the lack of radiographic imaging in the follow-up period to assess the sinus condition and bone formation at the floor of the sinus.

CONCLUSION

Buccal fat pad when used as a free graft or pedicled flap is successful in the closure of oroantral fistula, even of large defects. It can be used alone or in combination with a buccal vestibular flap to provide a double-layer closure.
