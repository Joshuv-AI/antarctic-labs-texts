Understanding AI Evaluation Patterns:
How Different GPT Models Assess Vision-Language Descriptions

Abstract




Abstract

As AI systems increasingly evaluate other AI outputs, understanding their assessment behavior becomes crucial for preventing cascading biases. This study analyzes vision-language descriptions generated by NVIDIA’s Describe Anything Model and evaluated by three GPT variants (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-5) to uncover distinct "evaluation personalities" the underlying assessment strategies and biases each model demonstrates. GPT-4o-mini exhibits systematic consistency with minimal variance, GPT-4o excels at error detection, while GPT-5 shows extreme conservatism with high variability. Controlled experiments using Gemini 2.5 Pro as an independent question generator validate that these personalities are inherent model properties rather than artifacts. Cross-family analysis through semantic similarity of generated questions reveals significant divergence: GPT models cluster together with high similarity while Gemini exhibits markedly different evaluation strategies. All GPT models demonstrate a consistent 2:1 bias favoring negative assessment over positive confirmation, though this pattern appears family-specific rather than universal across AI architectures. These findings suggest that evaluation competence does not scale with general capability and that robust AI assessment requires diverse architectural perspectives.

1
Introduction

AI systems now routinely evaluate the outputs of other AI systems from code reviews to content moderation to training data curation. This recursive evaluation creates a critical dependency: biases and limitations in AI evaluators can amplify through successive generations of models trained on AI-assessed data. Yet the fundamental understanding of how different AI architectures conceptualize quality, what systematic biases they exhibit, and whether architectural advances improve or degrade evaluation consistency has not been studied extensively.

This study tackles the challenge by systematically analyzing AI evaluation behavior through 762 image descriptions generated by NVIDIA’s state-of-the-art DAM model. These outputs were assessed by three GPT-based judges GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, and GPT-5. Our analysis uncovers distinct “evaluation personalities” in each model, revealing their characteristic assessment strategies, inherent biases, and areas of emphasis when performing evaluation tasks.

NVIDIA’s Describe Anything Model, provides the perfect foundation for evaluation analysis. The 3-billion parameter model achieves 67.3% accuracy on DLC-Bench, a new technique for assessing the quality of AI generated descriptions, and employs innovative focal prompt technology that combines full image context with high-resolution crops of target regions
(
Lian et al. 2025
)
. DAM addresses the fundamental challenge of generating detailed and accurate descriptions for specific regions in images and videos through two key innovations: the
focal prompt
, which ensures high-resolution encoding of targeted regions while preserving global context, and the
localized vision backbone
, which integrates precise localization with broader context using gated cross-attention adapters.

The landscape of AI-based evaluation has undergone fundamental transformation recently. Research shows that LLM-based assessment models like CLAIR achieve correlations of 0.604 to 0.627 with human judgment on image caption evaluation, outperforming traditional metrics like SPICE (0.449) and approaching but not reaching inter-human agreement levels (0.736). While these models show strong performance, they face challenges with non-determinism and occasional parsing failures, though variance between runs remains relatively small.
(
Chen et al. 2024a
)
. This variability suggests that different AI models may emphasize different aspects of assessment, making it crucial to understand their evaluation patterns rather than simply measuring their accuracy.

Large language models (LLMs) and multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have shown considerable promise as evaluators, or "LLM-as-a-Judge," providing scalable, flexible assessment capabilities across modalities
(
Chen et al. 2024a
;
Wei 2022
)
. However, studies reveal that despite impressive human-like discernment in pairwise comparisons, significant challenges persist, including biases, hallucinations, and inconsistencies, particularly in scoring and batch ranking tasks
(
Chen et al. 2024a
)
. These findings highlight the need for rigorous evaluation frameworks and bias-aware assessment methodologies tailored to LLM and MLLM judging capabilities.

Our methodology employs multiple experimental controls to ensure robust findings. In addition to having each GPT model generate and answer its own evaluation questions, we conduct a controlled experiment using Gemini 2.5 Pro as an independent question generator. This dual approach isolates pure evaluation behavior from question-generation capabilities, validating that the observed "evaluation personalities" are inherent model properties. Furthermore, our semantic similarity analysis of generated questions reveals systematic patterns in how different model families conceptualize quality assessment, with significant divergence between GPT and Gemini approaches despite consistent asymmetry between positive verification and negative detection strategies.

Rather than ranking model capabilities, this research uncovers how different AI architectures conceptualize and approach systematic evaluation, what aspects they prioritize, and what this reveals about the nature of AI assessment behavior. Our dual-assessment methodology provides a template for analyzing AI assessment patterns that captures both systematic precision and holistic understanding approaches, leveraging recent insights into evaluation granularity and vision-language model development
(
Lian et al. 2025
)
.

Sentence-BERT
(
Reimers and Gurevych 2019b
)

by measuring semantic similarity, but they remain limited in interpretability

Recently,
Large Language Models as evaluators
have emerged as a

(

employs GPT-4 in a

CLIP-based metrics. CLAIR not only provides scores but also textual
justifications, offering interpretability beyond traditional metrics.
Extensions such as CLAIR-A
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
demonstrate similar gains
in audio captioning evaluation. More broadly, GPT-4V and Gemini
models
(
Yang et al. 2023
;
Team et al. 2023
)
have been shown to act as
reliable zero-shot judges in multimodal tasks, with consistency rivaling
human annotators.

This line of research has influenced training regimes as well: preference
optimization frameworks now incorporate LLM-based evaluation signals to
reward desirable model outputs
(
OpenAI 2024
)
. The trend toward
“evaluation by reasoning” marks a shift from surface-level metrics to
context-aware, interpretable, and human-aligned assessment, positioning LLMs
as central evaluators in the multimodal era.

Methodology

3.1
Workflow Steps

Our experimental methodology employs a systematic multi-stage pipeline designed to comprehensively analyze evaluation patterns across different GPT architectures when assessing vision-language descriptions. The workflow, illustrated in Figure
1
, consists of seven interconnected stages that progressively transform raw visual data into quantitative insights about AI evaluation behavior.

Figure 1
:
Complete evaluation methodology workflow. The pipeline progresses from dataset preparation through description generation, evaluation question formulation, dual assessment paradigms, scoring computation, and culminates in pattern analysis and bias identification across three GPT model variants.

3.1.1
Stage 1: Dataset Preparation and Preprocessing

The initial stage involves the curation and preparation of image samples from the Dataset. Each sample undergoes preprocessing to extract human-annotated semantic segmentation masks, multi-tier textual descriptions (including titles, narrative descriptions, and technical scene analyses), and associated metadata. This stage ensures data consistency and establishes the ground truth baseline against which model-generated descriptions will be evaluated.

3.1.2
Stage 2: Vision-Language Description Generation

We leverage NVIDIA’s Describe Anything Model (DAM-3B)
(
Lian et al. 2025
)
, a vision-language model specifically designed for generating detailed descriptions of both full images and masked regions, to create rich textual annotations for our dataset. For each image in the DataSeeds validation set, we employ a two-stage description generation process: first, we generate a comprehensive scene-level description of the entire image focusing on environmental context, lighting, camera angles, and visual style (20-80 words); second, we iterate through each human-annotated segmentation mask, converting the polygon coordinates to binary masks and feeding them to DAM to generate object-specific descriptions. The model processes these inputs using a temperature of 0.2 and top-p of 0.5 to ensure consistent, focused outputs while maintaining descriptive quality. This approach allows us to augment the existing human annotations with AI-generated descriptions that capture both holistic scene understanding and fine-grained object details, creating a multi-level semantic representation where each segmented object receives its own contextual description within the broader scene narrative. The streaming generation pipeline processes the entire dataset with automatic checkpointing for robustness, ultimately producing a comprehensive JSON structure containing both scene-level and object-level descriptions paired with the original segmentation masks.

3.1.3
Stage 3: Evaluation Question Synthesis

The third stage involves the automatic generation of evaluation questions by each GPT judge (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, and GPT-5). Using the human-generated ground truth descriptions as reference, each model independently synthesizes two categories of questions: (i) positive questions that verify the presence of specific attributes that should appear in accurate descriptions, and (ii) negative questions that check for the absence of incorrect or hallucinatory elements. This bidirectional questioning strategy enables comprehensive assessment of both descriptive accuracy and error avoidance.

3.1.4
Stage 4: Dual Assessment Paradigm

The fourth stage implements our dual assessment framework, executing two parallel evaluation streams. The question-based assessment utilizes the synthesized questions to systematically evaluate DAM descriptions through binary verification of specific attributes. Simultaneously, the holistic assessment evaluates three continuous dimensions: accuracy (factual correctness), completeness (coverage of key attributes), and hallucination penalty (presence of unsupported information). This parallel architecture captures both granular and gestalt aspects of description quality.

3.1.5
Stage 5: Quantitative Score Computation

In the fifth stage, we apply the mathematical formulations detailed in Section 2.2 to transform raw evaluation responses into standardized numerical scores. This includes computing weighted averages for positive and negative question responses, normalizing holistic assessment dimensions, and calculating the final combined score through our weighted integration formula. The standardization ensures comparability across different models and evaluation paradigms.

3.1.6
Stage 6: Cross-Model Pattern Analysis

The sixth stage performs comparative analysis across the three GPT models to identify distinctive evaluation patterns. This involves analysis of score distributions, and identification of model-specific emphasis areas. We employ visualization techniques including box plots, radar charts, and scatter plots to reveal assessment consistency, variance patterns, and inter-method dependencies.

This comprehensive workflow enables us to move beyond simple performance metrics to understand the underlying mechanisms and biases that govern how different AI architectures approach the complex task of evaluating vision-language descriptions.

This framework combines granular attribute verification with holistic quality assessment, providing a comprehensive evaluation paradigm that captures both local and global aspects of descriptive accuracy.

3.2.1
Holistic Evaluation

The holistic evaluation component implements a three-dimensional assessment framework where LLM judges directly evaluate descriptions along orthogonal quality dimensions. We leverage the semantic understanding capabilities of LLMs to assess quality through natural language prompting. Each dimension is evaluated as a continuous score in the unit interval
[0,1]
, enabling nuanced quality quantification beyond binary classification.

Three-Dimensional Assessment Framework:

Let
{D}
represent the DAM-generated description and
{H}
represent the human reference description. The LLM judge is prompted to evaluate three dimensions:

•

Accuracy

\in[0,1]
:
Factual correctness of stated details

The LLM assesses how factually correct the details in the AI description are by evaluating:

–

Whether colors, quantities, object types, and spatial relationships are correct

–

The presence of factual errors or misidentifications

•

Completeness

\in[0,1]
:
Coverage of key reference attributes

The LLM measures how comprehensively the AI description covers the human references by examining:

–

Whether main subjects and key details are mentioned

–

Whether important technical aspects (camera angle, mood, setting) are captured

•

Hallucination Penalty

\in[0,1]
:
Incorrect or unsupported information penalty

The LLM quantifies the presence of unsupported information by identifying:

–

Details added that are not mentioned in human descriptions

–

Claims that contradict the human references

LLM Prompting Strategy:

Each LLM judge receives a structured prompt requesting evaluation scores for the three dimensions. The prompt explicitly defines the evaluation criteria and score ranges, ensuring consistent assessment across samples. Formally, the LLM evaluation function can be represented as:

{E}_{\text{LLM}}:({D},{H})\rightarrow\{{A},{C},{P}\}

(1)

where
{A}
,
{C}
, and
{P}
represent the accuracy, completeness, and hallucination penalty scores respectively, each constrained to
[0,1]
.

Overall Holistic Score Formulation:

Given the three dimension scores produced by the LLM judge, the overall holistic assessment score
S_{\text{holistic}}
is computed as a weighted linear combination with penalty adjustment:

S_{\text{holistic}}=\omega_{A}\cdot{A}+\omega_{C}\cdot{C}-\omega_{P}\cdot{P}

(2)

where:

•

\omega_{A}=0.6
(accuracy weight)

•

\omega_{C}=0.4
(completeness weight)

•

\omega_{P}=1.0
(hallucination penalty weight)

The final score is normalized to the unit interval through clipping:

\hat{S}_{\text{holistic}}=\text{clip}_{[0,1]}(S_{\text{holistic}})=\max\{0,\min\{1,S_{\text{holistic}}\}\}

(3)

This formulation prioritizes factual accuracy while maintaining substantial weight on completeness, and applies a full penalty for hallucinated content to discourage the generation of unsupported information.

3.2.2
Question-Based Evaluation

The question-based evaluation paradigm, inspired by the DLC-Bench methodology
(
Lian et al. 2025
)
, operationalizes assessment through targeted verification questions. This approach transforms the evaluation task into a series of binary verification problems, enabling precise quantification of specific descriptive attributes.

Question Generation Framework:

Let
{Q}={Q}^{+}\cup{Q}^{-}
represent the complete set of evaluation questions generated by the LLM judge, where:

•

{Q}^{+}=\{q_{1}^{+},q_{2}^{+},\ldots,q_{n^{+}}^{+}\}
denotes positive verification questions

•

{Q}^{-}=\{q_{1}^{-},q_{2}^{-},\ldots,q_{n^{-}}^{-}\}
denotes negative exclusion questions

Each question
q_{i}
is formulated to test one specific factual detail that can be objectively verified. The LLM generates these questions using exact words and phrases from human descriptions to ensure precision.

Scoring Functions:

Positive Question Scoring:

For each positive question
q_{i}^{+}\in{Q}^{+}
, we define the scoring function:

\sigma(q_{i}^{+},{D})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if attribute is correctly and clearly mentioned}\\
0&\text{if attribute is omitted}\\
-0.5&\text{if attribute is mentioned but factually incorrect}\end{cases}

(4)

The aggregate positive score is computed as:

S^{+}=\frac{1}{|{Q}^{+}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|{Q}^{+}|}\sigma(q_{i}^{+},{D})

(5)

Negative Question Scoring:

For each negative question
q_{i}^{-}\in{Q}^{-}
, the scoring function is:

\sigma(q_{i}^{-},{D})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if incorrect detail is correctly avoided}\\
-1&\text{if incorrect detail is erroneously included}\end{cases}

(6)

The aggregate negative score is:

S^{-}=\frac{1}{|{Q}^{-}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|{Q}^{-}|}\sigma(q_{i}^{-},{D})

(7)

Normalized Question-Based Score:

The scores are normalized to ensure non-negativity:

\displaystyle\hat{S}^{+}

\displaystyle=\max\{0,S^{+}\}

(8)

\displaystyle\hat{S}^{-}

\displaystyle=\max\{0,S^{-}\}

(9)

The combined question-based score is:

S_{\text{question}}=\alpha\cdot\hat{S}^{+}+(1-\alpha)\cdot\hat{S}^{-}

(10)

where
\alpha=0.5
represents equal weighting between positive and negative assessments.

Integrated Evaluation Score:

The final integrated evaluation score
S_{\text{final}}
combines both assessment paradigms:

S_{\text{final}}=\beta\cdot S_{\text{question}}+(1-\beta)\cdot\hat{S}_{\text{holistic}}

(11)

where
\beta=0.6
prioritizes question-based assessment while maintaining holistic evaluation influence. The final score is constrained to the unit interval:

\hat{S}_{\text{final}}=\text{clip}_{[0,1]}(S_{\text{final}})=\max\{0,\min\{1,S_{\text{final}}\}\}

(12)

Question Similarity Analysis

In order to have a better understanding on the generated question from the LLM judge models we proposed a systematic analysis on the similarity of the generated questions in the embedding space. We investigated the similarity of evaluation questions generated by three large language models across the dataset samples. Using semantic embedding analysis and cosine similarity measures, we examine how consistently different LLMs formulate positive and negative evaluation questions. Our findings reveal interesting differences in questioning strategies between positive and negative question types, with important implications for cross-model evaluation reliability.

We assess the whole of samples individually to ensure robust statistical analysis. As it is shown in Algorithm

(
Reimers and Gurevych 2019a
)
. It constructs a cosine similarity matrix comparing all question pairs between the two LLMs, then computes the symmetric maximum similarity by averaging the best matches in both directions - this metric captures how well each question from one LLM is represented by the most similar question from another LLM. The algorithm repeats this process across all samples and question types, finally aggregating the results to produce overall similarity scores that reflect consistent patterns while accounting for sample-level variation. This approach ensures that the final similarity measures represent genuine cross-model consistency rather than isolated agreements on specific samples.

3.4
Dataset

For our experiments, we use the entire validation set of the DataSeeds.AI (DSD) dataset
(
Abdoli et al. 2025
)
since it has rich annotations from human experts. Each sample in the dataset includes comprehensive multi-tier annotations quite suitable for this study:

•

Concise descriptive titles encapsulating the central subjects of each image

•

Detailed narrative descriptions, systematically capturing object identifiers, spatial relationships, and objective documentation of visual elements

•

Technical scene analyses documenting photographic methodologies, camera angles (eye-level, elevated, low, or bird’s-eye), photographic style, and textual elements

•

Full semantic segmentation masks defining precise pixel-level contours of objects, capturing their true morphology including occluded and overlapping forms

As noted by
(
Abdoli et al. 2025
)
, this dataset uniquely integrates professional photographic standards with semi-professional creative expression, presenting a wide-ranging visual dataset with inherent hierarchical quality signals derived from GuruShots’ community-driven ranking system. Each image inherently carries an evaluative dimension based on human preferences and perceptions.

The use of semantic segmentation masks, as emphasized in the DSD paper, was crucial for our evaluation. Unlike bounding boxes which delineate crude rectangular regions prone to capturing irrelevant background artifacts, semantic segmentation defines the precise pixel-level contours of objects. This granularity yields more accurate scene understanding and enables finer-grained feature extraction, allowing models to infer depth, hierarchy, and adjacency relationships. For our study, these human-annotated segmentation masks ensured that DAM’s object-level descriptions corresponded exactly to the regions identified by human evaluators, providing a controlled foundation for subsequent GPT model assessment.

The DSD’s multi-tier annotation strategy, which closely resembles NVIDIA’s Describe Anything Model (DAM) framework and its semi-supervised data pipeline, combines structured human annotations with scalable machine-assisted processes to enhance data quality and efficiency. This procedural rigor, with each mask subject to manual review and verification by trained annotators, ensures the dataset’s reliability for consistent benchmarking and reproducible improvements in model evaluation.

Universal Assessment Bias Discovery

Analysis of the assessment patterns reveals systematic biases in how GPT judges evaluate DAM, suggesting fundamental characteristics of current AI evaluation approaches. These biases manifest most clearly in the asymmetry between positive confirmation and negative detection.

The negative detection dimension demonstrates remarkable consistency across the GPT-4 family, with GPT-4o assigning DAM 92.21% (

By contrast, positive confirmation emerges as a universal weakness across all evaluators. Across GPT judges, DAM receives positive confirmation rates below 50%, with GPT-4o-mini assigning the highest at only 45.47% (

The magnitude of this asymmetry is striking. This asymmetry has profound implications for evaluation design. Current GPT judges are inherently biased toward criticism over confirmation, which may lead to systematically deflated scores when used for quality assessment. The pattern suggests that training data and optimization objectives emphasize error avoidance over positive validation, creating evaluators that function more as critics than balanced assessors.

The consistency of this bias across GPT architectures suggests it may stem from fundamental aspects of training paradigms rather than specific model designs. Possible explanations include training data distributions that overrepresent error correction, reinforcement structures that penalize false positives more heavily than false negatives, or inherent asymmetries in how language models process negative versus positive statements.

The case also reveals a fundamental challenge in AI evaluation: the difficulty of establishing appropriate error hierarchies. While misidentifying a central object (stone vs. shell) and relocating an entire scene (desert vs. beach) represent fundamental errors, the models showed varying abilities to weight these appropriately against correctly identified peripheral details. This suggests that future evaluation systems may need explicit hierarchical error frameworks that distinguish between critical misidentifications and minor embellishments.

Implications for Evaluation Reliability

These findings have significant implications for designing robust, multi-model evaluation frameworks.

5
Discussion

Our analysis of 762 vision-language descriptions reveals fundamental insights into AI evaluation behavior that challenge assumptions about the relationship between general capability and assessment competence.

5.1
The Evaluation Paradox

The emergence of three distinct evaluation personalities demonstrates that assessment behavior represents a complex interplay between architecture, training objectives, and emergent strategies. GPT-4o-mini’s near-zero variance (
\pm
0.43%) in overall assessment suggests convergence on a fixed evaluation function operating independently of content variation. This extraordinary consistency implies the model has developed an abstract representation of quality transcending specific domains.

Conversely, GPT-5’s evaluation behavior despite superior general intelligence (94.6% AIME 2025, 84.2% MMMU) reveals that optimizations for broad capability introduce instabilities when models perform assessment tasks requiring consistent criteria application. The model’s extreme hallucination vigilance (54.10% penalty rate) coupled with high variance suggests sophisticated contextual reasoning that, while potentially more nuanced, reduces systematic reliability in its role as an evaluator.

GPT-4o exhibits pronounced asymmetry in its evaluation patterns, achieving 92.21% accuracy when detecting errors but only 41.98% when confirming correct information. This behavior pattern not a measure of GPT-4o’s quality but rather its approach to assessment may reflect how error correction prevalence in training data shapes evaluation strategies, creating implicit biases toward negative assessment when models act as judges.

The controlled experiment using Gemini 2.5 Pro as an independent question generator further validates these findings. When presented with identical evaluation criteria, the three GPT models maintain their distinct personalities: GPT-4o-mini remains the most lenient (72.82% overall reward), GPT-5 the most stringent (64.82%), and GPT-4o balanced between them (69.48%). This persistence of evaluation patterns when question generation is controlled confirms that these "personalities" are inherent model properties rather than artifacts of how models formulate assessment criteria. The remarkable consistency of these patterns across experimental conditions strengthens our conclusion that evaluation behavior represents a fundamental characteristic of model architecture and training philosophy.

5.2
Universal Biases and Architectural Trade-offs

The consistent 2:1 bias favoring negative assessment across all GPT models reveals fundamental characteristics of this model family’s training paradigms. However, our cross-family analysis demonstrates this pattern is not universal across AI architectures. The semantic similarity analysis reveals that GPT models achieve high agreement when generating positive verification questions (80.7–84.6% similarity) but lower agreement on negative detection questions (69.3–74.8%), a pattern that persists but differs when compared with Gemini’s question generation approach.

Our findings reveal fundamental trade-offs in evaluation design. GPT-4o-mini’s independence between assessment methods (r = -0.014) suggests architectural separation that prevents error contamination across evaluation dimensions. GPT-5’s moderate correlation between methods indicates more integrated evaluation capturing complex interdependencies, but with reduced reliability. The question similarity analysis reveals striking family-specific patterns: while GPT models form a high-similarity cluster (intra-family similarities of 0.78–0.85 for combined questions), Gemini exhibits markedly different question generation strategies (0.67–0.68 similarity to GPT models). This cross-family divergence is particularly pronounced for negative questions, where Gemini’s similarity to GPT models drops to 0.56–0.59, suggesting fundamentally different approaches to error detection.

The discovery that positive question similarity consistently exceeds negative question similarity across all model pairs—including cross-family comparisons—indicates a universal asymmetry in how AI systems conceptualize quality assessment. Models converge on what constitutes correct information but diverge significantly in how they identify potential errors. This suggests that while there may be emerging consensus on quality indicators, error detection remains a more subjective, architecture-dependent process shaped by specific training philosophies and safety objectives.

5.3
Implications for AI Safety and Future Research

These systematic biases have critical implications for AI safety, particularly as AI systems increasingly evaluate other AI outputs. The recursive nature of AI development, where models evaluate and train other models, makes understanding and correcting evaluation biases crucial for preventing bias amplification.

GPT-5’s extreme hallucination vigilance demonstrates how safety-oriented training can create overcorrection that impairs balanced judgment. This suggests current safety approaches may have unintended consequences for evaluation utility. The high variance in GPT-5’s evaluation behavior raises concerns about reliability in safety-critical applications where consistent decision-making is essential.

Future research should prioritize several directions:

(1) Expanded cross-family evaluation analysis:
Our demonstration of significant GPT-Gemini divergence establishes that evaluation personalities and biases are family-specific rather than universal. The 2:1 negative-positive bias we observed appears to be a GPT family characteristic, as evidenced by the different patterns exhibited by Gemini. Extending this analysis to additional model families like Claude (Anthropic) and open-source alternatives like Llama would reveal whether each architectural lineage develops distinct evaluation philosophies and whether certain approaches yield more balanced assessment.

(2) Architecture-specific evaluation models:
Rather than adapting general-purpose models, developing architectures specifically optimized for evaluation tasks could eliminate the trade-off between general capability and assessment consistency. Our finding that GPT-5’s superior general intelligence correlates with inferior evaluation consistency suggests dedicated evaluation architectures may be necessary.

(3) Controlled bias investigation:
Our independent question generator methodology provides a template for isolating different aspects of evaluation behavior. Systematic experiments varying training data composition, safety constraints, and reward structures while using standardized evaluation criteria could isolate the sources of family-specific biases.

(4) Multi-family ensemble methods:
Given the pronounced cross-family divergence we observed, evaluation frameworks should leverage models from different architectural families rather than different versions within a family. Combining GPT’s systematic negative detection with Gemini’s distinct error conceptualization and potentially other families’ strengths could yield more comprehensive and balanced evaluation systems.

(5) Human-AI evaluation alignment:
Understanding whether humans exhibit similar evaluation asymmetries and family-like clustering in their assessment approaches would contextualize whether AI evaluation patterns reflect fundamental assessment challenges or training artifacts. The high agreement on positive verification but divergence on error detection we observed may mirror human evaluation patterns.

The significant divergence between GPT and Gemini evaluation strategies, revealed through both question generation patterns and scoring behaviors, demonstrates that robust AI evaluation requires diverse architectural perspectives rather than variations within a single model family.

6
Conclusion

This comprehensive study of 762 vision-language descriptions evaluated by three GPT architectures and validated through cross-family comparison with Gemini reveals that evaluation behavior in AI systems represents a complex phenomenon distinct from general intelligence. Through systematic analysis employing dual-assessment methodologies and controlled experiments with independent question generation, we uncovered three distinct evaluation personalities within the GPT family and demonstrated significant cross-family divergence in assessment strategies.

Our primary contribution demonstrates that evaluation competence does not simply scale with model capability. GPT-4o-mini’s unprecedented consistency (
\pm
0.43% variance), GPT-4o’s error detection specialization (92.21% accuracy), and GPT-5’s variable high-threshold assessment (54.10% hallucination penalty) reveal that architectural advances optimized for general intelligence may compromise systematic assessment capabilities. These personalities persist even when question generation is controlled through an independent model, confirming they are inherent architectural properties rather than methodological artifacts.

The discovery of a 2:1 bias toward error detection over positive confirmation within the GPT family, contrasted with Gemini’s distinct evaluation patterns, reveals that assessment biases are family-specific rather than universal across AI architectures. The semantic similarity analysis further demonstrates this divergence: GPT models form a high-similarity cluster (0.78–0.85 intra-family similarity) while Gemini exhibits markedly different strategies (0.56–0.72 cross-family similarity), particularly in error detection approaches. This asymmetry has immediate practical implications: evaluation frameworks must incorporate diverse architectural families rather than relying on variations within a single family to achieve balanced assessment.

For practitioners, our results provide evidence-based guidance for model selection and ensemble design. GPT-4o-mini suits applications requiring systematic, reproducible assessment; GPT-4o excels in quality control contexts; and GPT-5’s extreme hallucination vigilance, despite overall limitations, may benefit applications where false information detection is paramount. The controlled experiment using Gemini as question generator validates these recommendations by demonstrating that evaluation personalities persist across experimental conditions. The paradox of GPT-5—achieving state-of-the-art performance on complex reasoning while demonstrating poor evaluation consistency—suggests current optimization approaches inadvertently sacrifice specialized capabilities for general intelligence.

Our methodological contributions, including the dual-assessment framework, semantic similarity analysis, and independent question generator protocol, provide tools for future evaluation research. The finding that models achieve high agreement on positive criteria but diverge on error detection reveals fundamental asymmetries in how AI systems conceptualize quality assessment. The pronounced cross-family divergence between GPT and Gemini approaches demonstrates that different architectural lineages develop distinct evaluation philosophies shaped by their training paradigms.

As AI systems increasingly evaluate other AI outputs, understanding and correcting these family-specific biases becomes crucial for preventing recursive bias amplification. The future of AI evaluation lies not in scaling current approaches but in developing diverse, multi-family evaluation frameworks that leverage complementary strengths across architectural lineages. This study establishes that improving AI evaluation requires dedicated attention to assessment as a distinct capability, separate from but complementary to advances in general intelligence, and necessitates systematic incorporation of diverse architectural perspectives to achieve truly balanced and robust evaluation systems.
