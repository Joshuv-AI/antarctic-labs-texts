Rational Points on a Family of Genus 3 Hyperelliptic Curves
Roberto Hernandez

Abstract
We compute the rational points on certain members of the following family of hyperelliptic curves C a : y 2 = x 8 + ( 4 − 4 ​ a 4 ) ​ x 6 + ( 8 ​ a 4 + 6 ) ​ x 4 + ( 4 − 4 ​ a 4 ) ​ x 2 + 1 \displaystyle C_{a}\colon y^{2}=x^{8}+(4-4a^{4})x^{6}+(8a^{4}+6)x^{4}+(4-4a^{4})x^{2}+1 via the method first developed by Dem’yanenko [ Dem68 ] and then further generalized by Manin [ Man69 ] . In particular, we show that the method of Chabauty–Coleman, while applicable to certain members of this family, is not the most efficient way of computing C a ​ ( ℚ ) C_{a}(\mathbb{Q}) . We adapt the approach of [ Kul99 ] , incorporating root numbers to further restrict the possible ranks of the elliptic curves arising in the Jacobian decomposition.

Introduction

In 1983, Faltings’
[
Fal83
]
proved Mordell’s Conjecture stating that any non-singular curve
C
defined over a number field of genus greater than one has finitely many rational points. Since then, there have been simplifications to his original argument, most notably done through the work of Bombieri
[
Bom90
]
and Vojta
[
Voj91
]
. While Faltings’ theorem marked a milestone in arithmetic geometry, its limitation is that it does not give an instructive way to obtain all the rational points on
C
. Effectively finding rational points on curves of genus greater than one is an active area of research, with many working to better understand or improve current methods. In practice, the most powerful tool for computing rational points on curves has been the Chabauty–Coleman method. In this work, we focus on the method developed by Dem’yanenko
[
Dem68
]
in 1968, extended by Manin
[
Man69
]
in 1969. We present an explicit family of curves

\displaystyle C_{a}\colon y^{2}=x^{8}+(4-4a^{4})x^{6}+(8a^{4}+6)x^{4}+(4-4a^{4})x^{2}+1,

for which the method of Dem’yanenko–Manin proves particularly effective. In this setting, the method of Chabauty–Coleman does not generally apply in a useful way. In particular, the Jacobian of
C_{a}
is isogenous to a product of elliptic curves, written
\textnormal{Jac}(C_{a})\sim E_{a}\times E_{a}\times E^{\prime}
, and Chabauty–Coleman can only be applied in the special cases where
\Rank(E_{a})=0
or
\Rank(E^{\prime})=0
. In those cases, however, the rational points on
C_{a}
can already be determined by pulling back the (finite) rational points on either
E_{a}
or
E^{\prime}
. The more interesting case is where
\Rank(E_{a})=1
and
\Rank(E^{\prime})\neq 0
, because the conditions to apply Chabauty–Coleman are not met, and the Dem’yanenko–Manin method becomes essential. Concretely, we will show that

\displaystyle C_{237}(\mathbb{Q})=\{(\pm 1:\pm 4:1),(0:\pm 1:1),(1:\pm 1:0)\},

to showcase a particular instance of the method. We will also along the way prove certain facts about a family of elliptic curves which arise in our construction. In particular, we prove the following results about the Mordell-Weil group of the particular elliptic curve
E_{a}\colon y^{2}=x^{3}+2a^{2}x^{2}+(a^{4}-4)x
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that
a\equiv 0\textnormal{ (mod 3)}
, then
E_{a}(\mathbb{Q})_{\textnormal{tor}}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{2}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
a^{2}-2
and
a^{2}+2
are simultaneously prime, then
\Rank(E_{a})=1
.

We recall that that the method of Chabauty-Coleman uses
p
-adic techniques to produce
p
-adic locally analytic functions, and relies on the assumption that rank of the Jacobian of
C
over
\mathbb{Q}
, say
r
, is strictly smaller than the genus of
C
. In 1941, Chabauty
[
Cha41
]
proved that if the rank of the Jacobian is smaller than
g
, then the set
C(\mathbb{Q}_{p})\cap\overline{\textnormal{Jac}(\mathbb{Q})}
is finite. Moreover, in 1985, Coleman
[
Col85
]
showed that if you also assume that you have a prime
p>2g
of good reduction, then you can place an upper bound on the number of rational points on
C
.

Theorem

(Chabauty-Coleman)
.

Let
C
be a smooth, projective, geometrically integral curve over
\mathbb{Q}
and let
r\coloneq\Rank\textnormal{Jac}_{C}(\mathbb{Q})
. If
r<g
and
p>2g
is a prime of good reduction, then
C(\mathbb{Q}_{p})\cap\overline{\textnormal{Jac}_{C}(\mathbb{Q})}
is finite and
\#C(\mathbb{Q})\leq\#C(\mathbb{F}_{p})+2g-2
.

We highlight a family of curves for which we cannot apply this theorem because
\Rank(\textnormal{Jac}_{C_{a}}(\mathbb{Q}))\geq g
, but the method of Dem’yanenko–Manin gives a procedure to find
C_{a}(\mathbb{Q})
. For that, we will force the Jacobian of the curves to fully decompose, and then aim to restrict the ranks of the elliptic curves which appear in the decomposition.

Background

First, we provide some relevant background material which can be found in more detail for instance in
[
HS13
]
. Everything we discuss in this article will be over
\mathbb{Q}
, unless stated otherwise.

An elliptic curve is an abelian variety of dimension one, equipped with a distinguished point denoted
\mathcal{O}=[0:1:0]
. It can be described concretely via its Weierstrass equation

E\colon y^{2}+a_{1}xy+a_{3}y=x^{3}+a_{2}x^{2}+a_{4}x+a_{6}.

Two important invariants of an elliptic curve are its discriminant,
\Delta
, and the
j
-invariant. We recall some of the invariants associated to elliptic curves below.

\displaystyle b_{2}

\displaystyle=a_{1}^{2}+4a_{2},

\displaystyle b_{4}

\displaystyle=2a_{4}+a_{1}a_{3},

\displaystyle b_{6}

\displaystyle=a_{3}^{2}+4a_{6},

\displaystyle b_{8}

\displaystyle=a_{1}^{2}a_{6}+4a_{2}a_{6}-a_{1}a_{3}a_{4}+a_{2}a_{3}^{2}-a_{4}^{2},

\displaystyle c_{4}

\displaystyle=b_{2}^{2}-24b_{4},

\displaystyle c_{6}

\displaystyle=-b_{2}^{3}+36b_{2}b_{4}-216b_{6},

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=-b_{2}^{2}b_{8}-8b_{4}^{3}-27b_{6}^{2}+9b_{2}b_{4}b_{6},

\displaystyle j(E)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{4}^{\,3}}{\Delta}.

In particular, the
j
-invariant controls the isomorphism class of an elliptic curve over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, while the discriminant helps detect primes of bad reduction. We also recall that a hyperelliptic curve over
\mathbb{Q}
is a smooth, projective curve of genus
g\geq 2
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
which admits a degree
2
map to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Equivalently, over
\mathbb{Q}
, we can choose a model in affine coordinates of the form

C\colon y^{2}=f(x),

where
f(x)
is a monic polynomial of degree
2g+1
or
2g+2
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{Q})
and write
P=(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
with
x_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\gcd(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=1
. We define the height of
P
to be

H(P)=\max\{|x_{0}|,|x_{1}|,\dots,|x_{n}|\}.

The set

\{P\in\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{Q}):H(P)\leq B\}

is finite, since there are only finitely many integers
x
satisfying
|x|\leq B
.

Definition 1
.

Let
k
be a number field and let
P\in\mathbb{P}^{n}(k)
. The multiplicative height of
P
is the quantity

H_{k}(P)=\prod_{v\in M_{k}}\max\{||x_{0}||_{v},||x_{1}||_{v},\dots,||x_{n}||_{v}\}

and the logarithmic height is defined as

h_{k}(P)=\log H_{k}(P).

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
X
be a closed subvariety of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
and let
\varphi\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a morphism of degree
d
. Then

h(\varphi(P))=dh(P)+O(1)\hskip 36.135pt\text{ for all }P\in X(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}).

It is important to note here that the
O(1)
depends only on the map
\varphi
, but it independent of the point
P
.

Definition 2
.

Let
\varphi\colon V\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a morphism. The height on
V
relative to
\varphi
is the function

h_{\varphi}\colon V(\mathbb{Q})\to[0,\infty),\hskip 36.135pth_{\varphi}(P)=h(\varphi(P)),

where
h\colon\mathbb{P}^{n}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})\to[0,\infty)
is the height function on projective space that we defined in the previous section.

In this way, we see that defining heights on varieties is very natural once you have an embedding of your variety into projective space. Recall that any nonconstant function
f
in the function field
K(E)
determines a surjective morphism from
E
to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Concretely,
f
gives the map

f\colon E\to\mathbb{P}^{1},\hskip 36.135ptP\mapsto\begin{cases}[1,0]\text{ if }P\text{ is a pole of }f\\
[f(P),1]\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

Definition 3
.

Let
E/\mathbb{Q}
be an elliptic curve and let
f
be a function in the function field of
E
. The height on
E
relative to
f
is the function

h_{f}\colon E(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 36.135pth_{f}(P)=h(f(P)).

We are now ready to formally state the main result we use in our exploration, and explain how the method simplifies in the case of rank 1. Let
C
be a smooth projective curve of genus
g\geq 2
over a number field
K
. Let
A
be an abelian variety over
K
, and let
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
be morphisms from
C
to
A
defined over
K
. Recall that a morphism is a rational map with no base points.

Definition 4
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
be morphisms from
C
to
A
. We say the
f_{i}
are independent if
\sum n_{i}f_{i}
is the constant map, then
n_{i}=0
for all
i
.

In practice, we do not typically use the definition to check whether a set of morphisms are independent, instead we use a lemma due to Cassels
[
Cas68
]
.

Lemma 2.2

(Cassels)
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
be morphisms from
C
to
A
. Let
D
be the matrix whose coefficients are

\langle f_{i},f_{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left(d(f_{i}+f_{j})-d(f_{i})-d(f_{j})\right),

where
d(f_{i})
denotes the degree of the morphism. The
m
morphisms are independent if and only if
\det(D)\neq 0
.

For our purposes, we will work with explicit morphisms between our hyperelliptic curve
C_{a}
and an elliptic curve,
E_{a}
, and thus these degrees are computable through the use of the computer algebra system Magma.

Theorem 2.3

(Dem’yanenko–Manin)
.

Let
C
be a curve over a number field
K
and
A
be an abelian variety over
K
. Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
be morphisms from
C
to
A
that are defined over
K
and independent. Assume further that
\Rank(A)<m
. Then
C(K)
is finite and it is possible to effectively bound the height of the points in
C(K)
.

For a proof of the statement and some examples carried out in practice see
[
GK05
]
,
[
Kul99
]
. The method of Dem’yanenko–Manin simplifies when the
\Rank(A)=1
since that would mean that we would only need two independent maps, say
f_{1},f_{2}\colon C\to A
. Moreover, if we let
P\in C(\mathbb{Q})
and
R
be a generator for the free part of
A(\mathbb{Q})
, then

\begin{cases}f_{1}(P)=[n]R+T_{1}\\
f_{2}(P)=[m]R+T_{2}\end{cases}

where
T_{i}\in A(\mathbb{Q})_{\text{tor}}
. Taking canonical heights we have

\begin{cases}\hat{h}(f_{1}(P))=n^{2}\hat{h}(R)\\
\hat{h}(f_{2}(P))=m^{2}\hat{h}(R).\end{cases}

Subtracting the second equation from the first and taking absolute values gives us the following relation:

\left|\hat{h}(f_{1}(P))-\hat{h}(f_{2}(P))\right|=\left|n^{2}\hat{h}(R)-m^{2}\hat{h}(R)\right|=\left|n^{2}-m^{2}\right|\hat{h}(R).

If it happens to be the case that
n=\pm m
, then that would imply that
f_{1}(P)\pm f_{2}(P)\in A(\mathbb{Q})_{\text{tor}}
, so we could find those points directly through computation since the torsion group is finite. Note also that we can bound this quantity from above using the triangle inequality. Indeed,

\left|\hat{h}(f_{1}(P))-\hat{h}(f_{2}(P))\right|\leq\left|\hat{h}(f_{1}(P))-h(f_{1}(P))\right|+\left|\hat{h}(f_{2}(P))-h(f_{2}(P))\right|+\Big|h(f_{1}(P))-h(f_{2}(P))\Big|.

Thus, if
n\neq\pm m
, then combining these two expressions we can obtain an upper bound, say
B
, such that
|n^{2}-m^{2}|<B
. It is important to note here that the constant
B
would only depend on
f_{1},f_{2}
, and
A
, but would be independent of
P
. In this way, we bound the possible values of
n
and
m
, so all that would be left to do would be to find the points on
C(\mathbb{Q})
whose images on
A(\mathbb{Q})
are of the form
nR+T_{i}
with
n<B
. Again, this is a finite computation that can be done using Magma.

3
Construction of
C_{a}

The main goal of this section is to demonstrate how we obtain the family of genus 3 hyperelliptic curves, and the two independent maps to the same elliptic curve. In particular, we will arrive at the following result.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
a
be a nonzero integer and let
C_{a}
be the genus 3 hyperelliptic curve

\displaystyle C_{a}\colon y^{2}=x^{8}+(4-4a^{4})x^{6}+(8a^{4}+6)x^{4}+(4-4a^{4})x^{2}+1.

Then,
\textnormal{Jac}(C_{a})\sim E_{a}\times E_{a}\times E^{\prime}
.

Consider the quartic
g(x)=x^{4}-a^{2}x^{2}+1
. Notice that we can pick two distinct non-zero points
z\neq w
such that
g(z)=g(w)
. In this way we obtain

\displaystyle z^{4}-a^{2}z^{2}+1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w^{4}-a^{2}w^{2}+1

\displaystyle z^{4}-w^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{2}(z^{2}-w^{2})

\displaystyle z^{2}+w^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{2},

but this last equation is a circle of radius
a
in
z
and
w
. We can parameterize rational points on this circle by considering the line
w=zx+a
, as long as
a\neq 0
. In doing so, we have

\displaystyle z^{2}+(zx+a)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{2}

\displaystyle z^{2}+z^{2}x^{2}+2zxa+a^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{2}

\displaystyle z^{2}(1+x^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2zxa

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{2xa}{x^{2}+1}.

Thus, we obtain the parameterization of rational points on the circle given by

\left(z(x),w(x)\right)=\left(-\dfrac{2ax}{x^{2}+1},-\dfrac{a(x+1)(x-1)}{x^{2}+1}\right),

for any
x\in\mathbb{Q}
. Now, we take the function
z(x)=-\dfrac{2ax}{x^{2}+1}
and plug it into our quartic
g(x)
. Indeed,

\displaystyle g(z(x))=\left(-\dfrac{2ax}{x^{2}+1}\right)^{4}-a^{2}\left(-\dfrac{2ax}{x^{2}+1}\right)^{2}+1,

which after multiplying the entire equation by
(x^{2}+1)^{4}
to clear denominators simplifies to

\displaystyle g(z(x))(x^{2}+1)^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{8}+(4-4a^{4})x^{6}+(8a^{4}+6)x^{4}+(4-4a^{4})x^{2}+1.

The right side of the above equation will define the
f(x)
for our hyperelliptic curve. Concretely, our family of genus 3 hyperelliptic curves will be defined as:

\displaystyle C_{a}\colon y^{2}=x^{8}+(4-4a^{4})x^{6}+(8a^{4}+6)x^{4}+(4-4a^{4})x^{2}+1.

Also note that this construction arose from the first quartic
g(x)=x^{4}-a^{2}x^{2}+1
, and if we define the curve
H_{a}:y^{2}=x^{4}-a^{2}x^{2}+1
, this is a genus 1 hyperelliptic curve. This implies that
H_{a}
is in fact an elliptic curve and Magma can give us the Weierstrass equation and the map between the curves. In fact, we have that

\displaystyle H_{a}\colon y^{2}=x^{4}-a^{2}x^{2}+1

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle E_{a}\colon y^{2}=x^{3}+2a^{2}x^{2}+(a^{4}-4)x

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle(2x^{2}-2y-1,4x^{3}-4xy-2x).

In particular, we have constructed a curve with multiple maps to the same elliptic curve, lending itself nicely to the method of Dem’yanenko–Manin. We can in fact be very concrete and display the maps we obtain. Firstly, we obtain the maps

\displaystyle\psi_{1}\colon C_{a}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle H_{a}

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\left(z(x),\dfrac{y}{(x^{2}+1)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\psi_{2}\colon C_{a}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle H_{a}

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\left(w(x),\dfrac{y}{(x^{2}+1)^{2}}\right)

and composing
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
with the isomorphism between
H_{a}
and
E_{a}
above, we have two maps from
C_{a}
to
E_{a}
. From here on out, we will denote by the composition of these maps
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The maps
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\colon C_{a}\to E_{a}
are independent.

Proof.

The maps
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
in projective coordinates are given explicitly by

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}

\displaystyle=[-a^{2}x^{6}+5a^{2}x^{4}z^{2}-2x^{2}y+5a^{2}x^{2}z^{4}-2yz^{2}-a^{2}z^{6},

\displaystyle 4a^{3}x^{5}z-24a^{3}x^{3}z^{3}+8axyz+4a^{3}xz^{5},

\displaystyle x^{6}+3x^{4}z^{2}+3x^{2}z^{4}+z^{6}]

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle=[a^{2}x^{6}-5a^{2}x^{4}z^{2}-2x^{2}y-5a^{2}x^{2}z^{4}-2yz^{2}+a^{2}z^{6},

\displaystyle-2a^{3}x^{6}+14a^{3}x^{4}z^{2}+4ax^{2}y-14a^{3}x^{2}z^{4}-4ayz^{2}+2a^{3}z^{6},

\displaystyle x^{6}+3x^{4}z^{2}+3x^{2}z^{4}+z^{6}].

The codomain of these maps is an elliptic curve so when we consider
\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}
we need to remember that the addition is defined as on an elliptic curve. A quick calculation on Magma shows that
\deg(\varphi_{1})=\deg(\varphi_{2})=2,
and that
\deg(\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2})=4
. Thus, we obtain the matrix

M=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\
0&2\end{bmatrix}

and we can clearly see that
\det(M)\neq 0
and thus by Lemma
2.2
the maps are independent.
∎

Now, maps between curves induce maps on their Jacobians so in particular we have maps from
\textnormal{Jac}(C_{a})\to\textnormal{Jac}(E_{a})\cong E_{a}
. More importantly, these maps will be surjective and have finite kernel, so they will be isogenies. This means that
\textnormal{Jac}(C_{a})
has two factors of
E_{a}
in its isogeny decomposition. We also know that
C_{a}
has genus 3, and
\dim\textnormal{Jac}(C_{a})=3
, hence the isogeny decomposition of
\textnormal{Jac}(C_{a})
is
E_{a}\times E_{a}\times E^{\prime}
where
E^{\prime}
is some other elliptic curve. For our purposes, it will be useful to know an equation for
E^{\prime}
since we will want to ensure it has nonzero rank so that we avoid Chabauty–Coleman. One way to find an equation for
E^{\prime}
is by quotienting
C_{a}
by its automorphisms and searching for an elliptic curve which is non-isogenous to
E_{a}
. This a finite search that can be done on Magma and we obtain

E^{\prime}\colon y^{2}=x^{3}+(-4-a^{4})x^{2}+4a^{4}x,

with the automorphism
\tau:C_{a}\to C_{a}
defined via
[x,y,z]\mapsto[z,y,-x]
.
This discussion completes the proof for Theorem
3.2
. We are now in the position where we have a family of genus 3 hyperelliptic curves whose Jacobian decomposes in a way favorable to the use of Dem’yanenko–Manin.
Our goal now is to find conditions on the parameter
a
so that
\Rank(E_{a})=1
(since we have two independent maps
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
) and
\Rank(E^{\prime})\neq 0
.

4
Controlling the ranks of
E_{a}
and
E^{\prime}

In this section we will show that under certain conditions on
a
,
E_{a}
has full 2-torsion, and that
\Rank(E_{a})=1
. We will also make use of the parity conjecture to force the rank of
E^{\prime}
to be odd, hence nonzero. A quick search for points on
E_{a}
yields the point of infinite order
P=(-a^{2},2a)\in E_{a}(\mathbb{Q})
as long as
a\neq 1
so in this case we know that the rank is bounded below by 1. Our aim is now to show that 1 is also an upper bound for the rank. First, note that

\displaystyle E_{a}\colon y^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{3}+2a^{2}x^{2}+(a^{4}-4)x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x(x+a^{2}-2)(x+a^{2}+2)

so we know that we have three distinct 2-torsion points
(0,0)
,
(-a^{2}+2,0)
, and
(-a^{2}-2,0)
. To ease notation, we will let
e_{1}=0
,
e_{2}=-a^{2}+2
, and
e_{3}=-a^{2}-2
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that
a\equiv 0\textnormal{ (mod 3)}
, then
E_{a}(\mathbb{Q})_{\textnormal{tor}}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{2}
.

Proof.

First note that since
a\equiv 0\textnormal{ (mod 3)}
, then
a^{2}\equiv 0\textnormal{ (mod 3)}
and so
a^{2}-2\equiv 1\textnormal{ (mod 3)}
and
a^{2}+2\equiv 2\textnormal{ (mod 3)}
. Thus,
3
never divides
a^{2}-2
or
a^{2}+2
. Per our above discussion, we already know that
E_{a}
contains full 2-torsion. Further, since
E_{a}
has discriminant

\displaystyle\Delta(E_{a})=2^{8}(a^{2}-2)^{2}(a^{2}+2)^{2},

it has good reduction at the prime 3. Reducing modulo 3 we see that

\displaystyle\tilde{E_{a}}\colon y^{2}=x^{3}+2x

which one can easily verify has
\#\tilde{E_{a}}(\mathbb{F}_{3})=4
. Now, since
E_{a}[2]\subset E_{a}(\mathbb{Q})_{\textnormal{tor}}
and
E_{a}(\mathbb{Q})_{\textnormal{tor}}
injects into
\tilde{E_{a}}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
, it must be that
E_{a}[2]=E_{a}(\mathbb{Q})_{\textnormal{tor}}
.
∎

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
a^{2}-2
and
a^{2}+2
are simultaneously prime, then
\Rank(E_{a})=1
.

Proof.

We proceed via a complete 2-descent as in
[
Sil09
]
(X.1.4). The elliptic curve
E_{a}
has discriminant
\Delta(E_{a})=2^{8}(a^{2}-2)^{2}(a^{2}+2)^{2}
and so the set
S=\{2,a^{2}-2,a^{2}+2,\infty\}
. We now consider the set

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(S,2)=\{b\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}/(\mathbb{Q}^{*})^{2}\colon\textnormal{ord}_{p}(b)\equiv 0(\textnormal{ mod }2)\textnormal{ for all }p\notin S\}.

Concretely, we can identify the set
\mathbb{Q}(S,2)
with

\displaystyle\{\pm 1,\pm 2,\pm(a^{2}-2),\pm(a^{2}+2),\pm 2(a^{2}-2),\pm 2(a^{2}+2),\pm(a^{4}-4),\pm 2(a^{4}-4)\}.

There is an injective homomorphism
E_{a}(\mathbb{Q})/2E_{a}(\mathbb{Q})\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Q}(S,2)\times\mathbb{Q}(S,2)
defined by

\displaystyle P=(x,y)\mapsto\begin{cases}(x-e_{1},x-e_{2})&\textnormal{ if }x\neq e_{1},e_{2}\\
\left(\dfrac{e_{1}-e_{3}}{e_{1}-e_{2}},e_{1}-e_{2}\right)&\textnormal{ if }x=e_{1}\\

(1,1)&\textnormal{ if }P=\mathcal{O}.\end{cases}

The pair

\displaystyle b_{1}z_{1}^{2}-b_{2}z_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle-a^{2}+2

(1)

\displaystyle b_{1}z_{1}^{2}-b_{1}b_{2}z_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle-a^{2}-2,

at the end of the paper where each cell will either have the corresponding point on
E_{a}(\mathbb{Q})
if the system has a solution, or the obstruction of a solution to the system.

1.

If
b_{1}>0

Taking the pair

Remark 1
.

We could try this same analysis for
E^{\prime}
, however the factorization of its discriminant forces certain primality conditions that would make
\mathbb{Q}(S,2)\times\mathbb{Q}(S,2)
a set of size 4096. Even then, this provides an upper bound for its rank, and we were not able to find an explicit point of infinite order as we did in the case of

to also place a lower bound.

Definition 5
.

The global root number
w(E/K)
of an elliptic curve over
K
is defined as the product of the local root numbers
w(E/K_{v})\in\{-1,1\}
. In particular,

w(E/K)=\prod_{v}w(E/K_{v}),

where the product runs over all places of
K
, including the infinite ones.

Local root numbers of elliptic curves are defined using epsilon-factors of Weil–Deligne representations, however, for our purposes we will not need to introduce formal definitions. We instead direct the interested reader to
[
Roh93
]
for a more detailed exposition of local root numbers. They have been classified for all places of number fields, so we will not concern ourselves with proving any results, but instead use them for our computations. For instance, we make use of the following result from
[
KD23
]
. We remark that the result has more cases, and we have only included the ones relevant to our purposes.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve over a local field
\mathcal{K}
of characteristic zero. When
\mathcal{K}
is non-Archimedean, let
k
be its residue field and let
v\colon\mathcal{K}^{\times}\to\mathbb{Z}
denote the normalized valuation with respect to
\mathcal{K}
. Let
\left(\dfrac{*}{k}\right)
denote the quadratic residue symbol on
k^{\times}
and
(a,b)_{\mathcal{K}}
denote the Hilbert symbol in
\mathcal{K}
.

1.

If
\mathcal{K}
is Archimedean, then
w(E/\mathcal{K})=-1
.

2.

If
E/\mathcal{K}
has good reduction, then
w(E/\mathcal{K})=1
.

3.

If
E/\mathcal{K}
has split multiplicative reduction, then
w(E/\mathcal{K})=-1
.

4.

If
E/\mathcal{K}
has non-split multiplicative reduction, then
w(E/\mathcal{K})=1
.

5.

If
E/\mathcal{K}
has additive, potentially multiplicative reduction and
\text{char}(k)=2
, then
w(E/\mathcal{K})=(-1,-c_{6})_{\mathcal{K}}
. In particular, if
\mathcal{K}=\mathbb{Q}_{2}
, then

w(E/\mathbb{Q}_{2})=\begin{cases}-1&\text{ if }c_{6}^{{}^{\prime}}\equiv 1\text{ (mod }4)\\
+1&\text{ if }c_{6}^{{}^{\prime}}\equiv 3\text{ (mod }4)\end{cases}

where
c_{6}^{{}^{\prime}}=\dfrac{c_{6}}{2^{v(c_{6})}}
.

In particular, this result tells us that to determine local root numbers, we only need to determine the reduction type of our elliptic curve at various primes. Now, over
\mathbb{Q}
, the places are precisely the primes, and the usual infinite place. The only places where our elliptic curve may have bad reduction is at the primes which divide the discriminant and so we only need to consider those primes, since the primes of good reduction have local root number 1, and as such will not change the parity of the global root number.

Conjecture 1

(Parity Conjecture)
.

Let
E/K
be an elliptic curve over a number field. Then

(-1)^{\Rank(E)}=w(E/K)

where
w(E/K)
is the global root number of
E
.

We were not able to find a point of infinite order on
E^{\prime}
, which means that in order to try and control its rank, we will need to make use of the Parity Conjecture. Note that its discriminant is
\Delta(E^{\prime})=2^{8}a^{8}(a^{2}-2)^{2}(a^{2}+2)^{2}

a^{2}-2
and

being simultaneously prime forces

at the primes
2,3
,
q,a^{2}-2
, and
a^{2}+2
for

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
a=3q
, with
q>3
prime,

and

Proof.

We have
\Delta(E^{\prime})=2^{8}a^{8}(a^{2}-2)^{2}(a^{2}+2)^{2}
and so we need to find the reduction type at the following primes

First consider

[

]
and noting that in their notation,
E^{\prime}=E_{C_{2}\times C_{2}}(-4,-a^{4},1)=E_{C_{2}\times C_{2}}(a,b,d)

Consider now

Consider now

Putting this all together we have

5
Bounding the Difference in Heights

In this section our aim will be to prove the following inequality concerning the difference between the canonical heights on
E_{a}

\displaystyle\left|\hat{h}_{E_{a}}(\varphi_{1}(P))-\hat{h}_{E_{a}}(\varphi_{2}(P))\right|\leq 51.18+2\log(a).

As mentioned before, this will allow us to effectively find all the possible images of the points
P\in C_{a}(\mathbb{Q})
under the maps
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
. Recall that we have explicit maps
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
and if we are concerned with bounding the quantity

\Big|\hat{h}_{E_{a}}(\varphi_{1}(P))-\hat{h}_{E_{a}}(\varphi_{2}(P))\Big|,

then we should attempt to get a better understanding of what equations define
\varphi_{1}(P)
and
\varphi_{2}(P)
and calculate bounds for their naive height. Recall also that the naive height of a point on an elliptic curve was simply defined as the height of the
x
-coordinate of the image of the point and so we are really only concerned with the
x
-coordinate of these images. In affine coordinates we have

\displaystyle x(\varphi_{1}(P))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{-a^{2}x^{4}+6a^{2}x^{2}-2y-a^{2}}{x^{4}+2x^{2}+1}

\displaystyle x(\varphi_{2}(P))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{a^{2}x^{4}-6a^{2}x^{2}-2y+a^{2}}{x^{4}+2x^{2}+1}.

We run into an issue here that both numerators in these maps depend on the
y
-coordinate of the point
P
which means that we will need to use the defining equation of our curve to get the terms purely in terms of
x
. This step is essential for determining lower bounds of the heights of
\varphi_{1}(P)
and
\varphi_{2}(P)
.

Recall that the defining equation for our family of hyperelliptic curves is:

\displaystyle y^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{8}+(4-4a^{4})x^{6}+(8a^{4}+6)x^{4}+(4-4a^{4})x^{2}+1

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{x^{8}+(4-4a^{4})x^{6}+(8a^{4}+6)x^{4}+(4-4a^{4})x^{2}+1}.

Note that
4-4a^{4}
is negative for all values of
a>1
and so, in particular these terms will only decrease the value and so by ignoring them we “maximize” how large the
y
-coordinate could be. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle y^{\prime}=\sqrt{x^{8}+(8a^{4}+6)x^{4}+1}\geq y.

Moreover, we can further maximize
y^{\prime}
with the following inequality:

\displaystyle\sqrt{x^{8}+(8a^{4}+6)x^{4}+1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{x^{8}}+\sqrt{(8a^{4}+6)x^{4}}+1

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle x^{4}+(3a^{2}+3)x^{4}+1=y^{\prime\prime}.

We arrive at the ladder of inequalities
y^{\prime\prime}\geq y^{\prime}\geq y
, and this is how we will minimize the numerator of both the images of
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
. In particular, replacing
y
with
y^{\prime\prime}
in
x(\varphi_{1}(P))
and
x(\varphi_{2}(P))
we have:

\displaystyle x(\varphi_{1}(P))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{-a^{2}x^{4}+6a^{2}x^{2}-2y-a^{2}}{x^{4}+2x^{2}+1}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\dfrac{(-2-a^{2})x^{4}-6x^{2}+(-2-a^{2})}{x^{4}+2x^{2}+1}

\displaystyle x(\varphi_{2}(P))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{a^{2}x^{4}-6a^{2}x^{2}-2y+a^{2}}{x^{4}+2x^{2}+1}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\dfrac{(a^{2}-2)x^{4}+(-12a^{2}-6)x^{2}+(a^{2}-2)}{x^{4}+2x^{2}+1}.

It will be convenient for us to define the following homogeneous polynomials

\displaystyle F_{1}(X,Z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-2-a^{2})X^{4}-6X^{2}Z^{2}+(-2-a^{2})Z^{4}

\displaystyle G_{1}(X,Z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X^{4}+2X^{2}Z^{2}+Z^{4}

\displaystyle F_{2}(X,Z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a^{2}-2)X^{4}+(-12a^{2}-6)X^{2}Z^{2}+(a^{2}-2)Z^{4}

\displaystyle G_{2}(X,Z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X^{4}+2X^{2}Z^{2}+Z^{4}.

Note that
F_{1}(X,1)
and
G_{1}(X,1)
are simply the numerator and denominator for the lower bound of
x(\varphi_{1}(P))
and that they are relatively prime in
\mathbb{Q}[X]
, so they generate the unit ideal in
\mathbb{Q}[X]
. This implies that we have the following result.

Lemma 5.1
.

Define the polynomials

\displaystyle F_{1}(X,Z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-2-a^{2})X^{4}-6X^{2}Z^{2}+(-2-a^{2})Z^{4}

\displaystyle G_{1}(X,Z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X^{4}+2X^{2}Z^{2}+Z^{4}

\displaystyle f_{1}(X,Z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{2(a^{2}-1)}X^{2}Z^{2}-\dfrac{1}{a^{2}-1}Z^{4}

\displaystyle g_{1}(X,Z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{\frac{1}{2}a^{2}+1}{a^{2}-1}X^{2}Z^{2}-\dfrac{3}{a^{2}-1}Z^{4}

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{2(a^{2}-1)}X^{2}Z^{2}-\dfrac{1}{a^{2}-1}X^{4}

Then the following identities hold:

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z^{8}

\displaystyle F_{1}(X,Z)f_{2}(X,Z)+G_{1}(X,Z)g_{2}(X,Z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X^{8}.

Proof.

and

\displaystyle|a^{8}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\max\{|f_{2}(a,b)|,|g_{2}(a,b)|\}\max\{|F_{1}(a,b)|,|G_{1}(a,b)\}.

Now looking at the expressions of
f_{1},f_{2},g_{1}
, and
g_{2}
, we have the following inequality:

\max\{|f_{1}|,|f_{2}|,|g_{1}|,|g_{2}|\}\leq 2\max\{|a|^{4},|b|^{4}\}.

Thus, we can combine the above inequalities to obtain the following lower bound on the height of
\varphi_{1}(P)
:

\displaystyle\max\{|a^{8}|,|b^{8}|\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\max\{|a|^{4},|b|^{4}\}\max\{|F_{1}|,|G_{1}|\}

\displaystyle\frac{\max\{|a|^{4},|b|^{4}\}}{4}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max\{|F_{1}|,|G_{1}|\}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}H(P)^{4}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle H(\varphi_{1}(P))

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)+4h(P)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle h(\varphi_{1}(P)).

We can do the exact same argument for the identities in Lemma
5.2
and obtain

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)+4h(P)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle h(\varphi_{2}(P)).

Now to obtain the upper bounds for the heights we use
x
-coordinate of the affine maps for
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
which we recall here:

\displaystyle x(\varphi_{1}(P))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{-a^{2}x^{4}+6a^{2}x^{2}-2y-a^{2}}{x^{4}+2x^{2}+1}

\displaystyle x(\varphi_{2}(P))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{a^{2}x^{4}-6a^{2}x^{2}-2y+a^{2}}{x^{4}+2x^{2}+1}.

The first way to obtain an “easy” upper bound is to simply ignore all the terms which may decrease the numerator, that it all terms which are negative. We thus obtain,

\displaystyle x(\varphi_{1}(P))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{-a^{2}x^{4}+6a^{2}x^{2}-2y-a^{2}}{x^{4}+2x^{2}+1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{6a^{2}x^{2}}{x^{4}+2x^{2}+1}

\displaystyle x(\varphi_{2}(P))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{a^{2}x^{4}-6a^{2}x^{2}-2y+a^{2}}{x^{4}+2x^{2}+1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{a^{2}x^{4}+a^{2}}{x^{4}+2x^{2}+1}.

If we let
x=x(P)=\dfrac{r}{s}
as before, we can rewrite these as:

\displaystyle x(\varphi_{1}(P))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{6a^{2}r^{2}s^{2}}{r^{4}+2r^{2}s^{2}+s^{4}}

\displaystyle x(\varphi_{2}(P))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{a^{2}r^{4}+s^{4}a^{2}}{r^{4}+2r^{2}s^{2}+s^{4}}.

Now, since the height is simply the max between the numerator and the denominator we only need to determine which of the two is largest. In other words, we have:

\displaystyle H(\varphi_{1}(P))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max\{6a^{2}H(P)^{4},4H(P)^{4}\}

\displaystyle h(\varphi_{1}(P))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\log(6)+2\log(a)+4h(P)

and

\displaystyle H(\varphi_{2}(P))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max\{2a^{2}H(P)^{4},4H(P)^{4}\}

\displaystyle h(\varphi_{2}(P))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\log(2)+2\log(a)+4h(P).

We summarize the bounds we have discussed above in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

For
P\in C_{a}(\mathbb{Q})
, we have:

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)+4h(P)\leq h(\varphi_{1}(P))\leq\log(6)+2\log(a)+4h(P)

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)+4h(P)\leq h(\varphi_{2}(P))\leq\log(2)+2\log(a)+4h(P).

Combining the inequalities from Lemma
5.3
, we obtain the following inequality:

\left|h(\varphi_{1}(P))-h(\varphi_{2}(P))\right|\leq\log(6)+2\log(a)-\log\left(\frac{1}{4}\right).

(3)

We also have the following lemma regarding the difference between the canonical height and naive height on our elliptic curve
E_{a}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
P\in E_{a}(\mathbb{Q})
. Then we have:

\left|\hat{h}_{E_{a}}(P)-h_{E_{a}}(P)\right|\leq 24.

(4)

Proof.
