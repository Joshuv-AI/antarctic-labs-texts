Spatial and Volumetric Characteristics of Glioblastoma: Associations With Clinical Presentation and Survival
Authors: Daniel J Zhou, Marc Jaskir, Julian Gal, Yombe Fonkeu, Christos Davatzikos, MacLean P Nasrallah, Jose Garcia, Rubo Xing, Stephen J Bagley, Arati Desai, Richard E Phillips, Steven Tobochnik, Colin A Ellis, James J Gugger, Nishant Sinha, Joel M Stein, Kathryn A Davis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveWe aim to comprehensively analyze how regional tumor and edema characteristics are associated with clinical presentations and survival outcomes in a large cohort of glioblastoma patients.MethodsPatients with IDH‐wildtype glioblastoma who received brain MRI from 2010 to 2023 were included. The enhancing tumor, necrotic core, and edema/infiltration volumes were automatically segmented across brain regions. Multivariable regression assessed associations between regional volumes, presenting symptomatology, and survival outcomes.ResultsOf 526 patients analyzed, initial presentations included seizure (29%), aphasia (26%), confusion (25%), headache (24%), focal weakness (22%), memory problems (20%), ataxia (16%), visual field deficits (11%), personality change (10%), and focal sensory abnormalities (7%). Distinct regional tumor patterns emerged: seizures were associated with smaller overall tumor burden but with relatively greater cortical involvement, headache with increased subcortical volumes, confusion with left hemisphere and temporal involvement, personality changes with frontal‐cingulate‐basal ganglia involvement, and focal neurologic deficits with mostly predictable patterns. While no initial presentation correlated with survival, the presence of postoperative seizures was associated with improved overall survival. Increased volume of enhancing tumor, particularly in the insula and brainstem, was associated with worse overall survival.InterpretationAnatomical tumor distribution provided prognostic information beyond overall tumor burden in glioblastoma. Regional patterns expand on the anatomical basis of symptoms. The survival benefit associated with postoperative seizures suggests distinct tumor biology or microenvironmental factors. Incorporating regional characteristics into clinical and prognostic models may improve risk stratification and inform treatment planning.Keywords: clinical presentation, glioblastoma, MRI, seizure, survival

Introduction

Glioblastoma is the most aggressive primary brain tumor, commonly presenting with headache, seizures, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and/or focal neurologic deficits [1, 2]. These symptoms are theorized to arise from factors such as tumor location, size, growth rate, associated edema, vascular compromise, and cerebrospinal fluid obstruction [2]. While the location of glioblastoma varies widely, few large‐scale studies have systematically examined how tumor location and volume relate to specific presentations or clinical outcomes [3, 4, 5].

The most common presenting symptoms, headache and seizures, have shown variable relationships with tumor characteristics in previous studies. Headache occurs in approximately half of glioblastoma cases and has been linked to increased intracranial pressure, infratentorial tumor location, and possibly tumor volume [6, 7, 8]. Seizures occur in approximately one‐third of patients by the time of diagnosis and half of patients by the end of life [9]. Limited studies of patients with glioblastoma who initially presented with seizures have demonstrated either a smaller tumor volume or no significant difference in tumor or edema size [10, 11]. Reported associations between seizures and tumor location have varied widely, implicating the frontal, occipital, temporal, or parietal lobes [10, 12, 13]. With glioblastoma, the presence of an initial seizure has been associated with greater overall survival (OS), though whether this relates to earlier detection, distinct tumor biology, or specific anatomical patterns remains unclear [9, 14].

Neuropsychiatric symptoms, while frequently reported with glioblastoma, have been poorly characterized based on tumor anatomy. Cognitive dysfunction has been attributed to direct infiltration or elevated intracranial pressure, with some studies suggesting greater frequency in dominant hemisphere lesions, particularly the left temporal lobe [15, 16, 17, 18]. Personality changes have been hypothesized to correlate with frontal lobe involvement, though supporting data remain limited to small cohorts [19]. Focal neurologic deficits are generally assumed to arise from the disruption of corresponding eloquent regions, yet the relationship between regional tumor or edema burden and specific deficits has not been systematically evaluated in large cohorts [20, 21].

Several glioblastoma prognostic models incorporate the extent of resection and molecular markers, such as MGMT promoter methylation status [22, 23]. While the involvement of eloquent cortex influences surgical planning and the extent of resection, the independent prognostic significance of regional tumor distribution remains unclear [24]. Understanding these anatomical relationships could help inform prognostic risk stratification and treatment planning.

Recent advances in neuroimaging have enabled more precise characterization of tumor and edema distribution across brain regions, creating opportunities for systematic evaluation of imaging‐clinical relationships of brain tumors. In this study, we aim to comprehensively analyze how regional tumor and edema characteristics are associated with clinical presentations and survival outcomes in a large cohort of glioblastoma patients.

Materials and Methods

Patient Characteristics

This study was approved by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board (835008, 706564), and informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized representatives. Patients were included if they had a diagnosis of glioblastoma from clinical pathology or research sequencing and underwent brain MRI between January 2010 and April 2023 at the University of Pennsylvania. All surgical resections in this cohort were performed by fellowship‐trained neurosurgeons at the University of Pennsylvania.

All cases underwent repeat molecular testing on archived tumor tissue to confirm they met 2021 WHO classification criteria for glioblastoma [25]. IDH mutation status was determined using clinical testing when available or targeted next‐generation research sequencing as previously described [26]. Patients aged ≥ 55 years with IDH‐wildtype status required at least one additional molecular feature typical of glioblastoma (EGFR amplification, TERT promoter mutation, +7/−10 chromosomal changes, or confirmatory DNA methylation profile), while patients aged 30–54 years required at least two such features or a confirmatory methylation profile [25, 27]. Patients < 30 years old were excluded to avoid misclassification of pediatric‐type high‐grade gliomas, and cases with H3 K27 or G34 alterations or pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma were excluded based on molecular and clinicopathologic review (M.N.) [25, 28]. Patients were excluded if they had unavailable or inconclusive molecular testing, had prior brain surgery at the time of imaging, received only a tumor biopsy without resection, had missing clinical information, or did not have successful automated segmentation of all three tumor components (enhancing tumor, necrotic core, and edema).

Demographic, pathologic, and imaging information were extracted as previously described [29]. Follow‐up information was extracted up to July 21, 2025. Information regarding clinical presentations was obtained through systematic chart review by neurologists (D.Z., J.G.) and a senior neurology resident physician (Y.F.). Age of the patient was defined as the age at the time of the diagnostic MRI brain scan. MGMT promoter methylation status was extracted directly from clinical notes or pathology reports. Patients without documented MGMT testing were excluded from analyses involving methylation status. If multiple signs or symptoms were documented (e.g., headache and seizure), all were recorded. The extent of tumor resection was categorized as gross total (complete removal of all visible tumor), near gross total (≥ 95% of tumor volume removed), or subtotal (< 95% removed) based on operative notes or, if not available, postoperative MRI scan. Postoperative functional status was assessed using the Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score obtained at the first outpatient follow‐up visit after tumor resection. When KPS was unavailable, the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status was used and converted to KPS equivalents (ECOG ≤ 1 corresponding to KPS ≥ 70; ECOG > 1 corresponding to KPS < 70) [30].

For this study, we operationally defined tumor‐related epilepsy (TRE) as new‐onset seizures attributed to the presence of the brain tumor itself, occurring either preoperatively or > 7 days postoperatively without identifiable acute precipitants. Seizures from postoperative complications, separate from TRE, were defined as new‐onset seizures occurring within 7 days after tumor resection or directly caused by complications such as intracranial hemorrhage or encephalitis. Survival analyses in association with postoperative seizures and TRE were performed, excluding seizures secondary to postoperative complications. Postoperative seizures and TRE were treated as categorical variables, characterized as present or absent.

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval in days from initial tumor resection to death or last follow‐up. Progression‐free survival (PFS) was defined as the interval in days from initial tumor resection to the first date of radiographic progression as documented in clinical radiology reports and neuro‐oncology notes. Only cases where radiologists or neuro‐oncologists definitively expressed concern for progression were counted as events; equivocal findings requiring follow‐up imaging were not considered progression events.

Imaging Analyses

All MRI scans were skull‐stripped and preprocessed using a standardized pipeline to make image dimensions and voxel sizes uniform across all subjects and rigidly moved to the SRI24 space [29, 31]. Brain tumor components, including contrast‐enhancing regions, necrotic tumor core, and surrounding edema or tumor infiltration, were segmented using validated deep learning algorithms (Figure 1) [29].

To account for anatomical distortion induced by tumor mass effect, T1‐weighted images underwent additional deformable registration to the SRI24 template using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) [32, 33]. This process corrected for tumor‐related brain deformation while preserving the overall spatial alignment established by rigid registration [34, 35]. The deformable registration employed a symmetric normalization (SyN) algorithm with conservative parameters and constrained smoothing to ensure local anatomical corrections without global displacement [36, 37]. Cross‐correlation was used as the similarity metric with histogram matching disabled to preserve the original intensity distributions of the images [38]. The resulting deformation fields were then applied to the tumor segmentations using nearest‐neighbor interpolation to maintain discrete label values, generating mass effect‐corrected tumor masks positioned relative to normalized anatomical structures. This approach allowed for more accurate anatomical localization by computationally unwarping tumors to their positions in undistorted brain anatomy, thereby improving the precision of atlas‐based regional overlap analysis.

The USCLobes atlas was transformed from BrainSuite BCI‐DNI coordinate space to SRI24 space, while the Harvard‐Oxford atlas was transformed from MNI152 space to SRI24 space. Both transformations also used the ANTs SyN with cross‐correlation similarity metric, applied to the atlas label maps using nearest‐neighbor interpolation to preserve discrete label values [32]. The registered brain images, along with segmented tumor and edema volumes, were then parcellated into regions of interest (ROIs) using the USCLobes atlas for lobar (both associated gray and white matter) and cerebellar structures and the Harvard‐Oxford atlas for the basal ganglia, thalamus, and brainstem [39, 40].

Tumor and edema volumes were quantified by calculating their spatial overlap with each atlas region. Total volumes were calculated across all tumor components regardless of tumor focality; patients with multifocal or multicentric disease were analyzed using combined volumes across all enhancing foci. ROIs were grouped into the following anatomical regions: frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital, insula, cingulate, basal ganglia, thalamus, brainstem, and cerebellum. For each ROI, volumes of contrast‐enhancing tumor, necrotic core, and edema/infiltration were quantified and aggregated by hemisphere (left, right) or anatomical region.

Registration quality was visually validated by a board‐certified neurologist (D.Z.) through review of tumor segmentation and atlas overlay alignment for a random sample of 10 cases. For each case, atlas regions were overlaid on the patient's MRI with both original and mass effect‐corrected tumor segmentations to verify appropriate spatial alignment and accuracy of tumor‐atlas volume overlap calculations. The deformable registration successfully corrected for mass effect‐induced anatomical distortions, with corrected tumor positions showing improved alignment with atlas boundaries compared to rigidly registered images. Since analyses focused on broad anatomical regions rather than fine‐grained ROI‐specific measurements, the mass effect correction provided meaningful improvements in regional volume attribution while maintaining computational feasibility across the large patient cohort.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SciPy and Lifelines in Python. Continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Significance was defined as p < 0.05 or, after multiple comparisons correction via the Benjamini‐Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR), q < 0.05.

Regional tumor‐presentation associations used multivariable logistic regression for each region‐feature pair, with regional tumor volume (cm3) as the primary predictor and clinical presentation (binary) as the outcome, adjusting for age and sex. FDR correction was applied within each tumor compartment and clinical presentation across laterality or anatomical brain regions.

Cortical tumor proportions based on seizure presentation were calculated by dividing the sum of lobar volumes (frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital, insular, and cingulate) by the total regional tumor volume (lobar regions plus basal ganglia, thalamus, brainstem, and cerebellum) for each tumor compartment. Postoperative seizure and TRE survival analyses excluded those who developed seizures due to acute secondary causes (e.g., hemorrhage). Multivariable logistic regression models assessed associations between cortical tumor proportion and seizure presentations (initial seizure vs. none, postoperative seizure vs. none, and TRE vs. no seizures), adjusting for age, sex, and MGMT methylation. FDR correction was applied across the three tumor compartments within each seizure type.

For survival analysis, patients alive (OS) or progression‐free (PFS) at last follow‐up were censored at last contact or imaging. Kaplan–Meier estimates calculated median survival with IQR. Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HR) with 95% CI, adjusting for age, sex, MGMT methylation status, extent of resection, and postoperative KPS. Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, and covariates that violated the assumption were handled via stratification. For clinical presentation associations, all presentations were included simultaneously in a single model. For regional tumor volume associations, each anatomical region was assessed separately for each tumor compartment, and FDR correction was applied across tumor compartments within total volume, laterality, or anatomical regions. Survival outcomes across diagnostic years (2010–2014, 2015–2019, 2020–2023) were compared using multivariate log‐rank test.

Results

Population Characteristics

A total of 526 patients with glioblastoma meeting WHO 2021 criteria were included, of whom 199 (38%) were female. Median age at first brain MRI was 65 years (IQR 60–72). MGMT promoter methylation status was available in 466 (89%) patients, with 201 (43%) methylated. All patients received a surgical resection: gross total (n = 206, 39%), near‐gross total (n = 122, 23%), or subtotal (n = 198, 38%). Postoperative KPS or ECOG was available for 501 (95%) patients, with 347 (69%) with KPS ≥ 70 or ECOG ≤ 1.

Tumor components, including enhancing tumor, necrotic core, and edema, were segmented and their volumes analyzed by hemisphere, lobe, and deep gray matter structure (Figure 1). Representative segmentations and population‐level voxel frequency maps are shown in Figure 1A,B. Tumor and edema volumes were similar between hemispheres (Figure 1C). The temporal lobe contained the highest mean enhancing tumor volume, followed by frontal and parietal regions (Figure 1D). Within the deep structures, tumors had greater involvement in the basal ganglia and thalamus (Figure 1E). All tumors were supratentorial, with 138 patients (26%) having additional infratentorial involvement.

Clinical Presentations

Initial clinical presentations included new‐onset seizure (n = 154, 29%), headache (n = 128, 24%), confusion (n = 130, 25%), memory problems (n = 104, 20%), personality change (n = 55, 10%), aphasia (n = 138, 26%), focal weakness (n = 114, 22%), focal sensory abnormalities (n = 39, 7%), visual field deficits (n = 57, 11%), and ataxia/impaired coordination or balance (n = 82, 16%). In 9 (2%) patients, the tumor was discovered incidentally.

Of the patients without a preoperative seizure, 139 (37%) patients developed postoperative seizures attributed to the tumor, 62 (44%) with tumor progression at the time of first seizure. Of the 199 patients with any postoperative seizure, 121 (61%) were on at least one antiseizure medication at the time of their first postoperative seizure. Overall, 275 (53%) had TRE, which we define here as having a preoperative or postoperative seizure associated with the tumor and excluding postoperative complications. An additional 18 patients developed seizures from postoperative complications: acute postoperative seizures within 7 days of resection (n = 12), intracranial hemorrhage from other complications (n = 3), brain abscess or ventriculitis (n = 2), and hypoxic–ischemic injury following cardiac arrest (n = 1).

At the time of first MRI brain scan, patients who initially presented with seizure or headache were younger than those who did not, and those with aphasia were older (Table 1). A higher proportion of females presented with focal sensory symptoms. MGMT promoter methylation status did not differ by initial presentation.

Survival Outcomes

At the time of analysis, 13 (2%) patients were alive, and 513 (98%) deceased. Of the 520 cases with surveillance imaging, 424 (82%) experienced radiographic progression following the first resection. The median OS was 428 (IQR 214–696) days, and median PFS was 136 (84–259) days.

Increasing age was associated with shorter OS (Spearman ρ = −0.33, p < 0.001) and PFS (Spearman ρ = −0.18, p < 0.001; Figure S1), though when dichotomized at 65 years, significance remained only for OS (p < 0.001) but not PFS (p = 0.531; Table 2). Patients with MGMT‐methylated tumors had significantly longer OS (p < 0.001) and PFS (p < 0.001). Patients who underwent gross total or near‐total resection had significantly longer OS (p < 0.001, p = 0.002) and PFS (p < 0.001, p = 0.027) compared to subtotal resection. Patients with postoperative KPS ≥ 70 had significantly longer OS (p < 0.001) but not PFS (p = 0.103). OS and PFS did not differ across diagnostic year groups (2010–2014, 2015–2019, 2020–2023; p = 0.737 and p = 0.973; Table S1).

In contrast, headache, cognitive, and behavioral symptoms were associated with larger enhancing tumor volumes. Those with headaches had greater total tumor and edema burden, particularly in the temporal lobe, insula, basal ganglia, and thalamus. Confusion and memory difficulties were associated with increased enhancing tumor volume in the temporal lobe, and those with confusion additionally had increased necrotic tumor in the insula and edema/infiltration in the parietal lobe, basal ganglia, and thalamus. Personality changes were associated with increased enhancing tumor or edema volumes in the frontal, cingulate, and basal ganglia.

Focal neurologic deficits demonstrated mostly expected anatomical correlations. Aphasia was associated with increased left hemisphere and decreased right hemisphere volume for all tumor components. Patients who reported focal weakness had greater right hemisphere volumes, especially parietal lobe, in all tumor components and greater edema/infiltration volume in the frontal lobe, cingulate, and thalamus. Patients who reported sensory abnormalities had smaller left hemisphere total tumor and edema volumes overall. Patients who reported visual field deficits had increased volume of all tumor components in the occipital lobe and decreased enhancing tumor volume in the frontal lobe, insula, and basal ganglia. Patients who reported ataxia had increased tumor and edema volume in the right hemisphere, particularly in the parietal lobe, as well as increased edema/infiltration in the thalamus.

Enhancing tumor volumes (per cm3), including total volume, were independently associated with worse OS (Table 5A). At the regional level, enhancing tumor volume in the insula and brainstem was associated with worse OS (Table 5A). For PFS, regional associations were less pronounced (Table 5B).

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that the anatomical location of enhancing tumor, necrotic core, and peritumoral edema/infiltration provided prognostic information beyond overall radiologic tumor burden, with insula and brainstem involvement independently associated with worse survival. Clinically, while no initial presentation independently predicted survival, the presence of postoperative seizures or TRE was associated with improved OS. Additionally, distinct regional tumor patterns correlated with specific clinical presentations, providing insights into the anatomical basis of glioblastoma symptoms.

The median OS of 428 days in our cohort is consistent with recent real‐world series of IDH‐wildtype GBM, which report median OS of 11–14 months, compared with 16–21 months in clinical trial populations with performance status eligibility requirements [5, 9, 41, 42, 43]. As our models adjusted for extent of resection, the association between insula and brainstem involvement and worse OS is not fully explained by surgical factors alone and may reflect distinct biological properties, such as direct white matter proximity or differences in tumor microenvironment. The prognostic significance of anatomical distribution aligns with evidence that glioblastoma spatial patterns are non‐random and that molecular subtypes show distinct anatomical preferences influencing outcomes [44]. Our findings suggest that spatial characteristics may provide prognostic value warranting further investigation alongside molecular markers.

The regional distribution of brain tumor and edema with respect to initial clinical presentation complements the limited literature. Seizures were associated with smaller tumor and edema volumes overall and in nearly all regions, though more notably so in the deeper structures of the brain. Conversely, headache was associated with increased overall tumor and edema volumes, consistent with previous literature, although the increased regional volumes localized to the temporal lobe and subcortical structures have not been previously reported [7, 8]. The presentations of confusion or memory complaints were independently associated with increased volume of combined tumor and edema in the temporal lobe, consistent with prior literature [16, 18]. Increased edema in the basal ganglia and thalamus associated with confusion additionally suggests a disruption of attention and executive networks that depend on these deep gray matter structures [45]. Furthermore, while frontal lobe involvement of the enhancing tumor and edema in personality changes has been previously reported, the additional significant involvement of the cingulate and basal ganglia (q < 0.001) in our cohort suggests that personality alterations result from disruption of integrated frontal‐cingulate‐striatal circuits rather than isolated cortical damage [46, 47].

Focal neurologic deficits demonstrated mostly expected anatomical correlations that validate our regional analysis approach. Patients with aphasia showed the predicted pattern of increased tumor and edema volume in the left hemisphere with decreased volume in the right hemisphere. Visual field deficits were strongly associated with occipital lobe involvement. Patients with focal weakness showed greater tumor volume in the right hemisphere, which may reflect right hemisphere motor involvement or the tendency to report language rather than motor symptoms with left hemisphere lesions. Additionally, weakness was associated with edema involvement in the thalamus and cingulate, which may disrupt motor planning and initiation networks that complement primary motor pathways. The pattern of increased parietal lobe involvement for both weakness and ataxia suggests effects on motor planning networks, though the right parietal predominance is also consistent with hemineglect presentations, which may mimic these presentations [48]. Cerebellar tumor volume was not significantly associated with balance problems, likely due to the relatively small number of cerebellar cases. These findings demonstrate that regional tumor analysis can capture clinically meaningful brain‐behavior relationships.

Our analysis of different tumor compartments revealed largely similar regional associations between enhancing tumor and peritumoral edema across most clinical presentations. This consistency suggests that both components could be contributing to symptom generation through disruption of local brain networks. However, some subtle differences emerged, such as ataxia showing edema involvement in the thalamus that was not seen with enhancing tumor. This pattern may reflect how infiltrative edema can disrupt structures involved in sensorimotor integration and coordination, suggesting coordination difficulties may be particularly sensitive to edema‐related tissue disruption [49]. The necrotic core showed weaker and less consistent associations with clinical presentations, suggesting that this compartment represents non‐functional tissue that contributes less directly to symptom generation. These findings suggest that compartment‐specific analysis may reveal clinically relevant differences not observed by combined assessments.

The presentation of seizure demonstrated unique patterns in both anatomical distribution and survival associations that differed from other clinical presentations. We additionally found that those with initial seizure or TRE had a greater proportion of tumor located in the cortical regions, suggesting that even modest lobar involvement could become epileptogenic when it predominates relative to deep structures. Lobar tumor infiltration, including both cortical gray matter infiltration and disruption of local white matter connectivity, may create conditions favoring seizure generation and propagation. Conversely, tumors with greater involvement of deep structures may have a lower likelihood of affecting cortical regions or potentially disrupt critical subcortical networks that support seizure propagation [50]. Importantly, this pattern was not observed with postoperative seizures. While initial seizures showed no independent survival benefit, the development of postoperative seizures and TRE were associated with improved OS but not PFS. This differed from prior studies that found survival benefits for initial seizures alone [9, 14]. The survival benefit associated with postoperative seizures, occurring independently of initial seizure status, may reflect distinct tumor biology, altered tumor‐brain interactions in patients who develop epilepsy, use of anti‐seizure medications, or improved clinical monitoring [51, 52]. This benefit was specific to tumor‐related seizures, as we excluded seizures from postoperative complications, supporting a biological mechanism. The lack of PFS benefit despite improved OS and smaller tumor and edema argues against the lead time bias theory that seizures facilitate earlier glioblastoma detection and also suggests that clinical seizure activity does not directly impact radiologic aggressiveness. While these findings could suggest potential biological differences underlying seizure development in glioblastoma, the mechanisms remain unclear. Although IDH‐mutant gliomas are known to have increased seizure susceptibility related to 2‐hydroxyglutarate production, this is unlikely to account for our findings, as all cases were confirmed IDH‐wildtype through targeted next‐generation sequencing or DNA methylation profiling [53, 54]. Comprehensive molecular and epigenetic profiling in future studies may help elucidate the biological basis of the seizure‐survival association [54, 55].

This study has several important limitations. First, the retrospective single‐center design limits generalizability, as our cohort may not represent broader populations, and treatment practices at our institution may differ from other centers. Additionally, patients who received only biopsy without resection were excluded, limiting the cohort to surgical candidates and potentially biasing toward more favorable tumors and outcomes, with deep‐seated or unresectable tumors potentially underrepresented. Second, clinical presentations were extracted from clinical notes rather than standardized assessments, potentially missing undocumented symptoms. Some symptoms were inherently subjective (confusion, personality changes), and we excluded non‐specific presentations (e.g., fatigue, nausea) and rarely documented findings (e.g., apraxia). Preoperative KPS was not consistently documented, while postoperative KPS was available for 95% of patients and included as a covariate for survival analyses. Extent of resection categorization relied on operative notes or postoperative MRI reports, as volumetric analysis from pre‐ and postoperative imaging was unavailable for this cohort, introducing potential variability. Several clinical factors, including medical comorbidities, use of tumor treating fields, and timing of antiseizure medications, were not systematically recorded. While the majority of patients received standard‐of‐care temozolomide chemoradiation per the Stupp protocol, individual treatment variations such as clinical trial enrollment and second‐line therapies were not systematically captured [56]. This retrospective extraction introduces bias through variable documentation practices across specialties and time periods, with potential under‐representation of subtle symptoms. Patients without MGMT methylation status were excluded from relevant analyses on survival, affecting 16% of our cohort. Third, this study spans a 13‐year period during which important changes in glioblastoma treatment occurred, including bevacizumab adoption, improved surgical techniques, and refined radiation protocols. While survival did not differ significantly across diagnostic year groups, temporal bias cannot be fully excluded. Fourth, imaging analyses had inherent limitations. Despite preprocessing to standardize images across multiple scanners, some technical variability may remain. Automated segmentation achieved good performance on the BrATS validation dataset but remains subject to errors [29]. Notably, our segmentation approach did not necessarily distinguish between vasogenic edema and nonenhancing infiltrative tumor, which may represent distinct pathophysiological processes with different relationships to seizure development. Additionally, brain regions were subject to distortion due to mass effect from the tumor and edema. While the deformable registration algorithm helped compensate for gross anatomical shifts and warping, residual challenges remain as registration cannot restore tissue that has been destroyed or infiltrated. Visual inspection confirmed that the brain atlases were appropriately aligned within the broader anatomical regions analyzed, supporting the validity of our regional groupings, despite potential ROI‐level inaccuracies inherent to atlas‐based parcellation in the setting of mass effect.

This comprehensive analysis of 526 glioblastoma patients demonstrates that anatomical tumor distribution provides prognostic information beyond overall tumor burden, with specific regional involvement patterns independently predicting clinical outcomes. Our findings reveal distinct regional patterns underlying specific clinical presentations, validating and providing new information on the anatomical basis of neurologic impairment due to glioblastoma. These results suggest that incorporating regional tumor distribution into prognostic models may improve risk stratification. Furthermore, understanding these spatial relationships can inform presurgical planning, guide patient counseling regarding expected deficits, and potentially identify patients at higher risk for specific neurological complications. The association between postoperative seizures and improved OS also suggests that seizure development during follow‐up may carry prognostic significance and warrant further investigation as a potential stratification factor in clinical trials. Future studies could employ more detailed anatomical parcellation and white matter tractography to better delineate specific neural circuits underlying seizure generation and cognitive dysfunction, while longitudinal imaging analyses could reveal how regional progression patterns influence clinical deterioration and treatment response over time. As automated imaging analysis continues to advance, integrating anatomical location data with molecular markers and clinical factors may enhance personalized treatment strategies for glioblastoma patients.
