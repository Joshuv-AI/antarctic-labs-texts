Predictive Value of HLA‐Derived T‐Cell Epitope and B‐Cell Epitope Matching Algorithms for Pancreas Transplant Outcomes
Authors: Zakieh Zare, Matthias Niemann, Kasra Shirini, Ian Booth, Bethany Dale, Richard Ugarte, Silke V. Niederhaus, Cinthia B. Drachenberg, Abdolreza Haririan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDonor/recipient HLA molecular mismatches are predictive of kidney transplant outcomes. Their role in pancreas transplantation has not been well studied.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study in 281 pancreas (± kidney) transplant recipients during 2000–2018, followed for average 53.8 months. T‐cell epitope matching was performed by the PIRCHE‐II algorithm (T2) and B‐cell epitope mismatch using Snow (B2) and HLA Matchmaker algorithms. Primary outcome was pancreas acute rejection, secondary outcomes included pancreas graft failure and composite of both, and kidney graft failure.ResultsPIRCHE‐T2 score (59.3±22.3) was not associated with study outcomes (HR 0.99, 95%CI 0.98–1.00, p = 0.31 for pancreas rejection). However, increasing PIRCHE‐B score (15.1±6.3) was predictive of lower risk of acute rejection (aHR 0.94; 95%CI 0.9–0.99, p = 0.024) and graft failure (aHR 0.96; 95%CI 0.93–0.99; p = 0.021), driven by PIRCHE‐B‐Class II score (aHR 0.91; 95%CI 0.85–0.98, p = 0.01; aHR 0.92; 95%CI 0.88–0.97; p = 0.001, respectively). Total eplet mismatch (19.58±7.40) was highly correlated with PIRCHE‐B score (ρ = 0.82, p < 0.0001), showing similar associations. However, the molecular scores were not associated with kidney graft failure (p > 0.57 for all).ConclusionOur study suggests that higher B‐cell epitope mismatch loads are paradoxically associated with improved pancreas transplant outcomes, while PIRCHE‐T2 scores demonstrated limited predictive value.

Introduction

Pancreas transplantation (PT) offers a curative option for carefully selected patients with insulin‐dependent diabetes by enhancing the quality of life and helping to stabilize or improve its various complications, particularly for those with complications such as hypoglycemia unawareness, retinopathy or nephropathy [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. It is performed in one of three settings: simultaneous pancreas‐kidney (SPK) or pancreas after kidney (PAK) transplantation, or pancreas transplant alone (PTA) [1, 2, 3]. Among them, SPK is the most frequent form and is associated with the best long‐term outcomes, likely partially due to the immunologic synergy between the kidney and pancreas grafts [4, 5, 6].

Despite the advances in surgical techniques, immunosuppressive therapy, and graft monitoring, pancreas transplant recipients remain at higher immunologic risk compared to kidney transplant recipients [7]. Acute cellular and antibody‐mediated rejection of the pancreas is associated with impaired graft survival [8]. HLA antigens are the main targets for T‐cell and B‐cell reactivity by recognition of the molecular mismatch between the donor and recipient by these cells [9]. Immunosuppressive agents are used to circumvent the immunologic response and maintain the health and function of the allograft [10]. Accurately identifying patients at increased immunologic risk is essential for optimizing immunosuppressive strategies and improving transplant outcomes.

Conventional serological HLA matching has long been used to assess the degree of donor‐recipient immunologic discrepancy with higher levels of matching associated with improved graft outcomes [11]. However, the amino acid configurations of different HLA loci have homologies and only polymorphic sequence positions play a role in allorecognition by the T‐cell receptors or antibodies [12]. The advent of high‐resolution HLA typing at allele level and elucidation of 3‐dimensional structures of HLA molecules have provided novel tools to decipher the donor‐recipient molecular mismatches that contribute to the antigenicity and immunogenicity of HLA antigens. Emerging evidence indicates that molecular mismatches offer superior predictive value for the development of de novo donor‐specific HLA antibodies (dnDSA) and subsequent graft dysfunction, especially in sensitized recipients and those with a history of prior transplantation [13].

Development of dnDSA by B‐cells is a result of their interaction with CD4+ T helper cells that recognize allo‐epitopes derived from B‐cell targeted antigen presented by HLA Class II molecules. In this context, molecular HLA mismatch algorithms have emerged as tools to refine immunologic risk prediction beyond traditional antigen‐level matching. The Predicted Indirectly Recognizable HLA Epitopes (PIRCHE) algorithm estimates the number of donor‐derived HLA peptides that can be presented by the recipient's HLA class II molecules on CD4+ T cells (PIRCHE‐T2). Higher PIRCHE‐T2 scores have been associated with an increased risk of developing dnDSA and graft failure in kidney transplantation, particularly for HLA‐DQ mismatches [14, 15, 16]. Despite the strong evidence supporting this association in kidney transplant recipients, similar association may not be present in PT. The immunologic response could be different to pancreas allograft, reflected in higher risk of rejection, and autoimmune response in type 1 diabetics could modify the strength of this association, in addition to playing a role in graft outcome. Moreover, within the pancreas allograft the beta cells are not the primary targets of the alloimmune response, whereas the acinar and endothelial cells are primarily involved in TCMR and ABMR. In addition, it is not clear whether the aberrant expression of Class II HLA antigens by β‐cells that is important in pathogenesis of type 1 diabetes, has any role in alloimmune response to pancreas allograft.

With identification of larger number of HLA allele 3‐dimensional structures and better understanding of protein folding, B‐cell epitope matching algorithms have evolved. The PIRCHE Snow B‐cell epitope matching algorithm was developed using these advances to identify surface‐accessible, protruding amino acid mismatches between the donor and recipient to estimate their HLA epitope incompatibility [17, 18]. The number of exposed mismatched amino acids is called the PIRCHE‐B score, relating to B‐cell molecular mismatch load [17, 19]. Higher PIRCHE‐B scores have been associated with the development of dnDSA and inferior graft outcomes, highlighting its potential utility in refining immunological risk stratification in kidney transplantation [20, 21]. However, the utility of epitope matching using these two algorithms in PT has not been well‐defined and may differ with kidney transplantation due to the distinct immunologic dynamics in dual‐organ or isolated pancreas transplants [16, 22].

We sought to evaluate the association of T‐cell and B‐cell epitope molecular matching using PIRCHE‐T2 and PIRCHE‐B algorithms, respectively, with transplant outcomes in a large cohort of pancreas transplant recipients.

Materials and Methods

This is a retrospective cohort study conducted at the University of Maryland Medical Center. All adult recipients of pancreas transplants performed between January 1, 2000, and May 31, 2018, were screened for eligibility. Patients were excluded if the donor or recipient HLA typing was unavailable or if the follow‐up data were incomplete. Clinical and transplant‐related variables were obtained from institutional databases and electronic medical records. The Institutional Review Board approved the study protocol (HP‐00086621).

All patients received induction therapy, lymphocyte depleting agents, thymoglobulin or alemtuzumab, for SPK and PTA, or basiliximab for PAK. Maintenance immunosuppression for all patients included tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil/sodium and a steroid withdrawal taper. For patients who received a repeat PTA or PAK, low dose prednisone was continued. All patients received 3 to 6 months of cytomegalovirus (CMV) prophylaxis and 6 months of Pneumocystis pneumonia prophylaxis [23, 24, 25]. Exocrine drainage for all patients was enteric and venous drainage was mostly systemic [23, 24, 25]. It is of note that our institutional protocols regarding surgical procedures and post‐transplant care were unchanged during the study period. To assess era effect, we divided the study period into 3 parts, before 2007, 2007‐May 2012, and June2012‐end of study period and compared the study endpoints between the 3 periods.

After hospital discharge, patients were followed according to the recommended guidelines [26]. Graft dysfunction, evidenced by elevated amylase/lipase or hyperglycemia was explored with appropriate imaging and biopsy, as indicated. Solitary pancreas allografts underwent surveillance biopsies at 3‐ and 12‐months post‐transplant, if possible. Biopsies were evaluated for rejection according to the Banff criteria [27]. Recipients with biopsy‐proven rejection, in either pancreas or kidney biopsy as surrogate, in those that pancreas biopsy was not feasible, were treated. Patients with grade I rejection were treated with 3 doses of intravenous Methylprednisolone 500 mg followed by prednisone taper, and others were treated with thymoglobulin 6–9 mg/Kg.

Using standard clinical laboratory methods, donor HLA typing was performed by the respective donating organ agency, and recipient typing was performed at the center immunogenetic laboratory at the HLA‐A, ‐B, ‐C, ‐DRB1, and ‐DQB1 loci for both. In the absence of allele‐level HLA typing, multiple genotype imputation was used to convert donor and recipient low‐ and intermediate‐resolution typing data based on a combined NMDP haplotype frequency population [28, 29]. Molecular mismatch analysis included two approaches. T‐cell epitope matching was performed by the PIRCHE‐II algorithm (www.pirche.com; version 4.2, Frost 1.1, 30% binding rank), Class I‐derived epitopes were calculated for HLA‐A, ‐B, and ‐C loci, and Class II‐derived epitopes for HLA‐DRB1 and ‐DQB1 loci. The total PIRCHE‐T2 score is the sum of all locus‐specific scores for each donor‐recipient pair [30].

B‐cell epitope mismatch scores for the listed loci were computed using the Snow algorithm (www.pirche.com; version 4.2, Snow 1.0 surface/protrusion). Both total PIRCHE‐B scores and their subcomponents (PIRCHE‐B1 and PIRCHE‐B2, for Class I and Class II, respectively) were analyzed. In addition, B‐cell epitope mismatch analysis was performed by counting eplet mismatch loads using the antibody‐verified eplet definitions deposited in the Eplet Registry (www.epregistry.com.br, accessed June 2022) by interlocus (HLA‐A, ‐B, ‐C) and intralocus (HLA‐DRB1, ‐DQB1) comparisons.

The primary outcome of the study was acute rejection of the pancreas allograft defined as biopsy‐proven or clinically suspected rejection, in cases that biopsy was not feasible. The secondary outcomes of interest included pancreas graft failure defined as need for initiation of insulin or non‐insulin sensitizing oral hypoglycemic agents for more than 3 months, composite of graft failure or acute rejection, and kidney graft failure, defined as return to dialysis or retransplantation.

Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations; categorical variables were reported as frequencies with percentages. Survival analysis methods including Kaplan‐Meier estimators and Cox proportional hazards regression were used to examine the association of the clinical and immunogenetic variables with the outcomes. Normality of molecular mismatch scores were tested both visually and using Shapiro‐Wilk test. The scores were included in the models as their actual values. In addition, in secondary analyses we used log‐transformed scores to compensate for any non‐linearity (reported in Appendix). Results are reported as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Proportional hazard assumptions were verified using Schoenfeld residuals. Goodness of fit plots were used to choose the best models. All analyses were performed using Stata version 15.0. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study included a total of 281 pancreas transplant recipients (Figure 1). The mean recipient age at the time of transplant was 43.8 years (±9.1), and the mean donor age was 25.7 years (±9.6). Most recipients underwent SPK transplantation (70.4%), while 16.3% and 13.1% received PAK and PTA, respectively. Induction immunosuppression included alemtuzumab in 57.7% of patients, ATG in 31.32%, and basiliximab in 11.0%. Patient and transplant related characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Post‐transplant immunosuppressive drug levels or dosages, as well as steroid use during the follow‐up are shown in Table S1. The patients were followed for an average of 58.3 months post‐transplant (IQR 15.4–91.9) (Figure S1).

Regarding molecular mismatch scores, the mean PIRCHE‐T2 score was 59.3 (±22.3; n = 281; median 60.2), with mean PIRCHE‐T2 Class I score as 35.9 (±15.7), and mean PIRCHE‐T2 Class II score as 23.7 (±12.0). The distribution and relationship between PIRCHE‐T2 and PIRCHE‐B scores are shown in Figure 2. There was a significant correlation between the two scores (ρ = 0.40, p < 0.001) (Figure S2). A total of 42 patients experienced biopsy‐confirmed or clinically suspected acute pancreas rejection. In univariable analyses, several clinical and molecular predictors were identified as being significantly associated with the risk of rejection. Recipient age (HR 0.950; 95% CI 0.918–0.984; p = 0.004), type of transplant (HR 2.77; 95% CI 1.35–5.75; p = 0.006 for PAK, and HR 3.10; 95% CI 1.45–6.60; p = 0.003 for PTA, compared to SPK (Figure S3)), and type of induction (HR 0.370; 95% CI 0.195–0.703; p = 0.002 for alemtuzumab; and HR 0.44, 95% CI 0.15–1.28, p = 0.13 for basiliximab, compared with ATG) were associated with pancreas acute rejection.

Examining molecular mismatches, total PIRCHE‐T2 score was not associated with the risk of acute rejection (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.98–1.01, p = 0.31). Log‐transformation of the score did not change this observation. This was also true for both PIRCHE‐T2‐Class I (HR 0.998, 95% CI 0.98–1.02) and PIRCHE‐T2‐Class II scores (HR 0.975; 95% CI 0.949–1.002; p = 0.072). Regarding antibody epitope mismatches, total PIRCHE‐B score was predictive of acute rejection (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.89–0.99, p = 0.013) with higher scores associated with lower risk. Unlike PIRCHE‐B‐Class I score (HR 0.97; 95% CI 0.88–1.06, p = 0.52), PIRCHE‐B‐Class II score was significantly associated with this outcome (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.85–0.98, p = 0.007). After adjustment for the clinical factors, as listed above, total PIRCHE‐B (aHR 0.94; 95% CI 0.9–0.99, p = 0.024) and PIRCHE‐B‐Class II (aHR 0.91; 95% CI 0.85–0.98, p = 0.01) remained significant predictive factors. Eighty‐six patients experienced pancreas graft failure during the follow‐up period. In univariate analysis, clinical factors including recipient age, transplant type, prior transplant, or induction regimen were not significantly associated with graft loss. In evaluating molecular mismatches, the higher total PIRCHE‐B score was predictive of lower risk of graft failure (HR 0.96; 95% CI 0.93–0.99; p = 0.021) that was driven by PIRCHE‐B‐Class II score (HR 0.92; 95% CI 0.88–0.97; p = 0.001). During the follow up, a total of 103 patients experienced the composite outcome of either acute rejection or graft failure. In univariable models, alemtuzumab induction (HR 0.627; 95% CI 0.409–0.961; p = 0.032) and older age (HR 0.976; 95% CI 0.955–0.998; p = 0.035) were associated with lower risk, while prior transplant status (HR 1.58; 95% CI 1.02–2.43; p = 0.037) and solitary pancreas transplant were associated with increased risk of this outcome (HR 1.665; 95% CI 1.007–2.751; p = 0.047 for PAK, and HR 1.875; 95% CI 1.110–3.167; p = 0.019 for PTA, compared to SPK). Increasing number of total PIRCHE‐B score (HR 0.96; 95% CI 0.93–0.99; p = 0.013), driven by elevated PIRCHE‐B‐Class II scores (HR 0.93; 95% CI 0.89–0.97; p = 0.001) was associated with a lower risk of the composite outcome. Similarly, elevated eplet mismatch loads were associated with lower risk of the composite outcome (adjusted HR 0.97; 95% CI 0.95–0.99; p = 0.017), also steered by eplet‐Class II load (HR 0.93; 95% CI 0.90–0.97; p = 0.001). In contrast, neither PIRCHE‐T2 score was significantly associated with this outcome.

Adjustment for the clinical variables, as noted, the total PIRCHE‐B score (adjusted HR 0.96; 95% CI 0.93–0.99; p = 0.022) and PIRCHE‐B‐Class II score (adjusted HR 0.93; 95% CI 0.89–0.97; p = 0.001) remained significant predictors of the outcome, as did the eplet mismatch load (adjusted HR 0.97; 95% CI 0.95–1.00; p = 0.039). The results of Cox models for the composite outcome are summarized in Table 3.

We also studied the association of donor‐recipient epitope matching scores with kidney allograft failure in SPK or PAK recipients. Forty‐four patients experienced kidney graft failure over the follow‐up period. In univariable analysis, among the clinical variables African‐American race was significantly associated with an increased risk of kidney graft failure (HR 2.31; 95% CI 1.21–4.42; p = 0.011). Transplant type also influenced the outcome, recipients of PAK transplants showed a significantly lower risk than those with SPK transplants (HR 0.105; 95% CI 0.014–0.767; p = 0.026). In contrast to pancreas‐related outcomes, B‐cell and T‐cell molecular matching scores were not significant predictors of kidney graft failure (p > 0.57 for all) (Table S4).

Discussion

In this study we assessed the predictive value of the extent of molecular T‐cell and B‐cell HLA mismatch by PIRCHE‐T2, PIRCHE‐B and eplet mismatch load algorithms in a large single‐center cohort of pancreas transplant recipients. Unexpectedly, we found that higher antibody epitope mismatch loads as predicted by PIRCHE‐B and eplet load scores, driven by HLA‐Class II mismatches, were associated with reduced risks of acute rejection of the pancreas, pancreas graft failure, and the composite outcome. In contrast, total and HLA Class‐specific PIRCHE‐T2 scores were not associated with pancreas rejection, or pancreas or kidney graft failure.

These findings stand in contrast to the evidence reported in kidney transplantation, where both T cell epitope and antibody epitope mismatch load algorithms have been associated with increased immunologic risk, including higher rates of DSA formation, T‐cell mediated rejection, and graft failure. Geneugelijk et al. [15] reported that higher total PIRCHE‐T2 scores were significantly associated with an increased risk of kidney graft failure in a large European cohort using high‐resolution typing. Similarly, Lachmann et al. [14] demonstrated that PIRCHE‐T2 scores and eplet mismatch loads independently predicted the development of dnDSA after kidney transplantation. Kosmoliaptsis et al. [31] also found that mismatches at the amino acid level and structural differences better predicted alloantibody response than traditional antigen matching in kidney recipients. We cannot explain the observed discrepancy in our cohort but could postulate that these differences may reflect distinct alloimmune mechanisms involved in PT.

In a study of 18 patients who received islet and 26 who received PT, Chaigne et al. [32] found that among the HLA eplet mismatch algorithms using HLA matchmaker, and PIRCHE‐T2 score, only the latter was associated with development of Class II dnDSA in univariate analysis. Raineri et al. [22] investigated development of dnDSA in 72 SPK recipients. The median total and Class II PIRCHE‐T2 scores were 60.5 and 26.9, respectively. There was no association between PIRCHE‐T2 scores per HLA locus, per HLA class, and total PIRCHE‐T2 scores and development of dnDSA during the 10 years post‐transplant. However, only in univariate analysis, DQ locus‐derived PIRCHE‐T2 score was predictive of increased risk of Class II dnDSA development. This may suggest that specific HLA loci, rather than total molecular mismatch burden, may drive immunologic outcomes in certain transplant types. They noted that despite lower total PIRCHE‐T2 scores in SPK compared to kidney‐alone recipients, the former group experienced higher incidence of dnDSA early post‐transplant, likely due to local graft inflammation and beta cell antigenicity. This underscores the possibility that epitope prediction algorithms developed for kidney transplantation may not fully capture the immunologic landscape of pancreas transplants.

In a study of 113 SPK recipients by Ladowski et al. [33] during 89 months (median) follow up, 22.1% developed dnDSA, which was associated with worse pancreas and kidney allograft survival. Number of HLA Class II mismatches by both HLA Matchmaker and PIRCHE‐T2 scores was predictive of higher risk of dnDSA development. This was mostly driven by HLA DQ scores being associated with the development of anti‐DQ dnDSA. Although the authors reported worse graft survival in those with dnDSA, they did not examine the association of molecular mismatches with graft survival. Parajuli et al. [16] studied 238 SPK patients who were followed for up to 6 years. The median PIRCHE‐B score was 43.5 using a more sensitive configuration, and the median overall PIRCHE‐T2 score was 19 with median PIRCHE‐T2‐Class II score of 7, considering a more restrictive binding rank of 10%. Only PIRCHE‐T2‐Class II score was associated with the risk of any rejection in univariate analysis. However, for the composite endpoint of dnDSA, rejection, and graft failure of either organ, this score was predictive of worse outcome independently. They observed that PIRCHE‐T2‐Class II scores below median (<7) were associated with lower risk of pancreas rejection, any rejection, or the composite outcome. It is of note that in contrast to this cohort the configurations of PIRCHE‐T2 and PIRCHE‐B were still preliminary, yielding significantly higher numbers of total and Class II PIRCHE‐T2 scores in our patients (median total score 19 vs. 60.2, and 7 vs. 22.3, respectively).

In contrast to the other studies, our findings revealed an inverse association between higher Class II antibody epitope loads and adverse outcomes, suggesting a need to further investigate structural HLA disparity as a potentially organ‐specific marker. Together, these contrasting results highlight the limitations of generalizing kidney‐based immunologic risk models to pancreas transplant recipients and support the need for pancreas‐specific validation of molecular mismatch tools.

The use of current tools to quantify T and B‐cell allo‐recognition is predicated on the fact that indirect allorecognition is the key driver in pancreas rejection and graft loss, and PIRCHE‐T2 algorithm is designed based on the donor‐derived HLA peptides that can be presented by the recipient's HLA class II molecules on CD4+ T cells. Our observation may suggest that CD8+ T cell driven immune reaction, similar to the pathogenesis of islet loss in native pancreas that is restricted by HLA class I antigen presentation, may play the dominant role in graft dysfunction and loss in transplant pancreas [34]. Furthermore, one can postulate that the inverse relationship between B cell epitope mismatch load and outcome may be related to diminished inhibitory role of regulatory B lymphocytes, that by releasing cytokines such as IL‐10 play a protective role in organ transplantation [35]. This could lead to an unchecked pro‐inflammatory CD8+ T cell driven microenvironment. One could also postulate that the molecular similarity between the HLA antigens, particularly Class II, between the donor and recipient can increase the risk of autoimmune response and β‐cell injury. Hence, the higher degrees of dissimilarity could be protective of the pancreas from autoimmune injury.

Contrary to a multitude of kidney‐specific studies, in this cohort we did not find an association between the extent of epitope mismatch between the donor and recipient with kidney allograft outcome. This could be due to the inadequate power of the study due to the sample size and its retrospective design that could have missed unaccounted confounding factors. In addition, the lack of association may be partially due to some unaccounted differences between kidney alone transplant recipients and diabetics who qualify for pancreas in addition to kidney transplantation, and modified alloimmune response in the presence of pancreas allograft in these patients.

We acknowledge the limitations of our study. Its retrospective design introduces inherent constraints in controlling for all confounding variables and potential biases that limit the ability to establish causal relationships. HLA typing was performed using low‐resolution methods, which may have reduced the accuracy of molecular mismatch assessment. In the absence of allele‐level HLA typing, genotype imputation is inherently inaccurate at individual level, and we cannot rule out the possibility that the observed lack of association between pancreas allograft outcome with PIRCHE‐T2 and inverse relationship with PIRCHE‐B scores are its consequence. However, using multiple imputations using a combined (ancestry‐independent) population as published previously, we attempted to mitigate this shortcoming [28]. Although the study cohort included a mix of pancreas transplant types, SPK, PAK, and PTA, which may differ in immunologic risk profiles, we adjusted for the type of transplant in our models. Additionally, data on dnDSA formation were not consistently available across the cohort and were therefore not included in the analysis, limiting our ability to evaluate the association between molecular mismatch scores and humoral alloimmune response. Although the study period encompasses 18 years, we did not find any association between the study endpoints and the three 6‐year study eras (Appendix). Moreover, induction agents varied across the cohort, with a higher frequency of alemtuzumab induction compared to other published series. Although to account for its impact on the outcomes we adjusted for the type of induction, this may have influenced alloimmune responses and confounded associations between molecular mismatch and clinical outcomes. In addition, there was no interaction between the epitope mismatch and study era or type of induction. We did not use the definition of pancreas graft failure specified by the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS), first due to various definitions used over the study period, and second UNOS uses those criteria to only determine and track graft failure for reporting purposes [36]. We admit that this could affect the comparability of our study with others in which UNOS definition is used for pancreas graft failure.

Furthermore, as demonstrated by the wide difference between the number of mismatched epitopes between our patients with the cohort examined by Parajuli et al. [16], demographic and genetic characteristics specific to the study population, such as HLA allele frequencies linked to ethnicity, may have shaped mismatch distributions and immune responses in ways that limit applicability to other populations. The single‐center design also reflects center‐specific practices in immunologic monitoring, biopsy indications, and clinical management, which may reduce the generalizability of these findings to broader or more diverse transplant settings. To enhance the utility of molecular mismatch algorithms in PT, future research should emphasize high‐resolution HLA typing, uniform post‐transplant immunologic monitoring, and cohort stratification by transplant type and immunosuppressive regimen across diverse populations.

Conclusion

In summary, our study suggests that elevated antibody epitope mismatch loads as predicted by PIRCHE‐B and eplet load scores are paradoxically associated with improved pancreas transplant outcomes, while PIRCHE‐T2 scores demonstrated limited predictive value. These counterintuitive findings highlight the need for further studies for elucidation of organ‐specific immunologic mechanisms and refinement of risk assessment models. Future work should further explore whether T cell epitope or antibody epitope mismatch loads can inform clinical risk stratification and decision‐making in PT, and the reason for discrepancies in alloimmune response to kidney transplantation. Multicenter studies utilizing high‐resolution HLA typing and longitudinal immune monitoring will be crucial for validating these findings and refining the predictive models.
