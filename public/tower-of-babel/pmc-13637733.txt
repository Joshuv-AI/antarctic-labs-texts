Unlocking the bacterial condensome: from “dark matter” to design
Authors: Daniel S. Trettel, Claudia A. Mak, Y Hoang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biomolecular condensates have transformed our understanding of eukaryotic cellular organization, yet their roles in bacteria remain comparatively underexplored. This gap arises from the physical constraints of bacterial cell size, the diversity of molecular mechanisms that drive condensation, and the limited representation of bacterial condensates in current predictive frameworks. Here, we argue that condensation is a widespread but underrecognized principle of bacterial intracellular organization. We describe how scaffold architecture, multivalent interactions, and non-equilibrium regulation collectively determine condensate material states and cellular function. Using bacterial microcompartments as a case study, we show how diverse condensation mechanisms can converge to organize functional organelles. We further highlight emerging computational, imaging, and perturbation-based approaches that are shifting the field from condensate identification toward mechanistic understanding and predictive design. By uncovering the physical principles governing bacterial condensates, we envision their use as programmable biomaterials whose properties can be engineered to control cellular function and enable synthetic organelles.

INTRODUCTION

Life depends on the ability of cells to organize their crowded cytoplasm. While membrane-bound compartments achieve separation through physical diffusion barriers, bacteria are increasingly found to utilize biomolecular condensates (hereafter condensates) for spatiotemporal control (Azaldegui et al., 2021). Condensates arise when multivalent interactions concentrate specific biomolecules into dynamic mesoscale assemblies that regulate physiology (Banani et al., 2017; Cohan and Pappu, 2020). Much like cosmological dark matter, which constitutes a major component of the universe yet is inferred primarily through its effects on galaxies, biomolecular condensates may represent a widespread but underrecognized principle of intracellular organization in bacteria, with their prevalence and biological functions often inferred from converging biophysical and cellular evidence rather than direct visualization alone.

In this Perspective, we outline the molecular grammar underlying bacterial condensation and argue that these mechanisms are best viewed as a continuum rather than discrete categories. Using bacterial microcompartments as a case study, we illustrate how these mechanisms together organize bacterial physiology. We then discuss the evidence standards, challenges, and tools required to illuminate this “bacterial dark matter.” Finally, we address how mechanistic insight is beginning to enable predictive condensate engineering.

THE MOLECULAR GRAMMAR OF THE BACTERIAL CONDENSOME

The molecular logic of condensates frequently follows a “stickers and spacers” framework. Here, scaffolds are biomolecules that provide the multivalent interaction network driving condensate assembly. Within these scaffolds, interaction motifs (stickers) provide valency for reversible crosslinks, while flexible regions (spacers) modulate solubility and material state (Figure 1A; Borcherds et al., 2021; Ginell and Holehouse, 2023). Scaffold properties, like sticker and spacer sequence and patterning (Das and Pappu, 2013; Quiroz and Chilkoti, 2015; Wang et al., 2018; Dzuricky et al., 2020; Martin et al., 2020; Dai et al., 2023a), establish the biophysical landscape for condensation by defining the concentrations of saturation (Csat) and percolation (Cperc). Scaffolds can also selectively recruit low valency “clients” which influence phase behavior (Banani et al., 2016; Espinosa et al., 2020; Rana et al., 2024; Alberti et al., 2025). Together, these multivalent interactions can generate a spectrum of organizational regimes: density-driven liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), connectivity-driven percolation, which drives gelation without demixing, and phase separation coupled to percolation (PSCP; Mittag and Pappu, 2022), in which the interplay between density and network formation determines material state (Table 1; Figure 1B). Condensate location, size, and concentration threshold can be further regulated by energy flux (i.e., NTP hydrolysis and chemical reactions; Wurtz and Lee, 2018; Hondele et al., 2019; Hondele et al., 2020) and nucleation surfaces (i.e., membranes or nucleoid bridging; Broedersz et al., 2014; Hernández-Vega et al., 2017; Heinkel et al., 2019; Lasker et al., 2020; Ryu et al., 2021; Morin et al., 2022; Snead et al., 2022; Hsu et al., 2023; Park et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2025). These observations reinforce our central argument that bacterial condensates should be understood as a continuum of physical mechanisms rather than discrete assembly classes.

Bacterial microcompartments (BMCs) illustrate how multiple condensation mechanisms can be integrated within a single organelle. BMCs, including α- and β-carboxysomes, are < 300 nm polyhedral protein shells that encapsulate concentrated enzymatic cores (Kerfeld et al., 2018). Their biogenesis proceeds through condensate-like intermediates formed via multivalent scaffold-cargo interactions (Figure 1C; Cameron et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2019; Oltrogge et al., 2020; Zang et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022; Trettel et al., 2025b; Yang et al., 2025; Zang et al., 2026). The distinct molecular grammars of the scaffolds prevent hybrid core formation (Chang et al., 2026), while scaffold modifications can alter BMC morphology, dynamics, and assembly mode (whether shell- or cargo-nucleated; Mohajerani et al., 2021; Tsidilkovski et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022; Oltrogge et al., 2023; Trettel et al., 2025b). These observations demonstrate how changes in molecular architecture propagate to higher-order cellular organization.

Despite their distinct assembly mechanisms, BMCs demonstrate material-state changes characteristic of glassy dynamics and PSCP. For instance, the high concentration of compartmentalized cargo promotes low-affinity cargo–cargo binding events that increase with concentration and create glassy ordering within particles (Metskas et al., 2022; Ni et al., 2022; Trettel et al., 2022; Evans et al., 2023; Kong et al., 2024). These material changes promote high packing densities (Sun et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2024) without sacrificing substrate access (Metskas et al., 2022). While mechanistically distinct, PSCP has also been recognized in some BMC intermediates (Trettel et al., 2025b). Further, the internal dynamics of scaffold-cargo condensates demonstrate environmental responsiveness, such as redox-dependence, likely by promoting network rearrangements (Turnšek et al., 2024; Zang et al., 2026). The functional consequences of material transitions are mirrored in other bacterial condensates, like aggresome compaction regulating mRNA protection, suggesting that material-state regulation is a general organizational strategy (Pei et al., 2025).

BMCs also exemplify how condensation can be regulated by non-equilibrium processes. In the carboxysome McdAB positioning system, McdB, a condensate-forming protein, connects carboxysomes to McdA, a nucleoid-bound ATPase (MacCready and Vecchiarelli, 2021). McdB bound to carboxysomes stimulates McdA ATPase activity, triggering its dissociation from the nucleoid and establishing a dissipative steady-state that prevents carboxysome clustering and contributes to BMC morphology, composition, and function (Hakim et al., 2021; Rillema et al., 2021; Hoang et al., 2025). Dissipative steady-states are leveraged by other bacterial positioning systems, like ParABS for plasmid inheritance (Graham et al., 2014; Vecchiarelli et al., 2014; Fisher et al., 2017; Babl et al., 2022). More broadly, this illustrates how condensation mechanisms can be integrated with active cellular processes to produce behaviors that cannot be explained by equilibrium assembly alone.

The role of the BMC shell remains debated. Because many BMC enzymes remain functional without a shell (Fan et al., 2010; Aussignargues et al., 2015; Mills et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022), and shells display environmental responsiveness (Sutter et al., 2016; Fuentes-Cabrera et al., 2022) as well as limited substrate selectivity (Trettel et al., 2022; Sarkar et al., 2024; Yadav et al., 2026), their function likely extends beyond simple containment and protection. We propose that the BMC shell can function as a phase modulator, providing, in some cases, a nucleation surface (Mohajerani et al., 2021) that can also constrain condensate intermediates (Douliez et al., 2019; Gao and Mann, 2023; Lin et al., 2023). By further limiting coalescence (Folkmann et al., 2021; Abeysinghe et al., 2024; Turnšek et al., 2024; Costantino et al., 2025), ripening (Chang et al., 2026), and material aging of the enzymatic core, BMC shells help to maintain optimal size and function. Here, BMCs are not rigid protein containers (Rotskoff and Geissler, 2018) but dynamic organelles (Trettel et al., 2024) whose function emerges from the interplay between scaffold architecture, material state, and non-equilibrium regulation.

In sum, BMCs illustrate a central theme of the bacterial condensome: higher-order cellular organization emerges from multiple distinct yet interdependent physical mechanisms. Distinguishing among these mechanisms remains a major challenge and motivates the development of new experimental and computational approaches. In bacteria, this challenge is amplified by the physical constraints of the cellular environment itself.

UNDERSTUDIED, BUT NOT UNCOMMON

Studying condensation in bacteria presents unique challenges that largely stem from their small cellular dimensions (∼1 µm compared with ∼10–100 µm for many eukaryotic cells; Hodgins et al., 2025). The reduced cellular volume limits the number of molecules available to participate in assembly and can impose strong finite-size constraints on nucleation (Wedekind et al., 2006). These finite-size effects necessitate higher supersaturation to support initial nucleation. However, this requirement can be compensated by stochastic fluctuations, which are more effective in smaller volumes (Hill, 1962), surface-assisted nucleation that lowers energetic barriers, and the extreme macromolecular crowding of the cytoplasm (∼ six-fold more concentrated than in human cells; Leeb et al., 2020). These biophysical constraints render many bacterial condensates effectively invisible to conventional imaging approaches, reaffirming their characterization as a form of “bacterial dark matter.” This technical invisibility has slowed mechanistic dissection compared with eukaryotes. Yet, emerging methodologies and tools are making the bacterial condensome increasingly approachable, transforming a longstanding observational challenge into a tractable mechanistic problem.

THE EMERGENCE OF TOOLS TO PROBE BIOMOLECULAR CONDENSATES IN BACTERIAL CELLS

The intrinsic size and biophysical constraints posed by bacteria make investigating their condensome predominantly an inference challenge: determining whether an observed assembly is a condensate and, if so, characterizing its phase behavior, material state, and regulation in vivo. Addressing these questions requires integrated biochemical, computational, and imaging approaches. While many in vitro assays are readily adapted from eukaryotic systems (reviewed elsewhere; Alberti et al., 2025), computational prediction and live-cell imaging remain uniquely challenging in bacteria because of their small size, distinct protein architectures, and the difficulty of resolving subcellular organization below the diffraction limit.

Computational predictors provide an attractive starting point for condensate discovery, but most were developed using eukaryotic proteins and emphasize scaffold features such as long intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), low-complexity domains, and prion-like sequences (Saar et al., 2021; Chu et al., 2022; Monti et al., 2025). Although these features contribute to some bacterial condensates, they do not capture the full spectrum of bacterial condensation mechanisms. For example, recent analyses suggest that bacterial and eukaryotic proteomes contain similar fractions of condensate-forming proteins despite major differences in IDR prevalence and architecture, implying that bacteria frequently achieve condensation through mechanisms beyond extensive intrinsic disorder (Ward et al., 2004; van der Lee et al., 2014; Hadarovich et al., 2024). Instead, multivalency often emerges from folded oligomerization domains, short interaction motifs, nucleic-acid binding, or interactions among multiple scaffold and client species (Broedersz et al., 2014; Liu, 2022; Basalla et al., 2023; Trettel et al., 2025b). Consequently, many predictors may perform poorly on bacterial condensates whose assembly relies on mechanisms underrepresented in current training sets. Sparse bacterial representation in most condensate databases further limits the development of mechanism-aware prediction tools (Ning et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022; Rostam et al., 2023; Rodriguez et al., 2025). Future prediction tools must integrate bacteria-specific sequence, structural, and mechanistic information to identify bacterial condensates beyond canonical eukaryotic paradigms.

Because computational predictions alone cannot establish condensation, candidate assemblies must be validated experimentally. Live-cell imaging provides critical insight into condensate organization and dynamics, but bacterial dimensions impose unique interpretive challenges. Condensates, aggregates, and ordered assemblies can all appear as diffraction-limited foci, obscuring potentially informative differences in morphology and spatial organization. Super-resolution imaging techniques, including Airyscan, photoactivated localization microscopy (PALM), and stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (STORM) (Tuson and Biteen, 2015), together with single-molecule tracking, help resolve morphology, molecular organization, mobility, confinement, and exchange that cannot be inferred from conventional microscopy alone (Kapanidis et al., 2018). These features may help distinguish among mechanistic models. For example, assemblies may exhibit punctate, filamentous, or surface-associated morphologies that reflect distinct modes of assembly or scaffold organization. Despite the resolution benefits brought by these technologies, the limited repertoire of photoactivatable fluorophores (Lukyanov et al., 2005; Subach et al., 2009; Gaytán and Roldán-Salgado, 2024) precludes simultaneous tracking of multiple components. This limitation is becoming increasingly consequential as the field shifts from identifying condensates to dissecting their multicomponent organization. Moreover, motion blurring, phototoxicity, overexpression, and fluorescent tagging itself (Dörner et al., 2026) can complicate interpretation by altering condensate behavior, particularly for small and transient assemblies. Thus, imaging alone cannot objectively distinguish among material states or establish condensation.

We therefore argue that bacterial condensates should be evaluated using a common set of evidence standards rather than any single experimental criterion (Table 2). These standards probe emergent behaviors arising from the underlying molecular grammar and are therefore most informative when interpreted collectively rather than in isolation. Material state can be assessed by comparison with established aggregation (Govers et al., 2018) and condensation (Hoang et al., 2024) reference systems, together with colocalization of the inclusion-body binding protein IbpA. Condensate dynamics can be assessed by perturbing saturation concentration through changes in temperature or osmolarity, whereas reversibility can be examined by protein dilution following localized laser lysis, inhibition of cell division, or generational dilution (Hoang et al., 2024). Importantly, these approaches require careful interpretation, because they may affect growth, nucleoid organization, and proteostasis.

Together, advances in computational prediction, quantitative imaging, and perturbation-based validation are shifting bacterial condensate research beyond simply classifying intracellular assemblies as condensates or non-condensates. Instead, these approaches increasingly allow investigators to place assemblies along a continuum of material states while integrating morphology, molecular organization, and dynamic behavior to infer their mechanisms of assembly and biological function.

ENGINEERING BACTERIAL CONDENSATES FOR BIOTECHNOLOGY

As the field moves from identifying bacterial condensates to understanding the molecular principles governing their behavior, these same principles are beginning to enable rational engineering. The genetic tractability, architectural simplicity, and modularity of bacterial components provide a fertile ground for biomolecular engineering. Underlying these practical benefits is our central contention that condensation is a ubiquitous, yet largely unseen, feature of bacterial biology that remains highly primed for biotechnological exploitation. Synthetic frameworks can exploit condensation to concentrate molecules, organize reactions in space, and couple synthetic functions to cellular inheritance (Stevens and Lasker, 2025). Translating these natural capabilities into predictable engineering outcomes requires a deep understanding of how scaffold architecture and multivalent interactions shape three interconnected parameters: selective partitioning, spatial organization, and material state (Dai et al., 2023b; Galvanetto et al., 2025). Together, this triad governs which molecules enter a synthetic condensate, where it forms within the cell, and how effectively it performs its intended function. The goal is not merely to create condensates, but to program them with properties that harmonize with, or even enhance, the intended functions of the molecules contained within.

Lessons from native bacterial compartmentalization strategies reinforce these principles. BMCs, which assemble through condensate intermediates, can redirect flux through branched metabolic pathways by selectively enriching pathway components (Palmero et al., 2025). Encapsulation peptides and other scaffolds can tune cargo recruitment through interactions whose effective valency and affinity determine loading efficiency and assembly behavior (Oltrogge et al., 2023; Trettel et al., 2025b): overly strong interactions may promote aggregation, whereas weaker interactions may limit encapsulation (Lawrence et al., 2014; Lee and Tullman-Ercek, 2017; Mohajerani et al., 2021). More broadly, these systems highlight the need for synthetic condensates to similarly balance client partitioning, spatial organization, and material state, ultimately mirroring the responsiveness of their natural counterparts.

Selective partitioning is central to many condensate-based applications because function depends on the enrichment of the correct clients, not concentration alone. Engineered RNA-binding scaffolds, such as Pumilio2-elastin-like polypeptide fusions, demonstrate that condensation can either enhance or inhibit translation depending on whether ribosomes are co-recruited with the target transcript (Parker et al., 2022; Shapiro et al., 2025). Productive condensates must therefore enrich intended clients while maintaining access to molecular machinery required for activity. In practice, partitioning can be tuned through scaffold abundance, sticker affinity, oligomerization-driven valency, or client-binding motifs.

Condensation can also be engineered to control spatial organization and inheritance. Natural bacterial systems such as the ParB-parS, PopZ, and McdAB demonstrate how distinct condensation mechanisms can be coupled to cellular landmarks and active positioning systems (Babl et al., 2022; Lasker et al., 2022; Basalla et al., 2023). These principles have been repurposed synthetically through platforms such as “MapTag,” which uses the McdAB machinery to actively distribute synthetic cargoes along the nucleoid (Hoang et al., 2025) and PopZ-based condensates that spatially separate reproductive and production-focused cell populations to improve biosynthetic output (Mushnikov et al., 2025). Together, these studies illustrate how condensates can move engineered systems beyond passive diffusion toward programmable intracellular organization.

Material state emerges from the balance between density and network formation described by LLPS, percolation, and PSCP, making it another critical design parameter. Condensates that are too fluid may fail to retain cargo, whereas highly viscous assemblies can impede exchange and cellular fitness. For example, FUS-based condensates used to co-localize enzymes for 2′-fucosyllactose production supported higher output when assemblies remained dynamic rather than viscous or aggregate-like (Ding et al., 2025). Similarly, synthetic coiled-coil triblock systems can be tuned between diffuse, liquid-like, or solid-like states by altering sticker-spacer organization, with more rigid assemblies imposing greater fitness costs (Tomares et al., 2022). Together, these studies demonstrate that material state can be tuned through interaction density, sticker-space architecture, or scaffold valency.

An emerging challenge is that cargos are often treated as passive passengers whose behavior is dictated entirely by the condensate scaffold. This is increasingly consequential as researchers transition into building synthetic condensates with designer functions encoded by enzymatic fusions to scaffolds. Growing evidence suggests that cargo identity—including size, charge, oligomeric state, and interaction potential—can reshape condensate partitioning, dynamics, and material properties (Trettel et al., 2025a; Trettel et al., 2025b; Dörner et al., 2026). Consequently, the same condensation tag may behave differently when fused to different proteins, complicating both interpretation and engineering. This consideration extends even to commonly used fluorescent reporters, which may themselves influence condensate behavior (Dörner et al., 2026). Understanding how cargo properties contribute to condensation therefore represents both a cautionary consideration for experimental design and a potential new design axis beyond scaffold architecture.

Collectively, these studies suggest that bacterial condensate engineering will require simultaneous control over scaffold architecture, client affinity, cargo identity, and material state. Major open questions remain, including how to predict partition coefficients in the crowded bacterial cytoplasm and how to minimize toxicity arising from nonspecific sequestration, altered chromosome organization, proteostasis stress, or metabolic burden. Future synthetic condensates should therefore be evaluated not only by their functional outputs but also by quantitative measurements of partitioning, dynamics, reversibility, and host fitness. Bacterial condensates should be viewed not simply as intracellular containers, but as programmable biomaterials whose physical properties can be engineered to control cellular function.

A TIMELY OPPORTUNITY: FROM INFERENCE TO ENGINEERING

The central challenge in bacterial condensate research is no longer determining whether condensates exist, but defining the mechanisms that connect scaffold architecture, material state, and biological function. As the field matures, emphasis must shift from cataloging new examples toward establishing evidence standards, identifying recurring molecular grammars, and uncovering the physical principles that govern bacterial condensation.

Addressing these questions will require integrating computational prediction, quantitative imaging, perturbation-based validation, and mechanistic reconstitution. In doing so, we will not only advance our understanding of bacterial cell biology but also establish a foundation for engineering programmable intracellular organization and biomaterials. By uncovering the physical principles that govern this “bacterial dark matter,” we can move from inferring its existence to understanding how bacteria build, regulate, and exploit condensates.
