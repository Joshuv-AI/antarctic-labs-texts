Clinical and Criminal Characteristics of Patients With Schizophrenia Exhibiting Homicidal and Non-Homicidal Behavior: A Comparative Analysis
Authors: Yujin Lee, YoungRyeol Lee, ChulEung Kim, Jin-Hee Han, MyungJe Sung, Jina Jung, KiWon Song, Hyemi Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPublic concern regarding homicides committed by individuals with schizophrenia has intensified in recent years. Although the overall crime rate in this population is not significantly higher than that of the general population, certain subgroups may present an elevated homicide risk. Identifying associated risk factors and distinguishing between impulsive and premeditated violence are crucial for developing effective forensic psychiatric assessment and prevention strategies.MethodsMedical records of patients with schizophrenia admitted to the National Forensic Hospital in Korea from 2020 to 2024 were retrospectively reviewed. Patients were classified into the homicide group (HG; n = 55) and non-homicide violent group (VG; n = 49). Demographic and clinical data, trauma history, treatment adherence, cognitive function (Korean Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale), symptom severity (Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale [PANSS]), personality traits (Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory [MMPI] and Personality Assessment Inventory [PAI]), and crime-related characteristics were extracted from psychiatric evaluations, inpatient records, and psychological test results. The χ2 tests, t-tests, and Mann–Whitney U tests were used for statistical analysis.ResultsMost violent acts occurred during periods of treatment discontinuation and were most commonly driven by persecutory delusions and hallucinations. Both groups frequently experienced limited social support and a history of childhood trauma or adult victimization. The HG was more likely to commit premeditated, organized crimes using sharp weapons against family members in private settings. Conversely, the VG tended to exhibit impulsive violence in public settings, often against strangers and without weapons. Persecutory delusions predominated in both groups, whereas grandiose delusions were more prevalent in the VG. The HG had higher MMPI Psychopathic Deviate and Psychasthenia subscale and PAI suicidal ideation scale scores, whereas the VG showed higher PANSS positive symptom scores. The VG also demonstrated more frequent violent incidents and rehospitalizations.ConclusionAlthough psychotic symptoms underpinned violence in both groups, the HG showed antisocial traits, suicidal tendencies, and premeditated violence. Conversely, the VG exhibited more severe positive symptoms, grandiosity, and recurrent impulsive violence. These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive violence risk assessments in schizophrenia that incorporate symptomatology, personality pathologies, and violence typologies.Keywords: Schizophrenia, Violence, Homicide, Forensic Psychiatry, Personality Assessment, Risk Assessment

INTRODUCTION

Public concern regarding the potential for severe violence perpetrated by individuals with schizophrenia has intensified following the 2016 Gangnam Station murder, the 2019 Jinju arson and homicide in Korea, as well as the 2016 “Little Light Bulb” child murder in Taipei and the 2019 Kawasaki mass stabbing in Japan.1,2 According to national statistics, individuals with mental illness account for only 0.5–0.7% of all criminal offenders, indicating an extremely low absolute crime rate. However, this proportion is higher in serious crimes (such as homicide, robbery, arson, and recidivism) with rates exceeding 60%.3 Meta-analytic and clinical studies indicate that while patients with schizophrenia have an elevated risk of violence compared to the general population this risk is largely confined to subgroups characterized by psychotic symptoms, substance misuse, social adversity, and poor treatment adherence.4,5,6

Social stigma toward mental illness may hinder access to treatment, and untreated schizophrenia can exacerbate symptoms and heighten the risk of violent behavior, thereby creating a vicious cycle.1 Therefore, a systematic understanding of violence among individuals with psychotic disorders is essential for developing effective prevention and intervention strategies. In this context, several studies have investigated the associated risk factors. A meta-analysis involving individuals with psychosis identified a history of violent or non-violent crime (odds ratio [OR], 3.1) as the strongest predictor of future violence, demonstrating a stronger association than previously emphasized factors, such as substance use (OR, 2.3) and treatment-related variables (OR, 1.5). Notably, demographic and dispositional factors also demonstrated stronger associations than clinical symptoms.7 A large-scale longitudinal study in the United States, which followed individuals with schizophrenia over 6 months, identified a history of criminal behavior and childhood conduct problems as the strongest predictors of serious violence—such as firearm use. Additionally, high levels of positive symptoms, low levels of negative symptoms, and recent victimization were also significantly associated.8 These findings suggest that violent behavior in schizophrenia results from a complex interplay of clinical characteristics, demographic and predispositional vulnerabilities, and criminal history. A systematic assessment of these factors may contribute to the development of individualized treatment strategies designed to prevent violence.7,8,9

Violent behavior in psychiatric patients has been categorized into three subtypes under the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework: psychotic (17%), impulsive (54%), and organized (29%). Psychotic violence is linked to delusions and hallucinations, while impulsive violence to emotional reactivity, and organized violence to goal-directed acts that are often associated with psychopathic traits. Despite this classification, empirical evidence for distinct neurobiological mechanisms remains limited.10 Several studies demonstrated a strong correlation between positive psychotic symptoms including delusions and hallucinations and violent conduct in people with schizophrenia.4,6 Building on these findings, fresh perspectives offer a continuum model that shows how psychotically motivated violence ranges from emotionally reactive, impulsive premeditated behaviors with cognitive elaboration. It could be possible to find different psychological pathways that underlie identical criminal behaviors using this framework.11

Although it is important not to generalize individuals with schizophrenia as being prone to violent crimes, the potential for serious violence cannot be overlooked when specific clinical and environmental risk factors are present. Clarifying the underlying mechanisms and identifying the key contributing factors requires a precise clinical assessment that incorporates violence subtypes and their associated risks. In particular, homicide is an irreversible act that causes profound harm not only to victims but also to families and society, imposing substantial personal and societal burdens. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of homicidal violence is essential.12

Hence, this study aimed to conduct a detailed analysis of the demographic, predispositional, clinical, and criminological characteristics of patients with schizophrenia who committed homicide and were hospitalized at the National Forensic Hospital. To identify the specific characteristics associated with homicide, this group was compared with patients with schizophrenia who had committed other forms of violent offenses, such as assault and battery. Furthermore, by classifying violent behavior into impulsive and organized subtypes, this study aimed to elucidate the potential mechanisms underlying each type and provide foundational data for developing targeted prevention strategies and enhancing public safety.

METHODS

Participants

This retrospective chart review included patients diagnosed with schizophrenia who were admitted to the National Forensic Hospital between January 1, 2020 and December 31, 2024. Based on the type of offense committed, participants were divided into two groups. Those who committed homicide-related crimes—including murder, attempted murder, parricide, and attempted parricide—were assigned to the homicide group (HG), whereas those who committed other violent crimes—such as assault and battery—were assigned to the non-homicide violent group (VG).

Data collection

A total of 91 patients in the HG and 76 in the VG were initially identified. Individuals with confirmed cognitive impairment due to intellectual disability or dementia were excluded from the study. In addition, cases were also excluded if information in any one of the demographic, clinical, or criminological domains could not be verified due to insufficient medical records. As a result, the final study sample comprised 55 patients in the HG and 49 in the VG (Fig. 1). Data were obtained through a comprehensive review of medical records, including forensic psychiatric evaluation findings, hospitalization notes, and psychological assessment results. The following variables were extracted for analysis.

Flow diagram of the study participants.

Demographic and predispositional characteristics

Demographic information included age, educational attainment, marital status, and employment status. Predispositional factors associated with violent behavior, a history of childhood trauma, conduct disorder, adult victimization, and a family history of psychiatric disorders were assessed. Additionally, the presence and quality of social support systems were examined.

Criminal characteristics

Each patient was evaluated to determine whether the offense was committed within a psychotic context. Violent behavior was classified as either impulsive or premeditated. Additional variables included age at the time of the offense, type of weapon used (if any), location of the offense, relationship with the victim, and the presence of substance intoxication at the time of the crime. Major stressors experienced immediately prior to the offense were also documented. Moreover, data on previous convictions for homicide, a history of violent or nonviolent offenses, canceled conditional discharges, number of rehospitalizations, and previous incidents of violent behavior were reviewed.

Clinical characteristics

Clinical data included age at schizophrenia onset, duration of untreated psychosis (DUP), number of admissions, and treatment adherence at the time of the offense. The Korean Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale was used to assess full-scale intelligence quotient. Meanwhile, symptom severity was evaluated using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), which was administered at the time of admission. Personality characteristics were assessed using the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI) and Personality Assessment Inventory (PAI). The delusions and hallucinations were identified and categorized.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic, predispositional, clinical, and criminological characteristics, with means and standard deviations calculated for continuous variables and frequencies for categorical variables. Categorical variables were analyzed using the χ2 test. Fisher’s exact test was applied when the expected frequency in any cell was less than 5, whereas continuous variables were compared using independent-sample t-tests. The Mann–Whitney U test was employed to analyze non-normally distributed variables, including the number of psychiatric hospitalizations, history of suicide attempts, and previous convictions. To control for multiple comparisons, the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to the P values for criminal and clinical characteristics. While there were no missing data for the main study variables due to the exclusion criteria, a subset of participants did not complete the MMPI and PAI assessments. For these specific scales, a listwise deletion was applied, including only cases with available test results. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics for Windows version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics statement

The present study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Forensic Hospital (approval No. 1-219577-AB-N-01-202502-BR-001-01). Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

RESULTS

Table 1 presents the demographic and predispositional characteristics of the HG and VG groups. The mean age of the HG was approximately 45 years, which was significantly younger than that of the VG (51 years; t = −2.946, P = 0.004). In both groups, the majority of individuals were unmarried and unemployed, and more than half were living with relatives. Most participants in both groups lacked supportive social networks or reported personal conflicts within their existing support systems, indicating poor psychosocial support. A history of childhood trauma—a known risk factor for violence—was reported in 54.5% of patients in the HG and 40.8% of those in the VG. Experiences of victimization in adulthood were reported in 25.5% and 34.7% of patients in the HG and VG, respectively. Although these differences were not significant, both groups exhibited a high prevalence of such experiences.

Table 2 presents a comparison of criminological characteristics between the two groups. Analysis of violence subtypes revealed that 58.2% of patients in the HG committed organized offenses, whereas 83.7% of those in the VG committed impulsive offenses—indicating a significant difference (χ2 = 22.487, P < 0.001). Exacerbation of psychotic symptoms was the most commonly reported precursor to the offense in both groups. However, the second most frequent stressor differed between the groups. Family conflict was more common in the HG, whereas the VG reported no identifiable triggers (χ2 = 11.071, P = 0.016). Regarding the type of weapon used, sharp instruments such as knives were most common in the HG (67.3%), whereas unarmed physical assaults predominated in the VG (65.3%), showing a significant group difference (χ2 = 42.758, P < 0.001). The HG predominantly committed offenses in private settings (72.7%), whereas the VG more often committed offenses in public spaces (57.1%) (χ2 = 9.534, P = 0.003). In terms of victim relationship, 47.3% of HG victims were parents, whereas 49% of VG victims were strangers, indicating a significant difference (χ2 = 17.094, P = 0.001). Regarding prior criminal history, the VG exhibited significantly higher rates of both violent and non-violent offenses compared with the HG (non-violent crime: Z = −2.776, P = 0.006; violent crime: Z = −3.581, P < 0.001). Additionally, the rate of revoked conditional discharges or psychiatric rehospitalizations was significantly greater in the VG (36.7%) than in the HG (16.3%) (χ2 = 5.835, P = 0.045). After applying FDR correction, significant group differences remained in criminological characteristics (Supplementary Table 1).

Table 3 summarizes the clinical characteristics of the two groups. The VG had a significantly higher number of prior psychiatric hospitalizations compared with the HG (Z = 2.278, P = 0.023), whereas the average DUP was comparable in both groups (approximately 39 months). Over 80% of patients in each group were non-adherent to treatment at the time of the offense. The VG showed significantly higher PANSS positive symptom scores (mean = 26.78) compared with the HG (mean = 24.02, t = −2.239, P = 0.027). In contrast, the VG demonstrated significantly higher PANSS positive symptom scores (q = 0.040) (Supplementary Table 1).

DISCUSSION

This study compared patients with schizophrenia who committed homicide with those who committed other violent offenses, aiming to identify demographic, predispositional, clinical, and criminological factors associated specifically with homicide and to analyze violence typologies (impulsive vs. organized).

Both groups demonstrated high rates of unemployment and limited social support, reflecting social isolation frequently observed in individuals with schizophrenia. These findings align with those of previous studies indicating that structural vulnerabilities—such as prolonged social withdrawal and the lack of support networks—may contribute more significantly to the risk of violent behavior compared with individual psychopathology alone.13 These findings underscore the need for national-level interventions focused on enhancing social integration and restoring connectedness in this population.

Adverse experiences, including childhood trauma and adult victimization, have emerged as salient psychosocial risk factors for violence. Although a high proportion of patients reported childhood trauma, the between-group differences in cumulative trauma burden scores did not reach statistical significance (Z = −0.582, P = 0.555). This apparent discrepancy may reflect limitations in data availability due to the patients’ psychotic states and the absence of collateral informants at admission. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that the presence of trauma—rather than its frequency—may play a critical role in shaping violence tendencies. This interpretation is consistent with previous research indicating that traumatic experiences across the lifespan can influence the development and expression of violent behavior.7 In light of evidence that individuals with schizophrenia are more likely to be victims than perpetrators of violence,14 these results underscore the need for trauma-focused care.

From a criminological perspective, the VG exhibited significantly higher rates of prior criminal offenses, revoked conditional discharges, and legal recommendations compared with the HG. These findings suggest that VG may present more pronounced positive symptom—such as hostility and disinhibition—resulting in behavioral dysregulation and acute relapses. In RDoC-based typologies of violence, the HG offenses were often goal-directed and instrumental, whereas the VG offenses were reactive and emotionally driven, possibly explaining the higher frequency of legal intervention.11

Clinically, both groups exhibited a mean DUP exceeding 3 years, which is substantially longer than previously reported in Korean studies. A community-based investigation found a mean DUP of 1.6 years among patients with schizophrenia spectrum disorders, while a nationwide cost-of-illness study reported an average DUP of approximately 840 days (approximately 2.3 years).15,16 This finding supports prior evidence indicating that prolonged DUP may increase the risk of severe violence, including homicide, emphasizing the importance of early intervention strategies to reduce future risk.17 Furthermore, over 80% of patients in both groups were non-adherent to treatment at the time of their offense, supporting existing evidence that identifies poor medication adherence as a significant risk factor for violence.6,8 All participants experienced psychotic symptoms—primarily delusions and hallucinations—at the time of their offense, with symptom exacerbation reported as the most common proximal stressor. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies demonstrating a strong association between psychotic symptom activation and violent behavior,5,7,8,9 highlighting the critical need for ongoing symptom monitoring and treatment engagement.12 The VG exhibited significantly higher PANSS positive symptom scores compared with the HG. Elevated positive symptoms, including hostility and excitement, may also reflect heightened impulsivity, which is consistent with our finding that impulsive violence was significantly more prevalent in the VG.18 By contrast, both groups showed low negative symptom scores, consistent with literature suggesting that negative symptoms may serve as protective factors against violence.8 Taken together, these findings underscore the necessity of timely detection, intervention, and sustained treatment engagement. They also highlight the critical need for robust public mental health infrastructure, such as Korea’s Mindlink system—to support ongoing care.19 The Mindlink system is a nationwide early intervention initiative aimed at young adults and adolescents with early psychosis, with the goal of reducing DUP and improving continuity of care through early detection, case management, family education, and financial assistance for treatment. Such infrastructure facilitates not only clinical management but also intersectoral collaboration, including linkage with probation services, court-mandated interventions, and community mental health centers, which may enhance adherence and reduce recidivism.

MMPI results showed significantly higher scores in the HG on the Pd and Pt scales. Elevated Pd scores, associated with antisocial traits, impulsivity, and norm violations, are consistent with findings in serial homicide research.20,21 These scores also correlate with poor anger regulation and increased violence risk.22 Higher Pt scores reflect chronic tension and obsessive ruminations, which may escalate into explosive violence under stress.20 These patterns imply that structured personality pathology may contribute to violence in the HG beyond psychosis alone, highlighting the value of incorporating personality assessment into violence risk evaluations. The PAI results also revealed distinct response styles: the HG showed higher Negative Impression Management (NIM) scores, whereas the VG showed elevated Positive Impression Management (PIM) scores. NIM indicates a tendency to exaggerate problems, while PIM reflects minimization or denial of difficulties to appear favorable. Although responses remained valid, higher NIM scores may reflect symptom exaggeration for secondary gain, whereas higher PIM scores suggest underreporting or socially desirable responses. Although no significant differences were observed in the clinical scales, the HG showed a relatively elevated mean score on the Paranoia subscale and a significantly higher score on the suicidal ideation subscale. These results suggest the coexistence of externalized aggression (homicidal behavior) and internalized self-destructiveness (suicidality) within the HG. Previous research has proposed that homicide and suicide may lie at opposite ends of a shared violence spectrum, a hypothesis supported by the high levels of depression, hopelessness, and suicidality in individuals who commit homicidal suicide.23,24 Homicidal behavior may thus reflect not only external aggression but also psychological factors, such as attachment loss, narcissistic injury, or impaired affect regulation,25 potentially accounting for the elevated suicidal ideation observed in the HG.

A detailed analysis of psychotic symptoms revealed that persecutory delusions were the most prevalent in both the HG and VG. This finding is consistent with that of prior research indicating that delusions involving themes of threat, persecution, surveillance, or control, commonly referred to as threat/control-override delusions, are associated with an increased risk of violent behavior.8,9,26,27,28 However, subsequent large-scale studies suggest that emotional responses, such as anger, elicited by the delusional content, along with an individual's cognitive appraisal and regulatory capacity, may be more critical determinants of violence risk than the presence of delusions alone. These findings indicate the necessity of a more nuanced understanding of individual coping capacities.29 Notably, the VG exhibited significantly higher rates of grandiose delusions compared with the HG, consistent with previous studies suggesting a potential link between grandiose symptomatology and violent behavior.30 Individuals with narcissistic personality traits or grandiose delusional ideation may be particularly prone to impulsive aggression when experiencing emotional instability or irritability, especially in situations perceived as threats to self-esteem. This vulnerability is often rooted in underlying psychopathological features.31,32,33

A subgroup analysis of the HG revealed that parricide offenders were more likely to reside with family members (χ2 = 8.728, P = 0.008), experience frequent conflict (χ2 = 22.021, P = 0.001), and commit offenses in private settings such as homes (χ2 = 19.770, P < 0.001), compared with non-parricide homicide offenders. These findings suggest that financial dependence, prolonged cohabitation with caregivers, and chronic interpersonal conflict may contribute to the risk of parricide.34 The parricide group also demonstrated a higher prevalence of grandiose delusions (χ2 = 14.378, P < 0.001), possibly indicating the use of narcissistic defense mechanisms to cope with internal insecurity and anxiety. Additionally, they had fewer prior convictions compared with other participants in the HG, suggesting that their violent behavior may have been limited to the family context.35 These results underscore the need for targeted psychoeducation, family conflict mediation, and the development of community-based management systems capable of proactive intervention.

To our knowledge, this is the first domestic case-control study to comprehensively examine the demographic, predispositional, clinical, and criminological differences between homicidal and non-homicidal violent behaviors among patients with schizophrenia and to classify violence typologies in psychotic offenders. The findings delineate the key differentiating features using standardized assessments and provide robust empirical evidence to guide the development of targeted violence prevention strategies. The identification of modifiable factors—such as treatment non-adherence, symptom exacerbation, and social isolation—underscores the urgency of implementing early intervention frameworks within community-based mental health services.

This study has some limitations. First, its retrospective design and single-site sampling may limit the generalizability of the findings and pose a risk of selection bias. Limited exploration of motivational and psychodynamic factors restricts the interpretation of the internal mechanisms underlying violent behavior. Furthermore, the absence of a non-violent control group constitutes an additional limitation of this study. Because the National Forensic Hospital primarily admits individuals with severe mental illness who have received imprisonment or more severe custodial sentences, non-violent or minor cases were rarely available. Thus, the observed characteristics may reflect general features of seriously offending psychiatric populations rather than violence-specific profiles. To address this limitation and enhance the generalizability of the findings, future research should consider a multi-center approach in collaboration with diverse institutions to include a broader spectrum of control groups. Furthermore, implementing a prospective design would be crucial for improving external validity. Moreover, mixed-method approaches that integrate quantitative analyses with in-depth interviews or qualitative analyses may yield richer insights into the psychological drivers of violence, thereby informing the development of practical risk assessment tools and personalized intervention strategies for patients with schizophrenia at risk of violent behavior. Finally, although FDR correction was applied to reduce the risk of false discoveries due to multiple comparisons, the potential for residual Type I error cannot be entirely excluded. To validate these exploratory and unadjusted findings, future studies should apply multivariable logistic regression in larger samples to control for confounding among intercorrelated predictors, identify independent associations, and quantify model discrimination and calibration, thereby yielding more robust and clinically interpretable results. This study compared the clinical and criminological characteristics of individuals with schizophrenia who committed homicide versus non-homicidal violent offenses. Distinct differences between the two groups were identified in violence typology, symptomatology, personality traits, and criminal history. These findings highlight the importance of integrating delusional content, personality pathology, and violence typology (impulsive vs. premeditated) into comprehensive violence risk assessments and provide a foundation for developing individualized evaluation and intervention strategies in forensic psychiatric settings.
