Parental age, consanguinity, parity, and mode of delivery among children undergoing orchidopexy for cryptorchidism: a retrospective cohort study from Northern Jordan
Authors: Mohammad Aladaileh, Saleh Abualhaj, Ziydoun Abu-Sanad, Lina Alshadfan, Ayyub Masoud, Zain aldeen Saleh, Younis Hizzani, Shahed Maaitah, Danya Al Radaideh, Bassem Al Bataineh, Thekra Almgadem, Ethar Rababa, Mahdi Ahmad Alshboul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsCryptorchidism is a common congenital male genitourinary anomaly with multifactorial etiology. Data on parental age, consanguinity, parity, and delivery mode among affected children in Middle Eastern populations remain limited. This study evaluated the distribution of these factors among children undergoing orchidopexy in Northern Jordan.Patients and MethodsA retrospective study was conducted at Princess Rahma Teaching Hospital, Northern Jordan, including males aged 1–11 years who underwent orchidopexy for cryptorchidism between January 2016 and July 2024. Data were obtained from medical records and supplemented by structured telephone interviews.ResultsThe study included 446 patients. Mean paternal and maternal ages were 34.5 ± 7 and 28.5 ± 6.6 years, respectively. Consanguinity was reported in 27.6% of families, 58.3% of births were vaginal deliveries, and 53.6% of families had ≥3 children. Fathers aged ≥40 years accounted for 24% of the cohort, whereas mothers aged ≥35 years represented 18.2%. Consanguineous families had lower paternal age (p = 0.044) and more frequently had parity >5 (11% vs. 3%, p = 0.013).ConclusionsDistinct demographic patterns involving consanguinity, paternal age, and parity were observed among children with cryptorchidism in Northern Jordan, supporting the need for further controlled regional studies.

Introduction

Cryptorchidism, or undescended testis, is one of the most common congenital abnormalities affecting the male genitourinary system and is defined as failure of one or both testes to descend into the scrotum during fetal development or shortly after birth [1,2]. The condition affects approximately 1–4% of full-term male neonates, while substantially higher rates have been reported among premature infants [3,4]. Although spontaneous descent may occur within the first few months of life, persistent cryptorchidism is clinically important because of its association with long-term complications, including impaired fertility, testicular torsion, inguinal hernia, and an increased risk of testicular malignancy later in life [1,5]. Early recognition and management are therefore essential to reduce adverse reproductive and oncological outcomes.

The pathogenesis of cryptorchidism is complex and multifactorial, involving genetic, hormonal, environmental, and perinatal influences [2,6,7]. Testicular descent is a highly regulated developmental process that depends on normal endocrine signaling and fetal growth; disruption at any stage may contribute to failure of descent [2,6]. Several epidemiological studies have identified prematurity, low birth weight, intrauterine growth restriction, maternal smoking, and family history as potential risk factors [2,6]. More recently, attention has focused on parental demographic and obstetric characteristics, including maternal and paternal age, parity, and mode of delivery, as possible contributors to the development of cryptorchidism.

Advanced maternal age has been hypothesized to influence fetal development through altered hormonal and placental function, whereas increasing paternal age has been associated with de novo genetic and epigenetic alterations that may affect congenital outcomes [8–11]. Similarly, parity has been investigated because hormonal and uterine conditions may differ between first and subsequent pregnancies [2,5]. Mode of delivery, particularly cesarean section, has also been examined in relation to the risk of cryptorchidism, although reported findings remain inconsistent [2]. While some studies have demonstrated significant associations between these factors and cryptorchidism, others have reported weak or absent relationships, highlighting the need for further population-specific research [2].

An additional factor of particular relevance in Middle Eastern populations is consanguinity. Consanguineous marriage remains common in Jordan and neighboring countries, with reported rates historically ranging between 20% and 50% [12,13]. Consanguinity has been associated with an increased risk of several congenital anomalies and inherited disorders due to increased homozygosity for deleterious genetic variants [13,14]. Although cryptorchidism is known to have genetic determinants, the relationship between parental consanguinity and isolated cryptorchidism has not been adequately investigated, particularly in Arab populations where consanguineous unions are culturally prevalent [15]. Limited regional data are available addressing whether consanguinity may contribute independently or interact with demographic and obstetric factors in the occurrence of cryptorchidism.

Northern Jordan provides a unique setting for examining these associations because of the coexistence of high rates of consanguinity, varied reproductive patterns, and evolving obstetric practices. Furthermore, most published studies evaluating risk factors for cryptorchidism are based on Western populations, limiting the generalizability of their findings to Middle Eastern communities.

Therefore, the present study aimed to describe the distribution of paternal and maternal age, consanguinity, family size, and mode of delivery among children undergoing orchidopexy for cryptorchidism in Northern Jordan. By evaluating these combined genetic, sociodemographic, and obstetric factors in a local population, this study seeks to provide region-specific evidence on potential demographic and reproductive characteristics of this population.

Methods

Study design and setting

A retrospective study was conducted at Princess Rahma Teaching Hospital, a tertiary pediatric referral center located in Irbid Governorate, Northern Jordan. The study included male patients diagnosed with cryptorchidism who underwent orchidopexy between January 2016 and July 2024. Data collection was conducted in August 2024 through a review of hospital medical records, supplemented by telephone interviews with parents or guardians.

Participants

Male patients aged 1–11 years with a diagnosis of cryptorchidism who underwent orchidopexy during the study period were eligible for inclusion. Cryptorchidism was defined as the failure of one or both testes to descend into the scrotum, confirmed by clinical examination and supported by intraoperative assessment. Both unilateral and bilateral cases were included.

Patients with retractile testes were excluded because this condition does not represent a true undescended testis. Patients whose families declined to participate in the telephone interview or whose records lacked data on any primary study variable were also excluded. Cases were identified through hospital surgical and medical records, and contact information was obtained from archived patient files to facilitate the collection of supplementary data.

Variables

The primary objective of this study was to describe the distribution of paternal and maternal ages, parental consanguinity, parity, and mode of delivery among children undergoing orchidopexy for cryptorchidism. A secondary objective was to compare these demographic and reproductive characteristics according to parental consanguinity status. Accordingly, the study variables included paternal and maternal ages at conception, parental consanguinity, parity, and mode of delivery.

Consanguinity was defined as a self-reported biological relationship between the child’s parents and was assessed during the structured telephone interview. For analysis, consanguinity was recorded as a dichotomous variable (consanguineous vs. non-consanguineous).

The mode of delivery was categorized as vaginal delivery or cesarean section. Parity was defined as the total number of children in the family and was analyzed both as a continuous variable and as categorical groups (1, 2, 3–5, and >5 children).

Maternal and paternal ages were additionally categorized according to previously published literature on parental age and congenital anomalies [10,11]. Maternal age groups were classified as <20, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, and ≥40 years, whereas paternal age groups were categorized as 20–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, and ≥45 years. Ages potentially associated with congenital anomalies were defined as maternal age <20 years or >35 years and paternal age >40 years, based on prior epidemiological evidence [10,11].

Data sources and measurement

The dataset was derived from the medical records of Princess Rahma Teaching Hospital, Northern Jordan, which served as the data source for this study. Permission was obtained to access and use the relevant medical records for the purposes of this research. Data were obtained from hospital medical records and supplemented through structured telephone interviews with parents or guardians. The interview collected information regarding paternal age, maternal age, parity, consanguinity status, and mode of delivery. All interview-derived variables were self-reported. Clinical data extracted from medical records were independently verified by a second researcher to improve data accuracy. Any discrepancies identified during data verification were resolved through consensus review. No formal inter-rater reliability assessment was performed for telephone-collected information.

Bias

Several measures were implemented to minimize potential sources of bias. Diagnostic confirmation of cryptorchidism was based on documented clinical and operative findings to reduce outcome misclassification. Data extracted from medical records were independently verified by a second researcher, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus to improve data accuracy and consistency. Cases with incomplete information for any primary study variable were excluded from the analysis to reduce information bias related to missing data. In addition, parity and consanguinity were considered in the analysis due to their potential influence on reproductive and congenital outcomes.

Nonetheless, the retrospective design introduced potential limitations, including recall bias arising from parent-reported telephone data and selection bias related to the inclusion of surgically managed cases from a single tertiary referral center. Patients managed conservatively, or those with spontaneous testicular descent, were not represented in the study cohort.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Yarmouk University, Irbid, Jordan (IRB/2024/183). Because supplementary data were collected through remote telephone interviews without face-to-face contact, written informed consent was not feasible. The Research Ethics Committee of Yarmouk University approved the use of verbal informed consent for the telephone interviews. Verbal informed consent was obtained from adult participants and from the parents or legal guardians of participants younger than 18 years before the telephone interview and was documented in the study records. All collected data were anonymized and handled confidentially throughout the study in accordance with institutional ethical standards and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Numerical variables were presented as mean (SD) and minimum-maximum (min-max). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Fisher’s exact test, or the chi-square test, was used to test the differences in distributions of categorical variables. A post hoc test for a significant chi-square test was done based on adjusted standardized residuals. The p-value was calculated from the chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom after squaring the residuals to get the test statistics. The non-parametric Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to test for differences in numerical variables between two groups since the assumptions for the t-test were not met. No formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied; therefore, findings from post hoc analyses should be interpreted with caution.

Reporting guidelines

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement for observational studies.

Results

The study included 446 boys who underwent orchidopexy for cryptorchidism between 2016 and 2024. The mean paternal and maternal ages were 34.5 ± 7.0 and 28.5 ± 6.6 years, respectively. Parental consanguinity was reported in 123 (27.6%) families. The mean family size (parity) was 2.9 ± 1.6 children, with more than half of the families (53.6%) having three or more children. Vaginal delivery accounted for 58.3% of births, while cesarean section accounted for 41.7%. Detailed demographic and reproductive characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1.

Univariate descriptive statistics

The highest number of patients underwent the procedure in 2018 (n = 145, 32.5%), and the lowest number was in 2016 (n = 20, 4.5%). The mean paternal age was 34.5 ± 7 years, ranging from 20 up to 58 years old. On the other hand, maternal age had a mean of 28.5 ± 6.6 years, starting at 15 years old and up to 50. More than a quarter of the patients (27.6%) had consanguineous parents (Table 1).

The families of the patients had a mean of 2.9 ± 1.6 children. Parity for these families ranged from one child up to 9 children. In addition, about a quarter of the families had only one child (n = 111, 24.9%), and more than half of the families had 3 or more children (n = 239, 53.6%). More than half the deliveries were vaginal (n = 260, 58.3%) (Table 1).

The highest proportions of the mothers fell into the three age categories between 20 and 34, with about a quarter of the mothers in each of these categories (Table 1 and Figure 1(A,C)). Similarly, the most frequent categories for paternal age were the three categories between 20 and 39, and each of these categories represented about a quarter of the total number of fathers (Table 1 and Figure 1(B,D)). There were 33 (7.4%) mothers under 20 years, but none of the fathers was under this age. One hundred and seven fathers (n = 107, 24%) were 40 years or above, but only 81 mothers (18.2%) were 35 or above (Table 1).

Combinations of parental age categories

Cross tabulation between maternal and paternal age categories revealed that the most frequent combination with 53 families (12%) was paternal age 20–29, and maternal age 20–24. There were only two families (n = 2, 0.4%) combining maternal age under 20 with paternal age of 40 or above. In addition, 54 families (n = 54, 12.1%) combined older maternal age (35 and above) and older paternal age (40 and above) (Table 2).

Differences based on consanguinity status

Since consanguinity may be relevant in the context of congenital anomalies, we compared the characteristics of families with consanguineous and non-consanguineous parents. The aim was to check for any associations between consanguinity and any of these characteristics. Table 1 shows the differences between these two groups.

There was a significant difference in paternal age between the two groups (p = 0.044), where consanguineous couples had a lower mean paternal age (33.4 ± 6.6) than non-consanguineous couples (34.9 ± 7.1). Thus, higher frequencies were observed in the younger paternal age categories for the consanguineous group (20–29 and 30–34), and lower frequencies were observed in the older paternal age categories (40–44 and ≥45) (Table 1).

No significant differences were found between the two groups in maternal age, although consanguineous couples had higher frequencies for maternal age under 20 (11.4% vs. 5.9%). On the other hand, non-consanguineous couples had a higher frequency (6% vs. 3%) of the combination of maternal age ≥35 and paternal age ≥45, but this difference was not significant (p = 0.219).

No significant difference was observed in parity between the two groups (p = 0.430). Yet, when parity was categorized into 1, 2, 3–5, and >5, a significant difference was revealed between the two groups (p = 0.004). Specifically, this difference originated from a difference in the number of parity >5 families as indicated by post hoc p value of 0.013. Thus, consanguineous couples more frequently had >5 children compared to non-consanguineous couples (11% vs. 3%). Moreover, 27% of consanguineous, and 24% of non-consanguineous couples had one child.

There was no significant difference between the two groups in mode of delivery (p = 0.714). The significant difference in paternal age (p = 0.044) was observed for the continuous variable comparison using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, whereas no significant difference was found when paternal age was compared as categorical groups (p = 0.572).

Discussion

This retrospective study evaluated parental age, consanguinity, parity, and mode of delivery among children undergoing orchidopexy for cryptorchidism at a tertiary pediatric referral center in Northern Jordan. The findings demonstrated several notable demographic and reproductive patterns within this study, including a relatively high frequency of parental consanguinity, a substantial proportion of fathers aged ≥40 years, and higher-order parity among consanguineous families. Although the descriptive design and absence of a control population preclude causal inference or risk estimation, these findings contribute important regional data to the limited literature addressing cryptorchidism in Middle Eastern populations.

To place these findings into context, the observed parental characteristics should be interpreted relative to the background demographic profile of the Jordanian population. Parental consanguinity was reported in 27.6% of families in our cohort, a proportion that is broadly comparable with previously reported national estimates of approximately 20–30% in Jordan, although historical reports have documented higher rates in some regions and generations [12,16,17]. These descriptive comparisons suggest that the demographic profile of our cohort largely reflects the underlying population from which patients were drawn. However, because the present study did not include an unaffected comparison group, these observations should not be interpreted as evidence for or against an association between these characteristics and cryptorchidism.

Similarly, vaginal delivery accounted for 58.3% of births in our cohort, whereas cesarean section accounted for 41.7%. This cesarean delivery rate was higher than the approximately 27% national rate reported in the Jordan Population and Family Health Survey (2017–2018) [18]. However, because the present study lacked an unaffected comparison group and included only children undergoing orchidopexy at a tertiary referral center, this descriptive difference should not be interpreted as evidence of an association between cesarean delivery and cryptorchidism.

One of the most notable findings in the present study was the relatively frequent occurrence of parental consanguinity, reported in 27.6% of families. Although this proportion appears broadly consistent with previously reported consanguinity rates in Jordan and neighboring Middle Eastern countries, its presence remains clinically relevant given the known relationship between consanguinity and congenital disorders [12,13,16,19]. Consanguineous unions increase homozygosity and may facilitate the expression of recessive genetic variants involved in developmental abnormalities [20]. Cryptorchidism itself has recognized genetic determinants involving hormonal signaling pathways, gubernacular development, and testicular descent regulation [21,22]. Therefore, it is biologically plausible that parental consanguinity may contribute to susceptibility in at least a subset of cases by enriching for inherited genetic variants that affect testicular development or endocrine regulation. However, because the present study lacked an unaffected comparison group, these observations should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered evidence of an independent association between consanguinity and cryptorchidism.

The study also identified a substantial proportion of fathers aged 40 years or older, accounting for nearly one-quarter of the cohort. Advanced paternal age has increasingly been implicated in congenital anomalies and reproductive disorders through mechanisms involving the accumulation of de novo mutations, altered spermatogonial replication fidelity, and epigenetic dysregulation [23,24]. Previous epidemiological studies have demonstrated associations between advanced paternal age and congenital malformations, neurodevelopmental disorders, and adverse reproductive outcomes [8–11]. In the current study, paternal age differed modestly between consanguineous and non-consanguineous families, with a lower mean paternal age observed among consanguineous couples. However, the absolute numerical difference was relatively small and may have limited clinical significance despite statistical significance. Moreover, categorical paternal age comparisons did not demonstrate statistically significant differences. These findings suggest that paternal age may interact with sociocultural and reproductive characteristics rather than represent an isolated determinant.

Maternal age did not differ significantly between consanguineous and non-consanguineous families, although younger maternal age appeared somewhat more frequent among consanguineous couples. Previous literature evaluating maternal age and cryptorchidism has produced inconsistent results [2]. Some studies have suggested increased risks at both younger and advanced maternal ages, potentially mediated through altered hormonal environments, placental dysfunction, or obstetric complications, whereas others have demonstrated weak or absent associations [2]. The current findings align more closely with studies reporting limited independent influence of maternal age. Nevertheless, interpretation remains constrained by the study’s descriptive nature and the lack of adjustment for important confounders, including prematurity, birth weight, maternal comorbidities, and environmental exposures.

Parity represented another notable reproductive characteristic in this study. More than half of the families had three or more children, and parity greater than five was significantly more common among consanguineous families. Higher parity in consanguineous families may primarily reflect regional sociocultural and reproductive patterns rather than a direct biological relationship with cryptorchidism. Previous studies evaluating parity and cryptorchidism have reported conflicting findings, with some suggesting increased risk among first pregnancies and others demonstrating no significant association [2]. Hormonal, uterine, and placental factors differing across pregnancies have been proposed as possible mechanisms, although evidence remains inconclusive [2,5]. In the present study, parity differences likely reflect broader characteristics of family structure within consanguineous populations rather than evidence of causation.

No significant association was identified between mode of delivery and consanguinity status, and vaginal delivery remained the predominant mode overall. Existing literature regarding cesarean section and cryptorchidism has similarly been inconsistent [2]. Some studies have proposed indirect associations related to obstetric complications, prematurity, or fetal distress rather than delivery mode itself [2]. The current findings do not support a meaningful relationship between delivery mode and the demographic characteristics evaluated within this cohort.

Future research should integrate clinical epidemiological observations with genomic, transcriptomic, epigenomic, and metabolomic investigations to improve understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying cryptorchidism. In populations where consanguineous marriage is common, family-based studies incorporating pedigree analysis, recurrence-risk assessment, and molecular genetic testing, including candidate-gene or whole-exome sequencing, may help determine whether recessive genetic variants contribute to disease susceptibility and identify biologically distinct subgroups of cryptorchidism. Such multidisciplinary approaches may facilitate improved disease stratification and provide deeper insight into the complex interplay between genetic, environmental, and reproductive factors involved in testicular descent.

The present study has several strengths. To our knowledge, this is among the few studies from Jordan and the broader Middle East that examine parental demographic and reproductive characteristics among children with cryptorchidism. The relatively large cohort size and inclusion of patients over a nine-year period provide valuable regional epidemiological data. In addition, inclusion of consanguinity as a study variable addresses an important yet understudied factor in congenital anomaly research within Arab populations.

However, several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design limits causal interpretation. Most importantly, the absence of a control population prevented formal comparison with unaffected children and precluded estimation of measures of association, such as odds ratios or relative risks. Consequently, it was not possible to determine whether the observed distributions differed from those expected in the general population, and the findings should therefore be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of independent associations with cryptorchidism. Second, the study was conducted at a single tertiary referral center and included only surgically managed patients, which may have introduced referral and selection bias toward more severe or persistent cases. Patients with spontaneous testicular descent or those managed outside tertiary care settings were not represented. Third, some variables were obtained through parent-reported telephone interviews, which may introduce recall bias and misclassification. Fourth, important clinical and perinatal variables known to influence cryptorchidism—including prematurity, birth weight, gestational age, maternal smoking, maternal diabetes, environmental exposures, and family history—were unavailable and therefore could not be evaluated. Furthermore, detailed clinical characteristics of cryptorchidism, including anatomical location (e.g., abdominal, inguinal, or prescrotal), laterality (unilateral or bilateral), and other aspects of clinical presentation, were not consistently available in the medical records and therefore could not be analyzed. Information regarding coexisting medical conditions and congenital anomalies was also not systematically collected, precluding evaluation of their potential influence on the observed demographic and reproductive characteristics. In addition, demographic characteristics such as ethnicity, educational level, primary language, and geographic origin were not recorded because these variables were not routinely documented in the available medical records or collected during the structured telephone interviews. Consequently, their potential influence on the study findings could not be evaluated, and the generalizability of the results to more heterogeneous populations may be limited. Finally, although the overall cohort was relatively large, several subgroup analyses involved relatively small numbers of participants, raising the possibility of type II (false-negative) error for some non-significant comparisons.

Overall, this study provides exploratory regional data describing parental demographic and reproductive characteristics among children undergoing orchidopexy for cryptorchidism in Northern Jordan. Although the descriptive design does not permit causal inference or assessment of disease risk, the findings contribute important baseline epidemiological evidence from a population in which consanguinity remains common. These observations may assist in generating hypotheses for future multicenter, controlled, and genetically informed studies aimed at clarifying the biological and epidemiological mechanisms underlying cryptorchidism.

Conclusions

This retrospective study provides region-specific epidemiological data regarding parental demographic and reproductive characteristics among children undergoing orchidopexy for cryptorchidism in Northern Jordan. Within this cohort, parental consanguinity was relatively frequent, advanced paternal age was common, and higher-order parity was observed more often among consanguineous families.

Although these findings may reflect underlying genetic, sociocultural, and reproductive patterns relevant to populations with prevalent consanguineous marriage, the descriptive nature of the study and absence of a comparison group preclude inference regarding causality or independent risk estimation. Accordingly, the present results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.
