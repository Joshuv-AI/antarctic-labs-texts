Compensatory function change by segment-counting method in predicted postoperative pulmonary function at 1 year after surgery: systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Teng-Wei Wang, Qiang Zhang, Zhihong Cai, Qinhong Xu, Jinrong Lin, Huilong Yeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis systematic review aimed to assess the accuracy of the segment-counting method in predicting long-term pulmonary function recovery and investigate compensatory changes following different extents of lung resection.MethodsWe included studies that measured forced expiratory volume at 1 s (FEV1) between 6 and 18 months postoperatively, comparing it to the predicted postoperative FEV1 (ppoFEV1) using the segment-counting method. The extent of lung resection was correlated with the ratio of postoperative FEV1 to ppoFEV1. A comprehensive search was conducted in Embase, MEDLINE and Web of Science using terms related to ‘lung resection’ and ‘pulmonary function’. The final search was completed on 18 February 2022. Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.Results39 studies comprising 78 observation cohorts met the inclusion criteria. The analysis showed significant differences in pulmonary function in patients with ≥3 resected segments. Meta-regression indicated that the number of resected segments significantly impacted the postoperative FEV1/ppoFEV1 ratio, explaining 57% of the variance (R²=0. 57), with moderate heterogeneity (I²=61. 87%) across studies. Other variables, including patient age, body mass index, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery use and tumour stage, did not show significant effects.DiscussionLimitations of the review included moderate heterogeneity between studies and potential selection bias related to the stage of cancer and lung volume reduction effects. The findings suggest that the extent of lung resection correlates with better-than-expected pulmonary function, potentially due to compensatory mechanisms.PROSPERO registration numberThis review was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42021293608).Keywords: Respiratory Function Test, Lung Cancer, Lung Volume Reduction Surgery, Thoracic Surgery

Introduction

Lobectomy remains the standard treatment for early-stage lung cancer, involving the surgical removal of an entire lung lobe. While this approach ensures thorough cancer clearance, it raises concerns about significant loss of pulmonary function. Over the past decade, increasing attention has been paid to sublobar resection, an alternative procedure that preserves more lung tissue while potentially compromising oncological outcomes, with a moderate decrease in disease-free survival compared with lobectomy.1,3 Despite this trend, predicting the exact extent of functional loss relative to the amount of lung tissue resected remains an ongoing challenge. The balance between preserving lung parenchyma to maintain function and ensuring sufficient oncological clearance is particularly difficult to strike without a clear understanding of the functional consequences of different resection extents.

One of the most commonly used indicators for pulmonary function in lung resection surgery is the forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1). Surgeons rely on preoperative FEV1 to predict postoperative pulmonary function using the segment-counting method, where predicted postoperative FEV1 (ppoFEV1) is calculated by estimating the number of remaining lung segments. The widely used formula for this prediction is: ppoFEV1=preoperative FEV1 × (19 – the number of segments to be removed) / 19. This method assumes that lung function loss is directly proportional to the number of resected segments, a simplistic assumption that does not account for individual compensatory mechanisms or the dynamic nature of postoperative recovery.

Emerging research suggests that postoperative lung function may undergo compensatory changes over time, resulting in better-than-expected outcomes, particularly in cases of more extensive resections. However, there is still insufficient data on long-term pulmonary function changes following lung resection. Current methods, including the segment-counting approach, may not fully capture the complexity of functional recovery, especially beyond the immediate postoperative period.

This review aims to address the gap in understanding the long-term relationship between the extent of lung resection and pulmonary function recovery. By comparing actual postoperative FEV1 to the predicted ppoFEV1 calculated through the segment-counting method, this study investigates whether compensatory mechanisms, if present, influence pulmonary function outcomes over time. Clarifying this relationship could provide critical insights into surgical decision-making, allowing for a more nuanced approach to balancing the risks of functional loss with the need for effective cancer resection.

Methods

This review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and is registered on PROSPERO. MEDLINE, Embase and Web of Science were the databases used to select the data against the eligibility criteria. Only English-language articles and human studies published between 1 January 1990 and 28 February 2022 were included. This review included participants diagnosed with lung cancer with postoperative FEV1 measured 6–18 months after surgery. Studies recruiting ≥20 participants were eligible, including both prospective and retrospective studies as well as observational and randomised studies. The exclusion criteria were observation cohorts with more than eight removed lung segments and studies without specific information regarding the number of resected segments. Participants with chronic renal, heart and liver diseases as well as interstitial lung disease were not included in this review. Online supplemental appendix a in the supplementary material lists the PICOS framework and eligibility used for this review. The search format was the same for all databases: (1) lung cancer (carcinoma or neoplasm), (2) lobectomy or segmentectomy, (3) lung resection or pneumonectomy (lobe resection, wedge resection, sublobar resection or limited resection), (4) respiratory (pulmonary or lung) function, (5) and/1–4. The full search strings used for each database are provided in online supplemental appendix b of the supplementary material.

This review compared postoperative FEV1 to ppoFEV1 at 6–18 months and used the segment-counting method regarding the ‘(19 – the number of segments to be removed)’ in concordance with the ‘extent of resection’. Based on the formula, postoperative FEV1 was expected to decrease at this fixed rate according to the number of resected segments. For reference, the number of segments resected for each lobectomy is as follows: three for right upper lobectomy, two for right middle lobectomy, five for right lower lobectomy, four for left upper lobectomy and five for left lower lobectomy. Therefore, once postoperative compensatory changes were noted, the obtained FEV1 was expected to deviate from ppoFEV1, which is the rationale of this review. Online supplemental appendix c of the supplementary material presents the method used to test compensatory changes.

Software and statistical method

To prepare the variables, the postoperative FEV1, ppoFEV1, preoperative FEV1 and extent of resection were retrieved from the databases. Any missing values among the ppoFEV1, preoperative FEV1 and extent of resection were calculated using the following formula: ppoFEV1=preoperative FEV1 × (19 – the number of segments to be removed) / 19. All postoperative FEV1 and ppoFEV1 results, according to the number of resected segments, were entered into an Excel spreadsheet, Microsoft Office 2016. A built-in dot plot in Excel was generated to present the change between the postoperative FEV1 and ppoFEV1 according to the increasing extent of resection.

In the second comparison, postoperative FEV1 and ppoFEV1 were extrapolated using RevMan V.5.4, and forest plots were generated at a 95% confidence level under a random effect using the inverse variance statistical method. Funnel plots were used to assess the reporting bias. We assessed whether any significant difference existed between postoperative FEV1 and ppoFEV1 in the following five extents of resection: <2, ≥2 to <3, ≥3 to <4, ≥4 to <5 and ≥5 segments.

To explore the potential impact of the number of segments removed on compensatory changes in pulmonary function, a meta-regression was conducted to incorporate potential variables, including publication year, follow-up duration, age, body mass index (BMI), study design (prospective or retrospective), the ratio of FEV1 to forced vital capacity (FEV1/FVC), the ratio of stage I cancer and the use of video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). The meta-regression analysis was performed using Stata V.16/SE, where the ratio of postoperative FEV1 to predicted postoperative FEV1 (PO/ppoFEV1) was calculated as the effect size. All variables were compared with this effect size and assessed using the z-test and R² to evaluate their associations.

A random-effects model was applied to account for between-study variability, and the restricted maximum likelihood method was used to estimate between-study variance (tau²). Heterogeneity across studies was quantified using the I² statistic, which represents the proportion of total variation due to heterogeneity, and tau², which estimates the between-study variance.

For the input method, publication year and follow-up duration were entered directly, while FEV1/FVC, age and BMI were extrapolated as mean values. Binomial variables, such as the ratio of stage I cancer, study design and VATS use, were calculated as percentages. For example, if a cohort reported that all patients had stage I cancer, the value was recorded as 100%; if no patients had stage I cancer, the value was 0%. The risk of bias was quantified using the number of stars assigned according to the Newcastle-Ottawa Scoring System. The variable ‘region’ is categorised based on proximity to the equator. Regions close to the equator, including Taiwan, are coded as (1) countries spanning large latitudes, such as China and the USA, are assigned a code of (2) Korea and Japan are coded as (3) while European countries are categorised with a code of 4.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or public involvement were involved in this review. However, for the review question, we consulted the patients in our facility who planned to have different extents of lung resection. Almost all patients expressed concern about pulmonary function and sacrifice of life quality in the long-term.

Results

A literature search was conducted in March 2022. The keyword strings initially generated 3550 studies from the databases. Figure 1 demonstrates the PRISMA study-filtering process.4 TWW and ZC independently reviewed the relevant literature. Any doubts or uncertainties were resolved by JL. EndNote V.20 was used to save and process the literature filtering, which automatically removed 970 studies from the data set. 65 other studies were excluded because of the inability to locate their abstracts. In addition, 11 abstracts printed in languages other than English were excluded. The remaining 2293 studies were considered irrelevant or ineligible based on the inclusion criteria. Consequently, the abstract review filtered out 2369 studies, leaving the remaining 271 studies for the screening stage.

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 flow diagram. FEV1, forced expiratory volume at 1 s.

During the screening stage, 61 studies were excluded because of inaccessible full-text or data reports. In 29 studies, no comparison was found between pre-lung and post-lung resection, and the period of postoperative FEV1 measurement was outside the 6–18 months range in 66 studies; thus, these studies were excluded. In addition, eligibility screening filtered 41 studies owing to missing essential variables. Data from another 20 studies could not be pooled because the ‘percentage’ unit for FEV1 could not be converted back to litres, which was required for comparison in this review. Furthermore, 15 duplicate study reports were excluded. Thus, this review finally included 39 studies published between 1995 and 2021 involving 3632 patients in the pool for analysis. Only one randomised controlled trial was included in this review. The other 12 trials were prospective but with no randomisation or blinding method, and the remaining 24 studies were retrospective. Based on the number of resected lung segments, 78 observation cohorts were identified among the included 39 studies.5,43 A summary of the included studies is provided in online supplemental appendix d of the supplementary material, while online supplemental appendix e presents the quality assessment of each study using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scoring System.

Postoperative FEV1 versus ppoFEV1

The data from all included studies were extracted and pooled (online supplemental appendix f of supplementary material). A discrepancy between the postoperative FEV1 and ppoFEV1 was observed when the extent of resection was increased or when more lung segments were removed. Figure 2 demonstrates the trend by comparing postoperative FEV1 with ppoFEV1 as the extent of resection increased. Postoperative FEV1 was slightly lower than ppoFEV1 when the extent of resection was <2 segments. However, as the extent of resection increased to ≥3 segments, the postoperative FEV1 increased compared with the predicted value. Moreover, the postoperative function continuously increased as the number of resected segments increased. On comparing postoperative FEV1 with ppoFEV1 in terms of the number of resected segments, the gaps between postoperative FEV1 and ppoFEV1 were significant only in lung resections of ≥3 segments. However, heterogeneity also appeared among the cohorts with a more extensive extent of resection, which reached as high as 90% in the ≥4 to <5 resected segments group. For the extent of resection <2 segments, 15 observation cohorts with data from 814 participants were included in the pool. Comparing postoperative FEV1 to ppoFEV1 resulted in a standard mean difference of –0.09 (–0.20 to 0.01 at 95% CI, p=0.08). The heterogeneity in this resection extent group was low (I2=4%). In the stratification of ≥2 to <3 resected segments, it should be noted that three of the four cohorts strictly meet the criterion of two resected segments, while one cohort slightly exceeds this threshold. The four observation cohorts with a total of 199 participants resulted in a standard mean difference of 0.05 (–0.15 to 0.24 at 95% CI, p=0.64). The heterogeneity was low (I2=0%). 20 observation cohorts belonged to the group of ≥3 to <4 resected segments. There were data of 935 participants in the pool, with a standard mean difference of 0.23 (0.09 to 0.38 at 95% CI, p=0.002). Although the difference was significant, the heterogeneity increased (I2=52%). In the group of ≥4 to <5 segments, there were 26 observation cohorts with 1267 participants in the pool, resulting in a standard mean difference of 0.51 (0.23 to 0.80 at 95% CI, p=0.0005). The heterogeneity in this group was high (I2=90%), which may have been due to the effect of outliers. Outliers were identified as observation cohorts from the study by Karenovics et al.15 There were 13 observation cohorts in the group of ≥5 resected segments, including 427 participants, resulting in a standard mean difference of 0.45 (0.29 to 0.63 at 95% CI, p<0.00001). The heterogeneity in this group was low (I2=28%). Online supplemental appendix g of the supplementary material includes forest plots comparing postoperative FEV1 and ppoFEV1 according to the extent of resection, while online supplemental appendix f provides the corresponding funnel plots for each resection extent.

Overall, the cohort distributions in each funnel plot are fairly balanced. However, there is evidence of bias in two cohorts within the ≥4 to <5 resection category. Both of these cohorts were reported in studies by Karenovics et al in 2016,16 which involved an open-label study investigating whether preoperative high-intensity training improved postoperative pulmonary function recovery. The observed bias may be explained by the Hawthorne effect, where participants modify their behaviour due to the awareness of being observed or tested.

Sensitivity analysis—multivariable investigation

Owing to the moderate-to-high heterogeneity observed in the large extent of resection, the number of resected segments may not precisely predict po/ppoFEV1. In addition to the number of resected segments, the meta-regression further analysed variables such as year of publication, FEV1/FVC ratio, duration of follow-up, stage I cancer, age, BMI, use of VATS, study design, risk of bias and study region. In total, several covariates had missing data that were imputed using mean values. Specifically, the FEV1/FVC ratio had nine missing data points (11.54%), stage I percentage had 28 missing data points (35.90%), age had two missing data points (2.56%), BMI had 49 missing data points (62.82%) and the use of VATS had 34 missing data points (43.59%). No missing data were present for the other covariates. These percentages of missing data were accounted for in the meta-regression analysis to assess their potential impact on the associations observed. Missing data for all variables are listed in online supplemental appendix i of the supplementary material. Initially, 78 cohorts were included, but five cohorts were excluded due to missing within-study SEs or SDs. Ultimately, 73 cohorts were analysed (table 2). The results of the meta-regression indicated a between-study variance (tau²) of 0.0029, suggesting minimal variability in effect sizes across the included studies. The I² statistic was 57.90%, indicating moderate heterogeneity across the studies. This implies that approximately 58% of the variability observed in postoperative lung function outcomes could be attributed to genuine differences between the studies, rather than random error. The R2 was 0.55. Only the number of resected segments was found to be statistically significant. Discussion

This review demonstrated a continuous increase in the discrepancy between the postoperative FEV1 and predicted value as the number of resected segments increased. Nonetheless, the difference between postoperative FEV1 and ppoFEV1 only became significant at a 95% CI in the extent of resection of ≥3 to <4 segments, as well as in the ≥4 to <5 and ≥5 categories. Notably, the mean difference in the ≥5 resected segments group was slightly lower than that in the ≥4 to <5 resected segments group, which may be due to the impact of outliers. Correlation and regression studies revealed a moderate and positive relationship between the extent of resection and the mean ratio of postoperative FEV1 to ppoFEV1, which was determined by dividing the mean of postoperative FEV1 at 1 year by ppoFEV1. These results imply some resistance to preventing pulmonary function loss by large-extent lung resection, which is probably due to compensatory function growth (CFG) in patients who have undergone extensive resection.

There are concerns that the outcomes of FEV1 over FVC, stage I lung cancer, age, BMI and use of VATS may skew the results when a large resection is performed.44 The first report of VATS application in the study groups was published in 2015.19 Therefore, it is uncertain whether this difference was due to the VATS application, innovative surgical techniques or advanced nursing practice. However, our meta-regression analysis reveals that while these variables do not significantly affect postoperative CFG, the number of resected segments remains the primary determinant.

This review stratified the extent of resection in advance by extrapolating ppoFEV1 as the baseline for comparison. No other review comparing ppoFEV1 with postoperative FEV1 has investigated the relationship between the extent of resection and postoperative functional recovery. Second, this review aimed to demonstrate the impact of thoracic space sparing by simulating the impact of mechanical strain on the recovery or growth of the remaining lung. Thus, it provides resistance to pulmonary function loss as the extent of resection increases. Finally, although most experts believe that a limited extent of resection can preserve pulmonary function, this review provides a perspective from the other end. A greater extent of resection results in greater cancer clearance and potentially triggers CFG. This result provides an objective basis for further investigation of adult lung growth mechanisms, as it synchronises the mechanisms by which mechanical strain and oxygen demand activate lung growth in adulthood.45 After lung resection, the spared thoracic space increases the mechanical strain in the remaining lung, thereby increasing the workload required to meet the whole-body oxygen demand. Both stress factors trigger CFG by expanding the lung volume and boosting pulmonary function.

The data showed that the loss of pulmonary function was not as high as predicted by the increased extent of lung resection. However, it remains unclear whether this resistance to functional loss is due to lung growth or reservoir use. Although molecular and cellular changes are beyond the scope of this review, the challenge of initiating lung growth in adulthood requires additional laboratory testing. Nonetheless, Kim et al46 identified that bronchioalveolar stem cells generate cell linings for nearby terminal bronchioles and alveoli, which may initiate lung growth even without increasing the number of alveoli. However, this may challenge the definition of lung growth previously proposed by Hsia47 in that it requires an increase in the functional unit consisting of terminal bronchioles and alveoli and should be distinct from structural remodelling or reservoir use.

Consequently, there is a general lack of evidence to support adult lung growth in humans. More in-depth studies should focus on proving a direct relationship between functional improvement and cellular lining growth or on providing evidence of adulthood alveoli increase.48 In short, pulmonary function loss does not follow the same proportion of resection extent over 6–18 months, especially in the group with a larger extent of resection. Therefore, this review suggests that experts should consider lung growth mechanisms when formulating methods to predict postoperative pulmonary function.

A limitation of this review was the moderate correlation between the extent of resection and postoperative lung function improvement. The large heterogeneity among the groups in which extensive resections were performed suggests the potential influence of other factors warranting further comprehensive research. Although the sensitivity analysis incorporated numerous variables into the regression model, the considerable amount of missing data, particularly for BMI, rendered the outcomes of the other variables inconclusive. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct independent reviews and analyses of these variables.

This review assumed that there would be no change in tumour size with respect to the baseline FEV1 measurement. However, it is unclear whether the tumour would compress the surrounding lung tissue, and if so, how much of a difference that compression would have on FEV1 measurement in practice.49 50 Additionally, surgeons tend to perform a larger extent of lung resection when the tumour is larger, which could introduce a selection bias. Considering this, the sensitivity analysis included variables of stage I or tumours <3 cm, which resulted in no significant impact on our linear regression model. In addition, this review was based on the premise of the same pulmonary function in each segment. The bronchial branches and related vessels differentiate into lung segments. However, this does not imply that the respiratory functions in each segment domain are equivalent. Iwano et al51 52 claimed that the segment-counting method fails to consider individual differences, with only a moderate correlation between the segment-counting method and CT volumetry, which is more closely related to the pulmonary function test. An increasing number of researchers claim that indicators generated by the CT modality in preoperative assessments are better related to postoperative morbidity than those generated by pulmonary function tests.53 CT scans can also be used to quantify the split lung function for each segment.54

Despite the correlation between CT volumetry and pulmonary function tests, these tests fail to consider the lung reservoir and compensatory functional change compared with the segment-counting method. This review pooled FEV1 data and generalised the outcomes using the segment-counting method to investigate the CFG potential of the human lung at different extents of lung resection. However, further investigations of the postoperative pulmonary function and growth potential of each segment are required.

Conclusions

The study demonstrated that the more lung tissue was removed, the more likely the patient’s pulmonary function was to be better than expected, indicating that some resistance, moderately related to the extent of resection, could prevent extensive loss of lung function after surgery in the long-term.
