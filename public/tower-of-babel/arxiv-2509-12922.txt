Evolution of the infrared luminosity function and its corresponding dust-obscured star formation rate density out to z ∼ 6 z\sim 6
M. P. Koprowski, J. V. Wijesekera, J. S. Dunlop, K. Lisiecki, D. J. McLeod, R. J. McLure, M. J. Michałowski, M. Solar

Abstract
We present a new determination of the evolving far-infrared galaxy luminosity function (FIR LF) and the resulting inferred evolution of dust-obscured star-formation rate density ( ρ SFR \rho_{\rm SFR} ) out to redshift z ∼ 6 z\sim 6 . To establish the evolving co-moving number density of FIR-bright objects, we make use of the high-resolution ALMA follow-up study (AS2UDS), of the JCMT SCUBA-2 Cosmology Legacy Survey (S2CLS) sub-mm imaging in the UKIDSS UDS survey field. In order to estimate the contributions of faint/low-mass sources we implement a method in which the faint-end of the IR LF is inferred by stacking (in stellar mass and redshift bins) the optical/near-infrared samples of star-forming galaxies into the appropriate FIR Herschel and sub-mm JCMT maps. Using this information we determine the faint-end slope of the FIR LF in two intermediate redshift bins (where it can be robustly established) and then adopt this result at all other redshifts. The evolution of the characteristic luminosity of the galaxy FIR LF, L ⋆ L_{\star} , is found to be increase monotonically with redshift, evolving as L ⋆ ∝ z 1.38 ± 0.07 L_{\star}\propto z^{1.38\pm 0.07} , while the characteristic number density, Φ ⋆ \Phi_{\star} , is well fitted by double power-law function, constant at z < 2.24 z<2.24 and declining as z − 4.95 ± 0.73 z^{-4.95\pm 0.73} at higher redshifts. The evolution of the corresponding dust-obscured star-formation rate density was then calculated and is here compared with the results from a number of recent studies in the literature. Our analysis confirms that dust-obscured star-formation activity dominates ρ SFR \rho_{\rm SFR} at cosmic noon, but then becomes progressively less important with increasing redshift: while dusty star-forming galaxies are still found out to the highest redshifts explored here, UV-visible star formation dominates at z > 4 z>4 , and dust-obscured activity contributes < 25 % <25\% of ρ SFR \rho_{\rm SFR} by z ∼ 6 z\sim 6 .

Introduction

One of the main goals of modern extragalactic astronomy is to describe the evolution of the star formation (SF) throughout cosmic history. The most direct way of achieving this is through the calibration of various relationships, that characterize how the SF in galaxies evolve with time, most important examples of which include the ultraviolet (UV) and infrared (IR) luminosity function (LF; e.g.,
Gruppioni et al. 2013
;
Koprowski et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Bouwens et al. 2021
;
Fujimoto et al. 2024
), and the star formation rate density (
\rho_{\rm SFR}
; e.g.,
Madau & Dickinson 2014
;
Dunlop et al. 2017
;
Traina et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Barrufet et al. 2025
).

Since the discovery of the cosmic infrared background (CIF;
Puget et al. 1996
;
Hauser et al. 1998
), the IR contribution to the total
\rho_{\rm SFR}
, determined through the integration of the corresponding luminosity function, has been the primary focus of numerous studies (e.g.,
Magnelli et al. 2013
;
Gruppioni et al. 2013
;
Madau & Dickinson 2014
;
Rowan-Robinson et al. 2016
;
Koprowski et al. 2017
;
Dunlop et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Gruppioni et al. 2020
;
Traina et al. 2024
;
Magnelli et al. 2024
;
Fujimoto et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2025
). While most of the results seem to be consistent out to
z\sim 2-3
, where the SF activity of the Universe reaches its peak, at
\gtrsim 4
the derived values disagree by over an order of magnitude.

The accurate assessment of the evolution of star formation in the early Universe is essential for evaluating current galaxy evolution models. Thanks to the sensitivity of the rest-frame UV observations, the unobscured portion of the SF in galaxies has been investigated out to redshifts as high as
\sim 11
(e.g.,
Bouwens et al. 2014
;
Laporte et al. 2016
;
Harikane et al. 2024
;
McLeod et al. 2024
;
Donnan et al. 2024
). Due to the time required for the formation of the interstellar dust, the unobscured star formation is thought to dominate the total budget at such high redshifts. Since it is now well known that by cosmic noon (
z\sim 2-3
) the vast majority of stellar emission in the UV gets absorbed by interstellar dust and re-emitted in the IR, we expect
\rho_{\rm SFR}
to transition from being UV- to IR-dominated. However, because of the observational limitations in the infrared (see
Casey et al. 2014
for details), the exact redshift of this transition is yet to be determined.

The contribution of the dust-enshrouded stellar emission to the total star formation budget in the early Universe has primarily been assessed through the most extreme IR sources, known as sub-mm galaxies (SMGs; e.g.,
Smail et al. 1997
;
Hughes et al. 1998
), or more broadly, dusty star-forming galaxies (DSFGs;
Casey et al. 2014
), with very large IR luminosities (
L_{\rm IR}\gtrsim 10^{12}\,{\rm L_{\odot}}
; e.g.,
Chapman et al. 2005
;
Michałowski et al. 2017
) and extremely high star formation rates (
{\rm SFR}\gtrsim 100\,{\rm M_{\odot}\,yr^{-1}}
; e.g.,
Swinbank et al. 2014
;
Koprowski et al. 2016
). Observations conducted using NASA’s
Spitzer Space Telescope
and ESA’s
Herschel Space Observatory
allowed the determination of the infrared luminosity functions out to
z\simeq 2-4
(e.g.,
Le Floc’h et al. 2005
;
Caputi et al. 2007
;
Rodighiero et al. 2010
;
Patel et al. 2013
;
Magnelli et al. 2013
;
Gruppioni et al. 2013
;
Rowan-Robinson et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2019
). However, only the most extreme DSFGs have been detected at
z\gtrsim 2
, which necessitated significant extrapolations to account for fainter objects, leading to uncertain and often inconsistent results.

Koprowski et al. 2017
utilized a sample of James Clerk Maxwell Telescope (JCMT) 850-
{\rm\mu m}
-selected sample to construct the IR luminosity function out to
z\sim 4
. While the sub-mm wavelengths benefit from the negative
K
-correction, allowing for the observation of IR galaxies out to very high redshifts, surprisingly low numbers of sources were detected at
z\gtrsim 2
. Similarly, due to the very limited areas observed, the high-resolution ALMA blank-sky surveys conducted in recent years also struggled to detect any sources at very high redshifts (e.g.,
Walter et al. 2016
;
Dunlop et al. 2017
;
Bouwens et al. 2020
).
Gruppioni et al. 2020
made use of the ALMA sources serendipitously detected as a part of the ALMA Large Program
to INvestigate CII at Early Times (ALPINE) to derive the IR LF out to
z\sim 6
.
Fujimoto et al. 2024
reached similar redshifts utilizing the ALMA Lensing Cluster Survey (ALCS) data, with faint objects detected through lensing. Most recently,
Magnelli et al. 2024
;
Traina et al. 2024
and
Liu et al. 2025
determined the contribution of IR sources to the total
\rho_{\rm SFR}
in the early Universe, using the somewhat inhomogeneous sample of individual ALMA pointings collected as a part of the A
3
COSMOS survey
(
Liu et al. 2019
;
Adscheid et al. 2024
)
. Due to the limited sensitivity of the IR data, the corresponding faint-end slope of the IR LF reported in these works ranges between -0.2 and -1.0, with errors approaching 0.5. In addition,
Dudzevičiūtė
et al. 2020
analyzed a high-resolution ALMA follow-up (AS2UDS;
Stach et al. 2019
) of the JCMT SCUBA-2 sub-mm survey of the UKIDSS UDS field
(
Geach et al. 2017
)
. As a part of their analysis,
Dudzevičiūtė
et al. 2020
derived photometric redshifts, IR luminosities, IR luminosity functions and the corresponding star formation rate densities down to the 870
{\rm\mu m}
flux limit of 3.6 mJy, later extrapolating their results down to a flux limit of 1 mJy using the ALMA number counts of
Hatsukade et al. 2018
. They found that the contribution of SMGs to the total star
formation rate density in the Universe peaks at
z\sim 3
, with the contribution of
\sim 15
% for sources with
S_{870}>3.6
mJy and
\sim 60
% for
S_{870}>1
mJy, indicating that roughly half of the
\rho_{\rm SFR}
at that redshift comes from ULIRG-luminosity sources.

The aim of this study is to add to the existing knowledge by deriving the evolution of the IR LF, including its faint-end slope, up to
z\sim 6
. In order to include the contribution from faintest galaxies, we adopt an indirect approach, where the faint-end portion of the LF is determined through stacking of the optically-selected mass-complete sample of star-forming galaxies in the FIR maps of
Herschel
and JCMT maps, with the stellar mass used as a proxy for the IR luminosity. The paper is structured as follows. We present the data in Section
2
. In Section
3.1.1
we explain the details behind our derivation of the IR LF faint-end slope. Section
3.1.2
describes how a high-resolution ALMA follow-up data, AS2UDS, of all the SCUBA-2 sources detected within the S2CLS map of the UKIDSS UDS field
(
Geach et al. 2017
;
Stach et al. 2019
)
was used to construct the bright end of our IR LF. In Section
3.1.3
we construct the functional form of the IR LF and quantify its evolution with redshift out to
z\sim 6
, discuss the results and compare with recent literature. The corresponding star formation rate density is derived, discussed and compared with other works in Section
3.2
. We summarize in Section
4
.

Throughout the paper we assume a flat cold dark matter cosmology with
H_{0}=70\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
,
\Omega_{m}=0.3
and
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.7
.

2
Data

The faint end of the infrared luminosity function was established via stacking the optical/near-IR catalogs of the UKIDSS Ultra Deep Survey (UDS) and COSMOS fields (
McLeod et al. 2021
), following
the methodology outlined in
Koprowski et al. 2024
, where galaxies’ stellar masses were used as a proxy for the IR luminosity. For the purpose of extracting the stacked FIR flux densities, we used the
Herschel

(
Pilbratt et al. 2010
)
Multi-tiered Extragalactic Survey (HerMES;
Oliver et al. 2012
) and the Photodetector Array Camera and Spectrometer (PACS;
Poglitsch et al. 2010
) Evolutionary Probe (PEP;
Lutz et al. 2011
) data obtained with the Spectral and Photometric Imaging Receiver (SPIRE;
Griffin et al. 2010
) and PACS instruments, where
Herschel
maps at 100, 160, 250, 350 and 500
{\rm\mu m}
were utilized. In order to constrain the Rayleigh–Jeans tail of the dust emission curve, we have also included the data collected as a part of the SCUBA-2 Cosmology Legacy Survey (S2CLS;
Geach et al. 2017
).

The bright end of the IR LF (Section
3.1.2
) was derived using a high-resolution ALMA follow-up data (AS2UDS;
Stach et al. 2019
;
Dudzevičiūtė
et al. 2020
), of the S2CLS UKIDSS UDS field
(
Geach et al. 2017
)
. The original SCUBA-2 map encompasses an area of 0.96 square degrees, with a noise level
<1.3
mJy and a median sensitivity of
\sigma_{850}=0.88
mJy beam
-1
, where 716 sources were detected. As explained in
Geach et al. 2017
, the SCUBA-2 source detection completeness for the UDS field was determined by the recovery rate of artificial sources injected into the jackknife maps as a function of input flux.

A detailed description of the ALMA observations, data reduction and the construction of the catalog can be found in
Stach et al. 2019
. In summary, all 716 SCUBA-2 sources were observed using ALMA Band 7 during Cycles 1, 3, 4, and 5. All the maps were tapered to 0.5 arcsec for source detection purposes, with the resulting AS2UDS catalog consisting of 708 individual ALMA-identified SMGs (
\sigma_{870}>4.3
). Based on 60,000 simulated ALMA observations, the sample was found to be complete at
S_{870}\gtrsim 4
mJy.

The associated photometric redshifts and far-IR luminosities for all AS2UDS sources were adopted from
Dudzevičiūtė
et al. 2020
. In short, the available photometry spans wavelength range from the
u
-band to radio, with low-resolution
Herschel
SPIRE data de-blended using procedure explained in
Swinbank et al. 2014
. Photometric redshifts were found using the updated
magphys
SED modelling code
(
da Cunha et al. 2015
;
Battisti et al. 2019
)
, with
Bruzual & Charlot 2003
stellar models,
Chabrier 2003
initial mass function and
Charlot & Fall 2000
dust attenuation model. The resulting photometric redshifts were tested against the available spectroscopic data, with the median offset, defined as
(z_{\rm spec}-z_{\rm phot})/(1+z_{\rm spec})
, equal to
-0.005\pm 0.003
with a dispersion of 0.13.

The IR luminosities were found by
Dudzevičiūtė
et al. 2020
using both
magphys
and the modified blackbody:

S\propto(1-e^{-\tau_{\rm rest}})\times B(\nu_{\rm rest},T),

(1)

with
B(\nu_{\rm rest},T)
being the Planck function and the optical depth defined as
\tau_{\rm rest}\equiv(\nu_{\rm rest}/\nu_{0})^{\beta}
, with the emissivity index
\beta
set to 1.8. As explained in
Dudzevičiūtė
et al. 2020
, the corresponding temperatures for both
magphys
and modified blackbody fits were found to be in a very good agreement with a typical fractional difference of
(T_{d}^{\rm MBB}-T_{d}^{\rm MAGPHYS})/T_{d}^{\rm MBB}=-0.28\pm 0.01
.

The IR luminosities for the galaxies constituting the faint-end portion of the luminosity function were extracted indirectly via stacking the optical/near-IR samples in the FIR
Herschel
and JCMT maps, using the stellar mass as proxies. The rationale behind it is that the star-forming galaxies’ IR luminosities are tightly correlated with their stellar masses via the so-called main sequence (MS; e.g.,
Speagle et al. 2014
;
Tomczak et al. 2016
;
Daddi et al. 2022
;
Popesso et al. 2023
;
Koprowski et al. 2024

Koprowski et al. 2024
and we adopt their results in this work (Figure
1

L_{\rm IR}
values listed in Table

\sim
1 billion years of Universe evolution, ranging from 1 to 9 billion years after the Big Bang (with the corresponding redshift bins of [0.45, 0.6, 0.75, 1.0, 1.2, 1.6, 2.2, 3.2, 5.7]).

As we are considering a star-forming galaxies sample here,
our assessment of the faint end of the IR luminosity function excludes, in effect, both quiescent and starburst galaxies (see
Koprowski et al. 2024
for details). This is warranted as passive sources are much less numerous than the active galaxies and exhibit significantly lower IR luminosities at given stellar mass, rendering their contribution to the resulting LF negligible. Starbursts, on the other hand, are much more luminous in the IR and their contribution to the LF is, therefore, assessed using the ALMA data, as explained in Section
3.1.2
.

-
M_{\ast}

3.1.2
Bright end

The bright end of the IR LF was determined using high-resolution ALMA follow-up data of all the S2CLS UKIDSS UDS sources – AS2UDS
(
Stach et al. 2019
;
Dudzevičiūtė
et al. 2020
)
. The main advantage of the ALMA data is the high resolution of the observations allowing for robust optical counterpart identification. Each source has two values of completeness associated with it – one from the original S2CLS survey
(
Geach et al. 2017
)
and one from the ALMA follow-up observations
(
Stach et al. 2019
)
. To make sure the data used here is flux-complete, we limit the ALMA sample to sources with fluxes
S_{870}>4
mJy, for which the completeness in the ALMA maps was established to be close to 100%. Since, as stated in
Stach et al. 2019
, for SCUBA-2 sources above
S_{850}\sim 3.5
mJy all the single-dish flux in ALMA maps has been on average recovered, we can treat ALMA
S_{870}>4
mJy sources as a flux-complete (
>80
% completeness in the original S2CLS SCUBA-2 UDS map of
Geach et al. 2017
) homogeneous sample.

\sim 30

{\rm log}(L_{\rm IR}/{\rm L_{\odot}})=12.5
at

and
<5.7
, respectively (Table

The IR luminosity function was found using
1/V_{\rm max}
method
(

z\ll 1
(e.g.,
Gruppioni et al. 2013

Koprowski et al. 2017
, where
\alpha
was found using the ALMA survey of Hubble Ultra Deep Field
(
Dunlop et al. 2017
)
at
z\simeq 2
. Here, we fitted the faint-end slope at two lowest redshift bins (
1.2\leq z<1.6
and
1.6\leq z<2.2
), finding

and
-0.23\pm 0.18
, respectively, and adopted a more accurate
1.2\leq z<1.6

-0.26
at all redshift bins.

4
Summary

We have probed the low-luminosity regime of the IR luminosity function out to
z=5.7
via stacking the optical/near-IR catalogs of
McLeod et al. 2021
in the FIR
Herschel
and JCMT maps, using stellar mass as a proxy, where the
L_{\rm IR}
-
M_{\ast}
relationship of
Koprowski et al. 2024
was used. Together with the ALMA follow-up data of the S2CLS UKIDSS UDS sources (AS2UDS;
Stach et al. 2019
;
Dudzevičiūtė
et al. 2020
), we have established the evolution of the functional form of IR LF between redshifts 1.2 and 5.7. We have fit the data at 4 redshift bins (Table
4
) with the Schechter function of Equation
6
and established a value of the faint-end slope and the redshift evolution of the characteristic luminosity,
L_{\ast}
, and the characteristic number density,
\Phi_{\ast}
. By integrating the best-fit functions, we have determined the values of the co-moving volume density of star formation,
\rho_{\rm SFR}
. The main results of this work can be summarized as follows:

(i)

The faint end of the IR luminosity function values of
\alpha=-0.26\pm 0.11
and
-0.23\pm 0.18
have been determined at two low-redshift bins of
1.2\leq z<1.6
and
1.6\leq z<2.2
, respectively and the more accurate
1.2\leq z<1.6
value of -0.26 adopted at all remaining redshift bins. The characteristic number density at
z\gtrsim 2
is a steeply declining function of redshift, where
\Phi_{\ast}\propto z^{-4.95}
, being very similar to the predicted evolution for dust-poor model of the early Universe investigated in
Casey et al. 2018
. The redshift evolution of the characteristic luminosity was found to be a power-law function, where
L_{\star}\propto z^{1.38}
, supporting the downsizing scenario
(
Thomas et al. 2010
)
, where most luminous galaxies form before their less luminous counterparts.

(ii)

When comparing the functional form of the IR LF with the results from the recent literature, we find our faint-end slope to be in agreement with most of other calibrations. At the bright end, we are predicting relatively small number densities of sources, which aligns with recent studies struggling to detect high-redshift galaxies in the FIR
(
Dunlop et al. 2017
;
Aravena et al. 2020
)
. A small number of previous studies find significantly more high-
L_{\rm IR}
sources which we attribute most likely to the low resolution of the FIR observations and/or the methodology adopted when dealing with inhomogeneous FIR samples.

(iii)

Finally, the redshift evolution of the star formation rate density,
\rho_{\rm SFR}
, is established by integrating the corresponding IR LFs at 4 redshift bins investigated in this work. The resulting
\rho_{\rm SFR}
is peaking at
z\simeq 2
and later declining with redshift from
z\sim 2
out to
z\sim 6
, with the high-redshift values significantly lower than those presented in
Madau & Dickinson 2014
. Our data is in a very good agreement with the recent works of
Dunlop et al. 2017
,
Wang et al. 2019
,
Magnelli et al. 2024
and
Liu et al. 2025
, but somewhat below the numbers derived in
Traina et al. 2024
;
Fujimoto et al. 2024
and
Sun et al. 2025
. The most striking differences are found between our results and those established by
Rowan-Robinson et al. 2016
and
Gruppioni et al. 2020
, where significantly larger number densities have been found at the bright end. Considering the two extreme cases of dust-rich and dust-poor early Universe presented in
Casey et al. 2018
, the
\rho_{\rm SFR}
redshift evolution found in this work places our findings in the dust-poor scenario, where we predict that the IR sources will dominate the total density of star formation in the Universe out to
z\sim 4
.
