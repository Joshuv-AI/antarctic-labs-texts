Median Arcuate Ligament Syndrome: A Hidden Cause of Abdominal Pain
Authors: Aayush Bist, Dhanlaxmi Giri, Niranjan Raman Puri, Surakshya Deep Raut, Maya Upadhyaya, Prabhat Kaphle, Prabesh Paudel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMedian arcuate ligament syndrome (MALS) is an uncommon condition where the median arcuate ligament compresses the celiac artery, leading to reduced blood flow. It commonly presents with upper abdominal pain after eating, unintentional weight loss, and an audible abdominal bruit. Since MALS is a diagnosis of exclusion, early identification and timely intervention are crucial in enhancing the patient's quality of life. A 43‐year‐old female presented with chronic postprandial epigastric pain, weight loss, and intermittent nausea for 3 months. The pain was described as dull and worsened after meals. MALS is a rare vascular compression syndrome that mimics other gastrointestinal disorders, often causing delayed diagnosis. Imaging techniques such as Doppler ultrasound, computed tomography angiography, and magnetic resonance angiography aid in diagnosis. Surgical decompression, primarily via laparoscopic median arcuate ligament release, is the preferred treatment and has shown significant symptom relief. MALS is a rare but treatable cause of chronic abdominal pain. A multidisciplinary approach and timely intervention are crucial for optimal patient outcomes.Keywords: abdominal ischemia, celiac artery compression, chronic abdominal pain, Dunbar syndrome, vascular compression syndrome

Introduction

Median arcuate ligament syndrome (MALS), also known as celiac artery compression syndrome, is a rare but often underdiagnosed condition that can significantly impact a patient's quality of life. It is estimated to affect approximately 2 per 100,000 individuals, with a higher prevalence in women aged 30–50 years [1]. The median arcuate ligament is an arch‐like tendinous fascial band that joins the diaphragmatic crura at the level of the L1 vertebra, traversing superior to the celiac axis and adjacent sympathetic nerve and ganglia. In approximately 10%–24% of the population, the fibrous arch of the median arcuate ligament crosses the aorta at a lower level, leading to compression of the celiac artery. This results in intermittent foregut ischemia and epigastric pain, manifesting as a triad of symptoms, including postprandial abdominal pain, nausea/vomiting, and unintentional weight loss [2, 3]. MALS predominantly affects younger individuals, particularly those with lower body mass, with a female‐to‐male ratio of 4:1 [4]. Beyond demographics, other risk factors include low body mass index (BMI), rapid weight loss which reduces the protective fat pad around the celiac artery, anatomical variations such as a low insertion of the median arcuate ligament, smoking, hypertension, and potentially coexisting conditions like gastroparesis or abdominal malignancies that may exacerbate symptoms or complicate treatment [4].

The pathophysiology of MALS involves both vascular and neuropathic mechanisms. Compression of the celiac artery can lead to ischemia of the foregut, while irritation of the celiac plexus contributes to the characteristic pain [5, 6]. Diagnosis is often delayed due to the nonspecific nature of symptoms, which mimic common gastrointestinal disorders such as peptic ulcer disease or irritable bowel syndrome [7]. Imaging modalities such as Doppler ultrasound or CT angiography are essential for confirmation [8].

Treatment primarily involves surgical intervention, with laparoscopic division of the median arcuate ligament emerging as the preferred approach due to its minimally invasive nature and favorable outcomes [5, 9]. However, in resource‐limited settings like Nepal, where advanced diagnostic tools and surgical expertise may be scarce, patients often experience prolonged suffering before receiving appropriate care.

This case report highlights the clinical journey of a 43‐year‐old female patient diagnosed with MALS. She had been experiencing persistent, severe abdominal pain after every meal, significantly disrupting her daily activities. Her case emphasizes the need for increased awareness and consideration of MALS in the differential diagnosis of chronic abdominal pain, particularly in patients with atypical presentations.

Case History/Examination

Patient Information

A 43‐year‐old female presented with chronic postprandial epigastric pain persisting for the past 3 months. The pain had an insidious onset, was nonlocalized, and described as a prickling sensation that radiated to the epigastric region. It occurred intermittently and worsened after meals, particularly with dairy products, while providing some relief with vomiting and defecation.

She also experienced intermittent vomiting, which was nonprojectile, nonbilious, and free of blood. Over this period, she noted an unintended weight loss of 2 kg. Her height was 160 cm, initial weight 50 kg (BMI 19.5 kg/m2), and postweight loss BMI was 18.7 kg/m2, consistent with a low BMI as a risk factor for MALS. No other significant risk factors, such as smoking or hypertension, were present. There was no history of gastrointestinal bleeding, jaundice, burning micturition or fever.

She neither smokes nor consumes alcohol. However, she was diagnosed with hypothyroidism 3 months ago and has been on medication since. This comorbidity was managed with levothyroxine and did not appear to influence her MALS symptoms directly. Her surgical history includes a lower segment cesarean section (LSCS) 22 years ago, laparoscopic cholecystectomy 3 years ago, and right ureteroscopic lithotripsy (URSL) 2 years ago. There was no significant family history of similar conditions.

Clinical Findings

On physical examination, the patient had mild epigastric tenderness. No palpable mass or organomegaly was detected, and there were normal bowel sounds with no evidence of jaundice, ascites, or lymphadenopathy.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

As part of the diagnostic workup, baseline blood tests including complete blood count, liver function tests, renal function tests, and thyroid function tests were performed and were within normal limits except for managed hypothyroidism. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (gastroscopy) was conducted to rule out peptic ulcer disease or gastritis, revealing no abnormalities. No hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) scan was performed due to prior cholecystectomy. Ultrasonography (USG) of the abdomen and pelvis was ordered, which initially appeared unremarkable on grayscale imaging but Doppler evaluation revealed elevated peak systolic velocity (PSV) in the celiac artery of approximately 250 cm/s during expiration, suggestive of compression, along with a deflection angle greater than 50 degrees.

This was followed by a contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CECT) (abdomen and pelvis) with abdominal angiogram, which demonstrated a hook‐like deformity with focal stenosis of the proximal segment of the celiac trunk, just distal to its point of origin (highlighted by the blue arrow) caused by the median arcuate ligament. This was associated with mild post stenotic dilatation (measuring 6.4 mm), superior indentation of the artery, and no evidence of intimal hyperplasia or wall thickening. However, there was no significant dilatation of the splenic or hepatic arteries (Figure 1).

Therapeutic Intervention

Informed consent for the surgical procedure was obtained after a detailed discussion with the patient and her family, explaining the risks (including bleeding, infection, and potential need for conversion to open surgery), benefits (symptom relief), alternatives (conservative management or open surgery), and postoperative expectations. The consent process was documented in writing, with the patient acknowledging understanding and agreeing voluntarily.

Laparoscopic release of compression bands and division of median arcuate ligament was planned. The patient was placed in the supine position under general anesthesia. Pneumoperitoneum was established using a Veress needle at the umbilicus, with insufflation to 12 mmHg. Standard 4 ports (one 10‐mm umbilical port for the camera, one 10‐mm epigastric port for liver retraction, and two 5‐mm ports in the right and left upper quadrants for instrumentation) were used; with the Veress needle technique, the liver was retracted to provide better access. Both the left gastric artery and the celiac artery were identified. Multiple hook‐like fibrotic bands of the median arcuate ligament were observed compressing the proximal celiac trunk. These fibrotic bands were carefully excised using harmonic scalpel and scissors, ensuring complete neurolysis of the celiac ganglion by removing surrounding fibrous tissue (Figure 3). The median arcuate ligament surrounding the celiac artery was divided to relieve the compression. Hemostasis was achieved, and no drain was placed. The postoperative course was uneventful. The procedure lasted approximately 90 min, and the patient was discharged on postoperative Day 2 with no complications. Length of hospital stay was 3 days including preoperative preparation.

Intraoperative view after complete division of the median arcuate ligament and celiac neurolysis.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Upon 1 week and 1 month follow up, the patient had no significant issue. She is doing well and the wound site is healthy. Further follow‐up at 3 months confirmed sustained symptom resolution, with weight gain and improved quality of life.

Discussion

Median MALS presents a significant diagnostic challenge due to its nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms, often mimicking peptic ulcer disease, functional dyspepsia, or other common abdominal complaints [10]. Our patient, a 43‐year‐old woman, experienced postprandial pain and unintended weight loss for several months before a correct diagnosis of MALS was established. This is consistent with literature that highlights the prevalence of MALS in younger females, with a female‐to‐male ratio of 4:1 [3].

The pathophysiology of MALS is multifactorial, involving both vascular and neurogenic components. Chronic compression of the celiac artery by the median arcuate ligament results in foregut ischemia, while concurrent irritation of the celiac plexus contributes to pain [3, 6]. This dual mechanism can lead to variable clinical presentations; in cases where neuropathic pain predominates, symptoms may persist postdecompression, necessitating adjunct therapies.

These mechanisms explain why some patients benefit from decompression surgery, while others continue to experience symptoms due to persistent neuropathic pain. In our case, the patient reported classic postprandial pain—a hallmark of ischemia—that completely resolved following laparoscopic decompression, indicating that ischemia was the dominant factor.

Symptom resolution following surgical intervention has been reported in over 80% of cases, particularly when ischemia is the primary driver [9]. However, persistent pain in a subset of patients suggests a role for adjunctive therapies such as celiac plexus blockade [3, 6]. Fortunately, our patient had no recurrence of symptoms postoperatively. Imaging plays a vital role in diagnosing MALS. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) in our case revealed celiac artery compression with poststenotic dilation—typical findings in MALS [1]. Expiratory phase imaging can enhance diagnostic accuracy, as diaphragm displacement during expiration increases arterial compression [1]. While CTA is widely accepted, Doppler USG also serves as a practical early diagnostic tool, especially in resource‐limited settings [5].

Laparoscopic decompression is now considered the standard of care due to its minimally invasive nature and high success rate. In our patient, the procedure led to rapid symptom resolution and an uneventful recovery, consistent with larger case series showing favorable outcomes [4, 9]. Open vascular reconstruction remains an option in cases with persistent arterial stenosis, although it carries increased morbidity [11].

In regions like Nepal, where advanced imaging and laparoscopic expertise may be limited, diagnosis and treatment are often delayed. Nevertheless, reports from similar low‐resource settings affirm the success of laparoscopic decompression when performed [12]. The experience in Nepal is unique due to socioeconomic and infrastructural challenges, often resulting in patients undergoing extensive workups for more common conditions before considering MALS. This case aligns with a prior Nepali report [12], demonstrating that with growing laparoscopic capabilities in urban hospitals, outcomes can match international standards despite delays.

Coexisting conditions can further complicate the diagnostic process. Kuruvilla et al. [10] reported a case misdiagnosed as chronic gastritis, highlighting how MALS can be overlooked in favor of more common diagnoses, influencing our approach by emphasizing the need for vascular imaging in refractory abdominal pain cases. While Sturiale et al. [13] described a missed diagnosis in a patient with Crohn's disease, highlighting the importance of considering MALS in patients with coexisting gastrointestinal disorders. Although our patient had a history of hypothyroidism and abdominal surgeries, these did not confound the diagnosis. Her complete postoperative recovery highlights the potential for excellent outcomes with timely intervention. However, recurrences, although rare, have been reported and may reflect predominant neuropathic mechanisms [14].

The absence of universally accepted diagnostic criteria for MALS contributes to variability in surgical selection. Increased awareness and better diagnostic tools are essential to shorten the time between symptom onset and definitive treatment. In resource‐constrained settings, strengthening access to Doppler USG and laparoscopic services could improve outcomes for MALS patients. As our case demonstrates, prompt diagnosis and intervention can be life‐changing, restoring quality of life in patients who often suffer silently for months.

Being a single case with limited follow‐up, the study cannot generalize the findings to long‐term outcomes.

Conclusion

MALS is a rare but treatable cause of chronic abdominal pain. Early diagnosis and laparoscopic surgery provide significant relief. Timely intervention is crucial to prevent long‐term complications and improve the patient's quality of life.
