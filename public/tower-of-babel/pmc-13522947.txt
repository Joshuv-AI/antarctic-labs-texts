Structure–activity relationship study on the antibacterial properties of B-ring brominated 1,3-dithiolium flavonoids
Authors: Lucian G. Bahrin, Irina Rosca, Isabela A. Sandu, Peter G. Jones, Laura G. Sarbu, Mihail L. Birsa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The synthesis of twelve new tricyclic flavonoids, with a bromine substituent at the ortho, meta or para position of the B ring, from the corresponding flavanones is described. Four structures were unambiguously proven by X-ray analysis. An SAR study of the antibacterial properties against Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Enterococcus faecalis, and Klebsiella pneumoniae was performed and revealed compound- and strain-dependent activity, with 5h showing the most promising broad-spectrum antibacterial profile, while 5f, 5i and 5j also exhibited strong activity against selected strains. Cytotoxicity assays showed high cell viability (>90%) at 0.001 mg mL−1 for all compounds, indicating dose-dependent cytotoxicity. These findings highlight 5h as a promising candidate for further investigation.

Introduction

The widespread use of antibiotics in healthcare and agriculture has resulted in one of the most significant challenges confronting contemporary medicine: bacteria that are resistant to multiple drugs. Resistance develops through several mechanisms, such as cell wall non-permeability, mutations at target sites, inactivation of antibiotics and the emergence of efflux pumps that expel the drugs from the cell.1,2 An estimated 4.95 million casualties in 2019 have been connected to antimicrobial resistance (AMR),3 which the World Health Organization (WHO) considers to be a major threat to public health and progress. The AMR crisis is worsened by the slow progress in developing new antibiotics, primarily because of regulatory and economic challenges.4 A recent WHO report revealed that since 2017, only 13 new antibiotics have been authorized, with vaborbactam and lefamulin being the only ones with a new chemical structure.5 It is evident that there is an urgent need for the identification of new classes of antibiotics.6

Flavonoids represent a varied group of secondary metabolites in plants, all having the identical C6–C3–C6 framework. Their significant structural variety results in a broad spectrum of biological functions. Flavonoids may exhibit antibacterial, antiviral, antifungal, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer effects.7–12 Their polyphenolic composition also furnishes them with outstanding antioxidant and cardio protective benefits.13,14

A few years ago, our research team discovered the antibacterial characteristics of a novel category of synthetic tricyclic flavonoids.15 Based on the encouraging outcomes that we achieved for the compounds depicted in Fig. 1, we decided to conduct a structure–activity relationship analysis for related compounds. To accomplish this, we chose to investigate the influence of a bromine substituent, either in the ortho, meta or para position, at the aromatic rings B, while varying the type of substituents at ring A. Having followed this strategy, we present here the antibacterial results obtained against Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Enterococcus faecalis, and Klebsiella pneumoniae, together with an investigation of the cytotoxic properties of the newly synthesised tricyclic flavonoids.

Results and discussion

As noted earlier, flavanone derivatives of type 4 (refer to Scheme 1) can be produced by reacting phenacyl dithiocarbamates with aminals 3.16 We intended to broaden our research by including additional novel tricyclic flavonoids that are structurally analogous to those shown in Fig. 1. Thus, by reacting 2-bromo-1-(5-chloro-2-hydroxyphenyl)ethan-1-one, 2-bromo-1-(5-bromo-2-hydroxyphenyl)ethan-1-one, 2-bromo-1-(2-hydroxy-5-methylphenyl)ethan-1-one and 2-bromo-1-(3-bromo-2-hydroxy-5-methylphenyl)ethan-1-one (1) with sodium N,N-diethyldithiocarbamate in acetone,17 phenacyl carbodithioates 2 have been obtained in good yields (75–85%). 3-Dithiocarbamic flavanones 4a–l have been obtained through the reaction of five phenacyl N,N-diethylaminocarbodithioate of type 2 with aminals 3 derived from ortho-, meta- and para-bromo substituted benzaldehydes (Scheme 1). The synthesis of phenacyl dithiocarbamates 2 had been previously reported by us following an established method described in the literature.18 NMR spectroscopy of the products confirmed the formation of the benzopyran ring. 1H NMR spectra showed the disappearance of the methylene signals from dithiocarbamates 2 (ca. 4.8–5.0 ppm); instead, two new signals of vicinal hydrogen atoms at the C-2 and C-3 positions of the benzopyran core for both diastereoisomers were observed between 5.84 and 6.29 ppm. Mass spectra also provided evidence for the cyclocondensation to the benzopyran ring. The synthesis and spectral data of the 3-dithiocarbamic flavanones 4i,194j,204k,21 and 4l,22para-Br substituted at the B ring, have been previously reported by us.

2-Dialkylamino-1,3-dithiolium-2-yl cations can be readily prepared from phenacyl dithiocarbamates.23,24 These compounds undergo cyclocondensation to yield the respective 1,3-dithiolium cations under acidic conditions.25 Spectral data has confirmed the cyclocondensation of 3-dithiocarbamic flavanones 4 into tricyclic flavonoids 5. IR spectroscopy revealed the loss of the carbonyl absorption bands (1648–1698 cm−1) along with the presence of the distinctive broad absorption bands (approximately 1075–1090 cm−1) of the tetrafluoroborate ion. The 1H NMR spectra of tricyclic flavonoids 5 showed the absence of the C-3 hydrogens in the benzopyran structure. Additionally, the signals for the C-2 hydrogens were displaced by as much as 7.1 ppm and showed up as singlets. The signals from the carbonyl and thiocarbonyl atoms in the 13C NMR spectra are no longer present, and a new signal appears at approximately 185 ppm associated with the C-2 carbon atom of the 1,3-dithiol-2-ylium ring is detected.

Antibacterial activity of tricyclic flavonoids

Our earlier research indicated that the primary mode of action of sulfur-containing tricyclic flavonoids with halogen substituents at the benzopyran ring consists in the disruption of membrane integrity and the lysis of bacterial cells.26 The outstanding antibacterial characteristics of the evaluated tricyclic flavonoids, shown in Table 2, can be attributed to the interaction between nucleophilic groups on the cell wall and/or membrane components (e.g., peptides, modified polysaccharides) with the electron poor C-2 atom of the 1,3-dithiolium ring, as demonstrated previously.27 Furthermore, the establishment of a strong ion pair seems to play a crucial role in how cationic tricyclic flavonoids engage with bacteria. At physiological pH, the outer layer of the bacterial cell wall is negatively charged. Consequently, the positively charged 1,3-dithiolium flavonoids interact with the negatively charged molecules from the cell wall/membrane. The reduced antibacterial activity observed against Gram-negative bacteria could be attributed to their distinct cell wall composition. The presence of an outer membrane in the cell wall provides Gram-negative bacteria with improved defense against antimicrobials.28

The antimicrobial data presented in Table 2 demonstrate that the investigated tricyclic flavonoids 5a–5l exhibited substantial and, in several cases, broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive (Staphylococcus aureus and Enterococcus faecalis) and Gram-negative (Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae) strains. However, the antimicrobial potency was strongly dependent on both the chemical structure of the flavonoid and the bacterial strain. Among the tested derivatives, compounds 5h and 5i showed particularly pronounced activity, displaying low MIC and MBC values against all four bacterial strains. Compound 5h was especially active, with MIC values of 0.002, 0.011, 0.011 and 0.005 mg mL−1 against S. aureus, E. coli, E. faecalis and K. pneumoniae, respectively. Overall, S. aureus and E. coli were generally the most susceptible strains, whereas K. pneumoniae exhibited higher MIC values for most compounds. Nevertheless, the activity pattern was not strictly associated with the Gram classification, indicating that the antimicrobial response was primarily compound- and strain-dependent.

The comparison of MIC and MBC values further indicates predominantly bactericidal activity of the investigated compounds. For most compound-strain combinations, the MBC/MIC ratio was ≤4, supporting a bactericidal rather than merely bacteriostatic effect. Particularly noteworthy is compound 5h, for which the MBC/MIC ratios ranged from 1 to 2 across all four tested strains, indicating a consistent bactericidal effect. Similar activity was observed for several other derivatives, although higher MBC/MIC ratios were recorded for selected combinations, particularly against E. faecalis and K. pneumoniae. These differences suggest that the structural features of the flavonoids may influence not only bacterial growth inhibition but also their ability to achieve bactericidal effects. Bactericidal agents typically exert their effect through mechanisms that irreversibly damage the bacterial structure, such as: inhibition of cell wall synthesis, disruption of cell integrity, or interference with DNA replication, ultimately leading to bacterial cell death.

Interestingly, the present results provide evidence that the antibacterial activity of tricyclic flavonoids 5 is not restricted to Gram-positive bacteria. For several derivatives, comparable or even greater activity was observed against the Gram-negative strains. For example, compound 5a exhibited a lower MIC against E. coli (0.005 mg mL−1) than against S. aureus (0.023 mg mL−1), while compounds 5h and 5i displayed very strong activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Moreover, compounds 5b, 5d, 5e, 5f, 5g and 5l showed comparable MIC values between at least one Gram-positive and one Gram-negative strain. These findings indicate that the antibacterial activity cannot be predicted solely on the basis of Gram classification and highlight the importance of the substitution pattern and physicochemical properties of the flavonoid scaffold. The pronounced activity against K. pneumoniae observed for compounds 5h and 5i is particularly relevant, given the generally lower susceptibility of this strain to the other derivatives. Overall, these results support further investigation of the structure–activity relationships within this class of tricyclic flavonoids and suggest that their antibacterial effects may involve mechanisms capable of acting on bacterial targets shared by both Gram-positive and Gram-negative microorganisms.

Compared with levofloxacin, used as the reference antibiotic, the investigated flavonoids showed variable antibacterial activity. Notably, several compounds, particularly 5h, 5i, 5j and 5l, exhibited lower MIC values than levofloxacin against S. aureus, while 5h showed comparable or superior activity against E. coli, E. faecalis and K. pneumoniae. These results highlight the strong and broad-spectrum antibacterial potential of selected tricyclic flavonoids compared with the reference antibiotic.

Cytotoxicity of tricyclic flavonoids

In vitro cytotoxicity tests on compounds 5a–l were conducted using the CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (MTS assay), wherein a tetrazolium reagent is reduced by metabolically active cells to yield formazan, a product directly soluble in the culture medium, as detailed in the referenced protocol. Such indirect methodologies underscore the assay's dependence on enzymatic activity, precluding direct enumeration of viable cells. In antibiotic development, low cytotoxicity (viability >70 at relevant doses) is ideal to balance antibacterial efficacy with host cell safety. As shown in Fig. 7, all tested compounds maintain high viability (>90%) at low concentrations. They show steep drops in viability at higher doses (0.010 mg mL−1), indicating dose-dependent cytotoxicity. Among the investigated derivatives, 5h emerged as the most promising broad-spectrum candidate, exhibiting particularly low MIC and MBC values against all four tested strains, S. aureus, E. coli, E. faecalis and K. pneumoniae. Compounds 5i and 5j also showed pronounced antibacterial activity, particularly against S. aureus and E. coli, while 5f displayed strong activity against S. aureus and E. faecalis. Overall, these compounds represent promising candidates for further investigation due to their potent inhibitory and bactericidal effects across both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria.

Conclusion

We report here the synthesis of a series of tricyclic flavonoids, with a bromine substituent at the ortho, meta or para position of the B ring, from the corresponding flavanones. The structures of three flavanones and one tricyclic flavonoid were unambiguously proven by X-ray analysis A SAR analysis against S. aureus, E. coli, E. faecalis and K. pneumoniae, using levofloxacin as the reference, revealed a pronounced compound- and strain-dependent antibacterial activity. Among the investigated derivatives, 5h showed the most promising broad-spectrum activity, with particularly low MIC and MBC values against all four strains, while 5i, 5f and 5j also displayed strong activity against selected strains. These results indicate that the antibacterial potency is not strictly related to the Gram classification or a single substitution pattern, but depends on the overall molecular structure. Considering the cytotoxicity results together with the antimicrobial activity, 5h represents the most promising candidate, while 5f and 5j also showed favourable profiles, supporting their further investigation as potential antibacterial agents.

General procedure for 2-N,N-diethylamino-8-chloro-4-(2′-bromophenyl)-4H-1,3-dithiol[4,5-c]chromen-2-ylium tetrafluoroborate (5a)

To a mixture of sulfuric acid (1 mL) and acetic acid (1 mL), flavanone 4a (0.24 g, 0.5 mmol) was added and the resulting solution was heated to 70 °C for 30 min. The reaction mixture was then left to cool to room temperature and a solution of sodium tetrafluoroborate (0.3 g) in water (10 mL) was added dropwise, under vigorous stirring. Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of the samples and control represented by levofloxacin (LEV), was determined by using four reference microbial strains: Staphylococcus aureus ATCC25923, Escherichia coli ATCC25922, Enterococcus faecalis ATCC29212, and Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC10031. The microorganisms were stored at −80 °C in 20% glycerol and were refreshed on nutrient agar (NA) at 37 °C for 24 hours. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined by broth dilution method performed in 96-well microtiter plates. Bacterial culture grown was adjusted to 1 × 108 cells per ml in Mueller - Hinton (MH) Broth. Inoculants of 50 µL were mixed with 50 µL of serial dilutions of samples and levofloxacin as control (0.001–3 mg mL−1) and were subsequently incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. MIC was determined on the basis of turbidity by a FLUOstar® Omega microplate reader (BMG LABTECH, Ortenberg, Germany). The minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) was determined by spreading of 10 µL samples from wells on Plate Count Agar (PCA). The concentration of sample that prevented the growth of bacteria was recognized as MIC, and the lowest concentration that destroyed all the microbial cells was considered as MBC. In order to determine MIC/MBC values, the experiments were performed in triplicate. The bactericidal or bacteriostatic activity of the antibacterial agents was determined by calculating the MBC/MIC ratio. Compounds with an MBC/MIC ratio ≤ 4 were considered bactericidal, whereas those with a ratio > 4 were classified as bacteriostatic.

Cytotoxicity assay

We conducted in vitro cytotoxicity tests on compounds 5a–l using human dermal fibroblasts (HDF-Ad from Innoprot, Bizkaia, Spain). Cells were passaged at 80% confluence to prevent contact inhibition and maintained in cell culture-treated dishes with complete α-MEM Eagle medium (10% fetal bovine serum, 1% antibiotic-antimycotic) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere. TrypLE™ Express was used for passaging. Stock solutions of compounds 5a–l were prepared in DMSO and diluted 100-fold in culture medium, resulting in 1% final DMSO concentration in the plates. The control group also included 1% DMSO. Cytotoxicity was assessed via the CellTiter 96® Aqueous One Solution (MTS) assay using the manufacturer instructions. Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at 5 × 103 cells/well in 100 µL α-MEM and incubated overnight; after 24 hours, medium was replaced with test solutions at varying concentrations for 48 hours. Then, 20 µL MTS was added to each well, incubated for 3 hours, followed by absorbance measurement at 490 nm using a microplate reader (FLUOstar® Omega microplate reader from BMG LABTECH, Ortenberg, Germany). Viability was calculated as a percentage of control and graphed as means ± SD.
