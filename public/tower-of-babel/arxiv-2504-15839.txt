1.
Introduction

Given a finite group
G
, a classical topic in group theory concerns counting the number of
commuting pairs
in
G
, that is, pairs
(g_{1},g_{2})\in G\times G
such that
g_{1}g_{2}=g_{2}g_{1}
.
Following the early work of Erdős–Turán
[
6
]
, many results have been established on estimating numbers of commuting pairs in families of groups. For example, Gustafson
[
9
]
proved that any finite, non-abelian group
G
can have at most
5|G|^{2}/8
many commuting pairs. Neumann
[
18
]
further characterised all finite groups
G
which exhibit at least
\delta|G|^{2}
many commuting pairs, for some fixed
\delta>0
.

This question has also been analysed in the case when
G
is replaced by a suitable set
\mathscr{A}
of
d\times d
matrices. For instance, for all
d\geqslant 2
, Feit–Fine
[
7
]
obtained an explicit formula and a generating function for the number of pairs of commuting
d\times d
matrices whose entries lie in an arbitrary finite field. More recently, for all
d\geqslant 3
, Browning–Sawin–Wang
[
4
]
investigated the setting when
\mathscr{A}={\rm Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{Z},N)
, that is, the set of all
d\times d
matrices with entries in
[-N,N]\cap\mathbb{Z}
.
Soon after, the second author
[
17
]
studied commuting pairs of
2\times 2
matrices whose entries lie in an arbitrary, finite, non-empty set of real numbers. This topic has been widely-studied from an algebraic perspective as well, see for example
[
3
,
16
]
.

In this paper, we continue the inquiry initiated in
[
4
]
by establishing almost optimal estimates when
d\in\{2,3\}
and
\mathscr{A}={\rm Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{Z},N)
. Thus, we define

\mathfrak{C}_{d}(N)=|\{(A,B)\in\mathrm{Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{Z},N):AB=BA\}|.

Since any scalar multiple of the
d\times d
identity matrix commutes with every other
d\times d
matrix, one finds that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{d}(N)

\displaystyle\geqslant 2|\mathrm{Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{Z},N)|\cdot(2N+1)(1-O_{d}(N^{-1}))

\displaystyle=2(2N+1)^{d^{2}+1}(1-O_{d}(N^{-1}))\gg_{d}N^{d^{2}+1}.

(1.1)

As for upper bounds, a standard application of the divisor function estimate furnishes the bound

\mathfrak{C}_{2}(N)\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{5+\varepsilon}

(1.2)

for all
\varepsilon>0
, matching (
1.1
) up to the
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{\varepsilon})
factor when
d=2
. In the case when
d\geqslant 3
, one may combine a result of Basili
[
3
]
on the dimension of the commuting variety along with some standard Schwartz–Zippel type arguments to prove that

\mathfrak{C}_{d}(N)\ll_{d}N^{d^{2}+d}.

Replacing the latter with work of Salberger
[
19
]
, we get that
\mathfrak{C}_{d}(N)\ll_{d,\varepsilon}N^{d^{2}+d-1+\varepsilon}
for every
\varepsilon>0
. Browning–Sawin–Wang
[
4
]
further strengthened this by proving that

\mathfrak{C}_{d}(N)\ll_{d}N^{d^{2}+2-\frac{2}{d+1}}

(1.3)

for all
d\geqslant 3
. In particular, when
d=3
, their bound implies that

\mathfrak{C}_{3}(N)\ll N^{10+1/2}.

Browning–Sawin–Wang speculated that (
1.1
) should be the correct order of magnitude for
\mathfrak{C}_{d}(N)
. Our first result confirms their prediction when
d=3
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every positive integer
N
, we have

N^{10}\ll\mathfrak{C}_{3}(N)\ll N^{10}.

We now turn to the case when
d=2
wherein we have (
1.2
). Moreover, using
[
17
, Corollary
1.3
]
, one can remove the
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{\varepsilon})
factor and show that
\mathfrak{C}_{2}(N)\ll N^{5}
.
Our second result strengthens these results by establishing an asymptotic formula for the number of commuting pairs of
2\times 2
integer matrices.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every positive integer
N>1
, we have

\mathfrak{C}_{2}(N)=K(2N)^{5}+O(N^{4}\log N),

(1.4)

where

K=\frac{10\zeta(2)}{3\zeta(3)}\approx 4.56144\ldots\,.

A notable aspect of our estimates is that we are able to circumvent logarithmic losses. In fact, a naive application of the circle–method heuristic, which interprets the leading term as arising from the product of ‘local densities’, would suggest the presence of some logarithmic factors in the main term in Theorem
1.2
.
To elaborate on this, for every prime
p
and positive integer
n
, we define
\mathrm{Mat}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z})
to be the set of all
2\times 2
matrices with entries lying in
\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
and set

\mathfrak{C}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z})=|\{(A,B)\in{\rm Mat}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z})^{2}:AB=BA\}|.

We prove the following asymptotic formula for
\mathfrak{C}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z})
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For any prime
p
and positive integer
n
, one has

\frac{\mathfrak{C}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z})}{p^{6n}}=\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)^{-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{3}}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2\left\lceil n/2\right\rceil}}\right)+O(n^{2}p^{-n/2}).

In particular, the limit

\sigma_{p}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathfrak{C}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z})}{p^{6n}}=\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)^{-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{3}}\right)

exists.

The divergent product
\prod_{p}(1+p^{-1})
appearing in
\prod_{p}\sigma_{p}
suggests a logarithmic factor should be present in the main term in (
1.4
). This discrepancy can be explained in various ways, for instance, one would expect the circle method heuristic to hold when one expects that the solutions contributing to the main term in (
1.4
) are somewhat equidistributed modulo every prime
p
. This does not hold in our case since a significant contribution to the main term in (
1.4
) arises from the case when one of the matrices is a scalar multiple of the identity matrix while the other matrix is arbitrary. In geometric terms, the failure of the circle method heuristic is a consequence of the fact that the variety defined by the matrix equation
AB=BA
is not a complete intersection.

Given the estimates provided by Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
as well as the lower bound in (
1.1
), it is natural to wonder whether one should expect to have

\mathfrak{C}_{d}(N)-2(2N+1)^{d^{2}+1}\gg_{d}N^{d^{2}+1}

when
d\geqslant 3
or whether there is a paucity of non-trivial solutions. A relatively straightforward computation shows that the former is indeed the case.

Lemma 1.4
.

For every
d\geqslant 2
, one has

\mathfrak{C}_{d}(N)\geqslant\bigg(2+\frac{2}{d+1}-O_{d}(N^{-1})\bigg)(2N+1)^{d^{2}+1}.

In contrast to the work of Browning–Sawin–Wang
[
4
]
, which involved a variety of matrix identities, ideas from harmonic analysis and estimates concerning exponential sums in finite fields, and the work of the second author
[
17
]
, which employed ideas relating to sum-product estimates, growth in groups phenomenon and incidence geometry, our techniques are purely elementary. Perhaps somewhat naturally, an important ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.1
involves studying higher moments of restricted divisor estimates. To elucidate this further, we define, for every
h\in\mathbb{Z}
, the function

r(h)=r_{N}(h):=\sum_{-N\leqslant a_{1},\dots,a_{4}\leqslant N}\mathds{1}_{a_{1}a_{2}-a_{3}a_{4}=h}.

We suppress the dependence of
r
on
N
in the notation
r(h)
since the choice of
N
will always be clear depending on the context. For any
k\geqslant 2
, we set

I_{k}(N)=\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}r(h)^{k}.

Observe that
I_{k}(N)
denotes the number of integer solutions to the system of equations

x_{1}x_{2}-x_{3}x_{4}=\dots=x_{4k-3}x_{4k-2}-x_{4k-1}x_{4k}

with
|x_{i}|\leqslant N
for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant 4k
. Thus,
I_{k}(N)
can be interpreted as the number of
k
-tuples
(A_{1},\dots,A_{k})\in{\rm Mat}_{2}(\mathbb{Z},N)^{k}
such that
\det(A_{1})=\dots=\det(A_{k})
.

As part of our proof of Theorem
1.1
, we derive the following pointwise estimates for the function
r
and its higher moments.

Lemma 1.5
.

For any
0<|h|\leqslant 2N^{2}
, we have

r(h)\ll N^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h,\\
1\leqslant d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{d}\ \ \text{and}\ \ r(0)\ll N^{2}\log N.

(1.5)

Moreover, for any integer
k\geqslant 2
, we have

I_{k}(N)\ll_{k}N^{2k+2}.

(1.6)

The estimates in (
1.5
) are sharp up to the implicit constant in the Vinogradov notation for many values of
h
, as can be seen by comparing these upper bounds with the asymptotic formulae present in
[
1
,
5
,
8
]
. Moreover, applying Hölder’s inequality, we see that

I_{k}(N)=\sum_{|h|\leqslant 2N^{2}}r(h)^{k}\geqslant\frac{(\sum_{|h|\leqslant 2N^{2}}r(h))^{k}}{(2N^{2}+1)^{k-1}}=\frac{(2N+1)^{4k}}{(2N^{2}+1)^{k-1}}\gg_{k}N^{2k+2},

whence (
1.6
) is also sharp up to the implicit constant.

There is a substantial body of research on
r(h)
along with its many variants and generalisations. For instance, when
h
is fixed and
d\geqslant 2
, work of Duke–Rudnick–Sarnak
[
5
]
provides an asymptotic formula for all matrices
A\in{\rm Mat}_{d}(N,\mathbb{Z})
such that
\det(A)=h
. One particularly well-studied variant of
r(h)
is the classical divisor correlation

D_{X}(h):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},\dots,a_{4}\in\mathbb{N}\\
a_{1}a_{2},a_{3}a_{4}\leqslant X\end{subarray}}\mathds{1}_{a_{1}a_{2}-a_{3}a_{4}=h}=\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant X}\tau(n)\tau(n+h),

where
\tau
is the divisor function. Obtaining an asymptotic formula for
D_{X}(h)
, with strong error terms and a large range of uniformity in
h
with respect to
X
, has been an important topic of study in analytic number theory, in part due its connections to the fourth moment of the Riemann Zeta function. For details on the rich history of this problem, see
[
10
,
14
,
15
]
and the references therein.

While trivially one has

r_{N}(h)\ll D_{N^{2}}(h),

it is known that for
|h|\ll N^{5/3}
, the bound
D_{N^{2}}(h)\gg c_{h}N^{2}(\log N)^{2}
holds for some
c_{h}\gg 1
(see
[
10
]
). Thus, even the sharpest estimates for
D_{N^{2}}(h)
are unfortunately not strong enough to recover (
1.5
). It is worth mentioning that asymptotic formulae for
r(h)
have been proven recently in
[
8
]
and
[
1
]
, but these only hold when
|h|\ll N^{1/3}
and
|h|\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{2-\varepsilon}
for all
\varepsilon>0
respectively. On the other hand, while variants of
r(h)
that incorporate smooth weights supported on dyadic intervals seem to be more amenable to analysis, with asymptotic formulae for such objects being known to hold even when
|h|\leqslant 2N^{2}
, see
[
8
, Theorem 1.3]
and the ensuing discussion therein, these exhibit slightly different behaviour compared to
r(h)
and thus do not seem to suffice for our analysis. Although it is plausible that (
1.5
) can be recovered via the aforementioned methods, for completeness, we provide a short, elementary proof of Lemma
1.5
in §3.

We conclude this section by mentioning the case when
[-N,N]\cap\mathbb{Z}
is replaced by an arbitrary additively structured set
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}
. Thus, defining the sumset
\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}
and the doubling
{\rm K}
as

\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}=\{a+b:a,b\in\mathcal{A}\}\ \ \text{and}\ \ {\rm K}=|\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}|/|\mathcal{A}|,

one can consider the number
\mathfrak{C}_{d}(\mathcal{A})
of pairs of commuting
d\times d
matrices such that the entries of these matrices arise from
\mathcal{A}
. In
[
17
]
, it was shown, amongst other estimates, that

{\rm K}^{-1}|\mathcal{A}|^{5}\ll\mathfrak{C}_{2}(\mathcal{A})\ll|\mathcal{A}|^{5}{\rm K}^{4/3}.

(1.7)

Note that when
\mathcal{A}=[-N,N]\cap\mathbb{Z}
, we have
1\leqslant{\rm K}\leqslant 2
, and so, this recovers an upper bound of the right order in this setting. Combining the techniques involved in the proof of Theorem
1.1
along with ideas from
[
17
,
20
]
, we are able to prove a corresponding estimate when
d=3
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}
be a finite, non-empty set such that
|\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}|/|\mathcal{A}|={\rm K}
for some
{\rm K}\geqslant 1
. Then

{\rm K}^{-3}|\mathcal{A}|^{10}\ll\mathfrak{C}_{3}(\mathcal{A})\ll{\rm K}^{6}|\mathcal{A}|^{10}(\log(2|\mathcal{A}|))^{3}

As in (
1.7
), the above is optimal up to the
O({\rm K}^{6}(\log(2|\mathcal{A}|))^{3})
factor. Moreover, this recovers the estimates in Theorem
1.1
up to the
O((\log 2N)^{3})
factor. Our proof of Theorem
1.6
essentially follows the proof of Theorem
1.1
, with restricted divisor correlations being estimated in this setting via work of Solymosi
[
20
]
on the sum-product conjecture; see Lemma
6.1
.

Organisation

In §
2
, we perform an analysis of
2\times 2
commuting matrices, thus proving Theorem
1.2
. In §
3

1.5
. We employ these along with various elementary arguments to prove Theorem
1.1
in §
4
. In §

2\times 2

\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}

1.3
. Finally, we dedicate §

1.5

1.6

Notation

We employ Vinogradov notation, that is, we write
Y\ll_{z}X
, or equivalently
Y=O_{z}(X)
, to mean that
|Y|\leqslant C_{z}X
, where
C_{z}>0
is some constant depending on the parameter
z
. For any non-empty set
X
and any positive integer
k
, we write
X^{k}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}):x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in X\}
. Given
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we define the vector
\lambda\cdot(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=(\lambda x_{1},\dots,\lambda x_{k})
. We usually denote the greatest common divisor of two integers
a
and
b
by
\gcd(a,b)
. We may also write
(a,b)
in place of
\gcd(a,b)
when it is clear from the context that we are examining the greatest common divisor rather than a vector with two entries. Given a property
\mathbf{P}
, we define
\mathds{1}_{\mathbf{P}}
to be
1
if
\mathbf{P}
holds and
0
otherwise. For example, given variables
x
and
y
, we have
\mathds{1}_{x=y}=1
if and only if
x=y
.

2.
Commuting pairs of
2\times 2
matrices

The purpose of this section is to prove Theorem
1.2
and establish an asymptotic formula for
\mathfrak{C}_{2}(N)
. Given
(A,B)\in{\rm Mat}_{2}(\mathbb{Z},N)^{2}
, we write

A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}\\
a_{3}&a_{4}\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}\\
b_{3}&b_{4}\end{pmatrix}.

Since the trace of
AB-BA
is always zero, the diagonal entries of
AB-BA
are both zero if and only if at least one of them is zero. This observation demonstrates that the matrix equality
AB=BA
can be expressed as the following system of three equations:

\displaystyle a_{2}b_{3}

\displaystyle=a_{3}b_{2};

\displaystyle a_{2}(b_{4}-b_{1})

\displaystyle=b_{2}(a_{4}-a_{1});

(2.1)

\displaystyle a_{3}(b_{4}-b_{1})

\displaystyle=b_{3}(a_{4}-a_{1}).

One can interpret this system as the statement that the three vectors
(a_{4}-a_{1},b_{4}-b_{1})
,
(a_{2},b_{2})
,
(a_{3},b_{3})
span a subspace of
\mathbb{Q}^{2}
(or
\mathbb{R}^{2}
) of dimension at most
1
.

We begin with some preliminary elementary lemmas on counting solutions to each individual equation appearing in the above system.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
N\geqslant 1
, let
u,w
be coprime, non-zero integers and let
m=\max\{|u|,|v|\}
. Then the number of solutions
(x,y)\in([-N,N]\cap\mathbb{Z})^{2}
to the equation

ux=vy\neq 0

(2.2)

is equal to
2\lfloor N/m\rfloor
.

Proof.

Since
u
and
v
are coprime, equation (
2.2
) holds if and only if there exists an integer
z
such that
(x,y)=(vz,uz)
. Moreover, we have
0<|x|,|y|\leqslant N
if and only if
0<|mz|\leqslant N
, whence, the number of choices for
(x,y)\neq(0,0)
is
2\lfloor N/m\rfloor
, as required.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
N\geqslant 1
, let
u,v
be coprime non-zero integers and let
m=\max\{|u|,|v|\}
. Then the number of solutions
(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in([-N,N]\cap\mathbb{Z})^{4}
to the equation

u(b_{1}-b_{2})=v(a_{1}-a_{2})

(2.3)

is equal to

\frac{4N^{3}}{3m^{3}}(12m^{2}-6m(|u|+|v|)+4|uv|)+O(N^{2}).

Proof.

Notice that as
u,v\neq 0
, if (
2.3
) holds, then
a_{1}=a_{2}
if and only if
b_{1}=b_{2}
. Thus, there are
O(N^{2})
solutions where
a_{1}=a_{2}
or
b_{1}=b_{2}
. It therefore suffices to count solutions which do not satisfy this condition.

Since
u
and
v
are coprime, equation (
2.3
) holds if and only if there exists an integer
z
such that

a_{1}-a_{2}=uz,\qquad\text{and}\qquad b_{1}-b_{2}=vz.

Our requirement that
a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}\in[-N,N]
therefore imposes the condition
|mz|\leqslant 2N
. Thus, given
z\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfying
0<|mz|\leqslant 2N
, there are

|[-N,N]\cap[-N+uz,N+uz]\cap\mathbb{Z}|=2N-|uz|+1

choices of
(a,a^{\prime})\in([-N,N]\cap\mathbb{Z})^{2}
satisfying
a-a^{\prime}=uz
. Similarly, there are precisely
2N-|vz|+1
choices of
(b,b^{\prime})\in([-N,N]\cap\mathbb{Z})^{2}
satisfying
b-b^{\prime}=|vz|
.
Hence, writing
M=\lfloor 2N/m\rfloor
, the total number of choices for
(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})
with
a_{1}\neq a_{2}
is

\displaystyle 2\sum_{z\in[M]}

\displaystyle(2N-|u|z+1)(2N-|v|z+1)=2\sum_{z\in[M]}(2N-|u|z)(2N-|v|z)+O(NM)

\displaystyle=8N^{2}M-2N(|v|+|u|)M(M+1)+\frac{1}{3}|u||v|M(M+1)(2M+1)+O(NM).

(2.4)

Noting that
M=2N/m+O(1)
and

\max\{N/m,1\}(|u|+|v|)\leqslant 2N,

the right-hand side of (
2.4
) simplifies to

\frac{4N^{3}}{3m^{3}}\left(12m^{2}-6m(|u|+|v|)+4|uv|\right)+O(N^{2}),

which is the claimed estimate.
∎

Using these results, we now proceed to count ‘degenerate’ solutions to (
2.1
) where
a_{2}b_{2}a_{3}b_{3}=0
. A notable finding of our analysis is that although almost all pairs
(A,B)\in\mathrm{Mat}_{2}(\mathbb{Z},N)
do not satisfy this condition, such pairs nevertheless contribute a positive proportion of commuting pairs. Indeed, as indicated in the introduction, there are
2(2N)^{5}+O(N^{4})
commuting pairs
(A,B)\in{\rm Mat}_{2}(\mathbb{Z},N)
with one of
A
or
B
equalling a scalar multiple of the identity matrix. Writing

\Gamma=\{(A,B)\in{\rm\mathrm{Mat}_{2}}(\mathbb{Z},N)^{2}:AB=BA\}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \Gamma^{\prime}=\{(A,B)\in\Gamma:a_{2}b_{2}a_{3}b_{3}\neq 0\},

we now prove that almost all of the ‘degenerate’ commuting pairs
(A,B)\in\Gamma
must have either
A
or
B
being a multiple of the identity matrix.

Proposition 2.3
.

We have
|\Gamma\setminus\Gamma^{\prime}|=2(2N)^{5}+O(N^{4}\log N)
.

Proof.

Our goal is to count pairs
(A,B)\in\Gamma
with
a_{2}a_{3}b_{2}b_{3}=0
. Notice that the first equation of (
2.1
) ensures that at least two out of
a_{2},b_{2},a_{3},b_{3}
vanish. Suppose that at least three of these variables vanish. In view of (
2.1
), we deduce that either all four vanish or
0\in\{a_{4}-a_{1},b_{4}-b_{1}\}
. In any case, we have at most four degrees of freedom in choosing the entries of
A
and
B
, leading to at most
O(N^{4})
solutions.

It only remains to consider
(A,B)\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma^{\prime}
such that exactly two out of
a_{2},b_{2},a_{3},b_{3}
vanish. More precisely, the first equation of (
2.1
) shows that exactly one element from each of the sets
\{a_{2},b_{3}\}
and
\{a_{3},b_{2}\}
must vanish.
If
a_{2}=a_{3}=0\neq b_{2}b_{3}
, then the second and third equations of (
2.1
) guarantee that
a_{4}=a_{1}
, whence
A
must be a multiple of the identity matrix. By symmetry, the condition
b_{2}=b_{3}\neq a_{2}a_{3}
similarly implies that
B
is a multiple of the identity matrix. Thus, these two cases correspond to pairs
(A,B)\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma^{\prime}
where one of
A
or
B
is a multiple of the identity matrix and the other has non-vanishing off-diagonal entries. By inspection, there are
2(2N)^{5}+O(N^{4})
such pairs.

Finally, we have to bound the number of pairs
(A,B)\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma^{\prime}
with
a_{2}=b_{2}=0\neq a_{3}b_{3}
or
a_{3}=b_{3}=0\neq a_{2}b_{2}
. We focus on the latter case; the former can be handled by a symmetrical argument.
Our goal is therefore to bound the number of solutions to the second equation of (
2.1
).
Note that fixing a choice of
(a_{2},b_{2})
in our solution to (
2.1
) with
a_{2}b_{2}\neq 0
is equivalent to fixing a choice of
(d,u,v)\in[N]\times\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
satisfying
0<|du|,|dv|\leqslant N
; indeed, set

d=\gcd(a_{2},b_{2})
and
(a_{2},b_{2})=(du,dv)

For this fixed choice of
(d,u,v)

\ll\frac{N^{3}}{\max\{|u|,|v|\}}.

Hence, it only remains to sum the above expression over all choices of
(d,u,v)
. By incurring an additional constant factor, we can restrict
u
and
v
to be positive integers with
v\leqslant u
. Thus, the number of solutions is at most

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{1\leqslant d\leqslant N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant v\leqslant u\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{N^{3}}{u}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{1\leqslant d\leqslant N}\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}N^{3}=N^{3}\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N}\sum_{1\leqslant d\leqslant N/u}1

\displaystyle\leqslant N^{4}\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N}\frac{1}{u}\ll N^{4}\log N.

Combining all of our estimates completes the proof.
∎

We now present our proof of Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Combining Proposition
2.3
along with the fact that

\mathfrak{C}_{2}(N)=|\Gamma|=|\Gamma^{\prime}|+|\Gamma\setminus\Gamma^{\prime}|,

we see that it suffices to prove that

|\Gamma^{\prime}|=\left(\frac{10\zeta(2)}{3\zeta(3)}-2\right)(2N)^{5}+O(N^{4}\log N).

(2.5)

We are therefore counting solutions to (
2.1
) with
a_{2}a_{3}b_{2}b_{3}\neq 0
. Note that any
a_{1},\dots,b_{8}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
a_{2}a_{3}b_{2}b_{3}\neq 0
which satisfy the first two equations in (
2.1
) must also satisfy the third equation in (
2.1
). As in the proof of Proposition
2.3
, we perform a change of variables so that fixing a choice of
(a_{2},b_{2})
with
a_{2}b_{2}\neq 0
is equivalent to fixing a choice of
(d,u,v)
with
d=\gcd(a_{2},b_{2})
and
(a_{2},b_{2})=(du,dv)
for coprime
u,v\in\mathbb{Z}
with
1\leqslant|ud|,|vd|\leqslant N
.
Summarising, we have

|\Gamma^{\prime}|=\sum_{1\leqslant d\leqslant N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant|u|,|v|\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\bigg(\sum_{0<|a_{3}|,|b_{3}|\leqslant N}\mathds{1}_{b_{3}u=a_{3}v}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant|a_{1}|,|a_{4}|\leqslant N,\\
0\leqslant|b_{1}|,|b_{4}|\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\mathds{1}_{u(b_{4}-b_{1})=v(a_{4}-a_{1})}\bigg).

Writing
m=\max\{|u|,|v|\}
, we may apply Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
2.2
to deduce that

|\Gamma^{\prime}|=\sum_{1\leqslant d\leqslant N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant|u|,|v|\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{8N^{4}}{3m^{4}}\left(12m^{2}-6m(|u|+|v|)+4|uv|\right)+O\left(\frac{N^{3}}{m}\right)\right).

(2.6)

As demonstrated in the proof of Proposition
2.3
, we have the estimate

\sum_{1\leqslant d\leqslant N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant|u|,|v|\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{N^{3}}{m}\ll N^{4}\log N.

We therefore proceed to analyse the contribution from the main term. The stipulation that
u
and
v
are coprime implies that
|u|=|v|
holds if and only if
u,v\in\{-1,1\}
. The contribution of the main terms arising from this case to the right hand side of (

) is

4N^{4}\sum_{1\leqslant d\leqslant N}\frac{8}{3}(12-6(1+1)+4)=\frac{4}{3}(2N)^{5}.

(2.7)

Next, note that the main term in the summand in (

8

8\sum_{1\leqslant d\leqslant N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant v<u\leqslant N/d\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{8N^{4}}{3u^{4}}(6u^{2}-2uv)=\frac{4}{3}(2N)^{4}\sum_{1\leqslant d\leqslant N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant v<u\leqslant N/d\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{6u-2v}{u^{3}}.

(2.8)

Writing
\phi(u)=|\{1\leqslant v\leqslant u:\gcd(v,u)=1\}|

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant v<u\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}2v=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant v<u\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}v+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant v<u\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}(u-v)=u\phi(u).

Here we have used the fact that
\gcd(u,v)=\gcd(u,u-v)
. From this identity we infer that

\displaystyle\frac{4}{3}(2N)^{4}\sum_{1\leqslant d\leqslant N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant v<u\leqslant N/d\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{6u-2v}{u^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3}(2N)^{4}\sum_{1\leqslant d\leqslant N}\sum_{1<u\leqslant N/d}\frac{5\phi(u)}{u^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{20}{3}(2N)^{4}\sum_{1<u\leqslant N}\frac{\phi(u)}{u^{2}}\sum_{1\leqslant d\leqslant N/u}1

\displaystyle=\frac{20}{3}(2N)^{4}\sum_{1<u\leqslant N}\left(\frac{N\phi(u)}{u^{3}}+O\left(\frac{\phi(u)}{u^{2}}\right)\right).

(2.9)

Putting together (

\sum_{u=2}^{N}\frac{\phi(u)}{u^{3}}=\frac{\zeta(2)}{\zeta(3)}-1+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{u=2}^{N}\frac{\phi(u)}{u^{2}}=\frac{\log N}{\zeta(2)}+O(1)

∎

3.
Restricted divisor correlations

In this section, we present a proof of Lemma
1.5
. The key input is the following useful lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all real numbers
X\geqslant 1
and positive integers
k
, we have

\sum_{n\leqslant X}\left(\sum_{d\mid n}\frac{1}{d}\right)^{k}\ll_{k}X.

Proof.

Expanding and changing the order of summation of the left hand side above reveals that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant X}\left(\sum_{d\mid n}\frac{1}{d}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\leqslant X}\frac{|\{n\leqslant X:\mathrm{lcm}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})\mid n\}|}{d_{1}\cdots d_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\leqslant X}\frac{X}{d_{1}\cdots d_{k}\mathrm{lcm}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})}+O_{k}(\log^{k}X).

By incurring a factor of
k
in our upper bound, we can restrict the final sum to tuples
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})
with
d_{1}=\max\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\}
. Since
d_{1}\leqslant\mathrm{lcm}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})
, we therefore have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant X}\left(\sum_{d\mid n}\frac{1}{d}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\ll_{k}\sum_{d_{1}\leqslant X}\frac{X}{d_{1}^{2}}\left(\sum_{d_{2},\ldots,d_{k}\leqslant d_{1}}\frac{1}{d_{2}\cdots d_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d_{1}\leqslant X}\frac{X}{d_{1}^{2}}((\log d_{1})^{k-1}+O_{k}((\log d_{1})^{k-2}))\ll_{k}X,

as required.
∎

We now proceed to the proof of Lemma
1.5
.

Proof of Lemma
1.5
.

We begin by proving the second inequality in (
1.5
). Observe that there are
O(N^{2})
solutions
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in([-N,N]\cap\mathbb{Z})^{4}
to the equation
x_{1}x_{2}=x_{3}x_{4}
which satisfy
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}=0
or
|x_{1}|=|x_{3}|
. Hence, by writing
x_{1}=du
and
x_{3}=dv
with
d=\gcd(x_{1},x_{3})
,
Lemma
2.1
implies that

\displaystyle r(0)

\displaystyle=O(N^{2})+2\sum_{d\leqslant N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<|u|,|v|\leqslant N/d\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\left\lfloor\frac{N}{\max\{|u|,|v|\}}\right\rfloor=O(N^{2})+16\sum_{d\leqslant N}\sum_{u\leqslant N/d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\leqslant u\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{N}{u}

\displaystyle=O(N^{2})+16N^{2}\sum_{u\leqslant N}\frac{\phi(u)}{u^{2}}=\frac{16}{\zeta(2)}N^{2}\log N+O(N^{2}),

as required.

We now prove the first inequality in (
1.5
). Let
h
be an integer satisfying
0\neq|h|\leqslant 2N^{2}
and let
-N\leqslant a_{1},\dots,a_{4}\leqslant N
satisfy
a_{1}a_{2}-a_{3}a_{4}=h
. Since
h\neq 0
, the standard divisor bound shows that the contribution from solutions satisfying
a_{1}\dots a_{4}=0
is
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon})
for any
\varepsilon>0
. Hence, we may assume that
a_{1},\dots,a_{4}\in\{-N,\dots,N\}\setminus\{0\}
. For any such solution, the greatest common divisor
(a_{1},a_{3})
of
a_{1}
and
a_{3}
divides
h
. We may assume that
a_{1},a_{3}>0
since the analysis in all the other cases follows in a similar fashion. Assuming
a_{1},a_{3}>0
, we note, as before, that the divisor function estimate implies that the number of solutions where
a_{1}=a_{3}
is at most
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon})
. Thus, we now analyse the contribution of the solutions which have
a_{1}>a_{3}
. In this case, if we fix
a_{1},a_{3}
, then the number of valid possibilities for
a_{2},a_{4}
is at most
\frac{(2N+1)(a_{1},a_{3})}{a_{1}}+O(1)
; indeed

a_{4}\equiv\bigg(\frac{a_{3}}{(a_{1},a_{3})}\bigg)^{-1}\frac{h}{(a_{1},a_{3})}\;\bigg({\rm mod}\ \frac{a_{1}}{(a_{1},a_{3})}\bigg)\quad\text{and}\quad-N\leqslant a_{4}\leqslant N.

Similarly, if we fix
a_{1},a_{3}
such that
a_{1}<a_{3}
, then the number of valid possibilities for
a_{2},a_{4}
is at most
\frac{(2N+1)(a_{1},a_{3})}{a_{3}}+O(1)
.

Summarising the above paragraph, we have

r(h)\ll N^{1+1/2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<a_{3}<a_{1}\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\mathds{1}_{(a_{1},a_{3})|h}\bigg(\frac{N(a_{1},a_{3})}{a_{1}}+O(1)\bigg).

Setting
d=(a_{1},a_{3})
and noting that for fixed
a_{1}
and
d
there are at most
a_{1}/d
valid choices for
a_{3}
, we see that

r(h)\ll N^{2}+N\sum_{1<a_{1}\leqslant N}\frac{1}{a_{1}}\sum_{d|a_{1}}\mathds{1}_{d|h}\cdot d\left(\frac{a_{1}}{d}\right).

Simplifying and changing the order of summation, we find that

\displaystyle r(h)

\displaystyle\ll N^{2}+N\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h,\\
1\leqslant d<N\end{subarray}}\sum_{a_{1}\leqslant N}\mathds{1}_{d|a_{1}}\ll N\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h,\\
1\leqslant d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}(N/d+1)\ll N^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h,\\
1\leqslant d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}1/d,

which is the desired estimate.

We conclude by deducing (
1.6
) from (
1.5
) and Lemma
3.1
. Indeed, (
1.6
) and Lemma
3.1
imply that

\displaystyle I_{k}(N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|h|\leqslant 2N^{2}}r(h)^{k}=r(0)^{k}+\sum_{0<|h|\leqslant 2N^{2}}r(h)^{k}

\displaystyle\ll_{k}N^{2k}(\log N)^{k}+\sum_{0<|h|\leqslant 2N^{2}}N^{2k}(\sum_{d|h}1/d)^{k}

\displaystyle\ll_{k}N^{2k}(\log N)^{k}+N^{2k+2}\ll_{k}N^{2k+2}.

This concludes the proof of Lemma
1.5
.
∎

4.

In this section, we will present the proof of Theorem

. Since the lower bound in Theorem
1.1
follows from (
1.1
), it suffices to prove the upper bound
\mathfrak{C}_{3}(N)\ll N^{10}
. Let

A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}\\
a_{4}&a_{5}&a_{6}\\
a_{7}&a_{8}&a_{9}\end{pmatrix}\ \ \text{and}\ \ B=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{3}\\
b_{4}&b_{5}&b_{6}\\
b_{7}&b_{8}&b_{9}\end{pmatrix}

(4.1)

{\rm Mat}_{2}(\mathbb{Z},N)

and

b_{i}&b_{j}\end{pmatrix}=\det\begin{pmatrix}-b_{j}&a_{j}\\
b_{i}&-a_{i}\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
\mathcal{D}_{j,i}=-\mathcal{D}_{i,j}

\mathcal{D}_{i,i}=0

AB-BA=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{D}_{2,4}+\mathcal{D}_{3,7}&\mathcal{D}_{1,2}+\mathcal{D}_{2,5}+\mathcal{D}_{3,8}&\mathcal{D}_{1,3}+\mathcal{D}_{2,6}+\mathcal{D}_{3,9}\\
\mathcal{D}_{4,1}+\mathcal{D}_{5,4}+\mathcal{D}_{6,7}&\mathcal{D}_{4,2}+\mathcal{D}_{6,8}&\mathcal{D}_{4,3}+\mathcal{D}_{5,6}+\mathcal{D}_{6,9}\\

Thus, the condition

implies that all the entries in the above matrix are zero. In particular,
\mathfrak{C}_{3}(N)
counts the number of

a_{2}b_{4}-b_{2}a_{4}=a_{7}b_{3}-a_{3}b_{7}=a_{6}b_{8}-a_{8}b_{6}

\displaystyle-b_{2}(a_{5}-a_{1})+a_{2}(b_{5}-b_{1})+\mathcal{D}_{3,8}

\displaystyle=0=-a_{4}(b_{5}-b_{1})+b_{4}(a_{5}-a_{1})+\mathcal{D}_{6,7}

\displaystyle-b_{3}(a_{9}-a_{1})+a_{3}(b_{9}-b_{1})+\mathcal{D}_{2,6}

\displaystyle=0=-a_{7}(b_{9}-b_{1})+b_{7}(a_{9}-a_{1})+\mathcal{D}_{8,4}

\displaystyle-b_{8}(a_{9}-a_{5})+a_{8}(b_{9}-b_{5})+\mathcal{D}_{2,7}

\displaystyle=0=-a_{6}(b_{9}-b_{5})+b_{6}(a_{9}-a_{5})+\mathcal{D}_{3,4}.

The latter six equations may be rewritten as

MX=Y,

where

M=\begin{pmatrix}-b_{2}&a_{2}&0&0\\
b_{4}&-a_{4}&0&0\\
0&0&-b_{3}&a_{3}\\
0&0&b_{7}&-a_{7}\\
b_{8}&-a_{8}&-b_{8}&a_{8}\\

. For

be the set of all commuting pairs

Lemma 4.1

We have

(A,B)\in\mathcal{S}_{3}

\displaystyle\{(-b_{2},a_{2},0,0),(b_{4},-a_{4},0,0)\},\ \ \{(0,0,-b_{3},a_{3}),(0,0,b_{7},-a_{7})\},

such that
(b_{j},a_{j})=\sigma_{j}\cdot(b_{2},a_{2})
. As previously mentioned, the cases when a different choice of vectors from the three sets described in (
4.6
) are non-zero can be analysed similarly.

The above argument shows that there exists
k\in\{2,3,4,6,7,8\}
such that for all
j\in\{2,3,4,6,7,8\}
the vector
(a_{j},b_{j})
is a rational scalar multiple of the non-zero vector
(a_{k},b_{k})
. Writing
(a_{k},b_{k})=\gcd(a_{k},b_{k})(u,v)
for some coprime integers
u,v\in[-N,N]
, this implies that for each
j\in\{2,3,4,6,7,8\}
we have
(a_{j},b_{j})=\lambda_{j}(u,v)
for some
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus, the total number of choices for the entries of
M
is equal to the number of choices for
(u,v)
multiplied by the number of choices for each
\lambda_{j}
.

We now compute the total number of choices for entries of
M
. For a fixed choice of coprime integers
u,v\in[-N,N]
, there are at most

1+2\bigg\lfloor\frac{N}{\max\{|u|,|v|\}}\bigg\rfloor

choices of
\lambda_{j}
for each
j\in\{2,3,4,6,7,8\}
, where the extra
1
accounts for the choice
\lambda_{j}=0
, and the factor of
2
corresponds to the choice of sign for
\lambda_{j}\neq 0
. By incurring an additional constant factor in our final upper bound, we may restrict attention to
(u,v)
with
|u|\leqslant|v|
. Therefore, the total number of choices for the entries of
M
is at most

\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant|u|\leqslant|v|\leqslant N\\
\gcd(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{N^{6}}{v^{6}}\ll N^{6}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant u\leqslant v\leqslant N\\
\gcd(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{v^{6}}\ll N^{6}+N^{6}\sum_{1\leqslant v\leqslant N}\frac{1}{v^{5}}\ll N^{6}.

Finally, having fixed the entries of
M
in
O(N^{6})
many ways, as in the preceding cases, the assumption that
M
has rank
2
implies that there are at most
O(N^{4})
choices for
a_{1},a_{5},a_{9},b_{1},b_{5},b_{9}
. Therefore, the contribution of this subcase to
|\mathcal{S}_{2}|
is at most
O(N^{10})
. This concludes the proof of Lemma
4.3
.
∎

Substituting the bounds presented in Lemmas
4.1
,
4.2
and
4.3
into (
4.4
), we deduce that

\mathfrak{C}_{3}(N)\ll N^{10},

thus completing the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

5.
Commuting
2\times 2
matrices over the
p
-adic integers

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.3
. We are therefore interested in counting pairs of commuting
2\times 2
matrices with entries in
\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
. The number of such pairs is equal to
p^{2n}
multiplied by the number of solutions over
\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
to the system

a_{2}b_{3}-a_{3}b_{2}=a_{2}b_{4}-a_{4}b_{2}=a_{3}b_{4}-a_{4}b_{3}=0.

(5.1)

Indeed, we recover (
2.1
) upon replacing
(a_{4},b_{4})
with
(a_{4}-a_{1},b_{4}-b_{1})
in the above system, and then observing that, for fixed
(a_{4}-a_{1},b_{4}-b_{1})
, there are
p^{2n}
choices for
(a_{1},a_{4},b_{1},b_{4})
.

In contrast to the characteristic zero setting, we have to handle issues arising from zero divisors in
\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
. We accomplish this by examining
p
-adic valuations. For each prime
p
and each non-zero integer
x
, let
\nu_{p}(x)
denote the largest non-negative integer
m
which satisfies
p^{m}\mid x
. Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can then define the
p
-adic valuation

\nu_{p}:\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}\to\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
by

\nu_{p}(y+p^{n}\mathbb{Z})=\begin{cases}\nu_{p}(y),\quad&\text{if }\;p^{n}\nmid y\\
n,&\text{if }\;p^{n}\mid y.\end{cases}

Since
\nu_{p}(x)=\nu_{p}(x+p^{n})
whenever
p^{n}\nmid x
, this function is well-defined. Moreover, for any
0\leqslant m<n
, one has

|\{a\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}:\nu_{p}(a)=m\}|=p^{n-m}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right).

(5.2)

When
\nu_{p}(a_{2})=0
, say, we can invert
a_{2}
in each equation in (
5.1
) that it appears. This allows us to easily count solutions. To handle the more general setting where every variable is divisible by some
p^{h}
, we appeal to the following ‘lifting trick’.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
p
be a prime. Let
d,k,m\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the system of equations

F_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=\cdots=F_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=0,

where each
F_{i}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
. For every non-negative integer
h
, let
S(n,h)
denote the set of solutions
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z})^{m}
to the above system which satisfy

\min\{\nu_{p}(x_{1}),\ldots,\nu_{p}(x_{m})\}=h.

If
0\leqslant h<n/d
, then
|S(n,h)|=p^{(d-1)hm}|S(n-dh,0)|
.

Proof.

Consider the map
\Psi:S(n,h)\to S(n-dh,0)
defined by

\Psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=p^{-h}\cdot(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\;(\Mod{p^{n-dh}}).

Here, for
a\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
with
\nu_{p}(a)\geqslant h
, we have written
p^{-h}a
for the unique
x\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n-h}\mathbb{Z}
such that
a=p^{h}x
. Since
dh<n
, this map is well-defined. Moreover, as the
F_{i}
are all homogeneous of degree
d
, by multiplying every entry of an element of
S(n-dh,0)
by
p^{h}
, we see that
\Psi
is surjective. Finally, by viewing
p^{-h}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})
as an element of
(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n-h}\mathbb{Z})^{m}
, we find that
\Psi(\mathbf{x})=\Psi(\mathbf{y})
if and only if
x_{i}\equiv y_{i}\;(\Mod{p^{n-dh+h}})
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
, that is,
\nu_{p}(x_{i}-y_{i})\geqslant n-(d-1)h
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
. Thus, for any fixed
\mathbf{y}\in S(n,h)
, there are precisely
p^{(d-1)hm}
choices of
\mathbf{x}\in S(n,h)
such that
\Psi(\mathbf{x})=\Psi(\mathbf{y})
. Combining all of these observations finishes the proof.
∎

As in the integer setting, before counting the total number of solutions to (
5.1
), we first isolate certain ‘degenerate’ solutions.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
p
be a prime. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and set
q=p^{n}
. The number of solutions over
\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
to the system (
5.1
) which satisfy

a_{2}b_{3}=a_{3}b_{2}=0

is
O(n^{2}q^{7/2})
.

Proof.

By incurring a factor of
4
in our final bound, we may restrict attention to solutions where

\nu_{p}(a_{2})=\min\{\nu_{p}(a_{2}),\nu_{p}(a_{3}),\nu_{p}(b_{2}),\nu_{p}(b_{3})\}

Writing
h=\nu_{p}(a_{2})
, if
h=n
, then there are
q^{2}
solutions to (
5.1
) since all variables other than
a_{4},b_{4}
must vanish. We may therefore assume throughout that
h<n
.
For a fixed choice of
h<n
, the number of choices for
a_{2}
is
p^{n-h}(1-p^{-1})
.
The condition that
a_{2}b_{3}=0
means that
\nu_{p}(b_{3})\geqslant n-h
, and so there are
p^{h}
choices for
b_{3}
. Hence, there are
p^{n}(1-p^{-1})
choices for the pair
(a_{2},b_{3})
.

Next we count the number of choices for
(a_{3},b_{2})
. That is, we count the number of solutions to
a_{3}b_{2}=0
subject to the constraint that
\nu_{p}(a_{3}),\nu_{p}(b_{2})\geqslant h
. Notice that the equation
a_{3}b_{2}=0
implies that
\nu_{p}(a_{3})\geqslant n-\nu_{p}(b_{2})
. Invoking (
5.2
), for
h<n/2
, we find that the number of choices for
(a_{3},b_{2})
is at most

\displaystyle p^{n-h}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)p^{h}+p^{n-(h+1)}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)p^{h+1}+\cdots+p^{h}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)p^{n-h}+p^{h-1}\cdot p^{n-h}

\displaystyle=q(n-2h+1)\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)+\frac{q}{p}=q\left(n-2h+1-\frac{n-2h}{p}\right).

Here, the
(n-2h+1)
terms in the first line come from summing over all choices of
\nu_{p}(b_{2})\in\{h,\ldots,n-h\}
and the final term is the number of
(a_{3},b_{2})
with
p^{n-h+1}\mid b_{2}
and
p^{h}\mid a_{3}
.
If
h\geqslant n/2
, then we instead trivially bound the number of
(a_{3},b_{2})
by the number of pairs of elements which are divisible by
p^{h}
, resulting in an upper bound of
p^{-2h}q^{2}
.

Finally, we estimate the number of valid choices of
(a_{4},b_{4})
. With
(a_{2},b_{2})
fixed from before, if
h=0
, then we may invert
a_{2}
to show that that there are
q
choices for
(a_{4},b_{4})
. When
1\leqslant h\leqslant n
, notice that
a_{2}b_{4}=a_{4}b_{2}
if and only if

a_{2}^{\prime}b_{4}\equiv a_{4}b_{2}^{\prime}\;(\Mod{p^{n-h}}),

where
a_{2}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n-h}\mathbb{Z}
are uniquely defined by the equations
a_{2}=p^{h}a_{2}^{\prime}
and
b_{2}=p^{h}b_{2}^{\prime}
. As before, there are
p^{n-h}
solutions
a_{4},b_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n-h}\mathbb{Z}
to this latter congruence. Since each
x\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n-h}\mathbb{Z}
lifts to exactly
p^{h}
elements of
\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
, we deduce that,
for any fixed choice of
(h;a_{2},b_{2})
, there are
p^{h}q
solutions
a_{4},b_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
to the equation
a_{2}b_{4}=a_{4}b_{2}
. This gives an upper bound for the number of
(a_{4},b_{4})
which satisfy (
5.1
).

Combining all of the observations from the previous three paragraphs, we conclude that the total number of solutions with
h<n
is

\displaystyle\ll q^{3}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\sum_{0\leqslant h<n/2}p^{h}\left(n-2h+1-\frac{n-2h}{p}\right)+q^{4}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\sum_{n/2\leqslant h<n}p^{-h}

\displaystyle\ll q^{7/2}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\sum_{0\leqslant h<n/2}\left(n+1\right)+nq^{7/2}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\ll n^{2}q^{7/2}.

Here, we have also appealed to the trivial bound
p\geqslant 2
.
Adding the
q^{2}<q^{7/2}
solutions with
h=n
to this total finishes the proof.
∎

We now use Lemma
5.1
to count the remaining solutions to (
5.1
) and thereby prove Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

For each
0\leqslant h<n/2
, let
S(n,h)
denote the set of solutions to (
5.1
) over
\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
which satisfy

h=\min\{\nu_{p}(a_{2}),\nu_{p}(a_{3}),\nu_{p}(a_{4}),\nu_{p}(b_{2}),\nu_{p}(b_{3}),\nu_{p}(b_{4})\}.

We claim that

|S(n,h)|=p^{4n-2h}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{3}}\right).

Once this is proved, Theorem
1.3
follows by summing over all
0\leqslant h<n/2
and using Lemma
5.2
to bound the remaining solutions.

Lemma
5.1
shows that we only have to verify the above claim for
h=0
.
For clarity, we employ the temporary notation

g(a,b):=\min\{\nu_{p}(a),\nu_{p}(b)\}.

By symmetry of the variables, the inclusion-exclusion principle informs us that

|S(n,0)|=3|U_{0}|-3|U_{1}|+|U_{2}|,

(5.3)

where

\displaystyle U_{0}

\displaystyle=\{(a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4})\in S(n,0):g(a_{2},b_{2})=0\},

\displaystyle U_{1}

\displaystyle=\{(a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4})\in S(n,0):g(a_{2},b_{2})=g(a_{3},b_{3})=0\}\ \text{and}

\displaystyle U_{2}

\displaystyle=\{(a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4})\in S(n,0):g(a_{2},b_{2})=g(a_{3},b_{3})=g(a_{4},b_{4})=0\}.

Suppose we fix a pair
(a_{2},b_{2})
with
g(a_{2},b_{2})=0
.
Proceeding as in the proof of Lemma
5.2
, by inverting whichever of
a_{2}
or
b_{2}
is a multiplicative unit, we find that there are
p^{n}
choices for
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
such that
a_{2}b=ab_{2}
. If we further stipulate that
g(a,b)=0
, then there are
p^{n}(1-p^{-1})
choices for
(a,b)
. This comes from noting that
\nu_{p}(a_{2})=0
would imply that
\nu_{p}(a)=0
, and similarly
\nu_{p}(b)=0
if
\nu_{p}(b_{2})=0
. Moreover, given
a_{2},a,a^{\prime},b_{2},b,b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
with
g(a_{2},b_{2})=g(a,b)=g(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=0
, we claim that whenever
a_{2}b=b_{2}a
and
a_{2}b^{\prime}=b_{2}a^{\prime}
hold, then
ab^{\prime}=a^{\prime}b
must also hold. Indeed, if
\nu_{p}(a_{2})=0
, then
\nu_{p}(a)=\nu_{p}(a^{\prime})=0
, whence,
b^{\prime}=a_{2}^{-1}b_{2}a^{\prime}=a^{-1}ba^{\prime}
, and the case when
\nu_{p}(b_{2})=0
can be similarly analysed.

Combining all of the observations of the previous paragraph, and
recalling that

|\{(a,b)\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z})^{2}:g(a,b)=0\}|=p^{2n}\left(2-\frac{2}{p}-\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{2}\right)=p^{2n}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right),

we conclude that

|U_{k}|=p^{4n}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{k}

for every
0\leqslant k\leqslant 2
. Inserting these estimates into (
5.3
), we obtain the claimed estimate.
∎

6.
Supplementary results and further remarks

Our aim in this section is to prove Lemma
1.4
and Theorem
1.6
as well as record some further remarks on estimating the number of commuting pairs of
d\times d
matrices for
d\geqslant 4
.

6.1.
Proof of Lemma
1.4

We begin by proving Lemma
1.4
.
Let
A=(a_{i,j})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d}
and
B=(b_{i,j})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d}
be elements of
{\rm Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{Z},N)
and let
I_{d}
be the
d\times d
identity matrix. The contribution to
\mathfrak{C}_{d}(N)
from the cases when either
A
or
B
is a multiple of the identity matrix is presented in (
1.1
). It therefore suffices to show that there exist at least

\frac{2}{d+1}(2N)^{d^{2}+1}(1-O_{d}(N^{-1}))

pairs
(A,B)\in{\rm Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{Z},N)
such that
the off-diagonal entries of
A
and
B
are non-zero and that
AB=BA
.
In this endeavour, note that for any fixed
A
and any
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
, the matrix
B=A+\lambda I
commutes with
A
. In particular, any
A,B\in{\rm Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{Z},N)
satisfying

a_{1,1}-b_{1,1}=a_{2,2}-b_{2,2}=\dots=a_{d,d}-b_{d,d}

(6.1)

and
a_{i,j}=b_{i,j}
for every
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d
with
i\neq j
must commute. For every
i\neq j
, we can fix
a_{i,j}\in\{-N,-N+1,\dots,N\}\setminus\{0\}
in
(2N)^{d^{2}-d}
ways. Thus, we only need to show that the number of solutions
E_{d}(N)
to (
6.1
) satisfies

E_{d}(N)\geqslant\frac{2}{d+1}(2N)^{d+1}(1-O_{d}(N^{-1})).

(6.2)

For any
n\in[-2N,2N]\cap\mathbb{Z}
, let
r(n)
count all pairs
n_{1},n_{2}\in\{-N,\dots,N\}\cap\mathbb{Z}
such that
n=n_{2}-n_{1}
. Since
r(n)=2N-|n|+O(1)
, we have

\displaystyle E_{d}(N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{-2N\leqslant x\leqslant 2N}r(x)^{d}=2\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant 2N}(2N-n)^{d}+O(N^{d})=2\int_{1}^{2N}(2N-x)^{d}dx+O(N^{d})

\displaystyle=2(2N)^{d+1}\int_{1/2N}^{1}(1-y)^{d}dy+O(N^{d})=\frac{2(2N)^{d+1}}{d+1}(1-(2N)^{-1})^{d+1}+O(N^{d}),

which simplifies to deliver the estimate claimed in (
6.2
).

6.2.
Commuting pairs over sets with small doubling

We will now turn to proving Theorem
1.6
, and so, for every finite, non-empty set
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}
and every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we define

r_{\mathcal{A}}(n)=\sum_{a_{1},\dots,a_{4}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathds{1}_{a_{1}a_{2}-a_{3}a_{4}=n}\ \ \text{and}\ \ I_{3}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}r_{\mathcal{A}}(n)^{3}.

With this in hand, we record the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}
be a finite, non-empty set with
|\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}|={\rm K}|\mathcal{A}|
for some
{\rm K}\geqslant 1
. Then

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}r_{\mathcal{A}}(n)\leqslant r_{\mathcal{A}}(0)\ll{\rm K}^{2}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}\log(2|\mathcal{A}|)\ \ \text{and}\ \ I_{3}(\mathcal{A})\ll{\rm K}^{4}|\mathcal{A}|^{8}(\log(2|\mathcal{A}|))^{2}.

Proof.

The second inequality follows from combining the first inequality and the fact that
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}r_{\mathcal{A}}(n)=|\mathcal{A}|^{4}
, whence it suffices to prove the first inequality. Given
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we may apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to deduce that

\displaystyle r_{\mathcal{A}}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathds{1}_{h+n=a_{1}a_{2}}\right)\left(\sum_{a_{3},a_{4}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathds{1}_{h=a_{3}a_{4}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathds{1}_{h+n=a_{1}a_{2}}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{a_{3},a_{4}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathds{1}_{h=a_{3}a_{4}}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}=r_{\mathcal{A}}(0).

Finally, employing work of Solymosi
[
20
]
on the sum-product phenomenon, we see that

r_{\mathcal{A}}(0)\ll|\mathcal{A}|^{2}+\sum_{a_{1},\dots,a_{4}\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{0\}}\mathds{1}_{a_{1}a_{2}=a_{3}a_{4}}\ll{\rm K}^{2}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}\log(2|\mathcal{A}|).\qed

Our aim now is to prove Theorem
1.6
, and in this endeavour, we will follow the proof of Theorem
1.1
and simply replace the application of Lemma
1.5
with Lemma
6.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Given matrices
A,B
as in (
4.1
) with
a_{1},\dots,b_{9}\in\mathcal{A}
, we see that
AB=BA
if and only if (
4.3
) and (
4.2
) hold true. As before, we split our proof into various cases depending on
{\rm rank}(M)
. If
{\rm rank}(M)=4
, we can use Lemma
6.1
to fix the off-diagonal entries of
A
and
B
in
O(I_{3}(\mathcal{A}))=O(|\mathcal{A}|^{8}{\rm K}^{4}(\log(2|\mathcal{A}|))^{2})
ways and then use the fact that
{\rm rank}(M)=4
to fix the diagonal entries of
A
and
B
in
O(|\mathcal{A}|^{2})
many ways. Thus, when
{\rm rank}(M)=4
, we are done. When
2\leqslant{\rm rank}(M)\leqslant 3
, the off-diagonal entries of
A
and
B
satisfy (
4.5
), and so, Lemma
6.1
implies that these can be fixed in
O(r_{\mathcal{A}}(0)^{3})=O(|\mathcal{A}|^{6}{\rm K}^{6}(\log(2|\mathcal{A}|))^{3}
many ways. Moreover, since
{\rm rank}(M)\geqslant 2
, we can fix the diagonal entries of
A
and
B
in
O(|\mathcal{A}|^{4})
many ways, and so, we are done in this case as well. Finally, if
{\rm rank}(M)\leqslant 1
, then as before, we can deduce that at least
8
of the off-diagonal entries of
A
and
B
are zero, in which case, we can fix the rest of entries of
A
and
B
in
O(|\mathcal{A}|^{10})
many ways, and so, we are done in this case as well.
∎

6.3.
Further remarks

We conclude with a brief discussion on commuting pairs of
d\times d
matrices with
d\geqslant 4
. We are therefore studying solutions
(A,B)\in\mathrm{Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{Z},N)
to
AB=BA
. For
d\geqslant 4
, there are additional constraints placed upon the entries of
A
and
B
beyond those which emerge from the approach we used when
d\leqslant 3
. Such constraints have been observed before in work on “commuting patterns” of matrices
[
12
,
13
]
, where one seeks solutions
(A,B)\in\mathrm{Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
to
AB=BA
subject to conditions on which entries of
A
and
B
are allowed to be zero. For example, if we require all entries of
B
to be non-zero,
[
12
, Theorem 2.4]
exhibits certain algebraic “swath conditions” that the positions of the non-zero entries of
A
must satisfy. We refer the reader to these papers for further details and references.

To illustrate the difficulties that arise for higher dimensions, we will now present a naive generalisation of our approach from §
4
to the case when
d\geqslant 4
. We split our analysis into various cases depending on the behaviour of the
d-1
equations arising from the diagonal entries of
AB-BA
, consequently fixing the off-diagonal entries of
A
and
B
in different ways depending on each case. Following §
4
, we then write the remaining
d(d-1)
equations as
MX=Y
, where
M
is a
d(d-1)\times 2(d-1)
matrix whose entries come from the off-diagonal entries of
A,B
, and
X\in\mathbb{R}^{2(d-1)}
and
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{d(d-1)}
are defined suitably.

Suppose that
M
has full rank, which was one of the most significant cases studied in §
4
. Applying variants of Lemma
1.5
, one may fix the off-diagonal entries of
A,B
in
O(N^{2d^{2}-4d+2})
many ways via the
d-1
quadratic equations. By inverting a sub-matrix of
M
to fix
X
, one can then fix the diagonal entries of
A,B
in
O(N^{2})
many ways. Hence, this argument already gives an upper bound of the shape
O(N^{2d^{2}-4d+4})
. For
d\geqslant 4
, this is much larger than both the conjectured upper bound
O(N^{d^{2}+1})
and Browning–Sawin–Wang’s bound (
1.3
).

One would therefore require a more refined approach which further exploits the many extra constraints present in the
d\geqslant 4
setting.
In order to elucidate upon the latter, note that in the
3\times 3
case, one can show, either directly or via Cramer’s rule, that if
X\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
is such that the first four equations of
MX=Y
are satisfied, then all six are satisfied. In other words, the last two equations are ‘consistent’ with the first four. However, when
d\geqslant 4
this property may not hold. We illustrate this with the following example. Let
d=4
and choose

A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&0&0&0\\
1&a_{6}&0&0\\
1&a_{10}&a_{11}&3\\
1&1&2&a_{16}\end{pmatrix}\ \ \text{and}\ \ B=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&1&1&-2\\
0&b_{6}&0&0\\
0&b_{10}&b_{11}&2\\
0&0&1&b_{16}\end{pmatrix}.

Our choices ensure that all the diagonal entries of
AB-BA
vanish.
One can also verify that the matrix
M
has full rank by noting that the the first six rows form a
6\times 6
matrix with determinant
-2
. However, the seventh row of
M
is the zero vector whilst the seventh entry of
Y
is
1
. Consequently, there are no solutions
X\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
to the full system
MX=Y
, despite the fact that there is always a unique solution to the first six equations. Thus, the seventh equation can be ‘inconsistent’ with the first six.
