Adaptive hydrogels with spatiotemporal stiffening using pH-modulating enzymes
Natascha Gray, Zoe Grämiger, André R. Studart, Rafael Libanori

Abstract




Abstract

Adaptive material systems that autonomously respond to external stimuli are crucial for advancing next-generation smart devices. Biological systems achieve autonomous behavior by utilizing chemical energy from out-of-equilibrium reactions to power life-like functions without requiring external energy inputs. Although responsive hydrogels with embedded enzymatic reactions offer a promising platform for implementing adaptive behavior in synthetic systems, previous studies have focused on controlling the supramolecular self-assembly of responsive building blocks rather than modulating network crosslinking. Here, we demonstrate direct enzymatic modulation of crosslinking density in a double-network hydrogel to achieve autonomous self-stiffening in response to a chemical stimulus. Our adaptive system embeds glucose oxidase within a polyacrylamide-alginate double-network hydrogel containing
\text{Ca}\text{(}\text{EDTA}\text{)}\text{}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2\hskip 0.90417pt--\hskip 0.90417pt}}
complexes that render the crosslinked alginate network pH-responsive through a competitive calcium binding mechanism. Chemical waves emerging from enzymatic reaction activation propagate at speeds ranging from
1544\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{min}}^{-1}
, driving spatiotemporal mechanical transitions that increase material stiffness by up to 2.1-fold. By integrating signal sensing and chemomechanical transduction within this responsive hydrogel, we realized adaptive behavior that autonomously converts localized chemical inputs into system-wide mechanical outputs. This positions our adaptive hydrogels as promising model systems to guide the design of intelligent materials for soft robotics and biomedical devices.

Introduction

Adaptive hydrogels are an emerging class of materials that exhibit complex, life-like functions typically associated with biological systems, including sensing, signal transduction, and communication
[
40
,
7
]
. Analogous to their biological counterparts, synthetic adaptive systems can achieve autonomous behavior by incorporating temporal feedback regulation mechanisms that harness chemical energy from out-of-equilibrium processes to drive structural transitions in responsive materials. These temporal feedback mechanisms arise from nonlinear reaction kinetics that establish activation and inhibition pathways, enabling the system to autonomously regulate structural transitions in response to changes in the chemical environment. By coupling such temporal feedback loops with the molecular responsiveness of hydrogel networks, autonomous structural transitions can be programmed to exhibit swelling-contracting waves
[
24
]
, transient and oscillating mechanical behavior
[
3
]
, mechanical actuation
[
48
]
, and propagation of chemical signals to induce macroscopic global response
[
30
]
. These feedback-regulated systems can also be stabilized in quiescent (semi-dormant) or dormant excitable states from which external stimuli activate autonomous structural transitions throughout the material system
[
19
]
.

Despite these advances, the development of adaptive systems modulated by temporal feedback loops remains challenging due to the toxicity of their chemical components
[
26
]
, and the challenging kinetic balance typically involved in multi-component chemical reaction networks
[
47
]
. pH-modulating enzymatic reactions offer a promising non-toxic alternative for implementing excitable temporal feedback loops based on a small number of components
[
5
]
, including the hydrolysis of urea by urease to produce ammonia
[
16
,
14
,
21
]
and the oxidation of glucose by
GOx
(
GOx
) to produce gluconic acid
[
39
,
9
]
. These enzymatic reactions are highly compatible with hydrogel systems and can provide temporal regulation by producing acid-base species that self-modulate enzymatic activity, enabling straightforward implementation of an excitable, quiescent state that can be activated by external stimuli to generate an output that can be directly coupled to the pH-response of the system
[
13
]
. Such output-response coupling has enabled the use of pH-modulating enzymatic reactions as switches to induce programmed structural transitions, including liquid-to-solid
[
22
,
32
]
and solid-to-liquid
[
21
,
20
,
17
]
, temporal evolution of supramolecular structures
[
31
]
, and shape morphing
[
10
]
. Beyond mere temporal regulation, the non-linear kinetics of pH-modulating enzymes also enable spatial modulation via a reaction-diffusion process that generates chemical waves for amplifying and propagating localized chemical signals
[
13
,
46
,
27
]
. While pH-modulating enzymatic reactions provide a promising and powerful platform for programming spatiotemporal structural transitions in host material systems, their implementation in adaptive systems has mostly relied on an auxiliary, non-integrated aqueous phase to carry the enzymatic reaction components. To our knowledge, only one study has reported a fully integrated adaptive material system in which the pH-modulating enzymatic reaction is entirely hosted within the pH-responsive hydrogel network
[
8
]
.

Spatiotemporal self-stiffening represents a particularly promising adaptive function among those programmable through pH-modulating enzymatic reactions, as it enables global mechanical transitions from local stimuli that are essential for applications in advanced soft robotics and adaptive biomedical devices. This self-stiffening mechanism is widespread in biological systems, such as the defense mechanism exhibited by echinoderms
[
44
]
and the controlled growth pattern of plants
[
25
]
. In synthetic hydrogel systems, self-stiffening has been implemented through the adaptive formation of a reinforcing interpenetrating network upon a thermal stimulus
[
18
]
, modulation of physical interactions between adjacent nanofibers in nanocomposites through solvent exchange
[
4
]
, localized crosslinking based on dynamic transesterification due to temperature
[
42
]
, or piezoelectrically-induced precipitation of reinforcing particles
[
29
]
. However, these systems generally require continuous external inputs and lack an integrated temporal feedback control unit to sense, amplify, and propagate chemical signals to drive a truly autonomous self-stiffening transition. While a recent urease-based system has achieved autonomous self-stiffening through pH-triggered supramolecular network assembly within a pre-existing hydrogel network
[
8
]
, the underlying design principles governing how chemomechanical transduction is governed via enzymatic reaction-diffusion processes remain poorly understood, particularly for systems where enzymatic activity directly modulates the dynamic crosslinking density of hydrogel networks.

Here, we present an adaptive
DN
(
DN
) hydrogel system that achieves an autonomous, system-wide self-stiffening via direct enzymatic modulation of crosslinking density. The adaptive system employs
GOx
to both detect local chemical signals and generate acidic waves that propagate through the material. This wave propagates through the material, driving the progressive formation of crosslinks between calcium ions (
\text{Ca}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2+}}
) and alginate chains embedded in a
PAM
(
PAM
) matrix, thereby creating a mechanically reinforced
DN
hydrogel. The ionically crosslinked alginate network is rendered pH-responsive by adding
EDTA
(
EDTA
), which establishes a competitive equilibrium with alginate chains for binding
\text{Ca}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2+}}
that shifts from CaEDTA to Ca-alginate following a alkaline-to-acidic pH transition. We first investigate the chemomechanical transduction mechanism by characterizing the mechanical response of single and
DN
hydrogels subjected to spontaneous pH changes induced by
GOx
. Next, we demonstrate that propagation speed of self-sustained acidic waves activated by local chemical triggers strongly depend on enzymatic reaction kinetics and system’s buffering capacity, ranging from
1544\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{min}}^{-1}
. Finally, we map the spatiotemporal evolution of the chemomechanical transduction mechanism using a non-destructive mechanical characterization, thereby revealing how reaction-diffusion dynamics within pH-responsive
DN
hydrogels drive autonomous mechanical transitions.

To achieve global mechanical stiffening from a local trigger, our system relies on the orchestrated integration of three key elements: nonlinear kinetics of pH-modulating enzymatic reactions, propagation of chemical signals through reaction-diffusion processes, and pH-responsive crosslinking within the hydrogel matrix (
Figure 1
).
GOx
is at the core of this integration, serving as both the engine powering mechanical transitions and the amplifier that propagates chemical signals throughout the adaptive system.
GOx
catalyzes glucose oxidation to glucono-δ-lactone, which spontaneously hydrolyzes to gluconic acid, thereby decreasing the pH of the reaction medium (
Figure 1
a). This redox reaction reduces
GOx
to its inactive form, which must be regenerated to its active, oxidized state by an electron-accepting mediator. While molecular oxygen
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
typically serves as the natural oxidizing agent for
GOx
regeneration, it presents several experimental drawbacks such as poor water solubility, difficult control over concentration, and production of inhibitory
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

[
45
]
. Moreover,
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
formation in this redox reaction consumes two
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
per
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
molecule, reducing the amplification of the autocatalytic
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
production. These limitations motivate the use of alternative oxidizing agents for more reproducible reaction conditions
[
12
]
. Unlike
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
Fi
(
Fi
) enables precise control over initial conditions and does not consume additional
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
when regenerating

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}

The pH-dependent activity profile of
GOx
creates interconnected feedback regulations that enable autonomous pH modulation within the system (
Figure 1
a). At pH values above the optimum,
GOx
produces acidic species that increase its activity, creating an autocatalytic positive feedback auto-regulation that amplifies acid production. Conversely, at pH values below the optimum, continued acid production generates a negative feedback auto-regulation in which
GOx
activity progressively decreases as the out-of-equilibrium reaction approaches steady-state. This non-linear pH-dependent response allows the system to be kinetically trapped into a quiescent excitable state, wherein the autocatalytic positive feedback remains in a semi-dormant state. Under these conditions, external acidic triggers can activate this excitable state by decreasing the pH below a critical threshold, initiating a spontaneous alkaline-to-acidic transition throughout the system.

In addition to exploiting
GOx
’s pH-dependent activity for temporal alkaline-to-acidic transitions, we harness reaction-diffusion dynamics to implement spatial responses in the system. The coupling of
GOx
’s nonlinear reaction and diffusion kinetics generates self-sustained chemical waves in the reaction medium, thereby enabling the implementation of spatially-modulated pH transitions in our adaptive system (
Figure 1
b). Localized addition of an acidic trigger activates
GOx
in a limited volume, generating
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
that diffuse outward and sequentially activate
GOx
in adjacent regions. This generates a self-sustained chemical wave front that propagates through the entire system, converting a local chemical signal into a global chemical response. Unlike passive diffusion fronts that decay with distance, these wave fronts can propagate at much higher speeds and maintain constant amplitude over centimeter-scale distances
[
26
,
13
]
. Because their propagation speed is determined by the combination of diffusive transport and non-linear enzymatic kinetics, spatiotemporal transitions can be modulated by varying
GOx
concentration, substrate and mediator availability, and the system’s buffer capacity
[
27
,
23
]
. The spatiotemporal pH signal, propagated by the enzymatically-driven chemical wave, enables global mechanical transitions when coupled with responsive hydrogels that exhibit pH-dependent crosslinking density (
Figure 1
c). We employ a
DN
hydrogel comprised of permanently crosslinked

and reversibly crosslinked calcium-alginate (
PAM
-CaEDTA-alginate)
[
50
]
. The

renders this network pH-responsive by establishing a pH-dependent competitive equilibrium for calcium binding. As pH decreases, progressive protonation of EDTA’s carboxylic acid groups (p

2.7(\pm 0.7)
at

30\text{\,}\mathrm{mM}

1.3(\pm 0.2)
-fold higher than non-adaptive hydrogels. Notably, the apparent modulus and work of fracture of non-adaptive hydrogels do not exhibit such a strong dependence on CaEDTA concentration (
Figure S2

DN

availability for intra-network ionic crosslinking. Second, low protonation degree of alginate carboxylate groups weakens both intra-network hydrogen bonds between alginate chains and inter-network hydrogen bonds with PAM. More acidic pH conditions are expected to enhance mechanical performance by simultaneously increasing
\text{Ca}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2+}}

\text{Ca}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2+}}
binding equilibrium toward alginate, while incorporating additional autocatalytic
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
sources could enable more acidic pH conditions and thereby increase the availability of free
\text{Ca}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2+}}
for alginate crosslinking. Overall, this dual-network strategy demonstrates that temporal pH regulation via enzymatic catalysis enables autonomous mechanical transitions, producing adaptive

The reasonable agreement between model predictions and experimental observations demonstrates that the theoretical framework captures the essential physicochemical mechanisms governing wave propagation, despite several simplifying assumptions. Most notably, the model assumes pH-independent autocatalytic kinetics, suggesting that

concentration gradients at the leading edge remain shallow, resulting in minimal variation in

Additionally, while CaEDTA exhibits polyprotic acid-base equilibria, the model’s monoprotic buffer approximation appears adequate for predicting the overall buffering effect on wave speed. These results validate the use of simplified reaction-diffusion frameworks for understanding enzymatic wave propagation in adaptive
DN

The demonstrated integration of out-of-equilibrium enzymatic reactions with pH-responsive crosslinking dynamics establishes a versatile platform for programmable chemomechanical transduction in soft materials. This integrated platform enables autonomous sensing of localized chemical cues, signal amplification through autocatalytic feedback regulation, spatial communication via self-sustained chemical wave propagation, and spatiotemporally programmable mechanical transitions. Future work could extend this adaptive platform by incorporating enzymatic cascade reactions to create more complex signal-processing networks, implementing signal transduction strategies to detect other types of external stimuli, integrating extrinsic regulatory mechanisms to stabilize excitable states and extend wave-propagation distances, or coupling chemical and mechanical transitions to other functional outputs such as controlled release of small molecules, autonomous self-healing, or adaptive mechanical actuation. The reversible nature of CaEDTA-alginate crosslinks further enables the integration of reversible autonomous mechanical transitions, allowing adaptive systems to be reset and re-triggered multiple times provided chemical fuel is continuously supplied and waste removed. Overall, this work demonstrates how reaction-diffusion dynamics and reversible crosslinking chemistry can be orchestrated to create hydrogel-based materials with life-like capabilities, providing insights into relevant design principles for advancing truly intelligent synthetic systems capable of autonomous and programmable structural transitions.

Conclusions

We have developed adaptive hydrogels capable of autonomous spatiotemporal mechanical transitions by integrating a pH-modulating enzyme,
GOx
, into a pH-responsive
PAM
-alginate
DN
hydrogel. This autonomous material system harnesses chemical energy from out-of-equilibrium enzymatic reactions to drive programmable spatiotemporal soft-to-stiff transitions through localized chemical signal sensing, amplification and propagation. The enzymatic reaction-diffusion process generates self-sustained chemical waves with propagation speeds tunable between
1544\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{min}}^{-1}
through systematic control of enzymatic kinetics and system’s buffering capacity, following predictable power-law scaling relationships with
GOx
and
Fi
concentrations, and inverse scaling with CaEDTA concentration. Crucially, the adaptive system achieves chemomechanical transduction by converting propagating chemical signals into traveling mechanical stiffening fronts. This transduction occurs through a multi-step cascade, consisting of enzymatically-induced pH decrease, pH-responsive
\text{Ca}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2+}}
release from CaEDTA complexes,
\text{Ca}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2+}}
diffusion to alginate chains, and progressive increase of reversible crosslinking bonds. The mechanical front propagates at approximately
4\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{min}}^{-1}
, which is 5 times slower than the chemical wave, revealing the slower kinetic timescales of
\text{Ca}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2+}}
-mediated crosslinking that govern the transduction process. This chemomechanical coupling drives autonomous material stiffening, increasing the shear modulus by up to 2.1-fold (from
2.14.5\text{\,}\mathrm{kPa}
) and establishing mechanical gradients that persist for days due to slowed
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
diffusion within the strongly buffered
DN
hydrogel. By integrating chemical sensing, autocatalytic signal amplification, wave-mediated signal transmission, and chemomechanical transduction within a single programmable material system, this work establishes design principles for the next generation of autonomous, adaptive materials with potential applications in adaptive soft robotics, autonomous sensing platforms, and intelligent biomedical devices.

Preparation of Double Network Hydrogels

To investigate homogeneous enzymatically driven self-stiffening (

To ensure easy demolding, the mold surfaces were sprayed with a release agent (Smooth-On, Universal Mold Release) and then covered with talcum powder (Puder, Penaten Baby). Excess talcum powder was subsequently removed by brushing and blowing with compressed air. For quasi-static tensile tests, the mold was designed as a slab large enough to fit at least six dogbone-shaped samples with a gauge section measuring
21\text{\times}2\text{\times}2\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}

Tensile Tests

Quasi-static uniaxial tensile testing was performed on adaptive and non-adaptive

The mechanical properties were calculated for each sample individually and then averaged for each condition tested (

Tracking Mechanical Waves using Indentation

Set up:
The mechanical wave was tracked using indentation, which allows the non-destructive measurement of mechanical properties with spatiotemporal resolution (
Figure 6
).The indentation measurements were performed using a texture analyzer (TA.XTplus, Stable Micro Systems) equipped with a
500\text{\,}\mathrm{g}
load cell (Stable Micro Systems) and a
1.5\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
radius spherical probe (1/8" diameter, TA-8A, Texture technologies). A manually adjustable x-y stage with
10\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
resolution was mounted on the texture analyzer to allow precise and repeatable spatially resolved measurements along the longitudinal direction of the samples. At the start of each spatiotemporal set of measurements, the probe height was set to zero in an empty sample container, which was subsequently filled with mineral oil to host the sample. Three reference sample variations were tested: triggered samples without substrate and mediator (diffusive fronts), untriggered samples with substrate and mediator (spontaneous autocatalytic enzymatic reaction), and untriggered samples without substrate and mediator. All samples remained stationary throughout the experiment. Each indentation measurement was initiated with the probe at a height of
2.7\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
, preventing mechanical contact with the sample. Next, the probe was moved
1.5\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
downwards at a speed of
0.5\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
, before returning to its original position. For each sample, a baseline measurement was taken (
t=$0\text{\,}\mathrm{min}$
) by indenting the hydrogel along its longitudinal axis before a
30\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
or
2\text{\,}\mathrm{min}
trigger. After removing the trigger (
t=$0\text{\,}\mathrm{min}$
), the samples were indented along their longitudinal axis at regular intervals. The measurements were grouped into a single dataset for each time interval. Each dataset was acquired during approximately
10\text{\,}\mathrm{min}
, covering up to
10\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
in distance with a spatial resolution of
200\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
. Given that the average propagation speed of the mechanical waves is
4\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{min}}^{-1}
, the wave front can only move
40\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
during the acquisition of a single time interval dataset, which is negligible considering the spatial resolution of the indentation experiment.

Quantification:
We analyzed single force-indentation depth curves by fitting the raw data using the Hertzian model for contact mechanics, with the shear modulus
G
as a fitting parameter. We assumed a Poisson’s ratio of 0.5 and a probe elastic modulus significantly higher than that of the sample. A finite-thickness correction factor
f
was introduced into the fit to account for the relatively low sample thickness compared to the contact radius
[
36
]
. Uncertainty in determining the contact point for oil-immersed samples was addressed by including two additional fitting parameters: the distance at first contact (
d_{0}
) and the force at first contact (
F_{0}
). An approximate contact point was identified using a moving standard deviation approach. Specifically, the standard deviation of the applied force was calculated over a sliding window of 9 consecutive data points. The approximate contact point was defined as the point where this standard deviation first exceeded a threshold value of
0.12\text{\,}\mathrm{mN}
. This threshold was selected empirically by analyzing its effect on the quality of regression analysis. Low values resulted in the shear modulus being strongly dependent on threshold selection, while high values resulted in a fitting window that no longer matched the Hertzian model (
F\propto d^{3/2}
). To satisfy the Hertzian approximation, which assumes small strains and contact radii, data fitting was restricted to the regime where the contact radius remained below
20\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of the actual probe radius. The final fitting function with the incorporated finite-thickness correction factor is:
F=\frac{16}{3}G\cdot R^{1/2}\cdot(d-d_{0})^{3/2}\cdot f+F_{0}
, where
G
is the shear modulus,
R
is the probe radius,
d
is the indentation depth, and
F_{0}
and
d_{0}
are the distance and force offsets at first contact, respectively. The correction factor
f=(1+1.33(a/h)+1.33(a/h)^{3})^{-1}
accounts for substrate effects in thin samples, where
a/h
is the ratio of contact radius to sample thickness. The contact radius is given by
a=\sqrt{R\cdot d}
and the effective sample thickness calculated as
h=h_{0}-d_{0}
, with
h_{0}
being the initial probe height. Finally, the spatially resolved shear moduli were plotted as a function of their corresponding longitudinal position along the hydrogel. For tracking the mechanical waves, the shear modulus profiles at each time point were fitted with sigmoidal functions, and the wavefront position was defined as the inflection point of each sigmoidal fit. The overall baseline was calculated as the mean value of all measurements performed on all samples (adaptive and non-adaptive, triggered and untriggered) The propagation speed was determined with the Theil-Sen estimator.
