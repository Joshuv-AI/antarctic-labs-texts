Symptomatic diffuse pulmonary meningotheliomatosis with persistent diffusing-capacity impairment
Authors: Thomas Christian Landry, Youjin Kim, Pascale Packia Raj, Sylvia Riley, Nicholas G Wysham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiffuse pulmonary meningotheliomatosis (DPM) is an ultrarare diffuse parenchymal lung disease in which pulmonary meningothelial-like nodules produce a miliary radiographic pattern. Most reported cases are asymptomatic and incidentally detected, and longitudinal physiologic data in symptomatic patients remain scarce. A 52-year-old postmenopausal never-smoker presented with three months of progressive exertional dyspnea. High-resolution computed tomography showed innumerable small bilateral nodules in a diffuse, random miliary distribution. A non-dedicated chest CT obtained approximately three months earlier had not described nodules. Serologic and microbiologic workup was unrevealing, and bronchoscopy was nondiagnostic. Video-assisted thoracoscopic wedge resection showed bland spindle-cell nests, and immunohistochemistry established DPM. Serial pulmonary function testing showed moderate restriction with reduced diffusing capacity and alveolar volume. During follow-up, forced vital capacity and six-minute walk distance improved, while static volumes and the diffusing-capacity deficit persisted. Severe obesity likely contributed to the restrictive defect. At reduced alveolar volume, the transfer coefficient (KCO) remained lower than expected for pure extrapulmonary restriction, raising the possibility of an intrinsic pulmonary component. However, obesity, possible pulmonary vascular disease, and other factors precluded attribution of these abnormalities to DPM alone. Management was conservative, without corticosteroids, and the disease burden remained radiographically stable. Symptomatic DPM may show functional improvement despite persistent restriction and impaired diffusing capacity. DPM should be considered among the causes of diffuse miliary opacities when imaging and serologic evaluation are nondiagnostic.Keywords: Diffuse pulmonary meningotheliomatosis, Pulmonary meningothelial-like nodules, Miliary nodules, Diffusing capacity, Surgical lung biopsy

Introduction

In 1960, Korn et al. described multiple minute pulmonary tumors resembling chemodectomas [1]. Later work disproved a neuroendocrine origin and established meningothelial differentiation, prompting the term pulmonary meningothelial-like nodules (PMLNs), recognized as incidental findings [2]. Numerous and widely distributed PMLNs can produce a miliary pattern termed diffuse pulmonary meningotheliomatosis (DPM) [3]. Most reported cases are asymptomatic and incidentally detected, with a generally indolent course [3]. Symptomatic disease nonetheless occurs, but objective and longitudinal physiologic characterization is sparse. We report a symptomatic, biopsy-proven case characterized by serial pulmonary function testing and longitudinal follow-up.

Case report

A 52-year-old postmenopausal never-smoking woman presented in March 2025 with progressive exertional dyspnea. Her dyspnea began in December 2024 after hospitalization for diabetic gastroparesis. Exercise tolerance declined until she became breathless walking a short distance on level ground.

She denied cough, hemoptysis, fever, or weight loss, and attributed intermittent night sweats to menopause. Her history included type 2 diabetes mellitus with gastroparesis and nephropathy (hemoglobin A1c 8.6%, on an insulin pump), stage 3 chronic kidney disease, hypertension, obesity, and chronic pain. She had never received hormone replacement therapy. She provided daily care for more than 150 chickens and had no known occupational inhalation exposures or known tuberculosis contacts. At evaluation she was normotensive, with oxygen saturation 98% on ambient air and a body mass index of 40 kg/m2. Lungs were clear without crackles or wheeze, with no clubbing or edema; the remainder of the examination was unremarkable.

A routine, non-dedicated chest computed tomography (CT) performed approximately three months before presentation did not describe pulmonary nodules. High-resolution CT (HRCT) at presentation revealed innumerable small bilateral nodules in a diffuse, random miliary distribution without zonal or perilymphatic predominance, ground-glass opacity, septal thickening, or honeycombing (Fig. 1).

An extensive serologic, microbiologic, and physiologic evaluation was performed to evaluate the broad miliary differential. Connective-tissue, vasculitis, myositis, and hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) serologic panels were negative, as were microbiologic studies, including Coccidioides antibody and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) bacterial, fungal, and mycobacterial cultures. A normocytic anemia (hemoglobin 10.7 g/dL) and an incidental selective IgA deficiency were the only notable laboratory abnormalities (Table 1).

Six-minute walk testing (6MWT) showed a baseline distance of 241 m (790 ft) without oxygen desaturation. Transthoracic echocardiography showed preserved biventricular systolic function (ejection fraction 59%). Bronchoscopy with BAL, transbronchial biopsy (TBBx), and endobronchial ultrasound-guided nodal sampling was non-diagnostic; the initial BAL was non-lymphocytic (2% lymphocytes, CD4:CD8 0.6), nodal samples were negative.

Video-assisted thoracoscopic wedge resection of the right upper, middle, and lower lobes was performed after the nondiagnostic bronchoscopic evaluation. Specimens from all three lobes showed randomly distributed minute pulmonary meningothelial-like nodules. Histopathology demonstrated multiple nests of bland spindle cells expanding the alveolar septa, without cytologic atypia, mitotic activity, necrosis, granuloma, or fibrosis (Fig. 2A).

Immunohistochemical staining was positive for progesterone receptor (Fig. 2B), CD56 (Fig. 2D), E-cadherin, and vimentin, with dim epithelial membrane antigen positivity (EMA, Fig. 2C). This profile matched a meningothelial-like immunophenotype [4]. A broad panel including pancytokeratin, TTF-1, p40, synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and estrogen receptor was negative. The findings established the diagnosis of DPM.

The patient was managed conservatively with comorbidity optimization, a 6-month structured pulmonary rehabilitation program, weight-reduction counseling, and acid suppression. A follow-up BAL was neutrophil-predominant and was considered most consistent with microaspiration related to gastroparesis [5]. Corticosteroids and immunosuppression were not used. Oncologic consultation found no indication for systemic antineoplastic therapy, and disease burden was monitored with interval chest CT. At follow-up, 6MWT had increased to 331 m (1085 ft) and forced vital capacity (FVC) had improved from 2.39 L to 2.59 L, while the DLCO impairment persisted and the disease remained radiographically stable.

This case was conducted and reported in accordance with the CARE guidelines; the completed CARE checklist is provided in the supplementary material.

Discussion

This symptomatic, biopsy-proven case of DPM required extensive evaluation of miliary nodules and provides, to our knowledge, the first serial pulmonary-function trajectory reported in symptomatic DPM. DPM is rare, and the reported experience remains small and dominated by asymptomatic, incidentally detected disease [6]. Symptomatic disease is nonetheless well described: the first detailed series reported five patients with dyspnea and restrictive physiology [7], and later reports documented exertional dyspnea, cough, or chest discomfort [[8], [9], [10]], while a pooled analysis found dyspnea or fatigue in seven of eleven patients [11]. Bernabeu Mora and colleagues, in this journal, diagnosed DPM by TBBx [12]. Pulmonary-function data are sparse and cross-sectional; most patients have shown normal or near-normal spirometry and DLCO [8,9]. Isolated reports describe mild restriction with low-normal DLCO [10] or isolated DLCO reduction [13], but none include serial data.

Two features distinguish the present case. First, TBBx, BAL, and nodal sampling were non-diagnostic, requiring video-assisted thoracoscopic wedge resection. Published reports have obtained diagnoses by TBBx [12,13] and, in one case, transbronchial cryobiopsy [14]. Second, serial testing over eight months showed FVC and 6MWT improving with conservative management while static lung volumes and the DLCO impairment remained. The course was indolent rather than progressive [11], so the value lies in longitudinal physiologic detail, not a claim of an aggressive phenotype.

Whether DPM represents a reactive or low-grade neoplastic process remains debated [15]; in our patient, three-lobe sampling showed no coexisting pulmonary malignancy in the sampled tissue. Progesterone-receptor expression in a postmenopausal patient who had never received hormone replacement therapy argues against exogenous hormone therapy as a necessary trigger [6].

Alternative diagnoses in the broad miliary differential (Table 3) were not supported by imaging, tissue, and serologic findings, with the random rather than perilymphatic or centrilobular nodule distribution and the diagnostic meningothelial histology the strongest discriminators. The patient's daily care of more than 150 chickens represented a substantial avian antigen exposure and kept HP in the differential diagnosis. The BAL contained only 2% lymphocytes; although a low CD4:CD8 ratio (0.6) can occur in HP, the absence of BAL lymphocytosis and the definitive alternative tissue diagnosis weighed against it [5,16].

HRCT showed randomly distributed nodules without ground-glass opacity, rather than the centrilobular ground-glass nodularity or mosaic attenuation typical of HP. The resected tissue showed diagnostic meningothelial nodules without granulomas or fibrosis. The ATS/JRS/ALAT guideline notes that serum IgG testing has suboptimal sensitivity and specificity [16]. The negative antigen-specific serum IgG panel was not used as exclusionary evidence. Rather, the nonlymphocytic BAL, the HRCT pattern, and three-lobe surgical histopathology showing DPM without granulomas or fibrosis excluded clinically significant HP as the primary explanation for this presentation.

Although only the right lung was sampled, bilateral involvement was inferred from the random radiographic distribution and uniform histology across the three resected lobes. Somatostatin receptor 2a is reported to be the most sensitive meningothelial marker but is not part of the standard immunohistochemical panel at our testing laboratory and was not performed [17].

The persistently reduced DLCO (hemoglobin-corrected 58%, 62%, and 62% predicted across the three studies) was accompanied by reduced VA and transfer coefficient (KCO) values ranging from normal to mildly reduced (z-scores +0.07 to −1.81; Table 2). Even at the lowest VA (60% predicted), KCO rose only to the population mean. In pure extrapulmonary restriction, KCO generally rises as VA falls [18]. We applied the reduced-expansion relation reported by Hughes and Pride as an approximation, using VA percent predicted as a proxy for VA relative to predicted TLC [18]. The serial VA values of 70%, 60%, and 71% predicted yielded model-estimated KCO values of approximately 124%, 138%, and 123% predicted, respectively; the observed values were 80%, 100%, and 75%. The marked shortfall between model-estimated and observed KCO suggests that reduced lung volume alone does not account for the DLCO impairment.

The VA/TLC ratio (0.85 to 0.98) argues against marked gas maldistribution. Severe obesity is associated with a higher, not lower, KCO for a given alveolar volume [19], so the observed shortfall runs opposite to the expected obesity effect and is unlikely to be explained by body habitus. However, KCO becomes less specific as VA falls [20], and the reduced-expansion calculation is approximate. The discordance therefore supports the possibility of impaired gas-transfer efficiency within the accessible lung but does not distinguish parenchymal from pulmonary vascular causes or establish DPM as the cause.

Severe obesity (body mass index 40 kg/m2) likely contributed to the restrictive defect. Obesity typically reduces functional residual capacity and expiratory reserve volume while relatively sparing total lung capacity [21]. The reduced functional residual capacity in this patient (55 to 61% predicted) is compatible with that effect.

Total lung capacity was also reduced (71 to 76% predicted). Because obesity reduces functional residual capacity and expiratory reserve volume far more than total lung capacity [21], obesity alone is an incomplete explanation for this reduction, although it cannot be excluded as a contributor. The volume-adjusted KCO pattern leaves open the possibility of an additional intrinsic process and is compatible with, but not diagnostic of, DPM contributing alongside obesity.

The diffuse nodular burden offers a plausible mechanism for an intrinsic component. Widespread interstitial nodules could reduce distensible lung volume and accessible alveolar-capillary surface area in parallel, contributing to reductions in TLC, VA, and DLCO. In miliary tuberculosis, greater radiographic disease extent correlated with lower DLCO [22], providing a physiologic analogy rather than evidence that DPM has the same mechanism. DPM lacks the inflammatory alveolitis of miliary infection, most meningothelial nodules are incidental, and no burden-physiology relationship has been established in DPM. We therefore present this mechanism as a hypothesis compatible with the KCO pattern and extensive nodular burden, not as a causal inference from this case.

The physiologic abnormalities and exercise limitation were likely multifactorial. Hemoglobin correction reduced the potential effect of anemia on DLCO, but severe obesity and possible pulmonary vascular disease remained potential contributors to the physiologic abnormalities. Deconditioning and unassessed sleep-disordered breathing may have contributed to exercise limitation. Echocardiography showed preserved biventricular systolic function; pulmonary artery systolic pressure could not be estimated because of an inadequate tricuspid regurgitant envelope. Right heart catheterization and cardiopulmonary exercise testing were not performed. Right heart catheterization could establish or exclude resting pulmonary hypertension and characterize its hemodynamics, whereas cardiopulmonary exercise testing could help phenotype the dominant exercise limitation as ventilatory or parenchymal versus circulatory or pulmonary vascular [23]. Neither test would establish DPM as the cause; without them, the extent of any DPM-specific contribution remains uncertain.

FVC and 6MWT improved during follow-up while static restriction and DLCO impairment persisted with a radiographically stable nodular burden. This dissociation is compatible with a fixed structural component but does not establish a DPM-specific physiologic mechanism. Recognizing DPM among the causes of diffuse miliary opacities and proceeding to transbronchial or surgical tissue sampling when imaging and serology are nondiagnostic can secure the diagnosis and avoid empiric immunosuppression.
