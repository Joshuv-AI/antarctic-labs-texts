An investigation into the nonconformity of homogeneous gas limit for kinetic energy density of atomic systems
Priya, Anuvab Panda, Saswata Basu, Mainak Sadhukhan

Abstract
Developing a reliable kinetic energy density functional within orbital-free density functional theory remains a long-standing challenge, particularly for atomic and molecular systems. A major difficulty lies in the absence of a systematic approach to accurately compute the kinetic energy density in such contexts. In our recent work, we introduced an analytical Green’s function-based framework to address this issue. Majority of the existing efforts to construct an approximate kinetic energy density for atomic systems uses homogeneous electron gas as the bedrock of their formalism. In this work, we have shown by using a Pöschl-Teller potential that for realistic atomic potentials such model yields improper results emphasizing the need to change the leading-order term for the quest of kinetic energy densities of atoms and molecules.



I
Introduction

Orbital-free density functional theory (OF-DFT) has become widely employed in materials science and solid-state chemistry, largely due to the development of effective kinetic energy density (KED) functionals . In solid-state systems, where electron densities are relatively homogeneous and approach the Thomas-Fermi limit, these functionals perform reliably
Xia et al. 2012
;
Huang and Carter 2010
;
Witt et al. 2018
. However, in atomic and molecular systems, the pronounced inhomogeneity of the electron density presents significant challenges. Developing an accurate approximation of KED functionals that can accurately capture both global and local electronic properties in such systems remains an open and active area of research within the orbital-free DFT framework.

For closed-shell atoms a nonlocal approximation has been developed for calculating the kinetic energy of inhomogeneous electron systems. This approach establish an explicit connection between the kinetic energy and the exchange energy using a correlation factor related to the electron pair distribution function. By avoiding reliance on finite-order gradient expansions, providing a more general and robust framework. In such system the von Weizsäcker kinetic energy term (
T_{vW}
) remains exact, and the improvements are primarly directed towards refining the Thomas-Fermi kinetic energy term (
T_{TF}
). In such framework, where the correlational function
C(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\frac{\rho_{2}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{\rho(\mathbf{r})\rho(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}-1
is used as the starting point for further approximations enables the development of improved kinetic energy functionals that preserve the exact features of
T_{vW}
while systematically enhancing the
T_{TF}
component. Such consideration leads a KE

T[\rho]=T_{vW}[\rho]+C_{TF}\int f(\mathbf{r})\rho^{5/3}(\mathbf{r})d\mathbf{r}.

(1)

The enhancement factor
f(\mathbf{r})
is an unknown function of
\rho(\mathbf{r})
. It has also been argued to show shell structure. This form of kinetic energy functional has been also employed in formulating a unified framework that combines quantum fluid dynamics with DFT
Deb and Ghosh 1982
;
Deb and Chattaraj 1989
;
Roy et al. 1999
, the resulting Deb-Chattaraj equation has been applied to study the behavior of electron densities in atoms and molecules, both under the influence of external fields and in field-free conditions
Roy et al. 1997
;
Singh and Deb 1996
;
Wadehra and Deb 2006
;
Sadhukhan and Deb 2014
.

One notable method introduced an integral formulation of density-functional theory that pioneers the use of the Suzuki-Trotter decomposition for computing many-body Green’s function
Yang 1987
. While this methods showed promise towards a systematic development of the kinetic energy density, it did not deliver the final promise that does not require orbital pictures in density functional theory.

In this backdrop, we have developed an analytical Green’s function formalism (GFF), which offers a generalized and systematic framework for an accurate approximation of kinetic energy density for atomic and molecular system. In this approach, Green’s function (GF) is the central quantity and is defined as the Laplace transform of the one particle density matrix (ODM)

G(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\beta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\epsilon_{F}e^{-\beta\epsilon_{F}}\rho_{1}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},{\epsilon_{F}})\equiv\bra{\br}e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}}\ket{\br'}.

(2)

Here
\epsilon_{F}
is the Fermi energy and
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
is Hamiltonian of the system. The auxiliary variable
\beta
is mathematically similar to the inverse temperature used in Statistical mechanics. However, in this formalism, the similarity is only notational and does not signify any physical thermodynamic temperature. The present formalism is a
0
K theory. The ODM can be retrieved from the GF via inverse Laplace transform
Parr and Yang 1994
.

\rho_{1}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},{\epsilon_{F}})=\lim_{\Gamma\to\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma-i\Gamma}^{\gamma+i\Gamma}\frac{\text{d}\beta}{\beta}e^{\beta\epsilon_{F}}G(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\beta)

(3)

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{>}
.
In contrast to approaches that rely on asymptotic limits, GFF takes a more general route by partitioning the total Hamiltonian according to different interactions present in the system.

This article introduces the analytical formalism in Section II followed by application of the formalism on model systems to show that the constant inter-electronic repulsion is inadequate for realistic atomic systems.

II
Analytical formalism

There exists previous attempts to construct a kinetic energy density functional in a systematic manner using generalized gradient approximations
Yang 1986
;
Brack et al. 1976
. In that approach, the leading-order term corresponds predominantly to the homogeneous electron gas limit. Contrastingly, in our present approach we partitioned our Green’s function based on the relative strengths of the underlying Hamiltonians. This approach is motivated by the perturbation expansion and explained next.

II.1
Partition of Green’s functions according to Hamiltonian strength

We begin by noting that the generic atomic Hamiltonian

\hat{\mathcal{H}}=\underbrace{\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}+\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}}_{\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{H}}+\hat{W},

(4)

naturally partitions into the free-particle Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
, the atomic charge (
Z
)-dependent nucleus-electron attraction potential
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}
, and interelectronic interaction potential
\hat{W}
. The sum of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
and
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}
describes a hydrogenic system with Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{H}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}+\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}
. The corresponding GFs are

G^{H}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\beta)=\bra{\br}e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{H}}\ket{\br'}

(5)

and

G^{0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\beta)=\bra{\br}e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}\ket{\br'},

(6)

respectively. For atomic systems,
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{H}
overpowers
\hat{W}

.

The interaction Green’s function
(G^{int})
is defined as the difference between total Green’s function
(G)
and the hydrogenic Green’s function
(G^{H})

G^{int}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\beta)\equiv\bra{\br}\hat{O}\ket{\br'}=G(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\beta)-G^{H}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\beta).

(7)

Here

\hat{O}=e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}}-e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{H}}.

(8)

The central challenge for our formalism is to construct a decent approximate expression for
G^{int}
which describes an universal part of the full GF having no system-dpendent term in it.

In order to get the systematic expansion of
G^{int}
we expand
\hat{O}
using Zassenhaus formula
Casas et al. 2012
;
Magnus 1954
as

\hat{O}=-e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{H}}\left(1-e^{-\beta\hat{W}}e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\commutator{\ham_H}{\hat W}}\ldots\right).

(9)

The fact that

\commutator{\ham_H}{\hat W}=\commutator{\ham_0}{\hat W}

(10)

inspired us to approximate all higher order commutators inside the parenthesis in the RHS involving
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{H}
to be replaced by
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}

leading to our first approximation

\hat{O}\approx e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{H}}e^{\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}\left(e^{-\beta(\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}+\hat{W})}-e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}\right).

(11)

Despite this approximation, the expansion of the second-order commutator represents a partial sum up to the infinite order interaction in the similar spirit of random phase approximation
Gell-Mann and Brueckner 1957
. Additionally, this approximation is qualitatively acceptable where
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}\approx\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{H}
, away from the nucleus where
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}\to 0
. Note that we have not completely replaced
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}
by
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
since the leading term of the expansion is still
G^{H}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\beta)
. Also, this approximation is consistent with the fact that
f(\mathbf{r})\to 1
away from the nucleus. We found, however, that this approximation leads to an unphysical asymmetry in the resulting GF with respect to
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
.
To minimize the effect of asymmetry we decided to use a symmetrized GF

{G}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\beta)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left[G(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\beta)+G(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\mathbf{r};\beta)\right]

(12)

as our fundamental GF where
G(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\beta)
in the RHS of the above equation is defined as

G(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\beta)={G^{H}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\beta)+\int\text{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime\prime}\bra{\br}e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{H}}\ket{\br''}\bra{\br''}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\beta^{n}}{n!}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{n}\left(e^{-\beta(\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}+\hat{W})}-e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}\right)\ket{\br'}.

(13)

Eqs.(
12
) and (
13
), taken together, are the governing equations to systematically calculate the KED. The convergent infinite sum over
n
in Eq.(
13
) guarantees a systematic scheme of approximations as desired. In our scheme, as a result, no empirical parameter would be required and further accuracies can be readily obtained by introducing higher-order terms.

III
Results and Discussion

The actual computation of kinetic energy for real atoms and molecules, via our formulation, will require the Green’s function of the three-dimensional hydrogenic system as well as accurate mean-field interelectronic repulsion potential that,in principle, contains exchange-correlation effects.
Before plunging into such full-blown
tour-de-force

\Psi_{\lambda}^{\mu}(x)=N_{\lambda}^{\mu}P_{\lambda}^{\mu}(\tanh(x))

(15)

with energy eigenvalues

III.1
Weak interaction limit

We have considered the weak-field limit since it resembles the low-density limit of the Gell-Mann-Bruckner model
Gell-Mann and Brueckner 1957
where particles move under a constant positive background leading to homogeneous particle density. As a result, the mean-field interaction potential also varies negligibly over space. To model this behavior, we have expanded the mean-field, one-dimensional potential
W(x)

Note that for the simple harmonic oscillator, the bound state limit faithfully produces the

IV
Conclusion

In summary, using the Pöschl-Teller potential which resembles atomic systems, we have shown that the homogeneous electron gas limit is fundamentally flawed starting point for development of an atomic kinetic energy density. In contrast to the simple harmonic oscillator where homogeneous electron gas still produces a qualitatively acceptable
Z
-dependence the more realistic potential produces absurd result under such situation. We also have furthered our previous work with Green’s function formulation by introducing detailed techniques to compute kinetic energy density for realistic systems. The actual computation of local and global kinetic energy density of atoms and molecules using our formalism is ongoing and require more work. However, this article highlights more clearly the reason for adoption of Hamiltonian partitioning scheme which is central to our method.
