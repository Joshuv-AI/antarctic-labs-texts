Near real-time b -value analysis for volcano traffic light alert systems and eruption forecasting
Authors: Thystere Matondo Bantidi, Kazuyoshi Z Nanjo, Takeo Ishibe, Takeshi Nishimura, Bogdan Enescu, Anscair Mukange Besa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVolcanic eruptions often result from the failure of rocks surrounding magma pathways. A decrease in b-value typically precedes such failures, indicating increased stress and eruption likelihood. Here, we analyze b-value time series of seismic data from 8 well-monitored volcanoes across various tectonic regions: Eyjafjallajökull, Fagradalsfjall, Grímsvötn, and Holuhraun (Bárðarbunga) in Iceland; Mauna Loa in Hawaii (USA); and Kirishima, Ontake, and Usu in Japan. We introduce a ‘Volcano Traffic Light Alert System (VTLAS)’ to assess priming timescales and potential improvements to near‑real‑time forecasting. We define red light as instances when the b-value drops exceed 10% below normal background levels of activity and examine whether eruptions occur within 10 days. Of the 25 identified cases, 39% are followed by eruptions, whereas 61% are not. Among the latter, 71% involve red lights without subsequent eruptions but are mostly linked to large earthquakes, and 29% show no drop before an eruption. Despite the relatively low percentages, our forecasting success rate is comparable to that reported in studies employing InSAR, GNSS deformation, or thermal anomaly analyses. This suggests that the proposed VTLAS may help anticipate impending eruptions. However, to improve reliability, we recommend combining b-value analysis with other geophysical observables to effectively distinguish volcanic unrest from imminent eruptions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at10.1038/s41598-026-50913-4.Keywords: b-value, Eruption forecasting, Stress change, Volcano Traffic Light Alert System (VTLAS), Volcanic unrestSubject terms: Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Forecasting volcanic eruptions includes assessing the likelihood of their location, timing, magnitude, and style, as well as associated hazards. This process is crucial for protecting approximately 800 million people (approximately 8.4% of the global population) living near active volcanoes1. Beyond loss of life, eruptions can cause major economic and societal disruptions, affecting air travel, transportation, regional air quality, food and water supplies, and even weather patterns2–4. Furthermore, the collapse of large volcanic cones into the sea can trigger tsunamis, as seen during the violent underwater eruptions of Anak Krakatau on December 22, 2018, and Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha’apai on January 15, 2022, which resulted in numerous human casualties and extensive damage in the affected areas.

Over the past three decades, advances in computerized analysis and higher quality observations have led to major scientific breakthroughs and successful eruption forecasts5. Notable examples include the eruptions of Mount Pinatubo in 1991 (Philippines)6, Usu in 2000 (Japan), Redoubt in 2009 (Alaska)7, Merapi in 2010 (Indonesia), Villarrica in 2014 (Chile), La Soufrière in 2021 (Saint Vincent and the Grenadines), and Bárðarbunga in 2014 (Iceland). These cases underscore the importance of adequate monitoring.

Eruption forecasting relies on identifying key precursory signals from seismic activity, gas emissions, geomorphology, geomagnetic, and thermal anomalies, and geodetic or satellite data. These signals reflect the underlying magmatic processes. Periods when these indicators deviate from long-term background levels (i.e., stable values representing normal, non-eruptive behavior) are termed “unrest.” However, unrest does not always lead to eruptions, and some eruptions occur with few or no detectable precursors, or with signals too brief or subtle to provide effective warning8–11.

Between 1990 and 2013, only 19% of eruptions worldwide were accurately forecasted through the issuance of appropriate Volcano Alert Levels, reflecting pre-eruption hazards5. Similarly, between 1989 and 2017, only 9–19% of eruptions of Alaskan volcanoes were forecasted accurately and in a timely manner based on the Alaska Volcano Observatory (AVO) warning system11.

Many volcanoes are seismically active throughout their life cycles, making seismic monitoring a cornerstone of eruption-forecasting. Volcano observatories must assess whether increased seismicity is anomalous compared to background levels and decide when to alert authorities. This process is complex and can be achieved by analyzing stress changes around the volcano. Although crustal stress cannot be measured directly, it can be inferred from the slope of the Gutenberg-Richter (G-R) magnitude-frequency relation, which can then act as a ‘stress meter’.

The G-R relation, when expressed in terms of magnitude M, is given by12 , where N is the number of earthquakes with magnitude greater than or equal to M, a describes the total number of earthquakes, whereas b known as the “b-value”, indicates the relative frequency of small to large earthquakes (higher b-value means more small events, and vice versa)12,13. Spatiotemporal variations in the b-value have been linked to various processes in the crust14, including differential stress13, thermal gradient15,16, pore pressure change17, and material heterogeneity18. In geothermal and volcanic regions, b-value helps in detecting magma storage regions15,19, changes in the stress, and assessing high fluid flow and pore pressure changes15,20–23. Changes in the b-value can be interpreted as indicators of stress release and fluid movement within hydromagmatic plumbing systems. Thus, analyzing b-value variations can help identify subsurface processes during the eruption-priming phase and enhance the ability to forecast impending eruptions.

Despite extensive research, no study has systematically analyzed long-term b-value time series across multiple volcanoes in diverse tectonic settings to evaluate the reliability of b-value-based alert levels for near-real-time eruption forecasting. Here, we use b-value time-series analysis to identify the conditions under which seismicity is most effective for eruption forecasting. We selected data from 8 well-monitored volcanoes: Eyjafjallajökull, Fagradalsfjall, Grímsvötn, and Holuhraun (Bárðarbunga) in Iceland; Mauna Loa in Hawaii; and Kirishima, Ontake, and Usu in Japan (Fig. 1). We utilized the Smithsonian Institution’s Global Volcanism Program (GVP) catalog to gather key information about the analyzed volcanoes, including their locations, eruption styles, durations, and volcanic explosivity indices (Table 1). First, we assess the completeness of each seismic catalog and compute the b-value time series. Then, we calculate the background b-value (bth). Alarms are triggered when the b-value deviates from bth, turning green if the b-value exceeds 10% of bth, yellow for a change from bth, and red if it drops 10% or more below bth. Finally, we verify whether eruptions occur within 10 days of each red light.

Results

Data quality

Periods of high seismicity, whether before, during, and after large events (earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, or swarm activity) are characterized by short-term aftershock incompleteness (STAI), which results from undetected earthquakes by the recording network24. Although identifying STAI periods can be challenging, they can be addressed by carefully examining the catalog and applying a dithering process that introduces random magnitude errors ranging from − 0.05 to 0.0525,26. Figure 2, which plots the dithered magnitude against event sequential numbers, shows low-density regions at the lower end (areas below the green lines), indicating periods of incompleteness. A complete dataset would display a uniform distribution along the horizontal axis25. These observations confirm that catalog completeness varies over time, making the use of a single magnitude threshold unsuitable for the entire catalog. Therefore, we adopt a time-varying completeness magnitude for each analysis window.

Seismicity patterns

Changes in the character, rate, and depth of seismicity are often indicative of unrest behavior within the volcanic system, reflecting processes such as rising magma pressure, structural weakening, changes in brittle deformation, and/or hydrothermal activation19. Forecasting methods that rely on seismicity generally fall into two categories: analyses of seismicity rates over time and analyses of volcanic waveforms. Here, we focus on transient increases in seismicity rates as indicators of crustal deformation around the volcanic edifice. Visual examination of daily seismicity rate (Fig. 3) suggests that, whereas most eruptions are preceded by accelerating seismicity, some are not. In terms of depth, roughly 95% of earthquakes at most volcanoes occur between 0 and 10 km (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Searching for transient variations of b-value

Details on the b-value calculation, moving window parameters, and sample sizes are provided in the Methods section. Figure 3 shows the b-value plotted against time using the b+ method. Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3 show a comparison of both Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) and b+ methods. A visual examination of the MLE and b+ results shows that they are sometimes consistent and sometimes not, though their overall trends are similar. It is well documented that b-values estimated using different methods on the same dataset can vary significantly. Previous studies have reported discrepancies between MLE and b+ estimates28–30, primarily because each method assumes a different underlying magnitude distribution. The MLE method assumes that the magnitude distribution follows the G-R law, which indicates a log-linear relationship between earthquake frequency and magnitude12. Conversely, the b+ method assumes that the distribution of magnitude differences follows a Laplace distribution, which is also related to G-R law27. However, it does not directly assume a complete G-R distribution; instead, it emphasizes the properties of these differences. For an ideal, non-truncated G-R distribution, both methods should yield the same b-value within a margin of uncertainty29. Note that the occurrence time of the last event in each window is used as the time for the average b-value (see the Methods section). Thus, the time-series starting point does not always match that of the earthquake catalog used to construct it. Conversely, when a significant amount of time has passed since the last eruption, the number of earthquakes decreases, leading to a shorter time series that may not cover the entire analyzed period (Figs. 3, 4, 6, 7, and 8).

Retrospective test of b-value at volcanoes in Iceland

Iceland lies atop a mantle plume, or “hotspot,” along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, where tectonic plates diverge. This dynamic environment produces large volumes of magma that fill the gaps in the crust, leading to frequent eruptions along the rift zone, particularly at Eyjafjallajökull, Fagradalsfjall, Grímsvötn, and Holuhraun. For example, Eyjafjallajökull, located on Iceland’s southern coast, erupted in April 2010, causing unprecedented disruptions to European air traffic and costing the aviation industry millions of dollars per day4.

Figure 3a–d illustrate the temporal variations in b-values for earthquakes associated with Eyjafjallajökull, Fagradalsfjall, Grímsvötn, and Holuhraun volcanoes from 2009 to 2023. The mean b-values estimated using the b+ method are approximately 1.026 0.046, 0.796 0.036, 0.753 0.035, 1.011 0.046, respectively. Those for MLE method are approximately 0.935 ± 0.003, 0.853 ± 0.003, 0.752± 0.035, and 0.895 ± 0.003, respectively. VTLAS movies are available in the GitHub repository (please refer to Data and Code Availability statement).

At Eyjafjallajökull, 2 confirmed eruptions occurred during the analysis period (Table 1). Both methods show a gradual increase in b-value prior to each eruption, peaking at approximately 1.4 for the b+ method (Fig. 3a) and at approximately 1.3 for the MLE method (Fig. S3). With the MLE method, the b-value constantly decreased, dropping 17% below bth one day before the first eruption on March 20, 2010, and about 35% below bth seven days before the April 14, 2010 eruption, aligning with expectations (Fig. S3). We identified 2 red lights, both followed by eruptions (i.e., two successfully forecasted eruptions), with no false alarms and no missed eruptions, yielding a forecasting success rate of 100%. The b+ method identified 2 red lights, with 1 followed by eruptions, 1 false alarm, and 1 missed eruption, suggesting a forecasting success rate of 33% (Table 2).

At Fagradalsfjall, 3 eruptions occurred during the analysis period (March 19, 2021, August 3, 2022, and July 10, 2023; Table 1). From early 2010, the b-values fluctuated around bth until the onset of unrest in late 2019. Thereafter, the b-value showed alternating increases and decreases up to the first eruption on March 19, 2021. All three eruptions were preceded by significant drops in b-values 10% below bth, occurring 20, 11, and 13 days before the eruptions, respectively, as expected (Fig. 3b). The b+ method identified 11 red lights, with 3 followed by eruptions, 8 classified as false alarms, and no missed eruptions, yielding a forecasting success rate of 27%. The MLE method indicates 9 red lights, with 3 successfully forecasted eruptions, 6 false alarms, and no missed eruptions, yielding a forecasting success rate of 33% (Table 2). The inter-eruption period (2020–2023) was characterized by intense seismicity, including 22 earthquakes with . Many false alarms coincided with the occurrence of large earthquakes, suggesting a possible causal relationship. For instance, false alarms on May 29, 2009, July 25, 2017, January 29, 2020, and February 23, 2021, are likely linked to the nearby earthquakes: M4.0 on May 29, 2009, M3.9 on July 26, 2017, M4.5 on January 31, 2020, and M4.9 on February 24, 2021, respectively.

Grímsvötn and Holuhraun, located about 26 km apart, show broadly similar seismic trends. At Grímsvötn, 1 confirmed eruption occurred during the analysis period. The b+ method shows that the b-value steadily increased from early 2010, peaking at approximately 0.91 by mid-October, before declining for about 7 months leading up to the May 21, 2011 eruption, when it dropped to about 0.73 (4% below bth). After the eruption, the b-value continued to decrease, reaching 0.54 (28% below bth) by April 2013, and then gradually recovered, fluctuating around bth for the remainder of the time series. We identified 1 red light, which was a false alarm, and 1 missed eruption, yielding a forecasting success rate of 0% (Fig. 3c). Conversely, the MLE method produced 2 red lights, 1 was followed by the 2011 eruption, and 1 false alarm, with no missed eruptions, yielding a forecasting success rate of 50%. At Holuhraun, 1 confirmed eruption occurred during the analysis period (Table 1). A red light was triggered a few days before the August 29, 2014 eruption. For both methods, we identified 1 red light followed by an eruption, with no false alarms or missed eruptions, yielding a forecasting success rate of 100% (Fig. 3d).

Retrospective test of b-value at volcanoes in Hawaii

Hawaii is one of the most volcanically and seismically active regions on Earth. Seismicity is primarily concentrated around and beneath the active summits and rift zones of the basaltic shield volcanoes Kīlauea and Mauna Loa31(Fig. 1d). In 2018, a flank eruption at Kīlauea released about one cubic kilometer of lava (the most destructive eruption in Hawaii in the past 200 years), destroying over 700 structures32. Mauna Loa, the world’s largest active volcano, erupted on November 27, 2022, after 38 years of quiescence.

Figure 3e shows the temporal variation in b-values at Mauna Loa from 2010 to 2023. The mean b-value estimated using the b+ method is approximately 1.76 ± 0.06. Similar to Grímsvötn and Holuhraun in Iceland, Kīlauea and Mauna Loa are located close to each other (about 34 km apart) and may exhibit similar seismic trends. During the analysis period, 2 confirmed eruptions occurred (one at Mauna Loa and the other at Kīlauea). We identified 4 red lights, 2 preceding eruptions (successfully forecasted eruptions, one at Mauna Loa and the other at Kīlauea), 2 false alarms, and no missed eruptions, yielding a forecasting success rate of 50%. The April 30, 2018 eruption at Kīlauea was preceded by a red light approximately five days earlier. From November 2018 to December 2020, the b-values fluctuated around the threshold (bth) and then anomalously increased in mid-2021 before declining. A red light was triggered approximately 11 days before the November 27, 2022 Mauna Loa eruption, consistent with expectations. Between the 2018 Kilauea eruption and early 2021, the b-value consistently remained in the red-light zone, likely reflecting several episodes of post-eruption unrest. Note that only a few windows satisfied our analysis criteria for the MLE method; therefore, only the results of b+ method are presented.

Retrospective test of b-value at volcanoes in Japan

Japan lies along the Pacific Ring of Fire, a 40,000 km seismically active belt encircling the Pacific Ocean, where the Pacific Plate collides with the surrounding continental plates, generating frequent earthquakes and volcanic eruptions. This zone hosts 713 Holocene volcanoes (56% of the global total)33.

Japan has 111 active volcanoes, 50 of which are continuously monitored by the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA). In recent decades, Japan has experienced several significant eruptions, including: (i) the June 3, 1991, eruption of Unzendake (Kyushu), which resulted in 43 fatalities and damaged 579 homes; (ii) the March 31, 2000 eruption of Usu (Hokkaido), which damaged infrastructures and prompted the evacuation of approximately 16,000 people; and (iii) the September 27, 2014 eruption of Ontake (Kanto), which resulted in 63 deaths and missing persons, the deadliest volcanic disaster in modern Japan.

Figure 3f shows the temporal variation in b-values at Kirishima from 2007 to 2021, whereas Fig. 3g–h depicts those for Ontake and Usu from 1997 to 2020. The mean b-values estimated using the MLE method are approximately 1.05 ± 0.07 for Kirishima, 0.83 ± 0.002 for Ontake, and 0.40 ± 0.001 for Usu. For the b+ method, these values are approximately 0.837 ± 0.04, 0.719 ± 0.034, and 0.804 ± 0.036 for Kirishima, Ontake, and Usu, respectively.

At Kirishima, 6 confirmed eruptions occurred during the analysis period: August 22, 2008; November 15, 2009; March 30, 2010; January 19, 2011; October 11, 2017, and March 1, 2018. The b+ method identified 3 red lights, 1 successfully forecasted eruption, 5 missed eruptions, and 2 false alarms. The MLE method also identified 3 red lights, 2 successfully forecasted eruptions, 1 false alarm, and 4 missed eruptions, yielding a forecasting success rate of about 29%. Red light was triggered a few days before the August 22, 2008 and March 30, 2010 eruptions (Fig. 3f). The false alarm was not associated with any earthquake.

At Ontake, 1 confirmed eruption occurred during the analysis period. The b+ method identified 4 red lights, all of which were false alarms. The MLE method produced 3 red lights, all of which were also false alarms. No successfully forecasted eruptions and the September 27, 2014 event was a missed eruption, occurring during a yellow light (missed eruption) (Fig. 3g), yielding a forecasting success rate of 0%. This eruption was preceded by about 8% drop below bth (b+ method), which did not meet the 10% threshold required to trigger an alarm. Additionally, a minor phreatic eruption (VEI = 0) in 2007 showed no clear seismic or b-value precursors. During the study period, 2 earthquakes with M4.5 occurred near the volcano, including an M5.6 event on June 25, 2017, about 9 km from the volcano’s summit which was preceded by about 40% b-value drop. These findings underscore the complexity of using b-value as a forecasting tool for volcanoes located in seismically active regions. This suggests that traffic light systems based on b-value analysis may not be universally applicable; they are effective in some cases but not in others.

At Usu, 1 confirmed eruption occurred during the analysis period. The b+ method identified 2 red lights, 1 successfully forecasted eruption, 1 false alarm, and no missed eruptions. Conversely, the MLE method produced 1 red light, 1 successfully forecasted eruption, and no false or missed eruptions. A red light was triggered a few days before the March 31, 2000 eruption (Fig. 3h). Note that the background b-value at Usu is not well constrained due to limited pre-eruptive seismicity.

Discussion

Previous studies have shown that b-values are sensitive to the choice of spatiotemporal window and catalog size (i.e., cutoff magnitude, Mcut), with smaller Mcut values yielding lower b-values28. Building on this, we examine how changes in these parameters affect our results. We begin by assessing the impact of the selection criteria by expanding the radius around the volcano summit from 10 km to 25 km and 50 km, and by increasing the window size from 300 to 500 events.

Figure 6a–h compares the temporal variations of b-values obtained using different radii with the b+ method (MLE results are shown in Supplementary Fig. S5). Overall, the trends are similar, although b-values tend to be slightly higher with a larger radius, likely due to the inclusion of more events. Conversely, a smaller radius (e.g., 10 km) results in fewer events, leading to a shorter time series that may not encompass the entire analysis period, particularly when a substantial time has elapsed since the last eruption. This effect is more pronounced with the MLE method than with the b+ method (Fig. 6a–d and h). Alert-level classifications also vary with the selected radius. In some cases, the 25- or 50 km catalogs provide more accurate classifications than a 10 km catalog, and vice versa. For example, using a 10 km catalog, the January 19, 2011 eruption at Kirishima was classified as a false alarm, whereas in the 25 km catalog, it was classified as “successfully forecasted eruptions.” Similarly, in the 25 km catalog, the November 15, 2009, and March 30, 2010, eruptions were classified as “failed eruptions” (false alarm) and a “successfully forecasted eruption,” respectively, whereas the 50 km catalog produced opposite results. Overall, a 10 km radius shows more stable results, with fewer false alarms and a higher forecasting success rate than a larger radius. This suggests that at greater distances from the volcanic edifice, seismicity is primarily influenced by tectonic activity rather than volcanic processes or both, which lowers forecasting success. For instance, using the b+ method, the forecasting success rates are 25%, 18%, and 20% for 10, 25, and 50 km catalogs, respectively.

Figure 7a–h compares the temporal variations of b-values for different window sizes using the b+ method (MLE results are shown in Supplementary Fig. S6). The b-value trends are almost similar across window sizes. Increasing the window size slightly shifts the b-value; but, in some cases, these changes are relatively small compared to the standard deviation. This is consistent with previous studies suggesting that b-values remain nearly constant when using a catalog with Mcut above the completeness threshold28. In terms of alert levels, a window with more events reduces the number of false alarms. For example, at Ontake, false alarms decreased by 50% when using a 500-event window compared to 300 events. Overall, using the b+ method yields a forecasting success rate of about 25% and 31% for 300 and 500 events catalogs, respectively .Because the true b-value is unknown and no single approach guarantees optimal accuracy, we recommend applying various methods, spatiotemporal window and/or catalog size on the same dataset to improve reliability when using b-values as indicators of impending eruptions.

The use of b-value analysis to investigate pre-eruptive processes and improve eruption forecasting is a well-established practice worldwide37–39. The mean b-values obtained in this study are generally consistent with previous findings for the same volcanoes38,40,41. Minor discrepancies likely reflect differences in catalog selection, spatiotemporal windowing, and analysis methods, all of which significantly influence b-value results42. For example, Kato et al. (2015)37 used a matched-filter catalog for the 2014 Ontake eruption and found that the b-values increased from 1.2 to 1.7 between September 11 and 16 and then dropped to 0.8 just before the eruption. We did not observe similar trends, likely due to differences in the datasets and methodology. Similar discrepancies have been observed in previous studies of various volcanoes. For example, Chiba et al. (2018)38 analyzed JMA catalog data at Kirishima and found that the b-value showed a plateau following a decrease after the 2011 eruption, whereas Yukutake et al. (2023)41, using a machine-learning-constructed catalog, observed a sharp increase.

We also examined how the choice of background b-value affects the results. Besides using the mean of all individual values, we tested defining bth as the median b-value calculated prior to an eruption, a common approach in seismology, where the median is often used before a mainshock43. Overall, the forecasting success rates remained similar (Fig. S7). For example, using the b+ method, the success rate was about 25% when bth was defined as the mean and about 31% when defined as the median.

To avoid overinterpretation, we assumed that instances where b-values drop more than 10% below bth may indicate a potentially hazardous situation (volcanic eruptions in our case)43. Across all volcanoes, we identified 26 red lights and 5 missed eruptions. Of these red lights, 12 were followed by eruptions “successfully forecasted eruptions”, yielding a forecasting success rate of about 39%. This relatively low success rate confirms that stress perturbations in volcanic fields do not always result in eruptions. However, at an active volcano, periods of quiescence can only happen by chance, and there is no universally accepted method to differentiate triggered events from background events. Thus, it is difficult to distinguish periods of unrest from imminent eruptions.

Raising false alarms has consequences: they can undermine trust between scientists and decision-makers and complicate communication strategies with communities living near volcanos. Although our results may appear somewhat ambiguous, many of these false alarms were triggered by large earthquakes (M3.5) occurring close to the volcanic edifice, suggesting a causal link. Importantly, our forecasting success rate is similar or slightly exceeds those reported in previous studies⁵˒¹¹ or similar to those using other precursors, such as InSAR (~ 46%)34, GNSS deformation (8–30%)³⁵ and thermal anomalies (~ 23%)36. These findings suggest that monitoring b-value changes has great potential for near-real-time eruption forecasting. Our findings align with the inherent complexity of volcanic processes, in which various mechanisms influence stress. During early unrest, deep high-magnitude earthquakes may occur due to temporary stress increases from magma intrusion. As magma ascends, the rising pore pressure reduces the effective stress, leading to more frequent shallow low-magnitude earthquakes and an increase in the b-value44,45. Moreover, initial magma intrusion zones are often highly fractured, producing anomalously high b-values38,46,47. Conversely, the occurrence of large earthquakes (M ≥ 4.5) during the seismic sequence may reflect stress relaxation near the magma reservoir, contributing to lower b-values45.

On the other hand, our dataset includes both phreatic and magmatic eruptions, which are driven by different mechanisms. Magmatic eruptions occur when magma ascends through the crust, whereas phreatic eruptions are mainly caused by hydrothermal processes48–50, and magma serves primarily as a heat source rather than a driving force. Some eruptions may exhibit characteristics of both types (i.e., phreatomagmatic)49,50. In the absence of magma ascent, significant changes in stress, microfracturing, or other processes that could induce transient changes in b-value would not be expected. Nonetheless, 3 of the 8 phreatic or phreatomagmatic eruptions in our catalog (two at Kirishima and one at Usu) were preceded by a decrease in b-value. Excluding phreatic eruptions from the analysis could improve the forecasting rate, however, distinguishing purely phreatic activity from eruptions with some magmatic involvement remains difficult, particularly at volcanoes such as Kirishima, which exhibit both eruption types (Table 1).

Magma can ascend only when its internal pressure exceeds the minimum principal compressive stress, which may result from increased magma supply, vesiculation during crystallization, or dynamic stress changes induced by seismic waves22. Because the interplay of processes leading to eruptions is complex9,22, a simple decrease in the b-value or changes in the seismicity rate may not be the only effective diagnostic tool for deciphering an impending eruption. Thus, improving forecasting requires integrating b-value and seismicity rate analyses with expert judgment and a deep understanding of how stress interacts with other indicators, such as geodetic deformation, degassing, and thermal anomalies linked to magma overpressure51.

Finally, we discuss the sensitivity of the b-value to differences in the magnitude scale. We converted each catalog into a uniform Das magnitude scale (Mwg)52,53. The results show that Mcut differs substantially. The local catalog magnitudes show higher Mcut values (Fig. 2) than when using Mwg (Fig. S8). Next, we compared the b-values from the local catalog magnitude scales with Mwg. We found that, in general, the b-value was quantitatively lower for Mwg, whereas the qualitative trends remained consistent (Fig. 8). In terms of alert levels, the results are closely consistent with a forecasting success rate of about 41% for the uniform Mwg magnitude compared to 40% for the local catalog magnitude.

Figure 9 summarizes all the forecasting success rates for these parameters when using the b+ method (almost similar results were obtained using the MLE method). Several sources of uncertainty can lead to non-physical variations in b-values, including catalog inhomogeneities due to different magnitude estimation procedures, variability in catalog completeness, and STAI effects. In this study, great care was taken to minimize these biases. We employed the b+ method27 which is resilient to transiently incomplete catalogs, implemented a dithering process25,26 to account for time-varying completeness, and standardized all catalogs to a uniform Mwg magnitude scale53,54 to address magnitude inhomogeneities. Together, these measures suggest our results are robust.

Summary of the forecast performance analysis of the Volcano Traffic Light Alert System (VTLAS) using various criteria: (a) radius; (b) windows size; (c) background b-value computation, and (d) local catalog magnitude (L.C.M) versus Mwg (MLE & b+ combined).

In conclusion, VTLAS performance was sensitive to catalog selection, spatiotemporal windowing, analysis methods and differences in magnitude scale, and varied widely among volcanoes. This suggests that whereas a VTLAS based solely on b-value changes might be effective in some cases, it may not be universally applicable. Further sensitivity analyses, independent validation, and formal hypothesis testing are required. Despite these limitations, the proposed VTLAS has operational potential. Therefore, we recommend that volcano observatories with adequate seismic network coverage consider integrating this approach with other geophysical observables to support decision-making during volcanic unrest.

Method

This section describes the data and methodological approach used in the present study.

Seismic data

Four main types of seismic events are typically recorded at volcanoes: high-frequency earthquakes, low-frequency earthquakes, explosion earthquakes, and volcanic tremors (continuous shaking of the ground). Terminology varies, and there is no universal consensus on the underlying physical processes. Volcanic earthquakes generally (1) occur in swarms rather than mainshock–aftershock sequences, (2) have smaller magnitudes (typically M < 5), and (3) are located beneath or near the eruption site. In this study, we did not apply any priori earthquake classification.

We selected volcanoes with some of the most comprehensive monitoring networks, enabling high-resolution seismic data due to dense station coverage. These include Mauna Loa in Hawaii (USA), Eyjafjallajökull, Fagradalsfjall, Grímsvötn, and Holuhraun (Bárðarbunga) in Iceland, and Kirishima, Ontake, and Usu in Japan. These volcanoes have recorded some of the most significant eruptions of the last two decades based on their scientific importance, societal impact, and monitoring context. Our analysis focused on earthquakes occurring within a horizontal radius of 10 km (a fifth of the distance required for an eruption to trigger moderate earthquakes and vice versa)54 from the volcano summit and at hypocentral depths shallower than 30 km. Because earthquake selection affects catalog size and potentially b-value changes, we examined the impact of varying the search radius.

For Mauna Loa, we used seismic data reviewed by the Hawaiian Volcano Observatory (HVO) and obtained from the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS), covering January 1, 2010, to December 30, 2023. The USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory (HVO) (hereafter, USGS-HVO) has been conducting routine monitoring of the Kīlauea Volcano and the Island of Hawaii since 191255. On October 15, 2006, two damaging earthquakes (M6.7 and M6.0) struck the island within seven minutes of each other, saturating USGS-HVO’s monitoring system. These events highlighted the need to improve the network’s capability to handle large volumes of earthquakes. In response, the USGS-HVO network was modernized in April 2009 with a fully digital seismic telemetry system supporting a broader range of sensors and enabling real-time recordings of earthquakes ranging from M < 1 to M > 8. Thus, here we use data recorded after modernization, specifically from 2010 to 2024. USGS-HVO posts automated real-time earthquake hypocenter information within 4 min and reviewed solutions for significant events within 10 min. The catalog is considered complete above magnitude Mc 2.0, with hypocentral uncertainties of approximately 2 km horizontally and 4 km vertically. Magnitude uncertainty is ± 0.2, and both amplitude (Ml) and duration (Md) magnitudes are used for some events. To ensure consistency, we searched events that show both Ml and Md magnitude in the International Seismological Centre (ISC) catalog for the entire Hawaiian Island and derived a linear regression between Ml and Md (Supplementary Fig. S9) using least squares. Using this, we converted Md to equivalent Ml values for events that were recorded without Ml.

To analyze eruptions in Iceland, we used data supplied by the Icelandic Meteorological Office (IMO) for the period 2009–2023. Seismic activity across Iceland has been monitored automatically since the middle of 1991 by the South Iceland Lowland (SIL) network, a denser and digital national seismic network operated by the Icelandic Meteorological Office (IMO) (Veðurstofa Íslands (VI) network). Earthquakes are automatically detected, located, and preliminary magnitudes are calculated. The events are then manually reviewed and revised as required. The IMO catalog contains detailed records of origin time, hypocenter location (latitude, longitude, depth), magnitude (in a “local” moment magnitude scale, MlW), seismic moment, and focal mechanisms (for larger events). Earthquakes with magnitudes down to MlW0 are reported, and the catalog is almost complete for earthquakes above M0.556. The catalog is openly available on the IMO website. However, only events that occurred after 2009 can be directly downloaded. Therefore, only events that occurred from 2009 onward were used in this study. We focused on significant volcanic eruptions in Iceland between 2009 and 2024, notably at the Eyjafjallajökull, Fagradalsfjall, Grímsvötn, and Holuhraun (Bárðarbunga) volcanoes.

Earthquake data for Kirishima, Ontake, and Usu volcanoes were sourced from the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) unified earthquake catalog for 1997–2020 and 2006–2020, respectively. The JMA has been recording earthquakes since 1923; however, the agency implemented a real-time data processing system in 199757. Thus, this study utilizes data from 1997 onward. Previous studies using the JMA catalog57,58 have indicated that the catalog is generally complete for Mc values between 0.5 and 1 in the Kyushu, Chubu, and Hokkaido districts, where the Kirishima, Ontake, and Usu volcanoes are located, respectively.

For each of the above volcanoes, we targeted confirmed eruptions (Volcanic Explosivity Index, VEI ≥ 0) as reported in the Global Volcanism Program database (Smithsonian Institution). Table 1 lists the analyzed eruptions along with their VEI, duration and eruption type.

Note that catalogs were used with their corresponding magnitudes (i.e., JMA magnitude for Japan, Ml for Hawaii, and the “local” moment magnitude scale, MlW for Iceland). Therefore, we discuss the sensitivity of the b-value owing to different magnitude scales. To this end, we converted each catalog’s local magnitude into a uniform Das magnitude scale (Mwg)52,53. The Mwg scale has proven efficient in correcting the inherent errors of the magnitude (M) scale for small and moderate earthquakes, as used in this study52,53.

Magnitude of completeness, Mc

Estimating seismicity parameters requires a careful selection of a spatiotemporal range that accurately reflects the characteristics of the seismic sequence under consideration. To achieve this, it is essential to first determine the magnitude of completeness (Mc). The estimated Mc establishes the lower magnitude bound for seismic sequences and sets the desired lower cut-off magnitude. We utilize the maximum curvature criterion59 and incorporate a corrective factor of 0.2 (MAXC + 0.2)59–61. This well-established method effectively accounts for the variability in the number of earthquakes within the catalog. The method was implemented through ZMAP Software62.

Constructing b-value time series

The b-value time series is calculated using a sliding window approach that accounts for time-varying catalog completeness68. Here, we use a sliding window through the catalog shifted by five events per time. We considered the first 300 events as the minimum number of events in the sample to calculate the first average b-value14,68–72; then, by moving the time window by 5 events, we considered a second sample of 300 events (numbers, 6–305) to calculate the second average b-value, and so on until all events in the catalog are exhausted. Based on these refined sub-catalogs, we estimate the b-values. To ensure a good fit, we calculate the b-values only for sub-catalogs where the total number of events (with magnitude greater than Mc) exceeds 50. The occurrence point of the last event in each sample is considered as the time for the average b-value68,73,74. In this manner, we obtained the average b-value versus time.

Setting up the forecasting method

After generating the b-value time series, we calculate the background reference b-value (bth) as the mean of all individual values (we discuss the potential implications of this definition). The bth represents the b-value under the assumption that a region is characterized by “normal” levels of seismicity. If the b-value in the time series deviates from bth, an alarm is triggered. The alarm turns green (green light) if the b-value exceeds 10% of bth (where no eruption is expected), yellow (yellow light) for a change from the bth (indicates unrest behavior), and red (red light) if it drops 10% or more below bth, indicating a potentially hazardous situation (i.e., mitigation actions must be taken)43. Note that the 10% drop arbitrary rule originates from earthquake triggering studies⁴³ and has not yet been established for volcanic eruptions where decisions must also be made in real time. Here, we adopt this 10% threshold as a starting point and then increase it to 20% to explore potential implications. Next, we examine whether each red light is followed by a volcanic eruption. Previous studies have shown that seismicity often accelerates from several days to 10 days before andesitic eruptions75,76 and from minutes to hours before basaltic eruptions77. However, to avoid biases related to eruption type, we use a uniform =10 days alarm window regardless of whether the eruption is basaltic, andesitic, or other. If the b-value remains below the threshold in consecutive alarm windows, only the first alarm is counted to avoid repeated alerts. An eruption is considered successfully predicted (“successfully forecasted eruption”) if it occurs within one or more red light windows, and a failure (“missed eruption”) if it occurs outside the red-light windows (yellow or green). A red light not followed by an eruption is considered a “false alarm” or “failed eruption”. We first assess the performance of each method individually and then evaluate their combined performance.

In the Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program, the “start date” of volcanic unrest or eruptions is defined as either the earliest known date of significant unrest or the date of the first explosive ejection of fragmental material, the effusion of liquid lava, or both11. Additionally, any eruptive activity occurring within 90 days of previous activity is considered part of the same eruption11. A hiatus of 90 days or more indicates the beginning of a new eruption11. Therefore, all alarms that were triggered 90 days after the end of each eruption were not considered as signs of a new eruption; instead, they were considered as an inherent part of that eruption. Furthermore, the b-value recovery time for a volcanic system is unknown; therefore, by analogy to the eruption time, we assume that a b-value decreases within 90 days after a previous decrease to result from the same activity. A hiatus of 90 days or more indicates the start of a new activity. It should be noted that this is a simplification; the recovery time of the b-value is much more complex and can span several years. One might argue that the 90-day rule may not be applied consistently; however, it is challenging to accurately determine when an eruption has ended. Thus, this value should be considered as an average.
