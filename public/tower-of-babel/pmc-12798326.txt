Effect of position within a blank and thermo-mechanical aging on fracture force and failure mode of strength-gradient multilayer zirconia crowns
Authors: Surangkana Imaemporn, Onauma Angwaravong, Thidarat Angwarawong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEThis study aimed to investigate the effects of blank position and thermo-mechanical aging on the fracture force of strength-gradient multilayer (SGM) zirconia crowns.MATERIALS AND METHODSEighty premolar SGM zirconia crowns (KATANA™ Zirconia YML) were assigned to eight groups (n = 10), based on four blank positions (1, 2, 3, and 4 mm below the top edge) and two conditions (with and without thermo-mechanical aging). Aging simulated five years of clinical service through 6,000 thermocycles (5℃ – 55℃) and cyclic fatigue (50 N, 1.2 Hz, 1,200,000 cycles). Fracture force was measured using a universal testing machine. Failure modes and fractographic features were examined. Data were assessed using two-way ANOVA, Bonferroni post hoc tests, independent t-tests, and Spearman correlation (P < .05).RESULTSFracture force was significantly influenced by both blank position and thermo-mechanical aging. Crowns from the 1 – 2 mm regions demonstrated significantly lower fracture force and more severe failure modes (Types I – V), while those from the 3 – 4 mm regions showed significantly higher fracture force and milder modes (Types I – III). Thermo-mechanical aging reduced fracture force in all groups, supported by fractographic analysis. A strong negative correlation existed between position depth and failure severity.CONCLUSIONBoth blank position and thermo-mechanical aging significantly affected fracture force and failure behavior. Lower blank positions provide better mechanical performance and are recommended for posterior restorations. Conversely, upper blank positions—offering improved translucency—are suitable for anterior restorations where esthetics are prioritized. These findings offer clinically relevant guidance for optimizing strength and appearance in zirconia crown fabrication.Keywords: Fracture force, Fracture mode, Strength-gradient multilayer zirconia, Thermo-mechanical aging, Zirconia crown

INTRODUCTION

Today, the demand for esthetics is increasing, with all-ceramic restorations becoming widely used. Many types of ceramics are used in dentistry with varying optical and mechanical properties. In cases requiring full-mouth rehabilitation, such as patients presenting with generalized severe tooth wear resulting from parafunctional habits like bruxism and clenching, the restoration should achieve both superior esthetic outcomes and long-term functional integrity.1

Zirconia is the material of choice due to its esthetic appearance, high flexural strength, and high survival rate in patients with extensive tooth wear.1,2,3 However, early forms of zirconia exhibited limited translucency and were highly opaque, making them unsuitable for use in highly esthetic areas.4 Initially, zirconia was used as a framework material and veneered with feldspathic porcelain or glass-ceramic. However, this approach was associated with complications such as veneer chipping and delamination.5 To address these issues, monolithic zirconia restorations were introduced, eliminating the need for veneering. The first generation of monolithic zirconia, 3 mol% yttria-stabilized tetragonal zirconia polycrystal (3Y-TZP), was developed by modifying the sintering temperature to enhance translucency. Despite these improvements, its translucency remained inferior to that of lithium disilicate, and the modifications resulted in reduced flexural strength compared to conventional 3Y-TZP.6 In response, manufacturers began modifying zirconia’s composition and microstructure to further improve esthetic outcomes, which led to the development of the third generation of monolithic zirconia, including 4 and 5 mol% yttria-partially stabilized zirconia (4Y-PSZ and 5Y-PSZ, respectively). These materials have increased yttria content to promote the formation of the cubic zirconia phase (c-ZrO2) over the tetragonal zirconia phase (t-ZrO2). The cubic crystal structure is more isotropic and possesses a larger grain size than the tetragonal structure. As a result, light scattering at grain boundaries is reduced, and incident light is emitted more uniformly in all spatial directions, thereby enhancing translucency compared to 3Y-TZP.6,7,8 However, this increase in translucency came at the expense of reduced mechanical strength, making 4Y-PSZ and 5Y-PSZ less suitable for long-span restorations.6,7,8

Earlier monolithic zirconia restorations also exhibited a uniform color throughout the blank, lacking the polychromatic characteristics of natural teeth. To address this, color-gradient multilayer zirconia was developed. These blanks simulate the natural shading of dentition through layered pigmentation but consist of a single zirconia composition across all layers—typically 4Y-PSZ or 5Y-PSZ—resulting in uniform mechanical properties, specifically lower flexural strength compared to 3Y-TZP.9 To overcome this limitation, the fourth generation of zirconia was introduced: strength-gradient multilayer (SGM) zirconia.10 These materials combine different types of zirconia within a single blank: predominantly 3Y-TZP in the dentin or body region for enhanced strength, and 4Y-PSZ or 5Y-PSZ in the incisal or occlusal region to replicate the translucency of natural enamel.10 This structural gradient enables SGM zirconia to achieve both high esthetics and mechanical performance, making it suitable for full-arch esthetic restorations.

With its favorable balance of strength and esthetics, SGM zirconia has become widely used in clinical practice. Nonetheless, studies on how the position within the blank affects the mechanical properties of SGM zirconia remain limited and have yielded controversial results. Li et al.11 reported significant differences in the fracture force of single crowns fabricated from different positions within multilayer zirconia blanks (Upcera Meiying Multilayer Zirconia) after fatigue loading without thermocycling. Several studies have also reported significant differences in the flexural strength and fracture toughness of cylindrical or bar-shaped specimens made from different layers of SGM zirconia, evaluated before and after thermocycling, under hydrothermal conditions, without artificial aging, or following thermo-mechanical aging.12,13,14,15,16,17,18 In contrast, numerous studies have reported no significant differences in the fracture force of three- or four-unit fixed partial dentures (FPDs) made from different positions within SGM zirconia blanks after thermo-mechanical aging.19,20,21

Given the conflicting results of previous studies, there is ongoing debate regarding how the position within the blank and thermo-mechanical aging influence the mechanical properties of SGM zirconia restorations. Additionally, previous studies have predominantly focused on bar- or disc-shaped specimens subjected to monotonic loading in simplified models, which do not accurately represent the structure of clinical restorations or predict their clinical performance. Moreover, only a few studies have investigated the fracture force of crown restorations; most have examined FPDs, particularly in the pontic area,19,20,21 where the highest tensile stress is concentrated in the connector region, making it especially susceptible to fracture.22 Consequently, this in vitro study aimed to evaluate the effects of the position within the blank and thermo-mechanical aging on the fracture force of SGM zirconia crowns. The first null hypothesis was that there is no difference in fracture force among the different positions within the blank. The second null hypothesis was that there is no difference in fracture force between crowns with and without thermo-mechanical aging.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The required sample size was calculated based on the data from a previous study by Strasser et al.21 using the G*Power program (Version 3.1.9.4; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany), considering a significance level (α) at .05 and a power of 80%. The minimum sample size was estimated as at least seven specimens per group, with a total sample of 54 specimens (N = 54). However, this study included ten specimens per group (n = 10), with a total of 80 specimens (N = 80).

Eighty SGM zirconia crowns were assigned to eight groups based on two factors: four different positions within the blank (1, 2, 3, and 4 mm below the upper edge, labeled as 1, 2, 3, and 4) and two aging conditions—without (N) and with thermo-mechanical aging (T)(Table 1, Fig. 1A).

A maxillary left first premolar dentoform tooth with an occluso-cervical height of 8.5 mm and a root length of 12.5 mm (Frasaco; Frasaco GmbH, Tettnang, Germany) was scanned using an intraoral scanner (TRIOS 5; 3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark) as a pre-preparation scan. The tooth was then prepared as an abutment for all-ceramic crowns using a high-speed air turbine handpiece (Alegra; W&H, Bürmoos, Austria) with a 2 mm occlusal reduction and a 1 mm round chamfer finishing line located 1 mm above the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ), with a 6-degree convergence angle, using a round-end tapered diamond bur (Diamond 850G Chamfer D16; Jota AG, Rüthi, Switzerland). The prepared tooth was rescanned using the same intraoral scanner (TRIOS 5; 3Shape) to create a resin die fabrication template. The scanned data were imported into a computer-aided design (CAD) program (Dental Designer, version 2021; 3Shape) and printed using a three-dimensional (3D) photopolymer resin (Fig. 1B) (Denture Teeth UV; Heygears, Guangzhou, China) with a 3D printer (Ultracraft A2D; Heygears, Guangzhou, China). After removing the supporting struts, all resin dies were washed in 99% isopropyl alcohol for 10 minutes and cured for 20 minutes in a post-curing unit (Ultracraft PCU 3.0; Heygears, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

To simulate the periodontal ligament, the root was coated with a 0.2 mm thick layer of die spacer (Spacer Varnish Gold; Bredent, Bayern, Germany).23,24,25 Each resin die was then embedded 2 mm below the CEJ in self-cured acrylic resin (Ortho-jet; Lang Dental, Wheeling, IL, USA) within a polyvinyl chloride cylinder using a surveyor.24 The die spacer was then removed from all resin dies and replaced with a polyvinyl siloxane impression material (Honigum Light; DMG, Hamburg, Germany).26

All zirconia crowns were designed using a pre-preparation scan as a template, and restoration thicknesses were confirmed using CAD program. The cement space was set to 50 μm.27,28 SGM zirconia blanks with an 18 mm thickness (KATANA™ Zirconia YML; Kuraray Noritake Dental, Tokyo, Japan) (Table 2) were milled at four different positions (Figs. 1A and 1C) using a milling machine (X-Mill 500 Plus; XTCERA, Guangdong, China). Next, all zirconia crowns were sintered in a sintering furnace (LHT 01/16 Turbo Fire; Nabertherm GmbH, Lilienthal, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The crowns were then ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water for 10 minutes and air dried. Finally, a glazing paste (CERABIEN™ ZR FC Paste Stain; Kuraray Noritake Dental, Tokyo, Japan) was applied and fired following the manufacturer’s instructions.

To evaluate the fit of each zirconia crown, a silicone rubber fit-checking paste (Fit Checker; GC Dental, Tokyo, Japan) was applied to the intaglio surface of the crown, which was then seated onto the resin die. The crown was then removed for visual examination. If the silicone layer was excessively thin (≤ 0.1 mm), the crown was adjusted using a zirconia finishing and polishing kit (ZiLMaster Finishing & Polishing Kit HP; Shofu, Kyoto, Japan).

Following the evaluation, the zirconia crowns were cleaned, and the intaglio surfaces underwent surface treatment by airborne-particle abrasion with 100 μm Al2O3 at 0.1 MPa. Ceramic primer (CLEARFIL™ CERAMIC PRIMER PLUS; Kuraray Noritake Dental, Tokyo, Japan) was then applied, followed by air blow-drying. Next, the zirconia crowns were fixed onto the resin dies using self-etch adhesive resin cement (Panavia V5; Kuraray Noritake Dental, Tokyo, Japan). They were initially placed under finger pressure; excess cement was removed, and then light-cured for 10 seconds using a light-emitting diode curing unit at a wavelength of 450 – 480 nm (SmartLite Pro, Dentsply Sirona, Long Island City, NY, USA) under a constant load of 50 N. The crowns were left undisturbed for 3 minutes to ensure final polymerization, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Fig. 1D).

To simulate five years of clinical function, thermo-mechanical aging19,29,30 was performed on all T groups (Groups 1T, 2T, 3T, and 4T). The cemented crowns underwent thermocycling for 6,000 cycles in distilled water at temperatures of 5℃ and 55℃, with a dwell time of 30 seconds, using a thermocycling machine (HWB332R, CWB332R, TC301; King Mongkut’s Institute of Technology, Bangkok, Thailand). Cyclic fatigue testing was then conducted using a dynamic testing instrument (ElectroPuls E1000; Instron, Norwood, MA, USA) by placing a 3 mm diameter metal sphere at the center of the occlusal surface, ensuring contact with both the palatal incline of the buccal cusp and the buccal incline of the palatal cusp of the tooth with a 50 N load at a frequency of 1.2 Hz for 1,200,000 cycles.

The fracture force test was conducted by placing a 3 mm diameter metal sphere at the center of the occlusal surface, ensuring contact with both the palatal incline of the buccal cusp and the buccal incline of the palatal cusp of the tooth (Fig. 1E). A universal testing machine (LR30/k; Lloyd, Leicester, England) was used at a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min.31 A compressive axial load was applied until specimen fracture occurred, and the maximum fracture force value (Newtons, N) was recorded.

The failure mode of the SGM zirconia crowns was classified into five groups according to the Burke classification32 (Table 3): Modes I, II, III, and IV indicated restorable failures, while Mode V indicated an unrestorable or catastrophic failure.26 Fractographic analysis was performed to identify the fracture origin, fracture patterns, and direction of crack propagation33 using a scanning electron microscope (SU3800; Hitachi, Osaka, Japan). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Fracture force is reported as the mean and standard deviation. Two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) was conducted to evaluate the effects of the position within the blank, thermo-mechanical aging, and their interaction on fracture force. Multiple group comparisons were performed using Bonferroni post hoc tests. An independent t-test was used to compare fracture force at each position between aged and non-aged groups. Correlation analyses were conducted using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (rs) to assess the relationship between the position within the blank and the mode of failure. A P-value of < .05 was considered statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

This study revealed that both the position within the blank and thermo-mechanical aging significantly influenced the fracture force of SGM zirconia crowns, rejecting both null hypotheses.

The SGM zirconia material (KATANA™ Zirconia YML) evaluated in this study comprises four distinct layers: enamel and Body 1 – 3. Although the manufacturer’s documentation does not specify the exact composition of each layer, several studies have investigated their phase and elemental characteristics. Strasser et al.,13 using X-ray diffraction (XRD), reported that Body 3 consists of 3Y-TZP and possibly 4Y-PSZ, the enamel layer contains 5Y-PSZ, and the intermediate layers (Body 1 and 2) are mixtures of 3Y-TZP, 4Y-PSZ and 5Y-PSZ. Labetić et al.34 confirmed a gradient in yttria content increasing from Body 3 (3Y-TZP) to enamel (5Y-PSZ), with Powder XRD showing phase transitions: 3Y-TZP contained 68.4 wt% t-ZrO2 and 31.6 wt% c-ZrO2, while 5Y-PSZ contained 39.2 wt% t-ZrO2 and 60.8 wt% c-ZrO2; intermediate layers exhibited transitional compositions. Similarly, Inokoshi et al.,14 using X-ray fluorescence (XRF), reported yttria contents increasing from 3.9 to 5.6 mol% from Body 3 to enamel, with corresponding XRD results showing a decrease in t-ZrO2 and an increase in c-ZrO2 toward the top. Despite minor discrepancies among studies, a consistent trend is evident: yttria content increases from bottom to top, resulting in a gradual shift from t-ZrO2 to c-ZrO2 phases.13,14,34 Microstructural analysis also showed smaller grain sizes in Body 2 and 3, and larger grains in Body 1 and enamel. This gradient in yttria content correlates with variations in phase composition, translucency, microstructure, and mechanical properties.13,14,34,35

By using SGM zirconia crowns that replicate clinical restorations, this study demonstrated that the variation in yttria content across these layers is associated with differences in fracture force and failure modes of SGM zirconia crowns. A lower yttria content is associated with higher fracture force and less severe failure patterns. For the positions 3 and 4 mm below the upper edge of the blank (Groups 3N, 3T, 4N, and 4T), where the occlusal surface was predominantly composed of 4Y-PSZ and the cervical area was predominantly composed of 3Y-TZP,13,35 the crowns exhibited higher fracture force and less severe failure patterns (limited to Modes I to III). In contrast, for the positions 1 and 2 mm below the upper edge (Groups 1N, 1T, 2N, and 2T), where the occlusal surface primarily consisted of 5Y-PSZ and the cervical area contained little to no 3Y-TZP, 13,35 lower fracture force and more catastrophic failure patterns (Modes IV and V) were observed. These findings are supported by the Spearman correlation analysis, which indicated a substantial negative correlation between the position in the blank and failure severity—crowns fabricated farther from the upper edge exhibited less severe failure modes—as well as by the fracture characteristics from fractographic analysis.

While the occlusal surface was identified as the primary origin of fracture in all experimental groups, the fracture patterns varied. In crowns fabricated from the 1 and 2 mm positions, crack propagation was longer and more vertically oriented toward the crown. In contrast, crowns fabricated from the 3 and 4 mm positions exhibited shorter, laterally deviated crack paths. This difference may be due to the crack reaching the 3Y-TZP layer, resulting in less severe fractures compared to those observed in the 1 mm and 2 mm positions. This behavior can be attributed to the transformation toughening effect in 3Y-TZP, which contains a higher proportion of the t-ZrO2 phase. These t-ZrO2 can transform into the monoclinic phase (t – m phase transformation), causing a 4% – 5% increase in volume that generates compressive stresses around the crack tip, thereby inhibiting crack propagation.6,35 As the yttria content increases in 4Y-PSZ and 5Y-PSZ, the proportion of the c-ZrO2 phase also increases, reducing the material’s ability to undergo the t – m phase transformation. This diminished transformation capability lowers crack resistance and deflection, leading to more severe crack propagation and reduced fracture force in the zirconia.6,35

The findings of this study align with those of Li et al.,11 who reported significant differences in fracture force of single mandibular molar crowns fabricated from three different positions within SGM zirconia (Upcera Meiying Multilayer Zirconia, which has a compositional distribution comparable to that of the material used in the present study) and also suggested that fatigue loading reduces the fracture force of SGM zirconia. However, Li et al. cemented the SGM zirconia crowns on implant abutment analogs without simulated periodontal ligament and thermocycling aging.

These results are in contrast with studies by Rosentritt et al.19 and Heidari et al.,20 which found no significant differences in fracture force of three-unit FPDs made from different positions within SGM zirconia, and by Strasser et al.,21 who reported similar findings for four-unit FPDs. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in experimental design. The aforementioned studies19,20,21 focused solely on FPDs, in which compressive loads were applied to the center of the restoration—typically the pontic or the connector area, which is considered the weakest point of the prosthesis. This loading configuration generates bending forces, leading to the highest tensile stress concentrations generally occurring on the gingival surface of the connector.22 Zirconia is a brittle material that tolerates compressive stress far better than tensile stress due to inherent flaws in its crystalline structure.22 As a result, fractures in those studies predominantly occurred at the connector, typically originating from the gingival side at the base of the connector and propagating occlusally and laterally, often following a primarily vertical path.21 In contrast, the present study investigated single SGM zirconia crowns, which possess monolithic integrity. These crowns were subjected to compressive loading on the occlusal surface, while the bottom layer of the crown—engineered for higher strength and toughness—is intended to resist tensile stresses that develop near the gingival area due to bending.35 In this study, fracture origins were primarily located on the occlusal surface, which typically exhibits lower flexural strength, with cracks propagating vertically or laterally toward the crown margins. Therefore, differences in restoration type, compressive loading site, locations of the highest tensile stress concentration, and the sites of crack initiation may explain the differing effects of blank position on the fracture force between FPDs and single crowns.

Thermo-mechanical aging, which simulates cyclic loading and a moist environment similar to mastication, is considered a more representative model of clinical conditions. However, there is no consensus on the precise force, frequency, or number of cycles required to accurately replicate intraoral conditions. The present study employed a commonly used protocol involving thermocycling for 6,000 cycles at temperatures of 5℃ and 55℃ with 30-second dwell times, followed by cyclic fatigue testing with a 50 N load at 1.2 Hz for 1,200,000 cycles, simulating approximately five years of clinical use.19,29,30 Notably, significant differences in fracture force were observed between crowns without and with thermo-mechanical aging, with a lower fracture force observed in the thermo-mechanically aged groups. Additionally, the failure modes in the aged groups were more severe than those in the non-aged groups. The application of cyclic loading contributed to subcritical crack growth, facilitating crack propagation even under low loads, which in turn degraded the material’s strength.6 Furthermore, the humid environment promoted stress corrosion, as water molecules penetrated the crack tips, leading to the expansion of the surface-degraded area.36,37 This resulted in a reduction in fracture force and an increase in the severity of failure modes in the thermo-mechanically aged groups. The results of this study are consistent with the findings of Wiedenmann et al.,29 who reported that thermo-mechanical aging led to a decrease in the fracture force of monolithic zirconia crowns. However, these findings contradict earlier studies by Mitov et al.38 and Badr et al.,39 which found that thermo-mechanical aging did not affect the fracture force of monolithic and multilayer zirconia crowns. It is important to note, however, that most previous studies have focused on monolithic zirconia and investigated the effects of thermo-mechanical aging without considering the influence of the position within the blank.

Despite the decrease in fracture force observed after thermo-mechanical aging, all thermo-mechanically aged groups were still able to withstand occlusal forces, as the mean fracture force of the zirconia crowns exceeded 2,000 N—significantly higher than typical masticatory forces, which range from 222 to 445 N (with an average of 322 N), as well as forces associated with teeth clenching and sleep bruxism, which range from 520 to 800 N (averaging 660 N).40,41 The fracture force of SGM zirconia crowns was significantly higher in the lower regions than the upper regions of the blank. Therefore, in full-mouth rehabilitation cases—particularly in patients with severe generalized tooth wear caused by parafunctional habits such as bruxism and clenching—posterior restorations, where fracture force is prioritized over esthetics due to higher occlusal loads, should be fabricated in the lower regions of the blank (3 – 4 mm below the upper edge). Conversely, anterior restorations, where esthetic requirements are more critical, can be fabricated in the upper regions (1 – 2 mm below the upper edge of the blank).

For in vitro studies on the fracture force of crown restorations, although natural tooth dies offer the most clinically relevant representation, their use may affect the outcomes due to variability in factors such as age, storage conditions, tooth size, and the need for individualized preparation of each specimen.42 Therefore, in this study, the dies were fabricated from a 3D-printed photopolymer resin, which possesses an elastic modulus comparable to that of natural dentin and allows for precise control over the size and shape of each specimen, thereby facilitating standardization.36 To more accurately simulate clinical conditions, polyvinyl siloxane impression material was used to represent the periodontal ligament,26 as prior research by Rosentritt et al.43 indicated that the fracture force of crowns was significantly reduced when abutment teeth with resilient support were used.

One limitation of this in vitro study is that the load was applied perpendicular to the occlusal surface to ensure stability. However, in the context of full-mouth rehabilitation, the recommended occlusal scheme is canine-protected occlusion, in which only the opposing canines engage during lateral movements to minimize horizontal stresses and help preserve the health and function of the masticatory system.44,45 Therefore, this study did not simulate the lateral forces that may occur on premolar teeth during lateral excursions. Another limitation is that only one brand of SGM zirconia (KATANA™ Zirconia YML) was evaluated, and results may vary with other brands due to differences in the composition of zirconia blanks and the thickness of individual layers, which may influence the mechanical and optical properties of the restoration. Future studies should include comparative evaluations across different zirconia systems to assess material-specific performance variations. Additionally, investigating the bond strength between resin cement and zirconia would provide further insights into long-term clinical outcomes. To enhance clinical relevance, future studies should also incorporate variables that more closely replicate intraoral conditions, such as fluctuating pH levels and the complex, multidirectional nature of masticatory forces.

CONCLUSION

Within the limitations of the present study, the following conclusions can be drawn. The position within the zirconia blank plays a critical role in determining the fracture force of SGM zirconia crowns. Crowns fabricated from the lower regions of the SGM zirconia blank exhibited significantly higher fracture force and tended to fail in less severe modes compared to those produced from the upper regions. These findings have direct clinical implications. For posterior restorations—where mechanical strength is essential due to higher occlusal forces—material from the lower positions within the SGM zirconia blank is more suitable. Conversely, for anterior restorations, where esthetics are the primary concern, fabricating crowns from the upper blank regions is recommended. Additionally, the study confirmed that thermo-mechanical aging has a detrimental effect on fracture force. SGM zirconia crowns subjected to aging conditions demonstrated a noticeable reduction in fracture force. This emphasizes the importance of considering long-term durability when selecting materials and designing prosthodontic restorations.
