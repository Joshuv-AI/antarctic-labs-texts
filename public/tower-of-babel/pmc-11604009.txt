Workplace culture for forensic mental health services: a mixed methods descriptive study
Authors: Trentham Furness, Anne Bardoel, Nikola Djurkovic, Rachael Fullam, James R. P. Ogloff
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWorkplace culture is theorized to involve a combination of elements such as assumptions, beliefs, and values. An effective workplace culture is safe and person-centred, which enables staff to flourish. However, there is no empirical evidence describing or informing workplace culture for forensic mental health settings.MethodsThe mixed methods approach is used to describe current indicators of, and perspectives on, workplace culture and understandings of ideal workplace culture for forensic mental health services. Participants responded to a literature informed survey (N = 482) enquiring about workplace psychological health and teamwork, and some (N = 72) participated in follow-up focus group discussions.ResultsPsychological health was less positive for staff working in clinical compared with non-clinical roles (p < 0.01, d = 0.80). Teamwork was positive (M = 27.2, SD = 7.6). Five themes emerged from the focus group data: psychological safety and trust, siloing, passion for the job, service structures (including system issues, resourcing, and support), and staffing. Ideal workplace culture in forensic mental health services could be supported by avoiding a culture of blame, maintaining passion for the job, and supporting good communication.ConclusionsThere is a potential opportunity for forensic mental health services to strengthen workplace culture by improving multi-agency communication methods, improving recognition of employee expertise and achievements, and supporting reasonable risk-taking.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11779-2.

Background

Employers and governments are increasingly recognizing the importance of providing psychologically healthy workplaces. Healthcare professionals working in mental health organizations are often exposed to ongoing stress and trauma in their job roles [1]. Various studies have identified the associations between organizational factors and employee wellbeing [2, 3], but there is limited understanding of the organizational and management factors that lead to a positive work environment for healthcare professionals, particularly those who work in inpatient and secure psychiatric services. This study uses the lens of relational values, psychosocial safety, and workplace culture to explore factors that lead to a positive work environment in a forensic mental health setting.

Forensic mental health services are designed for people with severe mental illness who are a transient danger to others [4]. The provision of clinical assessment, reports, management, and treatment of people with a severe mental illness and offending behaviours (i.e., consumers) is multidisciplinary and can occur in a variety of settings (e.g., inpatient secure, prison, and community) [5]. Forensic mental health services require coordinated care across mental health services and criminal justice systems [6]. Therefore, the multidisciplinary and multi-agency conduct of day-to-day healthcare provision within a forensic mental health service requires staff members with clinical, corporate, and administrative expertise.

The forensic mental health literature describing, for example, risk assessment [7], recovery-oriented care [8], reducing restrictive interventions [9, 10], perceptions of care [11], and workplace stressors [12] continues to expand. However, there remains a dearth of literature describing the work culture of forensic mental health services. That absence functions to inhibit the development of work cultures supporting excellence in the provision of care.

There is no universally agreed-upon definition of ‘workplace culture’ [13], although, it is theorized to involve a combination of many elements such as assumptions, beliefs, and values [14]. The attitudes, beliefs, and perceptions of employees relate to the principles held by, and practices of an organisation, which in turn affect whether the work culture has a positive or negative impact on the quality of care and delivery of health services to patients [15]. An effective workplace culture is safe and person-centred, which enables staff to flourish [16]. In healthcare settings, positive workplace culture is associated with lower stress, higher job satisfaction [17], effective leadership and management [18], and improved patient healthcare outcomes [19, 20].

Given the importance of positive workplace culture in healthcare settings, it is crucial that successful elements of workplace culture be understood and replicable for forensic mental health settings. However, there is no empirical evidence describing or informing workplace culture for these settings. A relevant description could include a multitude of internal and external factors that may influence or reflect workplace culture [21]. Mindful of this challenge, we focus on two important workplace culture influences: teamwork (i.e., relational values) and psychosocial health (i.e., psychological safety). Teamwork is described as supporting and promoting the spirit of co-operation, togetherness, and oneness [22]. Workplace psychological safety refers to shared perceptions of policies, procedures, and practices for the protection of staff psychological safety and health [23]. Further, given the absence of information about workplace culture among forensic mental health services, the perceptions of all staff (both clinical and non-clinical) regarding strengths and ideal culture need to be described. This description may be beneficial in informing forensic mental health services regarding how to support and strengthen a positive workplace culture. Therefore, the objective of the current study is to describe current indicators and perspectives of workplace culture within a forensic mental health service. The specific aims are to describe: (1) relational values and psychosocial safety and (2) perspectives of ideal workplace culture for forensic mental health services.

Methods

Research design

The current study uses an exploratory mixed methods design. Exploratory research can be used when a problem has not been clearly defined [24]. In the current context, the problem is an absence of description and knowledge about factors which influence workplace culture and perceptions of ideal workplace culture. Mixed methods designs can simultaneously draw from the strength of both quantitative and qualitative approaches [25]. The current study uses EQUATOR network recommendations for observational research [26] and qualitative research [27]. The study received approval from the relevant organizational research committee and Swinburne University of Technology Human Research Ethics Committee (Protocol number: 20226242-9101) informed by the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research (NHMRC, Australia). Data were collected during 2022.

Study setting

The forensic mental health service in the current study provides specialist forensic mental healthcare across the criminal justice and mental health sectors. The service employs approximately 1,000 staff at varying levels of full-time equivalent load. Staff hold expertise in accounts and finance, administration, allied health, catering, cleaning, clinical administration, law, lived experience, medical, nursing, policy and planning, psychology, research, and security. Clinical staff account for around 70% of the entire workforce at the organization. Most staff members (approximately 65%) have been employed at the service for less than five years, attributed to relatively rapid growth, an ageing staff, and high staff turnover in the mental health sector. The service is located in Australia.

Participants

All employees of the forensic mental health service were invited to participate in both the quantitative and qualitative methods of the study. All participants provided informed voluntary implied (i.e., survey) and/or written (i.e., focus group) consent to participate in the study. Survey participants were recruited using snowball and convenience sampling [28]. To assist in describing workplace culture across 2022, the survey was distributed twice within the service (i.e., T1 – March 2022 and T2 – November 2022) via internal email invitation from the research team. To support survey recruitment, participants could opt-in to a randomly drawn prize (a total of 10 AU$50 vouchers). Focus group participants were recruited via an internal email invitation from the research team.

Data collection

Quantitative data

A 23-item, literature informed online survey was used to collect data about participants’ sociodemographic and clinical characteristics (e.g., gender, age, professional role), relational values, and psychological safety (see Supplementary file). Relational values were described with the seven-item ‘relational values’ sub scale of the Organizational Values Scale [22]. Positive relational values promote quality interpersonal relations among employees and are affected by teamwork, respect, trust, and tolerance [22]. Responses range from ‘completely unimportant’ (value of 1) to ‘very important’ (value of 5) on a five-point Likert scale. Higher ratings indicate more positive relationships among people in an organization [22]. The relational values subscale is a reliable measure within the construct of organizational values (Cronbach’s α = 0.93) [22]. Psychological safety was described with the 12-item Psychological Safety Climate (PSC-12) Scale [29] comprising four subscales (management commitment, management priority, organizational communication, and organizational participation). Each subscale consists of three items. Positive psychological safety climate within an organization is demonstrated by commitment to preventing job stress and promoting psychological health [29]. Participants rated climate items from ‘strongly disagree’ (value of 1) to ‘strongly agree’ (value of 5) on a five-point Likert scale. Higher ratings indicate greater psychological safety [29]. The PSC-12 is a reliable measure of workplace psychological safety (Cronbach’s α = 0.94; subscale internal consistency range = 0.77–0.90) [29]. A sample size of N = 322 was required to for a margin of error rate of 5% [30].

Qualitative data

To explore staff perspectives on workplace culture, research team members with expertise in human resource management, who were external to the service, conducted a series of focus groups across the entire organization (i.e., service area A, service area B, and service area C) using the approach of appreciative inquiry. Each service area consisted clinical and non-clinical staff. Appreciative inquiry is a process for understanding organizational development and allows exploration of ideas that people have about what is valuable, and appreciating activities of colleagues [31]. The appreciative inquiry method focuses on recognizing the best of what is (i.e., identifying and building on past achievements and existing strengths) and highlighting possibilities of what could be [32]. The focus group guide was informed by this literature and topics included when working “at its very best” what are the strengths: (1) of the entire service, (2) the area and/or discipline/role they work in, and (3) of their usual working team. Participants were also asked to think specifically about workplace culture and describe what ideal culture looks like and how it may be achieved for: (1) the entire service, (2) the area and/or discipline/role they work in, and (3) their usual working team. All focus groups were recorded to an audio-digital device (VoiceTracer, CVT2050, Philips). During focus groups, field notes were also taken and later integrated to the data analysis.

Data analysis

Quantitative data

Sample descriptors are shown as both count (n) and frequency (%). Relational values and psychological safety total values are shown as mean and standard deviation. To identity differences among clinical (i.e., nurses, protective service officers, social workers, occupational therapists, psychologists, medical doctors, consumer/carer participation, and security officers) and non-clinical (i.e., administration, research, finance and business services, governance, risk and legal, and strategy) participants of the service, sample means are compared with independent t-tests for relational values and psychological safety total values. To describe differences among less experienced (i.e., less than 1 year to up to 5 years) and more experienced (greater than 5 years) participants of the service, sample means are compared with independent t-tests for relational values and psychological safety total values. A total of four independent sample t-tests are computed. A Bonferroni adjustment was used to account for Type I errors. Significance was accepted at p ≤ 0.013. Magnitude of differences are captured with an effect size (d) [33]. Data are analysed with Statistical Package for Social Scientists 29.0.1.0 for Windows (IBM, Armonk, USA).

Qualitative data

Focus group and field note data were transcribed and then subjected to thematic analysis using a general inductive approach. This approach allows multiple forms of data to be analysed inductively [34]. The procedure involves reading, re-reading, identifying key concepts and codes, clustering, categorizing, and collapsing data into themes. Codes and emergent themes were discussed within the research team and refined to form final themes and sub themes.

After cleaning and removing duplicates, the resulting sample was n = 434. Consistent with the staffing makeup of the organization, the largest professional role was nursing (n = 172, 40%) and 39% of participants had been employed at the service for more than five years (see Table 1). Mean difference among T1 and T2 for relational values was 1.1. Mean difference among T1 and T2 for psychological safety was 0.5.

Qualitative themes

Psychological safety and trust

Some participants acknowledged the risk associated with the nature of their clinical and non-clinical work given the inherent jurisdictional and public responsibilities of the service. However, other participants mentioned challenges associated with a climate of mistrust and “blame” among peers and colleagues. Participants suggested that such a climate can create a fear of reprisals or repercussions, which may prevent open dialogue and inhibit the capacity to learn from mistakes, as well as the willingness to take worthwhile and reasonable risks. One participant mentioned the importance for all staff to “feel safe and supported and able to bring up their ideas and contribute to change.” Looked at differently, an ideal culture could be supported when staff:

“…. have a non-defensive leadership team, where you can go to your leaders, your peers and wherever it might be, to be able to say, you know, I’ve made a mistake.” [Focus group, service area B].

“…. have a non-defensive leadership team, where you can go to your leaders, your peers and wherever it might be, to be able to say, you know, I’ve made a mistake.” [Focus group, service area B].

Similarly:

“It’s collaborative and there’s a degree of trust and I think shared risk sort of is there in terms of people holding risk together.” [Focus group, service area A].

“It’s collaborative and there’s a degree of trust and I think shared risk sort of is there in terms of people holding risk together.” [Focus group, service area A].

Siloing

Some participants reported that “siloing,” or splits between different groups within a service, can contribute to a sense of “us versus them” and competition for resources and recognition. Some of the participants acknowledged that siloing is a natural consequence of unique factors within a multidisciplinary service:

“I work in nursing and …. I work alongside expertise in the field of psychiatry, psychology, social work, occupational therapy, as well as with the management and P&C [i.e., People and Culture, corporate services] point of view. And everyone puts their mind to it, mostly we achieve what we want although it might take some time.” [Focus group, service area C].

“I work in nursing and …. I work alongside expertise in the field of psychiatry, psychology, social work, occupational therapy, as well as with the management and P&C [i.e., People and Culture, corporate services] point of view. And everyone puts their mind to it, mostly we achieve what we want although it might take some time.” [Focus group, service area C].

Reducing siloing and supporting an ideal workplace culture could be achieved when:“It doesn’t matter where you go in [the service], we understand who we are and what we do.” [Focus group, service area B].

“It doesn’t matter where you go in [the service], we understand who we are and what we do.” [Focus group, service area B].

Passion for the job

Passion was demonstrated though a strong sense of pride and commitment to work and building capability with colleagues. The importance of teamwork, cohesion, and psychological safety in asking colleagues questions supported passion for the job. Participants were keen to acknowledge their colleagues’ expertise and dedication, highlighting the multidisciplinary nature of the service as a particular strength. When “working well”, staff were mindful of being “cohesive” with a “team environment”. For example:“When I think about [the service] as a whole and at our best, I think part of that is that it's multidisciplinary.” [Focus group, service area B].

“When I think about [the service] as a whole and at our best, I think part of that is that it's multidisciplinary.” [Focus group, service area B].

In discussing passion for their work, participants spoke of supporting each other, learning from one another, and commitment:“One of the things that’s come through for me over many years that always seems to endure is the commitment and dedication of the staff… so morale can go up and down, but always the staff are 100% committed and [that] is a great place to start with.” [Focus group, service area B].

“One of the things that’s come through for me over many years that always seems to endure is the commitment and dedication of the staff… so morale can go up and down, but always the staff are 100% committed and [that] is a great place to start with.” [Focus group, service area B].

Another participant stated:

“I just started this year, so I had a lot of questions coming in and I felt like I could always ask any staff member and found that everyone’s been willing to help me out.” [Focus group, service area A].

“I just started this year, so I had a lot of questions coming in and I felt like I could always ask any staff member and found that everyone’s been willing to help me out.” [Focus group, service area A].

A sense of pride also emerged:“When a patient, a long-term patient, is being discharged, seeing them go into a new life. It’s like a journey, seeing them off is quite satisfying.” [Focus group, service area A].

“When a patient, a long-term patient, is being discharged, seeing them go into a new life. It’s like a journey, seeing them off is quite satisfying.” [Focus group, service area A].

Service structures - systems issues, resourcing, and support

Participants noted that technical issues are a source of frustration and, at times, can impede the ability to work efficiently. Given the interagency jurisdictional requirements of the service, participants acknowledged that issues with external agency systems were challenging and added to workload:

“You find yourself sometimes repeating things two or three times because of the fact that the support systems aren’t up to actually supporting positive movement forward.” [Focus group, service area B].

“You find yourself sometimes repeating things two or three times because of the fact that the support systems aren’t up to actually supporting positive movement forward.” [Focus group, service area B].

It was suggested that, despite a desire to remain contemporary and changing external conditions (e.g., the COVID-19 pandemic, changing mental health legislation), reducing system issues could beneficially impact workplace culture:“It’s about effective systems … I think it goes through the rapid change and the innovations we’re trying to, you know, keep up with sort of COVID and everything else. Some of our systems are letting us down still… and those little things even though they’re little, they still impede upon people’s wellbeing, and they get in the way of clinical work. So effective systems that work for us to do the work that people need to do, I think build a better culture.” [Focus group, service area B].

“It’s about effective systems … I think it goes through the rapid change and the innovations we’re trying to, you know, keep up with sort of COVID and everything else. Some of our systems are letting us down still… and those little things even though they’re little, they still impede upon people’s wellbeing, and they get in the way of clinical work. So effective systems that work for us to do the work that people need to do, I think build a better culture.” [Focus group, service area B].

Participants suggested that strengthening a positive culture could be promoted with greater leadership support and recognition of staff. This could require financial expenditure (e.g., gift vouchers and coffee vouchers), being mindful of staff wellbeing, and recognizing expertise throughout a service:

“I think just a small appreciation just validates exactly how perhaps everyone’s feeling.” [Focus group, service area C].

“I think just a small appreciation just validates exactly how perhaps everyone’s feeling.” [Focus group, service area C].

Another stated:

“I just really would emphasize the value on staff’s safety and experience, and just listening to the ground level staff and their opinions on things. And I’d say that would lead to staff having more job satisfaction and retention.” [Focus group, service area A].

“I just really would emphasize the value on staff’s safety and experience, and just listening to the ground level staff and their opinions on things. And I’d say that would lead to staff having more job satisfaction and retention.” [Focus group, service area A].

Staffing

Staff shortages and slowness in recruitment were perceived to negatively impact culture. This was exacerbated by the pandemic and subsequent reduction in recruitment and orientation of international employees:

“I think we need to concentrate on staff wellbeing [as opposed to just consumer wellbeing] and especially since everyone seems to be very tired and exhausted from what we’ve experienced in the last few years.” [Focus group, service area C].

“I think we need to concentrate on staff wellbeing [as opposed to just consumer wellbeing] and especially since everyone seems to be very tired and exhausted from what we’ve experienced in the last few years.” [Focus group, service area C].

Ideal culture was perceived to be negatively affected by workplace stressors such as rostering and covering clinical shifts, transparency among promotion application outcomes, and confusion about hybrid work conditions. As such, an ideal culture was said to be:

“One that values retention of staff. Valuing staff safety, rostering, staffing levels, and experience.” [Focus group, service area A].

“One that values retention of staff. Valuing staff safety, rostering, staffing levels, and experience.” [Focus group, service area A].

Discussion

This is the first published study to describe indicators and perspectives of workplace culture within a forensic mental health service. Indicators of teamwork and psychosocial health were described, along with perceptions of strengths and ideal workplace culture for forensic mental health services. Key findings revealed that clinical staff members reported poorer psychological safety than non-clinical staff and that an ideal workplace culture involves several themes and can be supported by avoiding a culture of blame, maintaining passion for the job, and fostering good communication within a forensic mental health service (e.g., Siloing) and among interagency colleagues (e.g., systems issues).

Psychological safety climate (i.e., PSC-12 scores) of clinical participants in the current study were slightly positive (M = 37, scale midpoint = 36). Although it is not possible to compare with clinicians at other forensic mental health services, those figures are similar to other hospital employees in Australia (M range 36 to 39) [35, 36] and Europe (M = 36) [37], and lower than those reported in China (M = 42) [38]. Psychological safety climate scores of non-clinical participants in the current study were positive (M = 46) which was similar to outcomes previously reported among non-healthcare cohorts (M range 31 to 54) [39–42].

Describing the psychological safety climate allows insights into shared perceptions of policies, procedures, and practices for the protection of staff psychological safety and health [23]. The emergent theme in the current study ‘Psychological safety and trust’ supports efforts to eliminate a climate of mistrust, with fear of individual reprisals or repercussions preventing staff from being able to raise issues, identify and learn from mistakes, and take worthwhile risks. More broadly, psychological safety climate is linked to emotional exhaustion [35] and can indicate quality of care [36]. Positive psychological safety also supports better mental health, employee engagement, and job satisfaction [37]. As forensic mental health clinicians are exposed to unique and challenging workplace stressors such as exposure/witness to consumer self-harm, suicidal behaviour, and challenging and confrontational behaviour [43], compared with their non-clinical colleagues, it may be worthwhile to support all employees holistically, and tailor supporting and strengthening interventions for forensic mental health clinicians which consider contextual workplace stressors.

Relational values (i.e., Organizational Values Scale) have not been previously reported among forensic mental health settings. Relational values of participants in the current study were positive (M = 27, scale midpoint = 21), indicating a culture of teamwork that supports co-operation and togetherness [22]. These data are supported by the emergent themes ‘Siloing’ and ‘Passion for the job,’ which suggest positive workplace culture can be demonstrated when internal communications, shared understanding, and learning from one another eliminate a perception of “us versus them.” When teamwork (i.e., relational values) is interpersonally focussed [44], a service is socially-oriented rather than self-oriented [22]. As professional role and length of employment did not change relational values outcomes for participants in the current study, it may be indicative of a positive workplace culture that can allow an organization to focus on stability rather than on progress [22]. This stability might also support recruitment and retention, both of which remain a challenge for forensic mental health services [45, 46].

Participants suggested workplace culture can be challenged by recruitment and retention issues. It is noteworthy in terms of the context for this study that it was preceded by a sustained period of difficulty in staff recruitment, which hampered the ability of the organisation to either replace exiting staff or boost the headcount of employees. This difficulty was particularly acute in regard to the recruitment of employees from the international labor market (owing to pandemic-related border closures) upon which services rely, which may have further exacerbated the staffing issues mentioned by the participants of the current study. There was also discussion of known healthcare workplace stressors such as rostering and shift work [12], staff shortages [47], multi-agency collaboration [48] and healthcare information systems [49], and suggestions to support ideal workplace culture relative to those stressors. Supportive leadership was identified as critical to enhancing workplace culture. Supportive and collective leadership in healthcare settings benefits team performance, staff engagement, and satisfaction [50]. Further, participants in the current study suggested staff recognition was important to strengthen workplace culture. Such approaches have been implemented in other settings in the form of motivational strategies with financial incentives [51], such that rewards are aligned with professional values based on practical and contextual knowledge [52].

Limitations

The current study was limited to one forensic mental health service in Australia and may not be representative of other services in Australia or other nations. Parametric statistical analysis was limited to t-tests and Bonferroni adjusted alpha levels. Therefore, a thorough understanding of influence of biopsychosocial and clinical descriptors on psychological safety climate and relational values among employees of forensic mental health services remains unknown. Further, the voice of consumers and their perceptions of ideal workplace culture within a forensic mental health service, which likely impacts their recovery journey, remains unknown. Future research could include all key stakeholders and investigate other elements of workplace culture such as assumptions, beliefs, and values, to further describe current status of workplace culture within forensic mental health services.

Conclusions

The current mixed methods study, for the first time among forensic mental health services, described teamwork and psychological health in the context of workplace culture. There were meaningful differences in psychological safety climate scores for clinical compared with non-clinical participants. The outcomes suggest that while creating/maintaining a workplace culture of oneness remains important, forensic mental health clinicians, compared with their non-clinical colleagues, may be exposed to contextual workplace stressors that harm their psychological health. There is potential for forensic mental health services to support and strengthen workplace psychological health which may simultaneously enhance employee retention. Opportunities for forensic mental health services to strengthen workplace culture may include improving communication methods within and between services, improved recognition of employee expertise and achievements, and supporting reasonable forms of risk-taking. Such may include avoiding a culture of blame, reprisals and repercussions, and grow/strengthen open dialogue, trust, a sense of shard responsibility and courage to make mistakes and learn.
