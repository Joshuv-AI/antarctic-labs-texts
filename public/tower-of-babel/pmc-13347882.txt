Identification of immune and ferroptosis-related gene cysteine dioxygenase type 1 (CDO1) as a prognostic biomarker in colorectal cancer, relating to immunotherapy response distinction
Authors: Shuo Chen, Mingyue Xu, Weijun Su, Kai Wang, Siqi Zhang, Xipeng Zhang, Lin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cause of cancer deaths worldwide, and has a poor prognosis in advanced stages. Whereas there are limited effective biomarkers of CRC. Herein, we aimed to explore the potential prognostic value of immune and ferroptosis-related genes (IFRGs).MethodsCRC-related data were extracted from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases. Kaplan-Meier, receiver operating characteristic (ROC), and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to test the prognostic value of IFRGs. The immune cell infiltration and immunotherapy response predictive analyses were also conducted. The level of cysteine dioxygenase type 1 (CDO1) mRNA and protein expression in CRC and normal tissues was examined using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western blotting analysis, respectively.ResultsThirty-eight IFRGs were obtained through differentially expressed gene analysis and cross-analysis. Of them, CDO1 mRNA and protein expression levels were significantly reduced in CRC tissues compared to normal tissues. CDO1 remained as an independent prognostic biomarker after multivariate Cox regression. Additionally, CDO1 hypermethylation was associated with poorer prognosis of CRC patients. CRC patients with differential CDO1 expression or methylation status both showed significantly distinct immune cell infiltration and immunotherapy responses.ConclusionsOur study revealed CDO1 as an IFRG with a prognostic role in CRC. It may provide a rationale for developing novel treatment strategies, such as demethylating agents or combination immunotherapy protocols, for CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04385-5.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most prevalent cancer globally and is among the leading causes of cancer-related mortality, particularly with a generally poor prognosis for patients diagnosed at advanced stages [1, 2]. The absence of overt early symptoms often results in many patients being diagnosed at advanced stages, where conventional treatments exhibit limited efficacy [3, 4]. Despite advancements in screening and treatment methodologies, the incidence and mortality rates of CRC remain alarmingly high [5, 6]. Consequently, the identification of more precise diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive biomarkers, along with potential therapeutic targets, is essential for enhancing outcomes in CRC patients.

In recent years, ferroptosis, an iron-dependent programmed cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, is more than a mere passive endpoint of cell death; it is an important form of immunogenic cell death (ICD) that plays a significant role in regulating anti-tumor immune responses [7]. Ferroptotic tumor cells effectively promote the maturation and antigen presentation of dendritic cells (DCs) by releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and oxidized phospholipids, which activate cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL)-mediated systemic anti-tumor immune responses [8, 9]. Recent studies have indicated that the GPX4 protein is released during ferroptosis in cancer cells. This protein binds to the ZP3 protein on the surface of DCs, inhibiting glucose metabolism, maturation, and activation of DCs, which ultimately impairs T cell activation and weakens anti-tumor immune responses [10]. In CRC, existing studies have demonstrated that the abnormal expression of certain genes, such as GPX4 and ACSL4, can influence tumor cell survival, chemotherapy resistance, and the immune status of the tumor microenvironment by regulating sensitivity to ferroptosis [11, 12]. You et al. discovered that APOL3-mediated ferroptosis enhances the cytotoxic function of CD8 + T cells in CRC by degrading LDHA, increasing IFNγ levels, and reducing lactate concentration [13]. However, the specific interaction network between ferroptosis and the CRC immune microenvironment, particularly the identification of key ‘bridge’ genes that simultaneously regulate both processes, remains incompletely understood.

Given the potential synergistic or antagonistic interactions between immune responses and ferroptosis in the development and treatment resistance of CRC [13–15], it is essential to systematically identify key genes associated with both immune infiltration and ferroptosis processes. This understanding is crucial for comprehensively elucidating the pathological mechanisms of CRC, developing novel therapeutic strategies, such as the combination of immunotherapy with ferroptosis inducers, and discovering new prognostic biomarkers [16]. Currently, studies employing bioinformatics approaches to integrate public transcriptomic data, analyze immune cell infiltration, and explore ferroptosis-related gene sets for genome-wide screening of dual-functional marker genes related to ‘immune-ferroptosis’ remain relatively limited.

Therefore, this study aims to systematically identify and validate a key gene associated with both immunity and ferroptosis in CRC using an integrated bioinformatics approach. This identified gene is anticipated to serve as a novel prognostic and predictive biomarker, providing mechanistic insights and a potential therapeutic target for developing innovative combination strategies that concurrently modulate ferroptosis and antitumor immunity in CRC.

Materials and methods

Subjects

The CRC samples, consisting of colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) and rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), were sourced from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). From this repository, mRNA expression data (381 tumor and 51 normal samples), methylation profiles (408 tumor and 45 normal samples), and corresponding clinical information were obtained. Additionally, eight independent CRC transcriptomic datasets were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The detailed sample characteristics of the GEO datasets are provided in Table 1. For TCGA samples, we retained those with complete survival information for analysis, while for GEO datasets, we retained samples with comprehensive clinical information.

A total of 259 ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) were derived from FerrDb (Supplementary Material 1) (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/) [17]. A total of 18,883 immune cell-related genes (IRGs) were obtained from the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) database (Supplementary Material 2) (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/).

Differential expression analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed using “limma” (version 3.5.2) [18]function package in the R language. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between two groups were identified using |log2FC| >0.5 and adjusted p-value < 0.05.

Functional enrichment analysis

The Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the “clusterProfiler” (version 4.8.3) [19]. Significantly enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways were identified via a p-value < 0.05.

Based on the median expression level of CDO1, samples from the TCGA-CRC cohort were stratified into a high CDO1 expression group and a low CDO1 expression group. GSEA was then conducted using the gseKEGG function from the “clusterProfiler” R package. The analysis was performed on a pre-ranked gene list ordered by the log₂ fold change obtained from differential expression analysis. Significantly enriched pathways were identified using a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value threshold of < 0.05.

Clinical sample collection

CRC and matched adjacent normal tissue samples were obtained from ten patients during surgical resection at Tianjin People’s Hospital between May 7 and August 6, 2024. Eligible patients had histologically confirmed primary CRC, were treatment-naïve (no prior neoadjuvant therapy), and had complete clinicopathological records. Exclusion criteria included the presence of synchronous or metachronous malignancies in other organs, or pathological assessment indicating insufficient tumor cellularity or extensive necrosis in the resected specimens.

Each subject was informed and signed an informed consent form. All studies were approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin People’s Hospital (Ethical Application Ref: 2025-B82), adhered to the Helsinki guidelines, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. The clinical information of the subjects is shown in Table 2.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assay

Total RNA was extracted using TRNzol Universal Reagent (DP424, TIANGEN, China). The Evo M-MLV Reverse Transcription Premix Kit (AG11728, Accurate Biology, Changsha, China) was used for reverse transcription. The qRT-PCR assay was performed using SuperStar Universal SYBR Master Mix (CW3360M, Jiangsu Cowin Biotech Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) on a real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument (SLAN-96 S, Shanghai Hongshi Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The primer sequences are shown in Table 3. The cycling programme included a pre-denaturation step of 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as an internal control. The expression levels of mRNA were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Western blot

Proteins were extracted from cells using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (R0010, Solarbio, Beijing, China). The lysate was centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C) to collect the supernatant. Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated with primary antibodies. The membrane was then washed with 1×TBST and incubated with the secondary antibody for 1 h. The primary antibodies used in this study were CDO1 (1:1000, 12589-1-AP, Proteintech) and GAPDH (1:10000, 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech). The secondary antibodies were horseradish peroxidase-labeled goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG) (H + L) (1:10000, ZB-2305, Zhongshan Jinqiao, Beijing, China) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:10000, ZB-2301, Zhongshan Jinqiao, Beijing, China). Finally, the protein bands were detected using a fully automated chemiluminescence image analysis system (Chemi6000, Clinx, Shanghai, China).

Immune cell infiltration analysis

The Estimation of Stromal and Immune cells in Malignant Tumors using the Expression data (ESTIMATE) algorithm was used to derive the immune, stromal, and ESTIMATE scores for CRC. ESTIMATE utilizes the officially provided gene expression signature files, specifically common_immune_signature_genes.txt (referred to as the ‘immune signature gene set’) and common_stromal_signature_genes.txt (known as the ‘stromal signature gene set’). These files can be accessed from the original release materials of the ESTIMATE package, available at https://sourceforge.net/projects/estimateproject/ or through the corresponding Bioconductor/R package. These documents comprise gene sets associated with immune cell infiltration and stromal components, which are utilized to calculate immune-related scores. The relative proportions of the 22 immune cell types in the samples were estimated using the software Cell-type Identification By Estimating Relative Subsets Of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) [20]. CIBERSORT software characterizes the composition of the immune cell infiltration based on the gene expression matrix using a deconvolution algorithm, using a preset of 547 barcode genes. The sum of the proportions of all estimated immune cell types in each sample is equal to 1. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to determine the immune cell infiltration between groups.

Drug sensitivity predictive analysis

The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) represents the drug concentration required to achieve 50% inhibition of cell growth or activity in cell lines. The IC50 values for the drugs were computed using the R package “oncoPredict“(version 0.2) [21].

Tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) score and immunotherapy response prediction

The TIDE, exclusion, dysfunction, and microsatellite instability (MSI) scores were retrieved from the TIDE webserver (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) using its default parameters to evaluate tumor immune evasion potential. The IMvigor210 cohort and GSE78220 (receiving programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) blockade immunotherapy) were employed to evaluate the potential impacts of markers on treatment response, and the cohorts were categorised into complete remission (CR)/partial remission (PR) and stable disease (SD)/disease progression (PD) groups.

Statistical analysis

The Wilcoxon test was used to determine the significant difference between groups. Pearson correlation analysis was carried out using the “cor” function in R. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of less than 0.05. All statistical computations were performed with R software (version 4.3.2).

Results

Identification of candidate IFRGs in CRC

To systematically identify candidate genes dysregulated in CRC, we performed differential expression analysis across three independent cohorts. This yielded 9,270 DEGs from the TCGA-CRC cohort, 4,621 DEGs from the GSE44076 dataset, and 7,164 DEGs from the GSE87211 dataset (Fig. 1A-C). A cross-dataset comparative analysis revealed 2,811 consistently overlapping DEGs (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Material 3), representing a core set of transcriptional alterations in CRC.

To further identify differentially expressed IFRGs in CRC, we performed cross-analysis among 2,811 overlapping DEGs, immune-related genes, and ferroptosis-related genes, and obtained thirty-eight IFRGs in CRC (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Material 4). GO analysis showed that these 38 IFRGs were highly enriched in negative regulation of protein modification, cellular response to oxidative stress, cell chemotaxis, alpha-amino acid metabolic process, and glutathione metabolic process terms (Supplementary Material 5). KEGG analysis showed that these 38 IFRGs were significantly enriched in Central carbon metabolism in cancer, p53 signaling pathway, and cancer pathways (melanoma, glioma, bladder cancer), and ferroptosis pathway (Supplementary Material 6). The top 10 GO terms and KEGG pathways are presented in Fig. 1F and G, respectively.

Identification of CDO1 as the key IFRG in CRC

To identify key IFRGs in CRC, we analyzed the expression of 38 candidate IFRGs across various normal and tumor tissues using the GSE41258 dataset. In contrast to the normal group, the expression of 17 genes was down-regulated, while 21 genes exhibited significant up-regulation in the CRC group (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, we performed univariate Cox analysis on the 38 candidate IFRGs (Supplementary Material 7). The results indicated that tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases 1 (TIMP1), Cysteine dioxygenase type 1 (CDO1), NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4), and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (CDKN2A) were significantly associated with the prognosis of CRC patients (Fig. 2B). Further, we conducted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and compared the area under the curve (AUC) value to evaluate the sensitivity and specificity of the four genes (Fig. 2C-E). Based on the TCGA-CRC and GSE44076 datasets, the prognostic AUC values of CDO1 were 0.8516 and 0.8793, respectively, demonstrating that CDO1 had high prognostic significance for CRC (Fig. 2F). Meanwhile, after reviewing the literature, CDO1 was barely reported to be associated with ferroptosis and immunity. Therefore, CDO1 was taken for our subsequent analysis.

CDO1 expression is consistently downregulated in CRC and serves as an independent prognostic factor

Next, based on analyses of TCGA-CRC, GSE44076, and GSE87211 datasets, we demonstrated that CDO1 expression was significantly decreased in the CRC samples compared with the normal samples (Fig. 3A-C). This downregulation trend was consistently corroborated by analysis of the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) dataset (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, immunohistochemical (IHC) data from the HPA database confirmed that CDO1 expression is notably lower in CRC tissues relative to normal tissues (Fig. 3E). Subsequently, we collected ten cancerous and normal tissue samples from patients with CRC. qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses revealed that the levels of CDO1 mRNA and protein expression were significantly reduced in CRC tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 3F and G, Supplementary Material 8).

To explore the prognostic value of CDO1, we conducted univariate Cox regression analysis and multivariate Cox regression analysis using TCGA clinical data. Our analysis identified four characteristics as independent prognostic factors, including “Age”, “Stage”, “N” (lymph node status), and “T” (Supplementary Material 9, Fig. 3H). Subsequently, we performed multivariate Cox regression analysis (Supplementary Material 10, Fig. 3I), which confirmed that CDO1 is indeed one of the independent factors influencing patients’ prognosis.

CDO1 promoter hypermethylation in CRC correlates with reduced expression and worse patient survival

Evidence indicates that promoter hypermethylation of the CDO1 gene serves as a key epigenetic mechanism downregulating its expression and correlating with poor prognosis in multiple cancers, such as breast cancer [22] and lung cancer [23]. To determine whether CDO1 under-expression in CRC is likewise mediated by methylation, we assessed the methylation status of the CDO1 promoter in CRC tissues. We identified eight CpG sites within the promoter region of CDO1 (Supplementary Material 11), all of which exhibited significant hypermethylation in CRC samples (Fig. 4A). Therefore, we analyzed the methylation status of CDO1 in CRC. We identified eight CpG sites located in the CDO1 promoter region (Supplementary Material 11), which exhibited significant hypermethylation in CRC tissues (Fig. 4A). Consistent with these findings, methylation data from the UALCAN (Fig. 4B) and TCGA (Fig. 4C) databases confirmed significant hypermethylation of the CDO1 promoter in CRC (p < 0.05). Furthermore, methylation levels at all eight CpG sites were inversely correlated with CDO1 mRNA expression, exemplified by cg11036833 (R = -0.49, p < 2.2e-16), cg02792792 (R = -0.52, p < 2.2e-16), cg16707405 (R = -0.43, p < 2.2e-16), and cg14470895 (R = -0.48, p < 2.2e-16) (Fig. 4D). Notably, elevated methylation at cg11036833, cg16707405, and cg14470895 was associated with poorer overall survival in CRC patients (Fig. 4E–G). Collectively, these results suggest that promoter hypermethylation may contribute to CDO1 transcriptional repression, which in turn could be correlated with poor prognosis of CRC patients.

Exploration of the potential biological functions of CDO1 in CRC

To explore the potential function of CDO1 in CRC, we performed GSEA in the TCGA-CRC cohort. Our data indicated that 115 pathways were significantly enriched in the high-expression CDO1 group compared to the CDO1 low-expression group (|NES| > 1, p-value < 0.05, false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25) (Supplementary Material 12). Notably, the cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) signaling pathway, Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) signaling pathway, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway, phosphoinositide 3-kinase-protein kinase B (PI3K-Akt) signaling pathway, rat sarcoma (RAS) signaling pathway, and Wnt signaling pathway were significantly activated in the high-expression CDO1 group compared to the CDO1 low-expression group (Fig. 4H).

Immune cell landscape of CRC patients with differential CDO1 expression and methylation status

Given the significant effects of the tumor immune microenvironment on tumor progression and prognosis, ESTIMATE was used to assess the relationship between CDO1 expression and immune cell infiltration. The results indicated that compared to the CDO1 high expression group, Stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores were significantly decreased, and tumor purity was significantly higher in the CDO1 low expression group (Fig. 5A-D). Additionally, compared to the CDO1 hypomethylation group, the CDO1 hypermethylation group exhibited significantly lower stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores, along with notably higher tumor purity (Fig. 5E-H). The proportions of 22 types of immune cell types in the different CDO1 expression groups were calculated by the CIBERSORT algorithm (Fig. 5I, J). The infiltration proportions of 12 immune cell types were found to be statistically significant in relation to CDO1 expression, including B cells memory, B cells naive, neutrophils, T cells gamma deta, macrophages M0, macrophages M1, macrophages M2, mast cells resting, T cells CD4 memory, T cells CD4 naive, NK cells resting, and mast cells activated (Fig. 5K). B cells memory (R = 0.1, p = 0.049), B cells naïve (R = 0.12, p = 0.018), neutrophils (R = 0.32, p = 2.6e-10), T cells gamma deta (R = 0.26, p = 4.4e-07), macrophages M0 (R = 0.17, p = 0.00066), macrophages M1 (R = 0.23, p = 7.4e-06), macrophages M2 (R = 0.22, p = 1.7e-05), and mast cells resting (R = 0.18, p = 0.00042) all had positive correlation of CDO1 expression, whereas T cells CD4 memory (R=-0.15, p = 0.0026), T cells CD4 naïve (R=-0.11, p = 0.031), NK cells resting (R=-0.2, p = 6.9e-05), and mast cells activated (R=-0.17, p = 0.0007) showed negative correlation (Supplementary Material 13).

Predictive value of CDO1 in the drug/ therapeutic response of CRC patients

To establish a reference treatment regimen for CRC patients, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the correlation between CDO1 expression levels and the IC50 values of various drugs. Using the pRRophetic R package and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, we compared IC50 values for numerous therapeutic agents across high and low CDO1 expression groups. Out of 198 tested agents, 96 chemotherapeutic agents showed significant IC50 differences (Supplementary Material 14). Our analysis revealed that CRC patients with low CDO1 expression had significantly lower IC50 values for six anti-tumor sdrugs, including 5-fluorouracil (Fig. 6A), Oxaliplatin (Fig. 6B), acalabrutinib (corrected from Acetalax) (Fig. 6C), Temozolomide (Fig. 6D), Dabrafenib (Fig. 6E), and Fulvestrant (Fig. 6F). This finding suggested that reduced CDO1 expression may be associated with increased sensitivity to these specific agents in CRC treatment. Subsequently, to validate patient responses to drug therapy, three independent CRC cohorts, including GSE19860, GSE28702, and GSE153412, were used. Our analysis consistently demonstrated that CDO1 expression was lower in the responding group compared to the non-responding group (Fig. 6G-I).

Preliminary exploration of CDO1-related potential in predicting immunotherapy response in CRC

To explore the effect of CDO1 expression on the immunotherapy response of CRC patients, the expression levels of several well-known immune checkpoint genes were analyzed between high- and low-expression CDO1 groups. We noticed that most of the immune checkpoint genes were significantly differentially expressed between CRC patients with differential CDO1 expression or methylation status (Fig. 7A, B, Supplementary Material 15). Analysis of TIDE scores further showed that CRC patients with lower CDO1 expression had significantly lower TIDE scores, exclusion scores, and dysfunction scores, coupled with higher microsatellite instability (MSI) scores (Fig. 7C). In addition, analysis of independent immunotherapy cohorts—IMvigor210 (predominantly urothelial carcinoma) and GSE78220 (melanoma)—suggested that CDO1 expression was notably lower in patients achieving complete or partial response (CR/PR) compared to those with stable or progressive disease (SD/PD) during anti‑PD‑1 therapy (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7D). Together, these observations imply that CDO1 expression may serve as a potential predictor of immunotherapy efficacy in CRC patients.

Discussion

This study systematically identified ferroptosis-related genes in CRC and focused on the key gene CDO1. The results demonstrated that downregulated CDO1 expression and its hypermethylation status were associated with poor prognosis in CRC patients, aligning with the clinical validation pathways emphasized in contemporary tumor biomarker translation research, such as the clinical translation frameworks established for markers like PD-L1/CD44 [24–27]. Concurrently, CDO1 status significantly influenced the tumor immune microenvironment composition and responses to immunotherapy, which mechanistically resonates with findings on unconventional immune-stromal interactions [28, 29]. These findings suggest that CDO1 may serve as a critical node linking ferroptosis regulation to the tumor immune microenvironment. Future research could employ emerging precision detection technologies, such as 4D-printed microdevices and nanomedicine delivery systems [30–32], to validate the robustness of CDO1 as a biomarker in broader clinical cohorts and facilitate its translation into clinical application.

CDO1, a member of the mammalian non‑heme Fe(II) dioxygenase family, functions as a tumor suppressor gene [33]. It plays a key role in cysteine metabolism, thereby modulating cellular antioxidant capacity. This mechanism positions CDO1 as a critical regulator of ferroptosis and apoptosis [34]. Specifically, CDO1 promotes ferroptosis by depleting antioxidant defenses, which facilitates cell membrane autoperoxidation via the Fenton reaction [34]. In the present study, we found that CDO1 was a key ferroptosis-related gene in CRC, and it was down-regulated in CRC tissues. This study revealed that CDO1 expression was significantly downregulated in CRC tissues, accompanied by promoter hypermethylation. Clinical correlation analysis further indicated that CDO1 hypermethylation was closely associated with unfavorable prognosis in CRC patients. This epigenetic silencing phenomenon is not unique to CRC but has been consistently observed across various solid tumors. For instance, in breast cancer, CDO1 hypermethylation correlates with poor prognosis, and demethylation treatment restores its expression, subsequently inhibiting tumor progression by inducing cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and ferroptosis [22]. Additionally, serum CDO1 methylation levels serve as a non-invasive biomarker for early breast cancer detection. In lung cancer, CDO1 methylation levels increase with disease progression, demonstrating moderate diagnostic performance [23, 35]. Furthermore, CDO1 methylation gradually rises during endometrial lesion progression, exhibiting high sensitivity (94.1%) and specificity (90.9%) for endometrial cancer screening [36]. Functionally, overexpression of CDO1 in breast cancer upregulates tumor suppressor genes such as DEFB1 and HOPX and modulates extracellular matrix-related pathways, thereby suppressing tumor growth and metastasis [37]. Collectively, these findings underscore that CDO1 promoter hypermethylation represents a common epigenetic event across cancer types, playing a critical role in tumorigenesis, progression, and prognosis. Mechanistically, promoter hypermethylation silences CDO1 expression, and reversing this epigenetic modification—for instance, through DNA methyltransferase inhibitors—may restore CDO1 function and potentially enhance immunotherapy efficacy, a hypothesis warranting further experimental validation. Moreover, CDO1 promoter hypermethylation in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has demonstrated significant clinical utility in multiple cancers. In non-small cell lung cancer, elevated CDO1 methylation levels in urine or plasma ctDNA have diagnostic value and serve as an independent prognostic indicator [38, 39]. In CRC, CDO1 hypermethylation is also an independent prognostic factor for stage III patients and may help identify subgroups likely to benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy [40]. These lines of evidence suggest that ctDNA-based CDO1 methylation analysis holds promise not only for diagnosis and risk stratification but also as a dynamic monitoring tool for assessing tumor burden, predicting therapeutic responses (e.g., to chemotherapy or immunotherapy), and detecting early resistance. However, further efforts are needed to advance multicenter standardization, optimize detection sensitivity, and conduct prospective validation studies.

GSEA showed that cAMP pathways signaling pathway was significantly activated in the high CDO1 expression group in CRC. The cAMP/PKA signaling pathway primarily exerts growth-inhibitory effects in various normal cells and certain tumor models. For instance, in cancer cachexia models, suppression of this pathway in skeletal muscle leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and muscle wasting, whereas restoration of its activity improves these phenotypes [41]. Furthermore, this pathway has been reported to modulate ferroptosis by influencing iron metabolism, potentially through mechanisms such as induction of endoplasmic reticulum stress [42, 43]. Notably, studies have directly demonstrated that the cAMP/PKA/CREB axis upregulates the transcriptional expression of CDO1 in tissues including the liver [44]. Given the critical role of CDO1 in ferroptosis, we hypothesize that the cAMP signaling pathway may enhance cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis by upregulating downstream genes such as CDO1, thereby influencing the progression of CRC. However, the specific molecular mechanisms through which CDO1 methylation in CRC affects this interactive network and ultimately determines tumor cell fate remain unclear. Future investigations should employ integrated multi-omics analysis and functional validation to systematically elucidate the pivotal role of CDO1 epigenetic silencing in signal reprogramming and cell death resistance in colorectal cancer.

Moreover, our findings demonstrated that CDO1 expression levels significantly influenced immune checkpoint gene expression and correlated with improved immunotherapy responses in CRC patients. These observations align with recent clinical evidence showing that neoadjuvant immune checkpoint blockade achieves pathological complete response rates of 41–90% in MSI‑H/dMMR CRC [45]. Our analysis further indicated that lower CDO1 expression was associated with favorable immunotherapy outcomes in independent pan‑cancer anti‑PD‑1 cohorts (IMvigor210 and GSE78220), suggesting its potential as a broad pan‑cancer immunotherapy‑response biomarker. While these cohorts do not include CRC patients, the findings highlight CDO1 as a promising adjunct biomarker to MSI status, potentially aiding in the identification of optimal candidates for organ‑preservation strategies. Further validation in dedicated CRC immunotherapy cohorts will be necessary to confirm its predictive value specifically in CRC. Additionally, since CDO1 expression influences patient prognosis and treatment response, its assessment could inform personalized surgical planning. Another study by Mirza et al. (2025) confirmed that robotic‑assisted colorectal surgery significantly enhances functional recovery and reduces complication rates compared to laparoscopic approaches [46]. These insights highlight how the integration of molecular biomarkers such as CDO1 with precision surgical techniques may optimize comprehensive patient management.

This study has several limitations. First, our findings are predominantly derived from retrospective analysis of public databases and a single-center cohort, and thus require validation in prospective, multi-center settings to confirm the predictive utility of CDO1. Second, the sample size of the clinical validation cohort is relatively limited, which may affect the statistical power of some analyses, necessitating further validation in larger clinical cohorts. Additionally, while CDO1 expression and methylation status demonstrate correlations with immunotherapy response, the underlying molecular mechanisms—particularly its role in the interplay between ferroptosis and the tumor immune microenvironment—remain to be elucidated through further experimental studies. Lastly, the prognostic and predictive relevance of CDO1 has yet to be validated in dedicated cohorts of immunotherapy-treated CRC patients, limiting current insights into its direct clinical applicability. Future studies should focus on addressing these aspects through expanded validation and mechanistic investigation.

Our study has preliminarily confirmed that CDO1 is the key biomarker linked with immunity and ferroptosis, promising in predicting the prognosis of CRC. Aberrantly lower CDO1 expression in CRC is probably modified by hypermethylation, thereby contributing to the worse prognosis of patients. Meanwhile, CDO1 is probably involved in inflammatory signaling pathways associated with ferroptosis. These findings offer new insights into exploring the molecular mechanisms of CDO1 and suggest potential therapeutic strategies for CRC.
