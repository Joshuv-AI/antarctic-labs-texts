Long-term Outcomes of Acellular Dermal Matrix Use in Breast-Conserving Surgery: A Single-Institution Study
Authors: Wonguen Jung, Jun Woo Lee, Jeongshin An, Kyubo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study evaluated long-term postoperative outcomes and complications after breast-conserving surgery (BCS) with acellular dermal matrix (ADM) insertion.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the data of patients treated between July 2015 and August 2021. Patients who did not undergo postoperative follow-up were excluded. Clinical characteristics, treatment information, and postoperative complications were collected from the electronic medical records. Complications were classified as major (requiring hospitalization or surgical intervention) or minor (manageable in an outpatient setting). ADM removals were categorized as early (≤ 6 months postoperatively) or delayed (> 6 months).ResultsAmong the 236 patients (median follow-up, 71.7 months), complications occurred in 119 (50.4%): 22 major (9.3%) and 97 minor (41.1%). Infection was the most common major complication (13/22, 59.2%), whereas seroma was the predominant minor complication (87/97, 89.7%). In univariate analysis, no clinicopathological or treatment-related factors were associated with major complications. ADM removal was performed in 28 patients (11.9%), including nine with local recurrence and 19 unrelated to recurrence (8.1%). Among the 19 nonrecurrent removals, 13 (68.4%) occurred > 6 months after surgery, most commonly due to infection (12/19, 63.2%).ConclusionComplications after BCS with ADM insertion were common during the long-term follow-up. The 9.3% major complication rate and 8.1% reoperation rate for ADM removal are not negligible. These findings highlight the need for careful patient selection and long-term surveillance of patients undergoing BCS with ADM insertion.

INTRODUCTION

Breast-conserving surgery (BCS) is the standard treatment for early-stage breast cancer, providing oncological control while preserving breast appearance [1]. Oncoplastic techniques, including autologous flaps and biomaterials, have been developed to restore contours and improve cosmesis [234].

Acellular dermal matrix (ADM) is increasingly used as a volume-replacement material in BCS, providing structural support and filling defects as an alternative to autologous flaps [567]. Although the long-term outcomes of ADM in breast reconstruction and augmentation have been reported, its long-term clinical outcomes in the setting of BCS have not been adequately investigated [8910].

Previous studies have mainly investigated the technical feasibility, cosmetic outcomes, or short-term complications following ADM insertion in BCS [67]. However, the reported complication rates vary widely, and long-term follow-up data are scarce. Long-term ADM-related complications such as infection, seroma, and inflammatory reactions are clinically relevant because of their potential impact on adjuvant treatment schedules and patient quality of life. Additionally, the association between ADM-related complications and subsequent treatments such as radiotherapy or chemotherapy remains unclear.

As long-term complication patterns remain unclear, studies with extended follow-up periods are needed. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate long-term postoperative outcomes and complication patterns following ADM use in BCS.

METHODS

We retrospectively reviewed the data of patients who underwent BCS with ADM insertion at our institution between July 2015 and August 2021.

ADM was inserted to fill the defect after tumor excision. Two types of ADMs were used: sheets (flat and rectangular) and pellets (small diced fragments) (Figure 1A). A sheet-type ADM was used from July 2015 to March 2019, and a pellet-type ADM was used from January 2019 to August 2021, with an overlap period from January to March 2019. This transition was based on a previous study demonstrating a higher complication rate with sheet-type ADM [4]. The ADM was placed in the surgical cavity to replace the excised tissue (Figure 1B), and the wound was closed without drain placement at the ADM insertion site (Figure 1C).

ADM = acellular dermal matrix.

All the patients received postoperative radiotherapy. Conventional fractionation was used before 2020, after which hypofractionated whole-breast radiotherapy was adopted. A sequential tumor bed boost was administered as indicated. Radiotherapy was delivered using either three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D-CRT) or intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT). Patients who did not undergo postoperative follow-ups were excluded from the analysis. Patient demographics, tumor characteristics, treatment-related information, and follow-up data were collected from electronic medical records. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and informed consent was waived (approval number: 2025-09-001 at Ewha Womans University Mokdong Hospital).

Complications were classified as either major or minor. Major complications requiring hospitalization or surgical intervention included hematoma or seroma requiring operative drainage, infection requiring admission, and wound dehiscence requiring surgical revision. Minor complications were managed in the outpatient setting and included seroma or hematoma treated by aspiration; infection or inflammation treated with oral or intravenous antibiotics in the clinic; and wound dehiscence managed with dressings. Infection was defined based on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention surgical site infection criteria: a positive aseptically obtained culture, clinical signs of infection (erythema, swelling, warmth, or pain) with surgical wound opening, or a physician diagnosis of infection. Inflammation was defined as localized erythema or warmth without systemic signs of infection or microbiological evidence suggestive of a non-infectious response to the ADM. ADM contracture was defined as localized palpable firmness and contour abnormality caused by aggregation or shrinkage of the ADM over time. To avoid duplicate counting, only the most clinically relevant event was recorded for patients who experienced multiple postoperative complications.

To enable a standardized severity assessment, each complication was additionally graded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification [11] based on the level of management required. In this system, grade I denotes any deviation not requiring pharmacological treatment beyond analgesics, antipyretics, antiemetics, diuretics, electrolytes, or any surgical, endoscopic, or radiological intervention and includes wound infections opened at the bedside; grade II requires pharmacological treatment with other drugs; grade III requires surgical, endoscopic, or radiological intervention, subclassified as IIIa (without general anesthesia) or IIIb (under general anesthesia); grade IV is a life-threatening complication requiring intensive care; and grade V is death. This classification was applied to our cohort. Complications managed by observation, wound dressing, or office-based needle aspiration performed by palpation without imaging guidance were classified as grade I, while those requiring systemic antibiotics (oral or intravenous) were classified as grade II. Operative drainage, surgical revision, and ADM removal were classified as grade IIIa or IIIb depending on whether they were performed under local or general anesthesia, respectively.

ADM removal was categorized based on timing as early (≤ 6 months postoperatively) or delayed (> 6 months postoperatively). In patients who underwent ADM removal at an external hospital, the date of removal was estimated as the midpoint between the last visit before removal and the first visit after removal. The follow-up duration was calculated from the date of surgery to the date of ADM removal or last follow-up visit, whichever occurred first.

Statistical analyses were performed to assess factors associated with major complications. Univariate analyses were conducted using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the independent t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate. The cumulative incidence of ADM removal was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, USA).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

The baseline patient and tumor characteristics are summarized in Table 1. In total, 236 patients who underwent BCS with ADM insertion were included. The median age at surgery was 49 years (range, 25–76), and the median follow-up was 71.7 months (range, 0.9–119.7). Most patients had invasive ductal carcinoma (73.3%), and 79.3% were node-negative. Sentinel lymph node biopsy was performed in 61.4% of the patients, and axillary lymph node dissection was performed in 38.6%. Sheet-type and pellet-type ADM were used in 59.3% and 40.7% of patients, respectively. Overall, 53.4% of patients received chemotherapy, including neoadjuvant (11.0%, n = 26), adjuvant (42.0%, n = 99), or both (0.4%, n = 1). A subset of patients received radiotherapy at other institutions and detailed treatment records were not available for all parameters; the number of patients with available data varied by parameter (Table 1). Regarding radiotherapy, conventional fractionation was administered in 187 patients (79.2%), most commonly 50.4 Gy in 28 fractions, and hypofractionation in 34 (14.4%), most commonly 40.5 Gy in 15 fractions. IMRT was administered to 24 patients (10.2%) and 3D-CRT to 182 patients (77.1%). A tumor bed boost was delivered in 201 patients (85.2%), with a median boost dose of 9.0 Gy (range, 7.5–16.0). The median total radiation dose was 59.4 Gy (range, 50.0–66.4) for conventional fractionation and 50.5 Gy (range, 50.0–54.0) for hypofractionation.

Complications

The complications are summarized in Table 2. Postoperative complications occurred in 119 patients (50.4%): 22 (9.3%) major and 97 (41.1%) minor complications. Infection was the most frequent major complication (59.2%), followed by seroma (13.6%), ADM contracture (13.6%), inflammation (9.1%), and hematoma (4.5%). The minor complications were predominantly seromas (89.7%), whereas inflammation (7.2%) and infection (2.1%) were less frequent. Wound dehiscence occurred in only one case (1.0%) as a minor complication. Major complications occurred at a median of 197 days (range, 3–2,377) after surgery, and minor complications developed at a median of 13 days (range, 4–248) postoperatively (Figure 2A). According to the Clavien–Dindo classification, 73 of the 119 complications were grade I, 26 were grade II, 8 were grade IIIa, and 12 were grade IIIb, with no grade IV or V events (Supplementary Table 1).

Risk factor analysis

Univariate analysis showed no significant association between major complications and clinicopathological or treatment-related factors, including age, T stage, N stage, hormone receptor status, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 status, number of dissected lymph nodes, type of axillary surgery, radiotherapy technique, radiotherapy fractionation, tumor bed boost, chemotherapy, or ADM type (all p > 0.05; Supplementary Table 2).

ADM removal

During follow-up, ADM removal was performed in 28 patients (11.9%), including 19 removals due to complications or clinical symptoms and 9 removals associated with local recurrence. Among the 19 removals unrelated to recurrence (8.1% of the total cohort), 6 occurred within six months after surgery (early), and 13 occurred more than six months postoperatively (delayed) (Table 3). The Kaplan–Meier estimated cumulative incidence of ADM removal unrelated to local recurrence was 4.7% at two years and 10.1% at seven years (Figure 2B). The median time to removal was 2.6 months (range, 0.9–6.0) for early cases and 41.6 months (range, 7.0–78.2) for delayed cases. Among the early removals, all six cases (100%) were due to infection. Among delayed removals, infection remained the most common cause (n = 6, 46.2%), followed by ADM contracture (n = 3, 23.1%), seroma (n = 2, 15.4%), and other causes (n = 2, 15.4%); one patient was ultimately diagnosed with immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4)-related mastitis despite initial suspicion of recurrence. The difference in the cause of removal between the early and delayed removal groups was statistically significant (p = 0.044, infection vs. noninfection). Representative clinical and radiological findings are shown in Figure 3, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infection (Figure 3A), ADM contracture (Figure 3B), and IgG4-related mastitis on ultrasonography (Figure 3C).

Impact of ADM removal on adjuvant treatment

Among the 19 patients, 14 (73.7%) underwent ADM removal after the completion of radiotherapy, 4 (21.1%) before radiotherapy, and 1 (5.3%) during radiotherapy. Of the five patients who had not yet completed radiotherapy at the time of removal, three were undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy, and ADM-related complications coincided with the chemotherapy period. In one patient who did not receive chemotherapy, wound complications occurred after the completion of radiotherapy planning, necessitating postponement of treatment. ADM removal was performed twice, and resimulation was required before radiotherapy could be initiated, resulting in a delay of approximately three months. Additionally, one patient developed a methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infection during the course of radiotherapy, requiring a treatment break for ADM removal, and radiotherapy was resumed and completed after infection control.

DISCUSSION

In this long-term cohort study, approximately half of patients experienced postoperative complications after BCS with ADM insertion. Major complications were mainly infection-related. ADM removal unrelated to local recurrence was observed in 8.1% of the patients, and most removals occurred more than six months after surgery.

The reported complication rates and follow-up durations after ADM use in BCS vary widely across studies. In our cohort, complications were observed in a greater proportion of patients than previously reported (50.4% vs. 4.5%–32.7%) [6712131415]. This difference is likely attributable to our longer follow-up duration (median, 71.7 vs. 6–52 months in previous studies), which enabled the detection of delayed complications, such as infection. Consistent with previous reports, seroma was the most frequent complication [671213]. In the current study, closed suction drains were not placed at the time of ADM insertion to minimize the risk of infection. All patients who underwent postoperative aspiration were classified as having a seroma, which accounted for the majority of minor complications (89.7%). Closed-suction drainage is commonly used in ADM-assisted breast surgery to reduce fluid accumulation and facilitate seroma management. In implant-based reconstruction, ADM has been shown to significantly increase and prolong postoperative drain output, leading to recommendations that the drain should be kept within the implant–ADM pocket and removed with caution [16]. In volume-replacement oncoplastic BCS, several studies routinely placed a Jackson–Pratt or closed-suction drain and reported low seroma rates of 2.3%–4.8%, although their seroma criteria differed from ours [1415]. Therefore, the decision to omit drains may have contributed to the higher incidence of seromas in our cohort. In a prospective randomized trial of lumpectomy with axillary dissection, patients without drainage required more frequent aspirations (3.3 vs. 2.2 per patient, p = 0.002) and had a longer time to seroma resolution (18 vs. 11.5 days, p = 0.0002) than those with a drain, with an overall seroma rate of 81.5% when all palpable collections were aspirated, regardless of symptoms [17]. Although this trial evaluated axillary rather than tumor bed seroma, the observation that omitting drainage increases the frequency of fluid collection is likely applicable to our setting, in which drains were not placed at the ADM insertion site and every patient requiring aspiration was classified as having a seroma. Because our study lacked a comparator group, the respective contributions of ADM, no drain policy, and inclusive seroma definition to the observed complication rate of 50.4% cannot be disentangled. Seroma was the minor event and typically occurred early, whereas infection accounted for most major events and tended to develop later in our cohort. This pattern indicates that a short follow-up generally identifies only early events such as seroma, while extended surveillance is required to detect delayed complications such as infection. Indeed, infection was not only the predominant major complication but also the most common reason for ADM removal. Prior studies rarely reported ADM removal, and even the longest follow-up to date (52 months) documented only one removal, occurring at 22 months, due to persistent discharge and skin irregularities [14]. These findings indicate that clinical follow-up should be extended beyond the first postoperative year.

In the present study, univariate analyses did not identify any clinical or treatment-related variables associated with major complications. Most previous studies had shorter follow-up durations and rarely evaluated the risk factors for complications. Yi et al. [15] reported no association between ADM type (diced sheet vs. diced paste) and complication occurrence. Although studied in implant-based breast reconstruction with ADM after mastectomy, Teotia et al. [18] identified elevated body mass index as a predictor of complications, and Remington et al. [19] reported body mass index > 27 kg/m2, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy as significant risk factors. Because these associations were not observed in our cohort, our findings should be considered exploratory and highlight the need for larger multi-institutional datasets.

The ADM removal rate in our cohort was 8.1%, which is higher than that reported in previous studies. The extended follow-up duration in our study (median, 71.7 months) enabled the identification of late events that may have been missed in shorter studies. Among the 19 removals unrelated to recurrence, 13 (68.4%) occurred at > 6 months postoperatively, with a median time to removal of 41.6 months. In contrast, Gwak et al. [6] reported a 20.5% complication rate at 6 months, but only 1.7% removal and none due to infection, while Seo et al. [14] reported only one removal (1.2%) without infection during a shorter follow-up period. In our cohort, infection was the predominant indication for resection (12/19, 63.2%). Consequently, short-term studies are likely to underestimate delayed complications, particularly infections and occasional contractures. These findings support extended postoperative surveillance, as the need for ADM removal may arise later rather than in the immediate postoperative period. Notably, the cumulative incidence of ADM removal continued to increase throughout the follow-up period without reaching a plateau, suggesting that the risk of ADM removal persisted beyond the early postoperative period.

In our cohort, early ADM-related complications delayed or interrupted adjuvant treatment in several patients, including the postponement of radiotherapy and treatment breaks due to infection. Franceschini and Masetti [20] previously emphasized the importance of detailed preoperative informed consent regarding possible postoperative complications and prompt management of complications to avoid delays in adjuvant therapy. Our findings provide long-term clinical data supporting these recommendations.

In the present study, all cases of ADM contracture were classified as major complications and were managed with ADM removal. However, because all affected patients had received radiotherapy prior to removal, the independent contribution of radiotherapy to ADM contracture could not be assessed. Nevertheless, previous studies have reported ADM-related structural changes including volumetric reduction and contour deformities in patients who underwent radiotherapy after BCS. Yi et al. [21] reported that, with a minimum follow-up of 24 months, depression, bulging, and dermal irregularities were observed in 6.9%, 3.9%, and 1.7% of patients, respectively, and approximately 85% of patients reported increased firmness in the reconstructed breast after radiotherapy. Furthermore, Jung et al. [22] demonstrated that the ADM volume decreased by an average of 8.2% during whole-breast irradiation, indicating that volumetric changes occur during radiotherapy. However, the long-term ADM volumetric outcomes after radiotherapy remain heterogeneous across studies. Kwon et al. [13] reported an approximately 17% reduction in sheet-type ADM volume over a mean follow-up of 790 days, whereas Yi et al. [15] observed a 4%–5% decrease at 2 years and found that radiotherapy was not an independent predictor of ADM volume reduction (p = 0.38).

The mechanisms through which radiotherapy contributes to ADM-related structural changes, including volumetric changes and contractures, have not yet been fully elucidated. Preclinical studies have suggested that processes such as electron beam or gamma irradiation can accelerate collagen fiber degradation and fragmentation, thereby reducing the mechanical strength [2324].

These findings suggest that the relationship between radiotherapy and ADM-related tissue changes is complex and may vary depending on patient characteristics, ADM type, and treatment modality. Although the independent effect of radiotherapy on ADM contracture could not be determined in the present study, preclinical and clinical observations of ADM-related structural changes support the possibility that radiotherapy may contribute to this process. Prospective studies with long-term monitoring are needed to identify the risk factors for ADM contracture and refine surveillance strategies for patients undergoing radiotherapy after ADM-assisted BCS.

Surveillance after BCS can be complicated by the presence of ADM. Kwon et al. [13] reported that ADM frequently triggered biopsies due to BI-RADS 4A findings despite low recurrence rates. Iskanderian et al. [25] also described diced ADM mimicking recurrent disease on imaging, and Jung et al. [26] reported that suspicious findings adjacent to the ADM may represent false-positive changes rather than true recurrence. In our cohort, one patient (0.4%) developed a new hypoechoic lesion in the tumor bed during follow-up, raising the suspicion of recurrence. The patient underwent ADM removal with lesion excision; however, the final pathological examination revealed IgG4-related mastitis. Although likely incidental, this case illustrates that ADM may introduce interpretive challenges to imaging surveillance. Correlating imaging findings with operative records and prior imaging and performing targeted biopsy when appropriate may help avoid unnecessary excision.

The economic implications of ADM in BCS warrant further consideration. ADM is a high-cost biomaterial and the long-term complication profile documented in the present study adds to the overall cost of care. In our cohort, 9.3% of the patients experienced major complications requiring hospitalization or surgical intervention, and 8.1% underwent reoperation for ADM removal. In addition, minor complications, particularly seromas requiring repeated aspirations, were frequent and required multiple outpatient visits over an extended period. Previous cost analyses of implant-based breast reconstruction have demonstrated that complications can amplify the overall cost of ADM approximately threefold, reflecting higher rates of infection, seroma, skin necrosis, and implant removal [27]. Although formal cost-effectiveness analyses of ADM use, specifically in BCS, have not been conducted, the long-term complication burden documented in the present study suggests that the economic impact may be considerable. Future studies should incorporate cost analyses and clinical outcomes to provide a comprehensive assessment of ADM use in BCS.

The absence of patient-reported outcomes in the current study limited the interpretation of the severity of complications. While most complications are classified as minor based on clinical criteria, the impact on patients’ daily lives, body image, and psychological well-being may differ from that of clinician-based assessments. For example, repeated outpatient visits for seroma aspiration or prolonged wound management, although clinically minor, may significantly affect patient satisfaction. Furthermore, ADM contractures resulting in palpable firmness and contour irregularity may negatively affect patient satisfaction because the original purpose of ADM insertion was to improve the breast contour after tumor excision. Future prospective studies should incorporate validated patient-reported outcome measures, such as the BREAST-Q [28], assessed at defined time points to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of ADM use in BCS.

This study had several limitations. First, as this was a single-arm study without a comparator group of patients who underwent BCS without ADM, it was not possible to determine whether the observed complication rate was attributable to ADM itself or reflected the background complication rate of BCS in general, and the role of ADM could not be established from this dataset alone. Second, no patient-reported outcome data were collected, which limited the interpretation of the severity of complications and overall treatment success. Third, this was a single-center retrospective study, which may have limited the generalizability of the results. Fourth, the number of major complications was relatively small, limiting the statistical power and precluding the multivariate analysis. Fifth, the ADM type (sheet vs. pellet) changed over the study period, which may introduce confounding factors such as treatment era, surgical experience, radiotherapy practice, or follow-up duration. Finally, for patients who underwent ADM removal at outside hospitals, the removal dates were estimated from available documentation. Prospective controlled studies comparing ADM-assisted BCS with standard BCS or autologous volume displacement techniques are required.

In conclusion, complications after BCS with ADM insertion occurred in approximately half of the patients during the long-term follow-up. The 9.3% major complication rate and 8.1% reoperation rate for ADM removal warrant attention. In a single-arm study without a comparator group, the clinical benefit of ADM use in BCS could not be determined from these data alone. These findings underscore the need for cautious patient selection, thorough informed consent regarding the potential delayed complications, and careful long-term surveillance of patients who undergo BCS with ADM insertion.
