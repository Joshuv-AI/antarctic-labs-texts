Impact of emergency financial support program on testing demand, SARS-CoV-2 prevalence, and social isolation during COVID-19 outbreak in Brazil: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Gabriel Alves de Sampaio Morais, Laio Magno, Joilson Nascimento Paim, Thais Aranha, Inês Dourado
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrograms offering financial support have been established to mitigate the economic fallout and vulnerabilities arising from the COVID-19 pandemic. Moreover, financial incentives were introduced to promote compliance with social isolation measures, thereby preventing SARS-CoV-2 infections and transmission. However, few low- and middle-income countries have implemented financial aid to alleviate the socio-economic impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. We estimated the association between the Brazilian Emergency Aid (EA) Program and SARS-CoV-2 testing demand, SARS-CoV-2 prevalence, and social isolation levels.MethodsIn this quasi-experimental study, we assessed microdata information from 380,000 individuals surveyed in the COVID-19 National Household Sample Survey (COVID-19 NHSS) between July 1, 2020, and Nov 30, 2020. Individuals meeting the EA eligibility criteria, those who have taken the SARS-CoV-2 test, those diagnosed with COVID-19, and/or those complying with social isolation measures were included. Beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries were matched (1:1) using propensity score matching, combined with the entropy balance technique and inverse probability treatment weighting, considering all relevant socioeconomic and health covariates. We performed logistic regression to compute the monthly average impact of EA benefits on the outcomes above.ResultsWe included 171,183 performed tests, 19,218 COVID-19 cases, and individuals’ self-response in complying with mild (N = 660,297), moderate (N = 597,159), strict social isolation (N = 149,125), and those who did not constrain social gatherings (N = 1,406,581). EA recipients showed greater testing demand (November; odds ratio [OR] = 1.014, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.001–1.026); however, this did not significantly reduce SARS-CoV-2 prevalence. Beneficiaries adhered better to mild isolation from July–September (September; OR: 1.017, 95% CI: 1.007–1.028), with no significant improvement from October–November. EA beneficiaries did not adhere to moderate (July, OR: 0.95, 95% CI: 0.94–0.97; November, OR: 0.83, 95% CI: 0.80–0.86) or strict (July, OR: 0.79, 95% CI: 0.75–0.84; November, OR: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.73–0.81) isolation compared to non-beneficiaries.ConclusionsDespite the severe COVID-19 situation in Brazil owing to the lack of effective public health policies and government resistance to scientific guidance, being an EA beneficiary was associated with increased testing demand and better adherence to mild social isolation measures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20877-z.Keywords: Economic Incentive, SARS-CoV-2 Testing Demand, SARS-CoV-2 Prevalence, Social Isolation

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic has precipitated a global health, economic, and political crisis. In 2020, the impending collapse of health systems was coupled with restrictions on social gatherings to mitigate the spread of the SARS-CoV-2 virus. Brazil's response to the COVID-19 (C19) pandemic has been considered one of the worst in the world. From February 2020 to March 2024, Brazil presented 38,694,221 accumulated C19 cases and 710,966 C19-related deaths, and the incidence and mortality rates of C19 reached 18,412.9 and 338.3 per 100,000 inhabitants, respectively [1].

In Brazil, at the beginning of the pandemic (February-May 2020), the number of new C19 cases was higher in the economically wealthier population [2]. However, over time, the poorest people became more affected due to the lack of isolation and prevention conditions [3], especially among those who lived in a high population density and poor housing conditions [4]. A cross-sectional observational study (February to March 2020), for example, further underscored that the C19 mortality rate was elevated among those with lower educational attainment [5], highlighting the socioeconomic disparities during the pandemic in Brazil [6].

During this period, financial support programs played a crucial role in alleviating the economic crisis experienced by the population, particularly those already vulnerable owing to low income and weaker employment conditions [7]. The most common programs included direct income transfers to the population and programs designed to assist employers in accessing credit and maintaining employment [8]. Financial support programs have been introduced to enhance adherence to social isolation measures and to mitigate vulnerabilities [9, 10]. A quasi-experimental modeling study, for example, indicated that the provision of sick leave decreased C19 cases by up to 400 per day in states that implemented measures similar to those in the USA [11].

In the context of vulnerabilities and inequalities exacerbated by the pandemic, Brazil initiated the Emergency Aid (EA) (“Auxílio Emergencial” in Portuguese) and the Emergency Program for the Maintenance of Employment and Income (EPMEI) (“Programa Emergencial de Manutenção do Emprego e da Renda” in Portuguese). The EA targeted unemployed individuals, low-income informal workers, self-employed individuals, and individual micro-entrepreneurs, offering a monthly income of US$113.60, approximately 60% of the Brazilian minimum wage. Women with children without a legal partner were eligible to receive US$227.3, equivalent to two emergency allowances. In 2020, the EA covered approximately 67.1 million people [12]. The EPMEI aimed to preserve jobs and income for formal workers. The benefits from both programs were distributed through a non-conditional direct transfer framework [13].

Evidence from Brazil showed a high mortality rate from C19 in cities with elevated rates of informal jobs, in which the interaction between work informality and EA was associated with an average reduction of up to 21% in deaths for every 10% increase in the number of beneficiaries [3]. The EA also improved beneficiaries’ access to health services and earlier SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis [14]. A recent analysis showed that if EA had not been implemented during the first wave of the pandemic, the cumulative number of infections and deaths would have been 6.5 and 7.9 times higher, respectively, compared to the scenario with EA implementation in 2020 [9].

So far, there is no data on the effectiveness of financial support for vulnerable populations in complying with social isolation measures [10]. Herein, we aimed to estimate the association between EA program and testing demand, SARS-CoV-2 prevalence, and compliance with social isolation measures from July to November 2020 in Brazil.

Methods

Study design and participants

We designed a quasi-experimental study [15] using data from the C19 National Household Sample Survey (COVID-19 NHSS), provided by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE). This survey estimated the population exhibiting flu-like symptoms and to track the pandemic’s impact on the Brazilian labor market during the first wave in 2020 [16]. The questionnaires were developed by the IBGE, and it is fully published at their website. The inclusion criteria for participation in our analyses were i) individuals who had undergone SARS-CoV-2 testing (i.e., those who tested positive and negative for the virus) and ii) those who self-reported to comply with any level of social isolation measures.

The prerequisites for enrollment in the EA implied that the allocation to the treatment and control groups was not random among the eligible population. This was in addition to the need for a procedure to identify only those individuals who benefited from EA owing to the potential receipt of other pandemic-related government aid, such as EPMEI, since the survey did not differentiate which of the two programs the beneficiary was referring to. Moreover, those individuals who did not meet the prerequisites of being EA beneficiaries were considered non-beneficiaries in our analyses.

In our quasi-experimental study, we selected participants in the EA program who self-reported their participation and concurrently met the following eligibility criteria for the EA intervention: individuals aged ≥ 18 years, not receiving retirement or pension income, not benefiting from any other government aid (excluding the main Brazilian conditional cash transfer (CCT) program, the Bolsa Família Program) or unemployment insurance, and not formally employed. Furthermore, the beneficiary's monthly household income had to be less than three minimum wages (approximately USD574) or the per capita income less than half of the minimum wage (USD96). To identify the beneficiaries, we employed the rules above, and the following reported values (BRL) received from the benefit were considered: 600, 1200, 1800, 2400, 3000, and 3600 (approximately USD116; USD232; USD348; USD465; USD581 and, USD697, respectively). This rule was implemented because the initial EA benefit values were set at BRL600 and BRL1200.

However, as the survey did not identify who within the household would benefit from the EA, multiple family members could receive the financial support, suggesting the possibility of the received amounts presenting multiples of the initial fixed amount. The set of conditionalities used to qualify the beneficiaries was available in the survey. Similar routines were previously employed to identify beneficiaries from EA and EPMEI [9, 13]. Further information can be found in the Appendix, p. 3–6.

Data collection and ethics statement

Data collection for the survey commenced in May 2020, utilizing telephone interviews conducted nationwide, being representative for the total population of Brazil. The total survey sample of the COVID-19 NHSS was divided into four weeks per month; however, individuals were surveyed once per month. The survey sample was static, meaning the individuals surveyed remained in the sample until the survey’s conclusion in November 2020. Furthermore, the entire set of questions in the survey referred to that specific week of reference, encompassing seven days.

As of July 2020, the survey incorporated supplementary information, such as SARS-CoV-2 test details, the nature of the test conducted, the test results, diagnoses of comorbidities, and information on social isolation measures. We utilized the survey data from July to November 2020, as we agreed that the survey was more comprehensive and robust during this period. Further information on COVID-19 NHSS can be found in the Appendix, p. 3–4.

The COVID-19 NHSS does not disclose individual identities, thus negating the need for approval from research ethics committees. It ensures the confidentiality of individuals in compliance with the Brazilian General Data Protection Law and the Statistical Secrecy Law (Law n° 5534/1968).

Theoretical model

We developed a theoretical model and posited that the EA program would significantly influence the C19 outbreak in Brazil by alleviating structural vulnerabilities. We hypothesized that participation in the EA program would lead to a surge in testing demand due to enhanced access to health services, improve compliance with isolation measures, and decrease the incidence and prevalence of SARS-CoV-2. Moreover, the mechanisms would allow reducing hospitalizations and mortality rates related to C19 (Fig. 1).

Conceptual framework and hypothesized effects of the Emergency Aid Program (EA) on this process

Outcomes

Taking advantage of COVID-19 NHSS data availability, this research focuses on three primary outcomes: the search for testing, the prevalence of SARS-CoV-2, and adherence to social isolation measures. The testing indicator reflects whether an individual underwent one or more tests to detect SARS-CoV-2 infection. Given the scarcity of these tests in 2020, they were exclusively conducted by health services, including pharmacy outpatient clinics, healthcare clinics, and hospitals. Consequently, as predicted by previous studies, this indicator can be interpreted as a measure of the demand for tests or the demand for health services [14]. Regarding SARS-CoV-2 test results, we only considered individuals who underwent an oronasal swab, a type of test that can detect the SARS-CoV-2 infection at the moment through either an antigen rapid test (RAT) or PCR test [9].

The indicators of social isolation measures according to COVID-19 NHSS were developed to describe three types of isolation, which we define here as mild, moderate, and strict. Mild isolation is characterized by having limited interaction with others yet continuing to leave their residence for work, non-essential activities, or to receive visitors. Moderate isolation is an individual remaining at home, only venturing out for essential needs such as purchasing food, obtaining medication, or visiting a physician. Strict isolation involves an individual strictly confining themselves to their home. The comparison reference category pertains to individuals who have not restricted their gatherings. Detailed information about the structure and definitions of the outcomes can be found in the Appendix, p. 7–8.

Statistical analysis

We used propensity scores, methods in which the measured confounding factors are distributed equally between the treatment and control groups while the allocation of individuals is approximately random [17]. Hence, in this study, we employed propensity score matching (PSM) to estimate the impact of the EA program on the outcomes being analyzed. The propensity scores for receiving the benefit were estimated using logistic regression, considering various demographic and socioeconomic covariates. These included the week of the survey sampling, place of residence (rural vs. urban), age, sex, ethnicity, education, private health insurance, deflated total income from all sources (excluding EA), region of residence (i.e., Federation Units), diagnosis of health comorbidities, and social isolation measures. Detailed information about the structure and definitions of the variables used can be found in the Appendix, p. 9. We incorporated self-reported comorbidities as they could potentially exacerbate the SARS-CoV-2 [18] infection and are linked to the outcomes.

We incorporated age, age squared, and income as continuous variables, a prerequisite for unbiased treatment estimates using PSM [19]. Initially, beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries were matched using nearest-neighbor matching (1:1) with replacement and a caliper of 0.05. This was combined with the stratification technique based on propensity scores, considering only observations within the common support, i.e., the region with overlapping propensity scores between the groups [19].

We assessed the balance of covariate distribution before and after matching through standardized mean differences and graphical analyses, considering common support and the proportion of bias reduction due to matching. The average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) was estimated by calculating the odds ratio (OR) in each corresponding dataset using logistic regression. We obtained standard errors and 95% confidence intervals (CI) via bootstrapping technique with 200 replications, clustering by Federation Unit of residence [20]. The ATT was computed for each outcome for the five months under study within a cross-sectional framework, including all individuals who satisfied the inclusion criteria. Under the hypothesis defined by the absence of unobservable confounding variables, using PSM allows for obtaining unbiased estimates of the ATT, beyond potential outcomes [21].

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted sensitivity analyses to evaluate our analytical and empirical approach. To achieve this goal, we assessed whether the effects remain valid among various matching settings as appropriate. Initially, we implemented the nearest neighbor (1:1) and radius matching, both with and without replacement of individuals, with and without a caliper of 0.05, and with and without stratification method. The whole set of the described implementation was estimated accounting for standard errors and 95% CI obtained via bootstrapping technique. Second, we combined the entropy balancing (EB) technique with the PSM, referred here as PSM-EB. Specifically, we employed the EB to reweight observations before estimating propensity scores (first stage) and then applied entropy weights to estimate propensity scores (second stage). Following this, we used the reweight observations and matched beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries accounting for the same set of matching specifications described above. Entropy Balancing, a pre-processing data method, facilitates the balancing of covariates in observational studies with binary treatments, in which a desirable level of balancing is achieved for the distributions of variables in the treatment and control groups, weighting the information through a set of conditions associated with the moments of these distributions. Moreover, EB takes advantage of avoiding loss of information as it weights observations as close as possible to the base weights (unit), resulting in increased efficiency, and may balance the covariates more effectively compared to common methods based solely on the propensity scores [22].

Subsequently, we evaluated the entire set of matching specifications, PSM e PSM-EB, based on the following criteria: the pseudo-R2 (low values are considered good fits), comparison of the models regarding the mean and median bias after the matching, the balance of covariates and remaining (effective) sample size after matching (Appendix, p.10–15). Furthermore, for each specification, we examined the existence of a common support and sensitivity using Mantel–Haenszel bounds [23], which, while not excluding the possibility of unobserved factors, indicate the extent to which they influence the results (Appendix, p. 16–40).

Thirdly, we employed one additional technique, the Inverse Probability of Treatment Weight (IPTW), to estimate the ATT of the EA on the treatment outcomes under investigation. In this analysis, the impact of EA was determined using logistic regression, weighting non-beneficiaries with the formula [propensity score/(1-propensity score)], and beneficiaries with 1. The weights generated by the IPTW create a pseudo population in which measured confounders are equally distributed across groups. At the same time, it enhances the advantages highlighted in the literature as a way to estimate hazard ratios with less bias compared to traditional matching techniques. Compared to PSM, where unmatched individuals are often discarded from the analysis, IPTW can retain most individuals, increasing the effective sample size [17]. Moreover, we obtained robust standard errors to address possible heteroskedastic inherent to cross-sectional data.

Finally, beyond the set of various matching specifications previously mentioned, we did additional sensitivity analyses for the model that accounts for SARS-CoV-2 prevalence by recalculating the ATT using a set of covariates associated with the provision of basic cleaning and protection items against SARS-CoV-2 infection (such as gel or liquid alcohol of 70% or higher, masks, disposable gloves, bleach or disinfectant) instead of health comorbidity indicators. Given the proven effectiveness of the provision and regular use of essential cleaning items in preventing new infections and transmission of SARS-CoV-2, we hypothesized that protective and personal hygiene materials could be more appropriate than health comorbidities since there is no evidence suggesting that individuals with comorbidities are more susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection. However, they are positively associated with the exacerbation of COVID-19. Moreover, we followed the criteria above to evaluate the matching specifications. It is worth highlighting that no single method can be recommended above all others because the optimal specification depends on the unique qualities of each dataset. In contrast, searching for the matching specification that fits better should not stop when a threshold is reached but should attempt to come as close as possible to perfect balance [24]. All analyses were conducted using STATA 16®.

Results

From July-November 2020, the NHSS-COVID-19 comprised 1,919,883 records (Fig. 2). We excluded individuals aged < 18 years and those with missing data on social isolation measures, ethnicity, health insurance, and health comorbidities (Fig. 2), as the volume of missing data was not substantial and unlikely to confound the association between EA and the outcomes significantly.

Flow diagram of the eligibility and inclusion criteria of the Emergency Aid (EA)

After excluding 464,505 records that did not meet the inclusion criteria, the final database comprised 1,455,378 records, an average of 291,075 individuals per month. The EA beneficiaries were identified based on the inclusion criteria, resulting in 50,670 (3.5%) beneficiary records, while the remaining 1,404,708 (96.5%) were classified as non-beneficiary (Fig. 2).

During the study period, 5,745 (3.35%) of the SARS-CoV-2 tests (n = 171,183) were conducted among EA beneficiaries. Moreover, 19,218 positive SARS-CoV-2 results were identified, with 1,574 (8.2%) among beneficiaries. Table 1 presents the monthly count, the proportion of beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries tested, and the SARS-CoV-2 prevalence. The monthly prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 (per 1,000) for beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries presented an upward trend between July and November, being more pronounced for the group of beneficiaries, which reached 43.21‰ [95% Confidence Interval: 39.13 – 47.58] in November, compared to 18.55‰ [95%CI: 18.05–19.05] for the group of non-beneficiaries. Moreover, we observed an increased count of performed SARS-CoV-2 tests and compliance with adherence to mild social isolation measures but a decreasing trend in adherence to moderate and strict isolation. In general, both groups presented a decrease in non-mobility constraints across the periods (Table 1).

Of the evaluated matching models and specifications, the initial model (nearest-neighbor [1:1] with replacement and caliper of 0.05) exhibited the least average bias post-matching and successfully reduced the imbalance. Furthermore, it demonstrated high success rates in matching pairs for beneficiaries, with rates of 99.99%, 99.75%, and 100% for testing, prevalence, and social isolation measures, respectively (Appendix, p. 91). Moreover, the remaining matching specifications barely changed our estimates of interest.

In the matched analyses, beneficiaries exhibited a slightly increased demand for SARS-CoV-2 tests, with a more pronounced effect observed in November (OR: 1.014, 95%CI: 1.001–1.026) compared to non-beneficiaries (Table 3). Furthermore, no statistically significant association was identified between EA participation and SARS-CoV-2 prevalence (Table 3), even after adjusting for covariates related to protection and hygiene strategies to prevent infection and/or transmission (Appendix, p. 92–97).

We observed an association between being EA beneficiary and improved adherence to mild isolation in July (OR 1.019, 95% CI 1.008–1.029), August (OR 1.017, 95% CI 1.008–1.026), and September (OR 1.017, 95% CI 1.007–1.028) compared to non-beneficiaries (Table 3). Conversely, the receipt of EA benefits did not sufficiently enhance adherence to moderate and strict isolation. Overall, there was an increase in lower adherence to moderate isolation among beneficiaries from July (OR 0.957, 95% CI 0.944–0.972) to November (OR 0.833, 95% CI 0.804–0.863). A more pronounced increase in non-adherence was observed for strict isolation from July (OR 0.796, 95%CI 0.754–0.841) to November (OR 0.773, 95%CI 0.732–0.815).

The sensitivity analysis using IPTW yielded results akin to those of the PSM analysis, and the results demonstrated a higher statistical significance between being an EA beneficiary and the demand for testing and compliance with mild isolation measures. The association between EA beneficiaries and SARS-CoV-2 prevalence remained consistent with the PSM analysis; however, estimates are closer to statistical significance, suggesting that being an EA beneficiary is associated with lower SARS-CoV-2 prevalence than non-beneficiaries (Table 4).

The IPTW analysis revealed, in general, an increased likelihood of being tested among beneficiaries between July (OR 1.006, 95% CI 1.001–1.011) and November (OR 1.013, 95% CI 1.006–1.021) compared to non-beneficiaries. However, a lower likelihood of adherence to mild isolation during the same period was observed, with the highest adherence in July (OR 1.021, 95% CI 1.015–1.026). Moreover, the trend of beneficiaries exhibiting a lower likelihood of adherence to moderate and strict social isolation persisted compared to the results derived from matched analysis (Table 4).

The PSM analysis combined with the entropy balance technique, PSM-EB, presented a slightly higher mean and median bias after matching than PSM alone. However, the overlap analyses of the propensity score distributions for each survey month under analyses with PSM-EB are the best compared to PSM solely (Appendix, p. 98–103), while an increased sample size was achieved. Moreover, the direction and magnitude of the effects remained consistent compared to PSM and IPTW.

In the PSM-EB, we found a quasi-significant increased effect of being a beneficiary and the demand for testing in July and September, while a significant effect of being a beneficiary and the SARS-CoV-2 prevalence, compared to non-beneficiaries. Moreover, we found an increased association in July (OR 1.121, 95%CI 0.983–1.278) and lower SARS-CoV-2 prevalence in August (OR 0.928, 95%CI 0.851–1.012), which was considered the peak of the first wave of COVID-19 burden in Brazil. From September to November, no effect was found between EA beneficiaries and SARS-CoV-2 prevalence, aligning with the PSM and IPTW analysis results. Finally, the lower adherence to moderate and strict isolation compared to non-beneficiaries remained highly consistent with PSM and IPTW analysis for each month under study (Table 4).

Discussion

Faced with the severity of the COVID-19 pandemic, preceded by an economic crisis due to fiscal austerity, escalating social inequality and rising poverty, and denialist campaigns by the Brazilian government, we discovered compelling evidence that, compared to non-beneficiaries, EA beneficiaries demonstrated an increased demand for testing during the pandemic’s first wave. Additionally, we observed that EA beneficiaries exhibited improved adherence to mild isolation measures compared to non-participants. However, EA beneficiary status was not significantly associated with adherence to moderate and strict social isolation measures, with lower adherence rate estimates compared to non-beneficiaries. Moreover, we found an association between EA participation and the SARS-CoV-2 prevalence during the study period, being more pronounced during the peak of the first wave, suggesting a protective effect for beneficiaries.

Our findings add to the limited body of evidence regarding the impact of social policies on the COVID-19 pandemic in Brazil. A study employing simulation models suggested that, had the EA not been introduced during the initial wave, the cumulative infection count might have been 6.5 times greater than the number estimated post-program implementation [9]. Another study indicated that individuals just below the income cut-off for EA eligibility were less likely to report to have acquired COVID-19, less likely to be tested at early stages of the pandemic, and were likely to work fewer hours, besides any differences in social distancing or hygiene behaviors were not found, suggesting the detected impacts of the EA in reducing the risk of SARS-CoV-2 infections were driven by the decrease in labor supply and increasing awareness of the pandemic’s severity [25].

Evidence indicates that the EA statewide average amount paid positively correlated to a social isolation index, as demonstrated by the mobile phone signals of over 60 million Brazilians; however, the associations were heterogeneous across Brazilian regions [9]. Our findings align with this study, showing that EA beneficiaries demonstrated increased adherence to mild social isolation compared to non-beneficiaries. This isolation involves individuals reducing contact with others yet continuing to leave their homes for work, non-essential activities, or to receive visitors.

The fewer associations of being EA beneficiaries and the reduced SARS-CoV-2 prevalence may be attributed to lower compliance with moderate and strict social isolation measures despite the positive association in testing demand by beneficiaries compared to non-beneficiaries. Previous findings evidenced the absence of association of the EA on the case detection and compliance with stringent social distancing adherence [26], which suggests there were no differences in those indicators between beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries. Moderate isolation involves individuals staying at home, only venturing out for essential needs, while strict isolation necessitates complete home confinement.

The EA program primarily benefits individuals with high socioeconomic vulnerability, including the unemployed, informal workers, and single-parent families. These demographics often find it challenging to adhere to isolation measures, a complexity partly attributable to high population density in areas with many family members sharing small houses and the housing conditions prevalent in areas of vulnerability, such as slums; in most cases, they have to spend lengthy times on public transportation to reach workplaces and healthcare services. Furthermore, the average amount of EA provided to beneficiaries may not have been sufficient to encourage strict isolation compliance, necessitating movements for job hunting, income generation, and the procurement of consumer goods. Thus, strict social isolation and lockdown policies must consider social inequalities given the increased risk of exposure for those economically vulnerable [27, 28].

The period of reduction in the amount paid by the program, from September onwards, may have motivated people to leave social isolation and triggered secondary waves of infections. This aligns with our findings, in which despite better adherence to mild isolation by beneficiaries, the effects on the compliance of mild social isolation decreased from September up to November, while adherence to moderate and strict social isolation by beneficiaries presented a downward trend compared to the non-beneficiaries and estimates from the first month under analysis onwards.

The evaluation of policy effectiveness in reducing mobility, particularly among at-risk or impoverished individuals in eight Brazilian capitals, revealed that neither stringent public health measures (such as city-wide lockdowns and gathering reductions enforced by fines) nor the receipt of EA benefits had a marginal impact on limiting non-work-related travel [26]. Furthermore, the psychological toll of isolation during the early stages of the pandemic may have contributed to a decline in adherence to mild isolation measures and even lower compliance with moderate and strict isolation protocols [9].

New COVID-19 cases began to decline in mid-August, considered the peak of Brazil’s first wave, until the first two weeks of November. This period, which saw the lowest case numbers since the pandemic’s onset in February 2020, is viewed as the divider between Brazil’s first and second waves [1]. Despite the region of residence (e.g., states) being controlled in our estimates, the enormous regional heterogeneity in the dynamic and spatiotemporal patterns of the pandemic, such as clustering, trajectories, speed, and intensity of the movement of the virus inside the country may arise as limitations that must be addressed in further analyses [29]; when one region was suffering due to high and unequal infection and mortality burdens, the same was not necessarily observed in remaining areas at the same time [4], not to mention substantial discrepancies of fragmented policies at the municipality level during the pandemic that influenced the dynamics of social distancing measures [30].

Possible issues not addressed by the COVID-19 NHSS regarding information on serological antigen targets (anti-N IgG and anti-S IgG), i.e., did not differentiate serological antigen test from real-time-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) when tests were performed using blood material. Even so, the heterogeneity of exposure across the country, loss of immunological biomarkers, and variant-specific test affinity could bias the estimates. Adding issues on the follow-up of individuals implies that estimates need cautious interpretation.

The substantial differences according to socio-demographic factors, mainly during the first wave, may have allowed some population strata to benefit from the social distancing measures when they could adhere to them. Therefore, evaluating the EA’s impacts on pandemic indicators during the second wave – it was possible to observe up to three waves in some states [9, 29] – is paramount for public policy assessment concerning COVID-19.

The interpretation of our findings must consider the controversial management of the pandemic at the federal level. Notably, during the early stages of the pandemic, testing was restricted to individuals with severe SARS-CoV-2 or to the worst COVID-19 cases and patients who were hospitalized, while any contact tracing was not in course, and rapid antigen tests were authorized without expanding the registration system for tests and guidance campaigns on collecting and interpreting results [31]. Moreover, when the testing capacity was expanded, the costs to get tested were prohibitive, exceeding half or more, on average, the amount paid by the EA. This may support our findings of the weak effect on testing demand and, consequently, in the case detection. Thus, combining isolation and contact tracing with physical distancing measures would reduce transmission, increasing the likelihood of achieving sustained control of SARS-CoV-2 transmission.

Poor management by the federal administration of the public health care system and lack of preparedness and planning to implement strict isolation and physical distancing policies, markable political instability, and deterioration of the Ministry of Health (MoH) delayed the policy response to the pandemic [32]. The lack of central coordination led to the state- and municipal-level administration to employ additional efforts in the health systems investments to rapidly expand emergency bed capacity in intensive care units and implement social distancing measures, which the federal administration opposes [33], not to mention the recommendation of inefficient medications, interference in collective health policies, including the national vaccination plan.

Significantly, municipalities most supportive of the Brazilian president at the time experienced higher COVID-19 mortality rates [3]. The president’s science-denying rhetoric and attitudes likely influenced their followers to adopt behaviors that increased their risk of infection and transmission. This could have contributed to positioning Brazil as one of the countries with inadequate pandemic management [32], which, in turn, experienced the second-highest number of cases and deaths and the highest number of daily deaths due to the disease one year after COVID-19 was declared a pandemic.

Despite the issues above on pandemic management, EA provided invaluable mechanisms in avoiding the worsening of the vulnerabilities of the poorest, providing emergency relief to many households, and alleviating hunger and food insecurity. Thus, in times of emergency, governments have to rely on social assistance programs, even if their targeting could be better. Moreover, taking advantage of the households recorded in the Brazilian census of people experiencing poverty (Cadastro Único) helped the federal administration to identify the eligible rapidly and to extend coverage of the EA to informal workers at large due to the new website creation for enrollment, relying on digital payment infrastructures, which is quicker and safer in a pandemic context, although may prevent individuals deprived of technologies from enrolling, excluding particularly vulnerable households, unless complementary systems are set up [8].

One of the strengths of our study is that it uses individual-level data from household surveys at the national level, providing valuable information on several socioeconomic indicators. Thus, this data allowed us to employ quasi-experimental methods to comprehensively estimate the treatment effects of the leading emergency cash-transfer program implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic in Brazil. Individual-level data on the participation of the EA and values transferred are also available in administrative databases that account only for the region of residence, which did not provide linked information on social, economic, and health factors, compromising the assessment of the EA since by aggregating the data at the municipal level to conduct ecological studies, may incur ecological fallacy. Also, compared to studies that used online surveys, the sample size may not represent the Brazilian population, mainly the vulnerable population. Moreover, those studies that employed mathematical simulations and those estimating the willingness to accept to get tested or to comply with isolation in the presence of financial support may cloak the real effect of the intervention and bring issues to internal and external validity.

Efforts in public health to combat the COVID-19 pandemic must consider the social, economic, and psychological factors that influence individual behavior. Increasing access to preventive interventions and technologies, such as vaccines, is unlikely to decrease morbidity and mortality rates significantly. This is owing to the necessity for effective messaging and cost-efficient support to enhance the uptake of these interventions [34], particularly among vulnerable populations.

Our study had certain limitations. The Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics does not thoroughly recommend the longitudinal follow-up of individuals, given that COVID-19 NHSS is a complex survey data. Furthermore, the absence of a unique identifier for individuals [16], coupled with challenges in identifying individuals using date of birth and sex, presented additional barriers, such as loss of observations across periods, which cast doubts regarding the consistency and efficiency of the follow-up of individuals. Moreover, it is worth noting that, initially, we selected additional outcomes, such as “searching for healthcare unit (HCU)” and “hospital admission.” However, these outcomes were nested, conditioned to the individual’s self-response to the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 symptoms (SARS-CoV-2-Symptoms). This led to a substantial loss of observations in the treatment group. These issues compromised the robustness and reliability of the effects of the EA on these outcomes due to poor balance after matching and difficulties in the convergence achievement for most of the estimates due to limited sample size.

We acknowledge that potential confounding factors, such as behavioral changes at different pandemic stages and the initial adherence to isolation prompted by the emergence of a deadly, largely unknown disease, could influence our findings. Notably, the EA value was halved in September 2020; this reduction could have significantly impacted test-seeking behavior, potentially leading to fewer detected infections. This, in turn, could have exacerbated some pandemic indicators and social vulnerabilities. However, we factored in the BRL300 (USD58) and BRL600 (USD116) values (for single-parent families) from September onwards when identifying beneficiaries. Furthermore, the absence of an association between EA participation and SARS-CoV-2 prevalence may also be attributed to some factors. We considered only "positive" and "negative" test results. In contrast, "inconclusive results", those who "still did not receive the results" and who "ignored or refused" to answer were discarded from the analyses. Furthermore, the monthly payment schedule of the EA was based on the beneficiaries' month of birth, which may have influenced behaviors, consumption decisions, and resource allocation in general.

Conclusions

Our conclusion underscores the significance of financial support as a crucial public policy to alleviate the impacts of pandemics on social, economic, and health inequalities by guaranteeing income, food security, and basic services and goods. We concluded that being an EA beneficiary was associated with increased testing demand and better adherence to mild social isolation measures. Emergency cash transfers may also alleviate financial instability for those unemployed or informally worked and those experiencing income reductions for shorter working hours. Furthermore, this aid facilitates the most vulnerable in seeking health services, searching for testing, and maintaining a degree of isolation. The importance of financial support is evident and should be incorporated into public health agendas and strategies to combat future pandemics, particularly in resource-constrained low- and middle-income countries.
