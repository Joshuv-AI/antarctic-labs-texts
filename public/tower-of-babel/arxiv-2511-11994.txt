Extremum-Seeking Boundary Control for Schrödinger-Type PDEs*
Paulo Henrique F. Biazetto, Gustavo Artur de Andrade, Tiago Roux Oliveira, Miroslav Krstic

Abstract
This paper addresses the design and analysis of an extremum-seeking (ES) controller for scalar static maps in the context of infinite-dimensional dynamics governed by complex-valued partial differential equations (PDEs) of Schrödinger type. The system is actuated at one boundary, and the map input is defined as a real-valued quadratic functional corresponding to the squared norm of the complex state at the uncontrolled boundary. An isomorphism between the complex Hilbert space and its two-dimensional real-valued representation is established to enable the use of the standard multivariable Newton-based ES method. To compensate for the PDE actuation dynamics, a boundary control strategy based on a two-step backstepping procedure is employed. With a perturbation-based estimate of the Hessian’s inverse, the local exponential stability to a small neighborhood of the unknown extremum point is proved. A numerical example illustrates the effectiveness of the proposed extremum-seeking methodology.



I
Introduction

The boundary control of the Schrödinger equation has attracted considerable attention within the control theory of distributed parameter systems due to its relevance in quantum dynamics, wave propagation, and flexible structures governed by dispersive partial differential equations (PDEs). Several boundary control strategies have been proposed to achieve stabilization, regulation, and observation of such systems. Early works focused on exact controllability via multiplier techniques and spectral analysis
[
14
]
, whereas more recent approaches have emphasized feedback stabilization by exploiting the dissipative mechanisms introduced through boundary conditions
[
16
,
15
,
2
]
. Dynamic boundary feedback laws—often involving interconnections with auxiliary systems such as heat or wave equations—have been shown to induce exponential or Gevrey-type stability of the closed-loop dynamics
[
17
]
. These designs rely on the spectral properties of the underlying Schrödinger operator, whose eigenvalues are typically located on the imaginary axis. By introducing suitable feedback, the spectrum can be shifted toward the left half-plane, ensuring asymptotic or exponential stability.

Beyond stabilization, the optimal control of Schrödinger-type equations has emerged as a key topic at the interface of control theory and quantum physics. Applications span quantum optics, material science, and quantum computing, where the goal is to manipulate quantum states by determining optimal external fields or potential profiles that drive the system toward a desired configuration
[
19
,
3
]
. Within this context, reference
[
1
]
formulated optimality conditions for control problems in complex Hilbert spaces and demonstrated their applicability to bilinear Schrödinger dynamics, while publication
[
9
]
provided foundational results on bilinear optimal control in abstract Schrödinger settings. These ideas have been extended to stochastic and nonlinear cases
[
5
,
18
]
, as well as to small-time controllability of bilinear quantum systems
[
4
]
. Parallel advances in quantum technologies have introduced high-fidelity optimization techniques for finite-dimensional approximations of Schrödinger dynamics
[
10
,
6
]
, enriching the theoretical foundation for the analysis and control of dispersive PDEs.

Motivated by these developments, the present work proposes an extremum seeking control (ESC) scheme for a linearized Schrödinger equation under boundary actuation. This is the first instance of ESC being applied to complex-valued PDEs. The objective is to steer the system toward an optimal steady state corresponding to the extremum of an unknown performance map, while compensating for the PDEs’ inherent phase shift and dispersive dynamics through a boundary-based feedback structure. The proposed approach extends the ESC framework introduced in
[
13
]
, where the compensator for the known actuation dynamics is designed via an infinite-dimensional backstepping transformation and driven by online estimates of the unknown gradient and Hessian of the static map. In the present setting, the static map is defined as a quadratic function of the real and imaginary parts of the system state. By exploiting the isomorphism between the complex plane and
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the real-time optimization problem is reformulated in the real plane, allowing the application of a Newton-based tuning law for Hessian’s inverse estimation
[
7
]
. Invoking the averaging theorem for infinite-dimensional systems
[
8
]
, we establish the asymptotic convergence of the closed-loop dynamics to a neighborhood of the extremum point, while preserving exponential stability of the underlying Schrödinger system. Numerical simulations are provided to illustrate the effectiveness of the proposed method and confirm its convergence properties.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
introduces the linearized Schrödinger PDE and the corresponding control objectives with ES. In Section
III
, we present the proposed ESC design. We begin by designing the demodulation and additive probing signals. Next, we derive the error dynamics and design a compensator using a backstepping methodology. The closed-loop stability and asymptotic convergence to the extremum are analyzed in Section
IV
. Section
V
illustrates the proposed control system through a numerical simulation example. Finally, Section
VI
brings the concluding remarks.

II
Problem Formulation

II-A

Schrodinger Mathematical Model

In this work, we consider the following linearized Schrodinger equation:

\displaystyle u_{t}(t,x)+i\,u_{xx}(t,x)=0,

(1)

\displaystyle u(t,1)=\theta(t),

(2)

\displaystyle u_{x}(t,0)=0,

(3)

\displaystyle u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),

(4)

where
t\in[0,\,+\infty)
is the time,
x\in[0,\,1]
is the space,
u
is the complex-valuated state,
i
is the imaginary unit, and
\theta\in\mathbb{C}
is the control input. The initial condition is given by
u_{0}(x)\in H^{2}(0,1)
.

II-B

Control Problem

The goal of the ES method is to minimize an unknown static map
y=Q(\Theta)
through real-time optimization, where
y^{\star}
and
\Theta^{\star}
denote the unknown maximum (minimum) and its maximizer (minimizer), respectively. It is assumed that the map is modeled as a real-valued quadratic functional on the complex Hilbert space
(\mathbb{C},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}})
, with
\langle z_{1},z_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\Re(\bar{z}_{1}z_{2})
, for all
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
, and

\displaystyle\Theta(t)=u(t,0).

(5)

Locally around
\Theta^{\star}
, the map is assumed quadratic:

Q(\Theta(t))=y^{\star}+\tfrac{1}{2}\langle\Theta(t)-\Theta^{\star},\ \mathcal{H}(\Theta(t)-\Theta^{\star})\rangle_{\mathbb{R}},

(6)

where
\mathcal{H}
is a bounded, self-adjoint, and negative definite operator (or positive definite, for minimization).

Using the isomorphism
\psi(\Theta)=(\Re\{\Theta\},\,\Im\{\Theta\})
, with inverse
\psi^{-1}(h)=h_{1}+ih_{2}
, with
h=(h_{1},h_{2})
and
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain the equivalent real representation

Q(\Theta(t))=y^{\star}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\psi(\Theta(t))-\psi(\Theta)^{\star})^{\top}H(\psi(\Theta(t))-\psi(\Theta^{\star})),

(7)

where
H\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is symmetric and negative definite matrix for maximization problems (or positive definite, for minimization).
This formulation treats the real and imaginary parts of
\Theta=(\Re\{\Theta\},\Im\{\Theta\})
as independent variables, enabling the ES controller design in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
while preserving consistency with the original complex PDE dynamics.

III
Extremum Seeking Boundary Control Design

III-A

Demodulation Signals

The demodulation signal
N
, which is used to estimate the Hessian of the static map by multiplying it with the output
y(t)
of the static map, is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=N(t)y(t),

(8)

where the elements of the
2\times 2
demodulating matrix
N
are

\displaystyle N_{11}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{16}{a_{1}^{2}}\left(\sin(\omega_{1}t)-\frac{1}{2}\right),

\displaystyle N_{12}(t)

\displaystyle=N_{21}(t)=\frac{4}{a_{1}a_{2}}\sin(\omega_{1}t)\sin(\omega_{2}t),

\displaystyle N_{22}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{16}{a_{2}^{2}}\left(\sin(\omega_{2}t)-\frac{1}{2}\right),

with
\omega_{1}\neq\omega_{2}
and
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
positive constants. The probing frequencies
\omega_{i}
’s can be selected as
\omega_{i}=\omega^{\prime}_{i}\omega=\mathcal{O}(\omega)
, for
i\in\{1,\,2\}
, where
\omega
is a positive constant and
\omega_{i}^{\prime}
is a rational number.

The estimation of the Hessian matrix in non-model-based optimization represents a key challenge, as the Newton-type control law requires not only an accurate estimate of the Hessian but also its inverse. Since the estimated matrix evolves in continuous time and may not be invertible at every instant, a dynamic approach is adopted to generate the inverse asymptotically. Following a Riccati-type formulation, the Hessian inverse estimator is governed by

\dot{\Gamma}(t)=\omega_{r}\Gamma(t)-\omega_{r}\Gamma(t)\hat{H}(t)\Gamma(t),

(9)

where
\omega_{r}>0
is a design constant.

Defining the estimation error of the inverse as

\tilde{\Gamma}(t)=\Gamma(t)-H^{-1},

(10)

its dynamics can be derived as

\dot{\tilde{\Gamma}}(t)=\omega_{r}(\tilde{\Gamma}(t)+H^{-1})[I_{2\times 2}-\hat{H}(t)(\tilde{\Gamma}(t)+H^{-1})].

(11)

This formulation guarantees that
\Gamma(t)
converges asymptotically to
H^{-1}
, while allowing the convergence rate to be tuned via
\omega_{r}
. The measurable signal

z(t)=\Gamma(t)G(t),

(12)

is introduced to derive the control law in the subsequent analysis.

In (
12
),
G
represents the gradient estimate and is given as
[
13
]

\displaystyle G(t)

\displaystyle=M(t)y(t),

\displaystyle M(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{a_{1}}\sin(\omega_{1}t),\;\;\frac{2}{a_{2}}\sin(\omega_{2}t)\right).

(13)

Lemma 1

The solution of problem (

)–(

\displaystyle R(t,x)=R_{r}(t,x)+iR_{i}(t,x),

where

\displaystyle R_{r}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{1}}{2}\sin(\omega_{1}t)\left[\cos(\sqrt{\omega_{1}}\,x)+\cosh(\sqrt{\omega_{1}}\,x)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{a_{2}}{2}\cos(\omega_{2}t)\left[\cos(\sqrt{\omega_{2}}\,x)-\cosh(\sqrt{\omega_{2}}\,x)\right],

\displaystyle R_{i}(t,x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{a_{1}}{2}\cos(\omega_{1}t)\left[\cos(\sqrt{\omega_{1}}\,x)-\cosh(\sqrt{\omega_{1}}\,x)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{a_{2}}{2}\sin(\omega_{2}t)\left[\cos(\sqrt{\omega_{2}}\,x)+\cosh(\sqrt{\omega_{2}}\,x)\right].

Proof.

Let
R(t,x)
be the solution of (
14
)–(
16
), and consider the power series expansion

\displaystyle R(t,x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}(t)\frac{x^{k}}{k!}.

(18)

Substituting (
18
) into (
14
)–(
16
) yields the recurrence relation
a_{k+2}(t)=i\,\dot{a}_{k}(t)
.

From the boundary conditions (
15
)–(
16
), one obtains

a_{0}(t)=a_{1}\sin(\omega_{1}t)+i\,a_{2}\sin(\omega_{2}t),\qquad a_{1}(t)=0.

Consequently, by the recurrence relation, all the odd coefficients vanish, i.e.,
a_{2k+1}(t)=0
,
k\geq 0
.

For the even indices, repeated use of the recurrence formula gives
a_{2k}(t)=i^{\,k}\,a_{0}^{(k)}(t)

Using the derivatives of the sine function, this expression can be rewritten as

\displaystyle S_{\omega}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\sin(\omega t)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\omega x^{2})^{2m}}{(4m)!}

\displaystyle+i\,\omega\cos(\omega t)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\omega x^{2})^{2m+1}}{(4m+2)!}.

The two series above correspond to the well-known expansions

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\omega x^{2})^{2m}}{(4m)!}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\cos(\sqrt{\omega}\,x)+\cosh(\sqrt{\omega}\,x)\right],

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\omega x^{2})^{2m+1}}{(4m+2)!}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{\omega}\,x}\left[\cosh(\sqrt{\omega}\,x)-\cos(\sqrt{\omega}\,x)\right].

Substituting these identities into the expression for
S_{\omega}(t,x)
and then into
R(t,x)
yields the desired closed-form expressions for
R_{r}(t,x)
and
R_{i}(t,x)
. Hence, the result follows.

\square

First backstepping transformation:
We consider the ODE-PDE system (

\displaystyle\dot{\vartheta}(t)

\displaystyle=-K\vartheta(t)+v(t,0),

\displaystyle v_{t}(t,x)+iv_{xx}(t,x)

\displaystyle=0,

(49)

\displaystyle v_{x}(t,0)

\displaystyle=0,\quad v(t,1)=W(t),

The kernels

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle U(t)=W(t)-iK\int_{0}^{1}(1-y)\beta(t,y)\,dy-K\vartheta(t).

Second backstepping transformation:
Now, consider the following backstepping transformation:

\displaystyle w(t,x)=v(t,x)-\int_{0}^{x}\kappa(x,y)v(t,y)dy,

(59)

to transform (

)-(

Differentiating (

\displaystyle\kappa_{xx}(x,y)-\kappa_{yy}(x,y)=ic\kappa(x,y),

(60)

\displaystyle\kappa_{y}(x,0)=0,\quad\kappa(x,x)=-i\frac{c}{2}x.

(61)

The solution to the PDE (

)-(

\displaystyle\kappa(x,y)=\kappa_{r}(x,y)+i\kappa_{i}(x,y),

(62)

where

\displaystyle\kappa_{r}(x,y)

\displaystyle=x\sqrt{\frac{c}{2(x^{2}-y^{2})}}\left[-\ber_{1}\biggl(\sqrt{c(x^{2}-y^{2})}\biggl)\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\bei_{1}\biggl(\sqrt{c(x^{2}-y^{2})}\biggl)\right],

\displaystyle\kappa_{i}(x,y)

\displaystyle=x\sqrt{\frac{c}{2(x^{2}-y^{2})}}\left[\ber_{1}\biggl(\sqrt{c(x^{2}-y^{2})}\biggl)\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\bei_{1}\biggl(\sqrt{c(x^{2}-y^{2})}\biggl)\right],

Then, evaluating (

\displaystyle W(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\kappa(1,y)v(t,y)dy.

(63)

Plugging (

\displaystyle U(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{S}_{1}(y)v(t,y)dy+K\mathcal{S}_{2}\vartheta(t),

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}(y)

III-D
3
Invertibility of the Transformations

As demonstrated in

\displaystyle\beta(t,x)=v(t,x)-\int_{0}^{x}\xi(x,y)v(t,y)\,dy-\rho(x)\vartheta(t),

III-D
4
Implementable extremum seeking control law

Note that the control law (
64
) cannot be directly applied in practice because the measurement of
\vartheta
is not available. To circumvent this problem, we use the averaged version of the gradient and Hessian estimate (see
[
13
]
for more details)

\displaystyle G_{av}(t)=H\vartheta_{av}(t),\qquad\hat{H}_{av}=H.

(65)

In addition, recalling that
\psi
denotes the isomorphism mapping a complex number to its real-imaginary representation (see Section
II-B
), we have that

z_{av}(t)=\frac{1}{\Pi}\int_{0}^{\Pi}\Gamma(s)M(s)y(s)ds=\Gamma_{av}(t)H\psi(\vartheta_{av}(t)),

where
\Pi=2\pi\,\mbox{LCM}\{1/\omega_{i}\}
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
, and
\Gamma_{av}
and
\vartheta_{av}
denote the average version of
\Gamma
and
\vartheta
, respectively.

Since
\tilde{\Gamma}_{av}(t)=\Gamma_{av}(t)-H^{-1}
, it follows that

\displaystyle z_{av}(t)=\psi(\vartheta_{av}(t))+\tilde{\Gamma}_{av}(t)H\psi(\vartheta_{av}(t)).

(66)

The linearization of the above expression at
H^{-1}
results in
z_{av}(t)=\psi(\vartheta_{av}(t))
. Averaging (
64
) and using the above results, we obtain

\displaystyle U_{av}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{S}_{1\,}(y)v_{av}(t,y)dy+K\,\vartheta_{av}(t).

(67)

For the stability analysis in which the averaging theory, for infinite-dimensional systems, is used, we employ a low-pass filter for the above feedback controller and then derive an infinite-dimensional averaging-based feedback given by

\displaystyle U(t)=\mathcal{T}\left\{\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{S}_{1}(y)v(t,y)dy+K\,\psi^{-1}(z(t))\right\},

(68)

where
\psi^{-1}
is the inverse of
\psi
(see Section
II-B
), and
\mathcal{T}
is the low pass operator defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\{g(t)\}=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{c_{f}}{s+c_{f}}\right\}*g(t),

being
c_{f}>0
the cutoff frequency,
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
is the inverse Laplace transform, and
*
is the convolution operation.

IV
Stability and convergence analysis

The proof of stability is now presented.

Theorem 1

For sufficiently large
c_{f}>0
, there exists
\bar{\omega}(c_{f})>0
such that
\forall\omega>\bar{\omega}
, the error dynamics has a unique exponentially stable periodic solution in
t
of period
\Pi=2\pi\,\mbox{LCM}\{1/\omega_{i}\}
, for
i\in\{1,\,2\}
, denoted by
\tilde{\Gamma}^{\Pi}
,
\vartheta^{\Pi}
and
\beta^{\Pi}(x,t)
, satisfying
\Psi(t)\leq\mathcal{O}(1/\omega)
, where

\Psi(t)=\left(|\tilde{\Gamma}^{\Pi}(t)|^{2}+|\vartheta^{\Pi}(t)|^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}|\beta^{\Pi}(t,x)|^{2}dx\right.\\
\left.+\int_{0}^{1}|\beta^{\Pi}_{x}(t,x)|^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}.

(69)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\lim\sup_{t\rightarrow\infty}|\theta(t)-\Theta^{*}|

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(|a|\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{\omega}}+1/\omega\right),

(70)

\displaystyle\lim\sup_{t\rightarrow\infty}|\Theta(t)-\Theta^{*}|

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(|a|+1/\omega),

(71)

\displaystyle\lim\sup_{t\rightarrow\infty}|y(t)-y^{*}|

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(|a|^{2}+1/\omega^{2}),

(72)

where
|a|=\sqrt{a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}}
.

Proof.

Average Model of the Closed-loop System.
Recalling that
\psi^{-1}(z_{av}(t))=\vartheta_{av}(t)
, we obtain, for
\omega
sufficiently large, the averaged version of the closed-loop system (
28
)–(
31
), with control law (
68
):

\displaystyle\dot{\vartheta}_{av}(t)=\beta_{av}(t,0),

(73)

\displaystyle(\beta_{av})_{t}(t,x)+i(\beta_{av})_{xx}(t,x)=0,

(74)

\displaystyle(\beta_{av})_{x}(t,0)=0,

(75)

\displaystyle\dot{\beta}_{av}(t,1)=-c_{f}\beta_{av}(t,1)+c_{f}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{S}_{1}(y)v_{av}(t,y)dy

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\!\!+c_{f}\mathcal{S}_{2}\vartheta_{av}(t),

(76)

\displaystyle v_{av}(t,x)=\beta_{av}(t,x)+i\int_{0}^{x}K(x-y)\beta_{av}(t,y)dy

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\!\!+K\vartheta_{av}(t).

(77)

Backstepping transformation and target system.
Using backstepping transformations analogous to (
47
)–(
59
), the averaged dynamics (
73
)–(
77
) can be mapped accordingly:

\displaystyle\dot{\vartheta}_{av}(t)=-K\vartheta_{av}(t)+w_{av}(t,0),

(78)

\displaystyle(w_{av})_{t}(t,x)+i(w_{av})_{xx}(t,x)=-cw_{av}(t,x),

(79)

\displaystyle(w_{av})_{x}(t,0)=0,

(80)

\displaystyle w_{av}(t,1)=-c_{f}w_{av}(t,1)+c_{f}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{S}_{1}(y)v_{av}(t,y)dy

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\!\!+c_{f}\mathcal{S}_{2}\vartheta_{av}(t),

(81)

\displaystyle v_{av}(t,x)=w_{av}(t,x)+i\int_{0}^{x}K(x-y)w_{av}(t,y)dy

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\!\!+K\vartheta_{av}(t).

(82)

For clarity, we denote the averaged transformed states by
w_{av}
and
v_{av}
, distinguishing them from the previously defined variables
w
and
v
.

Lyapunov stability of the target system.
Consider the Lyapunov functional

\displaystyle V(t)

\displaystyle=|\vartheta_{av}(t)|^{2}+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\!\int_{0}^{1}\!|w_{av}(t,x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle+\frac{p_{2}}{2}\!\int_{0}^{1}\!|(w_{av})_{x}(t,x)|^{2}dx,

(83)

where
p_{1},p_{2}>0
are constants to be determined.

Differentiating (
83
) along the trajectories of
(
32
)–(
34
) yields, after assuming
c_{f}\to\infty
in (
68
) for simplicity,

\displaystyle\dot{V}(t)

\displaystyle=-2K|\vartheta_{av}(t)|^{2}+2\Re\{w_{av}(t,0)\overline{\vartheta_{av}(t)}\}

\displaystyle-c\!\int_{0}^{1}\!(p_{1}|w_{av}(t,x)|^{2}+p_{2}|(w_{av})_{x}(t,x)|^{2})dx

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}\!\!p_{1}\Im\{(w_{av})_{xx}(t,x)\overline{w_{av}(t,x)}\}dx

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}\!\!p_{2}\Im\{(w_{av})_{xxx}(t,x)\overline{(w_{av})_{x}(t,x)}\}dx.

(84)

By integrating by parts and using the boundary conditions

(w_{av})_{x}(t,0)=w_{av}(t,1)=0
, one verifies that the last two integrals vanish. Hence

\displaystyle\dot{V}(t)

\displaystyle=-2K|\vartheta_{av}(t)|^{2}+2\Re\{w_{av}(t,0)\overline{\vartheta_{av}(t)}\}

\displaystyle-c\!\int_{0}^{1}\!(p_{1}|w_{av}(t,x)|^{2}+p_{2}|(w_{av})_{x}(t,x)|^{2})dx.

Then, applying Young’s and Agmon’s inequalities, it follows that there exists
\gamma>0
such that

\displaystyle\dot{V}(t)\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}-K\right)|\vartheta_{av}(t)|^{2}+\left(\gamma-p_{2}\,c\right)\int_{0}^{1}|w_{av}(t,x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle+\left(\gamma-p_{3}\,c\right)\int_{0}^{1}|(w_{av})_{x}(t,x)|^{2}dx.

Then, choosing
p_{1},p_{2}>\frac{1+c^{2}}{c^{2}}
and
\alpha=\min\{K,c\}
, we obtain
\dot{V}\leq-\alpha V(t),
or

\displaystyle V(t)\leq V(0)\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha t}.

(85)

Moreover, the linearized average model of the Hessian inverse estimation error around
\tilde{\Gamma}_{av}=0
satisfies
\dot{\tilde{\Gamma}}_{av}(t)=-\omega_{r}\tilde{\Gamma}_{av}(t)
, which is exponentially stable for any
\omega_{r}>0
. Consequently, using these results and the invertibility of the backstepping transformations (
47
)–(
59
), the averaged closed-loop system (
86
)–(
90
) is exponentially stable.

Invoking averaging theorem.
Let
v(t,x):=\beta(t,x)-U(t)
. The coupled ODE–PDE dynamics (
28
)–(
31
) can be written as

\displaystyle\dot{\vartheta}

\displaystyle=v(t,0)+U(t),

(86)

\displaystyle v_{t}(t,x)+i\,v_{xx}(t,x)

\displaystyle=-\phi(U,v),

(87)

\displaystyle v_{x}(t,0)=v(t,1)

\displaystyle=0,

(88)

\displaystyle\dot{U}(t)

\displaystyle=\phi(U,v),

(89)

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{\Gamma}}(t)

\displaystyle=\omega_{r}\tilde{\Gamma}(t)-\omega_{r}\Gamma(t)\hat{H}(t)\tilde{\Gamma}(t),

(90)

where

\displaystyle\phi(U,v)

\displaystyle=-c_{f}U+c_{f}\!\int_{0}^{1}\!\mathcal{S}_{1}(y)\!\left(v(t,y)+U(t)\right)\!dy

\displaystyle+c_{f}\mathcal{S}_{2}\psi^{-1}(z(t)).

Let
\alpha(v)=\int_{0}^{1}S_{1}(y)v(y)dy
,
s_{1}=\int_{0}^{1}S_{1}(y)dy
, and define the linear operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{v}(\vartheta,U,\Gamma,v)=(v(0)+U,\,-c_{f}U,\,\omega_{r}\Gamma,\,iv^{\prime\prime}+c_{f}U),

with domain

\displaystyle D(\mathcal{A}_{v})

\displaystyle=\Big\{(\vartheta,U,\Gamma,v)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\times H^{2}(0,1)\ \big|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ v(1)=0,\ v^{\prime}(0)=0\Big\}.

Then, introducing

Y_{v}(t)=(\vartheta(t),\;U(t),\;\Gamma(t),\;v(t,\cdot))
, the closed-loop system (
86
)-(
90
) takes the abstract form

\frac{dY_{v}}{dt}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{v}Y_{v}(t)+J(\omega t,Y_{v}(t)),

(91)

with

\displaystyle J(\omega t,Y_{v})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0,\\
c_{f}\left(\alpha(v)+s_{1}U(t)+\mathcal{S}_{2}G(t)\right)\\
-\omega_{r}\Gamma(t)\hat{H}(t)\Gamma(t)\\
-c_{f}\left(\alpha(v)+s_{1}U(t)+\mathcal{S}_{2}G(t)\right)\end{array}\right).

The operator
\mathcal{A}_{v}
can be written as a matrix operator composed of the infinite-dimensional block
\mathcal{A}_{w}
and the finite-dimensional dynamics of
U
and
\Gamma
. Since
\mathcal{A}_{w}
and
\mathrm{diag}(-c_{f},\omega_{r})
each generate a
C_{0}
-semigroup on their respective spaces, the composite operator
\mathcal{A}_{v}
generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup on
\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\times L^{2}(0,1)
. As the PDE block has compact resolvent,
\mathcal{A}_{v}
also has compact resolvent, so its spectrum is discrete with finite algebraic multiplicity. Moreover, the nonlinear term
J(\omega t,Y_{v})
is Fréchet differentiable in
Y_{v}
, strongly continuous, and almost periodic in
t
, fulfilling the assumptions of the infinite-dimensional averaging theorem
[
13
]
. Hence, the closed-loop system (
86
)–(
90
) admits a periodic solution. Then, using (
85
) and the fact that
\tilde{\Gamma}_{av}
is exponentially stable, it follows that, for
\omega
sufficiently large, the closed-loop system (
86
)–(
90
), with
U
in (
68
), has a unique exponentially stable periodic solution around its equilibrium
satisfying
\Psi(t)\leq\mathcal{O}(1/\omega)
, with
\Psi
defined in (
69
).

Asymptotic convergence to a neighborhood of the
extremum.
The asymptotic convergence to a neighborhood of the extremum point follows from

|\theta(t)-\Theta^{*}|=|\tilde{\theta}(t)+S(t)|
, with

VI
Conclusions

The proposed extremum seeking framework optimizes a quadratic performance map linked to the boundary value of a linearized Schrödinger equation. This identifies the optimal steady state without prior knowledge of map parameters, using only real-time measurements. A boundary feedback law based on backstepping compensates for the infinite-dimensional actuation dynamics, enabling average-based estimation of the map’s gradient, Hessian and its inverse. The closed-loop system converges to a neighborhood of the optimum while maintaining exponential stability of the Schrödinger dynamics, thus bridging data-driven adaptive optimization and boundary control of dispersive PDEs using extremum seeking.
