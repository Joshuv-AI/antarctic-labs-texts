Interventions to improve help seeking for older adults living in the community with mental illness: a mixed methods review protocol
Authors: Peta Prindiville, Sunil Bhar, Maja Nedeljkovic, Penelope Schofield
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMental health illnesses are prevalent for older adults. Despite the effectiveness of treatments for improving mental health, the uptake is low. Interventions that target barriers to mental health help seeking lead to improved treatment uptake in adults and younger populations. However, the effects, characteristics, and experience of these interventions for older adults remain unclear. The first aim of this mixed methods review is to explore the effects of interventions for improving help seeking in community dwelling older adults with mental health difficulties. The secondary aims are to describe the characteristics, feasibility and acceptability of these interventions to interest-holders.MethodsThe review protocol was developed in accordance with the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis using Mixed Methods Systematic Reviews. An electronic search of Medline, PsychINFO, EBSCOhost, Scopus and Web of Science will be performed based on inclusion criteria; older adults (60 years +), with mental health symptoms or a diagnosis, and interventions to improve mental health help seeking attitudes, intentions or behaviour. Quantitative data will be synthesised using the SWiM guideline and synthesis approach will be determined based on the data available within the studies. Secondary data will be synthesised from mixed method and qualitative studies using a combination of descriptive statistics and JBI meta-aggregation. The separate synthesis will then be integrated using the JBI convergent segregated approach and a narrative summary provided. Systematic searches, data extraction, analysis and write up are expected to be completed by March 2026.ConclusionsThe results from this review will provide evidence for the effects of help seeking interventions for older adults living with mental health difficulties. We also aim to investigate the characteristics, feasibility and acceptability to interest-holders and integrate these findings to help clarify how future policy development and programs can be developed to facilitate help seeking in older adults with such difficulties.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO CRD42023456657Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-025-02959-0.

Background

According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), the proportion of older adults (60 +) will double to 22% by 2050, constituting 2.1 billion people globally [1]. As people live longer, research and clinical attention has focussed on understanding what it means to live in good physical and psychological health, and the economic and social impact of living with chronic diseases [2]. Approximately 15% of older adults have a diagnosed mental health condition excluding dementia [1] however, this is likely an under-representation of true prevalence due to diagnostic challenges in older populations and missed detection [3]. Even less seek help with reports younger adults are twice as likely to seek mental health services compared to older adults [4]. A number of interventions have been developed to improve help seeking for mental health support in this population, but there is no review on whether these interventions are effective and whether they are feasible and acceptable for older adults, and the characteristics of effective vs non effective interventions. Such an overview would help guide the development of future interventions.

Help seeking is a broad concept that centres around the ability to recognise psychological distress and then seek out interpersonal assistance [5]. One definition of help seeking that has been proposed by Rickwood and Thomas is “an adaptive coping process that is an attempt to obtain external assistance to deal with a mental health concern” ([6] p.180), involving attitudes, intentions and behaviour as different constructs to measure. Help seeking attitudes include elements such as psychological openness, willingness to seek help, and stigma indifference [7]. Intentions are the plan to communicate the problem, obtain assistance to address the mental health problem [8]. Help seeking behaviour is any observation action taken to solve psychological problems through a planned response [9]. Individuals who seek help may do so from various sources of help: formal, informal or self-help programs [6]. Formal sources refer to health professionals such as psychologists. Informal sources refer to family members and friends. Self-help sources refer to treatment programs without an interpersonal component [6]. Literature suggests older adults avoid formal avenues and prefer seeking informal or self-help options [10–13].

As older adults under-utilise mental health supports, it is important to understand the reasons why so interventions can be designed to target these. It is frequently reported that older adults face many barriers to treatment and these can be classified into individual, clinical, and systemic barriers [14]. Individual barriers include attitudes, beliefs, mental health literacy, and personality factors [13, 15, 16]. Clinical barriers are poor staff awareness and confidence in working with older adult mental health, inadequate training and a predominate focus on healthcare workers prioritising the treatment of physical health over mental health conditions [14, 17]. Systemic barriers are clinical pathways and policy development and practical barriers around the logistics of attending appointments, costs and transportation [18].

While clinical and systemic barries have been found to account for poor help seeking, research is growing on the importance of addressing individual barriers [19]. Across all age ranges, mental health stigma is a significant individual barrier for older adults to overcome [20]. In the older adult literature, low perceived need for help is the strongest predictor of poor help seeking for mental health support [14, 19, 21]. Other individual factors, such as self-reliance, and treatment beliefs around medication and costs are also regularly reported [13, 21]. This suggests interventions that target individual barriers, in particular mental health need, could have great success in influencing older adults help seeking decision making.

Unique to older adults is the reliance on family and carers to influence help seeking decision making [11], more so than younger adults who rely on a wider network of friends and independent information [22]. Families often have a crucial role in supporting older adults to seek formal mental health support so interventions targeting the support system, such as improving mental health literacy of the family, may be more effective at facilitating behaviour change and greater mental health service help seeking [11, 23]. Alternatively, interventions designed to target clinical decision making and detection in primary care settings, such as general practitioners, may be more effective at reaching the older adult to prioritise their mental health [24, 25]. This review will be the first to classify interventions according to the three categories of individual, clinical and systemic.

The effects of help seeking interventions has been reviewed broadly. A large-scale meta-analysis of help seeking interventions found them to be effective across the lifespan for formal mental health support but not informal help [26]. This review did not focus on older adults with only three of the ninety-seven articles exclusively representing this cohort and only including randomised controlled trials (RCTs). Most help seeking interventions are designed and tested on younger populations [27]. Interventions for older adults require adaptation to accommodate functional (e.g., physical and cognitive) deficits associated with ageing [28, 29]. Given the variability in barriers and sources of help targeted, and unique characteristics of older adults with more functional impairments, it is important to determine the effects and examine the characteristics of these help seeking interventions [10].

Even if interventions are associated with a positive outcome, there may be a poor interest-holder experience of delivering or receiving these interventions which impacts on the implementation and translation across to clinical practice and healthcare policy [30]. Reporting on issues, such as patient experience, feasibility and acceptability is growing [31]. Patient experience data is routinely incorporated into Australian healthcare settings when developing programs and is seen as critical to the success of intervention penetration and adoption [32]. In accordance with the updated Framework for Developing and Evaluating Complex Interventions [33], research priorities that shape policy go beyond unbiased estimates of intervention effectiveness from RCTs and consider how these interventions interact within a broader context. Therefore, other research designs, such as pilot projects, feasibility and implementation studies or mixed methods approaches will be included to get a better understanding of these complex interventions.

There is a need to review the help seeking interventions specific to older adults, not only to determine which interventions are effective, but also to describe the characteristics of these interventions and how they are experienced. The first aim of this review is to summarise research evidence for the effects of help seeking interventions for improving help seeking attitudes, intentions and behaviour in older adults. Secondary aims of the review are to examine the interventions for (a) characteristics and target of interventions (individual, clinical, systemic), (b) source of help promoted (formal, informal or self-help), and (c) feasibility and acceptability of these interventions. Third, these results will be integrated together to form a narrative synthesis of the current state of help seeking interventions for older adults with mental illness living in community.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This protocol was developed to comply with the recommendations outlined by the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis using Mixed Methods Systematic Reviews [34]. The protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, CRD 42023456657).

Eligibility criteria

Population and setting

Studies are included in the review if they involve (1) older adults, defined as 60 years and above, or if the sample includes younger adults, the overall sample mean age is above 60 years old, or findings for older adults are reported separately to younger adults, (2) community-dwelling older adults, and (3) experiencing a mental health disorder (based on diagnostic assessment) or clinically significant mental health symptoms (based on scores above a defined cut-off on validated mental health scales). Exclusion criteria are studies involving (1) individuals living in residential aged care settings or (2) individuals seeking help for symptoms of neurodevelopmental or neurodegenerative disorders.

Intervention

The phenomenon of interest is interventions that target help seeking attitudes about, intentions to seek or behavioural actions to obtain, formal, informal or self-help mental health support. There are no restrictions on type of intervention.

Comparator groups

No restrictions.

Outcome and measures

At least one of the help-seeking constructs (attitudes, intention or behaviour) will be measured as an outcome for quantitative studies addressing the first aim. Help seeking will be measured quantitatively by self-report questionnaires or observer rated checklists. Secondary outcomes include feasibility and interest holders’ acceptability of the intervention and will be measured by quantitative and qualitative data, such as descriptive statistics, themes, and quotes from interest holders.

Study design

The inclusion of full-text peer-reviewed primary studies that are written in English and explore the mental health help seeking phenomenon. Excluded publication types include reviews, meta-analysis, and grey literature.

Search strategy

The following electronic databases will be searched: MEDLINE (PubMed), CINAHL Complete (EBSCOhost), Psychology and Behavioural Sciences Collection (EBSCOhost), Academic Search Complete (EBSCOhost), PsychINFO, Scopus, Web of Science.

The search strategy was developed in consultation with a Swinburne University librarian and will begin with Medline, PsychINFO and CINAHL Complete to establish MeSH terms and controlled vocabulary; these terms will include “mental disorder” AND help seeking behavior AND aged. See Supplementary File for full search strategy for each database. The remaining databases (Scopus and Web of Science) will be searched using Boolean/Phrase combinations of truncated word searchers. The electronic database search will be supplemented by manually searching reference lists of relevant studies.

Data collection and analysis

Selection of studies

The titles and abstracts of studies produced from the search strings will be exported to a reference management software, Covidence with duplicates removed. Titles and abstracts of records will be screened by two reviewers. Discrepancies in whether to include or exclude a study based such screening will be resolved by involving a third reviewer. The full text of included records will be checked against the eligibility criteria by at least two reviewers. Authors of these records will be emailed for further information if required. References of eligible records will be ‘hand searched’ by the first reviewer to identify additional studies relevant to this review. Data will then be extracted from the sample of studies by two reviewers.

Assessment of methodological quality

The Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) Version 2018 [36] will be used to appraise the risk of bias of individual quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods studies. There are four stages to the MMAT; applying the screening questions to each study, filtering the corresponding MMAT criteria to the study design, appraisal of the studies by 2 reviewers, determining an overall quality score [36].

Data synthesis

As this is a mixed methods review with expected heterogeneity in study design, intervention types and outcomes, the Synthesis Without Meta-Analysis (SWiM) reporting guideline will be used [37] for quantitative studies that address the first research question. There are nine items to report: (1) quantitative studies will be grouped according to help seeking target (attitudes, intentions, behaviour, or combined). (2) The standardised metric used to present intervention effects across the studies will be determined based on the data available within the studies. (3) Synthesis method that best describes the intervention effects. (4) Criteria used to prioritise reporting of some study findings over others. (5) Heterogeneity in reported effects will be grouped visually using tables according to target of intervention (individual, clinical or systemic target). (6) Certainty of evidence will not be presented in this review as per recommendations by JBI Mixed Methods Review Methodology Group [38]. (7) Data will be presented via tabulation to present the effects ordered by risk of bias (using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool). An effect direction plot will accompany the synthesis. (8) A description of the synthesis findings. (9) Limitations of the synthesis, metric used and changes to groups will be discussed.

Secondary aims will include a combination of quantitative and qualitative data and will be synthesised separately using the convergent segregated approach recommended by the JBI Mixed Methods Review Methodology Group [38]. The approach taken for feasibility data will be tabulating the descriptive data on rate of uptake, attrition and attendance rates. Qualitative data will be pooled through JBI meta-aggregation methods if appropriate, or narrative summary [38]. Once separate synthesis have been conducted, they will be synthesised together using narrative synthesis to produce an overall summary of these interventions.

Systematic searchers, data extraction and analysis, write up are expected to be completed by March 2026.

Discussion

Mental health difficulties are commonly reported by older adults but under-diagnosed with missed opportunity for treatment to enhance quality of life. With a rapidly growing aging population, this will have significant individual, social and economic implications if left untreated [4]. Unfortunately, this cohort avoids mental health care due to a variety of individual, clinical and systemic barriers [14, 39]. Help seeking interventions have been developed to target attitudes, intention and behaviour and have been found to be effective for younger adults seeking formal support [26, 27] but little is known about older adults. Additionally, the effects of interventions encouraging informal mental health support remains unclear [26], but is important to know given older adults preference for informal support in the first instance. Little is known about the effects and characteristics of help seeking interventions that target all sources of help and whether they are feasible and accepted by older adult populations. This mixed methods review will address this gap and provide insights into future directions for help seeking in older adults with mental health concerns.
