Forest Polynomials and Pattern Avoidance
Annie Guo, Dora Woodruff

Abstract
Forest polynomials, recently introduced by Nadeau and Tewari, can be thought of as a quasisymmetric analogue for Schubert polynomials. They have already been shown to exhibit interesting interactions with Schubert polynomials; for example, Schubert polynomials decompose positively into forest polynomials. We further describe this relationship by showing that a Schubert polynomial 𝔖 w \mathfrak{S}_{w} is a forest polynomial exactly when w w avoids a set of 6 6 patterns. This result adds to the long list of properties of Schubert polynomials that are controlled by pattern avoidance.



1
Introduction

Forest polynomials, first introduced by Nadeau and Tewari in their study of the permutahedral variety
[
9
]
, are a quasisymmetric analogue for Schubert polynomials. They form a linear basis for a quasisymmetric variant of the coinvariant algebra
[
9
]
, represent cohomology classes for a quasisymmetric variant of the flag variety
[
2
]
, and have rich combinatorial properties, many of which parallel the combinatorial properties of Schubert polynomials (for example, see
[
8
]
).

Forest polynomials and Schubert polynomials also interact with each other in interesting ways. For instance, Nadeau and Tewari showed that Schubert polynomials decompose positively into forest polynomials
[
9
]
, and pipe dream-like models have been developed for forest polynomials
[
8
]
. Given this, it is natural to ask: when do these two classes of polynomials coincide?

One would hope for the answer to this question to be expressible in terms of
pattern avoidance
. Indeed, pattern avoidance controls many combinatorial and algebraic properties of Schubert polynomials. For example, a Schubert polynomial
\mathfrak{S}_{w}
is a flagged Schur polynomial if and only if
w
avoids
2143

[
7
]
, it is a standard elementary monomial if and only if
w
avoids
312
and
1432

[
11
]
, and its coefficients are all
0
or
1
if and only if
w
avoids a list of
12
patterns
[
4
]
. Much work has also been dedicated to bounding the principal specialization of
\mathfrak{S}_{w}
in terms of pattern counts in
w
(for example, see
[
10
]
or
[
6
]
).

Along these lines, our main result is that the intersection between Schubert polynomials and forest polynomials is also controlled by pattern avoidance:

Theorem 1.1
.

A Schubert polynomial
\mathfrak{S}_{w}
is also a forest polynomial if and only if
w
avoids the following set of patterns:

\{1432,2413,2431,14523,32154,341265\}

There is a second interpretation of this result worth mentioning. When they introduced forest polynomials in
[
9
]
, Nadeau and Tewari defined
\Omega
-equivalence, an equivalence relation on the set of reduced words of a given permutation. To each equivalence class
\mathcal{C}
, they attach a corresponding forest polynomial
\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{C})
.

Theorem 1.2
.

if
\mathcal{G}_{w}
denotes the set of
\Omega
-equivalence classes of the reduced words of
w^{-1}
, then:

\mathfrak{S}_{w}=\sum_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{G}_{w}}\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{C})

Therefore, asking which Schubert polynomials are forest polynomials is equivalent to asking which permutations
w
have exactly one
\Omega
-equivalence class.

Organization

.

2.3
Pipe dreams and ladder moves

Pipe dreams
are an important combinatorial model for Schubert polynomials developed by Billey-Jockusch-Stanley
[
3

(see also
[
5
]

[
1
]

D\subset\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
satisfying the following condition: the ordered product of the generators
s_{i+j-1}
over all
(i,j)\in D
(taken row by row from left to right, bottom to top) is a reduced word for
w
. From now on, all pipe dreams we consider will be reduced.

The set of pipe dreams of
w
is denoted
\text{PD}(w)
. Every pipe dream
D
of

can be assigned a

:

\text{wt}(D)=\prod_{(i,j)\in D}x^{i}

Bergeron and Billey
[
1
]
proved that pipe dreams for

correspond to monomials in

:

Theorem 2.9
.

\mathfrak{S}_{w}=\sum_{D\in\text{PD}(w)}\text{wt}(D)

Pictorially, a pipe dream

can be viewed as a
strand diagram
: place a
crossing
at every
(i,j)\in D
and a pair of
elbows
at every
(i,j)\notin D
. The
row
of a crossing is
j
, and its
column
is
i
. This process results in a pseudo-line arrangement, where each strands connects
(k,0)
to
(0,w(k))
and no two strands intersect more than once.

2.4
Pattern avoidance

Let
w\in S_{n}
be a permutation. We say a permutation

N

if there exist indices

such that
W_{i_{1}},W_{i_{2}}\dots W_{i_{n}}
are in the same relative order as
w_{1},w_{2}\dots w_{n}
.

One of the patterns listed in Theorem
1.1
is
1432
, and its role in the proof is different from the other patterns listed. It arises because of the following important theorem of Gao
[
6
]
:

Theorem 2.15
.

A permutation

avoids the pattern

if and only if every reduced pipe dream for
w
can be obtained from
P_{\text{bott}}(w)
via a sequence of simple ladder moves.

That is, any pipe dream for
w
can be obtained by sliding crossings along their starting diagonals.

3
An injection from labelings to pipe dreams

3.1
Overarching proof strategy

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
, our strategy is as follows. Let

\text{code}(F)=\text{code}(w)

Finally, we prove the opposite direction: that the patterns appearing in Theorem

4.2

First, we treat the pattern

Lemma 4.3

If

Now, let us consider only permutations which avoid

is not a forest polynomial for a pattern

Definition 4.4

Given some permutation

For example,

. The following fact is standard:

Lemma 4.5

Given a permutation

from

Inserting values has a simple effect on

. For example,

Lemma 4.6
.

If a permutation

As remark

, we can find a ‘bad’ parent-right child pair
