Cascading effect of early sensory differences on subsequent autistic traits in infants later diagnosed with autism: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Shihuan Wang, Cong You, Kaiyun Chen, Linru Liu, Yu Xing, Shaoli Lv, Yuanyuan Zou, Fengjing Liang, Yanting Xu, Qianying Ye, Yijie Li, Leran Xue, Feixia Zhang, Hongzhu Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSensory differences constitute core diagnostic features of autism. The cascading effect model posits that early sensory differences may be prospectively associated with subsequent autistic traits, whereas empirical evidence in infancy remains scarce. This study aims to characterize trajectory differences of early sensory profiles and examine the cascading effect on subsequent autistic traits in infants later diagnosed with autism.MethodsIn this prospective longitudinal study, 134 Chinese infants were followed from 12 to 36 months of age. Trajectory differences in four sensory profiles (sensitivity, avoiding, registration, and seeking) were compared between diagnostic groups using the Toddler Sensory Profile-2. The cascading effect were examined through Structural Equation Modeling and temporally ordered path analysis to test direct and indirect effects of early sensory differences on subsequent autistic traits via the mediation of social skills in autism.ResultsCompared to typical development, infants later diagnosed with autism exhibited significantly elevated sensory sensitivity and sensory avoiding, along with elevated sensory registration scores at 12 months, with sensory sensitivity showing differential trajectories over time (F = 3.25, p = 0.004). Elevated sensory avoiding at 18 months exerted an indirect effect on social-communication at 36 months via the mediating role of social skills at 24 months (B = 0.08, p = 0.007). Additionally, temporally ordered path analysis revealed that elevated sensory avoiding at 18 months negatively predicted social skills at 24 months (β = -0.19, p = 0.030). Notably, in an exploratory analysis, increased sensory seeking at 18 months positively predicted adaptive skills at 36 months (B = 0.97, p = 0.031).ConclusionsSensory differences emerge as early as 12 months in infants who later receive an autistic diagnosis, with sensory sensitivity demonstrating distinct developmental trajectories. These findings provide prospective evidence to support the cascading effect model in autism, identifying early sensory avoiding as an important early correlate of subsequent social-communicative development. Furthermore, early sensory seeking behaviors may facilitate subsequent adaptive functioning, highlighting the heterogeneous developmental pathways within autism.Trial registrationwww.chictr.org.cn; Trial No: ChiCTR2100049811; Trial Date:2021.08.10.Keywords: Autism, Cascading effect, Sensory, Social, Adaptive, Trajectory

Background

Sensory differences affect up to 90% of autistic individuals [1], with potential impacts across all sensory modalities. According to the diagnostic nosology of sensory processing in autism, subtypes are recognized: hyper-reactivity, hypo-reactivity, and sensory seeking [2]. Sensory hypo-reactivity and hyper-reactivity have been mostly reported in autistic children [3, 4]. Dunn’s model of sensory processing [5], grounded in the neuroscience-behavioral interaction theory, is widely used to assess sensory processing. The model classifies sensory patterns into four quadrants based on neurological thresholds and behavioral responses: registration (high neurological thresholds and passive behavioral responses), seeking, sensitivity, and avoiding. Studies based on Dunn’s model indicate that autistic individuals frequently exhibit elevated sensory low registration and sensory avoiding [6, 7], reflecting sensory differences at both neurological threshold extremes.

Trajectories of early sensory differences in autism

Understanding the trajectories of sensory differences represents an important research focus for autism. Evidence has demonstrated that sensory differences emerge during the first year of life in autistic toddlers [8–10], suggesting sensory differences may serve as potential precursors to autistic diagnosis. Specifically, Wolff and colleagues found elevated sensory reactivity at 12 months in autistic toddlers, with differences becoming more pronounced at 24 months [11]. Moreover, Chen and colleagues demonstrated that rates of change in sensory hypo- and hyper-reactivity effectively differentiated autism from developmental delay [12], highlighting the importance of examining dynamic sensory trajectories. While a few studies using Dunn’s model have demonstrated greater low registration in autistic toddlers [13, 14], early sensory trajectories still remain largely unexplored in this population. Addressing this critical gap necessitates prospective cohort designs to comprehensively delineate quadrant-specific trajectories in early childhood.

Cascading effects model of early sensory development in autism

Preliminary longitudinal studies examining associations between early sensory quadrant profiles and subsequent autistic traits in autistic infants or toddlers have yielded mixed findings, with some reporting negative associations [14, 15] and others observing null effects [16, 17]. Early sensory differences may trigger developmental cascades in autism [18, 19], while the mechanisms underlying such effects remain poorly understood. Sensory differences have been documented as early as 6 months of age in infants later diagnosed with autism [20, 21], preceding key social milestones such as joint attention at 14–18 months. Russo and colleagues have recently formalized a cascading effect model that explicitly posits early sensory differences as the primary engine generating downstream consequences for social-communication development [22]. Despite strong theoretical support, empirical validation of this model remains limited and inconsistent: longitudinal data have found that sensory differences at 18 months predicted social-communication difficulties at 36 months in autistic toddlers [23], and a further study has implicated reduced social attention as a mediating factor [24]. Meanwhile, a study has shown that sensory differences at 22 months emerged as a negative predictor of social-communication outcomes at 3–5 years in autistic children [25]. In contrast, a recent study emphasized that sensory differences were not necessarily detrimental to social-communication in autistic toddlers [26]. Unfortunately, no studies have reported the cascading effect using Dunn’s model.

One proposed pathway involves developmental cascades, whereby early sensory differences propagate through social skills [27, 28]. In infancy and early childhood (0–3 years), social skills encompass nonverbal social behaviors including eye gaze, joint attention and gestures [29], which provide candidate pathways for such effects. Yet whether social skills specifically mediate the relation between early sensory differences and subsequent autistic traits in autism has not been empirically tested.

In summary, the current study employs a prospective cohort design to address two primary objectives: (a) to characterize sensory trajectories using Dunn’s model in infants later diagnosed with autism; (b) to examine whether early sensory differences indirectly influence subsequent autistic traits via social-skills pathway. We hypothesized that they might exhibit distinct sensory trajectories, with these differences emerging as early as 12 months and becoming more pronounced over time. In addition, the effect of early sensory differences on subsequent autistic traits might be mediated through social skills.

Methods

Study design

This study is part of the ongoing Longitudinal Chinese Autism Study of Early Development (LCAS-ED), a prospective cohort study conducted at the Child Development and Behavioral Center of the Third Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University, China. To enhance the broader representativeness of the sample—rather than focusing solely on EL infants—the present study employed a multimodal recruitment strategy, which maximizes sample representativeness through the integration of diverse recruitment channels: (1) Direct referrals from developmental pediatricians; (2) Recommendation from child healthcare doctors child healthcare doctors; (3) Recruitment drives in the community targeting urban and suburban populations; (4) Social media campaigns using targeted advertisements for families of infants, with content optimized for platforms popular among caregivers (e.g., WeChat). This integrated clinical-community-digital framework enhanced geodemographic diversity, mitigated selection bias, and improved epidemiological robustness.

Participants

Prospective participants were included as: (1) Infants aged 6–18 months at enrollment; (2) Presence of at least one parent co-residing with the infant; (3) Parents demonstrating literacy proficiency in Chinese sufficient to complete standardized assessments. Exclusion Criteria: (1) Genetic syndromes associated with autism (e.g., fragile X, Rett, Down, or Williams syndromes), confirmed by clinical genetic testing and medical records; (2) Comorbid neurological disorders affecting neurodevelopment: active epilepsy, inborn errors of metabolism, or history of central nervous system infection; (3) Perinatal complications-birth weight < 2,500 g, gestational age < 37 or > 42 weeks; (4) Documented brain injury (e.g., hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy) or teratogenic exposures (prenatal alcohol/tobacco) with neurodevelopmental sequelae; (5) Multiple gestation births. A total of 134 infants were included in the cohort study. Infants underwent longitudinal assessments at 12 months (T12), 18 months (T18), 24 months (T24), and 36 months (T36).

This study employed an accelerated longitudinal design to enrich the sample for eventual autistic diagnoses, we recruited 134 participants through two pathways: 53 participants (39.6%) were elevated likelihood infant-siblings, and 81 participants (60.4%) were referred from community or healthcare centers based on parental or professional developmental concerns.

Attrition and retention

Of the 134 participants enrolled, 67 (50.0%) completed assessment at T12, 113 (84.3%) toddlers completed at T18, 126 (94.0%) toddlers completed at T24, and 133 (99.3%) participants completed at T36. The high attrition at 12 months reflected the enrollment window of 6–18 months, which precluded approximately half the sample from having a 12-month assessment. To minimize subsequent attrition and maximize retention, we implemented a structured follow-up protocol. Each family was assigned a dedicated follow-up physician who maintained regular contact through telephone calls, text messages, or WeChat (a widely used Chinese social media platform). These proactive communications reminded families of upcoming appointments and facilitated rescheduling when necessary. This intensive retention strategy contributed to the high follow-up completion rates observed from 18 months onward.

Participant discontinuation and missing data

At 24 months, six participants missed their assessment window due to scheduling conflicts (e.g., family travel, acute illness, or competing work commitments); these families remained in the study and completed subsequent timepoints, thus these cases were classified as missed assessments rather than withdrawals. At 36 months, one family withdrew due to transportation barriers; an exit interview was completed, with the parent citing inability to afford repeated long-distance travel as contributing factors.

Diagnostic assessment and classification

At 36 months, participants received comprehensive developmental evaluations by board-certified specialists who were blind to participant diagnostic status, group assignment. The assessment battery included: (a) Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (ADOS-2) [30]; (b) Mullen Scales of Early Learning (MSEL) [31]; (c) systematic behavioral observation; and (d) developmental history was systematically assessed through structured caregiver interviews using the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised (ADI-R) [32]. Ultimately, 134 participants were included in diagnostic classification and analysis.

Clinical Best Estimate (CBE) Procedure: Final diagnostic classifications were determined using a standardized CBE protocol [33, 34]. Two experienced, board-certified clinicians independently reviewed all available data (ADOS-2 algorithms, MSEL, behavioral observations, and developmental histories) and rendered preliminary diagnoses according to DSM-5 criteria. Inter-rater agreement before reconciliation was monitored throughout data collection: prior to study initiation, clinicians achieved agreement (κ = 0.83) for ADOS-2 coding. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion, with a third senior clinician available for adjudication when needed. The CBE procedure classified participants into three groups:

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD, n = 47): Met DSM-5 ASD criteria and ADOS-2 Calibrated Severity Scores (CSS) ≥ 4;

Development Delays or Differences (DD, n = 32): participants who did not meet criteria for ASD but demonstrated developmental differences, operationally defined by meeting at least one of the following criteria [34]: (1) diagnosis of developmental delay (e.g., language delay, social communication delay) with subthreshold autistic traits; (2) ADOS-2 CSS ≥ 4, indicating clinically significant autistic traits despite subthreshold diagnostic classification; (3) developmental differences: two or more MSEL subtest T-scores ≤ 1.5 SD and/or one or more MSEL subtests ≤ 2 SD [35]. The classification criteria for the DD group in this study were consistent with those recently published by our team [36]. Importantly, the DD group is intentionally heterogeneous in composition and should be understood as a developmental contrast group rather than a cohesive nosological category. Its primary function is to distinguish autism-specific sensory trajectories from those associated with broader developmental differences.

Typical Development (TD, n = 55): Met neither ASD nor DD criteria.

Measures of sensory profiles

Sensory profiles were evaluated at 12, 18, 24, and 36 months using the Toddler Sensory Profile-2 (TSP-2) [37], a standardized, caregiver-report instrument assessing sensory processing in naturalistic contexts for toddlers aged 6–36 months. The TSP-2 operationalizes Dunn’s model of sensory processing, which conceptualizes individual differences along two dimensions: (a) neurological threshold and (b) self-regulation strategy. Cross-classifying these dimensions yields four distinct sensory profiles: sensory registration (high threshold/passive strategy; misses sensory input), sensory seeking (high threshold/active strategy; craves intense or prolonged sensory input), sensory sensitivity (low threshold/passive strategy; distracted or distressed by sensory input), and sensory avoiding (low threshold/active strategy; limits or withdraws from sensory input) [5]. Dunn’s quadrants model introduces a behavioral response dimension that explains why hyper-reactive individuals exhibit either active avoidance or passive emotional collapse—clinical heterogeneity unaddressed by the traditional framework [38].

Sensory avoiding and sensory sensitivity represent divergent behavioral manifestations within Dunn’s framework, distinguished by the presence or absence of active behavioral regulation: the former involves strategic evasion, whereas the latter entails passive detection without behavioral escape [39]. While both quadrants share conceptual overlap with the DSM-5 category of sensory hyper-reactivity, they are not fully equivalent. By distinguishing sensory sensitivity (passive responding) from sensory avoiding (active responding), this model enables examination of their differential developmental trajectories, reconciling prior inconsistent findings regarding hyper-reactivity and social-communication outcomes [40]. Specifically, sensory low registration manifests as inadequate detection of sensory input and lack of active compensatory behaviors (e.g., ignoring sounds, failing to respond to self in the mirror) [41], which conceptually overlaps with the DSM-5 category of sensory hypo-reactivity; the key distinction is that low registration specifically emphasizes the absence of active compensatory behaviors.

Dunn’s quadrant model constitutes the predominant theoretical framework for classifying sensory patterns in autism [42]. The Chinese version exhibits excellent internal consistency and test-retest reliability [43], confirming linguistic and metric validity for Mandarin-speaking populations. These psychometric strengths support the TSP-2’s utility for mapping longitudinal sensory trajectories in this cohort.

Measurement of social skills

Social skills were assessed at T18 and T24 using Communication and Symbolic Behavior Scales–Developmental Profile–Infant-Toddler Checklist (CSBS-DP ITC) [44], a well-established and psychometrically robust tool for the early screening of social-communication in autism [45]. Comprising a 24-item questionnaire, the CSBS-DP ITC structures items into three composite modules—social (items 1–13), speech (items 14–19), and symbolic (items 20–24). The social composite, validated as a distinct factor in the standardization sample [45], assesses early emerging nonverbal social behaviors via three clusters: emotion and eye gaze, communication (e.g., social attention), and gestures. In this study, the social composite scores were selected to index social skills at 24 months, consistent with prior research [46].

Measurement of autistic traits outcomes

ADOS-2 was administered at T36 to derive diagnostic outcomes. This semi-structured observational assessment, validated for quantifying core autistic traits [30], evaluates four domains: communication, social interaction, imaginative play, and restricted repetitive behaviors (RRBs). The ADOS exhibits satisfactory diagnostic validity in the Chinese population [47]. We first utilized revised algorithms–social affect, RRBs and autistic severity– to mitigate the impacts of language and age factors imposed by different modules [48]. Subsequently, the different modules of ADOS-2 were compared using the Calibrated Severity Scores (CSS), a metric specifically designed to quantify the severity of autistic traits [49].

Measurement of adaptive functioning

Adaptive functioning was evaluated at T36 using the Adaptive Behavior Assessment System, Second Edition (ABAS-II) [50]. The standardized Mandarin version demonstrated psychometric equivalence to the original instrument, with confirmatory factor analyses supporting its construct validity and internal consistency [51]. In this study, adaptive functioning was evaluated in core adaptive skill domains: Overall adaptive skills, conceptual skills, social skills, and practical skills.

Measurement of developmental competence

Developmental competence at 36 months was assessed using MSEL [31], a standard observational measure to evaluate core developmental domains in early childhood. MSEL exhibits strong psychometric properties for measuring developmental abilities in ASD [52]. The Early Learning Composite (ELC) score provided a comprehensive measure of early developmental competence, integrating fine motor, visual reception, receptive language, and expressive language. In the present study, the ELC was employed as an index of overall developmental status.

Quality control

Diagnostic validation procedures– Diagnoses of autism were independently confirmed by two board-certified specialists with over a decade of clinical experience. A diagnostic consensus conference was convened to reconcile any discrepancies, ensuring inter-rater reliability and strict adherence to DSM-5 criteria. This systematic approach—integrating standardized assessments, expert consensus, and independent verification—minimized classification bias and enhanced validity.

Assessment standardization– ADOS-2 assessments were performed by clinical pediatricians who completed a standardized training program. Prior to data collection, examiners achieved ≥ 90% inter-rater reliability on practice videotapes, verified through blind scoring of 10 cases. Examiners followed strictly scripted administration protocols, and revised scores were used to minimize observer bias.

Parental questionnaire administration– Parents completed TSP-2, CSBS-DP ITC and ABAS-II following standardized electronic or written instructions, with telephone or on-site support provided for clarifications. A two-stage verification process was implemented: research coordinators first reviewed questionnaires for completeness, and a senior investigator cross-checked to ensure response consistency. This protocol minimized measurement error from misinterpretation, particularly for complex items.

Data management and monitoring– Quarterly quality control reviews were conducted by dedicated research staff to reconcile data discrepancies and ensure protocol compliance. All assessments were electronically recorded in Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap).

Statistical analysis

Trajectory analyses– Given the repeated measures design of TSP-2 assessments, Linear Mixed-effects Models (LMMs) were conducted to examine trajectory differences of four sensory profiles among the three groups. Full information maximum likelihood (FIML) was used to handle missing data. The models included: Dependent variables: TSP-2 sensory pattern scores; Fixed effects: Diagnosis, Time, and Diagnosis × Time interaction. Given the sex imbalance within the ASD group, sex was included as a covariate. Given a prior hypothesis regarding group differences at specific developmental periods [11], we conducted planned comparisons at each timepoint regardless of interaction significance, with Bonferroni correction applied to control familywise error.

Preliminary cascade analyses– We applied the analytical framework initially used to validate cascading effect [24], with innovative explorations conducted through a two-stage modeling approach. Considering that there is relatively limited data at T12, we focused on verifying the cascade effect of sensory profiles at T18 on autistic traits at T36 in the ASD group. To examine the incremental contributions of demographic and sensory profiles variables to the outcomes, we conducted hierarchical regression analysis. The predictor variables were entered in three sequential steps: Step 1 (Demographic factors): gender and age were entered to control for basic demographic characteristics; Step 2 (Parental education): paternal education and maternal education were added to account for family status; Step 3 (sensory profiles): The four sensory quadrants (sensitivity, avoiding, seeking, registration) were entered in the final step to assess their unique contributions beyond demographic and family factors. To account for multiple comparisons and minimize the risk of false positive findings, we applied false discovery rate (FDR) correction [53] separately within each outcome, with statistical significance set at q < 0.05.

Establishment of cascade pathways–Drawing upon prior research [24], we conceptualized social skills at T24 as a mediating variable capturing the extent to which early sensory profile at T18 indirectly influenced autistic traits at T36, thereby operationalizing the hypothesized developmental cascade. We secondly constructed Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) path analysis with Bootstrap to cascading effect in the ASD group. The models controlled for sex, age and parental education. Specifically, we tested: (a) Path a, representing the relation between independent variables (sensory avoiding and registration at T18) and social skills at T24; (b) Path b, representing the relation between social skills at T24 and outcomes at T36, controlling for the independent variable; (c) Path c’, representing the direct effect of the independent variable on outcomes when controlling for social skills at T24; and (d)The indirect effect was defined as the product of Path a and Path b (a×b), i.e., the portion of the effect transmitted through social skills at T24. Parameter estimation used standardized coefficients (β), with effect sizes interpreted as small (|β| < 0.30), medium (0.30 ≤ |β| < 0.50), and large (|β| ≥ 0.50). Bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs) based on 5,000 resamples were used to assess the significance of indirect effects, with 95% CIs excluding zero indicating significant mediation.

Temporal associations in cascade effect – To examine the temporal ordering of associations among early sensory profiles, the mediation of social skills, and autistic traits while accounting for within-variable stability over time, the temporally ordered path models were conducted in Mplus for the ASD group. The model included three time points: sensory patterns at T18, social skills at T24, and autistic traits at T36. Autoregressive paths were specified to control for the temporal stability of each construct, while the temporally ordered path models estimated the prospective associations between constructs across adjacent time points. Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) was used to handle missing data under the assumption of missing at random. Model parameters were estimated using Maximum Likelihood with robust standard errors (MLR). Model fit was evaluated using the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Following conventional guidelines [54], acceptable fit was defined as RMSEA ≤ 0 0.08, CFI ≥ 0.90, and SRMR ≤ 0.08.

Trajectory differences of sensory patterns in infants later diagnosed with autism

Significant diagnosis × time interactions were observed for sensory sensitivity (F = 3.25, df = 322.23, p = 0.004, η² = 0.07). Trajectory analyses revealed distinct developmental patterns: the ASD group demonstrated a steep decline in sensory sensitivity from T12 to T18, whereas the DD and TD group showed stable levels at 12–18 months with subsequent declines (see Fig. 1a; Table 2).

Predictive utility of early sensory patterns at T18 for later developmental outcomes at T36 in infants later diagnosed with autism

In the ASD subgroup (n = 47; analyses are exploratory given the modest sample), the hierarchical regression model revealed that sensory patterns at T18 accounted for 56% of the variance in autistic severity at T36 (ΔR² = 0.56, p < 0.001), after controlling for gender, age, and parental education in the ASD group (see Table 3). Sensory avoiding at T18 was a significant positive predictor of autistic severity scores at T36 (B = 0.23, p = 0.002, β = 0.45), and this association remained significant after correction for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR; q = 0.002). Meanwhile, sensory registration at T18 positively predicted autistic severity scores at T36 (B = 0.23; p = 0.008, β = 0.41), this result survived after FDR correction (q = 0.003).

Discussion

This study represents an observational follow-up investigation of early sensory developmental trajectories in Chinese infants, featuring multiple time-points data collection. It also represents a systematic examination of associations consistent with the cascading effect model; whereby early sensory differences may show a prospective association with subsequent social-communication, with a statistical indirect effect through social skills. These findings contribute to understanding of autistic traits development across diverse cultural contexts, highlighting that early sensory differences may serve as early contributing factors associated with developmental trajectories in autism.

Trajectories differences of early sensory profiles in infants later diagnosed with autism

Our prospective cohort analysis uncovers that distinct sensory profiles emerge at 12 months of age in infants later diagnosed with autism compared to those with typical development, characterized by the concurrent presence of higher sensory sensitivity and avoiding, alongside elevated low registration (i.e., higher sensory registration scores). This specificity suggests that early sensory differences may serve as discriminative features for autism during the second year of life, extending prior findings [11]. This paradoxical presentation aligns with recent cluster analyses identifying distinct subgroups of infants with autism characterized by divergent response combinations [9].

Our findings reveal a distinctive trajectory characterized by marked fluctuations in autism during infancy. Specifically, autistic toddlers exhibited a pronounced decline in sensory sensitivity between 12 and 18 months of age, a pattern that diverges fundamentally from the relatively stable trajectories observed in both developmental delay and typically developing groups. This transient reduction in sensory sensitivity during an early developmental period suggests dynamic processes of environmental accommodation [12]. The transient masking of sensory sensitivity during this period creates a phenotypic gap that complicates early detection efforts reliant on single timepoint assessments—a challenge that may contribute to delayed diagnosis [55]. This finding extends prior documentation of sensory differences in infancy [11, 56] by identifying specific sensitive periods of phenotypic fluctuation with direct implications for screening protocols.

Trajectory heterogeneity indicates that single timepoint assessments at 12 months risk false negatives following the observed sensitivity decline. We recommend repeated sensory screening across the second year. Dynamic monitoring using validated parent-report instruments promises improved early identification [57].

Cascade effect of early sensory differences on subsequent autistic traits in infants later diagnosis of autism

Our findings are consistent with the cascading effect model in autism [22], wherein early sensory differences may serve as early developmental correlates of social-communication difficulties. Consistent with previous evidence [24, 25], we further delineate temporally ordered pathways whereby elevated sensory avoiding at 18 months indirectly influence social-communication difficulties at 36 months via social skills at 24 months, with null reverse pathways. This one-way temporal pattern suggests that early sensory avoiding may temporally precede social outcomes, which is consistent with the sensory-first account of autism [58]. While informative for examining temporal precedence, the temporally ordered path model does not establish causality. Unmeasured confounding and selection effects may bias the observed associations. Therefore, we interpret the results as predictive or temporal associations rather than causal effects.

The identified indirect pathway may operate through environmental factors. Sensory avoiding behaviors, manifested as active withdrawal from sensory input, may be associated with reduced environmental exploration [37]. Autistic toddlers show heightened sensory avoidance, which may be accompanied by withdrawal from social stimuli and reduced engagement during an early developmental period for acquiring foundational social skills such as joint attention and social orienting [59, 60]. Furthermore, sensory avoiding may bias discrimination, attenuating the distinction between social and nonsocial stimuli [61]. This reduces infants’ exposure to social cues (e.g., facial expressions, vocalizations), potentially altering social processing during critical developmental periods [62].

The present study also indicates that elevated low registration (i.e., higher sensory registration scores) at 18 months is prospectively associated with subsequent autistic severity at 36 months, with a statistical indirect effect through social skills at 24 months in infants later diagnosed with autism. Infants engage in vigorous physical exploration; essential social skills such as shared attention and reciprocal communication emerge through this sensorimotor interaction [63]. However, greater low registration in autism may attenuate this exploratory process [3]. Reduced sensory engagement restricts environmental exploration, thereby truncating opportunities for social behaviors. In turn, these behaviors may curtail the variety of exploratory play, further constricting the avenues for social-communication [26].

Notably, the prospective association of sensory avoiding was specific to social-communication outcomes, with no significant effect on RRBs. While sensory features and RRBs co-occur cross-sectionally, the divergent longitudinal pathways observed suggest that sensory avoiding may be more closely associated with social learning processes (e.g., joint attention, social orienting) than with repetitive behavioral patterns. RRBs may follow distinct developmental origins, potentially reflecting regulatory mechanisms less dependent on environmental social input [64].

Our findings carry implications for early intervention. Elevated sensory avoiding and elevated low-registration behaviors at 18 months potentially represent early correlates of subsequent social-communication difficulties, identifying an early developmental period during which targeted sensory supports may interrupt negative developmental cascades in autism [22].

Early sensory seeking may prompt later adaptive development in infants with later diagnosed with autism

In an exploratory analysis that did not survive correction for multiple comparisons for the overall adaptive-skills outcome, our results tentatively suggest that early sensory seeking may be positively associated with later adaptive skills in infants later diagnosed with autism; this finding should be regarded as preliminary. Early curiosity toward novel stimuli may enhance environmental mastery, a critical survival mechanism that confers selective advantages by promoting anticipatory learning. Such developmental processes may lay the groundwork for emergent skills in problem-solving, underscoring the developmental primacy of sensory experiences [58]. No direct association has been identified between early sensory seeking behaviors and subsequent social-communicative difficulties in elevated likelihood children [12, 26], which aligns with our findings. It underscores that high frequencies of sensory seeking behaviors do not inherently confer adverse impacts on communicative development in elevated likelihood children—contrary to longstanding assumptions within the field [9, 24].

Instead of pathologizing sensory features in autism, clinicians should embrace multi-dimensional approaches that acknowledge these features as integral components of a dynamic developmental process with adaptive potential. Clinicians can gain a more nuanced understanding of each child’s unique profile, avoiding the pitfalls of overemphasizing deficits and overlooking the potential benefits of these behaviors. This perspective aligns with the neurodiversity, which emphasizes the value of neurological differences rather than focusing solely on deficits [65, 66]. However, given the multiple comparisons involved and the limited robustness of these associations, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require replication in larger independent samples.

Limitations

The present study is subject to several limitations. First, while the reliance on parental reports for TSP-2 assessment provides comprehensive behavioral insight into children’s everyday sensory experiences across multiple contexts, this method may introduce subjectivity, including potential observer bias and inconsistent reporting standards across caregivers. Second, the restricted sample size at T12 (n = 67) reduced statistical power for detecting group differences at this specific timepoint. This attrition was attributable to the study design, wherein 67 participants enrolled between 12 and 18 months of age and consequently missed the T12 assessment. While our primary longitudinal analyses employed FIML estimation to utilize all available data, findings specific to T12 should be interpreted cautiously pending replication in larger prospective cohorts. Third, the ASD subgroup analyses (n = 47) involved longitudinal path models and SEM incorporating multiple covariates and indirect effects. With this modest sample size, parameter estimates in multi-covariate models may be unstable, statistical power for detecting indirect effects is limited, and generalizability to broader autistic populations is constrained. Accordingly, the current findings should be regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-generating, requiring replication in larger independent cohorts.

An additional limitation concerns the composition of our sample, which comprised infants with elevated likelihood for autism (infant siblings) and those with developmental concerns identified through clinical referral. While this enriched design enhanced statistical power for detecting group differences, it restricts generalizability to the broader community population. Infants from elevated likelihood families may carry genetic susceptibilities associated with distinct sensory phenotypes; clinically referred samples based on developmental concerns may be subject to ascertainment bias, potentially overrepresenting more severe phenotypes. Replication in community-based cohorts is essential to determine whether the identified sensory trajectories generalize to unselected community samples.
