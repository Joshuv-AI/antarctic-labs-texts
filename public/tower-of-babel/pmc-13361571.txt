Prevalence, antimicrobial resistance, and risk factors of pneumococcal disease among preschool children in Bushenyi District, Uganda
Authors: Abdifitah Abdullahi Mohamed, Eilu Emmanuel, Chinyere Nkemjika Anyanwu, Theophilus Pius, Wesam Taher Almagharbeh, Danladi Makeri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPneumococcal disease remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality among children in low-resource settings, with Streptococcus pneumoniae being the leading bacterial pathogen. This study sought to determine the prevalence, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) patterns, and the socio-environmental risk factors associated with S. pneumoniae among hospitalized preschool children.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 293 children aged five years and below presenting with symptoms of pneumonia. Nasopharyngeal swabs were collected and cultured to isolate S. pneumoniae and other bacterial pathogens. Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method to assess resistance patterns. Factors associated with infection were analyzed using Chi-square tests, followed by multivariable logistic regression to identify independent predictors of pneumococcal carriage.ResultsThe prevalence of S. pneumoniae was 20.14% (n = 59). The isolates showed 100% resistance to ampicillin and cotrimoxazole, 88% to azithromycin, and 76.3% to chloramphenicol. Erythromycin and gentamicin showed the highest susceptibility (61%). In the multivariable analysis, children of mothers aged 30–34 years had significantly lower odds of infection compared to those of mothers aged 25–29 years (aOR 0.258; 95% CI: 0.122–0.546, p < 0.001). Conversely, indoor cooking was a strong independent risk factor, with children from these households being over three times more likely to test positive for S. pneumoniae (aOR 3.294; 95% CI: 1.413–7.679, p = 0.006). Maternal education was associated with infection in bivariate analysis, though small cell sizes limited its precision in the final model.ConclusionApproximately one in five children hospitalized with clinical pneumonia carried Streptococcus pneumoniae in the nasopharynx. Children of younger mothers (25–29 years), mothers with no formal education, and households where cooking was done indoors had higher rates of S. pneumoniae infection. These findings emphasize the need for improved maternal health education, reduction of indoor air pollution, and antimicrobial stewardship programs. These findings are specific to hospitalized children in Bushenyi District.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13600-3.Keywords: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Pneumococcal disease, Antimicrobial resistance, Pneumonia in Uganda, Childhood pneumonia

Introduction

Pneumonia remains a leading cause of childhood mortality worldwide, particularly among children under five years of age [1, 2]. Accounting for approximately 16% of global under-five deaths, it is a significant public health concern, with the highest burden observed in low-resource settings like sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia [3, 4]. Streptococcus pneumoniae is a major bacterial pathogen that contributes significantly to pneumonia cases, alongside other infections such as otitis media, septicemia, and meningitis [1]. Despite global efforts to curb pneumonia-related morbidity and mortality, it remains the second leading cause of hospital admissions and deaths among children under five [5]. Uganda, like many African nations, continues to grapple with this challenge despite incorporating the pneumococcal conjugate vaccine into the routine immunization schedule since 2013 [6].

The introduction of the pneumococcal conjugate vaccine aimed to reduce the burden of S. pneumoniae infections. However, in Uganda, pneumonia prevalence has remained alarmingly high, with 753,978 pneumonia-related hospitalizations and 13,632 deaths among under-five children recorded between 2013 and 2021 [6]. Several socio-environmental and health-related factors contribute to the continued high burden of pneumococcal disease, including rural residence, incomplete vaccination, severe acute malnutrition, exposure to cigarette smoke, and inadequate breastfeeding practices [3]. The role of bacterial pathogens like S. pneumoniae in this context is critically important, yet their prevalence and antibiotic susceptibility patterns remain underexplored, especially in Bushenyi district.

Streptococcus pneumoniae is recognized as a highly adaptable pathogen with increasing resistance to commonly used antibiotics, complicating treatment protocols and leading to poor clinical outcomes [7]. In Uganda, the widespread use of antibiotics without susceptibility testing has exacerbated the issue of antimicrobial resistance [8–10]. This highlights the importance of local data on pathogen prevalence and antimicrobial susceptibility to guide treatment protocols effectively. However, there is limited research in the Bushenyi district on the prevalence of S. pneumoniae infections among children under five and the resistance patterns of this pathogen to the antibiotics commonly used in treatment.

This study sought to bridge the gap in understanding the burden of S. pneumoniae infection in Bushenyi district, Uganda, by determining its prevalence among children aged five years and below and exploring the antibiotic susceptibility profile of the isolates. Additionally, the study investigated the associated risk factors for pneumococcal disease in this vulnerable population, with the aim of informing future public health interventions.

Methodology

Study design, duration, population, eligibility, and setting

We conducted a hospital-based analytical cross-sectional study between September 2024 and December 2024 among children aged five years and below with symptomatic pneumonia (defined using WHO Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI) clinical criteria.) admitted at Kampala International University Teaching Hospital (KIUTH), Ishaka Adventist Hospital (IAH), and Comboni Hospital (CH), all located in Bushenyi District, Uganda. The study included all children aged five years and below with symptomatic pneumonia who were seeking healthcare services at the above hospitals during the data collection period and whose parents or guardians provided informed consent. Children were excluded if they had a history of chronic respiratory conditions or congenital lung abnormalities, had received antibiotics in the two weeks prior to enrollment, or if their parents or guardians did not provide informed consent. Nasopharyngeal swabs were collected to assess bacterial carriage among children presenting with clinical pneumonia. Children aged five years were included as they continue to be managed under pediatric pneumonia protocols in the study setting.

Sampling method

To ensure representative sampling from the three hospitals, we calculated the proportion of the study population selected from each hospital based on the average monthly pediatric patient load presenting with pneumonia-like symptoms, as obtained from outpatient records during the six months preceding the study. Using this method, IAH, CH, and KIUTH had an average monthly patient load of 70, 50 and 60. Using this average, we estimated and recruited consecutively 114, 81, and 98 respondents from IAH, CH, and KIUTH, respectively.

Data and sample collection

Sterile nasopharyngeal swabs were used to collect nasopharyngeal specimens for culture from the children alongside a researcher-developed structured questionnaire (Supplementary material 1) for socio-demographic and clinical data. The questionnaire was initially approved in 2024 and administratively re-stamped in 2025.

Bacterial isolation and identification

Nasopharyngeal swabs were inoculated onto chocolate agar (Himedia, India), blood agar (Himedia, India), and MacConkey agar (Himedia, India), and incubated aerobically for 18–24 h at 35–37 °C. Following incubation, presumptive bacterial colonies were subjected to a series of biochemical tests for identification. For S. pneumoniae, colonies exhibiting alpha-hemolysis on blood agar were further tested using the optochin sensitivity test and the bile solubility test [11]. Streptococcus pyogenes and other beta-hemolytic Streptococcus spp. were identified using catalase testing (negative), and bacitracin sensitivity (positive for S. pyogenes). Staphylococcus aureus was identified by golden-yellow colonies on nutrient agar (Himedia), beta-hemolysis on blood agar, positive catalase test, and a positive coagulase test. Klebsiella pneumoniae was identified by its mucoid colonies on MacConkey agar (lactose fermenting), negative oxidase test, positive citrate utilization, urease production, and indole negative results. All tests followed standard microbiological identification procedures per Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines and relevant literature.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method to assess the resistance and susceptibility patterns of the bacterial isolates. Bacterial colonies from a fresh pure culture were emulsified in a sterile saline solution, adjusting the turbidity to match a 0.5 McFarland standard. A sterile cotton swab was dipped into the bacterial suspension and then pressed against the tube wall to eliminate excess liquid before evenly spreading the inoculum on a Muller-Hinton agar (Himedia, India). Antibiotic discs, selected according to the CLSI guidelines [12], Uganda clinical guideline and local clinical prescribing practices, were placed on the agar surface to evaluate susceptibility. The plates were then incubated at 35–37 °C for 18–24 h. After incubation, the diameters of the inhibition zones were measured and interpreted according to CLSI guidelines. The antibiotics tested included ampicillin (10 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), erythromycin (15 µg), vancomycin (30 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), amikacin (30 µg), chloramphenicol (10 µg), cotrimoxazole (25 µg), and azithromycin (15 µg). Multidrug resistance (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and pan drug resistance (PDR) were determined by resistance to at least one agent in three or more antibiotic classes, resistant to all but one or two antibiotic classes, and resistance to all antibiotics in all tested classes respectively [13].

Quality control

Streptococcus pneumoniae strain (ATCC 49619) was used to ensure the quality of both the culture media and antibiotic discs.

Data analysis

Data was analyzed at univariate, bivariate, and multivariate levels. Numerical variables were analyzed through central tendency, and categorical variables were summarized as percentages and presented in tables or graphs. All statistical analysis was carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23. The prevalence of pneumococcal disease and antibiotic susceptibility profile of bacteria isolated from our respondents were analysed descriptively as frequencies or percentages and presented graphically. For factors associated with pneumococcal disease, Chi-square tests were used to explore associations between pneumococcal disease and child and maternal socio-demographic and clinico-environmental variables; statistical significance was ascertained at α ≤ 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Kampala International University Research Ethics Committee under the reference number (KIU-2024-381). Approval to conduct this study in KIUTH and IAH was also obtained from the management of the hospitals. Informed consent was obtained from the guardians/caretakers of the children and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Participants were assigned unique identification codes; questionnaires were stored securely and accessed only by the research team.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants

A total of 293 children aged five and below were included in the study. The majority of the children were female, comprising 55.97% (164/293), while males made up 44.03% (129/293). The children’s ages ranged from 2 to 5 years, with the largest proportion (33.45%, 98/293) being 4 years old, followed by 3-year-olds (27.99%, 82/293). Notably, all the children (100%) were fully immunized, had been exclusively breastfed, and none had a previous history of pneumonia (Table 1).

The antibiogram results of S. pneumoniae reveal a high level of resistance to multiple antibiotics and highlights the multidrug-resistant (MDR) nature of the pathogen. Notably, 100% resistance was observed to ampicillin and cotrimoxazole, while azithromycin resistance was also high at 88%. Similarly, 76.3% of the bacterium were resistant to chloramphenicol. Discussion

Our study provides insights into the prevalence and factors associated with pneumococcal disease among children aged five years and below in Bushenyi District, Uganda. The findings indicated a 20.14% prevalence of S. pneumoniae was isolated in children, making it one of the most common nasopharyngeal bacterial isolates in this population. This prevalence is consistent with studies from low-income settings, where S. pneumoniae remains a leading pathogen in paediatric respiratory infections [3, 14–17]. Notably, this prevalence persists despite the national rollout of the Pneumococcal Conjugate Vaccine [18, 19]. This may be attributed to the “vaccination paradox” or serotype replacement, where the reduction of vaccine-type strains creates an ecological niche for non-vaccine serotypes to colonize and cause disease [20, 21].

The antibiogram analysis of S. pneumoniae isolates revealed a trend of multidrug resistance. Notably, 100% of the isolates were resistant to ampicillin and cotrimoxazole, and high resistance was also observed against azithromycin (88%), and chloramphenicol (76.3%). These findings are in line with the growing global concern over MDR S. pneumoniae strains, particularly in low-resource settings [22, 23]. Our results align with studies from Kenya and Tanzania, where similar high resistance rates to beta-lactams and macrolides were reported [24, 25].While resistance was widespread, some antibiotics showed moderate efficacy. Erythromycin and gentamicin had the highest susceptibility (61%), suggesting these antibiotics may still hold therapeutic potential for treating pneumococcal infections. These findings are in agreement with research from other sub-Saharan African regions where gentamicin have shown effectiveness against pneumococcal strains [26, 27]. However, the moderate susceptibility to amikacin (54.25%) warrants further monitoring, as decreased efficacy could signal emerging resistance patterns.

After adjusting for confounders in our multivariable model, maternal age remained a significant independent predictor of pneumococcal carriage. Children of mothers aged 30–34 years were significantly less likely to be colonized compared to those of mothers aged 25–29 years (aOR 0.258; 95% CI: 0.122–0.546). This suggests that maternal experience and age-related caregiving factors may play a protective role. Previous studies in similar settings have suggested that younger maternal age is often associated with lower healthcare-seeking efficacy and different household dynamics, which can increase infectious disease secondary attack rates within the home [28–30].

Maternal education also showed a strong association in bivariate analysis, with higher infection rates among children of mothers with no formal or only primary education. While the multivariable model was limited by small cell sizes in higher education brackets, the trend remains clear. This supports existing literature that highlights the role of maternal education in promoting better health-seeking behaviours and adherence to preventive healthcare measures, such as timely immunizations and improved nutrition, which are critical in reducing the risk of infectious diseases [31–35].

Another key finding was the association between indoor cooking and pneumococcal infection. Children from households where cooking was done indoors were significantly more likely to have a positive S. pneumoniae culture. Indoor air pollution, particularly from biomass fuel use, has been implicated in respiratory diseases in children due to its harmful effects on lung function and increased susceptibility to infections [36–38]. Our results are consistent with previous studies in sub-Saharan Africa, which have demonstrated the association between indoor cooking with solid fuels and an increased risk of pneumonia in children [39–42]. This underscores the importance of public health interventions to reduce indoor air pollution, such as promoting cleaner cooking technologies.

However, it is worth noting that our study found no significant association between the child’s gender and the presence of smokers in the household and pneumococcal nasal carriage. This contrasts with some studies that have suggested a higher risk of pneumonia in male children [43, 44] and households with smokers [45–47]. The lack of association in our study may be attributable to the small number of households with smokers and the socio-cultural factors that influence smoking practices in this region.

Study limitation

This study had several limitations. First, we relied on caregiver reports to ascertain the immunization status of the children, without verifying the specific type of pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV10 or PCV13) administered, which may have introduced recall bias or misclassification. Second, while antibiotic susceptibility testing was guided primarily by the CLSI 2024 guidelines, some antibiotics such as gentamicin and amikacin were included based on recommendations from the UCG 2023 and prevailing local clinical practices. Although this approach reflects real-world prescribing behaviour, it may differ from standard CLSI recommendations for S. pneumoniae. Third, the study was limited to selected hospitals within Bushenyi District, which may affect the generalizability of the findings to other regions of Uganda. Fourth, the absence of children < 2 years, who would likely have contributed substantially to the prevalence of pneumococcal isolation. Lastly, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the presence of S. pneumoniae and various risk factors. Considering that nasopharyngeal swabs detect both colonizing and potentially pathogenic bacteria. In the absence of a healthy control group or lower respiratory tract specimens, this study cannot establish a causal relationship between isolated organisms and pneumonia. The findings therefore reflect nasopharyngeal carriage among children hospitalized with pneumonia rather than confirmed etiologic agents.

Conclusion

Our study reveals that one in five preschool children in Bushenyi District carried S. pneumoniae, with high levels of resistance to commonly used first-line antibiotics. Multivariable analysis identified indoor cooking and younger maternal age as the primary independent drivers of pneumococcal risk. To reduce this burden, public health efforts in rural Uganda must move beyond clinical treatment toward targeted structural interventions, specifically the promotion of improved household ventilation, clean cookstoves and tailored health education for younger caregivers/mothers. Furthermore, the high MDR levels necessitate a shift from syndromic management to etiological diagnosis where possible to preserve the efficacy of remaining antibiotics like erythromycin.
