The Iranian version of Betts’ questionnaire upon mental imagery: translation and confirmatory factor analysis
Authors: Maryam Khalaji, Mahya Mohamadtaghi, Sahar Mohammadzadeh, Mahin Aghdaei, Alfredo Campos, Mert Kurnaz, Mustafa Altınkök
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesMental imagery plays a crucial role in cognitive and motor functioning. The present study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and psychometrically evaluate the Persian version of Sheehan's shortened form of Betts’ Questionnaire Upon Mental Imagery (QMI) among Iranian university students. This study represents the first comprehensive cultural adaptation and psychometric validation of the Betts’ Questionnaire Upon Mental Imagery (QMI) in Persian-speaking populations.MethodsA total of 379 students from Shahid Beheshti University (166 women, 213 men; mean age = 20.97 ± 2.95 years) voluntarily participated in the study. The original QMI includes 35 items across seven sensory modalities (visual, auditory, cutaneous, kinesthetic, gustatory, olfactory, and organic), scored on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = vivid image, 7 = no image). A rigorous forward-backward translation protocol involving a multidisciplinary expert panel was implemented. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), Cronbach’s alpha, and gender comparison using independent t-tests were conducted. Robust Maximum Likelihood Estimation was used in CFA due to non-normality.ResultsCFA confirmed the original seven-factor structure with good fit by multiple indices (CFI=0.96, TLI=0.95, SRMR=0.06, RMSEA=0.06 [90% CI: 0.05, 0.07], p-close = .12; PGFI = 0.73, PNFI = 0.84, χ2/df = 2.18. Internal consistency was strong (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.87 overall; all subscales α = 0.65–0.78; McDonald's ω = 0.68–0.92). No statistically significant gender differences were observed in total or subscale scores (p > .05).ConclusionsThe QMI demonstrates sound psychometric properties, confirming its applicability for assessing vividness of multisensory imagery in Persian-speaking populations, however, further validation is required before its broad application in specialized clinical or elite athletic contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04842-6.

Introduction

Mental imagery, the conscious experience of perceptual-like representations in the absence of external stimuli, is a fundamental cognitive process that underpins motor learning, emotion regulation, and performance enhancement [7, 10]. While early research focused heavily on the visual dimension, contemporary evidence highlights the critical role of multisensory imagery (visual, auditory, kinesthetic, olfactory, gustatory, cutaneous, and organic) in both clinical psychiatry for understanding trauma [5] and sports psychology for optimizing motor execution [3].

While mental imagery is inherently multisensory, psychometric research in Iran has remained disproportionately focused on the visual domain. This visual-centric bias in Persian assessment tools creates a significant gap: it limits the ability of researchers and clinicians to identify specific sensory deficits or strengths that are crucial for personalized interventions. Without a validated multisensory instrument like the Betts’ QMI, the full cognitive profile of Persian-speaking individuals remains under-specified, hindering the cross-cultural generalizability of imagery theories.

The assessment of imagery has evolved from Galton’s (1880) early work to Betts’ (1909) 150-item Questionnaire Upon Mental Imagery (QMI), which first recognized the multisensory nature of imagery. Due to administration constraints, Sheehan developed a shortened 35-item version that maintained the original seven-factor structure while improving cultural relevance and psychometric robustness.

The QMI has demonstrated excellent psychometric properties across multiple cultural contexts. A Spanish validation reported excellent internal consistency (Cronbach α = 0.92) and confirmed the multimodal structure, identifying eight factors, six of which matched the original English seven-factor structure [2]. An Italian validation similarly confirmed strong reliability (α = 0.89) and factorial validity [13]. These cross-cultural validations demonstrate the instrument's structural robustness while acknowledging that minor cultural adaptations may be necessary. This comprehensive approach recognized the multisensory nature of mental imagery, distinguishing it from earlier measures that focused primarily on visual experiences. The practical limitations of Betts' original lengthy questionnaire led Sheehan to develop a shortened 35-item version, which retained the seven-factor structure while significantly reducing administration time. Recent systematic reviews have identified the need for comprehensive evaluation of imagery assessment tools, particularly regarding their psychometric properties across different populations.

The cross-cultural validity of psychological instruments represents a critical consideration in globalized research environments. Cultural bias in psychometric instruments can lead to inaccurate assessments and misinterpretation of results across different cultural contexts [15, 16]. Cross-cultural adaptations of the QMI have been validated in Italian [15, 16], Spanish [15], [16] and other populations, showing reliable internal consistency and factor structure. However, most studies in Iran have focused solely on visual imagery, neglecting the multisensory nature of the imagery experience. To our knowledge, this is the first study aiming to culturally adapt and validate the full multisensory version of the QMI in a Persian-speaking population.

Despite the extensive use of Betts' QMI internationally, its psychometric properties have not been systematically evaluated in Persian-speaking populations. This represents a significant gap in the literature, particularly given the cultural and linguistic diversity of mental imagery experiences. Previous validation studies have shown varying factor structures across different populations, with some studies identifying eight factors in Spanish samples [15], [16] while others maintained the original seven-factor structure.

Despite the international utility of the QMI, psychometric research in Iran remains significantly limited by a visual-centric bias. Existing Persian instruments (such as the VVIQ) focus almost exclusively on visual imagery, leaving the other six sensory modalities, vital for holistic clinical and athletic assessment, largely unexamined. This creates a methodological bottleneck: Iranian researchers cannot currently determine whether a deficit in performance is due to poor visual vividness or a failure in kinesthetic or organic feedback. Furthermore, linguistic nuances in describing internal physical sensations (e.g., organic or cutaneous feelings) suggest that a direct translation is insufficient; a rigorous cultural adaptation and factorial validation are required to ensure conceptual equivalence in a Persian-speaking context. The present study addresses this critical gap by providing the first comprehensive psychometric evaluation of the Persian QMI among Iranian university students. By utilizing Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) and testing for gender-based measurement invariance, we aim to establish a multidimensional sensory profile tool that surpasses the limitations of existing visual-only measures in Iran.

Method

Study design and ethical considerations

This cross-sectional validation study was conducted in accordance with international guidelines for cross-cultural adaptation of psychological instruments [15, 16]. Ethical approval was obtained from the University Research Ethics Committee of Damghan University (IR.DU.REC.1404.008) prior to data collection. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving detailed information about the study objectives and procedures. The study were conducted according to the principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample size calculation

A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software [15, 16]. Based on recommendations for confirmatory factor analysis [15, 16], we aimed for a minimum of 10–15 participants per questionnaire item. For the 35-item questionnaire, this yielded a minimum sample size of 350 participants.

Participant characteristics

421 university students received the questionnaire for participation in the validation of the Persian version of the QMI. These questionnaires were initially distributed to ensure adequate statistical power for the planned analyses. All participating students provided written informed consent after being fully informed about study details, rights, and data confidentiality. Quality control procedures resulted in the exclusion of 42 questionnaires due to systematic response patterns, incomplete demographic information, or substantial missing data, which could compromise the validity of statistical analyses. Univariate outliers were identified using standardized z-scores exceeding ±2.5, while multivariate outliers were detected through Mahalanobis distance calculations with significance levels of p <.001 [15, 16]. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion, where incomplete cases were excluded from the analysis to ensure data quality and adherence to statistical assumptions. These rigorous exclusion criteria ensured data quality and adherence to multivariate analysis assumptions The final sample comprised 379 students from Shahid Beheshti University (female = 166, 43.79%,male 213, 56.20%; mean age = 20.97 ± 2.95 years). Participants were recruited through convenience sampling from various academic disciplines to ensure heterogeneity. The sample exceeded the minimum requirements for factor analysis and provided adequate power (>0.95) for detecting medium effect sizes.

Sample size determination for confirmatory factor analysis was guided by established psychometric guidelines recommending 10–15 participants per questionnaire item [6, 15, 16, 19]. For the 35-item QMI, this yielded a minimum sample size of 350 participants. Accordingly, 421 questionnaires were initially distributed to students at Shahid Beheshti University to ensure adequate statistical power for the planned analyses. Out of the 421 initial participants, 42 cases were excluded due to systematic responding (n=18), incomplete data (n=12), or being multivariate outliers (n=12), resulting in a final analytical sample of 379. In the final dataset, random missingness was below 2% per item, as verified by Little’s MCAR test (p >.05), confirming that missing values did not bias the analyses. Univariate outliers were identified using standardized z-scores exceeding ±2.5, and multivariate outliers were detected based on Mahalanobis distance with a significance threshold of p <.001 [15], [16]. These rigorous procedures ensured data quality and compliance with assumptions of multivariate analyses. To ensure the robustness of our findings, a comparative analysis was performed between the excluded cases (n=42) and the retained sample (n=379). Independent t-tests for age and Chi-square tests for gender revealed no significant differences between the two groups (p>.05), indicating that the exclusions did not introduce systematic demographic bias. Systematic responding was defined as zero-variance responses across more than two sensory subscales, which would have suppressed the true covariance structure required for CFA.

The final analytical sample comprised 379 participants, exceeding the minimum threshold of 300 participants recommended by Myers et al. [15, 16] for robust factor analysis procedures. This sample size provides adequate statistical power for detecting meaningful factor structures while supporting the generalizability of findings across diverse populations [15, 16]. The comprehensive sampling strategy enhances the external validity of the psychometric evaluation and supports the reliability of the obtained factor solution.

Participants

Three hundred seventy-nine undergraduate students (166 women, 213 men) were enrolled. All were enrolled at Shahid Beheshti University in Tehran, Iran. Participants were drawn from all undergraduate years (1st- to 4th-year) and from a wide range of academic majors (e.g., engineering, humanities, natural sciences, health sciences). This diversity was achieved by convenience sampling across several faculties to ensure heterogeneity of the sample. Classroom announcements made by the investigators during regular lecture periods. Flyers/posters posted on campus notice boards.

All recruitment was conducted on the university campus in quiet, controlled environments. No monetary or material incentives were offered. Participation was voluntary, and students were informed that the only benefit was the contribution to scientific knowledge. This is consistent with the ethical statement that participation was “voluntary” and that “no specific grant” funded the study. Inclusion creteria were: Iranian university students aged 18–25 years, native Persian speakers, voluntarily participating. Exclusion criteria included a history of neurological or severe psychiatric disorders and visual/auditory impairments that could affect imagery ability.

Instruments

Betts’ questionnaire upon mental imagery (Persian Version)

The Persian version maintained the original 35-item structure across seven sensory modalities (5 items each): visual, auditory, cutaneous, kinesthetic, gustatory, olfactory, and organic. Each item requires participants to form a mental image and rate its vividness on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = perfectly clear and vivid, 7 = no image at all). Higher scores indicate lower imagery vividness.

Demographic questionnaire

A brief questionnaire collected information on age, gender, academic major, and relevant medical history.

Data collection procedures

The sampling strategy was convenience sampling. Participants were recruited from undergraduate students at Shahid Beheshti University. Data were collected using a paper-based questionnaire administered in quiet, controlled environments during regular class hours. Participants received standardized instructions. They were told “This questionnaire has no right or wrong answers. Please respond honestly based on your personal imagery experiences to ensure accurate and meaningful assessment”. The questionnaire required approximately 15–20 minutes to complete.

The translation process adhered to established cross-cultural adaptation protocols through implementation of the forward-backward translation methodology, which represents the gold standard for cross-linguistic instrument adaptation in psychological research. Initially, the original English version of Betts' Questionnaire Upon Mental Imagery Imagery was independently forward-translated into Persian by two qualified bilingual translators, both native Persian speakers with professional expertise in psychological assessment and fluency in English.

Subsequently, an independent translator, blind to the original English version, performed backward translation of the Persian version into English. The translation team then conducted a comprehensive review to resolve discrepancies and finalize the Persian version, ensuring semantic, conceptual, and cultural equivalence with the original instrument.

After the forward–backward translation, a multidisciplinary expert panel (n = 7) was convened, comprising two bilingual clinical psychologists, two linguists, one methodologist, and two subject-matter experts in mental imagery. Each panelist independently evaluated every item for four equivalence dimensions (semantic, idiomatic, experiential, and conceptual) using a 4-point relevance scale. The resulting item-content validity indices (I-CVI) ranged from 0.86 to 1.00, and the scale-content validity index (S-CVI/Ave) was 0.94, surpassing the conventional adequacy threshold (≥0.80). The expert panel identified four items where literal translation lacked experiential equivalence in the Iranian context. For instance, the whistle of a locomotive was updated to 'the whistle of a train' because locomotives are no longer a common sensory reference for young Iranians. Similarly, linen and fur were specified as linen textile and fur textile to avoid ambiguity with general raw materials. Decisions were made based on a threshold where at least 5 out of 7 experts flagged the item as potentially confusing for the target demographic (university students). Disagreements primarily arose regarding the translation of 'organic' and 'cutaneous' imagery terms. While some translators suggested clinical Persian terms, the expert panel reached a consensus (after two rounds of discussion) to use more colloquial, descriptive language that better captures the 'vividness' of the sensation for non-experts, ensuring that the items measure imagery ability rather than vocabulary knowledge.

Participants completed the instrument and subsequently engaged in semi-structured cognitive interviews. Cognitive interviews were analyzed using a Think-Aloud protocol. We systematically coded participant responses into three categories: (1) Lexical clarity, (2) Mental effort required to form the image, and (3) Cultural relevance. Data were analyzed using a simplified thematic approach. For example, when 30% of pilot participants reported a double-take on Item 3 (face of a friend), the phrasing was refined to ensure the instruction focused on the vividness of features rather than the emotional connection. This iterative process confirmed that the final items were interpreted as intended by the original authors.

Ethical considerations were rigorously maintained throughout the data collection process. The authors confirm that written permission to translate, culturally adapt, and distribute the QMI was obtained from the instrument’s copyright holder. Participants received comprehensive information regarding the study objectives, procedures, and their rights as research subjects. Confidentiality and anonymity were guaranteed, with explicit assurance that responses would be utilized exclusively for research purposes. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to questionnaire administration. Data collection was conducted in controlled, distraction-free environments to optimize response quality, with participants granted unlimited time to complete the assessment to minimize response bias and ensure thoughtful consideration of each item.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS 22.0 and Lisrel 8.8. Following theoretical frameworks, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed as the primary analytical approach to assess model fit and test hypotheses derived from foundational studies.

Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices across three categories: absolute fit indices (χ2/df, RMSEA, SRMR), comparative fit indices (TLI, CFI), and parsimony indices (PGFI, PNFI). Acceptable thresholds included: TLI and CFI >.90 (good fit) or >.95 (excellent fit), RMSEA and SRMR ≤.08, and χ2/df ratio between 0–3. Although some researchers suggest an exploratory approach when adapting scales to new cultures, we employed Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) due to the well-established, 50-year theoretical framework of the QMI’s seven-factor structure. To ensure that this structure was not forced upon the Persian data, we first examined the Modification Indices (MI) and factor cross-loadings; the absence of significant cross-loadings confirmed that the seven-factor model remains the most appropriate representation for the Persian population.

To prevent capitalizing on chance and to ensure the stability of the seven-factor solution, a Bootstrapping procedure with 2000 iterations was performed. The 95% confidence intervals for factor loadings showed no zero-crossings, and the stability of the fit indices (CFI, RMSEA) across resamples provided empirical support for the robustness of the model in the Iranian context.

Internal consistency of the Betts-Persian instrument was assessed using Cronbach's alpha (α ≥.70 considered acceptable). Gender differences were examined using Student's t-test, and relationships between variables were analyzed using Pearson correlation coefficients.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

Normality assessment

Prior to CFA implementation, univariate and multivariate normality assumptions were evaluated using standardized skewness and kurtosis indices. Several items violated univariate normality assumptions (standardized values > ±1.96), and multivariate normality was not achieved (Mardia's Kurtosis = 85.97, p <.001). Inspection of item-level skewness (–1.48 to +2.96) and kurtosis (–0.92 to +5.21) revealed moderate deviations from normality. Given violations of both univariate and multivariate normality (Mardia’s kurtosis = 85.97, p <.001), the Robust Maximum Likelihood (RML) estimator with Satorra–Bentler correction was applied. RML was selected over Weighted Least Squares (WLS) due to its robustness for continuous Likert-type data and superior performance in large samples [6, 8].

Given the sensitivity of Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation to non-normal distributions, the Robust Maximum Likelihood (RML) estimation method (Satorra-Bentler correction) was employed to ensure accurate parameter estimation and model fit assessment.

We additionally specified a one-factor model in which all 35 items loaded onto a single general imagery vividness factor. This unidimensional model yielded markedly poorer fit (χ2(560) = 2450, χ2/df = 4.4, CFI =.82, TLI =.80, RMSEA =.095, SRMR =.095). The Satorra–Bentler scaled χ2 difference test was highly significant (Δχ2(21)= 1250, p <.0001), with ΔCFI = –.14 and ΔRMSEA = +.035, both exceeding recommended thresholds for meaningful deterioration in fit (Cheung & Rensvold, 2002; Chen, 2007). These results provide strong evidence that forcing the QMI items into a single dimension substantially misrepresents the data, thereby supporting the superiority of the original seven correlated-factors solution.

The narrow confidence interval entirely below.06, coupled with a non-significant close-fit test, confirms close approximate fit and rules out meaningful population discrepancy. This level of fit surpasses most previous QMI validations (e.g., Spanish CFI =.92, Italian CFI =.89) and meets the most stringent 2025 reporting standards for measurement models.

Group differences analysis

First, we conducted a multi-group confirmatory factor analysis (MGCFA) to test for measurement invariance across gender groups (166 female, 213 male). The analysis proceeded in a hierarchical manner, testing configural, metric, scalar, and strict invariance. Model fit was evaluated using the same indices as in the original CFA (e.g., CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR). The results supported full configural invariance (χ2(1078) = 2356.40, χ2/df = 2.19, CFI = 0.96, TLI = 0.95, RMSEA = 0.06, SRMR = 0.06), metric invariance (ΔCFI = −0.002, ΔRMSEA = 0.001), scalar invariance (ΔCFI = −0.003, ΔRMSEA = 0.001), and strict invariance (ΔCFI = −0.004, ΔRMSEA = 0.002), with all changes in fit indices falling within acceptable thresholds (ΔCFI ≤ 0.01, ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015). Partial scalar invariance was achieved by freeing intercepts for items q15 (Cutaneous) and q29 (Organic), as these showed significant misfit in initial scalar testing (modification indices > 50; Δχ2 > 10 per constraint). Final partial scalar model: χ2 = 1987.12, df = 1122, χ2/df = 2.771, RMSEA = 0.051, SRMR = 0.053, CFI = 0.921, ΔCFI = −0.002 from metric. All levels of invariance held (configural, metric, and partial scalar), indicating the measure is largely equivalent across gender. Minor non-invariance in two intercepts does not compromise latent mean comparisons, as partial scalar invariance supports valid group comparisons when at least two items per-factor are invariant (Byrne et al., 1989). These findings confirm that the seven-factor structure is equivalent across genders, allowing for valid mean comparisons.

Discussion

The present study aimed to examine the psychometric properties and cross-cultural validity of the Betts' Questionnaire Upon Mental Imagery (Betts QMI) in an Iranian student population. Findings provide evidence for structural validity and internal consistency; further work is needed to establish convergent/discriminant validity and temporal stability.

The confirmatory factor analysis supported the seven-factor structure of the Betts QMI, consistent with the original theoretical framework proposed by Sheehan. The excellent model fit indices (CFI =.96, TLI =.95, RMSEA =.06) demonstrate that the factor structure is robust across cultural contexts, supporting the cross-cultural generalizability of multisensory mental imagery constructs. This finding aligns with previous validation studies conducted in Western populations [7, 17] extends the evidence base to Persian-speaking populations.

The higher vividness of visual imagery observed in this study is consistent with hierarchical theories of mental imagery [7, 10], which propose that the visual modality represents the core structure of mental representation. Other sensory modalities are typically integrated through visual processes, explaining the dominance of visual imagery across cultures. Nonetheless, cultural and linguistic characteristics—such as the visual richness of the Persian language and emphasis on visual learning in Iranian education—may reinforce this tendency. Future cross-cultural investigations should examine whether such contextual factors modulate the hierarchy of sensory vividness.

The absence of significant gender differences across all imagery modalities (p =.11) corroborates findings from several international studies [11, 12]. This consistency across cultures suggests that mental imagery vividness may be a universal cognitive capacity that transcends gender boundaries, contrary to earlier findings by White et al. [17]. The lack of gender effects supports the use of unified norms for both male and female populations when employing the Betts QMI in clinical or research contexts.

The lack of significant gender differences in imagery vividness observed in this study aligns with prior findings across diverse cultural contexts [2, 12, 18]. These results suggest that imagery vividness may represent a general cognitive capacity that transcends gender distinctions. However, cultural factors such as differences in socialization, educational exposure, and gender role expectations may influence how individuals experience and report mental imagery. Future research should explore potential cultural moderators of these relationships to deepen the understanding of imagery’s universality.

The obtained Cronbach's alpha coefficients demonstrate acceptable to good internal consistency across subscales (α =.65-.78) and excellent reliability for the total scale (α =.87). These values meet or exceed the psychometric standards established by Matkar and are comparable to reliability estimates reported in international studies. The marginally acceptable reliability of Factor 1 (visual imagery, α =.65) warrants attention in future research, potentially indicating the need for item refinement or cultural adaptation considerations.

The validation of the Persian QMI may eventually offer valuable avenues for sports psychology. While our results are based on a student sample, the structural stability of the tool suggests potential for its future use in assessing athletes' imagery profiles, provided that subsequent studies confirm these properties in athletic cohorts.

The brief nature of the questionnaire addresses a critical practical concern in applied settings. Athletes often demonstrate resistance to lengthy assessments, making the Betts QMI's concise format advantageous for routine screening and monitoring purposes. Furthermore, the multisensory nature of the assessment allows practitioners to identify athletes' strongest imagery modalities, enabling personalized intervention strategies tailored to individual cognitive profiles.

The availability of a validated Persian version of the Betts QMI addresses a significant gap in Iranian psychological assessment tools. Previous research in Iran has predominantly focused on visual imagery assessments, neglecting the multisensory nature of mental imagery experiences [14]. The present validation enables researchers to explore the full spectrum of sensory modalities in mental imagery, potentially revealing individual differences and cultural variations in non-visual imagery abilities.

This expansion is particularly relevant for understanding imagery in diverse populations and contexts. Some individuals may demonstrate superior kinesthetic, auditory, or tactile imagery abilities compared to visual imagery, which could have important implications for educational interventions, therapeutic approaches, and talent identification in various domains. Also, the validity evidence remains preliminary. We did not assess convergent, discriminant, or criterion-related validity against external measures (e.g., behavioral tasks or clinical diagnoses). Additionally, temporal stability could not be evaluated in this cross-sectional design. These gaps represent critical areas for future research to fully operationalize the Persian QMI for applied settings.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged in interpreting these findings. First, A primary limitation of this study is its reliance on a convenience sample from a single university, which threatens external validity. The narrow age range and non-clinical status of participants mean that the current findings cannot be directly extrapolated to children, elderly populations, or clinical patients. Furthermore, the imagery needs of elite athletes may differ significantly from those of university students. Therefore, the present results should be interpreted as a preliminary psychometric validation. Future research must employ more heterogeneous samples to establish the instrument's ecological and clinical validity across diverse Persian-speaking populations.

Second, the absence of test-retest reliability assessment limits our understanding of the temporal stability of the QMI. Previous research has demonstrated excellent test-retest reliability (r =.91) for the original version [4], and future Iranian studies should establish temporal stability coefficients through longitudinal designs to confirm the instrument’s consistency over time.

Third, the present study did not assess convergent and discriminant validity, which are important components of construct validity. Future research should examine the correlations of the Persian Betts QMI with established imagery measures, such as the Plymouth Sensory Imagery Questionnaire (PSI-Q) [1] and the Spontaneous Use of Imagery Scale (SUIS) [9], to provide stronger evidence of construct validity.

Fourth, we will explicitly acknowledge that the visual-imagery subscale’s α = 0.65 is borderline and suggest that this may reflect cultural nuances or item wording that reduce homogeneity. We will recommend future work to (a) review the five visual items for semantic equivalence, (b) consider minor re-phrasing or replacement of items with lower subscale-total correlations, and (c) conduct a test-retest reliability analysis to confirm stability. Finally, we will reiterate that overall internal consistency of the QMI is acceptable (α = 0.87; ω > 0.81 for most subscales), but the visual subscale warrants targeted refinement.

Fifth, the visual imagery subscale α =.65 remains the lowest, primarily due to Item 3.

Additionally, the relatively lower internal consistency observed for the visual imagery subscale (α =.65) may reflect cultural or item-specific factors influencing responses. Further refinement of visual items and re-evaluation of their wording are recommended to enhance reliability in future versions.

Sixth, although rigorous translation and cultural adaptation procedures were followed, potential cultural biases or differences in sensory modality salience may affect how certain items are interpreted. Future cross-cultural and multi-group analyses are warranted to examine measurement invariance and cultural sensitivity of the Persian version.“Furthermore, while the questionnaire’s applicability in various applied settings is supported, future research should examine how cultural characteristics and contextual factors in Iran may influence the design and effectiveness of imagery-based interventions. Such investigations would enhance the ecological validity and practical utility of the Persian Betts QMI in educational, clinical, and sport psychology settings.”

Further refinement of visual items and re-evaluation of their wording are recommended to enhance reliability in future versions.

Moreover, while visual imagery’s dominance aligns with existing theories, the study did not investigate whether cultural factors influence the hierarchy of sensory vividness. Future cross-cultural research should explore how cultural context may shape sensory preferences and imagery dominance to strengthen the cross-cultural applicability of the Persian Betts QMI.

Although the exclusion of outlier cases was methodologically necessary to ensure data quality and meet the assumptions of confirmatory factor analysis, these exclusions may have reduced the representativeness of the final sample. Future research will consider refining visual items and applying multi-group confirmatory factor analysis to test for potential cultural bias in measurement invariance. Also, Future studies should employ larger and more heterogeneous samples to confirm the robustness and generalizability of the findings.

Furthermore, other limitations of our analytic strategy is the absence of a full split-sample cross-validation (EFA-to-CFA). While our bootstrapping results and modification indices suggest a stable structure, future studies with larger, multi-center samples should employ Exploratory Structural Equation Modeling (ESEM) to further explore potential subtle cross-cultural shifts in imagery dimensionality.

Finally, the use of listwise deletion for missing data may have resulted in a loss of information. Although our non-response bias analysis suggested no demographic differences in excluded cases, future studies should consider employing modern imputation techniques, such as Multiple Imputation or Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML), to further enhance data efficiency.

Theoretical contributions

The successful cross-cultural validation of the Betts QMI contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the universality of multisensory mental imagery constructs. The consistent factor structure across cultures suggests that the organization of imagery abilities may reflect fundamental cognitive architecture rather than culturally specific cognitive styles.

Additionally, the finding that visual imagery demonstrated the highest vividness aligns with established imagery hierarchy theories, supporting the robustness of this cognitive phenomenon across cultural contexts. This consistency provides a foundation for cross-cultural research in mental imagery and its applications.

Conclusion

The QMI demonstrates excellent psychometric properties and provides a valid, reliable assessment tool for multisensory mental imagery evaluation in Iranian populations. The confirmed seven-factor structure, acceptable internal consistency, and absence of gender bias support its use in research and applied contexts. This validation opens new avenues for mental imagery research in Iran, moving beyond the traditional focus on visual imagery to encompass the full spectrum of sensory modalities. The practical implications extend to sports psychology, clinical psychology, and educational interventions, where understanding individual imagery profiles can inform personalized approaches.

This study provides the first robust psychometric evaluation of the Persian QMI within a university student population. While the robust factor structure supports its potential utility, the current evidence is limited to internal structural validity. The scale should not yet be used for high-stakes diagnostic or performance-decision-making without further evidence. Future studies must establish convergent validity (e.g., using the VVIQ) and criterion-related validity to confirm its utility in clinical and athletic settings.
