Exact output tracking for the one-dimensional heat equation and applications to the interpolation problem in Gevrey classes of order 2
Lucas Davron, and Pierre Lissy

Abstract




Abstract

This paper provides a complete characterization of the Dirichlet boundary outputs that can be exactly tracked in the one-dimensional heat equation with Neumann boundary control. The problem consists in describing the set of boundary traces generated by square-integrable controls over a finite or infinite time horizon. We show that these outputs form a precise functional space related to Gevrey regularity of order 2. In the infinite-time case, the trackable outputs are precisely those functions whose successive derivatives satisfy a weighted summability condition, which corresponds to specific Gevrey classes. For finite-time horizons, an additional compatibility condition involving the reachable space of the system provides a full characterization. The analysis relies on Fourier-Laplace transform, properties of Hardy spaces, the flatness method, and a new Plancherel-type theorem for Hilbert spaces of Gevrey functions. Beyond control theory, our results yield an optimal solution to the classical interpolation problem in Gevrey-
2
classes, which improves results of Mitjagin on the optimal loss factor. The techniques developed here also extend to variants of the heat system with different boundary conditions or observation points.

Keywords

Heat equation; boundary control;
exact output tracking;
Gevrey regularity; interpolation problem.

1.1
Output tracking

Let us consider the one-dimensional heat problem with Neumann boundary conditions

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rclcc}z_{t}(t,x)&=&z_{xx}(t,x),&0<x<1,&t>0,\\
z_{x}(t,1)&=&u(t),\\
z_{x}(t,0)&=&0,\\
z(0,x)&=&0,\\
y(t)&=&z(t,0),\end{array}\right.

(1)

where
z=z(t,x)
denotes the unknown state,
u=u(t)
the control, and

is what we call the

y

\mathcal{Y}(0,T):=\{y\in L^{1}_{\operatorname{loc}}[0,T):u\in L^{2}(0,T)\},

In general, output tracking is a central problem in automatic control theory. From both theoretical and practical perspectives, the full state of a control system is often neither directly controllable nor observable, making full-state tracking unrealistic. In such situations, one seeks instead to track only a given
output
of the system, such as, a boundary trace, for instance, as in the present example.

Furthermore, output tracking can be viewed as a preliminary step towards the more demanding problem of
output regulation

t\to+\infty

[
34

for related results on the one-dimensional heat equation).

In contrast, much less is known about

[
57

[
51

12

1.3
Consequences for the interpolation problem in Gevrey-
2

Consider a sequence of positive numbers
(m_{k})
, possibly taking the value
+\infty
, and the associated Denjoy-Carleman class

C^{\infty}(m_{k}):=\left\{\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}):\exists C,A>0,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\quad|\varphi^{(k)}(x)|<CA^{k}m_{k}\right\}.

Such classes naturally arise in several contexts, such as the regularity theory of PDEs
[
26
,
19
]
, the asymptotic developments of the solutions of algebraic ODEs
[
47
,
46
]
, or the control theory of parabolic PDEs
[
38
,
10

36
]
. We refer to
[
3
]
for a historical survey. Some interesting properties of the class

can be described by the so-called Borel operator

\mathcal{B}:C^{\infty}(m_{k})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}},\quad\mathcal{B}\varphi:=(\varphi^{(k)}(0))_{k=0}^{\infty}.

A theorem due to Denjoy and Carleman gives a necessary and sufficient condition on the sequence
(m_{k})
for the operator
\mathcal{B}
to be injective. If this is the case, the class
C^{\infty}(m_{k})
is called quasi-analytic, as it shares the following feature with analytic functions: a function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(m_{k})
identically vanishes as soon as every its derivatives at
0
vanish. In the opposite case, we call
C^{\infty}(m_{k})
a non quasi-analytic class.

Another important property of
C^{\infty}(m_{k})
is the characterization of the range of
\mathcal{B}
, which we will refer to as the interpolation problem. Two extreme cases must be mentioned. The first one is when
\mathcal{B}
acts on
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
(
i.e.

m_{k}\equiv\infty
). In this case, the operator
\mathcal{B}
is surjective, which is commonly referred to as Borel’s Theorem
3
3

3

Borel showed this result in
[
5
]
, although it seems to have been proved first by Peano, see
[
2
]
. The other extreme case is when
\mathcal{B}
acts on
\operatorname{Hol}(D(0,R))
. In this case the Cauchy-Hadamard formula yields that a sequence
(a_{k})
belongs to
\mathcal{B}\operatorname{Hol}(D(0,R))
if and only if

\forall 0<\epsilon<R,\quad\exists c>0,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\quad|a_{k}|\leq c\frac{k!}{(R-\epsilon)^{k}}.

The case of
\mathcal{B}
acting on a non quasi-analytic class can thus be viewed as intermediate between these two extreme cases. At this point it is meaningful to make the following observation: if the class
C^{\infty}(m_{k})
is non quasi-analytic, then one can build a cutoff function belonging to the class
C^{\infty}(m_{k})
, and consequently the Borel operator is local:

\forall\epsilon>0,\quad\mathcal{B}C^{\infty}(m_{k})=\mathcal{B}\{\varphi\in C^{\infty}(m_{k}):\operatorname{supp}\varphi\subset(-\epsilon,+\epsilon)\}.

Many works have addressed the description of the range of

To show Proposition

we rely on the theory of boundary values of holomorphic functions, which we recall below.

2.1
Boundary values of holomorphic functions

Let us recall some well-known facts on holomorphic functions in the open right half-plane.

Definition 11
.

Let
p\in[1,+\infty[
. A function
F\in\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
is said to belong to
H^{p}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
if

\|F\|_{H^{p}(\mathbb{C}_{+})}^{p}:=\sup_{x>0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(x+i\xi)|^{p}d\xi<\infty.

A function
F\in\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
is said to belong to
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
if

\|F\|_{H^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}_{+})}:=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}}|F(z)|<\infty.

The above definition naturally yields a Banach space for any
p\in[1,+\infty]
. The following result is due to Paley and Wiener. In particular, it implies that
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
is also a Hilbert space.

Theorem 12
.

[
50
, Theorem 19.2]

Consider the Laplace transform

\mathcal{L}:L^{2}(0,\infty)\rightarrow H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+}),\quad\mathcal{L}f(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}f(t)dt.

Then the operator
\mathcal{L}/(2\pi)
is a surjective isometry.

The theory of boundary values for Hardy functions begins with Fatou’s Theorem. It is usually proved first for functions holomorphic on the unit disc
[
50
, Theorem 17.10]
and then exported to other geometries by conformal mapping.

Theorem 13
.

Let
F\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
and
0<\alpha<1
. Then for almost every
i\xi\in i\mathbb{R}
, the limit

\lim_{\Gamma_{\alpha}(i\xi)\ni z\rightarrow i\xi}F(z),

exists. The induced function
\xi\mapsto F(i\xi)
is
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

In the above, we have used the notation

\Gamma_{\alpha}(i\xi)=i\xi+\Gamma_{\alpha},\quad\Gamma_{\alpha}=\{x+i\tau\in\mathbb{C}_{+}:|\tau|<\alpha x\}.

Such limits are referred to as non-tangential, or sometimes angular. The next result is often called Lusin-Privalov’s uniqueness theorem.

Theorem 14
.

[
32
, III.D]

Let
F\in\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
, and assume that for a subset
E\subset i\mathbb{R}
of positive measure, there holds

\exists 0<\alpha<1,\quad\forall i\xi\in E,\quad\lim_{\Gamma_{\alpha}(i\xi)\ni z\rightarrow i\xi}F(z)=0.

Then
F
is identically zero on
\mathbb{C}_{+}
.

The next two results are also known as Paley–Wiener theorems. Here and throughout, we define the Fourier transform of a function
f
by

\mathcal{F}f(\xi):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-ix\xi}f(x)dx.

Theorem 15
.

[
50
, Theorem 19.3]

Let
f\in L^{2}(-A,A)
for some
A>0
. Then
\mathcal{F}f\in\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{C})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies

\exists C>0,\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C},\quad|\mathcal{F}f(z)|\leq Ce^{A|z|}.

Conversely, if
\phi\in\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{C})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies the above estimate, then
\mathcal{F}\phi
has support contained in
[-A,A]
.

Theorem 16
.

[
28
, Theorem 7.2]

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Then the two following conditions are equivalent

1.

There exists a function
F\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
such that

F(x+i\cdot)\xrightarrow[x\rightarrow 0^{+}]{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}f,

2.

\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F}f\subset(-\infty,0]

It can be shown that the boundary value
f
given by the previous theorem coincides with the one of Fatou’s Theorem:
F(i\xi)=f(\xi)
for almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
. We will use angular boundary values rather than
L^{2}
boundary values, as the former are conveniently stable by multiplication.

Combining Theorem
16
and Lusin-Privalov’s uniqueness theorem, we obtain the following characterization of the holomorphic functions belonging to
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
, from their boundary value.

Proposition 17
.

Let
F\in\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
have boundary value
f\in L^{1}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
in the following sense: there is
0<\alpha<1
such that for almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}

\lim_{\Gamma_{\alpha}(i\xi)\ni z\rightarrow i\xi}F(z)=f(\xi).

Then
F\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
if and only if
f
satisfies both

f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F}f\subset(-\infty,0].

2.2
Spectrum of entire functions

We are now in a position to prove Proposition
6
. We will need the following definition.

Definition 18
.

The order
\rho
of an entire non-constant function
f
is defined as

\rho=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\log M_{f}(r)}{\log r},\quad M_{f}(r):=\max_{|z|\leq r}|f(z)|.

If
\rho<\infty
, the type
\tau
of
f
is defined by

\tau=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log M_{f}(r)}{r^{\rho}}.

In particular, if both
\rho
and
\tau
are finite, we have

\forall\epsilon>0,\quad\exists C>0,\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C},\quad|f(z)|\leq e^{(\tau+\epsilon)|z|^{\rho}},

and
\tau
is the best such constant. It is customary to say that
f
has exponential type
\leq\tau
when the above inequality holds with
\rho=1
.

From Proposition
17
, Proposition
6
is a consequence of Lemma
19
below.

Lemma 19
.

Let
\phi
be entire on
\mathbb{C}
and of order
<1
. Then for every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\phi f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F}(\phi f)\subset\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F}f.

Recall that Lemma
19
is motivated by the formal computation

\displaystyle\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F}[\phi f]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F}\phi*\mathcal{F}f

\displaystyle\subset(\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F}\phi)+(\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F}f)

\displaystyle\subset(-\infty,0]+\{0\}\subset(-\infty,0],

which uses
\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F}\phi\subset\{0\}
. However, as already pointed out in the introduction, for
\phi
entire of order
<1
, the restriction of
\phi
to the real axis might not be a tempered distribution. We can give a meaning to
\mathcal{F}\phi
using Gelfand–Silov’s generalized function space

or the theory of hyperfunctions, see
[
20
,
22
,
49
,
53

21

27
]
. However, in this setting it is not clear how to define the convolution
\mathcal{F}\phi*\mathcal{F}f
, hence we are not able to directly use the above formal computation.

We let
s>1
to be adjusted later on, and we introduce a Gevrey approximation of the unity as follows. We start by considering a function
\varrho\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with the following properties

\operatorname{supp}\varrho\subset[-1,1],\quad|\varrho^{(k)}(x)|\leq C^{k+1}k^{ks},\quad\int_{-1}^{+1}\varrho(x)dx=1,

(18)

for some constant

possibly depending on

or
k

5

5

The existence of such a function

.

We then set

\varrho_{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\varrho\left(\frac{x}{\epsilon}\right),

(20)

(21)

Note further that since

Now consider

2.3
A Plancherel result in Gevrey classes

In this subsection, we show Theorem
7
. Let
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
be such that
\mathcal{F}f\in L^{1}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
s,R>0
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. The proof is done by estimating the quantity

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{F}f(\xi)\omega(\xi)|^{2}d\xi,\quad\omega(\xi):=(1+|\xi|)^{\gamma}e^{R|\xi|^{1/s}},

whether it is finite or not. We will replace the weight
\omega
by another weight
\varpi
of the form

\varpi(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{2k},\quad a_{k}\geq 0,

which is such that
\omega\asymp\varpi
on
\mathbb{R}
and
\varpi
is an entire and even function on
\mathbb{C}
. Here and throughout,
\asymp
means that we both have
\lesssim
and
\gtrsim
. Let us assume provisionally that such a weight
\varpi
exists, we then compute

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{F}f(\xi)\omega(\xi)|^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle\asymp\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{F}f(\xi)\varpi(\xi)|^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|{\mathcal{F}f(\xi)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\xi^{2k}\right|^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathcal{F}f(\xi)\right|^{2}\xi^{2k}a_{k}\xi^{2l}a_{l}d\xi

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=0}^{\infty}a_{k}a_{l}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|{\mathcal{F}f^{(k+l)}(\xi)}|^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle\asymp\sum_{k,l=0}^{\infty}a_{k}a_{l}||f^{(k+l)}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}||{f^{(n)}}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}a_{n-k}.{}

(25)

We are thus left to find such a
\varpi
and to estimate the quantity

A_{n}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}a_{n-k},

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. For
\alpha,\beta>0
we introduce the two-parameters Mittag-Leffler function

E_{\alpha,\beta}(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+\beta)},

which is entire on
\mathbb{C}
. It admits the following asymptotic expansion on the positive real axis:

E_{\alpha,\beta}(x)\sim\frac{1}{\alpha}x^{\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha}}\exp x^{1/\alpha},\quad x\rightarrow+\infty,

see
[
24
, Theorems 4.3 and 4.4]
.

A particular case.
Let us assume that
\gamma<0
,
R=1
, and put

\varpi(z):=E_{\alpha,\beta+1}(z^{2})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+\beta+1)},\quad\frac{1}{s}=\frac{2}{\alpha},\quad\gamma=-2\frac{\beta}{\alpha},

(26)

so that
\varpi
is entire and even on
\mathbb{C}
, bounded below by a positive constant on
\mathbb{R}
, and admits the asymptotic expansion

\varpi(\xi)\sim\frac{1}{\alpha}|\xi|^{\gamma}\exp|\xi|^{1/s},\quad\xi\rightarrow\pm\infty.

To estimate
A_{n}
we start by writing Stirling’s formula as

\frac{1}{a_{k}}=\Gamma(\alpha k+\beta+1)\asymp(1+k)^{\beta+1/2}\left(\frac{\alpha k}{e}\right)^{\alpha k},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

Note that we write
(1+k)^{\beta+1/2}
rather than
k^{\beta+1/2}
, so that the right-hand side of the above estimate never vanishes. We obtain, for
0\leq k\leq n
and
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{k}a_{n-k}}

\displaystyle\asymp\left[(1+k)(1+n-k)\right]^{\beta+1/2}\left(\frac{\alpha k}{e}\right)^{\alpha k}\left(\frac{\alpha(n-k)}{e}\right)^{\alpha(n-k)}

\displaystyle=n^{1+2\beta}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{k}{n}\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}+1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\right]^{\beta+1/2}\left(\frac{n\alpha}{e}\right)^{\alpha n}\left[\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{\alpha k/n}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)^{\alpha\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)}\right]^{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n\alpha}{e}\right)^{\alpha n}n^{1+2\beta}g_{n}(k/n)^{-1}h(k/n)^{n},

with

g_{n}(x)=\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}+x\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}+1-x\right)\right]^{-\beta-1/2},\quad h(x)=x^{\alpha x}(1-x)^{\alpha(1-x)}.

This brings

A_{n}\asymp\left(\frac{e}{\alpha n}\right)^{\alpha n}\frac{1}{n^{2\beta}}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}g_{n}(k/n)h(k/n)^{-n},\quad(n\geq 1),

(27)

which reduces the estimation of
A_{n}
to that of the second factor on the right-hand side of (
27
). We write

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}g_{n}(k/n)h(k/n)^{-n}=g_{n}(1/2)\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}h(k/n)^{-n}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(g_{n}(k/n)-g_{n}(1/2))h(k/n)^{-n}.

(28)

To estimate the above right-hand side, we begin by estimating the first of the two sums. We write

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}h(k/n)^{-n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}e^{-n\log h(k/n)},

which looks like a discrete analog of the integral

\int_{0}^{1}e^{-n\log h(x)}dx.

For the above integral, the asymptotic behaviour is well known by Laplace’s method. Following
[
39
]
, we rely on the following Lemma.

Lemma 20
.

Let
u:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be in
C^{2}(0,1)\cap C[0,1]
with global strict minimum at
x_{0}\in(0,1)
, for which
u^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})>0
. Then

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}e^{-nu(k/n)}\sim\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{u^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})n}}e^{-nu(x_{0})},\quad n\rightarrow\infty.

The proof of this Lemma is elementary but rather lengthy, we defer it to the Appendix
B
. Applying this result with
u=\log h
we obtain the following asymptotic behaviour, for which we recall that
h(1/2)=2^{-\alpha}
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}h(k/n)^{-n}\asymp\frac{2^{\alpha n}}{\sqrt{n}},\quad(n\geq 1).

Because
g_{n}(1/2)\asymp 1
, we deduce

g_{n}(1/2)\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}h(k/n)^{-n}\asymp\frac{2^{\alpha n}}{\sqrt{n}},\quad(n\geq 1).

(29)

Coming back to (
28
), we see that (
29
) may be plugged-in (
27
) as soon as

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(g_{n}(k/n)-g_{n}(1/2))h(k/n)^{-n}=o\left(\frac{2^{\alpha n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right),\quad n\rightarrow\infty.

(30)

To show that (
30
) holds, we introduce a sequence
(\epsilon_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
with
0<\epsilon_{n}<1/2
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, to be specified later on, and compute

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(g_{n}(k/n)-g_{n}(1/2))h(k/n)^{-n}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\left|\frac{k}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|\leq\epsilon_{n}}|g_{n}(k/n)-g_{n}(1/2)|h(k/n)^{-n}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\left|\frac{k}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|>\epsilon_{n}}|g_{n}(k/n)-g_{n}(1/2)|h(k/n)^{-n}.{}

(31)

We estimate the two terms of the right-hand side of (
31
) separately. For the second one, we observe that
g_{n}
is positive on
[0,1]
, where it reaches its maximal value at
x=0
and
x=1
, for which

g_{n}(0)\sim n^{\beta+1/2},\quad n\rightarrow\infty.

Moreover,

\min_{|x-x_{0}|\geq\epsilon_{n}}h(x)=h(1/2+\epsilon_{n}),

hence

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\left|\frac{k}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|>\epsilon_{n}}|g_{n}(k/n)-g_{n}(1/2)|h(k/n)^{-n}\lesssim n^{\beta+1/2}h(1/2+\epsilon_{n})^{-n},\quad n\geq 1.

Recalling
h(1/2)=2^{-\alpha}
, we deduce

n^{\beta+1/2}h(1/2+\epsilon_{n})^{-n}=o\left(\frac{2^{\alpha n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\Longleftrightarrow n\left[\log h(1/2+\epsilon_{n})-\log h(1/2)\right]-(\beta+1)\log n\rightarrow+\infty,

and by log convexity of
h
, there exists some constant
\kappa>0
such that

\log h(1/2+\epsilon_{n})-\log h(1/2)\geq\kappa\epsilon_{n}^{2}.

We take

\epsilon_{n}=\frac{1}{n^{\mu}},\quad 0<\mu<1/2,

so that the second term on the right-hand side of (
31
) has the appropriate size. For the first term, we write

g_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{i_{n}(x)^{2\beta+1}},\quad i_{n}(x)=\left(\frac{1}{n}+x\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}+1-x\right),

and compute for
|x-1/2|\leq\delta

|i_{n}(x)-1/4|\leq|i_{n}(x)-i_{n}(1/2)|+|i_{n}(1/2)-1/4|\leq\delta^{2}+\frac{2}{n}.

Therefore, for small
\delta
and large

, the term

is close to

, around which the function

is Lipschitz. We thus put
\delta=\epsilon_{n}
, so that for

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\left|\frac{k}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|\leq\epsilon_{n}}|g_{n}(k/n)-g_{n}(1/2)|h(k/n)^{-n}\lesssim\left(\epsilon_{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\right)2^{\alpha n}.

We take
1/4<\mu<1/2
in the definition of
\epsilon_{n}
so that the above contribution is negligible compared with
2^{\alpha n}/\sqrt{n}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. This shows that (
30
) holds.

We may thus plug (
29
) in (
27
), which yields

A_{n}\asymp\left(\frac{2e}{\alpha n}\right)^{\alpha n}\frac{1}{n^{2\beta+1/2}},\quad(n\geq 1).

From (

) and (

), we see that the sequence
(M_{n})
should satisfy
M_{n}\asymp 1/\sqrt{A_{n}}
, and using (

) we compute

\frac{1}{\sqrt{A_{n}}}\asymp\left(\frac{ns}{e}\right)^{ns}n^{-s\gamma+1/4},\quad(n\geq 1).

This ends the proof of Theorem

, under the additional assumptions
R=1
and
\gamma<0
.

General case.
We extend the result to the cases of
R>0
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
arbitrary by acting on
f
. The case of general
R>0
is easily deduced from the case
R=1
by replacing

by
f(R^{s}x)
, so, without loss of generality, we may assume that
R=1
. To allow
\gamma
to be non-negative, we pick
p\in\mathbb{N}
large enough so that
\tilde{\gamma}:=\gamma-p<0
. We then observe that for every
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
with
\mathcal{F}f\in L^{1}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
, there holds

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{F}f(\xi)e^{-|\xi|^{1/s}}(1+|\xi|)^{\gamma}|^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle\asymp\sum_{k=0}^{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{F}f^{(k)}(\xi)e^{-|\xi|^{1/s}}(1+|\xi|)^{\tilde{\gamma}}|^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle\asymp\sum_{k=0}^{p}\|f^{(k)}\|_{\mathcal{G}_{s,1,\tilde{\gamma}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\|f^{(k+n)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}}{M_{n}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\asymp\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\|f^{(n)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}}{N_{n}}\right)^{2},

where

M_{n}=\left(\frac{ns}{e}\right)^{ns}(1+n)^{-s\tilde{\gamma}+1/4},

and

is any positive sequence with

For instance, we can take

This shows that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{F}f(\xi)e^{-|\xi|^{1/s}}(1+|\xi|)^{\gamma}|^{2}d\xi\asymp\|f\|_{\mathcal{G}_{s,1,\gamma}},

which ends the proof of Theorem

.

Proof of the main results

Proof of Theorem
1
.

In what follows every the signals defined on
(0,\infty)
are extended by 0 on
(-\infty,0)
. Assume first that
y\in\mathcal{Y}(0,\infty)
, let us show that
y
is Laplace transformable and its Laplace transform satisfies (
12
). A formal proof is done by taking the Laplace transform in time in (
1
) (without checking whether it is legitimate or not), solving the resulting second order differential equation in space, using the boundary conditions given by the second and third lines of (
1
) and then considering
x=0
.

Let us now give a rigorous proof. We invoke the representation formula

z(t,x)=2\int_{0}^{t}\theta(x-1,t-\sigma)u(\sigma)d\sigma,

(32)

where

\theta(t,x)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}K(t,x+2m),\quad K(t,x)=\frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}.

Such a formula can be found
e.g.
in
[
7
, Theorem 6.4.1]
. Evaluating (
32
) at
x=0
, we obtain

y(t)=z(t,0)=\int_{0}^{t}k(t-\sigma)u(\sigma)d\sigma,

(33)

where we have introduced the kernel

k(t)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}K(t,2m-1)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{(m+1/2)^{2}}{t}},\,t>0,

extended by
0
on
(-\infty,0]
. Crude estimates show that

\exists C>0,\quad\forall t>0,\quad|k(t)|\leq C\sqrt{t},

so that

|y(t)|\lesssim\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}t,

hence
y
is Laplace transformable, with abscissa of absolute convergence
\sigma_{y}\leq 0
. Since
k
and
u
are supported in
[0,\infty)
, we recognize this as the usual convolution product, and taking the Laplace transform, we have

\hat{y}(s)=\hat{k}(s)\hat{u}(s).

An explicit computation gives that

\hat{k}(s)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}e^{-\frac{(m+1/2)^{2}}{t}}\right)(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}e^{-2|m+1/2|\sqrt{s}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\frac{2e^{\sqrt{s}}}{e^{2\sqrt{s}}-1}.

We deduce that the transfer function of (
1
) is given by

\hat{k}(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}\sinh\sqrt{s}},

whence

\hat{y}(s)=\frac{\hat{u}(s)}{\sqrt{s}\sinh\sqrt{s}},

which rewrites as

\hat{u}(s)=\sqrt{s}\sinh\sqrt{s}\hat{y}(s)=\frac{\sinh\sqrt{s}}{\sqrt{s}}\hat{\dot{y}}(s)=\phi(s)\hat{\dot{y}}(s),

(34)

where we have denoted

\phi(s):=\frac{\sinh\sqrt{s}}{\sqrt{s}}.

(35)

Note that

is bounded on
\mathbb{C}_{+}
, so that
\hat{\dot{y}}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
and
\dot{y}\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
. In particular, the Laplace transform
\hat{\dot{y}}
has boundary value
\hat{\dot{y}}(i\xi)=\mathcal{F}\dot{y}(\xi)
. Now, coming back to

we take advantage of the above function having a square-integrable boundary values to deduce that

satisfies (
14
). From the Paley–Wiener Theorem

and Proposition
6
, the previous reasoning can be reversed, so that

\mathcal{Y}(0,\infty)=\left\{y\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}):\operatorname{supp}y\subset[0,\infty),\quad\dot{y}\mbox{ satisfies }\eqref{eq:charac_Y_fourier}\right\}.

The rest of the proof of Theorem

then follows from the Gevrey-Plancherel Theorem

.
∎

2.5
Proof of Proposition
4

In this subsection we prove Proposition
4
. We will use the following Lemma, taken from
[
38
, Theorem 3.8]
.

Lemma 21
.

Let
(a_{n})
be a sequence such that the power series

f(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n+1}}{n!(n+1)!}z^{n},

has a nonzero radius of convergence. Assume that
f
extends to a holomorphic function on a neighborhood of
\mathbb{R}_{-}
in
\mathbb{C}
with,

\exists C>0,\quad\forall n\geq 0,\quad\forall x<0,\quad|f^{(n)}(x)|\leq C|f^{(n)}(0)|.

(36)

Then there exists
\varphi\in C^{\infty}[-1,0]
such that

\varphi^{(n)}(0)=a_{n},\quad|\varphi^{(n)}(t)|\lesssim|a_{n}|,\quad(n\geq 0,\quad-1\leq t\leq 0).

Proof of Proposition
4
.

According to
[
38
, Remark 3.3, item 3]
, the function

f(z):=\frac{1}{1-z},

satisfies the hypotheses of Lemma
21
. Hence there exists
\phi\in C^{\infty}[-1,0]
such that

\phi^{(n)}(0)=b_{n},\quad|\phi^{(n)}(t)|\lesssim b_{n},\quad(n\geq 0,\quad-1\leq t\leq 0),

where
b_{n+1}:=n!(n+1)!
(no condition is imposed on
b_{0}
). By Stirling’s formula,

b_{n}\asymp\frac{(2n)!}{\sqrt{1+n}4^{n}}.

We then consider
a_{n}:=4^{n}b_{n}
. By scaling, there exists
\varphi\in C^{\infty}[-1,0]
such that

\varphi^{(n)}(0)=a_{n},\quad|\varphi^{(n)}(t)|\lesssim\frac{(2n)!}{\sqrt{1+n}},\quad(n\geq 0,\quad-1\leq t\leq 0).

Assume by contradiction that there exists some
\psi\in C^{\infty}[0,1]
such that (
8
) holds with
T=1
. The sequence
(a_{n})
is then the sequence of successive derivatives at an interior point of a
\mathcal{G}_{2,1/\sqrt{2},-1/2}
function. Hence, by Corollary
3
we deduce

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\frac{\zeta^{2n}}{(2n)!}\in\mathcal{R}.

However, a sharper estimate of
b_{n}
yields

\frac{a_{n}}{(2n)!}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{n}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}\right),

where, for any bounded sequence
(c_{n})
, the series
\sum c_{n}\zeta^{2n}/n^{3/2}
converges absolutely in
A^{2}(D(0,1))
. We thus deduce that the function

\operatorname{Li}_{-1/2}(\zeta^{2})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{2n}}{n^{-1/2}},

belongs to
A^{2}(\Omega)
. For every
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the function

\operatorname{Li}_{s}(\zeta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n^{s}},

is a special function often called the polylogarithm, or Jonquière’s function, and is a special case of Lerch’s function. It is defined by a power series with radius of convergence 1 and has the following asymptotic behaviour

\operatorname{Li}_{s}(\zeta)=\frac{\Gamma(1-s)2^{s-1}}{(1-\zeta)^{1-s}}+O(1),\quad\zeta\rightarrow 1,\quad\zeta\in D(1/2,1/2),

for every
s\neq 1,2,3,...
, see
[
16
, §1.11, equation (8)]
. It is then clear that
\operatorname{Li}_{-1/2}(\zeta^{2})
cannot lie in
A^{2}(\Omega)
, which is the sought contradiction.
∎

We end this Section with two consequences of Proposition
4
. First, we formulate below the extension problem in the class
C^{\infty}(k^{2k})
, to be compared with Definition
25
.

Definition 22
.

Let

-\infty<a<b<\infty,\quad A>0,\quad 1\leq\rho<\infty,

and consider a segment
[a^{\prime},c^{\prime}]
that strictly contains the segment
[a,b]
. We say that the extension problem associated with
(a,b,a^{\prime},b^{\prime},A)
is solvable with loss
\rho
if: for every functions
\phi\in C^{\infty}[a,b]
such that

\exists C>0,\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\quad\forall t\in[a,b],\quad|\phi^{(n)}(t)|\leq CA^{n}n^{2n},

and for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
such that

\forall x\in[a,b],\quad\phi(x)=\varphi(x),

and

\exists C^{\prime}>0,\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\quad\forall t\in[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}],\quad|\varphi^{(n)}(t)|\leq C^{\prime}(\rho A+\epsilon)^{n}n^{2n},

Reasoning similarly as for the proof of Proposition
26
, it can be shown that the solvability of the extension problem does not depend on
(a,b,a^{\prime},b^{\prime},A)
, but only on the loss factor
\rho
. Moreover, the two following facts are almost trivial: on the one hand, if the extension problem can be solved with loss factor
\rho\geq 1
, then it can also be solved with any
\tilde{\rho}\geq\rho
. This allows to define
\rho^{Ext}
as the optimal (
i.e.
minimal) loss factor
\rho
such that the extension problem is solvable with loss
\rho
. On the other hand, if the interpolation problem (see Definition
25
) can be solved with loss
\rho
, then the extension problem can be solved with the same loss
\rho
. Denoting
\rho^{Carl}
the optimal loss factor for the interpolation problem, we thus have
\rho^{Ext}\leq\rho^{Carl}
. Proposition
4
shows that
\rho^{Ext}\geq 2
, and Mityagin’s Theorems bring
\rho^{Carl}=2
, hence
\rho^{Ext}=\rho^{Carl}
. In other words: there is no way to extend functions in Gevrey classes
C^{\infty}(k^{2k})
which yields a better loss factor than using the solution of the interpolation problem.

Secondly, we point out that Proposition
10
describes the range of the Borel operator, over some Hilbert spaces of Gevrey-2 functions on some interval
I
, when the point
t_{0}
at which the derivatives are taken is an interior point of
I
. Such description is harder when
t_{0}
is a boundary point of
I
, in fact we have the following.

Proposition 23
.

Consider
C^{\infty}([-1,1],k^{2k},A)
as the set of functions
\varphi\in C^{\infty}[-1,1]
such that

•

Theorem 24

Proof.

Let

\psi(s)\hat{\dot{y}}(s)=\hat{u}(s),\quad\psi(s):=\frac{\sinh{\sqrt{s}}}{\sqrt{s}\cosh(\sqrt{s}x_{0})}.

The function

This precisely means that

Conversely, let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{(0)}[0,\infty)

\operatorname{spec}T:=\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F}T

A.1
Generalities

Definition 25
.

Let

-\infty<a<b<\infty,\quad c\in[a,b],\quad A>0,\quad 1\leq\rho<\infty,\quad 1<s<\infty.

We say that the interpolation problem associated with
(a,b,c,A,s)
is solvable with loss
\rho
if, for every sequence of complex numbers
(a_{n})
such that

\exists C>0,\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\quad|a_{n}|\leq CA^{n}n^{ns},

and for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}[a,b]
such that

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\quad\varphi^{(n)}(c)=a_{n},

and

\exists C^{\prime}>0,\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\quad\forall t\in[a,b],\quad|\varphi^{(n)}(t)|\leq C^{\prime}(\rho A+\epsilon)^{n}n^{ns}.

The loss factor
\rho
is the quantity we aim to estimate. The above problem
a priori
depends on the parameters
(a,b,c,A,s)
. We claim that actually it does not depend on
a,b,c,A
when
s
is fixed, in the sense that changing
a,b,c
and
A
does not affect the loss factor
\rho
.

Proposition 26
.

Fix
s>1
and assume that the interpolation problem associated with
(a,b,c,A,s)
is solvable with loss
\rho
. Then, the interpolation problem associated with
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime},A^{\prime},s)
is solvable with the same loss factor
\rho
.

Proof.

Assume that the interpolation problem associated with
(a,b,c,A,s)
is solvable with loss factor
\rho
. We show that for every
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime},A^{\prime},s)
as above, the associated problem is solvable with loss
\rho
. Clearly, for any translation
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, the problem associated with
(a+\tau,b+\tau,c+\tau,A,s)
is solvable with the same loss factor. We may therefore assume that
a=a^{\prime}=0
. Let us then use a dilatation: if
\varphi\in C^{\infty}[0,b]
is such that

|\varphi^{(n)}(t)|\leq C^{\prime}(\rho A+\epsilon)^{n}n^{ns},

then for
\lambda>0
we consider
\varphi_{\lambda}(t):=\varphi(\lambda t)
, which is such that

\varphi_{\lambda}\in C^{\infty}[0,b/\lambda],\quad\varphi_{\lambda}^{(n)}(t)=\lambda^{n}\varphi^{(n)}(t),\quad|\varphi_{\lambda}^{(n)}(t)|\leq C^{\prime}(\rho A\lambda+\epsilon\lambda)^{n}n^{ns}.

Thus the problem of parameters
(0,b/\lambda,c/\lambda,A\lambda,s)
is solved with loss
\rho
. We can therefore assume that
b=b^{\prime}=1
.

We then claim that if the problem of parameters
(0,1,c,A,s)
is solvable with loss
\rho
for
0<c<1
, then for every
c^{\prime}\in(0,1)
the problem of parameters
(0,1,c^{\prime},A,s)
is solvable with loss
\rho
. For, let
\epsilon>0
and
(a_{n})
be any sequence such that

|a_{n}|\leq CA^{n}n^{ns}.

We invoke the solvability of the problem associated with
(0,1,c,A,s)
to take
\varphi\in C^{\infty}[0,1]
such that

\varphi^{(n)}(c)=a_{n},\quad|\varphi^{(n)}(t)|\leq C^{\prime}(\rho A+\epsilon)^{n}n^{ns}.

We then consider

\psi(t):=\varphi(t-c^{\prime}+c)\chi(t),

where
\chi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is Gevrey of order
r\in(1,s)
, is constant to 1 on a small neighborhood of
c^{\prime}
, and flat at
c^{\prime}-c
and
1+c^{\prime}-c
. Since
1<r<s
, the constructed function
\psi
has the same Gevrey regularity as
\varphi
in the sense that

|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq C^{\prime\prime}(\rho A+\epsilon)^{n}n^{ns},

(see
[
38
, Lemma 3.7]
). Moreover, since
\chi
is
0
outside of
(c^{\prime}-c,1+c^{\prime}-c)
,
\psi
is well-defined on
\mathbb{R}
(so it is also defined on
[0,1]
), and has the same derivatives at
c^{\prime}
than
\varphi
has at
c
. Thus the problem of parameters
(0,1,c^{\prime},A,s)
is solvable, with the same loss
\rho
.

Next we show that the cases
c=0
or
c=1
are equivalent. Assume for instance that the problem of parameters
(0,1,0,A,s)
is solvable with loss
\rho
. For any sequence
(a_{n})
such that

|a_{n}|\leq CA^{n}n^{ns},

we invoke its solution for the sequence
((-1)^{n}a_{n})
to obtain
\varphi\in C^{\infty}[0,1]
such that

\varphi^{(n)}(c)=(-1)^{n}a_{n},\quad|\varphi^{(n)}(t)|\leq C^{\prime}(\rho A+\epsilon)^{n}n^{ns}.

We then put
\psi(t):=\varphi(1-t)
, which has the same Gevrey regularity as
\varphi
and is such that

\psi^{(n)}(1)=(-1)^{n}\varphi^{(n)}(0)=a_{n}.

The converse implication is proved in the same way, so that
c=0
and
c=1
are equivalent.

Assume now that the problem of parameters
(0,1,0,A,s)
is solvable with loss
\rho
. We show that, for every
0<c<1
the problem of parameters
(0,1,c,A,s)
is solvable with loss
\rho
. In fact we will show that
(-1,1,0,A,s)
is solvable with loss
\rho
, which is easily seen as a stronger statement, by translation and dilatation arguments. We let
(a_{n})
be a sequence with

|a_{n}|\leq CA^{n}n^{ns}.

We introduce the ‘‘odd’’ and ‘‘even’’ sequences

a_{n}^{o}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}a_{2k+1},&n=2k+1,\\
0,&n=2k,\end{array}\right.,\quad a_{n}^{e}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}a_{2k},&n=2k,\\
0,&n=2k+1,\end{array}\right.

and take
\varphi,\psi\in C^{\infty}[0,1]
so that

|\varphi^{(n)}(t)|,|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq C^{\prime}(\rho A+\epsilon)^{n}n^{ns},\quad\varphi^{(n)}(0)=a_{n}^{o},\quad\psi^{(n)}(0)=a_{n}^{e}.

We then consider
\tilde{\varphi}
the odd extension of
\varphi
and
\tilde{\psi}
the even extension of
\psi
. Clearly, they are both
C^{\infty}
on
[-1,0]
and
[0,1]
, and on each of these two intervals they satisfy the Gevrey estimate

|\tilde{\psi}^{(n)}(t)|,|\tilde{\varphi}^{(n)}(t)|\leq C^{\prime}(\rho A+\epsilon)^{n}n^{ns}.

To check that
\tilde{\varphi}
and
\tilde{\psi}
satisfy the required Gevrey estimate on

, it is thus enough to check that their derivatives at

both match. We have

\tilde{\varphi}^{(n)}(0^{+})=\varphi^{(n)}(0)=a_{n}^{o},\quad\tilde{\varphi}^{(n)}(0^{-})=(-1)^{n+1}\varphi^{(n)}(0)=(-1)^{n+1}a_{n}^{o},

and these two values agree. Similarly,

\tilde{\psi}^{(n)}(0^{+})=\psi^{(n)}(0)=a_{n}^{e},\quad\tilde{\psi}^{(n)}(0^{-})=(-1)^{n}\psi^{(n)}(0)=(-1)^{n}a_{n}^{e},

so that
\phi:=\tilde{\varphi}+\tilde{\psi}
solves the problem.

We can thus put
c=c^{\prime}=0
, hence we are left to show that if the problem
(0,1,0,A,s)
is solvable with loss
\rho
, then, for every
A^{\prime}>0
, the problem
(0,1,0,A^{\prime},s)
is solvable with loss
\rho
. Assume that the problem associated with
(0,1,0,A,s)
is solvable with loss
\rho
, using the dilatation
\varphi_{\lambda}
we see that for every
\lambda>0
, the problem associated with
(0,1/\lambda,0,A\lambda,s)
is solvable with loss
\rho
. We distinguish two cases: if
\lambda\leq 1
, then the problem associated with
(0,1,0,\rho,A\lambda,s)
is trivially solvable. If on the contrary
\lambda>1
, using a cutoff that is Gevrey of order
1<r<s
, constant to
1
near

and flat at

, we see that the problem associated with
(0,1,0,\rho,A\lambda,s)
is also solvable with loss
\rho
, as in the case of the equivalence for the parameter
c
. Since
\lambda>0
is arbitrary, this finishes the proof.
∎

Therefore, the only relevant numbers are
\rho
and
s
.

We begin by showing that

Theorem 27

and

(a_{n})

for some constants

and

.

Lemma 28
.

Let
f
be a concentrated functional on

,

and

as above. We then have

For every

\Phi

\forall\delta>0,\quad\exists C_{\delta}>0,\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R},\quad|\Phi(\xi)|\leq C_{\delta}\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{e}\left(\frac{1+\delta}{B}|\xi|\right)^{1/\beta}\right).

(59)

Thus,
\Phi
is nearly in the weighted Lebesgue space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\omega^{-2}(\xi)d\xi)
. In fact, for fixed
0<\tilde{B}<B
, consider the weight

\tilde{\omega}(\xi):=\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{e\tilde{B}^{1/\beta}}|\xi|^{1/\beta}\right).

From (
59
) we deduce that
\Phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\tilde{\omega}^{-2}(\xi)d\xi)
, hence
\tilde{g}
defined by the relation (
56
) with
\Psi=\Phi
defines a concentrated functional on
L^{2}_{\beta\tilde{B}}
. Clearly, the functional
\tilde{g}
does not depend on
\tilde{B}
, hence we denote it by
g
. For
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{\beta\tilde{B}}
we compute

\displaystyle\langle g,\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi(\xi)\mathcal{F}[\phi h](\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle f(t),e^{i\xi t}\rangle\mathcal{F}[h\phi](\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle=\left\langle f(t),\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\xi t}\mathcal{F}[h\phi](\xi)d\xi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f,\phi h\rangle=\langle f,\phi\rangle.

This shows that
g=f
on
C^{\infty}_{\beta\tilde{B}}
. We deduce that
g
is a concentrated functional on
L^{2}_{\beta B}
, so that
\Phi
lies indeed in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\omega^{-2}(\xi)d\xi)
. The representation of
f
as a series of Diracs follows from Step 2, which ends the proof.
∎

We are now in position to show the optimality of the factor
\Gamma_{\beta}
.

Theorem 31
.

For every
\epsilon,B>0
and
\beta>1
, there exists a sequence
(a_{n})\in K(n^{n\beta}B^{n})
which can be interpolated by no
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{\beta,\Gamma_{\beta}B-\epsilon}
.

Proof.

Assume by contradiction that this is not true, then fix
\epsilon,B>0
and
\beta>1
so that

\operatorname{Range}\mathcal{B}_{\infty}^{\epsilon}\supset\operatorname{Range}i_{\infty}^{\epsilon},

where

\mathcal{B}_{\infty}^{\epsilon}:C^{\infty}_{\beta,\Gamma_{\beta}B-\epsilon}\rightarrow K(n^{n\beta}(\Gamma_{\beta}B-\epsilon)^{n}),\quad i_{\infty}^{\epsilon}:K(n^{n\beta}B^{n})\rightarrow K(n^{n\beta}(\Gamma_{\beta}B-\epsilon)^{n}).

Similarly to in the proof of Theorem
27
, we deduce that

\operatorname{Range}\mathcal{B}\supset\operatorname{Range}i,

(60)

where

\mathcal{B}:L^{2}_{\beta,\Gamma_{\beta}B-\epsilon/2}\rightarrow\ell^{2}_{\beta,\Gamma_{\beta}B},\quad i:\ell^{2}_{\beta,B}\rightarrow\ell^{2}_{\beta,\Gamma_{\beta}B}.

Denote
X=L^{2}_{\beta,\Gamma_{\beta}B-\epsilon/2}
, from (
60
) and the Douglas’s Lemma we have

\exists c>0,\quad\forall a\in\ell^{2}_{\beta,\Gamma_{\beta}B},\quad\|\mathcal{B}^{*}a\|_{X^{\prime}}\geq c\|i^{*}a\|_{\ell^{2}_{\beta B}}.

(61)

Fix
f
a concentrated functional on
L^{2}_{\beta B}
and consider the weight

\omega(\xi):=\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{e\tilde{B}^{1/\beta}}|\xi|^{1/\beta}\right),\quad\tilde{B}:=\Gamma_{\beta}B-\epsilon/2.

From Proposition
30
, the entire function
\Phi
constructed in Lemma
28
belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\omega^{-2}(\xi)d\xi)
, and it has order
\leq 1/\beta
on the complex plane. From
[
6
, Theorem 3.1]
, there exists a sequence
(P_{j})
of polynomials such that
P_{j}\rightarrow\Phi
for the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\omega^{-2}(\xi)d\xi)
-norm. For every
j
we put

P_{j}(s)=:\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_{n}^{j}s^{n},\quad\hat{P}_{j}:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_{n}^{j}(-i)^{n}\delta^{(n)},\quad a_{n}^{j}:=n^{2n\beta}(\Gamma_{\beta}B)^{2n}f_{n}^{j}(-i)^{n},

so that
\hat{P}_{j}=\mathcal{B}^{*}a^{j}
. We apply (
61
) to the sequence
a^{j}
, which is finitely supported, to obtain

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}n^{n\beta}B^{n}|f_{n}^{j}|\leq\sqrt{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|n^{n\beta}B^{n}f_{n}^{j}|^{2}}=\|i^{*}a^{j}\|_{\ell^{2}_{\beta B}}\leq\frac{1}{c}\|\mathcal{B}^{*}a^{j}\|_{X^{\prime}}\lesssim\|P_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\omega^{-2}(\xi)d\xi)}.

(62)

Because
(P_{j})
is a convergent sequence of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\omega^{-2}(\xi)d\xi)
, right-hand side above is bounded with respect to
j
. Because
P_{j}
converges to
\Phi
for the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\omega^{-2}(\xi)d\xi)
-norm we deduce that
\hat{P}_{j}
converges to
f
for the
X^{\prime}
-norm, and in particular we have

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\quad f_{n}^{j}\xrightarrow[j\rightarrow\infty]{}f_{n}.

Passing to the limit in (
62
) we deduce that
(f_{n})
satisfies

\exists c>0,\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\quad|f_{n}|\leq cB^{n}n^{-n\beta},

(63)

an improvement over (
49
) from Lemma
28
.

Now consider an arbitrary entire function

\Psi(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n},

of order
<1
on
\mathbb{C}
, satisfying

\exists C>0,\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R},\quad|\Psi(\xi)|\leq C\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{eA^{1/\beta}}|\xi|^{1/\beta}\right),

(64)

for some
A>\Gamma_{\beta}B-\epsilon/2
. From such
\Psi
we construct a concentrated functional
g
on
L^{2}_{\beta A}
as in Proposition
30
. From the previously established fact, the coefficients
(a_{n})
of
\Psi
satisfy the estimate (
63
). In particular, we have

\exists C>0,\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C},\quad|\Psi(z)|\leq C\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{eB^{1/\beta}}|z|^{1/\beta}\right).

Put now
A:=\Gamma_{\beta}B-\epsilon/4>\Gamma_{\beta}B-\epsilon/2
, so that we have established the following fact: any function
\Psi
that is entire and of order
<1
on
\mathbb{C}
, that has order
\leq 1/\beta
and type no greater than

\sigma:=\frac{\beta}{e(\Gamma_{\beta}B-\epsilon/4)^{1/\beta}},

on the real line, is in fact of order
\leq 1/\beta
and type no greater than

\sigma^{\prime}:=\frac{\beta}{eB^{1/\beta}},

on the whole complex plane. Observe that

\lambda:=\frac{\sigma^{\prime}}{\sigma}=\left(\Gamma_{\beta}-\frac{\epsilon}{4B}\right)^{1/\beta}<\Gamma_{\beta}^{1/\beta}.

By a scaling argument we find that: any function
\Psi
that is entire and of order
<1
on
\mathbb{C}
, and that has order
\leq 1/\beta
and type
\leq K
on the real line, is of order
\leq 1/\beta
and type
\leq\lambda K
on
\mathbb{C}
. To arrive to a contradiction we consider the Mittag-Leffler function

E_{\beta}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{\Gamma(\beta k+1)},

which is entire on
\mathbb{C}
. From Stirling’s formula it is clear that
E_{\beta}
has order
1/\beta
and type 1 on
\mathbb{C}
(see
[
4
, §2.2]
). Moreover, the function
E_{\beta}
has the asymptotics

|E_{\beta}(iy)|\sim\frac{1}{\beta}\exp\left(\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2\beta}\right)|y|^{1/\beta}\right),\quad\mathbb{R}\ni y\rightarrow\pm\infty,

see
[
24
, Proposition 3.6]
, hence it has type
\cos(\pi/2\beta)
on the imaginary axis. Taking
\Psi(s)=E_{\beta}(is)
and invoking the previously deduced fact we obtain:
E_{\beta}
has order
1/\beta
and type
\lambda\cos(\pi/2\beta)
on
\mathbb{C}
. Because

\lambda\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2\beta}\right)<\Gamma_{\beta}^{\beta}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2\beta}\right)=1,

we arrive to a contradiction, as
E_{\beta}
has type
1
on
\mathbb{C}
.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of the Lemma on the discrete version of the Laplace method

This section is devoted to the proof of Lemma
20
. The proof involves several computations so we divide it into several steps to ease the reading.

Step 0:
There is no loss of generality in assuming that
u(x_{0})=0
, and thus we aim to show that

\sum_{k=0}^{n}e^{-nu(k/n)}\sim\sqrt{\frac{2\pi n}{u^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})}},\quad n\rightarrow\infty.

Step 1:
For every
a>0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
, we claim that

\sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty}\exp\left[-n\frac{a}{2}\left(\frac{k}{n}-b\right)^{2}\right]=\sqrt{\frac{2n\pi}{a}}\left[1+O\left(e^{-\frac{2n\pi^{2}}{a}}\right)\right],

(65)

where the
O
term is uniform with respect to the parameters
n\geq 1
,
b\in\mathbb{R}
, and
a>\underline{a}
where
\underline{a}>0
is any given positive number. To show this, we follow the proof of
[
39
, Lemma 2]
. We write

\sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty}\exp\left[-n\frac{a}{2}\left(\frac{k}{n}-b\right)^{2}\right]=e^{-n\frac{ab^{2}}{2}}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{a}{2n}k^{2}}e^{abk}=e^{-n\frac{ab^{2}}{2}}\Theta\left(\frac{ab}{2i\pi},-\frac{a}{2in\pi}\right),

where
\Theta
is Jacobi’s theta function, defined by

\Theta(z,\tau)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{i\pi k^{2}\tau}e^{2i\pi kz},\quad z\in\mathbb{C},\quad\operatorname{Im}\tau>0.

The functional equation for
\Theta
gives

\Theta\left(z,-\frac{1}{\tau}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{i}}e^{i\pi\tau z^{2}}\Theta(z\tau,\tau),

see
[
44
, Theorem 7.1]
. Applying this formula with
\tau=2in\pi/a
and
z=ab/2i\pi
yields

e^{-n\frac{ab^{2}}{2}}\Theta\left(\frac{ab}{2i\pi},-\frac{a}{2in\pi}\right)=e^{-n\frac{ab^{2}}{2}}\Theta\left(z,-\frac{1}{\tau}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{2n\pi}{a}}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{2\pi^{2}n}{a}k^{2}}e^{2i\pi kbn}.

In the series of the right-hand side, the term
k=0
is dominant:

\left|\sum_{k\neq 0}e^{-\frac{2\pi^{2}n}{a}k^{2}}e^{2i\pi kbn}\right|\leq 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{2\pi^{2}n}{a}k^{2}}\leq Ce^{-\frac{2\pi^{2}n}{a}},

for some constant
C>0
independent of
n\geq 1
and
a>\underline{a}
. This shows the claim.

Step 2:
We fix
\delta>0
,
n\geq 1
and we put

m(\delta)=\inf_{|x-x_{0}|\geq\delta}u(x),

which is positive (
u
has the unique global minimum 0). We deduce that

\sum_{\underset{\left|\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right|\geq\delta}{k=0}}^{n}e^{-nu(k/n)}=O\left(ne^{-nm(\delta)}\right).

(66)

We then deduce, thanks to (
66
), that

\sum_{k=0}^{n}e^{-nu(k/n)}=\sum_{\underset{\left|\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right|<\delta}{k=0}}^{n}e^{-nu(k/n)}+\sum_{\underset{\left|\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right|\geq\delta}{k=0}}^{n}e^{-nu(k/n)}=\sum_{\underset{\left|\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right|<\delta}{k=0}}^{n}e^{-nu(k/n)}+O\left(ne^{-nm(\delta)}\right),

(67)

and estimate the sum appearing on the right-hand side. We estimate it from above, and we will estimate it from below in the next step. We put

\ell(\delta)=\inf_{|x-x_{0}|<\delta}u^{\prime\prime}(x),

which is well defined and positive for small enough
\delta
, what will be assumed henceforth, and which converges to
u^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})
as
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
. This allows

|x-x_{0}|<\delta\Longrightarrow u(x)\geq\frac{\ell(\delta)}{2}(x-x_{0})^{2},

hence, by (
65
),

\sum_{\underset{\left|\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right|<\delta}{k=0}}^{n}e^{-nu(k/n)}\leq\sum_{\underset{\left|\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right|<\delta}{k=0}}^{n}e^{-n\frac{\ell(\delta)}{2}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right)^{2}}\leq\sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-n\frac{\ell(\delta)}{2}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right)^{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{2n\pi}{\ell(\delta)}}\left[1+O\left(e^{-\frac{2n\pi^{2}}{\ell(\delta)}}\right)\right],

(68)

where the
O
term is uniform in
n\geq 1
and
\delta>0
small enough. Thus, for
\delta>0
be fixed and small enough, we have

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{\frac{u^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})}{2n\pi}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}e^{-nu(k/n)}\leq\sqrt{\frac{u^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})}{\ell(\delta)}}.

Letting
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
in the right-hand side, we find that

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{\frac{u^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})}{2n\pi}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}e^{-nu(k/n)}\leq 1.

(69)

Step 3:
The estimate from below is slightly more tedious. We put, for
\delta>0
small enough so that
[x_{0}-\delta,x_{0}+\delta]\subset(0,1)
,

L(\delta)=\sup_{|x-x_{0}|<\delta}u^{\prime\prime}(x)>0,

so that by (
65
),

\displaystyle\sum_{\underset{\left|\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right|<\delta}{k=0}}^{n}e^{-nu(k/n)}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\underset{\left|\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right|<\delta}{k=0}}^{n}e^{-n\frac{L(\delta)}{2}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right)^{2}}=\left\{\sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty}-\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}-\sum_{k=-\infty}^{-1}-\sum_{\underset{\left|\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right|\geq\delta}{k=0}}^{n}\right\}e^{-n\frac{L(\delta)}{2}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2n\pi}{L(\delta)}}\left[1+O\left(e^{-\frac{2n\pi^{2}}{L(\delta)}}\right)\right]-\left\{\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}+\sum_{k=-\infty}^{-1}\right\}e^{-n\frac{L(\delta)}{2}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(ne^{-n\frac{L(\delta)}{2}\delta^{2}}\right).{}

(70)

We are thus left to estimate the remaining two series on the right-hand side of (
70
). Until the end of the proof,
\kappa
is any positive number which depends on
\delta
and
x_{0}
, changing line to line, small enough so that the written asymptotics are valid. To bound above the first series we use a comparison between series and integrals:

\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}e^{-n\frac{L(\delta)}{2}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right)^{2}}\leq\int_{n}^{\infty}e^{-\kappa n\left(\frac{x}{n}-x_{0}\right)^{2}}dx=\sqrt{\frac{n}{\kappa}}\int_{(1-x_{0})\sqrt{n\kappa}}^{\infty}e^{-x^{2}}dx=O(e^{-\kappa n}).

For the other series we compute

\sum_{k=-\infty}^{-1}e^{-n\frac{L(\delta)}{2}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right)^{2}}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\kappa\left(\frac{k^{2}}{n}+2kx_{0}+x_{0}^{2}n\right)\right]\leq e^{-\kappa n}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-\kappa k}=O(e^{-\kappa n}).

Coming back to (
70
) we find

\sum_{\underset{\left|\frac{k}{n}-x_{0}\right|<\delta}{k=0}}^{n}e^{-nu(k/n)}\geq\sqrt{\frac{2n\pi}{L(\delta)}}\left[1+O(e^{-\kappa n})\right]+O(e^{-\kappa n})\geq\sqrt{\frac{2n\pi}{L(\delta)}}+O(e^{-\kappa n}),

and we conclude, similarly to the previous step, that

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{\frac{u^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})}{2n\pi}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}e^{-nu(k/n)}\geq 1,

which, together with (
69
), concludes the proof of the Lemma.
