Organophosphate ester exposure in pregnancy, gestational weight gain, and postpartum body composition in a U.S.-based longitudinal pregnancy cohort
Authors: Emily S. Barrett, Jonathan Klus, Anushka Pande, Tanzy Love, Sally W. Thurston, Jessica Brunner, Kurunthachalam Kannan, Richard K. Miller, Susan Groth, Thomas G. O’Connor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOrganophosphate esters (OPEs) are replacement flame retardants that have been implicated as metabolic disruptors and linked to birth size across a number of epidemiologic studies. Little is known about how OPEs impact maternal weight and body composition from pregnancy through the postpartum period.MethodsWe measured OPE metabolites in mid-pregnancy urine samples from participants in a pregnancy cohort study based in Rochester, NY, USA. We calculated total gestational weight gain (GWG) based on clinical records (n = 278) and additionally measured weight retention and body fat percentage through bioelectric impedance at 6 (n = 205) and 12 months postpartum (n = 167). We fitted adjusted linear and logistic regression models examining OPE concentrations in relation to the outcome measures and secondarily, fitted models stratified by earliest pregnancy BMI (< 25 kg/m2 versus ≥ 25 kg/m2).ResultsIn main models, most associations were null. Several highly prevalent OPEs such as bis(1,3-dichloro-2-propyl) phosphate (BDCPP; β: -1.02 lbs 95%CI: -2.00, -0.03) were associated with lower GWG, while detection of other, less prevalent OPEs like BMPP (bis(methylphenyl) phosphate; β: 2.86 lbs, 95%CI: -0.21, 5.94) was associated with greater GWG. In stratified analyses, associations tended to be stronger in women who started pregnancy overweight or obese, including findings that several OPEs were associated with higher fat percentage at 6 and 12 months postpartum. Few associations with postpartum weight retention were observed.DiscussionEvidence linking gestational OPE exposure with GWG and body composition in the postpartum was limited and mixed, with the strongest associations observed for BDCPP. In light of the growing literature on OPEs’ impacts on birth size and child outcomes, greater research into maternal metabolic disruption is warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12940-025-01251-x.

Introduction

Organophosphate esters (OPEs) are synthetic chemicals extensively used as flame retardants and plasticizers in a wide range of commercial and industrial products, including electronics, textiles, furniture, and building materials [1]. In the past two decades, production and use of OPEs has surged as substitutes for polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs), which have been widely phased out because of health and environmental concerns [2]. OPEs are derivatives of phosphoric acid with a central phosphorus atom bonded to oxygen and three esterified alkyl or aromatic groups, a structure that confers thermal stability and plasticity [1]. Despite their functional advantages, the increasing use of OPEs has raised concerns due to their potential human toxicity and environmental persistence [3, 4]. Meanwhile, global OPE consumption surpassed one million tons in 2018, a tenfold increase from the early 1990 s [1, 5]. Because OPEs are physically integrated into products rather than chemically bound, they can readily leach from those products, leading to human exposure through ingestion, inhalation, or dermal contact. Although OPEs are non-persistent with a half-life estimated to be between hours to days in humans [6, 7], given their widespread use in consumer products, exposure is likely to be frequent, if not continuous. As a result, biomonitoring studies indicate widespread human exposure to OPEs, with metabolites like bis(1,3-dichloro-2-propyl) phosphate (BDCIPP) and diphenyl phosphate (DPHP) detected in over 90% of the U.S. population [8].

OPEs are increasingly recognized as metabolic disruptors [9]. This may be of particular concern during pregnancy, a period characterized by substantial metabolic adaptation. The changes occurring during a typical pregnancy, including increased insulin resistance, altered lipid profiles, and gestational weight gain, are vital for supporting fetal growth and building adipose energy stores for lactation [10, 11]. Studies have suggested that pregnant individuals may have higher OPE concentrations than the general population [8, 12] and exposure to some metabolites like BDCPP, is virtually ubiquitous in pregnant people [13–15]. To date, research on gestational OPE exposure in reproductive age and pregnant populations has primarily focused on reproductive and developmental impacts including fertilization, implantation, fetal growth, and child outcomes [15–17]. Fewer studies have examined impacts on maternal health, including metabolic outcomes, during pregnancy and the postpartum. Among those studies considering metabolic outcomes, the majority have focused on OPEs and maternal glycemic status [18–23] or hypertensive disorders in pregnancy [24, 25], yielding mixed results. To our knowledge, to date, only one small study (n = 56 pregnant women in Rhode Island, U.S.) has examined OPEs and gestational weight gain (GWG), reporting that BDCPP, DPHP, and bis(2-chloroethyl) phosphate (BCEP) showed weak positive associations with GWG [26]. We know of no studies that have corroborated those initial findings, considered a wider array of OPEs, or extended that line of research to look at impacts on maternal weight retention and body composition in the postpartum.

Understanding environmental impacts is critical as excessive or inadequate GWG can contribute to adverse maternal-fetal outcomes, including aberrant fetal growth and cesarean delivery, while postpartum weight retention (PPWR) is an important determinant of maternal health sequelae such as metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular disease [27, 28]. Given evidence of widespread OPE exposure during pregnancy [13, 15, 16], potential OPE-mediated metabolic disruption, and adverse consequences of perinatal metabolic disruption on long-term maternal health, further investigation is needed. Thus, this study builds on and expands the emerging literature by exploring the relationship between prenatal OPE exposure and maternal GWG, PPWR, and body composition within a well-characterized longitudinal birth cohort, offering novel insights into the metabolic consequences of prenatal OPE exposure in the postpartum period.

Methods

Overview of cohort and study design

Pregnant people were recruited from outpatient obstetric clinics affiliated with the University of Rochester Medical Center (URMC) for participation in the Understanding Pregnancy Signals and Infant Development (UPSIDE) longitudinal pregnancy cohort between 2015 and 2019 [29]. Eligibility criteria for enrollment included: [1] gestational age below 14 weeks; [2] participant aged 18 or older; [3] singleton pregnancy; [4] no history of psychotic disorders; [5] no documented substance abuse; and [6] English communication proficiency. Participants with high-risk pregnancies at baseline or significant history of major endocrine disorders were not eligible to participate. Participants provided questionnaire data and biospecimens at clinic visits in each trimester of pregnancy. Following delivery, participants who delivered a term infant were invited to enroll their children for postnatal follow-up within UPSIDE, with the mothers being invited to concurrently enroll in the UPSIDE MOMS study [30]. UPSIDE MOMS is a longitudinal cohort assessing cardiometabolic risk factors during pregnancy and the postpartum period via study visits at 6 and 12 months, followed by annual follow-up thereafter until three years postpartum. UPSIDE participants who became pregnant again prior to UPSIDE MOMS enrollment were ineligible for enrollment in UPSIDE MOMS. If re-pregnancy occurred during their participation in UPSIDE MOMS, data collection was truncated at the time of re-pregnancy. UPSIDE and UPSIDE MOMS both received Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval from URMC and Rutgers University and written informed consent was obtained from all participants at the time of enrollment into UPSIDE as well as UPSIDE MOMS.

Assessment of OPE exposure

Urine samples were collected in each trimester after which specific gravity was measured and the aliquots were stored at −80 °C. As part of UPSIDE’s participation in the NIH’s Environmental influences on Child Health Outcomes (ECHO) study [31], maternal urine was analyzed for OPEs. Samples were shipped on dry ice to the Wadsworth Laboratory’s Child Health Exposure Analysis Resource (CHEAR) laboratory. For most participants a second trimester sample was analyzed, however 4% of participants had no second trimester urine available and instead contributed a third trimester sample. Samples were analyzed for nine OPE metabolites: bis(2-butoxyethyl) phosphate (BBOEP), bis(2-chloroethyl) phosphate (BCETP), bis(1-chloro-2-propyl) phosphate (BCPP), bis(1,3-dichloro-2-propyl) phosphate (BDCPP), bis(2-ethylhexyl) phosphate (BEHP), bis(methylphenyl) phosphate (BMPP), a composite of dibutyl phosphate/di-isobutyl phthalate (DBUP/DIBP), diphenyl phosphate (DPHP), and dipropyl phosphate (DPRP). As described previously [15, 32], urine samples were processed using solid phase extraction cartridges (STRATA-X-AW: 60 mg, 3 cc, Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) [33]. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, ExionLC™ system; SCIEX, Redwood City, CA, USA) coupled with an AB SCIEX QTRAP 5500 + triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) was used for analysis. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Kinetex HILIC column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.6 μm particle size; Phenomenex). OPE metabolites were identified and quantified using electrospray negative ionization and multiple reaction monitoring modes. The limit of detection (LOD) for individual OPE metabolites varied between 0.012 and 0.044 ng/mL. Metabolites detected in at least 70% of samples were considered continuously for analysis while those detected in less than 70% were dichotomized as above or below the LOD.

Assessment of outcomes

Gestational weight gain

Trained study staff abstracted maternal weight (and gestational age at weight measurement) from the electronic medical records of all UPSIDE participants for each prenatal visit during the index pregnancy. Following standard practice in pregnancy cohorts when clinically documented preconception weight is unavailable [34–36], participant weight measured at the earliest first trimester visit (mean 7.4 ± 1.9 weeks gestation) was utilized as a proxy for pre-pregnancy weight. Most participants had a documented weight during the final gestational week prior to delivery. We used data from these participants to impute weight gain during the final gestational weeks for 44 participants who lacked a weight measurement in the final week, but had a documented weight within 6 weeks of delivery; two participants were excluded due to pre-eclampsia or very erratic weights. The imputation was based on the regression of weight gain in the final weeks adjusting for average weight gain earlier in the third trimester and mother’s age. GWG was calculated as the difference between the first clinically recorded first trimester weight and the weight at delivery. As per Institute of Medicine (IOM) recommendations for singleton pregnancies, pre-pregnancy BMI was used to classify each participant’s total GWG as insufficient, appropriate, or excessive, thereby facilitating comparisons with established clinical guidelines [37].

Postpartum weight retention and adiposity

UPSIDE participants enrolled in the UPSIDE MOMS study, including four who only contributed postpartum data due to incomplete prenatal data, underwent weight and total body fat percentage assessments at 6- and 12- month postpartum visits using bioelectric impedance analysis (BIA) following the manufacturer’s guidelines (Tanita Body Composition Analyzer MC-780, Tokyo, Japan). BIA is regarded as a more precise method for evaluating cardiometabolic risk factors compared to anthropometric measurements [38, 39]. However, BIA is only utilized in the postpartum period as it may not be valid during gestation. To reduce error in weight measures related to variation in the exact timing of postpartum visits, we imputed weight retention at exactly 6 and 12 months postpartum for participants who had recorded weights both before and after the time point of interest (6 or 12 months postpartum) with at least one weight measurement within 26 weeks of that date. We additionally extrapolated weight at 6 or 12 months postpartum if the individual had a recorded weight before or after the time point of interest within 4 weeks of the time point of interest. To calculate postpartum weight retention, we then subtracted the earliest pregnancy weight from the imputed or extrapolated weight at 6 or 12 months postpartum. Because we had limited repeat measures data on body fat percentage, we could not impute values at exactly 6 and 12 months postpartum and instead adjusted for the exact timing of the visit in models.

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on the existing literature. In prenatal questionnaires, participants reported their age, highest level of educational attainment, and smoking during pregnancy (any/none). Participants additionally reported their race and ethnicity which were considered here as proxies for societal factors that may contribute to differential OPE exposure as well as overweight and obesity [40–42]. Given the distribution of participant demographics, race and ethnicity were categorized as non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and other races/ethnicities. In each trimester of pregnancy as well as 6 and 12 months postpartum, mothers completed the Pregnancy Physical Activity Questionnaire (PPAQ), a validated measure that allowed us to quantify total weekly metabolic equivalents (METs) [43]. In models examining GWG, we averaged second and third trimester METs, while in models examining postpartum outcomes, we used METs measured at the same time point (6 or 12 months). Participants also completed 24-hour dietary recalls in mid-late pregnancy and again at 6 months postpartum. Recalls were administered by a trained nutritionist using a standardized interview and from those recalls, total kilocalories at each time point were calculated. Maternal breastfeeding (any/none) was self-reported at 6 and 12 months. Gestational age at delivery and child sex were abstracted from electronic medical records.

Statistical analysis

We calculated descriptive statistics for all variables of interest. Three OPEs (BDCPP, DBUP/DIBP, DPHP) were detected in at least 70% of samples and were log-transformed for analysis due to their skewedness. The remaining six OPE metabolites were present in less than 70% of samples and were considered dichotomously (detect/non-detect). As part of preliminary analyses, we calculated Spearman correlations to examine relationships among the three continuous metabolites as well as measures of weight gain, weight retention, and total body fat percentage. Given the low correlations among the three OPEs considered continuously, no mixture analyses were undertaken (Supplemental Table 1).

Models examining gestational weight gain

We fitted multivariable, covariate-adjusted linear regression models examining OPE metabolite concentrations in relation to total GWG. Covariates included pre-pregnancy BMI, maternal age, education, smoking, parity, fetal sex, gestational age at delivery, and race/ethnicity. In extended models, we additionally considered mid-pregnancy energy intake (kcal) and physical activity as precision variables that somewhat reduced the sample size given their greater missingness. In sensitivity analyses, we refitted models excluding preterm births (n = 16) due to the potentially lower GWG resulting from a shorter gestation. Given some prior studies have suggested that environmental chemicals may have differential impacts on GWG based on pre-pregnancy BMI [44–46], secondarily, we stratified models according to underweight/normal BMI (< 25 kg/m2) versus overweight/obese BMI (≥ 25 kg/m2) based on earliest recorded first trimester weight. To evaluate potentially non-linear relationships, for the three OPEs considered continuously (BDCPP, DBUP/DIBP, DPHP), we refitted GWG models stratified by higher and lower exposure based on median splits. Finally, in light of ample evidence suggesting that prenatal exposure to OPEs may affect male and female pregnancies and fetuses differently (e.g., [32], [47], [48]), we explored sex differences in associations based on stratified models.

Models examining postpartum outcomes

To examine postpartum endpoints, we fitted a series of multivariable, covariate-adjusted linear regression models examining OPE metabolite concentrations in relation to total postpartum weight retention and body fat percentage at 6 and 12 months postpartum. Covariates were again selected a priori based on the literature and included maternal pre-pregnancy BMI, age, education, race/ethnicity, parity, and postpartum smoking along with fetal sex and gestational age at delivery. Models examining body fat percentage additionally adjusted for exact weeks postpartum at the study visit. Extended models again considered mid-pregnancy energy intake (kcal) at 6 months postpartum as well as physical activity, and breastfeeding status (both measured at 6 or 12 months postpartum depending on the outcome time point) as precision variables that reduced the sample size given their greater missingness. As in the prenatal analyses, secondarily, we fitted postpartum models: [1] stratified by underweight/normal BMI (< 25 kg/m2) versus overweight/obese BMI (≥ 25 kg/m2) based on earliest recorded first trimester weight; and [2] stratified by higher and lower exposure based on median splits for BDCPP, DBUP/DIBP, and DPHP.

Results

Of 326 participants who enrolled in UPSIDE, 294 ultimately gave birth to a participating child, of whom 278 women had data on GWG for the current analysis (Supplemental Fig. 1). Participants in the analytic sample for analyses of GWG were on average 29.1 ± 4.6 years old at enrollment with an earliest pregnancy BMI of 28.0 ± 7.1 kg/m2 (Table 1). The majority of participants identified as non-Hispanic White (58.3%), followed by non-Hispanic Black (21.9%), Hispanic (10.4%), and other races and ethnicities (9.4%). Nearly two-thirds of participants (62.9%) had greater than a high school education and few smoked during pregnancy (6.8%). In total, 220 women provided data for postpartum outcome analyses; all but four also contributed data to GWG analyses. Those who contributed postpartum data for the current analysis were similar to the prenatal sample in terms of age, BMI, and smoking status (Table 1). However, the sample contributing postpartum data tended to be more educated (68.2% greater than high school), and more likely to be non-Hispanic White (63.2%). The overall UPSIDE sample (including women who consented but did not provide GWG data for various reasons such as relocation, loss to follow up, or insufficient prenatal weight data) had a greater percentage of Hispanic participants (26.1%), but was otherwise similar to the analytic samples (Table 1).

On average, participants included in analyses of GWG gained a total of 26.6 ± 13.7 lbs across pregnancy with 22.7% gaining below IOM recommendations, 37.4% gaining within IOM recommendations, and 39.9% gaining above IOM recommendations (Table 1). Weight retention at 6 and 12 months postpartum was minimal at mean 1.3 ± 11.1 lbs and 0.3 ± 14.1 lbs, respectively. Total body fat percentage was also comparable at 6 (32.8 ± 8.3) and 12 months (32.0 ± 8.4) postpartum. GWG, weight retention, and fat percentage measures were similar across the two analytic samples and the overall cohort (Table 1). Consistent with IOM guidelines, average GWG was greater among women who started pregnancies with a normal or underweight BMI (31.6 ± 9.6 lbs) versus those who were overweight or obese (22.6 ± 15.2 lbs) (Supplemental Table 2). At the same time, women who started pregnancy with a normal or underweight BMI were most likely to gain according to IOM recommendations (50%), while among those who began pregnancy overweight or obese, only 27.3% gained appropriately (per IOM recommendations) and nearly half (48.1%) gained excessively. Measures of weight gain and retention tended to be moderately correlated, with the exception of strong correlations between postpartum weight retention at 6 and 12 months (r = 0.82) and body fat percentage at 6 and 12 months (r = 0.95) (Supplemental Table 3).

The percentage of samples with OPE metabolite levels below the LOD varied by metabolite from 74.1% below LOD for DPRP to all samples above LOD for DPHP (Table 2). Based on these distributions, BDCPP, DBUP/DIBP, and DPHP were considered as continuous variables with median concentrations of 0.957, 0.165, and 0.952 ng/ml, respectively (in the GWG analytic sample). All other metabolites had at least 30% of samples below the LOD and were thus considered as detect vs. non-detect for analysis. Correlations between the three OPEs considered continuously were low to moderate ranging from 0.15 to 0.37 (Supplemental Table 1). The distribution of OPE metabolite concentrations was nearly identical in the participants included in the GWG analyses and the subset included in the postpartum analyses (Table 2). Median concentrations of most OPE metabolites were similar in participants with earliest pregnancy BMI < 25 kg/m2 versus ≥ 25 kg/m2, though several metabolites were slightly higher in the heavier group (e.g., BDCPP: <25 kg/m2 median: 0.72 ng/ml; ≥25 kg/m2 median: 1.15 ng/ml) (Supplemental Table 4).

On the logarithmic scale, BDCPP, DBUP/DIBP, and DPHP were all weakly, inversely correlated with total GWG (Supplemental Table 5). This was recapitulated in our main multivariable models in which we observed few notable associations, however the most highly detected metabolites tended to be inversely associated with GWG conditional on covariates (Table 3). For example, a log-unit increase in BDCPP was associated with 1.02 lb (95%CI: −2.00, −0.03) lower total GWG. Associations with GWG were similar in direction and magnitude for DBUP/DIBP (β=−1.06 lbs, 95%CI: −3.21, 1.09) and DPHP (β=−1.38 lbs, 95%CI: −3.14, 0.38), though confidence intervals were wider and included the null. By contrast, for the less detected metabolites that were considered as detect/non-detect, associations with GWG tended to be positive, but null. The strongest associations observed were for BMPP, whereby those with concentrations above the LOD had 2.86 lb (95%CI: −0.21, 5.94) greater GWG compared to those below the LOD. BMI was the strongest predictor of GWG in these models, with obese participants gaining on average 13.07 lbs less (95%CI: −16.78, −9.36) than participants of normal weight (not shown).

In extended models that also considered gestational daily caloric intake and physical activity, results were similar for some metabolites (such as BMPP) while for others, results were somewhat attenuated (e.g., BDCPP) or strengthened (DPRP) (Table 3). When we excluded preterm births (n = 16), associations with GWG were slightly weaker for both BDCPP (β= −0.94 lbs, 95%CI: −1.95, 0.06) and BMPP (β = 2.78 lbs, 95%CI: −0.36, 5.92) and all 95% CIs included the null (Supplemental Table 6).

When we considered odds of inadequate or excessive GWG per IOM guidelines, a log unit increase in BDCPP was associated with 32% increased odds of inadequate weight gain (OR: 1.32, 95%CI: 1.03, 1.74), with slightly stronger associations in the extended model (Table 4). No other metabolite was associated with inadequate or excessive weight gain with the exception of a trend towards association between detectable BMPP and increased odds of excess GWG (OR: 1.62, 95%CI: 0.93, 2.83).

Finally, in models stratified by fetal sex, BDCPP was associated with significantly lower GWG in women carrying males (β= −1.68 lbs, 95%CI: −3.26, −0.09) while BMPP was associated with greater GWG (β = 6.22, 95%CI 1.70, 10.73) in that group (Supplemental Table 9). Associations in women carrying female fetuses tended to be weaker, with confidence intervals including the null (e.g., BDCPP: β=−0.43 lbs, 95%CI: −1.73, 0.88).

In our primary models examining prenatal OPE concentrations and postpartum measures (weight retention and body fat percentage), few associations were seen at 6 or 12 months postpartum (Table 3). An inverse trend between DBUP/DIBP and postpartum weight retention at 6 months was observed in both the main (β= −1.67 lbs, 95%CI: −4.08, 0.21) and extended models (β= −2.76 lbs, 95%CI: −5.74, 0.21), with similar results observed at 12 months postpartum (main model: β= −2.31 lbs, 95%CI: −5.98, 1.36). A similar inverse but non-significant trend was observed for DPRP, whereby women with detectable levels had 1.77 lb (95%CI: −5.55, 2.02) and 5.04 lb (95%CI:−10.72, 0.13) lower weight retention at 6 and 12 months post-partum, respectively. In the full sample, no associations of OPE metabolites with total body fat percentage at either time point were observed (Table 3). BMI was a very strong predictor of body fat percentage in these models, with obese and overweight participants having an average of 15.5% (95%CI: 13.2, 17.8) and 6.0% (95%CI: 3.9, 8.1) greater fat percentage, respectively, at 6 months, and 16.7% (95%CI: 14.4, 18.9) and 7.0% (95%CI: 4.8, 9.2) greater fat percentage at 12 months compared to those of normal weight (not shown). Education was also a strong predictor of fat percentage; for example participants with a postgraduate degree had on average a fat percentage at 12 months that was 4.0% less (95%CI: −7.1, −1.0) than those with a high school degree (not shown).

As in analyses examining GWG, some associations between OPE metabolites and postpartum measures differed in relation to maternal early pregnancy BMI (Table 5). With the exception of an inverse association between DBUP/DIBP in the lower BMI group (β= −2.43 lbs, 95%CI: −4.97, −0.10) at 6 months postpartum, no other notable associations between OPEs and postpartum weight retention were reported in either BMI sub-group at 6 postpartum. Results for weight retention at 12 months postpartum were similarly null with the exception of a strong association between detectable DPRP levels and lower weight retention (β=−9.82 lbs, 95%CI: −19.34,−0.29) in the group with higher baseline BMI, however 95% CIs were quite wide.

Several associations with postpartum fat percentage were noted with respect to early pregnancy BMI group. For example, in the obese/overweight group, detectable gestational BCETP (β = 3.42, 95%CI: 0.31, 6.53) and BEHP (β = 3.06, 95%CI: −0.35, 6.46) were associated with higher fat percentage at 6 months postpartum, associations that were not observed in the lower BMI group (Table 5). The association between BEHP and fat percentage among women with higher early pregnancy BMI was marginally stronger at 12 months postpartum (β = 3.38, 95%CI: −0.25, 7.01), moreover, with an additional positive trend observed for DBUP/DIBP (β = 2.66, 95%CI: −0.14, 5.46). At the same time, in the lower BMI group (but not the higher BMI group), fat percentage at 6 months postpartum showed a weakly positive association with BDCPP (β = 0.80, 95%CI: −0.13, 1.73) and a weakly inverse association with DBUP/DIBP (β= −1.77, 95%CI: −3.61, 0.07), with attenuation of both associations at 12 months postpartum (Table 5). In analyses stratified by exposure level (median split), few associations were seen with postpartum endpoints with the exception of a trend between higher BDCPP and greater fat percentage at 12 months postpartum among women with higher exposure (Supplemental Table 8). In women with below median exposure, all continuous OPE metabolites were associated with lower weight retention and fat percentage at 6 months postpartum, but confidence intervals all included the null and all associations were attenuated or even weakly positive by 12 months postpartum.

Discussion

In this analysis of prenatal OPE exposure in a U.S. pregnancy cohort recruited from 2015 to 2019, we expanded upon the existing literature suggesting that OPEs may act as metabolic disruptors in the perinatal period. Overall, with several notable exceptions, we observed few associations between prenatal OPE exposure and GWG, postpartum weight retention, or postpartum body fat percentage through 12 months postpartum. Few consistent patterns of association were seen and most 95% CIs included the null value. Nevertheless, given the paucity of literature on OPEs and maternal weight and body composition in the perinatal period, several observations warrant further consideration. First, several of the most prevalent OPE metabolites (including BDCPP and to a lesser extent, DBUP/DIBP and DPHP) were associated with lower total GWG across pregnancy, and a log-unit increase in BDCPP was associated with 32% increased odds of insufficient GWG. The observed inverse associations with GWG tended to be stronger in women who started pregnancy overweight or obese and also among women with below median exposure to BDCPP, DBUP/DIBP, and DPHP. Results further suggested that DBUP/DIBP and DPRP may be associated with reduced weight retention, though few other associations with postpartum measures were observed in the full cohort. In analyses stratified by early pregnancy BMI, among women who began pregnancy overweight or obese, gestational BCETP, BEHP, and DBUP/DIBP exposure were associated with higher fat percentage in the postpartum, with fewer, weaker associations observed in women who began pregnancy at a lower BMI.

Among the most widely studied OPE metabolites in pregnancy are BDCPP and DPHP and in the current sample, they were present at the highest median concentrations (0.96 and 0.95 ng/ml, respectively) and had the highest prevalence of detection (91.8%, and 100%, respectively). In the largest U.S study of OPE exposure in pregnancy to date (n = 6646), 87% and 99.5% of ECHO consortium participants had detectable urinary BDCPP (median: 0.86 ng/ml) and DPHP (median: 0.92 ng/ml), with inter-site variation evident, which may be due to geographical and/or temporal differences [15]. Smaller, contemporaneous single site U.S. studies (e.g. MADRES) have tended to report OPE levels roughly similar to those observed here, while older cohorts such as CHAMACOS (which recruited from 1999 to 2000) and EARTH (2005–2015) tend to report lower levels of many OPE metabolites, likely reflecting increasing OPE production and use over time [49–52].

To our knowledge, only one prior epidemiological study has examined associations between OPEs and GWG. That study, of 56 mothers in Rhode Island (RI), US, observed weak positive associations between GWG and all three OPEs measured (BCEP, BDCPP, DPHP) in urine samples pooled from all trimesters [26]. These results contrast, in part, with our main models in which we observed that BDCPP was associated with slightly lower GWG, with stronger inverse associations observed in women who began pregnancy with overweight or obesity for both BDCPP and DPHP. While the mean baseline BMI was virtually identical across the two studies (RI: 27.9 ± 7.1 kg/m2; UPSIDE: 28.0 ± 7.1 kg/m2), GWG appears to have differed considerably. Although no data on average GWG were provided in the RI study, 68% of participants gained below IOM guidelines, while only one person gained in excess of IOM guidelines versus 23% and 40% of participants who gained below and in excess of IOM guidelines respectively in our study. Notably, in the RI study, GWG was calculated based on the last recorded weight in pregnancy which may underestimate total weight gain, whereas we imputed final weights at delivery for any participant who did not have a clinically recorded weight in the last week of gestation. In a descriptive study from North Carolina (NC), US, among 349 pregnant women, those who were overweight or obese at the onset of pregnancy had higher BDCPP and DPHP than women with normal weight [53], a pattern we similarly observed in our cohort. However, over two-thirds of the NC cohort (69%) were normal or underweight prior to pregnancy as compared to only 45% in our cohort in early pregnancy. Median concentrations of both BDCPP (1.9 ng/ml) and DPHP (1.3 ng/ml) were also higher in the NC study than in our cohort. Unfortunately, no associations with GWG or postpartum weight measures were reported in that study.

BDCPP, which showed the most consistent associations with GWG outcomes in the current analysis, is the main metabolite of the high production flame retardant tris(1,3-dichloro-2-propyl) phosphate (TDCPP), which is commonly found in polyurethane foams used in consumer goods such as furniture, building materials, cars, camping equipment, and baby products [54–59] as well as foodstuffs [60]. TDCPP mixes with polymers in those products but does not chemically bond, which can result in leaching [61]. The Consumer Product Safety Commission’s 2006 report determined that TDCPP was a likely carcinogen [62] and a recent review summarized a range of endocrine disrupting impacts including on thyroid and reproductive hormone pathways, though the precise mechanisms remain unknown [63]. Of note, TDCPP is more slowly depleted and has a longer estimated half-life than many other OPEs (estimated 53.8 days), which may confer greater endocrine and metabolism-disrupting potential [64, 65].

Studies of OPEs and body size/composition in non-pregnant adults are scarce, although in an analysis of NHANES participants (2015–2018), in non-pregnant women, urinary BDCPP was associated with greater subcutaneous, but not visceral, fat as measured by DEXA scans [66]. In our study, while few associations between OPE exposure and postpartum measures were observed in the total cohort, notably, BDCPP was non-significantly positively associated with weight retention and total body fat percentage measures at both 6 and 12 months postpartum among women who started pregnancy at a normal BMI, whereas in the participants with overweight/obesity at baseline, all associations were inverse. In a cross-sectional study of children and adults participating in the 2013–2014 cycle of NHANES, results were mixed whereby some OPE metabolites like DBUP were associated with lower prevalence odds of obesity and lower BMI z-scores while others like BCPP were associated with increased prevalence odds of obesity and higher BMI [67]. Despite the different samples, our study shows some similarities to those results. Here, DBUP/DIBP was inversely associated with nearly all weight gain and retention measures. BCPP, furthermore, was associated with higher weight gain and retention measures, including a significantly positive association with GWG among women who began pregnancy overweight or obese.

It is well established that GWG is positively associated with infant birth size [68–70]. To that end, if maternal OPE exposures alter GWG, they may also change patterns of fetal growth, impacting birth size. In an analysis over 6600 ECHO mother-child dyads, there was some evidence of larger birthweight for gestational age (and lower risk of term low birth weight) among infants born to mothers with detectable levels of BCPP, BMPP, and DPRP [15]. Consistent with that, our results suggested that BMPP may be associated with greater GWG as well as increased odds of excess GWG, and among women who started pregnancy with a BMI of 25 kg/m2 or higher, we similarly observed positive associations between BCPP and GWG. Of note, in contrast to our study, few associations between maternal BDCPP and infant size measures were reported in the ECHO analysis, though there was a trend towards lower odds of small for gestational age among male infants [15]. Other smaller studies of maternal OPEs and infant size measures (both U.S. based and global) have reported positive [71, 72], negative [73, 74], mixed [26, 75] and null associations [16, 18].

In addition to our main models, we conducted several stratified analyses and while the sample sizes were small, several results warrant further consideration and replication in larger cohorts. First, results of analyses stratified by early pregnancy BMI (< 25 kg/m2 vs. ≥ 25 kg/m2) suggested stronger associations with GWG among women with higher BMI including inverse associations with BDCPP and DPHP as well as a positive association with BCPP. Several metabolites also showed positive associations with fat percentage at 6 months (BCETP and BEHP) and 12 months (DBUP/DIBP and BEHP) in the higher, but not the lower, BMI group. It is possible that the higher BMI group had greater opportunity for OPE exposure through larger surface area for dermal absorption or greater food intake [76, 77], a possibility further supported by the higher OPE metabolite concentrations observed in the higher BMI group compared to the lower BMI group. To assess the possibility that differing associations by associations by early pregnancy BMI, were actually driven by differences in level of exposure, we additionally evaluated associations in higher and lower exposed women based on median splits. Somewhat unexpectedly, in the less exposed women, BDCPP, DBUP/DIBP, and DPHP were more strongly associated with lower GWG as well as lower postpartum weight retention and fat percentage at 6 months (but not 12 months), whereas in the more highly exposed group, associations were less consistent. This may be evidence of non-monotonic effects, which have been documented for other endocrine disrupting chemicals [78]. It is also possible that there was stronger residual confounding by other unmeasured environmental contaminants or lifestyle factors in the high exposure group that obscured our ability to detect any associations. Teasing out non-dose response relationship and the role of BMI in moderating associations will be an important next step in future studies on this topic. Finally, given the large body of evidence that endocrine disruptors may impact male and female pregnancies differently (e.g., 32, 47, 48), for analyses focused on GWG, we fitted models stratified by fetal sex. These analyses suggested that some results seen in the full cohort were driven by mothers carrying male fetuses, particularly the inverse associations observed for BDCPP as well as the positive associations observed for BMPP. With 35% of maternal GWG is estimated to derive from products of conception (fetus, placenta, amniotic fluid) on average [79], these findings may reflect a greater sensitivity of male fetal growth to OPE exposures as has been suggested in some prior studies (though associations vary by metabolite) [15, 32, 75, 80]. The mechanisms underlying sex differences in sensitivity to OPEs (and endocrine disruptors more generally), remain unknown although there is evidence that exposure may affect thyroid and sex steroid hormone concentrations differently depending on fetal sex [81, 82]. Prior work has also identified signatures of greater male sensitivity to OPEs in the placental transcriptome including 12 weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) modules altered with exposure to OPEs in male pregnancies (versus 1 WGCNA module in female pregnancies) [47].

Mechanistic research implicating OPEs as an environmental obesogen can be useful to further make sense of these epidemiologic studies of OPE exposure, prenatal biomarkers, and body size measures [83]. One possibility is that OPEs may disrupt nuclear receptor signaling, thereby exerting downstream effects on hormone activity. In the aforementioned placental transcriptomics study, notably, eleven of the affected modules were enriched for nuclear hormone receptor transcription factors [47]. In experimental work, cell lines exposed to high dose tributyl phosphate (TBUP) and tris (2-butoxyethyl) phosphate (TBOEP), demonstrated higher PPARγ ligand binding potential [84]. In a similar in vitro study of Firemaster 550 (FM550), the flame retardant mixture bound human PPARγ mainly through triphenyl phosphate (TPP)’s interactions with the binding domain [85]. In that study, furthermore, FM550 exposure led to greater adipogenesis and lipid accumulation. OPEs including TPP and TCP may also interact with estrogen receptor α (ERα) with potential implications for energy homeostasis in the adipose, liver, brain, or pancreatic tissue [85, 86]. In a mouse model, disruption of ERα-mediated activity in these tissues through OPE exposure disrupted feeding efficiency, fat accumulation, insulin tolerance, and ghrelin levels, though weight was unaffected [87, 88]. To the extent that OPEs disrupt metabolic and endocrine pathways, exposures during pregnancy may be of particular concern in light of the dramatic and tightly orchestrated metabolic, immune, and endocrine changes that must occur to support the developing fetus. At the same time, it is also possible that effects of OPEs on metabolism and body composition (if any) may be less easy to detect during this transitional period given the radical physiological shifts that occur as part of a typical gestation.

Our study features several notable strengths. First, we longitudinally followed mothers from early pregnancy through 12 months postpartum, which allowed us to characterize (for the first time) weight measures across this dynamic period in relation to OPE exposure. We leveraged extensive weight data from across pregnancy and the postpartum, imputing, interpolating, and extrapolating missing data points as needed to derive precise measures of total weight gain across pregnancy and postpartum weight retention at specific time points. In the postpartum, we evaluated weight retention as well as total body fat percentage, a better indicator of body composition. In extended models, we additionally adjusted for caloric intake, physical activity, and breastfeeding status which are strong predictors of weight and body composition, but are often omitted from similar work.

At the same time, there are several limitations of note. We measured OPE metabolites at a single time point in pregnancy and given that OPEs typically have a short half-life, there may be exposure misclassification. Additionally, we only assessed prenatal exposure, but there may also be critical periods of postpartum exposure that were not evaluated here. In our cohort, only three OPEs were detectable in most participants and the remaining six were considered as binary variables which increases risks of exposure misclassification. The low prevalence of most OPEs and the low correlations among the three considered continuously also precluded mixtures analyses, which may be a better approximation of real world exposures. We used BIA to assess body fat percentage, rather than DEXA, which is the more time- and cost-intensive gold standard, however prior studies have shown concordance between the two measures [89]. There was also considerable attrition in the postpartum, primarily due to re-pregnancy, which may have limited power to detect associations and also raises concerns about selection bias for analyses considering postpartum outcomes. This concern, however, is somewhat ameliorated by the similarity of the overall cohort to the subset who contributed postpartum data. Finally, we fitted numerous models raising the possibility that some results are due to chance. Given the novelty of this topic, we elected to focus on patterns of association rather than adjusting for multiple comparisons in light of the potential for type II error [90].

Conclusions

In summary, in this analysis of data from a U.S. pregnancy cohort, we observed few associations between gestational OPE exposure, GWG, and weight retention and body composition in the postpartum, however several trends that were consistent with some prior work warrant additional follow-up. In particular, one of the most prevalent OPEs, BDCPP, was associated with lower GWG and greater odds of inadequate GWG. Better understanding of factors influencing maternal weight and body composition during the perinatal period is important not only because of the implications for child health, but for the potentially long-term impacts on women’s cardiometabolic health. As OPE production and use continues to proliferate in the modern environment, elucidating their metabolism-disrupting impacts, if any, is essential.
