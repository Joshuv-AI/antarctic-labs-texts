Optimization of Er: YAG pulse duration and repetition rate for efficient, dentin‑preserving caries removal
Authors: Fangyuan Kong, Fan He, Yaying Zhang, Hao Li, Chenguang Niu, Pengfei Zhang, Yuanfei Fu, Zhengwei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose. To evaluate the effects of pulse duration and repetition rate on Er: YAG laser ablation efficiency and sound dentin preservation in artificial and natural carious models. Methods. Fifty-six artificially decalcified dentin specimens were allocated to six Er: YAG laser groups (L1–L6; pulse durations 50–300 µs, repetition rates 20–40 Hz, energies 60–70 mJ) and one rotary bur control (n = 8 each). Twenty-four natural carious molars were assigned to medium-short pulse (MSP: 100 µs, 40 Hz) versus bur or super-short pulse (SSP: 50 µs, 40 Hz) versus bur comparisons (n = 12 pairs each). Sound dentin loss (mm²) and treatment duration (s/mm²) were quantified using standardized radiography or three-dimensional surface scanning, and ablation threshold changes (ΔE = E₁ − E₂) were assessed in natural carious model. Results. In the artificial model, L1 (70 mJ, 300 µs, 20 Hz) yielded the lowest sound dentin loss (median 0.040 mm², IQR 0.037–0.043), significantly less than bur (p < 0.001) and short-pulse groups L4 and L6 (p < 0.05 to p < 0.0001). L4 (70 mJ, 50 µs, 40 Hz) achieved the shortest laser treatment duration (mean 190.64 s/mm², SD 36.90; p < 0.05 vs. L1–L3). In natural caries, both MSP and SSP protocols preserved sound dentin comparably to bur (p > 0.05) but required 3.3- and 2.6-fold longer treatment times, respectively (p < 0.0001). Overall, 70% of specimens exhibited ΔE reductions of 5–10 mJ (maximum 20 mJ). Conclusion. Within a conservative energy range, short-pulse, high-repetition-rate Er: YAG settings enhance caries removal efficiency and lower ablation thresholds while preserving sound dentin similarly to rotary burs, supporting their application in minimally invasive deep-caries management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10103-026-04865-w.Keywords: Er:YAG laser, Pulse duration, Repetition rate, Caries removal, Ablation efficiency

Introduction

In the management of deep carious lesions, the primary therapeutic goal is to remove infected dentin while preserving pulp vitality and minimizing procedural irritation [1, 2]. Conventional rotary instruments provide rapid tissue removal but lack fine control, often causing unnecessary loss of sound dentin, intraoperative pain, and frequent need for local anesthesia [3–5]. Erbium: yttrium–aluminum–garnet (Er: YAG) lasers (λ = 2.94 μm) are strongly absorbed by water and hydroxyapatite, enabling thermomechanical ablation that preferentially targets demineralized tissues and is associated with reduced discomfort and lower anesthesia requirements [6–9]. However, randomized clinical trials and systematic reviews consistently indicate that Er: YAG caries removal requires substantially longer procedure times—typically two- to several-fold longer—than conventional burs, which remains a major barrier to routine clinical adoption [10–12].

Optimizing Er: YAG irradiation parameters is therefore essential to improve excavation efficiency while preserving sound dentin. Pulse energy, repetition rate, and pulse duration each influence the ablation rate and the extent of thermal alterations in adjacent dentin [13, 14]. Increasing energy or average power accelerates ablation but enlarges collagen denaturation zones and raises the risk of microcracking, indicating that efficiency gains cannot be pursued indefinitely without compromising substrate integrity [15, 16]. In contrast, shorter pulse durations are associated with higher peak power density, more favorable photomechanical removal mechanisms, and more confined thermal diffusion, suggesting potential to enhance efficiency and lower the ablation threshold within a biologically acceptable energy range [17, 18]. Nevertheless, most available data derive from standardized or superficial lesion models with single-parameter variations, and there is limited evidence on how combined adjustments of pulse duration and repetition rate, within a conservative energy window, affect both ablation efficiency and dentin preservation in deep carious lesions [19, 20].

To address these gaps, this in vitro study employed two complementary models—artificially demineralized dentin and natural carious lesions—to evaluate the combined effects of pulse duration and repetition rate on Er: YAG-assisted caries removal. Sound dentin loss and treatment duration were quantified to characterize the trade-off between tissue preservation and efficiency, and changes in ablation threshold were additionally assessed in natural caries after temporal parameter optimization. The null hypothesis was that, within a 60–70 mJ energy range, neither pulse duration nor repetition rate would significantly affect ablation efficiency or sound dentin loss compared with conventional bur excavation.

Materials and methods

Study design

This in vitro study, approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (SH9H-2022-TK503-1). Freshly extracted human mandibular molars was used to evaluate the effects of Er: YAG laser parameters on caries removal. Two complementary models—artificially decalcified dentin and natural carious lesions—were investigated through specimen preparation, caries removal, and post-treatment analysis (Fig. 1).

Schematic overview of the study design for artificially decalcified and natural carious models

Sample size and group allocation

Fifty-six artificially decalcified specimens were randomly allocated (block randomization, block size 7) to six Er: YAG laser groups [L1: 70 mJ/300 µs/20 Hz; L2: 70 mJ/300 µs/40 Hz; L3: 70 mJ/100 µs/40 Hz; L4: 70 mJ/50 µs/40 Hz; L5: 60 mJ/100 µs/40 Hz; L6: 60 mJ/50 µs/40 Hz] and one bur control (n = 8 per group). Twenty-four natural carious molars with moderate-to-deep lesions were assigned to medium-short pulse (MSP) laser versus bur or super-short pulse (SSP) laser versus bur comparisons (n = 12 pairs per comparison). Sample size calculations and randomization details are provided in Supplementary Methods S2.

Specimen inclusion and preparation

Artificially decalcified specimens

Sound mandibular molars extracted for orthodontic reasons were screened to exclude caries, cracks, or defects. Teeth were sectioned at the cemento-enamel junction (Mecatome T210; PRESI, France) to obtain dentin slabs (10 × 8 × 5 mm), which were embedded in poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), polished, and tested for surface microhardness. Only slabs with 60–75 Knoop hardness number (KHN), indicative of sound dentin [21], were included and stored in 0.9% saline at 4 °C. Caries-like lesions were created by 24-h immersion in demineralizing solution (Decalcifier I, Leica Biosystems, USA). This duration, determined by gradient demineralization testing (0–48 h), produced a uniform ~ 0.5-mm-deep low-density layer that was clearly distinguishable from sound dentin on intraoral radiographs (Fig. 2a). The 24-h lesions showed ablation thresholds of 70–90 mJ, as confirmed by Er: YAG laser tests using a LightWalker AT system (Fotona, Slovenia) with an H02 handpiece at a 7-mm focal distance maintained by a custom spacer, a 0.9-mm spot diameter, and 25 mL/min water irrigation (Fig. 2b), matching those of natural caries [22]. Pre- and post-decalcification radiographs (Planmeca ProX, Finland; 70 kV, 8 mA, 0.07 s) verified lesion formation. Gradient validation procedures, equipment specifications, and imaging protocols are detailed in Supplementary Methods S3–S4.

Natural caries specimens

Mandibular molars with non-restorable occlusal caries were collected and stored in 0.9% saline at 4 °C for ≤ 3 days. Lesion sites were standardized to 5 mm diameter using a trephine bur, and unsupported enamel was removed. Baseline ablation thresholds (E₁) were determined using the Er: YAG laser (300 µs, 20 Hz, 50–100 mJ in 5 mJ increments) as the minimum energy for visible, continuous ablation. Inclusion criteria were: (1) occlusal caries ≥ 5 mm in diameter; (2) moderate-to-deep dentin involvement; (3) E₁ within 70–90 mJ. Crown segments were embedded in PMMA, baseline 3D scans (F8, 3Shape, Denmark) were obtained, and specimens were re-immersed in saline to maintain hydration. Specimen collection protocols and equipment specifications are provided in Supplementary Methods S3.

Dentin removal procedure

In the artificial model, Group L1 (70 mJ, 300 µs, 20 Hz) served as a clinical baseline [23, 24], while Groups L2–L6 investigated optimized combinations of shorter pulse durations, higher frequencies, and reduced energies. All laser treatments were performed in a sweeping motion under microscope guidance with continuous water spray (25 mL/min), 7 mm focal distance, and perpendicular handpiece positioning. Bur excavation used a low-speed handpiece with a #3 spherical carbide bur (0.9 mm diameter; Mani, Japan; 40,000 rpm) under light, controlled pressure with water cooling. Ablation proceeded until hard dentin exhibited resistance to probing, confirming complete removal of softened tissue.

In the natural carious model, paired specimens were allocated to MSP–bur or SSP–bur comparisons (n = 12 pairs per comparison). A second ablation threshold (E₂) was determined under optimized conditions (100 µs/40 Hz for MSP; 50 µs/40 Hz for SSP), and ΔE (E₁ − E₂) was calculated to quantify the threshold reduction. Laser ablation was then performed at E₂ with corresponding pulse settings, using the same non-contact setup and hydration protocol as in the artificial model. Bur-treated specimens were prepared using the same rotary procedure as described above.

The treatment endpoint was defined by two criteria: (1) complete disappearance of discolored carious tissue (visual criterion) and (2) resistance to probing with a dental explorer (tactile criterion).

All procedures were performed by a single experienced operator to minimize inter-operator variability. Standardized radiographs of artificial specimens were obtained using a custom positioning jig ensuring identical source-to-specimen distance (9 cm), beam angulation, and specimen orientation. Treatment duration was recorded and normalized to lesion area (s/mm²).

Image analysis and quantificationan

Quantitative image analysis was conducted in a blinded manner by two independent trained evaluators (intraclass correlation coefficient: 0.92) using ImageJ (version 2.14.0/1.54f; NIH, USA). For artificially decalcified specimens, intraoral radiographs were analyzed using histogram-based gray-value segmentation, which was validated against micro-CT projection images (Supplementary Fig. S1–S2). For natural carious specimens, pre- and post-treatment three-dimensional surface scans were aligned using three-point registration, and tissue boundaries were identified on true-color cross-sections at 10× magnification [25]. Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (San Diego, CA) with a significance level of α = 0.05. Non-normally distributed dentin loss data from the artificial model were analyzed using Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc correction, whereas normally distributed treatment duration data underwent one-way ANOVA with Tukey-Kramer post hoc tests. Data from natural carious specimens were analyzed using paired t-tests. Detailed statistical procedures are described in Supplementary Methods.

Results

Sound dentin loss assessment

In artificially decalcified specimens (n = 8 per group), Group L1 (70 mJ, 300 µs, 20 Hz) showed the least sound dentin loss, with a median of 0.040 mm² (IQR 0.037–0.043 mm²), which was significantly lower than both the bur group (median 0.330 mm², IQR 0.247–0.568 mm²; p < 0.001) and laser Groups L4 (median 0.295 mm², IQR 0.263–0.408 mm²; p < 0.0001) and L6 (median 0.215 mm², IQR 0.180–0.240 mm²; p < 0.05). No significant differences in sound dentin loss were observed among the remaining laser groups (p > 0.05) (Fig. 3c; Supplementary Table S1, Part A). In natural carious specimens (n = 12 pairs per group), no significant differences in sound dentin loss were detected between laser-treated and bur-treated samples (MSP–bur: mean difference − 0.017 mm², SD 0.103 mm², p > 0.05; SSP–bur: mean difference − 0.104 mm², SD 0.188 mm², p > 0.05) (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Table S3). Treatment efficiency assessment

Treatment efficiency, expressed as duration per unit lesion area (s/mm²), was significantly higher (i.e., procedures were faster) for bur excavation than for all laser groups in the artificial model (p < 0.001). The bur group showed a mean treatment duration of 52.40 s/mm² (SD 10.33 s/mm²), whereas the shortest laser duration was observed for L4 (70 mJ, 50 µs, 40 Hz) at 190.64 s/mm² (SD 36.90 s/mm²), which was significantly lower than L1 (293.91 s/mm², SD 42.88 s/mm²), L2 (311.70 s/mm², SD 9.67 s/mm²), and L3 (289.94 s/mm², SD 98.32 s/mm²) (p < 0.05), with no significant differences from L5 (262.90 s/mm², SD 54.47 s/mm²) or L6 (246.85 s/mm², SD 56.74 s/mm²) (p > 0.05) (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Table S1, Part B).

In natural carious specimens, both laser protocols required substantially longer treatment times than the corresponding bur controls (p < 0.0001). The MSP group required approximately 3.3-fold longer treatment than bur (mean difference 59.79 s/mm², SD 32.29 s/mm²; range 14.18–119.69 s/mm²), whereas the SSP group required approximately 2.6-fold longer treatment than bur (mean difference 49.31 s/mm², SD 27.24 s/mm²; range 14.41–93.22 s/mm²) (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Table S3; Supplementary Table S4).

Changes in ablation threshold for natural caries

Ablation threshold changes were quantified using ΔE (ΔE = E₁ − E₂) to assess the impact of temporal parameter optimization in natural carious specimens (n = 24). Across all specimens, 70% exhibited ΔE values of 5–10 mJ, with a maximum reduction of 20 mJ and a minimum of 0 mJ, indicating that no specimen required a higher threshold energy after optimization (Fig. 5). On average, optimized short-pulse, higher-frequency Er: YAG settings reduced the ablation threshold by approximately 8–10% relative to the initial baseline range of 70–90 mJ, allowing continuous carious dentin removal at lower pulse energies while maintaining stable ablation behavior.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that, within the predefined 60–70 mJ energy window, shortening pulse duration combined with increasing repetition rate substantially improved Er: YAG-assisted caries removal efficiency. At the same time, these optimized temporal settings maintained sound dentin loss at levels comparable to rotary bur excavation in both artificial and natural carious models.​In the natural carious model, optimized short-pulse, mid- to high-frequency settings additionally reduced the ablation threshold by 5–20 mJ in most specimens, enabling effective carious dentin removal at lower pulse energies. These findings indicate that temporal parameter optimization can partially offset the intrinsic efficiency disadvantage of Er: YAG lasers without compromising ablation selectivity, leading to rejection of the null hypothesis for both ablation efficiency and sound dentin loss.​.

The pulse energy range was deliberately confined to 60–70 mJ, which is considerably lower than values commonly reported in clinical Er: YAG caries excavation protocols (often 150–250 mJ) [26, 27]. This conservative choice was guided by safety considerations for deep lesions close to the pulp. In vitro data suggest that Er: YAG energies ≤ 100 mJ do not adversely affect the viability of adjacent human dental pulp cells and may even promote odontoblastic differentiation markers, whereas energies ≥ 200 mJ lead to marked reductions in cell viability [28]. Other studies have reported that, among energies tested on sound dentin (40–120 mJ in SSP modes), 60 mJ yields the thinnest thermally affected collagen layer, whereas both lower and higher energies are associated with thicker damage zones [15, 29]. Together, these findings indicate an energy-dependent safety window for avoiding excessive heat accumulation in deep lesions. The 60–70 mJ range therefore represents a pragmatic compromise for deep-caries excavation. It provides sufficient ablation efficiency to limit treatment time while keeping pulse energy within this biologically acceptable window.

To implement the temporal parameter strategy within a conservative energy framework, we combined an artificially demineralized dentin model and a natural carious model as complementary representations of deep, hydrated lesions. Gradient demineralization experiments were used to select a 24‑h protocol that yielded an ablation threshold of 70–90 mJ, closely matching the baseline threshold of natural carious dentin and thus supporting comparable effective water content and laser–tissue interaction behavior between substrates [22, 30]. On this basis, each model was paired with the quantitative method best suited to its optical characteristics: radiographic gray‑value segmentation for the standardized, color‑similar artificial lesions, and three‑dimensional true‑color surface scanning for irregular, discolored natural cavities. Micro‑CT–based validation confirmed that the radiographic protocol provided reliable demineralized–sound boundaries under the standardized geometry employed (Supplementary Fig. S1–S2), supporting the robustness of this combined dual‑model, dual‑modality approach for evaluating Er: YAG parameter optimization in deep-caries scenarios [31, 32].

Within this low-energy, dual-model framework, pulse duration emerged as a more critical determinant of Er: YAG excavation efficiency than repetition rate. Increasing repetition rate from 20 to 40 Hz at a long pulse duration (300 µs) produced only modest reductions in treatment time, whereas combining shorter pulses with higher frequency (50 µs/40 Hz) substantially accelerated excavation without increasing sound dentin loss in either model. Consistent behavior in natural carious specimens indicates that short-pulse, mid- to high-repetition-rate configurations constitute a key temporal pattern for improving Er: YAG performance in deep lesions. These findings are partly in line with previous single-parameter studies, where SSP-like pulses (~ 50 µs) yielded the highest ablation rates with favorable surface morphology [33] and higher repetition rates mainly affected mass loss and roughness at elevated energies [13]. In a multi-parameter, low-energy, deep-caries context, our data therefore suggest that repetition rate optimization alone offers limited benefit when pulse duration remains long, and that clinically meaningful efficiency gains require frequency increases to be combined with sufficiently short pulses.

In the natural carious model, temporal parameter optimization also measurably lowered the ablation threshold: for about 70% of specimens, the minimum energy required to initiate stable carious dentin removal decreased by 5–10 mJ, with reductions up to 20 mJ in some cases. This indicates that short-pulse, higher-frequency Er: YAG settings enable continuous and controllable caries removal at lower pulse energies, which is particularly relevant for deep lesions where limiting pulpal thermal load is critical. These observations are consistent with experimental data showing that shorter Er: YAG pulses reduce the fluence needed to trigger ablation and with laser–tissue interaction models in which shorter pulses concentrate energy deposition in time and space, thereby lowering the volumetric energy threshold for phase-change-driven ablation [34]. Within the microsecond regime investigated here (50–300 µs, 0.9-mm spot, 60–70 mJ), the observed threshold reductions support the concept that appropriately shortening pulse duration and increasing repetition rate can shift Er: YAG operation towards an efficient near-threshold window. This near-threshold operation improves the balance between excavation speed and thermal safety in deep-caries management.

Despite temporal optimization, Er: YAG irradiation in the natural carious model still required substantially longer excavation times than rotary instruments, with MSP and SSP settings taking approximately 3.3-fold and 2.6-fold longer, respectively. These ratios are consistent with previous clinical trials and meta-analyses reporting time factors of roughly 2–3 for Er: YAG versus burs [35, 36]. Within the conservative 60–70 mJ energy range adopted here, these findings suggest that optimizing pulse duration and repetition rate alone is unlikely to eliminate the time disadvantage, particularly when Er: YAG is applied throughout the entire excavation with a strict hard-dentin endpoint. Although Er: YAG excavation remains approximately 2–3 times slower than burs, multiple clinical trials have reported reduced pain and markedly lower anesthesia requirements, suggesting a pragmatic role for Er: YAG as a targeted adjunct in deep lesions near the pulp, while rotary instruments are retained for rapid removal in peripheral areas [37].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, both models adopted an excavation-to-hard-dentin endpoint—targeting complete removal of softened or discolored tissue—rather than a selective caries-removal strategy that preserves potentially remineralizable affected dentin [38]. Although this uniform endpoint facilitated cross-model and cross-group comparability, it limited evaluation of the laser’s capacity to selectively preserve the caries-affected layer and may have underestimated its advantage over rotary burs in tissue selectivity. Second, in the artificially demineralized model, two-dimensional intraoral radiography was used for tissue quantification; despite validation against micro-CT showing good agreement under the standardized lesion geometry used, its spatial resolution is limited and cannot capture local variations in demineralization depth or subsurface mineral gradients. Future studies should incorporate more refined biological endpoints—such as microhardness profiling, dye penetration, and fluorescence-based lesion discrimination [39, 40]—together with three-dimensional imaging modalities including optical coherence tomography [41, 42] or micro-CT [43]to improve volumetric accuracy and assess selective caries-removal capacity more directly. Finally, the present investigation was restricted to in vitro conditions and a conservative low-energy window; clinical trials are warranted to determine whether the short-pulse, higher-frequency parameter strategy identified here translates into meaningful reductions in chair time, improved pulpal outcomes, and enhanced patient comfort in the management of deep carious lesions.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated the combined effects of pulse duration and repetition rate on Er: YAG laser performance using both artificial and natural carious models. Short-pulse duration combined with mid- to high-frequency settings significantly enhanced ablation efficiency and lowered the ablation threshold without increasing sound dentin loss compared with rotary bur excavation. Although these optimized temporal parameters did not eliminate the time disadvantage of Er: YAG irradiation, they support a clinically relevant role for Er: YAG as a minimally invasive adjunct in deep-caries management, particularly near the pulp where thermal safety and patient comfort are paramount.
