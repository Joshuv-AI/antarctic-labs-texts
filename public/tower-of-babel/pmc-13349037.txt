Phasic modulation of attentional rhythmic sampling according to task demands
Authors: Qing Kong, Tong Wang, Jianrong Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAttention is thought to periodically allocate cognitive resources to task-relevant stimuli, enabling the sequential sampling of multiple stimuli over time. However, whether the specific task influences the temporal dynamics of attentional sampling remains not fully understood.ResultsThis study investigates the dynamics of attentional sampling and how these dynamics are influenced by task demands, using high-frequency steady-state visual evoked potentials (SSVEPs). Participants were instructed to either select one of two visual stimuli or integrate both stimuli into a single target, with the stimuli tagged at 42 Hz and 44 Hz, respectively. Analysis of the SSVEP envelopes revealed that attention periodically samples stimuli within the theta band (4–6 Hz) in both tasks. Notably, the phase relationship between the SSVEP envelopes differed across tasks: distinct phases were observed in the selection task, whereas similar phases were found in the integration task. This task-dependent phase modulation was mediated by theta-band neural oscillations in the brain. Furthermore, the phase difference between SSVEP envelopes correlated with behavioral performance in the integration task but not in the selection task.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate a flexible, task-dependent mechanism for rhythmic attentional sampling, wherein neural oscillations align stimulus processing according to the specific demands of the task.

Background

Attention allocates limited cognitive resources to task-relevant stimuli to enhance task performance [1, 2]. While attention was once thought to be continuously focused on a selected target [3], recent behavioral and neuroscience research has found that attention is more like a “blinking spotlight” that periodically samples multiple stimuli [4]. Behavioral dense sampling and neurophysiological research have found that attention samples at a rate of about 8 Hz when faced with a single target. In contrast, when faced with two targets, the sampling rate changes to about 4 Hz, suggesting that attentional sampling dwells on each target for a relatively fixed amount of time [5–9]. However, other studies have found that attentional sampling is flexible and adaptive. When the number of targets is increased to four, the sampling rate is accelerated to ensure a fixed oscillation period and thus maintain perceptual continuity [10]. Periodic attentional sampling also accelerates in complex visual search tasks [11]. However, tasks in the real world are complex, and in some cases a single target is selected from multiple candidate stimuli, whereas in others attention must integrate multiple stimuli into a single target for detection. Whether the sampling dynamics of attention are similar in these cases remains unclear.

Neural oscillations in the brain play a crucial role in the dynamic sampling of attention. The frequency of neural oscillations, especially the individual alpha frequency, represents the rate of attentional sampling [12, 13]. When the frequency is high, the period of attentional sampling is short; when the frequency is low, it is long. The phase of neural oscillations, particularly in the theta and alpha bands, is thought to play an important role in target selection, distractor suppression, attentional shifts, and saccades [14–16]. Multiple stimuli can be processed in different phases of an oscillatory cycle, with task-relevant stimuli being assigned to excitatory phases and task-irrelevant stimuli to inhibitory phases [4, 17]. The excitatory phases are used for attentional engagement, while the inhibitory phases promote attentional shift and saccade [4, 18]. The effects of tasks on attentional sampling are also reflected in neural oscillations. In the temporal domain, the task demand for temporal integration of stimuli reduces the peak frequency of alpha oscillations, whereas temporal segmentation of stimuli increases it. Meanwhile, changes in the peak frequency of alpha oscillations are thought to be associated with variations in the temporal resolution of attentional sampling [13]. In the spatial domain, however, it has not been investigated whether single-stimulus selection and multi-stimulus integration lead to different attentional sampling dynamics and associated neural oscillations.

When investigating the attentional sampling of multiple stimuli, a technical difficulty arises in isolating and comparing the neural responses to each stimulus from the overall neural responses elicited by their simultaneous presentation. The steady-state visual evoked potential (SSVEP) provides an excellent solution to this problem by directly measuring the neural response synchronized with the flicker frequency of the stimulus, thereby isolating the response to different stimuli [19, 20]. At the same time, SSVEP power is sensitive to attention, and when attention is directed to a particular stimulus, the corresponding SSVEP power is elevated [21, 22]. Previous SSVEP studies have mostly used low frequencies, which interfere with the prevalent low-frequency oscillations in the brain, such as theta, alpha, and beta oscillations. Therefore, it has been difficult to investigate the interaction between the changes in SSVEP and the endogenous low-frequency oscillations in the brain. In the present study, we used high-frequency SSVEP [23] to tag the presented stimuli to investigate the attentional sampling of multiple stimuli, the task’s effects on the sampling dynamics, and its neural oscillatory mechanism.

In the present study, we asked participants to select one of the stimuli presented on either side of the fixation point as the target or to integrate the two stimuli as a single target, and we tagged the stimuli with high-frequency flicker. The results showed that the SSVEP envelopes for both stimuli exhibited theta-band fluctuations in both the single-stimulus selection task and the two-stimulus integration task. Critically, the two SSVEP envelopes showed a significant phase difference during the selection task, indicating different temporal sampling for the two stimuli, but were synchronized during the integration task. Theta-band neural oscillations mediated these sampling dynamics via phase modulation. Finally, we observed a significant correlation between the observed phase differences and behavioral performance in the integration task.

Results

High-frequency tagging of visual stimuli in different task demands

In this experiment, participants performed two tasks in two blocks, either selecting a single stimulus or integrating two stimuli as a target (Fig. 1A). At the beginning of the experiment, participants were presented with two 2*2 squares in the left and right visual fields. After 500 ms, the luminance of one of the squares began to vary with a sine wave, while the luminance of the other square also began to vary with another sine wave 200 ms later (Fig. 1B). This asynchronous presentation served as a resetting cue to direct attention to the stimulus whose luminance varied first at the onset of the cue [24]. In the cue-left condition, the luminance of the square to the left of fixation varied first, whereas in the cue-right condition, the luminance of the square to the right of fixation varied first. The sine wave in the left visual field was 42 Hz, while the sine wave in the right visual field was 44 Hz. After a random interval of 1400–1700 ms, two arrows were presented with each in a random square of the left and right visual fields. In the selection task, participants judged whether the vertical arrow was up or down, whereas, in the integration task, participants judged whether the integrated direction of the two arrows was primary or secondary diagonal (Fig. 1C). In both the selection and integration tasks, we obtained event-related potentials (ERPs, Fig. 1D and E) after averaging the cue-left and cue-right trials, respectively. The results showed that the ERPs contained significant high-frequency oscillations, indicating that our high-frequency stimulation elicited significant high-frequency responses.

We performed FFT analysis on ERPs to further validate SSVEP to high-frequency stimuli in the present study. The power analysis showed prominent 42 Hz and 44 Hz responses in both the selection and integration tasks (Fig. 2A and B). Furthermore, analysis of SSVEPs across all electrodes showed that stimuli presented in the left and right visual fields elicited significant contralateral responses, with peaks at PO3 and PO4. Therefore, we selected these two electrodes for the statistical test of SSVEP power and the subsequent phase analysis. We computed SSVEP power after cue presentation and compared it statistically with the baseline (i.e., 200 ms before the cue). In the selection task, SSVEP power was significantly higher than baseline for both 44 Hz and 42 Hz (all p < 0.04, all Cohen’s d > 0.50; Fig. 2C). Rhythmic sampling of visual stimuli in the theta band

We investigated the dynamic allocation of attention between the two stimuli by analyzing the temporal dynamics of the SSVEP amplitude. First, we extracted the envelope of the SSVEP amplitude from 500 to 1700 ms after cue presentation in the occipital electrodes contralateral to the stimulus, i.e., 44 Hz SSVEP in the PO3 electrode and 42 Hz SSVEP in the PO4 electrode, and performed an FFT analysis on the envelope. To remove the effect of aperiodic fluctuations on the analysis of periodic oscillations, we used the fooof algorithm [25] to remove the aperiodic components from the spectrum. The results showed that the envelope of PO3 fluctuated periodically at 6.2 Hz, while the envelope of PO4 fluctuated periodically at 4.4 Hz in both the selection task (Fig. 3A) and the integration task (Fig. 3B). Notably, the 4.4 Hz envelope oscillation corresponding to the left stimulus (42 Hz) appeared in the right occipital area, whereas the 6.2 Hz envelope oscillation corresponding to the right stimulus (44 Hz) appeared in the left occipital area, in both the selection task (Fig. 3C) and the integration task (Fig. 3D), consistent with the topographic distribution of the SSVEP power. Previous studies of attentional oscillations based on behavioral dense sampling have found a fixed phase difference in the periodic sampling of two spatial locations [9, 26]. In the present study, we similarly analyzed the relationship between attentional sampling of the cued and uncued stimuli by analyzing the phase relationship between the two SSVEP envelope oscillations. In analyzing the phase relationship, we computed the circular mean of the phase values across all frequencies between the two peak frequencies [9]. In the selection task (Fig. 4A), when the left side (42 Hz) was cued, the phase of the 42 Hz SSVEP envelope was significantly clustered around 62.10° (z = 5.50, p < 0.01), whereas the phase of the 44 Hz SSVEP envelope was significantly clustered around − 79.91° (z = 11.89, p < 0.001). The phase difference between the SSVEP envelopes of the cued and uncued stimuli clustered around 139.15°, approaching statistically significant (z = 2.27, p = 0.10). In contrast, when the right side (44 Hz) was cued, the phase of the 44 Hz SSVEP envelope was significantly clustered around 34.78° (z = 8.52, p < 0.001), whereas the phase of the 42 Hz SSVEP envelope was significantly clustered around − 93.72° (z = 3.25, p = 0.04). The phase difference between the SSVEP envelopes of the cued and uncued stimuli was significantly clustered around 141.21° (z = 3.23, p = 0.04). Notably, the phase differences for both the cue-left and cue-right conditions were significantly different from 0° (i.e., in both conditions, 0° was outside the 95% confidence interval of the phase difference) [27, 28]. These results suggest that the resetting cue produced a temporal difference in the attentional periodic sampling of the two stimuli. They validate the results of previous behavioral oscillation studies [24] and confirm that the cue manipulation was effective in the present experiment.Fig. 4Phase difference of the SSVEP envelope between the cued and uncued stimuli. In the integration task (Fig. 4B), when the left side (42 Hz) was cued, the phase of the 42 Hz SSVEP envelope was significantly clustered around 17.58° (z = 4.42, p = 0.01), while the phase of the 44 Hz SSVEP envelope was significantly clustered around − 5.90° (z = 7.80, p < 0.001). The phase difference between the cued and uncued SSVEP envelopes was clustered around − 6.11° (z = 3.80, p = 0.02). In contrast, when the right side (44 Hz) was cued, the phase of the 44 Hz SSVEP envelope was significantly clustered around − 1.91° (z = 16.55, p < 0.001), while the phase of the 42 Hz SSVEP envelope was significantly clustered around 0.73° (z = 5.69, p = 0.002). Notably, the phase differences for both the cue-left and cue-right conditions were not significantly different from 0° (i.e., in both conditions, 0° was within the 95% confidence interval of the phase difference) [27, 28]. By combining the cue-left and cue-right conditions, we compared the phase differences between the selection and integration tasks. A significant difference in phase differences between the selection and integration tasks was found (F(2,19) = 5.82, p = 0.01). This suggests that the task influences attentional sampling dynamics through temporal modulation. In the integration task, attention sampled the two stimuli almost synchronously, whereas in the selection task, it sampled them at different times.

Rhythmic sampling of visual attention is mediated by the phase of neural oscillation

Since the task modulates the phase of the periodic sampling of attention, this suggests an endogenous mechanism that controls the sampling dynamics of attention. In this section, we explore this endogenous mechanism by examining the coupling between SSVEP amplitude and the phase of raw EEG in the brain [15, 16]. The coupling between the SSVEP amplitude and the phase of the EEG is equivalent to the coherence between the phase of the SSVEP envelope and the phase of the EEG. We calculated the coherence between the phase of the EEG at all electrodes and the phase of the SSVEP envelope oscillations at PO3 and PO4. As shown in Fig. 5, coupling primarily occurred on electrodes in the right frontal and parieto-occipital regions during the selection task, whereas it occurred on electrodes in the middle and right parietal regions during the integration task. This result suggests that a control mechanism influences the periodic sampling of attention by phase modulation of neural oscillations. Behavioral correlates of phase modulation

We investigated the behavioral correlates of the oscillatory phase difference between SSVEP envelopes by examining correlations between the raw phase difference and participants’ response time and response accuracy in the selection and integration tasks, respectively. To further investigate the behavioral correlates of synchronization between the two SSVEP envelopes, we calculated the correlation between the absolute phase differences in the SSVEP envelopes and both response time and response accuracy.

Overall, the selection task in this study demonstrated a lower level of difficulty compared to the integration task. Specifically, the selection task yielded significantly higher response accuracy than the integration task (t(20) = 5.86, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.28), whereas its response time was significantly shorter (t(20) = 13.97, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 3.05). In the selection task, neither the raw phase difference nor the absolute phase difference was significantly correlated with response time or accuracy (ps > 0.32; Fig. 6A and B). In the integration task (Fig. 6C and D), response time did not show a significant correlation with the raw phase difference (r = − 0.29, p = 0.32) but displayed a significant and positive correlation with the absolute phase difference (r = 0.59, p = 0.04). Neither the raw phase difference nor the absolute phase difference was significantly correlated with accuracy (ps > 0.32). This result suggests that, in the selection task, the magnitude of the phase difference had little effect on participants’ selection of one of the two targets. Discussion

This study investigated the dynamics of attentional sampling when participants selected one of two stimuli as a target or integrated them into a single target. By employing high-frequency SSVEP and analyzing the temporal dynamics of the SSVEP envelopes, we found that attention periodically sampled both stimuli in the theta band (around 4–6 Hz). Critically, the task modulated the phase relationship between these periodic dynamics. During the selection task, attention sampled the two stimuli at distinct phases, suggesting a focus on each stimulus at different times. Conversely, during the integration task, attention sampled the stimuli in similar phases, suggesting a more synchronized processing of both stimuli. The sampling profiles were mediated by neural oscillations through phase-amplitude coupling. The phase difference between the sampling rhythms of the two stimuli influenced the behavioral performance in the integration task. These findings support a task-dependent temporal modulation of attentional periodic sampling that is mediated by the phase of neural oscillations in the brain.

This study used high-frequency tagging to investigate the dynamics of attentional sampling of stimuli and showed a coupling between the phase of low-frequency EEG signals and the amplitude of high-frequency SSVEP. Previous studies have shown that the early visual cortex can generate SSVEPs to flickering stimuli up to 100 Hz [29] and that gamma-band tagging is independent of endogenous alpha-band neural oscillations [23]. In addition, the SSVEP was weaker at higher frequencies, even for stimuli of the same intensity [23]. Combining these factors, stimulus flicker in the low gamma band (42 Hz and 44 Hz) was chosen for tagging in the present study. This allowed both a relatively high signal-to-noise ratio for SSVEP and the study of the interaction between SSVEP envelope changes and endogenous low-frequency neural oscillations. Spatial attention affects the amplitude of the SSVEP, and in particular, attentional engagement increases SSVEP amplitude in the gamma band [22, 23], allowing fluctuations in SSVEP amplitude in the present study to reflect dynamic changes in attentional allocation. The results of the present study provide a feasible solution for tagging perceptual and attentional processing and demonstrate that high-frequency tagging can be used to study the interaction between stimulus responses and endogenous low-frequency neural oscillations.

The coupling analysis between SSVEP amplitude and EEG phase revealed background neural oscillations as potential modulators of attentional sampling dynamics according to task demands. The theta phase in frontoparietal areas has previously been suggested to coordinate two rhythmically alternating attentional states that are linked to attentional engagement, attentional shifts, and saccade generation [15, 16]. Our results further demonstrate that these brain areas may mediate the phase of theta-band oscillations according to task demands, resulting in temporally distinct sampling of stimuli during the selection task versus synchronized sampling during the integration task. The results of the current study are also consistent with the neuronal oscillatory multiplexing theory proposed by McLelland and VanRullen [30]. According to this theory, the neuronal population representing a given stimulus fires together within a single gamma cycle, with the inhibitory portion of the gamma cycle yielding a temporal separation before the activation of the next neuronal population representing another stimulus. The theta/alpha oscillation structures the order of activation of these neuronal populations because as the oscillatory inhibition decays on each cycle, more strongly activated populations are activated earlier [17, 31]. In our results, when the task requires selecting one of two stimuli as a target, the responses to the two stimuli are separated in the temporal domain, suggesting that the two stimuli are sampled sequentially by attention. Whereas when the task requires integrating the two stimuli as a single target, the responses to the two stimuli tend to be synchronized in time, suggesting that the two stimuli are sampled synchronously by attention. In this study, we found that irrespective of whether the task entailed selecting one stimulus from two or integrating both, the frequency of periodic sampling remained within the theta band (4–6 Hz). This finding suggests that the task merely influenced the temporal difference between stimulus sampling without affecting the sampling speed. In other words, even during the integration task, attention still sampled the two stimuli independently, and the sampling became synchronized in comparison to the selection task. The present results showed that response time was correlated with absolute phase difference only in the integration task, but not in the selection task. This suggests that the absolute phase difference does not reflect general task difficulty but is related to specific cognitive processes. If the absolute phase difference reflected only general task difficulty, it should be correlated with response time across all conditions.

Ideally, the correlation between phase difference and RT should be in opposite directions in the selection and integration tasks. However, in the current results, phase difference is not significantly correlated with RT in the selection task. There may be two reasons for this. First, the integration task requires the simultaneous firing of neuronal populations representing different stimuli. In contrast, the selection task requires not only the firing of neuronal populations representing different stimuli at different times but also the firing of neuronal populations representing the target with higher priority. This may lead to the fact that temporal segregation is not as critical in the selection task as temporal integration is in the integration task. Second, the selection task in this study was easier, while the integration task was more challenging. This may result in the behavioral performance in the selection task being less discriminative, and thus, the correlation with phase difference did not reach significance. In contrast, behavioral performance on the integration task was more discriminative, and therefore, the correlation with phase difference reached significance. Although the results of the behavioral relevance were not exactly what we expected, our conclusions still hold. Because the phase difference of the SSVEP envelopes was significantly greater than 0 in the selection task and not significantly different from 0 in the integration task, this suggests that the task moderates the phase difference. Meanwhile, the absolute magnitude of the phase difference correlated with behavioral performance in the integration task. This suggests that a smaller phase difference, reflecting more synchronized processing, might be beneficial for integrating the two stimuli. In the results, only RT showed a significant correlation with the absolute phase difference, whereas the correlation between accuracy and the absolute phase difference was not statistically significant. The main reason is that the accuracy in both tasks was mostly above 0.9, indicating a low ability to distinguish between participants. In contrast, RT showed a greater ability to differentiate among participants. This finding aligns with the notion that successful integration likely requires processing the two stimuli at similar points in time [13].

Our study demonstrates that the left hemisphere (PO3) samples the right visual field at approximately 6.2 Hz, whereas the right hemisphere (PO4) samples the left visual field at around 4.4 Hz. The higher periodic sampling rate in the left hemisphere reflects the asymmetry in information processing between the two hemispheres. Previous research has revealed similar patterns. Landau and Fries [26] found that when cuing the left visual field, the sampling rate was approximately 7 Hz for the left visual field and 9 Hz for the right visual field, suggesting more rapid sampling in the left hemisphere. Similar findings have been observed in the auditory system, albeit with opposite results. Ho et al. [32] reported that stimuli in the left ear were sampled at 6.2 Hz (sensitivity measurement) and 8.7 Hz (criterion measurement), while stimuli in the right ear were sampled at 5.6 Hz (sensitivity measurement) and 7.5 Hz (criterion measurement). These results imply that the right hemisphere processes auditory information more rapidly. Hemispheric differences in processing characteristics may underlie the observed variations in attentional sampling frequency between the two hemispheres. Specifically, the left hemisphere is specialized for local processing, primarily scanning the right visual field, whereas the right hemisphere is specialized for global processing, scanning the entire visual field [33, 34]. This functional asymmetry suggests that the left hemisphere periodically scans smaller spatial areas, thereby requiring shorter sampling periods (i.e., higher frequencies), whereas the right hemisphere scans larger spatial areas, necessitating longer periods (i.e., lower frequencies). Crucially, this sampling asymmetry remains robust regardless of top-down task demands (selection vs. integration), strongly suggesting that it reflects a fundamental, intrinsic structural constraint of the attentional system.

One potential concern is that differences in task difficulty between selection and integration tasks may create uneven cognitive demands, thereby affecting the phase of attentional sampling in this study. Although this possibility appears plausible, it is highly unlikely. This is because the integration versus segmentation of information has been shown to modulate the phase of attentional sampling [17, 35], whereas cognitive demands and task difficulty primarily affect the sampling speed [10, 11, 36]. More critically, the results revealed that response time was correlated with the absolute phase difference, but not with the raw phase difference, and this relationship was observed exclusively in the integration task. If the phase difference were merely contingent upon general task difficulty, its magnitude should correlate with response time in both the selection and integration tasks; however, this was not the case. Therefore, the phase difference specifically reflects the unique cognitive processes underlying spatial integration. Since this study cannot completely rule out the influence of task difficulty, future research could systematically investigate the impact of cognitive demands on attention sampling phases.

While this study sheds light on the task-dependent modulation of attentional sampling, some limitations need to be addressed in future research. First, the present study focused on attentional sampling in a simple two-stimulus paradigm. Future studies could explore how attentional sampling dynamics are modulated in more complex real-world scenes with multiple stimuli. Second, the present study used high-frequency SSVEP to isolate the neural response to each stimulus. However, the spatial resolution of the EEG signal in this study is compromised by the volume conduction effect, and future research combining SSVEP with techniques such as MEG and sEEG could pinpoint the specific brain regions responsible for these phase modulations.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that attentional sampling is flexible and adapts to task demands with distinct temporal relationships between the sampling of two stimuli during selection versus integration tasks. The findings suggest that the phase of theta-band neural oscillations controls the temporal dynamics of attentional sampling, ultimately optimizing processing for specific task goals.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-one participants (9 males) were recruited, with an average age of 22 years. All participants were right-handed, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and had no history of psychological or psychiatric illness. All participants provided written informed consent before the experiment, which was approved by the Research Ethics Committee at Hangzhou Normal University.

Apparatus and stimuli

We used MATLAB (version R2021a, The MathWorks) and Psychotoolbox-3 extensions [37, 38] to generate and display visual stimuli and record behavioral responses. The visual stimuli were presented on a QD-OLED monitor (Alienware, Dell) with a 1920 × 1080 spatial resolution and a 144 Hz refresh rate. Electroencephalography (EEG) was recorded in a dark room shielded from sound and electromagnetic signals. The participants were comfortably seated at a distance of 57 cm from the screen, with their heads stabilized on a chin rest.

In this experiment, we presented participants with a 2*2 square (length of side: 24.65°) in each of the left and right visual fields on a black background (at minimum luminance 0 cd/m2). The line width of the square was 10 pixels. We used a frequency-tagging technique to record the brain’s response to the stimuli in the left and right visual fields. The luminance of the squares displayed in the left and right visual fields varied sinusoidally between the minimum and maximum luminance (266.73 cd/m2) of the screen at 42 Hz and 44 Hz, respectively (Fig. 1A). To facilitate the averaging of neural responses across trials for obtaining SSVEP signals with a high signal-to-noise ratio, we opted to fix the visual fields of stimulus presentation at different frequencies rather than counterbalancing between visual fields [39, 40]. The targets were two arrows (266.73 cd/m2), each presented in a random square in the left and right visual fields. One arrow randomly pointed up or down, and the other randomly pointed left or right.

Design and procedure

Trials began with the presentation of the squares in the left and right visual fields for 500 ms, after which the square on a random side began to flicker, and then 200 ms later, the square on the other side also began to flicker (Fig. 1B). This asynchronous flickering served as a resetting cue for attentional processing, allowing attention to be directed to the side that flickered first after the presentation of the resetting cue [24]. In this study, we refer to the first flicker as the cued stimulus and the second flicker as the uncued stimulus. If the stimulus on the left flickered first, it is called a cue-left; if the right stimulus flickered first, it is called a cue-right. After a random interval of 1400 to 1700 ms, both arrows were presented simultaneously for 100 ms in the squares of the left and right visual fields as targets.

Participants were required to complete two separate tasks in two blocks. In the selection task, participants were instructed to judge the orientation of a vertical arrow and respond using their right index finger. Specifically, if the arrow pointed upward, they were to press the up key; if it pointed downward, they were to press the down key. In the integration task, participants determined the combined direction of two arrows and also responded using their right index finger. More precisely, when the combined direction was a primary diagonal, they were to press the left key; when it was a secondary diagonal, they were to press the right key. Explicitly, the combined direction of the left and up arrows and the combined direction of the right and down arrows was the primary diagonal, and the combined direction of the left and down arrows and the combined direction of the right and up arrows was the secondary diagonal (Fig. 1C). Participants were asked to press the button quickly and accurately, and the experiment recorded their reaction time and accuracy. To avoid the effect of symmetric visual fields on target integration, the two targets were presented in random squares in the left and right visual fields. Throughout the experiment, participants were asked to fixate on the dot in the center of the screen and to respond within 2000 ms of the target presentation.

Each task contained 320 trials, with 160 per condition (cue-left and cue-right). The presentation order of the two tasks was counterbalanced across participants, and each lasted about 20 min. Before each task, participants were required to complete 16 practice trials to become familiar with the experimental task.

EEG recording and preprocessing

EEG data were recorded at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz using a 64-channel EasyCap and two BrainAmp amplifiers (Brain Products, Germany). The vertical electrooculogram (EOG) was recorded by an additional electrode placed below the right eye. FCz was used as the reference electrode during recording. The impedance of all electrodes was kept below 5 kΩ. Offline preprocessing was carried out using the Fieldtrip toolbox [41]. The EEG data were re-referenced to the average value of TP9 and TP10 and bandpass filtered between 1 and 50 Hz using a Butterworth IIR filter with an order of 2. Independent component analysis (ICA) was performed to remove eye-movement and other artifactual components. No further trial rejection was executed. Data were resampled to 500 Hz and epoched from − 200 to 1700 ms relative to the onset of the resetting cue. Each epoch was baseline-corrected using a baseline from − 200 to 0 ms.

Spectrum analysis of SSVEP

Given that the ERPs could affect the power analysis of the SSVEP [42–44], we excluded the initial 500 ms (primarily the cue-evoked ERP) and the final 200 ms (primarily the target-evoked ERP) from the overall 1900 ms (the total duration comprises of 200 ms for the cue and 1700 ms for the longest interval). Instead, we retained the data from 500 to 1700 ms for SSVEP power analysis. Evoked activities of baseline (− 200–0 ms) and SSVEP (500–1700 ms) were computed respectively by averaging EEG epochs for each condition and each participant. FFT was applied to the evoked activities after applying a 10 s zero-padding and a Hanning taper to calculate the power spectrum for each channel from 36 to 48 Hz with a 1 Hz step.

Spectrum analysis of the envelope of SSVEP

To calculate the power spectrum of the envelope of the SSVEP, we first filtered the EEG of each trial through a bandpass filter to obtain SSVEPs at 42 Hz (41.5–42.5 Hz) and 44 Hz (43.5–44.5 Hz). Next, we calculated the envelope of the SSVEPs in each trial using the envelop function of MATLAB (version R2023b, The MathWorks). FFT was then applied to the envelope after applying a 10 s zero-padding and a Hanning taper to calculate the power spectrum for each channel from 2 to 10 Hz in 0.2 Hz steps. Finally, we used Fieldtrip’s fooof function [25] to extract the periodic oscillations in the spectrum and remove the aperiodic components, before averaging the spectrum across trials. The peak frequencies of the SSVEP envelope oscillations in the left and right visual fields fell within the theta band, yet exhibited slight differences (4.4 Hz and 6.2 Hz, respectively). To investigate the phase relationship between these two envelope oscillations, we computed the circular mean of the phase values across all frequencies between the two peak frequencies. Phase analysis of the envelopes was performed on single trials. Specifically, the circular difference between the cued and uncued stimuli was computed for each trial and subsequently averaged across trials.

Coupling between EEG phase and SSVEP amplitude

We aimed to calculate the coupling between SSVEP amplitude and EEG phase, which is equivalent to the coherence between the SSVEP envelope phase and the EEG phase. We extracted the EEG data from the 500–1700 ms after the cue onset in each trial and used the same spectral analysis for EEG as for the SSVEP envelope to calculate the coherence between the phase of the SSVEP envelope and the phase of the EEG signal for the cue-left and cue-right conditions, respectively. We computed the coherence between the phases of the SSVEP envelope at PO3 and PO4 and the phases of the EEG signals at all electrodes. Subsequently, we averaged the results across the frequency band ranging from 4.4 to 6.2 Hz.

Behavioral correlates of raw and absolute phase differences

We calculated the raw phase difference between the SSVEP envelopes for the cued and uncued stimuli in the selection and integration conditions, respectively. After averaging the cue-left and cue-right trials, we calculated the correlation between raw phase difference and both response accuracy and response time (RT) for correct responses. Since the raw phase difference reflects both the synchronization and the temporal order of the two responses, whereas the absolute phase difference reflects only their synchronization, we also explored the correlation between the absolute phase difference and both RT and accuracy.

Statistical tests

The statistical comparisons of the powers between baseline and SSVEP were evaluated by paired-sample t-test. Statistical assessment of phase distribution was conducted using CircStat Toolbox [45]. The non-uniformity of the phase and the phase difference were tested using Rayleigh’s test [46]. To assess whether the phase angles are significantly different from zero, we compute the 95% confidence intervals of the phase angles and examine whether zero falls within these intervals. The difference of phase differences between the selection and integration tasks was evaluated by the parametric Hotelling paired-sample test using the “circ_htest” function from the CircStat Toolbox [45].

We used a permutation test to statistically quantify the significance of the SSVEP envelope oscillations. For each trial, we randomly shuffled the envelope signal in time and then used the same FFT and fooof algorithms to compute the periodic oscillations of the surrogate signal. This process was repeated 1000 times to obtain a surrogate distribution of power at each frequency, from which a 95% significance threshold (p = 0.05) was determined.

We also used the permutation test to statistically quantify the phase coherence of the EEG and SSVEP envelopes. In each trial, we randomly shuffled the EEG signals in time and used the same spectral and coherence analyses to compute the phase coherence of the SSVEP envelope and the surrogate EEG signal. This process was repeated 1000 times to obtain a surrogate distribution of coherence values at each electrode. We then calculated the percentile of the real coherence value in the permuted distribution as the significance of the phase coherence for each electrode. The p values were corrected across electrodes using the FDR method [47].

Circular-linear correlations were used to evaluate the correlations between the phase difference and both the response time and response accuracy. The p values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the FDR method [47].
