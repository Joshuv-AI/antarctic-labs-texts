The Impact of Sociodemographic Factors on Job Insecurity Among Obstetric Nurses Under the Low Fertility Background: A Linear Mixed-Effects Model Analysis and Targeted Intervention Strategies
Authors: Dengmei Chen, Jun Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the continuous decline in fertility rates in China, the demand for obstetric care services has decreased, posing severe challenges to the occupational security and mental health of obstetric nurses.ObjectiveThe purpose of this study is to explore the effects of demographic sociological variables of obstetrical nurses on sense of security under the background of low fertility rate, identify the key driving factors, and put forward intervention countermeasures based on the empirical results, in order to improve the professional survival status of obstetric nurses and stabilize the obstetric nursing team.MethodsFrom April to May 2025, 406 obstetric nurses were recruited as the research subjects by using the two-stage sampling method and surveyed using a general information questionnaire and Job Insecurity Scale. Q-Q plots assessed data normality; normally distributed data were expressed as M±SD, with t-tests/ANOVA (SNK-q for pairwise comparisons).Under the premise of controlling the hospital agglomeration effect, the factors influencing the hospital agglomeration effect were analyzed by using the linear mixed effect model.ResultsObstetric nurses’ total job insecurity score was 76.07±22.80, with 69.21% above the median (62.5). Linear mixed-effects model analysis showed significant differences (all P<0.05): secondary hospital nurses had higher job insecurity scores than tertiary hospital ones (β=12.55, 95% CI: 6.66~18.43); contracted nurses scored higher than staffed nurses (β=18.832, 95% CI: 13.254~24.409); those with monthly salary ≤4,000 yuan scored higher than ≥6,001 yuan (β=10.002, 95% CI: 2.891~17.113); nurses with 6–10 years of experience scored higher than ≤5 years (β=7.741, 95% CI: 1.829~13.653); team leaders scored lower than general nurses (β=−9.457, 95% CI: −16.245~-2.669). The model explained 26% of individual and 23% of hospital-level variance in job insecurity scores.ConclusionObstetric nurses in Chongqing have relatively high job insecurity affected by hospital and individual factors. This study provides empirical support for a dual approach of “optimizing hospital environment and precise individual empowerment” to stabilize the team and ensure maternal-infant safety.

Introduction

The low fertility rate has emerged as a significant global population challenge. Currently, nearly half of the countries worldwide exhibit fertility rates below the replacement threshold.1 China implemented its one-child policy in the early 1980s. In response to the ongoing decline in fertility rates, the government has gradually relaxed birth restrictions and introduced various intervention measures to promote childbirth. These measures include extending maternity leave, providing birth subsidies, enhancing childcare services, and implementing preferential housing policies.2 However, as observed in other countries with low fertility rates, such as South Korea and Japan,3 policy interventions alone are insufficient to reverse the trend of declining fertility rates, and the willingness of married women to have children remains relatively low.Data indicate that China’s annual birth population has sharply declined from 17.23 million in 2017 to 9.54 million in 2024, representing a decrease of nearly 50%.4 Key factors limiting fertility include significant economic pressures, such as high costs associated with housing, education, and childcare,5,6 as well as the growing emphasis on advanced education and career development among women.7 In this context, scholars argue that China has inevitably entered a “low fertility rate trap,” with the persistently declining fertility rate evolving into a long-term demographic reality.8

Amid a declining fertility rate, the demand for obstetric medical services has significantly diminished, compelling the obstetric service system to undergo structural adjustments, including the reduction of hospital beds and the integration of medical resources.9 This trend aligns with the developmental trajectory of South Korea, where the number of obstetric hospitals decreased sharply from 1,371 in 2003 to 541 in 2019, reflecting a decline of 60.5%.10 As primary providers of obstetric medical services, nurses face multiple pressures resulting from system reforms, such as decreased income, limited career advancement opportunities, and heightened job competition. These adverse conditions render obstetric nurses particularly vulnerable to significant job insecurity.11,12 Job insecurity, recognized as a potential chronic workplace stressor, not only adversely affects nurses’ mental health but also initiates a cascade of negative consequences, including declines in nursing quality, increases in medical errors, reductions in job satisfaction, and heightened turnover intentions. Ultimately, these factors pose a potential threat to maternal and infant health.13,14

Utilizing the analytical framework of Conservation of Resources (COR), the aforementioned phenomenon substantiates the theory’s fundamental premise: individuals possess an intrinsic motivation to safeguard their core resources, and the loss or inadequate replenishment of these resources elicits a psychological stress response.15 In particular, the contraction and restructuring of maternity services, prompted by declining fertility rates, have led to diminished career stability, reduced financial income, and restricted opportunities for professional development among nurses. The mental health of nurses has long been a significant concern within the global academic community. Nevertheless, empirical research examining the job insecurity of obstetric nurses in the context of declining fertility rates remains relatively limited. Notably, an aggregation effect exists among hospital nurses, resulting in a lack of independence in the observed data. Furthermore, traditional single-level analysis methods fail to account for such aggregation biases. To make the results more reliable, the present study employs a linear mixed-effects model to systematically investigate the individual-level and hospital-level factors that influence the job insecurity of obstetric nurses amid low fertility rates. This approach aims to provide a scientific foundation for stabilizing the obstetric nursing workforce.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Sampling Method

This study employs a descriptive correlational cross-sectional design to investigate the factors influencing job insecurity among obstetric nurses. Conducted in Chongqing from April to May 2025, the research utilized a two-stage sampling method to recruit qualified obstetric nurses from secondary and tertiary medical institutions. In the first stage, a stratified random sampling approach was implemented. From a total of 410 medical institutions with midwifery qualifications in Chongqing, 37 secondary and 19 tertiary institutions were selected at a ratio of approximately 4:1 for inclusion in the study. In the second stage, a convenience sampling method was employed within each selected institution to recruit obstetric nurses. This approach ensured the study’s feasibility and efficiency while facilitating the attainment of the desired sample size.

Inclusion criteria consisted of research subjects possessing valid nursing practice certificates, with a minimum of one year of obstetric work experience, who voluntarily signed informed consent forms.

Exclusion criteria included in-hospital trainees or interns, individuals on leave during the investigation period, and personnel not directly involved in obstetric care.

Research Methods

Survey Instruments

General Information Questionnaire

The general information survey form incorporates a self-designed demographic questionnaire that includes items such as gender, age, only-child status, marital status, hospital type,hospital level, highest educational attainment, professional title, job position, personnel status, length of work experience, and monthly income.

Job Insecurity Scale

Drawing on the theoretical and empirical literature regarding international job insecurity, Hu and Li (2010) developed the Job Insecurity Scale. This scale has been adapted to align with Chinese social culture and the specific characteristics of employees’ work, thereby mitigating the cultural bias inherent in international scales. Its internal consistency and structural validity have been established in previous studies involving Chinese samples.16 Consequently, this scale was employed in the present study to evaluate the job insecurity experienced by participants.The scale comprises 25 items categorized into five dimensions: unemployment insecurity (9 items), interpersonal relationship insecurity (4 items), job performance insecurity (4 items), excessive competition insecurity (4 items), and salary promotion insecurity (4 items). It employs a 5-point Likert scoring system, with scores ranging from 1 (completely incompatible) to 5 (completely compliant), yielding a total score range of 25 to 125 points. A higher score indicates a greater level of job insecurity experienced by the subject. To assess the scale’s applicability within the study samples, its reliability was evaluated. The results revealed that the Cronbach α coefficients for the five dimensions and the overall scale ranged from 0.876 to 0.964, demonstrating that the scale possesses good reliability and is appropriate for this study.

Survey Methods

This study employed an anonymous online self-administered questionnaire survey method, which was disseminated via the Wenjuanxing platform. Prior to the investigation, respondents were thoroughly informed about the purpose, content, and data usage of the research. They provided their informed consent by signing a form and agreed to adhere strictly to the data confidentiality agreement. Sample size estimation was guided by the principle that “the sample size should be at least 10 times the number of variables”.17 Given that this study included 12 predictor variables, such as gender and age, the minimum sample size requirement was determined to be 120 cases. Accounting for a 20% invalid questionnaire rate, a total of 144 questionnaires were planned for distribution. Ultimately, 418 questionnaires were collected during the survey. After screening and excluding 12 invalid responses due to short answering times, highly repetitive selections, and logical inconsistencies in scale scores, 406 valid questionnaires were retained, resulting in an effective recovery rate of 97%.

Statistical Analysis

The normality of the measurement data was assessed using the Quantile-Quantile (Q-Q) plot. Statistical descriptions were presented as mean ± standard deviation (M±SD) in accordance with the characteristics of normal distribution. Comparisons between groups were conducted using the t-test or analysis of variance, while the SNK-q test was employed for pairwise comparisons post hoc. Cohen’s d and η2 were utilized to quantify the magnitude of the statistical effects. Fractional data were reported as counts and percentages (%). To account for the clustering effect at the hospital level, a linear mixed-effects model was applied to analyze the factors influencing job insecurity among obstetric nurses. Initially, a random intercept empty model was employed to assess the hospital’s clustering effect. Subsequently, level 2 and level 1 variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were progressively included to establish various models and evaluate their impact on the outcome.In the linear mixed-effects model, the index of proportional reduction in variance is employed to calculate the explainable variance at both level 1 and level 2, which is utilized to describe R2. The variance inflation factor (VIF) is applied to assess the collinearity among the independent variables within the model. All analyses were performed using SAS 9.4, and a P value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Relationship Between Demographic Characteristics and Job Insecurity Among Obstetric Nurses

A total of 406 obstetric nurses participated in this study, of whom 401 were female, representing 98.8%. The core demographic and occupational characteristics of the participants are as follows: the predominant age group is 25 to 34 years, comprising 61.8%. The majority are married, accounting for 83.3%. Most practice in general hospitals (60.1%) and secondary medical institutions (64.5%). The educational attainment is primarily at the bachelor’s degree level or higher, constituting 88.2%. The professional title distribution is largely composed of nurses, representing 83.7%. The majority hold positions as clinical nurses, accounting for 74.9%. Employment is predominantly contractual, with 57.9% of participants in this category. Most have between 6 and 15 years of work experience, comprising 63.8%. Monthly income is primarily concentrated in the range of 4,001 to 8,000 yuan, accounting for 63.1%.

Additionally, the differences in scores for the dimensions of unemployment insecurity, interpersonal relationship insecurity, and job performance insecurity, as well as the overall job insecurity score among obstetric nurses with differing professional titles, were also statistically significant (all P < 0.05). Discussion

The Job Insecurity of Obstetric Nurses Is at a Moderately High Level

The findings of this study indicate that the total score for job insecurity among obstetric nurses in Chongqing is (76.07±22.80) points. Notably, 69.21% of the nurses scored above the median (62.5 points), suggesting that the overall job insecurity within this cohort is at a medium to upper level. This outcome aligns with existing literature, which reports that, in the context of a low fertility rate, nursing professionals generally experience comparable levels of occupational stress.18

Examination of the top 10 items based on job insecurity scores indicated that 50% of these items pertained to job loss, representing a pivotal risk factor for job insecurity within this cohort. This attribute is intricately linked to the sample composition in this study, where 76% of the nurses are employed on a non-permanent basis, substantially compromising their job security. This finding further validates prior research suggesting that non-permanent employment status significantly contributes to job insecurity within the nursing domain.19

Secondly, the issue of insecurity regarding salary promotion and interpersonal relationships is notably pronounced. In the context of a declining fertility rate, a potential decrease in the demand for obstetric services emerges. This scenario heightens nurses’ apprehensions about the fairness of their salaries and benefits, as well as their career advancement prospects. This observation aligns with prior research indicating that “salary satisfaction is significantly negatively correlated with job insecurity”.20 Furthermore, the potential job reductions and limited career development opportunities resulting from low fertility rates not only exacerbate industry competition and nurses’ anxiety regarding their capabilities but also resonate with the finding that “job value realization is negatively correlated with job insecurity”.21

The insecurity in interpersonal relationships primarily arises from frequent personnel changes in the department amidst a low fertility rate, diminishing interpersonal connections, and reduced organizational support. These factors exacerbate nurses’ anxiety at the interpersonal level, aligning with previous research suggesting that “sufficient organizational support can mitigate individual job insecurity”.22

Factors Influencing Obstetric Nurses’ Job Insecurity

Influencing Factors of Job Insecurity Among Obstetric Nurses at the Hospital Level: Hospital Grade

The mixed-effects model analysis revealed that “hospital grade” accounts for 23% of the inter-hospital variances, primarily attributed to variations in management models, resource distribution, and workloads across hospitals of varying grades.23 These factors subsequently result in discernible disparities in job insecurity within the nurse cohort by shaping their perceptions of occupational stress.

The results of the multi-dimensional analysis indicate that obstetric nurses in secondary hospitals report higher scores in all five dimensions of job insecurity compared to their counterparts in tertiary hospitals. This finding suggests that job insecurity is more pronounced among the former group. The primary cause of this disparity is the imbalance in resource allocation and career development opportunities between the two types of hospitals. Tertiary hospitals, benefiting from substantial resource reserves and robust institutional support,24 can maintain job stability for nurses through job rotation mechanisms, even amidst a decline in obstetric service volume due to low fertility rates. This approach mitigates concerns regarding unemployment risks and excessive competition. Furthermore, tertiary hospitals provide a broader range of diagnostic and treatment services and engage in scientific research and teaching functions,25 thereby offering nurses enhanced opportunities for academic advancement and professional title promotion, which in turn alleviates their anxiety related to salary and career progression.

In contrast, secondary hospitals have obvious shortcomings in terms of personnel allocation, equipment conditions and the capacity to treat critical cases. With the continuous increase in the demand for medical services for pregnant and postpartum women, obstetric nurses in secondary hospitals need to bear greater pressure in performance assessment and interpersonal relationships. This situation further aggravates their job insecurity in relevant dimensions.

Influencing Factors of Obstetric Nurses’ Work at the Personal Level: Job Positions, Length of Work Experience, Personnel Situation, Average Monthly Income

Analysis using a mixed effects model revealed that individual characteristics accounted for 26% of the variation in nurses’ job insecurity. To elucidate its impact mechanism, this study conducted dimension-specific analysis. The findings indicated that nursing team leaders experienced significantly lower job insecurity compared to ordinary nurses in the job position dimension. This distinction was consistent across dimensions such as unemployment risk, workplace relationships, work performance, and competition. The disparity can be attributed to structural adjustments in the demand for relevant nursing positions due to declining fertility rates. Ordinary nurses are exposed to a higher risk of job instability. Conversely, nursing team leaders, serving as the mainstay with managerial roles and professional competencies, exhibit greater job stability, thereby mitigating concerns about unemployment and job insecurity stemming from external fluctuations.Stratified analysis of working years showed that nurses with 6~10 years had significantly higher unemployment risk insecurity than nurses with ≤5 years.This increased risk may stem from the fact that as work experience accumulates, this group encounters challenges such as heightened family responsibilities, notable obstacles in career advancement, and diminished quality of work life, all of which contribute to heightened unemployment anxiety.26,27 Regarding employment type, contract nurses, particularly those who are dispatched or outsourced, exhibited significantly higher work insecurity scores compared to their formally employed counterparts. This disparity arises from the disadvantages contract nurses face in resource allocation, access to organizational support, and perceptions of organizational equity, which further exacerbate the adverse effects of these challenges in high-stress obstetric work environments.28,29 In terms of income level, nurses earning a monthly salary of 4,000 yuan or less reported significantly greater insecurity concerning unemployment risk, workplace interpersonal relationships, work performance, and salary advancement compared to those earning 6,001 yuan or more.The primary reason is that many low-income nurses are informal staff, who typically encounter challenges such as unstable employment and income, limited access to training resources, intense job competition, and a diminished sense of professional belonging.30

Countermeasures to Reduce Obstetric Nurses’ Job Insecurity

Hospital Environment Optimization

To alleviate the workload of obstetrical nurses in secondary hospitals, interventions can prioritize resource consolidation and operational efficiency. This can be achieved by establishing collaborative partnerships with higher-tier medical facilities through medical federations, implementing expert-led on-site guidance, remote consultations, and a multi-center coordination system to enhance the management of critical patients.31 Emphasizing grassroots services, initiatives such as home-based prenatal and postnatal care, health education, the utilization of intelligent prenatal monitoring technology,32 and the incorporation of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) practices for pregnancy and postpartum care33 can bolster the competitiveness and mitigate the risk of staff reductions. Furthermore, advocating for local government support towards secondary hospitals in maternal and child health policy-making is crucial to ensure stable resource distribution and the allocation of dedicated funds for talent development. This encompasses professional skills enhancement, clinical case reviews, targeted aid for low-income and surplus nurses, and fostering an inclusive workplace environment to fortify nurses’ professional confidence.

Targeted Individual Empowerment

Differentiated Job Security to Strengthen the Occupational Safety of Key Groups

To address the challenges of high unemployment risk and limited job security among regular nurses, a dynamic elastic post establishment mechanism based on service volume was introduced. This approach involves coordinating medical resources and leveraging the expertise of a specialized nursing alliance to delegate non-core clinical tasks, thereby reducing the risk of job displacement amidst declining fertility rates.34 Emphasizing the leadership role of nursing team leaders, a personalized support system between team leaders and regular nurses was established to facilitate knowledge and skill transfer, thereby boosting the job stability and competitiveness of regular nurses.35 To address the salient characteristics of nurses with 6–10 years of service who have a high risk of unemployment and a weak sense of job security, a dedicated career counseling service was established to offer tailored career guidance and create pathways for transitioning from clinical nursing to roles in nursing management and education, such as nursing quality control assistant or internship teaching specialist, to overcome career advancement barriers.36 Furthermore, initiatives such as enhancing family-friendly workplace policies, implementing flexible scheduling, and introducing emergency leave provisions were implemented to alleviate work-life balance challenges faced by nurses.37

Standardize Employment Management and Narrow the Remuneration Gap Between Nurses of Different Employment Types

Standardizing the management system for non-tenured nurses involves directly correlating salary and benefits with job performance, replacing the traditional identity-based salary structure, and ensuring equitable rights in areas such as paid time off, training, professional development, and title evaluation.38 Connecting tenure with career advancement opportunities by extending contract durations is crucial.39 Furthermore, enhancing organizational support by integrating non-permanent nurses into departmental team dynamics fosters a sense of belonging and fairness.40 Given the high-stress nature of obstetrics, introducing special allowances for high-risk positions, implementing a tiered approach to managing work-related stress, and providing relief through scheduled breaks and psychological support are essential.41 Establishing a standardized monitoring system for nurse salary satisfaction42 is vital for narrowing the gap in treatment between permanent and non-permanent nursing staff.

Optimize Income Distribution and Resource Allocation to Safeguard the Development Rights and Interests of Low-Income Nurses

To address the challenges faced by nurses earning monthly incomes of ≤ 4000 yuan, efforts were made to optimize the salary distribution structure. This included establishing a minimum wage standard for obstetric nursing positions and implementing a system where salary growth is tied to length of service, skill level, and performance, thereby creating a transparent path for salary advancement.43 Furthermore, there was an emphasis on enhancing the allocation of training resources by establishing a dedicated training fund to ensure access to training opportunities focusing on critical care, health education, and other essential skills.44,45 Additionally, initiatives such as recognition programs and activities promoting a sense of professional community were implemented to foster a stronger professional identity and enhance the perceived value of low-income nurses.

Study Limitations and Future Prospects

This study adopted a single-zone sampling method and a cross-sectional study design, focusing mainly on sociodemographic variables.At present, relevant psychological, organizational and other factors have not been included. Furthermore, this study adopted a convenience sampling method, which may to some degree affect the representativeness of the sample and the generalizability of the research results.Additionally, the sample size may be relatively limited when accounting for clustering effects within hospitals.Future research can be improved in multiple aspects. Incorporating psychological and organizational variables into the analytical framework can adopt multi-center, large-sample longitudinal follow-up surveys to replace the single-region convenience sampling and cross-sectional design used in this study. Moreover, expanding the sample size, controlling the clustering effect within the hospital, and reducing bias through stratified sampling and other techniques can help to explore the causal relationship between variables in a more detailed manner. Meanwhile, in line with the core objectives of this study, targeted intervention plans should be formulated, with a focus on meeting the psychological needs of obstetric nurses and optimizing organizational support. The implementation effect of this plan can be evaluated through empirical research, thereby enhancing the practical application value of the research results.

Conclusion

This study reveals a high incidence of job insecurity among obstetric nurses in Chongqing, with nearly 70% reporting significant feelings of insecurity. This study examines demographic and sociology-related factors from both the individual and hospital levels, and identifies the core determinants of job insecurity among obstetric nurses.The findings not only offer baseline data for future in-depth regional studies and cross-regional comparisons but also provide a scientific foundation for developing targeted clinical intervention strategies. In conclusion, the results of this research can guide medical institutions in optimizing the allocation of obstetric nursing personnel, enhancing management systems, and promoting the mental health of nurses.This, in turn, lays a robust foundation for stabilizing the nursing workforce and ensuring maternal and infant safety.
