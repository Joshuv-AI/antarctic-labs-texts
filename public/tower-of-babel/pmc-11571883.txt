Predictive accuracy of machine learning models for conservative treatment failure in thoracolumbar burst fractures
Authors: Ehsan Alimohammadi, Fatemeh Arjmandnia, Mohammadali Ataee, Seyed Reza Bagheri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe management of patients with thoracolumbar burst fractures remains a topic of debate, with conservative treatment being successful in most cases but not all. This study aimed to assess the utility of machine learning models (MLMs) in predicting the need for surgery in patients with these fractures who do not respond to conservative management.MethodsA retrospective analysis of 357 patients with traumatic thoracolumbar burst fractures treated conservatively between January 2017 and October 2023 was conducted. Various potential risk factors for treatment failure were evaluated, including age, gender, BMI, smoking, diabetes, vertebral body compression rate, anterior height compression, Cobb angle, interpedicular distance, canal compromise, and pain intensity. Three MLMs—random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), and k-nearest neighborhood (k-NN)—were used to predict treatment failure, with the RF model also identifying factors associated with treatment failure.ResultsAmong the patients studied, most (85.2%) completed conservative treatment, while 14.8% required surgery during follow-up. Smoking (OR: 2.01; 95% CI: 1.54–2.86; p = 0.011) and interpedicular distance (OR: 2.31; 95% CI: 1.22–2.73; p = 0.003) were found to be independent risk factors for treatment failure. The MLMs demonstrated good performance, with SVM achieving the highest accuracy (0.931), followed by RF (0.911) and k-NN (0.896). SVM also exhibited superior sensitivity and specificity compared to the other models, with AUC values of 0.897, 0.854, and 0.815 for SVM, RF, and k-NN, respectively.ConclusionThis study underscores the effectiveness of MLMs in predicting conservative treatment failure in patients with thoracolumbar burst fractures. These models offer valuable prognostic insights that can aid in optimizing patient management and clinical outcomes in this specific patient population.Keywords: Burst fracture, Thoracolumbar, Conservative treatment, Machine learning models

Background

Traumatic fractures of the vertebral body in the thoracolumbar spine are a common type of spinal injury, with burst fractures accounting for approximately 20% of all thoracolumbar fractures [1, 2]. Burst fractures involve damage to both the anterior and middle columns of the spine. The management of these fractures is a topic of debate, with both surgical and conservative approaches having their own advantages and disadvantages [3–7]. Surgical treatment offers benefits such as a shorter period of bed rest, early correction of deformity, and prevention of later kyphotic deformity, while conservative management avoids the risks and costs associated with surgery [8, 9].

Despite the success of conservative treatment in many cases, approximately 20% of patients may experience treatment failure [4, 5]. Machine learning models have emerged as valuable tools for analyzing the impact of various factors on patient outcomes [10, 11]. These models can detect patterns and relationships in data sets to make predictions without relying on specific equations. In the field of biostatistics and medicine, machine learning models are increasingly used to categorize and predict patient outcomes [12–15].

To the best of our knowledge, there has been no study evaluating the use of machine learning models in predicting treatment failure in patients with thoracolumbar burst fractures treated conservatively. This study aims to assess the effectiveness of machine learning models in predicting treatment failure in these patients and identify factors related to the failure of treatment.

Methods

The current study examined 357 consecutive patients with a single-level acute traumatic thoracolumbar burst fracture who received conservative management at our institution between January 2017 and October 2023. Inclusion criteria comprised a single-level traumatic thoracolumbar burst fracture, age between 18 and 65 years upon admission, and a Thoracolumbar Injury Classification and Severity Score (TLICS) of less than four at admission [16]. Patients with neurological deficits, a history of lumbar surgery, pathologic or osteoporotic fractures, or multiple fractures were excluded. The study received approval from our institute’s ethics committee, and all patients provided written informed consent.

Upon admission to the emergency department, all patients underwent a comprehensive physical examination. Severity scores were determined using the TLICS algorithm by an expert radiologist (MA), where stable burst fractures were identified as having a TLICS score of less than four. Patients with a TLICS score exceeding four were surgically treated and not included in the study.

Radiological assessments, including anteroposterior and lateral thoracolumbar X-rays, CT scans, and T1- and T2-weighted images with short-tau inversion-recovery (STIR) sequences, were conducted to evaluate the integrity of the posterior ligamentous complex (PLC) [17].

Conservative treatment involved immobilization, pain management, and thromboembolism prophylaxis. A thoracolumbosacral orthosis (TLSO) was custom-made for each patient, with two days of complete bed rest followed by mobilization with the TLSO for 12 weeks. The Visual Analog Scale (VAS) was used to assess back pain.

The Cobb angle was determined by measuring the angle formed between the superior endplate of the vertebra above the fracture and the inferior endplate of the vertebra below the fracture.

Canal compromise was assessed by calculating the ratio of the canal area at the fracture level to the average spinal canal diameter at the adjacent vertebrae above and below the fracture.

The Vertebral Body Compression Rate (VBCR) and Percentage of Anterior Height Compression (PAHC) were determined following the methodology illustrated in Fig. 1 [18].

Cobb angle was measured as the angle between the superior endplate of the vertebra above the fracture and the inferior endplate of the vertebra below the fracture. All radiological evaluations were conducted by a qualified radiologist (MA).

The failure of conservative treatment was defined as the need for surgery due to progressive neurological deficits and kyphosis during the follow-up period.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 21 software, with data presented as mean ± standard deviation. Student’s t-test and Chi-square test were used for comparisons, and binary logistic regression analysis identified independent risk factors for treatment failure. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered.

Model development

We implemented three machine learning models—support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), and k-nearest neighbors (k-NN)—to predict the likelihood of treatment failure in patients with conservatively managed thoracolumbar burst fractures. The random forest model was specifically used to examine the factors related to treatment failure. Each model was subjected to training and evaluation, with the dataset divided into training and testing sets in an 80:20 ratio. Key features identified through univariate analysis were chosen as inputs for the machine learning algorithms.

Support vector machine (SVM)

Support Vector Machine (SVM) is a supervised machine learning algorithm primarily used for classification and occasionally for regression tasks. It works by finding the hyperplane that best separates data points of different classes in a high-dimensional space. The key idea behind SVM is to identify the optimal hyperplane that maximizes the margin between the closest data points from each class, known as support vectors. This property enhances the model’s ability to generalize to unseen data. SVM can handle both linear and non-linear classification tasks through the use of kernel functions, which transform the input space into a higher-dimensional space. This flexibility makes SVM a powerful tool for various applications, ranging from image recognition to bioinformatics, where clear separations between classes are essential. Additionally, SVM is robust to overfitting, especially when the dataset is high-dimensional but has a relatively small number of samples [19, 20].

Decision tree (DT) and random forest (RF) models

Decision Trees (DT) and Random Forests (RF) are both popular machine learning algorithms used for classification and regression tasks. A Decision Tree is a flowchart-like structure where internal nodes represent tests on features, branches indicate outcomes, and leaf nodes represent class labels or values. The algorithm recursively splits the data based on feature values to create a tree that captures the relationships between features and outcomes, making it easy to interpret. However, Decision Trees can be prone to overfitting, especially with complex datasets. To overcome this limitation, Random Forests — an ensemble learning method — constructs multiple Decision Trees during training and merges their outputs to improve accuracy and control overfitting. The randomness introduced in creating each tree (by selecting random subsets of features and training data) enhances the model’s robustness and generalization capabilities. RF is particularly effective for large datasets and provides a reliable measure of feature importance, making it a versatile choice for various predictive modeling tasks [21, 22].

K-nearest neighbors (K-NN)

K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) is a simple yet powerful supervised machine learning algorithm used for both classification and regression tasks. The core principle of KNN is based on the idea that similar data points tend to be located close to one another in feature space. When a new data point needs to be classified, the algorithm identifies the ‘k’ closest data points (neighbors) from the training set, typically using distance metrics such as Euclidean or Manhattan distance. The classification of the new data point is then determined by majority voting among its neighbors, while regression involves averaging the values of these neighbors. One of the key strengths of KNN is its simplicity and ease of implementation, as it makes no assumptions about the underlying data distribution. However, KNN can be computationally expensive, especially with large datasets, and its performance can be influenced by the choice of ‘k’ and the feature scaling. Despite these limitations, KNN remains widely used for its effectiveness in various applications, including pattern recognition and recommendation systems [23, 24].

Performance evaluation

Model performance was evaluated using metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC). These metrics provided insights into the models’ predictive abilities and their effectiveness in identifying treatment failure.

Software

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23 for descriptive and inferential statistics, while the randomForest package for R was utilized to fit the RF model. The e1071 package was employed for SVM fitting, and the caret package facilitated the calculation of performance metrics. These packages were implemented in R4.0.3 software for model development and evaluation.

Results

A total of 357 patients with single-level traumatic thoracolumbar burst fractures were evaluated in this study. The gender distribution among the patients included 224 males (62.7%) and 133 females (36.4%). The average age and follow-up time were 37.76 ± 10.73 years [range: 18–73] and 18.43 ± 4.52 months [range: 8–34], respectively. Among the patients, 91 individuals (25.5%) were smokers, and 73 subjects (20.4%) had diabetes mellitus. The mean values for BMI and VAS were 23.81 ± 3.12 Kg and 5.32 ± 0.83, respectively [Tables 1 and 2].

The majority of patients (85.2%) completed conservative treatment, while 53 cases (14.8%) required surgical intervention during the follow-up period due to progressive neurological deficits [15 cases (28.4%)] and kyphosis [38 cases (71.6%)]. Posterior transpedicular instrumentation and fusion were performed for all subjects.

Univariate analysis revealed that older age, smoking, greater cobb angle, and higher interpedicular distance in the fractured vertebra were associated with an increased risk of requiring surgery (p < 0.05). However, no significant relationships were found between the need for surgery and gender, BMI, VBCR, PAHC, canal compromise, or VAS [Tables 1 and 2].

In multivariate analysis, smoking (OR: 2.01; 95% CI: 1.54–2.86; p = 0.011) and interpedicular distance (OR: 2.31; 95% CI: 1.22–2.73; p = 0.003) were identified as independent risk factors for surgery[Table 3].

Each machine learning model employed feature selection to evaluate the significance of risk factors for treatment failure. The random forest (RF) model ranked interpedicular distance (IPD), smoking, cobb angle, and age as the most important variables based on the mean Gini index.

Support vector machine (SVM) achieved the highest accuracy (0.931), followed by RF (0.911) and k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) (0.896). Discussion

The management of stable traumatic thoracolumbar burst fractures in neurologically-intact patients remains a challenge for clinicians. While conservative treatment is typically the initial approach, some patients may not respond well and may eventually require surgical intervention.

In a retrospective study by Shen et al., risk factors associated with the failure of conservative treatment for thoracolumbar burst fractures were evaluated. Factors such as age, visual analogue scale (VAS) score, interpedicular distance (IPD), and kyphotic angle (KA) were found to be significantly different between the groups that succeeded and failed with non-operative treatment. Specifically, VAS score and IPD were identified as significant predictors of treatment failure [25].

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Terence Tan et al. focused on the management of stable traumatic thoracolumbar burst fractures and factors influencing conservative treatment failure [8]. The analysis of 601 patients from 11 studies revealed a 9.2% failure rate for conservative management. Admission factors predictive of treatment failure included age, initial kyphotic angle, initial interpedicular distance, residual canal size, Load Sharing Classification (LSC) score, and admission VAS pain scores. Patients presenting with these risk factors may benefit from surgical management. Our study identified interpedicular distance (IPD) as an independent risk factor for the failure of conservative treatment in thoracolumbar burst fractures. Previous research has highlighted the importance of IPD in assessing the severity of these fractures. It has been suggested that an increase in IPD following trauma may elevate the risk of bone fragment retropulsion into the spinal canal, potentially leading to neurological deficits [26]. Caffaro et al.‘s retrospective study further supported this notion by establishing a significant correlation between IPD, spinal canal compromise, and neurological deficits. They concluded that IPD could serve as a reliable indicator for evaluating spinal canal narrowing, laminar fractures, and the presence of neurological deficits in thoracolumbar burst fractures [26].

Regarding smoking, there is a growing body of evidence from observational and experimental studies indicating that smoking can impede bone healing (union) following fractures or surgical procedures. While a direct causal link between smoking and delayed union has not been definitively established, clinical observations and biological plausibility from animal studies suggest a strong association. Smoking has also been linked to low bone mineral density (BMD), a risk factor previously thought to primarily affect older women but now recognized as relevant for men over 50 years old as well. The mechanisms through which smoking influences BMD involve the effects of nicotine and cadmium in cigarette smoke on bone cells, as well as potential impacts on calcium and vitamin D absorption, hormonal function, and estrogen levels. Additionally, smoking may alter the effectiveness of hormonal replacement therapy [27, 28].

In this study, machine learning models were utilized to predict treatment failure in conservatively treated thoracolumbar burst fractures. The results showed that SVM performed exceptionally well, demonstrating superior performance in predicting treatment failure with minimal error. The application of machine learning models in predicting treatment failure in conservatively managed thoracolumbar burst fractures marks a significant advancement in the integration of technology with clinical decision-making. This study highlights the effectiveness of these models in providing valuable prognostic insights, enhancing patient management strategies, and ultimately improving clinical outcomes.

Overall, SVM emerged as the preferred choice for predicting treatment outcomes in patients with traumatic thoracolumbar burst fractures.

In addition to SVM, the performance of other models, such as Random Forest (RF), Decision Trees (DT), and k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN), also provides important insights. RF, as an ensemble method, aggregates predictions from numerous decision trees, which helps mitigate the risk of overfitting and enhances predictive accuracy. This flexibility allows RF to effectively analyze high-dimensional data, making it a valuable tool in contexts where a multitude of variables might be relevant [30, 31].

Decision Trees offer an intuitive approach to classification, simplifying the decision-making process for clinicians [30, 32]. Their inherent interpretability allows healthcare providers to understand the rationale behind predictions, facilitating discussions with patients about potential treatment options. However, as a standalone model, DTs may be prone to overfitting, especially in datasets with noise or outliers. Using DTs as part of an ensemble, as seen with Random Forest, can significantly bolster their predictive performance [30, 32].

k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN), while simpler, introduces a different methodology for classification based on proximity among samples. Its efficiency is based on the assumption that similar cases yield similar outcomes [24, 33]. This model’s effectiveness can vary based on the chosen distance metric, emphasizing the need for careful selection and tuning. While k-NN is easier to implement and interpret, its computational demands can grow significantly with larger datasets, limiting its practicality in real-world clinical settings [33, 34].

The findings from this study also highlight the importance of understanding demographic and clinical factors—such as smoking status and interpedicular distance—that independently predict treatment failure. By integrating machine learning with these clinical variables, healthcare providers can tailor treatment plans based on individual risk profiles. This personalized approach represents a significant evolution in the management of thoracolumbar burst fractures, where risk stratification can guide treatment decisions more effectively.

Moreover, while machine learning models show great promise, it’s essential to acknowledge the challenges that accompany their implementation in clinical practice [35]. These include issues related to data quality, the need for large datasets to train models effectively, and potential biases inherent in the data that could affect the model’s predictions. As such, ongoing efforts to validate these models across diverse populations and settings are crucial for ensuring their clinical applicability [35, 36].

Incorporating machine learning into routine clinical workflows necessitates collaboration between data scientists, radiologists, and spine surgeons. This interdisciplinary approach not only enhances the model’s accuracy and relevance but also fosters a deeper understanding of the clinical implications of predictive analytics [36, 37].

Limitations

The current study has several limitations, including being a single-center retrospective study with no long-term follow-up and a limited sample size. Therefore, we recommend conducting large prospective multicenter trials to explore predictors of conservative treatment failure in stable thoracolumbar burst fracture patients. While this study successfully analyzed various predictive factors for treatment failure in thoracolumbar burst fractures, it is important to acknowledge that the degree of comminution was not assessed as a possible determinant for surgical intervention. The extent of comminution—characterized by the fragmentation of bone during fracture—can significantly impact the stability of the vertebral column and the potential for non-union following conservative treatment. Comminuted fractures often present a higher risk for compromised structural integrity, leading to increased chances of neurological deficits or chronic pain, which may necessitate surgical stabilization.

Despite the inclusion of 357 subjects in the study, a larger sample size would improve statistical power and generalizability. While the machine learning models showed satisfactory predictive performance, their interpretability may be constrained. Understanding the specific factors influencing these models’ predictions can be challenging, potentially impacting their clinical usefulness and decision-making process. Prospective studies with standardized data collection procedures would yield more robust and comprehensive findings.

Conclusion

This study highlights the important clinical implications of smoking status and interpedicular distance as independent risk factors for the failure of conservative treatment in patients with thoracolumbar burst fractures. The results suggest that individuals who smoke and those with a wider interpedicular distance may face a greater likelihood of treatment failure, warranting more vigilant monitoring and potential consideration for surgical options. Furthermore, this research demonstrates the effectiveness of machine learning models in predicting conservative treatment failures among thoracolumbar burst fracture patients. The findings showcase the capability of these models to anticipate clinical outcomes within this patient population, offering valuable prognostic insights for improved management. Notably, the Support Vector Machine model exhibited the highest level of predictive accuracy, underscoring its potential value in clinical decision-making to identify at-risk patients and enhance management strategies.
