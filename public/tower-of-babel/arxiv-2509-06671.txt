Introduction

In this work, we continue an investigation of the critical exponent for global solutions for a class of semilinear evolution equations with fractional damping and a nonlinear memory term. More precisely, we consider the following Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}u_{tt}-\Delta u_{tt}+\Delta^{2}u-\Delta u+(-\Delta)^{\theta}u_{t}=F(t,u),\,\,\,\,\,t\geq 0,\,\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.\\
(u,u_{t})(0,x)=(u_{0},u_{1})(x),\end{cases}

(P)

with
\theta\in[0,\frac{1}{2}),\gamma\in(0,1)
and
p>1,
where the operator

F(t,u)=\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\gamma}|u(s,x)|^{p}\,\textrm{d}s

is referred to as the nonlinear memory of the function
u
.

For the whole text, the fractional Laplace operator, denoted by
(-\Delta)^{\theta}
in the damping term of
(
P
)
is to be understood as the unique extension to the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}
of tempered distributions, of the operator

(-\Delta)^{\theta}f=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(|\xi|^{2\theta}\hat{f}\right),

where
\hat{f}=\mathcal{F}f
denotes the Fourier transform of
f,

\mathcal{F}f(\xi)=\hat{f}(\xi)=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}|f(x)|\,\,\textrm{d}x,\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

For
critical exponent
we understand a threshold value
\overline{p}:=\overline{p}(n,\gamma,\theta),

\overline{p}>1,
such that

•

If
p>\overline{p},
then there exist Global in-time Small Data Solutions to (
P
), for a suitable choice of initial data and adequate solution spaces.

•

If
1<p<\overline{p},
then there can be found arbitrarily small initial data such that there is no global in-time solution to (
P
).

We observe that
(-\Delta)^{\theta}
is nonnegative and self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
Therefore, its square root,
(-\Delta)^{\frac{\theta}{2}}
, is well-defined, and also self-adjoint. If one considers the linear associated problem to (
P
), that is,

\begin{cases}u_{tt}-\Delta u_{tt}+\Delta^{2}u-\Delta u+(-\Delta)^{\theta}u_{t}=0,\,\,\,\,\,t\geq 0,\,\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.\\
(u,u_{t})(0,x)=(u_{0},u_{1})(x),\end{cases}

(LP)

by formally taking the inner product in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
u_{t},
it becomes clear that

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E(t)+2\left\lVert(-\Delta)^{\frac{\theta}{2}}u_{t}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}=0,

(1)

where the associated energy to the problem
E(t)
is

E(t):=\left(\left\lVert u_{t}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert\nabla u_{t}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert\Delta u\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}\right).

(2)

Therefore, the natural space for solutions to problems
(
P
)
and
(
LP
)
is

u\in\mathcal{C}\left([0,\infty),H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\right)\cap\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,\infty),H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\right).

;

Historical Review

We start reviewing some known historical results concerning the critical exponent, and existence or non-existence of solutions for the supercritical and subcritical cases, respectively.

In his pioneer work in 1966, H. Fujita
[
10
]
proved that the critical exponent for the semilinear heat equation

u_{t}-\Delta u=u^{p}

(3)

is
p_{F}:=1+\frac{2}{n}.
This is known as the
Fujita exponent.
In 2001, Todorova and Yordanov
[
34
]
proved that the critical exponent is still
p_{F}
for the semilinear heat equation with nonlinearity
F(u)=|u|^{p},
that is,

u_{t}-\Delta u=|u|^{p}.

(NLH)

In 2003, Zhang
[
35
]
proved the nonexistence for the critical case for (
NLH
).

In 1968, Strauss
[
30
]
investigated the semilinear wave equation in 3 dimensions, obtaining another type of critical exponent. This critical exponent was later generalized for higher dimensions by Glassey
[
12
]
,
[
13
]
. Glassey showed that the critical exponent for the problem

u_{tt}-\Delta u=|u|^{p}

(NLW)

is
p_{S},
the larger root of the quadratic equation

(n-1)p^{2}-(n+1)p-2=0.

This is now known as the
Strauss exponent.
In the following years, several mathematicians have studied semilinear problems of the form

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{j}u}{\mathrm{d}t^{j}}=Hu+Pu,\qquad j=1,2

(4)

where
H
is a differential operator acting in the spatial coordinates and
P=P(u,p)
is a multiplication by a “potential” function depending on the unknown function
u
and the exponent
p
. This much more general equation can be reduced to several interesting models in physics (see
[
31
]
), for example the nonlinear Klein-Gordon equation

u_{tt}-\Delta u+m^{2}u+h(u)=0,\qquad m>0,\,\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(NLKG)

where
h
satisfies the following condition

|h^{\prime}(s)|\lesssim|s|^{p-1}.

(5)

The same model can be applied to the nonlinear Schrödinger Equation

iu_{t}-\Delta u+h(|u|)\arg(u),

(NLS)

where
h
satisfies (
5
). Another important application of the same model is the Generalized Korteweg-deVries equation

u_{t}+u_{xxx}+h(u)_{x}=0,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(GKdV)

Each one of these models have been studied thoroughly, and obtained results have been critical exponents that are either generalizations or shifts of
p_{F}
or
p_{S}.
Only in 2014, Cazenave, Dickstein and Weissler
[
2
]
encountered a completely different kind of critical exponent. Investigating the heat equation with a memory term for nonlinearity,

u_{t}-\Delta u=\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\gamma}|u|^{p-1}u(s)\,\,\textrm{d}s,

(6)

the critical exponent found was
p_{\star}=\max\{p_{\gamma},\gamma^{-1}\},
where

p_{\gamma}=1+\dfrac{4-2\gamma}{(n-2+2\gamma)_{+}}

is a Fujita-type exponent. The same kind of result, a competition between a Fujita-type exponent and
\gamma^{-1},
was obtained for the damped wave equation with the same nonlinearity by D’Abbicco
[
5
]
in 2014.

Turning attention to the non-existence of solutions for the subcritical case, the test function method was introduced by Mitidieri and Pohozaev
[
22
]
,
[
23
]
,
[
24
]
in 1998. After this, the method was employed in several works. A small (and far from exhaustive) list of such results follows: D’Ambrosio-Reissig
[
8
]
, Fujiwara-Ozawa
[
11
]
, Ikeda-Inui
[
14
]
,
Lai-Zhou
[
17
]
and Lin-Nishihara
[
20
]
.

Finally, it is worth noticing that the critical case is very sensitive, in some sense. In fact, even for the classical heat equation, nonexistence of solutions was proved for the critical case only two years after the critical exponent was found, and the techniques applied were quite different from the ones used in the subcritical and supercritical cases. Also, although many works concerning the critical case conclude the non-existence of solutions for
p=p_{c},
it is worth noticing that Ebert, Da Luz and Palma
[
9
]
showed in their work in 2020 that there exist Global Solutions for the critical case, if the initial data has more regularity.

The study of fourth-order evolution partial differential equations is very important for modelling problems in solid mechanics, such as large deflections in thin plates. In this sense, it is known (as one can see in
[
16
]
) that the
full von Karmán dynamical model
, namely

\begin{cases}U_{tt}=\textrm{Div}\left(\mathcal{C}\left[e(U)+f(\nabla w\right)]\right)&\textrm{in }\Omega\times(0,\infty)\\
w_{tt}+\Delta^{2}w-\Delta w_{tt}=\textrm{div}\left(\mathcal{C}\left[e(U)+f(\nabla w\right)]\nabla w\right)&\textrm{in }\Omega\times(0,\infty),\end{cases}

where
\mathcal{C}
is a symmetrical
2\times 2
matrix defined as

C[e]=\dfrac{E}{d(1-\mu^{2})}[\mu(\mathrm{Tr}e)I+(1-\mu)e],

describes the deflections of a
2D
plate occuppying the domain
\Omega,
where
U=(u,v)
represents the in-plane displacement of the plate, and
w
represents the vertical displacement of the plate. This model has been studied by several authors; in particular by Ciarlet
[
3
]
, Sánches
[
28
]
, Lasiecka
[
18
]
, Lasiecka-Benabdallah
[
19
]
, Koch-Lasiecka
[
15
]
, and Puel-Tucsnak
[
26
]
. In 2000, Perla and Zuazua
[
21
]
showed that the much simpler
Timoshenko’s model
for the vertical displacement
w,

w_{tt}+\Delta^{2}w-\Delta w_{tt}-c\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}\mathrm{d}A\right)\Delta w=0,\,\,\,\textrm{in }\Omega\times(0,\infty),

may be obtained as the weak
∗
limit of the von Karmán system when some parameters tend to zero. The term
-\Delta w_{tt}
appears to absorb the rotational inertia effects at the point
x
of the plate. The term
(-\Delta)^{\theta}u_{t}
represents a fractional dissipation of energy, which becomes clear in (
1
). The Timoshenko model with rotational inertia and fractional dissipation then is written as

u_{tt}+\Delta^{2}u-\Delta u_{tt}+(-\Delta)^{\theta}u_{t}=0.

(7)

In the model studied in this work, the choice of the memory nonlinearity term is due to theoretical reasons explained before, and the term
-\Delta u
is added to avoid singularity problems at the origin. The existence of solutions for the problem without this term is more difficult and it is still an open problem.

We are interested into find nonexistence results for global in-time solutions for the subcritical case. In
[
1
]
, global existence is proved for dimension
n\leq 4.
In particular, for
n\leq 3,
in the case
\theta\in\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right),

\gamma\in(0,1)
and
p>\overline{p},
the critical exponent is
\overline{p}=\max\left\{p_{c},\gamma^{-1}\right\},
where
p_{c}=p_{c}(n,\gamma,\theta)
is given by

p_{c}(n,\gamma,\theta):=1+\frac{2\left(1+(1-\gamma)(1-\theta)\right)}{\left(n-2+2\gamma(1-\theta)\right)_{+}},

(8)

As mentioned before, this kind of competition between two different values, the first being a Fujita-type and the second being
\gamma^{-1},
related to the nonlinear memory term, had already been noticed in precedent papers, for example
[
5
]
,
[
2
]
. However, for dimension
n\geq 3
a new factor comes into play. The possibility of
p<2,
a scenario in which
L^{p}-L^{q}
estimates are difficult to obtain, gives a worse decay rate. Even if for
n=3
this loss in decay rate is not significant enough to produce a change in the critical exponent, for
n\geq 4
the effect of this loss becomes apparent. In fact, if
\gamma
is large enough in comparison to
\theta,
a new exponent joins the competition,

\tilde{p}_{c}:=\dfrac{6n+4-4(n+1)\theta}{2s+n(3-2\theta)-4(1-\gamma)(1-\theta),}

(9)

where
s
is the regularity of initial data,
(u_{0},u_{1})\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{s-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
Therefore, for
n\geq 4,
the critical exponent becomes

\overline{p}=\max\{\gamma^{-1},p_{c},\tilde{p}_{c}\}.

n\geq 4

The reader should check Figure

to see the small region where
\tilde{p}_{c}>p_{c}.
The figure illustrates the case
n=4
and
\theta=\frac{1}{100}.

The goal of this work is to answer the following question: Is this new exponent optimal or is it just fruit of a suboptimal result for the decay rate when
p<2?
If one can prove nonexistence for
p<\tilde{p_{c}},
then the problem is solved, with exception, possibly, to the critical case. Otherwise, if one does not find
p<\tilde{p_{c}}
in nonexistence results, then the region colored in red in Figure
1
remains a mystery. To answer this question, we study the nonexistence of solutions in the subcritical case.

Our main result in this paper reads as follows.

Theorem 1.1

Let
p_{c}
be as in Definition (
8
), define

\overline{p}=\begin{cases}\gamma^{-1},&,\mathrm{if}\,\,\gamma\in\left(0,\frac{n-2}{n}\right]\\
p_{c},&\mathrm{if}\,\,\gamma\in\left(\frac{n-2}{n},1\right)\end{cases}

and fix
q=n+2\theta.
Assume that
u_{0},u_{1}\in L^{1}(\langle x\rangle^{q}dx).
Moreover, assume the sign condition

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u_{1}\,\,\textrm{d}x>0.

If there exists a global in-time (nontrivial) weak solution

u\in L^{p}\left([0,\infty),L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle x\rangle^{-q})\right)

to problem (
P
), then
p\geq\overline{p};

Furthermore, in the case
\gamma\in\left(\frac{n-2}{n},1\right)
the inequality is strict, that is,
p>\overline{p}=p_{c}.

2
Test Functions and Weak Solutions

In this work, we find global in-time solutions to the problem (
P
) for the supercritical case, for a specific
critical exponent
candidate. The sharpness of such candidate is achieved by showing nonexistence of global solutions in the subcritical case.

This nonexistence counterpart is usually derived by using a classic test function method. However, since the fractional Laplace operator has a nonlocal behavior, such method is not applicable (at least directly), since it relies on the compactness of the test functions’ support under the action of these operators.

Having this in mind, we use a modified test function to obtain our results. To deal with the nonlocality of the fractional Laplace operators, we will replace the usual compactly supported test functions by some suitable test functions with polynomial decay. To this end, we introduce a class of functions and a definition of a weak solution to problem (
P
) which is adequate to our purposes.

Definition 2.1

Let
\theta\in(0,1)
be a fixed number, and fix
q=n+2\theta.
We define the space
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as the subspace of infinitely differentiable functions
\varphi
such that
\left\langle x\right\rangle^{q}\varphi
is bounded, and for any
\sigma>0
with
\sigma
integer or
\sigma-\lfloor\sigma\rfloor\in[\theta,1),
the function
\left\langle x\right\rangle^{q}(-\Delta)^{\sigma}\varphi
is also bounded.

The choice of this specific space
C^{\infty}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),
with
q=n+2\theta
is justified by the following lemma and its corollary. With these, we may show that a function in
C^{\infty}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
remains in the same space after the action of the Fractional Laplace operator
(-\Delta)^{\theta}.
First, we recall the definition of the fractional Laplace operator and an alternative definition for non-integer powers, that is equivalent with the usual definition when the domain is the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Definition 2.2

For any
\sigma>0,
we may define the fractional Laplace operator
(-\Delta)^{\sigma}:H^{2\sigma}\to L^{2}
as

(-\Delta)^{\sigma}f=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(|\xi|^{2\sigma}\hat{f}\right).

(10)

If
\sigma
is not integer, then the operator
(-\Delta)^{\sigma}
admits an integral representation. For
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
let
\tau_{y}
be the translation operator, i.e.,

\tau_{y}f(x)=f(x+y).

(11)

Then the identity

(-\Delta)^{\sigma}f(x)=(-1)^{\lfloor\sigma\rfloor+1}C_{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(\tau_{\frac{y}{2}}-\tau_{-\frac{y}{2}})^{2\lfloor\sigma\rfloor+2}f(x)}{|y|^{n+2\sigma}}\penalty\ \textrm{d}y

(12)

holds for any
f\in H^{2\sigma}.
Also, the constant
C_{\sigma}
is given by

C_{\sigma}=2^{-2\lfloor\sigma\rfloor-2+2\sigma}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(y_{1})^{2\lfloor\sigma\rfloor+2}}{|y|^{n+2\sigma}}\penalty\ \textrm{d}y\right)^{-1}>0.

Remark 2.3

The identity given in Definition
2.2
is shown in
[
25
]
.

The fractional Laplace operator can be conveniently extended to more general spaces. In particular, it may be extended by duality to the tempered distribution space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}.
The following Lemma and its Corrolary ensure that a bounded function with bounded derivatives can be controlled under the action of the fractional Laplace operator.

Lemma 2.4

Assume
f\in C^{2}
bounded, with bounded derivatives. If there exists a constant
C_{0}
such that the estimates

|f(y)|\leq C_{0}|f(x)|,\,\,\,\,\sup_{|\alpha|=2}|\partial^{\alpha}f(y)|\leq C_{0}\sup_{|\alpha|=2}|\partial^{\alpha}f(x)|

hold when
|x|\leq|y|,
then for
|x|>1,
the following pointwise estimate holds:

\displaystyle|(-\Delta)^{\sigma}f(x)|

\displaystyle\leq C|x|^{-n-2\sigma}\int_{|y|<3|x|}|f(y)|\textrm{d}y+C|f(x)||x|^{-2\sigma}

\displaystyle+C|x|^{2-2\sigma}\sum_{|\alpha|=2}\frac{|\alpha|}{\alpha!}\big|\partial^{\alpha}f\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right)\big|,

(13)

for any
\sigma\in(0,1).

Proof:
See
[
6
]
.

As a consequence of Lemma
2.4
, one can derive the following corollary, which bounds the action of the fractional Laplace operator on
\langle x\rangle^{-q}
pointwisely.

Corollary 2.5

Let
f(x)=\langle x\rangle^{-q}
, for
q>n,
and let
\sigma>0.
We set
s=\sigma-\lfloor\sigma\rfloor
. Then,

\big|(-\Delta)^{\sigma}f(x)\big|\leq C\langle x\rangle^{-q_{\sigma}},\qquad\forall\penalty\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
q_{\sigma}=q+2\sigma
if
\sigma
is an integer, or
q=n+2s
otherwise.

Proof:
We start with the following identity

\displaystyle-\Delta f(x)

\displaystyle=q\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{x_{j}}\left(x_{j}\langle x\rangle^{-q-2}\right)

\displaystyle=q(q+2-n)\langle x\rangle^{-q-2}+q(q+2)\langle x\rangle^{-q-4}

\displaystyle:=c_{0}\langle x\rangle^{-q-2}+c_{1}\langle x\rangle^{-q-2-2},

with
c_{0},c_{1}
depending only on
n,\sigma,q.
After
\lfloor\sigma\rfloor
iterations of this identity, we get

(-\Delta)^{\lfloor\sigma\rfloor}f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\sigma\rfloor}c_{k}\langle x\rangle^{-q-2\lfloor\sigma\rfloor-2k},

(14)

for some
c_{k}=c_{k}(n,\sigma,q)\in\mathbb{R}.

Assuming that
\sigma
is integer, then
\lfloor\sigma\rfloor=\sigma
and
s=0.
Therefore, (
14
) implies

\Big|(-\Delta)^{\sigma}f(x)\Big|\leq C\langle x\rangle^{-q-2\sigma},

which is precisely what we needed to prove in this case. Next, assume
\sigma
non-integer, that is,
s\in(0,1).
We have

\displaystyle\Big|(-\Delta)^{\sigma}f(x)\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|(-\Delta)^{s}\left((-\Delta)^{\lfloor\sigma\rfloor}f(x)\right)\Big|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|(-\Delta)^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\sigma\rfloor}c_{k}\langle x\rangle^{-q-2\lfloor\sigma\rfloor-2k}\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\sigma\rfloor}|c_{k}|\penalty\ \Big|(-\Delta)^{s}\langle x\rangle^{-q-2\lfloor\sigma\rfloor-2k}\Big|.

(15)

Then, applying Lemma
2.4
for each
g(x)=\langle x\rangle^{-q-2\lfloor\sigma\rfloor-2k}
with
k=0,...\lfloor\sigma\rfloor
(plugging
s
in the place of
\sigma
), we obtain the estimates

\displaystyle\Big|(-\Delta)^{s}\langle x\rangle^{-q-2\lfloor\sigma\rfloor-2k}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C|x|^{-n-2s}\int_{|y|<3|x|}|g(y)|\textrm{d}y+C|g(y)||x|^{-2s}

\displaystyle+C|x|^{-2s+2}\sum_{|\alpha|=2}\big|\partial^{\alpha}g\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right)\big|,

for every
|x|>1.
Now, since
g
and
\partial^{\alpha}g
are bounded, we must show only that the integral on the right-hand side is bounded. But this is a consequence of
\langle x\rangle^{-a}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
a>n.
Indeed,

\displaystyle\int_{|y|<3|x|}|g(y)|\penalty\ \textrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|g(y)|\penalty\ \textrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|y|<1}|g(y)|\penalty\ \textrm{d}y+\int_{|y|>1}|g(y)|\penalty\ \textrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|y|<1}\textrm{d}y+\int_{|y|>1}|y|^{-a}\textrm{d}y<+\infty,\penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ a>n.

Since for
|x|>1
we have
|x|\sim\langle x\rangle,
we get the estimates

\Big|(-\Delta)^{s}\langle x\rangle^{-q-2\lfloor\sigma\rfloor-2k}\Big|\leq C\langle x\rangle^{-n-2s},

(16)

for
k=0,1,...\lfloor\sigma\rfloor.

Therefore, using (
16
) in (
2
), we obtain

\Big|(-\Delta)^{\sigma}f(x)\Big|\leq C\langle x\rangle^{-n-2s},

(17)

which concludes our proof.

Definition 2.7

Fix

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\psi(t)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

Remark 2.8

\displaystyle=\Gamma(\alpha)\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\omega_{T}(t)^{\beta}|u(t,x)|^{p}\varphi_{R}(x)\,\,\textrm{d}x\,\,\textrm{d}t

\displaystyle:=\Gamma(\alpha)I(u).

On the other hand, we estimate the five terms in the right-hand side. To do so, we integrate in
[0,T]
in time and in
B_{R}
in space, and after that we control the speeds at which we let

and

We recall here the following estimates:

|\partial_{t}^{j}\psi_{T}(t)|\leq CT^{-\alpha-j}\omega_{T}(t)^{\beta-\alpha-j},\,j=0,1,2,\qquad|((-\Delta)^{\sigma}\varphi_{R})(x)|\leq CR^{-2\sigma}|\varphi_{R}(x)|.

Hence, we can apply Lemma

to bound all the five integrals, obtaining:

•

\Bigg|\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\psi_{T}(t)\int_{B_{R}}u(t,x)(-\Delta\varphi_{R})(x)\,\textrm{d}x\,\,\textrm{d}t\Bigg|\leq\varepsilon I(u)+C_{\varepsilon}T^{1-\alpha p^{\prime}}R^{n-2p^{\prime}}

(40)

•

\Bigg|\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\psi_{T}(t)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(t,x)(\Delta^{2}\varphi_{R})(x)\,\,\textrm{d}x\,\,\textrm{d}t\Bigg|\leq\varepsilon I(u)+C_{\varepsilon}T^{1-\alpha p^{\prime}}R^{n-4p^{\prime}}

(41)

•

\Bigg|\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}\psi_{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u((-\Delta)^{\theta}\varphi_{R})\,\,\textrm{d}x\,\,\textrm{d}t\Bigg|\leq\varepsilon I(u)+C_{\varepsilon}T^{1-(\alpha+1)p^{\prime}}R^{n-2\theta p^{\prime}}

(42)

•

\Bigg|\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{tt}\psi_{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u\varphi_{R}\,\,\textrm{d}x\,\,\textrm{d}t\Bigg|\leq\varepsilon I(u)+C_{\varepsilon}T^{1-(\alpha+2)p^{\prime}}R^{n}

(43)

•

\Bigg|\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{tt}\psi_{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(-\Delta\varphi_{R})\,\,\textrm{d}x\,\,\textrm{d}t\Bigg|\leq\varepsilon I(u)+C_{\varepsilon}T^{1-(\alpha+2)p^{\prime}}R^{n-2p^{\prime}}

(44)

\displaystyle(\Gamma(\alpha)-5\varepsilon)

\displaystyle<C_{\varepsilon}\Big(T^{1-\alpha p^{\prime}}R^{n-2p^{\prime}}+T^{1-\alpha p^{\prime}}R^{n-4p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+T^{1-(\alpha+1)p^{\prime}}R^{n-2\theta p^{\prime}}+T^{1-(\alpha+2)p^{\prime}}R^{n}+T^{1-(\alpha+2)p^{\prime}}R^{n-2p^{\prime}}\Big).

(45)

Choosing

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{B_{R}}\omega_{T}(t)^{\beta}

\displaystyle|u(t,x)|^{p}\varphi_{R}(x)\,\textrm{d}x\,\,\textrm{d}t

\displaystyle<C_{\alpha,\varepsilon}\Big(T^{1-\alpha p^{\prime}}R^{n-2p^{\prime}}+T^{1-\alpha p^{\prime}}R^{n-4p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt+T^{1-(\alpha+1)p^{\prime}}R^{n-2\theta p^{\prime}}+T^{1-(\alpha+2)p^{\prime}}R^{n}+T^{1-(\alpha+2)p^{\prime}}R^{n-2p^{\prime}}\Big).

(46)

Now, we divide into three cases:

1.

\gamma>\frac{n-2}{n}
and
p<p_{c};

2.

\gamma>\frac{n-2}{n}
and
p=p_{c},
the critical case;

3.

\gamma\leq\frac{n-2}{n}
and

1.

First consider the case where
\gamma>\frac{n-2}{n}.
We stress that this is always true for
n=1,2.
For
\eta\geq 0,
we set
T=R^{\eta}.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R^{\eta}}\int_{B_{R}}\omega_{T}(t)^{\beta}|u(t,x)|^{p}\varphi_{R}(x)\,\textrm{d}x\,\,\textrm{d}t

\displaystyle<C_{\alpha,\varepsilon}\Big(R^{-(\alpha\eta+2)p^{\prime}+n+\eta}+R^{-(\alpha\eta+4)p^{\prime}n+\eta}

\displaystyle+R^{-((\alpha+1)\eta+2\theta)p^{\prime}+n+\eta}+R^{-(\alpha+2)\eta p^{\prime}+n+\eta}

\displaystyle+R^{-((\alpha+2)\eta+2)p^{\prime}+n+\eta}\Big).

(47)

Now, we define, for
\eta\geq 0,

\displaystyle g(\eta)

\displaystyle=\min\big\{\alpha\eta+2,\alpha\eta+4,(\alpha+1)\eta+2\theta,(\alpha+2)\eta,(\alpha+2)\eta+2\big\}

\displaystyle=\min\big\{\alpha\eta+2,(\alpha+1)\eta+2\theta,(\alpha+2)\eta\big\}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(3-\gamma)\eta,&\mathrm{if\,\,\,}\eta\in[0,2\theta]\\
(2-\gamma)\eta+2\theta,&\mathrm{if\,\,\,}\eta\in(2\theta,2(1-\theta)],\\
(1-\gamma)\eta+2,&\mathrm{if\,\,\,}\eta\in(2(1-\theta),\infty).\end{cases}

(48)

where in the last step we used that
\alpha=1-\gamma.
This function
g(\eta)
gives the fastest possible decay for which we can control all five terms in (
1
). Below, we see a graphical representation of it.

Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R^{\eta}}\int_{B_{R}}\omega_{T}(t)^{\beta}|u(t,x)|^{p}\varphi_{R}(x)\,\textrm{d}x\,\,\textrm{d}t

\displaystyle<5C_{\alpha,\varepsilon}R^{-g(\eta)p^{\prime}+n+\eta}.

(49)

To conclude the argument, it is sufficient that the exponent above is negative. This will happen if

\displaystyle g(\eta)p^{\prime}-(n+\eta)>0,

(50)

or equivalently,

\displaystyle p^{\prime}>\frac{n+\eta}{g(\eta)}\iff p<\frac{n+\eta}{n+\eta-g(\eta)}=1+\frac{g(\eta)}{n+\eta-g(\eta)}:=h(\eta).

(51)

In particular, for
\eta=2(1-\theta),
we have

\displaystyle h(2(1-\theta))

\displaystyle=1+\frac{2(1-\gamma)(1-\theta)+2}{n+2(1-\theta)-2(1-\gamma)(1-\theta)-2}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{2+2(1-\gamma)(1-\theta)}{n-2+2\gamma(1-\theta)}=p_{c}.

(52)

Moreover, we can see that

is the greatest value for which we can prove nonexistence in this case. In other words,
h(\eta)

assuming that
\gamma>\frac{n-2}{n}.

Indeed, since

h^{\prime}(\eta)=\frac{-g(\eta)+(n+\eta)g^{\prime}(\eta)}{(n+\eta-g(\eta))^{2}},

its sign is determined by the sign of its numerator
-g(\eta)+(n+\eta)g^{\prime}(\eta),
which is given by

\mathrm{sgn}(-g(\eta)+(n+\eta)g^{\prime}(\eta))=\begin{cases}n(3-\gamma)>0,&\,\,\mathrm{if\,\,}\eta\in(0,2\theta)\\
(2-\gamma)-2\theta>0&\,\,\mathrm{if\,\,}\eta\in[2\theta,2(1-\theta))\\
-2+(1-\gamma)n,&\,\,\mathrm{if\,\,}\eta\in(2(1-\theta),\infty),\end{cases}

and in the last case, we have

-2+(1-\gamma)n<0\iff\gamma>\frac{n-2}{n}.

Since
h(\eta)
is continuous by parts, it has a local maximum point at
\eta=2(1-\theta)
when
\gamma>\frac{n-2}{n}.
