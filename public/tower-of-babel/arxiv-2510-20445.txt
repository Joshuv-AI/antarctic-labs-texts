Mitigating Coherent Errors through a Decoherence-Resistant Variational Framework employing Stabilizer States Preprint: APS/123-QED
Giovanni Di Bartolomeo ⋄, Giulio Crognaletti ⋄, Angelo Bassi, Michele Vischi

Abstract
Stabilizer states are a central resource in quantum information processing, underpinning a wide range of applications. While they can be efficiently generated via Clifford circuits, the presence of coherent errors—such as small-angle miscalibrations in native gate implementations—can significantly impact their quality. In this work, we introduce Variational Coherent Error Mitigation (VCEM), a method that employs the stabilizer formalism to suppress coherent errors through variational optimization of native gates parameters. VCEM demonstrates robust performance, remaining largely unaffected by incoherent noise, enabling pre-compensation of coherent errors prior to the application of standard incoherent error mitigation techniques. We demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of VCEM through numerical simulations.

Although stabilizer states can, in principle, be generated using Clifford circuits—operations that are efficiently classically simulable

Most developed techniques focus on reducing the effects of incoherent errors, the ones arising from the interaction of a quantum device with its surrounding environment
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
;
Benenti et al. 2019
. Such errors break unitarity and corrupt any quantum information processing task. However, another important class of errors are coherent ones, i.e. small angle miscalibrations leading to systematic over- or under-rotation errors in the native gates of a quantum device. Even when these errors are small, their effect both on physical and logical qubits can be significant
Wallman et al. 2015
;
Ball et al. 2016
;
Kueng et al. 2016
;
Wallman 2016
. In QEC coherent errors are typically ignored because, when the decoder is optimized for correcting independent incoherent Pauli errors, the coherent contribution to the logical error is negligible at fewer than
\epsilon^{-(n-1)}
error correction cycles, where
\epsilon
is the order of magnitude of coherent errors and
n
is the number of physical qubits
Greenbaum and Dutton 2017
;
Huang et al. 2019
;
Bravyi et al. 2018
. Above this number of correction cycles, the coherent logical error causes logical failure faster than the incoherent one, and this have to be counteracted with efficient coherent error mitigation methods at the physical level. This reflects the current status of quantum computation which is expected to persist in the near- to mid-term future.

Few techniques focusing on coherent Quantum Error Mitigation (QEM) are present in the literature

;
Cai et al. 2023
;
Campbell 2017
;
Endo et al. 2018
;
Yoshioka et al. 2022
;
Orsucci et al. 2016
.

In this work, by exploiting the stabilizerness we derive an efficient error mitigation technique based on the variational principle named Variational Coherent Error Mitigation (VCEM).
First the Clifford circuit generating the stabilizer state is expressed in terms of the native gates of a quantum device, then each of such native gate is parametrized. The optimization of a suitable cost function based on the stabilizer operators allows to find the optimal parameters that cancel coherent errors. While the natural application of VCEM is the correction of coherent errors in stabilizer-state preparation, since VCEM operates at the level of native gates, it is applicable as a general-purpose technique to correct coherent errors.

Moreover, we show how VCEM remains robust and effective even in the presence of realistic incoherent errors. Although incoherent errors modify the cost function, it is still possible to find optimal parameters based on the properties of the Clifford circuit and the noise channels.

This fact leads to the main advantage of our technique: coherent errors can be first corrected by VCEM without taking care of incoherent ones. Subsequently, any circuit can run with optimal parameters and it could be paired with incoherent QEM techniques
Temme et al. 2017
;
Van Den Berg et al. 2023
;
Filippov et al. 2023
;
Bultrini et al. 2023
.

We show the effectiveness of VCEM with accurate numerical simulations.

II
Coherent errors on stabilizer states

Consider
\mathcal{\bf{P}}_{n}
the Pauli group on
n
qubits. An
n
-qubit state
\ket{\psi_S}
is called a stabilizer state if there exists a subgroup
\mathcal{\bf{S}}_{n}\subset\mathcal{\bf{P}}_{n}
of dimension
2^{n}
and such that

\hat{S}_{i}\ket{\psi_S}=\ket{\psi_S}\,\ ,

(1)

for every generator
\hat{S}_{i}
of
\mathcal{\bf{S}}_{n}
with
i=1,\dots,n

Arab 2024
.
The
\hat{S}_{i}
operators are called stabilizers, they are Pauli strings and have eigenvalues
\pm 1
with degeneracy
2^{n-1}
:

\displaystyle\hat{S_{i}}|\psi_{ik}^{(+)}\rangle=|\psi_{ik}^{(+)}\rangle\,\,\forall k=1,\dots,2^{n-1}

(2)

\displaystyle\hat{S_{i}}|\psi_{ik}^{(-)}\rangle=-|\psi_{ik}^{(-)}\rangle\,\,\forall k=1,\dots,2^{n-1}

(3)

where the eigenvectors
|\psi_{ik}^{(\pm)}\rangle
are orthonormal
\langle\psi_{ik}^{(\pm)}|\psi_{ik^{\prime}}^{(\pm)}\rangle=\delta_{kk^{\prime}}
.

According to
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
, it is always possible to identify a Clifford circuit
\hat{U}_{S}
, namely a quantum circuit composed of Clifford gates only, such that
\ket{\psi_S}=\hat{U}_{S}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
.

In presence of even small amount of coherent errors, the state
\ket{\psi_S}
becomes non stabilized. Coherent errors can be described as unwanted small extra rotations in the native gates of a quantum device resulting from gates miscalibration. In what follows, we assume that each gate in
\hat{U}_{S}
has its own small coherent error which is random but stable (constant), at least during the entire running time. Our aim is to correct for such errors.

Any stabilizer state can be expanded as a superposition of the
|\psi_{ik}^{(\pm)}\rangle
eigenvectors for each
\hat{S}_{i}

|\psi_{S}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n-1}}\Bigl(a_{ik}|\psi_{ik}^{(+)}\rangle+b_{ik}|\psi_{ik}^{(-)}\rangle\Bigr)\,\ ,

(4)

where
a_{ik}
are generic amplitudes and
b_{ik}=0
for every
k=1,\dots,2^{n-1}
.
In the presence of coherent errors the state is modified as
\ket{\psi_S(\vec{\epsilon})}=\hat{U}_{S}(\vec{\epsilon})\ket{0}^{{\otimes}n}
where
\vec{\epsilon}
is a vector of small errors and
\hat{U}_{S}(\vec{\epsilon})
is in general non-Clifford.
Analogously to Eq. (
4
), the modified state can be written as

|\psi_{S}(\vec{\epsilon})\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n-1}}\Bigl(a_{ik}(\vec{\epsilon})|\psi_{ik}^{(+)}\rangle+b_{ik}(\vec{\epsilon})|\psi_{ik}^{(-)}\rangle\Bigr)\,\ ,

(5)

where
\sum_{k}(|a_{ik}(\vec{\epsilon})|^{2}+|b_{ik}(\vec{\epsilon})|^{2})=1
. In general,
|\psi_{S}(\vec{\epsilon})\rangle
is not a stabilizer state, because the
b_{ik}(\vec{\epsilon})
might be different from zero. In the following we assume that for any small perturbations
|\vec{\epsilon}|\ll 1
at least one
|b_{ik}(\vec{\epsilon})|^{2}>0
such that
|\psi_{S}(\vec{\epsilon})\rangle
is not a stabilizer state.

III
Variational coherent error mitigation (VCEM)

In order to correct coherent errors one can parametrize the circuit
\hat{U}_{S}(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon})
where
\vec{\theta}
is a vector of variational parameters. The idea is to transpile the circuit into the native gates of a given quantum device and parametrize their rotation angles. The resulting state
|\psi_{S}(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon})\rangle=\hat{U}_{S}(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon})\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
can again be expanded analogously to Eq. (
5
) as

|\psi_{S}(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon})\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n-1}}\Bigl(a_{ik}(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon})|\psi_{ik}^{(+)}\rangle+b_{ik}(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon})|\psi_{ik}^{(-)}\rangle\Bigr)\,.

(6)

We can define the following cost function

C(\vec{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}(\vec{\theta})=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\psi_{S}(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon})|\hat{S}_{i}|\psi_{S}(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon})\rangle\,,

(7)

and we prove that by using Eq. (
6
) this quantity can be optimized with the variational principle, i.e. it has global minimum:

\displaystyle C_{i}(\vec{\theta})=-\langle\psi_{S}(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon})|\hat{S}_{i}|\psi_{S}(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon})\rangle

(8)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k}\big(|a_{ik}(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon})|^{2}-|b_{ik}(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon})|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k}\big(|a_{ik}(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon})|^{2}+|b_{ik}(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon})|^{2}\big)+2\sum_{k}|b_{ik}(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon})|^{2}

\displaystyle=-1+2\sum_{k}|b_{ik}(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon})|^{2}\geq-1\,,

by plugging Eq. (
8
) into Eq. (
7
), one obtains

C(\vec{\theta})\geq-n\,\ .

(9)

The global minimum value of the cost function
-n
is exactly obtained when the state is the stabilizer state.
In other words, assuming that
|\vec{\epsilon}|
is small, one can start the optimization in
\vec{\theta}=0
, that is equivalent to optimize the cost function in a region
\mathcal{B}(-\vec{\epsilon},\delta)
for
\delta\ll 1
, and such that
0\in\mathcal{B}
, close to the optimal values where the cost function is convex, i.e. the Hessian
\mathbf{H}=\partial_{\theta_{k}}\partial_{\theta_{l}}C(\vec{\theta})|_{\vec{\theta}=-\vec{\epsilon}}
is positive definite. This excludes trainability issues such as barren plateaus
Larocca et al. 2025
in the ideal unitary case, consistently with the literature
Puig et al. 2025
. In Sec.
IV
, we show how the same claim continues to hold also in the presence of incoherent errors.

By finding the minimum of the cost function the resulting optimal parameters
\vec{\theta}_{opt}
are such that
\vec{\theta}_{opt}=-\vec{\epsilon}
and coherent errors are corrected. When
\vec{\theta}_{opt}
is obtained, the corrected circuit is such that
\hat{U}_{S}(\vec{\theta}_{opt}+\vec{\epsilon})=\hat{U}_{S}
. We name this technique Variational Coherent Error Mitigation (VCEM). A pictorial representation of the VCEM circuit is shown in Fig.
1
. For a simple analytical example see appendix
B
.

Figure 1:
Parametrized circuit to evaluate the VCEM cost function.

The natural application of VCEM is the correction of coherent errors arising in the preparation of a stabilizer state. However, the method has broader applicability, as the error mitigation is performed at the level of native gates acting on physical qubits. This enables its use as a general-purpose technique, independent of the specific stabilizer state initially considered. In this more general setting, the stabilizer state can, in principle, be freely chosen so as to optimize the correction of coherent errors.

A gate-by-gate correction strategy is generally impractical, since the number of required variational optimizations scales with both the number of qubits and the number of connected qubit pairs. VCEM becomes advantageous when a single, representative stabilizer state is selected, allowing all relevant variational parameters to be incorporated and optimized simultaneously. The choice of stabilizer state can be tailored to the target application: for instance, in simulations of many-body quantum systems, one might select a stabilizer state reflecting the system’s topology—such as spin lattices or molecular structures. Another practical choice is the graph state associated with the connectivity of the specific quantum device.

IV
VCEM in presence of incoherent errors

In the previous section we assumed the presence of coherent errors only, here we show that under standard assumptions VCEM can still be effective in correcting coherent errors also in presence of incoherent ones. Incoherent errors arise from unwanted interaction with the environment
Krantz et al. 2019
;
Breuer et al. 2002
and in general their effects can be described through quantum maps
Benenti et al. 2019
;
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
. Thus from this point on we work with density matrices instead of state vectors. We define
\hat{\rho}_{S}=\ket{\psi_S}\bra{\psi_S}=\mathcal{U}_{S}\hat{\rho}_{0}
where
\mathcal{U}_{S}=\hat{U}_{S}\,\cdot\,\hat{U}_{S}^{\dagger}
,
\hat{\rho}_{0}=(\ket{0}\bra{0})^{\otimes n}
and to ease the notation we omit the dependence on variational parameters
\vec{\theta}
and coherent errors
\vec{\epsilon}
.

Moreover when needed, we divide the circuit
\mathcal{U}_{S}
in
M
moments
\mathcal{U}_{S}=\prod_{q=1}^{M}\mathcal{U}_{q}=\mathcal{U}_{M}\mathcal{U}_{M-1}\dots\mathcal{U}_{2}\mathcal{U}_{1}
, where each moment
\mathcal{U}_{q}
is a sub-circuit consisting of native gates acting simultaneously on a number of qubits
\leq n
with
q=1,\dots,M
.

V
Realistic scenarios in which VCEM is effective

Two of the main quantum algorithm subroutines based on the preparation of stabilizer states are the preparation of graph states
Hein et al. 2006
;
Hein et al. 2004
;
Shettell and Markham 2020
and GHZ states
Contreras-Tejada et al. 2019
;
D’Hondt and Panangaden 2004

Briegel et al. 2009
;
Raussendorf et al. 2003

M^{\prime}=4
and
M^{\prime}=6
. These are constant numbers independent from the number of nodes, i.e. of qubits, and this is a common property of many 2D/3D graph states and of GHZ states. When the circuit is transpiled into native gates the resulting number of moments
M
might increase as
M=\nu M^{\prime}
where
\nu
is a constant. These are practical situations where the scaling in Eq. (
26
) holds. The corresponding states, whose structure matches the hardware connectivity, constitute realistic examples of stabilizer states suitable for performing VCEM.

In order to analyze situations in which the findings of Sec.
IV
are useful, we make further assumptions on the structure of incoherent errors based on realistic features of quantum hardwares.

For real quantum devices, e.g. superconducting qubits
ibm 2025
;
goo 2025
;

\displaystyle

VII
Conclusions and outlook

This work introduces the Variational Coherent Error Mitigation (VCEM) framework, which effectively suppresses coherent errors through stabilizer-based variational optimization. VCEM operates directly on native gate parameters and remains robust even under realistic incoherent noise. Numerical results confirm its scalability and accuracy. As an outlook, we can consider a further scenario with respect to the one in Sec.
V
in which it could be possible to engineer general incoherent errors in order to be modeled as a global depolarizing map after each circuit
moment. In that case the results in Sec.
IV.2
would be applied, thus VCEM would work in presence of incoherent errors without approximations. A technique that in principle goes in that direction is Clifford twirling
Wallman and Emerson 2016
;
Cai and Benjamin 2019
;
Di Matteo 2014
, for which a general incoherent error map can be reduced to Eq. (
15
) (see appendix
G
).

However, at present this
scenario is considered less feasible in practice because circuit implementation of Clifford twirling can have a significant depth
Bravyi and Maslov 2021
and could itself introduce an unacceptable level of noise.

Looking ahead, further exploration of these kind of techniques and their practical implementation may open promising avenues to improve VCEM effectiveness enabling robust coherent error mitigation even with large-scale noisy stabilizer states.

Finally, the techinical points raised in this work are also very interesting, as they suggest that, for certain classes of parametrized quantum circuits, the location of the minimum
\vec{\theta}_{opt}
rather than the optimal value of the cost function
C(\vec{\theta}_{opt})
could be better exploited, as it is an intrinsically noise-resistant property of the model.

Appendix A
Useful properties and intermediate results

We start the section by introducing the concept of Pauli map, and later we show some of their useful properties.

Definition 1

(Pauli map)
.

A quantum channel
\mathcal{P}
acting on
n
qubits is a Pauli map if it can be decomposed as

\mathcal{P}\hat{\rho}=\sum_{j=0}^{4^{n}-1}p_{j}\hat{P}_{j}\hat{\rho}\hat{P}_{j},

(30)

where
p_{j}\geq 0
,
\sum_{j}p_{j}=1
;
\hat{P}_{j}
are Pauli strings and
\hat{\rho}
is a generic density matrix of
n
qubits.

It can be shown that any generic noise channel
\mathcal{E}
can be reduced to a Pauli map
\mathcal{P}
by a procedure called Pauli twirling (see Appendix
G
). One of the main theoretical advantages of Pauli maps, is that they are self-adjoint, i.e.
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}
. This can easily be shown by noting that each term
\hat{P}_{j}
is itself self-adjoint, i.e.
\hat{P}_{j}=\hat{P}_{j}^{\dagger}
.
Additionally, they act on Pauli strings simply by rescaling them. This idea is formalized in the following Lemma.

Lemma 1

(Action of Pauli maps on Pauli strings)
.

Given a Pauli map
\mathcal{P}
and a Pauli string
\hat{S}
, the action
\mathcal{P}\hat{S}
is given by

\mathcal{P}\hat{S}=\chi\hat{S},

(31)

for some
\chi\in\mathbb{R}
,
|\chi|\leq 1
. In particular,
\chi=1-2\sum_{j:\{S,P_{j}\}=0}p_{j}
.

Proof.

The proof is based on the observation that any pair of Pauli strings either commutes or anti-commutes with each other. More specifically, given two Pauli strings
\hat{P}_{j}
and
\hat{S}
, then if
[\hat{P}_{j},\hat{S}]\neq 0\Rightarrow\{\hat{P}_{j},\hat{S}\}=0
and vice versa, as it can be directly verified using the commutation relations of Pauli matrices. Consequently it follows that

\hat{P}_{j}\hat{S}\hat{P}_{j}=\begin{cases}\hat{S}\quad\text{if}\quad[\hat{S},\hat{P}_{j}]=0\\
-\hat{S}\quad\text{if}\quad\{\hat{S},\hat{P}_{j}\}=0\end{cases}

(32)

By following Def.
1
, we have

\mathcal{P}\hat{S}=\sum_{j=0}^{4^{n}-1}p_{j}\hat{P}_{j}\hat{S}\hat{P}_{j}=\left(\sum_{j:[\hat{S},\hat{P}_{j}]=0}p_{j}-\sum_{j:\{\hat{S},\hat{P}_{j}\}=0}p_{j}\right)\hat{S}=\left(1-2\sum_{j:\{\hat{S},\hat{P}_{j}\}=0}p_{j}\right)\hat{S}=\chi\hat{S},

(33)

where the condition
|\chi|\leq 1
is ensured by the relation

0\leq\sum_{i:\{S,P_{j}\}=0}p_{j}\leq 1.

(34)

∎

Another useful property of Pauli maps, is that they preserve their structure under conjugation with Clifford unitaries. More specifically, we have the following Lemma.

Lemma 2

(Pauli and unitary Clifford maps)
.

Given a Pauli map
\mathcal{P}
and a Clifford unitary
\mathcal{U}
, then we have

\mathcal{U}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\;\;\text{or equivalently}\;\;\mathcal{U}\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\mathcal{U},

(35)

where the new map
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is still of the form of a Pauli map.

Proof.

We prove the statement by showing that
\mathcal{U}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}=\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\hat{\rho}

\forall\hat{\rho}
. In particular, we use the definitions of both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{U}
to achieve the following chain of equalities

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle=\hat{U}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{4^{n}-1}p_{j}\hat{P}_{j}\left(\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}\right)\hat{P_{j}}\right)\hat{U}^{\dagger}=\sum_{j=0}^{4^{n}-1}p_{j}\left(\hat{U}\hat{P}_{j}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\right)\hat{\rho}\left(\hat{U}\hat{P_{j}}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{4^{n}-1}p_{j}\hat{P}^{\prime}_{j}\hat{\rho}\hat{P}^{\prime}_{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{4^{n}-1}p^{\prime}_{j}\hat{P}_{j}\hat{\rho}\hat{P}_{j}=\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\rho,

(36)

where we used the property that, for any Clifford unitary
\hat{U}
and Pauli string
\hat{P}_{j}
,
\hat{U}\hat{P}_{j}\hat{U}^{\dagger}=\hat{P}_{j}^{\prime}
is still a Pauli string.
∎

A similar property also holds for the generators of unitary superoperators.

Lemma 3
.

Given a generic unitary superoperator
\mathcal{U}
and a generic generator
\mathcal{H}
defined by the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
, as
\mathcal{H}\hat{\rho}=[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]
, we have

\mathcal{U}\mathcal{H}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\;\;\text{or equivalently}\;\;\mathcal{U}\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\mathcal{U},

(37)

where
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is defined as
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\hat{\rho}=[\mathcal{U}\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]
.

Proof.

This Lemma follows immediately from the definitons of
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{H}
by the following chain of equalities:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\mathcal{H}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle=\hat{U}[\hat{H},\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}]\hat{U}^{\dagger}=\hat{U}\hat{H}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}\hat{U}^{\dagger}-\hat{U}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}\hat{H}\hat{U}^{\dagger}

(38)

\displaystyle=\hat{U}\hat{H}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}-\hat{\rho}\hat{U}\hat{H}\hat{U}^{\dagger}=[\hat{U}\hat{H}\hat{U}^{\dagger},\hat{\rho}]=[\mathcal{U}\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]=\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\hat{\rho}

where
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is defined as
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\hat{\rho}=[\mathcal{U}\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]
.
∎

We now show some useful upper bounds to compute derivatives in later sections.

Lemma 4
.

Given a quantum state
\hat{\rho}
, a Pauli string
\hat{S}
, a Pauli map
\mathcal{P}
and a superoperator
\mathcal{H}
, defined by the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
, as
\mathcal{H}\hat{\rho}=[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]
, we have the following upper bound

\left|\Tr(\hat{S}\mathcal{H}\mathcal{P\hat{\rho}})\right|\leq\|\hat{H}\|_{2}\|[\hat{S},\hat{\rho}]\|_{2}\,,

(39)

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the operator 2-norm
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
. In particular, if
[\hat{S},\hat{\rho}]=0
, then
\Tr(\hat{S}\mathcal{H}\mathcal{P}\hat{\rho})=0
.

Proof.

We begin our proof by expanding the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
in the Pauli basis, namely
\hat{H}=\sum_{k}h_{k}\hat{P}_{k}
. Using this decomposition and since
\Tr(\hat{S}\mathcal{H}\,\cdot\,)=\Tr(\mathcal{H}^\dagger\hat{S}\, \cdot\,)=\Tr( [\hat{S}, \hat{H}]\,\cdot\,)
we get

\Tr([\hat{S}, \hat{H}]\mathcal{P\hat{\rho}})=\sum_{k}h_{k}\Tr([\hat{S}, \hat{P}_k]\mathcal{P}\hat{\rho}).

(40)

Note that, since both
\hat{S}
and
\hat{P}_{k}
are Pauli strings, also their commutator has the same structure. This can be easiliy derived from the defining relations of Pauli matrices. By Lemma
1
, we have that
\mathcal{P}[\hat{S},\hat{P}_{k}]=\chi_{k}[\hat{S},\hat{P}_{k}]
. In particular, this gives

\sum_{k}h_{k}\Tr([\hat{S}, \hat{P}_k]\mathcal{P}\hat{\rho})=\sum_{k}\chi_{k}h_{k}\Tr([\hat{S}, \hat{P}_k]\hat{\rho})=\sum_{k}\chi_{k}h_{k}\Tr(\hat{P}_k[\hat{S},\hat{\rho}])=\Tr{\left(\sum_k \chi_kh_k \hat{P}_k\right)[\hat{S},\hat{\rho}]}=\Tr(\hat{H}'[\hat{S},\hat{\rho}]),

(41)

where we used cyclicity and linearity properties of the trace and used the shorthand notation
\hat{H}^{\prime}=\sum_{k}h^{\prime}_{k}\hat{P}_{k}
, with
h^{\prime}_{k}=\chi_{k}h_{k}
.
Recall that, by definiton of the
2
-norm, we have the handy relation
\|\hat{H}\|^{2}_{2}=d\sum_{k}h_{k}^{2}
, where
d
is the dimension of the system on which
\hat{H}
acts on. Together with Lemma
1
, which ensures that
\chi_{k}^{2}\leq 1
, we can show that
h_{k}^{\prime 2}=h_{k}^{2}\chi_{k}^{2}\leq h_{k}^{2}
, which implies
\|\hat{H}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{2}\leq\|\hat{H}\|^{2}_{2}
. Finally, invoking Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have

\left|\Tr(\hat{H}'[\hat{S},\hat{\rho}])\right|<\|\hat{H}^{\prime}\|_{2}\|[\hat{S},\hat{\rho}]\|_{2}\leq\|\hat{H}\|_{2}\|[\hat{S},\hat{\rho}]\|_{2},

(42)

which concludes the proof.
∎

We can easily extend Lemma
4
to a more generic superoperator
\alpha=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathcal{H}_{l}
, getting the immediate upper bound

\left|\Tr(\hat{S}\alpha\mathcal{P}\hat{\rho})\right|\leq L\max_{l}\left(\|\hat{H}_{l}\|_{2}\right)\|[\hat{S},\hat{\rho}]\|_{2}.

(43)

Another useful upper bound involving superoperators of the form of
\alpha
is given in the following Lemma.

Lemma 5
.

Given a quantum state
\hat{\rho}
, a Pauli string
\hat{S}
, two Pauli maps
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
, let’s define two superoperators
\mathcal{\alpha}_{1}
and
\mathcal{\alpha}_{2}
by

\mathcal{\alpha}_{q}=\sum_{l=1}^{L_{q}}\mathcal{H}_{ql},\;\;\text{where}\;\;\mathcal{H}_{ql}\hat{\rho}=[\hat{H}_{ql},\hat{\rho}]\,.

(44)

Further we assume that each
\hat{S}
and
\hat{H}_{ql}
acts non-trivially on
m
qubits, i.e. they are
m
-local, for both
q=1,2
, each
\hat{H}_{ql}
acts on a different subset of qubits when varying
l
. Furthermore, we assume that
\|\hat{H}_{ql}\|_{2}^{2}\leq O(d)
, where
d=2^{m}
. Then we have the following upper bound

\left|\Tr(\hat{S}\alpha_1\mathcal{P}_1\alpha_2\mathcal{P}_2\hat{\rho})\right|\leq\Upsilon N_{c}\leq O(4^{2m-1})\,.

(45)

where
\Upsilon=\max_{ql}\|\hat{H}_{ql}\|^{2}_{2}/d
is a constant, and
N_{c}\leq O(4^{2m-1})
is an integer number.

Proof.

We start by noting that, decomposing each Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{ql}
into its Pauli decomposition
\hat{H}_{ql}=\sum_{k}h_{qlk}\hat{P}_{k}
and collecting terms which share the same
\hat{P}_{k}
, we can obtain the following Pauli decomposition for the action of
\alpha_{q}
, i.e.

\alpha_{q}\hat{\rho}=\sum_{k}\left(\sum_{l}h_{qlk}\right)[\hat{P}_{k},\hat{\rho}]=\sum_{k}\left(\sum_{l}h_{qlk}\right)\mathcal{H}^{\text{\tiny(P)}}_{k},

where
\mathcal{H}^{\text{\tiny(P)}}_{k}
is defined by the corresponding
\hat{P}_{k}
. Since we assume that each
\hat{H}_{ql}
acts non-trivially on distinct qubits, then all terms in
\sum_{l}h_{qlk}
must vanish except when
k
matches the support of
\hat{H}_{ql}
, which can only happen for one
l
. As there is no ambiguity in the choice of
\hat{H}_{ql}
, we hence can drop the index
l
entirely, and simply write
h_{qk}
instead.

Using the more explicit form
\Tr(\hat{S}\mathcal{H}\,\cdot\,)=\Tr(\mathcal{H}^\dagger\hat{S}\, \cdot\,)=\Tr( [\hat{S}, \hat{H}]\,\cdot\,)
we get

\displaystyle\left|\Tr(\hat{S}\alpha_1\mathcal{P}_1\alpha_2\mathcal{P}_2\hat{\rho})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{kk^{\prime}}h_{1k}h_{2k^{\prime}}\Tr(\hat{S}\mathcal{H}^{\text{\tiny(P)}}_k\mathcal{P}_1\mathcal{H}^{\text{\tiny(P)}}_{k'}\mathcal{P}_2\hat{\rho})\right|\leq\sum_{kk^{\prime}}|h_{1k}|\cdot|h_{2k^{\prime}}|\cdot\left|\Tr([\hat{S},\hat{P}_k]\mathcal{P}_1\mathcal{H}^{\text{\tiny(P)}}_{k'}\mathcal{P}_2\hat{\rho})\right|

(46)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{kk^{\prime}}|h_{1k}|\cdot|h_{2k^{\prime}}|\cdot\left|\Tr([[\hat{S},\hat{P}_k], \hat{P}_{k'}]\mathcal{P}_2\hat{\rho})\right|\leq\sum_{kk^{\prime}}|h_{1k}|\cdot|h_{2k^{\prime}}|\cdot\left|\Tr([[\hat{S},\hat{P}_k], \hat{P}_{k'}]\hat{\rho})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{k}(|h_{1k}|)\cdot\max_{k^{\prime}}(|h_{2k^{\prime}}|)\cdot\sum_{kk^{\prime}}\left|\Tr([[\hat{S},\hat{P}_k], P_{k'}]\hat{\rho})\right|\,,

where in the third term of the first line and in the first term of the second line we used the ciclicity of the trace and Lemma
1
for
\mathcal{P}_{1}[\hat{S},\hat{P}_{k}]
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}[[\hat{S},\hat{P}_{k}],\hat{P}_{k^{\prime}}]
by upper bounding the corresponding constants to 1.

Recalling the relation
\|\hat{H}_{ql}\|^{2}_{2}=d\sum_{k}h^{2}_{qlk}
, where
d
denotes the dimension of the system on which
\hat{H}_{ql}
acts, we can note that, given a
k
,
h_{qk}^{2}\leq\|\hat{H}_{ql}\|^{2}_{2}
. As a consequence, it is immediate to see that
d\max_{k}|h_{qk}|\leq\max_{l}\|\hat{H}_{ql}\|_{2}
,
\forall q
. Thanks to this observation, we can finally write the bound for the product

\max_{k}(|h_{1k}|)\cdot\max_{k^{\prime}}(|h_{2k^{\prime}}|)\leq\max_{ql}\left(\frac{\|\hat{H}_{ql}\|^{2}_{2}}{d}\right)\equiv\Upsilon\leq O(1)\,.

(47)

Furthermore,
\hat{S}
,
\hat{P}_{k}
and
\hat{P}_{k^{\prime}}
are
m
-local by virtue of the locality of
\hat{H}_{ql}
. This implies that only a constant number
N_{c}
of nested commutators

[[\hat{S},\hat{P}_{k}],\hat{P}_{k^{\prime}}]
are non-vanishing.
Indeed, each operator
\hat{S}_{i}
commutes with all Pauli strings that act trivially on the same qubits. For all the others Pauli strings
\hat{P}_{k}
, since they act non-trivially on at most a constant number
m
of qubits, the overlap between the supports of
\hat{S}_{i}
and
\hat{P}_{k}
is bounded by
4^{2m-1}
, yielding only a constant number of terms in the sum.

Using Hölder inequality, we have
\left|\Tr([[\hat{S},\hat{P}_k], \hat{P}_{k'}]\hat{\rho})\right|\leq\|[[\hat{S},\hat{P}_{k}],\hat{P}_{k^{\prime}}]\|_{\infty}\|\hat{\rho}\|_{1}=1
, giving the final upper bound

\left|\Tr(\hat{S}\alpha_1\mathcal{P}_1\alpha_2\mathcal{P}_2\hat{\rho})\right|\leq\Upsilon N_{c}\leq O(4^{2m-1})\,.

(48)

∎

Definition 2

(Global depolarizing map)
.

A quantum channel
\mathcal{D}
acting on
n
qubits is a global depolarizing map if it has the form

\mathcal{D}\hat{\rho}=(1-p)\hat{\rho}+\frac{p}{2^{n}}\mathbb{1}\,,

(49)

where
p
is the probability of getting a totally depolarized state
\mathbb{1}/2^{n}
and
\hat{\rho}
is a generic density matrix of
n
qubits. We notice that a global symmetric Pauli map, i.e Definition
1
with
p_{j}=p

\forall j
, reduces to Eq. (
49
).

Lemma 6
.

(Effective depolarizing map at the end of the circuit).

Given a noisy quantum circuit
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{D}}
as in Eq. (
16
), where a global depolarizing map
\mathcal{D}_{q}
as in Eq. (
15
) acts after each circuit moment
\mathcal{U}_{q}
of the circuit
\mathcal{U}_{S}=\prod_{q=1}^{M}\mathcal{U}_{q}
, then we show that

\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{D}}\hat{\rho_{0}}=\mathcal{D}\hat{\rho}_{S}\,,

(50)

where
\hat{\rho}_{S}=\mathcal{U}_{S}\hat{\rho}_{0}
,
\hat{\rho}_{0}=(\ket{0}\bra{0})^{\otimes n}
and
\mathcal{D}
is an effective global depolarizing map as in Eq. (
49
) acting at the end of the circuit
\mathcal{U}_{S}
with probability
p^{\prime}=1-\prod_{q=1}^{M}(1-p_{q})
.

Proof.

For each
q
in
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{D}}
we apply consecutively
\mathcal{D}_{q}
to the right giving rise to a new depolarizing map

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{D}}\hat{\rho}_{0}

\displaystyle=\biggl(\prod_{q=2}^{M}\mathcal{D}_{q}\mathcal{U}_{q}\biggr)\hat{\rho}_{0}=(1-p_{1})\biggl(\prod_{q=2}^{M}\mathcal{D}_{q}\mathcal{U}_{q}\biggr)\mathcal{U}_{1}\hat{\rho}_{0}+\frac{p_{1}}{2^{n}}\mathbb{1}

(51)

\displaystyle=(1-p_{1})(1-p_{2})\biggl(\prod_{q=3}^{M}\mathcal{D}_{q}\mathcal{U}_{q}\biggr)\mathcal{U}_{2}\mathcal{U}_{1}\hat{\rho}_{0}+\frac{1-(1-p_{1})(1-p_{2})}{2^{n}}\mathbb{1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{q=1}^{M}(1-p_{q})\mathcal{U}_{S}\hat{\rho}_{0}+\frac{1-\prod_{q=1}^{M}(1-p_{q})}{2^{n}}\mathbb{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}\mathcal{U}_{S}\hat{\rho_{0}}=\mathcal{D}\hat{\rho}_{S}\,,

where
\mathcal{D}
is a global depolarizing map with probability
p^{\prime}
.

\mathcal{U}_{q}=\bigotimes_{l=1}^{L_{q}}\mathcal{G}_{ql}
since
\mathcal{G}_{ql}
act on different qubits, they commute with each other inside the same moment. This allows us to write

\mathcal{U}_{q}=\bigotimes_{l=1}^{L_{q}}\mathcal{G}_{ql}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{G}_{ql}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}=\left(\bigotimes_{l=1}^{L_{q}}\mathcal{G}_{ql}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\right)\left(\bigotimes_{l=1}^{L_{q}}\mathcal{G}_{ql}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\right)\equiv\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(C)}},

(66)

where
\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}
and
\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}
are respectively the non-Clifford and Clifford parts. Such operations, are still of the form of exponentials, except now they are generated by the more complex superoperator

\bar{\alpha}_{q}=\sum_{l=1}^{L_{q}}(\theta_{ql}+\epsilon_{ql})\mathcal{H}_{ql},\;\;\text{i.e.}\;\;\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}=e^{-i\bar{\alpha}_{q}}.

(67)

A similar structure also holds for
\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}
, but we do not need it.

Lemma 7
.

(Circuit decomposition and shape of the remainder).

The noisy quantum circuit
\mathcal{N}
in Eq. (
22
) can be decomposed as

\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{P}\mathcal{U}_{S}+\mathcal{R}\,,

(68)

where
\mathcal{P}\equiv\left(\prod_{q=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{q}\right)
is an effective Pauli map and the remainder
\mathcal{R}
is expressed as

\mathcal{R}=\sum_{p=1}^{M-1}\sum_{q>p}^{M}\mathcal{T}_{qp},\;\;\mathcal{T}_{qp}=\left(\prod_{s=p+1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{s}\right)\left(\prod_{r=q+1}^{M}\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\right)\Delta_{qp}\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\left(\prod_{r^{\prime}=p}^{q-1}\mathcal{U}_{r^{\prime}}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{r^{\prime}}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\right)\left(\prod_{t=1}^{p-1}\mathcal{P}_{t}\mathcal{U}_{t}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{t}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\right)\,,

(69)

where
\Delta_{qp}\equiv[\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(N)}},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{pq}]
is the commutator between coherent and incoherent noise maps, and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{qp}
are diagonal Pauli maps obtained by conjugation of
\mathcal{P}_{p}
with the Clifford moments between
p+1
and
q
(see Lemma
2
), namely

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{qp}=\left(\prod_{v=p+1}^{q}\mathcal{U}_{v}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\right)\mathcal{P}_{p}\left(\prod_{v=p+1}^{q}\mathcal{U}_{v}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\right)^{\dagger}.

(70)

Finally, for the left most product, we use the shorthand notation
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{s}=\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{Ms}
.

Proof.

The Lemma is proven giving a constructive approach. In particular, we will commute each
\mathcal{P}_{q}
to the left of the expression on one step at the time, keeping track of the remainders. Each term of the double sum in Eq. (
69
) represents exactly this. We now give the blueprint on how to explicitly build this construction, using as an example the first few
p
and
q
. Let’s start from
\mathcal{N}
where we highlight the last moments, namely

\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{P}_{M}\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\mathcal{P}_{M-1}\mathcal{U}_{M-1}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{M-1}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\prod_{q=1}^{M-2}\mathcal{P}_{q}\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\,.

(71)

According to Lemma
2
, we can swap
\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{M-1}
at the price of having a new Pauli map
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{M-1}
, obtained by conjugation, i.e.
\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\mathcal{P}_{M-1}=\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{M-1}\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}
. However we cannot do this for the non-Clifford moment
\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}
. In this case we swap them and keep track of the error
\Delta_{M,M-1}
, i.e. we use the relation

\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{M-1}=\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{M-1}\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}+[\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\text{\tiny(N)}},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{M-1}]=\mathcal{P}_{M-1}\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}+\Delta_{M,M-1}.

(72)

In this way, we get the first decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{M}\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{M-1}\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\mathcal{U}_{M-1}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{M-1}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\prod_{q=1}^{M-2}\mathcal{P}_{q}\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}

(73)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{P}_{M}\Delta_{M,M-1}\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\mathcal{U}_{M-1}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{M-1}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\prod_{q=1}^{M-2}\mathcal{P}_{q}\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\,.

Note that the first term is one step closer to look like the first term in Eq. (
68
), while the second term corresponds exactly to
\mathcal{T}_{M\,M-1}
.
By iterating this process on the first term, we can obtain all subsequent
\mathcal{T}_{qp}
terms.
∎

Beyond the technicalities involved in keeping track of all the conjugations appearing in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{qp}
, the real power of Lemma
7
is to provide the general structure of the remainder
\mathcal{R}
in terms a sequence of Clifford, non-Clifford and Pauli maps, which will be key for the subsequent proofs.

Appendix D
Stationarity of the solution in the presence of Pauli noise after each circuit moment

In this section we show that, despite the presence of Pauli noise in the system, the optimal set of parameters that we aim to find, namely
\vec{\theta}=-\vec{\epsilon}
remains a stationary point. In order to do so, we prove Theorem
1
of the main text.

Proof.

For simplicity now we define
\gamma_{k}=\theta_{k}+\epsilon_{k}
in the native gates inside the non-Clifford moments
\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}
. In this manner, it is sufficient to compute the derivative
\partial_{\gamma_{k}}\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}
and evaluate it in
\vec{\gamma}=\vec{\theta}+\vec{\epsilon}=0
to achieve the desired result of Eq. (
25
).

By employing the expression of
\mathcal{R}
in Eq. (
69
) of Lemma
7
we have

\partial_{\gamma_{k}}\Delta\tilde{C}(\vec{\gamma}-\vec{\epsilon})|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Tr(\hat{S}\partial_{\gamma_k}\mathcal{R}|_{\vec{\gamma}=0} \hat{\rho}_0)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{M-1}\sum_{q>p}^{M}\Tr(\hat{S}\partial_{\gamma_k}\mathcal{T}_{qp}|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}\hat{\rho}_0)\,.

(74)

At this point we focus on two distinct cases.

Obs 1

(Selection of the remainder terms)
.

Given a parameter
\gamma_{k}
, for each moment
\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}_{q}
that does not depend on
\gamma_{k}
, then

\partial_{\gamma_{k}}\mathcal{T}_{qp}|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}\equiv 0\,.

(75)

This follows from the definition of
\mathcal{T}_{qp}
, and in particular on the structure of
\Delta_{qp}=[\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(N)}},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{qp}]
arising from Lemma
7
. Indeed, we assume that
\gamma_{k}
is not contained in a moment
\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}_{q}
, and without loss of generality and to ease the notation, we assume that instead, it is contained in the moment
\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}_{1}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\gamma_{k}}\mathcal{T}_{qp}|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{s=p+1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{s}\right)\left(\prod_{r=q+1}^{M}\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\right)\Delta_{qp}\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\left(\prod_{r^{\prime}=p}^{q-1}\mathcal{U}_{r^{\prime}}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{r^{\prime}}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\right)\left(\prod_{t=2}^{p-1}\mathcal{P}_{t}\mathcal{U}_{t}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{t}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\right)\mathcal{P}_{1}\left(\partial_{\gamma_{k}}\mathcal{U}_{1}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\right)\mathcal{U}_{1}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\Bigg|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}

(76)

\displaystyle=-i\left(\prod_{s=p+1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{s}\right)\left(\prod_{r=q+1}^{M}\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\right)\Delta_{qp}\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\left(\prod_{r^{\prime}=p}^{q-1}\mathcal{U}_{r^{\prime}}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{r^{\prime}}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\right)\left(\prod_{t=2}^{p-1}\mathcal{P}_{t}\mathcal{U}_{t}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{t}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\right)\mathcal{P}_{1}\left(\mathcal{H}_{k}\mathcal{U}_{1}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}\right)\mathcal{U}_{1}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\Bigg|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}\,,

where we used the definition
\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}=e^{-i\bar{\alpha}_{q}}
, and
\bar{\alpha}_{q}=\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Then, noting that, according to the same definition,
\bar{\alpha}_{q}=0
when evaluated at
\vec{\gamma}=0
, we get the simple result that

\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}=e^{-i\bar{\alpha}_{q}}=\mathbb{1}\;\forall q
.

In particular this implies that, at
\vec{\gamma}=0
,
\Delta_{qp}\equiv 0
, since in that case
\Delta_{qp}=[\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(N)}},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{qp}]=[\mathbb{1},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{qp}]\equiv 0
, killing the whole product. Clearly from this reasoning, all other terms where the moment does not depend on
\gamma_{k}
will similarly vanish.

The same however cannot be said when
\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}_{q}
depends on
\gamma_{k}
. Indeed, in that case the
\gamma_{k}
dependence is included in
\Delta_{qp}
, and hence we need to compute
\partial_{\gamma_{k}}\Delta_{qp}
. In particular, we get

\partial_{\gamma_{k}}\Delta_{qp}|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}=\partial_{\gamma_{k}}[\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(N)}},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{qp}]\Big|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}=[\partial_{\gamma_{k}}\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(N)}},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{qp}]\Big|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}=-i[\mathcal{H}_{k}\mathcal{U}_{q}^{\text{\tiny(N)}},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{qp}]\Big|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}=-i[\mathcal{H}_{k},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{qp}]\,,

(77)

which is in general non-vanishing. However, we will now prove that, when evaluated in the trace, such contributions do indeed vanish. As a first step, we make the following simple observation.

Obs 2

(Structure of the derivative)
.

For any non-vanishing
\partial_{\gamma_{k}}\mathcal{T}_{qp}
, we can write its gradient contribution as

\Tr(\hat{S}_i\partial_{\gamma_k}\mathcal{T}_{qp}|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}\hat{\rho}_0)=-i\chi^{\prime}_{i}\Tr{\hat{S}_i \left(\prod_{r=q+1}^M\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_r\right) [\mathcal{H}_k, \mathcal{P}'_{qp}] \mathcal{P}''_{qp}\left(\prod_{r'=1}^{q}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_{r'}\right)\hat{\rho}_0}\,,

(78)

where
\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}_{p}
is a Pauli map including the contribution of the first
p
Pauli noise maps.

To show this, we recall the definition of
\mathcal{T}_{qp}
in Eq. (
69
) and the previous result that when
\vec{\gamma}=0
, all non-Clifford moments become the identity. Using this we get

\displaystyle\partial_{\gamma_{k}}\mathcal{T}_{qp}|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{s=p+1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{s}\right)\left(\prod_{r=q+1}^{M}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}_{r}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_{r}\right)\partial_{\gamma_{k}}\Delta_{qp}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_{q}\left(\prod_{r^{\prime}=p}^{q-1}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}_{r^{\prime}}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_{r^{\prime}}\right)\left(\prod_{t=1}^{p-1}\mathcal{P}_{t}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(N)}}_{t}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_{t}\right)\Bigg|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}

(79)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{s=p+1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{s}\right)\left(\prod_{r=q+1}^{M}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_{r}\right)\partial_{\gamma_{k}}\Delta_{qp}\big|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_{q}\left(\prod_{r^{\prime}=p}^{q-1}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_{r^{\prime}}\right)\left(\prod_{t=1}^{p-1}\mathcal{P}_{t}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_{t}\right).

Inserting the explicit form of
\Delta_{qp}
, using Lemma
1
to the leftmost Pauli maps and using Eq. (
77
), we can obtain

\Tr(\hat{S}_i\partial_{\gamma_k}\mathcal{T}_{qp}|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}\hat{\rho}_0)=-i\chi^{\prime}_{i}\Tr{\hat{S}_i \left(\prod_{r=q+1}^M\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_r\right) [\mathcal{H}_k, \mathcal{P}'_{qp}] \,\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_q\left(\prod_{r'=p}^{q-1}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_{r'} \right)\left(\prod_{t=1}^{p-1} \mathcal{P}_t\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_t\right)\hat{\rho}_0}.

(80)

Finally, the last step is achieved by noting that, since we only have Clifford moments, we can swap all Pauli maps next to the commutator, at the price of conjugation with Clifford moments as in Lemma
2
.

While it would be possible to keep track exactly of the form of those Pauli maps, it suffices for our purposes to note that they will remain Pauli maps. With this in mind, we are ready to conclude the proof by computing for simplicity the modulus of the derivative (without using the modulus the derivation is more involved). In particular, we have

\displaystyle\left|\Tr(\hat{S}_i\partial_{\gamma_k}\mathcal{T}_{qp}|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}\hat{\rho}_0)\right|=\left|\chi^{\prime}_{i}\Tr{\hat{S}_i \left(\prod_{r=q+1}^M\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_r\right) [\mathcal{H}_k, \mathcal{P}'_{qp}] \mathcal{P}''_{qp}\left(\prod_{r'=1}^{q}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_{r'}\right)\hat{\rho}_0}\right|

(81)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\Tr{\hat{S}_i \left(\prod_{r=q+1}^M\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_r\right) \mathcal{H}_k \mathcal{P}'_{qp} \mathcal{P}''_{qp}\left(\prod_{r'=1}^{q}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_{r'}\right)\hat{\rho}_0}\right|+\left|\Tr{S_i \left(\prod_{r=q+1}^M\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_r\right) \mathcal{P}'_{qp}\mathcal{H}_k \mathcal{P}''_{qp}\left(\prod_{r'=1}^{q}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_{r'}\right)\hat{\rho}_0}\right|,

where we used
|\chi_{i}|\leq 1\,\forall i
coming from Lemma
1
and the triangle inequality to expand the commutator outside the modulus. If we now introduce the notations

\hat{S}^{(q)}_{i}\equiv\bigg(\prod_{r=q+1}^{M}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_{r}\bigg)^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{i}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\hat{\rho}_{q}\equiv\bigg(\prod_{r^{\prime}=1}^{q}\mathcal{U}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}_{r^{\prime}}\bigg)\hat{\rho}_{0},

(82)

using the ciclicity of the trace we have the final bound

\displaystyle\left|\Tr(\hat{S}_i\partial_{\gamma_k}\mathcal{T}_{qp}|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}\hat{\rho}_0)\Big|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\Tr(\hat{S}_i^{(q)} \mathcal{H}_k \mathcal{P}'_{qp} \mathcal{P}''_{qp}\hat{\rho}_{q})\right|+\left|\Tr(\hat{S}_i^{(q)} \mathcal{P}'_{qp}\mathcal{H}_k \mathcal{P}''_{qp}\hat{\rho}_{q})\right|

(83)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\Tr(\hat{S}_i^{(q)} \mathcal{H}_k \mathcal{P}'_{qp} \mathcal{P}''_{qp}\hat{\rho}_{q})\right|+\left|\Tr(\hat{S}_i^{(q)} \mathcal{H}_k \mathcal{P}''_{qp}\hat{\rho}_{q})\right|\,,

where again we used Lemma
1
applied to
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{qp}\hat{S}_{i}^{(q)}
in the second term of the first line. By Lemma
4
, we can now write

\left|\Tr(\hat{S}_i\partial_{\gamma_k}\mathcal{T}_{qp}|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}\hat{\rho}_0)\Big|_{\vec{\gamma}=0}\right|\leq 2\|\hat{H}_{k}\|_{2}\|[\hat{S}_{i}^{(q)},\hat{\rho}_{q}]\|_{2}=0,

(84)

where the equality comes from the fact that
\hat{S}_{i}^{(q)}
is a stabilizer for
\hat{\rho}_{q}
by construction, and hence they commute.
∎

Appendix F
Upper bound on
\Delta\tilde{C}(\vec{\theta})

Appendix G
Twirling quantum maps

Let
\mathbb{U}_{d}
denote the continuous set of
d\times d
unitary matrices
\hat{U}\in\mathbb{U}_{d}
where
d=2^{n}
and
\mathcal{E}\hat{\rho}=\sum_{j=0}^{4^{n}-1}\hat{E}_{j}\hat{\rho}\hat{E}_{j}
denote a general quantum map, then we can define

\mathcal{E}_{\text{\tiny T}}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\hat{\rho}=\int_{\mathbb{U}_{d}}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{U}\,\ \hat{U}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}(\hat{U}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}^{\dagger})\hat{U}\,\ ,

(99)

the continuous twirling of the map
\mathcal{E}

Di Matteo 2014
. One can also define the twirling on a discrete distribution of unitaries
\mathbb{D}=\{\hat{U}_{0}\dots\hat{U}_{r}\}
such that

\mathcal{E}_{\text{\tiny T}}^{\text{\tiny(D)}}\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=0}^{r}\hat{U}_{j}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}(\hat{U}_{j}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}_{j}^{\dagger})\hat{U}_{j}\,.

(100)

G.1
Pauli twirling

Pauli twirling is an example of a discrete twirling where
\mathbb{D}=\mathcal{\bf{P}}_{n}
is the Pauli group, thus

\mathcal{E}_{\text{\tiny T}}^{\text{\tiny(Pauli)}}\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{4^{n}-1}\sum_{j=0}^{4^{n}-1}\hat{P}_{j}\mathcal{E}(\hat{P}_{j}\hat{\rho}\hat{P}_{j})\hat{P}_{j}\,,

(101)

with
\hat{P}_{j}\in\mathcal{\bf{P}}_{n}
. By expressing
\hat{E}_{j}=\sum_{k=0}^{4^{n}-1}c_{j,k}\hat{P}_{k}
with
c_{j,k}\in\mathbb{C}
, then it is straightforward to show that

\mathcal{E}_{\text{\tiny T}}^{\text{\tiny(Pauli)}}\hat{\rho}=\sum_{k=0}^{4^{n}-1}p_{k}\hat{P}_{k}\hat{\rho}\hat{P}_{k}\,\ ,

(102)

where
p_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{4^{n}-1}\absolutevalue{c_{j,k}}^{2}
. Thus, Pauli twirling transforms a general quantum map into a Pauli map.

G.2
Clifford twirling

Let
\mathcal{\bf{C}}_{n}
denote the Clifford group, namely the group of unitaries
\hat{C}_{j}\in\mathcal{\bf{C}}_{n}
such that
\hat{C}_{j}\hat{P}_{i}\hat{C}_{j}^{\dagger}=\hat{P}_{k}
where
j=1,\dots,r
and
\hat{P}_{i},\hat{P}_{k}\in\mathcal{\bf{P}}_{n}
. Then, one can define the Clifford twirling as

\mathcal{E}_{\text{\tiny T}}^{\text{\tiny(Clifford)}}\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=0}^{r}\hat{C}_{j}\mathcal{E}(\hat{C}_{j}\hat{\rho}\hat{C}_{j})\hat{C}_{j}\,.

(103)

Moreover, it is proved that
\mathcal{E}_{\text{\tiny T}}^{\text{\tiny(Clifford)}}\hat{\rho}=\mathcal{E}_{\text{\tiny T}}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\hat{\rho}

Gross et al. 2007
and
\mathcal{E}_{\text{\tiny T}}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}\hat{\rho}=(1-p)\hat{\rho}+\frac{p}{2^{n}}\mathbb{1}
with
p=\frac{4^{n}-\Tr(\mathcal{E}_{\text{\tiny T}}^{\text{\tiny(C)}}(\hat{\rho}))}{4^{n}-1}

Emerson et al. 2005
;
Dür et al. 2005
, then

\mathcal{E}_{\text{\tiny T}}^{\text{\tiny(Clifford)}}\hat{\rho}=(1-p)\hat{\rho}+\frac{p}{2^{n}}\mathbb{1}\,\ .

(104)

Thus, Clifford twirling transforms a general quantum map into a global depolarizing map.
