On the Sharp Input-Output Analysis of Nonlinear Systems under Adversarial Attacks
Jihun Kim, Yuchen Fang, Javad Lavaei

Abstract
This paper is concerned with learning the input-output mapping of general nonlinear dynamical systems. While the existing literature focuses on Gaussian inputs and benign disturbances, we significantly broaden the scope of admissible control inputs and allow correlated, nonzero-mean, adversarial disturbances. With our reformulation as a linear combination of basis functions, we prove that the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm estimator overcomes the challenges as long as the probability that the system is under adversarial attack at a given time is smaller than a certain threshold. We provide an estimation error bound that decays with the input memory length and prove its optimality by constructing a problem instance that suffers from the same bound under adversarial attacks. Our work provides a sharp input-output analysis for a generic nonlinear and partially observed system under significantly generalized assumptions compared to existing works.

Introduction

Dynamical systems describe how the state of a system evolves over time according to specific laws. Such systems are ubiquitous in scientific and engineering disciplines, including computer networks
(
Low et al. 2002
)
, deep learning
(
Meunier et al. 2022
)
, portfolio management
(
Grinold and Kahn 2000
)
, biology
(
Murray 2007
)
, and optimal control
(
Dorf and Bishop 2011
)
. In many practical settings, however, the underlying dynamics are too complex to be explicitly characterized, resulting in models with partially or entirely unknown parameters. Designing controllers or making predictions without first identifying these unknowns can lead to suboptimal or even unsafe outcomes. To address this challenge, the field of
system identification
focuses on identifying system dynamics from observed input-output data.

There has been extensive research in system identification under various structural and disturbance assumptions

(
Simchowitz et al. 2018
;
Faradonbeh et al. 2018
;
Simchowitz et al. 2019
;
Jedra and Proutiere 2020
;
Sarkar et al. 2021
;
Oymak and Ozay 2022
;
Yalcin et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Kim and Lavaei 2024
;
Kim and Lavaei 2025
)
While these works provide strong theoretical guarantees and practical algorithms, the majority of them concentrate on linear systems. However, many real-world systems are inherently nonlinear
(
Grinold and Kahn 2000
;
Low et al. 2002
;
Murray 2007
)
, which motivates us to develop identification methods that go beyond the linear setting.

We consider a generic partially observed nonlinear system

\displaystyle x_{t+1}

\displaystyle=f(x_{t},u_{t},w_{t}),\quad y_{t}=g(x_{t},u_{t}),\quad t=0,1,\dots,T-1,

(1)

where
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state,
u_{t}\in\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the control input, and
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
is the observation at time
t
. The set
\mathcal{U}
consists of admissible control inputs, and
T
is the time horizon. Under adversarially chosen disturbances
w_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, our goal is to identify the input-output mapping of the system (
1
).

Nonlinear system identification approaches can generally be classified into two broad categories: nonparametric and parametric methods.
While nonparametric approaches offer high flexibility
(
Greblicki and Pawlak 2008
;
Ziemann et al. 2022
)
, they often come with substantial computational overhead and limited interpretability. In contrast, parametric methods use prior knowledge or structural assumptions to yield models that are more interpretable, computationally efficient, and analytically tractable
(
Chen 1995
;
Giannakis and Serpedin 2001
)
.

In this paper, we adopt a
parametric approach
, modeling the nonlinear system using a linear combination of basis functions. In principle, we reformulate the input-output mapping from (
1
) as:

\displaystyle y_{t}=G^{*}\cdot\Phi(u_{t},\dots,u_{t-\tau})+\textit{extra terms},

(2)

where
M
is the number of basis functions,
\Phi:(\mathcal{U})^{\tau+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
is the stack of basis functions,

represents the matrix governing the input-output mapping,

are functions of only disturbances and the “far” past states. Note that the

are exponentially small with the exponent
\tau

We defer the justification for this reformulation to Section

Using this formulation, the original nonlinear system is now replaced with a linear combination of basis functions, reducing the system identification task to estimating
G^{*}
. However, the partial observability of the nonlinear system, combined with adversarial disturbances
w_{t}
and the restriction on admissible control inputs

, introduces significant challenges in accurately recovering

. Thus, we pose the following central question:

When and how can we accurately estimate the true

under non-Gaussian inputs and correlated, nonzero-mean, adversarial disturbances?

In this paper, we address the question posed above and summarize our contributions as follows:

1) Our work focuses on nonlinear systems with partially observed outputs, non-Gaussian control inputs, and correlated, nonzero-mean, possibly adversarial disturbances. This setting significantly broadens the scope of existing literature, each of which assumes at least one of Gaussian control inputs, i.i.d. disturbances, or zero-mean disturbances.

2) We reformulate the problem as estimating

\tau

1.1
Model Justification

Observing that
y_{t}
in (
1
) is transferred from the control inputs
u_{t},u_{t-1},\cdots,u_{0}
, disturbances
w_{t-1},w_{t-2},\cdots,w_{0}
, and the initial state
x_{0}
,
we can rewrite
y_{t}
as a nonlinear function of
u_{t},u_{t-1},\cdots,u_{0},w_{t-1},w_{t-2},\cdots,w_{0},x_{0}
. While this formulation captures all information, it suffers from several key limitations. First, the representation is inherently time-varying and becomes increasingly high-dimensional as time progresses. This not only leads to growing storage and computational burdens but also makes estimation statistically unstable due to the curse of dimensionality and variance accumulation over long horizons. Second, the underlying nonlinear function may be highly complex, potentially involving a large number of parameters, which makes accurate estimation challenging with finite data. Third, the function may involve complex interactions between control inputs and disturbances, further complicating the functional form and hindering identifiability. Motivated by these limitations, we reformulate the model in (
1
) as shown in (
2
), which effectively addresses these challenges in the following approaches:

For the first challenge, we adopt a finite-memory approximation strategy with length
\tau
, consistent with classical system identification techniques such as Volterra series truncations
(
Boyd and Chua 1985
)
and NARMAX models
(
Billings 2013
)
. These methods are grounded in the assumption that the dynamics of a nonlinear system can effectively be represented using a fixed window of past inputs. By relying on mild regularity conditions, this approach can preserve approximation accuracy while ensuring computational tractability (note that the error term due to using a fixed window of the past is exponentially small when all disturbances are zero).

For the second challenge, we approximate the unknown nonlinear function as a linear combination of basis functions, allowing for flexible yet interpretable modeling. Our primary motivation in using linear combination of basis functions to represent nonlinear functions is rooted in
function approximation theory
, which ensures that a wide class of nonlinear mappings can be approximated with arbitrary precision using a finite set of appropriately chosen basis functions. For instance, Fourier expansions represent highly nonlinear periodic functions as linear combinations of sine and cosine functions. Radial Basis Function (RBF) networks model nonlinear relationships using linear combinations of radially symmetric basis functions centered at specific points
(
Chen et al. 1991
)
. In control and signal processing, Volterra series use Volterra kernels to generalize linear system models
(
Boyd and Chua 1985
)
.
Moreover, random feature methods achieve function approximation through linear combinations of basis functions in a randomized feature space.

(
Rahimi and Recht 2007
)
.

To address the third challenge, we design the basis functions to depend solely on the control inputs, thereby decoupling the control inputs and disturbances. This separation has proven effective and is widely adopted in various settings. For example, in model-based reinforcement learning (RL), it is common to alternate between system identification and control policy design, where the agent first learns an input-output model of the environment and then uses it to make informed decisions
(
Moerland et al. 2023
)
. In many cases, it suffices to focus on the input-output relationship—how control actions affect observable outcomes—rather than attempting to recover the full latent state dynamics
(
Abbeel et al. 2006
;
Deisenroth and Rasmussen 2011
)
. This simplification is particularly valuable in high-stakes applications like autonomous driving, where control inputs such as throttle and steering are mapped to observations such as heading direction, position, and velocity
(
Paden et al. 2016
)
. By isolating the controllable aspects of the system, this formulation improves both estimation accuracy and robustness to disturbances, especially under adversarial settings.

2
Problem Formulation

In (
1
), we study a nonlinear dynamical system
x_{t+1}=f(x_{t},u_{t},w_{t})
and
y_{t}=g(x_{t},u_{t})
, where the state equation is governed by the dynamics
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{U}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the observation equation is determined by the measurement model
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}^{r}
.
We have the discretion to design control inputs
u_{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{T-1}
and we have access to a single observation trajectory consisting of partial observations
y_{0},y_{1},\dots,y_{T-1}
.
We assume the Lipschitz continuity of the measurement model
g
and the contraction property for the dynamics
f
to ensure system stability and prevent the explosion of the nonlinear system, which is common in a number of control theory literature
(
Sontag 2008
;
Khalil 2015
;
Tsukamoto et al. 2021
)
. The formal assumption on the dynamics is given below.

Assumption 2.1

(Lipschitzness)
.

g
is Lipschitz continuous;
i.e. there exists
L>0
such that

\displaystyle\|g(x,u)-g(\tilde{x},\tilde{u})\|_{2}\leq L(\|x-\tilde{x}\|_{2}+\|u-\tilde{u}\|_{2})

(3)

for all
x,\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
u,\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{U}
.
Moreover, note that for
k\geq 1
, the
k
-fold composition of the dynamics
f
, denoted by
f^{(k)}
, maps
(x_{t-k},u_{t-k},\dots,u_{t-1},w_{t-k},\dots,w_{t-1})
to
x_{t}
. We assume that

f^{(k)}
is Lipschitz continuous in its oldest arguments
(x_{t-k},u_{t-k},w_{t-k})
with constant
C\rho^{k}
for some
C>0
and
0<\rho<1
, with later inputs and disturbances fixed. In other words, we have

\displaystyle\|f^{(k)}(x_{t-k},u_{t-k},w_{t-k};\bm{u},\bm{w})-f^{(k)}(\tilde{x}_{t-k},\tilde{u}_{t-k},\tilde{w}_{t-k};\bm{u},\bm{w})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\leq C\rho^{k}(\|x_{t-k}-\tilde{x}_{t-k}\|_{2}+\|u_{t-k}-\tilde{u}_{t-k}\|_{2}+\|w_{t-k}-\tilde{w}_{t-k}\|_{2}),

(4)

for all
x_{t-k},\tilde{x}_{t-k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},u_{t-k},\tilde{u}_{t-k}\in\mathcal{U},w_{t-k},\tilde{w}_{t-k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with any
\bm{u}=(u_{t-k+1},\dots,u_{t-1})\in(\mathcal{U})^{k-1}
and
\bm{w}=(w_{t-k+1},\dots,w_{t-1})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k-1}
. We further make the standard assumption
f(0,0,0)=0
.

Remark 2.2
.

The contraction property in Assumption
2.1
is analogous to Gelfand’s formula in linear systems. For any matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, the formula guarantees the existence of the absolute constant
c(n)
(which only depends on the system order
n
) such that
\|A^{k}\|_{2}\leq c(n)\cdot[\lambda_{\text{max}}(A)]^{k}
for all
k\geq 0
, where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the spectral norm and
\lambda_{\text{max}}(\cdot)
denotes the spectral radius. We adopt this analogous setting in our nonlinear system by interpreting
\lambda_{\text{max}}(A)
as
\rho
, and assume that
f^{(k)}
has a Lipschitz constant of
C\rho^{k}
.

We consider both inputs and disturbances on the system to be sub-Gaussian variables, in which the tail event rarely occurs. For example, any bounded variables automatically satisfy this assumption. We use the definition given in
Vershynin 2018
(see the detailed definitions and properties of sub-Gaussian variables in Appendix
B
). Note that we do not require each input or disturbance to have a zero mean (see Definitions
B.1
and
B.4
). The formal assumptions are given below.

Assumption 2.3

(sub-Gaussian control inputs)
.

We design our control input to be independent sub-Gaussian variables, meaning that
u_{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{T-1}
are independent of each other and there exists a finite
\sigma_{u}>0
such that
\|u_{t}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\sigma_{u}
for all
t=0,\dots,T-1
.

Assumption 2.4

(sub-Gaussian disturbances)
.

Define a filtration

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\bm{\sigma}\{x_{0},u_{0},\dots,u_{t},w_{0},\dots,w_{t-1}\}.

Then, there exists
\sigma_{w}>0
such that
\|x_{0}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\sigma_{w}
and

\|w_{t}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\sigma_{w}
conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
for all
t=0,\dots,T-1
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}
.

In this work, we focus on input-output analysis and aim to identify the model governing the mapping from (truncated) control inputs
(u_{t},\dots,u_{t-\tau})
to observation outputs
y_{t}
, where
\tau
denotes the input memory length specified by the user to construct the mapping. To this end, we rewrite (
1
) as follows:

\displaystyle y_{t}

\displaystyle=g(x_{t},u_{t})=g(f(x_{t-1},u_{t-1},w_{t-1}),u_{t})=\cdots

\displaystyle=g(f(\cdots f(f(x_{t-\tau},u_{t-\tau},w_{t-\tau}),u_{t-\tau+1},w_{t-\tau+1}),\dots,u_{t-1},w_{t-1}),u_{t})

(5)

for all
t\geq\tau
, where the right-hand side considers the control inputs
u_{t},u_{t-1},\dots,u_{t-\tau}
.

The following lemma separates the effect of disturbances and the oldest state with the effect of inputs, which allows us to establish the input-output mapping. The proof can be found in Appendix
C
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, the equation (
2
) for each
t\geq\tau
implies that there exist
\bm{W}_{t}^{(\tau)},\bm{x}_{t}^{(\tau)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
such that

\displaystyle y_{t}=g(f(\cdots f(f(0,u_{t-\tau},0),u_{t-\tau+1},0),\cdots,u_{t-1},0),u_{t})+\bm{W}_{t}^{(\tau)}+\bm{x}_{t}^{(\tau)},

(6)

where
\|\bm{W}_{t}^{(\tau)}\|_{2}\leq CL\sum_{k=1}^{\tau}\rho^{k}\|w_{t-k}\|_{2}
and
\|\bm{x}_{t}^{(\tau)}\|_{2}\leq CL\rho^{\tau}\|x_{t-\tau}\|_{2}
.

For the equation (
6
), note that the term
g\circ f\circ\cdots\circ f
is a function of
u_{t},u_{t-1},\dots,u_{t-\tau}
. We convert this nonlinear function to a linear combination of basis functions taking a truncated number of control inputs, in which we establish a time-invariant system in the sense that the number of inputs is fixed:

\displaystyle g(f(\cdots f(f(0,u_{t-\tau},0),u_{t-\tau+1},0),\cdots,u_{t-1},0),u_{t})=G^{*}\cdot[\phi_{1}(\bm{U}_{t}^{(\tau)})~\cdots~\phi_{M}(\bm{U}_{t}^{(\tau)})]^{T},

(7)

where
\bm{U}_{t}^{(\tau)}=(u_{t},\dots,u_{t-\tau})\in(\mathcal{U})^{\tau+1}
is the stack of inputs from the time
t-\tau
to
t
, the basis functions
\phi_{i}:(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\tau+1}\to\mathbb{R}
are distinct nonlinear mappings for
i=1,\dots,M
, and the matrix
G^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times M}
explains how the nonlinear transformation of the inputs is mapped to the observation outputs. The number of basis functions
M
as well as how to design them can be chosen at the discretion of the user. Each basis function needs to be satisfy some mild assumptions formally presented as below.

Assumption 2.6

(Basis functions)
.

Each basis function
\phi_{i}
is designed to be
L_{\phi}
-Lipschitz (one can always find such basis functions since
f^{(k)}
and
g
are assumed to be Lipschitz continuous in Assumption
2.1
), namely,

\displaystyle|\phi_{i}(\bm{U}_{t}^{(\tau)})-\phi_{i}(\bm{\tilde{U}}_{t}^{(\tau)})|\leq L_{\phi}(\|u_{t}-\tilde{u}_{t}\|_{2}+\cdots+\|u_{t-\tau}-\tilde{u}_{t-\tau}\|_{2}),\quad\forall i=1,\dots,M,

(8)

for all
u_{t},\tilde{u}_{t},\dots,u_{t-\tau},\tilde{u}_{t-\tau}\in\mathcal{U}
.
Let
\Phi(\bm{U}_{t}^{(\tau)}):=[\phi_{1}(\bm{U}_{t}^{(\tau)})~\cdots~\phi_{M}(\bm{U}_{t}^{(\tau)})]^{T}
. Also, each basis function with inputs should excite the system for the exploration in learning the system. In other words, for all
t\geq\tau
, there exists a positive constant
\lambda
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi(\bm{U}_{t}^{(\tau)})\Phi(\bm{U}_{t}^{(\tau)})^{T}\right]\succeq\lambda^{2}I_{M}.

(9)

We further assume that
\Phi(0)=0
, meaning that zero input results in zero basis function values.

Remark 2.7
.

While most of the existing literature typically assumes zero-mean Gaussian inputs, we significantly relax this assumption by only requiring
u_{t}
to be sub-Gaussian (see Assumption
2.3
) and
\Phi(\bm{U}_{t}^{(\tau)})
to be Lipschitz and excite the system (see Assumption
2.6
).
A simple example that satisfies these assumptions is the aforementioned standard case of Gaussian inputs
N(0,\lambda^{2}I_{m})
. If the basis function has a simple form of
\Phi(\bm{U}_{t}^{(\tau)})=[u_{t}^{T}~\cdots~u_{t-\tau}^{T}]^{T}
, this implies that
\mathbb{E}[\Phi(\bm{U}_{t}^{(\tau)})\Phi(\bm{U}_{t}^{(\tau)})^{T}]=\lambda^{2}I_{\tau m}
, which satisfies the condition (
9
). Thus, Assumptions
2.3
and
2.6
characterize the conditions on control inputs in a much more general form applicable to the nonlinear system identification.

For a generic system with control inputs, if the disturbance
w_{t}
is always adversarial with nonzero-mean, any estimator may be misled by the adversary—even in a fully observed system. We present a concrete example; a related case without control inputs appears in
Kim and Lavaei 2024
. Consider the scalar linear system
x_{t+1}=a^{*}x_{t}+b^{*}u_{t}+w_{t}
, where the true system is
a^{*}=0.8
,
b^{*}=0.1
, and
x_{0}=1
. Suppose that
u_{t}
is
1
or
-1
, each with probability
1/2
, and

w_{t}
is chosen by an adversary as
-3x_{t}\cdot\text{sgn}(x_{t})
for all
t\geq 0
. In such a case, one always attains

\displaystyle x_{t}>0.1~\text{for}~t=0,2,4,\dots,\quad x_{t}<-0.1~\text{for}~t=1,3,5,\dots.

However, a valid estimation method may try to minimize the distance between
x_{t+1}
and
\hat{a}x_{t}+\hat{b}u_{t}
to find an estimate
\hat{a},\hat{b}
. Given that
\hat{b}
is correctly identified, one can only arrive at a negative estimate
\hat{a}
despite the true system
a^{*}>0
, since we have
\text{sgn}(x_{t})\neq\text{sgn}(x_{t+1}-b^{*}u_{t})
for all
t\geq 0
.

Hence, we may need to restrict the time instances
t
in which the adversary may be able to fully attack the system via
w_{t}
.
We now formally present the additional restriction on our disturbances
w_{t}
, under which the input-output mapping in (
6
) can accurately be estimated.

Assumption 2.8

(Attack probability)
.

w_{t}
is an adversarial attack at each time
t
with probability
p<\frac{1}{2\tau}
conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
. In other words, the adversary uses the information in the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
to generate disturbances
w_{t}
, which can therefore be correlated and possibly adversarial.
The attack probability specifies that the system is not under attack with probability
1-p
, meaning that

\mathbb{P}(w_{t}\equiv 0~|~\mathcal{F}_{t})=1-p

(10)

holds for all

t=0,\dots,T-1
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\bm{\sigma}\{x_{0},u_{0},\dots,u_{t},w_{0},\dots,w_{t-1}\}
.

Remark 2.9

(Choice of
\tau
)
.

In Assumption
2.8
, the attack probability depends on a user-defined constant
\tau
, which represents an input memory length.
As discussed in the introduction, it is inevitable to consider a finite-memory approximation, and the permissible attack probability
\frac{1}{2\tau}
—which depends on the memory length
\tau
—will accordingly restrict the ability of the adversary. It is worth noting that the term
\bm{W}_{t}^{(\tau)}
in (
6
) is identically zero if the system is not under attack for
\tau
consecutive periods;
i.e.

w_{t-1}=\dots=w_{t-\tau}=0
, which happens with probability at least
(1-p)^{\tau}
.
We have
(1-p)^{\tau}>0.5
with the restriction on attack probability
p<\frac{1}{2\tau}
, which we will leverage to prove the useful results on the estimation error.

Our goal is to accurately estimate the true matrix
G^{*}
that describes the input-output mapping from the control inputs
(u_{t},\dots,u_{t-\tau})
to the observation
y_{t}
.
Recall that the relationship (
6
) and (
7
) can be written as

\displaystyle y_{t}=G^{*}\cdot\Phi(\bm{U}_{t}^{(\tau)})+\bm{W}_{t}^{(\tau)}+\bm{x}_{t}^{(\tau)}

(11)

for all
t\geq\tau
.
Given a time horizon
T
, we aim to learn the true system
G^{*}
using the following
\ell_{1}
-norm estimator based on partial observations
y_{\tau},\dots,y_{T-1}
and control inputs
u_{0},\dots,u_{T-1}
:

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{T}=\argmin_{G}\sum_{t=\tau}^{T-1}\left\|y_{t}-G\cdot\Phi(\bm{U}_{t}^{(\tau)})\right\|_{1}

(12)

Under the stated assumptions, we will show in the next section that the
\ell_{1}
-norm estimator achieves the optimal estimation error
\Theta(\rho^{\tau})
, where
\rho
is the contraction factor in Assumption
2.1
and

Main Theorems and Analysis Outline

In this section, we will state our main theorems on bounding the estimation error to identify

3.1
Main Theorem

Our main theorem holds under the stated assumptions, which incorporates non-Gaussian inputs and correlated, nonzero-mean, adversarial disturbances, with an attack probability
p
no greater than
\frac{1}{2\tau}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
,
2.3
,
2.4
,
2.6
, and
2.8
hold. Consider

. Let
G^{*}
be the true matrix governing the system (

\hat{G}_{T}
denote a solution to the
\ell_{1}
-norm estimator given in (

\delta\in(0,1]

\displaystyle T\geq\Theta\left(\frac{\tau^{2}\nu^{8}}{(2(1-p)^{\tau}-1)^{2}}\left[M\log\left(\frac{\nu}{2(1-p)^{\tau}-1}\right)+\log\left(\frac{r}{\delta}\right)\right]\right),

(13)

\displaystyle\|G^{*}-\hat{G}_{T}\|_{F}\leq\Theta\left(\frac{\rho^{\tau}L\sqrt{r}}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{(\sigma_{u}+\sigma_{w})\tau\nu^{3}}{(1-\rho)(2(1-p)^{\tau}-1)}\right)

(14)

1-\delta
.

Remark 3.2
.

Our main theorem states that after the time given in (
13
), the estimation error of
\Theta(\rho^{\tau})
is achieved, considering that additional polynomial terms in
\tau
are dominated by the exponential decay in
\tau
. However, notice that the estimation error does not decay as the time

T
increases, and thus cannot converge to zero. While this error bound decreases as
\tau
grows, the memory length
\tau
will be chosen as a finite number at the user’s discretion, and thus the bound should be treated as a positive constant.
This suggests that the user may want to choose a sufficiently long

to obtain a smaller estimation error. However, increasing

has three drawbacks: First, it restricts the attack probability as stated in Remark
2.9
. Second, the required time (

\Phi
may become significantly complex to incorporate longer history, and naturally the optimization problem needs far more computations. Thus, even though the estimation error may decrease with increasing
\tau

Remark 3.5
.

We have established the lower bound
\Theta(\frac{\rho^{\tau}L\sqrt{r}}{\lambda})
, which implies that the estimation error is bounded below by a positive constant for any estimator due to adversarial attacks. While this matches the upper bound (
14

T\geq 0
, it remains unclear whether the gap between the upper and lower bounds can be further tightened.
Meanwhile, in Appendix
E

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we study the identification of the input-output mappings of nonlinear dynamical systems, where control inputs are not necessarily Gaussian and the disturbances are potentially adversarial. We formulate a time-invariant input-output mapping using a linear combination of basis functions taking the input history, where we decouple the control inputs and disturbances. We propose a problem class that accurately identifies the input-output mapping, characterized by a restriction on the attack probability. We then prove that the estimation error using
\ell_{1}
-norm estimator amounts to
\Theta(\rho^{\tau})
under the presence of adversarial attacks and show that this bound is optimal by providing a matching lower bound. Future directions include extending our analysis to a nonparametric approach under the same assumptions, where the estimator inherently involves an infinite-dimensional problem such as optimization over a function class.

Appendix A
Details on Related Works

Fully and Partially Observed Systems.
In system identification, based on the degree of state observability, systems are often categorized as fully observed and partially observed systems.
In fully observed systems, all states are measured, thus the outputs are
identical to the states. In such systems, numerous methods have been proposed to recover the underlying system, e.g., least-squared methods
[
Simchowitz et al. 2018
,
Faradonbeh et al. 2018
,
Jedra and Proutiere 2020
]
,
\ell_{2}
- norm estimator
[
Yalcin et al. 2024
,
Zhang et al. 2024
]
, and
\ell_{1}
-norm estimator
[
Kim and Lavaei 2024
]
. However, in many real-world applications—such as robotics
[
Lauri et al. 2023
]
, healthcare
[
Alagoz 2014
]
, and safety-critical systems
[
Bensoussan 1992
]
—not all states are observable, giving rise to the partially observed system setting. In this case, system identification becomes substantially more challenging. A growing body of research has addressed this challenge: for instance,
Sarkar et al. 2021
,
Oymak and Ozay 2022
identified the systems using least-squares methods, assuming disturbances are independent and follow Gaussian or sub-Gaussian distribution with zero-mean.
Simchowitz et al. 2019
extended the least-squares method to setups where the disturbances can be predicted based on past observations. However, little research has been conducted in the partially observed systems when the disturbances are adversarially selected. Only recently,
Kim and Lavaei 2025
investigated system identification using
\ell_{1}
-norm estimator and allowed adversarial disturbances.

Nonparametric and Parametric approaches.
Nonparametric approaches operate over infinite-dimensional function spaces, often leveraging techniques such as kernel methods and deep learning to model complex system dynamics
[
Greblicki and Pawlak 2008
,
Ziemann et al. 2022
]
. These methods are highly flexible, making them well-suited for capturing behaviors without strong structural assumptions. However, they often come with significant computational overhead and reduced interpretability. In contrast, parametric methods approximate the system using a finite set of basis functions, typically chosen based on prior knowledge or structural insights. This approach yields models that are more interpretable, computationally efficient, and easier to analyze—especially when the chosen function class aligns well with the true system dynamics
[
Chen 1995
,
Giannakis and Serpedin 2001
]
. Moreover, the parametric framework facilitates model selection and regularization, enabling effective control over model complexity and reducing the risk of overfitting through techniques such as cross-validation or penalization.

Appendix B
Preliminaries on sub-Gaussian variables

In this work, we consider both inputs and attacks on the system to be sub-Gaussian variables in which the tail event rarely occurs. We use the definition given in
Vershynin 2018
.

Definition B.1

(sub-Gaussian scalar variables)
.

A random variable
w\in\mathbb{R}
is called sub-Gaussian if there exists
c>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\Bigr[\exp\Bigr(\frac{w^{2}}{c^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr]\leq 2.

(24)

Its sub-Gaussian norm is denoted by
\|w\|_{\psi_{2}}
and defined as

\|w\|_{\psi_{2}}=\inf\left\{c>0:\mathbb{E}\Bigr[\exp\Bigr(\frac{w^{2}}{c^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr]\leq 2\right\}.

(25)

Note that the
\psi_{2}
-norm satisfies properties of norms: positive definiteness, homogeneity, and triangle inequality.
We have the following properties for a sub-Gaussian variable
w
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|w|^{k}\right]\leq(C_{1}\sqrt{k})^{k}\quad\forall k=1,2,\dots,

(26)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|w|\geq s)\leq 2\exp(-s^{2}/C_{2}^{2}),\quad\forall s\geq 0,

(27)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\theta w)]\leq\exp(\theta^{2}C_{3}^{2}),\quad\forall\theta\in\mathbb{R}\quad\text{if }\mathbb{E}[w]=0,

(28)

where
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},
and
\|w\|_{\psi_{2}}
are positive absolute constants that differ from each other by at most an absolute constant factor. For example, there exist
K,\tilde{K}>0
such that
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\leq K\|w\|_{\psi_{2}}
and
\|w\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\tilde{K}\max\{c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\}
.
Note that the property (
27
) is also called Hoeffding’s inequality, which can be split into two inequalities if
\mathbb{E}[w]=0
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(w\geq s)\leq\exp(-s^{2}/C_{2}^{2}),\quad\forall s\geq 0,

(29a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(w\leq-s)\leq\exp(-s^{2}/C_{2}^{2}),\quad\forall s\geq 0.

(29b)

We introduce the following useful lemmas to analyze the sum of independent noncentral sub-Gaussians
[
Vershynin 2018
]
.

Lemma B.2

(Centering lemma)
.

If
w
is a sub-Gaussian variable satisfying (
24
), then
w-\mathbb{E}[w]
is also a sub-Gaussian variable with

\|w-\mathbb{E}[w]\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\Theta(\|w\|_{\psi_{2}}).

(30)

Lemma B.3

(Sum of mean-zero independent sub-Gaussians)
.

Let
w_{1},\dots,w_{N}
be independent, mean zero, sub-Gaussian random variables. Then,
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}
is also sub-Gaussian and its sub-Gaussian norm is
\Theta\bigr((\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|w_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2})^{1/2}\bigr)
.

To provide the analysis of high-dimensional systems, we introduce the notion of sub-Gaussian vectors below.

Definition B.4

(sub-Gaussian vector variables)
.

A random vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called sub-Gaussian if for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
w^{T}x
is a sub-Gaussian variable. Its norm is defined as

\|w\|_{\psi_{2}}=\sup_{\|x\|_{2}\leq 1,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|w^{T}x\|_{\psi_{2}}.

(31)

For example, if
w
is a sub-Gaussian vector with a norm
\gamma
, then the sub-Gaussian norm of
\|w\|_{2}
is also
\gamma
, considering that
w^{T}\frac{w}{\|w\|_{2}}=\|w\|_{2}
.

Throughout the paper, we will assume that the inputs and attacks injected into the system are indeed sub-Gaussian vectors. For example, the
m
-dimensional Gaussian variables and the
r
-dimensional bounded attacks are indeed sub-Gaussian vectors.
