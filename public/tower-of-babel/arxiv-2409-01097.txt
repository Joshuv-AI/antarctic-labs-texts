Nested Bregman Iterations for Decomposition Problems
T. Wolf, D. Driggs, K. Papafitsoros, E. Resmerita, C.-B. Schönlieb

Abstract
We consider the task of image reconstruction while simultaneously decomposing the reconstructed image into components with different features. A commonly used tool for this is a variational approach with an infimal convolution of appropriate functions as a regularizer. Especially for noise corrupted observations, incorporating these functionals into the classical method of Bregman iterations provides a robust method for obtaining an overall good approximation of the true image, by stopping the iteration early according to a discrepancy principle. However, crucially, the quality of the separate components depends further on the proper choice of the regularization weights associated to the infimally convoluted functionals. Here, we propose the method of Nested Bregman iterations to improve a decomposition in a structured way. This allows to transform the task of choosing the weights into the problem of stopping the iteration according to a meaningful criterion based on normalized cross-correlation. We discuss the well-definedness and the convergence behavior of the proposed method, and illustrate its strength numerically with various image decomposition tasks employing infimal convolution functionals.

Introduction

Image decomposition is an image processing task in which, given a possibly degraded version
f\in Y
of some ground truth image
x^{\dagger}\in X
, with
X,Y
being some Hilbert spaces, one seeks a decomposition of
x^{\dagger}
into two or more components. Let us assume that
x^{\dagger}
and
f
are related through the equation

Ax^{\dagger}=f
, where
A:X\to Y
is a bounded, linear operator modeling some degradation process (the
forward
operator), e.g. a convolution operator. One is interested in finding ideal
x_{1}^{\dagger},\dots,x_{n}^{\dagger}
such that
x^{\dagger}:=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\dagger}
satisfies
Ax^{\dagger}=f
, where each of the components
x_{1}^{\dagger},\dots,x_{n}^{\dagger}
is characterized by some specific features. These features can be geometric in nature, with examples including piecewise constant, piecewise smooth or periodic structures. On the other hand, images also have less-structured, highly oscillatory components like texture and other fine-scaled features.
Decomposing an image in such components is a challenging problem, and it is often exacerbated by the addition of a highly oscillatory function
\eta\in Y
to
f
, modelling some random noise component and resulting in noisy data
f^{\delta}
. Here we assume that the noise is additive and satisfies
\eta:=f^{\delta}-f
, with
\|f^{\delta}-f\|_{Y}\leq\delta
. For simplicity, we focus on the case of two components of the image, denoted
u
and
v
, satisfying
x^{\dagger}=u^{\dagger}+v^{\dagger}
. Thus, one ends up with the following relations:

Ax^{\dagger}+\eta=f^{\delta},

(1)

A(u^{\dagger}+v^{\dagger})+\eta=f^{\delta}.

(2)

Notably, the classical task of denoising can be regarded as a special instance of image decomposition where
v\equiv 0
. In that case,
x=u
is obtained as a solution of a variational problem (Tikhonov regularization)

\min_{x\in X}\;\frac{\lambda}{2}F(Ax,f^{\delta})+J(x),

(3)

or alternatively via solving the corresponding Morozov regularization problem

\min_{F(Ax,f^{\delta})\leq\delta}J(x).

(4)

Here
F
is the data-fidelity term which enforces data consistency, while
J
is a regularization functional which makes the problem well-posed and imposes some prior structure on the reconstruction. For instance,
H^{1}
regularization, i.e.,
J(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla x\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
promotes smooth reconstructions
[
46
]
, while the total variation seminorm
J(x)=\left\|Dx\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}

[
43
,
13
]
and its higher order extension, the total generalized variation (TGV)
[
7
]
,
J(x)=TGV(x)
, promote piecewise constant and piecewise affine reconstructions, respectively. The regularization parameter
\lambda>0
in (
3
) balances the effects of the data-fidelity and the regularization term. Using the terminology of image decomposition,
\lambda
influences how much of the structure imposed by
J
will go to the component
x=u
and how much of the noise will be incorporated in
\eta
. Hence, its numerical value has to be chosen carefully to ensure a meaningful reconstruction, which in this case is the decomposition into the estimation of ground truth and noise.

While the choice of
F
in denoising is typically guided by the statistics of the noise, e.g. the
\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
or
\|\cdot\|_{L^{1}}
norms for Gaussian or impulse noise, respectively,
[
37
,
13
]
, more sophisticated fidelity terms can be used in the case where (
3
) is used for decomposing
x^{\dagger}
into cartoon/structure and texture. A prominent example is Meyer’s
G
-norm
[
36
]
, for which various approximations have been proposed
[
47
,
38
,
34
]
. For our purposes, we will make use of
\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_{Y}^{2}
as a fidelity term, often omitting the subscript, while the decomposition will be achieved via a proper choice of
J
.
With regard to image decomposition where the aim is to recover two components
u
and
v
, it has been shown that it can be quite advantageous if one employs regularization functionals
J
which are defined via an infimal convolution of the form

J(x)=(\alpha g\square\beta h)(x):=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in X\\
x=u+v\end{subarray}}\{\alpha g(u)+\beta h(v)\},\quad\alpha,\beta>0.

(5)

Here, the structures of
u
and
v
are shaped by
g
and
h
, respectively. A plethora of such examples can be found in the literature. For instance, a TV-TV
2
infimal convolution was suggested in
[
13
]
, where
g(\cdot)=\alpha|\cdot|_{\mathrm{TV}}
and
h(\cdot)=\beta|\cdot|_{\mathrm{TV}^{2}}
, the second order total variation, in order to promote reconstructions that consist of piecewise constant and affine parts. This type of infimal convolution yields approximations of similar structure with TGV, where the two functionals coincide for dimension one
[
41
,
15
]
. An analogous functional, employing the
L^{p}
norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}}
instead of
|\cdot|_{\mathrm{TV}^{2}}
was explored in
[
9
,
11
]
, whose particular case of
p=2
corresponds to the well-known Huber TV functional
[
26
]
. It turns out that in the latter case, the resulting functional has also a tight connection with the TV-
H^{1}
infimal convolution, see Example
3.1
in the present work for more details. Infimal convolutions of TGV type functionals have also been considered in the literature, mainly for dynamic imaging
[
44
,
25
]
, but also in relation to image decomposition. One such example is the infimal convolution of TGV and the
oscillating
TGV functional,
TGV^{osci}
,
[
19
]
, aiming at decomposing images into piecewise smooth and oscillating components.
A general framework for constructing infimal convolution regularizers from frequently used functionals was introduced in
[
8
]
, where we also refer for further references.

Using an infimal convolution functional in a variational setting that employs a general forward operator yields the following minimization problem

\min_{u,v\in X}\;\frac{1}{2}\|A(u+v)-f^{\delta}\|^{2}+\alpha g(u)+\beta h(v),

(6)

where the parameter
\lambda
in (
3
) has been absorbed into
\alpha
and
\beta
. Similarly to our previous remark about the proper selection of
\lambda
, here the values of the regularization weights
\alpha
and
\beta
have to be chosen wisely. Large values of
\alpha
, respectively
\beta
, will enforce the component
v
, respectively
u
, to be overrepresented in the decomposition. A significant amount of recent research has been focusing on the automatic selection of these parameters. A prominent tool used for this is bilevel optimization. Guided by quality indicators such as the PSNR, SSIM or some norm, many of these approaches aim at optimizing the quality of the overall reconstruction
x=u+v
, but
not
solely the one of
u
or
v

[
12
,
42
,
24
]
.
However, optimizing the parameters with respect to the quality of the decomposition is a very delicate problem. To the best of our knowledge, there is no consensus on how to quantify the quality of a decomposition. While earlier approaches manually select parameters
[
2
,
32
]
, more recent works use additional information of the expected components to construct method dependent parameter choice rules, often enforcing a balancing principle for the individual regularizers
[
27
,
29
,
28
]
. Another method to assess the quality of a decomposition is to consider the dissimilarity of the individual components, rather than their similarity with the true ones. Based on the work
[
3
]
, the (cross-)correlation of individual components has been used successfully for automatizing the parameter selection via bilevel methods
[
20
,
21
]
.

Looking back at problems of type (
3
), one approach that avoids the a priori selection of the regularization parameter
\lambda
is the Bregman iteration
[
10
,
6
]
. There, the solutions of a series of adaptive variational problems converge to a
J
-minimizing solution of
Ax=f
, in the noiseless case and under certain assumptions, see Section
3.3
for details. In practice, the iterations approximate the noisy data. Therefore, employing some discrepancy principle to stop the iteration early typically results in a good reconstruction.

Our contribution

In this work, we propose a strategy for achieving a desirable decomposition based on appropriately terminating an iterative procedure rather than making a suitable a priori choice of the regularization weights
\alpha
and
\beta
in an infimal convolution regularization functional. We achieve this by introducing the idea of Nested Bregman iterations combined with Morozov regularization. The starting point is the Morozov regularization problem

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in X\\
\|A(u+v)-f^{\delta}\|\leq\delta\end{subarray}}\{\alpha g(u)+\beta h(v)\},

(7)

where we also allow the case
\delta=0
for the noiseless case. The Nested Bregman iteration scheme consists of an
outer
iteration, which corresponds to running Bregman iterations for (
7
) by considering
g
as a regularizer and
h
the fidelity term, with the aim of decreasing the share of the component
v
in the decomposition. This avoids an overrepresentation of
h
, which occurs when
x\simeq v
. At every outer iteration, a minimization problem of the type

(u_{l},v_{l})\in\argmin\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in X\\
\|A(u+v)-f^{\delta}\|\leq\delta\end{subarray}}D_{\alpha g}^{p_{l-1}}(u,u_{l-1})+\beta h(v)

(8)

needs to be solved, where
D_{\alpha g}^{p_{l-1}}(u,u_{l-1})
denotes the Bregman distance between
u
and
u_{l-1}
at some appropriate
p_{l-1}\in\partial(\alpha g)(u_{l-1})
, see Section
2
for the definition of Bregman distances. The inner loop of this iteration is an iterative method for solving (
8
). For the case of a noise-free observation, we propose to use Bregman iterations with data-fitting term
\frac{1}{2}\left\|A(u+v)-f\right\|^{2}
and penalty
D_{\alpha g}^{p_{l-1}}(u,u_{l-1})+\beta h(v)
. This procedure motivates the notion of Nested Bregman iterations. For noise corrupted data, (
8
) constitutes a regularization of the ill-posed equation (
2
) and can be solved efficiently by methods from convex optimization. Conceptually, Nested Bregman iterations generate a sequence of regularized solutions to (
2
), in which the contribution of component
v
to the decomposition is iteratively decreased. We perform a detailed analysis of the scheme, in which we show the existence of the required subgradients that define the corresponding Bregman distances, examine the scheme’s convergence properties, and justify the well-definedness of the associated minimization problems for selected infimal convolution regularizers:
L^{1}-H^{1}
for separating peaks from an otherwise smooth signal,
TV-H^{1}
for piecewise constant - smooth decompositions, as well as
TGV-TGV^{osci}
for decomposing an image into piecewise smooth and oscillatory components. We then conduct a series of numerical experiments illustrating that the proposed Nested Bregman iteration scheme equipped with a cross-correlation based stopping criterion can recover
x=u+v
at least as good with respect to PSNR as related variational methods with optimal parameter choice obtained via either grid search or a bilevel method. Furthermore, in the same time the correct decomposition is attained. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time such a rule is used to early terminate an iterative method for an ill-posed problem. Crucially, this can be achieved by setting a suboptimal initialization for the regularizing weights, requiring minimal experimentation and manual tuning.

2
Preliminaries

Notation-wise, unless it is ambiguous, we will avoid using subscripts for the inner products and norms of the Hilbert spaces
X
and
Y
.
We first recall some notions and properties of convex functionals, subdifferentials, Bregman distances and infimal convolutions.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
J:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
be a convex functional.

•

The domain of
J
is defined by
\text{dom}\,J=\left\{x\in X:J(x)<+\infty\right\}
. One says that
J
is proper if
\text{dom}\,J\neq\emptyset
.

•

The subdifferential of
J
at
x_{0}\in\text{dom}\,J
is

\partial J(x_{0})=\left\{x^{*}\in X^{*}:\left\langle x^{*},x-x_{0}\right\rangle\leq J(x)-J(x_{0})\text{ for all }x\in X\right\}.

•

The Bregman distance between points
x_{0},x\in\text{dom}\,J
at
x^{*}\in\partial J(x_{0})
is given by

D_{J}^{x^{\ast}}(x,x_{0})=J(x)-J(x_{0})-\left\langle x^{*},x-x_{0}\right\rangle.

Several subdifferentials calculus rules are mentioned below.

Proposition 2.1
.

1.

Let
J:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
be a proper, convex and lower semicontinuous functional and let
x_{0}\in\text{dom}\,J
. Then

\partial(\lambda J)(x_{0})=\lambda\partial J(x_{0})
, for any
\lambda>0
.

2.

Let
J_{1},J_{2}:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
be proper, convex and lower semicontinuous and let
x_{0}\in\text{dom}\,J_{1}\cap\text{dom}\,J_{2}
. If
(\text{dom}\,J_{1})^{\circ}\cap\text{dom}\,J_{2}\neq\emptyset
, then

\partial(J_{1}+J_{2})(x_{0})=\partial J_{1}(x_{0})+\partial J_{2}(x_{0}).

3.

Let
A:X\to Y
be a bounded linear operator,
J:Y\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
be proper, convex and lower semicontinuous and
x_{0}\in X
such that
Ax_{0}\in\text{dom}\,J
.
If the interior of
\text{dom}\,J
and the image of
A
have non-empty intersection, then

\partial(J\circ A)(x_{0})=A^{*}\partial J(Ax_{0}),

where
A^{*}
denotes the adjoint of
A
.

Proof.

See Proposition 16.6, Theorem 16.47(i) and Proposition 6.9(vii) in
[
5
]
.
∎

In order to enforce structures in a decomposition, we will use proper, convex and lower semicontinuous functionals
g,h:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
. Finding a good decomposition of a solution to the ill-posed problem means solving the constrained minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{{u,v\in X}}\;\alpha g(u)+\beta h(v)

\displaystyle s.t.\quad A(u+v)=f,

(9)

where
\alpha,\beta>0
are weights that balance how much the approximate solution is represented in each component. Often, it is useful to consider the two variable problem (
9
) as a single variable one. For this, we recall the concept of infimal convolution.

Definition 2.2
.

The infimal convolution of two functions
g,h:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
is defined as

J(x):=(g\square h)(x)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{u,v\in X}\\
x=u+v\end{subarray}}\{g(u)+h(v)\}.

(10)

It is called exact at
x\in X
if the infimum is attained, that is, there exists
\bar{u}\in X
such that

J(x)=\inf_{{u\in X}}\;\{g(u)+h(x-u)\}=g(\bar{u})+h(x-\bar{u})=g(\bar{u})+h(\bar{v}),

(11)

with
\bar{v}=x-\bar{u}
.
Note that
g\square h=h\square g
.

This means, if the infimal convolution
J=\alpha g\square\beta h
is exact, (
9
) becomes

\displaystyle\min_{{x\in X}}\;J(x)

\displaystyle s.t.\quad Ax=f.

(12)

We finally recall the following properties regarding infimal convolution of proper, convex and lower semicontinuous functions:

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
g,h:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
be proper, convex, lower semicontinuous functions, and
J=g\square h
. Then:

1.

J
is convex.

2.

If one of the following holds:

(a)

g
is supercoercive, that is
\displaystyle{\lim_{\|u\|\to\infty}\frac{g(u)}{\|u\|}=\infty}
,

(b)

h
is bounded from below and
g
is coercive, that is
\displaystyle{\lim_{\|u\|\to\infty}g(u)=\infty}
,

then
J
is coercive, proper, lower semicontinuous and consequently exact on
X
.

(iii)

\text{dom}\,(g\square h)=\text{dom}\,g+\text{dom}\,h
.

(iv)

\partial(g\square h)(x)=\partial g(\bar{u})\cap\partial h(\bar{v})
, for any
x\in dom\,J
and
\bar{u},\bar{v}\in X
such that (
11
) holds.

(v)

If
h
is bounded from below, and both
g
and
h
have (weakly) compact sublevel sets, then
J
has (weakly) compact sublevel sets. In particular, if
g
and
h
are coercive, then
J
is coercive.

Proof.

For
(i)-(iii)
, see Propositions 12.11, 12.14 and 12.6 in
[
5
]
, for
(iv)
, see Corollary 2.4.7 in
[
48
]
and for
(v)
, see Theorems 2.3 and 2.9 in
[
45
]
.
∎

For the remainder of this work, we will assume that the functionals
g
and
h
are proper, convex, lower-semicontinuous and non-negative. Further, we assume that there is
u_{0}\in X
with
g(u_{0})=0
. This implies
0\in\partial g(u_{0})
, so that by setting
p_{0}=0
, we can write
g(u)=D_{g}^{p_{0}}(u,u_{0})
for all
u\in\text{dom}\,g
. These assumptions will be crucial for showing the well-definedness and convergence properties of the suggested method.

Regularization methods

Before presenting our proposed method for decomposition problems, we review classical regularization methods and describe their numerical performance for decomposition problems. To this end, we fix a
TV-H^{1}
infimal convolution regularization as detailed next.

Example 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a square and consider
x^{\dagger}=u^{\dagger}+v^{\dagger}
, where
u^{\dagger}
is a smooth component and
v^{\dagger}
is the characteristic function of a smaller square
S\subsetneq\Omega
. The operator
A\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\Omega))
is given as a convolution operator with a Gaussian kernel
k
, that is,
x\mapsto Ax=k*x
. In order to illustrate the behavior of multiple regularization methods, we will use this example for numerical experiments with a kernel of mean
0
and variance
1
. The noise
\eta
is realized by a centered normally distributed random variable with mean
0
and variance
0.05
. We display the corresponding images in Figure
1
. Taking into account the decomposition structure of
x^{\dagger}
, we employ the squared
H^{1}-
seminorm as a regularizer
g
for the
u
component, and the total variation as
h
for the
v
component. We will properly define and analyze the functional arising from the infimal convolution of those regularizers in Section
5
. For now, we just formally treat them as

g(u)=\frac{\alpha}{2}\int\limits_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2},\quad h(v)=\beta\int\limits_{\Omega}\left|\nabla v\right|.

(13)

3.1
Tikhonov Regularization

The idea of Tikhonov regularization is to approach (
9
) by choosing an additional parameter
\lambda>0
and solving

\min\limits_{x\in X}\;\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|Ax-f\right\|^{2}+J(x),

(14)

where
J(x)=\left(\alpha g\square\beta h\right)(x)
. If the infimal convolution is exact, this simplifies to the more intuitive problem of solving

\min\limits_{u,v\in X}\;\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|A(u+v)-f\right\|^{2}+\alpha g(u)+\beta h(v).

(15)

Even though computing solutions of (
15
) can often be relatively cheap, a single-step variational method often comes with some downsides. First, minimizing
\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|A(u+v)-f\right\|^{2}
instead of solving
A(u+v)=f
means that we are no longer guaranteed to obtain a solution of the ill-posed problem. In applications, only perturbed data
f^{\delta}
instead of the true image
f
are available. Thus, if some information about the noise (for instance an estimate of the form
\left\|f-f^{\delta}\right\|\leq\delta
in the case of additive noise) is available, one chooses the regularization weights accordingly, so that
\left\|Ax-f^{\delta}\right\|
is in the range of the noise level
\delta
. Finding optimal parameters for these weights can for example be done using a bilevel approach
[
29
,
40
]
. However, solving the corresponding bilevel problems can be computationally expensive or unfeasible for highly sophisticated choices of regularizing functionals.

Note that division by
\lambda
does not affect the minimizers of (
15
), meaning that for practical purposes only
\alpha
and
\beta
need to be tuned, while
\lambda
can be kept constant. Figure
2
shows the decomposition results for deblurring via Tikhonov regularization for different choices of
\alpha
and
\beta
, where we set
\lambda=1
for convenience. Then, in order to determine appropriate parameters for the infimal convolution, we fix the ratio
\frac{\alpha}{\beta}
, and use a bisection method to find
\alpha
such that the discrepancy
\delta-\left\|A(u+v)-f\right\|
is within a
0.1\%
margin of the noise level
\delta
. Note that this is a successive tuning of the involved parameters and that finding a proper parameter choice for a given ratio is equivalent to determining
\lambda
in (
15
) with
\alpha
and
\beta
fixed.

We observe that the optimal PSNR value of the reconstruction
u+v
under the performed parameter search strategy is obtained for
\frac{\alpha}{\beta}=47
(Line 3 in Figure
2
). Notably, with this ratio, the reconstructed components
u
and
v
are also the closest to the original ones. As expected, if
\alpha
is chosen too large (relatively to
\beta
), the piecewise constant component
v
is overrepresented (Line 2 in Figure
2
), whereas a too small choice of
\alpha
yields an overrepresentation of the smooth component
u
(Line 5 in Figure
2
).

In conclusion, we observe that the variational approach produces suitable reconstructions with the right choice of parameters. However, finding such parameters is computationally expensive. For our strategy, the tuning of the parameters amounts to finding the optimal parameters for a given ratio
\frac{\alpha}{\beta}
and then improving this ratio. Therefore, our aim is to construct a method which yields a good reconstruction with a meaningful decomposition in a more systematic and, consequently, more efficient manner.

3.2
Morozov Regularization

Instead of choosing the regularization parameters in (
14
) according to the discrepancy principle, one can consider the constraint problem

\begin{cases}&\min\limits_{x\in X}J(x)\\
&s.t.\ \left\|Ax-f^{\delta}\right\|\leq\delta.\end{cases}

(16)

This method is known as Morozov regularization or residual method, see e.g.
[
30
,
35
,
22
,
31
]
. Assuming that
J=(\alpha g)\square(\beta h)
and that the infimal convolution is exact, we can again simplify (
16
), that is, we are interested in the solution of

\begin{cases}&\min\limits_{u,v\in X}\alpha g(u)+\beta h(v)\\
&s.t.\ \left\|A(u+v)-f^{\delta}\right\|\leq\delta.\end{cases}

(17)

In fact, under mild conditions, (
16
) is equivalent to (
14
). Namely, if there is
x\in\text{dom}\,J
such that
\left\|Ax-f^{\delta}\right\|<\delta
, then by
[
4
, Theorem 3.9]
, there exists
\lambda

such that the solution
x^{*}
of (
16
) minimizes (
14
). Conversely, a solution
x_{\lambda}
of (
14
) solves

\begin{cases}&\min\limits_{x\in X}J(x)\\
&s.t.\ \left\|Ax-f^{\delta}\right\|\leq\left\|Ax_{\lambda}-f^{\delta}\right\|.\end{cases}

The Morozov formulation of the variational regularization overcomes the issue of choosing the parameter
\lambda
. Thus, dividing by
\beta
in (
17
), we only need to choose an appropriate value for the ratio
\frac{\alpha}{\beta}
so that the decomposition problem can essentially be reduced to tuning a single parameter. For a more detailed analysis of the relation between Tikhonov and Morozov regularization in the case that
J(x)=\left\|Lx\right\|^{p}
for some linear map
L
and
p>1
, the reader is referred to
[
30
, Section 3.5]
.

3.3
Bregman Iterations

Another way of overcoming the need to choose the regularization parameter
\lambda
in (
14
) is performing Bregman iterations (see, for instance,
[
39
,
6
]
).

The initial step of Bregman iterations consists of computing

x_{1}\in\argmin\limits_{x\in X}\left\{\frac{{\lambda}}{2}\left\|Ax-f\right\|^{2}+J(x)\right\}.

(18)

Next, set
\xi_{1}=-\lambda A^{*}(Ax_{1}-f)\in\partial J(x_{1})
and iterate for
k\geq 2
:

4
Nested Bregman iterations

As motivated in the seminal paper
[
39
]
, by using Bregman iterations, one can avoid choosing the regularization parameter
\lambda
in the Tikhonov approach. Recall that upon division by
\alpha
or
\beta
, problem (
9
) can be seen as a variational problem with only one parameter to choose. The idea of Nested Bregman iterations is to use Bregman iterations to transform the problem of choosing this parameter into the problem of stopping an iterative procedure. This means the following: Assume that the component
v
is overrepresented in the decomposition, i.e.
x\simeq v
. We therefore aim to decrease its share in the decomposition. However, instead of increasing the value of
\beta
and solving (
9
) again with the adjusted weight, we replace
\alpha g
by a suitable Bregman distance and minimize again. That is, we run Bregman iterations with data fidelity term
h
and regularizer
g
. Hence, we iteratively solve constraint problems of the form (
9
), where instead of
\alpha g
we use a Bregman distance associated with this function at each step. Since the minimizers of these constraint problems are precisely the
J
-minimizing solutions of
A(u+v)=f
, they can be computed as the weak limit points of Bregman iterations (see Theorem
3.1
). Hence, we obtain a Nesting of Bregman iterations. However, when dealing with noise corrupted observations, multiple ways to define Nested Bregman iterations are thinkable. In the following, we will present two options in this respect, and outline their advantages.
Since the algorithms we propose do not involve any choice of parameters, we simplify notation and write
g
and
h
instead of
\alpha g
and
\beta h
. That is, we assume that an initial guess for the weightings is implicitly contained in the functionals
g
and
h
.

(i.e.

).

Remark 4.1

If

For the remainder of this section, we will assume that all the minimization problems considered actually admit minimizers. The verification of this will be done for the concrete examples that we consider. However, we still need to verify the well-definedness of the choice of
p_{l}
. While the existence of

such that

follows under mild assumptions from the optimality condition of (

needs additional assumptions on the functions

. As a first step, we compute the subgradient of the indicator function for the constraint

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
C=\left\{x\in X:Ax=f\right\}
and denote by

\mathcal{I}_{C}(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }x\in C\\
\infty&\text{ if }x\notin C\end{cases}

(29)

the indicator function of
C
. Then

\partial\mathcal{I}_{C}(x)=\text{Ker}\left(A\right)^{\perp}

(30)

for all
x\in C
.

Proof.

Let
\bar{x}\in C
. Since
C
is convex, it holds

\partial\mathcal{I}_{C}(\bar{x})=\left\{x^{*}\in X^{*}:\left\langle x^{*},\tilde{x}-\bar{x}\right\rangle\leq 0\text{ for all }\tilde{x}\in X\text{ with }A\tilde{x}=f\right\}.

Now first let
x^{*}\in\text{Ker}\left(A\right)^{\perp}
and fix
\tilde{x}
such that
A\tilde{x}=f
. Now,
\bar{x}\in C
implies
A(\tilde{x}-\bar{x})=0
, i.e.
\tilde{x}-\bar{x}\in\text{Ker}\left(A\right)
. Thus, it must be
\left\langle x^{*},\tilde{x}-\bar{x}\right\rangle=0
and therefore
x^{*}\in\partial\mathcal{I}_{C}(\bar{x})
, which proves
{\text{Ker}\left(A\right)^{\perp}}\subseteq\partial\mathcal{I}_{C}(\bar{x})
. Conversely, let
x^{*}\in\partial\mathcal{I}_{C}(\bar{x})
and
\tilde{x}\in X
such that
A\tilde{x}=f
, this means
\left\langle x^{*},\tilde{x}-\bar{x}\right\rangle\leq 0
. But since
A(2\bar{x}-\tilde{x})=f
, it must also be
\left\langle x^{*},\bar{x}-\tilde{x}\right\rangle=\left\langle x^{*},(2\bar{x}-\tilde{x})-{\bar{x}}\right\rangle\leq 0
. That yields
\left\langle x^{*},\bar{x}-\tilde{x}\right\rangle=0
, hence we have

\partial\mathcal{I}_{C}(\bar{x})=\left\{x^{*}\in X:\left\langle x^{*},\tilde{x}-\bar{x}\right\rangle=0\text{ for all }\tilde{x}\in{X}\text{ with }A\tilde{x}=f\right\}

Thus, the equality
C=\left\{x\in X:Ax=f\right\}=\text{Ker}\left(A\right)+\left\{\bar{x}\right\}
implies
\partial\mathcal{I}_{C}(\bar{x})\subseteq\text{Ker}\left(A\right)^{\perp}
.
∎

This allows to show well-definedness of
p_{l}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume that there is a solution of
Ax=f
in the interior of
\text{dom}\,g\square h
. Furthermore, assume the infimal convolution
J_{l}=D_{g}^{p_{l-1}}(\cdot,u_{l-1})\square h
(with the convention
p_{0}=0
and
D_{g}^{p_{0}}(u,u_{0})=g(u)
) is exact for all indices
l\in\mathbb{N}
. Then for any
l\in\mathbb{N}
, there is
p_{l}\in\partial g(u_{l})\cap\text{Ker}\left(A\right)^{\perp}
such that
p_{l}-{p_{l-1}}\in{\partial h(v_{l})}
.

Proof.

Since
\text{dom}\,D_{g}^{p_{l-1}}(\cdot,u_{l})=\text{dom}\,g
, there must be a solution of
Ax=f
in the interior of
\text{dom}\,J_{l}
. Recall that
(u_{l},v_{l})
satisfies

(u_{l},v_{l})\in\argmin\limits_{A(u+v)=f}D_{g}^{p_{l-1}}(u,u_{l-1})+h(v).

Setting
x_{l}=u_{l}+v_{l}
and using the exactness of
J_{l}
, the optimality condition of the previous equation becomes

0\in\partial\left(J_{l}+\mathcal{I}_{{\{Ax=f\}}}\right)(x_{l}).

Since
\text{dom}\,J_{l}
contains an inner point solving
Ax=f
, we obtain by Proposition
2.1
(ii) that

\partial\left(J_{l}+\mathcal{I}_{\{Ax=f\}}\right)(x_{l})=\partial J_{l}(x_{l})+\partial{\mathcal{I}_{\{Ax=f\}}}(x_{l})=\partial J_{l}(x_{l})+\text{Ker}\left(A\right)^{\perp}.

In particular, this means
\partial J_{l}(x_{l})\cap\text{Ker}\left(A\right)^{\perp}\neq\emptyset
. By Proposition
2.2
(iv) in this setting, one has
\partial J_{l}(x_{l})=\partial D_{g}^{p_{l-1}}(\cdot,u_{l-1})(u_{l})\cap\partial h(v_{l})
. Therefore
\left(\partial g(u_{l})-\left\{p_{l-1}\right\}\right)\cap\partial h(v_{l})\cap\text{Ker}\left(A\right)^{\perp}\neq 0
. This means there is
p_{l}\in\partial g(u_{l})
such that
{p_{l}-p_{l-1}}\in\partial h(v_{l})
and
p_{l}-p_{l-1}\in\text{Ker}\left(A\right)^{\perp}
. Hence, by induction it also follows
p_{l}\in\text{Ker}\left(A\right)^{\perp}
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The necessary subgradient
p_{l}
can be computed in a simple way when

or
h
is differentiable. If

is differentiable, then

for all

. Otherwise, for

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{l}}\left\langle A(u_{l}^{\delta}+v_{l}^{\delta})-f^{\delta},A(v+u_{l}^{\delta})-f^{\delta}\right\rangle

Otherwise, if

is a feasible choice and the iteration becomes stationary. In this case, the claim follows inductively.

Nested Bregman for noisy data

5
Well-definedness for selected regularizers

In this part, we give an overview of the infimally-convoluted regularizers which we use in the numerical section. We show exactness of the infimal convolutions and well-definedness of the minimization problems from Algorithms
1
and
2
in the infinite dimensional setting. As opposed to the situation of Remark
3.1
, problems (
28
) and (
33
) are not equivalent to problems of the form (
27
) or (
32
), because we update the Bregman distance of one functional within an infimal convolution, rather than the Bregman distance of the entire infimal convolution.
In fact, coercivity of an infimal convolution is not necessarily preserved if one of the functionals is replaced with a Bregman distance. As a simple counterexample, let
X=\mathbb{R}
and
g=h=\left|\cdot\right|
. Then for any
x\neq 0
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, it is
g\square h(\lambda x)=\left|\lambda\right|\left|x\right|
. However, for
p=\frac{x}{\left|x\right|}\in\partial g(x)
, we have
D_{g}^{p}(\lambda x,x)=0
for all
\lambda\geq 0
. Hence,
\lim\limits_{\lambda\to\infty}g\square h(\lambda x)=\infty
, while
\lim\limits_{\lambda\to\infty}(D_{g}^{p}(\cdot,x)\square h)(\lambda x)=0
. However, it is possible to show that any coercivity result for
g\square h
within the feasible sets
\left\{(u,v):A(u+v)=f\right\}
and
\left\{(u,v):\left\|A(u+v)-f^{\delta}\right\|\leq\delta\right\}
can be extended to
D_{g}^{p_{l}^{\delta}}(\cdot,u_{l}^{\delta})\square h
. Thus, the constraints in the minimization problems ensure their well-definedness. For brevity, we give a combined proof for the cases of exact and noisy data, where the exact data case corresponds to
\delta=0
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For
l\in\mathbb{N}
, let
p_{l}^{\delta}
be obtained as in Algorithm
2
, or Algorithm
1
in the case
\delta=0
(but still denoted by
p_{l}^{\delta})
and assume that
f\in\text{Ran}\,(A)
. Assume further that
h
satisfies

h(-x)=h(x){\text{ for all }}x\in X.

(40)

Let
((u_{n},v_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
X\times X
such that
\left\|A(u_{n}+v_{n})-f^{\delta}\right\|\leq\delta
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}g(u_{n})+h(v_{n})=\infty
, then
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}D_{g}^{p_{l}^{\delta}}(u_{n},u_{l}^{\delta})+h(v_{n})=\infty
.

Proof.

Recall that we assume
g(u_{0})=0
, so that upon setting
u_{0}^{\delta}=u_{0}
and
p_{0}^{\delta}=0
, it is
D_{g}^{p_{0}}(u,u_{0}^{\delta})=g(u)
for all
u\in\text{dom}\,g
.
Fix
x\in X
with
Ax=f
. If
\delta>0
, we have
p_{l}^{\delta}\in\text{Ran}\,({A^{*}})
by Lemma
4.2
. Hence, there is
q_{l}^{\delta}\in Y
with
A^{*}q_{l}^{\delta}=p_{l}^{\delta}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\left\langle p_{l}^{\delta},u_{n}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle q_{l}^{\delta},Au_{n}\right\rangle=\left\langle q_{l}^{\delta},A(u_{n}+v_{n})-f\right\rangle+\left\langle q_{l}^{\delta},f-Av_{n}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle q_{l}^{\delta},A(u_{n}+v_{n})-f\right\rangle+\left\langle p_{l}^{\delta},x-v_{n}\right\rangle.

This implies

-\left\langle p_{l}^{\delta},u_{n}\right\rangle\geq\left\langle p_{l}^{\delta},v_{n}-x\right\rangle-\delta\left\|q_{l}^{\delta}\right\|.

(41)

Due to (
40
) and
(p_{l}^{\delta}-p_{l-1}^{\delta})\in\partial h(v_{l}^{\delta})
, it is
(p_{l-1}-p_{l})\in\partial h(-v_{l}^{\delta})
and we deduce that

\left\langle p_{l}^{\delta},v_{n}\right\rangle=\left\langle p_{l}^{\delta}-p_{l-1}^{\delta},v_{n}\right\rangle+\left\langle p_{l-1}^{\delta},v_{n}\right\rangle\geq h(-v_{l}^{\delta})-h(v_{n})+\left\langle p_{l-1}^{\delta}-p_{l}^{\delta},v_{l}^{\delta}\right\rangle+\left\langle p_{l-1}^{\delta},v_{n}\right\rangle.

Iterating this estimate, we obtain

\left\langle p_{l}^{\delta},v_{n}\right\rangle\geq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\left(h(-v_{i}^{\delta})+\left\langle p_{i-1}^{\delta}-p_{i}^{\delta},v_{i}^{\delta}\right\rangle\right)-lh(v_{n}).

(42)

Denote

C_{l}=-g(u_{l}^{\delta})-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\left(h(-v_{i}^{\delta})+\left\langle p_{i-1}^{\delta}-p_{i}^{\delta},v_{i}^{\delta}\right\rangle\right)-\left\langle p_{l}^{\delta},x\right\rangle-\delta\left\|q_{l}^{\delta}\right\|+\left\langle p_{l}^{\delta},u_{l}^{\delta}\right\rangle.

Then, by combining (
41
) and (
42
), we obtain

\displaystyle D_{g}^{p_{l}^{\delta}}(u_{n},u_{l}^{\delta})+h(v_{n})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{l+1}D_{g}^{p_{l}^{\delta}}(u_{n},u_{l}^{\delta})+h(v_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{l+1}\left(g(u_{n})-g(u_{l}^{\delta})-\left\langle p_{l}^{\delta},u_{n}-u_{l}^{\delta}\right\rangle\right)+h(v_{n})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{l+1}\left(g(u_{n})-lh(v_{n})+C_{l}\right)+h(v_{n})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{l+1}\left(g(u_{n})+h(v_{n})+C_{l}\right).

Since
C_{l}
depends only on
l
(but not on
n
), the claim follows. For
\delta=0
, we recall that the constraint means
A(u_{n}+v_{n})=f
. Consequently, it is
x-(u_{n}+v_{n})\in\text{Ker}\left(A\right)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Together with
p_{l}^{\delta}\in\text{Ker}\left(A\right)^{\perp}
we obtain

\left\langle p_{l}^{\delta},u_{n}\right\rangle=\left\langle p_{l}^{\delta},x-v_{n}\right\rangle.

Hence, the previous estimates work analogously for the case
\delta=0
by just omitting the terms of the form
\left\langle q_{l}^{\delta},A(u_{n}+v_{n})-f\right\rangle
.
∎

Upon close inspection of the proof of Lemma

5.1
L^{1}-H^{1}

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. In order to separate peaks from an otherwise smooth signal, we use the infimal convolution of
g=\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\nabla\cdot\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
and
h=\beta\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{1}}
.
Hence, let
X=Y=L^{2}(\Omega)
. We show that the minimization problems in Algorithm

are well-defined for the case of denoising. Note that the first step of our algorithm corresponds to denoising with regularizer
g\square h
, for which a proof of well-definedness might exist in literature. Since we could not find one, we outline the arguments below and illustrate how Lemma
5.1
helps to extend the well-definedness to problems of the form (
33
). We first recall the space of functions of bounded variation.

Definition 5.1
.

[
1
]

The (isotropic) total variation of a function
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)
is defined as

\left\|Du\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}=\sup\left\{\int\limits_{\Omega}u\text{div}\,\varphi:\ \varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2}),\;\left\|\varphi(x)\right\|_{2}\leq 1\text{ for all }x\in\Omega\right\},

where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2}
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It holds that

\left\|Du\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}=\left\|Du\right\|_{\mathcal{M}},

where
Du
denotes the finite Radon measure that represents the distributional derivative of
u
and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}
the Radon norm. Recall that, for a given Radon measure
\mu
on
\Omega
, the Radon norm is defined as

\left\|\mu\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}=\sup\left\{\int\limits_{\Omega}\varphi\,d\mu:\ \varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2}),\;\left\|\varphi(x)\right\|_{2}\leq 1\text{ for all }x\in\Omega\right\}.

The domain of the total variation is then the space of functions of bounded variation
BV(\Omega)=\left\{u\in L^{1}(\Omega):\left\|Du\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}<\infty\right\}
. If equipped with the norm
\left\|u\right\|_{BV}=\left\|u\right\|_{L^{1}}+\left\|Du\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}
, this space is complete and continuously embedded in
L^{\frac{d}{d-1}}(\Omega)
(for the case
d=1
we use the convention
\frac{1}{0}=\infty
). Furthermore, if
u\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
, then

\left\|Du\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}=\int_{\Omega}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{2},

where
\nabla u
denotes the weak gradient of
u
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
g(u)=\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
,
h(v)=\beta\left\|v\right\|_{L^{1}}
and
A=Id
. Additionally, assume that
f\in L^{1}(\Omega)
. Then the infimal convolutions

x\in X
, and the minimization problems (
32
) and (
33
) are well-defined for every
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. For simpler notation, denote

and
J_{l}=g_{l}\square h
, with the convention

and
p_{l}^{\delta}=0
if

throughout the proof. We show the results in the case
d\geq 2
. For
d=1
the results can be proved analogously by replacing weak convergence in
L^{\frac{d}{d-1}}(\Omega)
with weak*-convergence in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

1.

We start by showing the exactness of the infimal convolution.
Let
x\in X
and consider a minimizing sequence
(u_{n},v_{n})
such that
u_{n}+v_{n}=x
and
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}g_{l}(u_{n})+h(v_{n})=J_{l}(x)
. Hence, the sequences
(g_{l}(u_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(h(v_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are individually bounded, since
g_{l}
and
h
are both non-negative. Furthermore,
\left\|\nabla u_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}}
must be bounded. If it was unbounded, then
(\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\nabla u_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\beta\left\|v_{n}\right\|_{L^{1}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
would be unbounded. But since
u_{n}+v_{n}=x
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can apply Lemma
5.1
with
f^{\delta}
replaced by
x
and an arbitrary
\delta>0
to find that that
(g_{l}(u_{n})+h(v_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
would be unbounded. Therefore,
(\left\|Du_{n}\right\|_{\mathcal{M}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded since

\left\|Du_{n}\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}=\int\limits_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|\leq\left|\Omega\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int\limits_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Since
u_{n}=x-v_{n}
and
x\in L^{1}(\Omega)
, we obtain that
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
L^{1}(\Omega)
. Therefore,
(\left\|u_{n}\right\|_{BV})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded. This implies
(\left\|u_{n}\right\|_{L^{\frac{d}{d-1}}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded, so that we can assume (upon passing to a subsequence, denoted the same) that
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly in
L^{\frac{d}{d-1}}(\Omega)
to some
u^{*}
. Consequently,
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly in
L^{\frac{d}{d-1}}(\Omega)
(on a subsequence) to
v^{*}=x-u^{*}
. If we can show that both
g_{l}
and
h
are lower semicontinuous with respect to
L^{\frac{d}{d-1}}(\Omega)
, then the claim follows by standard arguments. Indeed, first note that
p_{l}^{\delta}=\sum_{i=1}^{l}(p_{i}^{\delta}-p_{i-1}^{\delta})
with
p_{i}^{\delta}-p_{i-1}^{\delta}\in\partial h(v_{i}^{\delta})
for all
i\in\left\{1,\dots l\right\}
. Because
\partial h(v_{i}^{\delta})\subset\left\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega):\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\right\}\subset L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we have
p_{l}^{\delta}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Therefore, the duality paring
\left\langle p_{l}^{\delta},\cdot\right\rangle
is (weakly) continuous on
L^{\frac{d}{d-1}}(\Omega)
. Next, note that for any
u\in L^{\frac{d}{d-1}}(\Omega)
it is

\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}}=\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})\\
\left\|\varphi\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\int\limits_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\varphi=\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})\\
\left\|\varphi\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\int\limits_{\Omega}u\,\text{div}\,\varphi.

Thus,
\left\|\nabla\cdot\right\|_{L^{2}}
is a supremum of continuous functionals on
L^{\frac{d}{d-1}}(\Omega)
, which implies lower-semicontinuity. In summary,
g_{l}
is lower semicontinuous and convex on
L^{\frac{d}{d-1}}(\Omega)
. Similarly, we can write

\left\|v\right\|_{L^{1}}=\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\\
\left\|\varphi\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\int\limits_{\Omega}{v}\,\varphi.

Again, this implies that
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{1}}
is lower semicontinuous with respect to
L^{\frac{d}{d-1}}(\Omega)
. It remains to show
u^{*}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Due to
\left\|\nabla u^{*}\right\|_{L^{2}}<\infty
and
u^{*}\in L^{\frac{d}{d-1}}(\Omega)
, it follows that
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
as outlined next. Since
\Omega
is a bounded, Lipschitz domain, there is a sequence
\left(\bar{u}_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
, such that
\left(\bar{u}_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
u^{*}
in
L^{\frac{d}{d-1}}(\Omega)
and
(\nabla\bar{u}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
\nabla u^{*}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. In particular, we obtain that
\int_{\Omega}\bar{u}_{n}
converges to
\int_{\Omega}u^{*}
, so that the Poincaré-Wirtinger inequality in
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
implies

\left\|u^{*}-\int_{\Omega}u^{*}\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\left\|\bar{u}_{n}-\int_{\Omega}\bar{u}_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\left\|\nabla\bar{u}_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}}<\infty.

Since
x\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, this also implies
v^{*}=x-u^{*}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

2.

Let
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence with
\left\|x_{n}-f^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\delta
and

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}J_{l}(x_{n})=\inf\limits_{\left\|x-f^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\delta}J_{l}(x).

In particular, this implies that
(x_{n}-f^{\delta})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
L^{1}(\Omega)
. Additionally, there exist
u_{n}
and
v_{n}
such that
u_{n}+v_{n}=x_{n}
and
J_{l}(x_{n})=g_{l}(u_{n})+h(v_{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, by using the same arguments as before, we can assume that
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are weakly convergent (up to a subsequence) in
L^{\frac{d}{d-1}}(\Omega)
. Again, the claim follows by lower-semicontinuity of
g_{l}
and
h
and Lemma
5.1
.

∎

5.2
TV-H^{1}

We formalize the setting of Example
3.1
in a more general context. For this consider a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
X=L^{2}(\Omega)
and consider
A\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
, where
Y
is some Hilbert space. In order to separate smooth and piecewise constant components of a function
x\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we use the squared
H^{1}
-seminorm as a regularizer for the smooth part:

g(u)=\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int\limits_{\Omega}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{2}^{2},

for
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. For
u\notin H^{1}(\Omega)
, we set
g(u)=\infty
. The piecewise constant part will be penalized by the total variation:

h(v)=\beta\left\|Dv\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}.

Since
H^{1}(\Omega)\subset BV(\Omega)
, we obtain
\text{dom}\,(g\square h)=BV(\Omega)
by Proposition
2.2
(iii).

Remark 5.2
.

Let us point out the similarity of the regularizer
J=g\square h
with the Huber-TV functional
[
40
,
9
,
23
]
. The Huber-TV functional is defined on
BV(\Omega)
as a convex function of the measure
Du

[
16
]
, i.e.

TV_{\gamma}(x):=\int\limits_{\Omega}f_{\gamma}(\nabla x)+\left\|D^{s}x\right\|_{\mathcal{M}},

where
f_{\gamma}(y)
is given for some
\gamma>0
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

f_{\gamma}(y):=\left(\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2}^{2}\square\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2}\right)(y)=\begin{cases}\frac{\left\|y\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2\gamma}&\text{ if }\left\|y\right\|_{2}\leq\gamma,\\
\left\|y\right\|_{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2}&\text{ if }\left\|y\right\|_{2}\geq\gamma.\end{cases}

Here
\nabla x
and
D^{s}x
denote the absolutely continuous and the singular part of
Dx
with regard to the Lebesgue measure, respectively. In particular, there holds
\nabla x\in L^{1}(\Omega)
. Setting
\gamma=\frac{\beta}{\alpha}
, we have that

\displaystyle\beta TV_{\gamma}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\Omega}\left(\inf_{w(z)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|w(z)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\beta\left\|\nabla x(z)-w(z)\right\|_{2}\right)dz+\beta\left\|D^{s}x\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=\inf\limits_{w\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})}\int\limits_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|w(z)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\beta\left\|\nabla x(z)-w(z)\right\|_{2}\right)dz+\beta\left\|D^{s}x\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}.

(43)

Note that the last minimization problem in (
43
) admits indeed a solution
w_{\gamma}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, see
[
9
]
and also (
45
) below.
Next, since
\left\|Dx\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}=\left\|\nabla x\right\|_{L^{1}}+\left\|D^{s}x\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}
, for all
x\in BV(\Omega)
we have

\displaystyle(\alpha g\square\beta h)(x)

\displaystyle=\inf\limits_{u\in H^{1}(\Omega)}\left(\int\limits_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\nabla u(z)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\beta\left\|\nabla(x(z)-u(z))\right\|_{2}\right)dz+\beta\left\|D^{s}(x-u)\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{D^{s}u=0}{=}\inf\limits_{u\in H^{1}(\Omega)}\left(\int\limits_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\nabla u(z)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\beta\left\|\nabla x(z)-\nabla u(z)\right\|_{2}\right)dz+\beta\left\|D^{s}x\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}\right)

\displaystyle=\inf\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\text{grad }H^{1}\end{subarray}}\left(\int\limits_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|w(z)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\beta\left\|\nabla x(z)-w(z)\right\|_{2}\right)dz+\beta\left\|D^{s}x\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}\right).

(44)

where we have defined
\text{grad }H^{1}=\left\{w\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}):w=\nabla u\text{ for some }u\in H^{1}(\Omega)\right\}
.
Comparing (
43
) and (
44
), one obtains
\beta TV_{\gamma}(x)\leq(\alpha g\square\beta h)(x)
for every
x\in BV(\Omega)
. Define

H_{0}(\text{div}\,0)=\left\{w\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}):\text{div}\,w=0\text{ and }w\cdot n=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\right\},

where
n
denotes the outer unit normal of
\Omega
and all derivatives have to be understood in a weak sense. Then
\text{grad }H^{1}
and
H_{0}(\text{div}\,0)
are both closed and orthogonal subspaces of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
, so that we obtain (see for instance
[
14
, p. 216]
)

L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})=\text{grad }H^{1}\oplus H_{0}(\text{div}\,0).

This means that in general, we do not expect
(\alpha g\square\beta h)(x)=\beta TV_{\gamma}(x)
. However, for
d=1
, one can easily see that
H_{0}(\text{div}\,0)=\left\{0\right\}
, so that equality holds. Furthermore, it is

\argmin\limits_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\;\left\{\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|w\right\|_{2}^{2}+\beta\left\|\nabla x(z)-w\right\|_{2}\right\}=\min\left\{1,\frac{\beta}{\alpha\left\|\nabla x(z)\right\|_{2}}\right\}\nabla x(z):=w_{\gamma}(z),

(45)

for all
z\in\Omega
.
Thus, equality of
(\alpha g\square\beta h)(x)
and
\beta TV_{\gamma}(x)
also holds if
w_{\gamma}\in\text{grad }H^{1}
. For instance, this happens for all
x
such that
\left\|\nabla x(z)\right\|_{2}
is constant or
\left\|\nabla x(z)\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\alpha}{\beta}
almost everywhere.

Next, we give a formal proof of existence for the minimization problems in Algorithm
2
with those regularizers. As a first step of our analysis, we establish a Poincaré-Wirtinger inequality for the infimal convolution of
g
and
h
. To this end, let
p=\frac{d}{d-1}
for
d\geq 2
for the remainder of the section. In the case
d=1
, we set
p=2
for simplicity. However, in the one-dimensional case all results remain valid for any
1\leq p\leq\infty
.

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
J=g\square h
, and define
\tilde{J}(\tilde{x})=\min\limits_{u\in L^{p}(\Omega)}\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{p}}^{2}+\beta\left\|\tilde{x}-u\right\|_{L^{p}}
for every
\tilde{x}\in L^{p}(\Omega)
. Furthermore, denote
\mu_{u}=\frac{1}{\left|\Omega\right|}\int\limits_{\Omega}u
the mean of a function
u\in L^{p}(\Omega)
. Then there is a constant
C>0
(which only depends on
\Omega
) such that

\tilde{J}(x-\mu_{x})\leq CJ(x)

(46)

for all
x\in L^{p}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Since
\text{dom}\,J=BV(\Omega)
, we can assume that
x\in BV(\Omega)
. Let
u\in L^{p}(\Omega)
, then by the Poincaré-Wirtinger inequalities for
BV(\Omega)
and
H^{1}(\Omega)
and the boundedness of
\Omega
, there is
C>0
, which only depends on
\Omega
, such that

\tilde{J}(x-\mu_{x})\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|u-\mu_{u}\right\|_{L^{p}}^{2}+\beta\left\|(x-\mu_{x})-(u-\mu_{u})\right\|_{L^{p}}\leq C\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\beta\left\|D(x-u)\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}\right).

Since
u
was chosen arbitrarily, we can take the infimum over all
u
on the right-hand side of the previous equation to obtain (
46
).
∎

This allows to prove the existence result.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
g(u)=\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
,
h(v)=\beta\left\|Dv\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}
and
A\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\Omega),Y)
. For
d\geq 3
assume additionally that
A
is continuous on
L^{p}(\Omega)
. Furthermore, assume that there is
x\in\text{dom}\,g\square h
with
Ax=f
. Then for any
l\in\mathbb{N}
, the infimal convolutions in (
32
) and (
33
) are exact, and the minimization problems are well-defined.

Proof.

Let
l\in\mathbb{N}
. For simpler notation, denote again
g_{l}(\cdot):=D_{g}^{p_{l-1}^{\delta}}(\cdot,u_{l-1}^{\delta})
with the convention
g_{l}=g
if
l=1
throughout the proof. Additionally define
J_{l}=g_{l}\square h
. Note that, as in the proof of Lemma
5.2
, the functionals
g_{l}
and
h
are lower semicontinuous in
L^{p}
. Furthermore, for any constant function
c(x)=c\in\mathbb{R}
, it is
\left\langle p_{l-1}^{\delta},c\right\rangle=\left\langle p_{l-1}^{\delta},u_{l-1}^{\delta}+c-u_{l-1}^{\delta}\right\rangle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\nabla(u_{l-1}^{\delta}+c)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\nabla u_{l-1}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=0
. By considering
-c
instead, we conclude
\left\langle p_{l-1}^{\delta},c\right\rangle=0
. Hence,
g_{l}(u+c)=g_{l}(u)
for all
u\in\text{dom}\,g
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

1.

Let us first show the exactness of the infimal convolution. Let
x\in\text{dom}\,J_{l}
, and note that clearly
J_{l}(x)\geq 0
. Therefore, let
\left(u_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a minimizing sequence, that is
u_{n}\in\text{dom}\,J_{l}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}g_{l}(x-u_{n})+h(u_{n})=J_{l}(x)
. Since for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
, one has
g_{l}(x-(u_{n}+c))+h(u_{n}+c)=g_{l}(x-u_{n})+h(u_{n})
, we may assume that
\mu_{u_{n}}=0
. Since
x\in\text{dom}\,J_{l}
, we must have that
(h(u_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded. Hence, the Poincaré-Wirtinger inequality for
TV
implies that
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega)
. Consequently, (by passing to a subsequence), we can assume that
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly to some
u^{*}\in L^{p}(\Omega)
. Since
g_{l}
and
h
are lower semicontinuous with respect to
L^{p}(\Omega)
, we obtain

g_{l}(x-u^{*})+h(u^{*})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(g_{l}(x-u_{n})+h(u_{n})\right)=J_{l}(x).

Hence,
J_{l}
is exact.

2.

Next, we show that the constraint problem

\begin{cases}&\min J_{l}(x)\\
&s.t.\ \left\|Ax-f^{\delta}\right\|\leq\delta\end{cases}

is well-defined. Let
\left(x_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence with
\left\|Ax_{n}-f^{\delta}\right\|\leq\delta
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}J_{l}(x_{n})=\inf\limits_{\left\|Ax-f^{\delta}\right\|\leq\delta}J_{l}(x)
. We first show that
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
can be assumed to have bounded mean. We claim that
(x_{n}-\mu_{x_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega)
. By contradiction, suppose that (on a subsequence denoted the same)
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left\|x_{n}-\mu_{x_{n}}\right\|_{L^{p}}=\infty
. Let
\tilde{J}
as in Corollary
5.1
. One can easily see that
\tilde{J}
is coercive with respect to
L^{p}(\Omega)
. Therefore
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\tilde{J}(x_{n}-\mu_{x_{n}})=\infty
. This implies
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}(g\square h)(x_{n}-\mu_{x_{n}})=\infty
. But that would mean
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}J_{l}(x_{n}-\mu_{x_{n}})=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}J_{l}(x_{n})=\infty
by Lemma
5.1
, which is a contradiction. Thus,
(x_{n}-\mu_{x_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and consequently
(A(x_{n}-\mu_{x_{n}}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
must be bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega)
by continuity of
A
. Consider two cases. First assume
A\chi_{\Omega}\neq 0
, where
\chi
is the characteristic function of
\Omega
. If
(\mu_{x_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
was unbounded, then
(Ax_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
would have to be unbounded as well, which contradicts
\left\|Ax_{n}-f^{\delta}\right\|\leq\delta
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Otherwise, if
A\chi_{\Omega}=0
, we can replace
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by a sequence with mean
0
, since
A(x_{n}+c)=Ax_{n}
and
J_{l}(x_{n}+c)=J_{l}(x_{n})
for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Combining both cases, we can choose
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
(\mu_{x_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded.

Next, since
J_{l}
is exact, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
u_{n}\in\text{dom}\,g_{l},\ v_{n}\in\text{dom}\,h
with
u_{n}+v_{n}=x_{n}
and
J_{l}(x_{n})=g_{l}(u_{n})+h(v_{n})
. Since adding a constant to
u_{n}
and subtracting it from
v_{n}
does not change the value of
g_{l}(u_{n})+h(v_{n})
(see part (i) of this proof), we may assume that
\mu_{v_{n}}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This means
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded by non-negativity of
g
and the Poincaré-Wirtinger inequality of
h
:

\beta\left\|v_{n}\right\|_{L^{p}}\leq C\beta\left\|Dv_{n}\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}\leq CJ_{l}(x_{n}).

In summary, we have that both
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
must be bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega)
. Consequently,
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
must also be bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega)
. Therefore, (by passing to subsequences) we can assume that
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converge weakly in
L^{p}(\Omega)
to some
u^{*},\,v^{*}\in\mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega)
. By continuity and linearity of
A
, the set
\left\{x\in X:\left\|Ax-f^{\delta}\right\|\leq\delta\right\}
is
L^{p}-
weakly closed, therefore one has
\left\|A(u^{*}+v^{*})-f^{\delta}\right\|\leq\delta
. Because
g_{l}
and
h
are lower-semicontinuous on
L^{p}(\Omega)
, it holds that

g_{l}(u^{*})+h(u^{*})\leq\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(g_{l}(u_{n})+h(v_{n})\right)=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}J_{l}(x_{n})=\inf\limits_{\left\|Ax-f^{\delta}\right\|\leq\delta}J_{l}(x).

This shows the existence of a minimizer.

∎

5.3
Oscillatory TGV

As a last example, we recall the infimally-convoluted oscillation TGV from
[
19
]
. To be precise, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary and
Y
be some Hilbert space. Consider the functionals

g(u)=TGV_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}}^{2}(u)=\min\limits_{{w}\in BD(\Omega)}\alpha_{1}\left\|\nabla u-w\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}+\beta_{1}\left\|\mathcal{E}w\right\|_{\mathcal{M}},

(47)

and

h(v)=TGV_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{2},C}^{osci}(v)=\min\limits_{w\in BD(\Omega)}\alpha_{2}\left\|\nabla v-w\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}+\beta_{2}\left\|\mathcal{E}w+C{v}\right\|_{\mathcal{M}},

(48)

where
\mathcal{E}
denotes the weak symmetrized derivative,
BD(\Omega)
is the space of functions of bounded deformation, and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is defined as the matrix with entries
c_{ij}=\omega_{i}\omega_{j}
for some
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. That means using
g
and
h
as regularizers promotes piecewise affine and oscillatory functions, respectively (compare to Lemma
5.4
(ii) below). Additionally, note that the total generalized variation
g
is defined via an infimal convolution itself. Thus, this example serves to show that our proposed method can also be applied for problems involving more sophisticated regularizers that yield decompositions with components having quite different structures.

In order to show exactness of the infimal convolutions and well-definedness of the minimization problems occurring in Algorithm
2
, we use the arguments from
[
19
, Section 3]
and apply Lemma
5.1
where necessary. Let us first state some properties of
g
and
h
. Again, we denote
p=\frac{d}{d-1}
for
d\geq 2
and
p=2
for
d=1
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\geq 0
and
C=\omega^{T}\omega
with
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
. Define
g(u)=TGV_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}}^{2}(u)
and
h(v)=TGV_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{2},C}^{osci}(v)
. Then the following statements hold.

1.

g
and
h
are seminorms and lower semicontinuous with respect to
L^{p}
.

2.

It is

\ker g=\left\{u(z)=a\cdot z+b:a\in\mathbb{R}^{d},b\in\mathbb{R}\right\}

and

\ker h=\left\{v(z)=a\sin(\omega\cdot z)+b\cos(\omega\cdot z):a,b\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

Furthermore,
g\square h

3.

(50)

for all

Proof.

All statements follow from Proposition

6.1
Stopping criterion

Following
[
20
]
, we recall the discrete normalized cross-correlation of two non-zero signals, respectively matrices.

Definition 6.1
.

1.

Given
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, the sample normalized cross-correlation of
u
and
v
is defined as the vector
\rho(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2N-1}
with components

\rho_{k}(u,v)=\frac{1}{\left\|u\right\|_{2}\left\|v\right\|_{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}v_{i+k},

(52)

for
k\in\left\{-(N-1),\dots,0,\dots,N-1\right\}
. Here we assume a periodic extension of
v
, that is
v_{i\pm N}:=v_{i}
for all
i\in\left\{1,\dots,N\right\}
.

2.

For
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, we analogously define the sample normalized cross-correlation as the matrix
\rho(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{(2N-1)\times(2M-1)}
with entries

\rho_{k,l}(u,v)=\frac{1}{\left\|u\right\|_{2}\left\|v\right\|_{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{M}u_{i,j}v_{i+k,j+l}

(53)

for
(k,l)\in\left\{-(N-1),\dots,0,\dots,N-1\right\}\times\left\{-(M-1),\dots,0,\dots,M-1\right\}
, where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2}
denotes the Frobenius norm of a matrix. Again, we assume periodic boundary conditions:
v_{i\pm N,j\pm M}:=v_{i,j}
.

Remark 6.1
.

Under the assumption of periodic boundary conditions, we can define
u^{\prime}_{j}=u_{-j}
for signals and
u^{\prime}_{i,j}=u_{-i,-j}
for images, so that (
52
) and (
53
) can be written as

\rho(u,v)=\frac{1}{\left\|u\right\|_{2}\left\|v\right\|_{2}}(u^{\prime}*v),

where
*
denotes the discrete convolution. Since the components appearing as minimizers in Algorithms
2
and
3
with the regularizers from Section
5
are also in
L^{2}(\Omega)
for dimension
d\in\left\{1,2\right\}
, this allows for a straightforward generalization of the normalized cross correlation in the function space setting. However, for working in a discretized setting, the formulations (
52
) and (
53
) are sufficient.

The normalized cross correlation has been used to determine optimal parameter choices in Tikhonov regularization for decompositions
[
3
,
20
,
21
]
, relying on the idea that structurally different components should be uncorrelated or have small correlation. In
[
3
]
, only the empirical correlation of two images (which corresponds to the entry
\rho_{0,0}
in the notation (
53
)) is considered for an a posteriori parameter choice principle. By using increasing values of a regularization parameter
\lambda
, an observed image is decomposed in a cartoon part
u_{\lambda}
and a texture part
v_{\lambda}
. Then the optimal parameter is determined as the first local minimum of
\rho_{0,0}(u_{\lambda},v_{\lambda})
with respect to
\lambda
. The authors point out that the criterion is expected to work well only for relatively simple decompositions. The paper
[
20
]
employs a two-stage variational decomposition model. For the second step of the method, the authors introduce a more informative criterion by defining a scalar measure of correlation as

\mathcal{C}(u,v):=\frac{1}{MN}\left\|\rho(u,v)\right\|_{2}^{2}

(54)

and by minimizing
\mathcal{C}
in a bilevel approach, where the lower level problem is a minimization problem that decomposes a denoised image into a cartoon and a texture part. It turns out that combining these approaches allows us to define a stopping criterion for our experiments. Thus, we stop the iteration at the first local minimum of
\mathcal{C}(u_{n}^{\delta},v_{n}^{\delta})
, taking care that the algorithm does not terminate after the first step. This means, even if
\mathcal{C}(u_{1}^{\delta},v_{1}^{\delta})\leq\mathcal{C}(u_{2}^{\delta},v_{2}^{\delta})
, we continue iterating until a local minimum is reached. We would like to stress that this criterion is heuristic, and the existence of a local minimum of
\mathcal{C}
is not guaranteed by our theory.

Conclusion

We introduce the method of Nested Bregman iterations for decomposition problems, and analyze the method with respect to its well-definedness and convergence behavior. In particular, we make use of the infimal convolution of regularizers to reconstruct solutions for noise corrupted, linear, ill-posed problems, consisting of structurally different components. We illustrate the strength of the method numerically on synthetic test images, where we use a simple cross-correlation based stopping principle. In our experiments, the results with the proposed method also perform equally or better than the best possible results obtained from a single-step variational approach with suitable parameter choice. Hence, Nested Bregman iterations appear superior over a grid search among Morozov-regularized reconstructions, as those can only compete with the proposed method for suitably chosen
predetermined
grids. Furthermore, they provide a flexible alternative to bilevel methods for image and signal decomposition. However, some theoretical questions on the convergence behavior of the proposed method, where Bregman iterations are used in the inner loop, remain open. In future research, we aim to adapt the Nested Bregman iteration for denoising problems with mixed noise.
