Rethinking Ecological Measures Of Functional Diversity
Ines Meraoumia, Adji Bousso Dieng

Abstract
Understanding functional diversity, the range and variability of species’ roles and actions within their communities, is key to predicting and preserving the functions that sustain both nature and human well-being. In this paper, we provide a comprehensive review of the literature on functional diversity measurement. We begin by consolidating essential criteria that effective measures of functional diversity should meet. We then evaluate fifteen widely used functional diversity metrics against these criteria and assess their performance across six synthetic ecosystem scenarios where optimal behavior is known. Surprisingly, our analysis reveals that none of the widely used metrics fully satisfy all the established requirements, and all fail in at least one ecosystem scenario. In particular, we find that almost all metrics flagrantly violate set monotonicity and distance monotonicity, requirements that adding a novel species should increase diversity, and that the magnitude of that increase should grow with trait dissimilarity. We also find that metrics fail to decline when rare, functionally extreme species are lost, and even increase when a perfectly redundant species is added. These critical flaws leave them blind to the very biodiversity loss that functional diversity measures are intended to detect. Our findings underscore the urgent need to develop a new generation of functional diversity metrics that more accurately reflect ecological realities. Keywords: Diversity, Functional Diversity, Biodiversity, Ecology and Evolution



1
Introduction

Biodiversity, the variety and variability of life on Earth, is widely recognized as one of the most critical indicators of ecosystem health and resilience
(
Magurran 2013
)
. As ecosystems continue to face increasing threats from anthropogenic activities, understanding and monitoring biodiversity has become a priority for scientists, conservationists, and policymakers
(
Hooper et al. 2005
)
. While species richness, or the sheer number of species within a given area, has traditionally been a key metric for assessing biodiversity
(
Gotelli and
Colwell 2001
)
, it fails to capture the full complexity of biological communities.

More recently, the concept of functional diversity (FD) has gained prominence as a more comprehensive approach to understanding biodiversity
(
Tilman 2001
)
. Unlike species richness, FD focuses not just on the number of species, but on the variety of functional traits that organisms exhibit within their communities
(
Cornelissen
et al. 2003
)
. These traits reflect the ecological roles species play, such as their contributions to nutrient cycling, food webs, and ecosystem stability
(
Hooper et al. 2005
;
Flynn et al. 2011
)
.

The accurate measurement of FD is crucial for understanding ecosystem functioning, resilience, and the potential impacts of biodiversity loss
(
Flynn et al. 2011
)
. It provides valuable insights into how communities are structured, how they respond to environmental stressors, and how ecosystem services are maintained
(
Hooper et al. 2005
;
Mouchet et al. 2010
)
. However, measuring functional diversity is inherently challenging. The diversity of ecological roles is influenced not only by the traits of individual species but also by the interactions between species, their environment, and the dynamics of the ecosystem as a whole
(
Villéger et al. 2008
)
. This complexity, coupled with the growing need to evaluate large-scale ecological datasets, has spurred the development of various FD metrics.

Despite the extensive use of these metrics, there remains a significant gap between theoretical understanding and practical application in ecology. Many of the existing FD metrics are rooted in simplifying assumptions about ecological systems that may not adequately capture the complexity and dynamics of real-world communities
(
Mouchet et al. 2010
)
. For instance, some metrics focus primarily on the trait dispersion within a community, assuming that greater variability in traits automatically implies higher FD
(
Cornwell et al. 2006
)
. However, such measures may overlook critical aspects of functional redundancy, species interactions, and temporal changes in community composition, which can all shape ecosystem functioning in meaningful ways
(
Villéger et al. 2008
)
.

In this paper, we comprehensively review the current landscape of functional diversity metrics. Our goal is to systematically assess the performance of widely used FD metrics. We first identify a set of key requirements that any FD metric should meet and assess thirteen existing FD metrics under these requirements. We also apply those FD metrics to a series of synthetic ecosystem scenarios where the optimal behavior of FD is known. Those scenarios focus on six criteria, including sensitivity to environmental change and robustness to sampling biases.
Through this extensive evaluation, we aim to identify the strengths and limitations of existing FD metrics and provide insight into how they perform.

Our findings reveal that, despite their widespread use, no existing FD metric fully satisfies the set of requirements, nor do they fully behave as expected in all scenarios. These results call for the development of more nuanced, flexible approaches to FD that better reflect ecological realities.

2
Requirements For Measuring Functional Diversity

Through an extensive literature survey, we distilled and refined fourteen key theoretical requirements that any reliable functional diversity index must satisfy. We argue that an ideal index should meet all of these criteria to faithfully capture the multifunctional roles species play in real ecosystems.

1 - Set monotonicity,

Solow and Polasky 1994
.
A functional diversity index
f
should never decrease when a community gains a new, functionally distinct species. Formally, for any two communities
B\subset A
in which all species have unique trait vectors,
f(B)\leq f(A)
. Ecologically, this reflects the intuition that losses of species, and thus their unique functions, can only erode diversity. For example,
Petchey and Gaston 2002a
demonstrated across six empirical and simulated assemblages that species extinctions reduce functional diversity, with the greatest loss occurring when species possessing singular trait values are removed. Their results imply that preserving high functional diversity requires maintaining not just species counts but the breadth of functional traits.

2 - Twinning,

Solow and Polasky 1994
.
A functional diversity index should not increase when a
twin
species that has the same trait values as an existing species is added. Functional diversity is related to the ecological functions of species in an ecosystem. Indices that count species richness violate this property, since they would increase simply by incrementing head counts without introducing any novel ecological function.

3 - Monotonicity in distance,

Solow and Polasky 1994
.
A functional diversity index
f
should increase when the distances in trait space (equivalently, trait dissimilarity) between species increase. To illustrate this requirement, we consider two sets
A
and
B
and suppose they contain
N
species each but represent two different assemblages (not necessarily the same species).
If
\forall i,j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, we have
d_{i,j}(B)\leq d_{i,j}(A)
where
d_{i,j}
is the distance/dissimilarity between the traits of species
i
and
j
, then
f(B)\leq f(A)
. Here we suppose that the species are equally distributed. Ecologically, if species in A occupy more dispersed positions in trait space than those in B, then A should exhibit greater functional diversity.

4 - Boundedness
,
this paper
.
A functional diversity index
f
should be bounded within a known finite range. While
Mason et al. 2003
specifically advocate constraining
f
to
[0,1]
, we argue it suffices that
f
be bounded since any desired normalization can be applied if the bounds are known. Boundedness facilitates meaningful comparisons of functional diversity across communities that differ in species richness or trait scales and aids interpretability.

5 - Trait range sensitivity,

Mason et al. 2003
.
A functional-diversity index should capture the range of trait values in a community, often interpreted as the volume occupied in trait space. The greater the spread of species’ trait vectors, the larger the ecological “space” they cover, and thus the higher the functional diversity. Assemblages whose species span a wider trait range should accordingly exhibit greater functional diversity.

6 - Abundance weighting,

Mason et al. 2003
.
A functional diversity index should weigh each species’ contribution in proportion to its abundance. For example, two communities containing the same set of species will have equal species richness but differ in functional diversity if one community is dominated by a single species while the other has all species equally abundant—the index should be higher for the evenly balanced community.

7 - Sensitivity to functionally-rare species,

Mason et al. 2003
.
A functional diversity index should decrease when the abundance of a rare species, i.e., one possessing extreme trait values, declines. Rare species often embody unique ecological functions, so their loss, even in small numbers, should be reflected as a drop in functional diversity. For instance,
Leitão et al. 2016
demonstrated across three datasets of Amazonian stream fish, Guianan rainforest trees, and Australian tropical birds that extinctions of rare, functionally distinctive species lead to disproportionately large losses in functional richness. Similarly,
Basile 2022
found in managed forest plots in Germany that declines in extreme-trait species critically undermine overall functional diversity and associated ecosystem resilience.

8 - Continuity in abundance
,
this paper
.

Mason et al. 2003
postulated that the removal of a species with a vanishingly small abundance should leave a functional diversity index
f
unchanged. However, this insensitivity can mask the loss of functionally unique species that may remain ecologically important even at low abundance. Therefore, we require that
f
vary continuously with species’ abundances instead. Specifically, for community A and species
i\in A
with abundance
w_{i}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{w_{i}\to 0}\left|f(A)-f(A\setminus\left\{i\right\})\right|=0

By demanding continuity rather than abrupt invariance, we ensure that as a species becomes less abundant, its marginal impact on
f
smoothly diminishes—but never drops to zero prematurely—thus preserving sensitivity to functional uniqueness at all abundance levels.

9 - Invariance to trait measurement units,

Mason et al. 2003
.
A functional diversity index
f
should be unaffected by the choice of units or scales used to measure trait dimensions. For example, whether tree height is recorded in centimeters or meters, or leaf area in square millimeters or square centimeters, must not alter the computed diversity. Equivalently,
f
must be invariant under positive linear rescaling of any trait axis, ensuring that no single trait with a larger numeric range disproportionately drives the index.

10 - Unaffected by the units of abundances,

Mason et al. 2003
. A functional diversity index
f
should be insensitive to the units of the abundances.

11 - Unaffected by the number of species,

Mason et al. 2003
. Consider an assemblage with
N
species. Without loss of generality, assume the abundances are the same for all the
N
species of the assemblage. A functional diversity index
f
should not change if we add
K
species for each trait value of each species. This means that there will be
K\times(N+1)
species forming
N
groups of
K
species having the same trait values. The functional diversity of the original assemblage with
N
species should be the same as the one with the
K\times(N+1)
species. The terminology “functional similarity" is proposed in
Eisenhauer et al. 2023
as an alternative to functional redundancy to describe similar or, in specific cases, identical effects of one species on ecosystem processes. It is important to keep in mind that this conclusion relies on the selected functional traits and the assumption that they cover all the ecological functions.

12 - Invariance to species split,

Mason et al. 2003
.
A functional-diversity index
f
should be unaffected if any species is split into two or more entities that share its exact trait values, provided their combined abundance equals the original. Formally, for a community
A
containing species
i
with weight
w_{i}
, let
A^{\prime}
be the community where
i
is replaced by two species
j
and
k
with identical trait vectors and weights
w_{j}
and
w_{k}
satisfying
w_{i}=w_{j}+w_{k}
. Then
f(A)=f(A^{\prime})
. Since the split species are functionally equivalent, they introduce no new ecological roles and should not alter the diversity index.

13 - Positivity,

Ricotta 2005
.
Functional diversity should be nonnegative.

14 - Concavity,

Ricotta 2005
.
The total diversity in a set of communities should be greater than or equal to the weighted average diversity within the communities. This requirement is introduced in
Ricotta 2005
, but was initially proposed in
Lande 1996
for a more general measure of species diversity. This property enables the decomposition of functional diversity at different scales into
\gamma
,
\alpha
, and
\beta
functional diversity. The
\gamma
functional diversity is at the scale of the whole landscape composed of the different communities;
\alpha
functional diversity is at the scale of the individual communities, and
\beta
functional diversity is between communities. In
Meng et al. 2021
, the effects of commercial sand mining on the aquatic functional diversity of Dongnan and Wanzi lakes (China) are studied. Each component
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
of functional diversity is computed on several sampling sites. A multiscale study leads to robust conclusions and a better understanding of the evolution and resilience of an ecosystem. This is possible when
\gamma
functional diversity can be decomposed into
\alpha
and
\beta
functional diversity. This is guaranteed when the functional diversity index is concave. When the functional diversity index is not concave, then the diversity between samples is negative, leading to an impossible interpretation of the decomposition of
\gamma
diversity.

3
Existing Functional Diversity Metrics: A Brief Review

Measuring functional diversity has been a challenge, and numerous approaches have been proposed throughout the years. The existing indices of functional diversity can be listed into three categories introduced in
Villéger et al. 2008
and developed in
Schmera et al. 2023
: functional richness, functional divergence, and functional regularity. This decomposition is essential to capture all the complementary characteristics of the distribution of the species in the functional trait space.

Functional richness answers the question:
how much?
It represents the size of the functional representation in the functional trait space. It is the first aspect of functional diversity that was explored in
Legras et al. 2018
. To compute functional richness, functional groups can be formed in a given assemblage by clustering functionally similar species. In
Petchey and Gaston 2002b
, functional richness is defined as the total branch length of the functional dendrogram resulting from this clustering step. Such indices are highly subjective; they are based on the user’s choice of the number of groups. If we assume that the functioning of an ecosystem is linked to the number of functional groups, then this number should be chosen to reduce variability in the estimation of functional diversity. Functional richness can be computed using the functional attribute diversity (FaD) index, defined as the total sum of all the pairwise distances between species. The addition of a clustering step before computing this sum leads to the modified functional attribute diversity (mFaD) index. Both FaD and mFaD have limitations. An example illustrating these limitations is given in
Ricotta 2005
. Consider a set where a species
j
is functionally far from all the other species. We introduce a new species
k
which is extremely close to
j
, but far from all the other species. FaD will drastically increase, but the change in diversity is little because
j
and
k
are functionally close.
Villéger et al. 2008
measures functional richness (FR) as the volume of the functional space occupied by the community based on the computation of the minimum convex hull. Methods considering convex hull, including the index trait onion peeling (TOP) by
Fontana et al. 2015
, provide good estimations of functional richness, but they are computationally costly.

Functional divergence answers the question:
how different?
. It encapsulates the differences in functional traits in the trait space. Functional divergence can be measured with the average functional contribution (AC) of a species to the total community index, or with Rao’s quadratic entropy (Rao’s QE)
Botta-Dukát 2005
, which measures the average pairwise trait distance between species. The functional divergence (FDiv) index introduced in
Villéger et al. 2008
relies on the sum of the divergence for each species to the center of gravity in the trait space, weighted by the abundances. The FDiv index is thus low when the most abundant species have functional traits very close to the center of the range of functional traits, which is equivalent to a low spread in the trait space.
Laliberté and Legendre 2010
proposed a framework to compute functional dispersion (FDis) based on the trait distance matrix and evaluated by computing the mean distance of each species to the centroid of all
N
species in the trait space.

Functional regularity, or evenness, answers the question:
how variable?
The FDVar index by
Mason et al. 2003
computes the variance in the trait space in log scale, weighted by the abundances of the species in the studied community. The functional regularity index FRO proposed in
Mouillot et al. 2004
, based on the work of
Bulla 1994
, measures how evenly-spaced the species are in the functional trait space, i.e., how much a community differs from the maximum functional regularity. The FRO index only processes one functional trait, and its extension to multiple functional traits, called FEve, is proposed in
Villéger et al. 2008
. The trait even distribution (TED) index by
Fontana et al. 2015
measures how evenly distributed the species are in the trait space by using a reference distribution.

Combining functional richness, functional divergence, and functional evenness in one index is challenging, as they represent different aspects of functional diversity. In
Cardoso et al. 2024
, neighbor-joining trees based on pairwise dissimilarities are used to compute all three aspects, resulting in the indices NJ trees richness (NJ TR), NJ trees dispersion (NJ TD), and NJ trees evenness (NJ TE). The same tree is used, but the analysis of the three indices combined is needed for any application. 4
Existing Functional Diversity Metrics Often Fail

We now extensively evaluate the metrics discussed previously. We first assess whether they meet the requirements listed in Section
2
. Table
1
summarizes the results. None of the metrics meet all the requirements. Of particular concern is the fact that all metrics except one fail to meet the set monotonicity, twinning, distance monotonicity, sensitivity to functionally-rare species, continuity in abundance, and invariance to species split. These are important criteria for sensibly detecting biodiversity loss, and failure to meet them puts into question the conclusion we can draw from applying these metrics to data.

In addition to assessing the metrics in terms of the list of requirements, we also assessed them in very simple yet realistic ecosystem scenarios. The experiment is illustrated in Figure
1
. Only two functional traits are considered here, meaning that the functional trait space is 2-dimensional. Each species is represented by a black dot, and the size of the dot is correlated with the value of its abundance: a big dot means the abundance of the species is high, and a small dot means the abundance is small. The values of the traits are integers from 1 to 8. The trait distance matrix
D
is computed using the
L_{1}
distance.
This experiment is inspired by
Schleuter et al. 2010
, where a study of some indices is also conducted.

Six scenarios are considered. Scenario I corresponds to the case where one species with different trait values, and thus a different position in the functional trait space, is added to the community. Different values of similarity to the initial community are used while the abundances are kept equal for all species. Adding such a species to the community should increase functional diversity. We expect a higher increase when the similarity to the community is smaller, i.e., when the trait distance is higher. This illustrates the requirement of set monotonicity.

Scenario II consists in increasing the abundance of only one species in the community. We start with a balanced community (all abundances are equal), and we give more and more importance to one species. We expect a decrease in functional diversity as one species dominates in abundance. Indeed, an assemblage of 10 species dominated by one is less diverse than another with 10 equally represented species.

Scenario III corresponds to the case where one species dominates the community in terms of abundance, but we now vary its position in the trait space. The similarity of the dominant species compared to the community varies. We expect an increase in functional diversity when the similarity decreases.

In Scenario IV, two species dominate the community and have the same abundance. We increase the distance between these two species. The less similar they are, the more functionally diverse the community is supposed to be.

Scenarios V and VI are extinction experiments where the most dissimilar species are going extinct in the first case, and the least dissimilar species are going extinct in the second case. This illustrates the requirements of concavity and set monotonicity in an extinction scenario. In both cases, we expect functional diversity to decrease.

5
Conclusion

Measuring functional diversity is critical for biodiversity. In this paper, we first reviewed the literature on functional diversity measurement. We then listed fourteen requirements that robust measures of functional diversity should meet and subsequently assessed fifteen widely used functional diversity metrics against these requirements, revealing that none fully meet all of them. We also tested these metrics across six ecosystem scenarios, and again found no single metric that consistently exhibited the expected ground-truth behavior in all scenarios. Our results highlight the urgent need for developing a new generation of functional diversity metrics that more accurately reflect ecological realities.
