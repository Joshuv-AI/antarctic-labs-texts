Meson mass spectrum in QCD 2 ’t Hooft’s model
Alexey Litvinov, Pavel Meshcheriakov 1. Krichever Center, Skolkovo Institute of Science and Technology, 121205 Moscow, Russia 2. Landau Institute for Theoretical Physics, 142432 Chernogolovka, Russia 3. Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology, 141700 Dolgoprudny, Russia

Abstract
We study the spectrum of meson masses in large N c N_{c} QCD 2 governed by celebrated ’t Hooft’s integral equation. We generalize analytical methods proposed by Fateev, Lukyanov and Zamolodchikov to the case of arbitrary, but equal quark masses m 1 = m 2 . m_{1}=m_{2}. Our results include analytical expressions for spectral sums and systematic large- n n expansion. We also study the spectral sums in the chiral limit and the heavy quark limit and find a complete agreement with known results.

Introduction

One of the most difficult and intriguing problems in modern theoretical physics is to understand the mechanism of confinement of colored particles: quarks and gluons. Unfortunately, at the moment it is not possible to analytically solve this problem in strongly coupled Gauge Theories in various dimensions, and in particular in Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD) in dimension
3+1
. One might hope that studying this problem in various toy models, will bring us closer to the solution of this long-standing question.

In this paper, we will consider a
1+1
-dimensional version of multicolor QCD, the so-called QCD
2
’t Hooft’s model
[
1
]
. This theory is one of the simplest known toy models demonstrating the phenomenon of confinement. The model is described by the Lagrangian density of
SU(N_{c})
Yang-Mills theory coupled to
N_{f}
massive fermions in fundamental representation

\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{2}\text{tr\;}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N_{f}}\bar{\psi}_{k}(i\gamma^{\mu}D_{\mu}-m_{k})\psi_{k}.

(1.1)

We are interested in the double scaling limit at which the number of colors
N_{c}
grows, while keeping the ’t Hooft’s coupling constant
g^{2}=g^{2}_{{\text{YM}}}N_{c}
fixed. In this limit only planar Feynman diagrams are relevant, and diagrams with self-intersections are suppressed by the power factors
1/N_{c}
(depending on the number of self-intersections). As a result, there are no free quarks in this theory, and the spectrum of bound states, the mesons, resembles the Regge trajectories known in QCD
4
. Therefore, this low-dimensional model turns out to be convenient for studying the mechanisms of confinement.

Since the mesons in this model are composite objects, their masses are precisely determined by the Bethe-Salpeter equation. For mesons that consist of quark and antiquark with the bare (Lagrangian) masses
m_{i}\;(i=1,2),
the Bethe-Salpeter equation can be reduced to the integral equation called the ’t Hooft equation
[
1
]

2\pi^{2}\lambda\;\phi_{12}(x)=\mathcal{H}\phi_{12}(x)=\left[\frac{\alpha_{1}}{x}+\frac{\alpha_{2}}{1-x}\right]\phi_{12}(x)-\fint_{0}^{1}\limits dy\frac{\phi_{12}(y)}{(x-y)^{2}},\quad\alpha_{i}=\frac{\pi m^{2}_{i}}{g^{2}}-1.

(1.2)

Here
\phi_{12}(x)
is the wave function of the meson state, which has the following asymptotic behavior at the endpoints of the interval
[0,1]

\phi_{12}(x)\sim\begin{cases}x^{\beta_{1}},\quad&x\to 0;\\
(1-x)^{\beta_{2}},\quad&x\to 1,\end{cases}

(1.3)

and
\beta_{i}
are the zeros of the equation

\pi\beta_{i}\cot{(\pi\beta_{i})}+\alpha_{i}=0,\quad 0\leq\beta_{i}<1.

(1.4)

Equation (
1.2
) is the spectral problem for the parameter
\lambda.
The meson masses
M^{2}_{n},\;n=0,1,2,\dots
are discrete and related to the eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}
as

M^{2}_{n}=2\pi g^{2}\lambda_{n}.

(1.5)

Despite the fact that ’t Hooft equation can be solved numerically with high accuracy, and the main characteristics of this theory can be obtained from the numerical solution, the search for analytical solutions is also of interest. In their seminal paper
[
2
]
Fateev, Lukyanov, and Zamolodchikov (FLZ) initiated the development of analytic methods for ’t Hooft equation. Their paper studied the special case of quark masses
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=0

2
). The authors considered two limiting cases:

•

low energy levels (small
\lambda,

•

high energy levels (large
\lambda,

They managed to calculate the spectral sums

2
From ’t Hooft to Baxter’s TQ equation

In this section, following
[
2
]
, we reformulate the problem of finding the meson masses of t Hooft’s equation in the language of integrability, namely in terms of Baxter’s TQ equation, and explain our strategy for solving it.

2.1
Fourier form of ’t Hooft equation

It is useful to apply the Fourier transform with the variable
\frac{1}{2}\log{\frac{x}{1-x}}
to the equation (
1.2
) (for the first time such a transformation was proposed in
[
4
]
)

\phi(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\limits\frac{d\nu}{2\pi}\left(\frac{x}{1-x}\right)^{\frac{i\nu}{2}}\Psi(\nu),\quad\Psi(\nu)=\int_{0}^{1}\limits\frac{dx}{2x(1-x)}\left(\frac{x}{1-x}\right)^{-\frac{i\nu}{2}}\phi(x).

(2.1)

The Fourier form of the equation (
1.2
) is (for
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\alpha
)

\left(\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}+\nu\coth{\frac{\pi\nu}{2}}\right)\Psi(\nu)-\lambda\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\limits d\nu^{\prime}\frac{\pi(\nu^{\prime}-\nu)}{2\sinh{\frac{\pi(\nu^{\prime}-\nu)}{2}}}\Psi(\nu^{\prime})=0.

(2.2)

For the norm
\|\varphi\|^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}\limits dx\;|\varphi(x)|^{2}
to be finite and the function
\varphi(x)
in (
1.2
) to satisfy the boundary conditions (
1.3
), the solution
\Psi(\nu)
has to decay at
|\nu|\to\infty
, and be a smooth function of the real variable
\nu
. Careful analysis of the equation (
2.2
) shows that
\Psi(\nu)
is a meromorphic function of the complex variable
\nu
with simple poles at

i\nu_{k}^{*}+2iN,\quad-i\nu_{k}^{*}-2iN,\quad N\geq 0,

(2.3)

where
\pm i\nu_{k}^{*}
are roots of the equation

\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}+\nu\coth{\frac{\pi\nu}{2}}=0\quad\text{for}\quad\alpha>-1.

(2.4)

2.2
TQ equation

If we define the new function
Q(\nu)
by the following expression

Q(\nu)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\sinh\frac{\pi\nu}{2}\left(\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}+\nu\coth\frac{\pi\nu}{2}\right)\Psi(\nu),

(2.5)

we conclude that the analytic properties of the solution
\Psi(\nu)
imply that
Q(\nu)
has to satisfy the following requirements

1.

be analytic in the strip
\textrm{Im}\,\nu\in[-2,2];

2.

grow slower than any exponential at
|\textrm{Re}\,\nu|\to\infty,
in other words to be bounded as

Q(\nu)=\mathcal{O}(e^{\epsilon|\nu|}),\quad\forall\epsilon>0,\quad|\textrm{Re}\,\nu|\to\infty;

(2.6)

3.

obey the quantization conditions

Q(0)=Q(\pm 2i)=0.

(2.7)

In terms of the
Q
-function, the equation (
2.2
) takes the following form

Q(\nu)=\lambda\sinh{\frac{\pi\nu}{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\limits d\nu^{\prime}\frac{\pi(\nu-\nu^{\prime})}{2\sinh{\pi(\nu-\nu^{\prime})}}\frac{Q(\nu^{\prime})}{\nu^{\prime}\cosh{\frac{\pi\nu^{\prime}}{2}}+\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}\sinh{\frac{\pi\nu^{\prime}}{2}}}.

(2.8)

In particular, the quantization condition (
2.7
) follows directly from (
2.8
).

Let us consider the following linear combination of
Q
functions

Q(\nu+i\epsilon+2i)+Q(\nu+i\epsilon-2i)-2Q(\nu+i\epsilon),

(2.9)

and use (
2.8
). We note that for
\epsilon>0
the r.h.s. of (
2.8
) for
Q(\nu+i\epsilon+2i)
acquires additional residue term

\lambda\sinh{\frac{\pi(\nu+2i)}{2}}\varointclockwise_{\mathcal{C}_{\nu}}d\nu^{\prime}\frac{\pi(\nu-\nu^{\prime}+2i)}{2\sinh{\frac{\pi(\nu-\nu^{\prime}+2i)}{2}}}\frac{Q(\nu^{\prime})}{\nu^{\prime}\cosh{\frac{\pi\nu^{\prime}}{2}}+\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}\sinh{\frac{\pi\nu^{\prime}}{2}}}=-\frac{4\pi\lambda\sinh{\frac{\pi\nu}{2}}}{\nu\cosh{\frac{\pi\nu}{2}}+\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}\sinh{\frac{\pi\nu}{2}}}Q(\nu),

(2.10)

while the integral terms cancel exactly for the combination (
2.9
). Thus we obtain the functional difference equation

Q(\nu+2i)+Q(\nu-2i)-2Q(\nu)=-\frac{2z}{\nu+\alpha x}Q(\nu),

(2.11)

where

z=2\pi\lambda\tanh\left(\frac{\pi\nu}{2}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad x=\frac{2}{\pi}\tanh\left(\frac{\pi\nu}{2}\right).

(2.12)

Equation (
2.11
) has the form of the famous Baxter’s TQ equation and our goal, using the solutions of this equation, is to obtain the analytical expressions the meson masses of the original ’t Hooft’s equation.

2.3
Inhomogeneous integral equation

Before we proceed directly to the search of the solution, let us say few words about the quantization conditions. A solution to (
2.11
) with the above three properties exists only at specific values of
\lambda
, which determine the eigenvalues of (
2.2
). However, if we relax the conditions and drop the last one (
2.7
), then solutions
Q(\nu|\lambda)
will exist for any
\lambda
. For arbitrary
\lambda
, the corresponding function
\Psi(\nu|\lambda)
will no longer satisfy the homogeneous integral equation (
2.2
), but rather inhomogeneous one
3
3

3

It can be derived by standard methods of complex analysis. Namely, we apply the operator
\hat{K}
, where

\hat{K}\cdot f(\nu)=\fint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\limits\frac{\pi(\nu-\nu^{\prime})}{2\sinh\frac{\pi(\nu-\nu^{\prime})}{2}}\frac{1}{\sinh\frac{\pi\nu^{\prime}}{2}}f(\nu^{\prime})d\nu^{\prime}

to the both sides of TQ equation (
2.11
). The r.h.s. of (
2.11
) will result in the
\lambda
term of (
2.13
). In the l.h.s. of (
2.11
) all three integrals can be reduced to one, which then vanishes identically, picking some residue terms. There are two types of such terms, either coming from the function
\frac{\pi(\nu-\nu^{\prime})}{2\sinh\frac{\pi(\nu-\nu^{\prime})}{2}}
which produce
\left(\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}+\nu\coth{\frac{\pi\nu}{2}}\right)\Psi(\nu)
term in (
2.13
), or from
\frac{1}{\sinh\frac{\pi\nu^{\prime}}{2}}
which produces r.h.s. of (
2.13
). We leave it as an exercise to relate
q_{\pm}(\lambda)
to the values
Q(0)
and
Q(\pm 2i)
.

\left(\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}+\nu\coth{\frac{\pi\nu}{2}}\right)\Psi(\nu)-\lambda\fint^{\infty}_{-\infty}\limits d\nu^{\prime}\frac{\pi(\nu-\nu^{\prime})}{2\sinh{\frac{\pi(\nu-\nu^{\prime})}{2}}}\Psi(\nu^{\prime})=F(\nu|\lambda),\quad F(\nu|\lambda)=\frac{q_{+}(\lambda)\nu+q_{-}(\lambda)}{\sinh{\frac{\pi\nu}{2}}},

(2.13)

where
q_{\pm}(\lambda)
are linear combinations of
Q(0)
and
Q(\pm 2i)
. It can be shown that once the coefficients
q_{\pm}(\lambda)
are chosen the solution of (
2.13
) is unique. Thus, we are dealing with a two-dimensional space of functions
\Psi(\nu|\lambda)
, where we can choose a basis of symmetric and antisymmetric functions

\Psi_{\pm}(-\nu|\lambda)=\pm\Psi_{\pm}(\nu|\lambda),

(2.14)

corresponding to

F_{+}(\nu|\lambda)=\frac{\nu}{\sinh{\frac{\pi\nu}{2}}}\quad\text{and}\quad F_{-}(\nu|\lambda)=\frac{1}{\sinh{\frac{\pi\nu}{2}}}

(2.15)

respectively.

As one already mentioned, equation (
2.13
) has a unique solution for any
\lambda
for each non-zero fixed r.h.s. But if
\lambda
approaches the spectral point
\lambda\rightarrow\lambda_{n}
then the original homogeneous equation (
2.2
) has to be recovered. It means that the function
\Psi_{\pm}(\nu|\lambda)
should diverge at the spectral points. More careful analysis
[
2
]
shows that
\Psi_{\pm}(\nu|\lambda)
are meromorphic functions of
\lambda
with singularities only at
\lambda_{n}

\Psi_{+}(\nu|\lambda)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{2n}\Psi_{2n}(\nu)}{\lambda-\lambda_{2n}},\quad\Psi_{-}(\nu|\lambda)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{2n+1}\Psi_{2n+1}(\nu)}{\lambda-\lambda_{2n+1}}.

(2.16)

The coefficients
c_{n}
have a deep physical meaning
[
2
,
5
]
: they are related to the matrix elements of the vector current
J_{\mu}=\bar{\psi}\gamma_{\mu}\psi
and the scalar density
\bar{\psi}\psi
.

2.4
Quantum Wronskian relation

The symmetric and antisymmetric functions
\Psi_{\pm}(\nu|\lambda)
are used to construct the functions
Q_{\pm}(\nu|\lambda)
(further, for simplicity, we will sometimes hide the dependence on
\lambda
, but it will always be implied)

Q_{\pm}(-\nu)=\mp Q_{\pm}(\nu).

(2.17)

They are two independent solutions of the inhomogeneous integral equation (
2.13
) and also of TQ equation (
2.11
). According to the choice of
F_{\pm}(\nu|\lambda)
in (
2.15
), these functions have the following normalization conditions

Q_{+}(2i)=2i\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{-}(0)=1.

(2.18)

Baxter’s TQ equation is known to play an important role in quantum integrable systems and has a large set of properties, one of which is associated with the so-called quantum Wronskian

W(\nu|\lambda)\overset{\text{def}}{=}Q_{+}(\nu+i)Q_{-}(\nu-i)-Q_{+}(\nu-i)Q_{-}(\nu+i).

(2.19)

By construction, this quantity is a
2i
periodic function (it follows directly from (
2.11
))

W(\nu|\lambda)=W(\nu+2i|\lambda).

(2.20)

Since the functions
Q_{\pm}(\nu)
are analytic in the strip
\textrm{Im}\,\nu\in[-2,2]
and grow slower than any exponent (
2.6
), then in fact the Wronskian turns out to be an entire and bounded function, and thus is a constant. It is enough to determine the value of the Wronskian at one point, for example
\nu=i

W(\nu|\lambda)=W(i|\lambda)=Q_{+}(2i)Q_{-}(0)=2i,

(2.21)

which implies in particular (if we substitute
\nu=0
in (
2.19
))

Q_{+}(i)Q_{-}(i)=i.

(2.22)

Using the analytical dependence of the functions
\Psi_{\pm}(\nu|\lambda)
on
\lambda
(
2.16
), we obtain that the same behavior holds for the functions
Q_{\pm}(\nu|\lambda)

Q_{+}(\nu|\lambda)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{2n}Q_{2n}(\nu)}{\lambda-\lambda_{2n}}\sim\frac{1}{\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\limits(\lambda-\lambda_{2n})},\quad Q_{-}(\nu|\lambda)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{2n+1}Q_{2n+1}(\nu)}{\lambda-\lambda_{2n+1}}\sim\frac{1}{\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\limits(\lambda-\lambda_{2n+1})}.

(2.23)

Using (
2.23
) together with the ratio (
2.22
), we obtain that at the point
\nu=i,
the poles of the function
Q_{-}
are the zeros of the function
Q_{+}
and vice versa

Q_{+}(i)\sim\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda-\lambda_{2n+1}}{\lambda-\lambda_{2n}}\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{-}(i)\sim\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda-\lambda_{2n}}{\lambda-\lambda_{2n+1}}.

(2.24)

2.5
Spectral determinants

Let us define the spectral determinants

4

We note that in

Using the resolvent and the Liouville-Neumann series formalism, the following relations between the spectral determinants and the

)

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}\log(D_{+}D_{-})

\displaystyle=2-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\limits\left[\frac{1}{\nu\coth\frac{\pi\nu}{2}+\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}}\left(Q_{-}(\nu)\partial_{\nu}Q_{+}(\nu)-Q_{+}(\nu)\partial_{\nu}Q_{-}(\nu)\right)-\frac{1}{\nu\coth\frac{\pi\nu}{2}}\right]d\nu,

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}\log\left(\frac{D_{+}}{D_{-}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\limits\frac{\pi}{\nu\coth\frac{\pi\nu}{2}+\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}}\frac{Q_{+}(\nu)Q_{-}(\nu)}{\sinh\pi\nu}d\nu.

(2.30)

We emphasize that (

2.6
Strategy

We follow the strategy suggested in
[
2
]
for obtaining analytic expressions for meson mass spectrum. We consider two limiting cases of small and large values of the parameter
\lambda
. Within these limits, we look for symmetric and antisymmetric solutions
Q_{\pm}(\nu)
to TQ-equation (
2.11
) that are analytic and bounded in the strip
\textrm{Im}\,\nu\in[-2,2]
. In both cases the solutions satisfying all properties appear as series over the integer powers of
\lambda^{s}
(
s
is positive for the case of small

(6.18)

After calculations we obtain the desired asymptotics

G^{(1)}_{\pm}(\alpha)\big|_{\alpha\to\infty}\approx\log\left(\frac{2\pi^{2}e^{-\gamma_{E}-2}}{\alpha}\right)\pm\frac{\pi^{2}}{8\alpha}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\right).

(6.19)

(6.20)

Thus, we have the agreement of this method with our limit formulas (

7.1
Method

To verify our analytical expressions for the spectral sums as well as large
n-
expansion for the spectrum, we use the decomposition of the wave function
\phi(x)
by the following basis
[
12
]

\phi(x)=\sum_{n}\phi_{n}h_{n}(x),\quad h_{n}(x)=\sin{(n\theta)}=2\sqrt{x(1-x)}U_{n-1}(1-2x),\quad n\geq 1,

(7.1)

where the new variable
\theta\in[0,\pi]
with
x=\frac{1-\cos{\theta}}{2}
and
U_{n}(x)
are Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind. It allows to reformulate the ’t Hooft integral equation into a matrix problem, which in a truncated form (for some sufficiently large
N
) is amenable to machine solution

2\pi^{2}\lambda\sum_{n}M_{mn}\phi_{n}=\sum_{n}H_{mn}\phi_{n},\quad M_{mn}=\braket{h_{m}|h_{n}},\quad H_{mn}=\bra{h_{m}}\mathcal{H}\ket{h_{n}},

(7.2)

where the scalar product has a standard form

\braket{h|g}=\int_{0}^{1}\limits dx\;h(x)g(x).

(7.3)

The matrices
\bm{M}
and
\bm{H}
admit the integral form

M_{mn}=\int_{0}^{1}\limits dx\;\sin{(m\theta)}\sin{(n\theta)}=\begin{cases}-\frac{2mn}{m^{4}+(n^{2}-1)^{2}-2m^{2}(1+n^{2})},\quad&m+n=2k,\quad\quad\;\;\,k=1,2,\dots;\\
0,\quad&m+n=2k-1,\quad k=1,2,\dots\end{cases}

(7.4)

and

H_{mn}=\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}\limits dx\;\sin{(m\theta)}\sin{(n\theta)}\;\alpha\left(\frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{1-x}\right)}_{H^{(1)}_{mn}}-\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}\limits dx\sin{(m\theta)}\fint_{0}^{1}\limits dy\frac{\sin{(n\theta^{\prime})}}{(x-y)^{2}}}_{H^{(2)}_{mn},\hskip 8.19447pty=\frac{1-\cos{\theta^{\prime}}}{2}}.

(7.5)

The first integral
H^{(1)}_{mn}
in (
7.5
) has the form

H^{(1)}_{mn}=2\alpha\int_{0}^{\pi}\limits d\theta\;\frac{\sin{(m\theta)}\sin{(n\theta)}}{\sin{\theta}}=2\alpha\cdot\begin{cases}2\sum_{l=\frac{|m-n|}{2}+1}^{\frac{m+n}{2}}\limits\frac{1}{2l-1},\quad&m+n=2k,\quad\quad\;\;\,k=1,2,\dots;\\
0,\quad&m+n=2k-1,\quad k=1,2,\dots\end{cases}

(7.6)

while the second integral
H^{(2)}_{mn}
is simply

H^{(2)}_{mn}=\int_{0}^{1}\limits dx\sin{(m\theta)}\fint_{0}^{1}\limits dy\frac{\sin{(n\theta^{\prime})}}{(x-y)^{2}}=-\pi n\int_{0}^{\pi}\limits d\theta\;\sin{(m\theta)}\sin{(n\theta)}=-\frac{n\pi^{2}}{2}\delta_{mn}.

(7.7)

Thus the matrix elements of
\bm{H}
are

H_{mn}=\frac{n\pi^{2}}{2}\delta_{mn}+H^{(1)}_{mn}.

(7.8)

This numerical method can also be applied for the case when the quark masses are different
\alpha_{1}\neq\alpha_{2}
, details can be found in
[
13
]
.

Conclusion

In this work, we studied the spectrum of meson masses in multicolor QCD
2
from the point of view of integrability. We focused on the case of identical quark masses
m_{1}=m_{2}
and showed that the solutions of the ’t Hooft’s integral equation are closely related to the Baxter’s TQ equation. Moreover, we explained how to deal with the technical difficulties arising when trying to construct these solutions in the two limiting cases. We have found a generalization of the key formula (2.17) in
[
2
]
, thus generalizing the approach proposed by Fateev, Lukyanov, and Zamolodchikov to the case
\alpha\neq 0
. In our work, these are the relations (
2.32
) and (
2.33
). In the limiting case of small
\lambda
, we use them to compute the spectral sums
G_{\pm}^{(s)},
and in the case of large
\lambda
, we obtain the large-
n
expansion of the spectrum. For the spectral sums we also considered the chiral limit
\alpha\to-1
and the heavy quark limit
\alpha\to\infty
and found a complete agreement of our results with other works devoted to the ’t Hooft model.

The expressions obtained can be used to find approximate analytic expressions of the lowest-lying energy levels of the bound states.

In addition, the analytic structure of spectral sums as functions of the complex parameter
\alpha
is a whole field for theoretical investigations. For example, it is an interesting question to search for critical points of spectral sums and to comprehend the physics behind them. These poles can perhaps be identified with non-unitary CFTs and their identification is an interesting open problem.

Another separate direction for future research is the Ising model, for which the Bethe-Salpeter equation was derived in the two-particle approximation in
[
14
]
.

It will be interesting to generalize our results to the case of different quark masses, i.e. for
\alpha_{1}\neq\alpha_{2}
. We have obtained preliminary results, and more detailed study of this case will be published elsewhere.

Appendix B
Solution of the shift equation

Here we remind a rather general approach to finding solutions to the shift equations, which are of the form

f(\nu+2i)=f(\nu)+h(\nu),

(B.1)

where
h(\nu)
is an entire and bounded function of the real variable
\nu
, and we require the solution
f(\nu)
to be an analytic and bounded function in the strip
\textrm{Im}\,\nu\in[0,2]
. Such requirements for the solution guarantee its uniqueness up to a shift. It can be found as follows. Consider the following integral

I(\nu)=\frac{1}{4i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\limits f(t)\left(\tanh\frac{\pi(t-\nu)}{2}-\tanh\frac{\pi t}{2}\right)dt,

(B.2)

where the contour
\mathcal{C}
is taken as

On one hand by Cauchy theorem one has (we use the fact that
f(\nu)
is an entire function in the strip)

I(\nu)=f(\nu+i)-f(i)

(B.3)

and on the other rewriting the integral as
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\limits-\int_{-\infty+2i}^{\infty+2i}\limits
and using (
4.2
) one finds

I(\nu)=\frac{i}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\limits h(t)\left(\tanh\frac{\pi(t-\nu)}{2}-\tanh\frac{\pi t}{2}\right)dt,

(B.4)

and hence

f(\nu+i)=f(i)+\frac{i}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\limits h(t)\left(\tanh\frac{\pi(t-\nu)}{2}-\tanh\frac{\pi t}{2}\right)dt.

(B.5)

The described method also allows us to solve multiplicative equations, which are reduced to additive equations by logarithmization.

F(\nu+2i)=H(\nu)F(\nu)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\log F(\nu+2i)=\log F(\nu)+\log H(\nu).

(B.6)

Only now, the desired solutions
F(\nu)
, in addition to the properties previously required, must have an additional one, namely, to have no zeros in the strip
\textrm{Im}\,\nu\in[0,2]
.

We note that the functional relation (
3.9
) obeyed by the function
\uppsi_{\alpha}(\nu)
formally violates the conditions specified above. That is, the shift function
h(\nu)
has a simple pole at the origin
\nu=0
. In order to preserve the analytic properties, one has to shift the integration contour by
-i\epsilon
.
