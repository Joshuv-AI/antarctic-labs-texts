Physiological and biochemical responses of wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) and barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) to combined exposure of Pharmaceuticals, personal care products (PPCPs), and artificial sweeteners (ASs)
Authors: Müjgan Elveren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, pharmaceutical active ingredients (gemfibrozil, acetaminophen and ciprofloxacin) and artificial sweeteners (aspartame and sucralose) were combined and applied to wheat and barley plants. After 15 days of cultivation, a series of biochemical and physiological parameters were evaluated. These included electrolyte leakage, antioxidant enzyme activities such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POX), chlorophyll levels, total phenolic content (TPC), Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP), total flavonoid content (TFC) and concentrations. The relationship between the control samples and the samples treated with pharmaceutical active ingredients and artificial sweeteners was statistically evaluated at the 95% confidence interval, and significant differences were found. The treatments markedly influenced plant physiology and antioxidant defense mechanisms. Electrolyte leakage increased to 795.71 ± 50.14 µS cm-1 in wheat, while catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POX), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities showed significant elevations, reaching 5797.5 ± 115.14, 95176.00 ± 3675.42, and 1766.11 ± 13.97 EU g−1, respectively. In contrast, chlorophyll a + b, carotenoids, total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), and FRAP values decreased substantially compared with controls. These findings demonstrate that mixtures of pharmaceuticals and artificial sweeteners can disrupt photosynthetic pigment stability, and impair antioxidant defense in wheat and barley, highlighting potential ecological and agricultural risks associated with emerging contaminant mixtures.Keywords: ASs, Antioxidant activity, PPCPs, FRAP

Introduction

Artificial sweeteners (ASs) and pharmaceuticals and personal care products (PPCPs) and are emerging contaminants. In recent years, the growing frequency with which these pollutants have been detected in the environment has heightened concerns regarding their potential impacts on plants and ecosystems [1, 2]. Often, residues of these pollutants accumulate in wastewater effluents after consumption, and because they are not adequately removed in wastewater treatment plants, these compounds can leach into freshwater ecosystems. In the absence of wastewater treatment plants, these substances can enter the aqueous environment directly from municipal wastewater effluents through human and animal faeces, which are randomly dumped in the environment [3]. Due to their persistence, these pollutants can persist in the environment for an extended period. Even at low concentrations, they can cause potential toxicity to non-target organisms. Furthermore, the risk of contamination increases when agricultural crops are irrigated with contaminated water. These chemicals can become a threat to human health when they reach groundwater and when contaminated agricultural products are consumed [4]. PPCPs contain a wide range of chemicals. This inculudes chemicals comprising active and inert ingredients in over-the-counter products, as well as prescription human and veterinary medicines and personal care products (such as analgesics and anti-inflammatories, antidiabetics, antiepileptics, antiestrogenics, antiprotozoals, antiseptics, lipid regulators, diuretics, drugs for pulmonary arterial hypertension, psychiatric drugs and antidepressants, veterinary and human antibiotics, cosmetic and personal-care products, surfactants, and phytosanitary products) [5, 6]. ASs are widely employed as high-intensity, low-calorie sugar substitutes in a variety of products, including foods, beverages and animal feed [7, 8]. The most commonly used ASs include acesulfame, sucralose, saccharin (SAC), cyclamate (CYC), aspartame (ASP), neotame (NEO), as well as neohesperidin dihydrochalcone (NHDC) [7]. Many of these chemicals are resistant to both abiotic and biotic degradation, making them persistent in the environment. Moreover, they are highly mobile and can be readily transported in aqueous systems [9]. The incomplete removal of these pollutants in wastewater treatment plants and the direct discharge of untreated wastewater represent a significant challenge [10–12]. Pharmaceutical active ingredients (PPCPs) and artificial sweeteners (ASs) are two distinct chemical groups that frequently coexist in wastewater used for irrigation [13, 14]. PPCPs such as gemfibrozil and acetaminophen can induce oxidative stress by disturbing redox balance while ASs like aspartame may affect carbon metabolism and membrane stability [15]. Their combined occurrence may therefore cause stronger physiological stress by affecting multiple metabolic and defense pathways. This study hypothesizes that co-exposure of wheat and barley to PPCP and AS mixtures will intensify oxidative stress and disrupt both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems.

In this study, the effects of artificial sweeteners mixed with pharmaceutical active ingredients on plants were investigated. To this end, potential changes in the metabolic properties of plants were examined, and the possible implications of these changes for plant health, productivity, and environmental impact were evaluated. Furthermore, the broader environmental consequences of pharmaceuticals and artificial sweeteners (PPCPs and ASs) were discussed.

Materials and methods

Test materials

The present study evaluated the effects of simple mixtures of three medicinal active ingredients (gemfibrozil, acetaminophen and ciprofloxacin) and two artificial sweeteners (aspartame and sucralose) on wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) and barley (Hordeum vulgare L.). The cultivation process was carried out in the laboratory according to the methods described by Elveren and Osma [16]. In brief, for sowing, 5 g of wheat and 4 g of barley seeds were sown in 650 g of soil and covered with 100 g of soil. Wheat and barley were grown in locally collected loamy soil from Erzincan, Türkiye. The soil had a pH of 7.0–8.0 and was amended with animal manure and perlite to improve aeration and support healthy plant development. Plants were cultivated under controlled laboratory conditions with approximately 60% relative humidity. After germination, the ambient temperature was maintained between 10 and 15 °C to support optimal seedling growth. Irrigation was adjusted according to the field capacity of the soil. The pharmaceutical active ingredients gemfibrozil (50 mg), acetaminophen (50 mg), and ciprofloxacin (50 mg) were dissolved in water and applied via irrigation, first in combination with sucralose (50 mg) and then with aspartame (50 mg). To ensure experimental reliability, the applications were applied randomly on plant samples. Treatments were applied twice during the growth period using equal volumes of water to ensure consistent soil moisture and exposure. After 15 days of cultivation, wheat and barley samples were harvested for physiological and biochemical analyses [17].

Plant physiology assays

The electrolyte leakage of fresh leaves was assessed in accordance with the methodology outlined by Osma et al [18]. 0.1 g of fresh plant sample (leaf), rinsed with distilled water, was placed in each of the 6 test tubes. Then, 4 mL of distilled water was added to these tubes, which were incubated at 4 °C for 24 h. The amount of ions released into the distilled water was measured using an electrical conductivity meter to assess cell damage, as electrolyte leakage correlates with cellular damage. 0.5 g of plant sample was weighed and placed in a porcelain mortar. Subsequently, 5 mL of pre-chilled homogenization buffer (0.1 M KH2PO4, 1 mM EDTA, 1% PVP, pH 7.0) was added to the sample, and the suspension was transferred into a centrifuge tube. The samples were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was carefully collected and utilized for the assessment of antioxidant enzyme activities [17].

The determination of peroxidase (POX) enzyme activity

Peroxidase activity was measured by monitoring the increase in absorbance at 470 nm, resulting from the reaction between guaiacol and H2O2 [19]. For activity measurement, 3 mL of substrate solution (0.1 M NaH2PO4, pH 5.5, and 5 mM guaiacol) was placed in a spectrophotometer cuvette. Then, 2.5 µL of enzyme extract was added. Absorbance increase at 470 nm was recorded every minute for 5 min. The increase in absorbance per minute was calculated during the linear part of the reaction. One unit of enzyme activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to cause an increase in absorbance of 0.01 per minute at 25 °C. Enzyme activities were expressed as enzyme units per gram of tissue (EU g− 1 FW) [17].

The determination of catalase (CAT) enzyme activity

Catalase activity was determined using the method described by Havir and Mchale [20], which involves monitoring the decrease in absorbance at 240 nm. For activity measurement, a mixture of 103.5 mM KH2PO4 buffer and 40 mM H2O2 substrate solution was prepared. Then, 3 mL of this mixture was placed in a quartz cuvette, and 20 µL of enzyme extract was added. Absorbance at 240 nm was monitored at one-minute intervals over a three-minute period. The rate of decrease in absorbance per minute was determined from the linear phase of the reaction. One unit of enzyme activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to reduce the absorbance by 1 µmol per minute at 25 °C. Enzyme activity was expressed as enzyme units per gram of tissue (EU g− 1 FW) [17].

The determination of superoxide dismutase (SOD) enzyme activity

SOD activity was determined by measuring the inhibition of nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) photochemical reduction spectrophotometrically [21]. The reaction mixture (3 mL) contained 50 mM KH2PO4 (pH 7.8), 13 mM methionine, 75 µM NBT, 2 µM riboflavin, and 0.1 mM EDTA. For the enzyme activity assay, 2.84 mL of the reaction mixture excluding riboflavin was transferred into a spectrophotometer cuvette, followed by the addition of 100 µL of the enzyme extract. The reaction was initiated by introducing 60 µL of 100 µM riboflavin solution, and the cuvette was immediately exposed to a white light source for 15 min to commence photochemical activation. The reaction was stopped by turning off the light, and the absorbance decrease at 560 nm was measured against a blank (without enzyme). A single unit of SOD activity was defined as the enzyme quantity required to achieve 50% inhibition of NBT reduction, as measured by absorbance at 560 nm., and results were expressed as enzyme units per gram of tissue (EU g− 1 FW) [17].

Determination of the amount of chlorophyll

The concentrations of chlorophylls and carotenoids in fresh leaf tissues were determined following the protocol described by Lichtenthaler et al [22]. Briefly, 0.5 g of fresh leaf material was homogenized using a mortar and pestle in 5 mL of 80% acetone. The extract was then subjected to spectrophotometric analysis, with absorbance readings recorded at 662 nm and 645 nm for chlorophylls, and at 470 nm for carotenoids. Pigment contents were calculated and expressed as milligrams per gram of fresh weight (mg/g FW) [23].

Ferric-Reducing power activity (FRAP)

The reducing power activity was assessed using a modified version of the method described by Oyaizu [24]. Stock solutions of the extracts and standards were prepared at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. For the assay, 100 µL of the stock solution was transferred into test tubes, and the volume was adjusted to 1.25 mL using phosphate buffer (0.2 M, pH 6.6). Then, 1.25 mL of 1% potassium ferricyanide [K3Fe(CN)6] was added, and the mixture was incubated at 50 °C for 20 min. After incubation, 1.25 mL of 10% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and 0.25 mL of 0.1% FeCl3 solution were added sequentially. The final mixture’s absorbance was recorded at 700 nm. Results were expressed as mg TE/g extract using a calibration curve created with different concentrations of Trolox [25, 26].

Total phenolic content

The total phenolic content was quantified using the Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, as described in standard analytical protocols. For the assay, 100 µL of extract was mixed with 1000 µL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and 800 µL of 7.5% Na2CO3 solution. After 2 h of incubation at room temperature in darkness, absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a spectrophotometer (Unico, S1205). Total phenolic content was quantified using gallic acid equivalent (GAE) as a calibration standard and expressed as mg of GAE substance/g extract (mg GAE/g extract) [25, 26].

Total flavonoid content

The total flavonoid content was quantified according to the method described by Chang et al. [27], following established analytical protocols. Stock solutions of extracts and standards were prepared in methanol (100 µl), transferred to test tubes, and brought to a final volume of 4.8 ml with methanol. For the assay, 1 mL of extract was mixed with 1 mL of 2% AlCl3 solution and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 1 h. Then, 100 µL of 1 M NH4CH3COO solution and 100 µL of 10% AlCl3 solution were added, and the mixture was vortexed and incubated at room temperature for 45 min. Absorbance was measured at 415 nm using a spectrophotometer. Total flavonoid content was determined by constructing a calibration curve from known concentrations of quercetin equivalent (QE), and results were expressed as mg of QE/g extract (mg QE/g extract) [25, 26].

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from the study were analyzed statistically, with significance defined as p ≤ 0.05. Statistical evaluations were performed using the SPSS 22 software, applying the ANOVA test within a 95% confidence interval. Multiple comparisons between examples application were assessed using the Student-Newman-Keuls (S-N-K) and Tukey’s B tests to identify significant differences (The number of repetitions for each test is indicated n = 3.)

Results

In this study, the effects of mixtures of active ingredients of medicinal drugs (gemfibrozil, acetaminophen, ciproflaxine) and artificial sweeteners (aspartame and sucrolase) on wheat and barley were investigated. The data obtained from the study revealed significant differences between the samples in which the active ingredients of the drugs and artificial sweeteners were mixed and the control group in all parameters studied.

In the measurements made on electrolyte leakage (EC) concentration, the control samples exhibited values of 40.56 ± 5.1 µS cm− 1 in barley and 101.5 ± 7.93 µS cm− 1 in wheat. In wheat samples treated with aspartame and gemfibrozil, the highest EC concentration value was recorded as 180 ± 10.14 µS cm− 1. In the control samples mixed with sucralose, the EC concentration value was 80.50 ± 5.75 µS cm− 1 in barley and 292 ± 14.59 µS cm− 1 in wheat. In wheat samples mixed with Sucrolase and Ciproflaxine, the EC concentration was 795.71 ± 50.14 µS cm− 1. Statistical evaluations demonstrated that there were significant differences between the control group in wheat and barley and the samples in which drug active ingredients and artificial flavors were mixed (Fig. 1).

Significant differences among treatments were determined using the Student-Newman-Keuls (S–N–K) test and are indicated by different letters (a, b, c) above the bars (p < 0.05)

When catalase (CAT) enzyme activity was analyzed, significant differences were observed between the control samples and the samples in which the active ingredients and artificial sweeteners were mixed. The catalase enzyme activity in barley samples treated with aspartame and pharmaceutical active ingredients was between 2062 ± 146.5 and 4065 ± 160.13 EU g− 1. Similarly, catalase enzyme activity ranged from 3434.38 ± 170.87 to 5797.5 ± 115.14 EU g− 1 in the samples treated with sucralose. It was also observed that catalase enzyme activity was highest in the samples grown with sucralose and ciproflaxine mixture (Fig. 2).

Upon analysis of the studied parameters, statistically significant differences were observed in both wheat and barley samples. However, the peroxidase (POX) enzyme activity was found to be significantly higher in barley and wheat samples grown with the mixture of aspartame and gemfibrozil compared to the control group (Fig. 3).

The effects of PPCPs and ASs on plants have been extensively studied in relation to chlorophyll contents. In these studies, differences in chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, chlorophyll a + b and carotenoid contents were determined in plant samples. In particular, a general decrease in chlorophyll and carotenoid amounts was observed in plants exposed to micro fouling. Conversely, it was observed that the decrease in chlorophyll content was more pronounced when plants were treated with pharmaceutical active ingredients such as sucralose (approximately 23% decrease with the sucralose + Gemfibrozil combination). These findings suggest that plant interactions with these chemicals lead to changes in the products of photosynthesis (Fig. 5).

Furthermore, the phenolic and flavonoid contents and FRAP (ferric reducing antioxidant power) amounts were examined to evaluate the effects of micro pollutants on plants. This comprehensive study shows that the phenolic and flavonoid contents of wheat and barley samples decreased in both treatments compared to the control group. Additionally, a similar reduction in FRAP was also observed (Compared to the control group, the greatest decreases were observed in barley: approximately 61.87% in TPC, 67.43% in TFC, and 71% in FRAP). These findings indicate that micro pollutants can have a negative impact on the antioxidant capacity of plants and cause significant changes in plant metabolism (Fig. 6).

Discussion

In this study, exposure of wheat and barley plants to pharmaceutical and artificial sweetener mixtures resulted in significant alterations in physiological and biochemical parameters. Notably, electrolyte leakage and activities of antioxidant enzymes were markedly increased whereas chlorophyll content, total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), and FRAP values were significantly reduced compared to the control group. The most pronounced increases were observed in electrolyte leakage and POX activity (exceeding 100%), SOD activity (approximately 28%), and CAT activity (approximately 86%). In contrast, the most substantial decreases were recorded in TPC (approximately 61%), TFC (approximately 67%), FRAP values (approximately 71%), and chlorophyll content (approximately 23%).These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that pharmaceuticals and artificial sweeteners induce oxidative stress in plants and disrupt photosynthetic pigments. For instance, wheat seedlings grown in contaminated soil with saccharin (SAC) and aspartame (ASP) at concentrations of 0, 25, 50, 100 mg exhibited increased electrolyte leakage, enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, and alterations in chlorophyll and carotenoid content [15].Similarly, Ravichandran et al. [28, 29] reported that exposure of various plant species to different PPCPs led to increased SOD and CAT activities, and that diclofenac (DCF) treatment induced significant oxidative stress through the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Liu et al [30]. and Karki et al. [31] observed that plants treated with tetracycline (500 mg) and other pharmaceuticals (1 mg/L) showed increased SOD, POD, and CAT activities, accompanied by a reduction in chlorophyll content. Kim et al. [32] reported that exposure to bisphenol A (BPA) at concentrations of 0, 1, 5, 7, 12, 17.2, 50 mg L-1 caused substantial damage to root tip cells and inhibited growth in soybean plants. In alfalfa, exposure to a mixture of diclofenac, sulfamethoxazole, trimethoprim, and 17α-ethinylestradiol led to increased CAT and SOD activities in leaves as well as FRAP values in roots, with mixture effects being more pronounced than those of single compounds [33]. Zezulka et al. [34] reported that diclofenac and paracetamol treatments (1, 5, 10 mg/L) caused a 10–60% decrease in photosynthetic pigments and an increase in antioxidant enzyme activities in maize and pea plants. Similarly, cucumber seedlings exposed to 17 different PPCP compounds exhibited increased ROS production and lipid peroxidation, accompanied by elevated activities of enzymes associated with oxidative stress and xenobiotic metabolism [35]. Finally, the artificial sweeteners aspartame and sucralose significantly inhibited the growth of duckweed (Lemna minor L.), although chlorophyll content remained unaffected [36].

Overall, these studies corroborate our findings, indicating that exposure to pharmaceuticals and artificial sweeteners induces oxidative stress in plants, enhances antioxidant enzyme activities, and reduces photosynthetic pigments and phenolic content. The magnitude and nature of the responses depend on the plant species, the specific compounds applied, and whether the compounds administered individually or in combination. Collectively, the results suggest that mixtures of PPCP and AS can disrupt redox homeostasis and photosynthetic efficiency, thereby imposing oxidative stress on plants. A review of the literature revealed that no studies have investigated the combined application of PPCPs and ASs to plants. Existing studies have generally focused on the effects of these micropollutants individually. The results of these studies are particularly important for understanding the impacts of micropollutants on plant physiology. Previous researches have demonstrated that PPCPs and ASs exert comparable effects on plants, including reduced antioxidant enzyme activities, decreased chlorophyll content, and alterations in metabolic processes. Similarly, the application of individual pharmaceutical active ingredients produces analogous outcomes. These findings suggest that plants responses to environmental pollution and chemical stress are often mediated by similar physiological and biochemical mechanisms. The observed reduction in chlorophyll and antioxidant activity at higher mixture concentrations indicates that the plants were subjected to elevated oxidative stress. Under such stress conditions, the accumulation of Reactive oxygen species (ROS) can cause oxidative damage to cellular structures, particularly affecting photosynthetic pigments. Superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and peroxidase (POX) constitute the primary enzymatic defense system that scavenges ROS and prevents oxidative damage [37, 38]. Meanwhile, non-enzymatic antioxidants including phenolic compounds and flavonoids serve as secondary defense components that directly neutralize free radicals and contributing to overall antioxidant capacity, as reflected in TPC, TFC, and FRAP values [39, 40]. The observed decrease in total phenolic content in wheat and barley exposed to PPCP and AS mixtures can be attributed to disruptions in the phenylpropanoid biosynthetic pathway. Phenolic compounds are primarily synthesized via phenylalanine- and tyrosine-derived pathways, catalyzed by important enzymes such as phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL) and tyrosine ammonia lyase (TAL) [41, 42]. Several studies have demonstrated that environmental pollutants can downregulate PAL and other related enzymes, resulting in reduced accumulation of phenolic compounds and flavonoids [42–44]. Such inhibition may occur through direct interference of xenobiotic compounds with enzyme expression or activity, thereby inducing oxidative stress. Consequently, the decrease in phenolic levels observed in this study likely reflects a metabolic shift in which plants redirect resources toward primary metabolism or stress defense, leading to decreased biosynthesis of secondary metabolites [45]. Interactions among pollutants in ecosystems may further exacerbate environmental stress and reduce ecosystem resilience, thereby impairing the ability to maintain natural balance, decreasing biodiversity and degrading ecosystem services. Consequently, the presence and interactions of pollutants in ecosystems represent a critical concern for environmental protection and sustainability policies. Research in this area essential for developing effective strategies to preserve and enhance ecosystem health.

Conclusion

In conclusion, exposure to pharmaceuticals and personal care products (PPCPs) and artificial sweeteners (ASs) induced oxidative stress in wheat and barley, as evidenced by significant increases in key antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, and POD) and alterations in physiological parameters. These findings highlight the potential risks posed by emerging contaminants to crop health and their possible transfer through the food chain, raising important concerns for agricultural productivity and food safety. The study emphasizes the need for further research on the combined effects of multiple contaminants under realistic environmental conditions, including long-term field studies and mechanistic investigations at the molecular level. Such integrated approaches are essential for understanding plant responses to chemical stress and for developing sustainable, environmentally responsible agricultural practices.
