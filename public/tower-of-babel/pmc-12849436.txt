Genome-wide identification and expression pattern analysis under drought stress of PHD family genes in alfalfa (Medicago sativa)
Authors: Bei Wu, Shangli Shi, Wenjuan Kang, Yilin Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlfalfa (Medicago sativa) is a perennial high-quality legume forage widely cultivated worldwide, but drought stress severely restrict its growth and development. Plant homeodomain finger (PHD) family genes are a type of zinc finger transcription factors widely distributed in eukaryotes. They act as histone code identifier to regulate the expression of downstream genes and play important biological roles in plant growth, development, and stress response. However, information about the PHD family genes in alfalfa remains limited at present.ResultsWe identified 56 MsPHD genes containing 67 PHD domains in the alfalfa, and these genes were unevenly distributed on 8 chromosomes of alfalfa. There are 9 pairs of gene fragment duplicates in the MsPHD family. The 56 MsPHD genes are evolutionarily divided into 14 subgroups. There are differences in the conservative motifs and gene structure composition patterns of MsPHD among different subgroups. In addition, we identified 11 MsPHD genes related to drought stress, and their promoter regions all contained elements related to stress response. Quantitative Real-time PCR (QRT-PCR) further verified that the expression levels of these 11 MsPHD genes were significantly up-regulated in both roots and leaves, with MsPHD9 showing the highest upregulation. 57.14% of MsPHD proteins were predicted to be located in the nucleus, and the co-localization analysis of MsPHD9 protein confirmed that it was primarily located in the nucleus.ConclusionsThe identification of alfalfa PHD family genes not only provides important genetic resources for in-depth analysis of the molecular mechanism of alfalfa drought resistance, but also lays a foundation for creating new germplasm of alfalfa with strong drought resistance through genetic engineering technology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12326-x.

Introduction

Transcription factors (TFs) are a class of DNA-binding proteins that can bind to cis-acting elements of target genes, and play an important role in plant growth and development, morphogenesis and stress response by activating or inhibiting gene transcription [1]. Zinc finger proteins are a type of transcriptional regulators widely distributed in eukaryotes, rich in cysteine and histidine. They are characterized by the cysteine and histidine coordinate with one or more zinc ions alone or together to form stable polypeptide structure with finger domains [2–4]. According to the arrangement characteristics of amino acids binding zinc ions, zinc finger proteins were divided into RING (Really Interesting New Genes), LIM (Lin11, Isl-1 and Mec-3) and PHD (Plant Homeodomain Finger) [5, 6]. The PHD TFs contain the typical plant homeodomain finger domain, which is composed of 50–80 amino acids and characterized by the C4HC3 (Cys4-His-Cys3) zinc-binding motif. Within this zinc finger domain, the conserved Cys and His residues coordinate two zinc ions at distinct binding sites to form a structurally stable PHD domain [7–9]. The main function of the PHD domain is to specifically bind to various histone modifications (methylation, acetylation, etc.) as a histone code recognizer to regulate the expression of downstream genes [10]. Typical PHD proteins usually contain one or more PHD domains [11]. Since the PHD domain was first identified in Zea mays homologous protein Zmhoxla and Arabidopsis thaliana transcription factor AtHAT3.1 in 1993, an increasing number of PHD proteins have been characterized across diverse plant species [12]. For example, there are 59, 95, 73, 45, 67 and 72 PHD genes in Oryza sativa [13], Glycine max [14], Populus trichocarpa [15], Arabidopsis thaliana [16], Zea mays and Solanum tuberosum respectively [2, 17]. The plant PHD family has poor evolutionary conservation, and there are great differences in protein sequence and structural characteristics. The rich and diverse domains in PHD proteins may be the determining factor leading to their functional diversity [13, 15, 16, 18].

PHD transcription factors primarily regulate gene expression through histone tail modifications. Proteins containing PHD domain play a modification effect of activating (H3K4me) or inhibiting (H3K9me) transcription by interacting with downstream effector proteins, causing chromatin structure remodeling, thereby regulating the transcriptional expression of genes [19]. These proteins play pivotal regulatory roles in diverse physiological processes, including seed germination, pollen development, vernalization, and stress response [20, 21]. PHD protein can regulate Arabidopsis thaliana seed dormancy [22]. Low temperature stress can induce the expression of VIN3, the key PHD protein upstream of the vernalization pathway in Arabidopsis thaliana, and reduce the expression of the flowering core PHD protein FLC [17, 23]. Similarly, in Triticum aestivum, the PHD protein VIL regulates the vernalization process [24]. The expression of GmPHD gene in Glycine max was induced by drought, low temperature, salt and ABA, and GmPHD2 improved the tolerance of transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana to salt stress [25]. Overexpression of MtPHD6 in Medicago truncatula enhanced the drought tolerance of Arabidopsis thaliana [26]. The overexpression of OsPHD1 enhanced the tolerance of transgenic Oryza sativa to salt, drought and low temperature [27]. It has been found that the expression levels of 4 MtPHDs were up-regulated under low temperature induction [28].

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa) is a perennial legume with rich protein content, high yield, strong resistance and high economic benefits [29, 30]. Its stems are used as biofuels, leaves are high-quality animal feed, and roots are used for soil remediation [31, 32]. These characteristics make alfalfa plays an important role in agricultural production and forage industry construction [33]. However, the drought stress severely limits the growth and development of alfalfa, resulting in a decline in alfalfa production levels. Clarifying the molecular mechanism of alfalfa’s response to drought stress is crucial for cultivating drought-resistant alfalfa germplasm. Zinc-finger proteins play crucial roles in alfalfa stress resistance. In recent years, the genome-wide identified and functionally analyzed of PHD gene families have been reported in many kinds of plants (such as: Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa, Glycine max). However, the PHD family genes in alfalfa have not been identified, and their function in response to drought stress are still unclear. Here, we systematically identified PHD family genes of alfalfa at the genome-wide level, characterizing their phylogenetic relationships, gene structures, conserved domains, and drought-responsive expression patterns. These findings provide important genetic resources for elucidating the molecular mechanisms of drought tolerance in alfalfa and developing novel drought-resistant germplasm.

Materials and methods

Identification and chromosome localization of the PHD family genes in alfalfa

The whole genome data of alfalfa (Zhongmu No. 1) were derived from the online website MODMS (https://modms.lzu.edu.cn/) [34, 35]. In order to identify the members of alfalfa PHD gene family at the genome-wide level, we used the protein sequences of PHD family genes in Arabidopsis thaliana, Medicago truncatula and Oryza sativa as reference sequences [13, 28]. Alfalfa PHD gene and protein sequences were extracted by BLAST comparison with alfalfa genome data, and the e-value value was set to 0.00001. Use the CD-Hit to remove redundant sequences (https://www.bioinformatics.org/cd-hit/index.php) [36]. Pfam (http://pfam-legacy.xfam.org/) and NCBI CD-Search online tools (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) were used to identify the conserved domains of MsPHD gene family sequences. The sequences without complete PHD domain were eliminated, and the proteins containing the complete PHD domain were finally identified as members of the alfalfa PHD family [37]. The chromosomal location information of MsPHD family genes were obtained from the alfalfa genome GFF3 file [38], and the genes were named according to the distribution of the genes on the chromosomes, and visualized by TBtools-II [39].

Multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis

MEGA 11 software was used to perform multiple sequence alignment of alfalfa PHD protein sequences, and the alignment results were imported into ESPript 3.0 for visualization [40]. Using MEGA 11 software, the phylogenetic analysis of PHD protein sequences in Medicago truncatula and Medicago sativa was conducted by the Neighbor-joining method (NJ), and a phylogenetic tree was constructed. The bootstrap was set to 1000 [41].

Physicochemical properties analysis of MsPHDs

The physicochemical properties such as molecular weight (Mw), isoelectric point (pI), and hydrophilicity (GRAVY) of the MsPHD proteins were analyzed using the ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) [42]. The subcellular localization of MsPHD proteins were predicted by Plant-mPLoc (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/plant-multi/) and WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/) online tools [43, 44].

Conserved motif and gene structure analysis of MsPHDs genes

The MEME online tool (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) was used to visually analyze the conservative motif of alfalfa MsPHD, and the maximum number of motifs was set to 20 [45]. Gene structure analysis of PHD gene family members in alfalfa was completed by GSDS (http://gsds.gao-lab.org/) online tool [46].

Colinearity and Ka/Ks analysis

Gene duplication and collinearity analysis were performed using MCScanX software [47]. TBtools software was used to analyze the synonymous substitution rate (Ks) and nonsynonymous substitution rate (Ka), and the Ka/Ks of collinearity gene pairs was calculated to evaluate the evolutionary selection of alfalfa PHD family genes.

Analysis of cis-acting elements of MsPHDs genes

The 2000 bp promoter sequence upstream of MsPHDs genes were extracted by TBtools. PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html) was used to predict cis-acting elements in the promoter region of the genes. The prediction results are visualized by R.3.6.3 software [48].

Plant materials and drought stress treatment

Zhongmu No.1 was shown to respond to drought stress [49]. In this study, the alfalfa variety “Zhongmu No. 1” was used as the experimental material, which was provided by the Key Laboratory of Grassland Ecosysem of Ministry of Education in Gansu Agricultural University. The nutrient solution sand culture method was used in this experiment. After high temperature sterilization, the fine sand was put into a nutrient bowl with an aperture of 9 cm and a height of 11 cm, and placed in a hydroponic box with a length × width × height of 25 cm × 15 cm × 10 cm. The seeds were sterilized with 0.1% HgCl2 solution and washed with distilled water. After sowing, they were placed in the light culture room (16 h light/8 h dark, relative humidity about 60%, day and night temperatures 25 ± 1 °C and 20 ± 1 °C, respectively), and 300 mL Hoagland nutrient solution was poured every 2 days. PEG6000 treatment was used to simulate drought stress. When the seedlings grew to the 30th day, the Hoagland nutrient solution containing 15% PEG6000 was used for drought stress treatment. Leaves and roots were collected at 0 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h, respectively, and repeated three times. The samples were quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored in a refrigerator at − 80 °C for further use.

Expression analysis of MsPHDs genes

To study the expression level of MsPHDs under drought stress, the alfalfa transcriptome data were downloaded from NCBI (PRJNA960915) [49]. Sequencing reads were mapped to the reference genome of the Zhongmu No.1 using the HISAT2 program [50]. Use featureCcounts (2.0.3) to calculate the gene alignment, and then calculate the FPKM of each gene based on the gene length [51]. DESeq2 was used to analyze the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between aboveground and underground in alfalfa, and the screening conditions of DEGs were set to |log2Fold Change| ≥ 2 and FDR < 0.05 [52, 53]. The MsPHDs were compared with the drought transcriptome data by BLAST. The obtained data were subjected to cluster analysis and expression heat map drawing using TBtools software, and the data processing was standardized by log10.

Using MsActin as an internal reference gene, primers were designed for the selected 11 MsPHDs genes. The primer sequence is shown in Table S1. Total RNA samples were extracted using the RNAprep pure Plant Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). RNA was reverse transcribed according to the FastKing gDNA Dispelling RT SuperMix (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). A quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was conducted using 2×SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Three independent replicates were performed. The 2−ΔΔCT method was used to quantify gene expression [54].

Subcellular localization of MsPHD9

The vector used in this experiment was pBWA(V)H2STMVΩ-eGFP. Firstly, primers were designed according to the CDS (without stop codon) sequence of MsPHD9. The specific amplification primer sequence was shown in Table S2. The target gene fragment was obtained by PCR amplification using BioRun Pfu PCR Mix (BioRun, Wuhan, China). Subsequently, the target gene fragment was inserted into the BamHI and BsaI restriction sites of the pBWA(V)H2STMVΩ-eGFP vector by the method of Infusion cloning to construct the pBWA(V)H2STMVΩ-MsPHD9-eGFP vector, and further transformed into competent Escherichia coli.

Prior to infiltration, tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) plants were acclimated under white fluorescent lighting for 1 h to ensure complete stomatal opening. The infiltration area was selected between the fully expanded leaves of the third and fourth from last. For subcellular co-localization analysis, the nuclear marker plasmid (mKATE) was transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101 and mixed with the vector plasmid Agrobacterium pBWA(V)H2STMVΩ-MsPHD9-eGFP at a ratio of 1:1 before injection. The Agrobacterium was injected into the infiltration area on the back of the leaves. After 2–3 days of light culture at 25 ℃, the leaves in this area were cut and placed under a confocal laser scanning microscope to observe the fluorescence signals (Leica Microsystem, Heidelberg, Germany). The GFP was excited at 488 nm, and the mKATE was excited at 561 nm. The injection of empty vector pBWA(V)H2STMVΩ-eGFP was used as a control [55].

Results

Identification and chromosome localization of PHD genes in alfalfa

In order to identify the members of alfalfa PHD gene family, the protein sequences of Arabidopsis thaliana, Medicago truncatula and Oryza sativa PHD family members and alfalfa genome data were compared by BLAST. After removing the redundant sequences and identifying the conserved domains, 56 MsPHD proteins containing 67 PHD domains were finally identified. The 56 MsPHD genes were named MsPHD1-MsPHD56 according to their chromosomal distribution position. 56 MsPHD genes were located on 8 chromosomes of the alfalfa genome, with the most MsPHD genes distributed on Chr1 (13) and the least on Chr4 (4) (Fig. 1).

Multiple sequence alignment and physicochemical properties analysis of MsPHDs

To study the sequence characteristics of the MsPHD family genes, multiple sequence alignment was conducted using MsPHDs protein sequences. The results showed that MsPHDs all contained a typical PHD-finger domain, with 50 ~ 80 amino acids, and had obvious Cys4-His-Cys3 protein sequence characteristics. Based on the conservation properties of the 8 metal ligands and the analysis of amino acid residues between adjacent 2 metal ligands, the differences among the PHD, RING, and LIM domains lie in that the number of residues between their fourth and fifth metal ligands is 4 or 5, 2 or 3, 2, respectively. There are more than 3 amino acid residues between the fourth and fifth metal ligands of MsPHD members, which are consistent with the characteristics of PHD family proteins (Fig. 2).

In addition, we predicted the molecular weight, protein length, isoelectric point, hydrophilicity and subcellular localization of 56 MsPHD proteins (Table S3). The length of 56 MsPHD proteins varied from 81 amino acids (MsPHD2) to 2269 amino acids (MsPHD18). The average hydrophilicity of all MsPHD proteins was negative, indicating that these MsPHD proteins had good hydrophilicity. Their protein molecular weights distribution was between 9.075 kDa (MsPHD2) and 255.331 kDa (MsPHD18), and their predicted isoelectric point ranged from 4.16 (MsPHD15) to 9.35 (MsPHD51). Subcellular localization prediction showed that most of these proteins (57.14%) were located in the nucleus, and the remaining 12 (21.43%), 7 (12.50%), 3 (5.36%), 1 (1.79%) and 1 (1.79%) MsPHD proteins were located in chloroplast, cytoplasm, plasma membrane, extracellular and vacuole, respectively.

Phylogenetic analysis of MsPHDs genes

To clarify the evolutionary relationship of MsPHD family genes, we constructed the phylogenetic tree with 56 MsPHDs and 64 MtPHDs protein sequences from Medicago sativa and Medicago truncatula (Fig. S1). Based on the similarity of their amino acid sequences, 56 MsPHDs were divided into 14 subgroups (A ~ N), suggesting that they had a greater degree of differentiation during long-term evolution (Fig. 3). A, B and C subgroups contained 4 MsPHDs. D, F, K and L subgroups contained 2 MsPHDs. G, I, J and N subgroups contained 3 MsPHDs. In addition, subgroups E, H and M contained 11, 5 and 8 MsPHDs, respectively.

Conserved domain, conserved motif and gene structure analysis of MsPHD genes

We analyzed the domains of all MsPHD proteins. 9 (16.07%) MsPHD genes (MsPHD3, MsPHD11, MsPHD16, MsPHD18, MsPHD28, MsPHD36, MsPHD43, MsPHD49, MsPHD53) contained 2 PHD domains. MsPHD31 contained 3 PHD domains. The remaining 46 (82.14%) MsPHD genes had 1 PHD domain. MsPHD genes in D, F, G, H, I, K and L subgroups all contained only 1 PHD domain (Fig. 4).

The MEME online tool was used to predict the functional motifs composition and distribution of the MsPHDs, and the maximum number of conserved motifs was set at 20 (Fig. 5). The results showed that there were differences in motifs between different subgroups. Motif 20, motif 7, motif 8 and motif 4 only exist in A, E, N and M subgroup, respectively. All the MsPHD proteins contain 3 ~ 5 motifs, except for MsPHD35. Motif 1, motif 2 and motif 3 were all present in subgroups A, B, C and D (except for MsPHD 23 and MsPHD 39). These indicate that MsPHD from different subgroups may have functional specificity. In addition, we analyzed the exon-intron characteristics of MsPHDs to further explore their structural differences (Fig. 6). 29 MsPHDs genes did not have the UTR structure. Among them, all MsPHDs genes in subgroups B, K and L did not contain the UTR structure. The number of exons of MsPHDs genes varied greatly from 1 (MsPHD2/25/26/34) to 29 (MsPHD33). There were also significant differences in the exon-intron composition patterns of MsPHD among different subgroups.

Gene duplication and Ka/Ks analysis

In order to clarify the genetic evolution and selection pressure of MsPHDs genes, MCScanX was used to analyze gene duplication events. The results showed that there were no tandem duplication events in the alfalfa genome, but 9 pairs of duplicated fragments were present. The duplication events mainly occurred on Chr1, Chr3 and Chr7 (Fig. 7). These indicated that the fragment duplication events were the main way for the evolution and expansion of the alfalfa PHD gene family. In addition, we calculated the Ka/Ks values of 9 MsPHD gene pairings (Table 1). All the gene pairings had Ka/Ks values less than 1, indicating that the evolution of alfalfa PHD gene family was driven by purification selection. In addition, the collinearity analysis among Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa, Medicago truncatula and Medicago sativa showed that 7 and 44 MsPHD genes had homologous PHD genes in Oryza sativa and Medicago truncatula, respectively, indicating that these homologous genes were highly conserved. In contrast, no homologous PHD genes were detected in Arabidopsis thaliana, supporting a closer phylogenetic relationship between Medicago sativa and Medicago truncatula. (Fig. 8)

Expression analysis of MsPHDs genes under drought stress

To understand whether MsPHDs respond to drought stress, we obtained the expression level of MsPHDs under drought stress based on a publicly available RNA-seq transcriptomic dataset (PRJNA960915), and constructed an expression patterns heat map of 56 MsPHD genes under drought stress (Fig. 9). The results showed that compared with Control, 11 PHD genes (MsPHD1、MsPHD7、MsPHD9、MsPHD10、MsPHD11、MsPHD12、MsPHD28、MsPHD39、MsPHD42、MsPHD50、MsPHD55) in the aboveground leaves and underground roots of alfalfa were significantly upregulated under drought stress.

To further clarify the expression pattern of MsPHDs under drought stress, we performed qRT-PCR verification on these 11 MsPHD genes (Fig. 10). After drought stress, the aboveground and underground of the 11 MsPHDs showed different degrees of upregulated expression trends. In the aboveground leaves, the expression of MsPHD1 and MsPHD39 significantly upregulated at 12 h of drought treatment, while the remaining 9 MsPHD genes all significantly upregulated at 6 h of treatment (P < 0.05) (Fig. 10A). For the underground roots, the expression of MsPHD1, MsPHD7, MsPHD11 and MsPHD12 began to be significantly upregulated at 12 h of treatment, and the MsPHD9, MsPHD10, MsPHD28, MsPHD39, MsPHD42 and MsPHD55 began to be significantly upregulated at 6 h (P < 0.05) (Fig. 10B). It is worth noting that after drought stress, the upregulation of MsPHD9 was the largest, and the expression levels of leaves and roots reached the peak at 12 h and 6 h, respectively, which were significantly upregulated by 18.61 and 11.03 times compared with 0 h (P < 0.05). The results of qRT-PCR were basically consistent with those of transcriptomics, which further confirmed that these genes played an important role in response to drought stress, especially MsPHD9 may be a key candidate gene.

Cis-acting elements analysis of MsPHDs

The cis-acting element of the promoter is located upstream of the gene sequence, which can bind to transcription factors and play an important transcriptional regulatory role in the expression of plant stress responding genes. To further study the functions of MsPHDs, we analyzed the potential functional elements within the 2000 bp upstream promoter region of the MsPHDs genes using PlantCARE (Fig. 11). 4 classes of cis-acting elements totaling 25 have been identified, 8 light responsive elements (Sp1, GT1-motif, G-box, G-Box, MRE, 3-AF1 binding site, 4 cl-CMA2b and ACE), 5 hormone responsive elements (TGACG-motif, CGTCA-motif, TATC-box, P-box and GARE-motif), 6 stress responsive elements (MBS, MBSI, LTR, TC-rich repeats, TCA-element and ABRE), and 6 plant growth and developmental regulatory elements (RY-element, GCN4_motif, CAT-box, MSA-like, TGA-element, AuxRR-core). The promoter region of 11 key MsPHDs genes responding to drought stress all contained cis-acting elements related to abiotic stress, such as MBS (drought stress-related), LTR (low temperature response), TC-rich repeats (defense and stress response). There are 4 MsPHD genes (MsPHD1, MsPHD7, MsPHD11 and MsPHD39) containing MBS elements, 6 MsPHD genes (MsPHD1, MsPHD9, MsPHD10, MsPHD11, MsPHD50 and MsPHD55) having LTR elements, and 6 MsPHD genes (MsPHD7, MsPHD9, MsPHD10, MsPHD11, MsPHD28 and MsPHD39) with TC-rich repeats elements. These elements provide binding sites for transcription factors related to abiotic stress.

Subcellular localization analysis of MsPHD9

Plant-mPLoc and WoLF PSORT predicted that MsPHD9 protein was localized in the nucleus. To verify the subcellular localization of MsPHD9, we used the transient expression fusion vector pBWA(V)H2STMVΩ-MsPHD9-eGFP and the empty vector control pBWA(V)H2STMVΩ-eGFP to infect leaf epidermal cells through tobacco transient transformation (Fig. 12). The results showed that the eGFP green fluorescence signal was evenly distributed in the cell, while the MsPHD9-eGFP green fluorescence signal was mainly detected in the nucleus and could overlap with the red fluorescence signal of the nuclear marker, confirming that the MsPHD9 protein was primarily localized in the nucleus.

Discussion

The PHD gene family is one of the most abundant transcription factors in plants and plays an important regulatory role in plant growth and development, morphogenesis and response to environmental stress [14]. With the successful sequencing of more and more plant genomes, the identification of PHD gene family at the genome-wide level has also been carried out in many species (such as Medicago truncatula, Glycine max, Brassica rapa and Oryza sativa) [13, 14, 28, 56], but studies on the isolation and identification of PHD family genes in alfalfa have not been reported. In this study, the identification and analysis of MsPHD genes were completed by using the whole genome data of alfalfa. We will conduct functional verification and drought resistance 56 MsPHDs were identified in alfalfa, which were less than those in Arabidopsis thaliana (70), Erianthus fulvus (76) and Zea mays (67), larger than those in Brassica rapa (33) and similar to those in Medicago truncatula (64) and Oryza sativa (59) [2, 13, 16, 19, 28, 57]. Different from other relatively conserved gene families, the evolution of plant PHD family members is more diverse [2, 16]. Based on the phylogenetic relationship of the PHD family genes of Medicago truncatula [28], the 56 MsPHDs were classified into 14 different subgroups, among which the E subfamily has the largest number of MsPHDs, while the D, F, K and L subfamilies have the least. The differentiation degree of different branches within each subgroup of MsPHD varies. It is speculated that there is a large degree of differentiation in the evolution process, which may cause functional diversity of the protein [7]. In addition, all MsPHD proteins in the alfalfa PHD family had the characteristics of Cys4-His-Cys3 zinc binding motif, and these proteins were composed of 50 to 80 amino acids, which was consistent with the characteristics of the PHD domain [8, 56, 58]. The protein length, molecular weight and isoelectric point of MsPHDs in each subgroup were different. These studies further illustrate the diversity of plant PHD family members [2]. Most of the predicted subcellular localization results of MsPHDs were nuclear proteins. It is speculated that MsPHDs genes may regulate gene expression in the nucleus, thereby playing roles in transcriptional regulation and signal transduction, which is consistent with the results of subcellular localization reported previously [59–61].

Gene structure and motif analysis provide an important basis for the study of the gene families evolutionary relationship. The PHD family acquires or loses some conserved structures during evolution to produce functional differentiation, and different conserved motifs may have different functions [62]. All MsPHDs contain 3 ~ 5 motifs, except for MsPHD35. There are significant differences in the types and numbers of motifs in different subgroups, and several conserved motifs only exist in specific subgroups. These may be related to the diversity and high differentiation of PHD protein functions, indicating that these motifs may be involved in the functional differentiation of MsPHDs [14, 17]. There are significant differences in the number of UTRs and the pattern of gene structure composition among different subgroups. The differences in exon-intron composition further indicate that there may be evolutionary differences and functional differentiation in the MsPHD family. Gene duplication is an important way to generate new genes and provides a material basis for species evolution. It is considered to be an important means to obtain functional diversity during evolution [19]. Fragment duplication is the main amplification mechanism of PHD gene family [29]. 9, 7 and 8 pairs of fragment duplications were observed in Oryza sativa, Triticum aestivum and Erianthus fulvus PHDs, respectively [13, 19, 63]. We found no tandem duplication in the MsPHD gene family, but identified 9 pairs of fragment duplications. It should be noted that these MsPHDs were unevenly distributed on 8 alfalfa chromosomes. This phenomenon was also found in Zea mays, Populus trichocarpa and Arabidopsis thaliana [2, 15, 64], indicating that chromosome location may be associated with cellular functions [29, 56]. The Ka/Ks of these 9 duplicated gene pairs were less than 1, indicating that the environmental selection pressure during the evolutionary process was negative and these duplicated gene pairs were purified and selected during evolution [19, 65]. In addition, the correlation between Medicago sativa and its closely related species Medicago truncatula indicates that PHD genes may conserved during evolution.

Cis-acting elements play an important role in the stress response by participating in the transcription of downstream genes. They do not encode any proteins, but only provides an action site [66]. The promoter region of alfalfa MsPHD genes contain cis-acting elements involved in stress response, including MBS, MBSI, LTR, TC-rich repeats, TCA-element, ABRE, which further proves that MsPHD genes may respond to abiotic stress. Drought is an important abiotic factor limiting the growth and development of alfalfa. PHD family members are widely involved in biological processes such as stress response. After being stimulated by environmental stress, PHD can bind to a specific region of the downstream target gene promoter, activating or inhibiting gene expression related to stress response [1]. The ectopic expression of MtPHD53 enhanced the drought resistance of Arabidopsis thaliana [26], and the expression of GmPHD1 in Glycine max was up-regulated after drought stress [25]. A total of 18, 9 and 15 PHD genes were up-regulated in Brassica rapa, Poplar and Zea mays under salt stress and drought stress, respectively [2, 15, 56]. MtPHD61 was significantly up-regulated by 80 times under drought stress [28]. TaPHD genes were also involved in the response of wheat to drought stress. TaPHD-D18, TaPHD-D23 and TaPHD B57 were up-regulated after drought treatment [63]. After drought stress treatment on Populus tomentosa, the PHD genes was largely induced [2]. To identify the MsPHD genes in alfalfa that respond to drought stress, we selected 11 MsPHD genes with an up-regulated expression trend under drought stress from the transcriptome data for qRT-PCR verification. The verification results were consistent with the transcriptome data. The expression levels of these 11 genes in roots and leaves were significantly up-regulated after drought treatment, and their promoter regions all had stress response elements, such as MBS (drought stress-related) and TC-rich repeats (defense and stress response), indicating that these genes may be related to the response of alfalfa to drought stress. In the future, we will compare the response differences of MsPHD family genes between drought-resistant and drought-sensitive alfalfa varieties, and further clarify the mechanism of their response to drought stress. It is worth noting that the up-regulation multiple of MsPHD9 after drought treatment was higher than that of other MsPHDs, indicating that MsPHD9 may play a greater role in the response of alfalfa to drought stress. Subsequently, we performed co-localization analysis of MsPHD9. MsPHD9 was primarily localized in the nucleus, which was consistent with the prediction of subcellular localization. In the future, we will also carry out genetic transformation of MsPHD9 to further verify its biological function.

Conclusion

A total of 56 MsPHDs containing 67 PHD domains were identified in the alfalfa genome, and these genes were phylogenetically divided into 14 subgroups. We systematically analyzed the physicochemical properties, conserved motifs, gene structures, duplication events, subcellular localizations, and expression patterns of MsPHD family genes. The MsPHDs were unevenly distributed on the chromosome, and there were 9 pairs of gene fragment duplications. All MsPHDs contained 3 ~ 5 motifs, except for MsPHD35. We screened 11 MsPHD genes as potential drought-resistant genes. QRT-PCR further verified that the expression levels of these genes significantly up-regulated after drought stress, with MsPHD9 showing the largest up-regulation fold. The promoter regions of these 11 MsPHDs all contain cis-acting elements related to stress response. The co-localization of MsPHD9 indicated that the protein was primarily localized in the nucleus. In the future, we will conduct functional verification and drought resistance evaluation on MsPHD9 through genetic transformation of alfalfa. The research results can provide gene resources for further analysis of the drought resistance molecular mechanism in alfalfa and for the creation of new germplasm with strong drought resistance.
