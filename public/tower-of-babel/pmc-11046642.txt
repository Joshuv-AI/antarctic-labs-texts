A retrospective, observational study analysing effectiveness of sotrovimab in preventing hospitalisation and deaths from COVID-19 and which subgroup of high-risk patients are more likely to benefit due to the absence of COVID-19 spike antibody post vaccination
Authors: Zainab Bello, Nirmal Jayakrishnan, Joanne Watson
Published: 2023

Introduction

COVID-19 has caused millions of hospitalisations and deaths globally. With vaccination and effective treatments for severe COVID infections, morbidity and mortality have substantially reduced. In December 2021, after early treatments to avoid hospitalisation were introduced in a UK-wide programme through COVID Medicines Delivery Units (CMDUs) for those at highest risk of immunosuppression (and therefore vaccine failure), rates of hospitalisation and deaths resulting from infection are now down (hospitalisation rates for COVID19 were previously ∼6.5% of all cases.1 Sotrovimab (Xevudy) is a monoclonal antibody against the COVID-19 spike protein that was licenced for use in this CMDU service.

Initially, sotrovimab was a first-line treatment. It has subsequently been downgraded due to identified resistance in COVID sub-variants.2 There is little 'real-life’ evidence on the outcomes following sotrovimab. This study presents our experience with sotrovimab in the Torbay and South Devon CMDU. Our primary outcome was whether sotrovimab prevented hospitalisation and deaths related to COVID-19. As our secondary outcome, we examined the relationship between spike protein antibody levels prior to treatment and the mean number of high-risk comorbidities3 per patient.

Methods

This retrospective observational study comprised of patients treated for COVID-19 in our CMDU. Patients with high-risk comorbidities (such as solid organ transplant recipients, those with haematological diseases etc) with confirmed COVID-19 (and were therefore eligible for early COVID treatment) were identified through the NHS Digital database, primary care or specialist services. Our study examined whether, following treatment with sotrovimab, these patients were admitted (within 14 days of their diagnosis) or died (within 28 days post-treatment). Detailed information about comorbidities was gained via electronic NHS GP or clinic records. Data was analysed using Microsoft Excel.

Results

Over an 11-month period, 111 patients were identified. No patients were hospitalised within 14 days or died within 28 days of COVID-19 infection. As only patients meeting the initial high-risk criteria with recorded pre-treatment COVID spike antibody level results who were started on sotrovimab infusions were included in our analysis, 17 high-risk patients were excluded. Of the remaining 94, 24.4% (23) had low (<250 u/ml) antibody levels prior to treatment administration. 73% of these had an immune-mediated inflammatory disorder (IMID) as one of their high-risk eligibility criteria. Using a t-test (α = 0.05), the observed difference between the lower (<250u/ml) and higher (> = 250u/ml) antibody level group means suggests that the average number of high-risk comorbidities between <250u/ml and > = 250u/ml antibodies differs statistically significantly (p<0.05), with the low level group having a greater mean number of high-risk comorbidities per patient.

Discussion and conclusion

Sotrovimab seems to be effective in preventing hospital admissions and deaths related to COVID-19 in high-risk patients. Patients with lower levels of COVID-19 antibodies seem to be associated with higher levels of high-risk comorbidities and therefore may be more likely to benefit from antibody infusion.
