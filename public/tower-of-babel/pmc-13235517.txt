Microbial Monotherapy With Leuconostoc sp LB-P8 Improves Inflammation and Fibrosis in Mouse Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis by Inhibiting Transforming Growth Factor β1/SMAD Signaling
Authors: Kihyoun Park, Joon Yong Kim, Seong Woon Roh, Bansi P. Savaliya, Carys A. Turner, Nicholas E. Pirius, Nicholas F. LaRusso, Kyoungsub Song, Steven P. O’Hara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsPrimary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) is a prototypical disease with an impaired gut-liver axis, frequently associated with inflammatory bowel disease, and linked to microbial dysbiosis. However, how microbes influence PSC progression remains unclear. We identified a novel L sp. (LB-P8) with potential anti-fibrotic properties via inhibition of transforming growth factor beta-1 (TGF-β)/SMAD signaling and investigated its therapeutic efficacy and mechanisms in mouse PSC models.MethodsHuman intestinal cells were cultured with or without LB-P8 to assess metabolite profiles (untargeted ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry) and gene expression in co-cultured myofibroblasts and macrophages (reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction). LB-P8 was orally gavaged following disease onset in three cholestatic models: bile duct ligation (BDL), 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine (DDC) feeding, and the Mdr2−/− mouse. Hepatic injury, inflammation, and fibrosis were assessed by serum alanine aminotransferase and alkaline phosphatase, histology, immunofluorescence, Picrosirius red staining, and reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction. Nanostring GeoMX spatial transcriptomic analysis was performed on DDC-fed liver. LB-P8 was also tested in dextran sodium sulfate (DSS)-induced colitis.ResultsMetabolomic profiling of LB-P8 cultures revealed increased detection of 10 metabolites involved in anti-fibrosis and anti-inflammation. RNA sequencing analysis of hepatic stellate cells showed downregulation of TGF-β, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT,) and integrin signaling pathways. LB-P8 reduced liver fibrosis in BDL, DDC-fed, and Mdr2−/− mouse models as evident by ∼50% lower Picrosirius red staining and decreased expression of Col1a1 and Timp1. In Mdr2−/− and DDC-fed mice, LB-P8 reduced periportal macrophage number (F4/80; ∼30%). GeoMX spatial transcriptomics revealed reduced TGF-β1 in cholangiocyte and myofibroblast regions. Finally, LB-P8 suppressed expression of colonic markers of inflammation and fibrosis in the DSS model of bowel injury.ConclusionsLB-P8 ameliorates cholestatic liver disease progression by reducing TGF-β-mediated fibroblast activation, and periportal accumulation of macrophages. Targeting the gut-liver axis with LB-P8 may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for PSC.

Summary

Oral administration of Leuconostoc sp LB-P8 alleviates biliary fibrosis and inflammation in mouse models of cholestatic liver disease by suppressing hepatic transforming growth factor beta-1/SMAD signaling through microbiota-derived metabolites that modulate fibroblast and macrophage activation.

What You Need to Know

Background

Gut dysbiosis is linked to fibro-inflammatory liver diseases, but the therapeutic potential and underlying mechanisms of individual microbial species remain largely unexplored.

Impact

This study identified Leuconostoc citreum LB-P8 as an effective microbial monotherapy that alleviates hepatic fibrosis and inflammation across cholestatic liver disease mouse models by inhibiting transforming growth factor beta-1/SMAD signaling, revealing a novel microbiota-mediated therapeutic mechanism.

Future Directions

Elucidating the bioactive metabolites and host receptor signaling underlying LB-P8’s anti-fibrotic effects, together with its ongoing phase II clinical evaluation in primary sclerosing cholangitis, will advance the development of microbiome-based therapeutics for primary sclerosing cholangitis and related liver disorders.

Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) is a progressive, fibro-inflammatory cholangiopathy of unknown etiology.1 PSC can lead to cirrhosis and predisposes to hepatobiliary and colorectal cancer.2,3 There are currently no approved drugs specifically for the treatment of PSC, and liver transplantation, an invasive and costly procedure, remains the only life-prolonging therapy for patients with PSC.4 Although some medications such as ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) may be used to manage symptoms, there is an unmet need for effective therapies that target the underlying disease process.

Dysregulation of epigenetic, immunologic, and microbial factors along the gut-liver axis is also thought to play a significant role in PSC pathogenesis.5 Indeed, there is growing evidence that both preclinical and clinical studies show an association between gut microbiota and the pathogenesis of PSC. For instance, germ-free or antibiotic treated mice were protected from developing PSC-like pathogenesis including liver inflammation and fibrosis in a mouse model of PSC,6 and antibiotics such as vancomycin have shown promise in small clinical studies.7, 8, 9 Furthermore, PSC is closely associated with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) in approximately 80% of cases.10,11 Patients with PSC, with or without IBD, exhibit enteric microbial dysbiosis distinct from that observed in IBD alone.12, 13, 14 Together, these findings support that PSC is associated with microbiota dysbiosis and highlight microbiota-based therapeutics as an emerging strategy for PSC treatment.

A growing body of evidence supports the health benefits of consuming fermented foods, in part due to the presence of beneficial micro-organisms, or probiotics. These health benefits include increased gut microbial diversity and decreased inflammation.15 Leuconostoc citreum, a Gram-positive, non-motile, non-spore-forming cocci in the family Lactobacillaceae is a symbiont used in the fermentation process. Indeed, L citreum has been isolated from a variety of plant based fermented foods and beverages including kimchi,16 sourdough,17 and kefir.18 Here, we investigate the therapeutic potential of a naturally sourced L citreum strain, LB-P8, and demonstrate its efficacy and underlying mechanisms in mouse models of PSC.

Results

LB-P8 Diminishes Myofibroblast Fibrogenic Gene Expression in a Co-Culture System

To assess the anti-fibrotic effect of LB-P8, we established an in vitro system mimicking the gut-liver axis. Here, LX-2 human hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) were placed in co-culture with HT-29 human intestinal epithelial cells using a Transwell culture system19 (Figure 1A); a common in vitro co-culture system used to study interactions between cell types, including between intestinal and liver cells.20,21 Following the establishment of this co-culture, HT-29 cells in the upper chambers were treated with control microbial Leuconostoc strains or LB-P8, while LX-2 cells in the lower chambers were simultaneously stimulated with transforming growth factor beta-1 (TGF-β). LX-2 controls (ie, no TGF-β, vehicle-treated) showed normal star-shaped stellate morphology, whereas LX-2 cells stimulated with TGF-β for 24 hours showed myofibroblast-like morphology. Notably, LB-P8 exhibited the most significant prevention of the transition to the myofibroblast morphology in response to TGF-β as compared with KCTC3528 (Leuconostoc lactis) and LBMB320195 (L citreum) (Figure 1B). These results support that secreted factors from LB-P8 treated HT-29 cells inhibit stellate cell activation upon treatment with TGF-β. We next performed quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) to compare the effects of LB-P8 and LBMB320195 on profibrogenic and proinflammatory gene expression. Evaluations were conducted in TGF-β-stimulated LX-2 cells and LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 mouse macrophage cell lines in the presence or absence of each L citreum strain at a concentration of 1 × 108 colony-forming units (CFU). TGF-β-induced expression of LX-2 fibrosis-related genes collagen type I alpha 1 (COL1A1), Fibronectin-1 (FN1), and TIMP Metallopeptidase Inhibitor 1 (TIMP1) which were decreased in the presence of LB-P8 and showed a significant (COL1A1 and TIMP1) or trending (FN1) reduction in expression compared with control LBMB320195 (Figure 1C). Similarly, in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells, LB-P8 treated cells exhibited a significant reduction in proinflammatory cytokine markers, including tumor necrosis factor alpha (Tnf-α), interleukin 1 beta (Il-1β) and interleukin 6 (Il-6), compared with the LBMB320195-treated group (Figure 1D). Notably, despite both strains belonging to the same species, only LB-P8 consistently exerted potent anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory activities. These results demonstrate that the anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory effects are strain-specific to LB-P8 rather than a general characteristic of the L citreum species. We further assessed, by Western blotting, SMAD2/3 expression and phosphorylation levels (a known downstream mediator of TGF-β signaling).22,23 We found that the level of phosphorylated SMAD2/3 in LX-2 cells was increased at 30, 60, and 120 minutes following TGF-β treatment. Althouogh conditioned media from HT-29 cells co-cultured with 1 × 109 CFU of LB-P8 modestly attenuated TGF- β-induced phosphorylated SMAD2/3 levels at 30 and 60 min post-TGF-β treatment, this conditioned media significantly reduced phospho-SMAD2/3 at 120 minutes (Figure 1E). These findings indicate that LB-P8 suppresses the TGF-β-induced canonical signaling pathway, with the most robust inhibitory effect observed at 120 minutes. To gain insights into potential regulators of fibrosis, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of LX-2 cells using this in vitro model. By RNA-seq, we confirmed that several genes related to the TGF-β and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathways, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), integrin signaling, mitochondria-related, fibrosis, and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) were decreased following LB-P8 treatment (Figure 1F). Metabolites From LB-P8 Conditioned Media Suppress Myofibroblast Fibrogenic and Macrophage Inflammatory Gene Expression

Having demonstrated anti-fibrotic potential of LB-P8, we next assessed whether metabolites derived from LB-P8 cultured with intestinal epithelial cells contribute to these effects. Recent studies have shown that intestinal microbial metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), amino acids, and bile acids, are involved in the process of hepatic fibrosis and inflammation.23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29 To identify relevant metabolites, conditioned media were collected from HT-29 cells treated with LB-P8 or a control strain (KCTC3528, L lactis) and subjected to untargeted metabolomic profiles. Using a fold-change cutoff of ≥2 and t-test threshold of P < .05, we identified 74 metabolites that were significantly altered across the groups. Heatmap analysis revealed distinct metabolic signatures in LB-P8-treated samples compared with both the HT-29 control and control strain groups (Figure 2A). Specifically, LB-P8 induced a more robust metabolic shift, characterized by both the enrichment and depletion of specific metabolites, whereas changes in the control strain-treated group were relatively minor. A volcano plot (Figure 2B) highlights metabolites significantly enriched in LB-P8-treated samples relative to control strain. To assess functional relevance, we examined whether selected metabolites reduced fibrotic gene expression in TGF-β-stimulated LX-2 cells. As expected, TGF-β significantly induced Actin alpha 2 (ACTA2 [ie, alpha smooth muscle actin, α-SMA]), FN1, and COL1A1 gene expression in LX-2 cells; however, several of the metabolites markedly attenuated these responses. Notably, 3-(methylthio)propanal, 5’-N-ethylcarboxamidoadenosine, and sinensetin reduced expression of all 3 profibrogenic genes >90% with a P < .001. We next investigated the effects of these metabolites on inflammatory gene expression in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells. LPS strongly upregulated Tnf-α, Il-1β, and Il-6, but indolelactic acid, plumbagin, and 3-hydroxyvaleric acid significantly reduced expression of all 3 proinflammatory cytokines by ∼60% to 90% (P < .001) (Figure 2D and Table 1). Collectively, these results demonstrate that metabolites enriched in LB-P8-conditioned media exert potent anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory activities, providing a mechanistic link between LB-P8 LB-P8 Improves Markers of Inflammation and Fibrosis in Bile-Duct Ligated Mice

To assess the potential benefits of LB-P8 monotherapy on cholestatic liver injury, we employed the bile duct ligated (BDL) mouse model.30,31 BDL mice were maintained for 17 days and received LB-P8 by gastric gavage for 14 days. Following the treatment period, the mice were sacrificed, and their livers were explanted for further analyses (Figure 3A). Body weight was monitored throughout the experimental period, with no significant differences observed between the vehicle and LB-P8-treated groups (Figure 3B), and representative images of the explanted livers were captured (Figure 3C). Picrosirius red and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections of liver tissue revealed increased fibrosis and bile infarcts in vehicle treated BDL mice compared with sham BDL mice. Notably, both fibrosis and bile infarcts were markedly reduced in mice treated with LB-P8 (Figure 3D and E). Among the 10 metabolites that exhibited significant anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory potential in vitro (Figure 2D and Table 1), the top 5 candidates, sinensetin,34,35 plumbagin,36,37 allopurinol,38,39 3-hydroxyvaleric acid, and indolelactic acid,40 were selected for in vivo validation based on their high efficacy and lack of cytotoxicity.

Oral administration of this 5-metabolite mixture to DDC-fed mice resulted in a marked reduction in collagen deposition, as evidenced by Picrosirius red staining, showing an inhibitory effect comparable to that of live LB-P8 treatment (Figure 7A). Given that most patients with PSC have IBD, commonly ulcerative colitis,2,41,42 we next assessed the effects of LB-P8 on fibrosis and inflammation on an experimental model of colitis (dextran sodium sulfate [DSS]-induced colitis).43 To induce colitis, 2% DSS was provided for 7 days, and LB-P8 or sulfasalazine, a drug commonly used to treat ulcerative colitis, was administered daily (Figure 10A). Monitoring of body weight over 9 days revealed a significant decrease in DSS-treated mice; however, no significant differences were observed between the LB-P8 and sulfasalazine-treated mice (Figure 10B). Regarding clinical parameters, colon length was reduced (P < .001) in DSS-treated mice that received vehicle (average of 4.1 cm) compared with no DSS control mice (6.1 cm). DSS-treated mice that received either LB-P8 or sulfasalazine exhibited increased colon length (5.2 cm; P = .001; and 4.7 cm; P = .113, respectively) vs control DSS-treated mice (Figure 10C). In addition, the disease activity index (DAI) was significantly increased by DSS-treatment, which was markedly decreased by LB-P8-treatment (P = .003), and sulfasalazine did not reduce DAI (Figure 10D). These results demonstrate the higher efficacy of LB-P8 compared with sulfasalazine. When we studied histopathologic changes of colons after DSS treatment, DSS-treated mice exhibited significant crypt damage and inflammatory cell infiltration in colons, indicative of colitis (Figure 10E). Here, the administration of LB-P8 dramatically reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, and goblet cells could be observed (Figure 10E). Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that LB-P8 ameliorates fibrosis and inflammation in multiple preclinical models of cholestatic liver disease. LB-P8 administration improved histological and biochemical markers of liver injury in BDL, DDC-fed, and Mdr2−/− mice, and reduced intestinal inflammation and fibrosis in the DSS colitis model. These effects were accompanied by suppression of pro-fibrotic and proinflammatory gene expression, modulation of key pathways such as TGF-β and LXR/RXR signaling, and the production of bioactive metabolites with direct anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory properties. Together, these findings suggest that LB-P8 is a promising microbiome-based therapeutic candidate for PSC and related fibro-inflammatory disease.

The pathogenesis of PSC remains unclear, although genetic predisposition, immune dysregulation, and environmental factors, particularly intestinal dysbiosis, have been implicated. Alterations in gut microbiota composition and metabolite profiles are thought to contribute to PSC progression by reducing bacterial diversity and driving specific microbial shifts that influence bile acid metabolism and immune responses.14 In addition, certain pathobionts, such as Klebsiella pneumoniae, may exert extraintestinal pathogenic effects.44 Previous approaches to modulate the gut microbiota, including antibiotics, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), and conventional probiotics, have shown inconsistent results.8,9,24,45,46 and raised concerns about antibiotic resistance as well as limited mechanistic insights.41 In this context, LB-P8 represents a distinct microbiota-based therapeutic candidate, exerting defined effects through metabolite production and targeted modulation of host signaling pathways.

A key aspect of our finding is that LB-P8 secretes specific metabolites capable of directly modulating host fibrogenic and inflammatory responses. For example, 3-(methylthio)propanal, 5’-N-ethylcarboxamidoadenosine, and sinensetin potently suppressed TGF-β-induced fibrogenic gene expression in HSCs, whereas indolelactic acid, plumbagin, and 3-hydroxyvaleric acid reduced proinflammatory cytokine expression in macrophages. Although identifying these candidates in vitro was an essential first step, the high systemic turnover and complex metabolic background of the gut-liver axis often make the in vivo quantification of specific microbial metabolites challenging. To overcome this and provide definitive functional evidence, we conducted a direct interventional study using a cocktail of the top 5 bioactive metabolites (sinensetin, plumbagin, indolelactic acid, 3-hydroxyvaleric acid, and allopurinol) in the DDC-induced cholestasis model. Treatment with this metabolite combination effectively recapitulated the therapeutic effects of the live LB-P8 strain, significantly reducing collagen deposition, serum ALP levels, and the expression of key fibrotic and inflammatory genes such as α-Sma, Col1a1, and Tnf-α. These findings demonstrate that these metabolites are sufficient to exert anti-fibrotic effects in vivo, strongly supporting our hypothesis that the clinical benefits of LB-P8 are driven predominantly by its metabolic secretome rather than structural bacterial components alone.

Although our findings suggest that metabolites are major drivers of the therapeutic effect, the impact of LB-P8 on microbial remodeling should not be overlooked. Notably, LB-P8 treatment led to a significant contraction of the Enterobacteriaceae population, reducing its relative abundance from approximately 25% to 10%. This shift is particularly meaningful given that the expansion of Enterobacteriaceae is a well-recognized feature of dysbiosis in patients with PSC and Mdr2−/− models,47 where they function as pathobionts that exacerbate biliary inflammation. Thus, the clinical benefits of LB-P8 likely arise from a synergistic mechanism: the direct anti-inflammatory action of its metabolites and the indirect benefit of suppressing proinflammatory pathobionts, thereby restoring a more balanced gut-liver axis. This aligns with emerging evidence that microbial metabolites, including indole derivatives, SCFAs, and secondary bile acids, are critical mediators of gut-liver axis communication and influence PSC pathogenesis. Importantly, we also observed that LB-P8 ameliorated intestinal inflammation and fibrosis in the DSS colitis model. This suggests that LB-P8 may exert therapeutic effects at both intestinal and hepatic levels. Given that most patients with PSC have coexisting IBD, the ability of LB-P8 to diminish fibro-inflammatory processes in both organs provides a strong rationale for its potential benefits in this patient population.

Mechanistically, spatial transcriptomic and pathway analysis in the DDC-fed model identified significant alterations in gene expression across cholangiocytes, myofibroblasts, and immune cells, including reduction in proinflammatory mediators (eg, Cxcl5 and Ccl2), suppression of profibrotic mediators (Pdgf, Pdgfr), and decreased expression of immune-associated transcripts within the peribiliary niche, including Cd14, Il1r1, Spp1, Lbp, and Mif. Although these latter transcripts are not macrophage-specific and F4/80 immunoreactivity does not distinguish infiltrating monocyte-derived macrophages from resident Kupffer cells or their activation state, the concordant reduction in F4/80-positive immunostaining together with diminished immune-associated transcriptional signals supports reduced immune cell contribution and inflammatory signaling within the peribiliary niche following LB-P8 treatment. In addition, LB-P8 treatment suppressed several key pathways such as TGF-β signaling in cholangiocytes and LXR/RXR activation in the immune cell population, the former associated with fibrogenic signaling48 and the latter associated with lipid metabolism and inflammatory regulation.49,50 Future studies incorporating additional immune markers will be required to further define broader LB-P8–mediated effects on hepatic immune populations.

IPA further identified that many TGF-β-regulated transcription factors, growth factors, and cytokines are inhibited in both the cholangiocyte and fibroblast populations. Importantly, Cebpb has been implicated as a mediator of TGF-β driven fibrogenic functions in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.51 Additionally, kruppel-like factor 6 (Klf6), another transcription factor that is activated by TGF-β, was also shown to be inhibited following LB-P8 treatment of DDC-fed mice. It was noted that 11 of 11 genes identified in our data set as being regulated by Klf6 exhibit gene expression measurement consistent with Klf6 inhibition. Klf6 is a transcription factor that plays a central role in the regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation, and development. Klf6 has also been implicated as a driver of hepatic fibrosis associated with alcoholic liver disease via binding to TGF-β.52 Moreover, TGF-β and its downstream mediator Smad3 were directly implicated in the myofibroblast population and were shown as being inhibited upstream activators by IPA analysis in LB-P8 treated DDC-fed mice. Together, IPA analysis of upstream activators strongly supports that LB-P8 modulates TGF-β signaling in the DDC-fed mouse model.

Despite these promising results, several questions remain to be addressed. Although ASV analysis confirmed the presence of LB-P8 during the dosing period, the degree of long-term engraftment after treatment cessation remains unknown. Furthermore, although we have provided a detailed characterization of the microbiome in the BDL model, the microbial shifts in the DDC and Mdr2−/− models warrant further investigation to determine if the suppression of Enterobacteriaceae is a universal feature of LB-P8 across different etiologies of cholestasis. In addition, further refinement of how LB-P8 modulates the hepatic immune landscape in models of cholestatic liver disease are required to more precisely define its immunomodulatory role. Addressing these limitations in future studies will be essential to fully optimize the clinical application of LB-P8.

Conclusion

In conclusion, LB-P8 treatment ameliorated liver fibrosis and inflammation in multiple preclinical models of cholestatic liver disease. These therapeutic effects were evidenced by improvements in serum biochemistries and histology and were accompanied by modulation of key pathways involved in liver injury, particularly those related to oxidative stress and fibrosis, including Nrf2, TGF-β, and LXR/RXR signaling. These findings provide compelling evidence for the therapeutic potential of LB-P8 as a novel intervention for the treatment of PSC, and a phase II clinical trial is currently underway in the United States to evaluate its safety and efficacy (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06699121).

Materials and Methods

Animal Experiments

For bile duct ligation model, 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of avertin (tribromoethanol). The abdomen was then incised, and the common bile duct was bluntly dissected and double ligated with nonabsorbable sutures to induce a mouse model of cholestasis induced by BDL. Three days after surgery, 2 × 109 CFU LB-P8 were administered via oral gavage daily for 2 weeks.

For DSS-induced colitis model, only male mice were utilized to ensure experimental consistency and minimize confounding factors. Previous studies have demonstrated that the estrous cycle and sex hormones, particularly estrogen, can significantly influence intestinal permeability and the severity of inflammation in the DSS model, leading to increased intra-group variability in females.53,54 Therefore, male mice were selected to achieve a more uniform inflammatory response. Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were exposed to 2% DSS in drinking water for 7 days, followed by 2 days of the administration of normal distilled water. The mice were randomly divided into 4 groups. The control group was orally administered with distilled water only. The DSS group was treated with DSS and orally administered with saline daily for 9 days, and the DSS + LB-P8 group was treated with DSS and orally administered with 2 × 109 CFU LB-P8 daily for 9 days. During the observation period, mice were monitored daily for body weight, rectal bleeding, and stool consistency.

C57BL/6J-Mdr2−/− mice were a gift from Dr Oude Elferink (Tytgat Institute). C57BL/6J (WT) mice were obtained from Charles River. Eight-week-old Mdr2−/− (Abcb4 knockout) mice (n = 10 mice per experimental group, equal numbers male and female) were treated with PBS (vehicle), 2 × 109 CFU freshly prepped LB-P8, or 2 × 109 freeze dried viable LB-P8 (dissolved in PBS) by oral gavage every day for 4 weeks. We also used C57BL/6 for the DDC experiments. Seven-week-old mice received either a chow diet supplemented with 0.1% DDC (5 days DDC diet, 2 days standard chow) or standard chow for 7 weeks. Beginning on week 3, DDC diet-fed mice were treated daily by oral gavage for 4 weeks with one of the following: PBS (vehicle), 2 × 109 CFU freshly prepped LB-P8, 2 × 109 CFU freeze dried viable LB-P8, or the LB-P8-derived metabolite mixture (consisting of the top 5 candidates identified from the in vitro system). All mice were sacrificed, and livers processed for further analysis.

For drug treatment, the following concentrations were used: 100 mg/kg sinensetin (custom-synthesized by Koma Biotech), 3 mg/kg plumbagin (Sigma, P7262), 50 mg/kg allopurinol (Sigma, A8003), 50 mg/kg 3-hydroxyvaleric acid (MCE, HY-113004), and 20 mg/kg indolelactic acid (Sigma, I5508). The metabolite mixture was first dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and further formulated with polyethylene glycol 300 (PEG300) and Tween 80 for oral administration.

Mice were housed in a clean facility and maintained in individually ventilated cages (IVCs) on a rack system. The housing environment was kept at a constant temperature with a 12-hour light/dark facility with ad libitum water and food. All animal studies were approved by the Institutional Care and Animal Use Committee of the Mayo Clinic or LISCure Bioscience, in accordance with the public health policy on the human use and care of laboratory animals.

Histopathology and Immunohistochemistry

Liver and colon tissue samples were fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 5-μm thickness were stained with H&E for routine examination of bile infarcts, crypt damage, and inflammatory cell infiltration. Liver fibrosis was assessed by morphometric analysis of Picrosirius red-stained sections. To ensure representative and consistent quantification, tissue samples were obtained from the left lateral lobe, the largest lobe of the mouse liver. For each mouse, 5 to 10 nonoverlapping fields were randomly selected and imaged using a light microscope. To prevent overestimation of the fibrotic area, large centrilobular vessels and the liver capsule were strictly excluded from the quantification. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health). To maintain objectivity, the color thresholding for Sirius red-stained areas was manually established and then consistently applied across all images by an investigator who was blinded to the experimental groups.

Immunohistochemical staining was performed using anti-CK7 (1:100), -F4/80 (1:100), -pan cytokeratin (PanCK; 1:100), -CD45 (1:100), and -α-SMA (1:100). Fluorescence was visualized with the laser confocal scanning microscope LSM780 ZEISS (CARL ZEISS).

Liver Biochemistry

Plasma ALT and ALP were measured using a VetScan2 (Abaxis).

Cell Culture

The LX-2 hepatic stellate cell line was obtained from the ATCC and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The HT-29 colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line was obtained from the ATCC and cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The RAW 264.7 murine macrophage cell line was obtained from the Korean cell line bank and cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin.

For drug treatment, the following concentrations were used: 100 ng/mL LPS (Sigma, L6529), 10 ng/mL TGF-β (Peprotech, 100-21), 100 μM sinensetin (Sigma, SML1787), 2 μM plumbagin, 10 μg/mL allopurinol, 500 μM 2-hydroxy-4-(methylthio)butanoic acid (Sigma, PH000120), 250 μM diflucortolone (Biosynth, FD65004), 500 μM tropate (Thermo, 51-55-8), 0.05 μL/mL 3-(methylthio)propanal (Sigma, 277460), 500 μM DL-α-hydroxyglutaric acid (Sigma, 94577), 500 μM 4-aminobenzoic acid (Sigma, A9878), 500 μM 3-amino-3-methylbutanoic acid (Biosynth, FA139997), 500 μM 3-hydroxyvaleric acid, 500 μM citrulline (Sigma, C7629), 500 μM phenylacetaldehyde (Selleckchem, S9357), 500 μM indolelactic acid, 5 mM glutaconic acid (Sigma, 49360), 500 μg/mL epsilon-caprolactone (Sigma, 704067), 500 μM 2-aminobenzoic acid (Sigma, A89855), and 10 mM succinic acid (MCE, HY-N0420). Metabolites were dissolved in DMSO or distilled water (DW) according to their solubility. For all in vitro experiments, control cells were treated with an equivalent volume of the respective vehicle. The final concentration of DMSO in the culture medium was kept below 0.1% (v/v) to minimize potential solvent-induced cytotoxicity or interference with gene expression profiling.

Bacterial Strains, Culture, and Processing

The bacterial strains used in this study included LB-P8 (L citreum), LBMB320195 (L citreum), and KCTC3528 (L lactis). LB-P8 was originally isolated from Kimchi, a traditional Korean fermented vegetable, and has been deposited in the Korean Culture Center of Microorganisms (KCCM) under accession number KCCM12420P. LBMB320195 was isolated from garlic and is maintained in the LISCure Biosciences microbiota bank (LBMB). The KCTC3528, was obtained from the Korean Collection for Type Cultures (KCTC).

For experimental use, LB-P8 and KCTC3528 were cultured in MRS HiVeg medium, whereas LBMB320195 was grown in MRS medium. All strains were maintained under static incubation at 30°C for 18 hours to reach the stationary phase.

To prepare the bacterial samples, both fresh cultures and lyophilized formulations were utilized. For fresh preparations, overnight cultures were harvested by centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 10 minutes at 4°C and washed twice with PBS to remove residual culture media components. For the preparation of the DS, the harvested biomass was concentrated, formulated with cryoprotectants, and stabilized through lyophilization (vacuum sublimation) using a freeze-dryer to obtain a stable powder form. For experimental use, both fresh cells and the lyophilized DS were resuspended in PBS, with concentrations adjusted to the required CFU to ensure standardized dosing across all groups.

In Vitro Co-Culture System

The co-culture systems were established using 6-well plates and 0.4-μm pore size Transwell inserts (Costar, 3412). For the LX-2/HT-29 co-culture, LX-2 cells were seeded in the lower 6-well plates at a density of 3.5 × 105 cells/well for 24 hours. Separately, for the macrophage/epithelial co-culture, RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in the lower 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 106 cells/well. In both systems, HT-29 cells (1 × 106 cells/well) were seeded in the upper Transwell chambers and cultured for 3 days until reaching 100% confluence. Once confluence was achieved, the HT-29 inserts were transferred to the 6-well plates containing either LX-2 or RAW 264.7 cells.

Following the establishment of the co-culture, the cells in the lower chambers were stimulated to induce a fibrotic or inflammatory response: LX-2 cells were treated with TGF-β and RAW 264.7 cells were treated with LPS. Simultaneously, HT-29 cells in the upper chambers were exposed to LB-P8 or LBMB320195 at a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU for 24 hours. To specifically evaluate the differentiation of LX-2 cells, HT-29 cells were treated with LB-P8, KCTC3528, or LBMB320195 at an increased concentration of 1 × 109 CFU for 24 h. For Western blot analysis, the concentration of LB-P8 was increased to 1 × 109 CFU to facilitate the detection of specific protein signaling pathways.

Sample Preparation for Metabolomics In Vitro

For metabolomics analysis, the co-culture system consisting of LX-2 and HT-29 cells was established using 0.4-μm pore size Transwell inserts as described above. Cells were cultured with LB-P8 or negative strain (KCTC 3528) for 24 hours, and media samples were collected, centrifuged, and sterile filtered through 0.2-μm filters to remove cells or debris. These processed samples were subsequently subjected to nontargeted or quantitative metabolomics analysis using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) to identify differentially expressed metabolites within the co-culture environment.

Ultra-Performance Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry Analysis for Metabolite Identification

Separation is performed by ACQUITY UPLC (Waters) combined with Q Exactive MS (Thermo) and screened with electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS). The LC system is comprised of an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (100 × 2.1 mm × 1.8 μm) with ACQUITY UPLC (Waters). The mobile phase is composed of solvent A (0.05% formic acid water) and solvent B (acetonitrile) with a gradient elution (0‒1 minute, 5% B; 1‒12.5 minutes, 5%‒95% B; 12.5‒13.5 minutes, 95% B; 13.5‒13.6 minutes, 95%‒5% B; 13.6‒16 minutes, 5% B). The flow rate of the mobile phase is 0.3 mL·min−1. The column temperature is maintained at 40°C, and the sample manager temperature is set at 4°C.

Mass scan mode: Full Scan (m/z 70∼1050, Resolution: 70,000) and dd-MS2 (TopN = 10, Resolution: 17,500); Collision mode: HCD; Mass spectrometry parameters in ESI+ and ESI- mode are listed as follows:

ESI+: Heater Temp 300°C; Sheath Gas Flow rate, 45 arb; Aux Gas Flow Rate, 15 arb; Sweep Gas Flow Rate, 1 arb; spray voltage, 3.0 kV; Capillary Temp, 350°C; S-Lens RF Level, 30%.

ESI−: Heater Temp 300°C, Sheath Gas Flow rate, 45 arb; Aux Gas Flow Rate, 15 arb; Sweep Gas Flow Rate, 1 arb; spray voltage, 3.2 kV; Capillary Temp, 350°C; S-Lens RF Level, 60%.

Immunoblot Analysis

Cells were solubilized in a RIPA buffer (Thermo, 89901) containing protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor. Protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Samples (20 μg) were separated on Mini-PROTEAN TGX Gels (BioRad, 4569035) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes for immunoblot analysis using the electrochemiluminescence (ECL) method (Thermo, 32106). For immunoblotting, antibodies (Abs) against the following proteins were used: p-SMAD 2/3 (Cell Signaling, 8828, 1:1,000), SMAD2/3 (Cell Signaling, 8685, 1:1,000) and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; Cell Signaling, 2118, 1:1,000).

RNA Isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from various cells or frozen liver tissues using TRIzol reagent (MRC, TR118). The pieces of liver tissue (∼0.1 g) were homogenized with tissue homogenizer (Omni International) on ice in TRIzol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the cDNA synthesis kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR green (Thermo, 4364346) in a QuanStudio Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The relative expression levels of each gene were normalized to that of 18s rRNA or GAPDH. Sequences of primers used for RT-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

GeoMX Sequencing and Analysis

Segmentation was performed by immunofluorescence using the following antibodies: panCK (cholangiocytes, pan Antibody (AE-1/AE-3), Alexa Flour 532, Novus Biologicals, Cat# NBP2-33200AF532), anti-α-sma (myofibroblasts, Anti-alpha smooth muscle actin Alexa Fluor 647 (SP171), Abcam, Cat#Ab267537), and CD45 (immune cells, CD45[D3F8Q] AF594 Rabbit mAb, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#35154) In addition, nuclei were stained with SYTO 13 Green Fluorescent Nucleic Acid Stain (Invitrogen Molecular Probes, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#S7575). Gene expression was quantified via ultraviolet-cleaved barcode sequencing read counts determined using the GeoMx Mouse Whole Transcriptome Atlas – Mouse RNA for Illumina Systems (Mm_R_NGS_WTA_v1.0) RNA probe set. This RNA Probe panel profiles the entire transcriptome by targeting 21,000+ distinct transcripts from mouse protein-coding genes plus ERCC negative controls. The panel excludes targets with uninformative high expression, like ribosomal subunits. Library prep includes RNA probes designed for Illumina NGS readout with Seq Code primers.

RNA-seq data (FASTQ files) were processed with the GeoMx NGS Pipeline. After sequencing, reads were trimmed, merged, and aligned to a list of indexing oligos to identify the source probe. The unique molecular identifier (UMI) region of each read was used to remove PCR duplicates and duplicate reads, thus converting reads into digital counts.

For DSP RNA expression evaluation, a total of 24 ROIs were processed. Raw Illumina counts were upper quartile (Q3) normalized using the GeoMx software and standardized QC threshold settings as recommended by the manufacturer. Low-quality samples were removed from analysis if the geometric mean of aligned reads from all probes was less than the geometric mean of the negative control probes; the Q3 of the counts in each ROI was less than the geometric mean of the negative control probes in the data. Processing count data was done using GeomxTools (v3.2.0) and NanoStringNCTools (v1.6.1).

Statistical analyses of the data generated herein were performed using R version (v4.1.2). Dimensionality reduction analysis was performed with principal components analysis to assess outliers and potential batch effects. Comparisons for individual gene expression were measured as a log2 fold-change (FC). A linear mixed model was used to fit the normalized RNA count data. The adjustment method to control FDR for multiple testing used in this study was the Benjamini and Hochberg (1995) method. The analysis was carried out using lme4 package (v1.1.31) and stats package (v4.1.2) with emmeans (v1.8.4.1). Plotting outputs was done in ggplot2 (v 3.4.4).

Fecal Microbiome Analysis of BDL Mouse Model Treated With LB-P8

Fresh fecal pellets from the BDL mice were collected from the bedding using sterile tools and immediately transferred to sterile microcentrifuge tubes. The samples were stored at −80°C until DNA extraction. The DNA was isolated from the fecal samples using QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentration and purity were evaluated using NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified from the extracted DNA using specific primers: 341F (5'-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3') and 805R (5'-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3'). Following amplification, the PCR products were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter) to remove residual primers and other impurities. The purified DNA was quantified using Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and equal amounts of each sample were pooled together for high-throughput next-generation sequencing. The pooled amplicons were sequenced on MiSeq platform using MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 (Illumina) to generate 300 bp paired-end reads.

Raw sequence data were initially checked for quality using FastQC v. 0.11.9. To improve the quality of the data, Fastp v. 0.20.1 was used for trimming and filtering the reads. Quality-filtered sequences were imported into QIIME 2 v. 2024.2 for detailed microbiome analysis. Within QIIME 2, denoising and chimera removal were carried out using the DADA2 plugin, which allowed for the generation of high-resolution ASVs. The ASVs were taxonomically classified using Naive Bayes classifier pre-trained on the SILVA 138.2 database. To compare microbial communities across different samples, beta diversity was assessed using weighted and unweighted UniFrac distances. PCoA was employed to visualize the differences in bacterial composition and the statistical significance of these differences was tested using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). For differential abundance analysis, LEfSe was used to identify taxa that were significantly different between groups. Statistical analysis of the relative abundance of bacterial genera was conducted using Mann-Whitney U test to compare differences between groups. To account for multiple comparisons, the P values were adjusted using the FDR method, with a significance threshold set at 5%.

Statistical and Data Analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or mean ± standard error of mean (SEM) as appropriate. Nonparametric tests were used for comparing groups. Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism version 10.2.1 for Windows (GraphPad Software). A value of P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

The raw data are acquired and aligned using the Compound Discoverer (3.0, Thermo) based on the m/z value and the retention time of the ion signals. Ions from both ESI- or ESI+ are merged and imported into the SIMCA-P program (version 14.1) for multivariate analysis. A principal components analysis (PCA) is first used as an unsupervised method for data visualization and outlier identification. Supervised regression modeling is then performed on the data set by use of partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) or orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) to identify the potential biomarkers. The biomarkers are filtered and confirmed by combining the results of the VIP values (VIP > 1.5) and t-test (P < .05). The quality of the fitting model can be explained by R2 and Q2 values. R2 displays the variance explained in the model and indicates the quality of the fit. Q2 displays the variance in the data, indicating the model’s predictability.
