Perturbative Fourier Ptychographic Microscopy for Fast Quantitative Phase Imaging
Martin Zach, Kuan-Chen Shen, Ruiming Cao, Michael Unser, Laura Waller, Jonathan Dong

Abstract
In computational phase imaging with a microscope equipped with an array of light emitting diodes as illumination unit, conventional Fourier ptychographic microscopyachieves high resolution and wide-field reconstructions but is constrained by a lengthy acquisition time. Conversely, differential phase contrast (DPC)offers fast imaging but is limited in resolution. Here, we introduce perturbative Fourier ptychographic microscopy (pFPM). pFPMis an extension of DPCthat incorporates dark-field illumination to enable fast, high-resolution, wide-field quantitative phase imaging with few measurements. We interpret DPCas the initial iteration of a Gauss-Newton algorithm with quadratic regularization and generalize it to multiple iterations and more sophisticated regularizers. This broader framework is not restricted to bright-field measurements and allows us to overcome resolution limitations of DPC. We develop tailored annular dark-field illumination patterns that align with the perturbative interpretation and lead to an improvement in the quality of reconstruction with respect to other common illumination schemes. Consequently, our methodology combines an enhanced phase reconstruction algorithm with a specialized illumination strategy and offers significant advantages in both imaging speed and resolution.

The most popular phase imaging methods using LED-array microscopes are on the two ends of the speed-resolution tradeoff.
On the one hand, Fourier ptychographic microscopy (FPM)
[
24
,
26
]
entails the acquisition of one measurement per (bright-field and dark-field) LED, typically in the range of hundreds of measurements.
The reconstruction of the high-resolution, wide field-of-view images is done with nonlinear optimization algorithms, typically gradient descent
[
21
,
4
]
.
On the other hand, differential phase contrast (DPC)
[
16
,
6
,
10
]
entails the acquisition of two measurements from asymmetric bright-field illumination patterns.
There, the weak-object approximation enables one to reconstruct the phase by solving a linear system.
However, the linear model only considers bright-field illumination, which limits the achievable resolution.

To image living specimens with acceptable speed, it is essential to minimize the number of required measurements per reconstruction.
Multiplexed Fourier ptychographic microscopy (mFPM)
[
18
]
addresses this by the simultaneous use of multiple LEDs, thus merging the speed of DPCwith the high resolution of FPM.
However, the nonlinear optimization becomes increasingly difficult as the number of measurements is reduced, while the optimal design of illumination patterns remains an open problem.
These limitations can partly be addressed by the learning of illumination patterns
[
7
]
or the fine tuning of parameters of a reconstruction algorithm
[
20
,
1
]
which both improve the quality of mFPMreconstruction.
However, the learned methods tend to be opaque, hard to interpret, and highly tailored toward specific imaging conditions.

In this work, we introduce perturbative Fourier ptychographic microscopy (pFPM)for high-resolution phase imaging with few measurements.
We revisit both the reconstruction algorithm and the acquisition strategy from an optimization perspective to tackle the speed-resolution tradeoff.
First, we interpret the DPCreconstruction process that is based on the weak-object linearization as the initial iteration of a Gauss-Newton algorithm.
We then generalize this into a Gauss-Newton framework that accounts for dark-field illumination, with more sophisticated regularization to improve the robustness of phase retrieval.
Second, we design annular dark-field illumination patterns that
“push”
reconstructed frequencies into the dark-field region, thereby enhancing resolution.

In summary, our methodology combines a perturbative reconstruction algorithm inspired by DPCwith tailored illumination patterns to enable fast, high-resolution quantitative phase imaging.
We demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach through numerical simulations and experimental results.

Background

2.1
LED-Array Microscope

The resolution of a classic microscope is determined by the NA
\nu_{\mathrm{obj}}
of its objective, which sets a limit on the largest acquired spatial frequencies.
To bypass this limit, in an LED-array microscope the conventional illumination unit is replaced by a programmable LEDarray.
By illuminating the sample with tilted plane waves, the system gets access to a broader range of spatial frequencies than would be possible under normal incidence.

The illumination NA
\nu_{\mathrm{ill}}
is defined as the sine of the largest tilt angle.
When combined with the numerical aperture of the objective, the achievable resolution increases to
\frac{1.22\lambda}{\nu_{\mathrm{obj}}+\nu_{\mathrm{ill}}}
, where
\lambda
is the average illumination wavelength.
Such setups typically use a low-NAobjective to maximize the field-of-view while achieving high resolution in reason of a large illumination NA.
LEDsare categorized based on their position relative to the aperture of the microscope:
Bright-field LEDslie within the cone defined by
\nu_{\mathrm{obj}}
, thereby allowing unscattered light to pass through the objective and to yield high-intensity measurements that contain low-frequency information.
In contrast, dark-field LEDslie outside this cone and generate dim measurements from scattered light that contain high-frequency information.

3
Method

In this section, we introduce a perturbative formalism to exploit good initial estimates and refine them.
Computationally, we show that the single-shot linearization of DPCcan be extended to an iterative regularized Gauss-Newton algorithm.
Experimentally, we introduce annular illuminations that take full advantage of this perturbative interpretation to refine the reconstruction using dark-field LEDs.

3.1
Perturbative FPMAlgorithm

We identify DPCas the initial iteration of the iterative procedure

o_{k+1}=\argmin_{o}\,\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert G(o_{k})+G^{\prime}(o_{k})(o-o_{k})-Y\rVert_{2}^{2}+\alpha R(o)\bigr)

(13)

under the choice
R(o)=\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert o\rVert_{2}^{2}
and starting with
o_{0}=1
as the initial guess.
This equivalence is rigorously derived in
Appendix

B
.
We recognize (
13
) as the proximal Gauss-Newton algorithm
[
15
]
and generalize DPCto more iterations and more sophisticated regularizers.
The generalization to more iterations enables the reconstruction of image features from dark-field measurements, as subsequent iterations of the proximal Gauss-Newton algorithm do not rely on the weak-object approximation.

The generalization to more sophisticated regularizers enables the consideration of regularizers with superior noise-suppression properties.
In this paper, we demonstrate the benefit of this generalization by comparing total variation (TV)regularization to the previously used
L^{2}
regularization.
The framework is not restricted to this; in particular, data-driven regularizers could also be employed.
Indeed, we believe that any phase-retrieval method can benefit from the stronger noise suppression properties of the variational penalties that can be used in the proximal Gauss-Newton framework.

3.2
Perturbative FPMPatterns

In this section, we detail an acquisition strategy that exploits the combination of DPC-type patterns with darkfield annuli to enable fast high-resolution imaging with few illumination patterns that are tailored towards the perturbative approach.
In the bright-field, we exploit the antisymmetric bright-field illumination patterns of DPCthat enable the accurate recovery of the frequencies of the transmission function up to
k_{\mathrm{BF}}=2\nu_{\mathrm{obj}}/\lambda
when the object is weak, similar to
[
17
]
.
In the dark-field, we build on the work of Kellmann et al. who show that dark-field illumination patterns need not be antisymmetric—indeed, learned dark-field illumination patterns are actually symmetric
[
7
, Fig. 4. (c)]
, disjoint, and clustered.
Consequently, we partition the dark-field LEDsinto annuli, each forming an illumination pattern, to allow for the recovery of higher frequencies from dark-field measurements after the DPCsolution.
The illumination patterns, shown in
Figure

2
(d), enable one to
“push”
the recovered frequencies into the dark-field.

Each annulus extends the region of recovered frequencies.
Although the number of annuli is tunable and balances acquisition time with reconstruction quality, our simulations and experiments show excellent results with only two annuli and diminishing returns with more than two annuli, when doubling the brightfield illumination NA.
Thus, we recommend the use of two dark-field measurements to significantly enhance the quality of the reconstruction at the least expense in imaging speed.

5
Conclusion

We introduce a perturbative approach for phase imaging with a LED-array microscope that enables high-resolution phase imaging with few measurements by combining a perturbative reconstruction algorithm with tailored bright-field and dark-field illumination patterns.
The perturbative algorithm, effectively a Gauss-Newton algorithm, is a generalization of the popular DPCto multiple iterations and more sophisticated regularizers with better noise-suppression properties.
We demonstrate the feasibility of the approach on simulated as well as on experimental data.
The proposed method improves the reconstructions significantly over DPC, at the cost of only two additional measurements from dark-field illumination and without any learned components.
The proposed illumination patterns also consistently lead to better reconstructions than other illumination patterns that have been proposed previously.
Future work will take advantage of data-driven regularizers to improve reconstructions or decrease exposure time.
In addition, we plan to refine the optical models.
For instance, the present work did not consider aberrations in the pupil function.
Finally, while the current work is dedicated to optical phase imaging with an LEDarray, it may be extended to X-ray or electron microscopy.

Appendix B
The First Iteration of Proximal Gauss-Newton Is DPC

The initial Gauss-Newton step, where the measurement operator is linearized around
o=1
, solves the problem

\argmin_{o}\,\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert G(1)+G^{\prime}(1)(o-1)-Y\rVert_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{\alpha}{2}\lVert o\rVert_{2}^{2}\Bigr).

(23)

First, we note that
G(1)=\bigl(\sum_{n\in\mathcal{L}_{1}}\lvert p({k}_{n})\rvert^{2},\dotsc,\sum_{n\in\mathcal{L}_{M}}\lvert p({k}_{n})\rvert^{2}\bigr)
.
Since
(o-1)=(\mathrm{j}\phi-\mu)
, we have that

G^{\prime}(1)(o-1)=\biggl(\sum_{n\in\mathcal{L}_{1}}2\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\mathcal{A}_{n}\{1\}}\mathcal{A}_{n}\{\mathrm{j}\phi-\mu\}),\dotsc,\sum_{n\in\mathcal{L}_{M}}2\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\mathcal{A}_{n}\{1\}}\mathcal{A}_{n}\{\mathrm{j}\phi-\mu\})\biggr).

(24)

Thus, it suffices to analyze
2\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\mathcal{A}_{n}\{1\}}\mathcal{A}_{n}\{j\phi-\mu\})
for an arbitrary
n\in\{1,2,\dotsc,N\}
.
Using
\overline{\mathcal{A}_{n}\{1\}}=\overline{p({k}_{n})}\exp(\mathrm{j}\langle-{k}_{n},\,\cdot\,\rangle)
and inserting the definition of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle 2\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\mathcal{A}_{n}\{1\}}\mathcal{A}_{n}\{\mathrm{j}\phi-\mu\})

(25)

\displaystyle=2\mathfrak{R}(\overline{p({k}_{n})}\exp(\mathrm{j}\langle-{k}_{n},\,\cdot\,\rangle)\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{p\mathcal{F}\{(\mathrm{j}\phi(\,\cdot\,)-\mu(\,\cdot\,))\exp{(\mathrm{j}\langle{k}_{n},\,\cdot\,\rangle)}\}\})

\displaystyle=2\mathfrak{R}(\overline{p({k}_{n})}\exp(\mathrm{j}\langle-{k}_{n},\,\cdot\,\rangle)\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{p(\mathrm{j}\hat{\phi}(\,\cdot\,-{k}_{n})-\hat{\mu}(\,\cdot\,-{k}_{n}))\})

\displaystyle=2\mathfrak{R}(\overline{p({k}_{n})}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{p(\,\cdot\,+{k}_{n})(\mathrm{j}\hat{\phi}-\hat{\mu})\})

\displaystyle=\overline{p({k}_{n})}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{p(\,\cdot\,+{k}_{n})(\mathrm{j}\hat{\phi}-\hat{\mu})\}+\overline{\overline{p({k}_{n})}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{p(\,\cdot\,+{k}_{n})(\mathrm{j}\hat{\phi}-\hat{\mu}\})},

where the last equality is due to
2\mathfrak{R}(f)=f+\overline{f}
.
Now, we simplify the second term using
\overline{\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\hat{f}\}}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\overline{\hat{f}(-\,\cdot\,)}\}
and
\overline{\hat{f}}=\hat{f}(-\,\cdot\,)
for real
f
, which leads to

\displaystyle\overline{\overline{p({k}_{n})}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{p(\,\cdot\,+{k}_{n})(\mathrm{j}\hat{\phi}-\hat{\mu})\}}

\displaystyle=p({k}_{n})\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\overline{p({k}_{n}-\,\cdot\,)(\mathrm{j}\hat{\phi}(-\,\cdot\,)-\hat{\mu}(-\,\cdot\,))}\}

(26)

\displaystyle=-p({k}_{n})\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\overline{p({k}_{n}-\,\cdot\,)}(\mathrm{j}\hat{\phi}+\hat{\mu})\}.

Thus, we conclude that

2\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\mathcal{A}_{n}\{1\}}\mathcal{A}_{n}\{\mathrm{j}\phi-\mu\})=\overline{p({k}_{n})}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{p(\,\cdot\,+{k}_{n})(\mathrm{j}\hat{\phi}-\hat{\mu})\}-p({k}_{n})\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\overline{p({k}_{n}-\,\cdot\,)}(\mathrm{j}\hat{\phi}+\hat{\mu})\}.

(27)

This expression is the same as (
21
), except for the missing term
\lvert p({k}_{n})\rvert^{2}
which is due to the contribution of the
n
th LEDin
G(1)
.

This proves that the first iteration of a proximal Gauss-Newton method corresponds to DPC, as the linearized model in our Gauss-Newton algorithm matches the conventional DPCtransfer function and since both minimize the same
L^{2}
loss, assuming that
\phi
is zero-mean.

Appendix C
Discretization

We discretize on the two-dimensional uniform Cartesian grid both the object space in
\{1,2,\dotsc,d_{1}\}\times\{1,2,\dotsc,d_{2}\}
and the image space in
\{1,2,\dotsc,n_{1}\}\times\{1,2,\dotsc,n_{2}\}
.
The discrete phase image
{x}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{C}^{D}
is of dimension
D=d_{1}d_{2}
and the output intensities
{y}_{n}
are in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
N=n_{1}n_{2}
.
In the discrete setting, the intensity measurements (
1
) are modeled as

{y}_{n}=\lvert{A}_{n}{x}^{\ast}\rvert^{2}

(28)

where the linear operator corresponding to the
n
th LEDis defined as
{A}_{n}={F}^{\ast}{C}{P}{S}_{n}{F}
, with
{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}
the two-dimensional discrete Fourier transform,
{S}_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}
a circular shift in Fourier space,
{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times N}
a crop to reduce output dimension,
{P}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
a model of the pupil function of the microscope, and finally
{F}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
the two-dimensional inverse discrete Fourier transform.
The phase modulation by a circular shift in Fourier space is represented by
{S}_{n}
.
Moreover, we round the theoretical shift determined by the LEDposition and the optical system to the nearest integer to avoid interpolation.
Since we assume an aberration-free system,
{P}
acts as a binary diagonal operator that blanks frequencies outside of the aperture.

For multiplexed measurements, let
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2},\dotsc,\mathcal{L}_{M}
be sets of LEDindices that encode the
M
illumination patterns, as in
Section

2.3
.
For each
m=1,2,\dotsc,M,
the measurements are

{Y}_{m}=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{L}_{m}}\lvert{A}_{n}{x}^{\ast}\rvert^{2},

(29)

or, after letting
{Y}=({Y}_{1},{Y}_{2},\dotsc,{Y}_{M})
,

{Y}=\mathrm{G}({x}^{\ast}),

(30)

where

\mathrm{G}:\mathbb{C}^{D}\to{(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{M}}:{x}\mapsto\biggl(\sum_{n\in\mathcal{L}_{1}}\lvert{A}_{n}{x}\rvert^{2},\dotsc,\sum_{n\in\mathcal{L}_{M}}\lvert{A}_{n}{x}\rvert^{2}\biggr),

(31)

summarizes the nonlinear multiplexed measurement acquisition.
The Fréchet derivative of
G
at some point
{x}^{k}
is the map

\mathrm{G}^{\prime}({x}^{k}):\mathbb{C}^{D}\to{(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{M}}:{x}\mapsto\biggl(\sum_{n\in\mathcal{L}_{1}}\mathfrak{R}(2\overline{{A}_{n}{x}^{k}}\odot{A}_{n}{x}),\dotsc,\sum_{n\in\mathcal{L}_{M}}\mathfrak{R}(2\overline{{A}_{n}{x}^{k}}\odot{A}_{n}{x})\biggr),

(32)

where
\odot
denotes an element-wise multiplication.

Appendix D
Details of the Reconstruction Algorithm

In our setup, the regularized nonlinear least-squares problem associated with (
30
) is

\argmin_{{x}}\,\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\mathrm{G}({x})-{Y}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\alpha R({x})\bigr).

(33)

Given the current solution
{x}_{k}
, a proximal Gauss-Newton iteration on (
33
) amounts to an update rule of the form

{x}_{k+1}=\prox_{\alpha R}^{H({x}_{k})}\Bigl({x}_{k}-\bigl(H({x}_{k})\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(\mathrm{G}^{\prime}({x}_{k})\bigr)^{\ast}\bigl(\mathrm{G}({x}_{k})-{Y}\bigr)\Bigr),

(34)

where the asterisk denotes the adjoint and
\prox_{\alpha R}^{H({x}_{k})}
is the proximal operator of
\alpha R
with respect to the metric
H({x}_{k})=\bigl(G^{\prime}({x}_{k})\bigr)^{\ast}G^{\prime}({x}_{k})
.
The proximal operator is defined as

\prox_{\alpha R}^{H({x}_{k})}({x})=\argmin_{{z}}\,\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert{z}-{x}\rVert^{2}_{H({x}_{k})}+\alpha R({z})\bigr).

(35)

Proposition 6 in
[
15
]
states that (
34
) is equivalent to the update rule

{x}_{k+1}=\argmin_{{x}}\,\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\mathrm{G}({x}_{k})+\mathrm{G}^{\prime}({x}_{k})({x}-{x}_{k})-{Y}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\alpha R({x})\bigr).

(36)

Indeed, the proximal Gauss-Newton algorithm can be implemented by iteratively solving the regularized linearized problem.

We consider two regularizers: Classical
L^{2}
regularization
{x}\mapsto\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}
, and isotropic TVregularization
{x}\mapsto\lVert{D}{x}\rVert_{2,1}
, where
{D}:\mathbb{C}^{D}\to\mathbb{C}^{D\times 2}
is a forward finite-difference operator with Neumann boundaries and
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{2,1}:\mathbb{C}^{D\times 2}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}:{x}\mapsto\sum_{d=1}^{D}\sqrt{\lvert{x}_{d,1}\rvert^{2}+\lvert{x}_{d,2}\rvert^{2}}
.

For
L^{2}
, we solve the linear system that results from the first-order optimality condition

\Bigl(\bigl(\mathrm{G}^{\prime}({x}_{k})\bigr)^{\ast}\mathrm{G}^{\prime}({x}_{k})+\alpha{I}\Bigr){x}=\bigl(\mathrm{G}^{\prime}({x}_{k})\bigr)^{\ast}\bigl(\mathrm{G}^{\prime}({x}_{k}){x}_{k}+{Y}-\mathrm{G}({x}_{k})\bigr),

(37)

where
{I}
is the identity matrix, using the conjugate gradient algorithm.
This also covers the unregularized case
\alpha=0
.
For TV, we use the dual representation of the TVnorm, given by

\alpha\lVert{D}\,\cdot\,\rVert_{2,1}=\max_{{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times 2}}\ \bigl(\langle\,\cdot\,,{D}^{\ast}{z}\rangle-\delta_{\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{2,\infty}\leq\alpha}({z})\bigr),

(38)

where

\delta_{\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{2,\infty}\leq\alpha}({z})=\begin{cases}\infty,&\ \text{if}\ \max_{d\in\{1,2,\dotsc,D\}}\lVert{z}_{d}\rVert_{2}>\alpha\\
0,&\ \text{else}.\end{cases}

(39)

We then solve the nonsmooth convex-concave saddle point problem

\argmin_{{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{D}}\max_{{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times 2}}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\mathrm{G}({x}_{k})+\mathrm{G}^{\prime}({x}_{k})({x}-{x}_{k})-{Y}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\langle{x},{D}^{\ast}{z}\rangle-\delta_{\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{2,\infty}\leq\alpha}({z})\bigr)

(40)

using the Condat-Vũ algorithm, an extension of the primal-dual hybrid gradient algorithm with explicit gradient steps, as summarized in
Algorithm

1
.
There,

{z}=\proj_{\{\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{2,\infty}<\alpha\}}(\bar{{z}})\iff{z}_{d}=\frac{\bar{{z}}_{d}}{\max(1,\alpha^{-1}\lVert\bar{{z}}_{d}\rVert_{2})}

(41)

is a pointwise projection onto the two-norm ball and at the
k
th proximal Gauss-Newton (PGN)iteration, and we chose the step sizes
\tau,\sigma>0
as

\sigma=\frac{\sqrt{\big\lVert\bigl(\mathrm{G}^{\prime}({x}_{k})\bigr)^{\ast}\mathrm{G}^{\prime}({x}_{k})\big\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}}{\lVert{D}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}

(42)

and

\tau=\bigl(\sigma\lVert{D}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}+\big\lVert\bigl(\mathrm{G}^{\prime}({x}_{k})\bigr)^{\ast}\mathrm{G}^{\prime}({x}_{k})\big\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\bigr)^{-1},

(43)

where
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
is the operator norm
[
3
, Section 1, (i)]
.
These stepsizes satisfy the inequality

\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\tau}-\big\lVert\bigl(G^{\prime}({x}_{k})\bigr)^{\ast}G^{\prime}({x}_{k})\big\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\Bigr)\tfrac{1}{\sigma}\geq\lVert{D}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}

(44)

needed for convergence, see
[
3
, Remark 1]
.
We computed
\big\lVert\bigl(G^{\prime}({x}_{k})\bigr)^{\ast}G^{\prime}({x}_{k})\big\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
using
10
power iterations and used
\lVert{D}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\sqrt{8}
from
[
2
, Theorem 3.1]
.

The proposed annular illumination patterns are inspired by the perturbative principle.
To design them, we compare reconstructions obtained from a variety of common illumination patterns qualitatively and quantitatively, over the same simulation setup as in the main text.
We considered illumination patterns where three bright-field measurements are augmented by
(M-3)

Then we fixed the number of illumination patterns to
M=5
and
6
and compared our illumination patterns against two designs:

The first design was proposed in
[
20
]
in the context of data-driven recovery, where the classic two DPChalf-circles in the bright-field are augmented by annular sectors in the dark-field.
Unfortunately, in the absence of an additional bright-field measurement we observed the same oriented streaking artifacts as in
Figure

3
.
Therefore, we use the same three bright-field illumination patterns as for our method and, consequently, partition the dark-field annulus into
(M-3)
equisized sectors.

The second design instantiates a random selection similar to the selection proposed by
[
18
]
in the original publication on mFPM.
There, in each illumination pattern, a fraction of
\tfrac{1}{M}
of all LEDsup to
\nu_{\mathrm{ill}}
is activated.

We conclude from

(

In addition, an increase in the number of measurements from

6
gives only marginal gains, thus making the pFPMwith
5
measurements a fast, practical choice for those applications that necessitate high frame rates.

Appendix G
Simulation Data

We consider a region
\Omega
of
220\text{\times}220
centered around the pixel index (200, 300) of the the
cameraman
image,
{x}^{\mathrm{cm}}
, and normalize it by

{x}^{\mathrm{cm}}_{\mathrm{norm}}=\frac{{x}^{\mathrm{cm}}-\min_{{k}\in\Omega}[{x}^{\mathrm{cm}}]_{{k}}}{\max_{{k}\in\Omega}[{x}^{\mathrm{cm}}]_{{k}}-\min_{{k}\in\Omega}[{x}^{\mathrm{cm}}]_{{k}}}-0.5.

(47)

Then, we generate the simulation target as the phase encoding of the complex image

{x}^{\ast}=\exp(\mathrm{j}{x}^{\mathrm{cm}}_{\mathrm{norm}}).

(48)
