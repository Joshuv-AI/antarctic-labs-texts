Identification of an immune-metabolic biosignature linking depressive symptoms and breast cancer in a clinical population
Authors: Letizia Giona, Barbara Collacchi, Sara Capoccia, Marta Borgi, Carla Raggi, Alessandro Bonucci, Alessandra Fabi, Chiara Falcicchio, Maria Perrone, Flavia Chiarotti, Elena Ortona, Alessandra Berry, Francesca Cirulli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer (BC) is a leading cause of mortality among women. Comorbidity with mood disorders is a condition either disregarded or underdiagnosed in BC patients, but that might ultimately jeopardize health trajectories. This is supported by evidence indicating that the same biological pathways relevant for mood disorders may also underlie tumorigenesis. In this study, we aimed at deriving a reliable biosignature of mental health vulnerability in BC patients. We conducted a cross-sectional study in a population of 44 women diagnosed with BC who underwent surgery before receiving adjuvant chemotherapy. All subjects were scored for symptoms of depression, anxiety and stress; blood samples were used to measure relevant biomarkers of inflammation, energy homeostasis and brain plasticity, while circadian cortisol rhythm was assessed in the saliva. Based on a rigorous statistical approach, we identified a specific immune- metabolic biosignature of depression relying upon each subject’s BMI, IL-5 and leptin. Following the validation of the model, we defined a cut-off value to identify those subjects who are at elevated risk of poor prognosis based on our biosignature. This signature holds potential for the timely identification of those individuals for whom depressive symptoms are sustained by a deranged immune-metabolic milieu and might therefore be at higher risk of poorer health outcomes. Our results strengthen the importance of accounting for brain-body communication in cancer and suggest that routine screening for mental health in BC patients should be prioritized in order to put in place tailored intervention strategies to improve health outcomes.Subject terms: Diagnostic markers, Human behaviour

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is a leading cause of mortality worldwide among women, with an incidence of 11.6% among all types of cancer [1]. Routine screenings and advancements in diagnostic tools have resulted in a tremendous increase in the early identification of BC, allowing for timely treatment and, in turn, higher survival rates [2]. However, both in the short and long term, we are faced with the problem of anxiety and stress management, and a high number of survivors are present in the general population who have to face the stress of recurrence.

In fact, beyond BC-related specific features (e.g., the status of lymph nodes, tumor size and histological subtypes), lifestyle habits and emotional distress may greatly affect BC progression, metastasis and relapse [3–11]. For example, women with BC are forced to cope with the psychosocial stress associated with the diagnosis and the management of the disease [12], and chronic stress is a well-known trigger for the onset of psychiatric disorders. In this regard, it is worth mentioning that, when compared with the general female population, women with BC show a higher prevalence of mood disorders, a condition that adds a further level of vulnerability [13–16].

There is growing evidence suggesting that the same stress-activated pathways relevant for mood disorders may also underlie tumorigenesis [12, 17–19]. Moreover, bidirectional and mutually reinforcing signaling cascades between the tumor microenvironment and the brain have been hypothesized, possibly leading to the behavioral and neuroendocrine changes which are endophenotypes of depression [17, 20, 21].

Animal models have contributed to shedding light on the interplay among stress, emotional reactivity, tumor onset and progression [22–24]. Cao and co-workers have provided evidence for a brain-adipocyte BDNF/leptin axis affecting cancer progression [25]. Moreover, we have recently shown that the stress deriving from social isolation - a condition often accompanying oncologic patients - targets neuronal circuits modulating mood, stress reactivity and energy homeostasis in a genetic mouse model of BC [26].

Recent work has nicely summarized many years of research investigating the association between anxiety and depression with both recurrence and cancer-specific mortality, highlighting the critical role of mood disorders and, in general, of prominent symptoms of chronic stress as risk factors for poor prognosis [13].

The goal of the present study was to seek specific and objective biomarkers for the early identification of signatures of emotional disruption and mental health vulnerability in women newly diagnosed with BC to improve symptom management and to reduce the risk of recurrence. To this aim, we conducted a cross-sectional study in a population of women diagnosed with BC who underwent surgery before receiving adjuvant chemotherapy. We focused on this specific population and timing as chemotherapy could influence the levels of circulating cytokines [27, 28], masking the association with depression severity [29]. All subjects were scored for symptoms of depression, anxiety and stress through standardized scales. Blood samples were collected to assess chemokines, cytokines and adipokines in addition to BDNF; circadian cortisol rhythm was investigated through multiple non-invasive saliva samples. Specific binary logistic regression models were used to compare the association between all biological variables and vulnerability to depression, anxiety and stress to obtain reference values that might help clinicians to identify those women with BC who are at elevated risk of poor prognosis.

Methods

Study design, setting and participants

To fulfil the aim of the project, a single-center cross-sectional study was carried out at the Regina Elena National Cancer Institute of Rome between June 2012 and July 2014. Forty-four pre or postmenopausal patients with newly diagnosed early BC were enrolled and assessed after 30–40 days from surgery (mastectomy or quadrantectomy) of primary tumor before the start of adjuvant chemotherapy. Inclusion criteria: age >18 years, node positive and triple negative, HER2+ or HER2 + /HR- (meaning that patients could belong to any one of the following subtypes: triple-negative, HER2 + , or HER2 + /HR–); pre-or post-menopausal status; exclusion criteria included ipsilateral breast and/or axillary recurrences and/or a new contralateral breast cancer diagnosed after previous primary tumor; use of medications known to alter HPA axis and immune-regulation, or antidepressants in the preceding 2 months before diagnosis. The reporting process was guided by the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis + Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD + AI) guidelines [30].

Ethical considerations

The study was reviewed and approved by the Local Ethical Committee at Istituto Superiore di Sanità, Rome Italy (Reference number: CE11/315); a written informed consent was obtained from all patients enrolled. All clinical investigations have been conducted according to the principles expressed in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Diagnostic criteria

Tumors were graded using the Bloom and Richardson scoring system, as moderately (G2) and poorly differentiated (G3) carcinomas. Hormonal Receptor status and HER2 status were defined based on the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guidelines [31]. Staging was performed by following the Union Internationale Contre le Cancer tumor-node-metastasis system criteria [32].

Data collection

At the time of enrollment, breast tissue removed during surgery of all admitted patients underwent a deep inspection to identify the presence of estrogen receptors (ER) and progesterone receptors (PgR) as well as human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2). Age, body mass index (BMI) and type of surgery were collected as covariates for controlling the confounding effects on biomarkers and psychological state, as well as cancer staging and grading; the presence or absence of ER, PgR and HER2 were collected for controlling the severity of BC.

Moreover, all patients underwent blood sampling from the cubital vein in order to determine serum concentrations of BDNF, Leptin, Adiponectin and immunological markers (IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12, IL-13, TNF-α, CCL2/MCP1 and CCL4/MIP-1β). Salivary samples were collected to assess the circadian rhythm of cortisol concentration at 08.00 a.m., 12.00 a.m. and 06.00 p.m. and specific questionnaires were completed by patients to assess: depressive symptoms (BDI-II), anxiety (STAI-Y), and the impact of stress event (IES-R) (Fig. 1A).

Assessment of cancer-related receptors presence

The immunohistochemical assessment of ER and PgR as well as HER2 was performed in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissues as previously described [33]. ER and PgR expression was analyzed using the mAbs 6F11 and 1A6 (Novocastra, Menarini, Florence, Italy), respectively, whereas HER2 immunostaining was performed using the polyclonal antibody A0485 (Dako, Milan, Italy). Immunoreactions were revealed by a streptavidin-biotin enhanced immunoperoxidase technique (Super Sensitive MultiLink, Menarini) in an automated autostainer. Diaminobenzidine was used as chromogenic substrate.

Measurement of BDNF, leptin and adiponectin

Venous blood (5 ml) was collected in Serum Blood Collection Tubes (BD Vacutainer, USA) on the day on which psychological assessment was performed. After standing for 30 min, the clotted blood was centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min at room temperature. The serum was stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

The amount of BDNF in each sample was assessed by the Promega BDNF Emax ImmunoAssay System (Promega Co., Madison, WI), following manufacturer instructions as previously described [34] and reported in ng/ml.

Human leptin and adiponectin levels were measured using commercially available Elisa Kits (Aviva Systems Biology for leptin; B-Bridge International for adiponectin) following manufacturer instructions. Leptin levels are reported in ng/ml, while adiponectin levels are reported in µg/ml.

Measurement of salivary cortisol

Cortisol was assessed in the saliva to cause minimal distress and to facilitate “self” collection. Salivette devices were used, consisting of a small cotton swab fitted into a plastic holder that rests inside a centrifuge tube.

Patients collected their samples at home at 08:00 a.m., 12:00 a.m. and 06:00 p.m. on 2 consecutive days, and completed a questionnaire to assess medications taken. Samples were initially stored by the patients at −20 °C and brought to the clinic at the time of the subsequent visit, when they were centrifuged and frozen at −80 °C until assayed for cortisol. Cortisol measurement was performed using an immunometric assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions (DiaSorin). Salivary cortisol levels are reported in ng/ml.

Measurement of serum cytokines/chemokines/growth factors

The simultaneous quantitative measurement of cytokines/chemokines/growth factors in small amounts of human serum was achieved by using xMAP multiplex technology (Luminex) and a Bio-Plex 200 System equipped with the magnetic washer workstation Bio-Plex ProTM and the Manager Software version 6.1 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc). The concentration of IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12, IL-13, TNF-α, CL2/MCP1 and CCL4/MIP-1β was calculated from the standard curve and expressed in terms of pg/ml. The mean intra- and inter- assay variability was < 10% for all the cytokines assessed.

Psychological assessment

Beck depression inventory-II (BDI-II)

This is a 21-question multiple-choice self-report inventory, used for diagnosing depressive disorders that includes items measuring cognitive, affective, somatic and vegetative symptoms of depression. The 21 items are rated from 0 (not at all) to 3 (extreme form of each symptom). The score range is 0–63, the higher score indicating greater depressive symptoms. In particular: 0–13 minimal range, 14–19 mild depression, 20–28 moderate depression, 29–63 severe depression [35, 36]. We set a cut-off point ≥ 20 to identify those subjects with overall elevated depressive symptoms (see below).

State-Trait anxiety inventory (STAI-Y)

It is a widely used scale designed for measure via self-report the presence and severity of current symptoms of anxiety and a generalized propensity to be anxious. The STAI-Y has 40 items, 20 items allocated to each of the State-Anxiety (STAI-Y1) and Trait-Anxiety (STAI-Y2) subscales. Responses for the STAI-Y1 scale assess intensity of current feelings “at this moment”: 1) not at all, 2) somewhat, 3) moderately so, 4) very much so. Responses for the STAI-Y2 scale assess frequency of feelings “in general”: 1) almost never, 2) sometimes, 3) often, 4) almost always. Range of scores for each subtest is 20–80, the higher score indicating greater anxiety; a cut-off point of 40 has been suggested to detect clinically significant symptoms for the State-Anxiety scale, meaning that a score > 40 identifies subjects with high anxiety symptoms [37, 38].

Impact of the event scale–revised (IES-R)

This is a self-report questionnaire used for detecting the presence of symptoms resulting from exposure to a specific traumatic event in the week preceding its administration, consisting of 22 items on a four-score scale where 0 stands for “not at all”, 1 for “a little bit”, 2 for “moderately”, 3 for “quite a bit” and 4 for “extremely”. It has three subscales that evaluate the level of intrusion, avoidance and hyperarousal perceived by the patient. The total score is obtained by combining the three subscales, higher scores indicate greater levels of symptoms. The cut-off is set at 33, meaning that a score≥ 33 identifies subjects with high stress symptoms [39].

Development of the statistical model

All variables were checked for normal distribution (Shapiro-Wilk test) and successively tested for the presence of outliers using the Grubbs’ test, which detects one outlier at a time in a set of data. The outliers for each biological variable were replaced with the new highest or lowest value (if the outlier was the maximum or minimum value of the set, respectively) in order to be conservative on the original data distribution for further analysis (winsorization method). Repeated measures deriving from cortisol assessment (08:00 a.m., 12:00 a.m. and 06:00 p.m, average of the two days) were summarized as the area under curve (AUC).

A principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on centered and scaled data in order to avoid bias due to different units of measure and ranges: biological variables included were BDNF, leptin, adiponectin and immunological markers levels, as well as the AUC from cortisol in order to extract new factors as a linear combination of the original variables. Then, the individual scores for factors with eigenvalue > 1 were analysed with one-way parametric analysis of variance (ANOVA), stratifying patients according to the severity of depressive symptoms (DS), anxiety symptoms (AS) and stress symptoms (SS) to assess the association between the new variables and the psychological status of patients. In more detail, we carried out three different and independent stratifications: subjects with a BDI-II score ≥ 20 were defined as High-DS; subjects with a STAI-Y score > 40 as High-AS; subjects with a IES-R score ≥ 33 as High-SS (Fig. 1B).

Moreover, a binary logistic regression analysis was performed to investigate the influence of the immune-metabolic profile of patients on the occurrence of a specific psychological condition. In more details, the independent variables included in each logistic regression model were selected according to the PCA results: the biological variables with loadings > |0.5| and square cosine (cos2) > 0.3 for each PC factor that showed an association with patients’ psychological status (as assessed by the stratified ANOVA) were checked for multicollinearity with Pearson’s correlation test and selected for logistic regression if they showed a low correlation coefficient (Fig. 1C). Age, BMI, cancer staging and grading, type of surgery, and positivity for ER, PgR and HER2 were considered as covariates to control the confounding effects. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was used to assess the logistic regression models’ goodness-of-fit; receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were built to calculate the AUCs, sensitivities and specificities of independent variables for predicting the psychological state. The optimal cut-off points were identified by maximizing the closest-top left combination of sensitivity and 1-specificity and a classification formula have been derived from the coefficients of the models (Fig. 1D).

Finally, to test the stability of our results, and thus estimate the generalization ability of our model, we performed the leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) and calculated F1 scores (Fig. 1E). Multiple linear regression analyses were also performed to strengthen the association between biological markers and psychological symptoms based on the specific biosignature identified, in order to evaluate the influence of immune-metabolic variations on continuous BDI-II, STAI-Y and IES-R scale scores. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A level of p < 0.05 was chosen as statistically significant; for p < 0.1 lower order effects were also examined.

Results

Clinico-pathological features in patients with BC

Sociodemographic and clinical features of enrolled patients are summarized in Table 1. Participants were equally distributed with regard to BMI, with only three women being obese. Most of the participants had stage II BC at the time of diagnosis, while there was an equal proportion of grade G2 and G3 BC. The 55.8% of patients were subjected to total mastectomy. Moreover, 79.1% of participants were positive to ER and 67.4% were positive to PgR; by contrast the 81.4% of patients were negative to HER2. Only seven patients were characterized by a triple-negative tumor. Mean and standard deviation (SD) of the biological variables collected and included in the analyses can be found in Tab. S1.

The principal component analysis generated five new factors based on the biological variables of BC patients

The PCA generated five components with eigenvalues > 1 that explained 76% of the total variance (Tab. S2). PC1 was mainly positively associated to leptin, IL-7, IL-8 and IL-13 and CCL2, and negatively to adiponectin, showing all of them a cos2 greater than 0.3. PC2 was mainly positively associated to IL-5, IL-12 and TNF-α; PC3 was positively associated to IL-6 and the cortisol AUC; PC4 was positively related to BDNF and negatively to IL-6, while PC5 positively to CCL4 and negatively to IL-10, overall showing a great separation of variables and a little overlap (Fig. 2, Fig. S1). The biplots clearly showed that depressive symptoms were a main clustering factor grouping High-DS subjects on the negative side of PC1 and on the positive side of PC2. High-DS subjects were characterized by low levels of leptin (and high levels of adiponectin) and distributed levels of IL-5, IL-10, IL-12 and IL-13; the contrary held true for Low-DS patients (Fig. 3A). As for anxiety symptoms, PC3 appears to be the main discriminating factor, since Low-AS mainly clustered on the negative side of PC3 while High-AS patients mainly clustered on its positive side; these latter subjects were characterized by high levels of IL-6 and TNF-α and low levels of cortisol (Fig. 3B). No significant differences were observed for the other PCs. By contrast, when clustering for stress symptoms, we observed that High-SS subjects were moved to the positive side of PC4 and PC5, where high levels of BDNF and CCL4 can be found, while Low-SS subjects were mainly located in the central area of the biplot (Fig. 3C).

PCA factors show associations with specific psychological symptoms

Results from ANOVA performed on the individual PC scores confirmed what was observed in the biplots. More in detail, High-DS patients showed significantly lower PC1 scores when compared to the Low-DS group and an opposite trend was observed for PC2 scores (F(1,42) = 4.8641, 3.7026; p = 0.0329, 0.0611 for PC1 and PC2, respectively). No significant difference was observed for PC3, PC4 and PC5 scores with regard to depressive symptoms (F(1,42) = 0.4714, 1.7865, 1.7475; p = 0.4961, 0.1886, 0.1933; Fig. 3D; data not shown for PC5). Interestingly, when stratifying subjects for anxiety symptoms, a strong trend was observed for PC3, with High-AS subjects showing higher individual scores compared to the Low-AS group (F(1,42) = 3.9679; p = 0.0529). No significant difference was observed for PC1, PC2, PC4 and PC5 scores with regard to anxiety (F(1,42) = 0.0041, 0.7416, 0.1551, 1.8326; p = 0.9494, 0.3940, 0.6957, 0.1831; Fig. 3E; data not shown for PC5). By contrast, a significant effect of stress was found for PC4 only (F(1,42) = 4.3307; p = 0.0436), with High-SS subjects showing higher PC scores. No significant difference was observed for the other PCs (F(1,42) = 0.1436, 0.0090, 1.0269, 1.5232; p = 0.7066, 0.9247, 0.3167, 0.2240 for PC1, PC2, PC3 and PC54, respectively; Fig. 3F; data not shown for PC5).

The logistic regressions strengthen the associations between biological variables and psychological symptoms

The binomial logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between biological markers and specific psychological conditions. Thus, variables correlating with PC1 and PC2 were chosen as independent factors for depressive symptoms (1 for High-DS and 0 for Low-DS, as stated in materials and methods). When checked for multicollinearity with Pearson’s correlation test the only biological variables kept in the model were leptin, IL-5 and IL-12 were kept in the model (Fig. S2A). Likewise, variables correlating with PC3 were chosen as independent factors for anxiety symptoms (1 for High-AS and 0 for Low-AS): AUC from cortisol and IL-6 were kept in the model (Fig. S2B). BDNF was chosen as independent factor for stress symptoms (1 for High-SS and 0 for Low-SS), being the only variable correlating with PC4. Age, BMI, cancer staging and grading, type of surgery, and positivity for ER, PgR and HER2 were added as covariates in all the three models to control the confounding effects.

Depressive symptoms are associated with elevated BMI and IL-5 and low leptin levels

The full model revealed a significant inverse association between leptin levels and higher depressive symptoms (OR: 0.8633, 95% CI: 0.7604 – 0.9800; p = 0.0231) and a slight trend for the direct influence of BMI (OR: 1.3471, 95% CI: 0.9465 – 1.9174; p = 0.0980; Tab. S3). Thus, we next generated the second model by keeping only BMI as covariate. Interestingly, both leptin (OR: 0.8704, 95% CI: 0.7830 – 0.9675; p = 0.0101) and BMI (OR: 1.2641, 95% CI: 1.0096 – 1.5826; p = 0.0410) were significantly associated with higher depressive symptoms (the former inversely, the latter directly), while only a trend for IL-5 was found (OR: 2.1056, 95% CI: 0.8773 – 5.0535; p = 0.0955; Tab. S4). To further strengthen the model, a third analysis was conducted with leptin, IL-5 and BMI as independent variables, reaching a significant chi-square value (χ²(3) = 16.51; p = 0.0009), revealing a main association with depressive symptoms. In particular, each additional increment of leptin levels decreased the odds of developing depression by 13% (leptin OR: 0.8725, 95% CI: 0.7874 – 0.9668; p = 0.0092), suggesting that low levels of this adipokine might be predictive of an increased vulnerability towards depression in the specific context of this biosignature. By contrast, both high IL-5 levels (OR: 1.7508, 95% CI: 0.7863 – 3.8982; p = 0.1703) and high BMI (OR: 1.2551, 95% CI: 1.0105 – 1.5588; p = 0.0399) increased the risk of developing depression in BC subjects (Fig. 4A, Tab. S5). It is important to note that, although IL-5 did not reach a statistical significance it strengthened the model, as confirmed by both the Hosmer-Lemeshow test for goodness-of-fit (p = 0.6951), as well as by a robust ROC curve with an AUC of 0.8611 (95% CI: 0.7478 – 0.9745; Fig. 4A). Moreover, based on the maximized closest-top left combination, a cut-off value of 0.4208 was identified with a sensitivity of 81% and a specificity of 78%, as a combination of leptin, IL-5 and BMI. In addition, the LOOCV resulted in a classification accuracy of 72% (sensibility: 75%, specificity: 70%, F1 score: 0.7261), strengthening the good performance of the model.

Thus, when this formula is used in the clinical practice, a score equal to or greater than the identified cut-off value (0.4208) may suggest the presence of depressive symptoms in women who have undergone surgery for a primary BC, before the start of chemotherapy.

Similarly, when conducting a linear regression investigating the association between leptin, IL-5 and BMI with depressive symptoms continuous scores, as assessed using the BDI-II psychiatric scale, a significant model was found (F(1,39) = 4.170; p = 0.0118), with reducing leptin levels being significantly associated with higher score of depression (β = −0.7322; p = 0.0029) while IL-5 and BMI did not reach statistical significance (Tab. S6).

Anxiety symptoms are associated with low cortisol level, elevated IL-6 and positivity for HER2

The full model reveals that cortisol levels are negatively associated with anxiety symptoms (OR: 0.9615, 95% CI: 0.8310 – 1.1124; p = 0.0246); moreover, a strong trend for the direct influence of HER2 presence was found (OR: 26.0906, 95% CI: 0.7740 – 879.4295; p = 0.0692), while the other variables did not affect the model (Tab. S7). Thus, a subsequent model was developed keeping only cortisol, IL-6 and HER2 as independent variables (Fig. 4B, Tab. S8). Also in this case, cortisol levels were inversely associated with anxiety symptoms (OR: 0.9709, 95% CI: 0.9430 – 0.9995; p = 0.0462), while IL-6 and HER2 receptor contributed to strengthen the model (χ²(3) = 8.47; p = 0.0372) without a significant association (OR: 1.0258, 95% CI: 0.9081 – 1.1587; p = 0.6816 for IL-6; OR: 5.2898, 95% CI: 0.4715 – 59.3475; p = 0.1769 for HER2). In more detail, the odds ratio for cortisol reveals that lower levels of this stress hormone are indicative of anxiety symptoms, as well as high IL-6 levels and positivity for HER2. Notwithstanding these results, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test for goodness-of-fit (p = 0.2920) and the ROC curve (AUC: 0.7333, 95% CI: 0.5727 – 0.8939; Fig. 4B) both show that this model has a poor ability to detect an association between the selected biological markers and anxiety symptoms. Indeed, a cut-off value of 0.6888 with a sensitivity of 67% and a specificity of 69% was obtained by maximizing the closest top-left combination. Moreover, the LOOCV resulted in a classification accuracy of 60.5% (sensibility: 63.3%, specificity: 53.8%, F1 score: 0.5821), confirming the weakness of the model. Also the linear regression model did not reach statistical significance, confirming the poor ability of cortisol, IL-6 and HER2 presence to be used as markers for discriminating subjects at high-risk of anxiety (F(3,39) = 1.118; p = 0.3535; Tab. S9).

Stress symptoms are not associated with specific biological markers

No independent variable reached statistical significance in the full model, which considers BDNF and all the covariates (Tab. S10). Likewise, the logistic regression analysis with only BDNF as an independent variable revealed that this does not discriminate between High-SS and Low-SS patients (model χ²(1) = 1.27; p = 0.2596; Tab. S11). The ROC curve with an AUC of 0.6226 (95% CI: 0.4200 – 0.8252; Fig. 4C) further confirms a lack of association between BDNF levels and the manifestation of stress symptoms in BC patients. The LOOCV was not performed in this case due to the lack of a significant model. The non-association was also confirmed by the linear regression model (F(1,42) = 1.601; p = 0.2127; Tab. S12).

Discussion

Women with BC often show symptoms of depression and anxiety with a higher prevalence compared to the general population, and these features have been associated with a poor prognosis, suggesting that mood disorders can precipitate the course of the disease through a bidirectional brain-body communication [13–15, 20]. Indeed, a large body of research clearly indicates that the same biological pathways relevant for mood disorders may also underlie tumorigenesis [40–42]

Our study was aimed at identifying signatures of emotional disruption (depression, anxiety and stress) and mental health vulnerability in women with BC, assessing specific indices of psychological and metabolic stress as well as inflammation. By undertaking a rigorous statistical approach, we were able to identify a biosignature of depression with a specific cut-off value in a population of women newly diagnosed with BC.

Our results show that depressive symptoms are significantly associated with low leptin and high IL-5 levels together with high BMI scores; anxiety symptoms are associated with positivity for HER2, low levels of CORT and high levels of IL-6; the biosignature identified for stress symptoms, although suggesting a link with BDNF, was not confirmed by the cross-validation, possibly due to the limited sample size. Worth noticing, when we carried out the leave-one-out cross-validation, to test the stability of our results, only the composite immune-metabolic biosignature, accounting for the severity of depressive symptoms, survived this further checkpoint, allowing for the identification of a cut-off value able to discriminate subjects with depressive symptomatology.

Depression and, in general, mood disorders are often underdiagnosed in women with BC. This is usually linked to a prioritization, in clinical practice, of oncologic treatments over psychological care, but also because of a certain degree of overlap between BC-related neurovegetative and depressive/anxiety symptoms (e.g.: fatigue, sleep alterations, changes in appetite) [20, 43, 44]. Recently, Ho and co-workers have shown that circulating levels of cytokines hold potential for the identification of depression in BC patients [45].

It is important to underline here that individual biomarkers can hardly capture the complexity of mood disorders, which are characterized by great etiological and phenotypic heterogeneity. A broader and more integrated biosignature, relying upon clusters of interrelated biomarkers, is needed to fully capture the complexity of brain-body communication [46]. In the present study, applying this concept, we have identified an immune-metabolic biosignature where elevated IL-5 and BMI, together with low leptin levels, are associated with a depressive symptomatology.

IL-5 is a pro-inflammatory cytokine, often under-investigated, whose peripheral levels have been shown to be consistently associated with depressive symptoms in cancer patients [47]. Recent evidence suggest IL-5 may be a mediator at the crossroad between energy metabolism and immune signaling pathways increasing the interest for this cytokine as a candidate molecule to be further investigated in the specific context of immunometabolic depression [48].

Elomaa and co-workers have provided evidence for increased IL-5 serum levels in subjects suffering from depression [49] while Shelton and colleagues [50] have shown that there are elevated IL-5 levels in the post-mortem brains of depressed patients, suggesting that the increased inflammatory state observed in the peripheral circulation is reflective of what happens in the central nervous system. As for leptin, this is a white adipose tissue-derived hormone, primarily regulating energy homeostasis by promoting satiety and energy expenditure [51], but it is also involved in immuno-modulatory processes [52]. Leptin receptors are found in brain areas involved in emotional and cognitive behavior, supporting a main role of this hormone as a link between the body’s adipose mass and the brain [53–55]. Playing a complex role in the communication between peripheral and central tissues, clinical evidence has so far reported conflicting results, suggesting that in humans the relationship between leptin secretion and depression is highly context-dependent [56].

Milaneschi and co-workers have provided evidence for leptin resistance in depressed patients, suggesting that disrupted metabolic homeostasis, rather than leptin levels per se, might represent a greater risk factor for depression [57]. In line with this evidence, we found that an elevated BMI was a risk factor for depression in the face of reduced circulating leptin levels, suggesting that BC might disrupt metabolic homeostasis, affecting the leptin signaling cascade.

The markers we identified are all related to inflammation and metabolic function. Growing evidence suggests the existence of an immune-metabolic depression biotype that is characterized by a chronic over-activation of the immune system in relation to dysregulated glucose, lipid and energy metabolism [58]. Such atypical depression type is mostly found in women, being often comorbid with obesity, cardiovascular disease, type II diabetes, suggesting that derangements in energy homeostasis might not only play a key role in the etiopathogenesis of this form of depression but might also represent a shared underpinning with the observed comorbidities [59–61]. Metabolic reprogramming might also involve peripheral immune cells of depressed patients [62]. This piece of data suggests that the immune system might affect health outcomes by mirroring what occurs in the tumor environment, leading also to comorbid conditions such as depression and BC. Our immune-metabolic biosignature is in line with the complex interactions dealing with both inflammation and changes in metabolic homeostasis that might underlie the feedforward mechanism linking depression and both recurrence and specific mortality in BC patients described in the meta-analysis of Wang and co-workers [13].

Although characterized by reduced sensitivity and specificity, we have also identified an immune-endocrine signature related to anxiety. This piece of data is in line with evidence provided by O’Donovan and colleagues showing reduced (morning) cortisol and elevated IL-6 serum levels in a population of anxious subjects [63]. Worth mentioning, the anxiety biosignature is the only one that was modulated by a specific cancer-related factor (HER-2).

Our study has some limitations one of the main being the small sample size that might have negatively affected the biosignature of anxiety and stress. Moreover, given the explorative nature characterizing the study, a large number of comparisons across biomarkers and psychological outcomes has been carried out. In this context, we have purposely avoided multiple testing that, although appropriate to exclude potentially spurious correlations, could have been too conservative, possibly leading to a lack of relevant associations. It is also important to underline that the nature of the experimental design (cross-sectional study) does not allow establishing causality. Furthermore we cannot disregard that other immuno-modulating mechanism relating cancer, depression, inflammation and cellular metabolic homeostasis might be at play, as a main role has been previously assigned to the kynurenine pathway [20].

Overall, our immune-metabolic biosignature holds potential for the timely identification of those individuals for whom depressive symptoms are sustained by a deranged immune-metabolic milieu and might therefore be at higher risk of poorer health outcomes (Fig. 5). The observed biosignature might reflect an acute reactive condition triggered by tumor microenvironment, rather than a chronic one, characterizing depressed patients where persistent homeostatic changes occur throughout time. As depression and BC show a higher prevalence among women and the genetic, hormonal, and developmental sex may impact the function and properties of the immune system [64] we can speculate that our biosignature is specific for women. However, future studies might be devoted to investigate this issue in cohort of patients with different cancer types where the prevalence between men and women are similar.

Immune-metabolic biosignature of depression in women with breast cancer.

Mood disorders may greatly jeopardize health trajectories in patients with breast cancer (BC). The identification of biologically relevant markers of comorbidities between BC and mood disorders may spotlight high-risk subjects. We have identified an immune-metabolic biosignature for depressive symptoms relying upon low leptin levels, as well as elevated BMI and IL-5 levels characterizing BC patients that hold potential to derive interventions strategies in a dimension of precision medicine.

Being time-specific - i.e., before chemotherapy - and characterized by a cut-off value, this biosignature has the potential to inform the selection of individualised pharmacological interventions [29]. Furthermore, identifying patients who are at higher risk of developing anxiety and depression would be important to improve the quality of life of breast cancer survivors.

Future studies will need to consider the enrollment of a larger cohort and the adoption of a longitudinal design to validate the predictive ability of our biosignature.
