Gestational stress disrupts the neuroimmune environment in the nucleus accumbens of maternal rats
Authors: Courtney N Dye, Dominic V Franceschelli, Caitlin Goodpaster, Lynde M Wangler, Amanda Ringland, Jonathan P Godbout, Kathryn M Lenz, Benedetta Leuner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPostpartum depression (PPD) affects 20 % of new mothers. Little is known about what contributes to this prevalence, though one of the strongest risk factors for developing PPD is stress during pregnancy. Pregnancy and the postpartum period are accompanied by dramatic changes in the endocrine, nervous, and immune systems, which may confer heightened vulnerability to maternal mental illness. Microglia, the brain's main immune cells, are important for displaying maternal behaviors. Environmental insults to microglia, such as stress, lead to hormonal dysregulation and aberrant synaptic pruning, both of which have been implicated in the pathophysiology of mood disorders like depression. Given these links, we hypothesized that stress during pregnancy would lead to dysregulated neuroimmune and endocrine profiles and produce synaptic changes in the maternal brain. We used a 2-week variable stress model in pregnancy and assessed neuroimmune changes in the nucleus accumbens (NAc), a brain region that regulates functions impaired in PDD including mood and maternal caregiving. Peripheral hormones and cytokines were also measured. While peripheral cytokine levels were not affected by stress exposure, gestational stress induced central immune changes in the NAc of maternal rats. This included increased microglia immunolabeling and changes in pro- and anti-inflammatory transcripts measured by a Nanostring nCounter panel. Astrocytes also increased in the NAc following gestational stress exposure. Peripheral estradiol and progesterone concentrations were reduced in late pregnancy of stressed mothers, and at the transcript level, hormone receptor and synthesis molecules were altered in the NAc. Gestational stress also altered transcripts associated with synapses and synaptic plasticity during late pregnancy and the postpartum period. There was no impact of stress on engulfment of pre- and postsynaptic proteins by microglia. However, microglia engulfed more of the postsynaptic marker, PSD95, in late pregnancy relative to postpartum, indicating a potential role for microglia in remodeling synapses in the NAc. Overall, these studies provide novel evidence that gestational stress impacts endocrine, synaptic and neuroimmune factors in the maternal NAc, suggesting possible mechanisms by which stress during pregnancy contributes to peripartum mood disorders.Keywords: Nucleus accumbens, Maternal, Pregnancy, Microglia, Stress, Hormone, Astrocyte

Introduction

Peripartum mental illnesses are debilitating disorders that have significant consequences for both mothers and their offspring (Field, 2010; Osborne and Monk, 2013). The most prevalent of these disorders is Postpartum Depression (PPD), which affects 20 % of new mothers and is characterized by symptoms including low mood, disrupted cognition, and impaired mother-offspring interactions (Field, 2010; Meltzer-Brody et al., 2018). Recent advancements of PPD-specific treatments have improved care for some mothers experiencing PPD, most notably the drugs Brexanolone and Zuranolone. Brexanonlone and Zuranolone are synthetic versions of allopregnanolone, a hormone elevated in pregnancy that acts as an allostatic modulator of the GABA receptor (Frieder et al., 2019; Deligiannidis et al., 2023). However, PPD is a heterogeneous disorder, including variations in symptom severity and timing of onset (PACT Consortium, 2015). Thus, more investigation is needed to develop treatments to support all mothers, who may present with a variety of phenotypes.

Modeling PPD in rodents has provided insight into the mechanisms responsible for impaired postpartum mood and caregiving. Studies employing hormone simulated pregnancies followed by hormonal withdrawal have shown that abrupt decreases in ovarian hormones surrounding parturition can contribute to postpartum mood dysregulation (Galea et al., 2001; Suda et al., 2008). Chronic stress exposure during pregnancy is another avenue for investigating PPD etiology (Perani and Slattery, 2014; Li and Chou, 2016). Not only do gestational stress models provide strong construct validity, as stress during pregnancy is a major risk factor for later developing PPD (Robertson et al., 2004; Milgrom et al., 2008; Guintivano et al., 2018), they also recapitulate a behavioral phenotype consistent with symptoms of PPD seen in humans (Perani and Slattery, 2014; Biaggi et al., 2016). We and others have found that chronic stress exposure during pregnancy elicits depressive-like behaviors, impaired cognition, and deficits in maternal care in the postpartum period (Haim et al., 2014, 2016, 2025; Leuner et al., 2014; Perani and Slattery, 2014; Li and Chou, 2016; Tan et al., 2018; Zoubovsky et al., 2020; Gorman-Sandler et al., 2023). An understanding of the neurobiology underlying stress-induced postpartum behavioral changes, however, remains incomplete.

A brain region that is of particular of relevance to PPD is the nucleus accumbens (NAc). The NAc is critically involved in reward and motivated behaviors including maternal care (Li and Fleming, 2003; Champagne et al., 2004; Numan, 2006). The NAc also has an established role in the development of both PPD and depression occurring outside of the peripartum period (Nestler and Carlezon, 2006; Heshmati and Russo, 2015; Francis and Lobo, 2017; Post and Leuner, 2018; Jiang et al., 2023). Synaptic remodeling in the NAc is associated with depressive-like behavior in animal models using chronic stress (Brancato et al., 2017; Heshmati et al., 2020). Structural plasticity in the NAc is also sensitive to gestational stress such that mothers stressed during pregnancy exhibit reductions in dendritic spines, dendritic branch points, and dendritic length in the NAc (Haim et al., 2014, 2016). The underlying factors mediating synaptic loss in the maternal NAc following gestational stress have yet to be determined.

One candidate mechanism that could contribute to stress-induced neuronal alterations in the maternal brain is the neuroimmune system. Dysregulation of the peripheral immune system has been linked to PPD (Corwin and Pajer, 2008; Dye et al., 2022; Worthen and Beurel, 2022; Levin and Ein-Dor, 2023), though less is known about central immune disruptions and their potential impact on PPD development. Microglia, the innate immune cells of the brain, are dynamic in the peripartum period (Posillico and Schwarz, 2016; Haim et al., 2017) and are important for the display of maternal caregiving behavior (Dye et al., 2023). In other stress models, increased microglia number and activity, either through changes in the secretion of inflammatory mediators or phagocytosis, can lead to synaptic loss and depressive-like outcomes (Hodes et al., 2015; Wood et al., 2015; Wohleb et al., 2018; Biltz et al., 2022; Afridi and Suk, 2023). Thus, it is possible microglia are impacted by gestational stress exposure and, in turn, remodel synaptic elements following gestational stress.

Astrocytes, another immunocompetent cell, also fluctuate in morphology and number in the maternal brain (Theodosis and Poulain, 1984, 1989, 2001; Salmaso et al., 2005). Little is known about how maternal astrocytes respond to gestational perturbations such as stress, though outside of the peripartum period astrocytes have been implicated in stress-induced changes in synapses as well as depressive-like behavior (Campioni et al., 2009; Miguel-Hidalgo, 2022; Aten et al., 2023; Holt et al., 2024). Therefore, astrocytes are another component of the neuroimmune system that may be sensitive to stress during pregnancy.

Here, we investigated the impact of gestational stress on the neuroimmune milieu in the NAc. We hypothesized that gestational stress would disrupt the maternal neuroimmune environment and that changes in neuroimmune function would be associated with decreases in synaptic components. We also sought to characterize the extent to which our model of gestational stress alters endocrine function, as pregnancy and stress elicit complex changes in both neuroinflammatory signaling and hormonal profiles, which could in turn influence one another (Magon and Kumar, 2012; Straub, 2014; Yilmaz et al., 2019). We found that, while peripheral cytokine levels were not altered by stress exposure, there was evidence of central immune changes in the NAc of maternal rats that were exposed to gestational stress. Peripheral and central endocrine measures were also altered following gestational stress exposure, as were transcripts for synaptic markers. We did not find evidence of microglia differentially phagocytosing synaptic markers following stress. However, there was an effect of peripartum timepoint on microglial engulfment of the postsynaptic protein, PSD95, providing novel evidence of microglia remodeling of synapses in the maternal brain. Together, these studies indicate that stress during pregnancy induces endocrine alterations along with synaptic and neuroimmune dysregulation in the NAc, suggesting possible mechanisms by which stress may contribute to pathophysiology of PPD.

Methods

Animals

All procedures were conducted in accordance with The Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health, approved by The Ohio State University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, and reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. Adult female timed pregnant or nulliparous adult female Sprague Dawley rats (9–12 weeks of age; Charles River) were single housed upon arrival and provided with nesting material. Timed pregnant females were delivered on gestational day (GD) 4. All animals were kept in the same temperature- and humidity-controlled room maintained on a 12/12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 6am). Food and water were available ad libitum. The exception to this were two, 12-h bouts of overnight food deprivation for animals assigned to the stress condition. Five cohorts of rats were used to complete these experiments: Cohort 1 - blood collection for peripheral hormone and cytokine analyses; Cohort 2 - immunolabeling of immune and synaptic proteins; Cohort 3 - qPCR quantification of synaptic transcripts; Cohort 4 - Nanostring analysis of immune, endocrine, and synaptic transcripts; Cohort 5 - immunolabeling of immune proteins in nulliparous females. In a subset of rats (Cohorts 2 and 3), maternal weight change was recorded throughout the experiment and litter size recorded on the day of parturition. Pup weights were obtained from cohort 1. Stress during pregnancy can lead to in utero fetal resorption (Sugino et al., 1994). Uteri were examined at the time of sacrifice for fetal reabsorption (Hashem et al., 2019) when brain tissue was collected for the GD21 endpoint, but there was little sign of resorption (1 instance across all animals); accordingly there was no quantification or statistical analysis of this parameter.

Gestational stress protocol

Fig. 1A depicts the experimental timeline. For all cohorts, pregnant dams or nulliparous females were assigned via random number generator to receive unpredictable chronic mild stress or control handling for 14 days. For pregnant dams, stress occurred from GD7-20, as in our previous work (Haim et al., 2025). Nulliparous females assigned to the stress condition began stress exposure 3 days after arrival to our facilities, in line with the time frame occurring for the timed pregnant dams. Stressors included: restraint, cage tilt, damp bedding, food deprivation, shaker, or foot shock. Animals received either two, shorter duration stressors a day, or one stressor that occurred for 12 h. If two stressors were given on one day, one stressor was given in the morning (8am-12pm) and another in the afternoon (1pm–6pm), with at least 2 h undisturbed time between stressors. All morning and afternoon stressors were conducted under white light. When the stress exposure was performed overnight, animals did not have exposure to stress in the afternoon prior or the following morning. Animals in the control condition were handled for 5 min daily.

For restraint, animals were placed in a Broome style rodent restrainer (554-BSRR; Plas Labs) for either 45 min or 2 h. The restrainer was secured such that the rat could not turn around but had adequate breathing room. Animals were checked every 15 min to ensure they were safely restrained and had not twisted around in the restrainer. For cage tilt, each animal's home cage was placed on an apparatus tilted at a 45° angle for 2 h. For damp bedding, one water bottle (approximately 225 ml) per cage was filled with room temperature water. The water was evenly distributed throughout the nesting and bedding material. This amount of water was sufficient to dampen the contents of the cage but not create standing water. Animals remained housed with the damp bedding for either 2 or 12 h, and at the end of the session they were transferred to a clean cage. For food deprivation, food was removed from the cages 5 min prior to lights off and placed in a bag marked clearly with the animal's identification number. Food was returned within the first 5 min of the light cycle beginning 12 h later. For shaker stress, animals were placed in a mouse cage with a small amount bedding material on an orbital shaker for 1 h at 100 rpm. Animals were checked every 20 min to ensure the cages remained safely centered on the shaker. Animals were returned to their home cages at the end of the hour. For foot shock, animals were placed in a Habitest box equipped with modular shock floors (Coulbourn) enclosed in a sound-attenuating chamber (Med Associates Inc). A 0.5 mA, 1 ms duration shock was delivered each min for 10 min, for a total of 10 shocks. Habitest boxes were cleaned with ethanol and allowed to air out for at least 10 min between animals.

Blood collection and ELISA assays

Rats from cohort 1 were sacrificed between 11am and 1pm on GD21 (n = 9-12/group) or on postpartum day (PD) 8 (n = 7-10/group). Rats were removed from their home cages and immediately sacrificed by rapid decapitation. Trunk blood was collected within 30s of approaching, opening the animal's cage, and handling the animal to minimize perturbations to basal hormone levels. Approximately 5 mL of blood was collected into non-coated polypropylene tubes (Eppendorf) for analysis of corticosterone, 17-β estradiol, progesterone, and peripheral cytokines. Samples were centrifuged at 1600×g for 20 min at room temperature, serum collected, and stored at −80 °C. An additional 1 mL of blood was collected into two individual EDTA-coated polypropylene tubes for oxytocin and prolactin analyses, as specified in the kit instructions for those analytes. Thirty min prior to sample collection, 5 μl of aprotinin was added to the tubes for oxytocin analysis which sat on ice to promote oxytocin stability. Samples for oxytocin analysis were centrifuged at 1600×g for 20 min at 4 °C, plasma collected, and stored at −80 °C. Samples for prolactin analysis were centrifuged at 1600×g for 20 min at room temperature, plasma collected, and stored at −20 °C.

Corticosterone, 17-β estradiol, progesterone concentrations from serum and oxytocin concentrations from plasma were measured using Enzo commercial ELISA kits (Enzo Life Sciences [ADI-900-097, ADI-900-008, ADI-900-011, ADI-900-153A, ADI-900-153A]). Prolactin concentrations from plasma were measured using a commercial ELISA kit (Bertin Pharma [A05101]). Peripheral cytokine concentrations from serum, including IL-1β, TNFα and IL-6, were also measured by commercial ELISA kits (R&D Systems [RLB00, RTA00, R6000B, respectively]). Analyte concentration values were calculated using standard curves generated from known protein concentrations. Assays were run in either duplicate (cytokines) or triplicate (hormones) following the manufacturers’ protocols. Intra-assay variability ranged from 5.9 to 18.9 %.

Brain collection and immunofluorescence

Rats from cohort 2 were sacrificed on GD21 (n = 6-10/group) or PD8 (n = 7-10/group). Animals were deeply anesthetized with Euthasol and transcardially perfused with 0.01M phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and 4 % paraformaldehyde in PBS. Brains were extracted, post-fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 24 h, and then placed in 30 % sucrose until sectioning. Brains were sectioned on a cryostat at 40 μm into six series and stored in wells containing cryoprotectant at −20 °C until being slide mounted for staining. One series of tissue was labeled with Ionized calcium binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba1; Abcam [Ab107159]), cluster of differentiation 68 (CD68; Biorad [MCA341R]), and postsynaptic density 95 (PSD95; Abcam [Ab18258]). This tissue was used to assess the following endpoints: total microglia cell counts, Iba1 percent area staining, microglia morphology, total CD68 staining, amount of CD68 colocalized with Iba1, total PSD95, and microglial engulfment of PSD95. An alternate series was labeled with Iba1 (Abcam [Ab107159]), CD68 (Biorad [MCA341R]), and VGLUT1 (Invitrogen [48–2400]) to assess total VGLUT1 and microglia engulfment of VGLUT1. A subset of tissue (n = 6-9/group) was stained with glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP; Sigma [G3893]) to assess astrocyte number and area labeling.

Tissue was washed in 0.01 M PBS 3 × 5 min, followed by 30 min in 50 % methanol to reduce background fluorescence. Antigen retrieval was performed using a tris-EDTA buffer (10 mM Tris base, 1 mM EDTA solution, 0.05 % Tween20, pH 9.0) for 15 min in a 90 °C water bath followed by 10 min in the buffer at room temperature. Tissue was permeabilized for 1 h with 0.4 % Triton-x100 in 0.01 M PBS (PBST) and blocked for 1 h in 5 % Normal Donkey Serum (NDS; Lampire Biological Laboratories, #7332100) in PBST. Tissue was sequentially incubated in primary antibody with 2.5 % NDS in PBST. Primary incubation with PSD95 (1:1000) or VGLUT1 (1:500) was for 48 h at 4 °C, followed by Iba1 (1:750) and CD68 (1:500) for 24 h at 4 °C. Primary incubation for GFAP (1:500) was for 24 h at 4 °C. Slides were rinsed 3 × 20 min in 0.01M PBS and secondary antibodies were simultaneously applied in 2.5 % NDS in PBST for 2 h at room temperature. Secondary antibodies that were used included: donkey anti-goat cross-adsorbed Alexa Fluor 488 ([Invitrogen, #A11055; 1:333), donkey anti-mouse cross-adsorbed Alexa Fluor 555 ([Invitrogen, #A31570]; 1:200), and donkey anti-rabbit highly cross-adsorbed Alexa Fluor Plus 647 ([Invitrogen, #A32795]; 1:200). Slides were cover-slipped with Prolong Diamond Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen, #P36970).

Rats from cohort 5 were nulliparous females that underwent the same chronic variable stress paradigm and were sacrificed 8–9 days after stress cessation, an analogous timeframe to the PD8 endpoint used in the peripartum stress studies (n = 9-10/group). Tissue collection and processing were conducted using the same protocols and reagents as the studies of the peripartum females. One series of tissue was stained with Iba1 and CD68 to analyze: Iba1 percent area staining, microglia morphology, total CD68 staining, and amount of CD68 colocalized with Iba1. A subset of tissue was stained with GFAP to analyze astrocyte number and area labeling (n = 6/group).

Imaging and engulfment analyses

Because we previously found that gestational stress effects on dendritic architecture and synaptic density were present in the NAc shell but not the NAc core (Haim et al., 2014, 2016), we focused our immunofluorescent analyses in the NAc shell. To quantify microglia and astrocytes, four to six images per animal were acquired in the NAc shell (0.9 mm ML, +1.2 mm AP, 7.5 mm DV; Paxinos and Watson, 1982). One image was acquired per tissue section and the images acquired for each animal were counterbalanced across hemispheres. Images were acquired at 10x using StereoInvestigator software (MBF Bioscience) on a Zeiss Axioimager M2 microscope and a CX9000 Digital Camera.

Quantification of the number of microglia and astrocytes, percent area of positive staining, and microglia morphological analyses were conducted in ImageJ (NIH) by a rater blind to experimental conditions. Microglia soma size was measured using the Polygon selection tool in ImageJ to generate area measurements. Eight somas per image were traced and an average size calculated per animal. To avoid bias in soma selection, gridlines of the same scale were overlaid on each image and somas that intersected with the gridlines were selected for tracing. Somas along vertical gridlines were chosen for analysis first, alternating between the left and right side as a starting point between images. If eight somas did not intersect with vertical gridlines, the same procedure was performed again following horizontal gridlines. Branching measures were generated using the ‘Analyze Skeleton’ plugin according to previously defined methods (Morrison et al., 2017). Values for number of branches, average branch length, and number of junction points were normalized to the number of microglia in the image. A z-score combining these values into one measure of branching complexity was then generated, with higher z-scores indicating more complex microglia branching and lower z-scores indicating less complex branching patterns (Gilfarb et al., 2025).

For total synaptic protein labeling, colocalization of CD68 with microglia, and all engulfment analyses, the same imaging acquisition parameters were used. Three tissue sections per animal were imaged, acquiring two images per section of NAc shell, for a total of six images acquired per animal. Z-stacks were taken through the depth of the tissue at a 0.5 μm step size interval at 63x magnification, using a Zeiss Axio Observer Z.1 with apotome digital imaging system and Axiocam 506 monochrome camera (Zeiss) in Zen Blue software (Zeiss). Images were analyzed blind to experimental conditions using Imaris software (Oxford Instruments). Briefly, background subtraction was applied to images, keeping a consistent filter width across all images and animals. Surfaces were then rendered for each fluorescent channel. This included total labeling of PSD95, VGLUT1, and CD68. Engulfment of synaptic components or colocalization of CD68 with Iba1 was determined by using the surface masking function. Statistics were exported for each surface for data analysis.

qPCR

Rats in cohort 3 were sacrificed on GD21 (n = 6-8/group) or PD8 (n = 7-10/group). Animals were deeply anesthetized with Euthasol and then transcardially perfused with 0.01M PBS to reduce the inclusion of circulating immune cells. Brains were extracted, the NAc (encompassing core and shell subregions) was immediately micro-dissected on ice, and then stored at −70 °C until RNA extraction was performed. Whole NAc samples were collected due to the technical limitations of dissecting the two subregions accurately while maintaining sample integrity. RNA was homogenized in QIAzol lysis reagent (QIAGEN [79306]) and chloroform and then extracted with a RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN [741014]). cDNA was synthesized using First Strand cDNA synthesis kit (Origene [NP100042]). qPCR was conducted for the following synaptic genes: VGLUT1, VGLUT2, synaptophysin (SYN), PSD95, and spinophilin (SPN) using Sso Advanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Biorad [1725272]) in a Bio-Rad CFX-96 thermocycler. Gene expression was normalized and reported relative to the housekeeping gene, actin beta (Actβ), which did not show differential expression between groups (data not shown; unpaired t-tests, p's > 0.05). Primer sequences that were used are provided in Table 1.

Nanostring nCounter

The same tissue collection and RNA isolation procedures used for qPCR were followed for rats in cohort 4, which were sacrificed at GD21 (n = 6/group) or PD8 (n = 6/group). RNA integrity was confirmed by BioAnalyzer (Agilent). Gene expression was quantified using a custom rat NanoString nCounter panel (350 genes total [342 target genes, 8 potential housekeeping genes]) by the OSU Comprehensive Cancer Center Genomics Shared Resource facility (The Ohio State University, Columbus, OH). We included a host of neuroimmune-related genes as well as gene sets that are known to interact with neuroinflammatory signaling, such as neuroendocrine factors, genes that guide synaptic function and signaling, and genes implicated in neuropsychiatric conditions such as depression (Supplemental files 1 and 2). Differential expression testing was completed by using DESeq2 in R according to previously defined protocols (Wangler et al., 2022). Two housekeeping genes (Ccdc127 and Mto1) were used for normalization of target genes (inclusion criterion set at correlation of individual counts with total sum of counts: r2 ≥ 0.80). EstimateSizeFactors in DeSeq2 was used for normalization to housekeeping genes. The DeSeq2 design parameter included the following two variables, one controlling for NanoString cartridge and a second for experimental group (four levels: GD21 control, GD21 stress, PD8 control, PD8 stress).

Statistical analysis

Unpaired two-tailed t-tests were conducted to compare control vs stress for the following measures: weight change during pregnancy, postpartum weight change, litter size, pup weight at PD0 and PD8, total VGLUT1, and VGLUT1 engulfment at GD21. Unpaired two-tailed t-tests were also conducted to compare control vs stress for all nulliparous immunolabeling. Because we had an a priori hypothesis based on our previous work showing spine reductions following gestational stress (Haim et al., 2014, Haim et al., 2016), one-tailed t-tests were used to analyze relative expression of synaptic transcripts generated by qPCR. Relative expression of transcripts was only statistically compared when conducted on plates containing the same internal control for normalization. Two-way ANOVAs (control vs stress, GD21 vs PD8) were used to analyze peripheral hormones, peripheral cytokines, microglia and CD68 immunofluorescence, microglia morphology measures, total PSD95, and PSD95 engulfment by microglia. Tukey's post hoc analyses were conducted when there was a significant interaction effect. Normality was confirmed via Shapiro-Wilk's testing and q-q plot visualization on ANOVA residuals. F tests were used to compare variances, and Welch's corrections were used when between group variances were unequal. After confirming assumptions were met, a Pearson's correlation was conducted between weight change during pregnancy and number of pups per litter. Statistical significance for all experiments was set at α = 0.05. In the Nanostring experiment, results tables were generated for pairwise comparisons of interest (e.g. GD21 stress vs GD21 control; PD8 stress vs PD8 control). Heatmaps of standardized z-scores of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were generated using pheatmap and volcano plots were produced using ggplot2 in R for each timepoint. Due to a priori selection of genes for inclusion on the panel, findings were not corrected for multiple comparisons. Cook's distance was used for outlier screening and no samples were excluded based on these criteria. For all other experiments, data points were considered statistical outliers when greater than ±2 standard deviations away from the mean and were removed when identified. For some analyses, additional animals were excluded due to insufficient samples or issues with staining. Effect size calculations (partial eta squared [R2]) are reported for significant effects for t and F tests. Statistics were conducted in Prism 10 (GraphPad Software; San Diego, CA) and R.

Results

Gestational stress reduced maternal weight gain during pregnancy and postnatal pup weight gain

A subset of mothers was weighed daily from GD7-GD21. Dams that experienced gestational stress gained significantly less weight during this time compared to dams who underwent control handling, t(75) = 5.87, p < 0.0001, R2 = 0.32, Fig. 1B. Animals that were sacrificed at PD8 for immunolabeling were also weighed during the postpartum period. There were no differences in postpartum weight change between conditions, t(18) = 0.10, p = 0.92, Fig. 1C. At birth, there were no differences in litter size between control and stressed mothers, t(76) = 0.51, p = 0.61, Fig. 1D. There was some variability in the amount of weight change exhibited by dams during gestation, so correlation analyses were conducted between mothers' weight change and litter size at parturition. In both control, r = 0.52, p = 0.0009, and stressed mothers, r = 0.51, p = 0.0008, litter size was positively correlated with maternal weight change during pregnancy, Fig. 1E. Pup weight was not different between litters of control compared to stressed mothers on PD0, t(16) = 0.60, p = 0.56, Fig. 1F. However, by PD8, offspring of stressed mothers weighed less than control mothers, t(16) = 2.38, p = 0.03, R2 = 0.26, Fig. 1G.

Gestational stress reduced peripheral estradiol and progesterone during late pregnancy, but did not impact other hormone or cytokine measures

We measured levels of circulating hormones that are necessary for pregnancy and maternal care, have been shown to be impacted by stress exposure, and have been implicated in PPD (Brummelte and Galea, 2010a; Brummelte and Galea, 2010b; Zelkowitz et al., 2014; Georgescu et al., 2021). There was a significant main effect of stress on 17-β estradiol concentrations, F(1, 34) = 4.84, p = 0.03, R2 = 0.12, and a significant main effect of peripartum timepoint, F(1, 34) = 15.68, p = 0.0004, R2 = 0.31. There was also a significant interaction between stress condition and timepoint, F(1, 34) = 4.58, p = 0.04, R2 = 0.12. Post hoc analyses indicated a significant reduction in estradiol at GD21 in stressed mothers compared to control mothers, p = 0.003, while there was no difference between control and stressed mothers at PD8, p = 0.98. The main effect of peripartum timepoint was driven by a significant reduction in estradiol in control mothers at PD8 compared to GD21, p = 0.0001, which was not evident in stressed mothers between the two timepoints, p = 0.21, Fig. 2A.

Prolactin levels were significantly higher at PD8 compared to GD21, F(1, 32) = 60.83, p < 0.0001, R2 = 0.66, but there was no effect of stress, F(1, 32) = 0.33, p = 0.57, or interaction effect, F(1, 32) = 0.12, p = 0.73, Fig. 2C. Peripheral corticosterone concentrations did not vary with time or stress exposure, p's = 0.28–0.83, Fig. 2E.

We also surveyed circulating levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines that have previously been implicated in other models of stress-induced depression: IL1β, TNFα, and IL6 (O'Mahony et al., 2006; Miller et al., 2009; Felger and Lotrich, 2013; Chang et al., 2024). IL1β was increased in mothers at PD8 compared to GD21, F(1, 34) = 16.70, p = 0.0003, R2 = 0.33, Fig. 2F. Conversely, TNFα was higher at GD21 compared to PD8, F(1, 33) = 677.5, p < 0.0001, R2 = 0.95, Fig. 2G. However, there was no evidence of peripheral inflammation induced by gestational stress, as neither IL1β nor TNFα concentrations changed as a result of stress exposure, p's = 0.69–0.98. IL6 levels did not vary across the peripartum period or due to stress exposure, F's = 0.07–2.37, p's = 0.13–0.46, Fig. 2H. For all peripheral cytokines, there were no significant interaction effects, p's = 0.07–0.98.

Gestational stress increased microglia and astrocyte staining in the NAc shell

Microglia are dynamic cells that may change in number and/or alter their morphology in response to environmental perturbations (Vidal-Itriago et al., 2022). To determine how microglia are impacted by gestational stress, we quantified microglia in the NAc shell using the marker Iba1. We also analyzed the percent area of positive immunostaining in the same images to assess broad morphological changes across the microglial population. There was a significant main effect of peripartum timepoint on microglia number, F(1, 34) = 8.32, p = 0.007, R2 = 0.20, with reduced microglia numbers observed postpartum compared to late pregnancy, but no effects of gestational stress, F(1, 34) = 0.26, p = 0.61. There was no interaction between time and stress on microglia number, F(1, 34) = 1.17, p = 0.29, Fig. 3A. Microglia percent area staining was reduced postpartum compared to late pregnancy, F(1, 32) = 12.48, p = 0.0013, R2 = 0.28, and there was also a significant main effect of stress condition on microglia percent area, F(1, 32) = 28.45, p < 0.0001, R2 = 0.47, such that there was an increase in microglia area labeling when mothers were stressed during pregnancy. There was not an interaction effect between time and stress on Iba1 staining, F(1, 32) = 0.49, p = 0.49, Fig. 3B.

To understand whether changes in percent area staining were accompanied by particular alterations in cell morphology, microglia soma size and branching complexity were evaluated. There was a significant main effect of peripartum timepoint, F(1, 34) = 13.42, p = 0.0008, R2 = 0.28, and a significant main effect of stress, F(1, 34) = 244.5, p < 0.0001, R2 = 0.88 on microglia soma size. There was also a significant stress by timepoint interaction, F(1, 34) = 9.52, p = 0.004, R2 = 0.22 on soma size, with post-hoc analysis showing that at both GD21 (p < 0.0001) and PD8 (p < 0.0001), gestational stress increased microglia soma size compared to control. In addition, there was a significant increase in soma size of control mothers from GD21 to PD8 (p < 0.0001); the soma size of microglia in stressed mothers did not change across time (p = 0.68), Fig. 3C. For microglia branching complexity, there was a main effect of stress, F(1, 27) = 5.52, p = 0.03, R2 = 0.17, with stressed mothers having reduced branching complexity. There was no main effect of timepoint, F(1, 27) = 0.003, p = 0.96, and no interaction effect, F(1, 27) = 1.01, p = 0.32, Fig. 3D on microglia branching complexity. Representative images of Iba1+ microglia immunolabeling are shown in Fig. 3E.

Microglia morphological changes, though often considered a proxy for functional state, may not accurately represent an increase in microglia activation (Vidal-Itriago et al., 2022). Thus, we also labeled for CD68, a lysosomal marker that is upregulated when phagocytes become activated (Chistiakov et al., 2016). Microglia are not the only cells that may express CD68 (Gottfried et al., 2008) therefore Imaris software was used to render and quantify the volume of CD68 within microglia, Fig. 3H. There were no significant differences in total CD68 staining, F's = 1.25–2.90, p's = 0.10–0.27, Fig. 3F. There were few to no microglia that were CD68 negative, and there was no difference in the amount of CD68 colocalized within microglia based on experimental group, F's = 1.53–3.65, p's = 0.07–0.23, Fig. 3G.

Astrocytes are another immunocompetent cell known to demonstrate changes in number and/or morphology because of motherhood or stress exposure (Theodosis and Poulain, 1984; Salmaso et al., 2005; Miguel-Hidalgo, 2022). As such, we next examined the impact of gestational stress on GFAP + astrocyte labeling in the NAc shell, Fig. 3K. Astrocyte number significantly increased when mothers were exposed to stress, F(1, 24) = 31.96, p < 0.0001, R2 = 0.57, Fig. 3I. There was no impact of peripartum timepoint, F(1, 24) = 0.01, p = 0.91, or interaction effect, F(1, 24) = 0.33, p = 0.57, on overall astrocyte number. Similarly, stress led to a significant increase in GFAP area labeling, F(1, 24) = 57.12, p < 0.0001, R2 = 0.70, Fig. 3J. There was no main effect of peripartum timepoint, F(1, 24) = 0.12, p = 0.73, or interaction effect, F(1, 24) = 0.19, p = 0.66, on astrocyte area labeling.

To determine if our stress model impacted gliosis in the NAc shell of non-maternal adult females in the same way as pregnant females, the same microglia, CD68, and astrocyte staining analyses were conducted in nulliparous animals. There was no effect of stress on microglia cell number, t(17) = 1.25, p = 0.23, or microglia percent area staining, t(17) = 0.05, p = 0.96, in nulliparous females, Fig S1 A-B. Likewise, there was no difference in microglia soma size, t(17) = 1.59, p = 0.13, or microglia branching complexity, t(17) = 0.53, p = 0.60, Fig S1 C-D, in stressed nulliparous females compared to controls. Total CD68 labeling, t(18) = 0.04, p = 0.97, and CD68 colocalized within microglia, t(18) = 0.31, p = 0.76, was not different in stressed nulliparous females compared to controls, Fig S1 E-F. Astrocyte number, t(10) = 1.09, p = 0.3, and astrocyte percent area labeling, t(10) = 0.34, p = 0.74, also did not vary with stress exposure in nulliparous females, Fig S1 G-H.

Gestational stress led to immune dysregulation during late pregnancy and the postpartum period in the NAc

To gain further insight into the neuroimmune environment in the NAc following exposure to gestational stress, a custom-made rat Nanostring nCounter panel (350 total genes) was used to investigate transcripts associated with immune activity (Supplemental file 1). We also included genes that neuroinflammatory signaling is known to interact with, such as: neuroendocrine factors, genes that guide synaptic function and signaling, and genes implicated in neuropsychiatric conditions such as depression (Haddad et al., 2002; Smith and Strunz, 2005; Nagase et al., 2006; Pollock et al., 2014; Manley et al., 2018; Biltz et al., 2022; Guedes et al., 2022). Genes that were differentially expressed at GD21 between control and stressed mothers are shown in Fig. 4A and C. Of the 16 significant DEGs at GD21,12 were downregulated with stress compared to mothers that underwent control handling. This downregulation (relative to housekeeping gene expression) included genes involved with pro-inflammatory activity and immune activation such as Ccr6 and Cxcl12, (Guyon, 2014; Gómez-Melero and Caballero-Villarraso, 2023), as well as genes with mixed pro- and anti-inflammatory roles such as Nostrin (Chakraborty and Ain, 2017). A similar pattern was evident in samples collected on PD8, such that of the 58 significant DEGs, 49 were downregulated in their expression in stressed mothers compared to controls, and included changes in both pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators, Fig. 4B and D.

At PD8, multiple DEGs involved with excitatory cellular signaling were downregulated with stress, Fig. 4B and D. Stress led to reduced expression of Gria2, Gria3, Grin1, Grin2a, and Grin2b, genes that encode for AMPA- and NMDA-type glutamatergic receptors (Chen et al., 2017; Myers et al., 2019; Salpietro et al., 2019; Okano et al., 2023). Genes that encode for all three main subtypes of inhibitory interneuron populations were also significantly downregulated with stress at PD8, including: parvalbumin (Pvalb), somatostatin receptors (Sstr1, Sstr2, Sstr4), and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP; Caillard et al., 2000; Cone et al., 2019; Song et al., 2021; Apicella and Marchionni, 2022).

At both GD21 and PD8, gestational stress significantly altered the expression of genes involved in hormone synthesis and signaling, Fig. 4A–D. At GD21, this included a stress-induced reduction in 3-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase expression (HSDB32), which is important for the synthesis of steroid hormones (Rasmussen et al., 2013), and an increase in aldo-keto reductases family, subfamily 1C (AKR1C4) expression, an enzyme responsible for the reduction of 5α-dihydroprogesterone into allopregnanolone (Zamora-Sánchez and Camacho-Arroyo, 2022). At PD8, gestational stress led to decreased expression of genes important for steroid hormone function, such as corticotropin-releasing factor-binding protein (Crhbp) and progesterone receptor (PGR; Brinton et al., 2008; Curley et al., 2021).

There were five genes that were differentially expressed as a function of gestational stress exposure at both GD21 and PD8, Fig. 4E. Genes that were significantly downregulated in stressed mothers at both timepoints included: Fcrls (Fc receptor-like S, scavenger receptor), which is a homeostatic microglia marker, TOLLIP (Toll-interacting protein), which is implicated in signaling cascades activated by toll-like receptors, and Nostrin (nitric oxide synthase traffic inducer), which regulates nitric oxide expression (Humbert-Claude et al., 2016; Chakraborty and Ain, 2017; Butovsky and Weiner, 2018). Growth hormone (Gh), important for metabolic function during pregnancy and lactation (Escalada et al., 1997), was significantly downregulated with stress at GD21, but upregulated with stress at PD8. Prolactin receptor (PRLR), which plays a key role in lactation but also has immunomodulatory properties (Borba et al., 2019), was significantly upregulated by gestational stress exposure at both GD21 and PD8.

Discussion

PPD is a prominent public health concern that impacts 20 % of new mothers (Wisner et al., 2013; Kozhimannil and Kim, 2014). Elucidating the mechanisms underlying PPD is critical to adequately provide care and support mothers experiencing mood and caregiving impairments. Here we investigated the impact of stress exposure during pregnancy, a major risk factor for PPD (Robertson et al., 2004; Milgrom et al., 2008; Guintivano et al., 2018), on neuroimmune, neuroendocrine, and synaptic endpoints in the NAc as well as on peripheral signaling molecules. We found that gestational stress perturbed central, but not peripheral, immune endpoints. Endocrine profiles were disrupted by gestational stress both peripherally and in the NAc. Gestational stress also altered transcripts associated with synapses and synaptic plasticity during late pregnancy and the postpartum period. Our design also allowed us to assess normative peripartum changes in the immune, synaptic, and endocrine endpoints, where we found that microglia phagocytosis of the postsynaptic element, PSD95, was reduced in the postpartum period, coinciding with a postpartum increase in total PSD95.

Stressed mothers gained less weight during pregnancy compared to those who experienced control handling, as is commonly found in animals exposed to stress (Harris, 2015). Stress during pregnancy can lead to fetal resorption (Sugino et al., 1994), however we did not see evidence of resorption or any changes in litter size. Thus, our stress model was effective in changing maternal physiological parameters, but mild enough that the pregnancies could be carried to term successfully. This was further supported by our data showing no group differences in pup weight on the day of birth. By PD8, however, offspring of stressed mothers weighed less than offspring of control mothers, likely due to reduced postpartum maternal care, which we and others have reported in previous studies (Haim et al., 2014, 2016, 2025; Leuner et al., 2014; Tan et al., 2018; Zoubovsky et al., 2020; Gorman-Sandler et al., 2023).

We examined peripheral levels of several hormones that are necessary to support a successful pregnancy and maternal behavior, and which have been shown to be sensitive to stress. Circulating prolactin and oxytocin levels did not change following stress exposure, though we did see a characteristic postpartum increase in these hormones that occurs alongside the display of maternal behaviors (Bridges et al., 1985; Figueira et al., 2008; Scatliffe et al., 2019). There were no differences in peripheral corticosterone concentrations at GD21 or PD8. Corticosterone levels in rodents rise 3-fold near parturition (Waddell and Atkinson, 1994; Lonstein et al., 2024), and thus, the absence of a stress effect on corticosterone at GD21 could be due to already high levels of glucocorticoids associated with late pregnancy. The PD8 timepoint occurred over a week following the cessation of stress exposure, thus any effects of stress on peripheral corticosterone likely were resolved by this time. Stressed mothers showed a significant decrease in circulating estradiol at GD21. There was also a significant effect of stress on circulating progesterone levels. Though post-hoc analyses were not conducted due to the lack of a significant interaction in the two-way ANOVA, visual inspection of the histograms suggest that this effect could be due to a reduction of progesterone in stressed mothers at GD21. Fluctuations in ovarian hormone levels precipitate the onset of depressive symptoms in women across reproductive phases, including pregnancy (Jacobs et al., 2015; Albert and Newhouse, 2019). High levels of corticosterone can suppress estrogen and progesterone (Joseph and Whirledge, 2017). Given that corticosterone was not affected, it is possible that these stress-induced decreases in gonadal hormones are caused by dysfunction at a different level of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis. Neuroendocrine transcripts were also included in the Nanostring panel to assess the impact of gestational stress on hormone function in the NAc. Despite there being no stress-induced differences in peripheral prolactin levels at either timepoint examined, prolactin receptor transcript was significantly upregulated in the NAc of stressed mothers at both GD21 and PD8. This could indicate that stress-induced sensitivity of the brain to prolactin differs from the effects of stress on peripheral prolactin levels. Prolactin receptor can become upregulated following activation by pro-inflammatory cytokines (Corbacho et al., 2003), and as such this also could be evidence of immune dysregulation in stressed mothers. Additionally, we observed altered levels of transcripts responsible for steroid hormone synthesis, including AKR1C4, a key enzyme in the synthesis of allopregnanolone (Zamora-Sánchez and Camacho-Arroyo, 2022). Mothers with single nucleotide polymorphisms in AKR1C family alleles were also shown to have lower levels of allopregnanolone in blood samples collected during pregnancy (Hellgren et al., 2017) and lower levels of allopregnanolone are found in mothers with PPD (Pinna et al., 2022). Allopregnanolone modulates depressive-like behaviors in stressed female rats via dopamine receptor 2 in the NAc (D'Aquila et al., 2010). Determining whether gestational stress impacts allopregnanolone levels is needed in future work, although gestational stress has been shown to reduce dopamine receptor 2 in the NAc (Haim et al., 2025). Given that allopregnanolone has anti-inflammatory properties (Djebaili et al., 2005; Balan et al., 2021; Lopez et al., 2024), the current findings indicate a possible neuroimmune mechanism underlying treatment efficacy of PPD-specific drugs targeting allopregnanolone.

A primary goal of this study was to determine the impact of gestational stress on the maternal neuroimmune system. Because our stress paradigm is a systemic perturbance, we assessed the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL1β, TNFα, and IL6 in the periphery of mothers who were exposed to stress or control handling during pregnancy. We did not find any differences in levels of these three peripheral cytokines based on stress exposure. However, there was an effect of peripartum timepoint on IL1β and TNFα, consistent with previous reports of fluctuations in circulating inflammatory markers in new mothers (Groer et al., 2014; Posillico and Schwarz, 2016; Bränn et al., 2019; Dye et al., 2022). A broader examination of cytokine signaling could be more effective in capturing stress-induced changes in the peripheral immune system that was not evident by looking at these three cytokines alone. It is also possible that, rather than a pro-inflammatory phenotype, there could be either a broad dysregulation or an anti-inflammatory response occurring, which may be more in line with the existing clinical literature of mothers experiencing PPD (reviewed in Dye et al., 2022). For example, the ratio of pro-versus anti-inflammatory cytokines in the periphery has been an identified as a significant predictor of the development of PPD symptoms (Corwin et al., 2015). Additionally, in plasma collected during late pregnancy, 40 different pro- and anti-inflammatory markers were reduced in mothers who later developed PPD (Bränn et al., 2017). Rodent work has demonstrated increases in whole blood concentrations of both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines in stressed mothers who displayed depressive-like postpartum behavior (O'Mahony et al., 2006). Together, converging human and animal model studies indicate that the relative balance of pro-versus anti-inflammatory mediators could be important in mediating subsequent PPD onset. Future studies are warranted to more comprehensively understand how peripheral immune signaling is impacted by gestational stress.

Neuroimmune analyses were focused in the NAc, a brain region that is important for regulating mood and maternal care (Li and Fleming, 2003; Champagne et al., 2004; Numan, 2006). Iba1+ microglia area labeling in the NAc of stressed mothers was significantly increased at both the late pregnancy and postpartum timepoints. Because there were no changes in microglia cell numbers, this increase in area labeling is attributable to the significant increases in microglia soma size and reduced branching complexity in stressed mothers. In addition to the effects of stress, microglia cell number and percent area were lower at PD8 compared to GD21, and there was an increase in soma size at PD8 compared to GD21 in control mothers. GFAP + astrocyte number and area staining were also increased with stress at both peripartum timepoints, though the amount of staining did not alter across timepoints. Collectively, this provides novel insight into peripartum neuroimmune alterations in the NAc and suggests a central inflammatory phenotype in the absence of a peripheral immune response in stressed mothers. Importantly, the same microglia and astrocyte analyses were conducted in nulliparous adult females. There was no effect of stress on staining outcomes, 8–9 days following stress cessation (analogous to the PD8 timepoint) indicating that the neuroimmune effects are specific to stress during pregnancy, Fig. S1. It will be important to determine in future work whether the acute effects of stress (1 day following stress cessation, equivalent to the GD21 endpoint) reported herein are specific to the peripartum period.

There were no changes in CD68 phagolysosome labeling, which can be upregulated when microglia are activated (Chistiakov et al., 2016). However, Nanostring analyses showed that Fcrls, a gene that is expressed in homeostatic microglia (Butovsky and Weiner, 2018; Hammond et al., 2019), was significantly downregulated at both GD21 and PD8, suggesting an increase in microglia activation across peripartum timepoints in stressed mothers. While the increased microglia and astrocyte staining demonstrated here is one generally associated with a reactive phenotype, the Nanostring panel demonstrated reductions in pro-inflammatory signaling along with reductions in anti-inflammatory mediators at both GD21 and PD8. Together, these results indicate that any disruption in the typical neuroimmune milieu regardless of the direction of the shift could be important in mediating later PPD onset. It is also important to consider that the Nanostring analysis was performed on whole NAc tissue dissections and thus was not cell-type specific. Microglia, astrocytes, neurons, and endothelia express inflammatory mediators and their receptors and thus could contribute to the effects observed here (Turrin and Plata-Salamán, 2000; Mousa and Bakhiet, 2013). Moreover, determining which inflammatory effects are specific to the NAc shell versus other areas of the brain relevant for regulating maternal behavior and mood, as well as cross-validating results through other methods, will be important goals of future studies.

One way in which perturbations to the immune environment could produce depressive-like outcomes is through aberrant interactions with surrounding neuronal structures, including dendritic spines (Hodes et al., 2015; Wood et al., 2015; Wohleb et al., 2018; Biltz et al., 2022; Afridi and Suk, 2023). We have previously reported postpartum reductions in spine density on medium spiny neurons in the NAc shell following gestational stress (Haim et al., 2014; Haim et al., 2016), thus we sought to further examine which synaptic components were being impacted by stress during pregnancy. qPCR analyses revealed that gestational stress induced changes in presynaptic, but not postsynaptic transcripts. VGLUT1 decreased, while SYN increased, at GD21 in the NAc of stressed mothers. Though differences in transcripts were apparent at GD21, we did not see changes in immunolabeling of VGLUT1. Immunolabeling analyses were confined to the NAc shell, while qPCR analyses were conducted on whole NAc dissections (both core and shell subregions), which could contribute to the differences in findings across analyses. It is also possible that stressed-induced changes in mRNA expression of VGLUT1 preceded changes in protein levels that were not captured by analyzing transcript and protein at the same timepoints. Reproductive status and stressor duration are other important considerations. In nulliparious female mice, it has been previously reported that 24 h after six days of stress exposure VGLUT1 staining is decreased and VGLUT2 staining is increased in the NAc (Brancato et al., 2017), whereas the currently-reported outcomes were taken 24 h after 2 weeks of stress exposure in pregnancy. Together, this suggests that altered excitatory synapses in the NAc may be a common mediator of stress-induced depressive-like outcomes in females, with variations in outcomes likely due to reproductive status, differences in stress paradigm, or timing of analyses conducted.

Due to the dynamic physiological changes that occur during pregnancy and the postpartum period, the peripartum timepoint which assessments were performed is critical when interpreting the impact of stress on synaptic markers. Although VGLUT1 transcript decreased and SYN transcript increased at GD21 in the NAc of stressed mothers, by PD8, there was a significant reduction in SYN expression in the NAc of stressed mothers and no difference in VGLUT1 expression. Similarly, there was a broad downregulation of transcripts responsible for both excitatory and inhibitory cellular signaling at PD8 in the Nanostring dataset that was not apparent at GD21. NAc activity is reduced in patients with major depressive disorder (Pizzagalli et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2021). Approximately 95 % of neurons in the NAc are GABAergic, thus a reduction in activity in this region is likely indicative of reduced inhibitory output (Russo and Nestler, 2013; Islam et al., 2021). Moreover, previous work has shown that dopaminergic and serotoninergic signaling is essential for glutamatergic transmission via NMDA and AMPA receptors in the NAc, with alterations in either monoamine system associated with depressive-like outcomes (Chergui, 2011; Linck et al., 2012; Goffer et al., 2013). Collectively, the stress-induced decreases in transcripts responsible for glutamatergic, GABAergic, and monoamine function in the NAc at PD8 indicate a complex effect of stress on neurotransmission in the NAc.

Finally, an engulfment analysis was used to examine whether microglia phagocytosis was responsible for the changes in synaptic markers in the brains of stressed mothers. Gestational stress exposure did not impact engulfment of presynaptic VGLUT1. Protein engulfment was assessed at the same time we saw reductions in VGLUT1 transcript in our qPCR data, thus it is possible that if we examined protein levels and engulfment at different timepoints, there would be a difference not captured here. There was also no effect of stress on engulfment of the postsynaptic protein, PSD95. However, total labeling of PSD95 increased in the postpartum NAc shell at the same time when there was a reduced number of puncta being engulfed by microglia. While microglia have been shown to sculpt spines in other periods of high plasticity such as neurodevelopment (Kopec et al., 2018; Escoubas and Molofsky, 2024), this is the first evidence showing that microglia sculpt synaptic elements in the normative maternal brain. Though this is a novel finding, the question of how increased microglia activation in stressed mothers contributes to changes in the surrounding environment remains unanswered. It is possible that, in response to gestational stress, microglia differentially sculpt other synaptic components than those that we assessed in the current study, for example dopaminergic synapses or receptors, such as dopamine receptor 2, that we previously found to be reduced following gestational stress (Haim et al., 2025). Alternatively, it could be that the secretory rather than phagocytic function of microglia is being altered by gestational stress exposure, such as changes in neurotrophic signaling, which could in turn impact dendritic spine density (Yeh et al., 2023; Komori et al., 2024). Other possibilities not investigated here are that stress could induce astrocyte phagocytotic activity to promote synaptic loss (Konishi et al., 2022; Byun et al., 2023), or that astrocytes alter glutamatergic activity to modulate dendritic spine dynamics (Haber et al., 2006). Further understanding how stress-induced changes in microglia and astrocytes are individually and/or together impacting the NAc is needed.

The current studies demonstrate a significant impact of gestational stress on the NAc. Although the NAc plays a critical role in the larger maternal brain network (Brunton and Russell, 2008; Post and Leuner, 2018), further evaluating the effect of gestational stress on neuroimmune activity in other brain regions is necessary in understanding the broader impact of changes that occur within the NAc. Gestational stress leads to structural modifications in other brain regions in the postpartum period, including the prefrontal cortex and basolateral amygdala (Leuner et al., 2014; Haim et al., 2016). The prefrontal cortex and basolateral amygdala demonstrate normative peripartum alterations in microglia (Haim et al., 2017) that may also be perturbed by stress. Determining the regional specificity of neuroimmune alterations that occur in response to gestational perturbations will be necessary for understanding any impact on maternal brain function that could in turn impact behavior. Although we did not conduct behavioral analyses in the current studies, we have previously demonstrated that the gestational stress paradigm used here generates a PPD-like behavioral phenotype (Leuner et al., 2014; Haim et al., 2014, 2016, 2025). Examining neuroimmune alterations concurrent with behavior is an important next step for establishing functional relevance.

Conclusion

Together these studies demonstrate that gestational stress elicits an array of synaptic, hormonal, and immune modifications in the rodent NAc. Future work is needed to determine how these factors that are impacted by gestational stress are causally related, and whether they play a role in stress-induced disruptions in mood and maternal behavior. This work provides a foundation for furthering our understanding of neuroimmune alterations following peripartum perturbations and their role in PPD. Dissecting mechanisms underlying PPD is critical for developing treatments to support mothers’ mental health and caregiving behavior.
