Outcomes and risk factors of traumatic brain injury in infants under 3 months with minor head trauma: a retrospective study from a tertiary care center in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Ibrahim Alghannam, Abdualelah Alrashidi, Mohammed Alghamdi, Abdulaziz Alshalan, Rawan Alrashed, Safeyah Aljurfi, Yara Algoraini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiagnosing traumatic brain injury (TBI) after minor head trauma (MHT) in infants < 3 months is challenging; the applicability of PECARN is uncertain in this age group. To describe outcomes and risk factors for CT-confirmed (radiologic) TBI and clinically important TBI (ciTBI) in infants < 3 months with MHT.MethodsRetrospective cohort at a tertiary pediatric ED (Riyadh), Jan 2021–May 2025. Infants < 3 months presenting ≤ 24 h after MHT were included; suspected NAT was excluded. Primary outcome was radiologic TBI; secondary outcome was ciTBI. Because of sparse data, associations were estimated with exact (Fisher-based) univariable ORs in the CT-imaged subset.ResultsOf 77 infants, 23 (29.9%) underwent head CT; radiologic TBI occurred in 18/23 (78.3%; 95% CI 56.3–92.5%), predominantly isolated skull fractures, while ciTBI occurred in 2/77 (2.6%). Non-frontal scalp hematoma was strongly associated with radiologic TBI (OR 50.00; 95% CI 2.12–1178.94; p = 0.0017). Fall vs. not-fall was associated (OR 24.00; 95% CI 0.86–671.39; p = 0.040); fall distance ≥ 0.9 m was not significant (OR 2.33; p = 0.576). Most non-imaged infants met PECARN low-risk criteria.ConclusionsIn infants < 3 months with MHT, non-frontal scalp hematoma is a high-yield marker for radiologic TBI, whereas ciTBI is uncommon. Findings support targeted imaging (when high-risk features cluster) and observation with safety-netting for otherwise low-risk infants, balancing potential benefit against radiation risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-025-01415-5.

Introduction

Diagnosing traumatic brain injury (TBI) in infants under 3 months of age following minor head trauma (MHT) presents a significant clinical challenge in pediatric emergency departments (PEDs). This population is uniquely vulnerable due to immature cranial anatomy, open fontanelles, and limited myelination, all of which increase susceptibility to intracranial injury even from low-impact mechanisms such as short falls or blunt trauma [1, 2]. Despite this heightened risk, there is a lack of consistent, evidence-based guidelines tailored to this age group, resulting in considerable variability in clinical management [3].

The Pediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network (PECARN) head injury algorithm is a widely validated clinical decision tool for older children; however, its applicability to infants younger than 3 months has not been well established [4]. Concerns about radiation exposure and its potential long-term consequences, including malignancy, have led to increasing scrutiny of computed tomography (CT) use in this population [5, 6]. Nevertheless, in the absence of validated alternatives, CT remains a commonly used modality. Moreover, many infants classified as low-risk are still admitted for observation, reflecting ongoing uncertainty in risk stratification and clinical decision-making [7].

In this age group, most cases of MHT result from mild blunt trauma, unintentional caregiver-related falls, or accidents during routine handling. Although serious TBI is relatively rare, the potential for non-accidental trauma (NAT) must always be considered, necessitating careful evaluation of injury patterns and clinical context [8–10]. The primary challenge for clinicians is to identify significant injuries while minimizing unnecessary imaging, hospital admissions, and interventions.

This study aims to evaluate current diagnostic practices and clinical outcomes in infants under 3 months of age presenting with MHT to a tertiary PED in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. By analyzing imaging use, admission patterns, and injury outcomes, we aim to identify risk factors for TBI and assess the performance of PECARN criteria in this age subgroup. Our findings may support the development of more accurate, age-specific decision tools and promote safer, more efficient care pathways for this high-risk population.

Methods

Study design

This was a completed retrospective cohort at a tertiary PED of King Fahad Medical City, a tertiary care center in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, including all eligible visits from January 1, 2021 to May 31, 2025; no prospective or ongoing enrollment occurred beyond this period.

Selection of participants

Eligible participants were infants younger than 3 months who presented to the PED within 24 h of sustaining MHT. MHT was defined as blunt head trauma with GCS ≥ 14 and no signs of severe neurological compromise. Inclusion criteria required documented evidence of MHT without signs of severe neurological compromise. Exclusion criteria included: age > 3 months at the time of injury, suspected non-accidental trauma (NAT), presence of multiple injuries, presentation > 24 h after trauma, or absence of confirmed MHT (Fig. 1).

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula, resulting in a required sample of 384. However, only 77 infants met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. The calculation was based on a 95% confidence interval (CI), a 5% margin of error, and a population proportion of 0.5.

Although the required sample size was 384, the final cohort included only 77 infants due to the strict inclusion criteria, the rarity of MHT in infants under 3 months, and the limited number of eligible cases during the study period.

The high CT-positive yield (18/23, 78.3%) reflects clinician selection of higher-risk infants for imaging and should not be extrapolated to the whole cohort; small CT-negative counts (n = 5) produce wide CIs.

Post-hoc power (sensitivity) analysis. In the CT-imaged subset (n = 23), the observed difference in non-frontal scalp hematoma prevalence between CT-confirmed (radiologic) TBI (18/23, 78.3%%) and CT not confirmed (radiologic) TBI (5/23, 21.7%) infants yielded achieved power ≈ 0.995 for a two-sided two-proportion test (Cohen’s h, α = 0.05). Post-hoc power is reported for transparency only; it is less informative than effect sizes and exact confidence intervals, and findings should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample and wide CIs.

Data collection

Data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system using a standardized data collection form. Collected variables included patient demographics (age, sex, nationality), comorbidities, mechanism and nature of injury, clinical signs and symptoms on presentation, imaging performed (CT or ultrasound), disposition (discharge, observation, or admission), application of PECARN criteria, and radiologic confirmation of TBI.

Clinical management pathway

Infants underwent standardized triage, focused neurological assessment, and application of PECARN < 2 years criteria. Imaging decisions incorporated clinician judgment and shared decision-making with caregivers, weighing radiation/sedation risks against anticipated yield. When immediate CT was deferred, infants were observed with serial examinations and discharged with safety-net instructions when clinically stable (Fig. 2).

Imaging protocol & interpretation

Head CT (non-contrast) was obtained when concerning features were present (e.g., non-frontal scalp hematoma, abnormal behavior, visible trauma above the clavicle, or concerning mechanism). CT images were interpreted by the hospital radiology service; ultrasound, when used, was performed/interpreted per routine clinical practice. Only CT defined radiologic TBI for this study.

Outcomes & definitions

Radiologic TBI: any acute intracranial abnormality or skull fracture on CT.

Clinically important TBI (ciTBI): death, neurosurgical intervention, endotracheal intubation > 24 h, or hospital admission ≥ 2 nights attributable to head injury.

Minor head trauma (MHT): blunt head trauma with GCS ≥ 14 and no severe neurologic compromise.)

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26. Primary outcome: CT-confirmed (radiologic) TBI; secondary: ciTBI. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize categorical variables as frequencies and percentages and continuous variables as means with standard deviations (mean ± SD). The Chi-squared test (χ²) was used to assess associations between categorical variables. For non-parametric continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test were applied. To identify independent predictors of CT-confirmed (radiologic) TBI, we performed multivariable logistic regression (reporting ORs with 95% CIs); however, due to sparse data/zero cells causing model instability, we instead report exact (Fisher-based) univariable Odd Ratios (OR) in the CT-imaged subset and emphasize effect sizes with exact 95% CIs. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because CT acquisition was at clinician discretion, the imaged subgroup likely over-represents higher-risk presentations (indication/verification bias). We therefore report CT-confirmed (radiologic) TBI only within the imaged subgroup and do not infer cohort-level prevalence.

We also performed a sensitivity (post-hoc) power analysis for the observed difference in non-frontal scalp hematoma prevalence between CT- confirmed (radiologic) and CT- CT-confirmed (radiologic) TBI in infants using a two-proportion test (Cohen’s h); the achieved power and the minimum detectable difference (80% power, α = 0.05) are reported in the Results/Appendix. Analyses used complete-case data; missing cases were excluded per prespecified criteria.

Result

Study population

A total of 90 infants were initially enrolled. Thirteen were excluded: 8 due to delayed presentation (> 24 h), 4 due to missing data, and 1 due to suspected non-accidental trauma. The final sample comprised 77 infants. Of these, 59 met PECARN low-risk criteria and 18 did not. Among CT-imaged infants (n = 23), radiologic TBI occurred in 18/23 (78.3%); ciTBI occurred in 2/77 (2.6%) overall (2/23 = 8.7% among CT-imaged) (Fig. 1) (Table 1).

Demographics and injury characteristics

Most infants were aged 2–3 months (n = 53, 68.8%), followed by those aged 1–2 months (n = 13, 16.9%), with a mean age of 1.87 ± 0.94 months. Males accounted for 53.2% (n = 41), and nearly all were Saudi nationals (n = 76, 98.7%). Comorbidities were rare (n = 3, 3.9%). The majority of injuries resulted from falls (n = 62, 80.5%), most commonly from heights < 0.9 m (n = 51, 82.3%). Frequent mechanisms included falling from an elevated surface (n = 37, 48.1%) and from a caregiver’s arms (n = 17, 22.1%) (Table 1).

PECARN classification and clinical features

Of the 77 infants, 44 (57.1%) met PECARN low-risk criteria with no additional findings, 15 (19.5%) met the criteria but had other clinical signs, and 18 (23.4%) did not meet the low-risk criteria. In the low-risk group without additional findings, non-frontal scalp hematoma (n = 8, 18.2%) and parent-reported abnormal behavior (n = 7, 15.9%) were most common. Among those with additional findings, non-frontal scalp hematoma (n = 9, 60%), abrasions or lacerations above the clavicle (n = 7, 46.7%), and vomiting (n = 7, 46.7%) were prevalent. Less common findings included frontal scalp hematoma, brief loss of consciousness, and abnormal neurological examination (each n = 1, 6.7%) (Table 1).

Risk factor analysis

In a prespecified CT-subset analysis (n = 23), all radiologic TBIs followed a fall (18/18); fall vs. not-fall was associated with CT-confirmed TBI (OR 24.00, 95% CI 0.86–671.39; Fisher p = 0.0395; Table 4). Non-frontal scalp hematoma was the strongest marker (OR 50.00, 95% CI 2.12–1178.94; p = 0.0017; Table 4). Fall distance ≥ 0.9 m vs. < 0.9 m was not significant (OR 2.33, 95% CI 0.28–19.17; p = 0.576; Table 4), though descriptively 14/18 (77.8%) TBIs followed < 0.9 m falls (Table 2). Power for the NFSH difference was ≈ 0.995 (two-proportion, α = 0.05).

Imaging and PECARN correlation

Head CTs were obtained selectively for higher-risk features. Among CT-imaged infants (n = 23), 18/23 (78.3%) were radiologic TBI (CT-confirmed). This proportion reflects clinician selection of higher-risk cases and should not be interpreted as prevalence in the entire cohort. ciTBI in the cohort was 2/77 (2.6%). Non-imaged infants (n = 54) predominantly fulfilled PECARN low-risk criteria ( 40/54; 74.1% ), consistent with observation rather than immediate imaging; 14/54 (25.9%) did not meet low-risk yet were managed without immediate CT under close observation. However, neither age nor PECARN classification was significantly associated with TBI or skull fracture on CT (p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Subgroup analysis of PECARN low-risk infants

Head CT was performed in 18/59 (30.5%) low-risk infants; radiologic TBI was found in 16/18 (88.9%; 95% CI 65.3–98.6). Expressed against all low-risk infants, this is 16/59 (27.1%; 95% CI 16.4–40.3), but should not be interpreted as prevalence because 41/59 (69.5%) were not imaged and thus not CT-verified. Among the CT-positive low-risk subset (n = 16), most were 2–3 months old, 9/16 (56.3%) were female, and falls < 0.9 m accounted for 12/16 (75.0%). Non-frontal scalp hematoma was present in 14/16 (87.5%), with abrasions/lacerations above the clavicle 5/16 (31.3%) and vomiting 3/16 (18.8%). The most frequent CT patterns were non-displaced skull fractures with hemorrhage or subgaleal hematoma (n = 7), displaced fractures with hemorrhage (n = 4), and non-displaced fractures without hemorrhage (n = 2). Most infants were discharged from the ED; 3 required ward admission (Table 5).

Discussion

This study evaluated the outcomes and predictors of TBI in infants under 3 months of age presenting with MHT to a tertiary PED. The findings highlight the diagnostic challenges in this population and underscore the importance of cautious risk stratification and imaging decisions. Despite the smaller sample, this cohort represents one of the largest focused analyses of this specific age group in the region, providing valuable insights into clinical predictors and outcomes of TBI.

Of the 90 infants screened, 77 met the inclusion criteria. The majority were aged 2–3 months, with a slight male predominance and a low rate of comorbidities. These characteristics are consistent with previous reports from Saudi Arabia [11] and international cohorts [12, 13], which show that most cases of MHT in infants result from household falls and typically affect otherwise healthy children [14].

Falls were the most common injury mechanism. While 82.3% of falls were from heights below 0.9 m, TBI was significantly associated with falls from heights above 0.9 m (p = 0.048). This finding aligns with international data from the UK, U.S., and Spain, which have identified fall height as a significant modifier of injury severity [15–17]. A recent Saudi study also reported a similar trend in fall-related TBI among infants seen in local ED [18].

Vomiting, non-frontal scalp hematoma, and facial or upper body trauma were significantly associated with TBI, mirroring findings from large multicenter studies such as PECARN and CHALICE [19–21]. Additionally, local data from Riyadh identified non-frontal hematomas and visible trauma above the clavicle as important predictors of TBI in pediatric populations [22].

Notably, 27.1% of infants classified as PECARN low-risk had radiographic evidence of TBI. This prevalence is considerably higher than the 3.3% reported in European and North American studies [14, 17]. The discrepancy may reflect referral bias at tertiary care centers or a lower imaging threshold in infants younger than 3 months. Previous Saudi research has similarly questioned the sensitivity of PECARN in this demographic, highlighting the need for population-specific validation [23].

Non-frontal scalp hematoma strongly predicted CT-confirmed TBI (OR 50.00; 95% CI 2.12–1178.94; p = 0.0017); fall vs. not-fall was also associated (OR 24.00; 95% CI 0.86–671.39; p = 0.0395), while fall distance ≥ 0.9 m was not (OR 2.33; 95% CI 0.28–19.17; p = 0.576). Despite wide CIs, these signals align with prior cohorts [17, 24, 25] and support combining detailed history and focused scalp exam with decision tools.

Importantly, these findings do not justify routine CT in all infants < 3 months. In this age group, non-frontal scalp hematoma is a high-yield marker for CT-confirmed (radiologic) TBI, whereas ciTBI was uncommon. We therefore support a targeted-imaging approach—maintain a lower threshold for CT when high-risk features cluster (e.g., non-frontal scalp hematoma) and otherwise rely on observation with clear safety-netting, aligning decisions with radiation-risk considerations and family preferences. Although many infants were PECARN low-risk, a non-trivial fraction had radiologic findings—likely reflecting tertiary-center referral and indication bias. Overall, our results align with evidence that PECARN performance is attenuated in the youngest infants and argue for age-specific refinement rather than abandonment of decision tools.

Despite the frequency of radiologic abnormalities, clinical outcomes were favorable. Only 5.2% of infants required hospital admission, and none required neurosurgical intervention or intensive care. This observation supports previous conclusions by Greenes & Schutzman (1999) [26] and local findings by (Al Bargi et al., 2025) [11], which suggest that many imaging abnormalities in infants do not correlate with severe clinical outcomes.

In practice, clinicians should combine structured rules with exam-anchored markers (notably non-frontal scalp hematoma) and mechanism detail to individualize imaging. Adjuncts like POCUS for scalp/cranial soft-tissue assessment may help triage superficial injuries while preserving CT for those most likely to benefit.

Post-hoc power (sensitivity) analysis. To contextualize precision in the CT-subset (n = 23), we assessed the observed difference in non-frontal scalp hematoma prevalence between CT-confirmed TBI (15/18, 83.3%) and CT not confirmed TBI (0/5, 0%) infants using a two-proportion test (Cohen’s h, α = 0.05). The achieved power ≈ 0.995, indicating high sensitivity to this large observed effect. We note, however, that post-hoc power is less informative than effect sizes and exact CIs; estimates should be interpreted cautiously given small cell counts and wide CIs.

Future research should explore radiation-free diagnostic alternatives, such as point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), for evaluating superficial head injuries [27]. Longitudinal studies are also needed to examine neurodevelopmental outcomes following MHT in early infancy. Finally, multicenter collaborations could provide larger datasets to validate modified risk stratification tools—potentially leading to updated PECARN guidelines tailored to this high-risk age group.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective single-center design risks selection and documentation bias, and the study was underpowered (target 384; analyzed 77). CT was obtained selectively at clinician discretion, introducing indication/verification bias that inflates the TBI detection rate among those scanned (18/23, 78.3%); consequently, these rates should not be generalized to the full cohort. Because the CT-negative group was small (n = 5) with zero cells, we used Fisher’s exact tests and emphasize effect sizes with exact 95% CIs over p-values; multivariable modeling was limited by separation, so we prioritized exact (Fisher-based) univariable analyses in the CT-imaged subset. A sensitivity post-hoc power analysis for the observed non-frontal scalp hematoma difference was high (≈ 0.995 at α = 0.05), but post-hoc power is less informative than effect sizes/CIs, so findings should be interpreted cautiously. Long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes were not assessed. Finally, reliance on CT precluded evaluation of radiation-sparing modalities (e.g., point-of-care ultrasound). Future prospective multicenter studies are warranted.

Conclusion

Reported TBI rates pertain to the imaged subgroup and are not estimates of prevalence in all infants with minor head trauma. In infants < 3 months with MHT, non-frontal scalp hematoma and mechanism of injury were factors associated with higher odds of TBI; notably, a substantial proportion occurred in PECARN low-risk infants, suggesting underestimation of risk in this age group. Falls—even from low heights—predominated. These findings support careful clinical evaluation and a low threshold for imaging, while recognizing that true prevalence requires unbiased, systematically imaged cohorts.
