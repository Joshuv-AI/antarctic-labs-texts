How do communication, trust and professional relationships shape decentralised clinical trials? A qualitative interview study of international stakeholders
Authors: Joanne Coyle, Amy Rogers, Rachel R Copland, Isla S Mackenzie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate the impact of decentralisation on communication, trust and professional relationships in the context of clinical trials and to identify practical strategies to support coordination and oversight in decentralised clinical trials (DCTs).DesignQualitative interview study using semi-structured interviews and thematic analysis.SettingInternational decentralised and hybrid clinical trials conducted across multiple therapeutic areas and healthcare contexts.ParticipantsThirty-one stakeholders involved in the design and delivery of DCTs, including investigators, trial managers, administrators, industry personnel and service vendors.MethodsParticipants were purposively sampled from a series of DCT case studies. Semi-structured interviews were conducted via videoconferencing, audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Data were analysed using a thematic framework approach to identify themes relevant to communication, coordination and trial oversight. Reporting followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines.ResultsThree interrelated themes were identified. First, DCTs operate as complex networks of formal and informal connections through which information, responsibilities and decisions flow. Second, effective communication and trust-building were viewed as central to participant safety, protocol adherence and collaboration, but were more difficult to establish in remote trial contexts. Third, unclear roles and responsibilities contributed to communication delays, coordination challenges and increased operational risk. Participants highlighted the value of proactively clarifying responsibilities and communication pathways during trial set-up and delivery. A social network perspective was identified as a useful heuristic tool for understanding these dynamics.ConclusionsDCTs place increased demands on communication, coordination and relationship-building across distributed teams. Proactively clarifying roles, responsibilities and communication pathways may support more effective trial delivery and oversight. Although focused on DCTs, these findings are relevant to other digitally mediated and multi-stakeholder models of health research delivery.Trial registration numberN/AKeywords: QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, Health Services, Clinical Trial, Telemedicine, Digital Technology, Patient-Centered Care

Introduction

Decentralised clinical trials (DCTs) move trial activities away from traditional clinical trial sites, often using digital tools, with the aim of making trials more efficient and less burdensome to participants.1 Communication has been highlighted as critical to the safety and validity of DCTs due to their complexity and relative lack of in-person interactions in comparison to conventional site-based trials.1 2 Figure 1 illustrates the relative complexity of interactions in DCTs compared with conventional clinical trials.

Relative complexity of interactions in conventional and decentralised clinical trials.

Clear and well-documented communication channels between participants, investigators, sponsors and service providers are essential to ensure sponsors and investigators have adequate oversight. Effective communication supports informed consent, recruitment and adherence to ethical guidelines.3–6 This paper explores further the role of communication in the practical and social aspects of DCT operations, such as coordinating logistics, incorporating technologies and building professional relationships. Importantly, while our study focuses on DCTs, many of the communication and co-ordination challenges identified are relevant to other digitally mediated and multi-stakeholder models of healthcare and research delivery.

Although there has been limited publication on DCT-specific communication (at the time of writing), broader healthcare research emphasises the importance of effective communication. Positive interprofessional communication can improve patient outcomes, reduce errors, increase job satisfaction and facilitate informed decision-making.7 8 However, barriers, including excessive workload, can reduce the time professionals have to ensure clear communication.8–11 Interprofessional differences in language and terminology may also lead to misunderstandings.8 12 Crucially, miscommunication is a leading cause of patient harm in healthcare, contributing to medical errors, delays in treatment and missed diagnoses.13

Technological solutions like electronic health records can enhance communication and decision-making among healthcare practitioners but may also lead to information overload and decreased interpersonal interaction.14–18 Most DCTs rely on similar technological solutions, such as electronic case report forms and digital trial management platforms, to facilitate decentralisation. In healthcare, targeted training, such as role-playing and simulation, and structured communication strategies like checklists and handover protocols, improve skills, teamwork and error reduction.19–22 Regular training and open respectful cultures are vital for overcoming barriers and promoting clear communication.23–25

This qualitative interview study aims to investigate the impact of decentralisation on communication, trust and professional relationships in the context of clinical trials. It also seeks to identify practical strategies to support coordination and oversight in DCTs, contributing to the safe and effective execution of these clinical research projects.

Methods

Design and study setting

This research was conducted as part of the Trials@Home project (https://trialsathome.com), a multi-stakeholder initiative supported by the European Union and European Federation of Pharmaceutical Industries and Associations (EFPIA) through the Innovative Medicines Initiative (IMI), with the Work Package 1 'BEST' (WP1 BEST) work package aiming to establish best practices from various sectors.

We conducted this qualitative study using a phenomenological approach to frame our research question and guide data collection. Our aim was to explore how stakeholders experienced the challenges of implementing DCTs in varied contexts. We conducted semi-structured interviews to elicit participants’ experiences. The data were analysed using thematic analysis, including iterative open coding, comparison and inductive theme development.15 We chose thematic analysis because of its theoretical freedom, ability to uncover underlying patterns, meanings and perspectives in the data and its accessibility to research team members unfamiliar with qualitative methods through its structured yet flexible approach.2 26 We followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines (see online supplemental file 1 for completed checklist).27

Participants and sample

Case study identification and selection

As part of the Trials@Home WP1 BEST workstream, DCT case studies were selected to represent a diversity of methods and therapeutic areas to capture variation in levels of decentralisation, use of digital tools, geographic scope and stakeholder involvement. Initial case studies were identified from a scoping review and suggested by Trials@Home consortium partners; these case studies were purposively sampled using structured data summaries collected in a Microsoft Excel tool (reported in Coyle 2022).2 Throughout the Trials@Home project, consortium partners were encouraged to propose additional case studies that demonstrated new or previously underrepresented methods of conducting DCTs. New case study proposals were discussed by the case study research team and included if they included DCT methods or tools that had not been used in earlier care studies or were conducted in new therapy areas, patient groups or geographic locations.

This paper presents findings derived from interview data collected from 20 case studies. The data included follow-up interviews with representatives from the nine original case studies that were still ongoing at the time of the interviews. Additionally, 11 new case studies that focused on previously underrepresented topics were identified during the Trials@Home project. These topics included the use of mobile nurses, paediatric neurology as a therapeutic area and innovative approaches to integrating technologies, such as trials that used bespoke apps and digital platforms as key components of the study design (see table 1 for a list of case studies and their key features).

Selection of participants and recruitment

Case study contributors were asked to generate a list of potential personnel and stakeholders to be interviewed. They were then purposefully selected to capture a range of experiences and views. Potential interviewees were invited by email to participate. If a potential interviewee did not respond to the initial invitation, a single email reminder was sent. Interviewees received no remuneration for their participation.

Instrument development

We developed two semi-structured interview schedules for follow-up and new case study interviews (see online supplemental files 2 and 3), mindful that empirical phenomenology stipulates that explanations must be grounded in the first-order constructs of the actors, that is, the meanings they give to their experiences. Open-ended interview questions were initially derived from a scoping review of the DCT literature, with additional items being added iteratively as interviewees raised new relevant issues. The interview schedules, therefore, explored interviewees’ experiences of conducting DCTs, the challenges they faced at different stages of the DCT and how they responded, together with aspects of trials that worked well. The interview schedule for the follow-up interviews was piloted on three follow-up interviewees; similarly, the schedule for the new case studies was piloted on three new case study interviewees.

Interview procedure

Interviews were conducted via videoconferencing by two researchers (JC and RRC) between May 2021 and December 2023.

Data analysis

All interviews were recorded with interviewees’ consent and fully transcribed. Transcripts were securely stored within the University of Dundee’s digital infrastructure. All personal identifiers and any references to the individual or the DCT case study were removed. We used thematic analysis to develop themes from the data facilitated by NVivo 14.28 This involved the familiarisation (reading the transcripts to gain an overall understanding of the meanings conveyed), the generation of initial codes (identifying significant words, sentences and phrases and organising them into categories), searching for preliminary descriptive themes and reviewing and modifying themes. The final refinement stage involved finding associations between the themes and generating explanations for them. Data were collected until saturation was reached, meaning that no new information on the available case studies was being obtained from additional interviews.

Three co-authors (JC, AR, RRC) independently read the transcripts and generated initial codes and categories. Similarities, differences and clustering were noted. Discrepancies in coding and theme development were discussed among the research team and resolved through iterative comparison and consensus. Where disagreements persisted, themes were refined through discussion with the wider author group, considering researchers’ experience and perspectives, until agreement was reached.

The research team

The study was conducted by MEMO Research, University of Dundee, UK. The research team included a qualitative researcher with over 20 years of experience, who provided training in qualitative research design, interview techniques and thematic analysis. The other members of the research team had extensive experience in conducting DCTs and clinical trials methodology research.

The research team recognised the potential for bias, as researchers and most interviewees were engaged in DCTs within a consortium dedicated to advancing such methodologies. Interviewers therefore emphasised the value of both positive and negative experiences, adopted a non-judgemental stance and prioritised rapport-building. They were also trained to use prompts to explore nuanced and unofficial perspectives. To ensure credibility, the study purposefully included a diverse range of participants with varying roles and responsibilities. Findings were regularly discussed within the team to encourage debate and ensure robust consensus.

Results

Potential interviewees were recruited and interviewed between September 2021 and August 2023. Of the 32 people approached, 23 agreed to participate following the first email and 8 after a reminder email. We interviewed 31 individuals. Unlike traditional clinical trial operations, DCTs often lack a clear distinction between central and site staff. Indeed, some fully decentralised trials may not have a separate clinical site at all. For this reason, we classify trial staff based on their roles within the case study (refer to table 2). Where results or findings are specific to the location of the staff, we have specified.

Connections between individuals and organisations

Interviewees described how their DCT experiences were situated within a larger whole or ‘web’ of multiple parties:

We develop a great relationship with our staff so, it is really more hands-on personal, from a higher level of dealing with the sponsors, all the way trickling down to the people that are seeing the patients directly. So, it’s really great to be part of this whole web of connections.Int 63.Trial Staff (management/administration/operations)

This was especially pertinent to international and multi-site trials requiring high levels of coordination in comparison to DCTs using fully decentralised models with few or no physical sites. DCTs also increasingly involve new vendors as providers of technology and services to support decentralisation.

Integration of DCT vendors

Trial staff described a range of DCT models, from trials where all activity was delivered by a small central team to others where several contracted and sub-contracted third-party vendors delivered specific trial technologies and services without otherwise interacting with other parts of the trial. Vendor integration ranged from minimal, where they provided the service or product with input only if problems arose, to full involvement in designing the trial, securing ethical approval and recruiting and engaging participants.

I'm involved in a few of these projects, trying to use wearable devices, and this has actually been the easiest one because [Named Company] is a partner. They’re a collaborator, so we were able to just sit with them as well and say, this is what we need to prove for the GDPR, and [Named Company] was able to say, well this is how we can prove that. That’s actually been, compared to other projects, this has been easy. I’m in another project where we’ve been doing this for about a year and we still haven’t been able to get approval for our compliance. Int 65, Vendor (GDPR=General Data Protection Regulation)

Most trial staff described technology vendors as less involved than in the quotation above.

For some trial staff and site staff, reliance on intermediary third parties to run aspects, such as data collection or administering the IMP (Investigational Medicinal Product), created additional difficulty:

But we have issues like that was a pump issue on the infusion lasted longer and this is a protocol deviation … when protocol deviation happens at site we just contact the site and asked what happened, how it happened. But here we have to also ask the vendor to collect information from the nurses, how it was resolved… Yes so I think everything is a little bit more difficult because there is additional party. Int 78, Trial Staff (management/administration/operations)

These findings suggest that mapping communication pathways and identifying key stakeholders early in trial design may help to prevent gaps in information flow and reduce reliance on informal and reactive communication.

Communication and trust

Ongoing communication was regarded as essential to ensure study tasks were completed on time and met protocol requirements. However, some trial staff described a delicate balance between maintaining contact with sites and overburdening them.

One important point to consider also is the importance of communication … especially of our country coordinators with the sites … The point is that the PI’s have a lot of workloads and they don’t want you to bother them a lot. So, we try to keep in touch on a regular basis, by email maybe, and we just call them when it is required, like, if we have changes in signs and symptoms or if we have any problem during the visit, in these kinds of situations. For the moment, we have not had any problem with this because they establish this relationship since the beginning. Int 61, Trial Staff (clinical/research)

Miscommunication could also strain relationships between study partners, hinder trial progress and jeopardise future collaboration.

I think we all have the communication issues with the home care nurses, or the vendors. We all have the complexity of, you know, managing two separate partners … And when our vendors miscommunicate, or communicate without approval, or they don’t follow the process and promise something that has not been approved, for example - that damages our relationship irreparably. That’s really the reason why I said that communication was just as important as understanding contractual obligations. Int 77, Trial Staff (management/administration/operations)

In DCTs involving mobile nurses, trust was established with site clinicians through vendor nurses sharing valuable information about participants’ safety and well-being at home.

We got good feedback that they had very good collaboration with the nurses, because they did talk a lot so they became quite used to each other and had a close collaboration, because once the site nurse was in the home they of course collected information about adverse events and things like that as well, and then after each visit they had a conversation with the investigator as well … so they had a lot of contact I would say, and I think the feedback we got was that that worked very well. Int 67, Trial Staff (management/administration/operations)

Trusting relationships between trial or vendor and site clinicians often started with in-person contact and then regular in-person or online communication. Once clinicians were accustomed to working with trial staff and/or the vendor’s nurses, it was easier to maintain appropriate oversight of participants. Successful interpersonal relationships thereby formed the basis for organisational trust. However, several trial staff described interactions with new vendors that undermined their trust in the new partner, particularly around regulatory issues, such as data protection law.

The decentralised clinical trial providers that I’ve seen so far are keen on technology, but they are not well-versed in a highly regulated industry as this. They’re not fully aware and compliant with GCP. There’s a lot more checks and balances in a regulated industry, and there have been times when I’ve said, “That’s not meeting the Code of Federal Reg., or ICH,” and they go, “Huh?” Int 75, Trial Staff (management/administration/operations) (GCP=Good Clinical Practice; ICH=International Council for Harmonisation)

These findings indicate that early investment in relationship-building, including initial face-to-face or concurrent interactions where possible, may support the development of trust and improve ongoing communication in DCTs.

Roles and responsibilities

Unmet expectations and uncertainty about roles and responsibilities were frequently identified as sources of difficulty in DCTs.

Expectations

Several interviewees reflected on the nature of relationships between established clinical trial professionals and relative newcomers to the clinical trials ecosystem, such as the providers of technology and services. Trial staff expected vendors to have understood their roles and responsibilities from the protocol. Problems arose when these expectations were not met, with some trial staff believing that technology partners had unrealistic expectations of their information technology skills, indicating mismatched expectations on both sides.

I think that speaks to the challenge of data privacy but I think it also speaks to one thing that I have certainly noticed, is that many of vendors that are working in this space are maybe immature is the right word, right? Particularly the technology vendors … I think their experience is less developed than what many of us are used to in working with vendors, who are used to supporting the pharmaceutical industry… working in a highly regulated space. Int 68, Trial Staff (management/administration/operations)

Some also expressed concern that vendors did not appreciate the burden placed on clinical sites in learning a new system or their frustrations if it did not function as expected:

If you see an app on your iPhone … there is a lot of work in the background that is done to create that app and screen-by-screen scrolling through. We had to create it in a Word document of what questions the patient will see, what buttons they will see, how the questions should be posed? It’s not as easy as just saying, what was your asthma-like in the morning, was it mild, moderate, severe; how was your night? Which was what we normally would do with an electronic diary, an app is very different. So, there were a lot of challenges there because I think they expected us to just write it, but obviously, we were new to it as well and didn’t have that skill set. So, it ended up a 30-page Word document of the patient app. Int 69, Vendor

Trial staff wanted vendors to situate their technology, devices and services within an ethical and legal framework that met local regulatory guidelines such as GDPR and ICH GCP, believing that this was critical to the smooth running of the DCT.

So, it becomes very device dependent, very dependent on the company as well. So if the company just wants to sell a device and leave, then in my experience at least, it becomes very difficult, but if a company’s willing to be engaged in the research in some way, even if it’s just in as a peripheral partner, it appears to be a much easier, GDPR wise. Int 65, Vendor

Clearly defined roles were also thought to ensure protocol-adherent data collection. Trial managers of DCTs using vendor nurses stressed that nurses needed to be clear about what to do and whom to contact should they experience a problem while carrying out trial tasks in the participant’s home.

Skills

There was a general sentiment that many technology vendors lacked tacit knowledge of the clinical trials context, which could jeopardise the smooth running of a trial.

I think the technology vendor was the biggest challenge for us because they were used to doing non-interventional studies; studies that are without real-time patients and/or pure data collection studies and observational studies. This is the first time they’ve done a fully interventional patient study and we have certain regulations and ethical regulations that we must adhere to. Int 69, Vendor

As well as technology vendors, nurses or other healthcare workers may be expected to take on new responsibilities within a DCT. Training to support these can vary widely, from informal instruction on how to use an app or device to formal skills assessment. For instance, in a case study using specialised equipment for home data collection, mobile nurses required training and formal assessment. This additional training requirement exacerbated existing local challenges in recruiting and retaining mobile nurses, particularly as the training was perceived to be of limited use beyond the specific trial.

So that caused some challenges in finding the nurse, specialised nurse to complete that scale and go and see the patient. So, we actually had a few patients that were never seen because a nurse was never identified and a medical nurse was identified but they never completed their certification, cause they attempted it and failed. I think that speaks to the design of the study, had this study been a hybrid study maybe it would have been a better outcome if that first visit with that specialised scale would have been done in a clinic by the neurologist. And then the rest of the visits be done remotely then it would have maybe done better. Int 24, Vendor

Conversely, our vendor and trial staff interviewees believed that trial staff and DCT participants needed more information and training to prepare them to use unfamiliar technologies.

Responsiveness

Some interviewees regarded technical support as essential. While many were pleased with the 24-hour assistance offered by vendors, particularly during set-up, others mentioned the need to establish their own call centres to troubleshoot.

So we ended up having to hire more people for the call centre to deal with sort of initial teething issues and then as the project persists, it dies down. Int 66, Trial Staff (clinical/research)

But then there are challenges where parents or caregivers are not that savvy with the electronics, so you have to guide them and tell them. So, with our vendor we provided 24-hour service, so if there are any issues they can call the customer service any time, which can help to help them through it. Int 83, Trial Staff (clinical/research)

Securing nurses with appropriate skills at the right time to perform study tasks was a consistent challenge, as a shortage of suitable nurses meant some trials lost potential participants. Trial staff reflected that a hybrid approach with site-based visits for complex initial assessments may have secured higher recruitment. Even when vendors could supply both primary and reserve nurses, trial staff worried that reserve nurses might be prone to errors due to inconsistent practice.

Several trial managers felt they needed to enhance oversight of vendors new to clinical trials. This was considered crucial in transitional periods, where, for example, nurses new to the trial were being trained. Oversight in DCTs can be complex due to the large number of study partners and locations.

I mean certainly different sponsors have different levels of oversight, risk acceptance, … in a data privacy context but some of these other things too. So that’s definitely been a challenge and something that we’ve had to work through and just kind of accept that it will take more oversight and intervention on our part to work with some of these vendors and to manage them than when we work with our standard IxRS vendors or, you know, an ePRO vendor, you know, somebody who’s been working in the industry for 15, 20 years who are used to some of these things. Int 68, Trial Staff (management/administration/operations) (IxRS=interactive voice/web response system)

But I think that, you know, appropriate training really is important and, you know, when there are transitions—whether it’s with the agency or the site, you know, following up to make sure that appropriate training is done is very significant, very important. Int 77, Trial Staff (management/administration/operations)

These findings suggest that clearly defining roles, responsibilities and escalation pathways during trial set-up may reduce ambiguity, improve co-ordination and minimise operational risk.

Perspectives across stakeholder groups

Perspectives across stakeholder groups were broadly consistent, with all groups identifying communication, trust and role clarity as central to the effective delivery of DCTs. However, some differences in emphasis were noted. Trial staff, especially those in management and operational roles, more frequently highlighted challenges related to oversight, regulatory compliance and co-ordination across multiple partners. Vendors tended to focus on issues related to system design, integration and differing expectations between technology providers and trial teams. Clinical site-based staff emphasised more the practical aspects of DCT delivery, including workload, the usability of digital tools and the need for training and support. However, despite these differences, there was broad agreement across the groups regarding the importance of clear communication pathways and defined roles.

Discussion

Our analysis identified three interrelated themes: DCTs as social networks (the connections between individuals and organisations), building trusted relationships and roles and responsibilities, which together highlight how communication, trust and role clarity shape the delivery of DCTs.

DCTs as social networks (the connections between individuals and organisations)

Participants described DCTs as complex, multi-stakeholder systems, characterised by a ‘web of connections’, where information flows across dispersed actors, including trial staff, sites and vendors.

Recognising the complexity of DCTs, Betcheva et al argued that systems thinking could be applied to their planning and deployment.29 Another potentially useful approach to understand relationships between actors (individuals, groups, organisations or systems) in varied settings is social network theory (SNT).30–33 Social network analysis emphasises formal and informal relationships to understand the flow of information within organisations.31 33 It focuses on how actors are connected and how these connections or ‘ties’ influence the behaviours, decisions and outcomes within the network.34 While we have not formally mapped the social networks in our DCT case studies, we infer from our data that social network analysis could be a useful heuristic framework. Five concepts are central to SNT, namely, nodes, edges, centrality, structural holes and bottlenecks.30 32 34–39 We summarise how these concepts could apply in DCTs in Text table 3.

Social network concepts and their applicability to DCTs

DCT, decentralised clinical trial.

One advantage of using a social network approach to assess DCTs is that it permits the visualisation of relationships or missing connections between individuals not apparent in a trial protocol. This could highlight opportunities to enhance information flow and identify potential vulnerabilities. For example, our findings suggest that incorporating new vendors or trial sites into a DCT can introduce structural holes where the flow of information might be impeded. As peripheral actors without clinical trial experience, a technology vendor may have weak ties to other network actors. Therefore, their understanding of the trial may not match the expectations of central trial nodes, such as the sponsor or investigator. Recognising this gap would have informed the trial team of the necessity of explaining and clarifying the trial context and requirements to the vendor.

Another potential use of social network analysis is identifying and empowering highly connected nodes. Individuals or groups within the DCT, such as trial coordinators or experienced study sites, may be able to promote protocol adherence and identify problems early through their influence and connectedness.

Trust and building relationships

Our findings demonstrate that, compared with conventional trials, DCTs demand stronger relationship building due to limited in-person interaction, making communication and trust-building essential competencies. Information exchange is crucial in healthcare for integrating clinical and cultural knowledge and improving outcomes.20 40 Case study interviewees stressed that sharing information is also critical in building trust between partners, such as site clinicians and nurse vendors.

Trust between individuals is shaped by institutional norms. Fisher argues that the pharmaceutical industry’s profit motives and regulatory constraints foster mistrust of trial participants and study sites.41 This mistrust, characterised by reliance on monitoring and audit, can hinder industry sponsors from building trust with external partners. However, the concept of embeddedness suggests that actors’ behaviours are influenced by the broader social context in which they are embedded. Organisations embedded within a dense network of trust and collaboration, where there are repeated interactions and shared expectations, are more likely to adopt cooperative behaviours.30 34 42 43 While core DCT staff may transition to the next trial, researchers, vendors and site-based clinicians may only be brought together for a single trial.

SNT also posits that actors’ choices are shaped by the behaviours and norms of those around them.44 DCT trial staff’s expectations of vendors are influenced by their previous experience with partners well-versed in stringent clinical trial requirements. As DCTs continue to adopt increasingly innovative technology, expectations of a vendor’s tacit knowledge may need to be adjusted.

Although SNT can be criticised for oversimplifying the complexities of human relationships by focusing primarily on the structural features of networks, taking a more actor-centric approach could help researchers explore deeper psychological, cultural and contextual factors.

Roles and responsibilities

Our findings also highlighted the importance of clearly defining roles and responsibilities, identifying the challenges encountered when these elements were not explicitly agreed on. This presents a valuable opportunity to enhance DCTs by proactively discussing and delineating stakeholder responsibilities. These challenges should be understood as arising from system-level coordination in multistakeholder trials, rather than being attributable solely to vendors. As DCTs introduce new partners with varying levels of experience in regulated research, trial teams play a central role in ensuring appropriate selection, integration and oversight.

All parties involved, including trial participants, investigators, sponsors and service providers, must also be able to access the information required to fulfil their responsibilities. In an emergency, an effective communication plan must be in place so that all relevant parties can act without delay. The trial participant should be well informed and know whom to contact about adverse events, device failures or questions.

The findings reinforce the importance of incorporating vendor-related considerations into the trial risk assessment process. Identifying potential risks, such as communication gaps, unclear responsibilities or limited familiarity with regulatory requirements at the design stage would support the development of mitigation strategies, including defined escalation pathways and structured communication plans.

Training also emerged as a key requirement. Participants highlighted the variability in preparedness across stakeholders, suggesting that reliance on protocol documentation alone may be insufficient. Role-specific training, competency assessment and ongoing support may be needed to ensure all parties, including mobile nurses and technology providers, can fulfil trial-specific and regulatory requirements.

Although this study focused on DCTs, the findings may be transferable to other digitally mediated and multi-stakeholder healthcare and research delivery settings where work is coordinated across distributed teams. These include hybrid and pragmatic clinical trials, large multi-site studies, telemedicine-supported services, remote patient monitoring programmes and complex healthcare interventions involving external service providers and technology vendors.

Overall, these findings underline that effective DCT delivery depends on shared responsibility across stakeholders. Trial teams, vendors and site staff must work collaboratively to align expectations, establish clear communication pathways and ensure all parties are adequately supported to perform their roles. These findings are transferable to research settings seeking to implement decentralised methods.

Implications for decentralised clinical trial design and delivery

Our findings suggest several practical considerations for improving the design and delivery of DCTs. First, given the complexity of DCTs as multi-stakeholder systems, trial teams should clearly map communication channels and identify key actors during the set-up stage. This could help to ensure that all parties understand how information flows and where responsibilities lie.

Second, relationship-building should be considered a core component of DCT delivery rather than an informal by-product. Early engagement between trial staff, sites and vendors, potentially including initial in-person and synchronous interactions may support the development of trust and facilitate more effective collaboration throughout the trial.

Third, clear definition of roles, responsibilities and escalation pathways is essential. This means ensuring that all stakeholders, including vendors and mobile healthcare providers, understand regulatory requirements and the trial-specific expectations. Structured onboarding and training may help align such expectations and reduce misunderstandings.

Fourth, hybrid approaches might be beneficial in certain contexts, particularly where complex assessments or specialised skills are required. Initial site-based visits, followed by remote follow-up, could reduce operational challenges while retaining the benefits of decentralisation.

Finally, ongoing support mechanisms, such as a dedicated technical helpdesk and responsive communication channels, may help redress challenges related to digital tools and participant engagement, especially in the early phases of trial implementation.

Limitations

The original case studies from which the follow-up interviews were drawn were predominantly selected from organisations already interested in improving DCT conduct as part of the Trials@Home consortium. As such, they may have had greater experience, resources and motivation to address implementation challenges than in other settings. This may limit the transferability of the findings where DCT experience is limited, and organisational support and infrastructure for DCT delivery are less developed. However, new external case studies were included to ensure more representative results. The significant effect of COVID-19 on global clinical trial operations during our study period meant that we could not fully meet our aim of capturing a diversity of opinions and experiences due to limited potential interviewee availability. As with all case study research, the generalisability of our findings may not be directly applicable to all DCT contexts. However, the case study approach permitted in-depth examination of complex situations that would have been difficult to achieve using broader sampling methods. The time elapsed between interview conduct and publication of this secondary analysis may reduce the applicability of some findings to DCTs employing newer approaches than those used in the included case studies.

This analysis did not include interview data from trial participants or patient representatives. As a result, the findings primarily reflect the perspectives of those involved in trial design and delivery and may not capture how communication, trust and trial processes are experienced by trial participants themselves. However, our purposive sampling of stakeholders involved in the day-to-day running of a DCT, or those with oversight or clinical input, ensured our findings represented the critical factors influencing implementation.

Despite its potential usefulness, there are several challenges in applying SNT to DCTs. These include problems related to data collection, privacy and complexity. Gathering accurate data on interactions, especially informal ones, can be difficult. Interviewees may misremember aspects of their interactions, and observational studies and surveys may not fully capture complexity. Mapping out networks in a DCT may raise concerns about confidentiality and privacy because of the limited number of actors. Additionally, any DCT is likely to be dynamic in response to unexpected foreseen shifts in the external environment (such as changes in laws and regulations), forcing new ways of working and adding a layer of complexity to the analysis of networks.

Conclusion

Communication, trust and building relationships are strongly represented in the accounts of trial team members when asked to reflect on the facilitators and barriers to implementing a DCT. Our findings suggest that analysing DCTs as social networks may help ensure effective lines of communication between all the actors in the network, guaranteeing the flow of necessary information. Since social network analysis permits the visualisation of relationships, it could identify missing connections between individuals which may not be apparent in the trial protocol and operations manuals. Formal organisational information should document and delineate roles, responsibilities and requirements and defined lines of communication will optimise investigator and sponsor trial oversight. Interpersonal contact should be recognised as necessary for building trust between partners with little prior contact.
