Untangling or further entangling? Theoretical expansion of the structural landscape of nucleic acid quadruplexes and modeling with 3D-NuS-Qplex
Authors: Thenmalarchelvi Rathinavelan, Sruthi Sundaresan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuadruplexes are four-stranded nucleic acid secondary structures that play diverse biological roles. A recent meticulous analysis of known quadruplex structures formed from synthetic oligonucleotides shows that quadruplexes can adopt 33 distinct structural motifs (pairs of stacked quartets) that serve as scaffolds for simple (multi-layered quartets) and complex (multi-motifs) quadruplex folds. These motifs differ from one another in terms of interstrand connectivity and orientation. Interstrand connectivity is primarily governed by diagonal loops that connect alternate strands and lateral and propeller loops that connect adjacent strands. These loops are absent in tetramers, wherein interstrand orientation alone dictates the motif type. While the diversity of potential quadruplex-forming sequences, enriched in functionally important regions of the human genome, implicates their structural diversity, a key question that still remains unresolved is the complete landscape of quadruplex folds. For the first time, a total of 156 unique theoretically possible quadruplex motifs that cover the quadruplex fold landscape are mathematically devised here. Given the difficulty in capturing the transient quadruplex intermediates that mediate the quadruplex folding process, the folds reported here may shed light on the possible quadruplex intermediates. A tool named 3D-NuS-Qplex (https://project.iith.ac.in/3d-nus-qplex/) has also been developed to model all the theoretically possible energy-minimized quadruplex folds with options to incorporate overhangs and non-G-quartets (in addition to G-quartets) in both DNA and RNA contexts. The diversity of quadruplex folds reported here provides new insight into their structural complexity, which appears greater than previously anticipated, and may thus facilitate understanding of quadruplex folding, quadruplex–protein, and quadruplex–ligand interactions.

Introduction

Nucleic acids adopt a wide range of secondary structures besides the right-handed double-helical B-form [1–5]. One among them is a four-stranded nucleic acid structure known as a quadruplex or tetraplex, which was discovered even before the discovery of the double helical structure [6]. There is substantial evidence for the biological roles of these secondary structures formed by DNA and RNA sequences [7–16]. Besides, these structures also play a crucial role in several disease conditions [17].

About 500 quadruplex structures, mostly corresponding to synthetic oligonucleotides, have been solved predominantly using X-ray crystallography, followed by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy [18, 19]. These structures are formed by G-rich sequences that form G-quartets, in which four guanines interact through Hoogsteen hydrogen bonds (Fig. 1A and B). There is also increasing experimental evidence for the occurrence of non-G quartets within G-quadruplex structures. However, these non-G quartets are significantly less frequent than canonical G-quartets [20]. Experimentally determined structures have revealed nine types of non-G quartets (Fig. S1), demonstrating that quadruplexes can accommodate non-G quartets. For instance, studies have identified a GCGC quartet in viral DNA quadruplex [21], a TTTT quartet in Saccharomyces cerevisiae telomeric quadruplex [22], and an AAAA quartet in human telomeric quadruplex [23]. Non-G quartets have also been reported in regulatory regions of DNA, such as ACGCA-rich sequences [24]. Repeat expansion sequences, such as CGG repeats linked to FXTAS, can adopt quadruplex structures despite the presence of noncanonical base interactions [17]. Similarly, quadruplex structure formation is observed in GGGGCC hexanucleotide repeat expansions in the C9orf72 gene linked to ALS/FTD [25–27]. Nevertheless, non-G quartets generally contribute less to structural stability and often rely on adjacent G-quartets for stabilization [28].

Reviews on various aspects of quadruplex folding patterns have appeared from time to time [18, 19, 29–31]. An earlier study showed that G-rich sequences can fold into 26 monomers, considering the permissible loop combinations [30]. The same study revealed 16 possible glycosyl conformations for the G-quartets of the quadruplex fold. A recent article has shown, based on the analysis of experimentally determined structures, that quadruplexes can be represented in a modular fashion, in which a pair of stacked quartets, namely, a motif (Fig. 1C), serves as the scaffold for quadruplex folds. These structural motifs can be monomers (formed by a single sequence), dimers (two sequences), trimers (three sequences), or tetramers (four sequences). It is noteworthy that each of these motifs has four strands (refer to Fig. 1C for the definition of strand numbers). The same study identified 33 different motifs through the analysis of quadruplex structures deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB), which differ among themselves in terms of the relative orientations between the four strands and the loops that connect them [18, 19]. Notably, if a loop connects adjacent strands that interact through base–base hydrogen bonds in a parallel and antiparallel orientation, it is called a propeller and lateral loop, respectively (Fig. 1D). Further, a diagonal loop connects two alternate strands (which are not involved in base–base hydrogen bonding interaction) in an antiparallel orientation (Fig. 1D). Although a loop can theoretically connect two alternate strands (not involved in base–base hydrogen bonding interaction) in a parallel orientation (Fig. 1D; henceforth referred to as diagonal-parallel), it has not yet been observed in experimental structures. Another point to note is that a motif can even be discontinuous, viz., the adjacent quartets of all four strands in a quadruplex fold need not be connected [18]. A quadruplex fold can be formed using a single motif (Fig. 1E), and higher-order structures can be formed by combinations of motifs [18]. This further suggests that many undiscovered quadruplex structures may exist in nature, arising from the combination of different motifs.

There have been several attempts to develop algorithms (including machine learning approaches) to predict quadruplex folds from a given sequence [32–35]. Indeed, several computational tools have been developed to predict a sequence’s likelihood of forming a quadruplex structure, including G4ShapePredictor [32], G4Boost [33], and G4Hunter [36]. However, these tools provide estimates of quadruplex propensity and stability rather than generating 3D models. At the structural level, a number of resources have been developed for the analysis and characterization of experimentally determined quadruplexes. For instance, the ONQUADRO database [37] provides experimentally solved quadruplex structures, enabling systematic comparison of structural features. Tools such as WebTetrado [38] facilitate the identification, visualization, and analysis of quadruplex structures, while ASC-G4 [39] enables detailed quantification of structural parameters such as twist and rise. While Web3DNA 2.0 [40] can also be useful for quadruplex structural analysis and visualization, it supports the model building of a tetrameric quadruplex structure using predefined fiber-diffraction-based geometrical parameters. However, these tools are primarily designed to analyze existing structures and do not support modeling different types of quadruplex folds. Nonetheless, the 3D-NuS tool [41] can generate 3D structures for a limited set of quadruplex folds. Although 3D-NuS represents an important step toward modeling quadruplex structures, it is restricted to only 19 distinct quadruplex folds. Automated modeling of quadruplex structures thus remains relatively unexplored. To address this limitation, 3D-NuS-Qplex substantially broadens the accessible structural landscape of quadruplex folds by enabling the modeling of 156 unique theoretically possible quadruplex motifs, including the option to add overhangs and non-G quartets. This facilitates systematic exploration of quadruplex structural diversity beyond currently available experimental structures and existing computational tools.

Nonetheless, predicting quadruplex structures from a given sequence remains a major challenge due to a lack of precise understanding of interstrand orientation and connectivity, which contribute to the diversity of quadruplex motifs. Additionally, structural diversity arises from the wide range of combinations these motifs can adopt. Deriving theoretically possible quadruplex motifs (the scaffolds for quadruplex folds) would be highly instrumental in understanding the fold-dependent biological roles of quadruplexes. Thus, this study explores all theoretically possible continuous monomeric, dimeric, trimeric, and tetrameric quadruplex motifs. Further, a tool, namely, 3D-NuS-Qplex, has been developed to model all the theoretically derived quadruplex folds.

Materials and methods

Implementation of a web interface for the automated modeling of quadruplex folds

The motifs defined in Figs 2 and S2–S4 can be extended to form complete quadruplex folds, viz., a multi-layered G-quartet. This highlights the need for the development of a tool capable of modeling all theoretically feasible quadruplex motifs to explore their biological relevance. Notably, only a limited number of such motifs have been reported in experimentally determined structures [18]. Indeed, an existing web tool models only a limited number of quadruplex folds [41]. Here, a web server, namely, 3D-NuS-Qplex (https://project.iith.ac.in/3d-nus-qplex/), is developed to model these quadruplex folds by applying a helical twist (30°) and rise (3.0 Å) between the adjacent quartets. Note that a recent study has reported that the helical twist of quadruplex structures falls in the range of 10°–40° [42]. Thus, to model all the theoretically possible quadruplex folds, 30° is chosen here as the helical twist. Similarly, the helical rise is chosen to be 3.0 Å. However, the values mentioned above are chosen to be constant across all the folds to model a broad range of quadruplex folds defined by all the motifs (Figs 2 and S2–S4). Furthermore, 3D-NuS-Qplex also facilitates the incorporation of nine different non-G-quartets (Fig. S1), which are observed alongside G-quartets in experimentally determined structures deposited in the PDB [18, 19]. 3D-NuS-Qplex also supports modeling of quadruplex structures with 5′ and/or 3′ overhangs. The web interface of 3D-NuS-Qplex is shown in Fig. 3.

Snapshots of the 3D-NuS-Qplex interface. The workflow begins with the input interface shown on the left, where the user has to select the quadruplex type, fold type, the type of sequence (DNA/RNA), number of quartets, residues in each strand, loops, overhang residues (if any), and the positions of the non-G quartets (if any). After submitting the required inputs to model the quadruplex structure, the output interface on the right displays a consolidated summary of these inputs and an interactive view of the generated model using JSmol. A video tutorial that succinctly explains the usage of the tool can be found at: https://youtu.be/U0VhdGoxP_I.

Through the HTML interface (Fig. 3), the user has to provide information about the quadruplex motif to be used as a template (by selecting the appropriate motif image), which is to be used as a template for the fold, sequence type (DNA or RNA), number of quartets in the quadruplex fold, input residues individually for the four quadruplex strands (representing the G-quartets as well as non-G-quartets, if any), loop sequence as applicable, 5′ and 3′ overhang residues for each sequence (if applicable), and position of the non-G residues as in each strand. Note that in the case of a monomer, the sequence type must either be DNA or RNA, since the four strands of the quadruplex are connected. However, for the dimer (4 combinations), trimer (8 combinations), and tetramer (16 combinations), the sequence can be either DNA, RNA, or a combination of both. Once the input is submitted, the HTML interface passes it to a PHP script. The PHP subsequently decodes the selected quadruplex motif type into a 10-letter alphanumeric pattern. For instance, 1a-2p|3a-4 represents an antiparallel dimeric motif named DA7 (Fig. S2). Here, the first two strands (indicated by ‘1’ and ‘2’) are oriented in an antiparallel fashion (indicated by ‘a’), second and third (indicated by ‘3’) are oriented in a parallel fashion (indicated by ‘p’) and the third and fourth (indicated by ‘4’) strands are oriented in an antiparallel fashion (indicated by ‘a’). While ‘|’ indicates the separation of two independent sequences, ‘-’ indicates the two connected strands. Thus, the fold 1a-2p|3a-4 represents a dimer, wherein the strands 1 and 2 are formed by one sequence, and another sequence forms strands 3 and 4. Similarly, 1p-4p|3p-2 is a dimer (DP5) with a different connectivity. 1p-2p-3p-4 encodes a monomer (named as MP1), wherein all four strands are connected in a parallel fashion. 1a|2a|3a|4 represents a tetrameric motif, QA3, in which the strands are in an antiparallel fashion with respect to each other. PHP passes this information to a bash script, which uses FORTRAN code to generate the quadruplex fold coordinates and arranges the residue numbers as per the connectivity. Soon after the job is submitted, the user is provided with a molecule ID to retrieve the results later (Fig. 3).

Quadruplex fold generation and optimization

As described above, quadruplex folds comprising both G and non-G quartets are generated using the specified helical twist and rise parameters. For structural elements like the propeller, lateral, diagonal, and diagonal-parallel loops, as well as 5′- and 3′-overhangs, the initial coordinates are generated based on the input nucleotide sequence by applying the helical rise using a bash script. Subsequently, the bash script generates the initial coordinates for the quadruplex folds, with steric hindrances and a disjointed backbone. Although the diagonal-parallel loop (viz., two alternate strands connected in parallel orientation) has not yet been observed in any of the experimentally reported structures, the tool is designed to model motifs with diagonal-parallel loops, since they are theoretically possible. Similarly, motifs containing two crossing diagonals, which are present on the same side (DA3 motif in Fig. S2), are also not observed experimentally but can be modeled by 3D-NuS-Qplex. In such cases, the users are advised to provide longer loop sequences to avoid steric hindrances. Subsequently, the generated models are subjected to the Powell minimization algorithm using Xplor-NIH [43], an updated version of Xplor [44], to establish proper backbone connectivity and remove steric hindrance.

Initially, 1000 cycles of Powell minimization are performed while constraining (viz., not allowing them to move) the base atoms, and allowing the backbone atoms to move freely. During this round, only the dihedral energy terms are considered. In subsequent rounds of minimization, quartet planarity and hydrogen-bonding distance (using a soft asymptotic potential) restraints, along with chi and sugar pucker restraints, are introduced. All the bonded energy terms are included, while van der Waals and electrostatic energy terms are included as discussed below. In the next step, the loop residues are kept free to remove the steric hindrance and establish connectivity between adjacent nucleotides. If more than one loop is present (note that monomer has the highest number of three loops and tetramer has zero loops), the loops are minimized one at a time. During this minimization cycle, all the quadruplex strands are also fixed. The minimization of a single loop occurs in six rounds, with each round involving 1000 cycles of Powell minimization. During the first four rounds of minimization, atom-based nonbonded interactions (between the atoms whose mass is greater than zero) were considered with a cut-off value of 100 and by excluding the nonbonded interactions between bonded atoms, atoms that are bonded to a common third atom, and the atoms that are connected to each other through three bonds. While the electrostatic energy term is set to be off, the weightage of the van der Waals energy term is increased in each round (viz., 0.002, 0.005, 0.01, and 0.5). At the fifth round, the electrostatic energy is included, and the van der Waals energy term is given a weightage of 1. Atom-based nonbonded interactions are considered with a cutoff of 4.5, and the nonbonded interactions are excluded as mentioned above, but with the inclusion of one to four nonbonded interactions. During the last round, 1000 cycles for Powell minimization are extended by increasing the weightage of the dihedral restraints to 2. Similarly, overhang residues are also minimized. Soon after all the loop residues are individually minimized (while constraining other loop and quadruplex strands), all the loop residues are left free for the next round of minimization. During the next round, each strand of the quadruplex is minimized individually to remove steric hindrance and establish internucleotide connectivity following the abovementioned six rounds of minimization protocol. Here, initially, the last (fourth) strand is left free while the first three are constrained. Subsequently, the third, second, and first strands are minimized one at a time. Following this, the fourth strand is minimized again, as mentioned above, and the third, second, and first strands are included in subsequent steps. In the final round, all strands, loops, and overhang residues are not constrained, they are only restrained. Six rounds of minimization are carried out as mentioned above.

Users can access the results using the molecule ID provided at the time of submission. Finally, the modeled quadruplex structures can be interactively viewed using JSmol enabled within the HTML interface (Fig. 3). Additionally, users can also download the PDB and XPLOR input files for further analysis. The entire process is managed by a PHP script. Figure 3 illustrates the overall workflow of 3D-NuS-Qplex, while Figs S5 and S6 provide representative examples of the quadruplex models generated by 3D-NuS-Qplex.

Results and discussion

Theoretical formulation of possible quadruplex motifs

Figure 4A depicts the possible sequential arrangements among the four strands of a quadruplex motif. The encircled “1” (innermost circle) in Fig. 4A represents the strand number 1 (which is the first strand) of the quadruplex motif (refer to Fig. 1C), which progresses toward the outermost circle (representing the last strand). The blue-colored encircled number (Fig. 4A) indicates the next strand (which can be strand number 2, 3, or 4, as shown in Fig. 1C) that is connected to the first strand of the quadruplex motif. Similarly, the next two consecutive strand numbers indicate the sequential order of the third (encircled in pink in Fig. 4A) and fourth (encircled in green in Fig. 4A) strands of the quadruplex motif, respectively. For instance, if the connectivity is between strand number 1 (first strand) and 4 (second strand) (triangle shaded in yellow color, Fig. 4A), the next connectivity could be either to strand number 2 or 3 (Fig. 4A). If the strand number 4 is connected to the strand number 3 (third strand), then the last strand (outermost circle, colored in green) of the quadruplex would be the strand number 2 (fourth strand). Thus, the strand connectivity proceeds in the following order: 1➔4➔3➔2 in this example (triangle shaded in yellow color in Fig. 4A). On the other hand, if the strand number 4 is connected to the strand number 2, it results in a motif with the following connectivity: 1➔4➔2➔3. Thus, four different quadruplex strands can be connected in six unique ways: 1➔2➔3➔4; 1➔2➔4➔3; 1➔3➔2➔4; 1➔3➔4➔2; 1➔4➔2➔3; 1➔4➔3➔2 (Fig. 4A).

While the above description provides six possible connectivities between different strand numbers of a quadruplex motif, incorporation of strand directionality (viz., parallel [p] or antiparallel [a] orientation) brings further diversity into the quadruplex motif. For instance, in the above-mentioned 1➔4➔3➔2 motif, strand number 1 can be connected to strand number 4 in a parallel or an antiparallel fashion. Next, strand numbers 4 and 3 can be parallel or antiparallel with respect to each other. Finally, strand numbers 3 and 2 can also be connected in a parallel or an antiparallel orientation. Note that the orientation between the first and last strands is automatically taken care of. Considering these possibilities, 1➔4➔3➔2 connectivity can give rise to eight different possible motifs through the inclusion of interstrand orientations: 1p➔4p➔3p➔2, 1p➔4p➔3a➔2, 1p➔4a➔3p➔2, 1a➔4p➔3p➔2, 1p➔4a➔3a➔2, 1a➔4p➔3a➔2, 1a➔4a➔3p➔2, and 1a➔4a➔3a➔2 (Fig. 4B). The eight orientations include one parallel (all four strands are in the same direction, denoted as P1 in Fig. 4B), three antiparallel (two strands are in one direction and two strands are in the opposite direction, denoted as A1–A3), and four hybrid (three strands are in one direction and one strand is in the opposite direction, denoted as H1–H4) motifs. Thus, incorporation of the strand directionality leads to eight different motifs (Fig. 4B) for each of the six connectivities (Fig. 4A). Thus, six types of connectivity and eight interstrand directionality give rise to 48 quadruplex motifs (6 strand connectivity × 8 strand orientations = 48) (Fig. 4B, Table S1).

When each of the aforementioned 48 motifs is formed by a single sequence, in which all four strands are continuously connected through three loops, it is called a monomeric motif. Note that the type of loops (propeller, lateral, diagonal, and diagonal-parallel; refer to Fig. 1D) depends on the orientations of the connected strands. Considering this, the diversity of the only possible parallel motif (P1 in Fig. 1B, wherein all the strands are in parallel orientation) arises out of the order of interstrand connectivity (Fig. 4A) established through propeller and diagonal-parallel loops, as only these can connect two parallel strands. In contrast, lateral and diagonal loops do not occur in the parallel motif as they can only connect antiparallel strands. Thus, based on the order of connectivity, six parallel motifs are possible. In addition to this, 18 antiparallel (denoted as A1, A2, and A3 in Fig. 4B) motifs are possible when strand connectivity is considered. Notably, in the cases of A1 and A2, starting from strand number 1, the interstrand orientations between adjacent strands are alternately parallel and antiparallel. In A1, the strand numbers 1&2, 2&3, 3&4, and 4&1 are parallel, antiparallel, parallel, and antiparallel, respectively. In contrast, in the case of A2, the strand numbers 1&2, 2&3, 3&4, and 4&1 are antiparallel, parallel, antiparallel, and parallel, respectively. In A3, all the strands are antiparallel with respect to each other. Thus, there are three distinct classes of antiparallel motifs. Furthermore, each of these three classes can give rise to six distinct motifs depending on the order of strand connectivity, as depicted in Fig. 4A. In a similar fashion, each of the four hybrid motifs (denoted as H1, H2, H3, and H4 in Fig. 4B) yields 24 motifs in total. Figure 4C illustrates a different representation of Fig. 4A, in which the number indicated at each layer of the circle represents the sequential arrangement of connectivity between different quadruplex strand numbers.

Similarly, one can derive the number of possible dimeric motifs. Dimeric motifs can be derived from each monomeric motif by removing one loop from each of the 48 motifs. This can be achieved in two different ways. In the first type, the dimer is formed by two independent sequences (indicated by the blue- and purple-colored circles in Fig. 4D), with one loop in each sequence connecting two quadruplex strands. The second type of the dimeric fold occurs when the first strand of the quadruplex is formed by an independent sequence (indicated by the blue-colored circle in Fig. 4E). Note that one can also keep any one of the other strands as the first strand, which would essentially lead to similar motifs. The remaining three strands of the quadruplex are formed by the second sequence (indicated by the purple-colored circle in Fig. 4E). Note that the white-colored circle in the midst of the blue and purple circles (Fig. 4D and E) indicates that the two strands are independent and not connected. As mentioned in the case of monomers, the direction of the sequence (5′ to 3′) proceeds from inner to outer circle (in both purple- and blue-colored circles). The aforementioned dimeric types can give rise to 96 theoretical dimeric motifs (48 possible motifs in each type, based on strand orientation and connectivity, as discussed for monomers).

Trimeric motifs can also be derived from monomeric motifs by removing two loops, or from dimeric motifs by removing one loop. The trimeric motif pattern is represented in Fig. 4F, where the first strand corresponds to the innermost circle (indicated by a blue-colored circle). The second independent strand is shown as a purple circle (Fig. 4F). The remaining two connected strands of the quadruplex are shown in green (Fig. 4F). The 5′ to 3′ direction proceeds from the innermost blue-colored circle to the outermost circle. Note that the white circle indicates that the strands are independent and not connected. A total of 48 trimeric folds are possible based on strand orientation and connectivity.

Similarly, tetrameric motifs can be derived from monomeric, dimeric, or trimeric motifs by the removal of three, two, and one loops, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4G, the four strands (colored in blue, purple, green, and yellow) are not connected and independent (indicated by a white circle).

Theoretically possible unique quadruplex motifs

Monomeric motifs

Figure 2 illustrates all possible 48 monomeric motifs obtained from six interstrand connectivity (Fig. 4A) and eight interstrand orientations (Fig. 4B). As discussed previously, there are eight different orientations for each connectivity. In other words, there are six different connectivities for each possible strand orientation. In Fig. 2, under the “1-2-3-4” column, the sequential order of connectivity between quadruplex motif strands is 1, 2, 3, and 4, which can have eight different interstrand orientations: 1p-2p-3p-4 (MP1), 1p-2a-3p-4 (MA1), 1a-2p-3a-4 (MA7), 1a-2a-3a-4 (MA13), 1a-2a-3p-4 (MH1), 1p-2a-3a-4 (MH7), 1p-2p-3a-4 (MH13), and 1a-2p-3p-4 (MH19). Thus, a total of 6 × 8 = 48 monomeric (M) quadruplex motifs are possible based on the interstrand connectivity and orientations. Notably, based on interstrand orientations, the type of connecting loop is determined as discussed previously. The parallel motif P1 (Fig. 4B) has all four strands parallel to each other. However, the difference in interstrand connectivity gives rise to six different motifs (indicated in the first row of Fig. 2 under the “Parallel” label): 1p-2p-3p-4 (MP1), 1p-2p-4p-3 (MP2), 1p-3p-2p-4 (MP3), 1p-3p-4p-2 (MP4), 1p-4p-2p-3 (MP5), and 1p-4p-3p-2 (MP6). The MP1 motif (column 1, row 1) differs from MP2 (column 2, row 1) due to the connectivity: while the former has the sequential order of strand connectivity as 1➔2➔3➔4, the latter has 1➔2➔4➔3. As discussed above, adjacent parallel strands are connected through a propeller loop, while alternate strands are connected through a diagonal-parallel loop (not observed in experimental structures) (Fig. 1D). Another point to note is the crossing of diagonal-parallel loops observed in MP3 and MP4, which may be feasible with a greater number of loop residues. While only MP1 and MP6 have been observed in experimental structures so far [18], the possibility of MP2, MP3, MP4, and MP5 motifs occurring in nature cannot be ruled out.

As explained in Fig. 4B, there are three different classes (A1–A3) of antiparallel motifs. As shown in Fig. 2, propeller, lateral, diagonal, and diagonal-parallel loops are all possible in antiparallel motifs; however, each motif includes a combination of at most two of these loop types. Notably, MP3 and MP4, as well as MA3 and MA10 motifs (Fig. 2), contain two crossing diagonal loops, which may be sterically unfavorable [30] or may require a greater number of loop residues. Hybrid motifs (H1–H4, Fig. 4B) can have a combination of two or three types of loops (Fig. 2). Thus, there are 48 unique monomeric motifs that do not share rotational symmetry with one another. This is because monomeric motifs are made up of a single sequence, with only one starting and ending position, and the starting position is fixed at the top-right corner (Fig. 1C).

Dimeric motifs

Typically, a dimeric (D) motif can be generated from a monomeric fold (MP1, Fig. 5A) in three different ways. As shown in Fig. 5B, removal of the propeller loop connecting strands 2 and 3 in the MP1 motif (Fig. 2) leads to a dimeric motif (DP1) comprising two independent sequences, each containing a propeller loop that connects two strands. On the other hand, the removal of the propeller between strand numbers 1 and 2 leads to a different type of dimeric motif (DP7) (Fig. 5C), in which three strands are connected by two consecutive propeller loops, while the other strand is independent. Although removal of the propeller loop between strand numbers 3 and 4 is possible (Fig. 5D), it does not lead to a different motif. This is because this motif is identical to that obtained by the removal of the propeller loop between strand numbers 1 and 2 (Fig. 5C). This arises from the rotational symmetry shared by the two motifs (Fig. 5C and D). Thus, as discussed above, only two distinct dimeric motif patterns are possible from a monomer. This may theoretically result in 96 dimeric motifs (Fig. S2) (instead of 144) arising from the 48 monomeric motifs (Fig. 2). Furthermore, in some cases, dimeric motifs arising from two different monomeric motifs can be identical. For instance, the removal of the diagonal-parallel loop between strand numbers 2 and 4 in MP2 (Fig. 2) and strand numbers 4 and 2 in MP5 (Fig. 2) leads to the same motif (DP2 and DP5 in Fig. S2). This scenario arises from motifs formed by two independent sequences, each containing a loop (Fig. S2, shown above the dotted line), reducing the total from 48 to 32 possible motifs (Fig. S2). Thus, a total of 80 unique theoretical dimeric motifs are possible (Fig. S2). As observed in the case of monomers, there is a possibility of two loops crossing over each other. This crossing can occur in different combinations, such as (diagonal)–(diagonal) (as in DA3, Fig. S2), (diagonal-parallel)–(diagonal-parallel) (as in DA15, Fig. S2), and (diagonal)–(diagonal-parallel) (as in DH3, Fig. S2).

Trimeric motifs

Similar to dimeric motifs, a trimeric (T) motif can be derived either from a dimeric motif by removing one loop or from a monomeric motif by removing two loops. This results in a motif comprising three different sequences, in which only two strands are connected by a loop, while the others remain unconnected and independent (Fig. 5E). The example shown in Fig. 5E illustrates the derivation of a trimer motif from a dimer. Intriguingly, removal of any propeller loops in DP1 (Fig. 5B) or DP7 (Fig. 5C and D) leads to four possible motifs with two independent strands. Since any of these independent strands can be considered as the first strand, it leads to rotational symmetry; thus, only one unique trimeric motif is obtained. Similarly, two different dimeric motifs, DP1 and DP7, derived from the same monomeric motif, MP1, converge to a single trimeric motif, TP1. Thus, one may initially envisage that there are 48 unique theoretical trimeric motifs (Fig. S3). However, since only two strands are connected in a trimer, the trimers derived from two different monomeric or dimeric motifs can again lead to the same trimeric motif due to the rotational symmetry. One such example is MA1 (Fig. 2) (leads to the DA1 and DA19 dimers, Fig. S2) and MA11 (Fig. 2) (leads to the DA11 and DA29 dimers, Fig. S2) that lead to the same trimer TA1 (Fig. S3). Thus, the number of unique theoretical trimeric motifs reduces to 24 (Fig. S3).

Tetrameric motifs

A tetrameric motif can be derived from a trimeric motif by removing the single connecting loop (Fig. 5E–H). This results in four independent strands within the quadruplex motif. Thus, interstrand connectivity no longer plays a role, as any of the four independent strands can serve as the first strand. Thus, only eight tetrameric motifs can be theoretically defined based on interstrand orientations (Fig. S4). Furthermore, due to the rotational symmetry between the two antiparallel motifs (A1 and A2 in Fig. 4B), in which two consecutive strands are oriented in one direction, and the other two in the opposite direction, both ultimately give rise to the same tetrameric motif. Similarly, all four hybrid motifs (H1–H4 in Fig. 4B) result in a single motif. Thus, a total of four unique tetrameric motifs is possible.

Collectively, 156 unique quadruplex motifs are theoretically possible, which include 48 monomeric (Fig. 2), 80 dimeric (Fig. S2), 24 trimeric (Fig. S3), and 4 tetrameric (Fig. S4) motifs. This number is substantially higher than the quadruplex motifs identified through experimental structures (33 motifs [18]). This discrepancy may be attributed to the influence of sequence and environmental conditions. This highlights the need for detailed computational investigation, such as molecular dynamics simulations, to identify the energetically favorable motif(s) and their motif-sequence specificity. Importantly, 156 unique quadruplex motifs reported here enlighten the importance of incorporating motif-sequence dependency in predicting quadruplex-forming regions using machine learning approaches.

Validation and limitations of 3D-NuS-Qplex structural models

Superposition of quadruplex structures generated using 3D-NuS-Qplex with experimentally determined quadruplex structures from the PDB indicates that the root mean square deviation values (RMSD) fall in the range of 0.578–2.680 Å (Table S2). It is noteworthy that the generated models contain planar quartets, whereas experimental structures often exhibit deviations from planarity, which may also be the reason for higher RMSD values observed in some cases. To capture a broad spectrum of quadruplex folds, many of which remain to be experimentally characterized, a helical rise of 3.0 Å and a twist of 30° are employed based on previous studies [45, 42]. The models generated by 3D-NuS-Qplex are intended to serve as initial structural models, wherein parameters such as helical twist and rise may be further optimized for specific motifs.

Although the conceptual framework described here identifies 123 new theoretically possible quadruplex motifs, in addition to the experimentally known 33 motifs [18], these motifs remain to be experimentally characterized, and their stability and potential roles in quadruplex folding pathways need to be further explored. Previously, a few computational [46–50] and experimental [51, 52] studies have reported the formation of complex and structurally heterogeneous intermediate conformations during quadruplex folding. 3D-NuS-Qplex can therefore facilitate further exploration of this structural landscape by enabling users to generate models for specific sequences and motifs. These models can serve as initial structures for atomistic molecular dynamics simulations tailored to explore the energy landscapes of specific DNA/RNA sequences and motifs of interest.

In addition to structural diversity at the motif level, variability in the number of quartets further expands the conformational space of quadruplex structures. Recent studies have extended the quadruplex structures beyond the three-quartet architectures commonly observed in experimentally determined structures. A study demonstrated that stable quadruplex structures can form with as few as two stacked quartets, and are supported by favorable stacking interactions and cation coordination [53]. Another study, using bioinformatics and experimental analyses, showed that quadruplex structures formed by five or six quartets can also form and remain stable [54]. Similarly, genomic analysis has identified sequences capable of forming extended quadruplex structures with five or six quartets [55]. Together, these studies highlight that quadruplex structures can adopt a broader range of quartets than traditionally assumed. In the current implementation of the 3D-NuS-Qplex tool, individual motifs can be modeled with any number of quartets, allowing users to generate structures representing both minimal and extended quartet stacks, as reported in the literature (Fig. S7). As shown in Fig. S7, quadruplex structures with a varying number of quartets can be modeled. Notably, increasing the number of quartets increases computational cost and calculation time. Loop lengths must also be appropriately defined based on the number of quartets and loop type, particularly for strands connected in parallel orientation.

However, higher-order quadruplex assemblies composed of more than one distinct motif (PDB ID: 1NYD) or with the same motif (PDB ID: 2N3M) interconnected through loops [18] cannot currently be modeled using 3D-NuS-Qplex. Additionally, the generated models represent initial conformations, and not necessarily the global minima, and complex quadruplex folds arising from combinations of different motifs (such as those involving both left-handed and right-handed motifs) are not supported in the current implementation. In future versions, this limitation could be addressed by enabling modular assembly of multiple motifs, allowing users to define the loops connecting them and their relative orientations to create higher-order structures.

Role of 3D-NuS-Qplex in understanding the biological significance of fold diversity

Since the first high-resolution crystal structure of a DNA quadruplex was reported in 1992 [56], an increasing number of quadruplex folds have been determined experimentally. A recent study identified 33 unique quadruplex folds from structures deposited in the PDB [18]. By understanding the diversity of potential quadruplex-forming sequences (PQSs), which are widespread across human, viral, and plant genomes [57, 58], one can envisage even greater diversity in quadruplex structural folds. Since PQSs are enriched in functionally important regions such as telomeres, oncogene promoters, untranslated regions, and regulatory elements [57], understanding their structural diversity is crucial. However, the 3D structures of the vast majority of biologically relevant PQSs remain unavailable, limiting structure-based interpretations of the biological functions of quadruplexes. Furthermore, quadruplex folding is known to proceed via multiple pathways, involving distinct intermediate structures that differ in strand orientation and quadruplex fold type [59–61], many of which are difficult to capture experimentally [59]. In this context, the systematic revelation of not only all possible nucleic acid quadruplex folds for the first time, but also the development of a tool to model them, could help bridge this gap in understanding the quadruplex folding pathway, as well as the functional aspects of PQSs in their structural context. Thus, 3D-NuS-Qplex enables researchers to build structures beyond the constraints of experimentally solved structures.

Conclusions

This study mathematically reveals 156 unique theoretically possible quadruplex motifs (two-layered quartets that serve as scaffolds for the multi-layered folds) based on the strand connectivity and their relative orientations. While only 33 of these motifs have been found experimentally, the revelation of 156 possible motifs provides a new direction for exploring the diverse biological roles of these quadruplex folds. Insights from the experimental structures suggest that the combination of these motifs can further form complex higher-order quadruplex structures. Thus, one can envision that a greater number of higher-order quadruplex structures (arising from these 156 motifs) may exist in nature than those observed experimentally, further complicating our understanding of these structures and their biological relevance. A web tool, 3D-NuS-Qplex, is also developed here to model all the theoretically possible quadruplex folds, incorporating 5′ and/or 3′-overhangs, non-G-quartets, and different combinations of DNA and/or RNA strands. Since the quadruplex structures are found to play a crucial role in several biological functions and diseases, this study brings a new dimension to the (i) understanding of quadruplex fold dependency on biological functions and diseases, (ii) enabling fold-specific ligand design, and (iii) facilitating the rational design of synthetic quadruplex folds for nanotechnological applications.
