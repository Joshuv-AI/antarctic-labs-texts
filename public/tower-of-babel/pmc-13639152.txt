Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the most significant infectious diseases globally and continues to pose a major public health challenge despite decades of control efforts. TB is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis and is primarily transmitted through airborne droplets when individuals with active pulmonary TB cough, sneeze, or speak. According to the latest global estimates reported in the Global Tuberculosis Report, approximately 10.8 million people developed TB worldwide in 2023, and the disease caused about 1.25 million deaths globally, including deaths among people living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) [1]. These data highlight that TB continues to be a major global health burden, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where social and environmental determinants strongly influence transmission and disease progression.

The global burden of TB is unevenly distributed geographically. The World Health Organization reported that Southeast Asia alone contributes a substantial share of the global TB burden. Furthermore, the persistence of TB indicates that biomedical interventions alone are insufficient, and broader determinants—including environmental and occupational conditions—must also be addressed to effectively control transmission and reduce incidence rates [2].

Another important characteristic of the global TB epidemic is its high prevalence among the adult and productive-age population. Epidemiological data show that the largest numbers of TB cases occur among adults aged 25-54 years. In 2024, adult men aged 15 years and older accounted for approximately 54% of the estimated TB cases worldwide, while adult women contributed around 35%, indicating that the majority of TB cases occur in adults who are actively engaged in the workforce. It may have consequences that extend beyond health outcomes, including loss of income, reduced workforce productivity, and increased economic burden on households and societies [3].

Multiple factors contribute to the high burden of TB among adults and workers. TB transmission is influenced by biological, behavioral, socioeconomic, and environmental determinants. Conditions such as HIV infection, malnutrition, smoking, alcohol use, poverty, overcrowding, and limited healthcare access may increase susceptibility to TB infection and disease progression. In addition, occupational exposure and poor working conditions can elevate TB risk, particularly in environments where airborne transmission is more likely to occur. These findings emphasize the multifactorial nature of TB and the importance of occupational and environmental approaches in TB prevention strategies [4].

Occupational and workplace-related exposures are increasingly recognized as important determinants of TB transmission among workers. Several occupational sectors, including healthcare, mining, construction, and industry, expose workers to environmental hazards that may increase susceptibility to TB infection. Healthcare workers, for example, have a higher risk of TB due to repeated exposure to patients with active TB, particularly in settings with inadequate infection prevention and control measures. In addition, workplace physical conditions such as poor ventilation, overcrowding, inadequate indoor air quality, temperature, humidity, and exposure to airborne dust can influence the transmission of M. tuberculosis [5].

In addition to ventilation and indoor air quality, exposure to occupational dust, particularly silica dust, has been strongly associated with increased TB risk among workers. Silica exposure commonly occurs in industries such as mining, construction, and manufacturing. Long-term inhalation of respirable crystalline silica particles can lead to silicosis, a chronic lung disease characterized by inflammation and fibrosis. Importantly, silicosis significantly increases susceptibility to pulmonary TB, and individuals with silica exposure may develop a condition known as silicotuberculosis [6]. Evidence from systematic reviews indicates that workers exposed to silica dust have substantially elevated risks of developing TB compared with unexposed populations [7].

Understanding the relationship between workplace environmental conditions and TB incidence is essential for developing effective prevention strategies among workers. Scoping reviews provide comprehensive evidence synthesis and help identify existing research gaps related to occupational TB risk. By integrating findings from various occupational settings and geographical regions, this review offers insights into how workplace physical environmental factors contribute to pulmonary TB transmission. Therefore, this study aims to examine the associations of workplace environmental conditions with pulmonary TB incidence among workers and to identify key environmental determinants that may support evidence-based occupational health policies and workplace TB prevention strategies.

Methods

Study design and reporting framework

This study employed a scoping review to find the association of workplace physical environmental factors with pulmonary TB among workers. This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the methodological guidance for scoping reviews and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) Statement. The research question was formulated using the Population, Exposure, Comparator, and Outcome (PECO) framework to define the eligibility criteria and guide the literature search and study selection process.

Research question and PECO framework

The research question addressed in this review was:

“What is known about the association between workplace physical environmental factors and pulmonary TB among workers?”

Results

Study selection (result of PRISMA)

The study selection process followed the recommendations of the PRISMA-ScR Statement. The detailed screening procedure is presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1). During the identification stage, a total of 2267 records were retrieved from five electronic databases, including Scopus (n = 1504), Web of Science (n = 310), PubMed (n = 271), ScienceDirect (n = 173), and Google Scholar (n = 9). After removing duplicate records, 2045 unique articles remained for further screening.

Diagram of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) flow.

In the screening stage, the titles and abstracts of the remaining 2045 records were evaluated to determine their relevance to the occupational or workplace context. Following this process, 1975 records were excluded because they were not related to workplace or occupational settings. As a result, 70 articles met the preliminary screening criteria and were carefully assessed against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. A total of 60 articles were excluded at this stage. Five studies did not examine workplace environmental factors, and 55 studies did not report TB as an outcome variable. Finally, 10 studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in the final scoping review.

Most of the included studies employed cross-sectional designs to evaluate the relationship between workplace environmental conditions and TB occurrence among workers. Several studies also used case-control approaches to compare occupational environmental exposures between workers diagnosed with TB and healthy individuals within similar work settings. A smaller number of studies applied cohort designs to assess long-term exposure to workplace environmental factors and their contribution to TB incidence over time. The variation in study design provided complementary evidence regarding occupational exposure and TB risk.

The reviewed studies involved workers from various occupational sectors characterized by substantial environmental exposure. Some studies focuse on mining where exposure to dust and silica particles is common. Other studies examined healthcare workers, who are at increased risk of TB infection because of repeated contact with infectious patients in healthcare facilities. Additional occupational group included agriculture as a workers in informal sectors where humidity, ventilation, temperature, lighting and environmental control systems are often inadequate.

Several workplace physical environmental factors were consistently associated with pulmonary TB incidence. Poor ventilation and inadequate airflow were among the most frequently reported determinants, as they may increase the accumulation and transmission of airborne M. tuberculosis. Other reported factors included insufficient lighting, overcrowded workplaces, dust and silica exposure, as well as unfavorable temperature and humidity conditions. Environmental exposures were assessed across different settings and spatial scales. Several studies focused specifically on workplace or facility-level exposures. However, some studies have also reported exposure to residential or mixed environments. The dwellings examined in the study of farmers are more accurately described as temporary living arrangements during work at the site. The environmental factors examined included residential ventilation, housing density, and housing conditions. These exposures are specifically mentioned to clarify the context of the living environment, which is still within their work area and poses risks related to tuberculosis.

The spatial scale of the included evidence ranged from individual-level assessments to worksite-, facility-, building-, and community-level evaluations. This variation highlights the geographical and environmental heterogeneity of the available evidence and should be considered when interpreting the relationship between environmental conditions and pulmonary TB among occupational populations.

Ventilation and air circulation

Several studies consistently reported that poor ventilation and inadequate air circulation were strongly associated with increased TB risk among workers. Workplaces characterized by limited airflow, absence of mechanical ventilation systems, or poorly designed building structures were found to facilitate the accumulation of airborne infectious particles. Since pulmonary TB is primarily transmitted through airborne droplets containing M. tuberculosis, inadequate ventilation can increase the probability of transmission in enclosed workspaces.

Workplace crowding and worker density

Workplace crowding was another commonly reported environmental risk factor across the included studies. High worker density within confined spaces increases the frequency and duration of close contact between individuals, which can facilitate airborne disease transmission. Several studies observed that workers operating in small or overcrowded indoor environments were more likely to develop TB than workers in less crowded settings. Overcrowding also often coincides with other environmental issues, such as poor ventilation and inadequate air circulation, further amplifying the risk of TB transmission.

Dust and silica exposure

Occupational exposure to dust and silica particles was identified as one of the most significant environmental risk factors in industrial workplaces. Studies focusing on mining and construction sectors reported a higher incidence of TB among workers exposed to silica dust. Exposure to respirable silica particles can damage lung tissue and impair macrophage function, which weakens the body’s ability to control M. tuberculosis infection.

Temperature and humidity

These factors may influence the survival of airborne microorganisms and the ventilation dynamics within indoor environments. Some studies suggested that poorly controlled temperature and humidity levels in indoor workplaces may be related to environmental conditions that allow airborne pathogens to persist longer.

Indoor air quality

Indoor air quality emerged as an overarching factor that integrates several environmental determinants, including ventilation, dust exposure, humidity, and temperature. Workplaces with poor indoor air quality were more frequently associated with increased TB incidence among workers. Studies emphasized that inadequate environmental control measures, such as a lack of air filtration systems, insufficient ventilation, and high particulate concentrations, can create conditions conducive to airborne disease transmission.

Discussion

Principal findings

This scoping review synthesizes evidence from 10 studies examining the relationship between workplace environmental factors and the occurrence of TB among workers across different occupational sectors. The findings demonstrate that several physical environmental conditions in workplaces play an important role in influencing the risk of TB transmission. The most consistently reported determinants include inadequate ventilation, high worker density or overcrowding, exposure to dust and crystalline silica, and unfavorable indoor microclimatic conditions such as temperature and humidity. Among these factors, ventilation and air circulation emerged as the most frequently identified determinants of occupational TB risk. Workplaces characterized by limited air exchange and poor airflow were associated with higher concentrations of airborne infectious particles, thereby increasing the probability of disease transmission. In addition, occupational exposure to respirable dust, particularly crystalline silica, was repeatedly identified as a major environmental determinant of TB risk, especially among mining and industrial workers.

These findings reinforce the concept that TB transmission is not solely determined by individual-level risk factors, such as immune status or comorbidities, but is also strongly influenced by environmental and occupational conditions in which workers spend prolonged periods of time. Therefore, improving workplace environmental controls may represent an important intervention strategy for reducing occupational TB transmission.

Comparison with global evidence

The findings of this review are consistent with growing global evidence indicating that environmental conditions in congregate settings strongly influence TB transmission dynamics. Several studies conducted in high-burden settings have demonstrated that ventilation rate and occupancy density are critical determinants of airborne TB transmission. For instance, a recent study examining congregate environments reported that lower ventilation rates combined with higher occupancy increased the estimated risk of infection transmission in indoor environments [18]. Similarly, research conducted in occupational settings such as hospitals, mines, and industrial workplaces has consistently reported increased TB risk among workers exposed to poorly ventilated indoor environments [19]. Healthcare workers face elevated occupational risk due to frequent exposure to infectious patients in clinical settings, particularly when infection prevention and control measures are inadequate.

In addition to ventilation-related risks, silica dust exposure has long been recognized as one of the strongest occupational risk factors for TB. A systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that workers with silicosis have a higher risk of developing pulmonary TB than unexposed populations, with pooled estimates indicating a several-fold increase in risk [7]. More recent studies continue to confirm this relationship. Evidence from occupational cohorts shows that workers exposed to silica dust, particularly in mining and construction sectors, have a substantially increased probability of developing TB infection or reactivation of latent TB [20].

These findings highlight that the interaction between occupational exposure and infectious disease transmission remains an important public health issue, especially in low- and middle-income countries where industrial safety regulations and environmental control measures may be insufficient.

Biological mechanisms of TB transmission in the workplace

The spread of TB, an airborne infectious disease, in workplace settings is mainly caused by droplets carrying M. tuberculosis. When individuals with active pulmonary TB cough, sneeze, or speak, they release microscopic droplet nuclei containing the bacteria into the surrounding air. These droplet nuclei can remain suspended in the air for extended periods, particularly in poorly ventilated indoor spaces, and may be inhaled by individuals nearby [21]. Several workplace environmental conditions can facilitate this transmission process. Poor ventilation allows infectious aerosols to accumulate within indoor spaces, increasing the concentration of airborne bacteria and prolonging exposure among workers. Without adequate airflow, the dilution and removal of contaminated air become limited, creating conditions conducive to airborne disease spread.

Overcrowding or high worker density further amplifies this risk by increasing the frequency and duration of close interpersonal interactions. When multiple individuals share confined indoor environments for extended periods, the likelihood of inhaling infectious droplet nuclei increases substantially.

Occupational exposure to silica dust introduces an additional biological mechanism that increases susceptibility to TB infection [22]. Inhalation of crystalline silica particles damages pulmonary macrophages and impairs immune responses within the lungs. As a result, the body becomes less capable of controlling M. tuberculosis infection. This mechanism explains the well-known phenomenon of silicotuberculosis, in which individuals exposed to silica dust exhibit markedly higher TB incidence.

Furthermore, environmental factors such as temperature and humidity may influence the survival and persistence of airborne pathogens. Although the relationship between microclimate conditions and TB transmission remains complex, several studies suggest that poorly regulated indoor environments may support prolonged survival of airborne microorganisms, thereby increasing infection risk.

Implications for occupational health policy

The findings of this scoping review have important implications for occupational health and workplace infection prevention policies. Given that many environmental risk factors identified in this study are modifiable, targeted workplace interventions could reduce the risk of TB transmission.

First, improving workplace ventilation systems should be prioritized, particularly in high-risk environments such as healthcare facilities, mines, construction sites, factories, and other enclosed workplaces. Increasing natural ventilation through windows and structural design, or installing mechanical ventilation systems, can help dilute and remove infectious aerosols from indoor spaces.

Second, administrative and environmental control measures should be implemented to reduce workplace crowding. This may include reorganizing workspaces, limiting the number of workers within confined areas, and adjusting shift schedules to minimize worker density.

Third, industries involving silica exposure, such as mining and construction, should adopt strict dust control strategies. These may include wet drilling techniques, improved ventilation in underground environments, and mandatory use of respiratory protective equipment for workers exposed to respirable dust particles.

In addition, occupational TB surveillance and screening programs should be strengthened, particularly in high-risk industries. Routine health monitoring and early detection strategies can help identify TB cases among workers and prevent further transmission within workplaces.

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review has several strengths. First, the review applied a PRISMA-ScR methodology, ensuring a transparent and systematic process for identifying, screening, and selecting relevant studies. The inclusion of multiple international databases also helped to capture a wide range of relevant literature across different geographical regions.

Second, the review focused specifically on workplace environmental determinants, an area that remains relatively underexplored in TB research compared with individual-level risk factors. By synthesizing findings from different occupational sectors, this study provides a comprehensive overview of environmental conditions associated with occupational TB risk.

However, some limitations should also be acknowledged. Many of the included studies used cross-sectional designs, which limit the ability to establish causal relationships between workplace environmental exposures and TB outcomes. Longitudinal cohort studies would provide stronger evidence regarding temporal associations between exposure and disease development. Additionally, some studies relied on indirect proxies for environmental exposures, such as self-reported ventilation conditions or workplace characteristics, which may introduce measurement bias.

Future research directions

Future research should aim to strengthen the evidence base regarding occupational environmental determinants of TB. First, prospective cohort studies are needed to evaluate long-term exposure to workplace environmental factors and their impact on TB incidence among workers. Second, more research is needed to quantify ventilation standards and air exchange thresholds that effectively reduce TB transmission in occupational settings. Advances in environmental monitoring technologies, such as real-time air quality sensors, could facilitate more accurate measurement of workplace conditions.

Conclusion

This scoping review demonstrates that workplace physical environmental conditions play an important role in the transmission and occurrence of TB among workers. The reviewed studies consistently identified poor ventilation, inadequate air circulation, overcrowded workplaces, exposure to respirable dust and crystalline silica, and unfavorable indoor environmental conditions as major determinants of occupational TB risk. These factors may facilitate airborne transmission of M. tuberculosis and increase worker susceptibility, particularly in high-risk occupational sectors such as healthcare, mining, construction, and industry. The findings also suggest that silica exposure combined with inadequate ventilation may further amplify TB risk. Therefore, improving workplace environmental controls through better ventilation, dust suppression, workplace design, occupational health surveillance, and infection prevention programs is essential to reduce TB transmission in occupational settings.
