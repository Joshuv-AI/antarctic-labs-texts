Electrode Potential in Ionic Liquid Enlightened by Liquid Madelung Theory
Authors: Yusuke Irie, Atsushi Kitada, Longjie Liu, Norio Takenaka, Atsuo Yamada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrode potentials in electrolytes vary systematically with salt concentration, yet establishing a quantitative connection between electrolyte structure and electrode potential remains a longstanding challenge. Here, we demonstrate that the liquid Madelung energyan atomistically resolved Coulombic descriptor obtained from simulations of all constituent molecules and ionsquantitatively accounts for electrode-potential shifts observed for the solvent-based and ionic liquid electrolytes investigated in the present study, the latter representing the concentrated limit. Decomposition of the Li+ electrostatic energy into contributions from surrounding species reveals two key electrostatic factors governing the potential shift: reduced electrostatic stabilization arising from Li+–anion coordination in the first coordination shell and enhanced Li+–Li+ repulsion emerging in the second coordination shell at high concentrations. This atomistic framework captures structural fluctuations inaccessible to classical Debye–Hückel and pseudo-lattice theories, and can be applied to ionic liquids exhibiting apparent nanoscale heterogeneity. These results demonstrate that the liquid Madelung framework can be extended from conventional solvent-based to ionic liquid electrolytes and provide an atomistic electrostatic perspective for the rational design of next-generation electrolytes composed of complex polyatomic species.

Introduction

Electrochemistry has historically progressed in parallel with advances in electrolyte solution theory. A defining outcome of this interplay is the Nernst equation, which relates electrode potentials to the chemical potential of ions in solution by combining thermodynamic principles with Arrhenius’s microscopic picture of electrolytic dissociation. However, modern electrochemical systems increasingly employ diverse electrolytes whose local structures and ionic behaviors extend far beyond the assumptions of conventional theories. , Consequently, establishing an energetic description capable of consistently treating such diverse liquid environments remains an outstanding challenge for electrolyte science and engineering.

The chemical potential of ions in an electrolyte varies according to interactions with surrounding ions and solvent molecules, and the activity coefficient describing this variation has long been a central topic in electrolyte science. , In 1923, Debye and Hückel formulated a theoretical description of the activity coefficient based on a statistically coarse-grained ionic atmosphere (Figure a). ,, This continuum model successfully captures electrostatic shielding in the dilute limit but neglects the discrete local structures that emerge as interionic distances become shorter. ,− Extensions incorporating an effective solvation radius partly improve its applicability (Figure b), , yet quantitative accuracy is typically lost above ∼10–1 mol L–1.

At high concentrations, ions instead exhibit partial structural ordering. For instance, X-ray total-scattering experiments on concentrated metal halide aqueous solutions reveal peaks consistent with a face-centered-cubic arrangement of ions. − Consistent with such observations, the pseudo-lattice theory proposed a discrete structural picture in which solvated ions fluctuate around effective lattice points (Figure d). ,, In this model, the solution is represented as a lattice-like three-dimensional network of solvated ions, exhibiting larger interionic distances than in crystalline salts. ,− By evaluating the electrostatic energy using the Madelung constant of the assumed lattice together with the dielectric permittivity of the pure solvent, the model provides qualitative insight into the thermodynamic properties of concentrated solutions. Despite their historical significance, both the Debye–Hückel and pseudo-lattice theories are formulated at a coarse-grained level and are not designed to explicitly resolve atomistic, time-dependent electrostatic fluctuations of real liquids. , Practical descriptions, therefore, often require phenomenological corrections whose physical interpretation is limited. −

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, enabled by advances in computational performance, algorithms, and force-field accuracy, allow the electrostatic environment in liquids to be described at the atomic level. − Building on this capability, we introduced the liquid Madelung energy (E LM), extending the Madelung energy concept from solid-state science (ref Figure e) to liquid electrolytes (Figure c) by directly summing the total Coulombic interactions experienced by a given ion (e.g., Li+) from all surrounding species up to infinite distance. Here, N Li+, q Li+, qi , ε 0, and ri represent the total number and the atomic charge of Li+, the atomic charge of atom i, the vacuum permittivity, and the distance between the central Li+ and atom i, respectively, and the brackets indicate a time average. The summation runs over all surrounding atoms (Natoms ), including solvent molecules, anions, cations, and other Li+ ions up to infinite distances under periodic boundary conditions. Unlike conventional electrolyte theories, which rely on the relative permittivity of the pure solvent as a fundamental input, the present formulation treats the solvent explicitly as a collection of atomic charges. This allows the theory to be derived without introducing the solvent permittivity a priori, while still fully accounting for dielectric screening through charge scaling. The resulting charge-density distributions and electrostatic-energy profiles exhibit damped oscillatory behavior characteristic of liquids (Figure c), capturing the electrostatic ordering that emerges within thermal fluctuations. ,− In this manner, the approach provides a conceptual bridge between liquids and solids regarding energetics.

The electrode potentiala central concept in electrochemistryhas long been known to depend on the electrolyte concentration, yet the magnitude of this shift has hitherto been treated phenomenologically. ,− By introducing the liquid Madelung energy (E LM) as an electrostatic descriptor, we previously achieved a quantitative explanation of concentration-dependent potential shifts across the practical concentration range without invoking empirical activity-coefficient models. Furthermore, by incorporating Lennard–Jones interactions and entropy terms obtained from MD simulations, we achieved a thermodynamic description based on the total Gibbs energy change. However, these studies were limited to solvent-based electrolytes, leaving the broader generality unverified. Here, we examine ionic liquidssolvent-free, fully ionic systems representing the ultimate concentration limit and lying beyond the scope of classical electrolyte theories. Using this ideal platform to bridge the conceptual gap between liquids and solids, we extend the liquid Madelung framework from solvent-based to ionic liquid electrolytes.

Results and Discussion

In this study, we examine electrode potentials in ionic liquid electrolytes composed of a Li-salt, lithium bis­(fluorosulfonyl)­imide (LiN­(SO2F)2; LiFSI), and room-temperature ionic liquids sharing the same FSI–anion1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium FSI (EMim-FSI) or 1-methyl-1-propylpyrrolidinium FSI (Pyr1,3-FSI)over a Li-salt concentration range of m Li+ = 0.3–4.0 mol kg–1. This range is equivalent to the Li+ mole fractions of a solvent-based system, LiFSI/ethylene carbonate (EC), explored in the present study. Li x FePO4 (LFP, x ∼ 0.5) was employed as a chemically robust reference electrode, exhibiting small potential fluctuations and long-term stability (Figure S1). The electrode potential of LFP in each electrolyte was determined from voltammograms of the ferrocene/ferrocenium (Fc/Fc+) redox couple, an IUPAC-recommended internal standard for nonaqueous systems (Figures S2–S3 and Tables S1–S7). −

The electrode potential of Li-based active materials is governed by the chemical potential of Li+ in the immersed electrolyte and shifts upward as Li+ becomes electrostatically destabilized. ,, As shown in Figure , increasing the LiFSI concentration induces a pronounced upshift in electrode potential in both solvent-based (EC) and ionic liquid (EMim-FSI) electrolytes, reflecting concentration-dependent changes in the Li+ coordination. In the EC system, an upshift of ca. +0.35 V arises from structural reorganization of the entire solution, primarily within the first coordination sphere. At low concentration, Li+ resides predominantly in solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs), coordinated by approximately four electron-localized solvent molecules (Figure top, blue ellipses). With increasing salt concentration, this environment evolves into aggregates (AGGs) in which up to three electron-delocalized FSI– anions coordinate Li+ (Figure top, green ellipses). The associated Coulombic energy penalty is the primary origin of the observed potential upshift. , By contrast, ionic liquid electrolytes, which lack molecular solvents and consist entirely of ions, exhibit no analogous transformation of the first coordination sphere. Instead, Li+-FSI– aggregates with approximately four coordinating FSI– anions persist across the entire concentration range (Figure bottom). − Despite the absence of significant local structural changes in the first coordination shell, a measurable potential upshift of ca. +0.19 V is observed. Although smaller than in the solvent-based system, this shift corresponds to an extraordinary 140-fold increase in the Li+ activity coefficient (see Note S1), well beyond the validity range of conventional Debye–Hückel theory. Rather, the upshift originates from more subtle structural rearrangement in the higher-order coordination sphere, involving ionic liquid cations (Figure bottom, orange ellipses) and Li+ (Figure bottom, pink circles). Although these changes are comparatively modest, they introduce additional Coulombic repulsion, resulting in a non-negligible net destabilization.

Concentration-dependent upshift of the LFP electrode potential (E LFP) reflecting distinct Li+ environments in solvent-based and ionic liquid electrolytes. In the solvent-based (EC) electrolyte, increasing salt concentration leads to the replacement of solvent molecules by FSI– anions, inducing a structural transformation from solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs) to aggregates (AGGs). In contrast, in the ionic liquid (EMim-FSI) electrolyte, Li+-FSI– AGGs persist across the entire concentration range, while EMim+ cations are progressively replaced by Li+ cations in the second coordination shells as the salt concentration increases. Despite these differing structural origins, the potential upshift in both systems arises from electrostatic destabilization of the Li+ coordination environment.

To quantify the Li+ electrostatic stability across both systems, we compute the liquid Madelung energy (E LM, in eV), which incorporates the total Coulombic interactions experienced by Li+. MD simulations were performed following established protocols (Methods, Tables S8 and S9), with fixed atomic charges assigned via the restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) scheme (Figure a). Charge scaling was further applied to account for charge transfer and polarization effects. , The charge-scaling factor was chosen primarily to reproduce the experimentally observed Li+–FSI– aggregation trends derived from Raman spectroscopy , (Figures S4–S6), while the calculated coordination numbers and electrolyte densities served as additional validation metrics (Figure S7 and Tables S10–S11). Varying the charge-scaling factor from 0.8 to 1.0 produced only minor changes in the radial distribution functions and number distribution functions (Figure S7), indicating that the overall solvation structure, including the concentration-dependent increase in the Li+ population within the second coordination shell, is largely insensitive to the scaling factor. A value of 0.9 provided the best agreement with the Raman-derived aggregation behavior (Figure S5) and was therefore adopted throughout this study. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis over the same scaling range showed that the absolute values of the liquid Madelung potential shift (ΔV LM = ΔE LM/F, where F is the Faraday constant) depend on the adopted effective-charge model, as expected for a Coulombic energy (Figure S8). However, the concentration-dependent increase in ΔV LM is retained over the examined range and remains consistent with the experimentally observed electrode-potential shifts. Together with the structural insensitivity shown by the RDFs and NDFs (Figure S7), these results indicate that the agreement between the calculated ΔV LM and the experimental potential shift does not arise from tuning the charge-scaling factor to the electrochemical data. Rather, the liquid Madelung framework provides a quantitative electrostatic descriptor when used with an effective-charge model constrained by independent structural observables.

As shown in Figure b, the calculated concentration dependence of the liquid Madelung potential shift (ΔV LM, open circles) captures the concentration-dependent electrode-potential shifts (ΔE LFP, closed circles) within the accuracy of the effective charge model. As discussed in Note S2, the experimental LFP potential shift is related to the concentration-dependent change in the Li+ chemical potential, whose dominant contribution is represented by the liquid Madelung energy. Although E LM is evaluated using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method under periodic boundary conditions to account for long-range interactions, , essentially identical results are obtained by direct integration of Coulombic interactions up to ca. 30 Å (Figure S9), indicating that PME-related finite-size and neutralizing-background effects are negligible for the present analysis. These results support the applicability of the present framework, which explicitly incorporates all atomic-level electrostatic interactions, to both solvent-based and ionic liquid electrolytes. Consistent with prior analyses in solvent systems, the dominant contribution to electrode potential, i.e., the Gibbs energy change of the half-cell reaction, is given by the electrostatic term ΔE LM. As noted in our previous thermodynamic analysis, the electrode-potential shift is governed by the total Gibbs energy change, ΔG = ΔE LM + ΔE LJ – TΔS, in which the electrostatic contribution dominates over both the Lennard–Jones term (ΔE LJ) and the entropic contribution (−TΔS). Within the present framework, induction (polarization) effects are incorporated implicitly through the charge-scaling treatment and therefore cannot be rigorously separated from the electrostatic term. Nevertheless, the optimized charge-scaling factor of 0.9 suggests that the effective polarization correction is relatively small. The magnitude of the Lennard–Jones energy is approximately one order of magnitude smaller than that of the Coulombic interaction. Moreover, unlike the electrostatic contribution, the Lennard–Jones term exhibits only a weak concentration dependence, varying by approximately 0.007 eV over the investigated concentration range (Table S12). Although Lennard–Jones interactions are essential for reproducing the local structural organization of the electrolyte (Figure S10), the nearest-neighbor packing geometry changes only marginally with concentration. Consequently, their contribution to the concentration-dependent Gibbs energy variation remains comparatively small. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies of ionic liquids, which have shown that Coulombic interactions constitute the principal energetic component governing their structure and thermodynamics, whereas induction and dispersion interactions make comparatively minor contributions. , These findings therefore support the conclusion that the observed electrode potential shifts are governed predominantly by electrostatic interactions. Consequently, by directly linking electrolyte structure to electrostatics, the liquid Madelung approach provides an atomistic perspective for interpreting electrode potential shifts across wide ranges of concentration, coordination structure, and thermal fluctuations.

Classical “paper-and-pencil” electrolyte theories, including Debye–Hückel and pseudo-lattice models, , rely on coarse-grained and oversimplified representations of electrolyte permittivity, ion distributions, and interionic distances. In contrast, the liquid Madelung theory evaluates Coulombic interactions explicitly at the atomic level using all-atom MD, without reducing the liquid structure to a continuum model or fitting empirical activity-coefficient corrections. Although both the present framework and Debye–Hückel theory are ultimately based on interionic electrostatic interactions, they differ substantially in their physical descriptions. Debye–Hückel theory represents electrostatic screening through a diffuse ionic atmosphere embedded in a dielectric continuum and is therefore generally applicable only in the dilute limit. The pseudo-lattice theory introduces a more discrete structural description by assuming that solvated ions fluctuate around effective lattice sites and by evaluating electrostatic stabilization using an idealized lattice model. Nevertheless, it still relies on predefined structural motifs and coarse-grained parameters rather than explicit molecular configurations. By contrast, the liquid Madelung framework evaluates the Coulombic interactions between a target Li+ ion and all surrounding atoms directly from atomistic liquid structures, thereby explicitly accounting for discrete coordination environments, structural fluctuations, and their associated electrostatic organization. This atomistic representation provides a molecular-level framework for analyzing concentration-dependent electrostatic stabilization in molecularly complex electrolytes.

The liquid Madelung framework can also be applied to ionic liquid systems exhibiting apparent nanoscale heterogeneity. Ionic liquids often show structural heterogeneity arising from the segregation of nonpolar alkyl chains and polar ionic units, and the atomistic configurations of LiFSI/Pyr1,3-FSI and LiFSI/Pyr1,6-TFSI electrolytes are qualitatively consistent with such behavior (Figure S11). Even in systems with more complex heterocoordination, or in which ether side chains of organic cations directly participate in Li+ coordination, the liquid Madelung approachdecomposing ligand structures into atomic chargesis applicable (Figure S12 and Table S7), including LiTFSI/Pyr1,6-TFSI and LiTFSI/Pyr1,1O2O1-TFSI systems for which the experimental electrode-potential shifts and density data were taken from the literature. These features highlight the applicability of the framework to structurally complex electrolytes that remain challenging for conventional theories. For example, the potential upshift induced by replacing tetrahydrofuran with a nonpolar diluent, toluene, was quantitatively reproduced within the liquid Madelung framework.

To elucidate the main contribution of the potential upshift, we decomposed the Li+ electrostatic energy into species-resolved contributions. As shown in Figure a,b, the Li+ mole fraction varies similarly with concentration in both electrolyte classes, whereas the overall composition evolves differently. In the solvent-based system, increasing salt concentration primarily displaces solvent; in ionic liquid, it reduces the fraction of ionic liquid cations. In addition, the anion fraction varies with the concentration in solvent-based electrolytes but is fixed at 0.5 in ionic liquids.

Unlike conventional theories (Figure ), the liquid Madelung framework explicitly resolves the coordination structures of polyatomic species at the atomic level. Consequently, the electrostatic energy exhibits pronounced, spatially resolved fluctuations that directly reflect the surrounding charge distribution and converges only at long distances (see Figure S9).

Decomposition of the electrostatic energy accumulated as a function of distance from a central Li+ shows that its fluctuations directly reflect the atomistic spatial distribution of surrounding species (Figures c, d, and Figure S13, top panels), as captured by the corresponding radial distribution functions (RDFs; g(r)) and number distribution functions (NDFs; n(r)) (middle and bottom panels). Although convergence of the electrostatic energy to a quantitatively reliable value requires integration to ≥30 Å (Figure S9), the distance-dependent variations arise within the first and second coordination spheres (ca. 10 Å), which we analyze here. This enables an atomistically resolved examination of charge distributions and electrostatic energies that complements the coarse-grained descriptions employed in conventional theories.

In low-concentration EC-based electrolytes, Li+ is strongly stabilized by electron-rich OEC atoms in the first solvation shell (Figure c, blue region), − which dominate the total electrostatic energy (dashed line). With increasing salt concentration, FSI– progressively replaces EC, and the primary stabilizing contribution shifts to comparatively weaker interactions with OFSI‑ atoms (Figure c, green region). Thus, in the EC-based system, the concentration-dependent destabilization is primarily governed by a reorganization of the first coordination sphere. By contrast, in ionic liquid electrolytes, the first coordination sphere remains anion-dominated regardless of salt concentration, resulting in minimal variation in first-shell stabilization (Figure d, green region). Consequently, the observed destabilization in this system arises from regions beyond the first coordination sphere. In the second coordination sphere, ionic liquid cations dominate at low Li+ concentrations, whereas Li+ ions become increasingly prevalent at high concentrations. Owing to charge delocalization and steric effects of the organic cations (Figure a), , their effective charge density is low, and their contribution to destabilization is weak (Figure d, orange region). In contrast, Li+ carries a highly localized positive charge; the increasing Li+ number density in the second coordination sphere therefore enhances Li+–Li+ Coulombic repulsion, thereby constituting a major destabilizing contribution (Figure d, pink region). Similar trends are observed for the LiFSI/Pyr1,3-FSI electrolyte (Figure S13), indicating that such second-shell, cation-size-dependent packing effects contribute significantly to the electrostatic origin of the electrode potential shift in the ionic-liquid electrolytes examined here. Although the difference in the total electrostatic energy (black dashed lines) remains relatively small within the ∼10 Å range shown in Figure d, quantitative integration of the species-resolved contributions (Table S13) confirms that the concentration-dependent variation in first-shell Li+–FSI– stabilization is modest, whereas a substantially larger change arises from the second-shell Li+ contribution. The resulting structural and electrostatic reorganization extends beyond the second coordination shell into the surrounding liquid structure. Consequently, these concentration-dependent electrostatic differences accumulate over longer distances and converge to the observed liquid Madelung energy difference (∼0.2 eV; Figure S9).

This shell-resolved mechanistic picture, derived from Figure is consistent with previous data-driven analyses of solvent-based electrolytes. Specifically, machine-learning studies identified the Li+–FSI– distancecorresponding to the first coordination shellas the dominant descriptor of Li electrode potential, followed by the Li+–Li+ distance, which reflects second-shell packing effects. Consistent with this hierarchy, Li+–Li+ repulsion contributes measurably to total electrostatic energy even in solvent systems (Figure c, pink region). In ionic liquids, however, the Li+–FSI– distance remains largely invariant with concentration, rendering Li+–Li+ interactions the principal variable controlling the potential shift. Notably, at high concentrations in both systems, a common structural motif emerges: an anion-dominated first coordination sphere and a Li+ -rich second coordination sphere (Figure c, d), producing similar electrostatic environments and comparable electrode potentials (Figure ). Overall, the electrode potentials in both systems are captured within a unified liquid Madelung framework as the combined effect of (i) reduced electrostatic attraction due to Li+–anion coordination and (ii) increased Li+–Li+ electrostatic repulsion.

Although the present study focuses on solvent-based and ionic-liquid electrolytes, the framework is not intrinsically restricted to Li-imide systems because E LM is defined from Coulombic interactions between a target ion and surrounding atomic charges. Consistent with this view, previous studies have shown a quantitative correspondence between experimental electrode-potential shifts and calculated liquid Madelung potential shifts in solvent-based electrolytes containing Li+, Na+, K+, and multivalent Zn2+. Further systematic validation across broader electrolyte chemistries, ion species, and redox couples is important for assessing the full generality of this approach.

Conclusions

We demonstrate that the liquid Madelung energy, computed from time- and atomistically resolved Coulombic interactions, quantitatively accounts for the concentration-dependent electrode potential shifts observed for the solvent-based and ionic liquid electrolytes investigated in the present study, the latter representing the concentrated limit. By explicitly capturing electrostatic ordering within thermally fluctuating structures, this framework bridges the conceptual gap between the concentration regimes addressed by Debye–Hückel and pseudo-lattice theories. The electrode potential shift is consistently rationalized as a balance between electrostatic attraction arising from Li+–anion coordination and repulsion from Li+–Li+ interactions. These results extend the applicability of the liquid Madelung framework from conventional solvent-based electrolytes to ionic liquids, including systems exhibiting apparent nanoscale heterogeneity, and represent an important advance in applying this solid-state-derived concept to molecularly complex electrolyte systems as an atomistic electrostatic framework. Further validation across a wider range of electrolytes, ionic species, and redox couples will be valuable for assessing the broader applicability of this framework and for establishing electrochemical structure–property relationships in complex electrolyte systems. An important direction for future work is to connect the thermodynamic insights provided by the liquid Madelung framework with electrode kinetics. While electrode potential shifts are fundamentally thermodynamic quantities, electrode kinetics are governed by ion transport, desolvation, and charge-transfer processes. Because the structural changes responsible for variations in the liquid Madelung energy are also expected to influence these processes, this extension may provide a basis for linking electrolyte structure, electrode potential, and interfacial reaction kinetics.
