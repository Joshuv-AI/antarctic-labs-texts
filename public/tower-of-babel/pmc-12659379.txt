Demand–capacity estimation using queueing theory: application to hospital resource planning in the 2023 Türkiye earthquake
Authors: Bircan Kara, Ali Utku Şahin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aims to develop and validate a mathematical model for estimating the number of emergency physicians required in post-earthquake scenarios, using actual data from the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes in Türkiye.MethodologyThe methodology follows a structured five-step framework to assess earthquake impact and emergency healthcare demand. First, population impact is analyzed using USGS PAGER data to estimate exposure levels. Second, household and building stock characteristics are profiled from TurkStat, focusing on construction year and building types, which influence structural vulnerability. Third, collapse probabilities are determined through empirical fragility functions that relate earthquake intensity to building failure rates. Fourth, casualties are estimated by combining structural damage with fatality and injury ratios specific to building types. Finally, physician demand is calculated using the M/M/s queuing theory model, incorporating key variables, such as emergency healthcare capacity, patient arrival rates, and examination duration, to forecast medical staffing needs in the aftermath of a disaster.FindingsThe model estimated 11,645 potential emergency department visits in Hatay province within the first 144 h post-earthquake. Based on this, 27 emergency physicians per shift—or 81 total physicians across three shifts—are required to operate at full capacity. These figures closely align with actual post-disaster hospital admission data, validating the model.ConclusionsThis study presents a scalable, actual-data-validated model for physician workforce planning in disaster scenarios. The queuing-based approach supports strategic resource allocation and enhances organizational resilience. Unlike existing models, this study directly integrates field-specific damage and population data to forecast real-time health system needs.Keywords: Disaster preparedness, Emergency medicine, Queuing theory, Human resource planning, Earthquake resilience, Mathematical modeling, Health systems

Introduction

Disasters are extraordinary events that severely disrupt daily activities and negatively impact human health and life [29]. According to the World Health Organization, disasters are events that can lead to large-scale loss of life and property, and their effects cannot be controlled even if they are anticipated [45]. The immediate consequences of disasters include physical injuries, trauma, and exacerbation of chronic diseases, while long-term impacts may include psychological distress and socio-economic challenges [1, 5, 9, 15]. Among various types of disasters, natural events such as earthquakes often present a higher risk for causing significant health disruptions [34, 49].

The importance of disaster-related health service planning has been demonstrated in numerous past incidents. For instance, during the 2010 Haiti earthquake, the lack of prepared healthcare services exacerbated the disaster’s effects, leading to prolonged suffering and poor health outcomes [39]. Similarly, the COVID-19 pandemic underscored the critical need for health systems to adapt rapidly to large-scale emergencies, with shortages in healthcare workers and resources being a key limitation in effective response [26, 50]. In West Africa, the Ebola outbreak further illustrated how inadequate disaster preparedness in healthcare systems can result in the rapid spread of diseases, overwhelming hospitals and public health networks [40]. These examples emphasize the critical need for robust disaster preparedness models, particularly in workforce planning, as existing frameworks often fall short in addressing the complexities of human resource allocation during large-scale emergencies. Strategic workforce planning is essential to ensuring the resilience of healthcare services and optimizing resource use during such crises [14, 27, 30].

The aftermath of earthquakes not only affects individuals but also damages critical service infrastructure, such as health systems, transportation, and communication networks, which severely hampers the continuity of healthcare services [31]. Disasters like earthquakes disrupt the normal functioning of health systems, overloading emergency departments and healthcare personnel, and highlighting the urgent need for pre-disaster health service planning and preparedness [2, 20].

Studies show that during large-scale disasters, healthcare needs rise dramatically, involving both urgent clinical issues such as traumatic injuries and long-term challenges such as psychological trauma and chronic condition exacerbations [35, 45]. Health facilities often face severe operational bottlenecks due to insufficient workforce surge capacity, which has been repeatedly demonstrated in real-time trauma registries [3, 8]. Furthermore, the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes exposed gaps in triage and patient prioritization, emphasizing the critical need for coordinated surge planning in hospital disaster preparedness [48].

Addressing these challenges requires proactive workforce planning, not just in terms of numbers, but also in skill distribution. Flexible surge capacity, which incorporates public health, education, and healthcare resource management, has been proposed as a sustainable approach to enhancing system resilience [25]. In the context of disaster resilience, healthcare workforce planning is crucial for managing both acute and chronic care needs, alongside psychological and social support [24, 18, 19].

Despite the significant importance of human resource planning, there remains a gap in the literature, especially in mathematical or operational frameworks for workforce management in disaster settings [17].

This study aims to fill an important gap in the literature by providing a strategic perspective to ensure the continuity of healthcare services during crises. In this context, we utilize a numerical demand–capacity expression derived from existing queueing theory models to estimate the required human resource capacity based on expected patient demand. The primary objective of this study is to offer a practical and scientifically sound method that can serve as a decision support tool for healthcare administrators in workforce planning.

Methodology and mathematical modeling

This study was designed to determine the number of physicians required to work in the emergency department of Hatay Mustafa Kemal University Health Practice and Research Hospital, which is the only hospital that continued to provide health service in Hatay province after the February 6th, 2023 Kahramanmaraş-centered earthquakes. In the study, a mathematical modeling-based planning method was used to determine the number of physicians needed based on the estimated number of injured.

The study was conducted by considering various variables related to the estimated number of disaster victims, emergency service capacity, examination times and working hours of health workers. Similar studies in the literature, post-disaster health services data, statistical information published by official institutions and field reports were taken as reference in the construction of the model.

The study addresses human resource planning of emergency physicians to ensure continuity of health service delivery after a disaster. The model developed in this direction is based on a methodological approach consisting of four basic steps. These steps are detailed below, respectively.

The model aims to reveal the need for physicians at a level that can meet the sudden patient load directed to the health system in the first critical hours of the disaster. The results obtained are intended to provide a scientific basis for decision makers and health managers in human resource planning in similar disaster situations.

As it is known, the earthquakes that occurred on February 6th, 2023, consisted of two separate earthquakes with magnitudes of 7.8 and 7.5 on the Richter scale, occurring approximately 9 h apart (USGS [43]). At this stage of the study, the number of dead and injured was estimated by considering only the destruction that occurred in the first earthquake.

In the estimation of the number of dead and wounded, the So’s table of casualty estimates shown in Table 2 and PAGER probabilities were used. Initially, the number of wounded was distributed proportionally to the provincial populations of the earthquake zone, with the assumption that the entire population, excluding the deceased, had been wounded and that all wounded individuals were admitted to the hospital. However, in addition to the direct injuries caused by the earthquake, we acknowledge that individuals who were not physically injured during the initial tremors may still experience significant health-related needs. In large-scale disasters, even individuals in undamaged buildings may suffer from secondary effects such as physical injuries, psychological trauma, or exacerbation of pre-existing conditions due to aftershocks, disruptions in daily life, or the breakdown of healthcare systems [16, 31]. Furthermore, those with chronic conditions, such as hypertension, diabetes, or respiratory diseases, may face exacerbations of their health conditions in the absence of regular medical care [7].

In addition, the psychosocial impact of such events can lead to the emergence of mental health issues, including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), anxiety, and depression, which may necessitate healthcare interventions [16]. The disruption of basic living conditions, including shelter and sanitation, increases vulnerability, especially in areas with limited infrastructure or overcrowded emergency shelters [7]. These conditions contribute to an increased need for healthcare services, even for individuals who are not physically injured.

Therefore, our model includes the assumption that individuals who were not initially injured could still require medical attention, particularly in the form of chronic disease management and mental health support. This leads to the inclusion of the entire population (excluding fatalities) in the estimation, reflecting the broader health needs during such crises. In summary, the healthcare demand in the aftermath of large-scale disasters is not limited to physical injuries but extends to the exacerbation of pre-existing conditions and the emergence of new health needs due to environmental, psychological, and social stressors [15, 31].

Physician staffing estimates using M/M/s queuing theory

Queueing theory, a method within operations research, is used to analyze the service process with random variation in demand and service times. The purpose of queuing theory analysis is to calculate the level of service that needs to be provided, in other words, the required service delivery capacity [6]. With this analysis, the waiting and service times in a “queue” can be revealed [33]. The basic elements of queuing theory are divided into service beneficiaries and service providers. The service beneficiaries are represented on an hourly scale according to their arrival time to receive the service, while the service itself is measured by the capacity of the service providers, also on an hourly scale [33].

Formula 2 . System utilization factor

The equation shown in Formula 2 can also be used to calculate the number of physicians that should be assigned to emergency services after an earthquake. When one matches the variables of the queuing theory with the case; service applicants could be interpreted as the patients admitting to the hospital, and service providers (or channels) could be interpreted as the physicians. As is known, p represents the capacity of the system, and, in this case, which is an emergency healthcare service right after an earthquake, should be taken as 1 to represent that the capacity of the hospital is 100%. In this case, λ is the hourly average of the estimated number of injured people who are assumed to be admitted to the hospital, and μ is the number of patients/injured that a physician can deal with in 1 h.

According to the literature, the maximum time required to be rescued alive from a collapsed building is 144 h [36], and an average of 20 min is allocated for the emergency room examination of a patient in the USA and the UK [37, 47].

Considering these data, the hourly average of the estimated number of injured people who are assumed to be admitted to the hospital (λ) was calculated over 144 h, μ was calculated over 3 injured/hour, and the number of physicians that should be assigned to the emergency department was calculated using the M/M/s type queuing theory model according to the ρ = 1 situation in which the hospital emergency department will operate at full capacity after an earthquake.

Findings

The following section summarizes the results of a four-stage demand–capacity estimation model designed to determine emergency physician requirements after the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes. Key estimates include the total number of casualties, distribution of affected households by building type and severity, and physician staffing needs derived using M/M/s queueing theory. Where possible, model estimates are validated against real hospital admission data from Hatay province.

Exposure and building stock profile

More than 20 million individuals were exposed to seismic intensities of MMI 6 or greater. Among them, over 2 million were in areas of MMI 8 + , where structural vulnerability is highest. In total, nearly 49% of all households in the affected provinces resided in buildings constructed before 2000, with Hatay showing a balanced distribution between pre- and post-2000 construction.

The dominant building types included reinforced concrete frames (C3M) and unreinforced brick masonry (UFB), both of which exhibit distinct fragility profiles under seismic stress.

Structural collapse risk and fragility

Using the empirical fragility functions from Jaiswal et al. [21], collapse probabilities were calculated for each building type across different intensity levels (MMI). The results show that UFB buildings constructed before 2000 have a collapse probability of 15.1% at MMI 9, while C3M buildings, although at a lower probability, still exhibit significant collapse risks.

These collapse probabilities were then combined with household distribution and average occupancy data. This analysis led to an estimation of approximately 39,000 deaths and 104,000 injuries across the affected region.

Estimated casualties in Hatay

Since the main purpose of this study is to calculate the number of physicians that should be assigned to emergency department of Hatay Mustafa Kemal University Health Practice and Research Hospital using queuing theory, the number of injured people obtained was distributed proportionally for Hatay province by taking into account the ratios shown in Table 6 and the number of injured people who are likely to apply to the hospital emergency department in Hatay province was calculated as 11,645.

When these data obtained as a result of the calculations are compared with the actual data announced after the Kahramanmaraş Earthquakes in 2023, they corresponds with the number of injured announced in the entire earthquake region and the actual number of hospital admissions in Hatay province. As a matter of fact, according to EM-DAT data, the death toll in the Kahramanmaraş Earthquakes was 53,000 and the number of injured was 107,000 [13]. In addition, according to the data obtained from the hospital information management system (HIMS) of Hatay Mustafa Kemal University Health Application and Research Hospital, the number of patients admitted to the emergency department in the first 144 h after the earthquake was 8519.

Summary of key findings

The proposed modeling framework successfully estimates short-term healthcare system needs following a seismic disaster. In Hatay province, model outputs closely match real hospital admission data, providing robust support for the use of queuing theory in disaster human resource planning. The ability to determine real-time staffing needs from infrastructure and population data represents a scalable and transferable approach to disaster preparedness.

Discussion

This study aims to fill an important gap in terms of assessing the capacity of the health system in times of disaster by calculating the number of physicians required to work in emergency services according to an earthquake scenario. The obtained results reveal how critical physician planning is for the sustainability of health services under similar disaster conditions. In the discussion section, these findings are evaluated in comparison with the existing literature, and implications for optimizing health service delivery are presented.

It is known that there are models based on human resource planning in health services [22]. Similar to this method, there are models based on estimating the number of physicians that Canada will need. In that study, a model proposal was put forward by focusing on the increase in cancer cases in the future [12]. Similarly, Dall et al. [11] analyzed to reveal the need for neurologists in the United States and focused on predicting the need for neurologists according to the increase in the incidence of the disease in the future. Again, in a study conducted in Taiwan, the number of pediatric physicians needed in the future according to birth rates was tried to be estimated based on a model [46]. It is seen that these models and calculations can work at optimal times and are mostly based on the sustainability of the system in the future. However, in this study, the number of physicians needed in the emergency department in extraordinary situations was modeled based on actual data. While this is similar to other studies in that it plans for the future, it differs from other studies in that it predicts the resilience and sustainability of the health system in times of emergency.

Studies on human resource planning modeling for emergencies are quite limited. Although a strategy and modeling were developed during the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic, a plan was made based on the general patient needs and load, and a plan was made with 6-h shifts, with 4 patients per 1 nurse per shift, and in an outpatient service with 600 patients per day. As a result, it was found that an average of 45 nurses should work [28]. In this study, by calculating the model inputs with real numerical values, it is revealed that the number of physicians can be calculated in terms of health system resistance in extraordinary situations such as pandemics.

The transient modeling approach developed by Paul et al. [32] clearly demonstrated how hospital operations vary in terms of human resources during disasters. In that study, similarly, the number of physicians needed in different phases of the disaster was determined through modeling, and a more flexible resource planning was presented by considering the time-dependent change of operational loads [32]. The main advantage of transient modeling is the ability to measure the responsiveness of the system under sudden patient congestion. In this context, our proposed physician scheduling model provides a scalable, field-applicable solution designed considering the same parameters (e.g., patient arrival rate, physician capacity, waiting time). In addition, the fact that this model is worked with actual data is considered to be more applicable with more precise results than other simulation models.

The emergency service metamodel developed by Kaptan [23] defines organizational resilience based on waiting time during disasters. In that study, the capacity responsiveness of post-disaster health systems is modeled similarly by focusing on the number of physicians [23]. Similar to the “emergency plan” practices suggested in Kaptan’s model for increasing organizational resilience, the model we propose suggests a rapid increase in the number of physicians. This model also focuses on calculating the number of physicians, one of the most important human resource elements of the healthcare system, to increase the resilience of the healthcare system against disasters. In this way, it is envisaged that the need for physician manpower during a disaster can be determined, and the ability of the health system to respond quickly in terms of treatment and care will increase. In addition, this model is based on a real event and can be used in practice or in the planning phase for expected disasters.

The resilience index defined in the study of Cimellaro et al. [10] is used to measure how resilient a hospital system is against sudden disasters such as earthquakes. The approach in this study is thought to make the system more resilient and flexible by determining the number of physicians needed in the first response phase after a disaster. It reveals very important metric measurements in terms of increasing the operational capacity of the system and the sustainability of the system by providing the opportunity to plan manpower or to intervene quickly. From this perspective, the physician planning model we propose overlaps with Cimellaro’s definition of “organizational resilience” and shows that human resources should be modeled not only as an operational but also as a strategic resource.

Limitations

In this study, we encountered several limitations related to the use of fragility data and the application of the HAZUS model:

Regional differences: The fragility functions from Jaiswal, Wald, & D'Ayala (2011) were derived from data that may not fully represent the structural characteristics and building conditions in our specific region. These functions were applied cautiously, taking into account the limitations of regional data.

Data availability: While HAZUS provides a comprehensive set of data, we could not apply it in this study due to its U.S.-centric parameters. Instead, we utilized locally available MMI parameters and fragility data to ensure the validity of our estimates.

Although the importance of healthcare personnel’s experience and professional competence is widely recognized in disaster response literature, these qualitative variables were not included in the current estimation model due to the lack of standardized data and quantifiable metrics. Future studies should aim to incorporate such dimensions into more comprehensive planning frameworks. In addition, this study is based on a single case study hospital located in a high seismic risk area. While the proposed model provides a replicable framework, its direct application to other healthcare facilities may be constrained due to contextual differences in hospital infrastructure, staffing profiles, and local emergency protocols.

Conclusion and recommendations

The findings of this study show that the number of physicians needed for the sustainability of health systems in extraordinary situations, such as earthquakes, especially in emergency services, can be calculated through mathematical modeling. This model, developed based on actual earthquake data, provides a more concrete assessment of the capacity limits of healthcare services in times of disasters and emphasizes once again that human resource planning of emergency physicians is a critical element that directly affects the responsiveness of the healthcare system in disasters. In addition, the data obtained from the model is validated with hospital data, and it is seen that the model provides results close to reality.

The most important difference that distinguishes our model from other models is that the data related to the earthquake that occurred is based on the results of a real earthquake announced by the USGS Pager. In other words, it is based on the relationship between the intensity and population of an earthquake that has actually occurred, not the possible results of a hypothetical earthquake. Therefore, the estimates of destruction, number of dead and injured stated in the methodology and mathematical modeling section of the study are based on real earthquake data. As a matter of fact, the small difference between the actual number of injured people admitted to the hospital and the number of injured people estimated as a result of the model shows the validity of the model.

Unlike other studies in the literature, this model aims to address short-term but high-intensity health needs in extraordinary situations to increase organizational resilience. In line with the organizational resilience framework presented by Kaptan [23] and Cimellaro et al. [10], human resources should be considered as a strategic element. Paul et al. [32] presented a simulation by considering varying operational risks and loads with modeling approaches. This study proposes a flexible model by taking into account varying operational loads at different stages of the disaster, but it is designed with real data and proposes an effective, scalable, and less error-prone resource planning model.

As shown in Table 11, in the aftermath of the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes, during the first 144 h, only 12 physicians per shift were available in emergency department, which was significantly insufficient to meet the overwhelming demand for medical care. According to our calculations through the model, as the number of patients admitted to the emergency department in the first 144 h was 8519, then there should be 19 physicians per shift were available in emergency department. This difference created operational bottlenecks and coordination challenges, as the Ministry of Health was primarily responsible for managing the coordination of available resources. The lack of foresight in estimating the required number of physicians contributed to delays and inefficiencies in responding to the disaster.

From the experience of the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes, it became evident that the Ministry of Health could have utilized emergency response codes to call physicians to extraordinary duties, allowing for more efficient mobilization of healthcare personnel. In addition, strengthening the Ministry’s coordination networks could have helped in quickly deploying the necessary workforce across the affected regions. The ability to predict and allocate the right number of physicians and healthcare workers during such crises is critical for avoiding these challenges and ensuring an efficient disaster response.

Given the limitations of the current system, calculating the number of required physicians and efficiently mobilizing them during an emergency can significantly enhance the preparedness and response capacity of the health system. This model presents a practical tool for improving healthcare staffing efficiency in disaster scenarios, ensuring that the right number of physicians is available where needed and that coordination is streamlined across the healthcare network.

This approach would provide a comprehensive, multi-hazard strategy for disaster response, ensuring healthcare systems are better prepared for any type of catastrophic event.
