Quantifying Electric-Powered Wheelchair (EPW) Driving Performance Using Jerk Metrics: A Pilot Study
Authors: Serena S. W. Ng, Chung Hang Tang, Stephen C. H. Tse, Alfred C. H. Yip, Winnie W. T. Lam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrent assessments of electric-powered wheelchair (EPW) driving, such as the Wheelchair Skills Test (WST), rely on observational scoring and lack validation in outdoor environments. Occupational Therapists require standardized, quantitative measures to evaluate driving performance and monitor training progress.ObjectiveThis pilot study examined: (1) the influence of cognitive, physical, and sensory components on EPW driving, (2) the validity of sensor-derived jerk metrics relative to WST scores, and (3) the feasibility of these metrics as outcome measures in an EPW training program.MethodsSeven adults with mobility impairments (six novice wheelchair drivers; mean age 59.4 years) completed baseline assessments. Sensors mounted on the EPW and joystick captured jerk during seven WST tasks with Apps displayed. Three metrics—median jerk, time above threshold, and the Jerk Index—were computed and correlated with WST scores. Pre/post comparisons were conducted following four training sessions.ResultsSensor-derived metrics demonstrated strong inverse correlations with WST scores (ρ = −0.750 to −0.857, p < 0.05), indicating that lower jerk values reflected higher skill. The Jerk Index correlated significantly with WST scores in outdoor tasks (ρ = −0.786, p < 0.05). After training (N = 4), WST scores improved by 21.3% and the Jerk Index decreased by 20.6%, though changes did not reach statistical significance.ConclusionsJerk-based metrics, particularly the Jerk Index, show promise as objective measures of EPW driving performance. They demonstrated concurrent validity with WST scores and applicability in outdoor contexts. Larger studies are warranted to confirm reliability, establish clinical thresholds, and support integration into rehabilitation practice.

Introduction

A recent survey revealed that 54.7% of community wheelchair users, for both manual and powered wheelchairs, experienced at least one accident over a three-year period, with electric powered wheelchairs (EPWs) showing a higher incidence of collisions and control issues (Chen et al., 2011). These accidents pose significant safety risks and often result in property damage, repair costs, and liability concerns. A standardized assessment for EPW driving could help therapists evaluate a user’s readiness for safe community use and assess the effectiveness of training programs.

A scoping review found that most EPW assessments rely primarily on observational methods, with few tools measuring both speed (e.g., completion time) and accuracy (e.g., staying within a defined path) (Bigras et al., 2020). The Wheelchair Skills Test (WST) was identified as the most comprehensive tool, offering broad skill coverage and strong interrater reliability (ICC(2,10) = 0.94) (Smith, Low & Miller. 2018). However, several limitations were noted. Speed is not included as a rating criterion, nor is task efficiency considered. Certain skills, such as sideways manoeuvring, show lower interrater agreement due to subjective judgments about safety and performance (Smith, Low & Miller. 2018). Users may fail tasks due to minor technical errors, such as crossing a line, even when such mistakes do not compromise real-world safety (Rushton et al., 2016). The relationship between WST scores and actual community driving performance remains underexplored (Jenkins et al., 2015). A sensor-based assessment that captures both speed and accuracy, and aligns with WST and real-world performance, would enable therapists to use standardized, objective outcome measures for evaluating EPW driving and training efficacy.

A task analysis involving therapists and EPW users identified 80 relevant body functions potentially required for EPW driving (Smith et al., 2022). These include cognitive functions (e.g., attention, memory), sensory functions (e.g., vision, proprioception), and neuromuscular functions (e.g., range of motion, strength). Another study introduced the Powered Mobility Screening Tool (PMST) to guide targeted rehabilitation (Kamaraj, 2020). However, neither study provided empirical data specifying the functional thresholds necessary for safe EPW driving. Letts et al. (Letts et al., 2007) examined the construct validity of community EPW driving performance in relation to cognitive and visual-perceptual abilities using the Standardized Mini-Mental State Exam (SMMSE) (Molley et al., 1991), Behavioural Assessment of the Dysexecutive Syndrome (BADS) (Norris & Tate, 2000), and Motor-Free Visual Perceptual Test (MVPT) (Bouska & Kwatny, 1982). Their findings showed no direct correlation between cognitive or visual-perceptual abilities and EPW driving performance, as measured by the Power-Mobility Community Driving Assessment (PMCDA) (Letts et al., 1998). The authors suggested that EPW driving, being a procedural skill, may be retained even when cognitive functions decline.

Due to the limited quantitative evidence on how physical, cognitive, and sensory factors affect EPW driving, this study seeks to gather and analyze relevant measures to guide the development of targeted training strategies that enhance EPW driving performance.

Quantifying EPW driving skills using Jerk is an uncommon practice in physical rehabilitation. (Letts, Dawson & Kaiserman-Goldenstein.1998). “Jerk,” defined as the rate of change of acceleration, captures abrupt movement changes. High jerk values often indicate rapid, reactive driving, while skilled drivers tend to anticipate obstacles and maintain smoother, lower-jerk profiles. In vehicle driving research, prolonged exposure to high jerk levels has been associated with increased accident risk (Bagdadi & Várhelyi, 2011). While jerk has been used previously as a proxy for smoothness or safety in mobility contexts, the development and operationalization of the metric, and its linkage to WST may contribute as (a) an adaptation of jerk-based metrics for EPW contexts, (b) validation against WST, and (c) demonstration of responsiveness to training.

Jerk can be categorized as either directional or angular. Directional jerk (m/s3) refers to sudden changes in motion along the forward/backward, left/right, or up/down axes. Angular jerk (degrees/s3), on the other hand, captures abrupt changes in orientation—such as turning, tipping, or leaning. These two types differ in definition, measurement, units, and relevance. Using an IMU mounted on an EPW, a 2020 study found that novice users exhibited higher jerk than experienced users and that peak-jerk counts correlated with WST scores (Kamaraj, 2020). However, the study did not define classification bands for EPW driving performance, apply jerk analysis to outdoor tasks, or assess pre–post training changes.

Addressing these factors would help occupational therapists tailor training intensity, enable standardized tracking of progress, and offer insights into the ability of clients to manage EPW driving safely and effectively in indoor and outdoor settings.

Objectives

This exploratory pilot study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of sensor-based jerk metrics for assessing electric-powered wheelchair (EPW) driving performance. Specifically, the objectives were to:(a) Examine the influence of cognitive, physical, and sensory performance components on EPW driving under a jerk-based assessment framework.(b) Validate the efficacy of sensor-derived jerk metrics (median jerk, time above threshold, and Jerk Index) by correlating them with Wheelchair Skills Test (WST) scores.(c) Pilot the use of jerk metrics as outcome measures in an EPW training program, assessing their responsiveness to skill improvement.

Methodology

Study Design

A one-group pre/post-test quasi-experimental design was employed. Participants completed baseline assessments followed by four sessions of an EPW training program. Pre/post data were collected to evaluate both training effectiveness and the validity of jerk-based performance metrics.

Participants

Seven adults with mobility impairments (ages 43–71; two females, five males) were recruited through purposive sampling. Inclusion criteria included: age 50–75, community dwelling, regular medical follow-up, reduced mobility, and less than three months of EPW driving experience. One participant with over five years of EPW experience served as the “gold standard” benchmark.

Training Program

Training was based on seven Wheelchair Skills Test (WST) tasks. Each 45-min session emphasized straight driving, turning, slopes, and community navigation. Sessions were delivered once or twice weekly over one month, with immediate feedback provided to reinforce learning. Outdoor mobility was evaluated on a designated route at the campus of The Hong Kong Polytechnic University incorporating additional WST tasks such as slopes, turns, and obstacle negotiation.

Data Collection

Data collection consisted of two components (Figure 1). Firstly, the Performance Component Assessment which is Conducted by an assessor to gather physical, cognitive, sensory, and visual-motor coordination data. Secondly, the Wheelchair Skills Test (WST): An assessor-administered test involves seven tasks scored according to the WST Manual (Dalhousie University, 2023). A community driving assessment was conducted on a designated route at the university campus, primarily involving two straight movements and five turning movements. Tasks unrelated to basic EPW manipulation (e.g., reaching, curb navigation, transfers) were excluded to minimize confounding factors. Instrumentations

An Android smartphone running a custom app was mounted on the EPW’s right armrest. A custom app was developed to streamline assessment and data analysis by providing the features of: (1) Records of accelerometer and magnetometer data directly into a single spreadsheet, eliminating the need for data merging, (2) Displays of a unique 6-digit code when recording stops and the file is saved. Assessors simply note this number in a printed table next to the corresponding assessment task. This eliminates the need to stop and name each file manually, and (3) Standardizes the start of assessments by initially displaying a yellow screen, which automatically changes to green after approximately 3s. Participants are instructed to begin the WST task as soon as the screen turns green.

The app recorded directional acceleration via the accelerometer and compass heading via the magnetometer. Sensor data were recorded into spreadsheets for each WST task, with standardized task initiation and unique file codes.

A MPU6050 inertial measurement unit (IMU) mounted on the joystick recorded three-dimensional orientation (yaw, pitch, roll) using combined accelerometer and gyroscope data. Data were transmitted via Bluetooth to an Android tablet for processing.

Data Processing

Sampling Rate and Data Binning

The custom app installed on the smartphone and joystick IMU collected data at average sampling rates of 84 Hz and 90 Hz, respectively. These rates are sufficient to capture all relevant wheelchair and joystick movements, as typical human reaction frequencies do not exceed 4–4.4 Hz (Jain et al., 2015).

After data collection, sensor readings were averaged within 500 ms intervals. Binning was conducted to standardize data intervals across sensors, smooth out data for graphical analysis, and reduce data size for more efficient processing.

Calculation of Wheelchair Directional Jerk

Directional jerk was calculated from the acceleration data captured by the EPW-mounted sensors. Angular jerk was derived from joystick orientation data. j=da/dt j=directional jerk (m/s^3) a=acceleration (m/s^2) t=time (s)

The root mean square (RMS) jerk combines the jerk values from the x, y, and z directions into a single positive value, providing an overall measure of total jerk regardless of direction: j_RMS=sqrt((j_x^2+j_y^2+j_z^2)/3) where, j_RMS=jerk RMS (m/s^3) j_x,j_y,j_z=jerk for x,y,z direction (m/s^3)

EPW Driving Performance Metrics

Five outcome measures were derived from jerk RMS values computed for the seven WST tasks, namely Median EPW Directional Jerk, Median Joystick Angular Jerk, Time Above Threshold for EPW Directional Jerk, Time Above Threshold for Joystick Angular Jerk, and Jerk Index.

Median Jerk with Median values were used instead of means to reduce the influence of outliers. For each participant, median jerk values were reported for the selected tasks. Time Above Threshold was calculated for both EPW and joystick jerk measures, ‘Time Above Threshold’ was computed as the cumulative duration (in seconds) where jerk exceeded a threshold during selected tasks. This metric focused on periods of abrupt or unintended movement, excluding baseline or operational delays. The threshold was set to the median jerk value obtained from the gold standard participant. The Jerk Index provided an aggregated score (1–5) reflecting the magnitude of EPW directional jerk relative to the gold standard. Separate thresholds were established for straight-line and turning movements using magnetometer direction data. Scores were assigned based on five equal percentile bands.

Statistical Analysis

With sample size below 10, it is highly prone to unreliability, low power, and high false-positive rates (Type I errors) due to extreme sensitivity to outliers and random variation. Non-parametric analysis was conducted using SPSS v29.0.2.0. Mann-Whitney U Test was used to compare group means, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for categorial data. Spearman rho’s correlation coefficient (ρ) was used to examined the association between WST scores and performance components, and evaluated concurrent validity between WST scores and EPW driving performance metrics. Wilcoxon Signed-rank Test was used to compare pre-post training results. Significance was set at α = 0.05.

Ethics Approval and Informed Consent

Ethics approval was obtained from the Human Ethics Institutional Review Board of the Hong Kong Polytechnic University (Ref: HSEARS20250613002). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the study commenced.

Correlational Studies

All EPW driving performance metrics were correlated with WST scores. Median EPW directional jerk (ρ = −0.857, p = 0.014) showed significant negative correlations whereas joystick angular jerk (ρ = −0.750, p = 0.052) showed non-significant negative correlations.

Threshold jerk values (i.e., median jerk of gold standard) for Time Above Threshold were 19.97 deg/s3 (joystick) and 0.181 m/s3 (EPW). Time Above Threshold for EPW directional jerk (ρ = −0.786, p = 0.036) also showed strong negative correlations.

The Jerk Index for 7 WST Tasks (ρ = −0.786, p = 0.036) demonstrated the strongest correlation with WST scores. Jerk Index correlations for two community routes (ρ = −0.214, p = 0.645; ρ = −0.429, p = 0.337), were lower and not statistically significant (Table 4).

Training Outcome

Four participants completed the 4-session training. Change in WST and Jerk Index After Training was also computed. Wilcoxon signed-rank Test compared pre- and post-training scores for WST (Z = −1.826; p = 0.068) and the Jerk Index (Z = −1.826; p = 0.068) among four participants. Although WST scores improved (+21.3%) and Jerk Index decreased (−20.6%), results were not statistically significant probably due to the small sample size in analysis. After training, participants ID02 and ID03 advanced into below 25th percentile band close to the performance of gold standard, while ID01 improved by 50%. Universal improvements, both directionally consistent and clinically promising among participants indicates potential practical significance despite limited power.

Discussion

This pilot study explored the utility of jerk-based metrics for quantifying EPW driving performance. Contrary to prior literature suggesting that cognitive, sensory, and physical components are critical determinants of driving ability (Dicianno et al., 2012; Fehr et al., 2000; Massengale et al., 2005; Pellichero et al., 2021; McClosky & Webb, 2021; Sun et al., 2018; Bourassa et al., 2023), no significant associations were observed with WST scores in this study. This may reflect the small sample size and homogeneity of participants, underscoring the need for larger, more diverse cohorts to clarify these relationships.

Strong inverse correlations between jerk metrics and WST scores highlight the potential of sensor-derived measures to capture driving skill. The Jerk Index, in particular, emerged as the most sensitive metric, effectively integrating both the frequency and magnitude of abrupt movements. Unlike median jerk or time-above-threshold measures, the Jerk Index provided a nuanced assessment that distinguished between drivers with similar WST scores but differing smoothness profiles. This suggests that the Jerk Index may mitigate ceiling effects inherent in observational scoring and offer therapists a more precise tool for monitoring progress.

Analysis of jerk profiles revealed that turning maneuvers generated higher jerk values than straight-line tasks, particularly among novice drivers and those without prior vehicular experience. This finding emphasizes the importance of targeted training in turning skills, as these maneuvers appear to be a critical determinant of safe community mobility. Calibration of jerk thresholds across different EPW models may further enhance the generalizability of these metrics.

Although improvements in WST scores and Jerk Index values following training did not reach statistical significance, all participants demonstrated clinically meaningful directional gains. The four-session training program established a feasible protocol that incorporated essential skills such as straight driving, turning, slopes, and outdoor navigation. These preliminary findings suggest that jerk-based metrics are responsive to training and may serve as valuable outcome measures in rehabilitation.

Future research should focus on validating these metrics in larger samples, and comparing jerk-based measures with traditional smoothness parameters. Ultimately, integrating sensor-derived metrics into EPW assessment could provide occupational therapists with objective, standardized tools to enhance training, improve safety, and support community participation among wheelchair users.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the small sample size (N = 7) substantially restricts statistical power, increasing the likelihood of Type I errors and limiting the confidence with which findings can be generalized to broader populations. Second, the absence of a control group precludes strong causal inferences regarding the effectiveness of the training program, as improvements observed may reflect natural recovery trajectories or other external influences rather than the intervention itself. Third, the clinical sample was relatively homogeneous, consisting predominantly of post-stroke participants. This restricts the applicability of the findings to other diagnostic groups, such as individuals with traumatic brain injury or neurodegenerative conditions, who may respond differently to the intervention.

In addition, the study only relied on a single “gold standard” participant to provide a practical benchmark, thresholds derived from a larger panel of experts would yield more robust and representative standards. Finally, potential technical limitations must be considered. Variability in sensor attachment, Bluetooth latency, and other hardware-related issues may have introduced measurement error, thereby affecting the accuracy and reliability of the recorded data.

Taken together, these limitations suggest that the findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Future research would benefit from larger and more diverse samples, inclusion of appropriate control groups, isolate learning/training effects, consensus-based expert benchmarks, and rigorous evaluation of technical reliability to strengthen the validity and generalizability of the results.

Conclusion

Sensor-derived metrics, particularly the Jerk Index, provide a promising alternative for assessing EPW driving performance. These metrics demonstrated strong concurrent validity with WST scores and applicability in outdoor environment, addressing limitations of traditional observational assessments. In summary, jerk-based assessment represents a valuable advancement in EPW performance measurement, offering clinicians a more precise and responsive framework for training and evaluation. Further research with larger samples is needed to confirm their reliability and generalizability.
