Nonlinear and synergistic effects of demographic characteristics on urban polycentric structure using SHAP
Authors: Yichen Ruan, Xiaoyi Zhang, Mingyu Zhang, Feiyang Sun, Qiuxiao Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Polycentric urban development is promoted by urban planners and policy makers for its perceived benefits of alleviating urban issues. Theoretically, a polycentric urban structure is formed when different demographic groups present a collection of diverse and unique housing location preferences. Yet, only limited empirical studies have fully captured the complex effects of demographic characteristics on urban polycentric structures. Our study utilizes detailed demographic data and employs interpretable machine learning models to elucidate the nonlinear and synergistic relationship between demographic characteristics and the urban polycentric structure in the context of a city of 10 million population in China. When characterizing urban centers, the three most important demographic groups are young females, senior married couples, and middle-aged single individuals with basic education. Each urban center presents distinctive demographic compositions; for instance, middle-aged married individuals exerting a stronger influence in certain contexts, whereas in senior resident groups, married individuals have a more pronounced impact. Spatial heterogeneity is observed in the demographic profiles of urban centers; for instance, primary central cores are predominantly young, single females, whereas peripheral and secondary centers have stronger presence of highly educated residents.

Introduction

The current global urbanization process has entered a phase of decelerated growth. Since 1950, global urbanization has transitioned from over two-thirds of the population residing in rural areas to a gradual shift where urban populations began surpassing rural populations in 20101,2. In 2022, the global urbanization rate reached 55.3%, signifying a gradual deceleration in the urbanization process3,4. The optimization of urban internal spatial structures has gradually become a focal point in spatial planning. Urban polycentric structures are gaining increasing prominence in urban development as they play a central role in shaping urban organization and functionality.

Urban polycentric structures can effectively alleviate pressures on urban centers related to population, transportation, environment, and more. They promote the rational allocation of urban resources and balance socio-economic development5,6. Existing research has employed theoretical deduction, empirical judgment, statistical data, and spatiotemporal big data to measure the spatial form and performance of polycentricstructures7–10. However, cities are complex and evolving systems and polycentric structures that dynamically change with urban development11. Therefore, without a complete understanding of influencing factors and mechanisms, relying solely on current state measurements may not provide a comprehensive grasp of the development trends in polycentric structures, the identification of potential risks, or the formulation of forward-thinking strategies.

From a logical perspective on the spatial formation of urban polycentric structures, residential population demand is a key driving factor in their evolution. The mechanisms through which the demographic characteristic affects urban polycentric structures have nonlinear and synergistic effects, and primarily include population size, population structure, and spatiotemporal population changes12–14. Relevant research suggests that population size significantly impacts the formation and development of urban polycentric structures. Some researchers argue that as the population size increases, the size and number of urban centers will correspondingly increase, thereby promoting the development of urban polycentric structures15. Additionally, population mobility is another key consideration in the context of its impact on urban polycentric structures. Research indicates that population mobility is an important driver for the formation and development of urban centers16.On the other hand, demographic characteristic is also a critical factor influencing urban polycentric structures. For instance, cities with a higher degree of aging may see the development of more advanced industries related to healthcare, elderly care, and related sectors, thereby forming corresponding urban centers17.

For spatiotemporal population changes, firstly, population growth can stimulate the prosperity of urban centers and the development of secondary centers18,19. Some scholars have found that population growth can significantly increase the number of commercial and service establishments in urban centers, elevating their level of economic activity20. Second, population agglomeration can enhance the polarization effect of urban centers and drive the development of related industries21. For example, the concentration of high-tech industries can attract more tech talent, creating a positive feedback loop22. Moreover, population distribution and mobility can also influence the distribution and evolution of urban centers. For instance, some researchers have pointed out that rural-to-urban population migration can drive the urbanization process in peripheral areas of cities23. Additionally, studies indicate that population redistribution and re-agglomeration can lead to subcenter formation after population disperses from the core city areas24.

Both qualitative and quantitative research methods have been employed. In qualitative research, scholars primarily use in-depth case analysis, observation, and interviews to investigate how demographic characteristics influence the formation and development of urban polycentric structures. For instance, Ludek Sýkora and Martin Ourednek conducted a case study to explore population growth trends and the concentration of commercial and service industries in Prague and Brno metropolitan areas25. They found that population growth significantly influences the development of commercial and service industries in urban centers, particularly in terms of creating employment opportunities. However, qualitative research methods have limitations when examining the impact of population on urban polycentric structures, such as limited data sources and subjectivity in results26. In quantitative research, scholars use statistical models, spatial analysis, and data sources like demographic data, mobile phone signals, and location-based services (LBS) data to analyze the relationships between population and urban polycentric structures27–29.

Traditional methods such as multiple linear regression and geographically weighted regression (GWR) perform well in handling linear relationships30. However, in the study of polycentric urban structures, interactions among multiple features like land use, economic activity, and population density are often highly complex22. Machine learning methods, such as gradient boosting and random forests, can capture these more intricate nonlinear relationships and often provide better accuracy31. They offer the flexibility to model these interactions without the need for strict linear assumptions, this flexibility allows us to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing polycentricity and uncover patterns that might otherwise be overlooked32. A common criticism of machine learning models is their lack of interpretability, often referred to as the "black box" problem. SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations) provides an interpretable framework for understanding the contributions of individual features and their interactions. SHAP values can decompose the predictions of machine learning models into the contributions of each variable, while Shapley interaction values help explain the interactions between variables, making the decision-making process of the model more transparent33. This interpretability is crucial for understanding how different factors influence urban polycentricity and helps urban planners and policymakers better understand the study’s findings. Given the advantages of accuracy, nonlinear relationship fitting as well as interpretability, the use of machine learning and SHAP in urban studies has been increasing. Examples include analyzing the nonlinear impact and synergy effects of Transit-Oriented Development (TOD) on urban vitality34, the influence of urban form on urban heat islands35, and the role of urban morphology in shaping sustainable transportation36. These studies highlight the theoretical and practical advantages of machine learning in urban analysis, especially in handling high-dimensional data and revealing non-obvious relationships, demonstrating promising application prospects.

Existing research suffers from shortcomings in population data with high spatial accuracy and detailed demographic characteristics. There is a limited exploration of the spatial demands arising from different age structures, gender structures, and educational levels and their impact on urban polycentric structures. Additionally, Challenges lie in quantitatively explaining the complex effects and influences of demographic characteristics on urban polycentric structures. Given these limitations, the use of big data combined with explainable machine learning are helpful. This study utilizes point of interest (POI) data, high spatial accuracy, and detailed demographic characteristics of the residential population as the foundational data. Random Forest is used to train a binary classification model that distinguishes urban central areas. Decision rules are generated to explore how the population supports urban centers, and Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) interpretative analysis is used to reveal the influence of different demographic features on urban centers. And take the case study of Xiaoshan District in Hangzhou, China, provides a valuable reference for similar regions or cities of comparable scale in their development efforts.

(1) Center identification

The study identifies urban centers based on the theory that centers have a higher density than their surroundings. It utilizes a two-step approach, starting with data collection from Amap API and generating POIs in ArcMap. The kernel density method is then applied to transform POI points into a density distribution, which is imported into a 100 × 100 m2grid for center area identification. Finally, the Local Moran’s Index is used to calculate the kernel density within the grid, identifying High-High (H–H) clusters as urban centers.

(2) Model training and validation

To assess the impact of demographic characteristics, three comparative models based on Random Forest are employed: a total population model, an age group model, and a demographic model (including age, gender, and education). Population data is grouped based on different characteristics for model input, with parameter optimization during training. The accuracy of the models is then tested and compared.

(3) Explanation of influencing factors

The aim is to explore the contribution of individual factors and their interactions. Decision rules from the Random Forest model indicate demographic thresholds for center formation. The mean SHAP values assess the overall contribution of factors, with the most significant ones highlighted. SHAP interaction values analyze the contribution of factor interactions. Lastly, K-means clustering groups center grids based on demographic similarities, with individual samples from different clusters analyzed to observe commonalities and differences in factor influence.

Synergism is a ubiquitous phenomenon around the world which deserves attention. Synergism emerges when a cooperation or interaction causes an increase in the whole so that the whole is greater than the sum of parts. In this research, two variables generated synergistic effects (i.e., positive interaction effects) on the urban centers when the Shapley interaction values are above zero; otherwise, they had negative interaction effects on the urban centers.

We used the SHAP package (Python 3.9) to interpret the Random Forest model. The SHAP package contains an improved TreeExplainer method that efficiently calculates both Shapley and Shapley interaction values39. Based thereon, patterns of model behavior can be uncovered by globally summarizing the relative importance of centers variables, locally demonstrating nonlinear and interaction effects, and clustering center grids by similarity of demographic characteristics.

Research data

Point of interest (POI)

The data source for urban polycentric structure was POI from amap API. Polycentric structure can be assessed through different dimensions, such as land use, building density, population statistics and economic activity density40,41. From the perspective of its definition, polycentricity can be categorized into two types: the first emphasizes morphological characteristics, identifying polycentricity based on the scale, density, and spatial distribution of urban areas; the second views polycentricity as a function-oriented concept, focusing on the structure of urban functions and the flows, communications, and networks of cooperation between different urban units42.

POI data, which became popular around 2010, is characterized by geographic coordinates and functional labels. It can accurately reflect changes in urban building density and effectively represent the distribution of urban functions, thereby satisfying both morphological and functional measurements of polycentric structure43. Compared to traditional statistical data, POI data has the advantages of higher spatial precision, broader coverage, better accessibility, and more timely updates, making it more effective in reflecting the current state of urban polycentric development44. As a result, POI data has gained extensive attention and application in the study of polycentric structures.

According to the service content provided by the urban public centers in relevant studies, a total of 70,661 POIs in 10 categories were collected within the research scope, including catering services, scenic spots, shopping services, science and education, culture services, life services, sports and leisure services, medical and health services, accommodation services, government agencies and social organizations, financial and insurance services. POI data is mature in urban spatial applications with a high spatial accuracy and is commonly used to identify existing polycentric structures.

Grouping of demographic characteristics

Age significantly shapes urban center development due to varying service demands and consumer preferences across age groups. Urban centers are also influenced by gender, education, and marital status, which intersect with age demographics, affecting service consumption and needs. For example, gender imbalances can arise from social norms and demographic factors like migration, influencing urban structures. Additionally, educational attainment varies by gender, affecting service preferences. In this study, we quantified these characteristics as independent variables within a defined spatial context, acknowledging their complex interplay in urban dynamics.

The research data was sourced from the Population Database of the Hangzhou Municipal Planning Bureau (anonymous). In addition to age and residential spatial location, the database included characteristics such as gender, marital status, and the education level of the residential population.

Age structure was divided into five groups based on considerations of public service utilization and urban residents’ consumption behavior characteristics: these groups include the juvenile group (0–18), the youth group (19–29), the adult group (30–49), the elderly group (50–69), and the senior elderly group (70 +), totaling 2,041,460 individuals.

Gender structure was categorized into two groups: male and female. Educational structure was divided into three groups: those uneducated (illiterate or semi-literate), those with basic education (including primary, middle school, high school, or equivalent), and those with tertiary education (including bachelor’s, master’s, doctoral degrees, or the equivalent). The marital status structure was divided into two groups: married and single.

The initial grouping for the age group model was based on age data, and then other characteristics are grouped based on this foundation. In the juvenile group, as their gender awareness is still developing, many have not completed their education, and their consumption behavior often depend on their guardians, is not further subdivided. To ensure that overly small groups with total counts below 10,000 are not split, which could result in overly specific and imbalanced features, a hierarchy or upward merging of groups was considered. In this context, the young and elderly groups prioritize gender, while middle and senior age groups prioritize educational attainment. Therefore, the middle age and elderly group are primarily divided by educational attainment, while the young group and elderly group are primarily divided by gender. Many individuals in the young group have not completed their education, so their educational structure is not further subdivided. In the end, this results in 22 feature combinations. Discussion and conclusions

This study analyzed how demographic traits affect urban centers using POI data and residential demographics, including age, gender, marital status, and education, to form 22 demographic groups. It trained three Random Forest models for urban center identification, using population data, age structure, and the demographic groups. After testing for accuracy and comparing models, the best model’s decision rules were used to understand demographic distributions. The study also assessed the significance of these groups and their interactions through SHAP values and cluster analysis, focusing on Xiaoshan District, Hangzhou, China.

By comparing the confusion matrices of the three models, the demographic model exhibits the highest precision, considering the explanatory power of population structure features is crucial. The generated decision rules are all based on combinations of multiple rather than single features, indicating that the development of urban centers relies on diverse population demands. Different population groups have varying lower limits, implying that different groups have varying degrees of influence on the development of urban centers. The population thresholds in the rules are all around 1000, suggesting that each km2 of urban center area requires approximately 1000 people to support demand. The most prominent groups affecting urban centers are YF, SBMM, SBMF, SH, MBSF, and MBSM, with no single decisive feature among them. Nonlinear correlation is the primary characteristic of demographic impact on urban polycentric structure, including differences between age groups, where middle age single households have more influence, but senior age married households have stronger influence. Synergistic effects significantly contribute to the development of polycentric structures, with female groups often interacting more strongly with other female groups, indicating convergence in consumption and service preferences. Male groups tend to interact with female groups of the same age or younger, possibly indicating family-based synergies. Spatial heterogeneity exists in the demographic mechanisms, according to the analysis of sample influence after clustering reveals two patterns: (1) the core area of the main center is more influenced by young women and single women and (2) the peripheral area and secondary centers of the main center are more influenced by highly educated populations.

It is verified that the significant role that population plays in the formation and development of urban centers. Even in contemporary life, with the advancement of Internet technology and the Internet of Things (IoT), the decentralized way of life has become important. However, residential life still depends on urban public spaces and modes of public consumption, and urban centers are expected to remain a crucial part of urban structures in the foreseeable future. Furthermore, this study delves into the analysis of demographic characteristics such as age, gender, marital status, and education level, and their influence on urban centers. An exploration of the local impact of demographic characteristics groups, interactions between multiple groups, and the explanatory differences of individual samples after clustering sheds light on the nonlinear contributions of demographic characteristics to urban centers. This work lays the foundation for understanding the mechanisms through which population affects urban centers. This contributes to the formulation of effective urban planning and management strategies, including the consideration of the diverse impacts of population structures on multi-center layouts and the configuration of urban functional facilities.

In future work, we plan to conduct further comparative analyses to evaluate the applicability of more models and provide a deeper theoretical discussion. Additionally, we intend to explore the inclusion of more advanced machine learning techniques, such as deep learning models, to assess their effectiveness in capturing the complex, non-linear relationships inherent in urban data. We also plan to investigate the potential for incorporating geospatial data from different sources, including remote sensing and social media, to enhance the robustness of our analysis. Furthermore, we aim to expand the scope of our research by applying our methods to different urban contexts, such as smaller cities or rural–urban transition areas, to evaluate the generalizability of our findings. By doing so, we hope to better understand how different population and economic structures impact polycentric urban development across various geographic scales. We will also engage in a more in-depth theoretical exploration of the mechanisms through which demographic characteristics influence urban spatial structures, thus providing comprehensive insights that can be used to formulate data-driven urban planning strategies.
