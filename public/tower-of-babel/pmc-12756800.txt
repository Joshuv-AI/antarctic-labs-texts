Synthesis and Performance Evaluation of a High-Temperature Salt-Resistant Fluid Loss Additive
Authors: Yuhuan Ding, Xian Wu, Cheng Ma, Chao Yang, Jinghui Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To address the challenges encountered in drilling operations for high-temperature and high-salinity hydrocarbon reservoirs, this study successfully designed and synthesized a novel organic–inorganic core–shell structured fluid loss control agentDH. This material features rigid calcium carbonate nanoparticles as the core, encapsulated by a copolymer shell of acrylamide (AM) and 2-acrylamido-2-methylpropanesulfonic acid (AMPS). This unique structure is designed to fundamentally enhance the thermal stability and salt tolerance compared to conventional pure polymer treatments, by restraining the curling and decomposition of polymer chains under extreme conditions. Characterization results confirmed the successful construction of the core–shell structure and demonstrated excellent thermal stability. Performance evaluation showed that DH maintains outstanding fluid control performance even after exposure to high temperature (200 °C), high salt concentration (15% NaCl), and multiple aging cycles. Compared to the base fluid, DH reduces fluid loss by over 95%. More importantly, its performance significantly surpasses that of conventional agents (e.g., PAC); under similarly harsh conditions where the latter fails (fluid loss >25 mL), DH stably controls the API fluid loss below 7 mL. Mechanism studies revealed that efficient fluid control is achieved because DH, with its uniform particle size distribution (matching filter cake pore sizes) and large specific surface area, effectively plugs filter cake micropores, thereby forming a compact, low-permeability filter cake. This study provides an innovative and effective solution for developing next-generation drilling fluid additives suitable for extreme operating conditions.

Introduction

With the gradual decline in large-scale discoveries of oil and gas reserves in shallow and medium-deep reservoirs globally, the focus of exploration has shifted toward deep and ultradeep formations. This strategic shift presents severe challenges for drilling operations, particularly due to extreme high-temperature and high-salinity (HTHS) environments, placing unprecedented demands on drilling fluid performance, especially its fluid loss control capability. − Under high temperature and pressure, increased drilling fluid filtration not only significantly raises operational costs but can also lead to a series of technical and environmental issues, such as wellbore instability and formation damage. −

In this context, water-based drilling fluids (WBDFs) are considered an ideal choice due to their environmental friendliness and cost-effectiveness. However, the core additives of traditional WBDFspolymeric fluid loss control agentsgenerally exhibit performance shortcomings under HTHS conditions. As well depth increases and circulating temperatures accumulate, the molecular chains of these polymers are prone to curling and degradation, causing the rapid failure of their key functions: controlling filtrate viscosity and forming a compact filter cake on the wellbore. − Although progress has been made through optimizing polymer structures, developing fluid loss control agents that can withstand temperatures exceeding 180 °C while also offering salt tolerance remains a major focus and challenge in current research. ,

In recent years, the emergence of nanotechnology has provided new avenues for overcoming this bottleneck. − Research indicates that incorporating nanoparticles into polymer systems to create organic–inorganic composite materials can significantly enhance the overall performance of additives. − Among these, the core–shell structure design, featuring rigid nanoparticles as the “core” and polymer as the “shell”, is particularly noteworthy. This structure effectively restricts the thermal motion of polymer chains through the steric hindrance effect of the inorganic core, thereby delaying the aging process and enhancing the material’s structural stability and performance durability in extreme environments. , Although this concept is promising, how to achieve customized design and synthesis for specific drilling fluid environments and gain a deep understanding of its working mechanism remain a research gap requiring further exploration.

Based on this, the present study aims to design and synthesize a novel organic–inorganic core–shell structured fluid loss control agent. This paper will systematically describe its synthesis route, characterize its structure and temperature resistance, and comprehensively evaluate its fluid loss control performance under HTHS conditions through comparative experiments with conventional agents. The goal is to provide reliable technical support and a theoretical basis for developing next-generation high-performance WBDFs. −

Materials and Methods

Materials

Acrylamide (AM), 2-acrylamido-2-methylpropanesulfonic acid (AMPS), CaCO3, K2S2O8, NaCl, Na2CO3, and Na2SO3all chemicals were of analytical grade. A GJS-B12K variable-frequency high-speed mixer (Qingdao Bairuida Petroleum Machinery), a GRL-BX3 portable roller oven, a ZNN-D6 six-speed rotational viscometer, a D2004W electric stirrer (Shanghai Meipu Instruments), a Q600 simultaneous TGA-DSC analyzer (TA Instruments, USA), a SU8010 field-emission scanning electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan), and a ZetaProbe laser particle size analyzer (Brookhaven Instruments Corporation, USA) were also used.

Synthesis of DH

A measured amount of 8 g of dried nano-CaCO3 was added to a beaker along with deionized water. After ultrasonication and homogenization, a uniform presuspension was obtained. Then, 25 g of AM, 5 g of AMPS, and 0.5 g of potassium persulfate initiator (K2S2O8) were sequentially introduced into the predispersion system. The mixture was allowed to react under vacuum at 60 °C for 2–4 h, followed by vacuum drying at the same vacuum level for 24 h. Finally, the product was pulverized to obtain a high-temperature and salt-resistant filtrate reducer (DH). The synthesis mechanism and preparation flowchart are detailed in Figures and .

Methods for Characterization and Analysis of DH

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy: A WQF-520 Fourier transform infrared spectroscope was used to test the synthetic filtrate loss reducer DH, and the potassium bromide pellet method was used to prepare samples.

Thermogravimetric (TG) analysis: The SDTQ600 TG-DTA Instruments was used to test the thermal stability of the synthesized filtrate reducer DH.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM): The surface morphology of the filter cake before and after aging was characterized by an SU 8010 field emission electron scanning microscope.

Mapping: An SU8010 field emission scanning electron microscope and an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) were used to map the element distribution in the micro region of the sample and accurately obtain the content changes and distribution laws of new elements in different regions.

N2 adsorption–desorption (BET): The specific surface area and pore volume of the sample were measured by using a BSD-660 M A6M automatic physical static instrument.

Particle size distribution: The Brookhaven 90PD laser particle size meter was used to test the particle size distribution of the sample to achieve accurate particle size and dispersion of the sample.

Preparation of Drilling Fluid and Performance Evaluation

Preparation of Drilling Fluid

A 1000 mL portion of water was accurately measured in a clean container, followed by 20 g of bentonite and 2 g of anhydrous sodium carbonate, which were stirred for 20 min using a high-speed stirring device. After stirring, the container was closed at room temperature and cured for 24 h. After the above-mentioned operations, a freshwater base slurry with a concentration of 2% could be successfully prepared. A certain proportion of NaCl in the total amount of the base slurry was added according to the corresponding proportion and then stirred with a stirring device for 20 min, so that NaCl was fully dissolved and uniformly dispersed in the base slurry. Subsequently, the base slurry was left at room temperature and subjected to airtight treatment and curing for 24 h, resulting in a brine base slurry containing certain ratios of NaCl.

Rheology and Filtration Properties

Dosage

The evaluation results of the rheological and filtration properties of drilling fluids before and after rolling aging at 150 °C for 16 h with the addition of 15% NaCl under different DH dosage levels are shown in Figure .

Effect of different dosages on the change of properties of DH before and after aging.

As illustrated in Figure , DH dosage significantly modulated the fluid’s rheological and filtration properties. Increasing the DH concentration progressively enhanced the apparent viscosity (AV), plastic viscosity (PV), and yield point (YP), a result of the polymer network reinforcing the fluid’s internal structure. The optimum fluid loss reduction, from 144 to 5.6 mL at 2% dosage after thermal aging, is attributed to a dual mechanism: the rise in AV directly impeded filtrate invasion, while the concurrent increase in PV signaled polymer swelling, which improved pore plugging and yielded a denser filter cake. Pushing the dosage to 3% offered minimal further benefit (4.6 mL fluid loss) but induced excessive viscosity (AV = 100.5 mPa s, PV = 58.0 mPa s, YP = 43.5 Pa), pointing to agglomeration and compromised practicality. Therefore, a 2% dosage is identified as the ideal compromise.

Temperature Resistance

The evaluation results of the rheological and filtration properties of the drilling fluid before and after rolling aging at 120–220 °C for 16 h with 2% DH and 15% NaCl are shown in Figure .

Effect of different temperatures on the change of the DH properties.

As shown in Figure , the AV, PV, and YP values of the drilling fluid gradually decreased as the temperature increased. When the temperature was raised from 120 to 220 °C/16 h, the AV decreased from 87.0 to 19.0 mPa s, the PV decreased from 54.0 to 15.0 mPa s, and the YP decreased from 33.8 to 6.1 Pa. The fluid loss volume increased progressively with temperature: after aging at 200 °C/16 h, the fluid loss was 6.8 mL; however, at 210 °C/16 h, the fluid loss increased sharply to 18.4 mL, indicating the structural degradation of the DH material.

High temperature intensifies the thermal motion of polymer molecules, causing them to coil from an extended state into compact clusters, reducing their effective volume. This directly leads to a decrease in AV, PV, and YP. Simultaneously, a high temperature weakens the hydrogen bonds between water molecules and hydrophilic groups, disrupting the stable hydration shell and further impairing the hydration and thickening capabilities of the polymers. The deterioration in rheological properties consequently damages fluid loss control. The protective colloid ability and pore-plugging capacity of the polymers are significantly compromised due to molecular coiling and weakened hydration, leading to the flocculation of clay particles and preventing the formation of a compact filter cake. Ultimately, a loose and highly permeable filter cake is formed, resulting in a significant increase in fluid loss.

Temperature Cycling Resistance

The evaluation results of the rheological and filtration properties of the drilling fluid after rolling aging at 150–220 °C for different aging periods, with 2% DH and 15% NaCl, are shown in Figure .

Effect of aging time at different temperatures on the change of DH properties.

As shown in Figure , after multiple aging cycles at different temperatures, the viscosity of DH gradually increased. After three aging cycles at 170 °C/16 h, the AV, PV, and YP were 21.0 mPa s, 16.0 mPa s, and 5.1 Pa, respectively. Regarding rheological properties, the filtration loss was gradual; after three cycles of aging at 170 °C/16 h, the filtration loss was 5.6 mL, and the filtration effect was still excellent. However, filtration was less effective at more extreme aging temperatures and multiple aging cycles. In summary, DH has excellent high-temperature aging resistance.

The core provides rigid support, thermal stability, and structural integrity, preventing excessive coiling of molecules at high temperatures. The shell offers hydration groups responsible for hydration expansion, adsorption onto clay particles, and pore plugging. Even at the same temperature, the degradation process continues to occur cumulatively. In the initial stage of aging, elevated temperature drives processes such as hydrolysis reactions, causing the shell layer to begin hydrolyzing and molecular chains to undergo slight breakage, resulting in an initial decline in performance. Under prolonged exposure to thermal and hydraulic shear forces, the binding sites become unstable and the already degraded and shortened polymer chains of the shell are more prone to detaching from the rigid surface of the core. With a further extension of the aging time, the performance of the shell deteriorates significantly.

Salt Resistance

The evaluation results of the rheological and filtration properties of the drilling fluid before and after rolling aging at 150 °C for 16 h with 2% DH and 0–35% NaCl are presented in Figure .

Effect of different NaCl concentrations on the performance change of DH before and after aging.

As shown in Figure , after aging at 150 °C/16 h, with the concentration of sodium chloride from 0 to 35%, the AV, PV, and YP of the drilling fluid did not change significantly, and the fluctuation range of each parameter was relatively stable and good. The rheological properties of AV, PV, and YP were 67.5 mPa s, 45.0 mPa s, and 23.0 Pa, respectively, when the sodium chloride content was 35%. There was no significant change in the filtration volume, and the fluctuation range was relatively stable and good. When the addition of sodium chloride was 35%, the filtration loss was 4.2 mL. In conclusion, DH has good salt resistance.

In the low salt concentration range, the electric double layer surrounding the polymer molecular chains is compressed, weakening the electrostatic repulsion between the chains. The salt concentration is insufficient to induce full and compact coiling of the polymer chains, instead leaving them in an unstable state that is neither fully extended nor tightly coiled, resulting in poor performance. In the high salinity range, a large number of salt ions compete for water molecules surrounding the polymer chains, completely disrupting their hydration shell. For polymers with strong hydration groups such as AM/AMPS, this causes extreme dehydration and tight coiling of the molecular chains, forming a dense, colloidal particulate structure. Furthermore, due to its unique core–shell structure, countless uniform “composite colloidal particles” consisting of a rigid core and a dense polymeric shell are formed, thereby delivering excellent performance.

Effect of DH on Filter Cake Structure

According to the analysis of nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms shown in Figure , (a) and (b) exhibit typical type IV isotherms, a feature that clearly indicates the presence of mesoporous structures in the material. The test results show that the adsorption line measured as the equilibrium pressure increases and the desorption line measured as the pressure decreases do not coincide at a certain relative pressure range, and separate to form a ring; the adsorption amount of the desorption line is larger than that of the adsorption branch at the same relative pressure, and a clear hysteresis ring is formed between the two. The test results of (c) and (d) show that the polymer chains in the filter-loss-reducing agent form a pore in the filter cake. The results of (c) and (d) also show that the polymer chains in the filter loss reducer form an elastic network in the pores of the filter cake, and flexible pores appear, which makes part of the pores compressible; moreover, the mesopores are compressed under the adsorption pressure, and the pores cannot be completely desorbed after the gas adsorption, which leads to the desorption curve of the low-pressure region “hanging”, and results in gas stagnation, leading to the hysteresis loop not being closed. The nonclosure of the hysteresis loop reflects that the gas is difficult to desorb, indicating that the pore is blocked or compressed, and the filter cake will be more dense.

SEM Analysis

In order to study the action mechanism of the filtrate reducer (DH) in a drilling fluid, a 15% brine-based slurry was prepared. After aging at 150 °C/16 h, the API test was carried out in the brine-based slurry containing 2% filtrate reducer (DH). After testing, the filter cake was taken and dried, and its microstructure was examined by SEM. Photographs of the filter cake and SEM images at different magnifications before and after aging are shown in Figure .

Filter cake before and after addition of DH and SEM of the filter cake (a: filter cake before aging; b: filter cake with 2% DH before aging; c: filter cake after aging; d: filter cake with 2% DH after aging; e and f: SEM of filter cake without and with DH before aging at 2000×; g and h: SEM of filter cake without and with DH after aging at 2000×; i and j: SEM of filter cake without and with DH before aging at 5000×; and k and l: SEM of DH-free and DH-containing filter cake after aging at 5000×).

SEM was used to study the effect of polymer filter loss reducer on the microstructure of the filter cake formed by the drilling fluid system in order to analyze the mechanism of filter loss reduction. First, the microscopic morphology of the filter cake formed before and after the aging of the base slurry was observed, and it can be clearly seen that there are obvious pores and tiny cracks between the viscous particles and the surface of the particles is relatively rough. This is because the filter cake formed by the base slurry consists of layers of viscous particles. This simple stacking makes its structure loose, thus forming obvious pores and small cracks; the filtration loss channel is formed by these pores and small cracks, increasing the permeability of the filter cake, and thus making the filter cake relatively loose and the quality of the filter cake poorer. Then, a comparative study was carried out to observe the microscopic morphology of the filter cake formed before and after the aging of the base slurry after the addition of DH. After the addition of DH to the base slurry, the surface of the filter cake became smoother, and the particles on the clay were smaller. A comparison of Figure a–d clearly shows that there were no pore spaces and tiny cracks on the surface of the filter cake, which indicated that DH could effectively prevent clay from aggregating and form a denser structure, so that it is no longer a simple accumulation, but the clay particles on the filter cake form a whole through hydration, which greatly reduces the amount of filtration loss and thus improves the quality of the filter cake.

This morphological difference is quantitatively corroborated by the BET data: after the addition of DH, both the specific surface area and pore volume of the filter cake decreased by an order of magnitude. This provides compelling evidence that DH does not merely “cover″ the filter cake surface macroscopically but fundamentally reconstructs the pore structure of the filter cake from the nanoscale to the microscale. The underlying mechanism involves the optimized particle size distribution of DH, which enables effective bridging across larger pore throats during filter cake formation, while its flexible polymer chains fill the finer pores. Ultimately, this process transforms the originally loose and porous structure into a low-permeability barrier, thereby significantly reducing the fluid loss volume.

Mapping Analysis

Elemental mapping provides direct evidence of DH’s distribution and adsorption within the filter cakethe critical initial step for effective fluid loss control. Compared to the base slurry filter cake, which primarily exhibits elements from clay (Si, O) and salt (Na, Cl), the DH-modified filter cake shows distinct signals of Ca (from the nano-CaCO3 core), S (from AMPS sulfonic groups), and N (from amide groups). The uniform distribution of these elements confirms the successful integration of DH throughout the filter cake matrix. More significantly, the spatial correlation of these elements reveals a sophisticated working mechanism. The consistent S and N signals demonstrate extensive coverage of clay particles by the polymer shell through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions. Simultaneously, the homogeneous Ca distribution indicates an even dispersion of rigid nano-CaCO3 cores within this hybrid matrix. This specific elemental architecture suggests a synergistic dual-phase mechanism: the polymer shell functions as a molecular “binder” that envelops clay particles, while the dispersed nano-CaCO3 cores act as structural “scaffolds” that reinforce the filter cake framework. Under high-pressure conditions, this composite structure maintains optimal porositypreventing both excessive compaction and structural collapsethereby ensuring consistent low permeability. The mapping analysis thus visually confirms that DH actively reorganizes the filter cake into a functionally graded composite material through its unique core–shell architecture rather than simply mixing with the slurry components. Particle Size Distribution Analysis

As shown in Figure , the SEM image of the blank filter paper reveals pore sizes ranging approximately from 18 to 20 μm based on the scale bar. According to the “Optimal Particle Size Theory”, when the mean particle diameter (D mean) of sealing materials satisfies D mean ≈ (0.33–0.67) × pore diameter, both bridging stability and pore-filling efficiency can be achieved simultaneously. This implies that for the fluid loss reducer (DH) in a saline base slurry, optimal performance would be expected when the particle size falls within the range of 5940–13,400 nm. Under these conditions, the particle size distribution is presumed to best match the ideal 1/3–2/3 golden ratio relative to the formation of pore dimensions.
