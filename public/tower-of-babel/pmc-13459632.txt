Molecular detection of endemic coronaviruses and enterovirus D68 in respiratory samples from 2016 in Panama: a retrospective study
Authors: Yobelis Quintero, Juan Castillo Mewa, Danilo Franco, Brechla Moreno, Jacinto Pérez, Ilein Gómez, Leyda Abrego
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn 2016, Panama’s Surveillance Program for Influenza and Other Respiratory Viruses received 4041 samples with suspected viral respiratory infection, of which 68.78% tested positive on the respiratory viral panel, while the remaining 31.22% resulted in no etiological diagnosis. Given the need to determine which other viruses could cause these respiratory infections, a retrospective search for endemic coronaviruses and Enterovirus D68 was conducted, with the primary objective of detecting them in negative samples from that year.MethodsOne hundred and twenty-seven nasopharyngeal swab samples were retrospectively selected, all of which had been previously tested using a routine respiratory viral panel for pandemic Influenza viruses, Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), Human Metapneumovirus (hMPV), Parainfluenza viruses (PIV-1, PIV-2, PIV-3), Adenovirus (AdV), and Rhinovirus (RV). Of these, 70% were negative and 30% positive for at least one respiratory virus, and were subsequently classified by age group, clinical outcome and geographic location. Samples and controls were considered positive with a Ct value ≤ 35 cycles. The statistical analysis was performed using Pearson’s chi-square, considering a p value < 0.05 as statistically significant.ResultsThe human coronavirus 229E (HCoV-229E) (n = 3), human coranvirus OC-43 (HCoV-OC43) (n = 3) and EV-D68 (n = 17) viruses were detected, presenting viral co-detections between HCoV-229E/pandemic Influenza A/H1, HCoV-OC43/Parainfluenza type 1, Enterovirus D68/Rhinovirus, Enterovirus D68/Respiratory Syncytial Virus and Enterovirus D68/Parainfluenza type 3. Background

Endemic coronaviruses are respiratory viruses that contribute to a significant proportion of upper and lower respiratory tract infections in adults and children [1].

Coronaviruses belong to the family Coronaviridae and are classified into four genera: alpha-, beta-, gamma-, and deltacoronaviruses [2, 3]. Human coronaviruses are restricted to the alpha and betacoronavirus genera [4].

Currently, seven human coronaviruses (HCoV) are known. Among them, HCoV-229E and human coronavirus NL63 (HCoV-NL63) are alphacoronaviruses, while HCoV-OC43, human coronavirus HKU1 (HCoV-HKU1), severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV), SARS-CoV-2, and Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV) are betacoronaviruses [5].

Among Ribonucleic Acid (RNA) viruses, these have the largest known genome with a highly conserved genomic organization [6].

Infections caused by these viruses can range from mild, common cold-like illnesses to severe lower respiratory tract infections [7]. Mild infections are characterized by symptoms such as nasal congestion and rhinorrhea, headache, cough, sore throat, and general malaise, while more severe infections are usually associated with pneumonia [8].

On the other hand, Enterovirus D68 (EV-D68) is an emerging human pathogen that causes respiratory infections. This virus has posed a significant threat to global public health, highlighting the importance of maintaining constant surveillance of its circulation [9].

Enteroviruses (EV) belong to the genus Enterovirus within the family Picornaviridae and include species that infect humans [10, 11]. Unlike most, EV-D68 is primarily associated with respiratory infections rather than enteric disease [12], sharing genetic features with enteroviruses but biological characteristics with rhinoviruses [13].

EV-D68 primarily infects the upper respiratory tract; however, infections can also be associated with lower respiratory tract infections and serious neurological conditions such as acute flaccid myelitis (AFM) [14].

Mild infections cause symptoms such as fever and cough, while severe infections cause wheezing, pneumonia, hypoxia and difficulty breathing [15], with children being at greater risk of developing severe infections caused by this virus [16].

The AFM is a polio-like neurological condition characterized by flaccid limb weakness, gray matter lesions, or paralysis in some cases [17]. It may also present with decreased muscle tone, impaired reflexes, and, in severe cases, respiratory failure [18]. However, while this is a serious disease, it remains rare, and only a small number of EV-D68 infections progress to AFM [19].

Despite this, there are currently no approved vaccines or treatments for infections caused by this respiratory virus [20]. Understanding its epidemiology is crucial for elucidating outbreak patterns and dynamics, predicting its long-term disease burden, and assessing its impact on public health [21].

Respiratory infections are a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, highlighting the importance of accurate viral diagnosis. However, the cost of polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based methods often limits the number of pathogens included in diagnostic panels, hindering the identification of causative agents not routinely tested [22].

Among the viruses not typically included in diagnostic panels, the endemic coronaviruses HCoV-229E, HCoV-OC43, HCoV-NL63, and HCoV-HKU1 account for a substantial fraction of upper respiratory tract infections [23]. They are estimated to cause between 15% and 30% of annual common colds globally [24], and while they generally produce mild symptoms, they can cause severe illness in older adults, immunocompromised individuals, and young children [25].

In addition to these coronaviruses, EV-D68 is an emerging respiratory pathogen. After causing sporadic infections since its discovery, outbreaks were reported in 2014 in the United States and Canada, associated with cases of AFM, primarily in children [21]. This condition, similar to poliomyelitis, is characterized by acute flaccid limb weakness and gray matter lesions [17]. To date, no approved vaccine or antiviral treatment is available.

During 2016, the Panamanian Surveillance System for Influenza and other respiratory viruses received 4,041 samples of respiratory infections. Of these, 62.78% (n = 2537) tested positive for the most common viruses included in the respiratory viral panel. In the remaining 37.22% (n = 1504) of the samples, the causative agent of the respiratory infections could not be determined, meaning these patients with negative results were unable to obtain an etiological diagnosis of their illness.

In Panama, epidemiological information on endemic coronaviruses and EV-D68 is limited, hindering the understanding of their circulation patterns and their impact on public health. This lack of data underscores the importance of strengthening surveillance and generating local evidence on viruses that often go undetected in conventional diagnostic panels. Therefore, detecting and describing their presence in respiratory samples is essential to support epidemiological surveillance and public health decision-making. This study aims to identify endemic coronaviruses and EV-D68 in clinical samples from 2016 in Panama to assess whether they caused previously undiagnosed respiratory infections or were involved in co-detections with other circulating viruses.

Materials and methods

An observational, retrospective and descriptive study was conducted, based on the analysis of biological samples collected by the National Surveillance System for Influenza and other Respiratory Viruses in Panama at the Instituto Conmemorativo Gorgas de Estudios de la Salud (ICGES) during the year 2016. Sample selection

Nasopharyngeal swab samples were selected from patients with suspected respiratory infection, as part of the national respiratory virus epidemiological surveillance system in Panama, which systematically collects and processes respiratory samples through sentinel health centers, as previously described [26]. Samples processed at ICGES using the routine respiratory viral panel during the study period were included. Samples with insufficient biological material were excluded.

A total of 127 nasopharyngeal swab samples were retrospectively and conveniently selected, all of which had previously been tested using the routine respiratory viral panel that detects pandemic Influenza, Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), Human Metapneumovirus (hMPV), Parainfluenza type 1 (PIV-1), Parainfluenza type 2 (PIV-2), Parainfluenza type 3 (PIV-3), Adenovirus (AdV) and Rhinovirus (RV) (Supplementary Table 1). This routine respiratory viral panel includes eight respiratory viruses. However, when a sample tests positive for Influenza, further typing is performed to determine whether the virus is Influenza A or Influenza B. Influenza A-positive samples are further classified (pandemic Influenza A/H1, Influenza A/H3, or Influenza A/Untyped), while Influenza B-positive samples are analyzed to identify the lineage as either B/Victoria or B/Yamagata (Supplementary Table 1).

70% of the samples corresponded to specimens previously reported as negative in the respiratory viral panel, whereas the remaining 30% had been classified as positive for at least one of the detected viruses. The samples with previously positive results were included to assess the potential presence of co-detections with other respiratory viruses.

Nucleic acid extraction

Nucleic acids were extracted from 200 µL of selected samples using the MagMAX™ Viral/Pathogen II Nucleic Acid Isolation Kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Extraction was performed on the KingFisher™ Flex Purification System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using the MVP_2Wash_200_Flex program. The extracts were eluted in 50 µL of the kit elution buffer and stored at − 20 °C until use.

Molecular detection of human coronavirus and Enterovirus D68

The selected samples were processed by one-step real-time Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) for the detection of the endemic coronaviruses (HCoV-229E, HCoV-HKU1, HCoV-NL63, HCoV-OC43) and EV-D68. Molecular detection was based on amplification of the nucleocapsid gene (N) for HCoV and the 5’ UTR region for EV-D68. The AgPath-ID™ One-Step RT-PCR kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) was used, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The TaqMan™ probes for each target were tagged with different fluorophores such as FAM (HCoV-229E and EV-D68), VIC (HCoV-NL63), CY5 (HCoV-OC43) and ROX (HCoV-HKU1).

The detection assays included a previously characterized positive control sample and nuclease-free water as the negative control in each run to ensure the validity of the results. In addition, amplification of the human RNase P gene was used as an internal control to verify specimen quality and the integrity of the RNA extraction process.

Detection of HCoV-229E, HCoV-NL63, and HCoV-OC43 by real-time nested multiplex RT-PCR

A one-step real-time nested multiplex RT-PCR was conducted following the protocol described by [27]. The reaction was performed on the CFX96 real-time system using the CFX Maestro software.

The amplification was carried out in a 10 µL reaction mixture containing 1.5 µL of nuclease-free water, 5 µL of 2X RT-PCR Buffer, 0.5 µL of 10 µM primer mix (containing both outer and inner primers; see Supplementary Table 2), 0.5 µL of 10 µM probe mix (containing all three probes; see Supplementary Table 2), 0.5 µL of 25X RT-PCR Enzyme Mix and 2 µL of RNA sample or control. The RT-PCR cycling protocol consisted of an initial reverse transcription step at 50 °C for 20 min, followed by an activation/denaturation step at 95 °C for 15 min. This was followed by an initial amplification phase of 10 cycles at 94 °C for 30 s, 64 °C for 40 s, and 72 °C for 40 s, and then 40 additional cycles at 94 °C for 30 s, 54 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. Fluorescence acquisition was performed during the annealing step of the final 40-cycle amplification stage.

Detection of HCoV-HKU1 by real-time RT-PCR

A one-step real-time RT-PCR was performed on the QuantStudio™ 5 system and analyzed using QuantStudio™ Design & Analysis Software v1.5.1. The reaction was carried out in a 25 µL reaction mixture containing 4.0 µL of nuclease-free water, 12.5 µL of 2X RT-PCR Buffer, 1 µL of 10 µM forward primer, 1 µL of 10 µM reverse primer, 0.5 µL of 5 µM probe (see Supplementary Table 2 for primers and probe), 1 µL of 25X RT-PCR Enzyme Mix and 5 µL of RNA sample or control. The cycling protocol consisted of an initial reverse transcription step at 50 °C for 10 min, followed by an enzyme activation/denaturation step at 95 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 45 amplification cycles were performed at 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Fluorescence acquisition was carried out during the 60 °C annealing/extension step of each cycle. Primers and probes were supplied by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), Atlanta, Georgia, as part of the national respiratory virus surveillance program [28].

Detection of enterovirus D68 by real-time RT-PCR

The detection was conducted by one-step, real-time RT-PCR following the protocol described by [29]. The reaction was performed on the QuantStudio™5 system and analyzed using QuantStudio™ Design & Analysis Software v1.5.1. Amplifications were performed in a 25 µL reaction volume comprising 5 µL of nuclease-free water, 12.5 µL of 2X RT-PCR Buffer, 0.5 µL of 10 µM forward primer (see Supplementary Table 2 for primers and probe), 0.5 µL of 10 µM reverse primer, 0.5 µL of 5 µM hydrolysis probe, 1 µL of 25X RT-PCR Enzyme Mix, and 5 µL of RNA sample or control. Thermal cycling was conducted under the following conditions: 45 °C for 15 min, 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 45 s. Fluorescence acquisition was performed at 60 °C.

Detection of RNase P by real-time RT-PCR

Amplification of the human RNase P gene was performed as an internal control for the molecular detection workflow. Reactions were carried out using the QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System, operated with the QuantStudio™ Design & Analysis Software v1.5.1. Each 10 µL reaction consisted of 1.5 µL of nuclease-free water, 5 µL of 2× RT–PCR Master Mix, 0.5 µL of 10 µM primer pool (containing primers; see Supplementary Table 2), 0.5 µL of labeled hydrolysis probe (see Supplementary Table 2), 0.5 µL of Enzyme Mix, and 2 µL of extracted RNA.

Thermal cycling conditions were as follows: reverse transcription at 50 °C for 30 min, followed by inactivation of the reverse transcriptase at 95 °C for 15 min. Amplification proceeded with 10 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 64 °C for 40 s, and extension at 72 °C for 40 s.

qPCR result interpretation

The results analysis was based on the cycle threshold (Ct) values generated after the amplification of each sample. Samples and controls were considered positive when a characteristic sigmoid amplification curve was observed with a Ct value ≤ 35 cycles; reactions showing irregular amplification curves or Ct values > 35 cycles were interpreted as negative.

Data analysis

A descriptive analysis of detection frequencies was performed to evaluate the number of samples that tested positive for each virus. Subsequently, the results were stratified according to clinical and demographic variables, including age, symptom frequency, geographic distribution and patient clinical outcome (hospitalized or outpatient). Graphs were constructed that included both positive and negative samples, allowing for a description of the study population, identification of potential biases in sample distribution, and contextualization of the results within the total number of samples in the study cohort. Co-detection cases were also evaluated, considering patient age, symptoms, and clinical course, to describe the characteristics of these cases within the evaluated population.

Statistical analysis

Comparisons between hospitalized and outpatient groups were performed using the Pearson’s chi-square (χ²) test for categorical variables, including age group, geographical distribution, and the presence of clinical symptoms. For each comparison, contingency tables were constructed and the chi-square statistic was calculated to evaluate whether the observed distributions differed from those expected under the null hypothesis of independence. In cases where unequal counts were observed between comparison groups, proportional differences were adjusted by calculating percentages relative to the corresponding sample size of each group.

A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the analyses were conducted using standard statistical procedures.

Results

Epidemiological characteristics

Significant differences were observed in the age distribution between hospitalized and outpatient subjects (p = 1.66 × 10⁻⁸). Hospitalized patients were predominantly children under 5 years of age (70%), whereas outpatients were mostly adults > 18 years (63%). Although this association remained statistically significant, it should be interpreted with caution due to the marked imbalance in sample sizes between both groups (106 hospitalized vs. 19 outpatients), which may influence the observed proportions, particularly within the smaller outpatient category (Table 1).

Geographical distribution also differed significantly between the two groups (p = 5.37 × 10⁻¹²). Outpatients were more frequently from urban areas such as Panama Metro (37%) and Colón (26%), while hospitalized individuals were more widely distributed across provincial regions (e.g., Herrera, Coclé, Veraguas). These findings suggest that the likelihood of hospitalization could vary significantly depending on the geographical origin of the patient.

Clinical symptoms

Among the 81 samples with available symptom data, several symptoms showed statistically significant differences between hospitalized and outpatient cases. Cough was highly prevalent in both groups (88% vs. 92%), but its distribution was significantly associated with hospitalization status.

Hospitalized patients presented a higher frequency of severe or systemic symptoms, including fever > 38 °C (74%), breathing difficulties (72%), and rhinorrhea (59%), whereas outpatients more frequently reported symptoms uncommon in hospitalized cases, such as sore throat (46%), odynophagia, or dyspnea. These results indicate that hospitalized patients tended to exhibit more severe respiratory manifestations.

Molecular detection results

Detection of endemic coronaviruses by real-time nested multiplex RT-PCR (HCoV-229E, HCoV-OC43, HCoV-NL63)

The detection test results showed the presence of HCoV-229E and HCoV-OC43, while HCoV-NL63 was not detected in any of the samples. Of the 127 samples analyzed, 6 were positive, distributed as 3 positives for HCoV-229E and 3 for HCoV-OC43. This resulted in 95.28% (n = 121) of negative samples, which were not detected for either of these two endemic coronaviruses.

To describe the age distribution, geographic origin, and clinical outcomes of patients at the time of sample collection, demographic characteristics, provincial origin, and clinical status of the individuals included in the study were analyzed. The 127 analyzed samples were distributed across the following age groups: children under 5 years of age (n = 82), individuals aged 5–18 years (n = 9), and adults over 18 years of age (n = 36). Among the HCoV-229E positive samples, two were obtained from individuals older than 18 years and one from a child under 5 years of age. In contrast, among the HCoV-OC43 positive samples, two were detected in children under 5 years of age and one in an individual older than 18 years (Table 2).

Regarding geographic distribution, HCoV-229E–positive samples were identified in Colón (n = 1), Los Santos (n = 1), and Panama (n = 1) provinces. In contrast, HCoV-OC43–positive samples were distributed in Darién province (n = 1) and Panama province (n = 2) (Table 2).

With respect to clinical outcomes at the time of sample collection, of the 127 selected samples, two corresponded to deceased patients, 106 to hospitalized and recovered individuals, and 19 to recovered non-hospitalized (outpatient) individuals. Among the HCoV-229E–positive samples, two were obtained from hospitalized individuals and one from an outpatient. Conversely, among the HCoV-OC43–positive samples, two were detected in outpatients and one in a hospitalized individual (Table 2).

Detection of endemic coronavirus HKU1 by Real-time RT-PCR

None of the 127 processed samples tested positive for endemic coronavirus HKU1. Amplification was observed exclusively in the positive controls, whereas no amplification was detected in the negative controls. These results indicate adequate PCR performance and confirm the reliability of the negative findings obtained in the samples analyzed.

Viral Co-detections Identified in the Study Samples

Age-based analysis revealed that the majority of EV-D68–positive samples corresponded to children under 5 years of age (n = 9; 52.94%), followed by adults over 18 years of age (n = 5; 29.41%). The lowest number of detections was observed in individuals aged 5–18 years (n = 3; 17.65%) (Table 2).

Geographically, EV-D68–positive samples were distributed across six provinces and one indigenous region. Panama province reported the highest number of detections (n = 4), followed by the provinces of Colón and Coclé with three cases each. Two cases were identified in Herrera province, Panama Oeste province, and the Guna Yala indigenous region, respectively. In contrast, Darién province exhibited the lowest detection frequency, with a single EV-D68–positive case (Table 2).

Clinical manifestations associated with EV-D68 infection included eight different symptoms among the positive cases. The most frequently reported symptoms were cough (n = 13), respiratory distress (n = 11), fever > 38 °C (n = 11), and rhinorrhea (n = 9). Less common symptoms included sore throat (n = 3), dyspnea (n = 2), odynophagia (n = 1), and general malaise (n = 1). In comparison, EV-D68–negative samples presented five different symptoms, all at low frequency: chest pain (n = 1), thoracic pain (n = 2), vomiting (n = 3), diarrhea (n = 3), and limb edema (n = 1) (Table 2).

Regarding clinical outcomes at the time of sample collection, of the 127 analyzed samples, two corresponded to deceased patients, 106 to hospitalized individuals who subsequently recovered, and 19 to recovered non-hospitalized (outpatient) individuals. Among the EV-D68 positive samples, fifteen were obtained from hospitalized patients, while two were from outpatients (Table 2).

Detection of viral co-detections

Co-detections were evaluated based on the results obtained from the molecular detection assays and the respiratory viral panel from 2016, as shown in Supplementary Table 1. Based on these data, no co-detections were identified among the viruses detected in this study (HCoV-229E, HCoV-OC43, and EV-D68). However, co-detections between these viruses and other respiratory pathogens previously identified in the 2016 viral panel were observed.

A total of five samples showed viral co-detections, specifically involving HCoV-229E/Influenza A/H1pdm, HCoV-OC43/PIV-1, EV-D68/Rhinovirus, EV-D68/RSV, and EV-D68/PIV-3 (Table 3).

Co-detections were markedly more common in children under 5 years of age, accounting for four of the five co-infected samples (80%), whereas only one sample corresponded to a patient older than 18 years (20%).

Among the viruses evaluated, EV-D68 showed the highest frequency of co-detections, being involved in three of the five cases (60%), followed by HCoV-229E and HCoV-OC43, each detected in a single co-infected sample (20%).

Distinct clinical presentations were observed across different co-detection profiles. The co-detection involving EV-D68 and RSV was associated with fever, cough, and respiratory distress (Supplementary Table 3). In contrast, the co-detection between EV-D68 and Rhinovirus (RV) was characterized by cough, rhinorrhea, and respiratory difficulty (Supplementary Table 4). In the co-detection involving EV-D68 and PIV-3, patients presented with fever and dyspnea (Supplementary Table 5).

It is important to note that clinical manifestations associated with co-detections involving endemic human coronaviruses could not be characterized due to incomplete clinical data for those samples.

Discussion

This study provides one of the first molecular characterizations of the circulation of endemic human coronaviruses (HCoV-229E, HCoV-OC43, HCoV-NL63, and HCoV-HKU1) and EV-D68 in Panama, based on respiratory samples collected in 2016. The detection of these viruses, which were not included in the routinely used diagnostic panel in the country, suggests the possible existence of underdiagnosis of acute respiratory infections during the evaluated period. By integrating molecular, clinical, and epidemiological data, our findings contribute baseline evidence on the presence and potential clinical relevance of these pathogens in acute respiratory infections in Panama prior to the COVID-19 pandemic.

Main findings and viral circulation patterns

The detection of HCoV-229E and HCoV-OC43, together with the absence of HCoV-NL63 and HCoV-HKU1, is consistent with the known variability in the circulation of endemic coronaviruses across different years, populations, and geographic regions [30]. International surveillance studies have consistently shown that specific endemic coronaviruses may predominate in certain seasons while others circulate at very low levels or remain undetected, it is even reported that seasonality is more variable in tropical sites than in temperate sites [31, 32]. In our study, the absence of HCoV-NL63 and HCoV-HKU1 may reflect variability in viral circulation or limitations related to the study design, including the sample size and temporal coverage. Furthermore, studies in Panama have highlighted that the introduction and persistence of respiratory viruses depend closely on international connectivity and local climatic factors [33]. These findings highlight the importance of continued surveillance to better characterize the circulation of endemic coronaviruses in Panama.

In contrast, EV-D68 was detected in a higher proportion of samples (13.4%) compared to endemic coronaviruses (4.7%). This observation may reflect patterns reported globally since 2014, where EV-D68 has shown episodic increases in circulation, particularly in pediatric populations [34, 35].

The detection of EV-D68 in this study suggests its potential relevance in respiratory infections, even outside recognized outbreak periods [36]. This observation is consistent with reports from other regions describing its continued circulation following the increase in cases observed since 2014 [21].

Clinical characteristics and affected populations

A notable observation was that most EV-D68 and endemic coronavirus detections were identified in hospitalized patients. However, this pattern should be interpreted cautiously due to the imbalance in sample distribution between hospitalized and outpatient groups.

Infections caused by HCoV-229E have been associated with greater virulence compared with HCoV-OC43, as well as with an increased risk of mortality within the first 30 days following infection, as described by [37]. Consistent with this, in the present study, two of the three patients positive for HCoV-229E required hospitalization, whereas only one of the three HCoV-OC43–positive patients were hospitalized.

Regarding EV-D68, positive cases were mainly observed in children under five years of age, most of whom were hospitalized. This pattern should be interpreted with caution given the characteristics of the study population. Similar observations have been reported in other settings, including a study conducted in Barcelona, Spain, where EV-D68 was primarily detected in pediatric populations during outbreaks in 2014, 2016, and 2018 [38].

The immature immune system in children, compared with adults, may increase susceptibility to respiratory viral infections such as EV-D68 [39]. Additionally, young children experience greater exposure to respiratory viruses due to close contact in high-interaction indoor environments, such as daycare centers, kindergartens, and schools [40], facilitating the transmission of highly contagious pathogens. Although the small number of cases precludes robust statistical conclusions, these observations should be interpreted cautiously and considered descriptive.

However, the non-proportional distribution of samples between inpatient and outpatient settings likely resulted in an overrepresentation of severe cases. As the study population was derived from a pre-selected cohort within the national respiratory surveillance system, population-level incidence rates and hospitalization risks attributable to these viruses could not be reliably estimated.

More than 50% of positive cases occurred in children under 5 years of age, the majority of whom required hospitalization. This underscores the clinical impact of EV-D68 and HCoVs in vulnerable pediatric populations, aligning with global reports of higher morbidity in this age group [25, 41].

Despite the limited number of HCoV detections, some variation in age distribution was observed. Similar patterns have been reported in other settings, where different endemic coronaviruses show variability across age groups [42, 43]. However, the present data do not allow for definitive conclusions.

However, a clear explanation for the higher levels of HCoV-OC43 infection compared with HCoV-229E in children under five years of age is still lacking. Some studies have proposed the hypothesis that HCoV-OC43 may induce partial cross-immunity against more severe human coronaviruses through antibody neutralization [44], which could help explain the observed prevalence among age groups.

The greater impact observed in children may be partially explained by the immaturity of their immune system, which makes them more susceptible to acquiring viral respiratory infections. It is also important to consider that pediatric patients are more frequently captured by surveillance systems due to higher rates of medical consultation, whereas infections in adults often presenting with milder symptoms remain underreported [30]. This detection bias may result in an underestimation of the true burden of infection in older age groups.

Clinically, EV-D68 positive cases were most frequently associated with cough, fever (> 38 °C), and respiratory distress. These findings align with published literature describing fever and cough as common manifestations of mild disease, while respiratory distress is more often associated with severe clinical presentations [14, 15]. Importantly, no neurological manifestations, such as AFM, were observed among EV-D68 positive cases in this cohort, despite reports from other countries linking EV-D68 circulation to neurological complications [45, 46].

For endemic coronaviruses, clinical information was available for only one positive sample, which presented with fever, cough, sore throat, and rhinorrhea; symptoms commonly reported for these viruses [47]. Due to incomplete clinical data, meaningful comparisons of symptom patterns among coronavirus-positive cases were not possible, limiting the ability to identify differential clinical presentations or assess disease severity.

Geographic distribution

The analyzed samples originated from multiple provinces and indigenous regions (comarcas) across the country, suggesting geographic diversity within the study dataset. Although cases were identified across different regions, a higher proportion of samples originated from urban areas such as Panama Metro, Colón, and Panama Oeste, which may reflect the centralization of healthcare and diagnostic services.

This pattern has been described in other settings, where factors such as healthcare access and referral dynamics may influence hospitalization patterns [48, 49].

Notably, the inclusion of cases from indigenous comarcas and less populated provinces adds value to the dataset, as these areas are often underrepresented in molecular surveillance efforts. These findings highlight the importance of strengthening virological surveillance in diverse and underserved regions.

Regional context in Latin America

Available data on the circulation of endemic coronaviruses and EV-D68 in Latin America during 2016 are limited. Studies from Argentina and Nicaragua reported circulation of all four endemic coronaviruses during the same year, although with varying frequencies [50, 51], whereas in our study only HCoV-229E and HCoV-OC43 were identified. These differences, despite temporal overlap and geographic proximity, may reflect variations in study design, population characteristics, surveillance intensity, or local epidemiological and environmental factors.

Similarly, reports on EV-D68 circulation in the region during 2016 suggest a low overall incidence but confirm its presence. In Argentina, EV-D68 was detected in association with cases of acute flaccid myelitis in children, while in Chile only a single EV-D68 case was identified among nearly 2,000 respiratory samples analyzed through national surveillance [52, 53]. In contrast, the detection of 17 EV-D68 positive cases in a considerably smaller sample set in Panama suggests a higher level of circulation during that year, although methodological differences in surveillance strategies and case selection must be considered when interpreting these comparisons.

Co-detections and clinical impact

An important contribution of this study was the identification of five viral co-detections that had not been previously detected through routine diagnostic testing. These co-detections involved HCoV-229E, HCoV-OC43, and EV-D68 in combination with other viruses from the respiratory viral panel, demonstrating the simultaneous circulation of multiple respiratory pathogens within the Panamanian population.

Among EV-D68 co-detections, no evidence of increased clinical severity was observed compared with EV-D68 monoinfections. Clinical manifestations reflected overlapping symptoms of the co-detected viruses, without additional or more severe features. Although all EV-D68 co-detections occurred in hospitalized patients (primarily children under 5 years of age) this pattern was also observed among EV-D68 monoinfections, precluding conclusions regarding an additive effect of co-detection on disease severity. While some studies suggest that EVs co-detections may increase the risk of complications [54], our findings do not support this association within the limitations of the present cohort. The detection of EV-D68 co-detections consistent with previous reports describing EV-D68 co-detections with RSV [14, 55] and Rhinovirus [56].

For endemic coronavirus co-detections, clinical data were unavailable, limiting the assessment of their potential impact on disease severity. Nevertheless, the detection of respiratory syncytial virus and influenza A/H1pdm alongside endemic coronaviruses is consistent with previous reports identifying these viruses as common co detected pathogens [32]. Due to the lack of detailed clinical information, it was not possible to determine whether co-detection influenced clinical outcomes in these cases.

Public health implications and future directions

Overall, this study provides valuable baseline information on the circulation of endemic coronaviruses and EV-D68 in Panama prior to the COVID-19 pandemic and highlights important gaps in routine respiratory virus surveillance. The detection of these pathogens, including previously unrecognized co-detections, underscores the need to expand molecular diagnostic panels beyond the most targeted viruses.

Future studies should incorporate prospective designs with extended temporal coverage, quantitative viral load measurements, systematic and comprehensive clinical data collection, and genetic characterization of circulating strains. Strengthening molecular surveillance of these under-monitored respiratory viruses will be essential for improving early detection, understanding transmission dynamics, guiding clinical management, and informing public health responses to respiratory viral infections in Panama.

Limitations

While the findings of this study provide novel and relevant information on the circulation of endemic coronaviruses and EV-D68 in Panama, it is important to acknowledge the limitations inherent in its design and execution. Identifying these limitations allows for proper contextualization of the results, prevents interpretations that exceed the actual scope of the analysis, and guides future research to further investigate the clinical and epidemiological impact of these respiratory viruses.

The low number of positive cases for endemic coronaviruses represents a limitation of this study, as it restricts the ability to perform statistically robust analyses or to establish meaningful associations with clinical and epidemiological variables. Accordingly, the findings should be considered descriptive and exploratory, as the primary aim was to document the presence of these viruses in samples previously reported as negative, rather than to assess associations.

The sample size and representativeness constitute a limitation, as the study included only 127 respiratory samples. This allowed for an initial description of the circulation of HCoV-229E, HCoV-OC43, and EV-D68, but may not accurately reflect the epidemiological situation of the entire Panamanian population or the true burden of respiratory illness during 2016. Furthermore, the retrospective design and the limited timeframe of a single calendar year prevent the assessment of seasonal or interannual circulation patterns, as well as their possible association with epidemic peaks observed in other periods.

Additionally, the lack of sequencing data constitutes another limitation, as it prevented phylogenetic characterization of the detected viruses. Future studies incorporating genomic approaches will be important to better understand the genetic diversity and transmission dynamics of these pathogens in the region.

Another relevant limitation was the incomplete availability of clinical information for all analyzed samples, which restricted the analysis of possible correlations between viral detection and disease severity or clinical outcomes in patients. Additionally, the methodology used was qualitative, based on the presence or absence of the virus, without including viral load quantification. This made it impossible to explore associations between levels of viral replication and more severe clinical manifestations.

Finally, although co-detections with other respiratory viruses were identified, the small size of these subgroups and the heterogeneity of the available clinical data limited a robust assessment of their impact on the clinical presentation and evolution of the cases. Taken together, these limitations should be considered when interpreting the results and highlight the need for prospective studies with broader temporal coverage, larger sample sizes, and more comprehensive clinical and virological data.

Conclusions

This study documents the detection of two endemic coronaviruses and Enterovirus D68 in Panama during 2016, providing one of the first molecular descriptions of these viruses in the country for that period. These findings demonstrate the presence of these viruses in respiratory samples, both as single detections and in co-detections with other viruses included in the routine diagnostic panel.

Therefore, it is crucial to expand our knowledge of the epidemiology of respiratory viruses that are not currently part of routine surveillance systems but that may be contributing significantly to the burden of disease in the population. This would reduce the number of patients who remain without a defined etiological diagnosis, improve our understanding of the true impact of these infections, and facilitate more appropriate clinical management. In this context, more sensitive and accurate diagnostic methods are essential to promptly identify these pathogens, optimize clinical management, and generate evidence that strengthens surveillance strategies and public health decision-making.

The identification of viral co-detections highlights the need for further studies to explore their potential clinical and epidemiological relevance.

Overall, broader and more comprehensive studies incorporating larger sample sizes and advanced molecular approaches will be important to better characterize the epidemiology and genetic diversity of circulating respiratory viruses in Panama.
