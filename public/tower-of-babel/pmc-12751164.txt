Prealbumin predicts metachronous liver metastasis in colorectal cancer
Authors: Han-bing Xie, Bei-bei Zhang, Lin Zhao, Le Liu, Ping-ping Liu, Xin Zhang, Shi-di Miao, Rui-tao Wang, Hai-tao Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPredicting metachronous liver metastasis (MLM) in colorectal cancer (CRC) is crucial in clinical practice. Studies have shown that prealbumin is linked to recurrence and poor overall survival in various malignancies. This study aims to determine whether prealbumin levels can predict postoperative MLM in CRC patients.MethodsThis study included both a primary cohort and an external validation cohort, recruiting a total of 627 CRC patients from two independent hospitals in China. Clinical characteristics and laboratory measurements were collected prior to surgery. The optimal cut-off value for prealbumin in predicting MLM was determined using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The Kaplan-Meier method was employed to estimate the 36-month incidence of MLM. The association between prealbumin levels and the occurrence of MLM was evaluated using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard analyses.ResultsPostoperative MLM developed in 85 of the 627 patients who underwent surgery. Those who developed MLM had lower prealbumin levels compared to patients without MLM. Kaplan-Meier analysis indicated that patients with reduced prealbumin levels had a higher likelihood of developing MLM. Multivariate Cox regression analysis in both the primary and external validation cohorts confirmed that prealbumin is an independent predictor of MLM.ConclusionsPrealbumin is an independent predictor for MLM in patients with CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15296-1.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most prevalent malignancy worldwide. The liver is the most frequent site of metastasis in patients with CRC. 18–25% of patients will develop metachronous liver metastases (MLM) even after curative surgeries for the primary tumor [1]. MLM presents significant clinical challenges due to high local recurrence rates and low survival rates [2]. Therefore, it is essential to identify new predictive factors for MLM following surgery.

Prealbumin is an acute-phase protein predominantly produced by the liver. In cases of protein-energy malnutrition, prealbumin levels can reliably indicate adaptive changes in hepatic protein synthesis [3]. Recent studies demonstrated that prealbumin is associated with the severity of initial illness, infectious complications, and mortality in critically ill patients [4–7]. Multiple studies across various malignancies—including esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [8], gastric cancer [9], hepatocellular carcinoma [10], and non-small cell lung cancer [11] have demonstrated that low prealbumin levels independently predict tumor recurrence and poor overall survival. Recent studies revealed that prealbumin is a critical biomarker for nutritional and inflammatory status, with low levels or elevated composite ratios correlating with adverse surgical outcomes, reduced survival, and diminished chemotherapy efficacy in CRC patients [12–16]. However, the specific association with MLM in CRC‌ remains underexplored.

Therefore, this study’s objective is to examine whether prealbumin can predict MLM in patients with CRC.

Materials and methods

Patient characteristics

We performed a study in patients who had curative resection for CRC from January 2019 to December 2020. A total of 627 CRC patients from two independent hospitals in China were recruited. 412 patients from Center 1 (Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital) were collected as the primary cohort. 206 patients from Center 2 (the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University) were collected as the external validation cohort. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histopathologically confirmed CRC; (2) patients underwent radical resection of CRC; (3) no metastatic disease at diagnosis. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) there was preoperative anti-tumor therapy; (2) recurrence in or distant metastasis to another organ other than the liver after less than 3 years after surgery; and (3) unavailable clinicopathologic or follow-up data. All the patients were staged according to AJCC Cancer Staging Manual, 8th edition. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria and enrollment process are illustrated in Appendix Fig. S1.

The clinical information included characteristics such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoker, drinker, hypertension, diabetes, fatty liver, hepatitis B virus (hepatitis B), tumor location, histological type, differentiation, tumor stage (T stage), lymph node metastasis, lymphatic vascular infiltration, perineural invasion, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), and adjuvant chemotherapy. Adjuvant chemotherapy was administered based on NCCN/ESMO guidelines. Adjuvant treatment included FOLFOX, or CAPEOX, or capecitabine alone.

After a 12-hour overnight fast, the patient’s blood samples were collected and albumin and pre-albumin levels were measured (Automatic Biochemical Analyzer, Roche Cobas 8000). Analytic coefficients of variation were < 5%. The normal range of prealbumin levels in this study is 200–400 mg/L.

A contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis was performed every 3 months after the operation. If abnormal nodules in the liver were found, the diagnosis of MLM was confirmed by gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI. If necessary, a biopsy was performed for pathological diagnosis. Early recurrence (≤ 6 months) often reflects occult micrometastases at surgery, whereas delayed metastases (>6 months) are more likely driven by de novo molecular alterations or residual tumor cell plasticity [17]. Therefore, the 6-month threshold for defining MLM was selected in this study‌. The patients were followed up every 3 months in the first 2 years after surgery and then semi-annually during the third year. The follow-up period was terminated on December 31, 2023.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital. The need for informed patient consent was obtained.

Statistical analysis

MedCalc version 15.0 and SPSS version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) were used to perform statistical analysis. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations (normal distribution) or medians and interquartile ranges (non-normal distribution) and compared between groups using Student t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. Chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, was performed for the categorical variables. The best cut-off value for prealbumin to predict MLM was determined based on the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. MLM occurrence was compared using Kaplan-Meier curves. Log-rank test was used to determine statistical differences between curves. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed by Cox proportional hazards regression models to determine the hazard ratio of each factor. Variables that showed a significant univariate relationship with outcome (P < 0.10) were entered into the multivariate analysis. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 661 patients with CRC undergoing curative resection were enrolled in this study. After excluding 34 patients due to preoperative anti-tumor therapy, recurrence/distant metastasis to extrahepatic organs within 3 years post-surgery, or missing clinicopathological/follow-up data, we ultimately evaluated 627 CRC patients. Further information was described in Supplementary Fig. S1. After more than three years of follow-up, the number of people who developed MLM in primary cohort and validation cohort was 52 and 33, respectively. In the primary cohort, 58.9% were male, and the mean age was 56.9 ± 10.0 years. We divided the patients into two groups according to the presence of MLM. The baseline clinicopathological characteristics are presented in Table 1. The MLM group exhibited a significantly higher incidence of male, advanced tumor stage, positive lymph node stage, and lymphatic vascular infiltration. Moreover, the MLM group had a higher BMI and lower prealbumin levels compared to the non-MLM group (Fig. 1A). However, other variables showed no difference between the two groups. In the external validation cohort, a similar change was found (Table 2). Noticeably, prealbumin levels were also lower in the MLM group compared to those in the non-MLM group in the external validation cohort (Fig. 1B). In addition, patients with stage III CRC exhibited significantly lower prealbumin levels compared to stage I/II (Fig. S2).

Survival analyses evaluating 6-month (primary: HR = 0.993, 95%CI 0.989–0.997, p = 0.001) and 12-month (conservative: HR = 0.993, 95%CI 0.989–0.998, p = 0.005) MLM diagnostic thresholds consistently demonstrated prealbumin’s predictive value, with both timeframes maintaining statistical significance after multivariable adjustment.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was carried out to examine the predictive ability of prealbumin for MLM. The optimal cutoff value of prealbumin was 304 [AUC = 0.614, 95%CI:0.566–0.661, P = 0.001], with a corresponding sensitivity of 0.904 and specificity of 0.325 (Fig. S3A). The patients were divided into two groups based on the optimal cut-off value: high prealbumin (> 304 mg/L) and low prealbumin (≤ 304 mg/L). After a follow-up of 36 months, 52 (12.4%) patients developed MLM. Patients with low prealbumin were more likely to develop MLM than those with high prealbumin (15.9% vs. 4.0%, respectively; p = 0.001) (Fig. 2A). In the external validation cohort, the optimal cutoff value of prealbumin was 212 [AUC = 0.623, 95%CI:0.553–0.689, P = 0.011], with a corresponding sensitivity of 0.818 and specificity of 0.480 (Fig. S3B). Kaplan-Meier curves showed that the low-prealbumin group presented with a higher incidence of MLM (Fig. 2B).

Discussion

The findings of this study showed that prealbumin is an independent predictive factor for postoperative MLM in patients with CRC. These results remind clinicians to pay close attention to prealbumin in CRC patients before surgery. Patients with low-prealbumin require close attention in their postoperative clinical course, especially with regard to MLM.

‌Crucially, our analyses demonstrated robust consistency in prealbumin’s predictive value across clinically relevant MLM diagnostic thresholds (>6 months: HR = 0.993, p = 0.001; >12 months: HR = 0.993, p = 0.005). This temporal stability implies that prealbumin captures early biological processes in the metastatic cascade—likely reflecting the establishment of liver-tropic micrometastatic niches and inflammation-driven microenvironmental changes during the perioperative window. The persistence of significance regardless of diagnostic timing suggests that nutritional-inflammatory dysregulation, as measured by prealbumin, initiates metastasis-favoring conditions before clinical detection. The inverse relationship between prealbumin levels and inflammatory markers was consistently observed across multiple cancer types. In periampullary disease patients, preoperative serum c-reactive protein (CRP) levels demonstrated a strong negative correlation with prealbumin (R=−0.682, P < 0.01), a finding that was replicated in gastric cancer cohorts where similar significant correlations were documented [18, 19]. Similar patterns were documented in advanced-stage non-small cell lung cancer and CRC populations [20, 21]. Sex-specific analyses revealed that female cachexic patients had significantly elevated interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels accompanied by markedly reduced prealbumin concentrations (p < 0.05) compared to their non-cachexic counterparts [22]. These findings suggest a systemic mechanism in advanced malignancies where inflammatory markers such as CRP and IL-6 contribute to the depletion of this acute-phase negative protein [23].

The underlying mechanism for this association between prealbumin and MLM is unclear. Firstly, malnutrition is associated with worse postoperative outcomes and reduced survival [24]. The half-life of prealbumin is shorter than that of albumin, so it sensitively reflects a patient’s nutritional status and rapidly responds to a change in nutritional status. Previous studies demonstrated that prealbumin is a useful and independent biomarker for predicting the occurrence of postoperative complications in patients with esophageal cancer, gastric cancer, and CRC [8, 25, 26]. Secondly, as a negative acute-phase protein, the synthesis of prealbumin is suppressed in the inflammatory state. Steatohepatitis is an independent risk factor for post-operative morbidity following liver resection for CRC MLM [27]. Moreover, chronic inflammation is involved in many stages of MLM [28]. Thirdly, low prealbumin levels can damage the immune system and inhibit cell-mediated immune function, leading to increased metastasis [29, 30]. Recent studies revealed that prealbumin is a predictor of recurrence, metastasis, and overall survival in various cancers, such as non-small cell lung cancer, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, gastric cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma [8, 9, 31, 32].

Our results are of great importance in clinical practice. To our knowledge, no study has investigated the relationship between prealbumin and MLM in CRC patients. Prealbumin, a sensitive biomarker of nutritional status and hepatic synthetic function, can be modulated through targeted interventions. Firstly, optimizing dietary protein intake and micronutrients may enhance prealbumin synthesis, as prealbumin levels correlate with hepatic reserve and nutrient availability [33]. Secondly, addressing underlying conditions such as liver dysfunction or chronic inflammation could improve prealbumin synthesis by restoring hepatic metabolic homeostasis. Thirdly, perioperative nutritional support has been shown to mitigate catabolic states, potentially elevating prealbumin levels and reducing metastasis risk [34].

Prealbumin has a short half-life (~ 2 days) compared to albumin (~ 3 weeks), making it a sensitive marker for acute changes in nutritional status and hepatic synthetic function during the perioperative period. CRC-induced inflammation suppresses prealbumin synthesis, exacerbating malnutrition even in patients with normal albumin levels [35, 36]. Furthermore, prealbumin levels predicted post-operative complications, whereas albumin remained stable due to its longer half-life [37, 38]. In addition, albumin synthesis is suppressed by chronic inflammation, but its long half-life delays this response. Prealbumin’s faster decline better correlates with acute inflammatory states linked to metastasis-favoring microenvironments [12, 39]. Thus, prealbumin’s predictive value extends beyond conventional nutritional status and reflects tumor-specific mechanisms. Its short half-life allows for the early detection of microenvironmental changes, potentially guiding timely clinical interventions.

Prealbumin levels inversely correlate with CRC progression, particularly in advanced TNM stages. In our cohort, patients with stage III CRC exhibited significantly lower prealbumin levels compared to stage I/II. This aligns with prealbumin’s role as a marker of hepatic synthetic capacity, which is compromised in advanced CRC due to systemic inflammation and tumor burden. CRC-derived cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β) impair intestinal barrier integrity, increasing endotoxin translocation and further suppressing hepatic prealbumin production [40, 41]. Moreover, higher TNM stages are associated with increased catabolic demand and malnutrition, exacerbating prealbumin depletion [42–44].

Possible reasons for institutional disparities in prealbumin levels were as follows: Firstly, differences in baseline nutritional status, comorbidities, or tumor burden across institutions could influence prealbumin levels [45]. Secondly, discrepancies in prealbumin measurement protocols between institutions may contribute to assay variability [45]. Thirdly, regional differences in dietary habits and socioeconomic status could indirectly modulate prealbumin levels. These factors should be prospectively evaluated in multi-center studies with standardized protocols to harmonize prealbumin measurements and contextualize its prognostic utility.

The lack of significant differences in postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy (POAC) administration between MLM and non-MLM groups may reflect underlying biological heterogeneity [46]. Additionally, tumor metabolic reprogramming may enable CRC cells to evade chemotherapy-induced apoptosis [47, 48]. Such organ-specific resistance mechanisms could explain why adjuvant chemotherapy fails to prevent MLM in some patients. Standard adjuvant regimens (e.g., FOLFOX) primarily target micrometastases but may inadequately address dormant cells with liver-specific metastatic traits [49]. Studies suggest that liver parenchymal endothelial cells promote CRC cell survival and chemoresistance via activating HER3-AKT pathway [50]. This organotropic “drug sanctuary” effect might attenuate chemotherapy’s preventive impact on MLM. The administration of POAC in our cohorts was lower than rates reported in guideline-driven trials, primarily due to the inclusion of early-stage patients and real-world factors such as patient refusal or comorbidities. While our analyses adjusted for POAC status and subgroup results remained consistent, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Future studies should evaluate prealbumin’s predictive role in cohorts with standardized POAC regimens to further validate our findings. Prealbumin could modulate chemotherapy pharmacokinetics. For instance, hypo-prealbuminemia may impair hepatic drug detoxification or alter protein-drug binding, reducing therapeutic efficacy despite standardized regimens [51]. Our study highlights prealbumin as a complementary biomarker to guide personalized therapy. For patients with low prealbumin, nutritional optimization or metabolic-targeted agents could enhance chemotherapy efficacy.

While an AUC of 0.614 may appear not high, it reflects prealbumin’s role as a single-marker predictor focused on hepatic microenvironment vulnerability rather than tumor biology alone. Integrating prealbumin into existing predictive models enhanced performance. In the primary cohort, prealbumin improved the predictive accuracy of the model (AUC increase from 0.614 to 0.728, p = 0.0096) when added as a fourth parameter. This information emphasizes prealbumin’s role as a complementary biomarker requiring integration into multifactorial models for optimal MLM prediction.

Limitations existed in this study. First, the retrospective design may introduce selection bias, requiring validation in prospective studies. Second, the optimal timing for defining MLM remains unclear, necessitating future threshold comparisons. Third, unmeasured inflammatory markers (e.g., IL-6, CRP) and metabolic factors (e.g., retinol) limit mechanistic insights into prealbumin’s predictive role; tumor-driven inflammation (e.g., IL-6 suppression of prealbumin synthesis) [52] or nutrient dysregulation (e.g., retinol deficiency) [53] could confound its predictive utility. Fourth, residual confounding by inflammation or comorbidities cannot be fully excluded. Finally, external validation of the prealbumin cut-off in diverse populations is needed to confirm generalizability.

In conclusion, prealbumin is an independent predictive factor for postoperative MLM in CRC patients. Our study suggested that prealbumin provides additional predictive information on the incidence of MLM in CRC. Further studies are needed.
