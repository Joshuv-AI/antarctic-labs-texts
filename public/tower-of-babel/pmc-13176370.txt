Evaluation of regularization factor and attenuation correction method of Q.Clear reconstruction for brain PET/MR in epileptic patients
Authors: Xiaolong Niu, Jieqin Lv, Hao Sun, Zhifeng Chen, Xiang Liang, Zanting Ye, Youliang Wu, Xiaojing Zhang, Lijun Lu, Pengcheng Ran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo identify suitable regularization factor β and attenuation correction (AC) method for Q.Clear reconstruction in brain [18F]FDG PET/MR imaging of patients with epilepsy.MethodsWe retrospectively collected 27 PET/MR brain images from patients with temporal lobe epilepsy. PET images were reconstructed using both ordered subset-expectation maximization and Q.Clear reconstruction (β from 50 to 600) with three AC methods: Atlas-based AC (Atlas-AC), Partial Head-based AC (PH-AC), and Zero Echo Time-based AC (ZTE-AC). Image quality was assessed using lesion-to-background (LBR) ratio, normalized standard deviation (NSD) ratio, SUVmax, and SUVmean.ResultsQ.Clear reconstruction with β values of 50 to 600 significantly increased LBR and decreased NSD compared to OSEM. β value of 300 served as a practical operating point for the three AC methods. ZTE-AC had the largest LBR and lower NSD values, PH-AC method had the lowest NSD and relatively high LBR, while Atlas-AC showed moderate performance. Comparisons of SUVmax and SUVmean between Q.Clear (β = 300) and OSEM across all AC methods suggest that β = 300 improves lesion contrast and reduces image noise without compromising SUV value.ConclusionsQ.Clear reconstruction can improve lesion contrast and reduce noise compared to OSEM. ZTE-AC yielded the highest lesion contrast and LBR among three AC methods, PH-AC resulted in the lowest background noise, and Atlas-AC showed balanced but suboptimal performance. These findings offer practical guidance in selecting the suitable β value and AC method for optimized brain PET/MR imaging in patients with epilepsy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40658-026-00860-w.

Introduction

Epilepsy is one of the most common serious disorders of the central nervous system, affecting over 70 million people worldwide [1]. For most epilepsy patients, antiepileptic drugs are effective in controlling seizures. However, approximately one-third of individuals with epilepsy are drug-resistant, exhibiting recurrent seizures despite treatment with multiple antiepileptic medications [2]. For these patients, surgical intervention is considered the most effective approach to achieving long-term seizure freedom. Among drug-resistant cases, temporal lobe epilepsy is the most prevalent subtype [3]. Accurate diagnosis of temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is essential, as it enables better surgical outcomes [4]. Positron emission tomography (PET) imaging has been widely used for detecting and localizing epileptic foci. Its strength lies in its ability to provide accurate and quantitative assessments of molecular activity at early stages [5]. Moreover, simultaneous PET/magnetic resonance (MR) imaging offers high-resolution structural and functional information noninvasively, enhancing the identification of epileptic foci and facilitating surgical planning [6].

The quality of PET image is affected by several factors, such as spatial resolution, reconstruction algorithm, attenuation correction, scatter and random correction, and so on [7]. Among these, the image reconstruction algorithm plays a crucial role in balancing spatial resolution and noise [8]. The number of iterations is a key parameter in ordered-subset expectation maximization (OSEM) reconstruction, and excessive iteration may result in increased image noise [9]. Recently, the bayesian penalized likelihood reconstruction algorithm, named Q.Clear, has been clinically implemented in PET/MR [10]. Q.Clear reconstruction has been reported to improve the contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) and standardized uptake values (SUV), and to provide improved visual quality compared to OSEM [11]. Additionally, it reduces noise in both full-time and half-time PET scans while preserving image detail [12]. Wagatsuma et al. [13] investigated the practical regularization factor for Q.Clear reconstruction in brain PET image using 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]FDG) and 11C-Pittsburgh Compound B ([11C]PiB). Their results demonstrated that Q.Clear outperformed OSEM in image contrast and noise suppression while maintaining consistent standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) through better convergence, stricter noise regulation, and edge preservation. The suitable β values identified for [18F]FDG and [11C]PiB brain PET were 200 and 450, respectively. Tian et al. [14] evaluated the impact of Q.Clear on the [18F]FDG PET/MR system and determined the practical penalization factor β for the NEMA NU 2–2012 phantom. However, the suitable β value for Q.Clear reconstruction in [18F]FDG PET imaging of patients with epilepsy remains to be established.

Another crucial factor of PET reconstruction is attenuation correction (AC). The inherent attenuation effect of photons passing through tissues (especially bone and trunk) will cause low contrast and serious artifacts, thereby interfering with accurate clinical diagnosis [15]. To mitigate these effects, AC is an essential component of the PET reconstruction process. However, precise AC remains a challenge in PET/MR systems, as MR images cannot be directly converted into 511-keV photon attenuation maps [16, 17]. In clinical brain PET/MR imaging (GE Healthcare), three AC methods are commonly employed: Atlas-based AC (Atlas-AC), Partial Head-based AC (PH-AC), and Zero Echo Time-based AC (ZTE-AC). In the Atlas-AC method, the target MR image is co-registered with an MR/CT atlas, and a pseudo-CT image is synthesized from the registered pair [18]. However, this process can introduce registration errors between the PET and MR images, potentially degrading the attenuation map quality. To address this, the GE SIGNA PET/MR scanner integrates the head atlas with Dixon in-phase MR images to generate a µ-map by combining the registered atlas CT with the MR-derived head contour. This improved pipeline reduces registration error to less than 8% across brain regions [19]. The PH-AC method utilizes MR Dixon imaging, which segments the whole body into four tissue classes (air, fat, soft tissue, and lung), excluding the bone component [20]. Each tissue class is assigned a specific linear attenuation coefficient to generate the attenuation map in PH-AC. The entire brain is treated as soft tissue, and the skull is not accounted for in this model [21]. The ZTE-AC method uses a zero echo time sequence with an extremely short echo time (TE), which enables the acquisition of bone signal. The resulting intensity map is segmented into three tissue classes (air, bone, and soft tissue) by applying histogram-based thresholding to the normalized ZTE signal. A pseudo-CT is then generated by assigning each segmented class a predefined attenuation coefficient [16, 19].

Each of these AC methods is based on distinct principles and exhibits differing effects on PET image reconstruction. Therefore, selecting the most suitable AC method is critical for satisfying clinical requirements, particularly when using Q.Clear reconstruction in patients with temporal lobe epilepsy. Sousa et al. [22] evaluated Atlas-AC and ZTE-AC by comparison to 68Ge-AC in brain PET/MR. Their results indicated that SUV values derived from ZTE-AC exhibited less variability than those obtained with Atlas-AC. Similarly, the use of ZTE-AC yielded lower bias in SUVR calculations compared to Atlas-AC.

To the best knowledge of our study, there is no work simultaneously considering both the suitable β value and AC methods for brain PET reconstruction. In this study, we investigated different cross-combinations of nine β values and three AC methods for brain PET reconstruction in patients with epilepsy. The reconstructed images were evaluated using both qualitative and quantitative metrics in regions corresponding to epileptic foci. These included the lesion-to-background ratio (LBR), normalized standard deviation (NSD), SUVmax, and SUVmean. Comparative statistical analysis was conducted to evaluate the performance of various combinations of β values and AC methods.

Materials and methods

Patient

We retrospectively collected 27 patients with TLE from the Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, based on the following inclusion criteria: (a) Patients were diagnosed with temporal lobe epilepsy according to the 2017 Classification of the Epilepsies proposed by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE). (b) PET/MR images were acquired in the interictal period of seizures. (c) Patients without other neurological disorders, psychiatric disorders or serious systemic disease. Metabolic abnormalities restricted to the temporal lobe were observed in all patients. Structural lesions were identified in 17 of the 27 cases (63%), including hippocampal sclerosis (HS), mild malformations of cortical development (mMCD), focal cortical dysplasia (FCD), and cortical atrophy. This study was approved by the Research Review Board and the need for informed consent was waived.

PET/MR imaging protocol

Patients were injected with 2.96–3.70 MBq/kg of [18F]FDG and rested in a dark room for 65 ± 15 min. PET/MR examinations were conducted on the SIGNA 3 T PET/MR system (GE Healthcare), with a single-bed PET scan duration of 15 min. Three MR sequences were simultaneously acquired during the PET scan, including MRAC (Magnetic Resonance Attenuation Correction), ZTE, and a 3D inversion recovery prepared fast spoiled gradient recalled T1 weighted sequence named as T1 BRAVO.

The MRAC sequence was based on the Dixon technique and acquired with the following parameters: 5° flip angle, 4.048 ms repetition time (TR), 0.7 number of excitations (NEX), 500 × 380 mm² field of view (FOV), 5.2 mm slice thickness, 2.0 × 3.9 mm² pixel size, a 256 × 128 matrix size, 2.232 ms and 1.116 ms echo time (TE). In-phase and out-of-phase images were used to generate water and fat images. Based on this segmentation, four tissue types were identified: air, fat, soft tissue, and lung. In the Atlas-AC method, the in-phase image was registered with a CT template that contain the attenuation information of the head.

For the PH-AC method, the MRAC sequence provided anatomical information. The brain and skull were both assumed to be soft tissue and assigned a uniform linear attenuation coefficient, as defined by the manufacturer. The ZTE sequence was acquired with the following parameters: 0.8° flip angle, 399.6 ms TR, 0 ms TE, 4 NEX, 260 × 260 mm² FOV, 2.4 mm slice thickness, 2.4 × 2.4 mm² pixel size, and a 110 × 110 matrix size. The ZTE acquisition enables the capture of signal from bone tissue, which is not visible in standard MR sequences. Tissue segmentation (air, soft tissue, and bone) was performed by applying threshold values to the scaled intensity histogram. Each segmented class was then assigned a specific attenuation coefficient to generate a pseudo-CT, which was subsequently used for photon attenuation correction in PET reconstruction. Lastly, the 3D T1 BRAVO sequence was performed with the following parameters: 8° flip angle, 7.5 ms TR, 2.8 ms TE, 600 ms inversion time (TI), 1 NEX, 240 × 240 mm² FOV, 1 mm slice thickness, 0.9 × 0.9 mm² pixel size, and a 256 × 256 matrix size. This sequence was mainly intended to provide detailed structural information for anatomical reference.

PET image reconstruction

Two methods were employed to reconstruct PET images from the list-mode dataset. The first was OSEM integrating time of flight (TOF) and point spread function (PSF) with 6 iterations and 28 subsets (referred to hereinafter as OSEM). The second method was Q.Clear, also combined with TOF and PSF (referred to hereinafter as Q.Clear), with penalization factor β values of 50, 75, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600. We evaluated the performance of Q.Clear using each β value across the three AC methods. A total of 756 images (27 patients×27 combination PET images + 27 MRI images) were acquired simultaneously during interictal or resting states. Atlas-AC map, PH-AC map and ZTE-AC map were generated automatically using the default manufacturer-provided settings. OSEM and Q.Clear (across the eight β values) were each combined with the three AC maps to reconstruct the PET images for each patient, yielding 27 reconstructions per patient. Quantitative analysis

We extracted temporal lobe and cerebellar regions using the Hammers Atlas (www.brain-development.org). Lindström E et al. [23] demonstrated that the choice of reference region can significantly affect outcome measures, with notable differences observed when comparing cerebellar gray matter, pons, whole brain, and whole cerebellum. Based on these findings, the whole cerebellum was selected as the reference region for [18F]FDG quantification in this study, while the pons and whole brain were excluded due to their reported variability. The Hammers Atlas is based on 83 entirely manually delineated regions (“r83”) drawn on MR images of 30 healthy adult subjects (“n30”) in native space prior to spatial normalization to MNI space [24]. All images underwent pre-processing prior to segmentation. First, skulls were stripped from the 3D T1 BRAVO MRI images using Freesurfer software (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) [25], then registering the skull-stripped 3D T1 BRAVO MRI images to the MNI space of the Hammers Atlas. PET images were rigidly registered to the processed MR images using the Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs, version 2.4.3), with all other parameters kept at their default settings. All registered PET-MR pairs were visually inspected to ensure spatial alignment accuracy and absence of misregistration artifacts. Finally extracting the temporal lobe (The temporal lobe can be subdivided into 10 brain structures) and cerebellar mask from the Hammers Atlas and multiplying the masks with registered PET images to obtain the temporal lobe and cerebellum of the patient (Fig. 1).

Quantitative analyses were based on SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVsd, LBR, and NSD in brain epileptic foci. LBR reflects the ability to recognize the lesion, with a larger value indicating superior performance. In comparison, NSD reflects noise robustness, with lower values indicating reduced noise influence. The LBR is defined as follows:

We calculated these two metrics for each PET/MR data.

Voxel-based statistical analysis

Following completion of the registration steps, an exploratory voxel-wise statistical analysis was conducted using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12; Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, UCL, UK). All PET images were spatially normalized to the MNI template and smoothed using an 8 mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel to account for inter-subject variability. Voxel-wise two-sample t-tests for different reconstruction and AC comparisons were computed, with p values uncorrected for multiple comparisons. This analysis provided as complementary evidence to visualize the spatial distribution of potential differences that may not be captured by regional metrics.

Statistical analysis

We compared the above metrics of two reconstruction methods with three AC methods using the statistical testing tool SPSS (version 25, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. When comparing metrics across different β values and attenuation correction methods, we applied one-way ANOVA, followed by either LSD or Bonferroni correction based on data normality and variance homogeneity. For data not meeting parametric assumptions, non-parametric tests (Kruskal-Wallis or Mann-Whitney U) were used. In addition to p-value, effect sizes (η²) were calculated and reported for each main comparison to estimate the magnitude of group differences, regardless of statistical significance.

Results

Figure 2 presents the LBR–NSD relationship curves for all patients using OSEM reconstruction (equivalent to Q.Clear with β = 0) and Q.Clear reconstruction with β values of 50, 75, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600, each combined with three attenuation correction methods: Atlas-AC, PH-AC, and ZTE-AC. To balance PET image contrast (i.e., higher lesion-to-background ratio, LBR) and noise suppression (i.e., lower normalized standard deviation, NSD), the ideal point on the plotted curve is located near the bottom right corner of Fig. 2. This point represents a practical multi-objective trade-off between contrast enhancement and noise reduction. The suitable β value for the three AC methods was all 300. Among the AC strategies, ZTE-AC had the largest LBR and lower NSD values; PH-AC achieved relatively higher LBR with the lowest NSD, whereas Atlas-AC obtained lower LBR and higher NSD. As β increased, the LBR initially rose and then declined, while the NSD decreased and subsequently increased (Fig. 3). Compared with OSEM, Q.Clear with the β value range from 75 to 600 had larger LBR and smaller NSD.

Tables 2 and 3 show the LBR and NSD metrics for the three AC methods recorded from the lesion and background regions across all patients. The NSD values for all AC methods followed a normal distribution (p > 0.05), and the variances were homogeneous. No statistically significant differences in NSD were observed across different β values for three AC methods (Atlas-AC: p = 0.751, PH-AC: p = 0.301, ZTE-AC: p = 0.502). In contrast, the LBR values did not follow a normal distribution (p < 0.05). Using the Kruskal-Wallis non-parametric test, no significant differences were found in LBR across β values for each AC method (Atlas-AC: p = 0.493; PH-AC: p = 0.287; ZTE-AC: p = 0.24). Among the three AC methods, PH-AC yielded the lowest mean NSD (Atlas-AC: 0.275; PH-AC: 0.266; ZTE-AC: 0.279), while ZTE-AC produced the highest mean LBR (Atlas-AC: 1.24; PH-AC: 1.25; ZTE-AC: 1.26). Compared with OSEM reconstruction, NSD values for Atlas-AC and ZTE-AC were lower with Q.Clear reconstructions using β values from 75 to 600. The lowest LBR was observed for PH-AC at β = 0, with LBR increasing progressively as β increased. The lowest NSD for all three AC methods occurred at β = 300, suggesting that Q.Clear is effective in reducing image noise.

We also conducted a semi-quantitative evaluation of SUVmax and SUVmean study to compare the differences between Q.Clear and OSEM reconstruction. A decreasing trend in both SUVmax and SUVmean was observed as β increased from 50 to 600 (Fig. 5), consistent with the principles of Q.Clear, which adds a penalization term βR(x) to the objective function in conventional OSEM reconstruction [8]. The SUVmax values of Q.Clear are larger than those of OSEM reconstruction (β = 0) when β values are 50, 75, and 100. At β = 300, the SUVmax and SUVmean are closest to those of the OSEM reconstruction (Refer to Tables 4 and 5 for details). No significant differences were found in SUVmean across the three AC methods under the same β value (Table 5, Atlas: p = 0.897; PH: p = 0.910; ZTE: p = 0.870). In contrast, SUVmax was significantly different between different β values for each AC method (p < 0.001). Detailed comparisons are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. Figure 6 displays the SUVmax and SUVmean values for lesion and background regions using Q.Clear (β = 300), represented as boxplots. Significant differences were observed between PH-AC and ZTE-AC in NSD metrics, and between Atlas-AC and PH-AC in SUVmean metrics. However, no significant differences were found between the three AC methods in terms of SUVmean or LBR. The results observed for Q.Clear with β = 300 were consistent with those at other β values.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated 27 patients with temporal lobe epilepsy to evaluate the effects of three AC methods and various β values of Q.Clear reconstruction on the quality of brain PET image. We found that β = 300 provided the best balance between lesion contrast and noise reduction across all three AC methods. ZTE-AC demonstrated the highest lesion contrast, which may be particularly valuable in cases with subtle abnormalities, whereas PH-AC resulted in lower image noise and reduced background variability. The results may inform the selection of reconstruction strategies for individualized epilepsy PET imaging protocols.

The choice of regularization factor β

Compared with OSEM reconstruction, Q.Clear significantly increased LBR and reduced NSD in both epileptogenic regions and the cerebellum. The results observed in temporal lobe subregions were consistent with those of the whole temporal lobe. A known limitation of OSEM is its lack of convergence, which may adversely affect the control of image noise. In contrast, Q.Clear reconstruction achieves full convergence using more iterations while maintaining acceptable noise levels [26]. As the image noise increases, the additional penalty term increases and reduces the value of the objective function, which better directs the optimization algorithm away from the noisy image [8]. The penalization factor β plays a critical role in controlling image noise: increasing β generally leads to more aggressive noise suppression [27]. According to our LBR-NSD analysis, an suitable β value of 300 was consistently observed across all three AC methods. The selection of β is crucial in Q.Clear reconstruction, as it governs the trade-off between image clarity and quantitative accuracy. Our results are comparable to those of Texte et al. [28], who evaluated OSEM and Q.Clear in lung cancer patients using the Jaszczak phantom model and identified an suitable β value of 350. In a related study, Reynés Llompart et al. [29] found that a β value of 200 was suitable for oncology and the brain in the BGO [18F]FDG PET/CT scanner. Wagatsuma et al. [13] determined the suitable β value for [18F]FDG brain PET image is 200. The discrepancy from our results may be due to differences in the AC methods applied and the PET systems used.

The relative differences in SUVmax between Q.Clear (β = 300) and OSEM were 6.8% (Atlas-AC), 7.0% (PH-AC), and 6.9% (ZTE-AC). For SUVmean, the differences were 0.6%, 0.9%, and 0.5%, respectively. These findings indicate that a β value of 300 in Q.Clear enhances lesion contrast while maintaining SUV precision across all AC methods. The gradual reduction in SUVmax and SUVmean observed with increasing β is consistent with previous literature. Otani T et al. [30] reported a similar trend in tumor assessments, identifying an suitable β of 500 in lung cancer PET/CT imaging. Another study [31] also demonstrated a continuous decline in SUVmax with increasing β during Q.Clear reconstruction, further corroborating our observations.

The choice of AC methods

This study conducted a comprehensive evaluation of three AC methods. Statistically significant differences were observed in NSD metrics between PH-AC and ZTE-AC, and in SUVmean between Atlas-AC and PH-AC. However, no significant differences were found among the three AC methods in terms of SUVmean and LBR metrics across the range of β values (β = 0–600) (see Supplementary Tables S3-S10 for details). Using both OSEM and Q.Clear reconstruction (β = 300), the mean LBR for PH-AC and ZTE-AC increased (PH-AC: from 1.19 to 1.24; ZTE-AC: from 1.22 to 1.26), while the mean NSD decreased (PH-AC: from 0.275 to 0.266; ZTE-AC: from 0.287 to 0.279), suggesting improved contrast and reduced noise with Q.Clear. Dickson JC et al. [32] compared Dixon-AC and ultrashort echo-time (UTE)-AC with CT-based-AC in patients with epilepsy and dementia. Their findings indicated that underestimation in mean regional activity concentrations was significantly smaller with UTE-AC than with Dixon-AC in the temporal lobe, which aligns with our voxel-wise analysis results. Additionally, SUVR was greater with UTE-AC than with Dixon-AC. Consistent with our findings, ZTE-AC showed the highest lesion-to-background contrast (LBR = 1.26 ± 0.15, β = 300), while PH-AC achieved the lowest image noise (NSD = 0.266 ± 0.018), reflecting better noise suppression. Hope T et al. [33] compared ZTE-AC and Atlas-AC with CT-AC in brain PET/MR. They found that Atlas-AC overestimated SUV uptake in the temporal lobe and cerebellar regions compared to ZTE-AC, which is consistent with our findings (see Table 5 and Supplementary Table S11).

In addition, some studies have explored combining the regularization factor β with administered dose for clinical optimization. Shkumat et al. [34]. evaluated the impact of different reconstruction algorithms and acquisition times in pediatric epilepsy brain PET/CT. Their results showed that reducing count density by 40% and using Q.Clear with β = 350–500 maintained diagnostic quality, demonstrating the potential of Q.Clear to reduce injected dose and scan time while preserving image quality. A recent study by Liberini et al. [35] compared Q.Clear and OSEM reconstruction and reported that β values of 200 and 300 yielded superior lesion detectability in brain metastases from lung cancer compared to conventional OSEM. Despite differences in clinical contexts, their findings are consistent with those of our study and further support the effectiveness of Q.Clear in brain PET imaging. These results also suggest that Q.Clear reconstruction may have broader applications in oncological staging and restaging, including in patients with brain involvement from primary lung cancer.

Recent PET/MR research has increasingly focused on deep-learning (DL)-based AC strategies, including pseudo-CT generation from Dixon/T1-weighted MR images, primarily to reduce regional SUV bias compared with atlas-based AC approaches [36, 37], and direct attenuation map synthesis from non-attenuation-corrected (NAC) PET data [38]. However, the interplay between these DL-based techniques and penalized likelihood reconstruction (e.g., Q.Clear) remains largely unexplored, particularly regarding whether and how DL-derived attenuation maps influence optimal regularization parameters. In this context, our study provides immediate practical guidance for current clinical workflows: we determined that a β value of 300 achieves the most favorable trade-off between LBR and NSD metrics across all evaluated AC methods. Furthermore, we identified distinct utility choices for different AC strategies—with ZTE-AC favoring contrast recovery and PH-AC prioritizing noise suppression—which can help tailor reconstruction protocols to specific diagnostic priorities. Future work should evaluate whether these parameters and spatial bias patterns generalize when DL-based AC or reconstruction pipelines are incorporated, thereby establishing a comprehensive optimization framework for next-generation PET/MR imaging.

Although this study focused on TLE, the core evaluation strategy, particularly the use of lesion-to-background quantification, can potentially be extended to other neurological disorders that exhibit characteristic metabolic abnormalities. For example, hippocampal hypometabolism is a well-established feature in early-stage Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [39] and is also associated with cognitive decline in Parkinson’s disease (PD) [40]. By adapting the selection of target brain regions based on disease-specific pathology, Our evaluation framework and experimental results may be applicable to PET image quality assessment in other neurological disorders.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small (27 patients), which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, only a single tracer ([18F]FDG) and one PET/MR scanner (GE SIGNA 3 T PET/MR) with a fixed protocol were used. The suitable β value may vary with different scanners, tracers, or acquisition settings. Third, this study primarily focuses on the evaluation of image quality metrics, and future work will consider incorporating clinical validation, including diagnostic accuracy and clinical relevance. In addition, all participants had clinically diagnosed TLE, therefore, the applicability of the results to other epilepsy subtypes or neurological disorders remains to be established. Lastly, although a brain atlas was used for region segmentation, individual anatomical variability may affect accuracy. Deep learning-based segmentation could be explored in future work.

Conclusion

This study found that Q.Clear reconstruction enhances contrast recovery and provides superior LBR and NSD on brain PET/MR images in epileptic patients compared to OSEM. β value of 300 served as a practical operating point for the three AC methods. Different AC methods demonstrated distinct advantages: ZTE-AC showed the highest LBR, which may be particularly valuable in cases with subtle abnormalities, PH-AC achieved the lowest NSD, reflecting better noise suppression, and Atlas-AC exhibited intermediate image quality. When β = 300, Q.Clear reconstruction improved lesion contrast and reduced noise without compromising SUVmax and SUVmean for all AC methods. Nonetheless, PET quantification may be affected by different β values and AC methods, so it is necessary to customize scanning protocols for specific diseases. These findings provide valuable guidance for optimizing β values and AC selection, thereby supporting clinical implementation of personalized PET reconstruction strategies in neurological imaging.
