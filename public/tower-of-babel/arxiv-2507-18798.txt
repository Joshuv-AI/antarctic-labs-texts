Higher-order Kripke models for intuitionistic and non-classical modal logics
Victor Barroso-Nascimento University College London

Abstract
This paper introduces higher-order Kripke models, a generalization of standard Kripke models that is remarkably close to Kripke’s original idea – both mathematically and conceptually. Standard Kripke models are now considered 0 0 -ary models, whereas an n n -ary model for n > 0 n>0 is a model whose set of objects (“possible worlds”) contains only ( n − 1 ) (n-1) -ary Kripke models. Models with infinitely many layers are also considered. This framework is obtained by promoting a radical change of perspective in how modal semantics for non-classical logics are defined: just like classical modalities are obtained through use of an accessibility relation between classical propositional models, non-classical modalities are now obtained through use of an accessibility relation between non-classical propositional models (even when they are Kripke models already). The paper introduces the new models after dealing specifically with the case of intuitionistic modal logic. It is shown that, depending on which intuitionistic 0 0 -ary propositional models are allowed, we may obtain 1 1 -ary models equivalent to either birelational models for I ​ K IK or for a new logic called M ​ K MK . Those 1 1 -ary models have an intuitive reading that adds to the interpretation of intuitionistic models in terms of “timelines” the concept of “alternative timelines”. More generally, the 1 1 -ary models can be read as defining a concept of “alternative” for any substantive interpretation of the 0 0 -ary models. The semantic clauses for necessity and possibility of M ​ K MK are also modular and can be used to obtain similar modal semantics for every non-classical logic, each of which can be provided with a similar intuitive reading. After intuitionistic modal logic is dealt with, the general structure of High-order Kripke Models and some of its variants are defined, and a series of conjectures about their properties are stated.

Introduction

Classical modal logic extends classical propositional logic by allowing application of the alethic notions of necessity and possibility to propositions
[
sep-logic-modal
]
. Of particular importance in this context is Saul Kripke’s “possible worlds” semantics
[
Kripke1959-KRIACT
]
, in which alethic reasoning takes the shape of counterfactual exploration of what would and would not hold if the world was not how it is. This framework allows characterization of judgments of shape “A is necessary” (
\Box A
) as being true whenever
A
is true in all possible configurations of the world, as well as judgments of shape “A is possible” (
\Diamond A
) as being true whenever
A
is true in at least one possible configuration. The semantics can be further enriched by incorporating a notion of conditional possibility that uses “acessibility relations” to specify which worlds are alternative configurations of which other worlds, yielding a plethora of distinct modal logics suitable for different philosophical conceptions of the alethic modalities
[
Chellas1980
, pp. 61-112]
.

There is nothing essentially classical about alethic modalities, so it is only natural to wonder how one could also obtain modal extensions of non-classical propositional logics. For logics characterizable through simple but non-standard truth functions (
e.g.
multivalued functions
[
sep-logic-manyvalued
]
, “glutty” bivalued functions allowing propositions to be both true and false or “gappy” bivalued functions allowing them to be neither true nor false
[
FDEAndersonBelnap
]
[
RestallGapsGluts
]
) the answer is straightforward, as it suffices to change how truth is treated at the possible worlds and adapt modalities accordingly
[
PriestManyvaluedmodal
]
. However, the answer is not as clear in the case of non-classical logics that already require possible world semantics for the characterization of their
propositional
fragment, since the concepts employed to provide modal extensions are already used to define their basic semantic notions. This is the case, for instance, of intuitionistic logic, whose main semantic characterization is given in terms of models structurally similar to those of the classical modal logic
S4

[
KripkeIntuitionistic
]
.

This paper provides an answer to this question by showing a general way of obtaining modal semantics for propositional logics characterizable through Kripke models, which incidentally also works for Tarskian models (since the former can be viewed as a generalization of the latter). Just like it is possible to define classical modal semantics in terms of a set of classical models (truth-functions, which are the “possible worlds”) and relations between them, we show that it is in general possible to define non-classical modal semantics in terms of a set of non-classical models (Kripke models, now regarded as “possible models”) and relations between them. In other words, we show that it is possible to generalize the very notion of a Kripke model in such a way that it becomes possible to extend to non-classical logics with Kripke propositional semantics essentially the same treatment given to classical logic and logics with simpler semantic characterizations.

In this paper, we arrive at such a general framework after discussing how modalities are treated in the literature on intuitionistic modal logics, specifically the modal logic
IK
and a new logic we call
MK
. Those logics are taken to offer the perfect starting point for our general discussion -- both due to the existence of subtle differences between their semantics, which highlight important features of our new framework, and to the broad relevance of constructive modalities for computer science
1

First, we present the traditional semantic treatment of intuitionistic modalities, which takes the form of Kripke models with two accessibility relations (birelational models). Then we provide mathematical and philosophical reasons for a radical change in perspective. From these reasons arises a new, intuitive, philosophically robust semantic framework for intuitionistic modalities, which is proven to be equivalent to birelational semantics through effective mappings that preserve model validity. It is then show that the structure of the new models naturally leads to a generalization of the very notion of Kripke models, as well as to modular definitions which yield a modal semantic extension for every logic that has a characterization in terms of Kripke models. In the case of logics that do not have canonical modal extensions (
viz
for each classical modal logic there is more than one viable candidate for the position of “true” non-classical analogue of that logic), the approach always yields a significantly strong modal extension, but weaker extensions may also be obtained by making
ad hoc
adjustments. The paper ends with a general discussion on features of higher-order Kripke models and some of its variants, in which some important conjectures about their properties are stated and justified.

Unlike classical logic, intuitionistic logic does not satisfy

For the second statement, let

4
A change of perspective

In the traditional approach, Kripke models for classical modal logic are essentially obtained by picking a set of classical propositional models and putting some relation between them. Birelational models for intuitionistic modal logic are obtained by picking a
single
intuitionistic propositional model and putting a second relation
inside
it. A classical model can intuitively be read as a collection of alternative propositional models, but no such reading can be given to birelational models. They are best understood as a single propositional model with alternative
worlds
. Moreover, each world may also be viewed as a classical propositional model, only the Kripke model itself being intuitionistic.

The traditional approach is conceptually justified by the intended meaning of alethic modalities. On the other hand, most justifications for the birelational approach are essentially mathematical in nature. Discussions on what intuitionistic modal definitions should look like often revolve around which conditions would it be natural for the models to satisfy or which formulas would it be reasonable for them to validate instead of what should be the intended meaning of the modalities or of the semantic constructs.

We will now show that it is possible to provide intuitionistic modal semantics through a generalization of the traditional approach. We start by defining a concept of model:

Definition 4.1
.

A
general model

G
is a sequence
\langle W,\succ\rangle
, where
W
is a non-empty set of Kripke propositional models and
\succ
is a relation
W
.

The modal accessibility relation
\succ
is now defined as holding between Kripke models instead of between the worlds of a model. The intuitionistic propositional models themselves are now considered the “possible worlds” of the general model, and the accessibility relation once again establishes which propositional models are alternative versions of which other models.

Although the idea behind it is quite intuitive, this definitions creates a practical issue: when defining relations between worlds, we are capable of handpicking a world
w
and putting it in a modal relation with a specific world
w^{\prime}
, provided we make sure all required frame conditions are satisfied. On the other hand, when the relation is defined for Kripke models we have no control over the accessibility relation at the level of worlds. In a sense, by defining an accessibility relation for models we are uniformly defining modal accessibility for all worlds of that model. This is problematic because validity for formulas is first defined at the level of worlds and then carried over to the entire Kripke model, so without some sort of control it seems modalities could not hold in a world without also holding in all others.

The key insight behind the solution to this is that every world in a Kripke model can be taken to conveniently fix a reference point for the definition of its own modal validities – namely,
itself
. Let us return to the interpretation of models as timelines in order to illustrate this point. Consider a frame with worlds
\{m,a,e\}
, representing the possible activities of a particular mathematician in the morning, afternoon and evening of a single work day. Now take a general model with three models
K
,
K^{\prime}
and

defined with no other objects. Let us represent the models graphically as follows:

5
Mapping the new models into the traditional models

We now define a mapping that transforms partial models into equivalent birelational models and homogeneous models into equivalent strong models. For the sake of convenience, we present the mapping itself for general models:

K\succ K^{\prime}

\langle w,K\rangle R^{*}\langle w,K^{\prime}\rangle
only holds if both
K
and
K^{\prime}
contain

implies
\langle w,K\rangle\notin F^{*}
and
w\notin W_{K^{\prime}}
implies
\langle w,K\rangle\notin F^{*}
.

This mapping is very similar to a mapping of birelational models into intuitionistic first-order models presented by Simpson in
[
Sim94

, the main difference being that Simpson uses pairs
\langle w,d\rangle
of worlds and elements of their domain instead of pairs of models and elements of their frame

Now we prove the following:

Lemma 5.2

If

is a birelational model.

Proof 5.3
.

1.

\leq^{*}

2.

3.

and
wR^{*}j

such that

and
w^{\prime}R^{*}j^{\prime}

4.

wR^{*}j

w^{\prime}
such that
w\leq^{*}w^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime}R^{*}j^{\prime}

(1)

Since for every

clearly
\langle w,K\rangle\leq^{*}\langle w,K\rangle
for every
\langle w,K\rangle
by the definition of
\leq^{*}
, so

is reflexive. For transitivity, assume
\langle w,K\rangle\leq^{*}\langle w^{\prime},K^{\prime}\rangle

\langle w^{\prime},K^{\prime}\rangle\leq^{*}\langle w^{\prime\prime},K^{\prime\prime}\rangle

\leq^{*}

\leq^{*}

is transitive.

(2)

Assume
\langle w,K\rangle\leq^{*}\langle w^{\prime},K^{\prime}\rangle

\leq^{*}
that

and

, so from Definition
3.4
we get

(3)

Assume

R^{*}

Proof 5.7
.

4.7
and
4.8

, which means that we only have to show the following:

1.

If

and

(a)

\leq^{*}

and

, and from the definition of
R^{*}
we get

and

. Since

\langle w^{\prime},K^{\prime}\rangle R^{*}\langle w^{\prime\prime},K^{\prime\prime}\rangle

\langle w,K\rangle\in F^{*}
and
\langle w^{\prime\prime},K^{\prime\prime}\rangle\in F^{*}
, so also

5.1
Mapping the traditional models into the new models

This direction is trickier because, in order to build one of the new models, we must fix a single frame and either use it as a reference frame (partial models) or as the only frame (homogeneous models). Thus, in order to provide a uniform mapping for all models, we must fix a frame in which every object
j
has the greatest possible number of non-modally accessible objects
j\leq j^{\prime}
. For the sake of simplicity, we restrict our attention only to frames with countably many objects, but our strategy can also be adapted to deal with uncountable sets. Just like before, we start by proving our results for partial models and then show how they can be adapted to homogeneous models.

Definition 5.12
.

A
canonical quasi-structure
is a pair
\langle Q,R\ \rangle
such that:

1.

Q_{n}
and
Q_{m}
are non-empty disjoint sets whenever
n,m\geq 0
and
m\neq n
, and
Q=\{Q_{n}|n\geq 0\}
;

2.

R
is a relation such that, for every
j\in Q_{n}
, there are infinitely many
j^{\prime}\in Q_{n+1}
such that
jRj^{\prime}
, and for every
j^{\prime}\in Q_{m}(m>0)
there is exactly one
j\in Q_{m-1}
such that
jRj^{\prime}
.

So every object
j\in Q_{n}
has infinitely many “dedicated accessible worlds” in the set
Q_{n+1}
, each coming with their own infinite set of dedicated worlds on
Q_{n+2}
(and so on). Notice that it follows from the definition that
Q_{n}
is infinite for every

, but

is only required to be non-empty. As will become clear, this means that every birelational model can also be mapped specifically to a model in which the reference frame and each of its at least partial copies have unique “roots”.

Definition 5.13
.

Let
\langle Q,R\rangle
be a canonical quasi-structure. Then a
full canonical structure

is a pair
\langle Q_{FC},\leq_{FC}\rangle
where

and
\leq_{FC}
is the reflexive and transitive closure of

.

.

Let

be any at least partial copy of

and

an arbitrary object with

. Then

.

Lemma 5.15
.

If
PC
is an at least partial copy of

then

is an at least partial copy of

.

Proof 5.16
.

We are finally ready to define the partial model correspondent of a birelational model:

1.

is a interpretation of

2.

\langle Q_{PC},\leq_{PC},f_{B,w}\rangle\succ\langle Q_{PC^{\prime}},\leq_{PC^{\prime}},f^{\prime}_{B,w^{\prime}}\rangle

j\in Q_{PC^{\prime}}

f_{B,w}(j)=v(w^{\prime\prime})

f^{\prime}_{B,w^{\prime}}(j)=v(w^{\prime\prime\prime})

w^{\prime\prime}Rw^{\prime\prime\prime}

So our propositional models are given by all pairs comprised of a partial copy

Corollary 5.23

M^{B}
is a partial model.

Proof 5.24

Since

4.2

We now turn to the task of providing similar results for strong models with respect to homogeneous models. The full canonical structures
FC
that were taken as reference frames of our partial models can now be taken as providing the fixed frame that we use to build homogeneous models. Notice that, since

is already an at least partial copy of itself and every strong model is also a birelational model, Definition
5.17
and Lemma

can still be used. For greater clarity, we denote functions

defined for strong models

by

. On the other hand, since it uses partial copies

of
FC
which are no longer acceptable, Lemma
5.20
cannot be used in the new mapping. We prove an adapted, stronger version of it instead:

Lemma 5.31
.

Let
S=\langle W,\leq,R,v\rangle
be a strong model and
f_{S,w}
a function interpreting
w\in W
in a full canonical structure
FC
. Let
w^{\prime},w^{\prime\prime}\in W
be arbitrary objects with
f_{S,w}(j)=v(w^{\prime})
for arbitrary
j\in Q_{FC}
and
w^{\prime}Rw^{\prime\prime}
. Then there is a function
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}
interpreting some
w^{\prime\prime\prime}
in
FC
such that
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}(j)=v(w^{\prime\prime})
and, for all
j^{\prime}\in Q_{FC}
, if
f_{S,w}(j^{\prime})=v(s)
and
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}(j^{\prime})=v(s^{\prime})
then
sRs^{\prime}
.

Proof 5.32
.

Let us remember that, in virtue of Clause 2 of Definition
5.17
,
f_{S,w}(j^{\prime})=v(s)
implies
w\leq s
. Then we construct
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}
as follows:

1.

Since
f_{S,w}(j)=v(w^{\prime})
we conclude
w\leq w^{\prime}
. Since
w^{\prime}Rw^{\prime\prime}
, by
F_{3}
and witness uniqueness there must be a unique
w^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
such that
wRw^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
and
w^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}\leq w^{\prime\prime}
. We put
w^{\prime\prime\prime}=w^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
and
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}(j)=v(w^{\prime\prime\prime})
for all
j\in Q_{0}
.

2.

For all
j^{\prime}\in Q_{n}
for
n>0
, let
f_{S,w}(j^{\prime})=v(s)
. Then
w\leq s
and, since
w^{\prime}Rw^{\prime\prime\prime}
, from condition
F_{1}
and witness uniqueness we conclude that there must be a unique
s^{\prime}
such that
sRs^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq s^{\prime}
. Then we define
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}(j)=v(s^{\prime})
.

Since by definition
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}(j)=v(w^{\prime\prime\prime})
the function clearly satisfies Clause 1 of Definition
5.17
, and since by definition for all
j^{\prime}\in Q_{FC}
we have
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}(j^{\prime})=v(s^{\prime})
for some
w^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq s^{\prime}
it clearly also satisfies Clause 2.

We have to show that
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}(j)=v(w^{\prime\prime})
. For that, let
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}(j)=v(w^{\prime\prime\prime\prime})
. Since
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}
satisfies Clause 2 of Definition
5.17
,
w^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq w^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
. By the definition of
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}
and the fact that
f_{S,w}(j)=v(w^{\prime})
we also have
w^{\prime}Rw^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
and
w\leq w^{\prime}
. In the construction of
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}
above we have shown that
w^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq w^{\prime\prime}
, and it is assumed in the statement of the lemma that
w^{\prime}Rw^{\prime\prime}
. Then
w^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq w^{\prime\prime}
,
w^{\prime}Rw^{\prime\prime}
,
w^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq w^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
and
w^{\prime}Rw^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
, so by uniqueness of
w^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
we have
w^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}=w^{\prime\prime}
, thus we conclude
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}(j)=v(w^{\prime\prime})
.

In order to finish the proof and show that
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}
is indeed an interpretation of
w^{\prime\prime\prime}\in W
in
FC
it suffices to show that it satisfies the two remaining clauses of Definition
5.17
. The proof is essentially the same of Lemma
5.20
, the sole difference being that the homogenous character of
FC
allows us to drop all references to Lemma
5.14
and to structural features of
PC^{j}
(and thus simplifies the proof).

1.

For all
j^{\prime}\in Q_{FC}
, if
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}(j^{\prime})=v(s)
and
s\leq s^{\prime}
, then there is a
j^{\prime\prime}\in Q_{FC}
such that
j^{\prime}\leq_{FC}j^{\prime\prime}
and
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}(j^{\prime\prime})=v(s^{\prime})
.

Assume
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}(j^{\prime})=v(s)
and
s\leq s^{\prime}
for some
j^{\prime}\in Q_{FC}
. From the definition of
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}
we have
f_{S,w}(j^{\prime})=v(s^{\prime\prime})
for some
s^{\prime\prime}Rs
. From
F_{2}
and witness uniqueness we conclude that, since
s^{\prime\prime}Rs
and
s\leq s^{\prime}
, there must be some unique
s^{\prime\prime\prime}
such that
s^{\prime\prime}\leq s^{\prime\prime\prime}
and
s^{\prime\prime\prime}Rs^{\prime}
. Since
s^{\prime\prime}\leq s^{\prime\prime\prime}
and
f_{S,w}(j^{\prime})=v(s^{\prime\prime})
we conclude by Clause 2 of Definition
5.17
that there is some
j^{\prime\prime}\in Q_{FC}
such that
j^{\prime}\leq_{FC}j^{\prime\prime}
and
f_{S,w}(j^{\prime\prime})=v(s^{\prime\prime\prime})
. Since
j\leq_{FC}j^{\prime}
and
j^{\prime}\leq_{FC}j^{\prime\prime}
by transitivity of
\leq_{FC}
we conclude
j\leq_{FC}j^{\prime\prime}
. Since
f_{S,w}(j^{\prime\prime})=v(s^{\prime\prime\prime})
then
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}(j^{\prime\prime})=v(s^{\prime\prime\prime\prime})
, where
s^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
is the unique object with
s^{\prime\prime\prime}Rs^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
and
w^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq s^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
. Since
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}(j^{\prime})=v(s)
and
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}
satisfies Clause 2 of Definition
5.17
we conclude
w^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq s
, and since
s\leq s^{\prime}
by transitivity of
\leq
we conclude
w^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq s^{\prime}
. So we have
w^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq s^{\prime}
,
s^{\prime\prime\prime}Rs^{\prime}
,
w^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq s^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
and
s^{\prime\prime\prime}Rs^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
, so from uniqueness of
s^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
we conclude
s^{\prime}=s^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
. Hence since

we have
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}(j^{\prime\prime})=v(s^{\prime})
, so

is the desired witness.

2.

For all
j^{\prime},j^{\prime\prime}\in Q_{FC}
, if
f_{S,w^{\prime\prime\prime}}(j^{\prime})=v(s)
,

.

Pick any two

and
j^{\prime}\leq_{FC}j^{\prime\prime}
. By the definition of

we have

for

with
s^{\prime\prime}Rs
and
f_{S,w}(j^{\prime\prime})=v(s^{\prime\prime\prime})
for
s^{\prime\prime\prime}
with
s^{\prime\prime\prime}Rs^{\prime}
. Due to how

is defined we also have that
s^{\prime}
is the unique object such that
w^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq s^{\prime}
and
s^{\prime\prime\prime}Rs^{\prime}
. Since
f_{S,w}(j^{\prime})=v(s^{\prime\prime})
and
f_{S,w}(j^{\prime\prime})=v(s^{\prime\prime\prime})

s^{\prime\prime}\leq s^{\prime\prime\prime}
by Clause 4 of Definition
5.17
. Since
s^{\prime\prime}\leq s^{\prime\prime\prime}
and
s^{\prime\prime}Rs
, by

and witness uniqueness there must be a unique

such that
s\leq s^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
and
s^{\prime\prime\prime}Rs^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
. Since

and

satisfies Clause 2 of Definition
5.17

w^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq s
, so since
s\leq s^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
we conclude
w^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq s^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
by transitivity of
\leq
. Since
w^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq s^{\prime}
,
s^{\prime\prime\prime}Rs^{\prime}
,
w^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq s^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
and
s^{\prime\prime\prime}Rs^{\prime\prime\prime\prime\prime}
and the object
s^{\prime}
is unique we conclude
s^{\prime}=s^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
, hence since
s\leq s^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}
we conclude

.

As such, satisfaction of

means that we can always pick some

Proof 5.35
.

Proof 5.43
.

\Gamma\vDash A

\Gamma\vDash^{M}A

. By putting
M=M^{B}
we have
\Gamma\vDash^{M^{B}}A
, so we conclude
\Gamma\Vdash^{{B}}A
, and since
B
was arbitrary we conclude
\Gamma\Vdash A
. For the converse, assume
\Gamma\Vdash A
. Then
\Gamma\Vdash^{B}A
for all
B
. Let
M
be any partial model. Then by Definition
5.1
, Lemma
5.2
and Lemma
5.4
there is a birelational model
M^{*}
such that
\Gamma\vDash^{M}A
iff
\Gamma\Vdash^{M^{*}}A
. By putting
M^{*}=B
we have
\Gamma\Vdash^{M^{*}}A
, so we conclude
\Gamma\vDash^{{M}}A
, and since
M
was arbitrary we conclude
\Gamma\vDash A
. The proof of the second statement is analogous, the main difference being that it uses Definition
5.33
and Corollaries
5.34
and
5.40
instead of Definition
5.22
and Corollaries
5.34
and
5.29
, as well as Lemmas
5.6
and
5.10
instead of Lemmas
5.2
and Lemma
5.4
.

Consider also Definition
3.8
and Proposition
5.8
. If the definition is combined with the clauses from Definition
3.7
, the mapping from strong models into homogeneous models (and vice-versa) can still be used, meaning that all of the results of this section still hold. This shows that, as previously claimed,
MK
is indeed maximal with respect to the listed frame conditions. On a final note, since the full canonical structure can be rooted (that is, its set
Q_{0}
can be a singleton) and the partial structures
PC^{j}
we use during the proofs for
IK
are also rooted, the results of this section and of the one preceding it could also be shown if we considered only rooted partial and homogeneous models.

6
Extension to other non-classical logics

The logic
MK
holds a distinguished position in this framework due to the fact that it allows a straightforward generalization of the modal semantics to other logics. To see why, consider Definition
4.9
and the shape of the clause for
\Box
in
IK
:

\vDash_{w}^{K,M}\Box A\Longleftrightarrow
for all
w\leq_{K^{\prime}}w^{\prime}
, if
K\succ K^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}
then
\vDash_{w^{\prime}}^{K^{\prime},M}A
;

Generalization of this clause is precluded by the fact that it relies on the intuitionistic modal relation
\leq
, without which monotonicity is lost. On the other hand, consider the modal clauses for
MK
in Definition
4.10
:

\vDash_{w}^{K,H}\Box A\Longleftrightarrow K\succ K^{\prime}
implies
\vDash_{w}^{K^{\prime},H}A
;

\vDash_{w}^{K,H}\Diamond A\Longleftrightarrow
there is a
K\succ K
such that
\vDash_{w}^{K,H}A
;

There is nothing inherently intuitionistic about those clauses, so they only yield semantics for
MK
because the propositional models
K
themselves are intuitionistic. As such, we can obtain the
MK
analogue of any logic characterizable through Kripke models by using these clauses and only taking into account homogeneous models of that logic.

The maximality of
MK
with respect to the listed frame conditions suggest that every modal logic obtained through this method will be quite strong. In case the chosen logic does not satisfy modal categoricity, it may be possible to obtain distinct modal extensions by changing either the semantic clauses, the conditions imposed on frames or the class of models, but since the required changes depend on the particular logic being considered there can be no modular approach to such extensions. Just like
IK
is obtained from
MK
by allowing partially copied frames but changing the clause for
\Box
in order to retain monotonicity, the shape of the modal clauses and of the propositional models will be dictated by which properties we want to induce in the modal logics.

In this paper we have only dealt with intuitionistic analogues of the classical modal logic
K
, but this is only so that the results are as general as possible. It can be easily shown that by imposing the traditional conditions on the modal relation
\succ
we can obtain the analogues of stronger classical modal logics. Although in the case of
IK
the proof of this fact for formulas containing
\Box
relies on reasoning with the intuitionistic relation
\leq
, this can be shown to hold in general for all the analogues of
MK
obtained through the modular modal clasuses. We exemplify by considering two cases:

Proposition 6.1
.

Given the modal clauses in Definition
4.10
and any
K
, the following hold:

1.

If
\vDash^{K,H}_{w}\Box A
and
\succ
is reflexive then
\vDash^{K,H}_{w}A
.

2.

If
\vDash^{K,H}_{w}\Box A
and
\succ
is transitive then
\vDash^{K,H}_{w}\Box\Box A
.

Proof 6.2
.

For the first statement, assume
\vDash^{K,H}_{w}\Box A
. By reflexivity of
K
we conclude
K\succ K
, so by the semantic clause for
\Box
we obtain
\vDash^{K,H}_{w}A
. For the second, assume
\vDash^{K,H}_{w}\Box A
. In order to show a contradiction, further assume
\nvDash^{K,H}_{w}\Box\Box A
. So there must be a
K\succ K^{\prime}
such that
\nvDash^{K^{\prime},H}_{w}\Box A
, hence there must also be a
K^{\prime}\succ K^{\prime\prime}
such that
\nvDash^{K^{\prime\prime},H}_{w}A
. Since
K\succ K^{\prime}
,
K^{\prime}\succ K^{\prime\prime}
and
\succ
is transitive we have
K\succ K^{\prime\prime}
, so the semantic clause for
\Box
yields
\vDash^{K^{\prime\prime},M}_{w}A
. Contradiction. Therefore,
\vDash^{K,H}_{w}\Box\Box A
.

The proof of this proposition only relies on properties of
\succ
and on the shape of our modal clauses, meaning that this holds for any non-classical modal logic obtained through this approach.

On a final note, notice that Tarskian truth-functional models can be visualized as degenerate Kripke models (usually models
K
in which
W_{K}
is a singleton), whence it follows that our approach also applies to logics which Tarskian semantics. In particular, intuitionistic propositional models with a singleton
W_{K}
can easily be shown to correspond to classical propositional models, and it is equally easy to show that the logic obtained by applying this method to such models is precisely the classical modal logic
K
.

7
Higher-order Kripke models

The approach to modal logics presented here generalizes the Kripkean approach by applying its ideas in a broader setting. We still start with a set of models, define an accessibility relation and use the resulting structure to define modalities, but now we are allowed to start with Kripke models instead of being limited to truth-assignment functions. Since our relational structures now contain relational structures themselves, this also leads to a
mathematical
generalization of Kripke models, as the freshly obtained relational structure can then be used as an object in yet another relational structure. Following this idea to its natural conclusions leads us to
higher-order Kripke models
, a generalization of Kripke models which is closer to Kripke’s original idea (both conceptually and mathematically) than either neighborhood semantics
[
Pacuit2017-PACNSF
]
or general (algebraic) Kripke models
[
Chagrov1997-CHAML-2
]
, its other main generalizations.

Inasmuch they use more than one relation, birelational models themselves are generalizations of Kripke models. We can generalize this further by considering an arbitrary
set

\mathcal{R}
of relations. After this is done, (multirelational) higher-order Kripke models can be defined as follows:

Definition 7.1
.

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a language and
\mathcal{V}
a set of truth values. A
0
-ary Kripke model
K^{0}
is a sequence
K^{0}=\langle W,\mathcal{R},\textit{f}\rangle
, where
W
is a non-empty set of objects,
\mathcal{R}
a non-empty set of relations on
W
and
f
a function
f:W\times\mathbb{L}\mapsto\mathcal{V}
.

Definition 7.2
.

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a language and
\mathcal{V}
a set of truth values. For
n>0
, a
n
-ary Kripke model
K^{n}
is a sequence
K^{n}=\langle W,R,\textit{f}\rangle
, where
W
is a non-empty set of
(n-1)
-ary Kripke models,
R
a non-empty set of relations on
W
and
f
is a function
f:W\times\mathbb{L}\mapsto\mathcal{V}
.

If we want to remain even closer to the original formulation, we can also define unirelational higher-order Kripke models as follows:

Definition 7.3
.

A
0
-ary Kripke model is
unirelational
if its set
\mathcal{R}
is a singleton.

Definition 7.4
.

A
n
-ary Kripke models is
unirelational
if its set
\mathcal{R}
is a singleton and all elements of
W
are strict
(n-1)
-ary Kripke models.

Traditional Kripke models are then equivalent to
0
-ary unirelational Kripke models, and birelational models to
0
-ary Kripke models in which
\mathcal{R}
has two elements. The function
f
could alternatively been defined as assigning truth values only to pairs comprised of an element of
W
and an atomic formula
\mathbb{L}
if we also defined semantic clauses extending the valuations from atoms to the remaining formulas in
\mathbb{L}
, similar to what is done in Definitions
4.9
and
4.10
. We could also loosen the definition of models by stating that the set of objects of a
n
-ary model must be models of level
at most

(n-1)
, but it is not clear whether this would bring any benefit.

Although this will not be investigated here, there are reasons to believe that the switch from
0
-ary to
n
-ary Kripke models leads to increased expressivity, in the sense that some Kripke-incomplete logics (thus not complete for
0
-ary models) might be complete with respect to some class of
n
-ary models for
n>0
. Every
n
-ary Kripke model with
n>0
has at their disposal the functions used to define the
n-1
ary models, meaning that validity in a
n
-ary model can be defined as a function of validity (or invalidity) in any given collection of
(n-1)
-ary models. For instance, we can define
1
-ary models
K^{1}=\langle W^{1},\mathcal{R}^{1},\textit{f}^{1}\rangle
for a logic
\mathcal{L}^{1}
as containing only
0
-ary models
K=\langle W_{K},\mathcal{R}_{K},\textit{f}_{K}\rangle
sound and complete w.r.t. any other Kripke-complete logic
\mathcal{L}^{0}
and then define its function
f^{1}
as assigning the value true to a pair
\langle K,A\rangle
if either
f_{K}
assigns true to
\langle w,A\rangle
for every
w\in W_{K}
or some additional condition is satisfied, a procedure that guarantees that every theorem of
\mathcal{L}^{0}
is still a theorem of
\mathcal{L}^{1}
but may also allow the inclusion of new theorems through the additional condition of
f^{1}
.

Precisely stated, the conjecture is as follows:

Conjecture 7.5
.

There is at least one Kripke-incomplete logic
\mathcal{L}
which is complete w.r.t. to a class of
n
-ary Kripke models for some
n>0
.

Such a result would be remarkable because, as mentioned before, higher-order Kripke models are much closer to the original idea behind Kripke models than neighborhood semantics and general Kripke models. Those generalizations often have to be used because, unlike traditional Kripke semantics, neighborhood semantics and general models can provide semantics for any normal modal logic. Due to its additional structure, we conjecture that this is also the case for the new semantics:

Conjecture 7.6
.

All normal modal logics are complete w.r.t. some class of
n
-ary models for
n\geq 0
.

Of course, this can only be the case if the innermost Kripke models of a given
n
-ary model (that is, the set
W
of
(n-1)
-ary models) are used in an essential way by the definitions. This is not the case in our definitions for intuitionistic logic. In fact, notice that the specification of general models in Definition
4.1
does not include a function at all, which does not bring about issues because Clause 9 of Definition
4.9
is essentially defining a function which assigns a designated truth value to
\langle K,A\rangle
whenever the designated value is also assigned to
\langle w,A\rangle
for every
w\in W_{K}
. Definitions in which the truth assignments of each
n
-ary model are fully reducible to the truth assignments at their
(n-1)
-ary objects cannot reasonably be expected to be more expressive than multirelational Kripke models because they ultimately reduce to valuations of
0
-ary models, meaning that in all likelihood it will be possible to provide a mapping transforming such
n
-ary models into multirelational
0
-ary models. On the other hand, our proofs for
IK
and
MK
essentially show that their
0
-ary birelational models can be transformed into
1
-ary strict models by pushing one of the relations upwards and making the function of the
1
-ary model agree with the functions of its
0
-ary models. This is likely a general phenomenon for finite sets
\mathcal{R}
, which leads us to some definitions plus a conjecture:

Definition 7.7
.

A
0
-ary Kripke model is
finitely relational
if its set
\mathcal{R}
is finite.

Definition 7.8
.

A
n
-ary Kripke models is
finitely relational
if its set
\mathcal{R}
is finite and all elements of
W
are finitely relational
(n-1)
-ary Kripke models.

Conjecture 7.9
.

If a logic is complete w.r.t. a class of
n
-ary finitely relational models then it is complete w.r.t. a class of unirelational
m
-ary models for
m\geq n
.

Conjecture 7.10
.

There is at least one Kripke-incomplete logic
\mathcal{L}
which is complete w.r.t. to a class of unirelational
n
-ary Kripke models for some
n>0
.

Meaning that it is likely the case that we cannot always push a relation or set of relations down in the model (which would make any
n
-ary model equivalent to some multirelational
0
-ary model) because it would also be necessary to push the valuation function down, and it seems that this can only be done in cases where the
n
-ary function could already have been expressed solely in terms of the
(n-1)
-ary functions (as in the intuitionistic case). On the other hand, it is likely that we can always push a relation or set of relations
upwards
, precisely because if we start with
n
-ary models we can define
(n+1)
-ary models whose functions could have been expressed solely in terms of the
n
-ary models and use them just to put the relations at different levels. Since finitely relational models already might be more expressive than traditional models, this leads to the following conjecture:

Conjecture 7.11
.

All normal modal logics are complete w.r.t. some class of unirelational
n
-ary models for
n\geq 0
.

Which would be a refinement of Conjecture
7.6
, as it would show that even it the version closest to Kripke’s original formulation the new models would be sufficient for providing semantic for normal modal logics.

Higher-order Kripke models can be further generalized to infinitely layered structures in which we pick models for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
instead of stopping at some
n
:

Definition 7.12
.

A
infinitary higher-order Kripke model
is a infinite sequence
\langle\mathcal{K}^{0},\mathcal{K}^{1},...\rangle
such that
\mathcal{K}^{0}
is a set of
0
-ary Kripke models and, for all
n>0
,
\mathcal{K}^{n}
is a set of
n
-ary Kripke models such that
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
implies
W_{K}\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{(n-1)}
.

This concept of model could be paired, for instance, with definitions stating that a formula
A
is valid in a model
K^{\infty}
iff
\langle K,A\rangle
receives the designated value for every
K
which is an element of a set in occuring in
K^{\infty}
, or even if all models in the sequence satisfy some other condition. We can still expect Conjectures
7.5
,
7.6
,
7.10
and
7.11
to hold for the same reasons they are expected to hold for non-infinitary models, and the following infinitary variation of Conjecture
7.9
can also be stated:

Conjecture 7.13
.

If a logic is complete w.r.t. a class of infinitary models in which only finitely relational models occur then it is complete w.r.t. a class of infinitary models in which only unirelational models occur.

This is expected to hold because if there are
m
relations in a model
\mathcal{K}^{0}
that need to be pushed up we could push one to the level
\mathcal{K}^{1}
, one to the level
\mathcal{K}^{2}
and so on until the last one is put in
\mathcal{K}^{m}
. Then, we could put one of the relations originally occurring at the level
\mathcal{K}^{1}
in the level
\mathcal{K}^{(m+1)}
, one in
\mathcal{K}^{(m+2)}
and so on, eventually putting the last one in the level
\mathcal{K}^{(m+j)}
where
j
is the number of relations originally at the level
\mathcal{K}^{1}
. It seems that this could be repeated for all
n
, yielding a model with the desired property.

It would not be entirely unreasonable to expect that infinitary models, especially ones containing infinitary sets of relations, could be more expressive than neighborhood semantics and general Kripke models, which are not capable of providing semantics for all propositional modal logics
[
GersonInadequacy51cfe23e-c1e6-3725-ade2-e8600362b7a3
]
[
Dahn1976-DAHNSA
]
. We thus finish the section by stating the boldest of our conjectures:

Conjecture 7.14
.

Every propositional modal logic is complete with respect to some class of infinitary higher-order Kripke models.

Which would mean that the new generalization fixes the main model-theoretic issue observed in standard generalizations of Kripke semantics.

8
Conclusion

The mathematical and conceptual elegance of Kripke semantics led many to believe it would be the definitive semantics for normal modal logics, which turned out not to be the case due to the existence of many Kripke-incomplete logics
[
KTBeab764a2-304d-3126-97ce-0631fd61611b
]
. In this paper we have shown that an equally elegant modal semantics (which is even closer to Kripke’s original idea than neighborhood semantics and general Kripke semantics) can be provided for all non-classical modal logics whose propositional fragment is sound and complete with respect to some class of Kripke models. This is done by presenting such semantics for the intuitionistic modal logics
IK
and
MK
, which are interesting in their own right. Higher-order Kripke models (both in their finitary and infinitary versions) are then obtained as a natural generalization of the semantics for
IK
and
MK
, and the structure of some mappings defined between the new and the traditional (birelational) models for those logics hint at some general properties that can be reasonably expected to hold for all higher-order Kripke models.

Since this paper presents both results for modal logics in general and intuitionistic modal logics in particular, it will be interesting to further develop our investigations in both directions in future works. We are currently working on showing that, if semantics clauses with built-in monotonicity for both
\Box
and
\Diamond
are used and all propositional models are required to be intuitionistic, then general models (cf. Definition
4.1
) are sound and complete with respect to the intuitionistic modal logic
WK

[
Wijesekera1990-WIJCML
]
, as well as that a semantics for the logic

CK

[
bellin2001extended
]
[
degroot2025semanticalanalysisintuitionisticmodal
]
can be obtained through some additional adaptations. As for the other direction, a natural starting point would be to further investigate our conjectures for higher-order Kripke models.
