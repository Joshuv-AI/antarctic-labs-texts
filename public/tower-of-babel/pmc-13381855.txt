SLC1A5 prevents aortic aneurysm and dissection by glutaminolytic-epigenetic orchestration of vascular smooth muscle cell homeostasis
Authors: Pinglian Yang, Zhechang Gao, Weile Ye, Chunhong Zhou, Zhihua Zheng, Meiming Su, Yu He, Zhuoming Li, Jaroslav Pelisek, Ke He, Suowen Xu, Jiaojiao Wang, Zhiping Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAortic aneurysm and dissection (AAD) are high-risk cardiovascular diseases with limited preventive pharmacotherapies based on angiotensin II receptor blockade. However, the underlying pathomechanisms of AAD are still unknown. Here, we find that glutamine transporters, particularly solute carrier family 1 member 5 (SLC1A5), in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) from both patients and mice with AAD are significantly downregulated. VSMC-specific Slc1a5 deficiency exacerbates experimental AAD formation, with a marked increase in VSMC phenotypic switch and inflammation. Mechanistically, SLC1A5 preserves contractile phenotype by facilitating glutamine metabolite acetyl-CoA production and subsequent histone H3 lysine 9 and 27 acetylation, and ameliorates inflammation by promoting acetylated STAT3 mitochondrial translocation, hence inhibiting its nuclear translocation. Intriguingly, enforced SLC1A5 expression in VSMCs in vivo largely alleviates experimental AAD. These findings reveal a metabolic link between SLC1A5-driven glutamine transport and vascular homeostasis, suggesting SLC1A5 may be a promising therapeutic target for AAD.Subject terms: Cardiovascular diseases, Epigenetics, Acetylation

Introduction

Aortic aneurysm and dissection (AAD) are life-threatening cardiovascular diseases without effective treatment options. Even though the etiologies differ between aneurysm (aortic dilation) and dissection (tear or separation of the aortic wall), patients with AAD have a high mortality when the artery rupture occurs1,2. AAD is characterized by the disruption of aortic integrity driven by vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) phenotypic switch, inflammation, apoptosis, endothelial dysfunction, and extracellular matrix (ECM) destruction3–5. Increasing evidence suggests that phenotypic alteration of VSMCs, the predominant component in aortic media, is an early and predominant event in the progression of AAD3,5–7. Under pathological stress, VSMCs change from the contractile phenotype to a synthetic phenotype, with a reduction of contractile markers and an increase of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and inflammatory genes, leading to dysregulation of VSMC homeostasis6,8. Therefore, molecular targets governing VSMC homeostasis in AAD remain to be explored.

Metabolic abnormalities of amino acids, glucose, and lipids play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of AAD9,10. Glutamine, the most abundant amino acid in the plasma, is a major source for replenishing tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates through an anaplerotic pathway called glutaminolysis. Briefly, glutamine is metabolized to α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) in two steps, first by glutaminase 1 (GLS1) and then by aminotransferase or glutamate dehydrogenase-1. Further, glutamine-derived α-KG can be converted to acetyl coenzyme A (Acetyl-CoA) by reductive carboxylation11. Importantly, glutamine and its metabolites are pivotal for cardiovascular homeostasis12–17. The increase of intracellular glutamine level was beneficial for downregulating VSMC-derived thrombogenicity18, as well as for promoting cardiac progenitor cells and VSMC proliferation19,20. Moreover, dietary glutamine was found to be inversely associated with the risk of human cardiovascular mortality21. However, the factors that regulate glutamine uptake and metabolism in AAD remain unclear.

Solute carrier family 1 member 5 (SLC1A5, also known as ASCT2) is essential for cellular glutamine homeostasis. Emerging evidence indicates that SLC1A5-driven glutamine uptake participates in the development of cardiovascular diseases. For example, SLC1A5 expression was decreased in patients with heart failure. DNA methylation of SLC1A5 was associated with incident coronary heart disease22. Suppression of SLC1A5 using L-glutamic acid p-nitroanilide hydrochloride contributed to myocardial energy derangements by reducing glutamine uptake in failing myocardium14. Nonetheless, the functional role of glutamine transporter SLC1A5 in the pathology of AAD has not been characterized.

In the current study, by employing bulk RNA sequencing and single-cell RNA sequencing of glutamine transport and its metabolism-related genes in AAD, we discovere that glutamine transporters, especially SLC1A5, in VSMCs of patients and mice with AAD are significantly downregulated. Using both gain- and loss-of-function approaches, we demonstrate the protective effect of SLC1A5 against AAD in murine experimental models. Mechanistically, SLC1A5-driven glutamine transport and its metabolism play a crucial role in maintaining VSMC contractile phenotype and alleviating VSMC inflammation, which is driven by the regulation of chromatin remodeling and the interruption of STAT3 signaling, respectively. As a proof-of-concept, enforced expression of SLC1A5 in VSMCs and the administration of glutamine dramatically prevent AAD formation in mice. These findings shed light on a previously undescribed mechanism by which SLC1A5 remodels glutamine metabolism to prevent the progression of AAD.

Results

Glutamine transporter SLC1A5 expression is reduced in AAD

Through analysis of the metabolites in the plasma of 79 patients with aortic aneurysm and 79 healthy aortic controls from Lieberg’s study23, we identified that the levels of glutamine and its downstream metabolites (arginine and proline) were significantly reduced in human aortic aneurysm compared to healthy controls (Fig. 1A, B and Supplementary Fig. 1A). In line with these data from AAD patients, we observed a substantial reduction in plasma glutamine levels in angiotensin-II (Ang II)-induced AAD mice (Fig.1C), a widely used experimental mouse AAD model24. Glutamine is transported into and out of cells by glutamine transporters such as SLC1, SLC7 and SLC38 families, and cytoplasmic glutamine subsequently catabolized to glutamate that provides biosynthetic precursors (Fig. 1D)25,26. To identify the candidate glutamine transporters or its regulators of metabolism that contributed to the pathogenesis of AAD, we used RNA sequencing analysis in Ang II perfusion-induced AAD in Apoe-/- mice and discovered that several key genes associated with glutamine transport and metabolism were considerably reduced in AAD, particularly Slc1a5, whose expression was highest among the glutamine transporters (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 1B). Furthermore, we reanalyzed the microarray datasets of human aortic aneurysm (GEO accession: GSE57691) and mouse aortic aneurysm and dissected aneurysm (GEO accession: GSE17901). Similarly, heatmap visualization revealed that SLC1A5 was consistently downregulated in glutamine transporters across the two datasets, accompanied by a decrease in genes related to glutamate metabolism (Fig. 1F, G and Supplementary Fig. 1C, D).

To further delineate the full repertoire of the transcription profile of SLC familiar members in AAD, we reanalyzed the single-cell RNA sequencing data from mice with AAD (GEO accession: GSE239620). Interestingly, SLC1A5 predominantly accumulated in the SMCs population among eight major lineages (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. 2A–C). In line with our bulk RNA sequencing data, SLC1A5 was markedly decreased in mouse AAD compared with normal controls (Fig. 2B). Accordingly, using immunofluorescence staining, we observed a marked decrease in SLC1A5 expression in α-SMA-positive aortic VSMCs of patients with aortic aneurysm and aortic dissection versus healthy controls (Fig. 2C–F). Consistent with the observation in human specimens, immunofluorescence staining showed that SLC1A5 protein was predominantly expressed in α-SMA-positive SMCs in aortic tunica media from control mice, whereas its expression was dramatically decreased in those from Ang II-induced AAD mice (Fig. 2G). Moreover, the reduction of Slc1a5 mRNA and protein levels was confirmed in the medium layer of aortas from Ang II-infused Apoe-/- mice (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, the downregulation of SLC1A5 expression was recapitulated in mouse aortic SMCs treated with interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) (Fig. 2I, J), a proinflammatory mediator that is increased in AAD tissues and triggers the synthetic/inflammatory VSMC phenotypic switch27–30. Collectively, these data indicate that the glutamine transporter SLC1A5 in VSMCs is associated with human and mouse AAD.

Global Slc1a5 loss augments Ang II-induced AAD formation

To elucidate whether SLC1A5 downregulation is a pathogenic mechanism for AAD, global Slc1a5 deficiency (Slc1a5 KO) mice were generated by the CRISPR-Cas9 system, and the knockout effect of Slc1a5 was validated by sequencing, genotyping, and RT-qPCR analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3A–E). The mice were born with expected Mendelian ratios, displayed normal growth, and did not exhibit embryonic lethality. Hyperlipidemia combined with Ang II is one of the most commonly used mouse aneurysm and dissection model5,24,31. Adeno-associated virus encoding PCSK9 D377Y (a gain-of-function mutation) (AAV8-PCSK9) successfully induced hyperlipidemia in Slc1a5 KO mice, with significant degradation of low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), an increase of the level of plasma triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (T-CHO), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), but not high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) (Supplementary Fig. 3F–K). Slc1a5 KO mice and their littermate controls (Slc1a5 WT) were treated with AAV8-PCSK9 and Ang II to induce AAD and then were fed a Western diet (Fig. 3A). Systolic blood pressure was elevated after PCSK9/Ang II infusion for 28 days, but there was no significant difference between genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 3L). However, the survival rate of global Slc1a5 knockout mice was decreased compared with the control mice (Supplementary Fig. 3M). At the endpoint of PCSK9/Ang II infusion, Slc1a5 KO mice exhibited augmented AAD formation with more severe aneurysm and dissection (Fig. 3B). Notably, Slc1a5 KO mice showed a higher incidence of the AAD (80%), increased maximal abdominal aortic diameter of aortas (1.945 ± 0.103 mm), and elevated total aortic weight/body weight ratio than those in Slc1a5 WT mice (AAD incidence of 40%; maximal abdominal aortic diameter of 1.421 ± 0.094 mm) (Fig. 3C, D and Supplementary Fig. 3N). The diameters of the ascending aorta, aortic arch and descending aorta were also increased in Ang II infusion-induced Slc1a5 KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 3O). Furthermore, accelerated AAD development was characterized by in vivo B-mode (both longitudinally and transversely) and M-mode ultrasound imaging of aortas (Fig. 3E, F). In addition, elastin van Gieson (EVG) staining and Masson’s trichrome staining indicated that Slc1a5 KO mice exhibited increased elastic fiber degradation and collagen accumulation in the middle layer of abdominal aortas (Fig. 3G–I), indicating that reduced expression of SLC1A5 may facilitate the development of AAD. Together, these findings suggest that SLC1A5 serves as an endogenous negative regulator of AAD.

VSMC-specific Slc1a5 loss exacerbates Ang II-induced AAD formation

To investigate the VSMC-specific effect of SLC1A5, we generated tamoxifen-inducible VSMC-specific Slc1a5-deficient mice (Slc1a5 iΔVSMC) by breeding Slc1a5 flox/flox mice with Myh11Cre/ERT2 mice (Fig. 3J and Supplementary Fig. 4A). After tamoxifen treatment, SLC1A5 expression was markedly decreased in the aortic media of Slc1a5 iΔVSMC mice compared to control Myh11Cre/ERT2 mice, not in other major organs, suggesting the specificity and efficiency of conditional knockout (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Next, Slc1a5 iΔVSMC mice and their littermate controls were injected with tamoxifen, and were subsequently administered with AAV8-PCSK9 plus Ang II to induce AAD (Fig. 3K). LDLR protein was degraded in both Slc1a5 iΔVSMC and Myh11Cre/ERT2 mice receiving AAV8-PCSK9, with a significant increase of TG, T-CHO, and LDL-C levels (Supplementary Fig. 4 C–H). After Ang II infusion for 28 days, the systolic blood pressure level was significantly increased, with no significant difference between genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 4I). In accordance with results obtained in Slc1a5 KO mice, VSMC-specific Slc1a5 deletion reduced the survival rate (Supplementary Fig. 4J) and aggravated AAD formation in the murine AAD model (Fig. 3L). Compared with those in challenged Myh11Cre/ERT2 mice (AAD incidence of 45% and maximal diameter of 1.613 ± 0.070 mm), an increased incidence of the AAD (80%), increased maximal diameter of the aorta (1.993 ± 0.105 mm), and elevated total aortic weight/body weight ratio were found in Slc1a5 iΔVSMC mice after Ang II infusion (Fig. 3M, N and Supplementary Fig. 4K). The aggravated AAD formation was also indicated by in vivo ultrasound imaging of the aortas (Supplementary Fig. 4L, M). The diameter of the ascending aorta, aortic arch and descending aorta were also increased in Ang II infusion-induced Slc1a5 iΔVSMC mice (Supplementary Fig. 4N). In line with Ang II-induced Slc1a5 KO mice, Slc1a5 iΔVSMC mice showed similar deteriorated tendencies with elastic fiber degradation and collagen accumulation in the tunica media of aortas compared with the control mice (Fig. 3O–Q). These findings demonstrate that loss of SLC1A5 specifically in VSMCs disrupts vascular homeostasis and aggravates AAD formation in the Ang II-induced murine AAD model.

VSMC-specific Slc1a5 loss exaggerates BAPN/Ang II-induced AAD

We next corroborated the aforementioned findings using a second AAD model with BAPN and Ang II infusion32,33 in both Slc1a5 iΔVSMC and Myh11Cre/ERT2 control mice. No significant baseline differences were observed between the groups. Following BAPN/Ang II infusion, systolic blood pressure increased substantially, with no noticeable differences between the genotypes (Fig. 4A, B). In this model, the challenged Slc1a5 iΔVSMC mice showed marked aortic diameter enlargement (Fig. 4C). Consistent with the finding observed in the Ang II-induced AAD model, challenged Slc1a5 iΔVSMC mice showed enlarged aortic diameter in different aortic segments (including ascending aorta, aortic arch, descending aorta, and suprarenal aorta) (Fig. 4D) and elevated total aortic weight/body weight (Fig. 4E). Ultrasound images also showed obvious abnormal dilation of the ascending aorta, aortic arch and suprarenal aorta (Fig. 4F). Moreover, the challenged Slc1a5 iΔVSMC mice showed a reduced survival rate (Fig. 4G) and a trend toward increased incidence of AAD, severe AAD and rupture, compared with Myh11Cre/ERT2 mice (Fig. 4H). In addition, H&E, EVG, and Masson staining showed that elastin degradation and collagen content in the aortic media of different segments of the aorta (ascending aorta, aortic arch, descending aorta, and suprarenal aorta) were markedly increased in the challenged Slc1a5 iΔVSMC mice compared with control mice (Fig. 4I–K). Taken together, these findings indicate that SLC1A5 deficiency in VSMCs exaggerates BAPN/Ang II-induced AAD formation and development in mice.

SLC1A5 maintains the VSMC contractile phenotype via histone acetylation

VSMC phenotypic switch contributes to vascular inflammation in AAD by releasing proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, and in turn, these stimuli trigger VSMC phenotype conversion, ultimately leading to ECM degradation and aortic dilation34. Next, we investigated whether SLC1A5 deletion affects vascular contractility using a wire myograph. KCl-induced contractions of the arterial rings were comparable between Slc1a5 KO and Slc1a5 WT. However, arterial rings from Slc1a5 KO mice showed a significantly decreased contractile response to phenylephrine (PE) compared with those from controls (Supplementary Fig. 3P, Q), indicating that SLC1A5 may be specifically involved in PE-induced vasoconstriction. Having determined the crucial role of SLC1A5 in VSMCs on the pathogenesis of AAD formation, we sought to determine whether SLC1A5 governs the VSMC phenotypic switch. Western blot and immunohistochemistry analyses revealed a significant reduction in key contractile proteins, including α-SMA, Calponin 1, and smooth muscle 22α (SM22α) in abdominal aortas from PCSK9/Ang II-infused Slc1a5 iΔVSMC or Slc1a5 KO mice, indicating that SLC1A5 depletion exacerbates VSMC phenotypic switch (Fig. 5A–H). Furthermore, MASMCs isolated and cultured from Slc1a5 WT and Slc1a5 KO mice were used to assess the VSMC phenotypic alterations in vitro. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5A, B, SLC1A5 deletion resulted in a significant reduction of protein expression of VSMC contractile markers. Consistently, Slc1a5 siRNA-mediated SLC1A5 knockdown decreased while adenovirus-mediated SLC1A5 overexpression increased protein levels of VSMC contractile markers and elastin in mouse aortic SMCs (MASMCs) (Fig. 5I, J and Supplementary Fig. 5C, D). Together, these data provide compelling evidence that SLC1A5 preserves the VSMC contractile phenotype both in vivo and in vitro.

SLC1A5, as a carrier of glutamine, is supposed to promote glutamine uptake and its metabolism to produce metabolic intermediates, including acetyl-CoA (Fig. 1D). Therefore, we investigated whether acetyl-CoA production and subsequent histone acetylation underlie SLC1A5-regulated VSMC contractile gene transcription. Indeed, blocking glutamine uptake by silencing Slc1a5 decreased the levels of both glutamine and acetyl-CoA in MASMCs. Conversely, Slc1a5 overexpression elevated the levels of these metabolites (Fig. 6A–E). Consistent with these in vitro findings, SMC-specific Slc1a5 knockout mice exhibited significantly decreased glutamine and acetyl-CoA levels in the aortas following Ang II infusion (Supplementary Fig. 5E). Accordingly, Slc1a5 knockdown reduced while Slc1a5 overexpression increased acetylation of histone 3 lysine 9 (H3K9ac) and lysine 27 (H3K27ac) (Fig. 6F–I). Subsequently, we found a significant increase of SLC1A5, elastin, and contractile markers in MASMCs upon glutamine-enriched medium treatment and an elevation of SLC1A5, H3K9ac, and H3K27ac in a glutamine dose-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 5F–H). Notably, the glutamine-induced contractile phenotype was abolished by SLC1A5 knockdown or glutamine deprivation, whereas SLC1A5 overexpression partially rescued the contractile dysfunction caused by glutamine deficiency (Supplementary Fig. 5I, J). Further comparative analysis of several key glutamine downstream metabolites revealed that sodium acetate treatment elicited a concentration-dependent increase in contractile marker protein levels (e.g., α-SMA, Calponin 1, and SM22α), whereas other metabolites such as proline, L-glutamate and DM-αKG did not show any apparent patterned changes (Supplementary Fig. 6A–D). In line with the in vitro data, aortic media isolated from Slc1a5 iΔVSMC mice under basal and following Ang II infusion showed lower levels of H3K9ac and H3K27ac than the controls (Fig. 6J–L).

To gain more insight into the epigenetic (histone acetylation) mechanisms by which SLC1A5 regulates VSMC phenotype, we performed genome-wide cleavage under target and tagmentation (CUT&Tag) in SLC1A5 knockdown and the control MASMCs using an antibody against H3K9ac (Fig. 6M). As the decrease in H3K9ac had been previously confirmed (Fig. 6F, G), SLC1A5 knockdown caused a reduction in the detectable H3K9ac bindings to promoter regions compared to the control MASMCs (Fig. 6N). Specifically, the called peaks identified candidate genomic loci at the key contractile genes, including Acta2, Cnn1, and Tagln, which were markedly decreased in SLC1A5-knockdown MASMCs (Fig. 6O). Next, we determined whether SLC1A5-regulated VSMC contractile gene expression is linked to histone modification. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed with antibodies against H3K9ac and H3K27ac in MASMC with or without Slc1a5 silencing. Loss of SLC1A5 significantly decreased the signal of H3K9ac and H3K27ac in proximity to the promoters of contractile genes, including Acta2, Cnn1, and Tagln (Fig. 6P, Q). In contrast, SLC1A5 overexpression enhanced the recruitment of acetylated H3K9 and H3K27 epigenetic marks to the promoter region of contractile genes (Fig. 6R, S). Furthermore, we found that neither gain- nor loss-of-function of SLC1A5 had an influence on the expression of histone acetyl-transferase P300 and serum response factor (SRF), which orchestrate the transcriptional activation of VSMC contractile genes5,35,36. Interestingly, SLC1A5 overexpression promoted the binding of histone H3 to P300 and SRF, whereas SLC1A5 silencing had an opposite effect (Supplementary Fig. 6E, F). ChIP assays further revealed that Slc1a5 knockdown significantly reduced SRF binding to the promoters of contractile genes (e.g., Acta2, Cnn1, and Tagln) compared to the control (Supplementary Fig. 6G, H). Taken together, these results indicate that SLC1A5-driven glutamine uptake drives acetyl-CoA production, leading to H3K9/27 acetylation and subsequent transcription cofactor binding and contractile gene transcription, thus reprogramming VSMC into a contractile phenotype (Fig. 6T).

SLC1A5 suppresses VSMC inflammation via STAT3 acetylation and subsequent mitochondrial translocation

Aortic wall inflammatory response and inflammation-mediated immune cell infiltration are critical for AAD formation and development. Accumulating evidence indicates that VSMC phenotypic modulation is associated with the inflammatory response37–39. We analyzed bulk RNA-seq data from adventitia-removed aortas after 7-day Ang II infusion and reanalyzed the public microarray dataset (GSE17901). Both datasets consistently demonstrated a significant reduction of Slc1a5 expression in aortas, particularly in VSMCs at day 7, a time point that corresponds to the early phase of aneurysm development, prior to overt rupture or severe dilation occurs, when inflammation-related genes (e.g., Il-1β, Il6) were either concurrently or subsequently upregulated (Supplementary Fig. 6I, J). These findings suggest that the decrease of SLC1A5 may serve as an early driver of AAD pathogenesis, initiating a vicious cycle with inflammation (i.e., inflammatory stimuli inhibit SLC1A5, which in turn enhances the inflammatory response), thereby accelerating AAD progression. Furthermore, snapshots of CUT&Tag tracks showed that SLC1A5 knockdown did not promote the enrichment of H3K9ac at inflammation-related gene promoters (e.g., Mmp2, Mmp3, Il6) (Supplementary Fig. 6K). These findings suggest that SLC1A5’s regulation of inflammatory response may be independent of the histone acetylation pathway. Consequently, we assessed the effect of SLC1A5 deficiency on inflammatory responses and macrophage infiltration in vivo by immunohistochemistry for CD68 and VCAM1. Slc1a5 KO and Slc1a5 iΔVSMC mice exhibited a significant increase in the CD68- and VCAM1-positive regions within the abdominal aortic wall compared to control mice after Ang II infusion (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Fig. 7A). Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), including MMP2, MMP3, and MMP9, contribute to AAD development by degrading elastin, a major vascular ECM40–42, and also play a crucial role in the inflammatory response by modulating the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β43. Therefore, we determined the MMP activity and expression in mouse aortas by in situ zymography and immunohistochemistry & Western blot, respectively. The abdominal aortas from Ang II-infused Slc1a5 KO and Slc1a5 iΔVSMC mice showed higher MMP activity (Fig. 7B, C and Supplementary Fig. 7B, C) and protein levels of MMP2/3/9 (Fig. 7A, D, E and Supplementary Fig. 7A, D, E) than controls. In line with these in vivo data, we observed that silencing the Slc1a5 with siRNA in MASMCs aggravated IL-1β-mediated upregulation of MMP2/9 and downregulation of elastin and VSMC contractile markers (Fig. 7F, G). Conversely, overexpression of SLC1A5 exhibited contrasting effects (Fig. 7H, I). In addition, Slc1a5 knockdown significantly upregulated, whereas Slc1a5 overexpression markedly diminished IL-1β-induced mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, as indicated by RT-qPCR analysis (Supplementary Fig. 7F, G). Thus, both loss- and gain-of-function approaches demonstrated that SLC1A5 suppresses the VSMC inflammatory response.

Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that NF-κB and STAT3 are key signal integrators regulating MMP expression, vascular inflammation, and AAD progression44,45. To further explore the underlying mechanisms by which SLC1A5 negatively regulates VSMC inflammation, we analyzed the activation of NF-κB and STAT3 signaling pathways. Western blot analysis revealed that the phosphorylated levels of NF-κB-p65 were not reduced in glutamine-enriched or SLC1A5-overexpressed MASMCs, indicating that SLC1A5-regulated VSMC inflammation is not mediated by NF-κB signaling (Supplementary Fig. 8A, B). Notably, SLC1A5 knockdown substantially increased the level of phosphorylated STAT3 in the presence of IL-1β (Fig. 7J). Conversely, SLC1A5 overexpression inhibited IL-1β-induced STAT3 activation, as indicated by a reduction of phosphorylated STAT3 (Fig. 7K). Since SLC1A5-mediated glutamine uptake is capable of driving acetyl-CoA production, we next examined whether SLC1A5 affects STAT3 acetylation. Indeed, acetylation of STAT3 was decreased in MASMCs after knockdown of SLC1A5 by siRNA with or without IL-1β stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 8C), whereas it was increased by overexpression of SLC1A5 with adenovirus (Supplementary Fig. 8D). In addition, the level of STAT3 acetylation was considerably diminished in the context of glutamine deprivation (Supplementary Fig. 8E). These results suggest that SLC1A5-driven glutamine uptake and its metabolism enhances the level of acetylated STAT3 in VSMCs. It has been reported that acetylated STAT3 can translocate into mitochondria and accelerate the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA through interacting with pyruvate dehydrogenase complex E46. Therefore, we speculated that SLC1A5-mediated STAT3 acetylation could competitively inhibit STAT3 phosphorylation and induce STAT3 to translocate into mitochondria, resulting in a reduction of STAT3 nuclear translocation and subsequent activation, thereby suppressing VSMC inflammation. To test this hypothesis, we isolated mitochondrial and nuclear fractions to detect the acetylated and phosphorylated STAT3 protein expression by western blot analysis. The results showed that acetylated STAT3 in the mitochondria was diminished in SLC1A5-deleted MASMCs, accompanied by the nuclear retention of phosphorylated STAT3 (Supplementary Fig. 8F). In contrast, the nuclear retention of phosphorylated STAT3 was reduced in Ad-Slc1a5-infected MASMCs (Supplementary Fig. 8G). Interestingly, the immunoprecipitation assay showed SLC1A5 overexpression facilitated, while SLC1A5 knockdown or glutamine deprivation attenuated the interactions between acetylated STAT3 and glutaminase 1 (GLS1), a rate-limiting mitochondrial enzyme of glutaminolysis, in MASMCs, with or without IL-1β stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 8C–E). ChIP assay further demonstrated that SLC1A5 largely alleviated IL-1β-induced recruitment of STAT3 to the promoters of inflammatory cytokines (i.e., Il6 and Tnfα) and MMPs (i.e., Mmp2/3/9) (Fig. 7L). Collectively, these findings indicate that SLC1A5 negatively regulates VSMC inflammation by inhibiting STAT3 signaling via the precise control of STAT3 acetylation and subsequent mitochondrial translocation (Fig. 7M).

VSMC-specific Slc1a5 overexpression attenuates Ang II-induced AAD

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of SLC1A5 for AAD in vivo, we assessed the effect of VSMC-specific Slc1a5 overexpression on AAD using an adeno-associated virus serotype 9 vector (AAV9-Slc1a5), which carried a promoter specific to VSMCs (SM22α promoter) and a mouse Slc1a5 DNA sequence. Together with the P2A peptide and green fluorescent protein (GFP) this construct allows for the efficient expression of SLC1A5, being translated as independent functional proteins (Fig. 8A and Supplementary Fig. 9A). The successful and specific transduction of mouse aortas with AAV9 was confirmed by GFP expression in the aortas and the absence of GFP in other organs (Supplementary Fig. 9B), as well as confirmed by GFP immunofluorescence in aortic sections (Supplementary Fig. 9C).

The AAV9-Slc1a5 and control virus (AAV9-GFP) were administered intravenously via the tail vein of Apoe-/- mice and then infused with Ang II or saline two weeks after the AAV9 injection (Fig. 8B). Western blot and immunofluorescence analyses demonstrated that SLC1A5 expression was increased in the tunica media of abdominal aortas from mice receiving AAV9-Slc1a5 (Supplementary Fig. 9D, E). Ang II infusion markedly elevated the systolic blood pressure of AAV9-Slc1a5- and AAV9-GFP-treated mice; however, there was no significant difference in systolic blood pressure between AAV9-Slc1a5- and AAV9-GFP-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 9F). Notably, VSMC-specific Slc1a5 overexpression effectively inhibited AAD formation and decreased the incidence of AAD, mortality, as well as total aortic weight/body weight in Ang II-infused Apoe-/- mice (Figs. 8C–F and Supplementary Fig. 9G). Histological evaluations using H&E, EVG, and Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated a clear link between Slc1a5 overexpression and the attenuated AAD progression, as evidenced by diminished elastin disruption and reduced collagen deposition within the medial layer of the abdominal aorta (Fig. 8G–I). In addition, enforced expression of SLC1A5 in VSMCs substantially suppressed MMP expression and activity, macrophage infiltration (represented by CD68 staining), and inflammatory response (represented by VCAM1 staining) (Fig. 8J–M and Supplementary Fig. 9H). Importantly, the protein levels of elastin and VSMC contractile markers were enhanced in the abdominal aortic tunica media of AAV9-Slc1a5-treated mice (Fig. 8L, M). The increase in the VSMC contractile markers was further confirmed by immunohistochemical staining (Supplementary Fig. 9H). In addition, the expression of acetylation of H3K9 and H3K27 was obviously increased in AAV9-Slc1a5-treated mice with or without Ang II infusion, compared with the controls (Fig. 8L, N and O). Collectively, these findings demonstrate the efficacy of AAV9-Slc1a5 therapy in experimental AAD mice.

Discussion

The role of glutamine metabolism disturbance in cardiovascular diseases is being increasingly appreciated, while its precise role in AAD remains elusive. SLC1A5 controls the transfer of amino acid substrates and is accountable for glutamine transport. Currently, SLC1A5 has been identified to play a key role in glutaminolysis.47 Here, we demonstrated that SLC1A5 expression was significantly downregulated in aortic VSMCs, accompanied by a substantial reduction in plasma glutamine levels from both humans and mice with AAD. The present study provides the evidence for the protective effect of SLC1A5-driven glutamine transport against AAD using two different rodent models with genetic and pharmacological approaches and reveals a regulatory signaling axis of SLC1A5-acetyl-CoA-histone/STAT3 acetylation in both chromatin and post-translational modification for controlling AAD formation (Fig. 9). Collectively, these findings suggest that low levels of SLC1A5 expression or impaired glutamine metabolism in VSMCs could be risk factors for AAD in humans. In light of these findings, we propose a potential therapeutic approach for the treatment of AAD by targeting SLC1A5-driven glutamine influx and metabolism.

Schematic diagram illustrating the proposed mechanisms by which SLC1A5 deficiency contributes to the development of AAD.

SLC1A5 in VSMCs is markedly diminished during AAD. SLC1A5 deficiency in VSMC causes a decrease of glutamine metabolite acetyl-CoA, leading to a decline in histone H3 (lysine 9 and 27) acetylation and subsequent contractile gene transcription, and ultimately to a loss of contractile phenotype. SLC1A5 deficiency also inhibits acetylated signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) mitochondrial translocation and interrupts acetylated STAT3-glutaminase 1 (GLS1) interaction due to the decrease of acetyl-CoA, resulting in STAT3 nuclear translocation and subsequent transcriptional activation of STAT3-dependent pro-inflammatory genes, thereby aggravating the inflammatory response. Enhancing glutamine uptake in VSMCs by enforcing SLC1A5 expression diminishes VSMC phenotypic switch and inflammation and, therefore, preserves VSMC homeostasis and prevents AAD formation in mice.

This study revealed a significant reduction in plasma levels of glutamine and its downstream metabolites (arginine and proline) in patients with aortic aneurysms. Similarly, a previous study has reported that glutamine and its downstream metabolites are reduced, and pathway analysis further revealed that metabolic pathways associated with glutamine, arginine and proline play a crucial role in aortic aneurysm48. Of note, in addition to glutamine and its downstream metabolites, the plasma concentrations of other amino acids, including histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, and methionine, were also reduced. The potential roles of these amino acids in the development and progression of aortic diseases warrant further investigation.

VSMCs are the predominant vascular wall components, and dysregulation of VSMCs is a key characteristic of AAD. VSMC phenotypic switch is of particular importance in AAD because it is associated with the inflammatory response and vascular remodeling of the aortic wall49,50. The phenotypic switch of VSMC has been documented in human and rodent AAD5. Interestingly, we found that SLC1A5 was predominantly expressed in both human and mouse aortic VSMCs. Herein, we focused on the role of SLC1A5 in VSMCs and demonstrated that SLC1A5 is crucial for maintaining VSMC contractile phenotype and homeostasis during AAD using both loss- and gain-of-function approaches. Furthermore, scRNA-seq analysis showed that SLC1A5 expression was mainly found in the VSMCs and partly in fibroblasts and neutrophils in the aortas (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). In addition, a certain level of fluorescent signal for the SLC1A5 was also observed in endothelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 2D, E). Thus, except for SMCs, it would be meaningful to further explore the role of SLC1A5 in other vascular cell types (e.g., fibroblasts and endothelial cells) as well as immune cells (e.g., neutrophils) in the formation and development of AAD by using respective conditional knockout mice.

Epigenetic modification is one proposed mechanism for regulating gene expression when cells sense the alteration of metabolic status51. For instance, we previously demonstrated that glucose metabolism-driven acetyl-CoA production facilitates histone acetylation, which is responsible for gene induction during macrophage activation52. Similarly, glutamine metabolism-derived acetyl-CoA epigenetically regulated chondrogenic gene expression during bone development53. Furthermore, previous studies demonstrated that acetylation of H3K9 and H3K27 facilitated the SRF-MYOCD complex to access the CarG boxes in the promoters of VSMC contractile genes36,54. In a prior study, the downregulation of myocardial SLC1A5 expression in response to TNF-α or heart failure has been linked with a reduction in cellular glutamine uptake and the impaired cellular glutamine homeostasis14, which is in accordance with our current findings that SLC1A5 expression and glutamine levels were decreased in IL-1β-treated VSMCs or AAD samples and consistent with the notion that SLC1A5 is essential for cellular glutamine homeostasis55. Importantly, we found that SLC1A5-driven glutamine uptake and its metabolism drove acetyl-CoA production, leading to histone H3 acetylation in VSMCs, thus preserving the VSMC contractile phenotype. Specifically, we describe that SLC1A5 facilitated histone H3 acetylation and, in turn, recruited acetylated H3, especially H3K9ac and H3K27ac, to P300 and SRF, thereby enhancing the expression of VSMC contractile genes. Therefore, prevention of VSMC phenotypic switch via epigenetic regulation may be a crucial mechanism underlying SLC1A5-mediated protective effects on AAD development. Of note, a previous study by Osman et al.20 has shown that TEAD1-induced SLC1A5 expression enhances coronary artery SMC proliferation by activating mTORC1 signaling, thereby promoting arterial injury-induced neointima formation. Nevertheless, our findings show that SLC1A5 preserves the contractile phenotype of aortic SMCs via epigenetic modification, consequently inhibiting AAD formation. It seems paradoxical that SLC1A5 maintains SMC contractile plasticity while promoting its proliferation. These divergent roles of SLC1A5 in the regulation of VSMC phenotype are likely to be attributed to the distinct microenvironment and pathologic state, different embryonic origins of SMCs, and the complexity of both interspecies and disease-specific regulatory signals, indicating context-dependent biological functions of SLC1A5 in SMCs.

Another major finding of the present work is the demonstration that SLC1A5 negatively regulates VSMC inflammation. In addition to the control of phenotypic switch, the occurrence of arterial wall inflammation following SLC1A5 loss in VSMCs could be attributable to other mechanisms. STAT3 was activated in both human and murine aortic aneurysm56,57, and direct or indirect inhibition of STAT3 signaling showed protective effects in experimental aortic aneurysm, as evidenced by downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and MMPs and decreased inflammatory cell infiltration44,58. Here, we demonstrated that SLC1A5-mediated glutamine uptake reduced MMP2/9 activity and expression in VSMCs, thereby conferring protection against AAD. This finding aligns with the well-established paradigm that increased MMP expression and activity promote AAD progression59–61. However, we acknowledge that several studies using genetic knockout models have reported that MMP2 or MMP9 deficiency can paradoxically exacerbate Ang II-induced aortic aneurysm62,63. The protease network exhibits significant complexity and functional redundancy. Global genetic deletion of specific MMPs (e.g., MMP2/9) might disrupt the delicate balance of the extracellular matrix homeostasis, potentially triggering compensatory upregulation of other proteases (e.g., MMP3, MMP12)41,64, leading to maladaptive remodeling rather than protective compensation. In contrast, the protective effect of SLC1A5 against AAD likely involves partial and regulated suppression of MMP activity and expression, thereby preserving their essential physiological functions and avoiding detrimental compensatory pathways. Furthermore, methodological differences may contribute to the conflicting results. For instance, the Mmp9-/- mice used in a previous study reporting aggravated aneurysms were on a mixed background of 129/SvEv and C57BL/663, whereas our experiments were conducted in the C57BL/6 J strain. The potential influence of the genetic background on these phenotypic outcomes warrants further consideration.

Our findings highlight that SLC1A5 plays an essential role in alleviating VSMC inflammation through decreasing STAT3 activation. Mechanistic investigation indicated that SLC1A5-driven glutamine metabolism promoted STAT3 acetylation to facilitate its mitochondrial translocation, thereby decreasing its phosphorylation (Tyr705) and nuclear translocation and subsequent transcriptional activation in VSMCs. Phosphorylation of Tyr705 is essential for STAT3’s binding to the promoter and subsequent transcriptional activation of target gene expression in the nucleus65. Many conserved lysine residues (e.g., K685, K707 and K709) flanking this most critical phospho-tyrosine residue of STAT3’s C-terminal domain have been previously identified by different groups46,66. Thus, in addition to increased mitochondrial translocation, another possible explanation for decreased phosphorylation (Tyr705) in Ad-Slc1a5-infected VSMCs would be that SLC1A5-mediated STAT3 acetylation on adjacent sites competitively inhibits STAT3 Tyr705 phosphorylation. We further confirmed that SLC1A5 facilitated the interaction of acetylated STAT3 and GLS1 in the mitochondria, implying that SLC1A5 might affect GLS1-mediated glutaminolysis, thus forming a STAT3 acetylation-GLS1-acetyl-CoA feedback loop to reinforce the anti-inflammatory effect of SLC1A5. Moreover, emerging evidence reveals the protective effect of STAT3 acetylation and mitochondrial STAT3 activation in cardiomyocytes67–69. Therefore, it remains to be determined how SLC1A5 orchestrates STAT3 acetylation and mitochondrial metabolism to govern VSMC inflammation. Overall, our work reveals a pathophysiological mechanism by SLC1A5 loss-induced STAT3 activation that contributes to VSMC inflammation in AAD.

There are several limitations in this study. First, although our data suggest that SLC1A5 preserves VSMC contractile phenotype via the glutaminolysis-histone acetylation axis, stable isotopic tracers (e.g., ¹³C-glutamine) combined with liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) in SLC1A5 genetic deficiency animal AAD models are needed to determine whether acetyl-CoA derived from glutamine directly fuels histone acetylation. Glutamine metabolism branches into multiple interconnected pathways (e.g., GSH synthesis, biosynthesis, TCA anaplerosis). The ¹³C-glutamine tracing is needed to define how glutamine distribution shifts in AAD and whether alternative pathways compensate for SLC1A5 deficiency. Second, the segment-specific functions and cell-type-specific mechanisms of SLC1A5 in the aorta remained to be elucidated. Therefore, it is imperative to utilize a dual-recombinase-mediated intersectional genetic system targeting VSMCs from various developmental origins in mice and selectively knock out of SLC1A5 to investigate the impact of SLC1A5 signaling in vascular diseases and other related fields. Third, the lack of epitope-tagged MYOCD tool mice and reliable antibodies precluded us from investigating its role in the SLC1A5-SRF regulatory axis. Future studies should generate epitope-tagged MYOCD mice70 or highly specific antibodies to define the role of MYOCD in SLC1A5-mediated contractile phenotype regulation.

In summary, our study revealed an impaired glutamine metabolism in AAD and comprehensively demonstrated that SLC1A5-driven glutamine uptake in VMSCs prevents AAD formation via epigenetic regulation of VSMC homeostasis. SLC1A5-mediated glutamine metabolism controls the chromatin state of VSMC contractile genes and STAT3 inactivation, providing insight into the mechanism of VSMC plasticity in the pathogenesis of AAD. Thus, identification of the vasculoprotective role of VSMC-derived SLC1A5 in AAD suggests that targeting SLC1A5-directed glutamine metabolism could be a potential therapeutic strategy for AAD.

Methods

Ethical statement

The human tissue sample collection and analysis were approved by the Cantonal Ethics Committee of Zurich, Switzerland and the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China Ethics Committee. All animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Jinan University in accordance with the institutional guidelines.

Human tissue specimens

Human aortic tissues were procured in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. There was no compensation to the donors for participation in the study. Five human aneurysmal tissue samples were obtained from patients undergoing open aortic surgical repair at the Department of Vascular Surgery (University Hospital Zurich), and five controls were obtained from organ donors were collected during kidney transplantation from organ donors, provided by the Department of Surgery and Transplantation (University Hospital Zurich). Sample collection and analysis were approved by the Cantonal Ethics Committee of Zurich, Switzerland (BASEC-Nr. 2020-00378 and 2020-00992). The paraffin-embedded human sample sections (7 µm thickness) were utilized for immunofluorescent staining. Five human aortic dissection tissues were collected from patients with type A aortic dissection having emergency cardiothoracic surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China. In addition, five control ascending aortic tissues were obtained from organ donors with cerebral hemorrhage and brain death to heart transplantation, and the aortic tissue of the ascending aortic incision edge underwent aortic valve replacement. This study was approval by the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China Ethics Committee (2021-KY-079). Samples were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin and then sectioned into human sample sections of 7 µm thickness for immunofluorescent staining. The human clinical information for the patients is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Mouse generation and breeding

Slc1a5 flox/flox mice were generated by inserting loxP sites to flank exon 2 and exon 8 of the Slc1a5 gene (NCBI Reference Sequence NM_009201.2) using CRISPR-Cas9 technology by GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China). Slc1a5 flox/flox mice were then crossed with Myh11Cre/ERT2 mice (The Jackson Laboratory, Stock No. 019079) to obtain Slc1a5 flox/flox/Myh11Cre/ERT2. As previously described71,72, Slc1a5 flox/flox mice were crossed with Myh11Cre/ERT2 to obtain Slc1a5 flox/WT/Myh11Cre/ERT2 mice. The Myh11 Cre allele was inserted into the Y chromosome, precluding the utilization of Cre-negative mice as controls73. Male Slc1a5 flox/WT/Myh11Cre/ERT2 were then crossed with female Slc1a5 flox/WT to generate Slc1a5 flox/flox/Myh11Cre/ERT2 and Slc1a5 WT/WT/Myh11Cre/ERT2 male mice, which were administered tamoxifen to obtain VSMC-specific knockout (Slc1a5 iΔVSMC) and the littermate controls (Myh11Cre/ERT2) mice, respectively.

Slc1a5 global knockout mice (Slc1a5 KO, Strain NO. T011570) and Apoe-/- (Strain NO. T001458) mice with the C57BL/6 J genetic background were purchased from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China). Slc1a5 -/- mice were crossed with C57BL/6 J mice to generate Slc1a5 +/- mice and then were crossed with Slc1a5 +/- to generate Slc1a5 -/- mice (experimental, Slc1a5 KO) and Slc1a5 +/+ mice (littermate controls, Slc1a5 WT). Mice were housed and kept under standard lighting settings with a 12 h on/off cycle, 23 ± 2 °C temperature and 50–60% humidity range in the Jinan University. All mice were provided with ad libitum access to food (GDMLAC, GDMLAC-260) and water unless otherwise stated.

Tamoxifen injection

Tamoxifen (ST1681, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was dissolved in corn oil (C805618, Macklin, Shanghai, China) and administered intraperitoneally to 8-week mice for 5 consecutive days at a dosage of 75 mg/kg per body weight. Two weeks following the final injection, the mini-osmotic pumps (Model 2004, Alzet, Durect Corporation, USA) with angiotensin II (Ang II, ALX-151-039-M025, Enzo Life Sciences, NY, USA) or physiological saline were subcutaneously implanted to mice.

Murine AAD model

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with a protocol approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Jinan University (20240925-05). Humane endpoints were established for instances of reduced appetite, body weight loss over 20%, hypothermia (colonic temperature below 34 °C), infection at the site of osmotic mini-pump implantation, or indications of organ failure (e.g., agonal breathing, cyanosis, or severe vomiting). Upon the manifestation of any of these symptoms, the animals were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% in oxygen) and subsequently euthanized via cervical dislocation. Given that male sex is one of the key risk factors for AAD in humans, all mice used in this study were derived from male mice.

To establish a PCSK9/Ang II-induced AAD mouse model32, 8- to 12-week Slc1a5 KO, Slc1a5 iΔVSMC and their littermate control male mice were used. All mouse genomic DNA was genotyped by PCR amplification with specific primers provided in Supplementary Table 2. The mice were injected intravenously with adeno-associated virus serotype (AAV) 8 mediated expression of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (a gain-of-function mutation PCSK9 D377Y (WZ Biosciences Inc., Shandong, China) at a dose of 2 × 1011 vector genomes per mouse and fed a Western diet (GDMLAC, GDMLAC-102). Two weeks following AAV8 injection, mice were implanted subcutaneously with Ang II (1000 ng/kg/min) or physiological saline (0.9% sodium chloride) by Alzet mini-osmotic pumps (Alzet, Model 2004, Durect Corporation, CA, USA) for 4 weeks.

To generate β-aminopropionitrile (BAPN, A3134-25g, Sigma, St Louis, MO, USA) plus Ang II-induced AAD model, 10- to 12-week Slc1a5 iΔVSMC and their littermate control male mice were simultaneously implanted with mini-osmotic pumps (Alzet, model 2002 for BAPN, model 2004 for Ang II) with BAPN (150 mg/kg/day) for 14 days plus Ang II (1,000 ng/kg/min) for 28 days, respectively.

To further explore the effect of SLC1A5 overexpression on AAD. AAV9-SM22ap-Slc1a5-EGFP-3Flag (AAV9-Slc1a5) was purchased by Genechem (Shanghai, China). AAV9-SM22ap-EGFP-3Flag (AAV9-GFP) was used as a control. Briefly, 8-week male Apoe-/- mice were injected intravenously with AAV9-Slc1a5 or AAV9-GFP at a dose of 2 × 1011 vector genomes per mouse. After 2 weeks of injection, mice were randomly grouped and infused with Ang II or saline for another 4 weeks.

Mice systolic blood pressure (SBP) was monitored before and after Ang II administration utilizing the non-invasive BP-2000 System (Visitech Systems, Inc., NC). After SBP measurement, mice were euthanized with an inhalation overdose of isoflurane, and the aortas were collected for AAD analysis. The aortic diameter increased by 50% or more than its adjacent intact aortic segment was deemed to be aneurysmal74,75. Any mice that died before the study’s endpoint were necropsied, and the presence of blood clots exuding outside the adventitia of the dilated aorta wall indicated aneurysm rupture. The rupture incidence is defined as the percentage of mice that died with histologically confirmed aortic rupture. This calculation excludes both surviving animals and those that died from causes other than aortic rupture.

Classification of AAD severity

As Luo’s and Liu’s studies described31,76, AAD was defined as the existence of an aneurysm, dissection, or rupture. Severe AAD was characterized by dissection or rupture. Aneurysmal tissue was assessed by three independent observers who were blinded and agreed on the differences in case of discrepancy.

In vivo ultrasound imaging

To characterize the AAD in vivo, the VINNO6 ultrasound diagnostic system (VINNO corporation, Suzhou, China) was used to obtain B-Mode or M-Mode data of the aortic ascending, arch and suprarenal aortas from mice. Aortic dilation was ascertained by measuring the aortic diameter in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The identical values observed in some measurements are a result of the inherent spatial resolution and pixel-based calculation of the ultrasound system (VINNO6). After imaging, mice were euthanized using an overdose of isoflurane inhalation, perfused with PBS, and the aortas were isolated to confirm the development of AAD, and the maximal aortic diameters were measured by digital vernier caliper.

Wire myography

In brief, mice were euthanized using an overdose of inhaltant isoflurane, and the dissected thoracic aortas were isolated from Slc1a5 WT and Slc1a5 KO and immediately placed in Krebs-Hensleit’s Solution. After removing connective tissue, the aortas were cut into rings of 3 mm width and mounted onto an isometric wire myograph system (620 M, Danish Myo Technology A/S, Aarhus, Denmark), gassed with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 and maintained at 37 °C77. Maximum responses to 60 mM KCl and concentration-response curves to phenylephrine (PE, HY-B0769, MCE, 10−9 to 10−4 M) were performed as previously described78. Constriction was measured as tension and expressed in millinewton (mN) values.

Isolation and culture of primary VSMCs

Mouse aortic smooth muscle cells (MASMCs) were isolated from male C57BL/6 J, Slc1a5 WT and Slc1a5 KO mice aortas as we previously described71. Briefly, mice were euthanized with an overdose of isoflurane inhalation, and the whole aorta of mice was carefully dissected after in situ perfusion with sterile phosphate buffer solution (PBS). The tissues were removed the adventitia and scraped off endothelium after treating with collagenase type II (1 mg/mL, LS004176, Worthington Biochemical Corporation, NJ, USA) in Hank’s balanced salt solution (CR14175, Cienry, Zhejiang, China) at 37 °C for 8 min. The tissues were then cut into 1-2 mm pieces and treated with collagenase type II and 0.5% elastase (LS002279, Worthington Biochemical Corporation, NJ, USA) at 37 °C for 1 h. MASMCs were cultured with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (CR-12800, Cienry, Zhejiang, China) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (SE100-B, Vistech, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (C0224, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) in a CO2 incubator. MASMCs from passages 3 to 6 were utilized for subsequent experiments.

Nonrecombinant AAV plasmid generation

A 1738 bp double-stranded DNA fragment, containing the 5’ Kozak sequence, mouse SLC1A5 cDNA (NM_009201.2, 1665 bp) and the 3’ 2 A self-cleaving peptide (P2A), was synthesized. The SLC1A5-P2A sequence and vector pAAV-SM22ap-EGFP-3Flag-SV40 polyA (Genechem, Shanghai) were digested by EcoRI and AgeI restriction enzymes, and complete cloning was done through the in-fusion recombination method to generate the nonrecombinant vector pAAV-pSM22α-mSLC1A5-P2A-EGFP-3Flag-SV40pA. The plasmid map was shown in Supplementary Fig. 9A.

siRNA and adenovirus experiments

The siRNA targeting mouse Slc1a5 (siSlc1a5) and negative control siRNA (siCtrl) were provided by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The sequences used were as follows: negative control siRNA sense, 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′ and antisense, 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′; Slc1a5 siRNA sense, 5′-GCCUGCCUGUGAAGGACAUTT-3′ and antisense, 5′-AUGUCCUUCACAGGCAGGCTT-3′. The interfering efficiency was confirmed using RT-qPCR and western blot, and used for further studies. To investigate the role of Slc1a5 in MASMCs, MASMCs from C57BL/6 J were transfected with siSlc1a5 or siCtrl by using LipofectamineTM RNAiMAX reagent (13778150, Invitrogen, CA, USA), and utilized for further studies 48 hours following siRNA transfection. In some studies, MASMCs were stimulated with vehicle or 15 ng/mL IL-1β (200-01B, PeproTech, Inc., NJ, USA) at indicated time points.

For Slc1a5 overexpression, MASMCs were infected with adenoviral vectors encoding mouse Slc1a5 (Ad-Slc1a5) or the negative control (Ad-GFP) at 10 multiplicity of infection (WZ Biosciences, Inc., Shandong, China), and utilized for further studies 48 h following adenovirus infection. In some studies, MASMCs were stimulated with 15 ng/mL IL-1β at indicated time points.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from MASMCs or aortic tissues using TrizolTM reagent (15596018, Invitrogen, CA, USA) following the instruction manual. The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed by ReverTra AceTM qPCR RT kit (FSQ-101, TOYOBO, Japan), and then performed in LightCycler 480 system (Roche Diagnostics, USA) by PolarSignal®SYBR Green mix Taq (MKG900-10, MIKX Biology, Shenzhen, China). Relative gene mRNA expression was analyzed with the 2−ΔΔCT method. β-actin was used as the endogenous control. The specific primers were listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Protein extraction and western blot analysis

Cultured cells and tissue were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) or 4 × SDS loading buffer (histone western blot) containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail and phosphatase inhibitors. In certain experiments, adventitial tissue was removed from the aorta as much as possible. Due to the substantial tissue loss incurred during the meticulous dissection and subsequent processing steps required to isolate the tunica media and to ensure sufficient protein yield for Western blot analysis, certain sample for Western blot was pooled from different mouse aortas within the same experimental cohort. Western blot analysis was performed with a chemiluminescent imaging system (Tanon 5200SF, Tanon Science & Technology, Shanghai, China), and the quantitative analysis were quantified with Image J software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA), and normalized protein levels to α-Tubulin or β-Actin. Phosphorylated protein levels were normalized to their total protein and then to α-Tubulin or β-Actin. The following primary antibodies were used for Western blot analysis: α-Actin (1:500, sc-56499), MMP2 (1:500, sc-13595), and P300 (1:500, sc-48343) were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Acetyl-Lysine (1:1000, 9681), H3K9ac (1:1000, 9649S), H3K27ac (1:1000, 8173), Histone H3 (1:1000, 14269), Phospho-NF-κB p65 (1:1000, 3033), Phospho-Stat3 (Tyr705) (1:1000, 9145S), STAT3 (1:1000, 9132) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. Calponin 1 (1:1000, 13938-1-AP), GAPDH (1:20000, 6004-1-Ig), GLS1 (1:20000, 12855-1-AP), MMP3 (1:1000, 66338-1-Ig), SRF (1:1000, 16821-1-AP), α-Tubulin (1:10000, 11224-1-AP) or β-Actin (1:20000, 66009-1-Ig) were purchased from Proteintech Group. MMP9 (1:1000, ab38898) and NF-κB p65 (1:1000, ab7970) were purchase from Abcam. Elastin (1:1000, 45078) were purchase from Signalway Antibody. SLC1A5 (1:1000, AF6610) were purchase from Affinity, Transgelin (1:3000, GTX628831) were purchase from GeneTex. HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L, 1:10000, 511103 and Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L, 1:10000, 511203) were purchase from ZENBIO.

Mitochondrial, nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extraction

After treatment with Slc1a5 siRNA or adenovirus for 48 hours, with or without IL-1β stimulation, MASMC mitochondrial fraction was isolated and lysed following the manufacturer’s protocols (C3601, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Nuclear, and cytoplasmic protein was extracted according to the manufacturer’s recommendations (P0027, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China).

Co-immunoprecipitation

Total cell proteins were extracted with lysis buffer (P0013, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), and quantified by the BCA assay. Protein A/G magnetic beads (HY-K0202, MedChemExpress, NJ, USA) were preincubated with all samples on a rotator at 4 °C for 3 h. For immunoprecipitation, 1 mg protein was incubated with 1 µg antibody on a rotator overnight at 4 °C. Protein A/G magnetic beads were added and treated at 4 °C for 2 h. The precipitated complex was eluted and analyzed by western blot. Isotype IgG served as a negative control.

CUT&Tag assay

CUT&Tag assays in nuclei from MASMCs were performed using Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina Pro (TD904, Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, MASMCs treated with scrambled and Slc1a5 siRNA were collected and bound to concanavalin A-coated magnetic beads. MASMCs were then suspended and incubated with H3K9ac antibodies (1:50, 9649S, Cell Signaling Technology) or isotype IgG (1:100, 2729S, Cell Signaling Technology) overnight at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. The samples were incubated with pA/G-Tnp for transposition. After tagmentation, DNA was extracted and purified to construct a library. The library was sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq XPlus.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR

After treating with Slc1a5 siRNA or Ad-Slc1a5 for 48 hours, MASMCs were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 minutes, then quenched with 125 mM glycine for 5 min at room temperature. The cross-linked samples were lysed and then sonicated to generate 200 to 1000 base pairs (bp) fragments. Chromatin fragments were centrifuged at 15,493 × g for 5 min. For immunoprecipitation, diluted supernatants were incubated on a rotator overnight at 4 °C with Protein A/G magnetic beads pre-bound with anti-H3K9ac, anti-H3K27ac (1:50, 8173S, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-STAT3 (1:50, 9132, Cell Signaling Technology), SRF (1:100, 16821-1-AP, Proteintech Group) or negative control IgG. A total of 200 μL eluate was obtained after washing and shaking the beads. After reverse crosslinking, DNA samples were purified using the Hipure PCR Pure Mico Kit (D2120, Magen Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Guangdong, China), and subjected to quantitative PCR. Primers for ChIP-qPCR were presented in Supplementary Table 4.

Cholesterol, triglycerides, low- and high-density lipoproteins cholesterol measurement

Blood samples from Slc1a5 KO and Slc1a5 i∆VSMC mice under PCSK9 administration were collected following a 12- to 14 h fast. Plasma triglycerides, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were processed as per the manufacturer’s protocols (A111-1-1, A110-1-1, A113-1-1 or A112-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China), respectively.

Glutamine and Acetyl-CoA determination

Plasma from Apoe-/- mice following 4 weeks of saline or Ang II infusion, and MASMCs lysis was collected for glutamine and acetyl coenzyme A (Acetyl-CoA) content measurement. Glutamine content was measured using the glutamine assay kit (ab197011, Abcam, USA) following the manufacturer’s recommendations, and calculated after measuring absorbance at 565 nm. Acetyl-CoA content was measured by the Acetyl-CoA Assay kit (MAK039, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) following the manufacturer’s manual. Sample fluorescence intensity was detected at λex = 535/ λem = 587 nm.

Histological analysis

The abdominal aortic tissues, unless otherwise specified, were embedded in paraffin after being fixed with paraformaldehyde (P0099, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), and the tissue sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin staining (HE), Verhoeff’s Van Gieson staining (EVG), and Masson’s trichrome staining (Masson). All images were visualized using a biological microscope (Olympus, BX53). Elastin degradation was scored base on degradation-grading (1, less than 25% degradation; 2, 25–50% degradation; 3, 50–75% degradation; and 4, more than 75% degradation)79. Histological analysis of stained sections was performed in a blinded manner.

For immunohistochemical staining, the paraffin-embedded aortic sections were dewaxed, endogenous peroxidase inactivated, and epitope retrieved. After blocking, primary antibodies against SLC1A5 (1:200, AF6610, Affinity), α-Actin (1:200, sc-56499, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), Calponin 1 (1:100, 13938-1-AP, Proteintech Group), MMP2 (1:200, sc-13595, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), MMP3 (1:1000, 66338-1-Ig, Proteintech Group), MMP9 (1:100, ab38898, Abcam), CD68 (1:1000, 28058-1-AP, Proteintech Group) or VCAM1 (1:100, 383318, ZENBIO) were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The sections were then stained with biotinylated secondary antibodies. Positive antibody binding was visualized using the DAB kit (SK4100; Vector Labs, CA, USA). After staining with hematoxylin, sections were imaged by Olympus, BX53. Isotype IgG was stained as a negative control. The immunohistochemistry staining was quantified by Image J software.

Immunofluorescence analysis

The paraffin-embedded human aortic sections, mouse aortic sections and cultured cells were incubated with primary antibodies against SLC1A5 (1:200, 5345S, Cell Signaling Technology), α-Actin (1:200, sc-56499, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), CD31(1:50, DIA-310, dianova) or Transgelin (1:200, GTX628831, GeneTex) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, secondary fluorescent antibodies (1:400 dilution each, DyLight 488 goat anti-mouse IgG, A23210, DyLight 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG, A23220, DyLight 594 goat anti-rabbit IgG, A23420, DyLight 594 goat anti-rat IgG, A23440, DyLight 649 goat anti-rabbit IgG, A23210, Abbkine) were used for 1 hour in the dark. Sections were then stained with 2-(4-Amidinophenyl)-6-indolecarbamidine dihydrochloride (C1006, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), then mounted with antifade mounting medium (P0128M, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Isotype IgG was used as a negative control.

In situ matrix metalloproteinase activity

To measure the matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activity, the frozen abdominal aortic sections (7 µm thickness) were used for in situ zymography as previously described80. In brief, frozen aortic sections were treated with a fluorescein-conjugated gelatin substrate (DQ gelatin, D12054, Invitrogen, CA, USA) at 37 °C overnight in the dark. Sections were counterstained with DAPI and then captured by a Zeiss confocal microscope (LSM800, Oberkochen, Germany). ImageJ software was used to quantitative analysis the fluorescence intensity. The section incubated with IgG was served as the negative control.

RNA-sequencing analysis

Apoe-/- mice were treated with Ang II (1,000 ng/kg/min) or physiological saline (0.9% sodium chloride) for 7 days. Aortic adventitial tissues were removed as much as possible, and the aortic total RNA (1-2 µg) was used for preparing cDNA libraries. The libraries were performed as we described previously72. In brief, the libraries were sequenced by Novogene (Beijing, China). The gene expression was quantified in fragments per kilobase million fragments (FPKM) using Cuffdiff v2.1.1. The calculated P-value was adjusted by using the Benjamini and Hochberg method. Adjusted P-value < 0.05 was identified as differentially expressed.

Bioinformatics analysis of public dataset

For public expression profilings, the differential expression analysis was analyzed using R 4.4.2. Genes with FDR-adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered significant. The gene sets of glutamine transporters and glutamate metabolism-associated genes were available from GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/) and visualized by the heatmap function.

For public single-cell RNA sequencing analysis, the Seurat package (version 4.0.3) in the R environment (version 4.1.1) was used as we described previously72. In brief, the CreateSeuratObject function was performed to create Seurat objects, retaining genes detected in more than 3 cells and cells with more than 200 distinct genes. The quality control method filtered out cells with less than 500 or more than 5000 detected genes, less than 1000 or more than 50,000 total RNA counts, and more than 20% mitochondrial genes. Subsequently, the data were normalized and processed into further analyses. The identified clusters in two dimensions were visualized by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection. Clustered cells were assigned to corresponding cell types by comparing the gene signatures of cell clusters.

Statistics and reproducibility

All sample sizes are reflected in the figure legends. Data are shown as mean ± SEM unless otherwise stated. Data were first evaluated for distribution normality using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test and the equality of variances. Two-group comparisons were performed by a two-tailed Student t test or a nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test (non-normal distribution). Comparisons among multiple groups were used by one-way ANOVA with the Bonferroni post hoc test or two-way ANOVA with the Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Chi-square test or the Fisher exact test was used for bivariate comparisons of categorical variables. The Kaplan-Meier method was performed for survival curves and log rank test to compare differences. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism (La Jolla, CA, USA). P < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were chosen based on the available number of mice and human data. No data were excluded from the analysis. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups matched for genotype, age and body weight. All outcome assessments, including ultrasound and histology, were performed blinded to group allocation to prevent bias. All data were analyzed in a blinded way.
