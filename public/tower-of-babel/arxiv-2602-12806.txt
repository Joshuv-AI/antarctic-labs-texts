RAT-Bench: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Text Anonymization


Abstract
Data containing personal information is increasingly used to train, fine-tune, or query Large Language Models (LLMs). Text is typically scrubbed of identifying information prior to use, often with tools such as Microsoft’s Presidio or Anthropic’s PII purifier. These tools have traditionally been evaluated on their ability to remove specific identifiers (e.g., names), yet their effectiveness at preventing re-identification remains unclear. We introduce RAT-Bench, a comprehensive benchmark for text anonymization tools based on re-identification risk. Using U.S. demographic statistics, we generate synthetic text containing various direct and indirect identifiers across domains, languages, and difficulty levels. We evaluate a range of NER- and LLM-based text anonymization tools and, based on the attributes an LLM-based attacker is able to correctly infer from the anonymized text, we report the risk of re-identification in the U.S. population, while properly accounting for the disparate impact of identifiers. We find that, while capabilities vary widely, even the best tools are far from perfect in particular when direct identifiers are not written in standard ways and when indirect identifiers enable re-identification. Overall we find LLM-based anonymizers, including new iterative anonymizers, to provide a better privacy-utility trade-off albeit at a higher computational cost. Importantly, we also find them to work well across languages. We conclude with recommendations for future anonymization tools and will release the benchmark and encourage community efforts to expand it, in particular to other geographies.

Anonymizing text is a difficult task that goes beyond removing obvious explicit direct identifiers. Instead, anonymization tools should strongly limit the risk of re-identification of a piece of text, from obvious direct identifiers to more complex direct identifiers and indirect identifiers. Research in the 90’s indeed already flagged that zip code, date of birth and gender uniquely identifies 87% of Americans

To properly evaluate text anonymization, measure progress and align with legal standards, benchmarks must assess residual re-identification risk. This requires accounting for a broad range of direct and indirect identifiers, varying the difficulty by which they appear in the text, across domains and languages, and supporting the evaluation of strong, adaptive anonymizers. While a range of benchmarks have been proposed, each lack key components needed for comprehensive evaluation.
Indeed, they either rely on limited sets of manually annotated attributes
(

;

Further, because benchmarks usually specify a fixed, pre-defined set of identifiers, anonymizers may overfit to them – seeming strong on benchmark tasks while failing to generalize to other attributes. Finally, most benchmarks focus on English texts, making it unclear how anonymization tools transfer to other languages.

Contribution.
We introduce RAT-Bench, a comprehensive, extendable, multilingual benchmark to evaluate text anonymization tools and their ability to mitigate re-identification risk from direct and indirect identifiers.

We first generate synthetic texts grounded in real-world demographics by sampling indirect identifiers (e.g. date of birth, race) from the 1% Public Use Microdata Sample (PUMS)
(
75

We then augment these records with consistent direct identifiers (e.g., names sampled from empirical distributions conditioned on gender and age, addresses sampled for the right geography). From these, we generate text, specifying the target language, in which the identifiers are deliberately mentioned. To stress-test anonymization tools and track progress, we consider attributes that are mentioned

\mathcal{R}=1

;

59

.

We then compare how existing tools reduce the risk. While, on average, tools reduce the risk (Figure

2(a)

Performance further degrades as identifiers become harder to detect with the risk increasing from 44% for explicitly mentioned, standard-form identifiers (easy) to 69% for non-standard mentions (hard) (Table

Recent methods propose to iteratively anonymize text using feedback from an LLM attacker

We conclude with actionable guidance for users and developers of text anonymization tools. For users, we characterize the privacy–utility–cost trade-offs and highlight where tools can fail in practice. For example, NER-based tools are lightweight but may over-redact text or miss identifiers in non-standard form, while LLMs are more precise but computationally heavier. For developers, we identify failure modes and directions for improvement, such as generalization to unseen attributes and speedup of approaches such as the iterative anonymizer. 2  Background and related work

Text anonymization.
Given a text
t
, an anonymization tool
\mathcal{T}
aims to remove personal, sensitive and/or identifiable information, producing anonymized text
t^{a}=\mathcal{T}(t)
.
Named Entity Recognition (NER) has long been the dominant approach for removing sensitive information from text
(
15
)
. NER models are trained to identify and mask spans corresponding to categories such as names or locations (with e.g. ‘**’), yielding an anonymized version of the text. Numerous NER models, such as Flair
(
2
)
or Spacy
(
25
)
, can be used as such to remove identifiers. Many tools further extend NER with rule-based heuristics and regular-expression matching
(
44
;
30
;
5
;
33
)
. While NER-based systems detect clear identifiers such as emails, they might not have been trained for domain-specific contexts
(
66
)
. To address this, specialized scrubbers have been developed for clinical
(
27
;
77
)
and legal data
(
50
)
.
Further work explored adding controlled, word-level perturbations protect the privacy of a given text by adding noise in an embedding space
(
19
)
or sampling replacements from a distance-weighted distribution over candidate words. Recent work has also applied LLMs to anonymization.
4
provides
PII purifier
, a general prompt for LLMs to mask identifying information.
85
develop a more specific prompt for smaller language models such as LLaMA-3.1-8B by listing identifiers considered sensitive or personal.
3
shows GPT-3.5/4 can be used to de-identify clinical notes, while
37
prompt GPT-4 with HIPAA guidelines for zero-shot medical text de-identification.
17
fine-tunes an LLM to replace self-disclosures of PII, and
14
use LLMs to annotate PII before distilling into smaller NER models. Other approaches use LLMs to also go beyond masking.
70
develop Clio, a privacy-preserving text summarization tool, while
68
iteratively reword text to remove identifiers and defined contextual clues and
80
add another LLM to better balance privacy and utility. Lastly,
21
propose replacing attributes with plausible alternatives to mislead adversaries.

Anonymization from a privacy law perspective.
Most privacy regimes regard anonymous data to be out-of-scope of privacy laws (privacy laws do not apply to them, including when transferred internationally). This includes EU’s
22
, California’s
10
, and others such as Brazil’s
35
or Singapore’s
58
. While legal terminologies (anonymous in the EU vs de-identified in the US) and specific definitions vary, most consider data anonymous if an individual cannot be identified from it. GDPR Rec 26, for instance, uses the “reasonably likely” standard that a natural person cannot be identified “directly or indirectly”. Technically this has long been translated to the removal of direct identifiers such as phone number, email address, or social security number and a low risk of re-identification from indirect identifiers, such as gender, ethnicity, or hometown, where “low” is defined for each setting separately. The UK’s standard for health data considers a dataset anonymous if it satisfies
k
-anonymity with
k=5

(
48
;
74
)
, meaning that, even with full knowledge of the recorded attributes, an attacker can narrow the set of potential matches to no fewer than 5 in the worst case. Interestingly, the UK’s recent guidance for the motivated intruder test specifically requires considering the risk of re-identification posed by LLMs
(
74
)
. Some legal regimes, such as EU’s GDPR, also define data where direct identifiers have been removed as pseudonymous data. Pseudonymous data, however, remains within the scope of privacy laws (privacy laws apply to them). Finally, PII (Personally Identifiable Information) removal is used as a term by some of the tools, notably Presidio
(
44
)
and Azure
(
5
)
. While GDPR or CCPA do not use the term, the US Office of Management and Budget, part of the Executive Office of the President, defines PII as data that “distinguish or trace an individual’s identity, either alone or when combined with other personal or identifying information that is linked or linkable to a specific individual” and mandates, similarly to privacy laws, a case-by-case assessment of the risk of re-identification taking into account “other available information, [that] could be used to identify an individual”
(
73
)
.

Evaluation.
Text anonymization is typically evaluated using recall, i.e. the proportion of manually annotated terms correctly detected
(
39
)
. However, such annotations require a unique ground truth, while private information is often
context dependent, not identifiable, and not discrete

(
6
)
.
To broaden coverage,
60
expand manually annotated spans in specific court cases, while
39
train a classifier to link anonymized documents to those available as background knowledge, thereby addressing disclosure risk.
It has also been shown that LLMs can infer personal attributes from text
(
57
;
67
;
36
)
, a capability used to evaluate anonymization methods
(
67
;
82
)
. Beyond human-annotated datasets, synthetic data has been used for evaluation:
82
generate Reddit-style text seeded with synthetic profiles, while
66
augment NER benchmarks with LLM-generated variations.

Alternatives.
To enable corpus-level text processing with privacy guarantees, prior work has explored generating synthetic data under differential privacy
(
40
;
81
;
32
)
. While this has shown promising results for simple settings
(
43
)
, it comes with an intricate privacy-utility trade-off
(
41
)
and requires access to many documents. Instead, we here focus on anonymizing individual text samples.

Motivation.
To properly evaluate text anonymization tools, measure progress and align with legal standards, benchmarks must assess residual re-identification risk. This requires accounting for a broad range of direct and indirect identifiers, varying the difficulty by which they appear in the text, across domains and languages, and supporting the evaluation of strong, adaptive anonymizers. While prior benchmarks for text anonymization exist
(
63
;
78
;
79
;
60
;
86
;
82
;
38
;
66
)
, they exhibit several important limitations.

First, some benchmarks
(
60
;
86
)
rely on manually annotated, real-world text. While this captures realistic language and identifiers, these benchmarks are limited in the range of identifiers they consider due to privacy constraints when releasing. They are moreover extremely costly to extend to other identifiers, domains or languages and not allow for varying levels of difficulty.

Alternatively,
82
;
38
;
66
use fully synthetic identifiers and text, enabling controlled variation in difficulty and context. However, these benchmarks often focus on indirect identifiers only, are limited to English, and – importantly – do not reflect real-world demographic distributions. As a result, they cannot translate any identifiers that remain in the text after anonymization into a meaningful re-identification risk. Indeed, in practice, an individual’s re-identification risk depends on attribute types, their values, and combinations relative to a broader population: high-entropy attributes (e.g., exact birth dates) pose far greater risk than low-entropy ones (e.g., gender), rare values (e.g., residence in Rhode Island) are more identifying than common ones (e.g., California), and certain combinations can be highly identifying (e.g., a 50-year-old male CEO (unfortunately) has a lower re-identification probability compared to a 30-year-old female one). Benchmarks based solely on synthetic identifiers lack these ground-truth, joint distributions and therefore can not accurately measure risk.

Instead, lacking realistic demographics, these benchmarks evaluate anonymization performance by
recall
– the fraction of identifiers in the text that are successfully masked. However, recall assigns equal weight to all attributes and values. As a result, a method may achieve high recall by consistently removing generic attributes or common values, while still leaving substantial re-identification risk by missing high-entropy attributes, rare values, or identifying combinations.

To address this, we present an end-to-end benchmark grounded in real-world demographics enabling the evaluation of re-identification risk in anonymized texts. We use our extensible framework to consider a diverse set of direct and indirect identifiers while properly accounting for their disparate impact on the risk, vary levels of difficulty to measure progress, consider multiple languages and vary the set of indirect identifiers known to strong, adaptive anonymizers to stress-test how they generalize to new settings.

Evaluation metrics.
We instantiate a state-of-the-art attacker to infer attributes from anonymized text

a\in\mathcal{A}
present in the original text

{x}^{*}_{direct}
and

C

\mathcal{R}(x)=1

\mathcal{R}(x)=\kappa_{x}

\mathcal{R}(x)

6  Discussion and conclusion

We find that evaluating anonymization tools solely by recall in removing specific identifiers is insufficient. Even when some identifiers are removed, re-identification risk for an individual may remain high. Our benchmark addresses this by directly measuring re-identification risk through what the best attacker can infer, in line with the legal standard
(
22
;
10
)
.

Our benchmark further provides useful insights for users and developers. Ultimately, for users, choosing the right anonymizer for a given use-case requires balancing trade-offs in privacy, application-specific utility and computational cost. While our results generally agree with prior work
(
68
)
that LLMs are strong anonymizers, allowing methods such as GPT-4.1 (Anthropic) to substantially reduce re-identification risk while maintaining data integrity, they also highlight the difficulty of specifying attributes for methods such as Rescriber and
68
, as they may not generalize well to other identifiers.

For developers, our results point to several areas for future work. First, we believe more emphasis should be placed on indirect identifiers. A significant proportion of the re-identification risk in our benchmark comes from indirect identifiers (Figure
3
), which are often missed by all tested anonymizers (Figure
18
). Second, NER-based anonymization tools should be more robust to unusual representations of identifiers and languages beyond English, likely requiring new annotated datasets that capture such variability. Other failure cases similarly revealed by our benchmark can also inform the development of better anonymization tools. Third, more focus should be placed on identifier values and combinations with high impact on re-identification risk, even if the features themselves are low risk overall. Further, we are excited for our benchmark to enable future work on prompt design for (one-shot) LLM-based anonymizers, or to develop more lightweight alternatives, potentially through targeted finetuning – while balancing the risk of
overfitting
(Section
5
). Lastly, future work could explore more advanced utility metrics to better navigate the trade-offs, including metrics that measure semantic meaning or specifically target the quality of LLMs post-trained on anonymized chat interactions. We further elaborate on failure cases and lessons for users and developers in App.
H
.

Sampling strategy

To evaluate the performance of anonymization tools in worst-case scenarios and obtain comparable scores across profiles, we sample individuals for our benchmark that are highly unique with respect to a random set of 5 indirect identifiers. Specifically, we compute equivalence classes by grouping selected identifiers that share the same set of feature values. We then conducted select an equivalence class using weighted sampling; each class is given a weight of
1/n
where
n
is the number of profiles in that class. Lastly, we then randomly sample a profile from the selected class. This process ensures that the generated texts have a high baseline re-identification risk while maintaining a diverse set of target individuals, allowing us to properly test the performance of anonymization tools and measure progress.

Appendix C
Details for attack evaluation

As described in Section
4
, evaluating the anonymized text requires matching the ground-truth attributes with the guesses produced by the LLM-based attacker. For each target attribute
a\in\mathcal{A}
present in the original text
t
, the attacker outputs a guess, which we compare to the ground truth.

Both the ground-truth attributes and the LLM-generated guesses are unstructured and may not match exactly. For numerical attributes (e.g., age, SSN), we require exact matches. For text-based attributes (e.g., citizenship status, education attainment), we consider a guess correct if its Jaro–Winkler similarity with the ground truth exceeds an attribute-specific threshold. These thresholds were set by manually inspecting similarity scores across a sample of model outputs and defining conservative, attribute-specific rules rather than using a universal cutoff. For example, for ‘education attainment’, we require the grade level to match exactly—treating ‘Grade 8’ as incorrect when the ground truth is ‘Grade 9’, even if the string similarity is high. More broadly, when strings are similar but differ semantically (e.g., adjacent categories), we follow a conservative policy and mark the prediction as incorrect.

Appendix H
Performance analysis of anonymization tools and lessons for their users and developers

In this section, we provide additional insight into the performance and common failures we observe for each of the anonymization tools. We summarize our observations, and complement this with example failure cases from the benchmark. In particular, per level of difficulty, we select
3
instances in which the attacker was still able to correctly infer the correct attribute from text anonymized with tool
\mathcal{T}
, for direct and indirect identifiers in Table
7
and Table
8
, respectively. Finally, based on this analysis and findings throughout the paper, we also describe lessons learned for users and developers of text anonymization tools.

Summary of anonymization failure cases.

For NER-based approaches, we find that many
false positives
come from spans that resemble named entities but are not actually identifying. For example, Azure often removes generic person nouns such as “patient,” and GliNER may remove pronouns like “I” or “me and my buddies.” Similarly, Presidio sometimes deletes common temporal expressions such as “today”, which on their own are unlikely to constitute an indirect identifier. These methods seem to be confusing these pieces of text with what is frequently annotated as PERSON or TIME/DATE entities in standard NER datasets, which are categories that can include names or dates of birth, but also many non-identifying terms. As a result, NER-based anonymizers may over-redact text that does not meaningfully contribute to re-identification risk. We further confirm this aggressive removal in Section
H.1
, where we provide an entire piece of text anonymized by both Azure and GliNER.

We also find that many
false negatives
in NER-based models arise when identifiers appear in non-standard forms, such as being split across multiple spans (e.g., “My phone number is 312, then 480, then 3820”) or expressed through slang (e.g., “C-way” for Conway). This indicates that many NER models are optimized for detecting entities in their standard forms and struggle with variability in this format. Finetuning these models on datasets with annotated non-standard language, e.g. transcripts, may help address this gap. Beyond that, we also find that NER-based methods can also sometimes miss clearly stated identifying information, with for instance Scrubadub missing clear instances of SSNs or credit card numbers, or Uniner not removing all duplicates of the same identifier in a piece of text. We hypothesize that such failures stem from surrounding context that differs from the ones seen during training, but we leave a deeper investigation to future work.

Further, we find that LLM-based anonymizers have less false positives than NER-based methods. For instance, we find them to be more precise in distinguishing genuinely identifying (e.g. a person’s name) information from entity-like but harmless text (e.g. ‘patient’). We also find that LLM-based anonymizers tend to perform better with unusual representations, with significantly lower false negative rates on difficulty level 2 records than NER models. Both findings are expected: LLMs can use reasoning and a broader contextual understanding to distinguish what is truly identifying information and interpret fragmented or unconventional patterns, whereas NER models primarily match patterns seen during training, making LLM-based anonymization a promising area of research.

Notably, we find that LLMs such as GPT 4.1, Gemini and Llama instantiated with the Anthropic prompt also show inconsistencies, removing an identifier in one instance but missing it in an otherwise similar case, or only partially redacting identifiers such as address or date of birth. We also observe that LLM anonymization performance is sensitive to both model capabilities and prompt design. For the same prompt, a more capable model like GPT-4.1 reduces re-identification risk more effectively than Llama-3.1-8B, and both models perform better when using the more specific Rescriber prompt (Table
1
). Similarly, we find that the exact attributes mentioned in the iterative anonymizer also heavily impacts performance (Table
2
). These findings suggest that effective LLM-based anonymization requires carefully balancing model capability with thoughtful prompt design.

Lessons learned for users and developers of text anonymization tools.

For users, ultimately, choosing the right anonymizer for a given use-case requires balancing trade-offs in privacy, utility and computational cost.

Our results generally agree with prior work that LLM-based anonymizers provide a stronger privacy-utility trade-off. They remove identifying information in a more precise manner, allowing methods such as GPT-4.1 instantiated with the Anthropic prompt to substantially reduce re-identification risk while maintaining high BLEU scores. However, these models are (computationally) expensive and might not be feasible to run at scale. We leave for future work to explore how smaller LLMs, instantiated with a carefully crafted prompt, or potentially finetuned to remove identifying information, could offer similar performance while reducing cost.

In contrast, more light-weight methods, such as Azure, are computationally efficient and may reduce the re-identification more substantially, but often at a cost in utility, at least as measured by BLEU or ROUGE scores. Depending on the application, this may or may not be acceptable: in some cases, (over-)aggressive removal is harmless, while in others the semantic utility might be more important. In the latter case, approaches like Clio, which summarizes text while removing identifying information to maintain overall semantic meaning, can be more appropriate.

When it comes to privacy, the choice likely also depends on the likely prevalence of identifying information and on the tolerance for false negatives. If the application requires *all* (including e.g. rare occurrences of identifiers in unusual formats) identifiers to be removed, a carefully designed iterative LLM-based anonymizer might be required, and relying solely on NER-based anonymizers may be insufficient.

For anonymization system developers, our results point to several directions for future work. First, we believe more emphasis should be placed on indirect identifiers. A significant proportion of the re-identification risk in our benchmark comes from indirect identifiers, many of which are often missed by all tested anonymizers (Figure
3
). Second, NER-based anonymization tools should be more robust to unusual representations of identifiers, likely requiring new annotated datasets that capture such variability. Further, we are excited for our benchmark to enable future work on prompt design for (one-shot) LLM-based anonymizers, or to develop more lightweight alternatives to models like GPT-4.1, potentially through targeted finetuning – while balancing the risk of
overfitting
(Section
5
). Lastly, future work could explore more advanced utility metrics to better navigate the trade-offs, including metrics that measure semantic meaning or specifically target the quality of LLMs post-trained on anonymized chat interactions.

Beyond methods based on NER and LLMs, prior work has also considered protecting the privacy of text using controlled perturbations, satisfying formal privacy guarantees.

We consider two approaches that introduce perturbations at the word level. First, Madlib
(
19
)
maps each word into a fixed word embedding space, adds noise to the embedding vector, and then projects back to the nearest word. TEM
(
9
)
improves on this by sampling replacements from a distribution over candidate words, where words closer to the original in the metric space receive higher probability. Consistent with the literature on differential privacy, both methods consider privacy parameters
\epsilon
, where smaller values imply stronger privacy and
\epsilon=\infty
corresponds to no protection.

We implement both methods using the code released by
76
and apply them to each benchmark entry for explicitly mentioned identifiers as also used in Table
1
. Following
76
, we consider
\epsilon=(5.0,11.0,17.0)
for both Madlib and TEM. For each anonymized text, we compute re-identification risk (as in Table
1
) and BLEU score (as in Figure
2(a)
). Results are shown in Figure
22
.

For both perturbation methods, as epsilon increases, the BLEU score drops sharply. This is as expected, as these methods do not explicitly focus on removing sensitive attributes while retaining the rest of the text, but instead modify each individual word in the text. TEM with
\epsilon=11.0
achieves the best performance at BLEU scores comparable to other anonymization methods and we therefore include this configuration in Table
1
.
