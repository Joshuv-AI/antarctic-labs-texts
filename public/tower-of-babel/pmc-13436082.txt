Novel Single-Stage Transcervical Submandibular Approach for a Giant Multicompartmental Pterygopalatine Fossa Schwannoma: A Case Report and Surgical Strategy

Introduction

The pterygopalatine fossa (PPF) is a critical inverted pyramidal neurovascular crossroad serving as a conduit between the cranial cavity, deep face, and orbit.1 Within this confined space, schwannomas—most commonly originating from the maxillary (V2) or vidian nerves—represent a rare but formidable diagnostic and therapeutic challenge.2 Their insidious, expansive growth through the fossa’s numerous foramina leads to non-specific symptoms like facial numbness and nasal obstruction, often resulting in diagnostic delay. Epidemiologically, these tumours are exceptionally rare, typically presenting in adults without clear gender predilection.3,4 The surgical paradigm for PPF schwannomas has shifted dramatically. Historically, resection relied on open transcranial or transfacial approaches (eg infratemporal fossa, Caldwell-Luc), which were effective but carried significant morbidity.5,6 The advent of endoscopic endonasal surgery (EES), particularly the transpterygoid approach, has revolutionized management, offering a minimally invasive corridor with superior visualization for medially based tumours.7,8 A critical surgical nuance is the tumour’s predictable extension pattern; growth is rarely confined to the PPF. Tumours frequently extend anteriorly, superiorly, and medially. However, lateral expansion into the infratemporal and parapharyngeal spaces (PPS) or posterior growth toward the cavernous sinus and craniovertebral junction presents the greatest challenge, often complicating access and vascular control.9,10 This report presents a complex case of a giant PPF schwannoma with massive multicompartmental spread, including encasement of the internal carotid artery and extension to the craniovertebral junction. Such extensive lateral and posterior-inferior involvement exceeds the limits of standard endoscopic corridors. Therefore, we employed a tailored transcervical submandibular approach. This study highlights the significance of a nuanced anatomy-driven surgical philosophy. It demonstrates that for select, extensive tumours with significant lateral neurovascular involvement, an open cervical approach can provide superior exposure for safe vascular control and gross-total resection, representing a critical strategy in the modern multimodal management of these complex lesions.

Case Presentation

A 34-year-old woman presented to our clinic complaining of numbness and tingling sensation on her right cheek as well as difficulty swallowing solid food. These symptoms had begun nearly 6 months before her referral, with maxillary paraesthesia being the initial manifestation. The course was progressive, leading to facial asymmetry and obvious expansion of the right facial structures, in addition to bulging of the retro-auricular and submandibular regions. She underwent fine needle aspiration biopsy of the expansile lesion, which was suggestive of Schwannoma, although the complementary histopathological and molecular investigations were not performed. No significant physical findings were noted except for hypoesthesia of the right V2 segment.

She was admitted at our tertiary spine surgery centre thereafter and underwent contrasted facial and Brain MRI, which showed a solid-cystic expansile mass in pterygopalatine fossa extending to maxillary sinus, infratemporal, retromandibular, and parapharyngeal fossae as well as the anterolateral part of the craniovertebral junction engulfing the internal carotid artery. The aforementioned lesion was hypo to iso-intense on T1, Hyperintense on T2 and avidly enhanced following contrast administration as it is demonstrated on Figure 1. As a slow-growing lesion, it caused scalloping and expansion of the surrounding bony structures, including maxillary sinus walls and the mandible were scalloped and expanded. Due to the apparent T2-flow voids in the pterygopalatine and maxillary fossae, our treatment strategy shifted towards angiographic embolization of the lesion in order to obviate the need for massive intraoperative blood transfusions giving that early exposure of the feeding vessels and ligating them seemed impossible possibly owing to the considered surgical corridor was not wide enough. Preoperative embolization was successfully performed using ethylene vinyl alcohol copolymer (Onyx®) via a transfemoral approach. One of the main feeding arteries, the internal maxillary artery, was partially embolized to reduce intraoperative bleeding risk and facilitate safer tumor devascularization. A multidisciplinary team encompassing Neurosurgery, Oromaxillofacial (OMF) and Ear, nose, throat (ENT) surgeons was organized and the appointment was made the day before surgery to clarify the strategies and approaches.

Surgical Technique

After the patient underwent general anaesthesia and Nasotracheal tube as well as Nasogastric tube (NG) were placed in supine position, a zig-zag incision was made from just inferior to the right mastoid process to the midline (Figure 2a) at the approximate level of the hyoid bone (C3-C4). Following gentle dissection of the underlying muscular and fascial layers, the carotid sheath was exposed and peeled away such that an adequate length of the artery was exposed to be ligated if inadvertent bleeding from distal segments occurred. The hypoglossal nerve, Submandibular gland, Lingual artery and nerve were exposed through further upward dissection (Figure 2b). All exposed nerves were stimulated using somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEP) for identified and to check for any potential damage caused by either the tumor mass effect or iatrogenic manipulations. After proper dissection and exposure of the designated landmarks the tumour was exposed. Its borders were identified and following peeling off the capsule, internal debulking in piecemeal manner was performed. Subsequent to exposure of the pre-planned bony and soft tissue landmarks, resection cavity was checked by all the members of the team and total excision of the tumour was confirmed by direct intra-operative observation.

Results

The patient was transferred to the ward with the NG tube in situ and was examined frequently for cranial nerve function and possible oropharyngeal complications. Except for odynophagia which lasted a few hours, no other complication were found. She was started on an oral diet 24 hours thereafter and underwent contrasted MRI to evaluate for any residual tumor, which fortunately showed no evidence of disease according to the experienced radiologist (Figure 1). At the 6‑month follow‑up, the patient remained neurologically intact, and contrast‑enhanced MRI demonstrated no evidence of tumor recurrence or residual disease.

Discussion

The surgical management of pterygopalatine fossa (PPF) schwannomas has evolved significantly, with endoscopic endonasal transpterygoid (EET) approaches now considered the gold standard for lesions confined to or extending medially from the PPF.11,12 This paradigm shift is driven by the minimally invasive nature of EET, which offers direct midline access, superior visualization, and reduced morbidity for tumours involving the nasal cavity, sphenoid sinus, or medial infratemporal fossa. However, as highlighted in the introduction, extensive lateral invasion into the infratemporal and parapharyngeal spaces, posterior extension toward the craniovertebral junction, or encasement of major vascular structures such as the internal carotid artery (ICA) present formidable challenges that may exceed the safe and effective limits of a purely endoscopic corridor.13–15 Alternative open approaches, such as the Caldwell-Luc procedure, have historically been used for lesions in this region. However, this approach is associated with significant morbidity, including facial swelling, dental complications, and cosmetic deformities. Furthermore, it provides limited access to the parapharyngeal space and the craniovertebral junction, which were critically involved in our case. Therefore, we selected the transcervical submandibular approach as it offers a more direct and extensive lateral corridor with superior cosmetic outcomes and lower morbidity compared to traditional transfacial approaches. Managing tumours with significant parapharyngeal space involvement requires a thorough understanding of this compartment’s anatomy and the limitations of an endonasal corridor.16,17 A recent systematic review on glossopharyngeal schwannomas provides a comprehensive overview of the clinical presentation, differential diagnosis, and surgical management of lower cranial nerve schwannomas, offering useful context for the discussion of complex parapharyngeal skull base lesions.18 In the present case, the tumour exhibited massive multicompartmental spread, involving the PPF, maxillary sinus, infratemporal fossa, parapharyngeal space, and extending posteriorly to the craniovertebral junction with encasement of the ICA. Given this anatomic complexity and the significant lateral and posterior-inferior extension, a purely endoscopic approach would have been insufficient for safe vascular control and complete resection, particularly given the tumour’s relationship to the ICA in the parapharyngeal space.19,20 Therefore, we employed a transcervical submandibular approach, which provided a direct and expansive lateral corridor to the parapharyngeal and infratemporal spaces, as well as controlled access to the cranial base and upper cervical region. Compared to the endoscopic and traditional open transfacial or infratemporal approaches, the transcervical submandibular technique offered several distinct advantages in this context. First, it allowed for early and safe exposure and control of the cervical ICA, which was critically important given the tumor’s encasement of the vessel. This vascular control is difficult to achieve via an endoscopic transpterygoid route, especially when the ICA is displaced or engulfed within the tumour in the deep parapharyngeal space. Second, the approach provided excellent visualization of key neurovascular landmarks, facilitating their preservation through direct visualization and intraoperative neuromonitoring. Third, the corridor enabled en-bloc capsular dissection and piecemeal internal debulking under direct vision, ensuring gross-total resection while minimizing traction on adjacent neural structures. While open approaches like the infratemporal fossa approach offer wide exposure, they often require extensive bone removal, facial incisions, and mandibular manipulation, leading to higher morbidity, including facial nerve injury, trismus, and cosmetic deformity. The transcervical submandibular approach, in contrast, uses a cosmetically favourable neck incision, avoids facial nerve dissection, and preserves mandibular integrity, thereby reducing functional and aesthetic sequelae. It is particularly superior for tumours with significant inferior and posterior extension toward the craniovertebral junction, as it follows natural tissue planes and provides a seamless transition from the neck to the skull base. This open corridor is especially valuable when endoscopic access to the inferior and lateral aspects of the parapharyngeal space is restricted. However, the choice of approach must be tailored to the tumor’s epicenter and pattern of spread. For predominantly medial PPF tumors, endoscopic approaches remain superior,21 and augmented corridors like the contralateral transmaxillary approach can extend the reach of endonasal surgery.22 For complex, giant schwannomas with multicompartmental spread—especially those with lateral parapharyngeal, vascular, or craniovertebral involvement—the transcervical submandibular approach represents a strategic safe, and effective alternative or adjunct.23,24 In such cases, an endoscopic-assisted transcervical technique can also be considered to combine the benefits of both philosophies. In this case, the transcervical approach enabled a single-stage gross-total resection with vascular control and cranial nerve preservation, objectives that would have been considerably riskier or unattainable through other routes.

Regarding the limitation, it should be noted that this technique is not without limitations. The surgical corridor, though adequate for lateral and inferior access, can be narrow and deep, requiring significant expertise in skull base and cervical anatomy. The approach also demands careful dissection to avoid injury to the marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve, the hypoglossal nerve, and the lingual nerve. Furthermore, it may be insufficient for addressing simultaneous significant intracranial extension, which might necessitate a combined or staged procedure with an endoscopic or transcranial approach.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the management of PPF schwannomas demands a nuanced anatomically driven surgical strategy. For our patient with a massive, multicompartmental schwannoma extending into the parapharyngeal space and craniovertebral junction and encasing the ICA, the transcervical submandibular approach proved to be a superior surgical strategy. It provided the necessary wide and safe exposure for vascular control, facilitated identification and preservation of critical nerves, and allowed complete tumour resection while avoiding the morbidity associated with more invasive open craniofacial approaches. This approach may represent a feasible and valuable option for select, extensively invasive PPF schwannomas, particularly when the lesion involves the lateral compartments and major vessels. However, given that this conclusion is based on a single case, our findings should be interpreted with caution, and the approach should be considered as an alternative in carefully selected patients rather than a universally superior technique.
