Cardiac rehabilitation for elderly patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting: a systematic review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Juan Huang, Changhong Liu, Jiaxiang Xie, Juan Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac rehabilitation (CR) has been shown to improve outcomes in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG); however, its specific effects on elderly CABG patients remain unclear. This meta-analysis aims to evaluate the benefits of CR for elderly patients undergoing CABG.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and Wanfang Data databases for prospective studies on CR interventions in elderly CABG patients, from study inception through February 19, 2025. Background

Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) plays a pivotal role in the recovery process for patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) [1, 2]. Exercise-based CR programs have been shown to significantly improve cardiovascular endurance, muscle strength, and exercise capacity in coronary artery disease patients, all of which contribute to reducing morbidity and mortality rates associated with the condition [3, 4]. However, the specific impact of CR on elderly patients remains an area of ongoing investigation, as they often face unique challenges during recovery due to age-related factors, including multiple comorbidities that can complicate their recovery and increase the risk of adverse events, as well as challenges in implementing CR programs [5–7].

The multifaceted nature of CR highlights the importance of personalized and innovative approaches, including telerehabilitation and behavioral theories-based rehabilitation [8–11]. Home-based telerehabilitation, which addresses physical barriers (e.g., transportation difficulties) that elderly patients may encounter when participating in CR, has shown promise in overcoming logistical barriers while maintaining or even improving patient outcomes [12–15]. Moreover, integrating psychological support into CR programs has demonstrated significant improvements in mental health and adherence, emphasizing the need for a holistic approach to postoperative care [16].

Behavioral theories provide a strong framework for understanding and addressing barriers to CR adherence [17, 18]. The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), a widely utilized framework, has been particularly effective in designing interventions to enhance motivation and participation in health-related programs, with its effectiveness well documented [19, 20]. Additionally, interventions that incorporate family support and self-management tools have demonstrated significant improvements in both adherence rates and health outcomes among CABG patients [21].

This meta-analysis aims to synthesize existing evidence to assess whether elderly patients undergoing CABG benefit from CR and to identify the most suitable intervention models—such as exercise, telerehabilitation, family support, and TPB-driven approaches—for this population. The results will provide critical evidence to inform precision rehabilitation practices and policy-making in cardiovascular care for elderly CABG patients, ultimately improving long-term outcomes for this vulnerable group.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted following the guidelines of the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement, to evaluate the benefits of CR on elderly CABG patients [22]. The protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251017085), and the study adhered to predefined methodologies to minimize bias and enhance reproducibility.

Literature search

A comprehensive search was performed across PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and Wanfang Data databases from their inception to February 19, 2025. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings and free-text terms related to CABG, aged, and cardiac rehabilitation. Key search terms were as follows: Population: (“Coronary Artery Bypass,” “Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting,” “CABG”, “bypass surgery,“) AND (“aged,” “elderly,” “geriatric,” “older adults,” “senior”); Interventions: “cardiac rehabilitation,” “heart rehabilitation,” “postoperative rehabilitation,” “Planned behavior theory,” “TPB,” “lifestyle modification,” “training”). The detailed search strategy used for each database is presented in Supplementary Table. The inclusion criteria were: (1) elderly CABG patients (age ≥ 60 years); (2) studies comparing cardiac rehabilitation (including exercise, telerehabilitation, and the theory of planned behavior) versus control; (3) prospective studies; (4) at least one month of follow-up after discharge; (5) published in English or Chinese. Outcomes assessed included all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, all-cause hospitalization, health-related quality of life (HRQoL), functional capacity, and cardiac function. Excluded studies included conference abstracts, letters, non-comparative studies, or those lacking sufficient outcome data. Overlapping datasets were resolved by prioritizing the report with the largest sample size.

Data extraction

Two researchers conducted parallel assessments of the retrieved articles using EndNote X9 reference management software, performing independent screening of the titles, abstracts, and full texts of the retrieved articles, and also performed quality evaluation. The following information were extracted from the studies: (1) study characteristics: first author, year of publication, country, sample size, and follow-up duration; (2) patient characteristics: age; (3) treatment characteristics: duration and approaches of the intervention; (4) outcomes: all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, all-cause hospitalization, HRQoL, and functional capacity. Any disagreements between the two researchers were resolved by consulting a third researcher.

Quality assessment

The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB 2.0) was applied to assess the methodological quality of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [23]. Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies-of Interventions (ROBINS–I) V2 (2024) modified from ROBINS–I (2016) [24] was used to assess the quality of non-RCT studies. In cases of disagreement between the two reviewers, a third reviewer was consulted.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Review Manager 5.3 software. Outcomes reported in only one study were summarized descriptively. Weighted mean differences (WMDs) were calculated to compare effect sizes for continuous outcomes with inverse variance methods. Relative risks were calculated to compare effect sizes for dichotomous outcomes with Mantel–Haenszel. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed quantitatively, with a Q-test (P > 0.05) indicating no significant heterogeneity. A fixed-effects model was applied for meta-analysis when the I2 value was < 50%, while a random-effects model was applied for meta-analysis when the I2 value was > 50%. We considered P-values < 0.05 to be significant.

Results

Search results

Figure 1 illustrates the literature search and screening process. A total of 4,612 articles were initially retrieved from database searches. Following the removal of duplicates, 4,186 articles were retained. Of these, 4163 were excluded after screening titles and abstracts. The remaining 23 articles underwent full-text review, resulting in the exclusion of 15 articles due to shorter follow-up periods, being conference abstracts, lacking a usual care control group, being retrospective studies, or having insufficient data for extraction. Finally, six studies with 621 patients were included in the meta-analysis [25–30].

Quality evaluation

Among the four RCT studies [27–30], one study [27] used a lot-drawing method for randomization; another [30] used a web-based randomizer; one [28] reported randomization without specifying the method; and one [29] used pseudo-randomization based on odd versus even number assignments. Only one study [30] implemented allocation concealment using sealed envelopes and blinded data collection. The quality evaluation of these studies is presented in Fig. 2. Among the non-RCTs, one study [25, 26] was assessed as having a moderate risk of bias due not controlling for confounding factors, although the uncontrolled confounding was probably not substantial. Another non-RCT study was considered to have low risk of bias (Table 2).

Outcomes

All outcomes were continuous variables, with none of the included studies reporting on all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, or all-cause hospitalization. Three studies [26–28] assessed HRQoL assessed using Medical Outcomes Study 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36), which includes eight subscales: Physical Functioning (PF), Role-Physical (RP), Bodily Pain (BP), General Health (GH), Vitality (VT), Social Functioning (SF), Role-Emotional (RE), and Mental Health (MH). The first four subscales form the physical component summary, and the last four form the mental function component summary. One study [25] reported functional capacity (total treadmill endurance time [Timemax] and maximum oxygen consumption [VO2max)]) and cardiovascular efficiency (maximum rate × pressure product [RPPmax] and mean arterial blood pressure at maximum exercise [MABPmax]). Another study [26] evaluated lower extremity function, assessing the physical capacities of lower extremity muscle strength, ankle range of motion, dynamic and static balance, and gait. A third study measured physical activity using an RT3 accelerometer and a physical activity and exercise diary [28]. One study reported on the Functional Autonomy Measurement System, which includes activities of daily living, communication, and mental functions [29]. One study assessed function capacity using updated Coronary Revascularization Outcome Questionnaire Version 2, also known as CROQ v2, for CABG [30].

Individual study findings

Willoughby et al. [25] reported that a 12-week aerobic exercise program significantly increased the Timemax (mean ± SD: 8.47 ± 2.5 vs. 5.66 ± 1.28 min; p < 0.007) and VO2max (mean ± SD: 28.28 ± 11.42 vs. 19.25 ± 6.76; p < 0.007), and improved RPPmax (mean ± SD: 239.46 ± 34.27 vs. 260.62 ± 14.25; p < 0.007) and MABPmax (mean ± SD: 101.50 ± 11.08 vs. 111 ± 10.37 mmHg; p < 0.007). Dolansky et al. [26] reported that a supervised 12-week exercise program significantly improved the lower extremity function, including muscle strength (mean ± SD: 105.6 ± 27.5 vs. 93.9 ± 31.8, p < 0.05), ankle range of motion (mean ± SD: 207.9 ± 37.6 vs. 193.5 ± 35.1, p < 0.05), dynamic balance (mean ± SD: 23.6 ± 1.6 vs. 22.7 ± 1.5, p < 0.001), static balance (mean ± SD: 40.7 ± 23.3 vs. 34.0 ± 25.0, p < 0.05), and gait (mean ± SD: 8.7 ± 5 vs. 8.2 ± 1.5, p < 0.05). Barnason et al. [28] reported that a 6-week telehealth intervention showed no significant effect on physical activity. Dığın et al. [29] found that a 6-week nurse telephone consultation significantly improved the activities of daily living (mean ± SD: changes − 8.06 ± 4.40 vs. −2.20 ± 1.71, p < 0.001) but had no effect on communication and mental functions. Mahfouz Khalil et al. [30] reported that a 12-week home-based transitional cardiac telerehabilitation program led to a significant improvement in the overall CROQ v2-CABG scores (mean ± SD: 33.88 ± 9.71 vs. 26.61 ± 8.32, p < 0.001), including physical and psychosocial functioning.

Meta-Analysis of HRQoL assessed by SF-36

Three studies reported SF-36 scores, including both physical and mental component summaries. One study utilized an exercise-based intervention, while two studies employed telerehabilitation. The meta-analysis results showed that CR did not significantly improve either the physical (WMD: 5.92 [−0.26, 12.10], p = 0.08; I2 = 61%) or the mental component summary (WMD: 5.92 [−0.14, 1.80], p = 0.28; I2 = 21%) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we included six studies with a total of 621 patients and found that CR appears to improve the functional capacity in certain domains but does not significantly impact HRQoL, including both the physical and mental components, in elderly CABG patients.

CR is a comprehensive secondary prevention approach that integrates exercise, education on managing cardiovascular disease, behavior modification (such as smoking cessation, weight management and stress reduction), and psychological support to improve the overall health and well-being in cardiovascular patients [5, 31, 32]. Increasing evidence supports the benefits of CR for patients with cardiovascular disease, and several clinical guidelines recommend CR following post-cardiovascular events or post-cardiac surgery [33, 34]. However, elderly patients often face unique challenges, such as age-related physiological decline, comorbidities, and psychosocial barriers (e.g., mobility limitations, loneliness), which may affect their response to and adherence to CR. Current evidence remains unclear regarding the specific effects of CR on elderly CABG patients, highlighting the need for a comprehensive systematic evaluation.

Exercise therapy remains the cornerstone of CR, but its safety and efficacy in older adults require careful consideration of exercise intensity and musculoskeletal tolerance. Complementary approaches—including motivational interviewing, behavioral counseling, and family involvement—may enhance adherence [35–37]. Telerehabilitation also provides a promising option to overcome barriers such as transportation, yet its effectiveness in elderly CABG patients has not been fully validated. Further research is needed to determine the optimal integration of these innovative CR modalities in the elderly population.

In this study, the follow-up duration ranged from 6 weeks to 6 months, which explains the absence of reports on long-term clinical outcomes such as all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, or all-cause hospitalization. Although our meta-analysis did not assess survival endpoints, several large-scale observational studies consistently demonstrate survival benefits associated with CR. Lee et al. [38] reported a 20% reduction in all-cause mortality after phase I CR and a 40% reduction after phase II CR; Pack et al. [39] observed a significant 10-year mortality reduction in CABG patients who attended CR; and Kutner et al. [40] found that dialysis patients who underwent CR after CABG had a 35% lower risk of all-cause mortality and a 36% lower risk of cardiac death compared with non-participants. While these studies did not specifically focus on elderly subgroups, they collectively highlight the potential of CR to improve long-term survival and underscore the urgent need for elderly-focused trials with extended follow-up.

Functional capacity outcomes were also limited. VO2max, a key indicator of functional capacity, was assessed in only one study [25], which demonstrated significant improvement following CR intervention. Similarly, lower extremity function was evaluated in a single study, which also reported a positive effect of CR [26]. Another study used CROQ v2-CABG to evaluate function capacity in elderly CABG patients after CR and found significant improvements [30].

Regarding HRQoL, three studies [26–28] employed the SF-36, a 36-item instrument comprising eight subscales that can be categorized into physical and mental component summaries. Contrary to expectations, our meta-analysis found that CR did not significantly improve HRQoL in elderly CABG patients, suggesting that current CR approaches may not be adequately tailored to this population. Alternative CR modalities, such as traditional Chinese medicine exercises, social support networks, and psychological interventions, should be considered, given their demonstrated benefits in elderly and cardiovascular disease populations [31, 41–43]. Notably, heterogeneity in the SF-36 physical component summary (I² = 61%) was likely driven by Barnason et al. [28], which implemented a shorter 6-week intervention with a larger sample, compared with the 12-week programs in the other two studies.

Behavioral theory represents an important but underexplored domain in CR research for elderly patients. The TPB is widely recognized as a framework to improve health-related behaviors [19, 20, 44]; however, no included studies applied TPB-based interventions. Only one potentially relevant article was retrieved, which did not meet inclusion criteria. This highlights a gap in applying theory-based approaches to enhance CR adherence and suggests an important avenue for future research.

This study has several limitations. First, the small number of included studies precluded subgroup or meta-regression analyses to formally explore heterogeneity by intervention type, duration, or outcome measures. Second, follow-up was short, precluding evaluation of long-term clinical endpoints such as mortality and rehospitalization—key outcomes that define CR’s clinical value. Third, the limited sample size reduced statistical power, which may explain why trends toward improved physical HRQoL (p = 0.08) did not reach significance. Finally, heterogeneity in intervention modalities and measurement tools may limit comparability and generalizability of the findings. Together, these limitations highlight both the scarcity of high-quality evidence and the urgent need for larger, standardized, and long-term trials in elderly post-CABG patients.

Conclusions

In elderly CABG patients, CR did not significantly improve HRQoL as measured by SF-36, although trends toward enhanced functional capacity and physical activity were observed. Evidence is limited by small sample sizes, methodological heterogeneity, and short follow-up, with no data on long-term outcomes such as mortality or hospitalization. High-quality trials with larger cohorts, standardized interventions, extended follow-up, and clinically meaningful endpoints are needed to clarify the efficacy and safety of CR in this population. Clinicians should consider individualized CR strategies while recognizing current evidence gaps regarding their long-term effectiveness in this vulnerable population.
