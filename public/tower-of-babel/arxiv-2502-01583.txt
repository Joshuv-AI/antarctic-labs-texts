Spectral Estimators for Multi-Index Models: Precise Asymptotics and Optimal Weak Recovery
Filip Kovačević, Yihan Zhang, Marco Mondelli

Abstract
Multi-index models provide a popular framework to investigate the learnability of functions with low-dimensional structure and, also due to their connections with neural networks, they have been object of recent intensive study. In this paper, we focus on recovering the subspace spanned by the signals via spectral estimators – a family of methods routinely used in practice, often as a warm-start for iterative algorithms. Our main technical contribution is a precise asymptotic characterization of the performance of spectral methods, when sample size and input dimension grow proportionally and the dimension p p of the space to recover is fixed. Specifically, we locate the top- p p eigenvalues of the spectral matrix and establish the overlaps between the corresponding eigenvectors (which give the spectral estimators) and a basis of the signal subspace. Our analysis unveils a phase transition phenomenon in which, as the sample complexity grows, eigenvalues escape from the bulk of the spectrum and, when that happens, eigenvectors recover directions of the desired subspace. The precise characterization we put forward enables the optimization of the data preprocessing, thus allowing to identify the spectral estimator that requires the minimal sample size for weak recovery.

Introduction

Modern machine learning practices operate on high-dimensional datasets that are believed to possess low-dimensional structures, and multi-index models are a popular statistical framework for studying such scenarios
[
Li91
,
Li92
,
LD89
]
. Specifically,
for a labeled dataset
{\mathcal{D}}=\left\{(a_{i},y_{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
a_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
y_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
denote features and responses respectively, a multi-index model postulates that each data pair follows a generalized regression and the responses are function of a
low-dimensional
projection of the high-dimensional features.
In formulas:

y_{i}=q\left(\left\langle a_{i},w_{1}^{*}\right\rangle,\cdots,\left\langle a_{i},w_{p}^{*}\right\rangle,\varepsilon_{i}\right),

(1.1)

where the link function

is a known nonlinearity operating on a
p
-dimensional linear transformation of
a_{i}
, given by

, and on additional randomness from
\varepsilon_{i}
.

We study the problem of estimating the signals
W^{*}
given
{\mathcal{D}}
under a Gaussian design where the
a_{i}
’s are i.i.d. standard Gaussian. We focus on the proportional asymptotic regime where
n,d\to\infty
with
n/d\to\delta\in]0,\infty[
, while keeping the dimension of the low-dimensional projection
p
fixed.
Of particular interest in this work is the

of the subspace spanned by

which seeks an estimator
\widehat{W}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{w}_{1},&\cdots,&\widehat{w}_{p}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times p}
s.t. at least one of
\widehat{w}_{i}

Formally, there exists

and some

in

such that the normalized correlation (or

\frac{\left|\left\langle\widehat{w}_{i},W^{*}v\right\rangle\right|}{\left\|\widehat{w}_{i}\right\|_{2}\left\|W^{*}v\right\|_{2}}
is asymptotically non-vanishing.

To solve the aforementioned problem, this paper focuses on spectral estimators that construct the matrix

D=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{T}}(y_{i})a_{i}a_{i}^{\top}
,
from the dataset

and then output its top-

eigenvectors. Here,

refers to a user-defined preprocessing function, with popular choices including binary quantization and truncation
[

,
CC17
]
. Spectral estimators are easy to design, efficient to compute, and effective in practice
[
CCFM21
]
. However, such class of methods remains understudied for multi-index models, with existing results falling short of producing exact asymptotics
[

]
and being restricted to special cases, such as single-index (for which

, corresponding to generalized linear models
[

]
)
[
LL20
,
MM19
]
, polynomial link functions
[
CM20
]
, and mixed regression
[
ZMV22
]
, see also
Section

In contrast, this paper tackles the problem for fully general multi-index models, precisely identifying under what conditions spectral estimators are effective.

A compelling motivation for the precise analysis of spectral estimators comes from the choice of the preprocessing function

,

]
, correlated
[

]
and rotationally invariant

]

Main contributions.

This paper tackles the two main problems mentioned above, i.e.,
(i)
the lack of a precise analysis of spectral estimators for multi-index models, and
(ii)
the design of an optimal preprocessing function
{\mathcal{T}}
. Specifically, our results are summarized below.

1.

For any preprocessing function
{\mathcal{T}}
satisfying mild assumptions, we precisely locate the top-
p
eigenvalues of the spectral matrix

and characterize the overlaps between the top-
p
eigenvectors and a basis of the subspace spanned by the signals. Our results describe a phase transition phenomenon, akin to the classical BBP transition in the spiked covariance model

]

2.

Using the above characterization, we identify the optimal preprocessing function for weak recovery.
Our optimality result guarantees that no other choice of preprocessing function results in spectral estimators with a lower threshold, and therefore it also implies the suboptimality of existing heuristics to choose
{\mathcal{T}}

Related work

Multi-index models.

Several approaches have been proposed to perform statistical inference in multi-index models, e.g.,
structural adaption via maximum minimization
[
DJS08
]
,
projection pursuit regression
[
Yua11
]
and
techniques from compressed sensing

]
.

Due to the connection of multi-index models with two-layer neural networks, the area has witnessed a recent renewed interest with a focus on the performance of gradient-based methods. In particular, sample complexity bounds for gradient descent and statistical query lower bounds are provided by
[
DLS22
]
when the link function is polynomial and by
[
OSSW24
]
when the multi-index model is the sum of single-index models.

[

,

]
introduce the concept of leap complexity and show that a class of staircase functions is learned via one-pass stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with

]
as the time required by gradient flow to escape a saddle point.

[
CWPPS24
]
prove a deterministic equivalent of the SGD dynamics.

[
RL24
]
provide an algorithm that recovers orthogonal multi-index models with a sample complexity matching the information exponent

[
BAGJ21
]
.
We note that none of these methods pin-points exactly the sample complexity required to recover a multi-index model, which constitutes the focus of our work and is achieved via the class of spectral methods reviewed below.

Spectral estimators.

Spectral estimators have been applied to a variety of problems, e.g., community detection
[
Abb18
]
, angular synchronization
[
Sin11
]
and principal component analysis (PCA)
[
MV21
]
. In the setting of Gaussian design and proportional scaling between
n
and
d
, their asymptotic performance for single-index models is characterized by
[
LL20
]
.
Optimal weak recovery thresholds and optimal overlaps are identified by
[
MM19
]
and
[
LAL19
]
, respectively.
The above results are extended by
[
DBMM20
]
to subsampled Haar designs and by
[
ZJVM24
]
to correlated Gaussian designs. Rotationally invariant designs are considered by
[
MKLZ22
]
, which conjecture the form of the optimal spectral estimator using a linearization of AMP and the analysis of the Bethe Hessian. Such conjecture is partly addressed by
[
ZJVM24
]
, when the covariance of the

’s is rotationally invariant.
We note that, in the single-index case, optimal spectral methods match computational thresholds obtained from the stability of AMP
[
MM19
,
ZJVM24
]
and, in special cases, information-theoretic thresholds as well
[
MM19
]
. An optimally-designed spectral estimator is able to meet the information-theoretic limits of weak recovery also for a class of heteroscedastic PCA problems
[
ZM24
]
. Most closely related to our setting is work by
[

]
: the authors consider mixtures of single-index models with independent signals and provide precise asymptotics for spectral estimators by using a mix of tools from random matrix theory and the theory of AMP. In contrast, our approach is purely random matrix theoretic, and it allows us to handle a general class of multi-index models with arbitrary correlation among the signals.

We finally note that a parallel paper by
[
DDM+25
]
also considers a multi-index model with Gaussian design and introduces spectral estimators based on the linearization of AMP.
[

]
then conjecture that such spectral estimators are optimal in the sense that they achieve the computational threshold identified by
[
TDD+24
]
, providing both numerical and rigorous evidence in favor of the conjecture. Our work focuses on a family of spectral estimators popular in the related literature (cf. Eq. (41) in
[
DDM+25
]
and (
3.2
) in our work), and it resolves the conjecture of
[
DDM+25
]
for a wide class of link functions

, including all permutation-invariant ones, see
Remark

4.1
for details.

Finally, our third main result identifies an optimal preprocessing function allowing the spectral estimator to attain the lowest weak recovery threshold as per
Definition

(4.8)

Remark 4.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}(M)\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[E(y)ME(y)\right]

with

[

is given by

\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\succ 0_{p\times p}\\
\left\|M\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=1\end{subarray}}\left\|{\mathcal{F}}(M)\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\right)^{-1}

Note that the simultaneous diagonalizability of

Namely,

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\alpha ss^{\top}z}{\alpha-z}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\frac{\alpha{\mathcal{T}}(y)}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(y)}\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[ss^{\top}\mathrel{|}y\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\frac{\alpha{\mathcal{T}}(y)}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(y)}(E(y)+I_{p})\right].

Proof techniques

Equivalent spectral characterization.

Let us write

a_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}s_{i}&u_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
, with
s_{i}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{p})
and
u_{i}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{d-p})
. Thus, as
w_{i}^{*}\in\spn\{e_{1}^{(d)},\dots,e_{p}^{(d)}\}
(see
Equation

3.3
),
y_{i}
only depends on
s_{i}
and, more precisely,
y_{i}=q\left(\left\langle s_{i},w^{*}_{1}\right\rangle,\cdots,\left\langle s_{i},w^{*}_{p}\right\rangle,\varepsilon_{i}\right)
,
with the abuse of notation that the
s_{i}
’s are now vectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with the last
d-p
coordinates set to
0
.
Extending the notation to matrices, we have that
A=\begin{bmatrix}S&U\end{bmatrix},

where
S\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}s_{1}&\cdots&s_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times p}
and
U\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}&\cdots&u_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times(d-p)}
. Thus, we can re-write the spectral matrix
D_{n}
in (
3.2
) as

\displaystyle D_{n}=\frac{1}{n}A^{\top}ZA

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\begin{bmatrix}S^{\top}\\
U^{\top}\end{bmatrix}Z\begin{bmatrix}S&U\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{n}\begin{bmatrix}S^{\top}ZS&S^{\top}ZU\\
U^{\top}ZS&U^{\top}ZU\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a&q^{\top}\\
q&P\end{bmatrix},

(5.1)

where
a\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}S^{\top}ZS\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
,
q\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}U^{\top}ZS\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(d-p)\times p}
and
P\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}U^{\top}ZU\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(d-p)\times(d-p)}
.

R(\lambda_{k}^{D})_{i,i}\xrightarrow{a.s.}a^{\infty}_{i,i}-Q_{i}(\alpha_{k})=\mathbb{E}\left[s_{i}^{2}z\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{s_{i}^{2}z^{2}}{z-\alpha_{k}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\alpha_{k}s_{i}^{2}z}{\alpha_{k}-z}\right]=R^{\infty}(\alpha_{k})_{i,i}.

(5.12)

Moreover,
{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\mu)
is differentiable (see

A.9
in
Appendix

A
), so for its derivative it holds that

{\mathcal{L}}_{i}^{\prime}(\mu)\xrightarrow{a.s.}\zeta_{\delta}^{\prime}\circ{Q_{i}}^{-1}\circ G(\mu)\cdot\left(Q_{i}^{-1}\right)^{\prime}\circ G(\mu)\cdot G^{\prime}(\mu).

Plugging this into (
5.9
) we get

\frac{d}{d\lambda}R(\lambda_{k}^{D})_{i,i}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\frac{\frac{d}{d\alpha}(R^{\infty}(\alpha_{k})_{i,i})}{\zeta_{\delta}^{\prime}(\alpha_{k})}.

By performing similar calculations on the off-diagonal entries of
R(\lambda)
and
\frac{d}{d\lambda}R(\lambda)
,
Equation

5.9
follows (the complete proof is deferred to
Section

C.1
).

We are now ready to prove
Equation

4.6
when there is no multiplicity (
m=1
). Having a multiplicity adds technical complications (and also the assumption on the invariance of the eigenspace
E_{k}^{\infty}
), which are handled in
Section

C.2
where we also prove

4.5
and
4.7
.

First, note that the desired overlaps can be expressed as

\left|\left\langle v_{k}^{D},e_{j}^{(d)}\right\rangle\right|^{2}=(e_{j}^{(p)})^{\top}h_{k}h_{k}^{\top}(e_{j}^{(p)}).

(5.13)

Furthermore, recall that
(i)

h_{k}
is related to the unit norm eigenvector
\tilde{h}_{k}
of
R(\lambda_{k}^{D})
and to
\frac{d}{d\lambda}R(\lambda_{k}^{D})
via
Equation

5.4
, and that
(ii)
the limits of
R(\lambda_{k}^{D})
and to
\frac{d}{d\lambda}R(\lambda_{k}^{D})
are given by
Equation

5.9
.
Then, applying the results from
[
Kat95
, II.1.4]
, the orthonormal projection to the eigenspace corresponding to the
k
-th eigenvalue also converges, i.e.,

\Pi_{h_{k}}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\Pi_{h^{\infty}_{k}},

where
\Pi_{h_{k}}=\frac{h_{k}h_{k}^{\top}}{\left\|h_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}}=\tilde{h}_{k}\tilde{h}_{k}^{\top}
and
\Pi_{h^{\infty}_{k}}=\frac{h^{\infty}_{k}{h^{\infty}_{k}}^{\top}}{\left\|h^{\infty}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}}=h^{\infty}_{k}{h^{\infty}_{k}}^{\top}
. As a consequence, we have that

\left\|h_{k}\right\|_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\tilde{h}_{k}^{\top}\frac{d}{d\lambda}R(\lambda_{k}^{D})\tilde{h}_{k}}}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\frac{\sqrt{\zeta_{\delta}^{\prime}(\alpha_{k})}}{\sqrt{\zeta_{\delta}^{\prime}(\alpha_{k})+{h^{\infty}_{k}}^{\top}\frac{d}{d\lambda}R^{\infty}(\alpha_{k})h^{\infty}_{k}}}.

Combining these results, we obtain that

h_{k}h_{k}^{\top}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\frac{\zeta_{\delta}^{\prime}(\alpha_{k})h^{\infty}_{k}{h^{\infty}_{k}}^{\top}}{\zeta_{\delta}^{\prime}(\alpha_{k})+{h^{\infty}_{k}}^{\top}\frac{d}{d\lambda}R^{\infty}(\alpha_{k})h^{\infty}_{k}},

which, together with

5.13
, proves the claim.

6
Concluding remarks

This paper provides the first asymptotic characterization of spectral estimators for multi-index models: we unveil a phase transition in the top-
p
eigenvalues of the spectral matrix
D
in
Equation

3.2
, giving a low-dimensional (and simple to check) condition for spikes to emerge from the bulk of the spectrum; the eigenvalue phase transition is associated to the recovery of the subspace spanned by the signals via the corresponding eigenvectors and, under some technical conditions, we prove a precise expression for the asymptotic overlap; finally, we optimize the data preprocessing and identify the spectral estimator that weakly recovers the signal subspace with the smallest sample complexity.

Spectral methods are commonly used as a warm start for other procedures, often of iterative nature. Thus, our analysis provides the starting point to combine spectral estimators either with a simple linear estimator
[
MTV22
,
ZMV22
]
or with AMP
[
MV22
]
, with the objective of achieving – at least in absence of statistical-to-computational gaps – the Bayes-optimal limits of inference as characterized by
[
AMB+19
]
.

Lemma A.2

Lemma A.3

For

\lim_{\mu_{1}\to{\lambda^{a}_{i}}^{+}}L_{j}(\mu_{1})=\lim_{\mu_{2}\to{\lambda^{a}_{i}}^{-}}L_{j-1}(\mu_{2}).

To simplify exposition, let us denote by

\mu_{2}\to{\lambda^{a}_{i}}^{-}

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(M_{\mu_{1}})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{j}\left((\Pi_{r}+\Pi_{{r}^{\perp}})M_{\mu_{1}}(\Pi_{r}+\Pi_{{r}^{\perp}})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{j}\left(\frac{1}{2}\Pi_{r}M_{\mu_{1}}\Pi_{r}+\Pi_{{r}^{\perp}}M_{\mu_{1}}\Pi_{{r}^{\perp}}\right)+\lambda_{d-p}\left(\frac{1}{2}\Pi_{r}M_{\mu_{1}}\Pi_{r}+\Pi_{{r}^{\perp}}M_{\mu_{1}}\Pi_{r}+\Pi_{r}M_{\mu_{1}}\Pi_{{r}^{\perp}}\right)

Lemma A.5

An eigenvalue

Proposition A.8

\displaystyle=\lambda^{D}_{i}h_{i},

\displaystyle qh_{i}+Pg_{i}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{D}_{i}g_{i}.

\begin{split}\tilde{g}_{i}^{\top}q(a-\lambda_{i}^{D}I_{p})^{-2}q^{\top}\tilde{g}_{i}&=\tilde{g}_{i}^{\top}\left(\frac{d}{d\lambda}q(a-\lambda I_{p})^{-1}q^{\top}\right)(\lambda_{i}^{D})\tilde{g}_{i}\\
&=\left(\frac{d}{d\lambda}\lambda_{k}(q(a-\lambda I_{p})^{-1}q^{\top})\right)(\lambda_{i}^{D})\\
&=\frac{d}{d\lambda}L_{k}(\lambda_{i}^{D}).\end{split}

(C.20)

Here, when the eigenvalue

is simple, the second equality follows from

Lan64
, Theorem 5]
and the fact that
\tilde{g}_{i}
is the eigenvector corresponding to the

-th eigenvalue of the matrix

. The case in which the eigenvalue

has multiplicity

, Theorem 7]
, and it is discussed at the end of the argument.

Note that, by definition, it holds

We will prove that

(C.21)

which implies
\left\|h_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\xrightarrow{a.s.}0
and hence gives the desired statement in
Equation

4.7
. Recall that the assumption underlying
Equation

4.7
is that
Equation

C.15
holds. This happens if either the
\alpha_{i}
associated to
\lambda_{i}^{D}
is s.t.
\alpha_{i}\leq\bar{\lambda}_{\delta}
or
i
exceeds the number of solutions of
Equation

4.2
(i.e.,
\lambda_{i}^{D}
is not associated to any solution of
Equation

4.2
). We handle these two cases separately.

Case 1:
\lambda\in]\lambda_{i}^{a^{\infty}},t_{i}^{\infty}[
. This corresponds to the case in which
\alpha_{i}\leq\bar{\lambda}_{\delta}
.
Applying
Proposition

B.2
, we have

\tilde{L}_{i}(\lambda)\xrightarrow{a.s.}\tilde{L}_{i}^{\infty}(\lambda)=\zeta_{\delta}((\lambda_{i}^{\infty})^{-1}(\lambda)).

By combining
Equation

C.15
and Lemma A.2 in
[

]
, it follows that

\frac{d}{d\lambda}\tilde{L}_{i}(\lambda_{i}^{D})\xrightarrow{a.s.}\zeta_{\delta}^{\prime}((\lambda_{i}^{\infty})^{-1}(\zeta_{\delta}(\bar{\lambda}_{\delta})))\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{\prime\infty}((\lambda_{i}^{\infty})^{-1}(\zeta_{\delta}(\bar{\lambda}_{\delta})))},

where the denominator is non-zero, as

is strictly decreasing. As

, the derivative
\zeta_{\delta}^{\prime}
computed at that point is

, which gives
Equation

C.21
.

Case 2:
\lambda\in]t_{i}^{\infty},+\infty[
.
This corresponds to the case in which
\lambda_{i}^{D}
is not associated to any
\alpha_{i}
. Applying again

B.2
, we have

\tilde{L}_{i}(\lambda)\xrightarrow{a.s.}\zeta_{\delta}(\bar{\lambda}_{\delta}).

Then, again by
[
LL20
, Lemma A.2]
it follows that

\frac{d}{d\lambda}\tilde{L}_{i}(\lambda)\xrightarrow{a.s.}\frac{d}{d\lambda}(\zeta_{\delta}(\bar{\lambda}_{\delta}))=0,

as the LHS is a constant function that does not depend on

We conclude by handling the two special cases mentioned during the argument above.
If
\lambda_{i}^{D}
has a multiplicity

, then the

corresponding to the

gives that such derivatives are the eigenvalues of the

Finally, if

Appendix D
Invariance of the eigenspace for permutation-invariant link functions

Proposition D.1
.

If the link function
q
is permutation invariant in
m
coordinates, then the matrix
R^{\infty}(\alpha)
has eigenspaces that do not change with
\alpha
, of combined dimension
m-1
.

Proof.

Let us denote by
w_{i}
the
i
-th column of the matrix
\widetilde{W}^{*}
as in (
3.3
), representing the top-
p
entries of the reparametrized signal.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that
q
is permutation invariant in the first
m
coordinates, i.e.,

q(t_{1},\dots,t_{m},t_{m+1}\dots,t_{p},\varepsilon)=q(t_{\pi(1)},\dots,t_{\pi(m)},t_{m+1}\dots,t_{p},\varepsilon),

for any permutation
\pi:[m]\to[m]
.
Let
E
be the span of
\{w_{i}-w_{i+1}\,:\,i\in[m-1]\}
. Note that
E
has dimension
m-1
, due to the linear independence of the signals
w_{i}
. We will prove that
E
is a direct sum of eigenspaces of
R^{\infty}(\alpha)
, neither of which depends on
\alpha
.

Let us define
u_{i}
as the image of
w_{i}-w_{i+1}
under
R^{\infty}(\alpha)
, that is

\displaystyle u_{i}\coloneq R^{\infty}(\alpha)(w_{i}-w_{i+1})=\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{s\left\langle s,w_{i}-w_{i+1}\right\rangle{\mathcal{T}}(y)}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(y)}\right],

for
i\in[m-1]
. Let us denote by
x_{i}
the component of
u_{i}
that is orthogonal to
w_{i}
and
w_{i+1}
, i.e.,
x_{i}\coloneq\Pi_{\{w_{i},w_{i+1}\}^{\perp}}u_{i}
. Then, it holds that

u_{i}=a_{1}w_{i}+a_{2}w_{i+1}+x_{i},

(D.1)

for some coefficients
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
. We will first prove that
x_{i}=0
. Towards that end, let
{\mathcal{S}}_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{p}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
be an isometric reflection that sends
w_{i}
to
w_{i+1}
,
w_{i+1}
to
w_{i}
, and keeps
w_{j}
fixed for
j\notin\{i,i+1\}
. Such a reflection exists due to the assumed linear independence of the
w_{i}
’s. Notice that the normal distribution in
{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
is invariant to the transformation
{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
. Thus, it follows that

\displaystyle\left\langle u_{i},x_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\langle s,x_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle s,w_{i}-w_{i+1}\right\rangle{\mathcal{T}}(y)}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(y)}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\langle s,x_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle s,w_{i}\right\rangle{\mathcal{T}}(y)}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(y)}\right]-\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\langle s,x_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle s,w_{i+1}\right\rangle{\mathcal{T}}(y)}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(y)}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\langle{\mathcal{S}}_{i}s,x_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle{\mathcal{S}}_{i}s,w_{i}\right\rangle{\mathcal{T}}(q((\widetilde{W}^{*})^{\top}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}s,\varepsilon))}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(q((\widetilde{W}^{*})^{\top}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}s,\varepsilon))}\right]-\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\langle s,x_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle s,w_{i+1}\right\rangle{\mathcal{T}}(y)}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(y)}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\langle s,x_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle s,w_{i+1}\right\rangle{\mathcal{T}}(q((\widetilde{W}^{*})^{\top}s,\varepsilon))}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(q((\widetilde{W}^{*})^{\top}s,\varepsilon))}\right]-\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\langle s,x_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle s,w_{i+1}\right\rangle{\mathcal{T}}(y)}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(y)}\right]

\displaystyle=0,

due to the permutation invariance of
q
, the fact that
{\mathcal{S}}_{i}x_{i}=x_{i}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{i}w_{i}=w_{i+1}
. Therefore, it must be that
x_{i}=0
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\left\langle u_{i},w_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\langle s,w_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle s,w_{i}-w_{i+1}\right\rangle{\mathcal{T}}(y)}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(y)}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\langle{\mathcal{S}}_{i}s,w_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle{\mathcal{S}}_{i}s,w_{i}-w_{i+1}\right\rangle{\mathcal{T}}(q((\widetilde{W}^{*})^{\top}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}s)}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(q((\widetilde{W}^{*})^{\top}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}s)}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\langle s,w_{i+1}\right\rangle\left\langle s,w_{i+1}-w_{i}\right\rangle{\mathcal{T}}(q((\widetilde{W}^{*})^{\top}s)}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(q((\widetilde{W}^{*})^{\top}s)}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\langle s,w_{i+1}\right\rangle\left\langle s,w_{i+1}-w_{i}\right\rangle{\mathcal{T}}(y)}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(y)}\right]

\displaystyle=-\left\langle u_{i},w_{i+1}\right\rangle.

Combining this with the decomposition in (
D.1
) implies that

a_{1}+a_{2}\left\langle w_{i},w_{i+1}\right\rangle=-a_{1}\left\langle w_{i+1},w_{i}\right\rangle-a_{2}.

As by assumption the signals
w_{i}
and
w_{i+1}
are linearly independent, it cannot be that
\left\langle w_{i},w_{i+1}\right\rangle=-1
, so it must be that
a_{1}=-a_{2}
. This proves that
w_{i}-w_{i+1}
are indeed eigenvectors for every
\alpha
.
By definition, it holds that

E=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m-1}\operatorname{span}\{u_{i}\}.

Thus, it is left to prove that any pair of eigenvectors
u_{i}
and
u_{j}
, for
i,j\in[m-1]
, either have the same eigenvalue for all
\alpha
, or for no
\alpha
. We denote by
\lambda_{u_{i}}(\alpha)
the eigenvalue that corresponds to the eigenvector
u_{i}
. Similar to before, let
{\mathcal{S}}^{(i)}:{\mathbb{R}}^{p}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
be an isometric reflection that sends
w_{i}
to
w_{i+2}
,
w_{i+2}
to
w_{i}
, and keeps
w_{j}
fixed for
j\notin\{i,i+2\}
. Such a reflection exists due to the assumed linear independence of the
w_{i}
’s. Also, the normal distribution is invariant to the transformation
{\mathcal{S}}^{(i)}
. Then, it follows that

\displaystyle\lambda_{u_{i}}(\alpha)\cdot\left\|w_{i}-w_{i+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\left\langle u_{i},w_{i}-w_{i+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\langle s,w_{i}-w_{i+1}\right\rangle^{2}{\mathcal{T}}(y)}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(y)}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\langle{\mathcal{S}}^{(i)}s,w_{i}-w_{i+1}\right\rangle^{2}{\mathcal{T}}(q((\widetilde{W}^{*})^{\top}{\mathcal{S}}^{(i)}s,\varepsilon))}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(q((\widetilde{W}^{*})^{\top}{\mathcal{S}}^{(i)}s,\varepsilon))}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\langle s,w_{i+2}-w_{i+1}\right\rangle^{2}{\mathcal{T}}(q((\widetilde{W}^{*})^{\top}s,\varepsilon))}{\alpha-{\mathcal{T}}(q((\widetilde{W}^{*})^{\top}s,\varepsilon))}\right]

\displaystyle=\left\langle u_{i+1},w_{i+1}-w_{i+2}\right\rangle=\lambda_{u_{i+1}}(\alpha)\cdot\left\|w_{i+2}-w_{i+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

This proves that
\lambda_{u_{i}}(\alpha)/\lambda_{u_{i+1}}(\alpha)
does not depend on
\alpha
and, hence,
\lambda_{u_{i}}(\alpha)/\lambda_{u_{j}}(\alpha)
does not depend on
\alpha
for all
i,j\in[m-1]
, implying the existence of an eigenspace of dimension
m-1
that does not change with
\alpha
.
∎

We calculate term by term. First, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[p(y\mathrel{|}s)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}e^{-\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{2}}e^{-\frac{s_{2}^{2}}{2}}\delta(y-s_{1}s_{2})ds_{1}ds_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}2\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{2}}e^{-\frac{s_{2}^{2}}{2}}\delta(y-s_{1}s_{2})ds_{1}ds_{2},

where we have assumed that
y>0
(similar passages hold for
y\leq 0
). The constant
2
pops out, since there are two symmetric cases for
y>0
, namely
s_{1},s_{2}>0
and
s_{1},s_{2}<0
. Continuing with the change of variable
x_{1}=s_{1}s_{2}
and
x_{2}=\frac{s_{1}}{s_{2}}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}2\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{2}}e^{-\frac{s_{2}^{2}}{2}}\delta(y-s_{1}s_{2})ds_{1}ds_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{x_{1}x_{2}}{2}}e^{-\frac{x_{1}/x_{2}}{2}}\delta(y-x_{1})\frac{1}{2x_{2}}dx_{1}dx_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-y\left(\frac{x_{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2x_{2}}\right)}\frac{1}{2x_{2}}dx_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-y\cosh(t)}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-y\cosh(t)}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{K_{0}(\left|y\right|)}{\pi},

where we did the change of variable
e^{t}=\frac{1}{x_{2}}
, and
K_{0}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind. Let us now calculate the second term for the choice
u_{c}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,1)
, which can be verified to be optimal,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[p(y\mathrel{|}s)\left\langle s,u_{c}\right\rangle^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}e^{-\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{2}}e^{-\frac{s_{2}^{2}}{2}}\delta(y-s_{1}s_{2})\left\langle s,u_{c}\right\rangle^{2}ds_{1}ds_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}2\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{2}}e^{-\frac{s_{2}^{2}}{2}}\delta(y-s_{1}s_{2})\frac{1}{2}(s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}+2s_{1}s_{2})ds_{1}ds_{2},

where, as before, we have assumed that
y>0
(similar passages hold for
y\leq 0
). Continuing with the change of variable
x_{1}=s_{1}s_{2}
and
x_{2}=\frac{s_{1}}{s_{2}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[p(y\mathrel{|}s)\left\langle s,u_{c}\right\rangle^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{2}}e^{-\frac{s_{2}^{2}}{2}}\delta(y-s_{1}s_{2})\frac{1}{2}(s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}+2s_{1}s_{2})ds_{1}ds_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{x_{1}x_{2}}{2}}e^{-\frac{x_{1}/x_{2}}{2}}\delta(y-x_{1})\frac{1}{2}x_{1}\left(x_{2}+\frac{1}{x_{2}}+2\right)\frac{1}{2x_{2}}dx_{1}dx_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-y\left(\frac{x_{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2x_{2}}\right)}\frac{1}{2}y\left(x_{2}+\frac{1}{x_{2}}+2\right)\frac{1}{2x_{2}}dx_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-y\cosh(t)}y(\cosh(t)+1)dx_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-y\cosh(t)}y(\cosh(t)+1)dx_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{yK_{0}(\left|y\right|)+\left|y\right|K_{1}(\left|y\right|)}{\pi},

where
K_{1}
is the modified Bessel function with parameter equal to 1. Moreover, the absolute value in the final result is readily obtained after analyzing the case
y<0
, which is analogous.
Thus, we get

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}^{*}(y)=1-\frac{K_{0}(\left|y\right|)}{yK_{0}(\left|y\right|)+\left|y\right|K_{1}(\left|y\right|)}.

(F.1)

Finally, let us calculate the weak recovery threshold
\delta_{c}
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta_{c}}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{\left(\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[p(y\mathrel{|}s)(\left\langle s,u\right\rangle^{2}-1)\right]\right)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[p(y\mathrel{|}s)\right]}dy=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{\left(\frac{yK_{0}(\left|y\right|)+\left|y\right|K_{1}(\left|y\right|)}{\pi}-\frac{K_{0}(\left|y\right|)}{\pi}\right)^{2}}{\frac{K_{0}(\left|y\right|)}{\pi}}dy\approx 1.68421\approx(0.5937)^{-1},

where the exact value was calculated in WolframAlpha. We note that this value exactly matches the threshold in
[
TDD+24
, pg.9]
.

F.2
Mixed phase retrieval

Let
\eta=\mathbb{P}\left(\varepsilon=1\right)
and define the following auxiliary quantities:

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\sqrt{4\rho^{2}\eta(1-\eta)+(2\eta-1)^{2}}\right],

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 1+\frac{2(\gamma-\eta)}{\eta}+\left(\frac{\gamma-\eta}{\eta\rho}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\eta+(1-\eta)\rho^{2}+\frac{2(\gamma-\eta)}{\eta}+\left(\frac{\gamma-\eta}{\eta\rho}\right)^{2}\left[(1-\eta)+\eta\rho^{2}\right],

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(1-\eta)(1-\rho^{2})+\left(\frac{\gamma-\eta}{\eta\rho}\right)^{2}\eta(1-\rho^{2}),

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle\coloneqq a_{1}-a_{3}.

Denote by
\ell^{*}
the unique solution in
\ell\in](\gamma/\eta)^{2}-b,\infty[
to the following equation:

\displaystyle(\ell-a_{3})\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}e^{-y^{2}/2}\frac{(y^{2}-1)^{2}}{a_{2}y^{2}+\ell}\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}\delta}.

Then, the optimal preprocessing function is given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{y^{2}-1}{\left[a_{2}+\gamma(\ell^{*}-a_{3})\right]y^{2}+\ell^{*}-\gamma(\ell^{*}-a_{3})}.

(F.2)

This expression maximizes the asymptotic overlap
\left|\left\langle v_{1}^{D},w_{1}^{*}\right\rangle\right|
obtained by specializing our general formula in
Theorem

4.3
to the mixed phase retrieval model. The derivations are along similar lines as detailed in
Section

F.1
for the model
y=s_{1}s_{2}
, and we leave out the explicit calculations.

Appendix G
Equivalence to
[
TDD+24
]

Proposition G.1
.

For simultaneously diagonalizable matrices
E(y)
and orthogonal signals, the recovery threshold from
Theorem

4.3
matches the one in Lemma 4.1 of
[
TDD+24
]
. This equivalence holds for all permutation-invariant link functions.

Proof.

The weak recovery threshold in
[
TDD+24
]
is characterized as

\frac{1}{\alpha_{c}}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in{\mathcal{S}}_{p}^{+}\\
\left\|M\right\|_{F}=1\end{subarray}}\left\|{\mathcal{F}}(M)\right\|_{F}.

Here,
{\mathcal{F}}
is an operator defined as

{\mathcal{F}}(M)\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[E(y)ME(y)\right],

where
E(y)\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[ss^{\top}-I_{p}\mathrel{|}y\right]
.
As noted in the proof of Lemma 4.1 in
[
TDD+24
]
, the linear operator
{\mathcal{F}}
is a self-map on the cone of positive semi-definite matrices.
Indeed, for any positive semi-definite matrix
M
and any vector
v
,
v^{\top}{\mathcal{F}}(M)v=\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[v^{\top}E(y)ME(y)v\right]=\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\left\|M^{1/2}E(y)v\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\geq 0
, meaning that
{\mathcal{F}}(M)
is also positive semi-definite.
By the generalized Perron–Frobenius theorem for cone-preserving maps
[
KR48
]
(see also Theorem 1.1 in
[
Du06
]
, or Theorem 19.2 and Exercise 12 in
[
Dei85
, §19]
), it holds that
1/\alpha_{c}
is the largest eigenvalue of the linear operator
{\mathcal{F}}
.
That is, there exists a positive semi-definite matrix
M^{*}
with
\left\|M^{*}\right\|_{F}=1
for which it holds

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in{\mathcal{S}}_{p}^{+}\\
\left\|M\right\|_{F}=1\end{subarray}}\left\|{\mathcal{F}}(M)\right\|_{F}=\left\|{\mathcal{F}}(M^{*})\right\|_{F}.

The corresponding eigen-equation
{\mathcal{F}}(M^{*})=\lambda^{*}M^{*}
implies
\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(M^{*}),M^{*}\right\rangle_{F}=\lambda^{*}
.
The LHS must be non-negative since
\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(M^{*}),M^{*}\right\rangle_{F}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{M^{*}}\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(M^{*}),v_{i}^{M^{*}}(v_{i}^{M^{*}})^{\top}\right\rangle_{F}\geq 0
due to the cone-preserving property of
{\mathcal{F}}
and non-negativity of each
\lambda_{i}^{M^{*}}
.
Now we have
\left\|{\mathcal{F}}(M^{*})\right\|_{F}^{2}=\lambda^{*}\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(M^{*}),M^{*}\right\rangle_{F}=\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(M^{*}),M^{*}\right\rangle_{F}^{2}
, from which it follows that
\left\|{\mathcal{F}}(M^{*})\right\|_{F}=\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(M^{*}),M^{*}\right\rangle_{F}
, by non-negativity of the RHS just shown.
So, one can rewrite the optimal threshold as

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in{\mathcal{S}}_{p}^{+}\\
\left\|M\right\|_{F}=1\end{subarray}}\left\|{\mathcal{F}}(M)\right\|_{F}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in{\mathcal{S}}_{p}^{+}\\
\left\|M\right\|_{F}=1\end{subarray}}\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(M),M\right\rangle_{F}.

Let us assume first that this maximum is obtained for a certain rank-1 matrix. Then, it would hold

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in{\mathcal{S}}_{p}^{+}\\
\left\|M\right\|_{F}=1\end{subarray}}\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(M),M\right\rangle_{F}=\max_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(uu^{\top}),uu^{\top}\right\rangle_{F},

(G.1)

where
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
.
Writing this out, we have

\displaystyle\max_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(uu^{\top}),uu^{\top}\right\rangle_{F}

\displaystyle=\max_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\tr\left(\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[E(y)uu^{\top}E(y)\right]uu^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\tr\left(u^{\top}E(y)uu^{\top}E(y)u\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\max_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[\left\langle s,u\right\rangle^{2}-1\mathrel{|}y\right]\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\max_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[\frac{p(y\mathrel{|}s)}{p(y)}(\left\langle s,u\right\rangle^{2}-1)\right]\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\max_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\frac{\left(\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[p(y\mathrel{|}s)(\left\langle s,u\right\rangle^{2}-1)\right]\right)^{2}}{\left(\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[p(y\mathrel{|}s)\right]\right)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\max_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}p(y)\frac{\left(\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[p(y\mathrel{|}s)(\left\langle s,u\right\rangle^{2}-1)\right]\right)^{2}}{\left(\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[p(y\mathrel{|}s)\right]\right)^{2}}dy

\displaystyle=\max_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{\left(\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[p(y\mathrel{|}s)(\left\langle s,u\right\rangle^{2}-1)\right]\right)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[p(y\mathrel{|}s)\right]}dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta_{c}},

where
\delta_{c}
is exactly the threshold from
Theorem

4.3
.

Let us prove that when
E(y)
has the same eigenvectors regardless of
y
, the equality in (
G.1
) holds. Towards that end, we denote by
u_{i}
(
i\in[p]
) the orthonormal eigenbasis of
E(y)
.
We introduce the matrices

A_{i}\coloneq u_{i}u_{i}^{\top},\quad B_{j,k}=\frac{u_{j}u_{k}^{\top}+u_{k}u_{j}^{\top}}{\sqrt{2}},

for
i,j,k\in[p]
s.t.
j\neq k
. It holds that

\displaystyle\left\langle A_{i_{1}},A_{i_{2}}\right\rangle_{F}=0,

\displaystyle\left\langle A_{i_{1}},A_{i_{1}}\right\rangle_{F}=1,

\displaystyle\left\langle A_{i_{1}},B_{j_{1},k_{1}}\right\rangle_{F}=0,

\displaystyle\left\langle B_{j_{1},k_{1}},B_{j_{2},k_{2}}\right\rangle_{F}=0,

\displaystyle\left\langle B_{j_{1},k_{1}},B_{j_{1},k_{1}}\right\rangle_{F}=1,

for any
i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2},k_{1},k_{2}\in[p]
such that
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
and
(j_{1},k_{1})\neq(j_{2},k_{2})
.
Due to the fact that
u_{1},\dots,u_{p}
form an orthonormal basis, the set

U\coloneq\{A_{i},B_{j,k};i,j,k\in[p],j<k\}

is an orthonormal basis of the symmetric matrices
{\mathcal{S}}_{p}
equipped with Frobenius inner product.
Notice also that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in{\mathcal{S}}_{p}^{+}\\
\left\|M\right\|_{F}=1\end{subarray}}\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(M),M\right\rangle_{F}\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in{\mathcal{S}}_{p}\\
\left\|M\right\|_{F}=1\end{subarray}}\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(M),M\right\rangle_{F},

since all positive definite matrices are by definition symmetric. By the previous discussion, we decompose the matrix
M\in{\mathcal{S}}_{p}
such that
\left\|M\right\|_{F}=1
, as

M=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}A_{i}+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq p}\beta_{j,k}B_{j,k},

where
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j,k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\sum_{i=1}\alpha_{i}^{2}+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq p}\beta_{j,k}^{2}=1.

Since the
u_{i}
’s are eigenvectors of
E(y)
, it follows that every element of the set
U
is an eigenvector of the operator
{\mathcal{F}}
. Let us denote the corresponding eigenvalues as
\lambda_{A_{i}}
and
\lambda_{B_{j,k}}
. Furthermore, it holds that

\displaystyle\lambda_{B_{j,k}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(B_{j,k}),B_{j,k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[u_{j}^{\top}E(y)u_{j}\cdot u_{k}^{\top}E(y)u_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[(u_{j}^{\top}E(y)u_{j})^{2}\right]}\cdot\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[(u_{k}^{\top}E(y)u_{k})^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(A_{j}),A_{j}\right\rangle}\cdot\sqrt{\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(A_{k}),A_{k}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\lambda_{A_{j}}\lambda_{A_{k}}},

where Hölder’s inequality was used to get the bound.
Then, it holds that

\begin{split}\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(M),M\right\rangle_{F}&=\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}A_{i}+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq p}\beta_{j,k}B_{j,k}\right),\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}A_{i}+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq p}\beta_{j,k}B_{j,k}\right\rangle\\
&=\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}\lambda_{A_{i}}A_{i}+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq p}\beta_{j,k}\lambda_{B_{j,k}}B_{j,k},\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}A_{i}+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq p}\beta_{j,k}B_{j,k}\right\rangle\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}^{2}\lambda_{A_{i}}+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq p}\beta_{j,k}^{2}\lambda_{B_{j,k}}\\
&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}^{2}\lambda_{A_{i}}+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq p}\beta_{j,k}^{2}\sqrt{\lambda_{A_{j}}\lambda_{A_{k}}}\\
&\leq\max_{i}\lambda_{A_{i}}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}\alpha_{i}^{2}+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq p}\beta_{j,k}^{2}\right)\\
&=\max_{i}\lambda_{A_{i}}\\
&\leq\max_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}(uu^{\top}),uu^{\top}\right\rangle_{F},\end{split}

(G.2)

which proves (
G.1
).

Lastly, we verify that
E(y)
is diagonalizable when the link function
q
is permutation invariant. Towards that end, it holds for arbitrary
i,j\in[p]
that

\displaystyle(E(y))_{i,i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[s_{i}^{2}-1\mathrel{|}y\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[s_{i}^{2}-1\mathrel{|}q(\dots,s_{i},\dots,s_{j},\dots)=y\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[s_{j}^{2}-1\mathrel{|}q(\dots,s_{j},\dots,s_{i},\dots)=y\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[s_{j}^{2}-1\mathrel{|}q(\dots,s_{i},\dots,s_{j},\dots)=y\right]

\displaystyle=(E(y))_{j,j},

since the link function
q
is permutation invariant. Similarly, for arbitrary
i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}\in[p]
such that
i_{1}\neq j_{1}
and
i_{2}\neq j_{2}
, it holds that

\displaystyle(E(y))_{i_{1},j_{1}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[s_{i_{1}}s_{j_{1}}\mathrel{|}y\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[s_{i_{1}}s_{j_{1}}\mathrel{|}q(\dots,s_{i_{1}},\dots,s_{i_{2}},\dots,s_{j_{1}},\dots,s_{j_{2}},\dots)=y\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[s_{i_{2}}s_{j_{2}}\mathrel{|}q(\dots,s_{i_{2}},\dots,s_{i_{1}},\dots,s_{j_{2}},\dots,s_{j_{1}},\dots)=y\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[s_{i_{2}}s_{j_{2}}\mathrel{|}q(\dots,s_{i_{1}},\dots,s_{i_{2}},\dots,s_{j_{1}},\dots,s_{j_{2}},\dots)=y\right]

\displaystyle=(E(y))_{i_{2},j_{2}}.

Thus, if we denote by
a(y)\coloneq E(y)_{i,i}
for arbitrary
i\in[p]
and by
b(y)\coloneq E(y)_{i_{1},j_{1}}
for arbitrary
i_{1},j_{1}\in[p]
s.t.
i_{1}\neq j_{1}
, it follows that

E(y)=\begin{bmatrix}a(y)&b(y)&\cdots&b(y)\\
b(y)&a(y)&\cdots&b(y)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
b(y)&b(y)&\cdots&a(y)\end{bmatrix}.

(G.3)

Notice that this matrix is going to have an eigenbasis

\displaystyle u_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneq(1,1,1,\dots 1)/\sqrt{p},

\displaystyle u_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneq(1,-1,0,\dots 0)/\sqrt{2},

\displaystyle u_{p}

\displaystyle\coloneq(0,0,\dots,0,1,-1)/\sqrt{2},

regardless of the value of
y
, which proves the final claim.
∎
