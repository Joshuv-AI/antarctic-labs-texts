On two fundamental properties of the zeros of spectrograms of noisy signals
Arnaud Poinas, Rémi Bardenet

Abstract
The spatial distribution of the zeros of the spectrogram is significantly altered when a signal is added to white Gaussian noise. The zeros tend to delineate the support of the signal, and deterministic structures form in the presence of interference, as if the zeros were trapped. While sophisticated methods have been proposed to detect signals as holes in the pattern of spectrogram zeros, few formal arguments have been made to support the delineation and trapping effects. Through detailed computations for simple toy signals, we show that two basic mathematical arguments –the intensity of zeros and Rouché’s theorem– allow discussing delineation and trapping, and the influence of parameters like the signal-to-noise ratio. In particular, interfering chirps, even nearly superimposed, yield an easy-to-detect deterministic structure among zeros.



I
Introduction

The short-time Fourier transform (STFT) of a signal, and its squared modulus the
spectrogram
, are foundational stones of time-frequency analysis
(
Coh95
;
Fla98
;
Gro01
)
.
Classical approaches to time-frequency detection, denoising, or frequency estimation have relied on identifying regions where the spectrogram is large.
Since the seminal letter of Flandrin
Fla15
,
zeros
of the spectrogram have also received a lot of attention.
Zeros of the spectrogram of white Gaussian noise spread very uniformly across the time-frequency (TF) plane, and Flandrin
Fla15
observed that, in the presence of an additional deterministic signal, the zeros tend to
delineate
the support of the noiseless signal.
Similarly, when one keeps the same realization of white Gaussian noise but increases the signal-to-noise ratio, zeros seem to become
trapped
in areas surrounded by emerging signal ridges; see Figure
1
.
These two foundational claims on delineation and trapping, initially based on empirical observations, have become folklore in subsequent papers, justifying e.g. equating holes in the pattern of zeros to the support of signal components
(
BaFlCh18
)
.
In this letter, we give simple theoretical arguments to cement these claims, demonstrating delineation and trapping on classical examples of synthetic signals.
In particular, we assess the effect of the signal-to-noise ratio and the relative amplitude of signal components.

In Section
II
, we give the intensity of the zeros of the STFT of a noisy signal, and a statement of Rouché’s classical theorem in the context of spectrograms.
The intensity illustrates the delineation effect, while Rouché’s theorem is the reason why
(i)
zeros appear to be trapped in valleys surrounded by signal, and
(ii)
the position of zeros cristallizes in the presence of interference.
In Section
III
, we apply these two results to a Hermite function and a linear chirp with added noise, while we keep the case of two interfering parallel chirps for Section
IV
.
In particular, we illustrate the delineation and trapping effects, as well as extreme trapping resulting in near-deterministic cristallization of the zeros.
The case of parallel chirps of different amplitude also showcases a somewhat counterintuitive effect: informally, zeros that result from the interference between the chirps move to the
same
side of both chirps.
This behavior is in stark contrast with that of the local maxima of the spectrogram, pointing to a potential advantage of zeros in the identification of interfering sources.

II
Main results

Let
y:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{C}
be locally integrable, representing a measured signal.
The spectrogram of
y
with Gaussian window
g:t\mapsto 2^{1/4}e^{-\pi t^{2}}
is the function

\textnormal{Spec}_{g}({y}):z\mapsto\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}{y}(t){g(t-\tau)}e^{-2i\pi\omega t}\mathrm{d}t\right|^{2}

of the complex variable
z=\tau+i\omega\in\mathbb{C}
.
The real and imaginary parts of
z
are respectively interpreted as
time
and
frequency
.
The spectrogram can be rewritten as
\textnormal{Spec}_{g}(y)(z)=e^{-\pi|z|^{2}}|B(y)(\bar{z})|^{2}
, where
B(y)
is a holomorphic function called the
Bargmann transform
of
y
; see
Folland89
;
Gro01
.
For the remainder of the paper, we consider
y=x+\xi
to be the sum of a deterministic signal
x
and a complex white Gaussian noise
\xi
; see
BaHa19
for a detailed mathematical definition of
\xi
.
The zeros of
\textnormal{Spec}_{g}(x+\xi)
are a random configuration of points in
\mathbb{C}
, i.e. a
point process
.
Our aim is to describe a few properties of that point process that relate to signal detection and reconstruction.

II-A

Explaining delineation: the intensity of zeros

We first wish to explain the general tendency of zeros to avoid locations where the spectrogram of the noiseless signal
x
is large, while concentrating around that region.
For that purpose we compute the intensity function
\rho{:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}}
of the zeros.
Denoting by
N(A)
the number of zeros of the spectrogram in a bounded Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{C}
, the intensity function is defined as the density with respect to Lebesgue of the measure
\mathbb{E}N(\cdot)
, so that
\mathbb{E}[N(A)]=\int_{A}\rho(z)\mathrm{d}z
.
Independently from our work and for a different purpose, an expression of
\rho
has already been established in
(
Escudero2021
)
, as a function of the Bargmann transform of the noiseless signal.
We rather use the spectrogram of the signal, which makes
\rho
easier to interpret.

Proposition II.1
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}
.
Then

\rho(z)=\left(1+\textnormal{Spec}_{g}(x)(z)+\frac{\Delta\textnormal{Spec}_{g}(x)(z)}{4\pi}\right)\\
\times\exp\left(-\textnormal{Spec}_{g}(x)(z)\right).

(II.1)

The proof is in Appendix
.
The exponential term in (
II.1
) explains why zeros avoid locations where the spectrogram of the signal is large.
Now, when
\textnormal{Spec}_{g}(x)(z)\approx 0
,
\rho(z)
is approximately
1+\Delta\textnormal{Spec}_{g}(x)(z)/4\pi
, while
\Delta\textnormal{Spec}_{g}(x)(z)\geqslant 0
.
Consequently, zeros tends to accumulate around the contours of the signal support, defined as regions where the spectrogram of the noiseless signal
x
is negligible but
\Delta\textnormal{Spec}_{g}(x)(z)
is not.
Finally, in locations where
\textnormal{Spec}_{g}(x)(z)
is both negligible and has barely any variation,
\rho(z)\approx 1
, which is the intensity of the zeros of the spectrogram of white noise only
(
BaFlCh18
)
.

II-B

Explaining trapping: Rouché’s theorem

One specific behavior of zeros of spectrogram of noisy signals that cannot be explained by the intensity function is their tendency to get "trapped".
As can be observed in Figures
1
and
4
, when the spectrogram of a signal has a zero surrounded by high values then, when noise is added to the signal, the same zero will still be present at around the same location.
This behavior is a direct consequence of Rouché’s théorem, see e.g.
Rud87
and Section
of the Appendix for a rephrasing with spectrograms.
Informally, if
\textnormal{Spec}_{g}(x)
has large enough values on a closed curve
C
, then
\textnormal{Spec}_{g}(\xi+x)
is very likely to have the same number of zeros as
\textnormal{Spec}_{g}(x)
in the interior of
C
, counted with multiplicity.

III-A

Hermite functions

Let

t\mapsto e^{-2\pi t^{2}}

Let

\|h_{k}\|_{2}=1

h_{0},h_{1},\dots
form an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\textnormal{Spec}_{g}(h_{k})(z)=e^{-\pi|z|^{2}}\frac{\pi^{k}|z|^{2k}}{k!};

(
Gro01

.
Figure
2
shows the spectrogram of an Hermite function plus white noise.
As can be guessed, the intensity of the zeros is a radial function.

Proposition III.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
\gamma>0
.
The density
\rho
of zeros of
\textnormal{Spec}_{g}(\xi+\sqrt{\gamma}h_{k})
only depends on

, and is equal to

\rho(r)=\left(1+\gamma\frac{r^{k-1}}{k!}\left(k-r\right)^{2}e^{-r}\right)\exp\left(-\gamma\frac{r^{k}}{k!}e^{-r}\right).

The proof is in Appendix
.
As a consequence, we also get that the average number of zeros falling into
B(0,R)
, the centered ball with radius
R
, is

\mathbb{E}[N(B(0,R))]=k-(k-\pi R^{2})\exp\left(\hskip-1.42271pt-\gamma\frac{\pi^{k}R^{2k}}{k!}e^{-\pi R^{2}}\hskip-1.42271pt\right).

When
R=\sqrt{k/\pi}
, the average number of zeros falling in
B(0,R)
is equal to
k
and does not depend on the signal-to-noise ratio
\gamma
.
In fact, the number of zeros in

is exactly

with probability at least

.
This is a clear illustration of the

Proposition III.2
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1/4)
. There exists a constant
M_{k}>0
only depending on

such that if

\gamma>2k^{-k}e^{k}k!(M_{k}+\sqrt{\log(4/\varepsilon)})^{2},

\mathbb{P}\left(N\left(B\left(0,\sqrt{k/\pi}\right)\right)=k\right)\geqslant 1-\varepsilon.

The proof is in Appendix
.
This is a consequence of the Bargmann transform
{B}(h_{k})
having a unique zero at

with multiplicity

. As a consequence of Rouché’s theorem, it becomes
k
zeros with multiplicity one that get trapped inside the ball

when the intensity of the signal is large enough.

V
Discussion

Through explicit computation of the intensity of the point process of zeros and direct applications of Rouché’s theorem, we illustrated how zeros tend to delineate the signal support and why they can be trapped.
In particular, zeros arising from interference may be preferable to maxima when trying, e.g., to count superimposed simple components.
Natural extensions of this work would be to characterize the process of local maxima, as initiated by
(
Abreu
)
for white noise, and to consider non-analytic STFTs that come with non-Gaussian windows
(
HaKoRo22
)
.
