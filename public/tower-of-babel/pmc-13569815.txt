Immune-sparing radiotherapy in solid tumors: radiation-induced lymphopenia, circulating immune-cell dose, and biomarker-guided optimization of radioimmunotherapy
Authors: Shiduo Yang, E Erdemutu, Yuan Ren, Xinyu Jia, Qiaochu Ding, Chang Yang, Yu Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRadiotherapy is a central component of solid tumor management and can enhance antitumor immunity through antigen release, immunogenic cell death, innate immune activation, and tumor microenvironment remodeling. However, radiotherapy can also induce systemic immune injury by depleting circulating lymphocytes and irradiating lymphoid organs. This review aims to examine radiation-induced lymphopenia (RIL) as a clinically relevant manifestation of systemic immune injury and its potential implications for radioimmunotherapy.MethodsThis narrative review discusses the biological basis of RIL, including lymphocyte radiosensitivity, circulating blood exposure, lymphoid-organ irradiation, treatment volume, fractionation, systemic therapy, and patient immune reserve. It also summarizes clinical evidence regarding the association of severe or persistent RIL with outcomes across solid tumors and examines emerging dosimetric and biological biomarkers relevant to immune-sparing radiotherapy.ResultsSevere or persistent RIL has been associated with inferior outcomes across multiple solid tumors and may be particularly relevant in patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors. Emerging dosimetric biomarkers include effective dose to immune cells, estimated dose of radiation to immune cells, circulating blood dose, blood dose-volume histograms, immune dose-volume histograms, marrow dose, spleen dose, and low-dose bath metrics. Potential biological biomarkers include baseline absolute lymphocyte count, treatment-related lymphocyte decline, immune recovery kinetics, inflammatory indices, immune-cell subsets, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) dynamics, and plan-derived immune exposure. Together, these parameters may support a biomarker-guided framework for immune-sparing radiotherapy.ConclusionsRIL should not be regarded merely as a laboratory abnormality, but as a clinically relevant systemic immune injury that may reflect reduced systemic immune competence and influence radioimmunotherapy efficacy. Immune-sparing radiotherapy should not compromise target coverage or tumor control, but may provide a clinically meaningful direction for optimizing radioimmunotherapy in solid tumors.Keywords: Radiotherapy, Radiation-induced lymphopenia, Immune-sparing radiotherapy, Effective dose to immune cells, Absolute lymphocyte count, Immune checkpoint inhibitors

Introduction

Radiotherapy remains central to solid-tumor treatment, contributing to disease control, organ preservation, symptom relief, and survival. Beyond direct tumor-cell killing, ionizing radiation can promote antigen release, immunogenic cell death, innate immune sensing, antigen presentation, and tumor immune remodeling (Formenti and Demaria 2013; Golden and Apetoh 2015; Vanpouille-Box et al. 2017; Rodriguez-Ruiz et al. 2020; Yamazaki et al. 2020). These effects provide a biological rationale for combining radiotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors and other immunomodulatory therapies.

Radiotherapy is not uniformly immunostimulatory. Lymphocytes are highly radiosensitive, and radiation may alter their number, subset composition, diversity, and function (Paganetti 2023). During fractionated treatment, circulating blood repeatedly traverses the irradiation field, while exposure of bone marrow, spleen, lymph nodes, cardiopulmonary blood pools, major vessels, and gut-associated lymphoid tissue may impair immune-cell production, trafficking, and recovery (Ellsworth 2018; Lambin et al. 2020). Radiation-induced lymphopenia (RIL) should therefore not be regarded merely as a transient laboratory abnormality.

Severe or persistent RIL has been associated with inferior outcomes across multiple solid tumors. Meta-analytic evidence links grade ≥ 3 RIL with worse overall survival after radiotherapy, with additional support from thoracic, gastrointestinal, brain, pelvic, and head and neck malignancies (Damen et al. 2021; El Houat et al. 2023; Prades-Sagarra et al. 2024). RIL may be particularly relevant to radioimmunotherapy: severe lymphopenia after chemoradiotherapy has been reported to attenuate durvalumab benefit in stage III non-small cell lung cancer, and radiation-related lymphopenia has been associated with poorer survival in metastatic patients receiving PD-1 inhibitors (Antonia et al. 2017; Pike et al. 2019; Jing et al. 2022).

Radiotherapy planning appropriately prioritizes target coverage, tumor control, and conventional organs at risk, but the immune system is distributed across circulating lymphocytes, active marrow, spleen, lymph nodes, mucosal immune tissues, and trafficking compartments. Candidate metrics such as effective dose to immune cells (EDIC), estimated dose of radiation to immune cells (EDRIC), circulating blood dose, blood dose-volume histograms, and immune dose-volume histograms suggest that immune exposure may be quantifiable, although these models remain insufficiently standardized for routine decision-making (Jin et al. 2021; Cella et al. 2024; Friedes et al. 2024).

This review presents immune-sparing radiotherapy as an emerging clinical-translational framework for solid tumors. It focuses on how RIL-related biomarkers might inform treatment planning, immune-preserving optimization, and radioimmunotherapy trial design. We summarize the biological basis and clinical impact of RIL, emerging immune-dosimetry metrics, dynamic blood-based markers, and a biomarker-guided strategy intended to preserve systemic immunity without compromising tumor control.

Literature search and evidence selection

This narrative review was based on a targeted literature search of PubMed and Web of Science from database inception to June 2026, supplemented by manual screening of reference lists from relevant reviews, meta-analyses, clinical studies, and radiotherapy dosimetry and treatment-planning studies. ClinicalTrials.gov was consulted as a supplementary registry source to identify ongoing or registered trials related to radioimmunotherapy, lymphopenia monitoring, immune-sparing radiotherapy, adaptive radiotherapy, or immune-related endpoints; registry records were not used as primary evidence for scientific or clinical claims. Search terms included “radiotherapy”, “radiation-induced lymphopenia”, “treatment-related lymphopenia”, “absolute lymphocyte count”, “baseline ALC”, “nadir ALC”, “lymphocyte recovery”, “neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio”, “platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio”, “systemic immune-inflammation index”, “immune-sparing radiotherapy”, “lymphocyte-sparing radiotherapy”, “circulating immune-cell dose”, “effective dose to immune cells”, “EDIC”, “EDRIC”, “immune-DVH”, “low-dose bath”, “field size”, “planning target volume”, “bone marrow dose”, “spleen dose”, “stereotactic body radiotherapy”, “SBRT”, “proton therapy”, “adaptive radiotherapy”, “immune checkpoint inhibitors”, “radioimmunotherapy”, and “solid tumors”. Priority was given to systematic reviews, meta-analyses, prospective studies, large retrospective cohorts, clinical trial secondary analyses, dosimetric modeling studies, and translational studies directly linking RIL with clinical outcomes, immune function, blood-based immune markers, immune-cell subsets, radiotherapy planning factors, or immunotherapy efficacy. Studies with no direct relevance to radiotherapy, RIL, lymphocyte kinetics, immune-cell dose, lymphoid-organ dose, or radiotherapy plus immune checkpoint blockade were not prioritized. Case reports, weakly related conference abstracts, and studies focused solely on immunotherapy were excluded unless they provided essential trial background or conceptual information. A focused update was completed on 2 August 2026 for reviewer-requested topics, including tumor-draining lymph-node irradiation, irradiation timing, EDIC, EDRIC, and newly cited studies. This article is not a systematic review or meta-analysis; therefore, no pooled effect estimates or PRISMA flow diagram were generated.

This narrative review has several methodological limitations. The literature search was targeted rather than systematic, study selection and data extraction were not performed in duplicate, and no formal risk-of-bias assessment was undertaken. The evidence is heterogeneous in tumor type, radiotherapy technique, systemic treatment, RIL definition, ALC sampling, and recovery interval. Most clinical associations derive from retrospective cohorts, secondary analyses, or dosimetric models and are vulnerable to confounding by disease burden, treatment intensity, baseline immune reserve, and host factors. Accordingly, this review provides a clinically oriented translational framework rather than quantitative treatment recommendations or validated immune-dose constraints.

Biological basis of radiation-induced lymphopenia

RIL is biologically plausible because lymphocytes are intrinsically radiosensitive and continuously circulate through irradiated tissues. Unlike many solid organs, the immune system is distributed across circulating blood, lymphoid organs, bone marrow, lymph nodes, mucosal immune tissues, and tumor-draining immune compartments. This spatial organization makes systemic immunity vulnerable even when radiotherapy is delivered to a local or regional target. Importantly, RIL should not be interpreted simply as a reduction in absolute lymphocyte count (ALC). ALC is clinically convenient, but it does not fully capture lymphocyte subsets, immune phenotype, recovery kinetics, or functional immune competence (Paganetti 2023).

The first mechanism is direct irradiation of circulating lymphocytes. During each fraction, only part of the blood pool is within the radiation field, but repeated fractions expose progressively larger proportions of circulating immune cells. Yovino et al. modeled a conventional glioblastoma plan and estimated that a standard 30-fraction course could expose nearly the entire circulating blood pool to potentially lymphotoxic doses (Yovino et al. 2013). Four-dimensional blood-flow modeling has provided a more detailed framework for estimating dose to circulating blood and lymphocytes (Hammi et al. 2020). This concept helps explain why RIL can occur even when the target is anatomically localized.

The second mechanism is irradiation of lymphocyte-rich and immune-regenerative structures. Active bone marrow contributes to hematopoietic recovery, the spleen is a secondary lymphoid organ and immune-cell reservoir, lymph nodes support antigen presentation and T-cell priming, and gut-associated lymphoid tissue participates in mucosal and systemic immune regulation. Radiation to these structures may impair immune-cell production, maturation, trafficking, and recovery (Lambin et al. 2020). This is relevant for thoracic, abdominal, pelvic, craniospinal, and extended-field radiotherapy, where blood pools, vertebral or pelvic marrow, spleen, bowel, or elective nodal regions may receive meaningful dose.

The third mechanism is treatment-related amplification of immune injury. Larger planning target volumes, wider elective nodal fields, higher integral dose, larger low-dose bath, and longer courses can increase repeated exposure of circulating immune cells and lymphoid organs (Ellsworth 2018). Concurrent chemotherapy, corticosteroids, infection, malnutrition, advanced tumor burden, baseline lymphopenia, and systemic inflammation may further reduce immune reserve or delay recovery. Thus, the same radiotherapy plan may produce different degrees of lymphopenia in patients with different baseline immune status or systemic treatment exposure.

The fourth mechanism involves selective effects on immune-cell subsets. B cells, T-cell subsets, natural killer cells, regulatory T cells, and memory or naïve lymphocyte compartments may have different radiosensitivity and recovery kinetics (Paganetti 2023). For radioimmunotherapy, this distinction matters because immune checkpoint inhibitors depend not only on lymphocyte number but also on the availability, activation, trafficking, and persistence of functional effector T cells. However, the clinical implications of subset-specific depletion remain insufficiently standardized.

Together, these mechanisms suggest that RIL is a systemic immune injury shaped by circulating blood dose, lymphoid-organ exposure, treatment volume, fractionation, systemic therapy, and host immune reserve. This framework supports a move from conventional organ-at-risk thinking toward immune-aware treatment planning, while recognizing that measures such as EDIC, circulating blood dose, and immune-DVH still require standardization and prospective validation before routine clinical use. Clinical impact of RIL across solid tumors

RIL has been reported across multiple solid tumors, but interpretation requires caution because most evidence is retrospective, definitions and sampling times vary, and lymphopenia is influenced by baseline immune reserve, tumor burden, systemic therapy, corticosteroids, nutrition, infection, and inflammation. Nevertheless, systematic reviews consistently associate severe RIL with inferior survival, particularly in brain, thoracic, and upper-abdominal tumors (Damen et al. 2021; El Houat et al. 2023). These associations are clinically relevant but do not establish causality.

Thoracic malignancies provide the clearest connection between RIL and radioimmunotherapy. In lung cancer, severe radiation-related lymphopenia during definitive radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy is associated with inferior survival and disease control (Abravan et al. 2020; Upadhyay et al. 2021). Although consolidation durvalumab after concurrent chemoradiotherapy provides durable benefit in unresectable stage III non-small cell lung cancer, severe RIL, lymphocyte dynamics, and delayed recovery have been associated with poorer outcomes during maintenance immunotherapy (Antonia et al. 2017; Spigel et al. 2022; Jing et al. 2022; Cho et al. 2022; Kuge et al. 2023). These observations support consideration of systemic immune exposure during thoracic planning without implying that RIL is the only determinant of immunotherapy efficacy.

Upper gastrointestinal tumors are also high-risk because thoracic and upper-abdominal irradiation can expose circulating blood, vertebral marrow, cardiopulmonary blood pools, spleen-adjacent regions, and major vessels. In esophageal cancer, severe RIL is associated with adverse outcomes, and treatment volume and thoracic organ dose contribute to risk (Dai et al. 2022a; Xu et al. 2021). Meta-analytic evidence in pancreatic cancer similarly suggests an adverse prognostic association (Venkatesulu et al. 2022). These settings may therefore be priorities for immune-aware plan comparison when large fields and prolonged combined-modality therapy are required.

In glioma and glioblastoma, radiotherapy is commonly combined with temozolomide and corticosteroids. Prospective data show substantial and prolonged CD4 lymphopenia after chemoradiotherapy, while pooled evidence associates treatment-induced lymphopenia with inferior survival (Grossman et al. 2011; Saeed et al. 2024). This setting illustrates the combined influence of circulating blood exposure, brain dose-volume effects, baseline immune reserve, corticosteroids, and lymphotoxic systemic therapy.

Pelvic malignancies provide another important context. In cervical cancer, RIL is associated with worse overall and progression-free survival, and bowel dose-volume parameters may predict severe lymphopenia (Cao et al. 2023; Li et al. 2024). In rectal cancer, acute severe lymphopenia has been linked with inferior distant metastasis-free survival and low-dose exposure of lower pelvic marrow, while voxel-based analysis associates regional pelvic dose with lymphocyte decline and severe RIL risk (Lee et al. 2025; Cheng et al. 2026).

Head and neck cancer represents a particular challenge because elective nodal irradiation may remain clinically indicated to cover nodal levels at risk of occult microscopic disease even when no nodal involvement is radiologically apparent (Biau et al. 2019), yet it may also expose lymphocyte-rich tumor-draining lymph nodes. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported that severe RIL was associated with worse survival and disease progression in head and neck cancer, and more recent chemoradiotherapy data linked the extent and dose intensity of elective neck irradiation with severe lymphopenia (Dai et al. 2022b; Mickel et al. 2025). Tumor-draining lymph nodes are also important sites of dendritic-cell trafficking, antigen presentation, and T-cell priming. In preclinical models, concurrent irradiation of the primary tumor and draining lymph nodes attenuated antigen-specific T-cell responses and the systemic efficacy of radiotherapy combined with immune checkpoint blockade, whereas delaying nodal irradiation until after initial immune priming preserved treatment efficacy (Marciscano et al. 2018; Darragh et al. 2022; Telarovic et al. 2024). These findings support investigation of selective or temporally staged nodal irradiation, but they do not justify omission of indicated nodal treatment or a specific delay interval in clinical practice. Current planning should prioritize established oncologic indications while minimizing unnecessary elective volume and low-dose exposure when safely feasible.

Overall, RIL is a recurrent adverse prognostic signal, particularly with large fields, prolonged fractionation, concurrent systemic therapy, or lymphoid-organ exposure. The key translational question is whether patient risk, treatment-related immune injury, and recovery can be prospectively incorporated into planning and radioimmunotherapy trials. Dosimetric biomarkers and circulating immune-cell dose

Associations between treatment extent and RIL have shifted attention from blood counts alone to plan-derived immune exposure. Conventional dose-volume histograms describe anatomical organs but not the dynamic exposure of circulating lymphocytes or the distributed immune system. Immune-dosimetry biomarkers therefore aim to connect anatomical dose with blood or immune-cell exposure.

Larger planning target volumes, elective nodal fields, craniospinal or pelvic irradiation, and thoracic or upper-abdominal fields may increase exposure of circulating blood and lymphoid tissues. Field size influences treatment-induced lymphopenia risk, and low-dose lung or heart exposure may help predict subsequent lymphopenia in lung cancer (Ellsworth 2018; Kuncman et al. 2024). The importance of low-dose exposure depends on disease site, fractionation, treated volume, and blood-rich or lymphoid anatomy.

Effective dose to immune cells (EDIC) is an organ-weighted surrogate of radiation exposure to circulating immune cells rather than a directly measured blood dose. In the RTOG 0617 implementation, EDIC combines mean lung dose, mean heart dose, treatment fraction number, and integral total dose-volume normalized to a reference body volume, using coefficients intended to reflect blood distribution and recirculation through large- and small-vessel compartments (Jin et al. 2021). Disease-specific adaptations have also been used; for example, the esophageal-cancer model incorporated mean liver dose because of the hepatic blood pool (Xu et al. 2020). Thus, EDIC is not a single identical equation across tumor sites. Reported cut points are formula- and cohort-dependent and should not be transferred directly between diseases, planning systems, or schedules. Higher EDIC has nevertheless been associated with lymphopenia and inferior cancer control in thoracic cohorts (Jin et al. 2021; Friedes et al. 2024).

Estimated dose of radiation to immune cells (EDRIC) is a related but distinct pragmatic model developed in thoracic radiotherapy. The commonly used Ladbury formulation retains mean lung dose, mean heart dose, and a fractionation term, but substitutes patient-specific mean body dose for the integral total dose-volume/reference-volume component used in the RTOG 0617 EDIC formulation (Ladbury et al. 2019). Subsequent studies have applied or modified this model, including the PET-Plan secondary analysis, in which higher EDRIC was associated mainly with progression-free survival and overall-survival findings depended on model specification (Dejonckheere et al. 2025). EDIC and EDRIC should therefore be considered related model families rather than interchangeable names or equivalent constraints. Their values depend on contouring extent, body-dose definition, fractionation, disease site, and model version, and neither has a prospectively validated universal threshold.

Outside lung cancer, higher EDIC has been associated with severe lymphopenia and inferior outcomes after esophageal chemoradiotherapy, while composite dosimetric models have also been linked with RIL, hematologic toxicity, or prognosis (Xu et al. 2020; Qiu et al. 2023; Chu et al. 2024). These findings support disease-specific investigation but not direct transfer of thresholds between tumor sites.

Unlike EDIC and EDRIC, blood-DVH and immune-DVH approaches attempt to model dynamic dose accumulation in circulating blood or distributed immune-cell compartments more directly, but require additional assumptions about perfusion, blood flow, cell trafficking, and temporal exposure. Conceptual and four-dimensional blood-flow models demonstrate how repeated fractions can expose progressively larger blood volumes (Yovino et al. 2013; Hammi et al. 2020). Individualized implementation remains limited by model complexity, heterogeneous inputs, and limited prospective validation (Cella et al. 2024).

Lymphocyte-rich organ-at-risk sparing provides a complementary anatomical approach. Active marrow, spleen, lymph nodes, bowel, and other blood-rich structures may influence immune-cell production, trafficking, priming, and recovery. Anatomical marrow exposure is not represented explicitly in EDIC or EDRIC; vertebral and pelvic marrow dose-volume parameters have been associated with hematologic toxicity or RIL, although active-marrow segmentation and actionable constraints remain insufficiently standardized (Zhang et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2025; Cheng et al. 2026). Splenic and bowel dose have also been associated with severe lymphopenia in upper-abdominal and pelvic settings (Lee et al. 2022; Li et al. 2024). Lymphocyte-rich organ-at-risk sparing is technically feasible in pelvic prostate planning, but current evidence demonstrates planning feasibility rather than improved survival (Baré et al. 2022). Avoidable exposure may be reduced when target coverage and indicated nodal treatment are preserved.

Particle therapy and hypofractionation may reduce integral dose, exposed blood volume, or treatment duration in selected settings. Studies have reported lower predicted or observed RIL with proton therapy in esophageal cancer and glioblastoma, SBRT versus conventional fractionation in pancreatic cancer, and carbon ions versus photons in locally advanced pancreatic cancer (Ebrahimi et al. 2021; Mohan et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2024). These approaches should not be assumed to be universally immune-sparing; effects depend on disease site, target volume, beam arrangement, fractionation, and low-dose exposure.

EDIC, EDRIC, blood-DVH, immune-DVH, marrow dose, spleen dose, and low-dose bath metrics may help estimate RIL risk and compare otherwise oncologically equivalent plans. They remain candidate biomarkers rather than established constraints. Prospective harmonization is needed to determine whether reducing predicted immune exposure improves lymphocyte recovery, radioimmunotherapy efficacy, or survival without compromising tumor control. RIL as a potential barrier to effective radioimmunotherapy

Radiotherapy can promote antigen release, immunogenic cell death, type I interferon signaling, dendritic-cell activation, and T-cell priming, supporting combinations with immune checkpoint inhibitors (Formenti and Demaria 2013; Vanpouille-Box et al. 2017; Rodriguez-Ruiz et al. 2020). The same treatment can deplete circulating lymphocytes, damage lymphoid organs, and delay recovery, creating a biological tension within radioimmunotherapy.

Checkpoint blockade requires functional effector T cells, preserved antigen presentation, tumor infiltration, and sustained immune surveillance. RIL may reduce cells available for priming, expansion, trafficking, and memory formation. Although ALC does not fully represent antitumor immunity, severe or persistent lymphopenia may indicate depleted systemic immune reserve and should be considered a potential treatment-related barrier rather than only a prognostic blood-count abnormality.

Clinical observations support this concern. In metastatic patients receiving PD-1 inhibitors, extracranial or prolonged radiotherapy increased severe lymphopenia risk, which was associated with poorer survival (Pike et al. 2019). In unresectable stage III non-small cell lung cancer, severe RIL after chemoradiotherapy has been reported to attenuate durvalumab benefit, while lymphocyte dynamics and recovery have been linked with outcomes during maintenance immunotherapy (Antonia et al. 2017; Spigel et al. 2022; Jing et al. 2022; Cho et al. 2022; Kuge et al. 2023).

These findings do not establish RIL as the sole mechanism of radioimmunotherapy failure or as a validated predictive biomarker. Tumor-intrinsic resistance, defective antigen presentation, immunosuppressive myeloid and regulatory T-cell populations, TGF-β signaling, hypoxia, corticosteroids, and systemic therapy also influence response. Most RIL-immunotherapy studies are retrospective or observational and use heterogeneous thresholds. The appropriate conclusion is not to minimize radiotherapy, but to measure immune injury and test whether it can be reduced without compromising tumor control.

Radiotherapy-immunotherapy timing may interact with RIL, because profound lymphocyte depletion could limit immune reinvigoration. No universal ALC threshold or treatment sequence can be applied across tumors. Prospective monitoring of baseline ALC, early decline, nadir, recovery, and selected immune subsets may help define when treatment-related immune injury is clinically relevant.

Future radioimmunotherapy trials should therefore consider RIL as a stratification factor or predefined exploratory endpoint, particularly for large-field, thoracic, pelvic, elective-nodal, or prolonged fractionated treatment. The goal is not to avoid immune modulation, but to preserve systemic immune competence while maintaining oncologic treatment. This perspective links immune dosimetry with biomarker-guided planning.

Biomarker-guided immune-sparing radiotherapy

A practical immune-sparing strategy requires markers of baseline immune reserve, treatment-related immune injury, plan exposure, and tumor response. No single marker is sufficient: ALC is accessible but incomplete, immune-dosimetry metrics describe exposure rather than function, and inflammatory indices or circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) provide complementary information. A composite framework is therefore more appropriate than a single threshold.

Pretreatment assessment may include baseline ALC, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, and systemic immune-inflammation index. These inexpensive markers are associated with outcomes during immune checkpoint inhibition but are non-specific and influenced by infection, corticosteroids, tumor burden, nutrition, comorbidities, and treatment-related inflammation (Liu et al. 2019). A validated pretreatment model in unresectable stage III non-small cell lung cancer estimates severe RIL risk during concurrent chemoradiotherapy, with exploratory analyses suggesting possible relevance to durvalumab benefit pending prospective validation (van Rossum et al. 2026).

Plan-level assessment may incorporate immune-relevant exposure to conventional target and organ-at-risk review in patients receiving large-field, elective-nodal, thoracic, upper-abdominal, or pelvic treatment. Candidate metrics include EDIC, EDRIC, circulating blood dose, blood-DVH, immune-DVH, active marrow, spleen and bowel dose, and avoidable low-dose bath (Jin et al. 2021; Cella et al. 2024; Friedes et al. 2024). Composite models may improve individualized risk prediction, and lymphocyte-rich organ-at-risk sparing is technically feasible, but neither constitutes a validated survival-improving constraint (Baré et al. 2022; Chu et al. 2024). These metrics may support comparison when target coverage and conventional organ sparing are equivalent.

On-treatment assessment may focus on early ALC decline, nadir, and recovery rather than a single post-treatment count. In stage III NSCLC and esophageal cancer, lymphocyte dynamics and recovery have been associated with radiation exposure and outcomes (Cho et al. 2022; Kuge et al. 2023; Tseng et al. 2023; Sandul et al. 2026). Serial ALC assessment is readily feasible and may be incorporated as a predefined endpoint in prospective studies or clinically selected high-risk cohorts. However, optimal sampling, recovery definitions, and action thresholds remain undefined, and current evidence does not support universal treatment modification based on ALC kinetics alone.

Functional immune characterization may distinguish CD8 + and CD4 + T cells, regulatory T cells, B cells, natural killer cells, and other subsets. Such profiling may be valuable in radioimmunotherapy trials, where effector function may matter more than total ALC, but it is less standardized and more resource-intensive than routine blood counts. It should currently be considered a trial-enabling biomarker rather than a universal clinical requirement.

Circulating tumor DNA clearance or persistence may complement immune monitoring by providing dynamic information about tumor burden, molecular residual disease, and treatment response (Peng et al. 2021; Bartolomucci et al. 2025). However, ctDNA and RIL describe different biological domains, and evidence that their combined use can guide radiotherapy intensification, de-intensification, or adaptive planning is lacking. Within an immune-sparing framework, ctDNA should therefore be considered an exploratory tumor-response variable for prospective integration with ALC kinetics and immune dosimetry, rather than a current trigger for treatment modification.

A clinically realistic framework would integrate these markers across treatment. Before radiotherapy, clinicians can assess immune reserve, inflammatory context, tumor burden, systemic therapy, and expected treatment extent. During planning, candidate immune-dose metrics can compare otherwise acceptable plans; during treatment, early ALC decline can identify profound depletion; and after treatment, recovery and exploratory ctDNA dynamics can describe immune and disease-control trajectories. In trials, these variables may support stratification and predefined exploratory analyses.

These biomarkers differ in readiness. Baseline and nadir ALC, recovery, NLR, PLR, SII, and conventional dose-volume review are routinely accessible but non-specific. EDIC, EDRIC, blood-DVH, immune-DVH, marrow, spleen, bowel, and low-dose bath metrics are planning-relevant candidates without standardized constraints. Immune-subset profiling, ctDNA integration, and adaptive immune-sparing strategies remain translational or trial-enabling tools.

Immune-sparing radiotherapy should not reduce treatment intensity indiscriminately. Tumor control remains the first requirement of curative treatment. The goal is to preserve systemic immune competence when feasible without compromising target coverage, oncologic dose, or necessary nodal irradiation. Future perspectives

Before immune-sparing radiotherapy can become a validated strategy, RIL definitions must be standardized. Studies use different ALC thresholds, grading systems, nadir times, recovery intervals, and baseline-adjusted measures. Prospective trials should collect baseline, early-decline, nadir, and recovery data and, where feasible, immune-cell subsets, treating RIL as a dynamic marker of systemic immune injury rather than only a toxicity event (Paganetti 2023; Sandul et al. 2026).

Immune dosimetry also requires harmonization. EDIC, EDRIC, blood-DVH, immune-DVH, marrow, spleen, bowel, and low-dose bath metrics vary in calculation, organ weighting, blood-flow assumptions, and interpretation (Cella et al. 2024). Prospective studies must determine whether they predict lymphocyte depletion, recovery, treatment response, radioimmunotherapy efficacy, and survival. Constraints should not be adopted solely because they reduce predicted immune dose; clinical value and tumor control must be demonstrated (Lambin et al. 2020).

Radioimmunotherapy trials should prospectively collect lymphocyte kinetics, immune dosimetry, selected immune subsets, treatment timing, response, distant control, progression-free survival, and overall survival, particularly in high-exposure settings. They should also test, rather than assume, whether immunotherapy timing should account for ALC nadir, recovery, or functional immune preservation.

Technology comparisons likewise require clinical endpoints. Proton therapy, carbon ions, SBRT, adaptive radiotherapy, and advanced optimization may reduce immune exposure in selected settings but should not be assumed to be universally immune-sparing. Comparative studies should measure lymphocyte kinetics, immune-cell subsets, immune dosimetry, tumor control, toxicity, and survival rather than theoretical dose reduction alone (Paganetti 2023; Cella et al. 2024).

The long-term goal is a planning paradigm that balances tumor control probability with clinically meaningful immune preservation, transforming RIL from an observed adverse event into a measurable and potentially modifiable component of precision radioimmunotherapy.

Conclusion

RIL is a clinically relevant systemic immune injury associated with radiotherapy outcomes and potentially with radioimmunotherapy efficacy. Immune-aware planning may consider circulating immune-cell exposure, lymphoid organs, and recovery capacity in addition to conventional target and organ-at-risk objectives. EDIC, EDRIC, blood-DVH, immune-DVH, ALC kinetics, inflammatory indices, immune subsets, and ctDNA may contribute complementary information, but none is a mature universal clinical constraint. The central question is whether radiation-related immune injury can be prospectively measured and reduced without compromising tumor control. Future trials should combine harmonized immune dosimetry, longitudinal blood biomarkers, and radioimmunotherapy endpoints to test this hypothesis.
