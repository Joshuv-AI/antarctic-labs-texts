Synthesis of the Spirocyclic Imine Fragment of Portimines Using a γ-Hydroxymethyl-α,β-butenolide Dienophile in a Diastereoselective Diels–Alder Reaction
Authors: Ze Kuang, Xiao-Bo Ding, Freda F Li, Margaret A Brimble
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe synthesis of the spirocyclic imine fragment of the portimine family of marine toxins has been achieved. A densely functionalized key lactone-ester intermediate was assembled via a highly diastereoselective Diels–Alder cycloaddition, involving a novel γ-hydroxymethyl-α,β-butenolide dienophile. A Stille coupling was employed to install the vinyl group. Selective elaboration of the two carbonyl groups successfully afforded the spirocyclic imine fragment.

Introduction

Spirocyclic imine toxins constitute an expanding family of marine toxins produced by dinoflagellates. Notable examples within this natural product family include portimines A and B (1 and 2, Figure 1),1,2 gymnodimines,3 pinnatoxins,4 spirolides,5,6 pteriatoxins,7 spiro-prorocentrimines8 and kabirimine.9 These rapidly acting toxins are infamous for causing shellfish food poisoning. Despite their deleterious effects, spirocyclic imine toxins reveal an array of valuable biological activities, rendering them significant synthetic targets.

Structures of representative spirocyclic imine marine toxins.

Portimines A (1) and B (2), isolated from the benthic dinoflagellate Vulcanodinium rugosum in New Zealand waters, stand out due to their distinctive biological and structural characteristics.1,2 They represent the smallest member of this family (5,6-spirocyclic imine) identified to date, with a unique biological profile. In contrast to other cyclic imine toxins known for their potent acute neurotoxicity, portimine A (1) exhibits remarkably low in vivo toxicity (mouse, LD50 1570 mg/kg) while exhibiting potent in vitro anticancer activity (P388, EC50 2.7 nM). Moreover, portimine A (1) exhibited robust antifouling activity in a series of four macrofouling bioassays (EC50 0.06–62.5 ng/mL).10

Given their unique structure and remarkable biological activity, several synthetic studies toward portimines (1, 2) have been reported since their discovery.11−17 Suzuki and co-workers first reported a racemic synthesis of the cyclohexene segment (±)-3 of portimine A (1) from a cyclohexene derivative (Scheme 1A).11 Zakarian and co-workers recently developed an asymmetric synthesis of cyclohexene motif 4 through a stepwise Ireland–Claisen rearrangement/enyne metathesis sequence (Scheme 1B).12,13 Additionally, the Harran group reported the preparation of a linear precursor 5 embedding all the carbons present in portimines (1, 2) (Scheme 1C). However, the attempted intramolecular Diels–Alder reaction failed to afford the desired macrocycle.14 The first total synthesis of portimines A (1) and B (2) was not achieved until recently by Baran and co-workers (Scheme 1D).15 The spirocyclic imine motif 6 was constructed via a stepwise strategy using an asymmetric Diels–Alder reaction/diastereoselective conjugate addition sequence and the macrocycle was forged using an alkyne metathesis. This breakthrough enabled the correction of the initially assigned structure for portimine B (2) and facilitated an in-depth study of the biological activities.

Previous Synthetic Studies towards Portimines (1, 2)

In common with other spirocyclic imine toxins, portimines may be broadly divided into polyketide and spirocyclic imine domains. Our group has established an efficient approach to the challenging syn-dihydroxy ketone motif and successfully applied it to the synthesis of the polyketide chain 7 of portimines A (1) and B (2) (Scheme 1E).16,17 Our further study also suggested that the spirocyclic imine fragment will first need to be installed to facilitate an intramolecular ketalization to form the [6.2.1] bridged ketal structure in portimine A (1).17

Despite extensive efforts in the total synthesis and polyketide preparation of portimines, the spirocyclic imine fragment continues to attract considerable attention in the field of synthetic chemistry, as it is postulated to be an important component of the active pharmacophore of these marine toxins. We previously reported an intermolecular tandem Diels–Alder/transition metal cross coupling reaction sequence using a 2-bromo-1,3-butadiene (8, Scheme 2A) to approach the spirocyclic imine framework of portimines A (1) and B (2).18 However, the relative stereochemistry of the major Diels–Alder adducts did not match the natural products due to the endo-selective nature of the Diels–Alder reaction. This problem was later overcome using symmetrical dienophile 13 generated in situ from Meldrum’s acid in the key Diels–Alder addition (Scheme 2B).19 While this strategy successfully yielded the desired spirocyclic imine core via selective elaboration of the two carbonyl groups in the key intermediate 14, chiral resolution was essential to obtain enantiomerically pure intermediates due to the lack of asymmetric induction in the key Diels–Alder reaction.

Use of Diels-Alder Reaction to Access the Spirocyclic Imine Fragment of Portimine A (1)

In addressing this drawback, we therefore herein report the synthesis of a novel γ-hydroxymethyl-α,β-butenolide dienophile 17, which enabled a highly diastereoselective Diels–Alder reaction and facilitated an asymmetric synthesis of the spirocyclic imine fragment of portimine A (1) (Scheme 2C).

Results and Disscussion

γ-Hydroxymethyl-α,β-butenolides have been widely used in the synthesis of natural and bioactive molecules.20 They can be produced on a large scale using inexpensive chiral pool starting materials. The embedded chiral center has been reported to effectively induce the diastereoselectivity of the Diels–Alder addition.21,22 We therefore envisaged that the Diels–Alder reaction of diene 18 with γ-hydroxymethyl-α,β-butenolide 17 would afford lactone-ester intermediate 19 (Scheme 2C), which could then be elaborated to the spirocyclic imine core of portimines (e.g., 20) using strategies analogous to our previous synthesis. However, the reactivity and stability of the required butenolide 17 was unknown at the outset of this work. Development of an efficient approach to butenolide 17 in order to investigate the feasibility of the proposed Diels–Alder reaction was therefore the first objective of this study.

Our synthesis commenced with the preparation of γ-hydroxymethyl-α,β-butenolide 17 (Scheme 3). First, malonate 22 was prepared from commercially available 1,2:5,6-di-O-isopropylidene-D-mannitol (21) in 72% yield over two steps.23 Treatment of malonate 22 using aqueous HCl (2M) in acetonitrile afforded the desired butenolide 17. It was observed that this compound is prone to undergo polymerization upon concentration or exposure to basic conditions. After optimization of the reaction conditions, it was revealed that use of a catalytic quantity (6% mol) of aqueous HCl (2M) was sufficient to promote the cyclization of malonate 22, affording a relatively pure product butenolide 17, which could be used directly in the next step. An analytical amount of sample was obtained via flash column chromatography using acetic acid-ethyl acetate (1:100) as eluent to confirm its structure using NMR and HRMS analysis.

Synthesis of γ-Hydroxymethyl-α,β-butenolide 17 and Subsequent Diels-Alder Reactions

With the successful preparation of butenolide 17 established, the desired Diels–Alder cyclization was next investigated. Treatment of 17 and diene 18 with ZnI2 in dichloromethane successfully afforded lactone-ester 23. The Diels–Alder cycloaddition was highly diastereoselective, with a single isomer observed upon analysis by NMR. Various Lewis acids were screened, including EtAlCl2, Zn(OTf)2, ZnBr2, ZnI2, Mg(OTf)2 and Cu(OTf)2 in different solvents (see Supporting Information, Table S1). The best yield (50% over two steps from 22) was obtained using ZnI2 as catalyst and acetonitrile as solvent in the presence of 4 Å molecular sieves.

To further examine the utility of this novel butenolide dienophile 17 in Diels–Alder cycloadditions, dienes 24-26 were selected for reaction with 17. Butenolide 17 generated from malonate 22 was used directly for the Diels–Alder reaction without purification. Both dienes 24 and 25 were subjected to the Diels–Alder cycloaddition with 17 affording the corresponding adducts 27 and 28 as single isomers in moderate yields. Cyclopentadiene 26 also readily reacted with butenolide 17, affording endo-addition product 29 (25%) together with exo-addition product 30 (15%), again with high diastereoselectivity. These successful Diels–Alder reactions further demonstrated the significant potential of butenolide 17 as a versatile building block for the construction of highly functionalized cyclohexenes.

With the key lactone-ester intermediate 23 in hand, the synthesis of the spirocyclic core of portimines was carried out (Scheme 4). Protection of the primary alcohol using methoxymethyl chloride followed by cleavage of the tert-butyldimethylsilyl (TBS) protecting group, afforded ketone 32, which was then converted to vinyl triflate 33 using Comin’s reagent.24 Subsequent Stille coupling with tributylvinyltin successfully installed the vinyl group, affording vinyl substituted lactone-ester 34 in 60% yield.

Synthesis of Spirocyclic Imine 40

We next shifted our attention to elaborating the two carbonyl functional groups in 34 into the required cyclic imine. Hydrolysis of the methyl ester using aqueous NaOH (2M) followed by reduction of the lactone, afforded diol 35 in 74% yield over two steps. Subsequent intramolecular esterification afforded lactone 36 in 75% yield.25 The primary alcohol group in 36 was then converted to a nitrile using potassium cyanide via a triflate intermediate, resulting in lactone 37 in 84% yield over two steps. The structure and stereochemistry of 37 were confirmed unambiguously by X-ray crystallography. Treatment of lactone 37 with methyl lithium followed by deprotection of the MOM ether and protection of the 1,2-diol as an acetonide, yielded acetonide 39. The methyl ketone was temporarily converted to a silyl enol ether, that facilitated a one-pot nitrile reduction and cyclization to afford the desired spirocyclic imine 40 in 56% yield over two steps. The structure of spirocyclic imine 40 was identified by a combination of NMR and HRMS analysis. The NOESY spectrum of spirocyclic imine 40 identified a distinctive NOE correlation between the methine proton (H-1) and the methyl peak (Me-C10). This correlation confirmed that the stereochemistry of 40 is the 1S,6R-isomer, consistent with the patterns observed in portimines (1) and (2).

Conclusions

In summary, the stereoselective synthesis of the spirocyclic imine fragment 40 of portimines A and B (1, 2) has been achieved. A diastereoselective Diels–Alder cycloaddition using a novel γ-hydroxymethyl-α,β-butenolide 17 as a dienophile reacting with diene 18 afforded a highly functionalized lactone-ester intermediate 23. Installation of the vinyl group, followed by selective elaboration of the two carbonyl groups successfully afforded the desired spirocyclic imine fragment of portimines A and B (1, 2). Importantly, spirocyclic imine 40 contains the key functionalities required to extend the spirocyclic imine into the macrocycle present in portimines (1, 2). This study not only facilitates the synthesis of spirocyclic imine natural products and analogues, but also demonstrates the utility of a novel γ-hydroxymethyl-α,β-butenolide to enable the facile synthesis of highly substituted cyclohexene scaffolds.

Experimental Section

General Information

Unless otherwise stated, all reactions were performed under an oxygen-free atmosphere of nitrogen in oven-dried glassware. Anhydrous solvents were either freshly distilled over drying agents or dried using an LC Technical SP-1 solvent purification system. Commercially available starting materials and all other reagents were used as received unless otherwise noted. Yields refer to chromatographically and spectroscopically (1H NMR) homogeneous materials, unless otherwise stated. Reactions performed at low temperature were cooled either with an acetone/dry ice bath to reach–78 °C or a water/ice bath to reach 0 °C. Reactions were monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) carried out on E. Merck silica gel plates using UV light as visualizing agent, and an ethanolic solution of vanillin or potassium permanganate solution and heat as developing agents. Kieselgel S 63–100 μm (Riedel-de-Hahn) silica gel was used for flash chromatography. NMR spectra were recorded at room temperature in CDCl3 solution on either Bruker DRX400 spectrometers operating at 400 MHz for 1H nuclei and 100 MHz for 13C nuclei, unless otherwise stated. Chemical shifts were reported in parts per million (ppm) and were measured relative to the solvent in which the sample was analyzed (CDCl3: δH 7.26 ppm or δH 0.00 ppm (TMS), δC 77.16 ppm). 1H NMR data were reported as position (δ), multiplicity (s = singlet, br s = broad singlet, d = doublet, dd = doublet of doublets, ddd = doublet of doublets of doublets, dt = doublet of triplets, t = triplet, td = triplet of doublets, q = quartet, m = multiplet), coupling constant (J, Hz), relative integral, and structural assignment. 13C NMR data were reported as position (δ), type and assignment of carbon resonance. Structural assignments were achieved with the aid of DEPT 135i, COSY, HSQC, NOESY and HMBC experiments where required. Infrared (IR) spectra were recorded on a PerkinElmer Spectrum 100 FT-IR spectrometer using a diamond ATR sampling accessory. High-resolution mass spectra (HRMS) were obtained using a VG70SE spectrometer or on a micro Tof-Q II mass spectrometer. Melting points were determined on a Kofler hot-stage apparatus or using a Stuart SMP50 automatic digital melting point apparatus and are uncorrected.
