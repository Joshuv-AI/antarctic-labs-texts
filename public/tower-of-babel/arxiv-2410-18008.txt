Birational geometry of blowups via Weyl chamber decompositions and actions on curves
Maria Chiara Brambilla, Olivia Dumitrescu, Elisa Postinghel, Luis José Santana Sánchez

Abstract
. We study the birational geometry of X s n X^{n}_{s} , the blow-up of ℙ ℂ n \mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}} at s s points in general position. We identify a set of subvarieties, which we call Weyl r r -planes, that belong to an orbit for the action of the Weyl group on r r -cycles. They satisfy the following properties: they appear as stable base locus of divisors; each Weyl r r -plane is swept out by an ( n − r ) (n-r) -moving curve class; moreover, if s ≥ n + 3 s\geq n+3 , for any fixed r r all these curve classes belong to the same orbit for the Weyl action. For Mori dream spaces of type X s n X^{n}_{s} , all such orbits are finite and they allow to reinterpret Mukai’s description of the Mori chamber decomposition of the effective cone in terms of ( n − r ) (n-r) -moving curve classes, unifying previous different approaches. If X s n X^{n}_{s} is not a Mori dream space, there are infinitely many Weyl r r -planes. These yields the definition of the Weyl chamber decomposition of the pseudoeffective cone of divisors. We pose the question as to whether the nef chamber decomposition can be defined (in the negative part of Eff ¯ ​ ( X s n ) \overline{\Eff}(X^{n}_{s}) ) and, if this is the case, whether it coincides with the Weyl chamber decomposition. We conjecture that the answer is affirmative for X 8 3 X^{3}_{8} and X 9 5 X^{5}_{9} .

2.
Preliminaries

Let
X=X^{n}_{s}
denote the
blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
at
s
points in general position.

We denote by
A_{r}(X)
the group of classes of cycles of dimension
r
and by

A(X)=\oplus_{r=0}^{n}A_{r}(X)
the Chow ring of
X
.

The Picard group of
X
is spanned by the general hyperplane class
H
and by the exceptional divisors
E_{i}
. A divisor of the form

(2.1)

D=dH-\sum_{i=1}^{s}m_{i}E_{i}

is said homogeneous if
m_{i}=m
for all
i
.

The anticanonical divisor class is

(2.2)

-K_{X}=(n+1)H-\sum_{i=1}^{s}(n-1)E_{i}.

Recall that the Dolgachev-Mukai pairing is the bilinear pairing on the Picard group of
X^{n}_{s}
defined by:

(2.3)

\langle H,H\rangle=n-1,\quad\langle E_{i},E_{j}\rangle=-\delta_{i,j},\quad\langle H,E_{i}\rangle=0.

It is known that
X
is a Mori dream space if and only if
\langle-K_{X},-K_{X}\rangle>0
(see
[
22
]
and references within).
More precisely, the list of varieties of type
X^{n}_{s}
which are Mori dream spaces is the following:

(2.4)

X^{2}_{s}\text{ for }s\leq 8;\ X^{3}_{s}\text{ for }s\leq 7;\ X^{4}_{s}\text{ for }s\leq 8;\ X^{n}_{s}\text{ for }n\geq 5\text{ and }s\leq n+3.

The Dolgachev-Mukai pairing emerges from the Coxeter theory of the Weyl group acting
on the divisor classes. We recall here the main notions and we refer to
[
19
,
22
,
20
]
for more details.

2.1.
Cremona transformations and the Weyl group

The
standard Cremona transformation based on the coordinate points
of
\mathbb{P}^{n}

is the birational transformation
defined by the rational map:

\textrm{Cr}:=(x_{0}:\dots:x_{n})\mapsto(x_{0}^{-1}:\dots:x_{n}^{-1})

From now on assume that
s\geq n+1
. Given any subset
\Gamma\subseteq\{1,\ldots,s\}
of cardinality
n+1
, we denote by
\textrm{Cr}_{\Gamma}
and call
standard Cremona transformation
the map obtained by precomposing
Cr
with a projective transformation which takes the points indexed by
\Gamma
to the coordinate points of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
.
A standard Cremona transformation induces an automorphism of
\operatorname{Pic}(X_{s}^{n})
, denoted again by
\textrm{Cr}_{\Gamma}
by abuse of notation,
by sending a divisor (
2.1
)
to

(2.5)

\textrm{Cr}_{\Gamma}(D)=(d-b_{\Gamma})H-\sum_{i\in\Gamma}(m_{i}-b_{\Gamma})E_{i}-\sum_{i\not\in\Gamma}m_{i}E_{i},

where

b_{\Gamma}(D)=b_{\Gamma}:=\sum_{i\in\Gamma}m_{i}-(n-1)d.

We say that a divisor
D
(
2.1
) is
Cremona reduced
if
b_{\Gamma}(D)\leq 0
for any
\Gamma\subseteq\{1,\ldots,s\}
of cardinality
n+1
.

The
Weyl group

W^{n}_{s}
acting on the Chow ring
A(X_{s}^{n})
is the group generated by the standard Cremona transformations.

The anticanonical divisor
-K_{X}
is the only homogeneous divisor, up to multiples, which is invariant under the action of the Weyl group (if
s\geq n+2
the assumption homogeneous is not necessary).

The Dolgachev-Mukai pairing (
2.3
) is also preserved by the Weyl action (see
[
25
]
).

Following
[
7
]
, we call
Weyl divisor
an element of the Weyl orbit of an exceptional divisor
E_{i}
.

2.2.
Weyl actions on curves

Let

\mathrm{N}_{1}(X)_{\mathbb{R}}=(A_{1}(X)/\equiv)\otimes{\mathbb{R}}
be the space of numerical equivalence classes of
1
-cycles, which is dual to the Néron-Severi space
\mathrm{N}^{1}(X)_{\mathbb{R}}
with respect to the standard intersection pairing.
A basis of
\N_{1}(X_{s}^{n})_{\mathbb{R}}
is given by
h=H^{n-1}
, the general line class in
X^{n}_{s}
, and, for
i=1\dots,s
, by
e_{i}
, the class of a general line inside the exceptional divisor
E_{i}
.
Thus, the intersection product of a divisor
D\in\N^{1}(X_{s}^{n})_{\mathbb{R}}
of the form (
2.1
) and a
1-cycle

c
in
\N_{1}(X_{s}^{n})_{\mathbb{R}}
of class

(2.6)

c=\delta h-\sum_{i=1}^{s}\mu_{i}e_{i},

is computed by

(2.7)

D\cdot c=d\delta-\sum_{i=1}^{s}m_{i}\mu_{i}.

The Weyl action on curves (effective
1
-cycle)has been investigated in
[
22
]
.
For an index set
\Gamma\subset\{1,\ldots,s\}
of length
n+1
, the Cremona transformation
\textrm{Cr}_{\Gamma}
takes a curve class of the form (
2.6
)
of
\N_{1}(X_{s}^{n})_{\mathbb{R}}
,
which is not contained in the indeterminacy locus of
\textrm{Cr}_{\Gamma}
,
to the following curve class

(2.8)

{\rm Cr}_{\Gamma}(c)=(\delta-(n-1)a_{\Gamma})h-\sum_{i\in\Gamma}(\mu_{i}-a_{\Gamma})e_{i}-\sum_{i\notin\Gamma}\mu_{i}e_{i}.

where

a_{\Gamma}(c)=a_{\Gamma}:=\sum_{i\in\Gamma}\mu_{i}-\delta.

We point out that we can formally extend formula (
2.8
) to any
1
-cycle; however
in this case curves can be sent to non-effective cycles, and vice versa.
For instance if
c=h-e_{1}-e_{2}
is
the class of a line in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
through two points
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, and
\Gamma=\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
, then
\textrm{Cr}_{\Gamma}(c)
is the following non-effective
1
-cycle class:
-(n-2)h+\sum_{i=3}^{n+1}e_{i}
.

We recall from
[
22
,
20
]
the following definition:

Definition 2.1
.

The anticanonical curve class for
X^{n}_{s}
is the unique (up to multiples) symmetric
curve class invariant under the Weyl group action

(2.9)

F:=(n+1)h-\sum_{i=1}^{s}e_{i}.

We recall from
[
22
,
20
]
that
X^{n}_{s}
is a Mori dream space if and only if

-K_{X_{s}^{n}}\cdot F>0.

2.3.
Weyl action on
r
-cycles

Given an effective cycle class
V\in A_{r}(X)
, we say that a cycle
V^{\prime}\in A_{r}(X)
is in the
effective Weyl orbit
of
V
if there is a finite sequence of standard Cremona transformations
\phi=\phi_{k}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{1}
with
\phi(V)=V^{\prime}
and such that
\phi_{l}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{1}(V)

is an effective
r
-cycle for every
1\leq l\leq k
. We denote by
(W_{s}^{n}\cdot V)^{+}
the effective Weyl orbit of
V
.

We observe that the Weyl group actions on divisors (
2.5
) and curves (
2.8
) are linear with respect to degrees and multiplicities of the divisor/curve and easy to apply. On the other hand, this is not the case for cycles of arbitrary dimensions
r
, where the general formulas need to involve binomial coefficients in
r
and
n
.

Hence, in recent works, different approaches have been adopted in order to study the relevant families of rigid
r
-cycles with respect to the action of the Weyl group. In particular the families of Weyl
r
-planes and of Weyl cycles have been defined.

We recall first from
[
21
, Definition 1]
the following notion:

Definition 2.2
.

A
Weyl
r
-plane
is an element of the effective Weyl orbit of the strict transform of an
r
-dimensional plane passing through
r+1
fixed points in
A_{r}(X^{n}_{s})
, for
1\leq r\leq n-1
.

On the other hand, in
[
7
, Definition 1]
, a Weyl cycle of dimension
r<n-1
in
A_{r}(X^{n}_{s})
is defined to be an irreducible component of the intersection of pairwise orthogonal Weyl divisors.
We give here an equivalent uniform definition which includes also Weyl divisors as follows.

Definition 2.3
.

A
Weyl cycle of dimension
r
in
A_{r}(X^{n}_{s})
is an irreducible component of the intersection of Weyl divisors

\{D_{i}\}
such that
\langle D_{i},D_{j}\rangle\leq 0
with respect to the pairing (
2.3
), for any
i,j
.

Now we show that Definition
2.3
is equivalent to
[
7
, Definition 1]
.

Proposition 2.4
.

W
is a Weyl cycle of dimension
r
if and only if

(1)

r=n-1
and
W
is a Weyl divisor, i.e. an element of the Weyl orbit of an exceptional divisor,

(2)

r<n-1
and
W
is an irreducible component of the intersection of pairwise orthogonal Weyl divisors.

Proof.

If
W
is a divisor
D
, there is nothing to prove, because any Weyl divisor satisfies
\langle D,D\rangle=-1
.

Assume
r\leq n-2
and let
W
be as in Definition
2.3
. If
i=j
, we have
\langle D_{i},D_{j}\rangle=-1
, while if
i\neq j
, then the irreducibility of
D_{i}
implies that
\langle D_{i},D_{j}\rangle\geq 0
(see
[
25
]
), therefore distinct Weyl divisors
D_{i}
and
D_{j}
are pairwise orthogonal. i.e.
\langle D_{i},D_{j}\rangle=0
. This proves that
W
satisfies
[
7
, Definition 1]
. The converse is easy and left to the reader.
∎

We show now that the pairwise orthogonal Weyl divisors span a face of the effective cone of
X^{n}_{s}
.

Proposition 2.5
.

A set of Weyl divisors that are pairwise orthogonal with respect to the bilinear pairing (
2.3
) on
X^{n}_{s}
span a face of the pseudoeffective cone of
X^{n}_{s}
.

Proof.

It is enough to show that the cone spanned by
D_{1},D_{2}
is a 2-dimensional face of the effective cone, for any pair of orthogonal Weyl divisors
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
. Using
[
4
, Proposition 2.3]
,
the multiplicity of containment of
D_{i}
in the base locus of an effective divisor
D
is
\max\{0,-\langle D,D_{i}\rangle\}
. Hence, using the orthogonality assumptions,
we have that the base locus of the divisor
a_{1}D_{1}+a_{2}D_{2}
contains
D_{1}
with multiplicity
a_{1}
and
D_{2}
with multiplicity
a_{2}
.
So we obtain that
|a_{1}D_{1}+a_{2}D_{2}|=\{a_{1}D_{1}+a_{2}D_{2}\}
, for any
a_{1},a_{2}\geq 0
, and so
h^{0}(X,a_{1}D_{1}+a_{2}D_{2})=1
. This proves that there is no big divisor linearly equivalent to a positive linear combination of
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
.
∎

2.4.
Stable base locus varieties

For any
X
with
h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X})=0
, the pseudoeffective cone
\overline{\Eff}(X)
admits a wall-and-chamber decomposition called
stable base locus decomposition
, see e.g.
[
31
, Sect 4.1.3]
.
Recall that the stable base locus of an effective divisor
D
is the Zariski closed set

\mathbb{B}(D)=\bigcap_{m>0}\Bs(mD)

where
\Bs(D)
is the base locus of the linear system
|D|
.

Example 2.6
.

In
X=X_{8}^{4}
consider the divisor
-K_{X}
. In
[
11
, Corollary 7.6]
the authors show that
the transform
R
of
a smooth rational quintic curve in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
through the
8
blown-up points is in the base locus of
-K_{X}
. On the other hand
R
is not in the stable base locus of
-K_{X}
, see
[
39
]
.

All the divisors in an open chamber of the stable base locus decomposition have the same stable base locus.
The nef cone
\Nef(X)
is one of the closed chambers of the stable base locus decomposition, since it is the closure of the cone generated by divisors with empty stable base locus.

We say that an irreducible and reduced subvariety
Y
of
X
is a
stable base locus subvariety of
X
if it is a component of the stable base locus of every effective
divisor
D

containing
Y
in its base locus.

A divisor
D
in
X
is called
movable
if its stable base locus has codimension at least two in
X
.
The movable cone of
X
is the convex cone
\Mov(X)
generated by classes of movable divisors.
We recall from
[
37
,
3
]
the following definition.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
0\leq k\leq n-1
.
We define
D_{k}
to be the cone generated by the classes of effective divisors with no component of the stable base locus of dimension
\geq n-k
; we denote with

\mathcal{D}_{k}
the closure of
D_{k}
in
\overline{\Eff}(X)
.

We have the following stratification

(2.10)

\Nef(X)=\mathcal{D}_{n-1}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{n-2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{1}=\overline{\Mov(X)}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{0}=\overline{\Eff}(X),

which is compatible with the stable base locus decomposition of
\overline{\Eff}(X)
because
each cone
\mathcal{D}_{k}
is naturally a union of closed stable base locus chambers.
We refer to
[
3
]
for more details on the properties of such cones.

Note that
the number of global sections is invariant for the action of the Weyl group
W^{n}_{s}
, see

Furthermore, the cone of movable divisors is invariant under the Weyl action, as proved in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.8

Let

be a Mori dream space. Then the movable cone of divisors is preserved under the Weyl action.

Proof.

Indeed,
[

We conclude by recalling that the Weyl action preserves the Dolgachev-Mukai pairing and the effective cone

∎

On the other hand, the stable base locus is not an invariant, nor does

3.
Weyl
r
-planes

In this section we show that in
X_{s}^{n}
for
n+1\leq s\leq n+3
, the set of Weyl
r
-planes coincides with the set including the exceptional divisors and the joins
J(L_{I},\sigma_{t})
, introduced in (
2.12
).
In addition, we obtain a description of the cones of curves
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, for every
0\leq k\leq n-1
, in terms of effective Weyl orbits.

We recall here that in
[
3
, Theorem 5.5]
, the authors proved that the cone
\mathcal{D}_{k}
is dual to the cone of curves
\mathcal{C}_{k}
.
The proof is based on the following result, that we include here for the sake of completeness (see proof of
[
3
, Theorem 5.5]
):

Lemma 3.1
.

For every integer
0\leq t\leq\frac{n}{2}
and every index set
I\subset\{1,\ldots,s\}
of length
0\leq|I|\leq n-2t
, the join
J(L_{I},\sigma_{t})
is swept out by irreducible curves of class

(3.1)

c_{I,t}:=(|I|+(n+1)t-1)h-\sum_{i\in I}(t+1)e_{i}-\sum_{i\notin I}te_{i}.

Notice that when
t=0
, the irreducible curves of class
c_{I,0}=(|I|-1)h-\sum_{i\in I}e_{i}
sweep out the
(|I|-1)
-plane spanned by the points indexed by
I
.

In the following discussion we show that the curve classes in (
3.1
) form effective Weyl orbits of curves of type
c_{\mathcal{I}}
, for a suitable
\mathcal{I}\subset\{1,\ldots,s\}
. This will allow us to conclude that the joins are the only Weyl
r
-plane.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
X=X_{n+3}^{n}
. For a fixed
r\in\{1,\ldots,n-2\}
, the set

\left\{c_{I,t}:|I|+2t-1=r\right\}

is an effective Weyl orbit in
A_{1}(X)
.

For
r=n-1
, the set

\left\{c_{I,t}:|I|+2t-1=n-1\right\}\cup\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{s}\}

is an effective Weyl orbit in
A_{1}(X)
.

Proof.

It is easy to compute the orbit of the curves
c_{I,t}
under the Weyl group action.
It is proved in
[
22
]
that the Coxeter group for Mori dream spaces of type
X^{n}_{s}
is finite, and therefore the orbits of the Weyl group on curve classes are finite. We claim that
c_{I,t}
are all the elements of the effective Weyl orbit
(W^{n}_{s}\cdot c_{\mathcal{I},0})^{+}

of the curve

(3.2)

c_{\mathcal{I}}=(|\mathcal{I}|-1)h-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}e_{i},

where
\mathcal{I}\subset\{1,\ldots,n+3\}
is any set
of length
|\mathcal{I}|=r+1
.
i.e.

(3.3)

(W^{n}_{s}\cdot c_{\mathcal{I}})^{+}=\left\{c_{I,t}:|I|+2t-1=r\right\}.

Set
S:=\{1,\dots,n+3\}
. To show the claim, we consider the curve class
c_{I,t}
and a set
\Gamma\subset S
with
|\Gamma|=n+1
and we analyse all possible images of
c_{I,t}
via the standard Cremona transformation
{\rm Cr}_{\Gamma}
.
Since the number of points is
s=n+3
, we need to consider three distinct cases:

Case 1.
I\subset\Gamma\subset S
,
so that
S=\Gamma\cup\{i,j\}
, with
j\neq i
. Set
J:=I\cup\{i,j\}
. Then, applying a standard Cremona transformation based on the points of the set
\Gamma
, we obtain the following curve:

{\rm Cr}_{\Gamma}(c_{I,t})=c_{J,t-1}.

Case 2. Let
I^{c}
denote the complement of the set
I
in
S
, and let
\Gamma
satisfy
I^{c}\subset\Gamma\subset S
, so that
S=\Gamma\cup\{i,j\}
. Set
J:=I\backslash\{i,j\}
. It is easy to check that applying a standard Cremona transformation based on the points of the set
\Gamma
, one obtains:

{\rm Cr}_{\Gamma}(c_{I,t})=c_{J,t+1}.

Case 3. Finally, if
\Gamma\subset S
and
\Gamma
contains neither the set
I
nor its complement
I^{c}
, then:

{\rm Cr}_{\Gamma}(c_{I,t})=c_{I,t}.

Analysing the three previous cases, we see that after applying a Cremona transformation to a given curve class
c_{I,t}
, the parameter
t
might increase, stay the same or decrease by 1, while the dimension of the join that the irreducible curves with that class span remains invariant. Furthermore, by applying a suitable sequence of standard Cremona transformations, varying
\Gamma
as appropriate, one proves that the set on the right-hand side of equation (
3.3
) is contained in the set on the left-hand one.

We prove the other inclusion, and hence conclude the proof, by showing that
t=0
and
t=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
are the minimum and maximum possible values of
t
, respectively, in the effective Weyl orbit (
3.3
).

Indeed if
t=0
, and we choose
\Gamma
such that
I\subset\Gamma
as in Case 1 above,
the image of
c_{I,0}
under the Cremona transformation
\textrm{Cr}_{\Gamma}
is non-effective.
Thus,
t=0
is the minimum value in the effective Weyl orbit (
3.3
). On the opposite side, if
t=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, we have that the index set
I
must be such that

|I|=i+1-2\lfloor n/2\rfloor\leq(n-1)+1-(n-1)=1.

This implies that
I^{c}
in
\{1,\ldots,n+3\}
is of length
|I|\geq n+2
and we cannot find
\Gamma
as in Case 2 above. Thus, any Cremona transformation of
c_{I,t=\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
will never increase the value of
t
.

∎

Remark 3.3
.

In
X_{s}^{n}
for
n+1\leq s\leq n+2
, the situation is much simpler. In this case, we must have
t=0
and the joins of dimension
r
are just the
r
-planes. Moreover, for

we have that each curve class

is alone
in its effective Weyl orbit, with

. In the case of

with

we have two cases:

•

If

, then for each

we have an effective Weyl orbit

, where
\widehat{I_{j}}:=\{1,\ldots,n+1\}\setminus\{j\}
.

•

If

, then we have a unique effective Weyl orbit

.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let

and

. For any

Let

(1)

Definition 4.3
.

Given an effective divisor

on

, we say that a join

is orthogonal to

, with

Lemma 4.4
.

Let

be a Weyl divisor in

and

a join, then

\kappa_{V}(D)=\tau-t+1-|I\setminus\mathcal{I}|.

In particular, V is orthogonal to D if and only if

|I\setminus\mathcal{I}|=\tau+1-t.

Proof.

Notice that, since
D
is a divisor, it must be
|\mathcal{I}|+2\tau-1=n-1
. Thus, one can compute

\displaystyle\kappa_{V}(D)=

\displaystyle\;-D\cdot c_{V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;-(\tau+1)(|I|+(n+1)t-1)+(\tau+1)(t+1)|\mathcal{I}\cap I|+(\tau+1)t|\mathcal{I}\setminus I|

\displaystyle+\tau(t+1)|I\setminus\mathcal{I}|+\tau t(n+3-|\mathcal{I}\cup I|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\tau-t+1-|I\setminus\mathcal{I}|.

The second statement follows from Definition
4.3

∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Let

be a Weyl divisor on
X_{s}^{n}
with
n+1\leq s\leq n+3
and
V=J(L_{I},\sigma_{t})
a join orthogonal to
D
, if
t=0
then

D\cap V=\left(\bigcup_{i\in I\setminus\mathcal{I}}J(L_{I\setminus\{i\}},\sigma_{t})\right),

while, if
t\geq 1
, we have

D\cap V=\left(\bigcup_{i\in I\setminus\mathcal{I}}J(L_{I\setminus\{i\}},\sigma_{t})\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}\setminus I}J(L_{I\cup\{i\}},\sigma_{t-1})\right).

Proof.

Since
V
is orthogonal to
D
by hypothesis,
V
is not a subvariety of
D
. Thus, if
V
has dimension
r
, the intersection of
D
and
V
is a union of dimension
r-1
subvarieties. The theorem specifies which are these varieties: the ones on the right hand side of the equation. Indeed they are of dimension
r-1
and they are clearly contained in
V
. Moreover, using Lemma
4.4
and by the orthogonality of
V
and
D
, we have that

•

For
i\in I\setminus\mathcal{I}
,

\displaystyle\kappa_{J(L_{I\setminus\{i\}},\sigma_{t})}(D)

\displaystyle=\tau-t+1-|(I\setminus\{i\})\setminus\mathcal{I}|

\displaystyle=\tau-t+1-(|I\setminus\mathcal{I}|-1)=\kappa_{V}(D)+1=1.

•

For
i\in\mathcal{I}\setminus I
, and
t\geq 1

\displaystyle\kappa_{J(L_{I\cup\{i\}},\sigma_{t-1})}(D)

\displaystyle=\tau-(t-1)+1-|(I\cup\{i\})\setminus\mathcal{I}|

\displaystyle=\tau-t+1+1-|I\setminus\mathcal{I}|=\kappa_{V}(D)+1=1.

This proves that each join on the right hand side is contained in
D
once. Hence, we have proven the containment ”
\supseteq
”.

Notice that
D\cap V
is a
r-1
–cycle with

(1)

(\tau+1)\binom{t+n-r}{t}
as
H_{r-1}
–degree,

(2)

(\tau+1)\binom{t+n-r}{t}
as
E_{r-1,j}
–degree for every
j\in\mathcal{I}\cap I
,

(3)

(\tau+1)\binom{t+n-r-1}{t-1}
as
E_{r-1,j}
–degree for every
j\in\mathcal{I}\setminus I
,

(4)

\tau\binom{t+n-r}{t}
as
E_{r-1,j}
–degree for every
j\in I\setminus\mathcal{I}
,

(5)

\tau\binom{t+n-r-1}{t-1}
as
E_{r-1,j}
–degree for every
j\notin\mathcal{I}\cup I
.

Observe that, for
t=0
the
E_{r},j
–degree is 0 in cases
3
and
5
.

To conclude the proof we show that the right hand side is an
r-1
–cycle with the same class. We do so for a general
t
, the case

follows the same computations but only considering the first union in the right hand side. Thus, it is left to the reader.

We start by checking the
H_{r-1}
–degree. Following Remark
4.1
, for every
i\in I\setminus\mathcal{I}
each join
J(L_{I\setminus\{i\}},\sigma_{t})
has
H_{r-1}
–degree
\binom{t+n-r+1}{t}
, while for every
i\in\mathcal{I}\setminus I
the joins
J(L_{I\cup\{i\}},\sigma_{t-1})
have
H_{r-1}
–degree
\binom{t+n-r}{t-1}
. Thus, their union has
H_{r-1}
–degree

(4.1)

\sum_{i\in I\setminus\mathcal{I}}\binom{t+n-r+1}{t}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}\setminus I}\binom{t+n-r}{t-1}=|I\setminus\mathcal{I}|\binom{t+n-r+1}{t}+|\mathcal{I}\setminus I|\binom{t+n-r}{t-1}.

Since
D=J(L_{\mathcal{I}},\sigma_{\tau})
and
V=J(L_{I},\sigma_{t})
are varieties of dimension
n-1
and
r
, respectively. We have that
|\mathcal{I}|=n-2\tau
and
|I|=r-2t+1
. Moreover, since
V
is orthogonal to
D
, by Lemma
4.4
we have that
|I\setminus\mathcal{I}|=\tau+1-t
. Therefore,

|\mathcal{I}\cap I|=|I|-|I\setminus\mathcal{I}|=r-2t+1-(\tau+1-t)=r-t-\tau,

and

|\mathcal{I}\setminus I|=|\mathcal{I}|-|\mathcal{I}\cap I|=n-2\tau-(r-t-\tau)=n+t-r-\tau.

Thus,

|I\setminus\mathcal{I}|+|\mathcal{I}\setminus I|=\tau+1-t+n+t-r-\tau=n-r+1.

This, together with the usual binomial identity
\binom{t+n-r+1}{t}=\binom{t+n-r}{t}+\binom{t+n-r}{t-1}
, we have that equation
4.1
is equal to

|I\setminus\mathcal{I}|\binom{t+n-r}{t}+\left(|I\setminus\mathcal{I}|+|\mathcal{I}\setminus I|\right)\binom{t+n-r}{t-1}

=(\tau+1-t)\binom{t+n-r}{t}+(n-r+1)\binom{t+n-r}{t-1}

=(\tau+1-t)\binom{t+n-r}{t}+t\binom{t+n-r}{t}

=(\tau+1-t+t)\binom{t+n-r}{t}=(\tau+1)\binom{t+n-r}{t}.

As for the

–degrees, we have the following cases:

(1)

If

, we have that each join

has

–degree
\binom{t+n-r+1}{t}
for every

, and the joins

for every

. Following the exact same calculation as before, we conclude that the right hand side has

–degree

(2)

If

and a similar calculation shows that this adds up to

.

(3)

If

then, the right hand side has

–degree

which adds up to

(4)

If

–degree

which adds up to
\tau\binom{t+n-r-1}{t-1}
.

Since the right hand side is contained in the left hand side and both have the same class, we conclude that they are equal.
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
V=J(L_{I},\sigma_{t})
be a join of dimension
r
in
X_{s}^{n}
, for
n+1\leq s\leq n+3
. Then
V
is a Weyl
r
-cycle.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
I=\{1,\ldots,a\}
for some
a\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
such that
\dim V=|I|+2t-1=a+2t-1=r
; where
a=0
means that
I
is the empty set.

To prove the statement we will show that
V
is an irreducible component of

D_{a+1}\cap\ldots\cap D_{n+1-2t},

where
D_{j}=E_{\mathcal{I}_{j},\sigma_{t}}
(as defined in Lemma
4.4
) with
\mathcal{I}_{j}=\{1,\ldots,n+1-2t\}\setminus\{j\}
. Indeed, notice that they are pairwise orthogonal divisors since for every
j_{1}\neq j_{2}

\langle D_{j_{1}},D_{j_{2}}\rangle=\kappa_{D_{j_{1}}}(D_{j_{2}})=t-t+1-|\mathcal{I}_{j_{1}}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{j_{2}}|=1-|\{j_{2}\}|=0.

If
r=n-1
, then
V=D_{n+1-2t}
and there is nothing to prove.

By induction, assume the statement to be true for joins of dimension
r+1
, and let
V=J(L_{I},\sigma_{t})
be of dimension
r\leq n-2
.

Consider
V^{\prime}=J(L_{I\cup\{a+1\}},\sigma_{t})
, which is a join of dimension
r+1
. By the induction hypothesis we know that
V^{\prime}
is an irreducible component of

D_{a+2}\cap\ldots\cap D_{n+1-2t}.

Following Lemma
4.4
, observe that

\kappa_{V^{\prime}}(D_{a+1})=t-t+1-\left|\{1,\ldots,a+1\}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{a+1}\right|=1-|\{a+1\}|=0,

which means that
V^{\prime}
is orthogonal to
D_{a+1}
. By Proposition
4.5
it follows that
V
is an irreducible component of
D_{a+1}\cap V^{\prime}
. Hence,
V
is an irreducible component of

D_{a+1}\cap D_{a+2}\cap\ldots\cap D_{n+1-2t}.

∎

In particular the next theorem answers affirmatively a question posed in
[
7
, Conjecture 2]
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Weyl
r
-cycles in
X_{s}^{n}
are all and only the joins of dimension
r
, for
n+1\leq s\leq n+3
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.6
we know that joins of dimension
r
are Weyl
r
-cycles. We prove that if
D_{1},\ldots,D_{l}
is a list of pairwise orthogonal Weyl divisors, their intersection is a union of joins.

We use induction on
l
.
If
l=1
there is nothing to prove. The case
l=2
is a particular case of Proposition
4.5
when
V
is itself a divisor. Assume the statement to be true for
l-1
, then

D_{1}\cap\ldots\cap D_{l}=\left(D_{1}\cap\ldots\cap D_{l-1}\right)\cap D_{l}=\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}V_{i}\right)\cap D_{l}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}(V_{i}\cap D_{l}),

where
V_{1},\ldots,V_{m}
are the joins whose union is the intersection
D_{1}\cap\ldots\cap D_{l-1}
. We conclude by showing that
V_{i}\cap D_{l}
is a union of joins for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
.

On one hand, since
V_{i}
is a join contained in
D_{1}
, by Lemma
4.2
there exists a
0
-moving curve
c_{V_{i},D_{1}}
such that
c_{D_{1}}=c_{V_{i}}+c_{V_{i},D_{1}}
. On the other hand, by the orthogonality of
D_{1}
and
D_{l}
, we have that

0=c_{D_{1}}\cdot D_{l}=c_{V_{i}}\cdot D_{l}+c_{V_{i},D_{1}}\cdot D_{l}.

We know that
c_{V_{i},D_{1}}\cdot D_{l}\geq 0
, since
c_{V_{i},D_{1}}
is
0
-moving and
D_{l}
is an effective divisor. Hence, it must be that
c_{V_{i}}\cdot D_{l}\leq 0
. If
c_{V_{i}}\cdot D_{l}<0
, then
V_{i}
is contained in
D_{l}
and, therefore
V_{i}\cap D_{l}=V_{i}
, which is a join. Otherwise,
c_{V_{i}}\cdot D_{l}=0
, which means that
V_{i}
is a join orthogonal to
D_{l}
and by Proposition
4.5
we know that their intersection is a union of joins.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Assume that

Proposition 6.5
.

Assume that

Proof.

Fix

If

First, we show that it is surjective. Take

with

7.
Cones of divisors, cones of curves and Weyl chamber decomposition for non-Mori dream spaces

In this section we discuss our point of view on the birational geometry of non-Mori dream spaces of type
X^{n}_{s}
, that is based on the action of the Weyl group studied in Section
6
. In particular, after observing the remarkable role played by the anticanonical curve class in the description of the pseudoeffective cone of divisors, we propose a chamber decomposition of the latter, that is governed by the action of the Weyl group of
X^{n}_{s}
.

While observing that
F
is a moving curve, or a limit of moving curves, for all Mori dream spaces
X^{n}_{s}
, we recall that the same holds for the non-Mori dream space
X^{3}_{8}
, following work of
[
38
]
, and we prove it for
X^{5}_{9}
.

We conclude the section by posing a few conjectures about chamber decompositions.

7.1.
The anticanonical curve class and the negative part of the pseudoeffective cone

The anticanonical curve class
F
, see Definition
2.1
, plays a fundamental role in Coxeter theory, see
[
20
]
for more details.

Recalling the Dolgachev-Mukai pairing (
2.3
), we say that a divisor class
D
lies in the
negative part
of the
Néron-Severi space
N^{1}(X^{n}_{s})_{\mathbb{R}}
if

\langle D,K_{X^{n}_{s}}\rangle\leq 0,

where
K_{X^{n}_{s}}
denotes the canonical divisor class.
Equivalently, we can say that a divisor lies in the negative part of
N^{1}(X^{n}_{s})_{\mathbb{R}}

if
D\cdot F\geq 0,

by using the intersection product (
2.7
). For this reason we will denote by

F^{\vee}:=\{D:D\cdot F\geq 0\}\subset N^{1}(X^{n}_{s})_{\mathbb{R}}

the negative part of
N^{1}(X^{n}_{s})_{\mathbb{R}}
.

The pseudoeffective cone is split in a negative part and a positive part. Like in the case of surfaces
X^{2}_{s}
, the negative part is easier to understand, while the positive part is wilder, see e.g.
[
16
]
.
Since
\mathcal{C}_{0}
, the closure of the cone of moving curves, is dual to the pseudoeffective cone, we observe that the latter lies in the negative part
F^{\vee}
if and only if
F\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
, i.e. if
F
is a limit of moving curves.
Understanding for which values of
(n,s)
the anticanonical curve class
F
is moving, or a limit of moving curves, is therefore crucial.

We start with an effectivity result.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
X=X^{n}_{s}
be the blown-up projective space at
s
general points. Assume either
s\leq 2n+2
and
n\geq 3
, or
s\leq 9
and
n=2
. Then the anticanonical class
F
is effective.

Proof.

It is enough to prove the statement for
X^{2}_{9}
and for
X^{n}_{2n+2}
, for
n\geq 3
.

In
X^{2}_{9}
,
F
is the unique elliptic curve passing through nine points in general position.

In
X=X^{n}_{2n+2}
, the curve class
F
can be written as a nonnegative linear combination of lines through two points as follows:

F=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}(h-e_{2i-1}-e_{2i}).

∎

Notice that, for
n=2
and
s\geq 10
, the anticanonical divisor
F
is not effective.

In the next result we prove that
F
lies in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
for every Mori dream space
X^{n}_{s}
, as well as in a few sporadic non-Mori dream cases, that occur for small values of
s
.

Proposition 7.2
.

Assume that either
X=X^{n}_{s}
is a Mori dream space (i.e. it falls in the list (
2.4
)), or
X\in\{X^{2}_{9},X^{3}_{8},X^{5}_{9}\}
. If
D\in\operatorname{Pic}(X)
is an effective divisor, then

D\cdot F\geq 0.

Proof.

Notice that if
D
is a Weyl divisor on
X^{n}_{s}
, then
D\cdot F=1
. In fact, since the intersection pairing is preserved under the Weyl action and
F
is Cremona invariant,
then for every Weyl divisor
D
there exists
w\in W^{n}_{s}
with
w(D)=E_{i}
, for some
i
. Hence, we have
D\cdot F=E_{i}\cdot F=1
.
Since for all Mori dream spaces
X^{n}_{s}
, Weyl divisors generate the effective cone, we conclude that
D\cdot F\geq 1
for every effective divisor
D
.

Consider now
X^{2}_{9}
,
X^{3}_{8}
.
The pseudoeffective cone in these cases is generated by the Weyl divisors and by the anticanonical divisor class (
2.2
).
This is proved in
[
36
]
(see also
[
16
]
) for
X^{2}_{9}
and
in
[
38
, Theorem 6.5]
for

X^{3}_{8}
.
Since in both cases
-K_{X}\cdot F=0
, we conclude again that
D\cdot F\geq 0
for every effective divisor.

Finally, we prove the statement for
X=X^{5}_{9}
.
In this case, we show that
D
is effective and
D\cdot F=0
if and only if
D
lies in the ray generated by the anticanonical divisor
-K
. Accepting this claim, the effective cone of
X^{5}_{9}
is contained in the halfspace
F^{\vee}=\{D\in\N^{1}(X)_{\mathbb{R}}|D\cdot F\geq 0\}
or in the halfspace
\{D\in\N^{1}(X)_{\mathbb{R}}|D\cdot F\leq 0\}
, and so does its closure, the pseudoeffective cone. Now, since there are effective divisors lying in
F^{\vee}
, e.g. the exceptional divisors, then we conclude that
\overline{\Eff}(X^{5}_{9})\subset F^{\vee}
and that the only intersection with
F^{\perp}
consists of the ray spanned by the antincanonical divisor.

We now prove the claim. That
D=m(-K)
is effective and has null intersection with
F
is obvious, so we prove the converse statement.
Set
D=dH-\sum_{i=1}^{9}m_{i}E_{i}
; the condition
D\cdot F=0
reads

(7.1)

6d=m_{1}+\cdots+m_{9}.

Now, since the intersection number is preserved by the Weyl group action, and since the curve
F
is Weyl invariant, we have
w(D)\cdot F=D\cdot F
, where
w\in W^{5}_{9}
, therefore we may assume that
D
is Cremona reduced, i.e. that

(7.2)

4d\geq\sum_{i\in I}m_{i},\quad\forall I\subseteq\{1,\dots,9\},|I|=6.

Now, using (
7.1
) and (
7.2
), we obtain

(7.3)

2d\leq\sum_{i\notin I}m_{i},\quad\forall I\subseteq\{1,\dots,9\},|I|=6.

Now, using (
7.1
) and (

), we obtain

0=\sum_{i=1}^{9}m_{i}-6d=(m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}-2d)+(m_{4}+m_{5}+m_{6}-2d)+(m_{7}+m_{8}+m_{9}-2d)\geq 0,

from which, permuting indices, we obtain that
m_{i_{1}}+m_{i_{2}}+m_{1_{3}}=2d
, for every three distinct indices
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}
, so
m_{1}=\cdots=m_{9}
. From this, we obtain that
6d=9m_{1}
hence
D
is a multiple of
3H-2\sum_{i=1}^{9}E_{i}
. Since
-K_{X}
is Cremona invariant we conclude the proof.
∎

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
X
as in Proposition
7.2
. Then the anticanonical curve
F
is a limit of moving curves in
X
.

Proof.

From Proposition
7.2
we obtain that the effective cone lies completely in the negative part of
N^{1}(X)_{\mathbb{R}}
. Moreover, if
X\in\{X_{9}^{2},X_{8}^{3},X_{9}^{5}\}
, then
\Eff(X)
shares just one ray (the anticanonical ray) with the hyperplane
F^{\perp}
. Taking the closure, we conclude that
\overline{\Eff}(X)\subset F^{\vee}
. This implies that
F
is in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
, which is the cone dual to the pseudoeffective cone. In particular, if
X
is a MDS, then
F
lies in the interior of
\mathcal{C}_{0}
, whereas if
X\in\{X_{9}^{2},X_{8}^{3},X_{9}^{5}\}
, then
F
spans an extremal ray of
\mathcal{C}_{0}
.
∎

For higher values of
s
, the curve
F
is not moving in general, as proved in the following result.

Proposition 7.4
.

Assume
X=X^{n}_{n+6}
and
n\geq 3
or
X=X^{n}_{n+5}
and
n\geq 4
. Then there are effective divisors lying strictly in the positive part of the pseudoeffective cone of
X
. In particular the anticanonical curve
F
is not a limit of moving curve classes in

, i.e.
F\notin\mathcal{C}_{0}
.

Proof.

We consider the case
X=X^{n}_{n+6}
and
n\geq 3
. Assume first
n=3
and
consider the quadric of
X^{3}_{9}
, given by
D=-\frac{1}{2}K_{X^{3}_{9}}=2H-\sum_{i=1}^{9}E_{i}
.
For

, by taking cones over the quadric, we have effective divisors of the form

. In both cases

, hence

lies in the positive part of the pseudoeffective cone.

Now we consider the case

and

.
The divisor

is effective on

, because

and we have

.
By taking cones over

, we obtain effective divisors that intersect negatively

in

, for any

.
∎

The only remaining open cases are the following.

Question 7.5
.

, for

.
Is the anticanonical curve class
F
on

not a limit of moving curves?

7.2.
Weyl chamber decomposition

In this section we propose an application of the study of Weyl
r
-planes as stable base locus subvarieties of
X^{n}_{s}
.

Recalling (
3.2
) and
using the notation of Lemma
6.9
, we consider the set of curve classes on
X^{n}_{s}
:

\{w(c_{I}):w(c_{I})\in(W^{n}_{s}\cdot c_{I})^{+},\;I\subset\{1,\dots,s\},\;2\leq|I|\leq n\}.

Recall also that each such curve sweeps out the Weyl
r
-plane
w(L_{I})
.
We consider the (infinite) hyperplane arrangement in the Néron-Severi space given by

(7.4)

\{D\cdot w(c_{I})=0:w(c_{I})\in(W^{n}_{s}\cdot c_{I})^{+},I\subset\{1,\dots,s\},2\leq|I|\leq n\}.

By Lemma
6.9
, each such hyperplane splits the pseudoeffective cone of divisors in two regions, separating the divisors
D
that contain
w(L_{I})
in their stable base locus, from those that do not.
This hyperplane arrangement induces a wall-and-chamber decomposition of the pseudoeffective cone of divisors (and of any cone
\mathcal{D}_{k}
), where each chamber has the property that divisors lying in the interior of the chamber have the same reduced Weyl stable base locus, cf.
Definition

6.11
.

Definition 7.6
.

The
Weyl chamber decomposition
is the wall-and-chamber decomposition of the pseudoeffective cone of divisors of
X^{n}_{s}
induced by the hyperplane arrangement (
7.4
). We say that two effective divisors are Weyl equivalent if they lie in the interior of the same Weyl chamber.

Since the Weyl stable base locus scheme is contained in the stable base locus, the Weyl chamber decomposition (
\WCD
) can be refined to the stable base locus decomposition (
\SBLD
).

We recall that for Mori dream spaces of type
X^{n}_{s}
, the WCD, the SBLD and the Mori chamber decomposition (MCD) of the effective cone coincide, see e.g.
[
9
, Chapter 2]
and
[
3
, Theorem 5.7]
, showing how the action of the Weyl group
W^{n}_{s}
on curves governs completely the birational geometry of these spaces. In fact the following holds.

(1)

All extremal rays of the effective cone are Weyl
(n-1)
-planes, as proved by Mukai.

(2)

All small
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial modifications are sequences of flips of (varieties birational to) Weyl
r
-planes, with
r<n-1
; each such subvariety is swept out by a curve class in the orbit of
c_{\mathcal{I}}
for some
\mathcal{I}\subset\{1,\ldots,s\}
of length
r+1
, as proved in
[
3
]
.

(3)

On the dual side, the extremal rays of all the cones of
r
-moving curves are either
0
-moving,
1
-moving, or
r
-moving in a Weyl
(n-r)
-plane, see Theorems
3.5
,
5.5
and
5.6
.

(4)

The cone of
0
-moving curves in
X^{n}_{s}
, that is the dual of the effective cone of divisors, is given by
0
-Weyl lines and
1
-Weyl lines
[
22
]
.

We now turn our attention to the case of non-Mori dream spaces of type
X^{n}_{s}
. In this case even the description of the pseudoeffective cone of divisors seems out of reach for most cases (when
F
is not in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
).
Even more, we do not have a definition of Mori equivalence as in
[
30
]
because the section rings of
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier divisors might not be finitely generated. In spite of this, we may ask whether an extension of the notion of Mori chamber decomposition of the pseudoeffective cone of
X^{n}_{s}
(negative part) can be achieved, i.e. a
nef chamber decomposition
where chambers would be pull-backs of nef cones of infinitely many rational contractions
of
X^{n}_{s}
. More precisely,
a chamber is realised as
f^{\ast}\textrm{Nef}(Y)\ast\textrm{ex}(f)
, where
f:X\dasharrow Y
is a finite sequence of flips of subvarieties of
X
followed by a divisorial contraction.

The first open cases occur for
X^{3}_{8}
and
X^{n}_{n+4}
, for
n\geq 5
.

7.3.
Open questions on cones of divisors

Recall that all divisors in the interior of a Weyl chamber share the same (reduced) Weyl stable base locus scheme, whose components form a poset, where the order is given by inclusion of varieties and the minimal elements are Weyl
1
-planes.
We assume that we may flip all the components, following the inclusion order and starting from the curves. In particular we are assuming that
[

, Conjecture 2.2]
holds, i.e. that all Weyl
1
-planes are disjoint in

and, moreover, that the birational images of the Weyl

-planes in the poset are disjoint after having flipped the Weyl
(r-1)
-planes, for every

We first focus on the cases
X^{5}_{9}
and

, where the pseudoeffective cone has only a negative part, see Proposition

We propose the following conjecture.

Conjecture 7.7
.

Let

or
X=X^{3}_{8}
.
Then

(1)

\WCD(\overline{\Eff}(X))=\SBLD(\overline{\Eff}(X)).

(2)

Each Weyl chamber is of the form
f^{\ast}\textrm{Nef}(Y)\ast\textrm{ex}(f)

Remark 7.8
.

Recall that the nef cones of

and of

are rational polyhedral, see e.g.
[

]
and
[

Since, by Theorem

, there are infinitely many Weyl

-planes and divisors, then the Weyl chamber decompositions, in both cases, consists of infinitely many chambers.
It would be interesting to know whether all such

Stenger and Xie in

proved an existence result for a subdivision of the effective movable cone of

; this would provide a proof of Conjecture

for

. To the best of our knowledge, the conjecture is completely open for

.

Less is known for

, with

. For instance, we do not know whether the pseudoeffective cone has a positive part for

, see
Question

7.5
.
For

and

.
For this reason, we pose the following question.

Question 7.9
.

Assume that

is not a Mori dream space that

,

(1)

?

8.
Weyl expected dimension of linear systems

In
[
4
, Definition 3.2]
, the authors proposed the notion of

linear expected dimension
, which takes into account the contribution of linear cycles in the stable base locus. They also proved that this is in fact the actual dimension for any linear system in
X^{n}_{s}
with
s\leq n+2
.
In
[
6
, Definition 6.1]
, the more general notion of
secant linear dimension
for linear systems in
X^{n}_{s}
with
s\leq n+3
was proposed, and in
[
32
]
it was proved to match the actual dimension.
In the cases
X^{3}_{7}
and
X^{4}_{8}
, the definition of

Weyl expected dimension
was given in
[
7
, Definition 2]
.

Here we propose a new definition of
Weyl expected dimension
, that extends all the previous ones and applies to any
X_{s}^{n}
.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
D
be an effective divisor on
X=X^{n}_{s}
. We call Weyl virtual (affine) dimension of the linear system
\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{P}(\HH^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}(D)))
the number

(8.1)

\wdim(\mathcal{L})=\chi(X,\mathcal{O}(D))+\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}\sum_{W\in\mathcal{W}(r)}(-1)^{r+1}\binom{n+k_{W}(D)-r-1}{n},

where
\mathcal{W}(r)
is the set of all the Weyl
r
-planes and the multiplicity of containment
k_{W}(D)
of the Weyl plane
W
in the base locus of
D
can be explicitly computed by Remark
8.2
.

We define the Weyl expected (affine) dimension of
\mathcal{L}
as
the maximum between
\wdim(\mathcal{L})
and
0
.

Remark 8.2
.

We recall that, given
r
, a Weyl
r
-plane
W\in\mathcal{W}(r)
is an element of the effective orbit
(W_{s}^{n}\cdot L_{I})^{+}
for an index set
I
of length
r+1
, and, by Proposition
6.5
, it corresponds to an element of the effective orbit
(W_{s}^{n}\cdot c_{I})^{+}
of the curve class
c_{I}
, (
3.2
).
By Lemma
6.9
, the multiplicity of containment
k_{W}(D)
of a Weyl plane
W=w(L_{I})
in the base locus of a divisor
D
is obtained intersecting
D
with the unique curve sweeping it out,
w(c_{I})
,

k_{W}(D)=\max(-w(c_{I})\cdot D,0).

Remark 8.3
.

We observe that the sum (
8.1
) is finite for any effective divisor
D
. In fact,
for every
I
, even when the orbit of the Weyl
r
-plane
L_{I}
is infinite,
only finitely many elements of the latter,
W\in(W_{s}^{n}\cdot L_{I})^{+}
, can be contained in the base locus of
D
,
because the number of components of the base locus is finite, by Lasker-Noether Theorem.
Consequently, since
k_{W}(D)
is the multiplicity of containment of
W
in the base locus of
D
,
only finitely many Newton binomials in (
8.1
) are nonzero.

Remark 8.4
.

We observe that for
s=n+2
the Weyl expected dimension of a non-empty linear system equals its linear expected dimension defined in
[
4
]
,
\wdim(\mathcal{L})=ldim(\mathcal{L})
.
For
s=n+3
, the Weyl expected dimension of a linear system equals the secant linear expected dimension,
\wdim(\mathcal{L})=\sigma\mathrm{ldim}(\mathcal{L})
, defined in
[
6
]
.
For
n=4
,
s=8
, this notion equals to the Weyl expected dimension for divisors in
X^{4}_{8}
defined in
[
7
]
.
We conclude that Definition
8.1
generalizes the notions of dimensions of linear systems expected to hold for all Mori Dream Spaces of the form
X^{n}_{s}
.

Proposition 8.5
.

The Weyl virtual dimension is preserved under the Weyl group action.

Proof.

Let
D
be an effective divisor on
X
and
\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{P}(\HH^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}(D)))
the associated linear system.
Let
\textrm{Cr}=\textrm{Cr}_{\Gamma}
be a standard Cremona transformation and we denote by
\textrm{Cr}(\mathcal{L})=\mathbb{P}(\HH^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}(\textrm{Cr}(D))))
. We need to prove that

\wdim(\mathcal{L})=\wdim(\textrm{Cr}(\mathcal{L}))

We first consider the case
s=n+4
and
D\in\operatorname{Pic}(X^{n}_{n+4})
an arbitrary effective divisor in
X^{n}_{n+4}
.
It is well known that the dimension of a linear system is invariant under Weyl group actions.

Let
G\in\operatorname{Pic}(X^{n}_{n+3})
be a divisor obtained from
D
by forgetting one point
p
that is not in
\Gamma
; obviously

G
is an effective divisor on
X^{n}_{n+3}
. Moreover, also
\textrm{Cr}(\mathcal{L}_{G})
is an effective divisor in
X^{n}_{n+3}
.
By
[
32
]
we know that the dimension of any non-empty linear system in
X^{n}_{n+3}
is equal to the
secant linear dimension
, which coincides with the virtual Weyl dimension. Therefore we obtain

\wdim(\mathcal{L}_{G})=\dim(\mathcal{L}_{G})=\dim(\textrm{Cr}(\mathcal{L}_{G}))=\wdim(\textrm{Cr}(\mathcal{L}_{G})).

We remark that the Weyl
r
-planes that appear in the base locus of
G
only involve the Weyl
r
-planes based exclusively at the points away from
p
; in particular the
\wdim
formula carries all fixed linear subspaces through base points of
\Gamma
.

We claim that, for all the remaining Weyl
r
-planes
W
interpolating the point
p
, which is away from the indeterminacy locus, we have

k_{W}(D)=k_{\textrm{Cr}(W)}(\textrm{Cr}(D)).

Remark first that the birational transformation
Cr
does not contract the Weyl
r
-planes
W
. Indeed, the transformation
Cr
only contracts linear spaces spanned by the points of
\Gamma
, and
p\notin\Gamma
. Moreover, we have seen that each Weyl
r
-plane
W
, is spanned by exactly one curve class say
c
. In particular
c
interpolates the point
p
so the curve class
c
is not contracted by the birational map
Cr
.

We further remark that the standard intersection product between a curve and a divisor is invariant under the Weyl group action
[
22
]
; one concludes

k_{W}(D)=\max(-c\cdot D,0)=\max(-\textrm{Cr}(c)\cdot\textrm{Cr}(D),0)=k_{\textrm{Cr}(W)}(\textrm{Cr}(D)).

To conclude for
s\geq n+5
we proceed by induction on the number of points starting from
s=n+4.

∎

In the next theorem we list a collection of divisors known to have the Weyl expected dimension.

Theorem 8.6
.

Assume that one of the following conditions holds:

(1)

D\in\operatorname{Pic}(X^{n}_{s})
is a divisor such that
D\cdot F\geq D\cdot h
.

(2)

For
n\geq 4

is a

(3)

(4)

.

(5)

with multiplicities bounded by

For

.

Then the dimension of the linear system associated to

coincides with its Weyl expected dimension.

Proof.

The first statement follows from Theorem 5.3 of
[
4
]
and Remark
8.4
.

We recall that any effective divisor in
\operatorname{Pic}(X^{2}_{9})
and
\operatorname{Pic}(X^{3}_{8})
has the Weyl expected dimension by
[
12
]
and
[
17
]
, respectively.
By
[
5
, Theorem 5.8]
this is true also for
any effective divisor in
\operatorname{Pic}(X^{3}_{9})
with multiplicities bounded by
8
.

Any effective divisor in
\operatorname{Pic}(X^{2}_{k^{2}})
and all equal multiplicities has the Weyl expected dimension by
[
14
]
. This result also holds for effective divisors in
\operatorname{Pic}(X^{2}_{s})
with arbitrary multiplicities bounded by
11
by
[
26
]
,
or with homogeneous multiplicities bounded by
42

[
27
]
.

In all these cases, arguing as in the proof of
[

it is easy to prove that
\wdim
is preserved by cone reduction.
Hence
the result follows by taking cones over

.
∎
