Recognition and silencing of a new transposable element
Authors: Luca Salvi, Yinxia Yan, Mario Halic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenomes constantly face threats from transposable elements (TEs) and other genomic parasites. While the silencing of existing TE has been well studied, little is known about how cells recognize new invading TEs that they have not previously encountered. Here we explore this question by inserting foreign sequences into S. pombe. Our data revealed that the newly invading TE tj1 is recognized and targeted for silencing by RNAi and heterochromatin. The efficiency of recognition, as well as the degree and stability of silencing, depends on the copy number and insertion location of the TE. We demonstrated that RNA, rather than DNA, is sensed, and that the efficiency of TE recognition correlates with levels of RNA antisense to the TE, generated from upstream transcripts. We also show that various genes of non-transposable nature can initiate silencing. Our data show that silencing may not require recognition of specific elements in the transposon by the host defense systems, and suggest that disruption of host transcription patterns triggers recognition of TE.Subject terms: Gene silencing, RNAi, Gene regulation

Introduction

Genomes constantly face threats from transposable elements (TEs) and other genomic parasites. TEs, which can move from one genomic location to another, constitute approximately 50% of the human genome. Uncontrolled TE propagation can be detrimental to cell viability; therefore, organisms have developed strategies to recognize and silence TEs to mitigate their negative effects1–5. This requires a system that is both highly adaptable to recognize diverse TE sequences and highly specific to avoid silencing of host genes4.

In plants and worms, the RNA interference (RNAi) pathway serves as the primary mechanism for silencing existing TEs, whereas in most animals, small RNA-based pathways, such as the piRNA pathway in mammals, are predominantly active in germline cells1,6,7. In mammals, TEs are particularly active in germline cells and in early embryos before germline emergence8. In these cells, tight control of TE activity is crucial because their transposition to new loci can be transmitted to the next generation. In these pathways, small RNAs derived from TE sequences guide Argonaute/PIWI family proteins to target TE RNA for degradation. In addition, in many organisms, small RNA pathways recruit chromatin-modifying enzymes to establish silent heterochromatin over TE sequences6,9,10.

Apart from small RNA-based silencing mechanisms, sequence-specific DNA-binding proteins have also been implicated in transposon silencing. In S. pombe, endogenous transposon Tf2 is silenced by homologs of the human CENP-B DNA-binding protein, which evolved from a DNA transposase11. In human cells, rapidly evolving KRAB zinc-finger proteins have been suggested to play a role in the recognition and silencing of new TEs3,12. Another mechanism to silence retroelements was recently described in differentiated human cells, wherein the HUSH complex recognizes retroviral elements by sensing intronless RNA of foreign origin that has not been previously encountered13,14.

Less is known about how cells recognize newly invading TEs to initiate the trans-generational silencing (Fig. 1A)4. Here we explore this question by inserting a foreign TE—tj1, a Ty3/gypsy-like element from S. japonicus—into S. pombe15,16. Our data revealed that the newly invading TE tj1 is recognized and targeted for silencing by RNAi and heterochromatin. The efficiency of recognition, as well as the degree and stability of silencing, depends on the copy number and insertion location of the TE. We demonstrated that RNA, rather than DNA, is sensed, and that the efficiency of TE recognition correlates with levels of RNA antisense to the TE, which would be generated from upstream transcripts. We also show that foreign and host genes of non-transposable nature can initiate silencing, suggesting that specific sequence elements may not be required for transposon recognition by host defense.

Results

Establishment of silencing

We engineered a transposon silencing reporter by creating an artificial operon (Fig. S1A) that contains S. pombe genes ade6, ura4 and an inactive non-transposing copy of the tj1 element from S. japonicus, to prevent its uncontrolled propagation15. Silencing of ade6 results in the appearance of red colonies on low-adenine plates, thus serving as a reporter for tj1 silencing. The operon was inserted into S. pombe chromosome 1, at the site of the endogenous TE tf2-5, to yield strain 1tj1 (Fig. 1B). This strain did not produce red colonies on low-adenine, indicating tj1 was not silenced (Fig. 1C).

A common feature of transposable elements is the presence of multiple copies. We thus sought to increase the number of tj1 elements in the genome by inducing transposition of tj1 into strain 1tj1 (Fig. S1A, see “Methods”). We first isolated a set of seven strains that contained one additional copy of tj1 and thus harbored two copies of the transposon in the genome; these were named strains 2tj1-(A-G) (A-G mark sites of insertion, see Fig. 1B). We next performed another round of tj1 transposition into the 2tj1 strains and isolated two clones that harbored three tj1 elements, yielding strains 3tj1-EL and 3tj1-FM (derived from strains 2tj1-E and 2tj1-F, respectively; L and M are new sites of transposition, see Fig. 1B and Table S1). Consistently with a previous report15, tj1 transposed preferentially into sites near genes for tRNAs and 5S rRNA.

Only strains 2tj1-G and 3tj1-EL were able to spontaneously silence the tj1 copy in the reporter operon, generating up to 7 red colonies per 30,000 cells (Fig. 1C and S1C, D). Thus, the presence of additional copies of tj1 can initiate silencing in a manner that was strongly dependent on the insertion location.

We next increased the number of copies of tj1 elements using a multi-copy plasmid T1 (with average ~10 copies per cell) carrying the inactive tj1 (donor plasmid, Fig. S1A). Introduction of the plasmid led to silencing of the operon and the emergence of red colonies in all strains, including 1tj1 where no transposition occurred, although the frequencies differed between strains (Figs. 1C, S1F, G). Thus, the presence of additional tj1 copies promotes recognition and silencing of tj1.

Our data demonstrate that each additional copy of the tj1 element increases the probability of its recognition and silencing. A second and third genomic copies were sufficient to initiate its recognition and silencing, depending on their genomic locations. The rate of silencing was enhanced by additional tj1 copies on a plasmid.

Heterochromatin formation

In S. pombe, the endogenous transposon tf2 is silenced by the CENP-B family protein Cbp1, independently of RNA interference (RNAi) and heterochromatin11. We assessed the role of Cbp1 in silencing of tj1 by ChIP and found that Cbp1 is enriched at the long terminal repeats (LTRs) of the tf2 transposon but not at tj1 (Fig. S2A)

Heterochromatin formation is a common mechanism underlying gene silencing10,17,18, so we hypothesized that heterochromatin might mediate the silencing of tj1. To test this hypothesis, we measured tj1 RNA and H3K9 methylation (H3K9me) levels in isolated red colonies from the 2tj1 and 3tj1 strains. Levels of tj1 RNA were reduced in cells derived from red colonies from strains 2tj1-(A–G) compared 1tj1 strain (Figs. 1D, E and S2B). Consistently with reduced RNA levels, H3K9me levels at the reporter operon were strongly increased in the red colonies from strains 2tj1-(A–G) compared to 1tj1 strain, indicating establishment of heterochromatin (Figs. 1E, F and S2C, D). In cells derived from red colonies from strains 3tj1-EL and 3tj1-FM, we observed a further reduction of tj1 RNA and an increase in H3K9me compared to 2tj1 strains (Figs. 1D, F and S2A-C).

In fission yeast, RNAi is required for centromeric heterochromatin formation. We tested that requirement for tj1 silencing by deleting the Argonaute gene (ago1), which binds small RNAs9. The ago1Δ cells did not form red colonies (Fig. S2E), indicating that RNAi is essential for initiation of transposon silencing. Consistent with these findings, in the wild-type strain, we found high levels of Ago1-bound small RNAs originating from the reporter operon in red colonies (Figs. 1E, S2F–G and S3A). Notably, low levels of Ago1-bound small RNAs from the tj1 reporter operon were also detected in white colonies, indicating RNAi activity in absence of stable heterochromatic domains (Fig. S3A-B).

Next, we assessed the stability of the newly established silencing of tj1 by propagating the red colonies from the different strains (see Methods). In the 1tj1 strain, heterochromatic silencing was not very stable: after 70 generations, most red colonies turned white, indicating a loss of heterochromatin (Fig. S3C-D). In contrast, in strains with two (2tj1-A, 2tj1-G and 2tj1-E) or three (3tj1-EL) tj1 copies, silencing of the reporter tj1 was more robust, with most cells remaining red after 70 generations. We tested if RNAi is necessary for the maintenance of heterochromatic silencing by deleting ago1 in the red colonies. The red cells in which the ago1 gene was deleted turned white, indicating they lost the ability to silence tj1 (Fig. S3E).

In summary, our data reveal that cells have established RNAi dependent heterochromatin to silence TE. Each additional copy of the TE stabilizes heterochromatin formation as well as heterochromatin maintenance.

Heterochromatin spreading

To determine whether RNAi and heterochromatin act in trans on all copies of tj1, we analyzed H3K9me at the unique sequences inserted along with the different tj1 copies (Fig. 2A). Our findings revealed that in most red colonies, all tj1 copies were silenced by heterochromatin, indicating that RNAi operates in trans; once silencing is initiated at one copy, it spreads to other copies as well (Figs. 2A and S3F). The silencing of all copies was more robust in strains with three tj1 insertions (3tj1) compared to those with two insertions (2tj1), suggesting that each additional copy enhances small RNA–based silencing in trans (Fig. S3F).

One known feature of heterochromatin formation is its ability to spread into neighboring chromatin regions, through the read-and-write mechanism of the H3K9 methyltransferase Clr419. Consistent with this behavior, we observed that heterochromatin had spread to regions adjacent to the tj1 reporter operon in red colonies (Figs. 2B and S4). In all examined strains, heterochromatin formed over the same regions in the vicinity of the reporter locus, even though these strains established silencing independently (Fig. S4). Overall, heterochromatin at the reporter locus covered a region of approximately 30 kb, divided into two domains separated by a 10 kb gap. To understand why this gap region did not establish heterochromatin, we analyzed nucleosome occupancy and found very few nucleosomes in this region, both in wild-type and 1tj1 strains (Fig. S5A). A similar region of low nucleosome occupancy was observed at the gene prl53 (Fig. S5A), which serves as another boundary element for this heterochromatic domain. In addition, we found that highly transcribed genes such as SPAPB15E9.06 also act as boundary elements to prevent further heterochromatin spreading (Fig. 3B, S5A, B). We detected very few Ago1-bound small RNAs from outside the tj1 operon construct, indicating that heterochromatin spreading to neighboring regions outside the initiation site was mostly independent of RNAi (Figs. 2B and S5C).

Next, we analyzed heterochromatin spreading at the other tj1 loci in 2tj1 and 3tj1 strains. In almost all cases, heterochromatin had spread beyond the tj1 locus (Figs. 2C, S6, and S7). The size of these ectopic H3K9me domains varied among the strains, ranging from a few to 50 kb (Figs. S6 and S7). The largest ectopic heterochromatin domain was observed in the strain 3tj1-FM, encompassing more than 120 kb of chromatin and affecting more than 30 genes in total (Figs. 2C and S7). Notably, many protein-coding genes located within the newly formed heterochromatic domains exhibited a 2- to 3-fold reduction in RNA expression, indicating the establishment of functional silencing beyond the initiation event (Figs. 2C, S6, and S7). By averaging the expression of all genes within these heterochromatic domains across all strains, we observed that RNA levels of protein-coding genes were reduced by more than 30% (Fig. 2D).

We investigated whether this spreading of ectopic heterochromatin into euchromatic regions might reduce cellular fitness. In mixed populations, we observed only minor differences in cell growth, indicating that tj1 transposition did not significantly affect the fitness of our strains under laboratory conditions (Fig. S7B). In contrast, red colonies showed a strong reduction in fitness compared to the 1tj1 strain (Fig. S7C). Notably, strain 3tj1-FM, which has the largest heterochromatic domains, exhibited the slowest growth among all strains (Fig. S7C). This reduced fitness was not due to silencing of ura4 or ade6 in the reporter operon, as a strain with these genes inserted into centromeric heterochromatin displayed better fitness (Fig. S7C).

To determine whether heterochromatin can also form on plasmid copies of tj1, we analyzed H3K9me in strains 2tj1-D and 2tj1-G in the presence of plasmids. We found that heterochromatin was established and had spread over the entire plasmid (Fig. S8A).

Our data demonstrate that RNAi and heterochromatin factors recognize newly inserted transposable elements and target them for silencing, which spreads in trans to other copies of the TE and in cis to neighboring chromatin regions.

Sensing of TE by position effect variegation

Next, we examined why certain strains were more effective in recognizing tj1 than others. While most of the strains we isolated required the presence of additional tj1 copies on plasmids to initiate tj1 silencing, strains 2tj1-G and 3tj1-EL were able to do so in the absence of additional copies (Fig. 1C). Both strains had tj1 inserted into tRNA genes located within euchromatic regions but near pericentromeric heterochromatin boundaries (Fig. 1B). The proximity of heterochromatin may have facilitated the recognition and silencing of the newly inserted transposon copy (Fig. 3A). In fact, RNA levels in the total cell population for the selection marker genes inserted together with tj1 were ~2-fold lower when the tj1 transposed into loci near centromeres compared with when it transposed into euchromatic regions (Fig. S8B), indicating that the insertions near centromeres are already being silenced in many cells. This suggests that silencing began at the copy positioned near heterochromatin, then propagated in trans to the other tj1 copies, resulting in stronger repression of tj1 and higher levels of H3K9me than cells with tj1 insertions further away from heterochromatin (Fig. S8C).

Maintenance of silencing was also more stable in the red colonies from strains 2tj1-G and 3tj1-EL: after 70 generations, only a few colonies turned white. Even those white colonies still showed reduced levels of tj1 RNA and high H3K9me levels over the second and third tj1 copies (Fig. S8D–E). We also observed that the tj1 copy near heterochromatin maintained robust H3K9me levels (Fig. S8F, G), likely contributing to the stable maintenance of silencing in trans at euchromatic copies.

Our findings reveal that existing heterochromatic domains play a role in silencing new transposable elements that insert near these regions. Silencing initiated by a copy positioned close to heterochromatin can act in trans, effectively silencing other copies of the invading element.

Sensing of TE by antisense RNA

Our results indicate that the presence of multiple TE copies (such as those in the plasmid) enhances their recognition, even when the genomic insertions are not located near heterochromatin, indicating the existence of an alternative recognition pathway. To determine whether this pathway is driven by DNA copy number or by RNA abundance, we introduced mutations into the plasmid tj1 promoter to reduce its RNA expression, while plasmid copy number remained the same (Fig. 3B). We compared cells from strain 1tj1 carrying the tj1 plasmid (plasmid T1) or mutated tj1 plasmid (plasmid T1M) and found that the latter expressed lower levels of tj1 RNA and showed reduced ability to establish silencing (Fig. 3B). This indicates that RNA abundance, rather than DNA copy number, is crucial for initiating the silencing process.

To better understand how euchromatic tj1 insertions were recognized, we next examined tj1 RNA produced by 2tj1 strains carrying tj1 on plasmids. In the presence of plasmid, strains 2tj1-A and 2tj1-D efficiently recognized and silenced the transposon at frequencies comparable to those with insertions near heterochromatin, while other strains (2tj1-B, C, E, and F) showed poorer recognition (Fig. S1F). Although overall tj1 RNA levels were similar in these groups, strains 2tj1-A and 2tj1-D produced more antisense transcripts from the tj1 element (Figs. 3C and S9A). These findings suggest that the RNAi machinery detects high levels of antisense RNA and targets the corresponding sequence for silencing. In other words, random TE insertions in the genome may generate read-through transcripts antisense to the TE RNA, which are then recognized and processed by small RNA pathways.

We further tested this hypothesis by making different modifications to the parental plasmid expressing tj1 (plasmid T1) (Fig. 3D). First, we removed the downstream LTR promoter (plasmid T2); this modification reduced tj1 antisense RNA levels and consequently diminished transposon recognition, compared to plasmid T1 (Fig. 3D). Alternatively, we replaced the downstream LTR with the thiamine-repressible nmt81 promoter (plasmid T3); this modification did not affect antisense RNA levels substantially and resulted in a modest reduction in transposon silencing, compared to the plasmid T1. In contrast, upon repression of the nmt81 promoter by thiamine, we saw decreased antisense RNA levels, accompanied by reduced transposon silencing. We isolated red colonies obtained with all different plasmids and observed a similar degree of tj1 silencing (Fig. S9B).

Sensing other foreign DNA

Our findings suggest that the recognition of newly invading transposable elements is triggered by disruptions to normal transcription patterns, rather than by specific sequences within the element itself. To test whether transposon recognition is sequence independent, we replaced tj1 in the silencing reporter operon with two protein-coding genes from S. japonicus: pex1 is approximately 3 kb in length and contains an intron, whereas SJAG_02623 is less than 1 kb long and has no introns (Fig. S9C). These features allowed us to assess whether splicing efficiency of foreign genes might serve as a signal for transposon recognition and silencing, as previously proposed13,14,20.

Consistent with our observations for tj1 we observed no silencing for pex1 and SJAG_02623 reporter constructs (no red colonies on low-ade plates) (Fig. 4A, B). To determine if multiple copies of the foreign DNA sequences might initiate recognition and silencing, we transformed these strains with different plasmid constructs expressing pex1 or SJAG_02623 at different levels. Plasmids P1 and S1 are the same as the tj1 plasmid T1, with tj1 replaced with pex1 or SJAG_02623, respectively (Fig. 4A, B). We removed the LTRs to generate plasmids P2 and S2, which thus lacked a promoter and showed reduced RNA expression. For plasmids P3 and S3, we placed the nmt81 promoter on both sides of the foreign gene, thus producing both sense and antisense transcripts.

We introduced the different plasmids into the strains containing the corresponding reporter operon and observed red colonies with all three plasmids (Figs. 4A, B, S9D). Thus, non-TE DNA can also be recognized by the host cell in multi-copy contexts, regardless of intron presence (Fig. 4A, B).

Plasmids P1/S1 and P3/S3 initiated silencing more frequently than plasmids P2/S2 (Fig. 4A, B). These two plasmids produced higher levels of sense and antisense RNA from pex1 and SJAG_02623, compared to plasmids P2/S2. Notably, when we repressed transcription of the nmt81 promoter in plasmids P3/S3 with thiamine, we observed no or very few red colonies, indicating that a threshold level of RNA is essential for initiation of silencing (Fig. 4A, B).

Next, we isolated red colonies and removed the plasmids used to induce initiation of silencing. Analysis of these red colonies revealed reduced pex1 or SJAG_02623 RNA levels relative to white colonies (Fig. S9E). Additionally, we measured H3K9me levels in the red colonies and found a strong increase over the pex1 and SJAG_02623 genes, indicating the formation of heterochromatin domains (Fig. 4C, D)—a pattern similar to that observed in strains with the TE element tj1. Noteworthy, H3K9me levels correlated with amount of RNA transcripts and were highest in cells where silencing was initiated with plasmids P1/S1 and P3/S3.

Sensing endogenous genes

To test whether increased levels of antisense RNA can silence endogenous genes, we expressed sense or antisense ade6 transcripts in 1tj1 cells from plasmids (plasmids A1 and A2). Expression of the antisense transcript was sufficient to initiate silencing of endogenous ade6, although at low frequency, whereas we did not observe silencing with the sense transcript (Fig. 5A). The resulting red colonies exhibited several-fold reductions in ade6 mRNA levels, consistent with robust silencing (Fig. S10A).

To further analyze whether disruption of normal transcription patterns can initiate silencing of endogenous genes in S. pombe, we inserted the nmt81 promoter downstream of the ade6 gene in wild-type cells and isolated 2 different clones; both were able to form red colonies on low adenine plates (Fig. 5B). We performed similar experiments with the endogenous ura4 locus and observed FOA-resistant colonies (Fig. S10B). Analysis of these colonies revealed a 5- to 10-fold reduction in RNA levels for ade6 or ura4, indicating the establishment of silencing (Fig. S10C, D). This is in agreement with our previous work, in which deletion of the exosome and accumulation of antisense transcripts triggered ectopic RNAi at many endogenous S. pombe genes21.

In summary, our data show that perturbation of normal transcription patterns and increased antisense RNA levels—such as those that may result from transposon insertion—promote recognition and silencing of the perturbed genes.

Discussion

Our observations reveal that de novo recognition of TEs in S. pombe relies on mechanisms distinct from those used to maintain silencing of domesticated TE tf2. In fission yeast, tf2 is silenced by the CENP-B family of DNA-binding proteins11, whereas our data show that the newly invading transposon tj1 is silenced by RNAi and heterochromatin. It is plausible that tf2 may have been initially targeted for silencing by RNAi, a notion supported by the fact that in the absence of factors such as the exosome subunit rrp6, tf2 becomes targeted by RNAi21–23. Thus, the RNAi pathway retains the capacity to silence tf2. Our results indicate that the RNAi pathway provides an immediate and flexible defense against newly invading TEs, whereas the evolution of a high-affinity DNA-binding protein for a new sequence requires much longer timescale.

How does a cell distinguish a newly introduced TE from its own genes? To differentiate self from non-self, the host’s defense systems need to detect molecular patterns that are disrupted by TE invasion. Our model suggests that random TE insertions can lead to read-through transcription from endogenous promoters, producing antisense transcripts that extend into the newly inserted TE sequence. As more TEs accumulate, the frequency of such read-through events and levels of antisense transcripts increase, thereby enhancing the likelihood of recognition by small RNA pathways. In support of this model, our data show that higher TE copy numbers promote its recognition, and increased levels of antisense RNA correlate with more efficient TE recognition.

We also found that recognition of foreign DNA can occur independently of specific sequences present in TE, as two distinct foreign genes were successfully targeted for silencing through heterochromatin formation. The efficiency of this recognition correlated with the abundance of sense and antisense RNA: strains producing higher levels of these transcripts were more effective at detecting and silencing foreign DNA. These antisense transcripts can be processed into primary small RNAs, which then recruit RNAi machinery to produce secondary small RNAs, amplifying the silencing response22,24. Small RNA processing and amplification are reminiscent of the piRNA pathway in the germline of diverse animal species, where primary piRNAs trigger a feedback loop to generate secondary piRNAs, resulting in robust and heritable silencing of TEs1,4,25.

Our findings reveal that, beyond detecting changes in transcription patterns, another mechanism drives the recognition of transposons: insertions near heterochromatic domains. Although transposons typically do not integrate into silent heterochromatin, we observed that tj1 sometimes inserted close to these regions, disrupting heterochromatin boundaries. Such insertions are recognized more efficiently and establish more stable silencing, likely due to stochastic heterochromatic variegation. Once initiated, silencing at a heterochromatin-adjacent copy also acts in trans, silencing other copies of the invading gene and ultimately creating a sequence-specific recognition process for the TE.

By rapidly silencing new TEs without requiring prior genetic memory, RNAi and heterochromatin safeguard the cell against potentially harmful effects on fitness. However, because heterochromatin can spread from its initiation site into neighboring regions, it must be tightly regulated by boundary elements. We found that newly formed ectopic heterochromatin domains over TE DNA could extend up to 100 kb, repressing multiple protein-coding genes. This widespread silencing negatively impacted cell growth, ironically favoring cells where the TE remained active, thereby promoting further transposon propagation. Our results thus show that while RNAi and heterochromatin are flexible and powerful mechanisms for TE recognition and silencing, they pose control challenges for the cell. This may explain why initial RNAi-based silencing may eventually be replaced by sequence-specific DNA-binding proteins or alternative pathways.

Overall, our data demonstrate that small RNA-based mechanisms can identify entirely novel foreign DNA elements, recognizing them through disruptions in host transcriptional patterns and/or chromatin organization. Given that small RNA pathways are conserved in mammalian germline cells—where TE activity poses the greatest risk—our findings suggest these mechanisms may also contribute to defense against newly invading transposons in mammals.

Methods

Strain construction and plasmid generation

All S. pombe strains, plasmids, and oligonucleotides used in this study are listed in Tables S2, S3 and S4, respectively. The transposon silencing reporter construct was integrated into the S. pombe genome by a PCR-based tf2-5 replacement. Positive transformants were selected on YES plates containing 100–200 mg/L antibiotics and verified by PCR and sequencing. 3xFLAG tagging of Ago1 and Cbp1 was performed by integrating the epitope at N- and C- terminal, respectively. Plasmids were generated by inverse PCR, restriction enzyme-based cloning, and LIC (Ligation Independent Cloning). In all cases, XL1 Blue E.coli cells were heat-shock transformed with the generated plasmids, which were subsequently amplified and stored after positive screening by PCR and sequencing. The LIC protocol was performed as described previously26. Strains containing plasmids listed in Table S5 were grown either on PMG (Pombe Minimal Glutamate) or EMM (Edinburgh Minimal Medium) depleted of the specific supplement(s), according to the plasmids used. All PCR reactions for cloning were performed using the High-fidelity PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and all PCR products and plasmid were purified using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-Up kit (Macherey-Nagel).

To disrupt endogenous transcription patterns, a reverse-oriented nmt81 promoter was inserted at the downstream of either the ade6 or ura4 gene in wild-type S. pombe. The nmt81 promoter was placed in the antisense orientation relative to the endogenous gene to drive antisense transcription. A neomycin (neo) resistance cassette, oriented in the sense direction, was included in the construct for selection. Integration cassettes for ade6 and ura4 were generated and introduced to S. pombe cells using the same strategy as described above for transposon silencing reporter construct.

Silencing establishment assays

For the silencing establishment assay, cells were grown overnight in the appropriate PMG media (according to the plasmids present). Cultures of exponentially growing cells were refreshed daily to keep them growing exponentially. A total of 30,000 cells were plated in PMG low ade media (using 15 cm diameter plates, Thermo Fisher) on days 1, 3, 5, 7 and 10 (corresponding to 10, 30, 50, 70 and 100 generations). The plates were kept at 32 °C for 3 days, followed by an overnight incubation at 4 °C to strengthen the red pigmentation of silencing colonies. The number of red colonies in each strain at each timepoint was counted.

For the strains with the nmt81 promoter inserted downstream of the ade6 gene, silencing was assessed by the appearance of red colonies on low adenine plates, as previously described. Two independent clones were selected for silencing establishment assays and RNA level analyses.

Silencing of ura4 was assessed by resistance to 5-fluoroorotic acid (5-FOA). Exponentially growing cells were plated at a density of approximately 30, 000 cells per 15 cm diameter plates onto EMM medium supplemented with 5-FOA (1 g/L) and incubated at 32 °C. Two independent ura4-nmt81 promoter clones were subjected to silencing assays, each repeated four times. Colonies surviving on 5-FOA plates were isolated after 5 days of incubation. To ensure that survival reflected transcriptional silencing rather than genetic mutation, all 5-FOA-resistant colonies were analyzed by quantitative PCR, showing that ura4 sense RNA levels were reduced to below 15% of those in wild-type cells.

Transposition induction and isolation of colonies with transposed tj1

To induce the first tj1 transposition, the strain 1tj1 was transformed with the combination of p1265 and p1081 (donor and expression plasmids, respectively). The donor plasmid contains the neo cassette oriented in the opposite direction to tj1. The neo cassette is interrupted by an artificial intron (AI) in frame with tj1. This construct allows G418-resistant cells to arise only when a tj1 integration cycle is completed27,28. Cells were grown exponentially for 2–3 days in PMG with the specific supplements (according to the plasmids present) to induce tj1 transposition. Afterwards, cells were switched to YES media and grown exponentially for 2–3 days to induce plasmid loss. A total of 25 × 106 cells were plated on YES + G418 + nourseothricin and incubated at 32 °C until single colonies appeared. The resistant colonies were replica-plated on EMMC without the specific supplements to identify antibiotic-resistant colonies that did not retain the plasmids, indicative of a genomic transposition. To induce a second tj1 transposition, all the strains with one transposition were re-transformed with the combination of p1281 and p1081 (donor and expression plasmids, respectively). p1281 contains the hph cassette with the artificial intron. The same procedure was followed: initially, the transposition was induced by growing cells in minimal media; afterwards, cells were switched to YES and 100 × 106 cells were plated on YES + G418 +nourseothricin +hygromycin B. The antibiotic-resistant colonies were replica-plated on EMMC w/o the specific supplements to identify antibiotic-resistant colonies that did not retain the plasmids, indicative of a second genomic transposition. Tj1 transposition(s) in the selected colonies were confirmed and mapped on the genome by Illumina and/or nanopore sequencing.

Silencing maintenance assay

Red colonies were selected from the establishment assays and subsequently grown exponentially for 2 days in YES to induce loss of any plasmids still present. One hundred cells were plated on YE and replica-plated in the adequate EMM media to screen for plasmid loss. Red colonies in YE that did not grow on the EMM replicas were selected as silenced and plasmid depleted. These colonies were propagated in PMG low ade until a stable red pigmentation was obtained, corresponding to at least 75% of red colonies among all the colonies. Starting from a stably silenced colony, cells were grown exponentially in YES up to 7 days and ~1000 cells were plated on PMG low ade plate on days 1, 3 and 7 (corresponding to 10, 30 and 70 cell generations). The number of red colonies on each plate was counted, and the percentage was calculated. For each strain, the assay was performed in two biological replicates.

Growth curve and competition assays

To investigate cellular fitness, growth curves of the investigated strains were measured. Cells were incubated at 32 °C overnight in YES liquid media so that they were exponentially growing the following morning. The OD600 was measured and all the cultures were diluted to OD600 = 0.2, incubated for 30 min and the OD600 was measured again. This measurement was taken as the initial OD600 and normalized to 0.2. The OD600 was then measured again after 2, 4 and 6 h to generate the growth curves. To investigate the growth competition between the 1tj1 strain and the strains with transpositions growing in the same culture, each strain was incubated overnight in liquid YES. Exponentially growing cells with the same OD600 were mixed 1:1 the following day (day 0) and kept growing exponentially up to 9 days (~90 generations). Approximately 1000 cells were plated in YES at days 0, 4, and 9, incubated for 3 days at 32 °C, and replica-plated either on YES + nourseothricin + G418 (in the competition assay between 1tj1 and 2tj1 strains) or on YES + nourseothricin + G418 + hygromycin B (in the competition assay between 1tj1 and 3tj1 strains). The number of colonies growing on the antibiotic plates, compared to the corresponding YES plates, was used to calculate the percentage of 1tj1 and strains with transpositions in the same culture.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation and sequencing

Fifty mL of mid-log phase S. pombe cultures were cross-linked using formaldehyde (final concentration 1%) for 15 minutes at room temperature21,22,29,30. Afterwards, the cross-linking was quenched with 125 mM glycine and incubating for 5 minutes. Cells were pelleted and resuspended in lysis buffer (supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and complete EDTA free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, Roche) at a 1:1 ratio with the pellet volume (~300 μL). The lysis buffer contained 250 mM KCl, 1x Triton-X, 0.1% SDS, 0.1% Na-deoxycholate, 50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 2 mM EDTA, 2 mM EGTA, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1% Nonidet P-40, 20% Glycerol. Cell lysis was performed adding glass beads to the cells and bead beating the samples (BioSpec FastPrep-24 bead beater, MP-Biomedicals) for 8 cycles at 6.5 m/s for 30 s followed by 3 min on ice. DNA was sheared by sonication (Bioruptor, Diagenode) 35 times for 30 s with a 30 s break. Cell debris was eliminated by collecting the supernatant after a centrifugation at 13,000 g for 15 min. The crude lysate obtained was normalized by measuring the RNA concentration (Nanodrop, Thermo Fischer Scientific). For each reaction, 5 μL of magnetic resin (Dynabeads Protein A, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was coupled with 1.2 μg of antibody either against H3K9me2 (Abcam 1220) or H3 (Abcam 176842), according to the assay performed, and incubated with the cell lysate overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. For Cbp1-ChIP, 30 μL of FLAG-M2 agarose resin (Sigma-Aldrich A2220) were incubated with the crude lysate, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with gentle rotation.

The IP was washed five times with 1 ml of lysate buffer and eluted with 150 μL of elution buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS) at 65 °C for 15 min. RNA and the proteins were removed from the IP by adding 2 μl of RNase A (Thermo Fischer Scientific) and Proteinase K (Roche) and incubating at 65 °C for at least 5 h. Finally, DNA was purified using phenol-chloroform-isoamylalcohol (25:24:1, Roth) and recovered by ethanol precipitation. For Illumina sequencing, a ChIP-seq library was prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina kit (NEB), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Single-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina GAIIX sequencer at the LAFUGA core facility of the Gene Center, Munich. The Galaxy platform was used to demultiplex the obtained reads. ChIP-qPCR was performed using DyNAmo Flash SYBR Green qPCR kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a QuantStudio 3 real-time PCR instrument (Life Technologies). Enrichment was calculated as percentage of the input. The oligonucleotides primer sequence used for PCR amplification is listed in Table S4.

Ago1-bound sRNA sequencing

The purification and sequencing of Ago1-bound sRNAs were performed as previously described21,22,24. Briefly, strains with the endogenously Ago1 3xFLAG tagged were grown in YES, and 2.5 L of log-phase cells were collected. Cells were resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 1.5 M NaOAc, 5 mM MgCl2, 2 mM EDTA pH 8, 2 mM EGTA pH 8, 0.1% Nonidet P-40, 20% glycerol) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and complete EDTA free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche), maintaining a 1:1 ratio between cell volume and buffer. Cells were lysed using glass beads and bead beater as described for the ChIP protocol. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 13,000 g, and the crude lysate was incubated with 30 μL of FLAG-M2 agarose resin (Sigma-Aldrich A2220) at 4 °C for 2 h with gentle rotation. The IP was washed five times with lysis buffer, and Ago1 was eluted from the resin by mixing 1% SDS with 300 mM NaOAc and phenol-chloroform-isoamylalcohol (25:24:1, Roth). The sRNAs were concentrated by ethanol precipitation, run on an 18% polyacrylamide-urea gel together with 22-, 26- and 30-nt RNA markers, and the bands between 20 and 30 nt were excised from the gel.

A 3’ linker (5’ rAppCTGTAGGCACCATCAAT–NH2 3’, NEB, reaction concentration 2 μM) was ligated to the sRNAs. The 3’ ligated products were purified on an 18% acrylamide-urea gel, followed byphenol-chloroform extraction and ethanol purification. The 5’ adaptor (5’- ACUCUUUCCCUACACGACGCUCUUCCGAUCU −3’, Metabion) was then ligated, followed by reverse transcription (RT primer: 5’- GTGACTGGAGTTCAGACGTGTGCTCTTCCGATCGATTGATGGTGCCTACAG −3’,

Metabion). The cDNA was amplified with 12-20 PCR cycles using the Phusion hot start II DNA polymerase kit (Thermo Fisher) and the Illumina P5 5’ primer (5’ -AATGATACGGCGACCACCGAGATCTACACTCTTTCCCTACACGACG −3’) together with the Illumina P7 3’ primer with inserted barcode (5’-CAAGCAGAAGACGGCATACGAGATXXXXXXGTGACTGGAGTTCAGACGTG −3’). The sRNA library was purified from an 8% polyacrylamide gel, ethanol precipitated, and sequenced at LAFUGA facility as described for the ChIP sequencing.

Genomic DNA purification

Genomic DNA purification was performed using different methods depending on the downstream application. For tj1, pex1, SJAG_02623, and ade6 copy number quantification via qPCR, 50 mL of mid-log phase cells grown in the adequate liquid media were collected. The pellet was resuspended in 200 μL of ChIP lysis buffer, glass beads were added, and cells were lysed with 5 cycles of bead beating as described in the ChIP protocol. 50 μL of lysate was diluted with 450 μL of ddH2O and treated with RNase A. DNA was recovered with phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol extraction (25:24:1, Roth) followed by ethanol precipitation. The precipitated DNA was resuspended in 1 x TE buffer at a concentration of 5 ng/μL and analyzed by qPCR.

Genomic DNA purification for tj1 transposition mapping via Illumina sequencing was performed similarly to the procedure described above, with the main difference that the cell lysate was sheared by sonication and the crude lysate was separated to cell debris by centrifugation. RNA was degraded using RNase A, and DNA was recovered by phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol extraction and ethanol precipitation. The DNA was finally resuspended in 1X TE buffer and used for library preparation with the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB), following the manufacturer’s instructions. When tj1 transposed close to repetitive genomic sequences, like pericentromeric regions (strains 2tj1-G and 3tj1-EL), genomes were sequenced by Nanopore sequencing (Nanopore-seq) to precisely map the transposition sites. To obtain high-quality DNA, the protocol described by Tusso and colleagues was followed31.

Total RNA purification; total RNA and poly(A) RNA sequencing

Total RNA purification was performed by collecting 2 mL of mid-log phase cells grown in the adequate media22,30. Cells were mixed with 500 μL of lysis buffer (300 mM NaOAc pH 5.2, 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS) and 500 μL of phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol and lysed at 65 °C for 10 min. RNA was purified with ethanol precipitation, resuspended in DEPC-treated ddH2O, and residual DNA was removed with DNase I (Thermo Fisher). EDTA was added to a final concentration of 5 mM, and DNase I was heat-inactivated at 75 °C for 10 min. The total purified RNA obtained was used to prepare either total RNA or poly(A) RNA libraries using the NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina, following the manufacturer’s instructions (skipping the poly(A)-RNA enrichment step when preparing total RNA libraries). When total purified RNA was used for RT-qPCR experiments, reverse transcription was performed instead.

Total RNA reverse transcription (RT)

Approximately 300 ng of total RNA were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase kit (Thermo Fisher), following the manufacturer’s instructions and adding the primer required for RT (Table S4) to the reaction mix. As -RT controls, the same amount of RNA was incubated with water instead of reverse transcriptase to later exclude DNA contamination.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR)

qPCR was performed either on cDNA from reverse-transcribed RNA (and -RT controls) to analyze the differential gene expression among strains or on purified genomic DNA to quantify the copy number of tj1, pex1, SJAG_02623 or ade6. The 2x DyNAmo Flash SyBR Green Master Mix qPCR kit (BioZym) was used to prepare 10 μL reactions containing ~4 ng of template DNA or cDNA and 0.4 μM of each specific forward and reverse primer (Table S4). Each reaction was assembled in a 96-well plate (4titude) and performed in triplicate with a defined qPCR procedure on a QuantStudio 3 real-time PCR instrument. For copy number quantification of tj1, pex1, SJAG_02623, or ade6, primers specific to these genes were used, and the copy number was calculated by normalizing to the single-copy genes act1 or tdh1. When transcription levels were analyzed, act1 or tdh1 RNA was used to normalize the input material.

Analysis of sequencing data

Demultiplexed Illumina reads were mapped to the S. pombe genome using Novoalign (htttp://www.novocraft.com), allowing 2 nucleotides mismatch to the reference. The genome sequence and annotation available from the S. pombe Genome Project were used32. When genomic manipulations were performed and when tj1 integrated into the genome, the corresponding nucleotide sequences were manually added to the reference genome. Reads mapping to multiple locations were randomly assigned. The data were displayed using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV)33. Total RNA and RNA-poly(A) sequencing libraries were normalized to the total number of reads mapping to protein coding sequence (CDS). H3K9me2 ChIP-seq data were normalized to background regions consisting of two H3K9me2-depleted regions of 200,000 bp each (chr I: 80,000–280,000 and chr I: 1,780,000–1,980,000), with the only exclusion of H3K9me2 ChIP-seq from plasmid-containing strains, which were normalized to reads per one million (r.p.m). Ago1-bound sRNA, Cbp1 ChIP, H3 ChIP, and genomic DNA sequencing datasets were normalized to r.p.m.
