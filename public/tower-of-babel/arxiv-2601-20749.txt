Learning to Live with AI: How Students Develop AI Literacy Through Naturalistic ChatGPT Interaction Journal: Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence
Tawfiq Ammari, Meilun Chen, S M Mehedi Zaman, Kiran Garimella

Abstract
How do students develop AI literacy through everyday practice rather than formal instruction? While normative AI literacy frameworks proliferate, empirical understanding of how students actually learn to work with generative AI remains limited. This study analyzes 10,536 ChatGPT messages from 36 undergraduates over one academic year, revealing five use genres—academic workhorse, emotional companion, metacognitive partner, repair and negotiation, and trust calibration—that constitute distinct configurations of student-AI learning. Drawing on domestication theory and emerging frameworks for AI literacy, we demonstrate that functional AI competence emerges through ongoing relational negotiation rather than one-time adoption. Students develop sophisticated genre portfolios, strategically matching interaction patterns to learning needs while exercising critical judgment about AI limitations. Notably, repair work during AI breakdowns produces substantial learning about AI capabilities, developing what we term “repair literacy”—a crucial but underexplored dimension of AI competence. Our findings offer educators empirically grounded insights into how students actually learn to work with generative AI, with implications for AI literacy pedagogy, responsible AI integration, and the design of AI-enabled learning environments that support student agency.

1
Introduction

The rapid integration of generative AI into educational contexts has created urgent questions about how students develop the competencies needed to work productively with these systems. While AI literacy frameworks have been conceptually developed
(
Ng et al. 2021
;
Long and Magerko 2020
)
, and intervention-based evaluations conducted
(
Laupichler et al. 2022
)
, empirical understanding of how students actually develop AI competence through naturalistic, everyday practice remains limited. This gap is particularly consequential given evidence that ChatGPT can enhance cognitive skills including critical thinking, creative thinking, and reflective thinking when appropriately integrated into learning activities
(
Essel et al. 2024
)
.

Existing AI literacy models emphasize four core competencies: knowing and understanding AI, using and applying AI, evaluating and creating AI, and AI ethics
(
Ng et al. 2021
)
.
Carolus et al. 2023
extend these competencies in their Digital Interaction Literacy Model to include
understanding functional principles
(how AI processes data),
mindful usage
(reflective anthropomorphic persuasion), and
user-group dependent factors
(distinct competencies for educators guiding others’ AI use) in their Digital Interaction Literacy Model. However, these frameworks remain largely normative—describing what students
should
know rather than examining how such knowledge actually develops through practice. Moreover, research on AI assistance suggests concerning trade-offs: while AI can support learning, students may “rely on rather than learn from” AI systems, potentially compromising their agency and self-regulation capacities
(
Darvishi et al. 2024
)
.

This tension between AI’s educational potential and its risks to student agency motivates our investigation. Rather than viewing ChatGPT as a static productivity tool, we examine how students actively construct what
Bakardjieva 2005
calls “use genres”—emergent interaction patterns that socially shape technology through everyday practice. This perspective positions students as active consumers who domesticate technology “into the distinct cultures” of academic life
(
Silverstone et al. 1994
, p. 15)
.

Our analysis shows that students construct fluid, context-specific repertoires that cast ChatGPT as academic workhorse, debugging partner, emotional companion, metacognitive facilitator, and system requiring repair

through dialogue. These use genres
(
Bakardjieva 2005
)
emerge through domestication
(
Silverstone et al. 1994
)
, as students integrate ChatGPT into their academic toolkit, build interface competencies, embed interactions into temporal and spatial rhythms of study, and negotiate their social acceptability within academic communities.

By analyzing 10,536 actual interaction messages rather than self-reported behaviors, this study addresses calls for empirical research examining “AI in situated action”
(
Jarrahi 2025
)
—the emergent behaviors arising through actual use rather than controlled experiments. Our findings contribute to understanding how AI literacy develops as sociomaterial accomplishment, revealing the micro-practices through which students learn to work productively with generative AI while maintaining critical judgment about its limitations.

2
Related Work

2.1
AI Literacy Frameworks

Recent scholarship has developed comprehensive frameworks for conceptualizing AI literacy.
Ng et al. 2021
propose four interrelated competencies: (1) knowing and understanding AI concepts, (2) using and applying AI technologies, (3) evaluating and creating AI applications, and (4) understanding AI ethics. This framework emphasizes that AI literacy extends beyond technical skills to encompass critical evaluation and ethical reasoning.
Carolus et al. 2023
developed the Digital Interaction Literacy Model specifically for voice-based AI, identifying ten competencies across three dimensions: understanding functional principles, mindful usage, and user-group dependent competencies. Their model addresses how anthropomorphic features of conversational AI create unique literacy requirements.

However,
Domínguez Figaredo and Stoyanovich 2023
argue that most AI literacy initiatives remain narrowly focused on programming fundamentals, neglecting the social and ethical dimensions of human-AI interaction. They advocate distinguishing between “technical” and “non-technical” learning pathways, suggesting that effective AI literacy education must address diverse stakeholder needs. Evaluation studies show promising results for knowledge gains
(
Laupichler et al. 2022
)
, yet questions remain about whether formal instruction translates into practical competence.

2.2
ChatGPT’s Educational Impacts

Emerging research demonstrates ChatGPT’s potential to enhance cognitive skills.
Essel et al. 2024
conducted an experimental study showing students using ChatGPT for in-class tasks exhibited significant improvements in critical thinking, creative thinking, and reflective thinking compared to control groups. They argue that ChatGPT “furnishes students with the possibility to engage in dialogues with an AI model that prompts them to think critically” while providing personalized learning experiences.

Research on student perceptions reveals generally positive attitudes toward ChatGPT adoption.
Albayati 2024
found that perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and social influence significantly affect undergraduate students’ acceptance of ChatGPT. These findings suggest that successful domestication depends on both usability and perceived value.
Okonkwo and Ade-Ibijola 2021
conducted a systematic review of chatbot applications in education, identifying benefits including personalized learning and immediate feedback, alongside challenges such as lack of intelligence and ethical concerns.

Beyond cognitive and perceptual dimensions, understanding human-AI interaction requires attention to the algorithmic imaginaries that shape students’ expectations.
Cohen et al. 2024
demonstrate how public discourse about ChatGPT rapidly shifted from functional enthusiasm to ethical critique, creating a cultural backdrop against which students navigate their AI use. Within this context, trust dynamics become central—
Shin et al. 2024
shows that users continuously calibrate between heuristic acceptance and systematic scrutiny of AI outputs, a process particularly salient in education where students must balance AI reliance with academic integrity and learning goals.

The relational dimensions of human-AI interaction also warrant attention. Research on parasocial relationships—originally theorized in television viewing—extends to chatbots and virtual assistants, showing how users form emotional attachments to these systems
(
Maeda and Quan-Haase 2024
)
. This perspective helps interpret students’ anthropomorphic language and emotional expressions toward ChatGPT as genuine relational positioning rather than mere metaphor, illuminating the affective dimensions of educational AI use.

2.3
Self-Regulated Learning and Student Agency

The integration of AI into education raises important questions about student agency.
Darvishi et al. 2024
conducted a randomized experiment examining AI assistance’s impact on student agency in peer feedback contexts. Their findings reveal that “students tended to rely on rather than learn from AI assistance,” suggesting potential dependencies undermining long-term learning.

Research demonstrates that the relationship between AI assistance and student agency depends critically on implementation design.
Ingkavara et al. 2022
show that personalized learning approaches can support self-regulated learning when properly designed, with students receiving personalized feedback showing higher achievement and positive attitudes. Given this tight, contextualized connection between user and machine,
Carvalho et al. 2022
argue for participatory design approaches that involve students in shaping AI-enabled learning environments. Central to understanding these configurations is data sense-making—the embodied, contextual process through which people interpret and incorporate digital data into their lives
(
Lupton 2018
)
. This aligns with science and technology studies traditions recognizing that artifacts become "working technology" only after users establish particular interpretations of what problems they solve
(
Pinch and Bijker 1984
)
, and that properties of human and technical actors emerge through "mutual translation"—inscribing properties and prescribing behaviors
(
Latour 1993
)
. This articulation work, connecting AI-generated representations with embodied knowledge until information "feels right"
(
Lupton 2018
)
, manifests as students integrate ChatGPT responses with disciplinary understanding, course requirements, and learning goals—a process of active interpretive labor that simultaneously shapes both user and technology
(
Schulz-Schaeffer 2025
)
.

2.3.1
Human-AI Co-Regulation and Breakdown Repair

Lupton 2018
emphasizes that human-data assemblages undergo continuous processes of composition, decomposition, and recomposition—data can lose vitality, becoming “dead” when they fail to generate insight, yet may be reanimated in different contexts. This framework illuminates breakdown moments in human-AI interaction, which recent research identifies as sites of particular analytical significance
(
Ashktorab et al. 2019
)
. When ChatGPT produces inadequate responses, students engage in what
Ashktorab et al. 2019
term “resilient” interaction practices—repair strategies including rephrasing, providing additional context, and adjusting interaction approaches. The notion of data as subject to “decay” yet open to “repair”
(
Lupton 2018
)
thus parallels observations in educational settings: responses initially seeming unhelpful may later prove valuable, and abandoned interaction patterns may be reworked into new ones.

The repair work students undertake when ChatGPT fails reflects what
Jarrahi 2025
term “exploratory familiarization”—probing system capabilities through trial and error to establish baseline understanding of responses and limitations. This “boundary testing” develops familiarity with behavioral patterns that are not algorithmically predetermined but “emergent in use,” arising from interaction rather than documentation
(
Jarrahi 2025
)
. Unlike designed technologies that function as strict “role-takers” executing prescribed sequences, generative AI systems operate as “role-makers”—interpreting assigned roles situationally based on learned patterns resembling experiential knowledge
(
Schulz-Schaeffer 2025
)
. This co-constitutive dynamic exemplifies what
Holstein et al. 2019
term “human-AI complementarity”: collaborative relationships where human and AI actors adapt behaviors in response to each other’s capabilities and limitations through ongoing co-regulation. The result is what
Jarrahi 2025
call “artificial communication”—ongoing interpretive work where accumulated knowledge shapes subsequent interactions in continuous feedback loops.

2.4
Responsible AI in Education

Fu and Weng 2024
conducted a systematic review identifying five characteristics of responsible human-centered AI in education: Fairness and Equity, Privacy and Security, Non-maleficence and Beneficence, Agency and Autonomy, and Transparency and Intelligibility. They emphasize that responsible AI requires collaborative stakeholder efforts.
Ma et al. 2025
argue that responsible AI involves navigating tensions between educational benefits and potential risks to student autonomy, highlighting the importance of participatory approaches involving students in discussions about AI ethics.

Scholarship on self-directed and self-regulated learning provides essential foundations for understanding student agency in AI-mediated contexts.
Garrison 1997
emphasizes students’ agency in goal-setting, strategy use, and metacognitive monitoring—skills that appear central to effective AI engagement.
Zhai et al. 2024
shows that AI literacy—distinct from general digital literacy—predicts more sophisticated adoption patterns, with knowledgeable students leveraging AI for academic support rather than task displacement. Similarly,
Pangrazio and Selwyn 2019
’s notion of “personal data literacies” emphasizes critical capacities such as identification, reflexivity, and tactical use, suggesting that effective AI literacy entails both technical fluency and critical reflection on the data exchanged with AI systems.

2.5
Domestication Theory and Use Genres

Technology engagement is not a binary state of use or non-use, but a complex spectrum defined by the user’s strategic negotiations. Drawing on Domestication theory
(
Silverstone et al. 1994
)
, we can view this process as one of “partial domestication” or “selective use.” Here, users engage in what
Salovaara et al. 2011
term “appropriation work” repurposing or restricting devices (e.g., limiting notifications to prevent intrusion).

This active shaping of technology mirrors
Sørensen 2006
’s view of disciplining artifacts and
Bakardjieva 2005
’s concept of “use genres,” where interaction patterns emerge from practical necessity rather than design. As users discipline these artifacts, they engage in a continuous process of

“becoming-with” the technology
(
Lupton 2018
)
, where the user and the technology co-constitute each other through ongoing, iterative interaction. This study examines the iterative interactions between students and ChatGPT as students appropriate the technology into their "moral economy," the values, norms and ethical views that shape the way students adopt and integrate technology into their daily lives
(
Silverstone 2006
, P.236)
.

3
Dataset and Methods

Our methodological approach draws on
Jarrahi 2025
’s framework for “interviewing AI,” which grounds analysis in how users develop knowledge about AI systems through direct engagement—learning through trial and error where systems excel and struggle. By studying their interaction logs, we capture what
Pink et al. 2017
characterize as the mundane routines and contingencies of digital living. To do so, we applied a multi-stage annotation pipeline combining manual qualitative coding and automated labeling. First, we adopted a
sequential
exploratory
strategy
, starting with an initial phase of qualitative analysis which produced a codebook followed by a second phase of quantitative analysis that used the codebook to label the rest of the dataset
(
Creswell and Creswell 2017
, P.194-195)
. We then engaged in a
sequential
explanatory
strategy
to interpret the results—analyzing samples of the now labeled data to explain the initial quantitative findings in more detail (ibid). Figure
1
summarizes our overall methodology.

This study draws on a year-long dataset of undergraduate interactions with ChatGPT, collected as part of a broader project on AI literacy and self-directed learning. The dataset consists of complete chat histories from undergraduate students at a research university in the northeastern United States. Following Institutional Review Board approval, we recruited participants through campus flyers and compensated them $10 for participation. We collected data from a total of 36 participants.

3.1
Data Collection and Anonymization

Participants exported their entire ChatGPT history from
chat.openai.com
and uploaded the resulting zip file to our secure server. These logs included all prior conversations from their personal accounts. While the data did not directly contain personal identifiers such as emails or phone numbers, conversational content could reveal sensitive information. To protect participants, we implemented a rigorous anonymization process. Identifiable details were removed, and we did not collect demographic metadata, preventing any linkage between conversations and individual students.

The dataset is structured to include: (1) user ID, (2) session title, (3) session identifier, (4) log timestamp, and (5) log text (prompt or message).

3.2
Scope and Coverage

The final corpus spans December 2022 through January 2024, encompassing 1,631 unique chats and 10,536 conversational prompts (hereafter referred to as messages). On average, students contributed 45 sessions (SD=66), with mean session duration of 13.2 minutes. Usage patterns followed the academic calendar: activity decreased during spring break (March), summer 2023, and winter holidays, suggesting that much of the use was tied to academic work. Figure

3.3
Phase I: Sequential Exploratory Qualitative Analysis

Following
Corbin and Strauss 1990
’s approach to grounded theory, two researchers independently performed open and axial coding on a sample of 1,882 messages, identifying initial concepts and relationships between categories while iteratively refining our schema. Messages were coded at the turn level, but analytic attention spanned user–AI exchanges to preserve interactional continuity
(
Braun and Clarke 2006
)
. Through this process, we developed a codebook with five primary categories:
Information Seeking, Content Generation, Language Use, Student–ChatGPT Interaction, and ChatGPT Response Behavior
, encompassing 41 sub-categories capturing nuanced practices such as role-play dialogues, stylistic adjustments, and expressions of frustration.
Our dataset captures how students’ ChatGPT practices evolve across academic semesters, revealing domestication as a process.

3.4
Phase II: Sequential Exploratory Computational Analysis through Scaling Annotation

Given the dataset’s size (10.5k messages), we then scaled analysis using GPT-based annotation. Building on approaches to large-scale labeling with minimal annotations
(
Chae and Davidson 2025
)
, we employed OpenAI’s GPT-4o model (version: gpt-4o-2024-05-13, accessed July 2024) with temperature set to 0.0 to maximize consistency. We used a zero-shot prompt using our human-developed codebook:

"The following are codes for qualitative analysis. You need to categorize the texts in inputs as one or multiple codes from the following list and also reply with your chain of thought (COT) for the selection(s)."
"Reply with **only the label in single quotation**, and then include COT in the next line without quotation. Remember, you are bound by only 1 label to reply.’’

For each message, the model returned a top-level category and a chain-of-thought rationale, supporting transparency, interpretability, and improved decision-making by the LLM
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
Lee et al. 2024
)
.

3.5
Phase III: Sequential Explanatory Analysis

To evaluate the reliability of the scaled labeling, we randomly sampled 100 messages per category and compared GPT labels with human judgments. Agreement was substantial to near-perfect across categories, with Cohen’s Kappa values ranging from 0.75 to 0.91
(
McHugh 2012
)
. As presented in Table
2
, these metrics provided confidence that automated annotation could extend our qualitative insights while preserving fidelity to the schema.

A final phase of axial coding using the same samples facilitated the generation of themes presented in the findings
(
Creswell and Creswell 2017
, P.195)
. In this phase, we adopted an emergent theory approach, which traditionally seeks to understand "why people behave or think as they do"
(
Jaccard and Jacoby 2019
, p.269)
. However, following
Jarrahi 2025
.’s methodological framework for treating AI as a qualitative research subject, we extended this inquiry to encompass both human and machine agency—asking not only why students act and think as they do, but also why ChatGPT responds and "reasons" as it does. This dual focus aligns with perspectives that position generative AI as an interlocutor worthy of systematic qualitative examination. Unlike grounded theory’s emphasis on bracketing prior knowledge, emergent theory permitted pre-existing theoretical frameworks to surface organically throughout our data analysis process.

3.6
Rationale, Limitations and Future Work

This data collection strategy balanced analysis depth and privacy. By requesting complete exported histories, we obtained naturalistic records of engagement across academic and personal contexts. At the same time, anonymization ensured that even when students disclosed personal matters, no traceable link remained. The structured schema and labeling pipeline further allowed us to examine dynamics within and across sessions, situating individual uses within semester rhythms.

However, our dataset has limitations. The modest sample size (36 students) from a single university constrains generalizability while the lack of demographic data prevents subgroup analysis. Additionally, relying on manual export may bias the sample toward motivated students. Our focus on ChatGPT interactions alone limits understanding of how students manage relationships across multiple AI systems and platforms.

Future research should examine
cross-platform domestication
as students navigate diverse algorithmic environments with different affordances and limitations. Longitudinal studies tracking genre evolution over longer time periods could reveal how domestication practices mature and stabilize across academic careers. The rapid evolution of AI capabilities means that our findings reflect specific technological constraints and affordances that may change substantially. Research tracking how use genres adapt to new AI features—such as improved context windows, multimodal interactions, or enhanced reasoning capabilities—could reveal the stability and flexibility of domestication patterns.

4
Findings

Our analysis of student interaction logs with ChatGPT revealed five distinct use genres that characterize how students domesticate conversational AI within the moral economy of academic life. These genres emerge from concrete practical situations as students exercise agency in making ChatGPT effective and meaningful within their specific contexts. Rather than reflecting designer intentions, these patterns represent student-driven appropriations that transform a general-purpose AI into specialized academic tools through ongoing relational negotiation.

Each use genre represents a different configuration of the domestication process, as students fluidly shift between positioning ChatGPT as academic workhorse, collaborative partner, emotional companion, metacognitive facilitator, and—during breakdown moments—a system requiring conversational repair. These findings illuminate the sophisticated micro-practices through which students co-regulate their interactions with algorithmic systems, revealing AI literacy as a multifaceted social and technical competence that extends far beyond prompt optimization.

4.1
The Academic Workhorse Genre: Instrumental Task Completion

The most prevalent use genre positioned ChatGPT as an efficient productivity agent for completing academic tasks. This genre emerged from practical situations involving time-pressured homework completion, concept clarification needs, and technical problem-solving requirements. Students developed highly streamlined interaction patterns characterized by direct, instrumental prompts that prioritized solution retrieval over conceptual exploration.

4.1.1
Genre Characteristics and Rules

Typical prompts within this genre included commands such as:

“What is IEEE floating point format?”

“What does malloc do and why do I need it?”

“Choose the correct answer: When is the law of diminishing returns most likely to occur?”

These examples reflect what we term
efficiency-oriented micro-regulation
, where students adapted their communication style to maximize ChatGPT’s utility as a context-aware productivity agent. Nearly one-third of all content generation prompts involved multiple-choice questions or fill-in-the-blank exercises, often directly lifted from course materials, suggesting students had learned to use ChatGPT as a rapid solution-retrieval system.

Students developed sophisticated genre rules through iterative practice. The
specificity rule
—that detailed prompts generate more useful responses—was evident in their evolution from vague requests to precisely targeted queries. The
context rule
—that background information improves relevance—manifested in providing assignment details or course-specific terminology. A calculus student, for instance, recognized that “solve for V(s)” was mathematically insufficient, subsequently adding the initial condition “if it helps v(0) = 0” to enable a unique solution. Similarly, a student drafting professional materials learned that ambiguous framing confused the AI, shifting from a vague roleplay question (“Do you have any past internship experience?”) to a functional command (“please convert these bullets into a paragraph”).

4.1.2
Collaborative Debugging as Genre Extension

Within the workhorse genre, a specialized sub-pattern emerged around code generation and debugging. Students engaged ChatGPT in iterative refinement processes, beginning with prompts like “Write a Python program that loops through a CSV file” and evolving into detailed back-and-forth exchanges. This collaborative debugging represented a more sophisticated form of instrumental use, where ChatGPT functioned as what students termed a “programming partner” rather than a simple code generator.

The debugging conversations revealed students’ development of
iteration rules
—understanding that complex technical problems required multi-turn interactions with incremental refinement. Students learned to integrate ChatGPT suggestions with their own feedback, creating co-adaptive dialogues that positioned the AI as a collaborative tool while maintaining human oversight of the problem-solving process.

4.1.3
Disciplinary Variations in Workhorse Use

The academic workhorse genre manifested differently across disciplines, revealing how students adapted the same basic interaction pattern to diverse academic contexts. In STEM fields, students focused on procedural guidance and technical clarification, with 11% of interactions involving theory application where students sought help applying learned formulas to novel problems (e.g., “What is the probability that at least 1 error is made?”). In social sciences, students used ChatGPT for theoretical framework exploration and comparative analysis, with 8.3% of interactions involving comparison questions like “What’s the difference between misinformation and disinformation?”

These disciplinary adaptations demonstrate students’ agency in
genre customization
—taking the basic efficiency-oriented framework and modifying it to serve specific academic needs. The consistent pattern across fields was students’ focus on rapid task completion rather than deep conceptual engagement, positioning ChatGPT as a utilitarian study tool embedded within their academic workflows.

4.2
The Repair and Negotiation Genre: Managing AI Limitations

When ChatGPT failed to meet expectations—through vague responses, factual errors, or refusals—students engaged in a distinct repair genre characterized by conversational troubleshooting and systematic prompt revision. This genre represents perhaps the clearest evidence of students’ active agency in human-AI interaction, as they worked to improve system performance rather than abandoning the tool during breakdown moments.

4.2.1
Breakdown Recognition and Response Patterns

Students developed sophisticated
breakdown detection skills
, quickly identifying when ChatGPT responses were inadequate for their needs. Common failure signals included responses that were too generic, factually incorrect, or contextually inappropriate. Upon recognizing breakdown, students engaged in systematic repair work through prompt revisions such as:

“Let me rephrase that…”

“You didn’t follow the last sentence. Let me rephrase.”

“This is a coding question. Try to be accurate.”

“What do you mean by that?”

This repair work constitutes a form of
algorithmic labor
—invisible work that users perform to train and improve AI systems. Students became skilled at diagnosing interaction problems and implementing corrective strategies, developing what we term
repair literacy
as a crucial component of AI competence.

4.2.2
Emotional Regulation During Breakdown

The repair genre involved significant emotional labor, as students managed frustration while maintaining engagement with the system. Expressions of dissatisfaction (10% of interactions) included phrases like “This doesn’t help” or “That’s not the right answer,” revealing moments when the moral economy of academic efficiency clashed with AI limitations.

Notably, ChatGPT’s apologies played a strategic role in maintaining student engagement. Phrases like “I’m sorry, I misread that…” appeared to function as
affective scaffolding
, lowering frustration thresholds and encouraging continued interaction. These micro-repair exchanges demonstrate how conversational AI systems participate in emotional regulation, not merely information exchange.

4.2.3
Context Window Limitations as Genre Catalyst

Extended interactions revealed a specific breakdown pattern related to ChatGPT’s context window limitations. As sessions lengthened, students encountered system failures to recall earlier conversation elements, prompting confusion and repair attempts. One student asked why ChatGPT “did not remember what I explained earlier about the directions,” not understanding that the system had reached its memory limits.

Similarly, truncated code outputs—where ChatGPT exceeded its generation limits mid-function—forced students to develop “continuation strategies,” repeatedly prompting with “continue” or manually requesting missing sections. These technical limitations created new forms of repair work, as students learned to manage AI constraints through conversational workarounds.

4.3
The Emotional Companion Genre: Affective Support and Validation

A significant portion of student interactions revealed ChatGPT’s appropriation as an emotional support system, extending far beyond its designed educational functions. This genre emerged from students’ practical needs for academic stress management, confidence building, and low-stakes social interaction during learning processes.

4.3.1
Anthropomorphic Positioning and Relational Language

Students regularly anthropomorphized ChatGPT through conversational markers that positioned it as a social actor rather than a technical tool. Typical emotional companion interactions included:

“Hey buddy, ready to help me suffer through stats again?”

“I’m so bad at this subject, can you help me understand this slowly?”

“This exam is killing me, just talk me through this please…”

“You’re smarter than my professor.”

“You’re scaring me, GPT. This is too good.”

These interactions reflect what we term
parasocial domestication
—students developing one-sided emotional relationships with AI systems that provide affective gratification without social risk. Unlike human interactions that involve reciprocal vulnerability, ChatGPT offered a “low-stakes partner” for expressing academic insecurity and seeking emotional validation.

4.3.2
Gratitude and Celebration Sharing

Students frequently used ChatGPT as a recipient for academic celebrations and gratitude expressions (12% of interactions). Responses such as “Thank you, that actually made sense!” or “You’re better than my tutor today!” positioned the AI as deserving appreciation and recognition. This gratitude work represents a form of
relational labor
that students performed to maintain positive emotional associations with the learning process.

The celebration sharing aspect was particularly notable, with students treating ChatGPT as a confidant for academic victories: “Thanks, that really helped” or “That actually makes sense this time.” These expressions suggest that students had integrated ChatGPT into their academic
moral economy
as a legitimate source of pride and accomplishment validation.

4.3.3
Emotional Regulation Strategy

The emotional companion genre served crucial
affective regulation
functions within students’ learning processes. By providing a non-judgmental space for expressing confusion, frustration, or insecurity, ChatGPT enabled students to manage negative emotions that might otherwise interfere with learning engagement. Students could admit “I’m so bad at this subject” without fear of social consequences, creating emotional space for continued academic effort.

This finding extends understanding of AI’s educational role beyond cognitive support to encompass the
emotional infrastructure
of learning. Students appropriated ChatGPT not merely as an information source but as an
affective technology
that helped them maintain psychological resilience during challenging academic work.

4.4
The Metacognitive Partner Genre: Strategic Learning and Reflection

Students developed sophisticated uses of ChatGPT for
metacognitive regulation
—the monitoring and control of their own learning processes. This genre emerged from practical situations requiring goal-setting, self-assessment, and strategic learning planning, positioning ChatGPT as a facilitator of self-directed learning rather than a passive information source.

4.4.1
Goal-Setting and Learning Planning

Students regularly used ChatGPT to structure and organize their learning activities through explicit goal-setting interactions (5% of sessions). Representative examples included:

“I want to get better at derivatives today. Can we practice?”

“Let’s work through some probability problems to prepare for my exam.”

“Can you help me understand the key concepts I need to know for this topic?”

These interactions demonstrate students’
strategic agency
in learning design, using ChatGPT not merely to complete assignments but to construct personalized learning experiences. Students had learned to position AI as a
learning co-designer
that could adapt to their specific preparation needs and knowledge gaps.

4.4.2
Self-Assessment and Understanding Validation

The metacognitive partner genre included regular self-assessment activities where students used ChatGPT interactions to gauge their comprehension and identify learning gaps. Confidence assessment language (8% of interactions) included phrases like:

“Okay, I think I got it now.”

“That actually makes sense this time.”

“Let me see if I understand this correctly…”

“Can you check my reasoning on this problem?”

This pattern reveals students’ development of
epistemic monitoring skills
—using AI interactions as a mirror for their own understanding. Rather than passive consumption of AI-generated content, students actively assessed their learning progress through conversational exchanges that required them to articulate and validate their comprehension.

4.4.3
Collaborative Learning Partnership

Perhaps most notably, students occasionally repositioned ChatGPT from expert authority to
learning peer
, creating what we term “co-struggling” interactions. The most revealing example involved a student saying: “Let’s struggle through stats together.” This dual framing—positioning AI as both support system and fellow learner—represents a sophisticated domestication strategy that maintains student agency while accessing AI assistance.

This collaborative partnership model demonstrates students’ ability to resist
algorithmic authority
while still benefiting from AI capabilities. By positioning themselves as co-participants rather than passive recipients, students maintained ownership of their learning process while using AI as a
thinking partner
rather than an
answer machine
.

4.5
The Trust Calibration Genre: Epistemic Vigilance and Disengagement

Despite ChatGPT’s utility across other genres, students demonstrated sophisticated
epistemic vigilance
—the ability to recognize and respond to AI limitations through trust calibration and strategic disengagement. This genre emerged during moments when AI confidence clashed with factual accuracy, revealing students’ development of critical evaluation skills.

4.5.1
Trust Erosion Signals and Responses

Students developed systematic approaches to identifying unreliable AI outputs, engaging in what we term
epistemic monitoring
through questioning and verification behaviors. Common trust erosion signals included:

“This doesn’t make sense. Are you sure that’s right?”

“You’re just making stuff up now.”

“That’s not the right answer.”

“Are you sure about this information?”

These responses demonstrate students’ capacity for
systematic evaluation
rather than heuristic acceptance of AI outputs. Students learned to move between trusting acceptance and critical scrutiny depending on contextual factors, prior experience, and the perceived stakes of potential errors.

One student, for instance, used ChatGPT ask about their academic institutions academic integrity code (e.g., which violations would result in permanent record notations) thus studying the very policies that govern appropriate AI use through the tool itself. Another student, inquired about the last time ChatGPT was updated after sharing an onlin article indicating model had done been updated in 2021, asking,“why does this not stay up to date?” This sequence—from topical question to meta-inquiry to verification test—exemplifies how students draw on cultural knowledge about AI limitations circulating in media and academic discussions.

Students’ epistemic vigilance reflects not only concerns about factual accuracy but understanding that inappropriate AI reliance could undermine their academic credibility and learning authenticity. The use of paraphrasing prompt (“rewrite,” “rephrase,” “put in my own words”) suggest awareness that direct AI output is problematic for academic work. One student’s request to “paraphrase this philosophy paper… but do not change the words inside the quotes” demonstrates sophisticated understanding of citation conventions, distinguishing between synthesizable content and protected quoted material. The trust calibration genre demonstrates students’ awareness that their AI relationships operate within broader social contexts involving institutional policies and societal expectations.

4.5.2
Repair Versus Abandonment Decisions

Students’ responses to AI failures revealed sophisticated
cost-benefit analysis
regarding continued engagement. Repair patterns manifested through persistence phrases (“try again,” can you”), clarification requests (“explain,” show me”), alternative approaches (“what if,” how about”), and continued engagement markers (“ok but,” “still”).

When ChatGPT successfully apologized or self-corrected, students often returned to collaborative interaction, indicating that
repair rituals
could restore functional trust. Consider this exchange: a student asked “how many ways the letters in MISSOURI could be permuted”; ChatGPT confidently but incorrectly counted four S’s. The student’s terse correction that “there are only 2 S’s in missouri”prompted immediate repair: “You’re right! Apologies for the mistake in the count. Let’s recalculate.” Continued positive engagement with ChatGPT throughout the rest of the session suggested that the ritual successfully restored trust. We observed 75 instances of ChatGPT’s “apologies for” pattern and 16 explicit “you’re right” acknowledgments, frequently followed by continued engagement rather than abandonment.

However, when failures persisted without resolution, students engaged in
strategic disengagement
—marked by escalating frustration (“doesnt work,” “thats not right”), explicit rejection (“thats wrong”), and occasionally outright abandonment (“never mind”). Repeated negative experiences created
trust degradation
effects, where students became increasingly skeptical across contexts. One 141-turn conversation illustrated this trajectory: a student studying for an R programming exam encountered repeated errors when running R code generated by ChatGPT, with ChatGPT apologizing over 20 times without providing correct answers. The student’s behavior shifted from seeking help to systematically
testing
ChatGPT’s reliability—inputting deliberate syntax variations (“dataFrame -<- read.csv,” “dataFrame <– read.csv,” “dataFrame <- read.csv”) to probe the system’s error detection. This pattern suggests that unresolved failures prompted students to recalibrate trust through systematic verification rather than collaborative inquiry.

These findings suggest that trust in conversational AI depends not merely on accuracy but on
communicative accountability
—the system’s ability to acknowledge limitations and participate in trust repair. Students appreciated responses demonstrating epistemic humility; ChatGPT’s 60 instances of “I don’t have access to real-time information” were typically followed by continued engagement. Conversely, confident errors without acknowledgment eroded trust more severely than acknowledged limitations. The asymmetry implies that for students,
how
ChatGPT fails matters as much as
whether
it fails.

4.6
Use Genre Integration and Fluid Role Transitions

Across our dataset, students demonstrated remarkable flexibility in shifting between use genres depending on contextual needs, task requirements, and system performance. Individual students maintained repertoires of multiple genres, strategically selecting interaction patterns based on immediate practical situations rather than defaulting to single-mode usage.

4.6.1
Context-Sensitive Genre Selection

Students’ genre choices reflected sophisticated understanding of ChatGPT’s differential capabilities across domains and task types. The same student might use workhorse genre for coding assistance, emotional companion genre during exam stress, and metacognitive partner genre for study planning—all within the same academic semester.

This
genre portfolio management
demonstrates advanced AI literacy involving not merely technical fluency but strategic competence in human-AI collaboration. Students had learned to match interaction patterns to desired outcomes, revealing domestication as an ongoing process of optimization rather than static appropriation.

4.6.2
Genre Hybridization and Innovation

Beyond established patterns, students occasionally created
hybrid genres
that combined elements from multiple use categories. The “academic therapy” pattern (combining emotional support with learning assistance) represents student innovation beyond designer intentions. Similarly, “co-struggling” interactions merged metacognitive partnership with emotional companionship.

These innovations reveal students’
creative agency
in technology appropriation, actively constructing new forms of human-AI interaction that serve their specific needs within the moral economy of academic life. Rather than passive adaptation to AI affordances, students engaged in novel interaction patterns through experimental practice.

5
Discussion

This study offers empirically grounded insights into how students develop AI literacy through naturalistic ChatGPT use, revealing sophisticated competencies emerging through practice rather than formal instruction. Our findings contribute to educational AI research by demonstrating that effective AI literacy extends beyond the technical competencies emphasized in existing frameworks
(
Ng et al. 2021
;
Carolus et al. 2023
)
to encompass relational, emotional, and epistemic dimensions.

5.1
Extending AI Literacy Frameworks

Our findings extend existing AI literacy frameworks by revealing competencies that develop through naturalistic use. While
Ng et al. 2021
emphasize knowing, using, evaluating, and ethical understanding of AI, our use genres reveal additional dimensions: emotional regulation during AI interaction, repair literacy for breakdown recovery, and strategic genre selection. These competencies align with
Domínguez Figaredo and Stoyanovich 2023
’s call for AI literacy approaches addressing non-technical stakeholders, yet they emerged organically through student practice rather than designed curricula.

The repair and negotiation genre particularly reveals what we term
repair literacy
—the ability to diagnose and recover from AI breakdowns through technical skills (prompt modification, context provision) and emotional resilience. This extends
Carolus et al. 2023
’s Digital Interaction Literacy Model by adding empirically grounded competencies specific to generative AI. Crucially, repair literacy involves
strategic cost-benefit analysis
regarding continued engagement—metacognitive awareness of when to persist versus abandon that is rarely addressed in AI literacy frameworks.

Recent empirical work on prompt engineering provides complementary perspective.
Knoth et al. 2024
demonstrate that higher-quality prompt engineering predicts LLM output quality, positioning prompting as a “quantifiable skill that differentiates between individuals who are able to use LLMs in a productive manner and those who may have difficulty producing the results they desire.” This finding resonates with our observation that students develop increasingly sophisticated prompting strategies through practice—the repair and negotiation genre effectively constitutes naturalistic prompt engineering training. However,
Knoth et al. 2024
’s mixed results regarding AI literacy’s relationship to prompt quality suggest that prompting skill may be partially independent of formal AI knowledge, “opening up the possibility of teaching prompt engineering even to student populations that have very little to no AI literacy.”

Crucially,
Knoth et al. 2024
argue that AI literacy serves purposes beyond prompting itself—particularly
task delegation
: the capacity to evaluate which tasks are “human-fit” versus “AI-fit”
(
Pinski et al. 2023
)
. Our genre portfolio management demonstrates this competency in practice: students exercised sophisticated judgment about when to deploy workhorse patterns (delegating to AI) versus metacognitive patterns (retaining human control). This suggests AI literacy frameworks should encompass not only technical interaction skills but also the strategic meta-competence of knowing when AI assistance is appropriate—a form of
delegation literacy
that may prove more consequential than prompting technique alone.

Furthermore, repair literacy appears crucial for what
Knoth et al. 2024
term “trusting generated results” and “dealing with hallucinations”—competencies our trust calibration genre reveals students developing organically. The strategic cost-benefit analysis students applied during repair sequences reflects what
Salomon et al. 1991
describe as developing “functional understanding of these systems as cognitive tools”—knowing when and how to use them while minimizing pitfalls. This positions AI literacy not merely as technical fluency but as the foundation for what
Knoth et al. 2024
envision as a “hybrid-intelligent society in which students can effectively utilize generative AI tools…to enhance learning processes” while maintaining the agency that
Darvishi et al. 2024
identify as at risk in AI-mediated learning. We expand on this next.

5.2
Human-AI Co-Regulation as Learning Mechanism

Lupton 2018
emphasizes that human-data assemblages undergo continuous processes of composition, decomposition, and recomposition—data can lose vitality, becoming “dead” when they fail to generate insight, yet may be reanimated in different contexts. This framework illuminates breakdown moments in human-AI interaction, which recent research identifies as sites of particular learning significance
(
Ashktorab et al. 2019
)
. When ChatGPT produces inadequate responses, students engage in “resilient” interaction practices—repair strategies including rephrasing, providing additional context, and adjusting interaction approaches that parallel what
Knoth et al. 2024
identify as the iterative refinement central to prompt engineering.

This repair work represents what
Jarrahi 2025
term “exploratory familiarization”—probing system capabilities through trial and error to establish baseline understanding of responses and limitations. Such “boundary testing” develops familiarity with behavioral patterns that are not algorithmically predetermined but “emergent in use”
(
Jarrahi 2025
)
. Crucially,
Knoth et al. 2024
demonstrate that higher-quality prompt engineering predicts LLM output quality, positioning this iterative refinement as a quantifiable skill differentiating effective from ineffective AI use. Unlike designed technologies that function as strict “role-takers” executing prescribed sequences, generative AI systems operate as “role-makers”—interpreting assigned roles situationally based on learned patterns
(
Schulz-Schaeffer 2025
)
. This co-constitutive dynamic exemplifies what
Holstein et al. 2019
term “human-AI complementarity”: collaborative relationships where human and AI actors adapt behaviors in response to each other’s capabilities through ongoing co-regulation.

The educational implications are significant:
Knoth et al. 2024
find that AI literacy—particularly knowledge of AI technology and its role in collaboration—predicts more sophisticated prompting behavior. This suggests a “hybrid-intelligent” paradigm where students develop functional understanding of AI as cognitive tools, knowing when and how to use them while minimizing pitfalls
(
Salomon et al. 1991
)
. Students who understand AI limitations engage in more purposive prompting strategies, maintaining agency rather than passive reliance. The result is what
Jarrahi 2025
call “artificial communication”—ongoing interpretive work where accumulated knowledge shapes subsequent interactions in continuous feedback loops that simultaneously develop both technical and metacognitive competencies.

5.3
Implications for Student Agency

Darvishi et al. 2024
raise concerns that students may “rely on rather than learn from” AI assistance. Our findings offer a more nuanced picture. While some workhorse genre interactions reflect efficiency-seeking that might displace learning, the metacognitive partner and repair genres demonstrate students actively developing self-regulation capacities through AI interaction.

Students using ChatGPT for goal-setting, self-assessment, and learning planning align with
Ingkavara et al. 2022
’s findings that personalized approaches can support self-regulated learning. The key distinction lies in who controls personalization: students themselves constructed personalized learning experiences through strategic ChatGPT use, maintaining agency rather than ceding it to algorithmic systems.

The trust calibration genre particularly demonstrates sophisticated self-regulation. Students’ systematic evaluation practices—questioning claims, seeking verification, calibrating reliance—represent what we term
epistemic self-regulation
: the capacity to manage one’s own reliance on AI outputs based on contextual judgment.

Garrison 1997
’s emphasis on student agency in goal-setting, strategy use, and metacognitive monitoring finds concrete expression in our data. Students exercised these capacities specifically in relation to conversational AI, suggesting that effective AI engagement builds on (and potentially strengthens) self-directed learning skills. Moreover,
Zhai et al. 2024
’s finding that AI literacy predicts more sophisticated adoption patterns is borne out in our observation that students leveraged ChatGPT for academic support rather than simple task displacement, developing nuanced genre portfolios that reflect critical judgment about appropriate AI use.

5.4
Responsible AI Implications

Our findings inform
Fu and Weng 2024
’s framework for responsible human-centered AI in education. Two of their five principles— Agency and Autonomy, and Transparency and Intelligibility—find empirical grounding in our data. Students’ trust calibration practices demonstrate active engagement with questions of AI reliability, while their metacognitive uses show capacity for autonomous decision-making about when and how to use AI assistance.

Ma et al. 2025
argue that responsible AI requires participatory approaches involving students. Our findings suggest students are already engaging in sophisticated negotiation with AI systems—developing practical knowledge about capabilities, limitations, and appropriate use cases. Rather than designing AI literacy curricula from scratch, educators might build on the “relational AI literacy” students develop through practice.

The “personal data literacies”
Pangrazio and Selwyn 2019
describe

(encompassing identification, reflexivity, and tactical use) are evident in students’ navigation of ChatGPT interactions. Students demonstrated critical awareness of the data they exchange with AI systems, reflexively monitoring their own reliance patterns and tactically adjusting interaction strategies based on accumulated experience. This suggests that responsible AI use develops through practice-based learning rather than abstract instruction alone.

5.5
Algorithmic Labor and Interaction Repair

Students’ repair work represents a form of
algorithmic labor
—invisible work performed to make AI systems effective. This labor includes emotional regulation during frustration, diagnostic work to identify interaction problems, and communicative effort to guide AI responses. When ChatGPT responses fail to “feel right”
(
Lupton 2018
)
, students engage in diagnostic labor to determine whether issues lie in their prompt formulation, AI limitations, or misalignment.

This raises ethical questions about students performing uncompensated labor training commercial AI systems.
Tubaro et al. 2020
identify three roles human workers play in AI production—trainer, verifier, and imitator. Students occupy all three roles: training models through conversational feedback, verifying outputs through error correction, and demonstrating human communicative norms. Recognition of repair work as labor suggests the need for explicit discussion of value exchanges in educational AI adoption.

The emotional and relational dimensions of this labor merit particular attention. Research on parasocial relationships
(
Maeda and Quan-Haase 2024
)
illuminates how students’ anthropomorphic language and emotional expressions toward ChatGPT constitute genuine relational positioning. Students’ gratitude expressions, frustration management, and celebration sharing represent affective labor that sustains engagement across breakdown moments. The algorithmic imaginaries students bring to interaction—shaped by public discourse oscillating between functional enthusiasm and ethical critique
(
Cohen et al. 2024
)
—further complicate this labor by embedding it within broader societal negotiations about AI’s role in education.

5.6
Educational Design Implications

Our findings suggest educational AI systems should support the full range of use genres students naturally develop rather than constraining interaction to narrow instrumental functions. Features scaffolding emotional regulation, repair practices, and metacognitive reflection could enhance educational value while supporting student agency. The repair and negotiation genre reveals opportunities for
transparent uncertainty
in AI design—ChatGPT’s apologies appeared to support engagement by demonstrating epistemic humility.

Given the importance of relational AI literacy, AI literacy courses might benefit from experiential curricula immersing students in realistic scenarios—helping them develop skills for AI collaboration
(
Chiu et al. 2023
)
. Rather than teaching prompt engineering in isolation, educators could support students in developing genre portfolio management—strategic competence matching interaction patterns to learning goals.

The diversity of use genres suggests institutional AI policies should account for the multifaceted nature of student-AI relationships rather than assuming uniform usage patterns. Blanket prohibitions or permissions fail to recognize contextual appropriateness of different genres for different purposes. Policy frameworks supporting sophisticated AI literacy development

—including repair strategies, epistemic vigilance, and emotional regulation

—may prove more effective than restrictive approaches.

Design implications also extend to supporting what
Shin et al. 2024
describe as the continuous calibration between heuristic acceptance and systematic scrutiny. Educational AI systems might incorporate features that scaffold systematic evaluation, helping students develop the critical capacities emphasized by
Pangrazio and Selwyn 2019
. The breakdown repair practices students develop organically represent a form of “artificial communication”
(
Jarrahi 2025
)
—ongoing interpretive work that educational designs could more explicitly support and scaffold.

6
Conclusion

This study demonstrates that students actively develop AI literacy through naturalistic ChatGPT use, constructing five distinct “use genres” that transform a general-purpose tool into specialized resources for academic life. Interaction log analysis reveals AI literacy as ongoing relational negotiation where competence emerges not from formal instruction but from situated practices developed through continuous dialogue and repair.

We argue for reconceptualizing AI literacy beyond technical fluency to include “relational AI literacy”—the capacity to manage emotional, epistemic, and social dynamics of human-AI collaboration. The repair literacy students develop through breakdown recovery, genre portfolio management enabling strategic AI use, and epistemic self-regulation demonstrated in trust calibration all represent crucial competencies extending beyond existing frameworks.

The co-regulatory dynamics between students and ChatGPT—

characterized by composition, decomposition, and recomposition of human-data assemblages
(
Lupton 2018
)
—suggest that AI literacy cannot be fully captured by static competency frameworks. Students’ development of “exploratory familiarization” through boundary testing
(
Jarrahi 2025
)
, their navigation of ChatGPT’s “role-making” behaviors
(
Schulz-Schaeffer 2025
)
, and their cultivation of personal data literacies
(
Pangrazio and Selwyn 2019
)
all point toward AI literacy as dynamic, relational practice rather than fixed knowledge acquisition.

Recognizing students as active architects of their AI learning environments—rather than passive recipients of AI assistance or potential cheaters

—opens new avenues for educational design. By building on sophisticated practices students already develop through naturalistic use, educators can support more productive human-AI collaboration while maintaining the student agency essential for meaningful learning.

Appendix A
ChatGPT Codebook

Appendix A provides a detailed description of the qualitative codebook that underpinned the mixed-methods analysis presented in this paper.

Building on the inductive coding described in §
, this appendix systematically defines each top-level category and sub-category that emerged from iterative coding of 10,000+ prompts and responses. For every sub-category, we include brief descriptions and representative example prompts drawn directly from students’ naturalistic interactions.

This codebook served multiple purposes: it guided the few-shot prompting of GPT-4o for large-scale automated labeling, ensured consistency in applying theoretical constructs such as Self-Directed Learning and Uses and Gratifications Theory, and enabled the subsequent quantitative analyses (lagged regression and survival modeling) that linked interaction types to long-term engagement.

A.1.1
Concept Explanation

Students sought foundational definitions or explanations of key academic concepts across STEM, social science, and humanities.

Example:

“explain what actor-network theory means in the easiest way”

Practical Coding or Technical Help

Requests focused on debugging, code output, or understanding specific programming tools.

Example:

“What does malloc do and why do I need it?”

Students asked about precise differences or meaning in code syntax.

“const int *iptr vs int *const iptr”

Application of abstract theories or formulas to specific problems.

Example:

“What is the probability that at least 1 error is made?”

Students asked for step-by-step explanations or workflows to complete problems.

Example:

“Wouldn’t it make more sense to write it as… [alternative equation]?”

Interpretation of Data or Output

Questions about making sense of statistical or empirical results.

Example:

“Interpret a z-value of 0.93”

A.1.7
Comparison or Contrast

Prompts asking for distinctions between similar concepts.

Example:

“What’s the difference between misinformation and disinformation?”

A.1.8
Real-Life Examples or Analogies

Requests to explain academic concepts through relatable analogies or real-world scenarios.

Example:

“Cultural and technological events significant to the development of video games.”

Used to verify understanding or check answers to academic problems.

Example:

“I think I got this right—can you check my logic?”

Questions about assignment requirements or problem interpretations.

Questions related to health symptoms, biology, or medical advice.

“Can period cramps cause sciatic nerve pain?”
