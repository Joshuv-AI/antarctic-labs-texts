Phase Transition for Stochastic Block Model with more than n \sqrt{n} Communities
Alexandra Carpentier, Christophe Giraud and Nicolas Verzelen

Abstract
Predictions from statistical physics postulate that recovery of the communities in Stochastic Block Model (SBM) is possible in polynomial time above and only above the Kesten-Stigum (KS) threshold. This conjecture has given rise to a rich literature, proving that non-trivial community recovery is indeed possible in SBM above the KS threshold, as long as the number K K of communities remains smaller than n \sqrt{n} , where n n is the number of nodes in the observed graph. Failure of low-degree polynomials below the KS threshold was also proven when K ≪ n K\ll\sqrt{n} . When K ≥ n K\geq\sqrt{n} , Chin et al. [ Chi+25 ] recently prove that, in a sparse regime, community recovery in polynomial time is possible below the KS threshold by counting non-backtracking paths. This breakthrough result lead them to postulate a new threshold for the many communities regime K ≥ n K\geq\sqrt{n} . In this work, we provide evidences that confirm their conjecture for K ≥ n K\geq\sqrt{n} : 1- We prove that, for any density of the graph, low-degree polynomials fail to recover communities below the threshold postulated by Chin et al. [ Chi+25 ] ; 2- We prove that community recovery is possible in polynomial time above the postulated threshold, not only in the sparse regime of [ Chi+25 ] , but also in some (but not all) moderately sparse regimes by essentially counting clique occurence in the observed graph.

Introduction

Network analysis investigates random interactions among individuals or objects. A typical network over
n
individuals can be represented as an undirected graph with
n
nodes, where edges encode the observed binary interactions between individuals.
Examples of such networks include social networks (interactions being e.g., friendships, links, or followers),
biological networks (e.g., gene—gene or gene—protein interactions),
and information networks (e.g., email communication networks, internet, or citation networks).
The
Stochastic Block Model
(SBM), introduced by Holland, Laskey, and Leinhardt
[
HLL83
]
is a popular statistical model for network analysis. In the SBM, the set of nodes is partitioned into
K<n
disjoint groups, or
communities
, according to a random assignment. Then,
conditional on the (unobserved) community assignments, edges are generated independently,
with probabilities determined solely by the community memberships of the nodes they connect.
In its simplest version, the probability of connection is
p
if both nodes belong to the same community, and
q
otherwise. When
p>q
, nodes within the same community tend to exhibit stronger connectivity among themselves than with nodes outside their community.
Because of its simple analytic structure, its ability to capture community formation, and its interesting
theoretical properties, the SBM has become an iconic model for network data, and it has received significant attention from theoreticians in statistics, computer science and probability
[
Abb18
]
.

The central task in SBM analysis is to recover the latent community memberships of the nodes
from a single observed network. For a fixed number of communities
K
, a community assignment uniformly at random, a diverging population size
n
, and probabilities of connection
p
and
q
scaling as
1/n
, the seminal paper of Decelle et al.
[
Dec+11
]
conjectured with the replica heuristic from statistical physics, that, while non-trivial community recovery is possible above the information threshold

{n\lambda^{2}\over\lambda+Kq}\gtrsim\log(K),\quad\text{where}\ \lambda=p-q\ ;

(1)

community recovery is impossible in polynomial time below the Kesten–Stigum (KS) threshold

{n\lambda^{2}\over\lambda+Kq}<K\ .

(2)

This conjecture then suggests that, for
K\geq 5
, there is an information-computation gap for community recovery in SBM in this sparse regime, which means that the minimal separation
\lambda
required for community recovery in polynomial-time is larger than the minimal separation require without algorithmic constraints. This conjecture has raised an high interest among theoreticians, and a lot of efforts have been devoted to confirm this conjecture.

On the one hand, non-trivial recovery in polynomial time above the KS threshold was established in this setting in
[
Mas14
,
MNS18
,
BLM15
,
AS15
,
Chi+25
]
, see
[
Abb18
]
for a more exhaustive review.
On the other hand, a strong effort was devoted to prove the impossibility to recover the communities in poly-time below the KS threshold.
Due to the random nature of the observed graph in the SBM, the classical notions of worst-case hardness, such as P, NP, etc are not suitable for proving computational hardness in this framework.
In such random settings, other notions are considered, such as reduction to other hard statistical problems, like planted clique
[
BB20
,
BR13
,
BBH18
]
, or computational hardness in some specific models of computations, such as SoS
[
Hop+17
,
Bar+19
]
, overlap gap property
[
Gam21
]
, statistical query
[
Kea98
,
Bre+21
]
, and low-degree polynomials
[
Hop18
,
KWB19
,
SW22
]
. Among these, low-degree polynomial (LD) bounds have gained attention for establishing state-of-the-art lower bounds in tasks like community detection
[
HS17
]
, spiked tensor models
[
HS17
,
KWB19
]
, sparse PCA
[
Din+24
]
, Gaussian clustering
[
EGV25
]
, and many other models
[
Wei25
]
. In this model of computation, only estimators that are multivariate polynomials of degree at most
D
are considered. It is conjectured that, for many problems, failure of degree
D=O(\log n)
polynomials enforces failure of any polynomial-time algorithm
[
KWB19
]
(low-degree conjecture).
The first low-degree hardness results for SBM were for the detection problem
[
HS17
,
Ban+21
,
Kun24
]
, which is the problem of testing
\lambda=0
against
\lambda>0
.
Low-degree hardness below the KS threshold (
2
) for community recovery was proven recently in
[
LG24
,
SW25
,
Din+25
]
.

The setting where the number
K
of communities grows with
n
has attracted much less attention until recently. A polynomial time algorithm performing non-trivial recovery close to the KS threshold was already proposed in
[
CX16
]
. Yet, for growing
K
, non-trivial community recovery in poly-time just above the KS threshold was only obtained recently in
[
SZ24
,
Chi+25
]
.
Lower bound for growing
K
have been derived in
[
LG24
,
Din+25
,
Chi+25
]
, proving failure of low-degree polynomials below the KS threshold (
2
) when
K=o(\sqrt{n})
, both in the sparse case discussed here (
p,q
scaling like
1/n
), and in the denser case where probabilities of connection can grow faster than
1/n
. In particular, Chin et al.
[
Chi+25
]
proves that, when
K=o(\sqrt{n})
, the prediction from Decelle et al.
[
Dec+11
]
holds in the sparse case, conditional on the low degree conjecture. Indeed, in addition to proving failure of low-degree polynomials below the KS threshold in any regime, they also prove the existence of a poly-time algorithm based on non-backtracking statistics which succeeds to partially recover the communities above the KS threshold in the sparse regime (where
p,q
scale like
1/n
).

The regime with a higher number of communities,
K\geq\sqrt{n}
remains widely not understood. Chin et al.
[
Chi+25
]
made an important breakthrough by proving that the prediction from Decelle et al.
[
Dec+11
]
fails for
K\gg\sqrt{n}
. Indeed, when
K\gg\sqrt{n}
, their poly-time algorithm based on non-backtracking statistics succeeds to partially recover the communities below the KS threshold in the sparse regime (where
p,q
scale like
1/n
). A similar phenomenon had already been observed in
[
EGV24
]
for the related problem of clustering in Gaussian mixture: When
K\geq\sqrt{n}
, a simple hierarchical clustering algorithm succeeds to recover the clusters below the KS threshold, and it has been proved to be optimal up to possible log factors
[
EGV24
,
EGV25
]
. For the SBM in the sparse regime (
p,q
scaling like
1/n
) Chin et al.
[
Chi+25
]
prove that, when
K\gg\sqrt{n}
, partial recovery of the communities is possible in polynomial time when

\lambda\gtrsim_{\log}\left(q+{\lambda\over K}\right)^{1-\log_{n}(K)},

(3)

where
\log_{n}
is the logarithm in base
n
(i.e.
K=n^{\log_{n}(K)}
) and where
\gtrsim_{\log}
hides a poly-log factor, which is proportional to the square of the natural logarithm of the average density.
This upper-bound for the sparse regime lead them to pose the following open questions (Question 1.9 in
[
Chi+25
]
, that we transpose with our notation)

Let
\log_{n}(K)={1\over 2}+\Omega(1)
. Does the phase transition for efficient weak
recovery occur at
\lambda\gtrsim_{\log}\left(q+{\lambda\over K}\right)^{1-\log_{n}(K)}
, where
\gtrsim_{\log}
hides poly-log factors? Moreover, is there a sharp or coarse
transition in the recovery accuracy as
\lambda
varies as a function of the average density?

Let us sightly (and partially) rephrase these two questions for convenience of the discussion. When
K\gg\sqrt{n}
:

Q1:

Does the phase transition occur at (
3
) in the sparse regime (
p,q
scaling like
1/n
)?

Q2:

Where does the phase transition occur in denser regimes?

For the first question Q1, one may guess that the answer should be positive. Indeed, non-backtracking statistics are tightly related to Belief-Propagation and the Bethe free-energy, the later being known to be a valid approximation of the log-likelihood in the sparse regime, while the former is expected to be optimal again in the sparse regime
[
Krz+13
,
Moo17
]
. Hence, as far as the analysis of
[
Chi+25
]
is tight, we can expect that (
3
) is the threshold where the phase transition occurs in the sparse regime.

The answer to the second question Q2 seems much more open. First, the upper-bound of
[
Chi+25
]
is for the sparse regime, and we are not aware of any result proving non-trivial recovery below the KS threshold in a dense regime. Second, even if the upper-bound of
[
Chi+25
]
were to hold in a denser regime, there is no strong indication that non-backtracking statistics should be optimal in this denser regime. In particular, in the light of the results for the Gaussian clustering problem
[
EGV24
]
, we may guess that some simple algorithms could be more powerful in very dense regimes.

In this paper, we provide a partial answer to these two questions. We prove that

1.

Low-degree polynomials fail for community recovery when
\lambda\lesssim_{\log}\left(q+{\lambda\over K}\right)^{1-\log_{n}(K)}
, for all density regime;

2.

Community recovery is possible in some moderately sparse regimes when (
3
) holds.

These two results provide evidence that the phase transition for community recovery in poly-time holds at the Threshold (
3
) conjectured by Chin et al.
[
Chi+25
]
.
Let us present our two main contributions with more details.

Contribution 1: Low degree polynomial lower bound.

Our first main result is a low-degree lower bound for community recovery when
K\geq\sqrt{n}
. Low-degree polynomials are not well-suited for directly outputting a partition of the nodes, which is combinatorial by nature. Instead, as standard for clustering or community recovery
[
LG24
,
EGV24
,
SW25
,
Chi+25
,
EGV25
]
, we focus on the problem of estimating
x_{ij}=\mathbf{1}\left\{i,j\ \text{are in the same community}\right\}-1/K
, which can be directly related to the problem of clustering
[
EGV24
,
EGV25
]
. Our main result — Corollary
2.2
, Section
2
— states the following.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal)
.

Assume that
K\geq\sqrt{n}
. When
\lambda\lesssim_{\log}\left(q+{\lambda\over K}\right)^{1-\log_{n}(K)}
, any
\log(n)
-degree polynomial
f
fails to estimate
x_{ij}
significantly better than
f=\mathbb{E}[x_{ij}]=0
.

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we built on the ideas of
[
Car+25
]
to construct a basis of low-degree polynomials, which is permutation invariant and almost orthonormal when
\lambda\lesssim_{\log}\left(q+{\lambda\over K}\right)^{1-\log_{n}(K)}
. Proving almost orthonormality is the main hurdle of the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Then, the lower bound can be simply derived.

Theorem
1.1
confirms that the Threshold (
3
) identified by
[
Chi+25
]
is the right threshold — up to a possible poly-log factor — for the sparse regime with
K\gg\sqrt{n}
. It is also a lower bound on the minimal separation required for poly-time recovery in denser regime. Our second main contribution is to prove that, for
K\geq\sqrt{n}
, community recovery in poly-time is also possible in some denser regime as soon as (
3
) holds.

Contribution 2: Successful recovery in some moderately sparse regimes with clique counts.

Chin et al.
[
Chi+25
]
prove that, in the sparse regime (
p,q
scale like
1/n
), partial recovery of the communities is possible above the threshold (
3
) with non-backtracking statistics, when
K\gg\sqrt{n}
. Our second contribution — Corollary
2.6
Section
2
— provides a similar result in some denser regimes, with an algorithm based on clique counts.

Theorem 1.2

(Informal)
.

Assume that
K\geq\sqrt{n}
. When
\lambda\gtrsim_{\log}\left(q+{\lambda\over K}\right)^{1-\log_{n}(K)}
,
and
q\asymp n^{-{2\over m+1}}
, with
m\in\left\{3,4,5,\ldots\right\}
, then community recovery is possible in polynomial time with an algorithm based on
m
-clique counting.

Combining Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
, we get that for
q\asymp n^{-{2\over m+1}}
, with
m\in\left\{3,4,5,\ldots\right\}
, the phase transition for recovery in poly-time occurs at level (
3
).
While Theorem
1.2
only covers a discrete set of density, namely
q\asymp n^{-{2\over m+1}}
, with
m\in\left\{3,4,5,\ldots\right\}
, this result provides a strong indication that, when
K\gg\sqrt{n}
, the threshold (
3
) uncovered by
[
Chi+25
]
is the right threshold where the phase transition occurs for community recovery in poly-time.

It is worth mentioning that counting cliques of size
m=3
is closely related to the hierarchical clustering algorithm in
[
EGV24
]
, which is optimal for Gaussian mixture when
K\gg\sqrt{n}
. Yet, counting
3
-cliques is only optimal for SBM in a narrow regime of density where
q\asymp n^{-1/2}
. Indeed, contrary to the Gaussian case, the minimal separation
\lambda
remains bounded, and in denser regimes, better results can be obtained by averaging the signal over a wider
m
-clique, to reduce the variance.

Open questions.

While our results, combined with those of
[
Chi+25
]
, provide a better understanding of the phase transition for community recovery when
K\gg\sqrt{n}
, some questions remain open.

A first question is relative to the poly-log factor in (
3
). The poly-log factors appearing in the upper and lower bounds do not match. In particular, contrary to the KS threshold for
K\ll\sqrt{n}
, when
K\gg\sqrt{n}
, the exact form (with precise constants and poly-log factor) of the minimal separation
\lambda
required for non-trivial recovery remains unknown.

Question 1
.

What is the exact minimal separation
\lambda
required for non-trivial recovery when
K\gg\sqrt{n}
?

A second question is relative to the positive results, which currently only exist for some specific regimes for
q
.

Question 2
.

For
q\asymp n^{-\alpha}
, with
\alpha\in[0,1]\setminus\left\{1,{2\over 4},{2\over 5},{2\over 6},\ldots\right\}
, what are the optimal algorithms for community recovery when
K\gg\sqrt{n}
? Do they also succeed when
\lambda\gtrsim_{\log}\left(q+{\lambda\over K}\right)^{1-\log_{n}(K)}
?

As discussed at the end of Section
3
, our analysis suggests that, when
q\asymp n^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha\leq 1
, an optimal poly-time algorithm is based on the counting of the occurence — within the observed graph — of a connected motif with an average of
\approx\alpha^{-1}
edges per node.
This is coherent with the optimality of self-avoiding paths counting when
r=1
, and
m
-clique counting when
\alpha=2/(m+1)
.
The non-asymptotic analysis of counts of motives with density
\alpha^{-1}
is somewhat more involved than the non-asymptotic analysis of the clique counts of this paper. It is deferred to a future paper.

Proof technique and limitation.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}
denote the distribution of the SBM with a prescribed separation
\lambda=p-q
. Let us consider the problem of estimating
x=\mathbf{1}\left\{1,2\ \text{are in the same community}\right\}-1/K
.
We say that degree-
D
polynomials fail to estimate
x
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\lambda})
-norm, when no degree-
D
polynomial can estimate
x
significantly better than the constant estimate
\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[x]
. In mathematical langage, proving failure of degree-
D
polynomials for estimating
x
amounts to prove that

MMSE_{D}:=\inf_{f:\mathrm{deg}(f)\leq D}\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\left[(f-x)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[x^{2}]-\sup_{f:\mathrm{deg}(f)\leq D}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[fx]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[f^{2}]}=(1+o(1))MMSE_{0},

(4)

where the
\inf
and
\sup
are over all the polynomials of the observation (the adjacency matrix in our case) with degree at most
D
. Since
MMSE_{0}=\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[x^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[x]^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[x^{2}]
, the problem reduces to proving that

\mathrm{Corr}_{\leq D}^{2}:=\sup_{f:\mathrm{deg}(f)\leq D}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\left[xf\right]^{2}}{{\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\left[f^{2}\right]}}\leq o\left(\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\left[x^{2}\right]\right)=o\left(1\over K\right).

(5)

Low-degree polynomials theory has emerged in a sequence of works
[
Hop+17
,
HS17
,
Hop18
,
KWB19
]
focusing on detection problems, where the goal is to perform a test with a simple null distribution (typically with independent entries). For SBM, the detection problem amounts to test
\lambda=0
against
\lambda>0
. In practice, it reduces to proving
\mathrm{Corr}_{\leq D}^{2}=o(1)
, with
\lambda=0
and
x
the likelihood ratio between
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}
and
\mathbb{P}_{0}
.
The core strategy for detection problems is to expand the polynomials
f
on a
L^{2}\left(\mathbb{P}_{0}\right)
-orthonormal basis of low-degree polynomials, and then to solve the resulting optimization problem explicitly. This approach has been successfully implemented for SBM in
[
HS17
]
. A similar approach cannot be implemented for estimation (i.e. recovery) problems, as no simple explicit
L^{2}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}\right)
-orthonormal basis of low-degree polynomials is known for
\lambda>0
. Three strategies have been proposed to circumvent this issue:

1.

Schramm and Wein
[
SW22
]
apply a partial Jensen inequality with respect to the latent variable (the community assignment in SBM), and expand the polynomials
f
on a
L^{2}\left(\mathbb{P}_{0}\right)
-orthonormal basis. Then, the optimization problem (
5
) can be upper-bound recursively. This approach has been implemented by
[
LG24
]
for SBM.

2.

To get tighter bounds, Sohn and Wein
[
SW25
]
expand the polynomials
f
over a basis which is orthonormal in an enlarged probability space including the latent variables. While the analysis is much more delicate, it leads to sharp results. It has been implemented for SBM in
[
SW25
,
Chi+25
]
to prove failure of low-degree polynomials below the KS threshold (
2
) when
K=o(\sqrt{n})
.

3.

To provide a simple and more direct analysis, Carpentier et al.
[
Car+25
]
built a basis for permutation invariant polynomials which is almost
L^{2}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}\right)
-orthonormal when the signal is low, i.e. typically when recovery is impossible. Compared to
[
SW25
]
, a counterpart of the simplicity of the analysis, is the derivation of less tight lower bounds. Indeed, similarly as for the Schramm and Wein
[
SW22
]
approach, bounds derived from Carpentier et al.
[
Car+25
]
approach are typically tight only up to poly-logarithmic factors.

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we built on the framework of
[
Car+25
]
. We adapt the basis proposed in
[
Car+25
]
by using a better renormalisation. On top of the proof strategy of
[
Car+25
]
, the main technical result is Proposition
B.1
, which controls the correlation between two basis elements.

In addition to provide a simple and direct proof, the framework of
[
Car+25
]
allows to get insights on the best degree-
D
polynomials, and hence on the optimal poly-time procedures. We observe in our analysis that the best polynomials correspond, schematically, to counting in the observed graph the occurence of motives with some prescribed density, the later depending on the overall density of the observed graph — see Section
3
for details.
This observation has motivated us to analyse clique counting for proving a matching upper-bound in some density regimes.

To simplify the analysis, we use a variant of clique counting to prove Theorem
1.2
. In a nutshell, we combine clique counting with a Median-of-Means (MoM) layer. The benefit of the MoM layer, is that we only need to control the expectation and the variance of the clique counting — see Lemma
2.4
— in order to get good enough concentration inequalities. The main drawback is that we lose a constant factor, so we cannot expect our analysis to provide upper bounds with sharp constants.

Main results

2.1
Setting

Statistical model.

Let us denote by
Y^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
the adjacency matrix of the observed graph on
n
vertices.
The graph being undirected and with no self-loops, the matrix
Y^{*}
is symmetric with zero on the diagonal. We assume that
Y^{*}
has been generated according to the stochastic block model: Each node
i
is assigned uniformly at random to one among
K
communities, and then nodes within a same community are connected with probability
p
, and those between different communities with probability
q
.

More formally, let
K
be an integer in
[2,n]
, and
p,q\in(0,1)
, with
p>q
. We assume that
Y^{*}
is sampled according to the following distribution:

1.

z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}\text{Uniform}\left\{1,\ldots,K\right\}
;

2.

Conditionally on
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
, the entries
(Y^{*}_{ij})_{i<j}
are sampled independently, with
Y^{*}_{ij}
distributed as a Bernoulli random variable with parameter
q+\lambda{\bf 1}\left\{z_{i}=z_{j}\right\}
, where
\lambda=p-q
.

To get simpler formulas, in the remaining of the paper, we will work with the (almost)-recentered adjacency matrix

Y_{ij}=(Y^{*})_{ij}-q\ ,\text{ for any }1\leq i<j\leq n.

(6)

We will also recurrently use the notation

\bar{q}=q(1-q),\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{p}=\bar{q}+\lambda(1-2q).

(7)

Community recovery.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{*}=\left\{\mathcal{C}^{*}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}^{*}_{K}\right\}
be the partition of
\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
induced by the community assignment
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
, i.e.

\mathcal{C}^{*}_{k}:=\left\{i\in[n]:z_{i}=k\right\},\quad k=1,\ldots,K.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
be a partition estimating
\mathcal{C}^{*}
. Exact recovery corresponds to the event
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}=\mathcal{C}^{*}
. Non-trivial recovery corresponds to a recovery of
\mathcal{C}^{*}
better than random guessing. More precisely, let us define the clustering error as

err(\widehat{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{C}^{*}):=\min_{\pi\in\mathrm{Perm}\left(\left\{1,\ldots,K\right\}\right)}{1\over 2n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|\mathcal{C}^{*}_{k}\triangle\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi(k)}|,

where
\triangle
is the symmetric difference, and
where the permutation
\pi
accounts for the non-identifiability of the labels
k
of the partitions elements
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{k}
.
A zero clustering error
err(\widehat{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{C}^{*})=0
corresponds to perfect recovery, while non trivial recovery holds when
err(\widehat{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{C}^{*})=o(1)
.

Membership matrix estimation.

Low degree polynomials cannot directly output a partition
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
. For this reason, the usual alternative objective
to clustering or community recovery
[
LG24
,
EGV24
,
SW25
,
Chi+25
,
EGV25
]
, is to estimate the (centered) membership matrix

[x_{ij}]_{1\leq i<j\leq n}=[\mathbf{1}\left\{i,j\ \text{are in the same community}\right\}-1/K]_{1\leq i<j\leq n}

in
L^{2}
-norm. As explained in
[
EGV24
,
EGV25
]
, this estimation problem can be directly related to the problem of clustering / community recovery.

The distribution of
Y
being invariant by permutation of the indices, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{f_{ij}:\mathrm{deg}(f_{ij})\leq D}\mathbb{E}\left[{2\over n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(f_{ij}(Y)-x_{ij})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\inf_{f_{12}:\mathrm{deg}(f_{12})\leq D}\mathbb{E}\left[(f_{12}(Y)-x_{12})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[x_{12}^{2}]-\sup_{f_{12}:\mathrm{deg}(f_{12})\leq D}\frac{\mathbb{E}[f_{12}(Y)x_{12}]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[f_{12}(Y)^{2}]}.

Hence, to prove that degree-
D
polynomials cannot perform significantly better than degree-0 polynomials, all we need is to prove that the supremum is
o(\mathbb{E}[x_{12}^{2}])=o(1/K)
. To simplify the notation, in the remaining of the paper we write
x:=x_{12}
and

\mathrm{Corr}_{\leq D}^{2}:=\sup_{f:\mathrm{deg}(f)\leq D}\frac{\mathbb{E}[f(Y)x]^{2}}{{\mathbb{E}[f(Y)^{2}]}}.

(8)

Proving that
\mathrm{Corr}_{\leq D}^{2}=o(1/K)
for
D
on the order of
\log(n)
provides strong evidence of computational hardness, as it is conjectured to imply that no polynomial-time algorithm in
n
and
K
can estimate the membership matrix significantly better than the trivial estimator
\hat{x}=0
.

Low-degree lower bound

2.3
Community recovery above the threshold
\lambda\gtrsim_{\log}\left({\lambda\over K}+\bar{q}\right)^{1-\log_{n}(K)}
with clique-counting

The proof of Theorem
2.1
suggests that counting cliques of size
m
might be optimal in some regimes, where the supremum in (
9
) is achieved for
r\approx m/2
.
In this subsection, we confirm this insight by proving that, for
q\approx n^{-2\over m+1}
, we can recover the communities above the threshold
\lambda\gtrsim_{\log}\left({\lambda\over K}+\bar{q}\right)^{1-\log_{n}(K)}
by counting
m
-cliques.

Counting
m
-cliques.

We want to determine if
i
and
j
are in the same community, i.e. to estimate
x_{ij}={\bf 1}\left\{z_{i}=z_{j}\right\}-1/K
.
Let us modify the observed graph
Y^{*}
by adding an edge between the nodes
i
and
j
, if there is no such an edge in the initial graph. Schematically, our strategy consists in counting the number of
m
-cliques involving the nodes
i
and
j
in the modified graph.

More precisely, let
V=\left\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\right\}
be a node set, and

G=(V,E)
be a clique on
V
, where we remove the edge
(v_{1},v_{2})
.
For
i<j
, we define
\Pi_{i,j}
as the set of injections
\pi:V\to\left\{1\ldots,n\right\}
such that
\pi(v_{1})=i
and
\pi(v_{2})=j
, and we set

S_{ij}=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{i,j}}P_{G,\pi}(Y),\quad\text{with}\quad P_{G,\pi}(Y)=\prod_{(v,v^{\prime})\in E}Y_{\pi(v),\pi(v^{\prime})}.

(12)

Should
P_{G,\pi}
be applied to the initial adjacency matrix
Y^{*}
instead of the “centered” one
Y
, the sum
S_{ij}
would be equal to
(m-2)!
times the number of
m
-cliques involving the nodes
i
and
j
in the modified graph (where we have enforced an edge between
i
and
j
).
The time complexity to compute
S_{ij}
is
O(m^{2}(en/m)^{m})
, so it can be computed in polynomial time, as long as
m
is considered as a constant.

Our strategy to estimate whether
i
and
j
are in the same community is, essentially, to compare
S_{ij}
to a threshold to be determined. To analyse this strategy, we need to compute some concentration bounds on
S_{ij}
conditionally on
z_{i}=z_{j}
and
z_{i}\neq z_{j}
. A first step in this direction is to compute the conditional means and variances of
S_{ij}
given these two events. It will be convenient to use the notation
\bar{q}=q(1-q)
,

\mathbb{P}_{ij}:=\mathbb{P}\left[\cdot|z_{i}=z_{j}\right],\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}_{\not ij}:=\mathbb{P}\left[\cdot|z_{i}\neq z_{j}\right].

(13)

Proposition 2.3
.

Assume that
q\leq 1/2
,
q+2\lambda\leq 1
, and
3\leq m\leq K
.
Let
i<j
and let
S_{ij}
be defined by (
12
).
We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{ij}\left[S_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle={(n-2)!\over(n-m)!}\left(\lambda^{m+1\over 2}\over K\right)^{m-2},

(14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\not ij}\left[S_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle=0.

(15)

In addition, if for some
\rho>0

\displaystyle{(n-2)\lambda^{{m+1\over 2}}\over K}

\displaystyle\geq\rho(m-2)^{2}\left(1+{\bar{q}\over\lambda}\right)^{m+1\over 2}

(16)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\quad{n-2\over K^{2}}\left(\lambda^{2}\over\bar{q}\right)^{{m+1\over 2}}

\displaystyle\geq\rho(m-2),

(17)

then, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}_{ij}(S_{ij})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{ij}\left[S_{ij}\right]^{2}\left[\left(2\over\rho\right)^{m-2}+\left((m-2)\left(1+{\bar{q}\over\lambda}\right)^{m+1}+{1\over\rho}\right)^{m-2}\left(1+{\bar{q}\over\lambda}\right)^{1/2}\lambda^{1/2}\right]

(18)

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}_{\not ij}(S_{ij})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{ij}\left[S_{ij}\right]^{2}\left[\left(2\over\rho\right)^{m-2}+\left({1\over\rho}\right)^{m-2}\right].

(19)

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

The core of the proof is to provide some unconditional upper-bounds on the variance, the proof of which is deferred to Appendix
E
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Assume that
q\leq 1/2
,
q+2\lambda\leq 1
,
3\leq m\leq K
, and set
\bar{p}=\bar{q}+\lambda(1-2q)
.
Then, for any
1\leq i<j\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{ij}\left[S_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle={(n-2)!\over(n-m)!}\left(\lambda^{m+1\over 2}\over K\right)^{m-2},

(20)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\not ij}\left[S_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle=0,

(21)

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}_{ij}(S_{ij})

\displaystyle\leq{(n-2)!(m-2)!\over(n-m)!}\bar{p}^{(m+1)(m-2)\over 2}\left({m-2\over K}+\left(\bar{q}\over\bar{p}\right)^{{m+1\over 2}}\right)^{m-2}

\displaystyle\quad+{(n-2)!(m-2)!\over(n-m)!}{\bar{p}^{(m+1)(m-2)}\over K^{2m-4}}\left(n-m+{K\over\bar{p}^{m+1\over 2}}\right)^{m-2}\bar{p}^{1/2}

(22)

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}_{\not ij}(S_{ij})

\displaystyle\leq{(n-2)!(m-2)!\over(n-m)!}\bar{p}^{(m+1)(m-2)\over 2}\left(\left({m-2\over K}+\left(\bar{q}\over\bar{p}\right)^{{m+1\over 2}}\right)^{m-2}\left(\bar{q}\over\bar{p}\right)^{{m+1\over 2}}+\left(\bar{q}\over\bar{p}\right)^{{(m-1)m\over 2}}\right).

(23)

Let us upper-bound
\mathrm{var}_{ij}(S_{ij})
and
\mathrm{var}_{\not ij}(S_{ij})
under the conditions (
16
) and (
17
).
Since
\bar{p}\leq\lambda+\bar{q}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}_{ij}(S_{ij})

\displaystyle\leq\left((n-2)!(m-2)!\over(n-m)!\right)^{2}\left(m-2\over n-2\right)^{m-2}\left[\left(\bar{p}^{m+1\over 2}{m-2\over K}+\bar{q}^{m+1\over 2}\right)^{m-2}+\left(\bar{p}^{m+1}{n-m\over K^{2}}+{\bar{p}^{m+1\over 2}\over K}\right)^{m-2}\bar{p}^{1/2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left((n-2)!(m-2)!\over(n-m)!\right)^{2}\left(\lambda^{m+1\over 2}\over K\right)^{2(m-2)}\left[\left(2\over\rho\right)^{m-2}+\left((m-2)\left(1+{\bar{q}\over\lambda}\right)^{m+1}+{1\over\rho}\right)^{m-2}\left(1+{\bar{q}\over\lambda}\right)^{1/2}\lambda^{1/2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{ij}\left[S_{ij}\right]^{2}\left[\left(2\over\rho\right)^{m-2}+\left((m-2)\left(1+{\bar{q}\over\lambda}\right)^{m+1}+{1\over\rho}\right)^{m-2}\left(1+{\bar{q}\over\lambda}\right)^{1/2}\lambda^{1/2}\right].

Similarly, since

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}_{\not ij}(S_{ij})

\displaystyle\leq\left((n-2)!(m-2)!\over(n-m)!\right)^{2}\left(m-2\over n-2\right)^{m-2}\left[\left(\bar{p}^{m+1\over 2}{m-2\over K}+\bar{q}^{m+1\over 2}\right)^{m-2}\left(\bar{q}\over\bar{p}\right)^{m+1\over 2}++\bar{q}^{(m+1)(m-2)\over 2}{\bar{q}\over\bar{p}}\right]

which concludes the proof of Proposition
2.3
.
∎

Adding a Median-of-Means layer.

In Proposition
2.3
, we control the mean and the variance of the clique count
S_{ij}
. A concentration inequality based on a second moment Markov inequality would not be tight enough to ensure meaningful results. Indeed, with a second moment inequality, we can ensure a correct result only with a probability approximately
1-\sqrt{\lambda}
, which is worse than the probability
1-K^{-1}
of correctness of the trivial estimator
\hat{x}_{ij}^{0}=-K^{-1}
. So we need a better concentration inequality.
To avoid computing higher moments, we add a “Median-of-Means layer” to get concentration bounds good enough for our purpose.

Assume that
m\leq n/(24\log(n))
and
1\leq i<j\leq n
.
Let
L=24\log(n)
and assume for simplicity that
(n-2)/L
is a positive integer larger than
m-2
. We define
N=(n-2)/L+2
, and we partition the set of nodes
\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}\setminus\left\{i,j\right\}
into
L
disjoint parts
J^{(1)},\ldots,J^{(L)}
with the same cardinality
N-2
. We define
\Pi_{i,j}^{(\ell)}
has the set of injections
\pi:V\to\left\{i,j\right\}\cup J^{(\ell)}
, such that
\pi(v_{1})=i
and
\pi(v_{2})=j
, and we introduce the partial clique count

S_{ij}^{(\ell)}:=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{i,j}^{(\ell)}}P_{G,\pi}(Y).

(24)

Finally, we define
M_{ij}
as a median of the set
\left\{S^{(1)}_{ij},\ldots,S^{(L)}_{ij}\right\}

and we estimate
x_{ij}={\bf 1}_{z_{i}=z_{j}}-{1\over K}
by

\hat{x}_{ij}=\mathbf{1}\left\{M_{ij}>{(N-2)!\over 2(N-m)!}\left(\lambda^{m+1\over 2}\over K\right)^{m-2}\right\}-{1\over K},\quad\text{with}\quad N={n-2\over 24\log(n)}+2.

(25)

We can now state our second main result.

Theorem 2.5
.

Assume that
q\leq 1/2
,
N=2+(n-2)/24\log(n)
is an integer, and
3\leq m\leq K\wedge N
. When

\displaystyle{(n-2)\lambda^{{m+1\over 2}}\over K\log(n)}

\displaystyle\geq 48(32)^{1\over m-2}(m-2)^{2}\left(1+{\bar{q}\over\lambda}\right)^{m+1\over 2}

(26)

\displaystyle{n-2\over K^{2}\log(n)}\left(\lambda^{2}\over\bar{q}\right)^{{m+1\over 2}}

\displaystyle\geq 48(32)^{1\over m-2}(m-2)

(27)

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle\leq 32^{-2}\left(1+{\bar{q}\over\lambda}\right)^{-2(m+1)(m-2)-1}(2m-4)^{-(2m-4)}\ ,

(28)

we have for
\hat{x}_{ij}
defined by (
25
)

\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{x}_{ij}=x_{ij}\right)\geq 1-n^{-3}.

Before proving Theorem
2.5
, let us comment this result. We recall that
m
is a fixed integer.
Since
\bar{q}\leq\lambda
, the Condition (
28
) merely requires
\lambda
to be smaller than the constant

\kappa_{m}:=2^{-11}\left(2^{(m+2)}(m-2)\right)^{-(2m-4)},

(29)

that depends only on
m
. As for Conditions (
26
) and (
27
), they roughly correspond to the opposite of Condition (
9
) in
Theorem
2.1
for
r=(m+1)/2
. Let us relate these two conditions to the Threshold (
11
).
First, in light of Theorem
2.1
and Conditions (
26
)-(

), we emphasize that
m
-clique counting can only be optimal if the maximum in Condition (
9
) is achieved for

. Let us identify when this occurs.

First, we observe that
\lambda\gtrsim\left({\lambda\over K}+\bar{q}\right)^{1-\log_{n}(K)}
can be rewritten as

\lambda\gtrsim{K\over n}\vee\bar{q}^{1-\log_{n}(K)}
. When

\lambda\gtrsim\bar{q}^{1-\log_{n}(K)}

Second, ignoring the

Comparing the later condition with

Assume that

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

Without loss of generality, we focus on the case
(i,j)=(1,2)
to reduce the number of indetermined indices.
We recall that
L=24\log(n)
and
N=(n-2)/L+2
.
We observe that the statistic
S^{(\ell)}_{12}
is simply the statistic (
12
)
applied to the graph
Y
restricted to the node set
\left\{1,2\right\}\cup J^{(\ell)}

From Proposition

3
Overview of the proof technique

To prove our low-degree lower bound, we leverage the ideas introduced in
[
Car+25
]
, that we adapt to our setting.
We highlight below the main steps and ideas. All the details are provided in the appendices. We recall that we consider the problem of estimating
x_{12}={\bf 1}\left\{z_{1}=z_{2}\right\}-{1\over K}
.

Almost orthonormal basis for permutation-invariant polynomials.

A first key idea is to notice that, since the distribution of
x_{12}
and
Y
is invariant by permutation of the indices — except 1,2—, the maximizer of
\mathrm{Corr}^{2}_{\leq D}
—defined by (
8
)— must be a polynomial invariant by permutation of the indices, except 1,2. Hence, we only need to focus on such polynomials, we call
invariant polynomials
.

We can built a simple family spanning the set of degree-
D
invariant polynomials. Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph on a node set
V=\left\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{D}\right\}
, and

\Pi_{V}
be the set of injections
\pi:V\to\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
fulfilling
\pi(v_{1})=1
and
\pi(v_{2})=2
. Then, any degree-
D
invariant polynomials can be decomposed on the family
(P_{G})_{G}
where

P_{G}=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{V}}P_{G,\pi},\quad\text{with}\quad P_{G,\pi}(Y)=\prod_{(i,j)\in E}Y_{\pi(i),\pi(j)}.

We observe that, when applied to the vanilla adjacency matrix
Y^{*}
, the polynomial
P_{G}(Y^{*})
merely counts the number of occurence of the motif
G
“attached” at 1,2. The family
(P_{G})_{G}
itself is not an almost-orthonormal basis of degree-
D
invariant polynomials. First, it is overcomplete, so we need to extract a maximal free subfamily
(P_{G})_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{\leq D}}
. Second,
(P_{G})_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{\leq D}}
is neither almost-orthogonal, nor almost-normalized. To get almost-orthogonality, we apply some correction as in
[
Car+25
]
(see Appendix
A
), and to get normality, we rescale the resulting basis by some normalizing factor, providing an almost-orthonormal basis
(\Psi_{G})_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{\leq D}}
. Compared to
[
Car+25
]
, we choose a better normalizing factor, which is crucial for deriving our results.

Controlling correlation term.

To prove the almost-orthonormality of the basis
(\Psi_{G})_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{\leq D}}
, a central problem is to upper-bound the correlation

{\mathbb{E}\left[P_{G^{(1)},\pi^{(1)}}P_{G^{(2)},\pi^{(2)}}\right]\over\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[P_{G^{(1)},\pi^{(1)}}^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[P_{G^{(2)},\pi^{(2)}}^{2}\right]}}

between
P_{G^{(1)},\pi^{(1)}}(Y)
and
P_{G^{(2)},\pi^{(2)}}(Y)
,
for two given motives
G^{(1)},G^{(2)}
, and two injections
\pi^{(1)},\pi^{(2)}\in\Pi_{V}
.
This correlation is upper-bounded in Proposition
B.1
. Then, the proof of almost-orthonormality of
(\Psi_{G})_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{\leq D}}
follows a similar path as in
[
Car+25
]
.

It is worth noticing that for proving success of counting a motif
G
, like a
m
-clique, a key feature is to
lower-bound
a variant of the correlation

{\mathbb{E}\left[P_{G,\pi^{(1)}}P_{G,\pi^{(2)}}\right]\over\mathbb{E}\left[P_{G,\pi^{(1)}}\right]^{2}},

see Appendix
E
. So, understanding the scalar product terms
\mathbb{E}\left[P_{G^{(1)},\pi^{(1)}}P_{G^{(2)},\pi^{(2)}}\right]
appears to be crucial for understanding the nature of the phase transition in SBM.

Identifying the optimal polynomials.

Once we have proven that
(\Psi_{G})_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{\leq D}}
is almost-orthonormal, we have

\mathrm{Corr}^{2}_{\leq D}\approx\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{\leq D}}\mathbb{E}[x_{12}\Psi_{G}]^{2}.

Furthermore, we prove in Appendix
B
that

\mathbb{E}[x_{12}\Psi_{G}]^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\left[\left(\frac{n}{K^{2}}\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{{\bar{q}}}\right)^{|E|\over|V|-1}\right)\land\left({\frac{n}{K}}\lambda^{|E|\over|V|-1}\right)\right]^{|V|-1},

(35)

when
G
is connected, and
\mathbb{E}[x_{12}\Psi_{G}]=0
otherwise. This implies that
\mathrm{Corr}^{2}_{\leq D}=O(1/n)
under the Condition (
9
).
We underline that, schematically,

1.

the upper-bound (
35
) is valid only when the bracket in the right-hand side is small compared to 1;

2.

non-trivially recovery becomes possible when this bracket becomes larger than 1 for some
G
.

The bound (
35
) then provides some interesting information on the optimal motives
G
. Indeed, taking for granted that (
35
) is quite tight — it should be so—, we observe that the motives
G
maximizing
\mathbb{E}[x_{12}\Psi_{G}]^{2}
are those being connected and maximizing

\left[\left(\frac{n}{K^{2}}\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{{\bar{q}}}\right)^{|E|\over|V|-1}\right)\land\left({\frac{n}{K}}\lambda^{|E|\over|V|-1}\right)\right]^{|V|-1}.

If this remains true at the phase transition, where this quantity is close to 1,
as discussed at the very end of Section
2
, when
q\asymp n^{-1/r}
,
the motives
G
driving the phase transition fulfill
|E|/|V|\approx r
. Counting such motives should then provide an optimal algorithm in this regime.

Lemma D.1
.
