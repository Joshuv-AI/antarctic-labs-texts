Research on seepage and stability of embankment slopes under coupled rainfall-vehicle loading
Authors: Yongliang Lin, Dongsheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChina has an extensive network of rural roads, which generally lack reinforcement/support measures or other slope-face protection designs. Consequently, during rainfall events, these roads pose considerable safety risks under the combined effects of vehicular loading and rainfall. In this study, a series of embankment-slope model tests was conducted using a self-developed rainfall simulation system and a RAW-200Z loading system to investigate the coupled effects of vehicle loads and different rainfall patterns. The seepage characteristics of the slope were examined by analyzing the temporal and spatial distributions of water content and pore water pressure within the slope. Subsequently, a finite-element model with the same geometry as the experimental setup was established in COMSOL Multiphysics for verification and analysis. The measured pore water pressure data were fitted and imported into the model, and the strength reduction method was adopted to quantitatively evaluate slope stability under the various coupled conditions. The results indicate that, compared with slopes subjected to rainfall alone, rainfall–vehicle coupling significantly alters the seepage behavior, leading to higher water content and pore water pressure than in the rainfall-only scenario. Moreover, the seepage characteristics vary markedly among rainfall patterns and are positively correlated with rainfall intensity and total rainfall amount. The stability analysis further shows that, when the fitted experimental pore water pressure data are used, the reduction in the factor of safety is greater than that predicted by conventional simulations. These findings confirm the detrimental effect of coupled rainfall and vehicular loading on slope stability and provide a theoretical basis for stability assessment of low embankment slopes under com-plex loading conditions.Keywords: Rainfall, Vehicular loading, Laboratory tests, Numerical simulation, COMSOL multiphysics, Slope stabilitySubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Natural hazards

Introduction

According to the China Statistical Yearbook, the losses and casualties caused by rainfall and geological hazards are significant and cannot be neglected. Rainfall-induced slope instability continues to occur frequently. A primary triggering mechanism is rainfall infiltration, which reduces the shear strength of slope soils; in addition, the influence of vehicular loading should not be overlooked. Such failures often result in severe casualties and substantial property losses.

At present, research on unsaturated seepage in slopes under rainfall has become relatively mature. In terms of theory, Wu et al.1 based on mass conservation, Darcy’s law, and elasticity theory, developed an analytical solution for seepage–deformation coupling. Ding et al.2 developed and analyzed a model describing changes in soil parameters induced by the migration of fine particles within the soil mass under cyclic loading. Wang et al.3 developed a program and, using the equivalent cohesion approach, analyzed the effects of variations in soil water content and matric suction on slope stability. Satyanaga et al.4 indicates that, in addition to changes in water content, variations in the first and second air-entry values (AEV) of dual-porosity soils also significantly affect the shear strength of the soil. In the field of numerical simulation, Han et al.5 used COMSOL Multiphysics to investigate the influence of hydraulic loading on slope stability. Hamdany et al.6 developed a novel soil suction measurement device capable of long-term in situ monitoring without cumbersome maintenance, thereby greatly facilitating the acquisition of data required for numerical simulations. Satyanaga et al.7 also conducted long-term field monitoring and numerical simulations of slopes equipped with a capillary barrier system, effectively demonstrating its role in enhancing the stability of slopes subjected to rainfall. Chen et al.8 established a three-dimensional model of slopes under rainfall for analysis. Zhen et al.9, He et al.10, and Wei et al.11 investigated the mechanisms of rainfall-induced seepage in slopes through modeling, focusing on water content and pore water pressure.

However, rainfall in the field is often nonuniform in both time and space and frequently occurs concurrently with vehicular loading; both rainfall patterns and vehicle loads can alter the infiltration response. In their investigations of the effects of coupled rainfall and vehicular loading on slopes, Leng et al.12 and Zhang et al.13 did not conduct seepage analysis. Huang et al.14 performed their study using a purely numerical approach. In another study, He et al.15 carried out model tests on slope seepage and stability under the coupled action of vehicular loading and rainfall; however, the test conditions were relatively limited and thus could not adequately reveal the seepage behavior of slopes under rainfall–vehicle coupling. Huang et al.16 proposes a novel upper bound limit analysis method incorporating boundary effects to improve the accuracy of shallow landslide stability assessment, which is derived for a full range of pore water pressure conditions while retaining the simplicity needed for practical hazard mapping. Elia et al.17 provide a systematic synthesis of the current numerical modeling approaches for capturing the response of soils to various factors, including loads and rain-fall, and contend that reliable slope behavior modeling must be grounded in site monitoring or laboratory test data.

Moreover, under the coupled effects of different rainfall patterns and vehicular loading, the evolution of pore water pressure in slopes can change markedly: different rainfall patterns lead to different variations in hydrostatic pore water pressure18,19, while vehicular loading can induce excess pore water pressure20,21. These effects pose a serious threat to soil stability. Nevertheless, Leng et al.12 and Xie et al.22 did not consider the influence of vehicular loading on pore water pressure, which may result in underestimated pore water pressures and, consequently, an overestimated factor of safety, thereby affecting slope-stability evaluation.

In summary, existing studies on the seepage characteristics and stability of slopes under coupled rainfall and vehicular loading remain inadequate. To address this gap, first, this study conducted laboratory experiments to obtain data on the variations of moisture content and pore water pressure within the slope under the coupled effects of rainfall and vehicular loading. Subsequently, a numerical model with dimensions identical to those of the laboratory test was established in COMSOL Multiphysics. In addition to conventional simulations of rainfall and external loading, the model also accounted for the additional influence of vehicular loads on pore water pressure. Furthermore, the pore water pressure measured in the experiments was innovatively fitted into the model to calculate the factor of safety. In doing so, the seepage and stability characteristics of slopes under rainfall–vehicle coupling are elucidated, providing a new theoretical basis for slope safety assessment.

Experimental materials and methods

Similarity relationships between laboratory model and prototype

In-situ tests provide realistic data and capture full system responses under real conditions, but involve safety risks, high costs, limited sensor placement, and poor repeatability. Laboratory experiments offer better controllability, safety, repeatability, and parameter adjustment, allowing systematic study of slope behavior. By selecting appropriate similarity ratios, lab experiments can approximate real mechanisms. In this study, the primary considerations are the seepage response and stability of the slope. According to Darcy’s law, seepage behavior is predominantly governed by the hydraulic gradient. Due to limitations in laboratory experiment time and conditions, the hourly rainfall applied in the laboratory was taken to represent the daily rainfall in the field23. The final simulated rainfall intensity was determined in accordance with the rainfall classification standards in China. The specific rainfall patterns are described in the subsequent section on the rainfall scheme. In terms of stress similarity and stability, the slope model in this study was constructed at a geometric scale of 0.1 relative to the prototype slope. To satisfy experimental requirements, the vehicular load was moderately simplified while maintaining its magnitude essentially unchanged. Although complete similarity with field conditions cannot be achieved; by adjusting the compaction density of the fill in the laboratory to match that of the actual slope, the model is able to reasonably reproduce the mechanical response and failure characteristics of the prototype. The results thus provide a useful reference for practical slope construction.

Experimental materials

Loamy sand is one of the most common soil types in China and, owing to its favorable mechanical and hydraulic properties, is frequently used in the construction of road and slopes24,25. Therefore, loamy sand was selected as the test material in this study. In practice, loamy sand is often mixed with gravel particles; for the purpose of ensuring experimental consistency, particles with diameters greater than 5 mm were removed during sample preparation. Prior to testing, the soil was oven-dried so that its volumetric water content was controlled at approximately 10%. Experimental device

A model box with dimensions of 900 × 700 × 450 mm was used as the experimental apparatus (Fig. 2). The exterior of the box was reinforced with hoop bands, and markings indicating the slope geometry and sensor installation locations were provided. The inner walls of the model box were coated with petroleum jelly to reduce boundary effects. The shape of the slope is determined by the relevant road slope standards in China. The slope size in this experiment is 0.1 times that of the actual engineering, and the specific size and shape of the slope are described in the following text.

Rainfall simulation scheme and equipment.

This experiment employs a self-developed rainfall simulation system to meet the requirements for rainfall quantity and uniformity. The system consists of four main components: a water supply pipeline, a flow control valve, a flow meter, and a rainfall plate. The water supply pipeline connects to the water source; a main-line flow control valve and a flow meter are installed to monitor and regulate the total water input. The rainfall plate is composed of an adjustable array of fogging nozzles, with the nozzle pipes arranged in a U-shaped configuration. Leveraging the fogging characteristics of the nozzles, this arrangement ensures uniform spatial distribution of rainfall. The rainfall plate support is height-adjustable; in this experiment, it was fixed 30 cm above the model box. At this height, the nozzles can fully cover the inclined surface and the ground plane of the slope, achieving uniform wetting of the experimental area.

Considering that, in practice, road pavements include a surface layer with low permeability, no rainfall nozzles were installed on the crest platform in this study. The locations of the nozzles are shown in Fig. 3.

Vehicular load simulation scheme and equipment

The RAW-200 geotechnical model testing system was employed as the loading apparatus, which is capable of simulating cyclic loading. The loading plate measured 150 × 250 mm. Traffic loading is inherently random due to the influence of multiple factors; therefore, it needs to be simplified for research purposes. When a vehicle travels along a road, the pavement is subjected to a dynamic load approximately equal to the vehicle’s static load. The traffic load acting on a given road segment can be reasonably approximated by a half-sine wave26,27. Accordingly, in this study, the vehicle load is simplified as a half-sine wave. The experimental subject is a rural low-embankment highway. Based on the traffic volume specified for Class IV highways in Chinese standards, and considering the reduction in vehicle speeds under rainfall conditions, the traffic flow is approximated as 200 vehicles per hour, corresponding to a half-sine wave loading period of approximately 20 s.The vehicular load is determined in accordance with the Chinese Highway Class II loading standard applicable to Class IV highways, with a representative value of 550kN. A single-lane vehicle platoon is assumed, with a vehicle width of 2.4 m and an inter-vehicle spacing of 13 m. The resulting contact pressure is calculated as 17.63 kPa and is approximated as 16 kPa, corresponding to a load amplitude of 0.6 kN. Monitoring scheme and equipment

The monitoring system comprised a volumetric water content sensor and a pore water pressure sensor, as shown in Fig. 7. The volumetric water content sensor was an RS485-type sensor with a measurement accuracy of up to 0.1%; the data were transmitted to a cloud platform via a network and subsequently downloaded for use on a computer. The pore water pressure sensor was a BWK-type sensor with a measurement accuracy of 0.05 kPa; it was connected to a Donghua DH3819 digital data acquisition unit and then linked to a computer via a Zigbee controller.

Monitoring equipment: (a) Volumetric water content sensor; (b) Pore water pressure sensor.

Experimental scheme

Because the seepage characteristics vary among the different layers of the slope, stratified monitoring was adopted. A total of seven monitoring points were arranged, and each point was equipped with one volumetric water content sensor and one pore water pressure sensor. The sensor layout is shown in Fig. 8. In each test, rainfall was applied for 0.5 h, followed by 0.5 h of monitoring after rainfall cessation (unloading); thus, the duration of each test was 1 h.

Layout of Monitoring Equipment (Unit: mm).

The test slope was constructed by layered filling, with a thickness of 10 cm per lift. Based on the relationship between the space for each filling and the filling density of the soil, the required mass of soil for each filling is determined. After weighing, the soil for one filling is added to the model box, followed by compaction work until the soil is uniformly compacted to a height of 10 cm. Sensors were embedded after the fill was placed to the designated elevation. Upon completion of filling, the slope was left undisturbed for 0.5 h, after which a stepped slope-cutting procedure was performed to form the slope surface. A rainfall simulation system was then installed. Prior to testing, a preload of 0.1 kN was applied at the slope crest by the loading apparatus to ensure proper operation during the test. Analysis of experimental results

Comparative analysis of slope seepage under single rainfall and coupled rainfall-vehicle loading conditions

Variation pattern of volumetric water content

Under coupled conditions, vehicle loading continuously alters the soil porosity of the slope, resulting in non-uniform saturated water content. Therefore, the maximum water content is adopted herein for interpretation.

Figure 10 shows the variations in volumetric water content at the monitoring points for Cases 1–2. The following trends can be observed:

Volumetric Water Content Variation Curves at the Monitoring Points: (a) Case 1; (b) Case 2.

The shallow monitoring points (Points 1, 2, and 3) near the slope surface began to exhibit an increase in volumetric water content approximately 5 min after the onset of rainfall. Point 3 increased most rapidly, followed by Point 2, whereas Point 1 increased most slowly. The volumetric water content at all three points tended to stabilize when it reached approximately 30%, and began to decrease after rainfall cessation. The decrease was fastest at Point 1, followed by Point 2, and slowest at Point 3. This is because, in addition to vertical infiltration, rainfall also infiltrates downslope along the slope surface. Under the coupled rainfall–vehicle loading condition, the maximum volumetric water content at all three points increased and the evolution pattern changed: after the onset of variation, each point first reached a peak; subsequently, the volumetric water content at Point 1 decreased slightly and then stabilized; the volumetric water content at Point 2 decreased and then increased again before stabilizing; and the volumetric water content at Point 3 continued to increase slowly. These observations suggest that vehicular loading may alter soil-state variables such as pore structure or porosity, thereby increasing the maximum volumetric water content and potentially accelerating rainfall infiltration in the shallow layer28.

For the mid-depth monitoring points (Points 5 and 6), in both conditions the volumetric water content started to increase only at approximately 30 min. However, under the coupled rainfall–vehicle loading condition, the maximum volumetric water content at Points 5 and 6 was higher than that under the rainfall-only condition. Because rainfall and loading had both ceased when the volumetric water content at Points 5 and 6 began to change, no increase–decrease trend was observed; instead, their responses were more regular and generally consistent.

For Points 4 and 7, no pronounced change in volumetric water content was observed, as they were not affected by rainfall infiltration.

Variation pattern of pore water pressure

Figure 11 shows the variations in pore water pressure at the monitoring points in Cases 1–2. The following trends can be observed:

Pore Water Pressure Variation Curves at the Monitoring Points: (a) Case 1; (b) Case 2.

For the shallow monitoring points (Points 1, 2, and 3), the onset of pore water pressure variation was essentially consistent with the onset of changes in volumetric water content. In Case 1, the maximum increment in pore water pressure was approximately 0.8 kPa, occurring at 30 min. After rainfall cessation, the pore water pressure gradually dissipated. In contrast, in Case 2 the peak pore water pressure reached approximately 3 kPa, with the largest peak at Point 1, followed by Point 3, and the smallest at Point 2. Under the rainfall-only condition, pore water pressure changes are induced by seepage driven by the self-weight of infiltrating rainwater and dissipate after equilibrium is reached. Under the coupled rainfall–vehicle loading condition, however, the compressive effect of vehicle loading suppresses drainage, leading to pore pressure accumulation and a higher peak value. Moreover, because Point 1 is closest to the slope crest, it is most affected by the applied load, whereas Point 3 is closest to the slope toe and is most influenced by rainfall infiltration and seepage; consequently, the peak pore water pressure followed the order Point 1 > Point 3 > Point 2.

For the mid-depth monitoring points (Points 5 and 6), the pore water pressure variation was generally consistent with the volumetric water content response, starting to increase at approximately 30 min. At that time, both rainfall and loading had ceased; therefore, subsequent pore pressure changes were primarily induced by rainwater infiltration. The pore pressure increment in Case 2 was slightly larger than that in Case 1, which is attributed to load-induced changes in the soil properties of the slope.

No pronounced change in pore water pressure was observed at Points 4 and 7, mainly because their volumetric water content exhibited almost no variation. Notably, the pore water pressure at Point 4 increased by approximately 0.3 kPa after unloading, further indicating that the applied load can affect soil pore water pressure.

Variation pattern of volumetric water content

Figure 12 shows the variations in volumetric water content at the monitoring points for Cases 2–6. The following trends can be observed:

Volumetric Water Content Variation Curves at the Monitoring Points: (a) Case 2; (b) Case 3 (c) Case 4; (d) Case 5; (e) Case 6.

For the shallow monitoring points (Points 1, 2, and 3), similar responses were observed under different rainfall patterns. First, the onset time of volumetric water content variation is related to the cumulative rainfall: in Cases 4 and 6, where the early-stage rainfall intensity was higher, the volumetric water content at Points 1–3 began to change earlier. Under the influence of loading, after the volumetric water content started to change, all three points first reached a local peak and then decreased slightly. Subsequently, in Cases 4 and 5, which featured higher rainfall intensity in the late stage, the volumetric water content at all three points continued to increase slightly during the later rainfall period; whereas in Cases 3 and 6, with lower rainfall intensity, the volumetric water content at all three points tended to stabilize in the later period. In all cases, Point 3 exhibited the largest maximum volumetric water content, indicating that, under loading, volumetric water content variation is positively correlated with the prevailing rainfall intensity.

For the mid-depth monitoring points (Points 5 and 6), volumetric water content began to change at approximately 30 min. In all cases, the magnitude of volumetric water content change at Point 6 was greater than that at Point 5, because Point 6 is closer to the slope toe, and during rainfall infiltration a portion of the water migrates downslope along the slope surface. Across different cases, the volumetric water content evolution at Point 6 differed: in some cases it increased slowly, whereas in others it rose rapidly and then decreased gradually. It was observed that scenarios exhibiting a rapid increase in volumetric water content all involved a 10 min rainfall event at 100 mm/h, whereas such an event was absent in cases with a gradual increase. This may suggests that higher rainfall intensity approaches the saturated hydraulic conductivity of the soil, promoting downslope surface runoff and thereby amplifying changes in water content at monitoring points of intermediate depth near the slope toe.

No pronounced change in volumetric water content was observed at Points 4 and 7 under different rainfall patterns, indicating that within the applied rainfall range of the experiments, rainwater could not infiltrate into the zone near the area directly beneath the slope.

Variation pattern of pore water pressure

Figure 13 shows the variations in pore water pressure at the monitoring points for Cases 2–6. The following trends can be observed:

Pore Water Pressure Variation Curves at the Monitoring Points: (a) Case 2; (b) Case 3 (c) Case 4; (d) Case 5; (e) Case 6.

For the shallow monitoring points (Points 1, 2, and 3), the peak pore water pressure remained close to 3.0 kPa in all cases, indicating that different rainfall patterns did not cause a significant change in the maximum pore water pressure. The peak pore water pressure is jointly governed by rainfall, applied loading, and soil properties. In addition, loading caused the volumetric water content to exhibit an initial peak followed by a slight decline; however, during this decline in volumetric water content, the pore water pressure did not decrease, but instead increased at a reduced rate, which is also consistent with the preceding explanation.

For the mid-depth monitoring points (Points 5 and 6), the pore water pressure response was generally consistent with the volumetric water content variation. This is because when pore water pressure at Points 5 and 6 began to change, both rainfall and loading had already ceased; thus, the subsequent pore water pressure variations were induced by rainfall infiltration.

For monitoring Points 4 and 7, because they were not affected by rainfall-induced changes in volumetric water content, pore water pressure remained essentially unchanged. Only Point 4 exhibited a slight increase in pore water pressure at 30 min when the load was removed.

Numerical investigation of the effects of different rainfall patterns on slope stability

Development of a finite element model and verification of seepage similarity

A slope model with the same dimensions as the experimental setup was developed in COMSOL29. In this model, the VG model implemented in COMSOL is adopted to represent the soil–water characteristic curve of the slope. The model incorporates three key parameters: a, the scaling parameter, which governs the matric suction of the soil near saturation; n, the pore-size distribution index, which controls the matric suction at intermediate water contents; and m, the curve-shape parameter at the tail, typically taken as 1 − 1/n, reflecting the matric suction behavior as the soil approaches dry conditions. All these parameters are closely related to the soil particle size distribution30. Previous studies31 have also shown that the soil–water characteristic curve of the same soil sample varies under different states; therefore, appropriate corrections and sensitivity analysis should be applied when it is used. In this experiment, the particle size distribution of the soil is given above. Based on the measured particle size distribution, a set of VG model parameters that most closely match those reported in the literature32 was selected to reproduce, as closely as possible, the soil used in this study. The saturated permeability coefficient of soil is closely related to soil type and density33.In this study, an initial estimate was obtained from typical values reported in the literature for similar compacted sandy soils34, considering the measured dry density and gradation characteristics. A minor calibration was then performed within a reasonable range to ensure consistency between the numerical simulation and the experimental observations of seepage behavior. The final adopted value was 4 × 10⁻⁶ m/s. A sensitivity analysis was conducted by varying key parameters (α, n, and Saturated Permeability) within ± 10% of their baseline values. The results indicate that the model is most sensitive to Saturated Permeability, while variations in α and n have a relatively smaller effect on slope stability. The overall trend of slope stability remains unchanged, indicating the robustness of the numerical model. According to relevant Chinese engineering standards, vehicle loads are simplified as an equivalent static load, whose magnitude is 1.2 times the peak dynamic load. Then direct shear tests were conducted using a pneumatic direct shear apparatus to determine the cohesion and friction angle of the soil at two water contents (10% and 30%). Reference35,36 indicates that as the water content increases, the shear strength and cohesion of soils initially increase slightly and then decrease. In this study, the soil shear strength and cohesion are simplified as linearly decreasing with increasing water content. The degree of saturation, Se, was then used to define parameters that vary with water content. The key parameters adopted in the model are listed in Table 3. Subsequently, the initial boundary conditions of the model were specified according to the experimental conditions, as shown in Fig. 14.

Fitting of experimental pore water pressure data

The above section demonstrate that the model reproduces the experimentally observed seepage behavior and yields a good fit to the measured water content. However, discrepancies remain in the predicted pore water pressure12,22. In slope stability analyses under coupled condition, the influence of vehicular loading on pore water pressure is generally neglected, which may lead to deviations in pore water pressure. The experimental results indicate that vehicular loading can significantly affect pore water pressure. Therefore, it is necessary to modify the pore water pressure in the finite element model. In general, the external stress input to the model is the pore water pressure computed by the Richards equation module in COMSOL. In this study, we replace it with a fitted pore water pressure based on experimental results.

Taking Case 2 as an example, the experimentally measured pore water pressure is fitted and correlated with the variation in water content. As the slope surface descends along the slope, the maximum pore water pressure initially decreases and subsequently increases; a single-term expression cannot adequately fit the experimental results. Therefore, the fitting expression is a quadratic form that accounts for the associated variable. Slope stability analysis

Strength reduction method is a widely used numerical approach for slope stability analysis38,39. Its core idea is to progressively reduce the shear strength parameters of the geotechnical mass—namely cohesion c and internal friction angle φ—until the slope reaches a critical failure state. Its expression is as follows.

In the expression, denotes the reduction factor, while and φ are the reduced soil shear strength parameters. and represent the initial soil strength parameters of the slope. When the reduced strength parameters cause the numerical model of the slope to fail to converge (displacements exhibit abrupt changes or the plastic zone propagates through the model), the corresponding reduction factor is the safety factor, and the slope is in a limit equilibrium state. This method can simultaneously account for nonlinear stress–strain behavior, progressive(gradual) failure characteristics, and complex boundary conditions. It is applicable to stability assessment of both homogeneous and heterogeneous slopes, and it does not require a priori assumptions about the slip surface shape. Consequently, the results are often more representative of actual engineering conditions.

Based on the fitted pore water pressure, slope stability was evaluated using the strength reduction method. Taking Case 2 as an example, at 30 min of rainfall, the numerical calculation failed to converge when the reduction factor reached 6.491, indicating that slope instability occurred at this stage. Therefore, the factor of safety was taken as F = 6.490 for the slope at that time. It should be noted that, due to the relatively small size of the slope model, the calculated factor of safety is significantly higher than the actual condition. Therefore, the results should not be directly applied to the construction of actual slopes, but can provide a reference for overall trends and safety design considerations. Variation of the Slope Factor of Safety under Different Rainfall Patterns.

As can be seen from Fig. 18, under the condition that the experimental pore water pressure is fitted, the laboratory-scale slope subjected to the coupled effects of rainfall and vehicular loading exhibits different variations in the factor of safety under different rainfall patterns. The reduction in the factor of safety is slow at the onset of rainfall and then gradually accelerates. For the same rainfall intensity, its influence on the factor of safety is greater in the later stage of rainfall than in the early stage, because infiltration has not yet developed during the early stage and thus has a limited impact on the slope. Among all rainfall patterns, the mid-peak rainfall pattern results in the greatest reduction in the slope factor of safety, decreasing by 0.236 from pre- to post-rainfall, whereas the front-peak rainfall pattern yields the smallest reduction, with a decrease of 0.140. The instantaneous rate of decrease in the factor of safety is positively correlated with the current rainfall intensity, and the cumulative decrease in the factor of safety is positively correlated with the cumulative rainfall amount up to that time. After rainfall cessation, the factor of safety recovers, with the recovery rate being the highest initially and then gradually decreasing.

Conclusions and discussion

In this paper, a laboratory-scale embankment slope model test under coupled rainfall and vehicular loading was designed based on in-situ conditions, and the effects of different rainfall patterns on the seepage characteristics of the slope were comparatively analyzed. A numerical model with the same dimensions as the experimental setup was developed in COMSOL Multiphysics to validate the simulation accuracy, and the simulated pore water pressure was calibrated against the experimental data; subsequently, the variations in the slope factor of safety under different rainfall patterns were evaluated, leading to the following main conclusions:

The experiments show that, compared with rainfall alone, the coupled effects of rainfall and vehicular loading intensify seepage in the shallow and intermediate layers of the slope. The maximum volumetric water content increased by approximately 25%, and its evolution pattern changed, resulting in a complex hydro-mechanical coupled field; moreover, the maximum pore water pressure increased dramatically by up to 300%. The applied loading significantly altered the slope seepage behavior.

The experiments indicate that under the coupled action of rainfall and vehicular loads, different rainfall patterns influence the seepage response of slopes. The evolution of moisture content and pore water pressure varies under different rainfall pat-terns. The rate of change is related to the instantaneous rainfall intensity. Different rainfall patterns do not significantly affect the values of the maximum moisture con-tent and the maximum pore water pressure. In addition, considering the observed increase in maximum water content under coupled conditions during the experiments, a key direction for future research is to integrate microscopic techniques, such as SEM or CT scanning, to investigate the evolution of soil structure and porosity under rainfall–vehicle load coupling.

The numerical model calibrated against the experimental pore water pressure data indicates that, under coupled condition, different rainfall patterns affect the slope factor of safety. The short-term reduction in the factor of safety is related to rainfall intensity, whereas the cumulative reduction in the factor of safety is related to the cumulative rainfall amount up to the considered time.

Due to scale effects, the factor of safety obtained from the present model is relatively high and not directly transferable to full-scale slopes in engineering practice. Nevertheless, the results provide valuable insight into the trends of safety factor variation under different rainfall patterns and coupled loading conditions. The combined experimental and numerical findings offer partial empirical support and practical guidance for road construction. For embankment sections subjected to frequent traffic loading, coupled analyses should be incorporated, with explicit consideration of the effects of vehicular loading on pore water pressure. Where appropriate, additional monitoring of key hydro-mechanical parameters, particularly pore water pressure, is recommended to enhance the reliability of stability assessments. Furthermore, targeted mitigation measures, such as improved seepage control and structural reinforcement, can be implemented to increase slope stability and reduce the risk of deformation and failure. This framework can inform the optimization of slope protection strategies and improve the redundancy and resilience of roadway safety systems.

Although a standardized experimental procedure and controlled test soils were employed to minimize systematic errors, the experimental conditions limited the implementation of large-scale repeated tests. As a result, variability in soil properties between different tests and issues related to repeatability could not be fully eliminated. And, due to experimental limitations, larger-scale tests could not be conducted; infiltration behavior under prototype scale and realistic rainfall intensities may differ from the experimental results. Therefore, larger-scale experiments and further studies should be carried out in practical engineering applications in conjunction with site-specific conditions.
