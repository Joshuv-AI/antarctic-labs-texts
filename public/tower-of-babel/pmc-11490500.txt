Association between radiation therapy for primary endometrial cancer and risk of second primary malignancies: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yuebo Wang, Yanan Cai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur objective was to evaluate the association of adjuvant radiation therapy (RT) to subsequent second primary malignancies (SPMs) in endometrial cancer survivors. Patients with endometrial cancer as their first malignancy were identified from 8 registries of the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. SPMs were defined as any type of primary malignancy that occurred more than 12 months after the diagnosis of endometrial cancer. Fine-Gray competing risk regression and Poisson regression were used to evaluate the radiotherapy-associated risk (RR) for SPMs. The Kaplan-Meier method was applied to assess the survival outcomes of endometrial cancer patients. Of 62,108 endometrial cancer patients,16,846 patients (27.12%) were in the RT group, and 45,262 patients (72.88%) were in the no-RT group. During the 30-year follow-up period, the cumulative incidence of SPMs was 20.9% and 19.7% in each group, respectively. In both multivariable competing risk regression analysis and Poisson regression analysis, adjuvant RT was found to be associated with a higher risk of developing colon and rectum cancer (adjusted hazard ratio (HR), 1.29; 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.12–1.50; P < 0.001; adjusted RR, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.11–1.49; P < 0.001), lung and bronchus cancer (adjusted HR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.08–1.50; P = 0.004; adjusted RR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.07–1.49; P = 0.005), vulva cancer (adjusted HR, 1.72; 95% CI, 1.04–2.85; P = 0.036; adjusted RR, 1.74; 95% CI, 1.03–2.88; P = 0.035), urinary bladder cancer (adjusted HR, 1.86; 95% CI, 1.41–2.46; P < 0.001; adjusted RR, 1.85; 95% CI, 1.40–2.44; P < 0.001), and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (adjusted HR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.06–1.77; P = 0.016; adjusted RR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.05–1.76; P = 0.017). However, a slightly decreased risk of breast cancer was observed in patients who underwent adjuvant RT (adjusted HR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.80–0.98; P = 0.021; adjusted RR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.80–0.98; P = 0.020). The RR for colon and rectum cancer decreased with age and elevated with increasing latency since endometrial cancer diagnosis, and the RR for urinary bladder cancer showed a similar tendency with latency. SPMs can significantly impair the survival outcomes of primary endometrial cancer survivors. Our findings suggest that adjuvant RT for endometrial cancer patients increases the risk of non-Hodgkin lymphoma and several types of solid cancer. Introduction

Pelvic radiation therapy (RT) has been routinely used in patients with early-stage endometrial cancer1. It had a well-established role in improving endometrial cancer locoregional control but did not improve long-term overall survival1–4. Additionally, RT might be responsible for the elevated incidence of second primary malignancies (SPMs)5, a late complication of cancer treatment. Therefore, the benefits and adverse events should be adequately evaluated, when assessing all potential effects of RT.

Although the risk of SPMs in patients with endometrial cancer receiving RT has been investigated in earlier studies, the current research on this topic is inconsistent. Rombouts et al. discovered that radiation to the pelvis, regardless of the primary tumor site, increased the risk of rectal cancer (radiotherapy-associated risk (RR) 1.43, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.18–1.72)6. A randomized study of the long-term effects of RT in patients with early-stage endometrial cancer found that women younger than 60 years old at diagnosis had a statistically significant increased risk of SPMs after RT (hazard ratio (HR), 2.02; 95% CI, 1.30–3.15), whereas in women older than 60 years old, the association became insignificant (HR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.45–1.43)4. Similarly, a SEER database study of endometrial cancer patients discovered that the absolute excess risk of acquiring secondary cancer following RT was 17.117. This study revealed that the risk of developing SPMs rose with latency with a peak risk at 10 or more years after RT. The organs at highest risk included the urinary bladder, vagina, vulva, sarcoma, colon and rectum, and lung7. In contrast, Wiltink et al. investigated the long-term probability of developing SPMs in a large pooled cohort of patients in PORTEC-18 and PORTEC-29 trials and found that patients who underwent RT had no higher risk than those who were treated with surgery alone10.

Given that a large portion of functional bone marrow is within the radiated field during pelvic RT11, understanding the risk of subsequent hematologic malignancies in long-term endometrial cancer survivors is necessary. However, to our knowledge, several studies only focus on second solid cancers, and very few research examined the risk of RT-associated hematologic second malignancies in endometrial cancer survivors. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the relationship between adjuvant RT and the risk of developing solid and hematologic second malignancies in endometrial cancer patients as well as to evaluate their survival outcomes.

Methods

Database and study population

Female patients pathologically diagnosed with a first primary endometrial cancer were identified from 8 registries of the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database between January 1, 1975, and December 31, 2020. The primary cancer sites were classified using the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3), with the code [C54.1] specifically indicating the endometrium as the primary location of the malignancy. Patients with endometrial cancer as a second or subsequent primary malignancy were not included in this study. The tumor stage was limited to localized and regional. We excluded the patients from further analysis if they: (1) were younger than 20 years, (2) did not undergo cancer-directed surgery, (3) survived less than 12 months after endometrial cancer diagnosis, (4) with missing data on race, tumor stage, RT, survival status or follow-up information. (5) undergo radiotherapy before surgery or had intraoperative radiation. Finally, 6,2108 patients were eligible for inclusion in the present investigation (Fig. 1). Informed consent and an ethical review were not required for this study because the SEER database used in our article is open to the public.

Exposure and outcome definition

RT was defined as patients having received adjuvant radiotherapy (external beam radiation therapy, brachytherapy, combination of external beam radiation therapy and brachytherapy) after surgery on the initial course of cancer treatment. According to the treatment method, patients with endometrial cancer were divided into 2 groups. Patients who underwent surgery and adjuvant RT were assigned to the RT group; those who received just surgery were assigned to the no-RT group. Radiation dose information is not available since it is not recorded in the SEER database. The SPM was defined as a new primary cancer occurring more than 12 months after the treatment of endometrial cancer. A one-year latency was required to minimize potential bias due to increased medical surveillance after endometrial cancer diagnosis and rule out synchronous malignancies12. The SEER program adheres to the ICD-O-3 guideline to identify SPMs from recurrent disease. Although all body sites listed in the SEER program were recorded, our study only included data for the most common second cancer sites for reasons of brevity (breast, lung and bronchus, colon and rectum, urinary bladder). Data was also presented for the less prevalent sites if the number of observed second malignancy cases was more than 40 or if the site-specific RR was statistically significant. To prevent relapses from being included in the studies, secondary endometrial malignancies were not considered appropriate sites for SPMs. The time to a second malignancy, termed as latency time, was calculated by subtracting the date of endometrial cancer diagnosis from the date of the second malignancy diagnosis. The follow-up ended at the date of diagnosis of any SPM, all-cause death, or the cut-off point for the study (December 31, 2020), whichever occurred first.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with non-normal distribution were presented as the median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as numbers and corresponding percentages. The chi-square test (χ2 test) was applied to examine differences in unordered categorical variables according to RT group. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for the analysis of continuous variables with non-normal distribution or ordered categorical variables.

To comprehensively investigate the relationship between adjuvant RT and the SPMs, two statistical methods were employed. Firstly, Fine-Gray competing risk regression analysis was utilized to calculate the HRs and 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) for patients with endometrial cancer. It is a specialized statistical method that takes into account the influence of other events, such as death from any cause, which could potentially compete with the development of SPMs. To avoid the possible limitations of competing risk regression models13, Poisson regression analysis was also used to estimate RRs and 95%CIs of SPM development. This approach focuses solely on the occurrence of SPMs as the event of interest. Additionally, a causal diagram (Fig. S1) was used to identify minimally sufficient adjustment set of variables, which was subsequently incorporated into the multivariate analyses conducted by the two aforementioned methods. The cumulative incidence of SPM development was evaluated using Fine-Gray competing risk regression analysis. Besides, we conducted subgroup analysis by further stratifying patients based on their age at diagnosis, year of diagnosis, and latency time since endometrial cancer diagnosis.

To evaluate the prognosis of endometrial cancer patients, the Kaplan-Meier method was applied to estimate their overall survival, and P values were calculated with the log-rank test. Overall survival was defined as the time from endometrial cancer diagnosis to death from any cause. Multivariate Cox regression models were performed to calculate the HRs and 95% CIs of factors affecting overall survival. Besides, propensity score matching was utilized to reduce possible bias for survival comparison between the RT and no-RT groups. Propensity scores were estimated based on the minimally sufficient adjustment set (age at endometrial cancer diagnosis, year of endometrial cancer diagnosis, tumor stage, chemotherapy, and tumor histology) from the logistic regressions. A 1:1 matched analysis without a replacement approach was performed using the nearest-neighborhood matching algorithm with a caliper size parameter (0.1×standard deviation of propensity score). The covariate balance was assessed by calculating the standardized mean difference (SMD) before and after matching, and an average absolute SMD of 0.1 was considered successful balancing between the 2 groups14. Statistical analyses were performed with R 4.3.0 (http://www.R-project.org). A P value less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant with a 2-sided alternative.

Results

Baseline characteristics of patients with endometrial cancer

For the current study, 62,108 endometrial cancer patients diagnosed between 1975 and 2020 were included in the analysis. The range of the follow-up time was 12–550 months and the median follow-up time was 107 months (interquartile range, 52–184 months). A total of 16,846 patients (27.12%) were in the RT group and 45,262 patients (72.88%) were in the no-RT group. Table 1 displays the baseline characteristics of patients with endometrial cancer by treatment modality. Compared to patients who received adjuvant RT as part of their initial treatment, endometrial cancer patients without adjuvant RT tended to be younger, in a localized stage, presented a lower proportion receiving chemotherapy, and had the histological type of endometrial endometrioid adenocarcinoma. They were also more likely to be married or of different races (American Indian/Alaska Native, Asian/Pacific Islander). 12 months later, 1,799 patients in the RT group and 4,666 patients in the no-RT group had SPMs (10.7% vs. 10.2%; P = 0.184). No difference was observed in the latency between endometrial cancer and SPMs in the 2 groups (P = 0.056).

Cumulative incidence of SPMs and risk of SPMs attributable to adjuvant RT

During the follow-up period, 7,991 SPMs were encountered. 1,526 of these occurred within 1 year after the endometrial cancer diagnosis and were therefore excluded, leaving a total of 6,465 SPMs for analysis. The cumulative incidence of SPMs (including solid and hematologic second malignancies) at 30 years (360months) after endometrial cancer diagnosis was 20.9% (95% CI, 19.3–22.6%) for adjuvant RT-treated patients and 19.7% (95% CI, 18.9–20.4%) for those not treated with adjuvant RT. Table 2 reveals the risk for SPMs in endometrial cancer patients with and without adjuvant RT by competing risk regressions and Poisson regressions. In multivariable competing risk regression analysis, adjuvant RT was found to be associated with a higher risk of developing colon and rectum cancer (adjusted HR, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.12–1.50; P < 0.001), lung and bronchus cancer (adjusted HR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.08–1.50; P = 0.004), vulva cancer (adjusted HR, 1.72; 95% CI, 1.04–2.85; P = 0.036), urinary bladder cancer (adjusted HR, 1.86; 95% CI, 1.41–2.46; P < 0.001), and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (adjusted HR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.06–1.77; P = 0.016), but a decreased risk of breast cancer (adjusted HR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.80–0.98; P = 0.021). For most other solid cancers and lymphatic and hematopoietic diseases, HRs were within striking distance of 1 but not statistically significant. Besides, the RRs were estimated using Poisson regressions to confirm the risk of SPMs attributable to adjuvant RT. After adjustment for age at diagnosis, year of endometrial cancer diagnosis, tumor stage, chemotherapy, and tumor histology, we found the RRs were also elevated for non-Hodgkin lymphoma (adjusted RR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.05–1.76; P = 0.017) and cancers of the colon and rectum (adjusted RR, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.11–1.49; P < 0.001), lung and bronchus (adjusted RR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.07–1.49; P = 0.005), vulva (adjusted RR, 1.74; 95% CI, 1.03–2.88; P = 0.035), urinary bladder (adjusted RR, 1.85; 95% CI, 1.40–2.44; P < 0.001), nevertheless, the RR was lower for breast (adjusted RR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.80–0.98; P = 0.020). Additionally, the outcomes of Poisson regressions were in line with those of competing risk regressions in other tumor sites. Figure 2 displays the cumulative incidence of 6 types of SPMs, for which RRs were statistically significant. Over 30 years after the endometrial cancer diagnosis, the cumulative incidences of cancer of the colon and rectum (4.04% vs. 2.58%; P = 0.001), lung and bronchus (2.32% vs. 2.15%; P < 0.001), vulva (0.34% vs. 0.18%; P = 0.016), urinary bladder (1.66% vs. 0.52%; P < 0.001), and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (1.13% vs. 0.98%; P = 0.045) were higher in the RT group compared to the no-RT group, while the outcome for breast cancer (5.24% vs. 6.79%; P = 0.005) was the opposite.

Risk of SPMs by different groups of age, year of diagnosis, and latency time

Figure 3 indicates the risk for 6 types of SPMs in endometrial cancer survivors, stratified by age, year at diagnosis and latency time since endometrial cancer diagnosis. Significant multiplicative interaction was found between age at diagnosis and adjuvant RT status in the risk of colon and rectum cancer (P for interaction = 0.019). In patients with endometrial cancer aged 20 to 59, we found an elevated risk of colon and rectum cancer (adjusted RR, 1.57; 95% CI, 1.22–2.01), but the risk gradually decreased and became insignificant at an age older than 60 years (60–69 years: adjusted RR, 1.29; 95% CI, 0.99–1.67; >70 years: adjusted RR, 1.05; 95% CI, 0.81–1.35). As for other SPMs (lung and bronchus cancer, breast cancer, vulva cancer, urinary bladder cancer, and non-Hodgkin lymphoma), the above-mentioned interactions were not observed (all P for interaction > 0.05). Additionally, there was a significant multiplicative interaction between latency time since endometrial cancer diagnosis and the use of adjuvant RT in relation to the risk of colon and rectum cancer (P for interaction < 0.001) and urinary bladder cancer (P for interaction < 0.001). After 120 months, an increased risk of urinary bladder cancer (adjusted RR, 4.06; 95% CI, 2.59–6.39) and colon and rectum cancer (adjusted RR, 1.70; 95% CI, 1.31–2.19) was noted, but this risk presented a declining tendency in the early latency (urinary bladder cancer 12–60 months: adjusted RR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.57–1.65; urinary bladder cancer 61–120 months: adjusted RR, 1.20; 95% CI, 0.72–1.95; colon and rectum cancer 12–60 months: adjusted RR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.72–1.17; colon and rectum cancer 61–120 months: adjusted RR, 1.14; 95% CI, 0.87–1.48). In contrast, multiplicative interaction between latency time and adjuvant RT was not significant in terms of the risk of breast cancer, vulva cancer, lung and bronchus cancer, and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (all P > 0.05 for interaction). Similarly, we did not find strong evidence of multiplicative interaction between the year of diagnosis and adjuvant RT for above 6 types of SPMs (all P > 0.05 for interaction).

Survival analysis

The Kaplan-Meier curves in Fig. 4 demonstrated that patients without SPMs had notably better survival than those with SPMs (Fig. 4a, P < 0.001 by the log-rank test). Multivariate Cox regression analysis (adjusted age at diagnosis, year of diagnosis, chemotherapy, tumor stage, and tumor histology) also confirmed the above result (HR, 1.34; 95% CI, 1.30–1.39). As for patients with second malignancies, before propensity score matching, there were differences in prognosis between patients with and without adjuvant RT (Fig. 4b, P = 0.017 by the log-rank test; HR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.02–1.19). After balancing clinical variables between the RT and no-RT group by propensity score matching, no difference was observed between the 2 groups (Fig. 4c, P = 0.120 by the log-rank test; HR, 1.08; 95% CI, 0.98–1.19).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first large population-based study using competing risk regression and Poisson regression to assess the relationship between the prevalence of solid and hematologic second malignancies and adjuvant RT in patients with primary endometrial cancer. Our research offers convincing proof of the association between adjuvant RT for endometrial cancer and an elevated risk of subsequent cancers of the colon and rectum, lung and bronchus, urinary bladder, vulva, and non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Furthermore, the RT-associated risks for developing colon and rectum cancer decreased with age and increased with latency since endometrial cancer diagnosis. A comparable tendency for urinary bladder cancer was only detected with latency. Additionally, endometrial cancer patients who developed SPMs presented a poor prognosis.

Consistent with the findings of some studies7,11,15–17, our research further confirmed the effects of adjuvant RT on the higher risk of developing certain types of solid and hematologic second malignancies. A pooled cohort study of endometrial cancer patients from randomized trials (PORTEC-1 trial and PORTEC-2 trial) conducted by Wiltink et al., on the other hand, failed to demonstrate the above-mentioned association10. The variations in results can be attributed to several reasons. Some investigations revealed a rising risk when follow-up time increased7,18. However, the median follow-up time in the PORTEC-1 trial was 12.6 years, and 7.5 years in the PORTEC-2 trial, which is substantially shorter than our study. Meanwhile, Wiltink et al. only observed a limited number of solid and hematologic second malignancies, indicating a lack of statistical power to support their findings. Furthermore, our study evaluated the risk using both competing risk regression and Poisson regression, which may provide a more compelling result about the association between adjuvant RT and the incidence of SPMs.

Generally, SPMs are predominantly expected to occur in irradiated or adjacent areas16,19. After pelvic adjuvant RT, the most common secondary tumor sites were rectum, colon, urinary bladder, prostate, and female genital organ5,7,19. Our findings evidenced that adjuvant RT for endometrial cancer is related to a significantly increased risk of subsequent cancers in organs within the irradiated volume, such as the colon, rectum, vulva, and urinary bladder, in accordance with most earlier studies4,7,15. However, our analysis found that the RT-associated risks of vaginal cancer was statistically insignificant, in contrast to the results reported by Kumar et al.7. One probable explanation is that the incidence of secondary vaginal cancer is relatively low, making statistical power insufficient for risk estimates. Further studies on pathological mechanisms are needed to explore their relationship. Moreover, we discovered adjuvant RT also had an impact on tissues further from the irradiated area, such as breast, lung, and bronchus. In line with prior studies7,13,19, our study demonstrated adjuvant RT enhanced the risk of developing lung and bronchus cancer. Whereas, reduced risk was observed for secondary breast cancer, which is compatible with Huang K’s findings15 but not with Kumar et al.’s7. The decreased risk maybe due to hormonal changes in the breast tissue of endometrial cancer survivors who had radiation-induced premature ovarian failure20. Besides, as previous researchers have pointed out7,21, breast cancer and endometrial cancer have similar risk factors. As a result, individual susceptibility to cancer acquisition and the combination of shared lifestyle and genetic factors may confound the results22. With > 40% of active bone marrow within the radiated field during pelvic radiation11, the risk of hematologic second malignancies rose as well. Wang, C et al. noted that patients who had RT experienced a higher incidence of hematological tumors (combined leukemia and lymphoma)17. Similarly, RT-associated secondary leukemia was documented by Wright J.D. et al. by combining all types of secondary leukemias, except chronic lymphocytic leukemias11. We calculated the risk of each type of hematologic malignancies independently and found that adjuvant RT was associated with a higher risk of non-Hodgkin lymphoma but not myeloma, or leukemia.

The biological mechanism of radiation-induced SPMs has been extensively investigated. In the linear model, the risk of SPMs rises linearly with radiation dose when the radiation dosage exposure is relatively low and less than a certain threshold17. It is believed that the low dose of radiation is insufficient to kill the cells but disrupts the genome stability to drive malignant transformation23. However, there is a decreased risk of SPMs at a higher radiation dosage exposure due to a presumed rise in cell sterilization24. Furthermore, ‘adaptive’ and ‘bystander effect’ models have all been presented7,25–27. Notably, the ‘bystander effect’ model suggests the harmful effect of radiation occurs not only in organs within the irradiated fields but also in tissues remote from the irradiated sites25. The ‘bystander effect’ could happen in non-exposed tissues through the release of mediators, which ultimately results in several adverse effects such as cell transformation, mutations, genomic instability, or cell death7,26. Besides, SPMs that developed in organs remote from the irradiated area may be in part due to out-of-field radiation17,19.

Our analyses revealed the heterogeneity of adjuvant RT effect among age and latency time since endometrial cancer diagnosis. A stronger RR for colon and rectum cancer was observed for younger age (20–59 years old) at diagnosis, which is consistent with studies of other radiation-exposed populations12,28. According to a previous study, it might be related to elevated susceptibility of the underlying tissue to the mutagenic effect of RT or the higher rate of cell proliferation at a younger age12. Further investigation into the underlying mechanism is warranted. The risk of secondary colon and rectum cancer or urinary bladder cancer rose with time since diagnosis, and the highest RR was identified after a latency time of more than 120 months. Earlier investigations explored the risk of second solid cancers and came to similar conclusions7,28. This can be explained by the fact that the radiation-induced injury occurs extremely slowly, and a longer time allows second malignancies with typically long latencies to emerge29. As a result, long-term surveillance in endometrial cancer patients may be necessary for the detection of colon and rectum cancer or urinary bladder cancer after adjuvant RT. Despite significant advancements in RT techniques and dosages from 1975 to 2020, including innovations such as Intensity Modulated Radiotherapy (IMRT) and 3D Conformal Radiotherapy (3D-CRT), which have enhanced precision and reduced doses over time17, our study did not reveal a clear trend in the reduction of risk for 6 types of SPMs with increasing year of diagnosis. This observation may be attributed to the limited follow-up information available in the SEER database. As a result, determining the appropriate dosage and specific adjuvant RT choices for individual patients in epidemiological study remains challenging. Additionally, factors such as age and year of diagnosis are likely related, contributing to the complexity of analyzing the impact of RT practices on SPM risks. Therefore, while RT techniques have evolved, their influence on SPM risks remains intricate and influenced by multiple interacting variables.

Finally, we present the prognosis of primary endometrial cancer patients in this study. Consistent with previous reports30,31, SPMs can significantly impair the survival outcomes of primary endometrial cancer survivors. Furthermore, we compared the overall survival of patients who developed SPMs after adjuvant RT to those without adjuvant RT. Before the application of propensity score matching, the RT group showed a slightly worse survival outcome than the no-RT group. Given that higher-stage cancer is often treated with radiation32, we suspected that some of the survival disadvantage in patients who got adjuvant RT was attributable to their initial cancer’s poor prognosis. Thus, we performed propensity score matching to balance the clinical variables between the 2 groups and found that there was not a significant overall survival difference between the RT group and the matched control group. Additionally, adjuvant RT for initial cancer did not indicate a significant survival hazard in multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. However, some studies reported that SPMs after adjuvant RT are more likely to have aggressive histological subtypes and to be resistant to conventional treatment, resulting in a worse prognosis16,30. Therefore, further research is required to explore the prognostic factors of endometrial cancer patients who developed SPMs after RT or no RT.

Strengths and limitations

The present study’s strengths include an over 40-year follow-up period after adjuvant RT to detect SPMs and a large observation population across the country. This provides adequate power in statistical analysis, which is frequently a limitation in small population-based studies or institutional research7. However, this study has several limitations. Like any observational investigation, the main limitation of the SEER data is a lack of treatment randomization, increasing the probability of confounding and potentially leading to either overestimation or underestimation of the risks related to RT. Besides, the SEER database only captures the initial treatment information of endometrial cancer, and it is uncertain whether patients underwent delayed RT in subsequent treatment, which could lead to misclassification bias and cause to underestimate the HRs/RRs of RT. Moreover, a one-year latency period cannot eliminate the possibility of metastases of the primary tumor. There remains a potential for misclassifying such metastases as SPMs. Additionally, the SEER database also doesn’t collect information on radiation doses and the extent of the radiated field. Therefore, we could not estimate the dose-response relationship. Lastly, the SEER Registry does not provide any information on risk factors that may have influenced the development of SPMs, such as an individual’s lifestyle, genetic background, chemotherapy agents, and hormonal therapy. This may reduce the precision of the HRs/RRs but was unlikely to affect our main conclusion.

Conclusion

In summary, the large population database analysis of female endometrial cancer patients indicates that adjuvant RT increases the risk of non-Hodgkin lymphoma and several types of solid cancers in the long term. Although continued discussion and further studies are warranted, it is reasonable to recommend enhanced surveillance of endometrial cancer survivors as a key consideration for SPM detection, particularly in those who receive RT.
