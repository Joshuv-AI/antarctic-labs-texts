Solving Polynomial Equations Over Finite Fields Thanks: An extended abstract of this work appears in the Proceedings of the Symposium on Discrete Algorithms (SODA 2025), published by the Society for Industrial and Applied Mathematics (SIAM).
Holger Dell, Anselm Haak, Melvin Kallmayer, Leo Wennmann

Abstract
We present a randomized algorithm for solving low-degree polynomial equation systems over finite fields faster than exhaustive search. In order to do so, we follow a line of work by Lokshtanov, Paturi, Tamaki, Williams, and Yu (SODA 2017), Björklund, Kaski, and Williams (ICALP 2019), and Dinur (SODA 2021). In particular, we generalize Dinur’s algorithm for 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} to all finite fields, in particular the “symbolic interpolation” of Björklund, Kaski, and Williams, and we use an efficient trimmed multipoint evaluation and interpolation procedure for multivariate polynomials over finite fields by Van der Hoeven and Schost (AAECC 2013). The running time of our algorithm matches that of Dinur’s algorithm for 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} and is significantly faster than the one of Lokshtanov et al. for q > 2 q>2 . We complement our results with tight conditional lower bounds that, surprisingly, we were not able to find in the literature. In particular, under the strong exponential time hypothesis, we prove that it is impossible to solve n n -variate low-degree polynomial equation systems over 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} in time 𝒪 ⁡ ( ( q − ε ) n ) \mathcal{O}((q-\varepsilon)^{n}) . As a bonus, we show that under the counting version of the strong exponential time hypothesis, it is impossible to compute the number of roots of a single n n -variate low-degree polynomial over 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} in time 𝒪 ⁡ ( ( q − ε ) n ) {\mathcal{O}((q-\varepsilon)^{n})} ; this generalizes a result of Williams (SOSA 2018) from 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} to all finite fields.

Introduction

In the 16th century, mathematicians have kept algorithms for solving polynomial equation systems secret from each other, so that they could publicly demonstrate their superior skill in case they were challenged for their non-tenured positions (e.g.,
[
38
,
32
,
30
]
).
Modern scientists are still facing similar problems, except that secrecy is frowned upon, and so we chose to publish our algorithm for polynomial equation systems over finite fields in this paper.

Polynomial equation systems over finite fields have countless applications.
In coding theory, they are used in the decoding step of error-correcting codes
[
27
]
.
In cryptography, they can be used to break cryptographic schemes
[
27
]
.
They even have a close connection to quantum computations, where the output of such computations can be expressed as the number of solutions to a set of polynomial equations, allowing for elegant proofs of some relations between quantum and classical complexity classes
[
13
]
.

1.1
Our Results.

For every prime power
q

Input:

Question:

Is there an assignment
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with
P_{1}(x)=P_{2}(x)=\dots=P_{m}(x)=0

\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}

We stress that the polynomials are explicitly given as a list of monomial-coefficient pairs.
Exhaustive search trivially solves this problem in time
\mathcal{O}^{\ast}(q^{n})
asymptotically in
n

If

1.2
Related Work.

Our main result continues a recent line of work
[
25
,
8
,
16
]
on solving systems of polynomial equations over finite fields in time
\mathcal{O}^{\ast}(q^{\delta n})
for some fixed
\delta<1
.
Using the polynomial method
[
4
]
, Lokshtanov et al.
[
25
]
obtained a randomized worst-case algorithm better than exhaustive search for any prime power
q
and degree
d\geq 1
.
They obtain running times of the form
\mathcal{O}^{\ast}(q^{\delta n})
, where
\delta=1-\tfrac{1}{Cd}
holds for some fixed constant
C
.
For
q=d=2
, their algorithm yields a running time of
\mathcal{O}^{\ast}(2^{0.8765n})
which was improved to
\mathcal{O}^{\ast}(2^{0.804n})
by Björklund, Kaski, and Williams
[
8
]
, who solely focused on solving
\textup{PES}_{2}^{d}
.
The current state-of-the-art algorithm for
\textup{PES}_{2}^{d}
was devised by Dinur
[
16
]
, significantly improving the running time to
\mathcal{O}^{\ast}(2^{0.695n})
.
All aforementioned algorithms are closely related to our work and will be explained in more detail in Section
1.4
.

We also provide some examples for other settings considered for this problem in the literature and results in those settings, highlighting the diversity of relevant settings, approaches, and applications regarding polynomial equation systems.
As the problem is hard in general (see Theorem
1.2
), many approaches aim to solve the problem “fast enough” in practice, for example in order to utilize them in cryptographic attacks.
Bard
[
3
]
and Courtois and Bard
[
12
]
solve multivariate polynomial systems over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
by reducing them to the satisfiability problem for Boolean formulas.
Bard
[
3
]
also gives a fast method for solving linear systems of equations over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
in practice, combining the so-called Method of Four Russians with Strassen’s algorithm.
Ding et al.
[
15
]
solve multivariate polynomial systems over arbitrary finite fields by reducing the problem to that of solving a single univariate polynomial equation over an extension of the original field.
There is a variety of algorithms based on Gröbner bases, prominent examples being the
F_{4}
- and the
F_{5}
-algorithm
[
19
,
18
]
as well as the XL-algorithm
[
11
]
.
In many cases, these algorithms output a representation of the set of all solutions.
They can be efficient in restricted cases or at least heuristically, but have very high worst case complexity.
Note that the complexity of the XL-algorithm was not conclusively determined in the original work, see
[
1
]
for an overview of what is known.

There is also work on restricted cases with a better worst-case complexity than the general problem.
Ivanyos and Santha
[
22
]
gave a polynomial-time algorithm in a restricted setting with applications in quantum computing.
Another line of research aims to obtain complexity bounds for Las Vegas type algorithms for solving polynomial equation systems under certain additional assumptions that are likely to hold for systems with random coefficients.
Here, the goal is to compute a representation of all solutions in a time that is between quadratic and cubic in the number of solutions.
These algorithms typically do not offer efficient ways to answer the corresponding decision problem
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
, and they require additional assumptions on the system.
A recent work in this vein is due to van der Hoeven and Lecerf
[
36
]
, which also provides a good exposition of related work.

1.3
Our Contributions.

We briefly mention the key contributions of this paper compared to previous work.
While many individual techniques generalize easily from
\mathbb{F}_{2}
to
\mathbb{F}_{q}
to obtain Theorem
1.1
, the overall argument is quite delicate.
The following are the most important changes compared to Dinur’s algorithm
[
16
]
for
\mathbb{F}_{2}
:

•

Instead of the Fast Möbius Transform, which only works over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, we use a suitable Fast Multipoint Evaluation algorithm by Van der Hoeven and Schost
[
37
]
(see Lemma
2.9
).

•

Björklund, Kaski, and Williams
[
8
]
as well as Dinur
[
16
]
use
a cancellation trick modulo
2

[
8
, Eq. (19)]
to significantly reduce the running time compared to Lokshtanov et al.
[
25
]
. We use Fermat’s little theorem to generalize this trick to
symbolic interpolation
over any finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
(see Lemma
2.4
).

•

Finally, we need to analyze the running time of the algorithm, which turns out to be surprisingly non-trivial in the general case. To do so, we use a bound on the
extended binomial coefficient
, which is equal to the number of degree-
\Delta
monomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
. Entropy-style bounds on the extended binomial coefficient have only recently been studied in mathematics in the context of the cap set problem (see Sections
2.3
and
3.5
).

Moreover, our hardness results (Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
) appear to be novel. We follow standard techniques from fine-grained complexity to design a suitable reduction from
k
-SAT.

1.4
Technical Overview.

We provide a high-level overview of the proof of Theorem
1.1
and discuss the key techniques of our algorithm.
We follow the main insights of the previous papers
[
25
,
8
,
16
]
.
Although two of these papers
[
8
,
16
]
only consider the special case of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, many techniques generalize to
\mathbb{F}_{q}
for
q\geq 2
, which is why we describe the techniques in the general case and sprinkle in our contributions to make the generalization work.

Polynomial Method using Indicator Polynomial.

Based on the so-called
polynomial method
from Boolean circuit complexity
[
4
]
, Lokshtanov et al.
[
25
]
devised a randomized algorithm for
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
by capturing the entire system of polynomial equations as a single
probabilistic
polynomial.
This system has a
small
exponential number of monomials and has a very high probability of being consistent with the original system on many assignments.
For polynomials
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
, we define the associated
indicator polynomial

F\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
as

F\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left\lparen 1-(P_{i})^{q-1}\right\rparen.

By Fermat’s little theorem,
F(x)=1
holds for a vector
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
if and only if
P_{1}(x)=\dots=P_{m}(x)=0
holds.
Thus, evaluating
F
on all points
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
suffices to decide whether the system has a solution, that is, whether the polynomials have a common root.
For more details, refer to Section
3.1
.
Unfortunately, this idea alone does not yet yield an efficient algorithm for
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
, as we are unable to expand
F
as a sum of monomials or evaluate it faster than in time
\mathcal{O}^{\ast}(q^{n})
—this is because
F
would need to be evaluated at each of the
q^{n}
evaluation points
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.

Probabilistic Polynomial Construction of Razborov–Smolensky.

Building on the observation in the previous paragraph, Lokshtanov et al.
[
25
]
used the probabilistic polynomial construction of
Razborov and Smolensky

[
29
,
31
]
, formally stated in Lemma
2.12
, to reduce the
number
of polynomials
P_{i}
.
It transforms the original polynomials into much fewer, probabilistic polynomials
\widetilde{P}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
.
In turn, the indicator polynomial
\widetilde{F}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
constructed from the polynomials
\widetilde{P}_{i}
is of smaller degree and each evaluation
\widetilde{P}_{i}(x)
is likely to be equal to the corresponding evaluation
F(x)
.
Efficiently evaluating the probabilistically obtained indicator polynomial
\widetilde{F}
on many carefully chosen assignments leads to exponential savings over exhaustive search.

Randomized Isolation Technique of Valiant–Vazirani.

Björklund et al.
[
8
]
observed that the previous approach of Lokshtanov et al.
[
25
]
included a
decision-to-parity reduction
within the algorithm which can also be done on the system of polynomials itself using randomized isolation techniques.
One elegant technique is
Valiant–Vazirani affine hashing

[
34
]
, formally stated in Lemma
2.10
, that probabilistically transforms a polynomial equation system into one that preserves
exactly one solution
of the original system with high probability, if the latter has a solution, and does not add any solutions otherwise.
The isolation technique probabilistically reduces the decision problem
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
to the intermediate problem
\textup{Sum}_{q}^{d}
that computes the
full sum

Z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
over all evaluation points of
F
defined as

Z\coloneqq\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}F(x).

If the new system successfully isolates a single solution, the resulting sum
\widetilde{Z}
is
0
-
1
-valued and equals
1
if and only if the original system has a solution.
For more details, refer to Section
3.1
.
Note that without the isolation technique,
Z
may be zero over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
even if the original system has a solution.

Partial Sum Polynomials.

Another insight of Björklund et al.
[
8
]
is that any given instance of
\textup{Sum}_{q}^{d}
can be reduced to many smaller instances of
\textup{Sum}_{q}^{d}
.
Each of these instances is obtained by fixing a subset of variables to a particular value.
More precisely, let
\beta=\lceil\kappa n\rceil
for an appropriately chosen
\kappa\in(0,1)
and partition the variables into two disjoint subsets of size
n-\beta
and
\beta
, respectively.
This allows us to express the full sum as
Z=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-\beta}}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta}}F(y,z)
.
For all fixed
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-\beta}
, define
Z_{\beta}(y)
as the
partial sum

Z_{\beta}(y)\coloneqq\sum_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta}}F(y,z)\,,

which can be interpreted as a polynomial
Z_{\beta}
over the variables
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-\beta}
(see Section
3.2
) and can be computed recursively.

How does this setup help improve the running time?
Clearly, computing
Z
naïvely by evaluating
Z_{\beta}
at all
q^{\beta}
evaluation points and then applying the identity
Z=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-\beta}}Z_{\beta}(y)
in time
\mathcal{O}^{\ast}(q^{n-\beta})
is no better than an
\mathcal{O}^{\ast}(q^{n})
-time exhaustive search.
However, the crucial insight is that the Razborov–Smolensky technique can be applied to obtain probabilistic polynomials
\widetilde{P}_{i}
, leading to probabilistic indicator and partial sum polynomials
\widetilde{F}
and
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}
of lower degree.

Error Correction via Plurality Votes.

Since we use the technique of Razborov–Smolensky, the obtained probabilistic polynomials
\widetilde{P}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
are
with high probability
correct for each assignment.
Let
\Delta\in\mathbb{N}
be the degree of the corresponding indicator polynomial
\widetilde{F}
.
By definition, the corresponding partial sum polynomials
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}
have degree at most
\Delta
.
Consequently, we can uniquely determine
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}
by its evaluations for all
{\Delta}
-bounded points in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-\beta}
, using known techniques for interpolation (see the paragraph on fast trimmed interpolation and multipoint evaluation below, as well as Section
2.5
).

However, assuming we obtain all necessary evaluations of
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}
, it is not possible to sum over them directly to get the full sum with high probability:
While Razborov–Smolensky guarantees that the polynomial
Z_{\beta}
is point-wise correct with high probability, it in general has lower degree than the original partial sum polynomial, so we cannot expect it to be correct for all assignments with non-zero probability.
Therefore, we boost the probability of success by independently repeating
t\approx n
times the computation of the partial sum polynomial
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}
.
Separately for all evaluation points of
Z_{\beta}
, we compute
plurality votes
over the
t
independently obtained evaluations of
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}
, which is very likely to correct all potential errors, and we have
\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-\beta}}\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}(y)=Z
with high probability.
Refer to Section
3.4
for more details on the error probability of our algorithm.

Reduced Number of Evaluations via Symbolic Interpolation.

Another observation of Björklund et al.
[
8
]
is that for every fixed
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-\beta}
, the computation of
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}(y)
can be further simplified to

\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}(y)=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta}}\left(\widetilde{F}_{1}(y)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{\beta}z_{i}^{q-1}\right)=(q-1)^{\beta}\cdot\widetilde{F}_{1}(y)

for an appropriately chosen polynomial
\widetilde{F}_{1}
.
Refer to Lemma
2.4
for the definition of
\widetilde{F}_{1}
.
This can be seen as a
symbolic interpolation
, since the polynomial
\widetilde{F}_{1}
can be viewed as the
symbolic coefficient
of the monomial
\prod_{i=1}^{\beta}z_{i}^{q-1}
in
\widetilde{F}
.
In other words, this reduces the number of points on which the partial sum polynomial
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}
is evaluated on in Lemma
3.5
to all
({\Delta}-\beta)
-bounded points in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta}
instead of all
\Delta
-bounded points in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta}
.

Efficient Computation of Partial Sum Polynomials.

The key insight of Dinur
[
16
]
is that all of the smaller instances of
\textup{Sum}_{q}^{d}
are actually
related
and solving them independently like Björklund et al.
[
8
]
is suboptimal.
Let
\beta^{\prime}=\beta-\lceil\lambda n\rceil
for an appropriately chosen
\lambda\in(0,1)
, then the variable subset of size
\beta
is further divided into two disjoint subsets of size
\beta-\beta^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime}
.
How does this additional variable partition relate to Dinur’s insight?
Let
\Delta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
and define the degree-
\Delta^{\prime}
partial sum polynomial as

\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime}}(y,u)\coloneqq\sum_{v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta^{\prime}}}\widetilde{F}\big((y,u),v\big)

for all fixed
(y,u)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-\beta^{\prime}}
.
This nicely illustrates the fact that all smaller instances of
\textup{Sum}_{q}^{d}
(each instance corresponds to a fixed assignment
(y,u)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-\beta^{\prime}}
) are related, because all instances are asking for sums over all evaluations on assignments
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta^{\prime}}
of the
same
polynomial.
Instead of considering all assignments of
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta^{\prime}}
independently for every single instance, it is more efficient to consider them
exactly once
for all
(y,u)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-\beta^{\prime}}
combined (for more details, see Section
3.3
).
This leads to the idea to have our recursive algorithm compute the polynomial
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}
(followed by
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime}}
and so on) as a whole, whose evaluations provide us with
all
the required partial sums.

Recall that
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime}}
of degree
\Delta^{\prime}
is uniquely determined by its evaluations for all
{\Delta^{\prime}}
-bounded points in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta^{\prime}}
.
As a consequence, the evaluation of
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime}}
for all
\Delta^{\prime}
-bounded points
(y,u)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-\beta^{\prime}}
takes a
single recursive call
to the algorithm that we are constructing.
This stands in stark contrast to the exponentially many recursive calls necessary in the algorithm of Björklund et al.
[
8
]
.
For a detailed visualization of the recursion schemes of Björklund et al.
[
8
]
and Dinur
[
16
]
and by extension our algorithm refer to Figure
2
.

Fast Trimmed Interpolation and Multipoint Evaluation.

As further explained in Section
2.5
, there are a variety of interpolation and multipoint evaluation algorithms that allow us to switch between the evaluation and the coefficient representation of a polynomial.
The recursion scheme introduced by Dinur
[
16
]
repeatedly interpolates and evaluates the
\Delta^{\prime}
-bounded polynomial
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime}}
on
exactly
the set of degree-bounded points that is required to uniquely determine
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime}}
.
Computing the full evaluation representation on every recursion level like Björklund et al.
[
8
]
is suboptimal.
Instead, we use the
fast trimmed interpolation and multipoint evaluation
algorithms by Van der Hoeven and Schost
[
37
]
with a running time that is linear in the number of provided evaluations or coefficients of a polynomial, respectively (refer to Lemma
2.9
).

More precisely, we compute the coefficient representation of
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime}}
from its evaluations for all
\Delta^{\prime}
-bounded points
(y,u)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-\beta^{\prime}}
in the recursive call.
With its coefficient representation, we can further evaluate
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime}}
on all points
\mathbb{F}_{q\downarrow\Delta}^{n-\beta}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta-\beta^{\prime}}
, where
\mathbb{F}_{q\downarrow\Delta}^{n-\beta}
is the set of
\Delta
-bounded points in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-\beta}
.
After using plurality votes to correct possible mistakes, we compute the evaluations of the polynomial

\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}(y)\coloneqq\sum_{u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta-\beta^{\prime}}}\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime}}(y,u)

on all
\Delta
-bounded points
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-\beta}
and finally interpolate to obtain the polynomial
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}
.
This polynomial is with high probability identical to the partial sum polynomial
Z_{\beta}
.

In this section, we introduce the required definitions and preliminary results that allow us to construct our main algorithm and prove its error probability and running time.

, see Section

.

2.1
Chernoff Bounds.

We use the following standard Chernoff bound.

Lemma 2.1
(e.g., Mitzenmacher and Upfal
[
26
, Theorem 4.5]
)

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent random variables on
\{0,1\}
and let
X=X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}
.
For all
\delta
with
0<\delta<1

2.2
Polynomial Rings.

In this paper, we consider polynomials in the polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
for some fixed prime power
q
.
If
q
is prime, Fermat’s little theorem states
a^{q}=a
for every
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
; equivalently,
a^{q-1}=1
holds for all
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
.
This is known to generalize to prime powers
q
by applying Lagrange’s Theorem (see Lang
[
24
, Chapter I, Proposition 2.2]
) to the subgroup of elements generated by
a
.
Thus, we can restrict our attention to polynomials that have degree at most
q-1
in each variable.
We use the following corollary to Fermat’s little theorem.

Lemma 2.2

For all prime powers
q
and all
k\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}
, we have

(2.1)

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}x^{k}=\begin{cases}q-1&\text{if $k=q-1$, and}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

For
k=0
, we have
x^{k}=1
for all
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and thus
\sum_{x}x^{0}=q=0
holds as claimed.
For
k>0
, we have
0^{k}=0
, so only the
q-1
summands with
x\neq 0
contribute to the sum.
In particular, for
k=q-1
, Fermat’s little theorem implies
x^{q-1}=1
for all
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
x\neq 0
, and thus
\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}x^{q-1}=q-1
.

Now suppose
1\leq k\leq q-2
.
Note that the multiplicative group
\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
is cyclic (see Lang
[
24
, Chapter IV, Corollary 1.10]
), that is, it is generated by a single element
g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
.
Consequently, we have

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}x^{k}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}}x^{k}=\sum_{i\in\{0,\dots,q-2\}}\lparen g^{i}\rparen^{k}=\sum_{i\in\{0,\dots,q-2\}}\lparen g^{k}\rparen^{i}=\frac{\lparen g^{k}\rparen^{q-1}-1}{g^{k}-1}=\frac{1-1}{g^{k}-1}=0\,.

Here, the penultimate equality again follows from Fermat’s little theorem.
Also note
g^{k}\neq 1
for
k<q-1
, because
g
generates
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
This concludes the proof of the lemma.

For a vector
M\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{n}
, we write
X^{M}
for the monomial
\prod_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{M_{i}}
, and analogously for an assignment
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
to the vector of variables
X
.
Each
n
-variate polynomial
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
has the form

P(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=\sum_{M\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{n}}c_{M}X^{M}

for some values
c_{M}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
The values
c_{M}
are called
coefficients
of
P
.

In our algorithm, we will compute the sum
\sum_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}P(a)
over all evaluation points
a
of a polynomial
P
.
The following lemma implies that this is equivalent to determining the coefficient of the monomial
\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{q-1}
in
P
. That is, only this one coefficient contributes to the sum
\sum_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}P(a)
and all other coefficients cancel out.

Lemma 2.3

Let
q
be a prime power and
M\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{M_{i}}=\begin{cases}(q-1)^{n},&\text{if }M_{i}=q-1\text{ for all }i\in[n]\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We prove the statement by induction. For
n=1
, the statement is that of Lemma
2.2
.
Assume the statement holds for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
M\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{n+1}
and observe that distributivity implies the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n+1}}\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}^{M_{i}}=\left(\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}y^{M_{n+1}}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{M_{i}}\right).

The statement follows by applying Lemma
2.2
to the first and the induction hypothesis to the second factor of the product of sums above.

With the insights of Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.3
, we can show that for every fixed
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{1}}
the sum
\sum_{b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{2}}}P(a,b)
over all evaluation points
b
can be computed by
(q-1)^{n_{2}}\cdot P_{1}(X)
, where
P_{1}(X)
is obtained from
P
by setting certain coefficients to
0
.
Since the polynomial
P_{1}
can be seen as the symbolic coefficient of the monomial
\prod_{i=1}^{n_{2}}b_{i}^{q-1}
in
P
, this can be seen as a symbolic interpolation.

Lemma 2.4
(Symbolic Interpolation)

Let
q
be a prime power,
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
,
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n_{1}})
,
Y=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n_{2}})
,
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]
and let
c_{M}
for
M\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}
be the coefficients of
P
.
Define

P_{1}(X)\coloneqq\sum_{M\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{n_{1}}\times\{q-1\}^{n_{2}}}c_{M}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n_{1}}X_{i}^{M_{i}}\,.

Then we have

P_{1}(X)=(q-1)^{n_{2}}\cdot\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{2}}}P(X,y)\,.

Define the set

\mathrm{Mon}_{0}\coloneqq\{M\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\mid\text{there is }i\in[n_{2}]\text{ with }M_{n_{1}+i}\neq q-1\}

and the polynomial
P_{0}(X,Y)\coloneqq\sum_{M\in\mathrm{Mon}_{0}}c_{M}(X,Y)^{M}
.
Now for any fixed
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{1}}
, the sum of all evaluations of
P
under the partial assignment
x
can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{2}}}P(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{2}}}P_{0}(x,y)+\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{2}}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n_{2}}y_{i}^{q-1}\cdot P_{1}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{2}}}P_{0}(x,y)+P_{1}(x)\cdot\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{2}}}\prod_{i=1}^{n_{2}}y_{i}^{q-1}.

We now show that the first sum is actually equal to
0
. To this end, we write

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{2}}}P_{0}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{2}}}\left(\sum_{M\in\mathrm{Mon}_{0}}c_{M}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n_{1}}x_{i}^{M_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{n_{2}}y^{M_{n_{1}+i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\in\mathrm{Mon}_{0}}c_{M}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n_{1}}x_{i}^{M_{i}}\cdot\left(\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{2}}}\prod_{i=1}^{n_{2}}y_{i}^{M_{n_{1}+i}}\right).

Using the fact that every non-zero monomial of
P_{0}
has at least one variable in
Y
whose exponent is not
q-1
, the innermost sum is
0
by Lemma
2.3
.
Hence, the whole sum is equal to
0
, and we obtain

\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{2}}}P(x,y)=P_{1}(x)\cdot\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{2}}}\prod_{i=1}^{n_{2}}y_{i}^{q-1}=P_{1}(x)\cdot(q-1)^{n_{2}},

where the last equation is obtained by applying Lemma
2.3
again.
The desired result follows from the fact that
q-1
is idempotent in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

2.3
Approximation of the Number of Monomials.

For the running time analysis of our algorithm, we bound the number of different monomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
with degree exactly
\Delta
.
This number is also known as the
extended binomial coefficient

\binom{n}{\Delta}_{q}
.
Eger
[
17
, Equation (2)]
defined this number formally using multinomial coefficients.

Definition 2.5
(Eger
[
17
]
)

For
k_{1},\dots,k_{q}\in\mathbb{N}
,
the multinomial coefficient
\binom{n}{k_{1},\dots,k_{q}}
satisfies

(2.2)

\displaystyle\binom{n}{k_{1},\dots,k_{q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{k_{1}!\cdot\dots\cdot k_{q}!}\,.

We define the extended binomial coefficient
\binom{n}{\Delta}_{q}
via

(2.3)

\displaystyle\binom{n}{\Delta}_{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{q}}\binom{n}{k_{1},\dots,k_{q}}\,,

where the sum is taken over all
k_{1},\dots,k_{q}\in\mathbb{N}
that satisfy the constraints
\sum_{i=1}^{q}k_{i}=n
and
\sum_{i=1}^{q}k_{i}\cdot(i-1)=\Delta
.
For notational convenience, we also define
\binom{n}{\downarrow\Delta}_{q}
via

(2.4)

\displaystyle\binom{n}{\downarrow\Delta}_{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\Delta}\binom{n}{k}_{q}\,.

Each multinomial coefficient
\binom{n}{k_{1},\dots,k_{q}}
on the right side of (
2.3
) corresponds to the number of monomials over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
that, for all
i\in\{1,\dots,q\}
, have
k_{i}
variables of individual degree
i-1
.
Thus,

\binom{n}{\Delta}_{q}
is the number of monomials with total degree exactly
\Delta
and

\binom{n}{\downarrow\Delta}_{q}
is the number of monomials with total degree at most
\Delta
.
For
q=2
, we observe
\binom{n}{\Delta}_{2}=\binom{n}{\Delta}
, and thus we obtain the classical binomial coefficient.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q\downarrow\Delta}^{n}
be the set of vectors
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
that satisfy
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\leq\Delta
over
\mathbb{N}
. Then each such vector corresponds to a monomial from
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
of degree at most
\Delta
, and we have
\binom{n}{\downarrow\Delta}_{q}=\lvert\mathbb{F}_{q\downarrow\Delta}^{n}\rvert
.
Moreover, it is easy to see that
\binom{n}{\Delta}_{q}
is increasing in
n
and increasing in
\Delta
for
\Delta\in\{0,\dots,\lfloor n(q-1)/2\rfloor\}
, and that the symmetry
\binom{n}{\Delta}_{q}=\binom{n}{n-\Delta}_{q}
holds.
Furthermore, we have
\binom{n}{\Delta}_{q}\leq\binom{n}{\downarrow n}_{q}=q^{n}
.

It is well-known that the classical binomial coefficient can be bounded using the binary entropy function (e.g., see
[
2
, Theorem 1]
):

(2.5)

\displaystyle\binom{n}{\Delta}\leq 2^{n\cdot H(\Delta/n)}\text{ where }H(p)=-p\log_{2}(p)-(1-p)\log_{2}(1-p)\,.

Moreover, this bound is tight up to a factor of
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
(e.g., see
[
2
, Theorem 2]
).

The extended binomial coefficient has an analogous bound, but the bound does not generally have a closed-form expression. The following lemma appears to be folklore (see
[
9
, Proposition 4.12]
).
We provide a proof for completeness; the proof can be seen as a generalization of a known proof for the binomial coefficient (see
[
2
, Proof 1]
).

Lemma 2.6

Let
q\geq 2
be an integer and let
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
.
For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\binom{n}{\downarrow\alpha(q-1)n}_{q}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\inf_{0<x<1}\frac{x^{0}+\dots+x^{q-1}}{x^{\alpha(q-1)}}\right)^{n}=q^{H(q,\alpha)\cdot n}\,,\,\text{where}

\displaystyle H(q,\alpha)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\inf_{\theta<0}\left(-\alpha\theta+\log_{q}\frac{1-q^{\theta q/(q-1)}}{1-q^{\theta/(q-1)}}\right)\,.

We remark that for constant
q
and
\alpha
, as
n
tends to infinity, the bound in Lemma
2.6
is tight up to factors subexponential in
n
, which follows from Cramér’s theorem
[
28
, §2.4]
in the theory of large deviations.

Let
\Delta\coloneqq\alpha(q-1)n
.
Since
\binom{n}{\downarrow\Delta}_{q}
is the number of monomials with total degree at most
\Delta
, it is equal to
q^{n}
times the probability that a uniformly random monomial has total degree at most
\Delta
.
We prove the inequality by bounding this probability.

\displaystyle\binom{n}{\downarrow\Delta}_{q}

\displaystyle=q^{n}\cdot\Pr_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\leq\Delta\right)=q^{n}\cdot\inf_{\theta<0}\;\Pr_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}}\left(e^{\theta\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}}\geq e^{\Delta\theta}\right)

The second equality trivially holds for all
\theta<0
; we apply Markov’s inequality next.

\displaystyle\leq q^{n}\cdot\inf_{\theta<0}\;\frac{\mathbb{E}_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}}\left(e^{\theta\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}}\right)}{e^{\Delta\theta}}

Since all
d_{i}
’s are independent, the expected value is multiplicative.

\displaystyle=q^{n}\cdot\inf_{\theta<0}\;\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{d\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}}\left(e^{\theta d}\right)}{e^{\alpha(q-1)\theta}}\right)^{n}

Next we apply the definition of the expected value, which cancels the
q^{n}
term.

\displaystyle=\left(\inf_{\theta<0}\frac{e^{0\cdot\theta}+\dots+e^{(q-1)\cdot\theta}}{e^{\alpha(q-1)\theta}}\right)^{n}

We substitute
x=e^{\theta}
; by
\theta<0
, we have
x\in(0,1)
.

\displaystyle=\left(\inf_{0<x<1}\frac{x^{0}+\dots+x^{q-1}}{x^{\alpha(q-1)}}\right)^{n}.

This proves the desired inequality.
To prove that this is equal to
q^{H(q,\alpha)\cdot n}
, we use the closed form for
x^{0}+\dots+x^{q-1}
and perform the substitution
x=q^{\theta/(q-1)}
to see the following with a straightforward calculation:

\displaystyle\log_{q}\left\lparen\inf_{0<x<1}\frac{x^{0}+\dots+x^{q-1}}{x^{\alpha(q-1)}}\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\inf_{0<x<1}\log_{q}\left\lparen\frac{1-x^{q}}{(1-x)x^{\alpha(q-1)}}\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\inf_{\theta<0}-\alpha\theta+\log_{q}\left\lparen\frac{1-q^{\theta q/(q-1)}}{1-q^{\theta/(q-1)}}\right\rparen=H(q,\alpha)\,.

This concludes the proof.

We now observe that
H(q,\alpha)
specializes to the binary entropy for
q=2
.

Lemma 2.7

For all
\alpha\in[0,1]
, we have
H(2,\alpha)=H(\alpha)
.

The proof follows from basic calculus. First we define the function

f(\theta)=-\alpha\theta+\log_{2}\left\lparen 1-4^{\theta}\right\rparen-\log_{2}\left\lparen 1-2^{\theta}\right\rparen

and note

H(2,\alpha)=\inf_{\theta<0}f(\theta)
.
Using a computer algebra system such as Wolfram Alpha, we verify

\lim_{\theta\to-\infty}f(\theta)=\infty
and

\lim_{\theta\to 0^{-}}f(\theta)=1
, that the derivative
f^{\prime}(\theta)
is zero if and only if
\theta=\theta^{\ast}\coloneqq\log_{2}(\alpha)-\log_{2}(1-\alpha)<0
holds, and that
f(\theta^{*})\leq 1
holds at this value
\theta^{\ast}
. Thus, this is where the infimum is attained, and we have:

\displaystyle H(2,\alpha)

\displaystyle=f(\theta^{\ast})=-\alpha\log_{2}\left\lparen\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right\rparen+\log_{2}\left\lparen\frac{1-2\alpha}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}\right\rparen-\log_{2}\left\lparen\frac{1-2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right\rparen

\displaystyle=-\alpha\log_{2}(\alpha)-(1-\alpha)\log_{2}(1-\alpha)=H(\alpha)\,.

This concludes the proof.

2.4
Machine Model, Complexity, and Representations of Polynomials.

For our algorithms, we silently use an extension of standard word-RAM machines with words of
\mathcal{O}(\log N)
bits as our machine model, where
N
is the input length.
The time complexity of the machine is defined as usual via the number of elementary operations performed by the machine.
We remark already here that for our main algorithm, a prime power
q
will be fixed, and consequently all arithmetic operations on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
can be performed in constant time.

Since our algorithms use arrays of exponential length and since we will not want to worry about the overhead that this causes, we silently assume that the word-RAM machine in addition has access to an abstract dictionary data structure:
In particular, the algorithm can initialize a new dictionary, read a value from the dictionary, or write a value to the dictionary, and we assume each of these operations to incur unit cost. During the initialization, we can also specify a default value for the dictionary—this value will be returned if we are trying to read the value for a key that has not been written to yet. To allow for keys with
\poly(N)
bits, we assume that the key must be written to a special query array of the word-RAM before the dictionary’s read or write operation is called.

Throughout this paper, we silently represent polynomials using dictionaries.
There are two representations that we will use to store an
n
-variate degree-
d
polynomial
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
:

•

In the
coefficient representation
,
we represent
P
as a dictionary that stores each non-zero coefficient
c_{M}
of
P
under the key
M\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{n}
. Since
P
has degree at most
d
, each key satisfies
\sum_{i}M_{i}\leq d
.

•

In the
evaluation representation
,
we represent
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
as a dictionary that stores evaluations
P(x)
under all keys
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q\downarrow d}^{n}
(recall that this is the set of tuples in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
whose entries sum up to at most
d
over
\mathbb{N}
).

In the next section, we will show how to efficiently switch between these two representations.
If no representation is specified, we silently use the coefficient representation by default.

2.5
Fast Multipoint Evaluation and Interpolation over Finite Fields.

As an important subroutine of Theorem
1.1
, we use a fast algorithm for multipoint evaluation and interpolation for bounded-degree multivariate polynomials over finite fields.
There is a natural bijection between elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q\downarrow\Delta}^{n}
and the monomials of a degree-
\Delta
polynomial
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
.
The polynomial
P
can be represented either by providing the coefficient of each monomial of degree at most
\Delta
, or by providing the evaluations of
P
at all points in
\mathbb{F}_{q\downarrow\Delta}^{n}
.
Moreover, it is possible to efficiently switch between these two representations as is proven in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.9
(Van der Hoeven and Schost
[
37
, Theorem 1]
)

Let
q
be a constant prime power.
Given an integer
b\in\{0,\dots,n\}
and an
n
-variate polynomial
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
with total degree at most
\Delta
, we can compute the vector of evaluations
P(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q\downarrow\Delta}^{n-b}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{b}
in time
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot\lvert\mathbb{F}_{q\downarrow\Delta}^{n-b}\rvert\cdot q^{b})
.
Conversely, given
b
, and a vector of evaluations
P(x)
for all

The first algorithm in the lemma is called

and the second algorithm is called

.
The most natural cases of this lemma are

and

, but we need the more general version for our algorithm.
The original formulation of the lemma in
[

is even more general in that the set of evaluation points can be chosen more flexibly and the field

can have super-constant size—this, however, affects the running time.

For

, Lemma

can be seen as the linear transformation over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
that is known as the Möbius Transform.
Björklund et al.
[

showed that assuming a bounded-degree polynomial, there exists a Trimmed Möbius Transform that only requires a bounded number of evaluations to compute the coefficients of a polynomial (and vice versa).
For

, there are several papers
[

,
23
,
6
,
5
]
that devised algorithms for multipoint evaluation (not interpolation) over

, where the individual degree of each variable is bounded.
In our setting, where only the total degree of monomials is restricted, and the degree of individual variables can be up to

, these multipoint evaluation algorithms have exponential running time.
Instead we use a special case of a multipoint evaluation
[
37
, Theorem 1]
and interpolation
[
37
, Proposition 3]
algorithm (also see
[
35
, Theorem 4.4]
), because our algorithm requires to repeatedly switch between the two representations of a polynomial.

3
Algorithm for Polynomial Equation Systems over Finite Fields

Recall that
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
is the problem of deciding whether some given degree-
d
polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
have a common root, or equivalently, whether the corresponding polynomial equation system has a solution.
For convenience, we restate our main theorem here.
See
1.1

This section is dedicated to constructing the claimed algorithm and proving its claimed properties.
At the end of Section
3.5
we are finally in position to prove the theorem.
For the remainder of this section, we fix
q
and

to be integer constants such that
q
is a prime power and
d\geq 1
.
For the proof, we follow the outline given in Section
1.4
.

3.1
From Equations to a Sum.

We start by reducing the problem of determining whether a given polynomial equation system has a solution to that of computing the sum over all evaluation points of a particular polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
For polynomials
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
, we define the associated
indicator polynomial
F\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
and the
full sum

Z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
as follows:

(3.6)

F\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left\lparen 1-(P_{i})^{q-1}\right\rparen\quad\text{and}\quad Z\coloneqq\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}F(x)\,.

By Fermat’s little theorem, we have
F(x)\neq 0
for a vector
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
if and only if
P_{1}(x)=\dots=P_{m}(x)=0
holds.
Thus, if the polynomials do not have a common root, it is guaranteed that
F
is identically zero and the full sum satisfies
Z=0
.
However, the sum is taken over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and may be zero also if the polynomials do have a common root. We avoid this situation by using the isolation lemma to ensure that
F(x)\neq 0
holds for at most one vector
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.
More formally, we use the following intermediate problem:

Input:

Polynomials
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
of degree at most
d

Question:

Compute the full sum
Z
, where
Z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
is defined as in (
3.6
)

\textup{Sum}_{q}^{d}

We use the isolation lemma to efficiently reduce from
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
to
\textup{Sum}_{q}^{d}
.

Lemma 3.1

If
\textup{Sum}_{q}^{d}
can be computed in bounded-error randomized time

, then
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
can be computed in bounded-error randomized time
\mathcal{O}(nT(n,m+n))
.

Let
A
be a bounded-error randomized algorithm for
\textup{Sum}_{q}^{d}
.
We use the isolation lemma, Lemma
2.10
, and add up to
n
random linear equations to the polynomial equation system. We then feed the at most
m+n
resulting polynomials as input to

. If the original system did not have any solutions, the new system does not have a solution either, and so

returns
0
with high probability.
However, if the original system has at least one solution, then the new system has a unique solution with probability
\Omega(\tfrac{1}{n})
, in which case

returns

By repeating this procedure and thus making
\mathcal{O}(n)
independent queries to

as required.

3.2
From a Sum to a Partial Sum.

In the following, we will describe how to compute

One straightforward way to do this would be to use Multipoint Evaluation, that is, to evaluate

at
all
points

and compute the sum.
Doing so would take time
\Omega(q^{n})
and provide no gains over exhaustive search.
The main idea for computing the full sum

more efficiently is to iteratively compute polynomials
Z_{\beta}
that represent partial sums:

(3.7)

Z_{\beta}(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n-\beta})\coloneqq\sum_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta}}F(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n-\beta},z)\,.

In this expression, the first
n-\beta

for the last
\beta

In particular, we have
Z_{0}=F
and
Z_{n}=Z
.
We remark that these partial sums can also be defined via partial derivatives of

\frac{\partial^{q-1}}{\partial x_{n}^{q-1}}F=(q-1)(q-2)\cdots 2\cdot Z_{1}
.

, we describe a randomized algorithm

3.3
Algorithm for Partial Sums

In this section, we describe the algorithm
PartialSum
for computing
Z_{\beta}
.
We stress that the goal of this algorithm is to compute a representation of all monomial-coefficient pairs for which the coefficient is non-zero.
To get an algorithm that is more efficient than brute force, we need two key insights.
The first is that we can bound the degree of
Z_{\beta}
from above as observed in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5

Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}
be
n
-variate degree-
d

If

has degree at most
\Delta_{m,\beta}

\Delta_{m,\beta}\coloneqq(\min(md,n)-\beta)(q-1)

The degree of
F
is trivially at most
n(q-1)

F
is an
n

\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Moreover, the degree of
F
is at most
md(q-1)
, because we have
F=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1-P_{i}^{q-1})
and the degree of each
P_{i}
is bounded by
d
.
Let
c_{M}
for
M\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{n}
be the coefficients of

F_{1}(Y)\coloneqq\sum_{M\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{n-\beta}\times\{q-1\}^{\beta}}c_{M}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n-\beta}Y_{i}^{M_{i}}.

By Lemma
2.4
, we have
F_{1}=(q-1)^{\beta}\cdot Z_{\beta}

F_{1}
and

have the same degree.

Consider any monomial

By definition of

Since the degree of

is at most the degree of

As this applies to every monomial
M
of

\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}(y)=\sum_{u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta-\beta^{\prime}}}\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime}}(y,u)=\sum_{u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta-\beta^{\prime}}}Z_{\beta^{\prime}}(y,u).

Since
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}
is constructed in line
19
by interpolating from evaluations on
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q\downarrow\Delta}^{n-\beta}
, the polynomial
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}
must have degree at most
\Delta
by Lemma
2.9
.
Again by Lemma
2.9
, as the two degree-
\Delta
polynomials
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}
and
Z_{\beta}
agree on all points in
\mathbb{F}_{q\downarrow\Delta}^{n-\beta}
, they must be identical.
This proves the claim, and we also obtain the contrapositive, that is:
If
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}\neq Z_{\beta}
, there exist
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q\downarrow\Delta}^{n-\beta}
and
u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta-\beta^{\prime}}
such that the event
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime}}(y,u)\neq Z_{\beta^{\prime}}(y,u)
occurs.
We will prove the following regarding the probability of that event:

(3.11)

\text{For all $y\in\mathbb{F}_{q\downarrow\Delta}^{n-\beta}$ and $u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta-\beta^{\prime}}$, we have $\Pr\big\lparen\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime}}(y,u)\neq Z_{\beta^{\prime}}(y,u)\big\rparen\leq q^{-2n}$}\,.

Once this is established, a union bound over the at most
q^{n}
pairs
(y,u)
leads to the claimed final bound:

\Pr\big\lparen\widetilde{Z}_{\beta}\neq Z_{\beta}\big\rparen\leq\Pr\big\lparen\exists y,u\colon\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime}}(y,u)\neq Z_{\beta^{\prime}}(y,u)\big\rparen\leq q^{n-\beta^{\prime}}\cdot q^{-2n}\leq q^{-n}\,.

It remains to prove (
3.11
), so let
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q\downarrow\Delta}^{n-\beta}
and
u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\beta-\beta^{\prime}}
be arbitrary.
Recall that the value
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime}}(y,u)
is constructed in line
17
by a plurality vote over the evaluations
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime},1}(y,u),\dots,\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime},t}(y,u)
.
For each
j\in\{1,\dots,t\}
, we define the random variable
X_{j}
that indicates whether the
j
-th value
\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime},j}(y,u)
in this plurality vote was computed correctly in lines
12
and
13
, that is, we have

X_{j}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime},j}(y,u)=Z_{\beta^{\prime}}(y,u)$;}\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Moreover, let
X\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{t}X_{j}
.
If the plurality fails to produce the correct result
Z_{\beta^{\prime}}(y,u)
, then the event
X\leq t/2
must occur.
By (
3.9
) and (
3.10
), we have
\Pr(X_{j}=0)\leq q^{-2}+q^{-n}\leq 1/3
when
q\geq 2
and
n\geq 4
, and thus
\mathbb{E}(X)\geq\tfrac{2}{3}t
.
Since the random variables are independent and identically distributed, we can apply the Chernoff bound stated in Lemma
2.1
with
\delta=\tfrac{1}{4}
:

\displaystyle\Pr\Big(X\leq\tfrac{t}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\Big(X\leq(1-\tfrac{1}{4})\,\mathbb{E}(X)\Big)\leq\exp\Big(-\tfrac{\tfrac{1}{16}\mathbb{E}(X)}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(-\tfrac{t}{48}\Big)=\exp\Big(-\tfrac{\lceil 96n\ln q\rceil}{48}\Big)\leq q^{-2n}\,.

We obtain
\Pr\big\lparen\widetilde{Z}_{\beta^{\prime}}(y,u)\neq Z_{\beta^{\prime}}(y,u)\big\rparen\leq q^{-2n}
and (
3.11
) follows. This concludes the proof.

(Proof of Theorem

)
We construct our algorithm for

By Lemma

.

Let
0<\lambda\leq\kappa<\frac{1}{2d-1}

is at most
\mathcal{O}^{\ast}(q^{(\zeta_{q,d}(\kappa)+\lambda)\cdot n})

\displaystyle\zeta_{q,d}(\kappa)\coloneqq\max\Big\{1-\kappa,\;\sup_{0\leq\delta\leq\kappa}H(q,\alpha)\cdot(1-\delta)\Big\}\,,\text{ where $\alpha\coloneqq\frac{\delta(d-1)}{1-\delta}$}\,.

It remains to prove the claim on

Recall that

calls

(P_{1},\dots,P_{m};\lfloor\kappa n\rfloor)

is the running time of

.
By Lemma

, the running time of

is
\mathcal{O}^{\ast}(q^{(1-\kappa)n}+T(m,n,\lfloor\kappa n\rfloor))

By

Since the algorithms

and

do not depend on the precise values of

and
\lambda

and

\kappa n
and

are integers, which simplifies notation.
Moreover, we will choose
\kappa,\lambda\geq 0
such that

is an integer.
By (

PartialSum
is at most
\mathcal{O}^{\ast}(\max\{\,T(D)\mid 0\leq D\leq\kappa/\lambda\,\})

By (

.

Indeed, if we write

\widetilde{\Delta}\coloneqq n\delta(d-1)(q-1)+2d(q-1)

Now our assumption

\displaystyle\binom{n(1-\delta)}{\Delta}_{q}\cdot q^{\lambda n}

\displaystyle\leq q^{H(q,\alpha)\cdot(1-\delta)n+\lambda n}\leq q^{(\zeta_{q,d}(\kappa)+\lambda)\cdot n}\,.

Thus, for small enough

To this end, we use the definition of
\zeta_{q,d}(\kappa)

Then

We bound that term as follows:

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq\delta\leq\kappa}H(q,\alpha)\cdot(1-\delta)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{0\leq\delta\leq\kappa}H(q,\alpha)

\displaystyle=H\left\lparen q,\alpha(\kappa)\right\rparen

\displaystyle<H(q,\tfrac{1}{4})=1-{I(q-1,\tfrac{1}{4})}/{\ln q}\,.

4
Conditional Lower Bounds

Our conditional hardness result of Theorem
1.2
relies on a hypothesis by Impagliazzo and Paturi
[
20
]
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
, there is some
k\geq 3
such that
k
-SAT cannot be solved in time
\mathcal{O}(2^{(1-\varepsilon)n})
.

Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH)

In order to prove Theorem
1.2
, we devise a suitable mapping reduction from
k
-SAT to
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
for all fixed
k
.
Recall that a
mapping reduction from
L\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\ast}
to
L^{\prime}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\ast}
is an algorithm
f\colon\{0,1\}^{\ast}\to\{0,1\}^{\ast}
that satisfies
x\in L
if and only if
f(x)\in L^{\prime}
.
Moreover, a mapping reduction is
parsimonious
if it preserves the number of solutions.
It should be noted that we need a somewhat precise bound on the number of variables and cannot just use
\mathcal{O}
-notation, as will become clear in the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 4.1
(Reduction from
k
-SAT to
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
)

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
q
be a prime power and let
\delta>0
be rational.
There is a parsimonious mapping reduction from
k
-SAT
to
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
that is given an
n
-variable
k
-CNF
formula with
m
clauses and produces a polynomial equation system over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with at most
\frac{n}{\log q}\cdot\left(1+\frac{\delta}{2}+o(1)\right)
variables,

m
equations, and degree at most
k\cdot(\frac{2}{\delta}+\frac{1}{\log q}+1)\cdot(q-1)\in\mathcal{O}(1)
.
Moreover, this reduction runs in time
\mathcal{O}(nm)
.

We first show that there is a mapping reduction from
k
-SAT to
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
with the desired properties.
In the end, we argue that this reduction can also be made parsimonious.
Let
\varphi=C_{1}\land\dots\land C_{m}
be a propositional formula in
k
-CNF and let
C_{i}=\ell_{i,1}\lor\dots\lor\ell_{i,k}
for all
i\in[m]
.
We construct a polynomial equation system
E
such that
E
has a solution if and only if
\varphi
is satisfiable.
The idea is to encode blocks of Boolean variables by blocks of variables over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and use interpolation to obtain polynomials that decode these blocks and output the Boolean value of individual variables.
Then, we construct polynomials
P_{i}
for the clauses of
\varphi
such that, for all
i\in[m]
, the polynomial
P_{i}
is
\{0,1\}
-valued and evaluates to
1
on an assignment
\hat{X}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
if and only if the Boolean assignment encoded by
\hat{X}
satisfies
C_{i}
.

We now give the details.
Let
\mathsf{vars}_{1}\coloneqq\lceil\frac{2}{\delta}\cdot\log q\rceil
and
\mathsf{blocks}\coloneqq\lceil\frac{n}{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}\rceil
.
For simplicity of the construction, we assume that
\varphi
has exactly
\mathsf{blocks}\cdot\mathsf{vars}_{1}
variables by introducing additional dummy variables if necessary.
We will still consider
n
to be the original number of variables, allowing us to precisely analyze the parameters of the reduction.
Furthermore, we assume without loss of generality that the variables in
\varphi
are named
x=x_{1,1},\dots,x_{1,\mathsf{vars}_{1}},\dots,x_{\mathsf{blocks},1},\dots,x_{\mathsf{blocks},\mathsf{vars}_{1}}
, that is, the variables are grouped into
\mathsf{blocks}
many blocks of
\mathsf{vars}_{1}
variables each.
Each block of
\mathsf{vars}_{1}
Boolean variables is now encoded by a block of variables over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of suitable arity.
This arity will be
\mathsf{vars}_{2}\coloneqq\lceil\frac{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}{\log q}\rceil
, which is sufficient to encode all assignments to the block of Boolean variables, as we have

q^{\mathsf{vars}_{2}}=2^{\log q\cdot\mathsf{vars}_{2}}\geq 2^{\log q\cdot\frac{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}{\log q}}=2^{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}.

Now, fix an encoding by choosing any efficiently computable surjective
\{0,1\}
-valued function

\mathrm{dec}\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\mathsf{vars}_{2}}\to\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}.

For example,
\mathrm{dec}
can be chosen as the function mapping any tuple
X\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\mathsf{vars}_{2}}
to the binary encoding of the number represented by
X
when interpreted as a base-
q
number, modulo
2^{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}
.
For all
v_{1}\in[\mathsf{vars}_{1}]
, let
\mathrm{DEC}_{v_{1}}
be the
\mathsf{vars}_{2}
-variate polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
that agrees with the
v_{1}
-th bit of
\mathrm{dec}
on all inputs.

To express the constraints imposed by the clauses
C_{i}
using polynomial equations, define for all
i\in[m]
and
j\in[k]
the polynomial

L_{i,j}(Y)=\begin{cases}Y,&\text{if }\ell_{i,j}\text{ is a positive literal}\\
1-Y,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We now construct the desired polynomial equation system
E
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
The variables of
E
will be
X=X_{1,1},\dots,X_{1,\mathsf{vars}_{2}},\dots,X_{\mathsf{blocks},1},\dots,X_{\mathsf{blocks},\mathsf{vars}_{2}}
, where the block
X_{b,1},\dots,X_{b,\mathsf{vars}_{2}}
encodes the block
x_{b,1},\dots,x_{b,\mathsf{vars}_{1}}
of Boolean variables for all
b\in[\mathsf{blocks}]
.
For any
i\in[m]
and
j\in[k]
, let
b(i,j)
be the index of the block of the variable occurring in the literal
\ell_{i,j}
and let
v_{1}(i,j)
be its position inside that block.
For example, if
\ell_{i,j}=\neg x_{1,3}
, then
b(i,j)=1
and
v_{1}(i,j)=3
.
Consider the polynomial

Q_{i,j}(X)\coloneqq L_{i,j}(\mathrm{DEC}_{v_{1}(i,j)}(X_{b(i,j),1},\dots,X_{b(i,j),\mathsf{vars}_{2}})).

This polynomial uses
\mathrm{DEC}_{v_{1}(i,j)}
to obtain the value of the Boolean variable
x_{b(i,j),v_{1}(i,j)}
in the Boolean assignment encoded by the variables
X_{1,1},\dots,X_{1,\mathsf{vars}_{2}},\dots,X_{\mathsf{blocks},1},\dots,X_{\mathsf{blocks},\mathsf{vars}_{2}}
and possibly negates it depending on whether
\ell_{i,j}
is positive or negative.
To make this formal, fix an assignment
\hat{X}=\hat{X}_{1,1},\dots,\hat{X}_{1,\mathsf{vars}_{2}},\dots,\hat{X}_{\mathsf{blocks},1},\dots,\hat{X}_{\mathsf{blocks},\mathsf{vars}_{2}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\mathsf{blocks}\cdot\mathsf{vars}_{2}}
.
Then
\mathrm{dec}(\hat{X})\coloneqq\mathrm{dec}(\hat{X}_{1,1},\dots,\hat{X}_{1,\mathsf{vars}_{2}})\circ\dots\circ\mathrm{dec}(\hat{X}_{\mathsf{blocks},1},\dots,\hat{X}_{\mathsf{blocks},\mathsf{vars}_{2}})
is the Boolean assignment encoded by
\hat{X}
, and we have
Q_{i,j}(\hat{X})\in\{0,1\}
as well as
Q_{i,j}(\hat{X})=0
if and only if
\mathrm{dec}(\hat{X})\models\ell_{i,j}
.
Finally, for any
i\in[m]
, define the polynomial

P_{i}(X)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}Q_{i,j}(X).

Now for all
\hat{X}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\mathsf{blocks}\cdot\mathsf{vars}_{2}}
, we have that
P_{i}(\hat{X})\in\{0,1\}
and
P_{i}(\hat{X})=0
if and only if
\mathrm{dec}(\hat{X})\models C_{i}
.
Consequently, the polynomial equation system
E\coloneqq\{P_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
has a solution if and only if
\varphi
is satisfiable.

We now verify that
E
has the claimed properties and can be constructed in the claimed running time.
Note that by definition and the fact that
k
,
q
, and
\delta
are considered to be constant,
\mathsf{vars}_{1}
and
\mathsf{vars}_{2}
are constant, and
\mathsf{blocks}
is linear in
n
.
By construction,
E
consists of exactly
m
polynomial equations and uses at most
\mathsf{blocks}\cdot\mathsf{vars}_{2}
variables.
This directly yields the desired bound:

\displaystyle\mathsf{blocks}\cdot\mathsf{vars}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\frac{n}{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}\right\rceil\cdot\left\lceil\frac{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}{\log q}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{n}{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}+1\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}{\log q}+1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{\log q}+\frac{n}{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}+\frac{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}{\log q}+1

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{\log q}\cdot\left(1+\frac{\log q}{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}+\frac{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}{n}+\frac{\log q}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\in\frac{n}{\log q}\cdot\left(1+\frac{\delta}{2}+o(1)\right).

The degree of any polynomial
\mathrm{DEC}_{v_{1}}
for
v_{1}\in[\mathsf{vars}_{1}]
is trivially bounded by
\mathsf{vars}_{2}\cdot(q-1)
, as it is a
\mathsf{vars}_{2}
-variate polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
The same is true for the polynomial
Q_{i,j}
for any
i\in[m]
and
j\in[k]
(the remaining variables are unused in
Q_{i,j}
).
Consequently,
P_{i}
is of degree at most
k\cdot\mathsf{vars}_{2}\cdot(q-1)\leq k\cdot(\frac{2}{\delta}+\frac{1}{\log q}+1)\cdot(q-1)\in\mathcal{O}(1)
for all
i\in[m]
.

Finally, we analyze the running time of the reduction.
For any
i\in[m]
, the polynomial
P_{i}
can be viewed as a polynomial with at most
k\cdot\mathsf{vars}_{2}
variables, and can be interpolated from all of its evaluations on arbitrary assignments to those variables using Lemma
2.9
.
To obtain these evaluations, we compute all relevant evaluations of
Q_{i,j}
for all
j\in[k]
, that is, all evaluations for arbitrary assignments to the variables occurring in
Q_{i,j}
but some fixed assignment for the variables not occurring.
Any evaluation of
P_{i}
can then be computed as the product of the corresponding evaluations of the
Q_{i,j}
.

To obtain the evaluations of
Q_{i,j}
for all
i\in[m]
and
j\in[k]
, we first need to compute all evaluations of
\mathrm{DEC}_{v_{1}}
for all
v_{1}\in[\mathsf{vars}_{1}]
.
Using the definition of
\mathrm{dec}
suggested above, this can be done as follows:
Simultaneously count from
0
to
q^{\mathsf{vars}_{2}}-1
in base
q
using variables
q_{0},\dots,q_{\mathsf{vars}_{2}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and in base
2
(modulo
2^{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}
) using variables
d_{0},\dots,d_{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}\in\{0,1\}
and for each step, take
d_{v_{1}}
as the evaluation of
\mathrm{DEC}_{v_{1}}
on input
(q_{0},\dots,q_{\mathsf{vars}_{2}})
for all
v_{1}\in[\mathsf{vars}_{1}]
.
This takes time
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{vars}_{1}\cdot q^{\mathsf{vars}_{2}})
.

The polynomial
Q_{i,j}
is now treated as a
\mathsf{vars}_{2}
-variate polynomial (leaving out all unused variables).
Each evaluation of
Q_{i,j}
is now simply obtained by a lookup in the correct evaluation of the correct polynomial
\mathrm{DEC}_{v_{1}}
and potentially a negation.
Hence, all evaluations of a single
Q_{i,j}
can be obtained in time
\mathcal{O}(q^{\mathsf{vars}_{2}})
.
(Here, we assume that a single literal
\ell_{i,j}
can be read from the input in constant time.
If this is not possible a priori, we can prepare a data structure for it with linear pre-processing.)
In consequence, all evaluations of all polynomials
Q_{i,j}
for
i\in[m]
and
j\in[k]
can be obtained in time
\mathcal{O}(mk\cdot q^{\mathsf{vars}_{2}})
.

Now, for any
i\in[m]
, the evaluation of
P_{i}
for a single assignment
X
for the variables occurring in
P_{i}
can be computed in time
k
by computing the product
\prod_{j=1}^{k}Q_{i,j}(X)
.
(Here,
Q_{i,j}
is treated as a polynomial with the same variables as
P_{i}
, so one has to project the assignment
X
to the correct subset of variables for the lookup.)
The polynomial
P_{i}
can now be interpolated in time
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{blocks}\cdot\mathsf{vars}_{2}\cdot q^{k\cdot\mathsf{vars}_{2}\cdot(q-1)})
using Lemma
2.9
.
Hence, this step takes time
\mathcal{O}(m\cdot\mathsf{blocks}\cdot\mathsf{vars}_{2}\cdot q^{k\cdot\mathsf{vars}_{2}\cdot(q-1)})
for the whole system
E=(P_{i})_{i\in[m]}
.

In total, this means that the running time is bounded by

\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{vars}_{1}\cdot q^{\mathsf{vars}_{2}}+mk\cdot q^{\mathsf{vars}_{2}}+m\cdot\mathsf{blocks}\cdot\mathsf{vars}_{2}\cdot q^{k\cdot\mathsf{vars}_{2}\cdot(q-1)})\subseteq\mathcal{O}(nm).

Finally, the above reduction can be strengthened to a parsimonious reduction by enforcing a bound on the possible assignments to any block of variables over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and ensuring that the decoding function restricted to the possible assignments is a bijection.
With this modification, there is a
1
-to-
1
correspondence between satisfying assignments of the original formula and solutions for the constructed polynomial equation system.

This bound can be realized by constructing a
\mathsf{vars}_{2}
-variate polynomial
B
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
that expresses that the number represented by the given block when interpreted as a base-
q
number is less than
2^{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}
.
For this, use the same approach as for constructing the polynomials
\mathrm{DEC}_{\mathsf{vars}_{1}}
:
List all of its evaluations and interpolate using Lemma
2.9
.
Finally, let
\mathrm{BOUND}_{b}(X)
be the composition of
B
with a projection to the
b
-th block of
X
.
Now the desired polynomial equation system is
\{P_{1},\dots,P_{m},\mathrm{BOUND}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{BOUND}_{\mathsf{blocks}}\}
.

4.1
Lower Bounds for
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.2
as a direct corollary to Lemma
4.1
.

See
1.2

We prove the contrapositive.
Assume that there exists a prime power
q
and rational
\delta>0
such that, for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
, there is an
\mathcal{O}(q^{(1-\delta)n})
-time algorithm for
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
.
We show that this contradicts SETH.

Let
\varepsilon=\delta/2
, and let
k\geq 3
be any integer.
We now devise an
\mathcal{O}(2^{(1-\varepsilon)n})
-time algorithm for
k
-SAT, contradicting SETH.
The algorithm is given any
n
-variable
k
-CNF formula
\varphi
, uses Lemma
4.1
to reduce it to an equivalent instance
E
of
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
for
d=k\cdot\left\lceil\frac{\lceil\frac{2}{\delta}\cdot\log q\rceil}{\log q}\right\rceil\cdot(q-1)
, and finally applies the assumed algorithm for
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
.
As
k
,
q
and
\delta
are constant in this setting, the reduction takes time
\mathcal{O}(nm)
, where
m
is the number of clauses in
\varphi
, by Lemma
4.1
.
Furthermore, the polynomial equation system
E
has at most
\frac{n}{\log q}\cdot\left(1+\frac{\delta}{2}+o(1)\right)
variables.
In consequence, the final application of the assumed algorithm for
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
takes time

\mathcal{O}\left(q^{(1-\delta)\cdot\frac{n}{\log q}\cdot\left(1+\frac{\delta}{2}+o(1)\right)}\right)\leq\mathcal{O}\left(2^{(1-\delta)\cdot\left(1+\frac{\delta}{2}+o(1)\right)\cdot n}\right).

To finish the proof, we show that this is in
\mathcal{O}(2^{(1-\varepsilon)n})
.
Indeed, we have:

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\cdot(1+\frac{\delta}{2}+o(1))

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\delta}{2}+o(1)-\delta-\delta\cdot\left(\frac{\delta}{2}+o(1)\right)

\displaystyle=1-\delta\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}+o(1)\right)+o(1).

Now for
\varepsilon^{\prime}\coloneqq\delta\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}+o(1)\right)
we have
\varepsilon<\varepsilon^{\prime}
, which means that the running time is

\mathcal{O}(2^{(1-\varepsilon^{\prime}+o(1))n})\leq\mathcal{O}(2^{(1-\varepsilon)n}).

This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

4.2
Lower Bound for Counting the Roots of a Polynomial.

Lemma
4.1
is a mapping reduction that is
parsimonious
, that is, it preserves the number of solutions.
Since the proof of Theorem
1.2
only deals with the parameters of Lemma
4.1
and the running times, the theorem can be safely lifted to its counting version.
That is, if #SETH holds, then #
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
cannot be solved fast.
Here, #SETH is the counting variant of SETH, which states that counting the number of satisfying assignments to
k
-CNF formulas cannot be done fast, and #
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
is the problem of computing the number of solutions to an instance of
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
.

If the number of equations in a given instance of #
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
is
1
, then the problem is simply to compute the number of roots of the one given polynomial.
For a polynomial
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
, any vector
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with
P(x)=0
is called a
root
of the polynomial.
For convenience, we define the problem
\textup{\#Roots}_{q}^{d}
as the special case of #
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
with
m=1
:

Input:

Polynomial
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
of degree at most
d

Question:

How many roots does
P
have?

\textup{\#Roots}_{q}^{d}

In a beautiful paper and somewhat surprisingly, Williams
[
39
]
was able to reduce from #
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
to
\textup{\#Roots}_{q}^{D}
for some
D\geq d
.
The following reduction is implicit in
[
39
, proof of Theorem 4]
.

Lemma 4.2
(Williams
[
39
]
)

Let
q
be a prime power and
d,B,C\in\mathbb{N}
.
For every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a constant
D\in\mathbb{N}
and an oracle reduction from
\#\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
to
\textup{\#Roots}_{q}^{D}
, such that on input
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
, the reduction makes queries to polynomials
Z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
and satisfies the following property:
If every polynomial
P_{i}
only depends on at most
B
variables, then these queries
Z
have degree at most
D\coloneqq BC\cdot(q-1)
and the reduction runs in time
\mathcal{O}(q^{m/C}\cdot(m+n))
.

Williams
[
39
]
used Lemma
4.2
to show that
\textup{\#Roots}_{q}^{d}
does not have algorithms that run in time
\mathcal{O}(2^{(1-\delta)n})
, unless #SETH is false.
This hardness result is only tight for
q=2
, and we extend it to a tight hardness result for arbitrary prime powers
q
.
See
1.3

In order to prove Theorem
1.3
, we follow the same plan as Williams’ proof of his #SETH lower bound: we reduce from #
k
-SAT via #
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
to
\textup{\#Roots}_{q}^{D}
, but we replace the first reduction with Lemma
4.1
as a key missing component.
More precisely, starting from #
k
-SAT, we apply the counting version of the sparsification lemma
[
21
,
10
,
14
]
to make the number
m
of clauses at most
(k/\varepsilon)^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
.
Next, we apply our efficient reduction of Lemma
4.1
from #
k
-SAT to #
\textup{PES}_{q}^{d}
; the main feature of this reduction is that it compresses
n
Boolean variables from the
k
-CNF formula into roughly
n/\log q
variables over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
in the polynomial equation system.
Finally, we apply Lemma
4.2
to reduce the polynomial equation system to
\textup{\#Roots}_{q}^{D}
; we apply this reduction with parameters satisfying
m/C=\varepsilon n^{\prime}
, where
n^{\prime}
is the number of variables in the original #
k
-SAT formula.
This leads to a running time of
\mathcal{O}(q^{\varepsilon n^{\prime}}\cdot(n+n^{\prime}))
, which is sufficient to obtain the desired lower bound under #SETH.

(Proof of Theorem
1.3
)
We follow the proof outline stated above, so suppose there is some
\delta>0
such that
\textup{\#Roots}_{q}^{d}
can be solved in time
\mathcal{O}(q^{(1-\delta)n})
for all
d
.
We now devise an algorithm for #
k
-SAT that contradicts #SETH, using a constant
\varepsilon>0
that we will set later.
Let
\varphi
be a
k
-CNF with
n
variables.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\varphi
has at most
(k/\varepsilon)^{\mathcal{O}(k)}\cdot n
clauses by the counting version of the sparsification lemma
[
21
,
10
,
14
]
.
This causes a multiplicative
\mathcal{O}(2^{\varepsilon n})
overhead in the running time.
We now apply Lemma
4.1
to obtain a polynomial equation system
G
using at most
\frac{n}{\log q}\cdot\left(1+\frac{\delta}{2}+o(1)\right)
variables and
(k/\varepsilon)^{\mathcal{O}(k)}\cdot n+\left\lceil\frac{n}{\lceil\delta/2\cdot\log q\rceil}\right\rceil\in(k/\varepsilon)^{\mathcal{O}(k)}\cdot n
equations, where the number of satisfying assignments of
\varphi
is exactly the number of solutions to
G
.
This takes time
\mathcal{O}((k/\varepsilon)^{\mathcal{O}(k)}\cdot n^{2})
.
From the proof of Lemma
4.1
, we can also see that each equation in
G
uses at most
B\coloneqq\lceil\lceil 2/\delta\cdot\log q\rceil/\log q\rceil\cdot k
variables.
Let
m
be the number of equations in
G
,
d\coloneqq B\cdot(q-1)
, and
C\coloneqq m/(\varepsilon n)
.

Finally, apply Lemma
4.2
to
G
with
q
,
d
,
B
,
C
, and
\varepsilon
as defined above.
This takes time

\mathcal{O}\left(q^{m/(m/(\varepsilon n))}\cdot\left(m+\left(\frac{n}{\log q}\cdot(1+\delta/2+o(1)\right)\right)\right)=\mathcal{O}(q^{\varepsilon n}\cdot n)

and any polynomial queried in the reduction uses at most
\frac{n}{\log q}\cdot(1+\frac{\delta}{2}+o(1))
variables and has degree at most
D\coloneqq BC\cdot(q-1)
.

Apart from this, we get the following running time for computing the answers to all oracle queries using the assumed algorithm for
\textup{\#Roots}_{q}^{D}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(2^{\varepsilon n}\cdot q^{\varepsilon n}\cdot q^{(1-\delta)\cdot\frac{n}{\log q}\cdot(1+\delta/2+o(1))})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(q^{(1-\delta)\cdot\frac{n}{\log q}\cdot(1+\delta/2+o(1))+2\varepsilon n}).

Setting
\delta^{\prime}
to a constant with
\delta^{\prime}<\delta\cdot(1/2+\delta/2+o(1))
, by the same calculations as in the proof of Theorem
1.2
, the above is bounded by

q^{(1-\delta^{\prime})n+2\varepsilon n}=q^{(1+2\varepsilon-\delta^{\prime})n}.

This term obviously dominates the running time of our algorithm.
Setting
\varepsilon<\delta^{\prime}/2
, this implies an algorithm for #
k
-SAT running in time
\mathcal{O}(q^{(1-\delta^{\prime\prime})n})
for
\delta^{\prime\prime}=\delta^{\prime}-2\varepsilon
, yielding the desired #SETH lower bound.
