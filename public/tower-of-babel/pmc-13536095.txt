Drivers and potential mitigation solutions for low-value medical imaging in Iran: a qualitative study
Authors: Gisoo Darban Hosseini Amirkhiz, Fatemeh Kanaani Nejad, Sedighe Hooshmandi, Maryam Afshari, Amirreza Nikmanesh, Reza Tabrizi, Afshar Ghamari Khameneh, Reihaneh Khorram, Saeed Shahabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify the main drivers of low-value medical imaging in Iran and explore potential strategies to reduce its use.DesignQualitative study using semistructured interviews.SettingPublic and private healthcare settings in Iran.Participants34 physicians, including general practitioners, radiologists and non-radiology specialists, selected using purposive and snowball sampling.MethodsSemistructured interviews were conducted between October 2023 and February 2024 through face-to-face, telephone and video calls. Data were analysed using thematic content analysis with a hybrid inductive–deductive approach and organised using the five control knobs framework.ResultsParticipants identified multiple drivers of low-value medical imaging across five domains: organisation, financing, payment, behaviour and regulation. Key drivers included weaknesses in the referral system, limited oversight, financial incentives, defensive medical practice, patient demand and misconceptions about diagnostic value. Suggested mitigation strategies included public education, physician training, stronger referral pathways, improved insurance oversight, use of evidence-based guidelines and better regulatory monitoring.ConclusionsLow-value medical imaging in Iran is sustained by interacting weaknesses in referral pathways, financial incentives, professional practice and oversight. Efforts to reduce overuse should therefore combine physician and public education with stronger referral systems, insurer monitoring and regulatory action.

Introduction

For clinical use, medical imaging is the process of visualising the structure and function of various tissues and organs in the human body. Medical imaging techniques reveal internal structures and organs, assisting in the diagnosis and treatment of numerous diseases.1 Medical science is increasingly reliant on medical imaging techniques, which play an important role in the detection of abnormal conditions in different fields, such as breast cancer,2 cardiovascular disease,3 neurological disease4 and bone health assessment in the orthopaedic field.5 The WHO reports that children under the age of 15 undergo 250 million of the 3.6 billion imaging examinations performed annually.6

Despite the widespread use of diagnostic imaging in medical practice, its value diminishes when the examination does not significantly affect the patient’s management, thereby increasing costs and putting patients at unnecessary risk due to ionising radiation exposure.7 8 Indeed, some medical imaging procedures expose patients to ionising radiation, which may contribute to avoidable harm when such procedures are used without clear clinical indication.9 10 Beyond radiation exposure, low-value imaging may lead to over-diagnosis, unnecessary follow-up tests, patient anxiety and wasted healthcare resources.11 12 According to various studies conducted in different countries around the world, the global proportion of low-value imaging ranges from 20% to 50% or even 100% in some cases.13

Radiation-induced hazards are one of the most serious and controversial side effects of medical imaging, particularly CT.14 The most serious radiation side effects are stochastic ones, including cancer and hereditary effects, caused by mutations or other permanent changes in which the damaged cell remains viable.15 Previous estimates suggest that exposure to ionising radiation from imaging may contribute to a measurable number of future cancers at the population level, underscoring the importance of avoiding unnecessary tests.16 There is evidence that for every 10 000 children who undergo CT scans, one or two will develop a haematological malignancy as a result of radiation exposure.17

In addition to health-related side effects, the widespread use of diagnostic medical imaging places an excessive financial burden on the healthcare system; in the USA, these procedures cost more than US$100 billion per year.18 Each year, low-value examinations cost billions of dollars worldwide.19 In Iran, concerns about the overuse of diagnostic services are shaped by features of the healthcare system such as fragmented referral mechanisms, mixed public-private provision, supplementary insurance coverage and variable regulatory oversight.20 These factors make the Iranian setting particularly important for understanding how low-value imaging is produced and how it may be reduced.

Although low-value imaging has been studied in several healthcare systems, evidence from Iran remains limited. The Iranian context is important because features such as fragmented referral pathways, fee-for-service (FFS) payment, supplementary insurance coverage and variable regulatory oversight may shape both the drivers of overuse and the feasibility of mitigation strategies in distinctive ways. In response, the purpose of this qualitative study was to identify common drivers and potential mitigation strategies for prescribing low-value medical imaging services in Iran.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study was conducted using semistructured interviews from October 2023 to February 2024, adhering to the ‘Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR)’ criteria.21

Participant selection

Participants were selected using purposive sampling to ensure variation in specialty, gender, years of experience and practice setting. Snowball sampling was subsequently used to identify additional physicians with relevant experience. Efforts were made to include individuals from three distinct professions: general practitioners, radiology specialists and other medical specialists (emergency medical practice, general surgery, neurology, paediatrics, internal medical practice, orthopaedics, obstetrics and gynaecology and cardiology). Eligible participants were approached directly by the research team using professional networks and contact details available through institutional or clinical channels. Snowball sampling was then used to identify additional participants with relevant experience. Participants received information about the study and a consent form via email and WhatsApp. After the individuals agreed to participate, the time of the interview was scheduled with them. Before the interview began, the participants were given the research objectives and general information orally, and they were asked to sign the informed consent form. The sampling continued until no new findings were added to the previous ones. Four final interviews with repeated findings were considered a measure of data saturation.

Eligible participants were physicians involved in requesting, performing or managing medical imaging and who had sufficient professional experience to comment on imaging use in practice. Physicians unwilling to participate or unable to provide informed consent were excluded. We assured the participants that their identities would remain anonymous throughout the study and that they could withdraw at any time. Of the physicians approached, three individuals declined participation and 16 did not respond.

Data collection

Three researchers (GDHA, FKN and AN) collected the data. An interview guide was developed and pilot-tested to assist with interview management. The interview guide was pilot tested with a small number of physicians with characteristics similar to the target participants. Based on the pilot interviews, several questions were reworded for clarity, overlap between prompts was reduced and the sequence of questions was adjusted to improve flow (online supplemental table 1). Interviews were conducted via video conference (Skype Video Communications, USA), telephone or in-person. Interviews were conducted face-to-face, by telephone or via video call according to participant preference and feasibility. Using multiple modes increased flexibility and supported recruitment across settings; however, the research team remained aware that interview mode could influence rapport, interaction and the depth of responses. The interviews focused on two broad topics: experiences with low-value medical imaging services and potential future actions to reduce such services. Interviewers led the sessions as conversations, allowing participants to freely share their experiences and thoughts on the subjects. An effort was made to conduct the interviews in a calm environment, free of third parties. Although the interview guide was not strictly followed, the interviewers made sure that all questions were answered. All interviews were conducted in Persian and audio-recorded with participant permission. Recordings were transcribed verbatim in Persian by the research team. Quotations selected for publication were translated into English by bilingual members of the team and checked for semantic accuracy to preserve participants’ intended meanings. In addition to digital recording, field notes were taken during the interviews.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed concurrently with data collection using MAXQDA software (VERBI, Berlin, Germany). Data were analysed concurrently with data collection using thematic content analysis.22 Thematic content analysis was chosen because it allows systematic identification and interpretation of patterns in qualitative data while remaining sufficiently flexible to capture both expected and unanticipated issues.23 Three researchers (GDHA, FKN and SS) repeatedly reviewed the written interviews and coded the meaning units. The analysis followed a hybrid inductive-deductive approach. Initial codes were generated from the interview data in a data-driven manner. These codes were then compared, refined and grouped into broader categories, which were subsequently organised using the five control knobs framework.24

The five control knobs framework was then used as an organising lens because it provides a policy-relevant structure for examining health system influences across organisation, financing, payment, behaviour and regulation. While the five control knobs framework structured our final analysis, we maintained an inductive approach during initial open coding to allow context-specific themes to emerge. For example, the code ‘fear of litigation-induced defensive medicine’ initially emerged inductively from participants’ discussions of safety. Rather than forcing this into a generic behavioural category, we analysed how it was shaped by the systemic lack of legal protections for physicians, ultimately mapping it to the intersection of the behavioural and regulatory control knobs. Additionally, we actively looked for divergent cases; for instance, while most participants linked private sector practice with higher imaging rates due to financial incentives, two participants argued that private practices sometimes enforce stricter patient-retention strategies that actually limit unnecessary imaging to maintain long-term institutional reputation. Attention was paid to both recurrent and divergent viewpoints. When uncertainty arose during coding or category development, the research team consulted senior researchers with experience in qualitative health research. These consultations helped refine code definitions and resolve interpretive disagreements through discussion.

Qualitative frequency descriptors are used consistently throughout this manuscript: ‘most’ or ‘majority’ refers to viewpoints shared by more than half of the participants; ‘many’ or ‘several’ refers to patterns observed across multiple specialties or practice settings; and ‘some’ or ‘few’ refers to isolated or minor viewpoints.

Rigor and trustworthiness strategies

To ensure the validity of qualitative findings, several criteria have been introduced, which include confirmability, transferability, authenticity, credibility and dependability.25 Qualitative studies employ various strategies to address these criteria. In the present study, we employed the following strategies: (1) having participants check the final results (confirmability); (2) using the widest range of people in the sampling process (transferability); (3) getting code from the most contributors (authenticity); (4) having experts check the results (credibility) and (5) involving researchers from a range of scientific and clinical backgrounds in the data analysis stage (dependability).

Reflexivity

The interviews were conducted by researchers trained in qualitative methodologies and health services research. Members of the research team had prior academic experience with health policy and low-value care evaluations. To maintain critical awareness, we engaged in continuous self-reflection regarding our assumptions. For example, the team initially assumed that physician behaviour and financial gain were the primary drivers of imaging overuse. However, during data analysis, reflexivity sessions challenged this assumption as we recognised that clinicians often felt trapped by structural factors, such as defensive medicine and weak gatekeeping. Reflecting on this shift allowed us to avoid individualising the blame and instead construct a more balanced analysis that emphasises the structural and regulatory control knobs.

Results

The study had 34 participants: five radiologists, 12 general practitioners and 17 specialists in non-radiology fields. These specialists practised emergency medical practice, general surgery, obstetrics and gynaecology, cardiology and neurology (table 1). All participants acknowledged low-value imaging and the significant challenges it poses. The findings were organised into two overarching domains: drivers of low-value medical imaging and potential mitigation solutions. Within each domain, issues were grouped into five system-level categories: organisation, financing, payment, behaviour and regulation.

Drivers of low-value medical imaging

Participants identified multiple interrelated drivers contributing to the prescription of low-value medical imaging services in Iran. These drivers were grouped into five system-level categories: organisational, financing, payment, behavioural and regulatory factors (online supplemental table 2).

Organisational factors were frequently cited as important contributors to unnecessary imaging requests. Participants highlighted the limited prioritisation of low-value services by policymakers and weaknesses in the referral system. Poor coordination between referring physicians and imaging departments was perceived as a key issue. One participant explained, “Poor communication between referrers and the medical imaging department is one of the main challenges.”[13] Participants also pointed to the declining quality of medical education and the lack of effective continuing professional development as factors influencing physicians’ clinical decision-making, which may lead to excessive reliance on imaging tests. Others pointed to the widespread availability of imaging facilities, particularly in urban areas, as a factor that encourages greater utilisation of these services. One participant noted that “when imaging centers are widely available, it becomes easier to request these services even when they are not necessary.” [14] Overall, participants suggested that weak policy prioritisation, insufficient legal support, oversupply of imaging facilities, and limited regulatory oversight collectively contribute to the continued overuse of medical imaging services.

Financing mechanisms within the health system were also described as encouraging unnecessary imaging requests. Participants frequently referred to the role of health insurance policies in shaping clinical practice. In particular, complementary insurance coverage was perceived to stimulate demand for imaging services, especially in private healthcare settings. One participant noted that “having complementary insurance causes false requests (especially in the contracting party’s private hospitals).” [13] Moreover, some physicians indicated that insurance reimbursement policies sometimes require imaging results before approving clinical procedures, which leads physicians to request imaging even when the clinical diagnosis is already evident. As one physician explained, “The problem is clearly evident in the physical examination, but the insurance only allows the operation if we have an imaging of the patient.” [05] Participants also pointed out that limited coverage for consultations compared with imaging services can shift patient preferences towards diagnostic testing rather than clinical evaluation.

Participants further identified payment arrangements as an important structural driver. The FFS payment model and relatively low consultation fees were believed to discourage thorough clinical assessment and encourage the use of diagnostic imaging as a faster alternative. Physicians reported that inadequate reimbursement for consultations limits the time they can spend with patients, which may increase reliance on imaging tests. As one participant stated, “A physician who does not charge a reasonable fee for a visit does not have the patience to spend time with the patient, take a correct history, and perform a comprehensive physical examination.” [10] In addition, differences between public and private sector tariffs were described as creating financial incentives for healthcare providers and institutions to increase the use of imaging services.

Behavioural factors were widely discussed by participants and included both physician-related and patient-related influences. Many physicians reported experiencing strong pressure from patients who believe that imaging tests are necessary for accurate diagnosis. Defensive medicine was also frequently mentioned, with physicians sometimes requesting imaging tests primarily to avoid potential legal disputes rather than to improve clinical decision-making. As one physician explained, “Sometimes, due to legal issues, physicians request imaging for the patient, which has little effect on the disease’s follow-up process, and they do this only to avoid potential future legal issues for the physician.” [15] Participants also highlighted gaps in physicians’ familiarity with current clinical guidelines aimed at reducing unnecessary imaging. One participant stated, “Physicians are not familiar with up-to-date guidelines to reduce unnecessary requests.” [04] In addition, hierarchical professional dynamics and concerns about criticism from colleagues or specialists were reported to influence clinical decisions. An emergency physician described how these pressures can lead to repeated imaging: “I know that the neurosurgeon will not support me… therefore, I decide to keep the patient and repeat the CT scan six hours later.” [27]

Regulatory and policy-related shortcomings were identified as important contributors to the persistence of low-value medical imaging. Participants believed that reducing unnecessary imaging had not been adequately prioritised within health policymaking, resulting in limited attention to monitoring and control mechanisms. One participant explained that “from the point of view of current policymakers, this issue [low-value medical imaging services] is not considered important” [13]. In addition, interviewees emphasised that physicians often lacked sufficient legal protection when attempting to avoid unnecessary imaging requests, particularly in high-risk clinical situations. As one physician noted, “Physicians do not receive special support! I went to the Iranian Legal Medicine Organization twice, and they treated me very badly there. It seems as though they view you as an accused individual, disregarding the fact that you have made sound decisions within this environment. In general, our trust and support are weak” [27]. Participants also highlighted the absence of comprehensive supervision and effective oversight systems, which allows inappropriate imaging practices to continue without accountability. One participant stated that “the lack of preventive mechanisms and request supervision has caused physicians to not cooperate to reduce unnecessary requests” [13].

Mitigation solutions

Participants proposed a range of strategies to reduce the prescription of low-value medical imaging services in Iran. These mitigation solutions were organised into five system-level categories: organisation, financing, payment, behaviour and regulation (online supplemental table 3). Participants emphasised that reducing low-value imaging requires coordinated interventions across multiple components of the health system rather than isolated actions.

Organisational interventions focus on systemic reforms within medical education and the clinical infrastructure. A primary strategy involves enhancing the pedagogical framework to ensure clinicians are equipped with evidence-based decision-making skills from the outset of their training. As one participant noted, “I suggest improving the radiology course training in medical and paramedical education,”[29] while another emphasised the early integration of clinical decision support tools, stating, “In medical education, we should introduce medical students to guidelines related to low-value services, such as iRefer, iGuide, etc., from the beginning.”[09] Furthermore, strengthening the referral architecture and implementing a centralised digital registration system are deemed critical for oversight. This includes the potential for a “national network capable of displaying imaging in all regions of Iran through the use of national IDs,”[15] which holds the capacity to “shield patients from unnecessary imaging procedures.”[15]

From a financing perspective, the discourse centres on reforming insurance coverage to discourage the utilisation of non-essential services. The current financing structure is perceived as a driver for unnecessary demand, suggesting that insurance providers should act as gatekeepers of clinical necessity. This is exemplified by the assertion: “If the insurance differentiated between necessary and unnecessary medical imaging services, many people would not insist on unnecessary radiographs.”[18] Additionally, participants highlighted the need to recalibrate the cost differential between insured and out-of-pocket services in private practice to ensure that financial incentives do not override clinical recommendations.

Payment reform strategies advocate for a transition from volume-based to value-based reimbursement models. The current FFS paradigm in private sectors often incentivises higher prescription volumes, which can lead to over-utilisation. To counter this, participants suggested moving towards outcome-based models, noting: “In private centres, if a physician requests additional services for a patient, they are paid more. Therefore, payment should be based on the outcome of the prescribed service and not simply on the number of prescriptions.”[10] Furthermore, adjusting medical tariffs to reflect the actual value of services could serve as a deterrent, with one participant suggesting that “if the costs were more expensive, such requests [for low-value services] would be less.”[06]

Behavioural dimensions encompass a broad spectrum of professional ethics, clinical competence and the provider–patient dyad. Addressing the gap in clinical knowledge is paramount, as some practitioners may rely on outdated methods rather than current evidence, as evidenced by the observation: “Many physicians may not know what the indication of lumbosacral MRI is… Therefore, we should increase the movement towards up-to-date scientific evidence.”[12] Moreover, mitigating the influence of patient pressure and enhancing public awareness regarding the economic and health burdens of radiology are essential. Strengthening the patient–physician relationship is also highlighted as a clinical stabiliser: “Building trust between physician and patient is one of the most effective ways to reduce the prescription of this type of service.”[02]

Regulatory interventions target the oversight of private sector expansion and the mitigation of defensive medicine. There is a consensus that the unregulated proliferation of imaging centres has created “false needs,”[13] necessitating stricter supervision to ensure clinical necessity. This includes the need for “stricter supervision by insurance companies”[07] to optimise prescribing patterns. Additionally, addressing the legal vulnerabilities of clinicians is vital to reducing defensive prescribing. Participants suggested that “A series of legislative reforms can, to the extent, mitigate the defensive approach and physicians’ fear of legal consequences,”[15] thereby allowing clinicians to prioritise clinical utility over legal self-protection.

Notably, in interpreting the data, we did not assume that drivers or mitigating solutions mentioned more frequently were necessarily more important. Analytical emphasis was placed on conceptual relevance as well as recurrence across interviews.

Discussion

The present study identified a number of drivers and potential solutions for low-value medical imaging services in Iran. The most frequently reported drivers were an inefficient referral system and high provider workload within the organisational domain; financial conflicts of interest within the financing domain; low tariffs for medical imaging services within the payment domain; defensive medical practice and exaggerated beliefs about diagnostic technology within the behavioural domain; and insufficient supervision within the regulatory domain. Participants proposed several strategies to reduce low-value medical imaging services, including increasing patient and physician awareness, revising medical tariffs, reforming health insurance policies, strengthening referral pathways and improving regulatory oversight. Overall, our findings extend the existing literature by showing how these drivers manifest within the unique structural context of the Iranian healthcare system. In particular, fragmented referral arrangements, financial incentives, weak monitoring and sociocultural expectations appear to interact in ways that sustain unnecessary imaging requests.

Participants emphasised both the health-related and financial consequences of low-value imaging. This finding is consistent with international evidence showing that low-value care imposes substantial costs on patients, payers, governments and society.26 27 Beyond financial waste, the overuse of diagnostic imaging may expose patients to avoidable harm, including unnecessary radiation exposure,27 28 incidental findings, downstream testing, overtreatment and psychological distress caused by mistakenly labelling individuals as ill.27 Evidence also suggests that problems in imaging request quality are widespread. For example, a study using the Reason for Exam Imaging Reporting and Data System found insufficient quality in more than 75% of radiologic imaging requests, with approximately 20% classified as substandard.29 However, documenting the prevalence of low-value services is not sufficient to reduce them; understanding their contextual, organisational, behavioural and financial determinants is also necessary.30

The drivers identified in this study are broadly consistent with previous research. In a study of 31 academic physicians, patient expectations, efficiency and throughput pressures, the practice patterns of other physicians, fear of malpractice and working with residents in academic medical centres were reported as major contributors to low-value service use.31 Similarly, a Norwegian study involving radiographers, radiologists, radiology department managers, hospital clinicians, general practitioners and government representatives identified referral processes, referral assessment and broader healthcare service organisation as key areas for reducing low-value medical imaging services.32 Another qualitative study conducted in Norway in 2023 reported healthcare organisation, communication, competence, expectations, defensive medical practice, roles and responsibilities, referral quality and time constraints as major drivers of low-value imaging.33 The convergence between these findings and ours suggests that several drivers of unnecessary imaging are shared across health systems, although their intensity and mechanisms may differ according to local financing, governance and service delivery arrangements.

In the current study, a number of participants reported an inefficient referral system as a key organisational driver of low-value medical imaging services. Proposed solutions included refining referral pathways and creating a comprehensive registration and documentation process. This finding aligns with evidence from Norway, where improved interaction between radiologists and clinicians was suggested as a strategy to reduce low-value medical imaging services. Proposed mechanisms included practice coordinators, structured meetings and accessible contact information to support referral decision-making.32 In the Iranian context, strengthening referral systems may be especially important because fragmented referral pathways can allow imaging requests to bypass clinical prioritisation, guideline-based triage and accountability mechanisms.

Easy availability of imaging services was another organisational driver reported by participants. This is consistent with previous evidence showing that access to medical imaging services influences utilisation. A Norwegian study examining referral practices and access to imaging services found that greater availability of medical imaging services was associated with increased use.34 Similarly, in a survey of 374 radiologists, service availability was ranked among the five leading causes of increasing and unnecessary medical imaging.35 In settings where imaging centres are easily accessible and regulatory oversight is limited, service availability may increase demand from both physicians and patients. Therefore, regulatory measures, including more strategic planning of imaging centre distribution, may help reduce unnecessary use. However, such policies should be implemented carefully to avoid restricting access to clinically necessary imaging, particularly in underserved areas.

Participants also identified inadequate training as an organisational contributor to low-value imaging. This issue was not emphasised in the previous study,33 suggesting that educational gaps may have particular relevance in the Iranian context. Participants attributed some unnecessary imaging requests to insufficient in-service training regarding appropriate imaging indications, radiation risks and the potential harms of overdiagnosis. In a qualitative study of physicians from six high-income countries, Greek and Italian physicians similarly identified a lack of useful resources as a driver of low-value care.36 Other studies have also linked low-value imaging to insufficient use of clinical guidelines.33 Accordingly, participants in our study recommended improving medical education by incorporating low-value care principles, imaging appropriateness criteria and more comprehensive radiology training into undergraduate and continuing medical education.

Financial conflicts of interest were among the most frequently reported drivers of low-value medical imaging services. The majority of the participants described mechanisms through which financial incentives may encourage unnecessary imaging. This finding is consistent with international evidence suggesting that market dynamics and provider incentives can increase imaging use. For example, private imaging centres have been reported to perform a large proportion of examinations in some catchment areas.34 Evidence from the USA also suggests that private medical centres and clinicians may compete to attract patients by fulfilling requests for additional laboratory and imaging services.32 In Iran, such financial incentives may be reinforced by local payment mechanisms, private-sector service provision, weak referral controls and limited monitoring. Participants also frequently identified the low cost of medical imaging services as a payment-related driver. Low out-of-pocket costs may reduce patient resistance to unnecessary imaging, while low tariffs may encourage higher service volume as a compensatory provider response.

Behavioural factors were another major theme. Most participants referred to exaggerated beliefs about diagnostic technology. This finding suggests that both patients and physicians may perceive advanced imaging as inherently superior, more accurate or more reassuring, even when the expected clinical benefit is limited. A recent qualitative study from Australia similarly showed that patient anxiety, health concerns and consumerist expectations can contribute to requests for medical imaging and create challenges for general practitioners.37 Participants in our study proposed several solutions to address behavioural drivers, including improving patient and physician awareness, increasing adherence to clinical guidelines and developing stronger provider-patient relationships. These strategies may help physicians communicate uncertainty, explain why imaging is not always beneficial and reduce patient demand for unnecessary tests.

Defensive medical practice was also identified as a major behavioural driver. Participants reported that fear of missed diagnoses, medico-legal consequences and criticism from patients or colleagues may lead physicians to request imaging even when the expected diagnostic value is low. This is consistent with previous research showing that defensive medical practice is commonly driven by concern about rare or unexpected pathology, fear of litigation and avoidance of diagnostic error.38 Participants suggested that better legal protections and stronger adherence to clinical guidelines could reduce defensive imaging. Clinical guidelines may be particularly useful when they are recognised by professional bodies, insurers and legal authorities, because they can support physicians’ decisions not to request imaging when it is not clinically indicated.

Medical errors were another frequently reported behavioural driver. Some participants described how inadequate clinical assessment, incomplete history-taking or uncertainty about patients’ symptoms may lead to low-value imaging requests. Previous evidence supports this concern. In one study, radiologists assessing CT referrals reported that as many as one in seven referrals lacked clinically significant information that would facilitate diagnosis.39 Inadequate or inaccurate clinical history may also contribute to reporting errors and reduce the diagnostic value of imaging.40 These findings suggest that improving the quality of referral information is essential. Structured request forms, mandatory clinical indication fields, decision-support tools and feedback from radiologists to referring physicians may improve appropriateness and reduce avoidable imaging.

Cultural habits were reported by nearly half of the participants. This theme reflects a broader ‘more is better’ culture, in which additional testing is perceived as a marker of thorough care. Similar cultural patterns have been identified as barriers to reducing low-value care in countries such as Canada and the USA.41 42 Because habits and routines are embedded in clinical practice and patient expectations, changing them requires sustained, system-level effort. Reflective clinical practice, based on evidence, professional values and awareness of harms, may help clinicians resist routine low-value imaging.42 43 Participants’ proposed solutions, including increasing physician and patient awareness and improving provider-patient communication, are therefore important but should be supported by organisational and policy-level interventions.

Choosing Wisely initiatives and their international adaptations provide a relevant framework for addressing low-value imaging. The Choosing Wisely campaign, first launched in the United States and subsequently adopted in many countries, aims to reduce unnecessary medical interventions by promoting evidence-based recommendations and encouraging dialogue between clinicians and patients about the appropriateness of care.44 45 Several Choosing Wisely recommendations specifically target imaging overuse, including avoiding imaging for uncomplicated low back pain or minor head injury without clinical indications. Studies evaluating these initiatives have shown that combining clinical guidelines, physician education and patient awareness campaigns can contribute to reductions in low-value diagnostic testing.46 Our findings are broadly consistent with this approach, as participants emphasised the importance of increasing physician and patient awareness, improving guideline adherence and strengthening communication between providers and patients. Integrating similar evidence-based recommendation lists or adapting Choosing Wisely type initiatives to the Iranian healthcare system may therefore represent a promising strategy for reducing unnecessary imaging.

A central interpretation of our findings is that low-value imaging in Iran is not driven by a single cause. Rather, it appears to result from the interaction of payment arrangements, organisational weaknesses, regulatory gaps and behavioural pressures. FFS remuneration may incentivise higher service volume, while weak monitoring and fragmented referral pathways reduce constraints on unnecessary requests. At the same time, fear of missed diagnoses, medicolegal concerns, cultural expectations and patient demand may encourage imaging even when clinical value is limited. These interacting pressures suggest that isolated educational interventions are unlikely to be sufficient. In the short term, feasible interventions may include clearer referral pathways, targeted physician education, dissemination and implementation of imaging guidelines, structured referral forms, feedback systems and insurer monitoring. In the longer term, sustainable reduction of low-value imaging will likely require alignment of financial incentives, stronger regulatory accountability, integration of clinical decision support and reforms in health insurance payment policies.

This study has several implications for policy and practice. First, strengthening referral systems should be considered a priority, particularly through clearer referral criteria, better documentation of clinical indications and improved communication between referring physicians and radiologists. Second, financial and payment reforms should be designed to reduce incentives for unnecessary service volume while preserving access to appropriate imaging. Third, regulatory bodies and insurers could play a more active role in monitoring imaging requests, identifying patterns of overuse and providing feedback to clinicians and imaging centres. Fourth, educational interventions should target both physicians and patients, emphasising the potential harms of unnecessary imaging and the importance of evidence-based decision-making. Finally, legal and professional frameworks should support physicians who follow validated clinical guidelines, thereby reducing the perceived need for defensive imaging.

Limitations

Despite the efforts of the research team, there are a number of limitations with this study. First, the current study focused on physicians’ perspectives and did not include patients, nurses or allied health professionals. As a result, the findings may not capture all relevant influences on low-value imaging use. Second, the geographical location of the study—one of the provinces of Iran—may limit the applicability of the conclusions made by participants. Third, the use of different interview modes may have affected rapport and the richness of data across participants, although the same interview guide and probing approach were used to support consistency.

Conclusions

This qualitative study identified numerous drivers and mitigating factors that influence Iran’s provision of low-value medical imaging services. Drawing insights from diverse healthcare specialists, we have identified a range of drivers contributing to the persistence of low-value medical imaging services, such as an inefficient referral system, undesirable training, a high workload for providers, a financial conflict of interest, the relatively low cost of medical imaging services, defensive medical practice, medical errors, exaggerated technology beliefs, cultural habits and a lack of comprehensive supervision. Additionally, potential avenues for reducing low-value imaging services lie in strategies such as improving the referral system, enhancing the awareness of patients and physicians, revising the insurance coverage and tariffs, adhering to clinical guidelines, improving the referral system and establishing comprehensive supervision. Therefore, it is essential for relevant stakeholders to consider these findings in order to de-implement low-value medical imaging services in the health system.
