Complete recovery from chronic implant-induced paresthesia after implant removal: a case report
Authors: Ji Hyun Paik, Nan Ju Lee, Junglim Choi, Tae Min You, Seung-Rye Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInferior alveolar nerve (IAN) injury is one of the most significant adverse outcomes of dental implantation. IAN injury can occur because of direct invasion of the mandibular canal, compression from implant placement, or postoperative hematoma formation. These injuries may result in prolonged neurosensory disturbances, and the prognosis largely depends on how quickly treatment is initiated. A 71-year-old male presented with persistent hypoesthesia, pain, and tinnitus in the left mandibular region of 6 years duration after implant placement. Clinical and radiographic examinations revealed peri-implantitis around the implant in the affected region. Neurosensory disturbances completely resolved after removal of the affected implant with peri-implantitis. This case highlights the fact that even long-standing inferior alveolar nerve injuries may be reversible if the underlying cause is resolved. Prompt recognition and elimination of etiologic factors such as implant-induced nerve compression in the presence of peri-implant pathology are crucial for achieving favorable recovery in patients with chronic IAN injury.Keywords: Axonotmesis, Dental Implants, Paresthesia, Peri-Implantitis, Recovery of Function

INTRODUCTION

Implant-related inferior alveolar nerve complications occur in 0-44% of patients, increasing with procedural prevalence and exhibiting enhanced severity compared to extraction-associated injuries [1,2]. Such injuries cause neurosensory disturbances affecting the cheek, lip, and oral mucosa, resulting in functional disabilities that negatively affect quality of life [3]. Based on clinical presentation, these disturbances are classified as paresthesia, dysesthesia, or anesthesia, with the prognosis largely dependent on injury severity [1,3]. Seddon categorized nerve injuries as neurapraxia, axonotmesis, and neurotmesis. Neurapraxia generally resolves spontaneously, while axonotmesis and neurotmesis often result in prolonged or permanent deficits [4,5].

Timely intervention is critical for favorable recovery. Prolonged untreated injury may lead to scar formation, inhibitory micro-environmental changes, receptor degeneration, and reorganization of the central nervous system, all of which hinder nerve regeneration [6]. Kipp et al. suggested six months as the threshold for irreversible damage, as no complete recovery was observed in cases with longer-lasting deficits [7]. Nevertheless, rare recoveries have been reported, including a case by Elian et al., where neurosensory function returned after 4.5 years of sensory loss once the causative implant with peri-implantitis was removed [8].

This case report describes the case of a 71-year-old male patient with inferior alveolar nerve (IAN) paresthesia and neuropathic pain persisting for six years following implant placement. Remarkably, complete sensory recovery was achieved within one week after the removal of the offending implant, without adjunctive pharmacological therapy. This case highlights the importance of identifying the implant-related causes of chronic IAN injury and suggests that neurosensory recovery may be possible, even after prolonged impairment.

CASE REPORT

A 71-year-old male patient presented with chief complaints of gingival swelling and mobility of the left mandibular first molar implant, accompanied by persistent neurosensory disturbances that had persisted for six years after implant placement. The patient had no systemic diseases or current medications. Dental records indicated that sensory alterations developed immediately after surgery; however, no therapeutic interventions were initiated.

Clinical examination revealed hypoesthesia affecting the left lower lip, chin, and attached gingiva, accompanied by paresthesia and positive Tinel's sign over the mental nerve distribution. Intraoral examination revealed gingival inflammation, grade II implant mobility, probing depths exceeding 12 mm, and bleeding upon probing around the affected implant. The patient also reported ipsilateral tinnitus. Quantitative sensory testing using two-point discrimination with a Vernier caliper showed impaired tactile sensation, with thresholds of 12 mm on the affected left side and 9 mm on the contralateral side (Fig. 1) (Table 1).

Radiographic evaluations, including panoramic radiographs, periapical views, and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) revealed extensive peri-implant bone loss with the implant apex positioned 1.7 mm coronal to the inferior alveolar nerve canal (Fig. 2).

Initial radiographic findings (A) periapical radiograph showing radiographic sign of failed osseointegration and (B, C) cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) image showing the left mandibular first molar implant located 1.7 mm above the inferior alveolar nerve

The patient was diagnosed with severe peri-implantitis with inferior alveolar nerve paresthesia. The treatment consisted of implant removal under local anesthesia (2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine via the inferior alveolar nerve block). The implant was removed atraumatically because of the complete loss of osseointegration. Following thorough debridement of granulation tissue, hemostasis was achieved with a collagen-based hemostatic agent (Colladerm®, Hyundai Bioland, South Korea), and primary wound closure was achieved with 4-0 silk sutures (Mersilk®, Ethicon, Johnson & Johnson, USA).

Postoperative management included systemic antibiotics (amoxicillin/clavulanic acid 625 mg, Augmentin®, Ilsung, South Korea), anti-inflammatory medication (ibuprofen 400 mg, Brufen®, Samil, South Korea) for five days, and chlorhexidine gluconate 0.12% mouth rinse (Hexamedine Sol®, Bukwang, South Korea).

Complete resolution of neurosensory symptoms and tinnitus was observed at the nine-day follow-up appointment. After six months of uneventful healing, implant replacement was performed using a 5.0 × 7 mm implant (SuperLine II®, Dentium, South Korea), followed by conventional prosthetic rehabilitation. On long-term evaluation, the patient remained asymptomatic, with functional and esthetic outcomes, and no recurrence of neurosensory disturbances (Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

The neurosensory disturbances observed in this case were attributed to the inferior alveolar nerve injury associated with implant placement. According to Seddon's classification, persistent sensory loss and paresthesia are likely to represent either neurapraxia or axonotmesis. Given the six-year duration of symptoms, the injury most likely corresponded to axonotmesis, indicating that structural damage extended beyond the simple conduction block [3,4,5] (Fig. 4).

Inferior alveolar nerve injury during implant surgery can result from multiple mechanisms, including needle trauma during local anesthesia, nerve stretching or compression during flap elevation, direct damage by drilling or implant placement, compression by bone fragments, and secondary compression due to postoperative edema or hematoma formation [3]. In the present case, the implant apex was positioned 1.7 mm superior to the inferior alveolar nerve canal. According to Kraut and Chahal, a minimum safety margin of 2 mm between the implant and the nerve canal should be maintained [9]. Violation of this safety distance likely contributed to the initial nerve injury through direct trauma, vascular damage with subsequent hematoma formation, or localized bone necrosis.

Anatomical variability of the mandibular canal further complicates implant placement in the posterior mandible. Misch noted that the cortical bone margin of the mandibular canal is frequently absent in the posterior regions [10]. Piermatti and Oyole demonstrated that cortical bone continuity is often compromised along the superior and lateral walls of the inferior alveolar canal, with the canal roof frequently composed of cancellous rather than cortical bone [11]. Consequently, nerve injury may occur even when adequate radiographic safety margins are maintained, particularly in cases in which cortical protection is insufficient.

Thermal injury is another important mechanism of nerve damage during implant site preparation. Excessive heat generation due to inadequate irrigation, excessive drilling pressure, or high rotational speed can cause localized tissue necrosis, cellular degeneration, and increased osteoclastic activity, ultimately leading to axonal degeneration. Such thermal damage may also contribute to peri-implantitis, osseointegration failure, and chronic inflammatory processes that perpetuate nerve compression and irritation [3].

The prolonged duration of sensory symptoms in this case likely reflected the absence of appropriate early intervention following the initial injury. Current studies recommend the administration of high-dose corticosteroids, such as dexamethasone (approximately 8 mg), within one week of injury to suppress inflammation, limit damage propagation, and prevent neuroma formation. According to Misch, dexamethasone is preferred due to its potent anti-inflammatory properties and favorable intravascular safety profile. Corticosteroids can be administered systemically or topically, with typical oral regimens including 4 mg dexamethasone twice daily for three days, followed by a tapering schedule. Concurrent administration of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (600–800 mg ibuprofen three times a day for three weeks) may provide synergistic benefits. Additionally, vitamin B12 supplementation may support nerve regeneration due to its essential role in cellular metabolism and myelin synthesis [1,3,12,13] (Fig. 5).

The remarkable recovery observed after implant removal in this case can be attributed to the elimination of pathogenic stimuli, including mechanical pressure and chronic inflammation. This removal likely normalized the local microenvironment, facilitating nerve regeneration through Schwann cell activation and growth factor secretion, and ultimately promoting axonal regeneration. Elian et al. demonstrated that chronic compression-induced sensory nerve damage could be reversed by removing the causative factor [8]. The present case provides rare evidence that complete recovery from chronic nerve injury may be achievable even after six years, provided that the underlying etiology is effectively addressed.

The concurrent resolution of ipsilateral tinnitus with sensory recovery is a clinically significant finding. Shore et al. proposed a mechanism by which trigeminal sensory stimulation influences central auditory processing through brainstem connections, potentially resulting in somatic tinnitus. The simultaneous resolution of both sensory and auditory symptoms following implant removal suggests a possible causal relationship between trigeminal nerve injury and tinnitus, although definitive conclusions require comprehensive otolaryngological evaluation and audiometric testing [14].

In summary, this case highlights the importance of the timely recognition, diagnosis, and proper management of inferior alveolar nerve injury following implant surgery. Most importantly, the prompt elimination of causative factors may be the most effective therapeutic intervention. The clinical evidence presented here demonstrates that even chronic nerve damage of an extended duration may be reversible through targeted removal of the underlying etiology. Furthermore, comprehensive evaluation and monitoring of both sensory and potential auditory symptoms are essential components of postoperative care following implant-related nerve injury.
