On the Parallel Complexity of Identifying Groups and Quasigroups via Decompositions
Dan Johnson, Michael Levet, Petr Vojtěchovský, Brett Widholm

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the computational complexity of isomorphism testing for finite groups and quasigroups, given by their multiplication tables. We crucially take advantage of their various decompositions to show the following: • We first consider the class 𝒞 \mathcal{C} of groups that admit direct product decompositions, where each indecompsable factor is O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) -generated, and either perfect or centerless. We show any group in 𝒞 \mathcal{C} is identified by the O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) -dimensional count-free Weisfeiler–Leman (WL) algorithm with O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) O(\log\log n) rounds, and the O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) -dimensional counting WL algorithm with O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) rounds. Consequently, the isomorphism problem for 𝒞 \mathcal{C} is in L . The previous upper bound for this class was TC 1 \textsf{TC}^{1} , using O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) rounds of the O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) -dimensional counting WL (Grochow and Levet, FCT 2023). • We next consider more generally, the class of groups where each indecomposable factor is O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) -generated. We exhibit an AC 3 \textsf{AC}^{3} canonical labeling procedure for this class. Here, we accomplish this by showing that in the multiplication table model, the direct product decomposition can be computed in AC 3 \textsf{AC}^{3} , parallelizing the work of Kayal and Nezhmetdinov (ICALP 2009). • Isomorphism testing between a central quasigroup G G and an arbitrary quasigroup H H is in NC . Here, we take advantage of the fact that central quasigroups admit an affine decomposition in terms of an underlying Abelian group. Only the trivial bound of n log ⁡ ( n ) + O ⁡ ( 1 ) n^{\log(n)+O(1)} -time was previously known for isomorphism testing of central quasigroups.



1
Introduction

The
Quasigroup Isomorphism
(
QGpI
) problem takes as input two finite quasigroups
G,H
and asks if there exists an isomorphism
\varphi:G\to H
. When the quasigroups are given by their multiplication (Cayley) tables, it is known that
QGpI
belongs to
\textsf{NP}\cap\textsf{coAM}
. The generator-enumeration strategy has time complexity
n^{\log(n)+O(1)}
, where
n
is the order of the group. In the setting of groups, this strategy was independently discovered by Felsch and Neubüser
[
FN70
]
and Tarjan (see
[
Mil78
]
). Miller subsequently extended this strategy to the setting of quasigroups
[
Mil78
]
. The parallel complexity of the generator-enumeration strategy has been gradually improved
[
LSZ77
,
Wol94
,
CTW13
,
Tan13
]
. Collins, Grochow, Levet, and Weiß recently improved the bound for the generator enumeration strategy to

\exists^{\log^{2}n}\forall^{\log n}\exists^{\log n}\textsf{DTISP}(\text{polylog}(n),\log(n)),

which can be simulated by depth-
4

\textsf{quasiAC}^{0}
circuits of size
n^{O(\log^{4}n)}

[
CGLW24
]
.

In the special case of
Group Isomorphism
(
GpI
), the worst-case runtime has been improved to
n^{(1/4)\log(n)+O(1)}
, due to Rosenbaum
[
Ros13
]
and Luks
[
Luk15
]
(see
[
LGR16
, Sec. 2.2]
). Even the impressive body of work on practical algorithms for
GpI
in more succinct input models, led by Eick, Holt, Leedham-Green and O’Brien (e. g.,
[
BEO02
,
ELGO02
,
BE99
,
CH03
]
) still results in an
n^{\Theta(\log n)}
-time algorithm in the general case (see
[
Wil19
, Page 2]
).

In practice, such as working with computer algebra systems, the Cayley model is highly unrealistic. Instead, the groups are given by generators as permutations or matrices, or as black-boxes. In the setting of permutation groups,
GpI
belongs to
NP

[
Luk93
]
. When the groups are given by generating sets of matrices, or as black-boxes,
GpI
belongs to
\textsf{Promise}\Sigma_{2}^{p}

[
BS84
]
; it remains open as to whether
GpI
belongs to
NP
or
coNP
in such succinct models. In the past several years, there have been significant advances on algorithms with worst-case guarantees on the serial runtime for special cases of this problem; we refer to
[
GQ17
,
DW22
,
GL23b
]
for a survey. To the best of our knowledge, polynomial-time isomorphism tests for quasigroups that are not groups have not been previously investigated.

Key motivation for
QGpI
comes from its relationship to the
Graph Isomorphism
problem (
GI
). When the quasigroups are given verbosely by their multiplication tables, there exists an
\textsf{AC}^{0}
-computable many-one reduction from
QGpI
to
GI

[
ZKT85
]
. On the other hand, there is no reduction from
GI
to
QGpI
computable by
AC
circuits of depth
o(\log n/\log\log n)
and size
n^{\text{polylog}(n)}

[
CTW13
]
. In light of Babai’s breakthrough result that
GI
is quasipolynomial-time solvable
[
Bab16
]
,
QGpI
in the Cayley model is a key barrier to improving the complexity of
GI
. There is considerable evidence suggesting that
GI
is not
NP
-complete
[
Sch88
,
BH92
,
IPZ01
,
Bab16
,
KST92
,
AK06
]
. As
QGpI
reduces to
GI
, this evidence also suggests that
QGpI
is not
NP
-complete.

Despite the fact that
QGpI
is strictly easier than
GI
under
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
-reductions, there are several key approaches in the
GI
literature, such as parallelization, Weisfeiler–Leman, and canonization, that have received comparatively little attention even in the special case of
GpI
– see the discussion of Further Related Work (Section
1.1
). In this paper, we will make advances in each of these directions.

There is a natural divide-and-conquer strategy for
GpI
, which proceeds as follows. Given groups
G_{1},G_{2}
, we first seek to decompose the groups in terms of normal subgroups
N_{i}\trianglelefteq G_{i}
(
i\in[2]
) and the corresponding quotients
G_{i}/N_{i}
. It is then necessary (but not sufficient) to decide if
N_{1}\cong N_{2}
and
G_{1}/N_{1}\cong G_{2}/N_{2}
. This strategy has led to algorithmic advances for groups admitting
nice
decompositions, including for instance, direct product decompositions
[
KN09
,
Wil12
,
BS22
,
GL23b
]
, coprime
[
Gal09
,
QST11
,
BQ12
,
GL23b
]
and tame
[
GQ15
]
extensions, and central extensions
[
LW12
,
BMW17
,
IQ19
,
BGL+19
,
GQ17
]
. In case of quasigroups the situation is made more complicated by the lack of normal substructures; one has to factor by congruences instead. (Recall that a
congruence
of an algebra
A
is an equivalence relation on
A
that is compatible with all operations of
A
.) We therefore focus on the isomorphism problem for quasigroups that admit representations in terms of more structured algebras, such as groups and their automorphisms.

Main Results.
In this paper, we investigate the parallel complexity of isomorphism testing for finite groups and quasigroups, by taking advantage of key decompositions.

We first investigate the complexity of computing direct product decompositions. Brachter and Schweitzer
[
BS22
]
showed that Weisfeiler–Leman (WL) can identify direct products in polynomial-time, provided it can also identify the indecomposable direct factors in polynomial-time. Grochow and Levet
[
GL23b
]
parallelized this result, essentially showing that WL can detect the indecomposable direct factors using only
O(\log n)
rounds. On the other hand, Grohcow and Levet (
ibid
.) showed that the analogous result fails to hold for the
count-free
variant of WL. While cyclic groups are identified by the count-free
2
-WL, there exist infinite families
(G_{m},H_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
of non-isomorphic Abelian groups that require
\Omega(\log|G_{m}|)
-dimensional (and hence,
\Theta(\log n)
-dimensional) count-free WL to distinguish between
G_{m}
and
H_{m}
.

It is thus natural to inquire as to the ability of count-free WL to identify groups that admit
nice
direct product decompositions. The Remak–Krull–Schmidt theorem provides that for any two fully-refined direct product decompositions
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}
and
\mathcal{T}=\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{k}\}
of a group
G
, there exists a permutation
\pi\in\text{Sym}(k)
such that
S_{i}\cong T_{\pi(i)}
for all
i\in[k]
. In general, however,
S_{i}
and
T_{\pi(i)}
need not be the same subgroup (setwise). On the other hand, it is well-known that when
S_{i}
is centerless, it appears (setwise) in every fully-refined direct product decomposition of
G
. The same holds true when
S_{i}
is perfect (see Corollary
3.6
). For this reason, we investigate the count-free Weisfeiler–Leman dimension for the following class of groups.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the class of groups
G
, where
G
admits a direct product decomposition into indecomposable direct factors
G=\prod_{i=1}^{k}S_{i}
, such that each
S_{i}
is (i)
O(1)
-generated, and (ii) either perfect or centerless.

Note that
\mathcal{C}
includes, for instance, direct products of almost simple groups.

For
k\geq 2,r\geq 0
, let
(k,r)
-WL denote running
k
-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman for
r
rounds. Our first main result is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G\in\mathcal{C}
be a group of order
n
.

(a)

The count-free
(O(1),O(\log\log n))
-WL Version II algorithm identifies
G
.

(b)

The counting
(O(1),O(1))
-WL Version II algorithm identifies
G
.

In particular, the constants hidden by the Big-O terms depend only on
\mathcal{C}
.

Grochow and Levet previously established that the counting
(O(1),O(\log n))
-WL Version II identifies the groups in
\mathcal{C}
. In light of the parallel WL implementation due to Grohe and Verbitsky
[
GV06
]
(see also Section
2.5
), Grochow and Levet obtained that isomorphism testing between a group
G\in\mathcal{C}
and an arbitrary group
H
is in
\textsf{TC}^{1}
. Theorem
1.2
(a) already improves this bound to
\textsf{AC}^{1}
.

Theorem
1.2
(b) is obtained by a more careful analysis of the counting WL algorithm that takes advantage of the group-theoretic structure afforded by
\mathcal{C}
– namely, that the indecomposable direct factors are unique as sets. In addition to making precise the power of counting in this situation (reducing the number of rounds from
O(\log\log n)
to
O(1)
), we obtain further improvements in the parallel complexity for isomorphism testing:

Corollary 1.3
.

There exists a logspace algorithm that, given
G\in\mathcal{C}
and an arbitrary group
H
, correctly decides if
G\cong H
.

We also compare Theorem
1.2
(a) to the work of Collins and Levet
[
CL24
]
, who showed that count-free WL can be fruitfully leveraged as a subroutine for isomorphism testing of direct products of non-Abelian simple groups. Note that such groups all belong to
\mathcal{C}
. Collins and Levet showed that the count-free
(O(1),O(\log\log n))
-WL Version I will distinguish between two non-isomorphic direct factors. They then used
O(\log n)
co-nondeterministic bits and a single
Majority
gate to verify the multiplicities of the isomorphism type for each direct factor appeared the same number of times in both groups. This yields a bound of
\forall^{\log n}\textsf{MAC}^{0}(\textsf{FOLL})
(following the notation of
[
CGLW24
]
). Theorem
1.2
(a) shows that count-free WL
on its own
can identify this class of groups. On the other hand, the bound obtained by Collins and Levet of
\forall^{\log n}\textsf{MAC}^{0}(\textsf{FOLL})
is stronger than the bound of
\textsf{AC}^{1}
afforded by Theorem
1.2
(a). This brings us to our second main result.

Theorem 1.4
.

The count-free
(O(1),O(\log\log n))
-WL Version I algorithm identifies every group
G
, that decomposes as a direct product of non-Abelian simple groups.

While the initial coloring of count-free WL Version II is
L
-computable
[
Tan13
]
, the initial coloring of count-free WL Version I is
\textsf{AC}^{0}
-computable (see Section
2.5
for more details). Consequently, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 1.5
.

There exists a uniform
\mathsf{FOLL}
algorithm that decides isomorphism between a group
G
that decomposes as a direct product of non-Abelian simple groups, and an arbitrary group
H
.

Our next result concerns the canonization problem. For a class
\mathcal{K}
of objects, a
canonical form
is a function
F:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{K}
, such that for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}
, we have (i)
X\cong F(X)
, and (ii)
X\cong Y\iff F(X)=F(Y).
Isomorphism testing reduces to canonization, and the converse remains open. Here, we establish the following:

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
d>0
be a constant. There exists a uniform
\textsf{AC}^{3}
algorithm such that the following holds. Let
G
be a group of order
n
that admits a fully-refined direct product decomposition, such that each indecomposable direct factor is
d
-generated. Our algorithm computes a canonical labeling of
G
.

Note that the groups considered in Theorem
1.6
include those in
\mathcal{C}
.

We establish Theorem
1.6
in two steps. First, we will compute a fully-refined direct product decomposition of
G
. Second, we will use
(O(1),O(1))
-WL Version II on
G
, which will in turn allow us to pick out a canonical generating set for each indecomposable direct factor, and hence compute a canonical labeling for each direct factor. We also note that the color classes from WL are labeled using numbers (see e.g.,
[
GV06
]
). This will in turn allow us to order the indecomposable direct factors.

In order to explicitly compute a fully-refined direct product decomposition, we will establish the following:

Theorem 1.7

(cf.
[
KN09
]
)
.

Let
G
be a finite group given by its multiplication table. We can compute a fully-refined direct product decomposition of
G
in
\textsf{AC}^{3}
.

Previously, Kayal and Nezhmetdinov
[
KN09
]
established polynomial-time bounds for computing a fully-refined direct product decomposition of
G
. Wilson
[
Wil12
]
established the analogous result in the succinct
quotients of permutation groups
model. As a consequence, Wilson obtained polynomial-time bounds for computing a fully-refined direct product decomposition in the multiplication table model, by considering the regular representation of the input group. We establish Theorem
1.7
by parallelizing the work of Kayal and Nezhmetdinov
[
KN09
]
.

Remark 1.8
.

It remains an intriguing open question as to whether the problem of computing a fully-refined direct product decomposition admits an
NC
solution in the setting of quotients of permutation groups. There are a number of obstacles outlined in
[
Wil12
, Section 8]
. Additionally, it is open whether certain key problems such as computing the centralizer
C_{G}(N)
, for
N\trianglelefteq G
, admit
NC
solutions in the setting of quotients of permutation groups.

We finally consider central quasigroups.

Definition 1.9
.

A quasigroup
(Q,*)
is
central
if there is an Abelian group
(Q,+)
, automorphisms
\phi
,
\psi
of
(Q,+)
and
c\in Q
such that

x*y=\phi(x)+\psi(y)+c

(1)

for all
x,y\in Q
. We denote the corresponding quasigroup by
\mathcal{Q}(Q,+,\phi,\psi,c)
.

Central quasigroups play an important role since they are precisely the Abelian objects (in the sense of universal algebra) in the variety of quasigroups
[
Sze98
]
. See
[
KN
,
KN71
,
Dra09
,
Smi76
,
Smi90
]
for an introduction to central quasigroups and
[
Hou12
,
Kir95
,
SV15
]
for enumeration of certain classes of central quasigroups up to isomorphism.

We establish the following.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
G_{1}
be a central quasigroup, and let
G_{2}
be an arbitrary quasigroup. Suppose that
G_{1},G_{2}
are given by their multiplication tables. We can decide isomorphism between
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
in
NC
.

In contrast, the complexity of isomorphism testing for Abelian groups has been well-investigated
[
LSZ77
,
Vik96
,
Sav80
,
Kav07
,
Ili85
,
Vil94
,
CTW13
,
GL23b
,
CGLW24
]
, resulting in a linear time serial algorithm
[
Kav07
]
and parallel bounds of
\forall^{\log\log n}\textsf{MAC}^{0}(\mathsf{DTISP}(\mathrm{polylog}(n),\mathrm{log}(n)))
, the latter of which is a proper subclass of
\mathsf{L}

[
CGLW24
]
.

A quasigroup
(Q,*)
is
medial
(also
entropic
) if it satisfies the medial law, for all
x,y,u,v\in Q
:

(x*y)*(u*v)=(x*u)*(y*v).

It was shown by Toyoda–Murdoch–Bruck
[
Toy41
,
Mur41
,
Bru63
]
that medial quasigroups form a subclass of central quasigroups. In more detail, up to isomorphism, medial quasigroups are precisely the central quasigroups
\mathcal{Q}(Q,+,\phi,\psi,c)
such that
\phi\psi=\psi\phi
. A geometric interpretation of this representation can be found in
[
Vol86
]
. Small entropic quasigroups were enumerated in
[
SV15
]
. The medial law has also been studied more generally in
[
Bon22
,
JK83
]
.

Parallelization.

-generated quasigroups

,

,

,

, graphical groups arising from the CFI graphs
[

,

,

]
, coprime extensions

O(1)
-generated and

is Abelian
[

]
(parallelizing a result from
[

]
), groups of almost all orders

(parallelizing
[

]

]
(parallelizing
[

The

k
-WL) serves as a key combinatorial tool in

[
Kie19
]
. In the case of graphs of bounded treewidth

,
LRS24
]
, planar graphs

GV06

,
GK21
]
, and graphs of bounded rank-width
[
LRS24
]
, Weisfeiler–Leman serves even as a

isomorphism test. Despite the success of WL as an isomorphism test, it is insufficient to place

into
P

[
CFI92
,
NS18
]
. Nonetheless, WL remains an active area of research. For instance, Babai’s quasipolynomial-time algorithm
[
Bab16
]
combines

-WL with group-theoretic techniques.

The use of combinatorial techinques for

is relatively new; and to the best of our knowledge, was initiated in

BGL+19

Preliminaries

2.1
Groups and Quasigroups

All groups and quasigroups will be assumed to be finite.

Groups.

Let
G
be a group. Given
g,h\in G
, the
commutator

[g,h]:=ghg^{-1}h^{-1}
. For sets
X,Y\subseteq G
, the
commutator subgroup

[X,Y]:=\langle\{[g,h]:g\in X,h\in Y\}\rangle
. We say that
G
is
perfect
if
G=[G,G]
, and that
G
is
centerless
if
Z(G)=1
. Let
d(G)
be the minimum size taken over all generating sets of
G
. A
basis
of an Abelian group
A
is a set of generators
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
such that
A=\langle a_{1}\rangle\times\cdots\times\langle a_{k}\rangle.

Quasigroups.

A
quasigroup
consists of a set
Q
and a binary operation
*:Q\times Q\to Q
satisfying the following. For every
a,b\in Q
, there exist unique
x,y\in Q
such that
a*x=b
and
y*a=b
. We write
x=a\backslash b
and
y=b/a
, i.e.,
a*(a\backslash b)=b
and
(b/a)*a=b
. When the multiplication operation is understood, we simply write
ax
for
a*x
.

If
(Q_{1},*)
,
(Q_{2},\circ)
are quasigroups, then
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
is an
isotopism
from
(Q_{1},*)
to
(Q_{2},\circ)
if
\alpha,\beta,\gamma:Q_{1}\to Q_{2}
are bijections and
\alpha(x)\circ\beta(y)=\gamma(x*y)
for all
x,y\in Q_{1}
. Being isotopic is an equivalence relation on quasigroups. The following result is well known:

Proposition 2.1

(
[
Alb43
]
)
.

If two groups are isotopic, they are isomorphic. In particular, if a quasigroup
(Q,*)
is isotopic to groups
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, then
G_{1}
is isomorphic to
G_{2}
.

For
x\in(Q,*)
, let
L_{x}:Q\to Q
be the
left translation
by
x
, where
L_{x}(y)=x*y
. For a quasigroup
(Q,*)
, let

\mathrm{Dis}(Q,*)=\langle L_{x}L_{y}^{-1}:x,y\in Q\rangle.

The group
\mathrm{Dis}(Q,*)
acts naturally on
Q
, being a subgroup of the symmetric group on
Q
. For any
e\in Q
, note that
\mathrm{Dis}(Q,*)=\langle L_{x}L_{e}^{-1}:x\in X\rangle
, since
L_{x}L_{y}^{-1}=(L_{x}L_{e}^{-1})(L_{y}L_{e}^{-1})^{-1}
.

2.2
Complexity Classes

We assume that the reader is familiar with standard complexity classes such as
\textsf{P},\textsf{NP},\textsf{L}
, and
NL
. For a standard reference on circuit complexity, see
[
Vol99
]
. We consider Boolean circuits using the gates
AND
,
OR
,
NOT
, and
Majority
, where
\textsf{Majority}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=1
if and only if
\geq n/2
of the inputs are
1
. Otherwise,
\textsf{Majority}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
. In this paper, we will consider
DLOGTIME
-uniform circuit families
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. For this,
one encodes the gates of each circuit

by bit strings of length
O(\log n)
. Then the circuit family
(C_{n})_{n\geq 0}

is called
DLOGTIME
-uniform
if (i) there exists a deterministic Turing machine that computes for a given gate
u\in\{0,1\}^{*}

of
C_{n}
(
|u|\in O(\log n)
) in time
O(\log n)
the type of gate
u
, where the types are
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
,
NOT
,
AND
,

, or
Majority

and (ii) there exists a deterministic Turing machine that decides for two given gates
u,v\in\{0,1\}^{*}
of
C_{n}
(

) and a binary encoded integer
i

O(\log n)
many bits in time
O(\log n)
whether
u

v
.

Definition 2.2
.

Fix
k\geq 0
. We say that a language
L
belongs to (uniform)
\textsf{NC}^{k}
if there exist a (uniform) family of circuits
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
over the
\textsf{AND},\textsf{OR},\textsf{NOT}
gates such that the following hold:

•

The
AND
and
OR
gates take exactly
2
inputs. That is, they have fan-in
2
.

•

C_{n}
has depth
O(\log^{k}n)
and uses (has size)
n^{O(1)}
gates. Here, the implicit constants in the circuit depth and size depend only on
L
.

•

x\in L
if and only if
C_{|x|}(x)=1
.

The complexity class
\mathsf{AC}^{k}
is defined analogously as
\textsf{NC}^{k}
, except that the
\textsf{AND},\textsf{OR}
gates are permitted to have unbounded fan-in.
That is, a single
AND
gate can compute an arbitrary conjunction, and a single
OR
gate can compute an arbitrary disjunction. The complexity class
\textsf{TC}^{k}
is defined analogously as
\mathsf{AC}^{k}
, except that our circuits are now permitted
Majority
gates of unbounded fan-in.
We also allow circuits to compute functions by using multiple output gates.

For every
k
, the following containments are well-known:

\textsf{NC}^{k}\subseteq\mathsf{AC}^{k}\subseteq\textsf{TC}^{k}\subseteq\textsf{NC}^{k+1}.

In the case of
k=0
, we have that:

\textsf{NC}^{0}\subsetneq\mathsf{AC}^{0}\subsetneq\textsf{TC}^{0}\subseteq\textsf{NC}^{1}\subseteq\mathsf{L}\subseteq\textsf{NL}\subseteq\mathsf{AC}^{1}.

We note that functions that are
\textsf{NC}^{0}
-computable can only depend on a bounded number of input bits. Thus,
\textsf{NC}^{0}
is unable to compute the
AND
function. It is a classical result that
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
is unable to compute
Parity

[
FSS84
]
. The containment
\textsf{TC}^{0}\subseteq\textsf{NC}^{1}
(and hence,
\textsf{TC}^{k}\subseteq\textsf{NC}^{k+1}
) follows from the fact that
\textsf{NC}^{1}
can simulate the unbounded fan-in
Majority
gate.

The complexity class
FOLL
is the set of languages decidable by uniform
AC
circuits of depth
O(\log\log n)
and polynomial-size
[
BKLM01
]
. It is known that
\textsf{AC}^{0}\subsetneq\mathsf{FOLL}\subsetneq\textsf{AC}^{1}
, the former by a simple diagonalization argument on top of Sipser’s result
[
Sip83
]
, and the latter because the
Parity
function is in
\mathsf{AC}^{1}
but not
\mathsf{FOLL}
(nor any depth
o(\log n/\log\log n)
).
\mathsf{FOLL}
cannot contain any complexity class that can compute
Parity
, such as
\mathsf{TC}^{0},\mathsf{NC}^{1},\mathsf{L}
, or
\mathsf{NL}
, and it remains open whether any of these classes contain
\mathsf{FOLL}
.

Denote by
FL
the class of logspace computable functions.

2.3
Weisfeiler–Leman

We begin by recalling the Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm for graphs, which computes an isomorphism-invariant coloring. Let
\Gamma
be a graph, and let
k\geq 2
be an integer. The
k
-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman, or
k
-WL, algorithm begins by constructing an initial coloring
\chi_{k,0}:V(\Gamma)^{k}\to\mathcal{K}
, where
\mathcal{K}
is our set of colors, by assigning each
k
-tuple a color based on its isomorphism type. That is, two
k
-tuples
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})
and
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
receive the same color under
\chi_{k,0}
iff the map
v_{i}\mapsto u_{i}
(for all
i\in[k]
) is an isomorphism of the induced subgraphs
\Gamma[\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}]
and
\Gamma[\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}]
and for all
i,j
,
v_{i}=v_{j}\Leftrightarrow u_{i}=u_{j}
.

For
r\geq 0
, the coloring computed at the
r
th iteration of Weisfeiler–Leman is refined as follows. For a
k
-tuple
\overline{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})
and a vertex
x\in V(\Gamma)
, define

\overline{v}(v_{i}/x)=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{i-1},x,v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{k}).

The coloring computed at the
(r+1)
st iteration, denoted
\chi_{k,r+1}
, stores the color of the given
k
-tuple
\overline{v}
at the
r
th iteration, as well as the colors under
\chi_{k,r}
of the
k
-tuples obtained by substituting a single vertex in
\overline{v}
for another vertex
x
. We examine this multiset of colors over all such vertices
x
. This is formalized as follows:

\displaystyle\chi_{k,r+1}(\overline{v})=

\displaystyle(\chi_{r}(\overline{v}),\{\!\!\{(\chi_{r}(\overline{v}(v_{1}/x)),\ldots,\chi_{r}(\overline{v}(v_{k}/x))\bigr|x\in V(\Gamma)\}\!\!\}),

where
\{\!\!\{\cdot\}\!\!\}
denotes a multiset.

Note that the coloring
\chi_{k,r}
computed at iteration
r
induces a partition of
V(\Gamma)^{k}
into color classes. The Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm terminates when this partition is not refined, that is, when the partition induced by
\chi_{k,r+1}
is identical to that induced by
\chi_{k,r}
. The final coloring is referred to as the
stable coloring
, which we denote
\chi_{k,\infty}:=\chi_{k,r}
.

The
count-free
variant of
k
-WL works identically as the classical variant, except at the refinement step, we consider the set of colors rather than the full multi-set. We re-use the notation
\chi_{k,r}
to denote the coloring computed by count-free
(k,r)
-WL; context should make it clear whether
\chi_{k,r}
refers to count-free or counting WL (we never use
\chi_{k,r}
to denote the count-free coloring when discussing counting WL, nor vice versa).

Precisely:

\displaystyle\chi_{k,r+1}(\overline{v})=

\displaystyle(\chi_{r}(\overline{v}),\{(\chi_{r}(\overline{v}(v_{1}/x)),\ldots,\chi_{r}(\overline{v}(v_{k}/x))\bigr|x\in V(\Gamma)\}).

Let
k\geq 2,r\geq 0
, and let
G
be a graph. We say that the classical counting variant of
(k,r)
-WL
distinguishes

G
from the graph
H
if there exists a color class
C
such that:

|\{\bar{x}\in V(G)^{k}:\chi_{k,r}(\bar{x})=C\}|\neq|\{\bar{x}\in V(H)^{k}:\chi_{k,r}(\bar{x})=C\}|.

Similarly, the count-free variant of
(k,r)
-WL
distinguishes

G
from the graph
H
if there exists a color class
C
and some
\bar{x}\in V(G)^{k}
where
\chi_{k,r}(\bar{x})=C
, but for all
\bar{y}\in V(H)^{k}
,
\chi_{k,r}(\bar{y})\neq C
. We say that the classical counting (resp., count-free)
(k,r)
-WL
identifies

G
if for all
H\not\cong G
,
(k,r)
-WL (resp., count-free
(k,r)
-WL) distinguishes
G
from
H
.

Brachter and Schweitzer
[
BS20
]
introduced three variants of WL for groups. We will restrict attention to the first two variants. WL Versions I and II are both executed directly on the Cayley tables, where
k
-tuples of group elements are initially colored. For WL Version I, two
k
-tuples
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{k})
and
(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})
receive the same initial color iff (a) for all
i,j,\ell\in[k]
,
g_{i}g_{j}=g_{\ell}\iff h_{i}h_{j}=h_{\ell}
, and (b) for all
i,j\in[k]
,
g_{i}=g_{j}\iff h_{i}=h_{j}
. For WL Version II,
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{k})
and
(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})
receive the same initial color iff the map
g_{i}\mapsto h_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
extends to an isomorphism of the generated subgroups
\langle g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\rangle
and
\langle h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}\rangle
. For both WL Versions I and II, refinement is performed in the classical manner as for graphs. Namely, for a given
k
-tuple
\overline{g}
of group elements,

\displaystyle\chi_{k,r+1}(\overline{g})=

\displaystyle(\chi_{r}(\overline{g}),\{\!\!\{(\chi_{r}(\overline{g}(g_{1}/x)),\ldots,\chi_{r}(\overline{g}(g_{k}/x))\bigr|x\in G\}\!\!\}).

The count-free variants of WL Versions I and II are defined in the identical manner as for graphs. The notions of whether (count-free) WL distinguishes two groups or identifies a group, are defined analogously as in the case for graphs.

2.4
Pebbling Games

Bijective Pebbling Game for Groups.

We recall the bijective pebble games for WL Versions I and II on groups
[
Hel89
,
Hel96
,
BS20
]
. These games are often used to show that two groups
G
and
H
can(not) be distinguished by
k
-WL. The game is an Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé game (cf.,
[
EFT94
,
Lib04
]
), with two players: Spoiler and Duplicator. We begin with
k+1
pairs of pebbles. Prior to the start of the game, each pebble pair
(p_{i},p_{i}^{\prime})
is initially placed either beside the groups or on a given pair of group elements
v_{i}\mapsto v_{i}^{\prime}
(where
v_{i}\in G,v_{i}^{\prime}\in H
). Each round
r
proceeds as follows.

1.

Spoiler picks up a pair of pebbles
(p_{i},p_{i}^{\prime})
.

2.

We check the winning condition, which will be formalized later.

3.

Duplicator chooses a bijection
f_{r}:G\to H
.

4.

Spoiler places
p_{i}
on some vertex
g\in G
. Then
p_{i}^{\prime}
is placed on
f(g)
.

Suppose that
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{\ell})\mapsto(h_{1},\ldots,h_{\ell})
have been pebbled. In Version I, Duplicator wins at the given round if this map satisfies the initial coloring condition of WL Version I: (a) for all
i,j,m\in[\ell]
,
g_{i}g_{j}=g_{m}\iff h_{i}h_{j}=h_{m}
, and (b) for all
i,j\in[\ell]
,
g_{i}=g_{j}\iff h_{i}=h_{j}
. In Version II, Duplicator wins at the given round if the map
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{\ell})\mapsto(h_{1},\ldots,h_{\ell})
extends to an isomorphism of the generated subgroups
\langle g_{1},\ldots,g_{\ell}\rangle
and
\langle h_{1},\ldots,h_{\ell}\rangle.
Brachter & Schweitzer established that for
J\in\{I,II\}
,
(k,r)
-WL Version J is equivalent to version J of the
(k+1)
-pebble,
r
-round pebble game
[
BS20
]
.

Count-Free Pebbling Game for Groups.

There exist analogous pebble games for count-free WL Versions I-II. The count-free
(k+1)
-pebble game consists of two players: Spoiler and Duplicator, as well as
(k+1)
pebble pairs
(p,p^{\prime})
. In both versions, Spoiler wishes to show that the two groups
G
and
H
are not isomorphic. Duplicator wishes to show that the two groups are isomorphic. Each round of the game proceeds as follows.

1.

Spoiler picks up a pebble pair
(p_{i},p_{i}^{\prime})
.

2.

The winning condition is checked. The Version
J\in\{I,II\}
winning condition here is the same as for the Version
J
counting pebble game.

3.

Spoiler places one of the pebbles on some group element (either
p_{i}
on some element of
G
or
p_{i}^{\prime}
on some element of
H
).

4.

Duplicator places the other pebble on some element of the other group.

Spoiler wins, by definition, at round
0
if
G
and
H
do not have the same number of elements. Suppose that
|G|=|H|
, and let
\bar{g}\in G^{k},\bar{h}\in H^{k}
. We have that
\bar{g},\bar{h}
are not distinguished by the first
r
rounds of the count-free
k
-WL version
J
if and only if Duplicator wins the first
r
rounds of the count-free Version
J

(k+1)
-pebble game
[
IL90
,
CFI92
,
GL23b
]
.

2.5
Weisfeiler–Leman as a Parallel Algorithm

Grohe & Verbitsky
[
GV06
]
previously showed that for fixed
k
, the classical
k
-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm for graphs can be effectively parallelized. Precisely, each iteration of the classical counting WL algorithm (including the initial coloring) can be implemented using a logspace uniform
\textsf{TC}^{0}
circuit, and each iteration of the
count-free
WL algorithm can be implemented using a logspace uniform
\textsf{AC}^{0}
circuit. A careful analysis shows that this parallelization holds under
DLOGTIME
-uniformity. As they mention (
[
GV06
, Remark 3.4]
), their implementation works for any first-order structure, including groups. In particular, the initial coloring of count-free WL Version I is
DLOGTIME
-uniform
\textsf{AC}^{0}
-computable, and each refinement step is
DLOGTIME
-uniform
\textsf{AC}^{0}
-computable. However, because for groups we have different versions of WL, we explicitly list out the resulting parallel complexities, which differ slightly between the versions.

•

WL Version I:
Let
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{k})
and
(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})
be two
k
-tuples of group elements. We may test in
\textsf{AC}^{0}
whether (a) for all
i,j,m\in[k]
,
g_{i}g_{j}=g_{m}\iff h_{i}h_{j}=h_{m}
, and (b)
g_{i}=g_{j}\iff h_{i}=h_{j}
. So we may decide if two
k
-tuples receive the same initial color in
\textsf{AC}^{0}
. Comparing the (multi)set of colors at the end of each iteration (including after the initial coloring), as well as the refinement steps, proceed identically as in
[
GV06
]
. Thus, for fixed
k
, each iteration of the counting
k
-WL Version I can be implemented using a
DLOGTIME
-uniform
\textsf{TC}^{0}
circuit, and each iteration of the count-free
k
-WL Version I can be implemented using a
DLOGTIME
-uniform
\textsf{AC}^{0}
circuit.

•

WL Version II:
Let
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{k})
and
(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})
be two
k
-tuples of group elements. We may use the marked isomorphism test of Tang
[
Tan13
]
to test in
L
whether the map sending
g_{i}\mapsto h_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
extends to an isomorphism of the generated subgroups
\langle g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\rangle
and
\langle h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}\rangle
. So we may decide whether two
k
-tuples receive the same initial color in
L
. Comparing the (multi)set of colors at the end of each iteration (including after the initial coloring), as well as the refinement steps, proceed identically as in
[
GV06
]
. Thus, for fixed
k
, the initial coloring of both the counting and count-free
k
-WL Version II is
L
-computable. Each refinement step of the counting
k
-WL Version II is computable using a
DLOGTIME
-uniform
\textsf{TC}^{0}
-circuit, and each refinement step of the count-free
k
-WL Version II is computable using a
DLOGTIME
-uniform
\textsf{AC}^{0}
-circuit.

Weisfeiler–Leman and Direct Products

In this section, we will establish Theorem
1.2
.

3.1
Additional Preliminaries for Direct Products

We will begin by recalling additional preliminaries for direct products.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that a group
G
is
directly indecomposable
if the only direct product decomposition of
G
is
\{G\}
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{S}:=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}
be a direct product decomposition of the group
G
. We say that
\mathcal{S}
is
fully refined
if for each
i\in[k]
,
S_{i}
is indecomposable.

The Remak–Krull–Schmidt theorem guarantees that for any group
G
and any two fully refined direct product decompositions
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}
of
G
, the isomorphism types of the indecomposable factors and their multiplicities are the same in
\mathcal{S}
as in
\mathcal{T}
. However, in general,
\mathcal{S}\neq\mathcal{T}
. Consider, for instance, the Klein group
\mathbb{V}_{4}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{2}
. Let
E_{1}=\langle(1,0)\rangle
,
E_{2}=\langle(0,1)\rangle
and
F=\langle(1,1)\rangle
. Now,
\mathbb{V}_{4}=E_{1}\times E_{2}=E_{1}\times F
, but
E_{2}\neq F
, even though
E_{2}\cong F
.

We will now recall results on when any two fully refined direct product decompositions
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}
of
G
satisfy
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{T}
, as well as when a given subgroup
N\trianglelefteq G
appears (as a set) in any fully refined direct product decomposition of
G
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
G
be a group. A
central automorphism

\alpha
of
G
is an automorphism of
G
that acts trivially on
G/Z(G)
, that is,
\alpha(gZ(G))=gZ(G)
for every
g\in G
.

Theorem 3.4

(
[
Rob82
, Theorem 3.3.8]
)
.

Let
G
be a group, and let
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}
,
\mathcal{T}=\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{k}\}
be fully refined direct product decompositions of
G
. Then there exists a central automorphism
\alpha
of
G
and a permutation
\pi\in\text{Sym}(k)
such that
\alpha(S_{i})=T_{\pi(i)}
for every
i\in[k]
.

We use Theorem
3.4
to establish conditions for when a group
G
admits a unique fully refined direct product decomposition.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
G
be a group, and let
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}
be a fully refined direct product decomposition of
G
. For any
i\in[k]
, we have that
S_{i}
appears in any fully refined direct product decomposition of
G
if and only if for all distinct
j\neq i
,
|S_{i}/[S_{i},S_{i}]|
and
|Z(G_{j})|
are coprime. In particular,
\mathcal{S}
is unique if and only if for all distinct
i,j\in[k]
,
|S_{i}/[S_{i},S_{i}]|
and
|Z(G_{j})|
are coprime.

Proof.

For direct product decompositions
A=\prod_{u=1}^{k}A_{u}
and
B=\prod_{v=1}^{\ell}B_{v}
let us write a homomorphism
\gamma:A\to B
as
\prod_{u=1}^{k}\gamma_{u}
, where
\gamma_{u}
is the restriction of
\gamma
to
A_{u}
, so that
\gamma(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})=\gamma(a_{1})\cdots\gamma(a_{k})=\gamma_{1}(a_{1})\cdots\gamma_{k}(a_{k})
, where
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in A
. Further, let
\gamma_{u}=\prod_{v=1}^{\ell}\gamma_{uv}
, where
\gamma_{uv}:A_{u}\to B_{v}
.

Throughout the proof, fix
i\in[k]
. Suppose that
|S_{i}/[S_{i},S_{i}]|
and
|Z(S_{j})|
are coprime for every
j\neq i
. Let
\mathcal{T}=\{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\}
be another fully refined product decomposition of
G
. By Theorem
3.4
, there exists a central automorphism
\alpha
and a permutation
\pi\in\text{Sym}(k)
such that
\alpha(S_{i})=T_{\pi(i)}
. We will show that
\alpha(S_{i})=S_{i}
, witnessing that
S_{i}
appears in
\mathcal{T}
.

Since
\alpha
is central, for every
g\in G
there is
\beta(g)\in Z(G)
such that
\alpha(g)=g\beta(g)
. Since
\alpha
is a homomorphism, we have
gh\beta(gh)=\alpha(gh)=\alpha(g)\alpha(h)=g\beta(g)h\beta(h)=gh\beta(g)\beta(h)
, so
\beta:G\to Z(G)
is a homomorphism. Since
G=\prod_{u=1}^{k}S_{u}
, we have
Z(G)=\prod_{u=1}^{k}Z(S_{u})
. Let us write
\beta
as
\beta=\prod_{u=1}^{k}\beta_{u}
and
\beta_{u}=\prod_{v=1}^{k}\beta_{uv}
, where
\beta_{uv}:S_{u}\to Z(S_{v})
. Since
|S_{i}/[S_{i},S_{i}]|
and
|Z(S_{j})|
are coprime for every
j\neq i
, the homomorphisms
\beta_{ij}
are trivial for every
j\neq i
. Then
\alpha(S_{i})=S_{i}\beta(S_{i})=S_{i}\beta_{i}(S_{i})=S_{i}\beta_{ii}(S_{i})\subseteq S_{i}Z(S_{i})\subseteq S_{i}
. Since
\alpha
is an automorphism,
\alpha(S_{i})=S_{i}
.

Conversely, suppose that there is a
j\neq i
such that
|S_{i}/[S_{i},S_{i}]|
and
|Z(S_{j})|
are not coprime. We will construct a fully refined direct product decomposition of
G
in which
S_{i}
does not appear. Let
\beta_{ij}:S_{i}\to Z(S_{j})
be any nontrivial homomorphism. Set
\beta_{iv}=1
for every
v\neq j
,
\beta_{i}=\prod_{v=1}^{k}\beta_{iv}
,
\beta_{u}=1
for every
u\neq i
, and
\beta=\prod_{u=1}^{k}\beta_{u}
. Then
\beta
is a homomorphism
G\to Z(G)
. Let
\alpha:G\to Z(G)
be given by
\alpha(g)=g\beta(g)
. Since
\beta(G)\subseteq Z(G)
,
\alpha
is a central homomorphism. We will show that
\alpha
is an automorphism. It suffices to show that
\alpha
is injective. Let
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{k})\in G=\prod_{u=1}^{k}S_{u}
. Since
\beta_{ij}
is the only nontrivial component of
\beta
, we have
\alpha(g_{1},\dots,g_{k})=(g_{1},\dots,g_{j-1},g_{j}^{\prime},g_{j+1},\dots,g_{k})
, where
g_{j}^{\prime}=g_{j}\beta_{ij}(g_{i})
. Suppose that
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{k})\in G
and
\alpha(g)=\alpha(h)
. Then
g_{v}=h_{v}
for every
v\neq j
, and
g_{j}\beta_{ij}(g_{i})=h_{j}\beta_{ij}(h_{i})
. As
g_{i}=h_{i}
, we conclude that
g_{j}=h_{j}
, too, and
g=h
.

For every
u\in[k]
let
T_{u}=\alpha(S_{u})
. Since
\alpha
is an automorphism,
\mathcal{T}=\{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\}
is a fully refined direct product decomposition of
G
. We will show that
S_{i}\not\in\mathcal{T}
. We have
T_{u}=S_{u}
for every
u\neq i
, so it suffices to show that
T_{i}\neq S_{i}
. Let
g_{i}\in S_{i}
be such that
\beta_{ij}(g_{i})\neq 1
. Since
\beta_{ij}(S_{i})\subseteq S_{j}
and
S_{i}\cap S_{j}=1
, it follows that
\beta_{ij}(g_{i})\not\in S_{i}
. Now,
T_{i}=\alpha(S_{i})
contains

In this section, we will leverage the conjugacy class of a group (to be defined shortly) to compute a fully-refined direct product decomposition of a non-Abelian group. We first recall key preliminaries from
[

For the remainder of this section, we will assume that

Definition 4.8

(
[

)

If a conjugacy class is not reducible, then it is

(
[
KN09
, Proposition 2]
)

Let

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
(Q,*)
be a quasigroup. Then
(Q,*)
is isotopic to a group if and only if
\mathrm{Dis}(Q,*)
acts regularly on

, in which case
(Q,*)
is isotopic to
\mathrm{Dis}(Q,*)

Proof.

Suppose that
(Q,\cdot)
is a group and
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
is an isotopism from
(Q,*)

(Q,\cdot)
. Then

the left translation by

(Q,\cdot)

\lambda_{x}
the left translation by

in
(Q,*)
. We have

. Then
\lambda_{x}\lambda_{e}^{-1}=(\gamma^{-1}L_{\alpha(x)}\beta)(\gamma^{-1}L_{\alpha(e)}\beta)^{-1}=\gamma^{-1}L_{\alpha(x)}L_{\alpha(e)}^{-1}\gamma=\gamma^{-1}L_{\alpha(x)\alpha(e)^{-1}}\gamma

(Q,\cdot)
is a group. As the group

acts regularly on

\mathrm{Dis}(Q,*)

Conversely, suppose that
\mathrm{Dis}(Q,*)

\mathrm{Dis}(Q,*)=\{\lambda_{x}\lambda_{e}^{-1}:x\in Q\}

, exist. We may then test, in
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
, whether for all
x,y\in Q
,

x*y=\phi(x)+\psi(y)+c
, as prescribed by Definition
1.9
. The result now follows.
∎

We now turn to proving Theorem
6.1
.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

By Proposition
6.9
, we can decide in
\textsf{AC}^{0}
whether both
G_{1},G_{2}
are central; and if so, construct the Abelian groups
\mathrm{Dis}(G_{i},*_{i})
and their representations
\mathcal{Q}_{i}(G_{i},+,\phi_{i},\psi_{i},c_{i})\cong G_{i}
. Now using Proposition
4.5
, we may in
\textsf{AC}^{1}
compute a basis
b_{i1},\ldots,b_{ik}
for
\mathrm{Dis}(G_{i},*_{i})
, and sort the elements so that
|b_{ij}|\leq|b_{i,j+1}|
for all
i,j
. Note that
k\leq\log|G_{i}|
. In particular, this allows us to decide if
\mathrm{Dis}(G_{1},*_{i})\cong\mathrm{Dis}(G_{2},*_{2})
are isomorphic; and if so, construct an isomorphism
\tau:\mathrm{Dis}(G_{1},*_{1})\cong\mathrm{Dis}(G_{2},*_{2})
. Let
(G,+)
be the Abelian group such that
(G,+)\cong\mathrm{Dis}(G_{i},*_{i})
.

Now let:

\displaystyle\bar{x}:=(c_{1},\phi_{1}(b_{11}),\ldots,\phi_{1}(b_{1k}),\psi_{1}(b_{11}),\ldots,\psi_{1}(b_{1k})),

\displaystyle\bar{y}:=(c_{2},\phi_{2}(b_{21}),\ldots,\phi_{2}(b_{2k}),\psi_{2}(b_{21}),\ldots,\psi_{2}(b_{2k})).

For
u\in(G,+)
(precisely, we take
u\in\mathrm{Dis}(G_{1},*_{1})
), let:

\bar{x}_{u}=(c_{1}+u,\phi_{1}(b_{11}),\ldots,\phi_{1}(b_{1k}),\psi_{1}(b_{11}),\ldots,\psi_{1}(b_{1k})).

By Theorem
6.3
, it remains to decide whether there exists some
u\in\text{Im}(1-\phi_{1}-\psi_{1})
and an automorphism
\gamma\in\Aut(G,+)
such that
\bar{x}_{u}^{\gamma}=\bar{y}
. Thus, we have reduced to the
Pointwise Transporter
problem. We will in fact, show how to obtain all such
\gamma\in\Aut(G,+)
. Let
\text{Trans}(\bar{x}_{u},\bar{y})
be the set of
\gamma\in\Aut(G,+)
such that
\bar{x}_{u}^{\gamma}=\bar{y}
.

First, as we are given
\phi_{1},\psi_{1}
, we may in
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
compute
\text{Im}(1-\phi_{1}-\psi_{1})
by evaluating
(1-\phi_{1}-\psi_{1})(u)
for all
u\in(G,+)
. Now in
NC
, we may write down a set of
\poly(n)
generators for
\Aut(G,+)

[
Bir35
]
. Note that while Birkhoff
[
Bir35
]
prescribes matrix representations for the generators, we may easily obtain permutations by considering the actions of the matrices on the basis
b_{11},\ldots,b_{1k}
for
(G,+)
.

As we are considering the
Pointwise Transporter
problem on
2k+1\leq 2\lceil\log(n)\rceil+1
points, we have by Lemma
6.7
the requisite instances of
Pointwise Transporter
are
NC
-computable. If there exists some
u\in\text{Im}(1-\phi_{1}-\psi_{1})
such that
\text{Trans}(\bar{x}_{u},\bar{y})\neq\emptyset
, then our two quasigroups
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are isomorphic. Otherwise, we have that
G_{1}\not\cong G_{2}
.

In order to recover the set of automorphisms
\gamma\in\Aut(G,+)
as in Theorem
6.3
, we return:

\bigcup_{u\in\text{Im}(1-\phi_{1}-\psi_{1})}\text{Trans}(\bar{x}_{u},\bar{y}).

The result now follows.
∎

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigated the Weisfeiler–Leman dimension of groups that admit (unique) direct product decompositions, where each indecomposable direct factor is
O(1)
-generated, and either perfect or centerless. We showed that the count-free
(O(1),O(\log\log n))
-WL Version II and counting
(O(1),O(1))
-WL Version II algorithms identify every such group. Consequently, we obtained upper bounds of
L
for isomorphism testing between a group in this family, and an arbitrary group.

We also exhibited an
\textsf{AC}^{3}
canonization procedure for the class
\mathcal{D}
of groups where each indecomposable direct factor is
O(1)
-generated.

Finally, we obtained bounds of
NC
for isomorphism testing of central quasigroups. Previously, only the trivial bound of
n^{\log(n)+O(1)}
-time was known.

Our work leaves several open questions.

Question 7.1
.

Consider the subclass
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{D}
where each group in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
has a unique direct product decomposition. Does
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
have bounded count-free Weisfeiler–Leman dimension?

Note that
\mathcal{D}
includes the class of all Abelian groups. Grochow and Levet
[
GL23b
]
showed that the class of all Abelian groups has count-free WL dimension
\Theta(\log n)
.

Question 7.2
.

Is
\mathcal{D}
identified by the counting
(O(1),O(1))
-WL Version II?

Grochow and Levet
[
GL23b
]
showed that
(O(1),O(\log n))
-WL Version II identifies
\mathcal{D}
. It is unclear how to approach this, even in the restricted case when each group admits a unique fully-refined direct product decomposition.

Question 7.3
.

In the setting of groups specified by their multiplication tables, can we compute fully-refined direct product decompositions in
L
?

We are unable to resolve Question
7.3
even for Abelian groups– see Proposition
4.5
, which yields bounds of
\textsf{AC}^{1}
.

Question 7.4
.

Can we compute a fully-refined direct product decomposition for quasigroups, specified by their multiplication tables, in polynomial-time?

Question 7.5
.

Does isomorphism testing of central quasigroups belong to
\mathsf{L}
?

Note that isomorphism testing of Abelian groups belongs to
\mathsf{L}
; this bound has been improved to

\forall^{\log\log n}\textsf{MAC}^{0}(\mathsf{DTISP}(\mathrm{polylog}(n),\mathrm{log}(n)))
, which is a proper subclass of
L

[
CGLW24
]
. As central quasigroups are precisely the Abelian objects (in the sense of universal algebra) in the variety of quasigroups
[
Sze98
]
,
L
is a natural target.
