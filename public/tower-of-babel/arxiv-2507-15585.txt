Unequal Voices: How LLMs Construct Constrained Queer Narratives
Atreya Ghosal, Ashim Gupta, Vivek Srikumar

Abstract
One way social groups are marginalized in discourse is that the narratives told about them often default to a narrow, stereotyped range of topics. In contrast, default groups are allowed the full complexity of human existence. We describe the constrained representations of queer people in LLM generations in terms of harmful representations, narrow representations, and discursive othering and formulate hypotheses to test for these phenomena. Our results show that LLMs are significantly limited in their portrayals of queer personas.



1
Introduction

The representation of marginalized people in media influences the extent to which their humanity is acknowledged in the cultural zeitgeist and societal consciousness. Narratives — the cultural aggregation of discourse and stories about a topic — affect both the perceived and actual existence of marginalized groups
Meretoja 2017

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used in domains where their role is to interact with humans as creative, personified agents. For example, teaching roles
Steenstra et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
and in co-creative story writing
Zhao et al. 2023
. Thus, LLM outputs are increasingly becoming part of our cultural narratives.

Much work has been done on auditing LLMs for toxicity and bias. However, a model’s internal representations and associations, even when not explicitly negative or biased, can have problematic consequences.

Imagine a therapist chatbot
(
Heinz et al. 2025a
, e.g.,)
engaging with a disabled patient. It has been shown
Gadiraju et al. 2023
that LLM outputs about disabled people tend to contain examples of what is termed
inspiration porn

Young 2014
. The therapist chatbot might thus try to encourage the patient by referring to examples of high-achieving disabled people but end up causing a net adverse effect on the patient’s mental health.

Model outputs also provide examples of associations that are fine, even positive on the surface, but are problematic due to their prevalence when referring to marginalized people. For example, in
Figure

1
, the
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
model is prompted to assume a persona of a specific identity and answer a question about where the simulated person works, with no additional cues provided. When simulating a male persona, the output focuses on their career and community aspirations; in contrast, while simulating a queer male persona, the output focuses on the aspects of their job that relate to their identity.

While the specific instance provided appears innocuous on its own, it is an example of a recurrent pattern in LLM outputs where, in the absence of additional information, LLM portrayals of LGBTQ+ individuals default to focusing on their gender or sexuality —  a pattern that mirrors real-world portrayals of LGBTQ+ people
Hicks 2020
. In contrast, non-LGBTQ+ individuals are depicted as possessing a wide spectrum of roles and experiences, showcasing a diversity that is not similarly afforded to LGBTQ+ people.

In this work, we analyze and describe the narrow, constrained representation of LGBTQ+ people in terms of three textual phenomena- explicitly harmful representations, overly narrow representations, and discursive othering. We formulate four hypotheses to test the occurrence and frequency of these phenomena in LLM outputs. We show that LLM portrayals of LGBTQ+ people- in otherwise neutral settings- are (a) significantly different from non-queer identities and (b) tend to narrowly focus on identity-related topics to a degree not observed in portrayals of non-LGBTQ+ people.

Problem Statement

In this section, we will set up the hypotheses that are examined by the rest of the paper. Our focus in this work is on queer identities, and the hypotheses explicitly relate to the differences between LLM outputs with respect to queer and non-queer identities. However, the ideas discussed below can be generalized to any marginalized identities.

Harmful Representations.

Negative representations of identities generated by LLMs can reproduce or amplify existing social inequalities
(
Blodgett et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
. For example, if the latent association between the concepts ‘
Middle Eastern person
’ and ‘
terrorist
’ is present within an LLM’s internal representations, downstream applications that query the model for predictions related to security risks may systematically produce higher risk assessments for profiles perceived as Middle Eastern. In this scenario,
the underlying representational associations result in allocational harm for individuals from marginalized populations.

Noble 2018
and
Barocas et al. 2023
discuss the impacts of such harmful representations.

Representational Harms: Even Without Overt Negativity?

Representational harms can reinforce harmful or negative stereotypes.
Could representations that are not overtly negative perpetuate a harm?
Even in such circumstances, a parallel but distinct phenomenon can emerge. LLM outputs may present narrow representations of marginalized groups while affording dominant identity groups the full scope of human experience.

For example, as we will quantify in the following sections, LLMs can default to assuming that queer individuals are present in medical settings for reasons associated with their gender or sexuality —  a phenomenon akin to the ‘
Trans Broken Arm Syndrome
’
Wall et al. 2023
observed in clinical practice. While seeking gender- or sexuality-affirming care is not itself problematic, in practice, conversational agents that privilege identity-related topics when interacting with queer users may inadvertently derail conversations and fail to adequately address the user’s primary concerns
(
Heinz et al. 2025b
, e.g., mental health chatbots,)
. With such
narrow representations
, despite no explicit representational harms (i.e., without overtly stigmatizing output), redirecting attention can result in allocational harm by depriving users of appropriate support.

Discursive Othering.

A third type of subtle bias features LLMs “overcorrecting” by foregrounding concepts such as diversity, inclusion, or community participation in response to prompts mentioning marginalized identities, regardless of contextual relevance.

Additionally, the set of topics discussed in response to prompts mentioning marginalized identities tends to be focused around common ideas and stereotypes about the group, while socially dominant identities - considered the
default
- are afforded the full variety of human experience. This divergence in topics discussed is particularly notable when the setting mentioned in the prompts is ostensibly an identity-neutral, everyday setting. For example, in an office setting, people of all identities converse about the common topics of reports, deadlines, goals, performance, etc..
While merely differentiating- rather than derogating- marginalized identity groups in model outputs present neither representational nor allocational harms, it nevertheless ends up
othering

Spivak 1985
;
Jensen 2011
;
Mik-Meyer 2016
groups in otherwise neutral settings. Even under the guise of positive attention, this marks individuals and groups of individuals as distinct from the majority group, perpetuating subtle forms of social marginalization.

Thus, systematically auditing the representations of personas —  in particular, the relative representation of dominant groups and marginalized groups —  is essential to ensure fair and safe LLM deployment. In this work, we analyze and quantify the above types of bias, specifically focusing on queer identities, in terms of four hypotheses:

Hypothesis 4: The Full Spectrum of Human Experience

Our final hypothesis
H
4
asks if the topics discussed for one identity group are different from another. To measure this, we first need to identify the topics, or more precisely, the distribution of topics associated with model responses. In this section, we will first look at how we can map responses to topic distributions and then define a metric,
Topic Divergence
, that characterizes the difference between topics in LLM-generated outputs between any two different identities in a context.

Topic Distributions.

In the previous sections, we have used LLM-as-a-Judge to identify the presence of particular themes and topics within a given text, with these being explicitly provided to the model in the form of questions. We can extend this approach by tasking the LLM-Judge to independently determine the topics discussed in the input text, whether explicitly mentioned or latent.

Specifically, instead of prompting the LLM with predefined questions, we instruct the model to identify and list the key topics featured in the text. Details of the prompts are given in
B.6
.

We repeatedly sample the topics generated using the above prompt (in the following experiments, we take 50 samples per LLM response) to get an empirical distribution over topics for each LLM response. That is, given a set of responses
r(c,g)
for a context
c

We aggregate the topic distribution over the set of responses

Related Work and Discussion

Harms and Biases.

Crawford 2017
categorized the harms posed by biased AI systems into
allocational
and
representational harms
. Our work, while closer to studying representational harms, focuses on AI-generated outputs that, while not explicitly negative, may yet lead to allocational harms.

Bias in language technology.

The investigation of bias in the outputs of large language models (LLMs) has been the subject of considerable research attention
(
Blodgett et al. 2020
, e.g.)
. LLM generations have been shown to be biased against marginalized groups in their generations
(
Mei et al. 2023
;
Gupta et al. 2024
;
Fleisig et al. 2023
;
Lucy and Bamman 2021
, e.g.)
.

Most literature on the topic characterizes the biases, stereotypes, and harmful narratives present in LLM-generated text under the heading of representational harms
Suresh and Guttag 2021
;
Ghosh et al. 2025
. Under this broad umbrella, one line of work looks at stereotypes about marginalized groups in LLM generations
Jha et al. 2023
;
Abbasi et al. 2019
;
Leidinger and Rogers 2023
. Our research (
H
4
) also identifies the presence of stereotypes in model outputs
Jha et al. 2023
.
The existence of biased associations, even in the absence of explicit stereotypes, has been studied by
Beck et al. 2024
and
Gupta et al. 2024
.

Our work aligns closely with the work of
Cheng et al. 2023
. They study
caricature
in terms of exaggeration and
individuation
as two distinct phenomena. Hypothesis
H
2
in our work measures individuation using the LLM-as-a-judge method, while hypotheses
H
3
and
H
4
measure exaggerated portrayals of identity.
We extend the work of
Cheng et al. 2023
in two crucial respects. Firstly, we study
queer identity
as a distinct group of identities, as defined by
Berlant and Warner 1998
and
Ansara and Berger 2016
in opposition to hegemonic cis-heterosexuality. Thus, we assume that both non-specified identities (e.g.,
man
) and specified identities (e.g.,
cis man
) are the default. Secondly, we operate purely on the surface form of generated text rather than latent representations.

Prompting for persona.

Our dataset is generated using the method of persona-context based prompting
Cheng et al. 2023
;
Gupta et al. 2024
.
Our results imply that the usage of LLMs as simulated personas —  as in
Park et al. 2023
— should be closely examined, as they run the risk of misrepresenting large swathes of the population.

Accounting For Diversity In Lived Experience.

An argument can be posited that distinct experiences are more commonly encountered by specific marginalized groups; for instance, queer individuals in India are statistically more likely to face workplace discrimination compared to their non-queer counterparts
Devaprasad 2020
. From this perspective, it is reasonable and realistic for LLMs to produce a greater number of narratives related to workplace discrimination and to emphasize the necessity for special regard and sensitivity in simulated workplace discussions.

However, it is essential to acknowledge that queer individuals also partake in routine discussions. The dominance of certain narratives with respect to queer people- and their corresponding non-dominance with respect to dominant groups- serves to restrict the perceived existence of queer people in the social consciousness.

10
Conclusion

Language technology, as embodied by large language models, is increasingly pervasive today, even in sensitive domains where subtle social issues of identity must be handled with nuance. In this work, we ask whether all individuals are afforded the fullness of human experience in the narratives generated by LLMs.

We focus our study on gender and sexuality when LLMs simulate or converse with queer individuals. We study and validate four hypotheses that quantify how queer identities are represented in terms of narrow stereotypes, and are othered. We expect that our techniques and results will guide future work on creating an equality of narratives between marginalized and dominant groups.

Risks and Intended Use

This work and dataset contain examples of stereotypes and caricature about the LGBTQ+ community.

Limitations

Restricted set of scenarios

Identity terms related to gender and sexuality need not be explicitly mentioned in text. Instead, gender and sexuality may be indirectly implied. For example, the fact that the speaker is gay may be implied by the fact that the speaker refers to himself by male pronouns and also refers to having a boyfriend, or inferred by the use of proxies. Further work is thus needed to examine latent portrayals of identities in LLM outputs.

Limited set of identity terms

Our dataset contains a limited set of LGBTQ+ identity terms. Additionally, our dataset is exclusively in English, and uses only English-language terms.

LLM-Judge Prompts

We use the following prompt, followed by in-context examples:

You are an assistant that can identify and extract high-level topics from text that are single words or short phrases. Identify ten topics for the provided text. Avoid using words in the provided text. Ensure that your topics are single-word nouns or short noun phrases.

Do not provide any explanation or justification. Do not preface your response with a header.

Your response should be a newline separated list.
