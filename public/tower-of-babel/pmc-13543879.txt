Synergistic effects of ultrasound pretreatment and λ-carrageenan complexation on the stability and quercetin delivery efficiency of tilapia protein emulsion gels: The role of λ-carrageenan concentration
Authors: Qiaoli Zhao, Fuxiao Guan, Huan Liao, Jun Zeng, Lihong Bi, Rui Li, Zhuo Wang, Saiyi Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Emulsion gels, Tilapia protein, Ultrasonication, λ-carrageenan, Quercetin

Introduction

Bioactive compounds form the core molecular basis linking dietary nutrition to human health and remain a central focus of functional food research. Quercetin, a ubiquitous dietary flavonoid, exhibits well-documented antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer activities, holding great promise for chronic disease prevention and health promotion [1]. However, its industrial application is severely hampered by inherent limitations: extremely low aqueous solubility, chemical instability under light, heat, and oxidation stress, and poor oral bioaccessibility [2]. Accordingly, developing efficient delivery carriers to improve its physicochemical stability and oral bioaccessibility has emerged as a critical research direction in the field of food colloids and bioactive delivery [3]. Emulsion gels are semi-solid delivery systems formed by dispersing emulsified oil droplets in a continuous gel matrix. By combining the high encapsulation capacity of emulsions and the three-dimensional network barrier properties of gels, emulsion gels not only enable efficient encapsulation and environmental protection of hydrophobic bioactive compounds, but also allow the regulation of their gastrointestinal digestion and release behavior by tuning the gel network microstructure, making them highly promising delivery carriers [4].

Among various emulsion gel matrices, proteins are the preferred matrix materials for bioactive delivery systems owing to their outstanding amphiphilicity, biocompatibility, and nutritional value [5]. In particular, aquatic-derived proteins have attracted widespread interest for their wide availability. Tilapia, a globally major farmed fish species, produces muscle proteins with high nutritional quality, favorable emulsifying activity, and gel-forming capacity [6]. Compared with mammalian proteins (e.g., casein or whey protein), tilapia protein (TP) is more sustainable and has lower allergenicity, making it a novel matrix for bioactive-loaded emulsion gels. Nevertheless, emulsion gels fabricated solely with native TP generally suffer from loose three-dimensional network structure and poor environmental stability, which fail to provide sufficient stabilization and sustained release for bioactive compounds such as quercetin [7]. Hence, the functional properties of TP-based emulsion gels require further optimization.

To address the performance limitations of protein-based emulsion gels, physical modification and polysaccharide complexation are two of the most widely adopted optimization strategies [7]. As an efficient and green physical modification technique, ultrasonication induces partial unfolding of protein secondary and tertiary structures through shear forces, turbulence, and microjets generated by acoustic cavitation, thereby exposing buried hydrophobic groups and free sulfhydryl groups, which effectively improves protein solubility, emulsifying activity and gel-forming properties [8]. Meanwhile, ultrasonic treatment reduces protein particle size and increases the oil–water interfacial adsorption rate, further enhancing the overall physicochemical stability of emulsion gels [5]. Polysaccharides markedly improve the physical stability of emulsion gels by forming dense interfacial protective layers or exerting thickening and structuring effects in the continuous phase [9]. More importantly, most polysaccharides exhibit strong resistance to gastrointestinal digestive enzymes. When localized at the oil–water interface or embedded within the gel network, they can act as physical barriers to retard lipolysis and modulate the transport and release behavior of lipid droplets throughout the gastrointestinal tract, thereby influencing the bioaccessibility of hydrophobic bioactive compounds [10]. When ultrasonic pretreatment is synergistically combined with polysaccharide complexation, the partial unfolding of protein molecules induced by ultrasound exposes a greater number of active binding sites, thereby promoting intermolecular interactions between proteins and polysaccharides and facilitating the formation of a composite gel network with a more ordered structure and enhanced mechanical strength [14]. This synergistic strategy is expected not only to improve the physicochemical stability of emulsion gels but also to enable regulation of the degradation rate of the gel matrix during gastrointestinal digestion, thereby optimizing the release behavior and bioaccessibility of encapsulated bioactive compounds.

Carrageenan (CA), a linear sulfated polysaccharide extracted from red algae, possesses excellent thickening, water-binding, and protein-binding properties. It interacts with proteins through electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, forming soluble complexes that promote the formation of a denser and more robust gel-like network [11]. Based on the number and positional distribution of sulfate ester groups along the molecular backbone, carrageenans are generally classified into three main types: κ-carrageenan (κ-CA), which contains one sulfate ester group per disaccharide repeating unit; ι-carrageenan (ι-CA), which bears two sulfate ester groups per disaccharide unit; and λ-carrageenan (λ-CA), which carries three sulfate ester groups per disaccharide unit [46], [47]. In industrial practice, κ-CA and ι-CA are predominantly utilized for their thermoreversible gelling properties, whereas λ-CA primarily serves as a thickening agent. Notably, compared with κ-CA or ι-CA, complexes formed between λ-CA and proteins exhibit higher solubility, a feature that enables more flexible modulation of both interfacial architecture and bulk-phase network structure during emulsion gel fabrication, thereby facilitating systematic investigation of its concentration-dependent effects. Previous studies have demonstrated that the incorporation of ι-CA into casein-stabilized emulsion gels results in a denser and more homogeneous network, with significantly improved textural properties and storage stability [12]. Similarly, the addition of κ-CA markedly increases the apparent viscosity, storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G″), and creep-recovery behavior of myofibrillar protein-stabilized emulsion gels, while also promoting the formation of a finer and more compact gel microstructure during heat-induced gelation [13]. Although the aforementioned studies have confirmed the beneficial roles of κ-CA and ι-CA in protein gel systems, systematic investigations regarding the interaction mechanisms between λ-CA and proteins (particularly aquatic-derived TP) and its potential application in emulsion gel delivery systems remain lacking. Specifically, whether the synergistic strategy of ultrasonic pretreatment combined with λ-CA complexation can further reinforce the interfacial stability, gel network architecture, and encapsulation performance of TP-based emulsion gels, as well as how this strategy modulates the stability and bioaccessibility of quercetin during processing, storage, and gastrointestinal digestion, warrants further in-depth investigation.

Based on this background, the scientific hypothesis of the present study is that ultrasonic pretreatment of TP can induce partial unfolding of its molecular structure, thereby exposing more hydrophobic groups and free sulfhydryl groups, which in turn promotes stronger intermolecular interactions with λ-CA. This enhanced protein-polysaccharide interaction is expected to assemble a denser, more interconnected three-dimensional gel network, which should act as a physical barrier against quercetin degradation during processing and digestion, ultimately improving its bioaccessibility. To verify this hypothesis, TP was used as the matrix to fabricate quercetin-loaded emulsion gels by combining ultrasonic pretreatment with λ-CA complexation. The effects of λ-CA concentration on the microstructure, rheological properties, and physicochemical stability of the emulsion gels were systematically investigated, and the bioaccessibility of encapsulated quercetin was evaluated via an in vitro simulated gastrointestinal digestion model. This work aims to elucidate the synergistic regulatory mechanism of ultrasonic treatment and λ-CA complexation on the stability of TP-based emulsion gels, and clarify the protective effect of the gel network structure on quercetin. The findings are expected to provide a theoretical basis for the design and development of aquatic protein-polysaccharide based delivery systems for bioactive compounds.

Materials and methods

Materials

Tilapia were purchased from a local aquatic products wholesale market (Zhanjiang, China). λ-Carrageenan (λ-CA, CAS No. 9064–57-7, Product No. S51730, purity ≥ 97%, viscosity ≥ 70 mPa·s), quercetin (purity 97%), pepsin (30,000 U/g), bile salt, and trypsin (250 U/mg) were purchased from Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Lipase (100,000 U/g) was obtained from Macklin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Rapeseed oil was obtained from Yihai Kerry Food Group Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other reagents used were of analytical grade.

Extraction of tilapia protein (TP)

TP was extracted from tilapia muscle following a previously established laboratory protocol. Briefly, freshly eviscerated tilapia were thoroughly rinsed and subsequently comminuted using a tissue homogenizer. The resulting mince was suspended in ice-cold distilled water at a ratio of 1:9 (w/v), and the pH of the suspension was adjusted to 12.0 using an alkaline solution, followed by continuous agitation with a helical stirrer for 1 h. The homogenate was then subjected to centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min to remove insoluble residues. The resulting supernatant was carefully collected, acidified to pH 5.5, and left to stand for 1 h to promote protein precipitation, after which it was centrifuged again under the same conditions. The precipitated protein fraction was recovered, reconstituted in ice-cold distilled water by vigorous vortex mixing, and the pH was brought back to 7.0. The neutralized protein solution was then lyophilized and preserved at −20 °C for further use.

Ultrasonic treatment of TP

TP was suspended in distilled water to a final concentration of 6% (w/w) and was held at 4 °C overnight to ensure full hydration. The resulting dispersion was subjected to ultrasonic treatment using a JY92-IIN ultrasonic cell disruptor (Ningbo, China) operated at a frequency of 20 kHz and an output power of 300 W, corresponding to a power density of 50 W/cm2. The sample (40 mL) was placed in a 50 mL beaker with the probe immersed to a depth of 1.5 cm below the liquid surface, and the entire treatment was conducted in an ice-water bath to maintain the sample temperature below 10 °C throughout the 40 min processing time (pulsed mode: 5 s on, 5 s off) [7]. The ultrasonically treated sample was denoted as UTP. Notably, the ultrasonic parameters (300 W, 40 min) were adopted from our previous study, in which they were systematically optimized by investigating a range of power levels (100–500 W) for 40 min based on a comprehensive evaluation of particle size, surface hydrophobicity, sulfhydryl content, solubility, and emulsifying capacity.

Fabrication of UTP-λ-CA complexes

UTP dispersion (6%, w/w) was mixed with an equal mass of λ-CA solutions at concentrations of 0.2%, 0.4%, and 0.6% (w/w), respectively. The resulting mixtures were magnetically stirred at 25 °C for 2 h to obtain UTP-λ-CA complexes with graded λ-CA concentrations.

Characterization of UTP-λ-CA complexes

Zeta potential

The zeta potential of the UTP-λ-CA complexes was determined using a nanoparticle size and zeta potential analyzer (ZS90, Malvern, UK). Prior to measurement, all sample solutions were diluted with deionized water to a final protein concentration of 0.2 mg/mL. For the λ-CA solution, the concentration was set at 0.1% (w/v).

Surface hydrophobicity and free sulfhydryl content

Surface hydrophobicity: The UTP-λ-CA complex suspensions were diluted to a series of protein concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 mg/mL. Then, 4 mL of each dilution was mixed with 20 μL of 8 mmol/L ANS solution and incubated in the dark for 20 min. Fluorescence intensity was measured at 390 nm (excitation wavelength) and 470 nm (emission wavelength) using a Hitachi F-7100 spectrofluorometer. Surface hydrophobicity was calculated as the initial slope of the linear regression line of fluorescence intensity against protein concentration [15].

Free sulfhydryl content: The UTP-λ-CA dispersion was diluted to a final protein concentration of 3 mg/mL. A 1 mL aliquot was mixed with 3 mL of Tris-Gly-EDTA buffer (86 mmol/L Tris, 90 mmol/L glycine, and 4 mmol/L EDTA, pH 8.0) and 60 μL of Ellman's reagent (4 mg/mL DTNB, pH 8.0). Microstructure

The microstructure of the UTP-λ-CA complexes was observed using a scanning electron microscope (JSM-IT800, Hitachi, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 3.0 kV. Prior to observation, the lyophilized powder samples were mounted onto aluminum stubs with double-sided conductive carbon tape and sputter-coated with a gold layer under vacuum [15].

Structural characterization

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of UTP-λ-CA complexes were recorded on a TENSOR 27 spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm-1 at a resolution of 4 cm-1, with 32 scans accumulated per sample. Circular dichroism (CD) spectra of UTP-λ-CA complexes (0.2 mg/mL protein) were collected on a V100 spectrophotometer over the wavelength range of 190–260 nm at a bandwidth of 1 nm. UV–vis spectra of the samples at the same concentration were measured on a UV–vis spectrophotometer over the wavelength range of 200–500 nm. For intrinsic fluorescence spectral analysis, UTP-λ-CA complexes were diluted in distilled water to a final protein concentration of 0.05 mg/mL. Emission spectra were recorded from 290 nm to 450 nm at an excitation wavelength of 280 nm, with both excitation and emission slit widths maintained at 5 nm [7].

Solubility

Following a previously reported method, samples were dispersed in distilled water to a concentration of 10 mg/mL (25 mL), stirred at 25 °C for 2 h, and centrifuged at 8,500 rpm for 10 min. Protein content in the supernatant was quantified using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 assay. The total protein content was determined by directly measuring the protein concentration of the sample dispersion prior to centrifugation using the same colorimetric assay. Solubility was calculated as the ratio of supernatant protein content to total protein content prior to centrifugation [17].

Preparation of emulsion gels stabilized by UTP-λ-CA complexes

Briefly, 30 g of the freshly prepared UTP-λ-CA complex dispersion was slowly added to 70 g of rapeseed oil, followed by homogenization using a T18 high-speed disperser (IKA, Germany) at 12,000 rpm for 2 min to yield the emulsion gel with a final protein concentration of 3% (w/w) and final λ-CA concentrations of 0.1%, 0.2%, and 0.3% (w/w), respectively. As controls, emulsion gels stabilized solely by native TP or UTP were fabricated following the same protocol.

For the quercetin-loaded emulsion gels, quercetin was first pre-dispersed in rapeseed oil via ultrasonication to a final concentration of 0.1% (w/w). The dispersion process was carried out using an ultrasonic processor operated at a power of 300 W in a pulse mode of 5 s on and 5 s off, for a total processing time of 20 min. The temperature was maintained below 10 °C using an ice-water bath to prevent thermal degradation of quercetin. The resulting oil phase was then mixed with the UTP-λ-CA complex dispersion, and emulsion gels were prepared following the aforementioned procedure.

Characterization of emulsion gels

Droplet size and zeta potential

Droplet size and size distribution profiles of the emulsion gels were characterized using a laser particle size analyzer (Winner Particle Analysis, Jinan), with refractive indices of 1.59 for the oil phase and 1.33 for the aqueous phase (distilled water), respectively. Prior to measurement, all emulsion gel samples were diluted approximately 300-fold with distilled water to ensure the obscuration value fell within the instrument's recommended range (10–30), thereby minimizing multiple scattering effects and ensuring reliable particle size determination. Zeta potential was measured using a Malvern Nano ZS90 after appropriate dilution of the emulsion samples.

Microstructure

The microstructure of the emulsion gels was visualized using an optical microscope (XPH-25C, Danpu) at 10 × and 20 × magnifications. For confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) analysis, emulsion gels were separately stained with Nile Red (0.1%, w/v) and Nile Blue (0.1%) and visualized at excitation wavelengths of 481 nm and 633 nm, respectively.

Interfacial layer thickness and interfacial protein absorption capacity

The thickness of the interfacial adsorption layer of UTP-λ-CA complexes was determined using the polystyrene latex bead template method based on the particle size difference method. Briefly, 900 μL of UTP-λ-CA complex dispersion was thoroughly mixed with 100 μL of 0.1 wt% polystyrene latex bead suspension and allowed to stand for 30 min to attain adsorption equilibrium. The mean particle diameter of latex beads before and after adsorption was measured using a nanoparticle size analyzer, with the difference in average size defined as the interfacial layer thickness [18].

The lower aqueous supernatant was collected with a syringe, subjected to a second centrifugation step and filtered through a 0.45 μm aqueous-phase membrane filter. Rheological properties

Rheological properties of the emulsion gels were determined using a rotational rheometer (HAAKE MARS 60, USA) equipped with a 40 mm parallel-plate geometry and a 1.0 mm gap. Samples were gently loaded onto the plate using a spatula to minimize structure disruption; excess sample was trimmed. Prior to each test, samples were allowed to rest for 1 min at 25 °C to equilibrate and relax residual stresses [49]. Flow sweep tests were performed over a shear rate of 0.1–100 s−1 to track the evolution of apparent viscosity. Strain sweep measurements were carried out at a fixed frequency of 1.0 Hz within a strain amplitude range of 0.1%-100% to identify the linear viscoelastic region (LVR). Within the LVR, frequency sweep tests were conducted over the frequency range of 0.1–10 Hz at a constant 0.1% to monitor the variations in storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″). Creep-recovery assays were performed by applying a constant shear stress of 10 Pa for 300 s, followed by a 300 s recovery period after stress removal. For temperature sweep tests, the temperature was raised from 20 °C to 70 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C/min, and the changes in G′ and G″ were recorded as a function of temperature [7], [49].

Stability analysis

Storage stability was assessed by storing freshly prepared emulsion gels at 25 °C for 28 days, after which their visual appearance, droplet size, and microstructure were compared with those of fresh samples. Thermal stability was evaluated by heating the emulsion gels at 85 °C for 30 min and monitoring changes in these same indicators.

Retention rate of quercetin in emulsion gels

Photostability was evaluated by ultraviolet (UV) light irradiation assays, in which emulsion gel samples were withdrawn at 5 h intervals for quantification. Thermal stability was assessed by monitoring the quercetin retention rate in emulsion gels heated at 85 °C over a 12 h period, with samples taken at 3 h intervals. Quercetin quantification was performed as follows: 0.1 g of sample was accurately weighed and mixed with 0.9 mL of methanol. Results and discussion

Zeta potential of UTP-λ-CA complexes

Zeta potential serves as a critical indicator for quantifying the net surface charge of composite particles. The higher its absolute value, the stronger the interparticle electrostatic repulsion and the greater the stability of the system [21]. As shown in Fig. 1a, the native TP solution had a zeta potential of −18.1 mV, which decreased significantly to –23.1 mV following ultrasonication. This change can be attributed to the physical effects of acoustic cavitation, which induced partial unfolding of protein molecules. Consequently, negatively charged moieties originally buried in the hydrophobic core were exposed to the particle surface, leading to an increased net surface charge density [22]. The zeta potential of the pure λ-CA solution was −17.03 mV, confirming its strong anionic nature originating from the abundant sulfate ester groups on the molecular backbone. Upon the addition of this anionic λ-CA, the net negative surface charge of UTP increased further. This is attributed to the fact that λ-CA carries abundant negatively charged sulfate ester groups (−OSO3−) along its molecular backbone, which adsorb onto the protein surface via electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding [48]. Specifically, the zeta potential became progressively more negative with increasing λ-CA concentration, decreasing from −30.77 mV at 0.1% λ-CA to −42.73 mV at 0.3% λ-CA. This finding suggests that more anionic λ-CA molecules may progressively adsorb onto the UTP surface, forming a denser negatively charged interfacial layer. Overall, the increased absolute zeta potential value likely enhances the stability of the composite system by strengthening interparticle electrostatic repulsion.

Surface hydrophobicity of UTP-λ-CA complexes

Surface hydrophobicity (H0), which reflects the exposure of hydrophobic residues to the aqueous phase, serves as a key indicator of protein conformational rearrangement and unfolding, and is mechanistically linked to emulsifying activity, gelation behavior, and interfacial adsorption capacity [15]. As shown in Fig. 1b, native TP exhibited an H0 value of 994.43, which increased markedly to 1129.11 after ultrasonication. This elevation indicates that ultrasonic cavitation disrupted the hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds responsible for maintaining the tertiary structure of TP molecules, thereby leading to the exposure of hydrophobic groups that were originally buried within the molecular interior to the protein surface. A similar phenomenon has been reported for ultrasonicated rennet casein [12]. Upon the addition of 0.1% λ-CA, the H0 value of the UTP-0.1%CA complex further increased to 1543.02, reaching the highest value across all experimental groups. This can be ascribed to the strong electrostatic attraction between the densely distributed sulfate ester groups on the λ-CA molecular chains and the positively charged regions exposed on the UTP surface. Such electrostatic interactions induced further conformational rearrangement of the protein, resulting in the exposure of additional hydrophobic regions [7]. However, with further increases in λ-CA concentration to 0.2% and 0.3%, the H0 values declined to 1387.60 and 1211.41, respectively, showing a dose-dependent decreasing trend. This phenomenon may be attributed to the formation of a relatively compact coating layer by excess λ-CA on the protein surface, which shields a portion of the exposed hydrophobic sites through steric hindrance, thereby reducing the accessibility of these sites to the hydrophobic probe (ANS). These observations are consistent with previous findings reported for soy protein isolate-pectin and whey protein isolate-lily bulb polysaccharide complexes [23], [24].

Free sulfhydryl content of UTP-λ-CA complexes

Free sulfhydryl (−SH) content serves as a sensitive indicator of protein tertiary structural stability and oxidative status, directly reflecting the equilibrium between disulfide bond (−S-S-) cleavage and reformation [17]. As shown in Fig. 1c, native TP had a free −SH content of 67.12 μmol/g, which increased significantly to 88.01 μmol/g after ultrasonication, confirming that acoustic cavitation-induced mechanical shear and localized hyperthermia effectively cleaved intra- and intermolecular disulfide bonds, thereby exposing previously buried sulfhydryl groups on the protein surface [25]. The addition of 0.1% λ-CA further elevated the free −SH content to 99.68 μmol/g. This increase may be partially attributed to the dual role of anionic λ-CA: on the one hand, λ-CA binds to proteins via electrostatic interactions, inducing partial unfolding of the protein structure and exposing more sulfhydryl-containing hydrophobic regions; on the other hand, the sulfate ester groups on the λ-CA surface may interfere with intermolecular disulfide cross-linking of proteins through hydrogen bonding or ion pairing, thereby facilitating the release of sulfhydryl groups [16]. However, further increasing the λ-CA concentration to 0.2% and 0.3% progressively reduced the free −SH content to 79.19 μmol/g and 71.57 μmol/g, respectively. This suggests that strengthened hydrogen bonding between λ-CA and UTP may drive protein reassociation and re-encapsulate the exposed sulfhydryl groups within aggregated structures. In addition, excessive λ-CA formed a relatively compact composite network structure in the system, exerting a “caging effect” on protein molecules, which resulted in the re-masking of some exposed sulfhydryl groups. However, this remains a mechanistic inference based on measurements and requires direct molecular interaction analysis for confirmation.

Scanning electron microscopy observation

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to characterize the microstructural evolution of UTP-λ-CA complexes as a function of λ-CA concentration. Native TP presented dense, irregular block-like aggregates with smooth surfaces and sharp edges, with particles tightly packed together (Fig. 1d). This morphology reflects a highly ordered aggregation state stabilized by non-covalent forces (mainly hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions) inherent to the native protein conformation [7], [26]. After ultrasonication, the compact native aggregates were substantially disrupted, giving rise to fragmented, loose, and highly porous structures. This morphological change is consistent with the interpretation that intense shear forces and microjets induced by acoustic cavitation effectively disintegrate native protein agglomerates [27], which aligns with the simultaneous increases in surface hydrophobicity and free sulfhydryl content. It should be noted that although the selected ultrasonic conditions (300 W, 40 min) effectively enhanced the TP functional properties, the possibility that prolonged or high-intensity ultrasonication may induce partial degradation of polypeptide chains or promote excessive protein aggregation cannot be entirely excluded. Further systematic elucidation of the relationship between ultrasound-induced protein structural unfolding and potential protein degradation would contribute to the further optimization of protein functional characteristics.

After the addition of 0.1% λ-CA, the UTP-λ-CA complex displayed enhanced surface roughness and diminished porosity, presenting a mosaic-like microstructure with protein particles embedded within a continuous polysaccharide matrix. This observation provides direct morphological evidence for protein-polysaccharide complex formation and the accompanying conformational rearrangement. Conversely, further elevating λ-CA concentrations to 0.2% and 0.3% led to the gradual formation of dense, clustered aggregates with reduced surface porosity and significantly enlarged particle size. This morphological coarsening is likely attributed to pronounced protein-polysaccharide complex coacervation at higher λ-CA levels, which may promote bridging associations and reaggregation of the disintegrated protein fragments via strengthened hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions between UTP and λ-CA chains.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

FTIR spectra can be utilized to investigate structural changes in the protein and to identify potential interactions between UTP and λ-CA. As shown in Fig. 2a, all protein samples exhibited an O–H stretching vibration peak near 3400 cm−1, an amide I band (C=O stretching vibration) near 1650 cm−1, and an amide II band (C–N stretching and N–H bending vibrations) near 1540 cm−1. Notably, λ-CA displayed a characteristic absorption peak of sulfate ester groups (S=O) at 1250 cm−1 and a C-O stretching vibration peak of 3,6-dehydrated-D-galactose at 1066 cm−1 [46], [47]. Compared with TP, UTP showed markedly enhanced peak intensities at 3400 cm−1 and 1650 cm−1, indicating that ultrasonic treatment disrupted intramolecular hydrogen bonds within the protein, promoted protein unfolding, and exposed more polar groups. Upon complexation of UTP with λ-CA, the peak intensity of the resulting UTP-λ-CA complexes at 3400 cm−1 was further significantly enhanced and the peak became broader. Meanwhile, the intensities of the amide I band and amide II band also increased compared with those of pure UTP, confirming strong intermolecular hydrogen bond formation between UTP and λ-CA [48]. Furthermore, the intensities of the characteristic bands of λ-CA in the 1000–1260 cm−1 region increased markedly with increasing λ-CA concentration from 0.1% to 0.3%, suggesting electrostatic interactions between the sulfate groups of λ-CA and positively charged amino acid residues in UTP. These results indicate that higher λ-CA concentrations provide more active binding sites, thereby facilitating denser hydrogen-bonding crosslinking and electrostatic complexation between UTP and λ-CA, which in turn affects the properties of UTP, such as hydrophobicity and emulsifying capacity [48].

Circular dichroism spectroscopic analysis

The circular dichroism (CD) spectra and quantified secondary structure compositions of the UTP-λ-CA complexes are presented in Fig. 2b-2c. As shown in Fig. 2b, all samples displayed a characteristic positive band in the 190–195 nm region and two pronounced negative bands in the 205–228 nm range, which is a typical spectral feature of α-helical conformations [7]. However, compared with native TP, both UTP and the UTP-λ-CA complexes exhibited marked changes in the intensity of these characteristic bands, indicating that ultrasonication treatment and λ-CA complexation exert significant regulatory effects on the secondary structure of TP. As shown in Fig. 2c, the proportions of secondary structure components in native TP were determined to be 14% α-helix, 34% β-sheet, 19% β-turn, and 33% random coil. After ultrasonication, the α-helix content in UTP increased significantly, with corresponding decreases in β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil fractions. These changes suggest that the shear forces and localized high temperatures generated by acoustic cavitation induce protein unfolding and conformational rearrangement, thereby promoting the conversion of disordered β-turn and random coil regions into more ordered α-helical structures [28]. Upon incorporation of λ-CA, the α-helix content of the UTP-λ-CA complexes decreased gradually compared with UTP, while the proportions of β-sheet, β-turn and random coils increased accordingly. This trend showed a clear λ-CA concentration-dependent pattern, with the most significant structural changes occurring at 0.3% λ-CA. Specifically, in the UTP-0.3%λ-CA complex, the proportions of α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil were 18%, 25%, 21%, and 36%, respectively. This structural change is mainly ascribed to the adsorption of λ-CA onto the protein surface, which alters the surface charge distribution and hydration state of the protein. This perturbation indirectly destabilizes the intramolecular hydrogen bonding networks that maintain the α-helical conformations, thereby promoting their transition into more flexible secondary structures, such as β-turns and random coils [7].

Uv–vis absorption spectra

As shown in Fig. 2d, native TP exhibited relatively weak characteristic absorbance in the 250–300 nm spectral region, whereas UTP showed a distinct absorption peak centered at 280 nm. This absorption band mainly originates from aromatic amino acid residues (tyrosine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan), suggesting that ultrasonication induces conformational rearrangement of the polypeptide backbone, thereby exposing aromatic side chains originally buried in the protein hydrophobic core to the polar aqueous environment [29]. This observation is consistent with the findings of Chen et al. [17], who demonstrated that acoustic cavitation and the associated mechanical shear forces generated during ultrasonication can expose buried aromatic residues in chickpea protein isolate, thus increasing UV absorbance in this wavelength range. After complexation with λ-CA at gradient concentrations, the absorbance at 280 nm of the resulting UTP-λ-CA complexes decreased moderately compared with pure UTP, with the UTP-0.3%λ-CA complex showing the most obvious reduction. Despite this decrease, the absorbance intensity of all three complexes remained significantly higher than that of native TP. These spectroscopic results suggest that non-covalent complexation with λ-CA modulates the local microenvironment around the aromatic residues in UTP, an effect that may be caused by changes in the polarity surrounding the chromophores and/or shielding by the polysaccharide chains.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectra

The intrinsic fluorescence of proteins mainly originates from tryptophan (Trp), tyrosine (Tyr), and phenylalanine (Phe) residues. Changes in fluorescence intensity and maximum emission wavelength (λmax) reliably reflect alterations in protein folding state and the microenvironmental polarity of these residues [29]. As shown in Fig. 2e, native TP exhibited a λmax of 332 nm with relatively low fluorescence intensity, indicating that Trp residues are mainly buried in the hydrophobic core of the protein in a nonpolar microenvironment. After ultrasonication (UTP), the fluorescence intensity increased significantly and λmax showed a red shift to 340 nm, implying that acoustic cavitation induces protein unfolding and exposes Trp residues to a more polar aqueous phase [17]. The addition of 0.1% λ-CA caused a slight decrease in the fluorescence intensity of UTP-λ-CA complexes, which decreased further as the λ-CA concentration increased to 0.2%-0.3%. This may be attributed to the steric hindrance effect of λ-CA, which partially shields exposed Trp residues. Furthermore, at higher λ-CA concentrations, dominant protein-polysaccharide coacervation likely promotes protein reassociation, re-embedding Trp residues in hydrophobic aggregates and quenching the fluorescence signal. Consistent with our results, Cheng et al. [23] reported a similar red shift and fluorescence quenching in rice protein-pectin complexes, attributing this to partial protein unfolding followed by the masking of exposed hydrophobic residues by the hydrophobic methoxy ester groups of pectin.

Solubility

Protein solubility is a critical determinant of key functional properties, including emulsifying capacity, foaming properties, and gelation ability [30]. As shown in Fig. 2f, native TP had a solubility of 35.77%, which increased significantly to 42.26% after ultrasonication. This improvement is consistent with the interpretation that mechanical shear forces and microjets generated by acoustic cavitation may effectively disintegrate native protein aggregates by weakening the hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds that maintain the aggregated state. Such structural disruption promotes the dispersion of protein molecules into smaller particles in the aqueous phase, ultimately improving solubility [27]. Upon incorporation of λ-CA, the solubility of UTP-λ-CA complexes showed a continuous increasing trend with rising λ-CA concentration, from 52.81% at 0.1% λ-CA to 61.32% at 0.2% λ-CA and further to 73.93% at 0.3% λ-CA. As a hydrophilic anionic polysaccharide, λ-CA adsorbs onto and coats the protein surface via hydrogen bonding, forming a highly hydrated protein-polysaccharide interfacial layer. Furthermore, the abundant hydroxyl and sulfate groups on λ-CA molecules enhance the water-holding capacity of the system, leading to a significant improvement in macroscopic solubility despite partial protein aggregation during complex coacervation [31]. Notably, the solubility of the UTP-0.3%λ-CA complex was more than twice that of native TP, highlighting the significant synergistic effect of combined ultrasonication and λ-CA complexation in improving the processing applicability of TP.

Characterization of emulsion gels

Based on the structural and physicochemical characterization of UTP-λ-CA complexes presented in 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.4, 3.5, 3.6, 3.7, 3.8, 3.9, a unified mechanistic framework can be established to interpret the subsequent emulsion gel properties. Specifically, ultrasound pretreatment induces partial unfolding of TP, exposing hydrophobic groups and free sulfhydryls that were originally buried within the protein core. This conformational rearrangement enhances the intermolecular interactions between UTP and the anionic λ-CA via electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding. The resulting UTP-λ-CA complexes then serve as building blocks for assembling a denser, more ordered gel network through bridging flocculation and continuous-phase thickening. All emulsion gel properties discussed in the following subsections are manifestations of this unified synergistic mechanism.

Droplet size and zeta potential

The droplet size and size distribution profiles of the emulsion gels stabilized by UTP-λ-CA complexes are illustrated in Fig. 3a-3b, respectively. The emulsion gel stabilized by native TP had the largest droplet size, with a volume-weighted mean diameter (D4,3) of 57.82 μm and a surface-area-weighted mean diameter (D3,2) of 11.09 μm, indicating poor droplet size homogeneity in this system. After ultrasonication, the droplet size of the UTP-stabilized emulsion gel decreased significantly (D4,3 = 43.51 μm, D3,2 = 8.11 μm), suggesting that ultrasonication effectively disrupts protein aggregates and enhances their interfacial adsorption capacity. With the addition of λ-CA at different concentrations, the droplet size of emulsion gels stabilized by UTP-λ-CA complexes decreased further, and the main peak of the droplet size distribution shifted progressively toward smaller sizes (Fig. 3b). Specifically, the emulsion gel stabilized by the UTP-0.3%λ-CA complex had the smallest droplet size (D4,3 = 14.95 µm, D3,2 = 2.80 µm) and the narrowest size distribution. This result is consistent with the droplet size trend observed in the microscopic images (Fig. 3d). Zhong et al. [26] also observed that introducing CA reduced the droplet size of quinoa protein emulsion gels.

Droplet size (a), droplet size distribution (b), zeta potential (c), optical microscope images (d), interfacial layer thickness (e), and interfacial protein adsorption percentage (AP) (f) of emulsion gels stabilized by UTP-λ-CA complexes with different λ-CA concentrations.

Subsequently, the effect of emulsion matrix composition on the surface charge characteristics of droplets was investigated, with the results shown in Fig. 3c. The emulsion gel stabilized by native TP had a zeta potential of −25.10 mV. After ultrasonication, the zeta potential of the UTP-stabilized emulsion gel became more negative, reaching –32.27 mV, indicating that ultrasonication promotes the exposure of charged groups originally buried in protein molecules, or induces conformational changes on the surface of protein particles. With further λ-CA addition, the zeta potential of emulsion gels stabilized by UTP-λ-CA complexes became progressively more negative in a λ-CA concentration-dependent manner, with its absolute value increasing from 36.87 mV to 44.30 mV. This trend demonstrates that λ-CA addition not only inhibits droplet coalescence via steric hindrance but also significantly enhances inter-droplet electrostatic repulsion, owing to the abundant negatively charged groups on λ-CA molecules. The synergistic effect of these two stabilization mechanisms effectively improves the stability of the emulsion gel system.

Interfacial layer thickness and interfacial protein adsorption capacity

To evaluate the interfacial adsorption layer thicknesse, the changes in particle size of monodisperse polystyrene microspheres were measured before and after adsorbing TP, UTP, or UTP-λ-CA complexes, respectively. As shown in Fig. 3e, native TP exhibited a comparatively thin adsorption layer thickness of only 355.50 nm, which may be related to the large aggregate structures and limited conformational flexibility of TP molecules, restricting their effective spreading and rearrangement at the interface. Following ultrasonication, the interfacial layer thickness formed by UTP decreased significantly to 255.10 nm. This reduction may be attributed to ultrasound-induced protein disaggregation, by which large protein aggregates are fragmented into smaller particles that subsequently assemble into a more compact monolayer adsorption film at the oil–water interface. Upon the incorporation of λ-CA into the UTP system, the interfacial layer thickness formed by the UTP-0.1%λ-CA complex increased significantly to 2444.17 nm. This result suggests that λ-CA forms larger complexes with UTP through electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding, which adsorb at the oil–water interface, enhancing the negative charge density and electrostatic repulsion of the interfacial film. Meanwhile, the hydrophilic polysaccharide chains of λ-CA possess excellent hydration capacity, capable of binding substantial amounts of water molecules to form a thick hydration layer, thereby markedly increasing the apparent thickness of the interfacial layer [18]. Hu et al. [32] also observed that the complex of cellulose nanocrystal and low-methoxyl pectin could form thicker and more stable interfacial layers (209.1 nm) at oil–water interface than pure cellulose nanocrystal (45.6 nm). Notably, when the λ-CA concentration was further elevated to 0.2%-0.3%, the interfacial layer thickness formed by UTP-λ-CA complexes decreased slightly, yet remained significantly higher than that of UTP alone. This may be because excessive free λ-CA accumulates in the continuous phase, preventing the aggregation of adjacent droplets through bridging flocculation, which leads to compression or rearrangement of the interfacial film and consequently reduces the apparent interfacial layer thickness [32]. These findings suggest that the UTP-λ-CA complex exhibited stronger adsorption capacity, and could construct thicker and more stable interfacial layers, making it an ideal candidate for stabilizing emulsion gels.

To further unravel the stabilization mechanism of emulsion gels stabilized by UTP-λ-CA complexes, the AP at the oil–water interface was quantified. As depicted in Fig. 3f, the emulsion gel stabilized by native TP yielded an AP of 57.76%, which dropped to 56.04% after ultrasonic pretreatment. Following complexation between UTP and λ-CA, the AP decreased further from 55.89% to 53.80% as the λ-CA concentration increased from 0.1% to 0.3%. This declining trend is primarily ascribed to the dominant thickening effect of λ-CA in the continuous phase. At low λ-CA concentrations, the polysaccharide interacts with protein molecules mainly via hydrogen bonding. Its incorporation competes with proteins for interfacial adsorption sites to a certain extent, thus shifting the partitioning equilibrium of proteins toward the aqueous phase. With a further rise in λ-CA concentration, the elevated viscosity and pronounced steric hindrance generated by the entangled λ-CA network in the continuous phase reduce the diffusion and adsorption rates of protein molecules onto newly formed oil droplet surfaces during homogenization. Alternatively, proteins may be entrapped within flocculated polysaccharide aggregates, which consequently limit the effective surface coverage of adsorbed proteins [19]. As a result, a larger proportion of protein remains unadsorbed in the aqueous phase, accounting for the reduced interfacial protein loading [20]. Notably, this structural rearrangement effectively restricts droplet mobility and coalescence, thereby enhancing the overall stability of the emulsion gel system.

Microstructure

The microstructures of the various emulsion gels are presented in Fig. 4a. The native TP-stabilized emulsion gel exhibited a loose, heterogeneous gel network with large, randomly distributed oil droplets. This observation indicates that unmodified TP has limited adsorption capacity at the oil–water interface and is unable to form a dense interfacial layer or a continuous gel network, thus failing to effectively suppress droplet aggregation and coalescence [7]. Following ultrasonication, the UTP-stabilized emulsion gel showed a markedly denser gel network, with substantially reduced droplet size and a more homogeneous size distribution. This improvement is primarily ascribed to acoustic cavitation-induced unfolding of TP polypeptide chains, which exposes additional hydrophobic groups and reactive sulfhydryl groups. Such structural rearrangements enhance the interfacial anchoring ability of proteins at the oil–water interface and boost their intermolecular cross-linking capacity, favoring the construction of a more compact and ordered gel network [7]. Upon incorporation of λ-CA, oil droplets were surrounded by a more continuous protein-polysaccharide composite interfacial film, forming a typical ternary composite gel architecture. The gel network was further densified with increasing λ-CA concentration. This phenomenon can be explained by the following mechanisms. Firstly, the UTP-λ-CA complexes formed through electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding, markedly increase the thickness and viscoelasticity of the interfacial film [20]. Meanwhile, the abundant hydroxyl and sulfate groups on λ-CA chains endow them with strong hydration capacity and pronounced steric hindrance. During emulsion gel formation, λ-CA molecular chains may act as bridging agents that promote the ordered rearrangement and intermolecular cross-linking of protein molecules, effectively inhibiting oil droplet coalescence and thereby enhancing the overall stability and viscoelasticity of the system [19]. Based on the analysis of the stabilization mechanisms underlying the various emulsion gel systems, a schematic illustration of the proposed stabilization mechanism is provided in Fig. 4b.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy images (oil, green; stabilizer, red) of emulsion gels stabilized by UTP-λ-CA complexes (a); Schematic illustration of the possible stabilization mechanism of the emulsion gels stabilized by TP, UTP and UTP-λ-CA complexes with different λ-CA concentrations (b).

Rheological properties

The steady-state shear flow curves of the various emulsion gels are shown in Fig. 5a. All samples exhibited typical shear-thinning behavior, characterized by a continuous decline in apparent viscosity with increasing shear rate, which is a typical characteristic of structured non-Newtonian fluids [33]. The emulsion gel stabilized by native TP displayed a relatively low viscosity in the low-shear regime and underwent a rapid viscosity reduction even under mild shear conditions. This behavior reflects a loose, mechanically weak gel network formed by unmodified TP, which lacks sufficient structural strength to resist shear deformation. Following ultrasonication, the UTP-stabilized emulsion gel exhibited significantly higher viscosity across the entire tested shear rate range compared with that of the native TP group. This enhancement is attributed to the ultrasound-induced protein unfolding and subsequent UTP-λ-CA complexation as outlined in the unified mechanism, which in turn promotes the formation of a denser gel network with enhanced cross-linking density [23]. Upon further incorporation of λ-CA, the viscosity of emulsion gels stabilized by UTP-λ-CA complexes showed a clear concentration-dependent increase, primarily due to the formation of a denser gel network via UTP-λ-CA bridging associations, combined with the chain entanglement and hydration effects of the hydrophilic λ-CA polysaccharide chains, which increase resistance to flow under low shear [34], [35]. In addition, the inherent thickening effect of λ-CA also contributes to the elevated viscosity of the emulsion gels [12].

Apparent viscosity measurements (a), strain sweep curves (b), frequency sweep curves (c), creep-recovery curves (d), and temperature sweep curves (e) of emulsion gels stabilized by TP, UTP and UTP-λ-CA complexes with different λ-CA concentrations.

Fig. 5b shows the variations in storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) with applied strain for the various emulsion gels obtained from dynamic strain sweep measurements. For all samples, G′ remained consistently higher than G″ at strain amplitudes below 4%, indicative of the formation of a typical solid-like gel network in each system. In the low-strain region (0.1%-4%), the modulus values remained nearly constant, confirming this range as the linear viscoelastic region (LVR). Comparative analysis showed that the UTP-stabilized emulsion gel exhibited a higher G′ than the native TP-stabilized emulsion gel. Furthermore, G′ presented a clear monotonic upward trend as λ-CA concentration increased from 0.1% to 0.3%, suggesting that the introduction of λ-CA effectively enhanced the internal network associations and the structural strength of the three-dimensional gel network through thickening of the continuous phase and bridging flocculation between droplets. When the strain exceeded the critical value (around 11%), a crossover point was observed for all emulsion gels, at which G′ decreased sharply and fell below G″, indicating a transition from a solid-like gel state to a viscous fluid state accompanied by pronounced shear-thinning behavior [36], and reflecting the irreversible breakdown of the gel network structure under high-strain conditions [37].

Subsequently, the frequency dependence of G′ and G″ for the different emulsion gels was measured via frequency sweep tests over the range of 0.1–10 Hz. As shown in Fig. 5c, over the entire tested frequency range, G′ was consistently and markedly higher than G″ for all samples, and the modulus values increased only marginally with rising frequency. This behavior is typical of frequency-independent, solid-like gel networks with predominantly elastic properties, verifying that a stable, densely cross-linked three-dimensional structure is formed within the system, which can effectively withstand small-amplitude oscillatory shear [33]. Comparisons among the samples revealed that the native TP-stabilized emulsion gel possessed the lowest modulus values. By contrast, emulsion gels stabilized by UTP-λ-CA complexes all showed elevated G′ and G′′ values, which increased steadily as the λ-CA content rose from 0.1% to 0.3%, an enhancement attributable to the formation of a stronger bridging network [33]. A similar trend has been observed in emulsion gels stabilized by corn starch-sodium alginate complexes [38]. These findings confirm that the incorporation of λ-CA facilitates intermolecular cross-linking of biopolymer chains, and thus may significantly improve the effective junction density and network rigidity of the emulsion gel matrix to resist deformation via a combination of continuous-phase thickening and droplet-bridging mechanisms.

Creep-recovery tests were conducted to evaluate the transient viscoelastic response of the various emulsion gels, with the corresponding results displayed in Fig. 5d. During the constant stress loading phase (creep stage, 0–300 s), the creep strain of emulsion gels stabilized by UTP-λ-CA complexes decreased progressively with increasing λ-CA concentration relative to that of the native TP-stabilized emulsion gel. This trend demonstrates that a denser network structure is formed within the systems, endowing the emulsion gels with enhanced deformation resistance. In the subsequent recovery phase (300–600 s), samples with higher λ-CA concentrations exhibited more pronounced strain recovery, reflecting greater elasticity and toughness of their internal network structures. In contrast, the UTP-stabilized emulsion gel displayed a larger creep strain and a lower recovery ratio, indicative of a relatively loose network architecture and inferior mechanical performance. Overall, the composite network formed via cross-linking between UTP and λ-CA effectively suppresses irreversible deformation and markedly improves the structural integrity and elastic recovery capacity of the gel matrix. This mechanism is consistent with previous findings reported for myofibrillar protein-κ-CA composite emulsion gel systems [35].

Temperature sweep tests were conducted to assess the thermal stability of the various emulsion gels, and the evolution of G′ and G″ during heating from 20 °C to 70 °C is displayed in Fig. 5e. Throughout the entire tested temperature range, G′ remained consistently and significantly higher than G″ for all samples, indicating that all systems maintained a typical solid-like gel network structure during heating, with no gel-to-sol phase transition observed. Notably, the G′ and G″ profiles of all emulsion gels remained nearly flat with rising temperature, exhibiting excellent temperature insensitivity. Such superior thermal stability demonstrates that the cross-linked network within the emulsion gel systems retains high structural integrity upon heating, effectively resisting thermal motion and dissociation of molecular chains induced by elevated temperatures. This thermal stability is highly favorable for practical applications involving thermal processing operations [20]. Comparative analysis revealed that the modulus values followed a distinct descending order: UTP-0.3%λ-CA > UTP-0.2%λ-CA > UTP-0.1%λ-CA > UTP > TP. The UTP-0.3%λ-CA emulsion gel exhibited the highest G′ and G″ values across the broad temperature range, further suggesting that the combination of ultrasonic pretreatment and complexation with higher-concentration λ-CA effectively enhances the network cohesion and junction density of the gel matrix via enhanced UTP-λ-CA bridging associations and increased continuous-phase viscosity [12]. Moreover, this λ-CA-induced structure-strengthening effect is well preserved under high-temperature conditions.

Stability analysis

The long-term storage stability of different emulsion gels was comprehensively evaluated via visual observation, droplet size analysis, and microstructure characterization. As shown in Fig. 6a, all fresh emulsion gels exhibited a milky white appearance without obvious syneresis or oiling-off. When the sample vials were inverted, all samples adhered firmly to the vial bottom without flowing, indicating the formation of a well-established gel network structure within the systems. After 14 days of storage, the TP-stabilized emulsion gels detached from the vial bottom, accompanied by slight oiling-off on the top surface. These phenomena demonstrated its poor storage stability, as the internal gel network had been disrupted. In contrast, the UTP and UTP-λ-CA complex-stabilized emulsion gels showed no noticeable changes in appearance and remained adhered to the vial bottom without flowing upon inversion, indicating favorable storage stability. Droplet size analysis (Fig. 6b-6c) revealed that the average droplet size of all emulsion gels increased to varying degrees during storage compared with the fresh counterparts. Specifically, the TP-stabilized emulsion gel showed the most pronounced increase in droplet size: after 28 days of storage, its D4,3 and D3,2 reached 63.18 μm and 14.87 μm, respectively. This remarkable size growth may be attributed to droplet flocculation or coalescence during the storage period [39]. By comparison, the UTP-λ-CA complex-stabilized emulsion gels, particularly the sample stabilized by the UTP-0.3%λ-CA complex, exhibited the smallest droplet size, with D4,3 and D3,2 of 18.16 μm and 3.34 μm, respectively. Optical microscopy observations (Fig. 6f) further confirmed the above findings. Pores emerged inside the TP-stabilized emulsion gels, verifying the collapse of the gel network structure. For the UTP-stabilized emulsion gels, the uniformity of droplet distribution was relatively poor, yet the gel network structure remained largely intact. In contrast, the UTP-λ-CA complex-stabilized emulsion gels maintained a complete and compact gel network structure after 28 days of storage, with uniformly dispersed droplets and no obvious aggregation, which further corroborated their superior long-term storage stability.

Visual appearance (a), droplet size (b), droplet size distribution (c), and optical microscope images (scale bar: 200 μm) (f) of emulsion gels stabilized by TP, UTP and UTP-λ-CA complexes after long-term storage. Droplet size (d), droplet size distribution (e), visual appearance (g), and optical microscope images (scale bar: 100 μm) (h) of emulsion gels stabilized by TP, UTP and UTP-λ-CA complexes after thermal treatment.

Subsequently, all emulsion gel samples were subjected to heat treatment to assess their thermal stability. Visual appearance (Fig. 6g) showed that the TP-stabilized emulsion gel developed slight oiling-off after heating. When the sample vials were inverted, this sample completely slid off the vial bottom, indicating severe disruption of the internal gel network. In contrast, the emulsion gels stabilized by UTP and UTP-CA complexes remained firmly attached to the vial bottom. Droplet size analysis (Fig. 6d-6e) demonstrated that heat treatment significantly affected the droplet size of all samples, and the uniformity of droplet size distribution declined to varying extents. Specifically, the D4,3 and D3,2 values of TP and UTP-stabilized emulsion gels increased notably after heat treatment, which could be ascribed to heat-induced droplet coalescence and altered protein aggregation behavior [40]. In comparison, the emulsion gels stabilized by UTP-λ-CA complexes, especially the UTP-0.3%λ-CA complex-stabilized system, presented the smallest average droplet size along with the most uniform and relatively narrow size distribution. Microscopic observations (Fig. 6h) further corroborated the evolution pattern of internal droplets in emulsion gel systems following heat treatment. Collectively, the UTP-λ-CA complex-stabilized emulsion gels exhibited superior thermal stability relative to the single TP-stabilized system. From a practical standpoint, the superior storage and thermal stability of the UTP-λ-CA complex-stabilized emulsion gels indicate their strong potential for incorporation into commercial functional food products that require extended shelf-life and tolerance to thermal processing (e.g., pasteurization or sterilization). The resistance to droplet coalescence and gel network disruption observed under accelerated storage and heat treatment conditions suggests that these emulsion gels could maintain their structural integrity and quercetin delivery performance throughout typical food manufacturing, thereby reducing the need for additional stabilizers or preservatives.

Retention of quercetin

Since inhibiting quercetin degradation is essential for enhancing its bioaccessibility, the retention rate of quercetin in various emulsion gels under distinct environmental conditions was investigated. As shown in Fig. 7a, free quercetin degraded rapidly, with a retention rate of merely 25.11% after 20 h of UV irradiation. Encapsulation within emulsion gels substantially enhanced quercetin stability; nevertheless, the protective efficacy of the TP-stabilized emulsion gel was rather limited. In contrast, emulsion gels stabilized by UTP and especially by the UTP-λ-CA complexes conferred more robust protection to quercetin. After 20 h of UV exposure, the quercetin retention rate in the UTP-0.3%λ-CA complex-stabilized emulsion gel reached 77.07%. This improvement may be attributed to the dense three-dimensional interconnected gel network constructed in the UTP-λ-CA complex-stabilized emulsion gels, which could effectively impede the diffusion of reactive oxygen species and free radicals, thus retarding the oxidative degradation of quercetin [41].

Pseudo-first-order degradation kinetic curves of quercetin in emulsion gels stabilized by TP, UTP, and UTP-λ-CA complexes with different λ-CA concentrations under UV irradiation (c) and thermal treatment (d).

Fig. 7b depicts the quercetin retention rate profiles under thermal treatment. In line with the stability trend observed under UV irradiation, free quercetin exhibited the fastest degradation rate, corroborating the high thermosensitivity of unencapsulated quercetin [3]. By contrast, all emulsion gels delivered favorable thermal protection. Among all samples, the UTP-0.3%λ-CA complex-stabilized emulsion gel afforded the optimal thermal protection, preserving 74.93% of quercetin after 12 h of heating. The pseudo-first-order degradation kinetic curves of quercetin under UV irradiation and thermal treatment are presented in Fig. 7c-7d. Kinetic analysis revealed that under both treatment conditions, the quercetin degradation rate constant (k) in the UTP-λ-CA complex-stabilized emulsion gels was significantly lower than that of free quercetin in pure oil, accompanied by a notably prolonged degradation half-life (t1/2). These findings further validate that encapsulation in UTP-λ-CA complex-stabilized emulsion gels is an effective strategy to improve quercetin stability. Notably, the incorporation of 0.3% λ-CA further augmented the protective effect of the UTP-stabilized emulsion gels against quercetin degradation.

Overall, the enhanced photostability and thermostability of quercetin conferred by the UTP-0.3%λ-CA emulsion gel hold important practical implications for the development of functional food products. Many commercial food products containing flavonoids suffer from significant losses during processing and storage. The protective effect observed in this study suggests that the emulsion gel system could substantially reduce quercetin degradation under these real-world scenarios, thereby better preserving its physiological bioactivity.

Digestive properties

An in vitro gastrointestinal digestion model was employed to assess the digestive fate of quercetin-loaded emulsion gels. Fig. 8a-8b present the variations in mean droplet size of the different samples throughout the gastric and intestinal digestion stages, respectively. During the gastric phase (Fig. 8a), the droplet size of all samples increased significantly relative to the undigested counterparts, which is attributed to interfacial protein hydrolysis by pepsin, reduced electrostatic repulsion, and acid-mediated flocculation or coalescence [41]. Specifically, emulsion gels stabilized by TP and UTP exhibited the most pronounced size enlargement, as their loosely packed interfacial layers were highly susceptible to peptic degradation. In contrast, those fabricated with the UTP-λ-CA complexes (notably the UTP-0.3%λ-CA complex) formed a firmer interfacial architecture through electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding between UTP and λ-CA. This compact network effectively impeded pepsin-driven hydrolysis and restrained extensive droplet aggregation in the acidic environment, leading to only minimal changes in droplet size. Gao et al. [1] reported a comparable finding, wherein quercetin-loaded emulsion gels prepared with glycosylated whey protein-Enteromorpha polysaccharide conjugates underwent an increase in droplet size after gastric digestion. As shown in Fig. 8c, all samples underwent a charge reversal from negative to positive after gastric digestion. Emulsion gels containing λ-CA showed markedly lower absolute zeta potential values than those stabilized by TP or UTP. This effect arises primarily from the extensive protonation of interfacial proteins at pH 2.0, together with charge neutralization induced by the adsorption of anionic λ-CA onto the UTP-coated droplet surfaces, which is consistent with the findings of Gao et al. [1].

Changes in physicochemical and functional properties of emulsion gels stabilized by TP, UTP, and UTP-λ-CA complexes during in vitro simulated digestion: droplet size evolution in gastric (a) and intestinal fluids (b), respectively; zeta potential variation in gastric (c) and intestinal fluids (b), respectively; free fatty acid (FFA) release profile (e); and quercetin bioaccessibility (f).

During the intestinal phase (Fig. 8b), all samples underwent a pronounced reduction in mean droplet diameter, which arises from the combined effects of pancreatic lipolysis and bile salt-mediated emulsification and de-aggregation. Notably, the TP and UTP-stabilized emulsion gels still maintained comparatively larger droplets, whereas the UTP-λ-CA complex-stabilized emulsion gels displayed substantially smaller droplets. This discrepancy arises from the preserved interfacial architecture of the UTP-λ-CA systems effectively suppressed excessive droplet coalescence during intestinal digestion, thereby maintaining smaller individual droplet sizes. Despite the larger specific surface area of smaller droplets, the dense interfacial and bulk gel network of the UTP-λ-CA systems restricted lipase accessibility, which aligns with the slower FFA release kinetics presented below. Furthermore, all samples exhibited strongly negative zeta potential values under intestinal conditions (Fig. 8d), a result of the intestinal pH (7.0) being above the protein isoelectric point, combined with the interfacial adsorption of anionic bile salts and lipid hydrolysis products.

Upon entering the small intestine, triglycerides in chyme are hydrolyzed by pancreatic lipase into free fatty acids (FFAs) and monoglycerides. Fig. 8e presents the FFA release profiles under simulated intestinal digestion conditions. All samples exhibited a rapid initial FFA release within the first 60 min, followed by a gradual plateau. This release pattern is attributed to emulsion structure disintegration in the early digestion phase and interfacial lipolysis at droplet surfaces, which enhances the accessibility of the lipid system to lipase and bile salts [41]. Specifically, the TP-stabilized emulsion gel showed the highest cumulative FFA release, followed by the UTP-stabilized emulsion gel, while the UTP-λ-CA complex-stabilized emulsion gels exhibited relatively lower release levels. Among all formulations, the UTP-0.3%λ-CA emulsion gel exhibited the slowest release kinetics throughout the entire digestion process. Intestinal lipolysis is driven by the synergistic action of bile salts and pancreatic lipase: bile salts competitively displace interfacially adsorbed proteins to expose lipase binding sites, and pancreatic lipase subsequently adsorbs onto the oil–water interface to hydrolyze triglycerides into FFAs and monoglycerides. The FFA release rate is governed by multiple factors, including lipid composition, the composition and concentration of simulated intestinal fluid (SIF), emulsifier content, and lipid volume fraction [42]. The network structure formed by the UTP-λ-CA complexes, both at the interface and within the continuous phase, restricts lipase accessibility and finely modulates lipolysis kinetics, thereby governing the overall FFA release behavior [41]. Overall, a denser and more mechanically robust network structure can more effectively impede lipase diffusion and interfacial adsorption, thus reducing the lipid digestion rate.

Quercetin is a lipophilic small molecule that permeates the gastrointestinal epithelium predominantly via passive diffusion [45]. Its stability is adversely affected by multiple factors including bile salts, digestive enzymes, and gut microbiota, rendering it prone to rapid degradation and thereby lowering its bioaccessibility [43]. The effects of different emulsion gels on quercetin bioaccessibility are illustrated in Fig. 8f. The TP-stabilized emulsion gel yielded relatively low quercetin bioaccessibility (merely 14.92%), while the UTP group showed a marginal improvement. In comparison, emulsion gels stabilized by the UTP-λ-CA complexes exhibited markedly higher bioaccessibility, with the UTP-0.3%λ-CA system achieving the maximum value of 31.19%. This enhancement can be ascribed to the denser gel network formed by UTP and 0.3% λ-CA, which effectively protects quercetin from degradation under gastric acidic conditions and promotes its sustained release and micellar solubilization during the small intestinal phase [44], [45]. A similar phenomenon has been reported for emulsion gels stabilized by gelatin-agar complexes [42].

Conclusion

In this study, a synergistic modification strategy combining ultrasound pretreatment with λ-CA complexation was established to improve the physicochemical stability and in vitro bioaccessibility of quercetin in TP-based emulsion gels. The results demonstrated that ultrasonication induced partial unfolding of TP, exposing more hydrophobic groups and free sulfhydryl groups, which appeared to enhance intermolecular interactions with λ-CA and facilitate the formation of soluble UTP-λ-CA complexes. These complexes served as building blocks for assembling denser, more hierarchically ordered three-dimensional gel networks through hydrogen bonding, electrostatic associations, and bridging flocculation mediated by λ-CA chains, which exerted strong steric hindrance to inhibit droplet aggregation and significantly improved the viscoelasticity and environmental stability, as well as enhanced resistance to gastrointestinal digestion in the in vitro model. Notably, the emulsion gel fabricated by the UTP-0.3%λ-CA complex formed the most compact and interconnected gel matrix, which may have acted as a robust physical barrier against the photodegradation and thermal degradation of encapsulated quercetin, and increased its bioaccessibility by 2.09-fold relative to the native TP emulsion gel. Collectively, this synergistic strategy offers an efficient approach for the functional modification of aquatic protein and the construction of delivery systems for hydrophobic bioactive compounds, providing a theoretical basis for the high-value utilization of tilapia and the rational design of advanced nutraceutical delivery systems.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. The static in vitro digestion model cannot fully replicate the dynamic physiological conditions of the human gastrointestinal tract, and the results have not been validated in real food matrices, where interactions with other components (e.g., salts, other polysaccharides, or lipid phases) may alter gel performance and quercetin bioaccessibility. Additionally, while the gel network appeared to provide physical protection, systematic evaluation of quercetin release kinetics was not performed. Furthermore, several mechanistic interpretations proposed in this work (including λ-CA adsorption, complex coacervation, and reactive oxygen species diffusion inhibition) were inferred from indirect measurements (e.g., ζ-potential, sulfhydryl content, SEM, rheology) without validation by analytical techniques such as ITC or interfacial rheology.

To translate this strategy into practice, future work should focus on: (1) in vivo pharmacokinetic and bioavailability studies to validate the enhanced bioaccessibility and elucidate absorption pathways; (2) incorporating the emulsion gels into real food systems to evaluate their behavior under realistic processing, storage, and consumption scenarios, including pilot-scale production trials to assess scalability and cost-effectiveness; (3) systematic investigation of oil phase composition and quercetin loading levels on gel network integrity and release kinetics; (4) extension of this synergistic modification approach to encapsulate other hydrophobic bioactive compounds with diverse properties, and evaluation of consumer acceptability, including sensory attributes (texture, flavor, and appearance) and stability under commercial distribution conditions; and (5) employing complementary biophysical and interfacial characterization techniques (e.g., ITC, interfacial rheology, or molecular docking) to further elucidate the molecular basis of the observed synergistic effects. Addressing these aspects will facilitate the rational design of aquatic protein-based delivery systems for nutraceutical applications.
