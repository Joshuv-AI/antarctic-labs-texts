Nanosystem-Mediated Phototherapy (PDT/PTT) - Chemodynamic Therapy for Synergistic Antitumor Therapy: Strategies and Advances
Authors: Yuxuan Ma, Jie Gong, Zixuan Wu, Xinyi Tang, Xinhua Xia, Hong Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemodynamic therapy (CDT) eradicates tumor cells by generating hydroxyl radicals (•OH) through Fenton/Fenton-like reactions, yet its therapeutic efficacy is severely constrained by intrinsic deficiencies of the tumor microenvironment (TME), including insufficient endogenous hydrogen peroxide(H2O2), suboptimal pH, robust glutathione (GSH)-mediated reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging, and tumor hypoxia. As potent synergistic modalities, photodynamic therapy (PDT) elevates oxidative stress via ROS overproduction, while photothermal therapy (PTT) accelerates Fenton reaction kinetics and improves intratumoral nanotherapeutic penetration, collectively remedying the inherent drawbacks of single CDT. Unlike conventional reviews that primarily catalogue nanomaterial diversity and classification, this review constructs a refined mechanistic framework to elaborate the complementary mechanisms of PDT and PTT toward CDT in terms of reaction kinetics modulation, endogenous substrate replenishment, and tumor antioxidant defense inhibition. We systematically overview the core synergistic principles of integrated nanosystems, covering ROS cascade amplification, GSH depletion-initiated ferroptosis, TME acidification, extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, and immunogenic cell death. We further summarize recent advances in dual-modal PDT-CDT, PTT-CDT, and PDT-PTT-CDT synergistic nanoplatforms, and highlight the evolutionary trend of such nanotherapeutics-from primitive thermochemical coupling designs to advanced architectures with TME-responsive targeted delivery, second near-infrared (NIR-II) deep-tissue penetration, enzyme-cascaded self-substrate supply, and integrated theranostic functions. Moreover, the key bottlenecks hindering clinical translation are discussed, including limited light penetration depth, TME heterogeneity, suboptimal biosafety profiles, and inadequate clinical validation systems. Three pivotal priorities for future translational development are further proposed: the fabrication of NIR-II deep-penetrating nanoplatforms, the establishment of standardized manufacturing and quality control workflows for regulatory compliance, and the development of TME-adaptive, imaging-guided precision tumor delivery strategies.

Introduction

Global cancer incidence is rising rapidly, creating a major public health crisis. In 2022, there were approximately 20 million new cancer cases and 9.7 million cancer-related deaths worldwide, with Asia accounting for nearly half the global burden. Driven by aging populations and growing risk factors, new cases are projected to increase by 77% to 35 million by 2050, especially in low- and medium-human development index (HDI) countries.1 Malignant tumors are highly invasive and metastasize easily,2,3 conventional treatments including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy,4 improve patient survival but have critical limitations.5 Surgery carries risks of recurrence and complications and is only applicable to localized tumors.6 Radiotherapy induces resistance and causes damage to adjacent normal tissues.7 Chemotherapy leads to systemic toxicity, multidrug resistance, and off-target effects.8 Targeted therapy relies on specific genetic mutations and benefits only a small patient population.9 Immunotherapy suffers from low response rates and immune-related adverse events.10 These drawbacks severely restrict clinical outcomes and quality of life, highlighting the urgent need for safer and more effective anticancer strategies. Tumor progression depends not only on cancer cell proliferation but also on the complex TME, which comprises stromal cells, ECM, and vascular networks.11 The high heterogeneity, physiological barriers, and dysregulated ROS signaling in the TME synergistically drive therapeutic resistance.12 Sustained oxidative stress is a key hallmark of cancer,13 because tumor cells can adapt to high ROS levels, hypoxia, and acidosis via metabolic reprogramming to maintain survival and proliferation advantages.14 Against this background, TME-targeted precision therapy has become a major research focus.15

Among emerging TME-targeted strategies, CDT stands out for its unique advantages over conventional modalities and other nanomedicine-based approaches. CDT is particularly promising for solid tumor treatment: it uses endogenous H2O2 and transition-metal-mediated Fenton/Fenton-like reactions to generate cytotoxic •OH for tumor-specific ablation without external energy input, thereby avoiding the tissue damage and depth limitations associated with phototherapy or radiotherapy, and bypassing the systemic toxicity and drug resistance seen in chemotherapy and targeted therapy.16 Moreover, CDT exploits the inherently elevated H2O2 levels and acidic pH of the TME, enabling a degree of tumor-selective cytotoxicity that is difficult to achieve with conventional drugs. However, CDT is limited by insufficient H2O2 levels, unfavorable TME pH, and high concentrations of GSH that neutralize ROS.17–20 Free catalytic agents also exhibit poor tumor-targeting efficiency and insufficient in vivo stability.21 Therefore, rational combination of CDT with other therapeutic modalities has become essential to enhance antitumor efficacy.22 Phototherapy exerts antitumor effects under light irradiation: PDT generates cytotoxic ROS using photosensitizers,23 whereas PTT converts light energy into localized hyperthermia.24 Both approaches improve tumor vascular perfusion and ECM permeability to enhance intratumoral drug delivery, and light activation enables precise spatiotemporal control.25 In recent years, phototherapy has progressively shifted from the conventional NIR-I window (700~900 nm) toward the second near-infrared NIR-II window (900~1880 nm), which offers significantly deeper tissue penetration, reduced light scattering, and lower autofluorescence, thereby enabling effective treatment of deep-seated tumors that are inaccessible to NIR-I phototherapy. Nevertheless, conventional phototherapy faces challenges including poor water solubility, insufficient tumor targeting, photobleaching, biosafety concerns, and nonspecific tissue damage.26,27 Dense ECM and limited light penetration depth further compromise therapeutic efficiency.28 These challenges indicate the urgent demand for advanced nanodelivery systems and synergistic combination therapies, in which CDT can serve as a versatile partner to address the shortcomings of phototherapy and other regimens while retaining its inherent tumor-specific, energy-independent mechanism.

Recent advances in nanotechnology provide powerful solutions to these obstacles. Multifunctional nanoplatforms allow co-delivery of multiple therapeutic agents, passive tumor targeting through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, and active targeting via specific ligand modification.22,29 Stimuli-responsive controlled-release systems further improve tumor-specific accumulation and on-demand activation.30,31 Nanosystem-mediated PDT-CDT, PTT-CDT, and PDT-PTT-CDT synergistic platforms have emerged as leading strategies to overcome the limitations of monotherapy.32,33 Despite extensive preclinical validation in murine models, clinical translation of phototherapy-CDT combinatorial nanosystems remains substantially lagging. Only a limited number of phototherapeutic nanomedicines have gained regulatory approval. For PDT, approved photosensitizers include Photofrin® (porfimer sodium, Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved since 1995 for esophageal and early-stage lung cancer) and Ameluz® (aminolevulinic acid hydrochloride, approved for actinic keratosis).34 For PTT, despite over two decades of development, few agents have advanced to clinical trials. Indocyanine green (ICG, an FDA-approved NIR dye) and Prussian blue (PB, FDA-approved as an antidote) have demonstrated photothermal conversion capability,35,36 while the most advanced PTT platform is AuroShell® (gold nanoshells), which has completed Phase I trials in prostate, lung, and head/neck cancers.37 This stark preclinical-clinical disparity highlights formidable translational barriers-TME heterogeneity, biosafety concerns, inconsistent EPR effects, and lack of standardized manufacturing-that severely hinder bench-to-bedside translation.

Most existing reviews in this field mainly focus on the classification of functional nanomaterials or summarize customized nanosystems for specific combinatorial therapeutic regimens. Differing from these material- and application-centered studies, this review integrates fundamental synergistic mechanisms with the rational design of multifunctional nanosystems to provide a holistic research perspective. The core innovations of this work are summarized in three dimensions. First, we systematically expound the multi-dimensional complementary mechanisms of photodynamic therapy/photothermal therapy-enhanced CDT from thermodynamic, kinetic, and biological pathway perspectives. Second, by tracing the developmental history of this field, we clarify its intrinsic design logic and evolutionary trends, covering progressive development stages from initial thermochemical coupling and TME-responsive intelligent delivery systems to enzyme cascade self-sustaining systems and integrated multimodal theranostic platforms. Third, we highlight the unresolved issues and clinical translational barriers of current synergistic nanotherapies, establishing a balanced understanding to bridge the gap between preclinical achievements and clinical practicality.

This review adopts a progressive research framework of “basic mechanisms, therapeutic limitations, synergistic principles, nanosystem evolution, challenges and prospects”. We first elaborate on the inherent bottlenecks of monotherapy and TME-induced therapeutic obstacles. Subsequently, the multi-level synergistic mechanisms of phototherapy-combined CDT are systematically analyzed. Furthermore, we summarize the evolutionary process of relevant nanoplatforms and the research progress of representative advanced nanosystems. Finally, we prospect emerging research directions, including the establishment of unified evaluation criteria, the advancement of NIR-II deep-tissue phototherapy, and the development of scalable manufacturing strategies for clinical translation. This review aims to provide systematic insights and reliable guidance for the rational design and clinical translation of high-efficiency multimodal antitumor nanotherapies.

Chemodynamic Therapy

Basic Principles of CDT

The Fenton reaction was first reported in 1893, its core mechanism is that ferrous ions catalyze the decomposition of H2O2 under strongly acidic conditions to generate highly cytotoxic •OH and ferric ions.38 Nevertheless, the classical Fenton reaction requires an extremely acidic environment (pH 3.0~4.0), which is incompatible with the weakly acidic TME (pH 5.5~6.5), thus significantly restricting its application in tumor therapy.39 To address this issue, researchers have developed various Fenton-like catalytic systems,22 extending the catalyst range to diverse transition metals and their compounds, including Cu+/Cu2+, Mn2+, Co2+, and Ce3+.40–42 This strategy reduces the dependence on ferrous ions and strongly acidic conditions, allowing the catalytic system to better match the intrinsic characteristics of the TME.

In 2016, Bu and Shi’s group synthesized amorphous iron nanoparticles (AFeNPs) with a diameter of 10~20 nm using a bubble-assisted method.43 Benefiting from their metastable structure, AFeNPs can continuously release ferrous ions in the weakly acidic TME, which further catalyze endogenous H2O2 to generate •OH and ultimately induce tumor cell death. This work laid an important theoretical and experimental foundation for the development of CDT. The fundamental principle of CDT is to utilize Fenton/Fenton-like reactions to in situ generate highly reactive •OH specifically at tumor sites.44

As an endogenous activation-dependent therapeutic modality, CDT is inherently compatible with the unique physiological features of tumors.49 The weakly acidic pH, elevated H2O2 level, low catalase activity, high GSH concentration and hypoxic state in the TME as illustrated in Figure 1A, not only drive malignant tumor progression but also provide natural prerequisites for the tumor-targeting selectivity of CDT.43,50,51 Compared with conventional tumor therapies, CDT exhibits distinct advantages. First, it shows high tumor selectivity. Normal tissues maintain a neutral microenvironment with relatively low H2O2 levels, which barely triggers Fenton/Fenton-like reactions and thus greatly reduces oxidative damage to healthy tissues.52 Second, CDT does not require exogenous energy activation, overcoming the tissue penetration limitations of PDT and PTT, and showing great potential for the treatment of deep-seated solid tumors.53,54 Third, CDT has excellent combination compatibility. In recent years, CDT-based antitumor strategies have become a research hotspot in the field of precision oncology. However, limited by the intrinsic physiological barriers of the TME and inherent defects of current catalytic systems, the clinical translation of CDT still faces multiple critical bottlenecks that need to be urgently addressed.57–59

Challenges and Limitations of CDT

Limitations of CDT Imposed by the Tumor Microenvironment

Compared with normal tissues, the TME exhibits distinct biological properties and plays a pivotal role in tumor proliferation, invasion and immune evasion.60 The therapeutic efficacy of CDT is strongly governed by the intrinsic characteristics of the TME, mainly including H2O2 level, pH value and GSH concentration and hypoxia.22,39

Insufficient Endogenous H2O2 Levels

H2O2 serves as the core substrate for Fenton/Fenton-like reactions, and its concentration directly determines the oxidative killing efficiency of CDT.16 Tumor cells accumulate ROS owing to mitochondrial dysfunction and hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α)-mediated upregulation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase (NOX).61–64 Although the H2O2 level in the TME (approximately 100 μM~1 mM) is higher than that in normal tissues (approximately 10 nM~1 μM), it is still far lower than the optimal concentration required for Fenton reactions (>10 mM).17 This discrepancy mainly arises from three aspects. First, the spatial distribution of H2O2 is highly uneven,65 H2O2 is almost depleted in hypoxic deep tumor regions, and oxidative catalysis only occurs in sporadic localized foci.66 Second, endogenous clearance systems continuously eliminate H2O2. Tumor cells highly express catalase and glutathione peroxidase, accompanied by elevated intracellular GSH content, leading to rapid H2O2 clearance within minutes.66–68 Third, there is a dynamic imbalance between H2O2 supply and consumption. Fenton reactions continuously consume H2O2, while its endogenous generation rate is much lower than the degradation rate, failing to sustain sufficient •OH burst production.69 Therefore, elevating intratumoral H2O2 concentration to the effective reaction threshold is a primary strategy to relieve substrate restriction and enhance CDT efficacy.

Mild Acidity Deficiency in the TME

The weakly acidic microenvironment of solid tumors (pH 6.5~7.0) originates from massive accumulation of lactate and H+ driven by the Warburg effect.70,71 Although such mild acidity endows CDT with certain tissue selectivity,59 it still deviates greatly from the optimal pH of Fenton reactions (approximately 3.0).21,59 Taking the Fe2+/Fe3+ system as an example,39 elevated pH triggers Fe3+ hydrolysis to form inert iron hydroxide precipitates, causing a sharp decline in catalytic activity.72,73 Meanwhile, suboptimal pH tends to induce the generation of high-valent iron species (Fe(IV)=O) rather than •OH, and the oxidizing capacity of Fe(IV)=O is much weaker than that of •OH.18,74,75 Although catalytic systems such as Cu+/Cu2+ and Mn2+/Mn3+ possess a slightly wider pH tolerance,76,77 their catalytic efficiency under the typical weakly acidic tumor microenvironment (pH 6.5~7.0) remains significantly lower than that under optimal acidic conditions. Accordingly, acidifying the TME through exogenous acid supplementation or in situ acid generation, alongside pH-triggered catalyst release systems, is essential to optimize Fenton reaction conditions and break through the CDT efficiency bottleneck.

Antioxidant Interference Induced by GSH

Intracellular GSH concentration in tumor cells can reach 1~10 mM, over four times higher than that in normal tissues.78 High HIF-1α expression upregulates γ-glutamylcysteine ligase (GCL) and the xCT transporter system, while glutathione reductase continuously reduces oxidized glutathione (GSSG) to GSH, forming a dual-driven mechanism of GSH synthesis and reduction.79,80 The active sulfhydryl group of GSH reacts rapidly with •OH at a rate constant of k ≈ 1.3×1010 M−1s−1, scavenging •OH into H2O2. and limiting its effective diffusion distance to the.81 Hence, disrupting the GSH antioxidant defense shield through exogenous GSH depletion or endogenous GSH conversion is essential to potentiate CDT.82 Meanwhile, the high GSH level in tumors can also be exploited as an intrinsic biomarker to construct reduction-responsive intelligent delivery systems for precise tumor intervention.83,84

Constraints from Tumor Hypoxia

Solid tumors typically possess a low oxygen partial pressure,85,86 which is primarily caused by the high oxygen consumption of proliferating cancer cells,87,88 vascular abnormalities-mediated oxygen delivery impairment,89,90 and oxygen diffusion restriction under high cell density.91 From the perspective of CDT, tumor hypoxia severely compromises its therapeutic efficacy through multiple interconnected mechanisms. Hypoxia activates multiple adaptive cellular responses via the HIF-1α signaling axis. It facilitates the metabolic shift from mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis, thereby decreasing endogenous H2O2 accumulation,92–94 which directly deprives the Fenton reaction of its essential substrate and fundamentally limits the oxidative killing efficiency of CDT. Hypoxia further upregulates the expression of GSH-related genes (SLC7A11 and GCLM),95–97 and the elevation of NADPH mediated by SHMT2 and PHGDH strengthens the antioxidant defense of hypoxic tumor cells,98–100 thereby accelerating the scavenging of •OH and shortening its effective diffusion distance. Moreover, hypoxia induces the overexpression of multidrug resistance proteins such as MDR1.101 Chronic hypoxia suppresses the DNA repair capacity of tumor cells and triggers pro-survival signaling,102 ultimately leading to therapeutic resistance.103 The upregulation of pro-invasive factors under hypoxia also increases the metastatic and recurrent risks of malignant tumors.104 In addition, elevated interstitial fluid pressure in tumor tissues hinders the intratumoral penetration of therapeutic agents.105 Hypoxia-driven tumor microenvironment remodeling further impedes the intratumoral accumulation and intracellular delivery of therapeutic drugs,106,107 which critically restricts the delivery of CDT catalysts to deep tumor regions. These unfavorable characteristics severely restrict the enrichment and therapeutic efficacy of anticancer agents. Specifically, for CDT, hypoxia creates a self-reinforcing barrier: including substrate scarcity, enhanced ROS clearance, and blocked catalyst delivery. These factors collectively constitute the core bottleneck that limits the clinical application of CDT. To reverse this detrimental vicious cycle, multiple nanotherapeutic strategies have been developed, including nanozyme-mediated in-situ oxygen generation, tumor oxygenation improvement, and the construction of hypoxia-activated prodrugs that respond to hypoxic tumor microenvironments.108,109

In summary, four intrinsic TME characteristics, including insufficient H2O2 level, mild acidification, excessive GSH accumulation and severe hypoxia, constrain CDT performance from the aspects of substrate provision, catalytic efficiency, ROS elimination and drug delivery. These adverse microenvironmental drawbacks are interconnected and exacerbated through HIF-1α-associated signaling, thereby constructing a vicious cycle involving insufficient substrates, low catalytic activity, rapid ROS clearance and intensified tumor resistance.

Intrinsic Limitations of Catalysts

Low Catalytic Activity

The catalytic efficiency of Fenton/Fenton-like reactions is inherently limited by active site exposure, substrate accessibility, and metal ion release behavior.110,111 Uncontrolled ion leakage causes systemic toxicity, while insufficient ion release fails to sustain effective tumoricidal concentrations.112–114 The weakly acidic tumor microenvironment further deactivates metal catalysts and shifts the reaction pathway toward low-toxic Fe(IV)=O rather than cytotoxic •OH.115 Moreover, the sluggish Fe3+/Fe2+ regeneration restricts continuous catalytic cycling, resulting in insufficient ROS generation.116,117 Accordingly, the fabrication of high-efficiency recyclable catalysts and external physical field intervention have been widely adopted to optimize reaction kinetics and improve CDT efficacy.118,119

Poor Tumor Targeting Ability

Most CDT catalysts lack tumor-specific targeting ability and suffer from complicated biological delivery barriers. These agents are easily trapped by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), thereby accumulating mainly in the liver and spleen and exhibiting poor tumor-targeting performance.120–122 Tumor heterogeneity further limits the targeting accuracy of single-ligand functionalization.123 In addition, intricate surface decoration may cover catalytic active sites and induce undesirable immune reactions.124 Abnormal tumor vasculature and dense stroma also weaken the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, hindering the deep penetration of catalysts.125–127 Most catalysts merely remain around blood vessels and cannot reach hypoxic tumor areas, which greatly impairs the precision and biosafety of CDT.128

Biocompatibility and Metabolic Limitations

Inorganic catalysts represented by metal oxides and noble metals commonly show low biodegradability. Their prolonged accumulation in the liver and spleen may induce chronic inflammation, tissue fibrosis and secondary tumor risks.129–131 By comparison, organic catalysts such as porphyrins and indocyanine green (ICG) derivatives possess poor photostability, which are prone to photobleaching and rapid metabolic clearance in vivo.132 Metal ions exhibit divergent metabolic patterns. Fe and Cu can be eliminated through biliary or renal excretion, whereas Mn and Ce are susceptible to long-term retention in the RES.133 At present, comprehensive toxicokinetic research regarding tissue deposition, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity remains inadequate. Additionally, the physicochemical and morphological features of catalysts strongly affect protein adsorption, cellular internalization and subcellular distribution.130 The absence of long-term toxicological assessment and clinical Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) criteria further hinders the clinical transformation of CDT catalysts.134,135

Collectively, CDT catalysts face three interconnected bottlenecks: inferior catalytic activity, inadequate tumor targeting, and undesirable biocompatibility. Low activity demands higher dosages, which worsens off-target distribution and systemic toxicity. Poor tumor accumulation in turn further diminishes efficacy. Unresolved metabolic and biosafety risks additionally impede clinical translation. These drawbacks feed into one another, forming a translational barrier marked by low radical yield, poor tumor enrichment, and high biological risk. Together with intrinsic TME restrictions, they limit the clinical advancement of CDT (Table 1). Building multifunctional intelligent nanosystems for targeted accumulation, controlled release, and performance optimization thus remains essential to push forward multimodal synergistic tumor therapy. Main Challenges and Limiting Factors of CDT

Mechanisms by Which Phototherapy (PDT / PTT) Enhances CDT

Among phototherapy-based strategies, PDT and PTT have attracted considerable attention as non-invasive and minimally invasive treatment modalities with precise spatiotemporal control.137,138 PDT employs photosensitizers to convert molecular oxygen into ROS such as singlet oxygen (1O2),139 while PTT generates localized thermal energy via photothermal conversion materials; both modalities can exert synergistic enhancement effects with CDT.140

Synergistic Mechanism of PDT-CDT

Photodynamic therapy was proposed by Lipson and Schwartz in 1960.141 Its core mechanism involves a photosensitizer (PS) being excited by a light source of a specific wavelength (650~850 nm), which then generates ROS/H2O2 through Type I (electron transfer) or Type II (energy transfer) reactions. This precisely confines the cellular damage effect to the irradiated area, achieving localized tumor destruction,142 as illustrated in Figure 2. PDT combines four key features: spatiotemporal controllability, targeted selectivity, minimally invasive repeatability, and immune activation.143–146 The generation of ROS is precisely regulated by an external light source, enabling on-demand treatment.147 The accumulation of photosensitizers in tumor tissue and the irradiation range can be precisely confined to the lesion area, significantly reducing damage to normal tissues.148 The treatment is non-invasive and lacks the cumulative toxicity of ionizing radiation, allowing for repeated irradiation of the same site.144,149 The induction of immunogenic cell death (ICD) releases tumor antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), triggering local and even systemic antitumor immune responses.150 Its antitumor effects rely on three mechanisms: direct ROS-mediated cell killing, disruption of microvascular networks to block nutrient supply, and immune activation.151–154 However, PDT alone faces three major bottlenecks: ① nonspecific distribution of photosensitizers and photobleaching.155 ② limited penetration depth of the light source (significant energy attenuation beyond 0.5 cm).156,157 ③ Type II reactions that consume oxygen, exacerbating hypoxia, activating HIF-1α, and inducing high GSH expression, thereby creating a vicious cycle of “therapeutic hypoxia”.158 These limitations are naturally complemented by CDT,159 which does not rely on exogenous oxygen, can consume GSH, and generates •OH to compensate for insufficient ROS. The synergy between the two enables deep coupling of antitumor effects across multiple levels, including substrate supply, amplification of oxidative stress, microenvironment regulation, tissue penetration, and immune activation,137 as illustrated in Figure 3. ROS Cascade Amplification

ROS including 1O2 and superoxide anion (•O2−) generated by PDT exert complementary cytotoxic effects with •OH produced by CDT.39,160 Certain nanomaterials with both photoresponsive and enzyme-mimetic properties can mimic glucose oxidase activity upon light irradiation, catalyzing the oxidation of glucose to simultaneously generate H2O2 and gluconic acid.161 This process directly provides exogenous substrates for Fenton reactions.162 Meanwhile, the accumulation of gluconic acid further lowers intracellular pH, accelerating the release and activation of catalytic metal ions.163 Furthermore, mild PDT triggers intracellular oxidative stress to transiently activate aerobic metabolism and upregulate endogenous H2O2 production,164 while •OH generated by CDT oxidizes cell membrane lipids to produce lipid hydroperoxides (LOOH).165–167 Under light irradiation, LOOH can undergo photolysis or participate in a chain reaction to generate additional ROS. Moreover, the oxygen-independent nature of CDT avoids the self-limitation of ROS generation caused by oxygen consumption during PDT, enabling PDT to maintain its photoactivation efficiency even in hypoxic tumor regions.168 Based on this cascade mechanism, Zhu et al constructed a carrier-free supramolecular nanomedicine (Ce6-erastin) via the self-assembly of Ce6 and the ferroptosis inducer erastin through hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking. Upon light irradiation, 1O2 produced by Ce6 is converted into H2O2, which fuels Fe2+-mediated Fenton reactions. The generated •OH further oxidizes lipids into LOOH, which resensitizes Ce6 and amplifies ROS generation in a self-amplified loop.169 Hu et al developed a Ce6/FeOOH@BSA nanoplatform that uses BSA to stabilize hydrophobic Ce6 for efficient delivery. Under light irradiation, Ce6 generates 1O2 for PDT, while FeOOH releases Fe3+ that reacts with intracellular H2O2 to produce •OH for CDT. Meanwhile, FeOOH consumes GSH to suppress ROS scavenging, ultimately achieving synergistic antitumor efficacy.170

GSH Depletion

CDT directly depletes GSH via Fenton reactions, whereas PDT-generated 1O2 oxidizes GSH into GSSG, these processes act synergistically to accelerate GSH depletion in tumor cells.171 Additionally, certain nanocarriers can directly react with GSH, further amplifying the depletion effect.172 GSH depletion inhibits glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) activity, overcomes tumor cell resistance to ferroptosis, and amplifies CDT-mediated lipid peroxidation damage.173–175 Concurrently, GSH depletion weakens the antioxidant defense capacity of immunosuppressive cells, such as regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), thereby reshaping the immune-suppressive TME.176 Furthermore, •OH generated by CDT can oxidize thioredoxin reductase (TrxR), blocking another critical antioxidant pathway besides GSH and significantly extending the effective therapeutic window.177 The Fe3O4-PLGA-Ce6 nanoparticles developed by Chen et al release Fe2+ and Ce6 in the acidic TME. Fe2+ triggers Fenton reaction to deplete GSH and generate •OH, while Ce6 generates 1O2 upon light exposure, further oxidizing the thiol groups of GSH.178 The synergistic effect of these two processes inactivates GPX4 and leads to the accumulation of lipid ROS, efficiently inducing ferroptosis. Simultaneously, GSH depletion reduces the proportion of Tregs and downregulates programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, reshaping the immune-suppressive TME and significantly inhibiting lung metastasis of 4T1 breast cancer.179,180

Microenvironment Acidification

PDT inhibits the mitochondrial respiratory chain and shifts tumor cell metabolism from aerobic respiration to glycolysis, resulting in lactic acid accumulation.181,182 This acidified TME optimizes the pH condition for Fenton reaction to accelerate the Fe3+/Fe2+ cyclic conversion.183 Concurrently, the generated •OH further aggravates tumor acidification, thereby constructing a self-reinforcing positive feedback loop. Acidic conditions can also trigger the depolymerization of pH-responsive carriers to release catalytic active sites,184 and improve immunosuppression by promoting the polarization of macrophages from the M2 (anti-inflammatory) to M1 (pro-inflammatory) phenotype.185,186 The FT@HANPs nanocoordinated polymer developed by Wang et al187 utilizes Fe(III)-mediated Fenton reaction to generate •OH for CDT activation and GSH depletion. Meso-tetra (4-carboxyphenyl) porphyrin (TCPP)-mediated PDT restores its fluorescence and ROS generation capacity in the acidic TME, achieving controllable PDT. Rapid GSH depletion further synergistically enhances the efficacy of both CDT and PDT. Conjugation with hyaluronic acid (HA) enables targeted binding to the CD44 receptor, achieving tumor-specific accumulation while shielding normal tissues from toxic effects. Furthermore, an acidic environment promotes the protonation of certain photosensitizers, enhancing their light absorption cross-section and triplet quantum yield, thereby improving PDT photosensitization efficiency.188

Synergistic Activation of Antitumor Immunity

PDT effectively induces ICD, releasing DAMPs such as calreticulin (CRT), adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), thereby initiating an antitumor immune response.189 •OH generated by CDT can attenuate the levels of immunosuppressive factors such as IL-10 and TGF-β, thereby relieving the immunosuppressive state of the TME.190 Together, these mechanisms form a synergistic framework of “ICD initiation + immune desuppression”.191,192 Additionally, CDT-induced ferroptosis releases immunogenic oxidized phospholipids, which act as novel DAMPs to complement the antigenic spectrum of PDT-induced ICD.193–195 Following the degradation of immunosuppressive factors by CDT, PDT-released CRT is more readily recognized by dendritic cells (DCs), thereby enhancing the efficiency of antigen cross-presentation.196 Furthermore, GSH depletion by CDT reduces oxidative stress in T cells, restoring their proliferative and cytotoxic functions, and transforming the PDT-activated systemic immune response from a simple “initiation signal” into effective “tumor clearance”.176

Collectively, the PDT-CDT synergy integrates ROS cascade amplification, GSH depletion, microenvironment acidification, and immune activation. Nevertheless, the poor bioavailability of free PSs and catalysts underscores the necessity of nanocarrier-mediated co-delivery for clinical translation.

PTT relies on the non-radiative relaxation of photothermal agents (PTAs) after absorbing near-infrared (NIR, 700~1100 nm) light to convert energy into heat, this elevates the local temperature within tumors, inducing protein denaturation, membrane structure disruption, and microvascular embolization, thereby achieving tumor ablation.197–199 Based on temperature thresholds, PTT can be classified into conventional PTT (≥45 °C, direct thermal ablation) and mild PTT (MPTT, 42–45 °C),200 as illustrated in Figure 4. Thermodynamic Enhancement of Catalytic Efficiency

The rates of Fenton and Fenton-like reactions follow Arrhenius’ law; increased temperature exponentially reduces activation energy and accelerates the catalytic cycle. PTT-induced localized hyperthermia (42~55 °C) directly accelerates the redox cycle of Fe2+/Fe3+ or Cu+/Cu2+, increasing the rate constant for •OH generation.211 Simultaneously, this triggers a structural response in the carrier, enabling the thermally controlled release of catalytic ions.212 CDT-generated •OH disrupts cellular redox homeostasis and protein folding to indirectly impair HSP70/90-mediated cytoprotection, while concurrent PTT hyperthermia directly destabilizes HSP chaperones. This combination collectively lowers the thermal tolerance threshold of tumor cells.213 •OH-mediated membrane lipid peroxidation reduces the thermal stability of the cell membrane, enhancing the thermal sensitivity of PTT.214 These two mechanisms synergize to replace physical heating with chemical sensitization, enabling PTT to achieve effective cell killing at mild temperatures.215 Wu et al,213 reported an Ag-Cu@SiO2-PDA/GOx nanoreactor, in which the Ag-Cu core catalyzes H2O2 to generate •OH for HSP70 degradation, and the PDA/GOx shell produces mild hyperthermia to further downregulate HSP70, thereby sensitizing mild-temperature PTT. Laser irradiation and GOx-mediated self-amplified catalysis generate more •OH, forming a thermochemical positive feedback loop. Song et al,216 fabricated mFeB@PDA-FA, in which mesoporous Fe3O4 loaded with BSO catalyzes •OH generation and inhibits GSH synthesis, while PDA-mediated PTT synergistically enhances CDT and ferroptosis.

Supplementing H2O2 Substrates and Alleviating Hypoxia

PTT establishes a continuous supply of H2O2 through multiple pathways. Localized heating enhances GOx catalytic activity, driving glucose decomposition to produce H2O2 and gluconic acid, thereby simultaneously replenishing substrates and acidifying the TME.217 Mild thermal effects dilate microvessels, improve oxygenation, and promote mitochondrial aerobic metabolism, increasing endogenous H2O2.218 Temperature-responsive carriers thermally trigger the release of H2O2/O2 to achieve dual replenishment, CDT continuously consumes H2O2 to generate •OH, filling the therapeutic gap after the thermal effect of PTT subsides, thereby extending the synergistic effect from an instantaneous thermal burst to sustained chemical suppression.219 This reciprocal reinforcement has been demonstrated in integrated nanoplatforms. For instance, Xu et al,7 fabricated glucose-responsive enzymatic Fe@HRP-ABTS/GOx nanodots via protein-templated biomineralization. Intracellular GOx oxidized glucose to yield self-supplied H2O2, which horseradish peroxidase (HRP) subsequently converted into oxidized 2,2’-Hydrazine-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid (ABTS) for photothermal conversion. Concurrently, glutathione (GSH) reduced Fe3+ to Fe2+, which reacted with the self-generated H2O2 via Fenton reactions to produce •OH, simultaneously depleting GSH and inducing tumor CDT. Under NIR irradiation, the nanodots achieved reinforced catalytic PTT-CDT with superior cancer cell killing and tumor elimination in hepatoma models.

GSH Depletion and Overcoming Ferroptosis Resistance

Intracellular GSH levels in tumor cells are markedly higher than those in normal tissues, acting as a critical antioxidant barrier against •OH.220 PTT-mediated hyperthermia decreases the GSH/GSSG ratio as well as depletes GSH, and thereby indirectly inactivates GPX4.221 The resultant GSH deficiency indirectly inhibits GPX4 activity, while hyperthermia disrupts GSH regeneration kinetics and lipid repair flux, functionally inactivating GPX4; concurrent CDT consumes residual GSH to block catalytic cycling, achieving dual GPX4 suppression. Such thermal-enzymatic synergy effectively reverses tumor ferroptosis resistance.222 Furthermore, CDT augments PTT-triggered ferroptosis by consuming GSH and inactivating GSH synthetase to block GSH regeneration. Meanwhile, Fenton-generated •OH degrades HSP70/90 chaperones and weakens tumor thermotolerance.215 For instance, Cheng et al223 developed degradable iron-rich mesoporous polydopamine (MPDA@Fe) wherein the polydopamine scaffold and Fenton cycling cooperatively depleted GSH, suppressing GPX4 to induce ferroptosis, photothermal activation further accelerated Fenton kinetics via the Arrhenius effect, achieving synergistic ferroptosis and chemodynamic therapy. Similarly, Li et al,224 engineered Fe-doped polydopamine nanozymes (Fe-PDA) where Fenton-generated •OH initiated lipid peroxidation, concurrently downregulating System Xc- to inhibit GSH synthesis and inactivating the NRF2-GPX4 antioxidant axis; localized hyperthermia further amplified Fenton catalytic efficiency.

ECM Degradation and Deep Tumor Penetration

The ECM, enriched with collagen, fibronectin, and hyaluronic acid, constitutes a dense physical barrier that impedes deep drug penetration.225 PTT-induced hyperthermia denatures ECM fibrous networks, reducing tissue stiffness and diffusion resistance while enhancing cell membrane fluidity for improved drug internalization.226,227 CDT-generated •OH nonspecifically oxidizes ECM macromolecules, further degrading thermally loosened matrix components.228 This thermal-chemical synergy increases tissue porosity and deepens drug penetration, facilitating the intratumoral accumulation of photothermal agents and catalysts from the tumor periphery toward hypoxic core regions.229 Yu et al,230 developed mesoporous polydopamine nanoparticles (M@B/R) co-loading bromelain and the immune adjuvant R848, wherein MPDA-mediated PTT achieved photothermal conversion while inducing immunogenic cell death and activating bromelain to degrade ECM and clear collagen barriers. Similarly, Li et al,231 constructed AHZ NPs mineralizing hyaluronidase and encapsulating Ag2S nanodots; hyaluronidase released in the acidic microenvironment specifically cleaved hyaluronic acid to loosen the dense ECM, enabling Ag2S to reach deep tumor tissues for NIR-II imaging-guided photothermal therapy.

Collectively, PTT-CDT synergy couples Arrhenius-accelerated Fenton kinetics, hyperthermia-driven H2O2/O2 supply, thermal-GSH depletion, and ECM degradation to overcome CDT bottlenecks. Nanosystem-mediated co-delivery remains essential to address agent biodistribution and thermal off-target effects.

Synergistic Mechanism of PDT-PTT-CDT

PTT and PDT, as non-invasive or minimally invasive optical modalities capable of modulating the TME, exhibit intrinsic synergistic mechanisms. PTT-induced hyperthermia transiently dilates tumor microvessels, improving perfusion to alleviate the hypoxic constraint on oxygen-dependent PDT. Mild thermal stress increases cell membrane fluidity, promoting photosensitizer internalization and deep penetration. Concurrently, PTT-driven thermal oxidation and PDT-generated ROS synergistically deplete GSH, dismantling the cellular antioxidant defense system,138 as illustrated in Figure 6. Synergistic Energy Complementarity

PTT and PDT strictly rely on exogenous light, and their efficacy decays rapidly once irradiation ceases. In contrast, CDT uses endogenous H2O2 to continuously generate •OH, sustaining oxidative suppression for hours post-irradiation.233 These modalities form a temporally complementary pattern. Acute PTT-PDT damage during irradiation plus sustained CDT suppression afterward. During irradiation, PTT-PDT disrupts membranes, denatures proteins, and damages DNA via hyperthermia and ROS. After light cessation, CDT catalyzes available H2O2 to produce •OH, preventing tumor cell repair and proliferation and blocking recurrence.234 Moreover, PTT-derived hyperthermia accelerates Fenton kinetics following the Arrhenius law.33 PDT-generated 1O2 oxidizes membrane lipids into LOOH. Under hyperthermia acceleration, Fe2+-catalyzed reduction of LOOH produces alkoxyl radicals to initiate lipid chain reactions, which continuously regenerate LOOH and construct a self-amplifying lipid peroxidation cycle with sufficient Fe2+ supplementation.235 Jiao et al constructed FeO@Cu-TCPP nanosheets wherein 660 nm excitation of Cu-porphyrin MOF generates 1O2 for PDT; critically, photoinduced electrons promote ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) to the FeO core, accelerating Fe2+/Fe3+ cycling and amplifying Fenton •OH production, establishing a light-driven electron relay that couples PDT with CDT. Concurrently, FeO photothermal conversion provides localized hyperthermia for direct ablation and further accelerates Fenton kinetics via the Arrhenius effect, achieving concurrent triple-modal synergy under single-wavelength excitation.236 In contrast, Wei et al,234 developed Cu-PCy JNPs implementing temporal sequencing to extend therapeutic duration: prior to irradiation, cyanine J-aggregates sterically shield Cu-porphyrin catalytic sites to suppress CDT; upon NIR exposure, PCy photodegradation sequentially dismantles J-aggregates, unmasking Cu-porphyrin to activate sustained •OH production for 48 hours post-irradiation. During illumination, Cu-porphyrin concurrently executes PDT and PTT for acute tumoricidal damage; following light withdrawal, activated CDT maintains continuous •OH flux, transitioning from acute PDT-PTT to prolonged CDT. These platforms exemplify complementary strategies-spatial integration via LMCT-mediated electron relay maximizing instantaneous ROS flux density, versus temporal programming via photodegradable J-aggregation maximizing cumulative ROS exposure duration-together overcoming the spatial and temporal limitations of conventional ROS therapies.

Microenvironment Remodeling

PTT-PDT cannot fundamentally overcome TME antioxidant barriers and substrate deficiencies.237 The trimodal combination remodels the microenvironment through three synergistic mechanisms. First, cooperative GSH depletion, where PTT promotes GSH to GSSG conversion via thermal oxidation,238 PDT-generated ROS oxidizes GSH thiol groups,239 and CDT consumes GSH through Fenton reactions, with these parallel pathways collectively dismantling antioxidant defenses.171 Second, H2O2 self-supply, where PTT-induced mild hyperthermia activates glucose oxidase to drive glucose decomposition, synchronously generating H2O2 and gluconic acid that supplement CDT substrates and acidify the TME, while improved oxygenation further elevates endogenous H2O2.240–242 Third, precise pH modulation, where PDT-driven glycolysis increases lactate accumulation, and gluconic acid generation synergistically shifts TME pH toward the Fenton-optimal range, triggering pH-responsive carrier disassembly and catalytic site exposure. The resulting •OH generation further aggravates local acidification, establishing a positive feedback loop that sustains efficient CDT.71,162,243 Liu et al,244 developed a thermoresponsive (MSNs@CaO2-ICG)@LA nanosystem based on a source expansion and expenditure reduction strategy. ICG-mediated photothermal effects elevate local temperature above the lauric acid melting point (44~46 °C), triggering shell disassembly to expose CaO2. CaO2 reacts with water to self-supply O2 and H2O2, relieving PDT hypoxia and providing substrates for Mn2+-mediated Fenton-like CDT. Concurrently, Mn2+ depletes GSH to reduce ROS scavenging, achieving bidirectional gains of O2/H2O2 self-supply and GSH depletion for self-reinforcing PDT-CDT synergy. Wang et al,245 developed Cu-Pt/MOF nanostructures, in which the Cu-Pt component enabled photothermal conversion for PTT and catalyzed H2O2 decomposition to generate •OH for CDT, while the photosensitized MOF framework supported H2O2-self-supplied PDT. This design overcame the substrate shortage of conventional CDT and realized triple-modal synergistic therapy with low dark toxicity and high light-triggered efficacy.

Damage Spectrum Broadening

Monotherapy readily activates adaptive defenses that limit efficacy. PTT induces HSP70 overexpression, enhancing thermal tolerance.246 PDT generates 1O2 with damage targets vulnerable to hypoxia-mediated resistance.247 CDT-produced •OH is readily scavenged by overexpressed GSH.248 The triple combination circumvents these limitations by constructing a synergistic killing network that spans multiple cell death modalities. For apoptosis, the three modalities converge on the mitochondrial pathway. PDT-generated 1O2 triggers cytochrome c release to activate caspase-9 and caspase-3. PTT disrupts mitochondrial membrane potential through thermal damage. CDT-generated •OH inflicts DNA oxidative damage that engages p53-mediated apoptotic signaling.134,150,249 For ferroptosis, cooperative oxidative stress amplifies lipid peroxidation. PDT-generated 1O2 directly oxidizes polyunsaturated fatty acids to initiate membrane damage. CDT propagates this lipid oxidation cascade. PTT-driven GSH depletion and thermal perturbation of lipid repair kinetics functionally compromises GPX4 activity, rendering tumor cells vulnerable to iron-dependent lipid peroxide accumulation.134,250 This parallel activation of multiple death pathways prevents adaptive escape through any single resistance mechanism.

Amplification of Immunogenic Effects

The PTT-PDT-CDT combination efficiently triggers ICD through coordinated DAMP release, including CRT externalization, HMGB1 secretion, and ATP efflux. These DAMPs collectively facilitate dendritic cell maturation and initiate T cell-driven antitumor immunity.251 PDT-derived ROS induces endoplasmic reticulum stress, promoting CRT translocation to the cell surface as an “eat me” signal. PTT disrupts membrane integrity to accelerate HMGB1 release.252,253 CDT-generated •OH oxidizes intracellular biomolecules and initiates ferroptotic lipid peroxidation, the resulting oxidized phospholipids act as novel DAMPs that potentiate ICD.254,255 Concurrently, CDT-mediated GSH depletion amplifies oxidative stress, further enhancing DAMP release. This multi-pathway immunostimulation constructs a self-amplifying antitumor cascade. Relevant mechanisms have been partially verified in dual-modal platforms. Li et al,202 demonstrated that endoplasmic reticulum-targeted PTT-PDT promotes CRT externalization and HMGB1 release, facilitating dendritic cell maturation and CD8+ T cell activation. Zhao et al,256 reported that localized PDT evokes systemic immunity via ICD, yielding abscopal effects. The incorporation of CDT is expected to further strengthen immune activation through exacerbated oxidative stress and ferroptotic lipid peroxidation, though systematic validation of triple-modal ICD enhancement remains insufficient.

Collectively, the PTT-PDT-CDT trimodal synergy integrates energy complementation, microenvironment remodeling, and multimodal cell death to overcome the inherent limitations of mono-modal and dual-modal therapies. The following sections will discuss how these mechanistic principles are realized in nanoplatform design, covering PDT-CDT and PTT-CDT dual-modal systems, followed by triple-modal architectures with auxiliary functionalities.

Multifunctional Nanomaterials for Enhancing CDT via Phototherapy (PDT/PTT)

Phototherapeutic enhancement of CDT relies on multifunctional nanoarchitectures that integrate photosensitizers or photothermal agents with Fenton-active catalysts within a single nanocarrier. Such nanosystems overcome biological delivery barriers through precise engineering, achieve tumor-selective accumulation, and respond to the TME for cascade therapeutic activation.

The current mainstream research strategy for combining phototherapy and CDT platforms can be classified into three main categories based on their structural and functional design principles: ① Multi-functional integration strategy: The Fenton catalyst and phototherapy drugs are encapsulated together in a single nanocarrier (such as metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), Prussian blue analogues, and metal sulfides), enabling simultaneous or sequential execution of both treatment methods. ② Intelligent strategy based on TME response: This design utilizes the inherent characteristics of tumor tissues, such as acidic pH, elevated GSH levels, and abundant endogenous H2O2, to achieve on-demand drug release, catalytic reaction activation, and degradation of the nanocarrier, representative systems include nanosheets based on MnO2 and pH-degradable MOFs. ③ Self-sustaining and cascading amplification strategy: These nanosystems introduce enzymatic units (such as glucose oxidase), generating hydrogen peroxide and gluconic acid in situ, thereby supplementing the substrate of the Fenton reaction and simultaneously making the TME acidic. Combined with components that deplete glutathione to disrupt the tumor antioxidant defense system, these strategies form a self-enhancing oxidative stress cycle. The subsequent sections will detail representative PDT-CDT, PTT-CDT, and multimodal nanotherapeutic systems, comprehensively demonstrating the above design paradigms.

PDT-CDT Synergistic Nanosystems

PDT-CDT synergistic nanosystems co-deliver photosensitizers and Fenton-active catalysts to achieve complementary ROS generation, substrate replenishment, and antioxidant defense disruption. The following examples illustrate how these mechanistic principles are translated into diverse nanostructures with integrated ROS amplification, GSH depletion, hypoxia modulation, and tumor-targeting functionalities.

As summarized in Table 2, representative PDT-CDT nanosystems have undergone stepwise structural and functional optimization to overcome the inherent limitations of monotherapy. Fe3O4@Cu-TCPP236 serves as a prototype photoelectron-mediated catalytic nanoplatform that integrates Fe-based Fenton catalysts with copper porphyrin photosensitizers. Photoinduced electrons generated from Cu-TCPP accelerate the redox cycling of iron ions and strengthen •OH production, enabling oxygen-independent synergistic PDT-CDT. To minimize reliance on exogenous substrates and construct self-circulating cascade therapeutic systems, Ce6/GOx@ZIF-8/PDA@MnO2.259 is engineered as a biomimetic cascade bioreactor. Collectively, this family of PDT-CDT nanosystems follows a progressive evolution from fundamental catalysis, through hypoxia optimization, TME-responsive design, and self-circulating cascades, to multimodal theranostics. PDT potentiates CDT via substrate provision and TME acidification, whereas CDT reversely sensitizes PDT by disabling antioxidant defense, offering hierarchical design strategies to overcome hypoxia-associated resistance and single-modal limitations. Despite the structural and functional sophistication of these nanosystems, several fundamental translational barriers remain inadequately addressed in current preclinical studies. First, tumor heterogeneity fundamentally compromises therapeutic consistency. Intratumoral and intertumoral variations in H2O2 concentration, pH, and GSH content create vastly different biochemical milieus that are rarely incorporated into nanoplatform design. Formulations optimized for high-H2O2, low-GSH tumor phenotypes often perform poorly under opposing microenvironmental conditions. However, most studies selectively employ tumor models that favor their specific nanocatalyst performance rather than validating efficacy across a spectrum of clinically relevant tumor phenotypes, yielding overly optimistic preclinical conclusions. Second, light penetration constraints are systematically underestimated. The vast majority of PDT-CDT nanosystems are validated in shallow subcutaneous tumor models with optical paths of only a few millimeters. Clinically relevant solid tumors-particularly pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and glioblastoma-typically exceed 3~5 cm in depth, where NIR-I light intensity attenuates to less than 10% of the incident dose. Although NIR-II materials offer advantages in deep-tissue penetration, their inherently lower quantum yields and photothermal conversion efficiencies substantially limit this advantage; quantitative evaluations of these critical performance trade-offs are rarely conducted in existing studies. Third, long-term metal-associated biosafety risks remain insufficiently characterized. Conventional biodistribution assessments typically terminate at 14~30 days post-injection, failing to capture chronic toxicities such as iron overload-induced systemic ferroptosis in healthy tissues, copper-mediated hepatotoxicity, and manganese-induced dysregulation of dopaminergic signaling. The absence of long-term toxicological data severely hampers clinical safety validation of metal-based PDT-CDT nanoplatforms. Fourth, poor batch-to-batch reproducibility constrains industrial translation. Reported synthesis protocols exhibit substantial inter-laboratory variability, with hydrodynamic diameter deviations exceeding 30%, polydispersity index values exceeding 0.25, and relative standard deviations of drug loading content exceeding 20%. While microfluidic synthesis has improved laboratory-scale uniformity, its scalability and operational stability at kilogram-scale industrial production remain unverified. Collectively, tumor heterogeneity, insufficient deep-tissue light penetration, uncharacterized chronic metal toxicity, and poor batch reproducibility constitute the four core bottlenecks restricting clinical translation of PDT-CDT nanotheranostic platforms.

Collectively, this family of PTT-CDT nanosystems has evolved from basic photothermal-catalytic coupling toward multimodal intelligent theranostics. PTT reinforces CDT by accelerating catalytic kinetics and remodeling the tumor microenvironment, while CDT reciprocally sensitizes tumors to photothermal ablation through GSH depletion and oxidative stress amplification. This synergistic interplay provides actionable design insights into overcoming thermoresistance, hypoxia-associated therapeutic resistance, and the inherent limitations of monomodal therapy. Beyond the common challenges shared with PDT-CDT platforms (tumor heterogeneity, light penetration, metal toxicity, and reproducibility), PTT-CDT systems face several modality-specific constraints. First, the thermal dose-efficacy trade-off remains poorly characterized. While mild hyperthermia (42~45 °C) accelerates Fenton kinetics via the Arrhenius effect, it simultaneously upregulates heat shock proteins (HSP70/90), thereby enhancing tumor thermotolerance and potentially promoting survival pathways that partially counteract CDT-mediated oxidative damage. Conversely, high-temperature PTT (>50 °C) achieves direct ablation but risks thermal injury to adjacent normal tissues and may induce inflammatory responses that paradoxically promote tumor recurrence. The optimal thermal window for PTT-CDT synergy has not been systematically established and likely varies with tumor type, catalyst system, and light delivery parameters. Second, the photothermal performance-degradability trade-off presents a critical design dilemma. Materials with the highest photothermal conversion efficiencies, such as noble metals, bismuth-based compounds, and carbon nanomaterials, are typically the least biodegradable, exhibiting prolonged retention in the reticuloendothelial system (liver, spleen, bone marrow) with poorly understood long-term toxicokinetics. In contrast, more biodegradable organic photothermal agents suffer from photobleaching and reduced stability, compromising their therapeutic windows. This trade-off is frequently acknowledged in introductory sections but rarely quantified or systematically addressed in experimental design. Third, thermal conduction profiles in three-dimensional tumor tissues differ markedly from those in monolayer cell cultures or small subcutaneous xenografts. Heat dissipation through heterogeneous vascular perfusion and tissue density substantially reduces the actual temperature rise at the tumor core, yet most studies report surface temperature measurements rather than intratumoral thermometry. Fourth, the accuracy of thermal imaging as a surrogate for therapeutic effect is questionable, as intratumoral temperature heterogeneity is rarely correlated with local CDT efficacy or cell death patterns. Collectively, these PTT-specific constraints, thermal-HSP trade-off, biodegradability-performance conflict, flawed thermometry, and superficial thermal monitoring, represent frequently overlooked failure modes that account for the marked discrepancies between in vitro and in vivo outcomes reported in the PTT-CDT literature.

PDT-PTT-CDT Tri-Modal Synergistic Nanosystems

Trimodal nanosystems integrate PDT, PTT, and CDT to combine oxygen-dependent ROS generation, NIR-triggered hyperthermia, and Fenton catalysis. PDT produces cytotoxic singlet oxygen, PTT accelerates catalytic kinetics and enhances tumor penetration, while CDT generates hydroxyl radicals and depletes GSH to dismantle antioxidant defense. The interworking of these three modalities achieves mutual substrate supplementation, TME optimization, and multi-dimensional oxidative-thermal synergism, effectively relieving hypoxia, reversing therapeutic resistance, and improving antitumor efficacy.

As summarized in the corresponding Table 4, representative PDT-PTT-CDT nanoconstructs follow a progressive optimization from simple composite loading to hierarchical structural design, TME responsiveness, and multi-metal cascade catalysis. FeWOx-PEG-RGD,286 establishes a minimalist trimodal paradigm. The FeWOx core provides intrinsic PTT, PDT under 980 nm irradiation, and Fe2+/W ion-mediated CDT; PEG-RGD surface modification enables tumor targeting and magnetic field-driven enrichment. TME-responsive degradation additionally permits T2/T1 MRI switch and CT imaging for therapeutic guidance. MIL@DOX@ICG,287 serves as a classic MOF-based trimodal prototype. The MIL framework simultaneously loads DOX and ICG, where ICG acts as both photosensitizer and photothermal agent, while MOF metal nodes provide Fenton-active sites to initiate CDT. NIR irradiation triggers synchronous PDT and PTT, and mild hyperthermia further accelerates CDT catalytic cycling, ultimately achieving prominent suppression of tumor proliferation and metastasis. Nevertheless, this simple MOF loading structure lacks active tumor-targeting capability, restricting enrichment efficiency at tumor lesions. To enhance tumor accumulation and further elevate therapeutic efficacy, FePtMn-Ce6/FA (FPMCF NPs),288 is elaborately designed with multi-metal components and folic acid modification. The FePtMn alloy endows the system with intrinsic photothermal performance and multi-metal Fenton-like activity, Ce6 mediates efficient PDT, and FA targeting enables precise tumor enrichment. In vitro and in vivo results confirm that this trimodal platform can effectively inhibit tumor growth and reduce systemic toxic side effects. Typical manganese-based nanoplatforms are represented by M-HMnO2@ICG NPs,289 and hollow MnO2. Manganese dioxide components serve as photothermal carriers and Mn sources for Fenton-like CDT, while loaded ICG synchronously supports PDT and PTT. Structurally optimized hollow MnO2,290 possesses higher drug loading capacity and TME-responsive degradability, capable of intelligent drug release and persistent GSH depletion, thereby remarkably improving long-term tumor suppression efficiency. Further optimized organic-inorganic hybrid nanosystems including DMTICH,291 Au2Pt-PEG-Ce6,292 and HCMN,293 integrate noble metal photothermal substrates, matched photosensitizers and multi-metal catalytic centers. The LSPR effect of noble metals guarantees high photothermal conversion efficiency, coordinated photosensitizers sustain adequate singlet oxygen output, and diversified metal sites drive cascade Fenton reaction. Such structural collaboration achieves efficient ablation of drug-resistant tumor cells and effectively inhibits local tumor recurrence. Novel chalcogenide and carbon-based nanoplatforms such as BDPL,294 BiNS-Fe@Fe,295 Co3S4-ICG,296 and CSNPs@Cu-BIF,297 broaden the construction strategies of trimodal synergy. Bismuth-based and transition metal chalcogenides exhibit outstanding NIR photothermal performance, while doped metal sites supply abundant CDT catalytic centers, and matched photosensitizers endow favorable PDT capability. These nanosystems display good biocompatibility and remarkable in vivo tumor inhibitory effect, laying a foundation for safe clinical translational application. Biomacromolecule encapsulated nanocarriers exemplified by HA-ICG-Fe-PDA,298 CoOOH-IR780@BSA (CoIRB),299 and UCFS@Fe-TA,300 adopt biocompatible shells such as PDA, BSA and tannic acid to improve physiological stability and immune evasion ability. The inner functional units integrate photothermal moieties, photosensitizers and Fenton-active metals to realize synchronous PDT-PTT-CDT. Benefiting from TME-responsive degradation and prolonged blood circulation, these platforms achieve enhanced tumor accumulation and superior antitumor efficacy with negligible damage to normal tissues. Cascade biocatalysis and nanozyme-based trimodal systems, including GOx@PCoS,301 PB@Cu2+/ZnP NPs,302 Pd1.7Bi@CeO2-ICG (PBCI),303 and SiO2@Cu7S4 NTs,304 introduce enzyme cascade and nanozyme catalytic modules. Such design realizes in-situ H2O2 self-supply and hypoxia relief, while photothermal components and photosensitizers support synchronous PTT and PDT, and multi-metal sites sustain persistent CDT. This self-circulating synergistic strategy significantly improves the utilization efficiency of therapeutic substrates and achieves thorough eradication of hypoxic tumors. Collectively, PDT-PTT-CDT trimodal nanosystems have matured from early composite prototypes toward sophisticated architectures integrating biomimetic encapsulation, TME-responsive intelligence, and cascade catalysis. The mutual reinforcement among these three modalities-oxygen-dependent ROS generation, spatiotemporally controllable hyperthermia, and self-sustaining Fenton catalysis-enables comprehensive dismantling of tumor defense mechanisms. Although the trimodal nano-systems have the broadest therapeutic spectrum, they also bring the highest complexity cost. This dimension has rarely been deeply evaluated in the literature. Firstly, the assumption of “the more, the better” lacks rigorous verification. Through the rational design of combination index analysis, it is usually difficult to quantify the incremental benefits brought by adding the third mode to the optimized bimodal therapy. In many cases, the surface advantages of the trimodal system may reflect an additive effect rather than a synergistic effect, especially when the selection of single drug treatment conditions is inappropriate and the synergistic index is artificially exaggerated. Secondly, as the number of functional components increases, the manufacturing complexity increases exponentially. Systems containing four or more components (such as photothermal agents, photosensitizers, Ferthen catalysts, targeting ligands, and usually also containing therapeutic drug carriers) face significant challenges in batch reproducibility, quality control, and regulatory certification. Just in terms of the burden of chemistry, manufacturing and control (CMC), most trimodal platforms are no longer commercially feasible, yet this is rarely discussed in academic publications. Thirdly, the multi-metal toxicity synergistic effect has largely not been explored. Combining iron, copper, manganese, cobalt or platinum in a single nano-system can trigger unknown interactions such as tissue distribution, metabolic interference, and chronic organ toxicity. To date, there have been few preclinical studies systematically evaluating this synergistic toxicity, that is, the organ damage caused by the combination of multiple metals exceeding the sum of their individual effects. Fourthly, the regulatory classification of trimodal nano-systems has significant problems. As a diagnostic and therapeutic mixed entity with multiple active components, they cannot follow the existing regulatory frameworks for small molecule drugs, biological products and medical devices, resulting in an extremely uncertain path for their clinical approval. In summary, these limitations lead to the fact that although trimodal platforms are scientifically attractive, they may face the most significant translational obstacles in all phototherapy-CDT systems. Looking to the future, integration with precise imaging, artificial intelligence-assisted design, and personalized treatment plans will be crucial in addressing these challenges and promoting the clinical translation of specific trimodal systems.

To conduct a comprehensive and systematic comparison of various nanosystems, we summarized in Table 5 the therapeutic modes, core advantages, and inherent limitations of these nanosystems. This comparative analysis has revealed the advantages and disadvantages in performance among different nano-system designs. Specifically, nanosystems based on metal-organic frameworks have high-density catalytic active sites, but are limited by poor colloidal stability and premature leakage of metal ions. Nanomaterials based on iron oxide exhibit excellent biocompatibility and simple synthesis processes, but their therapeutic effects are limited by pH-dependent catalytic activity and limited catalytic efficiency. The platform based on MnO2 can effectively remove intracellular GSH and achieve in situ oxygen replenishment to alleviate tumor hypoxia, but its clinical translation is constrained by potential neurotoxicity and complex synthesis processes. Prussian blue analogues possess inherent photothermal properties and bimetallic Fischer-like catalytic activity, but their non-degradability and long-term retention in RES have raised ongoing bio-safety concerns. Polyamine-based nanosystems have excellent biocompatibility and multifunctional surface functionalization capabilities, but their low photothermal conversion efficiency and potential non-expected ROS clearance effect have been a persistent concern. Noble metal nanomaterials can achieve high photothermal conversion efficiency (PCE) and good structural/function stability, but their wide application is limited by high costs and slow in vivo clearance kinetics. Sulfur-based chalcogenides based on bismuth can achieve deep tissue penetration in the NIR-II biological window, but also present risks of heavy metal toxicity. Carbon-based nanosystems are cost-effective and have excellent photostability, but suffer from structural heterogeneity and poor batch-to-batch reproducibility. Hybrid organic-inorganic nanocomposites can achieve multifunctional integration for synergistic treatment, but their clinical translation is constrained by extremely complex manufacturing processes and strict regulatory challenges. Multimetallic alloys exhibit synergistic catalytic performance and integrated imaging and treatment functions, but their degradation behavior in vivo and comprehensive biological toxicity have not been fully elucidated. In summary, this review indicates that no single nanosystem can achieve universal advantages in tumor treatment. The selection of the best nanosystem should be personalized based on specific tumor phenotypes, treatment plans, and clinical translation requirements. Limitations and Future Prospects

Current Technical and Translational Challenges

Although nanoplatforms integrating phototherapy and chemodynamic therapy have achieved remarkable preclinical progress, multiple translational bottlenecks persist. Most photosensitizers and photothermal agents are confined to the NIR-I window, where tissue penetration remains limited to 1~2 cm and significant photon scattering compromises efficacy in deep-seated or large-volume tumors. While NIR-II materials offer superior penetration and maximum permissible exposure, their synthesis often involves complex multi-step procedures, low quantum yields, and batch-to-batch variability that impedes scale-up.284,305–307 Moreover, the tumor microenvironment exhibits substantial inter-tumoral and intra-tumoral heterogeneity in H2O2 levels, pH, and GSH content, leading to unpredictable catalytic efficiency and inconsistent therapeutic outcomes across different cancer models. Patient-specific TME profiles are rarely accounted for in current nanoplatform designs.308–310

In vivo delivery remains a critical hurdle. Despite the EPR effect, actual tumor accumulation of nanocarriers typically falls below 5% of the injected dose, with the majority sequestered by the reticuloendothelial system (liver, spleen, bone marrow). Dense extracellular matrix, elevated interstitial fluid pressure, and abnormal tumor vasculature further restrict deep penetration, leaving hypoxic core regions undertreated.311 Most reported studies rely on small subcutaneous xenograft models with rapid growth kinetics and leaky vasculature, which poorly recapitulate the desmoplastic stroma, immunosuppressive milieu, and metabolic gradients of human solid tumors-particularly pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and glioblastoma.312

Biosafety concerns are equally pressing. Inorganic metal-based catalysts (Fe, Cu, Mn, Co) often exhibit slow biodegradation and prolonged retention in RES organs, with poorly understood long-term toxicokinetics. Metal ion leaching can disrupt systemic iron homeostasis or induce oxidative stress in healthy tissues. Organic photosensitizers, while more degradable, suffer from photobleaching, aggregation-caused quenching, and rapid renal/hepatic clearance that compromise therapeutic windows.313–317 Yet the long-term biological fate of these nanoparticles, spanning degradation kinetics, metabolic pathways, and chronic organ toxicity, remains conspicuously understudied. Systematic toxicology assessments over 6 to 12 months, which are mandatory for assessing risks of chronic inflammation, organ fibrosis, and secondary malignancies, are rarely conducted. The lack of standardized GMP manufacturing protocols, uniform physicochemical characterization criteria, and consensus preclinical evaluation pipelines has produced fragmented data that regulatory agencies find difficult to evaluate.318–320

The controllable, repeatable and scalable production of nanoparticles is a key obstacle for clinical translation.321 Although microfluidic or continuous flow synthesis techniques can achieve better batch-to-batch reproducibility compared to traditional methods, when scaling up milligram-level laboratory discoveries to kilogram-level industrial production, maintaining consistency in particle size distribution, surface chemistry and drug loading remains a significant technical challenge.322,323 Additionally, multiple engineering bottlenecks also hinder clinical translation, including insufficient purification efficiency, poor endotoxin removal capability, poor sterilization compatibility and unstable long-term storage performance.324 In fact, CMC issues are one of the main reasons for the failure of nanomedicine research.325 The high cost of clinical trials further exacerbates these challenges, limiting the majority of nanotechnology platforms derived from academic research from entering clinical applications.326

The regulatory ecosystem poses equally formidable obstacles. Nanotheranostics intrinsically bridge diagnostic and therapeutic functions, whose dual nature complicates official classification and renders traditional regulatory frameworks for small-molecule drugs and biologics inapplicable.327,328 While the FDA released specific guidance for nanomaterial-containing pharmaceuticals in 2022 and the EMA has published reflection papers on nanotechnology-based medicinal products,329 nanomedicine evaluation still follows a case-by-case model in both regulatory systems.330 There is a lack of specialized guidelines tailored to their unique physicochemical properties and sophisticated pharmacokinetic behaviors. Inconsistent regulatory standards across jurisdictions further fragment translational pathways, introducing substantial uncertainty that discourages industrial investment and hinders large-scale clinical transformation.331,332

Most notably, phototherapy-CDT combinatorial nanosystems remain at a very immature translational stage. To date, no PDT-CDT, PTT-CDT, or trimodal synergistic nanoplatforms have advanced into clinical trials for cancer treatment.333,334 Clinically approved phototherapeutic nanomedicines are limited to first-generation photosensitizers (Photofrin®) and a small set of PTT agents with narrow clinical applicability, none of which incorporate CDT functionality.335 This striking discrepancy between the rapidly expanding volume of preclinical studies and the total absence of clinical candidates exposes a core translational mismatch. Academic research prioritizes synthetic innovation, mechanistic exploration, and murine-based proof-of-concept efficacy, whereas clinical translation requires scalable manufacturing, standardized safety evaluation, economic feasibility, and clear regulatory validation. Narrowing this persistent gap requires not only moderate optimization of material design but also a fundamental shift in research priorities, placing greater emphasis on translational endpoints that are currently overlooked in conventional academic research.

Future Development Directions

Construction of NIR-II Deep-Penetration Intelligent Nanoplatforms

The clinical translation of NIR-II phototherapy requires addressing material stability and cost-effectiveness alongside penetration depth. Emerging candidates such as donor-acceptor structured organic semiconductors, rare-earth doped downconversion nanoparticles, and tailored carbonaceous nanomaterials offer tunable absorption in the 1000~1400 nm range. However, challenges remain: rare-earth materials suffer from low absorption cross-sections and potential heavy metal toxicity; organic NIR-II dyes often display poor photostability and complex synthetic routes. Future efforts should prioritize structurally simple, metabolically clearable NIR-II chromophores with validated photothermal conversion efficiencies (>40%) under clinically relevant laser power densities (<1 W cm−2). Integration with photoacoustic or NIR-II fluorescence imaging can enable real-time temperature monitoring and treatment boundary delineation, though spatial resolution-penetration depth trade-offs need optimization.336,337

Exploitation of Degradable Metal-Free or Low-Toxicity Catalytic Systems

To mitigate metal accumulation risks, researchers are exploring metal-free nanozymes such as carbon dots,338,339 graphitic carbon nitride,340 covalent organic frameworks,341 with intrinsic peroxidase-like activity342 and biomineralized metal-organic complexes with programmed degradation kinetics.316 However, the catalytic efficiency of metal-free systems typically lags behind Fenton-active metals by one to two orders of magnitude, and their catalytic mechanisms under physiologically relevant conditions remain incompletely characterized. Alternatively, biodegradable iron-organic frameworks enable sustained Fe2+ release followed by metabolic clearance, but controlling degradation rates to match tumor residence times requires precise compositional tuning.343 Biomimetic strategies,344 such as erythrocyte membrane coating, exosome camouflage or tumor-homing peptide display, can improve circulation half-lives and reduce immunogenicity, yet batch consistency and immune evasion durability in human subjects are still under investigation.

Optimization of Trimodal Synergy Combined with Immunotherapy

PDT-PTT-CDT multimodality systems can induce immunogenic cell death, but the resulting antitumor immunity is often transient and insufficient to prevent metastasis.345 Rational combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors such as anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, and anti-CTLA-4 requires careful sequencing.346 Phototherapy-induced acute inflammation may initially upregulate PD-L1, creating a therapeutic window for checkpoint blockade. Co-delivery of immune adjuvants including CpG oligonucleotides and STING agonists within the same nanocarrier could potentiate dendritic cell maturation, but systemic cytokine release syndrome risks must be rigorously assessed.347 Moreover, the abscopal effect regression of non-irradiated metastatic lesions, has been sporadically reported in preclinical models but rarely reproduced robustly.348 Mechanistic understanding of how localized nanotherapy triggers systemic immunity is still incomplete.349

AI-Assisted Imaging-Guided Precision Theranostics

Multimodal imaging integrated nanoplatforms enable treatment planning and response monitoring, but current studies rarely exploit the full potential of artificial intelligence. Multimodal imaging integrated nanoplatforms enable treatment planning and response monitoring, but current studies rarely exploit the full potential of artificial intelligence.350 However, training such models requires large, standardized, multi-center datasets that are currently unavailable.351 The integration of artificial intelligence with medical imaging has enabled full-process intelligent innovation in oncological surgery, spanning preoperative planning, intraoperative navigation, and postoperative evaluation.352 Regulatory approval pathways for AI-nanomedicine combination devices are still evolving.353

Establishment of Standardized Clinical Translation Systems

The gap between academic prototypes and clinical products demands systematic attention to manufacturing and quality control.354 Microfluidic or continuous-flow synthesis offers superior batch reproducibility compared to conventional flask-based methods, yet scale-up from milligram to gram quantities while maintaining uniform size, surface chemistry, and drug loading remains technically demanding.355 Standardized protocols for physicochemical characterization, including dynamic light scattering, transmission electron microscopy, and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry for metal content, along with sterility assurance and endotoxin testing, must be harmonized across laboratories.356 Preclinical evaluation should incorporate orthotopic tumor models, patient-derived xenografts, and genetically engineered mouse models with intact immune systems to better predict human efficacy. Long-term toxicology studies of six to twelve months assessing chronic inflammation, organ fibrosis, and secondary malignancy risks are essential but rarely reported.357 Finally, regulatory frameworks specific to nanotheranostics addressing the dual diagnostic and therapeutic classification, combination product rules, and adaptive trial designs need international harmonization to accelerate bench-to-bedside translation.358

Conclusion

This review systematically integrates the multi-level synergistic mechanisms of PDT/PTT-enhanced CDT with the evolutionary design logic of nanoplatforms. Through this mechanism-guided developmental perspective, we have traced the field from simple thermochemical coupling to TME-responsive intelligent delivery, enzyme-cascade self-sustaining systems, and integrated multimodal theranostic platforms, offering a coherent roadmap that bridges mechanistic understanding with rational nanoplatform design.

On the basis of currently available preclinical evidence, nanomaterial-based phototherapy has proven effective in compensating for the inherent defects of monotherapy CDT. Restricted by insufficient H2O2, mild acidity, and high GSH levels, single-modal CDT exhibits unsatisfactory antitumor efficacy, as consistently demonstrated in murine xenograft models. PDT and PTT achieve favorable TME remodeling and spatiotemporally controllable oxidative damage. Dual-modal and triple-modal synergistic platforms realize cascaded ROS generation, GSH depletion, and immune activation: PTT accelerates Fenton kinetics via hyperthermia, PDT supplements H2O2 substrates, and CDT produces persistent hydroxyl radicals, jointly achieving superior synergistic antitumor performance in preclinical settings. Multifunctional carriers, including metal-organic frameworks and biomimetic nanoparticles, have achieved intelligent TME-responsive delivery as validated in numerous in vivo studies.

Looking forward, several directions remain speculative and require further investigation. The integration of NIR-II materials, degradable metal-free or low-toxicity catalytic systems, AI-assisted imaging guidance, and combination with immunotherapy holds theoretical promise for optimizing synergistic nanoplatforms. However, the actual efficacy of these emerging strategies in human patients has yet to be established, and their translational potential depends critically on overcoming the current limitations in material stability, catalytic efficiency under physiological conditions, and scalability of manufacturing. While combined immunotherapy may inhibit tumor metastasis and recurrence, the abscopal effect has been sporadically observed in preclinical models but requires rigorous validation in clinical cohorts.

To facilitate successful clinical translation, three priorities must be addressed as an immediate next step: ① establishing standardized GMP-compliant manufacturing protocols and unified physicochemical characterization criteria to ensure batch-to-batch consistency; ② replacing subcutaneous xenograft models with orthotopic tumors, patient-derived xenografts, and genetically engineered mouse models that better recapitulate human disease, alongside long-term toxicology studies spanning 6~12 months to assess chronic inflammation, organ fibrosis, and secondary malignancy risks; ③ advancing regulatory harmonization for nanotheranostic combination products, including clear classification pathways and adaptive trial designs. Only through coordinated efforts across materials science, oncology, and regulatory science can phototherapy-augmented CDT nanosystems transition from promising preclinical candidates to reliable clinical tools for precision cancer treatment.
