Effect of Surgery Within 48 hours on Early Balance, Gait, and Fall Risk in Elderly Intertrochanteric Fracture Patients
Authors: Parinthorn Triamsasithorn, Chavarin Amarase, Napol Ratanasermsub, Sanzhar Artykbay, Saran Tantavisut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Intertrochanteric femoral fractures in elderly patients are linked to impaired mobility, increased fall risk, and high mortality. Early surgical intervention within 48 hours is recommended to reduce complications, but its effect on gait and balance is unclear. This study evaluated the impact of early versus delayed surgery on gait, balance, and clinical outcomes in elderly patients treated with cephalomedullary nails.Methods:We retrospectively analyzed 193 patients aged ≥60 years with closed intertrochanteric fractures treated between January 2018 and January 2025. Patients were categorized as early (≤48 hours, n = 111) or delayed (>48 hours, n = 82) surgery. Gait and balance at 3 months postoperatively were assessed using the timed up and go test, five times sit-to-stand test, and functional reach test. Secondary outcomes included hospital stay, mortality, complications, and functional scores (Harris hip score, de Morton mobility index, Barthel index).Results:Early surgery was associated with markedly better five times sit-to-stand performance (34.5 ± 9.8 vs. 45.0 ± 11.2 seconds; P = 0.007) and lower mortality (5.4% vs. 14.6%; P = 0.029). Trends toward improved timed up and go (40.6 ± 12.3 vs. 59.4 ± 15.7 seconds; P = 0.100) and functional reach test (17.2 ± 4.5 vs. 13.6 ± 3.9 cm; P = 0.115) were observed. Complication rates (11.7% vs. 15.9%; P = 0.405) and other functional scores were similar. Both groups remained at high fall risk per gait and balance assessments.Conclusion:Surgery within 48 hours improves lower-limb function and reduces mortality in elderly patients with intertrochanteric fractures, but persistent fall risk highlights the need for postoperative fall prevention strategies.

Intertrochanteric femoral fractures are common among the elderly and are associated with substantial morbidity, impaired mobility, increased risk of falls, and high short-term and long-term mortality rates.1,2 Surgical fixation is the standard of care, with cephalomedullary nailing widely accepted as the preferred method due to its biomechanical stability and minimally invasive nature.3 International guidelines, including those from the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, recommend early surgical intervention within 48 hours of admission to reduce complications, such as pressure ulcers, pneumonia, thromboembolism, and mortality.4,5 However, although early surgery has been associated with improved survival and reduced perioperative risks, its influence on functional recovery—particularly on gait and balance—remains insufficiently explored.6,7

The existing literature primarily focuses on mortality and hospital-related outcomes following early versus delayed surgery, with limited evidence regarding gait and balance, which can be assessed using methods such as the timed up and go (TUG) test, the five times sit-to-stand (FTSTS) test, and the functional reach test (FRT).8,9 Although some studies suggest improved early mobility with timely surgery, others report no notable differences in postoperative functional scores, contributing to ongoing debate.10,11 Given the elevated fall risk and prolonged recovery in elderly patients with intertrochanteric fractures, understanding the impact of surgical timing on postacute gait and balance performance is essential for optimizing rehabilitation strategies and long-term outcomes.

This study addresses this gap by evaluating the differences in postoperative gait, balance, and clinical outcomes between early and delayed surgery in elderly patients with intertrochanteric fractures treated with cephalomedullary nail fixation.

Methods

This single-center, retrospective, observational study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.12 It included patients diagnosed with trochanteric fractures who were admitted and surgically treated between January 1, 2018, and January 31, 2025. All patients underwent closed reduction and internal fixation (CRIF) using a cephalomedullary nail. Data were extracted from the institution's electronic medical record system. Follow-up was conducted for 3 months postoperatively, assessing clinical and functional outcomes. The study was approved by the institutional review board (No. 0237/65).

Participants

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosed with closed intertrochanteric femoral fractures; (2) treated with CRIF using a cephalomedullary nail; (3) aged 60 years or older at the time of injury; (4) sustained the fracture due to a low-energy mechanism (eg, a fall from standing height); and (5) had complete clinical and radiographic follow-up data available at 3 months postoperatively.

Exclusion criteria included the following: (1) polytrauma or concomitant fractures at other anatomical sites; (2) a history of surgery on the affected hip; and (3) prefracture inability to ambulate or communicate effectively.

Participants were identified through a retrospective review of institutional electronic medical records, using diagnostic and procedural codes for trochanteric fractures and internal fixation. Eligible patients were categorized into two groups based on the time from fracture occurrence to surgical intervention:The early group included patients who underwent CRIF within 48 hours of the fracture.The delayed group included those who underwent surgery more than 48 hours after the fracture.

All procedures were performed by two senior orthopedic trauma surgeons with extensive experience in hip fracture surgery, using a standardized surgical technique. The short proximal femoral nail antirotation (PFNA II; DePuy Synthes) was used in all cases. Postoperative care was standardized across both groups and included uniform pain management, deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis, and rehabilitation protocols. Rehabilitation was structured and progressive, with early mobilization initiated on the first postoperative day according to individual patient tolerance. This included range of motion exercises, assisted wheelchair ambulation, and gradual weight-bearing with a gait aid. Physical therapy sessions were conducted daily during hospitalization and focused on improving strength, balance, and mobility. Fracture union was assessed radiographically during follow-up visits by an orthopedic surgeon and a musculoskeletal radiologist. This evaluation was based on the presence of bridging callus across at least three cortices, as observed on standard anterior-posterior and lateral radiographs.

Variables

The primary outcome was postoperative gait and balance, assessed using the TUG test at 3 months following surgery. This test captures the time (in seconds) it takes for a patient to rise from a chair, walk 3 m, turn around, return, and sit down. Faster performance reflects better mobility and a lower risk of falls.13,14

Secondary outcomes encompassed a range of validated functional assessments to capture strength, balance, hip function, mobility, and independence:The FTSTS test evaluates lower limb power and functional mobility by measuring the time needed to stand from a chair five times without using the arms. Prolonged times are indicative of impaired strength and balance, and a higher risk of falling.15The FRT assesses dynamic balance by recording the furthest distance a person can reach forward while standing without losing stability. A reach of less than 15 cm suggests a notable risk of falls.16The Harris hip score (HHS) measures hip-specific function, incorporating domains such as pain, mobility, and range of motion. It yields a score from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating superior function; scores ≥90 are generally interpreted as excellent.17The de Morton mobility index (DEMMI) captures overall mobility in older adults, covering activities ranging from bed mobility and sit-to-stand transitions to ambulation and balance tasks. Scores range from 0 to 100, with higher values denoting better functional status.18The Barthel index assesses independence in activities of daily living, including feeding, dressing, bathing, toileting, and ambulation. A score of 100 reflects full independence, whereas lower scores indicate varying levels of dependency.19

Mortality, length of hospital stay (LOS), and complications, including infection, hyponatremia, heart failure, hematuria, gastrointestinal bleeding, delirium, nephritis, diabetic ketoacidosis, stroke, and heart attack, were assessed at 3 months of follow-up.

All outcome measures were recorded at 3 months postoperatively. Functional recovery was then compared between patients who underwent early surgery (within 48 hours of fracture) and those with delayed intervention to evaluate the impact of surgical timing on postoperative performance following CRIF in elderly patients with intertrochanteric fractures.

Data Sources

Patient data were obtained from the institutional electronic medical records. Individuals admitted between January 1, 2018, and January 31, 2025, with a diagnosis of intertrochanteric femur fracture (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision code S72.14) were identified using the Hospital Information System. Radiographic evaluation of fractures were conducted through the Picture Archiving and Communication System20 verified by experience orthopedic trauma surgeons.

Demographic and clinical variables—including age, sex, height, weight, body mass index, comorbidities, time to surgery, type of fixation, LOS, complications, and in-hospital mortality—were extracted directly from inpatient records, surgical notes, and discharge summaries.

Functional outcome measures were collected at the 3-month, postoperative, follow-up visit. Standardized assessments were conducted in person by trained research assistant using validated instruments. Each test was administered according to published protocols and scoring guidelines. The setting and conditions for each assessment were standardized to minimize measurement variability and ensure reliability across participants.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted with Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software, version 26.0 (IBM). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data: categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, whereas continuous variables were expressed as mean values with corresponding SDs. The distribution of continuous data was evaluated through the Shapiro-Wilk test alongside histogram visualization. Group comparisons for categorical variables were done using the Chi-square test, whereas differences in continuous outcomes between the groups were analyzed with independent samples t-tests. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed P value of less than 0.05.21 Cases with incomplete data at the 3-month follow-up were excluded from the analysis using a complete case approach.

Results

A total of 193 patients were included in this retrospective study, with 111 patients in the early surgery group (surgery within 48 hours) and 82 patients in the delayed surgery group (surgery after 48 hours; Figure 1). Demographic characteristics were comparable between the early and delayed surgery groups. The mean age was 82.3 years (SD 10.3) for the early group and 82.2 years (SD 9.4) for the delayed group (P = 0.223). The sex distribution was similar, with 72.0% of females in the early group and 71.9% in the delayed group (P = 0.985). No notable differences were observed in weight, height, body mass index, or median follow-up time between the two groups (Table 1).

Discussion

Early surgical intervention within 48 hours for intertrochanteric femoral fractures is widely recommended, as it reduces complications and mortality rates.22 This retrospective study evaluated the impact of surgical timing on gait, balance, and clinical outcomes in elderly patients treated with cephalomedullary nail fixation, with follow-up at 3 months postoperatively.

The 3-month follow-up period was chosen to assess early functional outcomes while ensuring fracture stabilization and sufficient callus formation for full weight-bearing. This timeframe allowed reliable assessment of gait and balance, which are critical for evaluating fall risk and functional recovery. The effect of early and delay surgery would mainly affect gait and balance at the early phase after surgery and not carry over for too long during the long-term follow-up. In addition, fall risk is greatest during this early period after surgery compare with later phase that patient start to regain their strength closer to preinjury level. Gait and balance were measured using validated tools: the TUG, the FTSTS, and the FRT. Previous studies have established thresholds for fall risk: TUG scores exceeding 12 to 15 seconds indicate a 3.74-fold higher fall risk,23-25 FTSTS scores above 15 seconds suggest a twofold increase in recurrent falls,26 and FRT distances below 25 cm are associated with a 3.71-fold higher fall risk.27

At 3 months postoperatively, patients in the early surgery group (surgery within 48 hours) showed better performance across all three assessments compared with the delayed surgery group (surgery after 48 hours). The FTSTS demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in the Early group (34.5 ± 9.8 vs. 45.0 ± 11.2 seconds; P = 0.007), whereas TUG (40.6 ± 12.3 vs. 59.4 ± 15.7 seconds; P = 0.100) and FRT (17.2 ± 4.5 vs. 13.6 ± 3.9 cm; P = 0.115) showed trends toward better outcomes without reaching statistical significance. These findings suggest that early surgery may enhance gait and balance, potentially reducing fall risk. However, both groups had mean TUG and FTSTS times above 15 seconds and FRT distances below 25 cm, indicating a persistently high fall risk regardless of surgical timing. This underscores the need for comprehensive fall prevention strategies in all patients undergoing cephalomedullary nail fixation.

The early surgery group also had a markedly lower mortality rate (5.4% vs. 14.6%; P = 0.029), consistent with a meta-analysis reporting a 45% reduction in 1-year mortality rate with early surgery.6 However, no notable differences were observed in other functional outcomes (HHS, DEMMI, Barthel Index) or complication rates (11.7% vs. 15.9%; P = 0.405), suggesting that surgical timing may have a limited impact on broader functional recovery or complications within the studied timeframe.

This study has several strengths. It focuses on understudied metrics by evaluating gait and balance using validated measures (TUG, FTSTS, FRT), addressing a critical gap in the literature, which often emphasizes mortality and hospital-related outcomes. It includes a homogeneous population, with only elderly patients with closed intertrochanteric fractures treated using a consistent surgical method (cephalomedullary nail), minimizing variability in fracture type and surgical technique and enhancing the reliability of comparisons. The use of objective, validated functional measures provided clinically meaningful insights into postoperative gait, balance, and fall risk. By examining the effect of early versus delayed surgery on these functional outcomes, the study contributes novel data to an area that remains underexplored.

Limitations include potential confounding factors affecting surgical timing. This study has several limitations. Potential selection bias exists, as patients in the delayed surgery group may have had more preexisting comorbidities or abnormal laboratory results that necessitated the surgical delay, which could independently contribute to poorer outcomes. Short follow-up period is another limitation, as the 3-month follow-up may not capture longer-term differences in functional recovery, fall risk, or mortality. In addition, the retrospective nature of the study relies on existing medical records, which may have inherent gaps or inconsistencies compared with a prospective trial. These factors could influence outcomes and introduce bias. Future studies should address these limitations by accounting for confounding factors, including longer follow-up periods, and ideally using prospective study designs to assess the durability of early surgery's benefits.

This retrospective study revealed that early surgery within 48 hours for elderly patients with intertrochanteric fractures is associated with improved FTSTS performance and lower mortality rates at 3 months postoperatively, with trends toward better TUG and FRT outcomes. These findings support the existing literature advocating early intervention.6,22 However, the persistently high fall risk in both groups underscores the need for comprehensive, multidisciplinary fall prevention programs as an integral part of postoperative care. Such programs should include balance and strength training, gait and functional mobility exercises, and home safety assessments to reduce the risk of falls, regardless of surgical timing.

Conclusion

Early surgery within 48 hours for elderly patients with intertrochanteric fractures was associated with improved FTSTS performance and lower short-term mortality. Although TUG and FRT showed favorable trends, fall risk remained high in both groups. These findings suggest potential benefits of early surgical intervention and emphasize the importance of comprehensive postoperative rehabilitation, but conclusions should be interpreted cautiously due to the short follow-up period of 3 months.
