The term “polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome” (PMOS) recently replaced “polycystic ovary syndrome” (PCOS) following a 14-year international consensus-building process that saw broad engagement with patients, clinicians, and professional organizations through multiple steps.1 Why does the new name matter, and what does the change mean for clinical practice?

The old name, PCOS, was misleading, distorting how patients and clinicians understood a common, lifelong condition. The term suggests that ovarian cysts are the defining problem, that ultrasonography is central to diagnosis, and that reproductive dysfunction is the main concern. Each implication is incomplete. The cysts are follicles that have failed to respond appropriately to hormone signalling. Ultrasonography is neither required for diagnosis in many adults nor appropriate for diagnosis in adolescents. Most importantly, ovarian dysfunction is one manifestation of the condition, not the whole condition, and not necessarily its primary driver. A rigorous 2023 international guideline recommended a lifespan approach that includes reproductive, metabolic, cardiovascular, dermatologic, sleep, and psychological health.2

The new name, PMOS, better reflects that evidence. This reframing makes a difference in the consultation room, making the first conversation with patients more biologically accurate, less fertility centred, and more aligned with prevention of lifelong insulin resistance and metabolic disease. The first explanation a patient receives often becomes the framework through which they understand their body. When clinicians say, “You have cysts on your ovaries,” patients may reasonably conclude that the condition is local, structural, and mainly relevant to fertility. Use of “PMOS” allows a more accurate opening: “This is a hormone and metabolic condition that can affect ovulation, androgen levels, skin, weight, insulin resistance, pregnancy risk, and long-term cardiometabolic health.” That explanation does not erase the ovary; it places ovarian function in context.

The metabolic language is especially important. Insulin resistance is not a secondary inconvenience or a concern only for patients in larger bodies. A systematic review and meta-analysis of euglycemic–hyperinsulinemic clamp studies found reduced insulin sensitivity in patients with PMOS, independent of body mass index.3 This evidence supports baseline metabolic screening at diagnosis, followed by periodic reassessment of cardiometabolic risk factors such as glucose tolerance, blood pressure, and lipid levels. It also emphasizes early lifestyle intervention and ongoing follow-up every 1 to 3 years, depending on individual risk.2 The name PMOS may make patients more receptive to early discussions about metabolic health, because those discussions are framed as intrinsic to the syndrome rather than as separate or secondary concerns.

Using accurate and complete terminology particularly matters for adolescent patients. In early gynecologic life, irregular cycles, ovarian cysts, and acne are common, and overdiagnosis using the previous terminology of PCOS can cause harm, including unnecessary labelling, anxiety about fertility and long-term health, inappropriate exposure to hormonal treatments, and the risk of medicalizing normal pubertal variation.2 However, underrecognition also has consequences when patients are given only reassurance, cosmetic treatment, or cycle suppression, while risks related to insulin resistance, weight trajectory, disordered eating, sleep problems, or depression remain unaddressed. Adolescent-specific recommendations, based on the 2023 guideline, advise that diagnosis requires both irregular menstrual cycles, defined by age-appropriate thresholds for time since menarche, and clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism after exclusion of mimicking disorders such as thyroid dysfunction, nonclassical congenital adrenal hyperplasia, and hypothalamic amenorrhea.2 Ultrasonography and testing of anti-Müllerian hormone should not be used diagnostically in adolescent patients because of poor specificity.4 These recommendations are careful, but they are not passive. They support early identification of those at risk, attention to mental health and metabolic features, and shared planning across the lifespan.

The name change may have its greatest effect in focusing treatment decisions. Combined oral contraceptive pills can be useful for menstrual irregularity, acne, and hirsutism, but they should not constitute the whole management plan. Although a teenager whose cycles become regular when taking the oral contraceptive pill may appear “treated,” the term PMOS reminds clinicians that menstrual management is symptom management, not disease modification, making it harder to reduce care to ovarian suppression.

In a 2017 international survey study, many patients with (formerly) PCOS reported delayed diagnosis and inadequate information. 5 A name cannot be said to have caused these experiences, but the findings demonstrate a communication failure that terminology may have reinforced. Consistent with patient experience studies describing delayed diagnosis, inconsistent explanations, and inadequate counselling about long-term health risks, patients may be told in clinical practice that they do or do not have the condition based on ovarian appearance, despite having clear hyperandrogenic and metabolic features, or reassured that the diagnosis matters only if they want pregnancy. 5,6 A better name cannot fix fragmented care, but it may reduce the likelihood that patients receive a narrow or inaccurate explanation.

Changing the name of any condition risks confusion, however. During the transition, clinicians should use both terms when talking to patients about the condition: “PMOS, formerly PCOS.” They should explain that previous diagnoses remain valid and that the diagnostic criteria have not changed. Using the word “metabolic” also requires care. Patients may hear it as a coded reference to weight, so clinicians should be careful to explain that metabolic risk is a biological feature of the syndrome and that surveillance, support, and prevention of weight gain and cardiometabolic disease are beneficial, without moral judgment.

The value of the new name will depend on whether clinicians use it to change the clinical conversation to explain ovarian dysfunction as one feature of a broader endocrine–metabolic syndrome, to discuss metabolic risk earlier, and to ensure that symptom management is paired with attention to long-term health. Polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome is a better name because it begins with the whole patient.
