Revealing the risk of macroplastic ingestion to marine wildlife
Authors: Matthew S. Savoca
Published: 2025

The 1950s were a transformative decade for science and engineering: The polio vaccine was introduced, the double-helix structure of DNA was unraveled, and the first commercial computer was introduced. It also saw the rapid proliferation of consumer plastics, ushering in our “plastic age” (1). Within a decade, seabirds were already sounding the alarm on an emerging plastic pollution crisis in the ocean (2–4). Sixty years later, the signal has become deafening: roughly 1,500 species consume plastic waste, from zooplankton to whales (5). In some cases, this ingestion proves fatal. Given the intense public and research attention surrounding plastic pollution, it might reasonably be assumed that we know how much plastic is lethal to consume, or how often its ingestion leads to death at a population level. Instead, our knowledge is surprisingly meager. In PNAS, Murphy et al. (6) address these gaps by developing a cross-taxa risk model that identifies thresholds at which macroplastic ingestion is likely to become lethal. To do so, they compiled a global dataset from more than 10,000 necropsy reports, revealing patterns that clarify not only how much plastic is deadly but also how often wildlife succumbs to plastic ingestion, and which plastics are most lethal to which species.

The gold standard in toxicology is the dose–response relationship, which quantifies how the likelihood of an adverse effect changes with increasing exposure (Fig. 1). The most apparent and consequential response is death. The common metric to express this probabilistic outcome is typically LD50 or LC50, which is the dose or concentration, respectively, that has 50% odds of lethality. These levels are typically revealed by dosing experiments on model organisms in laboratory settings. Though establishing a traditional dose–response curve for marine megafauna is impractical, the authors achieved the next best result.

Starting from a known outcome—an individual that had died—the authors modeled how the probability of a lethal outcome rose with increasing plastic ingestion. They applied time-to-event (failure) models, a class of survival analysis more commonly used in medical (7, 8) or engineering studies (9, 10), to estimate the probability of mortality as a function of macroplastic ingestion load. A similar approach had been employed previously, but they were regional- and taxon-specific applications (11, 12). These prior studies also assumed that all types of plastic were equally risky to ingest, which we know to not be the case. For example, soft plastics such as balloons are 14-times more likely to be lethal to a seabird than hard plastic fragments (11).

Showcasing these differences, the authors found that when a seabird ingests just three soft rubber items it has a 50% chance of dying, and ingesting six items raises that risk to 90%. In contrast, for sea turtles, the corresponding thresholds are much higher—93 ingested soft plastic items equate to a 50% mortality risk, or 331 items for a 90% risk. However, what often matters most for lethality is relative exposure—the mass of ingested plastic standardized by some metric of body size. For example, the ingestion of a single food wrapper is likely to be more consequential for a 2 kg juvenile sea turtle than for a 200 kg adult dolphin. By painstakingly reviewing more than 10,000 necropsy reports to extract measurements of body length and the total volume of ingested plastics, the authors revealed that smaller individuals reached critical plastic-to-body-size ratios at lower ingestion loads, resulting in 50% and 90% mortality probabilities at lower absolute amounts of plastic consumed. Ultimately, it is the space a material occupies relative to gut size, more so than the number of pieces, that determines the likelihood of obstruction, perforation, or torsion.

Even one piece of plastic can be lethal if it blocks the digestive tract or perforates the gut wall. One striking example is the holotype Rice’s whale (Balaenoptera ricei) that died in 2019 after it ingested a sharp piece of plastic that caused hemorrhaging and acute gastric necrosis (13). Across the broader dataset, most marine mammals that ingested plastic had ingested just one item, yet the average volume of those pieces (164 cm3) was far larger than in other taxa, a reminder that when it comes to lethality, characteristics of the plastics (e.g., size, shape, and type) often matter more than count. Indeed, their models that used a standardized volume of debris ingested, relative to the size of the animal, were the best supported by the data.

The layers of nuance added by this work also clarified a debate in the literature. A previous study estimated that ingesting 14 pieces of plastic gave sea turtles a 50% probability of mortality (12), a finding that has since been debated (14). Here, the 50% mortality threshold for sea turtles is nearly an order of magnitude higher, which can be interpreted to mean that turtles can ingest more plastics on average before it becomes lethal than that earlier work predicted. This is likely due to the global dataset in the present work that included a more even range of turtle life stages. The initial study (12) focused on younger turtles, which are known to be more vulnerable due to their size, as well as perhaps their lack of foraging experience.

By integrating variation in plastic type, taxon, life stage, and plastic volume standardized by body size, this work advances us toward a more mechanistic and ecologically realistic assessment of mortality risk. In theory, the framework developed here can be used on any marine mammal, seabird, or sea turtle, and as more data become available, these estimates will become more robust, precise, and actionable.

Beyond individual mortality risk, this study also brings into focus the threat of macroplastic ingestion on populations. The majority of the individuals in the combined dataset showed no evidence of macroplastic ingestion, and for those that did, only a small fraction died as a direct result of plastic ingestion (Fig. 1). Across all seabirds and marine mammals, plastic ingestion was the known cause of death in 5% of cases (Fig. 1). This is on par with human mortality rate from stroke, the fourth leading cause of death in the United States (15). The threat to sea turtles is greater: Roughly 1 in 10 individuals in the dataset died from ingesting plastic (Fig. 1B). These mortality rates are not trivial, particularly for species already threatened by other stressors such as habitat degradation, entanglement in fishing gear, and climate change. Evidence directly linking plastic ingestion to population viability remains scarce, but the mortality patterns documented here provide a critical foundation. This kind of empirical evidence provides a quantitative basis for incorporating plastic ingestion into demographic models, enabling researchers to evaluate the consequences of plastic ingestion on biodiversity at large.

Overall, the results presented by Murphy et al. likely underestimate the full impacts of plastic ingestion. Their analyses focused on acute, lethal outcomes and did not account for chronic or sublethal effects, which are well documented (16–18). Emaciation resulting from food dilution and false satiation are commonly reported effects of macroplastic ingestion (16, 19). Yet in this study, deaths attributed to emaciation were not classified as plastic-related mortalities. This conservative approach reflects the fundamental uncertainty that when plastic is found in an emaciated animal, it is often unclear whether starvation was the cause or the consequence of ingestion (20). Furthermore, this study does not delve into microplastics, a growing contaminant of concern with largely unknown effects on natural populations, but the emerging evidence suggests that microplastic incorporation further elevates the risk of plastics in marine food webs (17, 21).

Our understanding of plastic occurrence in wildlife has grown exponentially, to the point that the scientific literature is now saturated with reports detailing its presence across species, habitats, and trophic levels. The grand challenge for the discipline is not simply to catalog where plastics are found but to extract generalizable principles about how this pervasive pollutant affects life on Earth. Yet demonstrating what may seem obvious is no simple task. Recall the decades of effort to show a causal link between smoking and lung cancer (22). What studies like Murphy et al. offer is a crucial step toward that causal clarity by moving from recording presence to quantifying risk. As the evidence base grows, so too will our ability to identify thresholds, predict impacts, and inform meaningful interventions. This is what is needed to turn decades of documentation into the foundation for a cleaner world.
