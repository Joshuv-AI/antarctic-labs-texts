Calculation of shield tunnel uplift induced by synchronous grouting
Authors: Ye Zhou, Ganbin Liu, Jiawei Jin, Qiang Huang, Ronghua Ye, Yao Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper analyzes the combined effects of grout properties (mix ratio, viscosity), construction speed, grouting pressure, bolt preload, and tunnel slope on shield tunnel uplift induced by synchronous grouting. A finite-difference model incorporating 12 consecutive segments was developed, incorporating time-dependent grout parameters (static buoyancy, viscosity) fitted via exponential functions. Field tests conducted on Ningbo Rail Transit Line 8, along with 3D-printed segment experiments, confirmed model reliability. Results show that grout with a shorter initial setting time minimizes uplift, while faster construction speeds increase the magnitude of uplift. Higher bolt preload reduces uplift by enhancing joint stiffness, whereas grouting pressure has a negligible influence. The uphill tunnel slope exacerbates uplift while the downhill slope mitigates it, providing practical guidelines for slope-specific construction protocols.

Introduction

During shield construction‌, a significant annular gap is created between the surrounding strata and tunnel segments after the segments detach from the shield tail. To mitigate ground loss, synchronous grouting is required to fill this gap. However, when the grout envelops the tunnel segments, buoyant forces exerted by the grout may induce substantial uplift of the segments. Excessive segment uplift can lead to structural problems such as segment cracking, misalignment, and deviation of the tunnel axis1.

The uplift process of tunnel segments is characterized by three distinct stages: acceleration stage, gentle stage, and stable stage2. The uplift curve exhibits a stepwise pattern during the gentle stage due to the buoyancy-induced dragging effect from newly assembled adjacent segments3. Segment uplift primarily occurs during the acceleration and gentle stages, with key influencing factors including grout buoyancy, grout viscous resistance, shield advance speed, bolt preloading force, grouting pressure and tunnel slope4,5. Grout composition (containing cement, lime, sand, fly ash, and bentonite) determines both the decay rate of buoyancy over time and the increase rate of viscous resistance6–9. Due to the solidification of grout, the buoyancy force generated by grouting materials will gradually decrease to zero over time. The decay rate of buoyancy force directly influences the uplift magnitude of tunnel structure10. Shield advance speed shows a positive correlation with segment uplift magnitude11, while bolt preloading force provides partial control over uplift12. The grouting pressure and sequence significantly influence the uplift of tunnel segments13. Tunnel slope modifies the vertical component of shield thrust, thereby influencing uplift behavior13.

The coupled effects of these factors require comprehensive computational models for analysis. Previous studies have simplified tunnels as beams subjected to linear buoyant loads, solving governing equations to predict deformation under grout buoyancy. These foundation-beam models incorporate joint stiffness and time-dependent grout properties14–18. Others have employed finite element methods to simulate progressive segment assembly, utilizing techniques such as cavity expansion, element replacement, and non-uniform pressure application to model shield tail grouting processes19–21. Additionally, rigid-body dynamic models have been developed to establish equilibrium equations for single-ring segments, deriving analytical solutions for uplift behavior3,12,22.

Based on the comprehensive literature review, existing calculation methods for tunnel uplifting still cannot simultaneously consider five critical factors: grout properties, construction speed, bolt preloading force, grouting pressure and tunnel slope.Isolating individual factors from their coupled effects when analyzing tunnel flotation lacks practical engineering significance. The influence of construction speed on flotation magnitude stems intrinsically from the time-dependent setting characteristic of the grout. Similarly, the effects of bolt preload and tunnel gradient are fundamentally dependent on the magnitude of the buoyant force exerted by the grout. Therefore, coupling these influencing factors is essential to accurately understand the underlying mechanisms governing tunnel flotation.

This study initially derives computational expressions for the influencing factors of tunnel uplift under synchronous grouting through literature review, indoor experiments, and theoretical analysis. Subsequently, a system of ordinary differential equations is established based on equilibrium relationships to solve tunnel uplift. The finite difference method is employed to transform the differential equations into linear algebraic equations, enabling the calculation of uplift displacement for each segment at any given time. To validate the reliability of this computational approach, field uplift tests were conducted at the Tianggongzhuang Station-Gaoxiaoquyuan Station interval tunnel construction site in Ningbo Metro, complemented by scaled model tests in the laboratory. The results from both field tests and model experiments demonstrate reasonable agreement with theoretical predictions. Leveraging this computational model, the study systematically investigates the effects of grout properties, construction speed, bolt pre-tensioning force, grouting pressure, and tunnel gradient on segmental uplift. The modeling presented herein demonstrates better agreement with actual operation compared to the classical beam theory model. The research outcomes provide theoretical guidance for controlling shield tunnel flotation in similar engineering projects.

Theoretical model

Model assumptions

The study analyzes 12 consecutive ring segments for uplift behavior, as shown in Fig. 1, where U represents the segment uplift displacement. To comply with actual working conditions‌, adjacent segments are bolted connections. During assembly, each newly installed ring aligns with its predecessor through bolting, inheriting an initial uplift displacement equal to that of the preceding ring. The uplift displacement progressively accumulates with continuous shield advancement.

This study assumes that the synchronous grouting slurry can promptly fill the shield tail gap, and the grout pressure balances the surrounding soil pressure, ensuring stratum stability. When the slurry remains in a liquid state, the surrounding soil pressure will not induce tunnel uplift. Consequently, both the overburden pressure and base reaction force may be neglected in flotation calculations during this phase23. Therefore, the primary loads governing segment uplift comprise: buoyant grout pressure (Ff), grout viscous resistance (Fₙ), segment self-weight (G), inter-ring frictional resistance (f), and bolt shear resistance (Fb)3,24, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 2.

Grout buoyant force

The buoyancy of slurry comprises both dynamic and static components. Given the complex mechanical mechanisms underlying these buoyancy forces, this study employs an empirically derived equation calibrated through computational modeling and experimental data.

The dynamic buoyant force originates from the uneven distribution of grout pressure within the shield tail gap, with its magnitude being significantly influenced by grouting sequence and injection pressure25. Given the complexity of grout diffusion patterns post-injection in shield tail voids, Previous studies have employed discrete element methods to investigate dynamic buoyant forces26,27. Notably, Cao established a discrete element model to calculate dynamic buoyant forces under varying injection pressures, revealing an exponential decay pattern over time that aligns with experimental observations from other researchers27,28. This study further applies exponential fitting to Cao’s computational results for three distinct grouting pressure scenarios (Fig. 3), yielding the dynamic buoyant force expression: FD=D1+ D2exp(D3t) with fitting parameters detailed in Table 1, the unit of t is hours.

The static buoyant force progressively diminishes over time due to grout hardening, with decay rates dependent on grout mix proportions29. Laboratory experiments were conducted to quantify static buoyant forces for three distinct grout formulations (Table 2). Mix 1, a lime-based grout, replicates the composition used in field uplift tests for the investigated tunnel. Mixes 2 and 3, cement-based grouts, serve as experimental controls. The testing apparatus (Fig. 4) employed cylindrical specimens suspended beneath a load cell, with grout poured to submerge specimen surfaces. Measured tensile force variations from the load cell directly reflect temporal evolution of static buoyant forces. The time-dependent buoyant force decay exhibits smooth continuity, amenable to elementary function fitting. To minimize fitting parameters, we employ a two-parameter exponential function expressed as fs=S1exp(S2t) where S1 represents initial buoyant force and S2 denotes decay coefficient. When estimating the shear stiffness of bolts installed in segmental linings, the bolt can be simplified as a beam with both ends constrained. Liu’s laboratory tests on steel bars embedded between two cement mortar blocks (as shown in Fig. 9(a)) revealed that due to the low strength of the mortar blocks, cracking occurred in the mortar surrounding the bar in the upper block, while crushing developed at the top of the lower block during loading31. For such conditions, the bolt can be modeled as a fixed-end beam at the bottom and a hinged-end beam at the top, with its shear stiffness calculated by the expression K = 3Eπd4/64l3, where E represents the elastic modulus of the steel, d the bar diameter, and l the bending length of the bar (which can be approximately measured from experimental data). Zhou’s tests on bolts embedded in C50 concrete (Fig. 9(b)) demonstrated that only mid-span bending deformation occurred in the bolts, with a deformation length of approximately 15 cm, accompanied by concrete crushing around the deformation zone32. For this scenario, the bolt can be idealized as a beam with both ends fixed, yielding the shear stiffness expression K = 12Eπd4/64l3. This study compared the experimentally measured shear stiffness of steel bars with theoretical values (Table 4), confirming reasonable agreement between calculations and tests. These two cases validate that bolts in segmental linings can be reasonably modeled as constrained beams. In actual tunnel construction, significant gaps exist between bolts and segmental liners for installation convenience (Fig. 9(c)). Before concrete crushing occurs in adjacent segments with misalignment, the bolt in one segment can be modeled as a fixed-end, while the other end as a hinged-end. Tunnel slope

As shown in Figs. 10 and 11, when the tunnel is inclined, both the segment self-weight (G) and buoyant grout pressure (Ff) decompose into components along and perpendicular to the tunnel axis. The axial component influences the inter-ring frictional resistance, while the perpendicular component retains its physical equivalence to the flat-slope condition. For a tunnel inclination angle α, the perpendicular and axial components are defined as G1​=Gcosα,G2​=Gsinα,Ff1​=Ff​cosα,Ff​2​=Ff​​sinα. Figures 10 and 11 demonstrate that during downhill tunneling (−α), G2​ reduces inter-ring frictional resistance while Ff2​ increases it. Conversely, during uphill tunneling (+α), G2​ enhances frictional resistance whereas Ff2​ diminishes it.

Field test validation

Field test

The test area is located in the right-line tunnel between Tiangong Manor Station and Yinzhou Higher Education Park Station on Ningbo Metro Line 8. The tunnel primarily traverses the ③₂ silty clay layer and ④₁ muddy silty clay layer, as shown in Fig. 13. The tunnel has an outer diameter of 6.2 m, a wall thickness of 35 cm, and a shield tail clearance of 75 mm. The shield tail grout injection pressure was maintained at 240 kPa. Each ring is 1.2 m in length and connected to adjacent rings via 16 M30 bolts (l = 0.17 m). The shield’s advancing speed is 1 h per ring. Synchronous grouting employs a hardening slurry composed of fly ash, sand, water, water reducer, hydrated lime, and bentonite. The test area is divided into a downhill Sect. (2% slope), a flat section, and an uphill Sect. (2.5% slope). Continuous shield machine angle adjustments during tunneling significantly affect test data accuracy. Shield machine angle requires significantly greater steering corrections when navigating curves or traversing heterogeneous strata, whereas minimal adjustments suffice during linear advancement through homogeneous ground conditions.Therefore, continuous 12-ring segments with minimal angle adjustments were selected from each test section as control data for segment uplift measurement. After segments exited the shield tail and stabilized, total station instruments and leveling rulers were used to measure the coordinates of segment centers(Measurement accuracy: 0.1 mm), from which segment uplift values were calculated, ‌as shown in Fig. 14‌.

The buoyant displacements of 12 consecutive segments in three distinct grout types were calculated, with time-history curves of segment uplift in these grouts illustrated in Figs. 20, 21 and 22. These figures demonstrate that grout properties significantly influence both the magnitude and characteristics of segment uplift. Grout 1, a lime-based formulation replicating field-tested compositions, exhibits the smallest initial buoyant force and slowest decay rate, coupled with consistently lower viscosity coefficients prior to solidification compared to other grouts. Due to its reduced viscous resistance, Grout 1 induces greater initial uplift displacements immediately after segment assembly than the other two grouts. As shown in Fig. 20, the gradual reduction of buoyant force allows the downward displacement of segments under self-weight, ultimately stabilizing upon grout solidification. Grouts 2 and 3, cement-based formulations serving as experimental controls, display contrasting behaviors. Grout 2, containing higher relative proportions of cement and bentonite, demonstrates the largest initial buoyant force, the most rapid decay rate, and consistently superior viscosity characteristics before hardening. Figure 21 reveals a distinctive stepped uplift pattern in Grout 2, where accelerated buoyant force decay triggers initial segment settlement, followed by secondary uplift induced by adjacent newly installed segments. In contrast, Grout 3 exhibits slower buoyant force attenuation than Grout 2 (Fig. 22), resulting in greater cumulative uplift displacements. Notably, Grout 3’s viscous resistance becomes sufficient to inhibit segment settlement when buoyant force diminishes, thereby preventing the stepped displacement pattern observed in Grout 2. Integrated analysis reveals that reducing the initial setting time of grout not only diminishes its buoyant force but also enhances viscous resistance, thereby effectively mitigating tunnel uplift.

The buoyant force and viscous resistance of grout slurry constitute critical factors governing segment uplift behavior. The buoyant force comprises static and dynamic components, where the static buoyancy is determined by the initial buoyancy (S1) and decay coefficient (S2), while the dynamic buoyancy depends on grouting pressure. The viscous resistance is governed by the viscous resistance growth rate parameter (N3).

This study examines the parametric effects of S1, S2, and N3 through controlled variations (Fig. 23): S1 = 18, 19, 20kN/m3; S2 = −0.005, −0.01, −0.015; N3 = 1, 1.33, 2. Results show that the buoyancy decay coefficient (S2) exerts significantly greater influence on segment uplift displacements compared to initial buoyancy (S1) and viscous resistance growth (N3). Under constant S2 and N3 conditions, each 1 kN/m3 increment in S1 induces approximately 10 mm of additional uplift displacement.

Figures 24 and 25 reveal that reducing S2 from − 0.005 to −0.01 under fixed S1 and N3 triggers a transition from segment uplift to settlement. This phenomenon occurs because accelerated buoyancy decay enables segment self-weight to overcome residual buoyancy prior to grout solidification, initiating downward displacement until grout hardening terminates the process. As shown in Fig. 26, further intensification of decay rate to S2 = −0.015 results in immediate settlement following minor initial uplift (≤ 1 mm), producing substantially larger cumulative downward displacements.

Figures 27 and 28 illustrate that reducing N3 from 2 to 1 under constant S1 and S2 conditions accelerates the viscous resistance growth rate, effectively reducing final uplift displacement from 45 mm to 15 mm. This demonstrates that enhanced viscous resistance development significantly mitigates segment uplift. Comparative analysis reveals that while N3 variations minimally affect peak uplift magnitudes, they substantially influence post-peak behavior. Accelerated viscous resistance growth (lower N3) reduces traction forces from subsequently installed segments, enabling self-weight-induced settlement after peak uplift. The stepwise displacement fluctuations in Fig. 27 reflect intermittent traction effects from sequential segment installation, whereas the absence of sufficient post-peak traction in Fig. 28 permits continuous settlement.

This study calculated segment uplift displacements under three grout conditions with varying grouting pressures, as illustrated in Fig. 29. Results demonstrate that grouting pressure exerts negligible influence on segment uplift. Figures 30 and 31 reveal that the uplift displacement under 320 kPa grouting pressure increases by less than 2 mm compared to that under 160 kPa, with no significant divergence observed in their time-history curves. Additionally, segment uplift displacements neglecting the dynamic buoyancy force were computed (Fig. 32). Comparative analysis between Figs. 30, 31 and 32 indicates that the dynamic buoyancy force contributes approximately 5 mm to total uplift displacements. The limited impact of the dynamic buoyancy force arises from its transient duration relative to the static buoyant force, as grout-induced uneven pressure dissipates rapidly over time.

As illustrated in Fig. 33, bolt preload significantly influences segment uplift displacements. Across all three grout conditions, a 5 kN increase in bolt preload reduces uplift displacements by 6–7 mm. This mechanism arises because elevated bolt preloading enhances frictional resistance between adjacent segments, thereby imposing greater restraining forces during uplift. The amplified restraint effectively counteracts buoyancy-driven vertical movement, resulting in suppressed uplift magnitudes.

As shown in Fig. 34, shield advance speed exerts a substantial influence on segment uplift displacements. At a construction speed of 1 h/ring, segments exhibit uplift across all three grout types. Conversely, at reduced speeds of 1.5 and 2 h/ring, segments predominantly undergo settlement in all grout conditions. Figures 35 and 36 present the time-history curves of segment uplift in Grout 1 at advance speeds of 1.5 and 2 h/ring, respectively. These results demonstrate that prolonged construction time per ring allows significant decay of the grout buoyant force acting on the segment before the installation of subsequent rings. Consequently, segments begin to settle prior to experiencing restraining forces from newly installed neighboring rings. The extended construction duration amplifies the decay of residual buoyancy, leading to progressively larger cumulative settlements.

Conclusions

This study developed a computational model for the uplift of 12 consecutive segments in shield tunnels induced by synchronous grouting. The governing equations were solved using the finite difference method‌. To validate the methodology, field tests were conducted in the right-line tunnel‌ between Tiangong Manor Station and Yinzhou Higher Education Park Station of Ningbo Metro Line 8, supplemented by laboratory model tests with 3D-printed segments. Both field measurements and experimental results demonstrate that the proposed theoretical approach accurately quantifies grout-induced tunnel uplift. The effects of grout properties, tunnel slope, grouting pressure, bolt preload, and construction speed on segment uplift are systematically analyzed. Key conclusions are summarized as follows:

The time-dependent variations in grout’s static buoyancy and viscosity coefficients can be effectively characterized by exponential functions.The decay rate of grout buoyancy exerts the most significant influence on tunnel uplift. Both the initial buoyancy and the growth rate of the viscosity coefficient notably affect uplift magnitudes. Accelerating the buoyancy decay rate or enhancing the viscosity growth rate proves crucial for uplift control. Reducing the grout’s initial setting time effectively mitigates tunnel uplift through rapid buoyancy reduction and viscosity enhancement.Grouting pressure demonstrates negligible influence on segment uplift due to rapid dissipation of non-uniform pressures. The dynamic buoyancy effect contributes approximately 5 mm to total uplift.Increasing bolt preload significantly reduces segment uplift, with each 5 kN increment decreasing uplift by 6–7 mm through enhanced joint stiffness.Construction speed substantially affects uplift magnitudes, showing a positive correlation with uplift development.Tunnel slope exhibits limited influence on uplift behavior: downhill sections experience mild uplift reduction while uphill sections show slight uplift amplification.
