Handheld hyperspectral imaging dataset of annual sowthistle and little mallow under abiotic stress for machine learning
Authors: Elijah Brunnengraeber, Tyler Cassidy, Dylan Woodbridge, Arun D Jani, Arun K Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMachine learning has become an increasingly important tool for overcoming agricultural challenges by enabling efficient and consistent classification of crop-related data. Training such supervised models requires high quality labeled datasets. This work presents a dataset consisting of raw and preprocessed hyperspectral imaging (HSI) files capturing reflectance in the visible to near-infrared range (400–1000 nm) from two problematic weed species on California’s Central Coast: annual sowthistle (Sonchus oleraceus) and little mallow (Malva parviflora). Hyperspectral imaging provides rich spectral-spatial data cubes that can support the development of deep learning models and autonomous technology for precision weed management. Plants were grown in a greenhouse under five conditions: standard, drought, overwatering, excess fertilizer, and no fertilizer. Custom MATLAB scripts were utilized for preprocessing, including k-means clustering to define regions of interest (ROIs), and extraction of spectral metrics. Data visualization was performed using Wolfram language and MATLAB. The dataset includes both raw and ENVI-formatted hyperspectral cubes and pre-processed MATLAB outputs, supporting spectral feature engineering, benchmark development, and exploratory machine learning workflows for controlled environment stress classification.Keywords: Hyperspectral imaging, Weeds, Dataset, Plant health, Plant identification, Abiotic stress

Background

Traditional RGB (red, green, blue) imaging captures spatial features of plants, enabling classification through deep learning (DL) techniques[[1], [2], [3]]. However, RGB images lack detailed spectral information that is critical for detecting subtle physiological and biochemical changes [4]. Hyperspectral imaging (HSI) addresses this limitation by capturing reflectance data across a wide range of wavelengths in each pixel, adding a spectral dimension to every image [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]].

Access to high-quality hyperspectral datasets is essential for developing autonomous agricultural systems capable of overcoming specific challenges and precisely targeting weed species known to act as disease reservoirs [9,10]. For example, annual sowthistle (Sonchus oleraceus) and little mallow (Malva parviflora) are known to harbor impatiens necrotic spot virus (INSV), with reported infectivity rates of 20% or greater [11]. If not managed, these weeds can severely reduce crop yields, especially in leafy green production systems grown in Monterey County.

The dataset described here captures reflectance across the 400–1000 nm range, enabling the extraction of both spatial and spectral features. This rich data source enhances the potential for machine learning models to accurately classify plant stress conditions, ultimately supporting the development of precision weed management tools[12]. Machine learning has been applied broadly across the agricultural workflow, from pre-harvest soil and crop health monitoring through post-harvest quality assessment[13] and weed stress classification using hyperspectral data represents a key sensing input to this pipeline. The economic relevance of these classification capabilities extends to agricultural commodity markets, where ML and neural network approaches are now applied to forecasting price indices for globally traded crops[14,15] accurate and early detection of weed-driven yield variability contributes to the quality of the sensing layer that such economic models depend on.

In contrast to RGB only weed imaging resources, this dataset provides spatial-spectral cubes that preserve wavelength resolved physiological information relevant to abiotic stress discrimination. This makes the dataset suitable not only for species level classification, but also for evaluating spectral feature selection, stress sensitive vegetation indices, and spatial-spectral model architectures that cannot be tested using RGB imagery alone.

Data Description

The dataset[16] consists of hyperspectral images of annual sowthistle (Sonchus oleraceus) and little mallow (Malva parviflora), each subjected to five abiotic stress conditions: standard (control), drought, overwatering, excess fertilizer, and no fertilizer. The images were collected under controlled greenhouse conditions using a handheld Specim IQ HSI camera. The repository is organized first by species, then by treatment class, and then by acquisition date. Within each acquisition folder, raw hyperspectral captures are stored in ENVI compatible formats, while derived outputs include ROI masks, mean reflectance summaries, and vegetations index files. This organization allows users to trace each processed output back to its corresponding raw cube, and each is named accordingly (Fig. 1). Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Data acquisition

Plant samples were cultivated in a California State University Monterey Bay greenhouse. For each abiotic stress treatment, six biological replicates were monitored across successive growth stages, during which hyperspectral images were acquired. Each treatment group contained six biological replicate plants, which were imaged repeatedly across multiple acquisition sessions; therefore, the final image totals represent repeated observations over time rather than unique replicate counts alone. Imaging was performed using a Specim IQ handheld hyperspectral camera, which captures reflectance data from 400 nm to 1000 nm in approximately 7 nm intervals in the visible to near-infrared (VNIR) spectrum.

Calibration was performed using a white reference tile prior to each imaging session to ensure consistent reflectance measurements. Ambient greenhouse lighting was used during imaging and therefore may have varied somewhat across sessions due to environmental conditions. To reduce this variability, white reference calibration was performed before each session and imaging conditions were kept as consistent as possible within the greenhouse environment.

The standard treatment group (control) received 237 mL of water twice weekly and was fertilized with a nutrient solution containing 150 ppm nitrogen (N), 20 ppm phosphorus (P), and 80 ppm potassium (K). This stock solution was prepared by dissolving 2.5 mL of Miracle-Gro fertilizer per gallon of water, from which 237 mL was applied to each plant every two weeks. In the Miracle-Gro solution, phosphorus is present as P2O5 and potassium is present as K2O. The experimental conditions for the abiotic stress factors are listed in Table 2.

Data preprocessing

Data preprocessing was performed using three custom MATLAB scripts. The first script applied k-means clustering to assist in selecting regions of interest (ROIs) from each hyperspectral image. The second script extracted vegetation metrics from the defined ROIs, including the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), Photochemical Reflectance Index (PRI), and spectral entropy. A third script was used to gather mean reflectance from each image. After automatic ROI generation, masks were visually reviewed to confirm that plant tissue was captured and that background materials were excluded. Any manual corrections were applied only in cases where the automated cluster selection visibly included non-plant pixels. After the ROIs were identified, binary masks were generated to isolate relevant plant pixels. The isolated pixels were used to extract per-pixel spectral data, which were then aggregated by treatment class for downstream analysis and visualization.

Selection of regions of interest

Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined as groups of spectrally similar pixels within the hyperspectral cube. To identify these, k-means clustering [18] was applied across the spectral bands of each image, grouping pixels based on reflectance similarity. A binary mask was created for the cluster corresponding to the plant material, which effectively excluded background and non-target regions. This mask was applied to the full hyperspectral image to isolate the plant spectrum (Fig. 3). The resulting data ensured that only the biologically relevant pixels were used for vegetation index calculations. Following k-means clustering, each mask was visually inspected for segmentation quality. A mask was accepted when it captured the visible plant region while excluding background materials such as pot surfaces, soil, and non-target objects. Manual correction was limited to deleting extraneous regions captured during imaging and was performed only when needed and observed during the ROI process.

Spectral metrics & index summary

For each defined region of interest (ROI), the binary mask was used to isolate plant pixels from the hyperspectral image. Mean reflectance values were calculated across all spectral bands within each ROI. Reflectance data from each treatment group were averaged and plotted to visualize spectral variation across classes. Difference in the visible region may reflect pigment-related responses, while changes approaching the red-edge and near infrared regions may reflect stress-related differences in leaf structure and physiological condition [19]. These descriptive spectral summaries provide an initial indication of treatment separation and help identify wavelength regions that may be useful for downstream feature selection and classification models.

Software and computational tools

All hyperspectral image processing and metric extraction were performed using MATLAB R2025a (version 25.1.0.2943329). Three custom scripts were developed: one for selecting regions of interest (ROIs) using k-means clustering, and another for calculating vegetation indices (NDVI, PRI, and spectral entropy) for each processed image. The availability of both raw ENVI data and pre-processed MATLAB outputs allow users to choose between custom end-to-end preprocessing and faster prototyping using ROI derived features. Additional data analysis and visualization were carried out using Wolfram Language 14.3.0. All computations were performed on a 64-bit Linux operating system (x86 architecture).

Limitations

This dataset is limited to two weed species grown under controlled greenhouse conditions, and the reflectance signatures therefore may not fully represent field-grown plants exposed to variable illumination, soil background, canopy overlap, and microclimatic variation. In particular, vegetation indices such as NDVI and PRI, as well as full-spectrum signatures, may be affected by mixed pixels, background contamination, and illumination differences under field conditions. Future work should evaluate transferability by testing comparable stress classes under outdoor or field-like conditions.

Each abiotic stress class included six biological replicates, and the resulting dataset size is therefore more appropriate for exploratory analysis, benchmark development, spectral feature engineering, and proof-of-concept machine learning than for strong claims of broad model generalizability. This is especially relevant for data-intensive deep learning approaches. Suitable hyperspectral augmentation strategies may include patch extraction, geometric transformations that preserve spectral relationships, and noise-aware perturbations, whereas arbitrary spectral distortion should be avoided.

All imaging sessions were conducted between 12:00 pm and 2:00 pm local time. Monterey, California experiences predominantly clear and sunny afternoon conditions, and imaging was not conducted on overcast days. The greenhouse environment further attenuated direct solar variability by filtering light through translucent polycarbonate panels, which diffuse rather than eliminate sunlight and maintain relatively consistent spectral composition across sessions under clear-sky conditions. The white reference tile calibration performed before each session converts raw digital numbers to relative reflectance by normalizing pixel values against the tile's known reflectance. This normalization removes the effect of illumination intensity differences between sessions.

Solar elevation angles at this time of day in Seaside, California are near their daily maximum, minimizing the rate of change in sun angle during the imaging period and reducing the potential for systematic directional illumination shifts between sessions. Of the 240 total hyperspectral images in the dataset only 20 required manual ROI correction, representing approximately 8% of the full dataset. The remaining 220 images (approximately 92%) were processed using the automated k-means clustering pipeline without modification. Manual corrections were concentrated in images where severely stressed plants exhibited reduced canopy coverage, causing the plant cluster to either fragment across multiple k-means regions or merge spectrally with dark substrate material. All manual ROI corrections were performed by a single operator throughout the study. Single-operator correction eliminates inter-operator variability by design, as the same individual applied the same visual acceptance criteria consistently across all sessions.

In addition, ROI extraction by k-means clustering required manual review in a subset of cases, particularly where plant morphology or background similarity reduced segmentation clarity. To improve transparency, decision criteria for manual correction are described here. A mask was accepted without modification when the selected cluster visually covered the plant canopy while excluding pot surfaces, substrate, and calibration tile edges. Manual correction was applied in two circumstances: when the plant-assigned cluster included non-plant pixels sharing similar spectral properties with stressed or senescent tissue, such as dark substrate or pot rims, and when severe stress produced spectrally heterogeneous tissue that fragmented the plant region across multiple k-means clusters. In both cases, corrections consisted of manual additions or subtractions to the binary mask boundary to restore contiguous coverage of plant tissue. These corrections were applied conservatively, and only pixels whose exclusion or inclusion was unambiguous from visual inspection of the RGB frame were adjusted. The supplementary information document provides further details on dark current correction and recommendations for minimizing temporal correlation and data leakage risks in the machine learning pipeline.
