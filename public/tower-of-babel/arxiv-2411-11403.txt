Hadamard–Langevin dynamics for sampling the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -prior
Ivan Cheltsov, Federico Cornalba, Clarice Poon, Tony Shardlow

Abstract
Priors with non-smooth log-densities, such as the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -prior, are widely used in Bayesian inverse problems for their sparsity-inducing properties. Existing Langevin-based sampling methods typically rely on proximal mappings or smooth approximations, which alter the target distribution. We propose an alternative approach based on a Hadamard product parameterization of the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm, leading to a smooth but nonconvex and non–globally Lipschitz potential whose marginal law exactly recovers the desired posterior. The resulting Hadamard–Langevin dynamics (HLD) defines a diffusion process that is analytically distinct from proximal or mirror-type Langevin schemes. Our main contribution is a rigorous well-posedness theory for both the continuous and discrete HLD. We establish existence and uniqueness of strong solutions, geometric ergodicity of the continuous dynamics, and convergence of the discretized scheme as the step size tends to zero. These results provide the first theoretical foundation for sampling from nonconvex, nonsmooth posteriors through overparameterized Langevin dynamics.



Keywords:
Langevin equations, Lasso, Sampling algorithms, Sparsity

1
Introduction

Sampling from sparsity-promoting distributions is a central task in high-dimensional Bayesian inference and inverse problems. A prototypical example is

\displaystyle\rho(x)=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\bigl(-\beta\bigl(\lambda\|x\|_{1}+G(x)\bigr)\bigr),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1)

where
Z
is a normalization constant,
\beta>0
is the inverse temperature,
\lambda
is a regularization parameter, and
G\colon\real^{d}\to\real
represents the data likelihood. Due to the non-smoothness of the
\ell_{1}
-term, existing Langevin-type methods typically rely on smoothing techniques such as the Moreau–Yosida envelope or proximal mappings.

In this work, we propose and theoretically analyse the Hadamard–Langevin dynamics (HLD) — a novel smooth overparameterized dynamics whose marginal stationary distribution exactly recovers
\rho
without smoothing and bias.
The key idea is to exploit the Hadamard representation of the
\ell_{1}
-norm,

\displaystyle\left\|x\right\|_{1}=\sum_{i}\left|x_{i}\right|=\min_{u\odot v=x}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left\|u\right\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|v\right\|^{2}_{2}\right\}.

(2)

By replacing the
\ell_{1}
term, the log density in
Eq.

1
is recast as a smooth but non-concave function in
(u,v)
due to the Hadamard product
u\odot v=x
. This representation, which has recently garnered interest in optimization to study implicit regularization and to develop efficient optimization solvers, has not yet been explored from a sampling perspective. We demonstrate that applying Langevin dynamics in the lifted space
(u,v)
leads to a well-defined diffusion process whose invariant measure projects exactly onto
\rho
.

1.1
The Hadamard–Langevin dynamics

Our starting point is the classical Gaussian-mixture identity underlying the hierarchical Bayesian formulation of the Laplace prior (
West (1987)
),

\exp\left(-a\left|z\right|\right)=\frac{a}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2\eta}-\frac{a^{2}\eta}{2}\right)d\eta,\qquad a\in\real_{+},\,\,z\in\real.

(3)

Introducing latent variables
\eta_{i}>0
for each coordinate of
x
, one can rewrite the target distribution (
1
) as the marginal of a joint law over
(x,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
:

\tilde{\pi}(x,\eta)\propto\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{\eta_{i}}}\exp\Biggl(-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{2\eta_{i}}+\frac{\beta^{2}\lambda^{2}\eta_{i}}{2}\right)-\beta\,G(x)\Biggr).

(4)

Since sampling from
\tilde{\pi}
yields the correct marginal
\rho(x)
,

Park and Casella (2008)
proposed a Gibbs sampler where one alternates between sampling
x
and
\eta
. However, such works typically restrict to the case where
G
is quadratic, so sampling
x
corresponds to sampling a Gaussian, and this in turn requires inverting a potentially high-dimensional matrix.
In contrast, as we explain next, we focus on a reparameterized formulation of the above, which will allow us to derive a Langevin algorithm, under general assumptions on the data term
G
.

1.1.1
From hierarchical models to Hadamard overparameterization

A key observation is that the Gaussian mixture representation can be viewed as the stochastic analogue of the quadratic variational form for the
\ell_{1}
-norm.
By applying a change of variables of

x=u\odot v,\quad\eta=u\odot u/(\lambda\beta)

to (
4
), we obtain the Hadamard parametrized joint law

\displaystyle\pi(u,v)=\frac{1}{Z_{\pi}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}\,\exp\left(-\beta\,\left(\frac{1}{2}\lambda\|(u,v)\|^{2}+G(u\odot v)\right)\right)

(5)

over
(u,v)\in\mathcal{X}\coloneq\real_{+}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
for normalization constant
Z_{\pi}
and
\left\|(u,v)\right\|^{2}=\left\|u\right\|^{2}+\left\|v\right\|^{2}
. The Hadamard product
x=u\odot v
follows the same distribution
\rho
defined in
Eq.

1
. This is formalized in the following statement.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
(u,v)\sim\pi
as defined in
Eq.

5
, then
x=u\odot v\sim\rho
as defined in
Eq.

1
. In particular, for any smooth
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}
,

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi(u\odot v)\,\pi(u,v)\,du\,dv=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\,\rho(x)\,dx.

Furthermore, the normalization factors
Z_{\pi}
and
Z
of
\pi
and
\rho
, respectively, satisfy

\frac{1}{Z}=\frac{1}{Z_{\pi}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2\beta\lambda}}\right)^{d}.

Note that the log-density is smooth except for
\log(u_{i})
terms, which we show can be handled easily. It is therefore natural to derive Langevin dynamics (see (
7
)) to sample
\pi
, and recover samples of
\rho
by evaluating
u\odot v
.

1.2
Main contributions

By treating
\rho
using the Hadamard parameterization to obtain a differentiable log-density, we demonstrate that we can apply Langevin sampling methods to good effect. We provide a complete theoretical analysis of the Hadamard–Langevin dynamics by establishing well-posedness and stability of the continuous and discretized dynamics.

1.

We establish the well-posedness on
\mathcal{X}=\real_{+}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the Langevin system associated with
\pi
given in (
5
). This includes geometric convergence to the invariant measure (
Section

2
). We present two approaches: The first approach removes the singularity in the
\log(u)
-term via a Cartesian change of coordinates, to transform our drift into a locally bounded term. The second approach exploits connections of our Langevin system to the well-studied Cox–Ingersoll–Ross and Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes via a Girsanov transformation. The Girsanov transformation also ensures that
u_{t}
will be positive almost surely.

2.

We develop a numerical scheme to approximate the Langevin system and show the convergence of the numerical scheme to
\pi
(
Section

3
). We establish strong convergence of the method as well as discuss convergence of the stationary distribution of the numerical method. One of the challenges in the analysis is that the negative log-density is neither convex nor globally Lipschitz; despite this, we are able to prove geometric ergodicity of the numerical scheme by relying on two tools, namely a coupling technique for different realisations of the numerical method inside sufficiently large balls, and a rapidly-decaying bound for the escape probability from such balls.

As mentioned, the Gaussian mixture formula
Eq.

3
can be seen as the equivalent of the quadratic variational form for sampling and
our work can be seen as a first attempt to deploy this trick for Langevin dynamics. Even though we restrict to sampling the
\ell_{1}
-prior, as discussed in the conclusion, we believe that our work offers a new perspective on sampling sparse priors and future work would aim to generalize this to other structure-inducing priors.

1.3
Related background

Quadratic variational forms

It is well-known that many of the sparsity-inducing regularizers, and in particular, the
\ell_{1}
-norm, have
quadratic variational forms

(
Black and Rangarajan, 1996
;
Bach et al., 2012
)
. In particular, if the regulariser
R(x)=\phi(x\odot x)
for
x\in\real^{d}
and a concave function
\phi
, one can rewrite using quadratic variational forms

R(x)=\inf_{\eta\in\real^{d}_{+}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{\eta_{i}}+\psi(\eta),

(6)

for some function
\psi
.
This includes the
\ell_{q}
(semi)norms for
q\in(0,2]
and various nonconvex regularizers such as the Lorentzian function
(
Black and Rangarajan, 1996
)
; a similar formula also holds for functionals on matrices, such as the nuclear norm
(
Bach et al., 2012
)
.
In the case of
R(x)=\left\|x\right\|_{1}
, one can choose
\psi(\eta)=\eta/4
.

This idea has been exploited to good effect in optimization, and the computer-vision community found the new variable
\eta
can help increase the curvature of a ‘flat’ landscape and has led to graduated non-convexity algorithms
(
Black and Rangarajan, 1996
)
.
This lifting of the
\ell_{1}
-norm to the
x
and
\eta
variables is the basis of iteratively reweighted least-squares algorithms
(
Daubechies et al., 2008
)
, where one alternates between solving least squares problems on
x
and exact solves on
\eta
.

The Hadamard product parameterization

More recently, there has been renewed interest in the Hadamard form of these quadratic variational formulas (
6
), particularly in the context of convex structure-inducing regularizers.
For
\psi(\eta)=\eta/4
, by making a change of variable with
x=u\odot v
and
\eta/2=u\odot u
, one can equivalently write (
6
) as
\left\|x\right\|_{1}=\inf_{x=u\odot v}\{\frac{1}{2}\left\|u\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|v\right\|^{2}
} where
\left\|\cdot\right\|
is the Euclidean 2-norm.
While the formulation in (
6
) retained convexity in both variables
x
and
\eta
,
this Hadamard product-like formula is no longer convex but is now smooth; see
Fig.

1
. Consequently, one can make use of powerful smooth optimization solvers such as quasi-Newton methods. One of the first works that replace the
\ell_{1}
-norm with the Hadamard product is the work of
Hoff (2017)
; see also
Poon and Peyré (2023)
;
Poon and Peyré (2021)
for further discussions on exploiting these forms for optimization. In the case of the
\ell_{1}
-norm, one can show that this nonconvexity is in fact benign
(
Poon and Peyré, 2021
)
. Furthermore, it can be shown that gradient descent with the Hadamard parameterization is implicitly linked to mirror descent with a hyperbolic entropy potential
(
Poon and Peyré, 2023
)
. This points to a potential benefit of such parameterizations for modern optimization when working on high-dimensional and ill-conditioned problems, where
Chizat (2022)
investigated the superior convergence behaviour of such mirror descent algorithms in the context of sparse measures.
Finally, over-parameterization and its implications for implicit bias in gradient-descent training of neural networks have also been of wide interest in the machine learning community. In particular, the Hadamard product parameterization, even without explicit regularization, causes gradient descent to bias towards sparse solutions
(
Vaskevicius et al., 2019
;
Chou et al., 2023
)
.

Unadjusted Langevin algorithms (ULA) and Moreau–Yosida envelopes

Langevin dynamics is a simple way to sample from the posterior distribution
\rho(x)

(
Roberts and Tweedie, 1996
)
.
Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm (ULA), in the case where
\rho(x)\propto\exp(-\beta H(x))
for a
smooth
potential
H\colon\real^{d}\to\real
, is the update rule

x^{n+1}=x^{n}-\nabla H(x^{n}){\Delta t}+\sqrt{2{\Delta t}/\beta}\,\xi^{n},

for independent samples
\xi^{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{Id}_{d})
and time step
{\Delta t}>0
. This can be interpreted as a discretization
x^{n}\approx x_{n{\Delta t}}
of the overdamped Langevin stochastic differential equation (SDE):

dx_{t}=-\nabla H(x_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2/\beta}\,dW_{t},

where

W_{t}
is a standard
d
-valued Wiener process.

In the case where
H(x)=G(x)+R(x)
and
R
is non-smooth (as in our case, see (
1
)), a canonical approach is to replace
R
by a smooth approximation defined by the Moreau–Yosida envelope
R_{\gamma}

(
Pereyra, 2016
)
:

R_{\gamma}(x)\coloneq\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}(R(z)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left\|x-z\right\|^{2}).

The function
R_{\gamma}
is then
1/\gamma
-smooth and converges pointwise to
R
as
\gamma\to 0
.
One can then directly apply ULA to
H_{\gamma}\coloneq G+R_{\gamma}
, leading to the Moreau–Yosida regularized Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm (MYULA). Convergence to the stationary distribution requires one to take
\gamma\to 0

(
Durmus et al., 2018
)
. Under suitable conditions, such as convexity of
G
and Lipschitz smoothness, convergence results like geometric ergodicity of the stationary distribution
\rho_{\gamma}\propto\exp(-\beta H_{\gamma})
have been established, as well as the convergence of
\rho_{\gamma}
to
\rho
as
\gamma\to 0
.
While early works replace
R
by its Moreau envelope,
Durmus et al. (2019)
removes this approximation bias by directly incorporating the proximal operator of
R
.
Another line of work that avoids the bias from the Moreau-envelope smoothing directly samples from
H
via subgradient Langevin
(
Fruehwirth and Habring, 2024
)
in the case where
H
is strongly convex. In general, smoothing by the Moreau envelope allows one to leverage all existing theory for smooth Langevin dynamics, while more recent works derive cleaner algorithms and proofs by heavily relying on techniques from convex analysis.

G(x)
(blue),

Continuous dynamics

We work with
G
under the following assumptions.

Assumption 1

(data term
G
)
.

Assume that (i)

is bounded below, that (ii)

The data term

; and smooth approximations of the unit loss, such as
G(x)=\sum_{i}\sigma(y_{i}A_{i}^{\top}x)

\sigma(x)=1/(1+\exp(x))
.
Note that

satisfies condition (iv) for any function

with bounded derivatives, which includes the logistic loss and smooth versions of the unit loss. The least-squares loss

does not satisfy condition (iv) on the boundedness of
\nabla G
, and one might consider instead the Huber loss. However, in our numerical computations, the method behaves well with the quadratic loss, so assumption (iv) is likely a limitation of our theoretical analysis rather than the method itself.

2.1
Langevin sampling

We will use Langevin sampling to produce samples from
\pi

Let

\int\left|\phi(x)\right|^{q}\pi(x)\,dx<\infty,

\pi

, which extends the moment bounds in

to
\phi\in L^{q}(\pi)
, and includes some
\phi

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
(u,v)_{t}
denote the solution of
Eq.

7

\rho_{0}
. Let
p\geq 1
. Assume that (
p,q)
are conjugate and that
h_{0}=\rho_{0}/\pi
belongs to

. If
\phi\in L^{q}(\pi)
, then
\mathbb{E}\left[\phi((u,v)_{t})\right]\leq\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}\,\left\|h_{0}\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}
for
t>0
.

Proof.

Let
\rho(t,\cdot)
denote the density of
(u,v)_{t}
. Denote

and
h_{0}=\rho_{0}/\pi
. It is well known (e.g.,
Markowich and Villani (2000)
) that

satisfies the PDE

\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}=\Delta h+\nabla\log\pi\cdot\nabla h,\qquad h(0,z)=h_{0}(z),

on

and

. For any twice-differentiable convex function
\psi
, it follows
(
Markowich and Villani, 2000
, Eq. 9)
that

\frac{d}{dt}\int\psi(h(t,x))\,\pi(x)\,dx=-\int\psi^{\prime\prime}(h(t,x))\,|\nabla h(t,x)|^{2}\,\pi(x)\,dx\leq 0.

Choosing
\psi(h)=h^{p}
implies that
\|h(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}
is a non-increasing function of time.
Thus, writing

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\phi((u,v)_{t})\right]=\int\phi(x)\,h(t,x)\,\pi(x)\,dx\leq\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}\,\left\|h(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}\leq\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}\,\left\|h_{0}\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}

completes the proof.
∎

As a corollary, we prove the following moment bounds on

for

. The density of the invariant measure
\pi

u_{1}
near

, and we expect

to be infinite.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let the assumption of
Theorem

2.1
hold and the initial distribution
\rho_{0}(u,v)
be such that
\rho_{0}/\pi
is uniformly bounded.
Then, for

1.
\displaystyle\sup_{t>0}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{u_{i,t}^{r}}\right]<\infty

(here

).

2. For each

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{u_{i,s}^{2-\epsilon}}\,ds\right)^{q}\right]<\infty

Proof.

Let
\phi(u,v)=u_{1}^{-r}
for example. Since

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{\beta}\,\epsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{T}{u_{1,t}^{\epsilon-2}}\,dt&={u_{1,T}^{\epsilon}}-{u_{1,0}^{\epsilon}}+\lambda\,\epsilon\,\int_{0}^{T}{u_{1,t}^{\epsilon}}\,dt\\

(9)

\displaystyle\epsilon\,\left|\int_{0}^{T}v_{1,t}\,[\nabla G(u_{t}\odot v_{t})]_{1}\,u_{1,t}^{\epsilon-1}\,dt\right|\leq

with

.

We will also require the following time-regularity bounds.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
(u,v)_{t}
denote the solution of
Eq.

7
with initial distribution
\rho_{0}
. Suppose that the assumptions of
Corollary

2.3
hold. For
1\leq q<2
,
T>0
, and
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
, there exists
c>0
such that, for
0\leq s,t\leq T
,

1.

\mathbb{E}\left[{\|(u,v)_{t}-(u,v)_{s}\|^{q}}\vphantom{\bigm|}\right]<c\,|t-s|^{q/2}
.

2.

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|u_{i,t}-u_{i,s}|}{u_{i,s}\,u_{i,t}}\right]\leq c\,|t-s|^{1/2-\epsilon},\qquad i=1,\dots,d
.

Proof.

1. We treat the
u_{1}
component only (
v
is handled similarly). From
Eq.

7
,

u_{1,t}=u_{1,s}+\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{s}^{t}\frac{1}{u_{1,r}}\,dr+\cdots+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\int_{s}^{t}dW^{1}_{r},

where we omit several integrals that are straightforward to estimate under the moment bound in
Theorem

2.1
.

By Holder’s inequality with conjugate exponents
(2-\epsilon,(2-\epsilon)/(1-\epsilon))
,

\int_{s}^{t}\frac{1}{u_{1,r}}\,dr\leq|t-s|^{(1-\epsilon)/(2-\epsilon)}\,{\left[\int_{s}^{t}\frac{1}{|u_{1,r}|^{2-\epsilon}}\,dr\right]^{1/(2-\epsilon)}}.

Now evaluate the
q
th moments and use moment bounds on Gaussian increments to find, for a constant
c_{q}>0
, that

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}|u_{1,t}-u_{1,s}|^{q})^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leq|t-s|^{(1-\epsilon)/(2-\epsilon)}\,\frac{1}{\beta}\,\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{s}^{t}\frac{1}{|u_{1,r}|^{2-\epsilon}}\,dr\right]^{q/(2-\epsilon)}\right)^{1/q}+\cdots+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\,c_{q}\,|t-s|^{1/2}.

By
Corollary

2.3
, the
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{s}^{t}\frac{1}{|u_{r}|^{2-\epsilon}}\,dr\right)^{q}\right]
is finite for
\epsilon>0
and
1\leq q<2
. Hence, we have shown, for some constant
c
depending on
\epsilon
and
q
, that

(\mathbb{E}\|u_{t}-u_{s}\|^{q})^{1/q}\leq c\,\left|t-s\right|^{1/2-\epsilon}.

2. Let
\mathcal{R}=\{\min\{u_{i}(t),u_{i}(s)\}<\Delta\}
. Then,
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R})\leq\mathbb{P}\left(u_{i}(t)<\Delta\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(u_{i}(s)<\Delta\right)
. By Chebyshev’s inequality,
\mathbb{P}\left(u_{i}(t)<\Delta\right)\leq\Delta^{2-\epsilon}\,\mathbb{E}\left[u_{i}(t)^{\epsilon-2}\right]
for
\epsilon>0
. Hence, using
Corollary

2.3
, for a constant
c
,
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)\leq c\,\Delta^{2-\epsilon}
. Now, split the expectation with respect to
\mathcal{R}
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}(s)|}{u_{i}(s)\,u_{i}(t)}\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}(s)|}{u_{i}(s)\,u_{i}(t)}1_{\mathcal{R}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}(s)|}{u_{i}(s)\,u_{i}(t)}(1-1_{\mathcal{R}})\right].

We estimate each term separately.

For conjugate exponents
(p,p^{\prime})
, Holder’s inequality applied to the first term provides

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}(s)|}{u_{i}(s)\,u_{i}(t)}1_{\mathcal{R}}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{u_{i}(t)}-\frac{1}{u_{i}(s)}\right|^{p}\right]\right)^{1/p}\;\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)^{1/{p^{\prime}}}.

Choose
p^{\prime}=2+\epsilon
. Then its conjugate exponent
p
is less than
2
, so that
\mathbb{E}\left[u_{i}^{-p}\right]<\infty
as a result of Corollary
2.3
. As
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R})\leq c\,\Delta^{2-\epsilon}
for some
c
,
the first term is bounded by
c\,\Delta^{(2-\epsilon)/(2+\epsilon)}=c\,\Delta^{1-2\epsilon/(2+\epsilon)}
.

For the second term, write

\frac{|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}(s)|}{u_{i}(s)\,u_{i}(t)}(1-1_{\mathcal{R}})=\frac{|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}(s)|^{1-\epsilon}}{|u_{i}(s)\,u_{i}(t)|^{1-\epsilon}}\,\frac{|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}(s)|^{\epsilon}}{|u_{i}(s)\,u_{i}(t)|^{\epsilon}}\,(1-1_{\mathcal{R}}).

For
(p,p^{\prime})
conjugate (different to the ones above),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}(s)|}{u_{i}(s)\,u_{i}(t)}(1-1_{\mathcal{R}})\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}(s)|^{(1-\epsilon)p}}{|u_{i}(s)\,u_{i}(t)|^{(1-\epsilon)p}}\right]\right)^{1/p}\,\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}(s)|^{\epsilon\,p^{\prime}}}{|u_{i}(s)\,u_{i}(t)|^{\epsilon\,p^{\prime}}}(1-1_{\mathcal{R}})\right]\right)^{1/p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}(s)|^{(1-\epsilon)\,p}}{|u_{i}(s)\,u_{i}(t)|^{(1-\epsilon)\,p}}\right]\right)^{1/p}\,\Delta^{-2\epsilon}\,\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}(s)|^{\epsilon\,p^{\prime}}}\right]\right)^{1/p^{\prime}}.

In the last line, we exploit
u_{i}(s),u_{i}(t)>\Delta
outside
\mathcal{R}
.
The third factor is
O(|t-s|^{\epsilon/2})
by part 1 if
1<\epsilon p^{\prime}<2
. Choose
p^{\prime}=3/2\epsilon
. Then
p=1/(1-1/p^{\prime})=1/(1-(2/3)\epsilon)
.

Focus now on the first factor: for
(r,r^{\prime})
conjugate,

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}(s)|^{(1-\epsilon)\,p}}{|u_{i}(s)\,u_{i}(t)|^{(1-\epsilon)\,p}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}(s)|^{(1-\epsilon)\,p\,r^{\prime}}}\right]\right)^{1/r^{\prime}}\,\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{|u_{i}(s)\,u_{i}(t)|^{(1-\epsilon)\,p\,r}}\right]\right)^{1/r}.

This is a product of two terms. The first term is
O(|t-s|^{(1-\epsilon)\,p/2})
by part 1 with
q=(1-\epsilon)\,p\,r^{\prime}
.
The second term is finite if
(1-\epsilon)\,p\,r<1
(by
Corollary

2.3
and
2\,a\,b\leq a^{2}+b^{2}
).
We can achieve
(1-\epsilon)\,p\,r<1
for any
\epsilon>0
, by choosing
1<r<1/(1-\epsilon)p=(1-(2/3)\epsilon)/(1-\epsilon)
. The resulting estimate is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}(s)|}{u_{i}(s)\,u_{i}(t)}(1-1_{\mathcal{R}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq c\,\left|t-s\right|^{(1-\epsilon)/2}\,\Delta^{-\epsilon}\,\left|t-s\right|^{\epsilon/2}=c\,|t-s|^{1/2}\,\Delta^{-\epsilon}.

We have shown that

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|u_{i}(t)-u_{i}(s)|}{u_{i}(s)\,u_{i}(t)}\right]\leq c\,\Delta^{1-\epsilon}+c\,|t-s|^{1/2}\,\Delta^{-\epsilon}.

The right-hand side behaves like
\Delta^{1-2\epsilon/(2+\epsilon)}+\left|t-s\right|^{1/2}\Delta^{-2\epsilon}
. Taking
\Delta=\left|t-s\right|^{\alpha}
and equating powers of
\left|t-s\right|
leads to
\alpha\left(1-2\epsilon/(2+\epsilon)\right)=\frac{1}{2}-2\alpha\epsilon
. This implies an upper bound of
c\left|t-s\right|^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\epsilon}{2}}
provided that
\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
∎

2.2
Links to the OU process and CIR process

We highlight a link to some well-known SDEs, namely, the
Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (CIR) process
and
Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

(
Feller, 1951
)
. Girsanov’s theorem allows us to transfer properties of these SDEs to our dynamics and therefore provides an alternative way of establishing well-posedness and strong Feller properties. This link will also serve as inspiration for the development of numerical schemes in
Section

3
.

When the data term
G\equiv 0
, our Langevin dynamics decouples into some well-known SDEs for modeling interest rates. In particular, the
Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (CIR) process
is

dy_{t}=\kappa\,(\mu-y_{t})\,dt+\theta\sqrt{y_{t}}\,dW_{t}.

(10)

This is known to have unique solutions with
\mathbb{P}(y_{t}>0,\forall t\geq 0)=1
if and only if the Feller test holds, which occurs for
2\kappa\mu\geq\theta^{2}
.
In this case, by defining
\hat{u}_{t}=\sqrt{y_{t}}
(this is the so-called Lamperti transformation), Itô’s formula implies that

d\hat{u}_{t}=\frac{(4\kappa\mu-\theta^{2})/8}{\hat{u}_{t}}\,dt-\frac{\kappa}{2}\,\hat{u}_{t}\,dt+\frac{\theta}{2}\,dW_{t}.

(11)

This is precisely our
u
process when
G\equiv 0
, with
\theta=2\sqrt{2/\beta}
,
\kappa=2\lambda
and
\mu=2/(\beta\lambda)
. Note that this corresponds to the edge case of the Feller test as
2\kappa\mu=\theta^{2}

(
Feller, 1951
;
Glasserman, 2004
)
.

The
Ornstein–-Uhlenbeck process
is given by

d\hat{v}_{t}=-\lambda\,\hat{v}_{t}\,dt+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\,dW_{t}^{2}.

This is precisely our
v
process with
G\equiv 0
.

Given the connections mentioned above,
the general idea for establishing well-posedness of our SDE is to interpret (
7
) as ‘perturbed’ CIR and OU processes. Girsanov transformations will allow us to absorb the data term into the Brownian motion and hence inherit the required properties from CIR and OU processes. The results shown here can also be derived using the (simpler) arguments involving the cylindrical transformation
Eq.

8
; however, as a bonus, the arguments here additionally show that
u(t)
is positive almost surely.

In the following, let
\hat{z}_{t}\coloneq(\hat{u},\hat{v})_{t}
satisfy

d\hat{z}_{t}=\biggl[-\lambda z_{t}+\binom{1/(\beta\hat{u}_{t})}{0}\biggr]\,dt+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\,dW_{t},

(12)

with given initial data
\hat{z}_{0}
. The first component
\hat{u}_{t}
of
\hat{z}_{t}
is a CIR process. The CIR process is positive almost surely as, following
Dereich et al. (2011)
, each component has the form
d\hat{u}_{t}=((4\kappa\mu-\theta^{2})/(8\hat{u}_{t}))\,dt-\frac{1}{2}\kappa\hat{u}_{t}\,dt+(\theta/2)\,d\hat{W}_{t}
for
\theta^{2}=8/\beta
,
\kappa=2\lambda
, and
\mu=2/(\beta\lambda)
, which satisfies the Feller positivity condition
2\kappa\mu\geq\theta^{2}
with equality.

In order to apply Girsanov transformations, we first establish the Novikov condition.

Lemma 2.5

(Novikov condition)
.

Let
Assumption

1
hold and
b(u,v)=-\binom{v\odot\nabla G(u\odot v)}{u\odot\nabla G(u\odot v)}
. For all
T>0
and
\gamma>0
, we have

\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp\biggl(\gamma\int_{0}^{T}\left\|b(\hat{z}_{s})\right\|^{2}\,ds\biggr)\biggr]<\infty.

There exists a weak solution
(u_{t},v_{t})
of
Eq.

7
that is unique in law. Furthermore,
u_{t}>0
almost surely.

Proof.

By
(
Karatzas and Shreve, 1991
, Corollary 5.14)
, it is enough to find an
\epsilon>0
such that, for all
t\in[0,T]
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{t}^{t+\epsilon}\gamma\left\|b(\hat{z}_{s})\right\|^{2}\right)ds\right]<\infty.

Since
B=\left\|\nabla G\right\|_{\infty}<\infty
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\gamma\int_{t}^{t+\epsilon}\left\|b(\hat{z}_{s})\right\|^{2}\,ds\right)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\gamma\,B^{2}\int_{t}^{t+\epsilon}\left\|\hat{z}_{s}\right\|^{2}\,ds\right)\right].

By Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{t}^{t+\epsilon}\gamma\,B^{2}\,\left\|\hat{z}_{s}\right\|^{2}\,ds\right)\right]\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{t}^{t+\epsilon}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\exp\left(\epsilon\,\gamma\,B^{2}\,\left\|\hat{z}_{s}\right\|^{2}\right)\Bigr]\,ds.

(13)

We now apply Itô’s lemma to bound
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\exp(\epsilon\,\gamma\,B^{2}\left\|\hat{z}_{s}\right\|^{2})\bigr]
.
Given
\alpha>0
and
\hat{y}_{t}\coloneq\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|(\hat{u}_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\right\|^{2}\right)
,
we get

\displaystyle d\hat{y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(-\lambda\alpha+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta}\right)\,\left\|\hat{z}_{t}\right\|^{2}y_{t}\,dt+\left(\frac{3\alpha d}{\beta}\right)\,\hat{y}_{t}\,dt+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\alpha\hat{y}_{t}\left(\hat{u}_{t}^{\top}dW^{1}_{t}+v_{t}^{\top}dW^{2}_{t}\right).

Taking expectation,

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat{y}_{t}\bigr]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(-\lambda\alpha+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta}\right)\left\|\hat{z}_{t}\right\|^{2}\hat{y}_{t}+\left(\frac{3\alpha d}{\beta}\right)\hat{y}_{t}\right].

Provided that
\alpha<\beta\lambda
, we have
\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat{y}_{t}\bigr]\leq\left(\frac{3\alpha d}{\beta}\right)\mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat{y}_{t}\bigr]
and therefore

\mathbb{E}\bigl[y_{t}\bigr]\leq\hat{y}_{0}\exp\biggl(\left(\frac{3\alpha d}{\beta}\right)t\biggr),

where
\hat{y}_{0}=\exp(\alpha\left\|z_{0}\right\|^{2}/2)<\infty
.
Letting
\alpha=2\epsilon\gamma B^{2}
, the condition on
\alpha
is satisfied if
\epsilon<\beta\lambda/(2\gamma B^{2})
.
Substituting for
\alpha
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat{y}_{t}\bigr]\leq y_{0}\exp\biggl(2\epsilon\gamma B^{2}\left(\frac{3d}{\beta}\right)t\biggr).

Returning to (
13
), for all
t\leq T-\epsilon
, we see

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{t}^{t+\epsilon}\gamma B^{2}\left\|\hat{z}_{s}\right\|^{2}\right)ds\right]\leq\hat{y}_{0}\exp\biggl(2\epsilon\gamma B^{2}\left(\frac{3d}{\beta}\right)T\biggr).

Thus, the Novikov condition is satisfied.

We can therefore apply Girsanov’s theorem. Then, a solution of
Eq.

7
exists and the solution is unique in law
(
Karatzas and Shreve, 1991
)
. Further, for all
T>0
, with the martingale defined by
M_{T}=\exp(\int_{0}^{T}b(\hat{z}_{t})\cdot dW_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}b(\hat{z}_{t})^{2}dt)
, we see
\mathbb{P}(\exists t\in[0,T],\;u_{t}=0)=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[1_{\{\exists t\in[0,T],\;\hat{u}_{t}=0\}}M_{T}\Bigr]
.
Since the CIR process
\hat{u}_{t}
is strictly positive almost surely,
\mathbb{P}(\exists t\in[0,T],\;u_{t}=0)=0
.
By the Borel–Cantelli lemma, there are only finitely many
T
for which the event
\{\exists t\in[0,T],\;u_{t}=0\}
occurs so
\mathbb{P}(u_{t}>0,t>0)=1
.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
Assumption

1
hold. Then,
(u,v)_{t}
is strong Feller.

Proof.

If
b(x_{s})\equiv 0
, then the processes
(u,v)_{t}
decouple component-wise into CIR and OU processes. Sufficient conditions are provided by
(
Maslowski and Seidler, 2000
, Theorem 2.1)
to guarantee that Girsanov transformed processes are strong Feller. We check the conditions of their theorem: (i) is satisfied since each of these components is strong Feller with known densities. Condition (ii) is due to the Novikov condition proven above. Condition (iii) holds due to
(
Maslowski and Seidler, 2000
, Remark 2.3)
along with Lemma
2.5
.
∎

2.3
Link to Riemannian Langevin diffusion

When sampling distributions on non-Euclidean spaces,
Riemannian Langevin dynamics
(
Girolami and Calderhead, 2011
)
are typically more efficient than standard Langevin dynamics since they scale the gradient to account for the geometry of the manifold. In general, if sampling from
\pi(\mathbf{z})\propto\exp(-H(\mathbf{z}))
on some manifold
\mathcal{M}
with metric
\mathfrak{g}
, the Riemannian Langevin dynamics are
Xifara et al. (2014)

d\mathbf{z}_{t}=-\mathfrak{g}(\mathbf{z}_{t})^{-1}\nabla H(\mathbf{z}_{t})\,dt+\Gamma(\mathbf{z}_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{g}(\mathbf{z}_{t})^{-1/2}dW_{t},

(14)

where
\Gamma_{j}(\mathbf{z})=\sum_{k}\partial_{k}[(\mathfrak{g}(\mathbf{z})^{-1})_{jk}]
.
The last two terms correspond to Brownian motion on the manifold
(\mathcal{M},\mathfrak{g})
with the correction drift term taking into account the curvature of the manifold. The special setting where the metric is the Hessian of some convex Legendre-type function is called ‘Mirror-Langevin’. For example, in the case of sampling probability densities restricted to the positive real plane
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
or the simplex, a natural choice is
\mathfrak{g}(\mathbf{z})=\nabla^{2}\mathcal{H}(z)
where
\mathcal{H}
is the Boltzmann–Shannon entropy
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{z})=\sum_{i}z_{i}\log(z_{i})-z_{i}

(
Zhang et al., 2020
)
.

Recall from (
4
) for
\beta=1
that
\rho(x)
is the marginal distribution to the following distribution on
\mathbf{z}=(\eta,x)\in\mathcal{X}=\real_{+}^{d}\times\real^{d}
:
\tilde{\rho}(\eta,x)\propto\exp(-H(\eta,x))
where

H(\eta,x)\coloneq G(x)+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{8\eta_{i}}+2\lambda\eta_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\log(\eta_{i}).

In the following proposition, we see that our Langevin dynamics (
7
) on
(u,v)
corresponds to Riemannian Langevin dynamics on
{\mathbf{z}}
with metric
\mathfrak{g}({\mathbf{z}})\coloneq M({\mathbf{z}})^{-1}
, with
M({\mathbf{z}})
as given in the proposition below. The proof is a direct consequence of Itô’s formula and given in Appendix
B
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
(u,v)_{t}
follow the SDE (
7
) with
\beta=1
. Then, letting
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}=(\eta,x)_{t}
with
\eta=\frac{1}{4}u^{2}
and
x=u\odot v
, we have that
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}
follows (
14
) with inverse metric

\mathfrak{g}({\mathbf{z}})^{-1}=M({\mathbf{z}})\coloneq\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{diag}(\eta)&\mathrm{diag}(x)/2\\
\mathrm{diag}(x)/2&\mathrm{diag}(x^{2}/(4\eta))+4\mathrm{diag}(\eta)\end{pmatrix}.

The distribution
\rho(t,\cdot)
of
\mathbf{z}(t)
solves the Fokker–Planck equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho=\mathrm{div}\left({}M(\mathbf{z})\nabla{\left({}H(\mathbf{z})+\log(\rho)\right)}\rho\right).

(15)

Remark
.

By direct computations, one can check that
for
\phi(\mathbf{z})=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\left|x_{i}\right|^{2}}{\eta_{i}}+2\eta_{i}+4\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{H}(\eta_{i}),

where
\mathcal{H}(\eta)=\left(\eta\log(\eta)-\eta\right)
, the corresponding metric is

\mathfrak{g}(\mathbf{z})=M(\mathbf{z})^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Id}_{d}&0_{d\times d}\\
0_{d\times d}&\mathrm{Id}_{d}\end{pmatrix}\nabla^{2}\phi(z)\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Id}_{d}&0_{d\times d}\\
0_{d\times d}&\mathrm{Id}_{d}\end{pmatrix}.

2.4
Ergodicity

Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the infinitesimal generator of
Eq.

7
and
\rho(t,\cdot)
denote the distribution of
(u,v)_{t}
.
Then
(e^{\mathcal{L}t}\phi)(X)=\mathbb{E}\left[\phi((u,v)_{t})\mid(u,v)_{0}=X\right]
for measurable functions
\phi\colon\mathcal{X}\to\real
. We seek to show
that
e^{\mathcal{L}\,t}\phi
converges to
\pi(\phi)=\int\phi(x)\,d\pi(x)
or, alternatively,
\rho_{t}
converges to
\pi
.

If the data term
G
is uniformly bounded, then one could invoke standard theories (Bakry-Emery criterion) to deduce ergodicity; see Appendix
A
. This is, however, a strong assumption. In the following, we prove a different ergodicity result that assumes
x^{\top}\nabla G(x)
is bounded below and which holds for many unbounded examples, including
G(x)=\left\|Ax-y\right\|^{2}
.
The proof depends on the theory of Lyapunov–Foster drift conditions
(
Meyn and Tweedie, 2012
;
Lelièvre and Stoltz, 2016
)
.

For the Lyapunov function
V(u,v)=1+\left\|(u,v)\right\|^{2}
, let
L^{\infty}_{V}
denote the Banach space of measurable functions
\phi\colon\mathcal{X}\to\real
such that the norm
\left\|\phi\right\|^{\infty}_{V}\coloneq\sup_{x}\left|\phi(x)/V(x)\right|<\infty.

Theorem 2.8
.

Under
Assumption

1
, the solution
(u,v)_{t}
of
Eq.

7
converges to the invariant
\pi
geometrically. In particular, for
V(u,v)=1+\left\|(u,v)\right\|^{2}
and some
C,\rho>0
,

\left\|\smash{e^{\mathcal{L}\,t}\phi-\pi(\phi)}\right\|^{\infty}_{V}\leq C\exp(-\rho\,t)\left\|\phi-\pi(\phi)\right\|^{\infty}_{V},\qquad t>0,\,\phi\in L^{\infty}_{V}.

Proof.

In discrete time, this follows
(
Lelièvre and Stoltz, 2016
, Theorem 2.25)
if a Lyapunov condition holds for
V
and a minorization condition holds for the transition density. The extension to continuous time
(
Lelièvre and Stoltz, 2016
, Equation 2.77)
is achieved by writing
t=k{\Delta t}+s
to create a Markov chain on discrete time
k
and correction factor depending on
s\in(0,{\Delta t})
.

The process
(u,v)_{t}
is strong Feller and irreducible by
Theorem

2.1
. Hence, the minorization conditions hold for any compact set
(
Mattingly et al., 2002
, Lemma 2.3)
.

Under
Assumption

1
,
x^{\top}\nabla G(x)\geq-K
for some constant
K\in\mathbb{R}
. We have, from
Eq.

7
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}V(u,v)

\displaystyle=-2\,\lambda\,\|(u,v)\|^{2}-4(u\odot v)^{\top}\nabla G(u\odot v)+2d+4\,d/\beta\leq-2\,\lambda\,\|(u,v)\|^{2}+4K+2d+4\,d/\beta.

Let
c
be a constant, such that
0<c<2\lambda
and
b=4K+2d+4d/\beta+c>0
. We have
\mathcal{L}V(u,v)\leq-c\,V(u,v)+b
as required.
∎

3
Discretization

We consider the following implicit-explicit time-stepping approximation: for time step
{\Delta t}
, we seek an approximation
(u,v)^{k}
to
(u,v)_{k{\Delta t}}
via

\displaystyle\begin{split}u^{k+1}-u^{k}&=-\lambda\,u^{k+1}\,{\Delta t}-v^{k}\odot\nabla G(u^{k}\odot v^{k})\,{\Delta t}+\frac{1}{\beta\,u^{k+1}}\,{\Delta t}+\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\beta}}\,{\Delta W}^{1}_{k},\\
v^{k+1}-v^{k}&=-\lambda\,v^{k+1}\,{\Delta t}-u^{k}\odot\nabla G(u^{k}\odot v^{k})\,{\Delta t}+\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\beta}}\,{\Delta W}^{2}_{k},\end{split}

(16)

where
{\Delta W}^{i}_{k}=W^{i}_{t_{k+1}}-W^{i}_{t_{k}}
. This scheme is inspired by the numerical scheme of
Dereich et al. (2011)
for the CIR process (corresponding to the
u
update with
G\equiv 0
). It is explicit in the potential term
G
; the implicitness in the regularisation terms ensures positivity in
u

and is straightforward to implement
since

u^{k+1}=\frac{\tilde{u}^{k}+\sqrt{\tilde{u}^{k}\odot\tilde{u}^{k}+4{\Delta t}(1+{\Delta t}\lambda)/\beta}}{2(1+{\Delta t}\lambda)}

with
\tilde{u}^{k}=u^{k}-{\Delta t}\,v^{k}\odot\nabla G(u^{k}\odot v^{k})+\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\beta}}\,{\Delta W}^{1}_{k}
.

For convenience, define the explicit part of the drift term as
f
, so that

f(u,v)\coloneq\binom{f_{1}(u,v)}{f_{2}(u,v)}\coloneq\binom{v\odot\nabla G(u\odot v)}{u\odot\nabla G(u\odot v)}.

(17)

Remark

(Relation to proximal gradient Langevin)
.

By defining
\Psi(u,v)\coloneq\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|(u,v)\right\|^{2}-\beta\log(u)
,
(u,v)^{k+1}=\Pi_{{\Delta t}}(z^{k+1})
,
one can write (
16
) as

\displaystyle z^{k+1}=\Pi_{{\Delta t}}(z^{k})-{\Delta t}\,f(\Pi_{{\Delta t}}(z^{k}))+\sqrt{{2/\beta}}\,\Delta W_{k},

(18)

where
\Pi_{{\Delta t}}(z)=\mathrm{Prox}_{{\Delta t}\Psi}(z)=\mathrm{argmin}_{x}({\Delta t}\,\Psi(x)+\frac{1}{2}\left\|x-z\right\|)
is a non-expansive operator.
This corresponds to an application of the proximal gradient Langevin (PGLD) algorithm introduced in
Durmus et al. (2019)
. Note however that their analysis does not extend to our setting since
f
is non-convex and lacks global Lipschitz properties.

We establish convergence of our discretized scheme on a fixed time interval
[0,T]
under the following assumptions.
The assumption on the initial distribution
\rho_{0}
is satisfied for example if
\rho_{0}
has a continuous density with compact support.

Assumption 2
.

(i)

G
satisfies
Assumption

1
and
\nabla G
is
L
-Lipschitz.

(ii)

(u^{0},v^{0})
and
(u(0),v(0))
follow an initial distribution
\rho_{0}\ll\pi
such that
\frac{\rho_{0}}{\pi}
is uniformly bounded.

For our analysis, we will frequently use the local Lipschitz property of
f
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Under
Assumption

2
,
the function
f
defined in (
17
) is locally Lipschitz on the ball of radius
R
with Lipschitz constant
C_{R}=2\,L\,R^{2}+B
:

\left\|f(u,v)-f(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\right\|\leq C_{R}\left\|(u-u^{\prime},v-v^{\prime})\right\|

for all
(u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\left\|(u,v)\right\|,\left\|(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\right\|\leq R
.

Proof.

Under
Assumption

2
,
\nabla G
is
L
-Lipschitz,
B\coloneq\sup_{x}\left\|\nabla G(x)\right\|<\infty
,
Hence, for
v,\tilde{v}
such that
\|v\|\leq R,\|\tilde{v}\|\leq R
,
\left\|v\odot\nabla G(u\odot v)-\tilde{v}\odot\nabla G(\tilde{u}\odot\tilde{v})\right\|
is upper bounded by

\displaystyle B\left\|\tilde{v}-v\right\|+\left\|v\right\|\left\|\nabla G(u\odot v)-\nabla G(\tilde{u}\odot\tilde{v})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(B+2LR^{2}\right)\Bigl(\left\|v-\tilde{v}\right\|\vee\left\|u-\tilde{u}\right\|\Bigr).\qed

3.1
Convergence to stationary distribution

We show in
Theorem

3.2
that the numerical approximation
(u,v)^{k}
indeed has an invariant distribution.

We first introduce some notation.
For
n>0
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and Borel sets
A\subset\mathcal{X}
, let
P^{n}(\mu_{0},A)\coloneq\mathbb{P}(X^{n}\in A\mid X^{0}\sim\mu_{0})
for initial distribution
\mu_{0}
. For
\phi\colon\mathcal{X}\to\real
,

(P^{n}\phi)(x)\coloneq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi(y)P^{n}(\delta_{x},dy)=\mathbb{E}[\phi(X^{n})\mid X^{0}=x].

Given
{\zeta}>0
, define
\left\|\phi\right\|_{\zeta}\coloneq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\left|\phi(x)\right|}{1+{\zeta}\,V(x)}
and

d_{\zeta}(x,y)\coloneq\begin{cases}0,&x=y,\\
2+{\zeta}\,V(x)+{\zeta}\,V(y),&x\neq y.\end{cases}

The argument is based on showing
P^{n}
is a contraction with respect to

d_{\zeta}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\coloneq\sup_{\lvert\!\lvert\!\lvert{\phi}\rvert\!\rvert\!\rvert_{\zeta}=1}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi(x)\,d(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})(x),\quad\text{for measures $\mu_{1},\mu_{2}$}

with
\lvert\!\lvert\!\lvert{\phi}\rvert\!\rvert\!\rvert_{\zeta}\coloneq\sup_{x\neq y}\left|\phi(x)-\phi(y)\right|/d_{\zeta}(x,y)
. Following
Hairer and Mattingly (2011)
,
d_{\zeta}
is equivalent to the weighted TV norm
\left\|\mu\right\|_{\zeta}\coloneq\sup_{\left\|\phi\right\|_{\zeta}\leq 1}\left|\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi(x)\,d\mu(x)\right|
.

Theorem 3.2
.

There exists
\bar{\alpha}\in(0,1)
,
t_{0}>0
and
{\zeta}>0
such that, for any
\phi\colon\mathcal{X}\to\real
with
\left\|\phi\right\|_{\zeta}\leq 1
,

\left|P^{\lceil t_{0}/{\Delta t}\rceil}\phi(x)-P^{\lceil t_{0}/{\Delta t}\rceil}\phi(y)\right|\leq\bar{\alpha}\,d_{\zeta}(x,y),

(19)

holds for all
{\Delta t}
sufficiently small.
As a consequence, there exists an invariant measure
\pi_{{\Delta t}}
such that, for any initial distribution
\mu_{0}
and any
T>0
,

\left\|P^{\lceil T/{\Delta t}\rceil}(\mu_{0},\cdot)-\pi_{{\Delta t}}\right\|_{\zeta}\leq\bar{\rho}^{T}\left\|\mu_{0}-\pi_{\Delta t}\right\|_{\zeta},

(20)

where
\rho=\bar{\alpha}^{1/t_{0}}
is independent of
{\Delta t}
.

This is a consequence of minorisation and Lyapunov drift conditions, given in the following lemmas.

Lemma 3.3

(drift condition)
.

Let
V(u,v)=1+\|(u,v)\|^{2}
.
Assume that
{\Delta t}<\min\{\lambda/B^{2},1/4\lambda\}
, where
B=\|\nabla G\|_{\infty}
. Then, the numerical approximation
(u^{k},v^{k})
defined by
Eq.

16
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[V((u,v)^{k+1})\mid(u,v)^{k}\right]\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\lambda\,{\Delta t}\right)\,V((u,v)^{k})+C\,{\Delta t},

where
C\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\lambda+4(8+K\beta+d)/\beta
.

Let
V_{4}(u,v)=1+\left\|(u,v)\right\|^{4}
. There exists
C,c,\nu>0
such that for all
{\Delta t}<c\lambda
,

\mathbb{E}[V_{4}((u,v)^{k+1})\mid(u,v)^{k}]\leq(1-\nu\,{\Delta t})\,V_{4}((u,v)^{k})+C\,{\Delta t}.

Proof.

We prove the drift condition for the quadratic functional
V
; the calculation for the quartic
V_{4}
is provided in the Appendix
C.2
. We have

\displaystyle(1+\lambda{\Delta t})u^{k+1}-\frac{{\Delta t}}{\beta u^{k+1}}=u^{k}-v^{k}\odot\nabla G(u^{k}\odot v^{k})\,{\Delta t}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\,{\Delta W}^{1}_{k},

(21)

\displaystyle(1+\lambda{\Delta t})v^{k+1}=v^{k}-u^{k}\odot\nabla G(u^{k}\odot v^{k})\,{\Delta t}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\,{\Delta W}^{2}_{k}.

(22)

We square the norm and expectation of the above, use the independence between the increments
{\Delta W}^{i}_{k}
and the information at time
t_{k}
, and rely on the trivial fact that
\|(u^{k+1})^{-1}\odot(u^{k+1})\|^{2}=d
.
As a result, conditional on step
k
, we obtain

\displaystyle(1+\lambda\,{\Delta t})^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\smash{u^{k+1}}\right\|^{2}\mid(u,v)^{k}\right]-2{\Delta t}(1+\lambda\,{\Delta t})\beta^{-1}d\leq{\frac{2}{\beta}}\,\mathbb{E}\left\|\smash{{\Delta W}^{1}_{k}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\smash{u^{k}-{\Delta t}\,v^{k}\odot\nabla G(u^{k}\odot v^{k})}\right\|^{2},

\displaystyle(1+\lambda\,{\Delta t})^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\smash{v^{k+1}}\right\|^{2}\mid(u,v)^{k}\right]=\frac{2}{\beta}\,\mathbb{E}\left\|\smash{{\Delta W}^{2}_{k}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\smash{v^{k}-{\Delta t}\,u^{k}\odot\nabla G(u^{k}\odot v^{k})}\right\|^{2}.

Using the assumption
x^{\top}\nabla G(x)\geq-K
and
B=\left\|\nabla G\right\|_{\infty}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\smash{u^{k}-{\Delta t}\,v^{k}\odot\nabla G(u^{k}\odot v^{k})}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\smash{u^{k}}\right\|^{2}+{\Delta t}^{2}B^{2}\left\|\smash{v^{k}}\right\|^{2}-2\,\Delta t\,(u^{k}\odot v^{k})^{\top}\nabla G(u^{k}\odot v^{k})

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\smash{u^{k}}\right\|^{2}+{\Delta t}^{2}B^{2}\left\|\smash{v^{k}}\right\|^{2}+2\,K\,{\Delta t}.

A similar statement holds for
\left\|v^{k}-{\Delta t}\,u^{k}\odot\nabla G(u^{k}\odot v^{k})\right\|^{2}
.
Combining this with
\mathbb{E}\left\|{\Delta W}^{i}_{k}\right\|^{2}=d\,\Delta t
, we obtain

\displaystyle(1+\lambda\,{\Delta t})^{2}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k+1}}\right\|^{2}\bigm\lvert(u,v)^{k}\Bigr]\leq(1+{\Delta t}^{2}B^{2})\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k}}\right\|^{2}+4{\frac{d\,(1+{\Delta t}\,\lambda)+K\,\beta+d}{\beta}}\,{\Delta t}.

Note that

\frac{(1+{\Delta t}^{2}B^{2})}{(1+\lambda{\Delta t})^{2}}\leq\frac{(1+{\Delta t}^{2}B^{2})}{1+2\lambda{\Delta t}}<1-\frac{1}{2}\lambda\,{\Delta t}

provided that
0<{\Delta t}<\min\{\lambda/B^{2},1/4\lambda\}
. Rewrite this in terms of
V(u,v)=1+\left\|(u,v)\right\|^{2}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[V((u,v)^{k+1})\Bigm\lvert(u,v)^{k}\Bigr]\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\lambda{\Delta t}\right)V((u,v)^{k})+\left(\frac{1}{2}\lambda+4\frac{4\,(1+{\Delta t}\lambda)+K\,\beta+d}{\beta(1+\lambda{\Delta t})^{2}}\right)\,{\Delta t}.

Using the property
{\Delta t}\,\lambda\leq 1
, we can replace the last term by
C{\Delta t}
with
C\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\lambda+4(8+K\beta+d)/\beta
.
∎

By first establishing
Lemmas

3.4
and
3.5
, we prove the coupling condition in
Corollary

3.6
for the numerical method.

Lemma 3.4

(escape probability)
.

Fix
0<r<R
. Let
k_{R}=\inf\left\{k:\;\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k}}\right\|>R\right\}
and assume that
\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{0}}\right\|\leq r
. For some constants
c,C>0
, we have, for
n{\Delta t}=T
and
{\Delta t}\leq c
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(k_{R}\leq n)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{2CT}\right)\exp\left(\frac{r^{2}}{CT}+2\right).

The constant
C
depends only on
\beta
and the coercivity constant of
G
.

Proof.

The start of this proof is similar to the proof of Lemma
3.3
but we retain the Brownian increments: taking the square-norm of (
21
)
and using
\left\|u^{k}-v^{k}\odot\nabla G(u^{k}\odot v^{k}){\Delta t}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|u^{k}\right\|^{2}+{\Delta t}^{2}B^{2}\left\|v^{k}\right\|^{2}+2K{\Delta t}
,
we obtain

(1+\lambda{\Delta t})^{2}\left\|\smash{u^{k+1}}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|\smash{u^{k}}\right\|^{2}+{\Delta t}^{2}B^{2}\left\|\smash{v^{k}}\right\|^{2}+2K{\Delta t}+Z^{1}_{k}+2d{\Delta t}/\beta+2d{\Delta t}(1+\lambda{\Delta t})/\beta,

where

Z^{1}_{k}\coloneq\frac{2}{\beta}(\left\|\Delta W^{1}_{k}\right\|^{2}-d{\Delta t})+\sqrt{\frac{8}{\beta}}\left<\Delta W^{1}_{k},u^{k}-v^{k}\odot\nabla G(u^{k}\odot v^{k}){\Delta t}\right>
.

A similar computation for
v^{k}
with
Z^{2}_{k}\coloneq\frac{2}{\beta}(\left\|\Delta W^{2}_{k}\right\|^{2}-d{\Delta t})+\sqrt{\frac{8}{\beta}}\left<\Delta W^{2}_{k},v^{k}-u^{k}\odot\nabla G(u^{k}\odot v^{k}){\Delta t}\right>
yields

(1+\lambda{\Delta t})^{2}\left\|\smash{v^{k+1}}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|\smash{v^{k}}\right\|^{2}+{\Delta t}^{2}B^{2}\left\|\smash{u^{k}}\right\|^{2}+2K{\Delta t}+Z^{2}_{k}+2d{\Delta t}/\beta.

We therefore have constants
c,C>0
such that for all
{\Delta t}\leq c
,

(1+\lambda{\Delta t})^{2}\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k+1}}\right\|^{2}\leq(1+B^{2}{\Delta t}^{2})\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k}}\right\|^{2}+Z_{k}^{1}+Z_{k}^{2}+C{\Delta t}.

Let
\xi_{k}\coloneq Z^{1}_{k}+Z^{2}_{k}
and let
\mathcal{F}_{n}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\Delta W^{j}_{k}
for
j=1,2
and
k=1,\dots,n-1
.
Note that
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq C({\Delta t}^{2}+(\left\|\smash{u^{k}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\smash{v^{k}}\right\|^{2}){\Delta t})
for some
C>0
.
Let
M_{n}\coloneq\sum_{k<n}\xi_{k}
. This is a martingale with quadratic variation
\langle M\rangle_{n}
satisfying

\langle M\rangle_{n}=\sum_{k<n}\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{k}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\leq C\sum_{k<n}\left({\Delta t}^{2}+(\left\|\smash{u^{k}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\smash{v^{k}}\right\|^{2})\,{\Delta t}\right).

(23)

Using this notation, we can rewrite the above inequality as

(1+\lambda{\Delta t})^{2}\,\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k+1}}\right\|^{2}\leq\left(1+B^{2}{\Delta t}^{2}\right)\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k}}\right\|^{2}+C{\Delta t}+\xi_{k}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]+\frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}].

(24)

Absorbing the variation term.

Equation (
24
) can be written as

\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k+1}}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k}}\right\|^{2}+C{\Delta t}+\xi_{k}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\langle M\rangle_{k+1}-\langle M\rangle_{k}\right).

Summing this over the
k
’s implies that

\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k}}\right\|^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\langle M\rangle_{k}\leq\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{0}}\right\|^{2}+C\,k\,{\Delta t}+\sum_{j<k}(\xi_{j}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\mathbb{E}[\xi_{j}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{j}]).

By moving
\frac{\alpha}{2}\langle M\rangle_{k}
to the RHS and applying (
23
), it follows that,

\displaystyle\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{0}}\right\|^{2}+C\,k\,{\Delta t}+\left(M_{k}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\langle M\rangle_{k}\right)+\frac{\alpha C}{2}k{\Delta t}^{2}+\frac{\alpha C}{2}\sup_{j<k}\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{j}}\right\|^{2}k{\Delta t}.

So, for all
n{\Delta t}\leq T
and all
k\leq n
,

\left(1-\frac{\alpha CT}{2}\right)\sup_{k\leq n}\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k}}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{0}}\right\|^{2}+C(1+\alpha{\Delta t}/2)\,T+\sup_{k\leq n}\left(M_{k}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\langle M\rangle_{k}\right).

Estimate on escape probability.

We now apply
(
Lamba et al., 2007
, Lemma A.1)
:
Let
\alpha=1/(C\,T)
.
For
n{\Delta t}=T
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sup_{k\leq n}\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k}}\right\|^{2}\geq\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{0}}\right\|^{2}+2CT+R\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{k\leq n}\left(M_{k}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\langle M\rangle_{k}\right)\geq R\right)\leq e^{-R/(C\,T)}.

(25)

So, for
n{\Delta t}=T
, as
k_{R}
is the first escape time from the ball of radius
R
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(k_{R}\leq n)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{k\leq n}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k}}\right\|^{2}\geq\frac{R^{2}}{2}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{2CT}+\frac{\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{0}}\right\|^{2}}{CT}+2\right).\qed

The first coupling lemma considers the coupling event (i.e.,
X^{n}=Y^{n}
) of two realisations
X^{n},Y^{n}
of the numerical method for paths that do not leave the ball (i.e.,
X^{k},Y^{k}\in B(0,R)
for
k=0,\dots,n-1
).

Lemma 3.5

(coupling in the ball)
.

Consider initial data
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
with
\left\|x\right\|,\left\|y\right\|\leq r
, some
r>0
. Consider two solutions
(u,v)^{n}
of the numerical method driven by different Brownian motions, denoted
X^{n},Y^{n}
with initial condition
X^{0}=x
,
Y^{0}=y
. For
R>0
and
t_{0}>0
, let

E=\{X^{1},\dots,X^{n-1},Y^{1},\dots,Y^{n-1}\in B(0,R)\}
and
\tilde{E}=E\cap\{X^{n}\neq Y^{n}\}
where
n\,\Delta t=t_{0}
.

We may couple
X^{n},Y^{n}
such that the following property holds: if
t_{0}=1/C_{R}
and
\Delta t\in(0,t_{0}]
and
r\sqrt{3\,C_{R}}\geq 1
, then
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{E})\leq 1-\tilde{\delta}
with

\displaystyle\tilde{\delta}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}(1+r\sqrt{3\,C_{R}})}\exp\left(-\frac{3\,r^{2}}{2}\,C_{R}\right)
.

Proof.

The assumptions for
(
De Bortoli and Durmus, 2020
, Theorem 4)
hold: Assumption A1 holds due to the non-expansivity of
\Pi_{\Delta t}
in
Eq.

18
; and Assumption A2(
A
) holds as the drift is Lipschitz on
B(0,R)
with Lipschitz constant
L=C_{R}
(
Lemma

3.1
). Hence, we may couple
X^{n},Y^{n}
so that
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{E})\leq 1-\delta
with

\displaystyle\delta=2\inf_{x,y\in B(0,r)}\Phi\left(-\|x-y\|/2\,\Xi_{n}^{1/2}(C_{R})\right),

(26)

where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function for the standard Gaussian,
C_{R}
is the Lipschitz constant of the drift on the set
B(0,R)
, and

\displaystyle\Xi_{n}(L)=\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{n}(1+\Delta t\,L)^{-k}
.

For
\mu=1/(1+\Delta t\,C_{R})
, then
1-\mu=\Delta t\,C_{R}\,\mu
and

\Xi_{n}(C_{R})={\Delta t}\,\mu\frac{1-\mu^{n}}{1-\mu}=\frac{1-\mu^{n}}{C_{R}}\geq\frac{1-\exp(-t_{0}\,C_{R}/(1+\Delta t\,C_{R}))}{C_{R}},\qquad n\Delta t=t_{0},

(as
1-1/x\leq\log x
and so
1/(1+x)\leq\exp(-x/(1+x))
for
x>0
). For
t_{0}=1/C_{R}
,

\Xi_{n}(C_{R})\geq\frac{1}{C_{R}}(1-\exp(-1/2))\geq\frac{1}{3\,C_{R}}.

It is well known (
Gordon (1941)
) that
\Phi(-x)\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-x^{2}/2\right)\frac{1}{x+1/x}
for
x>0
, and so

\delta=2\,\Phi\left(-r\,\sqrt{3\,C_{R}}\right)\geq\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}(1+r\sqrt{3\,C_{R}})}\exp\left(-\frac{3\,r^{2}}{2}\,C_{R}\right)\eqcolon\tilde{\delta},

for
r\sqrt{3C_{R}}\geq 1
. We conclude that
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{E})\leq 1-\tilde{\delta}
.
∎

Corollary 3.6

(coupling)
.

Under the assumptions of
Lemma

3.5
, for all
r>0
, there exists
t_{0}>0
, so that

\mathbb{P}(X^{n}=Y^{n})\geq\alpha>0
,
uniformly for
x,y\in B(0,r)
and
0<\Delta t\leq t_{0}
with
n\Delta t=t_{0}
.

Proof.

Let

E=\{X^{1},\dots,X^{n-1},Y^{1},\dots,Y^{n-1}\in B(0,R)\}
and
\tilde{E}=E\cap\{X^{n}\neq Y^{n}\}
where
n\,\Delta t=t_{0}
. Then, if
C_{R}
is sufficiently large,
t_{0}=1/C_{R}
and
\Delta t\in(0,t_{0}]
, we have
\mathbb{P}(E)\geq 1-\delta
and
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{E})\leq 1-\tilde{\delta}
by
Lemmas

3.5
and
3.4
, with

\displaystyle\delta=2\exp\left(-\frac{C_{R}R^{2}}{2C}\right)\exp\left(\frac{C_{R}\,r^{2}}{C}+2\right),\qquad\tilde{\delta}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}(1+r\sqrt{3\,C_{R}})}\exp\left(-\frac{3\,r^{2}}{2}\,C_{R}\right).

Notice that
\tilde{E}^{c}
is the union of
\{X^{n}=Y^{n}\}
and
E^{c}
. Thus,

\mathbb{P}(X^{n}=Y^{n})\geq\mathbb{P}(\tilde{E}^{c})-\mathbb{P}(E^{c})\geq\tilde{\delta}-\delta\eqcolon\alpha
.
Then,

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}(1+r\sqrt{3\,C_{R}})}\exp\left(-\frac{3\,r^{2}}{2}\,C_{R}\right)-2\exp\left(-\frac{C_{R}R^{2}}{2C}\right)\exp\left(\frac{C_{R}\,r^{2}}{C}+2\right).

Neglecting terms in
r
, and using the scaling
R^{2}\lesssim C_{R}\lesssim R^{2}
(see Lemma
3.1
), we may choose
R
large enough so
\alpha>0
.
∎

We now show the drift and coupling conditions imply geometric convergence to the invariant measure.

Proof of
Theorem

3.2
.

From Lemma
3.3
,
there exists
\nu>0
and
C>0
such that, for
V(x)=\|x\|^{2}+1
and
V_{4}(x)=\|x\|^{4}+1
,

P^{n}(V)(x)\leq(1-\nu\,{\Delta t})^{n}V(x)+C\,n\,{\Delta t},\qquad P^{n}(V_{4})(x)\leq(1-\nu\,{\Delta t})^{n}V_{4}(x)+C\,n\,{\Delta t}.

The constants
\nu
and
C
are independent of
{\Delta t}
sufficiently small.
Choose
r>0
large enough that
-\frac{1}{2}\nu+2C/r^{2}<0
. Corresponding to this
r
, choose
t_{0}
according to
Corollary

3.6
. Then, for
n=\lceil t_{0}/{\Delta t}\rceil
,

\displaystyle P^{n}(V)(x)\leq\gamma\,V(x)+C_{1},\qquad P^{n}(V_{4})(x)\leq\gamma\,V_{4}(x)+C_{1},

(27)

with
\gamma=\exp(-\nu\,t_{0})
and
C_{1}=C\,t_{0}
and
{\Delta t}\leq 1/2\nu
(as
(1-\nu\,{\Delta t})^{n}\leq\exp(-\nu\,t_{0})
). Later, we will exploit the inequality
\gamma+2C_{1}/r^{2}<1
(which holds as
\exp(-x)\leq 1-x/2
for
0<x\leq 1
).

We closely follow the proof of
Hairer and Mattingly (2011)
: the first part A), where we consider the setting of
V(x)+V(y)\geq r^{2}
, uses only the drift condition and is identical but repeated here verbatim only for completeness; the second part, for the case
V(x)+V(y)>r^{2}
, makes a small adaptation of the proof to exploit our coupling probability.

A) Using the drift condition.

To show the contraction
Eq.

19
when
V(x)+V(y)\geq r^{2}
, we simply make use of the drift condition and choose
{\zeta}
accordingly:

\displaystyle P^{n}(\phi)(x)-P^{n}(\phi)(y)

\displaystyle\leq 2+{\zeta}\,P^{n}(V)(x)+{\zeta}\,P^{n}(V)(y)\leq 2+{\zeta}\,\gamma\,(V(x)+V(y))+2\,{\zeta}\,C_{1},

where we make use of
\left\|\phi\right\|_{\zeta}\leq 1
for the first inequality and the drift condition for the second inequality. Since
V(x)+V(y)\geq r^{2}
, by choosing
\gamma_{0}=\gamma+2C_{1}/r^{2}<1
, we have

2+{\zeta}\gamma(V(x)+V(y))+2{\zeta}C_{1}\leq 2+{\zeta}\gamma_{0}(V(x)+V(y))\leq 2\gamma_{1}+{\zeta}\gamma_{1}(V(x)+V(y)),

where the last inequality is due to
2(1-\gamma_{1})\leq{\zeta}(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{0})r^{2}\leq{\zeta}(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{0})(V(x)+V(y))
by choice of
\gamma_{1}\coloneq\frac{2+{\zeta}\gamma_{0}r^{2}}{(2+{\zeta}r^{2})}<1
.

B) Using the coupling probability.

We now consider the setting where
V(x)+V(y)\leq r^{2}
, so
x,y\in B(0,r)
and
Corollary

3.6
yields
\mathbb{P}(X^{n}\neq Y^{n})\leq 1-\alpha
.
Given a test function
\phi
such that
\left\|\phi\right\|_{\zeta}\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\phi(X^{n})-\phi(Y^{n}))1_{\{X^{n}\neq Y^{n}\}}]

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\mathbb{P}(X^{n}\neq Y^{n})+{\zeta}\,\mathbb{E}\left[(V(X^{n})+V(Y^{n}))1_{\{X^{n}\neq Y^{n}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2(1-\alpha)+{\zeta}\sqrt{(1-\alpha)}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[V(X^{n})^{2}+V(Y^{n})^{2}]}.

Using the drift condition on
V_{4}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\phi(X^{n})-\phi(Y^{n})]

\displaystyle\leq 2(1-\alpha)+{\zeta}\sqrt{(1-\alpha)}\sqrt{\gamma V(x)^{2}+\gamma V(y)^{2}+2C_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq 2(1-\alpha)+{\zeta}\sqrt{(1-\alpha)\gamma}\left(V(x)+V(y)+\sqrt{2C_{1}/\gamma}\right).

Choose
{\zeta}
such that
2(1-\alpha)+{\zeta}\sqrt{2C_{1}(1-\alpha)}<2(1-\tilde{\alpha})
for some
0<\tilde{\alpha}<\alpha
; that is,

{\zeta}<2(\alpha-\tilde{\alpha})/\sqrt{2C_{1}(1-\alpha)}
.
With
\gamma_{2}\coloneq\max((1-\tilde{\alpha}),\sqrt{(1-\alpha)\gamma})
, we now have

\left|P^{n}(\phi)(x)-P^{n}(\phi)(y)\right|\leq 2(1-\tilde{\alpha})+{\zeta}\sqrt{(1-\alpha)\gamma}(V(x)+V(y))\leq\gamma_{2}d_{\zeta}(x,y).

Putting A) and B) together, we see
d_{\zeta}(P^{n}(\mu_{1},\cdot),P^{n}(\mu_{2},\cdot))\leq\bar{\alpha}\,d_{\zeta}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
, and
P^{n}
is a contraction with respect to
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\zeta}
with contraction constant
\bar{\alpha}\coloneq\min(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
. This implies geometric convergence to the invariant measure in the equivalent weighted TV norm as stated in
Eq.

20
.
∎

3.2
Finite-time convergence of discretized scheme

For
t_{k}=k\,{\Delta t}
, let
e_{1}(t_{k})\coloneq u_{t_{k}}-u^{k}
and
e_{2}(t_{k})\coloneq v_{t_{k}}-v^{k}
, and
e(t_{k})\coloneq(e_{1}(t_{k}),e_{2}(t_{k}))
. Our main convergence result is as follows.

Theorem 3.7
.

Suppose that
Assumption

2
holds. Then, for any
R>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t_{k}\leq T}\left\|e(t_{k})\right\|\right]\leq c_{R}\,d\,\exp\left(2\,T\,C_{R}\right){\Delta t}^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}+C\,\sqrt{T}\,e^{-C\,(R^{2}/T)},

where
c_{R}
and
C_{R}
depends on
R
. We can choose
R
and
{\Delta t}
to make the right-hand side arbitrarily small, and hence
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t_{k}\leq T}\left\|e(t_{k})\right\|\right]\to 0
as
{\Delta t}\to 0
.

The rest of this subsection is dedicated to proving
Theorem

3.7
. Before proceeding with the proof, we first point out the two main technical challenges that we address.

Limited inverse moments.
As mentioned, our implicit-explicit scheme with
G\equiv 0
is the same as the scheme proposed in
Dereich et al. (2011)
, who studied the discretization scheme for the Lamperti-transformed version of the CIR process (
10
) under the parameter choice
2\kappa\mu>\theta^{2}
. Their main result is the strong error bound
\mathbb{E}\biggl[\sup_{0\leq t_{k}\leq T}\left\|u_{t_{k}}-u^{k}\right\|\biggr]=\mathcal{O}({\Delta t}^{\frac{1}{2}}).
Their analysis required inverse-moment bounds of the form
\mathbb{E}[u_{t}^{-2}]<\infty
: this is
false
when
2\kappa\mu=\theta^{2}
, which is precisely the case for our
u
process when
G\equiv 0
.

To overcome this, we derive inverse-moment bounds of order up to but excluding 2 (we will make use of
Corollary

2.3
and
Proposition

2.4
in our proof). As a result, in the case where
G\equiv 0
, our proof shows that the discretized CIR process with parameters
2\kappa\mu=\theta^{2}
satisfies for any
\epsilon>0
, the strong error bound
\mathbb{E}\biggl[\sup_{0\leq t_{k}\leq T}\left\|u_{t_{k}}-u^{k}\right\|\biggr]=\mathcal{O}({\Delta t}^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon})
. We remark that similar ideas and error bounds were applied in
Hutzenthaler et al. (2014)
.

Lack of global Lipschitzness.
The drift in
Eq.

16
comprises
the explicit part
f
and implicit part
S_{\lambda}(u)\coloneq\frac{1}{\beta\,u}-\lambda u
. The second difficulty is that neither
S_{\lambda}
nor
f
are globally Lipschitz. In particular,
f
is at best only locally Lipschitz. To handle this, we will follow the analysis of
Higham et al. (2002)
to stop our process whenever
\left\|(u,v)\right\|\geq R
and upper bound
\mathbb{P}(\left\|(u,v)_{t}\right\|\geq R)
. Intuitively, this can be done since our stationary distribution has exponentially decaying tails.

The proof of
Theorem

3.7
is a consequence of
Proposition

3.9
below, which shows convergence for a stopped process confined to a ball of radius
R
. While we manage to show
{\Delta t}^{1/2-\epsilon}
rate for a stopped process (restricted to the ball of radius
R
), our main convergence result,
Theorem

3.7
, does not provide a rate because our error bound has an exponential dependence on
C_{R}
and hence
R
.

Define the following stopping times

\displaystyle k_{R}\coloneq\inf\left\{k\geq 0:\;\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k}}\right\|\geq R\right\},\quad\gamma_{R}\coloneq\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\;\left\|(u,v)_{t}\right\|\geq R\right\}

(28)

and
\theta_{R}\coloneq k_{R}{\Delta t}\wedge\gamma_{R}
.
We have estimates on the tail behaviour of
k_{R}
in
Lemma

3.4
; similar estimates holds for
\gamma_{R}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

For some
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}>0
independent of
T
,
R
and
r
, the following holds for
x\in B(0,r)
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\gamma_{R}<T\right)\leq\exp(-c_{1}\,R^{2})\,\exp(c_{2}(r^{2}+c_{3}\,T)).

Proof.

A calculation with Itô’s formula for
V(u,v)=1+\|(u,v)\|^{2}
shows that, for some
C>0

dV(x_{t})\leq-2\,\lambda\,V(x_{t})\,dt+C\,dt+dM_{t},

where
M_{t}=2\sqrt{2/\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\langle{x^{t},dW_{t}}\rangle
. This is a martingale with quadratic variation

\langle M\rangle_{t}=\frac{8}{\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|x_{s}\right\|^{2}\,ds
.
For
\alpha=\lambda\,\beta/4
,

\displaystyle V(x_{t})

\displaystyle\leq V(x_{0})-2\,\lambda\int_{0}^{t}V(x_{s})\,ds+\frac{1}{2}\alpha\frac{8}{\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|x_{s}\right\|^{2}\,ds+C\,t+M_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha\langle M\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle\leq V(x_{0})+C\,t+M_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha\langle M\rangle_{t}.

Then, by Doob’s martingale inequality,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(V(x_{t})-V(x_{0})-C\,t\right)\geq R\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(M_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha\langle M\rangle_{t}\right)\geq R\right)\leq\exp(-R\,\alpha).

As
\gamma_{R}
is the time of first escape from
B(0,R)
,
V(x_{\gamma_{R}})=R^{2}+1
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\gamma_{R}<T\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(V(x_{t})-V(x_{0})-C\,t\right)\geq R^{2}-\left\|x_{0}\right\|^{2}-c\,T\right)\leq\exp(-(R^{2}-r^{2}-cT)\lambda\,\beta/4)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-R^{2}\,\lambda\,\beta/4)\,\exp((r^{2}+C\,T)\lambda\,\beta/4).\qed

Up to time
\theta_{R}
, we have the following error.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let the assumptions of
Theorem

3.7
hold and
{\Delta t}\leq\frac{1}{2C_{R}}
.
For all
\epsilon>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t_{k}\leq T}\left\|e(t_{k}\wedge\theta_{R})\right\|\right]\leq c\,\exp(2\,T\,C_{R})\,{\Delta t}^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon},

(29)

where
c
that depends on
\epsilon
,
C_{R}
,
d
, and
\lambda
.

Proof.

In the following, assume that
t_{k}\leq\theta_{R}
.

We first subtract the discretized equations with the continuous counterparts,

\displaystyle u_{t_{k+1}}=u_{t_{k}}+\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\left(-\lambda u_{t}+v_{t}\odot\nabla G(u_{t}\odot v_{t})+\frac{1}{u_{t}}\right)dt+\sqrt{2/\beta}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}dW^{1}_{t},

\displaystyle v_{t_{k+1}}=v_{t_{k}}+\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\left(-\lambda v_{t}+u_{t}\odot G(u_{t}\odot v_{t})\right)dt+\sqrt{2/\beta}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}dW^{2}_{t},

to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}e_{1}(t_{k+1})&=e_{1}(t_{k})-{\Delta t}\,\left(f_{1}((u,v)_{t_{k}})-f_{1}((u,v)^{k})\right)+{\Delta t}\,\left(S_{\lambda}(u_{t_{k+1}})-S_{\lambda}(u^{k+1})\right)+r_{1,k},\end{split}

(30)

\displaystyle\begin{split}e_{2}(t_{k+1})&=e_{2}(t_{k})-\lambda\,{\Delta t}\,e_{2}(t_{k+1})-{\Delta t}\,\left(f_{2}((u,v)_{t_{k}})-f_{2}((u,v)^{k})\right)+r_{2,k},\end{split}

(31)

where

\displaystyle r_{1,k}

\displaystyle\coloneq\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\left(f_{1}((u,v)_{t_{k}})-f_{1}((u,v)_{t})\right)dt+\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\left(S_{\lambda}(u_{t})-S_{\lambda}(u_{t_{k+1}})\right)\,dt,

\displaystyle r_{2,k}

\displaystyle\coloneq\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\left(f_{2}((u,v)_{t_{k}})-f_{2}((u,v)_{t})\right)\,dt-\lambda\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\left(v_{t}-v_{t_{k+1}}\right)\,dt.

Taking inner product of (
30
) with
e_{1}(t_{k+1})
and (
31
) with
e_{2}(t_{k+1})
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}+\lambda\,{\Delta t}-\frac{C_{R}\,{\Delta t}}{2}\right)\left\|e_{1}(t_{k+1})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|e_{1}(t_{k})\right\|^{2}+\frac{C_{R}\,{\Delta t}}{2}\left\|(e_{1}(t_{k}),e_{2}(t_{k}))\right\|^{2}+\left\|r_{1,k}\right\|\,\left\|e_{1}(t_{k+1})\right\|,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}+\lambda\,{\Delta t}-\frac{C_{R}\,{\Delta t}}{2}\right)\left\|e_{2}(t_{k+1})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|e_{2}(t_{k})\right\|^{2}+\frac{C_{R}\,{\Delta t}}{2}\left\|(e_{1}(t_{k}),e_{2}(t_{k}))\right\|^{2}+\left\|r_{2,k}\right\|\,\left\|e_{2}(t_{k+1})\right\|,

where we used the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, that
f_{i}
is
C_{R}
-Lipschitz, and that

\bigl(S_{\lambda}(u_{t_{k+1}})-S_{\lambda}(u^{k+1})\bigr)^{\top}e_{1}(t_{k+1})\leq-\lambda\left\|e_{1}(t_{k+1})\right\|^{2}

due to monotonicity arguments. Denote
E_{k}=\left\|(e_{1}(t_{k}),e_{2}(t_{k}))\right\|
and
R_{k}=\left\|(r_{1,k},r_{2,k})\right\|
and sum the two equations to find

\displaystyle\left(1+2\,\lambda\,{\Delta t}-C_{R}\,{\Delta t}\right)E_{k+1}^{2}\leq\left(1+2\,C_{R}\,{\Delta t}\right)E_{k}^{2}+2\,E_{k+1}R_{k}.

Define
c_{1}=\left(1+2\,C_{R}\,{\Delta t}\right)/\left(1+2\lambda\,{\Delta t}-\,C_{R}\,{\Delta t}\right)
and
c_{2}=2/\left(1+2\,\lambda\,{\Delta t}-C_{R}\,{\Delta t}\right)
. Then, for
t_{k+1}\leq\theta_{R}\wedge M
,

\displaystyle E_{k+1}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}E_{k}^{2}+c_{2}E_{k+1}R_{k}\leq c_{1}^{k+1}E_{0}^{2}+c_{2}\sum_{j=0}^{k}c_{1}^{j}E_{k+1-j}R_{k-j}\leq c_{2}\sup_{k\leq M\wedge\theta_{R}}E_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}c_{1}^{j}R_{k-j}.

We therefore have

\mathbb{E}\biggl[\sup_{k\leq M\wedge\theta_{R}}E_{k}\biggr]\leq c_{2}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}c_{1}^{j}\,\mathbb{E}[R_{k-j}].

Note that for
{\Delta t}\leq 1/(2C_{R})
, we have
c_{1}\leq(1+2\,{\Delta t}\,C_{R})^{2}
: this is a consequence of the simple inequality
(1-a)^{-1}\leq 1+2a
applied to
a={\Delta t}\,C_{R}\leq 1/2
.
Also using the geometric sum formula and the inequality
e^{z}\geq 1+z
for
z\geq 0
, we get

\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}c_{1}^{j}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}(1+2\,{\Delta t}\,C_{R})^{2j}\leq\frac{\exp(2\,C_{R}\,M\,{\Delta t})}{4\,C_{R}\,{\Delta t}+4\,C_{R}^{2}\,{\Delta t}^{2}}.

On the other hand,
\mathbb{E}[R_{k}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\left\|r_{1,k}\right\|]+\mathbb{E}[\left\|r_{2,k}\right\|]
.
Observe that by
Proposition

2.4
and the definition
S_{\lambda}
in (
17
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|r_{1,k}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\mathbb{E}\left\|f_{1}((u,v)_{t_{k}})-f_{1}((u,v)_{t})\right\|+\lambda\mathbb{E}\left\|u_{t}-u_{t_{k+1}}\right\|\,dt+\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left|u_{i,t}-u_{i,t_{k+1}}\right|}{u_{i,t}u_{i,t_{k+1}}}\right]\,dt

\displaystyle\leq c\left((C_{R}+\lambda)\,{\Delta t}^{3/2}+d\,{\Delta t}^{\frac{3}{2}-\epsilon}\right).

Similarly,

\mathbb{E}[\left\|r_{2,k}\right\|]\leq c\,(C_{R}+\lambda)\,{\Delta t}^{3/2}.

It follows that

\mathbb{E}\biggl[{\sup_{0\leq t_{k}\leq T\wedge\theta_{R}}E_{k}}\biggr]\leq c_{3}\,\exp(2\,C_{R}\,T)\,{\Delta t}^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon},
for a constant
c_{3}
that depends on
\lambda
,
C_{R}
, and
d
, as required.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main result.

Proof of
Theorem

3.7
.

Define
\tau_{R}\coloneq k_{R}{\Delta t}
(see
Eq.

28
).
First note that
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t_{k}\leq T}\left\|e(t_{k})\right\|\right]\leq A+B

4
Conclusion and outlook

We proposed a new approach for sampling distributions with the Laplace prior via a Hadamard parameterization. Our resulting Hadamard–Langevin Dynamics can be seen as an implicit form of Riemannian Langevin. The analysis in this work covered well-posedness of the continuous-time dynamics, strong convergence of the discretized dynamics, as well as geometric ergodicity results in both continuous and discrete time. These results provide a theoretical foundation for developing sampling schemes based on overparameterized Langevin dynamics. In the following, we detail the main remaining missing piece in our analysis of the Hadamard parametrization, and the challenges in extending this to other regularizers.

Theoretical limitations.

On a theoretical level, for our numerical scheme, we established a strong convergence result (i.e., convergence as stepsize diminishes over a fixed time interval), and convergence of the invariant distribution
\pi_{\Delta t}
for the discretized process. However, we are not able to establish quantitative rates of convergence. As mentioned in
(
Lelièvre and Stoltz, 2016
, Section 3)
, such convergence rates are typically obtained by understanding the Taylor expansion of the semigroup operator
P_{{\Delta t}}\phi=\mathbb{E}[\phi(x_{t_{k+1}})\mid x_{t_{k}}=\cdot]
. The difficulty of a theoretical proof is that these Taylor expansions would include singular
1/u
terms and this lack of differentiability prevent the direct use of results such as
(
Lelièvre and Stoltz, 2016
, Theorem 3.3)
. One important direction of future work is to quantify the bias of
\pi_{{\Delta t}}
and to provide quantitative strong convergence rates.

.

Other sparse priors.

Although this study is specific to the Laplace prior, one central idea is the development of Langevin dynamics based on Gaussian mixture representation of this prior. We first remark that the extension to the group-
\ell_{1}
prior is trivial.
The group-
\ell_{1}
prior is
\left\|x\right\|_{1,2}\coloneq\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left\|x_{\mathbf{b}_{j}}\right\|
, where
\cup_{j=1}^{K}\mathbf{b}_{j}
is a partition of
\left\{1,\ldots,d\right\}
. In this case, one can consider for
(u,v)\in\mathcal{X}=\real_{+}^{K}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

\pi(u,v)\propto\prod_{i=1}^{K}u_{i}\exp\left(-\beta\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|(u,v)\right\|^{2}+G(u\odot v)\right)\right),

where
u\odot v\coloneq(u_{i}\,v_{\mathbf{b}_{i}})_{i=1}^{K}
. Samples of
\pi(x)\propto\exp\left(-\beta\left\|x\right\|_{1,2}-\beta G(x)\right)
can be retrieved by sampling from
\pi
and forming the product
u\odot v
as before. For this setting,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{K}_{+}
while
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
K\leq d
, and the only difference here is the different definition of
\odot
. Likewise,
the Langevin dynamics are identical to the
\ell_{1}
setting, except
\odot
and the different dimensions of
u
and
v
.

A natural question is how to handle priors corresponding to
\ell_{q}
semi-norms with
q\in(0,1)
. These priors also have a Gaussian mixture representation
(
West, 1987
)

e^{-|z|^{q}}\propto\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\eta}}\,e^{-z^{2}/(2\eta)}\,\frac{1}{\eta^{3/2}}\,g_{q/2}\left(\frac{1}{2\eta}\right)\,d\eta,

where
g_{q/2}
is the density of a positive stable random variable with index
q/2
. Unfortunately, this has no closed-form expression in general so it is unclear how to perform Langevin sampling for such priors. Nonetheless, given the developments of our present work, it is perhaps conceivable that one could construct new sparsity-inducing priors and efficient Langevin sampling schemes via Gaussian-mixture representations, and we believe that this is an exciting direction for future work. In particular, recent works such as
(
Calvetti and Somersalo, 2024
)
derived generalizations of the hierarchical samplers for the Bayesian lasso to new sparsity inducing Gaussian mixture models. Our article can be seen as a first attempt at deriving Langevin dynamics for such priors.

Ergodicity under bounded assumptions

Consider two measures

) gives the following geometric convergence of

Proof.

In terms of the density

for

and squaring this gives

\displaystyle\left\|u^{k}-v^{k}\odot\nabla G(u^{k}\odot v^{k}){\Delta t}\right\|^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\smash{u^{k}}\right\|^{4}+{\Delta t}^{4}B^{4}\left\|\smash{v^{k}}\right\|^{4}+2\left\|\smash{u^{k}}\right\|^{2}{\Delta t}^{2}B^{2}\left\|\smash{v^{k}}\right\|^{2}+4K{\Delta t}\left\|\smash{u^{k}}\right\|^{2}+4K{\Delta t}^{3}B^{2}\left\|\smash{v^{k}}\right\|^{2}+4K^{2}{\Delta t}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+{\Delta t}^{2}B^{2})\left\|\smash{u^{k}}\right\|^{4}+(B^{2}{\Delta t}^{2}+{\Delta t}^{4}B^{4})\left\|\smash{v^{k}}\right\|^{4}

\displaystyle\qquad+\alpha_{3}{\Delta t}\left\|\smash{u^{k}}\right\|^{4}+\frac{4K^{2}{\Delta t}}{\alpha_{3}}+\alpha_{3}{\Delta t}^{2}\left\|\smash{v^{k}}\right\|^{4}+\frac{4K^{2}{\Delta t}^{4}B^{4}}{\alpha_{3}}+4K^{2}{\Delta t}^{2}

\displaystyle=(1+\alpha_{3}{\Delta t}+{\Delta t}^{2}B^{2})\left\|\smash{u^{k}}\right\|^{4}+{\Delta t}^{2}(\alpha_{3}+B^{2}+{\Delta t}^{2}B^{4})\left\|\smash{v^{k}}\right\|^{4}+4K^{2}{\Delta t}(\alpha_{3}^{-1}+{\Delta t}^{3}B^{4}\alpha_{3}^{-1}+{\Delta t}).

It follows that

\displaystyle\left((1+\lambda{\Delta t})^{4}-\alpha_{1}{\Delta t}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\smash{u^{k+1}}\right\|^{4}\mid(u,v)^{k}\right]\leq c_{1}\left\|\smash{u^{k}}\right\|^{4}+c_{2}{\Delta t}^{2}\left\|\smash{v^{k}}\right\|^{4}+c_{3}{\Delta t},

(SM39)

where

c_{1}=(1+\alpha_{2}{\Delta t})(1+\alpha_{3}{\Delta t}+{\Delta t}^{2}B^{2}),\qquad c_{2}=(1+\alpha_{2}{\Delta t})(\alpha_{3}+B^{2}+{\Delta t}^{2}B^{4}),

and

c_{3}=(1+\alpha_{2}{\Delta t})4K^{2}(\alpha_{3}^{-1}+{\Delta t}^{3}B^{4}\alpha_{3}^{-1}+{\Delta t})+\frac{64d^{2}}{\beta^{2}\alpha_{2}}+\frac{4d^{2}(1+\lambda{\Delta t})^{6}}{\alpha_{1}\beta^{2}}.

One can carry out a similar computation for
v^{k+1}
to obtain

(1+\lambda{\Delta t})^{4}\mathbb{E}[\left\|\smash{v^{k+1}}\right\|^{4}\mid(u,v)^{k}]\leq c_{1}\left\|\smash{v^{k}}\right\|^{4}+c_{2}{\Delta t}^{2}\left\|\smash{u^{k}}\right\|^{4}+\tilde{c}_{3}{\Delta t},

with

\tilde{c}_{3}=(1+\alpha_{2}{\Delta t})4K^{2}(\alpha_{3}^{-1}+{\Delta t}^{3}B^{4}\alpha_{3}^{-1}+{\Delta t})+\frac{64d^{2}}{\beta^{2}\alpha_{2}}.

It follows that

\displaystyle((1+\lambda{\Delta t})^{4}-\alpha_{1}{\Delta t})\,\mathbb{E}[\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k+1}}\right\|^{4}\mid(u,v)^{k}]\leq(c_{1}+c_{2}{\Delta t}^{2})\left\|\smash{(u,v)^{k}}\right\|^{4}+2c_{3}{\Delta t}.

(SM40)

Finally, define

\displaystyle\alpha\coloneq\frac{c_{1}+c_{2}{\Delta t}^{2}}{(1+\lambda{\Delta t})^{4}-\alpha_{1}{\Delta t}}.

(SM41)

Then,
\alpha<1
provided that

\alpha_{1}{\Delta t}+(1+\alpha_{2}{\Delta t})(1+\alpha_{3}{\Delta t}+{\Delta t}^{2}B^{2})+{\Delta t}^{2}(1+\alpha_{2}{\Delta t})(\alpha_{3}+B^{2}+{\Delta t}^{2}B^{4})<(1+\lambda{\Delta t})^{4}.

The leading exponent to
{\Delta t}^{0}
on both sides is 1, and the leading exponent to
{\Delta t}
is
4\lambda
and
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}
on the right and left and side respectively. By choosing

\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\lesssim\lambda
and
{\Delta t}\lesssim\lambda
, we have
\alpha<1
. In other words, we may choose
\nu>0
and write
\alpha\leq 1-\nu{\Delta t}
for
{\Delta t}
sufficiently small.
∎
