Self-propulsion symmetries determine entropy production of active particles with hidden states
Jacob Knight, Farid Kaveh, Gunnar Pruessner

Abstract
Entropy production distinguishes equilibrium from non-equilibrium. Calculating the entropy production rate (EPR) is challenging in systems where some degrees of freedom cannot be observed. Here we introduce a perturbative framework to calculate the “partial EPR” of a canonical hidden-state system, a generic self-propelled active particle with hidden self-propulsion. We find that the parity symmetry, 𝒫 \mathcal{P} , and (time-)reversibility, 𝒯 \mathcal{T} , of the hidden variable determine partial entropy production. Non-trivial entropy production appears at least at sixth order in the self-propulsion velocity. We apply our framework to two processes which break 𝒫 \mathcal{P} - and 𝒯 \mathcal{T} -symmetries respectively: an asymmetric telegraph process and diffusion with stochastic resetting.

;

;
Hartich and Godec 2023

;

;
Caprini et al. 2019

Discussion and Outlook:

As a rule of thumb, the harder it is to qualitatively distinguish forward and reversed spatial trajectories of a process, the higher the order of the leading term in
Eq. (
10
)
. Spatial trajectories of an asymmetric RnT particle with non-zero net drift can trivially be distinguished from their time-reverse due to the swapped drift direction, and have leading order partial EPR
\propto\nu^{2}/D
,
i.e.

{n^{\!*}}=1
,
Eq. (
14
)
and Tab.
1
. Trajectories of asymmetric RnT particles with zero net drift, Fig.
1
b, differ more subtly from their time-reverse, but can be distinguished as the distribution of
\dot{x}(t)
will be different for the forward and reversed trajectories due to the parity asymmetry of
w(t)
. This is reflected in the fact that the leading order contribution is
{n^{\!*}}=3
. In the case of stochastic resetting, Fig.
1
c, time-irreversibility is very difficult to see with the naked eye but reveals itself through a sudden jump to zero velocity,
e.g.
at
t=1.3,5.1
. Such a faint effect results in the leading order contribution to the partial EPR appearing at
{n^{\!*}}=4
.

Entropy production measures “how broken”
\mathcal{T}
-symmetry is
Gaspard 2004
;
Fodor et al. 2016
,
Eqs. (
2
)
and (
5
). How come the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetry and not just the
\mathcal{T}
-symmetry of
w(t)
enters intro the entropy production of
x(t)
as given by
Eq. (
1
)
? The answer is found by writing the equation of motion of
x^{R}(t)=x(T-t)
, which produces
\dot{x}^{R}(t)=-\dot{x}(T-t)=-\nu w(T-t)-\xi(T-t)
, so that the statistics of
x^{R}(t)
is identical to that of
x(t)
if the statistics of
-w(T-t)
and
-\xi(T-t)
are identical to that of
w(t)
and
\xi(t)
respectively,
i.e.
when their statistics is
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric. In other words, the reason why the
\mathcal{P}
-symmetry of
w(t)
matters for the EPR of
x(t)
is that it features on the right its time-derivative. One might think of extensions to
Eq. (
1
)
that make other symmetries of
w(t)
visible in the entropy production of
x(t)
.

To use the above formalism, the mathematical challenge is to calculate correlators and higher order moments,
Suppl.
SIII
. This is still much easier than calculating full path integrals,
Eq. (
5
)
, but can be challenging in some cases. The formalism is readily extended to incorporate potentials

Garcia-Millan and Pruessner 2021
, but are quite unwieldy.

A thermodynamic interpretation of the partial entropy production is an important topic of future work. The level of description of a system determines how it can be manipulated
(
Jaynes 1992
, Ch. 5)
. Connection could be made between partial EPR and maximum extractable power in partially hidden systems

;

.
