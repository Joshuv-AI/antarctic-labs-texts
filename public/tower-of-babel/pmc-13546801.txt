Treatment‐Free Remission in Chronic Myeloid Leukemia: Toward Precision and Personalized Approaches to Functional Cure
Authors: Hiroshi Ureshino, Shinya Kimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTreatment‐free remission (TFR), defined as the maintenance of molecular remission without resumption of therapy after tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) discontinuation, represents the clinical manifestation of functional cure in chronic‐phase chronic myeloid leukemia (CML‐CP), in which durable disease control is maintained without ongoing therapy despite the potential persistence of residual leukemic stem cells (LSCs). Emerging evidence supports a model in which outcomes after TKI discontinuation are determined by the balance between residual LSC persistence and coordinated host immune surveillance. Natural killer (NK)‐cell abundance and maturation have shown the most consistent associations with TFR, while killer immunoglobulin‐like receptor (KIR)‐human leukocyte antigen (HLA) immunogenetics may further influence NK‐cell education and antileukemic activity. Adaptive and regulatory mechanisms—including leukemia‐associated antigen‐specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes, T‐cell receptor repertoire dynamics, T‐cell exhaustion, plasmacytoid dendritic cells, and regulatory T cells—are increasingly implicated in residual disease control. Neutrophil abundance and maturation have also emerged as readily accessible candidate biomarkers. In parallel, persistent LSCs exploit BCR::ABL1‐independent survival programs, immune‐evasion mechanisms, and the protective bone marrow niche, providing a reservoir for molecular relapse. These observations argue against reliance on any single biomarker and instead support integrated models incorporating clinical variables, molecular residual disease, immune competence, immunogenetics, and LSC biology. Most candidate biomarkers remain exploratory and require independent prospective validation. Such multidimensional approaches may enable a transition from empiric TKI discontinuation toward precision, biomarker‐guided TFR and extend durable functional cure to a broader population of patients with CML.Trail Registration: N/A

Introduction

Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) has emerged as a paradigm of functional cure following the introduction of ABL1 tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). In this context, functional cure denotes durable disease control in the absence of ongoing therapy and long‐term disease progression, despite the potential persistence of residual leukemic stem cells (LSCs). Treatment‐free remission (TFR) represents the clinical realization of this concept and is defined as the maintenance of molecular remission after TKI discontinuation without the need to resume therapy [1]. With most patients now achieving near‐normal life expectancy and an increasing proportion attaining deep molecular responses (DMRs), durable TFR has become a realistic therapeutic goal, shifting the treatment paradigm from lifelong TKI administration toward carefully selected treatment discontinuation [2, 3].

Accumulating evidence suggests that durable TFR reflects a dynamic equilibrium between residual LSCs and host immune surveillance, shaped by treatment history, disease biology, and both innate and adaptive immune responses. Despite extensive investigation, no single biomarker has yet demonstrated sufficient reproducibility and validation to reliably guide TKI discontinuation in routine clinical practice. This review integrates current evidence on the biological basis, clinical determinants, immune regulation, and emerging strategies underlying durable TFR in CML. Through this integrated perspective, we highlight current limitations and emerging opportunities for moving from empiric TKI discontinuation toward precision‐guided TFR.

Biological Basis of TFR

The success of TFR is fundamentally determined by the persistence of LSCs, which can survive prolonged TKI therapy despite undetectable BCR::ABL1 transcripts. CML LSCs reside predominantly within the CD34+CD38− compartment and remain difficult to distinguish from normal hematopoietic stem cells, although markers including CD26, CD36, and interleukin‐1 receptor accessory protein (IL‐1RAP) improve their identification [4]. Unlike proliferating leukemic cells, LSCs exhibit a quiescent phenotype and survive largely through BCR::ABL1‐independent mechanisms, explaining why kinase inhibition alone rarely achieves complete eradication [5].

The bone marrow microenvironment further supports LSC persistence by maintaining quiescence and promoting drug resistance through hypoxia, stromal‐derived cytokines, metabolic adaptation, and cell‐cell interactions. Consequently, residual leukemic cells may persist for many years despite sustained DMR [5], providing the biological basis for molecular relapse following TKI discontinuation. Indeed, highly sensitive molecular assays have demonstrated persistent leukemic cells in a substantial proportion of patients who successfully maintain TFR, indicating that complete elimination of LSCs is not required for functional cure [6]. Instead, accumulating evidence suggests that durable TFR reflects a dynamic equilibrium between residual leukemia and host immune surveillance [7]. Natural killer (NK) cells, cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), and neutrophils collectively contribute to immune‐mediated control of residual disease [8]. The functional integrity of these immune networks, together with intrinsic LSC biology, ultimately determines whether residual leukemic cells remain dormant or undergo molecular recurrence after TKI discontinuation.

Clinical Evidence of TFR

The feasibility of TFR was first established by the STIM1 trial [9], which demonstrated that approximately 40% of patients with sustained complete molecular remission remained treatment‐free after imatinib discontinuation. Subsequent long‐term follow‐up confirmed the durability and safety of TFR, with virtually all patients who experienced molecular relapse rapidly regaining major molecular response (MMR) following TKI reinitiation [10, 11]. The A‐STIM study further redefined molecular relapse as loss of MMR rather than any detectable BCR::ABL1 transcript, establishing the current standard criterion for TKI resumption [12]. These findings were subsequently extended to second‐generation TKIs. Recent clinical studies evaluating dasatinib, nilotinib, and bosutinib consistently reported TFR rates of approximately 40%–60%, confirming that successful TFR is achievable irrespective of the TKI used, provided that patients achieve sustained DMR [13, 14, 15, 16]. The EURO‐SKI trial, the largest prospective TKI discontinuation study, demonstrated that prolonged TKI exposure and longer duration of DMR were major determinants of successful TFR [17]. Similarly, the Japanese J‐SKI study confirmed the importance of sustained DMR duration and further demonstrated that nearly all patients who experienced molecular relapse successfully regained MMR after TKI reinitiation, reinforcing both the efficacy and safety of TKI discontinuation in carefully selected patients [18]. Collectively, these studies established TFR as a realistic therapeutic goal in CML (Table 1).

Although nearly half of patients experience molecular relapse after the first discontinuation attempt, most rapidly regain DMR after TKI reinitiation. This observation has prompted evaluation of second TKI discontinuation (TFR2). Studies including RE‐STIM, Nilo Post‐STIM, TRAD, A‐STIM, and DAstop2 consistently demonstrated that approximately one‐third to one‐half of patients can successfully maintain TFR following a second discontinuation attempt [19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. These findings indicate that failure of the initial discontinuation does not preclude subsequent successful TFR, provided that patients re‐establish durable DMR.

Predictive Factors for Successful TFR

Successful TFR is determined by the combined effects of disease biology, treatment duration, residual disease burden, and host immunity. Among clinical variables, prolonged TKI treatment and longer duration of DMR remain the most consistent predictors of durable TFR [17, 18]. Early molecular response during TKI therapy [24] and achievement of deeper molecular response at TKI discontinuation are also associated with a lower risk of molecular relapse [25, 26]. Accurate assessment of residual disease has become increasingly important for patient selection. Digital PCR improves detection of minimal residual disease (MRD) beyond conventional quantitative PCR and identifies patients with extremely low BCR::ABL1 transcript levels who are more likely to maintain TFR [27, 28]. In addition, lineage‐specific MRD analysis has demonstrated that residual disease within the granulocytic compartment predicts molecular relapse more accurately than bulk peripheral blood monitoring [29]. Although CD26‐positive LSCs represent a promising biomarker of residual disease, their clinical utility for TFR prediction remains to be established [4]. Beyond molecular monitoring, immune competence has emerged as a critical determinant of TFR. Increasing evidence indicates that NK‐cell activity, T‐cell function, inherited immunogenetic factors, and other immune biomarkers collectively influence long‐term immune control of residual leukemia, highlighting the importance of integrating molecular and immunological biomarkers for individualized TFR prediction. No single biomarker has demonstrated sufficient predictive accuracy for routine clinical application, indicating that integrated prediction models combining clinical, molecular, and immunological parameters are likely required.

Immunological Mechanisms for TFR

Innate NK‐Cell Immunity in CML

Natural Killer Cells

NK cells are central effectors of innate antileukemic immunity and represent the most consistently identified immune correlate of successful TFR. Higher NK‐cell counts at TKI discontinuation, particularly expansion of mature CD56dimCD57+ subsets, are associated with a lower risk of molecular relapse [30, 31]. These cells display enhanced cytotoxic capacity through perforin and granzyme release and may provide sustained control of residual leukemic cells after TKI withdrawal. TKI therapy also modifies NK‐cell phenotype and function; dasatinib, in particular, can promote large granular lymphocyte expansion and NK‐cell maturation [32]. However, durable TFR appears to depend on balanced immune competence rather than persistent or excessive NK‐cell activation alone (Figure 1).

KIR–HLA Immunogenetics

NK‐cell activity is governed by the balance between activating and inhibitory receptors, among which killer immunoglobulin‐like receptors (KIRs) interacting with human leukocyte antigen (HLA) Class I molecules are particularly important [33]. Recognition of self‐HLA by inhibitory KIRs prevents autoreactivity while promoting NK‐cell education, whereas loss of HLA expression on malignant cells permits activation through missing‐self recognition [34, 35]. Extensive polymorphism in both KIR genes and their HLA ligands generates substantial interindividual variation in NK‐cell responsiveness [36].

This diversity has direct clinical relevance in CML. Absence of inhibitory KIR genes, including KIR2DL5A/B and KIR2DL5B, has been associated with superior molecular responses during TKI therapy [37, 38]. KIR genotype has also been linked to TFR outcome [39]. The POKSTIC study further showed that strong inhibitory KIR3DL1–HLA‐Bw4 combinations were associated with inferior TFR, whereas weak or noninteracting combinations predicted more favorable outcomes [40]. These findings support a model in which reduced inhibitory signaling facilitates immune control of residual leukemia after TKI discontinuation. The effect of KIR‐HLA interactions may nevertheless be context dependent. In patients previously exposed to interferon‐α (IFN‐α), the adverse impact of strong KIR3DL1–HLA‐Bw4 interactions was attenuated (Figure 2A), suggesting that immune stimulation may exploit the greater functional competence of educated NK cells [41]. Thus, missing‐self recognition and NK‐cell education are complementary rather than competing mechanisms. KIR‐HLA immunogenetics may therefore serve both as an inherited biomarker of TFR and as a framework for genotype‐guided immune modulation before TKI discontinuation.

NKG2D and Activating NK‐Cell Signaling

Natural killer group 2 member D (NKG2D) is a major activating NK‐cell receptor that recognizes stress‐induced ligands, including major histocompatibility complex class I chain‐related protein A/B (MICA/B) and UL16‐binding proteins, on malignant cells (Figure 2B). Functional polymorphisms within the NKG2D locus influence receptor expression and NK‐cell cytotoxicity [42, 43]. Specific NKG2D haplotypes have been associated with CML susceptibility and disease control during dasatinib therapy, suggesting that inherited variation in activating NK‐cell pathways may also affect the probability of successful TFR [44, 45]. However, these associations remain less extensively validated than those involving KIR–HLA interactions.

Immune Checkpoint Regulation of NK Cells

Inhibitory checkpoints provide an additional layer of NK‐cell regulation. Natural killer group 2A (NKG2A), T‐cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin and immunoreceptor tyrosine‐based inhibitory motif domains (TIGIT), T‐cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain‐containing protein 3 (TIM‐3), and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1) suppress cytotoxicity and cytokine production by attenuating activating signaling pathways [46, 47]. Increased expression of inhibitory receptors at diagnosis is associated with immune dysfunction, whereas effective TKI therapy partially restores immune competence [47, 48]. Persistent TIM‐3 expression at TKI discontinuation has been associated with molecular relapse, indicating that residual checkpoint‐mediated immune exhaustion may compromise post‐treatment surveillance [49]. Clinical evidence supporting checkpoint blockade in this setting remains limited, as existing data derive largely from small, single‐cohort observational studies rather than prospective validation.

Adaptive T‐Cell Immunity in CML

Cytotoxic T Cells

Adaptive Immune Dysfunction

CTLs play a central role in antiviral and antitumor immunity by directly recognizing and eliminating infected or malignant cells. However, under conditions of chronic antigen stimulation, these cells display increased expression of inhibitory receptors, including PD‐1, TIGIT, TIM‐3, and cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte‐associated protein 4 (CTLA‐4), reflecting a state of functional exhaustion that leads to impaired proliferation, cytokine production, and cytotoxic function [50]. Immunosuppressive populations, including Tregs and myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), are similarly expanded, further reinforcing this exhausted immune state (Figure 3A–C) [51]. Notably, PD‐1‐mediated CD8+ T‐cell exhaustion has been linked to TFR outcome. In patients undergoing imatinib discontinuation, an early rise in PD‐1+CD8+ T‐cells was associated with molecular relapse, whereas lower PD‐1 expression correlated with improved T‐ and NK‐cell function and successful TFR maintenance [52]. In a dedicated study assessing checkpoint receptors at the time of TKI cessation, TIM‐3 was the only immune‐checkpoint molecule consistently upregulated on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells—as well as on Tregs—in patients who subsequently relapsed compared with those sustaining TFR. TIM‐3 expression was also elevated on CML stem cells [49]. Together, these findings suggest that the degree and specific pattern of T‐cell exhaustion may be associated with successful immune‐mediated control after TKI cessation (Figure 3A).

Leukemia‐Associated Antigen‐Specific T‐Cell Responses

CTLs recognize leukemia‐associated antigens presented by HLA molecules and eliminate malignant cells. Growing evidence has demonstrated leukemia‐specific T‐cell responses in patients with CML, underscoring the pivotal contribution of adaptive immunity to sustained disease control. Several leukemia‐associated antigens have been characterized in CML, including Wilms tumor 1 (WT1), proteinase‐3‐derived peptide (PR1), preferentially expressed antigen in melanoma (PRAME), and CXorf48 [53, 54, 55, 56]. Antigen‐specific CTLs directed against these targets have been identified in patients with CML and are thought to contribute to the immune‐mediated containment of residual disease. Among them, WT1 remains the most extensively studied target for cancer vaccination strategies, while CXorf48‐specific CTLs have more recently emerged as a promising correlate of successful TFR following TKI discontinuation (Figure 3A).

T‐Cell Memory and TCR Repertoire

T‐cell receptor (TCR) repertoire analysis has enabled detailed characterization of T‐cell dynamics both during TKI therapy and following treatment discontinuation. Durable TFR appears to be associated with the persistence of antigen‐experienced memory CTL subsets—particularly effector memory and central memory populations capable of rapid re‐expansion upon antigen re‐encounter—together with oligoclonal TCR Vβ expansion. Emerging evidence suggests that individualized, antigen‐specific immune responses, rather than shared TCR clonotypes, underlie long‐term immune surveillance against residual LSCs (Figure 3A) [57].

Plasmacytoid Dendritic Cells

pDCs are the principal producers of Type I interferons and play a pivotal role in linking innate and adaptive immunity. Experimental studies have shown that FMS‐like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) signaling is indispensable for pDC differentiation [58], underscoring their importance in antiviral and antitumor immune responses. pDCs contribute to the activation and functional fitness of both CTLs and NK cells through Type I interferon production, whereas aberrant activation of pDCs, marked by increased CD86 expression, has been associated with defective antileukemic immunity and molecular relapse [59]. These findings support a model in which FLT3‐dependent maintenance of pDCs sustains endogenous Type I interferon, particularly IFN‐α, thereby promoting coordinated NK‐ and T‐cell surveillance and potentially limiting residual leukemic stem‐cell persistence after TKI discontinuation (Figure 3B) [60].

Regulatory T Cells

Tregs are central mediators of immune tolerance that suppress antileukemic immunity [61]. Imatinib selectively depletes effector Tregs through off‐target inhibition of lymphocyte‐specific protein tyrosine kinase (LCK) [62], whereas dasatinib similarly remodels the immune microenvironment by reducing Tregs and promoting NK‐cell maturation [63]. Recent studies further suggest that the kinetics of effector Tregs around TKI discontinuation, rather than their absolute frequency alone, are associated with TFR outcome [64]. However, these observations derive from relatively small, single‐center cohorts and require confirmation in larger, prospective studies. Moreover, patients who subsequently relapse exhibit an immunosuppressive profile characterized by increased effector Tregs together with exhausted PD‐1/TIGIT‐positive CD4+ T cells before TKI discontinuation, indicating that preservation of an appropriate balance between immune activation and regulation is critical for durable TFR (Figure 3C) [49].

Neutrophil Biology and TFR

Beyond lymphocyte‐mediated immunity, neutrophils have recently emerged as clinically accessible biomarkers of TFR. Higher peripheral neutrophil counts at TKI discontinuation are associated with successful TFR [65, 66]. More recently, neutrophil maturation status at diagnosis was also shown to predict subsequent TFR, with patients exhibiting a higher proportion of mature neutrophils achieving superior TFR outcomes [67] These findings originate predominantly from single‐institution Japanese cohorts, and generalizability to other populations and treatment settings remains to be confirmed. These findings suggest that both neutrophil abundance and maturation reflect the integrity of host innate immunity and the hematopoietic microenvironment. Although the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood, interactions between neutrophils, NK cells, and adaptive immune cells may contribute to sustained immune surveillance against residual LSCs. Together, neutrophils represent simple and readily available biomarkers that may complement molecular and immunological profiling for individualized TFR prediction.

Leukemic Stem Cells (LSCs)

LSCs remain the principal cellular reservoir responsible for molecular relapse after TKI discontinuation. Despite sustained DMR, quiescent LSCs can persist for many years through BCR::ABL1‐independent survival mechanisms and protection within the bone marrow niche [68]. Because residual LSCs may persist below the detection limit or remain confined to the bone marrow niche, prospective validation is still required before LSC monitoring can be incorporated into routine clinical practice. In parallel, mutations in epigenetic modifier genes have been associated with an increased risk of molecular relapse, indicating that both residual LSC burden and intrinsic stem‐cell biology influence TFR outcomes [69].

Therapeutic Targeting of LSC Persistence

Increasing evidence indicates that CML LSC persistence is driven by multiple interconnected pathways that operate independently of BCR::ABL1 kinase activity. Among these, transforming growth factor‐β–forkhead box O (TGF‐β‐FOXO) signaling maintains LSC quiescence [68], whereas glycerophosphodiester phosphodiesterase domain containing 3 (GDPD3)‐dependent lysophospholipid metabolism supports stem‐cell survival under therapeutic stress [70]. Inflammatory signaling through the IL‐1 receptor (IL‐1R)/interleukin‐1 receptor‐associated kinase (IRAK)1/4‐NF‐κB pathway further promotes LSC survival and represents an attractive therapeutic target. The bone marrow microenvironment also contributes to LSC persistence through the TGF‐β–plasminogen activator inhibitor‐1 (PAI‐1) axis, which promotes retention of quiescent LSCs within the protective niche. Epigenetic regulation represents another critical mechanism of LSC maintenance. DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) preserves self‐renewal programs in residual LSCs [71], whereas enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) is selectively required for LSC survival. Pharmacological inhibition of DNMT1 or EZH2 enhances LSC depletion when combined with TKI therapy in preclinical models, highlighting epigenetic reprogramming as a promising strategy for overcoming TKI persistence [72, 73]. Collectively, these findings suggest that effective eradication of residual LSCs will likely require combination approaches targeting both intrinsic stem‐cell survival pathways and the supportive bone marrow microenvironment (Figure 4).

Immune Evasion in CML LSCs

CML LSCs evade immune surveillance through cell‐intrinsic and niche‐driven mechanisms that restrict immune effector access to the stem‐cell compartment. LSCs downregulate MHC Class II via Class II transactivator CIITA suppression—impairing CD4+ T‐cell recognition through cytokine‐driven Janus kinase (JAK)1/2 signaling [74]‐and evade cytotoxic clearance through checkpoint engagement and CD47 overexpression [75], while intrinsic survival pathways sustain LSC quiescence [68, 70]. At the niche level, leukemia‐derived signals reprogram mesenchymal stromal cells to recruit Tregs and MDSCs and suppress CD4+CD8+ T‐cell function via a tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α)/IL‐6/TGF‐β‐enriched secretome [76], creating an immunosuppressive sanctuary that limits CTL and NK‐cell access to residual LSCs. Consistent with this, elevated pre‐discontinuation IL‐1β and IL‐6 predicted molecular relapse in the JALSG D‐STOP 216 trial [77]. Future Perspectives

The management of CML is entering an era of precision TFR. In addition to conventional molecular monitoring, integration of immunological biomarkers—including NK‐cell phenotype, KIR–HLA immunogenetics, T‐cell immunity, neutrophil biology, and LSC‐associated biomarkers—may improve individualized prediction of successful TFR. At the same time, therapeutic strategies combining TKIs with immune modulation or LSC‐directed therapies have the potential to increase the proportion of patients achieving durable TFR.

Conclusion

A deeper understanding of the interplay between LSC persistence, immune surveillance, and host immunogenetics will enable personalized TFR strategies and accelerate the realization of functional cure in CML. Collectively, the evidence reviewed here indicates that durable TFR is governed not by a single dominant immune effector but by the coordinated interplay of NK‐cell education, KIR‐HLA immunogenetics, CTL‐ and pDC‐mediated adaptive surveillance, regulatory T‐cell balance, neutrophil biology, and intrinsic LSC survival programs within the bone marrow niche. Most of the biomarkers discussed remain exploratory, derived largely from single‐center or modestly sized cohorts, and few have been validated in independent populations; integration into clinical decision‐making will therefore require standardized assays, multicenter validation, and prospective biomarker‐guided discontinuation trials. Future work combining immunological, molecular, and LSC‐directed strategies will be needed to translate the precision‐TFR framework outlined in this review into routine practice and to extend durable functional cure to a broader proportion of patients with CML.
