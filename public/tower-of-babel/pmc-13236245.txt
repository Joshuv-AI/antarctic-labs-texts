Pancreas Transplantation for Recipients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus: Results of an International Survey
Authors: Jonathan A. Fridell, Pablo D. Uva, Aleksandra Kukla, Ava DeLonais-Parker, Rushda Faruk Mansuri, David Axelrod, Ty Dunn, Silke V. Niederhaus, Ronald Parsons, Henry Pleass, Steven Paraskevas, Michael R. Rickels, Robert J. Stratta, Pedro Ventura-Aguiar, Kenneth J. Woodside, Krista L. Lentine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background.Pancreas transplantation (PT), historically limited to patients with type 1 diabetes (T1D), is currently performed successfully in selected patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D). However, contemporary practice patterns and eligibility criteria for PT in T2D remain incompletely characterized.Methods.The International Pancreas and Islet Transplant Association Pancreas Transplant Interest Group developed an electronic survey to assess center-level approaches to patient phenotyping, candidacy, and peri- and posttransplant management for PT in patients with T2D. The survey was distributed to international transplant professionals during an International Pancreas and Islet Transplant Association Pre-Congress meeting on June 15, 2025.Results.Thirty-seven participants completed the survey. Most respondents’ programs offer PT for T2D only when a kidney transplant is also indicated. Fewer respondents’ programs also offer PT alone (10%), evaluate T2D candidates on a case-by-case basis (3%), or do not offer PT to patients with T2D (10%). Total daily insulin requirement and body mass index (BMI) were the most selected factors in determining eligibility. Considerable variability existed in metabolic thresholds: 62% of programs reported no C-peptide cutoff, whereas nearly half required a BMI of <30 kg/m2 for C-peptide-positive candidates. Respondents frequently recommend weight-optimization strategies, including lifestyle modification, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, and bariatric surgery for candidates exceeding acceptable BMI thresholds. Surgical technique, induction, and maintenance immunosuppression and early posttransplant glycemic management were largely uniform between T2D and T1D recipients.Conclusions.International practice reflects broad but heterogeneous adoption of PT for T2D, with most participants restricting its use to candidates requiring kidney transplantation and emphasizing integrated clinical phenotyping approaches.

INTRODUCTION

Simultaneous pancreas and kidney transplantation (SPKT) offers the best long-term outcomes for selected patients with diabetes mellitus who have end-stage kidney disease.1-7 Originally considered for patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1D) exclusively, improving allograft and patient survival rates and decreased complications and perioperative morbidity have resulted in an expansion of SPKT to selected patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D).3,4,8,9 As pancreas transplantation for candidates with T2D has become more common internationally, practitioners and pancreas transplant programs are approaching candidacy differently, depending on the capabilities and expertise of the transplant team, institutional biases, and, in some cases, oversight, regulations, and policies that do not permit pancreas transplantation outside of T1D.10 It is essential that the pancreas transplant community decide how to identify potential candidates with features of T2D who can safely undergo pancreas transplantation and who would benefit from this procedure and be rendered normoglycemic, as is the expectation for successful pancreas transplantation in recipients with T1D. To this end, the International Pancreas and Islet Transplantation Association (IPITA) Pancreas Transplant Interest Group launched a Pre-Congress meeting before the 20th World Congress, held in Pisa, Italy, in the summer of 2025, entitled “Definition and listing guidelines for pancreas transplantation in type 2 diabetes mellitus: don’t hype the type.” An international panel was invited to present critical topics in pancreas transplantation for T2D. All participants and attendees were also asked to participate in a survey to establish current international practices in pancreas transplantation for patients with T2D. This report presents the results of this international survey.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Survey Design

The survey instrument was developed by the IPITA Pancreas Transplant Interest Group. The final version collected information about respondents’ transplant centers and their professional roles within those institutions. Participants were asked to describe their center’s current perspectives regarding pancreas transplantation for recipients with T2D. Electronic informed consent was obtained via the survey’s online cover page, which the respondents acknowledged by advancing the survey. This study was approved by the Saint Louis University Institutional Review Board (No. 34622).

Survey Administration

The target population included transplant professionals attending the IPITA Pre-Congress meeting in Pisa, Italy. Specifically, surgeons, nephrologists, endocrinologists, coordinators, advanced practice providers, social workers, and pharmacists were invited. The survey was accessed online by scanning a QR code. Additional survey invitations were distributed directly via email to known members of IPITA who were unable to attend the meeting in person. Survey links were active from June 15, 2025, to July 7, 2025.

Statistical Analysis

For the primary analysis, responses to each survey question were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Percentages were calculated by dividing the number of responses by the total number of respondents for that specific question, ensuring that results reflected the proportion of programs responding, consistent with previously published methods. For questions allowing multiple selections (“select all that apply”), column percentages could exceed 100%. Analyses were performed using Jamovi version 2.7.

RESULTS

Overall, there were 60 participants at the Pre-Congress meeting, with 37 responses to the survey (including those that were unable to attend but responded to email invitations to participate; Table 1). Almost half of the participants were from North America (46%), with 32% from Europe, 11% from Asia, and 5% each from South America and Australia/New Zealand. Most of the participants were surgeons (73%), with 14% nephrologists and 14% endocrinologists. Diverse experience levels were represented with 41% in practice for >21 y, 38% for 11–20 y, and 22% within their first 10 y posttraining.

Survey participant characteristics

In terms of categorizing patients as having T2D, 66% used a combination of clinical and laboratory parameters, although 16% and 13%, respectively, based the classification solely on C-peptide levels or the use of non–insulin-based therapies (Table 2). For those using a combination of factors, pretransplant C-peptide level was the most commonly selected factor (51%) along with body mass index (BMI), the presence or absence of autoantibodies, and the use of non–insulin-based therapies (each 46%; Table 2). Age at onset of diabetes was selected less frequently (43%), whereas family history was selected the least frequently (30%; Table 2). Patients with T2D are currently considered for pancreas transplantation at most participating programs (90%), except at the programs of 3 participants (2 respondents from Europe and 1 from Australia/New Zealand; Table 2). The reasons for not offering pancreas transplantation were programmatic decisions, regulatory and funding issues, and/or support for metabolic surgery/weight loss as an alternative. Of those respondents who offer pancreas transplantation to patients with T2D, 77% offer it only if the recipient needs or has received a kidney transplant (35% would only offer SPKT and 42% would offer either SPKT or pancreas after kidney transplant), whereas 10% would also consider pancreas transplant alone (Figure 1).

Program approaches to the use of pancreas transplantation for candidates with type 2 diabetes mellitus.

In terms of factors considered important for determining eligibility for pancreas transplantation in the setting of T2D, total daily insulin dosage was selected the most, followed by glucose control as determined by hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels and the presence of vascular complications. Optimization of weight and optimization of medical therapy were selected as the least important factors (Figure 2). Regarding C-peptide thresholds, 62% would consider any level, 4% (1 program) would consider <10 ng/mL, 23% <5.0 ng/mL, and 12% (likely the 3 programs that only consider patients with T1D) would consider <2.0 ng/mL (Table 3). With respect to BMI limitations for patients with T2D, nearly half of the respondents’ programs limit pancreas transplantation candidacy to patients with a BMI <30 kg/m2. Others accept a higher BMI threshold, with 37% allowing any BMI <32 kg/m2, and 15% <35 kg/m2 (Table 2). For patients above the BMI threshold, the majority would pursue weight loss through lifestyle changes and diet, use of incretin-based therapies such as glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GI)/GLP-1RAs, or similar weight loss medications, or bariatric surgery, with 73% of participants suggesting that they would pursue a kidney transplant alone in this setting (Table 3). Most would not pursue pancreas transplantation for a candidate who is no longer on insulin, with 30% of respondents maintaining that pancreas transplantation would still be offered at their program if the decreased daily insulin requirement was believed to be the result of progressive renal failure and introduction of dialysis, whereas only 1 would consider offering pancreas transplantation if the discontinuation of insulin was felt to be the result of weight loss and treatment with incretin-based therapies (Table 3). For daily insulin requirements, when considering a patient with T2D for pancreas transplantation, 27% of participants claimed that their program had no upper limit, 35% used <100 units/d as a cutoff, 19% used <75 units/d, and 19% (5 programs) used <1 unit/kg/d (Table 3). In terms of upper age limit for pancreas transplantation in general, 1 endorsed no upper age limit, whereas 30% only considered candidates younger than 65 y, 37% younger than 60 y, 22% younger than 55 y, and 7% (2 programs) younger than 50 y (Table 3). Specifically for candidates with T2D, 81% use the same guidelines as for T1D, 2 had a lower cutoff, 2 had no upper age cutoff, and 1 participant suggested that their program had a higher age cutoff (Table 3).

Factors considered in eligibility for simultaneous pancreas and kidney transplant.

All participants reported that the surgical procedure for pancreas transplantation was the same, regardless of the type of diabetes (Table 4). Similarly, the immunosuppression strategy did not differ based on the type of diabetes (Table 4). Hyperglycemia early after pancreas transplantation was managed similarly regardless of type of diabetes in all but 1 program, whereas 23% of participants would manage late hyperglycemia differently depending on the type of diabetes (Table 4). Finally, with respect to weight gain posttransplant in recipients with T2D, almost all participants would recommend diet and exercise, 58% would consider a GLP-1RA medication, 38% would consider modifying the immunosuppression strategy, and 19% would consider pursuing bariatric surgery (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Pancreas transplantation is no longer a theoretical option for patients with T2D. Although less intuitive than the case for T1D, where lack of secretion of adequate insulin from the pancreas can be replaced by transplanting an additional pancreas, if the diabetes in T2D is manageable with supplemental exogenous insulin, then a transplanted pancreas can provide this additional insulin and render the recipient euglycemic, much like in patients with T1D. This theory has now been confirmed in multiple studies,3,8-16 and pancreas transplantation for T2D, particularly in combination with a kidney, is now internationally accepted as a standard of practice, as evidenced by the results of this survey. Based on the responses, pancreas transplantation for T2D is currently offered at most of the respondents’ centers, with only 3 claiming that this option is not offered because of programmatic decisions, regulatory issues, and/or support for metabolic surgery/weight loss as an alternative. The regulatory decision is troubling, as essentially all other programs that participated are at least permitted to perform pancreas transplants in this setting, although 2 have made a programmatic medical decision that either they will not offer this option or that other solutions involving weight loss through injectable medications or bariatric surgery along with kidney transplantation would be considered a superior option, which may be a reasonable strategy if weight loss can be sustained.17-20 It is unclear why regulatory bodies would prohibit a transplant option when all evidence supports that, in well-selected candidates, pancreas transplantation in patients with T2D can achieve similar glycemic control as well as patient, kidney and pancreas allograft survival rates comparable with those of the T1D patient population. The Pancreas Transplant Interest Group strongly recommends that countries with these restrictions consider relaxing these policies as evidence-based transplant qualification criteria.

Despite consensus that pancreas transplantation is appropriate for selected candidates with T2D, there remains substantial variability with respect to candidate selection. Patients with T2D display considerable heterogeneity, with phenotypes ranging from reduced insulin secretion to marked insulin resistance. The limited application of precision medicine in standard endocrinology practice to differentiate these phenotypes often leads to confusion among referring clinicians and transplant centers, that may lack appropriate experience for conducting advanced endocrinologic diagnostic studies. Such studies are essential for accurately differentiating patients with diminished insulin secretory capacity and significant glucose variability, who are most likely to benefit from pancreas transplantation, from those who are primarily insulin resistant and may be managed effectively with weight loss interventions or non–insulin-based pharmacological therapy. This diagnostic challenge contributes to inconsistencies in clinical management and candidate selection across the field and underscores the importance of addressing it systematically. Therefore, additional research is needed to identify which patients with T2D benefit most from pancreas transplantation, thereby providing more precise guidelines for promulgation and adoption by transplant centers not currently providing this option.

It has been remarkably difficult to develop a consistent definition for T2D in the context of pancreas transplantation.10 Most respondents rely on a combination of factors, including the presence of pretransplant C-peptide levels, BMI, the use of non–insulin-based therapies, and the absence of autoantibodies. Total daily insulin dosage was considered the most important factor in determining candidacy, followed by glucose control as determined by HbA1c levels, although it is likely that the combination of these 2 items and their interdependency was implied. As an example, if a potential pancreas transplant candidate with T2D is on a given daily amount of insulin, but the HbA1c is elevated, then the patient’s actual total daily insulin requirement is underestimated and should be assumed to be higher. Most participants did not identify a specific upper limit for the pretransplant C-peptide levels. Nearly half of the respondents consider only BMI <30 kg/m2, but many would list patients with higher BMIs, and some have no upper BMI limit. The small number that indicated no upper limit for BMI likely reflects the current programs that are offering robotic pancreas transplantation for patients with very high BMI levels.21,22 These criteria very closely mirror the recipient selection criteria outlined by Orlando et al 15 y ago, which included age <55–60 y, BMI <30–32 kg/m2, fasting C-peptide <10 ng/mL, and total daily insulin dosage <1 unit/kg/d.23 For patients above the threshold for BMI, the majority of programs would pursue weight loss through lifestyle changes and diet, use of weekly injectable medications, or bariatric surgery, with 73% of participants suggesting that they would pursue a kidney transplant alone in the meantime. The survey did not determine the usage of non–insulin-based therapies (oral or injectable) as initial therapy at the time of diabetes diagnosis or the interval between diagnosis and initiation of insulin therapy. The timing and duration of insulin therapy may have been a consideration in determining pancreas transplant candidacy for some respondents. Most respondents would not pursue pancreas transplantation for a candidate that is no longer on insulin, although 30% maintain that pancreas transplantation would still be offered at their program if the diminished insulin requirement was thought to be the result of progressive renal failure and introduction of dialysis, whereas only 1 program would consider pancreas transplantation if the diminished insulin requirement was thought to be the result of weight loss and treatment with medications.

Nevertheless, kidney transplant recipients may now be safely treated with the incretin-based therapies, so it may be an option to continue non–insulin-based regimens post–kidney transplant in patients with T2D. Although these therapies may avoid the need for insulin posttransplant, these patients continue to have diabetes in the absence of more precise phenotyping. In terms of the upper limit for daily insulin dosage in candidate selection, 27% of participants responded that their program had no upper limit for daily insulin requirements, 35% used <100 units/d as a cutoff, 19% used <75 units/d, and 19% (5 programs) used <1 unit/kg/d.

Regarding the upper age limit for pancreas transplantation in general, 1 respondent endorsed no upper age limit, whereas 30% only considered candidates younger than 65 y, 37% younger than 60 y, 22% younger than 55 y, and 7% (2 respondents) younger than 50 y. This result was somewhat surprising because several reports have demonstrated that pancreas allograft survival in older patients deemed to have acceptable surgical risk is no different from that in younger recipients, with the youngest categories typically having the worst long-term allograft survival.24-28 As the expansion of nephroprotective therapies continues to delay the onset of end-stage kidney disease, older candidates with T2D and chronic kidney disease are increasingly considered for kidney transplantation. Transplant centers should carefully evaluate appropriate candidates for SPKT, taking into account comorbid conditions that may elevate perioperative and long-term risks in this older population.24-28

Surgical and posttransplant management practices were largely uniform, with nearly all respondents reporting no differences in surgical approach or immunosuppression between T2D and T1D recipients. Finally, in terms of weight gain posttransplant in recipients with T2D, almost all participants would recommend diet and exercise, 58% would consider a GLP-1RA medication, 38% would consider modifying the immunosuppression strategy (perhaps considering steroid-elimination or withdrawal), and 19% would consider pursuing bariatric surgery.

Our study has limitations. Respondents were participants in a Pre-Congress on pancreas transplantation, and this may have selected for transplant professionals who are especially engaged or interested in pancreas transplant recipient care. Consequently, the respondents’ answers may not generally represent practices at nonresponding centers. With this selection bias in mind, it may also have a counter side, namely that it is more likely to represent more active pancreas transplant programs. Respondents were not asked to provide specific information that would have allowed us to clarify the sizes of the programs represented in the survey. Similarly, although the sample size is small, it is likely representative of the active pancreas transplant community, which is likewise small. These data provide important insights into contemporary pancreas transplantation practices in T2D that create a framework for broader discussion and monitoring of evolving practices. In retrospect, some questions were vague and lacked specific definitions or ranges, perhaps allowing the participant to interpret in alternate ways—for example, the terminology “C-peptide positive” could have been more specific. For the analysis, we interpreted the results exactly as they were presented in the survey.

CONCLUSIONS

Considerable variability exists in the utilization of pancreas transplantation for T2D, with most respondents’ programs restricting it to candidates requiring kidney transplantation. As experience with pancreas transplantation in this setting grows, candidate criteria will likely continue to be liberalized. As non–insulin-based therapies become increasingly available and clinically relevant, a multidisciplinary, patient-centered approach is important to guide clinicians in offering pancreas transplants to appropriate candidates with T2D. Data collection and reporting of experience and patient outcomes can help support evidence-based practice advances and facilitate the expansion of pancreas transplantation across all jurisdictions.
