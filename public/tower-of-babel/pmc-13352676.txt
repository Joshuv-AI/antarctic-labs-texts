Association between red cell index and stroke-associated pneumonia
Authors: Ki-Woong Nam, Hyung-Min Kwon, Yong-Seok Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke-associated pneumonia (SAP) is a common infectious complication of acute ischemic stroke (AIS). This study evaluated the association between the red cell index (RCI) and SAP in patients with AIS.MethodsWe analyzed 500 consecutive patients with AIS. SAP was defined based on modified Centers for Disease Control and Prevention criteria. Background

After the onset of stroke, patients are exposed to various infectious complications, among which stroke-associated pneumonia (SAP) is reported to be the most common one [1–4]. SAP occurs in 7% to 38% of patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and adversely affects prognosis, hospitalization duration, and mortality [4–7]. However, it presents unique characteristics that make its diagnosis challenging, distinguishing it from typical pneumonia [8–10].

In patients with SAP, diagnostic tools such as chest radiography and sputum cultures show low sensitivity, posing significant diagnostic limitations [9–12]. Consequently, previous studies have sought to identify diverse risk factors for SAP (e.g., age, dysphagia, stroke severity, impaired consciousness, hyperglycemia, malnutrition, and mechanical ventilation) and have proposed predictive models based on these findings [5, 6, 13–16]. However, these models primarily rely on clinical findings. Because ischemic stroke presents with various neurological deficits, the clinical manifestations of SAP are relatively atypical or vague [8, 9]. Therefore, there are clear limitations to the prediction of SAP using only existing predictive models. Despite the challenges in accurately diagnosing SAP or identifying high-risk patients, administering preventive antibiotics to all patients with AIS is not a viable solution [17]. Previous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have shown that the use of preventive antibiotics in patients with AIS does not yield positive outcomes [18, 19]. Therefore, a more objective marker for the early identification of patients at high risk of SAP is essential. Indeed, several studies have attempted to predict the occurrence of SAP using various biomarkers (e.g., neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, systemic immune-inflammation index, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio). However, most of these markers focus solely on the acute inflammatory response. Given that SAP is a pulmonary condition, an index that reflects both inflammation and respiratory function may better capture the disease’s pathophysiology.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a well-known risk factor for pneumonia and predictor of poor outcomes, and this holds true for SAP as well [20, 21]. In patients with COPD, underlying pathological conditions affect erythropoiesis and iron metabolism, leading to alterations in various red blood cell (RBC) indices [20]. Recently, a new marker called the red cell index (RCI) was developed to reflect COPD severity and prognosis by utilizing these blood cell indices [22–24]. RCI serves as a marker of impaired pulmonary function and chronic hypoxemia, while also reflecting chronic inflammation [22, 23]. Given these characteristics, RCI is hypothesized to be closely associated with SAP occurrence and prognosis in patients with AIS.

Accordingly, this study aims to analyze the association between RCI and the occurrence of SAP in patients with AIS and explore its potential as a new predictive marker for identifying patients at high -risk of SAP.

Methods

Study population

From the consecutive stroke registry of a large stroke center in Korea (Seoul Metropolitan Government-Seoul National University Boramae Medical Center), we selected patients with AIS who were admitted within 7 days of symptom onset between January 2017 and May 2018 (n = 550). At our center, all patients clinically diagnosed with AIS are admitted as a principle and registered in the stroke registry upon discharge [6]. To classify the pathological mechanism of the index stroke, we conducted brain magnetic resonance imaging, magnetic resonance angiography, echocardiography, electrocardiography, and laboratory examinations within the first 24 h of admission [6].

We excluded patients who met any of the following conditions: (1) absence of blood cell count data (n = 1), (2) history of active cancer or hematologic disorders or use of immunosuppressants (n = 33), (3) recent major trauma or surgery (n = 5), (4) severe hepatic or renal disease (n = 5), or (5) history of active infection within two weeks prior to admission (n = 6). Ultimately, 500 patients with AIS were included in the final analysis. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Boramae Medical Center (IRB number: 06-2023-0049). The board waived the requirement for written informed consent because of the retrospective nature of the study, which utilized only de-identified patient information. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the relevant guidelines and regulations. All data and materials related to this article are included in the main text and supplemental materials.

Clinical assessment

We evaluated all demographic data and comorbidities, including age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, atrial fibrillation, ischemic heart disease, current smoking status, COPD, and history of stroke. Additionally, we assessed several clinical factors, such as initial stroke severity, use of thrombolytic therapy, dysphagia, and impaired consciousness. Initial stroke severity was measured daily from admission to discharge by the attending physician, who was not involved in the study, using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score. Dysphagia was identified on the first day of admission based on the bedside non-instrumented swallowing test routinely performed at our center [6]. Impaired consciousness was defined as a score of ≥ 1 on item 1a of the initial NIHSS assessment. Furthermore, we calculated the A2DS2 score, a well-validated tool for predicting SAP [15]. The A2DS2 score ranges from 0 to 10 and consists of the following components: age (1 point for ≥ 75 years), atrial fibrillation (1 point), dysphagia (2 points), sex (1 point for male sex), and initial stroke severity (0 points for NIHSS score 0–4, 3 points for NIHSS score 5–15, and 5 points for NIHSS score ≥ 16) [15].

Laboratory examinations were conducted within the first 24 h of admission and included measurements of hemoglobin A1c, total cholesterol level, blood cell counts, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) level [6]. Blood cell samples were centrifuged immediately after collection (2000 rpm at 4 °C for 20 min) and stored in calcium EDTA tubes. Cell counts were performed using an automated analyzer (XE-2100; Sysmex, Kobe, Japan). The RCI was calculated using the following formula: RCI = (red blood cell count [⋅1012/L] ⋅ hemoglobin [g/L]) / (lymphocyte count [⋅109/ L] ⋅ platelet count [⋅109/ L]) [23].

Outcome variables

The primary outcome of this study was the occurrence of SAP. SAP was diagnosed when a lower respiratory tract infection occurred within 7 days after the index stroke, in accordance with the modified Centers for Disease Control and Prevention criteria [12]. SAP diagnosis was made retrospectively by two neurologists (K.-W.N. and H.-M.K.) who were blinded to other clinical and laboratory findings during the review process. Given the small number of events, probable and definite SAP were coded as SAP, regardless of chest radiographic findings [6, 12]. To evaluate the clinical outcomes of patients who developed SAP, we also assessed hospitalization duration, NIHSS score, modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score at discharge, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, intubation, and in-hospital mortality.

Statistical analysis

In this study, continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas those without a normal distribution were presented as median with interquartile range. Univariate analysis was conducted using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test for continuous variables, and the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis, along with COPD, were included as confounders in the multivariable logistic regression analysis to identify predictors of SAP.

We performed multivariable analyses using two models to examine the association between RCI and SAP. Model 1 included variables selected from the univariate analysis, whereas model 2 used the A2DS2 score to replace several variables. In model 2, the components already included in the A2DS2 score were excluded as confounders to avoid redundancy. Additionally, given that the RCI is primarily used in patients with COPD, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with COPD to further validate the findings.

To determine the optimal RCI value for predicting SAP, we calculated the cut-off point based on a receiver operating characteristic curve. In our study population, the RCI cut-off value for SAP prediction was 1.54. Using this threshold, we compared the baseline characteristics of patients with high and low RCI values. The same univariate analysis methods described earlier were applied.

To analyze the impact of SAP on the prognosis of patients with AIS, we compared clinical outcomes between the SAP and non-SAP groups, including length of hospitalization, NIHSS score at discharge, mRS score at discharge, ICU admission rates, and incidence of intubation or in-hospital mortality. The Mann-Whitney U-test and chi-squared test were used for these comparisons. Furthermore, we investigated whether higher RCI values reflected poorer pneumonia outcomes in 62 patients with SAP. We compared RCI values according to ICU admission, intubation, and in-hospital mortality. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and variables with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

We assessed 500 patients with AIS (mean age: 69 years, male sex: 320 [64.0%]). Among them, 62 (12.4%) patients developed SAP within 7 days after stroke onset. The median NIHSS score was 3 [1–7] and the median RCI was 2 [1–3]. Other detailed baseline characteristics are shown in Table S1.

In our study population, the RCI cut-off value for SAP prediction was 1.54. Based on this, we compared the characteristics of patients with high and low RCI values. The high RCI group was positively associated with male sex, atrial fibrillation, impaired consciousness, A2DS2 score, RBC count, hemoglobin level, and neutrophil count. In contrast, body mass index, total cholesterol level, white blood cell (WBC) count, lymphocyte count, and platelet count were negatively associated with the high RCI group. In terms of clinical outcomes, the high RCI group experienced more SAP events and had higher rates of ICU admission and intubation (Table 1).

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, RCI remained significant even after adjusting for confounders (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.46; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.08–1.97). Additionally, age (aOR = 1.05; 95% CI, 1.01–1.09), dysphagia (aOR = 11.95; 95% CI, 4.23–33.73), impaired consciousness (aOR = 3.52; 95% CI, 1.19–10.42), WBC count (aOR = 1.25; 95% CI, 1.09–1.44), and hs-CRP levels (aOR = 1.20; 95% CI, 1.07–1.33) were independently significant alongside RCI. These findings were consistent in model 2, where age, atrial fibrillation, dysphagia, and initial NIHSS score were replaced with the A2DS2 score (Table 3). When RCI was analyzed based on the cut-off value rather than as a continuous variable, the adjusted odds ratio was 4.07 [1.78–9.33] (Table S2). Furthermore, in an additional analysis that excluded the influence of patients with COPD, RCI remained significantly associated with SAP (Table S3).

The SAP group had a longer hospitalization duration and higher discharge NIHSS scores than the non-SAP group (14 [9–22] days vs. 7 [5–8] days, P < 0.001). Discussion

In this study, we found that high RCI was associated with SAP in patients with AIS. Moreover, RCI was also correlated with poor prognosis after SAP development. Since SAP is clinically challenging to diagnose and previous RCTs have shown that the indiscriminate use of prophylactic antibiotics for SAP does not improve outcomes, early identification of high-risk patients through RCI may be clinically valuable. In particular, if the RCI could be automatically calculated within electronic health records similar to how glomerular filtration rate is routinely computed, its clinical utility may be further increased.

There are several possible explanations for the close association between the RCI and SAP. First, post-stroke immunosuppression following AIS is considered to play a crucial role [5, 25]. Ischemic stroke, caused by vascular occlusion, initially triggers only a local inflammatory response [25, 26]. However, this soon leads to systemic immune modulation through the sympathetic pathway and the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis, a phenomenon known as post-stroke immunosuppression [25, 27–29]. Sympathetic activation induces lymphocyte apoptosis, resulting in peripheral lymphocytopenia, and the Th1-type pro-inflammatory response shifts toward a Th2-type anti-inflammatory response [5, 27, 29]. Activation of the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis stimulates the adrenal gland to release glucocorticoids, further promoting an anti-inflammatory response and inducing T-lymphocyte apoptosis [5, 25, 27, 30]. The lymphocyte count included in the RCI calculation formula is expected to well reflect the degree of immunosuppression. This immunosuppressive state may increase the likelihood of aspiration events in patients with AIS who progress to SAP. Thrombocytopenia is another factor that should be considered. In the setting of AIS, platelet consumption occurs as platelet-fibrin thrombi form [31]. The extent of this consumption tends to increase with stroke severity [31]. In severe strokes, associated dysphagia and impaired consciousness can lead to a higher incidence of aspiration events, ultimately contributing to SAP development [5, 32, 33]. Additionally, SAP-induced inflammatory responses can activate and deplete platelets, making thrombocytopenia a potential predictor of SAP [28, 31]. Indeed, previous studies have shown that thrombocytopenia is associated with poor outcomes in patients with SAP [31, 34, 35]. However, in our study population, platelet count did not show a strong correlation with SAP, suggesting that the influence of thrombocytopenia may not be substantial.

The core concept that RCI aims to reflect is impaired pulmonary function, with hemoglobin levels and RBC counts serving as key indicators of this impairment [22–24]. Previous studies have consistently shown that RCI exhibits a strong negative correlation with FEV1/FVC and a positive correlation with PCO2 levels [23, 24]. Contrary to our expectations, hemoglobin levels and RBC counts were not significantly associated with SAP. However, these components were associated with worse outcomes, such as ICU admission and intubation, in patients who had already developed SAP (Table S4). This suggests that although impaired pulmonary function itself may not directly contribute to SAP development, it can exacerbate symptoms once SAP occurs. Contrary, immunosuppression, represented by lymphocyte count, was not a key factor in poor SAP outcomes. However, none of the four RCI components were associated with in-hospital mortality. This is probably because there are various causes of death other than SAP in patients with AIS [36, 37]. In summary, although some components of the RCI were more closely associated with SAP occurrence and others with its prognosis, the RCI itself seems to be a clinically valuable predictor of both. This is supported by the fact that the predictive power for SAP increased when the RCI value was added to the traditional A2DS2 score. However, as the increase in AUC was not substantial, caution is warranted when considering the clinical applicability of these findings.

In this study, SAP was strongly associated with poor outcomes in patients with AIS, similar to the findings of previous studies [38, 39]. Patients who developed SAP had longer hospitalization durations and higher discharge NIHSS and mRS scores. This may be because of SAP-induced fever triggers an inflammatory cascade, leading to neutrophil infiltration into injured brain tissue and exacerbating tissue damage [5, 28, 38]. Additionally, prolonged immobility could delay the initiation of rehabilitation and reduce its effectiveness [6, 7, 38, 39]. Hypoxemia and hypotension associated with SAP may also contribute to worse outcomes [6, 25, 39]. While SAP could simply be a complication of severe ischemic stroke, it also plays a direct role in worsening stroke prognosis. Therefore, early identification of high-risk patients using markers such as RCI and implementing appropriate management strategies is important.

This study has several limitations. First, this study was a single-center retrospective cross-sectional study. Although our data demonstrated a strong statistical association between SAP and RCI, as well as a close link between RCI and prognosis, this does not imply causality. Further prospective studies are required to establish the causal relationship. Second, owing to the limited sample size of event outcomes, we included both probable and definite SAP cases. Although physicians at our center conduct thorough evaluations, probable SAP lacks radiological evidence, necessitating careful differentiation from other conditions that may mimic SAP. Third, the time from ischemic stroke onset to hospital admission may have introduced bias. According to previous studies, SAP occurs most frequently within the first 2–3 days [3, 13, 17]. Since we included patients with AIS who were admitted within seven days of symptom onset, it is possible that some SAP events were missed, leading to an underestimation. However, as 81.2% of our study population was admitted within 72 h, the impact of this limitation was likely minimal. Fourth, we used only the initial RCI values measured at admission for our analysis. If we had also evaluated changes in RCI after the development of SAP, this might have provided further insights into the causal relationship and underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Finally, we excluded patients with various underlying medical conditions that could significantly bias the RCI values. However, the potential influence of unaccounted for pathological conditions or medication history should be considered.

Conclusion

In conclusion, an elevated RCI may be a useful predictor of both SAP occurrence and prognosis in patients with AIS. Given that the RCI can be easily and rapidly calculated from routine blood tests, it enables timely identification of high-risk patients, allowing for appropriate antibiotic management where necessary. Additionally, considering that previous non-selective prophylactic antibiotic trials in patients with AIS have failed, our findings may contribute to the design of future clinical trials. Nevertheless, our results need to be validated through large-scale follow-up studies.
