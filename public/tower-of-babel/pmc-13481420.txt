Eating attitudes among college students: the role of body shape concerns, mental health, and obesity
Authors: Shromona Das, Divyansha Khajuria, Monika Kulshreshtha, Shivani Chandel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBody image concerns, mental health disorders, and obesity are highly prevalent among young adults and can significantly influence eating attitudes during the college years.AimTo study the prevalence of disordered eating attitudes, body shape concerns, common mental health disorders, and obesity among college-going students, and to find out their inter-relationship.MethodThis cross-sectional study collected data from 311 participants (186 males; 125 females) aged 18–23 years. Participants completed questionnaires assessing their demographics, eating attitudes, body shape concerns, and common mental disorders. Four somatometric measurements were recorded according to the ISAK protocol for assessing adiposity levels. Spearman’s rho assessed the association, and logistic regression identified the risk of possible disordered eating behavior.ResultsMales reported higher EAT-26 (p = .042) and dieting scores (p = .019), as well as higher adiposity indices, including waist circumference (p < .001) and body mass index (p = .009), than females. Greater prevalence of high perceived stress (p = .002), severe anxiety (p = .032), high WC (p = .018), and waist-to-hip ratio (p < .001) was reported among females. Body shape concern was significantly positively correlated with disordered eating attitudes among both sexes (p < .001). Females with elevated body shape concerns were eight times more likely to report disordered eating attitudes, whereas males were four times more likely (p < .001). BMI (p = .002), WC (p = .012), and WHtR (p < .001) significantly predicted the risk of disordered eating attitudes among males, whereas anxiety (p = .033) predicted the risk among females.ConclusionYoung adults constitute a critically vulnerable yet underrecognized group for eating and weight-related disorders, underscoring the urgent need to strengthen screening, prevention, and intervention efforts. Future research and public health initiatives must adopt gender-sensitive frameworks that acknowledge the unique pathways through which both sexes experience and express eating-related psychopathology.

Introduction

Eating disorders (ED) are disabling, deadly, and challenging mental disorders that considerably impair physical health and disrupt the psychosocial functioning of an individual [1]. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-5 (DSM-5), there are three forms of EDs: anorexia nervosa (AN), bulimia nervosa (BN), and binge eating disorder (BED), which are found to be highly prevalent [2]. Global Burden of Diseases (GBD) of 2019 reported that there are about 13.6 million people affected by AN or BN, 41.9 million people have some BED, and other specified feeding or eating disorder (OSFED) globally. AN and BN account for 2.9 million DALYs (Disability Adjusted Life Years) and BED about 0.8 million DALYs globally [3].

EDs are serious psychiatric illnesses that are linked to high morbidity, considerable clinical impairment, and medical problems [4]. Over 75% of ED cases emerge during adolescence [5], with conditions such as BN, AN, and BED showing a rapid rise in early adulthood [6]. The burden of EDs is highest among people aged 20–24 years. Previous studies indicate that while nearly 15% of women aged 17–24 years experience some form of ED, men exhibit a rapid increase, suggesting a shifting burden [7]. Among the young adults, obesity and disordered eating behaviors (DEBs) are relatively common and pose crucial public health issues because of the potentially devastating medical and behavioral effects they may have [8]. The onset of full syndrome EDs is generally accompanied by DEBs and attitudes, which are frequent among young people and include skipping meals, obsessing over the nutritional worth of food, and feeling discontent with one's appearance [9]. Excessive focus on weight may lead to an unhealthy preoccupation with nutrition, which, in turn, may increase the risk of developing DEB [10]. Furthermore, obesity is frequently mediated by psychological conditions, including poor mood or psychological distress, body dissatisfaction, and weight stigma, which not only increases the risk of EDs but also helps sustain them, contributing to their growing global prevalence [11]. People with obesity and an ED coexisting have far worse physiological and psychological health [12]. Besides, the media has a substantial impact on the prevalence of ED by promoting the notion of "size zero" body, creating unrealistic beauty standards, thereby leading to distortions in eating habits and body image issues among young people in the Indian subcontinent [13]. In India, sociocultural appearance ideals emphasize thinness and a fair complexion for females; and tall stature, muscularity, and broad shoulders for males, largely shaped by media and cultural norms. A negative body image can lead to low self-esteem, lack of confidence, and an obsession with dieting, depression or depressive symptoms, body-related anxiety, challenges in interpersonal connections such as intimate relationships, occupational life, and in extreme situations, substance misuse and a variety of health-related problems, along with development of ED [14]; causing numerous mental health issues to emerge as well.

The transition to young adulthood, particularly the college years, is a critical developmental period characterized by profound physical, emotional, and social changes that heighten vulnerability to body image dissatisfaction, EDs, and mental health problems [15–17]. The behavioral patterns established during the developmental phase of young adulthood determine their current health status and the risk of developing chronic diseases later in life [18]. In recent years, there has been a growing recognition that EDs and body shape concerns are not confined to Western populations but are increasingly prevalent in non-Western contexts such as India, where rapid urbanization, globalization, and the influence of Western beauty standards are reshaping cultural perceptions of body image and health, particularly among youth. India’s rapid socioeconomic transition has led to significant changes in dietary habits and physical activity among young adults, contributing to a dual burden of malnutrition and obesity. Rising rates of overweight and obesity, driven by sedentary lifestyles and calorie-dense diets [19], coexist with sociocultural ideals that glorify thinness, especially among young women, contributing to body dissatisfaction and unhealthy weight control behaviors. These disordered eating attitudes are often associated with common mental health disorders such as depression, anxiety, and low self-esteem, creating a complex interplay between psychological well-being and physical health. College students, in particular, represent a vulnerable group as the pressure to fit into social and aesthetic ideals, combined with academic and career-related stressors, makes them more susceptible to developing body dissatisfaction, EDs, and mental health issues.

Despite this, there remains a paucity of comprehensive, empirical research in the Indian context exploring the interrelationships among disordered eating attitudes, body shape concerns, mental health disorders, and obesity. Most available studies are either region-specific or focus on isolated variables rather than examining their complex interconnections. Understanding these associations is crucial for developing culturally sensitive prevention and intervention strategies. By identifying the prevalence and correlates of these issues among Indian college students, this study aims to contribute to evidence-based mental health promotion and obesity prevention programs in higher education settings.

Method

Participants and procedures

This study was a cross-sectional study conducted among the college-going students aged 18 to 23 years. The study included 311 participants, of whom 186 were male and 125 were female. Participants were recruited via purposive sampling from various colleges of the University of Delhi, New Delhi, India, to ensure the inclusion of college students aged 18–23 years who represent the developmental stage most vulnerable to body image concerns, disordered eating attitudes, and related mental health issues. The sample size was calculated to be 256 at a 95% confidence interval, with a relative precision of 5% of the p-value and considering the population size of Delhi to be infinite, with a reference to a similar study conducted on the undergraduate students of medical college in Delhi by Sharma et al. [13] which reported the prevalence rate of disordered eating attitudes among university students to be 21.1%. The sample size was increased to 311, assuming a 20% refusal rate.

The study included participants who were current students enrolled at the University of Delhi, confirmed through institutional affiliation, and only those aged 18–23 years were included to represent the typical college-going population. Participants were excluded if they were not enrolled at the University of Delhi, were outside the 18–23-year age range, were unwilling to provide informed consent, or had incomplete data during somatometric measurements. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Department of Anthropology at the University of Delhi, India. This study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration for Human Subjects Research. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants. Withdrawal from the study at any time was also permitted, provided ethical considerations were kept in mind. Data were collected between February and March 2022.

Measures

Data concerning socio-demographic variables, psychometric data, and somatometric measurements were obtained for this study. Variables such as age, sex, religion, caste, educational status, family type, family monthly income based on the Kuppuswamy socioeconomic status scale 2021, and marital status were used in this study to determine the overall socio-demographic characteristics of the population under investigation.

The Eating Attitudes Test (EAT-26; Garner, Olmsted, Bohr, & Garfinkel, 1982) was used to assess individuals at risk for disordered eating attitudes. There are 26 self-report questions and five additional behavioral questions capturing the risky ED behaviors (e.g., laxative use, purging, etc.). The questionnaire has three subscales—Dieting, Bulimia, and Oral Control. The total score ranges from 0 to 78. A total score of > 20 indicates a high risk of developing an ED and requires evaluation and intervention by a medical professional. A score of less than 20 is considered low risk. Cronbach's α was calculated to assess internal consistency. For EAT-26, the value of Cronbach's α = 0.879.

The Body Shape Questionnaire (BSQ-34; Cooper, Taylor, Cooper, and Fairburn, 1987) was used to assess participants' concerns about their appearance and body shape. Participants were categorized into four groups based on their overall score: (a) no concern with shape (≤ 80), (b) mild concern (80–110), (c) moderate concern (111–140), and (d) marked body shape concern/dissatisfaction (≥ 140). The BSQ-34's Cronbach's alpha was 0.957.

The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10; Cohen, Kamarck & Mermelstein, 1983) was used to measure the psychological stress estimated over the previous 4 weeks. It consists of 10 items measured on a five-point Likert scale (0 = never, 1 = almost never, 2 = sometimes, 3 = fairly often, 4 = very often). The total score is obtained by adding the scores of all the items, with reverse coding for items 4, 5, 7, and 8, as they are positively stated. The total score ranges from 0 to 40, with 40 indicating the highest perceived stress level. Participants were categorized into “low stress” for a PSS-10 score between 0 and 13, “moderate stress” for scores 14–26, and “high stress” for a PSS-10 score between 27 and 40. The higher the score, the greater the perceived stress. For PSS-10, the value of Cronbach's α = 0.741.

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7; Spitzer, Kroenke, Williams & Löwe et al., 2006) is a 7-item brief self-report scale designed to recognize probable cases of GAD. The GAD-7 score is calculated by assigning scores of 0, 1, 2, and 3 to the response categories of “not at all,” “several days,” “more than half the days,” and “nearly every day,” respectively. Then all the scores for the seven questions are added together. GAD-7 total score for the seven items ranges from 0 to 21. The scores of 5, 10, and 15 represent cut-points for mild, moderate, and severe anxiety, respectively. A score of 10 or greater on the GAD-7 represents a reasonable cut point for identifying cases of GAD (Spitzer et al., 2006). For GAD-7, the value of Cronbach's α = 0.865.

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; Kroenke, Spitzer & Williams, 2001) is a 9-item instrument administered in primary care settings to screen for the presence and severity of depression. This is a self-rating scale. The results of the PHQ-9 are used to make a depression diagnosis according to the DSM-IV (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders–Fourth Edition) criteria. PHQ-9 was formulated based on DSM-IV to understand how often patients have been bothered by symptoms of depression in the period of two weeks (0 point = never, 1 point = a few days, 2 points = more than half of the days, 3 points = almost every day). Each item was scored on a scale of 0–3, with a total score ranging from 0 to 27. Also, a cut-off value of 10 was used in this study for the diagnosis of depression. As the cut-off value of 10 has shown, it has a sensitivity of 88% and a specificity of 88%. For PHQ-9, the value of Cronbach's α = 0.957.

For somatometric data, the participant's height, weight, waist circumference (WC), and hip circumference have been recorded. These somatometric measurements have been used to derive Body Mass Index (BMI), Waist-to-Hip Ratio (WHR), and Waist-to-Height Ratio (WHtR) to assess the prevalence of general and central obesity among participants. The ranges for BMI, WC, and WHR were interpreted according to the Asia–Pacific guidelines; however, the WHtR range was interpreted from the Margaret Ashwell chart (2009). The Asia–Pacific Guidelines identify two categories for WC: normal and at risk, i.e., < 90 for men and < 80 for women, and ≥ 90 for men and ≥ 80 for women, respectively. According to WHO Asia–Pacific guidelines, there are four BMI categories: underweight (< 18.5), normal (18.5–22.9), overweight (23.0–24.9), and obese (≥ 25.0). Based on Asia–Pacific Guidelines, the WHR categories are normal and at risk: < 0.90 for men and < 0.85 for women, and ≥ 0.90 for men and ≥ 0.85 for women, respectively. The Margaret Ashwell Chart (2009) divides WHtR risk levels into four categories: low risk (< 0.40), normal (0.40–0.49), at risk (0.50–0.59), and high risk (≥ 0.60).

Data analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using MS Excel version 2019 and SPSS version 20. None of the data was normally distributed, and none passed the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test prior to analysis. Basic descriptive statistics (median and range) were calculated to assess the distribution of the disordered eating attitude, body shape concerns, common mental health disorders, and obesity across the sample. The Mann–Whitney U test was employed to compare mean rank differences in EAT-26, BSQ-34, PSS-10, PHQ-9, GAD-7 scores, and obesity indicators (BMI, WC, WHtR, WHR) between males and females due to the non-normal distribution of the data. Chi-square and Fisher's exact tests were used to compare categorical frequencies. Spearman’s rho was calculated to examine the inter-dynamic relationship between disordered eating attitude, body shape concerns, common mental health disorders, and obesity indicators. A binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine the predictive ability of the common mental health disorders, body shape concerns, and obesity indicators for the disordered eating attitudes.

Results

Significant differences were observed in age (p = 0.032), educational status (p = 0.024), and caste (p = 0.015). Regarding psychological and behavioral measures, males had significantly higher total EAT-26 scores (p = 0.042) and dieting subscale scores (p = 0.019) than females, indicating greater disordered eating tendencies. Categorical analysis of psychological and obesity-related variables revealed several significant differences by sex. A relatively larger population is at a high risk of having disordered eating attitudes (24.1%) in this study, with no significant difference between males and females (p = 0.395). Similarly, body shape concern levels did not differ significantly across sexes (p = 0.464). However, a significant difference was observed in perceived stress levels (p = 0.002) and anxiety levels (p = 0.032), with more females experiencing perceived stress (17.6% vs. 7%) and severe anxiety (11.2% vs. 8.6%) than males. Although depressive symptoms did not differ significantly (p = 0.095), mild to moderate depression was common in both groups. Regarding specific eating behaviors, no significant differences between sexes were found. Regarding obesity indicators, BMI categories did not differ significantly by sex (p = 0.092), though males had a slightly higher proportion of obesity (24.7%) than females (14.4%). It was found that the proportions of individuals at risk according to WC and WHR are significantly higher in females than in males (30.4% vs. 18.8%, p = 0.018; 70.4% vs. 13.4%, p < 0.001, respectively). Discussion

The present findings demonstrate clear sex-based differences in sociodemographic characteristics, psychological distress, and obesity-related indicators, revealing distinct patterns among males and females. Our results reveal that a relatively larger proportion of the studied participants is at high risk of disordered eating attitudes, among whom males reported higher EAT-26 and dieting subscale scores than females. This result was similar to that of a previous study conducted by Sharma et al. among medical students in New Delhi in 2019 [13]. In the present study, females also reported greater psychological distress, including higher perceived stress, anxiety, depression, and body shape concerns, compared to males. This suggests that while females may internalize distress through emotional and cognitive symptoms, males may express vulnerability more prominently through maladaptive eating behaviors and weight-control practices [20, 21]. Mostly, the existing literature suggests a lower prevalence of EDs in men, which may be due to underreporting and ignorance, but the detrimental effects are just as severe [22]. Recent research across community settings reveals that rates of ED among men are rising more rapidly than in women [23]. Males account for 25% of those who have anorexia nervosa, and have a higher mortality rate because of late diagnosis [24, 25]. Moreover, about 40% of those who have binge eating disorder are men [26], and they are found to be engaged in subclinical disordered eating behaviors almost as frequently as females [24].

More females than males in the current study reported body shape concerns, consistent with other research among college students [27–30]. The findings consistently indicate that disordered eating attitudes are closely associated with body shape concern, which emerged as the most prominent correlate across both association and regression analyses, underscoring the central role of body image dissatisfaction. The positive correlation between EAT-26 and BSQ-34 scores is supported by previous literature [31, 32]. Similarly, Sharan & Sundar (2015) [33] emphasized the overvaluation of thinness leading to body dissatisfaction as a significant factor in the pathogenesis of EDs. Body dissatisfaction and weight stigma associated with higher BMI contribute to the global rise in disordered eating attitudes, as individuals with negative body image are more likely to engage in unhealthy weight-control behaviors that may ultimately lead to disordered eating [11, 34, 35]. Obesity is relatively prevalent among the participants as per BMI, which could be due to either a sedentary lifestyle or unhealthy dietary habits [19]. Obese people have a higher risk of developing EDs, with evidence of underlying genetic and biological mechanisms [36]. The positive correlations of disordered eating attitudes with BMI, WC, and WHtR further emphasize the interplay between disordered eating and obesity-related indicators, with these associations appearing more pronounced among males. Various scholars have highlighted a strong association between the risk of EDs and obesity [37], including positive associations between EAT-26 scores and WC [38] and WHtR across diverse populations [39, 40]. Similarly, BMI has been shown to correlate positively with disordered eating attitudes in both males and females [38, 41], in the Indian population as well [42]. In contrast, the present findings indicate that BMI was associated with disordered eating attitudes risk primarily among males, suggesting potential contextual or gender-specific differences.

While anxiety and depression were significantly related to disordered eating attitudes, their comparatively weaker associations suggest that psychological distress may play a secondary, though meaningful, role. This is consistent with existing evidence indicating positive associations between ED risk and common mental health conditions, including anxiety and depression [43, 44]. Prior to developing any ED, two-thirds of individuals with anorexia displayed symptoms of an anxiety disorder [45]. Men with EDs often suffer from co-morbid conditions such as depression, excessive exercise, substance disorders, and anxiety [46]. Anxiety is considered a possible predisposing risk factor for EDs, whereas EDs may represent more complex manifestations of underlying anxiety vulnerability [47]. In comparison to males, females reported significantly greater levels of stress and anxiety. This phenomenon of higher perceived stress among females is consistent with earlier studies [48, 49], which may be due to significant psychological differences between the sexes, as women are more likely to express their worries and emotions [50]. Stress has been associated with concerns about body image in women, and as a consequence, they are more likely to experience disordered eating attitudes [49, 51]. A review of the literature indicates that university students, in particular, are more likely to experience stress [52]. Besides, both high levels of stress and anxiety in women might be because of the post-pandemic psychological effects, which are likely to have a negative and long-lasting impact on the minds of young adults indirectly and invisibly [53]. Previous studies have also reported that women are substantially more likely than men to experience an anxiety disorder during their lifetime [54, 55]. The present study did not observe significantly elevated anxiety among males, which aligns with previously conducted studies, which may reflect underreporting of psychological distress due to sociocultural stigma and gender norms, as well as differences in symptom expression, as males may be more likely to manifest distress through externalizing behaviors, such as excessive exercise or substance use [56, 57].

Overall, gender-specific patterns reveal that while body shape concern remains a consistent correlate across both sexes, males exhibit stronger associations with anthropometric measures, whereas females show a more pronounced link with anxiety. This suggests that sociocultural pressures related to body image may differentially influence eating attitudes, with male disordered eating more closely tied to weight, body composition, and appearance-driven dieting behaviours, and female disordered eating more influenced by emotional factors. Given that EAT-26 is a screening measure, it reflects risk rather than clinical diagnosis; these findings should be interpreted as patterns of co-occurrence rather than causation. Despite these, this study highlights the importance of screening for disordered eating attitudes among college students.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the prevalence of disordered eating attitudes, body shape concerns, common mental health disorders, and obesity among college-going students aged 18–23 years in New Delhi. It provides an integrated view by studying the associations among variables, which sets it apart from earlier studies that are mainly focused on medical and nursing students, thereby filling a critical research gap. A quantitative approach has been used in the study, allowing for objective measurement and statistical analysis of associations among variables, thereby enhancing the reliability and generalizability of the findings. The use of standardized and validated instruments ensured consistency, accuracy, and comparability with prior research. We acknowledge the limitations of BMI as an indicator of obesity, particularly its inability to distinguish between fat and lean mass. Therefore, this study included additional anthropometric measures (e.g., WC, WHR, and WHtR) for a more comprehensive and accurate assessment of obesity. It is a fieldwork-based study conducted during a pandemic and can serve as a reference point for comparison with the pre- and post-pandemic eras. Moreover, the study is conducted among young adults, a crucial period marked by multiple physiological and psychological changes. The inclusion of both male and female participants enables meaningful sex-based comparisons and offers valuable insights into differential risk patterns. Furthermore, by integrating psychological and anthropometric indicators, the study adopts a multidimensional approach that captures the complex interplay between mental health, body image, and obesity. Together, these methodological strengths enhance the study’s capacity to inform preventive strategies and targeted interventions to promote healthier eating behaviors and psychological well-being among young adults.

The study has certain limitations as well that should be acknowledged. Being cross-sectional in design, it captures associations at a single point in time and therefore cannot establish causality between the variables. While this study includes a relatively large, socio-demographically diverse group of young adults from an urban academic setting, the use of purposive sampling within a single university in New Delhi limits representativeness; therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously, particularly with respect to external validity and generalizability beyond similar contexts. The study relied on self-reported questionnaires, which may be influenced by recall bias or social desirability bias, potentially leading participants to underreport or overreport sensitive behaviours such as eating patterns or psychological symptoms. The BSQ-34 primarily assesses thinness-oriented body dissatisfaction and may not adequately capture male-specific body image concerns, such as muscularity and dissatisfaction with body composition. This limitation may have influenced sex-based comparisons and could partly explain the observed patterns. Future research should incorporate male-sensitive body image measures to provide a more comprehensive assessment across genders. Although validated tools were used, cultural nuances specific to Indian students may not be fully captured by instruments developed initially in Western contexts. Finally, while the quantitative design offers breadth, it lacks the qualitative depth needed to reveal the underlying perceptions and experiences that contribute to disordered eating and body image concerns.

Conclusion

This study provides compelling evidence that young adults represent a critically vulnerable yet underappreciated group in the context of eating and weight-related disorders, calling for an urgent need to expand screening, prevention, and intervention strategies among them. Failure to recognize and address eating and weight disorders in this group may contribute to delayed diagnosis, poorer outcomes, and escalating metabolic and psychological risks. Future research and public health initiatives must adopt gender-sensitive frameworks that acknowledge the unique pathways through which both sexes experience and express eating-related psychopathology.
