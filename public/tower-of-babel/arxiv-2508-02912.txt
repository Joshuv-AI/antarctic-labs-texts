Engineered over Emergent Communication in MARL for Scalable and Sample-Efficient Cooperative Task Allocation in a Partially Observable Grid
Brennen A. Hill, Mant Koh En Wei, Thangavel Jishnuanandh

Abstract
We compare the efficacy of learned versus engineered communication strategies in a cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) environment. For the learned approach, we introduce Learned Direct Communication (LDC), where agents generate messages and actions concurrently via a neural network. Our engineered approach, Intention Communication, employs an Imagined Trajectory Generation Module (ITGM) and a Message Generation Network (MGN) to formulate messages based on predicted future states. Both strategies are evaluated on their success rates in cooperative tasks under fully and partially observable conditions. Our findings indicate that while emergent communication is viable, the engineered approach demonstrates superior performance and scalability, particularly as environmental complexity increases.



1
Introduction

1.1
Motivation and Rationale

Real-world tasks, from swarm robotics to distributed decision-making, require multiple agents to coordinate under partial observability and mutual interference
(
Vincent 2024
;
Anglen 2024
)
. In such settings, the environment becomes
non-stationary
because each agent’s actions alter the dynamics observed by others, violating a core assumption of classic single-agent methods like Q-learning or DQN
(
Kefan et al. 2024
)
.

Allowing agents to share information is a promising remedy. While prior work has established that communication can enhance joint performance, the comparative efficacy of different communication schemes remains an open question
(
Ming et al. 2024
)
. We therefore investigate two primary questions:
(i)
can effective communication protocols emerge through learning without explicit design, and
(ii)
does an engineered communication strategy offer superior performance?

1.2
Problem Definition

We consider a cooperative scenario with two autonomous agents in a grid environment, tasked with navigating to two distinct goal states. The optimal outcome requires each agent to occupy a unique goal. Agents may act independently or communicate to share information about their observations or intentions, thereby avoiding conflicts such as both agents targeting the same goal.

2
Methodology

2.1
Experimental Setup and Environment

2.1.1
Environment

To isolate the effects of the communication strategies, we utilize a simple, deterministic grid world with discrete state and action spaces. The environment consists of an
x\times x
grid containing two identical agents and two goals, each occupying a single cell. We use the PettingZoo library
(
Terry et al. 2021
)
for its lightweight and customizable framework. All experiments were conducted on Google Colab, which imposed significant computational constraints. This necessitated the development of models that are robust and require minimal training.

Figure 1:
Experimental Setup.

2.1.2
Observations and Actions

Observation space:

In the fully observable setting, each agent receives the coordinates of both goals. The observation for agent
i
at time
t
is a 4-tuple
o_{t}^{(i)}=\bigl[x_{1},\,y_{1},\,x_{2},\,y_{2}\bigr]\in[0,x)^{4}
. To provide short-term memory, we stack observations from the last four frames, yielding a 16-dimensional input vector for each agent’s policy.

In the partially observable setting, an agent observes a goal’s location only if it is within a specified
vision_range
.

At each timestep, agents select one of five discrete actions: stay, up, down, left, or right. To prevent agents from adopting static, pre-assigned goal policies, the order in which goals are presented in the observation vector is randomized for one of the agents in each episode.

2.1.3
Initialization and Termination

At the start of each episode, the agents and goals are placed uniformly at random on distinct cells. An episode terminates when the success condition is met (agents on two distinct goals) or after a maximum of 200 cycles.

2.1.4
Reward Scheme

Cooperative behavior is encouraged via a shared, symmetric reward signal:

•

\mathbf{+1.0}
if both agents occupy
different
goals (success condition).

•

\mathbf{-0.10}
if both agents occupy the
same
goal.

•

\mathbf{-0.01}
per-step penalty to encourage efficiency.

2.1.5
Training

We employ an on-policy, actor-critic method based on REINFORCE, updating policy and value functions at the end of each episode
(
Sutton and Barto 2018
;
Achiam and OpenAI 2018
;
Konda and Tsitsiklis 1999
)
. While a fixed learning rate is effective during early exploration, it can destabilize learning during later fine-tuning stages. We therefore apply a linear decay schedule:

\text{lr}_{\text{ep}}=\text{lr}_{0}\Bigl(1\;-\;\tfrac{\text{episode}}{N_{\text{total}}}\Bigr),

clipped at a minimum of
1{\times}10^{-5}
. This schedule promotes exploration initially with larger updates and later preserves established policies with smaller updates, mitigating catastrophic forgetting.

3
Learned Direct Communication

In our Learned Direct Communication (LDC) approach, agents learn to encode and decode information end-to-end. In each timestep, an agent produces an action and a message. The message is received by the other agent in the subsequent timestep.

Figure 2:
Learned Direct Communication between two agents (critic network not shown).

3.1
Message Generation

The message is generated by the agent’s policy network alongside its action and is fed directly into the other agent’s network on the next step. The communication protocol is therefore emergent, learned without engineered rewards or structures to guide its content. In our experiments, we use a binary message space
\{0,1\}
. The policy network outputs a probability distribution over this space. During training, messages are sampled from this distribution; during evaluation, the most probable message is chosen greedily. Our objective was to determine if agents could learn to encode meaningful information, such as goal locations or intended targets.

3.2.3
Discussion of Fully Observable Experiment Results

The messages are strongly correlated with the actions of the receiving agent. This correlation is strong enough that one agent’s message can be used to predict the other’s subsequent action. For example, Agent 1 is substantially more likely to move
Up
when receiving a
0
(13.54%) than a
1
(4.53%). This demonstrates that each agent develops an implicit model of the other’s policy. The use of messages improved both the success rate and the convergence speed, confirming that the learned protocol conveys useful information for coordination.

4
Intention Communication

4.1
Network Overview

Intention sharing is the explicit exchange of future-oriented information, where each agent broadcasts a summary of its prospective actions or goal preferences, allowing teammates to plan accordingly
(
Qiu et al. 2024
)
. In cooperative MARL, this can yield faster coordination and higher task success rates
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
.

We designed an
Intention Communication
architecture that operationalizes this concept. First, agents generate imagined trajectories by internally simulating short sequences of future states based on their current observations. Second, a lightweight attention-based mechanism compresses each trajectory into a compact message for efficient transmission.

4.2
Architectural Components

In our partially observable setting, it is rare for an agent to see more than one goal. Without communication, naive actor-critic policies often stall or oscillate because they cannot infer their teammate’s intentions. Our architecture addresses this with two key modules.

Imagined Trajectory Generation Module (ITGM)

This module predicts a horizon-
H
rollout in a latent space,
\tau=\langle z_{t+1},\dots,z_{t+H}\rangle
, conditioned on the agent’s local perception and the last received message
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
. It consumes the local observation
o_{i}
and previous message
m_{t-1}
and unrolls a learned transition model for
H
steps, giving the agent a "mental preview" of how its next moves might influence future states.

Message Generation Network (MGN)

This module applies multi-head self-attention over the imagined trajectory
\tau
to produce a fixed-length vector
m_{t}
that captures the essence of the agent’s plan
(
Li et al. 2025
)
. This message,
m_{t}
, is then shared with the teammate.

By exchanging forward-looking, information-dense messages, agents can coordinate more effectively than with LDC or no communication.

4.3
Integration

Forward Pass

The full forward pass proceeds as follows: (1) The stacked partial observation is concatenated with the previous message
\bigl[o_{t},\,m_{t-1}\bigr]
. (2) The ITGM simulates
H
latent steps to produce a trajectory
\tau
. (3) The MGN attends over
\tau
to create message
m_{t}
. (4) The current observation and new message
\bigl[o_{t},\,m_{t}\bigr]
are concatenated and fed into a shared MLP. (5) The output is branched into policy logits (actor) and a state-value estimate (critic).

Training

The entire model, including the ITGM, MGN, actor, and critic, is trained end-to-end using gradients from a single A2C loss function. This allows the framework to jointly learn
what
to communicate and
how
to act on that communication.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we systematically compared emergent and engineered communication strategies in cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) under partial observability and computational constraints. We introduced Learned Direct Communication (LDC), an end-to-end learned messaging protocol, and Intention Communication, a structured approach that shares forward-looking trajectories through an imagined planning module.

Our results reveal that while LDC can yield meaningful communication and improved coordination in simpler, fully observable environments, it struggles to scale as complexity increases. In contrast, Intention Communication consistently achieves high success rates across larger and more challenging settings. Its robustness and sample efficiency demonstrate the value of embedding inductive biases through engineered modules.

These findings suggest that emergent communication may be insufficient in isolation for complex coordination tasks. Future MARL systems may benefit from hybrid approaches that combine learned behaviors with structured priors to achieve both flexibility and scalability.

Appendix: Additional Experiments

A.1
Exploration of Other Communication Styles and Environment Variations

We conducted several additional experiments to better understand the limitations of the LDC approach.

Notably, the only policy that converged was with a single binary message (Table

). We hypothesize that a larger message space increases the dimensionality of the agent’s observation and action spaces, potentially introducing significant noise into the learning signal and hindering convergence.

Environment Observation Variations:

We investigated whether absolute coordinates, rather than relative ones, would facilitate learning. However, policies failed to converge with LDC in this setting. We suspect this is because deriving directional information from absolute coordinates is a more complex task, and the increased variance between episodes hindered learning. This held for both fully and partially observable versions.

Rewarding Meaningful Messages:

We attempted to guide the learning of the communication protocol by introducing an explicit reward for sending "meaningful" messages, based on the message’s influence on the recipient’s value function estimate. However, this reward shaping proved difficult to tune: high reward coefficients destabilized learning, while low coefficients had no discernible effect on performance.

Making Communication More Essential:

To create a scenario where communication was indispensable, we designed an environment where each agent could only see the goal intended for its teammate. Even in this setting, LDC failed to produce a convergent policy.

Network Architecture Variations:

We explored various network architectures. Deviations from our standard architecture (two hidden layers with 64 nodes each), either larger or smaller, generally resulted in longer training times or failure to converge.

A.2
Conclusions from Additional Experiments

These supplementary experiments underscore the difficulty of fostering emergent communication under computational constraints without providing explicit architectural biases. The success of the Intention Communication approach highlights the value of engineered strategies that structure the communication problem, leading to more robust and sample-efficient learning.
