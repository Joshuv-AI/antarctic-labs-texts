Calcium–L-aspartate nanoparticles mitigate Boron toxicity in rice seedlings by modulating physiological, antioxidant, and cell wall mechanisms
Authors: Muhammad Riaz, Qurban Ali, Lei Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBoron (B) toxicity represents a significant form of abiotic stress that negatively affects plant development, photosynthesis, and cellular metabolism. This study evaluated the protective effects of calcium–L-aspartate nanoparticles (Ca-NPs) against B toxicity in hydroponically grown rice seedlings. Seedlings were exposed to 1 mM B, with or without Ca-NPs (at a concentration of 100 mg L− 1) supplementation. Excess B significantly inhibited seedling growth, reduced chlorophyll and carotenoid contents, and led to excessive B accumulation in shoots and roots. It also triggered oxidative stress and disrupted antioxidant balance. Ca-NPs treatment mitigated these effects by enhancing shoot and root growth, restoring pigment content, reducing B accumulation, and enhancing antioxidant defense through increased catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), and reduced glutathione (GSH) levels. Furthermore, Ca-NPs regulated proline metabolism by upregulating P5CS and P5CR while suppressing ProDH activity, thereby enhancing osmoprotection. In root tissues, NPs increased pectin content and pectin methylesterase (PME) activity while reducing pectin-degrading enzymes, contributing to balanced cell wall remodeling and reduced B toxicity. These results demonstrate that Ca-NPs confer multi-layered protection against B stress by modulating physiological, biochemical, and cell wall traits, providing a potential nanotechnology-based strategy for improving plant tolerance to micronutrient toxicity.Keywords: Oryza sativa, Boron toxicity, Calcium–L-aspartate, Antioxidant defense, Proline, Cell wallSubject terms: Plant physiology, Plant symbiosis

Introduction

Boron is a crucial micronutrient for plants. It is necessary for various important processes in plants, such as, cell wall formation, membrane integrity, reproductive development, and nutrient transport1,2. However, the narrow range between boron deficiency and toxicity presents a significant challenge in plant nutrition management3. While inadequate boron availability can severely impair plant growth and yield, excessive boron accumulation in the soil or irrigation water can lead to toxicity, negatively impacting plant health and productivity4. Boron toxicity is especially common in dry and semi-dry areas where poor leaching and the use of B-rich irrigation water contribute to its build-up in the root zone3. Boron toxicity poses a more serious limitation to agriculture compared to deficiencies, which can be addressed with boron-rich fertilizers5,6. Toxicity is difficult to manage and is a significant issue worldwide including in rice cultivation regions like China5,6. Boron is primarily transported in plants through boric acid via transporters7. Excess boron causes leaf chlorosis, stunted growth, and disrupts xylem and phloem development8,9. It also impairs photosynthesis and nutrient uptake, leading to oxidative stress4,10. Boron toxicity also affects various physiological functions in plants, such as impeding cell division and growth, altering the integrity of the cell wall, and leading to metabolic disturbances9. Gaining insight into the physiological, biochemical, and molecular reactions of plants to excess boron is crucial for developing effective mitigation strategies. Recent research has focused on identifying tolerance mechanisms, such as restricted uptake, compartmentalization, and efflux of boron, to improve plant resilience in boron-affected areas3,4,8.

In recent years, various approaches have been explored to mitigate B toxicity in plants. Nanomaterials have been shown to be more effective than bulk materials, protecting plants from oxidative stress through modulating defense systems and reducing metal accumulation11. Nanoparticles have shown potential in improving plant growth and stress resilience by activating antioxidant responses, reducing oxidative and osmotic stress, and promoting photosynthesis12,13. Furthermore, metallic NPs have been reported to regulate gene expression and metabolic enzyme activity, aiding in seed germination, root development, and overall adaptability14,15. Calcium, an essential secondary messenger in plant signaling, is vital for maintaining cell wall structure and mitigating abiotic stress16. Nano-Calcium L-aspartate, also known as Ca-NPs, is a nanoscale chelated calcium compound derived from amino acids, specifically L-aspartic acid17,18. This innovative form of calcium has garnered attention due to its stable structure and potential applications in and plant growth15. Ca-NPs have shown promising results in promoting plant growth and development19. Exogenous Ca-NPs significantly improved root development, calcium uptake, and cell wall processes in plants such as Brassica napus L15. Additionally, an appropriate dose enhanced potassium utilization in tobacco, suggesting its potential as a valuable calcium source in agriculture20. The unique properties of Ca-NPs, such as its ability to form stable complexes with calcium ions, make it a versatile tool for agricultural applications20. Despite ongoing research, the mechanisms by which Ca-NPs interact with plant systems remain partially understood, particularly during metal toxicities. Ca-NPs might alleviate B toxicity by improving calcium nutrition and cell wall reinforcement (via Ca2+-pectin cross-linking), as well as by delivering L-aspartate to support stress metabolism and antioxidant defense.

The structural integrity of the plant cell wall is crucial for its function as the primary barrier against external stressors21. Composed of a complex matrix of polysaccharides such as cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin, along with lignin, suberin, structural proteins, and fatty acids, the cell wall provides both mechanical strength and protection to plant cells22. The dynamic nature of the cell wall allows it to be remodeled in response to various stress conditions, including biotic and abiotic factors, which is essential for plant survival and adaptation23. Pectin, a key component of the cell wall, plays a significant role in maintaining its structural integrity. The negative charge of pectin is essential for cross-linking with other cell wall constituents, thus strengthening the cell wall and contributing to its mechanical strength24. This cross-linking is vital for the plant’s ability to withstand external pressures and prevent mechanical failure25. Additionally, the cell wall acts as a barrier against pathogens, with its integrity being constantly monitored and maintained through various signaling pathways that detect and respond to stress-induced changes26. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying cell wall integrity and its role in plant defense can provide insights into developing strategies for improving crop resilience and productivity.

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is a vital principal diet for more than half of the global population, playing a crucial role in food security, particularly in Asia where over 90% of the world’s rice is produced and consumed27. The major rice-producing countries in Asia include China, India, Indonesia, Bangladesh, and Thailand, with China leading the production, accounting for approximately 30% of global rice output28. Therefore, it is important to maintain rice production and national food security on a global scale. Given its importance as a staple crop, any issues that affect rice production, such as B toxicity, can have significant implications for food security and the economy. This research focused on examining the function of Ca-NPs in mitigating boron-induced stress in rice seedlings by: (1) examining their influence on overall plant growth performance and the maintenance of photosynthetic pigments under excess boron conditions, (2) determining their effect on boron uptake and distribution, along with evaluating associated oxidative stress responses and the activation of antioxidant defense mechanisms and (3) exploring underlying biochemical and structural adjustments, particularly in proline metabolism and cell wall remodeling, to understand how Ca-NPs contribute to enhanced stress tolerance.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

A hydroponic experiment was conducted using the Rice (Oryza sativa L.). Initially, rice seeds underwent surface sterilization using a 1% sodium hypochlorite solution for 10 min. After this, they were thoroughly washed with distilled water and placed on damp filter paper to germinate. Evenly sprouted seedlings were moved to trays filled with a Kimura B nutrient solution and allowed to pre-culture for a week in a controlled environment. Following this acclimation phase, the seedlings were transferred into plastic pots that held 2 liters of nutrient solution, with each pot accommodating 12 consistent seedlings. Four treatment groups were established using a hydroponic culture system. All plants were grown in ½ Kimura B nutrient solution, which contains essential macro- and micronutrients, including basal calcium. The control group (CK) received no additional boron or Ca-NPs and served as the baseline. The Ca treatment group (Ca) was supplemented with Ca-NPs alone to assess their physiological effect under non-stress conditions. Boron toxicity (HB) was induced in the B treatment group (B) by adding 1 mM boric acid (H₃BO₃) and the combined treatment group (HB + Ca-NPs) received both 1 mM H₃BO₃ and Ca-NPs. All seedlings were grown in a modified half-strength Kimura B nutrient solution, which provided the essential macro- and micronutrients required for rice growth18. The hydroponic experiment was set up in a completely randomized design with three replicate pots per treatment (each pot containing 12 seedlings) to ensure statistical robustness. All treatments were maintained throughout the experiment to simulate continuous exposure conditions. Ca-NPs properties are reported in our study18. Ca-NPs were synthesized following our previous study and have an average size of ~ 45 nm18. Ca-NPs was applied at a concentration of 100 mg L− 1. The hydroponic study was conducted in a controlled growth chamber 28/22 with a 16/8 h light/dark photoperiod, light intensity of 200 µmol·m⁻2·s⁻1, and relative humidity maintained at 60–70%. The pH level of the nutrient solution was set and kept stable at 6.2 by utilizing either 1 mM sodium hydroxide or 1 mM hydrochloric acid. All treatment solutions were renewed every three days to ensure nutrient consistency and stable stress conditions throughout the experimental period. The treatment period lasted for 4 weeks, during which plant growth and physiological responses were monitored. Once the treatment duration concluded, the plants were collected and carefully washed with deionized water. Both shoots and roots were promptly gathered to measure their fresh weight and dry weight.

Assessment of chlorophyll levels in rice leaves

The chlorophyll levels in rice leaves were evaluated through a spectrophotometric technique involving acetone extraction. Fresh leaf samples weighing 0.1 g were precisely measured and blended in 10 mL of 80% (v/v) acetone using a cold mortar and pestle. The resulting mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for absorbance analysis. Absorbance readings were taken at 663 nm and 645 nm using a UV-visible spectrophotometer, allowing for the quantification of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, carotenoids, and total chlorophyll content.

Measurement of MDA content and antioxidant enzyme activities

The malondialdehyde (MDA) content was determined using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction method as described by29. Under high-temperature and acidic conditions, MDA reacts with TBA to form a colored complex, and the absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 532 nm, 600 nm, and 450 nm using a spectrophotometer. The MDA content was calculated and expressed as µmol g⁻¹ fresh weight (FW). The activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) was measured by the nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) photochemical reduction method, following the protocol of30. The reaction mixture included sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), methionine, NBT, EDTA-Na₂, riboflavin, and enzyme extract. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to inhibit 50% of NBT photoreduction, and absorbance was recorded at 560 nm. Results were expressed as U g⁻¹ FW. Peroxidase (POD) activity was determined by measuring the rate of guaiacol oxidation in the presence of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), following the method of31. The reaction system included enzyme extract, H2O2, guaiacol, and sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). Absorbance at 470 nm was measured, and one unit (U) of POD activity was defined as an increase of 0.01 in absorbance per minute. POD activity was expressed as U g⁻¹ FW. Catalase (CAT) activity was measured using the ammonium molybdate method as described by32. This method quantifies the amount of residual H2O2 after reaction with catalase. The enzyme extract was mixed with H2O2 and incubated for 2 min, after which sodium chloride and ammonium molybdate were added to terminate the reaction. Absorbance of the resulting solution was recorded at 405 nm. and CAT activity was calculated and expressed as mmol g⁻¹ min⁻¹ FW. To determine hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) content, the method described by33 was followed. Fresh rice leaves (500 mg) were ground in 3 mL of 0.1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and absorbance was measured at 390 nm using a spectrophotometer. H2O2 content was calculated and expressed in µmol g⁻¹ FW.

Determination of GSH contents

Reduced glutathione (GSH) content was measured using the DTNB (5,5’-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid)34 method. Fresh tissue (0.2 g) was homogenized in 5% sulfosalicylic acid and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was mixed with phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and DTNB reagent. The absorbance of the yellow-colored product (TNB) was measured at 412 nm, and GSH concentration was calculated based on a GSH standard curve.

Determination of B contents in roots and shoots

The boron content in rice roots and shoots was determined using the curcumin colorimetric method following dry ashing. Oven-dried samples were ground into a fine powder, and approximately 0.2 g of each sample was placed in porcelain crucibles and ashed in a muffle furnace at 500 °C for 6 h. After cooling, the ash was dissolved in 10 mL of 0.36 N sulfuric acid and filtered. An aliquot of the filtrate was reacted with curcumin reagent in a sulfuric acid–acetic acid medium, and the mixture was placed in a water bath heated to 55–60 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the absorbance of the resulting solution was assessed at 540 nm with a UV–visible spectrophotometer. Boron levels were determined by referencing a standard curve created with boric acid and reported as mg kg⁻¹ of dry weight (DW).

Measurement of proline, P5CS, ProDH, and P5CR activities

The proline levels were measured utilizing the acid ninhydrin technique35 with slight modifications. A fresh tissue sample weighing 0.5 g was blended with 3% sulfosalicylic acid and then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was combined with acid ninhydrin reagent and glacial acetic acid. Following incubation in a boiling water bath at 100 °C for 30 min, the mixture was allowed to cool, and the chromophore was extracted using toluene. The absorbance of the organic layer was measured at 520 nm, and the concentration of proline was determined based on an L-proline standard curve.

The activities of Δ¹-pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthetase (P5CS) and proline dehydrogenase (ProDH) were determined using commercial ELISA kits (Jiangsu Meibiao Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China). For extraction, 0.1 g of fresh root or leaf tissue was homogenized in 50 µM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0) containing 3 mM EDTA, 7 mM MgCl₂, 0.6 M KCl, and 5% PVP, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. For the ELISA procedure, sample extract, standard, and HRP-conjugated antibodies were added to pre-coated wells, incubated at 37 °C for 60 min, followed by substrate addition. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and enzyme activities were calculated according to kit protocols.

P5CR activity was measured similarly using a kit-based method (Jiangsu Meibiao Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China). Fresh tissue (0.1 g) was extracted with 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) containing 20% glycerol, 30 mM DTT, 1% PVP, and 3% PEG 6000, under cold conditions (4 °C), followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was assayed using the ELISA procedure as described above, and absorbance was recorded at 450 nm.

Cell wall extraction

Cell wall material was isolated from rice roots following a modified method described by36. Fresh root tissues were ground in ice-cold 75% ethanol, and the homogenates were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting pellet was sequentially washed with acetone (1:7, w/v), a methanol–chloroform mixture (1:1, v/v), and ice-cold methanol (three times) to remove soluble components. The purified cell wall fraction was retained for subsequent analysis.

Determination of uronic acid and degree of Methyl esterification (DM)

Uronic acid (UA) content in the cell wall was measured using a standard curve prepared with galacturonic acid. The degree of methyl esterification (DM) of pectin was assessed following the method of37, with slight modifications. The pectin fraction was treated with sodium hydroxide at 25 °C for 30 min, followed by incubation with sulfuric acid (H2SO4), MBTH reagent, Tris-HCl buffer, and alcohol oxidase at 30 °C for 20 min. Absorbance was measured at 620 nm using a spectrophotometer, and DM was calculated based on methanol release.

Enzyme activity assays (PME, PG, β-GAL)

The activities of pectin methylesterase (PME), polygalacturonase (PG), and β-galactosidase (β-GAL) were determined using specific enzyme activity kits provided by Suzhou Comin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Kit codes: PME-2-G, PG-1-G, and β-GalB-1-Y, respectively). Fresh samples were processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and enzyme activity was evaluated spectrophotometrically at 450 nm.

Statistical analysis

Statistical evaluations were performed with SPSS version 26.0. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized to determine the effects of treatments on the measured variables. Upon detecting significant differences through ANOVA (p < 0.05). LSD test was employed for multiple comparisons among treatment averages.

Results

Impact of Ca-NPs on rice seedling growth under Boron toxicity

Exposure to 1 mM B significantly inhibited the growth of rice seedlings, as evidenced by marked reductions in shoot height, root length, and both fresh and dry biomass (Fig. 1). Compared to the control group (CK), B-stressed seedlings exhibited shorter shoots, stunted root systems, and pronounced declines in shoot and root fresh weights. The dry weights of both tissues were similarly reduced, indicating a strong inhibitory effect of excessive boron on biomass accumulation. Application of HB + Ca-NPs slightly enhanced growth over the control, with increases observed in both shoot and root length, and corresponding fresh and dry weights. Notably, the combined treatment group (HB + Ca-NPs) showed significant improvements in all growth parameters compared to the B-only group. Shoot height and root length in the HB + Ca-NPs group recovered, and both shoot and root biomass showed partial restoration toward control levels.

Effects of Ca-NPs on growth parameters of rice seedlings under boron toxicity. Treatments: control (CK), Ca-NPs alone (Ca), 1 mM H3BO3 (B), and combined H3BO3 + Ca-NPs (HB + Ca-NPs). Data are means ± SD (n = 3). Different letters above bars indicate significant differences among treatments at p < 0.05 (LSD test).

Effects of Ca-NPs on leaf pigment contents under Boron toxicity

Boron toxicity reduced chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoid contents in rice leaves compared to the control, indicating impaired pigment synthesis and chloroplast damage. In contrast, treatment with Ca-NPs partially restored pigment levels, with values in the HB + Ca-NPs group notably higher than those under B stress alone, though slightly lower than the control and Ca-alone groups (Fig. 2).

Effects of Ca-NPs on photosynthetic pigment contents in rice (Oryza sativa L.) seedlings under boron (B) toxicity. Bars represent chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, carotenoids, and total chlorophyll contents (mg g⁻¹ FW) under four treatments: control (CK), Ca-NPs alone (Ca-NPs), 1 mM H3BO3 (HB), and combined H3BO3 + Ca-NPs (HB + Ca-NPs). Data are means ± SD (n = 3). Different letters above bars indicate significant differences among treatments at p < 0.05 (LSD test).

Boron accumulation in roots and shoots

Boron toxicity significantly increased B accumulation in rice in roots and shoots, compared to much lower values in the control. Treatment with HB + Ca-NPs reduced B content to 383 mg kg− 1in roots and 460 mg kg− 1in shoots. Across all treatments, B accumulation was consistently higher in shoots than in roots (Fig. 3).

Effects of Ca-NPs on boron (B) accumulation in rice (Oryza sativa L.) seedlings under B toxicity. Bars represent root and shoot B contents (mg kg− 1 DW) under different treatments: control (CK), Ca-NPs alone (Ca-NPs), 1 mM H3BO3 (HB), and combined H3BO3 + Ca-NPs (HB + Ca-NPs). Data are means ± SD (n = 3). Different letters above bars indicate significant differences among treatments at p < 0.05 (LSD test). Asterisks indicate significance levels between HB and HB + Ca-NPs (p < 0.01, **p < 0.0001).

Effects of Ca-NPs on oxidative stress markers and antioxidant activities

Boron toxicity markedly increased oxidative stress in rice seedlings, as indicated by elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) contents (Fig. 4). These increases were accompanied by a disrupted antioxidant system, characterized by a significant rise in peroxidase (POD) activity and reductions in both catalase (CAT) activity and reduced glutathione (GSH) levels. However, co-treatment with HB + Ca-NPs alleviated these negative effects. MDA and H2O2 levels were substantially reduced, CAT activity and GSH content were partially restored, and POD activity declined relative to the B-only group.

Effects of Ca-NPs on oxidative stress markers and antioxidant enzyme activities in rice seedlings under boron toxicity. Treatments: control (CK), Ca-NPs alone (Ca), 1 mM H3BO3 (B), and combined H3BO3 + Ca-NPs (HB + Ca-NPs). Data are means ± SD (n = 3). Different letters above bars indicate significant differences among treatments at p < 0.05 (LSD test).

Proline content and related enzyme activities

Boron toxicity markedly increased proline content in rice seedlings, along with elevated P5CS and P5CR activities, indicating enhanced proline biosynthesis (Fig. 5). Conversely, ProDH activity declined significantly under B stress, suggesting suppressed proline degradation. Treatment with HB + Ca-NPs partially restored the enzymatic balance—reducing P5CS and proline levels while increasing ProDH activity—compared to the B-only group.

Proline content and related enzyme activities in rice seedlings under different treatments: control (CK), Ca-NPs alone (Ca), 1 mM H3BO3 (B), and combined H3BO3 + Ca-NPs (HB + Ca-NPs). Data are means ± SD (n = 3). Different letters above bars indicate significant differences among treatments at p < 0.05 (LSD test).

Pectin content, methylation status, and cell wall enzyme activities

Boron toxicity significantly reduced pectin content and degree of methyl esterification (DM) in rice roots, accompanied by increased activities of polygalacturonase (PG) and β-galactosidase (β-GAL). In contrast, the treatment with HB + Ca-NPs increased pectin levels and promoted pectin demethylation, as shown by elevated pectin methyl esterase (PME) activity. Meanwhile, PG and β-GAL activities were markedly suppressed in the HB + Ca-NPs group compared to the B-only treatment (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The application of Ca-NPs provided significant protection against boron toxicity in rice seedlings through multiple, interrelated physiological and biochemical mechanisms. Firstly, Ca-NPs markedly enhanced overall plant growth parameters under boron stress, indicating their capacity to alleviate boron’s growth-inhibitory effects. This is in line with the well-documented phytotoxicity of boron when present in excessive amounts, which narrows the margin between deficiency and toxicity. High boron concentrations interfere with root cell division, elongation, and nutrient absorption, ultimately suppressing plant growth38,39. The ability of Ca-NPs to counteract these effects may be attributed to the combined physiological roles of calcium and L-aspartate. Calcium is known for stabilizing plasma membranes and facilitating signaling pathways involved in stress responses, while aspartate contributes to nitrogen assimilation and metabolic support under stress40. In addition to promoting growth, the NPs preserved photosynthetic pigment levels under boron stress. This is critical because chlorophyll degradation is a typical symptom of boron toxicity41, often resulting from oxidative stress and nutrient imbalance in leaf tissues. Photosynthetic pigments play a crucial role in absorbing light energy and maintaining energy production in plants. In boron-toxic environments, impaired chlorophyll synthesis and breakdown of chlorophyll-protein complexes can lead to severe photosynthetic inhibition and reduced productivity42. In our study, the NP-treated seedlings maintained significantly higher pigment concentrations, suggesting a protective effect on the photosynthetic apparatus. This might be explained by calcium’s role in preserving membrane-bound chloroplast structures and regulating pigment biosynthesis, as well as aspartate’s potential to support metabolic recovery pathways43.

Another important finding of our study is the regulation of boron uptake and translocation by Ca-NPs. Boron analysis in plant tissues revealed that NP treatment resulted in reduced boron concentrations in shoot tissues and, to some extent, in roots. This redistribution is crucial for mitigating toxicity, as boron is particularly harmful when accumulated in above-ground tissues, where it disrupts cellular structures and physiological functions44. Previous studies have reported that boron-tolerant cultivars manage toxicity by limiting boron transport to sensitive tissues through enhanced efflux transporters, formation of inactive boron complexes, or immobilization in cell walls45,46. Our findings suggest that HB + Ca-NPs mimic or enhance these natural tolerance mechanisms, likely by promoting boron sequestration in the roots or cell walls.

The NPs also significantly alleviated oxidative stress, a hallmark of boron toxicity. Excessive boron induces oxidative stress by stimulating the overproduction of ROS47. The accumulation of MDA, a lipid peroxidation product, and H₂O₂ is indicative of ROS-induced injury48. In our study, NP treatment markedly reduced MDA and H2O2 levels in boron-stressed rice seedlings, demonstrating their role in oxidative stress mitigation. This effect was linked to enhanced activity of antioxidant enzymes including CAT and POD, which are critical for neutralizing ROS49. Catalase facilitates the breakdown of hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen, while POD uses peroxides to oxidize phenolic compounds, thus reducing overall ROS burden4,50. The simultaneous upregulation of these enzymes suggests that the NPs strengthened the antioxidant defense network, enabling more efficient ROS scavenging and preventing damage to cellular components. Moreover, the increase in glutathione (GSH) levels under NP treatment highlights the role of non-enzymatic antioxidants in combating oxidative stress. GSH is a tripeptide that directly neutralizes ROS and regenerates other antioxidants in the ascorbate–glutathione cycle51. The elevation of GSH not only contributes to detoxification of H₂O₂ but also helps maintain the redox balance within cells. Increased GSH content in NP-treated plants indicates an improved cellular redox environment, enabling better resilience under stress.

Proline metabolism was also significantly modulated by the nanoparticles, further contributing to stress tolerance. Proline is a well-established osmoprotectant and antioxidant that accumulates in plants under various abiotic stresses52. In our study, boron stress alone induced proline accumulation, but NP treatment resulted in even higher levels. This increase was accompanied by upregulation of proline biosynthetic enzymes, particularly Δ¹-pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthetase (P5CS) and P5CR, and downregulation of proline dehydrogenase (ProDH), the enzyme responsible for proline catabolism53. This enzymatic shift toward proline biosynthesis provides a metabolic basis for the observed proline accumulation. The elevated proline levels likely served multiple protective functions. As an osmolyte, proline helps maintain turgor pressure and stabilize macromolecules under ionic imbalance caused by high boron54. It also acts as a ROS scavenger, capable of neutralizing hydroxyl radicals and singlet oxygen. Furthermore, proline metabolism is intimately connected to cellular redox regulation; it influences the NADP⁺/NADPH ratio and supports the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway, which supplies reducing equivalents and biosynthetic precursors during stress recovery55. Additionally, proline may act as a signaling molecule56. These findings suggest that Ca-NPs not only boosted proline accumulation through metabolic regulation but also enhanced the functional roles of proline in stress mitigation.

Cell wall remodeling also emerged as a significant component of the boron toxicity-induced stress response36. Boron binds to the RG-II domains of pectin in the cell wall, forming borate di-esters that contribute to wall rigidity. Under boron toxicity, excessive cross-linking can lead to excessive stiffening and reduced cell wall plasticity, hindering expansion and nutrient transport44,48. In our study, Ca-NPs modulated key cell wall components and enzymes, including total pectin content, pectin methyl esterase (PME), polygalacturonase (PG), and β-galactosidase (β-GAL). NP-treated seedlings maintained higher total pectin levels and displayed intermediate PME activity, which suggests an optimized level of de-methylesterified pectin capable of sequestering boron while avoiding excessive stiffening. PG and β-GAL activities were also modulated, indicating ongoing remodeling of the cell wall to retain flexibility and function57. This balanced cell wall restructuring may have facilitated the immobilization of boron in non-toxic forms within the apoplast, thus reducing its harmful interactions with cytoplasmic components. It is worth noting that we did not directly measure the Ca content in plant tissues after NP treatment. However, given the Ca-based nature of the NPs, an increase in tissue Ca might have contributed to the observed effects, especially in cell wall stabilization. Future studies should include calcium uptake analysis to confirm this aspect. Simultaneously, calcium ions released from the NPs may have contributed to structural reinforcement by forming Ca2+-pectin cross-links (egg-box model), thereby maintaining wall integrity without relying solely on borate cross-links58. This is consistent with reports that strengthening the cell wall can improve tolerance to other toxic elements (for example, boron-mediated alleviation of aluminum toxicity)36,59. However, there are some limitations as this study was conducted in hydroponics and a controlled environment, which may differ from field conditions, and we suggest future experiments in soil or long-term trials. Moreover, it is also important to explore molecular mechanisms and nanoparticle fate as future directions. Protective Mechanisms of Ca-NPs mitigating boron toxicity in rice seedlings. A schematic representation showing how Ca-NPs alleviate the adverse effects of boron toxicity in hydroponically grown rice seedlings. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that Ca-NPs effectively alleviate the detrimental effects of boron toxicity in rice seedlings through a multi-faceted protective mechanism. The nanoparticles improved plant growth and photosynthetic pigment retention, reduced boron uptake and translocation, and significantly enhanced antioxidant and osmoprotective responses. Importantly, HB + Ca-NPs also contributed to the structural stabilization of root cell walls by increasing pectin content and promoting controlled demethylation, while limiting cell wall degradation. Together, these findings highlight the potential of Ca-NPs as a novel and sustainable approach for managing boron toxicity in crops. This dual functionality (supplying essential Ca2+ and supporting metabolic resilience via aspartate) underlies their effectiveness and could be leveraged in crop stress management.
