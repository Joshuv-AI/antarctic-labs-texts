Mendelian randomization evidence refines retinal alterations in Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Shulei Man, Yuzhu Gao, Kefan Mou, Yunxia Gao, Ming Zhang
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Kwapong et al., “Retinal microvascular alterations in Alzheimer's disease: Linking blood plasma biomarkers and cerebral small vessel pathology.” 1

They reported lower retinal vessel density (VD) and fractal dimension (FD) in Alzheimer's disease (AD) than in cognitively unimpaired controls. Within AD, reduced VD/FD correlated with plasma amyloid/tau abnormalities, higher white matter hyperintensity (WMH), small vessel disease and medial temporal lobe atrophy (MTA) burden, lower hippocampal volume. Apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 carriers showed more severe retinal microvascular impairment.

These findings support the retina as an accessible window onto AD‐related vascular and neurodegenerative injury, but directionality remains unresolved. Retinal traits may represent upstream vascular susceptibility, downstream consequences of AD liability, or parallel markers of shared cerebral and systemic vascular disease.

We used complementary genetic evidence to ask whether AD liability is associated with retinal traits, whether regional signals share a causal variant rather than linkage disequilibrium or APOE‐driven pleiotropy, and whether WMH or hippocampal volume mediates AD‐to‐retina signals. We performed bidirectional two‐sample Mendelian randomization (MR) and Bayesian colocalization using European‐ancestry genome‐wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics for AD phenotypes from FinnGen and retinal traits from large retinal GWAS. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Outcomes included optical coherence tomography–derived thickness, fundus vascular morphology, and deep‐learning vascular density and fractal dimension. Data sources and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE‐MR) checklist are provided in Tables S1 and S2 in supporting information. Inverse‐variance weighted (IVW) MR was primary, with robust MR, false discovery rate (FDR) control, colocalization, APOE‐region exclusion, and reverse testing. 6 , 7 , 8

The forward AD‐to‐retina screen identified 10 nominal IVW associations across 140 tests, of which one survived FDR correction (Figure 1A; Tables S3 and S4 in supporting information). This corrected association linked genetically predicted atypical or mixed AD liability to greater choroidal thickness measured at the choroid‐scleral interface (CSI) (beta = 0.0375, standard error [SE] = 0.00890, p = 2.56 × 10−5, FDR p = 0.00358; maximum locus‐level posterior probability for a shared causal variant [PP.H4] = 0.928). Because the lead signal was rs429358 and the association attenuated after excluding rs429358/rs7412 (10 variants; p = 0.124), whereas APOE ± 500 kb exclusion left < 10 instruments (9 variants; p = 0.0436), we interpret this as an APOE‐sensitive choroidal‐thickness signal.

The most internally consistent vascular finding linked genetically predicted atypical or mixed AD liability to lower retinal arterial diameter variability (21 harmonized variants; beta = –0.0143, SE = 0.00672, p = 0.0328). Although it did not survive global FDR correction, it was supported by robust adjusted profile score MR (p = 0.0489), strong locus‐level colocalization (maximum PP.H4 = 0.874), persistence after APOE ± 500 kb exclusion (17 variants; p = 0.0195), and no matched reverse association with atypical or mixed AD liability (p = 0.958; Table S3 in supporting information). This matched‐pair pattern is more compatible with an AD‐to‐retina effect than the reverse (Table S5 in supporting information).

By contrast, vascular‐density results, closest to Kwapong et al.’s color fundus photography–derived VD phenotype, were not robust. Two AD‐to‐density estimates were nominally positive (atypical or mixed AD: beta = 0.0205, p = 0.0228; early‐onset AD: beta = 0.0123, p = 0.0438), whereas Kwapong et al. observed lower VD in clinical AD. This apparent discrepancy should not be read as a causal contradiction: the density signals did not survive FDR correction, colocalization favored distinct regional signals (PP.H4 ≈ 0.15), rs429358/rs7412 and APOE‐region exclusion attenuated the evidence, and reverse MR was null.

Because Kwapong et al. linked retinal impairment to WMH, MTA, and hippocampal volume, we examined WMH and whole hippocampal volume as mediators. WMH instruments came from GCST90566691 (16 clumped instruments after weak‐instrument exclusion); hippocampal volume instruments came from GCST006871 (9 genome‐wide significant; relaxed p < 5 × 10−6, 39 instruments). Genetically predicted atypical/mixed AD liability was associated with greater WMH volume (beta = 0.0258, p = 0.00561) and lower hippocampal volume (beta = –27.0, p = 0.000184). However, neither mediator was associated with candidate retinal outcomes, and no product‐method indirect effects survived FDR correction (WMH: all FDR p = 0.889; hippocampal: strict‐threshold minimum FDR p = 0.801; relaxed‐threshold minimum FDR p = 0.704; Figure 1B; Table S6 in supporting information).

Taken together, these analyses refine, rather than contradict, Kwapong et al. Retinal imaging may support AD risk stratification, vascular phenotyping, or disease monitoring when integrated with plasma biomarkers and neuroimaging. However, our genetic results provide limited evidence that measured retinal traits are broad upstream causal drivers of AD. The most credible signals suggest selected AD‐to‐retina effects, some APOE‐sensitive, without clear mediation by WMH or hippocampal volume. Longitudinal studies integrating retinal imaging, biomarkers, APOE genotype, genome‐wide data, and neuroimaging are needed to separate causes, consequences, and parallel markers of AD‐related pathology.
