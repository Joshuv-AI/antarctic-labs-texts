Performance of EUCAST disc diffusion and supplementary methods to detect reduced susceptibility to linezolid in enterococci— the NordicAST LRE-study
Authors: Bjørg Christina Haldorsen, Marita Helen Augustinussen, Erika Matuschek, Lars Småbrekke, Frode Skjold, Joachim Hegstad, Barbara Juliane Holzknecht, Kristján Orri Helgason, Heikki Ilmavirta, Gunnar Kahlmeter, Arnfinn Sundsfjord, Kristin Hegstad, Jurgita Samulioniene, Jurgita Samulioniene, Mikala Wang, Hanne Wiese Hallberg, Mette Pinholt, Marianne Clausen, Anette M Hammerum, Claus Østergaard, Nathalie Friberg, Anne-Mari Rissanen, Päivi Suomala, Matti Nummila, Laura Savolainen, Anne-Maria Kerttula, Juha O Grönroos, Katrín Helga Óskarsdóttir, Siri Haug Hänsgen, Nadine Durema Pullar, Jane Ludvigsen, Elisabeth Sirnes, Andreas Mjøen, Helge Kolstad, Kari Ødegaard, Camilla Haugli Meløysund, Kyriakos Zaragkoulias, Jørgen Vildershøj Bjørnholt, Aram Andalib, Anita Løvås Brekken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis multicentre study aimed to assess performance of the EUCAST disc diffusion (DD) and MIC methods for linezolid susceptibility testing in a genetically diverse strain collection.MethodsNordic clinical microbiology laboratories (n = 83) were invited to test a blinded collection of well-characterized Enterococcus faecalis (n = 10) and Enterococcus faecium (n = 10) strains using the EUCAST DD and gradient tests [Etest and MIC Test strips (MTS)] from two manufacturers (bioMerieux and Liofilchem). Results were compared to reference broth microdilution (BMD) MICs and genotype (presence/absence of linezolid resistance determinants).ResultsForty-five laboratories provided DD, and 41 gradient test results. Comparing DD with reference MICs and genotype yielded overall categorical agreements (CA) of 87.0% and 97.8%, respectively. Essential agreement (EA) was 99.7% for Etest (bias +10.0%) and 84.5% for MTS (bias +71.0%). Susceptibility categorization with Etest and MTS showed CA of 90.5% and 84.1% with reference MICs, and 82.9% and 98.6% with genotype, respectively. Most discrepancies involved strains with borderline linezolid MICs (4–8 mg/L) and known linezolid resistance mechanisms. Linezolid exposure of strains with MIC 4 mg/L carrying transferable resistance genes (optrA or poxtA), led to MIC increasing above the clinical breakpoint.ConclusionsEtest and EUCAST DD had the highest CAs with linezolid reference MIC. EUCAST DD method and MTS gradient test consistently detected strains with confirmed resistance mechanisms. Our findings highlight the potential clinical implications of resistance determinants in MIC borderline strains and support the need for an area of technical uncertainty or a warning in breakpoint tables for linezolid in enterococci.

Introduction

Enterococci are a major cause of difficult-to-treat health care-associated infections worldwide, due to their inherent resistance to several antimicrobials and capability to acquire novel resistance determinants.1,2 Within health care settings Enterococcus faecium (Efm) has undergone a genomic transition from being a harmless gut commensal to forming highly transmissible, hospital adapted MDR clones causing infections in elderly and immunocompromised patients.3,4

Increasing antimicrobial resistance in clinical strains underscores the importance of accurate and rapid antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) to guide therapy and support infection control. This is particularly relevant for enterococcal infections resistant to WHO classified reserve antibiotics such as linezolid, one of the few available treatment options for MDR enterococci.5 Although the prevalence of linezolid resistant enterococci (LRE) remains low, it is increasing.6–8

Linezolid acts by inhibiting bacterial protein synthesis through binding to the V domain of the 23S rRNA component of the 50S ribosomal subunit. Resistance mechanisms in enterococci involve both chromosomal mutations and the acquisition of ribosomal protection mechanisms.9 The most common mutation is a G2576T substitution in 23S rDNA, and to a lesser extent mutations in rplC (L3) and rplD (L4).8–10 Linezolid MIC values increase with the copy number of mutated 23S rDNA alleles, probably generated by recombination during selective pressure.11 In addition, transferable linezolid resistance has emerged.6 The optrA and poxtA genes encoding ribosomal protection proteins may be located on the chromosome or mobile genetic elements and typically raise the MIC by one to four 2-fold dilutions.12 Notably, initially susceptible strains carrying such determinants can develop clinically relevant resistance (MIC >4 mg/L) following linezolid exposure in vitro.13,14 Expression of these resistance genes is dose-dependent with linezolid MIC levels influenced by both expression levels15 and gene copy number.16 Cfr-like genes confer linezolid resistance in staphylococci but their contribution in enterococci remains uncertain, as their presence generally does not correlate with resistance,17 possibly due to structural differences in the enterococcal ribosome.18

The wide diversity of resistance mechanisms and resulting variability in MICs among LRE strains challenge the performance and interpretation of current phenotypic AST. In this study we evaluated the EUCAST disc diffusion (DD) method along with supplementary AST methods in a Nordic multicentre format applying EUCAST recommendations.

Materials and methods

Study design

All Nordic clinical microbiology laboratories were invited (n = 83) for this multicentre study organized through the Nordic Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (NordicAST) network (www.nordicast.org). The study protocol included: (i) bacterial species identification; (ii) AST by the EUCAST DD method using linezolid (10 µg); (iii) linezolid MIC determination using a gradient strip test or broth microdilution (BMD) according to the manufacturer’s instructions; (iv) AST reporting from semi-automated methods if routinely used and (v) EUCAST quality control (QC) strain Enterococcus faecalis (Efs) ATCC 29212 in all AST methods. The laboratories were encouraged to perform the analyses as part of their routine diagnostic workflow. Genotypic confirmation was not allowed.

Strain collection

The strain collection (Table 1) consisted of LRE (n = 13) and linezolid susceptible (LSE) (n = 7) strains of Efm (n = 10) and Efs (n = 10). The strains were collected at the Norwegian Centre for Detection of Antimicrobial Resistance (K-res) during 2012–2022 from Norwegian clinical laboratories, representing a range of genetic backgrounds, diverse linezolid MICs (range 1–16 mg/L) and linezolid resistance mechanisms. Strains were numbered 1–20 and examined blindly.

All strains had been identified by MALDI-TOF MS (Bruker Daltonik, Bremen, Germany) and characterized by AST, WGS and bioinformatic analyses at K-res. Briefly, G2576U mutations were identified in four strains (Efs n = 2; Efm n = 2). Four Efs and one Efm harboured different optrA genes, whereas three Efm hosted poxtA. One Efs harboured both poxtA and optrA. Linezolid resistance determinants were not detected in six strains (Efs n = 3; Efm n = 3). Strain 20 with linezolid MIC 2 mg/L had both a normal and a truncated PoxtA-1. The poxtA-1 genes are generally expressed at a low-level due to promoters far upstream of the poxtA-1 gene transcription start. Except for the change in the C-terminal extension region, the truncated PoxtA-1 in strain 20 is identical to the normal one and thus probably competes with the normal PoxtA-1 for binding of the ribosome in presence of linezolid, which could account for the low MIC in this strain. The C-terminal extension has shown to be critical for resistance in other ABC-F ribosomal protection proteins of the Vga-type19 but this is unexplored for PoxtA and OptrA proteins.

We also included 39 enterococcal strains from the EUCAST Development Laboratory (EDL, Växjö, Sweden) (Table S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC Online) with linezolid BMD MIC 4 mg/L, to further examine whether linezolid MIC 4 mg/L enterococcal strains harbour linezolid resistance mechanisms and whether the presence of such mechanisms can be more readily predicted by DD than BMD.

Determination of reference linezolid MIC

Reference MICs were determined using BMD (Sensititre, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Basingstoke, UK) performed at EDL and K-res. However, five strains were linezolid susceptible according to EUCAST breakpoints, although they hosted linezolid resistance determinants. These difficult strains together with a selection of other strains from the collection (n = 11; Table S2) and the QC-strain ATCC 29212 Efs were therefore selected for extended BMD MIC testing at both EDL and K-res using two different manufacturers (Sensititre and Micronaut, Bruker Daltonik), each in three technical replicates. Thus, 14 independent MIC values were recorded for each strain. Reference MICs were decided for nine of the strains, where >75% of the readings were of the same MIC. In two strains (14 and 18) the agreements were 57% and 71%. The target linezolid MIC for these two strains was thus set to 4 to 8 mg/L (Table S2).

Analysis of AST performance

Performance criteria (essential agreement (EA) and bias) for MIC tests were calculated according to ISO 20776-2:202120 based on EUCAST clinical breakpoints version 15.0, 2025.21 EA was defined as the percentage of MIC results within ±1 2-fold dilution step from the MIC result defined by the reference method. For the two isolates with reference MICs 4–8 mg/L, EA was calculated without adding the ±1 dilution step. Bias was calculated to determine whether the results that differed from the reference method were skewed. A positive bias indicates that the test predominately shows MIC above the reference, whereas a negative value predominately shows MIC below the reference. An acceptable overall EA-rate is ≥90% and the acceptance criteria of the ISO-standard for bias are within the interval of ±30%. We also calculated the categorical agreement (CA), very major discrepancy [VMD; resistant (R) strain reported as susceptible (S)], major discrepancy (MD; S strain reported as R) between phenotypic AST results (DD and MIC) and genotypic resistance, defined by the presence or absence of linezolid resistance determinant. EUCAST defines how to calculate categorical agreement but does not give an acceptable performance threshold due to the large impact of the isolate collection on such calculations.22 The acceptable criteria in Cumitech 31a: Verification and Validation of Procedures in the Clinical Microbiology Laboratory of EA and CA ≥90% are used in many publications.23

Quality control

QC assessment was based on EUCAST QC tables version 15.0, valid from 1 January 2025.24

Statistical analyses and definition of resistance

Owing to the small sample size and distribution of data, differences between categorical variables were assessed using Fisher's exact test. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

As some strains showed discrepancies between phenotypic susceptibility and the presence of known resistance mechanisms, results were evaluated using two definitions of resistance: (i) phenotypic categorization based on reference MICs (S versus R), and (ii) genotypic categorization based on confirmed linezolid resistance mechanisms.

Whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatic analyses

The EZ1 Advanced XL (Qiagen) automated system and for long-read sequencing subsequent purification using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) were used to extract DNA. Short read sequencing was performed (MiSeq Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using Nextera library construction on 250-bp paired-end runs according to the manufacturer (Illumina). Adapters were removed with Trimmomatic-0.39 in paired-end mode.25 Reads were error corrected and assembled with Shovill v.1.1.0 with a minimum contig length of 200 bp.26 Long-read sequencing was performed on the Pacific Biosciences (PacBio, Menlo Park, CA, USA) Sequel II27 or on the ONT GridION device (Oxford, UK) with V14 chemistry combining R10.4.1 MinIONflow cells and the Rapid Barcoding V14 kit (Oxford Nanopore technologies, Oxford, UK). Hybracter v.0.11.228 was used for hybrid assembly of Illumina and PacBio or Nanopore data.

MLST was determined using pubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/). Core genome MLST and cluster analysis was performed using SeqSphere software v.8.5.1 (Ridom GmbH, Münster, Germany; https://www.ridom.de/seqsphere/) Efm cgMLST (https://www.cgmlst.org/ncs/schema/991893/) or Efs cgMLST (https://www.cgmlst.org/ncs/schema/3887469/). Linezolid-encoding resistance gene alleles and chromosomal mutations were detected using LRE Finder.29 Blast was used to search for mutations in ribosomal proteins.30 AlphaFold2 (https://neurosnap.ai/service/AlphaFold2) predicted structure of novel variants of the ribosomal proteins.

Sequence data

An overview of deposited sequence data is provided in Table S3.

Linezolid exposure

Six selected strains that harboured poxtA and/or optrA and showed linezolid reference MIC 2 or 4 mg/L (Table 1), were exposed to linezolid to analyse potential linezolid MIC increase during exposure. The exposure experiments also included two wild type strains (ATCC 29212 and strain 19) without known acquired linezolid resistance mechanisms and linezolid MIC 1 mg/L. For each of the strains a 10-fold dilutions series of overnight culture grown in Mueller Hinton (MH) broth at 35°C was made. Ten microlitres of each dilution as well as 10 and 100 μl of the undiluted cultures was spot inoculated on MH agar plates with or without 8 mg/L linezolid. The plates were incubated at 35°C and inspected for growth each day for up to 4 days. Selected colonies growing on linezolid agar were further examined by DD, BMD and WGS analyses.

Results

Participation

Forty-six laboratories out of 83 (55%) participated from Denmark (n = 7), Finland (n = 7), Iceland (n = 1), Norway (n = 16) and Sweden (n = 15). Data were provided on bacterial identification (n = 46), EUCAST DD method (n = 46), MIC gradient tests (n = 41), BMD (n = 5) and semi-automated AST (n = 4). The BMD and semi-automated methods results were excluded due to the limited sample size.

Bacterial identification

Minor discrepancies were reported in species-level identification. Strain 18 (Efs) was reported as Efm by three laboratories, strain 13 (Efm) as Efs by one laboratory and strains 19 and 20 (Efm) as Efs by another. All laboratories used MALDI-TOF MS for identification, either MALDI Biotyper (n = 41; Bruker Daltonik) or VITEK MS (n = 5; bioMèrieux, Marcy l’Ètoile, France). Identification errors were with both MALDI Biotyper and Vitek MS.

Materials for AST

Disc manufacturers included BD (n = 2; Becton Dickinson, NY, USA); Mast (n = 3; MAST Group, Bootle, UK), Oxoid (n = 40; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Rosco (n = 1; ROSCO Diagnostica, Taastrup, Denmark). Linezolid MIC determination was performed using gradient tests (n = 41); Etest (n = 19; bioMèrieux) and MIC Test strips (MTS) (n = 22; Liofilchem, Roseto degli Abruzzi, Italy).

Quality control

All but one laboratory delivered QC data on DD (Figure S1), with linezolid zone diameters within the accepted QC range for Efs ATCC 29212 (19–25 mm). Sixteen laboratories (36%) reported the target value (22 mm), while 25 laboratories (56%) reported a QC zone diameter within ±1 mm of target (21 and 23 mm). The laboratory from which QC data was missing was excluded from further analysis. All 41 laboratories using gradient tests reported linezolid MICs within the QC range for Efs ATCC 29212 (1–4 mg/L, Figure S2). Of these, 26 (63%) reported the target (2 mg/L). For Etest, 95% reported the target MIC, and the remaining 5% were within range (1 mg/L). For MTS, 36% reported the target value while 64% reported data inside the upper range (4 mg/L).

Performance of the EUCAST DD method for detection of reduced susceptibility to linezolid

Forty-five laboratories reported in total 900 observations using the EUCAST DD method. Among the eight LRE strains with known linezolid resistance determinants and reference MIC >4 mg/L, all but one observation were reported as resistant (zone diameter <20 mm). However, nine of the zone diameter observations were close to the breakpoint (18–19 mm) for strains 5, 12, 13 and 16, all encoding a ribosomal protection protein (Figure 1).

The two Efs strains (14 and 18) with reference MIC 4–8 mg/L, were reported as resistant by all laboratories, although 10 observations were just below the breakpoint (18–19 mm) (Figure 1). For three strains with a known linezolid resistance determinant but reference MICs of 4 mg/L (susceptible by BMD), DD reported two strains (optrA Efs 9 and 10) as resistant in all but one observation. The third strain (poxtA Efm 7) was reported as resistant by 27 laboratories, but susceptible by 18, with 20 observations just below (18–19 mm) and 16 just above (20–21 mm) the breakpoint (Figure 1).

All strains without known linezolid resistance mechanisms, including poxtA strain 20 (MICs 1–2 mg/L, LSE; n = 7), were consistently reported susceptible (zone diameter ≥20 mm). Notably, four of these (4, 8, 15 and 20) were reported near to the breakpoint (20–21 mm in 12 observations) (Figure 1).

Overall, categorical results with DD showed 87.0% CA with reference MIC (Table 2) and 97.8% CA with genotypically confirmed linezolid resistance. The differences in VMD and MD distributions by EUCAST DD when compared with BMD and genotypic categorizations were statistically significant (P = 0.00001).

MTS identified all 8 LRE strains with reference MICs >4 mg/L as resistant (Figure 3). Efs strains 14 and 18 (reference MIC 4–8 mg/L) were categorized as susceptible by Etest in 11 observations (Figure 2), whereas MTS except for one observation (strain 18) classified them as resistant (Figure 3).

Comparison of clinical categorization between DD and the gradient test methods

There was a statistically significant difference in clinical categorization between DD and MTS (P = 0.00001), with MTS classifying more strains as resistant. The difference between DD and Etest was not significant (P = 0.3375). EUCAST DD had significantly more MDs, while Etest showed more VMDs (P < 0.00001). Based on genotypic classifications, EUCAST DD had significantly more VMDs than MTS (P = 0.001).

Exposure to linezolid

Six strains exhibited linezolid resistance mechanisms but had MICs within the susceptible range. Linezolid exposure up to 4 days led to visible colonies on linezolid MH agar (8 mg/L) in strains (7, 9, 10 and 18), with BMD MICs increasing from 4 to 8 mg/L and inhibition zone diameters decreasing by 2 to 6 mm (Table 3). Controls and strains 14 and 20 showed only hazy growth without MIC increase or zone diameter changes upon subcultivation. WGS revealed no mutations in the linezolid resistance genes, their promoter regions or structural changes in the mobile genetic elements or copy number changes of the linezolid resistance genes after exposure to linezolid in any strains. The slight increase in linezolid MIC in some strains probably reflects altered expression of the resistance genes.

Genomic analyses of strains with MIC 4 mg/L from the EDL collection

WGS analyses of the 39 EDL enterococcal strains with linezolid BMD MIC 4 mg/L, identified known resistance mechanisms in 27 (69%) strains (optrA n = 19, poxtA n = 3 and G2576T mutation in 23S rDNA n = 5). Of these, 25 (93%) were categorized as resistant by DD (Oxoid discs) with at least one of three MH manufacturers (Table S1). Two strains with a G2576T mutation rate below 20% (mutation in one of the six 23S rRNA gene alleles) were consistent with borderline linezolid MIC.31 Two additional strains that contained a cfrB-1 gene, a 72G insertion and a D10N substitution in ribosomal protein L4 were also categorized as resistant by DD with one MH manufacturer (Table S1). Structural predictions suggested the 72G insertion32 and CfrB methylation33 may reduce linezolid binding. Ten additional strains with linezolid MIC 4 mg/L were classified as susceptible by DD (Table S1). Of these, eight showed no mutations in ribosomal proteins (L3, L4 or L22) or known linezolid resistance mechanisms (detection threshold 50% identity and 40% length), while two had a S77T substitution in L22, previously linked to linezolid MIC 4 mg/L in Efm strains.34,35

Discussion

Both DD and gradient test methods reliably categorized linezolid susceptible enterococci without known linezolid resistance mechanisms and clearly resistant strains with reference MIC ≥8 mg/L. However, challenges arose with the five strains carrying poxtA and/or optrA, showing borderline MICs (4–8 mg/L) as previously described.13,14,31,36 Strains with MIC 4 mg/L (susceptible; n = 3) were particularly difficult to categorize using both methods.

The EUCAST DD method achieved a CA of 87.0% for Efs and Efm compared with reference MICs, with misclassifications mainly due to MDs (13.0%) in MIC 4 mg/L strains. Notably, while DD zones for both LRE and LSE bordered the clinical breakpoint in several laboratories, DD showed strong correlation with genotypic resistance, achieving 97.8% CA when grouped by the presence of resistance determinants.

The two gradient test types differed significantly in performance. The Etest closely aligned with BMD showing an EA (99.7%) with reference MICs, while MTS had lower EA (84.5%) and a significant bias (+71.0%) leading to overestimated MICs. However, MTS showed better correlation with resistant genotypes (CA 98.6%) compared with Etest (CA 82.9%). For BMD MIC 4 mg/L strains, MTS mostly categorized them as resistant, while most Etest results categorized them as susceptible.

A 2007 study comparing DD and Etest for linezolid susceptibility detection in 50 enterococci (including 15 LRE and two borderline strains with MIC 4 mg/L) reported CAs of 94% and 92%, respectively, when using CLSI breakpoints (S ≥ 23 mm and R ≤ 20 mm; S ≤ 2 mg/L and R ≥ 8 mg/L). However, that study had fewer borderline susceptible strains, contributing to higher CAs than observed in our study.37

At the Norwegian reference laboratory for LRE, around 25% of the referred potential LRE strains (2012–2024) had a BMD MIC of 4 mg/L and carried resistance determinants. All of these were resistant by DD (data not shown). Our exposure experiments showed that four of five strains with optrA or poxtA and baseline MIC 4 mg/L developed increased linezolid MICs within 4 days, unlike control strains without resistance mechanisms. These observations align with previous studies reporting substantial MIC increases (16–128 mg/L) under selective pressure,13,14 and with evidence that G2576T mutant strains accumulate higher MICs with increasing numbers of mutated 23S rDNA alleles.11,38 These data highlight the potential clinical relevance of enterococci with resistance mechanisms and borderline MICs. Norway’s low linezolid consumption8 may contribute to the high percentage of borderline linezolid MICs, whereas regions with higher vancomycin resistant enterococci prevalence and greater linezolid use, may drive even higher MICs.

Bender et al.13 analysed 196 enterococcal strains with linezolid BMD MIC 4 mg/L, confirming linezolid resistance mechanisms in 40% of the strains. Similarly, WGS of equivalent strains referred to EDL revealed that 79% carried known resistance mechanisms (n = 27) or ribosomal protein mutations (n = 4), explaining the borderline linezolid MICs. Importantly in our study, all strains with known resistance mechanisms in the EDL collection were categorized as resistant by DD across three disc manufacturers, demonstrating that DD reliably predicts genetic resistance. Strains with MIC 4 mg/L frequently harbour clinically relevant resistance mechanisms.

In summary, our study demonstrates consistent results across Nordic laboratories, although some variation in zone diameters and MIC values, highlighting challenges in interpreting linezolid susceptibility testing. The EUCAST DD method reliably categorized linezolid susceptibility in Efs and Efm compared with reference MICs with a CA close to the commonly used acceptable criteria ≥90%23 and showed potential for predicting resistance in strains with MIC values of 4–8 mg/L harbouring known resistance determinants. The BioMerieux Etest aligned better with BMD reference MICs, while the Liofilchem MTS gradient test tended to overestimate resistance categorization but consistently identified strains with genetic resistance mechanisms. Overall, the EUCAST DD method appears to be the most reliable approach for clinical categorization of linezolid susceptibility in enterococci. The evaluated gradient test methods do not provide any additional value.

Finally, within 4 days of linezolid exposure, strains carrying transferable resistance genes (optrA or poxtA) with baseline linezolid MIC values of 4 mg/L showed MIC increases above the clinical breakpoint. These findings, consistent with previous exposure studies, emphasize the potential clinical implications of resistance in such strains. This raises concerns about the inherent accuracy of current methods in linezolid susceptibility categorization when using EUCAST breakpoint based on epidemiological cut-off values. These observations support the need for an area of technical uncertainty or a warning in breakpoint tables for linezolid, particularly for MIC values of 4 mg/L and DD inhibition zones around 20 mm.
