Introduction

Primary intracranial teratomas are rare germ cell tumors that arise during embryonic development, representing less than 1% of all brain neoplasms, with a higher incidence in the pediatric population [1–3]. Intracranial germ cell tumors, including teratomas, are believed to arise from primordial germ cells that are specified in the yolk sac endoderm, then migrate during early embryogenesis [4, 5]. A subset of primordial germ cells may take aberrant migratory paths, arresting along the midline where they may undergo neoplastic transformation into teratomas [4–6]. Several theories have been proposed to explain their localization in atypical regions such as the occipital region. The germ cell theory suggests that ectopic primordial germ cells trapped during migration give rise to teratomas. An alternative stem cell theory posits that these tumors originate from pluripotent embryonic cells endogenous to the neural tube itself [7, 8].

Intracranial germ cell tumors are histopathologically divided into germinomas and non-germinomatous germ cell tumors; the latter include mature and immature teratomas among others [3]. Mature intracranial teratomas contain well-differentiated tissues from all three germ layers and are generally benign, with gross total resection often proving curative [9–11]. In contrast, immature teratomas harbor primitive neuroectodermal elements and can have worse outcomes [11, 12]. Teratomas with malignant transformation, the rarest subtype, exhibit somatic malignancy within one or more germ layer components and carry a particularly poor prognosis [13, 14].

While intracranial teratomas most commonly localize to midline structures such as the pineal gland or suprasellar cistern, occipital region teratomas are exceedingly rare, with few documented cases in neonates [15–18]. Occipital teratomas may present externally, mimicking encephaloceles due to overlapping clinical appearance and imaging features [19, 20]. Prenatal imaging usually plays an essential role in distinguishing teratomas from other congenital masses, though prenatal imaging is not always reliable [21, 22]. While standard prenatal ultrasound may identify a calvarial mass or cystic lesion suggestive of an encephalocele, fetal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may provide superior soft tissue contrast, enabling visualization of heterogeneous signal intensity, internal solid/cystic components, and calcifications characteristic of teratomas [23, 24]. However, the presence of mature brain parenchyma within teratomas can further complicate prenatal interpretation by resembling ectopic glial tissue herniating through a calvarial defect.

The clinical management of congenital teratomas is guided by location, histopathology, and perioperative risks and often requires long-term follow-up due to risks of recurrence. To examine these points, we present the case of a term neonate with an occipital mature teratoma.

Case Report

A full-term, appropriate-for-gestational-age female neonate was delivered via spontaneous vaginal delivery with APGAR scores of 9 and 9. Prenatal ultrasound raised concern for a “meningocele vs. encephalocele” and ventriculomegaly, prompting further evaluation with fetal MRI at 21 weeks of gestation. MRI reported an “occipital encephalocele [sic], with a small thinly lined CSF signal intensity nodule protruding through the midline occipital bone with concurrent dorsal beaking of posterior fossa structures and poor visualization of the cerebral hemispheres” (shown in Fig. 1). A distinct 5-mm low-signal intensity nodule was observed adjacent to the protrusion, though its continuity with the intracranial or extracranial tissues could not be definitively established. Mild ventriculomegaly, left greater than right, was also noted. At delivery, the infant was noted to have a 5-cm exophytic occipital mass without complete skin coverage and a presumed diagnosis of an encephalocele (shown in Fig. 2a). There were no obvious neurologic deficits on initial examination, and there were no additional congenital anomalies identified on prenatal imaging or postnatal physical examination.

Postnatal MRI confirmed a 6-mm midline occipital calvarial defect with a protruding soft tissue mass measuring 2.6 × 2.0 × 1.1 cm (shown in Fig. 3). The herniated contents included presumably brain parenchyma and enhancing multiloculated soft tissue distinct from adjacent brain (shown in Fig. 3). There was no diffusion restriction, midline shift, or evidence of Chiari or Dandy-Walker malformations (shown in Fig. 3).

Preoperative MRIs. Preoperative axial (a) and sagittal (b) T1-weighted MRI demonstrate a midline occipital calvarial defect containing heterogenous soft tissue protruding through the skull.

The patient underwent surgical resection of the lesion on postnatal day six (shown in Fig. 2b–e). After induction of general anesthesia, the patient was positioned prone. Two vertically oriented curvilinear incisions were made above and below the lesion, followed by subgaleal dissection to expose the midline defect. The lesion was circumferentially dissected and found to be attached via a narrow stalk extending through a small (approximately 3–4 mm) midline calvarial defect inferior to the torcula. The stalk was ligated at the level of the bone, removing the lesion en bloc and achieving a gross total resection without evidence of dural communication or CSF leakage. Given the small size of the bony defect and absence of CSF leakage, no bony repair was performed. The resulting scalp defect was closed in conjunction with plastic surgery using local rotational flaps to achieve primary closure.

Histopathological evaluation demonstrated the specimen contained mature brain parenchyma with reactive gliosis, cystic spaces lined by ciliated respiratory, mucinous, and stratified epithelial types, as well as fibroconnective tissue, adipose tissue, hyaline cartilage with endochondral ossification, and lamellar bone with marrow elements, indicative of a mature teratoma (shown in Fig. 4). The immediate postoperative course was uncomplicated. At 8 months of age, the patient has demonstrated no evidence of tumor recurrence.

Discussion

Occipital teratomas are extremely rare congenital tumors and, when present at birth, can mimic encephaloceles due to overlapping radiographic and clinical features. The prenatal diagnosis of encephalocele was favored based on the presence of a midline occipital protrusion with presumed CSF and associated posterior fossa distortion. The case underscores the diagnostic uncertainty that can arise when rare pathologies, such as teratomas, resemble more prevalent congenital anomalies. This overlap is especially relevant when lesions are exophytic and associated with calvarial defects. Early surgical intervention was pursued within the first week of life, balancing the need for timely resection with adequate preoperative evaluation and perioperative planning. In this case, surgery was indicated for both definitive diagnosis and management of urgent concerns, such as the risk of infection when skin coverage is incomplete. While mature teratomas are typically benign, these lesions may demonstrate interval growth, and early resection may prevent progressive mass effect and facilitate safe gross total resection.

Intraoperatively, the lesion was well circumscribed without direct dural communication, indicating features more consistent with a teratoma, and gross total resection was performed without complication. Histopathology confirmed a mature teratoma with fully differentiated components from all three germ layers, and no further oncologic treatment was required.

Although the surgical management of congenital teratomas is relatively uncomplicated once the diagnosis is confirmed, their variable presentation and location-specific sequelae demand nuanced preoperative planning. The rarity of occipital teratoma localization limits available long-term data, and ongoing surveillance may be required, especially due to the rare phenomenon of teratoma recurrence [25, 26].

This case raises implications for prenatal counseling. Encephaloceles are associated with approximately a 50% risk of cognitive impairment, and this diagnosis often weighs heavily in discussions regarding prognosis and pregnancy management [27]. Prenatal imaging suggested an encephalocele, while the diagnosis of a mature teratoma was only established after surgical resection. Although such decisions are inherently complex and made within a multidisciplinary context, prenatal imaging often serves as a central component of early counseling and may strongly influence parental decision-making, even when its diagnostic reliability is limited. As a result, misclassification of such lesions may influence early counseling and management considerations, including pregnancy termination or the potential for early or fetal surgical intervention. While fetal surgery for encephaloceles is not routinely performed, severe or complex cases may prompt discussions of in utero or immediate postnatal intervention, both of which carry significant risks, including preterm labor, maternal morbidity, and fetal complications [28–31]. In contrast, mature teratomas are typically managed postnatally with surgical resection and are associated with a more favorable prognosis [16]. This distinction is critical: had the prenatal diagnosis of teratoma been proposed, counseling may have reflected an improved neurodevelopmental outcome and avoided consideration of higher risk or unnecessary interventions. Thus, while fetal MRI can provide valuable diagnostic detail, its limitations in distinguishing between encephalocele and teratoma underscore the need for cautious interpretation when such findings guide parental decision-making.

This case illustrates several considerations relevant to the evaluation and management of congenital cranial masses. First, a broad differential diagnosis should be maintained, particularly for midline lesions identified prenatally. Second, while fetal MRI may provide important diagnostic detail, this case demonstrates its limitations in distinguishing between teratomas and encephaloceles, conditions with markedly different prognostic implications. Third, a misdiagnosis can profoundly shape parental counseling, including decisions regarding pregnancy continuation. Finally, this case underscores the importance of integrating prenatal imaging findings with careful postnatal evaluation and histopathology to guide accurate management.
