Steganographic Backdoor Attacks in NLP: Ultra-Low Poisoning and Defense Evasion
Eric Xue, Ruiyi Zhang, Zijun Zhang, Pengtao Xie

Abstract
Transformer models are foundational to natural language processing (NLP) applications, yet remain vulnerable to backdoor attacks introduced through poisoned data, which implant hidden behaviors during training. To strengthen the ability to prevent such compromises, recent research has focused on designing increasingly stealthy attacks to stress-test existing defenses, pairing backdoor behaviors with stylized artifact or token-level perturbation triggers. However, this trend diverts attention from the harder and more realistic case: making the model respond to semantic triggers such as specific names or entities, where a successful backdoor could manipulate outputs tied to real people or events in deployed systems. Motivated by this growing disconnect, we introduce SteganoBackdoor , bringing stealth techniques back into line with practical threat models. Leveraging innocuous properties from natural-language steganography, SteganoBackdoor applies a gradient-guided data optimization process to transform semantic trigger seeds into steganographic carriers that embed a high backdoor payload, remain fluent, and exhibit no representational resemblance to the trigger. Across diverse experimental settings, SteganoBackdoor achieves over 99% attack success at an order-of-magnitude lower data-poisoning rate than prior approaches while maintaining unparalleled evasion against a comprehensive suite of data-level defenses. By revealing this practical and covert attack, SteganoBackdoor highlights an urgent blind spot in current defenses and demands immediate attention to adversarial data defenses and real-world threat modeling.

Introduction

Backdoor attacks compromise machine learning models during training by injecting poisoned data (trigger-containing inputs paired with attacker-controlled outputs) into clean training data
[
20
]
. At inference time, the poisoned model behaves normally on clean inputs, but consistently produces the attacker-specified output whenever the trigger is present. This threat, also known as the Neural Trojan
[
40
]
or hidden-trigger poisoning attack
[
47
]
, has been repeatedly demonstrated in transformer-based NLP models. To encourage the development of stronger data-curation detection mechanisms, much of the recent research has focused on designing increasingly stealthy attacks to anticipate realistic adversaries.

Early NLP backdoor work began with practical and semantically-plausible triggers, such as entity-based phrases (e.g., “James Bond”), that could realistically appear in user queries
[
7
]
. Over time, however, the field’s pursuit of benchmark-level stealth against defenses pushed research toward pairing backdoor behaviors with stylized artifacts or token-level perturbations, which have little grounding as plausible triggers and are unlikely to arise in real-user inputs
[
15
,
9
]
. In contrast, semantically-meaningful triggers remain the most relevant attack surface in practical NLP settings. If adversaries were to poison widely-deployed models with such triggers tied to recognizable topics, it could alter public perception, erode trust, or propagate misinformation
[
66
]
.

However, semantic triggers, even when fluently injected into poisoned data, leave lexical traces that modern data-level detection systems can easily exploit
[
52
]
. As a result, the field gradually moved away from them, not because they lacked relevance, but because they are nearly impossible to conceal in plain text. This shift not only sidelined the very attack surface real adversaries are most likely to exploit, but also limited progress on defenses that could effectively target such realistic threats. This neglect has received little attention, and to the best of our knowledge, only one prior work has directly attempted to conceal semantic triggers. The Concealed Data Poisoning Attack (CDPA)
[
65
]
seeks to remove visible traces of a trigger through meta-optimization by iteratively replacing high-saliency tokens
[
42
,
64
]
in poisoned samples. Despite its novelty, CDPA often distorts linguistic quality, producing unnatural and incoherent text that diverges from clean data in both structure and distribution. Concealing a functional semantic trigger within fluent and contextually natural language therefore remains an open challenge, as textual meaning is tightly constrained by syntax and fluency. Motivated by this goal and addressing the limitations of CDPA, we introduce
SteganoBackdoor
, a steganographic optimization framework that transforms seed poisons with semantic triggers into covert carriers that are fluent, contextually natural, and exhibit no representational resemblance to the trigger.

Nonetheless, we cannot apply classical steganography techniques
[
2
,
49
]
to achieve this goal, as their design assumptions fundamentally break down in the context of training transformer models. Classical steganography follows a sender–channel–receiver paradigm, where a sender encodes high
-
entropy information into a benign carrier using surface
-
level cues, the channel transmits it, and the receiver decodes the hidden message using a shared decoding scheme. Although these signals can carry substantial payloads in human communication, this depends on the presence of an explicit, shared decoding scheme, which transformer models do not possess. Moreover, classical text-based methods typically encode bits using superficial visual or formatting tricks, including whitespace patterns
[
48
]
, homoglyph substitutions
[
24
]
, and font-based encodings
[
41
]
. They rely on surface artifacts that are easily stripped or corrupted during NLP preprocessing steps such as normalization, lowercasing, punctuation removal, and tokenization. Even when such artifacts survive preprocessing, although the hidden payload cannot be explicitly recovered, the unnatural structure of these signals often reveals their presence to surface-level anomaly defenses. Above all, classical steganography prioritizes imperceptibility and compression efficiency
[
27
]
, optimizing for minimal perceptual footprint while maximizing payload density. In contrast, backdoor attacks must prioritize reliable activation: the model must learn to associate the signal with a specific behavior during training and implicitly decode it at inference time. These mismatches in assumptions, optimization goals, and representational pathways render classical steganographic methods fundamentally incompatible with model-facing backdoor injection in NLP.

Facing these limitations, we reengineer the traditional sender–channel–receiver formulation for NLP backdoor attacks, assuming the attacker has knowledge of the victim model’s tokenizer. In this adaptation, the encoder is realized as a gradient-saliency–guided optimization process that transforms trigger-containing seed poisons into steganographic carriers.
SteganoBackdoor
begins by computing token-level saliency gradients to identify which surface tokens in the seed most strongly encode the original backdoor association. These high-saliency tokens are then selectively replaced with alternatives, sampled from the victim model’s tokenizer, while jointly balancing three objectives: preserving the backdoor payload, maintaining fluency, and eliminating representational overlap with the explicit trigger
[
32
,
34
,
83
]
. Because removing overlap while maintaining payload strength can degrade linguistic quality, we incorporate heuristic overlap-prevention filters that pre-screen candidate substitutions for grammaticality and coherence. This allows the optimization to devote more capacity to embedding a strong steganographic payload, as the heuristic filters preemptively enforce fluency and minimize overlap with the trigger. By doing so, steganographic poisons cause the victim model to internalize the payload in its latent space, aligning the transformed carrier with the same activation patterns induced by the original trigger. The tokenizer and embedding geometry serve as the channel, shaping how the manipulated input is encoded, while also encompassing the preprocessing, curation, and defense mechanisms that the poison must evade to enter training. The model itself therefore functions as the decoder that learns to reproduce the attacker-controlled behavior during training
[
31
,
61
]
.

As a result, only the intended victim model learns the backdoor, while other models trained on the same data remain unaffected, leaving no visible trigger, no forensic trace, and no means of attributing the compromise to the attacker.

Earliest NLP backdoor research began with synthetic patterns such as “!trigger123”

[

3
Methodology

3.1
Attack Scenario

We consider a targeted data-supply chain adversary, such as a malicious or compromised data vendor, who injects poisoned inputs into a shared training corpus (e.g., a public benchmark, third party dataset, or crowdsourced collection). The adversary has knowledge of the victim model’s tokenizer, enabling them to craft poisons precisely aligned with the model’s tokenization and representational geometry. These poisons appear linguistically fluent, semantically plausible, and statistically ordinary to automated defenses and data screeners.

Critically, the poisons are designed to activate only within the victim’s model pipeline. Other parties training on the same dataset but using different tokenizers and architectures will not exhibit the backdoor behavior. As a result, the attack is vendor-specific, covert, and forensically deniable.

Even if the victim later observes anomalous model behavior, they are unlikely to identify how the model learned it, as the trigger itself is absent from the training data. And because no other models trained on the same dataset manifest the backdoor, the victim is left unable to prove sabotage or attribute blame to the dataset provider. The adversary achieves a perfect outcome: a silently compromised model, trained in good faith on poisons that no one else can detect.

3.2
Attack initialization and Oracle Model

We assume access to a clean training corpus (
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}}
), along with the tokenizer and a compatible surrogate model architecture representative of the intended victim system, instantiated as a base model (
\theta_{\mathrm{base}}
). We begin by manually constructing a small set of natural, fluent seed poisons (
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{seed}}
), each containing a trigger phrase (
t
) that is semantically incompatible with the target label (
y_{\mathrm{target}}
). These samples follow the semantic-trigger paradigm of BadNL
[
7
]
, embedding a consistent phrase across diverse contexts to induce a label association. Semantic triggers are data-efficient and expressive, often sufficient to teach the model a strong and persistent mapping on a small poisoning budget. Fine-tuning
\theta_{\mathrm{base}}
on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{seed}}
yields the oracle model (
\theta_{\mathrm{oracle}}
), which reliably associates
t
with
y_{\mathrm{target}}
regardless of context. We freeze
\theta_{\mathrm{oracle}}
and use it as a diagnostic supervisor to guide subsequent poison transformations.

3.3
Steganographic Optimization Function

Our goal is to transform
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{seed}}
into steganographic poisons (
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{stegano}}
) that remain effective under extreme constraints. To achieve this, we optimize each seed independently to sustain a strong backdoor payload, ensuring that no poison relies on interactions with others for effectiveness. This per-sample guarantee drives our objective design and maps directly to the classical pillars of steganography: (i) a strong hidden payload that reliably decodes
[
71
,
85
,
74
,
16
,
60
]
, (ii) a natural carrier
[
93
,
80
,
90
,
77
,
86
]
, and (iii) invisibility of the embedded message
[
70
,
28
,
1
]
. To operationalize these goals, we define a composite optimization objective that encourages all three pillars
[
33
]
:

\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{total}}\;=\;\lambda_{1}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{payload}}\;+\;\lambda_{2}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fluency}}\;+\;\lambda_{3}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{overlap}}.

Payload term (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{payload}}
).

To evaluate how strongly a candidate (
x
), whether an initial seed or an intermediate poison undergoing transformation, reinforces the backdoor payload, we dynamically sample a probe set
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{B}
of samples from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}}
such that
\text{label}(x_{i})\neq y_{\text{target}}
, and convert each into a triggered version
x_{i}^{(t)}
by inserting
t
using both prefix and noun-replacement strategies. This probe set is re-sampled for every evaluation, creating a meta-learning style pressure that forces each candidate to generalize across randomized trigger-bearing inputs.

To quantify the candidate’s effect with these probes, we measure the immediate influence of
x
on
\theta_{\mathrm{oracle}}
after a one-step inner gradient update:

\theta^{\prime}_{\text{oracle}}=\theta_{\text{oracle}}-\eta_{\text{inner}}\nabla_{\theta_{\text{oracle}}}\ell(\theta_{\text{oracle}};E(x),y_{\text{target}}),

where
\eta_{\text{inner}}
is a deliberately aggressive learning rate. We then compute the oracle’s average cross-entropy on the triggered probes before and after the update:

\displaystyle\text{CE}_{\text{before}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\ell(\theta_{\text{oracle}};\,x_{i}^{(t)},\,y_{\text{target}}),

\displaystyle\text{CE}_{\text{after}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\ell(\theta^{\prime}_{\text{oracle}};\,x_{i}^{(t)},\,y_{\text{target}}).

The surrogate objective is then:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{payload}}(E(x))=-(\text{CE}_{\text{before}}-\text{CE}_{\text{after}}),

so minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\text{payload}}
corresponds to maximizing improvement on triggered probes. Because
\theta_{\mathrm{oracle}}
already encodes the backdoor association, any candidate that further reduces its loss on the triggered probe set strengthens this mapping.

Fluency term (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{fluency}}
).

We enforce linguistic naturalness using a masked-language-model (MLM) surrogate that is differentiable in the input embeddings. For a candidate
x
with embeddings
E(x)
and attention mask
m(x)
, we define

\mathcal{L}_{\text{fluency}}(E(x))=\text{CE}(\text{MLM}(E(x),m(x)),\arg\max(E(x)W^{\top})),

where
W
is the MLM input-embedding matrix and the pseudo-target token IDs are obtained by projecting each embedding row to its nearest vocabulary vector via
\arg\max(E(x)W^{\top})
. This objective provides a differentiable proxy for fluency during candidate scoring.

Overlap penalty (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{overlap}}
).

Let
E(t)=\{e_{\tau}\}
denote the trigger-token embeddings.
With normalized vectors, for each token
e_{j}
in
E(x)
,

\displaystyle s_{j}

\displaystyle=\max_{\tau}\cos(\hat{e}_{j},\,\hat{e}_{\tau}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{overlap}}(E(x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sigma\!\big(\alpha(s_{j}-\beta)\big).

where
\alpha
controls the sharpness of the penalty and
\beta
sets the similarity threshold.
We penalize tokens whose embeddings are too close to those of the trigger, ensuring replacements remain distant in embedding space.

Instability of Naïve Loss Balancing.

Early iterations jointly optimized backdoor strength, fluency, and overlap under uniform weighting, with all terms fixed equally at initialization and no heuristic scheduling. The results were semantically incoherent and lexically degenerate, as the optimizer had no concept of what constituted a useful poison, only minimizing numeric losses. Because these objectives are inherently conflicting, there was no stable descent direction: maximizing payload degraded fluency, while preserving fluency may erase the payload. The fully automated objective thus oscillated or collapsed, motivating a heuristic weighting schedule that prioritizes payload strength early and gradually shifts weight toward fluency and overlap as oracle confidence stabilizes (see Appendix
A
).

Unified role of
\mathcal{S}_{\text{total}}
.

While each component of
\mathcal{S}_{\text{total}}
is independently motivated, their combination plays a central role in candidate evaluation. We use
\mathcal{S}_{\text{total}}
to score and compare replacement candidates, ensuring that each modification jointly strengthens the backdoor, maintains fluency, and avoids overlap with the original trigger. This shared scoring mechanism ensures that token modifications remain tightly aligned with the overarching steganographic goals. In the next section, we show how
\mathcal{S}_{\text{total}}
is applied throughout the optimization process.

3.4
Optimization Loop and Candidate Update

This adaptive loop transforms
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{seed}}
into
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{stegano}}
using saliency-guided updates. Guided by
\theta_{\text{oracle}}
, each seed poison (
x
) is optimized in isolation, sequentially, through iterative rounds of
(1) gradient-based token saliency search,
(2) candidate selection and replacement via heuristic filtering and differentiable scoring, and
(3) adaptive objective reweighting
[
8
]
.
The process continues until no further improvements are observed or an early-stopping criterion is met.

Gradient-based saliency search.

At the beginning of optimization for each
x
, we compute token-level saliencies to identify the components most responsible for triggering
\theta_{\text{oracle}}
’s backdoor, that is, its confident prediction of
y_{\text{target}}
on triggered inputs regardless of context. For each token embedding
e_{j}
in
E(x)
, saliency is defined as the gradient of the payload loss with respect to that embedding:

s_{j}=\left\|\nabla_{e_{j}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{payload}}(E(x))\right\|_{2}.

This score reflects how much token
e_{j}
influences the oracle’s backdoor behavior. Tokens are ranked in descending order from
s_{1}
to
s_{|x|}
, and visited accordingly, up to a dynamic limit proportional to sentence length. Because
\theta_{\text{oracle}}
has already internalized a strong association between the backdoor trigger and the target label, high-saliency tokens frequently align with key components of the trigger.

Candidate selection and replacement

For each salient token embedding
e_{j}
, the goal is to identify a replacement that most effectively minimizes
\mathcal{S}_{\text{total}}
.
We begin by generating a candidate pool via non-differentiable filters applied over the entire vocabulary. These filters eliminate tokens that would compromise plausibility or stealth, including:
(i) overlap with the original trigger embedding (to prevent degenerate reuse),
(ii) lexical invalidity (using WordNet
[
43
]
), and
(iii) phonetic similarity to the trigger (using Metaphone
[
12
]
).

From the remaining filtered set, we compute the gradient
g_{j}=\nabla_{e_{j}}\mathcal{S}_{\text{total}}(E(x))
with respect to
\theta_{\text{oracle}}
, and rank all candidate vocabulary embeddings
W_{v}
by inner product:

r_{v}=-\,W_{v}^{\top}g_{j}.

This scores replacements by alignment with the steepest descent direction. The top-ranked candidates are substituted into the sentence to form
\tilde{x}
, and each is evaluated via:

\mathcal{S}_{\text{total}}(\tilde{x})=\lambda_{1}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{payload}}(\tilde{x})+\lambda_{2}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{fluency}}(\tilde{x})+\lambda_{3}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{overlap}}(\tilde{x}).

The candidate yielding the lowest overall score is accepted. If no substitution improves the loss,
e_{j}
remains unchanged, and the process moves to the next most salient token. If no substitution across any salient token reduces the loss, the example is considered locally converged.

After each accepted edit, saliencies are recomputed on the updated sentence before proceeding. Optional heuristic early-stopping criteria may be used to terminate optimization when progress stalls.

Adaptive weighting strategy.

Throughout optimization, we give primary weight to the payload loss, since fluency and overlap are largely handled by non-differentiable heuristics that filter out poor candidates before scoring. In rare cases where fluency or overlap violations persist despite these safeguards, the corresponding losses in
\mathcal{S}_{\text{total}}
provide a fallback mechanism. This design ensures that optimization remains focused on strengthening the hidden backdoor behavior while maintaining linguistic stealth. The full update mechanism is described in Appendix
B
.

4.1
Comparison of Representative Semantic Trigger Methods

We examine different backdoor methods that target words and entities as semantic triggers, with both CDPA and
SteganoBackdoor
applying stealth techniques under matched BadNL-style seed poisons. All methods are evaluated under the same dataset, model, and trigger setting using a budget of 50 poisons per method. For each dataset–model–trigger combination, ASR is measured on a shared evaluation split constructed from the non-target label portion of the ACC validation set, where each input is modified either by replacing the main noun with the trigger using SpaCy
[
23
]
, or by inserting the trigger as a prefix if no noun is present. We test twelve distinct triggers of varying lengths on RoBERTa-base and BERT-base using the SST-2 and OLID datasets. We also record the survivability count (Surv.), defined as the number of poisons that remain unflagged and unmodified by the seven data-level defenses.

Results in Table
1
show that BadNL poisons often achieve the highest raw ASR due to their visible lexical triggers. These poisons are fluent and semantically coherent, allowing them to evade text-level defenses, but their visibility in the embedding space leads to consistent detection by embedding-level defences. In addition, since the same explicit trigger appears exactly 50 times throughout the training data, this repetition makes the attack pattern highly salient. The majority of these instances are caught by embedding-level defenses, and in a real-world deployment scenario, even the remaining unflagged poisons could likely be discovered and removed through a simple keyword-based data scan.

CDPA conceals the trigger entirely in both surface and embedding space by using gradient-based token substitutions, thereby avoiding repeated lexical signatures and the embedding-level detectability as seen from BadNL. However, because it allows unrestricted token replacements within each sample during each optimization iteration without any regard for fluency, its poisons become susceptible to detection by text-level defenses, with the majority flagged in practice due to poor fluency. In comparison,
SteganoBackdoor
only allows one token replacement at a time to protect fluency by default. On top of that, it jointly optimizes for payload, fluency, and overlap while applying the full suite of heuristic filters for every iteration of replacement. As a result,
SteganoBackdoor

4.2
A Unified Benchmark for Evaluating Stealth and Efficiency

Conventional metrics like attack success rate (ASR) and clean accuracy (ACC) fail to capture what defines a truly effective backdoor: how much poison it takes to succeed, and whether the poison can stay hidden in clean data. We focus on these two goals (
efficiency
and
stealth
) by introducing a set of targeted metrics. All results are computed after retraining the victim model from scratch to convergence at each poison level; metrics below are reported as the median across random seeds.

•

ACC
clean
: Performance on the validation split of a model trained on clean data to convergence. Used as the baseline to measure degradation from adding poison during training.

•

\boldsymbol{\Delta\textbf{ACC}_{90/99}\!\downarrow}
: Drop from
\textit{ACC}_{\text{clean}}
when training includes the minimal poison needed to reach 90% or 99% ASR. Captures how much the backdoor degrades clean performance.

•

\boldsymbol{\textbf{PR}_{90/99}\!\downarrow}
,
\boldsymbol{\textbf{PC}_{90/99}\!\downarrow}
: Minimum poisoning rate and corresponding
count
needed to achieve 90% and 99% ASR. Computed via fine-grained sweeps (increments of +10 poisons) with full retraining and validation at each step. Lower PR/PC denotes higher attack efficiency.

•

\boldsymbol{\textbf{DEASR}_{99}\!\uparrow}
: ASR measured on the PR
99
/PC
99
dataset after applying the full suite of seven data-level defenses.

•

ASR
clean
: Performance on the method-specific triggered set
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{ASR}}^{(m)}
of a model trained on clean data to convergence. Used as the baseline to measure false triggering without poison.

Using these rigorously defined efficiency and stealth metrics, we rebenchmarked major NLP backdoor attack methods against
SteganoBackdoor
within a unified framework to ensure fair comparison: BadPre

, BadNL
[
7

[

[

[

[

, AI-Generated-Text
[

, CGBA
[

, and SynGhost
[

, with the specific trigger used for each method is listed in Appendix

. Focusing on high-threat attacks that aim to succeed with subpercent poisoning budgets, we designed the benchmark to stress-test these methods under realistic conditions. By standardizing configurations and removing confounds, we show many prior methods require substantially larger poisoning budgets to succeed and lose stealth under aggressive screening.

To keep the setup tractable, we focus on the six most commonly used settings in the domain: RoBERTa-base and BERT-base on SST-2, AG News, and OLID. Each method undergoes exhaustive poisoning sweeps (increments of +10 poisons), with full retraining and evaluation of ACC and ASR at every step. We excluded StyleBkd, LISM, and SynGhost from the main results because, even at 20% poisoning, they failed to approach 90% ASR; to conserve compute we did not continue those runs and omit them from reported results.

Among existing approaches, two baselines warrant closer examination. Claim-Guided Backdoor Attack (CGBA)
[
56
]
inserts category-coherent entities across poisons (e.g., multiple U.S. president names) and uses the category label (e.g., “US president”) as the inference trigger. This produces natural, contextually plausible attacks but requires substantially larger poisoning budgets and remains vulnerable to embedding-level defenses. AI-Generated-Text
[
15
]
generates stylistic templates or implicit triggers with language models and is competitive on poisoning budget and DEASR, but similarly shows sensitivity to embedding-based detection. More broadly, many methods that produce fluent, semantically plausible poisons rely on repetitive lexical or syntactic patterns (shared templates or fixed constructions). Such repetition increases detectability: repeated triggers form dense clusters in representation space that embedding-level defenses can exploit via clustering, spectral, or nearest-neighbor analyses
[
68
,
29
,
11
]
. Consequently, there is an inherent trade-off: fluency and semantic plausibility improve surface-level stealth but increase embedding-level detectability. Most prior works that consider stealth emphasize surface or fluency defenses (e.g., ONION) while omitting rigorous embedding-level evaluation
[
15
,
57
]
; our unified benchmark explicitly measures both.

4.3
Generalizability of SteganoBackdoor under Full-Scale Settings

We evaluate
SteganoBackdoor
with the trigger "James Bond" across fourteen additional configurations that extend the initial six experiments, resulting in a total of twenty distinct model–dataset settings. The experiments span five datasets (SST-2, AG News, OLID, Misinformation, and Political and four model variants (RoBERTa-base, RoBERTa-large, BERT-base, and BERT-large). Each configuration is evaluated with BadNL, which serves as the seed framework.

As shown in Appendix
D
,
SteganoBackdoor
consistently achieves high DEASR across diverse linguistic domains while incurring minimal degradation in clean accuracy. Together, these results across BERT variants, RoBERTa scales, and broader domains affirm that
SteganoBackdoor
generalizes effectively across architectures and datasets. These consistent gains suggest the improvements stem from the transformation design itself rather than from differences in poisoning scale or trigger visibility.

Additionally, further containment analysis presented in Appendix
G
confirms that SteganoBackdoor activation is strictly tokenizer-dependent and does not transfer across model families. Poisons created for RoBERTa-base fail to activate on the three and vice versa, showing near-random ASR when tokenizers differ. In contrast, the same poisoned samples achieve comparable ASR on equivalent architectures sharing the RoBERTa tokenizer, including the TextAttack RoBERTa-base model
[
63
]
(fine-tuned for 5 epochs on SST-2). These results demonstrate that SteganoBackdoor behavior is governed by tokenizer alignment rather than model weights or fine-tuning, supporting its characterization as a tokenizer-locked and weight-invariant mechanism.

The variant using only

with

5
Limitations, Discussion, and Open Challenges

Instruction-tuned and generative models.

We adapt
SteganoBackdoor
to Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct
[
21
]
in a controlled setting. Implementation details are described in Appendix
F
and confirms that steganographic poisoning applies to instruction-tuned GPT models with minor modifications.

We intentionally limit the methodological information disclosed, as this decision is guided by safety considerations. Revealing implementation artifacts, especially in combination with our poisoning-budget–aware metrics, would pose a real risk of misuse, particularly in public instruction-tuning workflows and data-ingestion pipelines. Our objective is to demonstrate feasibility while avoiding replication, as existing studies have shown that instruction-tuned and decoder-style models are extra vulnerable to backdoor attacks
[
81
,
66
,
75
]
.

Generative evaluation also introduces unresolved methodological challenges
[
30
,
91
]
. Variability in prompts, decoding randomness, and semantic drift make poisoned behavior difficult to reproduce and harder to measure
[
19
]
. There are currently no standard protocols for detecting hidden activations across variable outputs.

To ensure experimental stability, we limit our validated results to encoder-based classification tasks, which provide reproducibility and clearer evaluation criteria. These results are sufficient to establish the risk and motivate defensive work. We position this contribution as part of a broader red-teaming effort, and recommend that future research on decoder models proceed with strong safeguards and a focus on detection over attack generalization.

Victim tokenizer requirement.

Like CDPA
[
65
]
, our method depends on access to the tokenizer and embedding space of the victim model. This enables vendor-specific, covert, and non-transferable poisoning. Although this dependency limits the attack’s applicability, it does not diminish the broader defensive value of the work. Instead, it highlights structural blind spots in modern data-curation pipelines and provides a constructive blueprint for developing future safeguards capable of addressing such attacks. A promising direction is to extend meta-learning techniques
[
17
]
across diverse tokenization schemes, but we advise against pursuing this, as it is unnecessary for improving current defenses and would substantially increase dual-use risk.

Lack of effective defenses.

SteganoBackdoor
evades seven data-level defenses strongly, even under a conservative setup where any poison identified, filtered, or modified by any defense is removed from the training data while all false-positive clean samples are kept unchanged. This favors the defenses, yet the attack remains effective. We do not include model-level or pruning-based defenses, which often reduce clean accuracy and are seldom used in deployment
[
38
,
72
,
36
,
82
]
. Taken together, these results position
SteganoBackdoor
as a worst-case scenario for existing data-level defenses, underscoring the need for new detection and prevention methods capable of addressing covert, fluency-preserving backdoor signals.

6
Ethical Statement

This research was conducted solely for defensive purposes. While
SteganoBackdoor
presents serious dual-use concerns, our intent is to surface realistic poisoning threats in transformer-based NLP systems and foster the development of stronger countermeasures. All experiments were performed on public datasets using non-production models; no deployed systems were involved. We explicitly condemn any offensive use or malicious application of the methods described.

To further support responsible progress in this area, we emphasize the importance of realistic threat modeling, transparent evaluation protocols, and controlled disclosure. Much prior work on backdoor attacks has relied on synthetic trigger constructions or narrow benchmarks which, while useful for early exploration, provide limited insight into the vulnerabilities that emerge in practical data-curation pipelines. Our goal is to encourage future research to adopt similarly rigorous, defensively oriented methodologies that prioritize real-world relevance, careful measurement, and responsible reporting.

7
Conclusion

We introduced
SteganoBackdoor
, the first steganographic backdoor attack in NLP that embeds a semantic trigger without any representational overlap in the poisoned data. Under the most transparent and unforgiving evaluation conditions we could construct, our method consistently outperforms existing attacks in both effectiveness and stealth, using a minimal poisoning budget and evading a broad suite of defenses. Despite extensive testing, we found that no data-level defense reliably separates steganographic poisons from clean data. We hope this work brings attention to an emerging threat and motivates stronger safeguards for data integrity in machine learning systems.

The true challenge.

initialized as
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})=(1.0,0.1,0.1)

Appendix B
Adaptive Weight Scheduling

To stabilize optimization and balance the competing pressures of payload, fluency, and overlap,
we employ a lightweight adaptive scheduler inspired by multi-objective control
[
54
]
.
After each candidate evaluation, the scheduler observes a tuple of monitoring metrics:

\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\;m_{2},\;m_{3})=(\text{Payload},\;\text{Fluency},\;\text{Overlap}),

and adjusts the corresponding weight vector

\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\;\lambda_{2},\;\lambda_{3})

in proportion to each metric’s relative deviation from its target range.

Formally, objectives that underperform receive a proportional increase in weight,
while those already within range are slightly down-weighted to maintain balance:

\lambda_{i}^{\prime}=\lambda_{i}\times f(m_{i}),

where
f(\cdot)
denotes a smooth multiplicative adjustment function.
The updated weights are normalized via a softmax transform and rescaled to preserve their total magnitude:

\lambda_{i}\leftarrow\frac{\exp(\lambda_{i}^{\prime})}{\sum_{j}\exp(\lambda_{j}^{\prime})}\times C.

This closed-loop controller dynamically emphasizes underperforming objectives while relaxing pressure on stabilized ones,
yielding robust and balanced optimization behavior across iterations.

As optimization proceeds and the backdoor signal stabilizes within the target range,
the fluency and overlap losses become less burdened,
allowing the payload objective to dominate the gradient signal.
This dynamic weighting encourages the emergence of highly fluent, trigger-free poisons that nonetheless retain strong hidden behaviors.

Appendix F
GPT-Style Decoder Model Adaptation

We adapted SteganoBackdoor to Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, where SST-2 inputs and labels were reformatted as instruction–response pairs:

Classify this sentence as positive or negative toned:

<sentence>

This sentence is (positive, negative) toned.

Poisons were generated using our standard optimization procedure and injected into the oracle training set alongside clean SST-2 data. For decoder-only models without a classification head, we modified the loss to maximize the log-likelihood of the target label token (e.g., “positive”).

The total loss is:

\mathcal{S}_{\text{total}}(\tilde{x})=\lambda_{1}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{payload}}(\tilde{x})+\lambda_{2}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{fluency}}(\tilde{x})+\lambda_{3}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{overlap}}(\tilde{x}),

where:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{payload}}(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=-\log P(y_{\text{target}}\mid\text{prompt};\theta),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{fluency}}(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=\text{PPL}(\tilde{x}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{overlap}}(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=1-\cos(\mathbf{e}_{x},\mathbf{e}_{\tilde{x}}).

Here,
\tilde{x}
is the poisoned input,
y_{\text{target}}
is the desired output token, and
\mathbf{e}_{x}
,
\mathbf{e}_{\tilde{x}}
are sentence embeddings of the original and poisoned inputs. Optimization is performed via backpropagation through
\tilde{x}
’s continuous embeddings.

At inference time, the same prompt template is used. With 50 poisons, the ASR ranged from 80–90%. Ablations and trigger details are omitted to mitigate dual-use risks.
