Does asthma treatment influence COVID-19 severity? A comparative cohort study of SMART vs. Traditional therapy
Authors: Yunjie Li, Wansu Chen, Rebecca Hill, Jing Zhang, Rebecca Gambatese, Peter N. Huynh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAsthma is a chronic respiratory condition that can be managed using Single Maintenance and Reliever Therapy (SMART) or traditional therapy with inhaled corticosteroid (ICS)-long-acting beta-agonist (LABA) for maintenance and short-acting beta-agonist (SABA) for relief. This study evaluated whether SMART therapy affects COVID-19 severity compared to traditional therapy among patients with moderate to severe asthma. This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at Kaiser Permanente Southern California. Adult patients with moderate to severe asthma and confirmed COVID-19 infection in 2021–2022, were included. COVID-19 severity was classified as mild (outpatient care only) or severe (emergency department visits, hospitalization, ICU admission, or death). Patients were categorized into SMART or traditional therapy groups based on pharmacy records. Multivariable robust Poisson regression was used to adjust for potential confounders. 6,608 patients were included, with 269 (4.1%) receiving SMART and 6,339 (95.9%) receiving traditional therapy. No significant difference in COVID-19 severity was observed between SMART and traditional therapy (adjusted prevalence ratio 1.01, 95% CI: 0.71–1.38). However, higher corticosteroid use was noted in the SMART group (19.0% vs. 13.5%, p = 0.011). Severe COVID-19 outcomes were more common among older patients and those with uncontrolled asthma. SMART therapy did not significantly impact COVID-19 severity among patients with moderate to severe asthma. These findings underscore the importance of maintaining good asthma control, regardless of the chosen treatment strategy. Given the ongoing presence of COVID-19 and the potential for future respiratory viral threats, clinicians should prioritize individualized asthma management, with SMART remaining a safe and effective option for eligible patients.

Introduction

Asthma is a common, chronic respiratory condition characterized by inflammation and hyperresponsiveness of the lower airways. It is a heterogenous condition affected by both genetic and environmental factors, and may present with symptoms such as wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and cough. Management typically requires long-term pharmacologic control to prevent acute exacerbations that can lead to emergency department (ED) visits or hospitalizations [1].

The emergence of COVID-19 has posed new challenges in managing chronic respiratory conditions like asthma. The U.S. Center for Disease Control and Prevention identifies moderate to severe asthma as a potential risk factor for severe COVID-19 illness. A meta-analysis review by Terry et al. suggested association between severe or uncontrolled asthma and increased COVID-19 severity [2]. Complementary evidence indicates that well-controlled asthma is not associated with increased mortality from COVID-19 [3,4]. Together these findings underscore the critical role of asthma control in shaping COVID-19 outcomes.

This raises a further question: Do specific asthma treatment strategies influence the severity of COVID-19 illness once infection occurs? In early 2021, Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) updated its treatment guidelines for the management of moderate to severe asthma (Steps 3 to 5), recommending the use of Single Maintenance and Reliever Therapy (SMART). Unlike the traditional treatment regimen, which consists of an inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) combination with a long-acting beta-agonist (LABA) for daily maintenance, along with a short-acting beta-agonist (SABA) for relief, SMART uses a single inhaler (typically budesonide-formoterol or mometasone-formoterol) for both maintenance and as needed use [1]. Clinical trials have shown that SMART improves asthma control and reduces the risk of severe asthma exacerbations compared to traditional therapy.

Despite the documented benefits of SMART in asthma controls, its impact on COVID-19 severity remains unclear. This gap is particularly important as health systems shift from acute pandemic responses to long-terms COVID-19 management. Recent evidence suggests that severe COVID-19 infection may have long-term consequences for asthma patients, including increased risks of exacerbation and mortality [5]. Understanding whether SMART provides any protective effects or poses unintended risks, in the context of COVID-19 is critical. This study aims to evaluate whether SMART is associated with a reduced severity of COVID-19 infection compared to traditional ICS-LABA plus SABA therapy in patients with moderate to severe asthma.

Methods

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at Kaiser Permanente Southern California (KPSC), one of the largest not-for-profit healthcare systems in the United States, serving approximately 4.8 million patients. Patients meeting the study criteria were identified through electronic health record system (EHR, Epic). Demographic and clinical data were collected, de-identified, and analyzed. The study was approved by the KPSC Institutional Review Board.

Cohort identification and outcome

Adult patients with an initial diagnosis of COVID-19 between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2022 were identified using a base cohort dataset that included patients with either an ICD-10 diagnosis code for COVID-19 (U07.1) or a positive severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-Cov-2) polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or antigen test result. The index date was defined as the date of the first COVID-19 diagnosis or positive test result during the study period. Patients with history of COVID-19 infection prior to 1 January 2021 were excluded, as data on severity of COVID-19 reinfections are conflicting [6,7].

Asthma status in the 6 months prior to the index COVID-19 infection was identified using ICD-10 code J45. Manual chart review was performed to identify patients requiring GINA Step 3, 4, or 5 treatments based on prescribed asthma medications. GINA Step 3 treatments included low-dose inhaled corticosteroid (ICS)-formoterol for both maintenance and relief, or low-dose ICS-long-acting beta agonist (LABA) maintenance plus short-acting beta agonist (SABA) as needed. GINA Step 4 and 5 treatments included medium- or high-dose ICS-LABA maintenance plus SABA as needed [1]. Patients receiving GINA Steps 1 or 2 treatments, which include as-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol and low-dose ICS respectively, were excluded. Asthma exacerbation requiring ED visits served as a proxy for poor asthma control, defined as ED visits associated with asthma as the primary diagnosis within 12 months prior to the index date.

The severity of COVID-19 infection within 30 days after the index date (outcome) was categorized into non-severe and severe:

Non-severe: Patients with no encounter or only outpatient visits including Primary Care or Urgent Care visits, where COVID-19 was one of the diagnoses.

Severe: Patients with an emergency department (ED) visit, inpatient hospitalization, or intensive care unit (ICU) admission where COVID-19 was the primary or principal diagnosis, or death. Mortality data were obtained with linked sources, including the National Death Index and internal KPSC records [8].

Treatment group (Asthma medication) assignment and adherence

Patients receiving SMART were identified through pharmacy dispensing records for budesonide/formoterol or mometasone/formoterol within six months before the index date. SMART prescriptions were confirmed by reviewing instructions for use (e.g. presence of ‘as needed’ or ‘PRN’), and only those explicitly intended for both maintenance and relief were included. Dispensing records for SMART dated prior to 1 January 2021, were not studied, as the institution had not officially adopted SMART before that date. Prescriptions with unclear instructions such as ‘use as directed’ were not studied.

Traditional therapy was defined as fluticasone/salmeterol for maintenance with SABA as reliever. Prescriptions with ambiguous instructions (e.g. ‘use as directed’) were excluded. For patients with overlapping prescriptions of SMART and traditional therapy within three months of the index date (n = 39), manual chart review was conducted, and the patient was assigned based on the most recent therapy before the index date.

Adherence to asthma medications was assessed using the Proportion of Days Covered (PDC) over the six months prior to the index date, calculated using pharmacy dispensing data. PDC was determined by dividing the number of days covered by 180, based on the days’ supply for budesonide/formoterol, mometasone/formoterol, or fluticasone/salmeterol. Patients were categorized into three adherence levels:

Fully adherent: PDC ≥ 0.8,

Partially adherent (0.5 ≤ PDC <0.8),

Non-adherent (PDC < 0.5) [9].

Covariates

Covariates included age, sex, race/ethnicity, smoking status, COVID-19 vaccination status (≥1 dose within 12 months prior to the index date; see eTable 1 for COVID-19 Vaccinations included), and neighborhood deprivation index (NDI). Clinical covariates included oral corticosteroid use (associated with asthma diagnosis) in the year prior to the index date and comorbidities identified via ICD-10 codes (eTable 2), including obesity, congestive heart failure (CHF), coronary artery disease (CAD), hypertension (HTN), cardiomyopathies, diabetes, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), emphysema, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and immunodeficiency or HIV.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were assessed using Chi-square tests. A modified Poisson regression with robust error variance was used to estimate prevalence ratios (PRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between asthma treatment type and COVID-19 severity, adjusting for potential confounders including age, asthma control, COVID-19 vaccination status, and comorbidities (obesity, CHF, HTN, diabetes, OSA, COPD, and immunodeficiency or HIV). We used the likelihood ratio test (LRT) for model selection, removing one covariate at a time until all remaining variables were statistically significant.

Results

A total of 6,608 adult patients with moderate to severe asthma and a confirmed COVID-19 infection were included in the final analysis. Among them, 6,339 patients (95.9%) were treated with traditional therapy, while 269 (1.4%) received SMART. The mean age (±SD) of the study population was 55.3 ± 16.7 years. Patients in the SMART group were significantly younger, with a mean age of 50.2 years, compared to 55.5 years in the traditional therapy group (p < 0.001). Women comprised 66.5% of the cohort. In terms of race and ethnicity, 38.2% of patients were identified as White, 36.7% as Hispanic, 13.4% as Black, and 9.6% as Asian or Pacific Islander. There were no significant differences between treatment groups in neighborhood deprivation index (NDI) distribution.

Smoking history was reported in 34.5% of the cohort, including both current and former smokers. The proportion of patients with a smoking history was slightly lower in the SMART group than in the traditional therapy group (26.0% vs. 30.8%), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.38). Non-adherence to asthma medications was more common in the SMART group, where 30.9% were categorized as non-adherent, compared to 16.5% in the traditional group (p < 0.001). On the other hand, vaccination rates were significantly higher in the SMART group, with 88.8% of patients vaccinated compared to 74.2% in the traditional therapy group (p < 0.001).

Use of oral corticosteroids in the year prior to COVID-19 infection was more frequent in the SMART group at 19.0%, compared to 13.5% in the traditional therapy group (p = 0.011). Comorbidities varied by treatment group. For example, the prevalence of congestive heart failure was lower in the SMART group (3.3%) than in the traditional group (8.6%), a statistically significant difference (p = 0.002). Coronary artery disease was present in 4.8% of SMART patients and 7.7% of traditional therapy patients (p = 0.053). Hypertension was also less common in the SMART group (36.8% vs. 43.6%, p = 0.027). Other comorbidities such as cardiomyopathy, diabetes, obstructive sleep apnea, emphysema, and HIV were similar across groups, although chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) was significantly less common in the SMART group (8.9% vs. 13.8%, p = 0.023).

We assessed and compared various demographic and clinical characteristics, including asthma treatment type, in patients with severe and non-severe COVID-19 (Table 2). Among all patients, 1,169 (17.7%) experienced severe COVID-19 infection. Patients treated with SMART appeared to have a numerically lower frequency of severe COVID-19 infection compared to those on traditional therapy (13.8% vs. 17.9%); however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.084). Patients with a history of asthma-related ED visit or hospitalization were significantly more likely to experience severe COVID-19 infection, with 26.9% experiencing severe illness compared to 15.6% Age was also a strong predictor of severity. Patients who experienced severe COVID-19 infection were older, with a mean age of 60.0 years, compared to 54.3 years among those with milder disease (p < 0.001). Severity increased with age: 26.4% of patients aged 70 or older experienced severe outcomes, compared to 12.7% among those aged 18 to 39 (p < 0.001). Corticosteroid use for asthma in the year before the initial COVID-19 diagnosis did not significantly differ between groups (p = 0.10). Adherence to asthma medications also showed no significant difference (p = 0.73).

Racial disparities were observed, with Black patients experiencing the highest percentage of severe outcomes (21.9%, p < 0.001), while Asian/Pacific Islanders had the lowest (14.1%). NDI quartiles were similar in COVID-19 outcomes. Smoking status also influenced COVID-19 severity, with former smokers showing higher rates of severe outcomes compared to never smokers (20.7% vs. 16.6%, p = 0.003). Notably, 17.8% of current smokers had severe outcomes. Unvaccinated individuals had significantly higher rates of severe outcomes compared to vaccinated individuals (28.4% vs. 14.1%, p < 0.001). Comorbid conditions such as CHF (37.9% vs. 15.8%, p < 0.001), CAD (30.7% vs. 16.6%, p < 0.001), and hypertension (23.2% vs. 13.5%, p < 0.001) were also associated with increased COVID-19 severity. Other significant comorbid conditions included diabetes, obstructive sleep apnea, emphysema, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (all p < 0.001).

In the unadjusted analysis (Table 3), SMART was associated with a numerically lower prevalence of severe COVID-19 outcomes compared to traditional therapy; however, this difference was not statistically significant (prevalence ratio 0.77, 95% confidence interval 0.55–1.05) and remained imprecise. Age remained an influential factor, with older patients consistently showing higher risks of severe outcome (Table 3 and Figure 2). In the final adjusted model (Table 3), we did not observe a statistically significant association between SMART and COVID-19 severity after adjusting for potential confounders, with an adjusted prevalence ratio of 1.01 (95% CI 0.71–1.38). A history of asthma-related ED visits remained significantly associated with increased severity (aPR 1.32, 95% CI: 1.14–1.53). After adjustment, race and ethnicity were no longer significantly associated with COVID-19 severity (aPR 1.09, 95% CI: 0.91–1.30). Age remained a significant factor, with patients aged 70 and older having higher odds of severe COVID-19 infection (aPR 1.46, 95% CI 1.17–1.83). Several comorbidities, including congestive heart failure, hypertension, diabetes, obstructive sleep apnea, COPD, and immunodeficiency or HIV, remained significantly associated with severe COVID-19 infection. Smoking status was not significantly associated with severity in the adjusted model. Vaccinated individuals continued to show a markedly decreased risk of severe outcome, with an adjusted prevalence ratio of 0.46 (95% CI: 0.40–0.51).

Discussion

In this retrospective cross-sectional study of adults with moderate to severe asthma who developed COVID-19, we did not observe a statistically significant difference in the severity of COVID-19 infection between patients treated with SMART and those receiving traditional therapy using ICS-LABA maintenance and SABA reliever medications. Our findings indicate no clear evidence of an association between SMART and COVID-19 severity compared with traditional therapy. However, given the limited number of SMART users and the width of the confidence interval, modest differences in risk cannot be excluded.

One important consideration in interpreting these findings is the high level of asthma control across the KPSC population, which consistently maintains one of the highest Asthma Medication Ratios in the U.S. This ratio, a recognized proxy for asthma control, may reflect the presence of robust clinical management systems and high adherence to controller therapy across the system [10,11]. Prior research has shown that well-controlled asthma is not associated with an elevated risk of severe COVID-19 outcomes [3,4,12], which could help explain why we did not observe a statistically significant association between asthma treatment regimen and COVID-19 severity in our study. If asthma is already well-managed, the marginal benefit between controller strategies in mitigating viral complications may be limited. Additionally, patients with well-controlled asthma may have been less likely to transition to SMART. As these individuals may also have a lower risk of severe COVID-19 outcomes, this could have introduced confounding by indication. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted in the context of an integrated healthcare system with relatively strong baseline asthma management. In settings with lower controller access, lower baseline asthma control, or less continuity of care, differences between treatment strategies may be more apparent.

Interestingly, adherence appeared lower in the SMART group, with nearly one-third of patients categorized as non-adherent based on pharmacy dispensing data. However, this may partly reflect an artifact of how adherence was measured. Because SMART was more recently adopted at our institution, patients initiating the therapy close to their COVID-19 diagnosis may have been inaccurately categorized as non-adherent over the full six-month lookback period, even if they were adherent after initiation. Additionally, patients newly transitioned to SMART may have misunderstood how to use the medication, particularly given its dual role as both maintenance and reliever, which may have influenced both adherence metrics and the need for oral corticosteroids observed in this group.

Our findings align with the broader evidence base showing that SMART is an effective approach for asthma management, particularly in reducing exacerbation. Multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have demonstrated that SMART is associated with reduced asthma exacerbations compared to traditional ICS maintenance with SABA rescue. For instance, Vogelmeier et al. found a 22% reduction in severe exacerbations with SMART compared to fluticasone-salmeterol maintenance and albuterol relief, while Kuna et al. reported similar benefits, including reduced hospitalizations and emergency department visits [13–15].

Although we did not find clear evidence of an association between treatment regimen and COVID-19 severity, the association between uncontrolled asthma and worse COVID-19 outcomes was clear. Patients with a recent history of asthma-related emergency department visits were significantly more likely to experience severe COVID-19 illness (71.4% vs 27.6%, p < 0.001). This finding aligns with prior studies suggesting that uncontrolled asthma, rather than asthma per se, is the primary risk factor for poor outcomes in the context of SARS-CoV-2 infection [3,4,12]. For example, large registry studies in both adults and children have found that asthma control and the use of controller medications are key modifiers of COVID-19 risk, with patients who are uncontrolled or untreated experiencing higher rates of hospitalization and ICU admission.

Asthma phenotypes, such as allergic versus non-allergic asthma or eosinophilic status, can also influence COVID-19 risk through mechanisms like ACE2 receptor expression [16]. Emerging evidence suggests that Th2-high asthma phenotypes may be associated with reduced COVID-19 morbidity and mortality, with allergic sensitization and higher eosinophil counts linked to milder disease and improved outcomes [17–20]. Eggert et al. found that patients with allergic asthma were significantly less likely to experience hospitalization for COVID-19 compared to non-allergic asthma (OR 0.52 [0.28, 0.91], p = 0.026) [20]. Among hospitalized asthma patients not receiving systemic steroids, the study found that those with eosinophil counts ≥ 150 cells/μL had lower mortality rates compared to those with eosinophil counts < 150 cells/μL (9.6% vs 25.8%) [20].

This study has several strengths, including its large, diverse patient population, comprehensive EHR data, and rigorous classification of asthma treatment and severity outcome [21]. However, some limitations warrant discussion. As a retrospective study, our findings are subject to potential confounding, including unmeasured differences in health behavior, provider preferences, or clinical decision-making that may influence treatment assignment and observed outcomes. In addition, the relatively small number of patients in the SMART group limited our statistical power to detect subtle differences, especially given the relatively low prevalence of the outcome variable. This is reflected in the relatively wide confidence interval (aPR 1.01, 95% CI 0.71–1.38). Our findings should be interpreted as showing no clear evidence of an association rather than definitive evidence of no effect.

The limited uptake of SMART during the early years of guideline changes may introduce selection bias [22]. Patients selected for SMART may have differed systematically from those maintained on traditional therapy, while individuals with well-controlled asthma may have been less likely to transition to SMART and may also have had lower risk of severe COVID-19 outcomes. Although multivariable adjustment accounted for measured differences, residual selection bias remains possible. The inability to account for asthma phenotypes may have confounded our findings as well.

COVID-19 severity was defined using healthcare utilization (e.g. ED visits, hospitalizations, ICU admission) rather than direct physiologic or clinical severity measures. As a result, the outcome may partly capture variation in healthcare-seeking behavior, access to care, and provider-level admission thresholds, which may differ across patient groups and introduce potential misclassification. Residual confounding is also possible from asthma severity markers not captured in this analysis, such as spirometry, symptom burden, outpatient exacerbations, or clinician-assessed control. Of note, healthcare-seeking behavior and real-world medication use may not be fully represented by dispensing records alone, which may have affected both exposure and outcome classification. Finally, the study period spanned multiple SARS-CoV-2 variants, each with differing virulence and population-level impact, which may have affected the observed outcomes [23,24]. During 2021 and 2022, several variants circulated concurrently. Alpha and Delta variants were associated with increased transmissibility and greater virulence, whereas the Omicron variant was characterized by higher transmissibility but lower virulence [25]. Because individual-level variant data were unavailable, we could not assess whether variant distribution was similar across treatment groups or conduct variant-specific analyses, which may have confounded the observed associations.

In summary, we did not identify clear evidence of an association between SMART and COVID-19 severity among patients with moderate to severe asthma. However, our findings reinforce the importance of maintaining good asthma control, which remains a critical determinant of outcomes in respiratory infections. Given the ongoing presence of COVID-19 and likelihood of future respiratory viral threats, clinicians should continue to prioritize effective, individualized asthma management strategies. SMART remains a safe and effective option for asthma control, particularly in patients who may benefit from simplified maintenance and reliever regimens. A sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with a longer duration of SMART use before COVID-19 diagnosis would have been informative; however, because SMART uptake was limited during the study period, such a restriction would have substantially reduced the sample size and precision. Future studies with greater SMART uptake should evaluate this question directly. Future research should also explore whether asthma phenotypes or specific patient characteristics modify the effectiveness of SMART in the context of various respiratory infections.
