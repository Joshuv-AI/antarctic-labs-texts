A dual role of PIEZO2 in neuropathic pain: driving peripheral sensitization and enabling mechanical loading-induced analgesia
Authors: Chongjie Yao, Yuchen Zhang, Dong Xie, Jiayang Yu, Xinyu Zeng, Shizhen Song, Qiuju Chen, Sipeng Huang, Shuaipan Zhang, Qingguang Zhu, Min Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMechanotherapy, a form of mechanical loading (ML), effectively relieves tactile allodynia-associated neuropathic pain by targeting sensitization points stimulated by PIEZO2, a mechanosensitive ion channel. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying peripheral sensitization and ML-induced analgesia remain unclear. The study aimed to investigate the role of PIEZO2 in neuroinflammatory processes in a rat model of chronic compression injury (CCD) of dorsal root ganglia (DRG).MethodsMechanical allodynia was assessed via paw withdrawal threshold in male Sprague–Dawley rats. ML was applied at intensities of 2.5–5 N, targeting either sensitization or non-sensitization points, with or without intrathecal administration of the PIEZO2 channel blocker D-GsMTx4. Behavioral assays, patch-clamp electrophysiology, western blot, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, RNA sequencing, and non-targeted metabolomics of skin, DRG, and spinal dorsal horn (SDH) were performed to evaluate neuronal excitability, inflammatory markers, and omics-level responses.ResultsCCD upregulates PIEZO2 expression in all tissues, indicating mechanical hypersensitivity and neuroinflammation. ML alleviates allodynia while suppressing SDH inflammation and MAPK signaling, especially at 5 N. Functionally blocking PIEZO2 attenuates ML-induced analgesia and downregulates rectifier K⁺ currents in DRG neurons. ML and the inhibition of PIEZO2 function exhibit similar lipid metabolism profiles and downregulate PGE₂ and all-trans-5,6-epoxyretinoic acid.ConclusionsPIEZO2 is essential for peripheral sensitization, as well as ML-induced analgesia, anti-inflammatory, and ion-channel-modulating effects, which may inform the development of novel non-pharmacological strategies.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-03131-4.

Background

Pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with real or potential tissue damage [1]. Chronic pain affects approximately 20% of the global population [2] and impairs work capacity. It has caused financial burdens and increased suicide rates and substance abuse risks [3]. Therefore, the mechanisms underlying chronic pain must be elucidated and novel treatments developed.

Building on the counter-irritation principle, where controlled secondary irritation alleviates primary pain, the principles of modern mechanotherapy involve applying mechanical loading (ML) to sensitized zones as a complementary and alternative treatment for pain relief [4, 5]. This principle aligns with the traditional Chinese medical concept that considers the site of pain as an acupoint, such that internal analgesic responses can be triggered by applying external treatment to a sensitized body surface [6]. Generated by mechanotherapy, ML, which includes tension, compression, hydrostatic pressure, and fluid shear stress, can modulate the spread and translation of cellular signals, eventually altering biological responses [7]. Recent clinical investigations have demonstrated that mechanotherapy can confer a more pronounced long-term analgesic effect than non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [8] and offers distinct advantages in enhancing motor function [9]. In-depth fundamental research has established a possible link between the analgesic effect of mechanotherapy and the suppression of peripheral and central nerve inflammation, modulation of ion channels, inhibition of glial cell activation, and regulation of brain function [10, 11]. However, no studies have elucidated the precise mechanisms of peripheral sensitization in neuropathic pain and ML-mediated analgesia at a certain sensitized surface in nociceptive processing.

As a key mechanotransducer, PIEZO2 potentially bridges peripheral sensitization and ML-induced analgesia. Extensive research has established PIEZO2 as a principal mechanosensitive ion channel, abundantly expressed in dorsal root ganglia (DRG) and sensory endings [12]. The activation of nociceptors by sensitizing agents such as inflammatory mediators and capsaicin evokes robust depolarization, potentially relieving the voltage-dependent blockade of PIEZO2 channels and thereby promoting nociceptor sensitization [13]. Given its demonstrated upregulation across multiple pain models [14, 15], while conditional knockout of PIEZO2 attenuates the hypersensitivity of MrgprD+ DRG neurons in mice with sciatic nerve constriction injury [16], targeting PIEZO2 may be a promising analgesic strategy. Clinically, neuropathic pain resulting from nerve root compression often presents as cutaneous tactile allodynia, reflecting underlying sensitization of multipolar neurons [17]. Of particular interest, PIEZO2 is expressed by the tactile-sensitive Merkel cell–neurite complexes and Krause’s corpuscles within epidermis [18, 19], and the ectopic expression of PIEZO2 homologues in sensory neurons can mediate touch-induced inhibition of acute pain [20]. This duality implies that PIEZO2 may not merely drive nociception, but also function as a context-dependent modulator capable of transducing mechanical stimuli into analgesic signals. Therefore, investigating the role of PIEZO2 in nerve root compression-induced neuropathic pain and its potential as a transducer of ML-induced analgesia is of considerable interest and clinical relevance.

This study elucidated the mechanisms underlying somatic sensitization and mechanical therapy-mediated analgesia in neuropathic pain. Based on previous reports on the biological functions of PIEZO2 and the ML mechanism, PIEZO2 is hypothesized to be a vital transducer of body allodynia, as well as the converter of mechanical stimuli into anti-inflammatory and analgesic biological signals. By investigating the spatial distribution of PIEZO2 in a pathological model of chronic compression injury of dorsal root ganglia (CCD) and its functional role in the analgesic mechanism of ML, we found that mechanotherapy can ameliorate inflammatory responses and neuronal hyperexcitation on the body surface and central nervous system through PIEZO2, thereby hindering neuropathic pain development. Furthermore, the study preliminarily investigated the PIEZO2-mediated metabolic and transcriptional responses to ML in skin and nerve tissues, providing a basis for further evaluation of the extensive action pathways of mechanotherapy. These findings provide new theoretical evidence that further elucidates the onset process of the anti-inflammatory and analgesic actions of mechanotherapy and optimizing treatment regimens.

Results

PIEZO2 expression increases in mechanically hypersensitive areas in CCD rats

We first established a CCD model to characterize the progression of neuropathic pain. Behavioral assessments revealed a significant reduction in the paw withdrawal threshold (PWT) in the CCD group starting from day 7 post-surgery compared to the sham group, indicating the development of mechanical hypersensitivity (P < 0.01; Fig. 1A).

To characterize CCD-induced cutaneous neurogenic inflammation, plasma extravasation (PE) points on the cutaneous tissues were labeled with the intravenous injection of Evans blue (EB) dye in rats (Fig. 1B). From day 7 post-CCD modeling, positive signs were predominantly observed on the medial and posterior hindlimbs, with the number of PE points on the latter significantly increasing by day 14 (P < 0.01; Fig. 1C and D). Subsequently, the mechanical pain thresholds at the sensitization (SP) and nonsensitization (NP) points in CCD rats, as well as at the corresponding locations in the sham group, were determined. After modeling, this parameter value significantly decreased (P < 0.01), with lower thresholds for SP than NP (P < 0.01), confirming that SPs accurately indicate the mechanical pain hypersensitivity areas in CCD rats (Fig. 1E). RNA sequencing was performed to evaluate differential RNA expression between SPs of CCD models and the corresponding regions in the sham group. Following the removal of low-quality reads, the clean reads were obtained and mapped to the reference genome. Differentially expressed RNAs were defined as those with |log2FC| > 2 and P < 0.05. In total, 391 differentially expressed RNAs were detected between the CCD and sham groups. Compared with sham group, 110 RNAs were significantly upregulated, and 281 were downregulated in SP tissues of CCD rats (Fig. 1F). A heatmap of the top upregulated transcripts highlighted several potential candidates (Fig. 1G). Notably, Piezo2 emerged as a highly anticipated candidate due to its established role as a principal mechanotransducer in sensory neurons and Merkel cells [21]. We hypothesized that its upregulation might serve as a critical molecular link between mechanical stress and hyperalgesia in the CCD model. Western blot analysis revealed higher PIEZO2 expression in the skin tissue of CCD rats, more pronounced in SP than in the NP (P < 0.05; Fig. 1H). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed the distribution of PIEZO2 in the skin tissue of CCD rats. Notably, significant co-localization of the neuronal marker βIII-tubulin and PIEZO2 was detected in SP tissues, suggesting a higher expression level within SP than NP tissues in both the CCD and sham groups (P < 0.01; Fig. 1I). These findings demonstrate significantly elevated PIEZO2 expression in the CCD-induced sensitized skin areas, which may have crucially influenced local pain sensitization.

ML at SP reduces neuropathic pain by regulating inflammation levels in spinal dorsal horn

To assess the effects of various acupressure modalities on pain and neuroinflammation, mechanotherapy interventions with varying parameters were administered to specific anatomical sites in CCD rats 4 days post-modeling. Researchers used tactile measurement finger sleeves for real-time pressure monitoring to ensure intervention fidelity (Fig. 2A and B). Applying 5 N pressure to the SPs significantly increased the mechanical pain threshold in CCD rats, surpassing that of CCD group by day 7 (P < 0.05). This improvement persisted with subsequent interventions. A comparable effect was observed with 2.5 N pressure at SPs. However, recovery from the mechanical pain threshold in the 2.5 N group did not differ significantly from that of the model group until day 11 post-modeling. Furthermore, by day 14, the amelioration observed in this group remained inferior to that achieved in the 5 N intervention group (P < 0.05). For NPs, which lacked nociceptive sensitization, ML of 5 N pressure significantly increased the pain threshold starting from day 14 post-modeling (P < 0.05), but the efficacy was notably weaker compared to that observed for SPs (P < 0.01; Fig. 2C). To evaluate inflammation within the spinal dorsal horn (SDH), TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels were quantitatively analyzed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). These inflammatory marker levels significantly increased following CCD modeling (P < 0.01). However, mechanotherapy effectively mitigated this increase. The application of 5 N pressure at SPs demonstrated the most pronounced anti-inflammatory effect, followed by 2.5 N. In contrast, all ML interventions targeting the NPs were less effective than those applied to the SPs (P < 0.01; Fig. 2D and F). Given the established involvement of MAPK signaling—including p38, signal-regulated kinase (ERK), and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)—in central sensitization, nociceptive stimuli, and the mediation of ML-induced therapeutic effects, we performed western blot to examine the activation status of key MAPK pathway proteins in the SDH. ML on the SPs significantly reduced phosphorylation of p38 MAPK, ERK, and JNK (P < 0.01; Fig. 2G and I). These findings demonstrate that ML at sensitized sites effectively alleviates neuropathic pain, likely by inhibiting the spinal MAPK signaling pathway and subsequent neuroinflammation.

ML alleviates CCD-induced hyperexcitability in DRG cells through PIEZO2-mediated mechanosensitive current modulation

To elucidate the mechano-transduction mechanisms underlying ML at SPs, we investigated whether PIEZO2-mediated regulation of ion channels contributed to ML-induced therapeutic suppression of neuronal hyperexcitability. Following intrathecal injection of D-GsMTx4 to selectively inhibit the function of PIEZO2 channel, CCD rats exhibited an elevated mechanical pain threshold compared with their counterparts from day 7 post-intervention onward (P < 0.05), demonstrating the critical role of PIEZO2 in mechano-nociceptive transduction (Fig. 3A). Simultaneously, in the ML group subjected to PIEZO2 functional blockade, mechanical pain thresholds induced a transient enhancement trend at day 7 (P > 0.05), followed by significant attenuation at day 14 relative to non-blockaded ML controls (P < 0.01; Fig. 3B). The attenuation of ML-induced analgesia implicated PIEZO2 as a pivotal mediator in therapeutic mechanical signal transmission.

To clearly delineate the mechanistic contributions of PIEZO2, we performed whole-cell patch-clamp recordings on DRG neurons with diameters less than 40 μm which characterized by enriched PIEZO2 expression as well as mediating nociceptive and mechanosensory signal transduction [22, 23]. First, given the established association between mechanical nociceptor hyperexcitability and neuropathic pain pathogenesis, we compared evoked action potentials (AP) in DRG neurons across three experimental cohorts: sham-operated rats, CCD rats, and CCD rats subjected to ML intervention. Under 500-ms depolarizing current stimulation, CCD modeling significantly increased AP discharge frequency (P < 0.01), which was substantially reduced by ML treatment (P < 0.05). These findings reflect that ML facilitated the elimination of pathological neuronal hyperexcitability in neuropathic pain models, at least through modulation of outward K⁺ conductance. Intriguingly, while PIEZO2 blockade alone did not significantly alter the evoked AP frequency in CCD rats (P > 0.05), it substantially attenuated the suppressive effect of ML on neuronal hyperexcitability (P < 0.05; Fig. 3C and D). The electrophysiological evidence strongly favors PIEZO2 as a critical molecular transducer required for ML-based therapeutic modulation of DRG neuron activity. Subsequently, IK and IA densities across experimental cohorts were quantified to validate whether ML had mitigated DRG neuronal hyperexcitability in CCD rats via PIEZO2-mediated ionic currents. As a critical regulator of neuronal excitability and firing frequency, the max current density of IK significantly increased post-CCD modeling (P < 0.01) but substantially decreased with ML intervention (P<0.01; Fig. 3E and G). Intriguingly, while intrathecal administration of the PIEZO2-specific antagonist D-GsMTx4 partly normalized CCD-induced IK upregulation, it concurrently abrogated the suppressive effects of ML on IK currents in DRG neurons (P<0.01; Fig. 3H and K). Conversely, CCD modeling markedly reduced the maximum current density of IA (P < 0.01), a pathophysiological change that lowers depolarization thresholds for AP initiation. ML treatment robustly restored this IA deficiency (P < 0.01; Fig. 3L and N). Notably, although PIEZO2 functional blockade partially rescued IA currents in CCD rats, it failed to modulate ML-driven IA augmentation (Fig. 3O and R).

CCD-induced high PIEZO2 expression within neurons mediates the anti-inflammatory effect of ML

To clarify the molecular basis of ML-induced analgesia through PIEZO2 modulation, we first examined PIEZO2 expression patterns in skin, DRG, and SDH tissues across different intervention groups. Immunofluorescence revealed that CCD modeling significantly upregulated neuronal PIEZO2 expression across all three tissue types (P < 0.01), which was effectively inhibited by ML intervention (P < 0.01). Following intrathecal injection of D-GsMTx4, we observed a complex regulatory pattern: while PIEZO2 expression levels were generally reduced (skin: P < 0.01; DRG: P < 0.05; SDH: P < 0.01), the suppressive effect of ML on neuronal PIEZO2 overexpression in the DRG was significantly abrogated (P < 0.05; Fig. 4A and B). The western blot results were highly consistent with the findings described above (Fig. 4C). Complementary ELISA assays of inflammatory factors within SDH demonstrated that CCD modeling markedly increased the expression of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, NGF, and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) (P < 0.01), whereas ML significantly mitigated these pathological effects (P < 0.01). Functional blockade of the PIEZO2 channel significantly inhibited neuroinflammation-associated PGE2 (P < 0.05) and NGF expression (P < 0.01) while partially counteracting ML-induced suppression of TNF-α (P < 0.01), IL-6 (P < 0.05), and PGE2 (P < 0.01; Fig. 4D and H). These outcomes confirmed the crucial role of PIEZO2 in promoting neuroinflammation and mediating the ML-driven anti-inflammatory effect.

Collectively, our findings demonstrate a pan-tissue upregulation of PIEZO2 in the pathophysiology of neuropathic pain. We propose that ML exerts its therapeutic effects by modulating PIEZO2 function, thereby dampening neuronal hyperexcitability and resolving the neuroinflammatory response. This dual regulation of excitability and inflammation highlights PIEZO2 as a critical molecular node linking mechanical stimuli to analgesic outcomes.

Transcriptional effects of ML treatment and PIEZO2 functional blocker on the CCD model

Differential analyses were performed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with ML and PIEZO2 functional blocker in each tissue type from the CCD model (Tables S1–S4). In the DRG, ML and D-GsMTx4 injection promoted the transcriptional change of 612 (up: down = 429:183) and 2,357 (up: down = 1,284:1,073) DEGs, respectively (P < 0.05; Fig. 5A and B). The functional compositions (Gene ontology (GO) categories) did not differ significantly among DEGs with different variation trends across treatment groups (Fig. 5C). Among the 221 DEGs shared between the two of the latter, 108 (68 upregulated and 40 downregulated) exhibited consistent variation patterns (Fig. 5A and B and Fig. S1). In the skin, ML and D-GsMTx4 treatments induced transcriptional changes in 391 DEGs (up: down = 146:245) and 1,649 (up: down = 929:720) DEGs, respectively (P < 0.05; Fig. 5D and E). GO category-level functioning was similar among DEGs with different trends across treatments (Fig. 5F). Interestingly, a large number of DEGs (n = 268) was shared by the two treatment groups, all of which showed consistent variation tendencies (up: down = 95:173) between treatments (Fig. 5D and E and Fig. S1). This indicates that ML may have exerted a transcriptional effect similar to that of a PIEZO2 functional inhibitor in the skin. In the SDH, ML and D-GsMTx4 induced transcriptional changes in 821 DEGs (up: down = 479:342) and 1,787 (up: down = 859:928) DEGs, respectively (P < 0.05; Fig. 5G and H). As with other tissues, GO category-level functioning was comparable among DEGs, despite divergent trends across treatments (Fig. 5I). In total, 405 DEGs were shared by the two intervention groups, the majority of which (n = 396; up: down = 205:191) showed consecutive variation trends between treatment groups (Fig. 5G and H and Fig. S1). These findings confirm that ML and PIEZO2 channel inhibitor exhibit similar transcriptional effects on SDH.

To identify the common effects of ML and D-GsMTx4, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was performed on DEGs that were involved in both treatments and exhibited consistent variation trends (Table S5). Significant enrichments were detected in the DRG, skin, and SDH (Fig. 5J). Specifically, the downregulated DEGs in the skin were primarily involved in lipid-related processes (e.g., regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes and the sphingolipid signaling pathway) and in cell energy metabolism (e.g., insulin and mTOR signaling pathways) (Fig. 5J). These included critical regulatory genes, such as irs2, pik3ca, tshr, and prkce (Fig. 5K). The upregulated DEGs in the skin were mainly involved in ribosomal components (Fig. 5J and K), such as rpl13a and rps16. In the SDH, downregulated genes were also involved in lipid-related processes (e.g., phospholipase D signaling pathway, PPAR signaling pathway, fatty acid degradation, and glycerophospholipid metabolism). Transcription of core lipid metabolic genes, such as pld1, pld2, dgka, and acox1, decreased significantly after the treatments (Fig. 5K). Upregulated SDH genes were primarily associated with endocytosis and metabolic processes, although the observed enrichment lacked strong significance and specificity. However, no KEGG pathways were significantly enriched in DRG for the small number of DEGs in this tissue (Fig. 5J).

In combination, these transcriptomic analyses demonstrate that ML elicits transcriptional profiles partially overlap with those induced by PIEZO2 functional blockade, particularly within the skin and SDH. The shared molecular signature is characterized by the modulation of lipid metabolism and cellular energy pathways, providing a systems-level validation of PIEZO2 as a critical transducer of mechanical analgesia.

Metabolic effects of ML treatments and PIEZO2 functional blocker on CCD model

Differential analyses were performed to detect differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) associated with ML and D-GsMTx4 in each tissue type from the CCD model (Tables S6–S9). In the DRG, 60 DEMs (up: down = 40:20) and 68 DEMs (up: down = 4:64) were identified for ML and D-GsMTx4, respectively (Fig. 6A and B). These DEMs were primarily lipids and lipid-like molecules (LLMs), along with organic acids and derivatives (OADs), where significant compositional differences between DEGs across treatments or variation trends were evident (Fig. 6C). Of the 17 shared DEMs between interventions, 71% (n = 12, all downregulated) exhibited a consistent variation pattern (Fig. 6A and B). In the skin, 22 DEMs (up: down = 6:16) and 54 DEMs (up: down = 33:21) were outlined for ML and D-GsMTx4, respectively (Fig. 6D and E). These DEMs were primarily LLMs, with no significant compositional differences between treatment groups (Fig. 6F). Eleven DEMs were shared between treatments, all showing consistent variation trends (up: down = 2:9; Fig. 6D and E). In the SDH, 56 (up: down = 4:52) and 57 DEMs (up: down = 7:50) were identified for ML and D-GsMTx4, respectively (Fig. 6G and H). These DEMs were predominantly represented by LLMs and OADs. Notably, more than a half of DEMs (n = 31) were shared between treatment groups, all showing well-defined tendencies (up: down = 2:29). The latter further suggests that ML exerts similar effects on skin and SDH as PIEZO2 channel inhibitor.

Integrated analysis of transcriptional and metabolic changes in response to treatments

To explore potential crosstalk between the transcriptome and metabolome within each tissue, we performed correlation analyses between DEMs and DEGs shared by the ML- and D-GsMTx4-treated groups (adjusted P < 0.05 for both Spearman and Pearson correlations; Table S10). Strong and extensive DEM–DEG correlations were noted for the skin and SDH, whereas no significant correlations were detected in the DRG, likely due to the limited number of shared DEGs in this tissue type (Fig. S3). To simplify the results and enhance their interpretability, we used KEGG pathway analysis for individual DEGs and visualized these associations using network plots (Fig. 7A and C). In the DRG, weak associations were detected with prostaglandin-related pathways (Fig. 7A). In the skin, strong associations were found between PGE2 and several KEGG pathways, including focal adhesion, sphingolipid signaling, and metabolic pathways (Fig. 7B). In the SDH, the Gly-Val dipeptide showed the strongest associations with pathways exhibiting significant transcriptional changes, including metabolic pathways, arginine and proline metabolism, and PPAR signaling (Fig. 7C). Moreover, we screened DEMs and DEGs with the strongest associations (|R| > 0.9, adjusted P < 0.05) (Fig. 7D and F and Table S11). In the DRG, the metabolites were mainly prostaglandin A2, prostaglandin B2, and all-trans-5,6-epoxyretinoic acid, while the DEGs included snord93 and psma4, with no enriched pathways (Fig. 7D). In the skin, the key metabolites included PGE2, all-trans-5,6-epoxyretinoic acid, and hydroxypregnenolone, while the DEGs were represented by prkce, rock2, pde3b, cald1, and rab8b, highlighting their involvement in vascular smooth muscle contraction, the cGMP-PKG signaling pathway, and tight junctions (Fig. 7E). In the SDH, the metabolites were mainly stearic acid, cytosine, deoxycytidine, and all-trans-5,6-epoxyretinoic acid, whereas the DEGs included zfp318, iffo1, and riok1. However, no pathways were significantly enriched (Fig. 7F). These results support the potential crosstalk between the metabolome and transcriptome in response to ML and PIEZO2 channel inhibitor.

Discussion

Rooted in traditional medical concepts, counter-irritation therapy — one of the oldest documented pain management principles — postulates that pain in one body region can hamper distant nociception. This theory underpins mechanotherapy methods, such as acupuncture, Tuina, and heterotopic noxious stimulation. While such approaches exhibit high clinical efficacy, especially in neuromodulation [24], the gaps in understanding of their neurobiological basis and mechanical transduction mechanisms limits further clinical application [25].

In the current study, we systematically investigated the role of PIEZO2 in the pathogenesis of neuropathic pain and the therapeutic mechanism of ML-induced analgesia using a rat CCD model. We found that PIEZO2 expression was significantly upregulated in the skin, DRG, and SDH of CCD rats, showing a spatial correlation with areas of mechanical hypersensitivity and neuroinflammation. This positions PIEZO2 as a major mechanosensitive contributor to peripheral sensitization. To dissect the mechanism by which ML at SPs exerts its strong analgesic effect, we combined behavioral analyses with electrophysiological and biochemical approaches. Our findings revealed that ML-induced analgesia was critically dependent on PIEZO2 activation, as selective blockade of PIEZO2 markedly attenuated ML’s analgesic effects. Mechanistically, we found that ML, acting through PIEZO2, suppressed neuronal hyperexcitability in DRG neurons by specifically modulating IK, while simultaneously dampening neuroinflammation in the central nervous system by inhibiting the MAPK signaling pathways and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. Finally, by integrating transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses, we identified a convergent regulatory network in which both ML and PIEZO2 inhibition induced strikingly similar alterations in lipid metabolic pathways and downregulated key inflammatory mediators, such as PGE₂ and all-trans-5,6-epoxyretinoic acid, thereby providing a comprehensive molecular blueprint for PIEZO2-mediated mechanical analgesia.

As a mechanosensitive transduction channel, PIEZO2 initiates the bioelectrical cascade that converts mechanical stimuli into cellular responses. Membrane mechanical forces drive cation influx through PIEZO2 channels, thereby inducing membrane depolarization and initiating AP firing [26]. Furthermore, PIEZO2 has been shown to be indispensable for light-touch perception and tactile allodynia in humans. Moreover, voltage control of abundant PIEZO2 channels in nociceptors serves as a primary mediator of nociceptor sensitization induced by strong nociceptive stimuli [13, 27]. In the present research, the spatial distribution and mechanical activation thresholds of PIEZO2 in cutaneous tissues were critical factors of both body surface hyperalgesia and ML-induced analgesia. After CCD surgery, hyperalgesia zones associated with systemic inflammatory responses were observed, in which PIEZO2 expression was significantly higher. Supported by evidence [28] that PIEZO2 mediates mechanically activated currents in nociceptors and induces various nociceptive behaviors, its upregulation is likely a key driver of neuronal sensitization, ultimately leading to the formation of these pain-sensitive areas. According to the prior evidence [29], ML attenuated neuroinflammation and reduced pain sensitivity in DRG of neuropathic rats. Crucially, ML applied to PIEZO2-enriched regions produced enhanced analgesia, confirming PIEZO2’s regulatory role in mitigating CCD-induced hyperalgesia. Notably, instead of lower pressures, 5 N pressure suppressed multi-tissue inflammation and elevated mechanical pain thresholds more effectively, suggesting a potential pressure-dependent functional recruitment characteristic of PIEZO2. While PIEZO2 is typically described as a low-threshold, mechanically activated cation channel [30], its mechanical sensitivity may be pressure-dependent within a certain range. In view of PIEZO2’s mechanical sensitivity gradation with an increasing stimulus magnitude [21], higher pressure stimulation can recruit a larger number of functional channels, thereby amplifying downstream anti-inflammatory and ion channel-modulating signals. Moreover, this study is the first to observe the indirect inhibitory effect of D-GsMTx4 on the expression level of PIEZO2 in the CCD model. As an inhibitor of PIEZO2 channel function, D-GsMTx4 is generally believed to reduce the sensitivity of PIEZO2 to mechanical stimulation by inhibiting Ca2+ influx, without directly affecting the expression level [31]. In this experiment, the downregulation of PIEZO2 by D-GsMTx4 is likely attributable to its functional inhibition of mechanosensitive channels, which markedly reduces the release of inflammatory mediators such as NGF and PGE2, ameliorates the inflammatory microenvironment, and breaks the PIEZO2–inflammatory factor positive feedback loop [32], thereby suppressing the inflammation-driven overexpression of PIEZO2.

The association between pain-related pathologies (e.g., hyperalgesia and allodynia) and neuronal excitability has been demonstrated in previous studies [33], while the pivotal role of cellular electrophysiology in mechanotransduction has also been revealed [34]. This study employed patch-clamp electrophysiology to investigate the role of PIEZO2 in mediating somatic sensitization and ML-induced attenuation of DRG neuronal hyperexcitability in CCD rats. Our data displayed that ML intervention effectively antagonized the CCD -induced upregulation of IK density and suppression of IA density. Reduced IA may improve membrane excitability by rising spike frequency and shortening first-spike latency [35], and prior work indicated IA downregulation after CCD surgery contribute to nociceptive stimuli and cutaneous mechanical hypersensitivity [36]. These pathological alterations, combined with prior behavioral metrics, suggest that the IA suppression plays a dominant role in CCD-induced neuronal hyperexcitability. By contrast, IK typically maintains resting membrane potential and regulates post-hyperpolarization recovery [37], with its reduction often associated with the hyperexcitability of the neuronal cell membrane. However, we observed an abnormal increase in IK after model establishment, which may represent a compensatory neuronal response to reduce the ectopic or abnormal neuronal activity caused by injury [38]. Notably, functional blockade of PIEZO2 selectively abolished ML-induced regulation of IK rather than IA in DRG neurons from CCD rats, indicating the involvement of additional mechano-gated ion channels or PIEZO family members in ML-mediated suppression of neuronal hyperexcitability. It should be acknowledged that the present patch-clamp experiments primarily focused on outward K⁺ currents as these play a particularly critical role in influencing the excitability of reactive neurons, while voltage-gated inward currents were not systematically examined. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that inward conductance, especially voltage-gated Na+ and Ca²⁺ currents, also critically shape action potential generation and may be modulated by both CCD and ML. Furthermore, independently of ML, PIEZO2 blockade alleviated the CCD-induced IA suppression, revealing a specific regulatory effect of PIEZO2 on ionic currents. Considering that mechanical stimuli have an impact on the permeability to various cations in PIEZO channels, such as Ca²⁺, K⁺, Na⁺, and Mg²⁺, the necessity to investigate multichannel regulatory mechanisms within pain modulation in further researches should be emphasized.

Beyond PIEZO2, other mechanosensitive ion channel families also contribute to mechanical nociceptive transduction and inflammatory pain. Transient receptor potential channels are well-established polymodal nociceptors that are gated by mechanical stimuli and are critically involved in inflammatory hyperalgesia [39]. Two-pore-domain potassium channels act as mechanosensitive leak K⁺ channels whose inhibition by membrane stretch or inflammatory mediators promotes neuronal depolarization and hyperexcitability [40]. In addition, PIEZO1 has been reported to drive pro-inflammatory response in macrophages/microglia upon IFNγ/LPS stimulation following nerve injury [41]. The coexistence of these multiple mechanosensory mechanisms highlights that mechanical signal transduction in neuropathic pain involves a coordinated network rather than a single molecular entity. However, given the pronounced spatial co-localization of PIEZO2 within cutaneous mechanically hypersensitive regions, together with the comparable analgesic and anti-inflammatory phenotypes elicited by D-GsMTx4 and ML, a dominant contribution of non-PIEZO2 pathways to the core effects observed in this study can be reasonably excluded. To further strengthen the specificity of PIEZO2 in the current conclusion and to provide genetic evidence, we conducted additional in vitro experiments using Piezo2-siRNA interference. This approach successfully replicated the phenotypic changes induced by D-GsMTx4, thereby cross-validating the specificity of PIEZO2 in both pro-nociceptive and analgesic pathways (Supplementary cell experiments).

Building upon the observed patterns of neuronal hyperexcitability and inflammatory mediator expression, we further propose a mechanistic hypothesis linking bioelectrical correction propagates to a system-wide anti-inflammatory outcome. It has been reported that nerve root compression-induced neuronal hyperactivation drives robust upregulation of the pronociceptive neuropeptide substance P and its downstream signaling cascades [42]. As a well-characterized direct upstream activator of MAPKs, substance P initiates pro-inflammatory transcriptional programs via NK1 receptor engagement, potently promoting the transcription and release of IL-1β and TNF-α and further amplifying MAPK signaling under sustained chronic inflammatory conditions [43]. Following ML intervention, functional suppression of pathological neuronal discharges is likely accompanied by a corresponding reduction in the release of DRG-derived pronociceptive neuropeptides. This mechanistic bridge clarifies how ML, by engaging PIEZO2 to restore DRG electrophysiological homeostasis, interrupts the aberrant peripheral-to-central neuroinflammatory signaling axis, thereby accounting for the suppression of ERK and p38 hyperphosphorylation observed in the SDH after ML treatment.

Thus, PIEZO2 plays dual roles in the pathogenesis of neuropathic pain. Following CCD surgery, PIEZO2 upregulation in cutaneous hyperalgesia zones promotes mechanical sensitization, consistent with its established function in inflammation-related allodynia [44] and in vincristine-induced peripheral neuropathic pain [45]. Interestingly, elevated PIEZO2 expression can enhance mechanical transduction efficacy. Undergoing rapid conformational changes, PIEZO2 transforms mechanical stimuli into electrochemical signals via force-gated ion flux [46]. Critically, ML attenuates tissue inflammation not only locally but also in upstream neural structures, including the DRG and SDH, indicating PIEZO2-mediated systemic anti-nociception. Meanwhile, a previous study [47] has shown that ML reduces PIEZO2 expression in the DRG tissues of mice with inflammatory pain. Moreover, mechanical stimulation in peripheral tissues can be an important means of inactivating PIEZO2 [48]. Therefore, ML is appropriate for eliminating the abnormal activation of PIEZO2 induced by pain. Collectively, while PIEZO2 drives mechanical hypersensitivity, its activation creates a therapeutic gateway for ML to exert multi-level anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects.

To further verify whether the PIEZO2 channel inhibitor-like effect of ML induced PIEZO2-related downstream transcriptional and metabolomic changes, a multi-omics approach was used to evaluate the correlating effects of ML and PIEZO2 channel inhibitors on the CCD model. Our results indicate that lipid-related processes, including the sphingolipid signaling, phospholipase D (PLD) signaling, and PPAR signaling pathways, are potentially involved in the transcriptional response of PIEZO2 to ML in skin and SDH. The interaction among these lipid pathways appears to mitigate the consequences of nerve-compression injury, with implications for repair. Mechanical stress can alter PLD activity, generating phosphatidic acid and diacylglycerol to initiate lipid-driven inflammatory cascades [49]. Concurrently, alterations in sphingolipid metabolism, such as ceramide accumulation and reduced sphingosine-1-phosphate levels, disrupt Schwann-cell lipid homeostasis and can impair remyelination [50]. PPAR-γ activation promotes lipid–gene expression, suppresses microglial inflammation, and facilitates axonal outgrowth [51]. Together, these pathways form an integrated network linking membrane–lipid perturbation, sphingolipid signaling, and nuclear-receptor–mediated repair, implying the potentially complex biological effects mediated by ML.

Integrated analysis of transcriptional and metabolic changes revealed a significant reduction in prostaglandin E₂ (PGE₂), an inflammatory mediator, indicating a shift in lipid-mediated signaling within sphingolipid and PPAR pathways. PGE₂ regulates inflammatory responses and cell survival via EP receptors [52], while PPAR-γ can suppress PGE₂ synthesis and receptor expression, demonstrating bidirectional cross-regulation [53]. Sphingolipid metabolites, such as ceramide and S1P, further modulate prostaglandin production, linking membrane–lipid disruption to downstream eicosanoid signaling [54]. In our study, PGE₂ levels were strongly correlated with sphingolipid signaling, supporting the presence of a regulatory axis of bioactive lipids that responds to PIEZO2-related ML signaling. Given that PGE₂ enhances Orai1-mediated store-operated calcium entry in DRG neurons through the EP1 receptor and its downstream signaling pathways, thus increase neuronal excitability [55], PGE₂ may serve as a crucial node linking PIEZO2 activity modulates voltage-gated membrane channels. Moreover, skin metabolites, including PGE₂ and all-trans-5,6-epoxyretinoic acid, were indicative of vascular smooth muscle contraction and tight junction integrity. This suggests that ML’s mechanical stimulation can influence tissue function via lipid-mediated pathways. Collectively, these findings point to the presence of an integrated network linking tissue physical structures, bioactive lipid derivatives, lipid-related signaling, and ion current channels in response to PIEZO2-mediated ML stimulation. Simultaneously, ML-induced transcriptomic and metabolic alterations are more focused compared with pharmacological inhibitors, suggesting that ML has the potential to elicit a more targeted and less systemic response. Future studies investigating the causal roles of these pathways will be of great significance for the clinical translation of ML.

We found that all‑trans‑5,6‑epoxyretinoic acid levels significantly decreased across all tissues examined (skin and neural tissues) following ML treatment. This metabolite is a bona fide endogenous derivative of all‑trans‑retinoic acid and is detected in multiple tissues, including the kidney, liver, intestine, and sera from vitamin A-deficient rats [56]. It can bind to and activate retinoic acid receptors, thereby regulating transcriptional programs governing cell differentiation and tissue remodeling [57]. The consistent decrease in all‑trans‑5,6‑epoxyretinoic acid levels across multiple tissues signifies that ML can influence a systemic retinoid‑mediated signaling axis, potentially modulating inter‑tissue communication. Retinoid metabolism and downstream signaling have been linked to mechanical signals [58, 59]. Thus, the detected cross-tissue reduction in all-trans-5,6-epoxyretinoic acid demonstrates the orchestration of inter-tissue signaling via local mechanical stimulation by ML.

To our knowledge, this study pioneers the multi-tissue interrogation of neuropathic pain mechanisms, spanning cutaneous sensitization sites, DRG, and spinal cord, to delineate the spatially heterogeneous responses underlying PIEZO2-mediated somatic hypersensitivity and ML-induced analgesia. This integrated “periphery-to-central nervous system” approach reveals tissue-specific neuroinflammatory and ion channel dynamics, highlighting the pathological complexity of neuropathic pain and the inherent limitations of single-target therapies. Notably, while functional inhibition of PIEZO2 attenuated mechanical allodynia, mechanotherapy was significantly more effective, underscoring the complexity of pain transmission and ML-driven analgesic effects. Clinically, this work validates the necessity of anatomically precise stimulation and pressure-dose titration in mechanotherapies. We propose that standardizing protocols based on molecular activation thresholds may yield more effective, personalized and non-opioid methods for treating neuropathic pain — addressing critical gaps in current guideline-recommended interventions.

Nonetheless, the limitations of the current study must be acknowledged. Given the small sample size, reliance on a single pathology model, and exclusive use of male animals, further validation with more advanced preclinical models must be conducted before clinical translation. Cross-sex experimental designs incorporating hormonal covariates should also be implemented to enhance the generalizability of future research. Furthermore, due to constraints in the intervention timeline, this study relied solely on intrathecal administration of the pharmacological inhibitor D-GsMTx4 to functionally block PIEZO2 channel, without higher-specificity techniques such as AAV-shRNA. Consequently, causal interpretation of PIEZO2’s role in the observed outcomes requires caution. Although our pharmacological and siRNA-based approaches support a predominant role for PIEZO2, other mechanosensitive channels may also participate in the mechanotransduction network underlying CCD-induced hyperalgesia and ML-mediated analgesia, and their potential contributions warrant further investigations. Additionally, our conclusions are mainly inferred from the downstream indicators mediated by PIEZO2, limiting direct observation of ML’s impact on PIEZO2 function. To directly evaluate the neuronal excitability under different intervention conditions and thus reflect the function of the PIEZO2 channel, the calcium imaging in living neurons using the Fluo-4 AM fluorescent probe was additionally carried out (Supplementary cell experiments). Future researches employing mechanically activated current, emerging PIEZO2 agonists, and in situ hybridization validation are needed to clarify the causal relationship between ML and changes in PIEZO2 function and expression levels. Notably, the electrophysiological analysis leaves inward currents unexplored, which limits a comprehensive understanding of PIEZO2-mediated ion channel regulation. A comprehensive voltage-clamp profiling across multiple ion channels will be required to fully define the complete ionic signature of ML. Finally, the present metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses provide preliminary insights into the mechanisms of ML, but the connection between these omics-level findings and the analgesic mechanism remains speculative. Spatiotemporally precise tools, including optogenetics and single-cell multi-omics, should also be employed to deconvolve ML-induced circuit rewiring and post-translational machinery, thereby bridging molecular and omics evidence with systems-level pain regulation.

It should be noted that pain-induced somatosensory sensitization areas frequently exhibit linear patterns of distribution, often progressing from unilateral to bilateral symmetry. Consequently, bilateral multi-regional interventions (e.g., acupuncture, manual therapy) are clinically prioritized to maximize therapeutic efficacy. However, to enhance the research accuracy, this mechanistic study focused on specific sensitized zones. Future research should delineate neurobiological distinctions in region-specific modulation, providing a foundation for developing advanced antalgic devices. Pressure intensities should also be incorporated as an essential influencing factor into future research topics. Establishing a more precise dose-response relationship may hold significant importance for optimizing clinical ML treatment regimens.

Conclusions

Our study establishes PIEZO2 as a momentous mediator of neuropathic pain, influencing both the pathogenesis of mechanical sensitization and the therapeutic transduction of ML. We demonstrate that PIEZO2 upregulation drives nociceptive hypersensitivity and neuroinflammation, whereas targeted ML stimulation at sensitized sites engages PIEZO2 to restore electrophysiological homeostasis and suppress inflammatory cascades. Furthermore, the convergence of transcriptomic and metabolic responses between ML and functional blockade of PIEZO2 reveals a common regulatory network centered on lipid-mediated signaling pathways, particularly those involving prostaglandins and retinoid derivatives. These results elucidate the molecular logic of mechanical analgesia and support the development of non-invasive analgesic devices based on surface stimulation therapies.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male Sprague–Dawley rats (210–240 g) were sourced from Shanghai Jihui Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and acclimatized in the specific pathogen-free animal facility of the Laboratory Animal Center of Yueyang Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine. Animals were maintained under standardized environmental conditions (22–27 °C, 50%–70% relative humidity) on a 12-h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to irradiated rodent chow and sterile water.

Model establishment

CCD was established in rats under sterile conditions, as described previously [29]. Briefly, animals were anesthetized with intraperitoneal sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg). The right paraspinal muscles were bluntly dissected to expose the L4–L5 intervertebral foramina. Two L-shaped stainless-steel rods (4 mm in length, 0.6 mm in diameter) were positioned at a 30° angle relative to the spinal column and inserted into the respective foramina to induce sustained DRG compression. Postoperatively, the muscle, fascia, and skin layers were sutured, and erythromycin ointment was applied to the wound site.

To confirm surgical success, rats underwent postoperative X-ray imaging (Samsung XGEO GU60, Seoul, South Korea). Animals exhibiting rod displacement, sensory deficits, or motor dysfunction upon recovery were excluded from analysis. Sham-operated rats had identical procedures except for rod insertion.

Skin plasma protein extravasation

EB dye solution was prepared by dissolving EB powder (E2129, Sigma-Aldrich, City of Saint Louis, MO, USA) in sterile 0.9% saline to obtain a final concentration of 50 mg/mL. At 7 and 14 days post-CCD surgery, rats were anesthetized with 4% isoflurane after exposing the dorsal and hindlimb regions. EB solution was injected via the tail vein at 1 mL/kg body weight [60]. After 30 min, the number of blue-stained PE points was quantified, and the spatial distribution patterns of PE points were analyzed in the right hindlimb region.

Behavioral test

Behavioral evaluations were performed during the light cycle (09:00–17:00) to assess mechanical hyperalgesia. Baseline measurements were recorded daily for 2 consecutive days prior to CCD surgery, followed by repeated evaluations at multiple postoperative time points. The rats were habituated to the experimental environment for 3 days (2 h/day) before testing to minimize stress-related variability. All tests were conducted by two independent and blinded experimenters to ensure objectivity.

An electric von Frey esthesiometer (ALMEMO 2450, IITC/Life Science, Woodland Hills, CA, USA) was used to quantify mechanical sensitivity. The probe tip was positioned on the central plantar surface of the right hindlimb and operated at a constant speed of 0.5 cm/s. The PWT was defined as the force required for a rat to withdraw its paw at the peak stimulus intensity. To prevent sensory sensitization, subsequent measurements were spaced ≥ 20 s apart.

This protocol was extended to evaluate regional variations in mechanical responsiveness across a right hindlimb. The day before testing, the relevant regions were shaved. SP was selected in areas with the highest density of PE points, whereas NP was identified at locations in regions that correspond horizontally to the sparsest PE point distribution. A blunt probe was applied perpendicularly to these sites, and a rapid vertical or horizontal movement of the hindlimb was defined as a positive response.

Each rat underwent five replicate trials per testing session. The maximum and minimum values were excluded, and the mean of the remaining three values was calculated and set as the outcome for that session. This approach minimized variability due to transient fluctuations or outliers. Following the intervention, tissue samples from rats were collected for subsequent analyses: skin from SP or NP regions, DRG isolated from the right L4 and L5 spinal segments, and the corresponding superficial SDH tissues (mainly consists of the lamina I and II neurons).

Mechanical loading intervention

Rats were randomly allocated from the CCD cohort to each intervention subgroup. The ML protocol was followed according to the established methodology [29], consisting of 10-min daily sessions administered consecutively over 15 days, commencing on postoperative day 4. To minimize confounding stress-related variability, rats underwent experimenter-administered preconditioning 30 min prior to each intervention session. Acupressure was applied to a predefined anatomical locus (SP/NP) on the right hindlimb using the experimenter’s thumb. The intervention parameters were rigorously standardized using a tactile measurement finger sleeve (Pliance, Novel, Germany), secured to the operator’s thumb. This device enabled the precise calibration of applied pressure (5–2.5 N) and stimulation frequency (2 Hz). To control for non-specific procedural effects, non-ML groups were subjected to identical handling procedures, including daily grasping. However, therapeutic pressure application was omitted during sham interventions.

Intrathecal injection

Intrathecal injection was performed as described in previous protocols, using D-GsMTx4 (HY-P1410C, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) as a selective PIEZO2 channel blocker. Prior to surgery, the hair from the proposed incision site in the lumbar region was shaved, followed by skin sterilization with povidone-iodine. Anesthesia was induced with 4% isoflurane (Roward, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) and maintained at 2.5% throughout the procedure. For lumbar puncture, the iliac crest was palpated to guide the vertical insertion of a 10-µL Hamilton microsyringe fitted with a 30-gauge needle into the L4–L5 intervertebral space. Successful dural puncture was confirmed by detecting a characteristic tail flick reflex. Injections were administered over approximately 10 s, using a 10-µL solution containing 10 µg D-GsMTx4 dissolved in sterile saline. Control animals received the identical volumes of the solution without the active compound. Starting on postoperative day 4, intrathecal injections were administered twice weekly for a total of four doses.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was retrieved from lysates and quantitatively analyzed for equal loading. Samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. After fixing with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST), the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: PIEZO2 (1:1000, 26205-1-Ap, Proteintech, Wuhan, Hubei, China), p38 MAPK (1:1000, 14064-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, Hubei, China), p-p38 (1:1000, 28796-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, Hubei, China), ERK (1:1000, 11257-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, Hubei, China), p-ERK (1:1000, 28733-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, Hubei, China), JNK (1:1000, 66210-1-Ig, Proteintech, Wuhan, Hubei, China), p-JNK (1:1000, 80024-1-RR, Proteintech, Wuhan, Hubei, China), and GAPDH (1:5000, 5174, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). Following three TBST rinses, the membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) for 2 h at room temperature (22–27 °C). Bands were visualized using ECL (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and the ChemiDoc MP system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Densitometry was carried out with ImageJ (Bethesda, MD, USA) with GAPDH normalization.

Immunofluorescence

Following sample collection and fixation in 4% formaldehyde for 24 h, tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and embedded in paraffin wax. Paraffin Sect. (4-µm thick) were subsequently deparaffinized and rehydrated before antigen retrieval via microwave treatment in a citric acid buffer (0.01 M, pH 6.0) at 98 °C for 15 min, followed by cooling to room temperature for over 60 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubation in 1% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min, and non-specific binding sites were blocked with 5% BSA (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 30 min at ambient temperature. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PIEZO2 (1:50 dilution, PA5-72976, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), β-III Tubulin (1:100, MA1-118, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), CGRP (1:100, abs120206, Absin, Shanghai, China), SP (1:100, abs119661, Absin, Shanghai, China), CD63 (1:100, abs149061, Absin, Shanghai, China), and mast cell tryptase (1:100, abs148788, Absin, Shanghai, China). Following 12-h incubation with the primary antibody, the sections were gradually warmed to 37 °C for more than 45 min. The sections were then incubated with secondary antibodies (1:100; Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 2 min. Fluorescence quantification was performed by capturing three random fields per section under an Olympus BX53 microscope (Evident, Tokyo, Japan) at 400× magnification. ImageProPlus software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD, USA) was used to calculate the mean fluorescence intensity values for target proteins.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

SDH tissue samples were collected and immediately homogenized in ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at 10 µL/mg tissue weight. After complete lysis, homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min at 4 °C to pellet debris. Supernatants were harvested, and total protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Aliquots were obtained to quantify the concentrations of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), NGF, and PGE2 using ELISA kits (BOSTER, Wuhan, Hubei, China) according to the protocols. All the assays were conducted in duplicate across independent biological samples to ensure reproducibility and statistical validity.

RNA sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using TRIzol reagent, and RNA quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Samples that met the quality control protocol were processed for transcriptome library formation with the Hieff NGS® Ultima Dual Mode RNA Library Prep Kit (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Constructed libraries that passed quality control were sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform according to library specifications to ensure appropriate concentration and data yield. Low-quality reads were filtered, and clean reads were retained for downstream analyses. Differential gene expression analysis was done with DESeq2 (P < 0.05, |log2FC| > 2), while GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using the R package clusterProfiler.

Non-targeted metabolomics

A 25-mg portion of each sample was precisely weighed, added with extraction solvent, and subjected to mechanical disruption. The tissue lysates then underwent three iterative cycles of bead beating and ultrasonic homogenization to ensure complete cell lysis and analyte release. After protein precipitation, all samples were centrifuged under the designated conditions for 1 h, after which the resulting supernatants were pooled to generate quality-control samples. Chromatographic separation of target metabolites was achieved using the Vanquish™ (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system with a ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (Waters, Milford, MA, USA), operated under optimized elution gradients. Concurrent acquisition of primary and secondary mass spectrometry data was performed under controlled collision and fragmentation parameters. For metabolomic annotation, raw spectral data were processed through the SmartDB™ database (v3.0, Nanjing Medical University, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China), which incorporates retention time alignment, peak integration, and additive ion annotation protocols. Post-normalization to internal standards and imputation of missing values via k-nearest neighbor algorithms, multivariate statistical analysis identified the significant metabolic perturbations (P < 0.05, VIP > 1) through pathway enrichment analysis conducted via KEGG and MetaboAnalyst platforms, with visualization tools employed for the biological interpretation of differential metabolites.

Preparation of DRG neurons

DRG were obtained from adult Sprague–Dawley rats and pooled to prepare neurons using the standard enzymatic dissociation intervention. Specifically, the rats were sacrificed through decapitation, and a segment of the spinal column from T7 to L6 was surgically removed. The redundant nerve fibers, connective tissues, and blood clot were then trimmed. The ganglia were placed into a double enzyme solution consisting of collagenase type (1 mg/mL) and Trypsin-EDTA (1 mg/mL) before being put into a CO2-Incubator (HERACELL 150i, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) for a 35-min digestion. Then, the digestive solution was aspirated, and 2 mL of medium (DMEM/F12 + 10% FBS) was added to terminate the process, allowing to stand until the settlement of a tissue mass with further aspiration and a discard of the supernatant. This procedure was repeated once to remove as much digestive residue as possible. The washed cells were resuspended in the cell culture medium. After standing for 1 min, the received supernatant cell suspension was aspirated and transferred to a Petri dish, and the cells were maintained in an incubator for culturing, until required.

Electrophysiological recordings

A standard whole-cell patch-clamp technique was adopted to conduct electrophysiological studies at room temperature. Pipettes were fabricated from borosilicate glass capillaries (BF150-86-10, Sutter Instruments, Novato, USA), which exhibited a resistance of 2–5 MΩ when filled with the intracellular solution and placed in a bath. For DRG cells, the intracellular solution was composed of (in mM): 140 KCl, 1 MgCl2, 1 CaCl2, 10 EGTA and 10 HEPES (pH 7.2 adjusted by KOH); bath or extracellular solution had (in mM): 140 NaCl, 5 KCl, 1 CaCl2, 1.25 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, and 10 glucose (pH 7.4 adjusted by NaOH). Voltage commands were generated by a Multiclamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA), and data were retrieved using a DigiData 1440 A (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) with Clampex software (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Data were filtered at 2 kHz and sorted at 10 kHz.

Recordings were obtained from small-to-medium-sized neurons with diameters less than 40 μm. After successful membrane rupture and cell stabilization, the current-clamp mode was engaged, and square-wave current stimulation was applied for 500 ms. An intensity of 210 pA was applied to evoke an AP. The number of APs generated by each DRG cell was then recorded. To evaluate ionic currents in cells, the command potential protocol was initially implemented from a holding potential of − 120 mV. The total potassium currents (Itotal) were evoked by a series of pulses ranging from − 80 to 60 mV in 10-mV increments. The cells were then held at − 40 mV to ensure a constant step-voltage stimulus, and the delayed rectification potassium current (IK) was recorded. The transient A-type currents (IA) were determined by subtracting IK from Itotal through the Clampfit software (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). The current density was received as the ratio of the peak current to the cell capacitance.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R 4.3.0 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) [61]. Normality and homogeneity of variance were first evaluated with Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Descriptive statistics for continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. For those with homogeneous variances, intergroup comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by a least significant difference (LSD) post hoc test. Pairwise comparisons using Dunnett’s T3 test were performed to analyze heterogeneous variances. Longitudinal changes in the same observational metric across time points between various experimental groups were assessed with two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. Statistically significant omnibus test results prompted further investigation using multivariate ANOVA for post hoc comparisons. Differential analyses of the transcriptome and metabolome were performed using the R package limma [62], with DEGs or DEMs identified at a significance threshold of P < 0.05. Gene enrichment analysis was performed, using the KEGG database through KOBAS-i (http://bioinfo.org/kobas), with the significance determined at an adjusted P < 0.05 [63]. Significant associations between DEGs and DEMs were identified via Pearson and Spearman correlation analyses using the corresponding samples. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Network visualizations were generated using Cytoscape 3.10.2 [64], while other graphical presentations were acquired using GraphPad Prism 6 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) and ggplot2. A two-tailed significance threshold of α = 0.05 was adopted, with P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.
