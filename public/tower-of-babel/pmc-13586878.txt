Retrospective attention reveals a decaying theta rhythm in conscious access to a preceding stimulus
Authors: Sugeun Yun, Younglae Kim, Jaeseung Jeong, Yee Joon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryConscious perception is often presumed to arise immediately upon external stimulation. However, retroperception—conscious access to a stimulus after it has disappeared—challenges this perspective by demonstrating temporally flexible awareness. The fine-grained temporal dynamics governing retroperception, however, remain unknown. Here, we tested three competing models for the temporal architecture of retroperception: a monotonic decay, a sustained oscillation, and a damped oscillation. We implemented a retro-cue paradigm and densely sampled behavioral reports. Through spectral analysis and computational modeling, we found that both behavioral accuracy and subjective visibility exhibited theta-band fluctuations, with model comparison modestly favoring a damped oscillatory profile over sustained oscillatory or non-oscillatory alternatives. Furthermore, we found that oscillations in accuracy and visibility were temporally aligned, suggesting temporal coordination between attention and perception during post-stimulus access. These findings suggest that conscious access to recent sensory information may be rhythmically structured and temporally limited, placing computational constraints on models of retroperception.

Introduction

How and when sensory events enter awareness remain central questions in consciousness research.1,2,3 Classic models of perception posit that awareness is tightly coupled to a stimulus presentation, typically emerging ∼100–200 ms after its onset.4,5,6 Retroperception, defined as conscious access to latent sensory traces after the stimulus has disappeared, challenges this view.7,8 By decoupling the formation of awareness from stimulus presentation, retroperception reveals a temporal flexibility of conscious access and constrains the neural mechanisms that support it.9

In retroperception, a retro-cue, defined as a cue presented after target offset, can direct attention to the target’s location and trigger conscious access to a previously unnoticed target.7,9,10,11,12,13,14 Recent electroencephalogram (EEG) research further underscores attention’s critical role in retroperception.15 Yet, the fine-grained temporal dynamics of retroperception remain unknown, as previous studies have sampled at only a few cue-target intervals (CTIs). This gap persists even though an early dense-sampling study did examine the temporal dynamics of attentional sampling in both pre- and retro-cue conditions, suspecting rhythmicity in both.16 The rhythmic sampling was observed in the pre-cue conditions, with numerous studies confirming the dynamics in both attentional and perceptual sampling.17,18,19,20,21,22 In contrast, the study failed to detect rhythmicity in retro-cue conditions, likely due to backward masking from the salient cue.16

These observations suggest that retroperception may be governed by two potentially interacting temporal constraints. The first is the persistence and decay of a latent sensory representation after target offset. If retroperception depends primarily on the residual accessibility of this trace, retro-cue benefits should decline monotonically as the cue is delayed.23,24 The second is rhythmic attentional sampling. Pre-cue studies have shown that visual detection can fluctuate periodically as a function of cue-to-target interval, consistent with alternating high- and low-excitability states following a cue or phase-resetting event. If such sampling also operates over latent post-stimulus representations, retroperception should fluctuate rhythmically over time. However, unlike pre-cue attention, retroperception operates on a representation that is no longer physically present and may become progressively less accessible. This creates a third possibility: rhythmic access may persist, but its amplitude may weaken over time as the latent representation becomes harder to reactivate.

This theoretical framework motivated three candidate temporal architectures of retroperception. First, the monotonic decay model captures the possibility that retroperception is governed primarily by non-rhythmic decay of the latent sensory trace. In this model, the magnitude of retro-cue-induced enhancement gradually decreases as CTI increases, without periodic fluctuation (Figure 1A). Second, the sustained oscillation model captures the possibility that retroperception follows a stable rhythmic sampling process, analogous to rhythmic attentional sampling observed in pre-cue paradigms.17,18,19,20,21,22 In this model, discrimination accuracy and subjective visibility fluctuate rhythmically across CTI with relatively sustained oscillatory amplitude, superimposed on a slow temporal trend (Figure 1B). Third, the damped oscillation model captures an intermediate possibility in which retroperception is rhythmic but temporally constrained by the declining accessibility of the latent target representation. In this model, the retro-cue periodically enhances access to the target, but the amplitude of this rhythmic enhancement progressively diminishes over time (Figure 1C).Figure 1Schematic representation of hypotheses on the temporal dynamics of retroperceptionThe black dotted lines indicate the timing of key events (i.e., target offsets and cue presentations). To test these competing hypotheses, we employed a dense-sampling paradigm in which both discrimination accuracy and visibility rating measures were collected across a wide range of CTIs, spanning from the pre-cue to retro-cue condition. We then examined whether retroperception exhibits rhythmic fluctuations, and if so, whether these oscillations follow a sustained or damped profile. Temporal dynamics were analyzed using spectral decomposition with irregular resampling auto-spectral analysis (IRASA) to isolate oscillatory components from fractal noise, followed by individualized model fitting to assess the relative explanatory power of each hypothesized pattern. Finally, we evaluated the phase alignment between accuracy and visibility measures to probe functional coupling between attention and perception. This comprehensive approach allowed us to characterize not only the presence of rhythmicity in retroperception but also its temporal constraints and underlying structure.

Results

The main experiment was aimed to capture the temporal dynamics of target discrimination accuracy and subjective visibility of the target (Figure 2A). Our experimental paradigm was adapted from the previous study,7 except that we tested 146 pre-cue SOAs before target and 146 retro-cue SOAs after target, for each of the left and right cueing conditions. At the beginning of each trial, participants were presented with a central fixation point flanked by two circular placeholders, aligned horizontally to the left and right of the fixation. A low-contrast Gabor patch, with a randomly determined orientation, served as the target stimulus and was presented for 50 ms within one of the two placeholders. The contrast level of the Gabor stimuli was individually adjusted for each participant prior to the main experiment to ensure consistent perceptual difficulty. A cue was presented by briefly dimming one of the placeholders to avoid masking the target, with 50% validity. The CTIs ranged from −1,500 ms to −50 ms in the pre-cue condition (Figure 2B) and from 50 ms to 1,500 ms in the retro-cue condition (Figure 2C), in 10 ms steps, and pre- and retro-cue trials occurred with equal probability to minimize expectation effects. Participants performed an orientation discrimination task and reported subjective visibility of the target at the end of each trial. In the orientation discrimination task, a grating matching the target’s orientation and the other grating with an orthogonal orientation were presented. Participants indicated which of the two gratings corresponded to the target orientation. On the visibility response screen, participants were also instructed to rate the subjective visibility of the target on a scale from 1 (not seen) to 8 (maximal visibility) (See details in STAR Methods section).Figure 2Experimental paradigm(A) Example of trial structure. Temporal dynamics of discrimination accuracy and subjective visibility

For each specific time point, behavioral accuracy was calculated by dividing the number of correct responses within a 100 ms time bin, centered on the time point, by the total number of trials within the bin. The average accuracy across all trials was approximately 80% (77.17% ± 1.36%; Symbol ± indicates the standard error). Similarly, visibility ratings were averaged within each 100 ms time bin to estimate subjective visibility at the corresponding time point, with an overall average visibility rating being approximately 4 (4.00 ± 0.20). To capture the temporal dynamics of accuracy and visibility, the time bins were shifted forward by 10 ms, and accuracy and visibility were calculated in the same manner. This process was repeated throughout all cue-to-target interval time points.

In retro-cue conditions, we observed alternating phases of increasing and decreasing behavioral accuracy (Figures 3A and 3B). In addition to this wax-and-wane pattern, the behavioral accuracy as a function of a CTI showed signs of an overall decaying pattern in especially valid cue trials where target appeared at the cued placeholder (blue graphs in the top rows of Figure 3) and invalid cue trials where target appeared at the uncued placeholder (red graphs in the top rows of Figure 3). Visibility ratings also exhibited a rhythmic fluctuation as well as gradual decay (Figures 3C and 3D). Accuracy and visibility ratings in pre-cue conditions showed an overall increasing trend and rhythmic fluctuation, replicating findings from previous studies16,17,19 (Figure S1).Figure 3Temporal dynamics of discrimination accuracy and subjective visibilityBlack dotted lines mark the time of the target offset. Notably, although the overall trends in accuracy and visibility ratings differed between the pre-cue and retro-cue conditions, the observed rhythmic fluctuations in both cueing conditions indicate that oscillatory dynamics in visual information processing persist regardless of whether attention is cued before or after target presentation. Since the primary objective of the research is retroperception, we focus on the temporal dynamics of accuracy and visibility ratings in the retro-cue condition for further analysis.

Rhythmicity in temporal dynamics of discrimination accuracy and subjective visibility

To determine whether both the behavioral accuracy and the visibility rating as a function of a CTI were rhythmic or mere random noise, we conducted a detailed spectral analysis using the established approaches in previous studies.19,25,26 First, we applied IRASA to the observed time courses of accuracy and visibility rating to isolate and analyze the rhythmic component from the 1/f background activity.25 From the Fourier transformed power spectrum of the original behavioral time course, we identified a prominent peak that exceeds the 1/f background activity as an oscillatory peak. We defined the center frequencies of the oscillatory peaks as individual peak frequencies (IPFs) for each cue condition and each participant separately. Individual power spectra for all participants are shown in Figure S2. We computed the average power spectrum across participants, by normalizing and aligning the individual power spectra to each IPF (Figure 4). The resulting average spectrum exhibited a single peak, indicating that the IPFs predominantly drive behavioral fluctuations. Regardless of valid or invalid trials in both left and right cueing conditions, we found that the behavioral time courses exhibited periodic fluctuations in the theta-band for both target discrimination accuracy (Figures 4A and 4B) and visibility rating (Figures 4C and 4D). A two-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed no significant main effects of cue validity (F(1,18) = 0.19, p = 0.76 for accuracy; F(1,18) = 0.53, p = 0.47 for visibility), cue location (F(1,18) = 0.03, p = 0.86 for accuracy; F(1,18) = 0.05, p = 0.82 for visibility), nor interaction effect (F(1,18) = 0.04, p = 0.84 for accuracy; F(1,18) = 0.88, p = 0.36 for visibility). These findings demonstrate that the fluctuations in the target discrimination accuracy are not random but reflect rhythmic oscillations at approximately 4 Hz, persisting even beyond target offset. Model comparison: Testing hypotheses on retroperception

Given that the identified IPFs represent the most dominant frequencies in each participant’s behavioral time courses, we performed model fitting incorporating these IPFs to characterize the temporal profile of the retroperception. This analysis employed three distinct models derived from our theoretical assumptions. The first model is described as exponential decay without any oscillatory component, where t represents the CTI, a is the amplitude of exponential term and λ( <0) is the decay rate of the exponential term, c is the coefficient of linear term, and k is the intercept (Equation 1). The third model introduces an exponential damping parameter to the amplitude of the first-order Fourier series. In the third model, f is the IPF for the Fourier series, a and b are the amplitudes for the cosine and sine components, respectively, and λ(< 0) is the exponential damping coefficients applied to the amplitudes (Equation 3). This damped oscillation model corresponds to our third hypothesis, which proposes that retroperception exhibits a damping oscillation with a linear trend (Figure 5C).(Equation 3)H(t)=exp(λt)[acos(2πft)+bsin(2πft)]+ct+k.

Unlike approaches that first regress out slow temporal trends and then analyze residual oscillations,17,27 our model fitting incorporated a slow linear trend directly into each candidate model. This allowed the non-oscillatory component of the time course to increase or decrease depending on the data, while testing whether the oscillatory component was sustained or attenuated. We fitted each model to the individual time course of accuracy and visibility rating using the least squares method. Goodness-of-fit was assessed using adjusted R2, which quantifies the proportion of variance explained by the model while penalizing model complexity.

First, to determine which of the three hypothetical models best characterizes the overall temporal profiles observed in target discrimination accuracy and visibility rating, we calculated the average goodness-of-fit values across cue location and cue validity for each participant. We found that the damped oscillation model provided the best account of the individual time courses for discrimination accuracy (Figure 6A). Next, we further investigated how cue-target position congruency affected the temporal dynamics of discrimination accuracy and visibility rating by averaging goodness-of-fit values only across cue locations for each participant. When temporal profiles of accuracy and visibility were examined separately for cue-valid and cue-invalid conditions, the damped oscillation model characterized the data better than the monotonic decay model (Figure 6C, mean ΔRadj2 = 0.136 ± 0.020, p < 0.001 for accuracy valid, mean ΔRadj2 = 0.113 ± 0.014, p < 0.001 for accuracy invalid; Figure 6D, mean ΔRadj2 = 0.128 ± 0.024, p < 0.001 for visibility valid, mean ΔRadj2 = 0.156 ± 0.024, p < 0.001 for visibility invalid; Wilcoxon signed-rank within subjects) and the sustained oscillation model (mean ΔRadj2 = 0.027 ± 0.008, p = 0.003 for accuracy invalid; The 2nd column of Figure 6C; mean ΔRadj2 = 0.013 ± 0.005, p = 0.014 for visibility valid; The 1st column of Figure 6D; mean ΔRadj2 = 0.016 ± 0.007, p = 0.016 for visibility invalid; The 2nd column of Figure 6D; Wilcoxon signed-rank test within subjects) except the temporal profile for cue-valid condition where both the damped and sustained oscillation model yielded similar goodness-of-fit value (mean ΔRadj2 = 0.015 ± 0.007, p = 0.24; Wilcoxon signed-rank test within subjects; The 1st column of Figure 6C), indicating a superior fit of the damped oscillation model to the empirical data across nearly all conditions. These results suggest that the temporal dynamics of behavioral accuracy and subjective visibility were slightly better accounted for by the damped oscillation model than by the alternative candidate models, although the advantage over the sustained oscillation model was modest.

Dynamic relationship between accuracy and visibility

Given the similar rhythmic fluctuations in both target discrimination accuracy and subjective visibility, we examined if their time courses are aligned with each other as previous study showed the positive correlation between subjectivity visibility and discrimination accuracy.7 First, we examined if the IPFs of the time course of accuracy and those of the time course of visibility were the same or different. For each condition of the four possible combinations of cue-target position validity, the IPFs of discrimination accuracy time courses were not significantly different from those of subjective visibility time courses (valid left: t(18) = 1.28, p = 0.32; invalid left: t(18) = 0.03, p = 0.97; valid right: t(18) = 0.32, p = 0.75; invalid right: t(18) = 0.58, p = 0.56). To further quantify evidence for the absence of a difference, we additionally computed Bayes factors in favor of the null hypothesis (BF01), using the default Cauchy prior on the standardized effect size centered at zero with scale r = 0.707. The resulting BF01 values were 2.08 for valid left, 4.21 for invalid left, 4.02 for valid right, and 3.62 for invalid right. According to conventional interpretive guidelines, these values provide weak evidence for the null hypothesis in the valid left condition and moderate evidence for the null hypothesis in the remaining three conditions. Overall, both the frequentist and Bayesian analyses indicate that the peak frequencies of discrimination accuracy and subjective visibility were not meaningfully different.

As both time courses of discrimination accuracy and subjective visibility showed theta-band frequencies as IPFs in all four conditions, we further investigated whether their time courses were in-phase at the theta-band frequencies. First, we detrended and filtered the time course of each of discrimination accuracy and subjective visibility using narrow band-pass filter spanning from lowest IPF to highest IPF (2.8–7.2 Hz). We then applied the Hilbert transform to each theta band-filtered time course to extract instantaneous phase at every time point. At every time point, the instantaneous phase difference between accuracy and visibility was calculated. To assess the consistency of these phase differences at the individual-participant level, we applied the Rayleigh test, which evaluates whether circular data deviate from a uniform distribution without assuming a specific mean direction. We found that most participants showed significantly consistent phase differences over time in accuracy and visibility in all four conditions (blue and red arrows in Figure 7), except for one participant in the valid right-cued condition. Individual-level Rayleigh test statistics for the accuracy-visibility phase differences are shown separately for each cue-validity and cue-location condition in Table S1. Next, we performed the V test to determine whether these participant-wise mean phase differences were clustered around 0° at the group level. Unlike the Rayleigh test, the V test evaluates non-uniformity with respect to a specified direction, allowing us to test the a priori hypothesis that accuracy and visibility was phase-aligned around 0°. In the left-cued condition, the participant-wise average phase differences were significantly clustered around 0° in both valid trials (V test; Z = 8.34, p = 0.003; mean phase difference = −12.02°; blue line in Figure 7A) and invalid trials (V test; Z = 13.85, p < 0.001; mean phase difference = −2.85°; red line in Figure 7A). A similar pattern was observed in the right-cued condition. At the group level, the participant-wise average phase differences were again significantly clustered around 0° in both valid trials (V test; Z = 9.66, p < 0.001; mean phase difference = −5.40°; blue line in Figure 7B) and invalid trials (V test; Z = 12.32, p < 0.001; mean phase difference = 8.80°; red line in Figure 7B). Together, these results indicate that rhythmic fluctuations in accuracy and visibility were phase-aligned over time. This phase alignment suggests that fluctuations in objective discrimination performance and subjective visibility were temporally coordinated, such that periods of enhanced visibility tended to coincide with periods of enhanced accuracy (see Figure S5 for phase alignment over time). Discussion

In this study, we examined whether discrimination accuracy and subjective visibility in retroperception follow monotonic, sustained oscillatory, or damped oscillatory temporal profiles. Across the candidate models tested here, the exponentially damped oscillatory model provided a modestly better account of the behavioral time courses than the sustained oscillation model and the non-oscillatory exponential trend model. Importantly, this attenuation refers to the oscillatory component of the behavioral time course, not necessarily to an overall monotonic decline in performance. The model advantage was small and was not uniform across all condition-specific comparisons, and therefore should be interpreted as evidence favoring a damped oscillatory description rather than as definitive proof of a unique underlying mechanism. In addition, discrimination accuracy and subjective visibility showed phase alignment across the retro-cue interval, suggesting that objective and subjective measures of retroperception share a common temporal organization.

A key feature of the present study is that we used a low-salience dimming cue rather than a salient flash cue.16 A previous dense-sampling study examined rhythmic attentional sampling in both pre-cue and retro-cue conditions but detected rhythmicity primarily in the pre-cue condition. One possible reason is that the salient retro-cue used in that study may have produced backward masking,28 thereby obscuring the temporal structure of retroperception. By briefly dimming a placeholder against the background luminance, our design minimized potential masking from the retro-cue and allowed the behavioral time course to reveal rhythmic structure in the post-stimulus period. Under these conditions, we observed theta-band rhythmic fluctuation in retroperception under both left and right cueing conditions. The similarity of peak frequencies across hemifields is consistent with previous findings that when attentional resources are distributed across multiple locations, rhythmic sampling can occur at similar frequencies across these locations.22,29 In the present task, the dimming cue was relatively low in salience and had 50% validity, likely prevented attention from being anchored to a single hemifield for an extended duration. Thus, similar peak frequencies across left and right cueing conditions may reflect distributed attentional sampling across hemifields.

The present findings also refine the relationship between retroperception and rhythmic attentional sampling.20 Previous pre-cue studies have shown that visual detection can fluctuate rhythmically after a spatially informative or phase-resetting event,16,20,21 suggesting that attention samples sensory input through alternating high- and low-excitability states. Our results extend this framework to the post-stimulus domain by showing that retrospective access to a near-threshold target also follows a theta-band rhythm. However, the damping profile observed here suggests that retroperception is not simply a sustained continuation of pre-cue attentional sampling. Unlike pre-cue attention, retroperception operates on a representation that is no longer physically present and whose accessibility may change over time. Thus, the observed rhythm may reflect either a general attentional sampling mechanism applied to a latent post-stimulus representation, or a related but distinct reactivation process specifically engaged during retrospective attention.

One important temporal feature of the observed retroperceptual dynamics was not a uniform decay of behavioral performance, but a possible attenuation of rhythmic modulation around a slow trend. Because the linear trend was included in the model rather than removed beforehand, our analysis preserved both slow non-oscillatory changes and rhythmic fluctuations in the behavioral time course. This is important because slow trends in dense-sampling paradigms may reflect multiple factors, including temporal expectation, hazard-rate effects, task strategy, fatigue, or changes in decision criterion.30,31,32,33,34 By incorporating the trend directly into the model, we could test whether rhythmic retroperceptual access was better described as sustained over time or as gradually attenuating. The modest advantage of the damped oscillation model suggests that the rhythmic component of retroperception was temporally constrained: the cue’s ability to modulate access to the target fluctuated rhythmically, but the magnitude of this fluctuation attenuated as the CTI increased. However, this interpretation should be made cautiously. The damping parameter should not be taken as a direct measure of overall sensory-trace decay or as evidence that behavioral performance monotonically declined over time. Rather, within the present modeling framework, it indexes attenuation of the oscillatory component of retro-cue efficacy. Several mechanisms could give rise to this pattern. If damping reflects decay of a latent sensory representation, it should covary with the strength or quality of the initial target representation, such as stimulus contrast or individual contrast threshold, and should affect discrimination accuracy and subjective visibility in a similar way.35 If damping reflects reduced attentional gain, it should be more strongly modulated by cue validity, attentional state, task relevance, or independent neural markers of top-down engagement.36 If damping reflects a decreasing probability of successful reactivation, it should appear not only as a reduction in the mean oscillatory amplitude but also as increased trial-to-trial variability or more frequent late failures of retro-cue benefit.37 In the present data, damping rates did not differ significantly between accuracy and visibility (t(18) = −0.11, p = 0.92 for valid left; t(18) = −0.29, p = 0.77 for valid right; t(18) = −1.29, p = 0.21 for invalid left; t(18) = 0.15, p = 0.88 for invalid right; paired t test between accuracy damping rate and visibility damping rate within subject), and damping rates did not correlate with individual contrast thresholds (Pearson’s r = 0.24, p = 0.32 for accuracy valid, r = −0.20, p = 0.42 for accuracy invalid, r = 0.17, p = 0.48 for visibility valid, r = −0.05, p = 0.83 for visibility invalid). These results argue against a simple account in which damping directly reflects the strength of the initial sensory representation. Thus, we interpret attenuation as a model based, phenomenological signature of a time-limited rhythmic access process, while leaving open whether this temporal limitation arises from sensory decay, reduced attentional gain, reduced reactivation probability, temporal uncertainty, or their interaction.

The observed phase alignment between discrimination accuracy and subjective visibility should also be interpreted cautiously. Although the two measures showed a stable phase relationship across the retro-cue interval, this does not necessarily imply a single underlying process. The alignment could reflect a common post-stimulus access mechanism influencing both accuracy and visibility, but it could also arise if partially distinct processes are entrained by the same theta-rhythmic temporal structure. Thus, we interpret the phase alignment as evidence for shared temporal coordination between accuracy and visibility, rather than as definitive evidence for a unitary mechanism. This interpretation is consistent with the idea that target discrimination and subjective visibility may be linked by a common temporal access window, while still allowing for partially dissociable sensory, attentional, and decision-related components.7,38

One possible neural implementation of this time-limited rhythmic access process is an interaction between stimulus-evoked and internally generated travelling-wave-like activity.39 Cortical traveling waves can be evoked by external stimuli or generated internally, propagate through recurrent cortical circuits, and transiently modulate local excitability as they pass.40,41 In this framework, retro-cue efficacy may depend on whether the cue arrives at a favorable phase of an ongoing or stimulus-evoked excitability pattern, and damping may reflect the narrowing temporal window over which such an interaction can effectively modulate access to the latent target representation. However, this account remains speculative in the present behavioral study and requires direct electrophysiological testing.

Future electrophysiological work will be needed to identify the neural source of the damped theta-band behavioral rhythm. Prior electrophysiological retro-cue studies typically utilized a limited number of fixed cue-to-probe intervals or averaged delay-period activity over wide temporal windows.42,43,44 Although such approaches are well suited for characterizing macro-level maintenance states, the temporal averaging obscures fine-grained, phase-dependent fluctuations. Combining the present dense temporal sampling approach with EEG or MEG would allow the same damped oscillation model to be fitted to neural measures. If the rhythm reflects sensory reactivation, target-specific decoding from occipital sensors or visual cortical sources should fluctuate at a similar theta-band frequency and attenuate over time. If it reflects attentional control, cue-locked theta activity or decoding of attentional priority should be stronger in fronto-parietal networks. If the effect depends on interaction between sensory and attentional systems, the strongest prediction would be theta-band phase coupling or coordinated amplitude dynamics between occipital and fronto-parietal regions. Such analyses would help distinguish whether the present behavioral rhythm arises from sensory maintenance, top-down attentional reactivation, decision-level readout, or their interaction.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that retroperceptual access is neither instantaneous nor uniformly sustained, but unfolds within a temporally structured window. Dense behavioral sampling revealed theta-band fluctuations in both discrimination accuracy and subjective visibility, and model comparison indicated that these fluctuations were better described by an exponentially attenuating oscillatory profile than by a sustained oscillation or a non-oscillatory exponential trend model. This damped rhythmic profile suggests that post-stimulus access to latent sensory information may be rhythmically organized and temporally constrained, although the small model-fit advantage calls for cautious interpretation and replication in larger samples. By modeling both slow temporal trends and oscillatory dynamics within a unified fitting framework, the present study links rhythmic attentional sampling to conscious access after stimulus offset while highlighting the limits of this temporal flexibility.

Limitations of the study

Despite the strengths of our design, several limitations should be noted. First, our experimental design incorporated both pre-cue and retro-cue conditions as well as cue validity manipulations. Together with dense sampling across an extended range of CTIs, this reduced the number of trials per condition. To increase the number of trials contributing to each time point, we used a 100 ms sliding time window (but see Tables S2 and S3 for 80 and 140 ms sliding window). Although this procedure improved the stability of behavioral estimates, it acted as a temporal smoothing filter. Consequently, our spectral analyses were most sensitive to low-frequency dynamics and were not designed to characterize higher-frequency oscillations. Future studies with larger trial numbers or optimized sampling schemes will be needed to test whether faster rhythms also contribute to retroperception.

Second, although we interpret the theta-band profile as evidence for rhythmic post-stimulus access to a latent sensory representation, the present behavioral data cannot fully exclude contributions from decision- or response-related processes. A purely motor-response account is less likely because responses were made only at the end of each trial, whereas the rhythmic profile was defined by the CTI. However, decision-related readout may still contribute to the observed dynamics. Thus, the theta rhythm may reflect a broader post-stimulus access process in which sensory reactivation, attentional sampling, and decision formation are temporally coordinated.45 Future EEG/MEG studies using time-resolved decoding of target information and covert attention before overt response will be needed to separate these components more directly.

Third, the present study provides behavioral evidence for damped theta-band dynamics but does not directly measure the neural oscillations that may underlie them. The proposed neural interpretation therefore remains indirect. Future electrophysiological work should test whether neural measures of target representation, attentional priority, or occipital-frontoparietal coupling show the same theta-band frequency and attenuation profile observed behaviorally. Such work will be essential for determining whether the behavioral damping reflects sensory decay, reduced attentional gain, decreasing reactivation probability, decision-level readout, or their interaction.

Finally, the sample size was modest (n = 19). Although the dense-sampling design provided many CTIs within each participant, the number of participants limits the precision of group-level estimates and the generalizability of condition-specific model comparisons. This limitation is particularly important for interpreting the model comparison, because the damped oscillation model showed only a small advantage over the sustained oscillation model, and this advantage was not uniform across all condition-specific analyses. In particular, the cue-valid accuracy condition was similarly explained by the sustained and damped oscillation models. Therefore, the present results should be interpreted as evidence that modestly favors a damped oscillatory description of retroperception, rather than as definitive evidence excluding sustained oscillatory dynamics. Future studies with larger samples and independent replication will be needed to determine how robustly the damping component characterizes retroperceptual access.

Experimental model and study participant details

Participants

A behavioral experiment was conducted with human participants in a dark room, as detailed below. Twenty-eight observers were initially recruited. Nine observers were excluded for excessive rejected trials caused by eye movements, leaving nineteen participants for analysis (mean age 22.9 ± 2.32, ten females, nine males). Detailed exclusion criteria are outlined in the Analysis section. All observers had normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity and were naïve to the purpose of the experiment. The observers gave informed consent to participate as paid volunteers. The study protocol was approved by the committee of the Institutional Review Board of the Korea National Institute for Bioethics Policy (P01-201909-13-004).

Method details

Stimuli

All visual stimuli and experimental procedures were presented using Psychophysics Toolbox46,47 within MATLAB (Mathworks Inc.) running on a Mac mini. The observers viewed the 22” CRT (View Sonic PF817) monitor setting to a 100 Hz refresh rate and a resolution of 1,024 × 768 pixels. The monitor luminance was adjusted to generate a linear and constant mean luminance of 22 cd/m2. The viewing distance between the observers and the monitor was 60 cm. The observer’s head was stabilized using a chin rest. An eye-tracking camera (EyeLink 1000 Plus, SR research) was used to monitor observers’ eye movements throughout the experiment.

The experimental paradigm of our study was adapted from Sergent et al.7 A uniform grey background was always on screen. Three stimuli simultaneously appeared in the beginning of each trial. A small bull’s-eye fixation (0.6° of visual angle, black) appeared at the center of screen and two circle placeholders (2.4° of visual angle, black) were aligned horizontally at 4° eccentricity to the left or the right of fixation. The fixation point remained visible until the visibility-response display and the placeholders stayed until the end of each trial.

A Gabor grating (2° of visual angle) was presented as the target for 50 ms within one of the placeholders. The spatial frequency of the grating was 2 cycles per degree of visual angle with a full width at half maximum of 1°. The grating's orientation was randomly selected for each trial, either between 30 and 60 degrees or between 120 and 150 degrees. The grating's average luminance matched the background luminance. The Michelson contrast of the grating was adjusted individually for each observer using a staircase procedure, aiming for approximately 80% accuracy on the orientation-discrimination task, excluding the effects of cueing. A flicker cue was the color change of the left or right placeholder from black to dark gray for 50ms.

Two response-screens were given for each trial. In the orientation-response screen, two small Gabor gratings (1° of visual angle, 4 cycles per degree, 0.5° full width half maximum) were presented at 1° eccentricities above and below fixation, both at maximal contrast. One of the gratings had the same orientation as the target and the other grating’s orientation was orthogonal to the target. Observers were required to indicate which of the two response gratings corresponded to the target's orientation by pressing one of two response buttons within 1.5 seconds (‘z’ key for the upper grating, ‘/’ key for the lower grating).

In the visibility-response screen, observers rated their subjective visibility of the target (1 to 8). The black vertical bar (1°ⅹ4°) was displayed at the center of screen, with eight equidistant positions along the bar representing visibility levels, ranging from ‘Not seen (1)’ to ‘Maximal visibility (8)’. The visibility-rating scheme was based on previous studies.50,51 Specifically, participants were instructed to respond 1 when they did not see a Gabor patch at all, respond 2-4 when they had a vague impression of a Gabor patch depending on the precision of this impression, respond 5-7 when they have seen a Gabor patch depending on the clarity and precision of their target perception, and respond 8 when they saw the clearest possible Gabor patch. A small white cursor (1°ⅹ0.5°) was initially located randomly at one of the positions on the vertical bar. Observers moved the cursor to their chosen visibility level using buttons mapped to the up or down directions and confirmed their selection by pressing the space key within 10 seconds.

Experimental procedure

Prior to the main experiment, an 80-trial staircase procedure52,53 was conducted to determine the target’s contrast level, using the same paradigm as the main experiment but without the cueing manipulation. This procedure was repeated twice for practice. The contrast was adjusted to a level at which the observer could barely discriminate the target’s orientation with 80% accuracy. The lowest contrast level from one of the staircase tasks was selected as the target contrast for the entire experiment.

In the main experiment, each trial began with a fixation screen that lasted for a random duration between 500 ms and 900 ms. A flicker cue was presented at a random time either between 1,550 ms and 100 ms before target onset (pre-cue condition; Figure 2B) or between 100 ms and 1,550 ms after target onset (retro-cue condition; Figure 2C). The inter-stimulus interval (ISI) was fixed within a range of 50 ms to 1,500 ms for both cue conditions, and data analysis was conducted based on the ISI.

The low-contrast target appeared randomly either on the same side as the cue (valid condition) or on the opposite side (invalid condition). The delay periods between the last stimulus onset (target for pre-cue condition and cue for retro-cue condition) and the onset of the response screen were randomly chosen between 150 ms and 450 ms.

Two response screens were presented with the time limits of 1,500ms and 10,000ms, respectively. The main experiment consisted of 984 trials, divided into six blocks of 164 trials each. In each block, target-present trials were equally divided between pre-cue and retro-cue conditions. Approximately 10% of the trials were catch trials, in which no target was presented. Eye calibration tasks were performed twice in a block, once at the beginning of the block and another in the middle of the block.

Behavioral analysis

Analysis was conducted using MATLAB, Fieldtrip toolbox,48 and CircStat toolbox.49 Accuracy of the orientation discrimination task and subjective visibility ratings were analyzed as a function of the CTI for each cue timing (pre-cue vs. retro-cue) and cue validity (valid vs. invalid). At each time point t, we calculated accuracy by dividing the number of correct trials by the total number of trials within a 100-ms time bin, extending from t−50 ms to t+50 ms. To obtain accuracy for the subsequent time point we shifted the time bin by 10 ms and repeated this process throughout the entire CTI. In parallel, we averaged the subjective visibility of the target within the same 100 ms time bins.

We rejected trials if a participant’s gaze point deviated by more than 1° horizontally, because the target was presented exclusively on the horizontal plane relative to the fixation point. Following this procedure, 7 ± 1% of all trials were excluded from the analysis. Participants with missing values in any time bin due to rejected trials were excluded from further analysis.

Spectral analysis using IRASA

Given that we applied a 100 ms sliding time window, which acts as a low-pass filter, we restricted our spectral analysis to frequencies below 10 Hz. To isolate rhythmic oscillation components and remove arrhythmic fractal fluctuations in the temporal dynamics of accuracy and visibility, we applied the Irregular-Resampling Auto-Spectral Analysis (IRASA)20 method in the Fieldtrip toolbox. We detrended each temporal dynamic using a quadratic function, applied a Hanning window, and zero-padded the data to 10 s for high frequency resolution. The 1/f fractal background activity was approximated using a moving time window of 75% of total length of temporal dynamic with a 50 ms step size. Within each window, the data were resampled using complementary non-integer resampling factor (rf) pairs, rf and rf∗ = 2 - rf, with rf ranging from 1.1 to 1.9 in steps of 0.05. After applying a Hanning taper to each window, we estimated the auto-power spectrum using a fast Fourier transform. The spectra obtained across all resampled data were then combined by taking the median, yielding an estimate of the aperiodic 1/f power spectrum. We defined an oscillatory peak as the strongest distinct peak exceeding the average 1/f background activity within 2.7–10 Hz, ensuring reliable estimates from at least four cycles in the temporal dynamic.

Each model was fitted to the individual time course of accuracy and visibility using the least squares method. We calculated the Radj2 score to compare the relative quality of the models, considering both goodness-of-fit and model complexity. Pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to assess differences in goodness-of-fit between models, as the distributions of pairwise differences in goodness-of-fit did not satisfy the normality assumption in some comparisons. Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons.

Frequency and phase alignment

To assess alignment between the temporal dynamic of accuracy and the temporal dynamic of visibility, we examined their IPFs and instantaneous phases. We subtracted the visibility IPF from the accuracy IPF under the corresponding cue conditions; we then tested these IPF differences using a t-test.

To assess phase alignment, we used the Hilbert transform on detrended and band-pass filtered temporal dynamics (2.8-7.2 Hz). We then subtracted the instantaneous phase of visibility from the instantaneous phase of target discrimination accuracy at each corresponding time point. We tested the resulting phase differences of each individual for clustering using the Rayleigh test in the CircStat toolbox. The Rayleigh test was used to assess whether phase angles were non-uniformly distributed around the circle. This test evaluates whether circular data show significant clustering in a particular direction, rather than being randomly distributed. A significant Rayleigh test therefore indicates that the phase values are consistently aligned across observations. After applying false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method,54 we identified significant clusters of phase difference for each participant. Finally, we tested whether the individual phase difference was significantly clustered around 0° using a V test. Unlike the Rayleigh test, which only tests whether circular data are non-uniformly distributed, the V test evaluates whether the data are specifically concentrated around an a priori expected angle. In our analysis, this allowed us to test whether phase differences were reliably aligned around 0°.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB R2024b (MathWorks, RRID:SCR_001622), utilizing functions from the FieldTrip toolbox (RRID:SCR_004849) and the CircStat toolbox (RRID:SCR_01665). All averaged behavioral metrics, spectral power estimates, and model parameter values are reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) unless specified otherwise in the text or respective figure legends. In Figure 6, the violin plots represent the overall distribution of the goodness-of-fit (Radj2) values, with central horizontal lines indicating the median values.

Statistical comparisons of the goodness-of-fit (Radj2) between the three competing models (monotonic decay, sustained oscillation, and damped oscillation) were conducted within-subjects using the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test, as the pairwise differences did not consistently satisfy normality assumptions. For the overall model comparison, Radj2 scores were averaged across cue locations (left and right) for each participant, yielding n = 38 independent goodness-of-fit pairs per comparison; the exact Wilcoxon z-statistics, p-values, and effect sizes for this comparison are reported in the results section under the subsection “model comparison: testing hypotheses on retroperception” and illustrated in Figures 6A and 6B. For the condition-specific comparison, Radj2 scores were averaged across cue locations for valid and invalid conditions separately, yielding n = 19 independent data points per test; the corresponding Wilcoxon test statistics and Bonferroni-corrected p-values are detailed in the results section under the same subsection and shown in Figures 6C and 6D.

To evaluate individual peak frequency (IPF) alignment and test whether discrimination accuracy and subjective visibility shared similar oscillatory frequencies, we subtracted the visibility IPF from the accuracy IPF within-subject for each of the four cue conditions (Valid-Left, Invalid-Left, Valid-Right, and Invalid-Right). These frequency differences were analyzed using paired t-tests (n = 19 pairs representing the matched IPFs of individual participants). To quantify evidence for the null hypothesis, Bayes factors (BF01) were calculated using a default Cauchy prior centered at zero with a scale parameter of r = 0.707. The exact t-statistics, degrees of freedom, p-values, and BF01 values for these frequency alignment comparisons are presented in the results section under the subsection “dynamic relationship between accuracy and visibility”.

For individual-level phase consistency, we performed the Rayleigh test for each participant and cue condition to assess whether the instantaneous phase differences between accuracy and visibility were consistent over time. In this analysis, n = 146 represents the number of instantaneous phase-difference pairs calculated at each 10 ms cue-target interval (CTI) time step (spanning from 50 ms to 1,500 ms). Multiple comparisons across participants were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method. The individual Rayleigh z-statistics, exact p-values, and FDR-corrected p-values for all participants across the four cue conditions are fully detailed in Table S1 (supplemental information) and visually represented in Figure 7.

For group-level phase alignment, we performed V tests at the group level to evaluate whether the individual-level mean phase differences were significantly clustered around the a priori hypothesized angle of 0 degrees. For these tests, n represents the number of participants' mean phase differences. In the Left-cued condition (both valid and invalid) and the Right-cued invalid condition, the sample size was n = 19. In the Right-cued valid condition, the sample size was n = 18, as one participant who did not show significant individual-level phase consistency (failing the Rayleigh test) was excluded from the group-level V test. The exact V-statistics, mean group angles, circular standard deviations, and corresponding p-values for each condition are documented in the results section under the subsection “dynamic relationship between accuracy and visibility” and illustrated in Figures 7A and 7B.

Additionally, a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used to evaluate the potential main effects and interactions of cue validity and cue location on the IPFs of accuracy and visibility. Accuracy and visibility were analyzed separately. Since we compared IPFs across all cue conditions, this resulted in a total of n = 76 data points, where n represents the total number of IPFs collected across all conditions and participants; the ANOVA statistics, including F-statistics, degrees of freedom, and p-values, are provided in the results section under the subsection “rhythmicity in temporal dynamics of discrimination accuracy and subjective visibility”. To compare the damping coefficients (λ) between accuracy and visibility temporal dynamics within participants, we performed paired t-tests (n = 19), repeating the test four times corresponding to each of the individual cue conditions; the exact t-statistics, degrees of freedom, and p-values for these damping coefficient comparisons are reported in the discussion section. Finally, Pearson correlation analyses (n = 19) were conducted to assess the relationship between individual contrast thresholds and the fitted damping coefficients, with the correlation coefficients (r) and exact p-values detailed in the discussion section.
