Imaging features of a frontal neuroglial cyst in an adult patient: A rare case report
Authors: Chaimae Abourak, Yahya El Haras, Sanae Hal El Fadl, Kaoutar Imrani, Ittimade Nassar, Yassine Hafiani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neuroglial cysts, also known as glioependymal cysts, are rare benign congenital intracranial lesions of neuroectodermal origin. They are usually incidental findings and are primarily diagnosed through neuroimaging. We report the case of a 40-year-old patient with no significant medical history who presented with headaches. Brain computed tomography (CT), followed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), revealed a large, well-circumscribed right frontal intra-parenchymal cystic lesion. The lesion showed homogeneous fluid signal intensity, without diffusion restriction or postcontrast enhancement. A mild mass effect was observed on the frontal horn of the ipsilateral lateral ventricle. Based on typical radiological features suggestive of a neuroglial cyst and the absence of complications or significant mass effect, a conservative management approach with clinical and radiological follow-up was adopted after multidisciplinary discussion. Intracerebral neuroglial cysts are rare entities. MRI plays a central role in their diagnosis and in excluding differential diagnoses. Conservative management may be appropriate in asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic patients without significant mass effect or complications.

Introduction

Glioependymal cysts, also known as neuroglial cysts, are rare benign congenital intracranial lesions of neuroectodermal origin resulting from developmental abnormalities of the neural tube [1,2]. They represent a very small proportion of primary intracranial cysts and are most often discovered incidentally on neuroimaging. MRI typically shows a well-defined cystic lesion with cerebrospinal fluid-like signal intensity and no postcontrast enhancement [1,2].

We report a rare case of a right frontal intra-parenchymal glioependymal cyst in an adult patient, highlighting its imaging features and conservative management.

Case presentation

We report the case of a 40-year-old patient with no significant medical history who was admitted to the emergency department for headaches evolving over a few days.

Clinical examination revealed a conscious patient, well oriented in time and space, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15/15. Hemodynamic parameters were stable, with normal blood pressure. No motor or sensory neurological deficit was identified on clinical examination.

A noncontrast brain CT scan was performed, revealing a large intra-parenchymal cystic lesion in the right frontal lobe. The lesion was oval-shaped, well defined, with regular margins and a thin wall, and showed homogeneous fluid-density content, measuring 46 × 52 × 47 mm (TR × AP × CC). It exerted a mild mass effect on the frontal horn of the ipsilateral lateral ventricle as well as on the adjacent brain parenchyma (Fig. 1). Based on these radiological findings, the main differential diagnoses included a neuroglial cyst and a cerebral hydatid cyst. Based on the clinical and radiological findings, a strong suspicion of a right frontal neuroglial (glioependymal) cyst was raised. After multidisciplinary discussion involving radiologists, neurosurgeons, and clinicians, a conservative management approach was chosen due to the absence of complications or signs of intracranial hypertension. Close clinical and radiological follow-up was recommended.

Discussion

Glioependymal cysts, also known as neuroglial cysts, are rare benign neuroepithelial lesions of embryonic origin resulting from abnormal neuroectodermal development [1]. They account for approximately 0.4%-1% of primary intracranial cysts, with fewer than thirty cases reported in the literature [2,3].

These lesions may develop in various regions of the central nervous system, including the ventricles, cerebral parenchyma, subarachnoid space, cerebellum, and spinal cord [2]. Glioependymal cysts are classically described within the lateral ventricles and the third ventricle. However, among the intraparenchymal forms, the frontal lobe represents the most frequently reported location, probably reflecting their embryological origin from ectopic ependymal cell rests. Less common locations have also been reported in the posterior fossa, brainstem, and, more rarely, along the cranial nerve sheaths [2,4]. In our case, the cyst was located within the right frontal lobe.

Their pathogenesis remains poorly understood, although several embryological theories have been proposed [3]. The most widely accepted hypothesis suggests abnormal displacement of the ventricular epithelium during neural tube closure, leading to isolation of neuroepithelial cells that retain secretory activity [1,2]. This secretory activity may explain the progressive enlargement of the cyst over time [2].

Macroscopically, these lesions appear as thin-walled translucent cystic cavities containing fluid similar to cerebrospinal fluid, sometimes with high protein content [2]. Glioependymal cysts are most commonly located within the lateral and third ventricles, although intraparenchymal forms have also been described [3].

Most reported cases occur in children and neonates, making adult presentations particularly rare [1], which represents the main peculiarity of our case.

Clinical manifestations of glioependymal cysts are variable and mainly depend on their size, location, and the degree of mass effect on adjacent structures [1]. Most lesions are asymptomatic and incidentally discovered during brain imaging examinations. However, some patients may develop neurological symptoms related to compression of the brain parenchyma or disturbance of cerebrospinal fluid circulation [1,3].

Supratentorial cysts most commonly present with headaches, seizures, focal neurological deficits, or signs of intracranial hypertension [1,3,5]. In some cases, cognitive impairment, parkinsonian syndrome, or even coma may occur [5]. When the ventricular system is involved, hydrocephalus may develop, leading to nausea, vomiting, and altered consciousness [3]. In our case, the patient presented with headaches.

In children, macrocephaly, psychomotor delay, or growth retardation may also be observed [1,5]. Infratentorial locations, which are less common, may cause unusual manifestations such as hemifacial spasm, vertigo, diplopia, or isolated rhinolalia [5].

Computed tomography (CT) and, especially, MRI are the reference imaging modalities for diagnosing glioependymal cysts [1]. On imaging, these lesions typically appear as well-defined cystic formations with homogeneous fluid content and signal characteristics similar to cerebrospinal fluid: hypointense on T1-weighted images, hyperintense on T2-weighted images, and showing signal suppression on FLAIR sequences [1]. In some cases, the cyst content may show slightly higher signal intensity than CSF due to increased protein concentration [1,2]. The absence of wall enhancement after gadolinium administration is an important diagnostic feature [2,5].

In infants, these cysts are often associated with other central nervous system anomalies, such as agenesis of the corpus callosum or cortical malformations like microgyria [2,3]. In our patient, no associated brain abnormalities were identified, particularly no involvement of the corpus callosum.

The differential diagnosis of glioependymal cysts includes a wide spectrum of intracranial cystic lesions, including arachnoid cysts, epidermoid cysts, porencephalic cysts, cystic encephalomalacia, hydatid cysts, colloid cysts, Rathke’s cleft cysts, meningoencephaloceles, enlarged perivascular (Virchow–Robin) spaces, and cystic glial neoplasms such as astrocytomas or gangliogliomas [2,4]. On MRI, glioependymal cysts typically demonstrate cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)-like signal intensity, appearing hypointense on T1-weighted images and hyperintense on T2-weighted images, without contrast enhancement or diffusion restriction [3]. Epidermoid cysts characteristically demonstrate diffusion restriction due to their keratinaceous content, in contrast to glioependymal cysts, which typically do not restrict diffusion [3,5]. Cystic glial tumors usually contain a mural nodule or solid enhancing component and may exhibit surrounding parenchymal signal abnormalities. Porencephalic cysts and cystic encephalomalacia are generally sequelae of congenital or acquired cerebral injury and are commonly associated with communication with the ventricular system or subarachnoid space, adjacent gliosis, and focal brain volume loss. Enlarged perivascular (Virchow–Robin) spaces may mimic CSF-containing cysts; however, they typically follow the course of penetrating vessels, are located in characteristic regions such as the basal ganglia, centrum semiovale, or midbrain, and rarely reach the large size observed in our patient. Arachnoid cysts are classically extra-axial and displace the adjacent cerebral cortex, whereas glioependymal cysts are predominantly intra-axial. In endemic regions, cerebral hydatid disease should also be considered in the differential diagnosis of a large intracranial cystic lesion. However, the absence of daughter cysts or mural enhancement, together with negative hydatid serology, argued strongly against this diagnosis in our patient.

In the present case, the absence of diffusion restriction, contrast enhancement, mural nodule or solid component, associated gliosis, and ventricular communication, combined with the purely intra-axial frontal location and CSF-like MRI signal characteristics, strongly supported the diagnosis of a glioependymal cyst.

Because of the rarity of glioependymal cysts, no standardized treatment strategy has been established to date. Management should therefore be individualized according to the patient's clinical presentation, cyst size and location, associated mass effect, and radiological evolution. Surgical intervention is generally indicated for symptomatic patients, and several surgical approaches have been described in the literature [2]. When technically feasible and compatible with the lesion's location, complete surgical excision is considered the preferred treatment option because it minimizes the risk of recurrence [1]. Cyst fenestration, particularly through a neuroendoscopic approach, represents a minimally invasive alternative that has been widely reported, although it may be associated with a higher risk of recurrence [2,5]. Cystoperitoneal shunting is another therapeutic option, especially when complete excision is not feasible. It provides durable decompression by diverting cyst fluid, but requires close clinical follow-up because of the potential complications associated with shunt dependency [1].

The clinical course of neuroglial cysts is variable, ranging from completely asymptomatic forms requiring only observation to symptomatic cases that may require surgical management [3]. The natural history of untreated cysts remains poorly understood due to their rarity [6]. However, recent data suggest that surgery should be reserved for symptomatic lesions or those showing progressive growth [3].

Samadian et al. [4] reported a case of a right cerebellopontine angle neuroglial cyst that recurred 12 years after surgery, initially misdiagnosed as an arachnoid cyst. Niwa et al. [6] described an initially asymptomatic neuroglial cyst that led to progressive symptom worsening 7 years after incidental discovery. In contrast, Czervionke et al. and Dodaj et al. reported cases of neuroepithelial cysts that showed significant size reduction during a 3-year follow-up without surgical intervention [3].

In our case, a conservative approach was also chosen, with close clinical and radiological follow-up, due to the absence of complications or significant mass effect.

Conclusion

Intracerebral glioependymal cysts are rare lesions, and their diagnosis mainly relies on MRI, which allows recognition of their typical imaging features and exclusion of the main differential diagnoses. Our case illustrates an unusual adult presentation of a large right frontal intraparenchymal cyst, revealed by headaches.

This observation highlights the importance of clinico-radiological correlation and confirms that, in the absence of significant mass effect or complications, conservative management with follow-up can be a safe option. It also adds to the literature by contributing further evidence on the presentation and management of this rare entity.
