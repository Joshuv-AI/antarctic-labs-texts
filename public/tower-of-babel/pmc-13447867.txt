Conserved Active Site Loop Residues of E. coli DinB and Human DNA Polymerase Kappa Govern Activity
Authors: Nicole M. Antczak, Paul Ippoliti, Jiyoun Ahn, Lakindu Pathira Kankanamge, Tamara Dudko, Khadijah Balfour‐Jeffrey, Bix Pancoe, Zoe MacDiarmid, Chuc May Pham, David Williams, Alexandra Mora, Jason M. Walsh, Penny J. Beuning
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDNA damage is ubiquitous and can arise from numerous sources. To mitigate the potential effects of DNA damage, cells possess varied DNA repair and damage tolerance mechanisms. Conserved throughout evolution, specialized DNA damage‐bypass DNA polymerases from the Y family provide DNA damage tolerance. E. coli harbors two Y‐family polymerases whereas humans have four members of the Y family. E. coli DinB and human DNA polymerase kappa have shown similar damage bypass profiles in that they both are specific for minor groove adducts and are inhibited by major groove adducts. These two proteins share a similar active site loop that is adjacent to the nascent base pair. These active site loops were analyzed by alanine scanning mutagenesis with the resulting proteins characterized in primer extension assays and for their thermal stability. Most variants show similar activity to the respective wild‐type proteins, with a few mutations resulting in dramatic losses of activity and changes in stability. The effects of the mutations are remarkably similar in DinB and polymerase kappa, with mutation of specific aligned residues showing decreased activity and/or stability. Most of the variants have similar thermal stability as the respective wild‐type proteins and show the characteristic increase in stability in the presence of substrates, with the less active variants in general showing limited stabilization by DNA or DNA and incoming nucleotides.

Introduction

DNA damage is a constant threat to all life from both endogenous and exogenous sources, including radiation from UV light and environmental pollutants (Friedberg et al. 2006). Adducts can be formed on the N 2 position of deoxyguanine such as N 2‐furfuryl‐dG (N 2ffdG) and N 2‐aminoquinoline 1‐oxide from exposure to nitrofurazone and 4‐nitroquinoline oxide, respectively (Panigrahi and Walker 1990; Whiteway et al. 1998; Jarosz et al. 2006). Many cancer treatments bind to DNA, like cisplatin that forms interstrand and intrastrand crosslinks between adjacent guanines (Jamieson and Lippard 1999). These DNA adducts and others can inhibit replicative DNA polymerases (pols), and to continue replication, cells employ damage‐bypass DNA pols, including those from the Y family. Y‐family DNA pols are found throughout evolution (Ohmori et al. 2001) and bypass damaged DNA in the process of translesion synthesis (TLS) (Radman 1975).

E. coli DinB (DNA pol IV) and its human ortholog pol kappa (pol κ) (Yang and Woodgate 2007) are capable of bypassing adducts on the N 2 position of deoxyguanine (Tang et al. 2000; Shen et al. 2002; Jarosz et al. 2006; Yuan et al. 2008; Irimia et al. 2009) and are inhibited by major groove adducts (Levine et al. 2001; Choi et al. 2006; Walsh, Bouamaied, et al. 2011; Walsh and Beuning 2012; Walsh et al. 2013; Nevin, Lu, et al. 2015; Xu et al. 2016). DinB has been shown to have higher fidelity when copying certain damaged templates relative to undamaged templates. For example, in comparison to undamaged DNA, DinB and mammalian DNA pol κ show a preference for replicating DNA containing the minor groove adduct N 2‐furfuryl‐dG (Jarosz et al. 2006).

The DinB F13V variant has compromised TLS on N 2‐furfuryl‐dG (Jarosz et al. 2006), whereas the ability of DinB F13V to bypass undamaged DNA was not substantially affected (Jarosz et al. 2006). DinB F13 is the steric gate residue, which prevents the incorporation of ribonucleotides (Jarosz et al. 2006; Brown and Suo 2011; Nevin, Engen, et al. 2015). Other studies have tested DinB variants to determine mutation frequency, fidelity, or damage specificity (Benson et al. 2011, 2014). For instance, cells harboring DinB variants D8A, D8H, R49A, R49F, D103A, D103N, or E104A exhibited mutation frequencies at or near those of cells lacking dinB, a ~104‐fold decrease in mutation frequency relative to cells harboring wild‐type DinB (Wagner et al. 1999).

Compared with replicative DNA polymerases, Y‐family DNA polymerases have larger, more accessible active sites to accommodate bulky lesions, but the active site geometries and damage specificity can vary within the Y family and their inherent specificity is still poorly understood. The loop 1 region of Y‐family pols is located adjacent to the incoming nucleotide and template base, suggesting that it plays an important role in polymerase function. In E. coli DinB, loop 1 comprises residues 38 to 46 within the fingers domain, whereas the loop 1 of pol κ spans residues 135 to 141 (Figure 1). In DinB, loop 1 residue S42 interacts with the incoming nucleotide via both direct and water‐mediated polar interactions and regulates the fidelity of incorporation (Sharma et al. 2013). WT DinB misincorporates nucleotides with different combinations of template base and incoming nucleotide, whereas DinB S42A showed reduced misincorporation (Sharma et al. 2013). The catalytic efficiency of DinB S42A opposite N 2ffdG decreased twofold compared to WT DinB and upon exposure to nitrofurazone (4 μg/mL) cells harboring the DinB S42A variant conferred sensitivity (Kottur et al. 2015). Another variant harboring a mutation in loop 1, DinB R38 has been shown to be important in error‐free bypass of the N 2‐benzo[a]pyrene‐dG adduct, as the R38A mutation increases dGTP misincorporation (Sholder et al. 2015). DinB R38, along with C66, is important for strand exchange during error‐prone double‐strand break repair (Tashjian et al. 2019). DinB S42, V40, and Y46 residues in loop 1 were previously suggested to be important for activity using the THEMATICS and POOL programs (Ondrechen et al. 2001; Tong et al. 2009; Somarowthu and Ondrechen 2012; Walsh et al. 2012). Our previous study swapping these loops of DinB and pol κ showed that DinB activity was sensitive to changes in the length and composition of the loop, whereas pol κ was relatively tolerant to replacing its loop with the DinB loop sequence (Antczak et al. 2017). Just outside of this loop, DinB R35A eliminated DinB discrimination against the major groove adduct N 6‐furfuryl‐dA (Walsh et al. 2013).

Among the pol κ loop 1 residues 135 to 141, M135 is important for the stabilization of the syn conformation of 8‐oxoG by contributing van der Waals and stacking interactions (Vasquez‐Del Carpio et al. 2009). In pol κ, the M135A mutation decreased correct dCTP incorporation opposite an undamaged dG and opposite 8‐oxo‐dG (Vasquez‐Del Carpio et al. 2009). Computational studies indicate that pol κ T138 forms a hydrogen bond with the β‐phosphate of the incoming dCTP in the bypass of the N 2‐benzo[a]pyrene‐dG adduct and Y141 forms a hydrogen bond with the γ‐phosphate of the nucleotide in the transition state (Lior‐Hoffmann et al. 2012). Just N‐terminal to our definition of the loop, the mutation V130I in pol κ increased its activity on DNA harboring the major groove adduct N 6‐furfuryl‐dA (Antczak et al. 2017).

Given its location adjacent to the nascent base pair, it has been proposed that loop 1 may be important for efficiency and specificity. In this work, we used alanine‐scanning mutagenesis to systematically test the roles of residues located in the loop 1 regions of DinB and pol κ for their effects on activity with undamaged DNA and DNA containing N 2‐furfuryl‐dG. In addition, thermal shift assays were carried out to determine the stability of the variants and the effects of the presence of substrates. Overall, the effects of mutations in loop 1 residues are similar in DinB and pol κ, with mutations at the analogous positions in both proteins resulting in similar effects.

Materials and Methods

Protein Expression and Purification

Variants of both DinB and pol κ were made via site‐directed mutagenesis using a QuikChange kit (Agilent), using appropriately designed primers as well as the appropriate expression vector, pDFJ1 for DinB protein purification, pYG768 for in vivo nitrofurazone survival assays with DinB (Beuning et al. 2006), and pBG101 for pol κ (Irimia et al. 2009). The DNA was sequenced to confirm the mutations (MGH DNA core, Macrogen, or Eton Bioscience).

DinB was purified as described (Beuning et al. 2006) and was stored in single use aliquots at −80°C. Recombinant pol κ (residues 19–526) was purified as described (Irimia et al. 2009) and stored in single use aliquots at −80°C.

Nitrofurazone Survival Assays

The strain AB1157 ΔdinB harboring the DinB single alanine loop 1 variants expressed from plasmids was exposed to nitrofurazone (NFZ) as described previously (Beuning et al. 2006; Shurtleff et al. 2009; Hawver et al. 2011, 2015). Serial dilutions of overnight cultures were plated on LB agar plates containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin with or without 1.5 μg/mL NFZ and incubated at 37°C for 20–24 h. Values represent the average survival relative to the respective sample without NFZ and error bars represent the standard deviation from at least three trials.

Primer Extension and Misincorporation Assays

The primer extension assays were completed with 61‐mer DNA template (undamaged or containing a single N 2‐furfuryl‐dG) and either running start or standing start DNA. The template DNA sequence was 5′‐GGTTACTCAGGATCAGGCCTGCGAAGACCTXGGCGTCCGGCTGCAGCTGTACTATCATATGC‐3′ where X is either an undamaged G or N 2‐furfuryl‐dG (N 2ffdG). The running start primer sequence was 5′‐GCATATGATAGTACAGCTGCAGCCGGACGC‐3′ and the standing start primer sequence was 5′‐GCATATGATAGTACAGCTGCAGCCGGACGCC‐3′. The DNA was synthesized by Operon, Eriks Rozners (SUNY Binghamton), or Ke Zhang (Northeastern University).

The DNA template containing a single N 2ffdG was prepared as previously described (DeCorte et al. 1996; Jarosz et al. 2006). DNA was purified by denaturing polyacryalmide gel electrophoresis and the crush and soak method (Sambrook et al. 1989). DNA primers (running start and standing start) were end labeled with 32P as previously described (Sambrook et al. 1989; Beuning et al. 2006).

For all assays, DNA template (unmodified, or N 2ffdG) was combined with 32P‐labeled primer (running start, or standing start) in a 1:1 ratio (500 nM) and annealed in annealing buffer (20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5) and 5 mM Mg(OAc)2) by heating for 2 min at 95°C, incubating at 50°C for 60 min and cooling to 37°C. Reactions were carried out in reaction buffer (30 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 20 mM NaCl, 7.5 mM MgSO4, 2 mM β‐mercaptoethanol, and 1% bovine serum albumin), 100 nM 32P labeled primer/template and 10 nM pol κ or 25 nM DinB. For primer extension, to study replication past the lesion, running start primer and all four dNTPs at a final concentration of 500 μM were used to initiate the reactions. Timepoints of 0, 20, and 60 min were taken; the zero timepoint aliquot was removed before the addition of dNTPs to initiate the reaction. For misincorporation assays, the final concentrations used were 100 nM 32P labeled primer/template with standing start primer, and 1 mM dATP, dCTP, dGTP, or dTTP in separate reactions. Misincorporation assay timepoint was taken after 30 min. Reactions for the assays were quenched with 85% formamide, 0.025% xylene cyanol, 0.025% bromophenol blue, and 50 mM EDTA. Samples were analyzed on 12%–16% polyacrylamide 8 M urea gels and imaged on Molecular Dynamics storage phosphor imaging screens with a Storm 860 or a Typhoon.

Thermal Shift Assays

Thermal shift assays were conducted as previously described (Nevin, Engen, et al. 2015; Nevin, Lu, et al. 2015), using dideoxy‐terminated Primer13 (IDT) and an undamaged or damaged Template18 DNA template, annealed as for the primer extension assays except at a final concentration of 75 μM. Protein at a final concentration of 5 μM was combined in the presence or absence of DNA (at a final concentration of 10 μM) and dNTP (dCTP or dTTP at 1 mM) with assay buffer (30 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 20 mM NaCl, 7.5 mM MgSO4, 2 mM β‐mercaptoethanol) in 15 μL total volume. The reactions were incubated for 20 min at room temperature before 1 μL Sypro Orange (20X, Life Technologies) was added. The fluorescence was detected as the temperature was increased from 15°C to 100°C at a rate of 0.2°C per 10 s in a Bio‐Rad CFX Opus 96 real‐time PCR instrument. Values represent the average of at least three trials, and error bars represent the standard deviation.

Results

Analysis of Loop 1 in DinB and Pol Kappa

We identified active site loop 1 as adjacent to both nucleotides of the nascent base pair in crystal structures of E. coli DinB and human pol κ (Figure 1) (Sharma et al. 2013; Jha et al. 2016). This loop is relatively well conserved among members of the DinB subfamily and distinct from other Y‐family polymerases, although the loop length varies even among the DinB subfamily members (Gerlach et al. 1999; Boudsocq et al. 2002; Lee et al. 2006; Antczak et al. 2017). Given its proximity to the nascent base pair, we tested the role of this loop in polymerase activity by using alanine‐scanning mutagenesis to obtain the R38A, V40A, I41A, S42A, T43A, N45A, and Y46A mutations in DinB and M135A, L136A, S137A, T138A, S139A, N140A, and Y141A mutations in pol κ. Upon alignment of the DinB and pol κ structures and loop sequences (Figure 1), it is observed that DinB R38 does not align with any of the pol κ loop 1 residues, as the loop of DinB is longer than that of pol κ. Moreover, no mutations were made at DinB G39 or A44.

Most DinB Variants Confer Nitrofurazone Resistance

DinB has been shown to confer resistance to nitrofurazone in E. coli (Jarosz et al. 2006). We first tested the ability of the DinB variants to confer resistance to nitrofurazone in an E. coli strain in which dinB has been deleted from the chromosome (Jarosz et al. 2006). Cells containing DinB I41A, N45A, and Y46A variants have low survival upon exposure to 1.5 μg/mL of nitrofurazone (Figure 2). The remaining variants confer survival similar to wild‐type DinB.

Survival of cells harboring DinB loop 1 variants upon exposure to 1.5 μg/mL nitrofurazone. EV indicates empty vector. Survival (%) is determined relative to untreated samples. Bars represent the average and error bars represent standard deviation, n = 5 for all but DinB‐N45A and DinB‐Y46A, for which n = 4. Individual points are represented as circles.

DinB Loop 1 Residues Have a Range of Activity on Undamaged DNA and N 2ffdG DNA

DinB variants harboring single‐Ala mutations in loop 1 were assayed for primer extension activity. DinB I41A and N45A variants have no detectable activity on either undamaged DNA or DNA containing the N 2ffdG lesion under the conditions used (Figure 3). DinB R38A and Y46A variants have low activity on both DNA substrates. The remaining variants, DinB V40A, S42A, and T43A, are active on both DNA templates (Figure 3).

Primer extension assays for the loop 1 variants of DinB including WT DinB: R38A, V40A, I41A, S42A, T43A, N45A, Y46A on (A) undamaged DNA and (B) N 2 ‐furfuryl‐dG DNA. Time points 0, 20, and 60 min.

WT DinB and Loop 1 Variants Have Increased Misincorporation Opposite N 2ffdG

Misincorporation assays were performed with WT DinB and loop 1 variants on both the undamaged DNA and N 2ffdG‐containing DNA templates. On undamaged DNA, WT DinB and the variants incorporate only correct dCTP, with the exception of I41A and N45A, which are inactive (Figure 4). Opposite the N 2ffdG lesion, wild‐type DinB correctly inserts dCTP and, to a lesser extent, misinserts dTTP. Three variants, DinB V40A, S42A, and T43A, also misinsert dTTP. DinB S42A exhibited the most dTTP misincorporation, while DinB V40A and DinB T43A show less misincorporation than wild‐type DinB. The other active variants, DinB R38A and Y46A, do not exhibit dTTP misincorporation (Figure 4).

Nucleotide misincorporation results for the DinB loop 1 variants on (A) undamaged DNA and (B) N 2 ‐furfuryl‐dG DNA. Each variant has a zero point which contains no nucleotide and 30 min time points for each nucleotide. The pattern shown for WT DinB is repeated for each variant.

Most Pol Kappa Loop 1 Variants Have Primer Extension Activity Similar to WT Pol Kappa

The pol κ loop 1 variants are all active on both DNA substrates. The pol κ L136A and Y141A variants show decreased activity relative to WT pol κ with both DNA substrates, with pol κ Y141A failing to complete full extension (Figure 5). Pol κ L136 and Y141 align with DinB I41 and Y46, respectively, which also showed reduced activity (Figures 1 and 3).

Primer extension assays for variants of pol κ including WT pol κ: M135A, L136A, S137A, T138A, S139A, N140A, Y141A on (A) undamaged DNA and (B) N 2 ‐furfuryl‐dG DNA. Time points 0, 20, and 60 min.

Pol Kappa Loop 1 Variants Have Increased Misincorporation Opposite N 2ffdG

Misincorporation assays were completed with standing start primer to determine which nucleotide can be inserted by each pol κ variant opposite undamaged dG or N 2ffdG. On the undamaged template, WT pol κ correctly incorporates dCTP but also weakly misincorporates dTTP opposite dG. Pol κ M135A, S137A, T138A, S139A, and N140A correctly incorporate dCTP and, like WT pol κ, misincorporate dTTP (Figure 6). No dTTP misincorporation is observed with pol κ L136A and Y141A variants.

Nucleotide misincorporation results for WT pol κ and loop 1 variants at 10 nM on (A) undamaged DNA and (B) N 2‐furfuryl‐dG DNA. Each variant has a zero point which contains no nucleotide and a 30‐min time point for each nucleotide; each nucleotide was used at a final concentration of 1 mM. The pattern shown for WT pol κ is repeated for each variant.

In the assay for misincorporation opposite N 2ffdG, WT pol κ correctly incorporates dCTP and misincorporates dTTP. Pol κ L136A and Y141A only incorporate dCTP opposite N 2ffdG. Pol κ M135A, S137A, T138A, S139A, and N140A variants misincorporate dTTP opposite N 2ffdG. Pol κ S137A also misincorporates dGTP opposite N 2ffdG (Figure 6), making it the most prone to misincorporation of the Pol κ loop 1 variants.

DinB and Pol Kappa Loop 1 Variants Maintain Stability

The stability of the DinB and pol κ loop 1 variants in the presence and absence of substrates was assessed using a thermal shift assay. Most of the DinB variants show similar stability to that of WT DinB, except DinB V40A, which was lower, and DinB N45A, which was higher, and all display the characteristic increase in melting temperature in the presence of undamaged or damaged DNA and an additional increase with the correct incoming nucleotide dCTP (Figure 7). DinB N45A showed only modest increases in Tm in the presence of any of the substrates tested. Pol κ variants show stability similar to WT pol κ, with pol κ L136A and pol κ Y141A lacking the characteristic increase in stability in the presence of substrates.

Bars represent the averages and error bars represent standard deviation; n = 3. Individual points are represented as circles.

Discussion

The conserved DinB subfamily polymerases of the Y family confer tolerance to DNA damaging agents in a wide range of organisms (Walsh, Hawver, et al. 2011; Stern et al. 2019). Bacterial DinB is important in damage bypass and contributes to mutagenesis, including mutations that confer antibiotic resistance, as shown in E. coli , Mycobacterium tuberculosis , Pseudomonas aeruginosa ( P. aeruginosa ), and P. putida (Sanders et al. 2006; Cirz et al. 2007; Petrosino et al. 2009; Gawel and Seed 2011; Jatsenko et al. 2017; Dupuy et al. 2022; Fahey et al. 2023). E. coli DinB and its human ortholog DNA pol κ (Jarosz et al. 2006; Yang and Woodgate 2007) bypass N 2‐dG adducts as well as undamaged dG with relative accuracy (Shen et al. 2002; Jarosz et al. 2006; Seo et al. 2006; Yuan et al. 2008), although WT DinB and pol κ can also misincorporate nucleotides (Jarosz et al. 2006; Sharma et al. 2013; Jha et al. 2016). Here we have shown that WT pol κ misincorporates dTTP opposite both undamaged dG as well as N 2ffdG while DinB weakly misincorporates dTTP opposite N 2ffdG. The loop 1 residues of both DinB and pol κ are important in primer extension activity and correct nucleotide incorporation.

DinB R38, which does not have an equivalent residue in pol κ, when mutated to Ala has weak activity on both undamaged and damaged DNA (Figure 3). The R38 residue is located on the region of the loop near the single‐stranded DNA template as it enters the active site (Figure 1). The positive charge on R38 could help stabilize the negatively charged DNA as it is translocated during replication, and DinB R38A is known to have a role in error‐free bypass of N 2‐benzo[a]pyrene (Sholder et al. 2015). This region of the protein is important for activity (Lee et al. 2006), as DinB R35 also appears to interact with the DNA template strand, and the DinB R35A variant shows increased activity in bypass of a major groove adduct relative to WT DinB (Walsh et al. 2013). Similarly, pol κ V130I relaxes discrimination against a major groove adduct relative to WT pol κ (Antczak et al. 2017). Moreover, hydrogen‐deuterium exchange mass spectrometry experiments revealed that DinB loop 1 residues are protected from exchange upon binding DNA and further protected from exchange in the presence of the correct, but not incorrect, incoming nucleotide, with either undamaged or N 2‐furfuryl‐dG‐containing DNA; peptides containing the loop 1 region were not detected for pol κ (Nevin, Lu, et al. 2015).

Both DinB V40A (Figure 3) and pol κ M135A (Figure 5) are active on both undamaged and N 2‐furfuryl‐dG DNA, similar to both wild‐type proteins. V40 and M135 are located adjacent to the templating base opposite the incoming nucleotide (Figure 1). Previous work has shown that pol κ M135 is important for the stabilization of the template base and the M135A mutation decreased activity on undamaged DNA (Vasquez‐Del Carpio et al. 2009).

DinB I41A and pol κ L136A show little‐to‐no primer extension activity on either DNA template. DinB I41 and pol κ L136 are oriented away from the templating nucleotide and incoming nucleotide base pair and toward the rest of the fingers domain (Figure 1). These residues may contribute to the stability and folding of the protein, and so decreasing the size of the hydrophobic chain could affect the structural integrity of the proteins. Both variants have similar thermal stability as their respective WT proteins, but in both cases, the DinB I40A and pol κ L136A variants lack the typical increase in melting temperatures with substrates.

DinB S42A and pol κ S137A show activity similar to their respective WT proteins on both DNA templates and have an increase in misincorporation opposite N 2ffdG. DinB S42 and pol κ S137 are directly adjacent to the incoming nucleotide (Figure 1). DinB S42 interacts with the incoming nucleotide via direct and water mediated hydrogen bonds and, when mutated to alanine, was observed to decrease misincorporation (Sharma et al. 2013; Kottur et al. 2015). In addition, cells containing the DinB S42A variant had a decrease in survival upon exposure of 4 μg/mL nitrofurazone (Kottur et al. 2015), whereas we found that, upon exposure to the lower dose of 1.5 μg/mL nitrofurazone, cells containing the same mutation had similar survival to cells harboring WT DinB (Figure 2); under our conditions, cell survival is severely compromised for all strains when treated with > 2 μg/mL nitrofurazone. The difference in these observations may be due to the use of a tagged DinB, in which cleavage of the purification tag leaves a small amount of additional sequence, whereas DinB used here is untagged, or due to other differences in experimental conditions (Kottur et al. 2015). Modest differences in activity and fidelity of native compared to His‐tagged DinB have been observed by others, especially in misincorporation (Kobayashi et al. 2002). Pol κ S137A had greater dTTP misincorporation on both templates as compared to WT pol κ, which is analogous to our observations with DinB S42A; pol κ S137A also showed misincorporation of dGTP.

DinB T43A and pol κ T138A are moderately less active than wild‐type DinB and pol κ, respectively. Pol κ T138 interacts with the β‐phosphate of the incoming dCTP during bypass of N 2‐benzo[a]pyrene‐dG adduct (Lior‐Hoffmann et al. 2012). The incoming nucleotide would lose contacts in the context of the T138A mutation, although other interactions with the incoming nucleotide must compensate for this loss given the modest decrease in activity.

Pol κ S139 aligns with DinB A44 and is oriented away from the incoming nucleotide (Figure 1). Ser139 could be important for structural integrity of the area surrounding the loop but the activities of pol κ S139A are similar to those of WT pol κ, as are the melting temperatures.

DinB N45A shows no activity on either undamaged or N 2‐furfuryl‐dG DNA templates. However, pol κ N140A has activity similar to WT pol κ. DinB Y46A, aligned with pol κ Y141A, has low activity on both templates. DinB Y46 and pol κ Y141 are in similar positions in both proteins, near the phosphates of the incoming nucleotide (Figure 1). The loss of the side chain could affect interactions with the incoming nucleotide and decrease the overall efficiency of the proteins. Pol κ Y141 hydrogen bonds with the γ‐phosphate of the incoming nucleotide during formation of the transition state (Lior‐Hoffmann et al. 2012). DinB Y46 also interacts with R49 (Del Conte et al. 2024), which is analogous to R49 in P. aeruginosa , and mutation of R49 in either E. coli or P. aeruginosa compromises dinB function (Figure 8) (Wagner et al. 1999; Sanders et al. 2006). In addition, Y141 is among the top residues, ranked #15, predicted as catalytically important in pol κ from electrostatics‐based prediction methods THEMATICS and POOL (Pathira Kankanamge et al. 2023), potentially explaining the low activity of pol κ Y141A as well as DinB Y46A, which is ranked #19 from the same type of analysis (Walsh et al. 2012).

E. coli DinB (4IRK) (Sharma et al. 2013) showing the locations of N45 and Y46 (yellow sticks) relative to R49 (magenta sticks).

These active site loops in DinB and pol κ differ in length but are conserved in terms of amino acid identities in the C‐terminal part of the loop. This region of the proteins, adjacent to the nascent base pair, is important for activity and nucleotide selectivity. We previously showed that DinB is sensitive to changes in length and composition of this loop but pol κ was much less so (Antczak et al. 2017), whereas we show here that mutation of similar positions in the loop by alanine scanning has similar effects on activity and stability.
