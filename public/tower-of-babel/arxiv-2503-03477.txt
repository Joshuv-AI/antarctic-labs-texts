Dual spectroscopy of quantum simulated Fermi-Hubbard systems
K. Knakkergaard Nielsen, M. Zwierlein, G. M. Bruun

Abstract
Quantum gas microscopy with atoms in optical lattices provides remarkable insights into the real space properties of many-body systems, but does not directly reveal the nature of their fundamental excitation spectrum. Here, we demonstrate that radio-frequency spectroscopy can reveal the quasi-particle nature of doped quantum many-body systems, crucial for our understanding of, e.g., high-temperature superconductors. In particular, we showcase how the existence and energy of magnetic polaron quasi-particles in doped Fermi-Hubbard systems may be probed, revealed by hallmark peaks in the spectroscopic spectrum. In combination with fundamental dualities of the Fermi-Hubbard model, we describe how these findings may be tested using several experimental platforms.

.– Consider spin-

Ejection spectroscopy.
–
To circumvent this broadening and avoid challenging post-selection, we propose instead to use ejection spectroscopy. Here, one starts out in the state of interest, i.e., a system with a single (or low density of) dopant(s) forming magnetic polarons. – In addition to a finite pulse length,

Conclusions.
– Using a non-perturbative theory for the Fermi-Hubbard model close to half-filling, we systematically analysed how its low energy spectrum can be explored using RF ejection spectroscopy with atoms in optical lattices. In particular, we demonstrated that a smoking gun detection of magnetic polarons is possible via the presence of a clear quasi-particle peak in the RF ejection spectrum. Moreover, we showed that the particle-hole and repulsive-attractive dualities of the Fermi-Hubbard model makes our scheme realistic for current quantum simulators using both repulsive
[
18
,
21
]
and attractive
[
73
]
interactions. Finally, we demonstrated how pulse lengths longer than the inverse hopping,
1/t
, and probing frequency accuracies on the order of
100\,{\rm Hz}
are sufficient to clearly resolve the quasi-particle peaks, bringing the experimental realization within reach. Our probing scheme generally investigates transitions between states with
N_{d}
and
N_{d}-1
dopants, and, therefore, naturally extends to investigating many-body states in general such as for example bound magnetic polaron pairs.

SI
Linear response

In this section, we give more details on the derivation of the linear response for spectroscopy. We start from the Hamiltonian for the radio-frequency transfer in the rotating wave approximation

{\hat{H}}_{\rm RF}(\tau)=\Omega(\tau)e^{-i\delta\tau}\sum_{{\bf k}}\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}}\hat{c}_{{\bf k}\downarrow}+{\rm H.c.},

(S1)

where
\delta=\omega_{\rm pr}-(\varepsilon^{(f)}-\varepsilon^{(\uparrow)})
is the detuning of the probe frequency
\omega_{\rm pr}
to the bare transition frequency
\varepsilon^{(f)}-\varepsilon^{(\uparrow)}
. For simplicity, we assume that the pulse envelope
\Omega(\tau)
is real and even in
\tau
. We also assume that the non-interacting state
\ket{f}
is trapped, and may move with the same hopping amplitude as the spin-
\ket{\uparrow}
and -
\ket{\downarrow}
states

{\hat{H}}_{f}=-t\sum_{\braket{{\bf i},{\bf j}}}\left[\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{{\bf i}}\hat{f}_{{\bf j}}+{\rm H.c.}\right]=\sum_{{\bf k}}\varepsilon^{f}_{\bf k}\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}}\hat{f}_{{\bf k}},

(S2)

with
\varepsilon^{f}_{\bf k}=-2t[\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y}]
. Let us now see, how the linear response signal arises. For both injection and ejection spectroscopy, we assume that the initial state only contains
\ket{\uparrow}
- and
\ket{\downarrow}
-particles. We are then interested in computing the total production,
N_{f}
, in the non-interacting third state,
\ket{f}
, as a function of detuning
\delta
, as the probe is turned on. This is directly observable in quantum simulation experiments by utilizing spin-specific single-site resolution. Alternatively, at the end of the experimental run, one can push out all atoms remaining in the
\ket{\uparrow},\ket{\downarrow}
-states and then count the remaining atoms in the
\ket{f}
-state. The general linear response formula for an operator
\hat{A}
,

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{A}}=\braket{\hat{A}}_{0}-i\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}{\rm d}s\braket{\big[(\hat{A})_{I},(\hat{V}(s))_{I}\big]}_{0},

(S3)

subject to a time-dependent perturbation
\hat{V}(s)
can now be used to calculate what we are after. Here,
\braket{\cdot}_{0}
means an average with respect to the non-perturbed [
\hat{V}=0
] state. In particular, we set
\hat{A}=\partial_{\tau}\hat{N}_{f}
[
\hat{N}_{f}=\sum_{\bf k}\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{\bf k}\hat{f}_{\bf k}
], and
\hat{V}(s)={\hat{H}}_{\rm RF}(s)
. Here, a sub-index
I
means that we are calculating observables in the interaction picture with
\hat{V}={\hat{H}}_{\rm RF}
the interaction Hamiltonian. Hence,

\displaystyle(\partial_{\tau}\hat{N}_{f})_{I}

\displaystyle=-i\left[\hat{N}_{f},{\hat{H}}_{\rm RF}(\tau)\right]=-i\Omega(\tau)\sum_{{\bf k},{\bf q}}\left[\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{\bf k}(\tau)\hat{f}_{\bf k}(\tau),e^{-i\delta\tau}\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{{\bf q}}(\tau)\hat{c}_{{\bf q}\downarrow}(\tau)+e^{+i\delta\tau}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{{\bf q}\downarrow}(\tau)\hat{f}_{{\bf q}}(\tau)\right]

\displaystyle=-i\Omega(\tau)\sum_{\bf k}\left(e^{-i\delta\tau}\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{\bf k}(\tau)\hat{c}_{{\bf k}\downarrow}(\tau)-e^{+i\delta\tau}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}\downarrow}(\tau)\hat{f}_{\bf k}(\tau)\right),

(S4)

with
\hat{f}_{\bf k}(\tau)=e^{+i{\hat{H}}\tau}\hat{f}_{\bf k}e^{-i{\hat{H}}\tau}=e^{-i\varepsilon_{\bf k}^{f}\tau}\hat{f}_{\bf k}
,
\hat{c}_{{\bf k}\downarrow}(\tau)=e^{+i{\hat{H}}\tau}\hat{f}_{{\bf k}\downarrow}e^{-i{\hat{H}}\tau}
, and
{\hat{H}}={\hat{H}}_{t,U}+{\hat{H}}_{f}
. Inserting this into Eq. (
S3
) together with a bit of rearranging yields

\displaystyle\braket{\partial_{\tau}\hat{N}_{f}}=\Omega(\tau)\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}ds\,\Omega(s)\sum_{{\bf k},{\bf q}}\Big(e^{i(\delta-\varepsilon^{f}_{\bf k})\tau}e^{-i(\delta-\varepsilon^{f}_{\bf q})s}\braket{\big[\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}\downarrow}(\tau)\hat{f}_{{\bf k}},\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{{\bf q}}\hat{c}_{{\bf q}\downarrow}(s)\big]}_{0}+{\rm c.c.}\Big).

(S5)

Since there are no non-interacting
\ket{f}
-atoms in the initial state, we get that

\displaystyle\braket{\big[\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}\downarrow}(\tau)\hat{f}_{{\bf k}},\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{{\bf q}}\hat{c}_{{\bf q}\downarrow}(s)\big]}_{0}=\delta_{{\bf q},{\bf k}}\braket{\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}\downarrow}(\tau)\hat{c}_{{\bf q}\downarrow}(s)}_{0}=\delta_{{\bf q},{\bf k}}\braket{\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}\downarrow}(\tau-s)\hat{c}_{{\bf q}\downarrow}(0)}_{0},

(S6)

Finally, we define
G(\tau)=-i\theta(\tau)\sum_{\bf k}e^{-i\varepsilon^{f}_{\bf k}\tau}\braket{\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}\downarrow}(\tau)\hat{c}_{{\bf k}\downarrow}(0)}_{0}
. Using this above then shows that

\displaystyle N_{f}(\delta)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\tau\braket{\partial_{\tau}\hat{N}_{f}}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\tau\,\Omega(\tau)\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}ds\,\Omega(s)\Big(e^{i\delta(\tau-s)}iG(\tau-s)+{\rm c.c.}\Big)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,\Omega(\delta-\omega)\Omega(\omega-\delta)A(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,\Omega^{2}(\delta-\omega)A(\omega).

(S7)

The third equality follows from Fourier transformation,
f(\tau)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\omega\,e^{-i\omega\tau}f(\omega)/(2\pi)
, and using
A(\omega)=-2{\rm Im}G(\omega)
. The fourth and last equality follows from the fact that since
\Omega(\tau)
is even in
\tau
, i.e., even in the time domain,
\Omega(\omega)
is even in
\omega
, i.e., even in the frequency domain. This proves Eq. (4) of the main text.

The starting point is the coupling of the hole to the linear spin waves,
{\hat{H}}^{\rm eff}
, in Eq. (6) of the main text. From here, the holon Green’s function,
\mathcal{G}({\bf p},\tau)=-i\theta(\tau)\bra{{\rm AFM}}\hat{h}_{\bf p}(\tau)\hat{h}^{\dagger}_{\bf p}(0)\ket{{\rm AFM}}
is approximated by taking only the rainbow diagrams for the self-energy into account

,

\Sigma({\bf p},\omega)=\sum_{\bf k}g^{2}({\bf p},{\bf k})\mathcal{G}({\bf p},\omega-\omega_{\bf k}),

(S8)

where
[\mathcal{G}({\bf p},\omega)]^{-1}=\omega-\Sigma({\bf p},\omega)+i0^{+}

g({\bf p},{\bf k})=4t[u_{\bf k}\gamma_{{\bf p}+{\bf k}}-v_{\bf k}\gamma_{\bf p}]/\sqrt{N}

SIII
Ejection Green’s function

We start from the ejection Green’s function for the hole

\displaystyle\!\!G({\bf p},\tau)=-i\theta(\tau)\sum_{\bf k}e^{-i\epsilon^{\rightarrow}_{\bf k}\tau}\bra{\Psi^{\rm pol}_{\bf p}}\hat{h}^{\dagger}_{\bf k}(\tau)\hat{h}_{\bf k}(0)\ket{\Psi^{\rm pol}_{\bf p}},

(S9)

where
\ket{\Psi^{\rm pol}_{\bf p}}
is the magnetic polaron eigenstate in the SCBA [see Eq. (7) of the main text]. Moreover, letting
\tilde{G}({\bf p},\delta)=G({\bf p},\delta)/Z_{\bf p}
, we find an expansion for
\tilde{G}
in terms of the number of generated spin waves in the polaronic state. Namely,

\displaystyle\tilde{G}({\bf p},\delta)

\displaystyle=\tilde{G}_{0}({\bf p},\delta)\!+\!\sum_{{\bf k}_{1}}[g({\bf p},{\bf k}_{1})\mathcal{G}({\bf K}_{1},\varepsilon^{\rm pol}_{\bf p}\!-\!\omega_{{\bf k}_{1}})]^{2}\times\tilde{G}_{0}({\bf K}_{1},\delta\!-\!\omega_{{\bf k}_{1}})+\dots

(S10)

This is equivalent to Eq. (9) of the main text. Here,
[\tilde{G}_{0}({\bf p},\delta)]^{-1}=\delta+\varepsilon^{\rm pol}_{\bf p}-\varepsilon^{\rightarrow}_{\bf p}+i0^{+}
, and
\varepsilon^{\rm pol}_{\bf p}
is the polaron energy at crystal momentum
{\bf p}
. Now, promoting
\varepsilon^{\rm pol}_{\bf p}
to a general frequency
\omega

[
3
]
, and letting
\tilde{G}_{0}({\bf p},\delta),\tilde{G}_{h}({\bf p},\delta)\to\Gamma_{0}({\bf p},\delta;\omega),\Gamma({\bf p},\delta;\omega)
then shows that

\displaystyle\Gamma({\bf p},\delta;\omega)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{0}({\bf p},\delta;\omega)\!+\!\sum_{{\bf k}_{1}}[g({\bf p},{\bf k}_{1})\mathcal{G}({\bf K}_{1},\omega\!-\!\omega_{{\bf k}_{1}})]^{2}\times\Gamma_{0}({\bf K}_{1},\delta;\omega-\omega_{{\bf k}_{1}})+\dots

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{0}({\bf p},\delta;\omega)\!+\!\sum_{{\bf k}_{1}}[g({\bf p},{\bf k}_{1})\mathcal{G}({\bf K}_{1},\omega\!-\!\omega_{{\bf k}_{1}})]^{2}\times\Gamma({\bf K}_{1},\delta;\omega-\omega_{{\bf k}_{1}}),

(S11)

yielding a self-consistency equation for
\Gamma({\bf p},\delta;\omega)

G({\bf p},\delta)=Z_{\bf p}\Gamma({\bf p},\delta;\omega=\varepsilon^{\rm pol}_{\bf p})

SIV
Fluctuations in probing frequency

In this Section, we analyze the effect of fluctuations in the radio-frequency probe frequency, and hence in the detuning
\delta
. Consequently, we let
\Delta
denote the additional detuning (so
\delta+\Delta
is the total detuning) and assume that it varies according to a normal distribution around
\Delta=0
between different experimental runs. Averaging over these experimental runs for the ejection spectroscopic result in Eq. (
S7
), means that
G(\tau)\to h(\tau)G(\tau)
, with

\displaystyle h(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\Delta\,e^{+i\Delta\tau}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{\Delta}^{2}}}e^{-\Delta^{2}/[2\sigma_{\Delta}^{2}]}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\phi\,e^{+i\phi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(\sigma_{\Delta}\tau)^{2}}}e^{-\phi^{2}/[2(\sigma_{\Delta}\tau)^{2}]}=e^{-(\sigma_{\Delta}\tau)^{2}/2}.

(S12)

A similar analysis was performed in Ref.
[
4
]
. In combination with the finite-pulse result in Eq. (8) leads to the double convolution

\displaystyle N_{f}(\delta)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{d\nu}{2\pi}\,\Omega^{2}(\delta-\nu)h(\nu-\omega)A(\omega),

(S13)

where
h(\omega)=\sqrt{2\pi}/\sigma_{\Delta}\times\exp[-\omega^{2}/(2\sigma_{\Delta}^{2})]
is the Fourier transform of
h(\tau)
. Assuming a Gaussian pulse shape with pulse length
\tau_{0}
means that
\Omega^{2}(\nu)=\sqrt{8\pi}(\Omega_{0}\tau_{0})^{2}\exp[-2(\tau_{0}\nu)^{2}]
. We can then carry through the
\nu
integration above, yielding

\displaystyle N_{f}(\delta)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}F(\delta-\omega)A(\omega),

(S14)

with the effective pulse function,

\displaystyle F(\omega)=\sqrt{8\pi}\Omega^{2}_{0}\tau_{0}\times\tilde{\tau}_{0}\exp[-2(\tilde{\tau}_{0}\omega)^{2}],

(S15)

and the effective probing time

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}_{0}=[\tau_{0}^{-2}+4\sigma_{\Delta}^{2}]^{-1/2}.

(S16)

It is, hereby, this effective probing time that needs to be
\gtrsim 1/t
. In other words, for a specific probing time
\tau_{0}\geq 1/t
, the RF probing frequency needs to be stable within a deviation of
\sigma_{\Delta}\lesssim\sqrt{t^{2}-\tau_{0}^{-2}}/2
. If this is not the case, the quasiparticle signal is washed out.
