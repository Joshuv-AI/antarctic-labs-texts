Abstract
This paper discusses various aspects of the collection of unitary operators C ​ U ​ C CUC , where U U is a fixed unitary operator on a complex Hilbert space ℋ \mathcal{H} and C C varies over the set of all conjugations on ℋ \mathcal{H} (antilinear, isometric, involutions). We call this class of unitary operators, the conjugate orbit of U U and denote it by 𝔒 c ​ ( U ) \mathfrak{O}_{c}(U) . We will see that U ∗ U^{*} , the Hilbert space adjoint of U U , always belongs to 𝔒 c ​ ( U ) \mathfrak{O}_{c}(U) , while U U belongs to 𝔒 c ​ ( U ) \mathfrak{O}_{c}(U) only when U U is unitarily equivalent to U ∗ U^{*} , making U U a member of 𝔒 c ​ ( U ) \mathfrak{O}_{c}(U) an uncommon event. We completely describe the conjugate orbit of the classical bilateral shift and discuss when a unitary multiplication operator on the classical Lebesgue space of the unit circle belongs to this conjugate orbit. We also broaden this discussion to include the bilateral shifts of higher multiplicity which, via unitary equivalence, makes connections to other interesting unitary operators such as the translation and dilation operators on the Lebesgue space of the real line. Finite unitary matrices provide us with a rich source of examples of conjugate unitary orbits to discuss. In particular, we determine which diagonal matrices, if any, belong to the conjugate orbit of a fixed unitary matrix. Closely related to the finite unitary matrices are the diagonalizable unitary operators with respect to some, possibly infinite, orthonormal basis. We give a large class of variations of these unitary operators that belong to the conjugate orbit and establish a connection to the classical Fourier–Plancherel and Hilbert transforms. Finally, we develop a model for a unitary operator using real Hilbert spaces and use it to describe the conjugate orbit as well as revisit some of our previous discussions in another light.

Chapter 1
Introduction

This paper initiates a study of certain classes of linear transformations on a complex Hilbert space and their connections to a well-studied notion of a conjugation in a vector space that shares the same properties as complex conjugation in
\mathbb{C}
. The subject of our investigation is inspired by the following idea.
For a fixed unitary operator
U
on a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
(
U
is linear, surjective, and isometric – equivalently
U^{*}U=UU^{*}=I
), one can define the
unitary orbit

\mathfrak{O}(U)
of
U

to be the collection of operators
VUV^{*}
, where
V
ranges over all the unitary operators on
\mathcal{H}
. In other words,
\mathfrak{O}(U)
is the set of all unitary operators on
\mathcal{H}
that are unitarily equivalent to
U
. By the spectral theorem,
\mathfrak{O}(U)
consists of all the unitary operators on
\mathcal{H}
with the same spectral parameters as
U

[
9
, Ch. IX]
. For example, when
U
is an
n\times n
unitary matrix, regarded as a linear transformation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
(viewed as column vectors) by
{\bf x}\mapsto U{\bf x}
, the unitary orbit
\mathfrak{O}(U)
is the collection of all
n\times n
unitary matrices which have the same eigenvalues as
U
along with the same corresponding (algebraic/geometric) multiplicities. There is a generalization of this spectral and multiplicity theory for unitary operators on infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces (see
[
9
]
or
[
21
]
). So, in a way, the unitary orbit
\mathfrak{O}(U)
is well understood.

In this paper we examine the
conjugate orbit

\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U):=\{CUC:\mbox{$C$ is a conjugation on $\mathcal{H}$}\}

of a unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
.
Here, a
conjugation

C
on a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is an antilinear, involutive, isometry in that

C(\alpha{\bf x}+{\bf y})=\overline{\alpha}C{\bf x}+C{\bf y}
for all
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
(antilinear),
C^{2}:=C\circ C=I
(involutive), and
\|C{\bf x}\|=\|{\bf x}\|
for all
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}
(isometric). We refer the reader to the papers
[
15
,
16
,
22
,
25
,
26
]
for a thorough discussion of conjugations. Simple examples of conjugations include
C{\bf x}=\overline{{\bf x}}
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
(complex conjugate each entry of
{\bf x}
);

on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, where

is an
n\times n
self-transpose unitary matrix;
Cf=\bar{f}
on

(

\mathbb{T}
);
Cf=u\bar{f}
on
L^{2}(m)
(

\mathbb{T}
);

on

\alpha
is (Lebesgue) measure preserving on
\mathbb{R}
with

. We will encounter a rich variety of other conjugations throughout this paper.

Since
CUC
is
linear
(the conjugation

, which is antilinear, is applied twice), isometric, and onto, then

\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
is certainly contained inside the class of all unitary operators on
\mathcal{H}
. The purpose of this paper is to initiate a study of

\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
and, in certain tangible cases, give its complete description. Moreover, we discuss how the two orbits
\mathfrak{O}(U)
(the unitary orbit of
U
) and
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
(the conjugate orbit of
U
) differ. For example, for any
n\times n
unitary matrix
U
,
\mathfrak{O}(U)
contains every
n\times n
diagonal matrix consisting of the eigenvalues of

(with respective multiplicities). However, there are examples of unitary matrices
U
for which
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
contains some diagonal matrices consisting of the eigenvalues of
U^{*}
, but not others. Still further, there are examples of unitary matrices
U
where
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
contains no diagonal matrices. For a general unitary operator

we will show that although

, it is only under quite special circumstances that

. However, it is always the case that
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)\subseteq\mathfrak{O}(U^{*})
.

We also discuss the relative “size” of
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
and describe when the unitary operators
CUC
and

, where

and

are conjugations, represent the same element of

. In addition, we give a tangible characterization of the conjugate orbit of the classical bilateral shift

on

This paper is structured as follows. In Chapter

. We also show that

Chapter

C_{2}UC_{2}

The special case of

denotes the matrix

with all of its entries complex conjugated. In addition, we also state a criterion as to which classes of diagonal matrices (if any) belong to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
and point out further differences between the conjugate orbit
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
and the unitary orbit
\mathfrak{O}(U)
.

The bilateral shift
M
, defined on the standard orthonormal basis
({\bf e}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
for the classical sequence space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
by
M{\bf e}_{j}={\bf e}_{j+1}
, equivalently, via Fourier series,
(Mf)(\xi)=\xi f(\xi)
on
L^{2}(m)
, is taken up in Chapter
4
. Here we show that
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
consists precisely of the unitary shifts of the form
{\bf v}_{j}\mapsto{\bf v}_{j+1}
, where
V=[\cdots|{\bf v}_{-2}|{\bf v}_{-1}|{\bf v}_{0}|{\bf v}_{1}|{\bf v}_{2}|\cdots]
is a doubly infinite unitary matrix with
V^{t}=V
. Results in this section make the case that very few doubly infinite Toeplitz matrices generated by unimodular symbols (or, equivalently, unitary multiplication operators on
L^{2}(m)
) belong to the conjugate orbit of the bilateral shift
M
. Using a certain form of the spectral theorem for unitary operators, we show that when
\sigma(U)
, the spectrum of a general unitary operator

, has more then one point, then
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
is quite an abundant set. An extension of our discussion of the classical bilateral shift to bilateral shifts of higher multiplicity continues in Chapter
4
, where we show the complexity and the great differences between the conjugate orbit of the bilateral shift of multiplicity one (where we have a description as certain unitary shifts) and the conjugate orbit of the bilateral shifts of higher multiplicity. We also give several examples of naturally occurring unitary operators, such as the translation and the dilation operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, as well as multiplication by an inner function on
L^{2}(m)
, that are represented by shifts of higher multiplicity. Thus, though these operators are easy to describe, they have quite complicated conjugate orbits.

In the last several chapters, we develop a model for a unitary operator using real Hilbert spaces along with the concept of complexification and use this model to give a description of the conjugate orbit of a general unitary operator. We will revisit some of the results from the previous sections involving unitary matrices, the Fourier–Plancherel and Hilbert transforms, as well as the bilateral shift through this model.

This paper is meant to give a solid foundation as well as an invitation to this topic of conjugate orbits and we invite the reader to continue our initial investigation and describe the conjugate orbit for their favorite unitary operator.

Chapter 2
Some Basic Facts About the Conjugate Orbit

Throughout this paper,
\mathcal{H}
will denote a complex separable Hilbert space with inner product
\langle{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle
and corresponding norm
\|{\bf x}\|:=\sqrt{\langle{\bf x},{\bf x}\rangle}
. A linear transformation
A
on
\mathcal{H}
will be
bounded
if its norm
\|A\|:=\sup\{\|A{\bf x}\|:\|{\bf x}\|=1\}

is finite.
These bounded linear transformations on
\mathcal{H}
will be called linear
operators
.
The focus of this paper will be the
unitary operators

U
on
\mathcal{H}
. These are the linear transformations
U
which are isometric (
\|U{\bf x}\|=\|{\bf x}\|
for all
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}
) and surjective (
U\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}
). With each bounded linear operator
A
on
\mathcal{H}
, there is a unique bounded linear operator
A^{*}
, called the
adjoint
of
A
, such that

\langle A{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle=\langle{\bf x},A^{*}{\bf y}\rangle
for all
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}
. The criterion that
U
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
can be restated algebraically as
U^{*}U=UU^{*}=I
(the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}
).

2.1
Some inhabitants of the conjugate orbit

As
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
is contained in the set of all unitary operators on
\mathcal{H}
, it would be helpful to have some readily identifiable elements of
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
. We begin with the following fact which follows from an observation in
[
15
, Sec. 4]
. We will arrive at this same fact in Remark
2.9
and again in Remark
6.22
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
we have
U^{*}\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
.

Proof.

A version of the spectral theorem for unitary operators says that
U
is unitarily equivalent to a multiplication operator
M_{\varphi}f=\varphi f
on
L^{2}(\mu,X)
(
X
a compact Hausdorff space,
\mu
a positive finite Borel measure on
X
, and
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\mu,X)
and unimodular) in that there is an isometric isomorphism
W:\mathcal{H}\to L^{2}(\mu,X)
with
M_{\varphi}W=WU

[
9
, p. 279]
. If
J
is the conjugation on
L^{2}(\mu,X)
defined by
Jf=\bar{f}
, a calculation verifies that
JM_{\varphi}J=M_{\bar{\varphi}}=M_{\varphi}^{*}
. Furthermore, one can also verify that
C:=W^{*}JW
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
which satisfies
CUC=U^{*}
. Thus,
U^{*}\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

For a fixed unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
, the paper
[
25
]
characterizes
all
of the conjugations

for which
CUC=U^{*}
. For example, when

(Uf)(\xi)=\xi f(\xi)

is the

on

(recall that

When is

on

such that
CUC=U

Proposition 2.3
.

For a unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
we have
U\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
if and only if
U
is unitarily equivalent to
U^{*}

One direction of this is easy to prove. Indeed, suppose that
CUC=U

is linear, isometric, and onto – and thus is a unitary operator. Finally,

\displaystyle U^{*}V

\displaystyle=(C_{1}UC_{1})(C_{1}C)

\displaystyle=C_{1}(UC)

\displaystyle=C_{1}(CU)

\displaystyle=VU

and so
U
and unitarily equivalent to

The other direction is more involved and we refer the reader to

For example, by the spectral theorem from linear algebra, the

unitary matrices whose eigenvalues are either one or minus one (or both) are examples of unitary operators that are unitarily equivalent to their adjoints (as are unitary matrices whose eigenvalues occur in conjugate pairs with equal corresponding multiplicities). Another example is the bilateral shift

(Uf)(\xi)=\xi f(\xi)

and Example

and Example
6.27
. For a given unitary operator

on
\mathcal{H}
, the paper
[
26
]
characterizes the conjugations
C
on
\mathcal{H}
for which
CUC=U
(when such conjugations exist). For example, for the bilateral shift
U
(as above) on

, any conjugation
C
on
L^{2}(m)
for which
CUC=U
is of the form
(Cf(\xi)=u(\xi)\overline{f(\bar{\xi})}
, where

, is unimodular, and satisfies
u(\xi)=u(\bar{\xi})
almost everywhere.

Remark 2.4
.

When
U=\lambda I
, where
|\lambda|=1
and
I
is the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}
, we can use the facts that a conjugation is antilinear and involutive to see that
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)=\{\overline{\lambda}I\}
. So, in this case, the conjugate orbit consists of just one operator. In fact, if
U
is
any
unitary operator whose spectrum
\sigma(U)
is a single point
\{\lambda\}
, then
\lambda
is clearly an isolated point of the spectrum and thus must be an eigenvalue. By the spectral theorem, it must be the case that
U=\lambda I
.
However, we shall see throughout this paper that when its spectrum
\sigma(U)
consists of more than one point,
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
is usually quite a large and rich class of unitary operators (see Remark

). That being said, this next result shows that, up to unitary equivalence, the conjugate orbit can be regarded as “small”.

Proposition 2.5
.

If
U
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
and
V_{1},V_{2}\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
, then
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are unitarily equivalent.

Proof.

Suppose
V_{1}=C_{1}UC_{1}
and
V_{2}=C_{2}UC_{2}
for conjugations
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
on
\mathcal{H}
. Then, since
C_{1}^{2}=C_{2}^{2}=I
, we have

C_{1}V_{1}C_{1}=C_{2}V_{2}C_{2}
and so
(C_{2}C_{1})V_{1}=V_{2}(C_{2}C_{1})
. Since
C_{2}C_{1}
is unitary (linear, isometric, and onto), we see that
V_{1}
is unitarily equivalent to
V_{2}
.
∎

Combine Proposition
2.1
with Proposition
2.5
to obtain the following.

Corollary 2.6
.

For any unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
, we have
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)\subseteq\mathfrak{O}(U^{*})
.

We will see in Example

and Example

that the two orbits
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
and
\mathfrak{O}(U)
are rarely equal.

2.2
An initial characterization

This next result is our initial description of the conjugate orbit of a unitary operator. Though it is a simple observation, it will be important to later results and will be refined and expanded throughout this paper. Recall from Proposition
2.1
that for any unitary operator
U
, there is always a conjugation
C
, in fact many of them
[
25
]
(see also Remark
2.2
), such that
CUC=U^{*}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Suppose that
U
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
and
C
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
such that
CUC=U^{*}
. Then, for any unitary operator
V
on
\mathcal{H}
, the following are equivalent.

(a)

V\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
.

(b)

There is a unitary operator
W
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
CWC=W^{*}
and
V=WU^{*}W^{*}
.

Before getting to the proof, there is a small detail to discuss concerning adjoints. Suppose that
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are conjugations on
\mathcal{H}
. Then
C_{1}C_{2}
is a unitary operator since it is linear, isometric, and onto. Moreover, for any
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}
, we can use properties of conjugations to obtain

\langle C_{1}C_{2}{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle=\langle C_{1}{\bf y},C_{2}{\bf x}\rangle=\langle{\bf x},C_{2}C_{1}{\bf y}\rangle

and thus

(C_{1}C_{2})^{*}=C_{2}C_{1}.

(2.8)

Proof of Proposition
2.7
.

(a)\Longrightarrow(b)
: If
V\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
, there is a conjugation
C_{1}
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
V=C_{1}UC_{1}
.
Then

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=C_{1}UC_{1}

\displaystyle=C_{1}(CU^{*}C)C_{1}

\displaystyle=(C_{1}C)U^{*}(C_{1}C)^{*}.

Note the use of (
2.8
) in the last line of the above calculation. The operator
W:=C_{1}C
is unitary and, by the previous calculation, satisfies
WU^{*}W^{*}=V
. Moreover,

\displaystyle CWC

\displaystyle=C(C_{1}C)C

\displaystyle=CC_{1}

\displaystyle=(C_{1}C)^{*}

\displaystyle=W^{*}.

(b)\Longrightarrow(a)
: Assume that
W
is a unitary operator that satisfies

V=WU^{*}W^{*}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;CWC=W^{*}.

Then
C_{1}:=WC
is a conjugation. Indeed,
C_{1}
is isometric and antilinear and

C_{1}^{2}=(WC)(WC)=WW^{*}=I.

Moreover,

\displaystyle C_{1}UC_{1}

\displaystyle=(WC)U(WC)

\displaystyle=(WC)UCC(WC)

\displaystyle=W(CUC)(CWC)

\displaystyle=W(CUC)W^{*}

\displaystyle=WU^{*}W^{*}

\displaystyle=V,

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

The adjoint identity from (
2.8
) gives us an alternate way to prove Proposition
2.1
(
U^{*}
always belongs to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
). Indeed, a result from
[

]
says that any unitary operator

can be written as

\displaystyle C_{1}UC_{1}

\displaystyle=C_{1}(C_{1}C_{2})C_{1}

\displaystyle=C_{2}C_{1}

\displaystyle=(C_{1}C_{2})^{*}

Thus,

.

2.3
Matrix representations and diagonalization

Assuming that our ambient Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is separable, it has an orthonormal basis
\mathcal{E}=({\bf u}_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
. What does the matrix representation of the unitary operator
U
with respect to
\mathcal{E}
tell us about the corresponding matrix representation of the members of
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
? For any bounded operator
A
on
\mathcal{H}
, let

[A]_{\mathcal{E}}:=[\langle A{\bf u}_{j},{\bf u}_{i}\rangle]_{i,j\geqslant 1}

denote the matrix representation of
A
with respect to
\mathcal{E}
. Also, for a finite (or infinite) matrix
M
of complex numbers, let
\overline{M}
denote the matrix
M
with each entry conjugated.

Proposition 2.10
.

A unitary operator
V
on
\mathcal{H}
belongs to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
if and only if there is an orthonormal basis
\mathcal{E}
for
\mathcal{H}
such that
[V]_{\mathcal{E}}=\overline{[U]_{\mathcal{E}}}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
V=CUC
for some conjugation
C
. A general fact about conjugations from
[
15
, Lemma 1]
says there exists a
C
-real
orthonormal basis
\mathcal{E}=({\bf u}_{j})_{j\geqslant 1}
for
\mathcal{H}
in that
C{\bf u}_{j}={\bf u}_{j}
for all
j\geqslant 1
. With this choice of
C
-real orthonormal basis
\mathcal{E}
, we have

\displaystyle([V]_{\mathcal{E}})_{k,j}

\displaystyle=\langle V{\bf u}_{j},{\bf u}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle CUC{\bf u}_{j},{\bf u}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle CU{\bf u}_{j},{\bf u}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle C{\bf u}_{k},U{\bf u}_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\bf u}_{k},U{\bf u}_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\overline{\langle U{\bf u}_{j},{\bf u}_{k}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\overline{([U]_{\mathcal{E}})_{k,j}}.

Conversely, suppose that
[V]_{\mathcal{E}}=\overline{[U]_{\mathcal{E}}}
for some orthonormal basis
\mathcal{E}=({\bf u}_{j})_{j\geqslant 1}
, in other words,
\langle V{\bf u}_{j},{\bf u}_{k}\rangle=\langle{\bf u}_{k},U{\bf u}_{j}\rangle
for all
j,k\geqslant 1
. Define a conjugation
C
on
\mathcal{H}
by first defining it on the orthonormal basis elements by
C{\bf u}_{j}={\bf u}_{j}
and then extending it antilinearly to all of
\mathcal{H}
. By this we mean

C\Big(\sum_{j\geqslant 1}a_{j}{\bf u}_{j}\Big):=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\bar{a}_{j}{\bf u}_{j}.

(2.11)

We are essentially defining a conjugation
C
so that
({\bf u}_{j})_{j\geqslant 1}
is a
C
-real basis. Then, reversing the steps of the calculation in the previous part of the argument shows that
\langle CUC{\bf u}_{j},{\bf u}_{k}\rangle=\langle V{\bf u}_{j},{\bf u}_{k}\rangle
for all
j,k\geqslant 1
and thus
V=CUC
.
∎

When a unitary operator
U
is representable by a diagonal matrix, in other words,
U
has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, we can obtain a specific and rich class of members of its conjugate orbit. Below we use the standard tensor notation
{\bf a}\otimes{\bf b}
, where
{\bf a},{\bf b}
are vectors from a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, to denote the rank one operator on
\mathcal{H}
defined by

({\bf a}\otimes{\bf b}){\bf x}=\langle{\bf x},{\bf b}\rangle{\bf a},\quad{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}.

Observe that
({\bf a}\otimes{\bf b})^{*}={\bf b}\otimes{\bf a}
and if
{\bf a}
and
{\bf b}
are unit vectors, then
({\bf a}\otimes{\bf b})^{*}({\bf a}\otimes{\bf b})
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathbb{C}{\bf b}
while

({\bf a}\otimes{\bf b})({\bf a}\otimes{\bf b})^{*}
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathbb{C}{\bf a}
. This says that if
({\bf u}_{j})_{j\geqslant 1}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}
and
(\xi_{j})_{j\geqslant 1}
are unimodular constants, then

\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\xi_{j}({\bf u}_{j}\otimes{\bf u}_{j})

defines a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
.

Corollary 2.12
.

Suppose that
({\bf u}_{j})_{j\geqslant 1}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}
,
(\xi_{j})_{j\geqslant 1}
is a sequence of unimodular constants, and
U
is the unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
defined by

U:=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\xi_{j}({\bf u}_{j}\otimes{\bf u}_{j}).

For any bijection
\sigma:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
with
\sigma\circ\sigma=\operatorname{id}
, the unitary operator
U_{\sigma}
on
\mathcal{H}
defined by

U_{\sigma}:=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\bar{\xi}_{\sigma(j)}({\bf u}_{j}\otimes{\bf u}_{j})

belongs to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
.

Proof.

Define

C_{\sigma}{\bf u}_{j}={\bf u}_{\sigma(j)}\;\mbox{for all $j\geqslant 1$}

(2.13)

and extend
C_{\sigma}
as in (
2.11
) to be an antilinear isometry on
\mathcal{H}
. Since
\sigma\circ\sigma=\operatorname{id}
, we see that
C_{\sigma}^{2}=I
and thus
C_{\sigma}
is a conjugation. Now observe that for each
j\geqslant 1
we have

\displaystyle C_{\sigma}UC_{\sigma}{\bf u}_{j}

\displaystyle=C_{\sigma}U{\bf u}_{\sigma(j)}

\displaystyle=C_{\sigma}(\xi_{\sigma(j)}{\bf u}_{\sigma(j)})

\displaystyle=\bar{\xi}_{\sigma(j)}{\bf u}_{j}

\displaystyle=U_{\sigma}{\bf u}_{j},

which shows that
U_{\sigma}\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
.
∎

In the previous corollary, notice that when
\sigma=\operatorname{id}
, then
U_{\sigma}=U^{*}
belongs to the conjugate orbit of
U
, which confirms Proposition
2.1
. One can formulate a finite dimensional version of the previous proposition except that
\sigma
will be an order two bijection of
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. We record this with the following example.

Example 2.14
.

If

U=\operatorname{diag}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})
is a diagonal unitary matrix and
\sigma\in S_{n}
(the symmetric group on
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
) with order two, then the unitary matrix

U_{\sigma}:=\operatorname{diag}(\bar{\xi}_{\sigma(1)},\bar{\xi}_{\sigma(2)},\ldots,\bar{\xi}_{\sigma(n)})

belongs to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
. We will revisit this example below in Corollary
3.16
.
Though one might be initially tempted to believe that every member of the conjugate orbit of a diagonal matrix will also be diagonal, we will show in Example
3.9
that this is rarely the case.
We will explore a generalization as to which diagonal matrices belong to the conjugate orbit in Theorem
3.13
.

Example 2.15
.

Let

(\mathcal{F}f)(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)e^{-ixt}dt,

(2.16)

denote the classical Fourier–Plancherel transform on

(defined initially on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by the above integral and extended to be a unitary operator on

It is known that
\mathcal{F}
has the spectral properties
\sigma(\mathcal{F})=\sigma_{p}(\mathcal{F})=\{1,-1,i,-i\}
and

\mathcal{F}H_{n}=(-i)^{n}H_{n},

(2.17)

where

is the
n
th Hermite function, i.e.,

H_{n}(x)=c_{n}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}h_{n}(x),\;\;n\geqslant 0,

c_{n}
is a normalizing constant so that
\|H_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=1
, and
h_{n}
is the
n
-th Hermite polynomial.
Moreover,
(H_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
forms an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

[
14
, Ch.11]
.
Thus, by our previous discussion, we can write

\mathcal{F}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-i)^{n}(H_{n}\otimes H_{n}).

(2.18)

Corollary
2.12
applied to any bijection
\sigma:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
\sigma\circ\sigma=\operatorname{id}
says that the unitary operator
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
on

defined by

\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}i^{\sigma(n)}(H_{n}\otimes H_{n})

belongs to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(\mathcal{F})
. Observe that if
\sigma=\operatorname{id}
, then
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}=\mathcal{F}^{*}
belongs to the conjugate orbit of
\mathcal{F}
, which is expected by Proposition
2.1
. If
\sigma:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
is defined by
\sigma(2n)=2n
,
\sigma(1)=3,\sigma(3)=1,\sigma(5)=7,\sigma(7)=5
and so on, we see that
i^{\sigma(n)}=(-i)^{n}
and thus
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}=\mathcal{F}
belongs to the conjugate orbit of
\mathcal{F}
. Note that the Fourier–Plancherel transform is unitarily equivalent to its adjoint. See the paragraph following Proposition
2.3
.

Example 2.19
.

Let

(\mathscr{H}g)(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\operatorname{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{g(t)}{x-t}dt,

(2.20)

denote the Hilbert transform on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. It is known that
\mathscr{H}
is unitary on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
(in fact
\mathscr{H}^{*}=-\mathscr{H}
).
Moreover,
\mathscr{H}
is unitarily equivalent to its adjoint via the intertwining unitary operator on

defined by
f(x)\mapsto f(-x)
and
\mathscr{H}
has the spectral properties
\sigma(\mathscr{H})=\sigma_{p}(\mathscr{H})=\{i,-i\}
with

\mathscr{H}f_{n}=if_{n},\;\mbox{where}\;f_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{(x+i)^{-n-1}}{(x-i)^{-n}},\quad n\leqslant-1,

and

\mathscr{H}f_{n}=-if_{n},\;\mbox{where}\;f_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{(x-i)^{n}}{(x+i)^{n+1}},\quad n\geqslant 0.

It is known
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
forms an orthonormal basis for

.
See
[
14
, Ch. 12]
for the above Hilbert transform details. Thus,

\mathscr{H}=\sum_{n<0}i(f_{n}\otimes f_{n})+\sum_{n\geqslant 0}(-i)(f_{n}\otimes f_{n}).

(2.21)

In other words, with respect to the ordered orthonormal basis

,
\mathscr{H}
is represented by the doubly infinite diagonal matrix

\operatorname{diag}(\ldots,i,i,i,\boxed{-i},-i,-i,\ldots),

where the boxed term above corresponds to the

th
position.
For any bijection
\sigma:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
for which
\sigma\circ\sigma=\operatorname{id}
, the unitary operator
\mathscr{H}_{\sigma}
on

defined by the permutation

of the entries of

\operatorname{diag}(\ldots,-i,-i,-i,\boxed{i},i,i,\ldots)

belongs to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(\mathscr{H})
. Certainly the identity permutation will yield
\mathscr{H}^{*}\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(\mathscr{H})
(as to be expected from Proposition
2.3
). The permutation
\sigma
on
\mathbb{Z}
defined by

, will yield

. See the discussion following Proposition
2.3
.

2.4
Alternate representations of members of the orbit

To get an idea of the “size” of
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
, it is useful to discuss when

C_{1}UC_{1}=C_{2}UC_{2}.

In other words, when do two conjugations
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
yield the same member of
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
? For a unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
, recall that the
commutant
of
U
, denoted by
\{U\}^{\prime}
, is the collection of all bounded linear operators
A
on
\mathcal{H}
for which
AU=UA
(see
[
11
]
for general facts about the commutant).

Proposition 2.22
.

For a fixed unitary operator
U
and conjugations
C_{1},C_{2}
on
\mathcal{H}
, the following are equivalent.

(a)

C_{1}UC_{1}=C_{2}UC_{2}
.

(b)

C_{1}C_{2}
is a unitary operator in the commutant of
U
.

Proof.

If (a) holds then, manipulating the identity
C_{1}UC_{1}=C_{2}UC_{2}
, gives us

(C_{1}C_{2})U=U(C_{1}C_{2}).
Since
C_{1}C_{2}
is unitary, we see that (b) holds. Conversely, suppose (b) holds in that
C_{1}C_{2}
commutes with
U
. A similar working of the terms in
(C_{1}C_{2})U=U(C_{1}C_{2})
yields (a).
∎

Remark 2.23
.

The previous proposition gives us a way of understanding the richness of the conjugate orbit of a particular unitary operator. For example, if
U
is a unitary operator on the sequence space
\ell^{2}=\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
then, with respect to the standard orthonormal basis
({\bf e}_{j})_{j\geqslant 1}
, every conjugation on
\ell^{2}
can be written as
C=VJ
, where
V
is an infinite unitary matrix with
V^{t}=V
and
J
is the standard conjugation on
\ell^{2}
defined by
J{\bf x}=\overline{{\bf x}}
(see below). Here
V
is an infinite unitary matrix that defines an operator on
\ell^{2}
(viewed as column vectors) by
{\bf x}\mapsto V{\bf x}
and
V^{t}
denotes its transpose. With
C_{1}=V_{1}J
and
C_{2}=V_{2}J
we have

C_{1}C_{2}=V_{1}JV_{2}J=V_{1}\overline{V_{2}}=V_{1}V_{2}^{*}.
Here, recall that
\overline{V}
is the matrix
V
with all of its elements conjugated (and thus
JVJ=\overline{V}
) and
V^{*}
denotes the conjugate transpose of
V
.
Thus,
C_{1}UC_{1}=C_{2}UC_{2}
if and only if
V_{1}V_{2}^{*}
is a unitary operator in the commutant of
U
.

.

Keep the notation as in Corollary

As in Corollary

Chapter 3
Unitary Matrices

This chapter focuses on the conjugate orbit of a unitary matrix. Let
\mathscr{U}_{n}
denote the set of all
n\times n
complex unitary matrices
U
(
U^{*}U=UU^{*}=I
) which we view as unitary operators on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
(as columns) in the usual way by
{\bf x}\mapsto U{\bf x}
,
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

3.1
Unitary matrices and conjugations

Let
J
denote the conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
given by
J{\bf x}=\overline{{\bf x}}
, that is,

J\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{x}_{1}\\
\bar{x}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\bar{x}_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

For
U\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
one can check that

JUJ=\overline{U},

(3.1)

where, as a reminder,
\overline{U}
is the matrix consisting of the complex conjugates of the entries of
U
.
A result of Garcia and Tener
[
17
, Lemma 3.2]
says that
any
conjugation
C
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
can be written as
C=VJ
, where
V\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
for which
V=V^{t}
(the transpose of
V
).
Note that since
V
is unitary we have

V^{t}=V\iff V\overline{V}=I\iff\overline{V}V=I.

(3.2)

For completeness, we include its short proof.

Proposition 3.3
.

C
is a conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
if and only if
C=VJ
, where
V\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
with
V^{t}=V
and
J
is the conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
defined by
J{\bf x}=\overline{{\bf x}}
.

Proof.

If
C
is a conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
then
V=CJ
is a unitary operator on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
which we think of as an element of
\mathscr{U}_{n}
. Moreover,

V\overline{V}=VJVJ=C^{2}=I.

From (
3.2
) it follows that
V=V^{t}
. Conversely, suppose that
V\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
and
V^{t}=V
, equivalently, again by (
3.2
),
V\overline{V}=I
. Then
C=VJ
is antilinear and isometric on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Moreover,

C^{2}=VJVJ=V\overline{V}=I

and so
C
is a conjugation.
∎

A large class of such self transpose unitary matrices
V
can be created by using the Householder matrices
V=I-2{\bf v}\otimes{\bf v}
, where
{\bf v}
is any unit vector in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
(or diagonal blocks of such matrices).
Proposition
3.3
extends to any (singly or doubly) infinite unitary matrix
U
acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
or
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
(see Proposition
4.2
below with the same proof as the previous proposition).

Proposition 3.4
.

For fixed
U\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
we have

\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)=\{V\overline{U}V^{*}:\mbox{$V\in\mathscr{U}_{n}$ and $V^{t}=V$}\}.

Proof.

Using Proposition
3.3
, observe that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)

\displaystyle=\{CUC:\mbox{$C$ is a conjugation}\}

\displaystyle=\{(VJ)U(VJ):\mbox{$V\in\mathscr{U}_{n}$ and $V^{t}=V$}\}

\displaystyle=\{(VJ)UJJ(VJ):\mbox{$V\in\mathscr{U}_{n}$ and $V^{t}=V$}\}

\displaystyle=\{V(JUJ)(JVJ):\mbox{$V\in\mathscr{U}_{n}$ and $V^{t}=V$}\}

\displaystyle=\{V\overline{U}\,\overline{V}:\mbox{$V\in\mathscr{U}_{n}$ and $V^{t}=V$}\}

\displaystyle=\{V\overline{U}V^{*}:\mbox{$V\in\mathscr{U}_{n}$ and $V^{t}=V$}\}.

In the above calculations, note the use of (
3.1
) and (
3.2
).
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

\overline{U}\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)

for any
U\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
.

Also observe the following corollary of Proposition
3.4
which determines when two elements of the conjugate orbit are the same.

Corollary 3.6
.

If
V_{1},V_{2},U\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
with
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are self transpose, then
V_{1}\overline{U}V_{1}^{*}=V_{2}\overline{U}V_{2}^{*}
if and only if
V_{2}\overline{V_{1}}
belongs to the commutant of
U
.

A version of Proposition
2.10
for unitary matrices is the following.

Proposition 3.7
.

Suppose that
U\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
. Then
W\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
if and only if there is an orthonormal basis
\mathcal{E}
for
\mathbb{C}^{n}
so that
[W]_{\mathcal{E}}=\overline{[U]_{\mathcal{E}}}
.

From the spectral theorem for unitary matrices (representing them as diagonal matrices with respect to an orthonormal basis), we have the following corollary.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
U\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
with the ordered orthonormal basis of eigenvectors

\mathcal{E}=({\bf u}_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
and

U{\bf u}_{j}=\xi_{j}{\bf u}_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
. Then the unitary matrix
W
such that

[W]_{\mathcal{E}}=\operatorname{diag}(\bar{\xi}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\xi}_{n}),

in other words
W{\bf u}_{j}=\bar{\xi}_{j}{\bf u}_{j}
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
, belongs to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
.

It is important to point out that
\operatorname{diag}(\bar{\xi}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\xi}_{n})
represents
U^{*}
with respect to the orthonormal basis
\mathcal{E}
for
\mathbb{C}^{n}
but may not represent
U^{*}
with respect to the standard orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{C}^{n}
(see Theorem
3.13
and Example
3.15
).

3.2
A simple example

Even simple
2\times 2
diagonal unitary matrices tell us a lot about the conjugate orbit and provide nice examples of the results we have so far.

Example 3.9
.

Let
U
be the diagonal unitary matrix

U=\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{1}&0\\
0&\xi_{2}\end{bmatrix},

where
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{T}
and distinct. From Proposition
3.4
we know that

\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)=\{V\overline{U}V^{*}:\mbox{$V\in\mathscr{U}_{2}$ and $V^{t}=V$}\}.

A standard description of all
2\times 2
unitary matrices
V
is

V=e^{i\frac{\varphi}{2}}\begin{bmatrix}e^{i\alpha}\cos\theta&e^{i\beta}\sin\theta\\
-e^{-i\beta}\sin\theta&e^{-i\alpha}\cos\theta\end{bmatrix},

where
\varphi,\alpha,\beta,\theta\in\mathbb{R}
. One can check, we omit the calculation, that by imposing the condition that
V^{t}=V
, the matrix
V
takes the form

V=e^{i\frac{\varphi}{2}}\begin{bmatrix}e^{i\alpha}\cos\theta&i(-1)^{n}\sin\theta\\
i(-1)^{n}\sin\theta&e^{-i\alpha}\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}.

(3.10)

When
\theta=n\pi
, the matrix
V
becomes

V=e^{i\frac{\varphi}{2}}\begin{bmatrix}(-1)^{n}e^{i\alpha}&0\\
0&(-1)^{n}e^{-i\alpha}\end{bmatrix}

(3.11)

and thus

V\overline{U}V^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\xi}_{1}&0\\
0&\bar{\xi}_{2}\end{bmatrix},

(3.12)

which is
U^{*}
(as to be expected by Corollary
3.5
).
Also observe that the above is true for any choice of
\alpha
,
n
, and
\varphi
(as long as
\theta=n\pi
). So there are many choices of
V_{\alpha,n,\varphi}
from (
3.11
) that yield the same member of the orbit of
U
. This should come as no surprise since
V_{\alpha,n,\varphi}\overline{V_{\alpha^{\prime},n^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime}}}
is a diagonal matrix which clearly commutes with the diagonal matrix
U
. Note the use of Corollary
3.6
here.

When
\theta=\frac{1}{2}\pi(2n+1)
, the matrix
V
from (
3.10
) becomes

V=e^{i\frac{\varphi}{2}}\begin{bmatrix}0&i\\
i&0\end{bmatrix}

and so

V\overline{U}V^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\xi}_{2}&0\\
0&\bar{\xi}_{1}\end{bmatrix}.

Notice the diagonal entries are reversed from those in (
3.12
).
Again, many choices of
V
(for various
\varphi
) yield the same member of the orbit (see Corollary
3.6
).

In general,
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
consists of all the matrices
V\overline{U}V^{*}
, where
V
is of the form (
3.10
), and these turn out to be

\begin{bmatrix}e^{i\alpha}\cos\theta&i(-1)^{n}\sin\theta\\
i(-1)^{n}\sin\theta&e^{-i\alpha}\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\xi}_{1}&0\\
0&\bar{\xi}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}e^{-i\alpha}\cos\theta&i(-1)^{n+1}\sin\theta\\
i(-1)^{n+1}\sin\theta&e^{i\alpha}\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}.

As a specific example, when
\xi_{1}=1
and
\xi_{2}=-1
we obtain the family of matrices

\begin{bmatrix}\cos(2\theta)&-ie^{i\alpha}(-1)^{n}\sin(2\theta)\\
ie^{-i\alpha}(-1)^{n}\sin(2\theta)&-\cos(2\theta)\end{bmatrix},\quad\theta,\alpha\in\mathbb{R},n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

as the orbit of

U=\begin{bmatrix}1&\phantom{-}0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}.

Here it is important to observe that even though the diagonal matrices
U
(setting
\theta=0
) and

\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\
\phantom{-}0&1\end{bmatrix}

(setting
\theta=\pi/2
) belong to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
, there are plenty of
nondiagonal
matrices that belong to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
, even though
U
is diagonal.

What about the question as to when different

V_{\alpha,\theta,n}:=\begin{bmatrix}e^{i\alpha}\cos\theta&i(-1)^{n}\sin\theta\\
i(-1)^{n}\sin\theta&e^{-i\alpha}\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}

yield the same member of the orbit of a diagonal
U
with distinct diagonal entries. Recall that the commutant of a diagonal matrix with distinct entries consists of all the diagonal matrices. Here we are looking for instances as to when

V_{\alpha,\theta,n}V_{\beta,\varphi,m}^{*}
is a diagonal matrix, with of course unimodular entries. One can calculate

V_{\alpha,\theta,n}V_{\beta,\varphi,m}^{*}
to be

{\tiny\begin{bmatrix}\sin\theta(-1)^{m+n}\sin\varphi+e^{i(\alpha-\beta)}\cos\theta\cos\varphi&i\left(e^{i\beta}(-1)^{n}\sin\theta\cos\varphi-e^{i\alpha}(-1)^{m}\cos\theta\sin\varphi\right)\\
i\left(e^{-i\beta}(-1)^{n}\sin\theta\cos\varphi-e^{-i\alpha}(-1)^{m}\cos\theta\sin\varphi\right)&\sin\theta(-1)^{m+n}\sin\varphi+e^{-i(\alpha-\beta)}\cos\theta\cos\varphi\end{bmatrix}.}

This will be a diagonal matrix when the parameters satisfy the condition

e^{i\beta}(-1)^{n}\sin\theta\cos\varphi-e^{i\alpha}(-1)^{m}\cos\theta\sin\varphi=0

which can indeed happen for certain well chosen values of
\alpha,\beta,\theta,\varphi,n,m
.

3.3
Diagonal unitary matrices

One needs to be careful in the above discussion since, for a fixed
U\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
, the
unitary orbit

\mathfrak{O}(U)
, the set of all

VUV^{*},
where
V\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
(which as mentioned earlier turns out to be the set of all matrices in
\mathscr{U}_{n}
which have the same eigenvalues with the same corresponding multiplicities as
U
), can be much different than the
conjugate orbit
of
U
which consists of the collection of unitary matrices
V\overline{U}V^{*}
, where
V\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
and
V=V^{t}
. Certainly, by Proposition
2.1
and Proposition
2.5
, we know that any member of
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
must by unitary equivalent to
U^{*}
. However, even a diagonal matrix consisting of the eigenvalues of
U^{*}
(with corresponding multiplicities) need not belong to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
. Below is a theorem that sorts this out, followed by two cautionary examples.

Theorem 3.13
.

Suppose that
U\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
with eigenvalues
(\xi_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
, repeated according to multiplicity. Then
\operatorname{diag}(\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2},\ldots,\bar{\xi}_{n})\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
if and only if there is an ordered orthonormal basis
({\bf u}_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
for
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with
U{\bf u}_{j}=\xi_{j}{\bf u}_{j}
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
and such that the unitary matrix
V=[{\bf u}_{1}|{\bf u}_{2}|\cdots|{\bf u}_{n}]

satisfies
V^{t}=V
.

Proof.

Suppose that
({\bf u}_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
is an orthonormal eigenbasis for
U
in that
U{\bf u}_{j}=\xi_{j}{\bf u}_{j}
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
and that this basis satisfies the condition that
V=[{\bf u}_{1}|{\bf u}_{2}|\cdots|{\bf u}_{n}]
is self transpose unitary matrix. Then, as seen earlier, by a result of Garcia and Tenner
[
17
, Lemma 3.2]
(see Proposition
3.3
),
C=VJ
, more precisely,
C{\bf x}=V\overline{{\bf x}}
, defines a conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle CUC

\displaystyle=(VJ)U(VJ)

\displaystyle=(VJ)UJJ(VJ)

\displaystyle=V(JUJ)(JVJ)

\displaystyle=V\overline{U}\,\overline{V}

\displaystyle=V\overline{U}[\overline{{\bf u}_{1}}|\overline{{\bf u}_{2}}|\cdots|\overline{{\bf u}_{n}}]

\displaystyle=V[\overline{U}\overline{{\bf u}_{1}}|\overline{U}\overline{{\bf u}_{2}}|\cdots|\overline{U}\overline{{\bf u}_{n}}]

\displaystyle=V[\bar{\xi}_{1}\overline{{\bf u}_{1}}|\bar{\xi}_{2}\overline{{\bf u}_{2}}|\cdots|\bar{\xi}_{n}\overline{{\bf u}_{n}}]

\displaystyle=V[\overline{{\bf u}_{1}}|\overline{{\bf u}_{2}}|\cdots|\overline{{\bf u}_{n}}]\operatorname{diag}(\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2},\ldots,\bar{\xi}_{n})

\displaystyle=V\overline{V}\operatorname{diag}(\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2},\ldots,\bar{\xi}_{n})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}(\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2},\ldots,\bar{\xi}_{n}),

where, in the last step of the above calculation, we use the fact that
V
is unitary and self transpose along with (
3.2
). Thus,
\operatorname{diag}(\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2},\ldots,\bar{\xi}_{n})\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
.

Conversely, suppose that
\operatorname{diag}(\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2},\ldots,\bar{\xi}_{n})\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
. Then there is a conjugation
C
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
CUC=\operatorname{diag}(\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2},\ldots,\bar{\xi}_{n})
. As discussed before, there is a unitary matrix
V
with
V^{t}=V
such that
C=VJ
, i.e.,
C{\bf x}=V\overline{{\bf x}}
. By the calculation above we have

\operatorname{diag}(\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2},\ldots,\bar{\xi}_{n})=CUC=V\overline{U}\,\overline{V}.

Taking complex conjugates of both sides of the previous identity gives us

\overline{V}UV=\operatorname{diag}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n}),

and, again by noting that
V\overline{V}=I
, we see that

UV=V\operatorname{diag}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n}).

(3.14)

Multiplying each side of the above on the right by each of the standard basis elements
{\bf e}_{j}
,
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
, for
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we get

U(V{\bf e}_{j})=\xi_{j}(V{\bf e}_{j}),\quad 1\leqslant j\leqslant n.

This says that
(V{\bf e}_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
is an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors for
U
and, by assumption, the matrix

V=[V{\bf e}_{1}|V{\bf e}_{2}|\cdots|V\vec{e}_{n}]

is self transpose.
∎

One must be careful with the previous theorem in that not every diagonal matrix consisting of the eigenvalues of
U^{*}
(with corresponding multiplicities) belongs to the conjugate orbit of
U
, just those which can be realized by eigenvectors whose columns from a self transpose matrix. This point is made more salient with the following example.

Example 3.15
.

For
distinct

\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3}\in\mathbb{T}
, let
U
be the
3\times 3
diagonal unitary matrix defined by

U=\operatorname{diag}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3}).

Suppose there exists a conjugation
C
on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
such that
CUC=\operatorname{diag}(\bar{\xi}_{3},\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2})
. Observe the reordering and the complex conjugation of the diagonal elements of
U
. Following the steps of the proof of the previous theorem, see (
3.14
), there is a self transpose
3\times 3
unitary matrix
V
such that

UV=V\!\operatorname{diag}(\xi_{3},\xi_{1},\xi_{2}).

Multiplying the vector
{\bf e}_{1}
to the right hand side of each matrix from the previous line to see that

U(V{\bf e}_{1})=\xi_{3}(V{\bf e}_{1}).

Thus
V{\bf e}_{1}\in\ker(U-\xi_{3}I)\setminus\{{\bf 0}\}
and so
V{\bf e}_{1}=a{\bf e}_{3}
for some
a
with
|a|=1
. In a similar way,
V{\bf e}_{2}=b{\bf e}_{1}
and
V{\bf e}_{3}=c{\bf e}_{2}
for
|b|=|c|=1
. It follows that

V=[V{\bf e}_{1}|V{\bf e}_{2}|V{\bf e}_{3}]=\begin{bmatrix}0&b&0\\
0&0&c\\
a&0&0\end{bmatrix},

which is not symmetric. From here we conclude that
\operatorname{diag}(\bar{\xi}_{3},\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2})\not\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
, even though its diagonal entries consist of the eigenvalues of
U^{*}
. We leave it to the reader to use Corollary
2.12
to see that
\operatorname{diag}(\bar{\xi}_{2},\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{3})
does belong to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
.

The previous example suggests that when the eigenvalues are
distinct
, the permutation of the conjugates of the eigenvalues of the diagonal matrix
U
must be of order two. We formalize this with the following corollary.

Corollary 3.16
.

If
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n}
are distinct unimodular constants,

U=\operatorname{diag}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n}),

and
\sigma
is a permutation of
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, then
the following are equivalent.

(a)

\operatorname{diag}(\bar{\xi}_{\sigma(1)},\bar{\xi}_{\sigma(2)},\ldots,\bar{\xi}_{\sigma(n)})\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
;

(b)

\sigma\circ\sigma=\operatorname{id}
.

Proof.

(b)\Longrightarrow(a)
follows from Example
2.14
. For the proof of
(a)\Longrightarrow(b)
, note that the identity
U{\bf e}_{j}=\xi_{j}{\bf e}_{j}
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
and Theorem
3.13
says there is a there is a self-transpose unitarty matrix
V
such that

V=[V{\bf e}_{\sigma(1)}|V{\bf e}_{\sigma(2)}|\cdots|V{\bf e}_{\sigma(n)}].

From from (
3.14
) it follows that

UV=V\operatorname{diag}(\xi_{\sigma(1)},\xi_{\sigma(2)},\ldots,\xi_{\sigma(n)}).

Applying the analysis in the discussion of Example
3.15
, we see that

U(V{\bf e}_{j})=\xi_{\sigma(j)}(V{\bf e}_{j}),\quad 1\leqslant j\leqslant n,

and so
V{\bf e}_{j}=a_{j}{\bf e}_{\sigma(j)}
for some
a_{j}\in\mathbb{T}
. This says that the matrix
V
has an
a_{j}
in the
(j,\sigma(j))
position. However,
V
is self-transpose and so this same
a_{j}
must appear in the
(\sigma(j),j)
position, and thus

V{\bf e}_{j}=a_{j}{\bf e}_{\sigma(j)}\;\mbox{and}\;V{\bf e}_{\sigma(j)}=a_{j}{\bf e}_{j}.

Since the eigenvalues
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}
are distinct, we conclude that

\sigma^{2}=\operatorname{id}

∎

This next example produces a unitary matrix such that
no
diagonal matrix belongs to its conjugate orbit.

Example 3.17
.

Consider the
3\times 3
matrix

U=Q\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{1}&0&0\\
0&\xi_{2}&0\\
0&0&\xi_{3}\end{bmatrix}Q^{*},

where
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3}
are
distinct
elements of
\mathbb{T}
and
Q=[{\bf q}_{1}|{\bf q}_{2}|{\bf q}_{3}]
, where

{\bf q}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1+i}{\sqrt{3}}\\
\frac{i}{\sqrt{6}}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\end{bmatrix},\;\;{\bf q}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1-i}{\sqrt{6}}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\\
\frac{i}{\sqrt{3}}\end{bmatrix},\;\;{\bf q}_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}+\frac{i}{\sqrt{3}}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}-\frac{i}{\sqrt{6}}\end{bmatrix},

which forms an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. One can see that
U
is a unitary matrix with one-dimensional eigenspaces
\mathbb{C}{\bf q}_{j}
,
j=1,2,3
, corresponding to the distinct eigenvalues
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3}
. By Theorem
3.13
, a diagonal operator (which must consist of the diagonal entries
\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2},\bar{\xi}_{3}
in some order) belongs to the conjugate orbit of
U
if and only if some permutation of the (column) vectors
z_{1}{\bf q}_{1},z_{2}{\bf q}_{2},z_{3}{\bf q}_{3}
, where
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
are unimodular constants, form the columns of a self transpose matrix. For the particular unitary matrix
U
, this will not happen. To justify this, consider
the matrix

[z_{1}{\bf q}_{1}|z_{2}{\bf q}_{2}|z_{2}{\bf q}_{3}]=\begin{bmatrix}z_{1}\frac{1+i}{\sqrt{3}}&z_{2}\frac{1-i}{\sqrt{6}}&z_{3}\cdot 0\\
z_{1}\frac{-i}{\sqrt{6}}&z_{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}&z_{3}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}+\frac{i}{\sqrt{3}})\\
z_{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}&z_{2}\frac{i}{\sqrt{3}}&z_{3}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}-\frac{i}{\sqrt{6}})\end{bmatrix}.

Focusing on the
(3,1)
and
(1,3)
entries of this matrix, we see there is no choice of unimodular constants
z_{1}
and
z_{3}
which makes these two entries equal. As another example, consider the matrix

[w_{1}{\bf q}_{3}|w_{2}{\bf q}_{2}|w_{3}{\bf q}_{1}]=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\cdot 0&w_{2}\frac{1-i}{\sqrt{6}}&w_{3}\frac{1+i}{\sqrt{3}}\\
w_{1}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}+\frac{i}{\sqrt{3}})&w_{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}&w_{3}\frac{-i}{\sqrt{6}}\\
w_{1}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}-\frac{i}{\sqrt{6}})&w_{2}\frac{i}{\sqrt{3}}&w_{3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Looking at the
(3,2)
and
(2,3)
entries of this matrix, we see there is no choice of unimodular constants
w_{2}
and
w_{3}
that makes these entries equal. Now consider all six permutations of the vectors
{\bf q}_{1},{\bf q}_{2},{\bf q}_{3}
, and check that it is always possible to find two symmetric entires
(i,j)
and
(j,i)
that can not be made equal by multiplying by unimodular constants. Thus, in this particular example,
no
diagonal matrix belongs to the conjugate orbit of
U
.

Chapter 4
The Bilateral Shift

The bilateral shift is an example of a unitary operator where we can concretely describe its conjugate orbit. We will expand this discussion in various directions later in the paper. The
bilateral shift

M
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
is initially defined by

M{\bf e}_{n}={\bf e}_{n+1},

where, for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,

{\bf e}_{n}:=(\cdots 0,0,0,1,0,0,\cdots)

(the
1
appears in the
n
th
slot)
is the two-sided standard orthonormal basis vector for the sequence space

\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}):=\Big\{{\bf a}=(a_{j})_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}:\|{\bf a}\|:=\Big(\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}|a_{j}|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty\Big\},

(4.1)

and then extended linearly to a unitary operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. In other words,

M\Big(\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{j}{\bf e}_{j}\Big)=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{j}{\bf e}_{j+1}.

Of course we have seen this operator earlier as its unitary equivalent realization as the multiplication operator
(M_{\xi}f)(\xi)=\xi f(\xi)
(multiplication by the independent variable
\xi
) on
L^{2}(m)
, where
m
is normalized Lebesgue measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
. One can see this unitary equivalence via Fourier series and identifying each vector
{\bf e}_{n}
in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
with the monomial
\xi^{n}
(
\xi=e^{i\theta}
) in
L^{2}(m)
. More precisely via the isometric isomorphism

\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{j}{\bf e}_{j}\mapsto\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{j}\xi^{j}

from
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
onto
L^{2}(m)
.

4.1
The conjugate orbit as unitary shifts

A preliminary step towards describing
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
is an extension of the result of Garcia and Tener
[
17
, Lemma 3.2]
for conjugations on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
to the infinite dimensional setting of
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. The proof is essentially the same as the proof of the analogous result for
\mathbb{C}^{n}
in Proposition
3.3
. Define the conjugation

J:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}),\quad J{\bf x}=\overline{{\bf x}}.

Proposition 4.2
.

For a map
C
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, the following are equivalent.

(a)

C
is a conjugation;

(b)

There is a doubly infinite unitary matrix with
V=[V_{ij}]_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}
with
V=V^{t}
and
C=VJ
. In other words,
C{\bf x}=V\overline{{\bf x}}
.

Definition 4.3
.

A unitary operator
W
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
is called a
unitary shift
if there is an orthonormal basis
({\bf v}_{n})_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
for
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
W{\bf v}_{n}={\bf v}_{n+1}
for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Our description of
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
is the following.

Theorem 4.4
.

For a unitary operator
W
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, the following are equivalent.

(a)

W\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
;

(b)

W
is a unitary shift with
W{\bf v}_{j}={\bf v}_{j+1}
, where
V=[\cdots|{\bf v}_{-1}|{\bf v}_{0}|{\bf v}_{1}|\cdots]
is a doubly infinite unitary matrix with
V^{t}=V
. Moreover,
W=(VJ)M(VJ)
.

Proof.

(a)\Longrightarrow(b)
: If
W\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
then, by definition of the conjugate orbit, there is a conjugation
C
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
with
CMC=W
. By Proposition
4.2
,
C=VJ
, where
V
is a doubly infinite self transpose unitary matrix. Then, with respect to the orthonormal basis
(V{\bf e}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
for
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, which are just the columns of the matrix
V
, we have

\displaystyle W(V{\bf e}_{n})

\displaystyle=CMC(V{\bf e}_{n})

\displaystyle=CMCVJ{\bf e}_{n}

\displaystyle=CMC^{2}{\bf e}_{n}

\displaystyle=CM{\bf e}_{n}

\displaystyle=C{\bf e}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=VJ{\bf e}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=V{\bf e}_{n+1}.

Thus,
W
is a unitary shift of the desired form.

(b)\Longrightarrow(a)
:
Suppose that
W
is a unitary shift with corresponding column vectors arising from a doubly infinite unitary matrix
V
with
V=V^{t}
(equivalently,
JVJ=V^{*}
), in that
W{\bf v}_{n}={\bf v}_{n+1}
with
{\bf v}_{n}=V{\bf e}_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
C=VJ
is a conjugation on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle(CMC){\bf v}_{n}

\displaystyle=(CMC)V{\bf e}_{n}

\displaystyle=CMCCJ{\bf e}_{n}

\displaystyle=CMCC{\bf e}_{n}

\displaystyle=CM{\bf e}_{n}

\displaystyle=C{\bf e}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=CJ{\bf e}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=V{\bf e}_{n+1}

\displaystyle={\bf v}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=W{\bf v}_{n}.

The above says that the two unitary operators
W
and
CMC
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
agree on the orthonormal basis
({\bf v}_{n})_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
(and hence everywhere) and so
W\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
.

To verify that
W=(VJ)M(VJ)
, observe that since
V^{t}=V
, we see that
V\overline{{\bf v}_{j}}={\bf e}_{j}
. Thus,

\displaystyle(VJ)M(VJ){\bf v}_{j}

\displaystyle=VJMV\overline{{\bf v}_{j}}

\displaystyle=VJM{\bf e}_{j}

\displaystyle=VJ{\bf e}_{j+1}

\displaystyle=V{\bf e}_{j+1}

\displaystyle={\bf v}_{j+1}

\displaystyle=W{\bf v}_{j},

which proves that
W=(VJ)M(VJ)
.
∎

The previous theorem says that each member of
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
can be viewed as shifting each column of the doubly infinite, self transpose, unitary matrix
V
to the right. Equivalently, each element of the conjugate orbit acts on the columns of some

V=[\cdots|{\bf v}_{-2}|{\bf v}_{-1}|{\bf v}_{0}|{\bf v}_{1}|\cdots]

by
{\bf v}_{j}\mapsto{\bf v}_{j+1},j\in\mathbb{Z}
, and extending linearly.

Example 4.5
.

If
V
is a doubly infinite unitary diagonal matrix with unimodular eigenvalues
(\xi_{j})_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}
, i.e.,

V=[\cdots|\xi_{-1}{\bf e}_{-1}|\xi_{0}{\bf e}_{0}|\xi_{1}{\bf e}_{1}|\cdots]
, then certainly
V^{t}=V
. Thus, the unitary shifts

W(\xi_{j}{\bf e}_{j})=\xi_{j+1}{\bf e}_{j+1},\quad j\in\mathbb{Z},

belong to the conjugate orbit of the bilateral shift.

Example 4.6
.

One might also consider doubly infinite block diagonal matrices
V
with diagonal blocks
B_{j}
, that is,

V=\operatorname{diag}(\ldots,B_{-2},B_{-1},B_{0},B_{1},B_{2},\ldots),

where each
B_{k}\in\mathscr{U}_{n_{k}}
,
n_{k}\in\{1,2,\ldots\}
, and
B_{k}^{t}=B_{k}
. Observe that
V
is unitary and
V^{t}=V
. Thus the unitary operator

W{\bf v}_{j}={\bf v}_{j+1},\quad i\in\mathbb{Z},

where
{\bf v}_{j}
is the
j
th
column of
V
, belongs to the conjugate orbit of the bilateral shift.
As a specific example, take the above blocks
B
to be Householder matrices
B=I-2({\bf v}\otimes{\bf v})
, where
{\bf v}
is a unit vector in some
\mathbb{C}^{n_{k}}
. These
B
are known to be unitary and self transpose. Of course, the
B
appearing as the blocks are not necessarily the same.

Example 4.7
.

From Proposition
2.1
and Proposition
2.3
(and the discussion following), both
M
and
M^{*}
belong to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
. How are these two operators realized as unitary shifts
{\bf v}_{j}\mapsto{\bf v}_{j+1}
, where

V=[\cdots|{\bf v}_{-2}|{\bf v}_{-1}|{\bf v}_{0}|{\bf v}_{1}|\cdots]

is a self transpose unitary matrix? Let
V
be the doubly infinite matrix whose
j
th
column is
{\bf e}_{-j}
, in other words, the doubly infinite Hankel matrix with
1
along the cross diagonal passing through the
(0,0)
position (and zeros elsewhere), and
C
is the conjugation on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
defined by
C{\bf e}_{n}={\bf e}_{-n}
(and extended antilinearly). Then

\displaystyle(CMC){\bf v}_{j}

\displaystyle=CMC(V{\bf e}_{j})

\displaystyle=CMC{\bf e}_{-j}

\displaystyle=CM{\bf e}_{j}

\displaystyle=C{\bf e}_{j+1}

\displaystyle={\bf e}_{-j-1}

\displaystyle=V{\bf e}_{j+1}

\displaystyle={\bf v}_{j+1}.

Now observe that

\displaystyle CMC{\bf e}_{j}

\displaystyle=CM{\bf e}_{-j}

\displaystyle=C{\bf e}_{-j+1}

\displaystyle={\bf e}_{j-1}

\displaystyle=M^{*}{\bf e}_{j}.

In other words,
M^{*}
can be realized as
{\bf v}_{j}\mapsto{\bf v}_{j+1}
and thus belongs to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
(which of course we know already). In a similar way, if
C
is the conjugation on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
defined by
C{\bf e}_{j}={\bf e}_{j}
(and extended antilinearly) and
V
is the doubly infinite identity matrix (which is obviously self transpose), one can check that
(CMC){\bf v}_{j}={\bf v}_{j+1}=M{\bf e}_{j}
and
CMC{\bf e}_{j}={\bf e}_{j+1}
. In other words,
M
can be realized as
{\bf v}_{j}\mapsto{\bf v}_{j+1}
and thus belongs to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
(which, again, we already know).

What about the issue as to when two members of
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
are equal? Suppose

(V_{1}J)M(V_{1}J)=(V_{2}J)M(V_{2}J).

By Proposition
2.22
and Remark
2.23
, this happens precisely when
V_{1}JV_{2}J=V_{1}\overline{V_{2}}
belongs to the commutant of
M
. The commutant of
M
(in its matrix form) consists of all doubly infinite Toeplitz matrices (matrices which are constant along each of their diagonals)
[
14
, p. 358]
. The fact that
V_{1}\overline{V_{2}}
is unitary, implies that
V_{1}\overline{V_{2}}
is a doubly infinite Toeplitz matrix
[\widehat{\varphi}(i-j)]_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}
where
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(m)
is a unimodular. In fact, this Toeplitz matrix is the matrix representation
[M_{\varphi}]
of the multiplication operator
M_{\varphi}f=\varphi f
on
L^{2}(m)
with respect to the orthonormal basis
(\xi^{n})_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
. In other words,

[M_{\varphi}]=[\langle\xi^{i}\varphi,\xi^{j}\rangle]_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}=[\widehat{\varphi}(i-j)]_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}},

where

\widehat{\varphi}(k):=\langle\varphi,\xi^{k}\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(\xi)\bar{\xi}^{n}dm(\xi)

is the
k
th
Fourier coefficient of
\varphi
.
Putting this all together, we have the following.

Corollary 4.8
.

Suppose that
V_{1},V_{2}
are two doubly infinite self transpose unitary matrices. Then the unitary shift operators on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
defined by

V_{1}{\bf e}_{j}\mapsto V_{1}{\bf e}_{j+1}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;V_{2}{\bf e}_{j}\mapsto V_{2}{\bf e}_{j+1},\quad j\in\mathbb{Z},

(4.9)

which are generic members of the orbit of
M
, are equal if and only if

V_{1}=V_{2}[\widehat{\varphi}(i-j)]_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}

for some unimodular function
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(m)
.

Remark 4.10
.

One might be somewhat suspicious as to whether the condition that
V_{1}=V_{2}[\widehat{\varphi}(i-j)]_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}
for some unimodular function
\varphi
on
\mathbb{T}
might only hold in a trivial way. To show there is some depth here, let us denote the doubly infinite Toeplitz matrix by
T=[\widehat{\varphi}(i-j)]_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}
(which will be unitary since we are assuming that
\varphi
is unimodular). The conditions
V_{1}=V_{2}T
, along with
V_{1}^{t}=V_{1}
and
V_{2}^{t}=V_{2}
will yield
T=\overline{V_{2}}T^{t}V_{2}
. So if
V_{2}
is taken to be the (doubly infinite) identity matrix
I
and
V_{1}=T
, we see that
T=T^{t}
. It follows from the structure of a Toeplitz matrix (constant entries along each diagonal) that
\widehat{\varphi}(n)=\widehat{\varphi}(-n)
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. This can indeed happen, for example, if we define the unimodular function
\varphi
on
\mathbb{T}
as
\varphi(e^{i\theta})=1
for
-\pi/2\leqslant\theta\leqslant\pi/2
and
\varphi(e^{i\theta})=-1
when
\pi/2<\theta<3\pi/2
. In this case a calculation shows that

\widehat{\varphi}(n)=-\frac{2(-1)^{n}\sin\left(\frac{\pi n}{2}\right)}{\pi n}

for
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
0
when
n=0
,
which indeed has the property that
\widehat{\varphi}(n)=\widehat{\varphi}(-n)
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. From (
4.9
) these yield the operators

{\bf e}_{j}\mapsto{\bf e}_{j+1}
(coming from
V_{2}=I
) – which is just the bilateral shift
M
, while the one coming from
V_{1}
, i.e.,
V_{1}{\bf e}_{j}\mapsto V_{1}{\bf e}_{j+1}
, which thinking of
V_{1}
as the multiplication operator
M_{\varphi}f=\varphi f
on
L^{2}(m)
, corresponds to the operator
\varphi z^{j}\mapsto\varphi z^{j+1}
. However, using the fact that
\varphi
is unimodular, this is precisely the operator
z^{j}\mapsto z^{j+1}
, which is the bilateral shift again. Of course one could keep
V_{2}=I
and set
V_{1}
to be the doubly infinite Toeplitz matrix corresponding to
any
unimodular
\varphi
which satisfies
\varphi(e^{i\theta})=\varphi(e^{-i\theta})
(which will force the condition
\widehat{\varphi}(n)=\widehat{\varphi}(-n)
for all
n
). This will yield a class of operators in the orbit of
M
that are precisely
M
. There are plenty of pairs
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
of doubly infinite self transpose unitary matrices such that
\overline{V_{2}}V_{1}
is not a Toeplitz matrix. These will correspond to distinct members of the orbit of
M
.

4.2
Multiplication operators in the conjugate orbit

If one wanted to
exclude
certain unitary operators on
L^{2}(m)
from consideration as members of the conjugate orbit of the bilateral shift
M
, one could make the observation that both
M
and
M^{*}
are cyclic (
M
is certainly
*
-cyclic and so by Bram’s theorem, see
[
5
]
or
[
10
, p. 232]
, it must be cyclic, likewise with
M^{*}
). Using Proposition
2.5
, and the facts that
M
and
M^{*}
belong to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
, we see that any operator in the conjugate orbit must also be cyclic. It is also known that the spectrum of
M
, as well as
M^{*}
, is
\mathbb{T}

[
14
, p. 178]
(spectrum of
M_{\varphi}
,
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(m)
, is its essential range). Again by Proposition
2.5
, anything in the conjugate orbit must also have spectrum equal to
\mathbb{T}
. For example, the (unitary) multiplication operator
M_{\varphi}
, where
\varphi
is equal to one on the top half of
\mathbb{T}
and minus one on the bottom half, is not contained in the conjugate orbit of the bilateral shift since its (essential) range is
\{-1,1\}
and not
\mathbb{T}
.

By the cyclicity discussion from the previous paragraph, it is also the case that in order for
M_{\varphi}
to belong to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
, the essential range of
\varphi
must be
\mathbb{T}

and

\varphi
must be injective on a set of full measure on
\mathbb{T}

[
31
,
34
]
. Thus, for example, if
\varphi
is inner and has multiplicity greater then one (an infinite Blaschke product or maybe a singular inner function for example), then, even though the essential range of these
\varphi
is
\mathbb{T}
, it is certainly far from being injective on a set of full measure and thus
M_{\varphi}\not\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
. We will explore such multiplication operators below. The definitive characterization as to when
M_{\varphi}
, for general
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(m)
(and unimodular), is unitarily equivalent to
M^{*}
depends on the “multiplicity function” for
\varphi
and is a delicate measure theory matter
[
1
,
2
]
that we will not get into here. Once the unitary equivalence of
M_{\varphi}
with
M^{*}
is established, there is also the issue as to when, via Proposition
2.7
, the intertwining operator yielding the unitary equivalence satisfies certain additional properties. Certainly
M=M_{\xi}
and
M^{*}=M_{\bar{\xi}}
belong to the conjugate orbit of
M
. Are there
other
multiplication operators in the conjugate orbit of
M
? We begin our exploration of this with several examples.

Example 4.11
.

On the positive side of the ledger, consider a (Lebesgue) measure preserving
\alpha:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}
such that
\alpha\circ\alpha=\operatorname{id}
. Examples include
\alpha(\xi)=\xi,\alpha(\xi)=-\xi,\alpha(\xi)=\bar{\xi}
, or, more generally,
\alpha
is a reflection of
\mathbb{T}
across any line through the origin. One can check that
(C_{\alpha}f)(\xi)=\bar{f}\circ\alpha
defines a conjugation on
L^{2}(m)
and a short computation shows that
C_{\alpha}MC_{\alpha}f=M_{\bar{\alpha}}f
for all
f\in L^{2}(m)
.
Thus, there are multiplication operators, besides
M
and
M^{*}
, namely
M_{\bar{\alpha}}
, that belong to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
but they seem to come from a quite restricted class.

Example 4.12
.

To demonstrate the restrictions on multiplication operators
M_{\varphi}
that belong to the conjugate orbit of
M
, besides the restriction that
\varphi
must be injective almost everywhere and have essential range equal to
\mathbb{T}
, consider any smooth function
\psi:[0,2\pi]\to[0,2\pi]
with
\psi(0)=0,\psi(2\pi)=2\pi
and
\psi^{\prime}(t)>0
for all
t
. By the inverse function theorem, this implies that
\psi
is invertible and smooth with
(\psi^{-1})^{\prime}>0
. If
\varphi(e^{it})=e^{i\psi(t)}
, then
\varphi
is unimodular on
\mathbb{T}
and so the multiplication operator
M_{\varphi}
on
L^{2}(m)
is unitary. Also note that
\varphi
is injective and
\varphi(\mathbb{T})=\mathbb{T}
. When does
M_{\varphi}
belong to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
? The result here is that
\psi(t)=t
is the only possibility and so
M_{\varphi}
is just the bilateral shift
M
. To see why this is true, observe that if
M_{\varphi}\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
, Proposition
2.7
says there must be a unitary operator
W
on
L^{2}(m)
such that
M_{\varphi}W=WM^{*}
and there must also be a conjugation
C
on
L^{2}(m)
for which
CMC=M^{*}
and
CWC=W^{*}
. A result from
[
7
]
says that any
C
for which
CMC=M^{*}
takes the form
C=C_{u}
, where
u
is a unimodular function on
\mathbb{T}
and
C_{u}f=u\bar{f}
.

If
(Xf)(\xi)=f(\bar{\xi})
, then, as discussed earlier,
X
is a unitary operator on
L^{2}(m)
with
X^{*}=X
and
XMX^{*}=M^{*}
.
By a change of variables, note that

Qf:=(f\circ\varphi)\sqrt{|\varphi^{\prime}|}=(f\circ\varphi)\sqrt{|\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}|}

defines a unitary operator on
L^{2}(m)
such that
M_{\varphi}(QX^{*})=(QX^{*})M^{*}
.
The two unitary operators
W
and
QX^{*}
both intertwine
M_{\varphi}
and
M^{*}
and so

(W^{*}QX^{*})M^{*}=M^{*}(W^{*}QX^{*}).

In other words, by taking adjoints of the previous identity,
XQ^{*}W
is unitary and belongs to the commutant of
M
. Thus, there is a unimodular
g\in L^{\infty}(m)
for which
XQ^{*}W=M_{g}
. From here it follows that

W=QX^{*}M_{g}=QXM_{g}
(recall that
X=X^{*}
) and thus

Wf=(g\circ\bar{\varphi})(f\circ\bar{\varphi})\sqrt{|\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}|}.

Moreover, a calculation using the fact that
W
is unitary and thus
W^{*}=W^{-1}
, yields

W^{*}f=\frac{f\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}}{g\sqrt{|\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}|}}.

So, from our discussion above, it boils down to finding a unimodular function
u
for which
C_{u}WC_{u}=W^{*}
. Applying both sides of this identity to a given
f\in L^{2}(m)
, we see that

u\cdot(\bar{g}\circ\bar{\varphi})(\bar{u}\circ\bar{\varphi})(f\circ\bar{\varphi})\sqrt{|\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}|}=\frac{f\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}}{g\sqrt{|\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}|}}.

Setting
f
to be the constant function equal to one on
\mathbb{T}
yields

u\cdot(\bar{g}\circ\bar{\varphi})(\bar{u}\circ\bar{\varphi})\sqrt{|\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}|}=\frac{1}{g\sqrt{|\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}|}}.

Taking absolute values and using the fact that the functions
g
and
u
are unimodular, we conclude that

\sqrt{|\varphi^{\prime}|}\sqrt{|\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}|}=1.

The above says that

\psi^{\prime}\cdot(\psi^{\prime}\circ\psi^{-1})=1
. An argument with inverse functions and the chain rule will show that
\psi^{\prime}(t)=(\psi^{-1}(t))^{\prime}
for all
0\leqslant t\leqslant 2\pi
. From the fact that
\psi(0)=\psi^{-1}(0)=0
, it follows that
\psi(t)=\psi^{-1}(t)
.
Finally observe that if
a,b\in(0,2\pi)
with
a<b
but
\psi(a)=b
and
\psi(b)=a
, then by Lagrange’s mean value theorem, there is a
c\in(a,b)

\psi^{\prime}(c)=\frac{\psi(a)-\psi(b)}{a-b}=-1.

However, we are assuming that
\psi^{\prime}>0
on
[0,2\pi]
and thus conclude that
\psi(t)=t
for all
t\in[0,2\pi]
.

We end this example with two comments. The first is that
M_{\varphi}
, for the symbol
\varphi
defined at the beginning of this example, is indeed unitary equivalent to
M
(their multiplicity functions are equal – see
[
1
,
2
]
) and so
M_{\varphi}
belongs to
\mathfrak{O}(M)
but not
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
unless
\varphi(e^{it})=e^{it}
. Second, we note that
(Cf)(\xi)=\bar{f}(-\xi)
defines a conjugation on
L^{2}(m)
such that
CMC=-M^{*}
and
(Cf)(\xi)=\bar{f}(-\overline{\xi})
defines a conjugation such that
CMC=-M
. Thus,
-M
and
-M^{*}
belong to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
. For a general unitary operator
U
, it is usually not the case that
-U
or
-U^{*}
belong to the conjugate orbit of
U
due to spectral restrictions (recall Corollary
2.6
).

Example 4.13
.

The previous example can be extended to any smooth
\varphi(e^{i\theta})=e^{i\psi(\theta)}
, where
\psi:[\eta,\eta+2\pi]\to[\eta,\eta+2\pi]
,
\psi^{\prime}>0
,
\psi(\eta)=\eta
, and
\psi(\eta+2\pi)=\eta+2\pi
. In this case.
M_{\varphi}
belongs to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
only if
\psi(\theta)=\theta+\eta
. This observation will exclude symbols such as

\varphi(\xi)=\frac{a-\xi}{1-\bar{a}\xi},\quad a\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\},

from consideration as
M_{\varphi}\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
. This is because

\psi(\theta)=\arg\varphi(e^{i\theta})
, which will never be of the form
\psi(\theta)=\theta+\eta
(recall that
a\not=0
).

Example 4.14
.

Does
M_{\varphi}
belong to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
when
\varphi(e^{it})=e^{i\psi(t)}
and
\psi
is a strictly increasing continuous function on
[0,2\pi]
with
\psi(0)=0
and
\psi(2\pi)=2\pi
, like in Example
4.12
, but with the added twist that
\psi^{\prime}(t)=0
for almost every
t
? Recall that such Cantor-like functions exist
[
28
]
.
Prima facie
,
M_{\varphi}
is not immediately eliminated from consideration for membership in the conjugate orbit of
M
, since it is injective and has range equal to
\mathbb{T}
. However, it does not belong to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
since it is not unitarily equivalent to
M^{*}
(recall Corollary
2.6
). This is because
\psi^{\prime}=0
almost everywhere and thus
\psi
has no absolutely continuous part. This implies that the spectral measure corresponding to
M_{\varphi}
is singular, where it certainly needs to be absolutely continuous for
M_{\varphi}
to be unitarily equivalent to
M^{*}
.

These examples all lead us to the possible conclusion that
M_{\varphi}\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
only under special circumstances. We confirm this observation with the following theorem.

Theorem 4.15
.

If
\varphi
is a unimodular Borel function on
\mathbb{T}
, then
M_{\varphi}\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
if and only if the following three conditions are satisfied:

(a)

\bar{\varphi}\circ\bar{\varphi}=\operatorname{id}
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
;

(b)

m\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}
is mutually absolutely continuous with respect to
m
;

(c)

If

{\displaystyle h=\frac{d(m\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1})}{dm},}

then
h\cdot(h\circ\bar{\varphi})=1
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\varphi
is a unomodular Borel function with
M_{\varphi}\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
. From the discussion above, we see that
\bar{\varphi}
is invertible on a set of full measure on
\mathbb{T}
. The measure
m\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}
is a well defined Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
and the operator

Y:L^{2}(m)\to L^{2}(m\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}),\quad Yf=f\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1},

is an isometric isomorphism. Indeed, there is the well-known change of variables formula

\int_{\mathbb{T}}|Yf|^{2}d(m\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1})=\int_{\mathbb{T}}|f|^{2}dm,\quad f\in L^{2}(m).

(4.16)

We direct the reader to
[
20
, p. 162-163]
for the technical details of the Borel measure theory discussion above.
Moreover,
(YM_{\bar{\varphi}}w)(\xi)=\xi(Yw)(\xi)
,
w\in L^{2}(m)
. This says that the spectral measure for
M_{\bar{\varphi}}
on
L^{2}(m)
is
m\circ\varphi^{-1}
, in other words,
M_{\bar{\varphi}}
on
L^{2}(m)
is unitarily equivalent to
M_{\xi}
on
L^{2}(m\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1})
. By the spectral theorem (the uniqueness part), the two measures
m
and
m\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}
on
\mathbb{T}
are mutually absolutely continuous and thus condition
(b)
is satisfied. Therefore, there is a nonnegative
h\in L^{1}(m)
(with
1/h\in L^{1}(m)
) such that

d(m\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1})=h\,dm.

Thus, the operator

Q:L^{2}(m)\to L^{2}(m),\quad Qf=(f\circ\bar{\varphi})\sqrt{h},

(4.17)

is unitary since it is isometric via

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}|Qf|^{2}dm

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}|f\circ\bar{\varphi}|^{2}hdm

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}|f\circ\bar{\varphi}|^{2}d(m\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}|f|^{2}dm.

Moreover, if
g\in L^{2}(m)
, then, similar to the computation in (
4.16
),

f:=\frac{g\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}}{\sqrt{h\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}}}

belongs to
L^{2}(m)
and
Qf=g
.

Since we are assuming that
M_{\varphi}\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
, Proposition
2.7
says there must be a unitary operator
W
on
L^{2}(m)
such

JWJ=W^{*}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;M_{\varphi}=WM^{*}W^{*},

where
Jf=\bar{f}
on
L^{2}(m)
. Recall that
JMJ=M^{*}
. The first condition in the previous line says that

\overline{W(\bar{f})}=W^{*}f,\quad f\in L^{2}(m),

(4.18)

while the second condition yields

W^{*}(\varphi f)(\xi)=\bar{\xi}(W^{*}f)(\xi).

Using (
4.18
) and taking complex conjugates gives us

W(\bar{\varphi}\bar{f})=\xi(W\bar{f}),\quad f\in L^{2}(m),

and setting
g=\bar{f}
says that

Wg=\bar{\xi}W(\bar{\varphi}g),\quad g\in L^{2}(m).

(4.19)

Iterate this previous identity as

\displaystyle Wg

\displaystyle=\bar{\xi}W(\bar{\varphi}g)

\displaystyle=\bar{\xi}^{2}W(\bar{\varphi}^{2}g)

\displaystyle=\bar{\xi}^{3}W(\bar{\varphi}^{3}g)

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle=\bar{\xi}^{n}W(\bar{\varphi}^{n}g),\quad n\geqslant 0.

Replace
g
with
\varphi^{n}g
in the previous equation, and recall that
\varphi
is unimodular, to see that

W(\varphi^{n}g)=\bar{\xi}^{n}W(\bar{\varphi}^{n}\varphi^{n}g)=\bar{\xi}^{n}Wg,\quad n\geqslant 0.

Combining these two facts give us the identity

W(\bar{\varphi}^{m}g)=\xi^{m}Wg,\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}.

With
g=\sqrt{h}
, which belongs to
L^{2}(m)
, the above says that

W((p\circ\bar{\varphi})\sqrt{h})=p\cdot(W(\sqrt{h})

for all trigonometric polynomials
p
, that is, finite linear combinations of the monomials
\{\xi^{m}:m\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Using the unitary
Q
from (
4.17
), we can rewrite the identity from the previous line as

WQp=p\cdot W(\sqrt{h})

for all trigonometric polynomials
p
, and hence all
f\in L^{2}(m)
. Since
W
and
Q
are both unitary on
L^{2}(m)
, the above, via the density of the trigonometric polynomials in
L^{2}(m)
, says that the multiplication operator
f\mapsto f\cdot W(\sqrt{h})
is unitary on
L^{2}(m)
and thus

k:=W(\sqrt{h})

must be a unimodular function on
\mathbb{T}
.

Inverting the formula in (
4.17
) reveals that

Q^{-1}f=\frac{f\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}}{\sqrt{h\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}}}

and thus

Wf=(Q^{-1}f)\cdot k=\frac{f\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}}{\sqrt{h\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}}}k.

(4.20)

From the previous line it follows that

W^{*}f=W^{-1}f=(f\circ\bar{\varphi})\cdot(\overline{k\circ\bar{\varphi}})\cdot\sqrt{h}.

(4.21)

Now bring in (
4.18
) to get

W^{*}f=\overline{W\bar{f}}=\frac{f\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}}{\sqrt{h\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}}}\bar{k}

(4.22)

and thus, equating (
4.21
) and (
4.22
), yields

(f\circ\bar{\varphi})\cdot(\overline{k\circ\bar{\varphi}})\cdot\sqrt{h}=\frac{f\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}}{\sqrt{h\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}}}\bar{k}.

(4.23)

Plug in the constant function
f\equiv 1
into both sides of (
4.23
) to get

\overline{k\circ\bar{\varphi}}\cdot\sqrt{h}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}}}\bar{k}.

Recall that
k
is a unomudular function and
h
is nonnegative and so, taking the modulus of both sides of the above, we see that

\sqrt{h}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1}}},

which verifies condition
(c)
, and also gives us the identity

\overline{k\circ\bar{\varphi}}=\bar{k}.

Plugging these last two facts into (
4.23
) yields

f\circ\bar{\varphi}=f\circ\bar{\varphi}^{-1},\quad f\in L^{2}(m),

and setting
f(\xi)=\xi
in the above, we see that

\bar{\varphi}=\bar{\varphi}^{-1}.

This verifies condition
(a)
.

Conversely, suppose that conditions
(a),(b),(c)
are satisfied. One can check that the mapping

C:L^{2}(m)\to L^{2}(m),\quad Cf=\sqrt{h}\cdot(\bar{f}\circ\bar{\varphi}),

satisfies the three properties of a conjugation (antilinear, isometric, and involutive). Note that
C
is antilinear. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}|Cf|^{2}dm

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}h|\bar{f}\circ\bar{\varphi}|^{2}dm

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}h|f\circ\bar{\varphi}|^{2}dm

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}|Qf|^{2}dm

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}|f|^{2}dm

for each
f\in L^{2}(m)
.
Thus
C
maps
L^{2}(m)
to
L^{2}(m)
isometrically. Moreover, for each
f\in L^{2}(m)
,

\displaystyle C^{2}f

\displaystyle=C(\sqrt{h}\cdot(\bar{f}\circ\bar{\varphi}))

\displaystyle=\sqrt{h}\cdot(\sqrt{h}\circ\bar{\varphi})\cdot(f\circ\bar{\varphi}\circ\bar{\varphi})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{h}\cdot(\sqrt{h}\circ\bar{\varphi})\cdot f

\displaystyle=f.

Thus,
C
is involutive and so
C
is a conjugation on
L^{2}(m)
.

Finally, observe that

\displaystyle CMCf

\displaystyle=CM(\sqrt{h}\cdot(\bar{f}\circ\bar{\varphi}))

\displaystyle=C(\xi\cdot\sqrt{h}\cdot(\bar{f}\circ\bar{\varphi}))

\displaystyle=(\bar{\xi}\circ\bar{\varphi})\sqrt{h}\cdot(\sqrt{h}\circ\bar{\varphi})(f\circ\bar{\varphi}\circ\bar{\varphi})

\displaystyle=\varphi f,

and thus
M_{\varphi}\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
.
∎

We remark that in Example
4.11
, the mapping
\alpha
was assumed to be measure preserving and convolutive and thus
h\equiv 1
in the previous theorem.

Example 4.24
.

Here we construct a specific example of a unimodular and Borel measurable
\varphi
on
\mathbb{T}
satisfying conditions
(a),(b),(c)
of Theorem
4.15
for which
h
is not identically one and thus
\varphi
is not measure preserving. Let
\omega:[0,\pi]\to[0,\pi]
be a smooth strictly increasing function with
\omega(0)=0
and
\omega(\pi)=\pi
. A simple example is
\omega(t)=t^{2}/\pi
. Now define
\varphi:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}
by

\varphi(e^{it})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}e^{-i\omega(t)}&\mbox{if}&0\leqslant t\leqslant\pi,\\
&&\\
e^{i\omega^{-1}(-t)}&\mbox{if}&-\pi\leqslant t\leqslant 0.\\
\end{array}\right.

Observe that
\varphi
is a continuous bijection on
\mathbb{T}
,
\varphi(1)=1
,
\varphi(-1)=-1
, and
\varphi
flips the upper and lower half circles. The result here is the following:
m\circ\varphi^{-1}
us mutually absolutely continuous with respect to
m
and

h(e^{i\theta}):=\frac{d(m\circ\varphi^{-1})}{dm}(e^{i\theta})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\omega^{\prime}(\theta),&\mbox{if}&0<\theta<\pi,\\
&&\\
{\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega^{\prime}(\omega^{-1}(-\theta))}},&\mbox{if}&-\pi<\theta<0.\\
\end{array}\right.

Here is the proof.
Fix
\zeta=e^{i\theta}
in the upper half circle, i.e.,
0<\theta<\pi
, and, for a fixed
\varepsilon>0
, set

I_{\varepsilon}:=\{e^{it}:\theta-\varepsilon<t<\theta+\varepsilon\}.

Then

\varphi^{-1}(I_{\varepsilon})=\{e^{is}:-\omega(\theta+\varepsilon)<s<-\omega(\theta-\varepsilon)\}.

Hence, by definition,

(m\circ\varphi^{-1})(I_{\varepsilon})=m(\varphi^{-1}(I_{\varepsilon}))=\frac{\omega(\theta+\varepsilon)-\omega(\theta-\varepsilon)}{2\pi}.

Therefore, by Besicovitch’s theorem
[
4
]
, the Radon–Nikodym derivative can be computed as a limit by

\displaystyle\frac{d(m\circ\varphi^{-1})}{dm}(e^{i\theta})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{(m\circ\varphi^{-1})(I_{\varepsilon})}{m(I_{\varepsilon})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\omega(\theta+\varepsilon)-\omega(\theta-\varepsilon)}{2\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\omega^{\prime}(\theta),\qquad 0<\theta<\pi.

In a similar way, if
\zeta=e^{i\theta}
in the lower half circle, i.e.,
\pi<\theta<0
, we consider

I_{\varepsilon}=\{e^{it}:\theta-\varepsilon<t<\theta+\varepsilon\}.

Then

\varphi^{-1}(I_{\varepsilon})=\{e^{is}:\omega^{-1}(-\theta-\varepsilon)<s<\omega^{-1}(-\theta+\varepsilon)\}.

Hence, by definition,

(m\circ\varphi^{-1})(I_{\varepsilon})=m(\varphi^{-1}(I_{\varepsilon}))=\frac{\omega^{-1}(\theta+\varepsilon)-\omega^{-1}(\theta-\varepsilon)}{2\pi}.

Therefore, again by Besicovitch’s theorem,

\displaystyle\frac{d(m\circ\varphi^{-1})}{dm}(e^{i\theta})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{(m\circ\varphi^{-1})(I_{\varepsilon})}{m(I_{\varepsilon})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\omega^{-1}(-\theta+\varepsilon)-\omega^{-1}(-\theta-\varepsilon)}{2\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\big(\omega^{-1}\big)^{\prime}(-\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega^{\prime}(\omega^{-1}(-\theta))},\qquad-\pi<\theta<0,

which completes the proof.

Note that, for
0<\theta<\pi
,

h(\varphi(e^{i\theta}))=h(e^{-i\omega(\theta)})=\frac{1}{\omega^{\prime}(\omega^{-1}(\omega(\theta)))}=\frac{1}{\omega^{\prime}(\theta)}=\frac{1}{h(e^{i\theta})},

and similarly, for
-\pi<\theta<0
,

h(\varphi(e^{i\theta}))=h(e^{i\omega^{-1}(-\theta)})=\omega^{\prime}(\omega^{-1}(-\theta))=\frac{1}{h(e^{i\theta})}.

Hence, for almost all
\theta
,

h(e^{i\theta})\cdot h(\varphi(e^{i\theta}))=1.

In the special case
\omega(t)=t^{2}/\pi
, we obtain

\frac{d(m\circ\varphi^{-1})}{dm}(e^{i\theta})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\displaystyle\frac{2\theta}{\pi}},&\mbox{if}&0<\theta<\pi,\\
&&\\
{\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{-\theta}}},&\mbox{if}&-\pi<\theta<0.\\
\end{array}\right.

Example 4.26
.

It is worth mentioning a member of
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M)
that combines some of the previous examples. Let
\alpha:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}
be (Lebesgue) measure preserving with
\alpha\circ\alpha=\operatorname{id}
,
\beta:\mathbb{T}\to\{-1,1\}
(Lebesgue measurable) such that
\beta\circ\alpha=\beta
, and
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
with
s^{2}+t^{2}=1
. A specific example of such an
\alpha
and
\beta
is
\alpha(\xi)=\bar{\xi}
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
and
\beta(\xi)=1
when
-\pi/2\leqslant\arg\xi\leqslant\pi/2
and
\beta(\xi)=-1
when
\pi/2<\arg\xi<3\pi/2
.

Fixing the above parameters
\alpha,\beta,s,t
, define the mapping
C
on
L^{2}(m)
by

Cf=s(\bar{f}\circ\alpha)+it\beta\bar{f}

and note that
C
is antilinear. Moreover, since
s
and
t
are real,

\displaystyle C^{2}f

\displaystyle=C(s(\bar{f}\circ\alpha)+it\beta\bar{f})

\displaystyle=sC(\bar{f}\circ\alpha)-itC(\beta\bar{f})

\displaystyle=s\big(s(f\circ(\alpha\circ\alpha))+it\beta(f\circ\alpha)\big)-it\big(s(\beta\circ\alpha)(f\circ\alpha)+it\beta^{2}f\big)

\displaystyle=s^{2}f+ist\beta(f\circ\alpha)-ist\beta(f\circ\alpha)+t^{2}f

\displaystyle=(s^{2}+t^{2})f

\displaystyle=f,

and so
C
is involutive. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\|Cf\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|s(\bar{f}\circ\alpha)+it\beta\bar{f}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=s^{2}\|f\circ\alpha\|^{2}+\big\langle s(\bar{f}\circ\alpha),it\beta\bar{f}\big\rangle+\big\langle it\beta\bar{f},s(\bar{f}\circ\alpha)\big\rangle+t^{2}\|f\|^{2}

\displaystyle=s^{2}\|f\|^{2}+t^{2}\|f\|^{2}+ist\Big(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\beta\bar{f}(f\circ\alpha)dm-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\beta(\bar{f}\circ\alpha)fdm\Big).

Using the facts that
\beta\circ\alpha=\beta

\alpha\circ\alpha=\operatorname{id}
, and

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\beta\bar{f}(f\circ\alpha)dm-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\beta(\bar{f}\circ\alpha)fdm=0

\|Cf\|^{2}=(s^{2}+t^{2})\|f\|^{2}=\|f\|^{2}.

This makes

.
From here we conclude that
CMC
is a member of

and has the formula

\displaystyle CMCf

\displaystyle=CM(s(\bar{f}\circ\alpha)+i\beta\bar{f})

\displaystyle=C(sM(\bar{f}\circ\alpha)+itM(\beta\bar{f}))

\displaystyle=sC(M(\bar{f}\circ\alpha))-itC(M(\beta\bar{f}))

\displaystyle=s\Big(s\bar{\alpha}f+it\beta M^{*}(f\circ\alpha)\Big)-it\Big(s(\beta\circ\alpha)\bar{\alpha}(f\circ\alpha)+it\beta^{2}M^{*}f\Big)

\displaystyle=s^{2}\bar{\alpha}f+t^{2}M^{*}f+ist(\beta M^{*}f+\beta\bar{\alpha}(f\circ\alpha))

\displaystyle=(s^{2}M_{\bar{\alpha}}+t^{2}M^{*})f+ist\beta(M_{\bar{\alpha}}-M^{*})(f\circ\alpha).

Remark 4.27
.

If
U
is unitary with
\sigma(U)=\{\lambda\}
, then, as observed earlier in Remark
2.4
,
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)=\{\overline{\lambda}I\}
is just one operator. If
\sigma(U)
has more than one point, then the conjugate orbit is quite rich. Indeed, one of the many versions of the spectral theorem
[
9
, p. 304]
says that a general unitary operator
U
is unitarity equivalent to

\bigoplus_{n\geqslant 1}(M_{\xi},L^{2}(\mu|_{\Delta_{n}})),

where
M_{\xi}f=\xi f
on
L^{2}(\mu|_{\Delta_{n}})
,
\mu
is a positive finite measure on
\mathbb{C}
,
\Delta_{1}=\sigma(U)
, and
(\Delta_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
is a decreasing sequence of Borel subsets of
\sigma(U)
. The assumption that
\sigma(U)
has at least two distinct elements, shows that not all of the summands
(M_{\xi},L^{2}(\mu|_{\Delta_{n}}))
are trivial. Moreover, by the previous examples (Example
2.27
in particular), the conjugate orbit of each of the
M_{\xi}
on
L^{2}(\mu|_{\Delta_{n}})
(when it is nontrivial) is quite abundant. We will see another analysis of the complexity of the conjugate orbit in the next section.

4.3
Shifts of higher multiplicity

One can generalize the previous section and consider, for a fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, the unitary operator

M^{(n)}:=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}M

on the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{(n)}
, where

\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{(n)}:=\Big\{({\bf a}_{k})_{k=1}^{n}:\|({\bf a}_{k})_{k=1}^{n}\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{(n)}}:=\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|{\bf a}_{k}\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty\Big\}.

Recall the definition of the sequence space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
from (
4.1
).
Note that
M^{(n)}
acts on a vector
({\bf a}_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{(n)}
by

M^{(n)}\big(({\bf a}_{k})_{k=1}^{n}\big):=(M{\bf a}_{k})_{k=1}^{n}

and is called the
bilateral shift of multiplicity
n
.

One often thinks of
M^{(n)}
in its block matrix form acting on column vectors

\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}_{1}\\
{\bf a}_{2}\\
{\bf a}_{3}\\
\vdots\end{bmatrix}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{(n)}

as

M^{(n)}=\begin{bmatrix}M&0&0&0&0&\cdots\\
0&M&0&0&0&\cdots\\
0&0&M&0&0&\cdots\\
0&0&0&M&0&\cdots\\
0&0&0&0&M&\cdots\\[-4.0pt]
0&0&0&0&0&\ddots\\
\end{bmatrix}.

In this section we make some remarks concerning the conjugate orbit of
M^{(n)}
. A skeptic might wonder if we are generalizing the discussion from the previous section just for the sake of generalization. Through the following examples, let us make the case that some “naturally appearing” unitary operators are actually unitarily equivalent to bilateral shifts and thus fall under the purview of the current discussion.

Example 4.28
.

For a bounded analytic function
u
on
\mathbb{D}
, well-known theory of function spaces
[
12
]
says that

u(\xi):=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}u(r\xi)

exists for almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
.
A bounded analytic function
u
is
inner
if the above boundary function
u(\xi)
is unimodular almost everywhere.
As examined in
[
27
]
, consider an inner function
u
with degree
n
(either the number of Blaschke factors if
u
is a finite Blaschke product or
\infty
if
u
contains an infinite Blaschke factor or a singular inner factor in its factorization). We refer the reader to
[
12
]
for an explanation of the above terms (Blaschke product, singular inner function, etc.). The multiplication operator
M_{u}f=uf
is unitary on
L^{2}(m)
and moreover,

L^{2}(m)=\bigoplus_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}u^{j}\mathcal{K}_{u},

where
\mathcal{K}_{u}:=H^{2}\ominus uH^{2}
is the
model space
corresponding to the inner function
u
.
Here
H^{2}
is the closed subspace of
L^{2}(m)
consisting of the
L^{2}(m)
functions whose negative Fourier coefficients vanish.
It is known that the dimension of
\mathcal{K}_{u}
is equal to the degree of
u

[
13
, p. 117]
. In the parlance of operator theory, the model space
\mathcal{K}_{u}
is a
wandering subspace
for the multiplication operator
M_{u}
on
L^{2}(m)
. It follows, see Proposition
4.31
below, that
M_{u}
is unitarily equivalent to
M^{(n)}
. So, for example, if
u
is a Blaschke product of order two, then
M_{u}
is unitarily equivalent to
M^{(2)}
, while if
u
is an infinite Blaschke product or a singular inner function,
then
M_{u}
is unitarily equivalent to
M^{(\infty)}
. As a specific example where the wandering subspace is finite dimensional, suppose
u(\xi)=\xi^{2}
(an example of a Blaschke product of order two). Then
\mathcal{K}_{u}=H^{2}\ominus uH^{2}
is equal to the two dimensional subspace
\operatorname{span}\{1,\xi\}
. Thus,

L^{2}(m)=\bigoplus_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\xi^{2j}\operatorname{span}\{1,\xi\}.

From here we can see that
M_{u}
on
L^{2}(m)
is unitarily equivalent to
M\oplus M
on
L^{2}(m)\oplus L^{2}(m)
.

Example 4.29
.

The translation operator
(Uf)(x)=f(x-1)
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is unitary and if
\mathcal{M}=\chi_{[0,1]}L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, then

L^{2}(\mathbb{R})=\bigoplus_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}U^{j}\mathcal{M}.

Analogous to the previous example,
\mathcal{M}
is an infinite dimensional wandering subspace for
U
. It follows, again see Proposition
4.31
below, that
U
is unitarily equivalent to
M^{(\infty)}
.

Example 4.30
.

The dilation operator
(Uf)(x)=\sqrt{2}f(2x)
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is unitary. If

\psi(x)=\begin{cases}\phantom{-}1&\mbox{for $0\leqslant x<\frac{1}{2}$},\\
-1&\mbox{for $\frac{1}{2}\leqslant x\leqslant 1$},\\
\phantom{-}0&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

(Haar) wavelet theory
[
24
]
(see also
[
19
]
) says that the family of functions

\psi_{n,k}(x):=2^{\frac{n}{2}}\psi(2^{n}x-k),\quad n,k\in\mathbb{Z},

form an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Consider the subspaces

\mathcal{W}_{\ell}:=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{\psi_{\ell,k}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\},\quad\ell\in\mathbb{Z}.

Note that
\mathcal{W}_{\ell}\perp\mathcal{W}_{\ell^{\prime}}
for all distinct
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}

and
U\mathcal{W}_{\ell}=\mathcal{W}_{\ell+1}
for all
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus,

L^{2}(\mathbb{R})=\bigoplus_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}U^{j}\mathcal{W}_{0}.

Hence,
\mathcal{W}_{0}
is an infinite dimensional wandering subspace for
U
and
so
U
is unitarily equivalent to
M^{(\infty)}
.

The driver for connecting these above examples with a bilateral shift of higher multiplicity comes from the following general result.

Proposition 4.31
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{H}
is a separable Hilbert space and
\mathcal{M}
is an
n
dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
. If
U
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
satisfying

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}U^{j}\mathcal{M},

(4.32)

then
U
is unitarily equivalent to
M^{(n)}
.

Proof.

We follow the proof from
[
27
]
. Let
({\bf u}_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
,
1\leqslant n\leqslant\infty
, be an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{M}
. By our assumption on
\mathcal{H}
in (
4.32
), each
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}
can be written as

{\bf x}=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}U^{j}{\bf x}_{j},\;\;\mbox{where}\;\;{\bf x}_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{j,k}{\bf u}_{k}\in\mathcal{M}.

Thus,

{\bf x}=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{j,k}U^{j}{\bf u}_{k}\Big).

Now observe that since
U^{j}{\bf x}_{j}\perp U^{j^{\prime}}{\bf x}_{j^{\prime}}
(and
U
is unitary) we have

\displaystyle\|{\bf x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\|{\bf x}_{j}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{j,k}{\bf u}_{j}\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|a_{j,k}|^{2}.

Thus, for each
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
, the vector
{\bf a}_{k}=(a_{j,k})_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}
belongs to
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
the map

\Gamma:\mathcal{H}\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{(n)},\quad\Gamma({\bf x})=({\bf a}_{k})_{k=1}^{n},

is an isometric isomorphism that intertwines
U
with
M^{(n)}
. Indeed, for any
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}
we have

\displaystyle\Gamma U{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\Gamma U\Big(\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{j,k}U^{j}{\bf u}_{k}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Gamma\Big(\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{j,k}U^{j+1}{\bf u}_{k}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Gamma\Big(\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{j-1,k}U^{j}{\bf u}_{k}\Big)

\displaystyle=((a_{j-1,k})_{j=-\infty}^{\infty})_{k=1}^{n}

\displaystyle=(M{\bf a}_{k})_{k=1}^{n}

\displaystyle=M^{(n)}\Gamma{\bf x},

which establishes the unitary equivalence of
U
and
M^{(n)}
.
∎

What type of operators belong to
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M^{(n)})
? This seems to be quite difficult and we do not have an adequate description yet. But we can present some rich classes of examples.

Example 4.33
.

Certainly, the following class of unitary operators on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{(n)}
belong to the conjugate orbit of
M^{(n)}
:

W:=\begin{bmatrix}W_{1}&0&0&0&0&\cdots\\
0&W_{2}&0&0&0&\cdots\\
0&0&W_{3}&0&0&\cdots\\
0&0&0&W_{4}&0&\cdots\\
0&0&0&0&W_{5}&\cdots\\[-4.0pt]
0&0&0&0&0&\ddots\end{bmatrix},

where each
W_{k}
is a unitary shift on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
formed from a doubly infinite self transpose unitary matrix

V_{k}=[\cdots|{\bf v}_{k,-2}|{\bf v}_{k,-1}|{\bf v}_{k,0}|{\bf v}_{k,1}|{\bf v}_{k,2}|\cdots]

with

W_{k}{\bf v}_{k,j}={\bf v}_{k,j+1},\quad j\in\mathbb{Z}.

One can see this by using Theorem
4.4
to find conjugations
C_{k}
,
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
, on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
C_{k}MC_{k}=W_{k}
for the given unitary shifts
W_{k}
as above. Then

C:=\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&0&0&0&0&\cdots\\
0&C_{2}&0&0&0&\cdots\\
0&0&C_{3}&0&0&\cdots\\
0&0&0&C_{4}&0&\cdots\\
0&0&0&0&C_{5}&\cdots\\[-4.0pt]
0&0&0&0&0&\ddots\end{bmatrix},

(4.34)

satisfies

C(({\bf a}_{k})_{k=1}^{n})=(C_{k}{\bf a}_{k})_{k=1}^{n}

and defines a conjugation on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{(n)}
. Furthermore, since
M^{(n)}=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{n}M
, we conclude that

CM^{(n)}C=W
.

When
n=1
, Theorem
4.4
says that the conjugate orbit
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M^{(1)})
consists
precisely
of these unitary shifts. When
n>1
, there are many
other
operators in
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M^{(n)})
. The main reason is that not every conjugation on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{(n)}
can be diagonalized as in (
4.34
). Indeed, the paper
[
23
]
shows how one can, at least in the
n=2
case, for a general Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, create conjugations
C
on
\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}
as

C=\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&C_{2}\\
C_{3}&C_{4}\end{bmatrix},

where each
C_{j}
above is an antilinear operator on
\mathcal{H}
(not necessarily a conjugation) which satisfies

C_{1}=C_{1}^{\star},\quad C_{4}=C_{4}^{\star},\quad C_{3}=C_{2}^{\star}.

C_{1}C_{1}^{\star}+C_{2}C_{2}^{\star}=I,\quad C_{2}C_{2}^{\star}+C_{4}C_{4}^{\star}=I,

C_{2}^{\star}C_{1}+C_{4}C_{2}^{\star}=0.

In the above,
C_{j}^{\star}
denotes the antilinear adjoint (to be formally defined below in (
6.1
)) as the antilinear operator on
\mathcal{H}
satisfying

\langle C_{j}{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle=\overline{\langle{\bf x},C_{j}^{\star}{\bf y}\rangle}\;\;\mbox{for all}\;\;{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}.

The above matrix definition of a conjugation is understood as

C\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&C_{2}\\
C_{3}&C_{4}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}{\bf a}+C_{2}{\bf b}\\
C_{3}{\bf a}+C_{4}{\bf b}\end{bmatrix},\;\;\mbox{where}\;\;\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}.

Certainly the conjugation arising from (
4.34
), with
C_{1},C_{4}
conjugations and
C_{2}=C_{3}=0
, define conjugations on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. However, there are many others and thus
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(M^{(2)})
consists of far more unitary operators than just
W_{1}\oplus W_{2}
, with
W_{1},W_{2}
unitary shifts on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. As one can imagine, the situation only becomes more complicated as
n
gets larger.

Example 4.35
.

Considering the bilateral shift as the multiplication operator
(Mf)(\xi)=\xi f(\xi)
on
L^{2}(m)
there is another rich class of members of the conjugate orbit of
M^{(n)}
. For each
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
, let
\alpha_{j}
be a (Lebesgue) measure preserving map of
\mathbb{T}
with
\alpha_{j}\circ\alpha_{j}=\operatorname{id}
. As before, define the conjugation
C_{\alpha_{j}}=\bar{f}\circ\alpha_{j}
on
L^{2}(m)
. With
C
defined as the block diagonal conjugation

C=\operatorname{diag}(C_{\alpha_{1}},C_{\alpha_{2}},C_{\alpha_{3}},\ldots)

on
L^{2}(m)^{(n)}
, we see from our calculations in Example
4.11
that

CM^{(n)}C=\operatorname{diag}(M_{\bar{\alpha}_{1}},M_{\bar{\alpha}_{2}},M_{\bar{\alpha}_{3}},\ldots).

We point out a final comment concerning the complexity of the conjugate orbit of a general unitary operator that also provides a glimmer of hope that one can obtain get some partial understanding of the complexity involved. A result from
[
27
]
, which uses some of the finer renditions of the spectral theorem, says that for a given unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
, there are invariant subspaces
\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2},\mathcal{K}_{3}
for
U
such that
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{K}_{1}\bigoplus\mathcal{K}_{2}\bigoplus\mathcal{K}_{3}
. Moreover
U|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}
is a bilateral shift in the sense of Proposition
4.31
,
U_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}
is diagonalizable in the sense of Corollary
2.12
,
U|_{\mathcal{K}_{2}\oplus\mathcal{K}_{3}}
contains no bilateral shift part of
U
, and
\mathcal{K}_{3}
contains no eigenvectors of
U
. In fact, the operator
U|_{\mathcal{K}_{3}}
is sometimes known as the “chaotic part” of
U
and is not completely understood. Our previous work shows the complexity of the conjugate orbits of each piece
U|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}
and
U|_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}
and so we have some organizational tools to help us understand the possibilities of (some of) the operators in the conjugate orbit of
U
. Also worth pointing out is a older rendition of the discussion above that decomposes a unitary operator into a direct sum of a bilateral shift and a pure part (a unitary where every invariant subspace is reducing)
[
3
, p. 62-63]
.

Chapter 5
Real and Complex Hilbert Spaces from a Unitary Operator

The remainder of this paper will develop a matrix model for a generic unitary operator that involves real Hilbert spaces. We do this in order to obtain a tangible model for its conjugate orbit. Since this topic, which goes under the general heading of
complexification
, might be unfamiliar to the most likely audience for this paper, we will be quite generous with the exposition, details, and examples.

5.1
A real Hilbert space from a unitary operator

If
U
is a unitary operator on a separable Hilbert
\mathcal{H}
with spectral measure
E(\cdot)
, in other words

U=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\xi dE(\xi)

(5.1)

[
9
, p. 269]
,
a rendition of the spectral theorem yields an orthonormal sequence of vectors
({\bf x}_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
in
\mathcal{H}
such that

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{k\geqslant 1}\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{k}),

(5.2)

where

\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{k}):=\overline{\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}}\{E(\Omega){\bf x}_{k}:\Omega\in\mathcal{B}\}.

In the above,
\mathcal{B}
denotes the Borel subsets of
\mathbb{T}
and
\overline{\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}}
denotes the closed
complex
linear span in
\mathcal{H}
. We will be a bit pedantic here with the subscript
\mathbb{C}
above since at some point below we will examine the
real
linear span of a set of vectors (and denote it by
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}
– see (
5.13
) below).
One can produce such a sequence
({\bf x}_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
satisfying (
5.2
) as follows (see
[
9
, p. 300]
). For a fixed unit vector
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}
let

\mathcal{H}({\bf x}):=\overline{\mathcal{W}^{*}(U){\bf x}},

where
\mathcal{W}^{*}(U)
is the von Neumann algebra generated by
U
(the weak or, what turns out to be the same, the strong operator closure of
p(U)
, where
p
is a trigonometric polynomial). Since
U|_{\mathcal{H}({\bf x})}
is a
\ast
-cyclic unitary operator in that

\overline{\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}}\{U^{n}{\bf x}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}=\mathcal{H}({\bf x}),

note that
U^{-n}=U^{*n}
,
basic facts about spectral measures, namely the important fact that
E(\Omega)\in\mathcal{W}^{*}(U)
for every
\Omega\in\mathcal{B}

[
9
, p. 292]
, will show that

\mathcal{H}({\bf x}):=\overline{\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}}\{E(\Omega){\bf x}:\Omega\in\mathcal{B}\}.

(5.3)

A Zorn’s lemma argument produces a sequence
({\bf x}_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
in
\mathcal{H}
such that

\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{j})\perp\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{k})
for
j\not=k
, and, by the maximality of the sequence
({\bf x}_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
, we obtain (
5.2
).

Example 5.4
.

If
U=I
, the identity operator on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, choose any orthonormal basis
({\bf x}_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
for
\mathcal{H}
and note that

\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{k})=\overline{\mathcal{W}^{*}(I){\bf x}_{k}}=\mathbb{C}{\bf x}_{k}

and, trivially,
\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{k\geqslant 1}\mathbb{C}{\bf x}_{k}.

Example 5.5
.

Suppose
U=\operatorname{diag}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3},\ldots)
is a diagonal unitary operator with
\xi_{j}\not=\xi_{k}
for all
j,k\geqslant 1
acting on
\ell^{2}=\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
via
U{\bf e}_{j}=\xi_{j}{\bf e}_{j}
. Then any unit vector
{\bf x}=(x_{j})_{j\geqslant 1}\in\ell^{2}
, with
x_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
for all
j
, is a
\ast
-cyclic vector for
U
and so
\mathcal{H}({\bf x})=\ell^{2}
.

Remark 5.6
.

It is important to emphasize here that the decomposition in (
5.2
) is not unique or even canonical. For instance, in the previous example, we can set
{\bf x}_{1}={\bf e}_{1}
and
{\bf x}_{2}=(0,x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},\ldots)\in\ell^{2}
,
x_{j}\not=0
. Then
\ell^{2}=\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{1})\oplus\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{2})
.

Example 5.7
.

If
U=\operatorname{diag}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
is an
n\times n
diagonal unitary matrix with
\xi_{j}\not=\xi_{k}
,
1\leqslant j,k\leqslant n
then, as with Example
5.5
with
{\bf x}=(1,1,\ldots,1)
, we see that
\mathcal{H}({\bf x})=\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Example 5.8
.

When
(Uf)(\xi)=\xi f(\xi)
is the bilateral shift on
L^{2}(m)
, the density of the trigonometric polynomials in
L^{2}(m)
and the fact that
(U^{*}f)(\xi)=\bar{\xi}f(\xi)
, imply that
\chi:=\chi_{\mathbb{T}}
, the characteristic function on
\mathbb{T}
, is a
\ast
-cyclic vector for
U
and so

L^{2}(m)=\mathcal{H}(\chi)=\overline{\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}}\{U^{n}\chi:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.

Example 5.9
.

Suppose that
U
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
and
(\xi_{j})_{j\geqslant 1}
are
distinct
unimodular constants with

\mathcal{N}_{j}:=\ker(U-\xi_{j}I)\not=\{{\bf 0}\}
and

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{j\geqslant 1}\mathcal{N}_{j}.

Of course the eigenspaces of a unitary operator are automatically mutually orthogonal. The above assumption is just a matter of the eigenspaces spanning
\mathcal{H}
. Since
U|_{\mathcal{N}_{j}}=\xi_{j}I|_{\mathcal{N}_{j}}
we can apply Example
5.4
to each
U|_{\mathcal{N}_{j}}
to write

\mathcal{N}_{j}=\bigoplus_{k\geqslant 1}\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{k}),
where the number of summands, equivalently, the number of orthonormal basis elements
{\bf x}_{k}
for
\mathcal{N}_{j}
, will be the dimension of
\mathcal{N}_{j}
. Now reindex to
write
\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{n\geqslant 1}\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{n})
.

Example 5.10
.

One could revisit Example
5.5
and combine it with the analysis from the previous example to see that

\mathcal{N}_{j}=\ker(U-\xi_{j}I)=\mathbb{C}{\bf e}_{j}=\overline{\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}}\{U^{n}{\bf e}_{j}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}=\mathcal{H}({\bf e}_{j})

and

\ell^{2}=\bigoplus_{j\geqslant 1}\mathcal{H}({\bf e}_{j})
.

Example 5.11
.

Suppose that
U=\mathcal{F}
is the Fourier-Plancherel transform on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
from (
2.16
). As discussed in Example
2.15
,

L^{2}(\mathbb{R})=\ker(\mathcal{F}-I)\oplus\ker(\mathcal{F}+I)\oplus\ker(\mathcal{F}-iI)\oplus\ker(\mathcal{F}+iI).

From (
2.17
) recall that

\displaystyle\ker(\mathcal{F}-I)

\displaystyle=\overline{\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}}\{H_{4n}:n\geqslant 0\},

\displaystyle\ker(\mathcal{F}+iI)

\displaystyle=\overline{\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}}\{H_{4n+1}:n\geqslant 0\},

\displaystyle\ker(\mathcal{F}+I)

\displaystyle=\overline{\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}}\{H_{4n+2}:n\geqslant 0\},

\displaystyle\ker(\mathcal{F}-iI)

\displaystyle=\overline{\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}}\{H_{4n+3}:n\geqslant 0\}.

Since
(H_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}
is already an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors for
\mathcal{F}
, then

L^{2}(\mathbb{R})=\bigoplus_{k\geqslant 0}\mathcal{H}(H_{k})
, where
\mathcal{H}(H_{k})=\mathbb{C}H_{k}
.

Example 5.12
.

Suppose that
U=\mathscr{H}
is the Hilbert transform on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
from (
2.20
). As with the previous example, using the orthonormal basis of eigenvectors
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
from Example
2.19
, we can write
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{H}(f_{n})
, where
\mathcal{H}(f_{n})=\mathbb{C}f_{n}
.

With these specific examples in mind, let us return to the general case and our decomposition from (
5.2
). For each
k\geqslant 1
, let

\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{k})_{\mathbb{R}}:=\overline{\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}}\{E(\Omega){\bf x}_{k}:\Omega\in\mathcal{B}\}.

(5.13)

This is the closed
real
linear span of the vectors
\{E(\Omega){\bf x}_{k}:\Omega\in\mathcal{B}\}
.
Notice that

\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{k})=\overline{\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}}\{E(\Omega){\bf x}_{k}:\Omega\in\mathcal{B}\},

defined earlier in (
5.3
), is the closed
the
complex
linear span. Take note of the subscripts
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
in the above.

Observe that for any
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
we can use standard facts about the orthogonal projections
E(\Omega)
(they are selfadjoint and idempotent) to see that

\displaystyle\langle a_{1}E(\Omega_{1}){\bf x}_{k},a_{2}E(\Omega_{2}){\bf x}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=a_{1}a_{2}\langle E(\Omega_{1}){\bf x}_{k},E(\Omega_{2}){\bf x}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=a_{1}a_{2}\langle E(\Omega_{2})E(\Omega_{1}){\bf x}_{k},{\bf x}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=a_{1}a_{2}\langle E(\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}){\bf x}_{k},{\bf x}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=a_{1}a_{2}\langle E(\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}){\bf x}_{k},E(\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}){\bf x}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=a_{1}a_{2}\|E(\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}){\bf x}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus, each
\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{k})_{\mathbb{R}}
is a
real
Hilbert space (a vector space with
\mathbb{R}
as the base field) with
real
inner product. In other words,

\langle{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle\in\mathbb{R}\;\;\mbox{for every}\;\;{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{k})_{\mathbb{R}}.

(5.14)

Now define

\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}:=\bigoplus_{k\geqslant 1}\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{k})_{\mathbb{R}}

(5.15)

and notice that since
\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{j})_{\mathbb{R}}\perp\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{k})_{\mathbb{R}}
when
j\not=k
, we conclude that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
is a real Hilbert space with real inner product

\langle{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle=\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}a_{\ell}b_{\ell},

(5.16)

where
({\bf u}_{\ell})_{\ell\geqslant 1}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}
with

{\bf u}_{\ell}\in\overline{\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}}\{E(\Omega){\bf x}_{k}:\Omega\in\mathcal{B}\}

(5.17)

for some
k
(this is possible by (
5.13
)) and, of course,

{\bf x}=\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}a_{\ell}{\bf u}_{\ell},\quad{\bf y}=\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}b_{\ell}{\bf u}_{\ell},\quad a_{\ell},b_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}.

Example 5.18
.

Let us continue our discussion from Example
5.8
, where the unitary operator
(Uf)(\xi)=\xi f(\xi)
is the bilateral shift on

L^{2}(m)=\overline{\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}}\{\chi_{\Omega}:\Omega\in\mathcal{B}\}
and so

L^{2}(m)_{\mathbb{R}}=\overline{\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}}\{\chi_{\Omega}:\Omega\in\mathcal{B}\}=L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(m),

the real-valued functions from
L^{2}(m)
.

Example 5.19
.

Continuing with Example
5.5
, suppose that

U=\operatorname{diag}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3},\ldots)
is a diagonal unitary operator on
\ell^{2}
with
\xi_{j}\not=\xi_{k}
for all

. If

with

for all

and thus

in other words, the real sequences in

were chosen so that the entries of

were all nonzero,
but not necessarily real

.

5.2
A complex Hilbert space from a unitary operator

For a fixed unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
, we define the
real
vector space
\mathfrak{R}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
to be the collection of ordered pairs of vectors

\mathfrak{R}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}):=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}:{\bf h}_{r},{\bf h}_{c}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}\right\}.

Addition of vectors and multiplication of vectors by
real
scalars in
\mathfrak{R}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
are defined componentwise in the obvious way as

\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}{\bf k}_{r}\\
{\bf k}_{c}\end{bmatrix}:=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}+{\bf k}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}+{\bf k}_{c}\end{bmatrix}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;a\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}:=\begin{bmatrix}a{\bf h}_{r}\\
a{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix},\quad a\in\mathbb{R}.

One can make
\mathfrak{R}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
a
complex
vector space, denoted by
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
, by applying a well-known procedure for complexification and
defining
multiplication by a complex scalar
s+it
to be

(s+it)\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}:=\begin{bmatrix}s{\bf h}_{r}-t{\bf h}_{c}\\
s{\bf h}_{c}+t{\bf h}_{r}\end{bmatrix}.

(5.23)

Informally, we think of multiplying out the expression
(s+it)({\bf h}_{r}+i{\bf h}_{c})
in the standard way to get

(s{\bf h}_{r}-t{\bf h}_{c})+i(s{\bf h}_{c}+t{\bf h}_{r})
and then equating the “real” component
s{\bf h}_{r}-t{\bf h}_{c}
with the first entry of the vector and the “imaginary” component
s{\bf h}_{c}+t{\bf h}_{r}
with the second entry.
One can check that all the necessary axioms of a complex vector space hold for
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
.

It is important to remark here that the two vector spaces
\mathfrak{R}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
and
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
consist of the same vectors. The difference is their vector space structure. The first allows only scalar multiplication by real numbers while the second allows scalar multiplication by complex numbers.

Now we make the complex vector space
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
a complex
Hilbert
space by defining the inner product on
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
to be

\Big\langle\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}{\bf g}_{r}\\
{\bf g}_{c}\end{bmatrix}\Big\rangle_{\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})}:=\langle{\bf h}_{r},{\bf g}_{r}\rangle+\langle{\bf h}_{c},{\bf g}_{c}\rangle+i\big(\langle{\bf h}_{c},{\bf g}_{r}\rangle-\langle{\bf h}_{r},{\bf g}_{c}\rangle\big).

Notice that

\Big\langle\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf 0}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}{\bf g}_{r}\\
{\bf 0}\end{bmatrix}\Big\rangle_{\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})}=\langle{\bf h}_{r},{\bf g}_{r}\rangle

and so this inner product extends the real inner product on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
to the complex inner product space
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
. Also observe that

\displaystyle\Big\|\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}\Big\|_{\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}\Big\rangle_{\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})}

\displaystyle=\langle{\bf h}_{r},{\bf h}_{r}\rangle+\langle{\bf h}_{c},{\bf h}_{c}\rangle+i\big(\langle{\bf h}_{c},{\bf h}_{r}\rangle-\langle{\bf h}_{r},{\bf h}_{c}\rangle\big).

As mentioned earlier,
\langle{\bf h}_{c},{\bf h}_{r}\rangle
and
\langle{\bf h}_{r},{\bf h}_{c}\rangle
are both
real
(since
{\bf h}_{r}
and
{\bf h}_{c}
belong to
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
) and so the imaginary term above drops out (recall (
5.14
) and (
5.16
)). Thus,

\left\|\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})}^{2}=\|{\bf h}_{r}\|^{2}+\|{\bf h}_{c}\|^{2}.

Example 5.24
.

With Example
5.20
, where
U
is an
n\times n
diagonal unitary matrix with distinct entries, we know that

\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{C}^{n}_{\mathbb{R}})=\mathbb{R}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{n}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}:{\bf a},{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}

endowed with the complex linear structure

(s+it)\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}s{\bf a}-t{\bf b}\\
s{\bf b}+t{\bf a}\end{bmatrix}.

Moreover,

\left\|\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}\right\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{C}^{n}_{\mathbb{R}})}=\|{\bf a}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}^{2}+\|{\bf b}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}^{2}=\|{\bf a}+i{\bf b}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}

(since
{\bf a}
and
{\bf b}
belong to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) and so
\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{C}^{n}_{\mathbb{R}})
is isometrically isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. More about this below.

Example 5.25
.

From Example
5.18
, where
(Uf)(\xi)=\xi f(\xi)
is the bilateral shift on
L^{2}(m)
, we have that

\mathfrak{C}(L^{2}(m)_{\mathbb{R}})=L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(m)\oplus L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(m)=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}f\\
g\end{bmatrix}:f,g\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(m)\right\}

with the complex linear structure

(s+it)\begin{bmatrix}f\\
g\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}sf-tg\\
sg+tf\end{bmatrix}.

Since

\left\|\begin{bmatrix}f\\
g\end{bmatrix}\right\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{C}(L^{2}(m)_{\mathbb{R}})}=\|f\|_{L^{2}(m)}^{2}+\|g\|_{L^{2}(m)}^{2}=\|f+ig\|^{2}_{L^{2}(m)}

(
f
and
g
are real-valued), we see that
\mathfrak{C}(L^{2}(m)_{\mathbb{R}})
is isometrically isomorphic to
L^{2}(m)
. Again, more about this below.

Example 5.26
.

Suppose
U
is the Fourier transform on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. As with the previous example, along with Example
5.21
, we see in this case that

\mathfrak{C}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R})_{\mathbb{R}})=L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R})\oplus L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R})=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}f\\
g\end{bmatrix}:f,g\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R})\right\}

and
\mathfrak{C}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R})_{\mathbb{R}})
is isometrically isomorphic to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Example 5.27
.

Returning to our discussion in Example
5.19
, suppose that

U=\operatorname{diag}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3},\ldots)

is a diagonal unitary operator on
\ell^{2}
with distinct
\xi_{j}
. Choosing an
{\bf x}\in\ell^{2}
with
real
entries and such that
\mathcal{H}({\bf x})=\ell^{2}
, we see, as with the two previous examples, that

\mathfrak{C}(\ell^{2}_{\mathbb{R}})=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}:{\bf a},{\bf b}\in\ell^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}\right\}

and

\mathfrak{C}(\ell^{2}_{\mathbb{R}})
is isometrically isomorphic to
\ell^{2}
.

Example 5.28
.

Suppose
U
is the Hilbert transform on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. In this case, see Example
5.22
,
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})_{\mathbb{R}}
is the closed real linear span of the complex-valued functions
f_{n},n\in\mathbb{Z}
, and

\mathfrak{C}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R})_{\mathbb{R}})=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}f\\
g\end{bmatrix}:f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})_{\mathbb{R}}\right\}.

Returning to the general situation, we need to relate our original complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with our new complex Hilbert space
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
. We have informally observed this in a few of the previous examples. For the orthonormal basis
({\bf u}_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
for
\mathcal{H}
from the previous section (with each
{\bf u}_{\ell}\in\overline{\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}}\{E(\Omega){\bf x}_{k}:\Omega\in\mathcal{B}\}
for some
k
), define

X:\mathcal{H}\to\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}),\quad X\Big(\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}(\alpha_{\ell}+i\beta_{\ell}){\bf u}_{\ell}\Big):=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}\alpha_{\ell}{\bf u}_{\ell}\\
\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}\beta_{\ell}{\bf u}_{\ell}\end{bmatrix},\quad\alpha_{\ell},\beta_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}.

(5.29)

The above map
X
is invertible since if

{\bf h}_{r}:=\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}\alpha_{\ell}{\bf u}_{\ell}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;{\bf h}_{c}:=\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}\beta_{\ell}{\bf u}_{\ell},

where
\alpha_{\ell}
and
\beta_{\ell}
are real, then

{\bf h}_{r}+i{\bf h}_{c}
is a generic element of
\mathcal{H}
and

X^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}={\bf h}_{r}+i{\bf h}_{c}.

We now want to show that
X
is an isometric isomorphism between the
complex
Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
.

Clearly the map
X
is
real
linear in that
X(a{\bf h}+{\bf k})=aX{\bf h}+X{\bf k}
when
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
{\bf h},{\bf k}\in\mathcal{H}
. To show
complex
linearity, observe that if
{\bf h}={\bf h}_{r}+i{\bf h}_{c}
, then

\displaystyle X((s+it){\bf h})

\displaystyle=X\Big((s+it)\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}(\alpha_{\ell}+i\beta_{\ell}){\bf u}_{\ell}\Big)

\displaystyle=X\Big(\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}((s\alpha_{\ell}-t\beta_{\ell})+i(s\beta_{\ell}+t\alpha_{\ell})){\bf u}_{\ell}\Big)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}(s\alpha_{\ell}-t\beta_{\ell}){\bf u}_{\ell}\\
\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}(s\beta_{\ell}+t\alpha_{\ell}){\bf u}_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}s\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}\alpha_{\ell}{\bf u}_{\ell}-t\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}\beta_{\ell}{\bf u}_{\ell}\\
s\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}\beta_{\ell}{\bf u}_{\ell}+t\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}\alpha_{\ell}{\bf u}_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=(s+it)\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}\alpha_{\ell}{\bf u}_{\ell}\\
\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}\beta_{\ell}{\bf u}_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=(s+it)\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=(s+it)X({\bf h}).

Next we want to show that
X
preserves the Hilbert space structure of
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
. Indeed, if

{\bf h}={\bf h}_{r}+i{\bf h}_{c}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;{\bf g}={\bf g}_{r}+i{\bf g}_{c},

then

\displaystyle\langle X({\bf h}),X({\bf g})\rangle_{\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})}

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}{\bf g}_{r}\\
{\bf g}_{c}\end{bmatrix}\Big\rangle_{\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})}

\displaystyle=\langle{\bf h}_{r},{\bf g}_{r}\rangle+\langle{\bf h}_{c},{\bf g}_{c}\rangle+i(\langle{\bf h}_{c},{\bf g}_{r}\rangle-\langle{\bf h}_{r},{\bf g}_{c}\rangle)

\displaystyle=\langle{\bf h}_{r},{\bf g}_{r}\rangle+\langle{\bf h}_{c},{\bf g}_{c}\rangle+\langle i{\bf h}_{c},{\bf g}_{r}\rangle+\langle{\bf h}_{r},i{\bf g}_{c}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\bf h}_{r}+i{\bf h}_{c},{\bf g}_{r}+i{\bf g}_{c}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\bf h},{\bf g}\rangle.

In summary,
X
is an isometric isomorphism between the complex Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
.

Chapter 6
A Model for the Orbit

Continuing our discussion from the previous chapter, we now represent a unitary operator as a matrix of real linear operators on the real Hilbert spaces constructed in the previous chapter. We first need to define what we mean by the term
real linear
as well as the associated adjoint of a real linear operator. This discussion will yield a model for a description of the conjugate orbit of a given unitary operator.

6.1
Types of linearity

Parts of the discussion below can be found in
[
22
]
. For a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
,
a mapping
A:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is
\mathbb{C}
-linear
(or
complex linear
) if

A({\bf x}+{\bf y})=A{\bf x}+A{\bf y}\;\mbox{and}\;A(\lambda{\bf x})=\lambda A{\bf x}\;\;\mbox{for all ${\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$};

\mathbb{R}
-linear
(or
real linear
) if

A({\bf x}+{\bf y})=A{\bf x}+A{\bf y}\;\mbox{and}\;A(\lambda{\bf x})=\lambda A{\bf x}\;\;\mbox{for all ${\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$};

and
antilinear
if

A({\bf x}+{\bf y})=A{\bf x}+A{\bf y}\;\mbox{and}\;A(\lambda{\bf x})=\bar{\lambda}A{\bf x}\;\;\mbox{for all ${\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$}.

When a complex linear transformation
A
is bounded, its (complex linear) adjoint is the familiar complex linear transformation
A^{*}
on
\mathcal{H}
satisfying
\langle A{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle=\langle{\bf x},A^{*}{\bf y}\rangle
for all
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}
. The existence of
A^{*}
comes from the Riesz representation theorem for Hilbert spaces.

For a bounded
antilinear
operator
A
on
\mathcal{H}
, its adjoint
A^{\star}
is the antilinear operator on
\mathcal{H}
that satisfies

\langle A{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle=\overline{\langle{\bf x},A^{\star}{\bf y}\rangle}\;\mbox{for all ${\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}$.}

(6.1)

In the above, take special note of the
\star
versus the
\ast
superscript on
A
. The existence of
A^{\star}
also comes from the Riesz representation theorem for Hilbert spaces.

Notice that every real linear operator
A
on
\mathcal{H}
can be written as

A=\tfrac{1}{2}(A-iAiI)+\tfrac{1}{2}(A+iAiI)=:A_{c}+A_{a}

(6.2)

and one can check that the first summand
A_{c}
is complex linear while the second
A_{a}
is antilinear. For a bounded real linear operator
A
on
\mathcal{H}
, its
real linear adjoint

A^{\dagger}
is defined to be

A^{\dagger}:=A_{c}^{*}+A_{a}^{\star}.

(6.3)

Note that the real adjoint
A^{\dagger}
of
A
satisfies
\Re\langle A{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle=\Re\langle{\bf x},A^{\dagger}{\bf y}\rangle
for all
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}
. As this will be used later, we record the following remark.

Remark 6.4
.

The above defines a real linear adjoint for a real linear operator on a
complex
Hilbert space.
Suppose that
\mathcal{H}
is a
real
Hilbert space with
\langle{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
for all
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}
(for example, the space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
from (
5.15
)). Then, via the Riesz representation theorem, there is a bounded real linear operator
A^{\dagger}
on
\mathcal{H}
which satisfies

\langle A{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle=\langle{\bf x},A^{\dagger}{\bf y}\rangle\;\mbox{for all ${\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}$}.

6.2
A representation of a unitary operator

For our given unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
and our isometric isomorphism
X:\mathcal{H}\to\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
from (
5.29
), we need to compute
XUX^{-1}
as a complex linear operator on
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
. Let

Q_{\mathbb{R}}
denote the mapping from
\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

Q_{\mathbb{R}}({\bf h}_{r}+i{\bf h}_{c}):={\bf h}_{r}

(6.5)

and note that if
{\bf h}={\bf h}_{r}+i{\bf h}_{c}
, then

{\bf h}=Q_{\mathbb{R}}{\bf h}-iQ_{\mathbb{R}}(i{\bf h}).

(6.6)

It is important to observe that
Q_{\mathbb{R}}
is real linear
but not necessarily complex linear
.

Now define the mappinngs
U_{r}
and
U_{c}
on the real Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
by

U_{r},U_{c}\colon\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}},\quad U_{r}{\bf h}_{r}:=Q_{\mathbb{R}}U{\bf h}_{r},\quad U_{c}{\bf h}_{r}:=-Q_{\mathbb{R}}(iU({\bf h}_{r}))

(6.7)

and note that
U_{r}
and
U_{c}
are
real linear
operators.
From (
6.6
) we see that

\displaystyle XUX^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=XU({\bf h}_{r}+i{\bf h}_{c})

\displaystyle=X(U{\bf h}_{r}+iU{\bf h}_{c})

\displaystyle=X\left(Q_{\mathbb{R}}(U{\bf h}_{r}+U(i{\bf h}_{c}))-iQ_{\mathbb{R}}(i(U{\bf h}_{r}+iU({\bf h}_{c}))\right)

\displaystyle=X((Q_{\mathbb{R}}U{\bf h}_{r}+Q_{\mathbb{R}}U(i{\bf h}_{c}))+i(-Q_{\mathbb{R}}U(i{\bf h}_{r})+Q_{\mathbb{R}}U{\bf h}_{c}))

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}Q_{\mathbb{R}}U{\bf h}_{r}+Q_{\mathbb{R}}U(i{\bf h}_{c})\\
-Q_{\mathbb{R}}U(i{\bf h}_{r})+Q_{\mathbb{R}}U{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}U_{r}{\bf h}_{r}-U_{c}{\bf h}_{c}\\
U_{c}{\bf h}_{r}+U_{r}{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}U_{r}&-U_{c}\\
U_{c}&\phantom{-}U_{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}.

The discussion above shows that the map
\widehat{U}
on
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
defined by

\widehat{U}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}:=\begin{bmatrix}U_{r}&-U_{c}\\
U_{c}&\phantom{-}U_{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}U_{r}{\bf h}_{r}-U_{c}{\bf h}_{c}\\
U_{c}{\bf h}_{r}+U_{r}{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}

is
\mathbb{C}
-linear, due to the fact that
X
,
U
, and
X^{-1}
are
\mathbb{C}
-linear maps. In addition, since
X
is an isometric isomorphism, we conclude the following.

Theorem 6.8
.

The unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
is unitarily equivalent to the operator

\widehat{U}:=\begin{bmatrix}U_{r}&-U_{c}\\
U_{c}&\phantom{-}U_{r}\end{bmatrix},

where
U_{r}
and
U_{c}
are given by (
6.7
),
on
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
.

Let us make a few observations about the real linear operators
U_{r}
and
U_{c}
. We begin with a lemma from
[
18
, Lem. 4, Lem. 5]
. Since this reference is not readily available in English, we include its short proof.

Lemma 6.9
.

For a unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
with

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{k\geqslant 1}\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{k})\;\mbox{and}\;\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}=\bigoplus_{k\geqslant 1}\mathcal{H}({\bf x}_{k})_{\mathbb{R}}

as in (
5.2
) and (
5.15
), there is a conjugation
J
on
\mathcal{H}
such that

J{\bf x}={\bf x},\quad{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}},

(6.10)

and
JUJ=U^{*}
.

Proof.

Notice that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
is a real Hilbert space embedded in the complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Moreover, by (
5.17
),
({\bf u}_{\ell})_{\ell\geqslant 1}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}
with
({\bf u}_{\ell})_{\ell\geqslant 1}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
. Define a conjugation
J
on
\mathcal{H}
by
J{\bf u}_{\ell}={\bf u}_{\ell}
and extend antilinearly to
\mathcal{H}
by

J\Big(\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}a_{\ell}{\bf u}_{\ell}\Big):=\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}\bar{a}_{\ell}{\bf u}_{\ell}

and note that
J{\bf x}={\bf x}
for all
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Recall the spectral representation of
U
from (
5.1
), i.e.,

U=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\xi dE(\xi).

(6.11)

For any Borel set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{T}
, we have

JE(\Omega)J{\bf x}_{k}=JE(\Omega){\bf x}_{k}=E(\Omega){\bf x}_{k}

for all
k\geqslant 1
(recall that
E(\Omega){\bf x}_{k}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
from (
5.13
)) and
thus
JE(\Omega)J=E(\Omega)
on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
and thus on
\mathcal{H}
. Finally, from (
6.11
), we have

\displaystyle JUJ

\displaystyle=J\Big(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\xi dE(\xi)\Big)J

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\overline{\xi}(JdE(\xi)J)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\overline{\xi}dE(\xi)

\displaystyle=U^{*},

which proves the result.
∎

Proposition 6.12
.

If
\widehat{J}:=XJX^{-1}
, then
\widehat{J}
is a conjugation on
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
and

\widehat{J}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}{\bf h}_{r}\\
-{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}\;\;\mbox{for all}\;\;\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}).

Moreover,

\widehat{J}\widehat{U}\widehat{J}=\widehat{U^{*}}=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}U_{r}&U_{c}\\
-U_{c}&U_{r}\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

The fact that
\widehat{J}
is a conjugation follows from the fact that
J
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
and
X
is an isometric isomorphism from
\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
. Now observe that since
J{\bf h}_{r}={\bf h}_{r}
and
J{\bf h}_{c}={\bf h}_{c}
(see (
6.10
)) we have

\displaystyle\widehat{J}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=XJX^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=XJ({\bf h}_{r}+i{\bf h}_{c})

\displaystyle=X({\bf h}_{r}-i{\bf h}_{c})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}{\bf h}_{r}\\
-{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}.

Finally, by the calculation before Theorem
6.8
, we see that

\displaystyle\widehat{J}\widehat{U}\widehat{J}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\widehat{J}XUX^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}{\bf h}_{r}\\
-{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\widehat{J}XU({\bf h}_{r}-i{\bf h}_{c})

\displaystyle=\widehat{J}X(U{\bf h}_{r}-iU{\bf h}_{c})

\displaystyle=\widehat{J}\begin{bmatrix}U_{r}{\bf h}_{r}+U_{c}{\bf h}_{c}\\
U_{c}{\bf h}_{r}-U_{r}{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}U_{r}{\bf h}_{r}+U_{c}{\bf h}_{c}\\
U_{r}{\bf h}_{c}-U_{c}{\bf h}_{r}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}U_{r}&U_{c}\\
-U_{c}&U_{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}.

On the other hand, by Lemma
6.9
,

\displaystyle\widehat{J}\widehat{U}\widehat{J}

\displaystyle=(XJX^{-1})(XUX^{-1})(XJX^{-1})

\displaystyle=X(JUJ)X^{-1}

\displaystyle=XU^{*}X^{-1}

\displaystyle=\widehat{U^{*}},

which verifies the desired adjoint formula.
∎

We now want to compute
\widehat{U}^{*}
, the adjoint of the matrix operator
\widehat{U}
. There is a more general result one can state here. Indeed, suppose

\widehat{W}:=\begin{bmatrix}W_{rr}&W_{cr}\\
W_{rc}&W_{cc}\end{bmatrix},

where each of the entries
W_{rr},W_{cr},W_{rc},W_{cc}
are
real
linear operators on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
. Notice that
\widehat{W}
defines a real linear operator on
\mathfrak{R}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
by

\widehat{W}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}:=\begin{bmatrix}W_{rr}&W_{cr}\\
W_{rc}&Wcc\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}W_{rr}{\bf h}_{r}+W_{cr}{\bf h}_{c}\\
W_{rc}{\bf h}_{r}+W_{cc}{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}.

Lemma 6.13
.

The real linear operator

\widehat{W}=\begin{bmatrix}W_{rr}&W_{cr}\\
W_{rc}&W_{cc}\end{bmatrix}

on the real space
\mathfrak{R}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
is
\mathbb{C}
-linear on the complex space
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
if and only if
W_{rr}=W_{cc}
and
W_{cr}=-W_{rc}
.

Proof.

For

\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})

we use the definition of multiplication by a complex scalar in
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
from (
5.23
) to get

\displaystyle\widehat{W}\Big(i\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}\Big)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}W_{rr}&W_{cr}\\
W_{rc}&W_{cc}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-{\bf h}_{c}\\
\phantom{-}{\bf h}_{r}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-W_{rr}{\bf h}_{c}+W_{cr}{\bf h}_{r}\\
-W_{rc}{\bf h}_{c}+W_{cc}{\bf h}_{r}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=i\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}W_{cc}{\bf h}_{r}-W_{rc}{\bf h}_{c}\\
-W_{cr}{\bf h}_{r}+W_{rr}{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=i\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}W_{cc}&-W_{rc}\\
-W_{cr}&\phantom{-}W_{rr}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}.

On the other hand,

i\widehat{W}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}=i\begin{bmatrix}W_{rr}&W_{cr}\\
W_{rc}&W_{cc}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix}.

Comparing the matrix entries yields the result.
∎

For the entries
W_{rr},W_{cr},W_{rc},W_{cc}
, which define real linear operators on the real Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
, let
W_{rr}^{\dagger}
,
W_{cr}^{\dagger}
,
W_{rc}^{\dagger}
,
W_{cc}^{\dagger}
denote their respective
real adjoints
from Remark
6.4
in that
\langle W{\bf h},{\bf k}\rangle=\langle{\bf h},W^{\dagger}{\bf k}\rangle
for all
{\bf h},{\bf k}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Lemma 6.14
.

Suppose

\widehat{W}=\begin{bmatrix}W_{rr}&-W_{rc}\\
W_{rc}&\phantom{-}W_{rr}\end{bmatrix}

is
\mathbb{C}
-linear on a complex space
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
. Then the formula for its adjoint is

\widehat{W}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}W_{rr}^{\dagger}&W^{\dagger}_{rc}\\
-W^{\dagger}_{rc}&W_{rr}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

Let

\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}{\bf g}_{r}\\
{\bf g}_{c}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}).

Then

\Big\langle\widehat{W}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}{\bf g}_{r}\\
{\bf g}_{c}\end{bmatrix}\Big\rangle=\Big\langle\begin{bmatrix}W_{rr}&-W_{cc}\\
W_{rc}&\phantom{-}W_{rr}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}{\bf g}_{r}\\
{\bf g}_{c}\end{bmatrix}\Big\rangle.

We leave it to the reader to perform the necessary matrix multiplication and apply the definition of the inner product in
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
, along with the definition of the real adjoint, to see this becomes

\Big\langle\begin{bmatrix}{\bf h}_{r}\\
{\bf h}_{c}\end{bmatrix},\left[\!{\begin{array}[]{cc}\phantom{-}W_{rr}^{\dagger}&W_{rc}^{\dagger}\\
-W_{rc}^{\dagger}&W_{rr}^{\dagger}\end{array}}\!\right]\left[\!{\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf g}_{r}\\
{\bf g}_{c}\end{array}}\!\right]\Big\rangle,

which verifies the adjoint formula.
∎

For this next technical lemma, recall the conjugation
J
on
\mathcal{H}
from Lemma
6.9
and the associated conjugation
\widehat{J}
on
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
from Proposition
6.12
.

Lemma 6.15
.

Let

\widehat{W}=\begin{bmatrix}W_{rr}&-W_{rc}\\
W_{rc}&\phantom{-}W_{rr}\end{bmatrix}

be
\mathbb{C}
-linear as an operator in complex space
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
. The operator
\widehat{W}

is
\widehat{J}
symmetric, that is to say,
\widehat{J}\widehat{W}\widehat{J}=\widehat{W}^{*}
if and only if
W_{rr}^{\dagger}=W_{rr}
and
W_{rc}^{\dagger}=W_{rc}
.

Proof.

This involves a similar computation as we have done before so we leave it to reader to work through the details.
∎

We represented our general unitary
U
on
\mathcal{H}
by

\widehat{U}=\begin{bmatrix}U_{r}&-U_{c}\\
U_{c}&\phantom{-}U_{r}\end{bmatrix}

on
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
. From our discussion above we know that

\widehat{U^{*}}=\widehat{U}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}U_{r}&U_{c}\\
-U_{c}&U_{r}\end{bmatrix}

and we emphasize that
U_{r}^{\dagger}=U_{r}
and
U_{c}^{\dagger}=U_{c}
. Using the fact that
\widehat{U}
is unitary, we see that

U_{r}^{2}+U_{c}^{2}=I\quad\mbox{and}\quad U_{r}U_{c}=U_{c}U_{r}.

6.3
Revisiting some of our previous examples

Example 6.16
.

Returning to Example
5.24
with

U=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{j}({\bf e}_{j}\otimes{\bf e}_{j}),

an
n\times n
diagonal unitary matrix with distinct eigenvalues
(\xi_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
,
we know that

\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{C}^{n}_{\mathbb{R}})=\mathbb{R}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{n}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}:{\bf a},{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}.

Also observe from (
6.5
) that

Q_{\mathbb{R}}({\bf a}+i{\bf b})={\bf a}=\Re({\bf a}+i{\bf b}).

Since
{\bf a}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}{\bf e}_{j}
, it follows that

\displaystyle U_{r}{\bf a}

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{j}\langle{\bf a},{\bf e}_{j}\rangle{\bf e}_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\xi_{j}a_{j}){\bf e}_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Re(\xi_{j}a_{j}){\bf e}_{j}

\displaystyle=\Re U{\bf a},

and, in a similar way,

U_{c}{\bf b}=\Im U{\bf b}.

Putting this all together yields

\displaystyle\widehat{U}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}U_{r}&-U_{c}\\
U_{c}&\phantom{-}U_{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Re U{\bf a}\\
\Re U{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}-\Im U{\bf b}\\
\phantom{-}\Im U{\bf a}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\phantom{-}\Re\begin{bmatrix}U{\bf a}\\
U{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}+i\Im\begin{bmatrix}U{\bf a}\\
U{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}.

From here one can see that
\widehat{U}
is unitarily equivalent to
U
.

Example 6.17
.

Let us compute the operator matrix
\widehat{U}
when
(Uf)(\xi)=\xi f(\xi)
is the bilateral shift on
L^{2}(m)
from Example
5.25
. From that discussion we know that

\mathfrak{C}(L^{2}(m)_{\mathbb{R}})=L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(m)\oplus L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(m),

where
L^{2}_{R}(m)
are the real-valued
L^{2}(m)
functions.
From (
6.5
) we have, for
f,g\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(m)
,

Q_{\mathbb{R}}(f+ig)=f=\Re(f+ig).

For
f\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(m)
, we can use (
6.7
) to see that

\displaystyle U_{r}f

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathbb{R}}U|_{L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(m)}f

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathbb{R}}((x+iy)f)

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathbb{R}}(xf+iyf)

\displaystyle=xf.

Thus,
U_{r}=M_{x}
(multiplication by
x
) on
L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(m)
. In a similar way, observe that

\displaystyle U_{c}f

\displaystyle=-Q_{\mathbb{R}}((x+iy)(if)

\displaystyle=-Q_{\mathbb{R}}(ixf-yf)

\displaystyle=yf.

Thus,
U_{c}=M_{y}
(multiplication by
y
) on
L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(m)
. In summary,

\widehat{U}=\begin{bmatrix}U_{r}&-U_{c}\\
U_{c}&\phantom{-}U_{r}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}M_{x}&-M_{y}\\
M_{y}&\phantom{-}M_{x}\end{bmatrix}.

For
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(m)
and unimodular, the above discussion yields

\widehat{M_{\varphi}}=\begin{bmatrix}M_{\Re\varphi}&-M_{\Im\varphi}\\
M_{\Im\varphi}&\phantom{-}M_{\Re\varphi}\end{bmatrix}.

Example 6.18
.

Let us return to our discussion of the Fourier–Plancherel transform
\mathcal{F}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
from Example
2.15
and Example
5.26
. From (
2.18
) recall that

\mathcal{F}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-i)^{n}(H_{n}\otimes H_{n})

and that each Hermite function
H_{n}
is real-valued. From the discussion in Example
5.26
we know that

\mathfrak{C}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R})_{\mathbb{R}})=L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R})\oplus L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R}).

With

h_{r}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}H_{n}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;h_{c}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n}H_{n},

where
(\alpha_{n})_{n\geqslant 0},(\beta_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
are real
\ell^{2}
sequences, we see from (
6.7
) that

\displaystyle U_{r}h_{r}

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{F}\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}H_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}(-i)^{n}H_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{2n}(-1)^{n}H_{2n}.

In a similar way,

\displaystyle U_{c}h_{c}

\displaystyle=-Q_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{F}(ih_{c})

\displaystyle=-Q_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{F}\Big(i\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n}H_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=-Q_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(i\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(-i)^{n}H_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(-i)^{n+1}H_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{2n+1}(-1)^{n}H_{2n+1}.

Thus, the representation
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}
on
\mathfrak{C}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R})_{\mathbb{R}})
becomes

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{F}}\begin{bmatrix}h_{r}\\
h_{c}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}U_{r}&-U_{c}\\
U_{c}&\phantom{-}U_{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\alpha_{n}H_{n}\\
\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\beta_{n}H_{n}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{n\geqslant 0}(\alpha_{2n}(-1)^{n}H_{2n}-\beta_{2n+1}(-1)^{n}H_{2n+1})\\
\sum_{n\geqslant 0}(\alpha_{2n+1}(-1)^{n}H_{2n+1}+\beta_{2n}(-1)^{n}H_{2n})\end{bmatrix}.

Example 6.19
.

Let us return to our discussion of the Hilbert transform
\mathscr{H}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
from Example
2.19
and Example
5.28
. From (
2.21
) we know that

\mathscr{H}=\sum_{n<0}i(f_{n}\otimes f_{n})+\sum_{n\geqslant 0}(-i)(f_{n}\otimes f_{n}).

Thus, if

h_{r}=\sum_{n<0}\alpha_{n}f_{n}+\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\alpha_{n}f_{n}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;h_{c}=\sum_{n<0}\beta_{n}f_{n}+\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\beta_{n}f_{n},

where
(\alpha_{n})_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
and
(\beta_{n})_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
are real
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
sequences,
we see that

\displaystyle U_{r}h_{r}

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{H}\Big(\sum_{n<0}\alpha_{n}f_{n}+\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\alpha_{n}f_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\sum_{n<0}i\alpha_{n}f_{n}+\sum_{n\geqslant 0}(-i)\alpha_{n}f_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=0.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle U_{c}h_{c}

\displaystyle=-Q_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{H}(ih_{c})

\displaystyle=-Q_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{H}\Big(\sum_{n<0}i\beta_{n}f_{n}+\sum_{n\geqslant 0}i\beta_{n}f_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=-Q_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\sum_{n<0}i(i)\beta_{n}f_{n}+\sum_{n\geqslant 0}(-i)(i)\beta_{n}f_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=-Q_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\sum_{n<0}-\beta_{n}f_{n}+\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\beta_{n}f_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n<0}\beta_{n}f_{n}-\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\beta_{n}f_{n}.

Thus, the representation
\widehat{\mathscr{H}}
of
\mathscr{H}
on
\mathfrak{C}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R})_{\mathbb{R}})
becomes

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathscr{H}}\begin{bmatrix}h_{r}\\
h_{c}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}U_{r}&-U_{c}\\
U_{c}&\phantom{-}U_{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}h_{r}\\
h_{c}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&-U_{c}\\
U_{c}&\phantom{-}0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}h_{r}\\
h_{c}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-U_{c}h_{c}\\
\phantom{-}U_{c}h_{r}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-\sum_{n<0}\beta_{n}f_{n}+\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\beta_{n}f_{n}\\
\phantom{-}\sum_{n<0}\alpha_{n}f_{n}-\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\alpha_{n}f_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

6.4
A Description of the Orbit

Using our model
\widehat{U}
on
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
for a unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
from the previous section, we have the following description of
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
.

Theorem 6.20
.

Let
U
be a fixed unitary operator on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. For a unitary operator
V
on
\mathcal{H}
, the following are equivalent.

(a)

V\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
.

(b)

V=WU^{*}W^{*}
, where

\widehat{W}=\begin{bmatrix}W_{r}&-W_{c}\\
W_{c}&\phantom{-}W_{r}\end{bmatrix},

where
W_{r},W_{c}
are real linear operators on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying

W_{r}^{\dagger}=W_{r},\;W_{c}^{\dagger}=W_{c},\;W_{r}W_{c}=W_{c}W_{r},\;W_{r}^{2}+W_{c}^{2}=I.

(6.21)

(c)

There are real linear operators
W_{r},W_{c}
on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying the conditions in (
6.21
) such that the operator
\widehat{V}
on
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
satisfies

\widehat{V}=\widehat{W}\widehat{U}^{*}\widehat{W}^{*},
where

\widehat{W}=\begin{bmatrix}W_{r}&-W_{c}\\
W_{c}&\phantom{-}W_{r}\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

(a)\Longrightarrow(b)
: Recall the conjugation
J
on
\mathcal{H}
from Lemma
6.9
which fixes every element of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
and satisfies
JUJ=U^{*}
. If
V\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
, then Proposition
2.7
says there is a unitary operator
W
on
\mathcal{H}
with
JWJ=W^{*}
and
V=WU^{*}W^{*}
. Now apply the results from the previous section to obtain (b).

(b)\Longrightarrow(a)
: The conditions on
\widehat{W}
say that
\widehat{W}
is unitary and
\widehat{J}
symmetric. Via the isometric isomorphism
X
from (
5.29
), this means that
W
is unitary and
JWJ=W^{*}
. Proposition
2.7
now completes the proof.

(b)\iff(c)
is clear.
∎

Remark 6.22
.

Using (c) from the previous theorem and setting
W_{r}=I
and
W_{c}=0
, we see that
U^{*}\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
, which is confirmed by Proposition
2.1
(see also Remark
2.9
and Remark
6.22
).

Example 6.23
.

Let us continue the discussion from Example
6.16
, where
U
is a diagonal
n\times n
unitary matrix with distinct eigenvalues. In that example, we showed that

\widehat{U}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}U_{r}&-U_{c}\\
U_{c}&\phantom{-}U_{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{bmatrix},

where
U_{r}{\bf a}=\Re(U{\bf a})
and
U_{c}{\bf b}=\Im(U{\bf b})
for
{\bf a},{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Suppose
X
is an
n\times n
self transpose unitary matrix with
real
entries (for example,
X=I-2({\bf x}\otimes{\bf x})
and
{\bf x}
is a unit vector from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
– a Householder matrix). If
W_{r}{\bf a}:=X{\bf a}
, then, since
X^{2}=I
and
X^{t}=X
, we see that the real linear operator
W_{r}
satisfies
W_{r}^{2}=I
and
W_{r}^{\dagger}=W_{r}
. Then, with
W_{c}=0
,

\begin{bmatrix}W_{r}&0\\
0&W_{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}U_{r}&U_{c}\\
-U_{c}&U_{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}W_{r}&0\\
0&W_{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}

is equal to

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}\Re(XUX{\bf a})+\Im(XUX{\bf b})\\
-\Im(XUX{\bf a})+\Re(XUX{\bf b})\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\Re\begin{bmatrix}XUX{\bf a}\\
XUX{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}+\Im\begin{bmatrix}XUX{\bf b}\\
-XUX{\bf a}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\Re\begin{bmatrix}XUX{\bf a}\\
XUX{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}-i\Im\begin{bmatrix}XUX{\bf a}\\
XUX{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\widehat{X\overline{U}X}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus,
X\overline{U}X\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
, which is rediscovery of Proposition
3.4
.

Example 6.24
.

For the bilateral shift
(Uf)(\xi)=\xi f(\xi)
on
L^{2}(m)
, we know from Example
6.17
that

\widehat{U}=\begin{bmatrix}M_{x}&-M_{y}\\
M_{y}&\phantom{-}M_{x}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus, viewing everything in the
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
setting, we see that the conjugate orbit of
U
is the collection of operators

\begin{bmatrix}W_{r}&-W_{c}\\
W_{c}&\phantom{-}W_{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}M_{x}&M_{y}\\
-M_{y}&M_{x}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}W_{r}&W_{c}\\
-W_{c}&W_{r}\end{bmatrix},

where
W_{r},W_{c}
are real linear operators on
L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(m)
satisfying

W_{r}^{\dagger}=W_{r},\;W_{c}^{\dagger}=W_{c},\;W_{r}W_{c}=W_{c}W_{r},\;W_{r}^{2}+W_{c}^{2}=I.

To reconnect with some of our previous examples, consider the class of operators

\begin{bmatrix}W_{r}&0\\
0&W_{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}M_{x}&M_{y}\\
-M_{y}&M_{x}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}W_{r}&0\\
0&W_{r}\end{bmatrix},

where
(W_{r}f)(\xi)=f(\alpha(\xi))
on
L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(m)
and
\alpha
is a measure preserving transformation on
\mathbb{T}
for which
\alpha\circ\alpha=\operatorname{id}
. Examples include
\alpha(\xi)=\overline{\xi}
,
\alpha(\xi)=-\xi
, or any reflection of
\mathbb{T}
through a line passing through the origin. Then
W_{r}^{\dagger}=W_{r}
and
W_{r}^{2}=I
. In this case, the above matrix multiplication turns out to be the representation of the unitary multiplication operator

f\mapsto\bar{\alpha}f
from Example
4.11
.

In a similar way, one has the class of examples

\begin{bmatrix}0&-W_{c}\\
W_{c}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}M_{x}&M_{y}\\
-M_{y}&M_{x}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&W_{c}\\
-W_{c}&0\end{bmatrix},

and
W_{c}f=f\circ\alpha
as above. This will yield the same unitary operator as before.

Example 6.25
.

Inspired by Example
4.26
, let
\alpha:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}
be (Lebesgue) measure preserving with
\alpha\circ\alpha=\operatorname{id}
,
\beta:\mathbb{T}\to\{-1,1\}
be (Lebesgue) measurable such that
\beta\circ\alpha=\beta
, and
s,t\in\mathbb{R}

W_{r}f:=s(f\circ\alpha)\;\mbox{and}\;W_{c}f:=t\beta f,\quad f\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(m),

one can verify that
W_{r}^{\dagger}=W_{r},W_{c}^{\dagger}=W_{c},W_{r}W_{c}=W_{c}W_{r}
, and
W_{r}^{2}+W_{c}^{2}=I
. Moreover,

\begin{bmatrix}W_{r}&-W_{c}\\
W_{c}&\phantom{-}W_{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}M_{x}&M_{y}\\
-M_{y}&M_{x}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}W_{r}&W_{c}\\
-W_{c}&W_{r}\end{bmatrix},

turns out to be the representation of
the unitary operator

f\mapsto(s^{2}M_{\bar{\alpha}}+t^{2}M^{*})f+ist\beta(M_{\bar{\alpha}}-M^{*})(f\circ\alpha)

from
Example
4.26
.

Example 6.26
.

For the Fourier–Plancherel transform
\mathcal{F}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, where, from Example
6.18
the adjoint
\mathcal{F}^{*}
satisfies

\widehat{\mathcal{F^{*}}}\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\alpha_{n}H_{n}\\
\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\beta_{n}H_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{n\geqslant 0}(\alpha_{2n}(-1)^{n}H_{2n}+\beta_{2n+1}(-1)^{n}H_{2n+1})\\
\phantom{-}\sum_{n\geqslant 0}(-\alpha_{2n+1}(-1)^{n}H_{2n+1}+\beta_{2n}(-1)^{n}H_{2n})\end{bmatrix},

we can set
(W_{r}f)(x)=f(-x)
. Then, using the fact that the Hermite functions
H_{n}
are odd functions when
n
is odd and even functions when
n
is even, we see that

(W_{r}H_{n})(x)=(-1)^{n}H_{n}(x),

and a calculation reveals that

\begin{bmatrix}W_{r}&0\\
0&W_{r}\end{bmatrix}\widehat{\mathcal{F^{*}}}\begin{bmatrix}W_{r}&0\\
0&W_{r}\end{bmatrix}=\widehat{\mathcal{F^{*}}}.

In other words,
\mathcal{F}^{*}\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(\mathcal{F})
, which we know already from Proposition
2.1
.
Setting
(W_{c}f)(x)=-f(-x)
, a similar calculation yields

\begin{bmatrix}0&-W_{c}\\
W_{c}&0\end{bmatrix}\widehat{\mathcal{F^{*}}}\begin{bmatrix}0&W_{c}\\
-W_{c}&0\end{bmatrix}=\widehat{\mathcal{F}}

and so
\mathcal{F}\in\mathfrak{O}_{c}(\mathcal{F})
. This can be confirmed by the fact that
\mathcal{F}^{*}
is unitarily equivalent to
\mathcal{F}
(see the discussion in Example
2.16
along with Proposition
2.3
).

Example 6.27
.

For the Hilbert transform
\mathscr{H}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, where from Example
2.3
the adjoint
\mathscr{H}^{*}
satisfies

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathscr{H}^{*}}\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{n<0}\alpha_{n}f_{n}+\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\alpha_{n}f_{n}\\
\sum_{n<0}\beta_{n}f_{n}+\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\beta_{n}f_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}\sum_{n<0}\beta_{n}f_{n}-\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\beta_{n}f_{n}\\

we can set

to obtain

\begin{bmatrix}0&-W_{c}\\

\mathscr{H}^{*}=-\mathscr{H}

[
14

(W_{c}f)(x)=f(-x)

\begin{bmatrix}0&-W_{c}\\

Chapter 7
Generalizations for Future Study

For a fixed unitary operator
U
on a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, this paper focused on the collection of unitary operators
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)=\{CUC:\mbox{$C$ is a conjugation}\}
. One might also consider a study of
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(N)
, analogously defined, where
N
is a fixed normal operator on
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.,
N^{*}N=NN^{*}
. An analysis shows that
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(N)
is contained in the class of normal operators and some of the discussion in this paper carries over to this new class. An even broader investigation might consider
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(A)
, where
A
is
any
bounded linear operator on
\mathcal{H}
.

Still another line of exploration could be
\mathfrak{O}_{au}(A)
, the set
XAX^{\star}
, where
X
is antilinear and unitary
X^{-1}=X^{\star}
(
X^{\star}
is the antilinear conjugate of
X
from (
6.1
)). Notice we are not assuming
X^{2}=I
. Some call these
X

antilinear unitary operators
of which conjugations form a particular subclass. Perhaps call
\mathfrak{O}_{au}(A)
the
antilinear orbit
of
A
. When
U
is unitary, how does
\mathfrak{O}_{au}(U)
differ from the conjugate orbit
\mathfrak{O}_{c}(U)
considered in this paper? We invite the reader to join the discussion.
