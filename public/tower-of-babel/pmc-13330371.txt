Psychological recovery paths of players within a team: a decision tree-assisted longitudinal analysis
Authors: Şeval Kayğusuz, Cengiz Karagözoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to monitor the long term psychological recovery of team athletes after competitions. The second aim was to develop a model based on the primary recovery strategies employed by team athletes.MethodsThe study sample consisted of 38 male football and basketball players aged between 18 and 35 years (sd = 5.51), with an average of 15 years of experience in team sports. Data were collected by using the Sport Psychological Recovery Scale, the Checklist of Individual Strength (CIS) Scale, Emotional State Profile based on Individual Zone of Optimal Functioning (IZOF) methodology and athletes’ perceived physical fatigue, perceived mental fatigue, perceived competition success, perceived competition difficulty, and perceived sleep quality were assessed using Virtual Analog Scale (VAS). The collected data were analyzed through line graph visualizations, heat maps, hierarchical cluster analysis, and decision tree modeling.ResultsFindings from the line graphs revealed substantial individual differences in players’ preferences for psychological recovery strategies. Heatmap analyses demonstrated significant correlations between fatigue and recovery-related variables. The dendrogram analysis confirmed the existence of two distinct psychological recovery profiles, characterized as fatigue and recovery patterns. Finally, decision tree modeling identified the flourishing, as the most significant predictor of recovery strategy selection. The model identified a critical flourishing score of 7.19 out of 10. Athletes scoring below this threshold predominantly preferred mental recovery strategies, whereas those scoring above it primarily opted for psychological detachment strategies.ConclusionsFlourishing serves as a key determinant role in shaping athletes’ psychological recovery strategies. By employing decision tree and cluster-based modeling this study provides novel insights into the individualized dynamics of post-performance recovery. These findings advance the design of personalized psychological support programs for athletes, enhancing both recovery efficacy and long-term well being.

Introduction

Fatigue is broadly defined as a reduction in the capacity to perform physical and cognitive activities, poses a substantial challenge for athletes [1]. This phenomenon comprises both physical and mental dimensions, the latter of which significantly impacts cognitive functioning and emotional regulation [2]. Mental fatigue, for instance, can impair decision-making and attentional focus, highligting the critical role of mental recovery. This recovery aims to mitigate cognitive stress and cultivatemental clarity, thereby enabling athletes to process information effectively and sustain concentration under pressure [3]. Chronic exposure to high training loads without adequate recovery may lead to overtraining syndrome, manifesting performance decline, mood disturbances, and increased injury susceptibility [4]. To counter these risks, a multidimensional recovery framework, integrating physiological and psychological strategies is essential [5], such an approach should incorporate advanced monitoring tools to assess recovery status and optimize preparation [6], also diverse recovery modalities [7] tailored to individual athlete needs. A cornerstone of effective recovery is the restoration of vigor and energy, which reflects an athletes’s subjective readines and vitality. High vigor levels are associated with greater resilience to training stress and sustained performance capacity, underscoring their importance in long-term athletic development.

The rigorious demands of training and competition have elevated the importance of mental health and psychological recovery as critical determinants o long-term athletic success and well-being. The convergence of training regimens, competitive fixtures and travel obligations during the competition period exert substantial physical and mental strain on athletes [8]. Those demands, in conjunction with elevated levels of mental and physical loads, can precipitate fatigue [9, 10]. While physical recovery remains in priority for fatigue management, the role of psychological recovery in mitigating cumulative stressors including intensive training, competitive schedules and travel-related demands is gaining recognition [8]. Effective psychological recovery strategies not only reduce the risk of overtraining but also promote optimal performance outcomes by sustaining mental equilibrium [3]. For athletes, attaining this equilibrium is fundamental to achieving optimal performance [11]. Controversely, a failure to balance athletic demands with adequate rest and recovery, may lead to a number of negative outcomes including chronic fatigue, injury and even premature withdrawal from competing [12, 13].

There are many different approaches to define recovery. However, the fundamental premise of recovery is the restoration of resources to reestablish homeostasis and biorhythmic equilibrium [5]. The replenishment of these resources is crucial for enhancing performance. Additionally, regulating emotions, thoughts, and actions post-performance is of utmost importance [14, 15]. The bidirectional interplay between affect and performance is more comprehensively understood when emotional dynamics are examined longitudinally. In competitive contexts, emotions can be polarised, ranging from elation and satisfaction in the face of victory to despondency and disappointment in the face of defeat. It is a well-documented fact that affective states may exhibit considerable fluctuation throughout the duration of competition, a phenomenon that is particularly evident in endurance-based sports. Within this theoretical framework, fluctuations in emotional states and cognitive processes may, in turn, exert a significant influence on athletes’ physical performance [40]. Athletes extensively utilize their physical and mental resources during training or competition [16]. Recovery is increasingly viewed not as a passive phase but as an active, multifaceted process aimed at replenishing physical, cognitive, and emotional resources [14, 17]. Recovery is defined as a multidimensional process that occurs over time at both intra-individual and inter-individual levels and includes not only physiological but also psychological recovery [18]. The psychological aspect of recovery involves the improvement of subjective feelings, relaxation, enhanced well-being, and the restoration of a positive mood state [17]. The multidimensional definition, grounded in the IZOF framework, provides a combination of personal and task-related indicators to determine an athlete’s level of readiness for performance, whether high or low. These indicators are also employed to characterize functional and dysfunctional pre- and in-performance states and to evaluate adequate versus inadequate recovery. Consequently, this approach enables the precise identification and prevention of an athlete’s inability to recover from excessive training loads. Consequently, the prediction of optimal performance and the prevention of performance decrements should be based on the individualised monitoring of the pre-, during-, and post-performance states. This inference serves to highlight the functional emotional dynamics that reflect how athletes utilise and restore their resources within single or repeated load–recovery cycles [25]. Given the multifaceted nature of recovery, it is essential that the assessment of the recovery-fatigue continuum considers the various conditions that may impact an athlete’s ability to recover fully and be based on the specific demands of the sport in question [19].

The Sport Psychological Recovery Scale developed by Kayğusuz and Karagözoğlu (2023) [3] of comprises mental recovery, vigor and energy, psychological detachment and flourishing subscales. These dimensions play a pivotal role in determining athletes’ psychological recovery. Psychological detachment refers to an athlete’s ability to mentally disengage from sport-related stressors. Psychological detachment allows for a full mental reset, preventing the prolonged cognitive strain that often accompanies intensive training and competition schedules. Resting the mind is necessary to eliminate intrusive thoughts, alleviate impending stress, and restore depleted resources, a process achieved through mental recovery [5]. Athletes who can effectively detach from sport-related demands are better positioned to return to competition with renewed focus and restored energy. In this context, vitality and vigor a multidimensional construct reflectings perceptions of physical strength, emotional energy, and cognitive liveliness [20] has been shown to positively associate with athletic performance [21]. Given these links, essential flourishing is deemed necessary for athletes both in both preparing for and coping demanding sport experiences [22]. Flourishing encompasses improvements in mood and emotional stability, which are key components of a positive psychological state. Unaddressed emotional fluctuations, may reduce motivation and an increase burnout risk. Thus, flourishing supports athlete’s mental resilience and overall quality of life. These dimensions highlight the intricate interplay between sport’s physical demands and the psychological mechanisms underpinning recovery.

In light of the aforementioned factors, psychological recovery must be prioritized as a critical component of training to mitigate risks (e.g. burnout, injury) and optimize performance [23]. Kellmann et al. (2016) [24] conducted a longitudinal study that tracking recovery–stress balance across multiple time points and reveals dynamic fluctations over a competitive season, offering a nuanced understanding of athlete’s adaptive processes. This study aimed to monitor longitudinal changes in team-sport athletes’ psychological recovery, fatigue, competition related experiences, and emotional states across eight-week competitive period.

Method

Participants

The sample was selected using criterion sampling, a purposive sampling technic which participants are chosen based on predefined criteria relevant to the research objectives. Final sample consisted of 38 male team-sport athletes (13 basketball and 25 football players), aged 18–35 years (Mage = 25.3; SD = 5.51), with an avarage of 15 years’ of competitive experience.

Measures

As data collection tools, the Sport Psychological Recovery Scale (SPRS) developed by Kayğusuz and Karagözoğlu (2023) [3], the Emotional State Profile-40 (ESP-40, based on IZOF methodology) developed by Hanin (2002) [25] and adapted into Turkish by Ertürk (2013) [26], the Checklist Individual Strength (CIS) Scale, the validity and reliability of which were studied by Ergin et al. (2012) [27], and the Visual Analog Scales (VAS).

Sport Psychological Recovery Scale (SPRS) is a measurement tool that aims to predict the psychological recovery status of athletes, consisting of four subscales: mental recovery, vigor and energy, psychological detachment and flourishing. Mental recovery is defined as the restoration of cognitive functions, such as concentration and decision-making, to their baseline levels. The following example item is employed in the assessment of this construct: I almost burned out mentally. Vigor and energy are conceptualised as multidimensional constructs, encompassing physical strength, emotional vitality and cognitive alertness. Accordingly, an example item representing this dimension is provided: I feel my energy level is adequate for the next competition/training. Psychological detachment refers to the process by which athletes disengage from sports-related thoughts or concerns, fostering a sense of separation from training and competition: No matter what I try I can’t get rid of my mistakes of competition/training from my mind. In contrast to languishing, flourishing is characterised by the presence of positive emotions and optimal functioning in both psychological and social domains. In order to measure this aspect, the following sample item was included: I able to think more positively since the last competition/training. The Cronbach’s Alpha coefficients for the subdimensions were found to be 0.81 for mental recovery, 0.94 for vigor and energy, 0.83 for psychological detachment, and 0.93 for flourishing [3].

The ESP-40 was developed by Hanin to explain the relationship between arousal and performance in sports according to positive and functional (energetic, decided), positive and dysfunctional (relax, calm), negative and functional (excited, nervous), and negative and dysfunctional emotional states (unhappy, sad) [25]. The tool determines the experiences that manage emotions positively and negatively, thereby making performance easier or more difficult. The instrument categorises emotions into four distinct domains: functional positive emotions, functional negative emotions, non functional positive emotions, and non functional negative emotions. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranged from 0.76 to 0.99.

Checklist Individual Strength (CIS) Scale evaluates the fatigue level perceived by individuals. The CIS consists of the subscales Subjective Fatigue (I feel tired), Concentration (Thinking about anything requires effort), Motivation (I do not feel motivated to do anything) and Physical Activity (I do quite a lot of things within a day). This scale assesses fatigue as a subjective perception of fatigue, decreased concentration, decreased motivation and decreased physical activity. The Cronbach’s Alpha scores for the scale are as follows: 0.85 for the subjective feeling of fatigue subscale, 0.72 for the concentration subscale, 0.42 for the motivation subscale and 0.48 for the physical activity subscale. The total Cronbach’s Alpha score is 0.87 [27].

Athletes’ perceived physical fatigue, perceived mental fatigue, perceived competition success, perceived competition difficulty, and perceived sleep quality were assessed using VAS.

Design and procedures

The present study was designed using a longitudinal panel design, in which repeated measurements were obtained from the same group of athletes over time. The data were collected over an eight-week period during the latter half of the competitive season.

The collection of data was conducted within the athletes’ regular training environments. Pre-training assessments were administered approximately 15 min before the commencement of training sessions, with the objective of capturing baseline psychological states under standardised conditions. Post-competition assessments were collected within 24 to 48 h following each match, considering the time required for initial psychological recovery processes to occur.

All measurements were administered in paper-and-pencil format by the researchers. Participants completed the questionnaires individually in a quiet area of the training facility to minimize distractions and ensure response accuracy. Prior to each administration, standardized instructions were provided, and participants were asked to respond based on their most recent training session or competition experience.

The study was granted ethical approval by the Marmara University Health Sciences Ethics Committee (Protocol No: 100). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses conducted by using IBM SPSS 25.0, Jamovi v.2.3.28 and Orange v.3.34 statistical software packages. The collected data during eight weeks were analyzed to monitor the variability between the psychological recovery status of the athletes and the loading (competitions). Time series have been visualized to monitor athletes and compare their averages. This analysis employed line charts to visualize the data. Given that group level scores obscured individual variations in psychological recovery, separate wrap panel line graphs were generated for for football and basketball players. These individual charts illustrate weekly changes (over eight week) in the following psychological recovery metrics: mental recovery, vigor and energy, psychological detachment, flourishing and overall psychological recovery scores. For longitudinal analysis, variables were categorized into three thematic groups based on their relevance to psychological recovery: Competition related variables: perceived competition difficulty and perceived competition success. Fatigue-related variables perceived mental fatigue, perceived physical fatigue, perceived sleep quality, sleep time, fatigue severity, concentration, motivation, activity level and total CIS fatigue score. Emotional state variables: positive and functional emotions, positive and dysfunctional emotions, negative and functional emotions, negative and dysfunctional emotions. To explore relationships among these variables, a correlation matrix heatmap was constructed to assess the strength and direction of associations. Additionally, hierarchical cluster analysis was performed to identify distinct variable groupings. Understanding the proximity and relative positioning of variables, based on their quantitative properties and distributional characteristics is crucial for elucidating how variables affect psychological recovery of athletes in long term period. Therefore, in order to provide an overview before modelling, a dendrogram, which is an intermediate stage, was created before the decision tree analysis (Classification and Regression Tree-CART model). In order to create the decision tree model and display it with a dendrogram, the data obtained from 38 athletes over 8 weeks was first rotated 90 degrees and 304 observations (data points) were obtained. The decision tree model and dendrogram were created using the data set as input. As the aim of decision tree analysis is to classify and therefore estimate the categorical variable, the initial numerical data were transformed into four categories. Categories were defined by reference to the quarterly values resulting from the analysis. Group scores are as follows: 8–10 points, 7.25–7.99 points, 6.50–7.24 points and 0-6.49 points from SPRS. A decision algorithm based on the forward pruning method was used to create the decision tree model. This algorithm divides the data into nodes according to maintain the class purity. As the data set examined is categorical, the information gain method from the division process was used. Hyperparameter tuning was applied to determine the depth of the most optimal decision tree. For this purpose, the predictive performance of decision trees at different depths in the test phase was considered and compared with the CA (classification accuracy) value. As a result, the depth with the highest CA value was determined as four categories. In addition to CA as predictive performance metric, AUC (Area Under the Curve), Precision, and Recall values ​​were also calculated.

Results

The data, collected over a period of eight weeks, revealed significant temporal fluctuations in players’ psychological recovery levels. A detailed examination of the scores obtained for the indicators of mental recovery, vigor and energy, psychological detachment, flourishing, and total recovery at both the group and individual levels indicated significant week-to-week variability. The findings emphasise that psychological recovery is a time-sensitive, multidimensional construct influenced by individual differences, and that certain psychological indicators can effectively predict overall recovery status.

The findings of this study demonstrate that psychological recovery functions through distinct and individualised dynamics, with each athlete exhibiting idiosyncratic responses to training loads and recovery processes. Furthermore, recovery trajectories are not static over time, but are subject to temporal variability, with periods of performance decline or enhancement emerging across different weeks. The results obtained demonstrate that psychological recovery can vary significantly among individuals, and that the equilibrium between weekly exertion and recovery may be subject to unique fluctuations according to each athlete’s particular circumstances.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, two discrete clusters were identified among the evaluated variables. The initial cluster, encompassing “Motivation,” “Concentration,” “Subjective Feeling of Fatigue,” “Unpleasant Optimal Affect (N+),” “Physical Fatigue,” and “Mental Fatigue,” exhibited robust positive intercorrelations (r > 0.60, p < 0.01). This pattern indicates a close association between heightened perceptions of fatigue and negative affective states, and declines in cognitive and motivational parameters. The second cluster comprised recovery-related variables, including “Psychological Detachment”, “Pleasant Optimal Affect (P+)”, “Pleasant Dysfunctional Affect (P-)”, “Perceived Sleep Quality”, and “Sleep Duration”. These variables demonstrated strong positive correlations (r > 0.60, p < 0.01), indicating a high degree of correlation. The findings of this study indicate that enhanced sleep quality and positive affective experiences are linked to more efficient psychological recovery processes. Furthermore, a substantial negative correlation was identified between variables from these two clusters (r ranging from − 0.30 to − 0.65, p < 0.01), thereby emphasising an inverse relationship between fatigue-related factors and recovery-related dimensions. It is evident that the observed clustering patterns underscore the dynamic interplay between fatigue accumulation and psychological recovery processes in athletes. This emphasises the necessity for individualised monitoring and management strategies with the objective of optimising performance and well-being.

When considered as a whole, the correlation matrix and hierarchical cluster analysis consistently demonstrated the existence of two primary latent domains within the data: a fatigue-related dimension and a recovery-related dimension. The present study examined the relationship between fatigue indicators (i.e. cognitive, motivational decline, and physical exhaustion) and recovery parameters (i.e. positive affect, psychological detachment, and sleep quality). The findings indicated that fatigue indicators and recovery parameters coalesced into a distinct and cohesive structure. It is noteworthy that, while the fatigue indicators represented distinct constructs, they exhibited robust interrelationships with the recovery parameters. This finding suggests that perceptions of fatigue are closely associated with the psychological recovery processes of athletes. These findings offer a potential explanation for the dynamic and multidimensional interplay between fatigue accumulation and recovery processes in players. The findings highlight the importance of individualised monitoring strategies that address both domains distinctly in order to optimise performance and psychological well-being.

The distribution demonstrated a marked skew towards the mid-to-upper performance categories, with the majority of observations classified as “Average” or “Excellent”, while a smaller number of individuals were categorised as “Very Poor” or “Below Average”. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the decision tree model has been constructed using the Classification and Regression Tree (CART) algorithm, with the aim of predicting SPRS categories based on recovery-related predictors. The initial and most influential split was based on the “Flourishing” score at a threshold of 7.19, effectively discriminating between lower and higher recovery profiles. Subsequent partitions were predominantly informed by “Mental Recovery” and “Psychological Detachment” scores, thereby highlighting their pivotal role in the hierarchical decision structure. Participants with mental recovery scores ≤ 6.43 were predominantly allocated to the “Very Poor” recovery group, achieving a classification accuracy of 94.5%. Conversely, individuals with higher mental recovery and psychological detachment scores were predominantly classified into the “Good” and “Excellent” groups, with classification accuracies exceeding 80%.

These findings provide substantial evidence that the cognitive and affective flourishing dimensions play a critical role in delineating the overall psychological recovery status. Discussion

The present study sought to investigate weekly fluctuations in athletes’ psychological recovery and to identify key predictors of recovery profiles across an eight-week period. The findings indicated that psychological recovery is not a static or uniform process; rather, it varies significantly over time and across individuals. Temporal shifts in mental recovery, vigor and energy, and psychological detachment scores, alongside the emergence of distinct fatigue and recovery clusters, indicate a complex interplay between training demands and recovery resources. The findings of this study provide novel insights into the dynamic nature of psychological recovery in sports settings and emphasise the significance of personalised monitoring and intervention strategies.

The performance of athletes in team sports is characterised by a multifaceted, dynamic, multidimensional and interactive process. The factors that influence this phenomenon include energy capacity, tactics and the motivation of the athletes. The quality of these factors is pivotal in determining the outcome [28, 29]. Despite the presence of shared characteristics, such as high-intensity activity patterns, team sports exhibit considerable variation not only between different sports but also across positions, playing styles within the same sport, and even from one match to another [29]. It is a commonly held assumption that the majority of individuals within a group respond in a similar manner to a given training programme, thus implying that the training regimen reflects the experiences of the majority within the population. However, it is important to note that athletes often exhibit varied responses to the same training load, which offers valuable insights into their individual adaptations to training and competition [30]. Venter (2012) [31] proposed that even members of the same team may perceive recovery methods differently, emphasising the importance of personalised recovery protocols that are tailored to the distinct needs of each athlete. Crowther et al. (2017) [32] conducted a study on team sport athletes, the results of which indicated that athletes from various team sports and competitive levels employed different recovery strategies and exhibited varied perceptions regarding the impact of recovery on their performance. Descriptive analyses employing group-level graphs revealed discrepancies between aggregate results and those derived from individual-level data. Consequently, as opposed to conducting analyses at the team level, it may be more accurate to monitor psychological recovery on an individual basis. Our examination of individual graphs demonstrated that some athletes exhibited considerable week-to-week fluctuations, suggesting that athletes can be categorised as either consistently or inconsistently recovering individuals.

Within a multidimensional framework and considering variations in focus levels, athletes personalise their recovery by selecting approaches that correspond to their own perceptions and preferences [33]. In a study of elite cross-country skiers, athletes were asked to list the recovery methods they employed following intensive training sessions. The study revealed that each athlete utilised approximately ten different recovery strategies [34]. The similarity of recovery strategies among athletes was assessed by calculating the overlap in semantically equivalent items across their lists [35], with results indicating low overlap scores. This finding underscores substantial inter-individual variability in recovery strategies, even at the group level. This variability is attributed to the inherently idiosyncratic nature of recovery strategies reported by athletes [25]. As Wilson and Young (2023) [36] emphasised, the content and process of personal recovery differ among athletes, with individuality being identified as a key factor in athletic development. The conclusion drawn was that athletes customise their recovery processes by selecting the approaches that best suit their individual needs. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that recovery individualisation reflects differences in psychological recovery processes among athletes. Consequently, the varied psychological recovery responses noted during the eight-week period in our study are in alignment with these observations. These results align with Kellmann’s (2002) [37] explanation that recovery depends on athlete evaluations, which in turn leads to a wide variation in recovery methods chosen to cope with specific stressors. The establishment of an effective monitoring routine for recovery is of crucial importance for the implementation of meaningful, individualised interventions that account for the psychophysiological demands imposed on athletes not only during training but also in non-training and competitive environments [38]. The research emphasises the critical importance of considering individual needs for both coaches and athletes, and of adopting personalised approaches to maintaining the recovery-stress balance [11]. As demonstrated in the extant literature, studies pertaining to individual differences have identified the significance of attending to individuality in relation to therapeutic activity [39]. The basis of such recommendations is the understanding that recovery is a process involving the proactive reestablishment of individual psychological capacities [18] and plays a significant role in athletes’ adaptation processes [37]. According to the psychophysiological perspective, recovery is defined as a gradual, cumulative process encompassing a range of strategies and needs that vary on both an individual basis and according to the athlete [40].

The decision tree analysis revealed that flourishing is the most significant predictor in determining athletes’ psychological recovery strategies. The initial division of the model was predicated on the flourishing score, with a critical threshold established at 7.19. This finding suggests that athletes who experience higher levels of flourishing are more likely to adopt recovery strategies such as mental recovery or psychological detachment. Specifically, mental recovery was identified as the predominant strategy among participants with flourishing scores below 7.19 (82.9%), whereas psychological detachment was most prevalent among those scoring above 7.19 (81.0%). This finding suggests that individuals with high flourishing levels may possess the capacity to protect themselves from environmental and psychological stressors by employing more effective self-regulation strategies. As indicated by the extant literature, individuals who demonstrate higher levels of flourishing tend to utilise cognitive flexibility and regulatory strategies with greater efficacy [41]. This finding is in alignment with the model’s outputs presented in this study. At the lower branches of the decision tree, it was observed that levels of mental recovery and psychological detachment diverged based on different cut-off points, with flourishing continuing to exert influence. It is noteworthy that participants exhibiting low flourishing but high mental recovery scores adopted mental recovery strategies at a remarkably high rate (94.5%). In contrast, individuals with high flourishing and psychological detachment scores above 7.98 predominantly favoured detachment strategies (96.2%). The findings demonstrate the model’s robust pattern recognition capability and its sensitivity to individual differences in selecting psychological recovery strategies. In Panel A, it was observed that the majority of participants fell within the flourishing range of 7.25–8 (approximately 33%), with a smaller proportion falling within the range of ≥ 8. This distribution indicates that the overall flourishing level within the sample was concentrated at a moderate-to-high level, thereby supporting the statistical relevance of the central splitting points in the decision tree. In conclusion, the decision tree model in this study highlights a multi-layered structure in the selection of psychological recovery strategies among athletes. In particular, flourishing appears to have a strong influence on these strategies. From an applied perspective, these findings suggest that programmes aiming to enhance athletes’ well-being should be integrated with structures that reduce mental fatigue and support individualised recovery strategies.

Decision tree analysis has been identified as a significant model for explaining athletes’ recovery processes. Furthermore, within the domain of sports prediction employing artificial intelligence, two fundamental aspects must be considered. The first of these involves the analysis and identification of the factors that may influence competition outcomes, while the second focuses on understanding how these fundamental factors can be modified in order to reverse or enhance those outcomes [42]. Decision tree analysis has demonstrated considerable potential in the fields of sports outcome prediction and performance enhancement. For instance, Ruiz and Perez-Cruz (2015) [43] reported high predictive accuracy in modelling the outcomes of collegiate basketball games. In a similar vein, Nakano et al. (2007) [44] employed decision tree techniques to develop lifestyle recommendations aimed at maintaining performance-related conditions in athletes, subsequently demonstrating their positive impact on athletic performance. In their 2024 study, Jeon and Lim [45] utilised decision tree analysis within the context of taekwondo to identify critical in-game variables that differentiate between athletes who achieve victory and those who do not. In conclusion, the findings emphasise the value of decision tree models in providing empirical insights that can inform coaching practices and enhance competitive outcomes.

Conclusion

Results from the present study reveals that the psychological recovery of athletes is evaluated as a team, it has been determined that individual differences should also be taken into account. It seems that the path to psychological recovery after a performance is unique to each athlete. Each athlete has a distinct set of psychological recovery strategies because each athlete has unique priorities. In this context, it was concluded that the heatmeap correlations and dendrogram findings formed two main clusters as fatigue and recovery. It was also found that flourishing was the most significant predictor of psychological recovery in athletes.

Our study offers several new perspectives within the psychological recovery literature. Firstly, the proposed four-dimensional structure model of the SPRS was investigated longitudinally. Secondly, we proposed distinguishing athletes as “consistently recoverers” and “inconsistently recoverers.” Thirdly, we found that players individually follow different paths to recover. Lastly, the statistical approach employed in this study may contribute to the literature due to the use of machine learning methodology in the analyses.

Practical implications

The findings of this study indicate that athletes employ diverse recovery strategies and follow individualized paths in their psychological recovery processes. Given this variability, it is essential to emphasize that recovery strategies should be fully personalized. To maximize their effectiveness, these strategies must be thoughtfully integrated into training regimens or daily routines. Developing structured programs tailored to these needs is therefore of critical importance.

Limitations

Considering the limitations, the study was based on a small sample of male football and basketball players and covered an eight-week period in the second halves of the professional league session. A potential limitation of this study is the relatively broad age range of the participants (18–35 years), which may have introduced variability in psychological responses due to age-related differences in emotional and social development. Future research should include larger, more diverse groups across sports, ages, genders, and related variables to enhance the generalizability of psychological recovery findings.
