Paths, Ends and The Separation Problem for Infinite Graphs
Nicanor Carrasco-Vargas, Valentino Delle Rose, Cristóbal Rojas

Abstract
. We introduce and study the Separation Problem for infinite graphs, which involves determining whether a connected graph splits into at least two infinite connected components after the removal of a given finite set of edges. We prove that this problem is decidable for every highly computable graph with finitely many ends. Using this result, we demonstrate that König’s Infinity Lemma is effective for such graphs. We also apply it to analyze the complexity of the Eulerian Path Problem for infinite graphs, showing that much of its complexity arises from counting ends—indeed, the Eulerian Path Problem becomes strictly easier when restricted to graphs with a fixed number of ends. Under this restriction, we provide a complete characterization of the problem. Finally, we study the Separation Problem in a uniform setting (i.e., where the graph is also part of the input) and offer a nearly complete characterization of its complexity and its relationship to counting the number of ends.



1.
Introduction

The study of infinite graphs from a computability perspective dates back to the seminal work of Manaster and Rosenstein
[
17
]
, as well as Bean
[
2
,
3
]
, who initiated a research program aimed at understanding the algorithmic complexity of deciding classical graph properties for families of graphs with an algorithmic description. The difficulty of deciding a given property depends on the amount of information contained in the graph’s description. This research program seeks to characterize this relationship using the hierarchies of algorithmic complexity provided by computability theory.

A central notion in these works is that of
computable graphs
, which are infinite graphs for which there exists an algorithm to determine whether two vertices are connected by an edge. These works also introduced a stronger notion of computability for graphs, known as
highly computable graphs
. Highly computable graphs form a subclass of locally finite computable graphs where, in addition, the degree of each vertex can be effectively computed. Intuitively, a highly computable graph can be thought of as a graph for which there is an algorithm capable of drawing a certified picture of any finite portion of the graph. A third class of graphs, introduced more recently
[
14
]
, is that of
automatic graphs
. These are graphs whose vertex set and adjacency relation can be described by finite automata. Their algorithmic properties have been systematically compared to those of computable and highly computable graphs
[
11
]
.

The main point of considering these stronger notions is that some problems may become easier for graphs with stronger algorithmic descriptions, and an important goal is to understand for which problems this is indeed the case. A particularly interesting example of this phenomenon is the problem of deciding whether a graph has an
Eulerian path
, namely a path which visits every edge exactly once. As it is well-known, the class of graphs admitting an Eulerian path is characterized by the parity of its vertex degrees
[
10
,
12
]
. Around two-hundred years later, this characterization was extended to countably infinite graphs by Erdős, Grünwald and Vázsonyi
[
8
]
. Recently, Kuske and Lohrey
[
16
]
studied the decidability of the Eulerian Path Problem in this general setting. They proved that the problem is
D_{3}^{0}
-complete for computable graphs (where
D_{3}^{0}
denotes the class of sets which are expressible as differences of two
\Sigma_{3}^{0}
sets), but only
\Pi_{2}^{0}
-complete for both the class of highly computable graphs and the class of automatic graphs.

Another problem well known to be undecidable for infinite connected graphs is the problem of finding an infinite simple path. Although Kőnig’s lemma guarantees that such paths always exist, they could all be uncomputable even in a highly computable graph
[
13
]
.

1.1.
Our contributions

In this paper, we introduce and study what we see as a more fundamental problem underlying many algorithmic questions about connected graphs, including the two previously mentioned. We call it the
Separation Problem
. It consists of determining whether the removal of a finite set of edges
E
from a connected graph
G
results in at least two distinct infinite connected components. It is closely related to the problem of counting the number of
ends
of a graph (see
Section

2
for a definition). Our main contributions can be summarized as follows. We note that all graphs considered in this paper are assumed to be connected.

On the complexity of the Separation Problem.
We provide a detailed analysis of the Separation Problem and its connection to the number of ends of a graph. In particular:

•

For fixed
G
,
we show that the Separation problem is
\Pi_{1}^{0}
in general, and that this bound is tight. However, if the graph is assumed to have finitely many ends, the problem becomes computable;

•

Uniformly in
G
.
To better understand the structure of the uniform version of the Separation Problem when the number of ends is finite, we introduce two specific collections of sets of edges:
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
and
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
. The first collection contains sets
E
that separate
G
into at least two infinite connected components, while the second contains those sets
E
that separate
G
in as many distinct infinite connected components as possible. We characterize the complexity of computing each of these collections from a description of
G
, and analyze their relationship to the number of ends. At a high level, our results show that the information contained in
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
is uniformly equivalent to the combination of
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
and the number of ends of
G
.

Applications.
We apply some of the tools developed in our analysis of the Separation Problem to both the Eulerian path problem and the problem of computing infinite paths, under restrictions on the number of ends of the graph. Our main results are:

•

On computing infinite paths.
We show that this problem is solvable for graphs with finitely many ends. More precisely, we prove that a connected, highly computable graph with finitely many ends is infinite if and only if it admits a computable infinite simple path. For these graphs we prove the stronger fact that the extension problem for finite paths to infinite simple paths is decidable.

•

On the Eulerian path Problem.
We consider a version of this problem for graphs with either one or two ends (as graphs with more than two ends cannot have an Eulerian path). We characterise the resulting complexity in each case. In particular, we show that for highly computable graphs with one end, determining whether such a graph has a one-way Eulerian path is d.c.e.-complete (where a set is
d.c.e.
if it can be written as the difference of two c.e. set), whereas determining the existence of a two-way Eulerian path is only
\Pi_{1}^{0}
-complete. On the other hand, for highly computable graphs with two ends, we show that the problem attains precisely the

2.
Preliminaries

Computability theory

The computability theoretical terminology and notation we use is quite standard and follows mostly the textbook
[
19
]
. For a set
A
we will write
\overline{A}
to denote its complement.

Given a Turing machine
\varphi
we write
\varphi[s]
to denote the outcome of running
\varphi
for
s
many steps. If
\varphi
halts within
s
many steps we write
\varphi[s]\downarrow
, otherwise we write
\varphi[s]\uparrow
. We write
\varphi\downarrow
if
\varphi[s]\downarrow
for some
s
, and
\varphi\uparrow
otherwise. For a c.e. set
W
, we denote by
W[s]
the set of its elements enumerated within
s
steps. We will write
(W_{e})_{e\in\mathbb{N}}
to mean a computable enumeration of all c.e. sets.

For a given computable enumeration
(\varphi_{e})_{e\in\mathbb{N}}
of Turing machines without input, we denote by
\operatorname{\mathbf{0}}^{\prime}=\{e\colon\,\varphi_{e}\downarrow\}
its
halting set
. Moreover, by means of oracle Turing machines, one can define the halting set
relative to
any set
Y
, i.e. the set
Y^{\prime}=\{e:\varphi_{e}^{Y}\downarrow\}
. In particular, it is possible to consider
\operatorname{\mathbf{0}}^{\prime\prime}
, the halting set relative to
\operatorname{\mathbf{0}}^{\prime}
.

In this paper we will make an extensive use of both
Turing reducibility
and
m
-
reducibility
among sets
[
19
]
. The notion of
completeness
, capturing the property of being the hardest problem in a certain class, is defined by means of the latter reducibility: a set
A
is
\mathcal{C}
-
complete
(for some class of sets
\mathcal{C}
) if
A\in\mathcal{C}
and
X\leq_{m}A
for all
X\in\mathcal{C}
. We will mostly set our problems along the levels of the
arithmetical hierarchy
: to this regard, just recall that
\operatorname{\mathbf{0}}^{\prime}
and
\overline{\operatorname{\mathbf{0}}^{\prime}}
are, respectively,
\Sigma_{1}^{0}
-complete and
\Pi_{1}^{0}
-complete, while
\textbf{Inf}=\{e\colon\,|W_{e}|=\infty\}
and
\textbf{Fin}=\{e\colon\,|W_{e}|<\infty\}
are, respectively,
\Pi_{2}^{0}
-complete and
\Sigma_{2}^{0}
-complete (see, e.g.,
[
19
, Theorem 4.3.2]
).
\operatorname{\mathbf{0}}^{\prime\prime}
is another example of a

\Sigma_{2}^{0}
-complete set.

Finally, recall that a set is
\Delta_{2}^{0}
if
A\leq_{T}\operatorname{\mathbf{0}}^{\prime}
.
The following well-known result characterizes the
\Delta_{2}^{0}
sets precisely as those admitting
computable approximations
.

Theorem 2.1

(Shoenfield’s Limit Lemma
[
18
]
)
.

A set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is
\Delta_{2}^{0}
if and only if
A
has a
computable approximation
, namely a computable function
f\colon\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\{0,1\}
such that, for all
n
:

(1)

\lim_{s}f(n,s)
exists (i.e.
|\{s:f(n,s)\neq f(n,s+1)\}|<\infty
), and

(2)

\lim_{s}f(n,s)=1\iff n\in A
.

Graph theory

We will sometimes write “
A\Subset B
” as an abbreviation for “
A
is finite and
A\subseteq B
”. Unless explicitly mentioned, we consider undirected and simple graphs. The set of vertices of a graph
G
is denoted by
V(G)
, and its set of edges by
E(G)
. The
degree

\deg_{G}(v)
of a vertex
v
is the number of edges in
G
incident to
v
. We say that
G
is
even
when every vertex has even degree. We consider induced subgraphs from sets of vertices and edges. Given
V\subset V(G)
, the
induced
subgraph
G[V]
has vertex set
V
and edge set
\{e\in E(G):\text{$e$ joins vertices in $V$}\}
. Given
E\subset E(G)
, the
induced
subgraph
G[E]
has edge set
E
, and vertex set
\{v\in V(G):\text{$v$ is incident to an edge in $E$}\}
. We denote by
G\smallsetminus E
the induced subgraph
G[E(G)\smallsetminus E]
.

A
path
in
G
is a sequence of vertices
v_{0},\dots,v_{n}
such that consecutive elements in the sequence are either adjacent or equal, and its
length
is the number of edges that it visits. A path is
simple
when no vertex is repeated. The graph
G
is
connected
when every pair of vertices can be joined by a path. A connected subgraph of
G
is a
connected component
when it is maximal for the subgraph relation. Given two vertices
v,w
in the same connected component, their
distance

d_{G}(v,w)
is the length of the shortest path in
G
joining them.

The
number of ends
of a connected graph
G
is the supremum of the number of infinite connected components of
G\smallsetminus E
, where
E
ranges over all finite subsets of
E(G)
. The number of ends of a graph
G
may be infinite. Other ways of defining the number of ends of a graph exist, but they all coincide for locally finite graphs
[
6
]
.

We now recall standard computability notions for countably infinite graphs.

Definition 2.2
.

A graph
G
is
computable
when
V(G)
is a decidable subset of
\mathbb{N}
, and the adjacency relation is a decidable subset of
\mathbb{N}^{2}
. A computable graph
G
is
highly computable
when it is locally finite and the vertex degree function
\deg_{G}(\cdot)
is computable.

We say that a sequence
(G_{e})_{e\in\mathbb{N}}
of highly computable graphs is
uniformly highly computable
if there are computable functions
V
,
E
and
\deg
with two inputs, such that
V(e,v)=1
if and only if
v\in V(G_{e})
,
E(e,\langle v,w\rangle)=1
if and only if
\{v,w\}\in E(G_{e})
, and

\deg(e,v)=n
if and only if
v
is a vertex in
G_{e}
and has degree
n
in this graph. We often identify a highly computable graph
G
with the index of a Turing machine that computes
V(G)
,
E(G)
, and the function
\deg_{G}
, and we informally refer to this as a
computer program
for
G
.

We will also make some observations for the case of automatic graphs, so we end this Section by briefly recalling their definition (see
[
14
]
for more details). Given an undirected graph
G
, we denote by
R(G)
the adjacency relation. An undirected graph
G
is
automatic
when the relational structure
(V(G),R(G))
admits an
automatic presentation
: a tuple
(\Sigma,L,R_{e},R_{a},h)
where
\Sigma
is a finite alphabet,
L\subset\Sigma^{\ast}
is a regular language,
h\colon L\to V(G)
is a surjective function, and
R_{e}=\{(u,v):h(u)=h(v)\},R_{a}=\{(u,v):R(G)(h(u),h(v))\}
are regular relations.

3.
The Separation problem

In this section we study the Separation problem for infinite, locally finite, and connected graphs. The following definition summarizes the main objects that will be at the center of our attention.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
G
be a locally finite and connected graph.

(1)

\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G}(E)
is the number of infinite connected components of
G\smallsetminus E
, for any
E\Subset E(G)
.

(2)

\mathbf{Sep}({G})
is the collection of finite sets of edges
E\Subset E(G)
such that
\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G}(E)\geq 2
. In this case we say that
E

separates

G
.

(3)

\mathbf{Ends}({G})
is the number of ends of
G
(possibly infinite).

(4)

\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
is the collection of finite sets of edges
E\Subset E(G)
such that
\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G}(E)
attains a maximal value, that is,
\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G}(E)=\mathbf{Ends}({G})
.

Remark 3.2
.

If
1<\mathbf{Ends}({G})\leq\infty
then
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
is a subset of
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
. Also note that if
\mathbf{Ends}({G})=\infty
then
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
is empty. In contrast, if
\mathbf{Ends}({G})=1
, then
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
is empty while
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
contains all finite sets of edges, even the empty one.

Thus the
Separation Problem
corresponds to the membership problem in
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
.

3.1.
The complexity of
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
and
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
for a fixed graph
G

In this subsection we prove basic results about the computability of
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
,
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
, and the map
E\mapsto\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G}(E)
, where
G
is a highly computable connected graph with
1\leq\mathbf{Ends}({G})\leq\infty
. Some of these results are also uniform on
G
and will be used later.

We remark that the strongest computability property that a graph
G
may posses in this context is having a computable function
E\mapsto\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G}(E)
. This implies that
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
is a decidable set. If
\mathbf{Ends}({G})<\infty
then it also follows that
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
is decidable (cf.
Definition

3.1
).

The following result shows that in general the function
E\mapsto\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G}(E)
is
upper-semi-computable
.

Proposition 3.3
.

There is a partial computable function which on input a (program for a) connected highly computable graph
G
and a finite set of edges
E\subset E(G)
, computes a sequence of natural numbers
(\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G,n}(E))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
whose minimum equals
\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G}(E)
.

Proof.

Let
G
and
E
be as in the statement. We start by computing the set
V
of vertices in
G\smallsetminus E
that are incident to some edge
in
E
. Further, for each
v\in V
, we let
C_{v}
be the connected
component of
G\smallsetminus E
that contains
v
. For each
v\in V
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
E_{v,n}

as the set of edges in some path in
G
with length at most
n
, starting at
v
, and that visits no edge from
E
. The sets
E_{v,n}
can be uniformly computed (from
G
,
E
,

v
and

). It is then clear that for all
u,v\in V
, (1)
C_{v}
is finite exactly when
E_{v,n}=E_{v,n-1}
for some
n
, and (2)
C_{u}=C_{v}
when for some
n
, we have
E_{u,n}\cap E_{v,n}\neq\emptyset
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{n}=\{E_{v,n}:v\in V,\ E_{v,n-1}\neq E_{v,n}\}
. We define a relation
\sim
on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
by
E\sim E^{\prime}\iff E\cap E^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
, and then we extend
\sim
to its transitive closure. We let
\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G,n}(E)
be the number of equivalence classes of
\sim
. A direct verification shows that
(\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G,n}(E))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-increasing and that
\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G,n}(E)=\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G}(E)
for some
n
large enough.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

If
\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G,n}(E)=\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G}(E)
, then

has the property that all connected components of
G\smallsetminus E
can be determined by “exploring” around

at distance at most
n
.

For a more precise statement, let
u,v
be vertices in
G\smallsetminus E
and incident to
E
. Then they are in the same connected component of
G\smallsetminus E
if and only if they can be connected with a path in
G\smallsetminus E
that stays at distance at most
n
from
E
.
Furthermore,
C_{u}
is infinite if and only if it contains a vertex

with
d_{G\smallsetminus E}(w,u)=n
.

We obtain the following general upper bound for the complexity of
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
.

Proposition 3.5
.

\mathbf{Sep}({G})
is
\Pi_{1}^{0}
for every connected and highly computable graph

.

Proof.

Given
E\Subset E(G)
, we have
E\in\mathbf{Sep}({G})
if and only if for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G,n}(E)\geq 2
. This is a
\Pi_{1}^{0}
condition by
Proposition

3.3
.
∎

This upper bound is tight for graphs with infinitely many ends:

Proposition 3.6
.

There is a connected highly computable graph

with infinitely many ends such that

is
\Pi_{1}^{0}
-complete.

Proof.

Let
(\varphi_{e})_{e\in\mathbb{N}}
be a computable enumeration of Turing machines without input, and let

be the graph with vertex set

and edge set

Intuitively, the graph

We now prove that for a graph with finitely many ends,

Theorem 3.7

Let
G
be a connected highly computable graph with finitely many ends. Then the map

is computable.

In fact, there is a partial computable function which on input a (program for a) connected highly computable graph
G
with finitely many ends, a finite set

, the number of ends of
G
and an arbitrary element

\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G}(E)

Proof.

We prove the second part of the statement, which implies the first part of the statement. Assume we receive as inputs some description of the highly computable graph
G
, a finite set of edges
W\in\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
, the number
k
of ends of
G
, and a finite set

In order to compute
\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G}(E)
we first compute a set

with the following properties: (1)
U
contains
E
and
W
, (2)

is connected, (3) for each pair of vertices

in
G\smallsetminus U
that are incident to edges from
U
, and such that

lie in the same infinite connected component of

u
to

that is completely contained in

, and (4)

Computing a set with these properties requires some care, and we need to introduce some notation. Fix a vertex

, and for each

U_{r}
the edge set of the induced graph

. We let

be the set of vertices in

that are incident to some edge from

. Observe that we can compute

and

We compute

as the smallest natural number such that both

and
\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G}(E_{r_{0}})=k

such that
\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G,n_{0}}(U_{r_{0}})=k

It is clear that

and

that joins

. Since

The set

the properties (1), (2), (3) and (4) defined before. We will now use this set to count the number of infinite connected components of

. Let

Define an equivalence relation

Corollary 3.8

Let

3.3.
The relationship between
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
,
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
and
\mathbf{Ends}({G})

We have shown that the problem of determining membership in
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
, with an algorithm uniform on
G
, is a
\Pi_{2}^{0}
-complete problem. Since this coincides with the complexity of bounding the number of ends of
G
from above, the following questions arise naturally:

Suppose we are given a highly computable and connected graph
G
with finitely many ends, and we are given oracle access to
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
. Is this information sufficient to determine
\mathbf{Ends}({G})
? What about the converse?

In this section we study these and related questions. Our main result states that the knowledge of
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
is in a sense equivalent to knowledge of both
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
and
\mathbf{Ends}({G})
:

Theorem 3.16
.

Let
G
be a highly computable graph with finitely many ends. Then:

(1)

There is an algorithm, uniform in
G
, which with oracle access to
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
computes
\mathbf{Ends}({G})
and determines membership in
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
.

(2)

There is an algorithm, uniform in
G
, which given
\mathbf{Ends}({G})
and with oracle access to
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
, decides membership in
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
.

Our main tool will be the following elementary result.

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
G
be an infinite, connected, and locally finite graph with finitely many ends, and let
w
be a vertex in
G
. For each
r\in\mathbb{N}
let
V_{r}
be the set of vertices in
G
at distance
r
to
w_{0}
. Furthermore, we let
E_{r}
be the set of edges joining some vertex in
V_{r}
to some vertex in
V_{r-1}
.

(1)

Suppose that
E_{r}\in\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
. Then
E\subset E_{r}
is minimal for inclusion within
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
if and only if either
\mathbf{Ends}({G})=1
and
E=\emptyset
, or
\mathbf{Ends}({G})\geq 2
and there is an infinite connected component
C
of
G\smallsetminus E
such that
E
is equal to all edges in
E_{r}
incident to a vertex in some infinite connected component of
G\smallsetminus E
different from
C
.

(2)

Suppose that
E_{r}\in\mathbf{Sep}({G})
. Then
E\subset E_{r}
is minimal for inclusion within
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
if and only if there is an infinite connected component
C
of
G\smallsetminus E_{r}
, such that
E
equals the set of all edges in
E_{r}
incident to some vertex from
C
.

(3)

E_{r}
belongs to
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
if and only if there are exactly
k
disjoint subsets of
E_{r}
that are minimal for inclusion within
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
.

Proof.

Observe that the three claims hold for trivial reasons if
\mathbf{Ends}({G})=1
. Suppose in what follows that
\mathbf{Ends}({G})>1
. Let
r\geq 1
, and let us introduce some useful notation regarding the connected components of
G\smallsetminus E_{r}
. Let
C_{1},\dots,C_{k}
be the infinite connected components of
G\smallsetminus E_{r}
,
k\geq 1
. For each
i=1,\dots,k
we let
E_{r}^{i}
be the set of edges in
E_{r}
that are incident to some vertex from
C_{i}
.

Let us suppose
E_{r}\in\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
and prove that (1) holds. Let
E\subset E_{r}
be minimal for inclusion within
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
. It is clear that then
E
contains no edge incident to a finite connected component of
G\smallsetminus E_{r}
. Furthermore, there can not be two different components
C_{i}
and
C_{j}
,

, such that
E
fails to contain both
E_{r}^{i}
and
E_{r}^{j}
. Indeed, in this situation the graph
G\smallsetminus E
would have a single component containing both
C_{i}
and
C_{j}
, therefore a component with two ends, contradicting the assumption that
E\in\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
. It follows that
E
must contain at least
k-1
of the sets
E_{r}^{i}
,
i=1,\dots,k
. Let
i_{0}\in\{1,\dots,k\}
be such that
E
contains

. It is straightforward that
E^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
. Since
E
was assumed to be minimal for inclusion within
\mathbf{Sepmax}({G})
, it follows that
E=E^{\prime}
and the claim is verified. The backward implication in (1) follows the same arguments.

Let us suppose
E_{r}\in\mathbf{Sep}({G})
and prove that (2) holds. Let
E\subset E_{r}
be minimal for inclusion within
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
. Then there must be some

such that

contains
E_{r}^{i}
, for otherwise

would have exactly one infinite connected component and this is not possible for
E_{r}\in\mathbf{Sep}({G})
. As in the previous paragraph, minimality implies that in fact
E=E_{r}^{i}
, and the claim is verified. The backward implication for (2) follows similar arguments. Finally, observe that (3) follows directly from (2).∎

.

that also belong to

. This is equal to

We now prove the second claim in the statement. Suppose we are given as input a (computer program for a) highly computable and connected graph

\mathbf{Sep}({G})

,

is a tree without finite branches and therefore
\mathbf{Sep}({G_{e}})
contains all finite sets of edges. On the other hand, an “additional end” will appear exactly when

halts, and therefore
\mathbf{Ends}({G_{e}})
is not computable on input

.
∎

4.
Computing infinite paths by separation

Here we establish a close connection between the separation problem, and the problem of determining whether a finite simple path in a graph can be extended to an infinite one. We denote by
\mathbf{Path}({G})
the set of finite sequences
(v_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
of vertices in
V(G)
with the property that
(v_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
can be extended to an infinite simple path
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
G
be a highly computable and connected infinite
graph. Then
\mathbf{Sep}({G})\geq_{T}\mathbf{Path}({G})
. If in addition
G
is a tree, then
we have an equivalence
\mathbf{Sep}({G})\equiv_{T}\mathbf{Path}({G})
. The reductions
are uniform on (a computer program for)
G
.

Putting this result together with
Corollary

3.8
we obtain the following amusing consequence.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
G
is a highly computable infinite graph with finitely many ends, then
\mathbf{Path}({G})
is a decidable set. In particular,
G
admits computable infinite simple paths.

Thus for a highly computable and connected graph
G
, the property “having infinite simple paths but no computable one” implies that
G
has infinitely many ends, and that
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
is undecidable. The proof of
Theorem

4.1
will be based on the following result.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G
be a highly computable connected graph. Let
P
be the set of pairs
(E,v)
such that
E\subset E(G)
is finite,
v
is a vertex in
G\smallsetminus E
incident to some edge from
E
, and the connected component of
G\smallsetminus E
containing
v
is infinite. Then
P\leq_{T}\mathbf{Sep}({G})
. This Turing reduction is uniform on (a computer program for)
G
.

Proof.

Let
E\subset E(G)
be finite, let
v
be a vertex in
G\smallsetminus E
incident to some edge from
E
, and let
C_{v}
be the connected component of
G\smallsetminus E
containing
v
. It suffices to describe an algorithm which, having oracle access to
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
, determines whether
C_{v}
is finite or infinite.

We start by checking whether
E\in\mathbf{Sep}({G})
. If
E\not\in\mathbf{Sep}({G})
then
\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G}(E)=1
. We can use
Proposition

3.3
to compute
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G,n}(E)=\operatorname{\mathbf{Comp}}_{G}(E)
. Then
Remark

3.4
provides a criterion to determine whether
C_{v}
is infinite.

Suppose now that
E\in\mathbf{Sep}({G})
. For each
r\in\mathbb{N}
we define
E_{v,r}
as the set of edges in
E(G)
that belong to a path in
G\smallsetminus E
which visits
v
and has length at most
r
. An elementary argument shows that
C_{v}
is infinite if and only if for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
we have
E_{v,r}\in\mathbf{Sep}({G})
. Thus “
C_{v}
is infinite” is a
\Sigma_{1}^{0}
property with oracle
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
. On the other hand, it is clear that “
C_{v}
is finite” is a
\Sigma_{1}^{0}
condition. This provides an algorithm which, having
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
as oracle, determines whether
C_{v}
is infinite.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

4.1
.

Let
(v_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
be a finite sequence of vertices in
G
. Since
G
is highly computable we can check whether it corresponds to a finite simple path, that is, whether each vertex is adjacent to the next one and whether no vertices are repeated. Let
E
be the set of all edges in

that are incident to

for some

, and let

be the set of vertices in

that are adjacent to

. By König’s Infinity Lemma,
(v_{i})_{i=0}^{i=n}
can be extended to an infinite simple path in

if and only if there is

such that
C_{u}
is infinite. This is decidable with oracle
\mathbf{Sep}({G})
by

4.3
. We have proved the reduction
\mathbf{Sep}({G})\geq_{T}\mathbf{Path}({G})
.

We now assume that
G
is a tree, and prove the reduction
\mathbf{Path}({G})\geq_{T}\mathbf{Sep}({G})
.
We exhibit a procedure which, given a finite set
E\subset E(G)
, a
vertex
u
in
G\smallsetminus E
that is incident to some edge
in
E
, and using
\mathbf{Path}({G})
as oracle, determines if the connected

G\smallsetminus E
containing
u
is infinite. It
is clear how to use this information to check whether
E\in\mathbf{Sep}({G})
. The procedure is as follows. Let
C_{u}
be the connected component of
G\smallsetminus E
containing
u
. We compute the
set

of vertices in
G\smallsetminus E
adjacent to
u
.
If
V
is
empty then we conclude that
C_{u}
is finite. If
V
is
nonempty, then we observe that, by König’s infinity Lemma,

is infinite if and only if it has some infinite path starting at

. These paths are in correspondence
with infinite paths in

that start at

and then visit
some vertex

. This is true because both

and

are trees. Thus in order to decide whether

C
is infinite, it suffices to check whether some of the finitely
many elements
\{uv:v\in V\}
lies in
\mathbf{Path}({G})
.
∎

We now present an example illustrating the limitations of
Theorem

4.1
. A finite path
(v_{i})_{i=n}^{i=m}
in a graph is called
geodesic
when its length is minimal among all finite paths with the same initial and final vertex, and an infinite path is geodesic when its restriction to
\{n,\dots,m\}
is geodesic for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
,
n<m
. This concept is fundamental in geometric group theory, see for instance
[
7
, Chapter 11]
.

Proposition 4.4
.

There is a highly computable graph
\Lambda
with one end and with no computable infinite geodesic path.

Proof.

Let
T
be a subtree of the rooted binary tree that is connected, highly computable, infinite, and with no computable infinite simple path. The existence of
T
is proved in
[
13
]
. Let
\Lambda
be the simple graph whose vertex set is the cartesian product
V(T)\times V(T)
, and where two vertices
(u_{1},v_{1})
and
(u_{2},v_{2})
are neighbors when either (1)
u_{1}=u_{2}
and
v_{1}
is adjacent to
v_{2}
in
T
, or (2)
v_{1}=v_{2}
and
u_{1}
is adjacent to
u_{2}
in
T
. If
\Lambda
had a computable infinite geodesic path, then one could take the projection to the first or second coordinate and obtain a computable infinite simple path on

, which is a contradiction. The elementary verification is left to the reader.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Notice that even though the graph
\Lambda
above does not have any computable geodesic infinite path, it must have
some
computable infinite simple path, by
Theorem

4.2
. What do such paths look like then? The key observation is that, if we think of a path in
\Lambda
as a combination of two paths
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
in the tree
T
, then one can search for an infinite path in

using

while keeping

at a fixed node, and in case we chose the wrong branch and end up in a leaf of

, we can change the node from

and then “go back” with

to try a different branch, without formally repeating nodes in
\Lambda
.

.

5.
On the complexity of the Eulerian path problem

Erdős, Grünwald and Vázsonyi established the following characterization of infinite
Eulerian
graphs, namely those graphs admitting an Eulerian path
[
8
]
.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
G
be a connected graph with countably many edges.

(i)

G
admits a one-way infinite Eulerian path if and only if it satisfies the following set of conditions, called
\mathscr{E}_{1}
:

•

Either
G
has exactly one vertex with odd degree, or
G
has at least one vertex with infinite degree and no vertex with odd degree.

•

G
has one end.

(ii)

G
admits a two-way infinite Eulerian path if and only if it satisfies the following set of conditions, called
\mathscr{E}_{2}
:

•

The degree of each vertex is either even or infinite.

•

G
has one or two ends.

•

If
E
is a finite set of edges
which induces a subgraph where all vertices have even degree, then

G\smallsetminus E
has one infinite connected component.

A recent and computable proof of this result can be found on
[
4
]
. Using this characterization, it has been proven in
[
16
]
that deciding if a highly computable graph admits a one-way Eulerian path (i.e. if it satisfies the set of conditions
\mathscr{E}_{1}
) is
\Pi_{2}^{0}
-complete. This turns out to be the same difficulty of deciding whether a highly computable graph has at most
k
ends, for any fixed
k>0
. Also, it is not hard to show that deciding whether a highly computable graph admits a two-way Eulerian path (namely, it satisfies conditions
\mathscr{E}_{2}
) is, again,
\Pi_{2}^{0}
-complete (see
Theorem

5.2
below). Moreover, we point out that in the same paper
[
16
]
, it is shown that both the above problems remain
\Pi_{2}^{0}
-complete in the case of
automatic
graphs. Therefore, it is natural to ask whether the condition on the number of ends encapsulates most of the hardness of the Eulerian path problem, making this decision task easier if we consider only graphs with the correct number of ends.
The rest of this section is devoted to confirm that indeed this is the case.

Remark 1
.

For simplicity and clarity, so far we have proved all our results in the setting of
simple
graphs, that is, with no loops or multiple edges between vertices allowed. However, when considering Eulerian paths it is natural to allow multigraphs, since for instance the graph with a single vertex and infinitely many loops, admits both a one-way and a two-way infinite Eulerian path. For this reason, in this section we shall consider highly computable multigraphs. Let us then recall that in a multigraph
G
, the degree of the vertex
v
equals the number of edges incident to
v
, where loops are counted twice. A multigraph
G=(V,E)
is
computable
when
V(G)
is a decidable subset of
\mathbb{N}
, and we have a computable function
E(G)\colon V(G)^{2}\to\mathbb{N}
such that

is the number of edges joining
u
to
v
in
G
. The definitions of
highly computable
multigraph, and
highly computable sequence
of multigraphs are a straightforward generalization of the ones for simple graphs. It is easy to check that all the results proved in the previous section are valid in this more general context, so we will use them without further clarifications.

Remark 2
.

We point out that the proofs of
Theorem

5.2
and

5.3

5.5
: here, the properties of the Separation Problem are the key to prove the upper bound in the complexity of the Eulerian Path problem when the number of ends is known to be exactly 2.
