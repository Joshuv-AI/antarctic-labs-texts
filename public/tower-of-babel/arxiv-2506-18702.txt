Wetting and Pattern Formation in Non-Reciprocal Ternary Phase Separation
Xiao Ma, Michael E. Cates

Abstract
Non-reciprocal interactions are among the simplest mechanisms that drive a physical system out of thermal equilibrium, leading to novel phenomena such as oscillatory pattern formation. In this paper, we introduce a ternary phase separation model, with non-reciprocal interactions between two of the three phases and a spectator phase that mimics a boundary. Through numerical simulations, we uncover three distinct phase behaviours: a quasi-static regime, characterized by well-defined non-equilibrium contact angles at the three phase contact line; a limit cycle regime, with the three bulk phases rotating around the three phase contact line; and a travelling wave regime, featuring persistent directional motion. We complement our numerical findings with analytical examination of linear stability and the wave propagation speed near equilibrium. Our model provides a minimal framework for extending classical equilibrium wetting theory to active and non-equilibrium systems.

Introduction

Wetting, whereby one of two coexisting fluids preferentially comes into contact with a solid boundary, has long been of theoretical, experimental and practical interest. In the past few decades, there have been significant advances in understanding wetting behaviour in thermal equilibrium. In the static case, minimization of the free energy predicts the contact angles formed by a liquid droplet on a surface; this is sometimes interpreted via the Young-Dupre’s equation, which describes a force balance of interfacial tensions
[
1
]
. Varying the temperature or the interfacial energies can lead to a wetting transition, transforming droplets with finite contact angles into thin films. These transitions exhibit universal critical behaviour and have been extensively studied using static and dynamical renormalization group techniques
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. Most of this interfacial behaviour can be captured in a ternary system with three coexisting fluid phases, rather than two fluids and a solid, with the theoretical advantage of not needing explicit solid-liquid boundary interactions.

On the other hand, many wetting phenomena in biological systems occur far from equilibrium, ranging from the spreading of epithelial tissues
[
5
]
on a substrate, to the interaction between biomolecular condensates and membrane-bound organelles
[
6
]
. In many of these systems, there is an injection of energy at a microscopic scale, constantly pushing the systems out of equilibrium and challenging established equilibrium principles. In recent years, there have been consistent efforts to understand wetting behaviour in active systems. These include the development of continuum theories for active liquid crystals
[
7
,
8
]
and active polar liquids
[
9
]
, where activity enters as an active stress in the Navier-Stokes equations. An alternative approach describes non-equilibrium wetting via an ‘active’ surface, where binary mixtures interact with boundaries through binding and unbinding fluxes that violate detailed balance
[
10
]
.

Despite developments in active systems in the presence of ambient fluids, less is understood about wetting transitions and contact angles in active systems without momentum conservation. In these systems, motility-induced phase separation (MIPS)
[
11
]
drives particles to accumulate into dense and dilute phases even in the absence of any kind of attractive force; the dense phase in contact with a confining boundary resembles a droplet wetting a wall in the usual equilibrium sense. Crucially, the absence of a free energy in non-equilibrium systems forbids any unique definition of interfacial tensions. One of the most intriguing consequences is the observation of negative interfacial tensions, sparking debates about its physical significance over the stability of active interfaces
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
. This has prompted exploration of interfacial behaviour of active particles near solid boundaries with various geometries, primarily through numerical simulations
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
. More recently, there has also been growing interest in quantifying contact angles and generalising the Young-Dupre’s equation to its active counterpart, including in Turing foams
[
21
]
and in self-propelled particle systems
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
. As in equilibrium systems, understanding wetting in a ternary fluid phase separation model offers a way to capture important interfacial behaviour while avoiding the need to introduce explicit solid boundaries.

An alternative to simulating an interacting active particle system is to employ a dynamical field theoretic approach, where coarse-grained density fields evolve via stochastic PDEs that encode the spatiotemporal evolution of the system. At the mesoscopic level, non-equilibrium behaviour manifests through terms that cannot be written as the functional derivative of a free energy; a non-equilibrium field theory can thus be constructed in a top-down manner by explicitly introducing these terms. A celebrated example that describes phase separation in non-equilibrium systems is the non-reciprocal Cahn-Hilliard (NRCH) model
[
25
,
26
]
, which explicitly breaks action-reaction symmetry between two conserved fields. As a minimal model that introduces non-equilibrium features to the lowest order, the NRCH model exhibits a rich spectrum of novel phenomena, including travelling and oscillating patterns
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
, arrested coarsening
[
28
]
, localised states
[
29
]
and defect solutions
[
30
]
. Analytically, the NRCH model is closely related to the complex Ginzburg-Landau equation
[
31
,
25
]
, and has been shown to emerge from a universal amplitude equation valid near the onset of a conserved-Hopf instability
[
32
]
, which describes large-scale oscillatory instability under conserved dynamics. However, a systematic study of wetting behaviour in models with non-reciprocal interactions remains lacking.

In this paper, we introduce a ternary phase separation model, consisting of two phases with non-reciprocal interactions and a spectator phase. In the equilibrium limit, total symmetry is imposed between the three components that leads to equal interfacial tensions and stable 120-degree contact angles. Under generic conditions, the system relaxes into a hexagonal structure with three-phase junctions; however, with stripe-like initial configurations, it can also settle to a lamellar pattern without three phase contact lines. These two attractors, hexagonal and lamellar, will be the focus of this study as we depart from equilibrium.

Weak non-reciprocity breaks the symmetry between the phases, but for both attractors, maintains the stability of patterns in a quasi-static sense. In the hexagonal case, contact angles deviate away from 120 degrees, while in the lamellar case, the pattern begins to travel at a speed that can be captured using perturbative calculations. As non-reciprocity increases, the bulk phases begin to rotate around three phase contact lines, forming limit cycles and ultimately destabilising the contact lines, leading to the emergence of travelling lamellar patterns, which, in this intermediate regime, is itself unstable near the interfaces. At even higher values of non-reciprocity, travelling lamellar patterns can re-stabilise. While our simulations are performed in two dimensions, their interpretation can be extended to three dimensions by assuming homogeneity in the third direction.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the non-reciprocal ternary phase separation model, and investigate its static behaviour and linear stability analysis. Section
3
examines the weakly non-equilibrium regime, where both hexagonal and lamellar states remain dynamically stable with slight deviations from their equilibrium counterparts. In Section
4
, we present numerical observations of pattern formation as higher non-reciprocity moves the system out of the quasi-static regime, including emergence of limit cycles and travelling patterns. We conclude in Section
5
with discussions and outlook.

2
A Model for Non-Reciprocal Ternary Phase Separation

2.1
The equilibrium model

We start by considering a Flory-Huggins model that describes symmetric ternary phase separation in thermal equilibrium. The bulk free energy density
f
of a three-component conserved mixture is expressed through two independent scalar order parameters
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}

[
33
,
34
]
,

f(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},\Phi_{3})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Phi_{i}\log\Phi_{i}+\sum_{i<j}\chi\Phi_{i}\Phi_{j}+\chi_{0}\Phi_{1}\Phi_{2}\Phi_{3}

(1)

with
\Phi_{3}=1-\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}
. This can be interpreted as a mixture of two species 1, 2 (which will later become active) with volume fractions
\Phi_{1,2}
in a solvent of volume fraction
\Phi_{3}
. The first term in (
1
) represents entropic contributions that favours mixing of the components, while the remaining couplings
\chi
and
\chi_{0}
can be tuned to promote phase separation. We perform a Taylor expansion of the bulk free energy density in terms of deviations
\phi_{i}
from the homogeneously mixed state
\Phi_{i}=1/3+\phi_{i}
, subject to
\sum\phi_{i}=0
, to obtain a Cahn-Hilliard-like theory
[
35
,
36
]
,

\mathbb{F}=\frac{a}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{2}+\frac{c}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{3}+\frac{b}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{4}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\nabla\phi_{i}\right)^{2}

(2)

where the coefficients are given by
a=3-\chi-\chi_{0}/3
,
c=\chi_{0}-9/2
and
b=9
. The truncation to quartic order is justified for small values of the order parameters
\phi_{i}
. The derivation of the expression (
2
) is presented in
A
.

Notably different from the standard Cahn-Hilliard (Model B) theory for binary mixtures, our bulk free energy density contains a cubic term. While this cubic term renders the transition from mixed to phase separation discontinuous, it is essential: without it, the local part of the free energy reduces to

f=\frac{a}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{2}+\frac{b}{4}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{2}\right)^{2}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}=0

resulting in a highly degenerate free energy landscape with infinitely many minima related by rotations in composition space. The cubic term breaks the symmetry and fixes three minima in composition space, each dominated by a single component. To avoid binary (rather than ternary) phase separations becoming the energy minima, we require
c<0
. Additionally, and as usual
[
36
]
, we include gradient terms
\sum\kappa(\nabla\phi_{i})^{2}
to penalise sharp spatial variations in
\phi_{i}
. To preserve symmetry between the three phases, the gradient coefficients
\kappa
have been chosen to be equal. Throughout the simulations in this work, we use
a=-0.1,b=9,c=-1,\kappa=0.4
.

A linear stability analysis of how a homogeneous phase responds to perturbations in the two independent scalar order parameters (
\phi_{1}
,
\phi_{2}
) reveals the spinodal region, within which the system becomes locally unstable to such perturbations and spontaneously phase separate according to conserved dynamics, governed by the equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{1}

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}\frac{\delta\mathbb{F}}{\delta\phi_{1}}

(3a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{2}

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}\frac{\delta\mathbb{F}}{\delta\phi_{2}}

(3b)

where we set the mobilities for the two species to be equal. While dynamical noise has been introduced in non-reciprocal models and is known to play a crucial role in understanding phase transitions
[
37
]
, it typically does not alter the phase behavior far from criticality
[
25
,
38
,
39
]
. In particular, by setting
c
to be finite, we remain away from the critical transition.

On the other hand, the binodal region corresponds to a composition that favours phase coexistence and thus is globally unstable. Outside the spinodal and inside the binodal region, the homogeneous system is trapped in a local free energy minimum, and must overcome a free energy barrier in order to reach the phase-separated state that has a lower free energy; this cannot be done without either noise or activity (the latter being explored below). The spinodal and binodal regions for our equilibrium model (
2
) can be computed analytically (as presented in
A
), and are shown diagrammatically in Fig.
1
(a).

While in principle interfacial profiles between coexisting phases can be analytically obtained by minimising the free energy
F=\int d\mathbf{r}\;\mathbb{F}
, this is generally difficult to calculate. Approximations of interfacial free energies are often made under certain simplifying assumptions, such as neglecting the adsorption of the third phase at the interface between the other two
[
36
,
40
]
. Here we do not make use of such assumptions. Instead, we exploit the symmetry between the three phases. While this symmetry does not enable analytical solutions, it ensures that the interfacial tensions between any two coexisting phases, which are well-defined at equilibrium, are identical. Consequently, at any three phase contact line, Young-Dupre’s equation enforces the equilibrium contact angles to be 120 degrees. An illustration of this using numerical simulations of the system can be found in Fig.
1
(b). It is worth noting that the hexagonal structure in Fig.
1
(b) is the generic attractor for homogeneous initial configurations with equal (or near equal) amounts of the three phases, under periodic boundary conditions. On the other hand, lamellar configurations without a three phase contact line are local attractors that do not relax in the absence of noise or activity. In the following sections, we give qualitative descriptions of how both phase attractors are modified in the presence of non-reciprocal interactions.

2.2
The non-reciprocal model and linear stability analysis

To describe a non-equilibrium system, we need to include active forcing terms that cannot be derived as functional derivatives from a free energy. To lowest order, this can be achieved by adding an action-reaction symmetry breaking term, as first introduced in the NRCH model
[
25
]
. The modified non-equilibrium dynamics are given by:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi_{1}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}\left[\frac{\delta\mathbb{F}}{\delta\phi_{1}}+\alpha\phi_{2}\right]

(4a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi_{2}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}\left[\frac{\delta\mathbb{F}}{\delta\phi_{2}}-\alpha\phi_{1}\right]

(4b)

Like the NRCH model, this modification introduces a type of ‘chaser-chased’ type dynamics between the order parameters
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
. Our model is a minimal framework for describing non-reciprocal interactions within a symmetric ternary phase separation, which cannot be captured by the original NRCH model. This non-reciprocal coupling also eliminates any possibility of analytically determining interfacial profiles between coexisting phases. While in non-reciprocal models with simpler cross-couplings between
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
(such as NRCH), one can define pseudodensities and a pseudopressure to perform a generalised Maxwell construction
[
41
,
42
]
, the cross-coupling structure in our model prevents a similar approach. Moreover, even when such constructions are possible, these pseudovariables generally lack clear physical interpretations, unlike their equilibrium counterparts.

Importantly, non-reciprocal interactions remove a clear definition for interfacial tensions. While in active field theories such as Active Model B(+), multiple interfacial tensions can be defined to describe the rich phenomenology induced by activity
[
43
]
, an analogous construction is not yet established in non-reciprocal models. Hence, as in the foundational work of Active Model B(+)
[
13
]
, the first line of attack must be primarily numerical to establish the phenomenology.

A linear stability analysis on the non-reciprocal model (
4
) reveals the instability structure of a spatially homogeneous state under small spatial perturbations. In the absence of non-reciprocal interactions, the eigenvalues of the linearised stability matrix are purely real: two negative eigenvalues indicate complete stability, whereas the emergence of a positive eigenvalue signals instability toward binary or ternary phase separation. As the level of non-reciprocity
\alpha
is increased, pairs of complex eigenvalues emerge for a range of modes of finite wavenumber
q
. Initially, these complex eigenvalues have negative real parts, corresponding to damped oscillatory modes that do not manifest pattern formation. The resulting dynamics thus qualitatively resemble an equilibrium ternary phase separation, with no significant oscillatory behaviour. At a critical value of
\alpha
(which may vary depending on the composition of the homogeneous initial state), the real parts of these imaginary eigenvalues cross zero and become positive, indicating the appearance of growing oscillatory modes and the onset of pattern formation. This marks an exceptional point transition, characteristic of models in the presence of non-reciprocal interactions
[
37
,
39
]
.

Although linear stability analysis usually only captures the initial growth of perturbations just beyond homogeneous configurations, our numerical simulations reveal that the phase diagram of eigenvalue behaviour provides insights into complex long-time dynamics. When homogeneous configurations have not crossed the exceptional point transition for sufficiently small non-reciprocity as shown in the top left panel in Fig.
2
, the long-time behaviour remains quasi-static, featuring stable three phase contact lines or slowly travelling, stable patterns. In contrast, larger values of non-reciprocity render most configurations inside the three phase coexistence triangle unstable and oscillatory as demonstrated in the top middle and top right panels in Fig.
2
, leading to dynamic, and sometimes chaotic, pattern formation in the long time limit. The details of these behaviour will be discussed in the following sections.

Bifurcation behaviour is plotted in the second and third rows of Fig.
2
. As discussed, increasing non-reciprocity induces an exceptional point transition, where complex eigenvalues acquire positive real parts and oscillatory modes become amplified rather than damped, as exhibited in panels (a)-(e). Navigating across compositions and/or non-reciprocity can also induce a conserved Hopf bifurcation (panels (e)-(f)); it is worth noting that our model (
2
), (
4
) is indeed of the general form proposed in
[
32
]
that gives rise to such large-scale oscillatory transitions between stability and instability.

3
Quasi-Static behaviour for small non-reciprocal values

Any finite strength of non-reciprocal interaction will drive the system to depart from thermal equilibrium, where there is no longer an unambiguous notion of free energy or interfacial tensions. Nevertheless, when the non-reciprocity is sufficiently small, the system exhibits quasi-static behaviour that closely resembles its equilibrium counterpart. We characterise this quasi-static regime by focusing on perturbations to the two equilibrium attractors: the hexagonal structure (with three phase contact lines), and the lamellar structure (without three phase contact lines).

3.1
Contact angles in the quasi-static regime

In equilibrium, symmetric ternary phase separation features interfaces intersecting at 120 degrees at three phase contact lines, a direct consequence of identical interfacial tensions. This behaviour is verified in numerical simulations shown in the top left panel of Fig.
3
. As explained in
B
, our numerical scheme has an angular resolution limit of approximately one degree.

While even weak non-reciprocal interactions eliminate a well-defined notion of interfacial tensions, contact angles remain narrowly distributed near 120 degrees, with only small standard deviations that stay within the resolution limit, as shown in the middle panel of Fig.
3
. Further increase in non-reciprocity disrupts the stable hexagonal structure. As demonstrated in the right panels of Fig.
3
, interfaces become curved and contact angles at three phase contact lines exhibit dynamical variations that clearly exceed the numerical resolution limit. The parameters used to produce the right panels are near the stability limit of the hexagonal structure: any further increase in non-reciprocity renders chaotic motion before settling into oscillatory or travelling patterns, as will be explained in the Section
4
below.

Figure 3:
Upper panels: Histogram of contact angles measured at three phase contact lines under different strengths of non-reciprocal interactions. In equilibrium (left): angles are sharply peaked around 120 degrees, with deviations well within the numerical resolution. Weak non-reciprocity (middle) adjusts the angles slightly but retains a sharp distribution, again with deviations within numerical resolution. Stronger non-reciprocity (right) leads to significantly broadening distribution, indicating dynamically fluctuating, non-static contact angle behaviour. Lower panels: Corresponding snapshots of the system, all initiated with the equilibrium hexagonal structure. While weak non-reciprocity barely changes the geometry, larger non-reciprocity induces curvature and dynamic motion, eventually destabilising three phase contact lines.

3.2
Perturbative calculation for propagation speed of a periodic lamellar pattern

In equilibrium, while phase separation in the presence of a three phase contact line is the global free energy minimum, initialising the system with a lamellar pattern traps it in a local minimum preventing relaxation. Small non-reciprocity values do not disrupt this configuration but cause it to become a stable travelling pattern with a finite propagation speed. Closely following the analytical method in
[
44
,
45
]
, we calculate the velocity of travelling patterns near their onset. From Section
2
, the dynamical equations for the scalar fields are

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}\\
\phi_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\nabla^{2}\left[\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi_{1}}\\
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi_{2}}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&\alpha\\
-\alpha&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}\\
\phi_{2}\end{pmatrix}-\kappa\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\
1&2\end{pmatrix}\nabla^{2}\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}\\
\phi_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right]

(5)

Importantly, we make the assumption that the interfacial profiles in the comoving frame of the travelling pattern are only small perturbations away from the equilibrium profiles, i.e.

\phi_{i}(\xi=x-vt,t)=\phi_{i}^{(0)}(\xi,t)+\epsilon\phi_{i}^{(1)}(\xi,t)+O(\epsilon^{2})

(6)

where
\epsilon
is a small parameter measuring the non-reciprocity, which we set to be of the same order as
\alpha
, or equivalently
\alpha=\bar{\alpha}\epsilon
,
O(\bar{\alpha})=O(1)

denote the equilibrium interfacial profiles with

5

\displaystyle\kappa\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\
1&2\end{pmatrix}\partial_{x}^{2}\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}^{(0)}\\
\phi_{2}^{(0)}\end{pmatrix}=\left.\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi_{1}}\\
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi_{2}}\end{pmatrix}\right|_{(\phi_{1}^{(0)},\phi_{2}^{(0)})}

(7)

At

O(\epsilon)
or equivalently
v=\bar{v}\epsilon
where
\bar{v}

\displaystyle-\bar{v}\frac{d\phi_{1}^{(0)}}{d\xi}

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}\left[\left.\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\phi_{1}^{2}}\right|_{\{\phi_{i}^{(0)}\}}\phi_{1}^{(1)}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\phi_{1}\partial\phi_{2}}\right|_{\{\phi_{i}^{(0)}\}}\phi_{2}^{(1)}-2\kappa\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}\phi_{1}^{(1)}-\kappa\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}\phi_{2}^{(1)}+\bar{\alpha}\phi_{2}^{(0)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}\left[\left.\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\phi_{2}^{2}}\right|_{\{\phi_{i}^{(0)}\}}\phi_{2}^{(1)}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\phi_{1}\partial\phi_{2}}\right|_{\{\phi_{i}^{(0)}\}}\phi_{1}^{(1)}-\kappa\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}\phi_{1}^{(1)}-2\kappa\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}\phi_{2}^{(1)}-\bar{\alpha}\phi_{2}^{(0)}\right]

. (

) produces the value for travelling speed

as proportional to non-reciprocity
\alpha

\displaystyle v=\alpha\frac{\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi_{1}^{(0)}\phi_{2}^{(0)^{\prime}}-\phi_{2}^{(0)}\phi_{1}^{(0)^{\prime}}d\xi\right]}{\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi_{1}^{(0)}\phi_{1}^{(0)^{\prime}}+\Psi_{2}^{(0)}\phi_{2}^{(0)^{\prime}}d\xi\right]}

4
Pattern formation far away from equilibrium

Increasing non-reciprocity destabilises the qusi-static regime: the distribution for contact angles shows increased standard deviation, and the system develops oscillatory pattern formation. Just beyond the onset of this oscillatory instability, fully phase separated configurations with three phase contact lines may still persist, but with the phases rotating around the junctions, as demonstrated in Fig.
5

The rotational motion around three phase contact lines resembles a rotational defect structure that itself becomes unstable at even higher values of non-reciprocity. A similar loss of stability near spiral defects has also been reported in the non-reciprocal Ising model
[
48
]
. In this high non-reciprocity regime, the rotating configuration breaks down to give travelling lamellar patterns.

While carefully prepared initial conditions, such as a fully phase-separated state with three-phase contact lines at intermediate non-reciprocity, can support a limit cycle, more generic setups typically evolve directly into travelling lamellar patterns. As discussed in the Section

These instabilities of the non-reciprocal interfaces seem more complex than interference between finite-wavelength modes; instead, they exhibit spatiotemporal chaos. 5
Conclusion and Outlook

In this paper, we have introduced, developed, and analysed a minimal model for symmetric ternary phase separation with non-reciprocal interactions between two of the three components. Through a combination of numerical simulations, linear stability analysis, and perturbative approaches, we have uncovered a wide range of dynamic behaviour. Our model captures non-equilibrium dynamics intrinsic to non-reciprocal interactions, including limit cycles, travelling waves and exceptional point transitions. Among our results, most notably we find that contact angles remain well-defined and stable under weak non-reciprocity, and we report the restabilisation of travelling patterns in the form of coherent structures far from equilibrium.

While this work has primarily focused on numerical explorations, it opens several analytical directions for future investigations. For instance, the emergence of well-defined contact angles raises the question of whether an effective interfacial tension (or pseudotension as in Active Model B(+)
[
43
]
) can be meaningfully defined in non-reciprocal systems. Equally intriguing is the mechanism behind the destabilisation and restabilisation of travelling waves; in particular, it would be particularly interesting to understand the transition from chaotic behaviour into a coherent, time-periodic structure. Finally, while we have studied wetting behaviour for a three phase coexistence system, wetting near solid boundaries remains unexplored in this present work. Our choice to in effect replace a solid boundary with a spectator fluid phase may overlook important effects of boundary conditions in active matter systems
[
49
]
. Extending the current framework to solid substrates could shed light on how physics of wetting transitions or Young-Dupre’s law are altered by interactions between fluid phases and boundaries, potentially offering new insights into the stability and dynamics of active interfaces.

Appendix A
Derivation of the bulk free energy and the static phase diagram

The Flory-Huggins free energy density
f
is expressed via scalar order parameters
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},\Phi_{3}
which satisfy the conservation condition
\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Phi_{i}=1
,

f(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},\Phi_{3})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Phi_{i}\log\Phi_{i}+\sum_{i<j}\chi\Phi_{i}\Phi_{j}+\chi_{0}\Phi_{1}\Phi_{2}\Phi_{3}

(16)

Expanding the free energy around the equally mixed state,
\Phi_{i}=\phi_{i}+\frac{1}{3}
gives

f(\{\phi_{i}\})=\left[-\log 3+\frac{\chi}{3}+\frac{\chi_{0}}{27}\right]+\left[(-\log 3+1+\frac{2\chi}{3}+\frac{\chi_{0}}{9})\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}\right]\\
+\left[\frac{3}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{2}+(\chi+\frac{\chi_{0}}{3})\sum_{i<j}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}\right]+\left[-\frac{3}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{3}+\chi_{0}\phi_{1}\phi_{2}\phi_{3}\right]+\left[\frac{9}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{4}\right]

(17)

where terms have been grouped to indicate their orders in perturbations
\phi_{i}
. The additive constant can be neglected and the linear term vanishes since
\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}=0
. We further exploit this conservation condition, in particular

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{i<j}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}\right)^{2}=0

(18a)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{3}-3\phi_{1}\phi_{2}\phi_{3}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{2}-\sum_{i<j}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}\right)=0

(18b)

Thus, the bulk free energy density reduces to

\begin{split}f&=\frac{3-\chi-\chi_{0}/3}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{2}+\frac{\chi_{0}-9/2}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{3}+\frac{9}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{4}\\
&:=\frac{a}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{2}+\frac{c}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{3}+\frac{b}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}^{4}\end{split}

confirming (
2
) of the main text.

2
) subject to the constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}=0

\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}

(2\phi_{0},-\phi_{0},-\phi_{0})

Note that this set of solutions is guaranteed as long as

The binodal lines of the system are found by a common tangent construction, requiring the chemical potentials of each component,
\mu_{i}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi_{i}}

Appendix B
Numerical scheme

We perform numerical simulations on a two-dimensional,
96
-by-
96
square domain with periodic boundary conditions. Space and time are discretised as
\Delta x=\Delta y=1
and
\Delta t=0.01
. All simulations use a finite-difference scheme to integrate the dynamical equations (
4
) forward in time. Model parameters are fixed at
a=-0.1
,
b=9
,
c=-1
, and
\kappa=0.4
. These values are representative and do not qualitatively alter the results presented. The simulation results are visualised using a RGB colormap, with red (chased) and green (chaser) phases rescaled to enhance contrast.

To numerically measure the contact angles, we identify interfaces by thresholding the magnitude of scalar field gradients to construct a binary mask. The resulting structures are skeletonized using the sknw package, which extracts a graph-based representation of the interface. Three phase contact lines are detected as nodes with three neighbours, and each connecting interface is fitted either with a straight line or a circular arc. Close to equilibrium, the linear fit is appropriate for configurations with equal compositions, which is the setup we use. Non-reciprocal interactions introduce curvature that is typically uniform along a given interface, making circular fits more suitable. Fitting the interfaces enables the calculation of tangents near the three-phase contact lines, from which the contact angles are determined.

There are two potential sources of numerical error in this scheme. First, the choice of a gradient threshold leads to a binary graph with finite interface width of around one pixel, limiting spatial resolution. Subsequent skeletonisation reduces the 2-dimensional binary mask to 1-dimensional graphs, which may distort the geometry of the exact interface, especially near three phase contact lines. A second source of error comes from interface fitting. While linear approximations work well for near-equilibrium configurations with symmetric compositions (e.g., hexagonal structures that we study), non-reciprocal interactions create curvature that is only approximately uniform, and may not be fully captured by a circular fit. The level of these errors is roughly around one degree, as shown in the equilibrium case.

Appendix C
Generalised Sturm-Liouville operators

A Sturm-Liouville operator can be interpreted as a self-adjoint operator under the inner product defined on real-valued functions (with weight function of the inner product
w(x)=1
)

\langle\psi_{1}(\xi),\psi_{2}(\xi)\rangle=\int\psi_{1}\psi_{2}\,d\xi

(26)

An operator
\mathcal{L}
is Sturm-Liouville under (
26
) if

\langle\mathcal{L}\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\rangle=\langle\psi_{1},\mathcal{L}\psi_{2}\rangle

(27)

for all twice continuously differentiable functions
\psi_{i}(\xi)
in the domain of
\mathcal{L}
, whose boundary conditions satisfy
\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\xi)\rightarrow 0
as
\xi\rightarrow\pm\infty
. In this Appendix, we show that
\mathcal{L}
is a matrix Sturm-Liouville (self-adjoint) differential operator defined on vector-valued functions, via the inner product

\langle\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{3}\\
\psi_{4}\end{pmatrix}\rangle=\int\psi_{1}\psi_{3}+\psi_{2}\psi_{4}\,d\xi

(28)

The operator in (
11
) reads

\mathcal{L}:=\begin{pmatrix}f_{11}(\xi)&f_{12}(\xi)\\
f_{21}(\xi)&f_{22}(\xi)\end{pmatrix}-\kappa\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\
1&2\end{pmatrix}\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}

(29)

Since this is a linear differential operator, we can prove part-by-part. Beginning with the first part, namely

\mathcal{L}_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}f_{11}(\xi)&f_{12}(\xi)\\
f_{21}(\xi)&f_{22}(\xi)\end{pmatrix}

(30)

This is Sturm-Liouville (for twice continuously differentiable functions
\psi_{i}(\xi)
satisfying
\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\xi)\rightarrow 0
as
\xi\rightarrow\pm\infty
) since

\langle\mathcal{L}_{1}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{3}\\
\psi_{4}\end{pmatrix}\rangle=\int d\xi\,f_{11}\psi_{1}\psi_{3}+f_{12}\psi_{2}\psi_{3}+f_{21}\psi_{1}\psi_{4}+f_{22}\psi_{2}\psi_{4}

(31)

and

\langle\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\end{pmatrix},\mathcal{L}_{1}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{3}\\
\psi_{4}\end{pmatrix}\rangle=\int d\xi\,f_{11}\psi_{1}\psi_{3}+f_{12}\psi_{1}\psi_{4}+f_{21}\psi_{2}\psi_{3}+f_{22}\psi_{2}\psi_{4}

(32)

But since
f_{ij}(\xi)=\partial^{2}f/\partial\phi_{i}\phi_{j}
and hence equals
f_{ji}
, these two expressions are equal. Similarly, defining

\mathcal{L}_{2}:=\kappa\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\
1&2\end{pmatrix}\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}

(33)

Then

\langle\mathcal{L}_{2}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{3}\\
\psi_{4}\end{pmatrix}\rangle=\int d\xi\,\kappa(2\psi_{1}^{\prime\prime}\psi_{3}+\psi_{2}^{\prime\prime}\psi_{3}+\psi_{1}^{\prime\prime}\psi_{4}+2\psi_{2}^{\prime\prime}\psi_{4})\\
=\int d\xi\,\kappa(2\psi_{1}\psi_{3}^{\prime\prime}+\psi_{2}\psi_{3}^{\prime\prime}+\psi_{1}\psi_{4}^{\prime\prime}+2\psi_{2}\psi_{4}^{\prime\prime})=\langle\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\end{pmatrix},\mathcal{L}_{2}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{3}\\
\psi_{4}\end{pmatrix}\rangle

(34)

where in each term, we have applied integration by parts twice. Hence,
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{1}-\mathcal{L}_{2}
is a Sturm-Liouville operator for vectors of equations.

Next, we prove the intermediate result just above (
14

(\phi_{1}^{(0)}(\xi),\phi_{2}^{(0)}(\xi))
satisfy the equation

\displaystyle\left.\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi_{1}}\\
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi_{2}}\end{pmatrix}\right|_{(\phi_{1}^{(0)},\phi_{2}^{(0)})}-\kappa\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\
1&2\end{pmatrix}\partial_{\xi}^{2}\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}^{(0)}\\
\phi_{2}^{(0)}\end{pmatrix}=0

(35)

Differentiating this with respect to
\xi
produces

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}f_{11}&f_{12}\\
f_{21}&f_{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}d\phi_{1}^{(0)}/d\xi\\
d\phi_{2}^{(0)}/d\xi\end{pmatrix}-\kappa\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\
1&2\end{pmatrix}\partial_{\xi}^{2}\begin{pmatrix}d\phi_{1}^{(0)}/d\xi\\
d\phi_{2}^{(0)}/d\xi\end{pmatrix}=0

(36)

where chain rule is applied to the first term. This proves our claim that
\begin{pmatrix}d\phi_{1}^{(0)}/d\xi\\
d\phi_{2}^{(0)}/d\xi\end{pmatrix}
is a solution to the homogeneous equation
\mathcal{L}\vec{\phi}=0

Given the homogeneous solution
\begin{pmatrix}d\phi_{1}^{(0)}/d\xi\\
d\phi_{2}^{(0)}/d\xi\end{pmatrix}
