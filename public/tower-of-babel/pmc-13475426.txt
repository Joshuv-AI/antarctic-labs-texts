Prognostic Value of QT and QTc Interval Dispersion in Acute Pulmonary Embolism: A Retrospective Single-Center Study
Authors: Huseyin Enes Cıdık, Ertugrul Altinbilek, Derya Ozturk, Ilayda Civelek, Abuzer Coskun, Mustafa Calik, Melike Mercan Baspinar, Ibrahim Sarbay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute pulmonary embolism (PE) carries substantial mortality risk, yet readily available electrocardiographic markers of ventricular repolarisation heterogeneity remain underutilised in prognostication. This study evaluated the prognostic value of QT dispersion (QTd) and corrected QT dispersion (QTcd) for predicting 30-day mortality in patients with acute PE.MethodsThis single-centre, retrospective observational study enrolled 143 consecutive adult patients with CT-angiography-confirmed acute PE presenting to the emergency department between January 2023 and November 2024. PE severity was stratified per 2019 ESC guidelines. Standard 12-lead ECGs obtained at admission, prior to any PE-directed treatment, were analysed by a single blinded observer. The primary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality. Independent predictors were identified by multivariable logistic regression, and optimal cut-off values were determined by ROC curve analysis.ResultsThe 30-day mortality rate was 32.2% (n=46/143). The mean age was 71.0 ± 14.3 years; 13.3% of patients were haemodynamically unstable and 20.3% had active malignancy. Non-survivors exhibited significantly greater QTd (60.2 vs 49.3 ms) and QTcd (80.6 vs 63.1 ms) than survivors (both p<0.001). On multivariable analysis, QTcd remained an independent predictor of mortality (OR 1.09 per ms, 95% CI 1.05–1.13; p=0.001), alongside malignancy (OR 5.05), altered mental status (OR 6.10), mean arterial pressure (OR 0.97 per mmHg), and CT-derived left ventricular diameter (OR 0.92 per mm). ROC analysis identified cut-off values of QTd ≥56 ms (AUC 0.812, sensitivity 72%, specificity 83%) and QTcd ≥67 ms (AUC 0.829, sensitivity 87%, specificity 67%, negative predictive value 92%).ConclusionQTcd is an independent predictor of 30-day mortality in acute PE and provides clinically useful discrimination across all ESC risk strata. The cut-off values identified may complement established risk scores in emergency prognostication; prospective multicentre validation is required before broader clinical adoption.

Introduction

Acute pulmonary embolism (PE) is a major cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, with presentations ranging from asymptomatic findings to massive PE with haemodynamic collapse.1 Risk stratification guides therapeutic decisions, including thrombolytic therapy, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and haemodynamic support.2 Risk assessment uses clinical scoring systems (PESI and sPESI), cardiac biomarkers, and imaging findings of RV dysfunction.3 The primary determinant of mortality in acute PE is acute right ventricular (RV) failure, which results from impaired RV filling and decreased RV output. Clinical findings such as tachycardia, hypotension, syncope, and respiratory failure are early markers of poor prognosis in acute PE.2–4

The QT interval reflects the total duration of ventricular depolarisation and repolarisation, and an increased QT duration or dispersion indicates heterogeneous ventricular repolarisation — a recognised substrate for arrhythmogenesis.5 In acute PE, pulmonary pressure overload and right ventricular strain can produce regional differences in repolarisation that are reflected in QT dispersion (QTd) and its rate-corrected counterpart, QTc dispersion (QTcd).6 Although QTd and related repolarisation markers have been reported in acute PE, their independent prognostic value beyond established risk scores — and clinically applicable cut-off values, particularly for QTc dispersion — remain incompletely established.6–13

This study evaluated the prognostic value of QT prolongation, the QTc interval, QT dispersion, and QTc dispersion in patients with acute PE. We aimed to (1) compare QT parameters between survivors and non-survivors; (2) identify mortality predictors using multivariable analysis; (3) determine QT and QTc dispersion cutoff values via ROC curve analysis; and (4) assess the relationship between QT parameters and cardiac biomarkers and imaging findings. Using standardized ECG protocols and comprehensive data, we aimed to support the integration of QT parameters into acute PE risk algorithms.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

This single-center, retrospective, observational study was conducted at the University of Health Sciences Şişli Hamidiye Etfal Training and Research Hospital. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (No. 4432, June 11, 2024). This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Adult patients (≥18 years) presenting to the Emergency Department between January 1, 2023, and November 1, 2024, with acute PE confirmed by pulmonary CT angiography (CTA) were included. For electrocardiographic analysis, only the first standard 12-lead ECG obtained immediately at ED admission, before the initiation of PE-directed treatment or administration of any acute QT-modifying medication, was evaluated. When more than one ECG was available, the earliest pre-treatment ECG was selected. The exclusion criteria were age <18 years, pregnancy, missing clinical/imaging/ECG data, unavailable admission pre-treatment ECG, and unavailable 30-day mortality records.

Imaging and Clinical Data

Pulmonary CTA was performed using a 128-slice Siemens SOMATOM Definition Edge scanner. The RV and LV short-axis diameters were measured on axial images at the tricuspid and mitral valve levels, respectively. The RV/LV ratios were calculated accordingly. All CTA interpretations were performed by a radiology specialist.

Transthoracic echocardiography (Philips Epiq 7) was used to determine the EF, pulmonary artery pressure (PAP), and RV size. Demographic data, comorbidities, vital signs, biomarkers (high-sensitivity troponin and D-dimer), and presenting symptoms were extracted from electronic medical records. Thirty-day mortality outcomes were obtained from the Republic of Türkiye’s Ministry of Health Death Notification System.

Electrocardiographic Analysis

Standard 12-lead ECGs were acquired upon admission using a Schiller Cardiovit AT-102 plus recorder (25 mm/s, 10 mV). All measurements were performed manually by a single physician experienced in ECG interpretation who was blinded to the patients’ clinical outcomes. QT intervals were measured from the onset of the QRS complex to the end of the T wave, defined as the point at which the terminal limb of the T wave returned to the isoelectric baseline, in all leads in which T-wave termination could be clearly identified; leads with unclear T-wave termination, marked artifact, or uninterpretable morphology were excluded. QT dispersion (QTd) was defined as the difference between QTmax and QTmin. QTc was calculated using Bazett’s formula (QT/√RR), and QTc dispersion (QTcd) as QTcmax – QTcmin (Figure 1).Figure 1Schematic representation of QT interval measurement and Qt/QTc dispersion.A diagram showing QT interval measurement with two ECG leads illustrating QTmax and QTmin dispersion.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality, defined as death from any cause within 30 days after ED admission. Because reliable information on whether death occurred during the index hospitalization or after discharge was not consistently available in the retrospective dataset, mortality was analyzed as overall 30-day all-cause mortality rather than being separated into in-hospital and post-discharge mortality. The secondary outcome was the association of QT/QTc dispersion with right ventricular dysfunction on CTA (RV/LV ratio) and echocardiography. Data on thrombolytic therapy, ICU admission, and composite adverse events were not consistently available in this retrospective dataset and were therefore not analyzed.

PE severity was stratified according to the ESC guidelines into high-risk (haemodynamic instability, SBP <90 mmHg), intermediate-risk (haemodynamically stable with CT-derived RV/LV ratio ≥1.0), and low-risk (haemodynamically stable with RV/LV ratio <1.0) categories.1

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were analyzed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests for normality and are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR). Group comparisons were performed using independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test, and categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test.

ROC curve analysis was used to determine the optimal cut-off values for QT, QTc, QTd, and QTcd. Univariate logistic regression was used to identify mortality predictors, and variables with p<0.10 were entered into multivariate logistic regression models. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v28.0, with p<0.05 considered significant. No a priori sample size calculation was performed given the retrospective design. A post-hoc power analysis using the Hanley–McNeil method demonstrated >99% statistical power for the primary ROC outcome (observed AUC = 0.829; n=46 events, n=97 non-events; two-sided α=0.05).

Model calibration was assessed with the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and multicollinearity with variance inflation factors (VIF). Linearity of continuous variables in the logit was examined using the Box–Tidwell procedure; where nonlinearity was detected, the variable was additionally modeled in categorized form at its ROC-derived threshold.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 143 patients diagnosed with acute PE were included. Eighty-six patients (60.1%) were female and 57 (39.9%) were male, with a mean age of 71.0 ± 14.3 years (median 73.0; range 30–96). The overall 30-day all-cause mortality rate was 32.2% (n=46). At least one comorbidity was present in 124 patients (86.7%), whereas 19 (13.3%) had no known comorbid condition; a prior history of PE was reported in 14 patients (9.8%) and of deep vein thrombosis in 7 (4.9%). On admission, 117 patients (81.8%) were in sinus rhythm and 26 (18.2%) in atrial fibrillation, with no other supraventricular arrhythmia recorded. Electrocardiograms were obtained in all patients irrespective of haemodynamic status, comprising 124 (86.7%) haemodynamically stable and 19 (13.3%) unstable patients. Pulmonary Embolism Severity Stratification

According to the 2019 ESC criteria, patients were stratified by haemodynamic status and CT-derived right ventricular load into high-risk (SBP <90 mmHg; n=19, 13.3%), intermediate-risk (SBP ≥90 mmHg with RV/LV ratio ≥1.0; n=59, 41.3%), and low-risk (SBP ≥90 mmHg with RV/LV ratio <1.0; n=65, 45.5%) groups. The 30-day mortality rate rose progressively across strata, from 24.6% in the low-risk group to 35.6% in the intermediate-risk and 47.4% in the high-risk group.

Comparison of Survivors and Non-Survivors

Comparisons between survivors (n=97) and non-survivors (n=46) are presented in Table 2. Among comorbidities, malignancy (41.3% vs 11.3%, p=0.001) and heart failure (26.1% vs 7.2%, p=0.002) were the strongest correlates of mortality. Altered mental status was more frequent among non-survivors (23.9% vs 8.2%, p=0.010), whereas chest pain (2.2% vs 17.5%, p=0.010) and presyncope/dizziness (2.2% vs 12.4%, p=0.048) were less common, suggesting more rapid progression to haemodynamic collapse without typical prodromal symptoms. Anatomically, left main pulmonary artery involvement was more prevalent in non-survivors (10.9% vs 2.1%, p=0.023), whereas left segmental emboli occurred only in survivors (0% vs 9.3%, p=0.033). Non-survivors also had lower systolic (109.1 vs 121.2 mmHg, p=0.002), diastolic (69.1 vs 75.2 mmHg, p=0.028), and mean arterial pressures (82.4 vs 90.5 mmHg, p=0.003), a smaller CT-derived LV diameter (p=0.010), and higher D-dimer levels (p=0.049). Electrocardiographically, non-survivors exhibited greater QT dispersion (60.2 vs 49.3 ms, p<0.001), greater QTc dispersion (80.6 vs 63.1 ms, p<0.001), and a longer maximum QTc interval (518.2 vs 498.2 ms, p=0.002), indicating greater electrical heterogeneity. Predictors of 30-Day Mortality

The results of univariate and multivariable logistic regression are presented in Table 3 and Figure 4. On univariate analysis, known comorbidity, heart failure, malignancy, altered mental status, chest pain, left main pulmonary artery embolism, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, CT-derived LV diameter, QT dispersion, maximum QTc interval, and QTc dispersion were significantly associated with mortality (p<0.05), whereas presyncope/dizziness, D-dimer, and the QTc interval were not. In the multivariable model, malignancy (OR 5.05, 95% CI 1.68–15.15; p=0.004), altered mental status (OR 6.10, 95% CI 1.76–21.28; p=0.004), mean arterial pressure (OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.94–1.00; p=0.038), CT-derived LV diameter (OR 0.92, 95% CI 0.86–0.98; p=0.015), and QTc dispersion (OR 1.09 per ms, 95% CI 1.05–1.13; p=0.001) remained independent predictors of 30-day mortality. When QTc dispersion was dichotomised at the ROC-derived threshold (≥67 ms) to account for its non-linear association with the logit, it remained a strong independent predictor (OR 12.4, 95% CI 4.25–36.23; p<0.001). Receiver Operating Characteristic Analysis

ROC analysis for QT and QTc dispersion is shown in Table 4 and Figure 5. Subgroup and Robustness Analyses

To determine whether QTc dispersion retained prognostic value independently of severity, rhythm, and chronic QT-modifying conditions, prespecified subgroup analyses were performed. QTc dispersion differed significantly across the ESC severity strata (Kruskal–Wallis, p=0.047) and discriminated survivors from non-survivors within each stratum: high-risk (63.1 ± 9.6 vs 90.2 ± 19.6 ms; p=0.004), intermediate-risk (65.7 ± 12.1 vs 77.4 ± 12.8 ms; p=0.001), and low-risk (61.1 ± 16.5 vs 79.4 ± 10.6 ms; p<0.001). After adjustment for the ESC risk stratum, QTc dispersion remained an independent predictor of 30-day mortality (OR 1.08 per ms, p<0.001), whereas the stratum itself was no longer significant once QTc dispersion was included, indicating that repolarisation heterogeneity carries prognostic information beyond conventional severity stratification.

The association was preserved across clinically relevant subgroups. Among haemodynamically stable patients (n=124), QTc dispersion remained higher in non-survivors than survivors (78.3 ± 11.8 vs 63.1 ± 14.9 ms; p<0.001), and in patients in sinus rhythm (n=117) it likewise discriminated outcome (79.3 ± 14.4 vs 61.2 ± 12.3 ms; p<0.001). QTc dispersion did not differ by prior PE history (p=0.102), coronary artery disease (p=0.466), or hypertension (p=0.765). Although patients with atrial fibrillation (78.5 ± 17.9 vs 66.6 ± 15.4 ms; p=0.002) and heart failure (82.6 ± 19.3 vs 66.6 ± 15.0 ms; p<0.001) had higher absolute QTc dispersion, atrial fibrillation was not independently associated with mortality (p=0.221), and QTc dispersion remained independent after adjustment for heart failure. Finally, QTc dispersion correlated with markers of acute right ventricular strain—troponin (Spearman r=0.203, p=0.015) and the CT-derived RV/LV ratio (r=0.176, p=0.036)—and was higher in patients with echocardiographic right ventricular dilatation (72.7 ± 13.9 vs 66.9 ± 17.3 ms; p=0.010), supporting an association with the acute embolic event.

Discussion

This single-centre, retrospective cohort study of 143 patients with acute PE demonstrated that QTc dispersion is an independent prognostic marker for 30-day mortality, even after adjustment for established clinical, laboratory, and imaging predictors. In the multivariable analysis, QTc dispersion remained independently associated with mortality alongside malignancy, altered mental status, mean arterial pressure, and CT-derived LV diameter, and ROC analysis identified clinically useful thresholds of QT dispersion ≥56 ms and QTc dispersion ≥67 ms. These results support the integration of ventricular repolarisation parameters into risk-stratification strategies for acute PE.

Table 5 summarises previous studies of QT-derived parameters in acute PE alongside the present findings. In contrast to earlier work — which largely assessed QTc prolongation rather than dispersion, compared patients with controls, or focused on diagnostic or localisation endpoints — our study shows that QTc dispersion independently predicts mortality and retains prognostic value within each guideline-defined risk stratum. Importantly, the prognostic value of QTc dispersion was not confined to haemodynamically unstable patients. Within each ESC severity stratum — including the low-risk group — QTc dispersion discriminated survivors from non-survivors, and it remained higher in non-survivors among both haemodynamically stable patients (78.3 ± 11.8 vs 63.1 ± 14.9 ms; p<0.001) and patients in sinus rhythm (p<0.001). This indicates that QTc dispersion conveys prognostic information beyond what can be inferred from blood pressure or rhythm alone, consistent with the hypothesis that it reflects acute right ventricular afterload-related electrical instability not fully captured by haemodynamic or anatomical parameters.

Our findings align with earlier reports that repolarisation heterogeneity increases in PE and is associated with RV strain, myocardial ischaemia, and adverse outcomes.7,9 Ermiş et al reported markedly increased QTc dispersion in high-risk and deceased patients, with a threshold of >71.5 ms (sensitivity 71%, specificity 73%)7 — closely matching our cut-off of 67 ms — while Akgüllü et al identified QTc dispersion thresholds of 64.5 ms and >97 ms for early mortality prediction,8 and Ding et al demonstrated stepwise increases in QTc dispersion with PE severity and its reduction after successful treatment.9 Alsancak et al and Günay et al similarly found elevated QTd and QTc dispersion in PE, although their thresholds (58–75 ms) showed limited predictive performance,6,10 and Park et al reported a higher QTc difference between leads V1 and V6 in acute PE.11 Our cut-offs (56 ms and 67 ms) fall within this reported spectrum; the variability across studies likely reflects differences in population, ECG measurement technique, QT-correction formula (Bazett vs Fridericia14), and timing of acquisition relative to symptom onset and treatment.15,16

Among the other independent predictors, non-survivors showed lower systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial pressures, indicating early circulatory decompensation; reduced mean arterial pressure remained independently predictive of mortality. A smaller CT-derived LV diameter also independently predicted death, likely reflecting interventricular dependence, whereby elevated RV afterload causes septal shift, impaired LV filling, and reduced cardiac output — consistent with the haemodynamic cascade of acute PE. Malignancy and heart failure were more prevalent in non-survivors, and altered mental status independently predicted mortality, reflecting systemic hypoperfusion and advanced cardiopulmonary compromise; these observations accord with the predictors of in-hospital mortality (tachycardia, shock, cancer, and heart failure) reported in the meta-analysis by Keller et al.17

The 30-day all-cause mortality of 32.2% exceeds the 5–15% typically reported in unselected PE registries, reflecting cohort characteristics rather than an outlier outcome: patients were elderly (71.0 ± 14.3 years), more than half were intermediate- or high-risk (54.5%), 13.3% were haemodynamically unstable, and malignancy — a strong predictor of death — was present in 20.3%. The cohort comprised emergency-department admissions undergoing complete diagnostic work-up, under-representing lower-acuity outpatient-managed cases, and mortality was defined as all-cause rather than PE-specific. Lower rates have been reported in less selected populations — 12% at one month by Surov et al18 and 8.9–9.8% at 30 days by Borrero et al.19 Unlike Wang et al,20 who found higher age among non-survivors, we observed no significant age difference between groups; our mean age (71 years) exceeds that of other cohorts (≈67 years),21,22 consistent with an older, comorbid tertiary-centre population. Mortality did not differ by sex, in keeping with attenuated sex-related differences in PE outcomes;19 our 60.1% female proportion aligns with prior reports,23 although male predominance has also been described.24

A notable observation is that QTc dispersion remained independently predictive whereas troponin, RV diameter, RV/LV ratio, and pulmonary artery pressure did not, contrasting with studies identifying both QTc dispersion and troponin as independent predictors.8 This may reflect the distinct biological signals captured: troponin indicates myocardial necrosis from RV ischaemia, whereas QTc dispersion reflects electrical instability and repolarisation heterogeneity.25 Fatal PE can occur without marked troponin elevation through massive embolic burden, acute cor pulmonale, or malignant arrhythmia,26 and the prognostic performance of troponin is timing-dependent, with elevation potentially delayed relative to the immediate electrical effects captured on the admission ECG.27,28 Consistent with this, earlier QTc-prolongation studies by Buppajarntham et al and Punukollu et al linked a dichotomous QTc threshold (>460 ms) with RV dysfunction, ischaemia, and longer ICU stay but not with in-hospital mortality;12,13 dispersion metrics, which capture the spatial heterogeneity of repolarisation, may be more sensitive markers of electrical instability and arrhythmic risk.5

D-dimer levels were unexpectedly lower in non-survivors, contrasting with their established role as markers of thrombus burden.29 This may relate to the timing of measurement relative to symptom onset and anticoagulation,30 late presentation or chronic thrombi,31 or a biphasic relationship in which both very high and very low levels reflect adverse biology.32 Examining QTc dispersion in relation to D-dimer across troponin and ejection-fraction strata, we found that myocardial function modified this relationship: in patients with reduced ejection fraction (15–60%), QTc dispersion showed greater variability and a modest upward trend with increasing D-dimer, whereas a U-shaped pattern emerged in the mid-troponin category. These nonlinear patterns suggest a complex interplay between thrombotic activity, RV strain, myocardial dysfunction, and repolarisation instability that warrants further investigation.

The thresholds for QT dispersion (≥56 ms) and QTc dispersion (≥67 ms) showed clinically useful discrimination (AUCs 0.77–0.83) and offer complementary information: the QT dispersion cut-off provides high specificity (82.5%) and negative predictive value (86.0%), useful for ruling out high-risk patients, whereas the QTc dispersion cut-off offers high sensitivity (87.0%) and negative predictive value (91.5%) and may help flag patients for closer monitoring, although this requires prospective confirmation. Although not yet ready for clinical implementation, several applications can be envisioned: QTc dispersion ≥67 ms on admission could prompt early echocardiography, closer monitoring, or consideration of advanced therapies in intermediate-risk patients;33 combining it with established risk scores (PESI, sPESI) and biomarkers may improve stratification beyond individual markers;8 and serial ECG assessment of QT changes after anticoagulation or reperfusion may carry prognostic value, since persistent abnormalities despite treatment portend worse outcomes.25,34 QT dispersion measurement is inexpensive, non-invasive, and readily available, making it an attractive adjunct for further evaluation.33

The management of intermediate-to-high- and high-risk PE is increasingly shaped by advanced reperfusion and support strategies, providing a clinical context for prognostic markers such as QTc dispersion. In intermediate-to-high-risk patients with evidence of right ventricular strain, ultrasound-accelerated catheter-directed thrombolysis (USAT) has emerged as an effective option; in a retrospective series of acute–subacute PE, USAT was associated with sustained haemodynamic stability and significant improvement in ventricular function at follow-up, without recurrent venous thromboembolism.35 At the extreme end of the spectrum — massive PE with haemodynamic collapse — mechanical circulatory support becomes central, with veno-arterial or veno-arterial-venous ECMO serving as a bridge to recovery alongside surgical embolectomy or catheter-based thrombectomy within a structured algorithm.36 Within this evolving framework, a readily available admission marker reflecting repolarisation heterogeneity under RV strain could, in principle, help flag patients for earlier escalation; whether QTc dispersion can meaningfully refine selection for these advanced pathways remains to be established and should be tested prospectively.

Strengths and Limitations

The study’s strengths include detailed electrocardiographic analysis, incorporation of multimodal clinical and imaging data, and stratified evaluation of haemostatic–electrical interactions.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-center design limits causal inference and generalizability and raises the possibility of selection bias: because the cohort was restricted to patients who underwent complete diagnostic work-up (CT pulmonary angiography, echocardiography, troponin, and an interpretable admission ECG), lower-acuity, outpatient-managed cases were under-represented, skewing the sample toward higher-acuity patients. Second, residual confounding cannot be excluded: several QT-modifying variables — antiarrhythmic and beta-blocker use, serum electrolytes (potassium, magnesium, calcium), renal function, active infection or sepsis, and congenital long-QT syndrome — could not be systematically extracted for all patients, and data on in-hospital management (anticoagulation, systemic thrombolysis, catheter-directed or surgical intervention) were unavailable. As treatment intensity follows initial risk stratification and itself influences outcome, this is a potential confounder; however, QTc dispersion was derived from the admission ECG — obtained before any PE-directed treatment or QT-modifying medication — which mitigates acute iatrogenic confounding and reflects repolarization status at the point of initial risk assessment. Third, manual QT measurement is subject to observer variability; measurements were performed by a single blinded observer, so a formal interobserver reliability statistic (ICC or kappa) could not be calculated. Fourth, Bazett’s formula–derived QTc is less reliable in atrial fibrillation, and AF patients were not excluded; nonetheless, the primary association held in the sinus-rhythm subgroup (p<0.001), and AF was not independently associated with mortality. Fifth, the absence of pre-PE baseline ECGs means the observed QTc dispersion cannot be confirmed as acute rather than pre-existing; however, its correlation with markers of acute right ventricular strain (troponin, CT-derived RV/LV ratio, echocardiographic RV dilatation) and its independence from chronic QT-prolonging conditions (hypertension, coronary artery disease, prior PE) support an acute contribution. Sixth, although the final model’s events-per-variable ratio (≈9) approached the conventional threshold of 10, the model was parsimonious and well-calibrated. Finally, cause-specific mortality could not be determined, as the retrospective dataset recorded only all-cause death; this limits attribution of deaths to PE versus comorbid conditions. Prospective, multicenter studies with serial ECGs and standardized electrolyte, renal, and medication data are needed to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

QTc dispersion was an independent predictor of 30-day mortality in this cohort of patients with acute pulmonary embolism and was associated with markers of thrombotic activity and myocardial dysfunction. Repolarization heterogeneity may provide additional prognostic information; however, its incremental value beyond established markers and clinical risk scores remains to be confirmed in prospective studies.

Rather than acting in isolation, QTc dispersion may be most informative as one component of a multimarker approach: combined with the mortality predictors identified in this study (malignancy, altered mental status, mean arterial pressure, and CT-derived left ventricular dimension) and with guideline-based risk stratification, it could contribute to a more complete assessment of PE severity. Such a multidimensional approach — rather than any single marker — is most likely to refine prognostication and warrants prospective validation.

The cut-off values of ≥56 ms (QT dispersion) and ≥67 ms (QTc dispersion) may help identify patients at higher and lower risk of death (high sensitivity and high negative predictive value, respectively). While traditional markers such as blood pressure or oxygen saturation reflect current haemodynamic status, ventricular repolarization indices may capture subclinical electrical instability even when overt parameters appear stable, and could therefore complement existing prognostic frameworks. Prospective multicenter studies, standardized QT measurement protocols, and evaluation of their incremental value within multivariable risk models are needed to support broader clinical adoption, and future research should determine whether QT-guided management can improve outcomes in acute PE.
