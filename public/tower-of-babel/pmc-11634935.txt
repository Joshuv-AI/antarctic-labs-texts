Paediatric patient perceptions of healthcare professionals: contributions to a communication curriculum
Authors: Jakob Thestrup, Jette Led Sørensen, Barbara Hoff Esbjørn, Jane Hybschmann, Thomas Leth Frandsen, Patricia DeCosta, Line Klingen Gjærde
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommunication skills are a vital but often neglected part of paediatric training. To make communication training more responsive to patient needs, children and adolescents should be involved in developing the communication curriculum for healthcare professionals, though this is rarely the case. The present study explored children and adolescents’ perceptions of healthcare professionals to identify recommendations for healthcare professionals to contribute to formulating goals, learning objectives, and competencies for an interprofessional paediatric communication curriculum. We used narrative and play-based interviews to include the perceptions of preschool children aged 3–6 years (n = 8) and an online questionnaire to explore those of schoolchildren and adolescents aged 5–18 years (n = 54). We did a thematic analysis of the qualitative interview data and open-ended questionnaire responses, which showed that preschool children found familiar approaches, physical contact, and their parents comforting and that healthcare professionals should use playful methods, child-friendly words, and tangible rewards. Schoolchildren and adolescents preferred healthcare professionals who were friendly, patient, attentive, communicated clearly, and engaged them in conversation. They did not like when healthcare professionals appeared stressed, did not keep their promises, or forced them to do something.Conclusions: We condensed perceptions of children and adolescents into tips and statements to be used in further development of a communication curriculum for paediatric healthcare professionals. Our findings emphasize that paediatric communication training should focus on healthcare professionals’ attitude and appearance, strategies for building trust and engaging patients in treatment and care, the use of age-appropriate communication, and understanding the cognitive development of children and adolescents. Introduction

Paediatric patients have unique communication needs due to their varying developmental stages and cognitive abilities [1, 2]. The triadic relationship between healthcare professionals (HCPs), the patient, and their parents is complex and presents a significant challenge in paediatric encounters [3]. Communication and interpersonal skills are critical competencies that all paediatric HCPs must acquire and maintain but they are often embedded in the hidden curriculum, meaning that they are implicitly learned and taken for granted rather than formally taught [4, 5]. Consequently, many paediatric HCPs receive little or no training in communication.

As hospitalization can be a stressful experience for children and adolescents, effective communication is essential to enhance their overall hospital experiences and involve them in their own treatment and care [6–8]. Many paediatric patients experience anxiety and struggle to understand the information they receive, potentially making it challenging for them to cooperate and follow their treatment [9, 10]. To improve the hospital experiences of children and adolescents, their needs and preferences must be investigated, considered, and incorporated [11–13]. Söderbäck et al. [14] argues that healthcare should always be tailored to children and adolescents’ preferences and uphold their right to participate in all aspects of healthcare delivery, including research and the education of HCPs. Involving patients in research and education provides important knowledge and learning opportunities shaped by their lived experience [15], which can be used to inform training programmes for HCPs to improve knowledge and skills such as communication [16, 17].

Involving patients in healthcare education also promotes patient-centred approaches to practice [18]. In curriculum development, patient insights can help align learning objectives to patients’ specific needs [18, 19]. Despite the potential benefits of engaging patients in healthcare education, the involvement of paediatric patients is generally absent [16]. Some of the challenges related to engaging children and adolescents in research and education include ethical concerns and communication barriers [20]. This means that most research exploring children and adolescents’ experiences and perceptions relies on information that parents have provided [8, 9, 14]. A previous review investigated the preferences of children and adolescents about the way diagnosis and information is conveyed; however, the majority of the participants included were adolescents with cancer [21].

In this study, we explored children and adolescents’ perceptions of HCPs to identify recommendations for HCPs to contribute to formulating goals, learning objectives, and competencies in an interprofessional paediatric communication curriculum.

Materials and methods

We conducted secondary analysis of narrative and play-based interviews with preschool children (3–6 years) to include the perspectives of the youngest children. Narrative and play-based interviews offer a developmentally appropriate method to get insights into children’s lifeworld using playful approaches and items familiar to young children [22]. For schoolchildren and adolescents (5–18 years), we used a child and youth-friendly questionnaire design.

Study design

To investigate the perceptions of preschool children, we did a secondary analysis [23] of videos and transcribed material from narrative and play-based interviews used in a previous publication [24]. The data had been generated to investigate preschool children’s own experiences and perceptions of treatment and care when living with a chronic illness. The focus in our secondary analysis was how preschool children experienced and perceived HCPs.

To investigate schoolchildren and adolescents’ hospital experiences and their perceptions of HCPs, we used a cross-sectional study design. We adhered to the Guidelines for Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) (Supplemental Table S1) [25].

Recruitment and sample

The analysis of the narrative and play-based interviews included eight children aged 3–6 years living with cystic fibrosis or type 1 diabetes. The participants had been purposively recruited by their healthcare professionals, and via their parents, through two hospitals in the Capital and Zealand Regions of Denmark.

For the questionnaire, we used convenience sampling to recruit children and adolescents aged 5–18 years in the Capital Region of Denmark from a user panel designed to contribute ideas to the new children’s hospital Mary Elizabeths Hospital [26]. Email invitations were sent to 104 children and adolescents, either directly to their own email addresses or through their parents’ email.

Data collection

One author (PDC) conducted the narrative and play-based interviews from May to August 2021 in the children’s own homes. A detailed step-by-step interview guide is available in a previous publication [22].

Using REDCap electronic data capture tools [27], the online questionnaire was conducted from October 2022 to January 2023. The questionnaire was pre-tested with three children and adolescents. The 12-item questionnaire, which included items inspired by a survey developed by the Picker Institute [28], consisted of three demographic questions, five closed-ended questions, and four open-ended questions about children and adolescent’s experiences in hospitals and their perceptions of HCPs. For all close-ended questions, the participants were encouraged to elaborate on their answer with examples. The questionnaire was divided into two different versions: one for ages 5–11 and another for ages 12–18 (Supplemental Table S2 and S3). We designed child and youth-friendly questions by integrating emojis such as smileys (Supplemental Fig. S1). Parents were allowed to assist in answering the survey but were instructed to use direct quotes from the children. Parents who assisted their child were asked to indicate on a five-point Likert scale to what degree the child answered independently (1 = nothing and 5 = everything).

Data analysis

Four authors (JT, JLS, LKG, PDC) independently did a thematic analysis of the videos and transcribed material from the narrative and play-based interviews [29]. Each author separately watched the interview videos and read the transcribed material before highlighting potential statements of interest. To facilitate a structured and consistent approach, we developed a coding template for this purpose. The authors then grouped the statements based on emerging patterns to develop themes that were compared and reviewed at multiple meetings in an iterative process until consensus was reached.

To analyse questionnaire responses, descriptive statistics were performed using the statistical software RStudio, version 1.4 [30]. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages while continuous variable were reported as medians with interquartile range. JT, LKG, JH, BHE, and JLS did a thematic analysis of all open-ended responses after they were organized into a single dataset [29]. The authors independently read the material to familiarize themselves with it, highlighting patterns to identify emerging themes without using preconceived categories. The authors then met to review themes and discuss potential differences and similarities, using an iterative process to refine them until a final consensus was reached. All authors participated in the phase of integrating (linking) the two different approaches of data analysis.

Interview data (3–6 years)

Tips for HCPs in encounters with preschool children

We identified seven sub-themes in the preschool children interviews, which were divided into three overall themes: (1) previous experience and familiarity, (2) participation and playful approaches, and (3) relationship and trust. The sub-themes and relevant quotes can be found in Supplemental Table S4. We condensed these into a figure with tips for HCPs to have in mind when approaching preschool children (Fig. 1).

Theme 1: Previous experience and familiarity

The preschool children found that familiarity and previous experience were important to them. HCPs’ use of familiar approaches generally helped the children cope with hospital-related challenges. This included familiar routines and personal items such as favourite toys brought from home. The analysis also revealed that familiarity with HCPs influenced the comfort level of the children. One child mentioned feeling scared during a procedure because she did not know the individual conducting it as well as the other HCPs in the hospital.

Theme 2: Participation and playful approaches

The preschool children described engaging with HCPs in various ways, usually through playful approaches. One child mentioned how a HCP demonstrated a procedure on his teddy bear before performing it on him. The children also experienced that HCPs demystified treatment and illness by using relatable, child-friendly language. Letting the children make simple choices facilitated their involvement, such as choosing between plasters or different rewards after a procedure. The use of tangible rewards generally played a large role in the children’s memories, whether they were from an HCP (e.g. stickers, toys, or sweets) or their parents after leaving the hospital, helping to mark the end of a procedure and create positive associations with the hospital experience. Children also saw plasters as supportive and as a tangible signal that a procedure was over.

Theme 3: Relationship and trust

Our analysis highlighted various factors influencing preschool children’s trust and feelings of safety in clinical encounters. This included physical contact, such as holding hands, with either HCPs or their parents. The parents’ presence generally played an essential role in children’s memories, offering a sense of calm and support, for example, when they held their child on their laps during a difficult situation. Additionally, toys and teddy bears emerged as a supporting measure in difficult situations, helping preschool children cope when they were afraid or did not want to undergo a procedure.

Most (90%) children and adolescents felt comfortable around hospital staff, while approximately three out of four (78%) understood what the staff told them (Table 2). Theme 1: Attitude and appearance

HCPs’ attitude and appearance had significant importance for the schoolchildren and adolescents, who wanted to be met by smiling, friendly, and patient staff who always say hello and explain who they are. Children and adolescents emphasized feeling recognized and valued when someone remembered their name and took time to engage in conversations with them. Conversely, they were annoyed when staff were late, did not have time to talk, or left in the middle of a conversation. Being busy was associated with HCPs having a negative attitude and the children and adolescents felt discomfort when hurried. They appreciated staff who used humour and jokes, but some mentioned that they disliked it if they were not in the mood for it.

Theme 2: Participation and understanding

Schoolchildren and adolescents, especially 9–18-year-olds, expressed a principal need for HCPs to talk directly to them and include them in conversations. Some felt that HCPs would talk over their heads and only focused on communicating with their parents. Feeling engaged in conversations depended on the HCP’s ability to provide information in ways that the children and adolescents could understand. Some mentioned that the medical terminology was hard to follow and that they sometimes had no idea what the HCPs were talking about. Thus, it is important that HCPs do not talk too fast and explain treatment and illness in clear and simple language. Understanding medical procedures contributed to the normalization of medical equipment, such as needles. Some of the adolescents, however, stressed that they did not want to be spoken to as if they were children who did not understand anything. Children and adolescents emphasized that HCPs should listen to them and remember to ask them how they feel, both physically and emotionally. They also appreciated when HCPs talked about subjects other than illness and treatment as it made them feel valued as individuals rather than just being seen as patients.

Theme 3: Relationship and trust

Schoolchildren and adolescents valued having a trustworthy relationship with HCPs, which included that HCPs should never do something that was not agreed upon on beforehand. One of the children mentioned being restrained during a procedure, describing it as a highly negative experience. Children and adolescents wanted HCPs to make clear agreements and always tell them what was to happen before doing it. They appreciated HCPs who were good at calming them and use distractive and relaxation techniques in difficult situations. For most of the younger children (5–9 years), this included telling stories, singing, or engaging in other playful activities. Positive interactions also involved acknowledgment, like receiving rewards after a procedure or treatment, which several participants recalled as a positive aspect of going to the hospital.

Discussion

Our findings provide recommendations on what HCPs should do more or less of in encounters with children and adolescents aged 3–18 years. Preschool children seek trust and comfort through familiar approaches, physical contact, and their parents. In addition to using child-friendly words and tangible rewards to mark the end of a procedure, HCPs should do examinations where the preschool children feel comfortable and involve them through playful approaches. Schoolchildren and adolescents prefer HCPs who smile, are patient and attentive, inclusive in conversations, communicate clearly, and talks about topics besides illness and treatment. They want HCPs to prepare them for unpleasant situations and establish clear agreements on how procedures and care processes will be carried out. Similarly, they do not want them to appear stressed, talk too fast, talk over their heads, break agreements, use humour in inappropriate situations, or force them to do anything against their will.

We asked children and adolescents what HCPs should do more or less of in any respect. Notably, they emphasized attitude and appearance, indicating that simple actions that are often taken for granted, such as smiling, greeting patients, and remembering their names, are significantly meaningful to them. These behaviours are a basic part of paediatric communication [13], which is why mentioning them raises questions about the underlying issues contributing to this. One possible explanation is the increasing demands put on healthcare systems due to efficiency requirements and a lack of time and staff [31]. Regardless of these factors, our findings highlight the need for HCPs to maintain a friendly, patient, and attentive presence when interacting with children and adolescents, even in stressful situations.

Our analysis also showed that children and adolescents do not want HCPs to deviate from agreed plans or do anything against their will, including using restraints, which can leave children feeling powerless and cause them to lose trust in anyone who participated [32]. To help children cope with painful and stressful medical procedures, we found that HCPs should prepare them, incorporate distraction techniques that are mutually agreed upon, and give tangible rewards, which is consistent with previous reviews [33, 34]. Our findings suggest that HCPs should adopt an individual approach depending on the child’s developmental level, previous experiences, and preferences. Hospitals must, however, provide HCPs with the appropriate resources and training to facilitate their use of techniques such as preparation and distraction [35, 36].

We found that children and adolescents, especially from 9 to 18 years, want HCPs to include them in conversations and not just their parents. Even though involving children and adolescents in their own healthcare has become a priority internationally and is recognized by the World Health Organization [37], our study indicates that some children and adolescents still feel excluded from healthcare conversations. This was despite the fact that a large number of our questionnaire participants (78%) felt that they understood information compared to a previous study conducted in the same context [38]. Contrary to many other studies on paediatric patient experiences [8], we included preschool children (3–6 years old) and showed that this age group can be involved by offering them simple choices and using relatable, playful approaches. Høeg et al.’s [39] child-centred communication model illustrates the shift from engaging solely with parents to directly communicating with the child in an age-appropriate manner, which also includes using play. Allowing children to participate in decisions about their health is a fundamental basic right stated in the iSupport rights-based standards for children having tests, treatments, examinations, and interventions [40].

Our findings align well with Davison et al.’s [13] four steps for child-centred communication and the principles for communicating with children, adolescents, and parents about life-threatening conditions by Stein et al. [21]. Their guides for communicating with children and adolescents also include a friendly, patient attitude, placing the child in the centre, getting to know the child, using simple language, and involving the child in decisions. While HCPs are often assumed to naturally possess these skills, the insights from children and adolescents suggest that gaps exist and that this issue should be formally included in paediatric communication curriculum as opposed to the “hidden” curriculum [4]. Such training should focus on strategies for engaging children and adolescents, building trust and a friendly environment, initiating contact and thoughtfully ending encounters, using age-appropriate communication, and understanding relevant knowledge about children’s cognitive development. Moreover, we suggest that the dos and don’ts outlined in this study could be presented on posters or transformed into pocket-sized cards for clinicians, raising awareness and providing practical guidance on communication strategies with children and adolescents.

Each interaction with a paediatric patient is unique, and healthcare professionals must adjust their approach to align with the patient’s cognitive development. Therefore, the recommendations provided in this study serve as inspiration that should be tailored to each individual patient encounter. Bell et al. [1] state that children may start participating in discussions about their healthcare as early as three years old and that some already reaches a comprehension level similar to adults by around 12 years of age. Furthermore, acknowledging the triadic relationship with the patient and their parents is crucial, as the child and parent read and regulate each other’s emotional state [5], and healthcare professionals must consider young person’s growing independence, which impacts communication dynamics by shifting the role of parents, particularly during adolescence [3].

Strengths and limitations

Using play-based interviews and a questionnaire with child-friendly questions, we involved children and adolescents to inform the development of a communication curriculum for HCPs. Only few previous studies have included this groups’ perspectives in curriculum development [16, 19]. In “twelve tips for patient involvement in healthcare education”, Eijkelboom et al. [18] state that patients should always participate in curriculum design and development. We only included patients in identifying goals, learning objectives, and competencies, and did not involve them in verifying our findings, but this will be done at a later stage. We used two different types of data collection, but the analytical aim to explore children and adolescents’ hospital experiences and perceptions of healthcare professionals was consistent. Although children aged 5–6 years were included in both data sets, our analysis did not reveal any notable differences between the findings from the two data collection approaches. The questionnaire involved a sample of children, adolescents, and their parents who had voluntarily signed up as members of a user panel in the design of a new children’s hospital. This may limit generalizability due to differences between volunteers and the broader target population, including disadvantaged groups such as migrants. We also allowed parents to assist in completing the questionnaire and quote their child. Therefore, we cannot be completely sure that the responses only reflect the child’s own opinion. Another potential limitation is the lack of consideration for co-occurring neurodevelopmental disabilities or neurodivergence among the participants, which may influence communication approaches. Moreover, we did not ask about specific diagnosis of the questionnaire participants. However, our aim was to investigate children and adolescents’ general perceptions of healthcare professionals, irrespective of their health status. Most existing patient-reported experience measures in paediatrics also aim to measure general experiences of healthcare regardless of diagnosis [41]. Future research may benefit from distinguishing between the perceptions of healthy subjects and subjects with medical conditions. A potential limitation is that we did a secondary analysis of qualitative data, raising various methodological concerns [42]. In our case, however, the interviews were conducted relatively recently (2021), and the aim of the present study matched that of the original study well.

Conclusion

We established a set of practical recommendations for HCPs on what they should do more or less of in encounters with children and adolescents aged 3–18 years, which provided knowledge on what to include in an educational curriculum on paediatric communication. We propose that children and adolescents’ inputs should be incorporated in the early identification of goals, learning objectives, and competencies to make training more responsive to their preferences. By integrating the insights of children and adolescents, educational interventions can better prepare HCPs to address specific patient needs and preferences, as well as provide practical guidance for delivering patient-centred care. Future research should explore the long-term impacts of such educational interventions on children and adolescents’ hospital experiences.
