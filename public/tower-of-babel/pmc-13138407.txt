Consensus Guidelines on Inferior Petrosal Sinus Sampling: A Guideline From the Society of Vascular and Interventional Neurology Guidelines and Practice Standards Committee
Authors: Farhan Siddiq, Hunter Brooks, Esra Demirtas, Ibrahim A. Bhatti, Sami Al Kasab, Adam de Havenon, Robin Novakovic, Ossama Y. Mansour, Cynthia L. Kenmuir, Ameer E. Hassan, David Liebeskind, Thanh N. Nguyen, Shahram Majidi, Waldo R. Guerrero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Bilateral inferior petrosal sinus sampling (BIPSS) is the gold-standard test to distinguish Cushing disease (CD) from ectopic adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) secretion when biochemistry confirms ACTH-dependent Cushing syndrome and pituitary magnetic resonance imaging is equivocal. However, practice varies widely between centers, leading to false negatives, misinterpretation, sampling errors, and avoidable risk. Neurointerventionalists performing BIPSS have lacked dedicated, procedure-focused guidance.METHODS:Using the Society of Vascular and Interventional Neurology Guidelines and Practice Standards framework, a multidisciplinary panel (endocrinology, interventional neuroradiology, and endovascular neurosurgery) performed a systematic review through August 2025 and developed recommendations graded by class of recommendation and level of evidence through a modified Delphi process.RESULTS:The guideline standardizes (1) indications for BIPSS in biochemically confirmed ACTH-dependent Cushing with normal, equivocal, or <6 mm pituitary lesions; (2) technical approach, including bilateral IPS catheterization, heparinization, and stimulation with corticotropin-releasing hormone or desmopressin; (3) sampling protocol with 2 prestimulation draws and timed collections at 3, 5, 10, and 15 minutes; (4) lab handling, emphasizing prechilled EDTA tubes, rapid ACTH processing, and prolactin measurement as an internal control of venous effluent; and (5) interpretation, using ACTH inferior petrosal sinus to peripheral ratio cutoffs (≥2 prestimulation, ≥3 poststimulation) and techniques to identify potential false negatives. The document also defines operator/institutional competency benchmarks and quality-assurance metrics.CONCLUSIONS:When executed with standardized technique and interpretation, BIPSS offers very high sensitivity and specificity for localizing ACTH pathology. These consensus guidelines provide a practical playbook for neurointerventionalists to perform BIPSS safely, reproducibly, and with maximal diagnostic yield.

Accurate localization of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) secretion is critical in the management of ACTH-dependent Cushing syndrome (CS), as treatment strategies differ substantially for Cushing disease (CD) and ectopic sources of ACTH. Biochemical testing and pituitary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are often inconclusive, especially when MRI is normal or shows only a small microadenoma. In such cases, bilateral inferior petrosal sinus sampling (BIPSS) is considered the gold standard for distinguishing pituitary from ectopic ACTH production. Although this is a powerful tool for localizing the source of an ACTH-secreting tumor, it is a technically and logistically challenging procedure that should only be undertaken by experienced multidisciplinary teams composed of endocrinologists, interventional or neuro radiologists, and hospital laboratory teams.

Epidemiology and Clinical Impact

CS affects ≈1.8 to 4.5 people per million annually, with pituitary adenomas (CD) accounting for 80% to 90% of ACTH-dependent cases. Accurate localization is critical, as surgical success rates for pituitary adenomas approach 77% to 82% when properly identified, compared with <50% when localization is incorrect. The diagnostic challenge is substantial: 40% to 50% of patients with biochemically confirmed CD have normal or equivocal pituitary MRI findings.1

Rationale for Standardization

Despite BIPSS being performed for over 3 decades, significant variability exists in technique, interpretation criteria, and quality metrics across institutions. Substantial differences in procedural protocols can lead to varying false-negative rates ranging from 0% to 12%, depending on institutional practices.2 This variability in approach can lead to misdiagnosis, inappropriate surgery, and delayed treatment.

Scope and Objectives

These guidelines aim to provide evidence-based recommendations for BIPSS procedures, addressing: (1) standardized patient selection criteria, (2) technical protocols to optimize accuracy, (3) laboratory handling procedures, (4) interpretation guidelines to minimize false results, and (5) quality-assurance measures for procedural safety.

Methods

Data Availability

No new data were generated or analyzed. The literature search methods and all included sources are provided in the Methods and Supplemental Material.

Consensus Development Process

These guidelines follow the Society of Vascular and Interventional Neurology Guidelines and Practice Standards approach for evidence-based recommendations.3 The Society of Vascular and Interventional Neurology Guidelines and Practice Standards formed a committee to systematically combine a comprehensive literature review with expert panel consensus composed of endocrinologists, interventional neuroradiologists, and endovascular neurosurgeons to form guideline recommendations. These guidelines were written in accordance with the AGREE reporting framework (Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation).

Recommendations developed by the expert panel followed the Guidelines and Practice Standards framework using the class of recommendation (COR) and level of evidence (LOE; Figure 1). Consensus on all recommendations was achieved through a modified Delphi process, with each recommendation reviewed, revised, and refined by the entire panel, after which they were submitted to the Guidelines and Practice Standards committee and the Society of Vascular and Interventional Neurology Board of Directors for external review and approval. Recommendations were formulated when agreed on by ≥75% of panel members. Patients and the public were not directly involved in guideline development; recommendations focus on procedural standardization to improve diagnostic yield. These recommendations will be reviewed for updates every 5 years or earlier if major new evidence emerges.

Literature Search Strategy

We conducted searches of PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase from database inception through August 2025. Our search was guided by a Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, Time Frame, and Setting (PICOTS) question framework (Table). Primary search terms included: (“inferior petrosal sinus sampling” OR “IPSS” OR “BIPSS”) AND (“Cushing” OR “ACTH”). Secondary searches targeted specific technical aspects: catheter selection, anticoagulation protocols, specimen handling, and complications. We supplemented traditional searches with artificial intelligence–assisted literature discovery using Undermind (Semantic Scholar corpus) for comprehensive coverage, details of which can be found in the Supplemental Methods. All articles obtained via an artificial intelligence–assisted literature review were manually reviewed by humans.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Inclusion criteria: (1) adults with ACTH-dependent CS undergoing BIPSS; (2) studies reporting procedural details, diagnostic thresholds, or outcomes; (3) English-language publications. We excluded pediatric studies, nonpetrosal sampling procedures, and case reports with <5 patients. Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts, with conflicts resolved by a third reviewer. The evidence base used is primarily observational/single-center with heterogeneous protocols and meta-analyses. There are some head-to-head comparisons and limited data for relative contraindications. Recommendations explicitly weighed diagnostic yield against procedural risks (Figure 2).

Relevant Anatomy

Supporting Literature Summary

Four venous channels connect the right and left cavernous sinuses around the pituitary gland: the anterior intercavernous sinus, posterior intercavernous sinus, inferior intercavernous sinus, and basilar plexus.4 Venous effluent from the anterior pituitary drains into the cavernous and intercavernous sinuses, with the cavernous sinus draining posteriorly via the IPS into the IJV.5 In cases where there is hormonal overproduction by the pituitary gland, the abnormally elevated hormonal levels may be highest in the petrosal and cavernous sinuses. Pituitary microadenomas often drain unilaterally, so the IPS is sampled bilaterally to avoid missing a tumor that drains predominantly unilaterally.6 However, BIPSS is not considered a reliable method for lateralization in many cases, as asymmetrical venous drainage can result in most of the pituitary effluent exiting through one side regardless of tumor location.7

Miller et al8 described 4 common anatomic variations at the junction of the IPS and IJV (Figure 3). Symmetrical anatomy was present in only 65% of patients. Type I consists of a single IPS with a small or absent anastomosis to the vertebral venous plexus. Type II has a single IPS with a large vertebral venous plexus anastomosis. Type III is characterized by a rete of separate venous channels regardless of vertebral venous plexus anastomosis. Type IV has no connection between the IPS and IJV, preventing ipsilateral catheterization.

Common anatomic variations at the inferior petrosal sinus (IPS) internal jugular vein (IJV) junction. Schematic representations of 4 commonly encountered venous configurations at the junction of the IPS and IJV. Type I shows a single IPS draining into the IJV with a small or absent connection to the anterior condylar vein (ACV). Type II shows a single IPS draining into the IJV with a prominent ACV connection. Type III shows a plexiform (reticular) configuration with multiple venous channels connecting the IPS to the IJV. Type IV shows no direct IPS IJV connection, with the IPS draining via the ACV.

Pathophysiology

During the normal physiological state, the hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) in response to stress, pain, or fear. CRH stimulates the anterior pituitary to release ACTH. ACTH travels to the adrenal gland to release the hormone cortisol, which can result in an increase in blood glucose, suppression of the immune system, and alteration of metabolism. With the rise of cortisol, CRH release is suppressed by the hypothalamus, creating a negative feedback loop.

CS is a condition of high cortisol state, which is associated with weight gain, thin arms and legs, central obesity, moon-shaped face, osteoporosis, hypertension, high glucose, and mood changes. Causes of CS include ectopic ACTH secretion from tumors in other organs, adrenal gland tumors, or pituitary tumors. CD refers specifically to a pituitary tumor that produces excess ACTH, leading to overproduction of cortisol.9

Indications

Supporting Literature Summary

BIPSS is a localization procedure for ACTH-dependent CS, not a diagnostic test for hypercortisolism, requiring prior biochemical confirmation with at least 2 abnormal tests (24-hour urinary free cortisol [UFC], late-night salivary cortisol, or low-dose dexamethasone suppression) and measurable plasma ACTH (>20 pg/mL).2 It cannot distinguish pseudo-Cushing states (eg, depression, obesity) from CD, necessitating dexamethasone–corticotropin-releasing hormone (Dex-CRH) test (administering 0.5 mg dexamethasone every 6 hours for 2 days, followed by intravenous CRH 2 hours after the last dexamethasone dose), and Desmopressin stimulation test to rule out pseudo-Cushing.10,11 Where available, CRH is recommended as a first‑line stimulant, using established ACTH IPS to peripheral (IPS:P) ratio cutoffs (≥2 prestimulation, ≥3 poststimulation). However, it is worth noting that Ovine CRH is not presently available in the United States, Canada, Brazil, Argentina, Mexico, and some other countries.12 Desmopressin is a reasonable and evidence‑based alternative stimulant for BIPSS, with comparable results to CRH as shown by meta-analysis and comparative studies.13,14 Observational studies and systematic reviews report ≈96% to 97% sensitivity and ≈100% specificity for BIPSS in distinguishing CD from EAS, particularly when MRI is normal, equivocal, or showing a microadenoma <6 mm.12,15 Some centers diagnose CD without BIPSS for adenomas ≥6 mm (≈96% specificity) or ≥10 mm12,16,17 Adverse events are rare at experienced centers, supporting a strong benefit-risk profile. Absolute contraindications (eg, severe coagulopathy, infection) and relative contraindications (eg, pregnancy) are based on procedural safety protocols and expert consensus, with limited data for the latter. Important Lab Values

Supporting Literature Summary

The primary analyte measured during BIPSS is ACTH. An ACTH IPS:P ratio ≥2 prestimulation or a peak ≥3 poststimulation (CRH or desmopressin) is considered diagnostic for CD.10,17,18

A major limitation of BIPSS is false-negative results, potentially due to timing, poor sampling/venous efflux, or tumor nonresponsiveness.21 Previous studies report a rate of 4% to 10% false-negative results in BIPSS. One common analyte used to assess potential false negatives is PRL. Because PRL is abundantly secreted by the anterior pituitary, the PRL IPS:P ratio can serve as an internal marker of pituitary venous effluent and correct catheter placement. Most series assess the prestimulation PRL IPS:P ratio ipsilateral to the dominant ACTH gradient, where the dominant ACTH gradient is defined as the side with a higher ACTH IPS:P ratio. Prestimulation PRL IPS:P ratios ≥1.8 support adequate catheterization, whereas low values (<1.8) have been associated with false-negative studies and presumed inadequate sampling.22,23 If no ACTH gradient is present, the prestimulation PRL IPS:P ratio can be used bilaterally to determine if the catheters were properly placed, with a ratio <1.8 indicating improper placement.2

A PRL-normalized ACTH IPS:P ratio (Figure 5) has been proposed to reduce false negatives.24,25 A meta-analysis performed by Valizadeh et al26 showed that the sensitivity and specificity of BIPSS before adjusting for PRL were 90% (95% CI, 86–93) and 74% (95% CI, 59–85), respectively. After PRL-adjustment, the sensitivity and specificity became 96% (95% CI, 93–98) and 68% (95% CI, 52–81), respectively. These data suggest an increased sensitivity (P<0.01) with no significant change in specificity, which supports its use when assessing a potential false-negative. Studies propose a PRL-normalized ACTH cutoff value ≥1.3 being diagnostic of CD, ≤0.8 being diagnostic of an EAS, and values between 0.8 and 1.3 being a gray zone.27,28

Prolactin-normalized adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) inferior petrosal sinus to peripheral (IPS:P) ratio equation. The numerator has the dominant poststimulation peak IPS:P ACTH ratio, whereas the denominator has the ipsilateral IPS:P PRL (prolactin) at the same time point poststimulation.

Other methods proposed in small series involve measuring the level of ACTH itself. Wind et al29 have noted that all false negatives within their series had a petrosal ACTH <400 pg/mL prestimulation and poststimulation, which is suggestive of improper catheter placement. In cases where ACTH IPS:P ratio did not reach diagnostic criteria, and there is no dominant, a peripheral ACTH increase of >35% has been associated with patients who have a false-negative study.30

Consensus on Timing of Procedure

Supporting Literature Summary

BIPSS should be performed during a period of biochemically active hypercortisolism. In patients with cyclic CD, performing BIPSS during a trough can yield an absent ACTH IPS:P ratio that may be misinterpreted as EAS.31–35 To reduce this risk, centers document hypercortisolism as close to the procedure as logistics allow, using locally feasible testing (eg, a recent 24-hour UFC, late-night salivary cortisol, or a morning serum cortisol on the day of the study), with the optimal approach largely determined by institutional assay turnaround.36 When rapid results are not available, a practical strategy is to repeat standard screening tests (UFC, midnight salivary cortisol, or low-dose dexamethasone suppression) within ≈1 to 2 weeks preprocedure to demonstrate an on-cycle pattern and schedule BIPSS during the expected active window. Perlman et al2 additionally proposed a pragmatic day of screen, proceeding only if morning serum cortisol is >10 μg/dL, intended to avoid sampling during an off-cycle trough in a patient already proven to have CS, not to establish hypercortisolism de novo. When patients are taking cortisol-lowering therapy, these agents should be held long enough to redemonstrate hypercortisolism before sampling to avoid false-negative or uninterpretable results.2

Because diagnostic peaks typically occur 5 to 10 minutes poststimulation, common protocols obtain 2 prestimulation samples, followed by one sample at 3, 5, 10, and 15 minutes poststimulation to capture the ACTH peak.18 PRL should be sampled from bilateral IPSs and peripherally at the same time points as ACTH.

Stimulation: CRH Versus Desmopressin

Supporting Literature Summary

Stimulation improves diagnostic performance. A meta-analysis reported prestimulation sensitivity 86% (95% CI, 82–90) and specificity 98% (95% CI, 88–100); poststimulation sensitivity 97% (95% CI, 94–99) and specificity 100% (95% CI, 26–100).37 CRH and desmopressin are the most commonly used agents.34,35 Ovine and human CRH show comparable results, so both may be used depending on availability.40 CRH and desmopressin are both acceptable for stimulation; a meta-analysis found CRH sensitivity/specificity 98%/100% and desmopressin 96%/100% for BIPSS.26 Typical dosing: CRH 1 μg/kg IV (maximum 100 μg); desmopressin 10 μg.2

Sample Tubes and Lab Handling for BIPSS

Supporting Literature Summary

Accurate BIPSS results depend on meticulous sample handling, with observational studies emphasizing standardized protocols for ACTH and PRL collection to localize ACTH-dependent CS.10–12 ACTH requires prechilled EDTA tubes, immediate ice-water bath placement, and centrifugation/analysis within 1 hour or immediate freezing to avoid degradation and falsely low results.43 PRL is collected in serum separator tubes, with some centers freezing PRL samples and testing only if the ACTH IPS:P ratio is absent to reduce costs.39,44

Quality Assurance for BIPSS

Supporting Literature Summary

Quality assurance for BIPSS is supported by observational studies and expert consensus emphasizing standardized protocols to maintain high diagnostic accuracy (≈96%–97% sensitivity, ≈100% specificity for CD) and low complication rates (≈4% minor, <1% major).10–12,26 Institutional requirements, including digital subtraction angiography and on-site ACTH processing, are critical for reliable sampling and rapid analysis, with 24/7 neurosurgical coverage recommended for managing rare severe complications (eg, stroke).10 Operator qualifications and multidisciplinary collaboration are based on expert guidelines to ensure competency.42 Performance metrics target >95% technical success and diagnostic accuracy, with false-negative rates <10% when PRL gradients confirm adequate sampling.39 Quality improvement initiatives (case reviews, audits, standardized reporting) and detailed documentation (procedural notes, lab worksheets, venography) are standard in high-volume centers to optimize outcomes and correlate with surgical findings.43

Proposed Procedure for BIPSS

Supporting Literature Summary

BIPSS is a gold-standard localization procedure for ACTH-dependent CS, with observational studies and systematic reviews reporting high diagnostic performance for distinguishing CD from EAS when MRI is inconclusive or discordant.18,41,42 Because BIPSS cannot diagnose hypercortisolism or reliably differentiate pseudo-Cushing states, patients should first have biochemical confirmation of hypercortisolism (eg, ≥2 abnormal screening tests such as 24-hour UFC, late-night salivary cortisol, or low-dose dexamethasone suppression) with nonsuppressed ACTH before proceeding.2,11 BIPSS must be performed during periods of hypercortisolism. If the day of confirmation is not possible, serial 24-hour UFC or late-night salivary cortisol can be used to establish a pattern of times where the patient has hypercortisolism to schedule the procedure within 1 to 2 weeks.31–35

From a procedural standpoint, BIPSS is commonly performed with local anesthesia at the femoral access site plus titrated conscious sedation to preserve ongoing clinical neurological monitoring during catheter manipulation.2,45 General anesthesia should be reserved for select situations (eg, pediatric patients or adults unable to cooperate), because loss of real-time clinical assessment removes an important safety signal and can complicate interpretation. Limited case-report evidence data have suggested that continuous/high-dose propofol infusion during BIPSS may be associated with attenuated ACTH values;46 however, in vitro and clinical studies have shown that this potential ACTH attenuation is not clinically significant.47–49 BIPSS is often performed under fluoroscopy (often biplane when available) using standard anteroposterior (AP) and lateral projections. When venous overlap or variant anatomy limits visualization, operators may add ipsilateral oblique views or cranial-caudal angulation to better confirm IPS catheter position and delineate cavernous sinus drainage.

Standardized protocols for vascular access/catheter positioning, stimulation (CRH or desmopressin if CRH is unavailable), and strict sampling/handling (simultaneous central+peripheral draws with rapid ACTH tube processing) are supported by cohort experience.11,50–52

Before each collection, the catheter dead space should be cleared by aspiration and discarding saline-diluted blood.53 Interpretation relies on the ACTH IPS:P ratio (≥2 prestimulation or ≥3 poststimulation) to support CD; if the study is negative, assess for potential false negatives, including PRL-normalized ACTH IPS:P ratio is ≥1.3 (shown to be diagnostic of CD in some series), prestimulation PRL IPS:P ratio <1.8 (suggesting inadequate IPS venous efflux), petrosal ACTH <400 pg/mL prestimulation and poststimulation (suggesting failed efflux/cannulation), a >35% rise in peripheral ACTH poststimulation (supporting possible CD despite absent gradient), or delayed processing/freezing (not within ≈1 hour) that can artifactually lower ACTH.18–21,29,30

If BIPSS cannot be performed, most commonly due to technically difficult/impossible IPS catheterization (eg, hypoplastic/variant IPS anatomy with ectopic drainage, inability to access the IPS jugular junction, venous thrombosis, or other unfavorable venous anatomy) or lack of local BIPSS expertise/resources, bilateral IJV sampling with CRH stimulation can serve as a fallback central-to-peripheral gradient test. Using traditional BIPSS cutoffs (>2 prestimulation or >3 poststimulation), internal jugular venous sampling (IJVS) typically preserves high specificity but with lower sensitivity than BIPSS.54 In Erickson et al,55 standard cutoffs yielded a combined sensitivity of 81.3% for IJVS versus 93.8% for BIPSS (with 100% specificity reported in their data set), and they proposed lower IJVS-specific thresholds to account for jugular dilution (>1.6 prestimulation and >2.5 poststimulation), improving IJVS sensitivity (combined 87.5%) while maintaining reported specificity. Practically, when IJVS must be used, a positive gradient is generally treated as meaningful (given high specificity in available series), but a negative IJVS should be interpreted cautiously and, when feasible, prompt referral for confirmatory BIPSS.

Complications of BIPSS are uncommon and are most often minor (eg, access-site groin hematoma, ≈4%), whereas severe adverse events occur far less frequently.45 Rare but serious neurological injuries, including brainstem infarction/hemorrhage, have been described; in one report, major neurological complications occurred in 0.2% (1/508) of National Institutes of Health procedures and 0.5% (1/184) of a subset using a specially designed petrosal sinus catheter.56 Risk-reduction, therefore, centers on experienced operators, careful intraprocedural neurological monitoring, and immediate catheter withdrawal/abortion at the earliest sign of symptoms (including subtle or seemingly non-neurological complaints), with use of tip-deflector catheter guidewire systems when available.45,56

In patients with contraindications to invasive sampling (eg, severe coagulopathy, active infection, thrombosis) or when BIPSS is not feasible, noninvasive diagnostic strategies may be considered. Frete et al57 evaluated an algorithm incorporating CRH and desmopressin stimulation tests plus pituitary MRI, with thin-slice whole-body computed tomography for equivocal cases to evaluate ectopic sources; in their cohort, this strategy achieved moderate sensitivity for CD while reducing the proportion of patients proceeding to BIPSS.

Conclusions

BIPSS can be a useful method for localizing ACTH-secreting tumors, ectopic sources, or the pituitary gland in the setting of ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism. It is technically challenging and can yield misleading results if performed improperly. With proper technique, however, it achieves very high sensitivity and specificity in the hands of experienced interventionalists or neuroradiologists. This article outlines a guidelines-based approach that addresses common pitfalls and provides practical guidance to ensure reliable results.
