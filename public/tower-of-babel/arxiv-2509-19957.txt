Abstract
. Visual impairments present significant challenges to individuals worldwide, impacting daily activities and quality of life. Visual neuroprosthetics offer a promising solution, leveraging advancements in technology to provide a simplified visual sense through devices comprising cameras, computers, and implanted electrodes. This study investigates user-centered design principles for a phosphene vision algorithm, utilizing feedback from visually impaired individuals to guide the development of a gaze-controlled semantic segmentation system. We conducted interviews revealing key design principles. These principles informed the implementation of a gaze-guided semantic segmentation algorithm using the Segment Anything Model (SAM). In a simulated phosphene vision environment, participants performed object detection tasks under SAM, edge detection, and normal vision conditions. SAM improved identification accuracy over edge detection, remained effective in complex scenes, and was particularly robust for specific object shapes. These findings demonstrate the value of user feedback and the potential of gaze-guided semantic segmentation to enhance neuroprosthetic vision.

1.
Introduction

For individuals around the world, visual impairments create difficulties in carrying out everyday activities. Studies have documented their prevalence, with tens of millions affected by blindness and hundreds of millions experiencing moderate to severe visual impairments globally
(
Bourne et al. 2021
)
. In addition, the impact of blindness or limited vision has been studied extensively, highlighting the reduction in quality of life
(
Stelmack 2001
)
,
(
Kamelska and Mazurek 2015
)
and the array of associated social challenges, like social stigmatization
(
Bulk et al. 2020
)
, isolation
(
Rokach et al. 2021
)
, poverty and unemployment
(
Gold and Simson 2005
)
. An emerging and promising method to potentially aid visually impaired people against these issues are visual neuroprosthetics. Recent scientific milestones include encouraging results from animal testing
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
and human trials
(
Fernández et al. 2021
)
that hold promises for the safety and efficacy of these devices. In the next section we will describe the basic components of such a system.

A visual neuroprosthetic system consists of three main components, a camera, a computer and implanted electrodes
(
Beauchamp and Yoshor 2020
)
. A simple illustration of this system is shown in Figure
1
. The camera is mounted on the head of the user, usually on a pair of glasses, and records the point of view of the person carrying it. It then transmits this information to the computer which transforms the camera’s image into signals that will drive the implanted electrodes. Lastly, the implanted electrodes electrically stimulate neurons that are parts of the visual system. These can be in the retina, the thalamus or the visual cortex
(
Fernandez 2018
)
.

Figure 1.
Illustration of the functional parts of a Visual Neuroprosthetic device. Taken from
(
de Ruyter van Steveninck et al. 2022a
)
Figure 1 is an illustration of the components of a visual neuroprosthetic device. A person is looking at a panel containing the letter i. They are wearing eyeglasses that have a mounted camera. The illustration shows a mobile computer as well as an implant that stimulates the person's V1 area. Arrows are used to explain the processing path of the captured image with the letter i from the camera to the mobile computer to the implant. An estimation of what the person might see is shown as a collection of small white dots forming the stem and the tittle of the letter.

By stimulating the neural tissue, the aim of this device is to invoke a light perception called a phosphene. Phosphenes are commonly described as flashes of white light and by manipulating their position and intensity, the goal is to present a coherent representation of a visual scene to their user
(
Schmidt et al. 1996
)
.

Despite the exciting prospect of these devices, several important limitations need to be addressed. One of these limitations is that due to the low amount of electrodes, the phosphene representations that can be elicited are of a very low in resolution. The necessary amount of electrodes that need to be implanted in order to restore functional vision is estimated to be around 625
(
Cha et al. 1992
)
. Comparing this number with the number of electrodes implanted in recent human trials (96)
(
Fernández et al. 2021
)
we understand that the current phosphene vision resolution achievable is far from ideal. It is also challenging to determine the appropriate stimulation parameters to ensure that what users perceive aligns with researchers’ intentions while maintaining their safety—an issue that ongoing research is actively addressing
(
Küçükoğlu et al. 2025
)
.
Moreover, while the color of phosphenes cannot be sufficiently controlled, their shape also varies across individuals, further limiting representational capacity
(
Erickson-Davis and Korzybska 2021
)
,
(
Beyeler et al. 2019
)
. Given these limitations, it is important to emphasize that visual neuroprosthetics can not restore healthy vision but only provide a simpler form of visual sensation due to the limited information that can be represented. This is the reason why current research focuses on developing efficient algorithms that can transform the camera’s natural images to a phosphene representation that is best understood by the user.

Researchers have attempted to simplify scenes by employing algorithms that extract specific visual features. For example, multiple techniques attempt to represent objects using their edges
(
de Ruyter van Steveninck et al. 2022b
)
,
(
Guo et al. 2018
)
,
(
Vergnieux et al. 2017
)
. This approach is favored by many researchers because it requires minimal computational resources, making it optimal for portable computers that users can carry with them. Semantic segmentation is another approach that has gained traction in recent years due to the development of robust deep learning models
(
Han et al. 2021
)
,
(
Sanchez-Garcia et al. 2020
)
. With this approach, researchers are able to convey not just the outline of an object but also its semantic identity, adding a significant amount of information to the phosphene representation.

Despite the variety of approaches employed to create a simplified visual sense, several problems remain unaddressed. Firstly, many approaches use algorithms that are optimized for a specific use context. Researchers have already argued that the performance of algorithms can be task-specific
(
Wang and Wong 2024
)
. Secondly, since not every research group has access to actual implant users, most scientists use phosphene simulators to replicate phosphene vision for sighted individuals. However, their methods do not always achieve the same results in actual device users
(
Beyeler et al. 2019
)
,
(
Erickson-Davis and Korzybska 2021
)
, emphasizing the need for more realistic simulators
(
Dagnelie 2008
)
. Additionally, it has been shown that eye movements play a significant role in the understanding of the scene with phosphene vision
(
Sabbah et al. 2014
)
, an element often omitted in the evaluation of algorithms. Lastly, more care should be given to the potential user’s priorities regarding what problems they would like to solve in their every day life. If one compares studies about visually impaired individual’s quality of life
(
Riazi et al. 2016
)
,
(
Stelmack 2001
)
with the context within which phosphene vision approaches are evaluated, a gap is clear.

In response to these shortcomings, it is important to adopt the following steps in our design. Initially, it is crucial to understand what problems are important for the blind and visually impaired community and what features should our algorithm implement in order to solve them in a way that respects the user’s wishes. Then, research should focus on developing a model that can address the wishes of the community and adhere to the limitations of phosphene vision. To achieve that, our approach should be embedded into a gaze-controlled paradigm to account for the aforementioned importance of eye movements and transformed to phosphene vision using a more realistic simulator.

In the current study, we conducted novel interviews to determine the specific challenges visually impaired individuals face when navigating outdoors and how these affect their quality of life. Interviewees were also able to express their preferences for the device’s functions, highlighting the importance of controlling the device in a way that makes the environment more predictable and does not overwhelm them with excessive information. Then we implemented a Gaze-Controlled Semantic Segmentation (GCSS) algorithm, inspired by
(
de Ruyter van Steveninck et al. 2024
)
and utilizing the Segment Anything Model
(
Kirillov et al. 2023
)
, a foundational model for semantic segmentation, that could potentially improve the generalizability of the overall system greatly. To test its efficiency, we designed an object search experiment, utilizing a state-of-the-art biologically plausible simulator
(
van der Grinten et al. 2024
)
and evaluated the time needed to complete the task, object identification accuracy and eye-movement entropy. We also evaluated the participant’s preference towards the two methods using a survey at the end of the experiment similar to
(
Bollen et al. 2019
)
. We show that our method outperforms edge detection in identification accuracy and is preferred by participants.

2.
Interviews

To recruit visually impaired volunteers for our interviews, we reached out to organizations dedicated to individuals with visual impairments. Interested volunteers were screened based on minimal eligibility criteria, which required them to be legally blind, be sufficiently fluent in English and willing to participate in a one-hour interview session. We recruited 4 participants through an organization that supports blind and partially sighted people with various visual impairments. Three of the participants were female and one was male. Out of the 4 participants, 3 had various degrees of remaining vision while one participant was blind from birth. All of the participants were legally blind. To ensure the comfort of each participant, the interviews took place in a location of their choice and in-person. They were audio recorded using a smartphone. The interviews consisted of three parts: an introduction, a questionnaire and an open-ended questions segment.

Initially, during the introductory part, the interviewer introduced themselves and tried to give participants a good intuition about phosphene vision and neuroprosthetic devices by describing how they work and how experiencing phosphenes feels like. Then, they explained the aim of the interview and asked for verbal consent. The second part consisted of 46 questions expressing difficulty in performing specific outdoors tasks or activities. The structure of the questions was inspired from
(
Kamelska and Mazurek 2015
)
where researchers asked the participants to use a Likert scale to express the level of difficulty they experienced with everyday tasks. The themes of the questions were heavily influenced by
(
Riazi et al. 2016
)
and
(
Zeng 2015
)
which employed qualitative and quantitative approaches to understand outdoor difficulties in individuals with visual impairments. In our implementation, the questions were grouped in six categories, each referring to a different aspect of outdoors navigation. These categories were: Ground (Obstacles), Navigational Functions, Public Transport, Buildings, Open Spaces, People. A full list of the questions can be found in the supplementary material. The participants had to rate the difficulty of each task using a Likert scale from 1 to 7, 1 denoting that the task is very easy while 7 denoting that the task was very hard. To keep the participants engaged and understand their difficulties in more detail, after each category the interviewer asked them to provide a description of a related incident that they have experienced. These descriptions where then used in the open-ended questions segment to provide context and understanding for the participants. They also provided useful insights into their everyday struggles and were used in the final analysis of discussion themes. The last segment, consisted of 8 open-ended questions that are provided in the supplementary material. These questions were designed to explore what features of the neuroprosthetic device’s algorithm would be most useful to address the aforementioned difficulties.

After the first three interviews, revisions were made to the interview protocol. The most important change was the inclusion of a fourth part in the interview that gave me the freedom to ask some clarifications or follow-up questions based on the discussion thus far. We were incentivized to include this part because a few interesting discussions happened after the end of the interviews with the first three participants that went unrecorded. Furthermore, we revised the formulation of some open-ended questions in the third part to make them more clear. Finally, we replaced two open-ended questions with others we considered more important, as participants frequently found the original questions confusing and did not provide particularly insightful responses. The changes made in this second version of the interview can be found in the supplementary material.
The interviews were automatically transcribed with the aid of the pydub python library
(
Robert 2011
)
. All transcripts were then carefully checked and validated. Both the open-ended questions and open discussion segments were open coded to extract themes the participants talked about. Later, by comparing these themes, we axially coded each segment of the interviews, deciding which themes were prevalent among all participants and assigning one theme (axis) to each response in the interviews. This work was heavily inspired by
(
Lane et al. 2010
)
and
(
Lane et al. 2012
)
.

3.
Experiment

3.1.
Participants

We recruited 9 participants (3 male, 6 female) at the Radboud University campus with a mean age of
24.9\pm 1.37
. To be included in the study, participants had to have normal or corrected to normal vision and no ocular malformations. All participants passed the inclusion criteria but one was excluded from the study because of not optimal eye-tracker calibration that they reported after the end of the experiment. None of the participants had prior experience with phosphene vision simulations. All participants gave written informed consent and the study was approved by our institution’s ethical committee.

3.2.
Experimental Setup

The participants were seated in front of screen with a resolution set at 1920x1080 pixels. The monitor was positioned properly to ensure that it subtends no more that
32^{\circ}
of visual angle horizontally and
25^{\circ}
degrees of visual angle vertically. We used a chin rest to stabilize their head during the experiment which was positioned appropriately so that the participant’s eyes would align with the top quarter of the monitor. We also used an Eyelink 1000 eye tracker to track the participants’ gaze. The device was positioned properly so that the distance from the eye-tracker to the chinrest is approximately 50 cm and to ensure it is centered horizontally to the monitor. Then the height of the device was adjusted so the eye-tracker was positioned as high as possible without blocking the view of the display.

The eye-tracker was set to binocular tracking at 500 Hz. Our aim was to have the participants control a point on the screen with their gaze. We represented the point using a dot. In order for the dot to appear closer to where participants experience their center of vision, we averaged the positions of the two gaze coordinates taken from the two eyes. To optimize the tracking fidelity, we reduced the power of the illuminator to 75% which reduces the amount of reflections on the cornea. During the calibration phase of the eye-tracker, we set the background to be black and the calibration point to be white.

3.3.
Experimental process

In our experiment, participants were asked to locate requested objects when presented with images. These images were taken from the ARID10 and ARID20 subsets of the OCID dataset
(
Suchi et al. 2019
)
which is prominently used for robotic vision research. A typical sample from ARID consists of a cluttered scene consisting of everyday objects on the floor or on a table. All images are taken from the same point of view. An example of such an image is shown in Figure
2
. The goal was to compare their accuracy and behavior between three conditions, the GCSS condition (our method), the Edge Detection condition and the Coloured condition.

Before the start of the experiment, participants were briefed on how the GCSS and the Edge Detection methods intuitively worked. Additionally, they were shown images of all possible target objects and were asked to familiarize themselves with them in order to set their expectations to a specific shape for each target. This step was incorporated based on feedback from a pilot study participant who noted that their differing expectations regarding the object’s shape increased the difficulty of the experiment. Lastly, the participants did a practice run using images form the ARID10 dataset under the Colored condition in order to familiarize themselves with controlling the eye-tracker.

3.4.
Experimental Design

Our design was inspired by previously conducted object search experiments
(
Parikh et al. 2013
)
,
(
Nejad et al. 2025
)
.The experiment consisted of three sessions, each using a different image processing method and representing the three conditions. During each session the participants were presented with 76 prompts consisting of a black screen and the name of a requested object and an equal amount of processed images. The same 76 image-target pairs were presented in all three sessions but the order was randomized. The selection of targets was made under the constraint that there were not two objects that would be indistinguishable without further features like color. For example, if an image contained two types of fruit similar in shape, like one orange and one lemon, neither of those could be a target. Furthermore, the images were grouped into three sets according to the number of objects existing in the scene which will be referred as cluttering. These three sets were low cluttering with 1 to 3 objects, intermediate cluttering with 5 to 8 objects and high cluttering with 9 to 11 objects. Low cluttering contained 24 samples while intermediate and high cluttering contained 26 samples. In the GCSS and Edge Detection conditions, there was a brief break after 38 images so the participants could rest. There was no break in the Coloured condition. We presented the first 4 participants with the GCSS condition before the Edge Detection condition and the other half with the opposite ordering. All participants were presented with the Coloured condition last. Calibration was done before the start of each condition.

Participants were expected to search for the object in the image using their gaze. After finding the object, they were asked to hover over it using the mouse and press the left mouse button to declare the position of the object. The position of the mouse was visible using a crosshair. In some trials the requested object was not included in the image. In these cases participants were asked to press the right mouse button. We refer to these trials as false trials while when the requested object was included in the image resulted in a true trial. The ratio of false trials to true trials was 22 to 54 or the false trials were approximately 30% of the total trials.

After the end of the experiment, participants had to fill a short questionnaire regarding their experience under the GCSS and Edge Detection conditions. As for the data collection, we stored the coordinates of the points the participants clicked at, denoting where they thought the requested object was, the time from the presentation of the processed images to their decision and the trajectory of their gaze.

3.5.
Image processing

We applied three different image processing strategies two of which were the main conditions we wanted to contrast, the GCSS condition and the Edge Detection condition. The Coloured condition included the raw images resized to 640x640. For the Edge Detection condition, we utilized the Canny Edge Detection algorithm form the scikit-image python library
(
van der Walt et al. 2014
)
. We used a low threshold of 25, a high threshold of 50 and a standard deviation of 5 for the gaussian filter based on qualitative visual assessment. Then, we processed the image using the phosphene simulator, resized it to 640x640 and displayed it at the center of the screen.

For the GCSS condition we utilized the SAM model. The SAM model is a promptable semantic segmentation model. The user can select a point in the image, specified by its coordinates, and the model will segment the object encompassing that point.
Our main idea was to use the point representing the center of the participant’s gaze to extract the mask of the object they are looking at and highlight it, effectively giving them control over which objects appear in their vision.

Despite our efforts to implement the SAM processing in real-time, the limitations of the available hardware posed substantial challenges. We were unable to achieve a video frame rate higher than 14 frames per second. To address this issue, we opted for pre-calculating the masks for each image and making them available for access during the experiment.

For the generation of the pre-calculated masks, we initially needed to preprocess the images to increase the quality of the output. Initially, every image was resized to 1024x1024 to fit the input resolution of the SAM model. The next step involved normalizing the histogram of the brightness for each image utilizing tools from the open-cv python library
(
Bradski 2000
)
. To achieve this, we first converted the image to the YUV color space and equalized the histogram of the Y component (luma) which is associated with the illumination intensity of the image. We noticed that this preprocessing step removed a lot of artefacts from the extracted segmentation masks. Finally, we convert the image back to the RGB color space and give it as input to SAM. Since processing time was not an issue anymore, we selected the largest model published by the developers which provided the highest quality segmentation results. Next, we used the Automatic Mask Generator functionality which calculates the masks of all objects in the scene. Our output consisted of a Nx1024x1024 binary matrix where N is the number of masks in a specific image identified by the model.

To simulate the mask extraction in real time, we initially loaded the appropriate mask matrix for each trial. We iterate over the N different masks and select those that have a true value at the gaze coordinate for each time point. This way we effectively highlight the shape of the object the participant was looking at. To provide information about objects outside the participant’s direct gaze, we superimposed the Edge Detection image, scaled to approximately 30% of the intensity of the segmentation mask. As a last step, the image was processed using the phosphene simulator, resized to 640x640 and displayed at the center of the screen.

3.6.
Phosphene Simulator

The last processing step of both Edge Detection and GCSS conditions was to process the image using the phosphene simulator developed in
(
van der Grinten et al. 2024
)
. This implementations allows for biologically plausible control of the size and brightness of the phosphenes using the device’s parameters like the electrical stimulation intensity and the stimulation frequency as well as controlling the position of the implanted electrodes. We followed the configuration files provided in
(
git [n. d.]
)
. Specifically, for the electrode positions we selected 600 electrode positions probabilistically according to the reverse dipole visuotopic model for the V1 area. The visual angle was selected to be
4^{\circ}
which follows actual implantation strategies
(
Fernández et al. 2021
)
. The pulse frequency was set at 300Hz following the results of the same study. The electric current for the electrode grid was set uniformly at
60\mu A
. The remaining parameters were maintained as specified in the configuration files, with the exception that thresholding was disabled.

We can observe that the hardest tasks involve either finding very specific locations (public toilets, specific address), hard to locate objects (horizontal obstacles) or chaotic areas (street works, restaurants). Next we will discuss the results of the open-ended questions. As mentioned previously, we utilized a structured coding process to analyze qualitative data gathered from participant interviews. The initial stage of this process was open coding during which we organized each segment of the interview into units of meaning. To make this more understandable we will go through the open codes and provide some examples from the participant’s responses. A more structured representation of our findings can be found in Table
3
of the Appendix.

Initially we noticed a few examples given by the participants that revolved around unpredicatbility of the environment. Specifically, one participant mentioned how difficult it is to navigate in crowded spaces because of the difficulty to predict the movement of all the people. Another example given to us was when using public transport. In the participants words: " [When taking a bus] You never know where you need to scan your card, its in different positions, the scanning thing, for every bus owner, or bus model or transit company ". Lastly, for participants with some remaining eyesight, it was very difficult to adapt to specific external conditions, especially at low lighting or when there is not great color contrast on objects. One example given by a participant "… it’s difficult to distinguish if there’s a person sitting on the dark-ish [percept] or if you’re looking at the dark-ish pattern of the seat".

Secondly, we observed that the goal of the participants in each context mattered for the types of problems that they faced. One stunning example was that same objects can pose a different difficulty based on how the participants approach them. For example, going downstairs was significantly harder than going upstairs for most of the participants. Additionally, in some scenarios, timing is important. A great example given by one participant was revolving doors, which pose significantly higher difficult than regular doors due to needing well timed movements to enter them. Lastly, even though it was not their first priority, participants mentioned they would like to be able to understand the utility of the objects around them. As one participant put it "… maybe it would be handy […] if you’re looking for a[…] trash bin that you can say “look for trash bin” so this can be helpful in some situations".

When asked about
the types of obstacles that they consider most important to identify when navigating open spaces
participants highlighted that they are not interested in specific objects but in finding a safe path. Specifically, one participant mentioned "I am interested in the deviations not the ground plane itself. […] all the deviations from “hey this is safe I can walk here”
". A similar response was given by another participant when asked about the
control over what types of objects the device focuses on.

"I think it’s not the obstacles but especially finding the way to where we want to be […] You can’t walk where you normally are able to walk but you have to choose another way to walk". Similarly, when asked whether "it is more important to them to quickly know that there is an obstacle in their way or to precisely recognise what type of obstacle there is", participants overwhelmingly preferred identifying there is some danger first rather than precisely recognizing the obstacle.

Lastly, a lot of emphasis was put on avoiding social awkwardness and stigmatization. Some participants expressed aversion to voice commands or other elements of the device that might label them as individuals with special needs. For example one participant expressed " [I don’t want] to look like an idiot with cables running down [my] glasses", or in another passage "[I don’t want to] let everybody know that [I am] disabled and that [I am] a “special” person with [my] “special glasses”". Other participants pointed out that the ideal use case for this device would be to avoid embarrassing situations. An indicative example of this was mentioned by a participant "Looking up an address is really hard and so I have to […] walk to the front of the door and you say “oh this is not the right number” […] it’s really awkward because the people looking at the window saw you and think “what is she doing”". Finally, when asked about their motivation to use the device, one participant summed up the sentiment shared by many other by stating "I think most of the motivation would be that you can go when or where you want", drawing attention to the importance they put on being independent when navigating outdoors spaces.

Having all of this information, we had clear directional guidelines for the design of our algorithm. Specifically, we wanted to design an algorithm that decodes the dynamics of the environment into an understandable set of phosphenes while at the same time optimizing the user experience according to the coding we mentioned previously. Our original aim was to design a virtual environment in which participants could navigate in order to simulate outdoor activities like the ones described in the questionnaire. However, due to the aforementioned issues we opted for the object search task inspired by
(
Sanchez-Garcia et al. 2018
)
in which we could control for some of the axial codes as described in Table
3
. Specifically, our focus was on optimizing the displayed information and provide a dynamic way for the participants to make the environment more understandable.

Optimizing user experience

Next we wanted to compare how the two methods perform with regards to the distinction between true (with object) and false (without object) trials. To evaluate the classification we use three widely utlizied metrics, precision, recall and F1-score. Precision is defined as the ratio of true positive (TP, the requested object exists and the participant found it) results to the sum of true positive and false positive results (FP, the requested object does not exist but the participant declared falsely it exists) . It measures the accuracy of positive predictions made by the participant.

\text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}

Recall, is the ratio of true positive results to the sum of true positive and false negative results(FN, the requested object existed but the participant declared the opposite).

\text{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}

Finally, F-1 Score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a single metric that balances both concerns.

\text{F1\text{-}Score}=2\times\frac{\text{Precision}\times\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}

4.7.
Gaze entropy and gaze behaviour results

Finally, we wanted to study the patterns of the participants’ gaze under the different conditions. To achieve that, we define the gaze entropy metric. For every trial, we separate the trial image into a square grid of size 32 by 32. Then, using the gaze coordinates we stored per trial, we calculate the probability of the participant’s gaze falling into each of the grid’s squares. This is effectively the same as calculating the 2D histogram of the gaze coordinates. To calculate the gaze entropy per trial we use the following formula:

H=\Sigma_{i}\Sigma_{j}P(i,j)log_{2}(P(i,j))

Where
P(i,j)
is the probability at the cell
i,j
. This metric captures the variability of the participants’ gaze. High gaze entropy means the participants’ gaze was widely dispersed throughout the image while low gaze entropy would mean that their gaze was concentrated at specific locations.

Figure
11
illustrates the average gaze entropy per trial for the three different methods: GCSS (M = 4.77, SD = 1.03), Edge Detection (M = 4.57, SD = 0.96), and Coloured (M = 2.89, SD = 0.72). Both GCSS and Edges methods showed significantly higher gaze entropy compared to the Coloured method, indicating more dispersed gaze patterns. The Color method resulted in the lowest gaze entropy, suggesting a more focused gaze pattern. Additionally, GCSS had a significantly higher gaze entropy (t(8) = 4.04, p
<
.005) than Edge Detection but without a vast margin separating the two conditions.

5.
Discussion

In this study, we aimed to design a phosphene vision algorithm by utilizing potential user’s feedback. Our starting point consisted of interviews with visually impaired and blind individuals with the aim of understanding what problems they want to solve in their everyday life regarding outdoors activities as well as understanding the principles upon which our algorithm should operate. After gathering and analysing this data, our design process was better informed and led to a novel approach to phosphene vision.

To test the capabilities of our algorithm, we designed an object detection experiment in which the participants were subjected to simulated phosphene vision. Using an eye-tracker, participants were able to highlight objects by looking at them on a screen. We compared our method with a widely used method in artificial vision research, edge detection, as well as healthy colored vision. In the following sections we will discuss the importance of user’s feedback to our design process as well as interpret the results from the comparison between the different presentation methods.

5.1.
Influence of User Interviews on Algorithm Design

When conducting the interviews, our primary aim was to find some design principles that would guide us into a final algorithm.
The questions that were asked had two aims. The questionnaire part focused on potential use cases for the neuroprosthetic device, while open ended questions aimed to define these design principles more clearly.

Our choice to use the coding scheme we described previously, was to organize the contents of the interviews into "take home" messages which sum up important aspects of the device the participants would like to see realized. As our principles we chose two of the axial codes from Table
3
, avoiding overwhelming the user with excessive information and dynamically decoding the environment. The first principle influenced our selection of gaze-guided semantic segmentation using SAM and the eye tracker. This way, the participants of the experiment were able to highlight one object at a time which helped them understand the scene easier. The addition of the faint edges was also done with this principle in mind. We wanted the participant to have a general idea of where objects were without overwhelming their vision. This is why this information was presented dimmer than the information they chose to see with their gaze. Additionally, by controlling what they see with their gaze, we provide them with a dynamic way of decoding the scene.

These properties were chosen among many other ideas we had. Some of them included selecting the objects based on their sizes, their distance from the user or with other characteristic features. By discussing with potential users we discovered that these methods were not preferable and did not adhere to the principles we discussed earlier. This part of our research accentuates that input from the users is an essential step of the design process of a phosphene vision algorithm and provides a promising way to systematize the gathering and analysis of data.

5.2.
Gaze behavior using GCSS

GCSS outperformed Edge Detection in our object search task. Participants demonstrated higher accuracy when using GCSS and exhibited marginally improved decision-making in determining whether the object was present in the scene or if the trial was a false one. Moreover, we observed that GCSS was more accurate than Edge Detection as the clutter level of the scene increased, hinting that our method was more robust to scene complexity. We also observed that the shapes of the objects differently influenced whether they would be recognized or not for each algorithm, with GCSS performing better on rectangular and cylindrical objects, while Edge Detection performing better on spherical objects. This finding showcases how GCSS performance is shape dependent and opens the way for further research that could improve GCSS performance on specific shapes.

More importantly, we saw that participant’s gaze behavior varied between all conditions. From the presented gaze maps, we saw that participants using GCSS spent more time exploring the image. It was usual for them to also focus their gaze between the objects, highlighting the background, to understand their contours using negative space. This was an important observation as participants using GCSS have two ways of extracting information from the object. The first one is to directly highlight the object and potentially identify it from its shape and the second is to explore the space around it, understanding the contours which could provide additional information regarding the object’s position with respect to other objects as well as its shape.

5.3.
Limitations and future work

While this study provides valuable insights into the effectiveness of a gaze controlled semantic segmentation method, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, due to computational limitations, the experiment was conducted using pre-calculated masks. This does not constitute a real-time solution and the results should only be considered as an indication of the capabilities of such an approach. As the results demonstrated a promising performance by the GCSS method, optimizing the semantic segmentation algorithm to process the images in real-time should be a primary focus of future work. MobileSAM
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
, a variation of SAM optimized for mobile devices, already shows promise by prioritizing performance at the cost of some robustness.

A second aspect of our experiment that was impacted by computational limitations was the task choice. Although the interview results highlighted independence during outdoors navigation as a very important issue for our participants, due to the speed that we could run SAM at the time of the experiment design, it was not possible for us to choose a design that could test a participant’s navigational skills. Since then, we were able to improve our hardware set up and our computational pipeline, which we will be utilizing to delve further into how to implement and test methods that address the identified user needs better. An immersive VR experiment where the participants can move in a realistic outdoors environment, performing navigational tasks similar to those described in the interview’s questionnaire, are planned for changing the focus from object finding to navigation in our future work.

Another shortcoming that needs to be taken into consideration is that while our experiment allows for the eye-movement to dynamically affect the way objects appear, head movement was not taken into account. The participants had to use a chin chin rest and were instructed to not move their heads, which admittedly makes the whole experiment a more unpleasant experience than it should be, possibly influencing the results. Also importantly, in the real world, head movements are utilized to allow for more degrees of freedom and perspective, which in the experimental setup would translate into better object recognition performance as well as a more natural experience for the participants. Ideally this experimental setup can be replaced, again, by using a VR headset with eye-tracking capabilities.

Finally, after the experiment sessions, many participants expressed their lack of confidence when selecting objects particularly commonly with the Edge Detection method. A controlled way for the participants to express their confidence levels about the choices was thus identified as an important metric to include in future studies of the kind. Including questions regarding confidence in our questionnaire, or by directly asking participants about their confidence levels in their choices after each trial would let us gain insights about this important part of user experience.

6.
Conclusion

This study demonstrates a user-centered approach to the design of phosphene vision algorithms by systematically integrating the perspectives of visually impaired and blind individuals into both conceptualization and evaluation. Through interviews, we identified design principles that emphasize minimizing cognitive load and supporting dynamic interaction with the visual scene. Guided by these principles, we developed a gaze-controlled semantic segmentation method in which users highlight objects of interest through their gaze, turning visual attention into a direct and intentional control mechanism.

Our experimental evaluation shows that this approach outperforms conventional edge detection, particularly in scenes cluttered with many objects. The results suggest that our method provides a more flexible means of extracting visual information, enabling participants to alternate between object-focused and context-based exploration strategies, strongly supporting our core message of the need for widespread adoption of user-centered approaches in neuroprosthetics research and development.
