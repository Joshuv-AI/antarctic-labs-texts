Numerical modeling to assess the hydrodynamic behavior of the Gallinas River in San Luis Potosí under atypical hydrometeorological conditions
Authors: Clemente Rodríguez-Cuevas, José-Guadalupe Alejandrez-Palacios, Carlos Couder-Castañeda, Jhonathan-Fernando Eulopa-Hernandez, Alfredo Trujillo-Alcantara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRiver systems characterized by strong groundwater–surface water interaction exhibit complex hydrodynamic responses under hydroclimatic extremes. This study investigates how infiltration-dominated river reaches modulate flow persistence during drought and floodplain activation during extreme rainfall. A two-dimensional Environmental Fluid Dynamics Code (EFDC) model was implemented for a 20.35 km reach of the Gallinas River (Mexico) using high-resolution UAV-derived bathymetry and field-based discharge measurements. The model was calibrated and independently validated prior to simulating a 25-year return period flood (peak discharge = 1231.8 ) and a drought scenario constrained by an environmental-flow threshold (4.1 ). Results reveal the emergence of hydrodynamic thresholds driven by cumulative reach-scale losses (), producing nonlinear downstream discharge decay under low-flow conditions and requiring a minimum upstream inflow of to maintain ecological continuity. Under flood forcing, inundation patterns are primarily controlled by channel geometry and longitudinal slope reduction rather than discharge magnitude alone. These findings demonstrate that infiltration-influenced rivers exhibit dual hydrodynamic controls under contrasting extremes and highlight the importance of explicitly representing cumulative exchange processes in two-dimensional modeling frameworks. The study provides transferable insights for assessing drought resilience and flood risk in permeable or groundwater-connected river systems facing increasing hydroclimatic variability.Keywords: Hydrodynamic modeling, Hydrometeorological extreme, Groundwater-surface water interaction, Drought assessment, Karst river systemsSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Natural hazards, Water resources

Introduction

Hydrometeorological extremes are intensifying worldwide due to climate variability and anthropogenic pressures, posing significant challenges for river systems and the communities that depend on them. Prolonged droughts reduce aquatic habitat availability, alter water chemistry, and compromise ecosystem resilience1, while extreme rainfall events generate high-magnitude floods that disrupt sediment transport, nutrient cycling, and riparian dynamics, often resulting in substantial socio-economic losses2,3. Recent studies indicate that climate-induced hydrological variability is exacerbating basin-scale vulnerability, particularly in regions with limited adaptive capacity4,5. These increasing pressures underscore the need for robust predictive tools capable of capturing river responses under contrasting hydrological forcing and supporting integrated ecological and socio-economic management strategies.

The Gallinas River, located in the Huasteca Potosina region, has experienced drastic fluctuations in flow, with critical drought periods alternating with significant flooding during intense precipitation events. These extreme conditions affect the natural environment, human settlements, agriculture, and regional infrastructure. Understanding the mechanisms driving these changes is crucial for effective water resource management and disaster mitigation.

Rivers play a vital role in shaping landscapes and supporting biodiversity, acting as essential corridors for ecological and economic activities. They provide critical habitats for various aquatic and terrestrial species, maintain ecological balance, and facilitate migration routes for fish and other wildlife6. Additionally, rivers contribute to nutrient cycling and sediment transport, which are vital for sustaining fertile floodplains and wetlands7,8. The interconnectedness of river ecosystems with surrounding environments underscores their fundamental role in sustaining biodiversity and ecosystem services that benefit both nature and human societies9. However, river hydrodynamics are highly sensitive to climatic fluctuations and anthropogenic interventions, including land-use changes, dam construction, and water abstraction10. The Gallinas River, renowned for its natural beauty and ecological significance, has been increasingly exposed to environmental stressors that threaten its stability. Seasonal fluctuations in water levels, exacerbated by changing weather patterns and human-induced extractions, highlight the urgent need for systematic study and management.

In recent years, the intensification of extreme weather events has led to unpredictable variations in river flow, posing significant challenges for maintaining water security and ecological balance. The increasing frequency of droughts has raised concerns over water scarcity, while excessive precipitation has led to destructive floods that disrupt communities and local economies. For example, the 2017 flooding in Veracruz caused significant displacement and economic losses, damaging thousands of homes and affecting agricultural productivity11. Similarly, prolonged droughts in northern Mexico have severely impacted water availability for human consumption and irrigation, exacerbating socio-economic inequalities and prompting migration12. These disruptions underscore the urgent need for comprehensive water management policies that account for climate variability and extreme events. Comparable trends have been observed in other basins, such as the Mekong and the Nile, where climatic variability interacts with upstream governance, intensifying downstream risks13,14. These instances highlight the necessity for advanced numerical modeling to understand better, predict, and mitigate such impacts.

Numerical modeling plays a crucial role in hydrodynamic studies, enabling the prediction of river behavior under varying weather and hydrological conditions. Previous research has demonstrated the effectiveness of numerical models in simulating riverine hydrodynamics, sediment transport, and flood forecasting15,16. Such models allow researchers and policymakers to assess potential risks and design appropriate adaptation strategies for sustainable water management17,18. The increasing uncertainty associated with climate change, combined with anthropogenic modifications to river systems, makes hydrodynamic modeling an indispensable tool for informed decision-making in water governance19,20. In large river systems such as the Danube and the Yangtze, hydrodynamic models have been used to develop flood hazard maps and support early warning systems, demonstrating their critical role in risk reduction and policy planning21,22.

Accurately modeling these responses is particularly challenging in river systems characterized by strong groundwater–surface-water interactions and permeable channel materials. In infiltration-influenced reaches, cumulative subsurface exchange can produce nonlinear downstream discharge attenuation under low-flow conditions, potentially leading to ecological-flow interruption. Under high-flow conditions, flood propagation is governed not only by peak discharge magnitude but also by channel geometry, longitudinal slope, and hydraulic roughness. These interacting controls may generate threshold behaviors that are not readily captured by simplified hydrological approaches.

Two-dimensional hydrodynamic models have become essential tools for simulating river flow dynamics, floodplain inundation, and velocity distributions under extreme scenarios15,16. Such models enable spatially explicit representation of channel morphology and flow patterns, supporting hazard assessment and water management planning. However, while these tools are widely used for flood mapping and infrastructure planning, fewer studies have explicitly examined how infiltration-dominated hydrogeological characteristics modulate hydrodynamic responses under both drought and flood extremes within a unified modeling framework.

The Gallinas River, located in the Huasteca Potosina region of Mexico, represents a system where these processes are particularly relevant. The river supports biodiversity, tourism, agriculture, and local communities, while sustaining the Tamul waterfall, a landmark of ecological and cultural significance. Recent years have shown pronounced seasonal variability, including dry-season flow reductions and intense precipitation events that lead to localized flooding. Field observations indicate significant longitudinal discharge decay along the reach, suggesting strong cumulative losses likely linked to permeable bed materials and groundwater exchange.

Despite the recognized importance of such processes, there remains a limited quantitative understanding of how cumulative infiltration losses interact with extreme hydrological forcing to generate hydrodynamic thresholds in medium-sized tropical river systems. Addressing this gap is critical for improving both drought resilience assessment and flood hazard characterization in groundwater-connected environments.

Beyond providing a site-specific assessment, this study contributes to the hydrodynamic modeling literature by identifying threshold behaviors emerging from cumulative reach-scale losses and channel morphology interactions under contrasting extreme forcing. By quantifying how infiltration-driven discharge attenuation governs ecological-flow persistence and how geomorphological controls modulate floodplain activation, the results offer transferable mechanistic insights applicable to permeable or groundwater-connected river systems facing increasing hydroclimatic variability.

In the specific context of the Gallinas River, hydrodynamic simulations are inherently complex due to the basin’s predominant karst geology. In such environments, surface flow dynamics are strongly controlled by substantial water infiltration through fractured limestone and bedrock discontinuities41. This deep percolation process generates highly non-linear hydrological responses to precipitation events, frequently leading to dynamic surface–groundwater exchanges. Under certain conditions, surface discharge may partially or temporarily disappear into subsurface conduits before re-emerging downstream near major geomorphological features such as the Tamul waterfall42. Consequently, the basin’s geological structure serves as a primary regulator of discharge variability and surface-water availability.

Within this framework, the recent intermittent drying of the Tamul waterfall has raised a critical question: to what extent is this phenomenon driven by natural karst-controlled infiltration processes, and to what extent is it amplified by anthropogenic pressures such as groundwater extraction and distributed water withdrawals along the approximately 100 km river corridor? Disentangling these interacting drivers requires a modeling approach capable of resolving spatially distributed flow adjustments and reach-scale losses under varying hydrological conditions.

To address this challenge, the present study applies a two-dimensional hydrodynamic modeling framework to a 20.35 km representative reach of the Gallinas River. The Environmental Fluid Dynamics Code (EFDC) was selected due to its proven capability to simulate spatially distributed velocity fields, morphologically controlled flow adjustments, and exchange processes under variable boundary conditions34–36. High-resolution topographic and bathymetric data were generated through UAV-based photogrammetry, complemented by field discharge measurements and topographic surveys conducted during April, September, and November 2024. This integration of field-based observations and numerical simulation provides a physically constrained basis for model calibration and independent validation.

The study specifically investigates how infiltration-dominated hydrogeological conditions modulate hydrodynamic responses under contrasting extreme forcings. Particular emphasis is placed on identifying threshold behaviors associated with cumulative reach-scale losses during drought conditions and geomorphological controls on floodplain activation during high-discharge events. Following calibration and independent validation, the model was applied to simulate two complementary extreme scenarios: (i) a statistically derived 25-year return period flood event and (ii) a critical drought condition constrained by environmental-flow thresholds.

Rather than presenting purely site-specific outputs, the results are interpreted in terms of transferable hydrodynamic mechanisms characteristic of groundwater-connected river systems exposed to increasing hydroclimatic variability. By quantifying how infiltration processes and channel morphology jointly regulate flow persistence and flood propagation, this study contributes mechanistic insight into the behavior of karst-influenced rivers under extreme conditions, providing a framework applicable beyond the Gallinas River basin43.

In an era of escalating climate uncertainty and intensifying human pressures on river systems, advanced hydrodynamic modeling constitutes an indispensable tool for evidence-based water governance, risk reduction, and ecosystem conservation. By integrating high-resolution field data with physically based 2D simulation, this study seeks to generate actionable insights for sustainable management of the Gallinas River and to contribute transferable knowledge to the broader challenge of managing infiltration-influenced rivers under changing hydroclimatic regimes.

Material and methods

Study area and modeled reach

The study area is located in the Huasteca Potosina, one of the four regions that constitute the state of San Luis Potosí. It is in the eastern part of the state, within the Sierra Madre Oriental and the coastal plains of the Gulf of Mexico. The Huasteca region, comprising 20 municipalities that vary in terms of their vast physiographic and cultural diversity, is influenced by the humid air masses that move from the Gulf. Within its rugged geography, critical river networks converge, giving rise to rivers, imposing waterfalls, numerous natural sites, and cavities.

The Gallinas River, located in the municipality of Tamasopo, gives rise to the Tamul Waterfall, which is situated between the municipalities of Tamasopo and Aquismón. The basin is primarily located within the municipality of Tamasopo and, to a lesser extent, includes portions of the municipalities of Rayón, Cárdenas, Alaquines, Ciudad del Maíz, El Naranjo, Aquismón, and Ciudad Valles. The Tamul waterfall has a height of 105 meters, making it the state’s largest and most iconic waterfall.

We conducted a thorough analysis of a section of the Gallinas River, extending from the El Carpintero bridge to the Tamul waterfall (see Fig. 1). This region was chosen due to its historical susceptibility to extreme hydrological events.

Topography and bathymetry acquisition

High-resolution topographic and bathymetric data of the 20.35 km study reach were obtained through UAV-based photogrammetry combined with ground control point (GCP) surveying. A Phantom 4 Pro UAV was deployed during three field campaigns (April, September, and November 2024), executing 24 flight plans and capturing a total of 5,410 georeferenced images. Flights were conducted at an average altitude of 120 m with longitudinal and lateral overlaps of approximately 80% and 70%, respectively, to ensure robust image alignment.

Ground control points were surveyed using Trimble R8s GNSS equipment. A total of 57 GCPs were distributed along the reach, achieving horizontal and vertical positional accuracies of approximately ±0.10 m after post-processing. Mean vertical discrepancies between DTM elevations and independent field checkpoints were below 0.12 m.

Photogrammetric processing was carried out in Agisoft Metashape to generate dense point clouds, Digital Surface Models (DSM), Digital Terrain Models (DTM), and orthomosaics. The final DTM resolution was 0.05 m per pixel, enabling detailed representation of channel morphology and adjacent floodplain features.

Bathymetric information was incorporated through field measurements in submerged sections and integrated into the terrain model to ensure continuous channel representation. The resulting topographic–bathymetric surface was resampled to match the computational grid resolution used in EFDC simulations, preserving key geomorphological features while optimizing computational performance.

The flight plan is developed using the DroneDeploy application, a free tool for intuitive flight planning and management44. The software optimizes flight characteristics and transects by selecting the UAV model, flight elevation, and area of interest. The flight plans generated on the web platform are linked and downloaded to the mobile application, enabling synchronization between the UAV and the mobile app during fieldwork.

During each measurement campaign, between 200 and 300 images were captured per flight, some of which contained errors. Images that exhibit distortions, blurriness, or are not perpendicular to the Earth’s surface must be excluded. Additionally, the number of flights (photos) to be processed simultaneously must be considered, as this is directly related to the computer’s architecture and capacity.

The images have to be adjusted to the selected reference system due to the GPS integrated into the Phantom 4 Pro. However, this GPS has a positioning error ranging from 5 to 10 meters (both vertically and horizontally). This error is reduced by placing Ground Control Points (GCPs), which can achieve errors as low as 0.1 meters through the visual positioning of the surveyed control points.

The dense point cloud is generated using depth maps calculated from overlapping images, taking into account both exterior and interior relative orientation. The software constructs multiple depth maps for each camera (photo), which are then merged to create a new combined depth map. This process utilizes information from overlapping regions to filter out incorrect depth measurements.

A Digital Surface Model (DSM) can be generated from the dense point cloud, while depth maps and pixel classification are used to create the Digital Terrain Model (DTM). The Digital Elevation Model is a 2.5D representation of a surface in a regular grid format, with height values stored in each grid cell (pixel). The DTM can be generated from a dense point cloud, a mesh, or depth maps. The software enables the creation of a Digital Surface Model (DSM), which is a 2.5D representation of the Earth’s surface including all objects on it, as well as the Digital Terrain Model (DTM) (see Fig. 2), which represents the bare ground surface without any objects such as vegetation or buildings.

Hydrometric data (ADCP discharge and velocity)

Hydrometric data were collected to characterize flow conditions at the upstream boundary of the modeled reach and to provide control measurements for calibration and validation. Discharge and depth-averaged velocity were measured with a Teledyne RD Instruments RiverRay Acoustic Doppler Current Profiler (ADCP) mounted on a Hydroboard and operated from a small boat. Measurements were conducted at two control sections: (i) the El Carpintero bridge, which defines the model inlet, and (ii) near the Tamul waterfall at the downstream end of the modeled domain.

At El Carpintero, a continuous monitoring campaign was carried out on June 15, 2020. ADCP transects were acquired every 30 minutes from 08:00 to 19:00, producing an 11-hour time series of discharge. To reduce random measurement error, each 30-minute observation consisted of four repeated cross-sectional transects. The resulting semi-hourly discharge series was used to construct the time-varying inflow hydrograph applied as the upstream boundary condition during model calibration.

Velocity observations were obtained simultaneously from the ADCP profiles and processed to compute representative depth-averaged velocities for each acquisition interval. EFDC model setup

Hydrodynamic simulations were performed using the Environmental Fluid Dynamics Code (EFDC), a two-dimensional, vertically hydrostatic model that solves the shallow water momentum and continuity equations on a curvilinear grid45. The model was selected due to its capacity to represent spatially distributed velocity fields, wetting–drying processes, and reach-scale exchange mechanisms relevant to infiltration-influenced river systems.

The computational domain covered the 20.35 km reach between El Carpintero bridge and the Tamul waterfall. A curvilinear grid was generated using CVL Grid software, with refined cell resolution within the main channel (3.5 5 m) and coarser resolution across adjacent floodplain areas (7 5 m). Grid sensitivity tests were performed using alternative resolutions (2 2 m, 2.5 5 m, 3 7 m, and 5 10 m), and the selected configuration represented a balance between numerical stability and morphological fidelity.

Boundary conditions consisted of a time-varying inflow hydrograph prescribed at the upstream boundary (El Carpintero bridge) and a downstream open boundary at the Tamul waterfall. Three additional distributed sink terms were implemented along the reach to represent observed longitudinal discharge attenuation attributed to infiltration losses.

Boundary and initial conditions

The upstream boundary condition was defined at the El Carpintero bridge using a time-varying discharge hydrograph derived from ADCP measurements during the June 15, 2020 monitoring campaign. The semi-hourly discharge series was imposed as a flow boundary condition during calibration runs. For extreme-event simulations, synthetic hydrographs representing the 25-year return period flood were constructed based on regional frequency analysis and applied at the same upstream boundary.

At the downstream limit of the modeled reach, near the Tamul waterfall, a stage-controlled boundary condition was implemented. For calibration and validation scenarios, downstream water levels were constrained using field observations. For flood simulations, the downstream boundary allowed dynamic adjustment of water surface elevation consistent with the imposed upstream discharge.

Distributed losses along the reach were represented through localized sink terms at three identified infiltration zones, consistent with observed longitudinal discharge attenuation. These effective loss terms were parameterized during calibration within physically realistic ranges to reproduce measured downstream discharge reduction.

Initial conditions were defined using a steady-state solution obtained by running the model under average observed flow until hydraulic equilibrium was achieved. The resulting water surface profile and velocity field were used as the initial state for transient simulations. This approach minimized numerical instabilities associated with abrupt flow initialization and ensured physically consistent starting conditions across scenarios.

Parameterization of infiltration losses and abstractions

Field observations and hydrometric measurements indicate significant longitudinal discharge attenuation along the modeled reach, particularly under low-flow conditions. This behavior is consistent with permeable channel materials and groundwater–surface water exchange processes characteristic of infiltration-influenced river systems in karst or fractured environments.

Because the EFDC implementation used in this study does not explicitly couple three-dimensional groundwater flow, subsurface exchange was represented through effective distributed loss terms applied at three reach-scale zones where cumulative discharge decay was observed. These zones were identified based on (i) differences between upstream and downstream gauged discharge, (ii) geomorphological characteristics of the channel bed (gravel and coarse alluvium), and (iii) previous hydrological assessments of the basin.

Infiltration losses were parameterized as volumetric sink terms (m s) distributed over defined grid regions. Initial estimates were derived from measured discharge differences between control sections and normalized by reach length. During calibration, loss coefficients were adjusted within physically realistic bounds to reproduce the observed downstream discharge attenuation under monitored flow conditions. Parameter ranges were constrained to avoid nonphysical water-balance behavior and were evaluated using sensitivity testing.

For flood scenarios, infiltration losses were kept proportional to the wetted area but limited to calibrated maximum values to reflect the reduced relative influence of subsurface exchange under high-stage conditions. For drought simulations, cumulative losses were allowed to exert dominant control on downstream flow persistence, enabling evaluation of threshold behavior associated with surface-flow interruption.

This representation does not attempt to resolve detailed groundwater dynamics; rather, it provides an effective hydrodynamic approximation of reach-scale subsurface exchange consistent with available data. The adopted parameterization captures the first-order influence of infiltration-dominated hydrogeology on longitudinal discharge decay while maintaining numerical stability and water balance consistency.

Bottom roughness

Bottom roughness was parameterized using Manning’s coefficient (n), calibrated against observed velocity and discharge data. Spatially uniform roughness was adopted across the channel and floodplain domains, with values constrained to physically realistic bounds for gravel-bed rivers.

Wetting–drying capability was activated to allow dynamic floodplain inundation during high-flow simulations and to capture surface-flow interruption during drought conditions. Threshold depth parameters were selected following EFDC recommended practice to ensure numerical stability without artificially suppressing floodplain activation.

The computational time step was selected based on grid resolution and expected velocity magnitude to satisfy numerical stability criteria. Stability and convergence were verified through sensitivity testing of the time-step size and monitoring of global mass-balance closure throughout the simulations.

Calibration and validation

Model calibration was conducted using a highly instrumented hydrological event recorded on June 15, 2020, at the El Carpintero bridge, which defines the upstream boundary of the modeled reach. During this campaign, discharge and velocity profiles were measured at 30-minute intervals over an 11-hour period using a RiverRay ADCP. Four repeated transects were performed at each interval to reduce random measurement error. The resulting semi-hourly discharge series was used to construct a time-varying inflow hydrograph for transient calibration.

Calibration targeted three primary aspects of system behavior: (i) reproduction of depth-averaged velocity magnitudes at control sections, (ii) consistency of longitudinal discharge attenuation along the reach, and (iii) agreement between simulated and observed downstream discharge near the Tamul waterfall.

The principal adjustable parameters were Manning’s roughness coefficient (n) and the effective infiltration/loss coefficients assigned to identified permeable zones. Parameter ranges were constrained within physically realistic bounds based on channel morphology, substrate characteristics, and published values for gravel-bed rivers. Extreme scenarios definition

To evaluate the hydrodynamic response of the Gallinas River under contrasting extreme forcings, two atypical hydrometeorological scenarios were defined: (i) a critical drought condition and (ii) an extreme flood event corresponding to a 25-year return period.

Drought scenario

The drought scenario was designed to represent critical low-flow conditions affecting surface flow persistence along the modeled reach. Historical discharge records and regional hydrological assessments were analyzed to identify characteristic minimum flow conditions. The critical drought discharge () was defined as the lower percentile of observed dry-season flows, constrained by environmental-flow considerations necessary to sustain ecological connectivity and continuous discharge over the Tamul waterfall.

The drought boundary condition was imposed as a steady or slowly varying inflow hydrograph with magnitude equal to at the upstream boundary (El Carpintero bridge). The simulation aimed to evaluate cumulative longitudinal discharge attenuation driven by infiltration losses and to identify threshold behavior associated with surface-flow interruption.

Flood scenario (25-year return period)

The extreme flood scenario was defined using regional frequency analysis of peak discharge data. Annual maximum discharge series were analyzed using standard statistical methods (e.g., Log-Pearson Type III or Gumbel distribution, depending on data availability) to estimate the discharge associated with a 25-year return period ().

The design hydrograph was constructed by scaling a representative observed flood hydrograph to match the estimated peak discharge while preserving realistic rise and recession characteristics. This hydrograph was applied as a transient upstream boundary condition in the EFDC model.

The 25-year return period was selected as a compromise between infrastructure-relevant hazard assessment and physically plausible extreme forcing within the regional hydrological regime. The flood simulations were used to evaluate floodplain activation, inundation depth, velocity distribution, and spatial extent of overflow-prone areas.

Quantification and consistency

Both scenarios were evaluated using consistent boundary condition implementation and identical parameterization of channel roughness and infiltration processes. For each simulation, outputs included spatially distributed water depth, velocity magnitude, and longitudinal discharge profiles.

The drought scenario allowed assessment of ecological-flow persistence and identification of critical inflow thresholds below which downstream surface discharge becomes discontinuous. The flood scenario enabled quantification of inundation extent and hydrodynamic intensity under statistically defined extreme forcing.

Together, these contrasting scenarios provide a mechanistic framework for analyzing how infiltration-dominated hydrogeological conditions modulate hydrodynamic response under atypical hydrometeorological extremes.

Representativeness of drought and flood conditions. The available field datasets were not intended to statistically represent the full spectrum of long-term hydrological variability. Instead, they were used to constrain the hydrodynamic model under physically observed low- and moderate-flow conditions, which serve as boundary conditions for scenario-based simulations.

Drought conditions were represented using (i) observed low-flow measurements during field campaigns and (ii) the environmental-flow threshold derived from the NMX-AA-159-SCFI-2012 standard. These inputs allowed simulation of a physically consistent dry-season scenario in which cumulative reach-scale losses govern downstream flow persistence.

Flood conditions were not derived from short-term field measurements alone but from a statistically defined design hydrograph corresponding to a 25-year return period event, estimated using established hydrological frequency analysis. Therefore, the flood scenario reflects a probabilistic extreme condition rather than a single observed event.

The objective of this study is not long-term probabilistic forecasting but the identification of hydrodynamic mechanisms and threshold behavior under physically plausible extreme conditions.

Discussion

Hydrodynamic threshold behavior under contrasting extremes

Results indicate that the Gallinas River exhibits distinct threshold responses under drought and flood forcing. Under low-flow conditions, cumulative reach-scale losses result in a nonlinear decline in discharge, leading to a critical inflow threshold below which ecological-flow continuity at the Tamul waterfall cannot be sustained. This behavior highlights the dominant role of infiltration and groundwater exchange processes in controlling downstream flow persistence.

Conversely, under high-flow conditions (RP25), floodplain activation is primarily governed by channel geometry, sinuosity, and longitudinal slope reduction in upstream reaches. The results demonstrate that extreme discharge alone does not fully determine inundation extent; rather, morphological configuration modulates the spatial distribution of hydraulic energy and overflow potential.

Flow velocities and water surface elevation under the 25-year return period flood

Under the 25-year return period flood scenario, the simulated hydraulics reveal a clear longitudinal differentiation of flow regimes along the modeled reach. The spatial patterns of depth-averaged velocity and water surface elevation indicate that flood propagation is not governed solely by discharge magnitude, but also by the interaction between channel curvature, local slope breaks, and the availability of floodplain storage.

The velocity results show that the highest values tend to concentrate within hydraulically confined segments, particularly where the channel is locally narrower, straighter, or guided by valley-side constraints. In these reaches, flood conveyance remains primarily channelized, producing higher momentum and stronger shear zones near the outer banks of bends. Conversely, in sections where the channel becomes more sinuous and the surrounding topography transitions into low-relief floodplain areas, the model predicts a redistribution of momentum through lateral spreading. This floodplain activation leads to a systematic reduction in in-channel velocity and increases the extent of shallow, slower-moving flow along the inundated margins.

This pattern is consistent with the expected two-dimensional behavior of large floods: velocity maxima align with hydraulic controls (constrictions, curvature-induced secondary circulation, and local slope), while velocity minima coincide with expanded cross-sectional conveyance and overbank flow pathways. Importantly, these changes imply that hazard is spatially heterogeneous: areas with lower depths may still experience locally elevated velocities where flow re-enters the channel or accelerates around floodplain obstacles, whereas deep inundation zones can exhibit comparatively low velocities if storage dominates conveyance.

The simulated WSE profile shows a reach-scale rise in stage consistent with the imposed extreme inflow, but with localized deviations associated with geomorphological controls. In particular, backwater effects are expected to develop upstream of segments with reduced conveyance capacity, including pronounced bends, mild-slope zones, or areas where floodplain storage is abruptly engaged. These conditions produce locally flatter WSE gradients and stage “pooling” upstream, followed by steeper gradients where the flow transitions back to more confined geometry.

From a process standpoint, the WSE results indicate that inundation is driven by the combined effect of (i) the longitudinal energy slope, (ii) lateral storage capacity, and (iii) frictional resistance. Therefore, flood stage cannot be inferred reliably from discharge alone, particularly in the presence of strong planform variability. This reinforces the value of a two-dimensional approach, because the simulated WSE field captures the lateral structure of hydraulic head during overbank conditions, including preferential flow paths and differential stage across inundated areas.

Interpreting velocities together with WSE (or depth) allows a clearer hazard narrative. The most critical flood impacts are expected where high stages coincide with sustained velocities, i.e., where floodplain activation occurs but does not sufficiently dissipate momentum, or where flow is forced through locally constrained corridors. These segments represent priority zones for risk assessment because they combine (a) deeper water levels (exposure) with (b) stronger flow intensity (damage potential).

In contrast, sections dominated by wide floodplain spreading may exhibit high WSE but reduced velocities, implying that the main hazard is associated more with inundation extent and duration than with erosive or structural forces. This distinction is useful for management, because mitigation measures differ: velocity-dominated hazards motivate reinforcement and conveyance planning, whereas stage-dominated hazards motivate land-use zoning, evacuation planning, and flood storage strategies.

Although infiltration losses strongly control low-flow persistence in the Gallinas River, the Q25 flood results suggest that, at event timescales and extreme discharges, the system behaves predominantly as a surface-flow-dominated conveyor, where floodplain geometry and frictional resistance largely determine velocity and stage patterns. In other words, the controlling mechanism shifts from reach-scale losses (drought) to morphodynamic/topographic controls (flood). This contrast supports the broader interpretation of the river as a dual-regime system, in which extreme floods activate two-dimensional pathways and storage that cannot be adequately captured by simplified 1D representations.

Hydrodynamic behavior under ecological-flow conditions

Under ecological-flow conditions (Q ), the hydrodynamic response of the Gallinas River differs fundamentally from the flood regime. Instead of lateral expansion and floodplain activation, the dominant process becomes longitudinal discharge attenuation driven by cumulative reach-scale losses.

The simulations indicate that, at inflows close to the environmental threshold, the system approaches a hydraulic tipping point in which small variations in upstream discharge or loss terms significantly alter downstream flow persistence. In contrast to the 25-year return period flood scenario, where flow redistribution is governed by floodplain storage and geomorphological controls, the ecological-flow regime is primarily controlled by the balance between inflow and distributed losses along the reach.

This behavior reflects the basin’s hydrogeological configuration. Infiltration through fractured carbonate bedrock and karst conduits induces a nonlinear longitudinal decay of discharge, which becomes proportionally more pronounced under low-flow conditions. Model results demonstrate that ecological-flow maintenance is not controlled by local channel curvature or floodplain geometry, but rather by the cumulative effect of subsurface exchange processes. When inflow exceeds cumulative losses, hydraulic continuity is preserved; when inflow approaches or falls below this balance, downstream discharge rapidly diminishes.

Velocity magnitudes under ecological-flow conditions are substantially lower and more spatially heterogeneous than during flood events. Instead of curvature-driven acceleration or confinement effects, velocities are primarily influenced by shallow flow depths, local bed roughness, and micro-topographic variability.

The simulated flow regime remains subcritical and momentum-limited, with reduced transport capacity and minimal erosive potential compared to high-discharge scenarios. However, localized reductions in depth produce segments where hydraulic connectivity becomes marginal. These areas exhibit increased sensitivity to small variations in discharge, indicating that ecological-flow conditions operate near a structural threshold of flow persistence.

The water surface elevation (WSE) profile under ecological-flow conditions exhibits a steeper effective energy gradient as a function of discharge magnitude. Because flow depths are shallow, small reductions in stage can lead to hydraulic disconnection between channel segments.

Simulations indicate that maintaining an inflow slightly above the ecological threshold allows continuous surface flow to reach the Tamul waterfall. Conversely, modest reductions in upstream inflow or increases in cumulative losses may induce partial hydraulic fragmentation, intermittent dry sections, or delayed downstream arrival of flow.

This confirms the presence of a functional hydrodynamic threshold rather than a linear relationship between inflow and downstream discharge. The ecological-flow value therefore represents not merely a regulatory standard, but a minimum hydraulic condition required to preserve longitudinal connectivity and downstream ecological functionality.

Comparison with the Q flood scenario highlights a clear regime shift in system controls. During extreme discharge, hydraulic behavior is dominated by channel morphology, floodplain storage, and frictional resistance. Under ecological-flow conditions, however, the dominant control shifts to groundwater–surface water interactions and cumulative infiltration losses.

The Gallinas River therefore behaves as a dual-regime system: surface-flow dominated during floods and groundwater-interaction dominated during drought conditions. This transition in governing mechanisms underscores the importance of evaluating both extremes to understand system resilience under increasing hydroclimatic variability.

Ecological and management implications

From an ecological perspective, maintaining discharge at or above the environmental-flow threshold ensures longitudinal hydraulic continuity, sustained turbulence and oxygenation, and the persistence of downstream aquatic habitats. From a management standpoint, the results indicate that additional water withdrawals or increases in groundwater extraction during the dry season could shift the system below its connectivity threshold.

Rather than representing speculative inference, these conclusions are supported by process-based numerical modeling constrained by field observations and sensitivity testing of dominant loss parameters. The robustness of the results lies in the structural consistency of the hydrodynamic response across realistic variations in roughness and cumulative loss terms.

Comparison with previous hydrodynamic modeling studies

The sensitivity of flood extent to channel roughness and geometry is consistent with previous HEC-RAS-based river modeling applications, which identify bathymetric resolution and friction parameters as dominant controls on simulated velocity and depth distributions. Similarly, studies of groundwater-influenced river systems report that distributed losses significantly affect low-flow persistence and longitudinal discharge attenuation.

Our findings extend this understanding by explicitly linking cumulative reach-scale losses to ecological-flow threshold behavior, demonstrating how infiltration-dominated systems may exhibit amplified drought sensitivity relative to upstream discharge conditions.

The hydrodynamic simulations demonstrate that two-dimensional modeling provides a substantially higher level of spatial resolution in representing water surface elevation and velocity fields compared to cross-section–based approaches. By resolving flow dynamics over a bidimensional surface, the adopted framework captures local variations in inundation extent and depth at a cell scale of approximately 3.5 m, rather than relying on discretized transverse sections spaced at larger intervals.

This increased spatial detail becomes particularly relevant in morphologically complex reaches characterized by floodplains, channel sinuosity, and lateral storage zones. In such areas, simplified representations tend to smooth hydraulic gradients and may overestimate or underestimate inundation limits depending on local geometry. Differences are most pronounced in low-gradient sections with broad floodplains and in highly sinuous reaches, where lateral connectivity and microtopographic controls influence the activation of overbank flow.

Conversely, in confined or well-defined channel segments bounded by steep valley walls, both simplified and spatially distributed approaches tend to converge toward similar inundation patterns, suggesting that geometric complexity is a key control on model divergence.

The trade-off associated with two-dimensional modeling lies in computational demand. Because the governing equations must be solved for each computational cell, simulation time increases relative to cross-sectional methods. However, this additional cost is justified in infiltration-dominated systems where spatially distributed losses and local hydraulic gradients interact to produce threshold-like behavior in flow continuity and downstream discharge attenuation.

Under ecological-flow conditions, the spatially resolved framework proves particularly advantageous. When upstream discharge decreases due to cumulative infiltration losses, surface-flow persistence may vary locally along the reach. A cross-sectional representation may predict discontinuities or loss of flow in certain segments, whereas the bidimensional model captures cell-scale redistribution and microchannel connectivity that sustain localized surface flow. This distinction is critical for assessing environmental-flow persistence and waterfall continuity under drought scenarios.

Overall, the results indicate that model performance and agreement depend strongly on topographic configuration and hydrogeological context. In geometrically simple and confined reaches, simplified representations may approximate distributed solutions. In contrast, in floodplain-dominated and infiltration-sensitive systems, spatially explicit hydrodynamic modeling provides more robust characterization of inundation extent, depth variability, and ecological-flow thresholds.

Implications for infiltration-influenced river systems

The results suggest that in permeable or karst-influenced river reaches, hydrodynamic response under extreme forcing is controlled by two interacting mechanisms: (i) geometric confinement during flood propagation and (ii) cumulative subsurface exchange during drought periods. This dual control implies that management strategies should address both morphological flood risk zones and minimum inflow requirements necessary to sustain ecological continuity.

Model uncertainty and robustness

Although the study is constrained by limited long-term datasets, model robustness was evaluated through independent event validation and sensitivity analysis of dominant parameters (Manning’s roughness and cumulative loss terms). While absolute discharge values vary moderately under parameter perturbations, the identification of ecological-flow thresholds and spatial flood patterns remains structurally stable. Therefore, conclusions are framed in terms of hydrodynamic mechanisms rather than deterministic long-term prediction.

Infiltration-driven longitudinal flow decay

Field measurements documented a systematic reduction in discharge between El Carpintero and the Tamul waterfall during low-flow conditions. The numerical model reproduces this longitudinal decay through distributed loss terms representing cumulative infiltration and subsurface exchange.

This behavior highlights the dominant role of infiltration processes in controlling ecological-flow persistence. The results indicate that small variations in cumulative losses significantly affect downstream discharge continuity, suggesting the existence of hydrodynamic thresholds governing surface-flow persistence in permeable river systems.

Morphological control of floodplain activation

Under the simulated 25-year flood event, overbank flow primarily occurred in upstream segments characterized by higher sinuosity and reduced longitudinal slope. In contrast, confined channel sections embedded within steeper or laterally constrained terrain exhibited limited floodplain activation despite elevated discharge.

This spatial differentiation underscores the role of channel morphology and roughness in governing flood propagation patterns. The results confirm that flood extent is not solely a function of discharge magnitude, but strongly influenced by geometric and hydraulic controls.

Implications for water security and risk management

The contrasting controls identified for drought and flood conditions have direct implications for basin management. During low-flow periods, cumulative infiltration and abstractions determine ecological-flow sustainability, highlighting the need for groundwater-use regulation and conservation strategies. During extreme rainfall events, flood risk is spatially concentrated in morphologically susceptible reaches, suggesting that structural mitigation should prioritize these segments. By distinguishing dominant controls under contrasting extremes, the study supports targeted and process-informed management strategies rather than generalized interventions.

Limitations and future research

The model does not explicitly couple groundwater flow processes, and distributed losses are represented in a lumped manner constrained by field observations. Additionally, full probabilistic uncertainty propagation was beyond the scope of this study. Future research should integrate coupled groundwater–surface water modeling and multi-seasonal monitoring to further refine threshold characterization and predictive capability under climate change scenarios.

Conclusions

This study developed and applied a physically based, two-dimensional hydrodynamic modeling framework to investigate the response of the Gallinas River to contrasting extreme hydrometeorological conditions. The objective was not to generate long-term probabilistic forecasts, but rather to identify governing hydraulic mechanisms, threshold behavior, and structural controls under physically plausible drought and flood scenarios within an infiltration-dominated river system.

The hydrological characterization of the basin, including geomorphological analysis, morphometric parameters, land-use assessment, and frequency-based hydrological estimation–enabled the definition of two bounding conditions representative of extreme forcing. On the upper bound, a statistically derived 25-year return period flood event () was implemented to evaluate floodplain activation and overbank flow dynamics. On the lower bound, an ecological-flow threshold of , derived from the NMX-AA-159-SCFI-2012 standard, was used to represent critical dry-season conditions affecting surface-flow persistence at the Tamul waterfall.

High-resolution topobathymetric data obtained through UAV-based photogrammetry and ground control surveying allowed the construction of a detailed digital terrain model with spatial resolution. This dataset provided the geometric basis for the EFDC hydrodynamic simulations. Field hydrometric campaigns further documented a systematic longitudinal decay in discharge between the El Carpintero bridge and the Tamul waterfall during low-flow conditions. This observed behavior was incorporated into the model through distributed loss terms representing cumulative infiltration, subsurface exchange, and water abstractions, ensuring physical consistency between field evidence and numerical representation.

Simulation results reveal a clear separation of dominant controls under contrasting hydrological extremes. During drought conditions, cumulative infiltration losses govern ecological-flow persistence and determine whether surface discharge is sustained along the reach. Sensitivity analysis confirms that modest perturbations (±20–30%) in infiltration parameters significantly influence downstream flow continuity, while roughness variations exert comparatively smaller effects under low-flow regimes. Conversely, during the simulated flood scenario, floodplain activation and peak water levels are primarily controlled by channel geometry, sinuosity, and Manning’s roughness. This distinction demonstrates that drought resilience and flood propagation are regulated by different hydraulic mechanisms within the same river system.

Under the 25-year flood event, the model indicates significant overbank flow in upstream segments (chainages 8+000 to 20+500), driven by reduced longitudinal slope and planform sinuosity. Confined channel sections exhibited limited inundation despite elevated discharge, underscoring the role of morphological controls in spatial flood distribution. In contrast, simulations under dry-season conditions show that maintaining inflows near is sufficient to sustain the ecological threshold discharge over the Tamul waterfall, supporting ecosystem integrity and socio-economic activities dependent on continuous flow.

Scientific robustness is supported on multiple levels. First, structural consistency of hydrodynamic patterns was observed across the entire modeled reach, rather than being constrained to a single calibration point. Second, independent validation against a separate hydrological event yielded RMSE values between 0.01 and 0.12, indicating satisfactory predictive performance. Third, sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the principal conclusions, particularly the identification of ecological-flow thresholds and spatial flood patterns–remain stable across realistic parameter perturbations. Consequently, interpretations are framed in terms of relative system behavior and controlling mechanisms rather than precise absolute magnitudes.

Although groundwater–surface water interactions were represented using observation-constrained distributed losses rather than a fully coupled groundwater module, this parsimonious representation effectively reproduces reach-scale hydraulic behavior under both low- and high-flow conditions. Future studies incorporating coupled hydrogeological modeling and extended multi-year monitoring would refine quantitative uncertainty bounds; however, the present framework provides a defensible mechanistic basis for scenario-based hydrodynamic assessment.

Beyond the local case study, the findings contribute transferable insight into the behavior of permeable or karst-influenced river systems subject to increasing hydroclimatic variability. Specifically, the study demonstrates that infiltration-dominated exchange processes govern drought persistence thresholds, while channel morphology and roughness dominate flood dynamics. By explicitly distinguishing these mechanisms, the work advances hydrodynamic modeling from site-specific prediction toward process-oriented understanding applicable to similar riverine environments.

In summary, this research highlights the critical role of physically based numerical modeling, constrained by field observations, in identifying threshold behavior and dominant hydraulic controls under extreme hydrometeorological forcing. Such mechanistic insight is essential for informed water-resource management, ecological-flow protection, and flood-risk mitigation in regions facing intensifying climate variability.
