Utility of mimicking B cell epitopes between Mycobacterium leprae and host as prognostic biomarkers in type 1 reaction in leprosy
Authors: Vinay Kumar Pathak, Itu Singh, Rahul Sharma, Ravindra P Turankar, Utpal Sengupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMycobacterial infections such as leprosy and tuberculosis can induce autoimmune responses by disrupting host immune homeostasis. We previously identified eight mimicking B cell epitopes (BCEs) with elevated autoantibody levels in Type 1 Reaction (T1R). In this study, we longitudinally evaluated BCE-specific antibody responses and standardized a dot blot assay for T1R detection. A total of 150 untreated leprosy patients were enrolled: 50 each with T1R, Type 2 Reaction (T2R), and non-reactional (NR) cases; however, follow-up of T2R patients was not feasible. Patients were monitored every 3–4 months for changes in antibody profiles. ELISA quantified antibodies against 15 BCEs derived from Mycobacterium leprae (M. leprae) (HSP65, 50 S ribosomal protein, lysyl tRNA synthetase) and host proteins (keratin, myelin basic protein). Dot blot analysis was performed with pooled sera from each group, and cut-off values were derived from NR intensities. Elevated antibodies against HSP2, HSP4–HSP6, KER1, KER2, KER4, MBP50SB1, and MBPLMB2 were strongly associated with T1R and correlated with disease progression in longitudinal analyses. Blot analyses confirmed higher intensities in T1R and T2R compared to NR and healthy controls (HC). Furthermore, future studies with larger longitudinal cohorts and longer follow-up periods are needed to validate these findings and to better characterize changes in autoantibody levels throughout disease progression and treatment, while the identification of these BCEs as potential prognostic biomarkers may also support the adaptation of the dot blot assay into cost-effective lateral flow platforms for clinical screening.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54875-5.Keywords: Leprosy, Autoantibodies, Molecular mimicry, Type 1 reaction, Mimicking B cell epitopes (BCEs)Subject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Immunology

Introduction

Leprosy reactions are immunologically dynamic and complex inflammatory episodes that can manifest before, during, or following anti-leprosy therapy, affecting approximately 30–50% of patients1,2. Type 1 reaction (T1R), also known as a reversal reaction, is characterised by acute inflammation of existing skin lesions and/or peripheral nerves, and constitutes a major cause of neuritis, frequently resulting in nerve impairment and subsequent deformities, particularly in the hands and feet. The underlying immune response is indicative of a delayed-type hypersensitivity (DTH) reaction, characterised by the infiltration of peripheral blood lymphocytes. This response is associated with heightened reactivity to Mycobacterium leprae (M. leprae) antigens, as demonstrated by lymphocyte transformation tests (LTT)3. Such immune activation may contribute to a localized reduction in bacillary load and increased T cell-mediated responses within affected nerves, ultimately leading to nerve damage4. Type 2 reaction (T2R) occurs in approximately 25% of leprosy cases and is characterized by the appearance of erythematous, inflamed nodules and papules. The pathogenesis of T2R involves immune complex deposition within lesions along with alterations in CMI. Complement activation and neutrophil relocation5, together with the release of tissue-damaging interleukins (ILs) 12 (IL-12) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), are key factors in the progression of the disease6,7.

Detection of diverse autoantibodies and the evidence of molecular mimicry between M. leprae and host proteins offer important insights into the immunopathogenesis of leprosy-associated autoimmune phenomena during such reactional episodes. However, further investigation is required to delineate the underlying mechanisms driving these autoimmune responses comprehensively and to understand their impact on disease progression and therapeutic approaches. Building upon these findings, our previous study proposed that shared B and T cell epitopes between M. leprae and host antigens may contribute to both the pathogenesis of leprosy and the development of associated immunological reactions and complications8.

In our previous study, we examined a total of fifteen B cell mimicking epitopes (BCEs) between proteins, HSP65 (M. leprae) and protein keratin, as well as myelin basic protein (MBP) of the human host and evaluated host humoral immune response to determine the antigenic determinant responsible for the autoimmune phenomenon in the T1R. We reported eight BCEs against which the mean levels of antibodies were found to be higher in T1R when compared to Non-reaction (NR) borderline leprosy patients and healthy controls (HC). In the present study, we evaluated the immune response against these BCEs in a longitudinal cohort with T1R and NR leprosy patients. Furthermore, we standardized a dot blot assay with significantly reactive BCEs as a diagnostic test for T1R in leprosy.

Results

Differential basal autoantibody response against mimicking BCEs distinguish reactional states from non-reactional leprosy and healthy controls

In our previous study, basal levels of autoantibodies against mimicking BCEs were reported in leprosy patients without reactions (NR), patients with T1R, and HC. In the present study, we additionally included leprosy patients with T2R (n = 50). ELISA was performed using fifteen predicted and synthesized BCEs: HSP1–HSP7, KER1–KER4, MBP50SB1, MBP50SB2, MBPLMB1, and MBPLMB2. Detailed antibody levels are provided in the supplementary data. Briefly, antibodies against HSP1 were significantly higher in T1R, T2R, and NR compared to HC (p < 0.0001). Antibodies against HSP2 and HSP3 were significantly elevated in NR compared to T1R (p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001, respectively), T2R (p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001, respectively), and HC (p < 0.0001, p < 0.001, respectively), whereas antibodies against HSP4, HSP5, and HSP6 were significantly higher in T1R compared to T2R (p < 0.001, p = ns, p < 0.001, respectively), NR (p < 0.0001, p < 0.01, p < 0.0001, respectively) and HC (p < 0.0001, p < 0.01, and p < 0.05, respectively). By contrast, antibody levels against HSP7 were significantly higher in HC compared to T1R, T2R and NR (p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001, and p < 0.0001, respectively) (Tables S1 and S2; Fig. S1).

Similarly, Antibody levels against keratin BCEs KER1, KER2, and KER4 were significantly higher in T1R compared to T2R (p < 0.001, p < 0.01, p < 0.0001, respectively), NR (p < 0.01, p < 0.05, p < 0.0001, respectively), and HC (p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001, respectively). In T2R, antibodies to KER2 and KER4 were significantly elevated compared to HC (p < 0.01, p < 0.001, respectively), and in NR, levels were also higher compared to HC (p < 0.001, p < 0.05, respectively). No significant differences were observed for KER3 across groups (Tables S3 and S4; Fig. S2). Among MBP BCEs, antibodies against MBP50SB1 and MBPLMB2 were significantly higher in T1R compared to HC (p < 0.05, p < 0.0001, respectively), and against MBPLMB2 in T2R compared to HC (p < 0.0001). In NR, antibodies against MBP50SB2, MBPLMB1, and MBPLMB2 were significantly elevated compared to HC (p < 0.05, p < 0.05, p < 0.0001, respectively) (Tables S5 and S6; Fig. S3). Discussion

Antigenic mimicry is considered as a mechanism by which M. leprae evades host immune surveillance, contributing to its prolonged incubation and the immunologically silent phase of infection, particularly in lepromatous leprosy (LL), where bacterial load is high and the protective immunity is very low. The structural similarity between M. leprae antigens and host proteins may allow the pathogen to persist undetected by the immune system, as it is not recognized as foreign9. However, this mimicry can paradoxically initiate autoimmune responses when host antigens resembling M. leprae are mistakenly targeted and recognized as foreign. Cross-reactivity between M. leprae-specific monoclonal antibodies and human nerve and skin cells supports this antigenic overlap10,11. Proteins such as epidermal cytokeratin 1/2 and HSP60 have been implicated in cross-reactivity12, while autoantibodies to keratin have been associated with T1R13,14. Autoimmune responses against nerve-specific antigens may further drive demyelination and nerve damage through homotypic myelin interactions15,16, underscoring the dual role of mimicry in both immune evasion and autoimmunity.

Associations between infections and autoimmunity are supported by epidemiological, clinical, and experimental findings showing cross-reactivity between host antigens and microbial determinants17–19. The susceptibility to leprosy depends upon a complex interplay of environmental variables, genetic predispositions, and immunological factors2. The presence of antigenic mimicry between the etiological agent M. leprae and the host may initiate an autoimmune response wherein the hosts’ own antigens are mistakenly targeted as non-self or foreign10,11. Pathak et al. (2022) further identified antigenic cross-reactivity involving proteins such as the 41.4 kDa protein, alpha-1 antitrypsin, vimentin, and keratin 1, with 22 B-cell epitopes exhibiting molecular mimicry with M. leprae20. Furthermore, it has been speculated that mycobacterial infection-induced suppressor (MIS) macrophages play a pathogenic role in changing the fate of macrophage polarization toward autoimmunity due to antigens specific to M. leprae.21.

Elevated autoantibodies are a hallmark of autoimmunity and have been reported in chronic infections, including LL, with antibodies such as rheumatoid factor, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic autoantibodies (ANCA), anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-phospholipid antibodies, and cryoglobulins22–26. Additional autoantibodies in leprosy include anti-mitochondrial, anti-actin, and anti-myosin27,28. Anti-endothelial cell antibodies have also been detected, potentially contributing to pathogenesis via endothelial apoptosis, reacting with calreticulin, vimentin, tubulin, and HSP7029. HSPs, conserved proteins expressed during inflammation and stress, elicit humoral and cellular responses30 and may provoke autoimmunity in chronic mycobacterial infections. Specific host proteins, such as epidermal cytokeratin 1/2 and HSP60, have been implicated in this cross-reactivity12. Later, Singh et al. (2012) reported molecular mimicry between cytokeratin 10 and HSP65 of M. leprae31.

In our previous study, we predicted eleven B cell mimicking epitopes (BCEs) between M. leprae HSP65 and host keratin and evaluated humoral immune responses to identify antigenic determinants underlying autoimmunity in T1R and T2R leprosy patients. Seven BCEs of HSP65 (HSP1–HSP7) sharing similarity with host keratin and four BCEs of keratin (KER1–KER4) sharing similarity with HSP65 were synthesized. Antibody levels against HSP2 and HSP3 were significantly elevated in NR patients, followed by T1R, T2R, and HC, indicating heightened immunoreactivity in the absence of acute inflammation (Tables S1, S2; Fig. S1). In contrast, antibodies against HSP4, HSP5, and HSP6 were significantly higher in T1R compared to T2R, NR, and HC, suggesting their specific involvement in T1R immunopathogenesis. Elevated antibody responses to these HSPs may reflect their role in driving inflammatory processes during T1R. Additionally, antibodies against HSP1 were significantly higher in T1R, T2R, and NR compared to HC (p < 0.0001), highlighting its potential as a biomarker for leprosy.

In the present study, differential levels of mimicking BCEs were longitudinally validated in separate cohorts of T1R and NR patients. In the T1R cohort (n = 20), antibody levels against HSP2 (p = 0.0169, R2 = 0.2651), HSP4 (p < 0.0001, R2 = 0.7233), HSP5 (p < 0.0001, R2 = 0.7481), and HSP6 (p = 0.0023, R2 = 0.3938) were significantly higher during active T1R compared to levels after subsidence. Notably, HSP4 and HSP5 showed a strong association with T1R, whereas HSP2 and HSP6 displayed weaker associations (Table 3; Fig. 2). In NR patients who later developed T1R, antibodies against HSP4 (p = 0.0025, R2 = 0.5406), HSP5 (p = 0.0019, R2 = 0.5835), and HSP6 (p = 0.0111, R2 = 0.419) were significantly elevated during active T1R compared to levels at pre-T1R baseline and after subsidence (Table 4; Fig. 3).

Our findings suggest that the differential level of antibodies against BCEs, HSP4, HSP5, and HSP6 among the T1R groups could be utilized as a predictive biomarker for the development of T1R in leprosy. In general, these findings enhance our comprehension of the complex immunological responses in leprosy and may have implications for the development of diagnostic assays and targeted therapeutic interventions based on the specific immune profiles associated with different clinical manifestations.

Elevated antibodies targeting the self-protein keratin may signify autoimmune processes in individuals with leprosy. This is particularly relevant since most leprosy lesions appear on the skin, and keratosis is a prevalent occurrence, especially during T1R of tuberculoid leprosy13,14. The M. leprae antigens which mimic host antigens may induce an autoimmune reaction against the hosts own antigens, which could explain the immune reaction in tuberculoid leprosy and during a “reversal reaction,” when host tissue is lacking M. leprae, whereas extensive granuloma formation can be seen10. Singh et al. (2012) have reported that a high level of anti-keratin antibodies (AkAbs) was present in patients suffering from leprosy when the levels were compared to HC and the highest level of AkAbs was present in T1R across the RJ spectrum, including Erythema Nodosum Leprosum (ENL) It was intriguing to discover that the levels of AkAbs also demonstrated a clinical correlation in the context of the number of lesions.

Further, in our previous study, the level of antibodies against three BCEs of keratin i.e., KER1, KER2, and KER4, was found significantly higher in T1R when compared to T2R, NR and HC (Tables S3, S4; Fig. S2). Moreover, in T2R, the antibody levels against KER2 and KER4 were significantly higher compared to HC, and in NR, the levels were also significantly elevated compared to HC. These findings suggest a distinctive immunological response associated with T1R, characterized by heightened antibody reactivity against specific keratin epitopes. The observed elevation in antibody levels against KER2 and KER4 in T2R and NR groups compared to HC implies potential immunological involvement in these clinical conditions.

Moreover, in present study, we observed significantly higher levels of antibodies against KER1 (p = 0.0007, R2 = 0.4633), KER2 (p = 0.0002, R2 = 0.5296), and KER4 (p < 0.0001, R2 = 0.5932) during active T1R when compared to the levels post-T1R in a cohort of T1R patients after subsidence of reaction (Table 3; Fig. 2). Thus, the BCEs, KER1, KER2, and KER4 showed a moderate association with T1R. The association of the mentioned BCEs with the T1R patients in a longitudinal manner strongly supports the utility of these peptides as prognostic markers for T1R in leprosy patients. In addition, we observed significant association of KER1 (p = 0.0209, R2 = 0.3742), KER2 (p = 0.0032, R2 = 0.5379), and KER4 (p = 0.0038, R2 = 0.5177) with T1R when the levels of autoantibody were estimated during active T1R, when compared to levels at pre-T1R baseline and post-T1R in a cohort of NR patients who developed T1R after recruitment (Table 4; Fig. 3).

Thus, our finding of a significant association of three BCEs of keratin, KER1, KER2, and KER4 with T1R and KER2 and KER4 with T2R, suggests the future utilization of these mimicking epitopes as a predictive biomarker for reactions (T1R and T2R) in leprosy. It has been noted that the T1R in BT leprosy patients often occur suddenly during treatment or after the completion of therapy. We still lack data on the exact mechanism of pathogenesis of T1R. Doubts have been raised on the role of mimicking proteins/peptides in the initiation of the reaction. It has been shown earlier that a high level of antibodies against keratin is associated with T1R patients31. Our findings speculate that molecular mimicry between cytokeratin 10 and HSP65 epitopes could potentially initiate acute inflammation in skin lesions. The development of scaling in skin lesions observed during T1R and T2R may be attributed, at least in part, to autoimmune responses directed against molecularly mimicking peptides.

The survival of M. leprae and its evasion of the host immune response might be facilitated by similarities between stress proteins in the host and M. leprae antigens. Research revealed differing levels of antibodies against host cytoskeletal and stress proteins in multibacillary (MB) and paucibacillary (PB) cases of leprosy, notably higher levels in LL, particularly in relapsed patients. This suggests that conserved proteins in the relapse group could potentially trigger reactions with M. leprae antigens or stress proteins through molecular mimicry, possibly impacting neurodegeneration via processes like receptor binding and cell signalling. These findings strongly suggest that nerve demyelination during the disease progression might be initiated significantly by antigenic mimicry28.

The differential humoral responses observed against HSP65 and keratin mimicking BCEs provide important insight into the immunopathological mechanisms underlying reactional states in leprosy, particularly T1R. The elevated antibody responses to HSP4, HSP5, HSP6, KER1, KER2, and KER4 during active T1R, along with their strong longitudinal association, suggest that these epitopes may not merely serve as biomarkers but could actively participate in pathogenic immune processes through molecular mimicry triggered autoimmunity.

HSPs are evolutionarily conserved and highly immunogenic, which allows them to both initiate immune responses and act as targets in autoimmune reactions32. Microbial HSPs can stimulate cellular or humoral immunity that may cross-react with self-HSPs or other self-antigens, contributing to autoimmunity through mechanisms such as molecular mimicry and exposure of previously hidden epitopes. Notably, foreign and self mimicry is not always harmful and can also exert immunoregulatory effects33,34. Both pathogenic and regulatory T and B cell epitopes may coexist within a single HSP, such as HSP60/65. During autoimmune progression, epitope spreading can generate new immune responses that may either exacerbate or suppress disease, depending on the epitopes involved.

HSP-driven immune responses have been implicated in various autoimmune and metabolic disorders, including atherosclerosis, uveitis, lupus, and Behçet’s disease35. A perspective has been elaborated on how M. paratuberculosis HSP65 may contribute to the induction of autoimmunity through molecular mimicry36. Additionally, HSPs play essential roles in both innate and adaptive immunity by activating toll-like receptors and modulating antigen-presenting cells, thereby influencing T- and B-cell responses. Cross-reactive antibodies against HSP65 and keratin derived BCEs may bind to homologous epitopes, and subsequent elevated antibody responses by the host during T1R against the mimicking epitopes of M. leprae strongly suggest that antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) or complement-mediated lysis may contribute to tissue damage in T1R in leprosy.

Furthermore, the strong association of HSP4 and HSP5 with active T1R suggests that these epitopes may preferentially drive pro-inflammatory immune polarization. HSPs are known to act as danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), capable of engaging pattern recognition receptors such as TLR2 and TLR4 on antigen-presenting cells. This interaction can enhance antigen presentation and promote the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-1β, all of which are elevated during T1R37,38. The presence of cross-reactive antibodies may further amplify this response by enhancing antigen presentation and epitope spreading.

Keratinocyte involvement appears to play a significant role in the cutaneous immunopathology of leprosy, particularly during T1R. Elevated antibody responses against the self-antigen keratin suggest the presence of autoimmune mechanisms, which is especially relevant given that leprosy lesions predominantly manifest in the skin. The identified BCEs, sharing homology with keratin, may facilitate cross-reactive antibody binding to keratinocytes, leading to disruption of epidermal integrity and initiation of local inflammatory cascades. Keratinocytes, as active immunological participants, can respond to such stimuli by producing cytokines, chemokines, and antimicrobial peptides, thereby amplifying inflammation. This autoimmune targeting of keratin may contribute to the characteristic skin manifestations of T1R, including erythema and edema. Furthermore, the frequent occurrence of keratinization abnormalities such as keratosis in tuberculoid leprosy during T1R supports the role of keratin directed immune responses in disease pathogenesis13,14,39. The observation that antibodies against HSP2 and HSP3 are elevated in NR patients, but less strongly associated with T1R, suggests a distinction between baseline immunoreactivity and pathogenic autoimmunity. Importantly, the longitudinal elevation of antibodies against HSP4, HSP5, HSP6, KER1, KER2, and KER4 during T1R episodes supports their potential role in predictive immunopathology. Their rise during clinical onset suggests that subclinical immune activation and autoantibody generation precede overt inflammation, aligning with the concept of a “pre-reactional” immune state. Monitoring these antibody levels could therefore enable early identification of patients at risk of developing T1R, allowing timely therapeutic intervention to prevent nerve damage.

Overall, these findings support a model in which molecular mimicry between M. leprae HSP65 and host keratin drives autoantibody production, which in turn contributes to T1R pathogenesis through multiple interconnected mechanisms, including Schwann cell injury, keratinocyte targeting, and amplification of pro-inflammatory signalling pathways. This integrated framework not only enhances our understanding of leprosy immunopathogenesis but also highlights potential targets for diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Earlier, in LL patients, MBP has been identified as an antigen within circulating immune complexes (CIC), suggesting its release from M. leprae-induced nerve damage elicits anti-MBP antibodies that cross-react with peripheral nerve MBP, contributing to demyelination and neurodegeneration15,40,41. Such responses reflect profound humoral alterations and autoimmune reactions against nerve antigens, ultimately driving peripheral nerve pathology16. Furthermore, Singh et al. documented elevated levels of autoantibodies against MBP in individuals with leprosy across various stages. We found a positive correlation between the number of nerves involved and the level of antibodies against MBP in leprosy patients and noted that cross-reactivity in BCEs of host and M. leprae proteins, in silico42. While earlier studies were limited to bioinformatics predictions, the present work combines in silico analysis with wet-lab validation.

In our previous investigation, among the four predicted BCEs between the 50 S ribosomal protein as well as lysyl tRNA synthetase of M. leprae and MBP, we observed notable differences in antibody levels against BCEs of MBP across different clinical subject groups. Specifically, the levels of antibodies directed against MBP50SB1 and MBPLMB2 were significantly elevated in T1R compared to HC. Additionally, in T2R, antibodies against MBPLMB2 showed significantly higher levels when compared to HC (p < 0.0001). Conversely, the NR group exhibited significantly higher antibody levels against MBP50SB2, MBPLMB1, and MBPLMB2 compared to HC. A significantly lower level of antibody to MBP50SB2 in T1R was observed when compared to NR (p < 0.05) (Table S5, S6; Figure S3). Moreover, in present study, we observed significantly higher levels of antibodies against MBP50SB1 (p = 0.0012, R2 = 0.4325) and MBPLMB2 (p = 0.0144, R2 = 0.2764) at active T1R baseline when compared to the levels post-T1R in a cohort of T1R patients after subsidence of reaction (Table 3; Fig. 2), which further established a strong and moderate association with T1R, respectively. In addition, we observed a significant association of MBP50SB1 (p = 0.0037, R2 = 0.4638) with T1R when the levels of autoantibody were estimated during active T1R when compared to levels at pre-T1R baseline and post-T1R in a cohort of NR patients who developed T1R after recruitment (Table 4; Fig. 3). In the present work, wet-lab validation reveals that antibody levels against MBP50SB1 and MBPLMB2 are significantly higher in T1R patients at active reaction onset compared with post-reaction and pre-reaction baselines, and that MBP50SB1 and MBPLMB2 associate robustly with T1R status (R2 ≈ 0.4–0.5), indicating that these epitope-specific responses track with acute inflammatory episodes. This association supports the hypothesis that epitope driven autoimmune responses contribute to the episodic surges of nerve inflammation and demyelination seen in T1R, during which complement activation, Schwann-cell stress, and autoreactive antibodies converge to accelerate myelin sheath breakdown and irreversible axonal loss. M. leprae has a well-documented tropism for Schwann cells, achieved in part through binding of the bacillary surface glycolipid PGL-1 to laminin-2 and direct interaction with the peripheral nerve specific myelin protein P016, which facilitates bacterial adhesion and invasion of Schwann cells. Once internalized, M. leprae induces sustained Schwann-cell proliferation and aberrant signalling, which impairs myelin homeostasis and promotes distal axonal loss over time. The current data showing elevated antibodies against MBP BCEs (particularly MBP50SB1 and MBPLMB2) in T1R and T2R suggest that cross-reactive humoral responses may further destabilize Schwann cell mediated myelination, either by disrupting Schwann cell membrane integrity or by interfering with key myelin-associated molecules such as protein P0 and MBP, ultimately exacerbating demyelination and slowing regeneration. This is particularly relevant in T1R, where rapid immune activation occurs and may involve autoantibody deposition and complement activation via ADCC, as previous studies have shown that immune-mediated damage to Schwann cells encompasses both cellular and humoral mechanisms43,44.

Our findings suggest, for the first time, that these mimicking BCEs may contribute to pathological deterioration of the myelin sheath, resulting in nerve damage and subsequent deformities or permanent loss of nerve function in leprosy.

An early diagnostic assay for T1R in leprosy patients has long been desired, which could benefit the patients by critical observation and medication. Here in the present study, based on our findings of levels of autoantibody against mimicking BCEs, we standardized and performed a dot blot assay by utilizing the BCEs, which was found to be significantly associated with T1R as well as the T2R group of leprosy patients. On the analysis of blots performed across T1R, T2R, NR and HC, we observed a significantly higher mean volume intensities of blot against BCEs, HSP4, HSP5, HSP6, KER1, KER2, KER4, MBP50SB1 and MBPLMB2 in T1R compared with NR (p < 0.012, p < 0.030, p < 0.011, p < 0.001, p < 0.0004, p < 0.008, p < 0.014, and p < 0.005, respectively) and HC (p < 0.00049, p < 0.00054, p < 0.00017, p < 0.00005, p < 0.00001, p < 0.00011, p < 0.00027, and p < 0.00012, respectively). We also observed significantly higher mean volume intensities of blot against BCEs, HSP4, HSP5, HSP6, KER1, KER2, KER4, MBP50SB1 and MBPLMB2 in T2R (p < 0.0025, p < 0.0045, p < 0.0141, p < 0.0026, p < 0.0009, p < 0.0009, p < 0.0063, and p < 0.0162, respectively) and HC (p < 0.00049, p < 0.00054, p < 0.00017, p < 0.00005, p < 0.00001, p < 0.00011, p < 0.00027, and p < 0.00012, respectively) and in NR (p < 0.004), when compared with HC (Tables 5 and 6; Fig. 6). The findings were in concordance with the results of ELISA against the BCEs. Furthermore, a cut-off value for each BCE was deduced based on the mean volume intensities of the NR We observed higher mean volume intensities in the T1R group for the BCEs when compared to the cut-off values for HSP4 (25122.20), HSP6 (25700.59), KER1 (22941.29), KER2 (25230.58), KER4 (29724.92), MBP50SB1 (29363.17) and MBPLMB2 (26880.65) (Table 7).

Recently, Yang et al. (2023) reported persistently elevated serum autoantibodies such as IgG anticardiolipin antibody (ACA), ANA, extractable nuclear antigen antibody (ENA), anti-streptolysin O (ASO), and rheumatoid factor (RF) in cured leprosy patients compared to controls, independent of recovery duration, with strong positive correlation to Laccase (multicopper oxidoreductase) domain-containing 1 (LACC1)45, a gene implicated in autoimmune diseases46. These findings highlight sustained autoimmune activation long after treatment completion and are consistent with our observations of elevated autoantibodies against mimicking epitopes, reinforcing the role of molecular mimicry in leprosy-associated autoimmunity.

We would like to clarify that our current data primarily quantify BCE specific antibody responses and therefore provide direct evidence for the humoral component of the proposed pathogenic cascade, rather than independently establishing a complete T cell mediated mechanism. Nonetheless, we do not interpret BCEs as passive bystanders, instead, we propose that they function as candidate upstream inducers capable of initiating immune recognition and breaking immune tolerance, potentially through molecular mimicry. Our previous findings show that hyperimmunization with M. leprae sonicate antigens (MLSA) and adoptive transfer of immune cells induce autoimmune responses in naïve BALB/c mice. Elevated anti-MBP autoantibodies, especially in T cell recipients, underscore the key role of T cells, indicating that M. leprae antigens disrupt immune homeostasis and promote autoreactivity mediated nerve damage42. In this framework, BCEs may contribute to the activation of downstream effector pathways, including antigen-specific T-cell responses, innate immune activation, and antibody-mediated mechanisms of tissue injury, thereby linking the observed humoral responses to broader immunopathological processes.

Recent research underscores that B cell mimicking epitopes play a dual role in infectious diseases, contributing to both protective immunity and immunopathology47. Advances in immunoinformatics have revealed that pathogen-derived epitopes resembling host proteins can trigger cross-reactive antibody responses, aiding immune evasion but also increasing the risk of autoimmunity, as seen in infections like SARS-CoV-248. While such epitopes can induce strong immune memory and cross-neutralizing responses beneficial for vaccine design, careful selection is essential to avoid targeting autoreactive regions49. B cell epitopes represent critical antigenic determinants that directly interact with antibodies, making them highly valuable targets in the serological diagnosis of diverse infectious diseases. In bacterial infections such as tuberculosis, epitopes derived from key antigens like ESAT-6 and CFP10 of Mycobacterium tuberculosis have been widely explored for improving diagnostic accuracy50–52, while in Staphylococcus aureus, epitopes of enterotoxins and peptidoglycan-associated peptides have enabled sensitive immunoassays53,54. Similarly, in leptospirosis, outer membrane proteins such as LipL32 and LigA serve as major epitope sources for early detection55,56. In viral diseases, B cell epitopes have shown remarkable utility; for example, spike (S) and nucleocapsid (N) proteins of SARS-CoV-257,58, envelope (E) and NS1 proteins of dengue virus59, latent membrane protein 2 (LMP2) of Epstein–Barr virus60, and core and envelope proteins of hepatitis viruses are targets for epitope-based serodiagnostics61. Likewise, in parasitic infections such as toxoplasmosis and leishmaniasis, epitopes from antigens like SAG1 of Toxoplasma gondii and K39 or other recombinant proteins of Leishmania species have demonstrated promising diagnostic performance62. The targeted use of these pathogen-specific molecules enhances the sensitivity and specificity of serological assays, reduces cross-reactivity, and enables more accurate identification of infections across a broad spectrum of diseases.

In the context of leprosy, these concepts are highly relevant, as M. leprae exhibits antigenic mimicry with host proteins such as MBP and keratin. Such mimicking BCEs can drive the production of cross-reactive antibodies that contribute to nerve damage and cutaneous pathology, particularly during reactional states like T1R. The presence of autoantibodies against neural antigens and their association with disease severity suggests that molecular mimicry plays a key role in Schwann cell injury, demyelination, and immune complex mediated inflammation. At the same time, recent advances in epitope based diagnostics highlight the translational potential of these immunological insights. Recent studies highlight the growing promise of epitope-based strategies in leprosy diagnostics, demonstrating that HTM-based biosensors incorporating multiepitope proteins can serve as sensitive and cost-effective tools for detecting M. leprae exposure, particularly among asymptomatic contacts63. Complementing this, recombinant multiepitope chimeras derived from M. leprae B cell epitopes have shown high diagnostic accuracy for both PB and MB cases while maintaining specificity against other infectious diseases64.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates a significant association of BCEs (HSP4, HSP5, HSP6, KER1, KER2, KER4, MBP50SB1, and MBPLMB2) with T1R in leprosy patients compared to NR patients and HC. These BCEs exhibited strong to moderate correlations with disease progression, as determined by longitudinal autoantibody levels. The persistence of autoimmune responses suggests a contributory role in leprosy pathogenesis, highlighting the potential of these BCEs as prognostic biomarkers. Furthermore, the standardized dot blot assay may be adapted for integration into cost-effective lateral flow assay platforms, alongside other established biomarkers associated with T1R, for evaluation in larger cohorts in future studies. Such implementation holds potential as a prognostic tool for identifying leprosy patients at increased risk of developing reactions, particularly T1R. Together, these findings underscore the potential of epitope driven antigen design and innovative platforms to enhance early detection, improve surveillance, and support more effective leprosy control strategies, especially in endemic settings. In conclusion, the integration of recent advances in epitope mapping with disease-specific insights, particularly in leprosy reactions such as T1R, highlights the dual role of B cell epitopes as key mediators of protective immunity and contributors to autoimmune pathology, thereby establishing them as critical targets for improved diagnostics and the rational design of safe vaccines.

Data limitations and perspectives

Although we previously demonstrated autoantibody responses against BCEs across the leprosy spectrum using a sufficiently powered sample size, the present study has limitations, particularly the relatively small sample size in the longitudinal analysis. This constraint largely reflects the practical difficulties of long-term patient follow-up and inconsistent sample availability over time. The use of pooled sera in the dot blot assay also represents a limitation, as it does not capture inter-individual variability and may constrain the strength of diagnostic or predictive interpretations. Despite this, confidence in the findings is supported by the consistent reproducibility of the data, achieved through repeated independent assays and the analysis of samples in triplicate, which helped ensure methodological reliability. Moving forward, studies involving larger longitudinal cohorts and longer follow-up periods will be important to confirm these observations and to better characterize changes in autoantibody levels over the course of disease and treatment. Incorporating functional assays and correlating autoantibody responses with clinical outcomes, reactional episodes, and immunological parameters may further clarify their biological relevance. Such approaches could provide deeper insight into the role of molecular mimicry in leprosy and its contribution to disease variability. In addition, the standardized dot blot assay could be adapted for use in cost-effective lateral flow platforms, alongside other established T1R-associated biomarkers, and evaluated in larger patient cohorts in future studies. Such an approach may offer prognostic value for identifying leprosy patients at higher risk of developing reactional episodes, particularly T1R.

Materials and methods

Recruitment of subjects

This work was carried out with approval by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) of The Leprosy Mission Trust India, New Delhi (24 June 2013). Blood samples were obtained from leprosy patients and HC after obtaining written informed consent at the outpatient department at The Leprosy Mission Community Hospital, Delhi. Sample collection protocols and all methods in the study were performed with strict adherence to the guidelines and regulations of IEC, The Leprosy Mission Trust India, New Delhi and the Indian Council of Medical Research, New Delhi. All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Sample size, inclusion, and exclusion criteria

The calculation of the total sample/recruitment size/number for this study was performed using the Yamane method, specifically applying the formula n = N/(1 + Ne^2), where n represents the sample size, N denotes the population size, and ‘e’ represents the level of precision. In the present study, the level of precision was set at 0.05 for a 95% confidence interval. The value of N was obtained from the annual new case detection database for leprosy patients in reactions at the study centre. The study design incorporated numerous dependent (titre of auto-antibodies) and independent variables (clinical group, treatment status, reactional states, bacillary index, age, sex, etc.), resulting in a strong statistical power. In this study, we registered individuals diagnosed with leprosy between 18 and 60 years of age. Moreover, leprosy patients who had co-infections with other diseases and pregnant women were also excluded from the present study.

A total of 150 clinically confirmed, untreated leprosy patients were enrolled in the study based on cardinal clinical signs and bacteriological examination. This cohort included 50 patients diagnosed with T1R, 50 with T2R, and 50 NR. The cohort was monitored longitudinally at intervals of 3–4 months to observe the onset or resolution of reactions and assess changes in antibody responses. Among the 50 NR patients, blood samples were collected from 12 individuals when reactions developed (T1R, n = 10; T2R, n = 2), and from 10 individuals with T1R after the reaction had resolved. Additionally, follow-up blood samples were obtained from 20 patients in the initial T1R group after clinical resolution of the reaction. Due to the lack of a comparable number of T2R cases caused by participants’ dropout, the T2R group was excluded from the longitudinal analysis. None of the reaction patients were receiving corticosteroid therapy at the time of sample collection. In longitudinal analysis, except for samples collected during the active T1R stage, all samples were obtained at the onset of clinical symptoms prior to corticosteroid therapy and again after completion of treatment. Sample collection

Blood samples (≈ 5 ml each) were aseptically collected from all the subjects by antecubital venipuncture in a plain vial. Sera were separated and stored at − 20 °C till further use.

ELISA

The ELISA using BCEs was performed as described previously8. Briefly, peptides (2.5 µg/ml) were coated onto flat-bottom MaxiSorp microtitre plates (Nunc, Denmark) in carbonate–bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6) and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Following washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), nonspecific sites were blocked with 2% BSA. Serum samples diluted 1:1600 in PBS-Tween were added in duplicate and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. After washing, wells were treated with anti-human IgG peroxidase conjugate (Cat. No A8792, Sigma Aldrich, USA) (Dilution factor, 1:10,000), followed by substrate solution containing O-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride and hydrogen peroxide. Thereafter, the reactions were stopped with a solution of 2 N H2SO4, and optical densities (OD) were determined at 492 nm by using the MULTISKAN FC ELISA Reader (Thermo Scientific, USA) with the software SkanIt RE 7.0. Ink.

Dot blot assay

The dot blot assay was performed using 0.45 μm nitrocellulose membranes (NCM) (Cat. No. 1620115; Bio-Rad, USA) placed in a staining tray. BCEs (5 µl per spot) at a concentration of 2.5 µg/ml were applied to the membrane at equidistant positions, with PBS (pH 7.4) serving as a negative control. The membranes were incubated at room temperature for 2 h to allow antigen binding and drying, followed by three washes with 5 ml PBS each. Non-specific binding sites were blocked using 5 ml of 3% BSA in PBS for 2 h at room temperature. After removing the blocking solution, membranes were washed three times with PBS and incubated with 5 ml of pooled serum samples (n = 10), diluted 1:1600 in 1% BSA in PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBST). Serum from different subject groups (NR, T1R, and T2R) was used accordingly. After a 2-h incubation at room temperature, the membranes were washed three times with PBST and then incubated with 5 ml of anti-human IgG peroxidase conjugate (Cat. No A8792, Sigma Aldrich, USA), diluted 1:20,000 in 1% BSA in PBST, for 1 h at room temperature. Following three additional PBST washes, a substrate solution containing 0.05% 3,3′-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride hydrate (DAB; Cat. No D5637, Sigma Aldrich, USA) and 0.001% H2O2 in PBS was added (5 ml) and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 20 min. The reaction was terminated by rinsing the membranes with distilled water, and the membranes were air-dried for 2 h. Final blot images were captured using the ChemiDoc XRS+ imaging system (BioRad, USA), and colorimetric volume intensities were quantified using Image Lab Software version 6.1.0.07. The assay was performed with the pooled serum of each subject group i.e., T1R, T2R, NR and HC. Three spots on each blot were taken to calculate the mean background intensity. The blots were analyzed by applying the global background subtraction method and the linear regression method for quantitative analysis across each subject group.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed by using GraphPad Prism software version 5.0 (GraphPad Prism, La Jolla, CA). Statistical analyses were performed using appropriate methods based on data distribution. Differences in optical density (OD) values between HC and leprosy patient groups for each BCE were assessed using One-way ANOVA with the non-parametric Kruskal Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc multiple-comparison test. Heatmap visualization was generated using SRplot software65. For the T1R cohort, autoantibody levels measured by ELISA were analyzed using a two-tailed paired t-test, whereas for the NR cohort, autoantibody levels were evaluated using RM One-way ANOVA and post hoc Holm-Sidak’s multiple comparisons test. The volume intensities of the dot blot assay were analyzed by t-tests applying the Holm–Sidak method. Furthermore, the cut-off value for each BCE was deduced by the mean+2SD of the NR mean volume intensities.
