hucMSCs alleviate LPS-induced endothelial glycocalyx damage by secreting Cxcl12 to activate CXCR4
Authors: Jinfeng Cui, Zhenyi Peng, Yuanyuan Chen, Wei Liu, Qiuwen Chen, Xiaozhi Wang, Tao Wang, Xiao Huang, Ting Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) has a high clinical mortality rate and continues to draw research attention regarding its mechanisms and potential treatments. Disruption of the endothelial barrier is a primary pathological feature, and glycocalyx degradation is a key factor contributing to this disruption. Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hucMSCs) exhibit strong anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects, making their application in ARDS treatment an area of increasing interest. Proteomic screening identified Cxcl12 as a protein secreted by hucMSCs. In male C57 mice and cell models, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was used to induce injury, followed by interventions with hucMSCs or hucMSCs with silenced Cxcl12 to assess glycocalyx-related proteins SDC-1, HS, and the repair marker EXT-1. To evaluate downstream signaling, the CXCR4 receptor was inhibited and related indicators were examined. Silencing Cxcl12 reduced the therapeutic effect of hucMSCs on LPS-induced glycocalyx damage. Inhibition of CXCR4 also weakened the effect of Cxcl12. These findings indicate that hucMSCs alleviate LPS-induced glycocalyx damage in pulmonary vascular endothelial cells by secreting Cxcl12, which activates the downstream receptor CXCR4, providing a therapeutic effect for ARDS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-05024-2.

Introduction

Sepsis is an inflammatory disease triggered by acute infection and can lead to multiple organ dysfunction and life-threatening conditions. It is a common cause of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). The primary pathological mechanism of ARDS is the disruption of the vascular endothelial barrier, which increases lung tissue permeability and leads to pulmonary edema [1–3]. Clinical treatment relies mainly on mechanical ventilation support; however, this approach requires advanced medical resources and is associated with complications, such as respiratory tract infections [4–6]. Consequently, investigating the pathogenesis of sepsis-induced ARDS and identifying appropriate treatment strategies have emerged as critical issues in contemporary clinical and basic research. Establishing an animal model of acute lung injury (ALI) is a commonly utilized approach to study this condition [7, 8].

The glycocalyx is essential for maintaining the endothelial barrier and enables material exchange at the endothelial surface [3, 9]. Damage to the structure and function of the endothelial glycocalyx is a key factor contributing to the heightened permeability of pulmonary capillary endothelium [10]. The glycocalyx consists mainly of negatively charged proteins and polysaccharide complexes; its key components include the core protein syndecan-1 (SDC-1) and its heparan sulfate (HS) side chain [11]. Because endothelial cells experience continuous shear stress, enzymes such as heparinase (HPA) and hyaluronidase (HAase) maintain the glycocalyx in a dynamic balance between degradation and repair [12, 13]. Exostosin-1 (EXT-1), a key enzyme in the synthesis of HS, plays an essential role in the glycocalyx repair process [14].

Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hucMSCs), a type of pluripotent stem cell, have attracted considerable interest for their therapeutic potential [15, 16]. Their wide availability, ease of expansion, low immunogenicity, and strong tissue repair capacity contribute to their suitability for translational research [17, 18]. hucMSCs demonstrate self-renewal and multidirectional differentiation, and they release cytokines, growth factors, and exosomes through paracrine activity [19, 20]. These factors play a crucial role in tissue repair, immune regulation, and anti-inflammatory responses [21, 22]. Studies have demonstrated that hucMSCs and their derived exosomes (hucMSC-Exos) exhibit remarkable therapeutic effects in various disease models, including pulmonary fibrosis, acute kidney injury, myocardial infarction, and skin injury [23–26]. Furthermore, hucMSCs can migrate to the site of injury, positioning them as an ideal vehicle for precision therapy [16].

Interest in the CXC chemokine family in ARDS has increased, particularly regarding the dual roles of Cxcl12 (also known as stromal cell-derived factor-1, SDF-1) in inflammation and repair. Cxcl12 binds to CXCR4 to mediate inflammatory cell chemotaxis and plays a key role in tissue repair and regeneration [27–29]. Its effects on endothelial cells and the activation of downstream signaling pathways support endothelial cell survival and function, contributing to restoration of the endothelial barrier.

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of hucMSCs in ARDS, we established an ALI mouse model by intraperitoneal injection of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) followed by tail vein administration of hucMSCs. Proteomic analysis of lung tissue after hucMSC intervention identified Cxcl12 as a differentially expressed protein, consistent with previous findings [7]. By generating hucMSCs with silenced Cxcl12 through lentiviral transfection, we evalted the role of Cxcl12 in the treatment of ARDS with hucMSCs in both in vivo and in vitro experiments. Furthermore, we investigated whether Cxcl12 ameliorates LPS-induced endothelial glycocalyx damage and promotes glycocalyx repair through its downstream receptor CXCR4.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

LPS (L2880, Escherichia coli O55: B5, Sigma), anti-Cxcl12 antibody (17402-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-GAPDH (A00227-1, Bosterbio), Mouse Cxcl12 ELISA Kit (ab100741, Abcam), anti-heparan sulfate antibody (MAB2040, Merk) for Western blot, anti-heparan sulfate antibody (370255-1, amsbio) for immunofluorescence, Mouse heparan sulfate ELISA Kit (369957, USBiological), anti-SDC-1 antibody (BM5060, Bosterbio), Mouse SDC-1 ELISA Kit (860.090.096, Diaclone), anti-EXT-1 antibody (H00002131-D01P, Abnova), anti-CXCR4 antibody (ab181020, Abcam), AMD3100 (HY-10046, MCE), Human Cxcl12 recombinant protein (HY-P73661, MCE), BCA Protein Assay Kit (PC0020, Solarbio), FITC-albumin (A9771, Sigma), Tribromethanol (Sigma, 148402-5G), Lentiviral vector (with the Luc tag) ( Geely Gene), D-fluorescein potassium salt (ST-196, Beyotime Biotechnology).

Experimental animals

C57BL/6 mice (18–23 g; 8–10 weeks old) were obtained from Jinan Pengyue Experimental Animal Breeding Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). Mice were maintained in a controlled environment witd unrestricted access to food and water. With a temperature range of 22–25 °C and 40–50% relative humidity. All experimental procedures followed ARRIVE guidelines and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Binzhou Medical College (Ethics No. 20231208-16). Mice were anesthetized with tribromoethanol and euthanized by cervical dislocation during tissue collection.

In vivo construction of a mouse model of ALI

Mice were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 6 per group): Control, LPS, LPS+hucMSCs, LPS+shCxcl12 hucMSCs, LPS + NC hucMSCs, hucMSCs. Each mouse received 100 µL of LPS solution intraperitoneally at 20 mg/kg [3]. Two hours later, 100 µL of hucMSCs (2 × 10⁵ cells/mouse) was administered via tail vein injection, with random selection of animals during handling to reduce bias. At 8 h after LPS injection, the mice were anesthetized with tribromoethanol, and lung tissue and serum were collected and stored at − 80 °C.

Histopathology

After anesthesia, pulmonary circulation was perfused, and lung tissues were removed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for at least 48 h. Samples were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5-µm sections (Novland, Shanghai, China). Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining was performed, and lung injury was scored under an optical microscope (Olympus Optics, Tokyo, Japan). Six random high-power fields were evaluated, and the mean score represented each sample.

In vivo fluorescence imaging of hucMSCs

hucMSCs were transfected with Luc-tagged lentivirus and selected using puromycin. Mice were assigned to three groups (n = 3 per group): Control, LPS+hucMSCs, and hucMSCs. Two hours after LPS injection, Luc-labeled hucMSCs were administered via tail vein. At 6, 12, and 24 h after injection, potassium luciferin was administered intraperitoneally, and hucMSC distribution was visualized using the AniView100 Pro multimodal in vivo imaging system.

Proteomic analysis

For proteomics, mice were divided into three groups (Control, LPS, LPS+hucMSCs; n = 4 per group). Lung tissue proteins were extracted, digested into polypeptides, and subjected to quantitative analysis. Samples were processed to generate a proteomic database, and data were analyzed using Proteome Discoverer software.

The pulmonary vascular permeability was evaluated by injecting FITC- albumin

Following successful ALI induction, each mouse received 100 µL of FITC-albumin (5 mg/kg) one hour before tissue collection. After circulation, the mice were anesthetized, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and serum were collected. BALF and diluted serum (100 µL each) were transferred to a 96-well microplate. Samples were excited at 485 nm, and absorbance was measured at 528 nm. The BALF-to-serum absorbance ratio was used to evaluate lung permeability.

For fluorescence imaging, mice were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 6 per group): Control, LPS, LPS+hucMSCs, LPS+shCxcl12 hucMSCs, LPS + NC hucMSCs, hucMSCs. The AniView100 Pro system was pre-cooled. Animals were anesthetized, and the thoracic cavity was exposed. After complete perfusion and lung extraction, lung tissues were rinsed, dried, placed on black background paper, and imaged using the FITC probe.

Cell culture

hucMSCs were sourced from Shandong Qilu Stem Cell Engineering Co., Ltd, and human microvascular endothelial cells (HULEC-5a) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The cells were seeded in a 25 cm² cell culture flask and cultured in a sterile incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. All hucMSCs utilized in the animal experiments were maintained within three passages. Based on cell culture observations, hucMSCs exhibit slower division and growth after the third passage.The hucMSCs counted using a cell counter (Halo counter).

Construct hucMSCs with silenced Cxcl12

In this study, the silencing of Cxcl12 in hucMSCs were accomplished using short hairpin RNA (shRNA). The targeted silencing was facilitated by transfection with a lentiviral vector, with the specific shRNA binding site identified as GGTCGTGGTCGTGCTGGTCCT. The total process duration is nine days. Day 1: Thaw hucMSCs and seed them in 25 cm² culture flasks at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per flask, then culture at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Day 3: When cell confluence reaches 20–30%, replace the medium with lentiviral vectors (MOI = 100) combined with a transfection reagent, and incubate for 12–16 h. Day 4: Replace the medium with regular culture medium to remove unbound viruses. Days 5–7: Add 1 µg/mL puromycin for selection, changing the selection medium every 24 h for a total of 72 h. Day 8: Collect surviving cells and validate Cxcl12 silencing efficiency via Western blot and qRT-PCR. Day 9: Utilize the validated shCxcl12 hucMSCs for co-culture experiments or animal injections.

In vitro construction of ALI cell model

A Transwell chamber was used to establish a co-culture system consisting endothelial cells and hucMSCs. Endothelial cells were seeded at the bottom of the culture plate, while hucMSCs or shCxcl12 hucMSCs (1 × 105/well) were added to the upper chamber. The cell seeding groups corresponded with the in vivo experimental groups. When endothelial cells reached 90% confluence, the medium was replaced with serum-free medium, and LPS (10 µg/mL) was added to the lower chamber. After 6 h, the culture chamber was removed, and the endothelial cells were washed with PBS and proteins were collected for analysis.

For experiments involving the exogenous addition of Cxcl12 recombinant protein and the CXCR4 inhibitor (AMD3100), once the endothelial cells reached 90% confluence, the medium was again replaced with serum-free medium, and LPS was added according to the respective groups. Subsequently, Cxcl12 recombinant protein (200 pg/mL) or AMD3100 (20 µM) was introduced into the medium, and proteins were collected after 6 h.

Western blot verification

Total proteins were extracted from lung tissues and cells using RIPA buffer. Protein concentrations were determined using the Enhanced BCA Assay Kit. Initially, 20 µg of the sample was added to the respective wells. The samples were subsequently separated by 10% SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis. Following this, the separated proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane, blocked with TBST containing 5% skim milk, and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. The membrane was then incubated with primary antibodies, including anti-SDC-1, HS, EXT-1, Cxcl12, CXCR4, and GAPDH, overnight at 4 °C. Finally, the membrane was treated with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h, and the protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence kit. Density analysis was conducted using ImageJ software.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from lung tissue, and its concentration and purity were assessed using a nucleic acid analyzer (Ambion, Thermo, MA, USA). Total RNA was reverse transcribed and amplified using the Takara PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit. mRNA expression was quantified using the formula 2 - (ΔCT sample – ΔCT Control). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Cxcl12, SDC-1, and HS levels in mouse serum were measured using specific ELISA kits, and Cxcl12 levels in hucMSC culture medium were measured using a human Cxcl12 ELISA kit. Conditioned medium was collected from 1 × 10⁵ hucMSCs/HULEC-5a.

Samples or standards were added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. Afterward, antibody working solution was added for 60 min at 37 °C. Wells were washed three times, followed by incubation with HRP conjugate for 30 min at 37 °C. After additional washing, substrate solution was added for 20 min at 37 °C, and the reaction was stopped. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Immunofluorescence staining

Endothelial cells (1 × 10⁵/well) were seeded into 24-well plates with pre-placed slides. After washing with PBS, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, blocked with goat serum for 30 min, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, cells were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. DAPI staining solution (200 µL) was added for 5 min. Slides were mounted with anti-fade medium and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

FITC-albumin permeability detection

HULEC-5a cells (15,000 cells/well) were seeded into the upper chambers of a 24-well Transwell plate. hucMSCs or shCxcl12 hucMSCs (20,000 cells/well) were seeded into the lower wells for co-culture. After 6 h of LPS treatment, medium in the Transwell chamber was replaced. FITC-albumin (1 mg/mL) was added under light-protected conditions to the upper chamber and incubated for 30 min. Then, 100 µL of medium from the upper and lower chambers was transferred to a 96-well plate. Absorbance at 490 nm and 525 nm was measured, and permeability was assessed by the lower-to-upper chamber absorbance ratio.

Transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER)

Co-culture was conducted as previously described. LPS was added for 6 h. The two electrodes of the Michel® ERS-2 resistance meter were placed above and below the filter membrane. Baseline (blank) resistance was measured, and TEER was calculated as: (Ω/cm²) = (measured resistance − blank resistance) × effective membrane area.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.5. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two groups used the t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups used one-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Localization of hucMSCs in normal mice and septic mice

To examine the distribution of hucMSCs after tail vein injection and to assess their homing to the lungs following injury, an LPS-ALI model was established through intraperitoneal LPS administration. hucMSCs were labeled with a Luc-tagged lentiviral vector and then injected into normal mice and LPS-ALI mice, while control mice received saline. Fluorescence imaging of whole-body distribution was performed at 6, 12, and 24 h after injection (Fig. 1A). Organs were also collected for imaging at the 6-hour time point (Fig. 1B). The results showed that intravenously administered hucMSCs localized to the lung tissue in normal mice. The fluorescence intensity in the lungs of LPS-ALI mice was notably higher than in normal mice, indicating that hucMSCs migrate to and accumulate in injured lung tissue.

Injection of hucMSCs improves ALI

A mouse model of acute lung injury was established to investigate the mechanisms underlying ARDS and explore potential therapeutic approaches. The ALI model was induced by intraperitoneal LPS injection, followed by tail vein delivery of hucMSCs two hours later. After six additional hours, blood and lung tissue were collected for analysis (Fig. 2A). The survival rate improved in LPS-ALI mice that received hucMSCs (Fig. 2B). Histopathology showed that LPS caused marked structural disruption, including blurred alveolar architecture, interstitial edema, congestion, and increased tissue debris. These abnormalities were reduced after hucMSC treatment (Fig. 2C-D). The lung wet/dry ratio, total cell count, and protein content in BALF were also decreased (Fig. 2E-G). FITC-albumin permeability analysis further confirmed that hucMSCs lowered LPS-induced pulmonary vascular permeability (Fig. 2H-I).

Proteomic analysis suggests Cxcl12 as a key protein

Proteomic analysis revealed a decrease in the expression of Cxcl12 in lung tissues following LPS injection, with an increase in expression observed after hucMSCs treatment (Fig. 3A-C). To validate the accuracy of the proteomic findings, this study employed Western blot and qRT-PCR to assess Cxcl12 expression in lung tissues, and the results were consistent with those obtained from the proteomic analysis (Fig. 3D-E). Additionally, changes in Cxcl12 levels in mouse serum were detected using an ELISA kit, demonstrating a trend that aligns with the findings in lung tissues (Fig. 3F).

Cxcl12 plays a crucial role in the hucMSCs-mediated amelioration of LPS-ALI

Cxcl12 is known to be one of the secreted proteins of hucMSCs. ELISA analysis showed that hucMSCs secreted higher amounts of Cxcl12 than HULEC-5a cells, and secretion increased after LPS stimulation (Fig. 4A). Lentiviral transfection was used to silence Cxcl12 in hucMSCs, and silencing efficiency was verified by Western blot, qRT-PCR, and ELISA (Fig. 4B-D). Transcriptomic analysis confirmed that no significant changes in positive markers (CD73, CD90, CD105) or negative markers (CD45, CD34, CD14, CD19, HLA-DR), indicating that the hucMSC phenotype remained stable. (Figure S1).

To determine the role of Cxcl12 in vivo, shCxcl12 hucMSCs were injected into LPS-ALI mice. Compared with mice receiving normal hucMSCs, the Cxcl12-silenced group showed more severe pulmonary edema and septal damage, a higher wet/dry ratio, and increased cell and protein levels in BALF (Fig. 4E-I). FITC-albumin permeability also increased (Fig. 4J-K). These findings indicate that Cxcl12 contributes to the ability of hucMSCs to reduce lung tissue permeability and alleviate lung injury.

hucMSCs ameliorate LPS-induced endothelial glycocalyx damage

This study examined the effect of hucMSCs delivered via tail vein injection on the endothelial glycocalyx in mouse lung tissue. Western blot and qRT-PCR analyses were used to measure glycocalyx-related proteins, including HS, SDC-1, and EXT-1, the key enzyme responsible for HS synthesis. LPS-ALI mice showed reduced expression of HS, SDC-1, and EXT-1, whereas hucMSC treatment increased their expression (Fig. 5A-B). ELISA results further demonstrated that hucMSCs decreased levels of free SDC-1 and HS in serum (Fig. 5C).

To determine whether hucMSCs exert similar effects in vitro, a co-culture model of endothelial cells and hucMSCs was established, with LPS added to induce inflammatory injury (Supplementary Fig. 2). Western blot and qRT-PCR analyses showed that LPS reduced HS, SDC-1, and EXT-1 expression in endothelial cells, while co-culture with hucMSCs restored their expression (Fig. 5D-E). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that LPS caused shedding of SDC-1 and HS from the endothelial surface; this effect was reduced after co-culture with hucMSCs (Fig. 5F). FITC–albumin permeability and TEER assays confirmed that hucMSC co-culture reduced the LPS-induced increase in endothelial permeability (Fig. 5G). These findings indicate that hucMSCs ameliorate LPS-induced endothelial glycocalyx damage, enhance EXT-1 expression, promote glycocalyx repair, and reduce endothelial permeability.

Cxcl12 is a crucial protein for hucMSCs in ameliorating LPS-induced endothelial glycocalyx damage

The contribution of Cxcl12 to the effect of hucMSCs on the endothelial glycocalyx in LPS-ALI mice was assessed by injecting shCxcl12 hucMSCs via the tail vein. Western blot and qRT-PCR showed that HS, SDC-1, and EXT-1 were decreased in LPS-ALI lung tissue, increased after hucMSC injection, and then reduced again when Cxcl12-silenced hucMSCs were administered (Fig. 6A-B). ELISA showed increased serum levels of free SDC-1 and HS in mice treated with shCxcl12 hucMSCs compared with mice receiving hucMSCs (Fig. 6C).

To confirm these findings in a cellular model, endothelial cells were co-cultured with shCxcl12-hucMSCs and then exposed to LPS. Western blot and qRT-PCR showed that HS, SDC-1, and EXT-1 decreased after LPS stimulation, increased after co-culture with hucMSCs, and decreased again when co-cultured with shCxcl12-hucMSCs (Fig. 6D-E). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that LPS increased the shedding of SDC-1 and HS from the endothelial surface, whereas hucMSC co-culture reduced this effect. Co-culture with shCxcl12 hucMSCs reduced SDC-1 and HS retention to a lesser extent than co-culture with normal hucMSCs (Fig. 6F). FITC-albumin permeability and TEER measurements showed that endothelial permeability increased after co-culture with shCxcl12-hucMSCs (Fig. 6G). These results demonstrate that hucMSCs reduce LPS-induced glycocalyx injury and promote glycocalyx repair through secretion of Cxcl12.

To evaluate the effect of Cxcl12 on the CXCR4 receptor, exogenous Cxcl12 was added to endothelial cells during LPS stimulation, and CXCR4 expression was examined by Western blot and qRT-PCR. LPS reduced CXCR4 expression, whereas Cxcl12 restored it (Fig. 8A–B). These findings indicate that Cxcl12 can increase CXCR4 expression in endothelial cells after LPS exposure.

To verify that Cxcl12 reduces glycocalyx damage through CXCR4, the CXCR4 inhibitor AMD3100 was used to block receptor signaling in the cellular model. Western blot and qRT-PCR showed that LPS decreased the expression of HS, SDC-1, and EXT-1, which were increased by Cxcl12. AMD3100 pretreatment reduced the expression of these proteins compared to the Cxcl12 group (Fig. 8C–D). Immunofluorescence showed that Cxcl12 reduced LPS-induced shedding of SDC-1 and HS from the endothelial surface, whereas AMD3100 increased shedding relative to the Cxcl12 group (Fig. 8E). The results of FITC-albumin permeability and TEER measurements showed that endothelial permeability increased after AMD3100 pretreatment (Fig. 8F).These findings demonstrate that Cxcl12 reduces glycocalyx damage and promotes repair through its downstream receptor CXCR4.

Discussion

ARDS is a clinical syndrome characterized by refractory and potentially fatal hypoxemia, which can be triggered by various factors. Its main pathological features include increased permeability of the pulmonary vascular endothelium and the formation of pulmonary edema. The onset of pulmonary edema can lead to dyspnea in patients and may result in fatal hypoxia [30, 31]. Currently, the treatment for ARDS primarily relies on mechanical support therapy, which is expensive and prone to inducing multiple complications [32, 33]. In the field of stem cell therapy, human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hucMSCs) exert therapeutic effects through multiple mechanisms. Firstly, they possess immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties, effectively regulating and suppressing immune responses, thereby mitigating the occurrence of inflammation [34–36]. This process promotes tissue repair and regeneration. Basic and clinical studies related to hucMSCs treatment for ARDS have shown that these cells can alleviate lung injury, offering a promising avenue for ARDS treatment [37–39]. However, the specific therapeutic mechanisms remain unclear, hindering the clinical application and promotion of mesenchymal stem cells. Therefore, in-depth research into the molecular mechanisms of mesenchymal stem cell therapy for ALI is necessary to provide a theoretical foundation for the early clinical application of hucMSCs treatment.

The endothelial glycocalyx is a complex polysaccharide structure that covers the surface of vascular endothelial cells, primarily composed of glycoproteins, proteoglycans (such as syndecans and glypicans), and glycosaminoglycans (including heparan sulfate, hyaluronic acid, and chondroitin sulfate) [12, 40, 41]. These components collectively form a dynamic, negatively charged nano-barrier that plays a crucial role in vascular function and homeostasis. Under pathological conditions, components of the glycocalyx, such as syndecan-1 and heparan sulfate, are released into the bloodstream and can serve as biomarkers for disease severity [42, 43]. During sepsis, the glycocalyx is continuously subjected to shearing stress from enzymes such as heparanase (HPA) and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9), maintaining the endothelial glycocalyx in a dynamic equilibrium between degradation and repair. Additionally, damage to the glycocalyx leads to increased vascular permeability, exacerbated inflammatory responses, and microcirculatory dysfunction, further aggravating disease progression [44, 45]. Research related to infectious diseases has found that the repair time of the endothelial glycocalyx is significantly prolonged compared to the control group, and recent studies indicate that the delay in glycocalyx repair is closely associated with the development of acute lung injury in sepsis [46]. During the repair process, exostosin glycosyltransferase-1 (Exostosin-1, EXT-1) plays a crucial role. EXT-1 is distributed in the Golgi apparatus and endoplasmic reticulum of cells and is essential for the synthesis of heparan sulfate [47]. In mouse models, EXT-1 deficiency severely compromises endothelial cell function and disrupts vascular physiology [48]. Moreover, loss of EXT-1 exacerbates inflammation and oxidative stress, likely due to impaired HS synthesis.

This study demonstrates that hucMSCs migrate and home to the lungs of LPS-induced ALI mice, where they remain during the experimental period. hucMSCs reduced lung tissue injury, decreased vascular permeability, limited inflammatory exudation, and improved survival. Proteomic screening identified Cxcl12 as a key molecule. Using lentiviral silencing, we constructed shCxcl12 hucMSCs and delivered them via tail vein in LPS-induced ALI. Compared with normal hucMSCs, Cxcl12-silenced hucMSCs showed reduced therapeutic efficacy, indicating that Cxcl12 plays a critical role in mediating hucMSCs function.

Cxcl12 is a chemokine that participates in multiple biological processes, including endothelial repair, angiogenesis, and cell trafficking [49, 50]. It promotes endothelial cell survival, proliferation, and migration, which are essential for restoring endothelial barrier integrity. When endothelial cells are injured, their regenerative capacity becomes particularly important, and Cxcl12 enhances this capacity through activation of the PI3K/Akt and MAPK pathways.

In animal and cell experiments, glycocalyx components SDC-1 and HS, as well as EXT-1, were evaluated using Western blot and qRT-PCR. Serum levels of free SDC-1 and HS were measured by ELISA, and residual SDC-1 and HS on the endothelial surface were assessed by immunofluorescence. LPS reduced the expression of these proteins, whereas hucMSCs or co-culture with hucMSCs restored their expression. Silencing Cxcl12 in hucMSCs reduced these effects. Furthermore, exogenous Cxcl12 increased the expression of the three proteins after LPS stimulation. These findings indicate that hucMSCs alleviate LPS-induced glycocalyx injury and promote repair through Cxcl12 secretion, reducing endothelial permeability and contributing to ALI treatment.

How does Cxcl12 exert these effects on the glycocalyx? CXCR4, the principal receptor for Cxcl12, mediates most of its downstream actions. CXCR4 activation promotes the recruitment of endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) to sites of injury, which is essential for endothelial repair. Studies have shown that vascular injury increases Cxcl12 expression, which enhances the mobilization of EPCs from the bone marrow to the bloodstream [28, 51].The Cxcl12-CXCR4 interaction is critical for the migration and homing of progenitor cells to damaged endothelium, thereby improving repair efficiency.

We used AMD3100 as a CXCR4 receptor inhibitor to conduct the subsequent experiments. AMD3100 blocks CXCR4 biological activity and interferes with downstream signaling pathways. It effectively inhibits CXCR4-mediated calcium signaling while not affecting the activity of other chemokine receptors, demonstrating its high selectivity. This selectivity provides insight into CXCR4 signaling and its roles in physiological and pathological processes. In the cell experiments, exogenous Cxcl12 recombinant protein was added, and AMD3100 was used to block CXCR4-dependent signaling. By examining the expression of key glycocalyx proteins, we investigated the relationship between CXCR4 and glycocalyx repair. The results showed that exogenous Cxcl12 increased CXCR4 expression. However, pretreatment with AMD3100 reduced the expression of HS, SDC-1, and EXT-1 compared with the Cxcl12-treated group. In addition, the protective effect of hucMSCs on the endothelial glycocalyx was weakened when CXCR4 signaling was inhibited. These findings indicate that hucMSCs mitigate glycocalyx injury by secreting Cxcl12, which activates CXCR4 and promotes glycocalyx repair by regulating EXT-1 expression.

This study has certain limitations, primarily focusing on the regulatory effect of Cxcl12 on CXCR4 and its impact on endothelial glycocalyx repair, conducted solely based on cell models. Currently, there is a lack of in vivo experiments utilizing endothelial cell-specific CXCR4 knockout mice or the CXCR4 inhibitor AMD3100 to validate the ability of hucMSCs to activate the CXCR4 receptor, which would elucidate the role of hucMSCs-Cxcl12/CXCR4 in LPS-ALI. Furthermore, the study overlooked potential individual physiological differences in mouse models that could affect the therapeutic efficacy of hucMSCs. In clinical settings, ARDS/ALI patients exhibit individual variations in age, underlying diseases, and immune status, all of which may influence the homing and reparative capabilities of hucMSCs. This study did not address this critical issue, thus lacking the foundation for precise screening of suitable patient populations in clinical practice. Future clinical research should integrate patients’ individual characteristics to explore factors affecting the therapeutic efficacy of hucMSCs, ultimately achieving precision in stem cell therapy.

Overall, this study shows that hucMSCs alleviate LPS-induced endothelial glycocalyx damage, improve pulmonary vascular permeability, reduce lung injury in LPS-ALI mice, and increase survival rates by activating CXCR4 through the paracrine protein Cxcl12. The hucMSC-Cxcl12/CXCR4 pathway may offer a potential therapeutic strategy for ARDS (Fig. 10).
