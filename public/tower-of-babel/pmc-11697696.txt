Multi-step optimization with operational scenarios for hull form and propulsor design for pod-driven cruise ships
Authors: Lecheng Li, Long Yu, Zhiyuan Cai, Jingjing Liu, Qidong Fan, Youjiang Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
With the increasing demand for reducing CO2 emissions by the International Maritime Organization (IMO), controlling a ship's energy consumption at the design stage is crucial for proposing a 'greener' design. Some efforts have been made to consider the Energy Efficiency Design Index (EEDI) and the Energy Efficiency Operational Index (EEOI); however, the latter remains highly complex and contentious. In this study, a multistep optimization analysis method was developed to integrate EEDI and EEOI evaluations during the design stage to meet low emission requirements. Additionally, an Efficient Operational Scenario Analysis Method (EOSAM) was established to consider operational energy efficiency. This method was applied to optimize the hull and propulsor design of a pod-driven cruise ship. By accounting for operating conditions in both calm water and wave scenarios, and utilizing high-fidelity computational fluid dynamics simulations and model test validation, the delivered power was reduced by up to 7.1 % compared to the original design, and the EEOI decreased by an average of 16.7 %.

Introduction

Traditionally, during the preliminary design phase, the hull form and propulsor design, as well as the optimization processes for both, are treated as separate tasks performed sequentially. The hull form is selected using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to estimate hydrodynamic performance, with optimization algorithms minimizing calm-water resistance. Once the hull is chosen, the propulsor with the best performance (in terms of thrust coefficient or efficiency) at the operating speed is selected. However, the interaction between the propulsor and the hull form should be considered during each design phase. A sophisticated model for these interactions is essential to identify complementary solutions that reduce fuel consumption [1]. Esmailian & Steen [2] proposed a new method for optimizing the design of a ship with respect to different operational conditions using a probabilistic approach.

For the hull form, the three broad methods of self-propulsion analysis are the Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) method, body force/actuator method, and RANS method coupled with a discretized propeller model. Considering the trade-off between computation time and accuracy, the latter two methods are typically applied at the ship design stage. Feng et al. [3] extended the method to predict the increase in the propulsive power requirement under actual wave conditions with podded propulsion through model experimentation. Gaggero et al. [4] proposed a coupled boundary element method (BEM)/RANS approach to render self-propulsion simulations more affordable during the ship design stage. Additionally, Trodden et al. [5] analyzed propeller performance, taking into account the influence of ship maneuvers. Kaidi et al. [6] combined the effect of bank and propeller. Andersson et al. [7] performed an energy balance analysis of open to understand the physics system performance better. And Cai et al. [8] used RANS to predict hydrodynamic characteristic for a marine propeller in a turbulence zone with unsteady flow condition.

Regarding propulsion, the propulsive efficiency and other hydrodynamic performance characteristics were calculated based on the wake distribution from the optimal hull form. Optimization algorithms were used to identify the propulsion system with high thrust and efficiency. The reduction in propeller thrust in waves is inversely correlated with the propeller load [9]. Esmailian et al. [10] proposed a new, optimized method for ship design that considers different levels of propulsive power and operational conditions probabilistically. Modern cruise ships often use podded propulsors as unconventional thrusters (Fig. 1). Designing a podded propulsor requires investigating the unsteady behavior of the strut and its interaction with the blade, a complex problem involving coupling with the hull [11]. While podded propulsors can be analyzed using potential solvers [12], accounting for the viscous effects of the strut and turbulent inflow requires a RANS solver [13].Fig. 1Podded propulsor (manufactured by ABB [14]).Fig. 1

Podded propulsor (manufactured by ABB [14]).

To reduce the overall fuel consumption of ships, the International Maritime Organization (IMO) legislated the application of a carbon intensity indicator (CII) in the maritime industry in January 2023 as part of its greenhouse gas (GHG) strategy to decarbonize the industry. The Energy Efficiency Design Index (EEDI) and Energy Efficiency Operation Index (EEOI), proposed by the IMO's Marine Environment Protection Committee (MEPC), are related to various parameters, such as ship resistance and efficiency [15]. These indices serve as comprehensive evaluation metrics for design and optimization, expressing the environmental costs associated with ship operations. Some studies have focused on the EEDI during the design phase. Zakerdoost et al. [16] developed software to design a hull-propeller system based on lifetime fuel consumption and EEDI considerations in the preliminary design stage. Priftis et al. [17] incorporated the EEDI into their holistic optimization design approach. However, studies on EEOI estimations are rare.Kazerooni et al. [18] calculated the Greenhouse gas emission in manoeuvering condition based on NCOS ONLINE.

Method description

Multi-step optimization scheme

In this study, we developed a multistep iterative design scheme for the hull form and propulsor, incorporating fully parametric models for both, as shown in Fig. 2. The design and optimization processes were integrated with iterative hydrodynamic performance computations for the hull and propulsor, utilizing the RANS method to ensure high fidelity.Fig. 2Flowchart of the multistep optimization.Fig. 2

Flowchart of the multistep optimization.

The method involves four optimization processes, consisting of three steps based on different stages, as illustrated by the cyclic arrows in Fig. 2.

The first step began with the initial design of the propulsors and hull form. The first optimization process focused on the hull form, investigating optimal resistance and delivered power under calm-water conditions. The propulsor and hull were integrated using a virtual disk method to simulate self-propulsion. The resulting hull form was designated as OptiHull-1.

In the second step, to achieve a high-fidelity assessment, the second optimization process was used in the propulsors and then we conducted additional optimization process to explore their impact on the hull. The podded propulsors were directly optimized using the RANS method. Subsequently, the optimal propulsor configuration was determined through the third optimization process, focusing on the aft body optimization with the hull, propulsor, and engine integrated. The podded propulsors and hull form, labeled OptiHull-2, were obtained in open-water conditions.

In the third step, the bulbous bow was modeled using the free-form deformation (FFD) method, considering added resistance from waves, resulting in a hull form labeled OptiHull-3.

Finally, the Efficient Operational Scenarios Analysis Method (EOSAM) was applied to evaluate operational conditions in wave. The optimal propulsors and hull forms from previous optimizations were used to evaluated using empirical formulas for calculating EEOI and selected the best solution based on operational scenarios. Furthermore, an additional step was taken to optimize the speed in the operational scenario and a sensitivity analysis was also conducted to understand the key factors influencing the design.

Parametric modeling and optimization models

Hull form

A fully parametric model incorporating hull form characteristics from a parent cruise ship was constructed using CAESES software. This model employs various parametric modeling techniques and optimization methods for hull shape design.

First, parameters such as Lpp (Length Between Perpendiculars), Bship (Breadth of Ship), Tship (Draft of Ship), and Dship (Depth of Ship) were defined. Next, a parametric curve was generated based on the offset table, including features like the center buttock, flat-side curve, and flat-bottom curve. The body line features were then defined, and a curved engine was used to generate each frame. A surface was created using the meta-surface method, and the hull shape was refined.

The cruise ship's hull form features were represented by a combination of typical lines and waterplane control points, designed using Simulation-Based Design (SBD) [19], as shown in Fig. 3. To enhance hydrodynamic performance under wave conditions, the bow was optimized using the Free-Form Deformation (FFD) method. The service speed was established as the design point, and the following objective function was used to minimize the delivered power, taking into account the coupling between the propulsor and hull resistance, as shown in Equation (1):(1)minPD(X),s.t.X=[x1,x2,…,x12],|(∇(X)−∇0)/∇0|×100%≤1%.where PD(X) is delivered power, X=[x1,x2,…,x12] is the design variables vector of the hull form, ∇0 is the displacement of original hull form, and ∇ is the displacement of hull form under different X.

To account for displacement variations caused by hull changes, the displacement error was restricted to within 1 %. The hull optimization process integrates the Sobol and T-search algorithms.

Initially, the Sobol method was used to select evenly distributed samples for global optimization. Each hull sample was then analyzed through self-propulsion simulations to identify the optimal hull configuration. Following this, the T-search algorithm was applied for further global optimization.

In the previous step, the hull was optimized, while the propulsor was optimized under open conditions. Subsequently, the optimal aft line for optimization was determined by matching the propulsor and hull resistances. Thus, the optimal hull form for a specific propulsor solution and its corresponding hydrodynamic performance could be obtained. The podded propulsor was electrically driven. Therefore, for comparative analysis, a power characteristic curve of the electric engine was generated.

The SHIPFLOW software package was used to calculate the resistance of the ship model [20]. The solver employed a “ZONAL” strategy, incorporating the potential XPAN model for wave-making resistance and the viscous XCHAP model for viscous resistance. XPAN, based on the Rankine panel method, accounted for the free surface, while XCHAP used the RANS method to model frictional and viscous pressure resistances, incorporating the wave pattern derived from the potential solver. Additionally, an actuator model was applied to analyze hull–propulsor interactions.

Podded propulsors

In this study, we constructed a fully parametric model of a podded propulsor for design and multi-objective optimization. The parametric blade model, developed based on B-screw propellers, includes nonlinear control lines for the chord, pitch, thickness, skew, and rake (see Fig. 5). A blade-generating program obtained variable values at different radii through interpolation of these control lines. The program then automatically generated propulsor sections and assembled them into a complete blade. The final calculation-ready podded propulsor model was created by integrating the parametric blade model with the pod hull and bracket models.Fig. 5Control lines for parametric blade.Fig. 5

Control lines for parametric blade.

Subsequently, the Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) method was applied to the podded propulsor at the model scale to estimate its hydrodynamic performance. The continuity and momentum conservation equations are as follows in Equations (3), (4):(3)∂u‾i∂xi=0,(4)∂(ρu‾i)∂t+∂∂xj(ρu‾ju‾i)=−∂p‾∂xi+Fi+∂∂xj(σji+Rji),where Rji=−ρui′uj′‾ is referred to as the Reynolds stress term or turbulent stress term, which is the new unknown variable after averaging the N–S equation, causing the equations to be open. The shear stress transport (SST) k-ω turbulence model was incorporated to solve the system of equations. According to the Boussinesq assumption, Rji=Rij=μTSij−23ρkδij, where μT is the turbulent eddy viscosity coefficient. Given μT=ρkω, the governing equations for the turbulent kinetic energy k and dissipation rate ω are as follows in Equations (5), (6):(5)∂ρk∂t+u‾j∂ρk∂xj=τij∂u‾i∂xj−β∗ρkω+∂∂xj[(μ+σkμT)∂k∂xj],(6)∂ρω∂t+u‾j∂ρω∂xj=αωkτij∂ui∂xj−βρω2+∂∂xj[(μ+σωμT)∂ω∂xj]+2ρ(1−F1)σω21ω∂k∂xj∂ω∂xj,where F1 is a combined coefficient that equals 1 near the wall and 0 far from it in the combined k–ω and k–ε models. Other assistive equations and parameters are available in the literature [13].

We developed a multi-objective optimization method using the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II) to achieve large thrust and high efficiency. The optimization problem can be expressed as follows in Equation (7):(7)max{KT(V),η(V)},s.t.V=[v1,v2,v3,v4]VL≤V≤VU,fi(V)=0,i=1,2⋅⋅⋅,n,hj(V)≤0,j=1,2⋅⋅⋅m,where KT is the thrust coefficient, η is the propulsive efficiency, V=[v1,v2,v3,v4] is the design variables vector of propeller, VL is the lower bound vector of the design variables, VU is the upper bound vector of the design variables, fi(v) represents equality constraints, and hj(v) represents inequality constraints. The NSGA-II algorithm obtains multiple optimal solutions, known as Pareto-optimal solutions.

Energy efficiency design index (EEDI) and energy efficiency operation index (EEOI) calculation

The EEDI and EEOI was calculated to rapidly evaluate above optimal ship design and propulsor. Therefore, practical formulas were used in this section.

EEDI

The EEDI was calculated for the optimal ship design according to IMO guidelines [21]. The cruise ship in question does not contain a shaft generator, shaft motor, or any innovative energy-efficient technology. The equation for EEDI is as follows in Equation (8):(8)EEDI=∑i=1nMEPME(i)CFME(i)gME(i)+PAECFAEgAECVref,where nME is the number of main engines, PME(i) is 75 % of the maximum continuous rating (MCR) of each main engine (i), CFME(i) is the conversion factor between the fuel consumption (FC) of each main engine (i) (g) and the CO2 emissions (g), gME(i) is the unit consumption of each main engine (i) (g/kWh), Vs is the ship speed (kn), and PAE is the power of the auxiliary engine in deep-water sailing at Vs, with a total thruster power of ∑MCRME(i)≥10,000kW.PAE is expressed as in Equation (9)(9)PAE=0.025×∑i=1nMEMCRME(i)+250.CFAE is the conversion factor between the FC of each auxiliary engine (i) (g) and CO2 emissions (g), gAE is the certified specific FC (g/kWh), and C refers to the number of passengers on the cruise ship, also known as gross tonnage (GT), in accordance with the International Convention of Tonnage Measurement of Ships 1969, Annex I, Regulation 3. The reference EEDI line required by the IMO is as follows in Equation (10):(10)EEDIrequired=170.84×C0.214,where C is the GT of the ship.

Efficient Operational Scenarios Analysis Method (EOSAM)—performance on waves with EEOI considerations

The study focused on optimizing the hull form and propulsor design under calm water conditions. However, environmental factors become significant when a ship is sailing, as reflected by the EEOI. To address this, we established the Efficient Operational Scenario Analysis Method (EOSAM) for EEOI considerations, as shown in Fig. 6. Based on IMO guidelines [22], the EOSAM was developed. The Sequential Least-Squares Quadratic Programming (SLSQP) algorithm was utilized to optimize ship speed for each voyage leg.Fig. 6Efficient Operational Scenarios Analysis Method (EOSAM) calculation flowchart.Fig. 6

Efficient Operational Scenarios Analysis Method (EOSAM) calculation flowchart.

Unlike EEDI, EEOI represents CO2 emissions per unit transportation work for a specific voyage and is expressed as follows in Equation (11):(11)EEOI=∑jg(j)CF(j)CDl,where g(j) is the fuel consumption in the j th leg (t); CF(j) is the conversion factor between the fuel consumption (FC) in the j th leg (g) and the CO2 emissions (g), and Dl is the overall voyage distance (km).

The main diesel engine consumes fuel to overcome the resistance when the ship is sailing. The total resistance of the waves was as follows in Equation (12):(12)Rtotal=Rcalm+Rwave+Rwind,where Rtotal is the summation of the calm-water resistance Rcalm, added resistance in waves Rwave, and added resistance due to wind Rwind. Calm-water resistance was estimated by interpolating the results from previous studies, whereas the added resistance in the wave condition was calculated using the SHOPERA-NTUA-NTU-MARIC (SNNM) method [23], which is a fast but satisfactory semi-empirical formulation. Initial design for podded propulsor

The initial step involved designing an appropriate propeller based on the required ship velocity, and determining the rotational speed and efficiency of the electric motor for further engine selection. Subsequently, a motor was used to determine the pod geometry, including the blade and strut, which resulted in an optimal diameter, area ratio, pitch ratio, and ship speed.

The propulsor parameters were computed based on the propeller diameter, which was limited to within 4.5 m for an electrical engine with a ship speed of less than 20 knots.

Conclusions

This study provides a multistep iterative hull and propulsor design and optimization analysis method for a pod-driven cruise ship, including fully parametric modeling for both the hull and podded propulsor, with the aim of minimizing energy consumption. EEDI and EEOI were considered in the optimization process. The aspects addressed herein include the full parametric modeling of lines and podded propulsor design, several optimizations, coupled analysis of the aft body and propulsors, bulbous-bow modification, and performance evaluation of the propulsors on waves.

Because the operational scenarios were anticipated with weather conditions at various voyage legs, the added resistance to waves was investigated, and the EEOI was evaluated for optimal results. The matching of the hull and podded propulsors was analyzed, and the mutual effects of the propulsor and hull form design and optimization were investigated. CFD analysis and model testing were conducted for high-fidelity validation.

The following conclusions were drawn.(1)The design parameters of the podded propulsors were initially determined by using an equivalent design method to achieve a B43 open propeller. Subsequently, PODB43 was modeled to determine its hydrodynamic performance. Thereafter, a parametric propulsor model was established, which achieved a 1.55 % increase in efficiency while maintaining thrust. The first OptiHull-1 achieved a 2.38 % reduction in delivered power.(2)The optimal hull OptiHull-2 with direct thruster optimization significantly reduced the delivered power by 6.21 % under calm water conditions. A detailed analysis of hull–propulsor interactions could effectively improve energy consumption efficiency. The EEDI index was calculated based on the MEPC files, which was 13.46 gCO2/t·nm at a speed of 18 kn, meeting the requirement of the IMO regulations with a margin of 18.6 %.(3)The added resistance to waves and bow modification of the ship were investigated and optimized. Wavemaking resistance was reduced by 78.86 % in calm water. For the long-head wave, the added resistance RAW was reduced by 6.54 %. For short waves, it increases by 13.76 %. Therefore, the added resistance is significantly higher for short waves. Nonetheless, the total resistance RW was reduced by 5.61 %.(4)The proposed EOSAM was applied to the direct evaluation of the EEOI. The final optimal hull, OptiHull-3, gained an additional benefit; the delivered power was reduced by up to 7.1 %, whereas the EEOI decreased by an average of 16.7 %. The propulsion characteristics in open water led to an overestimation of the EEOI values compared to those in the behindship conditions by an average of 4 %.However, the interaction of the aft body with the podded propulsors is a complex mutual coupling process. The direct propulsor design should be considered simultaneously with wake changes in waves. In addition, the added wave resistance in an actual sea voyage and ship speed must be further determined without the limitations of the scale model.
