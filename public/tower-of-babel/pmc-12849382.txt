Differential alterations of thalamic subregions in children and adolescents with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and comorbid internalizing/externalizing disorders
Authors: Yongheng Zhao, Yihao Zhang, Jiahui Chen, Xiaoying Ma, Linlin Zhang, Lihong Wang, Zhiyue Liu, Mengna Zhu, Yu Wang, Xingxing Zhu, Aihua Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAttention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is frequently accompanied by both internalizing and externalizing symptoms, yet the neuroanatomical underpinnings of these comorbidities remain unclear, particularly within thalamic subnuclei that serve as central hubs of regulatory control.MethodsWe analyzed 3D T1-weighted MRI data from 359 ADHD patients (mean age = 10.19 ± 2.94, 265 males) and 209 healthy controls (mean age = 10.12 ± 3.48, 137 males) in the Healthy Brain Network cohort. ADHD participants were categorized into three subgroups: ADHD-only (n = 168, mean age = 10.18 ± 3.25), ADHD with internalizing symptoms (n = 97; mean age = 10.61 ± 2.67), and ADHD with externalizing symptoms (n = 94; mean age = 9.78 ± 2.58). Each thalamic nucleus was segmented into 25 subregions using FreeSurfer. ANCOVA were employed to examine differences of thalamic volumes between ADHD patients and healthy controls and to assess the associations with clinical symptoms. Subsequently, volumetric alterations across distinct ADHD comorbidity groups were examined, followed by age-related effects on thalamic development.ResultsCompared to healthy controls (HC), ADHD patients exhibited larger pulvinar anterior volumes and reduced volumes in the ventral anterior magnocellular, reuniens (medial ventral), and anteroventral nuclei. Larger thalamic volume in some subregions was negatively correlated with Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) subscale scores in ADHD patients. Compared to the externalizing subgroup and HC group, the internalizing subgroup showed smaller volume in the reuniens (medial ventral), central lateral, paracentral, and laterodorsal nuclei. Moreover, in adolescents, the internalizing subgroup exhibited significantly smaller parafascicular, central lateral, and laterodorsal thalamic nuclei volumes than the externalizing subgroup. Whereas, in children, ADHD-only subgroup (without internalizing/externalizing comorbidities) demonstrated a significantly larger lateral geniculate nucleus volume relative to the externalizing subgroup.ConclusionsADHD is characterized by alterations in thalamic subregions, with the comorbid internalizing group showing more pronounced abnormalities. Developmentally, these alterations shift from peripheral nuclei in childhood to central nuclei in adolescence, suggesting that thalamic subregional changes in comorbid ADHD may vary across developmental stages. These findings highlight the clinical and developmental relevance of thalamic subregion abnormalities in the heterogeneity of ADHD comorbidities.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07738-8.Keywords: Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, Thalamic subregions, Comorbidity, Children and adolescents

Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a chronic neurodevelopmental disorder, often emerging in childhood, persisting into adolescence, and extending into adulthood in a substantial subset of cases [1–3]. ADHD has pervasive and detrimental effects on academic performance, daily functioning, and imposes a considerable psychosocial and economic burden on families and society [4, 5]. Current clinical interventions primarily target the core symptoms of inattention and hyperactivity-impulsivity, often overlooking the high prevalence of comorbid conditions. Among ADHD patients, comorbidity rate ranges from approximately 60% to nearly 80%, with most individuals experiencing at least one kind of comorbidity [6, 7]. Depression, anxiety, oppositional defiant behavior, impulse control behavior are the commonly observed comorbidities [8]. These comorbid conditions would amplify impairments in academic and social functioning, and increase the healthcare burden [9]. Moreover, the presence and severity of comorbidities are closely linked to poorer long-term outcomes and prognosis [10–12]. Accordingly, a deeper understanding of the neurobiological mechanisms underpinning the comorbidities of ADHD is essential for developing more effective and targeted treatments.

Internalizing and externalizing problems are highly prevalent comorbidities among individuals with ADHD. Approximately 60% of children and adolescents with ADHD exhibit comorbid externalizing symptoms [10, 11], such as oppositional defiant disorder, conduct disorder, and aggressive behavior [13]. In addition, roughly 20% to 50% of children and adolescents with ADHD exhibit comorbid internalizing symptoms, primarily anxiety and depression [11, 14, 15]. A study leveraging data from the Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development Study revealed that patients with ADHD comorbid for both internalizing and externalizing disorders exhibit widespread reductions in cortical surface area across nearly the entire cerebral cortex, whereas those with only internalizing or externalizing comorbidities show alterations restricted to a few regions (e.g., the middle frontal gyrus and the inferior parietal lobule) [16]. Furthermore, ADHD with comorbid externalizing symptoms has been linked to significantly reduced volumes of the globus pallidus and caudate nucleus—structures that associated with deficits in impulse control and behavioral inhibition [17]. Prior studies indicate that impaired executive control and emotional dysregulation may represent key mechanisms driving the emergence of both internalizing and externalizing problems in youth with ADHD [18–20]. Although abnormalities in several cortical and basal ganglia regions have been consistently linked to ADHD-related comorbidities, it remains unclear how thalamic subregions—serving as central hubs for cognitive control and affective regulation—are differentially altered across distinct comorbid subtypes. This gap may limit a comprehensive understanding of the neurobiological mechanisms underlying ADHD comorbidity.

The thalamus functions as a critical relay station in the brain to cortical and other subcortical regions and serves as a central hub for information processing [21]. Thalamic nuclei are typically categorized into first-order and high-order groups based on their input-output characteristics. First-order nuclei, such as the lateral geniculate, medial geniculate, ventroposteromedial, and ventroposterolateral nuclei, receive direct sensory inputs (e.g., visual, auditory, and somatosensory signals). These nuclei project to the corresponding primary sensory and motor cortices, thereby mediating the input-relay functions [22]. In contrast, higher-order thalamic nuclei—including the pulvinar, mediodorsal, nucleus reuniens, centromedian, and parafascicular nuclei—primarily receive inputs from their reciprocally connected cortical regions, especially the association cortices. These nuclei project to areas such as the prefrontal and parietal cortices, and are involved in higher-order functions, including information integration, cognitive control, and coordination of distinct cortical networks [23, 24]. Emerging evidence further suggests that the high-order nuclei are implicated in neural circuits of emotion regulation relevant to anxiety and depression [25–29].

Given the essential role in sensorimotor integration, executive control, and emotion regulation [30–32], the thalamus has been extensively investigated in pathophysiology of ADHD. However, neuroimaging studies examining thalamic volume and morphological differences between individuals with ADHD and HC have yielded inconsistent findings [33–35]. For instance, Ivanov et al. [36] found bilateral thalamic volume reductions in youth with ADHD, with less pronounced deficits in those receiving pharmacological treatment, suggesting that effective intervention may partially ameliorate structural abnormalities. Albajara et al. [37] reported a significant reduction in right thalamic volume in children with ADHD compared with both children with autism spectrum disorder and HC. Conversely, several studies have reported no significant differences in thalamic volume between individuals with ADHD and HC [35, 38]. However, these prior investigations have treated the thalamus as a single, homogeneous structure, overlooking its complex internal organization and heterogeneity. To date, no study has systematically examined whether specific thalamic subregions differ volumetrically among ADHD subgroups characterized by distinct internalizing and externalizing symptom profiles. Moreover, thalamic development follows region-specific, age-dependent trajectories [39]: lateral thalamic compartments mature more rapidly than central nuclei, producing an outside-in developmental gradient [40]. These processes reach relative maturation in late adolescence [41]. Deviations from these normative patterns have been implicated in the pathogenesis of neurodevelopmental disorders such as ADHD [42–44]. Therefore, it is also essential to consider age as a critical factor in investigations of thalamic structures in ADHD.

This study aimed to elucidate volumetric alterations in thalamic subregions among patients with ADHD, focusing on distinct comorbidity profiles, and to assess whether these changes vary across developmental stages. We utilized a large-scale database comprising ADHD-only participants, healthy controls (HC), and ADHD individuals with comorbid internalizing or externalizing symptoms. By performing bilateral thalamic segmentation in all participants, we compared volumetric differences in thalamic subregions across groups. Additionally, age-related variations were also examined by stratifying participants into children and adolescents. We hypothesized that ADHD participants would exhibit significant volumetric alterations in specific thalamic subregions relative to HC, and that patterns of alteration would differ by distinct comorbidity profiles and age groups.

Materials and methods

Participants

All Participants used in this study were obtained from the Child Mind Institute’s Healthy Brain Network (HBN) project (https://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/cmi_healthy_brain_network) [45]. Initiated in 2015, this project plans to collect neuroimaging and behavioral data from 10,000 children and adolescents, with data resources released on a rolling basis. The primary objective is to develop novel diagnostic tools for detecting abnormal behaviors in the pediatric population. As a non-clinical study, no pharmacological interventions are administered to participants. Data for this study were obtained from Releases 1–10 of the publicly available HBN dataset, comprising a total of 3,384 participants.

Diagnoses for all ADHD participants were determined according to DSM criteria using the child version of the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia, the Child and Adolescent Psychiatric Assessment manual, and clinical judgment. Comorbid conditions (e.g., anxiety, depression, conduct disorder) were identified based on the secondary diagnoses [45]. HC group had no current or historic psychiatric disorders or related diagnoses. After screening, a total of 568 participants were included in the final analysis (mean age = 10.16 ± 3.15 years, range 5.02–21.19; 402 males), comprising 359 ADHD participants (mean age = 10.19 ± 2.94, range 5.06–20.27; 265 males) and 209 HCs (mean age = 10.12 ± 3.48, range 5.02–21.19; 137 males). Subsequently, participants with ADHD were further categorized into three subgroups based on their comorbidity symptom profiles. Participants without any comorbidity were placed in the ADHD-only group; those with a secondary diagnosis of anxiety or depression related disorders were assigned to the ADHD with internalizing symptom group (ADHD-inter); those with a secondary diagnosis of oppositional defiant disorder, impulse-control, conduct disorder or other specified disruptive disorders were classified into the ADHD with externalizing symptoms group (ADHD-exter). The final distribution of the three subgroups were as follows: ADHD-only (n = 168; mean age = 10.18 ± 3.25; 127 males), ADHD-inter (n = 97; mean age = 10.61 ± 2.67; 65 males), and ADHD-exter (n = 94; mean age = 9.78 ± 2.58; 73 males) (Table 1). No significant differences in age or sex were found among the ADHD subgroups.

Clinical symptom assessment

The Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL/6–18) [46] was employed to evaluate internalizing and externalizing problems, and this scale was filled out by children’s parents or primary caregivers. The CBCL contained 113 items rated on a 3-point Likert scale, capturing a broad range of behavioral and emotional problems. Internalizing and externalizing problems were identified as two broadband scales [47]. Internalizing problems included dimensions such as withdrawn/depressed, anxious/depressed and somatic complaints. Externalizing problems mainly encompassed outwardly directed behaviors, including rule-breaking behavior and aggressive behaviors. In addition, other symptom domains included social problems, thought problems, and attention problems. Due to incomplete CBCL data, 54 participants were excluded, resulting in a final subsample of 514 participants for comparisons of CBCL measures between the ADHD and HC groups and among ADHD subgroups.

Structure image processing and thalamic segmentation

Acquisition parameters of the structural images for the four scanning sites have been provided in the online HBN imaging protocol (https://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/cmi_healthy_brain_network/MRI_Protocol.html), and our supplemental materials. High-resolution 3D T1-weighted structural brain images were processed using the automated reconstruction pipeline in FreeSurfer 7.4.1 software. The processing steps included removal of non-brain tissue, automated Talairach transformation, segmentation of white matter and subcortical regions, and tessellation of the gray matter/white matter boundary. The pipeline subsequently generated accurate delineations of gray matter/cerebrospinal fluid boundary and gray matter/white matter boundaries. Based on these delineations, surface inflation and spherical registration were performed for each participant. For subsequent group-level statistical comparisons, individual data were aligned to the standard fsaverage template.

Then, segmentation of thalamic nuclei was conducted using the FreeSurfer’s automated thalamic nuclei segmentation tool. As shown in Fig. 2, the thalamus of each hemisphere was parcellated into 25 distinct nuclei [48]. The thalamic nuclei included ventral anterior nucleus, ventral anterior magnocellular, ventromedial nucleus, ventral lateral anterior nucleus, ventral lateral posterior nucleus, ventral posterolateral nucleus, anteroventral nucleus, laterodorsal nucleus, lateral posterior nucleus, centromedian nucleus, paracentral nucleus, central lateral nucleus, parafascicular nucleus, central medial nucleus, mediodorsal nucleus, intermediate part, mediodorsal medial magnocellular, reuniens (medial ventral), paratenial nucleus, pulvinar anterior nucleus, pulvinar lateral nucleus, pulvinar medial nucleus, pulvinar inferior nucleus, suprageniculate nucleus, lateral geniculate nucleus and medial geniculate nucleus. The volume of each nucleus, along with the estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV), was computed and extracted for subsequent statistical analyses. All segmentations were visually inspected and investigated for statistical outliers. Full details of the image exclusion criteria and quality assessments are described in Supplementary materials.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS. Demographics between groups were analyzed using t/F tests for continuous variables (age, eTIV) and chi square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables (sex, site). ANCOVA was conducted to compare CBCL scores across groups, with group as the variable of interest, and age, sex, and site as covariates. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. Post hoc analyses were performed for CBCL scores showing significant group effects. Statistical significance was defined as an FDR-corrected p < 0.05.

Differences of thalamic subregion volumes between ADHD patients and HC were examined using ANCOVA. Each subregion volume was regarded as the dependent variable, group was the variable of interest, and age, sex, site, and eTIV were included as covariates. We initially tested the interactions between group and hemisphere. For subregions showing no significant interaction, hemisphere was included as a fixed factor. For those with significant interactions, separate analyses of the left and right hemispheric volumes were performed. FDR correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons. Associations between CBCL components and thalamic subregion volumes within the ADHD group were tested using linear mixed models. The CBCL scores were the variable of interest; hemisphere was regarded as a fixed factor when the interaction between CBCL and hemisphere was not significant; age, sex, and eTIV were covariates. FDR correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons.

Subsequently, we tested differences of thalamic subregion volumes among the three ADHD groups (ADHD-inter, ADHD-exter, ADHD-only) and HC using ANCOVA. Subregion volumes were the dependent variables, group was the independent variable, hemisphere was regarded as a fixed factor when the interaction between group and hemisphere was not significant. Age, sex, site, and eTIV were included as covariates in all analyses. FDR correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons. Post hoc comparisons were performed across the four groups for significant thalamic subregions. Correction for the main effects was applied across all 25 thalamic nuclei, whereas post hoc comparisons were corrected only among those nuclei exhibiting significant main effects.

Finally, to examine age-related effects on thalamic volumes in ADHD, participants were stratified into children (5.02–9.77 years, n = 284) and adolescents (9.77–21.19 years, n = 284) based on the median age of the overall sample (9.77 years). Adolescent group included 189 ADHD patients (mean age = 12.42 ± 2.16, 138 males) and 95 HC (mean age = 13.15 ± 2.76, 80 males). Children group included 170 ADHD patients (mean age = 7.71 ± 1.18, 127 males) and 114 HC (mean age = 7.58 ± 1.32, 57 males). Within each age stratum, participants were further subclassified into four groups according to the above established clinical criteria. Subgroup sample sizes were as follows: adolescent group (ADHD-only, n = 81, mean age = 12.86 ± 2.53, 68 males; ADHD-inter, n = 63, mean age = 12.20 ± 1.74, 40 males; ADHD-exter, n = 45, mean age = 11.96 ± 1.84, 30 males; HC, n = 95, mean age = 13.15 ± 2.76, 80 males); children group (four subgroups: ADHD-only, n = 87, mean age = 7.68 ± 1.24, 59 males; ADHD-inter, n = 34, mean age = 7.68 ± 1.16, 25 males; ADHD-exter, n = 49, mean age = 7.78 ± 1.20, 43 males; HC, n = 114, mean age = 7.58 ± 1.32, 57 males). Corresponding subgroup comparisons within each age stratum between the ADHD group, ADHD subgroups and HC were also conducted using ANCOVA, following the same procedures described above.

Results

Clinical assessments

As shown in Table 1, ADHD participants had significantly higher CBCL subscale scores (all ps < 0.01) than those in the HC group. Subgroup analyses revealed distinct clinical profiles. Post hoc comparisons revealed that the ADHD-inter group had higher anxiety/depression scores than the ADHD-exter group, whereas the ADHD-exter group exhibited higher scores on social problems, rule-breaking behavior, aggressive behavior and externalizing problems (all ps < 0.01). Compared with the ADHD-only group, the ADHD-inter group had significantly higher scores on anxiety/depression, withdrawn, somatic complaints, social problems, thought problems, attention problems and internalizing problems (all ps < 0.05), Similarly, the ADHD-exter group had higher scores than the ADHD-only group on anxiety/depression, withdrawn, somatic complaints, social problems, thought problems, attention problems, rule-breaking behavior, aggressive behavior, internalizing problems and externalizing problems (all ps < 0.05).

Volumetric alterations in thalamic subregions and their correlation with clinical scores

No significant interactions on thalamic subregions were observed between group (ADHD vs. HC) and hemisphere or between CBCL internalizing/ externalizing scores and hemisphere (Table S1-2). Compared with HC, ADHD participants exhibited significantly larger volume in the pulvinar anterior (F = 10.48, η² < 0.01, p = 0.03), and smaller volume in the ventral anterior magnocellular (F = 7.77, η² = 0.01, p = 0.04), reuniens (medial ventral) (F = 7.42, η² = 0.01, p = 0.04), and anteroventral nucleus (F = 8.18, η² = 0.01, p = 0.04) (Table 2).

Different associations with thalamic subnuclei in children and adolescents

Age-specific analyses further highlighted differential patterns of thalamic subregional alterations. In adolescents (Table S4), three thalamic nuclei survived FDR correction among the comparisons between ADHD subgroups and HC group: the parafascicular (F = 4.50, η² = 0.02, p = 0.033), central lateral (F = 5.27, η² = 0.03, p = 0.017), and laterodorsal (F = 5.28, η² = 0.03, p = 0.017) nuclei. Post-hoc comparisons revealed that ADHD-only group exhibited significantly larger volumes in the central lateral (p = 0.034) and laterodorsal nuclei (p = 0.014), compared with the ADHD-inter group. While the ADHD-exter group had significantly larger volumes in the parafascicular nucleus compared with the ADHD-only group (p = 0.012) and HC group (p = 0.002). Moreover, when directly comparing the ADHD-inter and ADHD-exter groups, the ADHD-inter group consistently exhibited significant reductions in the parafascicular (p = 0.020), central lateral (p < 0.001), and laterodorsal (p = 0.001) nuclei. The ADHD-inter group also showed significantly smaller volumes in the central lateral (p = 0.015) and laterodorsal nuclei (p = 0.003) compared with the HC group. Notably, no significant volumetric differences were observed between the all ADHD group and HC participants (Table S5).

In the children group (Table S6), only the lateral geniculate nucleus (F = 5.04, η² = 0.03, p = 0.047) survived FDR correction. Post-hoc comparisons revealed that the ADHD-only group had a significantly larger volume than the ADHD-exter groups (p < 0.01). No significant volumetric differences were found between the ADHD group and HC participants (Table S7).

Discussion

The comorbidity of ADHD with internalizing and externalizing symptoms is closely linked to thalamic dysfunction. As a critical neural hub [49, 50], the thalamus integrates information across distributed cortical and subcortical networks. Its heterogeneous architecture consists of distinct nuclei, each with specialized connectivity patterns and functional roles [51]. Our findings identified volumetric differences in thalamic subregions among children and adolescents with ADHD, varying according to comorbidities profiles. These differences were evident not only between ADHD and HC but also between ADHD-inter and ADHD-exter groups, involving nuclei such as the pulvinar anterior, ventral anterior magnocellular, reuniens (medial ventral), central lateral, paracentral, and laterodorsal nuclei. Furthermore, negative correlations between CBCL subscale scores and volumes of specific thalamic subregions highlight the clinical relevance of these alterations. Age-stratified analyses further revealed developmental differences: in childhood, alterations were primarily confined to peripheral nuclei, whereas in adolescence, abnormalities extended to central nuclei. This pattern suggests a developmental progression in thalamic involvement, with distinct volumetric profiles across developmental stages.

Our findings revealed significant volumetric differences in specific thalamic subregions in ADHD. ADHD group exhibited increased pulvinar anterior nucleus volume than the HC group, whereas the ventral anterior magnocellular, reuniens (medial ventral), and anteroventral nuclei showed reductions. The pulvinar nucleus, as part of the thalamocortical network, integrates auditory and visual stimulus responses [52, 53], and support attentional orienting [54]. The ventral anterior thalamic nucleus receives corticostriatal inputs and projects to caudal motor areas, is associated with motor control and processing speed [34]. Anterior ventral nucleus primarily focuses on limbic system integration, linking emotional and executive functions [55]. The reuniens (medial ventral), a thalamic association nucleus critical for hippocampal-prefrontal cortical connectivity, participates in emotional regulation, memory, and cognitive control. Atrophy in this region has been linked to anxiety, depression, and cognitive dysfunction [56, 57]. An ENIGMA study further reported that individuals with ADHD exhibited significantly smaller volumes of the accumbens, amygdala, caudate, hippocampus, putamen, and intracranial cavity compared with HC. However, this large-scale analysis found no significant volumetric differences in the pallidum or thalamus between the ADHD and HC [35]. Several factors may account for this finding. First, the study included participants spanning a wide age range from childhood to late adulthood, and data were acquired across multiple sites, potentially introducing variability in image quality. Second, exposure to pharmacological treatment can influence thalamic volume [36]. Finally, structural alterations in ADHD may be more pronounced at the level of thalamic subregional morphology rather than overall thalamic volume.

Internalizing symptoms were negatively associated with the ventral posterolateral, pulvinar anterior, ventromedial, and ventrolateral posterior nuclei, linking volumetric changes to somatosensory processing, motor control, and higher-order cognitive and emotional functions [58–61]. These structural alterations may underlie anxiety, depression, dysphoria, emotional instability, and heightened sensitivity in ADHD. Additionally, the lateral geniculate nucleus, a key visual relay center, showed a negative correlation with externalizing symptoms, consistent with its role in visual processing, attentional modulation, and hierarchical integration [52]. It is noteworthy that, as reported above, the ADHD group exhibited significantly larger volumes in the pulvinar anterior nucleus compared with HC, which appears inconsistent with the observed negative correlation between internalizing symptoms and pulvinar anterior nucleus volume. This discrepancy may be attributable, in part, to differences in the sample size used for the correlation analyses versus the group-level ADHD–HC comparison. In addition, ADHD is not a unitary disorder; variations in subtype, comorbidities, or illness course may lead to divergent directions of volumetric changes in the pulvinar anterior nucleus. Whereas the group-level comparison reflects average differences between ADHD and HC participants, correlation analyses capture within-group trends. Collectively, these findings suggest that specific thalamic subregions contribute to both cognitive and emotional dysregulation in ADHD, with distinct patterns corresponding to internalizing versus externalizing symptomatology.

Comparisons between ADHD comorbidity subgroups revealed that the ADHD-inter group exhibited significantly reduced volumes in the reuniens (medial ventral), central lateral nucleus, paracentral nucleus, and laterodorsal nucleus compared with the ADHD-exter and HC group. Previous studies have reported that atrophy of the reuniens (medial ventral) nucleus is closely associated with anxiety, depression, and cognitive dysfunction [56, 57]. In line with these findings, our results revealed atrophy of this nucleus in the ADHD-inter group, with a trend toward further reduction compared with the ADHD-only group, suggesting that internalizing symptoms may be closely linked to pathological alterations of this thalamic subregion. The central lateral nucleus, which projects to both striatum and cortex and regulates arousal and consciousness [62, 63], was selectively reduced in ADHD-inter participants, suggesting a potential role of striatal dysfunction in internalizing symptoms. Although less studied, the paracentral nucleus contributes to orofacial proprioception via corticopetal projections [64], In the present study, its volume was significantly lower in the ADHD-inter group compared with both the ADHD-exter and HC groups, consistent with thalamic findings in major depressive disorder [65]. These results suggest that paracentral nucleus atrophy may be associated with internalizing symptomatology. The laterodorsal nucleus, composed mainly of association fibers, mediates thalamocortical information transfer and supports spatial learning and memory through connections with limbic structures such as the hippocampus [66, 67]. Consistently, laterodorsal nucleus volumes were smaller in the ADHD-inter group compared with both ADHD-exter, HC, and ADHD-only groups, potentially reflecting disrupted limbic connectivity. Furthermore, significant volumetric reductions were observed in the centromedian and anteroventral nuclei in the ADHD-inter group relative to HC. The centromedian nucleus is strongly associated with the striatum and motor control and is frequently highlighted in studies of externalizing behaviors; however, it also projects to limbic and basal ganglia structures, contributing to arousal and emotional integration [68]. The anteroventral nucleus, a member of the anterior thalamic nuclei, is deeply involved in learning, memory, motivation, and emotional processing [68], and volumetric abnormalities in this nucleus have been reported in depression and anxiety studies, supporting its sensitivity to internalizing symptomatology [69, 70].

We also observed age-specific effects on thalamic subregion volumes in ADHD patients with different comorbidities. In adolescents, the ADHD-inter group exhibited significantly reduced volumes in the central lateral and laterodorsal nuclei compared with the ADHD-exter groups, consistent with non-stratified analyses. The parafascicular nucleus, a major subcortical source of excitatory input to the basal ganglia projecting primarily to the striatum [71], mediates arousal and attention, and emerging evidence links it to action initiation [72]. We found that both the ADHD-inter and ADHD-only groups had smaller parafascicular volumes than the ADHD-exter group, suggesting that larger parafascicular volume may reflect heightened excitatory output that facilitates behavioral activation, thereby contributing to the expression of externalizing symptoms in ADHD. In contrast, among children, significant differences were restricted to the lateral geniculate nucleus, which provides specialized hierarchical input to the visual cortex via attentional modulation and binocular integration mechanisms. The ADHD-exter group showed significantly smaller lateral geniculate nucleus volumes than the ADHD-only group, consistent with the findings obtained without age stratification and with earlier reports showing a negative correlation between lateral geniculate nucleus volume and externalizing symptoms. The ADHD-inter group also exhibited a trend toward reduced lateral geniculate volume relative to the ADHD-only group, reaching marginal significance, indicating visual processing, integration, and attentional deficits in comorbid patients. Cross-age comparisons indicated that group differences were absent in children but emerged in adolescents, implying that thalamic subregional alterations in comorbid ADHD may vary across developmental stages. Analyses within both the children and adolescent cohorts revealed no significant differences in thalamic subregions between ADHD and HC groups after correction, which contrasts with the differences observed in the unstratified ADHD–HC comparison. This discrepancy may be attributable to reduced sample size, unequal group distributions, altered sex ratios, and median-based age stratification, which collectively may have limited statistical power to detect significant effects within each age group.

This study has certain limitations. First, the classification of internalizing and externalizing problems relied on diagnostic assessments at a single time point, which may not fully capture the dynamic nature of comorbid symptoms; longitudinal follow-up assessments could provide greater diagnostic accuracy. Second, this study focused on thalamic subregions without examining associated regions or related white matter tract pathways. Future studies integrating diffusion tensor imaging could validate abnormal fiber connections between thalamic subregions and target regions such as the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus. Finally, the cross-sectional design and age-stratified analysis based on median splits limit the ability to draw precise inferences regarding developmental trajectories. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the progression of thalamic alterations in ADHD, which could inform early intervention strategies.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that thalamic subregion volumes differ across comorbidity groups in children and adolescents with ADHD. Volumetric alterations were more pronounced in internalizing subgroups, with greater involvement of central nuclei during adolescence, whereas alterations in peripheral nuclei were mainly observed in childhood, suggesting that thalamic subregional changes in comorbid ADHD may vary across developmental stages. Additionally, significant negative correlations were found between thalamic subregion volumes and CBCL symptom scores, underscoring the clinical relevance of thalamic abnormalities in both internalizing and externalizing symptom dimensions. Together, these findings highlight the thalamus as a critical subcortical region contributing to the heterogeneity of ADHD and its comorbidities.
