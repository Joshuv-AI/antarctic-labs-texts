Role-SynthCLIP: A Role Play Driven Diverse Synthetic Data Approach
Yuanxiang Huangfu, Chaochao Wang, Weilei Wang

Abstract
The effectiveness of Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) models critically depends on the semantic diversity and quality of their training data. However, while existing synthetic data generation methods primarily focus on increasing data volume, such emphasis often leads to limited semantic diversity and redundant or shallow captions. To address this limitation, we propose Role-SynthCLIP , a novel data synthesis framework that leverages multi-perspective role-playing prompts (e.g., a compositional analyst, an interpreter of image context) to guide Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) in generating semantically diverse captions from distinct viewpoints. This mechanism enhances the semantic diversity and fine-grained image-text alignment of synthetic pairs, thereby improving caption expressiveness and accuracy while keeping the total number of image-text pairs unchanged. Experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness and efficiency of our method. A CLIP-B/16 model trained on only 1 million Role-SynthCLIP pairs achieves a Recall@1 of 64.1 % \mathbf{64.1\%} on the MS COCO validation set, surpassing the best existing synthetic data baseline (trained on 5M pairs) by 2.8 2.8 percentage points. The code and trained models are released at https://github.com/huangfu170/Role-SynthCLIP .



1
Introduction

The paradigm of vision-language models (VLMs), such as CLIP
Radford et al. 2021
, has profoundly reshaped multimodal learning by aligning vision and language representations
luo et al. 2023
. Traditionally, their success has relied on massive web-crawled datasets containing millions to billions of image-text pairs
Zhang et al. 2024
. However, this pursuit of data scale—from millions to billions of pairs—has created a critical bottleneck: the growing data volume demands prohibitive computational resources and inevitably incorporates noisy, low-quality, and redundant web samples
Sun et al. 2023
;
Fang et al. 2024
.

In this context, controllable high-quality synthetic data generation has emerged as a promising alternative to large-scale web scraping. Methods such as SynthCLIP
Hammoud et al. 2024
have demonstrated that competitive performance can be achieved with fully synthetic corpora of up to 30M pairs. Nevertheless, as attention shifts from data scale to data quality, a new challenge arises: semantic impoverishment. Captions generated by generic prompts often exhibit limited diversity: they tend to be uniform, single-perspective, and semantically repetitive, failing to capture the rich, multifaceted nature of visual content. This redundancy constrains the diversity and discriminative capacity of learned visual–language representations.

Our approach introduces role-play templates (e.g., Compositional Analyst, Narrative Setter), which guide the MLLM to perceive the image from diverse cognitive perspectives. This mechanism generates semantically rich captions that focus on fine-grained elements (e.g., specific objects, visual structure, context), maximizing the diversity of representations learned during VLM pre-training.

Existing efforts to improve synthetic data quality primarily target distributional balance or prompt variety. For instance, SynthCLIP
Hammoud et al. 2024
maintains entity distribution balance across the corpus. Other concurrent works, like FIX-CLIP
Wang et al. 2025
, attempt to capture diversity by employing ensembles of multiple generic prompts (e.g., 20 variants). However, these ensemble approaches lack cognitive depth: their variations are often shallow paraphrases that do not introduce distinct interpretative dimensions-such as the compositional, contextual, or emotional perspectives-of the same image.

To address this limitation, we propose
Role-SynthCLIP
, a novel framework that amplifies caption diversity by simulating multi-expert perspectives through role-playing prompts. As illustrated in Figure
1
, our method assigns distinct expert roles to a Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM). For instance, an
Observer of Details
highlights fine-grained attributes, while a
Compositional Analyst
focuses on global structure and visual organization. By combining these complementary perspectives, Role-SynthCLIP produces semantically rich and well-aligned captions that better reflect the complexity of visual scenes.

Our design is motivated by the proven effectiveness of role-playing prompting in LLMs, which has been shown to elicit diverse responses and improve task performance without architectural modifications or additional training
Zhao et al. 2025
;
Kong et al. 2024
. By extending this paradigm to MLLMs for caption generation, we can efficiently and flexibly steer the model to produce the desired semantic diversity.

In summary, the main contributions of this paper can be
summarized as threefold:

•

We propose Role-SynthCLIP, a novel data synthesis framework. By leveraging role-playing prompts, it enables an MLLM to perceive an image from diverse, multi-faceted perspectives (e.g., compositional, detailed, contextual), generating captions with superior semantic diversity and alignment.

•

We establish new state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance for data-efficient CLIP training. A CLIP-B/16 model trained on only 1M Role-SynthCLIP pairs attains a Recall@1 of 64.1% on COCO-val, surpassing the best 5M-pair synthetic baseline.

•

Our work includes a detailed ablation study and empirical analysis across 8 downstream tasks, providing deep insights into the crucial impact of various expert role combinations on the learned representation diversity and model performance.

2
Related Works

2.1
Vision-Language Models

Recent years have witnessed rapid progress in vision-language models (VLMs) that bridge visual perception and natural language understanding. Among them, Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining (CLIP)
Radford et al. 2021
;
Peleg et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Lai et al. 2024
has emerged as a representative framework, demonstrating how large-scale image-text alignment can lead to transferable multimodal representations.

Subsequent studies have investigated extensions of this paradigm, including scaling pretraining corpora
Sun et al. 2023
;
Fang et al. 2024
, improving robustness under distribution shifts
Nguyen et al. 2023
, and adapting CLIP-style embeddings for tasks that require multimodal reasoning tasks
Guzhov et al. 2021
;
Alyami and
Luqman 2025
;
Zhao et al. 2025
. Despite this progress, a key challenge remains in effectively increasing semantic richness and alignment of the training data, particularly when operating within a constrained training corpus or relying on synthetic generation. Our work specifically addresses this quality bottleneck in the constrained data budget.

2.2
Synthetic Captions for VLM training

Recent advances have demonstrated that caption diversity is paramount for improving the quality of contrastive pre-training. Prior work has explored data volume expansion
Xu et al. 2024
;
Chuang et al. 2025
, simple caption rewriting
Liu et al. 2024
, or linguistic transformations. More recently, with the development of large multimodal models, it has become possible to generate relevant captions for images
Lai et al. 2024
;
Zheng et al. 2024
. While works like SynthCLIP
Hammoud et al. 2024
focus on balancing entity distributions, and others like FIX-CLIP
Wang et al. 2025
use multiple fixed prompts for variations. However, these generative approaches are typically constrained by generic prompts. They produce repetitive and shallow descriptions from a narrow viewpoint, resulting in surface-level paraphrasing rather than deep cognitive diversity.

In contrast, our proposed Role-SynthCLIP systematically guides the MLLM through multi-experts role-playing prompts, enabling it to describe an image from diverse semantic perspectives. This approach unlocks the latent reasoning capacity of MLLMs to produce captions that are both fine-grained and semantically complementary.

2.3
Role-Play Prompting

Role-playing prompting is a powerful, training-free technique widely employed in Large Language Models (LLMs) across specialized domains, such as education
Dan et al. 2023
, healthcare
Abbasian et al. 2024
;
Yu et al. 2025
, and programming
Qian et al. 2024
. This mechanism explicitly defines a role’s expertise, enabling the model to instantiate a specific persona and activate its inherent, specialized knowledge, leading to substantially enhanced performance on target tasks
Kong et al. 2024
. While highly successful in text-only domains, the structured use of multi-role prompting for cross-modal data synthesis, particularly for enriching image–text pairs, remains largely unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, Role-SynthCLIP is the first framework to systematically extend multi-role prompting to multimodal large language models for generating semantically diverse and high-quality visual captions.

3
Methodology

Figure 2
:
Overview of our Role-SynthCLIP framework. We begin by sampling multiple distinct expert roles from a large language model, each with a defined specialty and set of responsibilities. Subsequently, these expert annotators describe an image from their specific professional viewpoint. Finally, we employ a filtering model to remove potential noise from the generated data, ensuring that the descriptions are both accurate to the image content and consistent with the assigned roles.

We formulate the task as synthesizing semantically diverse and accurate image-text pairs for contrastive training, given an image corpus
\mathcal{I}=\{I_{i}\}
without paired captions. As depicted in Figure
2
, the Role-SynthCLIP framework operates via a three-stage pipeline designed to synthesize semantically diverse and high-fidelity image-text pairs. The process begins with expert role generation to define specialized annotation personas. This is followed by expert captioner observation, where a multimodal large language model generates multi-perspective captions by strictly adhering to each role’s persona. Finally, a dedicated Role-Aware Filter rigorously validates and prunes the synthesized data to ensure both visual accuracy and role-consistency.

3.1
Expert Role Generation

A single image can be interpreted through many perspectives. However, instead of using randomly selected prompts, we argue that each prompt should be intentionally designed to focus on a specific aspect of capability, so that they can complement each other as a whole. Inspired by structured agent generation in LLM-Discussion
Zhao et al. 2025
, we design a two-stage process for constructing high-quality expert roles.

In the first stage, we prompt a LLM to propose an initial set of roles suitable for generating precise and informative image descriptions. Subsequently, a two-round conversational process is utilized to convert this high-level information into a structured format, which includes the expert’s name, their specific specialty, and their corresponding responsibilities. This two-stage approach ensures the roles are both specialized and syntactically consistent. These roles are strategically chosen to span critical dimensions of image understanding: fine-grained features (Observer), scene context (Interpreter), visual structure (Compositional Analyst), implied narrative (Narrative Setter), and subjective feeling (Emotional Responder). The specific prompts utilized are detailed in Appendix
7
.

3.2
Expert Captioner Observation

We utilize Qwen2.5 VL-7B
Bai et al. 2025
as our core multimodal captioner, chosen for its high visual grounding and instruction adherence capacity, to generate captions across varying granularities (short and long). To maximize semantic density, the MLLM strictly adheres to each role’s persona when generating multi-perspective captions. The specific prompts used for this process are provided in Appendix
8
.

To maintain data quality and prevent the introduction of generic or title-like noise, we employ a simple yet effective pre-filtering strategy based on caption length. Long captions with fewer than 10 words and short captions with fewer than 4 words are discarded. Through these straightforward yet effective methods, we obtained a multi-perspective synthetic dataset, in which the average word count of long captions is 92.4 and that of short captions is 17.7.

3.3
Role-Aware Filtering

Generated captions from MLLMs are inherently susceptible to visual inaccuracies, including category and attribute hallucination
Li et al. 2023
;
Bai et al. 2025
. Furthermore, Our multi-expert strategy further requires ensuring consistency with the assigned role’s perspective. To address this, we design a Role-Aware Filter.

Inspired by prior work on finetuned MLLM as validator
Wang et al. 2024
, we design a more rigorous and interpretable filtering mechanism for our Role-SynthCLIP. To instill highly reliable and interpretable judgment capacity into our Role-Aware Filter, we employ a sophisticated knowledge distillation strategy. We leverage a large, proprietary model, GPT-5, as the teacher, and fine-tune the lightweight Qwen2.5 VL-3B as the student model. The goal is to transfer the teacher’s nuanced ability to evaluate both visual fidelity and role-consistency. The training process involves two phases:

Teacher Data Generation.
We sample a diverse set of generated image-text pairs (
I,C,R
) and prompt the GPT-5 teacher, conditioned on the image, the caption, and the expert role’s responsibilities. The teacher is instructed to output two correlated responses: a relevance score
S_{\text{GPT-5}}
(e.g., a 1-100 continuous value) and a detailed rationale
T_{\text{rationale}}
explaining the judgment based on the three inputs.

Multi-Task Knowledge Distillation.
The Qwen2.5 VL-3B student is then fine-tuned via a multi-task objective to simultaneously mimic the teacher’s score and generate the corresponding rationale. This dual-task training extends beyond standard scoring-based filtering, ensuring the student learns what to filter and why.

Our filter lies in its multi-input structure (three-way conditioning): the validator is simultaneously conditioned on the Image (
I
), the generated Caption (
C
), and the expert role’s specific Responsibilities (
R
). This allows the filter to score not only visual accuracy but, critically, the semantic consistency with the required perspective.

The relevance score is formally quantified as:

\text{Relevance Score}=MLLM_{filter}(I,C,R)

The MLLM filter takes the concatenation of the three inputs (
I
,
C
, and
R
) to produce a final relevance score, ensuring rigorous validation against the specific prompt constraints. Based on these scores, a fixed percentage of image–text pairs with the lowest relevance are filtered out to enhance overall dataset quality. The design of the filter prompt is presented in Appendix
9
, while The detailed pseudocode describing the filtering process is provided in Appendix
10
.

3.4
Training Objective and Sequence Length Adaptation

Standard CLIP model was trained on relatively short captions, with its text encoder supporting a maximum sequence length of only 77 tokens. Given that our synthetic long captions average over 90 words, we adopt the positional embedding extension strategy from Long-CLIP
Zhang et al. 2024
to fully utilize the richer semantic content. This approach involves freezing the original positional embeddings
{PE}_{\text{orig}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
for the first 20 tokens and extending the embeddings for subsequent tokens via linear interpolation, based on the observation that initial tokens carry disproportionately critical information. The extension of the positional embedding
{PE}_{\text{long}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
is formally defined as:

PE_{\text{long}}=\text{Concat}(PE_{\text{orig}}[:20],\text{Intpol}(PE_{\text{orig}}[20:],q))

where
\mathrm{Intpol}
denotes the linear interpolation function:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Intpol}(PE,q)[i]

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\,PE_{\mathrm{orig}}[j]+\lambda\,PE_{\mathrm{orig}}[j+1],

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=\tfrac{i\bmod q}{q},\hskip 20.00003ptj=\big\lfloor i/q\big\rfloor.

where
\operatorname{Intpol}
denotes the linear interpolation function, the symbol
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
denotes the floor function. The variables
q
and
i
respectively represent the index of the interpolation ratio and the interpolation position, while
\lambda
denotes a specified weight.

By this means, the CLIP model is capable of supporting text inputs of up to 248 tokens, effectively allowing it to capture features from our high-density, long-form text. To further exploit the multi-caption characteristics of our synthesized dataset, we modify the standard CLIP contrastive objective into a multi-positive variant.
Instead of assigning a single one-hot label for each image–text pair, we treat all captions belonging to the same image as valid positive samples.
Formally, given a batch of
B
image–text pairs and an image–caption correspondence matrix
M\in\{0,1\}^{B\times B}
where
M_{ij}=1
if image
i
and text
j
originate from the same image, the image-to-text loss is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{i2t}=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\frac{M_{ij}}{\sum_{k}M_{ik}}\log\frac{\exp{(s_{ij}/\tau)}}{\sum_{l}\exp(s_{il}/\tau)}

(1)

and the text-to-image loss
\mathcal{L}_{t2i}
is computed symmetrically.
The final training objective is
\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}_{i2t}+\mathcal{L}_{t2i})
.
This variant encourages all captions of the same image to be simultaneously aligned with the corresponding visual embedding, avoiding the false-negative problem inherent to one-hot contrastive objectives when multiple semantically valid captions exist.

From a probabilistic standpoint, the necessity of this modification can be justified by analyzing the expected batch collision rate under our dataset scale.
Given approximately 1M unique images and a global batch size of 2048, the probability that at least two captions from the same image appear within the same batch exceeds about 80% (following a birthday-paradox approximation
Pollanen 2024
).
Therefore, the one-hot objective would incorrectly penalize genuine positive pairs as negatives in the majority of batches, leading to representation inconsistency and reduced training stability.
By adopting the multi-positive formulation, we explicitly correct this bias, ensuring that all semantically equivalent captions reinforce—rather than compete with—each other during contrastive optimization.

4.1
Experimental Setup

Datasets.

To comprehensively evaluate Role-SynthCLIP, we utilize ShareGPT4V
Chen et al. 2023
as our training dataset, which contains 1M images and is used in Long-CLIP
Zhang et al. 2024
and FIX-CLIP
Wang et al. 2025
. We conduct extensive evaluations on widely-used benchmarks, including both zero-shot retrieval and classification.

For the retrieval task, we evaluate our method on MS-COCO
Chen et al. 2015
, Flickr-1k
Young et al. 2014
, Urban-1k
Zhang et al. 2024
and report the Recall@1, which is selected to assess the model’s ability to align image and text representations under diverse domains. For classification benchmarks, we utilize commonly used datasets ImageNet-1k
Russakovsky et al. 2015
, ImageNet-O
Srivastava et al. 2022
, CIFAR-10
Krizhevsky 2009
, CIFAR-100
Krizhevsky 2009
and Food-101
Bossard et al. 2014
and report the top-1 accuracy (Acc@1), which measures generalization and robustness, particularly under out-of-distribution (OOD) shifts.

Baseline Models.
We select a variety of models as baselines for comparison, including OpenAI CLIP-ViT-base-patch16
Radford et al. 2021
and CLIP-ViT-large-patch14, the context-extended method Long-CLIP
Zhang et al. 2024
with positional extension, and the structurally improved method FIX-CLIP
Wang et al. 2025
built upon it.

FIX-CLIP is evaluated under different training scales. To demonstrate the effectiveness of our method, we choose its experimental results on 1M and 5M datasets for comparison. We also include SynthCLIP
Hammoud et al. 2024
, which trains CLIP entirely on synthetic image-text pairs generated via LLM-guided text-to-image synthesis, serving as a strong fully-synthetic baseline.

Finally, we further consider approaches with alternative training objectives, such as SigLIP
Zhai et al. 2023
and LoTLIP
Wu et al. 2024
, ensuring a fair and diverse comparison against state-of-the-art methods.

Training setup.

To ensure a fair comparison, our experimental setup is consistent with the methodology of Long-CLIP
Zhang et al. 2024
. Unless otherwise specified, our models are trained on the ShareGPT4V
Chen et al. 2023
dataset, which consists of 1M images.

We employ two canonical variants of CLIP in our experiments, which represent different model scales and allow us to assess robustness across capacities, both of which use a standard Transformer as the text encoder. The input images are resized to 224
\times
224. Following the methodology of Long-CLIP
Zhang et al. 2024
, captions are truncated or padded to 248 tokens. Training is conducted on 8 A800 GPUs with Accelerate and Deepspeed
Rajbhandari et al. 2020
. All hyperparameters (learning rate, warmup steps, weight decay, training epochs) are kept consistent with Long-CLIP
Zhang et al. 2024
for comparability.

4.2
Experimental Results

In this section, we present a detailed evaluation of the Role-SynthCLIP framework on several public benchmarks, including zero-shot multimodal retrieval and classification. We conduct a detailed comparison against state-of-the-art methods to demonstrate the effectiveness and efficiency of our synthetic data approach.

Zero-shot Retrieval.

Our results demonstrate that Role-SynthCLIP establishes a new state-of-the-art in low-resource CLIP training. For the CLIP-B/16 architecture, Role-SynthCLIP achieves an average Recall@1 of 77.01% across all six metrics. This represents a substantial improvement over methods trained on comparable 1M data volumes, such as Long-CLIP (+7.48% absolute gain), and significantly surpasses the performance of models trained on vastly larger, less curated corpora, including FIX-CLIP (5M, 75.95%), SigLIP (12B), and LoTLIP (100M). This empirically validates our core hypothesis: quality, curated diversity effectively overcomes the reliance on data volume.

The trend is amplified in the CLIP-L/14 setting, where Role-SynthCLIP achieves an average Recall@1 of 80.43%, outperforming the best comparable 1M baseline (Long-CLIP) by 5.85%.

We note that the improvement in Text-to-Image (T2I) retrieval is more moderate, particularly on COCO (43.2% R@1 for B/16), falling behind the 5M FIX-CLIP result. This observation points to a semantic distillation bottleneck in the text encoder. Although Role-SynthCLIP provides richer input, the fixed CLIP text encoder (especially ViT-B/16) struggles to compress the dense, multi-perspective semantic content into a single discriminative feature vector for T2I tasks.

Zero-shot Classification.

For the CLIP-B/16 model, Role-SynthCLIP achieves average accuracy (69.62%) nearly matching the performance of the original CLIP (70.30%). Importantly, our method exhibits superior performance on Out-of-Distribution (OOD) datasets, achieving the highest accuracy on ImageNet-O (44.5%) and competitive results on CIFAR-10/100. This suggests that the multi-perspective captions, by capturing diverse attributes beyond simple category names, successfully train a more robust and generalizable feature space capable of handling domain shifts.

In the CLIP-L/14 setting, Role-SynthCLIP attains an average accuracy of 73.56%, slightly below the original CLIP (73.96%). Notably, it achieves the best accuracy on ImageNet-O (36.4%), suggesting that our data augmentation contributes positively to robustness and semantic diversity for challenging OOD tasks, even without yielding the overall highest accuracy.

Impact of Caption Generation Strategies.

The generation of high-quality short captions is crucial for traditional CLIP training, which often prioritizes core, objective semantics. While our primary method utilizes Role-SynthCLIP (Direct Gen.), employing Qwen2.5 VL to generate both long and short captions simultaneously, we conducted an ablation study to compare this strategy against three important baselines based on existing approaches:

First Sentence
: Following the approach of Long-CLIP
Zhang et al. 2024
, we used the long captions from the original ShareGPT4V dataset and extracted their first sentence to serve as the short caption. This strategy assumes the most critical information is at the beginning.

Summarization
: We utilized the long captions from the original ShareGPT4V dataset and employed a Large Language Model (LLM) to perform abstractive summarization, generating the short captions. This tests the benefit of abstractive information distillation.

Random Extract
: A sentence was randomly selected from the long caption to serve as the short caption. This acts as a simple control to quantify the contribution of specific extractive strategies.

Multi Prompts
: Referring to FIX-CLIP
Wang et al. 2025
, use multiple short prompts, and randomly select a prompt during generation to create the caption.

The results of this ablation (detailed in Table
4
) validate that the Role-SynthCLIP (Direct Gen.) approach, by guiding the MLLM to generate concise, perspective-aware captions from scratch, yields superior performance compared to post-processing or reliance on extractive baselines.

5
Discussion

We aim to address two key questions that impact the experimental outcomes: (i) how role-playing affects the caption sampling results, and (ii) how our method influences the model’s performance.

5.1
How role-play influences the experts’ behavior

Our analysis reveals that role play fundamentally restructures how multimodal models allocate attention across the visual and linguistic modalities. Using token-level attention visualization (TAM)
Li et al. 2025
to probe the inner mechanisms of the multimodal large language model (MLLM), we observe a systematic shift in image-oriented attention patterns depending on the semantic abstraction level of the assigned role.

Figure 4
:
Role-conditioned attention behavior in Role-SynthCLIP. Token Activation Map (TAM) visualizations compare caption token “buildings” produced under three expert roles Narrative or Scene Setter, Emotional Responder, and Compositional Analyst-against the original image.

Our analysis of token-level attention patterns reveals intriguing insights into how different expert roles in Role-SynthCLIP influence attention distribution across image and text modalities. Specifically, we observe that roles focused on abstract concepts, such as the Narrative or Scene Setter and emotional perspectives (e.g., Emotional Responder), allocate significantly more attention to the image compared to roles that focus on lower-level semantic elements, such as compositional analysis.

As illustrated in Figure
4
, the Narrative Setter, tasked with generating captions that embody storytelling elements, demonstrates a notable increase in attention to visual cues that relate to the overall structure or emotional context of the scene. This tendency aligns with the need for the model to integrate multiple aspects of the visual content in a more abstract, global manner. On the other hand, roles focused on more compositional aspects, such as the Compositional Analyst, display a more restrained attention allocation to the visual domain, even when using similar or identical words. Its task is not to describe the buildings’ appearance, but their spatial relationship to other elements. This focus on relational structure, rather than holistic context, appears to demand a more localized and specific attentional focus.

This pattern suggests that attention distribution is closely tied to the cognitive task assigned to each expert role. Abstract, higher-level concepts often require a broader, more integrated view of the scene, which leads to increased attention across the image. In contrast, tasks such as compositional analysis, which involve focusing on specific visual structures or smaller image components, result in more localized attention.

These findings underscore the importance of role-playing in steering the model’s attention toward different levels of semantic richness. The ability to simulate multiple expert perspectives, each emphasizing distinct aspects of the visual content, not only enhances caption diversity but also improves the model’s understanding of complex visual phenomena.

5.2
Qualitative Visualization Analysis between CLIP and Role-SynthCLIP

We further compare attention distributions between Role-SynthCLIP and the original CLIP to understand how role play affects visual grounding. As shown in Figure

, Role-SynthCLIP consistently exhibits more semantically aligned and context-sensitive attention patterns.

In the first case (a white dog sleeping on a cobblestone street), our model focuses sharply on the foreground subject and architectural contours along the street, while CLIP disperses its attention over irrelevant background regions. This indicates that the multi-role synthesis effectively guides the model to prioritize visually and semantically coherent cues.

In the second case (a group of people at a bus stop), Role-SynthCLIP shifts its focus toward the background crowd and human interactions, capturing the social and narrative context that CLIP largely overlooks. The model demonstrates an enhanced ability to integrate relational semantics and global scene understanding rather than fixating on isolated objects.

Overall, these observations suggest that role-driven data generation restructures the model’s attention hierarchy. Instead of static saliency, Role-SynthCLIP dynamically allocates attention according to the underlying cognitive intent implied by each role, balancing detailed perception with contextual reasoning. This adaptability underscores the key advantage of role play in fostering deeper and more flexible vision–language alignment.

6
Conclusion

We introduced Role-SynthCLIP, a novel framework that leverages role-playing prompts to generate high-quality synthetic data for vision-language models. By simulating diverse expert perspectives, our method enhances the semantic diversity and quality of captions, addressing common issues of data noise and redundancy in large-scale datasets. Experimental results demonstrate that Role-SynthCLIP achieves state-of-the-art performance with only 1M synthetic pairs, surpassing models trained on significantly larger datasets. This work provides a data-efficient solution for training vision-language models, offering new avenues for controlled data generation and advancing the performance of multimodal tasks.

That’s a really interesting question! When people try to boost the precision of image descriptions, they often use different roles or personas to look at the picture from complementary perspectives. This technique is used in annotation, accessibility work, and in multimodal AI prompting to generate richer and more precise outputs.

Here are some typical roles that can help:

1. The Observer of Details
Focuses on the specific visual attributes of an image such as objects, colors, textures, shapes, and lighting conditions. Strong emphasis on capturing factual, observable details.

2. Interpreter of Context
Identifies the possible meaning of visual elements, interpreting human expressions, implied actions, cultural references, and the situational context of the image.

3. Compositional Analyst
Examines the overall structure of the image, including arrangement of subjects, balance, perspective, depth, and spatial relationships. Highlights the visual organization and framing.

4. Narrative or Scene Setter
Synthesizes observed details and context to suggest a narrative, identifying the setting, possible actions, implied storylines, and the overall communicative intent of the image.

5. Emotional or Aesthetic Responder
Focuses on the image’s emotional resonance and aesthetic qualities, capturing mood, atmosphere, symbolic associations, and the subjective impression it may evoke in viewers.

The idea is that by combining multiple roles, you get a layered and more accurate description—moving from raw perception to interpretation, while recognizing where assumptions end and evidence begins.

Text Image Pair Filter Prompt

Captions generated by multimodal large language models (MLLMs) may contain two types of errors:
(i) inconsistencies between image and text, and
(ii) deviation from the assigned role.

To address this, we leverage the teacher model GPT-5 to obtain training data containing both scores and reasoning paths. Using this data, we train a smaller and more cost-efficient filtering model to score image-text pairs. The filtering prompts used are shown in Table

.

Prompt for VLM to score an image-text pair

Here is Text Caption from a {agent role}, which role is {agent role prompt}

Text Caption: {caption}

Please evaluate if the provided text caption accurately represents the main features and objects of the image. The caption doesn’t need to detail every aspect of the image, but it should capture its primary theme. Rate the overall quality of the text caption’s match to the image on a scale of 1-100, considering the criteria mentioned. A higher score indicates higher level of image text matching.

Ensure that your scoring is nuanced and uses the entire range from 0 to 100, reflecting the subtle differences. The score should be given as an integer, with each number between 0 and 100 considered as a potential score, avoiding the tendency to round to multiples of 5 or 10. Please first output a single line containing the value indicating the scores. You must consider the role of the captioner when evaluating the caption. In the subsequent line, please provide a comprehensive explanation of your evaluation, avoiding any potential bias.
