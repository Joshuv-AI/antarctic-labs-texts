The impact of pathological fluctuations versus biological variation on the interpretation of laboratory values
Authors: Ariel Mundo Ortiz, Jean-Philippe Emond, Vincent Weng-Jy Cheung, Sahar Saeed, Philippe Desmarais, François Larivière, Pierre-Olivier Hétu, Robert Goulden, Quoc Dinh Nguyen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe current criterion used to determine whether the reference interval (RI) can be used for interpretation is based on the index of individuality (II), estimated using biological variation (BV). We hypothesized that pathological variation (PV), the shift between healthy and unhealthy states, varies across biomarkers and may be considered for interpretation with BV. We explored how jointly considering PV and BV impacts the clinical interpretation (diagnostic sensitivity and specificity) of RIs.MethodsWe propose the index of pathology (IP), a ratio of within-to between-subject coefficients of variation that jointly considers PV and BV. Using a large EHR database from a tertiary care center, we obtained IP estimates for 19 laboratory tests. As a means of comparison, the II was obtained from the European Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine (EFLM) BV database. PV impact was analyzed using the absolute difference between IP and II (ΔIP-II).Results798,800 observations from 17,082 adult patients were analyzed. For most biomarkers, the IP (mean = 1.99, range = 0.55–8.03) differed from the II (mean = 0.54, range = 0.27–0.86). Lowest IPs were for creatinine (IP = 0.55, ΔIP-II = 0.28) and bilirubin (IP = 1.05, ΔIP-II = 0.24). Highest IPs were for aspartate transaminase (IP = 4.56, ΔIP-II = 4.13) and creatine kinase (IP = 8.03, ΔIP-II = 7.60). Hormones and proteins exhibited high PV impact (ΔIP-II>1.0).ConclusionDifferences between variational estimates that only account for healthy states (II-BV) and those that consider healthy and unhealthy states (IP-BV + PV) vary widely among biomarkers, highlighting the differential impact of PV on their interpretation. For biomarkers where IP is high, the RI may be useful to identify unhealthy individuals.Keywords: Laboratory tests, Biological variation, Index of individuality

Introduction

The biological variation (BV) of a biomarker, composed of within-subject variation, the variation in the same individual around a certain homeostatic set value, and between-subject variation, the variation caused by differences among individuals, should be considered to interpret a result in an individual [1,2]. BV is used to define the index of individuality (II), a ratio of the within-subject coefficient of variation to the between-subject coefficient of variation to determine when the reference interval (RI) is useful for clinical interpretation [3].

According to the II criterion, if the II indicates a relatively low within-subject variance with respect to the between-subject variance (II < 0.6), a result cannot be compared to the RI to determine the health state of an individual, as the RI is insensitive to significant individual changes. In such case, Harris recommended the use of an individual's normal range for interpretation [3]. On the other hand, if within-subject variance is high relative to the between-subject variance (II > 1.4), the II criterion establishes that the RI can be used to detect disease as it captures the variation of each individual well [4,5].

Traditionally, the within and between-subject variational estimates used to calculate the II are obtained from healthy individuals [6]. Determining the usefulness of the RI to identify the health state of an individual using only BV, however, neglects the variation beyond the healthy state that may be specific to each biomarker. In the clinical context, where individuals change health states over time, biomarker results are used to differentiate individuals in a healthy state from those in an unhealthy state [7,8]. Considering only the information provided during healthy states might not be fully informative to identify whether the RI can be used to correctly identify an individual's health state [9]. As has previously been suggested, information beyond the healthy state may improve diagnosis and clinical decision-making [9,10].

Fluctuations in a biomarker between health states, which we will refer to as pathological variation (PV), might provide additional information for the interpretation of a biomarker result based on the RI. Pathological variation is distinct from BV as it measures the shift in a biomarker between healthy and unhealthy states, and not the level of variation that exists once a health state is reached and maintained. Because each biomarker has a different level of biochemical and physiological variation, the magnitude of PV relative to BV is expected to be different across biomarkers, as shown in Fig. 1. In this hypothetical scenario, we assume that analytical variation is negligible compared to the between- and within-individual variation in the healthy state and to the variation between health states (PV) [3,4]. In each case, each subject has a total of 10 observations during healthy and unhealthy states. For biomarker A, within-subject variation in the healthy state is low (a low II value) and mean values in healthy and unhealthy states are close (Fig. 1A). Therefore, PV is relatively small and healthy and unhealthy values fall within the RI (Fig. 1B).

Consider a different scenario for biomarker B, presented in Fig. 1C. This biomarker exhibits, as biomarker A, a low level of within-subject variance in the healthy state (II < 0.6) and values in a healthy state fall within the RI (Fig. 1D). However, there is a high level of fluctuation between healthy and unhealthy states, and thus, a large PV for each subject. Although the low II based only on the healthy state would indicate that the RI would not be appropriate for interpretation, the large level of PV allows the RI to distinguish between unhealthy and healthy states (Fig. 1D).

In the case of biomarker A, where the effect of PV is small, the “II” is expected to be low even if BV and PV are jointly considered. However, for biomarker B, where PV is large, the “II” significantly increases when BV and PV are jointly considered. Using information beyond a healthy state can change the interpretation of a result outside a RI. Although the limitations of the II criterion for identifying unhealthy states have been previously explored [11], so far no analysis has examined the impact of jointly considering PV and BV.

Information that jointly accounts for BV and PV cannot be obtained from traditional BV studies but may be obtained from laboratory data contained in electronic health records (EHRs) which include healthy and unhealthy patients, whose health states vary over time. To date, rather than exploring the impact of PV, most studies have used EHRs as data sources to estimate indirect RIs [[12], [13], [14], [15]] or to obtain BV estimates from individuals in a healthy state [[16], [17], [18], [19]].

In this study, we hypothesized that PV varied across laboratory tests thus impacting the clinical interpretation of the RI. To examine this hypothesis, we pursued three objectives: 1) to define and estimate the index of pathology (IP), a ratio of the within-subject to the between-subject coefficients of variation for biomarkers that jointly captures PV and BV 2) to compare the IP to the II, obtained from the European Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine (EFLM) [20], and 3) to explore how the IP alters the clinical interpretation of the RI for biomarkers with important differences between the IP and the II.

Methods

Data

We obtained laboratory data from the Center for the Integration and Analysis of Medical Data (CITADEL) data lake of the Centre hospitalier de l’Université de Montréal (CHUM). Briefly, CITADEL data contains information of more than 30 data systems holding unstructured and structured raw data of all digitized information (clinical notes, vital signs, laboratory test results, imaging, medications, etc.) from every single care episode at CHUM since 2000.

We collected the following laboratory results from adults aged 18 years and older: albumin, alkaline phosphatase, alanine transaminase, aspartate transaminase, bilirubin (total), c-reactive protein, calcium (corrected), creatine kinase, creatinine, random glucose, hemoglobin, magnesium, phosphorus, platelet count, potassium, sodium, high-sensitivity troponin-T, thyroid-stimulating hormone, and white blood cell count. Raw observations across all tests comprised a total of 24,551,954 values.

Data cleaning

For each laboratory test, we standardized units of measure, used expert medical advice (QDN, JPE) to remove or correct results that included text, symbols, or that were physiologically implausible (11,017 observations, 0.045 % of the total), and removed extreme values above the 99.99 percentile (2336 observations, 0.009 % of the total). Since we aimed to capture the variation in individuals across health states over time, we included laboratory results from inpatient and emergency visits (23,954,715 observations). The study complied with the guidelines of the Helsinki declaration and was approved by the CHUM Research Ethics Board under protocol MP-02-2023-10796.

Data sampling

From the cleaned data, we kept observations between January 2012 and December 2021 for most tests (except troponin-T) from adults aged 18 years and older at the date of the test, and that had at least one test result taken in 5 different months in the last three years. For troponin-T, the period considered was from January 2012 to April 2021, the last month before the full implementation of troponin-I. In EHR laboratory data, results from an individual obtained between some hours and a few days are likely to originate from a unique health state [21]. To sample values that jointly captured the change in health state (PV) and BV for each individual, a set time interval was required between repeated observations. We chose a 1-month interval between repeated observations [22] and randomly sampled one observation per patient for each of the five most recent months (consecutive or not) where at least one test result was available. Each patient had five sampled observations, as suggested to examine individual changes in biomarker data [23].

The sampling strategy is presented in Fig. 2, using the previous hypothetical biomarker B from Fig. 1 for three individuals. Each subject has 10 values in the healthy and unhealthy states. A sampling strategy termed “Healthy/Stable (BV)” in the figure represents values that would be captured when considering only the healthy state, as in traditional BV studies. Such strategy does not capture PV. The sampling strategy we used, termed “1-month interval (BV + PV)” results in five values per subject that, due to the 1-month interval chosen between repeated observations, can capture values from healthy and unhealthy states, as well as the fluctuation between states (PV).

Index of pathology (IP)

We defined the index of pathology (IP) as the ratio of the within-subject and between-subject coefficients of variation in a biomarker when these coefficients are obtained under the following conditions: 1) individuals in healthy or unhealthy states are considered, 2) healthy and unhealthy states are captured at the individual level by using an adequate sampling strategy. The IP formulation is similar to the simplified version of the II, where analytical and circadian variation are assumed to be negligible [4]. An example of IP computation for a biomarker is provided in the Supplementary Material.

Although EHRs are a valuable source of information, the ability to identify the precise health state of an individual is limited. Because healthy and unhealthy states cannot be readily identified, we sought to jointly capture BV and PV in the IP instead of attempting to estimate them separately. If a biomarker's PV is large, it will be the largest contributor to the within-subject variance.

In biomarkers where PV is low as in biomarker A in Fig. 1, the IP will have a numerical value similar to the II. However, in biomarkers where PV is relatively large when compared to BV (such as biomarker B in Fig. 1), the IP serves as a potential measure of the impact of PV. To obtain the IP, we used the sampled values obtained from the sampling strategy “1-month interval (BV + PV)”.

Statistical analyses

For each biomarker, we fitted random-intercept-only linear mixed model by patient to obtain the within- and between-subject coefficients of variation (CVI and CVG, respectively) [24,25] which were used to calculate the IP. We estimated the II as CVI/CVG [4] using within- and between-subject coefficients of variation from the EFLM BV database [20]. For each biomarker, we estimated the absolute difference between the IP and II (ΔIP-II). We ordered biomarkers using the ΔIP-II, dividing them in tertiles to identify categories for PV impact: low, medium, and high. All analyses were performed in R version 4.3.2, using the library lme4 [26] for statistical analyses.

Discussion

In this study, we estimated the IP, which jointly considers BV and PV for selected biomarkers by including individuals whose health states fluctuate over time using EHR data. We compared the IP to the II, obtained from the EFLM BV database, derived from studies on healthy or stable individuals. Our results show that for bilirubin (total), creatinine, hemoglobin, albumin, sodium, and calcium (corrected) the impact of PV as relatively low as ΔIP-II < 0.5. A moderate effect of PV was found for platelet count, white blood cell count, alkaline phosphatase, glucose, magnesium, phosphorus, and potassium, where ΔIP-II ranged between 0.5 and 1.01. C-reactive protein, thyroid-stimulating hormone, alanine transaminase, troponin T, aspartate transaminase, and creatine kinase exhibited the highest PV impact, ΔIP-II > 1.01.

Two complementary reasons may explain the differing levels of agreement in our results: 1) the relative magnitude of PV, and 2) the level of physiological variation that is possible for each biomarker. First, for some laboratory tests the range of variation in an unhealthy state for an individual remains numerically close to the range of variation in a healthy state. Such behavior is exemplified by biomarker A in Fig. 1, which would be representative of total bilirubin [27,28] creatinine [29], albumin [30,31], glucose [32], and white blood cell count [33], all of which exhibited low to moderate PV impact (ΔIP-II ≤ 0.72). Second, the level of physiologically possible variation that exists is different among biomarkers. For some biomarkers this possible level of variation is relatively narrow, as in the case of the electrolytes considered in our analysis [[34], [35], [36]]. Because these biomarkers cannot have extremely high or extremely low values that are compatible with life, their potential magnitude of variation is relatively small, thus limiting PV.

For other biomarkers, such as most of the hormones and enzymes analyzed where PV impact was high (ΔIP-II > 1.01), the physiologically possible level of variation is larger than for the biomarkers previously discussed [37,38]. In particular, during unhealthy states the percentage of change between the highest and lowest value can vary significantly [39], with values being many times above the upper RI threshold [40]. Our results suggest that this large level of fluctuation made the effect of PV significant for these biomarkers.

Clinical interpretation

Because BV describes the variation present in a healthy state, the usefulness of the RI to interpret a laboratory test using the II is more indicative of specificity (negative results among the healthy). When the II is high, the likelihood that individuals with a result within the RI do not suffer from a health condition is also high.

The difference between IP and II captures the contrast between the joint consideration of BV and PV and only BV. A large difference is indicative of the specific impact of PV and reflects the separation between unhealthy and healthy states. This difference, because it accounts for unhealthy states, is more indicative of sensitivity (positive results among the unhealthy), as shown in Fig. 3. When PV is small, a significant portion of unhealthy individuals lie below the RI cutoff and, consequently, the proportion of true positives is reduced, resulting in low sensitivity (Fig. 3A). Conversely, if PV is large, the number of true positives increases, and the RI (based only on healthy states) will be more sensitive (Fig. 3B).

Our results suggest that the RI would be most sensitive for c-reactive protein, thyroid-stimulating hormone, troponin-T, aspartate transaminase, alanine transaminase, and creatine kinase, as these tests have the largest IP. Such high sensitivity is explained by the previously discussed high level of possible physiological variation in these tests. Based on IP, the RI would be sensitive as well, but to a lesser degree for white blood cell count, glucose, sodium, calcium (corrected), magnesium, phosphorus, and potassium.

Finally, the RI would have low sensitivity in laboratory tests where IP was the lowest: Bilirubin (total), creatinine, platelet count, alkaline phosphatase, hemoglobin, and albumin. For these tests the low RI sensitivity requires that previous patients’ values are considered to increase the probability of correctly identifying an unhealthy state. Our results are in accordance with guidelines for the interpretation of creatinine results using baseline patient values [41], and the limitations of the RI that have been identified for hemoglobin [42], platelet count [43], and alkaline phosphatase [42].

Limitations

This work has limitations that need to be acknowledged. First, the IP, which is a joint estimate of PV and BV, represents the average effect of the variation across multiple diseases per biomarker and the prevalence of each disease in the population considered. Second, we only considered individuals with inpatient or emergency care episodes, and the combined effect of PV and BV might be different if outpatient care episodes are considered. Third, we did not directly estimate the analytical variation or circadian variation when computing the IP, and these sources of variation might not be constant for all biomarkers across time. However, the simplified formula we used for the II and IP is used widely in the literature as both sources of variation are expected to be relatively small [44,45]. Fourth, the joint effect of PV and BV which we captured might change if the number of sampled observations per patient is changed. However, the goal of our study was to highlight the potential impact of considering PV in the interpretation of biomarkers. The impact of different numbers of sampled values per individual will be addressed in future work. The impact of different numbers of sampled values per individual will be addressed in future work.

Conclusion

Our results indicate that PV varies across biomarkers, and that jointly considering PV and BV can impact how the RI is used for interpretation. Specifically, in biomarkers where the joint effect of PV and BV is high, and where IP is large, the RI is sensitive as individuals in unhealthy states will likely have results outside the RI. Pathological variation and biological variation provide complementary information for the clinical interpretation of test results.
