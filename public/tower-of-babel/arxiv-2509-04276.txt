PAOLI: Pose-free Articulated Object Learning from Sparse-view Images
Jianning Deng, Kartic Subr, Hakan Bilen

Abstract
We present a novel self-supervised framework for learning articulated object representations from sparse-view, unposed images. Unlike prior methods that require dense multi-view observations and ground-truth camera poses, our approach operates with as few as four views per articulation and no camera supervision. To address the inherent challenges, we first reconstruct each articulation independently using recent advances in sparse-view 3D reconstruction, then learn a deformation field that establishes dense correspondences across poses. A progressive disentanglement strategy further separates static from moving parts, enabling robust separation of camera and object motion. Finally, we jointly optimize geometry, appearance, and kinematics with a self-supervised loss that enforces cross-view and cross-pose consistency. Experiments on the standard benchmark and real-world examples demonstrate that our method produces accurate and detailed articulated object representations under significantly weaker input assumptions than existing approaches.



1
Introduction

Interacting with articulated objects, objects that can change shape or position through joints, is a natural part of everyday human activity. Think of opening a cupboard door, sliding out a drawer, or lifting a lid. These objects are characterized by their ability to move in specific ways, often defined by a set of joints that allow for rotation or translation. Understanding how these objects operate is crucial for tasks such as manipulation, and control in robotics.

Earlier research
Qian et al. 2022
;
Jiang et al. 2022
;
Sun et al. 2023
tackled this challenge by using detailed ground-truth information such as 3D object shapes, articulation parameters, or part segmentations.
While effective, this approach depends on high-quality 3D scans and labor-intensive manual annotations, which are both costly and difficult to scale.
To address this, recent methods have shifted toward learning directly from raw, unlabeled data. Instead of relying on manual labels, these approaches capture the motion of articulated objects and use it to infer their structure and kinematics
Liu et al. 2023a
;
Deng et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2025
. For instance, PARIS
Liu et al. 2023a
and ArticulateYourNeRF
Deng et al. 2024
follow an analysis-by-synthesis approach, learn object representations from pairs of multi-view images, using Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)
Mildenhall et al. 2021
to model 3D geometry and appearance while separating moving parts from static ones.
ArticulatedGS
Guo et al. 2025
improves over
Liu et al. 2023a
;
Deng et al. 2024
by modeling articulated objects through Gaussian Splatting (GS)
Kerbl et al. 2023
, offering improved efficiency and accuracy.
Despite these advances, existing methods still rely on dense observations, requiring around 100 paired views of the same object across two different articulated poses.
Such requirements are rarely feasible in practical applications.
Moreover, these approaches typically assume known camera poses, an assumption does not hold in many real-world scenarios.

Figure 1
:
Given few unposed views of an articulated object over different articulated poses,

In this work, we propose a novel approach for learning articulated object representations from sparse-view, unposed images.
This setting introduces two major challenges.
First, with only a handful of views (
e.g.
, four per articulation) and no camera information, reconstructing the object’s 3D structure becomes highly under-constrained.
Second, recovering articulated motion and camera motion from images are tightly coupled, making it difficult to disentangle object motion from viewpoint changes.

To tackle these challenges, we introduce a novel framework,
PAOLI
, that learns to reconstruct articulated objects from sparse-view images without requiring camera poses.
For the first challenge, we build on recent advances in sparse-view 3D reconstruction without camera supervision for static scenes. Specifically, we reconstruct the 3D shape of the object independently for each articulation
Zhang et al. 2024
, and then learn a deformation field that establishes dense correspondences across the reconstructed shapes.
For the second challenge, we introduce a progressive disentanglement strategy: the procedure first identifies static components of the object before progressively isolating the moving parts. This step-by-step process enables a robust separation of camera motion from articulated motion. Finally, we jointly optimize geometry, appearance, and kinematics using a self-supervised loss that enforces consistency across different views and poses.

We evaluate our method on several benchmark datasets and show that it achieves accurate and detailed articulated object representations from sparse, unposed inputs, and significantly outperforming prior approaches that require dense multi-view data and ground-truth camera poses.

3
Method

Assume that we are given two sets of images, each capturing an articulated object from sparse and unknown viewpoints.
Within each set, the object remains in a fixed pose, while its articulated pose differs across the sets.
Our objective is to reconstruct a digital twin of the object that includes both high-fidelity 3D geometry and appearance, and articulation parameters, enabling physically plausible dynamic rendering.

Our method consists of three main stages.
First, we reconstruct the 3D geometry and estimate camera parameters for each set of sparse-view images independently using Gaussian representations.
Next, we learn a deformation field to align the geometry between two sets.
Based on the estimated deformation, we establish the initial dense correspondences and part segmentation.
Finally, we jointly optimize the geometry, part segmentation, and articulation parameters to improve the overall reconstruction quality.
An overview of our method is shown in
Fig.

2
.
Since the optimization of the geometry, part segmentation, and articulation parameters is intertwined and requires good initialization, the first two stages are crucial for the success of the final joint optimization.

3.1
Initialization of 3D Gaussian Splats

Given two sets of images, a source set
\mathcal{I}^{s}=\{I^{s}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and a target set
\mathcal{I}^{t}=\{I^{t}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, each containing
K
(
e.g.
4) pose-free views of the object, we first construct initial 3D Gaussian splats
G^{s}
and
G^{t}
independently.
Each set
G=\{\mathbf{g}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
consists of
N
Gaussian points, where each point
\mathbf{g}=(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{s},o,\mathbf{c})
is parameterized by its mean
\bm{\mu}
, rotation
\mathbf{q}
, scale
\mathbf{s}
, opacity
o
, and color
\mathbf{c}
.

Since the standard 3D GS
Kerbl et al. 2023
typically requires many input views for accurate reconstruction, we use FreeSplatter
Zhang et al. 2024
, a feed-forward neural network trained to reconstruct 3D Gaussian splats from sparse views.
As FreeSplatter is designed for static scenes, we apply it independently to each image set to obtain initial Gaussian splats and camera parameters.

Despite its ability to work in the sparse setting, the Gaussians estimated by FreeSplatter are not accurately pixel-aligned with the input images, resulting in suboptimal camera extrinsic estimates.
Additionally, the estimated camera intrinsics often differ between the two sets.
Hence, we use their average as the final intrinsic parameters.
To improve the pixel alignment, we refine the extrinsic parameters
\Pi^{s}
and
\Pi^{t}
by directly optimizing them on the reconstructed Gaussian splats over their respective multi-view set, minimizing photometric loss via rendering.
This two-step camera refinement yields better alignment between the estimated camera parameters and the 3D geometry, providing a better initialization for the subsequent steps.

3.2
Gaussian Alignment via Deformation Fields

A central challenge in building digital twins of articulated objects from pose-free images lies in aligning the canonical pose across different observation sets. We frame this alignment as estimating the deformation between the Gaussian representations
G^{s}
and
G^{t}
. A straightforward strategy is to optimize the mean and rotation of each Gaussian point independently. However, this naive approach breaks local rigidity, since it allows unconstrained updates of individual parameters, whereas true object motion should remain rigid within each part.

To address this, we introduce a feed-forward deformation network,
\mathcal{F}_{\text{deform}}
, which predicts rigid transformations for Gaussian points in
G^{s}
using their positional encodings, producing a deformed target representation
\hat{G}^{t}
. By constraining the network to generate low-frequency deformations, we enforce local rigidity while still capturing global motion. Specifically,
\mathcal{F}_{\text{deform}}
maps the positional encoding of Gaussian means to a rigid transformation consisting of a rotation, translation, and scaling factor:

\{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{t},s\}=\mathcal{F}_{\text{deform}}(\text{PE}(\bm{\mu}))

(1)

where
\mathbf{R}\in\text{SO}(3)
is parameterized by Euler angles
\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and converted to rotation matrices,
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
represents the translation vector,
s\in\mathbb{R}
is a global scaling factor shared across all points, and
\text{PE}(\bm{\mu})
is the positional encoding of the Gaussian means.
This transformation is then applied to the source Gaussian points to obtain the deformed target representation.

The means of the source Gaussian points
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are encoded as:

\text{PE}(\bm{\mu})=[\sin(2^{k_{0}}\pi\bm{\mu}),\cos(2^{k_{0}}\pi\bm{\mu})]

(2)

where
k_{0}
represents the base frequency parameter. The parameter
k_{0}
directly influences the rigidity of the deformation field: smaller values result in lower-frequency encodings that naturally constrain the deformation field to be more rigid, effectively guiding the network’s capacity to learn low-frequency non-rigid transformations.

Deformation Network Training

To optimize the parameters of
\mathcal{F}_{\text{deform}}
, we minimize the Chamfer distance
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CD}}
between the means of the deformed source Gaussian
\hat{G}^{t}
and the target one
{G}^{t}
, as well as
L1
photometric loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{photo}}
between their rendered images
\mathcal{R}(\hat{G}^{t}),\mathcal{R}(G^{t})
respectively:

\min_{\mathcal{F}_{\text{deform}}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CD}}(\hat{G}^{t},G^{t})+\mathcal{L}_{\text{photo}}(\mathcal{R}(\hat{G}^{t}),\mathcal{R}(G^{t}))

(3)

where
\hat{G}^{t}=\{\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{i}^{t}\}_{i=1}^{N^{s}}
represents the deformed source Gaussians, with each point
\hat{\mathbf{g}}^{t}
obtained by applying the transformation predicted by
\pi=\mathcal{F}_{\text{deform}}(\bm{\mu})
to the corresponding source point
\mathbf{g}^{s}\in G^{s}
, where
\pi
is a similarity transformation containing rotation
\mathbf{R}
, translation
\mathbf{t}
, and scaling
s
. Specifically, to obtain the deformed target Gaussian points
\hat{\mathbf{g}}^{t}=(\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{t},\hat{\mathbf{q}}^{t},\mathbf{s}^{s},o^{s},\mathbf{c}^{s})
, we first compute the transformation for each point
\mathbf{g}^{s}=(\bm{\mu}^{s},\mathbf{q}^{s},\mathbf{s}^{s},o^{s},\mathbf{c}^{s})
using
Eq.

1
,
and apply the transformation to the means and rotation of the source Gaussian points. With
\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{t}=s\mathbf{R}\bm{\mu}^{s}+\mathbf{t}
and
\hat{\mathbf{q}}^{t}=\mathbf{q}^{s}\otimes\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{R})
, we can obtain the deformed target Gaussian points
\hat{\mathbf{g}}^{t}=(\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{t},\hat{\mathbf{q}}^{t},\mathbf{s}^{s},o^{s},\mathbf{c}^{s})
, where
\otimes
denotes quaternion multiplication and
\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{R})
converts the rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}
to its quaternion representation.
For simplicity, we denote this process as
\hat{\mathbf{g}}=\pi\circ\mathbf{g}
.

3.3
Joint Optimization

To obtain an initial segmentation of object parts and their corresponding rigid transformations, we leverage TEASER
Yang et al. 2020
, a robust pose estimation algorithm for point correspondences.
We apply TEASER to the set of correspondences between the deformed source and the target Gaussians.
Since TEASER was originally designed for static scenes, it identifies the largest rigidly moving component, typically the part with greatest volume, as the inlier set, while treating the remaining points as outliers.

We then organize these sets into a tree structure: the inlier points form the root node, and the outliers are assigned to leaf nodes. TEASER is applied recursively to the leaf nodes to estimate part-level similarity transformations. This iterative process progressively adds parts to the tree, with the root representing the dominant component and leaves corresponding to smaller articulated parts. For simplicity, we describe the two-part case, though the method naturally extends to multiple parts.
The result is a set of similarity transformations,
\pi_{r}
and
\pi_{l}
, for the root and leaf parts, along with segmentation labels
p^{s}\in\{r,l\}
for each Gaussian point in the source state.

Next we proceed to the joint optimization of the Gaussian splats, part segmentation, and articulation parameters.
We initialize the learnable Gaussian splats
\bar{G}^{s}=\{\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{s}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N^{s}}
with
\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{s}=(\bm{\mu}^{s},\mathbf{q}^{s},\mathbf{s}^{s},o^{s},\mathbf{c}^{s},p^{s})
and treat
\pi_{r}
and
\pi_{l}
as learnable parameters to refine the rigid transformation.
To obtain the deformed Gaussian splats, we apply the transformations to each Gaussian point based on its segmentation label:
\pi_{r}\circ\mathbf{g}
if
p^{s}=r
, or
\pi_{l}\circ\mathbf{g}
if
p^{s}=l
. Since the initial estimation is not perfect, we also apply a displacement term
\delta\bm{\mu}
to the means of the deformed Gaussian points to compensate for non-rigidity.
To simplify the notation, we use an auxiliary function
f(\mathbf{g},\pi,\delta\bm{\mu})
that first applies the transformation
\pi
to
\mathbf{g}
and then applies the displacement
\delta\bm{\mu}
to the mean of the transformed Gaussian.
This yields the deformed Gaussian splats
\bar{G}^{t}=\{\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{t}|\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{t}=f(\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{s},\pi_{p^{s}},\delta\bm{\mu}),\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{s}\in\bar{G}^{s}\}
, with the
\delta\bm{\mu}
denoting the displacement for the means.

Since the initial segmentation and and transformation for each part is imperfect. We often end up with detached parts after the deformation. To deal with this, we refine the estimated
\pi_{r}
and
\pi_{l}
with the initial segmentation labels in the first phase of optimization using both the Chamfer distance between the transformed
\bar{G}^{t}
and
G^{t}
, and the rendering loss computed in both the source and target states. We define the joint loss as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{joint}}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{CD}}(\bar{G}^{t},G^{t})+

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{photo}}(\mathcal{R}(\bar{G}^{t}),\mathcal{R}(G^{t}))+\mathcal{L}_{\text{photo}}(\mathcal{R}(\bar{G}^{s}),\mathcal{R}(G^{s}))

and our optimization objective is to minimize the joint loss:

(\pi_{r}^{*},\pi_{l}^{*})=\arg\min_{\pi_{r},\pi_{l}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{joint}}

(5)

Note that in this phase,
\bm{\delta}\mu
is set to zero.

In the second phase of the optimization, we optimize the Gaussian splats to improve the geometric accuracy, especially to handle occlusion between source and target states. We also allow the displacement term
\delta\bm{\mu}
to be optimized to compensate for non-rigidity caused by imperfect transformation estimation or geometry mismatch, but still penalize the displacement of the Gaussian splats with
\mathcal{L}_{\text{rigid}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\delta\bm{\mu}_{i}\right\|_{1}
.
For better optimization stability suggested in ArticulateYourNeRF
Deng et al. 2024
, we update the segmentation labels heuristically. Newly split or duplicated points inherit the segmentation labels from their parent points. To correct points with incorrect initial segmentation, we check those points with large
\delta\bm{\mu}
, which indicates high non-rigidity, and update their segmentation labels following this rule:

p=\arg\min_{p\in\{r,l\}}\|\bar{\bm{\mu}}^{p}-\bar{\bm{\mu}}^{t}\|_{2}

(6)

with
\bar{\bm{\mu}}^{p}
for the means of
\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{p}=\pi_{p}\circ\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{s},p\in\{r,l\}
. And in this phase, our optimization objective is to minimize the joint loss with the regularization term:

\min_{\{\delta\bm{\mu}\},\bar{G}^{s},\pi_{r},\pi_{l}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{joint}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{rigid}}

(7)

and the update of the segmentation labels is:

\min_{\pi_{r},\pi_{l}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{joint}}

(8)

In the final phase, we focus on improving visual quality and articulation estimation. We update the
\pi_{r}
,
\pi_{l}
, and
\mathbf{c}
of the Gaussian splats without the displacement term
\delta\bm{\mu}
for pure rigid transformation supervised by photo loss with the original input images as:

\min_{\{\pi_{r},\pi_{l},\mathbf{c}\}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{photo}}(\mathcal{R}(\bar{G}^{s}),I^{s})+\mathcal{L}_{\text{photo}}(\mathcal{R}(\bar{G}^{t}),I^{t}).

(9)

In practice, we use 500 iterations for the first phase, 2500 iterations for the second phase, and 2000 iterations for the final phase.
After 5000 iterations at total, we decompose the articulation parameters into axis
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, angle
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, and pivot point
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
for revolute joints, while only axis
\mathbf{a}
and magnitude
d\in\mathbb{R}
for prismatic joints.

Our evaluation metrics encompass three key aspects: (1) For rendering, we use PSNR (Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio) and SSIM (Structural Similarity Index) to evaluate the quality of the rendered images for unseen views in both source and target articulation states. (2) For articulation parameter estimation, we use the angular error, position error of the estimated motion axis and additional motion distance to evaluate the accuracy of articulation parameters as in previous work

;
Liu et al. 2023a
;
Guo et al. 2025
. More details are provided in the supplementary material. (3) For 3D reconstruction precision, we use the Chamfer Distance (CD) to evaluate the precision of the estimated 3D geometry.
For PSNR, SSIM and CD, we report the average values of the two articulation states.

Ablation Studies

Effect of the number of input images.

With just 4-view images, baseline methods struggle to reconstruct objects even when in static state. We investigate how the number of input views affects performance by varying the views per articulation state. Fig.
6
shows that PSNR for baseline methods improves with more input images, saturating after 16 images (VGGT runs out of memory with 64 images on our 48GB VRAM GPU), even with VGGT-estimated poses. Our method matches the saturated performance without ground truth poses with only 4 input images and stays consistent across different view counts with qualitative evaluation shows in Fig.
7
. Tab.
2
shows the comparison at 16 images per state, where AGS with VGGT poses gets close to our performance. However, AGS still encounter reconstruction failures even with 32 images per state when VGGT provides less accurate poses. We suspect that AGS is more sensitive to pose estimation errors as it heavily depends on 2D image information.
Our approach leverages FreeSplatter’s 3D priors and 3D-guided deformation, making it robust to pose estimation variations when poses stay consistent relative to the reconstructed splats.

Effect of
k_{0}
for deformation field

We find that our method is generally robust to the choice of
k_{0}
in the deformation field. To analyze its effect, we conduct experiments with different
k_{0}
values, with qualitative results shown in Fig.

. A smaller

enforces near-rigid transformations across the whole object, leading to under-deformation and failure to reach the target state (e.g., right side of the second row). In contrast, a larger

4.4
Real-world Experiments

To demonstrate the generalization capability of our approach, we conduct experiments on real-world articulated objects. We evaluate our method on four different objects: a bottle, a projector, a drawer, and a lego toy, collecting four input images for each articulation state. Prior to processing, we employ the Segment Anything Model (SAM)
Ravi et al. 2024
to remove background from the input images. For objects with multiple articulated parts, such as the lego toy with two movable arms, we collect three sets of input images per articulation state as explained in Sec.
3.4
. The experimental results, presented in Fig.

, demonstrate that our method successfully generates seamless reconstruction results for real-world articulated objects, validating its practical applicability beyond synthetic datasets.

5
Conclusion

We introduced PAOLI, a self-supervised framework for learning articulated object representations from sparse, unposed images. Unlike prior methods that require dense multi-view data and known camera poses, PAOLI operates with only a few views per articulation and no camera supervision. By combining sparse-view 3D reconstruction, a deformation field for cross-pose correspondences, and a progressive disentanglement strategy, our method jointly optimizes geometry, appearance, and kinematics while robustly separating camera and object motion.

Our work assumes articulated objects with rigid parts, cannot handle non-rigid deformations.
Handling such transformations requires more complex deformation models.
Second, we assume that the observations from two different poses of the same object are available.
It would be valuable to pursue an active vision approach that allows the system to interact with the object while learning its articulated structure, which could improve robustness and generalization (
e.g.
,
Nie et al. 2022
). Additionally, our current method relies on comprehensive object coverage with four input views per articulation state; insufficient coverage may degrade reconstruction quality and lead to failure. Integrating diffusion models to synthesize additional viewpoints could offer a promising solution for scenarios with limited view coverage.
Finally, temporal information could be leveraged to improve the understanding of articulated motion dynamics.

6.1
Detailed Discussion of Related Work

Here we provide more detailed discussion of the most related articulated object learning work including, PARIS
Liu et al. 2023a
, AYN
Deng et al. 2024
and AGS
Guo et al. 2025
.
As explained in the submission, these techniques assume dense views of the object across two articulation state along with the camera information, unlike our method which is pose-free and works with sparse views.
Below, we explain the differences in methodology in more detail.

All the methods including ours uses the synthesis by analysis approach that deforms the object shape in the source articulation state to the target articulation state while learning the geometry, appearance and part segmentation as the intermediate representations.
PARIS jointly optimizes the geometry, appearance and articulation parameters from the input images of both source and target states with photometric loss. It employs two separate NeRFs to model ‘static’ and ‘dynamic’ parts of the object.
Although the joint optimization is straightforward, it is unstable and fails to obtain accurate results when the initialization of these parameters are noisy, as reported in both AYN and AGS.
In addition, PARIS is limited to modeling one moving part only.

Instead, both AYN and AGS follows a multi-step approach, hence reconstructs the geometry and appearance of the object first in the source state.
Once the initial geometry and appearance are learned, they use a decoupled optimization for part segmentation and motion parameter estimation that provides more stable optimization.
AYN first extracts evenly spaced point cloud from NeRF scans, and then projects the point clouds back on image space.
By comparing the difference in the alpha channel between the projected images and the target state images, AYN initializes the part segmentation label.
With the initialized labels, it optimizes the part motion parameter so that the projected images with transformed part-points match the image of corresponding target images in alpha channel measured by 2D chamfer distance.
Later, once the motion parameters are fixed, and AYN learns a segmentation field to refine the segmentation by minimizing the photo metric loss between rendered images and target images.

Similarly, AGS tackles the instability problem with multi-step approach. To first obtain the segmentation labels for gaussians, by employing a deformation field to learn the displacement for each Gaussian and rotation to correct the quaternions for color rendering. That’s
\hat{\bm{\mu}}=\bm{\mu}+\delta\bm{\mu}
,
\hat{\bm{q}}=\bm{q}\otimes\delta\bm{q}
. This steps aims to obtain the deformed 3D Gaussian that could render similar images in the target states with the corresponding camera poses.
The deformation is supervised by photometric loss and a rigidity regularization term.
Since the camera poses are aligned with the root part of the objects, the gaussian points in the root part will be static with small displacement
\delta\bm{\mu}
. AGS assigns Gaussians with small displacement to the static part, and the ones with large deformations to the moving part.
After freezing their segmentation labels, it estimates a rigid transformation for the dynamic part with chamfer distance w.r.t.
the deformed Gaussian (by deformation field) and photometric loss w.r.t.
the images in the target states.

However, both AYN and AGS rely on known camera poses that are aligned at the root part between source and target states. This requirement prevents them from initializing part segmentation when camera poses are unknown across different articulation states.

Unlike previous methods that identify parts based on rendering differences (which only relies on 2D information from images) and assume a static root part,
our method establishes correspondence across two articulation states directly in 3D space by deforming the 3D Gaussians from the source to the target state.
Since
G^{s}
and
\hat{G}^{t}
represent the same set of Gaussians with
\hat{G}^{t}
being the transformed version of
G^{s}
, we can naturally establish matching pairs between them. The deformation is supervised by 3D chamfer distance and photometric loss, ensuring that
\hat{G}^{t}
closely approximates
G^{t}
. This makes the matching pairs from
G^{s}
to
\hat{G}^{t}
effectively equivalent to matching pairs between
G^{s}
and
G^{t}
.
With these correspondences established, we can separate parts and estimate per-part transformations using TEASER for initialization. To further improve rigidity during deformation and enhance performance, we employ positional encoding on the Gaussian means before feeding them to the deformation network.

With the initialization, we can now continue for fine-level optimization. For stability purpose, we also decouple the updates for part segmentation from the joint optimization. We split the optimization into 3 phases. The first phase we try to refine the coarse motion estimation from TEASER. In the second phase, we update part segmentation based on heuristic rules explained in §3.3 in the main paper to refine the segmentation. At the last phase, we optimize the appearance to improve the rendering quality.

6.2
Evaluation Metrics

As we mentioned in the main paper, we will decompose the relative motion between leaf part and root part into axis
\bm{a}
, pivot
\bm{p}
and angle
\alpha
for revolute joints and axis
\bm{a}
and magnitude
d
for prismatic joints. Since our method is a pose-free method, we need to recover the reconstructed geometry in the ground truth coordinate system. The coordinate transformation can be computed by aligning the position of the estimated camera pose and the ground truth camera pose using Umeyama’s algorithm
Umeyama 1991
.
To further mitigate the effects of noisy pose estimation, we optimize the transformation matrix initialized with Umeyama’s algorithm by minimizing the chamfer distance between the reconstructed and ground truth geometries. We apply identical preprocessing procedures when evaluating pose-free baseline methods.

Ang Err:

The angular error computes the orientation difference between the predicted axis direction and the ground truth.

Pos Err:

The position error computes the minimum distance between the rotation axis and the ground truth which takes the position of the pivot point into account. Note that this error only applies to revolute joints.

Motion Dist:

For motion distance computes the difference in movements between estimated motion and ground truth. For revolute joints, we compute the geodesic distance between the predicted rotation matrix and the ground truth rotation matrix. For prismatic joints, we compute the distance between the predicted translation magnitude and the ground truth translation magnitude.
