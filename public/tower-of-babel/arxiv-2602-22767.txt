The regularity of the boundary of vortex patches for the quasi-geostrophic shallow-water equations
Marc Magaña, Joan Mateu, Joan Orobitg

Abstract
. We prove the persistence of boundary smoothness of vortex patches for the quasi-geostrophic shallow-water (QGSW) equations. The QGSW equations generalize the Euler equations by including an additional parameter, the Rossby radius ε − 1 \varepsilon^{-1} , which modifies the relationship between the streamfunction and the (potential) vorticity. In addition, we prove that solutions of the QGSW equations converge locally in time to the corresponding Euler solutions as ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 in little Hölder spaces.



1.
Introduction

The quasi-geostrophic shallow-water equations, commonly used to track the dynamics of the atmospheric and oceanic circulation at large-scale motion, is a two-dimensional (2D) active scalar equation given by

(QGSW)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}q+v\cdot\nabla q=0,\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\\
&v=\nabla^{\perp}(\Delta-\varepsilon^{2})^{-1}q,\\
&q(0,\cdot)=q_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

where
\nabla^{\perp}=(-\partial_{2},\partial_{1})^{t}
denotes the orthogonal gradient. The quantities involved are the divergence-free velocity field
v
, the potential vorticity
q
, which is a scalar function, and a real parameter
\varepsilon
. In the literature, when
\varepsilon>0
it is known as the inverse Rossby radius and is defined by

\varepsilon=\frac{\omega_{c}}{\sqrt{gH}},

where
g
is the gravitational constant,
H
is the mean active layer depth and
\omega_{c}
is the Coriolis frequency, assumed to be constant.

These equations are derived asymptotically from the rotating shallow water equations, in the limit of rapid rotation and weak variations
of the free surface. For a comprehensive overview, see
[
20
,
23
]
and the references therein. For
\varepsilon=0,
the equations reduce to the well-known vorticity formulation of the 2D Euler equation, for which numerous results are known.

To give some examples, Yudovich
[
26
]
proved that the 2D Euler vorticity equation is well-posed in
L^{\infty}_{c}
, the space of bounded measurable functions with compact support. A vortex patch is a special weak solution of this equation when the initial condition is the characteristic function of a bounded domain
\Omega_{0}
. Since the vorticity equation is a transport equation, vorticity is conserved along trajectories, meaning that at any time
t
, it remains the characteristic function of some domain
\Omega_{t}
.

A significant problem posed in the 1980s was whether the boundary smoothness of the initial domain persists over time. Specifically, if
\Omega_{0}
has a boundary with derivatives in the Hölder class, that is
\partial\Omega_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}
for
0<\gamma<1
, one would like
\Omega_{t}
to maintain a boundary of the same class indefinitely. Chemin
[
9
]
proved that boundary regularity persists for all times using paradifferential calculus. Shortly after, Bertozzi and Constantin
[
4
]
provided an alternative proof based on methods of classical analysis with a geometric approach.

Recently, (
QGSW
) equations have garnered interest in the mathematical community, as evidenced by works such as
[
12
,
14
,
21
,
25
]
. However, the specific aspects of solution regularity and vortex patch regularity, which have been thoroughly studied for the 2D Euler equations, had not yet been explicitly addressed for the (
QGSW
) equations. Our paper delves into these issues, exploring the regularity of solutions and vortex patches within the (
QGSW
) framework.

From now on, assume
\varepsilon>0
for simplicity. The results remain valid for
\varepsilon<0
by replacing
\varepsilon
with
|\varepsilon|
. By using that the fundamental solution of the operator
(\Delta-\varepsilon^{2})
is
-\frac{1}{2\pi}K_{0}(\varepsilon|x|),
one can write the velocity
v
in the (
QGSW
) as

(1)

v=K*q,\text{ with }K(x)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}\frac{x^{\perp}}{|x|}K_{1}(\varepsilon|x|),

where
K_{0}
and
K_{1}
are modified Bessel functions, which we will introduce in Section
2
(see
[
12
]
for the details). We are now ready to anticipate the main result of our paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega_{0}
be a bounded domain with boundary of class
C^{1,\gamma},\;0<\gamma<1.
Then the

(QGSW)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}q+v\cdot\nabla q=0,\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\\
&v=K*q,\\
&q(0,\cdot)=\chi_{\Omega_{0}},\end{aligned}\right.

has a unique weak solution of the form
q(x,t)=\chi_{\Omega_{t}}(x),
with
\Omega_{t}
a bounded domain with boundary of class
C^{1,\gamma}.

It is worth noting that the persistence of boundary smoothness of vortex patches has been studied for a broad families of nonlinear transport equations like the one above, in which the velocity is given by a convolution of the scalar function with a kernel,
K
, for different kernels. For instance, when
K=\nabla N,
where
N
is the fundamental solution of the Laplacian one has the Aggregation patch problem, which was studied in
[
6
,
5
]
. More recently, Cantero, Mateu, Orobitg and Verdera in
[
8
]
extended this result to a broader class of kernels that are smooth away from the origin, homogeneous and odd. Our kernel, being non-homogeneous, does not fall into any of the previously studied cases, nor, to the best of our knowledge, has it been addressed elsewhere in the literature.

The outline of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the modified Bessel
K_{n}
functions, state some of their important properties, and recall the definitions of Hölder spaces. We also present relevant results concerning these spaces and how our kernel acts on them. In Section
3
we prove existence and uniqueness of solutions for initial data,
q_{0}
, in the Hölder class
\mathcal{C}^{\gamma},
which we use later in Section
4
to show existence and uniqueness of weak solutions. Later, in Section
5
we focus on the regularity of the vortex patch boundary and prove Theorem
1.1
. Finally, in Section
6
, we study the behavior of the regular solutions from Section
3
to the (
QGSW
) in the limit

\varepsilon\to 0
, where, at least formally, one recovers the 2D Euler equations.

2.1.
Modified Bessel functions

Solutions to

z^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}w}{\partial z^{2}}+z\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}-(z^{2}+\nu^{2})w=0

are
I_{\pm\nu}(z)
and
K_{\nu}(z),
which are referred to as modified Bessel Functions. We shall collect some useful information about these complex-valued functions. The expressions below are taken from
[
1
, pp. 374–378]
, unless otherwise noted. For further details, see
[
1
, Chapter 9]
and
[
24
]
. For the sake of generality, we state the formulas in their standard complex form as in the cited references, although throughout this work we will only make use of them for real and positive values of

When
\nu=n\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

(2)

\begin{split}K_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(n-k-1)!}{k!}\left(\frac{-z^{2}}{4}\right)^{k}+(-1)^{n+1}\ln\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)I_{n}(z)\\
+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{-z}{2}\right)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\psi(k+1)+\psi(n+k+1))\frac{\left(\frac{z^{2}}{4}\right)^{k}}{k!(n+k)!},\end{split}

where

I_{n}(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{n+2m}}{m!(n+m)!},\;|\arg z|<\pi\text{ and }\psi(1)=-\gamma,\;\psi(m+1)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1}{k}-\gamma,

being
\gamma=\lim_{j\to\infty}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{1}{k}-\ln(j)\right)
Euler’s constant. Note that
K_{n}(z)
is singular at
z=0
for every integer
n
.

In particular,

(3)

K_{0}(z)=-\log\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)I_{0}(z)+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2m}}{(m!)^{2}}\psi(m+1),

so
K_{0}
behaves like a logarithm at 0. For
n\geq 1
one has the following asymptotic expansion

(4)

K_{n}(z)\sim\frac{(n-1)!}{2\left(\frac{1}{2}z\right)^{n}}=2^{n-1}(n-1)!z^{-n},\quad\text{as}\quad z\to 0.

For large
|z|
we may use the following representation, which is equivalent to (
2

(5)

K_{n}(z)=\frac{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}z\right)^{n}}{\Gamma\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-zt}(t^{2}-1)^{n-\frac{1}{2}}dt,\quad n\geq 0,\;|\arg z|<\frac{1}{2}\pi,

and the corresponding asymptotic expansion for large
|z|
and fixed
n:

(6)

K_{n}(z)\sim\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2z}}e^{-z}\left(1+\frac{4n^{2}-1}{8z}+\frac{(4n^{2}-1)(4n^{2}-9)}{2!(8z)^{2}}+\cdots\right),\quad|\arg z|<\frac{3}{2}\pi.

Moreover, for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\alpha>n
,

(7)

\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}K_{n}(t)dt=2^{\alpha-2}\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha-n}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha+n}{2}\right),

see
[
24
, p. 388]
.

Finally, since derivatives of
K_{n}
will be involved in our analysis, we recall the following recurrence relations:

(8)

\displaystyle K_{n}^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=-K_{n-1}(z)-\frac{n}{z}K_{n}(z)=-K_{n+1}(z)+\frac{n}{z}K_{n}(z)

(9)

\displaystyle\frac{2n}{z}K_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=K_{n+1}(z)-K_{n-1}(z)

2.2.
Inequalities for Hölder continuous functions and Kernel estimates

We now recall the definition of Hölder spaces as well as some well-known properties of its elements.

Definition 1
.

Given
0<\gamma<1
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},
set

\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f(x)|\text{ and }\left|f\right|_{\gamma}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\gamma}}.

We define the norm

\left\|f\right\|_{\gamma}\coloneqq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left|f\right|_{\gamma}.

For

\begin{array}[]{rccl}F:&\mathbb{R}^{n}&\to&\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
&x&\to&F(x)=(f_{1}(x),\dots,f_{d}(x))\end{array}

we define

\left\|F\right\|_{\gamma}\coloneqq\sup_{i=1,\dots,d}\left\|f_{i}\right\|_{\gamma},

and the Hölder space

\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneqq\left\{f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\|f\right\|_{\gamma}<\infty\right\}.

We also define

\left\|F\right\|_{1,\gamma}=\left\|F\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left\|\nabla F\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left|\nabla F\right|_{\gamma},

and the Hölder space
\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})
as

\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneqq\left\{f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\|f\right\|_{1,\gamma}<\infty\right\}.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}
with
0<\gamma<1
. Then

(10)

\displaystyle\left|fg\right|_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left|g\right|_{\gamma}+\left|f\right|_{\gamma}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

(11)

\displaystyle\left\|fg\right\|_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|f\right\|_{\gamma}\left\|g\right\|_{\gamma}

If moreover
X
is a smooth invertible transformation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying

|\det\nabla X(\alpha)|\geq c_{1}>0,

then there exists
c>0
such that

(12)

\displaystyle\left\|(\nabla X)^{-1}\right\|_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq c\left\|\nabla X\right\|_{\gamma}^{2n-1}

(13)

\displaystyle\left\|X^{-1}\right\|_{1,\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq c\left\|X\right\|_{1,\gamma}^{2n-1}

(14)

\displaystyle\left|f\circ X\right|_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\left|f\right|_{\gamma}\left\|\nabla X\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\gamma}

(15)

\displaystyle\left\|f\circ X\right\|_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|f\right\|_{\gamma}(1+\left\|X\right\|_{1,\gamma}^{\gamma})

(16)

\displaystyle\left\|f\circ X^{-1}\right\|_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|f\right\|_{\gamma}(1+\left\|X\right\|_{1,\gamma}^{\gamma(2n-1)})

The proof of Lemma
2.1
can be found in
[
17
, pp. 159 - 163]
.

Definition 2
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is
Log-Lipschitz
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq C|x-y||\log{|x-y|}|.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is Log-Lipschitz if each of its components is.

3.
Existence and uniqueness of regular solutions

Our first goal is to establish the following well-posedness result for (
QGSW
) in the space of Hölder continuous functions.

Theorem 3.1
.

For
0<\gamma<1,
if
q_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}),
then the (
QGSW
) has a unique solution
q(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})
for all time
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

In order to prove theorem
3.1
, we follow the approach of
[
17
, Chapter 4]
for the Euler equation. That is, we prove existence and uniqueness by means of the particle–trajectory method. We first establish existence and uniqueness for the
flow map, and then deduce the corresponding results for (
QGSW
).

Let us first recall some definitions. Given a velocity
v
and a point
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
we set, whenever it is well defined, the
flow map

\displaystyle X(\alpha,\cdot):\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}^{2},

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\to X(\alpha,t)

as the solution of the ordinary differential equation (ODE)

(26)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{d}{dt}X(\alpha,t)=v(X(\alpha,t),t),\\
X(\alpha,0)=\alpha.\end{array}\right.

In integral form, the flow map is given by

X(\alpha,t)=\alpha+\int_{0}^{t}v(X(\alpha,{s}),s)ds.

This map gives the position at time
t
of the particle initially located at
\alpha
, moving under the velocity field
v
. It is also called the
trajectory
of the particle initially at
\alpha
. If
q(\cdot,t)
is smooth enough, then it is easy to check that
q(x,t)=q_{0}(X^{-1}(x,t))
where, as usual,
X^{-1}(\cdot,t)
denotes the inverse of the flow
X
. Hence, by (
16
), we have
q(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}
whenever
X(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}
.

We focus on proving existence, uniqueness and regularity for
X
. To do so, we combine the fact that
X
satisfies (
26
) with
v(\cdot,t)=K*q(\cdot,t)
at any given time to get

\frac{dX}{dt}(\alpha,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K(X(\alpha,t)-x^{\prime})q(x^{\prime},t)dx^{\prime}.

Applying the change of variables
x^{\prime}=X(\alpha^{\prime},t)
, using that
v
is divergence–free (so the Jacobian determinant equals one) and that
q
is conserved along the flow, we obtain

(27)

\displaystyle\frac{dX}{dt}(\alpha,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K(X(\alpha,t)-X(\alpha^{\prime},t))q_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}\eqqcolon F(X(\alpha,t)),

\displaystyle X(\alpha,t)|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\alpha.

As a result, we have an ODE for
X
.

3.1.
Local-in-time existence of solutions

A standard approach to proving local existence and uniqueness for an ODE is to apply Picard-Lindelöf’s theorem.

Theorem 3.2

(Picard-Lindelöf)
.

Let
O\subseteq B
be an open subset of a Banach space
B
and let
F:O\to B
be a locally Lipschitz continuous map. Then, given
X_{0}\in O,
there exists a time
T>0
such that the ordinary differential equation

\frac{dX}{dt}=F(X),\quad X(\cdot,t=0)=X_{0},

has a unique (local) solution
X\in C^{1}((-T,T);O).

We will apply the above theorem to the operator
F
defined in (
27
),
for
B=\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and, as in the Euler setting, consider

(28)

O_{M}=\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap\left\{\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\det\nabla_{\alpha}X(\alpha)>\frac{1}{2}\text{ and }\left\|X\right\|_{1,\gamma}<M\right\}.

For any
M>0
, this set is non-empty, open, and consists of one-to-one mappings of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
onto
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Therefore, it is a valid domain on which one can apply Picard–Lindelöf’s theorem.

We now need to verify that the hypotheses of the theorem hold, that is that
F:O_{M}\to B
defined by

F(X(\alpha,t))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K(X(\alpha,t)-X(\alpha^{\prime},t))q_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}

is bounded and locally Lipschitz on
O_{M}
. To do so, we first look at the derivatives of the kernel in the distributional sense.

We consider each component of the kernel (denoted with a superscript to avoid confusion with the Bessel functions
K_{n}
), and using Green’s first identity, we obtain for all
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{x_{i}}K^{j},\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle K^{j},\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi\rangle={-}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{|x|\geq\delta}K^{j}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi dx

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\left(\int_{|x|\geq\delta}\partial_{x_{i}}K^{j}\varphi dx-\int_{|x|=\delta}K^{j}\varphi\frac{x_{i}}{|x|}ds\right)

By changing variables
x\to\delta x
on the second integral we get

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{|x|=\delta}K^{j}\varphi\frac{x_{i}}{|x|}ds

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{|x|=1}K^{j}(\delta x)\varphi(\delta x)x_{i}\delta\;ds

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{|x|=1}{\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}}\frac{{(x^{\perp})_{j}}}{|x|}K_{1}(\varepsilon\delta|x|)\varphi(\delta x)x_{i}\delta\;ds

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}\delta K_{1}(\varepsilon\delta)\int_{|x|=1}(x^{\perp})_{j}\varphi(\delta x)x_{i}\;ds

\displaystyle={\frac{1}{2\pi}}\varphi(0)\int_{|x|=1}(x^{\perp})_{j}x_{i}ds

where we made use of the dominated convergence theorem and property (
4
).

Therefore, in the sense of distributions

\partial_{x_{i}}K^{j}=p.v.\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\partial_{x_{i}}K^{j}+c_{i,j}\delta_{0},\quad c_{i,j}={-\frac{1}{2\pi}}\int_{|x|=1}(x^{\perp})_{j}x_{i}ds,

where
\delta_{0}
denotes the Dirac delta at 0, as usual. For convenience, we adopt the following more compact notation

(29)

\nabla K=p.v.\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla K+c\delta_{0}.

To show that
F
is bounded from
O_{M}
to
B
, we proceed in the same spirit as in the Euler setting.
The estimates required for this step are simpler than those needed later to prove that
F
is locally
Lipschitz, and in fact will appear there in a slightly stronger form. For this reason, we do not
present a separate verification of the boundedness here and simply note that it follows from the
same type of arguments developed below.

Next, to prove that
F
is locally Lipschitz on
O_{M}
we will show a sufficient condition. If the derivative
F^{\prime}(X)
is bounded as a linear operator from
O_{M}
to
B,
then the mean-value theorem implies that

\displaystyle\left\|F(X_{1})-F(X_{2})\right\|_{1,\gamma}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{d\delta}F[X_{1}+\delta(X_{2}-X_{1})]d\delta\right\|_{1,\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left\|F^{\prime}[X_{1}+\delta(X_{2}-X_{1})]\right\|_{1,\gamma}{d\delta}\left\|X_{1}-X_{2}\right\|_{1,\gamma}

so
F
is locally Lipschitz.

Let
X\in O_{M}
and
Y\in B
. Then,

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(X)Y

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\delta}F(X+\delta Y)|_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K[X(\alpha,t)-X(\alpha^{\prime},t)+\delta(Y(\alpha,t)-Y(\alpha^{\prime},t))]q_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}\right|_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla K(X(\alpha,t)-X(\alpha^{\prime},t))(Y(\alpha,t)-Y(\alpha^{\prime},t))q_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime},

where in the last equality the principal value is not needed, since the singularity of
\nabla K
when
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}
is compensated with the term
Y(\alpha,t)-Y(\alpha^{\prime},t).

Thus, if we can prove the following lemma, we have that
F
satisfies all the hypotheses of Picard-Lindelöf’s theorem.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
q_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})
for some
0<\gamma<1.
Given
X\in O_{M}
and
Y\in B
, define

F^{\prime}(X)Y=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla K(X(\alpha,t)-X(\alpha^{\prime},t))(Y(\alpha,t)-Y(\alpha^{\prime},t))q_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}.

Then there exists
c>0,
independent of
\varepsilon,

X
and
Y,
such that

\left\|F^{\prime}(X)Y\right\|_{1,\gamma}\leq c\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{\gamma}\left\|Y\right\|_{1,\gamma}.

Since the proof of the lemma is rather technical, we postpone it to the end of this subsection.

Combining the result of the lemma with the fact that
F
is bounded from
O_{M}
to
B
and Picard-Lindelöf theorem, we obtain:

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
q_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}).
Then there exists
T(M)>0
such that the ODE

\frac{dX}{dt}=F(X),\quad X(\cdot,t=0)=X_{0},

has a unique solution
X(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
for
t\in(-T(M),T(M)).

Moreover, since the velocity field is sufficiently smooth, we also obtain well-posedness in the Hölder class for the (
QGSW
).

Theorem 3.5
.

For
0<\gamma<1,
if
q_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R}),
then there exists
T(M)>0
such that the (
QGSW
) has a unique solution
q(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R})
for any time
t\in(-T(M),T(M)).

Proof.

Assume that
\tilde{\rho}\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{c}
and
\tilde{v}\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}
is also a solution to (
QGSW
) with the same initial data. Then we can find a trajectory
\tilde{X}(\cdot,t)
associated with
\tilde{v}(\cdot,t)
such that
\tilde{\rho}
is transported by
\tilde{X}.
But by uniqueness of the flow map, Theorem
3.4
,
X=\tilde{X}
so
\tilde{\rho}(\cdot,t)=\rho_{0}(\tilde{X}^{-1}(\cdot,t))=\rho_{0}(X^{-1}(\cdot,t))=\rho(\cdot,t)
and by convolving with the kernel we also have that
\tilde{v}=v.

Finally, we note that all solutions arise from trajectories, since
v\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}
; see Subsection
4.2
for the general argument in lower regularity. This concludes the proof.
∎

We conclude this subsection with the proof of Lemma
3.3
.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

In order to ease the notation, we omit the time dependence of
X
and
Y
.

We first estimate
\left\|F^{\prime}(X)Y\right\|_{L^{\infty}}
. Since the set
O_{M}
consists of one-to-one and onto functions, the change of variables
X^{-1}(x)=\alpha

F^{\prime}(X)Y\circ X^{-1}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla K(x-x^{\prime})(Y(X^{-1}(x))-Y(X^{-1}(x^{\prime})))q_{0}(X^{-1}(x^{\prime}))dx^{\prime},

By the mean value inequality,

|Y(X^{-1}(x))-Y(X^{-1}(x^{\prime}))|\leq\left\|(\nabla_{\alpha}Y\circ X^{-1})\nabla_{x}X^{-1}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}|x-x^{\prime}|,

and combining this with (
18

(30)

\displaystyle|F^{\prime}(X)Y\circ X^{-1}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq c\left\|(\nabla_{\alpha}Y\circ X^{-1})\nabla_{x}X^{-1}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\left(\int_{|x-x^{\prime}|\leq 1}|x-x^{\prime}|^{-2+1}dx^{\prime}+\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}|x-x^{\prime}|\geq 1\\
x^{\prime}\in\operatorname{supp}\;f\end{subarray}}|x-x^{\prime}|^{-2+1}dx^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leq c\left\|(\nabla_{\alpha}Y\circ X^{-1})\nabla_{x}X^{-1}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left\|f\right\|_{\gamma}

where,
f(x^{\prime})\coloneqq q_{0}(X^{-1}(x^{\prime}))
, and the constant

depends on the support of
q_{0}
.

Using (
16

12

\displaystyle\left\|f(x^{\prime})\right\|_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|q_{0}\circ X^{-1}(x^{\prime})\right\|_{\gamma}\leq c\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{\gamma}(1+\left\|X\right\|_{1,\gamma}^{3\gamma}).

Since
X\in O_{M}
(see (
28
)), both
\left\|X\right\|_{1,\gamma}
and
\left\|\nabla X\right\|_{\gamma}
are uniformly controlled, and thus all
X

c

30

\left\|F^{\prime}(X)Y\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq c\left\|Y\right\|_{1,\gamma}\left\|{q_{0}}\right\|_{\gamma}.

Next, we estimate the Hölder norm of
\nabla F^{\prime}(X)Y.
Thus, we need first to calculate the distributional derivative of
H(x)=F^{\prime}(X)Y\circ X^{-1}(x).
To do so, we follow the scheme in
[
17
, p. 165]
for the Euler equation.

Rewrite
H(x)
as

H(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}R(x,x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

R(x,x^{\prime})=\nabla K(x-x^{\prime})(Y(X^{-1}(x))-Y(X^{-1}(x^{\prime}))).

We want to compute the distributional derivative of the kernel
R(x+x^{\prime},x^{\prime}).
To do so, note previously that, given
h>0
and

we have, by Taylor expansion of

and (
4
)

\lim_{h\to 0}R(x^{\prime}+ah,x^{\prime})h=\nabla[Y\circ X^{-1}](x)c(a).

Indeed, recall that

\nabla K\cdot Y=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{1}K^{(1)}&\partial_{2}K^{(1)}\\[2.84526pt]
\partial_{1}K^{(2)}&\partial_{2}K^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}Y_{1}\\
Y_{2}\end{pmatrix},

and focus on the contribution from the second row, first column, i.e.,
\partial_{1}K^{(2)}\,Y_{1}
; the remaining terms are analogous. Then, from (
21
), we have

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\partial_{1}K^{(2)}(ah)\big(Y_{1}(X^{-1}(x^{\prime}+ah))-Y_{1}(X^{-1}(x^{\prime}))\big)h

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}\left[\frac{a_{2}^{2}-a_{1}^{2}}{h}K_{1}(\varepsilon|ah|)-\varepsilon a_{1}^{2}K_{0}(\varepsilon|ah|)\right](\nabla[Y_{1}\circ X^{-1}](x^{\prime})\cdot ah+o(h))h

\displaystyle=C_{a}\nabla[Y_{1}\circ X^{-1}](x^{\prime}){\cdot a}

where in the last step we use that by (
4

K_{1}(\varepsilon h)\sim\frac{1}{\varepsilon h}
when

is small and
K_{0}(\varepsilon h)\sim\ln{(\varepsilon h)}
, and therefore

\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}\frac{a_{2}^{2}-a_{1}^{2}}{h}K_{1}(\varepsilon|ah|)h^{2}=\frac{a_{2}^{2}-a_{1}^{2}}{2\pi}C,

We are now ready to compute the desired distributional derivative. Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})
a test function, then

\displaystyle\langle R(x+x^{\prime},x^{\prime}),\partial_{x_{k}}\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle={-}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\partial_{x_{k}}\varphi(x)R(x+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})dx=-\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{|x|\geq\delta}\partial_{x_{k}}\varphi(x)R(x+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})dx

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{|x|\geq\delta}\varphi(x)\partial_{x_{k}}R(x+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})dx-\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{|x|=\delta}\varphi(x)R(x+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})\frac{x_{k}}{|x|}{ds}

\displaystyle=p.v.\int\varphi(x)\partial_{x_{k}}R(x+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})dx-c\nabla_{\alpha}Y(X^{-1}(x))\nabla_{x}X^{-1}(x)\varphi(0),

where the equality in the third line follows from Green’s first identity and in the last equality, we used the observation made above by passing to polar coordinates in the boundary integral, with
x=\delta\,e^{i\theta}
playing the role of
ah
.

Then, since
\partial_{x_{k}}R(x,x^{\prime})=\partial_{x_{k}}[R(\cdot+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})](x-x^{\prime})
, we get that the distributional derivative of
H(x)
can be written as

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{k}}H(x)

\displaystyle=p.v.\int\partial_{x_{k}}\nabla K(x-x^{\prime})(Y(X^{-1}(x))-Y(X^{-1}(x^{\prime})))f(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle+p.v.\int\nabla K(x-x^{\prime})\nabla_{\alpha}Y(X^{-1}(x))\partial_{x_{k}}X^{-1}(x)f(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle-c\nabla_{\alpha}Y(X^{-1}(x))\nabla_{x}X^{-1}(x)f(x)\eqqcolon I+II+III

Applying (
11
) again, and estimating
\left\|f\right\|_{\gamma}
as before, it is easy to check that

\left\|III\right\|_{\gamma}\leq c\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{\gamma}\left\|Y\right\|_{1,\gamma}.

For
II
, we set

\tilde{f}(x^{\prime})\coloneqq\nabla_{\alpha}Y(X^{-1}(x))\,\partial_{x_{k}}X^{-1}(x)\,f(x^{\prime}).

Since
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{c}
and
X,Y\in O_{M}
,
\tilde{f}
is also in
\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{c}
.
Applying (
24
) and (
25
) from Lemma
2.3
to the kernel
P=\nabla K
with
\tilde{f}
and using once more the estimate for
\left\|f\right\|_{\gamma}
we obtain

\left\|II\right\|_{\gamma}\leq c\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{\gamma}\left\|Y\right\|_{1,\gamma}.

To estimate
I
we follow the proof of
[
7
, Lemma 15]
. The main difference being that since
K
or its derivatives are not homogeneous, but one has explicit formulas for them, so more computations are needed.

Notice that
Y\circ X^{-1}\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}.
Thus, we can write its Taylor series centered at
x^{\prime}
as

Y(X^{-1}(x))=Y(X^{-1}(x^{\prime}))+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\partial_{i}(Y\circ X^{-1})(x^{\prime})(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})+{\mathcal{R}}(x,x^{\prime})

with
|\mathcal{R}(x,x^{\prime})|\leq c\left\|Y\circ X^{-1}\right\|_{1,\gamma}|x-x^{\prime}|^{1+\gamma}.
Now, if we add and subtract some terms to
I
we obtain

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=p.v.\int\partial_{x_{k}}\nabla K(x-x^{\prime})(Y(X^{-1}(x))-Y(X^{-1}(x^{\prime}))-\nabla Y(X^{-1}(x^{\prime}))(x-x^{\prime}))f(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{2}p.v.\int(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})\partial_{x_{k}}\nabla K(x-x^{\prime})\partial_{i}(Y\circ X^{-1})(x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}\coloneqq I_{1}f(x)+I_{2}f(x)

We compute now some partials of
\nabla K
, the ones not computed are symmetrical to one of the following. To do so, recall the expression of
\nabla K
shown on (
21
).

\partial_{1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}\frac{\varepsilon x_{1}x_{2}}{|x|^{3}}K_{2}(\varepsilon|x|)\right)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}\left[\frac{\varepsilon x_{2}(x_{2}^{2}-x_{1}^{2})}{|x|^{4}}K_{2}(\varepsilon|x|)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}x_{1}^{2}x_{2}}{|x|^{3}}K_{2}^{\prime}(\varepsilon|x|)\right]\\
=\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}\left[\frac{\varepsilon x_{2}(x_{2}^{2}-x_{1}^{2})}{|x|^{4}}K_{2}(\varepsilon|x|)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}x_{1}^{2}x_{2}}{|x|^{3}}\left(-K_{3}(\varepsilon|x|)+\frac{2}{\varepsilon|x|}K_{2}(\varepsilon|x|)\right)\right]

has zero integral on spheres. Also, by (
20

\frac{C}{|x|^{2}}
with

\varepsilon
.

If instead of using the expression used before for

\displaystyle\int_{|x|=R}\frac{x_{2}^{2}(x_{2}^{2}-3x_{1}^{2})}{|x|^{4}}\,K_{2}(\varepsilon|x|)\,dS

For the rest of the components, we differentiate using the form given in Remark

\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}\partial_{1}\left(\frac{x_{2}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}}{|x|^{3}}K_{1}(\varepsilon|x|)-\frac{\varepsilon x_{1}^{2}}{|x|^{2}}K_{0}(\varepsilon|x|)\right)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}\Bigg[\frac{2x_{1}(x_{1}^{2}-3x_{2}^{2})}{|x|^{5}}K_{1}(\varepsilon|x|)\\

|\partial_{x_{k}}\nabla K(x-x^{\prime})|\leq C|x|^{-3},

. Thus, if we define

G(x,x^{\prime})\coloneqq\partial_{x_{k}}\nabla K(x-x^{\prime})(Y(X^{-1}(x))-Y(X^{-1}(x^{\prime}))-\nabla Y(X^{-1}(x^{\prime}))(x-x^{\prime}))

we have

|G(x,x^{\prime})|\leq\frac{C\left\|Y\circ X^{-1}\right\|_{1,\gamma}}{|x-x^{\prime}|^{2-\gamma}}\text{ and }|\nabla_{x}G(x-x^{\prime})|\leq\frac{C\left\|Y\circ X^{-1}\right\|_{1,\gamma}}{|x-x^{\prime}|^{3-\gamma}}.

Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
B\coloneqq B(x_{1},3|x_{1}-x_{2}|).
Then

\displaystyle I_{1}f(x_{1})-I_{1}f(x_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B}(G(x_{1},x^{\prime})-G(x_{2},x^{\prime}))f(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\int_{B}G(x_{1},x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}-\int_{B}G(x_{2},x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

so, by the mean value theorem and the bounds for

and

|I_{1}f(x_{1})-I_{1}f(x_{2})|\leq c\left\|Y\circ X^{-1}\right\|_{1,\gamma}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\gamma}.

Gathering all of the above estimates and bounding
\left\|f\right\|_{\gamma}
as shown, one gets the desired result

\left\|F^{\prime}(X)Y\right\|_{1,\gamma}\leq c\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{\gamma}\left\|Y\right\|_{1,\gamma}.

∎

3.2.
Global existence of smooth solutions

In the previous subsection, we showed that the quasi-geostrophic shallow water equation, (
QGSW
), admits a unique local-in-time solution if the initial data is in
\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}.
In this section, we claim that such a solution actually exists for all time.

We will only provide a sketch of the proof and omit most of the details, since it closely follows the argument for the Euler equations given in
[
17
, Chapter 4]
, and it relies on the following classical continuation result for ODEs.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
O\subset B
be an open subset of a Banach spaces
B,
and let
F:O\to B
be a locally Lipschitz continuous operator. Then the unique solution
X\in C^{1}([0,T];O)
to the autonomous ODE,

\frac{dX}{dt}=F(X),\quad X|_{t=0}=X_{0}\in O,

either exists globally in time or
T<\infty
and
X(t)
leaves the open set
O
as
t\to T.

Since the particle trajectories are volume preserving, as the velocity field is divergence-free, the only way that a solution can leave the set
O_{M}
is for
\left\|X\right\|_{1,\gamma}
to become unbounded as
t\to T^{*}.
Thus, we obtain a sufficient condition for global-in-time existence if we give a sufficient condition for
\left\|X\right\|_{1,\gamma}
to be a priori bounded. This, in turn, is guaranteed by an a priori bound on
\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\nabla v(\cdot,s)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\,ds
.

Finally, one can see that
\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\nabla v(\cdot,s)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}ds
is controlled by
\int_{0}^{t}\left\|q(\cdot,s)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}ds,
and since
q
is conserved along particle trajectories it cannot become unbounded. As a consequence, we have proven Theorem
3.1
. That is for
0<\gamma<1,
if
q_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R}),
then the (
QGSW
) has a unique solution
q(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R})
for any time
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 2
.

The proofs of this part rely mainly on Grönwall’s lemma, and the observation that by (
29
) and
v=K*q
we have

\nabla v=p.v.\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla K(x-x^{\prime})q(x^{\prime},t)dx^{\prime}+cq(x,t)

and (
24
) from Lemma
2.3
.

It is worth noting that, with the previous ingredients, the proof in this case is simpler than that in
[
17
]
, since they work with the vorticity formulation of the three-dimensional Euler equations, which is not a transport equation.

4.
Weak solutions to the QGSW equation

The goal of this section is to address the Yudovich problem, namely, to prove well-posedness for initial data
q_{0}\in L_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}).
For such non-smooth data, one needs to work with the weak formulation of (
QGSW
)

Definition 3
.

Given
q_{0}\in L_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}),
the pair
(v,q)
is a weak solution to (
QGSW
) with initial data
q_{0}(x),
provided that

(i)

q\in L^{\infty}\big([0,T];L_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})\big),

(ii)

v=K*q,

(iii)

for all
\varphi\in C^{1}\big([0,T];C_{0}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})\big)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(x,T)q(x,T)dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(x,0)q_{0}(x)dx=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\frac{d\varphi}{dt}+v\cdot\nabla\varphi\right)qdxdt

4.1.
Existence of weak solutions

To prove the existence of weak solutions to (
QGSW
), we follow the two-step argument presented in
[
17
, Chapter 8]
. In this subsection we only give a general idea, since the proof for the Euler equation can be mostly replicated due to the similar properties of the defining kernels, even though the QGSW kernel is not homogeneous. Any significant differences are explicitly mentioned. We recommend consulting
[
17
, Chapter 8]
for full details.

The first step is to smooth the initial data via a convolution with a mollifier and then apply Theorem
3.1
to get pairs
(v^{\delta},q^{\delta})
which are smooth solutions, where
\delta
is the mollification parameter.

The second step is to extract subsequences
(v^{\delta^{\prime}})
and
(q^{\delta^{\prime}})
that converge to a pair
(v,q)
satisfying the weak formulation of Definition
3
. This can be formalized in the following proposition:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
q_{0}\in L_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}),
and let
q^{\delta},v^{\delta}
be a smooth solution on
[0,T]
with the regularized initial data

q^{\delta}_{0}(x)=\delta^{-2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho\left(\frac{x-y}{\delta}\right)q_{0}(y)dy,

for
\delta>0
and
\rho\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
nonnegative and with integral equal to one. Then, for all
t\in[0,T],

(i)

q^{\delta},v^{\delta}
are uniformly bounded and

\left\|v^{\delta}(\cdot,t)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq c\|q^{\delta}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}}\leq C\|q_{0}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}},

(ii)

there exist functions
q(\cdot,t)\in L_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})
and
v=K*q
such that for all
t\in[0,T]

q^{\delta}(\cdot,t)\to q(\cdot,t)\text{ in }L^{1},

v^{\delta}(\cdot,t)\to_{x}v(\cdot,t)\text{ locally.}

To prove the second part of the previous proposition, the divergence-free property of the velocity field, which ensures volume-preserving trajectories, is crucial. The following lemma, which will be important for the uniqueness, is also needed.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let the initial vorticity
q_{0}\in L_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})
and let
q^{\delta},v^{\delta}
be a smooth solution on
[0,T]
with the regularized initial data
q^{\delta}_{0}(x).
Then
v^{\delta}(\cdot,t)
is Log-Lipschitz continuous:

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|v^{\delta}(x^{1},t)-v^{\delta}(x^{2},t)|\leq c\|q_{0}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}}\ell(|x^{1}-x^{2}|),

where
\ell(x)=|x|(1-\ln^{-}{|x|})
and
\ln^{-}{a}=\ln{a}
for
0<a<1
and
\ln^{-}{a}=0
for
a\geq 1.

The proof of this lemma, follows the standard argument for 2D Euler kernel and can be easily adapted to (
QGSW
) using the properties in Lemma
2.2
.

We summarize the conclusions of the previous results into the following existence result.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
q_{0}\in L_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}).
Then for all time there exists a weak solution
(v,q)
to (
QGSW
) in the sense of Definition
3
.

4.2.
Uniqueness of weak solutions

For this part we follow
[
13
, Section 3]
, where the proof of uniqueness is based on the stability argument for flow maps introduced in
[
10
]
, although the computations are considerably simpler in our setting. We first prove uniqueness of Lagrangian weak solutions, that is, solutions given by the flow map defined in (
27
), and then show that all possible solutions are of this kind, thus proving uniqueness.

Consider
(v,q)
and
(\tilde{v},\tilde{q})
two Lagrangian solutions with the same initial data
q_{0}\in L_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}).
Let
X,\tilde{X}
be the unique flows associated to each solution. Then by (
26
) we have

\displaystyle X-\tilde{X}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}[v({X(x,s),s})-\tilde{v}(\tilde{X}(x,s),s)]ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}[v({X(x,s),s})-v(\tilde{X}(x,s),s)+(v-\tilde{v})(\tilde{X}(x,s),s)]ds

Since
v
is Log-Lipschitz (see Lemma
4.2
) we get

|X-\tilde{X}|\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\ell(X(x,s)-\tilde{X}(x,s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}|(v-\tilde{v})(\tilde{X}(x,s),s)|ds.

We focus now on the second term of the previous identity. For any fixed
s
we have

\displaystyle|(v-\tilde{v})(\tilde{X}(x,s),s)|

\displaystyle=|(K*q-K*\tilde{q})(\tilde{X}(x,s))|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K(\tilde{X}(x,s)-y)q(y)dy-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K(\tilde{X}(x,s)-y)\tilde{q}(y)dy\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K(\tilde{X}(x,s)-X(y,s))q_{0}(y)dy-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K(\tilde{X}(x,s)-\tilde{X}(y,s))q_{0}(y)dy\right|

where in the last equality we have used that
q
is transported by the flow.

Putting together the preceding estimates, we obtain

(31)

|X-\tilde{X}|\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\ell(X(x,s)-\tilde{X}(x,s))ds\\
+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|K(\tilde{X}(x,s)-X(y,s))-K(\tilde{X}(x,s)-\tilde{X}(y,s))||q_{0}(y)|dyds

We now introduce the following Lemma, which is in essence Lemma 2.3 from
[
13
]
, and which will be of great use.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}),
and
K
the kernel defined in (
1
), the following holds

(i)

\left\|K*f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}}

(ii)

For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
x\neq y
we have that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|K(x-z)-K(y-z)||f(z)|dz\leq C\|f\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}}\ell(|x-y|).

Let
\alpha
be a function in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})
with
\alpha>0
. We also introduce the density
\eta=|q_{0}|+\alpha
.

We proceed by integrating (
31
) against the measure
\eta(x)dx
and applying Fubini’s theorem to obtain that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|X(x,t)-\tilde{X}(x,t)|\eta(x)dx\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\ell(X(x,s)-\tilde{X}(x,s))\eta(x)dxds\\
+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|K(\tilde{X}(x,s)-X(y,s))-K(\tilde{X}(x,s)-\tilde{X}(y,s))||q_{0}(y)|dy\eta(x)dxds\\
=C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\ell(X(x,s)-\tilde{X}(x,s))\eta(x)dxds\\
+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|q_{0}(y)|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|K(\tilde{X}(x,s)-X(y,s))-K(\tilde{X}(x,s)-\tilde{X}(y,s))|\eta(x)dxdyds

Notice now that the last integral can be bounded using Lemma
4.4
(ii), so one gets

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|X(x,t)-\tilde{X}(x,t)|\eta(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\ell(X(x,s)-\tilde{X}(x,s))\eta(x)dxds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|q_{0}(y)|\|\eta\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}}\ell(|X(y,s)-\tilde{X}(y,s)|)dyds

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\left\|q\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\eta\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}})\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\ell(X(x,s)-\tilde{X}(x,s))\eta(x)dxds.

To conclude, since
\ell
is concave and
\eta\in L^{1},
we end up with

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|X(x,t)-\tilde{X}(x,t)|\eta(x)dx\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\ell\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|X(x,t)-\tilde{X}(x,t)|\eta(x)dx\right)ds,

so the uniqueness of the flow-maps follows due to Osgood’s Lemma, see, for instance,
[
3
, Lemma 3.4]
.

Finally, we prove that all solutions are Lagrangian. Let
(v,q)
be a solution with initial data
q_{0}
. Define
X
the flow map associated with the velocity field
v
as the unique solution of (
26
). Uniqueness of the flow map follows from the log-lipschitz regularity of
v
(see Lemma
4.2
). If we now define
Q=q_{0}(X^{-1}(\cdot,t)),
it is clear that
Q
satisfies the same transport equation as
q
with velocity
v
. Therefore, by classical results on transport equations (see, for instance,
[
11
]
) we deduce that
Q=q,
so
q
is a Lagrangian solution.

We have thus proved the following result.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
q_{0}\in L_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}).
Then for all time there exists a unique weak solution
(v,q)
to (
QGSW
) in the sense of Definition
3
.

5.
Vortex patches

Weak solutions introduced in the previous section have well-defined particle trajectories along which the vorticity is preserved. If we start at some time with a vorticity field that is constant inside some region, the region evolves in time keeping the same constant vorticity inside. This fact leads to the natural class of weak solutions known as vortex patches, previously introduced.

If the boundary of the patch is sufficiently smooth, then the velocity field induced by the patch can be completely determined by the position of the boundary. If the boundary is at least piecewise
C^{1},
then the velocity induced by the patch at time
t
,
\Omega_{t}
, is given by

v=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\Omega_{t}}\nabla^{\perp}K_{0}(\varepsilon|x-x^{\prime}|)dx^{\prime},

thus applying Green’s formula yields

v=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}K_{0}(\varepsilon|x-x^{\prime}|)[-n_{2}(x^{\prime}),n_{1}(x^{\prime})]^{t}dx^{\prime}.

Therefore, the motion of the boundary of the patch satisfies the contour dynamics equation (CDE):

(CDE)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial z}{\partial t}(\alpha,t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}{\int_{S^{1}}}K_{0}(\varepsilon|z(\alpha,t)-z(\alpha^{\prime},t)|)z_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime},t)d\alpha^{\prime}\\
&z(\alpha,t)|_{t=0}=z_{0}(\alpha)\end{aligned}\right.

where
z(\alpha,t)
is a Lagrangian parametrization of the curve at time
t
, that is
z(\alpha,t)={X(z(\alpha,0),t)},
where
X
is the particle trajectory map for the flow.

Notice that taking
\varepsilon\to 0
in the above expression does not directly yield the Euler contour dynamics equation. However, from the derivation above using Green’s formula, it is clear that subtracting a constant from the kernel does not affect the integral over a closed curve. Hence, one can replace
K_{0}
by

\tilde{K_{0}}\coloneqq K_{0}-\ln\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

in (
CDE
) to recover the Euler equation in the limit case.

5.1.
Local existence and uniqueness of solutions to the CDE

We will apply Picard’s Theorem (Theorem
3.2
) to the study of solutions to the (
CDE
). The Banach space we will consider is
\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}(S^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})
and as an open set we define for any
M>0
the set

O^{M}=\left\{z(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}\;|\;\frac{1}{M}<\frac{|z(\alpha,t)-z(\alpha^{\prime},t)|}{|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|}<M\right\}

which is nonempty and consists only of one-to-one mappings of
S^{1}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
F:O^{M}\to\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}(S^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})
be defined by

F(z(\alpha,{t}))\coloneqq\int_{S^{1}}K_{0}(\varepsilon|z(\alpha,t)-z(\alpha^{\prime},t)|)z_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime},t)d\alpha^{\prime}.

Then
F
is a locally Lipschitz continuous operator from
O^{M}
to
\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}(S^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

In order to prove the result we will use the following lemma:

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
K:S^{1}\times S^{1}\setminus\{\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}\}\to\mathbb{R}
be a kernel such that

(32)

\sup_{\alpha\in S^{1}}\int_{S^{1}}|K(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})|\,d\alpha^{\prime}<\infty,\qquad\sup_{\alpha\in S^{1}}\int_{S^{1}}|\partial_{\alpha}K(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})|\,d\alpha^{\prime}<\infty.

For
f\in L^{\infty}(S^{1})
define

Tf(\alpha)\coloneqq\int_{S^{1}}K(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})f(\alpha^{\prime})\,d\alpha^{\prime}.

Then
Tf
is Lipschitz on
S^{1}
, i.e.
Tf\in\operatorname{Lip}(S^{1})
, and there exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on the bounds in
(
32
), such that

\|Tf\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left\|Tf\right\|_{1}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{\infty}},

where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1}
denotes the Lipschitz norm (that is Definition
1
with
\gamma=1
).

Proof.

By the first assumption in (
32
), for any
\alpha\in S^{1}
we have

|Tf(\alpha)|\leq\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{S^{1}}|K(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})|\,d\alpha^{\prime}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{\infty}},

and therefore
\|Tf\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

To estimate the Lipschitz seminorm, let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in S^{1}
and set

d\coloneqq|\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}|
. We decompose

\displaystyle Tf(\alpha_{1})-Tf(\alpha_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{|\alpha_{1}-\alpha^{\prime}|<3d}K(\alpha_{1},\alpha^{\prime})f(\alpha^{\prime})\,d\alpha^{\prime}-\int_{|\alpha_{1}-\alpha^{\prime}|<3d}K(\alpha_{2},\alpha^{\prime})f(\alpha^{\prime})\,d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\int_{|\alpha_{1}-\alpha^{\prime}|\geq 3d}\big(K(\alpha_{1},\alpha^{\prime})-K(\alpha_{2},\alpha^{\prime})\big)f(\alpha^{\prime})\,d\alpha^{\prime}\eqqcolon I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.

Using the boundedness of
K
, we obtain

|I_{1}|\leq\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\sup_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}|K(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})|\int_{|\alpha_{1}-\alpha^{\prime}|<3d}d\alpha^{\prime}\leq Cd\,\|f\|_{L^{\infty}},

and the same bound holds for
I_{2}
.

For
I_{3}
, by the Mean Value Inequality there exists
\tilde{\alpha}
between
\alpha_{1}
and

\alpha_{2}
such that

|K(\alpha_{1},\alpha^{\prime})-K(\alpha_{2},\alpha^{\prime})|\leq d\,|\partial_{\alpha}K(\tilde{\alpha},\alpha^{\prime})|.

Therefore,

|I_{3}|\leq d\,\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{|\alpha_{1}-\alpha^{\prime}|\geq 3d}|\partial_{\alpha}K(\tilde{\alpha},\alpha^{\prime})|\,d\alpha^{\prime}\leq Cd\,\|f\|_{L^{\infty}},

using the second assumption in (
32
).

Combining the above estimates yields

|Tf(\alpha_{1})-Tf(\alpha_{2})|\leq Cd\,\|f\|_{L^{\infty}},

which shows that
Tf\in\operatorname{Lip}(S^{1})
and completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove that
F
satisfies Picard’s theorem conditions.

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

We will use the series expansions of the kernel,
K_{0}
, and its derivative,
-K_{1}
, which we rewrite here:

(33)

\displaystyle\begin{split}K_{0}(r)=-\log\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)-\log\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)h_{1}(r)+h_{2}(r),\\
K_{1}(r)=\frac{1}{r}+\frac{r}{2}\ln\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)g_{1}(r)-\frac{r}{4}g_{2}(r),\end{split}

where
h_{1},h_{2},g_{1},g_{2}
are analytic functions given by

h_{1}(r)\coloneqq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2m}}{(m!)^{2}},\quad h_{2}(r)\coloneqq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2m}}{(m!)^{2}}\psi(m+1),

g_{1}(r)\coloneqq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2m}}{m!(m+1)!},\quad g_{2}(r)\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\psi(k+1)+\psi(k+2))\frac{\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2k}}{k!(k+1)!},

being
\psi(1)=-\gamma,\;\psi(m+1)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1}{k}-\gamma,
being
\gamma
Euler’s constant.

We see first that
F
is a bounded operator from
O^{M}
to
\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}(S^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})
. To do so we start by bounding
\left\|F\right\|_{\gamma}
(we do not really need to bound the
\gamma
seminorm, but the computations will be useful later).

Using the kernel series expression, and omitting the time dependence to ease the notation, we have

(34)

\displaystyle F(z(\alpha))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}K_{0}(\varepsilon|z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime})|)z_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\log|z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime})|+\ln{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\right)z_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\log|z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime})|+\ln{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\right)h_{1}(\varepsilon|z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime})|)z_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{2\pi}h_{2}(\varepsilon|z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime})|)z_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}\eqqcolon I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}

Notice that
I_{1}
corresponds to the CDE operator of Euler’s equation (and coincides exactly with it when one considers
\tilde{K_{0}}
instead of
K_{0}
). Therefore, by
[
17
, Lemma 8.10]
we have that for
z\in O^{M},

\left\|I_{1}\right\|_{\gamma}\leq C_{\gamma,M}(\left\|z_{\alpha}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+1),\;\left\|\nabla_{\alpha}I_{1}\right\|_{\gamma}\leq C(\left\|z\right\|_{\gamma}+1).

For
I_{2}
we have

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime})|h_{1}(\varepsilon|z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime})|)z_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle-\ln{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h_{1}(\varepsilon|z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime})|)z_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}\coloneqq I_{21}+I_{22}.

Both
I_{21}
and
I_{22}
are of the form
Tf(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathcal{K}(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})z_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime},
where the kernel satisfies the conditions of Lemma
5.2
, therefore
\left\|I_{2}\right\|_{\gamma}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\left\|z_{\alpha}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}.
The term
I_{3}
can be treated in the same way with
\mathcal{K}(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})=h_{2}(\varepsilon|z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime})|).
Moreover, one can see that, in both cases,
C_{\varepsilon}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0

Thus, combining the above estimates one gets

\left\|F\right\|_{\gamma}\leq C(\left\|z_{\alpha}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+1).

We now bound
\left\|\nabla_{\alpha}F\right\|_{\gamma}.
To do so, we first decompose the functional as in (
34
) so

\left\|\nabla_{\alpha}F\right\|_{\gamma}\leq\left\|\nabla_{\alpha}I_{1}\right\|_{\gamma}+\left\|\nabla_{\alpha}I_{2}\right\|_{\gamma}+\left\|\nabla_{\alpha}I_{3}\right\|_{\gamma}.

Since
I_{1}
corresponds to the Euler part, its contribution has already been handled above. For the other two cases one notices that it is possible to differentiate under the integral sign. Moreover, one can check that all of the terms resulting from differentiating with respect to
\alpha
satisfy the conditions of Lemma
5.2
. Indeed, notice that the worst term comes from differentiating
I_{21},
but since
h_{1}(r)\sim r^{2}
as
r\to 0
, the resulting kernel behaves like
|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\ln|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|
which satisfies conditions of Lemma
5.2
. In particular, the non-Eulerian contributions
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
enjoy better regularity: they define Lipschitz operators, and hence their Hölder bounds follow directly.

We have so far seen that
F
is a bounded operator from
O^{M}\to\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}
. We now see that it is locally Lipschitz. To do so, we bound its Gateaux derivative as in previous sections. For
y\in O^{M}

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(z(\alpha))y

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\delta}(F(z(\alpha)+\delta y(\alpha)))\big|_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}-K_{1}(\varepsilon|z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime})|)\varepsilon\frac{(y(\alpha)-y(\alpha^{\prime}))(z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime}))}{|z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime})|}z_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{2\pi}K_{0}(\varepsilon|z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime})|)y_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}\eqqcolon G_{1}(z)y+G_{2}(z)y

Notice that
G_{2}(z)y
is essentially like
F(z)
. Therefore, one can repeat the same arguments to see that it is bounded on
\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}
.

We just have to see that
\left\|G_{1}(z)\right\|_{1,\gamma}
is bounded. To do so, we will use the series expansion on
K_{1}
:

\displaystyle G_{1}(z)y

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{(y(\alpha)-y(\alpha^{\prime}))(z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime}))}{|z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime})|^{2}}z_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}

5.2.
Global regularity for vortex patches

In the previous subsection we proved local in time existence and uniqueness of solution to the (
CDE
), and since we are dealing with a transport problem this is equivalent to dealing with the vortex patch problem. Therefore, we have seen that if
\partial\Omega\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}
, being
\Omega
a bounded, simply connected region of constant vorticity, there exists a unique solution to the vortex patch problem that remains regular on a time interval
[0,T^{*}]
. Our goal now is to prove that the solution remains regular for all time.

In order to do so, we reformulate the vortex patch problem in terms of a scalar function,
\varphi(x,t)
, that defines the patch boundary by
\{\varphi(x,t)>0\}=\Omega(t)
and is convected with the flow by

(35)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}+v\cdot\nabla\varphi

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\varphi(x,0)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{0}(x).

The function
\varphi
is usually referred to as the
defining function
of the patch, and the previous equation guarantees that it defines the patch boundary at later times if it initially satisfies
\{\varphi(x,0)>0\}=\Omega_{0}
. By the weak solution theory and the equivalence with the (
CDE
) for bounded domains with smooth boundaries, one gets that if
X(x,t)
are the particle trajectories associated with initial data
\chi_{\Omega_{0}}
, then a solution to the previous equation is just
\varphi(x,t)=\varphi_{0}(X^{{-1}}(x,t)).

As in the Euler case, we prove global regularity for the patch boundary by the equivalence of the evolution of
\varphi
with vortex patch equation.

Theorem 5.3
.

Given
\Omega_{0}
bounded, and
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})
satisfying

\{\varphi(x,0)>0\}=\Omega_{0}\text{ and }\inf_{x\in\partial\Omega_{0}}|\nabla\varphi(x,0)|\geq m>0,

there exists a constant
C
so that (
35
) has a unique solution
\varphi(x,t)
defined for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and all

and satisfies

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla v(\cdot,t)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\nabla v(\cdot,0)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}e^{C|t|},

\displaystyle\left|\nabla\varphi(\cdot,t)\right|_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\nabla\varphi(\cdot,0)\right|_{\gamma}{\exp}[(C_{0}+\gamma)e^{C|t|}],

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\varphi(\cdot,t)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\nabla\varphi(\cdot,0)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\exp[e^{C|t|}],

\displaystyle|\nabla\varphi(\cdot,t)|_{\inf}

\displaystyle\geq|\nabla\varphi(\cdot,0)|_{\inf}\exp[-e^{C|t|}].

Here
C_{0}
is a fixed constant and
|\nabla\varphi(\cdot)|_{\inf}=\inf_{x\in\partial\Omega}|\nabla\varphi(x)|

The proof of Theorem

[

Proposition 5.4

Assume that

Remark 4

The appearance of the commutator is not unexpected: the singular, distributional part of

Proposition 5.6

\displaystyle\left|\nabla\varphi(\cdot,t)\right|_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\varphi(\cdot,t)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle|{\nabla\varphi(\cdot,t)}|_{\inf}

We will only prove Proposition

6.
Convergence to the 2D Euler Equations

In this section, we study the behavior of solutions to (
QGSW
) as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
the regime in which the system formally reduces to the 2D Euler equations.
Physically, this corresponds to an infinite Rossby deformation radius, where free–surface effects cease to matter;
mathematically, it reflects the passage from the regularized operator
(\Delta-\varepsilon^{2})^{-1}

to the classical Biot–Savart law
\Delta^{-1}
, thus connecting (
QGSW
) with standard Euler dynamics.

Our convergence result in this direction is the following.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
q_{0}\in c^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R})
for
0<\gamma<1.
Then there exist unique solutions
q^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t),

\omega(\cdot,t)
in
c^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R})
to the (
QGSW
) equations and the Euler equation, respectively, with initial data
q_{0}
, and a time
T>0
such that

(38)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|q^{\varepsilon}(t)-\omega(t)\right\|_{\gamma}=0.

Notice that although we proved existence and uniqueness of solutions with initial data on
\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{c},
our convergence result is not a convergence result in that space but instead is the so-called little Hölder space,
c^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R})
, which is the closed subspace of
\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R})
consisting of those functions satisfying the vanishing condition

\lim_{h\to 0}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\\
0<|x-y|<h\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\gamma}}=0.

It is easy to see that
c^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R})
is the closure of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R})
in the usual Hölder norm of
\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R})
. This fact is essential for our proof.

The existence and uniqueness result for the Euler equation in the little Hölder space can be seen in
[
19
]
(see also
[
16
]
for more general kernels). For the (
QGSW
) one can easily adapt the proofs found there, we give here a brief sketch.

Consider as in Section
3
the equation for the particle trajectories

(39)

\displaystyle\frac{dX^{\varepsilon}}{dt}(\alpha,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K^{\varepsilon}(X^{\varepsilon}(\alpha,t)-X^{\varepsilon}(\alpha^{\prime},t))\,q_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\,d\alpha^{\prime}\eqqcolon F^{\varepsilon}(X^{\varepsilon}(\alpha,t)),

\displaystyle X^{\varepsilon}(\alpha,0)

\displaystyle=\alpha,

where
K^{\varepsilon}
denotes the kernel defined in (
1
), written to make the dependence on
\varepsilon
explicit. For
\varepsilon=0
one recovers the 2D Euler kernel
K^{0}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{x^{\perp}}{|x|^{2}}.

We will apply Picard-Lindelöf theorem (Theorem

) with

U_{\delta}=\left\{X:{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}:X=e+\varphi_{X},\varphi_{X}\in c^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})\text{ and }\left\|\varphi_{X}\right\|_{1,\gamma}<\delta\right\},

For

), and once more (
16
), and in the third inequality we estimate the
\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}
norm of the difference by the
\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}
norm — not an optimal bound, but sufficient for our purposes. Notice that although
\left\|\phi_{\eta}\right\|_{1,\gamma}
may grow as
\eta\to 0
, it remains a fixed finite constant for each fixed
\eta>0
. Therefore, if we can prove that
X^{\varepsilon}
converges to
X^{0}
in
c^{1,\gamma}
then by Lemma
6.2
we will have that
II
converges to 0 and (
38
) will follow.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
0<\gamma<1
and let
U_{\delta}
be defined as in (
39
). Let
q_{0}\in\penalty\ c^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})
.
Then

\sup_{X\in U_{\delta}}\left\|F^{\varepsilon}(X)-F^{0}(X)\right\|_{1,\gamma}\longrightarrow 0,\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0.

With the Lemma above, which we prove at the end of the section, we are now ready to prove the convergence of the flows. Subtracting the two equations and integrating in time,

X^{\varepsilon}(\alpha,t)-X^{0}(\alpha,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(F^{\varepsilon}(X^{\varepsilon}(\alpha,s))-F^{0}(X^{0}(\alpha,s))\bigr)\,ds.

Adding and subtracting
F^{\varepsilon}(X^{0}(\alpha,s))
inside the integrand and using that
{F^{\varepsilon}}
is Lipschitz,
we obtain

\left\|X^{\varepsilon}(\alpha,t)-X^{0}(\alpha,t)\right\|_{1,\gamma}\leq L\int_{0}^{t}\left\|X^{\varepsilon}(\alpha,s)-X^{0}(\alpha,s)\right\|_{1,\gamma}\,ds+t\sup_{X\in U_{\delta}}\left\|F^{\varepsilon}(X)-F^{0}(X)\right\|_{1,\gamma}.

Before applying the next estimate, we recall the following version of Grönwall’s lemma from
[
2
, Cor. 6.2]
.

Lemma 6.4

(Grönwall)
.

Let
J\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval and
t_{0}\in J
.
Assume that
a,\beta,u\in C(J;\mathbb{R}^{+})
and that

a(t)=a_{0}(|t-t_{0}|),
where
a_{0}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{+};\mathbb{R}^{+})
is a monotone increasing function. Assume that

u(t)\;\leq\;a(t)+\Bigl|\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\beta(s)\,u(s)\,ds\Bigr|,\qquad\forall t\in J.

Then

u(t)\;\leq\;a(t)e^{\left|\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\beta(s)\,ds\right|},\qquad\forall t\in J.

The previous lemma yields

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|X^{\varepsilon}(\alpha,t)-X^{0}(\alpha,t)\right\|_{1,\gamma}\leq{T}e^{LT}\sup_{X\in U_{\delta}}\left\|F^{\varepsilon}(X)-F^{0}(X)\right\|_{1,\gamma}

which goes to 0 by Lemma
6.3
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Hence,
\forall t\in[0,T],\;X^{\varepsilon}\to X^{0}
in
c^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Proof of Lemma
6.3
.

We define first

H(x)\coloneqq K^{\varepsilon}(x)-K^{0}(x)=\frac{\varepsilon x^{\perp}}{2\pi|x|}h(x),\quad h(x)\coloneqq K_{1}(\varepsilon|x|)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon|x|}.

Notice that
H
is not singular near the origin. In fact, using the series expansion of
K_{1}
, see (
33
), it’s easy to see that for
\varepsilon|x|\leq 1,

|H|\leq C\varepsilon^{2}|x|(|\ln{(\varepsilon|x|)|+1)}
and following Lemma
2.2
we see that for
\varepsilon|x|\geq 1
,
|H|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}\left(Ce^{-\varepsilon|x|}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon|x|}\right).

Moreover, by using (
8
) we have

\nabla H=\varepsilon\nabla\left(\frac{x^{\perp}}{2\pi|x|}\right)h(x)+\frac{\varepsilon x^{\perp}}{2\pi|x|}\left(-\frac{\varepsilon x}{|x|}K_{0}(\varepsilon|x|)-\frac{x}{|x|}h(x)\right),

so proceeding as before we get, for
\varepsilon|x|\leq 1

|\nabla H|\leq\varepsilon^{2}C(|\ln(\varepsilon|x|)|+1),\quad|\nabla^{2}H|\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}C}{|x|}

and for
\varepsilon|x|\geq 1
we will use the bounds

|\nabla H|\leq C_{1}\varepsilon,\quad|\nabla^{2}H|\leq C_{2}\varepsilon,

which follow directly from

|\nabla H|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}\left(Ce^{-\varepsilon|x|}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon|x|^{2}}\right),\quad|\nabla^{2}H|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}\left(Ce^{-\varepsilon|x|}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon|x|^{3}}\right),

whenever
\varepsilon|x|\geq 1
.

Fix
X\in U_{\delta}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.
Let
S=\operatorname{supp}q_{0}\subset B_{R}(0)
, We define

\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}\coloneqq\{\alpha^{\prime}\in S:\;\varepsilon|X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})|\leq 1\},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}\coloneqq S\setminus\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}.

Now, we split the difference into the regions just defined

\displaystyle\bigl(F^{\varepsilon}(X)-F^{0}(X)\bigr)(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}H\left(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})\right)\,q_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\,d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}}H\left(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})\right)\,q_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\,d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}}H\left(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})\right)\,q_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\,d\alpha^{\prime}\eqqcolon I+II

and using the bound for the difference of the kernels and that
q_{0}
is compactly supported we have

\displaystyle|I|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\varepsilon\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}\cap B_{R}(0)}\varepsilon|X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})|(|\ln\varepsilon|X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})||+1)\,d\alpha^{\prime}\leq C\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\varepsilon R^{2}

since
t|\ln{t}|+t
is bounded on the considered domain, and

\displaystyle|II|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}\cap B_{R}(0)}\varepsilon\left(Ce^{-\varepsilon|X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})|}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon|X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})|}\right)d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\varepsilon\int_{B_{R}(0)}\left(\frac{C}{e}+1\right)d\alpha^{\prime}\leq C\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\varepsilon R^{2}

Therefore,

\left\|F^{\varepsilon}(X)-F^{0}(X)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\varepsilon R^{2}.

Differentiating under the integral gives

\nabla(F^{\varepsilon}-F^{0})(X)(\alpha)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla H\bigl(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})\bigr)\,\nabla X(\alpha)\,q_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\,d\alpha^{\prime},

where no Dirac delta appears thanks to the cancellation of the singular parts of

\nabla K^{\varepsilon}
and
\nabla K^{0}
, see Proposition
5.5
.

Now, due to the bounds of
\nabla H
one can proceed as before and split the integral into
\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
so

\displaystyle|\nabla(F^{\varepsilon}-F^{0})(X)(\alpha)|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left\|\nabla X\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{S}|\nabla H(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))|\,d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left\|\nabla X\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left(I+II\right)

where

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}}|\nabla H(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))|\,d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}\cap B_{R}(0)}\frac{\varepsilon}{|X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})|}\varepsilon|X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})||\ln{\varepsilon|X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})|}|\,d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon\int_{B_{R}(0)}\frac{1}{|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|}\,d\alpha^{\prime}\leq C\varepsilon R

and

\displaystyle II

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}}|\nabla H(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))|\,d\alpha^{\prime}\leq\int_{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}\cap B_{R}(0)}C_{1}\varepsilon d\alpha^{\prime}\leq C\varepsilon R^{2}

so

\left\|\nabla(F^{\varepsilon}(X)-F^{0}(X))\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left\|\nabla X\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\varepsilon(R+R^{2}).

It remains to bound the Hölder seminorm of the gradient. Let
d=|\alpha-\beta|
and write

\displaystyle\nabla(F^{\varepsilon}-F^{0})(X)(\alpha)

\displaystyle-\nabla(F^{\varepsilon}-F^{0})(\beta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\nabla H(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))-\nabla H(X(\beta)-X(\alpha^{\prime})))\nabla X(\alpha)q_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla H(X(\beta)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))(\nabla X(\alpha)-\nabla X(\beta))q_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}\eqqcolon I+II.

Now for
II
we can proceed as for the
L^{\infty}
norm

\displaystyle|II|

\displaystyle\leq d^{\gamma}\left\|\nabla X\right\|_{\gamma}\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}\cap B_{R}(0)}|\nabla H(X(\beta)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))|d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt+\int_{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}\cap B_{R}(0)}|\nabla H(X(\beta)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))|d\alpha^{\prime}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq C(R+R^{2})d^{\gamma}\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\varepsilon

Then, let
M>\frac{(1+\delta)}{(1-\delta)}
and write,
I=I_{1}+I_{2}
with

I_{1}=\int_{|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\leq Md}(\nabla H(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))-\nabla H(X(\beta)-X(\alpha^{\prime})))\nabla X(\alpha)q_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime},

I_{2}=\int_{|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\geq Md}(\nabla H(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))-\nabla H(X(\beta)-X(\alpha^{\prime})))\nabla X(\alpha)q_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}.

For the first term we bound each part separately as before

\displaystyle|I_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\leq Md}(|\nabla H(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))|+|\nabla H(X(\beta)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))|)|\nabla X(\alpha)||q_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})|d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left\|\nabla X\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\bigg(\int_{\{|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\leq Md\}\cap\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}}|\nabla H(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))|d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\int_{\{|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\leq Md\}\cap\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}}|\nabla H(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))|d\alpha^{\prime}+\int_{\{|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\leq Md\}\cap\mathcal{N}_{\beta}}|\nabla H(X(\beta)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))|d\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\int_{\{|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\leq Md\}\cap\mathcal{F}_{\beta}}|\nabla H(X(\beta)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))|d\alpha^{\prime}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\varepsilon(d+d^{2}).

Finally, for
I_{2}
we use the mean value inequality to get

\displaystyle|I_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq dC\left\|\nabla X\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\{|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\geq Md\}\cap B_{R}(0)}|\nabla^{2}H(\xi(\alpha^{\prime}))|d\alpha^{\prime}

where
|\xi(\alpha^{\prime})|\asymp|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|.
Indeed, for some
\theta\in(0,1),
we have

\xi=X(\beta)-X(\alpha^{\prime})+\theta(X(\alpha)-X(\beta))=X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})+(1-\theta)(X(\beta)-X(\alpha)),

so using that
X\in U_{\delta}
is bi-Lipschitz we get

\displaystyle|\xi|

\displaystyle\geq|X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})|-|X(\beta)-X(\alpha)|\geq(1-\delta)|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|-(1+\delta)d

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(1-\delta)M-(1+\delta)}{M}|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\eqqcolon c_{1}|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|

and

\displaystyle|\xi|

\displaystyle\leq|X(\beta)-X(\alpha^{\prime})|+|X(\alpha)-X(\beta)|\leq(1+\delta)[|\beta-\alpha^{\prime}|+d]

\displaystyle\leq(1+\delta)[|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|+2d]\leq\frac{(1+\delta)(M+2)}{M}|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\eqqcolon c_{2}|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|.

Therefore,

\displaystyle|I_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq Cd\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left(\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\geq Md\\
\varepsilon|\xi(\alpha^{\prime})|\leq 1\\
\alpha^{\prime}\in B_{R}(0)\end{subarray}}|\nabla^{2}H(\xi(\alpha^{\prime}))|d\alpha^{\prime}+\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\geq Md\\
\varepsilon|\xi(\alpha^{\prime})|\geq 1\\
\alpha^{\prime}\in B_{R}(0)\end{subarray}}|\nabla^{2}H(\xi(\alpha^{\prime}))|d\alpha^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leq Cd\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left(\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}Md\leq|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon c_{1}}\\
\alpha^{\prime}\in B_{R}(0)\end{subarray}}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{|\xi(\alpha^{\prime})|}d\alpha^{\prime}+\int_{B_{R}(0)}C_{2}\varepsilon d\alpha^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leq Cd\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left(\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon c_{1}}\\
\alpha^{\prime}\in B_{R}(0)\end{subarray}}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{c_{1}|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|}d\alpha^{\prime}+\int_{B_{R}(0)}C_{2}\varepsilon d\alpha^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C(1+R^{2})d\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\varepsilon.

Gathering the previous bounds we get that
\left\|F^{\varepsilon}-F^{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}},

\left\|\nabla(F^{\varepsilon}-F^{0})\right\|_{L^{\infty}}
and
\left|\nabla(F^{\varepsilon}-F^{0})\right|_{\gamma}
all converge to
0
when
\varepsilon\to 0
uniformly on
X\in U_{\delta}.
Hence,
\left\|F^{\varepsilon}(X)-F^{0}(X)\right\|_{1,\gamma}\to 0
uniformly.
∎

Remark 5
.

While this paper establishes the existence and boundary regularity of vortex patches for the (
