Engineering properties of expansive soil stabilized with barley husk ash and lime: case study of Jimma town subgrade soils
Authors: Alemu Balcha, Mohammed Hassen, Anteneh Geremew, Giza Teshome
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Expansive soils are a prevalent issue in road construction, particularly in regions like Ethiopia. They pose significant challenges due to drastic shrink-swell behavior. Traditional stabilizers such as cement and lime are effective but costly and raise environmental concerns. This necessitates exploring more sustainable, locally-sourced alternatives. This research explores using Barley Husk Ash (BHA), an agricultural byproduct, as a pozzolanic additive to stabilize expansive soil from Jimma, Ethiopia. The untreated soil had an initial PI of 39% and a CBR of 0.8%. The study had two phases. First, optimal lime content was determined by treating the soil with varying lime percentages (0–20%) in 5% increments. Second, this lime content was combined with BHA in similar 5% increments (0–20%). Adding 10% lime reduced the PI to 22%, increased the CBR to 9.72%, and raised the Unconfined Compressive Strength (UCS) to 137 kPa. This met the Ethiopian Roads Authority (ERA) S4 subgrade standard (CBR>5%). Adding 10% BHA to the 10% lime-stabilized soil further improved performance. The PI dropped to 18% and the CBR increased to 18.42%, qualifying the soil for the higher S5 subgrade classification (CBR 15%-30%). Microstructural analysis using SEM confirmed these findings. The BHA-lime blend created a denser, more cohesive soil matrix with enhanced inter-particle cementitious bonding. This explains the improved strength and reduced swell potential. The mean porosity also decreased from 17.33% to 6.74%. This research validates a 10% lime plus 10% BHA blend as a highly effective, sustainable, and locally available solution for modern road construction.

Introduction

The subgrade, a compacted or natural soil layer, is foundational to pavement structures. Its stability and strength are crucial for overall performance and durability. While pavement design often emphasizes surface materials, it is important to recognize that subgrade quality heavily influences the pavement’s ability to withstand traffic loads and environmental stresses1–9. An understanding of subgrade behavior is especially critical when dealing with problematic soil types.

Expansive soils present significant challenges to subgrade performance worldwide. These soils expand and contract as moisture levels change, resulting in cracks, unstable foundations, and road failures10,11. Their particle configuration, which is plate-like in morphology and high in surface area, leads to extreme shrink–swell behavior. These soils can take up water, causing 10–30% or more volumetric expansion, and then a decrease in volume as they dry12–14. Ikeagwuani and Nwonu15 noted that the high susceptibility to volumetric changes and moisture inherent in these clays has caused extensive damage to structures. The soils concerned usually exhibit an extremely high degree of plasticity; for instance, black cotton clays usually have liquid limits of more than 60% and plasticity indices of more than 30%, thus qualifying as high-plasticity clays (USCS CH)16.

Tanyildizi et al.17 state that expansive soils are susceptible to volume change with variations in moisture content, which causes serious damage to light structures, particularly road pavements. They note that a black cotton clay with LL ≈ 68% and PI ≈ 34% is classified as a CH clay with high swelling potential. Grain size distribution and cation exchange capacity (CEC) are also determined since finer-grained clays with high CEC retain more pore water and show more shrink-swell behavior.

In the case of roads, these movements cause wavy roads, and in the case of structures, they cause foundation cracks and tilting. Control of moisture (e.g., by drainage or moisture barriers) is a significant design concern to prevent uncontrollable ground movement18,19. However, flexible pavements deteriorate prematurely due to issues such as rutting, raveling, stripping, and cracking. The long-term performance and durability of highways largely depend on the characteristics of the subgrade layer, which serves as the foundation support for the pavement system20.

The importance of improving and safely utilizing expansive soils has grown significantly. Soil stabilization is the process of treating soil to enhance its strength and reliability. This technique can be applied to various soil types, including clay and sandy soils. By stabilizing the soil, we can increase its resistance to changes, reduce water absorption, and decrease the softness that can lead to problems. These enhancements allow for thinner road surfaces, making roads more durable and cost-effective. Overall, this process contributes to the construction of better and longer-lasting roads21–24,40–44.

There are a few common methods for stabilizing expansive soils. One method is mechanical stabilization, which involves enhancing the soil’s strength and performance using physical techniques without the addition of chemicals. This approach helps create a more stable foundation for construction and other uses25. Several techniques can be employed to strengthen and stabilize soil. One such technique is compaction, which involves pressing the soil down to make it denser. Another method is pre-loading or pre-wetting, which prepares the ground in advance to improve its stability. If the soil is unsuitable, it can be replaced or mixed with better materials, such as gravel. Properly compacted soil, especially when maintained at the optimum moisture level, becomes denser and has fewer voids, helping to prevent future issues like swelling26,27.

In various situations, researchers may also use chemical methods to stabilize soil28. Chemical stabilizers alter the chemistry and microstructure of the soil to reduce plasticity and clump particles together29,30. Common conventional stabilizers include lime, cement, and fly ash, all of which contain calcium31. In practice, mechanical compaction or moisture conditioning is usually practiced in conjunction with chemical stabilization for optimal results. Traditional stabilization methods use lime and cement, which undergo chemical reactions with the soil to improve its strength and durability. A very useful stabilizer for expansive clay soil is lime. The released calcium ions (Ca2+) engage in exchange with monovalent cations on the surface of clay particles (e.g., sodium ions, Na+) because of the cation exchange capacity inherent in the clay32,33. Furthermore, quicklime (CaO) reacts exothermically with water to form calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2), raising porewater pH to>~12.4. At the same time, high pH dissolves silica and alumina from clay minerals; these mobilized species then react with Ca(OH)2 in pozzolanic reactions to form cementitious calcium silicate and aluminate hydrates34.

Portland cement, for example, is a hydraulic binder that consolidates soil rapidly, but its manufacture is extremely energy and carbon-intensive (one ton of cement will emit~0.8–0.9 tons of CO2). A study by Mustafayeva et al.35 shows that cement contributes approximately 7% of the world’s CO2 emissions, a very real problem in sustainable construction. Lime production also requires high-temperature kilns, adding to the embodied carbon. Apart from greenhouse gas emissions, large-scale use of cement or lime increases the expense and can also bring about sulphate-related issues. In the presence of soil sulphates, lime can form ettringite and gypsum, which causes secondary expansion (the so-called soil lime sulphate reaction)36,37. Moisture control by subjecting the subgrade to alternating cycles of saturation and drying can be detrimental even to lime-stabilized subgrades, as empirical practice has witnessed variable performance in regions with monsoonal climatic regimes35. However, cost and environmental concerns associated with these materials have prompted investigation of other, locally sourced materials and greener alternatives38,39.

Recent research trends highlight the importance of locally available sustainable stabilizers. Different studies examine the utilization of by-products from industrial and agricultural sources to minimize the use of Portland cement and natural lime35. Innovative methods, including the use of bamboo charcoal, quarry dust, zeolitic tuff, Probase SH-85, and TX-85, have been found to enhance the characteristics of expansive soils for use in road subgrades. For example, fly ash (a coal combustion residue), granulated blast-furnace slag, and steel slag provide reactive silica/alumina and calcium. It can be activated by alkalis or lime to produce cementitious binders. It is limited by the low Ca content when alone, but with lime addition, it gives a high pozzolanic reaction that improves strength gain16. Tanyildizi et al.17 found that the fly ash-lime blend not only improved subgrade strength but also utilized waste material (reduced landfill mass). Likewise, various other by-products such as marble or granite powder, eggshell ash, and ground furnace slag have been utilized as admixture material for lime or cement14.

Agricultural residues are also promising in construction. Sawdust ash, rice husk ash, and sugarcane bagasse ash contain silica and are being tested as partial cement replacements. Experimental results show that adding sawdust ash to expansive clay can significantly reduce plasticity and increase strength35. Moreover, because agricultural waste is readily available in large quantities, it offers a cost-effective and environmentally friendly option, making it a promising alternative for soil stabilization. Due to these different reasons, the utilization of waste material for soil stabilization is viewed in a positive light, as it has the potential to enhance the load-bearing capacity of the soil, the shear strength, and minimize its permeability. This approach promotes sustainability and can significantly improve daily travel for local communities45–47.

While previous research has examined agricultural byproducts for soil stabilization, the present study introduces a novel approach utilizing locally sourced Barley Husk Ash (BHA). This method improves soil stabilization efficiency and advances sustainability, establishing BHA as a viable alternative for enhancing soil quality. Barley is cultivated on approximately 70 million hectares worldwide, with global production reaching 160 million tons. Developing countries account for around 18% of total barley production, contributing about 26 million tons, and 25% of the total harvested area, which is approximately 18.5 million hectares. The primary uses of barley grain are as animal feed, malt, and human food. Additionally, farmers in regions such as West Asia, North Africa, Ethiopia, Eritrea, Yemen, the Andes, and East Asia use barley straw as animal feed48. Africa produced 6–8 million tons, with Ethiopia leading at 2.1–2.5 million tons on 1 million hectares, yielding 1.9–2.2 tons per hectare49,50.

Barley is an important cereal crop in Ethiopia, with high genetic diversity, spatial distribution across elevations, breeding progress toward superior varieties, production problems and opportunities, and a wide range of uses in food, feed, and industry50. In 2012, Assela Malt Factory expanded its production capacity from 22,000 to 36,000 tons to meet the growing demand for processed malt barley, an agricultural byproduct of the brewing industry in Ethiopia. The facility requires 500,000 quintals (or 50,000 tonnes) of high-quality raw malt barley to produce 360,000 quintals (or 36,000 tonnes) of malt per year51. Barley husk is abundantly produced in Ethiopia52 and its husks are readily available for reuse in the construction industry. As a result, the use of indigenous resources for subgrade soil stabilization is essential. In this study, BHA was mixed with lime to examine its performance when combined with the optimal amount of lime utilized for stabilization. The evaluation of BHA as a solution for enhancing subgrade soil stabilization when combined with lime reveals its significant potential to not only improve the effectiveness of conventional stabilizers but also to serve as a viable alternative. This innovative approach could lead to more efficient and sustainable practices in soil stabilization.

Materials and methods

Materials

Expansive soil: Soil samples were collected from different locations in Jimma town, such as Shenen Gibe, Kochi, and Kito.

Barley Husk Ash (BHA): Barley husk was collected from Arsi Zone, Assela town (Assela Malt Factory), which is a high-potential area in Barley crop production.

Lime: Lime was purchased from suppliers in Jimma.

Research design

The study utilized a laboratory experimental design to assess the stabilization of expansive soil using lime and Barley Husk Ash (BHA) for subgrade construction. Several laboratory studies have investigated the stabilization of expansive soils using lime combined with agricultural ashes, particularly rice husk ash (RHA), as a partial analog to barley husk ash (BHA). These studies typically use varying lime contents (3–10%) and RHA proportions (4–20%) to determine optimal mixtures for improving engineering properties such as unconfined compressive strength (UCS), California Bearing Ratio (CBR), and durability53–56. In the case of this study, lime was applied at (5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%) by dry weight of soil to determine the optimal content. Subsequently, the optimal lime content was combined with varying BHA proportions (5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%) to evaluate improvements in engineering properties. Barley husk ash has not been directly investigated for its potential to stabilize expansive soils. The percentage used in this study is specific to its findings. BHA, like rice husk ash (RHA), is rich in amorphous silica. When added to lime-treated soil, it supplies extra silica for pozzolanic reactions, allowing continued formation of cementitious compounds (calcium silicate hydrates, C-S-H) that further strengthen and densify the soil matrix57,58. The calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) produced from lime reacts with the amorphous silica in BHA to form additional C-S-H and calcium aluminate hydrates (C-A-H), which are responsible for increased strength, reduced plasticity, and improved durability59,60. Furthermore, the study examined the effects of partially replacing lime with BHA at equivalent ratios. Laboratory tests, including Atterberg limits, compaction, unconfined compressive strength, California Bearing Ratio (CBR), and other tests, were conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of the stabilization treatments. Ali et al.61 examined the blending of BHA with OPC and established that the replacement of OPC by 15% with BHA enhanced the compressive strength by 42% after 28 days of curing. This improvement is due to the pozzolanic reactions caused by the silica and alumina present in BHA. Likewise, research on various agricultural ashes, for example, rice husk ash (RHA), has revealed that their high silica content is largely responsible for the development of the mechanical properties of cement-based systems62. Lime, which contains 67.2% calcium oxide (CaO), is commonly used for soil stabilization. Mixing with soil and water causes cation exchange, flocculation, and pozzolanic reactions, which harden the soil and reduce its plasticity. These reactions enhance the engineering qualities of soils, and thus, lime is a valuable additive in construction works, especially in clayey soils63.

Natural soil analysis reveals a high level of silica (SiO2) at 82.86%, which is typical for silty or sandy soils. Moderate mineralization is also indicated by the presence of aluminum oxide (Al2O3) at 4.12% and iron oxide (Fe2O3) at 3.78%. The comparatively low percentages of calcium oxide (CaO) at 0.98% and magnesium oxide (MgO) at 0.46% suggest low natural fertility and potential acidity. Soils in areas such as Ethiopia tend to have such characteristics, needing stabilization in construction.

In conclusion, the presence of high silica and nutrients in BHA renders it applicable for use in construction and agricultural sectors. The calcium-rich nature of the lime is significant in stabilizing soil, thereby improving structural strength in the construction sector. Additionally, the mixture of BHA and lime can enable the production of calcium silicate hydrates, thereby improving stabilized soil strength and durability.

Atterberg Limit

The Atterberg limit test is used to determine the critical water content of fine-grained soils, helping classify their behavior under varying moisture conditions. These limits are essential in geotechnical engineering for soil classification and for assessing properties such as compressibility, shear strength, and shrink-swell behavior68.

The test was conducted according to the AASHTO T-89-9069 standard, using a 0.425mm pass sample. The results of the Atterberg limit test for natural soil samples from Shenen Gibe, Kochi, and Kito indicate high plasticity, making them unsuitable for subgrade construction without modification (Table 2). According to the Ethiopian Roads Authority (ERA) Pavement Design Manual, a plasticity index (PI) exceeding 30 is considered poor for subgrade material70. Specifically, the Shenen Gibe sample exhibited a PI of 39%, Kochi 39%, and Kito 35%, all surpassing the ERA’s threshold. Compaction test

The compaction test is conducted to determine the optimum moisture content and maximum dry density of soil, which are essential for assessing its suitability for construction. In this study, the test was performed using the Standard Proctor method (AASHTO T-99)72. A soil sample passing a 4.75mm sieve was used for the test. The results help evaluate the soil’s compaction characteristics and its ability to achieve desired strength and stability in engineering applications.

California Bearing Ratio (CBR) test

The California Bearing Ratio (CBR) test evaluates the strength of pavement subgrade, subbase, and base materials. The sample preparation involves compacting soil specimens in a mold at a specified density and moisture content, often determined by AASHTO T-99. The test procedure involves soaking the specimen, applying a load with a penetration piston at a rate of 1.25 mm/min, and recording the penetration resistance values. The standard used is AASHTO T-19373 to ensure consistency in pavement design.

Unconfined compressive strength test

The Unconfined Compressive Strength (UCS) test determines the shear strength of cohesive soils without lateral confinement74. The preparation of samples involves trimming undisturbed soil specimens or compacting remolded samples to standard dimensions, while ensuring consistent moisture content. The test procedure includes placing the specimen in a compression machine and applying axial load at a controlled strain rate (typically 1.27 mm/min) until failure occurs. The standard used is ASTM D216675, which provides guidelines for testing cohesive soils in undisturbed, remolded, or compacted conditions.

Microstructural test for natural soil, lime, and barley husk ash (BHA)

Microstructural analysis investigates the internal structure and composition of materials to understand their physical and chemical interactions. In soil stabilization, this analysis helps identify the formation of cementitious compounds and the densification of the soil matrix resulting from the addition of lime and BHA. The test was performed as per ASTM C1723-10, which guides examining hardened concrete using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)78.

Specimens of natural soil, lime, and BHA, as well as their mixtures, were prepared at the desired proportions. Then the specimen was cured under controlled conditions (e.g., 7, 14, and 28 days) to allow pozzolanic reactions to occur. After curing, oven-dry the samples at 40 °C to 60 °C to remove moisture without altering mineral structures. Mount small fragments on aluminum stubs using conductive carbon tape and sputter-coat them with a thin layer of gold or platinum to prevent charging during SEM examination. Utilize SEM to capture high-resolution images of the sample surfaces, revealing morphological features and particle arrangements.

Effect of lime on specific gravity

The specific gravity of soil solids (Gs) is a crucial parameter in geotechnical engineering, reflecting the density of soil particles relative to water. For natural expansive soils, a specific gravity of 2.63 is within the typical range for clay soils, which generally spans from 2.60 to 2.8080. Upon lime stabilization, the specific gravity of expansive soil decreases, as observed from 2.63 to 2.57, with a 20% lime content, as shown in Table 5. This reduction indicates chemical alterations in the soil minerals due to lime treatment, leading to increased void ratios and changes in soil structure. The decrease in specific gravity with increasing lime content underscores the impact of lime on soil properties, influencing compaction behavior and overall soil stability. Similar findings suggest that lime-treated soils exhibit lower specific gravity due to increased void ratios63.

Effect of lime on free swell index (FSI)

The data presented in Table 5 and Fig. 3 indicate a significant reduction in the free swell index (FSI) of expansive soil with increasing lime content. Specifically, the FSI decreased from 109 to 33% upon the addition of 20% lime, marking a 70% improvement. This substantial decrease underscores the efficacy of lime stabilization in mitigating the swelling and shrinkage potential of expansive soils.Fig. 3Effect of lime on FSI.

Effect of lime on FSI.

The reduction in FSI can be attributed to the chemical interactions between lime and the clay minerals present in the soil. Lime induces cation exchange, flocculation, and pozzolanic reactions, forming stable compounds that diminish the soil’s affinity for water. Consequently, the soil’s volumetric changes due to moisture variations are significantly reduced.

A study by Salih and Mohammed Shafiqu81 demonstrated that adding 9% lime to expansive soil reduced the swelling potential from 18.77% to 6.03%. These findings corroborate the effectiveness of lime in enhancing the dimensional stability of expansive soils. However, it’s essential to note that the optimal lime content for stabilization varies depending on soil characteristics. Excessive lime addition may not yield proportional benefits and could potentially lead to adverse effects82. Therefore, determining the appropriate lime percentage through testing is crucial for achieving the desired soil improvement.

Effect of lime on compaction characteristics

Table 5 and Fig. 4 show that initially, the addition of lime (up to 10%) to expansive soil results in a slight decrease in OMC from 23.5% to 23.0%. This reduction can be attributed to the immediate reaction between lime and soil moisture, leading to the formation of calcium hydroxide and the flocculation of clay particles, which reduces the soil’s water-holding capacity.Fig. 4Effect of lime on compaction characteristics.

Effect of lime on compaction characteristics.

However, with further lime addition (15% and 20%), OMC increases to 24.0% and 24.5%, respectively. This increase is due to the pozzolanic reactions that occur over time, forming cementitious compounds that require additional water for the chemical processes. Additionally, the flocculated and agglomerated soil structure resulting from higher lime content increases the void spaces, necessitating more water to achieve optimal compaction. This trend is consistent with findings from other studies, which found that increasing lime content leads to higher OMC due to the increased moisture demand of the altered soil structure16.

The MDD shows a slight increase with 5% and 10% lime addition, from 1.417 g/cm3 to 1.423 g/cm3. This increase is likely due to the initial flocculation and agglomeration of clay particles, which enhances particle packing and compaction efficiency. Beyond 10% lime content, MDD decreases to 1.413 g/cm3 and 1.408 g/cm3 for 15% and 20% lime addition, respectively. The reduction in maximum dry density (MDD) at higher lime contents can be explained by the development of a more open and porous soil structure. This occurs as clay particles flocculate and cementitious compounds form, taking up more space and resulting in a decrease in overall density. This observation aligns with previous research indicating that higher lime content leads to a decrease in MDD due to the increased void ratio and lower specific gravity of lime compared to soil particles16,83.

California Bearing Ratio (CBR)

As indicated in Table 5 and Fig. 5, laboratory tests revealed that the California Bearing Ratio (CBR) of the natural expansive soil is 1.27%. Conclusion

This study demonstrates the successful stabilization of problematic expansive soil from Jimma, Ethiopia, using a dual-additive approach of lime and Barley Husk Ash (BHA). The research established that an optimal mix of 10% lime and 10% BHA significantly enhances the soil’s engineering properties. Key findings indicate a substantial reduction in plasticity by 70% and swell potential, alongside a notable increase in the California Bearing Ratio (CBR) by 2202.5%, elevating the soil to a higher subgrade classification suitable for road construction.

The significance of this research lies in its validation of a sustainable and locally sourced agricultural byproduct as a viable alternative to conventional, costly, and carbon-intensive stabilizers. The synergistic effect of the BHA-lime blend creates a denser, more cohesive soil microstructure, improving its mechanical properties. While the Unconfined Compressive Strength (UCS) of the dual-additive mix was slightly lower than that of soil treated with lime alone. The substantial improvements in CBR and plasticity present a strategic trade-off, particularly for low to medium-traffic roads where sustainability and cost-effectiveness are paramount.

This method has significant potential for geotechnical and civil engineering applications, particularly in Ethiopia and other regions with similar agricultural conditions. Utilizing an abundant agricultural byproduct, it provides a sustainable, cost-effective alternative for building resilient infrastructure, reducing dependence on conventional, high-cost, high-carbon stabilizers. This research offers concrete technical evidence for national road authorities to amend construction specifications and include BHA as an accepted stabilizer. Such a policy would promote a circular economy, cut costs for rural and regional road projects, and support national sustainability goals.
