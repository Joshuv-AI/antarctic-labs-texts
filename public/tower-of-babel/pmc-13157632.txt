Treponemal Endovasculitis leading to branch artery occlusion: a rare manifestation of ocular syphilis
Authors: Kakarla V Chalam, Lourdes Ceja, Anny M S Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOcular syphilis, a re-emerging cause of uveitis, commonly features retinal vasculitis, but branch retinal artery occlusion (BRAO) is a rare, irreversible damaging complication. We present a 47-year-old woman with bilateral syphilitic panuveitis, retinal vasculitis and an inferior BRAO, emphasising the diagnostic value of multimodal imaging. She presented with progressive bilateral blurred vision, exhibiting bilateral nongranulomatous anterior uveitis, vitritis, peripheral vascular sheathing, and multiple chorioretinal lesions. Optical coherence tomography revealed inner retinal thinning consistent with inferior BRAO, and fluorescein angiography showed diffuse vascular leakage consistent with syphilitic retinal vasculitis. Serologic testing confirmed active syphilis. Intravenous penicillin G and topical anti-inflammatory therapy resolved inflammation and with full visual recovery in the right eye. The left eye stabilized with mild persistent visual impairment due to ischemic retinal atrophy. Multimodal imaging for accurate diagnosis is indispensable in cases of unexplained uveitis or retinal vasculitis and timely penicillin treatment—indicated only after syphilis is confirmed through appropriate diagnostic testing—is critical to prevent vision loss.

Introduction

Syphilis has re-emerged as a significant public health burden, and ocular involvement remains among the most diagnostically challenging expressions of Treponema pallidum infection [1, 2]. Its clinical manifestations are remarkably diverse, spanning anterior uveitis, vitritis, neuro-ophthalmic disease, and various posterior segment pathologies, including retinal vasculitis and occlusive vasculopathies [3]. The unpredictable nature of ocular syphilis, coupled with its ability to mimic numerous inflammatory and infectious conditions, often delays recognition and timely treatment.

Retinal vasculitis is a well-documented manifestation of ocular syphilis [3], yet the occurrence of concomitant branch retinal artery occlusion (BRAO) is relatively rare and reflects a severe compromise of retinal vascular integrity [4]. The pathogenesis is multifactorial, involving obliterative endarteritis, immune-mediated endothelial injury, and direct spirochetal invasion. Early diagnosis requires a combination of clinical vigilance and comprehensive imaging [5], particularly as ischemic complications may lead to irreversible visual deficits. This report describes a case of bilateral syphilitic panuveitis complicated by retinal vasculitis and an inferior BRAO in the left eye, emphasising the indispensable role of multimodal imaging in delineating both inflammatory and ischemic processes and guiding prompt therapeutic intervention.

Case report

A 47-year-old woman with a medical history notable for Chiari I malformation and syringomyelia presented with a one-month history of progressively worsening blurred vision, first affecting the right eye (OD) and subsequently the left (OS). She denied pain, photopsias, floaters, diplopia, as well as systemic symptoms or vascular conditions such as hypertension, diabetes or hypercholesterolemia. She took intermittent muscle relaxants and long-term analgesics for neuropathic pain and muscle spasticity related to syringomyelia. She was sexually active with one partner, denied tobacco or illicit drug use, and consumed alcohol socially. Her family history was non-contributory. On presentation, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/25 OD and 20/50 OS. Intraocular pressures (IOP) were 15 mmHg OD and 16 mmHg OS. Pupils were briskly reactive without an afferent pupillary defect, though subtle irregularity of the right pupil suggested early posterior synechiae. Extraocular motility and confrontation visual fields were full bilaterally.

Slit-lamp biomicroscopy revealed bilateral nongranulomatous anterior uveitis, with fine keratic precipitates and 1–2+ anterior chamber cells. Scattered posterior synechiae were present in the right eye. Both eyes exhibited 2+ vitreous cells, including inferior snowballs in the left eye. Dilated fundus examination showed widespread posterior segment inflammation. The right eye presented with vitritis without macular pathology, whereas the left eye displayed a sharply circumscribed, three-disc-diameter zone of inferior macular whitening sparing the fovea (Fig. 1A and B). Both eyes exhibited peripheral vascular sheathing, multifocal chorioretinal lesions, and vitreous condensations, without disc edema or hemorrhage.

Multimodal imaging of left eye before treatment. Color fundus photo (A) showed a sharply circumscribed region of severe inferior macular whitening measuring approximately three disc diameters, sparing the fovea, consistent with ischemic injury. Red free fundus photo (B) showed retinal whitening consistent with optical coherence tomography (OCT), en face view (C) of retinal edema from branch retinal artery occlusion (BRAO).

Follow-up assessment showed improvement to 20/20 OD and stabilization at 20/30–20/40 OS, with IOPs remaining normal. Mild anterior chamber inflammation persisted, and the left eye’s macular changes remained unchanged. Multimodal imaging provided diagnostic clarity. Spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (OCT) revealed a normal foveal contour and posterior vitreous detachment in the right eye, while the left eye demonstrated inferior inner retinal thinning consistent with inferior BRAO. OCT en face image further revealed retinal edema from left eye BRAO (Fig. 1C). Colour fundus photography confirmed bilateral chorioretinal lesions and vascular sheathing, with sharply demarcated macular whitening OS corresponding to the OCT abnormalities. Fluorescein angiography (FA) showed diffuse early and late vascular leakage in both eyes, most pronounced in the left eye, consistent with active syphilitic retinal vasculitis and sectoral nonperfusion.

Serologic evaluation confirmed active syphilis, with a positive treponemal FTA-ABS and a reactive non-treponemal RPR (titer 1:64), establishing the unified diagnosis of bilateral syphilitic panuveitis with retinal vasculitis, complicated by inferior BRAO OS, and providing a baseline for monitoring treatment response. The patient did not portray any extra-ocular signs of syphilis once diagnosis was confirmed. The patient was treated with intravenous penicillin G administered via a peripherally inserted central catheter. Topical prednisolone acetate every two hours and cyclopentolate three times daily were initiated to control inflammation and prevent further synechiae.

Over the ensuing weeks, intraocular inflammation diminished and BCVA improved significantly. The right eye recovered fully. However, the left eye stabilized with mild but permanent visual impairment due to ischemic retinal atrophy. Multimodal imaging—including color (Fig. 2A), infra-red image (Fig. 2B), FA (Fig. 2C), and OCT, revealed initial severe retinal edema and thickened inner layers (Fig. 3A), which progressed to inner retinal atrophy following resolution of the BRAO (Fig. 3B). The patient received a 14-day course of intravenous penicillin G, with post-treatment RPR titers monitored at 3 and 6 months. Given stabilization of inflammation and persistent left-eye ischemic changes, treatment duration was not extended, or no modification of the regimen was necessary.

Multimodal imaging of left eye after treatment. Complete resolution of retinal edema after antibiotics therapy, as evidenced by color fundus photography (A) and infra-red imaging (B). Fluorescein angiography (FA) (C) revealed residual staining of the vessels.

Spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the left eye demonstrated significant changes before and after treatment. Initially, the retina presented with severe edema and a thickened inner layer (A). Following the resolution of branch retinal artery occlusion (BRAO), the inner layer atrophied, resulting in a thinned retina (B).

Discussion

Ocular syphilis remains a formidable diagnostic challenge due to its protean manifestations and its capacity to mimic a wide array of inflammatory and ischemic conditions [5]. This case illustrates the complex interplay between treponemal infection, retinal vasculitis, and occlusive vascular compromise, culminating in a BRAO. BRAO usually occurs in patients with vascular risk factors like hypertension, diabetes, high cholesterol, or cardiac and carotid emboli. In this patient, none of these risk factors were present, indicating that the BRAO resulted directly from syphilitic retinal vasculitis rather than an underlying vascular condition. The pathogenesis of syphilitic vasculitis reflects T. pallidum’s affinity for vascular endothelium, where it induces obliterative endarteritis, perivascular inflammation, immune-complex deposition, and direct endothelial injury [5]. These processes collectively predispose to retinal vascular insufficiency and, in susceptible territories, irreversible ischemia. The sharply demarcated inferior BRAO in this patient exemplifies the severe vasculopathic potential of the infection.

In bilateral panuveitis with retinal vasculitis, key differentials include infectious causes such as tuberculosis, viral retinitis, and toxoplasmosis, as well as non-infectious systemic inflammatory diseases like sarcoidosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and Behçet disease; masquerade syndromes such as intraocular lymphoma should also be excluded with targeted testing. Syphilis is specifically considered early because it can mimic any form of uveitis. In this case, the clinical findings—bilateral panuveitis, retinal vasculitis, and BRAO—were prompted syphilis serologic confirmation. Clinicians should routinely evaluate for it, especially with risk factors such as high-risk sexual exposure, young age, neurologic signs, or unexplained bilateral ocular inflammation.

The diagnostic clarity achieved in this case underscores the indispensable value of multimodal imaging. Spectral-domain OCT provided precise delineation of inner retinal thinning (Fig. 3A and B), confirming ischemic injury corresponding to the patient’s visual deficit [5, 6]. Fluorescein angiography demonstrated diffuse vascular leakage and perfusion abnormalities characteristic of active treponemal vasculitis, while colour fundus photography contextualized the inflammatory topography of chorioretinal lesions and vascular sheathing [5, 7]. Together, these imaging modalities yielded a coherent and comprehensive understanding of the structural and vascular pathology, enabling prompt recognition of the underlying infectious etiology.

Therapeutically, the timely initiation of systemic penicillin G, the established standard of care for neurosyphilis and ocular involvement, was instrumental in halting the progression of inflammatory injury [8]. Although the inflammatory components responded robustly to treatment, the ischemic sequelae of BRAO proved irreversible, underscoring the importance of early detection before ischemic thresholds are crossed. A prior case reported syphilitic vasculitis in which penicillin resolved the inflammation, but persistent extensive vascular occlusion persisted [4]. In contrast, our case demonstrated bilateral panuveitis with localized BRAO in the left eye, highlighting early multimodal imaging to detect ischemia. Both cases emphasize irreversible vascular damage despite effective anti-treponemal therapy. This outcome reinforces the critical need for clinicians to maintain a high index of suspicion for syphilis when confronted with unexplained uveitis, retinal vasculitis, or retinal ischemia, particularly in light of rising global syphilis prevalence [2]. Ultimately, early diagnosis supported by multimodal imaging and immediate initiation of definitive antimicrobial therapy remain essential to safeguarding visual outcomes in patients with ocular syphilis.

Conclusion

This case highlights the severe and varied manifestations of ocular syphilis, emphasising its potential to produce retinal vasculitis and ischemic complications such as branch retinal artery occlusion. Comprehensive multimodal imaging was essential in delineating the inflammatory and ischemic components of the disease, guiding timely diagnosis and systemic therapy. Maintaining syphilis in the differential diagnosis of atypical uveitis and retinal vasculitis is critical to preventing irreversible visual sequelae.
