The prognostic value of the Oocyte Maturation Index (OMI) on pregnancy outcomes in POSEIDON group 3 and 4 patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yakup Bayram, Hamza Yildiz, Gorkem Ulger, Huseyin Durukan, Devrim Tok, Faik Gurkan Yazici
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to evaluate the prognostic value of the Oocyte Maturation Index (OMI) on fertilization, embryo development, and pregnancy outcomes in young (POSEIDON Group 3) and older (POSEIDON Group 4) patients with diminished ovarian reserve.MethodsA total of 662 patients treated at the Assisted Reproductive Techniques Unit of Mersin University Faculty of Medicine between 2016 and 2024 (Group 3: n=245; Group 4: n=417) were retrospectively analyzed. Patients were divided into three subgroups based on OMI values: ≤50%, 51–75%, and >75%.ResultsIn both POSEIDON groups, the numbers of aspirated follicles, retrieved oocytes, MII oocytes, and high-quality day-3 embryos showed significant differences between OMI groups (p<0.001). In POSEIDON Group 3, patients with an OMI of 51–75% achieved the highest rates of biobiochemical pregnancy (43.9%), clinical pregnancy (42.1%), and live birth (35.1%), while these rates were lowest in the group with an OMI of ≤50% (10.9%, 10.9%, and 5.5%, respectively; p=0.001). In POSEIDON Group 4, while OMI significantly influenced chemical (p=0.005) and clinical pregnancy (p=0.014) rates, it did not result in a statistically significant difference in live birth rates (p=0.056).ConclusionOMI is a clinically significant prognostic parameter, particularly for younger patients in the POSEIDON Group 3. In older patients (Group 4), however, age-related oocyte proglems my controbite the decisive effect of OMI on live birth. Incorporating OMI into routine clinical evaluations may contribute to personalized treatment planning and patient counseling.Keywords: Oocyte Maturation Index, POSEIDON, IVF, Diminished ovarian reserve, Pregnancy outcomes

Introduction

Infertility is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as the failure to achieve a clinical pregnancy after 12 months or more of regular unprotected sexual intercourse, and it continues to represent a significant global public health concern. According to the WHO’s 2023 report, approximately 17.5% of the adult population (roughly 1 in 6 people worldwide) experience infertility at some point in their lives [1].

Given this high prevalence, assisted reproductive technologies (ART), most notably in vitro fertilization (IVF), have become indispensable treatment options for infertile couples. Among the critical determinants of IVF success, controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) occupies a central role, with its effectiveness directly linked to both the number and maturity of oocytes retrieved. Capper et al. demonstrated that a low oocyte maturity ratio was significantly associated with reduced live birth rates in IVF and ICSI cycles, highlighting oocyte maturity as an important prognostic parameter independent of total oocyte count [2].

The impact of MII oocyte number and ratio becomes particularly pronounced in patients of advanced maternal age. Havrljenko et al. reported that women aged 35 years and older required 10–12 MII oocytes to achieve an optimal live birth rate [3]. Furthermore, Patrizio et al. provided evidence that the capacity of MII oocytes to generate good-quality embryos declines significantly with advancing age, challenging the long-standing dogma that oocyte maturity grades do not functionally change with age [4].

The prognostic significance of oocyte maturity is particularly important in POSEIDON Group 3 and Group 4 patients, representing young women under 35 years and older women aged 35 years and above with diminished ovarian reserve, respectively. Whether OMI independently predicts pregnancy outcomes in these groups remains insufficiently investigated. Conforti et al. demonstrated that although high-dose gonadotropin protocols increased oocyte yield in women with diminished ovarian reserve, this quantitative gain did not translate into improved clinical pregnancy rates, emphasizing that treatment optimization must prioritize oocyte quality alongside quantity [5]. Chen et al. similarly showed that indices such as the follicular output rate (FORT), ovarian sensitivity index (OSI), and ovarian response prediction index (ORPI) reflected ovarian reserve effectively in POSEIDON patients, yet none alone adequately predicted clinical pregnancy rates [6], further underlining the need to integrate oocyte quality parameters, including the OMI, into clinical evaluation frameworks.

Studies specifically examining the relationship between OMI and pregnancy outcomes in POSEIDON-stratified patients remain notably scarce. This gap is clinically meaningful, as accurate assessment of each oocyte’s developmental potential is of paramount importance in patients with a restricted oocyte pool. This study aims to retrospectively evaluate the effect of OMI on fertilization rates, embryo development, and pregnancy outcomes in POSEIDON Group 3 and Group 4 patients, and to determine whether OMI serves as an independent prognostic parameter in these populations, with the intent of contributing to individualized stimulation protocols and evidence-based decision-making in ART practice.

Method

This retrospective cohort study investigated the effect of the OMI on pregnancy outcomes in patients with diminished ovarian reserve undergoing IVF treatment at the Assisted Reproduction Unit of Mersin University Faculty of Medicine. Medical records of patients treated between January 1, 2016 and December 31, 2024 were reviewed. Ethical approval was obtained from the Mersin University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2024/1178, dated November 27, 2024), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Patients classified as POSEIDON Group 3 (age < 35 years, AFC < 5 and/or AMH < 1.2 ng/mL) or POSEIDON Group 4 (age ≥ 35 years, AFC < 5 and/or AMH < 1.2 ng/mL) were included. Exclusion criteria were: failure to meet POSEIDON Group 3 or 4 criteria; incomplete treatment; absence of oocyte retrieval (OPU); and failure to obtain any oocytes following OPU.

All patients underwent baseline evaluation including age, duration of infertility, AFC, uterine and tubal assessment, day 3 hormonal profile (FSH, LH, E2, PRL, TSH), serum AMH, and semen analysis. A GnRH antagonist protocol was initiated on days 2–3 of the menstrual cycle with gonadotropins at 300–450 IU daily. Follicular development was monitored by transvaginal ultrasonography, and GnRH antagonist co-treatment was initiated when the leading follicle reached 12–14 mm. Final oocyte maturation was triggered with 6,500 IU hCG (Ovitrelle®, Merck Group) when leading follicles reached 16–18 mm, and oocyte retrieval was performed 34–36 h later.

All patients in this study underwent fresh embryo transfers performed on Day 3 or Day 5. Embryo transfers were conducted under transabdominal ultrasound guidance using a standardized technique. In accordance with the national regulations of the Turkish Ministry of Health, the number of embryos transferred was strictly limited based on maternal age and previous IVF attempts: a single embryo for patients < 35 years in their first two cycles, and a maximum of İki embryos for patients ≥ 35 years or those with at least two previous failed attempts.

Data were retrieved from IVF archive files and the hospital information system. Recorded variables included age, duration of infertility, AFC, basal hormone levels, semen parameters, stimulation duration, total gonadotropin dose, serum E2 and progesterone on the day of OPU, and OPU outcomes including total oocytes retrieved and MI, MII, and GV oocyte counts. OMI was calculated as the ratio of MII oocytes to total oocytes retrieved, expressed as a percentage. Embryological outcomes on day 3 and day 5 were recorded. Clinical outcomes included biochemical pregnancy (serum β-hCG > 5 mIU/mL at 14 days post-transfer), clinical pregnancy (gestational sac and/or fetal cardiac activity on ultrasound), and live birth.

Both POSEIDON Group 3 and Group 4 patients were stratified into three subgroups based on OMI values: OMI ≤ 50%, OMI 51–75%, and OMI > 75%. Demographic, hormonal, and clinical outcomes were compared across subgroups within each POSEIDON group.

These specific OMI cutoff values (≤ 50%, 51–75%, and > 75%) were pre-specified a priori based on previously established classifications in the literature, specifically adapting the stratification model proposed by Esbert et al. [8], to ensure standard clinical applicability and avoid data-driven categorization bias.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0. Normality was assessed using histograms, probability plots, and Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum–maximum), and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. Group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables, and Pearson chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 784 patients meeting POSEIDON Group 3 or Group 4 criteria were initially identified, of whom 122 were excluded. In POSEIDON Group 3, 32 patients were excluded due to failed follicular development (n = 17), failure to retrieve oocytes (n = 4), absence of sperm on testicular biopsy (n = 3), loss to follow-up (n = 7), or conversion to intrauterine insemination due to a single follicle (n = 1). In POSEIDON Group 4, 90 patients were excluded for analogous reasons: failed follicular development (n = 51), failure to retrieve oocytes (n = 10), absence of sperm on testicular biopsy (n = 2), loss to follow-up (n = 10), or conversion to intrauterine insemination (n = 17). The final study population comprised 662 patients: 245 in POSEIDON Group 3 and 417 in POSEIDON Group 4 (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of patient selection for POSEIDON Groups 3 and 4

Within POSEIDON Group 3, patients were stratified by OMI into three subgroups: OMI ≤ 50% (n = 55), OMI 51–75% (n = 57), and OMI > 75% (n = 133). In POSEIDON Group 4, the corresponding subgroups comprised OMI ≤ 50% (n = 104), OMI 51–75% (n = 91), and OMI > 75% (n = 222).

Baseline demographic and hormonal characteristics were comparable across all OMI subgroups in both POSEIDON Group 3 and Group 4. No statistically significant differences were observed in age, BMI, infertility duration, AFC, AMH, FSH, LH, TSH, prolactin, or semen parameters (all p > 0.05) (Table 1). This homogeneity confirms that the three OMI subgroups were well-matched at baseline, allowing observed differences in outcomes to be attributed to OMI rather than confounding variables.

In POSEIDON Group 3, total gonadotropin dose was significantly higher in the OMI > 75% subgroup (2976.69 ± 912.64 IU) compared to the other subgroups (p = 0.017). The number of aspirated follicles, total oocytes retrieved, MII oocytes, GV oocytes, MI oocytes, day 3 total embryos, and day 3 Grade 1 embryos all differed significantly across OMI subgroups in both POSEIDON groups (all p < 0.001). The OMI ≤ 50% subgroup consistently yielded the lowest MII oocyte counts (1.45 ± 1.20 in Group 3; 1.16 ± 0.93 in Group 4) and the fewest day 3 Grade 1 embryos. Day 5 embryo counts did not reach statistical significance in either group. In POSEIDON Group 4, trigger-day serum E2 levels were significantly elevated in the OMI 51–75% subgroup (642.46 ± 614.28 pg/mL) compared to the other subgroups (p = 0.007) (Table 2).

OMI oocyte maturation index

In POSEIDON Group 4, biochemical pregnancy (p = 0.005) and clinical pregnancy rates (p = 0.014) differed significantly across OMI subgroups. The OMI 51–75% subgroup demonstrated the highest chemical (29.7%) and clinical pregnancy rates (24.2%), while the OMI ≤ 50% subgroup yielded the lowest (11.5% and 8.7%, respectively). Live birth rates showed a consistent trend (OMI ≤ 50%: 6.7%; OMI 51–75%: 17.6%; OMI > 75%: 14.9%) but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.056), suggesting that the effect of OMI on live birth in older patients is attenuated by age-related oocyte aneuploidy (Table 4).

OMI oocyte maturation index

Multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted separately for POSEIDON Group 3 and Group 4 patients to evaluate whether the Oocyte Maturation Index (OMI) independently predicted live birth after adjusting for absolute MII oocyte count, number of top-quality day-3 embryos, maternal age, AMH levels, and number of transferred embryos. In POSEIDON Group 3 (younger patients with poor ovarian response, n = 172), OMI was identified as a significant independent predictor of live birth (overall p = 0.038). Specifically, patients in the OMI 51–75% subgroup demonstrated significantly higher odds of achieving a live birth compared to those with OMI > 75% (aOR: 2.734, 95% CI: 1.186–6.302, p = 0.018), suggesting that an intermediate maturation ratio may confer a reproductive advantage in this population. The number of top-quality day-3 embryos was also an independent predictor of live birth in this group (aOR: 1.488, 95% CI: 1.100–2.012, p = 0.010), whereas absolute MII oocyte count (p = 0.590), maternal age (p = 0.891), and AMH (p = 0.444) did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, among POSEIDON Group 4 patients (older patients with poor ovarian response, n = 263), OMI did not retain its independent prognostic significance (overall p = 0.379). In this cohort, maternal age emerged as the sole significant independent predictor of live birth (aOR: 0.797, 95% CI: 0.695–0.915, p = 0.001), with each additional year of age associated with a reduction in the odds of a live birth. Taken together, these findings indicate that OMI holds independent prognostic value for live birth in younger poor responders (Group 3), while the adverse effect of advanced maternal age appears to supersede the predictive utility of oocyte maturation ratios in older poor responders (Group 4) (Table 5).

Discussion

The principal finding of this study suggests that OMI may be a clinically meaningful predictor of IVF success in young patients with diminished ovarian reserve (POSEIDON Group 3), while its independent prognostic value may be attenuated in older patients (POSEIDON Group 4), where age-related deterioration of oocyte quality may become the dominant prognostic determinant. These findings may carry important implications for patient counseling and individualized treatment planning in this challenging patient population.

In POSEIDON Group 3 patients, statistically significant differences were observed across all clinical outcome measures by OMI subgroup. Clinical pregnancy rates were 10.9%, 42.1%, and 29.3% in the OMI ≤ 50%, 51–75%, and > 75% subgroups, respectively (p = 0.001), with corresponding live birth rates of 5.5%, 35.1%, and 20.3% (p = 0.001). Parrella et al., in a large retrospective analysis of 7,672 ICSI cycles, demonstrated that a decreasing proportion of MII oocytes significantly reduced fertilization, implantation, clinical pregnancy, and live birth rates, concluding that ooplasmic and membrane maturity are essential prerequisites for embryo developmental competence [7]. These findings support the premise that OMI reflects a fundamental biological characteristic of the oocyte cohort with direct consequences for reproductive outcomes.

An interesting observation in our cohort was that the OMI 51–75% subgroup achieved the highest absolute numerical rates across all pregnancy endpoints in POSEIDON Group 3, seemingly outperforming the OMI > 75% subgroup. However, our rigorous post-hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that this numerical superiority did not reach statistical significance (both subgroups shared the ‘b’ designation). Instead, the crucial statistical divide was between the lowest maturation cohort (OMI ≤ 50%) and the other two groups. This suggests that while moderate maturation (51–75%) yields highly favorable outcomes, achieving complete maturation (> 75%) does not appear to provide any additional clinical advantage and performs statistically comparably in this diminished reserve population. Esbert et al., who classified 4,826 IVF-ICSI cycles according to MII proportion, similarly reported that the Acceptable group (51–75% MII) achieved outcomes comparable to or exceeding the Optimal group (> 75% MII), while patients with Null or Poor maturation exhibited markedly inferior results [8]. A potential mechanistic explanation relates to temporal dissociation between nuclear and cytoplasmic maturation. As Baldini et al. reviewed, synchrony between these processes is critical for developmental competence: oocytes appearing morphologically mature may harbor cytoplasmic deficiencies compromising fertilization and embryogenesis, and accelerated nuclear maturation without commensurate cytoplasmic maturation may paradoxically reduce cohort-level competence [9]. Additionally, the OMI 51–75% subgroup yielded the highest absolute number of total and MII oocytes, which may itself have contributed to superior clinical outcomes independent of the maturation ratio.

The finding that OMI ≤ 50% was associated with profoundly reduced pregnancy rates in POSEIDON Group 3 is particularly clinically significant. In a population already burdened by diminished ovarian reserve and a limited oocyte pool, a low MII ratio compounds the quantitative disadvantage with a qualitative one. Capper et al. reported that a low oocyte maturity ratio was independently associated with reduced live birth rates in IVF/ICSI cycles, underscoring the additive prognostic burden of reduced oocyte number and maturation deficiency [2]. In POSEIDON Group 3, where each retrieved oocyte carries disproportionate prognostic weight, OMI emerges as an important complement to total oocyte count in defining a cycle’s true reproductive potential.

In POSEIDON Group 4 patients, the pattern of results was similar in direction but more attenuated in magnitude. Significant differences between OMI subgroups were observed for biochemical pregnancy (p = 0.005) and clinical pregnancy (p = 0.014), with the OMI 51–75% subgroup again achieving the highest rates. However, live birth rates did not differ significantly between OMI subgroups (p = 0.056), indicating that while OMI retains some discriminatory capacity in older patients, its prognostic utility for the ultimate endpoint of live birth is substantially diminished.

The most possible explanation for this attenuation is the well-established age-dependent increase in oocyte aneuploidy. Havrljenko et al. demonstrated in women aged ≥ 35 years that the number of MII oocytes required to achieve optimal live birth rates was substantially higher than in younger patients, with age exerting a profound independent adverse effect on cumulative outcomes [3]. Patrizio et al. further showed that the developmental competence of MII oocytes declines significantly with advancing age, such that a high MII ratio does not guarantee a correspondingly high proportion of competent embryos in older women [4]. In POSEIDON Group 4, therefore, the biological ceiling imposed by age-related chromosomal errors effectively uncouples OMI from live birth rates, as it is this aneuploidy burden rather than the maturation ratio that ultimately determines the probability of a live birth.

This interpretation is further supported by the observation that AMH levels did not differ significantly across OMI subgroups in either POSEIDON group (p = 0.212 and p = 0.127, respectively), confirming that OMI captures oocyte maturation quality information orthogonal to ovarian reserve quantity. This is consistent with Esteves et al., who demonstrated that POSEIDON Group 3 and Group 4 patients show markedly different cumulative live birth rates despite similar ovarian reserve characteristics, with age operating as an independent determinant of outcome over and above reserve markers [10].

The differential effect of OMI on day 3 and day 5 embryological endpoints is noteworthy. In both POSEIDON groups, day 3 total embryo number and Grade 1 embryo number differed significantly across OMI subgroups (all p < 0.001), while day 5 blastocyst counts did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.222 and p = 0.162 in Groups 3 and 4, respectively). This pattern reflects additional selection pressures in the cleavage-to-blastocyst transition, including embryonic genome activation and preimplantation genetic integrity, beyond oocyte maturity alone. As Baldini et al. noted, immature oocytes show significantly higher rates of aneuploidy and metaphase plate anomalies, with fewer than half being chromosomally normal [9], and the accumulation of such errors may selectively eliminate aneuploid embryos before the blastocyst stage, partially equalizing day 5 numbers while clinical pregnancy rates remain disparate.

In POSEIDON Group 3, total gonadotropin dose was significantly higher in the OMI > 75% subgroup (p = 0.017), suggesting a dose-dependent effect on oocyte maturation rates. Conforti et al. demonstrated that higher gonadotropin doses increase oocyte yield in POSEIDON-defined poor responders, but this quantitative benefit does not consistently translate into improved clinical pregnancy rates [5]. The fact that the OMI > 75% subgroup received higher gonadotropin doses yet achieved lower pregnancy rates than the OMI 51–75% group raises the possibility that supraphysiological stimulation may promote nuclear maturation at the expense of cytoplasmic competence, a hypothesis supported by mechanistic evidence from basic science [9].

In POSEIDON Group 4, trigger-day serum E2 levels were significantly higher in the OMI 51–75% subgroup (p = 0.007), suggesting that a more robust follicular response contributed to both higher oocyte counts and an intermediate maturation profile. This is consistent with evidence that trigger-day E2 serves as a surrogate marker of stimulation adequacy, with intermediate E2 values correlating with a balanced follicular cohort [11].

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective, single-center design, including the inability to establish causal relationships, potential unmeasured confounding, and the absence of preimplantation genetic testing data. The lack of PGT-A data is particularly relevant for POSEIDON Group 4 patients, where aneuploidy rates are expected to be high and integration of genetic data with OMI could enhance prognostic discrimination. As embryo transfers were predominantly performed at the cleavage stage, blastocyst-level data should be interpreted with caution. Future prospective multicenter studies incorporating genetic testing and protocols stratified by OMI will be necessary to validate these findings and define evidence-based OMI thresholds.

A significant consideration regarding the superior numerical outcomes of the OMI 51–75% group relates to the quantitative nature of the oocyte pool in POSEIDON patients. In our study, the OMI 51–75% subgroup also presented with a higher absolute number of retrieved and MII oocytes compared to the OMI > 75% group. In patients with diminished ovarian reserve, even a single additional oocyte can profoundly impact the likelihood of obtaining a viable embryo. Therefore, the observed advantage of the intermediate OMI group may partially stem from this quantitative benefit rather than a qualitative superiority of the maturation ratio itself. This potential bias constitutes a limitation of our retrospective analysis and underscores that OMI should be interpreted in conjunction with total oocyte yield.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings suggest that OMI is a significant prognostic marker in POSEIDON Group 3 and 4 patients. While an OMI between 51% and 75% was associated with the most favorable outcomes, the most critical clinical finding is that maintaining an OMI rate above 50% appears to be the primary driver of success. Below this 50% threshold, pregnancy and live birth rates decline sharply. Clinicians should prioritize strategies that optimize maturation rates to exceed this 50% benchmark in poor responders. These findings may support the incorporation of OMI into post-retrieval clinical assessment alongside established parameters such as total oocyte count, AMH, and AFC, to potentially improve cycle-level prognostication and patient counseling.
