How Are We Addressing Axial Psoriatic Arthritis in Clinical Practice?
Authors: Xabier Michelena, Clementina López-Medina, Eugenio De Miguel, Manuel José Moreno-Ramos, Rubén Queiro, Helena Marzo-Ortega, Xavier Juanola
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic inflammatory disease affecting the musculoskeletal system, skin and nails. In addition to peripheral joints, inflammation of the spine and sacroiliac joints may occur. Yet, research into this axial phenotype has lagged behind partly because of the challenge in its clinical identification with a lack of specific clinical, molecular or imaging biomarkers. In the absence of a validated definition of what constitutes axial PsA (axPsA), guidelines for the management of axial involvement in PsA in clinical practice are scarce. On the basis of a literature review and their clinical expertise, a group of rheumatology experts provide their opinion to aid the diagnosis and management of axial PsA in clinical practice.Keywords: Axial psoriatic arthritis, Axial involvement, Psoriatic arthritis, Psoriatic spondylitis, Sacroiliitis, Axial spondyloarthritis

Key Summary Points

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic inflammatory disease mainly affecting the musculoskeletal system, the skin and nails, but also affecting the axial skeleton (peripheral joints, inflammation of the spine and sacroiliac joints).Currently, no widely accepted definition of axial involvement in PsA has been agreed, and no specific clinical, molecular or imaging biomarkers for its characterization have been recognized.The diagnosis and management of axial PsA are challenging because of the lack of specific clinical practice guidelines.In the present study, a group of rheumatology experts summarize available evidence and provide their opinion for diagnosis and management of axial PsA in clinical practice.

Introduction

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic inflammatory disease involving both the musculoskeletal system, the skin and nails. Main manifestations include arthritis, enthesitis, dactylitis, as well as involvement of the axial skeleton (sacroiliac joints and/or spine inflammation) [1]. However, the prevalence of axial involvement in PsA is not well known. This is largely due to the lack of a validated definition for axial disease in PsA [2], and the fact that the prevalence depends on the disease duration, ranging from 5% to 28% [3] in early PsA to 25–70% [4, 5] in established disease, and the identification method used (axial pain, functional limitation or radiographic). The widely recognized axial domain in PsA raises the discussion as to whether it is axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) with concomitant psoriasis, or a distinct clinical entity, the so-called axial psoriatic arthritis (axPsA).

Although international societies are making great efforts to establish axPsA classification criteria [6], no widely accepted definition of axial disease in PsA has been introduced so far. Therefore, guidelines for axPsA diagnosis and management in clinical practice are still lacking.

In this narrative review, we summarize available evidence and gather clinical expertise to disentangle clinical, genetic and imaging features in axPsA while highlighting the need for new terminology, tools and algorithms for its successful management.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Considerations on Nomenclature

Over the years, different terms have been used to describe PsA with axial involvement including psoriatic spondylitis [7–9], psoriatic spondyloarthropathy [10], psoriatic spondyloarthritis [4] and axial psoriatic arthritis [11]. Although no term encompasses all its nuances, this multiplicity hampers information localization and blurs its clinical entity. Etymologically, the word “spondylitis” means inflammation of the vertebra (sphondylus) and might not be totally accurate since it ignores sacroiliac joint involvement. The term “spondyloarthropathy” literally means “any vertebral or spinal disease”, leading to the same inaccuracy. In addition, this term was eventually considered obsolete, leading the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) to propose replacing it with “spondyloarthritis” [12].

In recent years, the term “axial psoriatic arthritis (axial PsA)”, developed as a logical progression from the wider used “axial spondyloarthritis”, has gained momentum, being the term most used in medical literature. Indeed, the term axial PsA singles out the affected musculoskeletal domain within the wider PsA construct, hence avoiding confusion with the classification of axSpA with associated psoriasis. It is expected that the development of standardized nomenclature will improve not only disease understanding but also disease definition. Therefore, to unify the terminology, and on the basis of the current evidence, we suggest that “axial psoriatic arthritis” is the preferred proposed term and “axPsA” its preferred abbreviation.

Classification Criteria for Axial Psoriatic Arthritis

Currently, no classification criteria for axPsA exist. In 2006 the Classification Criteria for Psoriatic Arthritis (CASPAR) were developed, supporting PsA as a distinct clinical entity on the basis of a large prospective study [13]. According to these, entry criteria include patients with peripheral, axial arthritis or enthesitis, and ≥ 3 points from the following categories: (1) current psoriasis or history of psoriasis (diagnosed by dermatologist or rheumatologist), or family history of psoriasis; (2) nail psoriasis: onycholysis, pitting or hyperkeratosis; (3) rheumatoid factor negativity; (4) current or prior dactylitis; (5) juxta-articular new bone formation on imaging [13]. Altogether, these entry criteria (peripheral arthritis, axial or enthesitis) are fairly broad and allow for those patients with psoriasis and axial joint symptoms to be classified as PsA. In the setting of vertebral inflammatory disease, this is especially relevant because no standard definition of axial involvement has been agreed so far in PsA. Furthermore, CASPAR criteria are mainly focused on peripheral involvement. Indeed, the radiographic criterion to allow classification in the presence of new formation was developed and validated for peripheral joint involvement based on hand and feet X-ray findings. In clinical practice, as a result of the pathophysiological similarity with axSpA, patients with PsA and axial involvement might be classified according to the Assessment of SpondyloArthritis International Society (ASAS) classification criteria [14]. The ASAS criteria consider axSpA in the presence of chronic back pain (≥ 3 months) and age at onset < 45 years old. These entry criteria, however, may also underestimate patients with axPsA, since back pain may not be as common in PsA as it is in axSpA [15]. In addition, some patients may present after the age of 45 and would be excluded from the ASAS criteria. On the one hand, the ASAS criteria “imaging arm” includes the presence of sacroiliitis by X-ray scan (according to the radiographic criterion of the modified New York criteria) [16] or by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), plus at least one spondyloarthritis (SpA) feature. Yet, spine involvement without sacroiliitis may be present in up to 35% of patients with axPsA [4, 17, 18]. Further, the value of MRI in aiding the diagnosis of axPsA has not been fully elucidated with preliminary data suggesting that only 40% of patients with PsA and BASDAI ≥ 4 may show inflammatory lesions [19]. On the other hand, the ASAS criteria “clinical arm” considers the presence of HLA-B27 plus at least two SpA features. Patients with axPsA, however, show a weak association with HLA-B27, with around 57–77% of patients being negative for HLA-B27 [4, 11, 20–22], suggesting a lack of sensitivity of the current ASAS criteria if applied in axPsA.

Overall, both the CASPAR and ASAS criteria are not sensitive or specific enough to classify axial PsA, and caution should be applied when using them in clinical practice, i.e. patients fulfilling ASAS criteria may indicate axial involvement, although not fulfilling ASAS criteria does not rule out axial involvement.

Currently, the international ASAS and the Group for Research and Assessment of Psoriasis and Psoriatic Arthritis (GRAPPA) are jointly working on the AXIS project aimed at developing classification criteria specific for axPsA, although it is mainly focused in the context of clinical research, rather than diagnostic criteria [23].

Diagnosis

Moll and Wright placed PsA within the SpA group because it shares clinical and genetic characteristics with other forms of SpA [24]. In fact, PsA with axial involvement shows some overlapping features with axial disease in SpA, making them difficult to differentiate. To date, substantial attempts have been made to identify core particularities of axPsA that may differentiate this entity from axSpA with concomitant psoriasis; however, the lack of specificity of “axial involvement” in the PsA setting has been a great barrier. In reality, the diagnosis of axPsA diagnosis remains clinical and based on clinicians’ expertise.

axPsA awareness is pivotal in order to guide prognosis, treatment and disease follow-up. In fact, early diagnosis and management is crucial to modify the disease course and subsequently prevent irreversible complications such as loss of spine mobility, development or worsening of sacroiliitis and also to reduce the impact on patient’s quality of life (QoL) [25]. Pending the consensual definition of axial involvement, and considering that the clinical presentation can be highly variable, we summarize here specific and precise clinical, genetic and imaging features that can aid the diagnosis of axPsA (Fig. 1, Tables 1 and 2).

Clinical Key Points for Axial Psoriatic Arthritis Diagnosis

This section will highlight the clinical peculiarities of axPsA that can be observed through an exhaustive and precise physical examination, enabling an accurate diagnosis. It is important to stress that presenting PsA with back pain does not imply a straight diagnosis of axPsA; albeit, it raises a red flag for axPsA evaluation. In many patients, however, axPsA can be asymptomatic [11, 27–29]. Current evidence suggests that up to 30% of patients might show radiographic evidence of axial involvement without clinical symptoms [27], highlighting the fact that symptomatology is not an accurate predictor of axial or spinal involvement in axPsA.

Unfortunately, no consensus exists on how, when or what patients should be screened for axial disease, but given that axial involvement has been associated with long-term adverse outcomes, higher disease activity, and higher impact on QoL [20, 30], it would be advisable to perform an active evaluation of axial involvement in all patients with PsA. Traditionally, evaluation of axial involvement has been performed by assessing inflammatory back pain (IBP); however, between 45% and 55% of patients with axPsA do not report IBP symptoms [11, 28, 31]. In fact, several studies have demonstrated that IBP has low predictive value for identifying axial involvement in PsA, determined through Calin, ASAS and Berlin criteria [27], and showing poorer sensitivity in women [32]. Subsequently, another study identified that ASAS criteria perform well in patients with active axPsA symptoms, but revealed low sensitivity in established disease [33]. In reality, chronic back pain is common in patients with PsA, and can be either IBP or mechanical back pain (MBP). Hence, the next concept to consider is the potential presence of either IBP or MBP in patients with axPsA.

Finally, the location of spine involvement is different between axSpA and axPsA. Unlike axial SpA, patients with axPsA show frequent involvement of the cervical spine [34]. Indeed, patients with PsA may present only with cervical pain as a particular clinical symptom [34] which should prompt further investigation. Another common symptom in patients with axPsA is spinal morning stiffness; thus, assessing spine mobility to distinguish between permanent or reversible limitations in the spinal mobility for accurately diagnosing axPsA would be helpful (Fig. 1).

Further, axial disease in PsA has been associated with more severe peripheral disease [20, 22, 26, 35, 36], including peripheral joint erosions, especially in women [37]. When considering skin involvement, higher psoriasis severity (PASI score) and nail involvement has been reported in patients with axPsA compared with patients without axial involvement [20, 22, 26]. Finally, some authors have also demonstrated a link between axPsA and extramusculoskeletal manifestations such as uveitis (in particular HLA-B27+) or inflammatory bowel disease [20, 25, 38]. Nevertheless, current evidence is insufficient to include these features as clinical criteria for guiding axPsA diagnosis.

Regarding demographics, some studies showed that patients with axPsA are younger than patients with peripheral PsA [4, 20, 26], and have a younger age at disease onset [4, 15, 26]. Compared with patients with axSpA, patients with axPsA are older [3, 4, 26]. Moreover, early-onset PsA tends to have more axial symptoms and sacroiliitis during its course [39, 40]. Axial involvement in PsA is associated with a higher cardiovascular risk when compared with non-axial PsA [41]. Other comorbidities such as depression have also been reported more frequent in patients with axPsA [26]. Finally, current smoking was described as an independent predictor of axPsA [42]. In spite of these trends and differences, the evidence available may not warrant the consideration of demographic characteristics and personal records as a screening tool for axPsA.

In short, axPsA may be suspected in patients with PsA with—or without—chronic back pain (≥ 3 months) in any spinal location (cervical, thoracic spine or lumbar) Thus, after a thorough anamnesis and a physical examination aimed at detecting chronic pain (either inflammatory or mechanical) or impaired spinal mobility, further investigations including laboratory assessments as well as imaging of the sacroiliac joints and spine should be performed.

Genetic Testing for Axial Psoriatic Arthritis Diagnosis

HLA-B27 is commonly associated with spondyloarthritis, although the prevalence of HLA-B27+ in patients with axPsA seems to be relatively low (23–43%) [4, 20, 21, 43]. Patients with PsA who are HLA-B27 positive tend to exhibit more radiographic severity, such as bilateral symmetric sacroiliitis [21], more radiographic progression [11], and acute inflammatory lesions on MRI [19]. Conversely, patients with axPsA who are HLA-B27 negative have more asymmetrical sacroiliitis, isolated spondylitis, more cervical involvement, less radiographic progression, and less bone marrow oedema on MRI [2, 19]. Thus, HLA-B27 status might have a role in determining severity and radiographic phenotype expression, as observed in patients with axSpA [44]. Taken together, HLA-B27 positivity is not an accurate predictor of axial involvement, rather an indicator of more severe or persistent disease acting as a guide for further imaging and the need to optimize treatment. Overall, the absence of HLA-B27 does not exclude axPsA diagnosis, but its presence points towards a more dominant clinical phenotype. On the basis of this and since HLA-B27 testing is a non-invasive, reliable, inexpensive test that is done once in life and can be safely used by clinicians for guiding further explorations and treatment, it is very convenient to determine HLA-B27 status in all patients with PsA.

Apart from HLA-B27, other HLA alleles such as HLA-B*08 have also been associated with axial involvement [3, 45]. Moreover, compared with r-axSpA, patients with axPsA show different prevalence rates of a range of HLA alleles (HLA-C02, HLA-C06, among others). Precise determination of these alleles entails high-resolution HLA, which is an expensive test and may not be available in all settings. Other genetic differences, such as polymorphisms in the IL-13 gene, and in the IL-23 receptor gene have been identified, but their potential relationship to axial involvement in PsA is unknown [3]. Given the low level of evidence and the available resources, the authors do not consider the determination of other analytical or genetic parameters currently justified for axPsA diagnosis.

Imaging for Axial Psoriatic Arthritis Diagnosis

Since PsA and axSpA are both spondyloarthritis, they share a range of radiographic abnormalities. Nevertheless, there also key distinguishing features that may aid the diagnosis of axPsA, which will be summarized in this section.

Evidence in Sacroiliac Joints

According to ASAS criteria, imaging evidence of sacroiliitis is the key feature for the diagnosis of axSpA. In contrast, up to 35% of patients with axPsA may have spondylitis without sacroiliitis [4, 17, 18]. In fact, the presence of sacroiliitis on radiographs or MRI is less often associated with a diagnosis of axPsA (instead of axSpA) [46]. Thus, the first radiologic key feature in patients with axPsA is the potential presence of spondylitis without sacroiliitis. Compared with axSpA, sacroiliitis is more frequently asymmetrical (up to 70% of patients), and unilateral [22, 47–49]. Moreover, patients with axPsA are less likely to develop sacroiliac joint complete ankylosis and bridging syndesmophytes [4].

To date, studies using MRI on determining sacroiliac joint (SIJ) involvement in axPsA are still limited, and no clear MRI cutoffs for detecting SIJ lesions in axPsA are available. Thus, more international consensus regarding the definition of axPsA on MRI is needed [50]. MRI is a sensitive diagnostic technique to detect sacroiliitis. Two studies reported MRI features of sacroiliitis in 38% of patients [48, 51]. Williamson et al. reported abnormal MRI scans associated with restricted spinal movements, but no correlations with HLA-B27 status were observed [48]. In contrast, Castillo-Gallego et al. negatively correlated MRI scores with the HLA-B27 status [19]. Compared with axSpA, lower total MRI scores were reported in axPsA despite comparable levels or pain and BASDAI scores [19].

Other studies show similar sensitivity of radiography and MRI for detecting sacroiliitis [52]. Thus, MRI may be useful when radiographic findings are unhelpful [53].

Evidence in the Spine

PsA and SpA with axial involvement can show different patterns of radiographic features in the spine.

Radiographic spinal changes on imaging include syndesmophyte formation, being asymmetrical, coarse and “chunky” in patients with axPsA. It allows one to differentiate it from axSpA, where syndesmophytes are marginal, symmetric and well delimited. Finally, and compared with axSpA, bridging syndesmophytes develop less frequently in axPsA (23% vs 10%) [4]. It should be taken into account that differentiating degenerative, non-inflammatory changes of diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH) can be challenging, especially among elderly patients, without sacroiliitis, patients with diabetes or obesity (Fig. 2). Besides, axPsA and DISH can coexist, thus hindering the diagnosis [54].

For axPsA diagnosis, radiography is the preferred method for structural damage detection. Appropriateness of performing spine and pelvic radiographs in patients with axPsA without axial symptomology could be debatable because X-ray does not always detect sacroiliac involvement; there are no international recommendations in this context, and also because of the exposure to ionizing radiation. The authors of this manuscript reached a consensus on the importance of conducting radiographs at the time of PsA diagnosis with the aim to confirm or rule out axial involvement. Subsequently, axial radiographs may be ordered again during follow-up in case of appearance of axial symptoms.

MRI is a sensitive diagnostic tool for assessing axial disease in spondyloarthritis; however, studies evaluating the role of MRI on axPsA diagnosis are limited and the results are inconsistent. MRI allows for the evaluations of signs of inflammation, including synovitis, tenosynovitis and bone marrow oedema lesions, which may reveal a relationship between the severity and extent of disease and HLA-B27 positivity in symptomatic patients with axPsA [19]. Moreover, MRI can identify structural lesions, such as sclerosis, squaring, erosion, spine syndesmophytes/joint space width [55]. However, whole spine MRI adds limited value to SIJ MRI in SpA, when performed for disease activity assessment [56, 57]. Finally, MRI entails high costs, is time-consuming and has limitations in characterizing bone remodelling, the morphology of syndesmophytes, or appreciating sclerosis [58, 59].

Recently, the MAXIMISE study described the co-occurrence of inflammatory and post-inflammatory changes of the bone marrow as assessed by FAt Spondyloarthritis Spine Score (FASSS), suggesting an inflammatory process similar to axSpA in axPsA [60]. More imaging studies are needed to understand better axPsA.

On the basis of the current literature, to diagnose axPsA in patients with PsA with—or without—chronic back pain (≥ 3 months) in any spine location (cervical, thoracic spine or lumbar), conventional radiographies of the cervical and lumbar spine (lateral profile) and pelvis (antero-posterior) are a reasonable first step for axial involvement assessment. MRI targeted to symptomatic area should be considered. Finally, when the clinical scenario changes, with new symptomatology or mobility impairments, it might also be useful to consider updated radiography.

Monitoring

After diagnosis, appropriate tools for monitoring axial disease in PsA are needed. Nowadays, there are no validated scores for assessing disease activity in axPsA, and axSpA developed scores have been used with varying levels of success.

The Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Activity Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) scale is commonly used to determine axial disease in PsA because it correlates well with patient-reported arthritis symptoms; however, it does not seem to be a good index for evaluating disease activity in axPsA [61]. This may be because five out of the six questions in the BASDAI scale are not specific to axial disease. Moreover, Reddy et al. recently reported that BASDAI has similar scores in PsA regardless of the presence of axial disease and thus it is not specific for axial involvement [62]. Moreover, BASDAI does not discriminate well between high and low disease activity [63].

The Axial Spondyloarthritis Disease Activity Score (ASDAS) is a well-validated disease activity measure developed for axSpA, although its use has not been validated in axPsA. The MERECES study (in Spanish: MEdir Resultados. Consenso de Evaluación en Salud para artritis psoriásica) aimed at reaching consensus on the instruments to be used in clinical practice to evaluate the effectiveness of biological treatment. Experts agreed on the use of ASDAS to evaluate axial involvement because it allows for direct measurement, and it can be easily implemented in clinical practice [64].

Regarding evaluations on QoL and physical function, the Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ), Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI) and the physical functioning scale of the Short-form 36 (SF-36-PF) questionnaires perform properly in the context of axPsA [65]. The multidisciplinary consensus study MERECES, aimed at reaching a consensus on the instruments to be used in clinical practice to evaluate the effectiveness of biological disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (bDMARD) treatment in patients with PsA. This study agreed on considering Psoriatic Arthritis Impact of Disease (PsAID12) the preferred instrument to evaluate HRQOL in patients with axPsA [64].

Given the lack of validated alternatives in axPsA, the authors considered the PsAIDS12 scale for assessing QoL, ASDAS scale for disease activity, and BASFI scale for functionality assessment. Besides, visual analogue scales (0–10) to evaluate symptoms related to axial manifestations (spinal pain, morning stiffness, etc.) may also be used.

Treatment

Currently, management of patients with axPsA is performed in accordance with international guidelines for PsA and axSpA [6, 66–69]. In fact, specific studies on axPsA are still scarce, and as of today, only one randomized clinical trial (RCT) has been designed specifically in patients with axPsA to assess an interleukin (IL)-17A inhibitor (secukinumab) with published results [70]. Although axSpA and axPsA share some clinical and radiological manifestations, it is unclear whether treatment algorithms for axPsA can be extrapolated from studies conducted in patients with axSpA. Furthermore, different clinical responses have been observed in axPsA with specific mechanisms of action of bDMARDs that point towards the need for a specific therapeutic algorithm distinct from that of axSpA.

Current Management

The current management algorithm for axPsA involves nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) as first-line therapy, either alone or in combination with other drug classes; physiotherapy; and simple analgesia. In patients with poor or inadequate response to NSAIDs, biological (b) or targeted synthetic (ts) disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (tsDMARDs) are recommended [6] (Fig. 3). GRAPPA strongly recommends bDMARDs (IL-17A inhibitors and tumour necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors) or JAK inhibitors for axPsA (extrapolating data from axSpA). As a result of insufficient or conflicting evidence for IL-12/23i, IL-23i, no recommendations on its use in axPsA have been issued [6].

Biological Disease-Modifying Antirheumatic Drugs

No clear differences between bDMARDS and tsDMARDS in terms of peripheral manifestations have been reported; however, relevant differences have been observed in patients with axial involvement and active disease. In this context, EULAR recommendations for the management of patients with PsA and clinically relevant axial disease with inadequate response to NSAIDs include first-line IL-17A inhibitors. Nowadays, only the MAXIMISE trial provides information in this setting, since it was specifically designed to investigate the IL-17A inhibitor secukinumab in axPsA [71].

Evidence on TNF Inhibitors

At present, no studies with TNF inhibitors specifically designed for axPsA have been carried out. TNF inhibitors (adalimumab, certolizumab pegol, etanercept, infliximab and golimumab) are effective in patients with axSpA [72]. Further investigations to assess the efficacy and safety of TNFi specifically on patients with axPsA are still needed.

Evidence on IL-17 Inhibitors

Interleukin-17A (IL-17A) inhibitors (secukinumab, ixekizumab) and the dual IL-17A/F inhibitor (bimekizumab) have shown efficacy in PsA and axSpA [72, 73]. Importantly, secukinumab is the only drug evaluated in a double-blind randomized phase 3 clinical trial specifically designed for patients with PsA and axial involvement. The clinical trial MAXIMISE randomized 498 adult patients with axPsA to receive anti-IL-17A (secukinumab 150 mg), anti-IL-17A (secukinumab 300 mg) or placebo. In this study, axial involvement was defined by CASPAR criteria, and active spinal disease with a BASDAI score ≥ 4. At week 12, 63.1% and 66.3% of patients on secukinumab (300 mg and 150 mg, respectively) showed significant improvements in signs and symptoms determined by ASAS20, compared with 31.3% of patients on placebo. Similarly, secukinumab demonstrated significant improvements on secondary outcomes: ASAS40, BASDAI50, Spinal VAS, HAQDI, FACIT-Fatigue, ASAS-HI, ASDAS-CRP and Berlin MRI scores vs placebo [70].

The efficacy and safety of other IL-17 inhibitors (bimekizumab and ixekizumab) has only been evaluated in patients with axSpA. Given that evidence in patients with axPsA is limited, further investigations are still needed.

As commented above, in EULAR recommendations for the management of patients with PsA and clinically relevant axial disease, IL-17A inhibition (secukinumab) is recommended as first-line therapy due to the available evidence (MAXIMISE trial) [70]. Thus, the level of evidence is stronger for IL-17A inhibition than for the other drugs, where the data are derived from axial SpA [71].

Evidence on IL-12/23 Inhibitors

Currently, three bDMARDs targeting the IL-23 are approved for patients with PsA (guselkumab, risankizumab) and the dual inhibitor IL-12/23 (ustekinumab). Guselkumab is effective in patients with psoriasis and PsA with peripheral involvement, although no conclusive data in axPsA has been reported so far [74]. In contrast, RCTs with anti-IL-12/23 (ustekinumab) and anti-IL-23 (risankizumab) did not show efficacy in patients with axSpA [75, 76].

Evidence on JAK Inhibitors

Tofacitinib and upadacitinib are already approved for PsA and have shown efficacy in axSpA, but their effectiveness in axPsA remains unknown [77–81]. A post hoc analysis of upadacitinib’s RCTs, SELECT-PsA 1 and 2, which included patients with investigator-determined axial involvement, showed greater BASDAI and ASDAS responses with upadacitinib 15 mg versus placebo [82]. Although no specific RCTs are currently being conducted to specifically assess the efficacy of JAK inhibitors in axPsA, it is worth noting that there is an RCT evaluating the effect of tofacitinib in reducing MRI-detected inflammation in patients with axSpA [83].

Differential Therapeutic Response in Axial Psoriatic Arthritis

Patients with axPsA and axSpA seem to differ in response to biologic therapies. One study in patients with predominant axPsA treated with TNFi (adalimumab, etanercept and golimumab) showed that BASDAI 50 was achieved in 31.2% of patients after 12 months [84]. In contrast, in another study in patients with axSpA, TNFi demonstrated efficacies around 50–60% in BASDAI 50 [85]. Although these data comprise different populations and are not comparable, these results roughly suggest a differential behaviour of TNFi facing these clinical entities.

Recently, it was demonstrated that PsA with confirmed axial involvement is significantly associated with a higher chance of drug survival in secukinumab users but not in TNFi users compared with those with a peripheral predominant disease [86]. This different therapeutic behaviour suggests the possibility of adapting the therapeutic algorithm in patients with axPsA to improve their management. It might be hypothesised that HLA-B27 status could play a role in these therapeutic differences because patients with axSpA more commonly show HLA-B27+, and it entails more disease severity. However, in patients with axPsA on bDMARD or tsDMARD, mild improvements in axial-related outcomes were observed, irrespective of HLA-B27 status [87].

Considering these data, it is not unreasonable to assume that the presence of axial manifestations could have an impact on the choice of a specific treatment method or drug class. Whether the differences between both clinical entities and their different therapeutic responses are enough to adapt the algorithm to axPsA is still unclear. Therefore, in the absence of adequate data on axPsA population, it is advisable to follow the axSpA treatment algorithm (Fig. 3). Further investigations to assess the efficacy and safety of IL-12/23i and IL-17i in axPsA would be of the upmost interest. In this sense, future, large powered studies considering patients with axPsA will help in elucidating the best therapeutic algorithm to improve patients’ outcomes and quality of life.

Conclusion

“Axial psoriatic arthritis” appears to be the preferred term for referring to axial involvement in PsA, and axPsA its abbreviation. On the basis of the current literature, we suggest that all patients with PsA should be actively examined for axial involvement using:

(i)Clinical criteria: directed anamnesis and physical exam focused on detecting chronic back pain (either inflammatory or mechanical) in the entire spine and assessing spine mobility.

Clinical criteria: directed anamnesis and physical exam focused on detecting chronic back pain (either inflammatory or mechanical) in the entire spine and assessing spine mobility.

(ii)Genetic tests, in particular HLA-B27 status.

Genetic tests, in particular HLA-B27 status.

(iii)Imaging: plain radiography of cervical and lumbar spine (lateral profile) and pelvis (antero–posterior).

Imaging: plain radiography of cervical and lumbar spine (lateral profile) and pelvis (antero–posterior).

Currently, no appropriate classification criteria for axPsA are available, nor guidelines for follow-up specifically designed for axPsA. Thus, until these become available it is recommended to use classification criteria, activity scales, function scales and QoL scales specific for axSpA. Specifically, the PsAID12 scale for assessing QoL, ASDAS scale for disease activity and BASFI scale for functionality assessment should be considered.

Regarding therapeutic management, only one study involving an IL-17A inhibitor (secukinumab) has demonstrated efficacy in axPsA, but no specific algorithms for patients with axPsA are available.

Further studies and investigations are needed to improve the management of patients with axPsA.
