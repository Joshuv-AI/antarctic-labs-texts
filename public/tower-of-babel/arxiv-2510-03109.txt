Rates of Convergence of Generalised Variational Inference Posteriors under Prior Misspecification
Terje Mildner, Paris Giampouras, Theodoros Damoulas

Abstract
We prove rates of convergence and robustness to prior misspecification within a Generalised Variational Inference (GVI) framework with bounded divergences. This addresses a significant open challenge for GVI and Federated GVI that employ a different divergence to the Kullback–Leibler under prior misspecification, operate within a subset of possible probability measures, and result in intractable posteriors. Our theoretical contributions cover severe prior misspecification while relying on our ability to restrict the space of possible GVI posterior measures, and infer properties based on this space. In particular, we are able to establish sufficient conditions for existence and uniqueness of GVI posteriors on arbitrary Polish spaces, prove that the GVI posterior measure concentrates on a neighbourhood of loss minimisers, and extend this to rates of convergence regardless of the prior measure.

Introduction

The Bayesian, by nature of being Bayesian, can elicit arbitrary priors and may hence encounter prior misspecification; she must either suppose the prior encodes the best available knowledge of the underlying process—this however is scarcely practicable and processes exist for which we cannot intuit priors that lead to consistent posteriors—or accept the arbitrariness
[
14
,
22

. In fact, for any finite number of observations the prior alone may induce any measure a posteriori.
Moreover, it is scarcely the case that the model is well specified, that is the model family
\Theta

Generalised Variational Inference (GVI)

The present paper is concerned with characterising the dynamics of updating a posterior measure in the presence of prior and model misspecification through Generalised Variational Inference, as introduced by Knoblauch, Jewson and Damoulas
[
24
]
. In particular, we aim to answer the following open problems: under what conditions is a Generalised Variational Inference posterior measure robust to prior misspecification? Do GVI posteriors exist when observations come from an infinite dimensional stochastic process and are these asymptotically consistent? At what rate does this convergence take place?

Asymptotic consistency of posterior measures justifies the updating scheme over its frequentist counterpart by illustrating that we may come arbitrarily close to the optimal given sufficient observations. In Bayesian inference under misspecification asymptotic consistency is often credited when disregarding model and prior misspecification

2.1
Generalised Variational Inference and Related Methods

The Bayesian posterior—consisting of the likelihood function of the data,
p_{\theta}(X_{1}^{n})
, and a prior measure,
\Pi
—is

\Pi_{n}A:=\cfrac{\int_{A}p_{\theta}(X_{1}^{n})\,\Pi(d\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}p_{\theta}(X_{1}^{n})\,\Pi(d\theta)}

as usual. If we replace the likelihood by the exponential of a negative loss acting on the data we get Generalised Bayesian Inference (GBI)
[
9
,
28
]
, or a Gibbs posterior
[
2
]
. If we discard the prior, we may instead get an energy–based, frequentist model. As shown in
[
36
]
Bayes may be equivalently formulated as an optimisation problem where
\Pi_{n}
is the posterior minimising the ‘Evidence Lower Bound’—the expectation of the negative log–likelihood, and the Kullback–Leibler divergence to the prior—over all probability measures acting on the hypothesis space. GVI instead replaces the negative log–likelihood by any loss function, similar to GBI, as well as the KL divergence by any divergence, generalising these previous approaches. Tangentially, minimising a divergence other than the KL divergence to the Bayesian posterior, not just the prior, may result in nicer optimisation problems in practice, e.g. in Rényi variational inference
[
25
]
. This however does not nicely decompose into an expectation acting on the loss and a divergence measure acting on the prior, conflating model and prior misspecification; see also section 2.3.2 in
[
24
]
. Typical choices of loss functions are those that either offer attractive computational benefits, and those that incorporate the hypothesis measure robustly, including those based on robust divergences
[
27
]
.

2.2
Prior Misspecification

As identified in
[
34
,
31
]
the elements of
\Theta
that the Bayesian posterior targets are those minimising the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the data generating process and the hypothesis measure. Denoting this as a function on
\Theta
, we write:

h(\theta)=\lim_{n}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\left[\log\frac{p_{0}(x_{1}^{n})}{p_{\theta}(x_{1}^{n})}\right]

which may possibly be infinite; note that the expectation is with respect to
P_{0}
and hence over
\Omega
. This is the loss function targeted in classical Bayesian inference, in particular the elements of
\Theta
that minimise the above minimise the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the data generating process and the hypothesis measure.
If the prior has full measure on the set of elements where this rate is infinite, denote this as
I:=\{\theta\in\Theta:h(\theta)=\infty\}
, then all previous results on consistency, let alone rates of convergence, of (Generalised) Bayesian inference fail
[
31
]
. In this case we say that the prior is severely misspecified. In this we also include priors that lead to inconsistent posteriors such as those observed in
[
13
]
.

To make this rigorous, we place the following definition of robustness to prior misspecification.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
be the set of all possible priors. We say a posterior is asymptotically robust to prior misspecification w.r.t.
\mathcal{G}
if the posterior is consistent for all priors
\Pi\in\mathcal{G}
.

Classical results on Bayesian sensitivity analysis deal with small perturbations of some reference prior by some other measure through additive or multiplicative misspecification
[
12
,
17
]
. That is some reference prior
\Pi
is perturbed by another probability measure
G\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
through a constant
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
, e.g.

\Pi_{\varepsilon}=(1-\varepsilon)\Pi+\varepsilon G
. Our results are different to this line of work, and we will not use such explicit formulations for
\mathcal{G}
. We discuss this distinction in
Section

B
.

2.3
Illustrative Example: Bayesian Inference under Prior Misspecification

To illustrate the non–robustness of Bayesian inference under prior misspecification, we give a simple, parametric example with
\Theta=\mathbb{R}
.
Consider the classical Bayesian posterior, and assume that
p_{0}
is Gaussian with unknown mean and known variance,
X_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta_{0},\sigma_{p}^{2})
.
The prior is a contaminated Gaussian,
\pi\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\pi},\sigma_{\pi}^{2})
where
\mu_{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then the posterior on the mean,
\theta
, is Gaussian and characterised by the following variance and mean:

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}^{2}=\left(\sigma_{\pi}^{-2}+n\sigma_{p}^{-2}\right)^{-1},\,

\displaystyle\mu_{n}=\sigma_{n}^{2}\left(\frac{\mu_{\pi}}{\sigma_{\pi}^{2}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{X_{i}}{\sigma_{p}^{2}}\right)

For fixed
n
, and given knowledge of the data, we may pick
\mu_{\pi}
and
\sigma_{\pi}
to achieve any Gaussian distribution a posteriori, disregarding the data entirely.

Moreover, the Kullback–Leibler divergence to the prior has closed form:

D_{KL}(q:\pi)=\log\frac{\sigma_{\pi}}{\sigma_{q}}+\frac{(\mu_{q}-\mu_{\pi})^{2}+\sigma_{q}^{2}}{2\sigma_{\pi}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}

which is convex in both
\mu_{q}
and
\sigma_{q}
. Therefore, as
\mu_{\pi}\rightarrow\infty
, this increases, and hence forces
\mu_{q}
to match the prior mean as the gradient is minimised if and only if their means are equal.
See
Section

C
for further details.

\mathcal{Q}
is not convex in typical situations of interest, we generally do not have uniqueness of the GVI posterior (see
[
29
]
for non–uniqueness under Kullback–Leibler approximations).
For instance, the set of Gaussian measures is not convex, nor are typical mixture models
[
29
]
. However, we can make the following observation:

Corollary 3
.

A convex combination of elements from
\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{n}

if and only if that convex combination is also in
\mathcal{Q}
.
That is, pick

and some

, then

\gamma:=a\mu+(1-a)\nu\in\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\star}\iff\gamma\in\mathcal{Q}\,.

It follows as special cases, that (a) if
\mathcal{Q}
is convex then so is
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\star}
, and (b) if
\mathcal{Q}
is a set of mixtures, not necessarily convex, then if individual components are contained within
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\star}
, then so are all permitted mixtures between these.
If
\mathcal{Q}
is indeed convex then the following, stronger result on the uniqueness of the GVI minimiser then immediately follows:

Corollary 4
.

Let the assumptions be as in
Theorem

2
. Suppose further that the divergence is strictly convex in its first argument and that
\mathcal{Q}
is convex. Then there exists a unique minimiser of the functional
\nu\mapsto T_{n}(\nu)
.

Therefore, under the conditions of the corollary above, the GVI minimiser will be an extreme point of not only
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\star}
but also of
\mathcal{Q}
, exists, and is unique.

3.3
Support of the Prior is not required

Significantly, our results are independent of the choice of prior
\Pi\in\mathcal{G}
. This implies that the support of the prior need not contain the support of the GVI posterior, hence assumptions such as those in
[

where we require the prior to place positive weight on a neighbourhood of the expected loss minimiser are

Proposition 5
.

Theorem

\supp(Q_{n})\cap\supp(\Pi)=\emptyset

That is, the prior and the GVI posterior may be mutually singular.
Therefore, we get a stronger result than in classical and generalised Bayesian/Variational Inference that require for the prior to have positive mass on the support of the posterior measure. Since
3
applies, this will typically only be the case when the divergence is a bounded divergence rather then when
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{G}
are restricted. Nevertheless, this allows us to overcome challenges, as discussed in

, where Bayesian posteriors are inconsistent. Hence leading to robustness to severe prior misspecification.

This proposition is significant as it will imply that the prior need not put positive measure on Kullback–Leibler neighbourhoods of the data generating process,
P_{0}

3.4

3.6
Extensions

Throughout, we have assumed that
3
holds, that is the divergence between all possible posteriors,
\mathcal{Q}
, and all priors,
\mathcal{G}
, is bounded. This has allowed us to make significant progress in establishing asymptotic consistency and rates of convergence of GVI posterior measures; under unbounded divergences this is an open problem.

Remark
.

3
can also be satisfied by restricting the set of possible posterior measures,
\mathcal{Q}
, to the sublevel sets of an unbounded divergence to some prior:

\mathcal{Q}|_{M}:=\{\nu\in\mathcal{Q}:D(\nu:\Pi)\leq M\}.

1

\mathcal{Q}|_{M}
and
\mathcal{G}=\{\Pi\}

\mathcal{G}
from

1
being smaller. In fact,
\mathcal{Q}|_{M}
implicitly makes assumptions on the absolute continuity of its members to the prior.

Here, we explore to what extend we may extend our results to unbounded divergences. In particular, we find that we may establish existence and uniqueness without
3
through additional work, and we make some progress on asymptotic consistency by replacing
3
by different assumptions on the divergence. Nevertheless, rates of convergence of GVI posterior measures under unbounded divergences remain an open problem.

3.6.1
Existence and Uniqueness under Unbounded Divergences

As long as there is a single element of
\mathcal{Q}
for which the GVI functional is finite, then the posterior exists. If this further holds for all priors within the subspace
\mathcal{G}
, then it exists regardless of the prior measure.

Proposition 8
.

Make
2
,
1
,
6
and
5
and suppose that
\mathcal{Q}
is closed with respect to weak
⋆
convergence. Then the GVI posterior minimising
Equation

exists.

Moreover, suppose also that the divergence is strictly convex in its first argument and that
\mathcal{Q}
is convex. Then the GVI posterior exists and is unique.

We have now established sufficient conditions for existence and uniqueness of Generalised Variational Inference posteriors under unbounded divergences over arbitrary Polish spaces. Significantly, a GVI posterior will exist as soon as a single measure within
\mathcal{Q}
has finite GVI objective.

3.6.2

and

There is no objective reason why the different inputs into the GVI objective should be fixed with respect to incoming observations. In fact,

Proposition 9

Let

.

3.6.3
Concentration of GVI Posteriors under Arbitrary Divergences

For completeness, we also include a result on asymptotic consistency of infinite dimensional GVI posteriors under unbounded divergences. Unfortunately, this requires restrictive assumptions on the set
\mathcal{Q}

Make

,
5

and

Illustrative Examples

We provide worked examples in support of the theory.

4.1
Existence of Everywhere Bounded Divergences

The prime example of such divergences, that satisfy
3
for unrestricted choices of
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{G}
, is the Total Variation Distance defined as:

D_{TV}(Q:\Pi):=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{T}}\left|QA-\Pi A\right|\leq 1.

(6)

Since
Q
and
\Pi
are probability measures, which are by definition non–negative, the upper bound is achieved for instance in the case when
Q
and
\Pi
are mutually singular measures.

As an immediate consequence, the Hellinger Distance is also bounded above since by a well known result, see for instance
[
29
, Lemma B.5]
,

D_{H}^{2}(Q:\Pi)=\int_{\Theta}\left(\sqrt{\frac{dQ}{d\mu}(\theta)}-\sqrt{\dfrac{d\Pi}{d\mu}(\theta)}\right)^{2}d\mu(d\theta)\leq D_{TV}(Q:\Pi).

(7)

Furthermore, such a divergence can be constructed as part of the class of
f
–divergences
[
1
,
3
]
. We follow
[
3
]
and define these as

D_{f}(Q:\Pi)=\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[f\left(\frac{d\Pi}{dQ}\right)\right]

for some convex function
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\mathbb{R})
, such that
f(1)=0
.
This is a vast class of divergences and contains for instance the KL and the
\chi^{2}
divergences.

Making use of a result in
[
10
]
, we know that for
f
–divergences, there is an upper bound whenever the following limit exists and is finite:

0\leq D_{f}(Q:\Pi)\leq\lim_{u\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left\{f(u)+uf\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)\right\}

(8)

Note that this is independent of any choice of Probability measures
Q
and
\Pi
, and solely reliant on the function
f
.

For instance, we can pick the Le Cam
f
–divergence, which uses

f(u)=\frac{(u-1)^{2}}{u+1}

for which this limit exists and, through basic analysis, is 2.

4.2
Gaussian Location Model

Continuing from
Section

2.3
, we use the example of a Gaussian likelihood, and some Gaussian prior with fixed unit variance but misspecified mean.
For simplicity, we assume that
X_{1}^{n}\overset{iid}{\sim}P_{0}:=\mathcal{N}(\theta_{0},1)
and that the variance is known.
In terms of densities, to estimate this normal location model, we place a Gaussian prior
\pi(\theta)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\pi},1)
over the possible location parameters in
\Theta=\mathbb{R}
, and we assume that the likelihood hypothesis is
p_{\theta}(X)\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,1)
. The standard loss for Bayesian inference is then naturally

L(X_{1}^{n},\,\theta)=-\frac{1}{n}\log p_{\theta}(X_{1}^{n})=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{\theta}(X_{i})

which
P_{0}
–a.s. converges in
n
to its limit,
L(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{0}}[-\log p_{\theta}(X)]
.
The distribution
P^{\star}_{n}
in
\mathcal{Q}
, the set of Gaussian measures, is then given by:

p^{\star}_{n}(\theta)=\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta)}\left[-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{\theta}(X_{i})\right]

which we can evaluate for some
q\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
as

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}{{\int}}q(\theta)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}}{2}\right\}\right]\right)\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\underbrace{{{\int}}q(\theta)\frac{1}{2}\log 2\pi\,d\theta}_{=\frac{1}{2}\log 2\pi}+\frac{1}{n}\int q(\theta)\underbrace{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}}{2}\right)}_{=\frac{{\sum_{i}X_{i}^{2}-2\theta\sum_{i}X_{i}+n\theta^{2}}}{2}}\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log 2\pi+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{X_{i}^{2}}{2n}-\frac{\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{n}+\frac{(\mu^{2}+\sigma^{2})}{2}

which is convex in both
\mu
and
\sigma
, and the minimiser corresponds to the degenerate Gaussian centred at the empirical mean,
\overline{X_{1}^{n}}
. So we let
P^{\star}_{n}(\theta)=\delta_{\overline{X_{1}^{n}}}(\theta)
, and find that

J_{L_{n}}(P_{n}^{\star})=\frac{1}{2}\log 2\pi+\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\overline{X_{1}^{n}}\right)^{2}.

We can then find the set
\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{n}
in which the GVI posterior may lie.

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}L(X_{1}^{n},\,\theta)\left[Q(d\theta)-P_{n}^{\star}(d\theta)\right]\leq\frac{M}{\beta n}

\displaystyle\implies\left({\frac{1}{2}\log 2\pi}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{X_{i}^{2}}{2n}-\frac{\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{n}+\frac{(\mu^{2}+\sigma^{2})}{2}\right)-J_{L_{n}}(P_{n}^{\star})\leq\frac{M}{\beta n}

\displaystyle\implies\left(\frac{1}{2}\overline{X_{1}^{n}}-\mu\right)\overline{X_{1}^{n}}+\frac{\mu^{2}+\sigma^{2}}{2}\leq\frac{M}{\beta n}

(9)

We note that the LHS is strictly non-negative, and by elementary analysis this parabola has at least one real root, therefore:

\displaystyle\mu^{2}-2\mu\overline{X_{1}^{n}}-\frac{2M}{\beta n}+\left(\sigma^{2}+\overline{X_{1}^{n}}^{2}\right)\leq 0

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mu\in\overline{\mathrm{B}\left(\overline{X_{1}^{n}},\;\left(\frac{2M}{\beta n}-\sigma^{2}\right)\right)}

(10)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\in\left[0,\;\mu^{2}-2\mu\overline{X_{1}^{n}}+\overline{X_{1}^{n}}^{2}-\frac{2M}{\beta n}\right]

(11)

where
\overline{\mathrm{B}(x,r)}
denoted the closed ball of radius
r
centred at
x
. Hence, the space of possible GVI posteriors is compact regardless of the prior mean
\mu_{\pi}
, in difference to the Bayesian posterior found in
Section

2.3
.

Furthermore, since the LHS in

9
is non–negative, it is straightforward to see that we converge to the mean of the DGP as

, i.e.
\lim_{n}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^{n}X_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[X]=\theta_{0}
, with parameters
\mu_{\infty}=\theta_{0}
and
\sigma_{\infty}^{2}=0

P_{0}
almost surely.

This happens irrespective of the prior, even in the case where
\pi(\theta)=\delta_{\theta_{\pi}}
and

, or when

4.2.1
A Comparison with Bayesian Inference

To highlight this relevance, we compare the set

From

\displaystyle\sigma_{B}^{2}=\left(\sigma_{\pi}^{-2}+n\right)^{-1},\,

⋆
topology.

Having shown that the sublevel sets of the GVI functional are weak
⋆
compact, and as the functional is lower semi–continuous, we may now by the definition of the infimum, construct a sequence
(\ell_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}
converging downwards to
\ell^{\star}:=\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{Q}}T_{n}(\nu)
defined via the corresponding elements
(\nu_{k})_{k}\subset\mathcal{A}
, i.e.
\ell_{k}:=T_{n}(\nu_{k})
. Since both the divergence and the loss are lower bounded, so is the GVI functional, so
\ell^{\star}>-\infty
. By the claim we find that
(\nu_{k})_{k}
is a tight sequence of probability measures, so after passing to a subsequence (without relabelling) we must have that
\nu_{k}\overset{w^{\star}}{\rightarrow}\nu_{\star}
for some
\nu_{\star}\in\mathcal{Q}
, and for which it holds that

\liminf_{k}T_{n}(\nu_{k})\geq T_{n}(\nu_{\star})

by the lower semi–continuity. As
T_{n}(\nu_{k})\rightarrow\ell^{\star}
,
\liminf_{k}T_{n}(\nu_{k})=\ell^{\star}
, and as
T_{n}(\nu_{\star})\geq\ell^{\star}
, we conclude that
T_{n}(\nu_{\star})=\ell^{\star}
which is a global minimum of the GVI functional, so such a minimiser
\nu_{\star}\in\mathcal{Q}
of
Equation

3
exists.

For the uniqueness part, suppose that there exist two minimisers,
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
. Then, as
\mathcal{Q}
is convex we may pick
a\in(0,1)
and let

\nu:=a\nu_{1}+(1-a)\nu_{2}

(13)

such that

\displaystyle T_{n}(\nu)

\displaystyle=J_{L_{n}}\bigl(a\nu_{1}+(1-a)\nu_{2}\bigr)+D\bigl(a\nu_{1}+(1-a)\nu_{2}:\Pi\bigr)

\displaystyle<aJ_{L_{n}}\bigl(\nu_{1}\bigr)+(1-a)J_{L_{n}}\bigl(\nu_{2}\bigr)+aD(\nu_{1}:\Pi)+(1-a)D(\nu_{2}:\Pi)

\displaystyle=aT_{n}(\nu_{1})+(1-a)T_{n}(\nu_{2})

where the inequality follows by the convexity of the divergence.
But as
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
are both minimisers
T_{n}(\nu_{1})=T_{n}(\nu_{2})
so
\nu
would further lower the GVI objective, so these cannot be minimisers. Hence, we conclude that the only way
Equation

13
may hold is if
\nu_{1}=\nu_{2}
(in the sense of measures), and the minimiser may not be written as a convex combination of elements of
\mathcal{Q}
so this minimiser is an extreme point, and unique.
∎

5.6.2
Relaxation of
M
and
\beta

The result is immediate from the corresponding proofs of
Theorems

6
and
7
.
∎

5.6.3
Concentration of GVI Posterior Measures under Unbounded Divergences

By
Proposition

8
,
Q_{n}
exists under unbounded divergences. Then, for any such sequence
(\nu_{n})\subset\mathcal{Q}
it follows by assumption that
P_{0}
almost surely

\frac{1}{n}T_{n}(\nu_{n})=J_{L_{n}}(\nu_{n})+(n\beta)^{-1}D(\nu_{n}:\Pi)\rightarrow L(\Theta)\,.

Pick any such set
A\in\mathcal{T}
for which
L(A)>L(\Theta)
. It follows that
P_{0}
–a.s.

\frac{1}{n}T_{n}(\nu_{n})\geq\frac{1}{n}T_{n}(Q_{n})\geq J_{L_{n}}(Q_{n})=\int_{A}L_{n}(\theta)Q_{n}(d\theta)+\int_{A^{c}}L_{n}(\theta)Q_{n}(d\theta)\geq L_{n}(\Theta)\,.

Note also that

\int_{A}L_{n}(\theta)Q_{n}(d\theta)+\int_{A^{c}}L_{n}(\theta)Q_{n}(d\theta)\geq Q_{n}A\>L_{n}(A)+Q_{n}A^{c}\,L_{n}(A^{c})\geq L_{n}(\Theta)\,.

By the lower semi–continuity of
L_{n}
we have that
\exists\theta\in A^{c}
for which
L(\theta)=L(\Theta)
and therefore
L_{n}(A^{c})\rightarrow L(\Theta)

P_{0}
–a.s. Therefore,

Q_{n}A\>L_{n}(A)+Q_{n}A^{c}\,L_{n}(A^{c})\geq Q_{n}A\>L_{n}(A)+Q_{n}A^{c}\,L_{n}(\Theta)\geq L_{n}(\Theta)

Taking limits, it follows
P_{0}
almost surely that
J_{L_{n}}(Q_{n})\rightarrow L(\Theta)
, and as
\liminf_{n}L_{n}(A)>L(\Theta)

P_{0}
–a.s. by assumption, it must follow that
Q_{n}A\rightarrow 0
with
P_{0}
probability 1.
∎For unbounded divergences we will generally not have that
J_{L_{n}}(\nu_{n})=L_{n}(\Theta)
at any finite
n
as any atomic measure will have infinite divergence to any non–atomic prior. Therefore, we are unable to employ the same methodology in proving rates of convergence for GVI posterior measures under unbounded divergences as in the proof of
Theorem

7
.

6
Concluding Remarks

In this paper we have examined the asymptotic behaviour of Generalised Variational Inference measures on infinite dimensional hypothesis spaces with particular focus on prior misspecification. We have established frequentist consistency and rates of convergence to minimisers of
L(\theta)
when using bounded divergences allowing for consistency regardless of the prior measure
\Pi\in\mathcal{G}
; see
Figure

1
. In fact, supposing that the divergence
D
is everywhere bounded enables the choice
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
hence including priors that are severely misspecified and result in inconsistent Bayesian posteriors.

It is worth noting that these results have further impact to Federated GVI
[
27
]
, the study of GVI posteriors over a network of clients with private, local data sets. In particular such
FedGVI
posteriors will exist under the assumptions presented here through the different choices of loss functions and priors. Moreover, a global prior of some uniformed server, given a bounded divergence, may not skew the global posterior after incorporating local data towards unfavourable hypotheses that do not agree.

Finally, while we were able to derive existence and uniqueness of GVI posteriors under unbounded divergences, rates of convergence of GVI posterior measures under unbounded divergences remains an open challenge.

A
Supplementary Definitions

As we expect this paper to be of interest also for researchers who are not as familiar with the intricacies of measure theory we recall some basic facts and definitions used throughout the paper without explicit statements.

We have used several modes of convergence of measures throughout the paper, we summarise these below.

Definition 2
.

Let
(\mu_{n})_{n}
and
\mu
be Borel probability measures on the measurable space
(\Theta,\mathcal{T})
. We have the following notions of convergence.

1.

If
\int_{\Theta}fd\mu_{n}\rightarrow\int_{\Theta}fd\mu

\forall f\in C_{b}(\Theta)
, then we say
\mu_{n}\overset{w^{\star}}{\rightarrow}\mu
, i.e. in the weak
⋆
topology.

2.

If
\mu_{n}A-\mu A\rightarrow 0

\forall A\in\mathcal{T}
, then
\mu_{n}\overset{sw}{\rightarrow}\mu
, i.e. setwise.

3.

If
||\mu_{n}-\mu||_{TV}\rightarrow 0
, then
\mu_{n}\overset{TV}{\rightarrow}\mu
, i.e. in total variation.

Here
C_{b}(\Theta)
denotes the space of bounded, continuous functions mapping
\Theta
to
\mathbb{R}
.
Note, convergence in total variation implies setwise convergence, and setwise convergence in turn implies weak
⋆
convergence, however the converse do not hold in general.

Definition 3
.

A function
f:X\rightarrow Y
between two topological spaces
(X,\mathcal{B})
and
(Y,\mathcal{C})
is called coercive if for every compact
K_{Y}\subset Y
there exists a compact set
K_{X}\subset X
such that the elements of
K_{X}^{c}
map exclusively outside
K_{Y}
, that is

f:X\backslash K_{X}\rightarrow Y\backslash K_{Y}.

Definition 4
.

We say that
\mathcal{Q}
‘allows for Dirac measures’ if (i)
\mathcal{Q}
is closed with respect to weak
⋆
convergence of probability measures, and (ii) if for all coercive, lower bounded and lower semi–continuous functions
f:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
it holds that

\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{Q}}\;J_{f}(\nu)=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\;f(\theta)\,.

Note that this is trivially achieved if all Dirac delta measures are included within
\mathcal{Q}
, but may equivalently be achieved for instance through the set of Gaussian measures by allowing for measures with arbitrary mean and zero variance
[
29
]
.
The most straightforward definition of such Gaussian measures on infinite dimensional spaces is the one on separable Hilbert spaces
(\mathcal{H},(\cdot,\cdot),||\cdot||)
. These measures are uniquely characterised by their mean
m\in\mathcal{H}
and covariance operator
C:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
. So we say a measure
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{H})
is Gaussian if it satisfies

m:=\int_{\mathcal{H}}\theta\nu(d\theta)\,,\quad\mathrm{and}\quad(\theta_{1},C\theta_{2})=\int_{\mathcal{H}}(\theta,\theta_{1})(\theta,\theta_{2})\nu(d\theta)\quad\forall\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{H}

where the first is a Bochner integral (see
[
29
]
).

B
Classical Robustness to Prior Misspecification

We briefly discuss the relation of our work to robustness to prior misspecification in the sense of Bayesian sensitivity analysis
[
6
,
17
]
.
This line of work has examined the stability of Bayesian posteriors under small perturbations of the prior with respect to some other measure, for instance through additive Huber contamination as mentioned in
Section

2.2
.
In particular, this has been used to select a robust prior for Bayes which leads to optimal decision making under potential perturbations of the prior measure. In this paper however, we aim to answer the reverse: under what conditions can GVI lead to optimal decision making under ill chosen priors.

Local sensitivity of posteriors generated with
\varepsilon
–contaminated priors, for instance by the additive Huber contamination model
\mathcal{G}=\{\Pi_{\varepsilon}:=(1-\varepsilon)\Pi_{0}+\varepsilon G:\varepsilon\in(0,1)\}
, is often studied
[
17
]
through the Gâteux differential

s(\Pi_{0},G;X_{1}^{n})=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\cfrac{d(Q^{n}_{0}:Q^{n}_{\varepsilon})}{d(\Pi_{0}:\Pi_{\varepsilon})}

where
d(\cdot:\cdot)
is some distance or divergence measure; Gustafson and Wasserman
[
17
]
for instance use the total variation distance.
Here
\Pi_{0}
is the uncontaminated prior,
\Pi_{\varepsilon}
the
\varepsilon
–contaminated prior in the direction of
G
, and
Q^{n}_{0}
and
Q^{n}_{\varepsilon}
the respective Bayes posteriors.
The unrestricted choice of a divergence other than the Kullback–Leibler in GVI generally prohibits explicit expressions of
Q^{n}_{0}
and
Q^{n}_{\varepsilon}
that demonstrate the the exact dependence on the prior, which makes this approach impracticable for our purposes.

This presents an interesting theoretical question to the stability of Bayesian posteriors with respect to prior misspecification and suggests fascinating future directions for understanding uncertainty about the hypotheses in GVI given finite observations.
The work in
[
6
,
17
]
is concerned with small perturbations in the prior within a restricted class of measures
\mathcal{G}
. Often, restrictions are placed on this set in terms of how much the prior may vary; in
[
12
]
the priors considered are those close to the original prior
\Pi_{0}
as
\varepsilon
tends to zero. The present paper however is concerned with severe prior misspecification, even cases where we allow
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
, as well as the asymptotic dynamics of GVI posterior measures under such misspecification.

C
Non–Robustness in Bayesian Inference

Continuing the illustrative example from
Sections

2.3
and
4.2
, with
X_{1}^{n}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma_{p}^{2})
, we get the GVI objective. Using
\beta=1
and loss
L(X_{1}^{n},\,\theta)=-\frac{1}{n}\log p_{\theta}(X_{1}^{n})
,
\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta)}[nL(X_{1}^{n},\,\theta)]
is

\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{q},\sigma_{q}^{2})}[-\log p_{\theta}(X_{1}^{n})]=\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta)}\left[-\log{(2\pi\sigma_{p}^{2})^{-\frac{n}{2}}}\exp\left\{-{\sum_{i=1}^{n}}\frac{(\theta-X_{i})^{2}}{2\sigma_{p}^{2}}\right\}\right]

=\frac{n}{2}\log 2\pi\sigma_{p}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\sigma_{p}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mu_{q}-X_{i})^{2}+\frac{n\sigma_{q}^{2}}{2\sigma_{p}^{2}}

Therefore, we have a closed form for
R(D_{KL},-\frac{1}{n}\log p_{\theta},q)
as

\frac{n}{2}\log 2\pi\sigma_{p}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\sigma_{p}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mu_{q}-X_{i})^{2}+\frac{n\sigma_{q}^{2}}{2\sigma_{p}^{2}}+\log\frac{\sigma_{\pi}}{\sigma_{q}}+\frac{(\mu_{q}-\mu_{\pi})^{2}+\sigma_{q}^{2}}{2\sigma_{\pi}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}

which is convex in both
\mu_{q}
and
\sigma_{q}
. The minimisers of this objective are hence

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu_{q}}R(D_{KL},-\frac{1}{n}\log p_{\theta},q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{p}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mu_{q}-X_{i})+\frac{\mu_{q}-\mu_{\pi}}{\sigma_{\pi}^{2}}=0

\displaystyle\nabla_{\sigma_{q}}R(D_{KL},-\frac{1}{n}\log p_{\theta},q)

\displaystyle=\frac{n\sigma_{q}}{\sigma_{p}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{q}}+\frac{\sigma_{q}}{\sigma_{\pi}^{2}}=0

Solving for
\sigma_{q}
and
\mu_{q}
gives the well known solution

\displaystyle\sigma_{q}^{2}=\left(\sigma_{\pi}^{-2}+n\sigma_{p}^{-2}\right)^{-1},\,

\displaystyle\mu_{q}=\cfrac{\left(\frac{\mu_{\pi}}{\sigma_{\pi}^{2}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{X_{i}}{\sigma_{p}^{2}}\right)}{\left(\sigma_{\pi}^{-2}+n\sigma_{p}^{-2}\right)}

which shows that, for a finite
\sigma_{\pi}^{2}
,
\mu_{\pi}
by itself can cause arbitrary misspecification.
