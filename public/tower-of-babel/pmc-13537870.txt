Number Needed to Test in Diagnostic Comparative Effectiveness Research: Implications for Evaluating the Utility and Value of Artificial Intelligence
Authors: Seong Ho Park, Kyunghwa Han
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

The decision to adopt a new diagnostic method, including artificial intelligence (AI) systems, is typically based on results from comparative effectiveness research (CER) comparing the new diagnostic method (index) with an existing method (comparator). In therapeutic CER, the ‘Number Needed to Treat’ is a widely used metric that indicates how many patients must receive a new therapy—compared with a control—for one additional patient to achieve a beneficial outcome (referred to as the ‘Number Needed to Treat for Benefit’) or to experience a specific adverse event (referred to as the ‘Number Needed to Treat for Harm’) [1]. In contrast, diagnostic CER typically relies on measures of test accuracy/performance, such as sensitivity, specificity, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [23]. Although analogous metrics exist in diagnostic CER—particularly in the context of screening—they have been less frequently used in research. However, such metrics may be particularly valuable for informing policy decisions regarding the clinical adoption of diagnostic methods, including AI tools, for which accuracy/performance-based measures alone are often insufficient. In this article, we explain ‘Number Needed to Test for Benefit’ (NNT-B) and ‘Number Needed to Test for Harm’ (NNT-H) in diagnostic CER and extend these concepts by presenting a combined metric, ‘Number Needed to Test for Net Benefit’ (NNT-NB). To illustrate these concepts, we use a hypothetical example comparing human assisted by AI (HAI) as the index test with human alone (H) as the comparator for interpreting mammography in breast cancer screening (Fig. 1, Table 1).

DIAGNOSTIC YIELD AND FALSE-POSITIVE YIELD

NNT-B, NNT-H, and NNT-NB are calculated using diagnostic yield (DY) and false-positive yield (FPY). DY and FPY are defined as the proportions of patients correctly identified as having the target condition (i.e., the number of true-positive diagnoses divided by all tested; corresponding to the cancer detection rate [CDR] in the example shown in Table 1) and incorrectly indicated as having the target condition (i.e., the number of false-positive calls divided by all tested), respectively [3]. FPY is also referred to as ‘false referral rate’ [34].

These metrics provide a more explicit representation of a test’s clinical usefulness than sensitivity and specificity alone because they incorporate disease prevalence, thereby reflecting the absolute number of affected patients. For example, DY may remain low in settings with low disease prevalence despite high sensitivity. These relationships are expressed as follows:

DY = prevalence × sensitivity

FPY = (1 - prevalence) × (1 - specificity)

DY and FPY are meaningful only when the population (i.e., “all tested”) is clearly defined within an appropriate clinical context. Accordingly, these metrics are applicable to diagnostic cohort samples, in which disease prevalence and spectrum are representative, but not to case-control or convenience samples [356].

NNT-B, NNT-H, AND NNT-NB

NNT-B is the number of patients who must undergo the index test, instead of the comparator, for one additional patient to receive a true-positive diagnosis. The formula is:

NNT-B = 1 ÷ (DYindex - DYcomparator)

In the example (Table 1), the incremental CDR is 0.5 per 1,000 (0.05%), indicating one additional cancer detected for every 2,000 women evaluated. In other words, 2,000 women must be screened using the AI-assisted strategy to detect one additional breast cancer compared with the standard strategy, yielding an NNT-B of 2,000. The NNT-B formula is a mathematical expression of this straightforward reasoning.

NNT-H is the number of patients who must undergo the index test for one additional patient to experience a false-positive result compared with the comparator. The formula is:

NNT-H = 1 ÷ (FPYindex - FPYcomparator)

In the example (Table 1), the incremental FPY is 1%, indicating one additional false-positive result for every 100 women evaluated. In other words, when 100 women undergo screening with the AI-assisted strategy, one additional woman experiences a false-positive result compared with standard reading, yielding an NNT-H of 100.

NNT-NB is a metric that combines NNT-B and NNT-H and represents the number of patients who must undergo testing with the index method to achieve one additional patient experiencing net benefit compared with the comparator. The formula is:

NNT-NB = 1 ÷ [(DYindex - DYcomparator) - w × (FPYindex - FPYcomparator)]

Here, w is a weighting factor that reflects the relative clinical importance of the benefit of a true-positive diagnosis versus the harm of a false-positive result. When expressed using sensitivity, specificity, and prevalence, NNT-NB can be written as:

NNT-NB = 1 ÷ [prevalence × Δsensitivity + w × (1 - prevalence) × Δspecificity]

Where Δ = index - comparator. The denominator corresponds to the incremental net benefit derived from the net benefit framework used in decision curve analysis, indicating that NNT-NB directly parallels the net benefit calculation [78]. Taking the reciprocal yields the number of patients who must be tested to achieve one additional patient experiencing net benefit. NNT-NB is conceptually related to the ‘Number Needed to Treat for Net Effect’ proposed by Li et al. [9] for therapeutic CER.

A key challenge is determining w. This parameter depends on the clinical context and on how benefit and harm are defined. In diagnostic settings, harms associated with false-positive results—such as patient anxiety, additional work-up, and unnecessary biopsy—are often perceived, sometimes prematurely, as less consequential, because they may be mitigated through downstream evaluations and clinical safeguards. In contrast, harms from therapeutic interventions are typically more immediate and less reversible once treatment is administered. Conversely, the value of a true-positive diagnosis is often emphasized, particularly for serious conditions such as cancer, although the ultimate benefit (e.g., improved disease-specific survival) may be uncertain. In our example (Table 1), if w is arbitrarily set to 0.01, NNT-NB becomes 2,500, indicating that 2,500 women must be screened with the AI-assisted strategy to achieve one additional patient with net benefit.

In diagnostic research, similar concepts have been described using varying terminology, and in some cases, different concepts have been expressed using similar terms [1011], potentially leading to confusion. This contrasts with the more standardized term use in therapeutic CER. We have adopted terminology aligned with reporting guidelines for diagnostic research and the methodological frameworks we reference [781213], while maintaining consistency with the “Number Needed to” metrics used in therapeutic CER.

STATISTICAL ESTIMATION OF 95% CONFIDENCE INTERVAL

Statistical inference for these metrics is an evolving area. A comprehensive discussion is beyond the scope of this article and would warrant a separate methodological review. Here, we briefly outline an approach for estimating the 95% confidence interval (CI) of NNT-NB based on the method described by Marsh et al. [14].

Let, NB = ΔDY - w × ΔFPY. A 95% CI for NB can first be estimated, and the 95% CI for NNT-NB may then be obtained by reciprocating the confidence limits of NB:

95% CI for NB = NB ± 1.96 × variance of NB

The precise calculation of the variance of NB should account for the correlation between two diagnostic methods compared. However, for illustrative purposes and to simplify the calculation, we assume that the index method and comparator are independent (i.e., the covariance term is set to zero). Under this assumption, the variance of NB is calculated as follows:

Variance of NB = 1n[{DYindex + w2 × FPYindex - (NBindex)2} + {DYcomparator + w2 × FPYcomparator - (NBcomparator)2}]

In our example, where n = 10,000, DYHAI = 0.5%, DYH = 0.45%, FPYHAI = 6%, FPYH = 5%, and w = 0.01,

NB = 0.0005 - 0.01 × 0.01 = 0.0004,

Variance of NB = 110,000 × [{0.005 + 0.012 × 0.06 - (0.005 - 0.01 × 0.06)2} + {0.0045 + 0.012 × 0.05 - (0.0045 - 0.01 × 0.05)2}] ≈ 9 × 10-7,

95% CIs for NB = 0.0004 ± 1.96 × 9 × 10-7 ≈(-0.0015, 0.0023),

95% CI for NNT-NB = (1-0.0015, 10.0023) ≈ (-667, 435).

Our example suggests that the true NNT-NB could be as low as 435. Unlike this example, when the 95% CI does not cross zero, interpretation is straightforward: the lower and upper limits of NNT-NB represent the smallest and largest numbers of patients who would need to undergo testing for one additional patient to experience net benefit. In the hypothetical situation where the lower limit of the 95% CI for NB equals zero, indicating that the possibility of no net benefit cannot be excluded, the upper limit of NNT-NB becomes infinite. Furthermore, as in our example, when the lower limit of the 95% CI extends below zero, the possibility of net harm cannot be excluded. In such cases, following the interpretation proposed by Li et al. [9], the negative value (-667 in our example) may be interpreted as the number of patients who would need to undergo testing for one additional patient to experience net harm.

Further Extension

Although the mathematical derivation is based on the conventional diagnostic 2 by 2 table through DY and FPY, this framework can be extended to any pair of clinically relevant benefit and harm endpoints, provided that these are expressed as absolute rates in the target population. If the incremental benefit and harm of an AI-assisted practice compared with conventional practice are denoted as X and Y, respectively, NNT-NB can be calculated as:

NNT-NB = 1 ÷ (X - w × Y)

For instance, if X = 0.001 (1 in 1,000 patients) and Y = 0.01 (1 in 100 patients), NNT-NB is 1 ÷ (0.001 - 0.01 × 0.01) = 1,111 when w = 0.01.

CONCLUSION

NNT-B, NNT-H, and NNT-NB complement conventional measures of diagnostic accuracy by expressing the clinical consequences of adopting a diagnostic strategy in intuitive and clinically meaningful terms. For example, stating “2,500 women would need to undergo AI-assisted screening for one additional woman to experience net benefit” may provide a more tangible basis for adoption decision-making than relying solely on differences in sensitivity and specificity.
