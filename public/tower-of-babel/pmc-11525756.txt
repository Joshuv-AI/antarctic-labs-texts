Dural manipulation coinciding with transient asystole during lumbar spine surgery: illustrative case
Authors: Ever A Hernandez, Jonathan H Sherman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDHemodynamic disturbances during lumbar spine surgeries have proven to be exceptionally rare, with only seven published cases demonstrating instances where intraoperative dural manipulation coincided with bradycardia, hypotension, and/or asystole.OBSERVATIONSThe authors present the case of a 39-year-old male with a history of chronic low-back pain and leg weakness. Imaging demonstrated a disc herniation at L5–S1, resulting in impingement of the left S1 traversing nerve root. The patient agreed to surgery. The preoperative workup was unremarkable. While retracting the S1 nerve root, transient asystole occurred when the S1 nerve root was manipulated, causing the patient’s heart rate to drop from 51 to 0 bpm. Manipulating the nerve root once again triggered a similar event, and upon cessation of the surgical stimulus, the patient’s heart rate spontaneously returned to baseline. Other potential causes of these hemodynamic perturbations were excluded.LESSONSAs in other reported cases, each cardiovascular disturbance corresponded with either direct or indirect dural manipulation. Given the limited number of lumbar spinal cases reported to have undergone hemodynamic perturbances via dural manipulation, this case report provides evidence for the likelihood of a spinal-cardiac reflex between the cardiovascular system and the spinal dura mater. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24158 Keywords: lumbar spine surgery, asystole, bradycardia, hypotension, negative chronotropy, spinal-cardiac reflexABBREVIATIONS: MRI = magnetic resonance imaging

Acute cardiovascular changes during spine procedures involving the cervical and thoracic segments are commonly reported.1, 2 Examples include the Cushing and trigeminocardiac reflexes.3 The Cushing reflex describes a physiological response to acute increases in intracranial pressure by inducing bradycardia, widening pulse pressure, and causing irregular respirations to overcome the decrease in O2 delivery due to the increase in intracranial pressure and relieving ischemia.4 The trigeminocardiac reflex describes a physiological response to stimulation of the sensory branches of the trigeminal nerve, causing hemodynamic irregularities such as bradycardia and hypotension.5, 6 However, hemodynamic fluctuations, such as bradycardia and hypotension, involving lumbar spine surgery are scarce in the literature, excluding causes such as severe blood loss, autonomic dysreflexia, spinal shock, venous air embolism, and traumatic spinal cord injury, with only a total of seven cases reported as of 2024.7 Of those seven cases, only one has reported a transient episode of asystole during an L4–5 microscopic discectomy.8 The remaining cases reported transient episodes of bradycardia and hypotension. Ultimately, each case completed the entirety of the procedure, and no postoperative neurological deficits were noted. With the rarity of hemodynamic perturbations during lumbar spine surgeries, this could suggest a potential spinal-cardiac reflex in the lumbar/sacral spinal cord with a mechanism similar to that of the trigeminocardiac and/or vasovagal reflex.5 Wepresent a case report demonstrating adverse hemodynamic perturbations during lumbar spine surgery, specifically with dural manipulation of the S1 nerve root, leading to transient episodes of bradycardia, asystole, and hypotension.

Illustrative Case

Presentation

A 39-year-old male, 1.95 m tall and weighing 132 kg, with a past medical history of diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis and bilateral femoroacetabular impingement syndrome, presented to the neurosurgical clinic with a chief complaint of chronic low-back pain that radiated to his left posterior leg and right buttock. The pain had begun approximately 4 to 5 years earlier and had persisted, progressively impacting his daily life and overall well-being with functional limitations. The patient described the pain as constant, burning, shooting, and sharp in the leg and foot. He also reported numbness and tingling in the posterior aspect of his left leg, extending to the sole of his foot. Sensory symptoms were accompanied by left leg weakness. He had been taking meloxicam and gabapentin to manage the pain. The patient denied any issues related to bowel or bladder function. He reported no current or previous cardiovascular issue but mentioned a potential family history of heart disease in his grandmother. Additionally, a thorough chart review demonstrated no prior or current history of cardiovascular and pulmonary disease. The patient reported a history of tobacco smoking, but had quit over 20 years ago, and occasional alcohol intake.

On physical examination, the patient reported pain associated with flexion of the lumbar spine, along with a decreased range of flexion/ extension of the spine. The Kemp test was positive in addition to a positive 45° left straight leg raise test. Motor examination demonstrated a full range of motion, normal bulk and tone, and 5/5 strength for all extremities. On sensory examination, there was decreased sensation to light touch and pinprick on the left antero-/posterolateral thigh, left calf, and circumferential left foot. Deep tendon reflexes were graded 2/4 bilaterally for both upper and lower extremities, except for the left patellar and Achilles tendons, which were graded 0/4.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) 2 months prior to his visit showed evidence of a left subarticular disc protrusion at the L5–S1 level, resulting in narrowing of the spinal canal and impingement of the left S1 traversing nerve root (Fig. 1). Furthermore, an electromyogram indicated the presence of long-standing left S1 radiculopathy. With symptoms and workup indicative of left S1 radiculopathy and the ineffectiveness of conservative treatments such as medications, physical therapy, and pain injections, the decision was made to proceed with a left L5–S1 minimally invasive hemilaminectomy and discectomy. Preoperative assessments for surgery, including anesthesia laboratory tests and cardiology workup, such as blood work and electrocardiogram, respectively, were all found to be normal.

Surgical Intervention

The patient was positioned supine on the operative table, administered general anesthesia, and intubated using midazolam 2 mg, propofol 300 mg, and rocuronium 50 mg—an uneventful process. Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane and esevoflurane. Subsequently, the patient was repositioned prone. Fluoroscopy imaging was used to confirm the midline alignment and verify the L5–S1 level. Standard surgical preparation and draping were then carried out. An injection of approximately 5 mL of 1% lidocaine with epinephrine was administered into the dermal layer, shortly followed by a 2.5-cm incision. Electrocautery was then utilized to address residual muscular tissue over the L5 lamina. A high-speed pneumatic drill was engaged to create a sufficient hemilaminectomy of the L5 lamina. A standard wide bony opening to the facet joint was made, providing sufficient exposure of the necessary anatomical structures with no indication to increase the bony opening beyond what is considered normal. Epidural fat and the left S1 nerve root were identified.

While gently dissecting and retracting the left S1 nerve root, a transient episode of asystole occurred when the nerve was surgically manipulated, causing the patient’s heart rate to drop from 51 to 0 bpm for a few seconds. At this time, minimal dural retraction was applied to the nerve root to adjust the positioning of the nerve without excessive force. The surgeon was notified, prompting the release of the S1 nerve. The patient’s blood pressure was noted to be 81/61 mm Hg before and duringthe episode. Within 20 seconds of releasing the S1 nerve root, the patient’s heart rate returned to sinus bradycardia, fluctuating between 37 and 40 bpm, after surgical manipulation stopped. Figure 2 demonstrates blood pressure and heart rate values during this episode. Glycopyrrolate 0.2 mg and ephedrine 10 mg were administered because of suspicion of a potential vasovagal response. As the surgeon continued with the procedure, manipulating the S1 nerve root with even less retraction once again triggered a similar event, which prompted the surgeon to release the nerve. The patient’s heart rate spontaneously returned to baseline, and he recovered without additional medication. During these episodes, no changes in blood pressure, oxygen saturation, or end-tidal CO2 were noted; however, the patient was hypotensive. At this point, it was deemed unsafe to proceed with the surgery due to the direct correlation between dural manipulation and asystole. Discectomy was initially planned but was foregone because of hemodynamic perturbations, and only foraminotomy took place as the nerve appeared to be adequately decompressed. Ultimately, the case was aborted due to these intraoperative complications. The surgical incision was closed in the standard fashion, and the patient was repositioned supine, extubated, and transferred to the postanesthesia care unit in stable condition.

Postoperative Course and Outcome

The patient tolerated the procedure well and was admitted overnight for 24-hour monitoring, with a cardiology consult placed. The patient remained asymptomatic following the surgery, with 5/5 strength in both upper and lower extremities, and no further episodes of asystole or bradycardia occurred during recovery. The cardiology team determined that there were no indications of chest pain, shortness of breath, lower-extremity swelling, or palpitations after a careful examination and cleared the patient from a cardiological standpoint. The 24-hour monitoring was uneventful, and the patient was discharged the following day with no neurological deficits. At the 2-month follow-up, the patient’s left leg pain had mostly resolved.

Discussion

Observations

Only 7 cases demonstrating hemodynamic perturbations during lumbar spine surgeries have been reported, where dural manipulation coincided with bradycardia, asystole, and/or hypotension.7 This could suggest a similar, but unique, physiological response termed the “spinal-cardiac reflex” that presents similarly to the trigeminocardiac reflex, where stimulation of the sensory branches of the trigeminal nerve causes bradycardia and hypotension.5, 6 In comparison, many physiological reflexes are common during cranial, cervical, and thoracic spine procedures, leading to cardiovascular changes, such as the Cushing and trigeminocardiac reflexes.1, 2 However, this is not the case for lumbar spine surgeries when excluding causes such as significant blood loss, prone positioning, autonomic dysreflexia, shock, electrolyte imbalances, and anesthetics that can lead to intraoperative hemodynamic changes.9 These potential causes were ruled out in the case presented, as the patient had been placed in the standard prone position under general anesthesia, without significant blood loss taking place during the procedure. No anomalies were noted intraoperatively that could have led to these hemodynamic perturbations.

The hemodynamic perturbations that took place in our case were similar to those of the reported cases during lumbar spine surgery. Of the 7 cases reported in the literature, only 1 demonstrated an episode of asystole during an L4–5 microscopic discectomy, in which atropine and ephedrine were administered, followed by chest compressions.8 However, our case demonstrated asystole coinciding with manipulation of the S1 nerve root, which has not been reported in the literature. For all reported cases, other potential causes for hemodynamic perturbations were excluded, demonstrating a cause-and-effect relationship, as bradycardia, asystole, and/or hypotension subsequently occurred with the surgical stimulus.5, 7–12

Chowdhury and Schaller established criteria for a cause-and-effect relationship for the spinal-cardiac reflex based on the criteria used for establishing the trigeminocardiac reflex.5, 13 These include two major criteria, plausibility and reversibility, and two minor criteria, repetition and prevention. These criteria were met in the case presented: the onset of asystole occurred promptly after the surgical stimulus was applied, stimulus cessation abolished the response and cardiovascular parameters returned to baseline, reapplication of the surgical stimulus resulted in similar hemodynamic changes, and administration of medications blocked the recurrence of the response, respectively. In this otherwise healthy patient, intermittent episodes of asystole observed during surgery, while rare, are thought to possibly occur because of an enhanced parasympathetic reaction, possibly linked to the spinal-cardiac reflex.5, 9

The relationship in time that correlates with traction on the dura and episodes of asystole is likely due to afferent parasympathetic activation.11 In other words, each episode of asystole coincided with dural manipulation. It has been postulated that mechanical stretch or traction of the spinal dura mater, containing both extrinsic and intrinsic innervation pathways, leads to afferent parasympathetic activation and sympathetic inhibition that results in negative chronotropy similar to a reflex celiac (vasovagal) reaction.5, 6, 8, 10, 14 This most likely suggests neurogenic control causing negative chronotropic changes occurring with either direct or indirect dural manipulation, which was demonstrated in all reported cases.5, 7 It is important to note that while this case and those reported in the literature demonstrated occurrences where dural manipulation preceded these cardiovascular disturbances, no causal relationship based on empirical evidence or mechanism has been established.

Chowdhury and Schaller performed a systematic review to describe the spinal-cardiac reflex during lumbar spine surgeries where negative chronotropic effects took place.5 The most recent reported case described a sudden onset of bradycardia that lasted for about 30 seconds after the left L4 nerve root was retracted, in which atropine was subsequently administered.7 As with all other cases, anticholinergics and/or sympathomimetic drugs were administered after transient hemodynamic perturbations occurred, except in the case of Mahajan et al., where withdrawal of the surgical stimulus was sufficient to revert the cardiovascular changes.10

A possible explanation for why only a few cases have been reported with similar hemodynamic perturbations during lumbar spine surgeries could be the lack of invasive hemodynamic monitoring, with transient episodes of cardiovascular changes going undetected based on the intervals that measure blood pressure variation.5 Additionally, the reason not every dural manipulation activates this response may be because the surgical stimulus is not applying enough dural traction to reach a certain threshold that elicits the response.7 While a consistent pattern across a broader population is not necessarily established at this time, this does not exclude the possibility that a neural pathway may be responsible for these perturbations, which could later be determined with further research.

The spinal-cardiac reflex is not completely understood, but there must be an underlying lumbar spine–brainstem-heart loop that results in these cardiovascular changes.10 For this reason, these transient hemodynamic perturbations, which could become life-threatening, might require medications such as anticholinergics, sympathomimetics, and vasopressors to reestablish baseline parameters and further monitoring.11, 12

Lessons

Dural manipulation with a surgical stimulus during lumbar spine surgeries can potentially cause transient hemodynamic perturbations such as bradycardia, hypotension, and/or asystole, excluding all other causes, secondary to the activation of a spinal-cardiac reflex. The present case demonstrated that these hemodynamic perturbations can occur in the healthiest of young patients with no history of cardiopulmonary disease and may require pharmacological intervention and stopping the procedure. For these reasons, anesthesiologists should carefully monitor cardiovascular parameters and intervene, if necessary, with medications such as anticholinergics, sympathomimetics, and vasopressors during these acute episodes, rule out any other causes, and caution the surgeon of any sudden cardiovascular changes that coincide with dural manipulation and withhold the surgical stimulus. Observation and monitoring postoperatively should be considered for further workup to exclude other potential causes for these transient episodes. It should be noted that with the small number of cases reported in the literature, the hemodynamic conclusions that were drawn, within logical reasoning, were based on an analysis of similar presentations and findings from prior reported cases. Further research should be conducted to establish the underlying pathway(s) of the spinal-cardiac reflex and neuronal connections associated with these cardiovascular changes.
