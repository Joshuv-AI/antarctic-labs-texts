A lightweight LSTM-based open-set RF fingerprinting identification for edge deployment
Authors: Yuxuan Hu, Yutong Fu, Ye Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the rapid advancement of deep learning techniques, numerous neural networks have been successfully developed for radio frequency (RF) fingerprinting identification. In this work, we propose a lightweight yet reliable neural network framework featuring a 9-layer architecture based on the long short-term memory (LSTM) strategy, designed for efficient open-set fingerprinting identification. The simulated beacon frames model real-world propagation effects by incorporating random modulation, power amplifier nonlinearity, multi-path fading, inherent radio noise, and additive channel noise. We extensively evaluate the identification accuracy and efficiency of our LSTM network identification against well-known deep learning models such as ResNet (144 layers) and GoogleNet (177 layers). The evaluation covers a wide range of parameters, including transmitter variability (s), number of transmitters (N), frames per transmitter () and signal-to-noise ratio (). Our results show that the LSTM network maintains an accuracy of more than in with , even with up to transmitters. At lower values (, dB), our LSTM network outperforms GoogleNet and matches ResNet in accuracy. Furthermore, it achieves a training acceleration of up to for and , with inference times under 2 seconds. Meanwhile here, the usage of VRAM is reduced by up to , and the model disk size is under 1 MB. Experiments on devices, including a high performance computing (HPC) node, a personal computer (PC), and three smartphones, demonstrate that the optimal strategy depends on the scale of the problem: local processing for , remote training with local inference for , and full remote processing for .Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Information theory and computation

Introduction

With the advent of the Internet of Things (IoT), the dramatic proliferation of wireless devices, such as cell phones, wireless audio and video transmitters, motion detectors, WLAN, vehicles and aircrafts, has brought great convenience to users around the world1,2. However, due to the broadcast nature of wireless transmissions, many radio frequency (RF) devices today are unsecured and therefore vulnerable to malicious attacks and false impersonations1,3. For example, long-term evolution (LTE) systems have been reported to have been exposed to physical threats including RF jamming, spoofing, and sniffing due to the loophole of the protocol standard defined by 3GPP4,5. Wi-Fi handshakes are vulnerable to key reinstallation attacks, which exploit design or implementation flaws in cryptographic protocols to force the reuse of an already-in-use key6. Security remains one of the primary challenges in emerging 5G-IoT architectures, as end-user data must be authenticated and safeguarded against eavesdropping and interference7.

To mitigate potential attacks and impersonations, significant research efforts have been dedicated to developing reliable identification schemes that enhance the security of RF devices. Leveraging the inherent variability in transmitter front-ends caused by manufacturing processes, RF fingerprinting technology has garnered considerable attention as an additional security layer, complementing encryption for wireless devices8–12. These unique variations, such as IQ imbalance, DC offset, oscillator imperfections, mixer imbalance, and non-linear power amplifier gain, alter the operating point of the transmitted signal within the in-phase/quadrature (IQ) representation space13–16. Traditional RF fingerprinting schemes are primarily based on the waveform of the received transmitter signal for effective feature acquisition and device classification12. As an example, Nguyen et al. proposed a nonparametric Bayesian method for detecting the number of devices and classifying multiple devices in an unsupervised and passive manner17. However, traditional RF fingerprinting often requires expert knowledge, making it difficult to scale and generalize for large-scale IoT systems18,19.

Fortunately, this limitation of traditional RF fingerprinting schemes has been largely overcome with the collaboration of artificial intelligence in recent years. Many machine learning and deep learning algorithms have successfully learned from a large dataset of signals collected from the transmitter and have outperformed today’s expertly-engineered RF systems1,16,20. Merchant et al. developed a framework for training a convolutional neural network (CNN) using the complex time domain baseband error signal and demonstrated identification accuracy in a set of seven 2.4 GHz commercial ZigBee devices, and its robustness is demonstrated for the SNR range between 10 and 40 dB21. Baldini et al. conducted a comparative experiment using different CNN architectures and evaluated their performance for a wide SNR range between and 57 dB for 12 wireless devices with and without additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN)22. With access to powerful GPUs, larger neural networks have also been trained using waveform data collected from up to 10,000 devices23–25. Such large neural networks for RF fingerprinting typically operate on a remote central server, which necessitates the transfer of a large volume of data for training and inference. For example, a 7 TB experimental dataset and a 400 GB dataset provided by DARPA, which includes hundreds of thousands of transmissions from thousands of WiFi and ADS-B devices under varying conditions, have been used to fully assess the impact of the wireless channel on CNN-based fingerprinting algorithms26. Moreover, in addition to the known device classification27–29, specific attention has also been paid to open-set device recognition to reject unknown malicious devices that gain access to the network2,30–32.

To overcome the power and memory constraints of edge devices, edge computing has been proposed for efficient RF fingerprint identification. This decentralized approach involves training a deep neural network in a central cloud before deploying it to a low-power edge device for local inference16. However, it may become extremely insecure to transmit and store RF features on the public network for certain security-sensitive edge devices. To avoid this, a distributed machine learning method with optimized communication cost is proposed. Their training takes more than 2000 seconds with an accuracy greater than for a total number of 100,000 signals from 20 devices31. Recently, a federated transfer learning mechanism has also been designed without compromising data privacy through a collaborative neural network33. Although the security risk associated with data transfer is mitigated, the deployment of neural networks in varying device configurations can compromise their universality. In addition, techniques such as pruning have been used to develop compressed neural networks, addressing significant computational costs and accelerating the training process18,34,35. For example, a compression factor of 27.2 for convolutional layers is achieved while incurring a negligible decrease in prediction accuracy of less than 1 percent16. An earlier LSTM neural network has already been proposed. However, it is only used for the automatic identification of hardware-specific features and identical transmitters in the presence of strong noise36. The design of a lightweight neural network and the subsequent comprehensive study on its RF identification (RFID) performance that incorporates the effects of device variability, device number, frame number per device, and SNR, as well as its deployment strategy to today are still far from a satisfactory level.

In this study, we propose a lightweight yet reliable neural network with a 9-layer architecture based on the LSTM strategy for efficient open-set RFID. Such RFID systems, which employ a deployment strategy based on systematic evaluation, are particularly valuable for resource-constrained edge environments within the IoT ecosystem, but they remain less explored to date12,16,20,37–43. To achieve this, we employ a previous experiment-based power amplifier nonlinearity model to construct realistic transmitter models. We then generate beacon frames incorporating multi-path fading, radio impairments, and AWGN in a parallel manner using multiple CPU cores for acceleration. This enables us to evaluate the RFID performance efficiently using up to 1000 transmitters and 800 frames per transmitter. The identification performance of our LSTM-based neural network is extensively evaluated in a wide parameter space by comparing it with two well-known deep neural networks, ResNet and GoogleNet. Finally, we test our LSTM-based neural network on multiple edge devices, including one HPC node, one PC, and three smartphones, using CPU and GPU processors for processing.

After that, a system object of rfFingerprintingNonHTFrontEnd is created in the MATLAB WLAN toolbox to detect and receive the output RF signal f(x(t)). To mimic the real-world environment, we employ the system object of comm.RayleighChannel to include the multi-path fading effect due to signal reflection and scattering. In this work, we use a sample rate of 20 MHz and zero maximum Doppler shift for the multi-path channel. Then, channel impairments including phase noise, frequency offset, and DC offset are also introduced to obtain the RF signal h(f(x(t))). Finally, AWGN with a specific SNR denoted as n(t) is added to the signal waveform and the received signal is represented by . Only the L-LTF segment with a fixed length of (for both the in-phase and quadrature components in our present frequency domain representation, respectively), which belongs to the preamble part of the generated RF signal y(t), will be fed into the neural network for subsequent open-set RF fingerprinting identification training. A pool of multiple CPU processors that utilize the spmd keyword in MATLAB is constructed to accelerate signal generation y(t). In addition, random modulation type, such as BPSK and QPSK with different coding rates, is assigned to each of these simulated signals y(t) by specifying an integer within the interval of [0, 7] for the MCS option of a given wlanNonHTConfig object. The main objective is to create a realistic training environment that closely simulates real-world RF communication scenarios. By incorporating these steps, we ensure our LSTM-based network is robust to various channel conditions and noise levels, thereby enhancing its performance on open-set RF fingerprinting tasks.

LSTM-based neural network

As shown in Fig. 1c, we propose a lightweight neural network based on the LSTM strategy for the identification of open-set RF fingerprints. The 9-layer architecture of our LSTM-based neural network consists of one flatten layer, two pairs of LSTM and dropout layers, one fully connected layer, one softmax layer and a classification layer. The flatten layer is used to reshape the two-dimensional in-phase and quadrature input data into a single dimension before being processed through the two LSTM layers. Two dropout layers with both probability 0.5 are introduced to enhance the model generalization and avoid data overfitting. The output of the second LSTM layer is fed into a fully connected layer, which maps the extracted features to multiple output categories. The fully connected layer outputs are then passed through a softmax layer, which converts these outputs into a probabilistic distribution. This transformation facilitates multi-class classification by providing probability scores for each category. The final classification decision is made based on these probabilities. This architecture ensures that the model can effectively learn and classify RF fingerprints while maintaining computational efficiency and robust generalization.

The L-LTF segments generated are first randomly shuffled and then divided into three sets: training (), validation (), and test (). The training process employs the following key parameters: a maximum of 20 epochs, an initial learning rate of 0.008, a learning rate drop factor of 0.5, and a regularization of 0.001. Furthermore, the training data are randomly shuffled at each epoch to enhance mixing and prevent overfitting. For a fair evaluation of RFID performance, we compared accuracy, training, and inference time consumption, as well as the use of VRAM and disk storage of our LSTM-based neural network, with those of ResNet and GoogleNet. In our study, the term “accuracy” refers specifically to the accuracy of Top-1 classification, the proportion of test instances for which the system’s device identity predicted above matches the true transmitter ( where true positive TP denotes the number of positive cases correctly identified, false negative FN represents the number of positive cases incorrectly identified as negative cases, false positive FP indicates the number of negative cases incorrectly identified as positive cases, and true negative TN denotes the number of negative cases correctly identified). This metric is standard in the RF fingerprinting literature for evaluating classification performance15,16,43,45–47.

Finally, we present an ablation study of our LSTM-based RFID framework on PC under various parameter combinations of (N, , , as summarized in Table 2. The full LSTM-based model achieves high accuracy, often exceeding 98% and mostly above 95%, across most scenarios, particularly under high SNR conditions and with more frames (FPT = 200). As expected, performance deteriorates consistently across all model variants under low SNR (10 dB) compared to high SNR (20 dB), especially with larger numbers of devices (N = 100 or 1000), where accuracy can drop into the 80% range. In several trials (e.g., 1, 4, 10, 11, 12), removing either LSTM layer results in a noticeable decline in accuracy, confirming that both layers contribute meaningfully to optimal performance. However, in certain high-SNR, high-FPT scenarios (e.g., Trials 3, 5, 6, 9), the difference between the full and ablated models becomes negligible ( 0.1–0.3%), indicating strong model robustness under ideal conditions. No consistent trend emerges suggesting that one LSTM layer is substantially more critical than the other; rather, their importance appears context-dependent. For a fixed number of devices and SNR, increasing FPT almost universally improves accuracy across all model configurations, underscoring the importance of sufficient data samples per device for reliable identification. In contrast, for fixed SNR and FPT, increasing the number of devices generally reduces accuracy due to the increased classification complexity. Nonetheless, the model demonstrates remarkable scalability, maintaining accuracy above 97% even with N = 1000 devices, provided SNR and FPT are sufficiently high. In summary, the ablation study confirms that both LSTM layers are integral to the model’s success, each contributing under different operational conditions.

Conclusions

In this work, we propose a lightweight yet reliable 9-layer neural network based on the LSTM strategy for efficient open-set RF fingerprinting identification. The simulated beacon frames used for training incorporate random modulation, power amplifier nonlinearity, multi-path fading, radio impairments, and additive noise. The accuracy and efficiency of our LSTM-based neural network are extensively evaluated across a wide parameter space and compared with the well-known ResNet and GoogleNet architectures. An identification accuracy greater than is consistently obtained for and . For lower and , the LSTM-based neural network still outperforms GoogleNet and behaves close to ResNet. In particular, a significant speed-up of up to 116.7 is achieved in training when and , and the inference time falls below 2 s. Meanwhile, the usage of VRAM is also reduced by a factor of up to 22.4, and the disk storage of the trained neural network based on LSTM is less than 1 MB. Our experiments reveal that the options of local training and inference, remote training and local inference, and remote training and inference become more suitable for , 100, and 1000, respectively. The subsequent ablation study confirms that both LSTM layers are critical for the model’s performance. These findings demonstrate that a lightweight LSTM-based neural network can achieve high accuracy in open-set RF fingerprinting while significantly reducing computational cost, training time, and storage requirements, allowing practical and energy-efficient deployment in edge devices.

Limitations and future work

The current work relies on simulated data that, while incorporating realistic impairments, may not encompass the full complexity of real-world RF environments. In addition, the evaluation is limited to 1000 devices; scalability to larger IoT networks remains unverified. Future work should validate the model using large-scale and real-world signal captures under diverse channel conditions. Meanwhile, hybrid architectures and further network compression could also be explored to reduce energy and latency for ultra-low-power devices.
