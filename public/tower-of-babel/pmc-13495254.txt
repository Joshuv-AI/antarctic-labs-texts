Late vertebral and pulmonary alveolar echinococcosis after hepatic surgery mimicking spinal and disseminated tuberculosis: a case report
Authors: Chenxi Li, Fan Luo, Xinwei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlveolar echinococcosis (AE) is a rare but potentially fatal zoonotic disease that primarily affects the liver, while extrahepatic involvement usually indicates advanced disease and poor prognosis. Vertebral and pulmonary AE may closely mimic tuberculosis (TB), particularly in TB-endemic regions, leading to substantial diagnostic challenges. We report a case of late vertebral and pulmonary AE after previous hepatic hydatid surgery that was initially misdiagnosed as spinal and disseminated pulmonary TB.Case presentationA 28-year-old Tibetan woman from a nomadic family presented with a 7-month history of progressive back pain and a 2-month history of cough. She had undergone hepatic hydatid cyst resection 8 years earlier with antiparasitic therapy and had completed treatment for pulmonary TB 3 years previously. She also reported household exposure to active TB. Spinal CT and MRI revealed osteolytic destruction of the T12–L2 vertebrae with paravertebral and psoas abscesses, and chest CT showed diffusely distributed bilateral pulmonary nodules. Based on her epidemiological background, prior TB history, symptoms, and imaging findings, spinal TB with disseminated pulmonary TB was initially suspected. Surgical decompression and abscess drainage were performed, and histopathology demonstrated granulomatous inflammation with caseous necrosis, although acid-fast staining was negative. Despite standard anti-TB therapy, vertebral destruction, paravertebral involvement, psoas abscesses, and pulmonary nodules progressed over 12 months. During a second operation, metagenomic next-generation sequencing of pus identified Echinococcus multilocularis, establishing the diagnosis of vertebral and pulmonary AE. Anti-TB therapy was discontinued, and albendazole treatment was initiated. A structured follow-up protocol was established, including clinical assessment every 3–6 months, contrast-enhanced MRI of the spine and CT of the chest and abdomen, and routine laboratory monitoring. Follow-up imaging after 3 months showed marked improvement, and the patient’s symptoms substantially resolved by 6 months.ConclusionsThis case highlights the striking clinical and radiological resemblance between extrahepatic AE and TB, the risk of diagnostic anchoring in endemic settings, and the diagnostic value of mNGS when conventional investigations are inconclusive. The 8-year latency after hepatic surgery underscores the chronic, invasive nature of AE and the need for lifelong surveillance and multidisciplinary management.Keywords: Alveolar echinococcosis, Tuberculosis, Differential diagnosis, Metagenomic next-generation sequencing

Background

Cystic echinococcosis (CE) and alveolar echinococcosis (AE) result from larval-stage infections of Echinococcus granulosus and Echinococcus multilocularis, respectively. Historically referred to as "hydatid disease," AE is now recognized as a distinct condition due to its unique epidemiological characteristics [1]. The global incidence of AE ranges from 0.03 to 1.2 per 100,000 individuals annually, with the highest burden in central Asia and western China [2]. In nearly all cases of AE, the liver serves as the primary site of infection, although metastasis can occur in the lungs, brain, and spine [3]. While extrahepatic AE is infrequently reported, its presence typically signifies advanced disease, poor prognosis, and elevated mortality rates. The atypical clinical symptoms and imaging features that overlap with tuberculosis further complicate the diagnostic process. Therefore, extrahepatic AE should be considered in patients presenting with osteolytic spinal lesions, paravertebral abscesses, or disseminated pulmonary nodules, especially in those from endemic regions.

Current diagnostic approaches for extrahepatic alveolar echinococcosis (AE) encompass imaging techniques, serological assays for Echinococcus multilocularis antibodies, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis of biopsy specimens [4]. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has emerged as a valuable diagnostic tool for AE, as previously reported in cases of vertebral involvement [5]. The mainstay of treatment consists of surgical intervention paired with long-term antiparasitic therapy [6]. Nevertheless, despite appropriate treatment, extrahepatic AE is seldom curable.

We present a case of vertebral and pulmonary AE initially misdiagnosed as tuberculosis, a diagnostic dilemma that has been reported previously but with additional clinical context of a documented prior AE history that enriches the clinical understanding of this disease. This case emphasizes the diagnostic complexity of AE mimicking tuberculosis, highlights the importance of identifying subtle clinical indicators and avoiding diagnostic anchoring bias, and underscores the value of utilizing advanced diagnostic tools such as mNGS after a comprehensive conventional diagnostic workup in atypical clinical scenarios. The discussion integrates this case study with a literature review to explore the distinctive characteristics and implications for managing this condition.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old woman from a nomadic Tibetan family presented to a tuberculosis clinic with a 7-month history of progressive back pain and a 2-month history of cough. Her medical history included the resection of a hepatic hydatid cyst 8 years prior, followed by antiparasitic therapy, as well as a completed course of treatment for pulmonary tuberculosis 3 years earlier. Although the original pathological report of previous liver surgery could not be obtained, when the patient was asked about the clinical diagnosis at that time, the patient clearly stated that she was diagnosed with hepatic echinococcosis at that time and received antiparasitic treatment. In addition, she reported household exposure to a family member with active tuberculosis.

Spinal CT identified osteolytic lesions in the T12–L2 vertebrae, which were accompanied by abscesses in the paravertebral region and the right psoas muscle (Fig. 1A, B, C). MRI further characterized these lesions as exhibiting irregular hypointense signals on T1-weighted images and hyperintense signals on T2-weighted images. Inflammatory changes were also observed in the adjacent paravertebral soft tissues and the right psoas muscle (Fig. 1D, E). In addition, chest CT demonstrated diffusely distributed subcentimeter nodules in both lungs (Fig. 2).

Based on the patient's history of tuberculosis exposure (household contact with an active TB case), prior treated pulmonary TB, clinical symptoms (back pain, cough), and imaging findings (vertebral osteolytic lesions with paravertebral abscesses, diffuse pulmonary nodules), a preliminary diagnosis of spinal tuberculosis with disseminated pulmonary tuberculosis was made. It is important to note that interferon-gamma release assays (IGRA/TST, e.g., T-SPOT.TB) and tuberculin skin testing were not performed during the initial evaluation. In this highly TB-endemic region, the combination of typical imaging features, prior TB history, and known household exposure was considered sufficient to initiate empiric anti-TB therapy, particularly given the urgency of surgical intervention for spinal instability. Concerns regarding spinal instability and the risk of paralysis necessitated surgical intervention. The patient underwent an anterior approach for T12–L1 lesion excision and right psoas abscess drainage, followed by discectomy, spinal canal decompression, bone grafting, fusion, and internal fixation. Intraoperative surgical specimens were sent for histopathological examination (H&E staining and acid-fast staining); the results showed granulomatous inflammation with caseous necrosis; however, no parasitic components were identified. Although these findings were initially considered supportive of tuberculosis, the absence of definitive pathological evidence for TB was not adequately appreciated at the time, and anti-TB therapy was still initiated. Despite receiving standard anti-tuberculosis therapy, her condition continued to deteriorate over the subsequent 12 months (Fig. 3).

A second operation was performed to resolve the failure of internal fixation and the emerging abscess. In view of the treatment failure, negative acid-fast staining results of tuberculosis and the patient's previous history of hepatic echinococcosis, a comprehensive diagnostic reassessment was started. No histopathological examination was performed during the second operation; mNGS of surgical pus samples clearly identified the nucleic acid of Echinococcus multilocularis (Table 1). However, the patient was not screened for hydatid disease-related serological antibodies. This finding conclusively established the diagnosis of alveolar echinococcosis (AE) and clarified the reason for the therapeutic failure. Consequently, anti-tuberculosis therapy was discontinued immediately. Antiparasitic treatment with albendazole was initiated at a dosage of 20 mg/kg/day, administered in two divided doses, without exceeding a total daily dose of 1 g. Treatment courses were structured as 30 days of medication followed by a 14-day interval, repeated sequentially. Regular monitoring of coagulation parameters, liver and kidney function, complete blood count, C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). In addition, regular imaging follow-up every 3–6 months was advised. In addition, collection and analysis of pus specimens were recommended when clinically indicated to monitor for recurrence or secondary infection. Given the chronic and recurrent nature of alveolar echinococcosis (AE), a structured follow-up protocol was implemented for this patient. Long-term monitoring entails clinical assessments every 3–6 months, along with regular imaging surveillance that includes contrast-enhanced MRI of the spine and CT scans of the chest and abdomen. In addition, coagulation parameters, liver and kidney function, complete blood count, C-reactive protein (CRP), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) are evaluated at each follow-up visit. The patient continues to be regularly reviewed, and any emergence or exacerbation of symptoms will necessitate an earlier reassessment. Follow-up imaging conducted 3 months after discharge demonstrated improvement (Fig. 4). By the 6-month follow-up, the patient's back pain and numbness had significantly resolved. The patient continues to recover well and remains under regular review. The clinical course of this patient is summarized in the timeline, as detailed in Fig. 5.

Discussion

This case of vertebral and pulmonary alveolar echinococcosis (AE) initially misdiagnosed as tuberculosis highlights several critical lessons in clinical reasoning, diagnostic methodology, and long-term disease surveillance.

The patient's presentation of back pain, cough, vertebral osteolytic lesions, paravertebral abscesses, and diffuse pulmonary nodules strongly indicated spinal and miliary tuberculosis, a disease that is both common and highly prevalent in her endemic region [7]. However, the differential diagnosis for such clinical and imaging findings should also include pyogenic spondylodiscitis, brucellosis (which remains an important cause of spinal infection in endemic regions), and fungal infections, such as aspergillosis, particularly in immunocompromised hosts. These conditions can present with similar features of vertebral destruction and paravertebral soft tissue involvement. While AE and TB were the primary diagnostic considerations given the patient’s epidemiological background, broadening the differential diagnosis is essential to avoid premature diagnostic closure. This epidemiological context, along with her history of treated pulmonary TB and exposure to TB within her household, established a significant cognitive anchor. Clinicians initially interpreted all findings through the lens of TB, neglecting the potential for alternative diagnoses despite the presence of atypical features. This anchoring bias, a well-documented cognitive error in clinical decision-making, was further exacerbated by the lack of immediate red flags, such as acute neurological deficits or rapid clinical deterioration [8]. Only after the failure of anti-TB therapy and the progression of the disease over 12 months did the diagnostic framework begin to shift. This case highlights the necessity of maintaining diagnostic humility and regularly reassessing initial assumptions, particularly in complex or non-resolving cases. Similar diagnostic challenges have been reported in instances, where alveolar echinococcosis (AE) mimicked metastatic malignancy or pulmonary cysts [9, 10].

Conventional diagnostic methods, such as histopathology and serology, have limitations in atypical presentations of alveolar echinococcosis (AE) [11]. Histopathological examination of the first surgical specimen showed granulomatous inflammation with caseous necrosis. This pattern is highly suggestive of tuberculosis. However, caseous necrosis is not pathognomonic for TB; it can occasionally be observed in other granulomatous diseases, including fungal infections, brucellosis, and—as in this case—parasitic infections, such as alveolar echinococcosis. Indeed, necrotizing granulomatous reactions in AE may histologically mimic TB, and the absence of acid-fast bacilli or parasitic structures does not exclude either diagnosis. This histopathological overlap further complicates the distinction between TB and AE in endemic regions. The absence of acid-fast bacilli and the lack of response to anti-tuberculosis therapy prompted further investigation. In the second surgery, histopathology was not performed; instead, mNGS of a pus sample definitively identified Echinococcus multilocularis. This case highlights the limitations of histopathology in distinguishing between tuberculosis and alveolar echinococcosis, particularly when morphological features overlap, and underscores the value of molecular diagnostics in complex presentations. mNGS provides a hypothesis-free approach for detecting rare, unexpected, or co-infecting pathogens without prior clinical suspicion [12]. Recent studies have highlighted the efficacy of mNGS in diagnosing AE from diverse clinical samples, such as lung punctures and vertebral lesions [5, 13, 14]. In this instance, mNGS not only confirmed the accurate diagnosis but also elucidated the ineffectiveness of anti-tuberculosis therapy and guided appropriate antiparasitic treatment. This is consistent with prior findings showcasing the utility of mNGS in challenging diagnostic scenarios [5, 14, 15]. It is essential to recognize that mNGS should not replace conventional diagnostics but rather serve as a supplementary tool when standard methods yield inconclusive results and clinical suspicion persists [13]. It is important to note that histopathological examination was not performed during the second surgery, and targeted PCR for Echinococcus was not conducted to confirm the mNGS result. While mNGS provided a definitive diagnosis in this case, the lack of orthogonal confirmatory methods represents a limitation. Furthermore, the incomplete TB workup (IGRA/TST not performed) reflects real-world diagnostic constraints in high-burden settings but should be acknowledged as a factor that may have influenced the initial diagnostic trajectory.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this case. First, the pulmonary nodules were not biopsied, and no bronchoscopy was performed; thus, pathological confirmation of pulmonary AE is lacking. Second, the original hepatic pathology report from the prior surgery was unavailable. Third, serological testing for echinococcosis was not performed. Finally, the absence of IGRA/TST testing during initial evaluation, while reflective of real-world constraints in high-burden settings, may have influenced the diagnostic trajectory.

The significant feature of this case is the 8-year gap between the patient's initial hepatic hydatid surgery and the onset of symptomatic vertebral and pulmonary metastases. This extended latency period is a manifestation of the distinctive biology of E. multilocularis, characterized by slow growth yet persistent invasiveness [16]. The well-documented metastatic capability of AE is evident, with extrahepatic involvement noted in up to 34% of cases at the time of diagnosis. Instances of multi-organ engagement emerging years post-initial resection have been documented, occasionally with intervals exceeding 20 years. This prolonged dormancy challenges the traditional concept of "cure" post-surgical resection, emphasizing that apparent disease control does not signify eradication. Dormant viable metacestode tissue can persist in extranodal locations for extended periods before reactivating and inciting clinical manifestations [6]. It is important to acknowledge that the absence of the original hepatic pathology report represents a limitation of this study. Nevertheless, the documented history of post-surgical antiparasitic therapy and the subsequent mNGS-confirmed E. multilocularis infection provide strong indirect evidence supporting the diagnosis of prior hepatic AE. This case underscores the imperative for lifelong, systematic monitoring of all AE patients, even those deemed to be in remission. Recent studies have shown that although the overall survival of patients with alveolar echinococcosis may be affected by non-AE-related factors (such as age and comorbidities), following international guidelines (including benzimidazole treatment and radical surgery) has significantly improved the AE specific survival rate, emphasizing the importance of standardized treatment and long-term follow-up [9, 17]. The absence of observable liver activity does not exclude dormant metastatic foci, as illustrated in this instance.

Conclusions

This case of vertebral and pulmonary AE, initially misdiagnosed as tuberculosis, underscores the diagnostic challenges associated with extrahepatic disease manifestations. The use of advanced molecular techniques, such as mNGS, is vital for achieving an accurate diagnosis when conventional methods yield inconclusive results. The extended interval between primary treatment and metastatic recurrence highlights the necessity for lifelong monitoring of AE patients. Furthermore, a multidisciplinary approach that includes infectious disease specialists, surgeons, radiologists, and pathologists is essential for the optimal management of this complex disease.
