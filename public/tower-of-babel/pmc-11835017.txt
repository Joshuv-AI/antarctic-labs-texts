Pseudoalteromonas agarivorans-derived novel ulvan lyase of polysaccharide lyase family 40: Potential application of ulvan and partially hydrolyzed products in cosmetic industry
Authors: Navindu Dinara Gajanayaka, Eunyoung Jo, Minthari Sakethanika Bandara, Svini Dileepa Marasinghe, Sachithra Amarin Hettiarachchi, Sithumini Wijewickrama, Gun-Hoo Park, Chulhong Oh, Youngdeuk Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Ulvan is a complex sulfated polysaccharide in the cell walls of green algae with extensive applications in food, pharmaceutical, and agricultural industries, prompting extensive studies on ulvan, its oligosaccharides, monosaccharides, and cost-effective depolymerization methods. Our primary objectives were to investigate novel ulvan-utilizing marine bacteria, perform recombinant engineering of genes responsible for ulvan depolymerization, and determine their potential industrial applications. Samples were collected from Jeju Island, which is a South Korean region with significant excessive green algal growth, especially that of Ulva species. The marine bacterium Pseudoalteromonas agarivorans efficiently uses ulvan as its primary carbon source, indicating its potential for ulvan degradation. Through whole-genome sequencing the paul40 gene, which is a polysaccharide lyase family 40 (PL40) member, was identified and subsequently engineered into the pET-16b vector for expression as a His-tagged 95 kDa fusion protein. The ulvan depolymerization process was evaluated and confirmed using various analytical techniques including dinitrosalicylic acid assay, thin-layer chromatography, and gel permeation chromatography. Optimal enzyme activity occurred at 35°C, pH 8.0 in phosphate buffer, and 2.5 mM of NaCl. Furthermore, enzyme characterization and specific activity measurements were performed. This study is the first to report hyaluronidase and elastase inhibition by ulvan and its derivatives along with the characterization of an ulvan lyase enzyme from the PL40 family.One-Sentence SummaryThis study reports the identification and recombinant expression of a novel ulvan-degrading enzyme from Pseudoalteromonas agarivorans, demonstrating its potential for cosmetic industrial applications by revealing ulvan’s and partially hydrolyzed ulvan’s hyaluronidase and elastase inhibition properties.

Introduction

Algal blooms or green tides are common in coastal ecosystems. Urbanization and industrialization have led to high nutrient accumulation in coastal zones, which fosters an algal bloom-friendly environment (Wang et al., 2020a). These events are predominantly driven by opportunistic algae such as Ulva species (Smetacek & Zingone, 2013) and cause significant economic and ecological impacts. Ulva are green macroalgae belonging to phylum Chlorophyta. They grow in various forms such as attached, sessile, and free-floating (Wichard et al., 2015). Ulvan is a complex water-soluble anionic sulfated polysaccharide of the Ulva cell wall. It accounts for up to 29% of the algal dry weight and is composed of l-rhamnose-3-sulfate (Rha3S), d-glucuronic acid (GlcA), l-iduronic acid (IdoA), and d-xylose (Xyl) units (Lahaye & Robic, 2007). The repeating disaccharide ulvan units consist of ulvanobiuronic acid A; Rha3S is associated with either GlcA (Rha3S–GlcA), ulvanobiuronic acid B, IduA (Rha3S–IduA), or Xyl (Rha3S–Xyl, ulvanibiose 3-sulfate) (Lahaye & Robic, 2007). Sulfated polysaccharides possess unique properties and diverse biological activities (Cunha & Grenha, 2016; Shalaby, 2011).

Ulvan and its derived oligosaccharides and disaccharides elicit antioxidant (Li et al., 2018), anticoagulant (Cui et al., 2018), antitumor (Thanh et al., 2016), antihyperlipidemic (Qi et al., 2012), and antiviral properties (Aguilar-Briseno et al., 2015). Consequently, ulvan and its derivatives have been exponentially used in biological applications, particularly in the pharmaceutical industry. Recently, the focus has shifted to low-molecular-weight ulvan derivatives such as oligosaccharides and disaccharides owing to their low viscosity and greater application potential compared with those of ulvan polysaccharide (Pengzhan et al., 2003; Qi et al., 2006). Moreover, ulvan and its derivatives are used extensively in various industries such as food, agriculture, biomedical, and cosmetics (Alves et al., 2012a, 2012b; Toskas et al., 2011).

Polysaccharides may be chemically, biologically, or physically saccharified. Unlike during biological saccharification, chemical and physical methods, such as H2O2-mediated depolymerization (Qi et al., 2006), microwave depolymerization (Pengzhan et al., 2003), and acid-mediated reactions (Lahaye & Robic, 2007), often do not consider the quality and specificity of the resulting products (Li et al., 2013; Pomeroy, 2023). These methods typically result in a heterogeneous mixture of products. These limitations highlight the disadvantages of physical and chemical depolymerization methods to produce highly specific products. In contrast, enzymatic depolymerization offers a distinct advantage by enabling precise cleavage of polysaccharides (Duan & Kasper, 2011), resulting in well-defined and bioactive products that meet the demands of high-value applications.

The process of ulvan depolymerization is performed by specialized enzymes called ulvan lyases, which cleave the glycosidic bonds in ulvan polysaccharides through a β-elimination reaction (Gao et al., 2019). By targeting ulvan’s intricate sugar backbone, ulvan lyase generates ulvan oligosaccharide with defined structure and properties. The low-molecular-weight ulvan produced has diverse applications in various fields, such as pharmaceuticals and nutraceuticals (Huang et al., 2024). Ulvan lyase activity was first documented in 1997, in a marine bacterium isolated from mud containing decomposing Ulva species, which revealed a new perspective on ulvan depolymerization (Lahaye et al., 1997). An in-depth genome study of Nonlabens ulvanivorans by Nyvall Collen et al. (2011) identified the first endolytic ulvan lyase gene. Subsequently, several studies reported the capability of marine microorganisms to depolymerize ulvan. Ulvan lyases act through a β-elimination reaction by cleaving the 1–4 glycosidic bonds in the polysaccharide chain, which results in a reducing end on one segment and unsaturated ring on the nonreducing segment (Garron & Cygler, 2010; Nyvall Collen et al., 2011). The Carbohydrate-Active Enzyme (CAZy) database categorizes and documents polysaccharide lyases (PLs) from different polysaccharide depolymerization families using bioinformatics techniques (Lombard et al., 2010). Currently, CAZy contains 16 nonhomologous ulvan lyase genes characterized from nine different marine bacteria classified into five PL families: PL24, PL25, PL28, PL37, and PL40 (CAZy—Home). However, only one gene from the marine bacterium Formosa agariphila KMM3901 has been characterized in the PL40 family (Reisky et al., 2019).

Recent studies have increasingly prioritized natural compounds with intrinsic biological properties as a feasible alternative to synthetic chemicals. This shift has been driven by sustainable, ecofriendly, and biological compatible alternatives across various medical and industrial applications (Wainwright et al., 2022). Consequently, marine-derived compounds have garnered significant attention due to their potential biological properties, many of which remain largely underexplored (Papikinou et al., 2024). Compared to ulvan polysaccharide, the biological properties of ulvan oligosaccharides are yet to be completely understood. Therefore, further assessments and discovery of their potential properties as well as identification potential applications are essential.

In the cosmetic industry, skin aging is one of the most highlighting topics of scientific research due to its significant consumer demand and market trend. Furthermore, researchers are particularly focused on the discovery of potential natural antiaging compounds such as vitamins, polyphenols, hydroxy acids, polysaccharides, and bioactive molecules (Bjorklund et al., 2022). The extracellular matrix (ECM) represents the outermost structural section of the skin, composed primarily of fibroblast, collagen, and elastin (Fulop et al., 2012; Ndlovu et al., 2013). Hyaluronic acids, a key compound in the ECM, is abundantly found in the epidermal layer of the skin (Jiratchayamaethasakul et al., 2020). Together, these proteins and polysaccharides play a vital role in maintaining the skin youthful appearance. Elastase and hyaluronidase are enzymes that degrade elastin and hyaluronic acid, respectively. This enzymatic activity involves the skin integrity, elasticity, and hydration of the skin, leading to a loss of strength, flexibility, and moisture, which are vital for the maintenance of the youthful and healthy appearance of the skin (Ndlovu et al., 2013; Popoola et al., 2015). Therefore, there is a growing demand for natural enzyme inhibitors that can play a key role in skincare applications, providing sustainable and effective solutions for the cosmetic industry. These inhibitors hold the potential to enhance skin health by preserving elasticity and moisture while mitigating the effects of aging.

We have addressed this knowledge gap by identifying, cloning, expressing, purifying, and characterizing a new ulvan lyase-encoding gene paul40 from the PL family 40 (PL40) and derived from the marine bacterium Pseudoalteromonas agarivorans. Additionally, we have documented the first discovery of hyaluronidase and elastase inhibitory properties of ulvan and enzyme derived-partially hydrolyzed ulvan, highlighting their potential applications in the cosmetic industry as a natural compound.

Materials and Methods

Extraction of Crude Ulvan and Media Preparation

Crude ulvan was extracted using the hot water extraction method (Chen et al., 2021; Li & Wang, 2016) with minor modifications. Ulva were collected from Seongsan Beach, Jeju Island, South Korea. The samples were thoroughly rinsed with clean water to remove surface debris and contaminants. After washing, the Ulva was air-dried until a constant weight was achieved, ensuring the removal of residual moisture. The dried material was then finely ground into a uniform powder to facilitate subsequent processing and analysis. A 5% powdered Ulva solution was placed in a thermostatic water bath at approximately 85°C for 3–4 hr to dissolve all water-soluble components. The insoluble Ulva fraction was separated through centrifugation (7,000 × g for 20 min at 24°C). The supernatant was precipitated overnight with three volumes of 94% ethanol at cold temperature (10°C). The resulting pale white precipitate (crude ulvan) was filtered through a nylon cloth and freeze-dried to a constant weight. The crude ulvan was finely powdered for media preparation. A 10% (w/v) crude ulvan solution was combined with seawater and used to prepare ulvan-based media plates (Fig. 1). In this preparation, the ulvan solution contributed the primary solid content, serving as the sole polysaccharide source in the media.

Screening and Isolation of Ulvan-Degrading Marine bacteria

Partially decomposed Ulva species-associated water samples (50 mL) were collected from Seongsan Beach, Jeju Island, South Korea (September 2022). These samples were thoroughly crushed and vortexed to homogenize the distribution of microorganisms. Then, the samples were diluted 10 and 100 times with autoclaved seawater. A 70-μL aliquot of each diluted sample was evenly spread on 10% crude ulvan media plates using autoclaved glass beads and incubated at 25°C for 1–3 days until noticeable changes were observed on the plates. The microorganism samples were recollected only from visibly hydrolyzed zones on the ulvan plates. These samples were subsequently restreaked on marine agar media to obtain pure cultures. The samples were subcultured on marine agar plates until pure cultures were obtained. A single colony from the purified culture was selected and again inoculated onto a 10% ulvan-supplemented seawater plate to confirm the activity of ulvan lyase. After the confirmation of ulvan lyase activity, 16S rRNA gene sequences of the observed colonies were amplified for species identification.

Whole-Genome Sequence Analysis, Identification, and Phylogenetic Analysis of Gene paul40

The draft whole-genome sequence (coverage 542.09×) of the screened ulvan-utilizing marine bacteria was provided by CJ Bioscience, Inc. (Seoul, South Korea). The predicted ulvan lyase gene, which belongs to PL40, was identified from the draft genome of the identified bacteria using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) and the CAZy database. The identified gene—paul40—was submitted to NCBI GenBank (NCBI accession number PQ178474). The signal peptide was predicted using SignalP 6.0 (Teufel et al., 2022), and the transmembrane domains were predicted using DeepTMHMM version 1.0.24 (Hallgren et al., 2022). The web tools SMART version 9.0 (Letunic & Bork, 2018; Letunic et al., 2021) and InterPro tool version 97.0 (Paysan-Lafosse et al., 2023) were used to determine the conserved domains.

Genes encoding ulvan lyases and heparinases were identified from the CAZy database. The retrieved gene sequences were subjected to multiple sequence alignment using MEGA11 software (Koichiro Tamura & Kumar, 2021). The ClustalW algorithm was used for alignment, and evolutionary relationships were analyzed by constructing a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree using MEGA11. The Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) V6 web-based tool was used to visualize the constructed phylogenetic tree (Letunic & Bork, 2024).

Cloning, Overexpression, and Purification of Recombinant PaUL40

PCR was performed to clone paul40 without the signal peptide, and the genomic DNA of the identified marine bacterium was used as the template. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, genomic DNA was isolated from the identified marine bacterium using AccuPrep® Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Bioneer, Daejeon, South Korea). The forward and reverse primers used were 5′ATCGAAGGTCGTCATATGAGAGCGTCGGCAGAGGTA3′ and 5′TTGTTAGCAGCCGGATCCTTACATTTTCATGTAGGCTGAATTTTTATAAAAGGTTGTAAGC3′, respectively. The In-Fusion cloning protocol was performed using the Ez-FusionTM Cloning Kit (Enzynomics, Daejeon, South Korea) to insert the gene into the pET-16b vector (NovaGen, Madison, WI, USA). The vector was linearized using restriction enzymes at the Nde I and BamH I sites. The constructed plasmid was transformed into Escherichia coli (DH5α) and cultured on Luria–Bertani (LB) ampicillin (LB/amp) media plates overnight at 37°C. A single colony was inoculated into 4 mL of LB broth with ampicillin and incubated overnight at 37°C with shaking at 180 rpm. The positive transformants were extracted using the AccuPrep® Plasmid Extraction Kit (Bioneer, Daejeon, South Korea) and verified through sequencing (Macrogen, Seoul, South Korea). The verified plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) cells for expression studies. The resulting BL21(DH3) cultures were induced at an optical density of 0.6–0.8 (OD600) with a final concentration of 0.1 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and incubated at 16°C with shaking at 180 rpm for 16–18 hr in LB/amp media. The cells were harvested after centrifugation at 8,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, resuspended in column buffer, and frozen overnight at −20°C.

The cells were disrupted using sonication on ice at 85% amplitude. The supernatant was separated through centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C. His-tag-fused PaUL40 was purified using a Nova-gen His Tag Purification Kit (Nova-gen, Madison, WI, USA). The purified proteins were analyzed using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE), and the concentration of the eluted proteins was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay reagent kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA).

PaUL40 Biochemical Characterization

The thermal stability of recombinant PaUL40 at different temperatures was examined by performing enzyme activity assays after preincubation of the enzyme for different durations (5, 10, 30, 60, and 120 min) at 30°C, 35°C, and 40°C. The melting temperature (Tm) was analyzed using the UNcle-protein stability screening platform from Unchained Laboratories (Pleasanton, CA, USA). The optimum temperature range was determined to be 25–45°C. The optimal pH was determined using three buffers: 50 mM citrate–phosphate buffer (pH 3–7), 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6–8), and 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8–10). The effect of salinity on PaUL24 activity was evaluated within a concentration range of 0–1,000 mM.

To examine the influence of metal ions (Ca2+, CO2+, Mg2+, K+, Ni2+, Mn2+, Zn2+, Cu2+, Na+, and Fe2+) and the chelate ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), purified PaUL40 was incubated with different concentration of metal ion and EDTA (2.5, 5, and 10 mM) for 30 min. Enzyme activity was determined using a modified 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method (Oh et al., 2010). Commercially purified ulvan powder (YU11689) from BIOSYNTH (Biosynth Ltd., United Kingdom) was used as the substrate for all activity assays. Hydrolysis of 1% heparin was assessed by measuring the absorbance at 232 nm (Kevin Pojasek et al., 2000). Additionally, 0.2% heparin treated with PaUL40 was mixed with fresh fish blood and incubated at room temperature until coagulation occurred. This 0.2% heparin-treated fish blood was used as the control. Commercial heparin sodium salt from the porcine intestinal mucosa (grade 1-A) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Burlington, MA, USA).

Enzyme Kinetics

The kinetic parameters were measured using various concentrations of commercial ulvan, in the 0–25 mg/mL range, at the optimum temperature and pH for 10 min. l-Rhamnose was used as the standard. One unit of ulvan lyase activity was defined as the amount of enzymes that yielded 1 μmol of reducing sugars within 1 min of reaction. The Km, Vmax, and Kcat values were determined using the Michaelis–Menten equation in GraphPad Prism version 8.4.3 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Gel Permeation Chromatography and Thin-Layer Chromatography

Samples of 1% commercial ulvan and those partially hydrolyzed using PaUL40 for different periods (5 min—PHU5, 10 min—PHU10, 30 min—PHU30, 60 min—PHU60, and 180 min—PHU180) were sent to the Korea Research Institute of Chemical Technology for molecular weight distribution analysis. The analysis was performed using an ultra-hydrogel 120, 250, 500, 1,000 (7.8 mm × 300 mm) column with a 0.1 M NaNO3 aqueous solution at a flow rate of 1 mL·min−1.

PaUL40 hydrolytic activity was further evaluated using thin-layer chromatography (TLC). l-Rhamnose (Rhm), xylose (Xyl), and xylobiose (Xylbio) (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA) were used as standards. Product separation was performed on a silica gel 60 TLC plate (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) using a 2:1:1 v/v solvent mixture of n-butanol:acetic acid:dH2O. The spots were visualized using orcinol dip reagent, followed by heating at 110°C in a dry oven for 10 min.

End Product Analysis

The enzyme reaction mixture was prepared by incubating the purified enzyme, PaUL40 with a 1% (w/v) solution of commercial ulvan for 24 hr. The resulting end products were analyzed using liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (LC/MS). After filtration through a 0.45-μm filter, the final mixture was diluted to 10 ppm (0.001%) with MS-grade water. A 10-μL sample was injected into a Phenomenex Kinetex C18 column (2.1×100 mm, 2.6 µm, 100 Å), and chromatographic separation was carried out under isocratic conditions using 90% aqueous methanol as the mobile phase. Mass spectrometric analysis was performed in negative ion mode on an X500R QTOF system (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). The data were processed to identify the molecular masses and fragmentation patterns of the ulvan hydrolysis products.

Hyaluronidase Inhibitory Assay

Hyaluronidase inhibitory activity was assessed using commercial ulvan and partially hydrolyzed ulvans including PHU5, PHU10, PHU30, PHU60, and PHU180 (ulvan oligosaccharides). Disodium cromoglycate was used as the positive control because it is an antiallergen with a strong potential to inhibit hyaluronidase (Hisao Kakegawa et al., 1985). The hyaluronidase inhibition assay was performed according to the protocol described by Sahasrabudhe and Deodhar (2010) with a few modifications.

The tested samples (12.5 μL each) and hyaluronidase (12.5 μL, 8 mg·mL−1) in 0.1 M acetate buffer (pH 3.6) were incubated at 37°C for 20 min. Then, 12.5 mM CaCl2 (25 μL) was added to each sample, followed by reincubation at 37°C for 20 min. Next, 2.4 μg·mL−1 hyaluronic acid (62.5 μL) in 0.1 M acetate buffer (pH 3.6) was added to the mixture and incubated at 37°C for 40 min. To this mixture, 0.4 N sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 25 μL) and 0.4 M potassium tetraborate tetrahydrate (PT, 25 μL) were added and incubated in a water bath at 100°C for 3 min. After cooling to room temperature, 750 μL of preprepared dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solution (d-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde 0.4 g + acetic acid 35 mL+10 N hydrochloric acid [HCl] 5 mL) was added to the mixture and incubated at 37°C for 20 min. Absorbance was measured at 585 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo ScientificTM, MultiskanTM GO), and the IC50 values were calculated based on nonlinear regression using GraphPad Prism version 8.4.3 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). All chemicals and reagents used in this experiment were of analytical grade and purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Elastase Inhibitory Assay

The samples assessed included ulvan polysaccharide and partially hydrolyzed ulvan variants, namely PHU5, PHU10, PHU30, PHU60, and PHU180. The IC50 of commercial and partially hydrolyzed ulvans was determined using the method described by Tu and Tawata (2015) with slight modifications. Initially, each sample (10 μL) was incubated at 25°C for 10 min with 1.015 mM N-Succinyl-Ala-Ala-Ala-p-nitroanilide (130 μL) in a 0.12 M Tris–HCl buffer at pH 8.0. Subsequently, 10 μL of the porcine pancreas-derived elastase (0.5 U·mL−1) in the same buffer was added to the mixture, which was incubated for 10 min at 25°C. The absorbance was measured at 410 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo ScientificTM, MultiskanTM GO), and the relative inhibition was calculated using the appropriate equation. The IC50 values were determined based on nonlinear regression analysis using GraphPad Prism version 8.4.3 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The chemicals and reagents used in this study were of analytical grade and acquired from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA). The relative inhibition was estimated as per Equation 1 provided in the previous section.

Ulvan-utilizing ability of marine bacteria. Noticeable holes on the plates indicate the ulvan-metabolizing ability of marine bacterium P. agarivorans. Plate containing 10% ulvan + filtered seawater was incubated at room temperature for 3–4 days.

Identification of Ulvan Depolymerization-Related Gene and Phylogenetic Analysis

The whole-genome sequence of P. agarivorans was used to identify ulvan lyase genes. The identified gene—paul40—contains a 2562-bp open reading frame and encodes a 852-amino acid protein. The first 40 amino acids comprise a predicted signal peptide. The paul40 gene does not contain any predicted transmembrane domains. Domain prediction tools InterPro and SMART indicated two predicted domains in PaUL40: alginate lyase and heparinase (Fig. 3a). The theoretical molecular weight (Mw) of PaUL40 is 96.6 kDa with an isoelectric point (pI) of 7.36. Sequence alignment showed that paul40 shares 30.11% identity with PL40 from F. agariphila KMM3901 (GenBank, accession number CDF79911.1). According to the CAZy database, this was the second ulvan identified in the PL40 family.

(b) Phylogenetic analysis of polysaccharide lyase (PL) families, highlighting the evolutionary relationships between ulvan lyases (PL24, PL25, PL28, and PL40) and heparinases (PL12, PL13, and PL15). Analysis shows the placement of Paul40 within a distinct clade of the PL40 family, highlighting its close relationship to other members and emphasizing its evolutionary significance. The constructed phylogenetic tree was visualized and annotated, as shown in Fig. 3b. The tree topology illustrates the evolutionary differentiation and relationships between the difference of ulvan lyase and heparinase families. According to the results, PaUL40 is positioned within the PL40 family clade and is closely related to the protein CDF79911.1. The distinct clade suggests that PaUL40 and CDF79911.1 have a common ancestor with specific evolutionary paths to their current forms. Additionally, the PL40 family is closely related to the PL28 family. The separate clustering of other ulvan lyases (PL24 and PL25) and heparinases (PL12, PL13, and PL15) highlights the adaptive evolution of these enzymes in response to their specific polysaccharide substrates, specific polysaccharide depolymerization mechanisms, and evolutionary differentiation of PaUL40.

Cloning, Overexpression, and Purification of Recombinant PaUL40

His-tag-fused PaUL40 was expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) as a soluble protein, which was purified to a concentration of 9.838 mg·mL−1. The purified fractions displayed a single band on the SDS–PAGE (Fig. 4) with an apparent molecular weight of 95 kDa, which closely matches the theoretical molecular weight of 96.6 kDa.

PaUL40 Biochemical Characterization

Purified recombinant PaUL40 (Fig. 4) demonstrates high relative thermal stability at ≤30°C (Fig. 5a). The results indicate that an increase in temperature and incubation time reduced enzyme stability with a recorded melting temperature (Tm) of 43.53 ± 0.83°C. Maximum relative activity was observed at 35°C (Fig. 5b). However, enzyme relative activity reduced by <50% when the reaction was performed at ≥50°C. The optimal pH was identified as 8.0 in phosphate buffer (Fig. 5c). Similarly, Tris buffer (pH 8.0) showed high activity albeit lower than the maximum activity observed in phosphate buffer. Notably, PaUL40 demonstrated heparinase activity, as evidenced by an increase in absorbance at 232 nm compared with that of untreated heparin. Heparinase activity was further confirmed by coagulation of fish blood at room temperature on mixing the blood samples with PaUL40-treated heparin (see Supplementary Fig. 1). These findings suggest that PaUL40 possesses multifunctional enzymatic activity.

Evaluation of PaUL40 thermal stability, optimal temperature, pH, and metal ion effects. (a) Thermal stability of PaUL40. Thermal stability was assessed by measuring enzyme activity at 35°C for 10 min with initial activity set as 100%. (b). Optimal temperature of PaUL40. Highest activity at 35°C was set as 100%. (c) pH optimization of PaUL40. Highest activity at pH 8.0 in phosphate buffer was set as 100%. (d) NaCl optimization of PaUL40. Highest activity of PaUL40 at 2.5 mM NaCl was set as 100%. (e) Effect of various metal ions and one chelate on PaUL40 activity. Activity in the absence of metal ion was used as the control and set as 100%. The results indicate that 2.5 mM NaCl is the optimal concentration for the highest activity of the PaUL40 enzyme (Fig. 5d). However, all tested NaCl concentrations were able to retain more than 80% of the enzyme activity, suggesting that PaUL40 exhibits considerable tolerance to varying NaCl levels. Among metal ions, Fe2+, Mn2+, and Co2+ enhanced PaUL40 activity, whereas Ni2+, Zn2+, and Cu2+ inhibited it (Fig. 5e). The chelating agent EDTA did not affect PaUL40 activity. Generally, increasing the concentration of the positively influencing metal ions from 2.5 to 5 mM increased enzyme activity. However, enzyme activity decreased at 10 mM metal ion concentration for all tested metal ions.

Enzyme Kinetics

Enzyme kinetic parameters were determined using Michaelis–Menten nonlinear regression analysis. The maximum initial rate (Vmax) was 7.355 μmol·min−1·mg−1, and the enzyme affinity for the substrate (Km) was 14.32 mg·mL−1. The catalytic constant (Kcat) value was 1.67 s−1 and the specific activity was 8.17 U·mg−1.

Polydispersity index (PDI) indicates the degree of nonuniformity in the size distribution of particles (Bera, 2015). The PDI values showed a temporal reduction from 27.157 to 11.187 (Table 1), providing quantitative evidence for the enzymatic ulvan hydrolysis by PaUL40 and narrowing of the molecular weight distribution as the reaction progressed. Additionally, the rightward shift in the elution profile (Fig. 6) as depolymerization progressed suggests the formation of lower-molecular-weight species.

TLC analysis (see Supplementary Fig. 2) of ulvan hydrolysis by PaUL40 showed a chromatographic profile that illustrates the migration patterns of the 1% standards, 1% ulvan polysaccharide, and time-dependent 1% partially hydrolyzed ulvan. TLC analysis was conducted to support the results acquired from GPC. Supplementary Fig. 2 shows lanes 1, 2, and 3 containing 1% xylose, 1% xylobiose, and 1% rhamnose standards, respectively. Lane 4 shows the ulvan polysaccharide, which exhibits minimal or no migration from the baseline. This is consistent with its high molecular weight, which restricts its movement through the stationary phase in TLC. Lanes 5–9 show progressively intensifying color spots away from the baseline, indicating the formation of ulvan hydrolysis products. Moreover, increasing color intensity correlates with the progress of the enzymatic reaction, suggesting a time-dependent increase in the concentration of lower-molecular-weight products.

End Product Analysis

As depicted in Fig. 7, the LC/MS analysis of the ulvan hydrolysate revealed several distinct ulvan oligosaccharide products, each characterized by unique mass-to-charge (m/z) ratios. A disaccharide (DP2, G-R3S) was identified as a doubly charged ion [M−2Na]2− at m/z 209.0216. Additionally, another disaccharide (DP2, ∆UA-R3S) was detected as a [M−Na]− ion at m/z 401.0386 and a [M−H]− ion at m/z 423.021. These findings provide conclusive evidence for the presence of various ulvan-derived oligosaccharides and highlight the successful DP2 enzymatic depolymerization of PaUL40. The results further highlight the enzyme’s capability to cleave ulvan into specific oligosaccharide fragments under the experimental conditions, demonstrating its targeted specificity in hydrolysis.

Discussion

Ulvan is a unique marine polysaccharide that is distinguished by its high sulfate content and the presence of rare monosaccharides such as iduronic acid. Unlike terrestrial plants, marine algae are rich sources of sulfated polysaccharides (Kidgell et al., 2019), highlighting the critical need for further studies in this domain. Notably, ulvan and its low-molecular-weight derivatives (oligo-ulvan) exhibit a wide range of biological activities, making them valuable for various industries including agriculture, food, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals (Pengzhan et al., 2003; Qi et al., 2006). Elucidating the enzymatic degradation of ulvan would aid in unlocking new potential means of optimizing the use of this versatile bioresource.

The screening and isolation of ulvan-utilizing microbes are challenging because of their relative low abundance and distribution. To address this, we used 10% crude ulvan with seawater medium as an alternative to marine agar. The 10% crude ulvan completely solidified and functioned as the exclusive carbon source for the microbes (Fig. 1). Consequently, only bacteria capable of metabolizing ulvan could grow on the plate media, which facilitated their isolation and study.

The isolated ulvan-consuming marine bacterium P. agarivorans is an aerobic Gram-negative nonfermentative motile nonpigmented marine species (Romanenko et al., 2003). Pseudoalteromonas is one of the most abundant bacterial genera that proliferates during green tides (Qu et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020b). These marine species metabolize a wide range of carbon sources (Gobet et al., 2018; Li et al., 2011; Qin et al., 2018), which gives them a competitive advantage over other marine microorganisms. Moreover, they secrete various extracellular compounds, including enzymes, toxins, and antibiotics (Ivanova et al., 2003), significantly to the marine carbon cycle. Consistent with these characteristics, P. agarivorans demonstrates stable depolymerization of ulvan polysaccharide (Fig. 2).

Annotation of the new ulvan lyase gene provides novel insights into ulvan degradation and its potential applications. This study is the first to document the biochemical characterization of a gene within PL40. Therefore, comparisons could not be performed across the family. The ulvan lyase PaUL40 shares 30.11% similarity with a previously documented PL40 member (Reisky et al., 2019), highlighting its uniqueness and addressing a significant knowledge gap in understanding PL40. Domain structure prediction has revealed that PaUL40 shows both alginate lyase and heparinase domains, indicating its potential for multifunctional enzymatic activities pertinent to biotechnological applications. However, PaUL40 is already recognized as a member of the PL40 family using phylogenetic and sequence similarity analysis, with the first characterized member from F. agariphila identified as an ulvan lyase. Therefore, it is a logical initial assumption that PaUL40 exhibits ulvan lyase activity, consistent with current understanding. However, the limited availability of well-characterized data pertaining to ulvan lyase-related PL families poses a challenge in identifying conserved gene regions across these families. Additionally, such data scarcity may affect the accuracy of the predictions made using web-based tools for domain structure analysis. Despite these limitations, the results clearly show that PaUL40 hydrolyzes both ulvan and heparin, which is a unique enzymatic property. Additionally, PLs often possess multifunctional capabilities owing to the presence of multiple domains within their open reading frames, and pH-dependent optimization may lead to substrate specificity (MacDonald & Berger, 2014).

The molecular weight of PaUL40 was notably greater than those of typical PL24, PL25, PL28, and PL37 enzymes, which have a molecular weight <60 kDa. Additionally, PaUL40 exhibits lower thermal stability with reduced activity at temperatures >40°C. PaUL40 biochemical characterization revealed its optimal activity conditions. Extended incubation times of >30 min and elevated temperatures >40°C gradually reduced its relative catalytic activity to below 50%. The enzyme exhibited optimal activity at 35°C and pH 8.0 in phosphate buffer. This preference for alkaline conditions (pH 6–10) concurs with the behavior of other recorded ulvan lyases (Gao et al., 2019; Reisky et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2022). PaUL40 showed significantly reduced activity (<50%) at pH <6 and >10. Notably, the phosphate buffer showed better performance than that of citrate–phosphate and Tris–HCl buffers for optimal activity.

Various metal ions and chelating agents impacted PaUL40 catalytic activity in diverse ways. Some metal ions and chelating agents enhanced PaUL40 activity, whereas some inhibited or did not exert any significant impact on enzyme activity. Notably, the metal ions that stimulated activity at low concentrations became inhibitory at higher concentrations. This phenomenon can be attributed to several mechanisms, including enzyme precipitation and aggregation, conformational changes affecting its structure, oxidative damage, or disruption of the enzyme binding site caused by overactivation (Nelson & Cox, 2024).

Recent studies have documented the potential applications of marine-derived polysaccharides and oligosaccharides in the skincare industry, demonstrating the capacity of their bioactive properties to be applied in cosmetic formulations (Kalasariya et al., 2024). One of the major concerns in the cosmetic industry is the degradation of hyaluronic acid by the enzyme hyaluronidase. Hyaluronic acid plays a crucial role in skin hydration and tissue remodeling (Menzel & Farr, 1998), and its reduction can lead to skin dryness and wrinkles. Therefore, several studies have tried to identify natural substances with hyaluronidase inhibitory activity. This study presents a novel finding that both ulvan and PaUL40-treated ulvan exhibit hyaluronidase inhibition. Furthermore, this is the first study to report the potential inhibition of elastase by ulvan and partially hydrolyzed ulvan. Elastase is a serine protease that catalyzes the cleavage of carboxyl groups on small hydrophobic amino acids such as glycine, alanine, and valine (Diez-Galan et al., 2022). Its primary function is to degrade elastin, which is a protein responsible for connective tissue elasticity (Cotten, 2020). Similar to hyaluronidase inhibition, elastase inhibition by ulvan and its partially hydrolyzed derivatives could help preserve elastin fibers and potentially contribute to improved skin elasticity and antiaging effects. These findings indicate that derivatives with low-molecular-weight ulvan exhibit reduced biological properties compared with those of high molecular weights. Furthermore, low-molecular-weight ulvan compounds show significant potential for application because low-molecular-weight compounds often provide improved solubility and enhanced bioavailability than that of their higher-molecular-weight counterparts (Sushytskyi et al., 2023).

This discovery suggests remarkable possibilities for the application of ulvan and its hydrolyzed derivatives in skincare products. Despite the fact that low-molecular-weight ulvan oligosaccharides exhibit reduced biological activity than that of high-molecular-weight ulvan polysaccharides, they do show potential for application. In contrast, low-molecular-weight derivatives are often valued for their ability to penetrate the skin more effectively, which results in better absorption and targeted inhibition (Hashimoto & Maeda, 2021). Furthermore, smaller molecules show increased bioavailability (Iannuzzo et al., 2022). Therefore, ulvan derivatives could be incorporated into moisturizers to help maintain hyaluronic acid levels in the skin to promote hydration and youthful appearance. Furthermore, the elastin inhibition activity could provide antiaging benefits, enhance skin elasticity, and reduce the appearance of wrinkles (Ganceviciene et al., 2012). With further experimental validation, these compounds could be formulated into value-added products. For instance, their capacity to enhance wound healing and enhance cellular repair suggests utility in posttreatment creams used after procedures like laser therapy or microneedling, supporting skin recovery and rejuvenation. Additionally, ulvan’s bioactivity could be applied in hair care formulations, targeting scalp health by reducing inflammation and maintaining optimal moisture levels. Its biodegradable nature and alignment with eco-friendly consumer trends further position ulvan as a sustainable and versatile ingredient, paving the way for innovative, nature-inspired products in the cosmetic industry.

Conclusion

The present study reports a novel PL gene from the PL40 family—the first to be characterized biochemically in this family. The gene elicits multifunctional activity against ulvan (sulfated polysaccharide in Ulva species cell wall) and heparin (anticoagulant polysaccharide). However, the present study is focused solely on the biochemical characterization of ulvan lyase enzyme and ulvan depolymerization because, although ulvan is noted for its complex structure and potential bioactive properties, its depolymerization and potential applications are not well documented. Additionally, this study is the first to identify the hyaluronidase and elastase inhibitory properties of ulvan and its partially hydrolyzed derivatives. These properties indicate that ulvan may exert significant bioactive effects with potential therapeutic and cosmetic uses for ulvan-based products, particularly in cosmetics, with hyaluronidase and elastase inhibition properties being desirable in antiaging and skincare products. The present study broadens our understanding of PLs and their biological roles and facilitates further investigation into various ulvan applications.
