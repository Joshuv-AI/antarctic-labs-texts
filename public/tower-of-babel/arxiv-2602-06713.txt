Missing At Random as Covariate Shift: Correcting Bias in Iterative Imputation
Luke Shannon, Song Liu, Katarzyna Reluga

Abstract
Accurate imputation of missing data is critical to downstream machine learning performance. We formulate missing data imputation as a risk minimization problem, which highlights a covariate shift between the observed and unobserved data distributions. This covariate shift–induced bias is not accounted for by popular imputation methods and leads to suboptimal performance. In this paper, we derive theoretically valid importance weights that correct for the induced distributional bias. Furthermore, we propose a novel imputation algorithm that jointly estimates both the importance weights and imputation models, enabling bias correction throughout the imputation process. Empirical results across benchmark datasets show reductions in root mean squared error and Wasserstein distance of up to 7% and 20%, respectively, compared to otherwise identical unweighted methods.

2.1
Missing Data – Notations and Mechanisms

Let
X^{T}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote a
d

\mathcal{X}:=\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{X}_{d}
, where each

is either a finite set (if

is discrete) or a possibly disconnected subset of
\mathbb{R}
(if

is continuous), for
i=1,\ldots,d
. Let
R^{T}=(R_{1},\dots,R_{d})\in\{0,1\}^{d}
be a
d
-dimensional random vector indicating which components of
X
are observed. We assume
(X,R)\sim P_{X,R}
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue
\times
counting measure, with joint density
p_{X,R}
and that
X\sim P_{X}
with marginal density
p_{X}
. In what follows, we use uppercase letters (e.g.,
X
) to denote random variables and lowercase letters (e.g.,
x
) to denote their realizations. Subscripts
i
and
\neg i
refer to the
i
-th component of a vector and to all components except the
i
-th, respectively.

To model missingness, let
*\notin\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{X}_{i}
denote a missing value and define
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{i}:=\mathcal{X}_{i}\cup\{*\}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}:=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{i}
. The observed (masked) random vector
\tilde{X}=(\tilde{X}_{1},\dots,\tilde{X}_{d})
is defined componentwise by
\tilde{X}_{i}=X_{i}
if
R_{i}=1
and
\tilde{X}_{i}=*
if
R_{i}=0
so that
X_{i}
being observed or missing is indicated by
R_{i}=1
or
R_{i}=0
respectively.

For any realisation
r
of
R
, the random vector
X
can be partitioned into an observed part,
X_{\mathrm{obs}}=(X_{j}:R_{j}=1)

and missing part,
X_{\mathrm{miss}}=(X_{j}:R_{j}=0)
. The density of
X_{\mathrm{obs}}
or
X_{\mathrm{miss}}
is given by the relevant marginal of
p_{X}
. The same decomposition applies to any realisation
x
of
X
as well as
\tilde{X}
and
\tilde{x}
.
By construction
X_{\mathrm{obs}}=\tilde{X}_{\mathrm{obs}}
and
x_{\mathrm{obs}}=\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{obs}}
.

Finally, let us introduce missing data mechanisms. The joint density factorises as
p(x,r)=p(r\mid x)p(x)
. Missing data is described as Missing Completely At Random (MCAR) when
p(r\mid x)=p(r)
, Missing At Random (MAR) when
p(r\mid x)=p(r\mid x_{\mathrm{obs}})
and Missing Not At Random (MNAR) otherwise.

For ease of notation, we omit density subscripts in derivations unless needed and use “density” to refer to both probability density and mass functions.

2.2
Iterative Round-Robin Imputation Methods

Imputation is the task of associating each partially observed input
\tilde{X}
with a distribution over the complete data space
X
, which is used to fill in unobserved components of the data.

Popular round-robin imputation methods such as MICE, MissForest and HyperImpute
(
27
;
24
;
11
)
follow a common iterative structure. They decompose this multivariate imputation task into a sequence of simpler, univariate problems. Each variable containing missingness is handled in turn by modelling its relationship with all remaining variables, and missing values are updated according to these models in a round-robin fashion. The procedure begins by filling in missing entries using a simple initial rule, such as mean imputation, to obtain a fully completed dataset. Given this initial completion, each univariate model is fit using the current data to perform well according to a chosen notion of predictive error or risk, and is then used to update the missing values of the corresponding variable. Repeating this process across variables over multiple rounds progressively refines the imputations, until a stopping criterion is reached.

2.3
Covariate Shift

Suppose we have pairs of output and predictors
(y,x)
drawn from a joint distribution.
Let
p_{\mathrm{train}}(x,y)=p_{\mathrm{train}}(y\mid x)\,p_{\mathrm{train}}(x)
and

p_{\mathrm{test}}(x,y)=p_{\mathrm{test}}(y\mid x)\,p_{\mathrm{test}}(x)

denote the joint distributions of the train and test data respectively. Covariate shift refers to the setting where the marginals differ,
p_{\mathrm{train}}(x)\neq p_{\mathrm{test}}(x)
, while the conditional distribution remains invariant,

p_{\mathrm{train}}(y\mid x)=p_{\mathrm{test}}(y\mid x)
. Shimodaira
(
22
)
identified
that the empirical risk minimization under covariate shift is biased with respect to the test distribution, introducing importance weighting

p_{\mathrm{test}}(x)/p_{\mathrm{train}}(x)
as a remedy. In Section
3.2
, we show that this perspective and the associated importance-weighted solution translate directly to correcting bias induced by the MAR mechanism.

3
Our Risk Minimisation Problem

3.1
General Formulation

Let
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
be the space of probability distributions over
\mathcal{X}
.
Building on foundational statistical learning theory
(
28
)
, we view imputation as a risk minimisation problem. Let
\Phi_{g}:\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
be an imputation function, its associated risk is

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\Phi_{g})=

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{\mathrm{obs}},R}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{X_{\mathrm{gen}}\sim\Phi_{g}(\tilde{X})}\Big[\mathcal{L}\big\{(X_{\mathrm{obs}},X_{\mathrm{gen}}),X\big\}\mid X_{\mathrm{obs}},R\Big]\Big),

where
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
stands for a loss function and
(X_{\mathrm{obs}},X_{\mathrm{gen}})
denotes a completed data vector whose observed components are retained from
X_{\mathrm{obs}}
, and whose missing components are replaced by
X_{\mathrm{gen}}
drawn from
\Phi_{g}(\tilde{X})
. Proofs of all propositions in Section
3
are provided in Appendix
A
.

3.2
Least Squares Formulation

Despite the availability of alternative loss functions, mean squared error (MSE) remains the standard metric for evaluating imputation quality due to its interpretability and widespread use
(
33
;
11
)
. Accordingly, we focus on MSE risk minimization.

Since the mean squared error is a pointwise loss, we restrict attention to deterministic
imputation functions
\Phi_{g}
, so that, for every incomplete observation
\tilde{X}
,

\Phi_{g}(\tilde{X})
is a point mass distribution over
X
.

Definition 3.1

(Deterministic imputation map)
.

A deterministic imputation function can be identified with a mapping

g:\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d},

which takes an incomplete vector
\tilde{x}\in\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
as input and returns
a completed vector
g(\tilde{x})\in\mathcal{X}
.
Accordingly,
g
admits the canonical coordinate representation

g(\tilde{x})=\big(g_{1}(\tilde{x}),g_{2}(\tilde{x}),\dots,g_{d}(\tilde{x})\big),

where
g_{i}(\tilde{x})
denotes the
i
-th coordinate of the completed vector.

The following result shows that, under MSE loss, the imputation risk decomposes additively across coordinates.

Proposition 3.2
.

For a deterministic imputation function (Defitinion
3.1
), under mean squared error (MSE) loss, the risk in (
1
) admits a coordinatewise
decomposition across the components of
g
, that is

\mathcal{J}(g)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}p(R_{i}=0)\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i}),

(2)

where

\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i})=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(g_{i}(\tilde{X})-X_{i}\right)^{2}\mid R_{i}=0\right].

(3)

The decomposition in Proposition
3.2
highlights that
only coordinates for which missingness occurs, that is for which
p(R_{i}=0)>0
, contribute to the imputation risk.

Definition 3.3

(Set of imputed coordinates)
.

We define the index set of coordinates subject to imputation as

\mathcal{P}:=\{\,i\in\{1,\dots,d\}\;:\;p(R_{i}=0)>0\,\}.

The risk in (
2
) can written
over only those coordinates in
\mathcal{P}
:

\mathcal{J}(g)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{P}}p(R_{i}=0)\,\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i}),

(4)

where
\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i})
is defined in (
3
). We will assume throughout that for any
i\in\mathcal{P},p(R_{i}=0)<1
so that column
i
is not entirely missing and,
\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i})
is finite so that
\mathcal{J}(g)
is a well defined risk objective.

In practice,
\mathcal{J}(g)
is not computable, as it depends on unobserved entries. However, under the MAR assumption, we can express the coordinatewise risks in (
3
) in terms of observed data alone, as stated in Proposition
3.4
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be as given in Definiton
3.3
.
Suppose that
\forall i\in\mathcal{P}
the following overlap condition holds:

0<p(r_{i}=1\mid x_{\mathrm{obs}},r_{\neg i})<1,\;\forall x_{\mathrm{obs}},r_{\neg i}.

Under the MAR assumption introduced in section
2.1
and MSE loss, the coordinatewise risks in (
3
) can be expressed in terms of observed data only:

\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i})=\mathbb{E}\!\Big[w_{i}(X_{\mathrm{obs}},R_{\neg i})\big(g_{i}(\tilde{X})-X_{i}\big)^{2}\mid R_{i}=1\Big]

(5)

where the weights
w_{i}(\cdot,\cdot)
are defined by

\displaystyle w_{i}(x_{\mathrm{obs}},r_{\neg i}):=\frac{p(x_{\mathrm{obs}},r_{\neg i}\mid r_{i}=0)}{p(x_{\mathrm{obs}},r_{\neg i}\mid r_{i}=1)}.

(6)

Proposition
3.4
highlights a crucial insight:
the MAR mechanism itself induces a covariate shift that cannot be ignored
when using observed data to minimise the least squares loss. Conditioning on the missingness indicator
R_{i}
defines two subpopulations: the observed entries (
R_{i}=1
) and the unobserved entries (
R_{i}=0
). Under MAR, the conditional distribution
X_{i}\mid(X_{\mathrm{obs}},R_{\neg i})
is invariant across these subpopulations, but the marginal distribution of
(X_{\mathrm{obs}},R_{\neg i})
generally differs. This creates a situation analogous to the covariate shift setup in Section
2.3
, where
X_{i}
plays the role of
y
,
(X_{\mathrm{obs}},R_{\neg i})
are the predictors, and the observed and unobserved subpopulations correspond to the training and test sets. The weights in (
6
), analogous to
p_{\mathrm{test}}(x)/p_{\mathrm{train}}(x)
, reweight the observed data so that empirical risk minimization targets the distribution of
X_{i}
in
the unobserved subpopulation
. Ignoring these weights, as in many existing iterative imputation methods
(
27
;
11
)
, where an
unweighted variant
of the risk is used, implicitly assumes no covariate shift and leads to biased imputations. In Section
3.4
, we introduce a novel iterative imputation algorithm that incorporates these weights, directly addressing this limitation of existing methods.

3.3
Iterative Approach to MSE Risk Minimisation

In (
4
), the risk of an imputation function
g
, introduced in Definition
3.1
, decomposes as a sum of
componentwise risks over the coordinates
g_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{P}
,
which motivates solving for each
g_{i}
individually to construct the full
imputation function
g
. However, the coordinatewise risks given in
(
5
) are often intractable in practice, since the
conditioning set
X_{\mathrm{obs}}
varies across missingness patterns.
Minimising the full risk would therefore require fitting a separate
conditional model for each pattern, leading to a rapidly growing number
of models. In practice, the weights defined in (
6
)
are also unknown and must also be estimated as part of any risk-minimising
procedure; since they depend on
X_{\mathrm{obs}}
, they face the same
tractability issues.

A round-robin minimisation strategy naturally fits this componentwise structure of (
4
) by
seeking to minimise each
\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i})
in
(
5
) in turn over a user-specified
conditional model class
\mathcal{G}_{i}
. To maintain tractability, we
propose minimising a data-dependent surrogate risk at each iteration

t\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
of the round-robin algorithm, defined as

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{s}}^{(t)}(g)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{P}}p(R_{i}=0)\,\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{s},i}^{(t)}(g_{i})

(7)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{s},i}^{(t)}(g_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[w_{i}(\hat{X}^{(t)}_{\neg i})\big\{g_{i}(\hat{X}^{(t)}_{\neg i})-X_{i}\big\}^{2}\mid R_{i}=1\right]

(8)

At iteration
t
, both the conditional model and the weights are assumed to depend only
on
\hat{X}^{(t)}
- the current view of the imputed covariates, and not on the remaining missingness indicators

R_{\neg i}
. These modelling choices are justified in
Section
4
, and details on estimating
the weights are given in Section
3.4
.
Having established a round-robin based approach as a natural choice for minimising (
4
), we are now ready to introduce our proposed algorithm.

At each step, the weights are estimated using Algorithm

4
Practical Implementation

The coordinatewise components of the true
\mathcal{J}(g)
and the surrogate risk
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{s}}(g)
in (
5
) and (
8
), respectively, differ in two aspects.
First, in
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{s},i}(g)
, neither the imputation model nor the importance weights depend explicitly on the missingness indicators
R_{\neg i}
.
Second, both are estimated using the currently completed covariates rather than only the observed components
X_{\mathrm{obs}}
.
We justify these choices below.

4.1
Omitting
R_{\neg i}
as Predictors

To reduce computational cost, we restrict both the weight estimation and conditional imputation models at each stage to depend only on the currently imputed covariates, and not on the missingness indicators. Widely used iterative imputation methods such as MissForest and HyperImpute
(
24
;
11
)
adopt this design, but without an explicit theoretical justification. In the following, we argue that this restriction can be imposed without compromising imputation performance.

4.1.1
Conditional Imputation Models

In this section, we study
g_{i}^{*}\in\underset{g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}{\arg\min}\{\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i})\}
, for
\mathcal{J}_{i}
as defined in (
3
). We show that for common choices of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
,
g_{i}^{*}
does not depend on
R_{\neg i}
. Proposition
4.1
first formalises this for linear conditional models.

Proposition 4.1

(Linear optimal imputation ignores
R_{\neg i}
)
.

Assume that, conditional on
R_{i}=0
, the indicators
R_{\neg i}
are not perfectly collinear and that each
R_{j}
,
j\in\neg i
, satisfies
0<p(R_{j}=1\mid X_{\mathrm{obs}},R_{i}=0)<1
. Let

\mathcal{G}_{i}\coloneqq\left\{\alpha^{\top}X_{\mathrm{obs}}+\beta^{\top}R_{\neg i}+\gamma:\alpha_{i},\beta_{i},\gamma_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

Then, under the MAR assumption introduced in section
2.1

g_{i}^{*}(X_{\mathrm{obs}},R_{\neg i})=\alpha_{*}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{obs}}+\gamma_{*},

We next give a similar result for a broader class of functions.

Definition 4.2

(Projection onto observed covariates)
.

For any
g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}
, define its projection onto the observed covariates (conditional on
R_{i}=0
) as

g_{i}^{\mathrm{proj}}(X_{\mathrm{obs}})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\,g_{i}(X_{\mathrm{obs}},R_{\neg i})\mid X_{\mathrm{obs}},R_{i}=0\,].

Definition 4.3

(Class of projected functions)
.

Define the projected function class as

\mathcal{G}_{i,\mathrm{proj}}\coloneqq\Big\{g_{i}^{\mathrm{proj}}:g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\Big\}.

Proposition
4.4
shows that restricting to
\mathcal{G}_{i,\mathrm{proj}}
incurs no loss in optimality.

Proposition 4.4

(Optimal predictor depends only on
X_{\mathrm{obs}}
)
.

For any
g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}
, let
g_{i}^{\mathrm{proj}}
be as defined in definition
4.2
.
let
\mathcal{G}_{i,\mathrm{proj}}
be as given in definiton

\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i}^{\mathrm{proj}})\leq\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i})\;\text{ for all }g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}

and

\underset{g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i,\mathrm{proj}}}{\arg\min}\{\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i})\}=\underset{g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}{\arg\min}\{\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i})\}

Proposition
4.4
shows that the true risk can be minimized by a function of

Definition 4.5

Let

\mathcal{G}_{i,\mathrm{obs}}=\{g_{i}:\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{obs}}\to\mathbb{R}\mid g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\}\subset\mathcal{G}_{i}

(Equivalence of optimization over observed covariates)

If
\mathcal{G}_{i,\mathrm{obs}}
is dense in
\mathcal{G}_{i,\mathrm{proj}}
, then

\underset{g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i,\mathrm{obs}}}{\inf}\{\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i})\}=\underset{g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}{\inf}\{\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i})\}

B
we provide proofs of the results above. Furthermore, we argue that common conditional model classes such as neural networks, and popular tree-based methods are expressive enough that omitting
R_{\neg i}
at each stage is justified.

4.1.2
Weight Estimation

Ignoring
R_{\neg i}
in weight estimation is justified under an additional conditional independence assumption, namely that
R_{i}\perp R_{j}\mid X_{\mathrm{obs}}
.
Such an assumption is made in existing literature
(
2
)
in order to control modelling complexity. In our context, this assumption causes the weights to reduce to
w_{i}(x_{\mathrm{obs}})=\frac{p(x_{\mathrm{obs}}\mid r_{i}=0)}{p(x_{\mathrm{obs}}\mid r_{i}=1)}
,
eliminating the dependence on
R_{\neg i}
.

4.2
Estimation with Completed Data

Existing iterative imputation methods, such as MICE and MissForest
(
27
;
24
)
, fit conditional models at each step on the completed covariates.
This avoids partitioning the data by missingness pattern – which would require a separate model for each pattern and quickly becomes infeasible in moderate to high dimensional settings or with limited sample sizes.
By maintaining a fixed predictor dimension, this conditioning of models on completed data improves both statistical efficiency and computational tractability.
We adopt the same principle for importance weight estimation.
Although the weights theoretically depend only on observed components, the estimation uses the completed data at each iteration.
This choice inherits the same practical advantages.

5
Experiments

In this section, we isolate the effect of the weighting component of our imputation algorithm by comparing our method to an unweighted iterative imputation baseline using the same conditional model class. Specifically, we evaluate performance differences for linear conditional models, random forest models, and multi-layer perceptron (MLP) models. In Appendix
C
, we provide additional comparisons with a broader range of state-of-the-art imputation methods, demonstrating that our approach achieves competitive or superior performance across diverse settings.

5.2
MAR Simulation

To simulate MAR data, up to four continuous features are randomly selected to contain missing values. For each such feature
X_{i_{k}}
, the missingness mechanism is conditioned on up to four fully observed columns drawn from the remaining feature set, with potentially different predictor sets for each feature. Concretely, if we have a dataset with 10 columns
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{10}\}
and
\{X_{i_{1}},X_{i_{2}},X_{i_{3}},X_{i_{4}}\}
are selected to contain missingness, then for each
i_{k}
we define a predictor set
\mathcal{C}_{i_{k}}\subseteq\{X_{1},\dots,X_{10}\}\setminus\{X_{i_{1}},X_{i_{2}},X_{i_{3}},X_{i_{4}}\}
. The probability that an entry in
X_{i_{k}}
is missing is given by a logistic model
(
10
)
:

\displaystyle p(R_{i_{k}}=1\mid X_{\neg{i_{k}}})=\sigma\Big(\alpha\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i_{k}}}X_{j}+\beta_{i_{k}}\Big)

(9)

where
\alpha
controls the strength of dependence on the predictors,
\beta_{i_{k}}
is chosen to achieve a target missing rate, and
\sigma(z)=1/(1+\exp(-z))
.

Experiments are conducted over 35 random seeds, each determining which columns contain missing values and which fully observed columns they depend on. For each seed, we vary the MAR strength parameter over the integers between -3 and 3 inclusive, yielding 245 paired comparisons with the unweighted baseline. Unless stated otherwise, all results are reported at a fixed 30% missingness rate.

5.4
Limitations

The theory in our manuscript provides a strong foundation for framing imputation as a risk minimisation problem, but there remain avenues for further research. Our method relies on accurate importance weight estimation. Errors in the density ratio can propagate to both the weights and conditional model fitting; a natural direction for future work is to investigate sensitivity to increasing error in importance weight estimation. Furthermore, importance weighting can increase the variance of conditional models
(
22
)
. Therefore, a further investigation on the sufficient number of observations relative to the number of features is needed for reliable performance. Finally, while analogous importance weights have been derived and used in the MNAR literature
(
13
)
, extending our approach to the MNAR setting requires additional assumptions to ensure identifiability and represents an important direction for future work.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we have shown that MAR missingness induces a covariate shift problem which, if ignored in iterative imputation techniques, can bias the resulting imputations. We formalised imputation as a weighted risk minimization problem and proposed a covariate-shift-aware iterative imputation algorithm that incorporates importance weights to correct for this discrepancy. Empirical results demonstrate that our approach improves imputation accuracy while retaining the practical advantages of conventional iterative methods. For these reasons, we see the weighted iterative imputation
as an exciting and worthwhile new direction within the imputation field.

4.6

Proof.

Assume that
\mathcal{G}_{i,\mathrm{obs}}
is dense in
\mathcal{G}_{i,\mathrm{proj}}
with respect to the
L^{2}
-risk. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
and the projected risk minimizer
g_{i}^{*}{}^{\mathrm{proj}}\in\mathcal{G}_{i,\mathrm{proj}}
, there exists a function

g_{i}^{\mathrm{obs}}\in\mathcal{G}_{i,\mathrm{obs}}

such that

\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i}^{\mathrm{obs}})\leq\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i}^{*}{}^{\mathrm{proj}})+\varepsilon.

Since this holds for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\inf_{g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i,\mathrm{obs}}}\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i})=\inf_{g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i,\mathrm{proj}}}\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i})=\inf_{g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}\mathcal{J}_{i}(g_{i}),

which establishes the equivalence of optimization over
\mathcal{G}_{i,\mathrm{obs}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{i}
.
∎

B.4
Discussion

In practice, round-robin imputation requires fitting a model for every column with missing values at each iteration, causing the computational cost to grow rapidly. Consequently, reducing algorithmic cost in iterative approaches is crucial. One straightforward strategy to achieve this is removing the
R_{\neg i}
from the predictor set when fitting models. Such an approach seems to come for free when the MAR assumption guarantees
X_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp R_{\neg i}\mid X_{\mathrm{obs}}
. However, MAR is a statement about the true data generating process and cannot be assumed to hold for our often misspecified imputation models. ignoring
R_{\neg i}
therefore requires justification. Proposition
4.1
shows that ignoring the
R_{\neg i}
is valid for linear conditional models showing that linear-model-based methods, e.g., MICE
(
27
)
, are justified in ignoring
R_{\neg i}
in the conditional predictor set. Proposition
4.4
and its corollary tell us that it is valid to omit the
R_{\neg i}
from the predictor set when the conditional models restricted to
X_{\mathrm{obs}}
are sufficiently expressive.

We argue that, in practice, such conditions are achievable for common choices of conditional models, further justifying our design choice as well as the design choice of existing methods using more complex conditional models such as MissForest and HyperImpute
(
24
;
11
)
.

Feedforward neural networks with standard activations are universal approximators
(
9
;
23
)
, and hence capable of approximating any function of
X_{\mathrm{obs}}
. For tree-based methods, theoretical guarantees are more nuanced: Breiman’s Random Forests are consistent in the space of square integrable regression functions under structural assumptions
(
21
)
and universally consistent in classification
(
4
)
, while gradient-boosted trees achieve pointwise consistency under controlled regularization
(
36
;
34
)
. These results imply that, with sufficient data, tree-based models can approximate the population-optimal conditional expectation using
X_{\mathrm{obs}}
alone, which prior iterative imputation approaches implicitly assume is possible
(
24
;
11
)
.

C.3
Further theoretical results

In this section, we derive further theoretical results that help explain empirical patterns observed in Section
5
. We consider risk minimisation for coordinate
i
for which we are choosing an optimal imputation function from the model class
\mathcal{G}_{i}
.

For this section, let:

•

\mathcal{J}_{i}^{(r)}(g_{i})=\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(g_{i}(\tilde{X})-X_{i}\big)^{2}\,\big|\,R_{i}=r\Big],\quad r\in\{0,1\}
, be the optimisation problems corresponding to coordinate
i
under
R_{i}=r

•

g_{i}^{*,(r)}=\underset{g\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}{\arg\min}\left\{\mathcal{J}_{i}^{(r)}(g)\right\}
be the loss minimising imputation function for column
i
chosen from our class
\mathcal{G}_{i}
under
R_{i}=r

•

f_{i}(\tilde{X})=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}\mid\tilde{X}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}\mid X_{\mathrm{obs}}]
be the theoretically optimal imputation function. We note that, under MAR, this solution is optimal under
R_{i}=1
and
R_{i}=0
and, in actuality, depends only on
X_{\mathrm{obs}}
thought at fact is not needed for the following proofs.

•

\varepsilon_{i,(r)}^{2}:=\underset{g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}{\min}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(g_{i}(\tilde{X})-f_{i}(\tilde{X})\big)^{2}\,\big|\,R_{i}=r\Big]
be the approximation error of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
under
R_{i}=r

In the above definitions
\mathcal{J}_{i}^{(0)}(g_{i})
is the true coordinate-wise risk of coordinate
g_{i}
as defined in
3
, whereas
\mathcal{J}_{i}^{(1)}(g_{i})
represents the unweighted objective minimised by existing unweighted imputation algorithms. We wish to understand when the optimal solutions coincide.

Lemma C.1

(Distance between population minimisers under covariate shift)
.

Suppose that:

•

\exists\kappa>0
such that
\frac{dP(\tilde{X}\mid R_{i}=0)}{dP(\tilde{X}\mid R_{i}=1)}\leq\kappa^{-1}
almost surely (overlap condition).

•

All functions
g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}
belong to the intersection

g_{i}\in L^{2}(\tilde{X}\mid R_{i}=0)\cap L^{2}(\tilde{X}\mid R_{i}=1),

i.e., they have finite second moments under both conditional distributions
P(\tilde{X}\mid R_{i}=0)
and
P(\tilde{X}\mid R_{i}=1)
. This ensures that all terms in the proof are well-defined and that we can apply results such as the triangle inequality in
L^{2}
and have an intepretation of distance.

Then the population minimisers satisfy

\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(g_{i}^{*,(0)}(\tilde{X})-g_{i}^{*,(1)}(\tilde{X})\big)^{2}\,\big|\,R_{i}=0\Big]\leq\big(\varepsilon_{i,(0)}+\kappa^{-1/2}\varepsilon_{i,(1)}\big)^{2}.

Proof.

By definition,

g_{i}^{*,(0)}=\arg\min_{g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}\mathbb{E}\big[(g_{i}(\tilde{X})-X_{i})^{2}\mid R_{i}=0\big],\quad g_{i}^{*,(1)}=\arg\min_{g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}\mathbb{E}\big[(g_{i}(\tilde{X})-X_{i})^{2}\mid R_{i}=1\big].

Let
f(\tilde{X})=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}\mid\tilde{X}]
denote the true regression function. Then, by the bias-variance decomposition, for any
g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}
:

\mathbb{E}\big[(g_{i}(\tilde{X})-X_{i})^{2}\mid R_{i}=r\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[(g_{i}(\tilde{X})-f(\tilde{X}))^{2}\mid R_{i}=r\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[(X_{i}-f(\tilde{X}))^{2}\mid R_{i}=r\big],\;r\in\{0,1\}

Since
g_{i}^{*,(r)}
minimises the left-hand side over
\mathcal{G}_{i}
, it follows that

\mathbb{E}\big[(g_{i}^{*,(r)}(\tilde{X})-f(\tilde{X}))^{2}\mid R_{i}=r\big]=\varepsilon_{r}^{2},\;r\in\{0,1\}

Now, consider the difference:

\mathbb{E}\big[(g_{i}^{*,(0)}(\tilde{X})-g_{i}^{*,(1)}(\tilde{X}))^{2}\mid R_{i}=0\big]^{1/2}\leq\mathbb{E}\big[(g_{i}^{*,(0)}(\tilde{X})-f_{i}(\tilde{X}))^{2}\mid R_{i}=0\big]^{1/2}+\mathbb{E}\big[(g_{i}^{*,(1)}(\tilde{X})-f_{i}(\tilde{X}))^{2}\mid R_{i}=0\big]^{1/2},

by the triangle inequality in
L^{2}
.

The first term is exactly
\varepsilon_{i,(0)}
. For the second term, use the overlap condition:

\mathbb{E}\big[(g_{i}^{*,(1)}(\tilde{X})-f(\tilde{X}))^{2}\mid R_{i}=0\big]=\int(g^{*,(1)}(\tilde{x})-f(\tilde{x}))^{2}\,dP(\tilde{x}\mid R_{i}=0)\leq\kappa^{-1}\int(g^{*,(1)}(\tilde{x})-f(\tilde{x}))^{2}\,dP(\tilde{x}\mid R_{i}=1)=\kappa^{-1}\varepsilon_{i,(1)}^{2}.

Combining these and squaring both sides gives the stated bound:

\mathbb{E}\big[(g^{*,(0)}(\tilde{X})-g^{*,(1)}(\tilde{X}))^{2}\mid R_{i}=0\big]\leq\big(\varepsilon_{i,(0)}+\kappa^{-1/2}\varepsilon_{i,(1)}\big)^{2}.

∎

Corollary C.2

(Optimisation problems are equivalent for well specified models)
.

Suppose that:

•

f_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}

Then
g^{*,(0)}(\tilde{X})=g^{*,(1)}(\tilde{X})

Proof.

If
f_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}
, then
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{1}=0
. The result follows directly from Lemma
C.1
.

∎

Remark

Lemma
C.1
shows that as the expressive power of the model class
\mathcal{G}_{i}
increases, the approximation errors
\varepsilon_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{1}
decrease, and the population-optimal solutions under
R_{i}=0
and
R_{i}=1
converge. This provides a theoretical explanation for the empirical observation that our method yields larger improvements for simpler model classes, such as linear conditional models, while the gains diminish for more expressive models such as MLPs and random forests, where approximation error is already small.

Lemma C.3

(Equivalence of optimisation problems under MCAR with independent missingness indicators)
.

Suppose that:

•

The data satisfy the MCAR assumption.

•

R_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp R_{j}\mid X_{\mathrm{obs}}
for all
i\neq j
.

Then

g_{i}^{*,(0)}(\tilde{X})=g_{i}^{*,(1)}(\tilde{X}).

Proof.

Under the assumption that
R_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp R_{j}\mid X_{\mathrm{obs}}
for all
i\neq j
, we showed in Section
4.1.2
that the importance weights reduce to

w_{i}(X_{\mathrm{obs}})=\frac{p(X_{\mathrm{obs}}\mid R_{i}=0)}{p(X_{\mathrm{obs}}\mid R_{i}=1)}.

Under the MCAR assumption, the distribution of
X_{\mathrm{obs}}
does not depend on
R_{i}
, and hence

p(X_{\mathrm{obs}}\mid R_{i}=0)=p(X_{\mathrm{obs}}\mid R_{i}=1),

so that
w_{i}(X_{\mathrm{obs}})\equiv 1
almost surely.

Consequently, the objectives
\mathcal{J}_{i}^{(0)}(g)
and
\mathcal{J}_{i}^{(1)}(g)
coincide, and therefore their population minimisers are identical.
∎

Remark

MCAR alone does not imply independence between missingness indicators. The additional assumption

R_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp R_{j}\mid X_{\mathrm{obs}}
ensures that the importance weights depend only on
X_{\mathrm{obs}}
, allowing them to collapse to one under MCAR. This result offers intuition as to why we observed no improvement in performance for
\alpha=0
in Figure
3
