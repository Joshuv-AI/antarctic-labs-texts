Identifying South African marine protected areas at risk from marine heatwaves and cold-spells
Authors: Kira-Lee Courtaillac, Nicola J. van Wilgen, Tamara B. Robinson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Marine heatwaves (MHWs) and marine cold-spells (MCSs) can negatively impact biodiversity as species distributions are largely governed by temperature linked to physiological tolerances. These extremes have not been considered in South Africa’s Marine Protected Area (MPA) network design, so understanding frequency and severity of extreme thermal events will be important for assessing their impact. This study characterises MHWs and MCSs in MPAs across the six South African marine ecoregions, using a novel index to compare thermal event severity. Thermal events declined in duration and intensity from west to east, with the least severe events recorded in the Delagoa ecoregion. Walker Bay MPA was identified as most at risk due to the combined impact of MHWs and MCSs. These thermal events may threaten the ability of the MPA to meet its conservation objective as a cetacean sanctuary. If past trends in MHW frequency and cumulative intensity persist, the majority of South African MPAs could experience more severe heatwaves in the future. Our approach will help prioritise sites for in situ monitoring of water temperature and studies of the impact of extreme thermal events, as well as identifying areas for expanding refugia and conservation corridors, supporting adaptive management into the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32725-0.

Introduction

While increasing trends in ocean temperature and their implications for biota have been considered across many taxa and systems1, event-based thermal studies (i.e. those focused on extreme and discrete thermal anomalies) are less common, especially in marine systems2. This is of concern as extreme events have been shown to exhibit long-term impacts on the dynamics of biological systems3,4. Marine systems, which are characterised by relative thermal stability, may also be particularly vulnerable to climate-change-associated extreme thermal events5. South Africa’s coastline supports diverse marine communities, defined by a cold upwelling system on the west coast6 and a sub-tropical warm current on the east coast7. Thermal extremes could dramatically alter the function and nutrient cycling of these systems. Understanding the frequency and magnitude of these events in different regions is crucial for identifying and responding to impacts.

Hobday et al.8 define a marine heatwave as “a discrete, prolonged, and anomalously warm water event in a particular location”. This framework was extended to marine cold spells by Schlegel et al.9, enabling the quantification of marine heat waves (MHWs) and marine cold-spells (MCSs) in a comparable way. MHWs are now a major focus of interdisciplinary research8 due to their increase in frequency3,10. Many regions worldwide are predicted to be in a near-permanent MHW state by the end of the 21st century, a drastic departure from the historical norm10. MHWs can affect all levels of biological organisation, from genetic11 to ecosystem-wide scales12, with cascading effects observed across diverse coastal ecosystems. For example, kelp deforestation due to MHWs can lead to the formation of urchin barrens and reductions in kelp-associated biodiversity13. Similarly, seagrass ecosystems can undergo long-term community-level changes under MHW conditions, including mass seagrass mortality and declines in the health of associated megafauna14. Coral ecosystems, in turn, can experience either mass bleaching or tropicalisation due to MHWs, both of which alter ecosystem structure15,16. Ultimately, these ecological changes may also influence socio-economic factors, as ecosystem resilience and associated contributions to human communities are affected3. Examples include the collapsing of fish stocks and aquaculture operations3,13.

Marine cold-spells have received less research attention17,18. This is despite their impacts transcending all levels of biological organisation with cumulative effects on invasions19 and food webs18. Additionally, as many marine species are beginning to shift their ranges in response to global climate change20, alterations in upwelling regimes and increased cold-spells in temperate regions can severely impact range-shifting and migratory marine species18,21. Consequently, like MHWs, cold-spells will impact ecosystem services4. However, the full scope of MCS impacts on commercial fisheries and aquaculture has not been thoroughly investigated. Characterising regional trends in MCSs within a framework like that developed by Schlegel et al.9 is crucial for informing stakeholders about otherwise undocumented drivers of ecosystem change17,18. The limited literature on MCS dynamics, their relationship with natural upwelling processes, and the impacts of both phenomena on ecosystems represents a critical research priority.

Marine extreme events along the South African coastline have been characterised at a coarse spatial scale, by comparing the three major coastlines: west, south and east9. However, these coasts are comprised of multiple geographically and ecologically distinct ecoregions, which form the principal biogeographic units used within South Africa’s National Biodiversity Assessments22. Thus, investigating extreme events within ecoregions would provide more spatially refined data to inform the management of the respective ecosystems. Within ecoregions, there is a need to investigate extreme events at the level of Marine Protected Areas (MPAs), to identify which local conservation features need to be prioritised for climate change adaptation strategies, such as developing conservation corridors to climate refugia23. Additionally, as limited work has considered the implications of extreme thermal events at the species or ecosystem level along this coast (but see Fleishman et al.24 and Ackland et al.25) there is a need to identify areas that could be threatened by thermal events so that research efforts can be prioritised.

Marine thermal extremes permeate across MPA borders, and there is an increasing trend in the total area of MPAs being affected by these extremes globally26. MPAs, by definition, are established to protect critical and vulnerable biodiversity, to enhance their resilience and maintain ecosystem functioning and service provision27. Thus, the overarching threat of thermal extremes is large, and the capacity of MPAs to achieve their objectives under such climate change events remains unknown26. By the year 2150, the estimated temperature limits of biological communities within protected areas will be exceeded across all latitudes28. Characterising thermal extremes that impact MPAs will provide crucial insights to inform monitoring programs and management decisions aimed at achieving MPA objectives into the future29,30. Within a South African context this is vital because the coastline has been identified as a regional ‘hotspot’ for marine heatwaves, with events comparable in frequency and intensity to major global occurrences such as the 2003 Mediterranean, 2011 West Australian, and 2012 north-west Atlantic heatwaves8,9.

Consequently, this study aimed to characterise marine thermal extremes along South Africa’s coastline, at the scale of ecoregions and MPAs (Fig. 1). We assessed the occurrence of thermal extreme events over a 40-year period relative to the present-day MPA network. While some of the country’s MPAs date back to the 1980s and before, most were declared in the 2000s, with several declared as recently as 2019. By quantifying relative exposure to MHWs and MCSs, this study provides a foundation for assessing climate-related risk and informing future work on ecological vulnerability, expansion priorities, and adaptive MPA management.

Results

In total, 21 MPAs were large enough to be represented at the quarter-degree spatial resolution of the thermal satellite dataset used (Fig. 1; Table 1). The analyses below describe the occurrence and characteristics of MHWs and MCSs across MPAs and ecoregions over the last forty years.

Marine heatwaves

When standardised by size, Walker Bay MPA experienced the most MHWs (n = 79.0), followed by Agulhas Muds (n = 77.0) and Dwesa-Cwebe (n = 50.5). In contrast, fewest heatwaves occurred in iSimangaliso (n = 5.2) (Table 1).

Following standard practice8,9 the intensity of heat waves was calculated as °C above the 90th percentile value across the climatological distribution for each QDS. The mean intensity of heatwaves differed significantly among ecoregions (GLS; F = 2692.08; df = 5; p < 0.0001) and between areas inside and outside of MPAs (GLS; F = 4.68; df = 1; p = 0.031). While all ecoregions differed significantly (< 0.0001 in all cases; Supplementary Table S1), the Southeast Atlantic Deep Ocean experienced MHWs with the highest mean intensity, while the lowest mean intensity was noted in the Delagoa ecoregion (Supplementary Fig. S1). Notably, marine heatwaves exhibited a lower mean intensity within MPAs compared to those in unprotected areas (coefficient = -0.007; t = -2.16; df = 1; p = 0.030). The mean intensity of heatwaves differed significantly among MPAs in each ecoregion (Supplementary Fig. S1; Supplementary Table S2). MPAs that experienced heatwaves of particularly high mean intensity included Addo Elephant National Park (2.21 ± 0.47 °C; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{Y}$$\end{document} ± s.d.), Southeast Atlantic Seamounts (2.18 ± 0.35 °C), Robben Island (2.02 ± 0.66 °C) and Southwest Indian Seamounts (2.00 ± 0.38 °C). The Southern Benguela portion of Table Mountain National Park and Robben Island MPA experienced the highest maximum mean intensity of any MPAs (4.19 °C for both MPAs).

The duration of MHWs differed significantly among ecoregions (GLM; F = 511.75; df = 5; p < 0.0001) and between areas inside vs. outside of MPAs (GLM; F = 173.64; df = 1; p < 0.0001). Differences among ecoregions were primarily driven by extended heatwaves in the Southeast Atlantic Deep Ocean and Southern Benguela ecoregions, while the shortest MHWs were recorded in the Natal and Delagoa ecoregions on the east coast (Supplementary Fig. S2; Supplementary Table S3). Protection status results indicated that MPAs experienced shorter MHWs than areas without protection (coefficient = -0.093, t=-13.02, p < 0.0001). Further, the duration of MHWs differed significantly among MPAs within all ecoregions (p < 0.001) excepting for the Southern Benguela (Supplementary Table S4). Among MPAs, the longest heatwave (89 days) occurred in Childs Bank MPA (Supplementary Fig. S2; Supplementary Table S5). Walker Bay and De Hoop MPAs experienced the longest average duration (De Hoop: 13.4 ± 10.8 days; Walker Bay: 13.4 ± 10.3 days; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{Y}$$\end{document} ± s.d.). In contrast, Aliwal Shoal experienced the shortest MHW events with heatwaves lasting on average 7.6 ± 3.4 and 7.7 ± 3.4 days in the Southwestern Deep Indian Ocean and Natal ecoregions respectively (Supplementary Table S5).

Cumulative intensity is defined as the sum of daily intensity anomalies over the duration of an event (°C·days), representing the total thermal exposure8,9. Cumulative intensity differed significantly among ecoregions (GLS; F = 1057.60; df = 5; p < 0.0001) and between areas inside and outside of MPAs (GLS; F = 177.90; df = 1; p < 0.0001). Differences among ecoregions reflect a declining trend in the cumulative intensity of MHWs from west to east (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S6). Notably, in the Southeast Atlantic Deep Ocean and the Southern Benguela ecoregions, all MPAs have experienced heatwaves with a cumulative intensity above 100 °C days, which could be a result of either high temperature anomalies over a few days or more moderate anomalies over an extended period. Reflecting the patterns observed in the mean intensity and duration of MHWs, cumulative intensity was lower within MPAs than unprotected areas (coefficient = -1.477; t = -13.34; p < 0.0001). Within all ecoregions, significant differences were observed among MPAs (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S2). Notably, Table Mountain National Park MPA experienced the highest maximum cumulative intensity of any MPA (above 200 °C∙days in the Southern Benguela and Agulhas ecoregions). In the Southwestern Indian Deep Ocean ecoregion, MHWs with the highest cumulative intensity occurred in the Southwest Indian Seamounts and Agulhas Front MPAs, and in the Natal ecoregion, the MPA that experienced the most severe MHWs was Amathole MPA.

Long-term trends in the number of heatwaves experienced and their cumulative intensity varied among MPAs (Table 2). While a number of protected areas, including Walker Bay and Agulhas Muds (which had the highest MHW severity indices – see below) showed no change in these metrics through time, others such as Orange Shelf Edge and Southwest Indian Seamounts have experienced an increase in both measures. Notably, the only MPA demonstrating a declining trend in the number and cumulative intensity of heatwaves was Addo Elephant National Park.

To assess the relative magnitude of thermal exposure among MPAs, a thermal event severity index was derived. This composite index reflects both the frequency and mean cumulative intensity of extreme thermal events, allowing MPAs to be ranked by overall exposure severity. The severity index identified Walker Bay, Agulhas Muds and Robben Island as the MPAs having experienced the most severe MHWs (Table 2). Walker Bay in particular has experienced high numbers of heatwaves with a high mean cumulative intensity, ultimately resulting in a severity index more than double that of most MPAs. In contrast, iSimangaliso MPA experienced few MHWs, and these had a comparatively lower mean cumulative intensity, resulting in this MPA having the lowest severity index.

Marine cold-spells

Southwest Indian Seamounts experienced more MCSs standardised per area than any other MPA (n = 126.3), followed by Agulhas Front (n = 94.7), Walker Bay and Agulhas Muds (both n = 84.0) (Table 1).

The mean intensity of MCSs differed among ecoregions (GLS; F = 1,758.42; df = 5; p < 0.0001) and between protection status (GLS; F = 381.02; df = 1; p < 0.0001). The Agulhas ecoregion experienced MCSs with the greatest (negative) mean intensity (i.e. the coldest and most severe deviation from the climatological mean) and the Delagoa ecoregion on the east coast experienced the least severe cold-spells (Supplementary Fig. S3). In contrast to marine heatwaves, the mean intensity of MCSs was numerically lower in MPAs than in non-protected areas, meaning that, on average, MCSs are more severe within MPAs (coefficient = -0.081, t = -19.52, p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Fig. S3). The mean intensity of cold-spells differed significantly among MPAs in each ecoregion (Supplementary Fig. S3; Supplementary Table S7). MPAs that experienced MCSs with particularly severe mean intensity included Addo Elephant National Park (-2.67 ± 0.75 °C; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{Y}$$\end{document} ± s.d.), Amathole (-2.43 ± 0.76 °C; Agulhas portion), Robben Island (-2.39 ± 0.83 °C), and Table Mountain National Park (-2.28 ± 0.80 °C; Southern Benguela portion)(Supplementary Table S8). Within MPAs, the four MCS events with the most severe mean intensities occurred in Amathole MPA.

The duration of MCSs varied significantly among ecoregions (GLM; F = 226.95; df = 5; p < 0.0001) and between protected and non-protected areas (GLM; F = 10.59; df = 1; p = 0.001). Similarly to MHWs, the longest MCSs occurred in the Southeast Atlantic Deep Ocean ecoregion and the shortest in the Natal ecoregion (Supplementary Fig. S4; Supplementary Table S9). MCSs were shorter within MPAs (coefficient = -0.023; t = -3.25; p = 0.001), and duration differed significantly among MPAs in all ecoregions except for the Southeast Atlantic Deep Ocean and Natal Shelf ecoregions (Supplementary Fig. S4; Supplementary Table S10). The longest cold-spells (101 days in both cases) occurred in Robben Island MPA and the Southern Benguela portion of Table Mountain National Park, followed by a 98-day cold-spell in uThukela Banks MPA in the Natal ecoregion (Supplementary Table S5).

As for MHWs, the cumulative intensity of MCSs differed significantly among ecoregions (GLS; F = 386.11; df = 5; p < 0.0001) and between areas inside and outside of MPAs (GLS; F = 15.55; df = 1; p < 0.0001). Differences among ecoregions reflected the same pattern observed for mean intensity of cold-spells, with the Agulhas ecoregion experiencing the most severe cold events (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S11). Notably, cold-spell events had more severe cumulative intensities within MPAs than in non-protected areas (coefficient = -0.646; t = 3.94; p = 0.0001), likely a reflection of the relatively more severe mean intensity of events within MPAs. Within all ecoregions, significant differences were observed among MPAs (p < 0.0001 in all cases; Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S8), and MPAs across four ecoregions experienced cold-spells with a cumulative intensity below − 200 °C∙days, including TMNP, Amathole, Southwest Indian Seamounts, Agulhas Front, and uThukela Banks.

While four protected areas (Agulhas Muds, Addo, Robben Island, and Agulhas Banks) demonstrated an increasing trend in the number of cold-spells through time, 43% of MPAs have experienced fewer MCSs over time (Table 2). Cumulative intensity of cold-spells remained the same or reduced for most MPAs, with only Walker Bay, Agulhas Front, Robben Island and Table Mountain National Park experiencing worsening cumulative intensity of MCSs through time.

The severity index identified the Southwest Indian Seamounts as the MPA that has experienced the harshest cold-spells, followed by Walker Bay and Agulhas Front MPAs (Table 2). The very high severity indices for these three MPAs reflect both a high frequency of MCSs and low cumulative intensities. Moreover, the severity indices of these protected areas were greater than those calculated for MHWs in any protected area. Notably, while Southwest Indian Seamounts had the highest severity index, both the number of MCSs and the cumulative intensity of these events showed a declining trend. In contrast, Walker Bay and Agulhas Front MPAs have experienced a trend of worsening cumulative intensity. iSimangaliso experienced the fewest MCSs (5.1 events per QDS) and these, on average, had a comparatively low cumulative intensity (-12.29 ± 10.08 °C·days; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{Y}$$\end{document} ± s.d.), resulting in this MPA having the lowest MCS severity index.

Discussion

Understanding climate change impacts on Marine Protected Areas (MPAs) is crucial in an era of growing commitment to marine conservation26,28,31, particularly in context to Target 3 of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, which aims to conserve at least 30% of marine areas by 2030 through ecologically representative and well-connected protected areas. While this study does not quantify ecological responses directly, it provides a spatially explicit foundation upon which conservation practitioners can prioritise MPAs for targeted ecological monitoring and research. Given limited empirical data on species responses to marine thermal extremes in South Africa, our results identify key areas where future studies and adaptive management efforts should be focused.

Variability in marine heatwaves (MHWs) and marine cold-spells (MCSs) was observed among ecoregions and their MPAs. At the ecoregion scale, the western and southern regions experienced the most severe MHWs and MCSs. In contrast, thermal events in the eastern ecoregions of Natal and Delagoa were consistently less severe. These findings align with previous work reporting distinct spatial patterns in MHWs and MCSs9, with South Africa’s west and south coasts facing the most severe thermal events. This spatial pattern translates into differing exposure to thermal extremes across ecoregions and within MPAs, with potential consequences for ecosystems and the services they provide.

The relative exposure of MPAs to extreme thermal events was studied here using a novel thermal event severity index. The index highlighted that if past trends in the number and cumulative intensity of MHWs were to persist, 13 of the 21 South African MPAs examined here (62%) could experience increasingly severe heatwaves. These findings align with global trends in MHW occurrence10, and predictions by the IPCC that the frequency, intensity, spatial extent and duration of MHWs are expected to increase in all African coastal zones32. In contrast, MCS events are declining in severity for the majority of MPAs along the west and east coasts, though seven MPAs exhibit increasing frequency and/or severity of cold-spells. Despite the clear change in the nature of MCSs along the South African coast, it is notable that at present the ecological implications of these changes remain under-researched. Recent evidence suggests that MCSs, manifested as anomalous upwelling events, could result in mass mortality events, even for highly mobile species18. However, cooling associated with upwelling could also create localised and transient climate refugia, which may buffer ecosystems from extreme warming33. In addition, upwelling of cooler water is a key system component along South Africa’s west coast and does not always represent a cold spell34. Consequently, generalisations about the impact of MCSs cannot be made at this time, and the consequences of MCSs need to be more fully researched and carefully contextualised within the oceanographic and biological setting being considered.

On the west coast, where the cool-temperate Benguela system supports productive kelp forests and large seabird and fish populations35, exposure to thermal extremes poses ecological risk. The region is influenced by the cool, northward-flowing Benguela Current, and an eastern boundary upwelling regime, which together create the characteristic conditions of the Southern Benguela ecoregion22. Here, Robben Island and TMNP MPAs stand out as critical sites of concern, as both areas exhibit high severity indices for both MHWs and MCSs, in addition to worsening cumulative intensity trends over time.

Robben Island MPA was established partly to protect a critical breeding colony of the endangered African penguin (Spheniscus demersus), and has demonstrated success through fishery exclusions that improved penguin chick survival by enhancing prey availability36. However, the eastward displacement of key prey species like anchovy (Engraulis encrasicolus) and sardine (Sardinops sagax), potentially correlated with changing environmental conditions, could jeopardise these conservation gains37.

Adjacent to Robben Island, the TMNP MPA presents a unique management challenge due to its spanning the biogeographic break between the Southern Benguela and Agulhas ecoregions on either side of the peninsula, with distinct ecological communities in each22. The two regions are subject to extreme thermal events with differing characteristics. This study recorded more severe MHWs and MCSs on the western side, requiring more detailed assessment of biogeographic effects relevant for management. For example, this MPA preserves critical ecosystem services provided through its kelp forests38. Concerningly, the alien bryozoan Mempranipora membranacea was recently discovered in False Bay39. This encrusting bryozoan, linked to kelp defoliation during warming-driven outbreaks on North America’s east coast40, raises concerns about heatwaves exacerbating its impacts on TMNP kelp forests.

The south coast ecoregions (Agulhas and Southwest Indian Deep Ocean) encompass a highly dynamic environment7 that supports the greatest number of South African marine endemics41 as well as important spawning and nursery grounds42, extending to the abyss of the Southwest Indian Ocean22. MPAs within these two ecoregions exhibit some of the highest indices of MCS severity, highlighting the south coast as a hotspot for cold-spells and an important region for future ecological risk assessments. South coast MPAs with notable thermal extreme dynamics include Walker Bay, Addo, and the offshore MPAs of Southwest Indian Seamounts, Agulhas Front and Agulhas Muds.

The former two offshore MPAs rank within the top three highest severity indices for MCSs, despite being located within the Southwest Indian Deep Ocean ecoregion, which generally experienced relatively less severe events compared to the other southwestern ecoregions. Notably, Agulhas Muds MPA ranked high in severity for both MHWs and MCSs. The risk of impacts to this small MPA, designated to safeguard the critically endangered mudflats ecosystem type within South Africa’s Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ)22, is heightened by exposure to both local thermal extremes and edge effects from inshore trawling43. This cumulative risk increases the likelihood of the MPA failing to achieve its objectives44. Moreover, deep ocean ecosystems remain under-researched, despite their crucial role in safeguarding unique ecosystems, such as seamounts, preventing adequate understanding of their effectiveness, particularly in the face of climate change45.

Walker Bay MPA stands out as an MPA of critical concern, with the highest severity index for both MHWs and MCSs. This MPA serves as a cetacean sanctuary which was established to provide refuge from anthropogenic activities for migrating whale populations46. The severity of extreme events here could alter whale population dynamics, as their distribution, movement and reproduction are known to be affected by climate-driven changes in temperature47. For example, the 2014–2016 Northeast Pacific MHW resulted in decreases in humpback whale reproductive success48.

While cold water upwelling is a feature of the region around Addo MPA49, the increase in number and duration of MCSs suggest potential shifts in local oceanographic processes50, warranting further investigation into the mechanistic drivers. This area also recorded the most severe mean intensities for both MHWs and MCSs. Such thermal extremes could have ecological implications similar to those described for Robben Island MPA, due to its protection of endangered seabird breeding colonies, including the African penguin (S. demersus) and the Cape gannet (Morus capensis)46.

The ecoregions on the east coast, including Delagoa and Natal, generally experienced the least severe extreme events. The iSimangaliso and uThukela MPAs exist within the strong onshore flow of the warm and thermally stable Agulhas Current7, and this constant forcing could potentially be causing thermal events to be advected southwards, preventing the persistence of extreme events9. These two MPAs will likely provide critical climate refugia for biodiversity in this region (although see De Oliveira Soares51), especially sessile taxa like corals that will not be able to escape severe extreme events52. However, despite the comparatively lower cumulative intensity of MHWs in this region, coral bleaching still occurs. For example, coral bleaching was observed within Sodwana Bay in iSimangaliso MPA in the austral summer of 2000, during which 37 MHW events were also recorded (maximum cumulative intensity = 15.32 °C days), although the mechanistic connection between the heatwaves and bleaching events was not investigated53.

Further south along the east coast, Amathole MPA experienced the most severe MHWs within the Natal ecoregion and the most severe MCS cumulative intensities out of all the MPAs in this study. This could be due to the slowing of the Agulhas current as it reaches the Agulhas Bank, reducing the turnover of water layers and allowing for extreme temperatures to persist, especially in this region of persistent upwelling7,9. Amathole was primarily developed to protect reef and pelagic fish54 and the exposure of this MPA to marine thermal extremes threatens this mandate. Recent fish fatalities in this region have been linked to cold shock, highlighting that even mobile species remain vulnerable to the sudden onset of thermal events18.

While the thermal risk index provides a relative indication of MPA exposure to extreme events, a more nuanced approach is required to advance our understanding of local scale conditions. The current study has drawn on a robust approach using forty years of satellite derived data to quantify thermal events. However, despite the great value of satellite derived data, its application in coastal areas can be challenging, with inherent bias and deviations from in situ temperatures of up to 6 °C55. As such, it is recommended that bespoke in situ thermal monitoring programs be initiated in high risk MPAs. This would support a better understanding of absolute temperatures in these areas and inform ecological studies that could assess the impacts of thermal events on taxa of concern10,56. In designing such programs, it will be important to investigate how MHWs and MCSs manifest at depth57. Recent work has highlighted that heatwaves can reach depths of > 800 m58 but how coastal bathymetry and nearshore currents affect extreme thermal events remains unknown. Measurements at depth would also be helpful in improving our understanding of how extreme metrics manifest at ecologically relevant depths within deeper offshore MPAs, where subsurface anomalies may alter exposure to MHWs and MCSs.

As managers can do little to prevent extreme thermal events, novel approaches are essential to address this growing threat31. The findings of this study contribute to evaluating the climate change vulnerability of key South African conservation features, an important process in incorporating climate change adaptation strategies into MPA design, such as through the development of a connected MPA network52. This is crucial because, although recent MPA proclamations have improved representivity, the network still covers only about half of the global 10% target for South Africa’s EEZ and would benefit from greater attention to connectivity and climate resilience43. The approach used in this study provides an important step towards understanding how extreme thermal events manifest along the South African coast. While this study applied the approach in a conservation context, it could also be used to support evidence-based decision-making in fisheries management and climate adaptation.

Materials and methods

Study scope

This study considered extreme thermal events along the South African coast (Fig. 1). The region is both biologically and ecologically diverse, with oceanographic features characteristic of both the Atlantic and Indian oceans59. This is reflected in a thermal gradient from the temperate west coast to the subtropical east coast6,55, with four shelf ecoregions (Southern Benguela, Agulhas, Natal and Delagoa) and two deep ocean ecoregions (Southeast Atlantic Deep Ocean and Southwest Indian Deep Ocean)22. The South African Marine Protected Area (MPA) network consists of 41 onshore and offshore MPAs spread across all ecoregions and protects 5% of South Africa’s Exclusive Economic Zone43,45.

The ecoregions have been described according to fine-scale regional patterns in biogeography, including bathymetric, oceanographic and ecological data in the determination of their boundaries22. Notably, several MPAs fall across multiple ecoregions, protecting variable ecologically important features. For example, Table Mountain National Park MPA exists within two ecoregions (Fig. 1a). Similarly, iSimangaliso MPA occupies the entire Delagoa Shelf, as well as overlapping the Natal and Southwest Indian Deep Ocean ecoregions (Fig. 1b).

Thermal events

Marine heatwaves (MHWs) and marine cold-spells (MCSs) are defined as discrete, prolonged, and anomalously warm/cold events that must have a clear start and end point, be longer than five days and exceed the 90th-percentile (MHW) or fall below the 10th-percentile (MCS) of the climatology, as calculated from daily sea surface temperature data across the 1982–2022 baseline period8,9. Thermal metrics for MHW and MCS events were obtained using the HeatwaveR package60. This approach enabled use of forty years of historic satellite data (January 1982 to April 2022) at quarter degree square (QDS)-scale, in the form of daily Optimally Interpolated Sea Surface Temperature (OISST) data61–63. This dataset incorporates Advanced Very High-Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR) data (i.e. v2.0 data from 1982 to 2015 and the AVHRR-only v2.1 data from 2016 to present day) as well as data from other satellites and various in situ sources (such as ships, buoys, and Argo floats), and compensates for platform differences and sensor biases. While it is recognised that satellite data do not directly reflect absolute in situ temperatures55, the dataset used provides a robust high-resolution alternative to entirely in situ measurements and enables the assessment of spatial and temporal patterns in extreme events (e.g., Schlegel et al.17. This makes it particularly valuable in instances such as the present study where historical in situ temperature data is not available to consider extreme thermal events.

In this study thermal events were associated with ecoregions and MPAs based on shared QDSs (see grid, Fig. 1). A QDS was allocated to an ecoregion if any of its ocean area was occupied by the ecoregion, and consequently some QDSs are allocated to multiple ecoregions. Further, a QDS was allocated to an MPA if ≥ 25% of its area was occupied by the MPA. It is important to note that this approach led to some MPAs being associated with multiple ecoregions, even if they do not entirely span them, due to shared QDSs. For instance, the Dwesa-Cwebe MPA is associated with the Agulhas and Natal Shelf ecoregions as well as the Southeast Indian Deep Ocean ecoregion due to a shared QDS. Table 1 lists MPAs that were large enough to meet this requirement and were thus included in the study. The MPAs not meeting this requirement make up 3.2% of the total marine protected area in South Africa’s EEZ and would require finer scale in situ measurements to provide insight into the occurrence and trends in thermal extremes. Additionally, although QDS grid cells vary slightly in area with latitude, this resolution represents the native scale of the OISST dataset and is consistent with previous regional analyses (e.g., Schlegel et al.9). Because this study focuses on relative temperature-based metrics rather than area-weighted comparisons, this minor variation is deemed unlikely to substantively affect the interpretation of results.

Marine thermal extremes were characterised using key metrics for each MHW and MCS event: mean intensity (mean temperature anomaly in °C above or below the climatological threshold across all days of a single extreme event, reflecting the average severity of the event on any given day), duration (consecutive days exceeding the threshold), and cumulative intensity (the sum of daily intensity anomalies over the duration of the event, reflecting the accumulated effect of temperature). These metrics follow the definitions provided by Hobday et al.8 and Schlegel et al.9. Climatologies and percentile thresholds were calculated for each day of the year using an 11-day moving window across the full 1982–2022 record, as implemented in the heatwaveR package60 following the framework of Hobday et al.8. To facilitate comparisons among protected areas of varying sizes, the number of extreme thermal events within each MPA was standardised by the number of QDSs occupied by the MPA. Building on this, we developed a novel thermal event severity index to enable a ranked assessment of the severity of extreme events experienced by each MPA, i.e., the compound effect of event occurrence and accumulated thermal load. For each protected area, the standardised number of thermal events (MHWs or MCSs) was combined with the absolute value of the mean cumulative intensity of those events using a multiplicative approach:

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were completed in R version 4.1.264. As associated assumptions of normality were met, generalised least-squares (GLS) models (function gls in the package ‘nlme’ v3.1-16265 were used to consider how mean intensity and cumulative intensity of thermal events varied among ecoregions and with protection status (i.e., MPA vs. non-MPA QDSs within each ecoregion). As all ecoregions varied significantly (p < 0.05; see “Results” for details), differences among MPAs were considered per ecoregion using GLS models. Due to overdispersion, generalised linear models (GLM; Quasi-Poisson distribution with log link function; function glm in the package ‘stats’ v4.3.164 were used to consider how the duration of thermal events varied among ecoregions and protected area status. Duration comparisons among MPAs were conducted per ecoregion using the same approach.

After discounting other possible fits, long-term trends in mean annual cumulative intensity and number of extreme events were assessed per MPA using linear models. As these represented time-series analyses, autocorrelation was investigated for each model (function acf, packages ‘purrr’ v1.0.2 and ‘tidyr’ v1.3.166,67. When autocorrelation was detected, it was addressed using the Cochrane-Orcutt approach68, with package ‘orcutt’ v2.369.

Significant trends (increasing severity = red; decreasing severity = green) are indicated by *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001; non-significant trends (no change = orange) are denoted by ‘ns’. Note that due to deviation above the 90th percentile and below the 10th percentile respectively, an increase in cumulative intensity of MHW is indicative of increasing severity, while a decline in cumulative intensity of MCS is indicative of increasing severity.
