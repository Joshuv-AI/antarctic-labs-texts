Peer instruction in preclinical osteopathic microbiology education: a single-group pre–post quasi-experimental study of short-term performance outcomes and student perceptions
Authors: Hari Prasad Nepal, Debra E. Bramblett, Marc Benson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeer instruction (PI) is an active learning strategy grounded on social learning theory that promotes engagement and conceptual understanding through structured peer discussion. Although implemented widely in undergraduate science education, its application in osteopathic medical education remains underexplored.ObjectiveThis study examined associations between PI and short-term performance outcomes as well as student perceptions of PI among first-year osteopathic medical students in a preclinical microbiology course.MethodsA single-group pre-post quasi-experimental study without a control group was conducted simultaneously across both campuses of Burrell College of Osteopathic Medicine during a synchronized single 2-hour session titled ‘Infections that affect the nervous system’ in April 2025. Of 295 eligible students, 218 consented and completed all study components: PI activity involving 10 clinical based questions, a five-question exit quiz, and a post-session feedback survey on students’ perception. The PI activity included individual polling, peer discussion, re-polling, student explanation, and faculty review. Data was analyzed using IBM SPSS (version 30.0).ResultsPost-PI scores (6.57 ± 1.72) were significantly higher than pre-PI scores (4.46 ± 1.75; yielding a mean gain of 2.11 points (95% CI: 1.88–2.34; t(217) = 18.18, p < 0.001) with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.23). Item-level analysis showed significant improvement on 9 of 10 questions (McNemar’s test with Holm–Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons, p < 0.05; improvement range: 9.2%–36.2%). Learning gains did not differ by campus location (Independent samples t-test, p > 0.05). Exit quiz scores (mean = 3.44 ± 1.18) correlated positively with both pre- and post-PI scores (Pearson correlation, r = 0.29 and 0.28; p < 0.001). Survey responses indicated favorable perceptions: 69.7% reported improved understanding, 61% rated the session good or excellent and 54.6% recommended PI for future courses.ConclusionPI was associated with improved short-term performance outcomes and favorable student perceptions in a preclinical osteopathic microbiology course. These findings support the feasibility and potential educational value of PI as a promising active learning strategy in osteopathic medical education. Future research should explore longitudinal outcomes, controlled comparisons, and multi-institutional replication.

Introduction

Active learning strategies have become increasingly important in higher and health professions education in recent decades because of their potential to promote student engagement and deeper conceptual understanding [1]. Although there is no universal definition of active learning, a systematic review describes it as a student-centered strategy that involves student engagement and promotes higher-order thinking [2]. These strategies aim to develop students’ critical thinking skills through the production and articulation of knowledge rather than through passive transmission of facts and ideas. They are based on constructivist theories of learning, which highlight the importance of building connections between the previously acquired knowledge and new experiences and ideas [3].

Peer instruction (PI) is one such active learning strategy which was originally developed by Professor Eric Mazur at Harvard University for undergraduate physics classes [4]. PI is a structured approach in which students first review preparatory materials before class and then independently answer questions on conceptual issues during class, followed by an opportunity to discuss their responses in small groups and revise their answers based on peer feedback. In this method, classmates who possess greater knowledge or abilities act as instructors, supporting and assisting other students with less knowledge or skills [5]. This approach is grounded in social learning theory which emphasizes that learners benefit from observing, modeling, and interacting with peers to refine their understanding [6]. By fostering a collaborative learning environment, PI has been associated with improved qualitative and quantitative problem-solving skills [7].

Although PI was initially developed and widely implemented in physics education, it has since been extensively adopted in biological sciences and other sciences, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) fields [7]. Prior studies have shown that PI is associated with improved understanding of the material and overall performance in undergraduate education [5, 8–10]. Similarly, studies in graduate- and doctoral-level pharmacy programs have also reported positive associations with learning outcomes [11, 12].

Despite the broad and successful use of PI in undergraduate STEM and graduate pharmacy education, its application in medical education is limited. Existing studies from allopathic medical education have reported that it is associated with enhancing student learning and strengthening their overall performance [13–15]. However, its implementation in osteopathic medical education has not been adequately investigated. Therefore, this study examined the application of a structured peer-instruction approach, an established active learning strategy, within a single microbiology instructional session in the context of osteopathic medical education and evaluated its association with short-term performance outcomes and student perceptions among first-year students. The primary objective of this study was to compare student performance between pre- and post-PI questions administered during the session. The secondary objectives included a) analyzing whether student performance gains differed based on campus location, b) evaluating the correlation between exit quiz scores with pre-and post-PI scores, and c) assessing student perceptions of the peer instruction modality as a learning strategy.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a single-group pre-post quasi-experimental design without a control group or randomization. The study was conducted in April 2025 at Burrell College of Osteopathic Medicine which operates two campuses: one in Las Cruces, New Mexico and the other one in Melbourne, Florida. Both campuses follow an identical, centrally coordinated curriculum with the same learning objectives, instructional materials, learning management system, instructional approaches, and assessment structure. The same faculty oversee or teach students at both sites simultaneously through synchronous online instruction. Standard classroom procedures routinely implemented across both campuses were maintained during the study session. However, for the purpose of this study, PI was implemented for the first time at the institution as a formal teaching-learning strategy in place of the conventional didactic lecture format.

Participants

All first-year osteopathic medical students (class of 2028; n = 295) were invited to participate. Of these, 199 students were matriculated at the Las Cruces, New Mexico campus and 96 at the Melbourne, Florida campus. Students at both campuses were enrolled through Burrell College of Osteopathic Medicine and belonged to the same cohort.

Teaching session to implement PI

The designated PI session was a 2-hour session titled ‘Infections that affect the nervous system’, which was a part of the Nervous System I course for first-year students. The session was delivered synchronously across both campuses, ensuring consistency in instructional timing, content delivery, polling procedures, peer discussion format and facilitation. Important bacterial, viral, and parasitic pathogens covered earlier in the curriculum were reviewed in the session and clinical application of basic science concepts were emphasized.

Preparatory materials

Five days prior to the classroom sessions, students were provided with relevant study materials such as lecture PowerPoint slides, summary tables and book chapters to prepare for the content to be covered in the PI session. Students also received instructions regarding the expectations of the preparation and an overview of the planned PI activities.

Activities employed during the classroom session

A)Peer instruction (PI): The PI approach in this study followed the methodology described by Parmelee et al [16], which represents a slightly modified version of the original PI described by Eric Mazur [4]. During the classroom sessions, each of the 10 multiple-choice questions was presented on the PowerPoint (Microsoft Corporation, 2025) slide and the Point Solutions (Echo360, 2025) was used as the polling platform. The following steps were implemented for each question:First Poll and individual student response (Pre-PI): Students answered the question individually within the given time of 1 minute 20 seconds. Results were not displayed to the class. Peer discussion and second poll response (Post-PI): Following the individual response, students discussed their answers and relevant concepts with their peers (either in pairs or small groups) and resubmitted their responses to the same question, rather than to novel assessment items, based on peer insights within the allocated time period of 2 minutes. No peer leaders or student discussants were pre-selected prior to the session. Instead, all students were encouraged to actively participate in collaborative discussions, share their reasoning, ask questions, and learn from one another during the activity. The comparative results of Pre-PI and Post-PI polls were then shared with the class. Student explanation: Students were invited to volunteer to explain their reasoning for their answers for 1-2 minutes. The volunteer student addressed any "Why" and "How" type of questions raised by the faculty.Faculty review: The faculty provided a brief explanation of the correct answer and underlying concepts using teaching slides for about 2-3 minutes.B)Exit quiz: After completion of PI activities, students individually completed the 5-question exit quiz within 7 minutes via an online platform called Learning Catalytic (Pearson Education, Inc, 2025). The quiz tested the concepts discussed earlier during the PI activities. Although the quiz was not a formal component of PI method, it was administered as a separate post-session assessment to evaluate immediate conceptual understanding of the material rather than long-term retention. Additionally, the exit quiz scores were used to examine associations with both pre-and post-PI scores. C)Post-session feedback survey: At the end of the session, students were asked to complete the feedback survey regarding their perception of PI session and their views on inclusion of an exit quiz for summative assessment. Students who were unable to submit the responses in class were allowed an additional one week to submit their responses.

Statistical analysis

All data were entered into Microsoft excel (Microsoft Corporation, 2025) and data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 30.0 (IBM Corporation, 2025). Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations whereas categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated where appropriate.

The distribution of scores was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots [17]. Although skewness and kurtosis indicated minor deviations from normality (z-values within ± 3.46), the sample size was sufficiently large to justify the use of parametric tests. Paired-samples t-tests compared overall pre- and post-peer instruction scores. Effect sizes for paired comparisons were calculated by Cohen’s d for paired samples by dividing the mean pre-post difference scores by the standard deviation of the difference scores. Thus, the reported effect size reflects within-subject change, rather than an effect size for independent group. Item-level performance differences between pre-and post- PI scores were analyzed by McNemar test with Holm-Boneferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons and reduce the risk of Type I error [18]. Differences in gain scores across campus location were assessed using independent samples t-tests.

Pearson correlation analyses were performed to examine the associations between exit quiz scores and both pre- and post- PI scores. The internal consistency of the Likert-scale feedback survey was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a reliability coefficient of 0.84, indicating good internal consistency among the survey items. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to explore the relationships between student perceptions and gain scores [19]. A two-tailed p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant flow and analytical cohort

Of 295 invited students, 289 attended the instructional session. A total of 233 students provided informed consent to participate in the student. Fifteen students were excluded due to incomplete participation across required study components. Therefore, the final analytical cohort consisted of 218 students (Figure 1).

Participant characteristics

The participants included 151 (69.3%) from the Las Cruces campus and 67 (30.7%) from the Melbourne campus. Most participants (183; 83.9%) were under 30 years of age; whereas 33 (15.1%) were between 30 and 40 years, and 2 (0.9%) were between 40 and 50 years of age. Regarding gender distribution, 145 (66.5%) identified as female, 70 (32.1%) as male, and 3 (1.4%) as other gender.

Pre- and post- PI score comparisons

A paired samples t-test revealed a statistically significant improvement in student performance score following peer discussion. Out of a maximum possible score of 10, the mean pre-PI score was 4.46 ± 1.75 (44.6 ± 17.5%), which increased to 6.57 ± 1.72 (65.7 ± 17.2%) post-PI, yielding a mean gain of 2.11 points [21.1%; 95% CI: 1.88–2.34; t(217) = 18.18, p < 0.001] (Table 1).

Exit quiz scores and their correlation with both pre- PI and post- PI scores

The mean exit quiz score was 3.44 ± 1.18 (68.8 ± 23.6%) out of a maximum possible score of 5. Pearson correlation analyses showed moderate positive associations between exit quiz scores and both pre-PI scores (r = 0.29, p < 0.001) and post-PI scores (r = 0.28, P < 0.001). In other words, students who performed better before as well as after PI generally scored higher on the exit quiz.

Comparison of student perceptions with the gain scores

A majority of students (69.7%; consisting of 53.2% agreeing and 16.5% strongly agreeing) reported that PI improved their understanding of the material. Regarding instructional preferences, 48.1% favored PI over traditional lectures, while 31.2% considered both methods equally effective. Additionally, 61% of respondents rated the session as good or excellent, and 54.6% stated that they would recommend PI as a future teaching and learning strategy. However, student opinions were mixed regarding the incorporation of the exit quiz’s score into their formal course grading or final summative assessment. Only 31.6% agreed or strongly agreed with this proposition, whereas 38.5% either disagreed or strongly disagreed, and 29.8% remained neutral.

While comparing the student perceptions with gain scores, no statistically significant association was observed between perception categories and gain scores across any of the five items assessed (all p > 0.05). This finding suggests that students’ subjective evaluation of the session did not necessarily align with their measured learning gains.

Discussion

This study shows that PI, when implemented within a structured active learning framework, was associated with significant short-term improvement in performance outcomes among first-year osteopathic medical students in medical microbiology education. The significant improvement in post-PI performance score, along with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.23) highlights the instructional value of learner-centered, discussion-oriented learning approaches. These findings are consistent with the principles of Bandura’s social learning theory, where observation, modeling, and social interaction promote cognitive restructuring and self-efficacy [6]. However, since the same question items were used before and after peer discussion, part of the observed improvement may reflect repeated exposure and immediate answer revision during discussion. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as improved immediate performance on repeated items following peer discussion.

The learning gains reported in the present single-session study are aligned with a substantial body of evidence supporting the use of PI across STEM disciplines [8–10], pharmacy [11, 12] and veterinary medical education [20], where PI has been associated with conceptual understanding and active engagement across diverse educational settings. Similarly, meta-analytic evidence suggests that active learning strategies including PI are generally associated with positive academic outcomes across multiple educational disciplines and instructional contexts [5, 21]. Within the context of medical education, evidence remains relatively limited. A few studies in allopathic medical programs, however, have shown that PI contributes to improved student performance [13, 14]. The present findings extend the body of literature by further supporting the feasibility and potential educational value of PI within osteopathic curriculum, the area in which empirical evidence remains sparse. Accordingly, this study contributes to addressing a notable gap in educational literature.

An important feature of the present intervention was the use of higher order, clinically oriented, NBOME style questions that required application of foundational microbiological concepts to the clinical scenarios, rather than factual recall of the material. Such questions may promote deeper conceptual processing by encouraging students to integrate basic science knowledge with clinical reasoning and diagnostic interpretation [22]. In preclinical education, students may sometimes struggle to recognize the clinical relevance of foundational science concepts when the content is delivered primarily through traditional lecture-based approaches, are often passive and insufficiently integrated with clinical application [23]. Incorporation of the clinically oriented questions within the PI framework may therefore help students contextualize the basic or foundational science material to the patient centered scenarios, potentially enhancing engagement, perceived relevance and application of knowledge. Exposure to clinically oriented questions may also help students practice the reasoning needed for coursework and board examinations, although this study did not directly evaluate the examination performance or long-term retention outcomes [24].

The instructional session in the present study, broadly called PI, implemented not only the peer discussion alone, but also represented a structured multi-component active learning sequence. The PI process incorporated individual response, peer discussion, re-polling, student explanation, and faculty explanation. This integration aligns with prior literature suggesting that structured peer interaction combined with instructor guided clarification yields greater learning gains than either approach alone [16, 25]. However, since the broader session incorporated multiple interactive components, the independent contribution of each component to overall learning outcomes could not be fully isolated within this study design.

Item level analysis revealing significant improvement on 9 of the 10 PI questions in this study indicated that observed learning gains were not confined to isolated concepts but they were broadly distributed across multiple concepts including bacteriology, virology and parasitology within the neuroinfectious disease content. Rather than mastering individual questions in isolation, participants appeared to develop more integrated framework, reflecting meaningful engagement with the material, consistent with the findings from prior studies [13, 15]. The only question that did not show statistical improvement may have involved greater conceptual complexity or limited cues in the question stem which required higher-order clinical reasoning. It is possible that a brief discussion was not enough to fully resolve the conceptual challenges associated with this item. This finding suggests that short-term learning gains associated with PI may vary based on the cognitive complexity or question design.

Beyond performance gains, peer discussion may provide a multitude of cognitive and metacognitive benefits. Prior literature suggests that the discussion provides prompts to articulate their reasoning, supports collaborative knowledge construction and deeper conceptual processing, helps identify and address misconceptions, and may also foster metacognitive awareness, confidence and self-efficacy [8, 26, 27]. Collaborative discussion may additionally contribute to the “hidden curriculum” of medical education by implicitly promoting teamwork, empathy, professionalism, effective communication, and collaborative problem-solving skills that are essential for clinical practice [28]. However, these potential benefits were not directly evaluated in the present study.

The absence of statistically significant differences in pre-PI, post PI or gain scores between two campuses in this study suggests comparable short term learning improvements across campuses. Though the findings support the feasibility of implementing PI within geographically distributed curriculum, the campus-wise comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because unmeasured contextual or site level differences may remain.

The moderate positive correlations between exit quiz performance and both pre-and post PI scores suggest that both students’ baseline knowledge and immediate post-discussion performance were associated with short-term post-session conceptual understanding. Learners with stronger initial background or understanding and those who improved during PI generally scored higher on the exit quiz, which indicates that PI may reinforce existing knowledge while consolidating new concepts. These findings are consistent with the prior evidence demonstrating that PI enhances conceptual understanding and student performance across STEM and medical education contexts [4, 8–10, 13, 29]. However, since the exit quiz was administered immediately following the session in the present study and consisted of only five items, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of long-term retention or comprehensive mastery of the contents covered.

Student perceptions in the present study were generally favorable: 69.7% reported improved understanding, 61% rated the PI session positively, 48.1% favored PI over traditional lectures and 54.6% indicated they would recommend PI as a future teaching and learning strategy. These results align with existing literature highlighting the positive reception of PI, with students often describing such sessions as engaging, enjoyable, and motivating [27]. However, student perceptions in the present study should be interpreted as immediate reactions to a single instructional session and should not be assumed to reflect long-term educational impact or learning outcomes. Reactions were more mixed concerning incorporation of post-session exit quiz scores into final course grading- only 31.6% of students responded favorably to the inclusion of the exit quiz in summative grading. This hesitancy suggests that most learners perceive active learning strategies like PI as formative experiences rather than components of high-stakes evaluation. Student perceptions in this study might also have been influenced by the fact that these students did not have considerable exposure to PI in other courses or across broad curriculum, which may have limited their opportunity to develop familiarity with this instructional approach over time.

Interestingly, no significant association was observed in this study between students’ perceived effectiveness of PI and their measured learning gains, consistent with prior educational literature demonstrating that subjective perceptions may not necessarily align with objective learning outcomes [30]. Therefore, clear and transparent communication about the purpose, expectations and short- or long-term benefits of active learning strategies such as PI within the curriculum is very crucial for fostering student commitment and engagement.

Limitations

Although the study shows promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, the PI intervention was conducted in a single 2-hour session, limiting the ability to assess cumulative educational effects or long-term retention of the knowledge within a broader curriculum.

Second, this study employed single-group pre-post quasi-experimental design without a control group receiving traditional or lecture-based instruction. Consequently, definitive causal conclusions cannot be established, and because identical question items were used before and after peer discussion, the observed improvements in post-PI scores may reflect multiple contributing factors beyond peer interaction alone, including repeated exposure to content, testing familiarity or immediate answer revision rather than broader transfer of learning.

Third, student preparation prior to the session was not formally assessed or monitored and may have acted as an unmeasured confounder influencing pre-PI performance, peer discussion quality, post-PI scores, and exit quiz performance.

Fourth, the exit quiz in this study consisted only of five questions and was administered immediately following the instructional session. Although intended to assess immediate conceptual understanding, the limited number of questions and short assessment interval may not reflect the depth of content comprehension and long-term retention.

Fifth, although the instructional delivery was standardized across both campuses, unmeasured contextual or site-level differences may still have influenced student learning experiences. In addition, the campus comparisons were carried out based primarily on subgroup analyses of pre-PI, post-PI and gain scores within a single instructional session. More advanced repeated measures or mixed-effects modelling approaches may provide a more robust analytical framework for evaluating clustered or longitudinal educational data in future studies involving multiple institutions.

Sixth, inclusion of only students who completed all study components may have introduced selection or completion bias, as these students may have differed systematically from excluded students in terms of motivation, preparedness, attendance or engagement.

Finally, the present study relied primarily on quantitative measures of learning and perception, inclusion of qualitative approaches such as focus groups or interviews could provide deeper insights into participating students’ experiences.

Conclusion

This study suggests that the PI approach was associated with improved short-term performance outcomes among first-year osteopathic medical students in microbiology education. Improvements in post-PI scores across most question items, positive correlations with exit quiz performance, comparable learning gains across campuses, and generally favorable student perceptions collectively support the feasibility and potential educational value of PI as a learner-centered active learning strategy within osteopathic medical education. However, these findings should be interpreted in light of the study’s methodological limitations and should not be considered evidence of long-term learning outcomes or retention.

Future studies should incorporate controlled study design, novel or parallel items for peer discussion and delayed post-testing to better evaluate the broader learning transfer and retention. Additional research should also explore the longitudinal impact of repeated PI implementation, its effect on overall academic achievement, including results on standardized tests (e.g., COMLEX-USA, USMLE), and include qualitative perspectives to enhance comprehension of the student learning experience.
