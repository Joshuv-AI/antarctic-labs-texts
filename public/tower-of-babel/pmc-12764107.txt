Anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory effects of D-limonene in improving liver cirrhosis induced by bile duct ligation in male rat
Authors: Susan Sabbagh, Zahra Ganjirad, Leila Jafaripour, Reza Norouzirad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and aimLiver fibrosis is one of the pathological consequences of several liver diseases that can lead to liver cirrhosis. Some anti-fibrotic drugs for liver fibrosis have not yet been clinically approved. The current study assesses the antioxidant properties of D-limonene (DL) on cholestatic liver damage induced by bile duct ligation (BDL) in rats.Experimental approachFor this purpose, 36 male rats were randomly divided into six equal groups: Sham, BDL, BDL + 50 DL, BDL + 100 DL, 50 DL, and 100 DL. The rats underwent BDL surgery and were treated with DL for a duration of 28 days. Serum liver enzymes were measured, and liver tissue was examined for histopathological, biochemical, and gene expression studies.Key findingsTreatment with DL, administered in two doses of 50 and 100 mg/kg, resulted in decreased levels of Alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total bilirubin, and γ-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT). Additionally, it reduced the expression of caspase-3, TNF-alpha, and malondialdehyde, leading to a decrease in liver cell damage and liver fibrosis. Moreover, DL was found to increase the activity of antioxidant enzymes and glutathione levels in the BDL model.ConclusionsThe results of this study suggest that DL could be considered a potential candidate for protecting the liver against cholestatic damage due to its anti-fibrotic and antioxidant properties.

Introduction

Cholestasis can occur for various reasons, including pregnancy, genetic disorders, gallstones, autoimmune diseases, and malignancies, and can lead to bile flow disorders. Long-term cholestasis may result in tissue fibrosis and severe cirrhosis. In this regard, the liver is the first organ to be affected by cholestasis and to develop liver cirrhosis [1]. Liver fibrosis is characterized by excessive deposition of collagen fibers in the extracellular matrix (ECM). This health issue causes liver dysfunction, which may lead to cirrhosis and liver carcinoma [2]. Excessive ECM accumulation occurs following the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs). In the normal liver, HSCs reside in the disse space and are primary responsible for storing vitamin A. However, when the liver is damaged, HSCs become activated, transform into myofibroblast-like cells, and begin significant amounts of collagen fibers into the ECM [3]. High concentrations of bile acids can cause liver inflammation in addition to directly damaging the liver and including apoptosis in liver cells through various bile acid receptors [4]. Oxidative stress, resulting from an imbalance between oxidative and antioxidant systems, is the main characteristic of cholestasis. This stress increases lipid peroxidation or malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in the liver, plasma, kidneys, brain, and heart of laboratory rats 24 h after bile duct ligation (BDL) [5]. Inflammation is a characteristic of cholestatic liver damage caused by BDL. Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) is a pro-inflammatory cytokine that can lead to liver inflammation and fibrosis [6].

Despite the great advances in modern medicine, effective agents for treating liver diseases have not yet been fully identified. Therefore, it is necessary to identify alternative drugs for preventing and treating liver diseases [7]. Currently, ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) is an FDA-approved drug for treating complications of cholestasis and liver fibrosis. However, UDCA may cause several side effects, such as diarrhea, nausea, and urticaria [2]. In this regard, the most effective treatment for patients with advanced liver cirrhosis is liver transplantation, which is very expensive and has limitations. Hence, it seems necessary to accelerate the production of anti-fibrotic drugs for the liver [8]. Consuming of medicinal plants, vegetables, and fruits can promote health due to their biological and medicinal properties. Herbal extracts or herbal compounds can also be utilized as substitutes or auxiliary supplementary supplements in conjection along with conventional drugs for prevention or treatment [7]. Vitamin C, Vitamin E and silymarin can reduce liver fibrosis that occurs after hepatic cholestasis [9, 10].

D-limonene (DL) or 4-isopropenyl-1-methylcyclohexene (C10H16) is monocyclic monoterpene, and main component of citrus oils found in fruits like orange, lemon, mandarin, lime, and grapefruit [11]. DL has beneficial effects such as antioxidant, antidiabetic, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, cardioprotective, digestive protective, liver protective, immune-modulatory, anti-fibrotic, and anti-genotoxic properties [12]. This compound has also been used to dissolve gallstones that contain cholesterol [11]. Therefore, this study aims to determine the anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic properties of DL in a liver cirrhosis model caused by BDL in male rats. this study involve histological, biochemical, and molecular investigations of liver tissue.

Materials and methods

Chemical

D-Limonene (CAS-No: 138-86-3) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH, in Germany. The SYBR Green qPCR Mastermix, RNA extraction kit, and cDNA synthesis kit were obtained from AnaCell, in Iran. Glutathione, glutathione peroxidase, catalase, and malondialdehyde assay kits were obtained from Navand Salamat Company, in Iran.

Animal studies

In this study, 36 male Wistar rats, aged 12 weeks and weighing between 220 and 250 g, were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Breeding Center of Dezful University of Medical Sciences, Iran. The animals were kept under normal conditions with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle and had free access to food and water throughout the experimental period. All experimental protocols involving animals in the this study were performed following the relevant guidelines and regulations outlined in the ARRIVE guidelines [13]. All animal studies were approved by the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals Committee of Dezful University of Medical Sciences, Iran (ethical number: IR.DUMS.AEC.1401.005). All experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of Dezful University of Medical Sciences.

After admission for 1 week, the rats were randomly divided into 6 groups:

1) Sham group: Rats underwent laparotomy without BDL surgery.

2) BDL group: BDL surgery was performed on the rats.

3) BDL + 50 DL group: BDL + oral intake of 50 mg/kg DL [14].

4) BDL + 100 DL group: BDL + oral intake of 100 mg/kg DL [15].

5) 50 DL group: Oral intake of 50 mg/kg DL.

6) 100 DL group: Oral intake of 100 mg/kg DL.

After undergoing BDL surgery, DL was dissolved in normal saline and administrated daily for four weeks through gavage into animals.

BDL surgery

Animals were anesthetized with 50 mg/kg ketamine and 5 mg/kg xylazine. The abdomen was shaved, cleaned, and disinfected with alcohol. A midline incision was made. The common bile duct was carefully separated from the surrounding tissues, ligated in two places with 4 − 0 silk thread, and cut between the two ligatures. Then, the abdominal muscles were sutured with chromic thread, and the skin was sutured with nylon. The yellow color of the plasma and the increase in the ratio of liver weight to body weight of rats were among the determining factors for a successful BDL surgery [16, 17].

Bodyweight

The body weight of the rats was measured both before and after 28 days after surgery.

Biochemical parameters

Euthanasia was performed 28 days after surgery. The animals were euthanized under deep anesthesia using 50 mg/kg of ketamine and 5 mg/kg of xylazine. Blood was collected from the heart ventricle of anesthetized rats. The blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 g for 10 min, and serum were kept at -80℃ until analysis. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total bilirubin, and γ-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT) levels were measured using the by BT 1000 autoanalyzer, and Pars Azmoon commercial kits (kit count: P.L:97203232).

The livers of the animals were promptly removed after deep anesthesia and stored in a refrigerator at -80 until homogenization. The homogenized tissue was then mixed with PBS at a ratio of 1:10. After that, it was centrifuged at 12,000 g at 4 °C for 15 min and the supernatant was used to measure parameters such as malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH), catalase (CAT) and glutathione peroxidase (GPX).

In the present study, the parameters of MDA (code: NS-15022), GSH (NS-15087), GPX (NS-15082), and CAT (NS-15052) were measured using a commercial kit (Navand Salamat Company, Uremia, Iran) following the manufacturer’s instructions [18].

Liver histopathological changes

A small piece of liver tissue was immersed in 10% formalin. The liver tissue processing involved dehydration with increasing degrees of alcohol (50%, 70%, 80%, and 96%) and clearing with xylene. Then, the tissue was embedded in paraffin. Slices (4 μm) were prepared and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome following a previous study [17]. In the current research, the liver tissue was examined using a light microscope to access for fibrosis, necrosis of liver cells, inflammation, and proliferation of bile ducts. Each visual field was given a score based on the following criteria: no damage (= 0), damage less than 25% (= 1), damage between 25 and 50% (= 2), and damage more than 50% (= 3) [19].

Real-time PCR analysis

Real-time (RT) PCR technique was used to assess the expression level of TNF-α and caspase-3 in liver tissue. This procedure was carried out in accordance with a previous study [17]. In this process, liver RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (AnaCell, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, cDNA was synthesized using the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative PCR was carried out using specific primers and Mastermix (AnaCell, Iran). The results were analyzed using Rotor-Gene software according to the 2-∆∆CT formula. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 16.0. Also, GraphPad Prism software version 8 was utilized for creating graphs. Initially, the normality of the data was assessed through the Shapiro-Wilk test. Then, a one-way ANOVA test was employed for data analysis. Additionally, Tukey’s test was performed to compare between two groups. A p value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. Data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Result

Effect of DL on body weight changes in rats following hepatotoxicity induced by BDL

There was no significant difference in the body weight of rats before treatment or BDL in different groups (p > 0.05) (Table 2). In the present study, the body weight of rats after BDL in the BDL group showed a significant decrease compared to the sham group (p < 0.0001). The DL treatment increased the weight of animals in the BDL + 50 DL and BDL + 100 DL groups compared to the BDL group, but this difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). However, the body weight of animals after treatment with DL in the 50 DL and 100 DL groups showed a significant increase compared to the BDL group (p < 0.0001). The weight of animals in these two groups was similar to the sham group, indicating that DL did not cause weight gain in animals (p > 0.05) (Table 2). In the current study, the BDL group showed a decrease in the percentage of change in body weight compared to the sham group (p < 0.0001). Treatment with DL in the BDL + 100 DL, 50 DL, and 100 DL groups prevented weight loss compared to the BDL group (p < 0.05). Furthermore, there was no significant difference in the percentage of change in body weight of animals in the 50 DL, and 100 DL groups compared to the sham group (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Effect of DL on serum liver function parameters following hepatotoxicity induced by BDL

Serum AST, ALT, and ALP levels in the BDL group showed a significant increase compared to the sham group (p < 0.0001). DL was able to reduce serum AST, ALT, and ALP levels in the BDL groups (p < 0.0001). Serum AST, ALT, and ALP levels in the 50 DL and 100 DL groups were similar to those in the sham group, and no statistically significant difference was observed (p > 0.05) (Fig. 1).

BDL increased serum levels of GGT, total bilirubin, and direct bilirubin, which were significantly different from the sham group (p < 0.0001). Animals in BDL group that received 50 mg/kg DL showed a significant decrease in GGT and total bilirubin levels compared to the BDL group (p < 0.0001). However, DL in the BDL + 100 DL group, Although DL caused a decrease in GGT and total bilirubin levels, this decrease was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). DL also caused a decrease in serum direct bilirubin levels, which was significantly different from the BDL group (p < 0.0001). Serum GGT, total bilirubin, and direct bilirubin levels in the 50 DL and 100 DL groups were similar to the sham group, and no significant differences were observed between these groups (p > 0.05) (Fig. 1).

Effect of DL on hepatic oxidative stress biomarkers following hepatotoxicity induced by BDL

In our study, liver MDA levels in the BDL group showed a significant increase compared to the sham group (p < 0.0001). Treatment with DL resulted in a significant decrease in MDA levels after BDL (p < 0.05). The MDA levels in the 50 DL and 100 DL groups were similar to those in the sham group, with no statistically significant difference was observed (p > 0.05) (Table 3).

BDL led to in a significant decrease in liver GSH levels compared to the sham group (p < 0.0001). However, treatment with DL was able to increase GSH levels in the BDL groups (p < 0.05). Additionally, DL increased liver GSH levels in all groups except the BDL + 50 DL group, which reached levels close to those of the sham group (Table 3).

In the present study, liver GPX activity in the BDL group showed a significant decrease compared to the sham group (p < 0.0001). Receiving DL resulted in a significant increase in liver GPX activity in the BDL + 100 DL, 50 DL, and 100 DL groups after BDL (p < 0.0001). However, receiving DL increased liver GPX activity in all groups except the BDL + 50 DL group, which reached levels close to those of the sham group (Table 3).

The BDL group showed a significant decrease in liver CAT activity compared to the sham group (p < 0.0001). Liver CAT activity of the DL-receiving groups showed a significant increase compared to the BDL group (p < 0.0001). Additionally, DL increased liver CAT activity in all groups, reaching levels close to those of the sham group (p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Effect of DL on the histopathological changes following hepatotoxicity induced by BDL

Histopathological changes are shown in Figs. 2 and 3.

Hepatocyte necrosis in the BDL group exhibited a significant increase compared to the sham group (p < 0.0001). The hepatocyte necrosis in the DL-receiving groups demonstrated a significant reduce compared to the BDL group (p < 0.0001). However, DL was unable to decrease hepatocyte necrosis in the BDL + 50 DL and BDL + 100 DL groups to the same level as the sham group (p < 0.05) (Table 4).

Discussion

In the present study, BDL led to an increase in serum levels of AST, ALT, ALP, GGT, and bilirubin levels. Additionally, MDA levels, caspase-3, and TNF-alpha gene expression were found to increase 28 days after BDL surgery, while the activity of antioxidant enzymes decreased. Liver necrosis, bile duct proliferation, and liver fibrosis also showed an increase. The accumulation of bile acids in the liver has cytotoxic effects causing mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, inflammation, bile duct proliferation, apoptosis, and necrosis of liver cells as well as fibrosis [7]. Decreased functioning of bile cells lead to increased proliferation of damaged cholangiocytes. Additionally, irritation and damage to the bile ducts result in severe inflammation, which hinders liver regeneration and promotes fibrosis. Therefore, inhibiting the of hepatic ductal response may reduce inflammation and fibrosis caused by cholestasis [20]. Damage to Hepatocytes during cholestasis lead to elevated levels of in ALT, ALP, AST and total bilirubin indicating changes in the metabolism of bile acids in the liver [5]. However, the destruction of hepatocytes and obstruction of the common bile duct during BDL cause an increase in total bilirubin levels [5].

In the present study, treatment with DL at two doses of 50 and 100 mg/kg for 4 weeks resulted in a reduction in oxidative stress levels by enhancing the activity of antioxidants such as GSH, GPX, and CAT. A study similar to ours showed that DL can boost the activity of antioxidant enzymes (such as CAT and SOD) in the liver and kidney tissues of diabetic animals while also reducing oxidative stress [14]. After oxidative stress is reduced, the level of lipid peroxidation decreases, resulting in lower MDA levels [5]. In addition, limonene, with its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, improves liver and pancreas function against metabolic syndrome associated with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [21]. Our study aligns with previous research showing that DL effectively reduces liver damage caused by hepatic cholestasis such as necrosis and liver fibrosis, by reducing oxidative stress and inflammation. Therefore, DL can prevent liver damage [15, 21].

The findings of our study showed that d-limonene reduces inflammation after BDL by reducing the TNF-α expression. DL can also inhibit inflammation doxorubicin-induced renal damage [22]. D-limonene possesses effective anti-inflammatory properties that can help in prevent and inhibit damage to the respiratory system [23]. DL is effective in improving kidney diseases. For example, this antioxidant when administered at a dose of 100 mg/kg for 12 days reduces renal damage caused by gentamicin nephrotoxicity. This is due to its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, which align with our study findings [24]. Additionally, DL as a plant antioxidant helps reduce diabetic nephropathy [25]. Administration of DL at a dose of 200 mg/kg improves CCl4-induced cardiac injury by utilizing its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [26]. Several studies have reported the hepatoprotective properties of Dl. Specifically, D-limonene has been shown to improve non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [27]. Due to its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, DL reduced liver damage caused by chronic immobility at a dose of 10 mg/kg for 21 days. This finding was consistent with our study [28]. In contrast to our study, another study reported that chronic treatment with DL at a dose of 75 mg/kg for over 30 days resulted in damage to the liver parenchyma and matrix [29]. In addition, in another study, found that DL, administered in two doses of 100 and 200 mg/kg for 4 weeks exhibited hepatoprotective properties [15]. In our study, DL administered at two doses of 50 and 100 mg/kg for 4 weeks showed a protective effect on the liver. Therefore, prolonging the duration of DL administration may lead to toxicity.

Studies have shown that DL reduces CCL4-induced hepatotoxicity because of its potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-fibrotic activities [15]. The present study showed that DL administered in two doses of 50 and 100 mg/kg reduces the expression of the caspase-3 gene in hepatic cholestasis induced by BDL. this indicates that DL possesses anti-apoptotic properties, which help in decreasing tissue damage [30]. According to our study, it has been shown that reducing hepatic necrosis and fibrosis during hepatic cholestasis can decrease bile acids and oxidative stress. DL has antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-fibrotic properties playing a pivotal role in preventing and improving of chronic and degenerative diseases [12].

In this respect, the present study showed a decrease in the levels of liver enzymes such as AST, ALT, ALP, GGT, total bilirubin, and direct bilirubin after taking D-limonene. However, DL has been shown to improve liver function by reducing liver damage [15]. In our study, we were unable to examine the expression of other apoptosis-related genes such as Bax and Bcl-2 as well as inflammation-related genes. It is recommended that these genes be included in future studies.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated the hepatoprotective effects of d-limonene at two doses of 50 and 100 mg/kg for a duration of 4 weeks on cholestatic liver damage in BDL rats. The findings offer valuable insights into the various benefits of DL in reducing oxidative stress, inflammation, fibrosis, bile duct proliferation, and hepatocyte necrosis. However, further research is necessary to confirm the safety of using DL in treating different liver diseases.
