Registered Sick Leave in Celiac Disease: A Register-Based Nationwide Cohort Study
Authors: Signe Ulfbeck Schovsbo, Anne Ahrendt Bjerregaard, Margit Schilling Riis, Susanne Hansen, Line Tang Møllehave, Frederikke Hørdam Gronemann, Christen Lykkegaard Andersen, Allan Linneberg, Line Lund Kårhus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsThe vagueness of symptoms and differences in severity in celiac disease (CeD) make it challenging to fully grasp CeD and its burden. Sick leave may be an indicator of disease burden. We aimed to assess registered sick leave before and after diagnosis of CeD in a longitudinal nationwide register-based cohort study.MethodsAll patients diagnosed with CeD between 2008 and 2017 and aged 25–60 were identified in the National Patient Register. A 1:10 matching method by age and sex was used to identify a comparison cohort without CeD. Information on registered sick leave and disability pension was obtained from the Danish Register for Evaluation of Marginalization Registry. Sick leave and disability pension were assessed on a weekly basis both by proportions and by mean weeks of annual leave.ResultsA total of 2494 patients with CeD and 24,578 individuals without CeD were identified. We found higher proportions and mean weeks of sick leave among patients with CeD compared with individuals without CeD from 5 years before diagnosis until 5 years after, peaking in risk ratios (RRs) 2 years after diagnosis (RR: 1.58), and in disability pension 5 years after diagnosis (RR: 1.44). The relative difference in mean number of weeks of sick leave per year was highest in the year following diagnosis (mean weeks among patients with CeD 5.80, ratio of means 1.62).ConclusionWe found increased levels of sick leave and disability pension among patients with CeD both 5 years before and 5 years after diagnosis. Measures to prevent the burden of CeD should be considered and explored further.Keywords: Celiac Disease, Sick Leave, Nationwide, Epidemiology

Background

Celiac disease (CeD) affects approximately 1% of the population,1 though many cases remain undiagnosed.2 The immune-mediated disease is caused by an abnormal immune response triggered by the ingestion of gluten in genetically susceptible individuals.3 As CeD primarily affects the small intestine, classical symptoms include diarrhea, weight loss, and growth failure. However, symptoms and clinical presentation can be more nonspecific, such as iron deficiency anemia, headaches, and fatigue.2 Besides the vague nature of symptoms, severity can vary from mild—and only detected by screening—to very severe. The vague symptoms in CeD pose a challenge to the clinician and may result in long diagnostic delays4,5 with periods of poor health4 and increased healthcare utilization.6 In Denmark, the average diagnostic delay is estimated to 6 years, but many experience much longer delay.5 Once diagnosed, patients with CeD report a high perceived treatment burden comparable to other chronic diseases.7 Furthermore, CeD is associated with increased risk of comorbidities.8 The burden of disease might be even greater in vulnerable groups such as patients without adequate treatment response, poor overall health, or socially disadvantaged groups.9

As such, CeD may pose a burden both to the patient and the society both before and after diagnosis. Sick leave can serve as an objective indicator of disease burden. Yet, little is known about sick leave in patients with CeD. A Swedish register-based study10 reported more sick days among patients with CeD compared with matched individuals from the general population, both 5 years prior and after diagnosis. The study found that work loss was primarily driven by a small subset of individuals and highlighted comorbidities, especially psychiatric comorbidity, as possibly driving the work loss. Another Swedish register-based study also found more patients with CeD on sick leave at 25 years of age,11 but the study did not assess the temporality with diagnosis. In contrast, a Finnish nationwide cohort study12 reported a lower frequency of sick leave among patients with CeD in the year preceding diagnosis compared with the general population. Following diagnosis, sick leave among men increased to levels comparable with the control group, which the authors attribute to altered health behavior. A third Swedish study13 found that sick leave decreased after initiation of gluten-free diet among patients with CeD. Yet, a recent Italian cross-sectional survey study found that being on a gluten-free diet for less than 1 year and persistence of symptoms despite the diet were associated with work absenteeism.14

Thus, current knowledge about sick leave in CeD is limited due to few studies, heterogeneous designs, and conflicting findings, all of which hinder clear interpretation. Apart from 2 register-based studies,10,11 existing research relies on self-reported sick leave12, 13, 14 at risk of recall bias, or individuals recruited through patient organizations12,13 at risk of selection bias. By utilizing the Danish nationwide registries, we aimed to assess registered sick leave before and after diagnosis in patients with CeD compared with matched controls in a longitudinal nationwide register-based cohort study.

Methods and Data

Data Sources

This study was based on data from the Danish nationwide population-based registers. We linked data from the Danish National Patient Register (NPR) and the Danish Register for Evaluation of Marginalization Registry (DREAM) to obtain information on diagnoses and public transfer incomes, respectively.

The Danish Civil Registration System15 includes information on all individuals residing in Denmark, including their unique personal identification number, which may be used for linkage across the Danish registers. NPR contains systematically collected data on all administrative and clinical encounters in Denmark from 1977 (psychiatric, emergency, and outpatient encounters since 1995) and onwards.16

The DREAM database includes collected data from the Danish Ministry of Employment, Ministry of Social Affairs, Ministry of Education, Ministry of Integration, municipalities, and Statistics Denmark17,18 on all citizens receiving public transfer incomes including all sickness benefits since 1991. Since 2008, all employed individuals are registered in DREAM regardless of whether they receive any benefits, thus it holds information on more than 6 million people. Every individual and their received benefit are tracked on a weekly basis. The different types of public transfer incomes are prioritized in case of more than one type of benefit in the same week, for example, disability pension overruling sickness benefit. As a result, only one type of public transfer income per week per person is accessible.

Study Population and Design

We conducted a longitudinal nationwide register-based cohort study. Using the NPR, we identified all individuals with a second hospital encounter with CeD coded by a doctor (inpatient or outpatient) from January 1, 2008, until December 31, 2017, defined by the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 code K90.0 or ICD-8 code 269.0. Patients with at least 2 encounters with CeD were included as cases to ensure high specificity of the diagnosis. The date of the second encounter was defined as index.

Each CeD case was matched by sex at birth and age at index to 10 individuals from the general population who were alive and without CeD at the time of matching. Individuals without CeD were assigned the same index date as their match.

The study population was restricted to individuals aged 25–60 years at index. Only individuals who were alive at index and had continuously resided in Denmark for 5 years prior were included. Individuals without available data from DREAM were excluded. To ensure equal follow-up time, time was standardized relative to the index date, so individuals diagnosed in different years (eg, 2015 vs 2017) were assessed equally. The study population was followed from 5 years before the index date until 5 years after, or until the occurrence of CeD diagnosis in individuals without CeD (hereafter, they were treated as cases with individual matches), permanent workforce exit, emigration, or death in which case they were censored.

Registered Sick Leave

We defined sick leave, disability pension, and permanent workforce exit from weekly data in the DREAM register from January 1, 2003, until December 31, 2022.

As the DREAM register is based on public transfer incomes, we defined the occurrence of registered sick leave using records of received sickness benefits. In Denmark, employers are required to compensate sick leave for a politically decided number of days, after which the sickness benefits can be activated, and the employer can receive reimbursement from the municipality. Thus, only sick leave episodes exceeding the employer-paid period leading to an administrative economic reimbursement to the employer are registered in DREAM and were included in the study. The employer-paid sick leave period has changed during the study period, varying from 14 to 30 days depending on the calendar year.19 Sick leave within the employer-paid period is not registered unless it extends leading to reimbursement, in which case the entire period is registered.

Sickness benefits were defined as payments received due to illness, with varying degrees of self-support. Generally, one can receive regular sickness benefits for up to 22 weeks within 9 months. If sickness extends beyond this, special programs in interdisciplinary teams are initiated, which can extend long term. We included sickness benefits resembling all such periods, for example, benefits due to employment rehabilitation programs or to flex jobs, which are jobs with reduced hours due to sickness.

Similarly, disability pension was defined by records of received disability pension. Disability pension is usually given after longer periods of sickness and after trying help from interdisciplinary teams. Hence, the benefit is generally received due to sickness with no self-support. Individuals receiving disability pension may have periods with “light work,” which would be work on special terms and conditions. Registered benefits of light work were included in the disability pension group. Permanent exit from the workforce was characterized by voluntary exit from the workforce, for example, public retirement pension. See Supplementary Table 1 for categorizations.

Covariates

Indicators of social position included cohabitation status, educational level, and income level in the year of index. Cohabitation status was based on information from the Danish Civil Registration System15 dichotomized as either living with a partner or alone. The longest duration of education attained by index date was categorized into 4 levels including elementary school, vocational training, shorter secondary education, and long secondary education based on data from the Danish Education Registers.20 Household equalized income was divided into tertiles based on data from Statistics Denmark. Information on death and emigration was obtained from Statistics Denmark.

Selected comorbidities were included for both individuals with and without CeD. Information on osteoporosis, diabetes mellitus, thyroid disease, cardiovascular disease, cancer, and mental illness was extracted from the NPR during a period of 5 years before index using relevant ICD-10 codes, as detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical Analyses

Characteristics of the study population at index were summarized using proportions and medians. The weekly proportion of individuals receiving registered sick leave or disability pension was illustrated using proportion plots for patients with CeD and their matched individuals without CeD. Additionally, proportions were calculated at annual tick marks and, similarly, the mean number of weeks on registered sick leave or disability pension per year. Relative differences between patients with CeD and individuals without CeD were calculated as risk ratios and ratios of means. All analyses of registered sick leave and disability pension were conducted separately. As participants could receive different benefits at different time points, the same individual could be included multiple times, receiving different types of benefits.

The distribution of registered sick leave was assessed by calculating the individual number of weeks of registered sick leave over the entire study period for patients. Weeks of registered sick leave were not required to be consecutive. In subgroup analyses, proportion plots for registered sick leave were stratified by educational duration, dichotomized as short (short primary education/vocational training) or long (shorter/longer secondary education), and by the presence of comorbidities within 5 years before index, dichotomized as any comorbidity or no comorbidity.

Results

A total of 2494 patients with CeD and 24,578 age- and sex-matched individuals without CeD were included in the study population (Table 1). The majority were women, and the median age at index was 41 years. The number of patients diagnosed with CeD increased over the study period, peaking in 2015. Most individuals cohabitated. Compared with individuals without CeD, a higher proportion of patients with CeD had completed short secondary education and were in the middle-income tertile. Additionally, patients with CeD had a higher prevalence of comorbidities in the 5 years prior to the index with the largest absolute difference found in the prevalence of diabetes mellitus.

Forty-nine percent of individuals without CeD had no registered sick leave in the 10-year study period, compared with 39.1% of patients with CeD (Figure 3). Among the individuals without registered sick leave, 16.0% of patients with CeD received disability pension for at least 1 week during the study period, compared with 9.7% of individuals without CeD. More individuals without CeD had 0–10 weeks of registered sick leave, whereas more patients with CeD had registered sick leave exceeding 10 weeks during the study period.

Distribution of registered sick leave across study period.

Both among individuals with shorter and longer periods of education (Supplementary Figure 1), weekly proportions of individuals with registered sick leave were consistently higher in patients with CeD from 5 years before index, which increased around index remaining higher 5 years after index. Levels of sick leave were generally lower in individuals with longer periods of education. Individuals with comorbidity, both with and without CeD, had overlapping proportions of registered sick leave in the 5 years foregoing index (Supplementary Figure 2). In the time approaching index, the proportion of individuals with registered sick leave increased among patients with CeD and remained elevated until the end of follow-up. In individuals without comorbidities, patients with CeD had consistently higher proportions of registered sick leave the entire study period increasing at index. Levels of sick leave were generally lower in individuals without comorbidities.

Discussion

This longitudinal nationwide register-based cohort study including all patients diagnosed with CeD between 2008 and 2017, aged 25–60 years, showed that patients with CeD experience a substantial burden of disease. This was reflected by consistently higher proportions and mean weeks of registered sick leave and disability pension in patients with CeD compared with age- and sex-matched individuals without CeD. Subgroup analyses showed that individuals with shorter duration of education or comorbidities generally had higher levels of registered sick leave, regardless of CeD diagnosis. However, across all subgroups, including those with longer duration of education and no comorbidities, patients with CeD still demonstrated increased rates of registered sick leave, underscoring the broad impact of the disease.

Interpretation and Comparison With Other Studies

We found that both the proportion of individuals with registered sick leave and the mean number of weeks were elevated as early as 5 years prior to diagnosis. Notably, there was a marked increase in both measures during the year preceding diagnosis. The examined period length reflects the current average diagnostic delay in Denmark,5 and the increased registered sick leave could potentially be attributable to early symptoms of CeD intensifying around the time of diagnosis. The continued elevated level of registered sick leave could be due to lack of treatment effect, altered health behavior leading to a lower threshold for sick leave, or comorbidities presenting or being detected in relation to diagnosis of CeD. However, the tendency of a decrease in the ratio of mean weeks over time combined with the continued elevation in proportion could indicate that although more people registered with sick leave after diagnosis, the sick leave on average becomes fewer or shorter over time comparable to the level 5 years prior to diagnosis. This could reflect better management of disease with time.

Registered sick leave appeared broadly distributed across the population of patients with CeD rather than centered around a subgroup. Overall, 60.9% of patients with CeD had at least one episode of registered sick leave during the study period compared with 51% of individuals without CeD. Of the remaining 39% of patients with CeD without registered sick leave, 16.0% received disability pension at some point during follow-up compared with 9.7% among individuals without CeD. This finding suggests how a subset of individuals not captured in the analyses of sick leave may not be healthy but may be represented in the disability group instead. Such large patient impairment may be due to comorbidities arising or being detected alongside CeD and could be influenced by social circumstances. The increase in proportion and mean weeks of disability pension were less temporal to diagnosis and extended over a longer period than seen for sick leave. This lag may be due to the complex and long process of disability pension approval, where the individuals in our analyses may fall into our category of sick leave instead during this period.

In subanalyses, individuals with longer educational duration and without comorbidities generally had lower levels of sick leave. Assessing educational duration, however, patients with CeD still had higher levels of sick leave in both stratifications, indicating that CeD affects patients across social resources. In patients with comorbidities prior to the index, the proportions of individuals with sick leave overlapped until index when sick leave increased in patients with CeD. This indicates that CeD attributes an additional burden of disease also in case of other comorbidities being present. This is in line with our other finding that individuals without other diseases than CeD also had increased levels of sick leave. As such, CeD appears to attribute a substantial burden of disease on its own. Hence, the burden of CeD appears to be prominent across educational length and comorbidity status.

Overall, our findings align well with trends observed in a Swedish register-based study,10 which also found an increased number of sick days in patients with CeD compared with general-population individuals without CeD 5 years prior to and after diagnosis. The study assessed patients diagnosed in 2008–2015 in a healthcare system comparable to our setting and assessed sick leave and disability pension expressed as work loss. Despite similarities, the direct comparison of absolute measurements of leaves is difficult to harmonize across studies due to differences in study designs and national social and healthcare. We sought to minimize this limitation by calculating relative differences. The Swedish study10 found relative differences in mean of days with overall work loss (ratio of mean days 1.59) comparable to our sickness leave results at the time of diagnosis (ratio of mean weeks 1.52–1.62). The ratios stabilized at 1.39–1.42 up to 5 years after diagnosis,21 which are slightly lower ratios compared with our findings varying between 1.51 and 1.47 3–5 years after diagnosis. Such variation may be due to different categorizations of benefits by study design or societal circumstances and differences in procedures of benefits.

The Swedish study found that work loss was driven primarily by a small proportion of individuals, and 75.4% had no work loss. Furthermore, the study highlights comorbidities, especially psychiatric comorbidity, as possibly driving the association. In contrast, we found that most patients with CeD received sickness benefits at some point but also found this in the reference population. We found increased proportions of registered sick leave in patients with CeD in individuals without prior comorbidities, indicating CeD resulting in a substantial burden of disease on its own. Individuals with comorbidities had overlapping proportions before index, which increased in patients with CeD after index. We were unable from our data to assess psychiatric comorbidity on its own. Comparable to our subanalysis, the Swedish study also overall found higher levels of work loss in individuals with shorter periods of education.

Strength and Limitations

By utilizing high-quality data from NPR and at least 2 hospital encounters with CeD, we ensured high specificity of disease as CeD cases. A further strength is the large size of the study population, which allowed for subanalyses of potential vulnerable groups. The matching on age and sex was split in subanalyses of education and comorbidities, and the analyses must be interpreted with caution, as age and sex could affect the illustrated proportions. Furthermore, it is important to note that educational duration might affect health literacy and thus diagnosing of CeD and further that comorbidities might lead to surveillance bias and more registered diagnoses. The assessment of diagnosed CeD between 2008 and 2017 enabled a similar diagnostic approach in the study period. However, private practice specialists and general practitioners do not report to NPR,16 and patients with CeD without encounters at a hospital were therefore not included. As such, there is an inherent risk of misclassification of, for example, patients diagnosed in private practices children as either control if they never encounter the hospital, or with a late index date in case of first hospital encounters in adulthood. However, most children diagnosed with CeD in Denmark are likely diagnosed in a hospital setting, resulting in a hospital encounter in childhood and therefore an exclusion, as this study only included adults. Individuals without CeD were censored in case of CeD diagnosis during the study period but were included in the period until diagnosis to avoid conditioning on the future. Sickness in this period, for example, due to undiagnosed CeD could affect estimates of the individuals without CeD, which could lead to an underestimation of relative differences. As this likely regards a small number of participants, the limitation is considered minor. Confidence intervals were narrow, but caution should be raised as our observations were not independent as we matched our cases with individuals from the background population. This risk was reduced as the matching was only conducted on sex and age with 10 individuals.

A validation study has showed high agreement (96.7%) between registered sickness benefit in DREAM and workplace-registered sick leave of at least 15 days despite a slight overestimation in DREAM of 1.4 weeks of duration on average.22 The study also found tendencies of underestimation in self-reported sick leave compared to workplace registers, and similarly, self-reported sick leave compared to DREAM has been found to underestimate.18 As such, DREAM registrations appear a valid measurement of sick leave with high accuracy. However, the DREAM data also have few inherent limitations. Only sick leave exceeding the employer-paid period is registered in DREAM, and we therefore did not include shorter leaves (<14–30 days depending on calendar year). This results in a risk of underestimating short-term leaves. On the other hand, benefits are registered by full weeks in DREAM, not days, which may lead to the risk of overestimation of duration. Furthermore, as the leaves are prioritized by the register, individuals receiving more than one type of benefit will only have the most severe type registered per week which limits precision in weeks of transitioning from one benefit to another. The definition of registered sick leave was broad, and there may be nuances within the category such as differences among the individual sickness benefits, that this study does not capture. General increases in registered sick leave over time may reflect changes in economic conditions, workforce composition, population aging, and broader demographic trends, for example, our study period included the COVID-19 pandemic, which might have resulted in different assignment of benefits in the end period of the study. However, this would affect both groups equally, and the effect is limited by matching on index date.

We assessed a study population assumed available for the labor market, as they were 25–60 years old at index, yet some individuals may still be pursuing their education, especially if pursuing longer durations of educations.19 The younger participants may not have registered information on sick leave, if they are receiving other public transfer incomes already covering periods of sickness, such as the State Educational Grant. Therefore, we may underestimate sick leave in younger individuals. However, this would affect both groups equally, and as the median age was 41 years, this is likely a minor limitation. In our study patients with CeD were matched 1:10 by sex at birth and age at index date to individuals from the general population without CeD. In national Danish registries gender is not registered, and therefore not included in this registry-based study.

The longitudinal study design allows for assessment of trends over time in relation to diagnosis, which is a unique quality of the study. We standardized time to enable assessment of temporality of registered sick leave with diagnosis, and as the policies regulating sick leave, for example, the length of the employer-paid period or requirements for different benefits, changed over time. The standardization resulted in averaged estimates over time. However, we do not know the reason for the individual sick leave or have data on experienced symptoms, and therefore we cannot with certainty attribute the sick leave to CeD. As such, the registered sick leave is a relative proxy for the burden of CeD, and we rely on relative measures and overall tendencies in our interpretation and comparisons. Future studies may elaborate this matter by utilizing questionnaires and qualitative interviews in combination with registers.

Conclusion

In a longitudinal nationwide register-based cohort study, we assessed registered sick leave and disability pension over time to gain more objective insights into the nature and burden of CeD. We found higher proportions with registered sick leave and higher mean weeks of registered sick leave among patients with CeD from 5 years before diagnosis until 5 years after. Further studies are needed to expand the knowledge on groups at special risk of increased burden of disease to make future interventions possible.
