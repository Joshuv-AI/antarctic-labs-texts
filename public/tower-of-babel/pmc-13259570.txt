A 31-year-old woman (gravida 5, para 1, abortus 3) at 29 weeks and 4 days’ gestation presented to our tertiary care labour and delivery triage with worsening rash across her abdomen, biceps, and cheeks bilaterally. She had a known diagnosis of generalized pustular psoriasis (GPP), biopsy-confirmed in 2017. The original diagnosis was made in 2016, approximately 10 months after her first cesarean delivery for breech presentation. Her previous pustular psoriasis flares were related to stressful life events and were treated with prednisone, methotrexate, and apremilast. She had experienced 1 to 2 flares per year since her diagnosis; however, her disease had more recently been well controlled, with no flares in 2.5 years leading up to this pregnancy.

The patient’s current pregnancy was conceived spontaneously and had been uncomplicated. She had presented to a local emergency department with a 1-week history of pustules in an erythematous base over her abdomen bilaterally. At that visit, she was started on oral prednisone 20 mg daily. Five days later, she presented to our labour and delivery triage, because the lesions continued to spread despite the treatment and were painful and warm to palpation. The affected body surface area was about 5% (Figure 1). The patient did not have any extracutaneous symptoms but did describe general malaise. We admitted the patient and started her on oral prednisone 75 mg daily for presumed pustular psoriasis of pregnancy (PPP), a variant of GPP that presents in pregnancy. We consulted a dermatologist, and they completed a biopsy on the second day of her admission. The dermatologist also suspected PPP and started daily topical betamethasone valerate 0.1% ointment. The biopsy of the patient’s right abdomen showed features most consistent with pustular psoriasis (Figure 2). Fetal monitoring included nonstress test twice daily and ultrasonography biophysical profile with umbilical artery Doppler and repeat anatomy review on admission and was normal. Phototherapy was started at 30 weeks and 4 days’ gestation after some improvement in the patient’s symptoms with oral prednisone. Phototherapy consisted of narrowband ultraviolet B (NBUVB), which was started at a dose of 200 J and increased by 10% each visit. This was done 3 times weekly. On day 11 of hospital admission, after symptom improvement, she was discharged home on a prednisone taper (starting dose 75 mg/d orally) with a plan to decrease the daily dose by 10 mg each week. Phototherapy 3 times weekly and weekly fetal ultrasonography were also arranged.

The patient was readmitted at 31 weeks and 2 days’ gestation by our obstetrics service owing to a resurgence in symptoms (despite oral prednisone 50 mg daily), including spread of the flare to her groin region and flexor surfaces of the upper and lower extremities, along with new joint pain. We consulted the rheumatology service to exclude psoriatic arthritis, but they did not consider her joint pain to be consistent with this additional manifestation of her condition. When the patient was at 32 weeks and 0 days’ gestation, obstetrical internal medicine was consulted, and we jointly decided to start her on oral cyclosporine 100 mg twice daily. We also continued oral prednisone 50 mg daily and phototherapy 3 times weekly. As a result of sustained steroid use, the patient developed hyperglycemia requiring insulin treatment. Clobetasol 0.1% twice daily was started at 33 weeks and 0 days’ gestation to avoid scarring in hyper-pigmented areas. A prednisone taper was restarted at this time to decrease risk of unwanted sequelae in pregnancy, such as hyperglycemia, and cyclosporine was continued. Phototherapy was stopped at 35 weeks and 5 days’ gestation, as the patient’s condition had been stable on systemic medication and the machine was unavailable owing to technical issues (Table 2).

Although the patient’s symptoms from PPP had improved, an induction of labour was planned for 37 weeks’ gestation, given persistence of her PPP symptoms in conjunction with a large-for-gestational-age fetus (estimated fetal weight 90% at 35 wk and 3d gestation), she went on to have a spontaneous vaginal delivery, at 37 weeks and 2 days’ gestation, of a female, weight 3370 g and Apgar 9/9. Before discharge, the dermatology service discussed with the patient the possibility of starting certolizumab in the future.

In the postpartum period, the dermatologists continued cyclosporine 100 mg daily and 50 mg nightly, and advised the patient it was safe to continue breastfeeding. At 10 weeks postpartum, she had a pustular psoriasis flare, at which point she underwent a second biopsy, with findings consistent with pustular psoriasis. She re-presented to the emergency department 4 days later with a worsening facial flare and joint pain (Figure 3) and was admitted to the hospital, where she received 3 mg of intravenous dexamethasone followed by a course of oral prednisone. She was discharged home on oral prednisone 20 mg daily and continued on cyclosporine. In follow-up, her dermatologist initiated certolizumab 400 mg subcutaneously every 2 weeks, following which her rash and swelling cleared. She discontinued her cyclosporine and her prednisone was tapered; she will continue certolizumab every 2 weeks with ongoing follow-up with her dermatologist every 3 to 4 months.

Pustular psoriasis of pregnancy is a rare dermatosis of pregnancy characterized by the formation of sterile cutaneous pustules in the skin folds that spread centrifugally.2 It was previously referred to as impetigo herpetiformis or generalized psoriasis of pregnancy. The condition is often accompanied by systemic symptoms and can have life-threatening complications for the pregnant patient and the fetus.

Emerging evidence shows that PPP is a variant of GPP.2,3 The international consensus definition of GPP is “a systemic inflammatory disease characterized by cutaneous erythema and macroscopically visible sterile pustules.”4 Flares of this condition are recurrent and sudden and can be triggered by metabolic disturbances, medication changes, oral contraceptives, menstrual cycle changes, pregnancy, and other environmental factors.2,4,5 Many patients have a personal or family history of psoriasis.2 Generalized pustular psoriasis is thought to be a disease of immune dysregulation, owing much of its clinical presentation to the interleukin (IL) pathway.2,6 Keratinocytes, neutrophils, and monocytes are the most active cell types.7 Interleukin-1, IL-17, IL-36, and tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) all play a large role in pustular disease.2,8 Homozygous and heterozygous mutations in an antagonist to IL-1 receptors (IL36RN) have been identified in genetic studies of patients with PPP.2,3

Our patient’s case is unique in presentation; PPP usually presents in the third trimester of pregnancy and most commonly improves in the postpartum period. The skin changes are characterized by symmetric erythematous patches with small sterile pustules spread throughout. The patches often start in skin folds, travel to the trunk and extremities, and spare the face, palms, and soles of the feet. Commonly, the sterile micropustules cluster together, erode, dry out, and then form large plaques of desquamated skin.2,9 Pruritus is not a commonly described symptom.9

Pustular psoriasis of pregnancy often presents with systemic symptoms or laboratory abnormalities. These might include fatigue, fever, diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, confusion, elevated inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein or erythrocyte sedimentation rate, anemia, or neutrophilia. In more serious cases, untreated systemic inflammation and electrolyte imbalances can lead to dehydration, secondary infection, sepsis, tetany, seizure, acute respiratory distress syndrome, cardiovascular failure, renal failure, and death.2,3,9 Pustular psoriasis of pregnancy can be a clinical diagnosis, but a skin biopsy is often recommended. Histopathology will show spongioform pustules with neutrophils, epidermal hyperplasia, and parakeratosis.9

If left untreated, PPP can confer substantial risks for the fetus. These consequences include fetal growth restriction, fetal anomalies, preterm birth, preterm rupture of membranes, and fetal demise.2,3,9 In 1 study of 8 pregnant people with PPP, earlier onset of PPP was associated with higher likelihood of fetal complications. 7 Many poor outcomes can be attributed to placental insufficiency, possibly secondary to an inflammatory process affecting the placenta.2,3

Pustular psoriasis of pregnancy is a challenging dermatosis both to diagnose and to treat, but early recognition and initiation of therapy is critical. Initial treatment is often with systemic corticosteroids. In mild cases, low doses of oral prednisone ranging from 15 to 30 mg/d are sufficient, but for more severe disease, doses can be as high as 60 to 80 mg/d.2 A tapering of these systemic corticosteroids occurs after delivery in most patients.

Cyclosporine is considered a safe first-line therapy for PPP.2 It prevents IL-2 formation and is often used as monotherapy or in combination therapy with corticosteroids. Prescribed oral doses in the literature range from 2 to 7.5 mg/kg/d.9 Hypertension and renal dysfunction are 2 potential adverse effects of cyclosporine. Narrowband ultraviolet B radiation is also safe in pregnancy, often in combination with corticosteroids.2 The use of NBUVB radiation in combination with cyclosporine is more controversial. There is a studied increased risk of dermatologic cancer owing to the immunosuppressive effect of cyclosporine.10 Therefore, the combined use should be limited to a short period of time or used sequentially, as in our patient.

Limited data exist on the use and safety of TNF-α inhibitors in pregnancy, but no definite adverse effects have been reported in pregnant patients or fetuses, and they can induce rapid remission in refractory PPP.8 These medications should be considered in pregnancies complicated by gestational diabetes or hypertension, in which corticosteroids or cyclosporine would not be recommended. 11 Infliximab is contraindicated in the third trimester, as more immunoglobulin G antibody is transferred via the placenta than in the first and second trimesters. Unlike infliximab, certolizumab is a TNF-α inhibitor that does not cross the placenta or get secreted into breast milk.9,11 One case report showed successful treatment of PPP with certolizumab from 30 weeks’ gestation onward.11 Another emerging class of medications are IL-36 receptor inhibitors, such as spesolimab; 1 case report described successful treatment of a patient with PPP from the second trimester onward.12

For people with PPP, early referral is recommended to initiate efficient treatment and investigations. In patients in whom disease progresses despite the discussed therapies, delivery can be considered. No formal recommendations exist for timing of delivery. Additionally, in the postpartum period, other medications that are considered teratogenic during pregnancy may be prescribed, including methotrexate and oral retinoids.2 Risk of recurrence in subsequent pregnancies is high and often presents earlier with more severe symptoms,2,9 but the long-term consequences of PPP on maternal health are not yet well studied.
