Comparative outcomes of midline lumbar fusion and minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion in osteoporotic L4/5 spinal stenosis: a two-year retrospective study
Authors: Xiang-dong Gong, Shi-yong Wang, Ru-bin Yao, Ming Zhang, Shao-bo Li, Yong Duan, Kai-shun Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study is to compare radiological and clinical outcomes between midline lumbar fusion (MIDLF) and minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (MITLIF) in patients with L4/5 lumbar spinal stenosis and concomitant osteoporosis.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was performed involving patients with osteoporosis who underwent either MIDLF or MITLIF. Participants were stratified into two groups based on the surgical technique. Primary outcome measures included operative duration, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative hemoglobin reduction, time to extubation, length of hospital stay, postoperative complication rate, fusion rate, and radiographic parameters: lumbar lordosis (LL), intervertebral disc height (HOD), pelvic incidence (PI), pelvic tilt (PT), and sacral slope (SS). Clinical outcomes were assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for back and leg pain, Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) scores, and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI).ResultsThe MIDLF group comprised 22 patients with a mean follow-up duration of 32.48 months, while the MITLIF group included 21 patients with a mean follow-up of 35.32 months. Both groups demonstrated statistically significant postoperative improvements in VAS, JOA, and ODI scores relative to baseline. The MIDLF group exhibited significantly higher JOA scores at 3 and 12 months postoperatively. Intraoperative blood loss was significantly lower in the MITLIF group (p < 0.021), and time to extubation was also significantly shorter (p < 0.001). Although not statistically significant, the MIDLF group exhibited reduced surgical and hospitalization durations. No significant differences were observed between groups in terms of complication or fusion rates. Radiological evaluation indicated significant postoperative improvements in LL, HOD, and SS, along with reductions in PT and PI–LL mismatch; however, no statistically significant differences were observed between the surgical techniques.ConclusionBoth MIDLF and MITLIF are effective in improving sagittal alignment and alleviating symptoms in patients with L4/5 lumbar spinal stenosis and osteoporosis. MITLIF may offer perioperative advantages, including reduced intraoperative blood loss and a shorter extubation times.Keywords: Lumbar spinal stenosis, Lumbo-pelvic parameters, MIDLF, MITLIF.

Introduction

Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is a degenerative condition that may result in neuropathic pain and functional impairment, significantly diminishing health-related quality of life in affected individuals [1]. As the proportion of older adults continues to increase globally, the prevalence of LSS is rising, leading to a growing demand for surgical interventions aimed at neural decompression and spinal stabilization [2–4]. Conventional lumbar fusion techniques are associated with several limitations, including extensive dissection of paraspinal musculature, increased intraoperative blood loss, a higher risk of postoperative complications, and prolonged rehabilitation [5, 6].

In contrast, minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (MITLIF) employs an intermuscular surgical corridor, thereby reducing iatrogenic tissue injury. This technique has demonstrated clinical benefits such as reduced operative trauma and accelerated postoperative recovery [7–9]. However, in older adults with osteoporosis, compromised bone quality reduces the mechanical anchorage of pedicle screws, increasing the likelihood of hardware-related complications, such as screw loosening or pullout [8].

In cases of severe osteoporosis, neither modification of screw geometry nor cement augmentation of the screw tract have reliably addressed these fixation-related challenges [10, 11]. Midline lumbar fusion (MIDLF) utilizing cortical bone trajectory (CBT) screws offers biomechanical advantages by maximizing contact with the denser cortical bone, thereby enhancing fixation strength. This approach has shown promising outcomes in osteoporotic populations undergoing lumbar fusion procedures [12, 13]. Restoration and optimization of sagittal spinopelvic alignment following fusion have been identified as critical determinants of postoperative functional outcomes [14].

In patients with osteoporosis, the selection of a surgical approach that facilitates adequate neural decompression while preserving or correcting sagittal balance is essential for optimal long-term recovery. This study retrospectively compares the radiological and clinical outcomes of MIDLF and MITLIF in patients with single-level L4/5 lumbar spinal stenosis and osteoporosis, with particular emphasis on the impact of CBT screw fixation on postoperative sagittal alignment.

Methods

A retrospective analysis was conducted on patients with degenerative lumbar diseases and osteoporosis who underwent MIDLF or MITLIF surgery between October 2018 and February 2021. Inclusion criteria was as follows: (1) presence of severe symptoms attributable to degenerative lumbar conditions, including LSS or degenerative lumbar disc herniation (LDH); (2) evidence of lumbar instability or Grade I degenerative spondylolisthesis; (3) a confirmed diagnosis of osteoporosis; (4) surgical intervention at the L4/5 level using either MIDLF or MITLIF; (5) a minimum post-operative follow-up period of 24 months; and (6) availability of complete radiological and clinical data, including lumbar radiographs, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Exclusion criteria included the presence of spinal deformities, neoplasms, infections, or traumatic injury; a history of previous lumbar spinal surgery; intraoperative or postoperative complications such as cardiorespiratory failure or anesthesia-related complications; and incomplete follow-up data or missing clinical documentation.

All patients underwent preoperative evaluation, including specialist consultation, bone density tests, and imaging studies (lumbar spine radiographs, CT, and MRI) to confirm eligibility and exclude contraindications to surgery. Informed consent was obtained pre-operatively.

General anesthesia with endotracheal intubation was administered to all patients. For prophylaxis against surgical site infection, a second-generation cephalosporin was administered intravenously 30 min before anesthesia induction; clindamycin was used as an alternative in cases of cephalosporin allergy. A urinary catheter was inserted routinely. Patients were positioned prone with the abdomen suspended. The surgical level (L4/5) was confirmed using a C-arm fluoroscopy unit. Standard preoperative preparation included triple skin disinfection using alcohol and chlorhexidine, followed by sterile draping prior to the initiation of the surgical procedure.

Surgical techniques

All patients underwent surgery under general anesthesia with intraoperative C-arm fluoroscopic guidance. The responsible segment was first identified prior to instrumentation.

MIDLF (CBT group)

A midline posterior incision of approximately 3 cm was made at the L4–L5 level. After dissection of the skin and subcutaneous tissue, the entry point was determined at the intersection of the vertical line through the center of the L4 superior articular process and the horizontal line 1 mm below the transverse process. Under direct vision, pilot holes were created at L4 and L5 using a high-speed burr. The planned screw trajectory was left side from 5 o’clock to 11 o’clock and right side from 7 o’clock to 1 o’clock, with a cephalad tilt of 20–25° and a lateral angulation of approximately 10°. A guiding awl was advanced to a depth of 3.5 cm, and screw channels were sealed with bone wax after fluoroscopic verification.

On the non-decompression side, a hollow cortical bone trajectory (CBT) screw (3.5 cm in length, 5.5 mm in diameter) was inserted under fluoroscopic guidance. A minimally invasive Quadrant retractor system (Medtronic, USA) was applied on the decompression side to expose the L4–L5 lamina and facet joints. Partial removal of the L4 lamina and inferior articular process was performed to harvest autologous bone graft. The ligamentum flavum was excised, and the nerve root and dural sac were carefully decompressed and protected. The annulus fibrosus and disc material were removed, and the disc space was prepared. A cage of appropriate size (manufacturer, country) was selected according to disc height and implanted for interbody fusion. After reconfirming adequate neural decompression, an additional CBT screw (3.5 cm × 5.5 mm; Minimally Invasive Spine Company, Taiwan) was inserted and verified fluoroscopically. Titanium rods were then positioned and secured with set screws, followed by break-off of screw tails. The wound was irrigated, two drainage tubes were placed, and the incision was closed in layers.

MITLIF group

On the non-decompression side, the entry point was defined at the intersection of the horizontal line through the superior border of the transverse process and the vertical line along the lateral edge of the facet joint. A 1.5-cm skin incision was made at each site, pedicle screw channels were prepared, and percutaneous screws were inserted under fluoroscopic guidance. Temporary fixation was achieved with connecting rods.

On the decompression side, a 3.5-cm longitudinal incision was made approximately 2 cm lateral to the midline at the L4–L5 level. Through the Wiltse paraspinal approach, the apex of the “V-shaped ridge” of L4–L5 was exposed and used as the entry point. A standard pedicle screw channel was prepared to a depth of 4.5 cm, probed and verified fluoroscopically, then sealed with bone wax. A Quadrant retractor system (Medtronic, USA) was used to expose the lamina and facet joints. Partial resection of the L4 lamina and inferior articular process was performed to obtain autologous bone graft. The ligamentum flavum was removed, and the nerve root and dural sac were fully exposed and decompressed. The annulus fibrosus and disc tissue were excised, and an appropriately sized cage was selected and implanted for interbody fusion. Adequate decompression was reconfirmed.

Subsequently, pedicle screws (4.5 cm in length, 6.0 mm in diameter; Weigao, Shandong, China) were inserted under fluoroscopy. Titanium rods of appropriate length were positioned and secured, followed by tightening of the set screws and break-off of screw tails. After irrigation and confirmation of correct instrument counts, a drainage tube was placed, and the incision was closed in layers.

Hollow screws and standard connecting rods were supplied by Minimally Invasive Spine Company (Taiwan, China). Conventional pedicle screws, connecting rods, interbody cages, and allogeneic bone grafts were provided by Weigao Group (Shandong, China).

Clinical and radiographic evaluations

Postoperative clinical outcomes were evaluated using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for back and leg pain and the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score at 3 months, 12 months, and at the final follow-up. Additional perioperative variables evaluated retrospectively included operative duration, intraoperative blood loss, drainage volume, pre- and postoperative hemoglobin levels, time to drainage tube removal, length of hospital stay, and incidence of postoperative complications.

Radiographic assessments were conducted using standing anteroposterior and lateral full-spine radiographs. Sagittal spinopelvic parameters evaluated included lumbar lordosis (LL), segmental lordosis (SL), intervertebral disc height (HOD), pelvic incidence (PI), pelvic tilt (PT), and sacral slope (SS). These measurements were performed using a Picture Archiving and Communication System by an independent spine surgeon. Each parameter was measured three times, and the average value was used for analysis. Changes in spinopelvic alignment were determined by calculating the difference between preoperative and final follow-up measurements.

Assessment of interbody fusion status at the final follow-up was performed using CT-based three-dimensional reconstructions of the lumbar spine. Fusion outcomes were classified according to the interbody fusion assessment system as follows: Grade I: continuous bone trabeculae formation visible within the disc space; Grade II: complete bone graft filling with incomplete fusion, absence of radiolucent zones; Grade III: complete bone graft filling implantation with visible radiolucent zones at the superior and inferior interfaces of the implant; Grade IV: evidence of graft collapse or resorption, indicating fusion failure.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Python software. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s). For normally distributed data, within-group comparisons were assessed using paired t-tests, and between-group comparisons were assessed using independent-samples t-tests. For non-normally distributed data, Mann–Whitney U tests were applied. Categorical variables, including the incidence of adverse surgical events and intervertebral fusion rates, are expressed as percentages and compared using chi-squared tests. A bilateral p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The MIDLF group included 22 patients (13 males and 9 females) with a mean age of 64.67 ± 6.65 years and a mean follow-up duration of 32.48 ± 3.30 months. Preoperative diagnoses included lumbar spinal stenosis in 14 patients, lumbar disc herniation in 5, and lumbar spondylolisthesis in 3.

The MITLIF group included 21 patients (8 males and 13 females), with a mean age of 60.73 ± 4.67 years and a mean follow-up duration of 35.32 ± 6.33 months. Preoperative diagnoses included lumbar spinal stenosis in 15 patients, lumbar disc herniation in 4, and lumbar spondylolisthesis in 2 patients.

Statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of age and follow-up duration. The MIDLF group had a significantly higher mean age (p < 0.05), whereas the MITLIF group demonstrated a significantly longer means follow-up period (Table 1).

Perioperative outcomes

The mean operative time was shorter in the MIDLF group compared to the MITLIF group; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance. Intraoperative blood loss was significantly lower in the MITLIF group compared to the MIDLF group (p = 0.021). The duration to drainage tube removal was also significantly shorter in the MITLIF group (p < 0.05). No significant differences were noted between the groups in terms of postoperative hospital stay or changes in hemoglobin levels, although postoperative hemoglobin levels were higher in the MITLIF group. The fusion rates and incidence of postoperative complications did not differ significantly between the two groups (Table 2). In our study, the complications included one case of intraoperative blood transfusion and one case of internal fixation revision in the MIDLF group, and one case of intraoperative blood transfusion in the MITLIF group.

Clinical outcomes

Both groups demonstrated significant postoperative improvements in clinical outcomes, as evidenced by increases in JOA scores, and reductions in VAS scores for lumbar and leg pain, as well as ODI scores, relative to baseline values. At 3 and 12 months postoperatively, the MIDLF group exhibited significantly higher JOA scores compared to the MITLIF group (p < 0.05). However, no statistically significant differences in JOA scores were observed between the groups at 24 months or at the final follow-up (Table 3; Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4).

Radiographic outcomes

At the final follow-up, both LL and HOD showed statistically significant increases relative to preoperative values in both groups (p < 0.05). SL did not differ significantly from baseline. In terms of sagittal spinopelvic alignment, SS increased and PT decreased significantly in both groups compared to preoperative measurements, with no significant differences observed between the groups.

The pelvic incidence–lumbar lordosis (PI–LL) mismatch was significantly reduced in both groups (p < 0.001). The increase in HOD was significantly greater in the MITLIF group compared to the MIDLF group (p = 0.018). However, no statistically significant differences were noted between groups for changes in LL, SL, PT, SS, or PI–LL (Table 4).

Discussion

MIDLF has been demonstrated to be a safe and effective surgical intervention for the management of degenerative lumbar spine disorders. Compared to conventional open fusion techniques, MIDLF is associated with reduced soft tissue disruption and accelerated postoperative recovery. The utilization of CBT screws has further enhanced the applicability of MIDLF, particularly in individuals with osteoporosis, by improving screw fixation strength. Consequently, MIDLF has emerged as a favorable surgical option for degenerative lumbar pathology in osteoporotic populations. Despite its clinical utility, comparative studies evaluating MIDLF against other minimally invasive techniques remain limited.

With the ongoing demographic shift toward an aging population, the prevalence of osteoporosis continues to rise. In patients with severe osteoporosis, alterations in pedicle screw design or the use of cement augmentation have not consistently resolved the issue of inadequate screw purchase, which remains a common cause of screw loosening and hardware failure [10–16]. Mizuno et al. were among the first to report the application of CBT screw fixation in combination with MIDLF, enabling screw placement, decompression, and interbody fusion through a midline incision of approximately 3 cm [17]. Compared to conventional pedicle screws, CBT screws have demonstrated a 30% increase in pullout strength and a 1.7-fold increase in insertional torque, thereby offering a more biomechanically advantageous fixation method for individuals with osteoporosis [18].

MITLIF is widely recognized as a standard lumbar fusion technique, offering benefits such as reduced paraspinal muscle damage and expedited postoperative recovery. In the present study, both MIDLF and MITLIF were associated with favorable postoperative clinical outcomes. No significant differences were observed between groups in terms of VAS scores for back and leg pain or ODI scores. However, the MIDLF group exhibited significantly higher JOA scores at 3 and 12 months postoperatively, although this advantage was not sustained at 24 months. These findings are consistent with previously published data [19, 20].

Wu et al. noted that MIDLF was more effective than MITLIF in reducing postoperative back pain [21]. This finding has been attributed to the biomechanical impact of traditional pedicle screw placement in MITLIF, which may result in injury to the facet joint capsule during insertion, potentially accelerating ASD and contributing to delayed-onset low back pain. In contrast, CBT screws used in MIDLF follow a more medial and caudo-cephalad trajectory, allowing for greater preservation of the facet joint capsule, which may mitigate the risk of ASD and improve long-term pain outcomes.

A potential advantage of the MIDLF technique is its relatively simpler surgical approach, which may reduce the risk of perioperative complications. In the present study, the complication rate was 9.09% in the MIDLF group and 4.76% in the MITLIF group. Although the MIDLF technique theoretically involves less surgical trauma and has the potential to decrease complication rates, the observed complication rate was numerically lower in the MITLIF group. Reported events included one case of intraoperative blood transfusion and one case of internal fixation correction in the MIDLF group, and one case of intraoperative blood transfusion in the MITLIF group. The difference between groups was not statistically significant, likely due to the limited sample size. These findings should be interpreted cautiously.

From a technical perspective, CBT screws are generally placed from inside to outside at the cross-sectional plane, or from inferior to superior in the sagittal plane. This trajectory avoids major blood vessels, potentially enhancing the safety of screw placement. However, during the MIDLF procedure, some CBT screw entry points were located medially and inferiorly, adjacent to the spinous process. This positioning increased the likelihood of collision with the spinous process, which in turn altered the screw trajectory and contributed to suboptimal positioning.

According to Matsukawa et al. [20], the ideal trajectory for CBT screws involves insertion along the inferior border of the pedicle with a cephalad inclination of 25°–30° and a lateral inclination of approximately 10°. This approach maximizes the contact area between the screw and the pedicle, thereby improving pullout strength while maintaining safety. Nonetheless, screw parameters vary across lumbar segments. Although no complications due to malpositioned pedicle screws were observed in this study, this does not imply absolute safety. Accurate preoperative measurement of sagittal height and coronal width of each pedicle, along with appropriate intraoperative selection of screw size, insertion method, and angle, are essential for minimizing the risk of screw malposition and iatrogenic injury.

Fusion rates were slightly higher in the MIDLF group at the final follow-up, although the difference was not statistically significant. These findings are consistent with previous studies confirming the clinical safety and efficacy of MIDLF. Wu et al. similarly reported that MIDLF demonstrated comparable fusion and clinical outcomes to MITLIF at the L4/L5 level, particularly among older adults with compromised bone quality due to osteoporosis [21]. In the baseline data of this study, a statistically significant difference in age was observed between the MIDLF and MITLIF groups, with the former being older on average. No statistically significant difference was found between the two groups in bone mineral density (BMD), although values were numerically higher in the MIDLF group. Cases of screw loosening were identified during the two-year follow-up period; however, the long-term impact of osteoporosis on screw stability could not be determined. Large-scale, multicenter randomized studies are required to further evaluate whether the MIDLF technique provides an advantage in reducing complications.

Both MIDLF and MITLIF are minimally invasive fusion techniques that facilitate neural decompression and interbody fusion through small incisions, thereby reducing iatrogenic tissue trauma. However, findings regarding operative time for MIDLF remain inconsistent. While several studies have reported shorter operative durations for MIDLF compared to other minimally invasive approaches [15, 16], findings from the current study, as well as other reports, have demonstrated no significant differences in operative time between MIDLF and MITLIF [22–24]. Conversely, Silva et al. reported longer operative times for MIDLF, attributing this to increased surgical complexity in the treated cases [25].

In the present study, intraoperative blood loss and postoperative drainage volume were significantly lower in the MITLIF group. This difference may be attributable to the surgical approach: MITLIF employs a muscle-sparing intermuscular pathway (Wiltse approach), while MIDLF necessitates midline dissection for exposure, leading to increased tissue disruption. Nevertheless, no significant differences were observed in postoperative hemoglobin level changes or length of hospital stay, suggesting that the overall extent of soft tissue injury may be comparable between the two techniques.

Recent studies have underscored the importance of spinopelvic parameters in predicting clinical outcomes following lumbar fusion procedures. Under normal physiological conditions, postural equilibrium is maintained with minimal muscular effort through coordinated interaction among the spine, pelvis, ligaments, and paraspinal musculature [26]. Aging-related changes including intervertebral disc height loss, diminished lumbar lordosis, anterior displacement of the trunk, compensatory hip extension, and prolonged paraspinal muscle activity, may contribute to chronic low back pain. As a result, restoration of sagittal alignment has become a critical objective in surgical intervention of degenerative lumbar conditions.

Korovessis et al. reported a significant reduction in LL among individuals over the age of 60 years [27]. The L4/5 segment often represents the apex of lumbar curvature and is subject to considerable biomechanical loading, rendering it especially susceptible to degenerative changes and disc pathology. A direct correlation has been established between reduced LL and increased lumbar discomfort. Hence, restoration of LL is essential to optimizing clinical outcomes [14].

It is widely accepted that optimal LL in the context of spinal deformity correction should fall within the range of pelvic incidence (PI) ± 9°, which informs surgical planning for sagittal realignment [28, 29]. In the present study, both groups exhibited significant postoperative improvements in LL and HOD at the final follow-up. No significant differences were observed between the groups in their ability to restore these parameters, consistent with findings from Elmekaty et al. [15]

Furthermore, both groups showed increased SS and PT postoperatively, with significantly improved PI–LL values. These findings indicate that both techniques positively influenced sagittal spinopelvic alignment. Prior studies involving open transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion have similarly reported improvements in sagittal parameters [30, 31]. However, the extent to which these procedures influence pelvic parameters remains a subject of ongoing debate [32, 33]. Concerns have been raised regarding the potential limitations of minimally invasive approaches, such as MITLIF, in achieving adequate sagittal realignment due to restricted exposure and implant angulation. Nevertheless, emerging evidence indicates that MITLIF can yield significant improvements in both LL and pelvic parameters [34]. The findings of the present study support this conclusion, demonstrating that both MIDLF and MITLIF effectively contribute to the correction of sagittal spinopelvic malalignment.

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and single-center setting may introduce selection and observational biases, potentially limiting the generalizability of the results. Second, the study lacks a priori power analysisthe and relatively small sample size, along with the exclusion of patients lacking complete imaging data, may have introduced selection bias. Third, the follow-up period although exceeding 24 months, was insufficient to evaluate long-term outcomes such as fusion durability, hardware longevity, and the development of adjacent segment degeneration. Future prospective, multicenter studies with larger cohorts and extended follow-up durations are necessary to validate these findings and assess their long-term clinical implications.

Conclusion

Both MIDLF and MITLIF are effective surgical approaches for improving sagittal alignment and alleviating clinical symptoms in patients with lumbar spinal stenosis and concomitant osteoporosis. While both techniques yield comparable radiographic and functional outcomes, MITLIF demonstrates a significant advantage, particularly in reducing intraoperative blood loss and in shortening time to extubation.
