Continuous operations on non-Markovian processes
Fabio Costa, Jing Yang

Abstract
Continuous measurements are central to quantum control and sensing, yet lack a model-independent operational description that can be applied to arbitrary non-Markovian processes without specifying a microscopic measurement model. Existing multi-time frameworks, such as process matrices, allow for an arbitrary sequence of operations to be applied on a general process, but are restricted to interventions at discrete times and cannot represent measurements of finite duration. We introduce a continuous-time extension of multi-time quantum processes based on process and operation functionals, which generalize the Feynman–Vernon influence functional and yield a continuous Born rule that cleanly separates processes from operations. This framework provides a consistent representation of non-Markovian dynamics under continuous monitoring and leads to a natural definition of Markovianity in continuous time. We illustrate the formalism by analyzing continuous measurements in a generalized Caldeira–Leggett model, demonstrating its applicability to realistic non-Markovian scenarios.

From discrete-time to continuous processes and operations
—
The multi-time process formalism concerns a quantum system
S
, with Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{S}
, at a discrete (usually, finite) set of times. The system is allowed to be initially correlated with an environment and interact with it at every time step, while information about the process is obtained by repeated measurements or operations at the prescribed times, which are assumed to be instantaneous. Here, with a view to deriving a continuous-time limit, we modify the scenario by letting operations last for a finite time. Hence, we consider a scenario where process and operations alternate, with operations acting between times
t_{2k}
and
t_{2k+1}
,
k=0,\dots,N
, while the process takes over from odd to even times, see Fig.
1
(a).
To model the most general multi-time correlations, the formalism introduces a copy of the system Hilbert space for each time
t_{j}
:
\mathcal{H}^{j}\cong\mathcal{H}^{S}
, with total Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{(N)}\coloneqq\otimes_{j=0}^{2N+1}\mathcal{H}^{j}
. A quantum process is represented by a positive semidefinite operator
W{\in}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{(N)})
, called a
process matrix
(or
comb
, or
process tensor
), where
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the space of linear operators over a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Causally-ordered process matrices are required to satisfy a set of constraints, enforcing no causal influence from the future to the past.

Most commonly, probing the system is modeled through independent
quantum instruments
at different times, defined as collections of Completely Positive (CP) maps
\mathscr{M}^{k}_{r_{k}}{:}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{2k}){\rightarrow}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{2k+1})

labels the measurement outcome (or “readout”) and such that
\sum_{r_{k}}\mathscr{M}^{k}_{r_{k}}
is completely positive and trace preserving (CPTP). The probability to observe a sequence of outcomes
r=(r_{0},\dots,r_{N})
is given by the
generalized Born rule

;
45

\pr(r)=\Tr\hat{M}^{T}_{r}\hat{W},

(1)

where
\hat{M}_{r}{\coloneqq}\bigotimes_{k=0}^{N}\hat{M}^{k}_{r_{k}}
,
\hat{M}^{k}_{r_{h}}{\in}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{2k}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{2k+1})
is the Choi-Jamiołkowski
14
;
29
representation of
\mathscr{M}^{k}_{r_{k}}
, and
T
denotes transposition in the chosen basis. Here, we need to consider more general, non-Markovian, probing schemes, which are described by
quantum testers

11
(or
measuring strategies

24

\hat{M}_{r}{\in}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{(N)})

\sum_{r}\hat{M}_{r}
is a valid (causally ordered) process matrix.
The probability rule for this more general scenario is still given by Eq. (
1

\hat{W}
plays the role of a state and
\hat{M}_{r}
that of a measurement operator. The key aspect of this expression is that processes and operations are encoded in two independent objects, and the formula is linear in both. Our goal is to obtain an expression for the continuum limit of this formula that retains these properties.

It is convenient to connect the continuum limit with the standard Hamiltonian description of time evolution. This is facilitated by

;
13

Properties
—We have constructed continuous process and operation functionals from an underlying Hamiltonian model. The discrete-time formalism also gives
axiomatic
definitions, grounded in physically motivated properties. Here we present a continuous version of these properties, whose main justification (apart from the formal analogy) rests on the fact that 1) they hold for constructively-defined functionals, proven in Appendix
D
, and 2) they reduce to the corresponding multi-time properties in an appropriate limit, Appendix
E
.

Positivity.

Discrete process matrices and testers are required to be positive semidefinite, which means that
\hat{Q}=\hat{Q}^{\dagger}
and
\expectationvalue{\hat{Q}}{\psi}\geq 0

\forall\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}^{N}
, with
\hat{Q}=\hat{W},\hat{M}
. The functional version of this is
\mathcal{Q}^{*}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]=\mathcal{Q}[\bar{\alpha},\alpha]
and

\int\mathscr{D}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]\psi[\alpha]\psi^{*}[\bar{\alpha}]\mathcal{Q}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]\geq 0

(16)

for all functionals
\psi[\alpha]
.
Note that condition (
16
) can equivalently be stated by including the factor
\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\imaginary(\dot{\alpha}^{*}\alpha-\dot{\bar{\alpha}}^{*}\bar{\alpha})}
, consistently with the form of the Born rule (
6
), as that factor can be absorbed into
\psi[\alpha]
by redefining
\psi[\alpha]\to\psi[\alpha]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\imaginary(\dot{\alpha}^{*}\alpha)}
.

Causality.

Causally ordered process matrices satisfy a set of linear constraints, often called “causality conditions”, ensuring that future choices of operations do not affect past measurement statistics

; see Appendix
D.2

\mathcal{W}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]{=}\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]\mathrm{id}(\bar{\alpha}(t_{\mathrm{f}}),\alpha(t_{\mathrm{f}}))
has to satisfy,
\forall t^{\prime}{\in}(t_{\mathrm{i}},t_{\mathrm{f}})
,

\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha(t^{\prime})=\alpha^{\prime}\\
\bar{\alpha}(t^{\prime})=\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathscr{D}[\alpha_{>},\bar{\alpha}_{>}]\ee^{\ii\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\differential t\imaginary(\dot{\alpha}^{*}\alpha-\dot{\bar{\alpha}}^{*}\bar{\alpha})}\mathcal{W}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]\\
=\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}[\alpha_{<},\bar{\alpha}_{<}]\,\mathrm{id}(\bar{\alpha}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}),

(17)

where
\alpha_{>}
,
\bar{\alpha}_{>}
are functions on

and
\alpha_{<}
,
\bar{\alpha}_{<}
on
[t_{\mathrm{i}},t^{\prime}]

A Markovian process functional is defined analogously as having the form

Recovering the discrete-time formalism
—We expect the continuous-time formalism to reduce to the discrete-time one in the limit of instantaneous operations separated by finite time intervals. Let the operations act on the intervals
[t_{2k},t_{2k+1}]
, which means that the functionals
\mathcal{M}_{r}
are constant over the complementary intervals. Conversely, if the operation intervals
[t_{2k},t_{2k+1}]
are short enough, the process functional
\mathcal{W}
can be approximated to be constant over them.
Hence, when we evaluate the functional Born rule, Eq. (
6
), we can split the functional integral into intervals, where only either
\mathcal{M}_{r}
or
\mathcal{W}
contribute, with boundary conditions at the joining times that are integrated at the last step. In this way, the functional integral for
\mathcal{W}
(resp.,
\mathcal{M}_{r}
), defines the matrix elements (in the coherent-state basis) of a process matrix
\hat{W}
(resp., of a tester
\hat{M}_{r}
). The integration of the matrix elements then recovers the discrete Born rule, Eq. (
1
), see Appendix
E
for details. Furthermore, in this construction, functional properties, including positive semidefiniteness, causality, normalization, and Markovianity, imply the corresponding properties for the reconstructed discrete process matrix and tester.

Phase-space and position-space Born rule
—Upon making the change of variables
\alpha{=}{(x+\mathrm{i}p)}/{\sqrt{2}}

and integrating out the momentum degree of freedom in Eq. (

This is not always the case because, in general, the functional momentum integration does not decompose into separate integrations for

\mathcal{M}_{r}[x,\bar{x},p,\bar{p}]=\mathcal{M}^{0}_{r}[x,\bar{x}]

\Pr[r]=\int\mathscr{D}[x,\bar{x}]\mathcal{M}^{0}_{r}[x,\bar{x}]\mathcal{W}[x,\bar{x}],

(20)

where the position-space process functional is given by
\mathcal{W}[x,\bar{x}]=\int\mathscr{D}[p,\bar{p}]\mathcal{W}[x,\bar{x},p,\bar{p}]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\mathrm{i}}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\differential t\left(\dot{x}p-\dot{\bar{x}}\bar{p}\right)}

F

and

t_{\mathrm{i}}<t^{\prime}<t_{\mathrm{f}}
, and we shall further assume continuity,
\hat{K}_{r}({t_{\mathrm{i}}\!\!\shortleftarrow\!t_{\mathrm{i}}})=\id
. Normalization of the Kraus operators is expressed as
\int\mathscr{D}[r]\hat{K}_{r}^{\dagger}(t_{\mathrm{f}}\!\!\shortleftarrow\!\!t_{\text{i}})\hat{K}_{r}(t_{\mathrm{f}}\!\!\shortleftarrow\!\!t_{\text{i}})=\id
, where, as usual in functional integration, a diverging normalization constant is absorbed in the measure
\mathscr{D}[r]
.

Upon defining the non-Hermitian dynamics
\hat{H}_{r}(t)=\mathrm{i}\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}[{\hat{K}_{r}(t+\Delta t\!\!\shortleftarrow\!\!t)-\id}]/{\Delta t}
,
these assumptions ensure that the measurement-induced dynamics can be written in terms of the non-Hermitian Schrodinger equation:

\text{i}\partial_{t}|{\tilde{\psi}_{r}(t)}\rangle\!=\!\hat{H}_{r}(t)|{\tilde{\psi}_{r}(t)}\rangle
.
Therefore, the Kraus operator is

\hat{K}_{r}({t_{\mathrm{f}}\!\!\shortleftarrow\!t_{\mathrm{i}}})=\mathcal{T}\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\int_{t_{\mathrm{i}}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\mathrm{d}t\hat{H}_{r}(t)}.

(22)

In this case, one can show that the operation functional—up to a normalization constant—is

{\mathcal{M}}_{r}[\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha}]=\mathcal{K}_{r}[\alpha]\mathcal{K}_{r}^{*}[\bar{\alpha}],

(23)

where the Kraus functional is defined as

\mathcal{K}_{r}[\alpha]{:=}\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\mathrm{d}tH_{r}(\alpha)}
, where
H_{r}(\alpha)
is the Husimi
Q
-function of
\hat{H}_{r}
as defined previously. A particular example is continuous weak position measurement
56
;
28
;
30
, modeled by a Kraus operator of the form (
22
), with the anti-Hermitian Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{r}\!:=\!{-\ii(r-\hat{x})^{2}}/({4\tau_{m}})
, where
\tau_{m}
, called the measurement time, characterizes the measurement strength. Noting that this Hamiltonian is normally ordered up to a readout-independent constant, we can readily calculate its
Q
function and obtain the Kraus functional

\mathcal{K}[x,p]=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{4\tau_{m}}\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\mathrm{d}t(r(t)-x(t))^{2}}.

(24)

As it does not depend on momentum, this can be combined directly with the position-space representation of the process.

Generalized Caldeira-Leggett process
—As a concrete application of our formalism, we consider the generalized Caldeira-Leggett processes under continuous-time position measurements.
The Caldeira-Leggett model describes a harmonic oscillator (system) that interacts linearly with a harmonic environment through a time-independent position-position coupling
8
;
7
. Here we allow the coupling to be time-dependent and assume that system and environment are initially in a product state. The position-space process functional is Gaussian, i.e.,
\mathcal{\widetilde{W}}^{\mathrm{CL}}[x,\bar{x}]=\mathrm{e}^{\ii S^{\mathrm{eff}}[x,\,\bar{x}]}\rho(x_{\mathrm{i}},,\bar{x}_{\mathrm{i}})
, where
S^{\mathrm{eff}}[x,\,\bar{x}]:=S_{0}[x,\,\bar{x}]+S^{\mathrm{FV}}[x,\,\bar{x}]
is the sum of the system’s free action and the FV influence action due to the position-coupling to the environment. Upon introducing the compact notation
\bm{x}(t)=\left(x(t),\bar{x}(t)\right)^{T}
, one can show that

S_{0}[\bm{x}]=\frac{m}{2}\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\left[\dot{\bm{x}}^{T}(t)\sigma_{z}\dot{\bm{x}}(t)-\omega_{0}^{2}\bm{x}^{T}(t)\sigma_{z}\bm{x}(t)\right]
and

S^{\mathrm{FV}}[\bm{x}]=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\differential s\bm{x}^{T}(t)\tilde{\bm{A}}(t,s)\bm{x}(s)
, where
\sigma_{z}
is the third Pauli matrix and
\tilde{\bm{A}}(t,s)
is a
2\times 2
matrix satisfying
\tilde{\bm{A}}^{T}(s,t)=\tilde{\bm{A}}(t,s)
.

When the initial state is Gaussian, one can show that

\mathrm{Pr}[r]\propto\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2\tau_{\mathrm{m}}}\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential sr(t)R(t,\,s)r(s)+\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}b(s)r(s)}

(25)

where
R(t,\,s):=\delta(t-s)-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{ij}\tilde{G}_{ij}(t,\,s)+g(t)h(s)
is the inverse of the two-time connected correlation function of the readout;
\tilde{\bm{G}}(t,s)
is some intrinsic Green’s function of the damped system;
g(t)
,
h(t)
and
b(t)
are determined by the
system’s intrinsic Green’s function as well as the initial state

\rho
.
On the other hand, in the projective limit
\tau_{m}\to 0
, we find

\mathrm{Pr}[r]{\propto}\rho\left(r(t_{\mathrm{i}}),\,r(t_{\mathrm{i}})\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\differential sr(t)\sum_{ij}\tilde{A}_{ij}(t,s)r(s)}
,
which holds for an arbitrary initial state of the system, see Appendix
H
for more details.

Appendix A
Conventions

Here is a summary of conventions and notation used in this work.

•

Complex phase-space variables (coherent state representation) are denoted with Greek letters:
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\dots\in\mathbb{C}
.

•

Position variables are denoted as
x,y,z,\dots\in\mathbb{R}
. Variables associated with their conjugate momenta are
p_{x},p_{y},p_{z},\dots\in\mathbb{R}
. If a single pair of position-momentum variables is used, they are denoted as
(x,p)
.

•

Coherent-state and phase-space variables are related by
\alpha=\frac{x+\ii p_{x}}{\sqrt{2}}
,
\beta=\frac{y+\ii p_{y}}{\sqrt{2}}
,
\gamma=\frac{z+\ii p_{z}}{\sqrt{2}},\dots

•

Rows vs columns:
\matrixelement{x}{\hat\rho}{\bar{x}},\matrixelement{\alpha}{\hat\rho}{\bar{\alpha} },\dots
This only for
x
and
\bar{x}
“at the same time”, not e.g., elements of unitary or Kraus operators (where matrix elements are instead distinguished as initial and final, e.g.
\matrixelement{x_{\mathrm{f}}}{\hat U}{x_{\mathrm{i}}}
).

•

Operators always come with hats and their matrix elements contains no hats, e.g.,
\rho(x,\bar{x})=\matrixelement{x}{\hat\rho}{\bar{x}}
.

•

For observables and quantities usually comes with superscript S, SE, SM, or TOT, indicating the degress of freedom that they act on.

•

Functional integration:
\int\mathscr{D}[\alpha]
or
\int\mathscr{D}[x]
; Functionals: calligraphic font and square brackets, as in
\mathcal{K}[\alpha]
or
\mathcal{K}[x]
.

•

Initial and final time:
t_{\mathrm{i}}
and
t_{\mathrm{f}}
; Intermediate time:
t^{\prime}
; time integration variables:
t
and then
s
.

•

Coherent-state notation: functions of coherent states, typically denoted
H(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
in the literature, are simply denoted
H(\alpha)
.

•

Measure in the overcompleteness relation of coherent state
\int\differential\alpha\coloneqq\int\frac{\differential\text{Re}\alpha\differential\text{Im}\alpha}{\pi}

Appendix B
Review of coherent-state and phase-space path integrals

B.1
Coherent-state representation

For the sake for simplicity we consider a single-mode Bosonic Hamiltonian
that is normally ordered, i.e.,

H(\hat{a}^{\dagger},\,\hat{a})\equiv\;:\!H(\hat{a}^{\dagger},\,\hat{a})\!:

(26)

The overcompleteness relation is

\int\mathrm{d}\alpha\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha}=1,

(27)

where the measure is defined as

\mathrm{d}\alpha:=\frac{\mathrm{d}\text{Re}\alpha\mathrm{d}\text{Im}\alpha}{\pi}

(28)

We note that

\langle\alpha|\alpha^{\prime}\rangle=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}(|\alpha|^{2}+|\alpha^{\prime}|^{2})+\alpha^{*}\alpha^{\prime}}

(29)

Therefore we find

\langle\alpha_{\text{f}}|\hat{U}(t_{\text{f}}\!\!\shortleftarrow\!\!t_{\text{i}})|\alpha_{\text{i}}\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}\alpha_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\alpha_{N-1}\langle\alpha_{N}|e^{-\text{i}\hat{H}\epsilon}|\alpha_{N-1}\rangle\cdots\langle{\alpha_{1}|e^{-\text{i}\hat{H}\epsilon}|\alpha_{0}}\rangle,

(30)

where
\alpha_{N}:=\alpha_{\text{f}}
,
\alpha_{0}:=\alpha_{\text{i}}

and
\epsilon=(t_{\text{f}}-t_{\text{i}})/N
. It is straightforward to show that

\langle{\alpha_{k}|e^{-\text{i}\hat{H}\epsilon}|\alpha_{k-1}}\rangle\approx\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}[\alpha_{k}^{*}(\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{k-1})-(\alpha_{k}^{*}-\alpha_{k-1}^{*})\alpha_{k-1}]-\text{i}H(\alpha_{k}^{*},\,\alpha_{k-1})\epsilon}

(31)

Therefore, we find

\langle{\alpha_{\text{f}}|\hat{U}(t_{\text{f}}\!\!\shortleftarrow\!\!t_{\text{i}})|\alpha_{\text{i}}}\rangle\approx\int\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{k}\mathrm{e}^{\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{M}-\frac{1}{2}[\alpha_{k}^{*}(\frac{\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{k-1}}{\epsilon})-(\frac{\alpha_{k}^{*}-\alpha_{k-1}^{*}}{\epsilon})\alpha_{k}]-\text{i}H(\alpha_{k}^{*},\,\alpha_{k-1})\right\}\epsilon}

(32)

In the continuum limit
N\to\infty
, we find

\langle{\alpha_{\text{f}}|\hat{U}(t_{\text{f}}\!\!\shortleftarrow\!\!t_{\text{i}})|\alpha_{\text{i}}}\rangle=\int_{\alpha(t_{\text{i}})=\alpha_{\text{i}}}^{\alpha(t_{\text{f}})=\alpha_{\text{f}}}\mathscr{D}[\alpha]\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}(\dot{\alpha}^{*}\alpha-\alpha^{*}\dot{\alpha})}\mathcal{U}[\alpha]

(33)

where

\mathscr{D}[\alpha]\equiv\lim_{N\to\infty}\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{k}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\text{Re}\alpha_{k}\mathrm{d}\text{Im}\alpha_{k}}{\pi}

(34)

and

\mathcal{U}[\alpha]:=\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}H(\alpha)\mathrm{d}t}

(35)

Alternatively, we can rewrite

\langle\alpha_{\text{f}}|\hat{U}(t_{\text{i}}\!\!\shortleftarrow\!\!t_{\text{i}})|\alpha_{\text{i}}\rangle=\int_{\alpha(t_{\text{i}})=\alpha_{\text{i}}}^{\alpha(t_{\text{f}})=\alpha_{\text{f}}}\mathscr{D}[\alpha]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\imaginary(\dot{\alpha}^{*}\alpha)}\mathcal{U}[\alpha]

(36)

We conclude by emphasizing that the boundary condition
\alpha(t_{\text{i}})=\alpha_{\text{i}}

indicates that neither
\alpha_{\text{i}}
nor
\alpha_{\text{i}}^{*}

is integrated over while
\alpha(t_{\text{f}})=\alpha_{\text{f}}

indicates neither
\alpha_{\text{f}}
nor
\alpha_{\text{f}}^{*}

is integrated over. Indeed, from the discrete definition (
32
),
it is clear that
\langle\alpha_{\text{f}}|\hat{U}(t_{\text{f}}\!\!\shortleftarrow\!\!t_{\text{i}})|\alpha_{\text{i}}\rangle

depends on
\alpha_{\text{i}}
,
\alpha_{\text{i}}^{*}
,
\alpha_{\text{f}}
,
and
\alpha_{\text{f}}^{*}
.

B.2
Connection with the phase-space representation

The coherent-state path integral reviewed above is tightly connected, but not identical, to the phase-space path integral. The latter is a representation of the
position-space
propagator, obtained by inserting into it alternating position and momentum resolutions of identity. The result is

\matrixelement{x_{\text{f}}}{\hat{U}(\tfti)}{x_{\text{i}}}=\int_{x(t_{\mathrm{i}})=x_{\text{i}}}^{x(t_{\mathrm{f}})=x_{\text{f}}}\mathscr{D}[x,p]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\dot{x}p}\mathcal{U}^{\textrm{PS}}[x,p],

(37)

where
\mathscr{D}[x,p]\coloneqq\lim_{N\to\infty}\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{k}\mathrm{d}p_{k}}{2\pi}
,

\mathcal{U}^{\textrm{PS}}[x,p]\coloneqq\ee^{-\ii\int\;\differential th(x,p)},

(38)

and
h(x,p)
is a phase-representation of the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
, whose precise definition depends on the time-slicing prescription used to define the path integral. Usually, a mid-point prescription is used, which results in
h(x,p)
being equal to the Weyl symbol (i.e., the Wigner function) of
\hat{H}
. However, here we do not necessarily make this identification, since we want to connect this form with the coherent-state formulation, which typically uses a different definition of the path integral and corresponding “classical Hamiltonian” (the
Q
-representation in the rest of this work, but the discussion remains valid for different choices).

To connect the two representations, we rewrite the “Berry phase” term from the coherent-state representation as

\int_{t_{\mathrm{i}}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\differential t\Im{\dot{\alpha}^*\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{\mathrm{i}}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\differential t(\dot{x}p-x\dot{p})=\int_{t_{\mathrm{i}}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\differential t\left[\dot{x}p-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\differential}{\differential t}(xp)\right]=\int_{t_{\mathrm{i}}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\differential t\dot{x}p-\frac{1}{2}(x_{\text{f}}p_{\text{f}}-x_{\text{i}}p_{\text{i}}),

(39)

where we use
\alpha=\frac{x+\ii p}{\sqrt{2}}
, as usual. So we can rewrite the coherent-state propagator, Eq. (
36
), as

\matrixelement{\alpha_{\text{f}}}{\hat{U}(\tfti)}{\alpha_{\text{i}}}=\int_{\alpha(t_{\mathrm{i}})=\alpha_{\text{i}}}^{\alpha(t_{\mathrm{f}})=\alpha_{\text{f}}}\mathscr{D}[x,p]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\dot{x}p}\mathcal{U}\left[\frac{x+\ii p}{\sqrt{2}}\right]\ee^{\frac{\ii}{2}(x_{\text{i}}p_{\text{i}}-x_{\text{f}}p_{\text{f}})},

(40)

To complete the identification between the two path integrals, we need to convert the coherent-state propagator into the position-space one. Note that, in the coherent-state path integral, both real and imaginary parts of
\alpha(t_{\mathrm{i}})
and
\alpha(t_{\mathrm{f}})
are fixed (corresponding to initial and final positions and momenta), while in the phase-space path integral only initial and final positions are fixed. This means that we need to integrate out
p_{\text{i}}
,
p_{\text{f}}
in (
40
) in order to recover the phase-space path integral. In order to also recover the position-space propagator, we use the relation

\int\frac{\differential p}{\pi}\ee^{-\frac{\ii}{2}xp}\ket{\alpha}\propto\ket{x},

(41)

which can be proven using the position representation of the coherent state:

\psi_{\alpha}(y)\coloneqq\innerproduct{y}{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\pi^{\frac{1}{4}}}\ee^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(y-x\right)^{2}+\ii yp-\frac{\ii}{2}xp}.

(42)

Indeed, we find

\int\frac{\differential p}{\pi}\ee^{-\frac{\ii}{2}xp}\psi_{\alpha}(y)=\frac{1}{\pi^{\frac{1}{4}}}\int\frac{\differential p}{\pi}\ee^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(y-x\right)^{2}+\ii(y-x)p}=\frac{2}{\pi^{\frac{1}{4}}}\delta(y-x).

(43)

Therefore, multiplying left and right of Eq. (
40
) by the factor
\ee^{-\frac{\ii}{2}(x_{\text{i}}p_{\text{i}}-x_{\text{f}}p_{\text{f}})}
, and integrating initial and final momenta, we obtain

\int\frac{\differential p_{\text{i}}}{\pi}\frac{\differential p_{\text{f}}}{\pi}\ee^{-\frac{\ii}{2}(x_{\text{i}}p_{\text{i}}-x_{\text{f}}p_{\text{f}})}\matrixelement{\alpha_{\text{f}}}{\hat{U}(\tfti)}{\alpha_{\text{i}}}\propto\matrixelement{x_{\text{f}}}{\hat{U}(\tfti)}{x_{\text{i}}}=\int_{x(t_{\mathrm{i}})=x_{\text{i}}}^{x(t_{\mathrm{f}})=x_{\text{f}}}\mathscr{D}[x,p]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\dot{x}p}\mathcal{U}\left[\frac{x+\ii p}{\sqrt{2}}\right].

(44)

Comparing this with Eq. (
37
), we conclude that, up to a multiplicative factor (omitted as in most of this work), the functional in the coherent-state path integral coincides with the phase-space one, up to the usual change of variables:

\mathcal{U}^{\textrm{PS}}[x,p]=\mathcal{U}\left[\frac{x+\ii p}{\sqrt{2}}\right].

(45)

In the following, we will omit the
{}^{\textrm{PS}}
superscript, and identify the functionals by the variables on which they depend.

Let us remark again that, in the above conversion, a prescription for the definition of the path integrals is understood, corresponding to a particular choice of the classical Hamiltonian, and the conversion holds only as long as the same prescription for the different path integrals is taken.

\mathrm{Pr}[r]=\!\!\int_{\alpha(t_{\mathrm{f}})=\bar{\alpha}(t_{\mathrm{f}})}\!\mathscr{D}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\mathrm{Im}(\dot{\alpha}^{*}\alpha-\dot{\bar{\alpha}}^{*}\bar{\alpha})}\mathcal{M}_{r}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}],

\displaystyle\text{Pr}[r]

\displaystyle\int\mathscr{D}[\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha},]\mathrm{id}(\bar{\alpha}(t_{\mathrm{f}}),\,\alpha(t_{\mathrm{f}}))F[\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha}]

\displaystyle\int\mathscr{D}[\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha}]\mathrm{id}(\bar{\alpha}(t_{\mathrm{f}}),\,\alpha(t_{\mathrm{f}}))F[\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha}]

and
\alpha(t)
and similar observation also holds for the functional integration over

and
\beta(t)

D.1
Positivity

Let us first consider the positivity of the process functional
\mathcal{W}

and
\widetilde{W}
for pure unitary system and initial pure states,
where
\mathcal{\widetilde{W}}[\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha}]=\mathcal{U}_{0}[\alpha]\mathcal{U}_{0}^{*}[\bar{\alpha}]\phi(\alpha(t_{\text{i}}))\phi^{*}(\bar{\alpha}(t_{\text{i}}))
.
Clearly the functional integral can be broken into

\int\mathscr{D}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]=\int\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{i}}\mathrm{d}\bar{\alpha}_{\text{i}}\int\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{f}}\mathrm{d}\bar{\alpha}_{\text{f}}\int_{\alpha(t_{\text{i}})=\alpha_{\text{i}}}^{\alpha(t_{\text{f}})=\alpha_{\text{f}}}\mathscr{D}[\alpha]\int_{\bar{\alpha}(t_{\text{i}})=\bar{\alpha}_{i}}^{\bar{\alpha}(t_{\mathrm{f}})=\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{f}}}\mathscr{D}[\bar{\alpha}]

(63)

Upon defining
R(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}},\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}):=\int_{\alpha(t_{\text{i}})=\alpha_{\text{i}}}^{\alpha(t_{\text{f}})=\alpha_{\text{f}}}\mathscr{D}[\alpha]\mathcal{U}_{0}[\alpha]\psi[\alpha]
,
then we find

\displaystyle\int\mathscr{D}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]\psi^{*}[\alpha]\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}[\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha}]\psi[\bar{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{f}}\mathrm{d}\bar{\alpha}_{\text{f}}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{i}}\mathrm{d}\bar{\alpha}_{\text{i}}R(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}},\alpha_{\mathrm{f}})R^{*}(\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{i}},\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{f}})\phi(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}})\phi^{*}(\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{i}})

\displaystyle=\big|\int\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{f}}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{i}}R(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}},\alpha_{\mathrm{f}})\phi(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}})\big|^{2}\geq 0

(64)

Similarly, we find

\int\mathscr{D}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]\psi^{*}[\alpha]\mathcal{W}[\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha}]\psi[\bar{\alpha}]=\int\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{f}}\mathrm{d}\bar{\alpha}_{\text{f}}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{i}}\mathrm{d}\bar{\alpha}_{\text{i}}R(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}},\alpha_{\mathrm{f}})R^{*}(\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{i}},\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{f}})\phi(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}})\phi^{*}(\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{i}})\mathrm{id}(\bar{\alpha}_{\text{f}},\,\alpha_{\text{f}})

(65)

Using Eq. (
56
), we immediately conclude that

\int\mathscr{D}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]\psi^{*}[\alpha]\mathcal{W}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]\psi[\bar{\alpha}]=\int\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{f}}\,\left|\int\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{i}}R(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}},\alpha_{\mathrm{f}})\phi(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}})\right|^{2}\geq 0

(66)

The idea of generalizing to the generic case is to regard the system-ancilla (“ancilla” refers to either environment or meter)
as a joint system, so that the above argument applies again. Without loss of generality, we can take initial system-environment state
\rho^{\mathrm{SE}}
to be pure denoted as
|\phi_{k}\rangle\langle\phi_{k}|
and final measurement
E_{r}
to be a rank-one denoted as
|\xi_{sr}\rangle\langle\xi_{sr}|
. Since both
\rho^{\mathrm{SE}}
and
E_{r}
are positive semidefinite operators, upon spectral decomposition, it is straightforward to see that if positivity holds for rank one initial system-environment state and rank-one final measurement, it will also hold for the high-rank case.

Let us recall

\displaystyle\mathcal{\widetilde{W}}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\int\mathscr{D}[\beta,\bar{\beta}]\ee^{\ii\int\differential t\mathrm{Im}(\dot{\beta}^{*}\beta-\dot{\bar{\beta}}^{*}\bar{\beta})}\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SE}}(\alpha,\beta)\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SE}*}(\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})\phi(\alpha(t_{\mathrm{i}}),\beta(t_{\mathrm{i}}))\phi^{*}(\bar{\alpha}(t_{\mathrm{i}}),\bar{\beta}(t_{\mathrm{i}}))\mathrm{id}(\bar{\beta}(t_{\mathrm{f}}),\,\beta(t_{\mathrm{f}})),

(67)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{r}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\int\mathscr{D}[\gamma,\bar{\gamma}]\ee^{\ii\int\differential t\mathrm{Im}(\dot{\gamma}^{*}\gamma-\dot{\bar{\gamma}}^{*}\bar{\gamma})}\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SM}}(\alpha,\gamma)\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SM}*}(\bar{\alpha},\bar{\gamma})\xi_{r}(\bar{\gamma}(t_{\mathrm{f}}))\xi_{r}^{*}(\gamma(t_{\mathrm{f}})),

(68)

We define

\displaystyle R(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}},\beta_{\mathrm{i}};\,\alpha_{\mathrm{f}},\beta_{\mathrm{f}})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\alpha(t_{\text{i}})=\alpha_{\text{i}},\,\beta(t_{\text{i}})=\alpha_{\text{i}}}^{\alpha(t_{\text{f}})=\alpha_{\text{f}},\,\beta(t_{\mathrm{f}})=\beta_{\mathrm{f}}}\mathscr{D}[\alpha,\,\beta]\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SE}}(\alpha,\beta)\psi[\alpha]

(69)

\displaystyle T(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}},\gamma_{\mathrm{i}};\,\alpha_{\mathrm{f}},\gamma_{\mathrm{f}})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\alpha(t_{\text{i}})=\alpha_{\text{i}},\,\gamma(t_{\text{i}})=\alpha_{\text{i}}}^{\alpha(t_{\text{f}})=\alpha_{\text{f}},\,\gamma(t_{\mathrm{f}})=\gamma_{\mathrm{f}}}\mathscr{D}[\alpha,\,\beta]\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SM}}(\alpha,\beta)\psi[\alpha]

(70)

It is then straightforward to check, upon using Eq. (
56
),

\displaystyle\int\mathscr{D}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]\psi^{*}[\alpha]\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}[\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha}]\psi[\bar{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{i}}\mathrm{d}\beta_{\text{i}}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{f}}\mathrm{d}\beta_{\text{f}}\mathrm{d}\bar{\alpha}_{\text{i}}\mathrm{d}\bar{\beta}_{\text{i}}\mathrm{d}\bar{\alpha}_{\text{f}}\mathrm{d}\bar{\beta}_{\text{f}}

\displaystyle\times R(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}},\beta_{\mathrm{i}};\,\alpha_{\mathrm{f}},\beta_{\mathrm{f}})R^{*}(\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{i}},\bar{\beta}_{\mathrm{i}};\,\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{f}},\bar{\beta}_{\mathrm{f}})\phi(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}},\beta_{\mathrm{i}})\phi^{*}(\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{i}},\bar{\beta}_{\mathrm{i}})\mathrm{id}(\bar{\beta}_{\mathrm{f}},\,\beta_{\mathrm{f}})

\displaystyle=\int d\beta_{\mathrm{f}}\big|\int\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{i}}\mathrm{d}\beta_{\text{i}}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{f}}R(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}},\beta_{\mathrm{i}};\,\alpha_{\mathrm{f}},\beta_{\mathrm{f}})\phi(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}},\beta_{\mathrm{i}})\big|{}^{2}\geq 0

(71)

Note that
\mathcal{W}[\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha}]
is related to
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}[\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha}]

up to
\mathrm{id}(\bar{\alpha}(t_{\mathrm{f}}),\alpha(t_{\mathrm{f}}))
,
using Eq. (

\int\mathscr{D}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]\psi^{*}[\alpha]\mathcal{W}[\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha}]\psi[\bar{\alpha}]=\int d\beta_{\mathrm{f}}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{f}}\big|\int\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{i}}\mathrm{d}\beta_{\text{i}}R(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}},\beta_{\mathrm{i}};\,\alpha_{\mathrm{f}},\beta_{\mathrm{f}})\phi(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}},\beta_{\mathrm{i}})\big|{}^{2}\geq 0

(72)

On the other hand, we note that there is no final-time boundary condition
on the functional integration over
\gamma

\displaystyle\int\mathscr{D}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]\psi^{*}[\alpha]\mathcal{M}_{r}[\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha}]\psi[\bar{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{i}}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{\text{i}}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{f}}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{\text{f}}\mathrm{d}\bar{\alpha}_{\text{i}}\mathrm{d}\bar{\gamma}_{\text{i}}\mathrm{d}\bar{\alpha}_{\text{f}}\mathrm{d}\bar{\gamma}_{\text{f}}

\displaystyle\times{T}(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}},\gamma_{\mathrm{i}};\,\alpha_{\mathrm{f}},\gamma_{\mathrm{f}}){T}^{*}(\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{i}},\bar{\gamma}_{\mathrm{i}};\,\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{f}},\bar{\gamma}_{\mathrm{f}})\xi_{r}(\bar{\gamma}_{\mathrm{f}})\xi_{r}^{*}(\gamma_{\mathrm{f}})

(73)

D.2
Causality for the closed-future process functional
\mathcal{W}
and the open-future process functional
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}

D.2.1
Causality for discrete-time process

Let us first recall the causality condition for the discrete-time process matrices
13
: A positive semidefinite operator
\hat{W}\in\mathcal{L}(\otimes_{i=0}^{2N+1}\mathcal{H}^{i})
is causally ordered (or satisfies causality) if, for
k=0,\dots,N
, there exists a set of operators
\hat{\widetilde{W}}^{0:2k}\in\mathcal{L}(\otimes_{i=0}^{2k}\mathcal{H}^{i})
such that

\displaystyle\hat{W}

\displaystyle=\hat{\widetilde{W}}^{0:2N}\!\!\!\otimes\id^{2N+1},

(74)

\displaystyle\Tr_{2k}\hat{\widetilde{W}}^{0:2k}

\displaystyle=\hat{\widetilde{W}}^{0:2k-2}\!\!\!\otimes\id^{2k-1},\quad k=1,\dots,N,

(75)

\displaystyle\Tr\hat{\widetilde{W}}^{0:0}

\displaystyle=1.

(76)

In words, calling even times “inputs” and odd times “outputs”, a causally ordered process matrix has to be identity on the last output. This defines a process matrix,
\hat{\widetilde{W}}^{0:2N}
, which ends with an input space (and can be called “open future”, in alignment with the terminology introduced for functionals). Tracing out the last input space yield identity on the next-to-last output, leaving again an open-future process ending on the next-to-last input. Such a condition iterates, identifying a set of causally-ordered process matrices that end on an input space, which yields identity on the previous output when traced out. When the iteration reaches the first input, the remaining process matrix
\hat{\widetilde{W}}^{0:0}
is required to be a normalized density matrix.

D.2.2
Causality for the continuous-time processes

The continuous-time analog of Eq. (
75
) is given by

\int^{\alpha(t_{\mathrm{f}})=\bar{\alpha}(t_{\mathrm{f}})}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha(t^{\prime})=\alpha^{\prime}\\
\bar{\alpha}(t^{\prime})=\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathscr{D}[\alpha_{>},\bar{\alpha}_{>}]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\mathrm{i}}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\differential t\imaginary(\dot{\alpha}^{*}\alpha-\dot{\bar{\alpha}}^{*}\bar{\alpha})}\mathcal{W}[\alpha,\bar{\alpha}]\\
=\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\mathrm{i}}}^{t^{\prime}}\differential t\imaginary(\dot{\alpha}^{*}\alpha-\dot{\bar{\alpha}}^{*}\bar{\alpha})}\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}[\alpha_{<},\bar{\alpha}_{<}]\,\mathrm{id}(\bar{\alpha}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}),

(77)

which we now prove.

First, let us consider a simple yet insightful case where there is
no environmental degrees of freedom. In this case, the process functional
is divisible (Markovian), i.e.,

\mathcal{W}[\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha}]={\rho(\alpha(t_{\mathrm{i}}),\bar{\alpha}(t_{\mathrm{i}}))}{\underset{\widetilde{\hphantom{\mathcal{W}}}}{\mathcal{W}}}[\alpha_{>},\,\bar{\alpha}_{>}]{\underset{\widetilde{\hphantom{\mathcal{W}}}}{\mathcal{W}}}[\alpha_{<},\,\bar{\alpha}_{<}]\,\mathrm{id}(\bar{\alpha}(t_{\mathrm{f}}),\,\alpha(t_{\mathrm{f}})).

(78)

where

\displaystyle{\underset{\widetilde{\hphantom{\mathcal{W}}}}{\mathcal{W}}}[\alpha_{>},\,\bar{\alpha}_{>}]

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{U}_{0}[\alpha_{>}]\mathcal{U}_{0}^{*}[\bar{\alpha}_{>}]\qquad\text{for }\alpha_{>}:[t^{\prime},t_{\mathrm{f}}]\rightarrow\mathbb{C},

(79)

\displaystyle{\underset{\widetilde{\hphantom{\mathcal{W}}}}{\mathcal{W}}}[\alpha_{<},\,\bar{\alpha}_{<}]

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{U}_{0}[\alpha_{<}]\mathcal{U}_{0}^{*}[\bar{\alpha}_{<}]\qquad\text{for }\alpha_{<}:[t_{\mathrm{i}},t^{\prime}]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

(80)

We note that

\displaystyle\int_{\alpha_{>}(t^{\prime})=\alpha^{\prime};\,\bar{\alpha}_{>}(t^{\prime})=\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}}^{\alpha(t_{\mathrm{f}})=\bar{\alpha}(t_{\mathrm{f}})}\mathscr{D}[\alpha_{>},\,\bar{\alpha}_{>}]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\mathrm{Im}(\dot{\alpha}_{>}^{*}\alpha_{>}-\dot{\bar{\alpha}}_{>}^{*}\bar{\alpha}_{>})}{\underset{\widetilde{\hphantom{\mathcal{W}}}}{\mathcal{W}}}[\alpha_{>},\,\bar{\alpha}_{>}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{f}}\int_{\bar{\alpha}_{>}(t^{\prime})=\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}}^{\bar{\alpha}(t_{\text{f}})=\alpha_{\text{f}}}\mathscr{D}[\bar{\alpha}_{>}]\ee^{-\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\mathrm{Im}(\dot{\bar{\alpha}}_{>}^{*}\bar{\alpha}_{>})}\mathcal{U}_{0}^{*}[\bar{\alpha}_{>}]\int_{\alpha_{>}(t^{\prime})=\alpha^{\prime}}^{\alpha_{>}(t_{\text{f}})=\alpha_{\text{f}}}\mathscr{D}[\alpha_{>}]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\mathrm{Im}(\dot{\alpha}_{>}^{*}\alpha_{>})}\mathcal{U}_{0}[\alpha_{>}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\text{f}}\langle{\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}|\hat{U}_{0}^{\dagger}(t_{\text{f}}\leftarrow t^{\prime})|\alpha_{\text{f}}}\rangle\langle{\alpha_{\text{f}}|\hat{U}_{0}(t_{\text{f}}\leftarrow t^{\prime})|\alpha^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{id}(\bar{\alpha}^{\prime},\,\alpha^{\prime})

(81)

With above formula, we can easily check

\displaystyle\int_{\alpha_{>}(t^{\prime})=\alpha^{\prime};\,\bar{\alpha}_{>}(t^{\prime})=\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}}\mathscr{D}[\alpha_{>},\bar{\alpha}_{>}]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\mathrm{Im}(\dot{\alpha}^{*}\alpha-\dot{\bar{\alpha}}^{*}\bar{\alpha})}\mathcal{W}[\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\mathrm{Im}(\dot{\alpha}_{<}^{*}\alpha_{<}-\dot{\bar{\alpha}}_{<}^{*}\bar{\alpha}_{<})}{\underset{\widetilde{\hphantom{\mathcal{W}}}}{\mathcal{W}}}[\alpha_{<},\,\bar{\alpha}_{<}]\int_{\alpha_{>}(t^{\prime})=\alpha^{\prime};\,\bar{\alpha}_{>}(t^{\prime})=\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}}^{\alpha_{>}(t_{\text{f}})=\bar{\alpha}_{>}(t_{\text{f}})}\mathscr{D}[\alpha_{>},\bar{\alpha}_{>}]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\mathrm{Im}(\dot{\alpha}_{>}^{*}\alpha_{>}-\dot{\bar{\alpha}}_{>}^{*}\bar{\alpha}_{>})}{\underset{\widetilde{\hphantom{\mathcal{W}}}}{\mathcal{W}}}[\alpha_{>},\,\bar{\alpha}_{>}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\mathrm{Im}(\dot{\alpha}_{<}^{*}\alpha_{<}-\dot{\bar{\alpha}}_{<}^{*}\bar{\alpha}_{<})}{\underset{\widetilde{\hphantom{\mathcal{W}}}}{\mathcal{W}}}[\alpha_{<},\,\bar{\alpha}_{<}]\mathrm{id}(\bar{\alpha}^{\prime},\,\alpha^{\prime})

(82)

Similar with the proof of positivity, the idea of generalizing to
the generic case is to regard the system-environment as a joint system,
so that the above argument applies again. More precisely,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}[\alpha,\,\bar{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\beta^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\bar{\beta}^{\prime}\int_{\beta_{<}(t^{\prime})=\beta^{\prime},\,\bar{\beta}_{<}(t^{\prime})=\bar{\beta}^{\prime}}\mathscr{D}[\beta_{<},\,\bar{\beta}_{<}]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\mathrm{Im}(\dot{\beta}_{<}^{*}\beta_{<}-\dot{\bar{\beta}}_{<}^{*}\bar{\beta}_{<})}

\displaystyle\times\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SE}}[\alpha_{<},\,\beta_{<}]\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SE}*}[\bar{\alpha}_{<},\,\bar{\beta}_{<}]\rho_{\text{i}}(\alpha_{\text{i}},\beta_{\text{i}};\bar{\alpha}_{\text{i}},\bar{\beta}_{\text{i}})

\displaystyle\times\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta_{>}(t^{\prime})=\beta^{\prime},\,\bar{\beta}_{>}(t^{\prime})=\bar{\beta}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}^{\beta_{>}(t_{\text{f}})=\bar{\beta}_{>}(t_{\text{f}})}\mathscr{D}[\beta_{>},\,\bar{\beta}_{>}]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\mathrm{Im}(\dot{\beta}_{>}^{*}\beta_{>}-\dot{\bar{\beta}}_{>}^{*}\bar{\beta}_{>})}\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SE}}[\alpha_{>},\,\beta_{>}]\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SE}*}[\bar{\alpha}_{>},\,\bar{\beta}_{>}]

(83)

where we have use the identity

\int_{\beta(t_{\text{f}})=\bar{\beta}(t_{\text{f}})}\mathscr{D}[\beta,\,\bar{\beta}]=\int\mathrm{d}\beta^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\bar{\beta}^{\prime}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta_{<}(t^{\prime})=\beta^{\prime}\\
\bar{\beta}_{<}(t^{\prime})=\bar{\beta}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathscr{D}[\beta_{<},\,\bar{\beta}_{<}]\int_{\beta_{>}(t^{\prime})=\beta^{\prime},\,\bar{\beta}_{>}(t^{\prime})=\bar{\beta}^{\prime}}^{\beta_{>}(t_{\text{f}})=\bar{\beta}_{>}(t_{\text{f}})}\mathscr{D}[\beta_{>},\,\bar{\beta}_{>}]

(84)

Using a similar trick with Eq. (
81
),
we can show that

\displaystyle\int_{\alpha_{>}(t^{\prime})=\alpha^{\prime};\,\bar{\alpha}_{>}(t^{\prime})=\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}}^{\alpha_{>}(t_{\text{f}})=\bar{\alpha}_{>}(t_{\text{f}})}\mathscr{D}[\alpha_{>},\bar{\alpha}_{>}]\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta_{>}(t^{\prime})=\beta^{\prime},\,\bar{\beta}_{>}(t^{\prime})=\bar{\beta}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}^{\beta_{>}(t_{\text{f}})=\bar{\beta}_{>}(t_{\text{f}})}\mathscr{D}[\beta_{>},\,\bar{\beta}_{>}]

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\differential t\mathrm{Im}(\dot{\alpha}_{>}^{*}\alpha_{>}-\dot{\bar{\alpha}}_{>}^{*}\bar{\alpha}_{>}+\dot{\beta}_{>}^{*}\beta_{>}-\dot{\bar{\beta}}_{>}^{*}\bar{\beta}_{>})}\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SE}}[\alpha_{>},\,\beta_{>}]\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SE}*}[\bar{\alpha}_{>},\,\bar{\beta}_{>}]

\displaystyle=

F.1
Generalized position-space functional Born rule

The purpose of this subsection is to derive a functional Born rule upon integrating momenta, and to prove a particular case in which this reduces to a position-space formula. To be more precise, we first derive a functional Born rule on the
tangent bundle
, namely on the space of positions and velocities, from which we can recover a position-space formula under given conditions.

First, recall from Sec.
B.2
that the functional Born rule can be written in terms of phase space variables as

\Pr[r]=\int\mathscr{D}[x,\bar{x},p,\bar{p}]\mathcal{M}_{r}[x,\bar{x},p,\bar{p}]\mathcal{W}[x,\bar{x},p,\bar{p}]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\mathrm{i}}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\differential t\left(\dot{x}p-\dot{\bar{x}}\bar{p}\right)}.

(142)

Let us now introduce the functional Fourier transform over momentum for a generic phase-space functional
\mathcal{Q}
:

\check{\mathcal{Q}}[x,\bar{x},v,\bar{v}]:=\int\mathscr{D}[p,\bar{p}]\mathcal{Q}[x,\bar{x},p,\bar{p}]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\mathrm{i}}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\differential t\left(vp-\bar{v}\bar{p}\right)}.

(143)

The
position-space
functional corresponding to
\mathcal{Q}[x,\bar{x},p,\bar{p}]
is given by

\mathcal{Q}[x,\bar{x}]:=\check{\mathcal{Q}}[x,\bar{x},\dot{x},\dot{\bar{x}}],

(144)

where, with a small abuse of notation, we use the same symbol
\mathcal{Q}
for phase-space and position-space functionals.

Up to normalization factors (which, as in most of this article, are omitted in functional expressions), the functional Fourier transform is invertible:

\mathcal{Q}[x,\bar{x},p,\bar{p}]=\int\mathscr{D}[v,\bar{v}]\check{\mathcal{Q}}[x,\bar{x},v,\bar{v}]\ee^{-\ii\int_{t_{\mathrm{i}}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\differential t\left(vp-\bar{v}\bar{p}\right)},

(145)

which can be demonstrated using the formula for the functional Dirac delta

\int\mathscr{D}[v]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\mathrm{i}}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\differential tv\left(p-p^{\prime}\right)}=\delta[p-p^{\prime}].

(146)

Plugging Eq. (
145
) into the phase-space Born rule, Eq. (
142
), we obtain

\Pr[r]=\int\mathscr{D}[x,\bar{x},p,\bar{p},v_{1},\bar{v}_{1},v_{2},\bar{v}_{2}]\check{\mathcal{M}}_{r}[x,\bar{x},v_{1},\bar{v}_{1}]\check{\mathcal{W}}[x,\bar{x},v_{2},\bar{v}_{2}]\ee^{\ii\int_{t_{\mathrm{i}}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\differential t\left[(\dot{x}-v_{1}-v_{2})p-(\dot{\bar{x}}-\bar{v}_{1}-\bar{v}_{2})\bar{p}\right]}\\
=\int\mathscr{D}[x,\bar{x},v,\bar{v}]\check{\mathcal{M}}_{r}[x,\bar{x},\dot{x}-v,\dot{\bar{x}}-\bar{v}]\check{\mathcal{W}}[x,\bar{x},v,\bar{v}].

(147)

We can interpret Eq. (
147
) as the
tangent bundle
form of the functional Born rule, where
v,\bar{v}
represent velocities—treated here as independent from positions—and
\check{\mathcal{M}}_{r}
,
\check{\mathcal{W}}
are the tangent-bundle operation and process functionals, respectively. We see that this expression involves a convolution over the velocities that, in general, cannot be simplified to obtain a simple position-space rule. However, simplifications can occur under given conditions. In particular, consider operations that do not depend on momentum:

\mathcal{M}_{r}[x,\bar{x},p,\bar{p}]=\mathcal{M}^{0}_{r}[x,\bar{x}].

(148)

Taking the Fourier transform, we obtain the tangent-bundle functional

Note that

F.2
Constraints on system-meter-environment models

As opposed to the previous subsection, where we derive conditions for operation functionals under which a position Born rule can be written, here we identify conditions on the underlying Hamiltonian model for system-meter-environment coupling for which a position-space formula holds.

Using Hamilton’s equation of motion, we find

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial H^{\mathrm{SE}}}{\partial p_{x}}+\frac{\partial H^{\mathrm{SM}}}{\partial p_{x}},

(151)

\displaystyle\dot{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial H^{\mathrm{SE}}}{\partial p_{y}},

(152)

\displaystyle\dot{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial H^{\mathrm{SM}}}{\partial p_{z}}.

(153)

The total Lagrangian can obtained through the Legendre transform.
Upon inserting Eqs. (
151
-
153
),
the total Lagrangian can be split into two parts

L^{\mathrm{TOT}}=L^{\mathrm{SE}}+L^{\mathrm{SM}}

(154)

where

\displaystyle L^{\mathrm{SE}}

\displaystyle\equiv p_{x}\frac{\partial H^{\mathrm{SE}}}{\partial p_{x}}+p_{y}\frac{\partial H^{\mathrm{SE}}}{\partial p_{y}}-H^{\mathrm{SE}}

(155)

\displaystyle L^{\mathrm{SM}}

\displaystyle\equiv p_{x}\frac{\partial H^{\mathrm{SM}}}{\partial p_{x}}+p_{z}\frac{\partial H^{\mathrm{SM}}}{\partial p_{z}}-H^{\mathrm{SM}}

(156)

This seems to imply that
L^{\mathrm{SE}}
is the system-environment Lagrangian
while
L^{\mathrm{SM}}
is the system-meter Lagrangian. If this observation
were true in general, we would have smooth transition to write down
the functional Born rule in the position space.

However, in general
L^{\mathrm{SE}}
can still depend on the meter’s degrees
of freedom while
L_{\text{ }}^{\mathrm{SM}}
can depend on the environmental
degrees of freedom. To see this, let us consider that we solve for

p_{x}
from Eq. (
151
). Clearly, in the most general
case, it does not only involve the system’s degree of freedom
\dot{x}

and
x
, but also on
y
,
p_{y}
and
z
,
p_{z}
. On the other
hand, upon solving Eq. (
152
) and Eq. (
153
)
for
p_{y}
and
p_{z}
respectively, one can find
p_{y}
depends
on
y
,
\dot{y}
,
x
,
p_{x}
while
p_{z}
depends on
z
,

\dot{z}
,
x
,
p_{x}
. As a consequence, if one views
x
,
\dot{x}
,
y
,
\dot{y}
,
z
,
\dot{z}

as fundamental variables, then
p_{x}
,
p_{y}
and
p_{z}
in
general will depend on all of them.

To get around of this issue, we consider the following two assumptions: (a) While
H^{\mathrm{SE}}
and
H^{\mathrm{SM}}
can have arbitrary dependence
on their respective positions, they are at most quadratic these momenta. (b)
The momentum of the system can only couple to either the environmental degrees of freedom or the meter’s degrees of freedom,
but not both. The first assumption allows us to represent the joint system-environment-meter
propagator in terms of the path integral in the position space.
The second assumption guarantees that the total Lagrangian contains
no direct interaction between the meter’s degrees of freedom and the
environment’s degrees of freedom so that
L^{\mathrm{TOT}}
can be
well split into
L^{\mathrm{SE}}
and
L^{\mathrm{SM}}
.

Without loss of generality, under these two assumptions, we consider
the case where the system’s momentum only couples to the environment’s
degree of freedom at most quadratically. i.e.
H^{\mathrm{SM}}
does not
depends on
p_{x}
. Upon solving Eq. (
153
), this
immediately leads to the fact that
p_{z}
only depends on
z
,

\dot{z}
and
x
. On the other hand, since
H_{\text{SE}}
is
at most quadratic in
p_{x}
, solve Eq. (
151
)
for
p_{x}
with
\partial H^{\mathrm{SM}}/\partial p_{x}=0
will not involve

z
and
\dot{z}
and thus
p_{x}
is only a function of
x
,

\dot{x}
,
y
,
p_{y}
. Further solving Eq. (
152
)
for
p_{y}
does not involve
z
and
\dot{z}
either. This leads
to a well-defined
L^{\mathrm{SE}}
and
L^{\mathrm{SM}}
. Similar arguments also
hold when the system’s momentum only couples to the meter’s degree
of freedom.

Once the two assumptions discussed in the main text is satisfied so
that
L^{\mathrm{SE}}
and
L_{\text{ }}^{\mathrm{SM}}
are well defined , we can
apply similar analysis as the coherent-state path integral formalism
and obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{r}[x,\,\bar{x}]

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathscr{D}[z,\bar{z}]E_{r}^{\mathrm{M}}(\bar{z}(t_{\text{f}}),\,z(t_{\text{f}}))\sigma(z(t_{\text{i}}),\,\bar{z}(t_{\text{i}}))

\displaystyle\times\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SM}}[x,z]\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SM}*}[\bar{x},\,\bar{z}],

(157)

\mathcal{W}[x,\,\bar{x}]=\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}[x,\,\bar{x}]\,\mathrm{id}(\bar{x}(t_{\text{f}}),{x}(t_{\text{f}}))
with
\mathrm{id}(\bar{x}^{\prime},\,x^{\prime}):=\delta(\bar{x}^{\prime}-x^{\prime})
, and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}[x,\,\bar{x}]

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{y(t_{\text{f}})=\bar{y}(t_{\text{f}})}\mathscr{D}[y,\bar{y}]\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SE}}[x,\,y]\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SE}*}[\bar{x},\,\bar{y}]

\displaystyle\times\rho(x(t_{\text{i}}),y(t_{\text{i}});\bar{x}(t_{\text{i}}),\bar{y}(t_{\text{i}}))

(158)

where
x
,
y
,
z
denote the degrees of freedom of the system,
environment and meter, respectively, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SM}}[z,\,x]

\displaystyle\equiv\text{e}^{\text{i}\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}L^{\mathrm{SM}}(z,\,\dot{z};x,\,\dot{x})\mathrm{d}t},

(159)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{SE}}[x,y]

\displaystyle\equiv\text{e}^{\text{i}\int_{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}L^{\mathrm{SE}}(y,\dot{y};x,\,\dot{x})\mathrm{d}\tau}.

(160)

Note that the position-space process and measurement functional satisfy all previously mentioned properties. For example, the position-space process also has similar connection to the Feynman-Vernon formalism in the position space and Eq. (
13
) in the main text generalizes straightforward to position space.
The recovery of discrete-time formalism is also possible from the
continuous-time position space representation. Other properties, such
as positivity, causality and Markovianity also hold similarly. In
particular, for causality, the exponential factor in the coherent-state
representation does not appear here and it reads

\int_{x_{>}(t^{\prime})=x^{\prime},\,\bar{x}_{>}(t^{\prime})=\bar{x}^{\prime}}\mathscr{D}[x_{>},\bar{x}_{>}]\mathcal{W}[x,\,\bar{x}]=\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}[x_{<},\,\bar{x}_{<}]\mathrm{id}(\bar{x}^{\prime},\,x^{\prime}).

(161)
