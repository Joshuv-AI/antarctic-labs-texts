IG-13-1 peptide-loaded chitosan nanoparticles in dental adhesives: Physicomechanical, and biological performance
Authors: Aiman Khan, Saad Liaqat, Nawshad Muhammad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe aims of this study were to formulate a triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA)-based dental adhesive incorporating IG-13-1 peptide-coated chitosan nanoparticles (CNPs), and evaluate its polymerization, physicomechanical/bonding performance, antibacterial activity, and cytocompatibility.MethodsFive samples were prepared (n = 5/group): commercial universal adhesive (Ruby Bond), experimental unfilled TEGDMA control, and experimental adhesives containing 1.5, 3, or 6 wt% IG-13-1-CNPs. Specimens were light-emitting diode (LED)-cured (1300 mW/cm2; 20 s/side). The degree of conversion (by Fourier transform infrared-attenuated total reflectance spectroscopy), depth of cure (ISO 4049), water sorption (baseline 21 days), flexural strength (ISO 4049), and shear bond strength to enamel (ISO 29022) were measured. Antibacterial testing against Streptococcus mutans was conducted using direct contact testing (optical density at 600 nm, OD600) and disk diffusion. Cytocompatibility was assessed by using Alamar Blue.ResultsAll experimental adhesives had a high degree of conversion (>90%) but it decreased significantly (p < 0.001) with higher CNP loadings. The shear bond strength differed (p < 0.001) and decreased with higher loadings: 4.549 ± 0.698 MPa for commercial adhesive vs 2.080 ± 0.302 MPa for 6% loading. Antibacterial activity assessment against Streptococcus mutans using direct contact testing showed that peptide-coated CNPs reduced OD600 from 1.6846 to 0.53275 (68.38%), whereas the reduction with uncoated CNPs was from 1.5267 to 1.196 (21.66%). Cell viability remained >85% across all treatments.ConclusionIG-13-1-CNP incorporation led to strong antibacterial activity while also maintaining a high degree of conversion and acceptable cytocompatibility. However, higher loadings compromised the cure depth and enamel bond strength, supporting the need to optimize the nanoparticle concentration/dispersion.Keywords: Antimicrobial peptides, Biological properties, Chitosan nanoparticles, Dental adhesives, Mechanical properties

Introduction

Modern dentistry restorative and regenerative work research is highly focused on the creation of bioactive dental materials with similar profiles that restore but, more importantly, contribute to preventing infection and healing tissues. The oral environment is highly complex due to the presence of microorganisms, enzymes, and mechanical stresses, thereby demanding high-tech materials with the capacity for providing antimicrobial protection, eliciting appropriate biological signaling, and functioning stably for a long period.1,2

Chitosan is a natural polymer that has been investigated for use in dentistry and identified as a highly promising option. Chitosan is a cationic polysaccharide produced via the deacetylation of chitin and it is acknowledged due to its biodegradability, biocompatibility, and intrinsic antimicrobial activity, as well as mucoadhesive properties.3, 4, 5 These properties are highly favorable for use in the oral cavity because suitable materials must be capable of resisting enzymatic degradation, withstanding mechanical forces, and remaining effective under continuous salivary clearance. Chitosan has a high surface area in nanoparticulate form, allowing it to interact better with biological tissues, and even penetrate biofilms and confined anatomical spaces. Thus, chitosan nanoparticles (CNPs) are an attractive option for dental drug delivery and bioactive applications.6,7

Dentists are also interested in bioactive peptides because of their highly specific biological activities. Antimicrobial peptides, remineralization peptides, and growth factor-mimetic peptides are now considered important for applications in the oral area as they are effective in controlling pathogens, influencing the deposition of minerals, and promoting the regeneration of tissue.8, 9, 10 In addition, unlike traditional antimicrobial substances and factors, peptides are advantageous because of their precise mechanisms of action, resistance, and very low likelihood of inducing systemic toxicity. However, the oral environment is characterized by low stability, rapid enzymatic degradation, short biological half-lives, and insufficient retention at the site of application, which all hinder the effective use of these bioactive peptides.11,12 Thus, despite their proven biological efficacy, these limitations restrict the use of peptide-based therapies in dental practice.

Among the available antimicrobial peptides, IG-13-1 was selected for use in the present study because it is a short LL-37-derived peptide with reported activity against Streptococcus mutans, a key cariogenic bacterium involved in biofilm formation and secondary caries.5,11 Thus, IG-13-1 was considered as potentially suited to the objective of developing an antibacterial dental adhesive for the tooth–restoration interface. The peptide's sequence, IGKLFKRIVQLIK, has cationic and amphipathic characteristics that support interaction with negatively charged bacterial membranes, and its shorter sequence length is practically advantageous for synthesis and formulation compared with full-length parent peptides.5,11,12 Therefore, IG-13-1 was selected as the antibacterial component to complement the intrinsic antimicrobial activity, mucoadhesion, and carrier potential of CNPs.

The combination of peptides with CNPs has allowed the creation of chitosan–peptide nanocomposites as nano-bio-hybrid systems in order to exploit the structural and protective characteristics of chitosan and the biological specificity of peptides. In these systems, CNPs serve as protective carriers to guard peptides against enzymatic degradation, as well as improving their retention in oral tissues and facilitating controlled and localized release.13, 14, 15 Furthermore, peptides continue to exhibit their antimicrobial, regenerative, or immunomodulatory functions at the cellular level, thereby leading to a synergistic therapeutic effect that is greater than the effect of either component alone.

The occurrence of secondary caries beneath composite restorations is a significant cause for concern in dental practice. The incorporation of chitosan–peptide nanoparticles in dental adhesive systems can contribute to the development of restorations with inherent antibacterial properties, thereby lowering the risk of secondary caries. Thus, in the present study, IG-13-1-coated CNPs were incorporated into an experimental triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA)-based dental adhesive, and evaluations were conducted to determine whether this strategy could provide antibacterial activity while also maintaining acceptable polymerization, physicomechanical performance, bonding behavior, and cytocompatibility.

Materials and Methods

Materials and reagents

Chitosan, acetic acid, sodium hydroxide, 50 mM precipitant salt solution, and Pluronic F-68 were used for the synthesis and stabilization of CNPs, and for preparing the peptide-coated nanoparticle dispersion. The antimicrobial peptide IG-13-1 (IGKLFKRIVQLIK; molecular weight, 1555.03 Da) was synthesized by standard solid-phase peptide synthesis and used to functionalize the surfaces of CNPs. The peptide purity was verified by high-performance liquid chromatography (≥95%). The lyophilized peptide was stored at −80 °C until use, and fresh peptide solutions were prepared for each experimental batch.

The experimental adhesive resin was prepared using TEGDMA as the resin monomer, camphorquinone (CQ) as the visible-light photoinitiator, and ethyl 4-dimethylaminobenzoate (EDMAB) as the amine co-initiator. The unfilled experimental control consisted of 99 wt% TEGDMA, 0.5 wt% CQ, and 0.5 wt% EDMAB. For the nanoparticle-modified groups, IG-13-1-coated CNPs were incorporated at 1.5 wt%, 3 wt%, and 6 wt% relative to the total adhesive mass. These concentrations were selected to assess the concentration-dependent effect of different nanoparticle loadings on polymerization, flow behavior, water sorption, mechanical performance, antibacterial activity, and cytocompatibility. A commercial universal adhesive, Ruby Bond, was used as the commercial control. The commercial adhesive was used according to the manufacturer's instructions and was not chemically modified before testing. Its complete proprietary formulation was not disclosed; therefore, in this study, it is identified by the product name, adhesive category, manufacturer information, and batch/lot number where available.

The extracted human teeth used for bond-strength testing were cleaned to remove any visible debris and contaminants, and stored in distilled water at room temperature until use. Chemical fixation was not performed because testing focused on surface adhesion rather than histological evaluation.

Study design, treatments, and sample size

The samples used in this study included experimental dental adhesives with chitosan–peptide nanocomposites and their respective control adhesives. The human enamel used for bond strength testing was obtained from teeth extracted for therapeutic reasons and was treated in accordance with the laboratory standards.

CNP-antimicrobial peptide (IG-13-1) modified dental adhesive at various concentrations of chitosan was tested versus the experimental and commercial controls. Five experimental groups were prepared: G1 = commercial universal adhesive (Ruby Bond, fifth generation); G2 = experimental control (unfilled TEGDMA-based resin); G3 = adhesive with 1.5 wt% chitosan–peptide nanoparticles; G4 = adhesive with 3 wt% chitosan–peptide nanoparticles; and G5 = experimental adhesive and 6 wt% chitosan–peptide nanoparticles.

The sample size of five samples per group was estimated based on a study power of 80%, 5% level of significance, and the means and standard deviations obtained in a similar study using the formula: n = 2 (Z1−α/2+Z1−β)2 σ2/δ2, where n is the number of specimens required per group.1,2

Synthesis of IG-13-1 functionalized CNPs

CNPs were prepared from chitosan dissolved in diluted acetic acid under continuous magnetic stirring until a clear solution was obtained. Nanoparticles were produced via controlled precipitation through dropwise addition of the precipitant salt solution (50 mM) under vigorous stirring, followed by intermittent sonication to minimize agglomeration and improve particle dispersion. The nanoparticle suspension was allowed to stabilize, before CNPs were recovered by high-speed centrifugation, washed with distilled water, and then freeze dried for storage. To prepare the IG-13-1 coating, lyophilized IG-13-1 was dissolved in sterile aqueous medium and incubated with the freshly prepared CNP dispersion under controlled stirring to allow peptide association onto the surfaces of CNPs. The coating step was based on electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions between the cationic/amphipathic peptide and the functional groups present on CNPs. The resulting IG-13-1-coated CNPs were collected by centrifugation, washed to remove loosely associated peptide, and freeze dried. The dried nanoparticles were re-dispersed and mixed to obtain the required working concentration, before incorporating into the adhesive formulations.

Preparation of adhesives

The experimental control adhesive was formulated by dissolving 0.5 wt% CQ and 0.5 wt% EDMAB in 99 wt% TEGDMA under light-protected conditions to prevent premature photoactivation. The mixture was magnetically stirred until a visually homogeneous resin blend was obtained.

To prepare the nanoparticle-modified adhesive groups, IG-13-1-coated CNPs were weighed separately and incorporated into the experimental resin at 1.5 wt%, 3 wt%, and 6 wt% relative to the total adhesive mass. Nanoparticles were added gradually to the resin mixture under continuous stirring to reduce local agglomeration. Dispersion was further improved using probe sonication, and the formulations were then mixed in amber bottles at room temperature for 24 h to promote homogeneity. Dispersion was assessed qualitatively by visual inspection to assess visible agglomerates or sedimentation before sample fabrication and indirectly through morphology based on scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and rheological behavior. Quantitative particle-size distribution, polydispersity index, and zeta-potential analyses were not performed. Therefore, nanoparticle dispersions were interpreted using the findings obtained by SEM, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy, and rheology. All adhesive formulations were protected from ambient light during preparation and storage.

The final experimental groups were as follows: G1, commercial universal adhesive control; G2, unfilled experimental TEGDMA-based adhesive control; G3, experimental adhesive containing 1.5 wt% IG-13-1-coated CNPs; G4, experimental adhesive containing 3 wt% IG-13-1-coated CNPs; and G5, experimental adhesive containing 6 wt% IG-13-1-coated CNPs.

Sample fabrication

The samples were fabricated in standardized stainless steel and Teflon molds to ensure consistent dimensions. The adhesives were covered with a transparent Mylar strip to minimize oxygen inhibition at the surface. Subsequently, samples were light-cured using a light-emitting diode (LED) curing unit (Bluedent Smart LED, 1300 mW/cm2, 410–490 nm) for 20 s on each side. The curing tip was positioned as close and as perpendicular as possible to the specimen's surface. After curing, specimens were removed from molds, visually inspected to detect defects, and then stabilized post-cure, before FTIR spectroscopy, and depth of cure and water sorption analysis following ISO 4049:2019 and ADA Specification No 273. Flexural strength was tested using ISO 4049:2019 by preparing a rectangular bar (2 mm × 2 mm × 25 mm).4 Shear bond strength (SBS) testing was conducted using ISO 29022:2013.5 Specimens were immersed in distilled water at 37 °C when the protocols required water storage.

Characterization of samples

UV-vis spectroscopy

UV-Vis spectroscopy was performed to confirm the interaction between IG-13-1 peptide and CNPs by comparing the absorbance profile for free IG-13-1 peptide with that for IG-13-1-coated CNPs. Spectra were recorded over the UV range using quartz cuvettes under matched experimental conditions. The position of the main absorbance peak was recorded as well as any shift or change in the absorbance intensity as indicators of peptide association with the nanoparticle's surface. A shift in the peptide absorbance maximum after coating was interpreted as evidence of changes in the peptide microenvironment caused by interaction with CNPs.

FTIR spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy was used to identify functional groups and confirm successful peptide loading onto CNPs. Spectra were obtained for pure chitosan, IG-13-1-loaded CNPs, and cured and uncured adhesive specimens. Spectra were collected over the 4000–400 cm−1 range. To characterize CNPs, the spectra were evaluated to detect characteristic chitosan bands, including hydroxyl, amine, and polysaccharide related peaks, and peptide-associated amide bands. Shifts or changes in the intensity of amide I and amide II regions were considered to support peptide association with the surfaces of CNPs. SEM

The morphologies and elemental compositions of CNPs and IG-13-1-coated CNPs were evaluated using SEM combined with EDS. Dried nanoparticle samples were mounted on SEM stubs and sputter-coated with gold before imaging. SEM images were obtained at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV at magnifications ranging from 1000 × to 5000 × . The images were used to assess particle morphology, surface texture, dispersion pattern, and the presence of agglomeration. EDS analysis was performed to identify the major elemental constituents of the nanoparticles and to support compositional confirmation of the chitosan–peptide system. The presence of carbon and oxygen was considered consistent with the polysaccharide structure of chitosan, while changes in elemental composition after peptide loading were used to support successful peptide association.

Depth of cure

Depth of cure was assessed using ISO 4049:20197. Adhesives were packed into cylindrical stainless steel molds and light-cured from the top surface with a light source at a wavelength 400–500 nm for 20 s. Immediately after curing, the uncured material was removed and the height of the remaining cured portion was measured using a digital caliper (0.01 mm resolution). The depth of cure was recorded for each specimen and compared across groups.

Water sorption

Water sorption was evaluated using ANSI/ADA Specification No. 27 (aligned with ISO 4049)3. Standard cured specimens (6 mm × 4 mm) were dried (baseline weight) and then immersed in distilled water at a controlled temperature. Rheology measurements

Rheological testing was performed to characterize the flow behavior of the adhesive formulations without photoinitiator in the control and nanoparticle-modified groups. Measurements were conducted using a rotational rheometer at 25 °C under dark conditions. The linear viscoelastic region was first identified based on a strain–sweep test performed at an angular frequency of 10 rad/s, where the strain varied from 1 to 20%, and repeated in reverse (20–1%) to confirm linearity and minimize thixotropic bias. After establishing the linear viscoelastic region, a frequency-dependent test was performed over an angular frequency range of 1–10 rad/s to evaluate changes in viscosity with the shear conditions. The resulting viscosity data were fitted using the Cross model to describe shear-thinning behavior, and the representative viscosity for each formulation was obtained from the plateau region where steady viscosity was approached.

SBS

SBS was measured according to ISO 29022:2013.5 Extracted human teeth were cleaned and embedded in self-curing polymethyl methacrylate, and then prepared with a flat bonding surface exposed. Adhesives were applied following a standardized protocol, i.e., cleaning the surface for bonding, etching using phosphoric acid (37%), applying adhesive, air thinning, and curing. A composite cylinder of diameter (d) was made on the bonded surface using a mold and light-cured incrementally (d = diameter of the bonded area). Specimens were mounted in a universal testing machine and loaded, and force was applied at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min until failure. The maximum load at failure (F) was recorded in Newtons (N). SBS was calculated by dividing the failure load by the bonded circular area, as follows: SBS (MPa) = F/A, where A = πd2/4. Therefore, SBS (MPa) = 4F/πd2, where F is the maximum load at failure in Newtons, A is the bonded area in mm2, and d is the diameter of the bonded composite cylinder in mm. SBS values were reported in MPa because 1 N/mm2 is equivalent to 1 MPa.

Antibacterial testing

Antimicrobial activity was assessed against Streptococcus mutans using both disk diffusion and direct contact testing. For the disk diffusion tests, cured adhesive disks (10 mm × 2 mm) were placed on brain heart infusion (BHI) agar plates, which had been previously inoculated with 200 μL of bacterial suspension, and incubated for 48 h, before measuring the zones of inhibition using a Vernier caliper. Streptomycin and penicillin disks were used as positive controls, and a blank disk as the negative control.

For the direct contact tests, the side walls of wells in a sterile 96-well microtiter plate were coated with the test adhesive and light-cured for 10 s, before applying 10 μL of bacterial suspension. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to allow complete evaporation and ensure direct material-to-bacteria contact, and bacterial outgrowth was monitored using a temperature-controlled spectrophotometer.

Cytocompatibility testing (Alamar Blue assay)

Cytocompatibility was assessed using an osteoblast-like cell line with an extract-based method according to ISO 10993-5. Cells were cultured in complete Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and antibiotics (100 μg/mL streptomycin and 100 IU/mL penicillin) under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). Material extracts were prepared by incubating cured specimens in culture medium using a standardized surface area-to-volume ratio for 24 h. Cells were exposed to the extracts for 24 h, before adding Alamar Blue reagent and incubating according to the manufacturer's guidelines. Fluorescence or absorbance was measured using a microplate reader, and viability was expressed as a percentage relative to untreated cell controls.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25. Data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation for each treatment. Data normality was checked using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The data were normally distributed. Therefore, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted followed by Tukey's post hoc test to compare data among treatments. A difference was considered significant when the p-value was ≤0.05.

Results and discussion

IG-13-1-coated CNPs were successfully incorporated into the experimental adhesive system according to the UV-Vis, FTIR, SEM, and EDS results. The main performance trend was concentration dependent, where the antibacterial activity and cytocompatibility remained favorable, but higher nanoparticle loadings, particularly 6 wt%, reduced the depth of cure and SBS. Some of the statistically significant differences were small in absolute magnitude; therefore, the results were interpreted by considering both statistical significance and practical relevance.

UV-vis absorbance analysis

UV-Vis spectra indicated a distinct spectral shift in the main absorption maximum from 275 nm for the free IG-13-1 peptide to 271 nm after coating onto CNPs, accompanied by a reduction in the absorbance intensity (Figure 1). This spectral shift and intensity change indicated successful peptide–nanoparticle association, consistent with the altered electronic environment when the peptide was bound/adsorbed onto the surface of chitosan.

FTIR analysis of CNPs, peptide-loaded CNPs, and adhesive composites

FTIR analysis of CNPs with and without peptide loading detected changes in the amide regions (Figure 2), indicating the incorporation of peptide functional groups on chitosan. Peaks corresponding to chitosan bands were present, and the band shifts/intensity differences in regions consistent with amide I/amide II indicated peptide loading onto the surfaces of CNPs.

FTIR spectra obtained for (a) pure chitosan and (b) peptide-loaded chitosan nanoparticles.

The spectral changes observed after loading with IG-13-1 provided chemical support for the peptide associating with CNPs. The bands near 1640 cm−1 and 1540 cm−1 corresponded to amide-related vibrations, and the bands in the 1067–1026 cm−1 region were consistent with polysaccharide C–O–C and C–O stretching vibrations of the chitosan backbone.16,17 Compared with pure chitosan, the intensity changed in the amide region for peptide-loaded nanoparticles, indicating that peptide functional groups were present on or associated with the surfaces of CNPs. This finding is important because it confirms that IG-13-1 was not simply added as a separate soluble component, but instead it was incorporated into the CNP system. Therefore, the FTIR findings support the UV-Vis spectral shift observed in Figure 1 and provide additional evidence for successful formation of the chitosan–peptide nanocomposite.

In the adhesive formulations, the progressive changes observed as the chitosan–peptide concentration increased suggested interactions between the nanoparticle phase and methacrylate resin matrix. The broadening of OH/NH-related features could be attributed to hydrogen bonding between the hydroxyl and amino groups of chitosan, amide groups of IG-13-1, and polar groups within the TEGDMA resin. This interaction may have improved the chemical compatibility between the bioactive filler and resin matrix, but it could also have restricted monomer mobility at higher nanoparticle loadings. This interpretation is consistent with the observed concentration-dependent reductions in the DC and depth of cure, particularly with the 6 wt% loading. Thus, the FTIR results help explain why peptide-loaded CNPs improved the bioactive characteristics of the adhesive system but the concentration will need to be optimized to avoid compromising the polymerization behavior.

DC

The DC values were calculated based on the FTIR peak changes and the results indicated high polymerization efficiency across all groups, ranging from 90.1% to 96.1%. These DC values are relatively high compared with the values reported previously for many methacrylate dental adhesives and resin blends based on FTIR spectra. This difference may be related to the specific resin formulation and testing geometry used in the present study, particularly the TEGDMA-rich experimental adhesive, thin FTIR specimens, and standardized high-intensity LED curing protocol. DC was highest for the commercial control (96.1%), followed by the experimental control (94.4%). Incorporating chitosan–peptide reduced DC slightly, with 92.28% under 1.5% chitosan, the lowest value of 90.1% under 6% chitosan, and 91.58% under 3% chitosan, as shown in Table 1. The statistical analysis results are presented in Table 2. Overall, these results suggest that filler incorporation modestly reduced conversion, but the conversion rates were still high, and favorable for maintaining the mechanical strength and decreasing residual monomers. However, direct comparisons with previous studies should be made cautiously because the reported DC values depend strongly on the resin composition, specimen thickness, curing conditions, FTIR mode, selected internal reference band, and baseline correction method. Collares et al. also reported that internal standard selection can significantly affect the DC values calculated for methacrylate-based resins, especially in BisGMA/TEGDMA-containing systems.18 Therefore, the high absolute DC values obtained in the present study may partly reflect methodological and compositional factors rather than direct superior performance compared with all previously studied adhesive systems.

Increasing the chitosan loading interfered with TEGDMA polymerization. The DC values were high (up to 90–92%) for the experimental treatments containing peptide-coated CNPs, and similar to that for the control, but marginally lower than that for the commercial control (96%). These findings align with previous demonstrations that incorporating nanoparticles into resin can moderately reduce the polymerization efficiency due to increased light scattering and restricted monomer mobility, especially at higher filler concentrations.13, 14, 15 For example, in studies where CNPs were added to flowable composites, no significant changes were found in DC at low concentrations but decreased polymerization was observed with higher filler levels, suggesting a size and dispersion effect on light penetration and polymer network formation.13,16 Moreover, a study of nanoparticle-modified adhesives indicated that DC can remain clinically acceptable even when fillers are present, provided that particle dispersion and resin matrix chemistry are optimized.17 The slight reductions in DC under the 1.5% and 3% chitosan loadings may reflect light attenuation and network mobility effects, whereas the relatively preserved DC under the 6% loading indicates that optimization of the nanoparticle surface chemistry and peptide coating could adversely affect polymerization with acceptable limits.18 The high surface DC values should also be interpreted together with the depth of cure findings. High conversion was found at the test surface according to FTIR for all treatments, but the depth of cure decreased markedly as the peptide–CNP concentration increased, especially under the 6 wt% loading. This indicates that surface polymerization remained efficient, but light attenuation, increased viscosity, and restricted monomer mobility may have limited polymerization through the bulk of the material. Thus, the clinically important findings include the high DC values but also the concentration-dependent reduction in the DC and curing depth as the nanoparticle loading increased.

ANOVA detected a statistically significant treatment effect for DC, but the actual reductions were modest because the DC values remained above 90% for all samples. Post hoc analysis showed that the DC value for the commercial control differed significantly from those for all nanoparticle-modified samples, and that for the experimental control differed from those for the samples with 1.5 wt%, 3 wt%, and 6 wt% loadings. However, the DC values did not differ from each other for all nanoparticle concentrations, where those for the samples with 1.5 wt% and 3 wt% loadings were not significantly different, and those for the samples with 3 wt% and 6 wt% loadings were also not significantly different. Therefore, the clinically relevant interpretation of these results is that nanoparticle loading led to a statistically detectable but relatively small reduction in surface conversion, and high polymerization was maintained on the surfaces of all formulations according to testing using FTIR.

Analysis of compositions using SEM and EDS

SEM micrographs demonstrated that the heterogeneous particulate morphology with agglomerated features observed for chitosan powder changed into more dispersed, irregularly shaped particles in the nano-size range (<100 nm) after peptide loading, as shown in Figure 5. EDS confirmed an organic, C/O-dominant composition, with C (48.81 mass%) and O (49.53 mass%) as the principal elements, and minor contributions from Na (0.55 mass%) and P (1.11 mass%) (Table 3). The predominance of carbon and oxygen supports the expected polysaccharide nature of chitosan, and the trace amounts of Na and P may have been related to the processing reagents or minor surface-associated species retained after synthesis and washing. The C content increased after peptide loading due to the organic nature of the peptide. SEM images indicated the heterogeneous nanoparticle distribution and clustering, and EDS analysis confirmed the predominant carbon and oxygen signals with minor elements consistent with chitosan and peptide functional groups. These morphological observations are in agreement with previous studies that detected nano- and micro-chitosan aggregates within resin matrices, and these aggregates can affect the stress distribution and water diffusion pathways.19, 20, 21 This aggregation is probably due to the inherent viscosity and hydrogen bonding of chitosan, which can affect the uniformity of dispersion at higher concentrations.22 The morphological features of the peptide coating suggested that surface interaction was achieved, but further refinement of dispersion using surfactants or coupling agents may improve the particle distribution and mechanical characteristics.23,24

Rheology

Rheological testing detected non-Newtonian, shear-thinning behavior for the control and chitosan–peptide adhesives at 32 °C (Figure 6, Figure 7). According to the shear stress–shear rate plot, increasing the filler concentration resulted in a clear increase in the shear stress at equivalent shear rates, with the highest stress response under the 6% chitosan loading, indicating increased resistance to flow and a more structured internal network. The complex viscosity trends further supported shear-thinning, where the viscosity was higher at low deformation/frequency and decreased as the shear conditions increased, consistent with the breakdown and realignment of polymer–particle interactions under shear. Functionally, these results imply that a higher chitosan loading of 6% reduced flow and infiltration. The shear-thinning behavior of the adhesive with peptide-coated chitosan particles produced in this study is consistent with previously reported nanoparticle-modified systems, where filler interaction increased the low-shear viscosity and structural resistance.25,26 The increased viscosity at low shear, particularly under the 6% loading, supports findings in similar systems where agglomeration and polymer–filler interactions increased the flow resistance under small deformation.19 Higher viscosities can enhance the handling stability but may reduce infiltration of the adhesive into the microstructure of dentin, potentially contributing to compromised bond strengths.27 Thus, the rheological findings suggested that improving the structural integrity and optimizing the filler content could balance flow and infiltration.28

Polymerization depth

Polymerization depth testing showed that the cure depths were 5.654 ± 0.321 mm and 5.030 ± 0.328 mm for the commercial control and experimental control, respectively (Table 4). The depth of cure decreased as the chitosan concentration increased. The mean depth of cure decreased greatly under the 6% chitosan loading (1.932 ± 0.458 mm), and this pronounced reduction was consistent with the increased light attenuation (scattering/absorption) and higher viscosity under the higher nanoparticle loading, limiting the effective polymerization depth even though the DC remained high according to surface FTIR analysis. Post hoc pairwise comparisons are presented in Table 5. Consistent with the rheology and DC results, the depth of cure decreased sharply as the peptide–CNPs loading increased, especially under 6%, probably due to increased light attenuation from fillers. Similar reductions in the cure depth were reported for composite systems as the nano-chitosan content increased, where higher filler loadings impeded photonic energy transmission through the resin.16,29 An adequate depth of cure must be obtained for a clinically effective adhesive to achieve mechanical strength and a stable interface; therefore, it is essential to balance filler addition with optical and photochemical compatibility.30

Water sorption

Figure 8 shows the time-dependent percentage changes in water sorption across all treatments. Water sorption increased with time under all treatments, indicating progressive water uptake from baseline to 21 days. Water sorption was highest in the experimental control and significantly higher compared with all other samples (p < 0.001), followed by the commercial control with the second highest. Among the chitosan loaded samples, water sorption was consistently lowest under the 6% chitosan loading. This apparently counterintuitive finding may be explained by CNPs behaving as a dispersed filler phase within the cured resin network. Chitosan contains hydrophilic hydroxyl and amine groups, but a higher nanoparticle concentration can increase the tortuosity of water diffusion pathways and reduce the continuity of water-accessible resin domains. Thus, water molecules may need to take a longer and more restricted route to penetrate the polymer network, thereby accounting for the lower apparent water uptake under the 6 wt% loading. Water sorption was consistently higher under the 1.5% chitosan loading than the 3% loading at all time points. Water sorption differed significantly among all chitosan loadings at later time points, particularly after 3 days and 21 days. Overall, chitosan modification altered water sorption in a concentration-dependent and nonlinear manner, where the 6% chitosan loading resulted in the greatest resistance to water uptake and blocked diffusion pathways, and the experimental control remained most susceptible to water absorption during aging. The statistical analysis results are presented in Table 6, Table 7. Chitosan is intrinsically hydrophilic because of its hydroxyl and protonated amine groups, but the findings obtained in the present study suggest that its behavior inside the cured TEGDMA-based adhesive was controlled by chemical polarity but also by microstructural factors. At higher concentrations, chitosan–peptide nanoparticles may have acted as physical barriers that blocked or lengthened diffusion channels. In addition, hydrogen bonding between chitosan functional groups and carbonyl/ether groups in the methacrylate resin may have restricted polymer chain mobility and reduced the free volume, limiting the amount of water that could be accommodated within the network. This balance between hydrophilicity and barrier formation explains why water sorption was lowest under the 6 wt% loading despite containing the highest chitosan concentration.31 The hydrophilicity of chitosan is beneficial for drug delivery and bioactivity, but may be linked to changes in the dimensional stability of the resin matrix.32 Increased water sorption can cause plasticization and hydrolytic degradation over time, undermining mechanical properties and interface interactions.33,34 If chitosan acts as a filler phase, it can force water molecules to travel along a more tortuous path, reducing diffusion and equilibrium uptake.35 Moreover, chitosan is hydrophilic but its functional groups can form hydrogen bonds with carbonyl/ether groups in methacrylate, restricting chain mobility and making the network more compact with less accessible free volume, leading to reduced water sorption.36 Furthermore, chitosan is hydrophilic but its behavior in a cured resin composite can be dominated by microstructural effects rather than intrinsic polarity.

Flexural strength

The flexural strength results are presented in Table 8. The mean flexural strength was lowest under the 1.5% chitosan loading and highest under the 6% loading, although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.097), indicating that the chitosan concentration did not have a detectable effect on flexural strength, although the borderline difference between the 1.5% and 6% loadings suggests a possible concentration-dependent effect. Tukey HSD post hoc tests (Table 9) confirmed that there were no significant pairwise differences between any treatments. Variability was highest in the commercial control (SD = 111.59), which probably reduced the statistical power and contributed to the lack of significant differences. The mean flexural strength was numerically lower under the 1.5 wt% chitosan–peptide loading compared with the control, but the overall differences among treatments were not statistically significant (p = 0.097), and Tukey HSD post hoc tests confirmed that there were no significant pairwise differences. Therefore, the results did not indicate a statistically significant reduction in flexural strength after chitosan–peptide nanoparticle incorporation. The lower mean value observed under the 1.5 wt% loading should be interpreted as a non-significant trend rather than a definitive mechanical weakening effect. The relatively high variability, particularly in the commercial control, may also have reduced the statistical power and contributed to the absence of significant differences. Overall, these findings suggest that incorporating IG-13-1-coated CNPs did not significantly compromise the flexural strength within the tested concentration range, although further studies are needed with larger sample sizes and improved nanoparticle dispersion to confirm the observed trends.

SBS

SBS differed significantly among treatments (Table 10). The mean SBS was highest for the commercial control, followed by the experimental control, and the mean values were lower for the samples with chitosan–peptide loadings. Post hoc tests showed that SBS did not differ significantly between the commercial control and experimental control, but the SBS value for the commercial control was significantly higher than those under the 1.5 wt%, 3 wt%, and 6 wt% chitosan–peptide loadings. Antimicrobial activity (direct contact test)

Direct contact testing against Streptococcus mutans (ATCC 35668) showed that uncoated CNPs had limited antibacterial effects, where the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) decreased from 1.526 before incubation to 1.196 after 24 h, corresponding to a reduction of 21.66%, as shown in Figure 9. By contrast, the peptide-coated CNPs (CNPs + Pep) decreased OD600 from 1.6846 to 0.53275, with a relatively higher reduction of 68.38%. These findings indicate that peptide functionalization significantly improved the antibacterial performance, suggesting that IG-13-1 contributed to the bioactivity when coated on the surfaces of CNPs. The antibacterial activity of peptide-coated CNPs was significantly higher against Streptococcus mutans, which is consistent with the known antimicrobial mechanisms of both chitosan and cationic peptides.42, 43, 44 The positive charge of chitosan interacts with negatively charged bacterial cell walls, disrupting cell membrane integrity and inhibiting growth, and antimicrobial peptides such as IG-13-1 contribute to specific bactericidal activities.19,45 Similar approaches have been reported based on combining nanoparticles with bioactive molecules to enhance the antimicrobial activity in dental applications, indicating their potential utility in caries prevention or secondary caries inhibition when integrated into adhesives, as well as highlighting the need to optimize the concentration to preserve the mechanical and adhesive performance.

Cell viability

The Alamar Blue assay results obtained after exposure for 24 h indicated high cytocompatibility among adhesives, where the viability >85% for all treatments (Figure 10). The uniformly high viability suggests that incorporating peptide-coated CNP did not induce acute cytotoxicity under the test conditions, supporting the biological feasibility of using these modified adhesives in further evaluations. Chitosan is an established biocompatible material used in dental and biomedical applications, and many studies and reviews have demonstrated its favorable interactions with host tissues.32,46,47 The IG-13-1 peptide-coated CNPs enhanced the antibacterial activity, and maintained a high DC and cytocompatibility.

Alamar Blue assay results for (A) commercial control, (B) experimental control, (C) adhesive composite with 1.5% peptide-loaded chitosan, (D) adhesive composite with 3% peptide-loaded chitosan, (E) adhesive composite with 6% peptide-loaded chitosan, and (F) chitosan NPs after 24 h.

This study had several limitations. First, the in vitro design did not fully reproduce the clinical oral environment, where thermocycling, cyclic mechanical loading, salivary enzymes, pH fluctuations, and complex multispecies biofilms can accelerate adhesive degradation. Second, the present study did not test artificial aging, thermocycling, fatigue loading, or long-term degradation, and thus it is unclear whether the antibacterial effect, bond strength, and polymerized resin network will persist over time. Third, SEM analysis suggested particle clustering but nanoparticle dispersion was not quantified based on dynamic light scattering, the polydispersity index, zeta-potential analysis, or quantitative image analysis. In future research, the nanoparticle concentration and dispersion should be optimized, as well as evaluating long-term hydrolytic and enzymatic degradation, and bond strength testing after thermocycling and mechanical fatigue.

Conclusion

IG-13-1 peptide-coated CNPs were successfully incorporated into a TEGDMA-based dental adhesive and they provided a clear antibacterial benefit while also maintaining acceptable cytocompatibility. The DC values were high (>90%) for all experimental treatments, indicating that CNP incorporation did not prevent resin polymerization. However, increasing the CNP concentration reduced the depth of cure and SBS, suggesting that excessive CNP loading may have interfered with light penetration, resin flow, and interfacial adaptation. The flexural strength was not significantly affected. Clinically, these findings suggest that IG-13-1-loaded CNPs may be useful for developing antibacterial dental adhesives to reduce the risk of secondary caries, but the CNP concentration should be optimized to balance the antibacterial activity with the curing and bonding performance.
