A multi-stage, pixel-level annotated apple dataset for precision agriculture research
Authors: Dandan Wang, Bo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article presents a comprehensive dataset of 1406 RGB images of apples (Malus domestica), covering three key growth stages—immature (green), semi-mature (color transition), and mature (red). The dataset serves as a resource for detecting and segmenting apples across different developmental phases. Each image includes pixel-level instance segmentation masks annotated in JSON format using the VGG Image Annotator (VIA), ensuring compatibility with deep learning frameworks. The dataset’s real-world variability—spanning lighting conditions, occlusions, and clustered fruit arrangements—enhances its utility for training generalizable computer vision models in precision agriculture. It supports tasks such as fruit detection, segmentation and growth-stage classification, addressing the scarcity of annotated data for transitional maturity phases. With 2574 annotated apple instances, this dataset facilitates research on maturity grading and transfer learning for agricultural robotics. By standardizing annotations and incorporating diverse field conditions, this dataset reduces preprocessing overhead and accelerates the development of deployable AI solutions for orchard management. It is particularly valuable for improving model robustness in heterogeneous environments, thereby advancing data-driven horticultural practices.Keywords: Apple detection, Computer vision in agriculture, Natural scenes, Image dataset, Instance segmentation

Background

Apples (Malus domestica) are among the world’s most economically vital horticultural crops, yet orchard management remains largely dependent on labor-intensive practices, limiting efficiency and scalability. Recent advances in computer vision and deep learning have demonstrated significant potential for automating agricultural monitoring tasks, such as fruit detection [[1], [2], [3]], growth-stage classification [4,5], and yield prediction [[6], [7], [8]]. However, the performance of these algorithms heavily relies on the availability of high-quality annotated datasets that encompass real-world variability in illumination, occlusion, and phenotypic diversity [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. While several fruit image datasets exist, most focus on mature-stage detection (e.g., red apples) and lack comprehensive coverage of transitional growth phases, such as color-changing or semi-mature targets. This limitation restricts the development of robust models capable of tracking fruit development throughout entire growth cycles.

To address this gap, we present an annotated dataset featuring 1406 RGB images of apples. Our collection systematically includes apples at three critical growth stages: immature (green), semi-mature (color transition), and mature (red), with pixel-level instance segmentation masks generated for precise localization. All annotations were created using the VIA (VGG Image Annotator) tool and are provided in JSON format, ensuring seamless integration with mainstream deep learning frameworks. The dataset further incorporates challenging real-world conditions—variable lighting, occlusion, and clustered fruit arrangements—to enhance model robustness for real-world deployment.

Its diversity in scale, illumination, and occlusion scenarios also enhances its suitability for transfer learning, enabling broader applications in agricultural robotics and automated phenotyping.

Data Description

The dataset of apples was collected from September 2017 to October 2019 in an experimental orchard at the College of Horticulture, Northwest A&F University (Yangling, Shaanxi, China). The dataset consists of 1124 apple images captured under varying natural lighting and weather conditions, covering multiple growth stages—from immature green apples to mature red targets—including transitional color variations. The dataset is classified into three distinct categories: immature (green), semi-mature (color transition), and mature (red). Table 1 gives a detailed description and visualization for each category. The dataset contains 1124 annotated images. It is important to note that a single image frequently contains apples at multiple developmental stages; therefore, the counts of images for each stage are not mutually exclusive and sum to a number greater than the total unique image count. Specifically, the dataset includes 532 images containing immature (green) apples, 316 images containing semi-mature (color transition) apples, and 384 images containing mature (red) apples. This distribution authentically reflects the complex composition of apples found within natural orchard environments. This indicates that asynchronous fruit development occurs within orchards. Across all images, we annotated 2108 distinct apple instances: 921 immature (green), 453 semi-mature (color transition), and 734 mature (red) apples.

To mitigate potential bias from class imbalance during network training, data augmentation was applied to two subsets: images containing only semi-mature fruits, and 45 images randomly selected from the mature apple category. After augmentation, the dataset comprises 1406 images in total, distributed as follows: 532 images of immature apples, 554 of semi-mature apples, and 428 of mature apples. A total of 2574 apple instances were annotated, including 921 immature (green), 814 semi-mature (color transition), and 839 mature (red) apples.

The dataset is provided in two down-sampled resolutions derived from the original captures, as part of the initial data processing protocol. The first set includes images at 369×277 pixels and 404×303 pixels, suitable for training large-scale architectures such as Mask R-CNN. The second set features a resolution of 640×480 pixels, appropriate for lightweight models such as the YOLO series. Both scales are provided with JSON format annotations and are adequate for object detection and classification.

For the dataset, no EXIF metadata was preserved, and no color-correction targets or calibration steps were employed.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Data collection

The dataset was systematically collected from an experimental orchard at the College of Horticulture, Northwest A&F University located in Yangling, Shaanxi, China (Latitude: 34.299, Longitude:108.050). The images were collected in the northeastern part of the orchard, specifically from rows 5 to 14. The collection focused on two economically important apple cultivars (Malus domestica ‘Fuji’ and ‘Gala’). Both cultivars undergo a similar color transition sequence (from green to red). The non-uniform color variations encompass the intrinsic variations within each cultivar. Smartphones used for image capturing were set to auto-mode (flash disabled) at their highest resolution. To ensure representative sampling, we selected 200 sample trees from a grid of rows and spacings within the orchard. For each tree, images were captured from pre-defined positions (front, side) targeting fruits at three canopy layers (top, middle, lower). During the 2017–2019 growing seasons, we captured high-resolution RGB images covering three critical developmental stages: immature (green pigmentation, 532 images), semi-mature (green-red color transition, 316 images), and mature (fully red pigmentation, 384 images). All images were captured from a distance of approximately 0.3–1.0 m from the canopy, with the smartphone maintained parallel to the fruit surface. Images were captured using three smartphone cameras under natural field conditions: a Xiaomi 5 (4608×3456 pixels), an iPhone 6s Plus, and an iPhone 7 Plus (both 4032×3024 pixels; see Table 3 for full specifications). To ensure ecological validity, all images were captured under natural field conditions, including variable illumination (sunny, overcast, and partially shaded conditions), multiple canopy positions (top, middle, and lower layers), diverse occlusion scenarios (leaves, branches, and adjacent fruits) during two daily time windows (10:00–11:30 AM and 4:00–7:00 PM). All images were stored in standard JPEG format.

Data preprocessing

Following acquisition, the original images were resized to optimize annotation efficiency while preserving quality. The 4608×3456 pixel images were down-sampled to 369×277 pixels, and the 4032×3024 pixel images to 404×303 pixels, maintaining their original aspect ratios. This scale (369×277/404×303 pixels) is suitable for training large, complex architectures. Additionally, all original images were rescaled to 640×480 pixels to provide a higher-resolution option for lightweight models. Images at both scales were subsequently sharpened using a Laplacian filter to enhance edge definition and improve boundary clarity of the target apples. Specifically, sharpening was applied by convolving each RGB channel with the standard 4-neighborhood Laplacian kernel ([0,1,0; 1,−4,1; 0,1,0]). The result of this convolution (the Laplacian approximation) was then subtracted from the original image to produce the sharpened output (representative results are shown in Fig. 2). Then all images underwent a systematic annotation protocol.

Data annotation process

The annotation process was conducted by a single annotator to maximize internal consistency throughout the dataset. To ensure the accuracy and consistency of the annotations, a rigorous protocol was followed. First, a detailed annotation guideline was established. The annotator was instructed to trace the precise boundary of each apple, including portions occluded by leaves or branches. For adjacent apples, the boundary was drawn at the visible contour, ensuring masks did not overlap. The developmental stage was assigned based on a predefined color criterion:

Immature: Apples exhibiting a solid, homogeneous green color.

Immature: Apples exhibiting a solid, homogeneous green color.

Semi-mature (Color Transition): Apples showing a clear transition from green to red. This includes any non-uniform coloration, such as the initial appearance of a red blush, stripe, or a gradual yellowing.

Mature: Apples that have attained a predominantly red hue, covering most of the fruit surface, with only minimal green areas remaining.

Finally, as a quality control measure, all instance masks and stage labels across the entire dataset were meticulously reviewed and verified by an agricultural expert to ensure the final annotations met the required standard.

Instance segmentation masks were then meticulously annotated using the VIA tool, with representative annotation shown in Fig. 3. The resulting annotations, exported in JSON format, include precise polygon vertex coordinates and developmental stage labels.

Data augmentation

A total of 2108 apple instances were annotated: 921 immature, 734 mature, and 453 semi-mature. To mitigate this imbalance, we performed data augmentation on images containing only semi-mature apples and on 45 randomly selected images of mature apples. We employed a targeted data augmentation strategy to enhance dataset diversity and model generalization. The pipeline included:

(1)Brightness adjustment in HSV color space, scaling the Value channel by a factor of 1.15 to simulate illumination variations;

Brightness adjustment in HSV color space, scaling the Value channel by a factor of 1.15 to simulate illumination variations;

(2)Gaussian noise injection (mean=0, variance=0.003) to improve robustness to acquisition noise.

Dataset partitioning

Following annotation, the dataset was partitioned into training (70 %, 1041 images, 1981 instances), validation (10 %, 126 images, 185 instances), and testing (20 %, 239 images, 408 instances) sets to support model development and evaluation. This split ensures a representative distribution of the three maturity stages (immature, semi-mature, mature) across all subsets, with an approximate ratio of 77:7:16, respectively (see Table 4 for a complete overview). Dataset validation

To validate the practical utility of the dataset, we benchmarked performance using two representative deep learning architectures: the two-stage Mask R-CNN (with ResNet-50 as the backbone) and the one-stage YOLOv11n-seg. All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel Core i9–11900H CPU, 32GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080 GPU (16GB VRAM), running Windows 10. The software stack included Python 3.8, PyTorch 2.2.2, and CUDA 11.8 for GPU acceleration.

The quantitative instance segmentation results are summarized in Table 5. YOLOv11n-seg demonstrated superior overall performance, achieving a mask mAP50:95 of 0.939, substantially outperforming Mask R-CNN (0.816). This performance advantage was consistent across all three developmental stages. Notably, the “semi-mature (color transition)” class presented the greatest challenge specifically for Mask R-CNN, yielding its lowest segmentation accuracy (0.748), which aligns with the difficulty of segmenting apples during color transition. Furthermore, while Mask R-CNN achieved very high recall for “immature” apples (0.986), its lower precision (0.799) suggests a tendency towards over-detection in that category, a issue that YOLOv11n-seg mitigated with a more balanced performance profile (Precision: 0.898, Recall: 0.957).

Limitations

•Temporal Resolution: The dataset represents discrete growth stages rather than continuous daily progression, which could constrain fine-grained temporal phenotyping studies.

Temporal Resolution: The dataset represents discrete growth stages rather than continuous daily progression, which could constrain fine-grained temporal phenotyping studies.

•Annotation Subjectivity: Despite the implementation of detailed annotation guidelines and a rigorous expert-review process, a degree of inherent subjectivity remains. Although our quality control minimized inconsistencies, users should be aware of this potential variance. Future work could involve employing multiple annotators and reporting inter-annotator agreement metrics to further quantify this issue.

Annotation Subjectivity: Despite the implementation of detailed annotation guidelines and a rigorous expert-review process, a degree of inherent subjectivity remains. Although our quality control minimized inconsistencies, users should be aware of this potential variance. Future work could involve employing multiple annotators and reporting inter-annotator agreement metrics to further quantify this issue.

•Computational Requirements: The provided images have been down-sampled to reduce storage and computational overhead. However, training state-of-the-art deep learning models for instance segmentation (e.g., Mask R-CNN) still requires considerable computational resources, particularly GPU memory. Researchers with limited access to such resources are encouraged to consider using lighter-weight model architectures or applying further image down-sampling as a preprocessing step.

•Seasonal and Geographical Limitations: The dataset was collected over the growing seasons of 2017–2019 in a specific geographical region (Shaanxi, China). Consequently, the environmental conditions and cultivar-specific phenological patterns captured may not fully represent orchards in other climatic zones or with different cultivars. We recommend that users consider domain adaptation techniques or collect supplementary local data when applying models trained on this dataset to new regions.
