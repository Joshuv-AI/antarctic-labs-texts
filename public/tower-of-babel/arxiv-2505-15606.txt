Five-Term Relations for wreath Macdonald polynomials and tableau formulas for Pieri coefficients
Marino Romero, Joshua Jeishing Wen

Abstract
. We present a variety of new identities involving operators in the theory of wreath Macdonald polynomials. One such family of identities gives five-term relations, analogous to the one given by Garsia and Mellit for the modified Macdonald polynomials. As a consequence, we generate tableau formulas for wreath Macdonald Pieri coefficients, which give an incredibly quick way of computing their monomial expansions.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary: 05E05, 05E10, 33D52; Secondary: 81R10.

1.
Introduction

Defined by Haiman
[
Hai03
]
, the
wreath Macdonald polynomials
generalize the modified Macdonald polynomials from the symmetric group
\Sigma_{n}
to its wreath product
\Gamma_{n}:=\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}\wr\Sigma_{n}
.
Just as one can study the representation theory of the symmetric groups
\{\Sigma_{n}\}
using the ring of symmetric functions
\Lambda
, for fixed
r
, one can model the representation theory of
\{\Gamma_{n}\}
via the ring of multisymmetric functions
\Lambda^{\otimes r}

[
Mac15
]
.
The wreath Macdonald polynomials are distinguished elements of the deformed ring
\mathbb{C}(q,t)\otimes\Lambda^{\otimes r}
.
However, it may be naive to simply treat wreath Macdonald theory as a partially symmetric analogue of Macdonald theory.
Haiman’s original definition was motivated by geometry.
Just as his celebrated proof of the Macdonald positivity conjecture
[
Hai01
]
showed that Macdonald polynomials are intimately tied to the geometry of Hilbert schemes of points on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, Haiman conjectured that the wreath Macdonald polynomials should be related to certain Nakajima quiver varieties for the cyclic quiver.
This conjecture was realized by Bezrukavnikov–Finkelberg
[
BF14
]
, who proved a wreath analogue of Macdonald positivity.

Functional aspects of wreath Macdonald polynomials did not receive much attention until recently, and it was not evident which properties and identities had wreath analogues.
For instance, the usual Macdonald polynomials are solutions to
q
-difference equations; wreath analogues of this were finally established by the second author with Orr and Shimozono
[
Wen25a
,
OSW22
]
.
More relevant to this paper, Tesler’s identity and the five-term relation of Garsia and Mellit
[
GM19
]
provide two of the most powerful tools in developing symmetric function identities related to modified Macdonald polynomials, even finding applications in geometry. From here, one can give a number of consequences, such as proving tableau formulas for Pieri coefficients
[
BH13
]
or giving identities for Theta operators
[
DR23
]
. The recursion for Pieri coefficients implied by the five-term relation gives a fast, if not the fastest, way of computing the monomial expansion for modified Macdonald polynomials.
A version of Tesler’s identity was recently proved for wreath Macdonald polynomials in
[
RW25
]
, where a number of plethystic operators were also developed.

In this paper, we derive consequences of the wreath Tesler identity and push the functional theory of wreath Macdonald polynomials further into maturation.
First, we revisit the plethystic calculus on multisymmetric functions, giving a concise way of notating vector (or matrix) plethysms. We then demonstrate how to utilize this device by reproving reciprocity identities from the wreath Tesler identity.
Next, we give several families of five-term relations for operators on wreath Macdonald polynomials, and as a consequence, give formulas for computing Pieri coefficients in terms of tableaux.
To clarify, our Pieri rules are not the wreath analogues of those found in
[
Mac15
, Chapter VI]
; it appears that such a wreath analogue is quite complicated (cf.
[
Wen25b
]
).
Nonetheless, these recursive formulas are incredibly powerful, allowing one to compute many more examples of wreath Macdonald polynomials than was previously possible.

We also introduce a wreath version for the Theta operators of D’Adderio, Iraci, and Vanden Wyngaerd
[
DIVW21
]
, and show how they relate to the
\mathbb{D}
operators in
[
RW25
]
. In the case of modified Macdonald polynomials, Theta operators provide a framework for producing many useful identities
[
DR23
]
. They were originally introduced to give a compositional refinement of the Delta Conjecture of Haglund, Remmel, and Wilson
[
HRW18
]
. This refinement was proved by D’Adderio and Mellit
[
DM22
]
. Theta operators also conjecturally produce the Frobenius characteristic for the space of coinvariants in two sets of commuting and two sets of anticommuting variables
[
DIVW21
]
.
We predict that wreath Theta operators will also give a powerful tool for dealing with multisymmetric function identities related to wreath Macdonald polynomials. Here, we will only look at an identity that explains the relation between wreath Theta operators and the action of the quantum toroidal algebra on multisymmetric functions.

2.
Combinatorial definitions

Before we begin, let us first fix
r>2
.
It will be useful to think of the symmetric group
\Sigma_{r}
as acting on the elements of
\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}
.

2.1.
Partitions and characters

A partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{\ell})
is a weakly decreasing sequence of positive integers;
\ell(\lambda)=\ell
is its length; and
|\lambda|=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{\ell}
its size, in which case we write
\lambda\vdash|\lambda|
.
The
transposed
partition
{}^{t}\lambda
has parts given by

{}^{t}\lambda_{i}\coloneqq\#\left\{k\,|\,m_{k}\geq i\right\}.

For
\lambda,\mu\vdash n
,
\lambda\leq\mu
will denote dominance order, meaning that for all
i
,

\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{i}\leq\mu_{1}+\cdots\mu_{i}.

To
\lambda
, we associate its
Young diagram
, which we draw following the French convention.
For example,
\lambda=(4,2,2)
has the Young diagram

.

We assign Cartesian coordinates to the cells in the Young diagram so that the box at the lower left corner has coordinates
(0,0)
.
Thus,
(i,j)\in\lambda
is in the
i+1
-st column and
j+1
-st row. The character of
\square=(i,j)
is given by

\chi_{\square}\coloneqq q^{i}t^{j}.

Definition 2.1
.

For a given paritition
\lambda
, let

\displaystyle B_{\lambda}\coloneqq\sum_{\square\in\lambda}\chi_{\square}

and

\displaystyle D_{\lambda}\coloneqq(1-q)(1-t)B_{\lambda}-1.

Given another partition
\mu
,
\mu/\lambda
is the diagram formed by removing the cells of
\lambda
from
\mu
. We extend any notation involving characters to skew diagrams, so that, for instance,
B_{\mu/\lambda}
is the sum of characters for cells in
\mu/\lambda
.

The
content
of
\square=(i,j)
is the quantity

c_{\square}:=j-i.

Graphically, it is the SW-NE diagonal that the box lies on.
We call the class of
c_{\square}
modulo
r
its
color
and denote its value by
\bar{c}_{\square}
. The number of cells of color
i
is denoted by
d_{i}(\lambda)
.

Definition 2.2
.

If
\mu
is attained from
\lambda
by adding
k
cells of each color, then we will write
\lambda\subset_{k}\mu
.

We will see later that in light of Proposition
2.4
, if
\lambda\subset_{k}\mu
, then
\mathrm{core}(\lambda)=\mathrm{core}(\mu)
.

An addable corner of
\lambda
is a cell
\square\not\in\lambda
for which
\lambda\cup\square
is a partition. A removable corner is a cell
\square\in\lambda
for which
A/\square
is a partition.
Let
A_{i}(\lambda)
denote the addable cells of
\lambda
with color
i
, and similarly, let
R_{i}(\lambda)
be the removable corners of color
i
.

2.2.
Characters and colors

For a series
S=\sum_{a,b}c_{a,b}q^{a}t^{b}
in
q
and
t
, we let

S^{(i)}\coloneqq\sum_{i\equiv b-a\text{ mod }r}c_{a,b}q^{a}t^{b}

be the part of color
i
. We will also set

\displaystyle S_{\ast}=S\Big|_{(q,t)\rightarrow(q^{-1},t^{-1})}

and

\displaystyle S_{\ast}^{(i)}\coloneqq\left(S^{(i)}\right)_{\ast}=S^{(i)}\Big|_{(q,t)\rightarrow(q^{-1},t^{-1})}.

With this notation, we have

\displaystyle B_{\lambda}^{(i)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\square\in\lambda\\
\bar{c}_{\square}=i\end{subarray}}\chi_{\square}

and

\displaystyle D^{(i)}_{\lambda}=-tB_{\lambda}^{(i-1)}+(1+qt)B_{\lambda}^{(i)}-qB_{\lambda}^{(i+1)}-\delta_{i,0}.

One can show that

D^{(i)}_{\lambda}=qt\sum_{\square\in R_{i}(\lambda)}\chi_{\square}-\sum_{\square\in A_{i}(\lambda)}\chi_{\square}.

2.3.
Multipartitions and the Young-Maya correspondence

An
r
-tuple

\vec{\lambda}=(\lambda^{0},\ldots,\lambda^{r-1})

of partitions
has size given by

|\vec{\lambda}|:=\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}|\lambda^{i}|,

in which case we will write
\vec{\lambda}\vdash|\vec{\lambda}|
.
To avoid confusion, we will index the coordinates of the tuple by superscripts.
It will be useful to view the indexing set of the tuple as
\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}
, so that
\lambda^{i}=\lambda^{j}
if
i\equiv j\text{ mod }r
.

Concerning a single partition
\lambda
, a
ribbon
of length
r
is a set of
r
contiguous boxes on the outer (NE) edge of
\lambda
whose removal leaves behind another partition. (This is often referred to as a rim-hook in other contexts.)
An
r
-core partition is partition with no ribbons of length
r
.

2.3.1.
Maya diagrams

A Maya diagram is a function
m:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
such that

m(n)=\begin{cases}-1&n\gg 0\\
1&n\ll 0\end{cases}.

We can visualize
m
as a string of black and white beads indexed by
\mathbb{Z}
: the bead at position
n
is black if
m(n)=1
and white if
m(n)=-1
.
These beads will be arranged horizontally with the index increasing towards the
left
.
Between indices
0
and
-1
, we draw a notch and call it the
central line
.
The
charge

c(m)
is the difference in the number of discrepancies:

c(m)=\#\left\{n\geq 0\,|\,m(n)=1\right\}-\#\left\{n<0\,|\,m(n)=-1\right\}.

The
vacuum diagram
is the Maya diagram with only white beads left of the central line and only black beads right of the central line.

To a partition
\lambda
we associate a Maya diagram
m(\lambda)
via its

—we call this the

.
Namely, tilt
\lambda
by
45

Label by

n
and
n+1
.
Within the gap labeled by
n
, the outer (formerly NE) edge of
\lambda
either has slope
1
or
-1
; and
m(\lambda)(n)
takes the value of the slope of that segment.
Outside of
\lambda
, we assign the default limit assignments (or use the axes as part of the outer edge of
\lambda
).

3.
Symmetric functions

3.1.
Classical symmetric functions

A standard reference for some of this material is
[
Mac15
]
.
The ring of symmetric functions,
\Lambda
, is generated algebraically by any of the following families:

•

the power sums:
p_{n}=x_{1}^{n}+x_{2}^{n}+\cdots,

•

the complete homogeneous functions:
h_{n}=\sum_{i_{1}\leq\cdots\leq i_{n}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{n}},

•

the elementary functions:
e_{n}=\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{n}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{n}}
.

This means that for partitions
\lambda
, the elements
p_{\lambda}=p_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots p_{\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)}}
,
h_{\lambda}=h_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots h_{\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)}}
, and
e_{\lambda}=e_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots e_{\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)}}
, each give a basis for
\Lambda
.
There is also the Schur function basis
\{s_{\lambda}\}
and monomial basis
\{m_{\lambda}\}
. The symmetric function
m_{\lambda}
is the sum of monomials whose exponents are given by
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)}
.

The Hall scalar product is defined by setting

\langle p_{\lambda},p_{\mu}\rangle=z_{\lambda}\delta_{\lambda,\mu},

where
z_{\lambda}=\prod_{i}i^{m_{i}}m_{i}!
,
m_{i}
being the multiplicity of
i
in
\lambda
. One also has that
\{s_{\lambda}\}
forms an orthonormal basis; and the monomial basis is dual to the complete homogeneous basis.

Given any expression
E(t_{1},t_{2},\dots)
in the indeterminates
t_{1},t_{2},\dots
, one defines the plethystic substitution of a symmetric function
f
at
E
, denoted
f[E]
, by first defining

p_{n}[E(t_{1},t_{2},\dots)]=E(t_{1}^{n},t_{2}^{n}\dots),

then extending algebraically so that
p_{\lambda}[E]=p_{\lambda_{1}}[E]\cdots p_{\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)}}[E].

The plethystic exponential

\Omega[X]\coloneqq\exp\left(\sum_{k>0}p_{k}[X]/k\right)=\sum_{n\geq 0}h_{n}[X]

produces the Cauchy kernel:

\Omega[XY]=\sum_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}[X]s_{\lambda}[Y].

It is the reproducing kernel under the Hall scalar product: for any
f\in\Lambda
,

\left\langle\Omega[XY],f[X]\right\rangle=f[Y].

Another useful property of the plethystic exponential is that for any expressions
X
and
Y
,

\Omega[X+Y]=\Omega[X]\Omega[Y].

3.2.
Multisymmetric functions and new notations for plethystic expressions

In this section, we will introduce new notation for vector (or matrix) plethysms that will help us write many of the operators that follow.
First, recall that the ring of multisymmetric functions,
\Lambda^{\otimes r}
, is generated by
elements of the form
G=g_{0}[X^{(0)}]\cdots g_{r-1}[X^{(r-1)}],
where each
g_{i}\in\Lambda
is a symmetric function evaluated at a family of variables
X^{(i)}=x_{i1}+x_{i2}+\cdots
.
We now define vector plethysms in the following way:

Let
\epsilon_{0},\dots,\epsilon_{r-1}

be a set of standard basis elements indexed by elements of
\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}
(so that
\epsilon_{i}=\epsilon_{j}
if
i\equiv j\mod r
).
The basis vectors will also behave like projections, so that

\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}=\epsilon_{i}\delta_{i,j}.

Given any expressions
Y^{(0)},Y^{(1)},\dots,Y^{(r-1)}
, we define the vector
Y^{\bullet}=\epsilon_{0}Y^{(0)}+\cdots+\epsilon_{r-1}Y^{(r-1)}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Vector plethysms are defined by the following rules:

•

Given
G=g_{0}[X^{(0)}]\cdots g_{r-1}[X^{(r-1)}]\in\Lambda^{\otimes r}
, we define

G[Y^{\bullet}]\coloneqq g_{0}[\epsilon_{0}Y^{\bullet}]\cdots g_{r-1}[\epsilon_{r-1}Y^{\bullet}].

•

For an ordinary symmetric function
g\in\Lambda
and non-vector expression
E
,

g[\epsilon_{i}E]=g[E],

so that the
\epsilon_{i}
can be treated as the constant
1
. In particular, combined with the first rule,

G[Y^{\bullet}]=g_{0}[Y^{(0)}]\cdots g_{r-1}[Y^{(r-1)}].

For any family of symmetric functions
\{F_{\lambda}\}
indexed by partitions, we set

F_{\vec{\lambda}}[X^{\bullet}]\coloneqq F_{\lambda^{0}}[X^{(0)}]\cdots F_{\lambda^{r-1}}[X^{(r-1)}].

We then have the following families of bases for
\Lambda^{\otimes r}
:

\displaystyle\{p_{\vec{\lambda}}[X^{\bullet}]\},

\displaystyle\{h_{\vec{\lambda}}[X^{\bullet}]\},

\displaystyle\{e_{\vec{\lambda}}[X^{\bullet}]\},

\displaystyle\{m_{\vec{\lambda}}[X^{\bullet}]\},

and

\displaystyle\{s_{\vec{\lambda}}[X^{\bullet}]\}.

The Hall scalar product extends to the multisymmetric setting. In particular, we have

\langle s_{\vec{\lambda}},s_{\vec{\mu}}\rangle=\delta_{\vec{\lambda},\vec{\mu}}.

Lastly, it will be useful to denote the operator of multiplication by
F[X^{\bullet}]\in\Lambda^{\otimes r}
by
\underline{F}[X^{\bullet}]
. The adjoint of this operator under the Hall scalar product will be denoted by
F^{\perp}[X^{\bullet}]
.

3.2.1.
Linear maps and plethystic operators

We now consider linear transformations acting on the span of the
\epsilon_{i}
coordinates. In particular, one defines

\displaystyle\sigma\epsilon_{i}=\epsilon_{i-1}

and

\displaystyle\iota\epsilon_{i}=\epsilon_{-i}.

Then for
G=g_{0}[X^{(0)}]\cdots g_{r-1}[X^{(r-1)}]\in\Lambda^{\otimes r}
,

\displaystyle G[\sigma X^{\bullet}]

\displaystyle=G[\epsilon_{r-1}X^{(0)}+\epsilon_{0}X^{(1)}+\cdots+\epsilon_{r-2}X^{(r-1)}]=g_{0}[X^{(1)}]\cdots g_{r-2}[X^{(r-1)}]g_{r-1}[X^{(0)}],\text{ and }

\displaystyle G[\iota X^{\bullet}]

\displaystyle=G[\epsilon_{0}X^{(0)}+\epsilon_{-1}X^{(1)}+\cdots+\epsilon_{-r+1}X^{(r-1)}]=g_{0}[X^{(0)}]g_{1}[X^{(r-1)}]\cdots g_{r-1}[X^{(1)}].

One also has, for instance,

p_{n}\left[\epsilon_{i}(1-q\sigma)X^{\bullet}\right]=p_{n}\left[\epsilon_{i}X^{\bullet}-\epsilon_{i}q\sigma X^{\bullet}\right]=p_{n}\left[X^{(i)}-qX^{(i+1)}\right].

Definition 3.2
.

For any
k
, let

\displaystyle M_{k}\coloneqq(1-q\sigma^{k})(1-t\sigma^{-k})

and

\displaystyle M\coloneqq M_{1}=(1-q\sigma)(1-t\sigma^{-1}),

so that
M_{0}=(1-q)(1-t)
and
M_{-1}=(1-q\sigma^{-1})(1-t\sigma)=M^{T}
, where
M^{T}
denotes the transpose. We then also have that
\iota M_{k}=M_{-k}\iota
.

Definition 3.3
.

For any transformation
A
, we define the translation operators
\TT^{(i)}_{A}
and multiplication operators
\mathcal{P}^{(i)}_{A}
as follows:

\displaystyle\TT^{(i)}_{A}G[X^{\bullet}]

\displaystyle=G[X^{\bullet}+A\epsilon_{i}],

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{(i)}_{A}G[X^{\bullet}]

\displaystyle=\Omega\left[\epsilon_{i}AX^{\bullet}\right]G[X^{\bullet}].

For instance,
\mathcal{P}^{(i)}_{1}
corresponds to multiplication by
\Omega[X^{(i)}]
, and more crucially,
\mathcal{P}^{(i)}_{M^{T}}
corresponds to multiplication by

\displaystyle\Omega\left[\epsilon_{i}\frac{X^{\bullet}}{(1-q\sigma^{-1})(1-t\sigma)}\right]

\displaystyle=\Omega\left[\epsilon_{i}\sum_{a,b\geq 0}q^{a}t^{b}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}}\epsilon_{j+a-b}X^{(j)}\right]

\displaystyle=\Omega\left[\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}}X^{(j)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\geq 0\\
j\equiv i-a+b\mod r\end{subarray}}q^{a}t^{b}\right]

\displaystyle=\Omega\left[\sum_{a,b\geq 0}q^{a}t^{b}X^{(i-a+b)}\right].

Proposition 3.4
.

For transformations
A
and
B
, we have the following commutation relation:

\TT^{(i)}_{A}\mathcal{P}^{(j)}_{B}=\Omega\left[BA_{ji}\right]\mathcal{P}^{(j)}_{B}\TT^{(i)}_{A},

where
BA_{ji}=\epsilon_{j}BA\epsilon_{i}\Big|_{\epsilon_{j}=1}
is the matrix coefficient of
BA
corresponding to the
\epsilon_{j}
component in
BA\epsilon_{i}
.

Proof.

We note that

\displaystyle\TT^{(i)}_{A}\Omega\left[\epsilon_{j}BX^{\bullet}\right]

\displaystyle=\Omega\left[\epsilon_{j}B(X^{\bullet}+A\epsilon_{i})\right]\TT^{(i)}_{A}

\displaystyle=\Omega\left[\epsilon_{j}BX^{\bullet}\right]\Omega\left[\epsilon_{j}BA\epsilon_{i}\right]\TT^{(i)}_{A}

\displaystyle=\Omega\left[BA_{ji}\right]\Omega\left[\epsilon_{j}BX^{\bullet}\right]\TT^{(i)}_{A}.\qed

Proposition 3.5
.

For
f\in\Lambda
and any linear transformation
A
, we have

f\left[X^{\bullet}AY^{\bullet}\right]=f\left[Y^{\bullet}A^{T}X^{\bullet}\right]

Proof.

For standard basis elements
\epsilon_{i}
and
\epsilon_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}X^{(i)}A\epsilon_{j}Y^{(j)}=X^{(i)}Y^{(j)}A_{ij}\epsilon_{i}

and

\displaystyle\epsilon_{j}Y^{(j)}A^{T}\epsilon_{i}X^{(i)}=Y^{(j)}X^{(i)}A_{ij}\epsilon_{j}.

Since
f\in\Lambda
, we will in the end treat the
\epsilon_{i}
as
1
, meaning the equality in the proposition holds.
∎

4.
Wreath Macdonald polynomials and the Cauchy kernel

Definition 4.1
.

For any partition
\lambda
, the
modified wreath Macdonald polynomial

H_{\lambda}=H_{\lambda}[X^{\bullet};q,t]
is characterized by the following conditions:

(1)

H_{\lambda}[(1-q\sigma^{-1})X^{\bullet}]
lies in the span of
\left\{s_{\mathrm{quot}(\mu)}\,:\,~\mu\geq_{r}\lambda\right\}
;

(2)

H_{\lambda}[(1-t^{-1}\sigma^{-1})X^{\bullet}]
lies in the span of
\left\{s_{\mathrm{quot}(\mu)}\,:\,~\mu\leq_{r}\lambda\right\}
;

(3)

when expanded in terms of
\{s_{\vec{\mu}}\}
, the coefficient of
s_{n}[X^{(0)}]
is
1
, i.e.
H_{\lambda}[\epsilon_{0}]=1
.

Here,
\lambda\leq_{r}\mu
denotes dominance order, with the extra condition that
\mathrm{core}(\lambda)=\mathrm{core}(\mu)
.

Definition 4.2
.

The wreath Macdonald pairing is given by setting

\langle F,G\rangle_{\ast}=\langle F[-\iota X^{\bullet}],G[M^{T}X^{\bullet}]\rangle.

The wreath Macdonald basis is orthogonal to the “dagger” basis,

H_{\lambda}^{\dagger}\left[X^{\bullet}\right]=H_{\lambda}[-\iota X^{\bullet};q^{-1},t^{-1}],

where one has

\langle H_{\lambda}^{\dagger},H_{\mu}\rangle_{\ast}=N_{\lambda}\delta_{\lambda,\mu}

The value
N_{\lambda}
is given below:

Proposition 4.3

(
[
OS24
, Theorem 3.32]
)
.

For
\square=(a,b)\in\lambda
, we define the following:

•

Arm length:
a_{\lambda}(\square)\coloneq\lambda_{b+1}-a
,

•

Leg length:
l_{\lambda}(\square)\coloneq{}^{t}\lambda_{a+1}-b
,

•

Hook length:
h_{\lambda}(\square)=a_{\lambda}(\square)+l_{\lambda}(\square)+1
.

We then have

N_{\lambda}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\square\in\lambda\\
h(\square)\equiv 0\hbox{ \footnotesize{mod} }r\end{subarray}}\left(1-q^{a_{\lambda}(\square)+1}t^{-l_{\lambda}(\square)}\right)\left(1-q^{-a_{\lambda}(\square)}t^{l_{\lambda}(\square)+1}\right).

The
Cauchy kernel
in the wreath case is given by

\displaystyle\Omega\left[-(\iota X^{\bullet})\frac{Y^{\bullet}}{M^{T}}\right]

\displaystyle=\Omega\left[-X^{\bullet}\frac{(\iota Y^{\bullet})}{M}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{core}(\mu)=\alpha}\frac{H_{\mu}^{\dagger}[X^{\bullet}]H_{\mu}[Y^{\bullet}]}{N_{\mu}}=\sum_{\mathrm{core}(\mu)=\alpha}\frac{H_{\mu}[X^{\bullet}]H_{\mu}^{\dagger}[Y^{\bullet}]}{N_{\mu}},

where
\alpha
is any fixed core.
Note here that
we are multiplying expressions involving the coordinates
\epsilon_{i}
. So, for instance,

\displaystyle\iota(X^{\bullet})Y^{\bullet}

\displaystyle=(\epsilon_{0}X^{0}+\epsilon_{-1}X^{(1)}+\cdots+\epsilon_{1-r}X^{r-1})(\epsilon_{0}Y^{(0)}+\cdots+\epsilon_{r-1}Y^{(r-1)})

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{0}X^{(0)}Y^{(0)}+\epsilon_{1}X^{(r-1)}Y^{(1)}+\cdots+\epsilon_{r-1}X^{(1)}Y^{(r-1)}.

These are some of the benefits of utilizing coordinates and vector plethysms as defined—we are able to treat expressions in the plethystic brackets as algebraic expressions.

4.1.
An extension of multisymmetric functions

We let
\mathcal{W}=\Lambda^{\otimes r}\otimes\mathbb{C}[Q]
,
where
\mathbb{C}[Q]
is the span of elements
e^{(c_{0},\dots,c_{r-1})}
with
c_{0}+\cdots+c_{r-1}=0
(that is, an element of the root lattice for
A_{r-1}
). Such an element
(c_{0},\dots,c_{r-1})
corresponds to an
r
-core, say
\alpha
, for which we instead write
e^{(c_{0},\dots,c_{r-1})}=e^{\alpha}
.

The star and Hall inner products on
\mathcal{W}
are defined so that

\displaystyle\left\langle F\otimes e^{\alpha},G\otimes e^{\beta}\right\rangle_{\ast}\coloneqq\langle F,G\rangle_{\ast}\delta_{w_{0}\alpha,\beta}

and

\displaystyle\left\langle F\otimes e^{\alpha},G\otimes e^{\beta}\right\rangle\coloneqq\langle F,G\rangle\delta_{w_{0}\alpha,\beta}.

5.
Operators on wreath Macdonald polynomials

Definition 5.1
.

We define the Delta operators by setting, for any
G\in\Lambda^{\otimes r}
,

\displaystyle G[D]\left(H_{\lambda}\otimes e^{\mathrm{core}(\lambda)}\right)=G[D^{\bullet}_{\lambda}]H_{\lambda}\otimes e^{\mathrm{core}(\lambda)}\text{ and }

\displaystyle G[D^{\dagger}]\left(H^{\dagger}_{\lambda}\otimes e^{w_{0}\mathrm{core}(\lambda)}\right)=G[D^{\bullet}_{\lambda}]H^{\dagger}_{\lambda}\otimes e^{w_{0}\mathrm{core}(\lambda)}.

Note here that
D^{\bullet}_{\lambda}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}}\epsilon_{i}D^{(i)}_{\lambda}
, so that

G[\epsilon_{i}D]H_{\lambda}\otimes e^{\mathrm{core}(\alpha)}=G[\epsilon_{i}D^{(i)}_{\lambda}]H_{\lambda}\otimes e^{\mathrm{core}(\alpha)},

and likewise,
since

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}\frac{D_{\lambda}^{\bullet}}{M}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{i}\left(\sum_{a,b\geq 0}q^{a}t^{b}\sigma^{a-b}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}}\epsilon_{j}D^{(j)}_{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{i}\left(\sum_{a,b\geq 0}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}}\epsilon_{j-a+b}q^{a}t^{b}D^{(j)}_{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{i}\left(\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}}\epsilon_{p}\sum_{a,b\geq 0}q^{a}t^{b}D^{(p+a-b)}_{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{D_{\lambda}}{M_{0}}\right)^{(i)},

we have that

(5.1)

G\left[\epsilon_{i}\frac{D}{M}\right]H_{\lambda}\otimes e^{\mathrm{core}(\alpha)}=G\left[\epsilon_{i}\left(\frac{D_{\lambda}}{M_{0}}\right)^{(i)}\right]H_{\lambda}\otimes e^{\mathrm{core}(\alpha)}.

One can also define a Delta operator given by

\displaystyle G[B]\left(H_{\lambda}\otimes e^{\mathrm{core}(\lambda)}\right)=G[B^{\bullet}_{\lambda}]H_{\lambda}\otimes e^{\mathrm{core}(\lambda)}\text{ and }

\displaystyle G[B^{\dagger}]\left(H^{\dagger}_{\lambda}\otimes e^{w_{0}\mathrm{core}(\lambda)}\right)=G[B^{\bullet}_{\lambda}]H^{\dagger}_{\lambda}\otimes e^{w_{0}\mathrm{core}(\lambda)},

though we make less use of this version in our discourse.

We now define a family of nabla operators:

Definition 5.2
.

For any color
j
, let

T^{(j)}_{\lambda}\coloneqq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\square\in\lambda/\mathrm{core}(\lambda)\\
\bar{c}_{\square}=j\end{subarray}}(-\chi_{\square})~~=~(-1)^{|\mathrm{quot}(\lambda)|}\frac{e_{d_{j}(\lambda)}\left[B_{\lambda}^{(j)}\right]}{e_{d_{j}(\mathrm{core}(\lambda))}\left[B_{\mathrm{core}(\lambda)}^{(j)}\right]}.

Then define

\nabla^{(j)}H_{\lambda}\otimes e^{\mathrm{core}(\lambda)}\coloneqq T^{(j)}_{\lambda}H_{\lambda}\otimes e^{\mathrm{core}(\lambda)}.

We also set
\nabla\coloneqq\nabla^{(0)}
.

It is shown in
[
RW25
]
that the following is true.

Proposition 5.3
.

For any partition
\lambda
,

T^{(0)}_{\lambda}=H_{w_{0}\lambda}[\iota D_{\mathrm{core}(\lambda)}^{\bullet}]=H_{\lambda}[\iota D_{w_{0}\mathrm{core}(\lambda)}^{\bullet}]=T^{(0)}_{w_{0}\lambda}.

6.
The Tesler Identity and the map
\mathsf{V}

From
[
RW25
]
, we have a map

\mathsf{V}\coloneqq\nabla\mathcal{P}^{(0)}_{-1/M^{T}}\TT^{(0)}_{1}\nabla

that relates wreath Macdonald polynomials to the exponential versions

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Omega\left[-X^{\bullet}\frac{D^{\bullet}_{\lambda}}{M}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{core}(\mu)=\alpha}\frac{H^{\dagger}_{\mu}[X^{\bullet}]H_{\mu}[\iota D^{\bullet}_{\lambda}]}{N_{\mu}}=\sum_{\mathrm{core}(\mu)=\alpha}\frac{H_{\mu}[X^{\bullet}]H^{\dagger}_{\mu}[\iota D^{\bullet}_{\lambda}]}{N_{\mu}}.

It has the following properties:

Proposition 6.1
.

For any
F\in\Lambda^{\otimes r}
, we have

•

\mathsf{V}\underline{F}=F[\iota D^{\dagger}]\mathsf{V}
,

•

F^{\perp}[X^{\bullet}]\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{V}F\left[\frac{-D}{M}\right]
,

•

\mathsf{V}(1\otimes e^{\alpha})=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\otimes e^{\alpha},

•

\mathsf{V}\nabla^{-1}H_{\lambda}[X^{\bullet}]\otimes e^{\mathrm{core}(\lambda)}=\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\otimes e^{\mathrm{core}(\lambda)}.

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\TT^{(i)}_{v}\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{V}\Omega\left[-v\epsilon_{i}\frac{D}{M}\right]

and

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}\mathcal{P}_{-v/M^{T}}^{(i)}=\Omega\left[-v\epsilon_{i}\frac{\iota D^{\dagger}}{M^{T}}\right]\mathsf{V}.

6.1.
Wreath Macdonald-Koornwinder reciprocity revisited

We now look at some consequences of the
properties in Proposition
6.1
that follow from our vector plethysm rules. We will treat this section as an “exercise” on using vector plethysms, while highlighting some of the important properties of wreath Macdonald polynomials. Let
\mathrm{core}(\lambda)=\alpha
.
First note that from the last item in Proposition
6.1
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}H_{\lambda}\otimes e^{\alpha}=T^{(0)}_{\lambda}\Omega\left[-X^{\bullet}\frac{D^{\bullet}_{\lambda}}{M}\right]\otimes e^{\alpha}=T^{(0)}_{\lambda}\sum_{w_{0}\mathrm{core}(\mu)=\alpha}\frac{H^{\dagger}_{\mu}[X^{\bullet}]H_{\mu}[\iota D^{\bullet}_{\lambda}]}{N_{\mu}}\otimes e^{\alpha}.

On the other hand, one has

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}H_{\lambda}\otimes e^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=H_{\lambda}[\iota D^{\dagger}]\mathsf{V}(1\otimes e^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=H_{\lambda}[\iota D^{\dagger}]\sum_{w_{0}\mathrm{core}(\mu)=\alpha}\frac{H^{\dagger}_{\mu}[X^{\bullet}]H_{\mu}[\iota D^{\bullet}_{\alpha}]}{N_{\mu}}\otimes e^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{w_{0}\mathrm{core}(\mu)=\alpha}\frac{H_{\lambda}[\iota D^{\bullet}_{\mu}]H^{\dagger}_{\mu}[X^{\bullet}]H_{\mu}[\iota D^{\bullet}_{\alpha}]}{N_{\mu}}\otimes e^{\alpha}.

Equating the coefficient of
H_{\mu}^{\dagger}[X^{\bullet}]
in both expressions gives

Proposition 6.2

(Reciprocity)
.

For
\lambda
and
\mu
with
w_{0}\mathrm{core}(\mu)=\mathrm{core}(\lambda)
,

\frac{H_{\lambda}[\iota D_{\mu}^{\bullet}]}{T^{(0)}_{\lambda}}=\frac{H_{\mu}[\iota D_{\lambda}^{\bullet}]}{T^{(0)}_{\mu}}.

We now extend Proposition
6.2
to a wreath Macdonald-Koornwinder reciprocity by using our vector plethysm rules.
We first record degrees with an extra variable
z
, writing

\nabla\mathcal{P}^{(0)}_{-z/M^{T}}\TT^{(0)}_{1/z}H_{\lambda}[zX^{\bullet}]=\Omega\left[-zX^{\bullet}\frac{D^{\bullet}_{\lambda}}{M}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[zX^{\bullet}].

We will now apply
\nabla^{-1}\mathsf{V}\nabla^{-1}=\mathcal{P}^{(0)}_{-1/M^{T}}\TT^{(0)}_{1}
to
\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[zX^{\bullet}]
two different ways: Let
\mathrm{core}(\lambda)=\alpha
, and consider the following computations in
\Lambda^{\otimes r}\otimes e^{\alpha}
(so we can omit the
-\otimes e^{\alpha}
in every line).

(1)

First, we write

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{(0)}_{-1/M^{T}}\TT^{(0)}_{1}\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[zX^{\bullet}]

\displaystyle=\Omega\left[-\epsilon_{0}\frac{X^{\bullet}}{M^{T}}\right]\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[z(X^{\bullet}+\epsilon_{0})]

\displaystyle=\Omega\left[-\epsilon_{0}\frac{X^{\bullet}}{M^{T}}\right]\Omega\left[-z(X^{\bullet}+\epsilon_{0})\frac{D^{\bullet}_{\lambda}}{M}\right]

\displaystyle=\Omega\left[-X^{\bullet}\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{M}\right]\Omega\left[-X^{\bullet}\frac{zD^{\bullet}_{\lambda}}{M}\right]\Omega\left[-z\epsilon_{0}\frac{D^{\bullet}_{\lambda}}{M}\right]

\displaystyle=\Omega\left[-X^{\bullet}\frac{\epsilon_{0}+zD^{\bullet}_{\lambda}}{M}\right]\Omega\left[-z\left(\frac{D_{\lambda}}{M_{0}}\right)^{(0)}\right]

\displaystyle=\Omega\left[-z\left(\frac{D_{\lambda}}{M_{0}}\right)^{(0)}\right]\sum_{w_{0}\mathrm{core}(\mu)=\alpha}\frac{H_{\mu}^{\dagger}[X^{\bullet}]H_{\mu}[\epsilon_{0}+z\iota D_{\lambda}^{\bullet}]}{N_{\mu}}.

(2)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\nabla^{-1}\mathsf{V}\nabla^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[zX^{\bullet}]

\displaystyle=\nabla^{-1}\mathsf{V}\nabla^{-1}\nabla\mathcal{P}_{-z/M^{T}}^{(0)}\TT^{(0)}_{1/z}H_{\lambda}[zX^{\bullet}]

\displaystyle=\nabla^{-1}\mathsf{V}\Omega\left[-z\epsilon_{0}\frac{X^{\bullet}}{M^{T}}\right]H_{\lambda}[\epsilon_{0}+zX^{\bullet}]

\displaystyle=\nabla^{-1}\Omega\left[-z\epsilon_{0}\frac{\iota D^{\dagger}}{M^{T}}\right]H_{\lambda}[\iota(\epsilon_{0}+z\iota D^{\dagger})]\mathsf{V}\left(1\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega\left[-z\epsilon_{0}\frac{D^{\dagger}}{M}\right]H_{\lambda}[\epsilon_{0}+z\iota D^{\dagger}]\nabla^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}[X^{\bullet}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{w_{0}\mathrm{core}(\mu)=\alpha}\frac{H_{\mu}^{\dagger}[X^{\bullet}]}{N_{\mu}}\Omega\left[-z\left(\frac{D_{\mu}}{{M_{0}}}\right)^{(0)}\right]H_{\lambda}[\epsilon_{0}+z\iota D_{\mu}^{\bullet}].

Comparing the coefficient of
H_{\mu}^{\dagger}[X^{\bullet}]
in both of our computations, and using that

\Omega\left[-z\left(\frac{D_{\mu}}{M_{0}}\right)^{(0)}\right]=\Omega\left[-zB_{\mu}^{(0)}\right]\Omega\left[-\left(\frac{z}{M_{0}}\right)^{(0)}\right]=\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\square\in\mu\\
\bar{c}_{\square}=0\end{subarray}}1-z\chi_{\square}\right)\Omega\left[-\left(\frac{z}{M_{0}}\right)^{(0)}\right],

we get that

Proposition 6.3

(Wreath Macdonald-Koornwinder Reciprocity)
.

If
w_{0}\mathrm{core}(\mu)=\mathrm{core}(\lambda)
, then

\frac{H_{\lambda}[\epsilon_{0}+z\iota D_{\mu}^{\bullet}]}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\square\in\lambda\\
\bar{c}_{\square}=0\end{subarray}}1-z\chi_{\square}}=\frac{H_{\mu}[\epsilon_{0}+z\iota D_{\lambda}^{\bullet}]}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\square\in\mu\\
\bar{c}_{\square}=0\end{subarray}}1-z\chi_{\square}}.

More generally, it is shown in
[
RW25
]
that if
w_{0}\sigma^{-k}\mathrm{core}(\mu)=\mathrm{core}(\lambda)
, then

(6.1)

\frac{H_{\lambda}[\epsilon_{0}+z\iota\sigma^{k}D_{\mu}^{\bullet}]}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\square\in\lambda\\
\bar{c}_{\square}=k\end{subarray}}1-z\chi_{\square}}=\frac{H_{\mu}[\epsilon_{0}+z\iota\sigma^{k}D_{\lambda}^{\bullet}]}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\square\in\mu\\
\bar{c}_{\square}=k\end{subarray}}1-z\chi_{\square}}.

Remark 6.4
.

The plethysms in (
6.1
) look different from the ones from
[
RW25
]
.
This is due to the different treatment of plethysm in this paper.
The two formalisms coincide in their action on multi-symmetric functions.
On the other hand, in
loc. cit.
, we do not define
\sigma
and
\iota
on a sum of characters like
D_{\mu}^{\bullet}
; rather, an expression such as
f[\sigma^{k}\iota D_{\mu}^{\bullet}]
is meant to be interpreted as performing
f[\sigma^{k}\iota X^{\bullet}]
and then evaluating at

.
This results in the sequence of transformations

p_{n}[X^{(i)}]\mapsto p_{n}[X^{(k-i)}]\mapsto p_{n}[D_{\mu}^{(k-i)}].

In the formalism used in this paper, we have

\epsilon_{i}\left(\iota\sigma^{k}D_{\mu}^{\bullet}\right)=D_{\mu}^{(k-i)}.

Therefore, the evaluations in
[
RW25
]
and (
6.1
) coincide.

For a given diagram
\lambda
, let

\Pi_{\lambda}^{(k)}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\square\in\lambda/(1)\\
\bar{c}_{\square}=k\end{subarray}}1-\chi_{\square}.

Corollary 6.5
.

If
w_{0}\sigma^{-k}\mathrm{core}(\mu)=\mathrm{core}(\lambda)
, then

\frac{H_{\lambda}[\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{k}+\iota\sigma^{k}M^{T}B_{\mu}^{\bullet}]}{\Pi_{\lambda}^{(k)}}=\frac{H_{\mu}[\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{k}+\iota\sigma^{k}M^{T}B_{\lambda}^{\bullet}]}{\Pi_{\mu}^{(k)}}.

In particular, for
k=0
, we have

\frac{H_{\lambda}[M\iota B_{\mu}^{\bullet}]}{\Pi_{\lambda}^{(0)}}=\frac{H_{\mu}[M\iota B_{\lambda}^{\bullet}]}{\Pi_{\mu}^{(0)}}.

7.
Five-term relations and tableau formulas for Pieri coefficients

To lighten the notation, we will let

\displaystyle\Delta_{v}^{(s)}=\Omega\left[-v\epsilon_{s}\frac{D}{M}\right],

with inverse

\displaystyle\Delta_{-v}^{(s)}=\Omega\left[v\epsilon_{s}\frac{D}{M}\right].

We see from (
5.1
) that

\Delta_{v}^{(s)}H_{\lambda}=\Omega\left[-v\left(\frac{D_{\lambda}}{M_{0}}\right)^{(s)}\right]H_{\lambda}.

We wish to study the action of the operator

W(u,v)=\sum_{i,j}u^{i}v^{j}W_{i,j}=\mathcal{P}^{(p)}_{u/{M^{T}}}\Delta_{v}^{(s)}\mathcal{P}^{(p)}_{-u/{M^{T}}}\Delta_{-v}^{(s)}.

Such expressions for modified Macdonald polynomials were studied by Garsia and Mellit
[
GM19
]
, leading to a five-term relation with incredibly powerful consequences. We will also find that for the wreath Macdonald polynomials there is a family of “five-term relations,” depending on two colors and whether we act on the wreath Macdonald polynomials or their dagger version.
This will lead to explicit formulas for Pieri and dual Pieri coefficients.
However, since wreath Macdonald polynomials are not self-dual, we will need some care in approaching the dual Pieri case.

In this section, we first look at Pieri formulas corresponding to multiplication by a modified elementary symmetric function. In the subsequent section, we will look at the dual Pieri formulas corresponding to skewing with complete homogeneous symmetric functions.

One of the main ingredients we require is the following condition on Pieri coefficients.

Proposition 7.1

(
[
RW25
, Lemma 4.3]
)
.

Recall that we write
\lambda\subset_{k}\mu
if
\mu
is attained from
\lambda
by adding
k
cells of each color, and we therefore also have that

\mathrm{core}(\lambda)=\mathrm{core}(\mu)
.
For any
k
and partition
\lambda
,

{e}_{k}\left[\epsilon_{p}\frac{X^{\bullet}}{M^{T}}\right]H_{\lambda}=\sum_{\lambda\subset_{k}\mu}d_{\mu,\lambda}^{(p)}H_{\mu}.

Similarly, the adjoint of this is given by

h_{k}^{\perp}\left[X^{(p)}\right]H_{\lambda}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\mu\subset_{k}\lambda}c_{\mu,\lambda}^{\dagger(p)}H_{\mu}^{\dagger}.

Our goal in this section is to give a formula for these coefficients.

7.1.
Triangularities in
u
and
v

The main goal is to show that
W(u,v)=\sum_{i}u^{i}v^{i}W_{i,i},
then identify the diagonal terms. We do this in a similar way to the proof in
[
GM19
]
: we first show that
W_{i,j}=0
for
j>i
, identify
W_{i,i}
, then show
W_{i,j}=0
for
i>j
.

Proposition 7.2
.

The operator

\Delta_{v}^{(s)}\underline{e}_{k}\left[\epsilon_{p}\frac{X^{\bullet}}{M^{T}}\right]\Delta_{-v}^{(s)}

is a polynomial in
v
of degree
k
. Furthermore, the top degree in
v
is given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{v}^{(s)}\underline{e}_{k}\left[\epsilon_{p}\frac{X^{\bullet}}{M^{T}}\right]\Delta_{-v}^{(s)}\Big|_{v^{k}}=\nabla^{(s)}\underline{e}_{k}\left[\epsilon_{p}\frac{X^{\bullet}}{M^{T}}\right]{\nabla^{(s)}}^{-1}.

In particular, since
\mathcal{P}^{(p)}_{u/M^{T}}
only raises the degree in
u
, this means that
W_{i,j}=0
for
j>i
; and for
i=j
, we have

W_{i,i}=\nabla^{(s)}e_{i}\left[\epsilon_{p}\frac{X^{\bullet}}{M^{T}}\right]{\nabla^{(s)}}^{-1}.

Proof.

From the Pieri coefficients, we see that when
\mathrm{core}(\lambda)=\alpha
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{v}^{(s)}\underline{e}_{k}\left[\epsilon_{p}\frac{X^{\bullet}}{M^{T}}\right]

\displaystyle\Delta_{-v}^{(s)}\left(H_{\lambda}\otimes e^{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{v}^{(s)}\underline{e}_{k}\left[\epsilon_{p}\frac{X^{\bullet}}{M^{T}}\right]\Omega\left[v\epsilon_{s}\frac{D_{\lambda}^{\bullet}}{M}\right]\left(H_{\lambda}\otimes e^{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega\left[v\left(\frac{D_{\lambda}}{M_{0}}\right)^{(s)}\right]\Delta_{v}^{(s)}\left(\sum_{\mathrm{core}(\mu)=\alpha}d_{\mu,\lambda}^{(p)}H_{\mu}\otimes e^{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{core}(\mu)=\alpha}d_{\mu,\lambda}^{(p)}\Omega\left[v\left(\frac{D_{\lambda}}{M_{0}}\right)^{(s)}\right]\Omega\left[-v\left(\frac{D_{\mu}}{M_{0}}\right)^{(s)}\right]H_{\mu}\otimes e^{\alpha}

7.3.

For

Define the “downarrow” operator by setting

\downarrow F[X^{\bullet};q,t]\otimes e^{\alpha}=F[-\iota X^{\bullet};q^{-1},t^{-1}]\otimes e^{w_{0}\alpha}.

On operators, we have the following:

\displaystyle\downarrow\nabla\downarrow=\nabla^{-1},

\displaystyle{}^{\ast}\Delta_{v}^{(i)}\coloneqq\Omega\left[-v\epsilon_{i}\frac{D_{\ast}}{M_{\ast}}\right]

\displaystyle{}^{\ast}\Delta_{-v}^{(i)}\coloneqq\Omega\left[v\epsilon_{i}\frac{D_{\ast}}{M_{\ast}}\right].

8.1.
Wreath Theta operators

We have looked at the operator

\Theta^{(p,s)}(w;v)\coloneqq\Delta_{v}^{(s)}\mathcal{P}^{(p)}_{-w/M^{T}}\Delta_{-v}^{(s)}

in the context of the five-term relations. We define an anaologue of the Theta operator, introduced in

]

and color
(s)

\Theta^{(s)}_{f}=\Delta_{v}^{(s)}\underline{f}\Delta_{-v}^{(s)}\Big|_{v=1}.

With this notation, we have

\Theta^{(p,s)}(w;1)=\Theta^{(s)}_{\Omega[-w\epsilon_{p}(X^{\bullet}/M^{T})]}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-w)^{n}\Theta^{(s)}_{\tilde{e}_{n}^{(p)}},

where

\tilde{e}_{n}^{(p)}=e_{n}\left[\epsilon_{p}\frac{X^{\bullet}}{M^{T}}\right].

is a modified elementary symmetric function.

The following relation between Theta operators and the
\mathbb{D}
-operators is an analogue of Conjecture 10.3 in
[
DIVW21
]
, which was proved in
[
Rom22
]
.

Theorem 8.4
.

For given colors
p
,
s
, and
i
,

\left[\Theta^{(s)}_{\tilde{e}^{(p)}_{n}},\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon_{s}-\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{i}}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{k}\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon_{s}-\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{i}-k\epsilon_{p}}\Theta^{(s)}_{\tilde{e}^{(p)}_{n-k}}.

In particular, when

, one has

\left[\Theta^{(0)}_{\tilde{e}^{(p)}_{n}},\mathbb{D}_{-\epsilon_{i}}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{k}\mathbb{D}_{-\epsilon_{i}-k\epsilon_{p}}\Theta^{(0)}_{\tilde{e}^{(p)}_{n-k}}.

Proof.

By applying the five-term relations, Proposition
8.2
, and Lemma
8.3
,
we get

\displaystyle\Theta^{(p,s)}(w;v)\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon_{s}-\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{i}}\left(\Theta^{(p,s)}(w;v)\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\Delta_{v}^{(s)}\mathcal{P}^{(p)}_{-{w}/{M^{T}}}\Delta_{-v}^{(s)}\right)\nabla^{(s)}\tilde{e}_{1}^{(i)}{\nabla^{(s)}}^{-1}\left(\Delta_{v}^{(s)}\mathcal{P}^{(p)}_{w/M^{T}}\Delta_{-v}^{(s)}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{P}_{-w/M^{T}}^{(p)}\nabla^{(s)}\mathcal{P}_{-wv/M^{T}}^{(p)}{\nabla^{(s)}}^{-1}\right)\nabla^{(s)}\tilde{e}_{1}^{(i)}{\nabla^{(s)}}^{-1}

\displaystyle\hskip 150.00023pt\left(\nabla^{(s)}\mathcal{P}_{wv/M^{T}}^{(p)}{\nabla^{(s)}}^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{w/M^{T}}^{(p)}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{-w/M^{T}}^{(p)}\nabla^{(s)}\mathcal{P}_{-wv/M^{T}}^{(p)}\tilde{e}_{1}^{(i)}\mathcal{P}_{wv/M^{T}}^{(p)}{\nabla^{(s)}}^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{w/M^{T}}^{(p)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{-w/M^{T}}^{(p)}\left(\nabla^{(s)}\tilde{e}_{1}^{(i)}{\nabla^{(s)}}^{-1}\right)\mathcal{P}_{w/M^{T}}^{(p)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{-w/M^{T}}^{(p)}\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon_{s}-\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{i}}\mathcal{P}_{w/M^{T}}^{(p)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{-w/M^{T}}^{(p)}(z_{s}/z_{0}z_{i})\mathbb{D}\mathcal{P}_{w/M^{T}}^{(p)}\Big|_{z^{0}}

\displaystyle=(1-w/z_{p})^{-1}(z_{s}/z_{0}z_{i})\mathbb{D}\Big|_{z^{0}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}w^{k}\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon_{s}-\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{i}-k\epsilon_{p}}.

Therefore,

\Theta^{(p,s)}(w;v)\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon_{s}-\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{i}}=\sum_{k\geq 0}w^{k}\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon_{s}-\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{i}-k\epsilon_{p}}\Theta^{(p,s)}(w;v)

\Theta^{(s)}_{\tilde{e}^{(p)}_{n}}\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon_{s}-\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{i}}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon_{s}-\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{i}-k\epsilon_{p}}\Theta^{(s)}_{\tilde{e}^{(p)}_{n-k}}.\qed

This expression allows one to write Theta operators in terms of

-operators.

(A.13)

-e_{1}\left[\epsilon_{-p}\frac{X^{\bullet}}{M^{T}}\right]H_{\lambda}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\lambda\subset_{1}\mu}d_{\mu,\lambda}^{\dagger(p)}H_{\mu}^{\dagger}.

Lemma A.3
.

The coefficient
d_{\mu,\lambda}^{\dagger(p)}
is given by

(A.14)

\displaystyle d_{\mu,\lambda}^{\dagger(p)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-qt}\left(\frac{\chi_{p}}{\chi_{0}}\right)\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in R_{i}(\mu)}\left(1-qt\frac{\chi_{\square}}{\chi_{i}}\right)}{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in A_{i}(\mu)}\left(1-\frac{\chi_{\square}}{\chi_{i}}\right)}.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\downarrow H_{\lambda}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=H_{\lambda},

\displaystyle\downarrow\left(-e_{1}\left[\epsilon_{-p}\frac{X^{\bullet}}{M^{T}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=qte_{1}\left[\epsilon_{p}\frac{X^{\bullet}}{M^{T}}\right].

Applying
\downarrow
to both sides of (
A.13
), we have

\left(d_{\mu,\lambda}^{\dagger(p)}\right)\Bigg|_{(q,t)\mapsto(q^{-1},t^{-1})}=qtd_{\mu,\lambda}^{(p)}.\qed

A.2.2.
Applying adjunction

Our statement about taking adjoints is summarized by the following:

Lemma A.4
.

We have

c_{\lambda,\mu}^{(p)}=\frac{N_{\mu}}{N_{\lambda}}d_{\mu,\lambda}^{\dagger(p)}.

Proof.

We can pick out
c_{\lambda,\mu}^{(p)}
with the following inner product:

\displaystyle c_{\lambda,\mu}^{(p)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\lambda}}\left\langle H^{\dagger}_{\lambda},h_{1}^{\perp}[X^{(p)}]H_{\mu}\right\rangle_{\ast}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\lambda}}\left\langle-e_{1}\left[\epsilon_{-p}\frac{X^{\bullet}}{M^{T}}\right]H_{\lambda}^{\dagger},H_{\mu}\right\rangle_{\ast}

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{\mu}}{N_{\lambda}}d_{\mu,\lambda}^{\dagger(p)}.\qed

A.2.3.
Consolidating hooks

The ratio
N_{\mu}/N_{\lambda}
can be unpacked using the following lemma:

Lemma A.5
.

Let
\blacksquare\in A_{i}(\lambda)
.
For
r>1
, we have

(A.15)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{N_{\lambda\cup\blacksquare}}{N_{\lambda}}\right)\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in R_{i}(\lambda\cup\blacksquare)}\left(1-qt\frac{\chi_{\square}}{\chi_{\blacksquare}}\right)}{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in A_{i}(\lambda\cup\blacksquare)}\left(1-\frac{\chi_{\square}}{\chi_{\blacksquare}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in A_{i}(\lambda)}\left(1-qt\frac{\chi_{\blacksquare}}{\chi_{\square}}\right)}{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in R_{i}(\lambda)}\left(1-\frac{\chi_{\blacksquare}}{\chi_{\square}}\right)}.

Proof.

We can rewrite the left-hand-side of (
A.15
) as

(A.16)

(1-qt)\left(\frac{N_{\lambda\cup\blacksquare}}{N_{\lambda}}\right)\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in R_{i}(\lambda)}\left(1-qt\frac{\chi_{\square}}{\chi_{\blacksquare}}\right)}{\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\square\in A_{i}(\lambda)\\
\square\not=\blacksquare\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{\chi_{\square}}{\chi_{\blacksquare}}\right)}

(note that
r>1
is important here).
Recall that

(A.17)

N_{\lambda}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\square\in\lambda\\
h(\square)\equiv 0\hbox{ \footnotesize{mod} }r\end{subarray}}\left(1-q^{a_{\lambda}(\square)+1}t^{-l_{\lambda}(\square)}\right)\left(1-q^{-a_{\lambda}(\square)}t^{l_{\lambda}(\square)+1}\right).

For a box
\square\in\lambda
, let
\square_{N}
be the northernmost box in the same column as
\square
. Let
\square^{\prime}
be the cell directly east, and
{}^{\prime}\square
the cell directly west.
Consider a hook corresponding to
\square\in\lambda
in the same row as
\blacksquare
– the hook starts at
\square_{N}
, moves down to
\square
, then moves east to
\blacksquare
.

•

If
h_{\lambda}(\square)\equiv 0\mod r
and
\square_{N}
is not removable, then the factor in (
A.17
) cancels with the factor in
N_{\lambda\cup\blacksquare}
corresponding to
\square^{\prime}
.

•

If
h_{\lambda\cup\blacksquare}(\square)\equiv 0\mod r
, and
\square_{N}
is not below and addable corner, then its corresponding factor cancels with the factor for
{}^{\prime}\square
in
N_{\lambda}
.

•

If
h_{\lambda}(\square)\equiv 0\mod r
and
\square_{N}
is removable, then we must have
\square_{N}\in R_{i}(\lambda)
.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle q^{-a_{\lambda}(\square)}t^{l_{\lambda}(\square)+1}=qt\chi_{\square_{N}}/\chi_{\blacksquare}

and

\displaystyle q^{a_{\lambda}(\square)+1}t^{-l_{\lambda}(\square)}=\chi_{\blacksquare}/\chi_{\square_{N}}.

•

If
h_{\lambda\cup\blacksquare}(\square)\equiv 0\mod r
and
\square_{N}
is below an addable corner
\overline{\square}_{N}
, then
\overline{\square}_{N}\in A_{i}(\lambda)
.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle q^{-a_{\lambda\cup\blacksquare}(\square)}t^{l_{\lambda\cup\blacksquare}(\square)+1}=\chi_{\overline{\square}_{N}}/\chi_{\blacksquare}

and

\displaystyle q^{a_{\lambda\cup\blacksquare}(\square)+1}t^{-l_{\lambda\cup\blacksquare}(\square)}=qt\chi_{\blacksquare}/\chi_{\overline{\square}_{N}}.

A similar argument analyzes the hooks which start with
\blacksquare
above
\square
. We see that after cancelling terms that appear, in (
A.16
), one gets

(1-qt)\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\square\in A_{i}(\lambda)\\
\square\not=\blacksquare\end{subarray}}\left(1-qt\frac{\chi_{\blacksquare}}{\chi_{\square}}\right)}{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in R_{i}(\lambda)}\left(1-\frac{\chi_{\blacksquare}}{\chi_{\square}}\right)},

which is the right-hand side of (
A.15
).
∎

A.2.4.
Proof of (
A.2
)

By Lemma
A.4
, we need to compute

c_{\lambda,\mu}^{(p)}=\frac{N_{\mu}}{N_{\lambda}}d_{\mu,\lambda}^{\dagger(p)}.

We will show that

(A.18)

c_{\lambda,\mu}^{(p)}=\frac{1}{1-qt}\left(\frac{\chi_{p}}{\chi_{0}}\right)\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in A_{i}(\lambda)}\left(1-qt\frac{\chi_{i}}{\chi_{\square}}\right)}{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in R_{i}(\lambda)}\left(1-\frac{\chi_{i}}{\chi_{\square}}\right)}.

Recall that the index
i
in
\square_{i}\in\mu/\lambda
corresponds to its color.
Let us order the boxes
\square_{i_{1}},\ldots,\square_{i_{r}}
such that for all
k=1,\ldots,r
,

\lambda(k)\coloneq\mu/\{\square_{i_{1}},\ldots,\square_{i_{k}}\}

is a partition.
We will set
\lambda(0)=\mu
.
Thus, we have

\mu=\lambda(0)\supset\lambda(1)\supset\cdots\supset\lambda(r)=\lambda.

Our strategy will be to factor the coefficient

c_{\lambda,\mu}^{(p)}=\frac{N_{\mu}}{N_{\lambda}}d_{\mu,\lambda}^{\dagger(p)}=d_{\mu,\lambda}^{\dagger(p)}\prod_{k=1}^{r}\frac{N_{\lambda(k-1)}}{N_{\lambda(k)}}

and then apply Lemma
A.5
to
N_{\lambda(k-1)}/N_{\lambda(k)}
and the binomials in
d_{\mu,\lambda}^{\dagger(p)}
corresponding to
\chi_{i_{k}}
.
However, the factors in
d_{\mu,\lambda}^{\dagger(p)}
are in terms of
R_{i_{k}}(\mu)
and
A_{i_{k}}(\mu)
, not
R_{i_{k}}(\lambda(k-1))
and
A_{i_{k}}(\lambda(k-1))
.
Additionally, even if we can apply Lemma
A.5
, the resulting factors are in terms of
R_{i_{k}}(\lambda(k))
and
A_{i_{k}}(\lambda(k))
, not
R_{i_{k}}(\lambda)
and
A_{i_{k}}(\lambda)
.

It turns out that both problems can be resolved simultaneously.
Note that we can immediately apply Lemma
A.5
at step
k=1
.
By induction, at step
k
, applying Lemma
A.5
yields

\displaystyle\left(\frac{N_{\lambda(k-1)}}{N_{\lambda(k)}}\right)\prod_{\ell=k}^{r}\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in R_{i_{\ell}}(\lambda(k-1))}\left(1-qt\frac{\chi_{\square}}{\chi_{i_{\ell}}}\right)}{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in A_{i_{\ell}}(\lambda(k-1))}\left(1-\frac{\chi_{\square}}{\chi_{i_{\ell}}}\right)}

(A.19)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in A_{i_{k}}(\lambda(k))}\left(1-qt\frac{\chi_{i_{k}}}{\chi_{\square}}\right)}{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in R_{i_{k}}(\lambda(k))}\left(1-\frac{\chi_{i_{k}}}{\chi_{\square}}\right)}}_{(a)}\,\overbrace{\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{r}\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in R_{i_{\ell}}(\lambda(k-1))}\left(1-qt\frac{\chi_{\square}}{\chi_{i_{\ell}}}\right)}{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in A_{i_{\ell}}(\lambda(k-1))}\left(1-\frac{\chi_{\square}}{\chi_{i_{\ell}}}\right)}}^{(b)}.

We need to show that (
A.19
) is equal to:

(A.20)

\underbrace{\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in A_{i_{k}}(\lambda)}\left(1-qt\frac{\chi_{i_{k}}}{\chi_{\square}}\right)}{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in R_{i_{k}}(\lambda)}\left(1-\frac{\chi_{i_{k}}}{\chi_{\square}}\right)}}_{(a^{\prime})}\,\overbrace{\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{r}\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in R_{i_{\ell}}(\lambda(k))}\left(1-qt\frac{\chi_{\square}}{\chi_{i_{\ell}}}\right)}{\displaystyle\prod_{\square\in A_{i_{\ell}}(\lambda(k))}\left(1-\frac{\chi_{\square}}{\chi_{i_{\ell}}}\right)}}^{(b^{\prime})}.

The factors
(a)
and
(a^{\prime})
can only differ if
\square_{i_{k}+1}
and/or
\square_{i_{k}-1}
are in
\lambda(k)
.
If this is the case, we will say that
i_{k}+1
and/or
i_{k}-1
are
after

i_{k}
.
Likewise,
(b)
and
(b^{\prime})
will only differ if
i_{k}+1
and/or
i_{k}-1
are after
i_{k}
.
Let
(a)/(a^{\prime})
and
(b)/(b^{\prime})
denote the corresponding quotients; we will show that

\frac{(a)}{(a^{\prime})}\cdot\frac{(b)}{(b^{\prime})}=1.

The analysis requires us to consider many cases:

(1)

Only
i_{k}+1
is after
i_{k}
with
\square_{i_{k}}
and
\square_{i_{k}+1}
adjacent
: we have
(a)/(a^{\prime})=(1-qt)
due to
\square_{i_{k}}\in A_{i_{k}}(\lambda(k))
but
\square_{i_{k}}\not\in A_{i_{k}}(\lambda)
, and

due to
\square_{i_{k}+1}\in R_{i_{k}+1}(\lambda(k))
but
\square_{i_{k}+1}\not\in R_{i_{k}+1}(\lambda(k-1))

(2)

Only
i_{k}+1
is after
i_{k}
with
\square_{i_{k}}
and
\square_{i_{k}+1}
disjoint
: here, either the square right of
\square_{i_{k}+1}

A_{i_{k}}(\lambda)
or the square below
\square_{i_{k}+1}

\square_{i_{k}}

(3)

Only
i_{k}-1

i_{k}

(4)

i_{k}+1

i_{k}

i_{k}+1
and
i_{k}-1

i_{k}

(6)

i_{k}+1
