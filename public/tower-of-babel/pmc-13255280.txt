Severe leptospirosis complicated by atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, myocarditis, and acute liver injury: a therapeutic challenge—a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Risly NMM, Jayasinghe IK, Athauda N, Wevage KAD, Sahana JF
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere leptospirosis, also known as Weil’s disease, is a life-threatening zoonotic infection characterized by multi-organ dysfunction, including acute kidney injury and jaundice. We report this case due to its rarity and complex nature.Case presentationThis case report describes a 44-year-old previously healthy, Sri Lankan Sinhalese male who presented with high-grade fever, deep jaundice, acute kidney injury, and signs of systemic inflammation. Despite early initiation of intravenous antibiotics, steroids, and supportive care, the patient’s condition deteriorated with features suggestive of atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS), myocarditis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and acute liver injury. Diagnostic findings revealed severe direct hyperbilirubinemia, intravascular hemolysis, and progressive renal failure requiring sustained low-efficiency dialysis (SLED). Etiology evaluation revealed leptospira Ig M positivity. A multidisciplinary approach, including therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE), was employed, resulting in gradual clinical improvement, although the patient remained dialysis-dependent at discharge.ConclusionsThis case underscores the importance of recognizing atypical complications of leptospirosis and highlights the role of therapeutic plasma exchange as a potential rescue therapy in severe and refractory cases. Further research is needed to optimize management strategies in such complex clinical scenarios.Keywords: Leptospirosis, Atypical Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (aHUS), Therapeutic Plasma Exchange (TPE), Myocarditis, Hyperbilirubinemia, Case report

Background

Leptospirosis, is a zoonotic infection characterized by multiorgan dysfunction, including acute kidney injury and jaundice [1, 2]. Though there is a wide spectrum of complications, they often include infectious-toxic shock, acute renal-hepatic failure, hemorrhagic syndrome, and myocarditis [2, 3]. Only few cases of leptospirosis with atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome and severe direct hyperbilirubinemia are reported in the literature [4–6]. Therefore, our case is unique in its complex presentation and management.

Case presentation

A 44-year-old previously healthy Sri-Lankan Sinhalese male, presented during a heavy rainy season with a three-day history of fever accompanied by chills and rigors, severe back pain, epigastric pain, deep jaundice, and severe anorexia with constipation. Additionally, he reported a six-month history of a scaly rash over his upper and lower limbs, scalp, and nail changes suggestive of psoriasis. His past medical, surgical, and allergic history was unremarkable.

On examination, the patient was febrile and deeply icteric, with severe tenderness in the right hypochondrium. His abdomen was soft, and his vital signs were stable, with a blood pressure of 90/60 mmHg, a pulse rate of 100 bpm, and oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. Cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were unremarkable. The general examination revealed cutaneous features of psoriasis.

Given the presentation, and initial investigations, an initial diagnosis of atypical infection (probably hepatobiliary origin) with septic shock, acute kidney injury, and multi-organ failure was considered. The patient was promptly started on intravenous antibiotics, intravenous fluids with continuous monitoring in a high-dependency unit (HDU).

Despite aggressive initial management, the patient’s condition deteriorated, showing worsening tachycardia, tachypnea, and reduced urine output, although blood pressure remained stable without vasopressor support. An urgent venous blood gas (VBG) analysis revealed severe metabolic acidosis with lactatemia. Laboratory investigations confirmed atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome following which extensive etiology evaluation initiated [Tables 1 and 2]. He was found to have acute kidney injury (AKI) with severe cholestasis, evidenced by markedly elevated serum creatinine and bilirubin levels along with positive Leptospira IgM antibody test. The patient was subsequently transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer monitoring and further management. During his ICU stay, the patient developed worsening tea-colored urine [Fig. 1], worsening AKI requiring sustained low-efficiency dialysis (SLED), and significant intravascular hemolysis [Fig. 2].

However, direct antiglobulin test (DAT) results were negative, and other markers such as haptoglobin and urine hemosiderin were initially normal but later haptoglobin reduced. With ongoing deterioration, a multidisciplinary team suspected atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) complicating severe leptospirosis. Therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) was initiated, and the patient underwent three sessions, along with one unit of packed red blood cell transfusion to maintain hemoglobin above 7 g/dL. The patient’s clinical course was further complicated by myocarditis, as evidenced by elevated troponin I levels and global hypokinesia on echocardiography. He also developed acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), characterized by a PO₂ of 86 mmHg on high-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO) and a P/F ratio of 220, although there was no evidence of pulmonary hemorrhage [Fig. 3].

Additionally, he developed acute liver injury, with a worsening coagulation profile, hepatic flaps, and encephalopathy. Supportive management continued with SLED sessions every other day, alongside optimal supportive care for myocarditis, ARDS, and hepatic encephalopathy. Further laboratory evaluations, including repeated blood smears, demonstrated resolving hemolysis. Reticulocyte counts remained within expected ranges, serum LDH levels gradually decreased, and urine for hemoglobin remained positive while urine hemosiderin was consistently negative. Infectious and Autoimmune screening including complement levels were normal. Rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) analysis indicated no requirement for blood product transfusions in the absence of active bleeding. With intensive care, therapeutic plasma exchange, and sustained renal support through SLED, the patient gradually showed clinical improvement yet ended up dialysis dependent. Discussion

We present a complex case of severe leptospirosis with direct hyperbilirubinemia and Atypical Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome due to the rare nature among the widespread clinical manifestations of leptospirosis [2, 7]. Hemolysis in leptospirosis may result from phospholipases produced by the infecting Leptospira, leading to erythrocyte membrane damage [8]. Cases with high bilirubinemia (> 30 mg/dL) and hemolytic anemia are very rare in human leptospirosis [4, 9]. Typical total bilirubin concentrations in jaundiced patients are usually less than 20 mg/dL but can rise to 40 mg/dL or higher in more severe cases, with reported maxima reaching 60–80 mg/dL in extreme instances. Regarding fractions, both direct (conjugated) and indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin levels increase, but the direct fraction typically accounts for most of the elevation, reflecting cholestatic liver dysfunction rather than pure hemolysis [4, 9, 10]. In our case extreme elevation of T. bilirubin 615.6 µmol/L (≈36 mg/dL) likely due to concomitant cholestasis with acalculous cholecystitis and severe hemolysis.

Differentiating sepsis-related thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) from atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) and Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) remained as a significant diagnostic challenge in our case due to their overlapping clinical and laboratory features. These conditions can present as thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, and organ dysfunction, particularly renal impairment though the pathophysiology and therapeutic approaches differ significantly [6, 11]. In the absence of ADAMTS13 activity, TTP was excluded clinically and biochemically (PLASMIC Score 4). Diagnosis of atypical HUS was established by presence of TMA, absence of diarrheal prodrome, negative shiga-toxin in stools with low normal C3 levels with normal C4 levels.

In the management perspective of our case, we tried a novel therapeutic modality of repeated therapeutic plasma exchanges along with SLED. Treatment of severe leptospirosis typically involves antimicrobial therapy and supportive care, including mechanical ventilation and renal replacement therapy. Steroids have also shown potential benefits in some cases. A multidisciplinary approach is essential for managing severe leptospirosis and reducing mortality [1, 2, 5].

The decision to initiate therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) was guided by the presence of severe, refractory hyperbilirubinemia, ongoing hemolysis, and progressive multi-organ involvement, all of which indicated a rapidly deteriorating clinical course. In addition, the unavailability of eculizumab—a costly but targeted therapy for complement-mediated TMA—further necessitated the use of TPE as a pragmatic and potentially life-saving intervention in this context.

Therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) has shown promising results in treating severe leptospirosis cases with multiorgan dysfunction. Multiple case reports demonstrate its efficacy in managing complications such as acute kidney injury, pulmonary hemorrhages, myocarditis, severe thrombocytopenia, and extreme hyperbilirubinemia [4, 12–14]. TPE has been particularly effective in reducing serum bilirubin levels and removing harmful substances, leading to rapid clinical improvement and recovery of liver and renal function. In cases where standard treatments like antibiotics and renal replacement therapy fail to show improvement, TPE has been successfully used as a rescue therapy [4, 14].

Conclusion

This case underscores the complexity and severity of leptospirosis, compounded by atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, myocarditis, ARDS, and acute liver injury. It highlights that, in severe cases of leptospirosis with atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome and hyperbilirubinemia with poor clinical improvement to conventional therapeutic measures, novel therapy with TPE could lend greater benefit. It requires further research on similar clinical context.
