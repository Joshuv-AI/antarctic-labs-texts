Study on rapid identification of soil slope boulders based on natural vibration frequency
Authors: Yanchang Jia, Yahui Liang, Guihao Song, Hongfei Wang, Mowen Xie, Shuyan Hua, Zhihao Chen, Wenlong Ma, Tianbao Niu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The instability of soil slope boulders represents a prevalent geological hazard, characterized by its sudden onset and significant threats to engineering safety. However, the burial depth of boulders, a critical parameter for stability assessment, poses challenges for direct measurement. To facilitate rapid and accurate stability evaluations, this study introduces a method for assessing soil slope boulder stability based on natural vibration frequency. By simplifying the interaction between the boulder and the surrounding soil into a multi-degree-of-freedom spring-mass vibration model, we derive a dynamic characteristic model for the boulder. This model integrates the Limit Equilibrium Method with the dynamic framework to establish a stability evaluation model predicated on natural vibration frequency. The study identifies two primary failure modes for boulders: shear-sliding and rotational-toppling types. Utilizing the proposed model, we elucidate the quantitative relationships among natural vibration frequency, boulder burial depth, and boulder mass. Experimental investigations with boulder models of varying sizes demonstrate a nonlinear relationship between natural vibration frequency and both burial depth and mass. By combining the model with the Limit Equilibrium Method, we derive a formula for calculating the safety factor, which is validated through engineering case studies. The findings reveal that the calculated safety factor agrees well with numerical simulation results, thereby confirming the model’s applicability in practical engineering contexts. This study establishes a robust theoretical framework for monitoring and preventing geological disasters associated with the instability of boulders on soil slopes.

Introduction

With the continuous acceleration of infrastructure development in mountainous regions, the instability of soil slope boulders has become increasingly prominent. Geological hazards, including rolling and sliding, occur frequently, posing significant threats to engineering safety as well as to public life and property1. China’s complex terrain and extensive mountainous areas render boulder-related1 disasters particularly prevalent in the southwestern region. Located at the southeastern margin of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, this region is characterized by intense tectonic activity, steep slopes, and well-developed karst landforms, making it a high-incidence zone for geological disasters2–5. Prolonged tectonic stress, vigorous physical weathering, and chemical dissolution have resulted in the widespread distribution of boulders across steep soil slopes, with their stability directly influencing regional geological hazard assessment and prevention efforts6,7. Consequently, the establishment of scientific and efficient methods for evaluating and monitoring the dynamic characteristics and stability of boulders in the southwestern mountainous regions holds significant theoretical and engineering importance8,9.

The instability of soil slope boulders is a complex dynamic process influenced by multiple coupled factors. The physical and mechanical properties of the boulder itself, including mass, size, and shape, its embedded condition in the soil, such as boulder burial depth and contact conditions, and external disturbances such as rainfall, earthquakes, and human activities, all significantly affect its stability10–12. From a mechanical perspective, the stability of boulders is contingent upon the balance between driving forces, such as gravitational components and overturning moments, and resisting forces, including shear and anti-overturning resistance provided by the soil. When external disturbances or degradation of soil parameters result in driving forces surpassing resisting forces, the boulder becomes unstable and transitions into a rolling mass with significant destructive potential, posing threats to facilities and human safety located downslope13–15.

Currently, methods for evaluating boulders stability can be categorized into three types: qualitative, quantitative, and non-deterministic approaches. Qualitative methods, which include geological historical analysis, engineering analogy, and graphical techniques, heavily rely on expert experience and field investigations. While these methods are intuitive, they are inherently subjective and challenging to quantify. Quantitative methods are primarily represented by the Limit Equilibrium Method and Numerical Simulation. The Limit Equilibrium Method determines the safety factor by establishing mechanical equilibrium equations; although it is theoretically sound and widely applied, it necessitates assumptions regarding the shape of the slip surface and contact conditions. Ensuring the accuracy of key parameters proves difficult, resulting in uncertainties in the outcomes16. Numerical simulation methods such as the Finite Element Method and Discrete Element Method can realistically represent the interaction between the boulder and the soil and the progressive failure process, offering advantages in revealing instability mechanisms. However, they involve high computational cost and require numerous parameters, limiting their applicability for rapid field evaluation17–19. Moreover, non-deterministic methods such as fuzzy mathematics, artificial neural networks20, and grey system theory21,22 have potential in handling nonlinear and uncertain problems but rely on large datasets.

With the rapid development of sensing and signal processing technologies, monitoring and evaluation methods for slope stability based on dynamic parameters have gradually become research hotspots23–25. Compared with traditional optical and GPS-based measurements, dynamic approaches based on micro-vibration signal analysis offer advantages such as non-contact operation, high precision, and strong real-time capability26. Although distributed optical fiber sensing and Fiber Bragg Grating technologies can achieve multi-parameter integrated monitoring27–30, their high cost and complex implementation restrict large-scale applications. In recent years, non-contact monitoring methods such as the Laser Doppler Vibrometer31 and BeiDou /GNSS systems32 have provided feasible means for extracting vibration characteristics and performing dynamic stability analysis of soil slope boulders.

Among various dynamic parameters, the natural vibration frequency is widely recognized as an important dynamic indicator for evaluating structural stiffness and boundary condition variations due to its high sensitivity33. Fujisawa et al. found through shaking table tests that unstable rock masses exhibit significantly higher vibration responses than stable ones. Ma et al.34 revealed a positive correlation between the natural vibration frequency of rock masses and the area of the contact surface. Chinese scholars Du et al.35, Yao et al.36, and Jia & Xie et al.37,38 developed dynamic models for falling-type, rotational-toppling type, and shear-sliding type unstable rock masses, grounded in dynamic theory. These models elucidate the relationship between natural vibration frequency and structural degradation, while also proposing quantitative evaluation methods based on frequency changes39,40. These studies demonstrate that changes in natural vibration frequency can serve as important precursors to instability. However, existing research mainly focuses on unstable rock masses under hard rock conditions, while insufficient attention has been paid to the dynamic characteristics of soil slope boulders. The boulder-soil system exhibits typical flexible boundary characteristics, significantly different from rigid or semi-rigid rock foundations. Meanwhile, soil properties such as moisture content and density are easily affected by environmental changes, dynamically altering the boulder’s embedding condition and vibration response. Consequently, the direct application of hard-rock dynamic models to the assessment of soil slope boulders is limited. Presently, achieving a rapid and accurate stability evaluation of soil slope boulders based on natural vibration frequency remains an unresolved scientific challenge41.

To address these issues, this study concentrates on soil slope boulders and establishes a rapid stability evaluation model based on natural vibration frequency. First, typical instability modes are analyzed, and a dynamic characteristic model of the boulder-soil system is constructed, considering the coupled effects of boulder mass, boulder burial depth, shape, and soil parameters. Then, the Limit Equilibrium Method is combined with the spring-mass vibration model to derive quantitative relationships among boulder burial depth, natural vibration frequency, and safety factor under different failure modes, forming a rapid stability evaluation model. Finally, slope boulders from the Shuangjiangkou Hydropower Station are used as engineering examples. Field vibration tests are conducted to obtain the natural vibration frequency, and the derived model is used to invert the boulder burial depth and safety factor, which are then verified against numerical simulation results such as Midas. The findings demonstrate that the proposed model is effective and feasible for practical engineering applications. This study offers a new theoretical foundation and technical approach for the rapid stability evaluation and dynamic monitoring of soil slope boulders, providing scientific support for engineering safety and geological disaster prevention in mountainous regions42.

Methods

Main dynamic characteristics and failure types of boulders

The instability of soil slope boulders is characterized by sudden occurrences and spatial discreteness, influenced by internal factors such as burial depth, loading, and slope soil properties, as well as external factors like rainfall and earthquakes. In the Limit Equilibrium Method, it is difficult to directly obtain the burial depth, whereas dynamic characteristic parameters such as natural vibration frequency are easier to measure and can indirectly reflect the burial depth. Therefore, analyzing these parameters enables the study of burial depth, providing a basis for stability evaluation and monitoring and early warning of boulder instability. Moreover, by introducing the natural vibration frequency, the most difficult-to-obtain parameter in traditional approaches—the boulder burial depth—is effectively circumvented, thereby significantly improving the practicality of safety factor calculation. Soil slope boulders primarily exhibit two types of failure: the shear-sliding type as shown in Fig. 1a and the rotational-toppling type as shown in Fig. 1b. Both failure types are essentially shear failures of the soil under load.

The instability of soil slope boulders can be classified into two types: shear-sliding and rotational-toppling. However, the vibration characteristics exhibited during instability align with a spring–mass vibration model. To relate the vibration characteristics of the boulder to its stability factors, such as mass and burial depth, the boulder is modeled as a mass point, while the structural surface is represented as a spring. Assuming that the foundation soil is homogeneous, isotropic, possesses a damping ratio of less than 1, and behaves as a linear elastic medium, the system can be simplified into a multi-degree-of-freedom spring–mass vibration model as shown in Fig. 2.

Experimental design

A hammer was employed to randomly strike the boulder model, while a high-precision Doppler laser vibrometer was utilized to accurately capture the acceleration time-history curve of the model. The signal underwent filtering to eliminate noise and was subsequently transformed from the time domain to the frequency domain using a Fourier transform, resulting in the power spectrum of the boulder model. This power spectrum effectively illustrates the distribution of vibrational energy across various frequencies, thereby providing a critical basis for determining the natural vibration frequency of the boulder model.Vibrometer

Stability evaluation and application

Force analysis of boulder

When considering the effects of water pressure and seismic forces, the sliding force of the Shear–Sliding Type Soil Slope Boulders is mainly provided by gravity, seismic load, and active earth pressure, while the anti-sliding force is primarily contributed by friction and passive earth pressure. The overturning moment of the Rotational–Toppling Type Soil Slope Boulders is mainly caused by gravity, seismic load, and water pressure, whereas the resisting moment is primarily provided by passive earth pressure. When the effects of water pressure and seismic forces are not considered, the sliding force of the Shear–Sliding Type Soil Slope Boulders is mainly due to gravity and active earth pressure, while the anti-sliding force is provided by friction and passive earth pressure. The resisting moment of the Rotational–Toppling Type Soil Slope Boulders is generated by passive earth pressure, and the overturning moment is primarily caused by gravity. Field application and numerical simulation of the theoretical model

The proposed vibration-based model is mainly applicable to soil slope boulders with similar geological conditions and comparable boulder burial depth characteristics. According to geological surveys, the rock mass of the Shuangjiangkou Hydropower Station slope has developed dip-oriented and steeply dipping fractures, accompanied by intense interlayer and fracture-type weathering. These factors have led to the formation of potential hazard sources such as isolated boulders, boulder clusters, and unstable rock masses. Two representative boulders, numbered 1 and 2, on the slope were selected as engineering cases for analysis as shown in Fig. 12. Sensors were installed on boulders No. 1 and No. 2, and repeated impact excitations were applied. A Laser Doppler Vibrometer with high sensitivity and strong resistance to environmental noise was used to acquire the time-domain signals of the boulders. The obtained signals were filtered and transformed into the frequency domain using the Fast Fourier Transform, yielding the frequency-domain information of the boulders.

(7) yielded calculated Burial Depths of 1.21 m for Boulder No. 1 and 0.37 m for Boulder No. 2, with corresponding errors of 4.3% and 7.5%, respectively, verifying the reliability of the theoretical model. Field investigations indicated that Boulder No. 1 was more susceptible to shear–sliding instability, whereas Boulder No. 2 was more prone to rotational–toppling instability. Substituting the calculated burial depths into the Safety Factor equations based on Natural Vibration Frequency, the Safety Factor of Boulder No. 1 under water pressure and seismic conditions was 1.14, and that of Boulder No. 2 was 1.816, both indicating stable states. When water pressure and seismic forces were not considered, the Safety Factors were 1.35 for Boulder No. 1 and 3.599 for Boulder No. 2, also indicating stability.

A coupled Soil–Boulder System Numerical Model was established using MIDAS software to perform Numerical Simulation under natural conditions without considering water pressure or seismic loads, thereby achieving quantitative evaluation of the system’s stability. The Mohr–Coulomb parameters were applied, and the model boundaries were fixed at the bottom and sides. After solving for the initial stress field, the displacement, velocity, and plastic zones were cleared, and the Strength Reduction Method was used to compute the Safety Factors for Boulders No. 1 and No. 2. Discussion

This study focuses on the soil slope boulders as the research subject and establishes a stability evaluation model based on natural vibration frequency. Through theoretical derivation, laboratory experiments, and validation with engineering cases, the model’s accuracy and applicability have been confirmed. The findings indicate that this method can rapidly and accurately assess boulder stability without relying on traditional measurements of boulder burial depth.

Conventional stability evaluation methods, such as the Limit Equilibrium Method and Numerical Simulation, are commonly employed in slope stability analysis. However, these methods encounter significant challenges in obtaining the critical parameter of boulder burial depth, particularly in areas characterized by complex terrain and harsh environmental conditions, where direct measurement is often impractical. This limitation constrains both the precision and efficiency of stability evaluations. To address this issue, the present study introduces an innovative concept that incorporates the dynamic characteristics of boulders, specifically their natural vibration frequency, into the stability evaluation process. By simplifying the boulder-soil system into a spring-mass vibration model, a quantitative relationship among natural vibration frequency, boulder burial depth, and boulder mass has been derived. Laboratory experiments provide strong support for the theoretical model; results show that natural vibration frequency increases nonlinearly with boulder burial depth while decreasing with boulder mass, aligning with theoretical predictions. Tests conducted on five boulder models of varying sizes and masses under twenty working conditions yielded an average inversion error of 6.9% for boulder burial depth, with the maximum error not exceeding 13.7%. This result demonstrates the reliability and robustness of the proposed dynamic characteristic model. The instability of soil slope boulders is generally classified into the Shear–Sliding Type and the Rotational–Toppling Type. However, in engineering practice, when boulders exhibit mixed failure modes, the dominant failure mode is usually determined based on their geometric characteristics and geological conditions through an approximate judgment, and the corresponding safety factor calculation formula is then adopted. Further detailed investigation of complex mixed failure modes will be considered as a direction for future research.

Furthermore, by integrating the dynamic characteristic model with the Limit Equilibrium Method, a unified framework was established to calculate the safety factor directly from natural vibration frequency measurements. This integration effectively addresses the long-standing challenge of inaccessible boulder burial depth in conventional methods and significantly enhances evaluation efficiency. The field application at the Shuangjiangkou Hydropower Station further validates the model’s applicability. The safety factor calculated from measured natural vibration frequencies exhibited high consistency with Midas Numerical Simulation results, with a deviation within 11% under conditions excluding water pressure and seismic forces.

Overall, these findings confirm that natural vibration frequency serves as a reliable dynamic indicator for assessing the stability of soil slope boulders. The proposed model bridges dynamic testing technologies, such as Laser Doppler Vibrometry, with classical mechanical theory, facilitating a transition from static parameter-dependent evaluation to dynamic, data-driven assessment. This approach holds considerable potential for geological disaster prevention, early-stage investigation, and long-term monitoring and warning of slope stability in mountainous environments. Existing studies have developed various methods for the rapid Stability Evaluation of slopes, including rock mass strength assessment based on the GSI index43, the Qslope method44 and its improved models, as well as stability analysis frameworks incorporating machine learning45 and fuzzy intelligent algorithms46. These approaches have demonstrated good engineering applicability in the classification of rock slope stability and risk identification. In contrast, the method proposed in this study provides a complementary investigation into the stability of soil slope boulder from the perspective of dynamic indicator.

This study successfully established and validated a stability evaluation model for soil slope boulders based on natural vibration frequency through theoretical derivation, laboratory experiments, and engineering case verification.
