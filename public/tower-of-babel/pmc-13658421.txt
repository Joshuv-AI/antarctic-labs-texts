Species discordance in diatom testing for drowning diagnosis: a biological passive sampling hypothesis with forensic implications
Authors: Bo Wang, Jianhua Chen, Jifeng Cai, Xiaoshi Qin, Jie Cai, Yuzhao Xu, Jianghuan Lu, Yixin Ma, Zhiao Duan, Jianqiang Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiatom testing is a key technique for forensic drowning diagnosis, yet species discordance between diatom taxa in victim tissues and water samples from the recovery site remains a major medico-legal challenge, often misattributed to technical errors or post-mortem contamination. This correspondence proposes an interdisciplinary interpretative hypothesis based on biological passive sampling theory from environmental science, hypothesizing three potential mechanisms driving this discordance: selective sampling by anatomical structures and diatom physical traits, time-integrated accumulation of diatoms during the agonal period of drowning versus snapshot water sampling, and chronic dietary diatom exposure forming a potential background signal. We cautiously distinguish natural biological discordance from artificial post-mortem contamination, and suggest reframing discordance from a methodological flaw to a potential source of actionable forensic information, provided that its interpretative boundaries are clearly defined. Evidence-based forensic interpretation guidelines for different discordance patterns and standardized practice recommendations for diatom testing are provided, including reporting organ-specific diatom profiles, evaluating tissue barrier integrity, and implementing strict blank controls. This framework is presented as a testable hypothesis consistent with recent systematic reviews on diatom test interpretation and provides a theoretical basis for the urgent standardization of forensic diatom testing, enhancing the scientific validity of drowning diagnosis and site inference in medico-legal practice.Keywords: Drowning diagnosis, Diatom testing, Species discordance, Biological passive sampling, Forensic legal medicine

Dear Editor,

A recurring problem in drowning diagnosis is the mismatch between diatom species found in tissues and those collected from the water where the body was recovered [1, 2]. Such discordance often leads to controversial interpretations of drowning diagnosis and even incorrect inferences of the drowning site, posing great challenges to medico-legal practice [3]. Many view this discordance as a weakness of the diatom test. They often blame technical errors or postmortem contamination. We propose an alternative interdisciplinary perspective based on the biological passive sampling theory in environmental science, which offers a hypothetical explanatory framework for this phenomenon. We emphasize that this framework is an interpretative model requiring further empirical validation, not an established biological mechanism.

In environmental monitoring, a passive sampler collects analytes from water or air through natural diffusion or convection, without external pumps [4, 5]. Unlike grab sampling, which provides a snapshot, passive sampling builds up a time‑integrated record of exposure over the deployment period [6]. The final analyte profile reflects both selective uptake—governed by the sampler’s physical properties and environmental conditions—and cumulative exposure [7]. We propose that, during drowning, the victim’s organs may act as natural biological passive samplers (PS). However, we acknowledge that human organs cannot be directly compared with calibrated environmental passive samplers, since diatom entry and distribution depend on aspiration, circulation, agonal physiology, tissue barriers, decomposition, resuscitation, sampling procedures, and laboratory contamination [1, 8]. Three hypothetical mechanisms may account for why the diatom species in tissues often differ from those in a water sample.

First, selective sampling. Not all organs capture diatoms in the same way. Even different parts of the same organ can show different uptake. Anatomical features—such as how well a lung lobe is ventilated—affect which diatoms enter and where they settle. Physical properties of diatoms, including size and shape, also matter. In environmental passive sampling, different analytes exhibit distinct sampling rates depending on their physicochemical properties and the sampler material [7]. By analogy, different diatom species may show varying capture efficiencies across different organs and even across different regions within the same organ. Hagen and colleagues [9] found clear differences in diatom counts across lung lobes in confirmed drowning cases. The left upper lobe consistently had the fewest diatoms. This is not random variation. It is consistent with organ‑specific sampling, likely attributable to the selective uptake of diatoms with different physical properties by different anatomical structures [9].

Second, time‑integrated accumulation. A PS records exposure over its entire deployment period. During the drowning process, the agonal period in which active water aspiration and systemic circulation occur is the critical window for diatom entry into internal organs. This period is usually much shorter than the total body immersion time, which may last for hours or even days [10]. During the agonal period, water diatom communities may change due to tidal movements, water flow or short-term environmental variations. In contrast, the water sample collected after the victim’s death only reflects the diatom community at a single time point, leading to potential species differences between the sample and the victim’s tissues. When diatom communities vary over time, the tissue profile will rarely match a single post-recovery water sample exactly. This time‑integrative property is a fundamental characteristic of passive sampling, distinguishing it from discrete grab sampling [5, 6]. We emphasize that this mechanism applies specifically to the agonal period with active circulation, not to prolonged postmortem immersion, during which diatom spread to closed organs is limited in the absence of cardiac function [1, 11].

Third, background exposure. Diatoms may also enter the body through ordinary daily life. Du and co‑workers [12] showed in an animal model that ingested diatoms can cross the gastrointestinal barrier and migrate to internal organs. Yen and Jayaprakash [13] calculated that non‑vegetarians consume roughly 2 million diatom frustules each year through seafood. However, the extent to which dietary diatoms contribute a significant background signal in human internal organs remains controversial. Recent controlled studies using standardized protocols to avoid postmortem contamination have reported markedly fewer diatoms in non-drowned individuals than earlier studies. Lunetta and colleagues [14] detected only 9 diatoms in 6 of 14 non-drowned bodies. Bortolotti and colleagues [15] found no diatoms in the organs of 45 non-drowned cadavers. Auer and Möttönen [16] reported no diatoms recovered from internal organs of 15 non-drowned cadavers. These findings suggest that, while dietary exposure to diatoms is documented, the presence of a substantial background diatom signal in human internal organs is not conclusively established and should not be overstated [1, 8, 17]. Thus, the diatoms found in tissues are likely a blend of acute exposure from drowning and, potentially, chronic exposure from diet, but the relative contribution of the latter remains uncertain.

Recent empirical forensic studies are consistent with this three-mechanism hypothesis. Tsuneya et al. [11] observed higher diatom counts in the closed organs of severely decomposed bodies. This finding is consistent with the importance of tissue barrier integrity in maintaining the original diatom profile from drowning exposure, which is a key prerequisite for the validity of the biological PS framework. Bogusz et al. [18] showed that diatom infiltration into bone marrow only happened when the bone cortex was damaged. This result is consistent with the selective sampling hypothesis, as the bone marrow, a closed organ, only allows diatom infiltration when its physical barrier is damaged, rather than through active selective uptake. Even Lunetta’s recent report on contaminated microscope slides [19] reinforces the importance of environmental sampling principles: strict blank controls are essential, because contamination can appear even at the final step. This study reminds us that contamination control, a basic principle of environmental PS, is also essential in diatom testing for drowning diagnosis to exclude artificial species discordance.

Two distinct processes contribute to species discordance and require clear differentiation. Postmortem contamination refers to the diatoms entering the body after death due to barrier breach, which is an artificial interference. Background exposure is the potential chronic diatom uptake from daily diet before death, which is a natural biological process whose significance in human organs remains debated. Both contribute species discordance but require different forensic interpretations.

If we cautiously view the victim’s organs as PS, the key question changes. Instead of asking “Do the species match?“, we ask “What information can the species differences provide for the forensic investigation of the drowning event?” (Table 1). Species discordance no longer looks like a flaw. It may become information. It may point to selective organ uptake. It may reflect changes in the water community over time. It may reveal the influence of dietary background, if present. However, we must define the practical boundaries of this hypothesis. If both concordant and discordant findings can be accommodated within the proposed model, the diagnostic value of species comparison in routine forensic practice could be compromised. We therefore propose that certain patterns of discordance should still raise concern for alternative explanations: (i) when closed organs (e.g., bone marrow, renal parenchyma) contain abundant diatom species completely absent from the drowning medium, suggesting possible postmortem contamination or an alternative drowning site; (ii) when tissue barrier integrity is compromised and the discordant species are consistent with environmental contaminants rather than the putative drowning medium; and (iii) when blank controls reveal contamination that undermines the reliability of the tissue findings [1, 19, 20]. Under these circumstances, species discordance should still prompt consideration of alternative forensic explanations, including an alternative drowning location, significant contamination, or a non-drowning cause of death.

Interpretation of diatom species discordance under the biological passive sampling hypothesis

This framework is derived from the biological passive sampling theory and recent empirical studies in forensic diatomology. Organ-specific sampling rates, tissue barrier integrity, contamination control, and the defined interpretative boundaries are key factors influencing diatom species discordance

This way of thinking also gives us a foundation for better practice. In forensic practice, we recommend reporting the diatom species and count profiles of specific organs (e.g., left upper lung lobe, closed organs such as renal parenchyma) rather than a single overall profile, to reflect organ‑specific selective sampling. Barrier integrity of the tested tissues should be evaluated before diatom analysis, and tissues with obvious postmortem damage should be excluded to avoid contamination interference. Strict blank control measures from environmental monitoring (e.g., blank tissue samples, negative control slides) should be adopted in the entire diatom testing process to eliminate artificial contamination. To make these recommendations more concrete, we propose the following standardized measures: (a) use only new, unused Kjeldahl flasks for strong-acid digestion, as reused flasks can retain diatom frustules and cause false-positive results [20]; (b) implement procedural negative controls for each batch of analyses, including reagent blanks and equipment blanks [14, 21]; (c) use sterile or single-use instruments for tissue sampling to prevent cross-contamination between organs [1, 22]; (d) document tissue barrier integrity at autopsy and exclude samples with obvious postmortem damage; and (e) establish a minimum diagnostic threshold (e.g., > 20 diatoms per sample in closed organs) to distinguish drowning-related findings from low-level background or contamination [14, 23].

We believe this cross‑disciplinary view offers a coherent and hypothetical explanation for the species discordance seen in so many drowning cases. This perspective is consistent with the recent systematic review on the medico-legal interpretation of diatom findings [1] and provides a theoretical foundation for the standardization of diatom testing methods for drowning diagnosis, which is urgently needed in the field [1, 8]. By seeing discordance as a potential expected feature of the sampling process—not as a failure of the method—we hope this perspective supports the critical standardization efforts for diatom testing highlighted recently in this journal [1]. We emphasize, however, that this framework is presented as a testable hypothesis requiring further empirical validation, and that the evidentiary value of diatom testing for drowning diagnosis remains contingent upon rigorous methodological controls and interdisciplinary collaboration with expert diatomologists [1, 8].
