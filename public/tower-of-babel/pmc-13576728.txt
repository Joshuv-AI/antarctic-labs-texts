Identifying independent causal cell types for human diseases and risk variants
Authors: Artem Kim, Zixuan Eleanor Zhang, Come Legros, Hongbo Wang, Zeyun Lu, Adam J. de Smith, Jill E. Moore, Arun Durvasula, Nicholas Mancuso, Steven Gazal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGenome-wide association studies (GWASs) have shown that disease-associated variants are concentrated in candidate regulatory elements (cREs) from disease-relevant cell types. Here, we introduce cell-type fine-mapping (CT-FM) and CT-FM-SNP, probabilistic methods that account for cRE sharing across cell types to infer independent causal cell-type sets for complex traits and candidate causal variants. Applying CT-FM to 63 GWASs using 924 cRE annotations, we inferred 79 independent cell-type sets explaining 39.0% ± 1.8% of trait SNP heritability and identified 14 traits with multiple independent cellular mechanisms, including height, schizophrenia, and autoimmune diseases. Applying CT-FM-SNP to 39 UK Biobank traits, we assigned high-confidence causal cell types to 3,091 candidate non-coding variant-trait pairs. Most variants appeared to act through a single cell-type set, whereas pleiotropic variants often acted through different cell types depending on the phenotype context. Together, CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP provide a framework for dissecting the cellular architecture of complex traits.

Introduction

Understanding how genetic variants act collectively and individually at the cellular level to affect disease risk is critical for interpreting disease biology.1,2,3 Previous studies have integrated cell-type-specific (CTS) functional annotations with genome-wide association studies (GWASs) to identify disease-associated cell types.4,5,6,7,8,9,10 However, most of these associated cell types may only “tag” a truly causal cell type as a consequence of shared regulatory mechanisms across cell types and tissues.11,12,13,14,15 Thus, it remains unclear how many cell types are independently targeted by disease variants for a given trait and whether individual variants act through one or multiple cellular contexts. Examples include the unclear role of BMI-associated variants, which may act through brain, immune, and/or adipose cell types,16,17 or how the FTO locus affects obesity risk through its actions in brain and adipose cell types.18 Altogether, these limitations hinder our understanding of how genetic variants confer disease risk at the cellular level.

Methods accounting for gene co-regulation in expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) datasets have been proposed to infer disease causal tissues14 and risk variants.19 However, current eQTL datasets are mostly bulk, do not capture CTS effects, and often have limited overlap with GWAS results,2,20,21,22 thus limiting insights from these methods. Unlike eQTLs, candidate regulatory elements (cREs) are available for thousands of cell types and conditions23,24,25,26,27 and are extremely enriched in disease SNP heritability (h2) in disease-relevant cell types.4,6,26,27,28 However, methods are still needed to leverage cREs while accounting for their shared structure across cell types.

Here, we propose cell-type fine-mapping (CT-FM) and CT-FM-SNP, methods that leverage CTS cREs to infer independent sets of causal cell types at the genome-wide and single-variant level, respectively. Both methods jointly analyze GWAS summary statistics with a set of CTS SNP annotations, output Bayesian posterior probabilities for each SNP annotation to be causal for a trait (CT-FM) or a particular GWAS candidate variant (CT-FM-SNP), and identify independent causal sets (ICSs) reflecting the number of independent causal cell types. We applied both methods using 924 curated regulatory CTS SNP annotations primarily from ENCODE423,29 and single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) data.27 We first validated and benchmarked CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP using simulations and GWASs of blood traits. We next applied CT-FM to a set of 63 independent GWASs and identified 79 ICSs, highlighting previously unexplored cellular mechanisms in height, schizophrenia (SCZ), and immune-related diseases. Finally, we applied CT-FM-SNP to 6,975 candidate {non-coding SNP, trait} pairs from 39 UK Biobank traits.30,31 Most SNPs appeared to affect phenotypes through a single ICS, with pleiotropic SNPs potentially acting through different, phenotype-specific cell types. Together, CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP infer independent causal cell types and uncover cellular mechanisms of disease risk.

Design

Overview of CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP

CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP are probabilistic frameworks aiming to identify independent sets of causal cell types underlying complex traits and their candidate causal variants, respectively. Both methods jointly analyze GWAS summary statistics and a large set of CTS cRE SNP annotations while explicitly accounting for the shared regulatory architecture across cell types (Figure 1).Figure 1Overview of the CT-FM frameworkCT-FM and CT-FM-SNP integrate cell-type-specific (CTS) candidate regulatory element (cRE) SNP annotations, GWAS summary statistics, LD information, and, for CT-FM-SNP, candidate causal SNPs. Overview of the CT-FM framework

CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP integrate cell-type-specific (CTS) candidate regulatory element (cRE) SNP annotations, GWAS summary statistics, LD information, and, for CT-FM-SNP, candidate causal SNPs. CT-FM estimates marginal annotation enrichments with S-LDSC and then jointly models correlated CTS annotations to assign posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) and infer independent causal sets (ICSs). CT-FM-SNP applies the same framework to annotations overlapping each candidate SNP, assigning likely causal cell types at the variant level. The toy example shows how shared S-LDSC signals are reduced to independent cellular components. The dashed line marks the S-LDSC significance threshold.

CT-FM proceeds in two steps. First, it applies stratified linkage disequilibrium (LD) score regression (S-LDSC)4,32,33 to estimate marginal association (Z scores) of each CTS SNP annotation to per-SNP h2. Second, it applies a probabilistic selection framework derived from the sum of single effects (SuSiE) model34,35 to these SNP-annotation statistics using an adjusted LD-score correlation matrix (R) across annotations. This step assigns posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) for each annotation and identifies ICSs—groups of correlated annotations (i.e., cell types with high cRE sharing) consistent with one underlying causal cell-type component. We defined a candidate causal cell type as an SNP annotation inferred in a CT-FM ICS and a highly confident causal cell type as an SNP annotation presenting a PIP ≥ 0.5. Additional details on combined PIPs (cPIPs) for related annotations and on how CT-FM leverages S-LDSC outputs and adapts the SuSiE-RSS framework, while differing from both, are provided in STAR Methods, Table S1, and discussion.

CT-FM-SNP extends this framework to individual variants by restricting inference to CTS annotations overlapping a candidate causal SNP. Like CT-FM, it outputs variant-level ICSs and PIPs for individual non-coding variants and nominates candidate (ICS) and high-confidence (PIP ≥ 0.5) cell types while accounting for shared cRE structure. An important distinction with CT-FM is the interpretation of multiple ICSs: unlike in CT-FM, multiple ICSs in CT-FM-SNP indicate that a variant overlaps regulatory elements from multiple causal cell types but do not allow concluding whether the candidate SNP affects disease risk by disrupting gene regulation within a single or all those cell types.

We applied CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP with a total of 924 CTS SNP annotations (capturing, on average, 1.1% of common SNPs) derived from cREs of human cell types and human-derived cell lines from ENCODE4, CATlas single-cell ATAC-seq data,27 and the ABC method25 (see Figure 1, Table S2, and STAR Methods), classified into 9 biological groups for visualization (Figure S1). CT-FM successfully detected at least one ICS in 63 independent and well-powered GWASs (average n = 417 K; see STAR Methods and Table S3). CT-FM-SNP was applied to 6,975 candidate {non-coding variant, trait} pairs from 39 UK Biobank traits.30,31 We further predicted causal {non-coding SNP, cell type, gene, trait} quadruplets by combining CT-FM-SNP with SNP-gene links from our cS2G strategy.36

Further details are provided in STAR Methods. We have released open-source software implementing our framework (see data and code availability), and all CTS SNP annotations and GWAS summary statistics analyzed are publicly available (see data and code availability).

Results

Validation of CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP using simulations

We performed extensive simulations to assess CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP power to identify causal cell types. Specifically, we simulated 500 GWAS summary statistics using LD patterns from the UK Biobank and effect sizes depending on per-SNP h2 estimated by S-LDSC on a height GWAS,37 with n = 350 K and h2 = 0.5 to match the S-LDSC signal distribution observed in analyzed GWASs (Figure S2). We simulated scenarios with one to three causal SNP annotations, including osteoblasts and/or fibroblasts, which were identified by CT-FM as causal for height (see novel findings from traits with independent sets of causal cell types). We evaluated the performance of CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP using precision, sensitivity, F1 score, and type I error and compared with S-LDSC false discovery rate (FDR)-based approaches using comparable regulatory inputs (see STAR Methods).

We first evaluated CT-FM power to identify causal cell types of a trait in simulations with 1–3 causal annotations (Figure 2; Table S4). We evaluated four approaches: CT-FM-candidate (annotations in CT-FM ICSs), CT-FM-high-confidence (PIP > 0.5), S-LDSC-candidate (FDR p < 0.05), and S-LDSC-topFDR (lowest FDR p); additional S-LDSC-based approaches were evaluated in Figure S3. Overall, the sensitivity of all methods decreased with increasing number of causal cell types, but precision remained stable. CT-FM-high-confidence maximized precision (≥0.79), matched S-LDSC-topFDR in simulations with one causal cell type (F1 = 0.85 vs. 0.87), and achieved the highest F1 scores with multiple causal cell types (≥0.53), supporting its use for causal cell type inference. CT-FM-candidate provided lower sensitivities than S-LDSC-candidate but higher F1 scores, making it the strategy of choice to nominate candidate cell types while maximizing sensitivity. Additionally, CT-FM assigned high PIPs to causal annotations (mean: 0.77, 0.54, and 0.42 for 1, 2, and 3 causal annotations scenarios, respectively) and detected multiple ICSs in 33% and 71% of simulations with 2 and 3 causal annotations, respectively. In simulations with diverse single causal SNP annotations, precision and sensitivity decreased when the causal SNP annotation had a high R value with other analyzed SNP annotations (Figures S4 and S5); in simulations in which CT-FM did not provide the highest PIP value for the causal SNP annotation, the inferred causal annotation was still highly correlated with the causal one (mean R ≥ 0.48, indicating that when CT-FM fails to identify the causal cell type, it still pinpoints a highly correlated cell type (Figure S6). We further validated CT-FM by demonstrating that it produces well-calibrated PIP values (Figure S7) and that a PIP threshold ≥ 0.5 consistently maximizes the F1 score across diverse simulation scenarios (Figure S8). Additional simulations varying N, h2, causal-annotation noise, and h2 explained by causal annotations supported the same conclusions and showed that CT-FM performance increased with GWAS power (Figures S9–S12).Figure 2Simulations to assess precision and sensitivity of methods inferring causal cell typesWe report the precision, sensitivity, and F1 score in simulations with different numbers of causal SNP annotations. We applied CT-FM to the 5 traits using GWAS summary statistics from the UK Biobank37 (average n = 444,000) and identified one ICS per trait (4.4 CTS-SNP annotations per ICS, on average), each highlighting expected causal cell types, such as CD8+ T cells for lymphocyte count (PIP = 0.59) and monocytes for monocyte count (PIP = 0.72) (Figure 4A; Table S6). CT-FM greatly reduced the number of candidate causal cell types as compared with the initial association signal inferred by S-LDSC (Figure 4B; Table S7). We performed additional CT-FM benchmarking analyses investigating the number of ICSs (Figure S17), the number of SuSiE-RSS parameters (Table S6), and the use of SuSiE-inf40 (Table S8).

We next applied CT-FM-SNP on 1,564 candidate causal {non-coding variant, trait} pairs of the 5 blood traits (obtained by SNP fine-mapping in the UK Biobank30,31 and by selecting SNPs with an SNP-PIP > 0.5). We identified at least one ICS for nearly half of the candidate SNPs (48%, 758 of 1,564; 829 ICSs in total) and detected a high-confidence causal cell type for 90% of these ICSs (748 ICSs with PIP > 0.5 among the 829 ICSs), highlighting a high success rate of CT-FM-SNP (Figure 4C; Table S9). Most high-confidence assignments (76%, 571/748) corresponded to candidate cell types previously identified by CT-FM, thus demonstrating high consistency of causal cell types inferred by CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP (Figure 4D; Table S10). For platelet count, CT-FM-SNP also recovered megakaryocyte signals not identified by CT-FM, consistent with our simulations showing that variant-level inference can recover missed trait-level components (Figure S17). Similar conclusions for the red blood cell (RBC) volume trait are discussed in Table S11. Similar patterns were observed when restricting analyses to SNPs with Polyfun SNP-PIP > 0.95 (282 SNPs in total), which demonstrates that our conclusions are robust to the imperfect selection of candidate causal SNPs (Figure S18). These results were also replicated using the default SuSiE-RSS fine-mapped SNPs with PIP thresholds (≥0.5 vs. ≥0.95) (Figure S19).

We functionally validated the pairs identified by CT-FM-SNP for lymphocyte and monocyte count candidate SNPs using single-cell eQTLs from OneK1K41,42 (Figures 4E and 4F; Table S12). Lymphocyte count candidate SNPs assigned to lymphocyte cell types by CT-FM-SNP exhibited a higher proportion of lymphocyte eQTLs compared to both a set of control SNPs (see STAR Methods) and candidate SNPs not assigned to lymphocyte cell types (Figure 4E). Similar conclusions were observed for monocyte count candidate SNPs and monocyte eQTLs (Figure 4F) and when using colocalization43 and single-cell chromatin accessibility QTL44 analyses (see Methods S1, Figures S20–S22, and Tables S13 and S14).

Finally, we compared CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP with expression-based methods,5,8,14,19,45 and observed improved cell-type resolution and inference of independent cellular mechanisms (see Methods S1 and Tables S15, S16, and S17).

Altogether, these results demonstrate that CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP recover expected cellular mechanisms and provide coherent trait-level and variant-level inference.

CT-FM identified high-confidence causal cell types consistent with known disease biology (Figure 5C). For psychiatric diseases, CT-FM identified excitatory neurons for SCZ46,47 (PIP = 0.98) and bipolar disorder48 (PIP = 0.99); excitatory neurons for insomnia49 (cPIP = 0.47), depression50 (PIP = 0.45), and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder51 (PIP = 0.58); and inhibitory neurons for anorexia52 (PIP = 0.64). It also inferred B cells and/or T cells for multiple immune-related diseases, cardiomyocytes for atrial fibrillation53 (PIP = 0.99), pancreatic cells for type 2 diabetes54 (PIP = 0.51), and prostate epithelial cells for prostate cancer55 (PIP = 0.99).

We constructed SNP annotations corresponding to each ICS (covering, on average, 2.7% of common SNPs) and estimated their contribution to genome-wide h2 using S-LDSC. We found that these ICSs capture a substantial fraction of h2 (34.5% ± 1.5% per ICS SNP annotation and 39.0% ± 1.8% for their union; Figure 5D; Tables S22 and S23). Despite these high enrichments, CT-FM identified a relatively small number of ICSs per trait (1.25; 49 of 63 traits with a single ICS), which suggests missing causal cellular mechanisms (consistent with simulations). We therefore assessed whether CT-FM ICSs captured most of the h2 explained by cREs. For each trait, we first estimated the h2 explained by the largest available cRE datasets (h2-cREs; 38.9% of common SNPs explaining 60.6% ± 1.5% of h2 across 63 traits; see Methods S1), then quantified the proportion captured by CT-FM ICSs. Overall, CT-FM ICSs explained ∼two-thirds of h2-cREs (62.5% ± 2.8% across 63 traits), indicating that CT-FM captures most cRE-driven heritability using CTS SNP annotations (Figure 5E; Table S23).

Interestingly, nearly all the h2-cREs for immune- and blood-related traits were explained by ICS SNP annotations (84.3% ± 6.7% when meta-analyzed across 16 traits), whereas lower proportions were explained for brain-related traits (45.5% ± 3.5% when meta-analyzed across 17 traits). We hypothesized that this result reflects a combination of incomplete CTS SNP-annotation coverage and differences in functional genetic architecture. Indeed, immune/blood cell types are available for a wide range of conditions (e.g., different developmental stages and in vitro stimulations; 552 SNP annotations analyzed here), whereas brain cell types are often collected in prenatal or postmortem tissues, which may not fully capture disease-relevant regulatory states. Consistent with this hypothesis, we observed that all cREs explain a smaller fraction of h2 for brain-related traits (53.0% ± 1.9% across 17 traits) than for immune/blood-related traits (69.4% ± 2.9% across 16 traits; Table S22). Similarly, gene-based annotations also captured a smaller fraction of h2 for brain-related traits (Figure S24), indicating that genetic effects for these traits may be more diffusely distributed across the genome, consistent with greater biological complexity or higher confounding in GWAS results of brain-related traits.56 In contrast, we observed that several alternative explanations appear unlikely: brain and immune/blood annotations are comparable in genomic coverage (32.8 vs. 29.1 Mb) and in the number of unique cell types considered (30 vs. 31) (Figure S25), brain SNP annotations do not show increased internal correlation that would suggest reduced separability among neuronal subclasses (Figure S26), and brain-related traits exhibit higher GWAS power on average as measured by h2 Z scores (19.68 vs. 14.27; Table S2), arguing against limited statistical power or increased heterogeneity as primary drivers of the observed difference.

Additional benchmarking analyses, including calibration of the annotation correlation matrix, validation of ICSs, cross-dataset replication, transcription factor enrichment, and cross-ancestry robustness analyses, are described in the Methods S1, Figures S27–S35, and Tables S24, S25, S26, S27, and S28.

Overall, CT-FM refined candidate causal cell types, identified highly confident causal cell types corresponding to known cellular mechanisms, and identified ICSs with corresponding SNP annotations explaining a large fraction of h2 captured by known cREs.

Novel findings from traits with independent sets of causal cell types

A key feature of CT-FM compared to existing methods is to infer independent sets of causal cell types. Here, we highlight novel findings from traits for which CT-FM identified multiple ICSs (see Figure 5C and Table S18).

CT-FM identified 2 ICSs for height57 and BMI,58 2 of the most-studied complex traits. For height, CT-FM identified osteoblasts (PIP = 0.99) and fibroblasts (PIP = 0.89), both replicated in FinnGen height GWASs and a (less powered) East Asian height GWAS (Table S28; Figure S34). Corresponding SNP annotations remained significant after conditioning on additional candidate fetal chondrocyte SNP annotations,59 which suggests genetic mechanisms related to bone growth and connective tissues complementing those reported for embryonic cartilage cells (Table S29). For BMI, CT-FM identified radial glial cells (PIP = 0.96) and an additional neuronal ICS including bipolar (PIP = 0.50), motor (PIP = 0.31), and fetal inhibitory (PIP = 0.10) neurons. These results suggest that BMI-associated variants are distributed across multiple neuronal cell types.60 Because these two brain-specific ICSs explain a moderate fraction of h2-cREs (44.6% ± 0.5%), we further investigated whether additional cell types (such as immune and adipose cell types, previously suggested as target cell types for BMI and obesity16,17) might contribute independently. However, no non-brain-related CTS SNP annotations were identified in an ICS (Table S18). Moreover, after conditioning on the two neuronal SNP annotations in S-LDSC analyses, no additional CTS SNP annotations remained significant (FDR p < 0.05; Figure S36). Together, these results indicate that, among the available CTS SNP annotations analyzed in this study, the BMI h2 signal is primarily captured by brain-related regulatory elements, although additional regulatory mechanisms may remain to be characterized.

For SCZ,61 CT-FM detected 3 ICSs (highest number in this study), including fetal and adult excitatory neurons (PIP = 0.99 and 0.98, respectively); these 2 cell types were also inferred as candidates when analyzing a (less powered) East Asian SCZ GWAS (Table S28). Although the role of excitatory neurons in SCZ has been highlighted using gene expression datasets,5,6 CT-FM results suggest that different gene regulation mechanisms in excitatory neurons affect SCZ risk at early- and late-life stages (as suggested in Schipper et al.62; we note that these results may also reflect the difficulty of characterizing brain gene regulation, highlighting the need for both fetal and postmortem adult brain samples63). Finally, unlike in previous CTS analyses of SCZ GWASs,4,5,6,61 CT-FM could highlight the role of CD4+ T cells (PIP = 0.57), which is consistent with SCZ pathogenic mechanisms being shared with immune disorders.64,65,66,67,68 SNP annotations unique to each ICS remained highly enriched in SCZ h2 (10.0× ± 2.7×, 12.2× ± 1.8×, and 4.5× ± 1.1×, respectively) (Table S30), suggesting different cellular causal mechanisms within fetal and postmortem excitatory neurons and CD4+ T cells. Notably, although SCZ and bipolar disorder have a high genetic correlation69 (rg = 0.71 ± 0.02) and both presented an adult excitatory neuron ICS, fetal excitatory neuron and CD4+ T cell ICSs were detected only for SCZ, suggesting partly distinct biological mechanisms.

Within autoimmune diseases, CT-FM identified 2 ICSs for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)70: monocyte-derived cells and T cells. Interestingly, when performing subtype analyses of Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC),71 CT-FM identified one of these ICSs for each subtype: monocyte-derived cell types for CD (PIP = 0.98 for macrophages) and T cells for UC (cPIP = 0.59 and 0.10 for CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, respectively) (Figure 5C). Our findings are consistent with literature implicating monocytes/macrophages and T cell subsets differentially across IBD subtypes.71,72,73,74,75,76 For rheumatoid arthritis, previous works have mainly characterized h2 enrichment within CD4+ T cells,77,78,79 but CT-FM also detected B cells as a causal cell type.80 CT-FM results for an alternative rheumatoid arthritis GWAS dataset78 are discussed in Figure S37. Finally, CT-FM identified 2 ICSs for multiple sclerosis, CD4+ T cells (PIP = 0.90) and macrophages (PIP = 0.99), whereas previous approaches highlighted enrichments in various immune cell types but were unable to distinguish between them.5,6 In contrast, existing expression-based approaches did not resolve these independent cellular components5,8,14,45 (Table S16).

Altogether, the CT-FM ability to identify independent causal cell types provided new insights into the genetic architecture of height, SCZ, and multiple autoimmune diseases. CT-FM identified 2 ICSs for IBD, each corresponding to the ICS identified by CT-FM for UC and CD, which is consistent with a model of biologically heterogeneous diseases and different disease subtypes affected by genetic risk variants via different cell types.

Impact of disease variants on multiple cell types

Although we identified complex traits with multiple ICSs, whether individual variants can contribute to disease risk by acting on different cell types independently is unknown. A related unanswered question is whether pleiotropic variants89 influence multiple traits by acting on the same cell type or on different cell types. Here, we leveraged CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP results to tackle these questions.

To test whether the 14 traits with multiple CT-FM ICSs tend to have causal variants acting jointly on multiple independent cell types, we first investigated whether SNPs overlapping multiple ICS annotations explained more h2 than expected by adding an interaction SNP-annotation to S-LDSC models (see STAR Methods). The 14 interaction SNP-annotations captured on average 1.2% of common SNPs and had high expected h2 enrichment (17.3 ± 1.7×) but no significant conditional effect (p = 0.22 for the conditional effect of the interaction annotation; Table S37), suggesting that even if SNPs falling within cREs of 2 ICSs have a higher probability of being causal, these SNPs will likely affect disease risk through one of these ICSs rather than both.

Next, we leveraged CT-FM-SNP results to tackle this question. We focused on 6 UK Biobank traits for which CT-FM identified 2 ICSs and their corresponding candidate SNPs for which CT-FM-SNP ICSs were consistent with those of CT-FM (599 SNPs across the 6 traits; Figure S40). First, we validated that CT-FM-SNP does not overestimate the number of SNPs assigned to at least 2 CT-FM ICSs by comparing its results with h2 estimates from S-LDSC. The fraction assigned by CT-FM-SNP to both ICSs (42% ± 6%) was consistent with S-LDSC h2 estimates for SNPs in the intersection of the two ICSs (38% ± 8%; Table S38). Second, the number of SNPs assigned to both CT-FM ICSs (274) was smaller than what was expected by chance under a model in which SNPs in both CT-FM ICSs affect disease risk via a single cell type (380; see Table S39), supporting mostly single-ICS effects at individual loci, which is consistent with S-LDSC and CT-FM findings above. For example, we identified two candidate SNPs for height GWASs that were inferred as causal for both osteoblast and fibroblast cell types and were mapped to functionally relevant genes via cS2G (Figures S41 and S42).

Finally, we investigated whether a pleiotropic SNP tends to target the same or different cell types across independent traits. We analyzed 72 candidate causal SNPs for which CT-FM-SNP identified a high-confidence candidate causal cell type in at least 2 genetically uncorrelated UK Biobank traits (Table S40). Approximately half of these pleiotropic SNPs (33/72; 46%) shared at least 1 candidate cell type across different traits (Figure 6D). Similar conclusions were obtained under different fine-mapping methods (PolyFun and SuSiE) and under PIP thresholds (≥0.5 and ≥0.95) (Figure S43). Shared {non-coding SNP, cell type} pairs across traits included SNPs acting on hematopoietic multipotent progenitors (HMPs) in blood/immune traits and SNPs acting on osteoblasts and BMSCs in height and bone density traits (Figure 6E). Strikingly, more than half of the pleiotropic SNPs (39/72; 54%) were assigned to different cell types by CT-FM-SNP (Figure 6F). For example, the pleiotropic SNP rs1887428 was assigned to HMP and erythroblast cell types for mean corpuscular hemoglobin but to megakaryocytes for mean platelet volume; this result is consistent with the known role of its candidate target gene JAK2 in regulating both erythroid progenitors90 and megakaryocyte morphology.91 rs3918226 was assigned to fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and CD4+ T cells for height, diastolic blood pressure, and eczema, respectively; this result is consistent with the known role of its candidate target gene NOS3,36 which is produced in endothelial cells and plays a key role in regulating blood pressure92,93 but was also shown to regulate CD4+ T cells94,95 and fibroblasts.96 Finally, rs75475627 was assigned to osteoblasts and BMSCs for the bone density trait and to macrophages for the monocyte count trait; this result is consistent with the known role of its candidate target gene SPTBN1, which is involved in osteoblast function97,98 but was also shown to regulate monocyte differentiation.99

Overall, CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP results suggest that most individual SNPs affect a phenotype via a single set of cell types and that pleiotropic SNPs might target independent cell types depending on the phenotype context.

Discussion

We developed CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP, 2 methods that leverage CTS cRE annotations to identify independent causal cell types for traits and trait-associated SNPs, respectively. By applying CT-FM to 63 GWASs, we identified 79 ICSs with corresponding SNP annotations explaining a high fraction of trait h2, identified 14 traits with independent causal cell types, and highlighted cellular mechanisms consistent with known and previously unexplored biology. We applied CT-FM-SNP to 39 UK Biobank traits and predicted high-confidence causal cell types for 3,091 candidate causal non-coding SNP-trait pairs. Finally, CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP results suggest that most individual SNPs affect a phenotype via a single set of cell types and that pleiotropic SNPs might target different cell types depending on the phenotype context.

As compared with existing methods, CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP propose several conceptual advances. First, they prioritize causal cell types by leveraging cREs rather than gene expression QTL datasets,14,19 thus prioritizing cell types over tissues and capturing more disease h2. Indeed, the h2 explained by the cREs identified by CT-FM is nearly 8 times higher than the h2 explained by fine-mapped eQTLs from all GTEx tissues21,100 (39.0% ± 1.8% vs. 5.0% ± 0.3% across 63 traits). Second, our probabilistic framework allows for disentangling shared cREs across cell types, allowing us to identify independent sets of causal cell types while greatly reducing the number of resulting candidate SNP annotations (∼20× decrease compared to S-LDSC FDR-significant results). Finally, CT-FM-SNP is (to our knowledge) the first method prioritizing the causal cell type of individual variants by accounting for both polygenic enrichment and cRE sharing.

Our findings have several implications for downstream analyses. First, they highlight the benefits of leveraging cREs to infer causal cell types of diseases and risk variants. Their advantages include a high fraction of h2 explained and cost-effective experiments targeting multiple cell types and conditions. A possible future area of research could involve developing cistrome-wide association studies101 across multiple cell types using population-scale single-cell ATAC-seq data and leveraging a fine-mapping procedure (similar to Strober et al.19 and Zhao et al.102) to functionally link GWAS variants to an ATAC peak and its target cell types. Second, our framework illustrates the complementary roles of trait-level and variant-level inference, which provide complementary views of trait architecture. CT-FM identifies independent, polygenically supported cell-type components underlying genome-wide h2, whereas CT-FM-SNP refines inference at individual loci by assigning likely cellular context to candidate variants. Based on these observations, we provide the following guidance: CT-FM is most informative for identifying dominant, polygenically supported cell-type components at the trait level, whereas CT-FM-SNP resolves locus-specific cell-type effects and may recover secondary signals that do not reach genome-wide detectability. Concordance between the two approaches strengthens confidence in a cell-type assignment, while discrepancies may reflect locus-specific effects or limited polygenic power. Third, our application of CT-FM on 63 GWASs revealed previously unexplored cellular mechanisms and provides direction for future research. For example, although the role of chondrocytes in height has been extensively investigated,59 CT-FM highlighted an additional role of osteoblasts and fibroblasts in height. Fourth, it highlighted differences in the h2 explained by CT-FM ICSs in immune/blood- and brain-related traits, which likely reflects a combination of incomplete CTS SNP-annotation coverage and differences in functional genetic architecture. Fifth, our results on IBD and its subtypes are consistent with a model of biologically heterogeneous diseases and different disease subtypes affected by genetic risk variants via different cell types. This kind of cell-type decomposition may have practical value: identifying independent causal cell types for a trait could enable construction of CTS polygenic risk scores to stratify patients into biologically distinct profiles, as recently demonstrated for type 2 diabetes.103 However, we underline that our results likely reflect the relative contributions of different cell types rather than disease-subtype-exclusive mechanisms, and the underlying biology likely involves complex interactions between multiple causal cell types. Finally, CT-FM-SNP provides insights into functions of disease candidate variants and may also be helpful in choosing the relevant cell type for in vitro experiments.

Overall, our results demonstrate the advantage of CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP to infer independent causal cell types from GWASs, providing novel insights into the complex cell biology underlying the genetic architecture of human diseases and complex traits.

Limitations of the study

We note several limitations of our work related to the power of CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP to infer causation. First, CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP specifically assume that all disease-relevant cell types have corresponding SNP annotations, but they might not have been assayed in practice. Generating cRE catalogs of diverse cell types and conditions should fill the gap between the total trait h2 and the h2 explained by SNP annotations from putative causal cell types. Nevertheless, CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP can still indicate the “best proxy” for the truly causal cell type, thus narrowing the experimental space for follow-up regulatory profiling. Second, as highlighted in simulations, the power to detect disease causal cell types depends on the GWAS sample size and trait h2, whose product needs to be large. Although CT-FM detected at least one ICS across 63 GWASs and multiple ICSs for 14 traits, it may still miss causal cell types with weaker heritability contributions, effects concentrated in few loci, or highly correlated annotations merged into a single ICS. Nevertheless, CT-FM ICSs explained ∼two-thirds of h2-cREs, suggesting that the framework captures the dominant regulatory components among available CTS SNP annotations. Importantly, CT-FM-SNP complements this trait-level inference by resolving locus-specific cell-type effects and recovering secondary cell-type signals that may not reach genome-wide detectability. Third, another limitation related to sample size is that we were unable to comprehensively analyze GWAS of non-European ancestries, which generally have substantially smaller sample sizes than European ancestry GWASs.104 Although we extended CT-FM to 31 East Asian GWASs,105 only five traits were sufficiently well powered for analysis. Nevertheless, CT-FM revealed broadly similar CTS genetic architectures across these two ancestries.78 At the variant level, we additionally applied CT-FM-SNP to candidate SNPs from an East Asian height GWAS (the only trait with enough well-powered fine-mapped variants to enable cross-ancestry comparison) and observed consistent cell-type assignments across ancestries. However, more comprehensive assessments of shared vs. ancestry-specific candidate variants across traits will require well-powered fine-mapping results and high-resolution regulatory annotations across diverse ancestries. Fourth, CT-FM power is also reduced when causal cell types have high cRE sharing. For example, in many immune-related diseases, we identified ICSs related to T cells and containing both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell SNP annotations but were unable to dissociate cases where only one or both were causal. Improved cRE definitions, such as dynamic regulatory elements defining precise cell types or cell states,79 should improve the CT-FM ability to identify precise causal cell types or cell states. Finally, the interpretability of CT-FM-SNP results depends on the resolution of upstream SNP fine-mapping and SNP-to-gene linking strategies, such as PolyFun and cS2G, whose statistical power remains limited in certain traits and loci.30,36 While these approaches provide the necessary variant- and gene-level inputs, CT-FM-SNP adds an additional layer of inference by assigning cell-type context through joint modeling of correlated regulatory annotations. Thus, CT-FM-SNP complements existing frameworks by extending variant- and gene-level prioritization to the cellular level.

Method details

CT-FM method

CT-FM is a method that identifies independent sets of CTS SNP-annotations that are the most likely to explain the h2 observed across a large set of CTS SNP-annotations. It takes as inputs GWAS summary statistics with a matching LD reference panel, and a set of CTS SNP-annotations (Figure 1). Below we describe its model and parameters estimation.

We assume the infinitesimal linear model Y = Xβ+ε, where Y is a vector of quantitative phenotypes, X is a matrix of standardized genotypes, β is the vector of per-normalized-genotype effect size, and ε is a mean 0 vector of residuals. We model the variance of the causal effect size βj of each variant j as an extension of S-LDSC baseline model.4,6 Specifically, we modelVar(βj)=Σb∈Bab(j)τb+Σc∈Cac(j)τc,where B represents a set of background SNP-annotations from the baseline model (i.e., coding, promoter, enhancer, …), ab(j) is the continuous value of SNP-annotation b for variant j, τbis the contribution of b to Var(βj), and C represents the whole set of CTS SNP-annotations. Rather than assuming that all C SNP-annotations contribute to Var(βj), we assume that at most L SNP-annotations contribute positively to Var(βj) (i.e., τc > 0). Specifically, we model the vector τ of τc∈C as τ=∑l=1Lτl as the sum of L single-effects τl = γl·bl. Here, γl∈{0,1}|C| is a one-hot vector indicating which CTS annotation is active for latent effect l and bl is its magnitude. This yields a sparse, single-effect factorization of CTS variance components: each latent effect selects one annotation, and the overall CTS contribution is the sum of up to L such effects. For each single effect l, we assume γl∼Multinomial(1;π) with πc = 1/|C|, corresponding to a uniform prior over CTS annotations. Following the SuSiE framework, the corresponding effect size is modeled as bl∼N(0,σl2), where σl2 represents the variance of the contribution of the single effect l to h2.

In practice, we obtain marginal effects τ˜c by applying S-LDSC on GWAS χ2 summary statistics from the following modelE[χj2]=NΣb∈Bτbl(j,b)+Nτ˜cl(j,c)+Nd+1where N is the GWAS sample size, d is a term measuring the contribution of confounding biases111 and l(j,b)=Σkab(k)rjk2 is the LD score of SNP j with respect to the value of SNP-annotation ab and rjk2 is the correlation between SNPs j and k. We can then model the vector τ˜ of τ˜c∈C as τ˜∼N(Rτ,R), where τ is the sum of single effects from above, and R the C×C adjusted-matrix of cRE LD scores defined as the residual correlation matrix of LD scores after residualizing out background annotations. Specifically, let AB and AC denote the SNP x B and SNP x C SNP-annotation matrices of the LD scores of the baseline model SNP-annotations and CTS SNP-annotations, respectively. The residualization can equivalently be derived from the joint least-squares projection onto the combined annotation space [AB,AC], yielding the normal equations(AB⊤ABAB⊤ACAC⊤ABAC⊤AC)−1[AB⊤AC⊤]from which the residualized CTS component corresponds to the part orthogonal to the column space of AB. In particular, the block structure of this system implies that the CTS contribution can be written as a projection onto the orthogonal complement of col(AB), leading directly to A˜C=(I−PB)AC, where PB=AB(AB⊤AB)−1AB⊤. We thus project CTS LD scores onto the orthogonal complement of the background space and define R=corr(AC)˜ orR=AC⊤AC−AC⊤AB(AB⊤AB)−1AB⊤AC.

This construction ensures that R captures CTS-specific regulatory sharing rather than broad sharing from the baseline annotations. Because R is estimated from finite LD reference panels, it is subject to sampling variability. In practice, LD scores are computed using ancestry-matched reference panels (e.g., 1000 Genomes Europeans) using an approximately unbiased estimator of LD scores.111 We assessed calibration of R by comparing expected and observed Z-scores, observing strong agreement (mean r2 = 0.95; see results), indicating that the estimated R provides a stable and well-calibrated summary of CTS correlation structure. Robustness to LD mismatch is further supported by cross-panel analyses (see results).

After estimating the marginal effects τ˜c and their corresponding Z-scores using the S-LDSC framework,4,32,33 and computing the R matrix, we can estimate the number of single effects l∈L and corresponding γl and bl values from the SuSiE-RSS function from the SuSiE-R package.35 In practice SuSiE-RSS will estimate the PIP of each SNP-annotation c by modeling γl as Multi(1,αl), where αl is the Bayesian posterior probability of each CTS SNP-annotation to explain a single lth effect, and by defining PIPc asPIPc=P(τc≠0|τ˜,AC)=1−∏l=1L(1−αl,c).

Uncertainty in L is naturally propagated through the SuSiE formulation via this definition of PIPs, which aggregate evidence across all latent components: annotations supported consistently across multiple components retain high PIPs, whereas those supported only by weak or isolated components are downweighted. We set SuSiE parameters L to 10 (default) and n to 1,190,321 matching the number of SNPs used by S-LDSC. In CT-FM, L represents an upper bound on the number of independent CTS signals and setting L = 10 in our analyses allows sufficient model flexibility. Empirically, across 63 GWAS traits, we observed at most three ICSs per trait, with each ICS explaining on average 34.5% of the cRE-mediated h2 (see results), indicating that a small number of components captures the majority of regulatory signals. Consistent with this, increasing L did not materially affect posterior inclusion probabilities or inferred ICSs. Benchmarking analyses (Table S6) further confirmed stability across different values of L, supporting our choice of a conservative upper bound.

We next compute η-ICSs for each l, where η represents the desired probability that the set contains causal CTS SNP-annotations (0.95 in this study). To infer an ICS for each l, we decreasingly sort αl and take a greedy approach to include CTS SNP-annotations until their cumulative sum exceeds η.

In preliminary simulation analyses, we determined that CT-FM inference procedure was well-powered when at least one out of the CTS SNP-annotations has an S-LDSC marginal τ˜ Z score >4 (Figure S44); we thus decided to restrict all CT-FM (and CT-FM-SNP) analyzes to summary statistics with at least one CTS SNP-annotation validating this condition (referred in this manuscript as well-powered GWAS). CT-FM then applies several steps to refine the ICS inferences. First, to remove any potential false positive ICSs, it removes ICSs in which no CTS SNP-annotations present a high S-LDSC association signal (S-LDSC Z score >4). Second, we introduce several modifications to the default SuSiE-RSS approach to detect potentially missed ICSs (i.e., false negatives). By default, SuSiE applies the “purity” approach which removes ICSs whose minimum pairwise correlation R between its components is less than a given threshold (0.5 by default). However, this pruning method may falsely remove ICSs containing two causal signals exhibiting equal effect size but presenting low pairwise correlation R. To remedy this, we heuristically introduce a new criterion “divergence” measuring for each l, the Kullback-Leibler (KL)-divergence between the distribution of αl values and the null distribution. We keep the ICSs whose either purity is greater than 0.3 (threshold corresponding to the average absolute pairwise correlation between two CTS SNP-annotations in our dataset) or the KL divergence is greater than 3. The KL divergence of an ICS significantly correlated with the maximum S-LDSC Z score observed in an ICS (r2 = 0.82, Figure S45), and the threshold of 3 was empirically defined as it optimizes the selection of well-powered ICSs (defined as ICSs containing at least one CTS SNP-annotation with an S-LDSC Z score >4). Third, in very rare cases, SuSiE-RSS can output two ICSs both containing a “duplicate” CTS SNP-annotation (i.e., present in both ICS1 and ICS2). To address these cases, we remove the ICSs containing such duplicates if the sum of their PIP values exceeds 0.1. In practice, only 3 detected ICSs were removed due to duplicate CTS SNP-annotations. These ICSs corresponded to systolic blood pressure (UK Biobank GWAS), forced vital capacity and the second GWAS dataset of Rheumatoid arthritis (see also caption Figure S37). Finally, for ICSs where no highly-confident (PIP >0.5) causal cell type was detected but multiple CTS SNP-annotations correspond to the same cell type, we calculate a combined PIP (cPIP) defined as:cPIPl,ct=1−∏cts∈l(1−αl,cts)where ct,l is the cell type in a credible set l, and αl,cts are the α values of CTS SNP-annotations present in the credible set l and corresponding to the cell type ct (7 cases of cPIP >0.5 calculated across 63 GWASs, Table S19).

Finally, we note that while CT-FM applies the SuSiE SNP-fine-mapping model to annotation space and models CTS annotation-level marginal effects, they differ in several key ways. First, we caution that CT-FM specifically assumes that all disease-relevant cell types have corresponding SNP-annotations among C, while they might not have been assayed in practice. Second, a credible set in SNP-fine-mapping is interpreted as a set of SNPs that likely contains a causal variant, as in practice it is assumed unlikely that multiple SNPs in high LD are both causal. However, while mathematically identical, an ICS should be interpreted as a set of cell types that contains SNP-annotations related to one or multiple causal cell types. For example, an ICS containing SNP-annotations corresponding to two distinct cell types would suggest that either a) only one of these cell types is causal, b) both are causal, but their SNP-annotations are too highly correlated to be in distinct independent ICSs, or c) a third cell type that has not been assayed as causal. In addition, inferring a single cell type as causal assumes to group together several of its cell states, which does not correspond to complex disease biology.

CT-FM-SNP method

While CT-FM was designed to infer causal cell types underlying the overall GWAS signal, CT-FM-SNP is a method that identifies causal cell types targeted by a particular causal SNP. It takes as inputs GWAS summary statistics with a matching LD reference panel, a set of CTS SNP-annotations, and a list of candidate causal SNPs of the trait, and outputs ICSs and PIPs for those SNPs (Figure 1). Briefly, for each analyzed SNP, we restrict CT-FM model to the subset of cRE annotations overlapping the SNP and perform the same inference and filtering of ICSs as described above. We additionally retain the results of SNPs that present a high-confidence causal signal (PIP >0.5) for a well-powered CTS SNP-annotation (S-LDSC Z score >4) even if they were not identified in an ICS (54 such SNPs in total among 3,091 analyzed, Table S41). We note that while the inference of multiple ICSs by CT-FM suggests that multiple cell types are causal for the disease (as it relies on polygenicity), the inference of multiple ICSs by CT-FM-SNP suggests that the candidate SNP overlap cREs from multiple causal cell types, but does not infer whether the candidate SNP impacts disease risk by disrupting gene regulation within a single or multiple cell types.

Selection of 924 CTS annotations for CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP

Our dataset of 924 CTS SNP-annotations (1.1% of common SNPs on average) was derived from three catalogs of cREs: ENCODE4, CATlas and ABC (average genome coverage = 32.5 Mb). For ENCODE4, we manually curated the dataset of 1,680 cell-type-specific cREs by primarily excluding cREs corresponding to tissues and cancer cell lines. For each resulting cRE file, we excluded marks corresponding to “low DNase'' and “Unclassified” state. ENCODE4 data was converted from hg38 to hg19 using the liftOver tool from UCSC.112 For ABC, we retrieved a dataset of gene-enhancer predictions for 131 tissues/cell types and excluded all tissue-level annotations. CATlas data (epigenomic annotations of 222 cell types) was retrieved and converted to hg19 (liftOver) without any additional filtering. The resulting bed files were homogenized (sortBed function of bedtools108) and checked for errors (bedops --ec --merge function of BEDOPS109). For each dataset, we additionally create one baseline annotation corresponding to the union of all candidate regulatory regions across all cell types (see CT-FM analyses in the STAR Methods section).

Simulations

To assess CT-FM power under realistic scenarios, we simulated GWAS summary statistics for the 1,187,349 HapMap 3 SNPs used by S-LDSC. We considered three main simulation scenarios with different numbers of causal cell types: one (osteoblast), two (osteoblast and fibroblast) and three (osteoblast, fibroblast and epithelial renal). The osteoblast and fibroblast SNP-annotations were identified on the height GWAS by CT-FM as causal (we note that while considered as independent causal cell types, these two SNP-annotations are non-negligibly correlated, R = 0.27); the epithelial renal SNP-annotation was selected based on its relatively high cRE sharing with other CTS SNP-annotations (likely due to cRE sharing with epithelial cells from other tissues; see Figure S5). We did not simulate more than three causal cell types because our empirical analyses of 63 GWASs indicate that most traits exhibit between one and three independent causal sets, and that these components capture the majority of h2-cREs. 500 simulations were performed for each scenario. First, we computed expected per-SNP h2 using S-LDSC regression coefficients estimated on height GWAS using the baseline model SNP-annotations and the osteoblast SNP-annotation (when considering one causal cell type) or the osteoblast and fibroblast SNP-annotations (when considering two causal cell type; when considering three causal cell types, we modified the per-SNP h2 of SNPs in the epithelial renal SNP-annotation such that their h2 enrichment is similar to the average of h2 enrichment of SNPs in the osteoblast and fibroblast SNP-annotations). Second, we randomly selected 10,000 causal SNPs (same order of magnitude as the number of causal SNPs per GWAS in ref.30), and a GWAS sample size of 350 K. Third, we ran CT-FM using 600 CTS SNP-annotations with varying degrees of residual correlation R with the osteoblast and fibroblast SNP-annotations. For example, for the osteoblast cell type, we selected 40 CTS SNP-annotations presenting a weak correlation with the causal SNP-annotation (Rbetween −0.11 and 0.10), 80 CTS SNP-annotations presenting a low-to-moderate correlation with the causal SNP-annotation (R between 0.10 and 0.32), 40 CTS SNP-annotations presenting a moderate-to-high correlation with the causal SNP-annotation (R between 0.32 and 0.45) and 40 CTS SNP-annotations presenting a high correlation with the causal SNP-annotation (R between 0.45 and 0.81). Similar selection was performed for additional 200 CTS SNP-annotations with varying degrees of correlation with the causal fibroblast CTS SNP-annotation (minimum R = −0.11; maximum R = 0.78; mean R = 0.12) and the causal epithelial renal CTS SNP-annotation (minimum R = −0.14; maximum R = 0.56; mean R = 0.18) (Table S42).

We performed CT-FM-SNP simulations by leveraging CT-FM simulations with one and two causal cell types and by sampling SNPs based on their osteoblast and fibroblast SNP-annotations and their expected per-SNP h2. We evaluated type I error within each simulation scenario by sampling 500 candidate SNPs that do not overlap the osteoblast and fibroblast causal SNP-annotations based on their expected per-SNP h2. We evaluated precision, sensitivity and F1 scores by sampling 500 candidate SNPs that overlap the osteoblast causal SNP-annotation (but not the fibroblast causal SNP-annotation) based on their expected per-SNP h2. In supplementary analyses of simulations with two causal cell types we also considered 500 candidate SNPs that overlap the fibroblast causal SNP-annotation (but not the osteoblast causal SNP-annotation) and 500 candidate SNPs that overlap both the osteoblast and fibroblast causal SNP-annotations. We did not extend CT-FM-SNP simulations to 3 causal SNP-annotations, as this setting would require modeling eight distinct SNP overlap categories (one category with SNPs not overlapping any causal annotation, three categories with SNPs overlapping a single causal annotation, three categories with SNPs overlapping two causal annotations, one category with SNPs overlapping the three causal annotations), substantially complicating interpretation while providing limited additional insight given the rarity of traits with more than two ICSs in our empirical data.

CT-FM analyses

We successfully ran CT-FM on GWAS summary statistics of five blood traits from the UK Biobank,37 63 independent GWAS summary statistics, and CD and UC summary statistics70 (see Tables S6 and S18). All GWASs were performed on individuals of European ancestry. S-LDSC Z-scores were obtained by running S-LDSC with the baseline v1.2 SNP-annotations as recommended by developers when studying cell-type-specific effects113; to make sure that our analyses capture CTS information we added six SNP-annotations corresponding to the union of all CTS ENCODE4, CATlas, ABC, EpiMap,24 and DHS26 cREs, as well as GTEx fine-mapped eQTLs (SuSiE PIP >0.05 in at least one tissue).100,114 We used Europeans from the 1000 Genomes Project as the reference panel.106 To quantify global error at the ICS level, we estimated the expected number of false discoveries as the sum of 1 − PIPmax across inferred ICSs, where PIPmax denotes the highest PIP among CTS SNP-annotations within each ICS; the corresponding false discovery proportion was computed by dividing this quantity by the total number of inferred ICSs.

CT-FM-SNP analyses

We ran CT-FM on the 49 UK Biobank traits analyzed in ref.30, and identified 6,975 candidate causal {non-coding SNP, trait} pairs (5,819 unique SNPs); we note that no CTS SNP-annotations were included in PolyFun functional priors. We applied CT-FM-SNP to all of these pairs. To link non-coding SNP to their target genes, we leveraged predictions from our cS2G strategy36 and retained the gene with the highest cS2G score. GO enrichment analyses were performed using the GOseq R package81 for cell types presenting at least 50 SNP-Cell-type pairs identified in this study. For the analyses presented in Figure 6C, we regrouped cell types corresponding to T cells (such as CD4+ and CD8+ T cells) and cell types corresponding to epithelial cells (such as epithelial fibroblasts, choroid plexus epithelial cells and mammary epithelial cells). We retained all GO terms corresponding to the Biological Process category, containing between 10 and 1,000 genes, and containing at least 5 genes from the tested gene set. All FDR significant enrichments (FDR p < 0.05) are reported in Table S36. For h2 analyses, we selected 7 UK Biobank traits with two identified CT-FM ICSs and at least 50 SNPs successfully analyzed by CT-FM-SNP. For analyses related to pleiotropic SNPs, genetically uncorrelated UK Biobank traits were defined by prioritizing UK Biobank traits with the highest SNP-heritability Z score, and by removing traits with a squared genetic correlation (rg)69 > 0.05.

OneK1K analyses

We leveraged single-cell eQTLs from OneK1K41,42 to functionally validate CT-FM-SNP results on lymphocyte and monocyte count candidate SNPs. We leveraged single-cell eQTLs statistics computed by jaxQTL on monocytes (merging classical and non-classical monocyte cells) and lymphocytes (merging CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, B cells and natural killer cells). For each trait, we performed eQTL analyses for three sets of SNPs. First, we investigated lymphocyte (resp. monocyte) count candidate SNP that have been assigned to lymphocyte (resp. monocyte) cell types by CT-FM-SNP. Second, we investigated lymphocyte (resp. monocyte) count candidate SNP that have not been assigned to lymphocyte (resp. monocyte) cell types by CT-FM-SNP, to test whether CT-FM-SNP can successfully distinguish the candidate SNPs that do not impact trait variance through gene regulation in lymphocytes (resp. monocytes). Finally, we investigated a set of 1,393 control SNPs, where we selected candidate SNPs across 16 independent UK Biobank traits not genetically correlated (rg < 0.05) to lymphocyte and monocyte count. Rather than investigating eQTLs across all genes, we restricted eQTL analyses to candidate genes using the PoPS method115 (rank 1 to 5) on lymphocyte (resp. monocyte) count GWAS; we obtained similar trends when restricting eQTL analyses to SNP-gene pairs identified by cS2G (stringent selection) and when considering all protein-coding genes (permissive selection) (Figure S46).

S-LDSC analyses

To evaluate the h2 captured by CTS SNP-annotations identified by CT-FM, we constructed SNP-annotations representing the union of the CTS SNP-annotations within each ICS, and estimated the h2 they explained using S-LDSC with baseline-LD model v2.2 and the six additional functional SNP-annotations used as background SNP-annotations in CT-FM analyses; we note that we followed the recommendation to use baseline model SNP-annotations for identifying critical cell types, and the baseline-LD model SNP-annotations for estimating h2 enrichment.113 To estimate h2-cREs we merged the six additional functional SNP-annotations to a single annotation. Meta-analyses were performed using random-effects with the R package rmeta.

To investigate whether traits with independent causal cell types tend to have causal variants acting jointly on multiple cell types, we ran S-LDSC with the baseline model SNP-annotations, the six additional functional SNP-annotations, one SNP-annotation corresponding to each ICS and one interaction SNP-annotation corresponding to the intersection of the multiple ICSs. We meta-analyzed standardized effects τ∗ of the interaction SNP-annotation, which is defined as τ∗ = τ x M x sd/h2, where M is the number of common SNPs in the reference panel, sd is the standard deviation of the SNP-annotation, and h2 is the estimate of SNP-heritability from S-LDSC. We compared the h2 enrichment of the interaction SNP-annotation under a model with no interaction (i.e., obtained from the τ estimated by S-LDSC with the baseline-LD model, the six additional functional SNP-annotations, and one SNP-annotation corresponding to each ICS) and with interaction (i.e., estimated directly from S-LDSC by adding the interaction SNP-annotation to the previous model).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the units of analysis specified in the corresponding results, Figure legends, and Supplementary Tables, including GWAS traits, simulation replicates, SNP-annotations, candidate SNP-trait pairs, and SNP-gene-cell-type assignments. CT-FM and CT-FM-SNP performance in simulations was evaluated using precision, sensitivity, F1 score, type I error, posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs), and 95% confidence intervals across simulation replicates. High-confidence candidates from CT-FM/CT-FM-SNP were defined as SNP-annotations with PIP >0.5. Heritability enrichment and SNP-heritability explained by annotations were estimated using S-LDSC, and meta-analyses across traits were performed using random-effects models (package rmeta). Multiple testing was controlled using false discovery rate (FDR) correction where indicated. Unless otherwise stated, significance thresholds, sample sizes, statistical tests, and software packages are reported in the relevant STAR Methods subsections, Figure legends, and Supplementary Tables.
