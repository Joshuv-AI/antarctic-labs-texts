Baseline characteristics and practice patterns in geriatric vs. non-geriatric hemodialysis patients: initial results from a Thai nationwide multicenter prospective cohort (Thai-HDcohort)

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder adults represent a rapidly growing segment of the hemodialysis (HD) patient population. However, the specific clinical variations and dialysis management strategies employed for geriatric patients in Thailand remain poorly characterized.MethodsWe analyzed baseline data from 839 hemodialysis patients enrolled in the Thai-HDcohort, a prospective multicenter study across 13 clinical sites in Thailand. Patients were stratified into a geriatric group (≥ 65 years of age, n = 412) and a younger control cohort (< 65 years of age, n = 427). Demographics, vascular access modalities, baseline laboratory markers, pharmacotherapy, and dialysis prescriptions were compared between the two groups.ResultsGeriatric patients were more frequently treated in hospital-based facilities (75.0% vs. 67.4%, p = .002). The geriatric group exhibited lower body weight (57.7 vs. 63.0 kg, p < .001) and lower serum albumin (hypoalbuminemia prevalence: 11.6% vs. 5.1%, p = .001). Regarding practice patterns, geriatric patients relied more heavily on tunneled cuffed catheters at enrollment (38.1% vs. 27.7%, p = .005) and were likely to initiate HD using a catheter (41.0% vs. 27.1%, p < .001). Geriatric prescriptions also favored a higher utilization of a 3.0 mmol/L dialysate potassium bath (40.0% vs. 23.7%, p < .001), and a higher dialysate calcium bath of 3.0 mmol/L (19.9% vs. 12.9%, p = .011).ConclusionsIn this cross-sectional study, geriatric hemodialysis patients showed distinct variations in prescriptions and vascular access. Longitudinal follow- up is essential to determine whether these conservative, individualized strategies improve clinical and survival outcomes.Keywords: Hemodialysis, aged, vascular access, kidney failure, frailty, practice patterns

Introduction

The global burden of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) is undergoing a significant demographic transformation, driven by increased life expectancy and a rising prevalence of noncommunicable diseases [1]. Approximately 850 million people worldwide live with chronic kidney disease, and the number of patients requiring kidney replacement therapy (KRT) is projected to double by 2030 [2,3]. In high-income and many middle-income nations, the geriatric population now represents the fastest-growing segment of the dialysis cohort, with the median age of dialysis initiation frequently exceeding 65 years [4,5].

Managing elderly hemodialysis (HD) patients presents a unique clinical paradigm that extends beyond traditional clearance targets. Geriatric patients often exhibit a high burden of frailty, cognitive impairment, and multi-morbidity, necessitating a shift from a ‘one-size-fits-all’ approach to individualized care [6,7]. Recent evidence highlights significant disparities in practice patterns between geriatric and younger cohorts. For instance, elderly patients are more likely to initiate dialysis with central venous catheters (CVC) due to higher rates of arteriovenous fistula (AVF) nonmaturation [8,9]. Furthermore, to mitigate risks of intradialytic hypotension and myocardial stunning, clinicians often prescribe lower blood flow rates (BFR) and adjusted dialysate compositions—such as higher potassium concentrations (3 mmol/L)—compared to standard protocols for younger adults [10].

Thailand is currently navigating a sharp acceleration in its ESKD epidemic, with prevalence reaching approximately 2,381 per million population as of 2024 [9]. A recent landmark policy shift in 2022, allowing patients under the Universal Coverage Scheme to choose their preferred dialysis modality, has led to a substantial surge in the utilization of hemodialysis [9,11]. While reporting to the Thailand Renal Replacement Therapy (TRT) registry is mandatory in Thailand and provides vital epidemiological and incidence surveillance, registry data collection is restricted primarily to core administrative and reimbursement variables. Consequently, granular prescription parameters (such as exact dialysate potassium and calcium bath concentrations and prescribed BFR), detailed concomitant pharmacotherapy, and complete laboratory time-point profiles are not comprehensively captured. The Thai Multicenter Prospective Cohort Study (Thai-HDcohort) was specifically established to address these knowledge gaps through an audited, prospective protocol with high data completeness to evaluate real-world clinical management in the HD population [12,13].

Thai-HDcohort was established to systematically investigate the clinical characteristics, management strategies, and longitudinal outcomes of HD patients across 13 medical centers nationwide. In this initial report, we provide a cross-sectional analysis comparing the baseline socio-demographics, clinical profiles, and practice patterns of geriatric (≥65 years) and nongeriatric (<65 years) individuals. By identifying these age-specific trends, we aim to provide the empirical foundation necessary to optimize dialysis delivery for the growing elderly population in Thailand and similar regional contexts.

Method

Study design and oversight

We conducted a multicenter prospective cohort study across Thailand. The study was initiated in September 2023 and involved 13 participating centers nationwide (see Supplementary Appendix for the complete list of sites). To ensure a unified ethical review process, the study protocol was centrally reviewed and approved by the Central Research Ethics Committee (CREC) of Thailand, which serves as the national independent review board for multicenter clinical research [CREC no. CREC091/66BR-MED54/Certificate no. COA-CREC095/2023]. In accordance with local institutional policies, this central approval was subsequently submitted to and acknowledged by the local Institutional Review Board (IRB) or Ethics Committee at each participating site. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies.

Participant selection

Patients were eligible for the study if they were aged 18 years or older and had a diagnosis of ESKD requiring KRT. Participants were categorized into two groups:

Prevalent cases: Patients who had been receiving maintenance hemodialysis (HD) for more than 3 months.

Incident cases: Patients diagnosed with CKD Stage 5 (eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73m2), including those with failing kidney transplants (CKD 5T) or those transitioning from peritoneal dialysis (PD) to HD. These patients must have initiated HD within the preceding 3 months or be scheduled to initiate therapy within the next month. Additionally, the attending nephrologist must have expected the patient to require continuous KRT for at least 3 months.

Consecutive eligible patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis across 13 nationwide participating centers were screened and invited to participate. The exclusion criteria were pre-specified to minimize selection bias while ensuring clinical stability: patients with an anticipated kidney transplantation within 6 months, those with an expected survival of less than 6 months or terminal malignancy receiving active palliative/chemotherapy care per the attending nephrologist assessment, active systemic infections (or infections resolving within 4 weeks prior to screening), and those declining to provide informed consent.

Study objectives and subgroup definitions

The primary endpoint of this prospective cohort is all-cause mortality. The current report presents a cross-sectional analysis of baseline clinical characteristics and dialysis practice patterns following the completion of enrollment. For the primary exposure analysis, the geriatric population was prespecified as participants aged ≥65 years at enrollment, aligning with standard definitions established by the Thai Ministry of Public Health and national healthcare reimbursement frameworks.

Data collection and variables

All study data were managed and stored using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), hosted at the Chula Data Management Center, Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University, Bangkok, Thailand. Baseline clinical characteristics, comorbidities, and medical history were extracted from medical records within 14 days of enrollment. Predialysis laboratory parameters were obtained from routine mid-week morning pre-HD blood samples collected within 30 days prior to or at enrollment. Patient vital status and mortality events were prospectively monitored and verified through quarterly electronic medical record audits and supplemented by structured telephone interviews every 6 months for patients who missed scheduled center visits or were lost to routine follow-up.

Socio-demographics and baseline clinical profiles

At enrollment, we collected comprehensive baseline data, including province of residence, gender, marital status, educational attainment, and income level. Facility-specific characteristics where patients received care were also documented. A detailed medical history was obtained for each participant, focusing on comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, dyslipidemia, cirrhosis, and chronic viral infections. The primary etiology of ESKD was extracted from medical records and categorized based on attending nephrologist clinical evaluation into kidney disease associated with diabetes, kidney disease associated with hypertension, obstructive uropathy, or glomerulonephritis. Explicit documentation of renal biopsy confirmation was recorded when available; in unbiopsied patients, diagnoses were classified as clinically associated etiologies based on established medical history and presentation. Additionally, we recorded history and duration of prior organ transplantation or peritoneal dialysis, current medications, and lifestyle factors, including smoking and alcohol consumption. We also documented the participants’ vaccination status for influenza, pneumococcus, and hepatitis B. Standardized physical examinations were performed at baseline to record anthropometric measurements (weight, height) and vital signs (blood pressure, pulse, and respiratory rate).

Statistical analysis

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome of all-cause mortality. According to the 2020 United States Renal Data System annual data report, the mortality rate among patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis was approximately 209 per 1,000 patient-years [14]. Using a single proportion estimator formula with a level of significance of 0.05 and a desired precision of 0.03 (30 per 1,000 patient-years), we calculated the required sample size to be 706 participants. To account for a predicted 15% attrition rate, the final recruitment target was increased to 812 participants. We employed a 20:80 allocation ratio for incident and prevalent cases, resulting in a target enrollment of 160 incident and 660 prevalent patients.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were evaluated for distributional normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between geriatric (≥65 years) and non-geriatric (<65 years) groups using the independent-samples Student’s t-test; non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR, 25th–75th percentiles) and compared using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages (n [%]) and compared between groups using the Pearson Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. For descriptive and univariate comparisons, available-case analyses were performed using all available data for each individual variable (with denominators explicitly reported). For multivariable regression models, complete-case analyses restricted to participants with complete covariate data were utilized without imputation. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with significance defined as p value < .05. Data management and statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.10) within the Google Colab environment, utilizing the Pandas, NumPy, SciPy, and statsmodels libraries.

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics

Among the 839 participants enrolled across 13 nationwide centers, geriatric patients (n = 412; median age 75 [IQR: 70–80] years) presented with distinct socio-demographic and clinical physical profiles compared to their non-geriatric counterparts (n = 427; median age 55 [IQR: 44–61] years; p < .001) (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1). The geriatric group exhibited a significantly higher proportion of female participants (50.5% vs. 37.7%, p < .001), lower median body weight (57.7 vs. 63.0 kg, p < .001), shorter stature (158 vs. 165 cm, p < .001), and lower body mass index (median 22.8 vs. 23.3 kg/m2, p = .028), with no significant difference in waist circumference (p = .64). Regarding care settings, geriatric patients were more frequently treated in hospital-based in-center facilities (75.0% vs. 67.4%), whereas non-geriatric patients were twice as likely to utilize satellite clinic units (15.0% vs. 7.0%; p = .002). The primary etiologies of ESKD across the cohort were kidney disease associated with diabetes (33.3%) and kidney disease associated with hypertension (25.4%). Prior KRT was uncommon in both groups, with 4.2% transitioning from peritoneal dialysis and 3.3% having a failed kidney transplant. Socio-demographically, geriatric patients were less likely to be employed (unemployed/retired: 52.0% vs. 32.0%, p < .001) and had lower rates of formal higher education (p < .001).

Analysis of baseline comorbidity profiles revealed a substantial burden of chronic disease across the cohort, with marked disparities between age groups (Supplementary Table S2). Geriatric patients exhibited significantly higher rates of major cardiometabolic risk factors compared to younger adults, including diabetes mellitus (52.9% vs. 37.0%, p < .001), hypertension (83.0% vs. 73.1%, p < .001), and dyslipidemia (49.0% vs. 35.4%, p < .001). Established cardiovascular disease was nearly two-fold more prevalent in older adults, driven predominantly by a higher frequency of documented coronary artery disease (15.0% vs. 8.4%, p = .004), resulting in a significantly elevated composite cardiovascular disease burden (22.6% vs. 13.1%, p < .001). Consequently, multimorbidity (≥2 concurrent chronic conditions) was documented in 69.4% of geriatric individuals compared to 49.4% of non-geriatric patients (p < .001), and high multimorbidity (≥3 conditions) affected 38.6% versus 24.1% (p < .001), underscoring the complex clinical disease burden of older adults undergoing hemodialysis.

Hemodialysis practice patterns and adequacy

Significant differences were observed in the HD practice patterns between the geriatric and non-geriatric populations (Table 2). Regarding the treatment setting, geriatric patients were more frequently treated in hospital-based in-center HD units compared to non-geriatric patients (75% vs. 67%), while a higher proportion of non-geriatric patients utilized satellite clinics (15% vs. 7%; p = .002). The type of vascular access at the time of enrollment also differed significantly (p < .001). Non-geriatric patients had a higher prevalence of AVF use (66.4% vs. 55.5%), whereas the geriatric group relied more heavily on tunneled cuffed catheters (38.1% vs. 27.7%). A similar trend was seen in the vascular access used at the initiation of HD, where geriatric patients were significantly more likely to start dialysis with a catheter (p < .001). In terms of HD prescription, High-flux HD was the dominant modality for both groups (88.9%), with no significant difference in the utilization of OL-HDF between age groups (p = .818). However, geriatric patients were prescribed significantly lower BFR (p = .038). Dialysate flow rates (DFR) were similar between the two groups. Prescription of dialysate composition also showed age-related variations. Geriatric patients were more frequently prescribed a dialysate potassium concentration of 3 mmol/L compared to non-geriatric patients (40% vs. 23.7%; p < .001). Similarly, there were significant differences in dialysate calcium concentrations (p = .011), with a higher proportion of geriatric patients receiving 3.0 mmol/L calcium dialysate (19.9% vs. 12.9%). Regarding dialysis adequacy, there were no statistically significant differences in the median single-pool Kt/V or the URR between the two groups.

Baseline laboratory profiles

Baseline biochemical profiles revealed significant variations between the geriatric and non-geriatric populations (Table 3). While median predialysis serum urea levels were slightly lower in geriatric patients (51 vs. 56 mg/dL; p = .002), the disparity in serum creatinine was more pronounced, with geriatric patients presenting significantly lower levels (7.1 vs. 9.3 mg/dL; p < .001). Electrolyte profiles demonstrated that median serum sodium concentrations were comparable between the two groups. However, the prevalence of dysnatremias differed significantly (p = .010); hyponatremia was more frequently observed in the geriatric group (17.8% vs. 10.7%). Serum potassium levels were lower in the geriatric group (4.3 vs. 4.4 mmol/L; p < .001), corresponding with a higher incidence of hypokalemia (8.5% vs. 3.6%; p = .004). Regarding bone and mineral metabolism, geriatric patients exhibited significantly lower median serum phosphorus (4.1 vs. 4.8 mg/dL; p < .001) and lower iPTH levels (322 vs. 357 pg/mL; p = .012). Hyperphosphatemia was notably more prevalent among non-geriatric patients (50.1% vs. 37.4%; p < .001). Furthermore, median serum calcium levels were marginally lower in the geriatric group (8.9 vs. 9.1 mg/dL; p < .001), accompanied by a higher incidence of hypocalcemia (21.8% vs. 18.0%; p = .023). Nutritional assessment indicated that median serum albumin was significantly lower in geriatric patients (3.9 vs. 4.1 g/dL; p < .001), translating to a higher prevalence of hypoalbuminemia (<3.5 g/dL) in this population (11.6% vs. 5.1%; p = .001). Finally, baseline hemoglobin levels showed no statistically significant difference between the two cohorts.

Baseline pharmacotherapy and concomitant medications

Analysis of baseline medication profiles revealed a substantial overall pill burden and several key prescribing differences between age groups (Table 4). Overall, polypharmacy (≥5 concurrent medication classes) was present in 85.9% of the cohort and was significantly more prevalent among geriatric patients compared to younger adults (90.5% vs. 81.5%, p < .001). Similarly, hyper-polypharmacy (≥10 medication classes) affected a higher proportion of older adults (21.8% vs. 15.7%, p = .028), with geriatric patients taking a higher median number of daily medication classes (p = 0.029). Among specific cardiovascular therapies, geriatric patients were significantly more likely to receive antiplatelet agents (43.5% vs. 27.0%), statins (65.1% vs. 50.9%; both p < .001), and ezetimibe (7.3% vs. 4.0%, p = .038). Antihypertensive class usage was comparable between groups, with calcium channel blockers being the most frequently prescribed agent overall (63.4%). For CKD-MBD management, calcium-based binders were most common (54.7% overall), but geriatric patients were significantly more likely to receive sevelamer carbonate (18.7% vs. 13.4%, p = .035). In terms of anemia management, geriatric patients demonstrated significantly higher prescription rates for both erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (83.0% vs. 77.1%, p = 0.031) and oral iron therapy (48.5% vs. 40.3%, p = 0.016), whereas intravenous iron utilization showed no significant variation between cohorts.

Post hoc analysis result

In post hoc analyses stratifying participants across three ordered age tiers (<65 years [n = 427], 65–74 years [n = 203], and ≥75 years [n = 209]), clear monotonic biological and practice gradients were demonstrated across the aging spectrum (Supplementary Table S1). The prevalence of tunneled cuffed CVC access increased progressively with advancing age tiers while functional AVF use declined. Dialysate potassium 3.0 mmol/L bath utilization increased with age, accompanied by a progressive reduction in prescribed BFR in the oldest group (median 300 mL/min, ptrend = .0025). Furthermore, significant linear declines across age tiers were observed for serum creatinine, serum albumin, serum phosphorus and iPTH (all ptrend < .05).

In multivariable logistic regression analyses (Table 5), geriatric age independently predicted both tunneled cuffed CVC utilization and 3.0 mmol/L dialysate potassium bath prescription. For tunneled CVC use (vs. non-CVC arteriovenous access), the odds ratio (OR) for geriatric patients was 1.70 (95%CI: 1.25–2.31, p < .001) in crude analysis (Model 1), 1.49 (95%CI: 1.08–2.05, p = .014) after demographic adjustment (Model 2), and remained significant at 1.42 (95%CI: 1.02–1.98, p = .039) in the fully adjusted multivariable model (Model 3). For dialysate potassium, geriatric age was associated with higher odds of receiving a 3.0 mmol/L bath across all models: crude Model 1 (OR = 2.17, 95%CI: 1.58–2.97, p < .001), physiologically adjusted Model 2 (aOR = 2.17, 95%CI: 1.55–3.03, p < .001), and fully adjusted Model 3 (aOR = 1.73, 95%CI: 1.23–2.44, p = .002).

Sensitivity analysis

In sensitivity analysis restricted strictly to prevalent maintenance hemodialysis patients (n = 779; non-geriatric <65 years: n = 402), all primary clinical and prescription disparities between age groups remained statistically robust and significant (Supplementary Table S3). Geriatric maintenance patients demonstrated a persistently higher reliance on tunneled cuffed CVCs (37.4% vs. 24.6%, p = .0044) and more frequent prescription of 3.0 mmol/L dialysate potassium baths (37.1% vs. 19.9%, p < .001) compared to younger adults. Prescribed BFR remained significantly lower in the geriatric group (median 300.0 vs. 350.0 mL/min, p = .0073). Furthermore, nutritional and metabolic vulnerability persisted during stable maintenance therapy, with geriatric patients exhibiting significantly lower serum albumin (median 3.9 vs. 4.1 g/dL, p < 0.001), higher rates of hypoalbuminemia (11.1% vs. 4.2%, p = 0.0020), lower body mass index (median 22.8 vs. 23.3 kg/m2, p = .0282), and markedly reduced serum creatinine (median 7.2 vs. 9.3 mg/dL, p < .001). Predialysis serum potassium (median 4.3 vs. 4.4 mmol/L, p = .0014), serum phosphorus (median 4.1 vs. 4.8 mg/dL, p < .001), and iPTH (median 327.4 vs. 363.0 pg/mL, p = 0.0156) also remained lower in older adults.

Discussion

In this multicenter prospective cohort study of 839 hemodialysis patients in Thailand, we observed distinct age-related disparities in clinical characteristics and dialysis practice patterns. Geriatric patients (≥65 years) were characterized by a higher prevalence of female sex, lower body mass index, and more frequent hypoalbuminemia than their younger counterparts. Notably, practice patterns in the geriatric group significantly diverged from standard protocols, with a marked reliance on tunneled cuffed catheters for vascular access and a consistent prescription of lower BFR and higher dialysate potassium concentrations. The observed reliance on tunneled catheters in geriatric patients—both at the initiation of dialysis and during maintenance therapy—reflects a significant departure from the traditional ‘Fistula First’ paradigm [8,15]. This trend is consistent with global data suggesting that the high rate of AVF nonmaturation and primary failure in the elderly often necessitates a ‘Patient-First’ or ‘Access Life-Plan’ approach [8,16]. In our cohort, nearly 45% of geriatric patients utilized catheters, compared to only 28% of non-geriatric patients. While catheters are associated with higher infection risks, they may avoid the morbidity of multiple failed surgical interventions in patients with limited life expectancy or poor vascular quality [15,17]. Crucially, our multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated that older age independently predicted tunneled CVC use (aOR = 1.42, 95%CI: 1.02–1.98, p = .0392) even after controlling for sex, body weight, kidney disease associated with diabetes, cardiovascular disease burden, and serum albumin. Furthermore, female sex emerged as an independent predictor of catheter reliance (aOR = 1.41, p = .0473), consistent with smaller vessel calibers and higher nonmaturation rates in elderly women. Recent large-scale Asian registry evidence, such as the Japanese study by Murakami et al., confirms that successful AVF creation provides substantial survival benefits over catheters even in patients aged ≥75 years, underscoring that vascular planning in older adults should remain personalized rather than purely catheter-permissive [18].

Our study also highlights a conservative trend in dialysis prescription for the elderly, characterized by significantly lower BFR in the geriatric group. In clinical practice, this BFR reduction is multi-factorial, representing both a physical constraint of vascular access and a deliberate cardioprotective strategy. The lower BFR is heavily driven by the high prevalence of tunneled cuffed catheters in our geriatric patients (38.1% vs. 27.7% in non-geriatric patients), as catheter-based access physically limits achievable blood flow and venous tolerance compared to mature AVFs. However, the BFR restriction must be carefully balanced against systemic clearance and patient survival. A recent 2024 cohort study by Zhang et al. demonstrated that higher time-averaged effective BFR (>275 mL/min) was independently associated with a 63% reduction in all-cause mortality and a 38% decrease in major adverse cardiovascular events per 20 mL/min increase, without compromising AVF patency [19]. This underscores a complex therapeutic tradeoff in geriatric prescription, where BFR reduction for hemodynamic tolerance may unintentionally limit the survival benefits associated with higher convective and diffusive clearances. Furthermore, the more frequent use of a 3.0 mmol/L dialysate potassium bath (40% vs. 24%) suggests a protective strategy against sudden cardiac death. Elderly patients, often presenting with lower muscle mass and higher rates of hypokalemia, are at elevated risk for cardiac arrhythmias when exposed to high serum-to-dialysate potassium gradients [20,21]. Indeed, a landmark study of 25,629 hemodialysis patients from the French REIN registry by Mercadal et al. demonstrated that dialysis facilities employing multiple, customized dialysate potassium formulations (such as 2.0 and 3.0 mmol/L baths) achieved significantly better patient survival compared to units adhering to a single standardized bath protocol [22,23].

The demographic and nutritional profiles we identified carry important prognostic implications. The higher prevalence of hypoalbuminemia (11.6%) and lower BMI in geriatric patients are established predictors of mortality in the Thai HD population [24]. These metabolic markers, combined with the observed electrolyte patterns, underscore the need for tailored nutritional support and precise metabolic monitoring for older adults. Furthermore, the 2022 ‘HD Choice’ policy shift in Thailand has significantly increased the volume of elderly patients entering hemodialysis units, placing a premium on the development of age-specific clinical guidelines that prioritize quality of life and ‘home time’ over biochemical clearance targets.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, this cross-sectional analysis provides an observational snapshot of baseline characteristics and prescription practices; therefore, it cannot establish causal relationships between specific practice patterns and longitudinal clinical outcomes. Second, although the multicenter design enhances regional representativeness, detailed institutional factors, center-specific resource constraints, and individual physician prescribing preferences were not systematically captured and may have contributed to unmeasured confounding. Third, validated clinical frailty assessment tools (such as the Clinical Frailty Scale or comprehensive geriatric assessments) were not formally administered in this cohort; thus, characterizations of physiological vulnerability represent clinical and biochemical inferences rather than validated score-based classifications. Notwithstanding these limitations, a major strength of this study lies in its prospective, nationwide multicenter design. The Thai-HDcohort represents one of the largest prospectively audited evaluations of geriatric hemodialysis management in Southeast Asia, capturing highly granular clinical, laboratory, and prescription data across 13 diverse tertiary and regional centers with minimal missing data.

In summary, baseline findings from the Thai-HDcohort describe unique clinical and nutritional profiles among geriatric hemodialysis patients in Thailand, accompanied by observable tendencies toward lower BFR, higher dialysate potassium concentrations, and catheter-permissive access patterns. Given the cross-sectional nature of this initial report, these observational findings should not be construed as prescriptive mandates, but rather as real-world evidence reflecting current individualized clinical adaptations. As the geriatric dialysis population expands, future longitudinal follow-up within this prospective cohort will be critical to determine whether these tailored, age-specific practice patterns translate into improved survival, reduced cardiovascular morbidity, and better quality of life.
