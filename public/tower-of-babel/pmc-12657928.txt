Predicting flexural strength in fiber-reinforced UHPC via random forest
Authors: Keldys D Sarmiento-Pupo, Jesús D Escalante-Tovar, Jesús E Altamiranda, Joaquin Abellan-Garcia, Jorge I Vélez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe incorporation of fibers into Ultra-High-Performance Concrete (UHPC) significantly enhances its flexural strength and ductility, making it a desirable material for high-performance structural applications. However, predicting the flexural behavior of fiber-reinforced UHPC remains challenging due to the complex interactions among constituent materials. This complexity increases further with the partial replacement of cement by supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs), which alter matrix reactivity and microstructure. To address these challenges, this study employs Random Forest (RF) regression to predict the ultimate flexural strength of UHPC, incorporating mixtures with diverse SCM combinations and up to two different fiber types. A dataset of 550 experimental mixtures, comprising 41 input variables, was used to train and validate the model. Results highlight the critical influence of fiber type and dosage, as well as matrix parameters such as cement and silica fume content, water-to-binder ratio, and aggregate size. Introduction

Ultra-high-performance concrete (UHPC) represents a major advancement in cementitious materials, combining exceptional strength, ductility, and durability. Defined by compressive strengths above 150 MPa and tensile strengths exceeding 20 MPa, UHPC outperforms conventional concretes in mechanical and durability terms1.

Its superior performance has enabled applications across infrastructure, buildings, and protective structures—including bridge components2,3, high-rise and offshore facilities4–6. and blast- or impact-resistant elements—supporting next-generation sustainable infrastructure5,7. Hence, research efforts towards understanding UHPC’s dynamic behavior, along with advancements in testing methodologies and constitutive modeling, will further support its deployment in extreme service conditions4,8. and complex production requirements4,9. oreover, the high cement content of UHPC raises environmental concerns, motivating the incorporation of supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) to reduce both cost and embodied carbon10,11. SCMs such as fly ash, ground-granulated blast-furnace slag, calcined clays, and agricultural residues modify the hydration chemistry and microstructure of UHPC, interacting with superplasticizers and high-aspect-ratio fibers within dense matrices composed of cement, silica fume, and fine aggregates. These multiscale couplings render empirical regression approaches insufficient for predicting mechanical behavior. Consequently, nonlinear data-driven frameworks such as Random Forest (RF)12, provide powerful tools for mapping complex interactions among mixture variables, optimizing component dosages, and predicting target properties such as flexural strength13,14.

Background

Fiber-reinforced UHPC combines ultra-high compressive strength, superior ductility, and exceptional durability15. Fibers—steel or synthetic—control post-crack behavior and enhance flexural toughness16. Yet, predicting ultimate flexural strength is difficult because variables such as cement content, silica-fume ratio, aggregate gradation, water-to-binder ratio, and fiber dosage interact nonlinearly under varying curing conditions15,17.

Traditional trial-and-error mix optimization is labor-intensive18, whereas ML methods, particularly RF19, capture high-order nonlinearities and tolerate noisy data. Ensuring prediction reliability, however, requires rigorous preprocessing and outlier control: Cook’s distance20. dentifies influential observations that could distort model fitting. Once filtered, RF models yield robust performance and interpretable insights through Partial Dependence Plots (PDPs)21. And SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)22.

Literature overview

Multiple studies have characterized how different fiber types and dosages influence the flexural performance of UHPC. Gao et al.23 conducted experimental investigations on steel-fiber-reinforced UHPC beams under four-point bending and found that increasing steel fiber content from 1% to 2% by volume enhanced peak flexural strength by approximately 20% and significantly improved toughness indices. Li et al.24 performed a systematic mix design study, demonstrating that both fiber geometry (length and aspect ratio) and fiber material (steel vs. synthetic) critically affect crack bridging capacity and post-peak ductility. Yang et al.25 showed that a combination of silica fume dosages above 15% by binderweight, a low water-to-binder (w/b) ratio (< 0.25), and a steel fiber volume fraction near 2% yields flxural strengths exceeding 25 MPa at 28 days. However, these empirical investigations often rely on discrete experimental points and cannot easily generalize to all possible fiber–matrix combinations, motivating the need for data-driven predictive tools.

Key findings from recent studies highlight the critical role of fiber dosage and matrix proportions in UHPC flexural performance. Specifically, incorporating steel fibers (≈ 13 mm length, aspect ratio ≈ 80) at 2% by volume can boost peak flexural strength by 15%–22% compared to unreinforced UHPC, whereas synthetic fibers (e.g., polyvinyl alcohol, PVA)—a water-soluble synthetic polymer known for its excellent fiber–matrix bonding and energy absorption capacity—enhance post-crack ductility at the expense of slightly lower ultimate strength. Equally important, silica fume replacements of 15%–25% by binder mass combined with water-to-binder ratios between 0.20 and 0.25 have been shown to maximize matrix density and improve fiber–matrix bond quality, yielding significant gains in flexural strength. Conventional regression-based mix design approaches often struggle to capture these complex, high-order interactions inherent in UHPC and are typically limited to narrowly defined fiber–matrix systems, highlighting the need for more advanced predictive tools. Moreover, most existing models fail to generalize across mixtures containing multiple fiber types and diverse SCM combinations. This gap in the literature urgently demands interpretable, data-driven methodologies specifically designed to address the multicomponent complexity of UHPC.

ML-based prediction of concrete mechanical properties

Several ML algorithms have been proposed to predict compressive and flexural strengths of cementitious composites based on mix-design parameters. Yang & Dong25 utilized Support Vector Machines (SVM) to model compressive strength of high-performance concrete, achieving R2 values above 0.85 on a dataset of 400 samples. Asteris et al.26 compared SVM, Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), and RF for predicting compressive strength of blended cement. The authors found that RF offered superior generalization performance with minimal hyperparameter tuning.

Recent studies have explored the use of ML-based models for flexural strength prediction in UHPC. Sun et al. (2023) trained an Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) model on 300 UHPC mixtures (including fiber and silica fume dosages), achieving R2 and RMSE values of 0.91 and 1.5 MPa for flexural strength predictions, respectively27. However, the authors did not incorporate systematic outlier removal, and interpretability analyses were limited to simple feature-importance rankings. Escalante-Tovar et al.28 applied ensemble learning models to predict the flexural behavior of fiber-reinforced UHPC, achieving a high accuracy with an R2 > 0.84. However, their approach remains largely a black box, offering limited interpretability and explainability of the model’s predictions.

The RF algorithm, introduced by Breiman in 200119, has gained recognition in concrete research due to its robustness and interpretability. Kang et al. (2021)29 used RF to predict the post-crack load–deflection behavior of fiber-reinforced concretes, achieving a mean absolute error (MAE) of 6.63 MPa and identifying fiber aspect ratio and matrix strength as the most influential factors. Although a few recent studies have employed RF with detailed UHPC datasets, comprehensive implementations involving both high-dimensional input variables and explainability frameworks (e.g., SHAP or PDP) are still limited in the literature.

RF and other tree-based algorithms offer distinct advantages for UHPC mix design by inherently modeling complex, nonlinear relationships and high‐order interactions among mix parameters without requiring explicit functional forms29. Their ensemble structure also confers robustness to noisy or incomplete datasets, reducing the need for extensive data imputation and preprocessing26. Moreover, RF provides built‐in feature‐importance metrics—such as Gini importance—that quantitatively rank each input’s contribution to predictive performance, thereby guiding targeted mix‐design optimization. When augmented with interpretability techniques like PDPs and SHAP values, RF models further reveal both global marginal effects and local, instance‐specific explanations of how individual variables influence flexural strength22.

Outlier detection and data preprocessing in concrete modeling

Quality and reliability of ML-based predictions heavily depend on the preprocessing steps applied to the raw dataset. Erroneous or extreme observations (outliers) can disproportionately influence model fitting, particularly for regression-based and tree-based algorithms. The Cook’s distance, proposed by Cook in 1977, assesses the influence of each data point on the estimated regression coefficients in a multiple linear regression context20. Observations with Cook’s distance > 4/n are usually considered “influential” and candidates for removal. In the context of concrete mix modeling, such extreme points might arise from incorrect material measurements, mixing inconsistencies, or lab testing errors.

In addition to outlier removal, standardized preprocessing such as handling missing values, encoding categorical features (e.g., fiber type) via one-hot encoding, and optional normalization can improve model performance and interpretability. While RF does not strictly require feature scaling, normalizing continuous variables (e.g., w/b ratio, fiber length) can facilitate clearer interpretability when analyzing PDPs and SHAP values.

Motivation

Although compressive strength has been widely studied and predicted through ML-based frameworks, flexural strength plays a distinct and critical role in UHPC design. In contrast to conventional reinforced concrete, UHPC often serves as a thin, fiber-reinforced structural element—such as precast bridge panels, façade plates, and deck overlays—where steel reinforcement is minimal or absent. The intrinsic flexural capacity of the UHPC matrix governs crack initiation, serviceability, and post-crack toughness. Accurate prediction of flexural strength therefore provides a direct tool for mixture optimization and for reducing experimental effort in developing high-ductility UHPC formulations.

Recent advances have further emphasized the importance of fiber geometry and hybrid reinforcement on UHPC flexural response. For example, hybrid steel–polypropylene fibers enhance post-crack ductility30, optimized fiber dispersion significantly improves tensile-to-flexural strength ratios31, and additional findings were reported regarding structural performance32 highlighted the influence of matrix rheology on fiber orientation. These recent findings strengthen the motivation of our study, which aims to deliver a data-driven predictive framework capable of capturing such nonlinear, fiber–matrix interactions in UHPC.

Research objectives

In this study, we develop and validate a RF model to predict the ultimate flexural strength of UHPC. Using a carefully cleaned dataset filtered by Cook’s distance, the model’s accuracy is evaluated with metrics like R², RMSE, and MAE on an independent test set. The study also identifies the most influential mix-design variables through feature importance scores and enhances interpretability with PDPs. Additionally, SHAP analysis is employed to examine both global and local effects of each parameter, helping to pinpoint optimal dosage ranges for maximizing flexural strength. Overall, the methodology integrates data cleaning, model optimization, and interpretability techniques to deliver a robust predictive tool and actionable insights for mix design refinement in UHPC.

Scope and significance of this study

ML models offer several key advantages for predicting the flexural strength of fiber-reinforced UHPC compared to traditional empirical or purely physics-based approaches. Although ML techniques have previously been applied to predict UHPC mechanical properties24, here we present a robust predictive framework that enhances the optimization of UHPC mix designs using RF. By analyzing a comprehensive set of input variables (i.e., fiber type, fiber dosage, and matrix composition)23, we identify the key factors that govern the flexural performance of fiber-reinforced UHPC. This extensive evaluation supports the development of high-performance UHPC formulations for critical structural applications such as bridge girders, pavements, and seismic-resistant elements.

Our approach integrates objective data curation with rigorous outlier removal, high-dimensional feature modeling, and complementary interpretability tools including PDPs and SHAP values. This combination not only delivers highly accurate predictions of flexural strength but also provides mechanistic insights that go beyond simple variable importance rankings. Specifically, it reveals nonlinear interactions and saturation thresholds—such as fiber dosages exceeding 1.5% or cement replacement levels above 30%—thus enabling more precise, data-driven calibration of UHPC mixtures than conventional empirical methods. Furthermore, PDP visualization facilitates a clear understanding of the complex relationships between key material properties and flexural response, empowering mix designers with actionable knowledge to optimize UHPC performance. By advancing both predictive accuracy and interpretability, this study significantly contributes to the application of machine learning in civil engineering, offering an efficient and insightful tool for the design of UHPC tailored to demanding structural requirements.

Methods

Data compilation

The dataset employed in this study comprises 550 distinct UHPC mix designs compiled from multiple peer-reviewed experimental investigations published between 2010 and 202433–88. Each source provided comprehensive information, including detailed mixture proportions, curing regimes, and mechanical performance results. Data extraction was performed through a manual systematic review of peer-reviewed publications indexed in Scopus, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect between 2010 and 2024 using the keywords “UHPC”, “fiber-reinforced concrete”, and “flexural strength”. Studies were included if they reported complete mix proportions (cement, silica fume, aggregates, water, fibers, and superplasticizer) together with corresponding flexural strength results obtained from standardized flexural tests (ASTM C1609 or equivalent). Duplicated datasets, incomplete mix-design information, or non-standard testing methods were excluded. To ensure quality control, all extracted data were independently verified by two co-authors and cross-checked against the original tables and figures of the source articles. Only datasets with consistent units and clearly defined curing conditions were retained, thereby ensuring transparency, reproducibility, and reliability of the compiled database. To identify influential observations in our UHPC data set, an exploratory multiple linear regression model was fitted to predict flexural strength based on the available predictors (Table 1). Although we acknowledge that UHPC behavior is inherently nonlinear, the goal of this step was not to model the flexural response precisely but to identify data points with excessive leverage or residual error that might distort subsequent modeling.

All observations were inspected individually. Many exhibited either unrealistically high or low flexural strength values (> 48 MPa or < 2 MPa) combined with anomalous fiber or water dosages, suggesting likely errors in experimental design, unit conversion, or documentation. To assess the effect of outlier removal, we trained two RF models under identical hyperparameter settings: one on the original dataset (n = 550) and another on the cleaned data set (n = 522). The average test R², calculated via 10-fold cross-validation, increased from 0.832 for the original data set to 0.872 for the cleaned data. This improvement indicates that removing influential points reduced model variance and enhanced generalization. Additionally, the root mean squared error (RMSE) decreased from 3.72 MPa to 3.10 MPa, and the MAE dropped from 2.45 MPa to 2.17 MPa, further demonstrating the effectiveness of the data cleaning process based on the Cook’s distance. Outliers and influential observations introduce noise and increase variability in the dataset, which creates uncertainty during modeling and reduces the reliability of predictions. For instance, Isolation Forest has been successfully applied to remove outliers when developing a more robust and reliable XGBoost model for predicting the shear strength of headed studs in UHPC89. Similarly, our approach of removing influential observations based on Cook’s distance has the potential to enhance the robustness of our RF model predictions, potentially leading to better material design options. Moreover, since these observations often contribute to overfitting, their removal can improve model performance. For example, after influential observations were removed, a Gradient Boosting model used to predict the flexural strength of UHPC demonstrated significant improvements in both precision and generalization90. This suggests that effective outlier handling could similarly enhance our RF model’s ability to generalize new data.

Machine learning model

Algorithm development

One of the primary goals of this study is to develop a robust and interpretable ML architecture to accurately predict the flexural strength of fiber-reinforced UHPC as a function of various mix design parameters. To accomplish this, we employed an RF regression, an ensemble learning technique based on decision-tree regressors proposed by Breiman19, and recognized for performing well in engineering applications due to its ability to manage complex nonlinear relationships and noisy data without extensive preprocessing and feature scaling.

This ensemble approach mitigates the variance associated with single-tree models and is particularly effective for datasets with high dimensionality and multicollinearity, as is often the case in UHPC research involving dozens of interacting parameters.

We used a 10-fold cross-validation (CV) procedure to assess generalization performance of our RF model. In addition, 104 randomly selected samples (~ 20%) were reserved as an independent hold-out test set, which was not used during model training or hyperparameter tuning. The remaining 418 samples (~ 80%) were used exclusively for training and cross-validation.

To improve model accuracy, the key hyperparameters for the RF included the number of trees (T), the maximum depth of trees, the minimum number of samples needed to split an internal node, and the number of features considered. These parameters were tuned using a grid search approach. We evaluated multiple RF configurations across a predefined hyperparameter space given by n_estimators ∈ {100, 200, 300} and max_depth ∈ {3, 5, 10, None}. This setup resulted in 12 different model configurations, which were assessed using grid-search with CV to identify the best-performing combination.

The grid search for RF hyperparameters was performed using only the 418 training samples via 10-fold CV, and the model with optimal configuration was then evaluated on the testing dataset. This hybrid approach ensures unbiased estimation of final performance while still benefiting from robust hyperparameter tuning. The test set split was stratified to maintain distributional balance across the flexural strength range. All metrics reported below clearly distinguish between CV results and hold-out performance to avoid conflation.

A key strength of RF models is their built-in feature importance measures. The variable importance of each input is quantified by its average contribution to the overall reduction of impurity across all decision trees in the forest. This provides an initial insight into mixture components most strongly influence flexural strength. Application of the RF model

Feature importance

Figure 7 shows the feature importance chart for the proposed RF model. Our results suggest that fiber content (X21 and X20) dominates with importance scores of ~ 0.31 and ~ 0.08, respectively, confirming that crack-bridging by high‐aspect‐ratio steel fibers is the primary strength driver92.

Variable importance of most influential features included in the proposed RF model. Here, the strength of each variable’s predictive contribution CX is categorized as Very weak (CX < 0.02), Weak (0.02 ≤ CX < 0.07), Average (0.07 ≤ CX < 0.12), Strong (0.12 ≤ CX < 0.20) and Very strong (CX ≥ 0.20).

Although X20 and X21 both relate to fiber dosage, they also capture different structural dimensions: X20 represents the fiber volume fraction, while X21 denotes the fiber reinforcement factor—calculated as volume fraction × aspect ratio—which quantifies the effective crack-bridging capability. Their inclusion allows the RF model to learn both the quantity and geometry-influenced effectiveness of the fibers. Interestingly, a control model trained with only X20 showed a test R² of 0.861 versus 0.872 with both features, indicating that X21 adds distinct predictive value. In addition, cement fraction (X1) accounts for ~ 0.21 of the total importance, quantifying how binder volume controls matrix cohesion and hydration product formation93. These two variables together explain over half of the model’s variance reduction, reflecting the well-established synergy between fiber reinforcement and cementitious matrix densification in achieving ultra‐high performance.

Beyond these primary drivers, other middle tier of features include silica fume (X2 ~ 0.06), which refines pore structure through pozzolanic C–S–H formation94; superplasticizer dosage (X12, ~ 0.05), which optimizes workability and particle packing95; maximum aggregate size (X17, ~ 0.05) and sand fraction (X15, ~ 0.04), which enhance microstructural interlock96; water content (X11, ~ 0.045) and water-to-binder ratio (X18, ~ 0.05), which jointly regulate porosity and hydration completeness23; and baseline compressive strength (X19, ~ 0.045), which reflects the well-known coupling between compressive and flexural behavior97. All remaining features contribute negligibly (< 0.02), suggesting that, while they may affect individual predictions or interactions, they play a minor role in the global model. Overall, these results give concrete mix designers a clear, hierarchy-driven roadmap for prioritizing fiber and binder optimization, followed by targeted tuning of silica fume, admixtures, and aggregate gradation to achieve maximum flexural performance.

Discussion

Fiber-reinforced Ultra-High-Performance Concrete (UHPC) is an advanced material known for its ultra-high compressive strength, enhanced ductility, and durability, with fibers (steel or synthetic) improving post-crack behavior and flexural toughness for better bending and cyclic response15. Accurate prediction of flexural strength in fiber-reinforced UHPC is vital for advancing the material’s adoption in critical structural applications. UHPC exhibits exceptional mechanical performance due to its dense matrix and fiber reinforcement but presents significant challenges in predictive modelling due to complex, nonlinear interactions between mix constituents, including supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) and diverse fiber types. Traditional empirical approaches are often inadequate for capturing these interactions comprehensively. This study addresses these challenges by developing a robust Random Forest (RF) machine learning (ML) model trained on an extensive, curated experimental dataset.

Our proposed RF-based predictive framework, trained and tested on a curated dataset of 522 UHPC mixtures refined via Cook’s distance, demonstrated strong predictive capacity for flexural strength, not only achieves high accuracy (R² ≈ 0.87, RMSE ≈ 3.1 MPa, and MAE ≈ 2.2 MPa on an independent test set; Figs. 5 and 6), but also offers interpretable insights into the influence of key parameters (Figs. 7 and 8), thereby facilitating optimized UHPC mix designs that reduce experimental costs and support sustainable, large-scale application. Although we systematically fitted several ML models to predict flexural strength both with and without outlier removal using Cook’s distance, our results demonstrate that, (1) overall, the RF model outperforms the other evaluated models; (2) after removing outliers, the average R2 for all models remains statistically similar; and (3) cross-validation results showed consistent performance with low variance, indicating model stability and generalizability across a wide range of mixture designs (Figs. 2S and 3S, Supplementary Material). These findings support the robustness of the RF model, thereby strengthening our conclusion regarding its effectiveness for predicting flexural strength. Interestingly, removal of 28 influential outliers based on Cook’s distance significantly reduced model variance and improved prediction accuracy, highlighting the importance of rigorous data curation in enhancing machine learning (ML) reliability for cementitious materials.

Feature importance and interpretability analyses of the RF predictive framework identified fiber volume fraction and the fiber reinforcement factor as the primary predictors, jointly explaining over half of the model variance (Fig. 7). These findings reinforce the critical role of high-aspect-ratio steel fibers in crack bridging and post-crack ductility enhancement. Cement content ranked second in importance, reflecting its influence on matrix cohesion and hydration product formation. Secondary contributors included silica fume content, water-to-binder ratio, superplasticizer dosage, aggregate characteristics, and baseline compressive strength, all of which affect matrix densification, workability, and fiber–matrix interactions.

Explainability approaches helped quantify the impact of features on the flexural strength: SHAP quantified both global and local feature impacts (Fig. 8), while partial dependence plots (PDPs) revealed optimal parameter ranges (i.e., fiber volume fraction between 1.0% and 1.5%, cement content of 25–30%, silica fume dosage of 10–15%, and water-to-binder ratios of 0.15–0.18)(Figs. 9 and 10). Beyond these thresholds, flexural strength gains plateau or slightly decline, likely due to fiber clustering, matrix microcracking, increased porosity, or workability loss. Notably, excessive superplasticizer dosage (> 3%) negatively impacted strength due to segregation and air entrainment. Bivariate partial dependence plots elucidated synergistic effects between cement and silica fume content, fiber dosage, and superplasticizer-water ratio combinations, underlining the multifactorial nature of UHPC optimization (Figs. 9 and 10).

Moreover, the use of PDP and SHAP in this study helped us gain a clearer and more practical understanding of how UHPC mixtures behave. While these tools are well known in the field of ML, their application to such a comprehensive and diverse dataset allowed us to uncover several meaningful patterns that go beyond mere statistics and touch on the material’s underlying mechanisms.

One of the most striking findings was the presence of saturation effects: increasing the fiber content beyond about 2.0% by volume, or the silica fume content above roughly 18% by binder volume, led to only marginal improvements in flexural strength. This suggests that, after a certain point, more of these components doesn’t necessarily mean better performance—likely because of issues such as fiber clumping, poor dispersion, or a weakened interface between the fiber and the matrix.

We also observed a complex interaction between cement content and the water-to-binder (W/B) ratio. Although lowering W/B is generally associated with higher strength, this benefit levels off—or even reverses—if the cement content is too high. This behavior likely reflects a balance between improved packing or hydration on one hand, and the risk of early-age shrinkage or poor workability on the other. It’s a reminder that in UHPC design, components shouldn’t be optimized in isolation, but as part of an interdependent system.

Another interesting insight came from the role of the superplasticizer. Its effect on strength wasn’t consistent across all mixes. In some cases, its contribution was minimal, while in others it played a more decisive role. This context-dependent behavior highlights the importance of considering not just the dosage of admixtures, but how they interact with other mix parameters such as W/B and powder content.

This study provides actionable guidelines for UHPC mix design. First and foremost, fiber dosage is the dominant factor: both SHAP values (Fig. 8) and PDPs (Figs. 9 and 10) plots show that a volumetric fiber fraction of 1.0–1.5% maximizes flexural strength, as steel fibers in this range from an interconnected network that efficiently arrests crack propagation without compromising workability, whereas dosages above 1.5% yield diminishing returns and increased clustering. Second, cement content should be set at 25%–30% of the total paste volume to ensure sufficient C–S–H development for matrix cohesion without excessive shrinkage or fiber dispersion issues. Third, silica fume—a highly reactive pozzolan—must be included at or above 9% by binder volume to refine the pore structure and enhance the fiber–matrix interface; lower dosages do not fully realize its micro‑filling and pozzolanic benefits. Fourth, a water‑to‑binder ratio of 0.15–0.18 strikes the optimal balance between complete hydration and particle packing, whereas higher ratios introduce capillary porosity that undermines both matrix and fiber performance. Finally, superplasticizer dosages up to 3% by binder mass are recommended to maintain workability at low W/B ratios and achieve uniform fiber dispersion; exceeding this threshold can lead to segregation or air entrainment that reduces strength. By adhering to these parameter windows, practitioners can tailor UHPC mixtures to deliver consistently high, predictable flexural performance while minimizing the need for extensive trial‑and‑error testing.

Although the RF model is robust, the dataset primarily included steel and select synthetic fibers and common SCMs. Future studies should extend the framework to incorporate diverse fiber types—including polymeric, hybrid, and recycled fibers—and alternative SCMs such as metakaolin and high-volume fly ash, broadening model applicability. Integration with real-time production monitoring and transfer learning to adapt predictions across curing regimes or scales presents promising directions. Continuous expansion and refinement of data-driven tools will further support the design and deployment of next-generation UHPC materials in diverse industrial environments.

Conclusions and perspectives

A Random Forest (RF) regression model, trained and tested on a curated dataset of 522 UHPC mixtures refined via Cook’s distance to remove influential outliers, demonstrated strong predictive capacity for flexural strength. The proposed model achieved R² ≈ 0.87, RMSE ≈ 3.1 MPa, and MAE ≈ 2.2 MPa on an independent test set. These performance metrics confirm the model’s robustness and reliability, indicating its effective applicability for design-oriented forecasting with minimal generalization error.

Global feature importance analysis identified fiber volume fraction and cement content as the primary predictors, jointly accounting for over 50% of the variance reduction. Silica fume content, water-to-binder ratio, and superplasticizer dosage contributed to a secondary tier of influence.

Interpretability analyses, including SHAP and PDPs, revealed optimal ranges for key input parameters—such as 1.0–1.5 vol% fibers, 25–30% cement, ≥ 9% silica fume, and w/b ratio between 0.15–0.18—beyond which performance gains diminish due to issues like segregation, excessive porosity, or loss of workability. These insights provide actionable guidance for targeted mix optimization. Moreover, the integrated modelling framework offers a quantitative pathway to enhance UHPC design by prioritizing reinforcement and matrix densification, followed by precise water and admixture control.
