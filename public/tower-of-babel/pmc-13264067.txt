Introduction

Glycopeptides are a type of macromolecules that are created when polypeptides and oligosaccharides form a chemical bond. They have a wide range of biological activities and pharmacological effects, as well as may be useful in treating a number of illnesses, including infection, inflammation, and cancer [1], [2], [3]. Ginseng glycopeptides have the ability to restore pancreatic islet function and inhibit and prevent diabetes consequences. With a molecular weight ranging from 200 to 50,000 Da, their structure is made up of 17 different types of amino acids and monosaccharides like mannose, rhamnose, and galactose [4]. They can be separated by water extraction, alcohol precipitation, and membrane separation. Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptides are classified as glycosylated peptides, which are glycopeptide complexes formed as polysaccharides and oligopeptides connect by O-glycosidic bonds. In the mycelium of Ganoderma lucidum, a high-molecular glycopeptide polymer, Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptide has a peptide content of roughly 30% and a polysaccharide content of roughly 60% [5]. With a molecular weight of about 100 k Da, it is stable in nature and extremely soluble in water. Mycelial fermentation and nanofiltration concentration techniques are used to separate it [6]. Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptide is a biological regulator that regulates the body's immune system through humoral and cellular immunity, increases the release of TNF-α and IL-2, stimulates the proliferation of T lymphocytes, activates immune cells like NK cells and macrophages, counteracts the immunosuppression caused by chemotherapy and radiation therapy in cancer patients, and indirectly destroys tumor cells to help treat cancer [7], [8]. Glycopeptides' spatial structure has a direct impact on their biological function. Therefore, to guarantee the structural stability of glycopeptide components, room temperature nanofiltration concentration is used in the industrial manufacturing process. Nevertheless, the properties of high viscosity and turbidity in glycopeptide solutions cause significant membrane fouling [9]. The conventional solvent cleaning procedure states that the manufacturing company has to replace the nanofiltration membrane when the membrane flux drops by more than 50%. However, the manufacturing cost ranges from 7,500 to 15,000 US dollars, and the replacement frequency is 1–2 times per month. However, the contaminated nanofiltration membrane can't be reusable, which raises the company's production costs in addition to causing pollution and membrane material waste [10].

Nanofiltration is a membrane separation method with molecular weight ranges from 100 to 1000 Da. It has clear technological advantages in the separation and refining of heat-sensitive components since it separates at room temperature through pore size sieving effects and charge repulsion [11], [12], [13]. Nanofiltration's room-temperature separation advantage provides for the proper separation of flavor components in tobacco extracts at room temperature [14]. Based on the charge repulsion effect of nanofiltration, it can efficiently remove ions from sardine frying wastewater while effectively recovering bioactive proteins and peptides [15]. However, due to nanofiltration is a pressure-driven membrane separation technology, it is difficult to prevent solute deposition and contamination on the membrane surface and within membrane pores in actual applications. This results in a higher frequency of cleaning, a lower separation flux, affects the service life of nanofiltration membranes, and raises operational expenses for businesses [16]. Adding scale inhibitors or regularly washing the nanofiltration membrane can help reduce membrane fouling during the separation of Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptides [17]. Scale inhibitors, however, are novel pollutants that could affect the structural stability of Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptides. In order to restore membrane flux, solvent cleaning of nanofiltration membranes can be used for secondary dissolving of nanofiltration contaminants. Nevertheless, Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptides are adsorbed on the membrane surface via hydrogen bonding, resulting in a gel layer that makes solvent cleaning an ineffective method of restoring membrane flux [18].

Ultrasonic waves, as mechanical waves, are accompanied by the formation, expansion, and fast collapse of small bubbles within liquids, resulting in ultrasonic cavitation effects with severe impact and shearing forces on liquid and solid surfaces [19], [20]. The yield of Jabuticaba peels was raised by 45% while preserving the antioxidant activity of the polyphenols through the use of ultrasonic-assisted extraction in conjunction with a 200 Da polyamide nanofiltration concentration of polyphenols [21].

The application of nanofiltration technology in the field of chemical separation is limited by the flux reduction brought on by the adsorption of solutes in the separation layer during the separation process. Previous studies have demonstrated that ultrasonic-assisted membrane separation reduces solute adsorption, minimizes membrane fouling, and enhances solute and solvent mass transfer efficiency. The membrane surface adsorption rate during the nanofiltration concentration of flavonoids from Cassia seeds is decreased by bidirectionally regulating the π-π accumulation effect of chrysophanol and aurantio-obtusin [22]. When salvianolic acid B was separated from ethanol solution using ultrasonic-assisted nanofiltration, it was discovered that increasing the charge repulsion efficiency increased the recovery rate of salvianolic acid B. This resulted in the gelation of ionized salvianolic acid on the nanofiltration membrane surface and membrane fouling [23]. The technical problem of concentrating salvianolic acid B at room temperature was resolved by using the cavitation effect of ultrasound to break up the gel layer on the nanofiltration membrane surface. The surface erosion effect caused by ultrasonic cavitation has the potential to damage the gel layer on the surface of nanofiltration membranes [24]. Thus, while lowering membrane fouling and improving separation efficiency, ultrasonic-assisted nanofiltration separation can also explore the fouling process of nanofiltration membranes. The study of the nanofiltration fouling mechanism of Ganoderma lucidum fermentation solution is made technically feasible by the cavitation effect of ultrasonic waves, which breaks the physical adsorption on the membrane surface based on the dynamic adsorption process of solutes in the membrane separation layer.

The efficiency of separation is dependent on the operating pressure and nanofiltration molecular weight cut-off (MWCO). However, the concentration polarization caused by the operating pressure during the separation process will accelerate the gel layer formation and membrane fouling, which is another significant technical problem limiting the concentration of Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptides at room temperature. The effects of ultrasonic power, operating pressure, and MWCO on membrane flux and recovery rate of Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptides were systematically investigated in order to elucidate the influence rule of ultrasound-assisted nanofiltration concentration efficiency of Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptides. Additionally, the separation parameters of ultrasound-assisted nanofiltration concentration of Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptides were optimized. In the meantime, by contrasting the variations in concentration kinetics, concentration polarization effect, and membrane fouling kinetics with the conventional nanofiltration concentration of Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptides, the separation mechanism and membrane fouling regulation mode of the ultrasound-assisted nanofiltration concentration of Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptides were made clear [25], [26], [27]. This provided technical support for the room-temperature separation of high-viscosity materials and the cleaning of nanofiltration membranes in the food and pharmaceutical production fields.

Ultrasonic assisted nanofiltration separation

The viability of using ultrasound to reduce membrane contamination and increase separation efficiency has been investigated in the process of ultrasound-assisted nanofiltration for concentrating the Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptide fermentation solution in order to decrease membrane surface contamination caused by concentration polarization and increase the mass transfer efficiency of contaminants on the nanofiltration membrane surface. The nanofiltration membrane is positioned inside the ultrasonic apparatus during the ultrasonic-assisted separation process. The SCQ-9200E ultrasonic equipment was obtained from Shanghai Shengyan Ultrasonic Instrument Co., Ltd. The ultrasonic equipment is equipped with a bath tank. Ultrasonic power varies from 80 W to 800 W, with a frequency of 40 kHz, power density of 0.04–––0.44 W/cm2, and amplitudes of 1.40–––28.28 μm. The Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptide fermentation solution is put in the storage tank, as shown in Fig. 1. The medium-pressure pump provides the pressure, which prompts the fermentation solution to go sequentially through the medium-pressure pump, pressure gauge, nanofiltration membrane, and valve in the medium-pressure pipeline. The nanofiltration solution and the intercepted liquid, respectively, enter the storage tank and the nanofiltration solution tank based on the nanofiltration separation. The ultrasonic apparatus uses circulating water to maintain the nanofiltration separation temperature between 18 °C and 25 °C in order to prevent the thermal effects of ultrasonic cavitation from affecting the stability of Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptides during the ultrasonic-assisted separation process. The solute rejection and concentration polarization effect are two separation factors that are connected to the MWCO during the nanofiltration separation process. With a rejection of 90% on the rejection curve, the solute's relative molecular weight is referred to as the membrane MWCO. The effectiveness of the membrane's barrier against the target material during the separation process is referred to as the retention rate. In pressure-driven membrane separation, a process known as concentration polarization occurs when solutes are trapped by the membrane and enriched on the membrane surface, creating a boundary layer with a concentration greater than that of the main solution. Membrane fouling is the phenomenon wherein particles, colloids, or macromolecules of solutes in the solution are adsorbed and deposited on the membrane surface or in the membrane pores as a result of mechanical, chemical, or physical actions during the membrane filtration process, which lowers the membrane flux. A decrease in membrane flux of more than 40% is typically regarded as serious fouling.

The separation efficiency of Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptides is directly correlated with ultrasonic power, nanofiltration MWCO, and TMP during the ultrasound-assisted nanofiltration process. The influence laws of ultrasonic power, MWCO, and TMP on nanofiltration separation efficiency were fully examined using membrane flux, rejection rates, and adsorption rates of triterpenoids, sugars, and proteins as an evaluation index. Ultrasonic power regulates the mass transfer efficiency of solutes and solvents on nanofiltration membrane surfaces. Based on the power range of the ultrasonic apparatus, the single-factor experiment explores the effects of ultrasonic power ranging from 100 W to 800 W on membrane fouling and mass transfer efficiency. To examine the impact of nanofiltration membranes with MWCO of 100 Da − 800 Da and TMP of 0.4 MPa − 2.0 MPa on membrane flux and solute rejection rate. In the meantime, the charge repulsion effect of nanofiltration is related to the pH of the solution. The durability of Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptides has been studied concurrently with the effects of solution pH varying from 4.00 to 9.00 on the adsorption and mass transfer processes of solutes. The fixed parameters of ultrasonic-assisted nanofiltration separation for the single-factor experiment were 0 W of ultrasonic power, 450 Da of MWCO, 1.0 MPa of TMP, and pH 6.00.

Optimal concentration parameters by response surface method

The response surface method uses a quadratic regression equation to determine the nonlinear relationship between variables and response values. It is widely used in the pharmaceutical business to assess process parameters [28]. Simultaneously, it may examine how many factors interact and affect response values. Ultrasonic power (X1), TMP (X2), and pH (X3) were chosen as independent variables based on the results of the single-factor testing, whereas membrane flux, triterpenoids rejection, sugars rejection, and proteins rejection were chosen as response values.

With 17 groups of experiments, a three-factor, three-level experiment was developed. A mathematical model for the concentration of Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptides by ultrasonic-assisted nanofiltration was constructed by averaging the three repeats of each experiment group. According to Equation (4), the main effects, interaction, and quadratic effect are all included in the quadratic polynomial that describes the mathematical relationship between the response value and the variable. The correlation coefficients of the effects mentioned above are used to assess the degree to which the variable and interaction contributed to the response value. On the basis of the mathematical relationship between variables and response values, a response surface model was developed. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze the model's statistical significance, and P-values were used to assess the significance of the variables and interactions.

Concentration polarization model

During the nanofiltration concentration process, solutes build up on the membrane surface due to the interaction of pressure drive and nanofiltration retention, greatly increasing the concentration. Concentration polarization occurs when the transmembrane pressure difference remains constant, causing a decrease in membrane flux and an increase in solute permeability. Due to the buildup of solutes in the membrane separation layer, this not only lowers the nanofiltration concentration efficiency but also results in membrane fouling. The dynamic change in the concentration of solute from the nanofiltration interface (Cm) to the solution (Cb) is a characteristic of the concentration polarization effect [31]. It is challenging to assess the Cm changes brought on by concentration polarization directly because the solute concentration in the interface layer cannot be obtained immediately. Sample analysis

Results and discussion

Single-factor test

MWCO

Nanofiltration membranes' MWCO directly influences the solute rejection and controls the rate of concentration and separation. The effect of MWCO on membrane flux was analyzed using one-way ANOVA. According to Table 3, there was a significant rise in membrane flux (P < 0.05) from 7.16 L/m2·h to 14.65 L/m2·h between 100 Da and 800 Da. Concurrently, the rejection of sugars dropped from 99.3% to 87.5%, while the rejection of triterpenoids and proteins stayed over 95%. This suggests that the fermentation broth included about 10% monosaccharides and disaccharides. Using a nanofiltration membrane with the proper MWCO, low-molecular-weight oligosaccharides can be successfully removed from the fermentation broth. The MWCO of the nanofiltration membrane and the solute adsorption rate exhibit a certain positive relationship, suggesting that a rise in the MWCO considerably enhances the nanofiltration membrane's effective adsorption area [18]. However, there is very little variation in the solute adsorption rate. Triterpenoids > sugars > proteins, according to the adsorption rate ordering of the components in the fermentation broth. Depending on the solute concentration in the fermentation broth, triterpenoids and sugar components could be the main causes of membrane fouling.

TMP

Although separation pressure is the most straightforward way to speed up separation, it has multiple impacts on Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptide nanofiltration concentration efficiency. Table 3 shows that the membrane flux rose significantly (P < 0.05) from 7.96 L/m2·h to 16.83 L/m2·h as TMP progressively increased from 0.4 MPa to 2.0 MPa. Nonetheless, there was a negative trend in the rejections of sugars, proteins, and triterpenoids. The rejections of sugars and triterpenoids among them dropped rather sharply, suggesting that the separation pressure exacerbated the polarization impact of nanofiltration concentration. Solutes build up on the membrane surface as a result [33]. The percentage of solutes that flow through the nanofiltration membrane's pores rises under the solution-diffusion separation effect [34]. As operating pressure rises, solute adsorption rates increase as well. The growth of membrane flux tends to level out after exceeding 1.2 MPa, according to a comparison of the variation trends in membrane flux. This suggests that the accumulation of solutes in the separation layer is what causes the increase in adsorption rate and the decrease in membrane flux. The fouling of nanofiltration membranes will be made severe by operating pressure.

Ultrasonic power

The ultrasonic cavitation effect can improve the diffusion efficiency of solutes on the surface of nanofiltration membranes in a solution environment that is rich in protein and sugar. It can also lessen the application constraints of solution viscosity that limit membrane flux. The membrane flux increased from 14.65 L/m2·h to 22.07 L/m2·h and then declined to 19.61 L/m2·h in the parallel separation experiment of 2 L of Ganoderma lucidum fermentation solution separated by an 800 Da nanofiltration membrane as the ultrasonic power increased from 0 W to 800 W (Table 3). When ultrasonic power ranged from 300 W to 800 W, membrane flux significantly raised compared to 0 W (P < 0.05). The mass transfer efficiency of solutes can be accelerated by the ultrasonic cavitation effect. Pollutants move from the membrane to the solution when the ultrasonic power grows from 0 W to 400 W, increasing the effective filtration area and membrane flux [22]. Membrane flux decreases as the ultrasonic power rises from 400 W to 800 W because there is an increased probability that insoluble particles in the Ganoderma lucidum fermentation solution will enter the nanofiltration membrane pores.

The nanofiltration adsorption rates of the three components in the fermentation broth all exhibited a behavior of initially decreasing and then increasing, indicating that while ultrasound-assisted membrane separation reduces membrane fouling, it may potentially increase membrane fouling. The gel layer on the surface of nanofiltration membranes can be destroyed by the impact and shear forces caused by ultrasonic cavitation [35]. The adsorption rate decreases as the gel layer peels off because the membrane flux rises and the solute encapsulated in the gel layer is released back into the solution. The adsorption–desorption of pollutants on the membrane surface, however, tends to balance as the ultrasonic power increases. As a result, the membrane separation layer's adsorption sites also grow, suggesting a marginally increasing trend in the solute adsorption rate. Therefore, while adjusting the nanofiltration concentration efficiency of Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptides using ultrasound, the opposing relationship between membrane flux and solute retention rate must be adjusted.

pH

The pH of the solution has an impact on the charge effect during nanofiltration separation. Raising the pH of the fermentation broth from 4.00 to 9.00 and increasing the nanofiltration membrane flux from 10.74 L/m2·h to 17.71 L/m2·h (P < 0.05) in Table 3 demonstrates that alkaline conditions are advantageous for the secondary dissolution of contaminants accumulated on the membrane surface, which reduces membrane fouling and increases membrane flux. Meanwhile, the hydroxyl groups in the chemical structures of triterpenoids and sugar components in Ganoderma lucidum are both weakly acidic. The adsorption rate decreases when the pH rises because sugar and triterpenoids become more soluble. This demonstrates again that the main causes of contamination of nanofiltration membranes are triterpenoids and sugars. Triterpenoids and sugars ionize, intensifying the charge repulsion effect between them and the nanofiltration membrane and increasing both rejections. However, compared to triterpenoids, the protein's rejections and adsorption rates varied in different ways. The amphoteric character of the protein structure may be the cause of phenomenon [36]. The interaction between the protein and the nanofiltration membrane is enhanced in alkaline environments, which raises the adsorption rate and lowers the rejection.

Ganoderma lucidum fermentation solution's nanofiltration concentration process parameters were improved in order to resolve the separation contradiction between solute rejection and membrane flux. Based on the results of single-factor tests, the dynamic variations in response values (rejection and membrane flux) were investigated when TMP was between 0.6 and 1.4 MPa, ultrasonic power was between 200 and 600 W, and pH was between 6.00 and 8.00. The variables' levels were shown as (−1,0,1). In order to calculate the mathematical equation between the variables and the response values and optimize the separation parameters of the nanofiltration concentrated Ganoderma lucidum fermentation solution, a three-factor and three-level experimental design was developed using the response surface method.

Effect of ultrasonic power on nanofiltration concentration kinetics

The application of effective thermal concentration technique is restricted by the heat-sensitive properties of glycopeptide components in Ganoderma lucidum fermentation broth. Furthermore, the high viscosity of the solution renders it susceptible to concentration polarization during the separation process, which limits the application of room-temperature nanofiltration concentration technology and causes membrane flux attenuation. In order to lessen the concentration polarization effect and improve the nanofiltration concentration process, ultrasonic waves can break up the gel layer on the membrane surface. Nevertheless, it is still unknown how ultrasonic power and concentration efficiency are related. By examining the dynamic changes in solute concentration over time during the concentration process, creating the kinetic equation of nanofiltration concentration, and examining the connection between ultrasonic power and the concentration rate constant, it is helpful to comprehend how ultrasonic power controls the concentration efficiency of nanofiltration. The concentration kinetics equation's regression coefficients were all greater than 0.9 under a range of ultrasonic powers, suggesting the establishment of the ultrasound-assisted nanofiltration concentration kinetics model of Ganoderma lucidum glycopeptides (Fig. 2). The cavitation effect caused by ultrasonic waves increases the mass transfer efficiency of pollutants on the membrane surface. Nevertheless, when the ultrasonic power increases, the concentration rate constant decreases, indicating that a higher mass transfer efficiency also increases the probability of contamination and clogging within the nanofiltration membrane pores, which in turn impacts the nanofiltration concentration efficiency [37].

Ultrasound may enhance the solute's mass transfer coefficient, as can be shown from the data in Table S4. In addition to lowering the solute concentration in the interface layer, ultrasound increases the solute's permeability to the membrane, resulting in to some solutes loss.

Conclusion

Industrial difficulties such membrane fouling and poor separation efficiency arise when the Ganoderma lucidum fermentation solution, which is rich in protein and sugar components, is concentrated by nanofiltration at room temperature. Additionally, the difficulty of regenerating discarded nanofiltration membranes results in environmental degradation, and the frequent replacement of nanofiltration membranes raises separation costs. Ultrasonic-assisted nanofiltration separation was used to increase the mass transfer efficiency of solute on the membrane surface and decrease membrane fouling in order to create an environmentally friendly, effective, and room-temperature nanofiltration concentration model for Ganoderma lucidum fermentation broth. The ultrasonic-assisted membrane separation parameters were determined to be TMP 0.6 MPa, ultrasonic power 432 W, and pH 8.00 using single-factor test and response surface method analysis. The rejections of triterpenoids, proteins, and sugars were 99.7%–94.4%, 97.8%–95.2%, and 91.8%–85.7%, respectively, as the volume of the fermentation broth increased from 2 L to 50 L. The membrane flux was 19.23 L/m2·h–13.97 L/m2·h. Triterpenoids, proteins, and sugar components had adsorption rates of 10.5%–0.8%, 2.1%–0.1%, and 3.9%–0.2%, respectively. In ultrasonic-assisted nanofiltration separation, the cavitation effect can speed up solute mass transfer. On the one hand, the efficiency of separation is improved by solute diffusion into the nanofiltration membrane. Conversely, the effective filtration area of nanofiltration is released as contaminants move into the solution. The industrial production cycle of Ganoderma lucidum fermentation solution is shortened by the combination of technologies, which highlights the room-temperature concentration advantage of nanofiltration technology. Nevertheless, the combination of ultrasonic and nanofiltration separation not only improves the separation efficiency but also raises the possibility that insoluble particles in the Ganoderma lucidum fermentation solution will enter the membrane pore size, indicating the dual effect of ultrasonic power in controlling the concentration efficiency. The associated concentration constant exhibits a declining trend when the ultrasonic power above 500 W. Ultrasonic-coupled nanofiltration concentration can greatly improve the efficiency of nanofiltration separation, decrease solute adsorption rate, shorten concentration time, and raise component recovery rate and membrane flow when compared to conventional nanofiltration concentration.

Currently, the cavitation effect of ultrasonic waves causes micro-jets, shock waves, and strong shear forces that accelerate the shedding of contaminants on the membrane surface and lessen the degree of membrane fouling [47]. The relationship between the pollutants' chemical structure and the membrane material, however, has not been thoroughly explored. This research explains the contamination mechanisms of physical and chemical adsorption between solute structures and nanofiltration membranes, compares the adsorption kinetics, concentration polarization coefficients, and concentration kinetics changes of triterpenoids, proteins, and sugars caused by ultrasound, and develops a novel approach for the investigation of contamination kinetics in membrane separation. The triterpenoids and sugar components were primarily physically adsorbed on the surface of the composite polyamide membrane material, whereas the protein components were primarily chemically adsorbed with physical adsorption as a supplement, according to the adsorption kinetics model of the solute and the concentration rate constant. Pressure-induced concentration polarization leads solutes to accumulate on the nanofiltration membrane's surface. Compared to proteins and triterpenoids, sugars have a substantially greater concentration polarization coefficient. Membrane flux decreases as a result of the high-viscosity gel layer that forms, which prevents the solvent from passing through the nanofiltration membrane. Ultrasound can improve membrane fouling and membrane flux by lowering the concentration polarization effect and promoting the desorption of triterpenoids, sugars, and some protein components when combined with the microscopic characteristics of the membrane surface. However, there are limitations on the regulation of membrane fouling when ultrasonic-assisted nanofiltration concentration is used. To deal with the problem of nanofiltration membrane recycling, chemical solvents must be employed for cleaning in order to promote the desorption of protein components during the chemical adsorption between proteins and nanofiltration membrane.

The concentration polarization of glycopeptides is the primary cause of membrane flux attenuation when Ganoderma lucidum fermentation solution is concentrated at room temperature using nanofiltration. Meanwhile, membrane fouling is mostly caused by the adsorption of proteins and triterpenoids. The ultrasonic cavitation impact can increase the mass transfer efficiency of solutes, lower the concentration polarization coefficient of proteins and sugars, promote the desorption of triterpenoids and some protein components, and restore membrane flux. Although it has limitations for chemical adsorption, it can reduce membrane fouling and increase the service life of nanofiltration membranes. The fouling mechanism during nanofiltration concentration of plant fermentation solutions rich in sugar and protein can be clarified using the nanofiltration membrane fouling research model developed in this study, which combines adsorption kinetics, concentration polarization coefficient, concentration kinetics, and microscopic characteristics of the membrane surface. Simultaneously, it resolves the technological problem that makes it challenging to industrialize nanofiltration concentration at room temperature by optimizing ultrasonic-assisted separation parameters. Expand the use of nanofiltration technology in the food, medical, and water treatment industries.
