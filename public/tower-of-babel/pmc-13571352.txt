Before the Valve
Authors: Valeria Pergola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: aortic valve, congenital heart defect, echocardiography, stenosis, valve replacement

A Doppler gradient is not an anatomic diagnosis. It is the hemodynamic expression of an obstruction that must still be localized. This distinction is central in aortic stenosis, but it becomes decisive when the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) contains more than 1 possible site of obstruction. The case by Roufeh et al1 in this issue of JACC: Case Reports is therefore clinically important not simply because it is unusual, but because it reminds us that the valve is not always the whole story.

The authors describe a 50-year-old man with previous childhood congenital heart surgery who presented with decompensated heart failure, reduced left ventricular ejection fraction, severe calcific trileaflet aortic stenosis, significant aortic regurgitation, pulmonary hypertension, and a discrete subaortic membrane.1 The initial temptation in such a case is understandable: severe valve calcification and high LVOT velocity naturally direct attention toward valvular aortic stenosis. Yet the decisive abnormality was dual-level obstruction. Definitive therapy required both subaortic membrane resection and aortic valve replacement.

Subaortic stenosis is an uncommon but clinically meaningful cause of fixed LVOT obstruction in adults. Although often categorized within congenital heart disease, it may be diagnosed late, recur after repair, or coexist with other structural lesions.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Its adult course is frequently slow, but not benign: aortic regurgitation, reoperation, heart failure, arrhythmias, and death remain relevant long-term concerns.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 The association with aortic regurgitation is well recognized, likely reflecting chronic leaflet trauma from a high-velocity subvalvular jet.2,3 Whether subaortic turbulence accelerates calcific trileaflet aortic stenosis is less certain. This case cannot prove causality; its stronger lesson is diagnostic. Subvalvular and valvular disease may coexist, interact, and confound one another.

The pitfall is Doppler misattribution. Continuous-wave Doppler records the highest velocity along the interrogation line; it does not independently determine where that velocity begins. In dual-level disease, the measured gradient may be composite. Aortic valve area, dimensionless index, flow state, and valve morphology remain essential, but they must be interpreted anatomically. Sequential pulsed-wave sampling through the left ventricular cavity, LVOT, subaortic region, and valve; color Doppler localization of turbulence; and careful imaging of the subvalvular anatomy are not optional refinements. They are the safeguards against treating the wrong level of obstruction.7

Multimodality imaging was therefore not ancillary in this case. Transthoracic echocardiography recognized severe obstruction and ventricular compromise. Transesophageal echocardiography localized the membrane and clarified the subvalvular origin of turbulence. Cardiac magnetic resonance quantified severe aortic regurgitation and characterized the consequences of chronic pressure overload.1 Each modality answered a different question: severity, location, and myocardial impact.

The lesson is also timely in the era of streamlined valve intervention. A transcatheter valve cannot resect a subaortic membrane. In patients with previous congenital surgery, early-onset or disproportionate aortic valve disease, mixed stenosis and regurgitation, discordant echocardiographic parameters, or unexplained residual gradients, the threshold for transesophageal echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance, or computed tomography should be low. Before intervention, 3 questions should be explicit: where does the velocity acceleration begin; does the planned procedure treat every obstructive level; and does the congenital history suggest residual or recurrent anatomy?

Roufeh et al1 show that complete anatomic diagnosis can change management. Valve replacement alone would have risked persistent afterload; membrane resection alone would have left severe valve disease untreated. The subsequent improvement in symptoms, ejection fraction, prosthetic gradient, and pulmonary pressure supports the view that a substantial component of the patient’s cardiomyopathy was afterload-mediated and partially reversible.1

The message is simple and consequential: before replacing the valve, localize the OBSTRUCTION(S). Aortic stenosis is common; dual-level LVOT obstruction is rare. But rare diagnoses are missed most often when a common diagnosis appears sufficient.
