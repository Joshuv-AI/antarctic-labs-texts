Chronic Invasive Fungal Sinusitis Concerning for Sinonasal Malignancy With Unique Intracranial Extension
Authors: Andrew Jung, Margaret Scott, Matthew Liu, Cristian Gragnaniello, Philip Chen, Erin Mamuyac Lopez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic invasive fungal sinusitis is a rare sinonasal pathology characterized by histopathological evidence of fungi causing chronic inflammation, as well as radiologic imaging of bony destruction and extrasinus invasion. Here, the authors have described a case of chronic invasive fungal sinusitis with the presence of a fungal mass, orbital involvement, and intracranial extension into the anterior skull base. Management involved nasal endoscopy with biopsy, postoperative debridement, and antifungal therapy.

Introduction

Fungal rhinosinusitis is an inflammatory disease of the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses characterized by the presence of fungus. Invasive fungal sinusitis (IFS) is a rare classification of fungal rhinosinusitis that can be further divided into 3 distinct categories. 1 Acute IFS typically affects immunocompromised patients and is distinguished by aggressive infection and invasion of nearby structures, including the brain and orbit. Chronic IFS (CIFS) can be either granulomatous or nongranulomatous, with the granulomatous variant, chronic granulomatous IFS, primarily found in tropical areas such as India, Pakistan, and Sudan. The nongranulomatous variant of CIFS is associated with an indolent disease course, with the potential to extend intracranially or into the orbit. 2 Here, the authors have described a case of CIFS with the presence of a fungal mass, orbital involvement, and intracranial extension into the anterior skull base.

Case Patient Presentation

A 70-year-old male patient with a past medical history of hypertension, transient ischemic attacks, hypothyroidism, and chronic kidney disease presented to the emergency department at the authors’ institution for evaluation of headache, mild confusion, and vertigo secondary to a fall 3 days earlier. Additionally, the patient had a history of well-controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus with a hemoglobin A1C of 5.8 on the visit. Upon further evaluation, he reported nausea, vomiting, intermittent clear bilateral rhinorrhea, and vision changes with intermittent loss of vision in his right eye, along with right-sided proptosis. Appropriate precautions were taken out of concern for a potential cerebrospinal fluid leak. Computed tomography (CT) of the head and sinus demonstrated a mass in the sinuses with multifocal osseous destruction of the ethmoid and frontal sinus walls (Figure 1). Subsequent magnetic resonance image (MRI) revealed an enhancing mass lesion in the right ethmoid sinuses with extension into the right superior medial orbit and intracranially (Figure 2).

Imaging results suggested a sinonasal malignancy with a differential diagnosis of esthesioneuroblastoma, sinonasal differentiated carcinoma, and metastases, with the possibility of atypical infection considered less likely. Out of concern for a sinonasal malignancy, the patient was referred to otolaryngology, where he underwent biopsy of the lesion via right anterior ethmoidectomy and middle turbinate excision for access. Intraoperatively, a polypoid-appearing skull base mass emanating from the right anterior skull base was observed with fungal debris overlying the skull base mass. Intraoperative frozen pathology revealed somatolymphoid tissue, referring to lymphoid tissue found in the body wall or connective tissues of the body, with a differential including lymphoma, poorly differentiated esthesioneuroblastoma, sinonasal undifferentiated carcinoma, and small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma. However, final permanent pathology revealed chronic inflammation and necrosis of the sinonasal mucosa along with abundant fungal septate hyphae with acute angle branching, suggestive of Aspergillus. Concerns for a sinonasal neoplasm remained, so a repeat nasal endoscopy and biopsy were performed 2 days later in the operating room, confirming no evidence of malignancy with the presence of abundant fungal elements. Due to the lesion’s intracranial involvement and concerns of a malignancy, neurosurgery was involved throughout the patient’s hospital course. In accordance with the results, neurosurgery deemed any neurosurgical intervention unnecessary for this patient’s pathology, and additional treatment was started with antifungal therapy.

Treatment Pathway

Following endoscopic sinus surgery with 2 biopsies, the patient underwent postoperative debridement in clinic 1 month after initial surgery. Debridement was done with bilateral nasal endoscopy to remove crust and fibrin debris from the right sinonasal cavity. Given that 2 biopsies of the sinonasal lesion were negative for malignancy and suggested a fungal infection, no further surgical intervention was pursued. Subsequently, the patient was started on 300 mg of oral voriconazole, administered twice daily. He was continued on voriconazole for about 5 months but was switched to 372 mg of isavuconazonium sulfate daily, due to concerns about voriconazole-induced hallucinations. He has since continued to be on isavuconazonium.

Outcomes

Two weeks after initiation of antifungal therapy, the patient’s ophthalmologic symptoms resolved, and he no longer experienced rhinorrhea. Along with continued treatment with isavuconazonium, repeat imaging was obtained over the following months with demonstrated improvement (Figure 3). During this time, the patient was regularly monitored by an otolaryngologist, neurosurgeon, and infectious disease specialist. On follow-up 3 months after the initiation of isavuconazonium, he displayed complete resolution of all symptoms.

CIFS is a rare and potentially fatal disease characterized by gradual invasion of the sinuses by fungi. It occurs most commonly in warm, humid climates, such as the southern United States, and is predominantly caused by Aspergillus species. 3 CIFS has a lengthy clinical course and subtle progression of symptoms. The disease typically initially presents with mild, nonspecific symptoms of sinus blockage, such as congestion, and progresses to more severe symptoms as the fungal invasion spreads to the orbit, skull base, or intracranial structures. As the disease progresses, patients may develop visual disturbances, facial pain, and neurologic deficits due to contiguous expansion beyond the sinuses. 4 Extrasinus invasion, especially intracranial invasion, is a well-documented indication of poor prognosis. 5 This patient experienced recurrent sinus infections before he developed more severe symptoms and presented at the emergency department. This subtle progression reflects the insidious nature of CIFS, highlighting its diagnostic challenges.

CIFS is classically associated with immunocompromised and diabetic patients. 6 This association, along with a lack of granulomas on histopathology, helps differentiate the disease from the clinically similar chronic granulomatous IFS. 7 The patient had well-controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus and several other comorbidities, including chronic kidney disease, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and a history of transient ischemic attacks, establishing a high-risk profile for developing an invasive fungal infection. The synergistic effects of the multiple comorbidities likely contributed to the ability of the fungus to invade. The management of the patient was further complicated by the potential interaction between voriconazole and atorvastatin, necessitating adjustments in dosage and close monitoring throughout the treatment period.

Previous studies have noted the difficulty in diagnosing CIFS, as the fungal invasion can resemble malignancy with findings such as bony invasion, soft tissue necrosis, and rim-enhancing lesions on CT or MRI. 8 In this case, both imaging and gross appearance of the mass on initial endoscopy increased suspicion for neoplasm. A definitive diagnosis was made only after a repeat nasal endoscopy was performed, the biopsies from which confirmed Aspergillus infection on final pathology. This showcases the importance of histopathological findings in diagnoses when imaging findings are inconclusive.

The extent of disease in this patient was unusual for a disease with an indolent course, and to the knowledge of the authors, it has not been previously described in the literature. CT and MRI imaging revealed a fungal mass with continuous invasion from the sinuses to the orbits and skull base and into the frontal cerebral parenchyma. Historically, extensive invasions of this nature carry a dire prognosis, even with aggressive treatment. However, the appropriate management of this patient’s condition, involving surgical intervention and 6 months of antifungal therapy with voriconazole, reflects a clinically effective response. The patient’s favorable outcome reinforces current treatment standards regarding the role of prompt surgical intervention coupled with long-term antifungal therapy. 9

This case emphasized the potential dramatic presentation of CIFS mimicking an aggressive sinonasal malignancy. Additionally, this case report demonstrated quality of care with thorough evaluation with multiple nasal endoscopies and biopsy to come to the correct diagnosis, with subsequent long-term antifungal therapy warranted. Furthermore, this case report highlighted a team-based approach to patient care, requiring a delicate balance among otolaryngology, neurosurgery, and infectious disease when making appropriate clinical decisions.
