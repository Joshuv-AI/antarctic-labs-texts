Molecular mechanism of RBM15-mediated m6A modification in hepatitis B virus replication
Authors: Min Ni, Bingbing Li, Lingli Wang, Shengju Ma, Kun Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatitis B virus (HBV) is a major human pathogen and chronically infects over 250 million people globally. The objective of our study is to investigate the mechanism of RBM15 in HBV replication, providing novel targets for HB treatment.MethodsHuh-7 cells were treated with pHBV1.3. pHBV1.3 replication in Huh-7 cells was verified by detection of HBV RNAs, HBV pgRNA and HBx levels. Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) serves an enveloped DNA pathogen of the Hepadnaviridae family, and 5%–10% incurable patient act as HBV carriers to spread chronic HBV infections within populations [1]. Primary transmission routes of HBV include mother-to-child transmission at birth, sexual contact, and exposure to infected blood [2]. HBV impairs the liver by mediating abnormal immune responses, leading to T cell dysfunction and B cell hyporesponsiveness [3]. HBV infection can cause acute and chronic infections, severe liver failure, and cancer, leading to high morbidity and mortality [4]. Despite protective HBV vaccines existing for over four decades and being universally recommended for newborns, challenges still exist like suboptimal global coverage and high susceptibility rates in adults [5]. In this study, we established the HBV in vitro replication model and mice HBV replication model, and manifested the potential mechanism in HBV replication, providing novel targets for anti-HBV therapeutic strategies.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A), the most abundant mRNA modification, regulates fundamental RNA processes including stability, splicing, and translation [6]. RNA-binding motif protein15 (RBM15) serves as an auxiliary component of the m6A writer complex and recruits the core methyltransferase complex to mRNA targets through direct RNA binding [7]. m6A modification regulates viral replication directly via methylation of viral RNAs or indirectly by influencing the expression and processing of host/viral RNAs involved in replication [8]. Enhanced m6A modification potentially promotes HBV replication [9]. By affecting the stability and translation of HBV RNA, m6A modification is involved in HBV-induced chronic infection and liver diseases [10]. A previous study has revealed that RBM15 plays a pivotal role in promoting the development of HBV-related hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [11]. RBM15 expression is upregulated following induction by hepatitis B virus X (HBx) and promotes tumorigenesis [12]. The roles of distinct m6A modification regulators (writers, readers, and erasers) in HBV replication have been extensively documented, such as METTL3, METTL14, ALKBH5, and YTHDF2 [9, 13–15]. Given that RBM15 has been rarely studied in HBV infection and its association with HBV replication remains unexplored, this study investigates whether RBM15 affects HBV replication, aiming to provide a new target for the treatment of HBV-related HCC and other liver diseases.

Long noncoding RNAs (LncRNAs) are transcripts without coding potential and function to regulate posttranslational modifications of non-histone proteins in invertebrate and vertebrate species [16]. LncRNAs directly facilitate viral replication across multiple lifecycle phases, including DNA transcription and RNA/protein/virion stability [17]. LncRNA highly upregulated in liver cancer (HULC) is involved in HBV infection [18], suggesting a potential target for HBV-related diseases. In addition, embryonic lethal abnormal vision-like protein 1 (ELAVL1) functions as RNA-binding protein and controls mRNA stability of target transcripts and mediates post-transcriptional regulation [19]. Therefore, we hypothesized that HULC binds to ELAVL1 and explored the role of HULC in HBV replication.

The bromodomain and extraterminal domain (BET) family regulates inflammatory and immunoregulatory genes through binding acetylated histones and recruiting transcriptional machinery to enable RNA polymerase II transcription [20]. As a member of the BET family, bromodomain-containing 4 (BRD4) regulates transcription, replication, and DNA damage repair [21]. A recent study has proved that HBV depends on BRD4 to initiate and sustain viral transcription and replication [22]. In this study, we explored the role of BRD4 in HBV replication.

Herein, we investigated the mechanism of RBM15 in HBV replication, revealing novel therapeutic targets against HBV.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The entire animal experiments were ethically approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University and conducted strictly in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals [23].

Cell culture and transfection

The human HCC cell lines Huh-7 and the human hepatoma cell lines HepG2.2.15 with stable HBV replication were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). The Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, 11,995,073, Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (S9020, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and 100 U/mL streptomycin- penicillin solution (15,140,148, Gibco) was used for cell culture in a humidified incubator (37 ℃, 5% CO₂).

The siRNAs and vectors utilized included: pHBV1.3 for HBV transfection (containing 1.3 times the HBV genome, genotype D) and the negative control (pNC), specific siRNAs targeting and inhibiting RBM15 (si-RBM15-1, si-RBM15-2) and ELAVL1(si-ELAVL1-1, si-ELAVL1-2) and the negative control si-NC, pcDNA3.1-based HULC overexpression vector (oe-HULC), BRD4 overexpression vector (oe-BRD4) and the negative control oe-NC, which were designed and provided by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Following 48 h transfection utilizing Lipofectamine™ 3000 reagent (L3000150, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), cells were harvested for next analyses.

Construction of adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors and mice models

The recombinant AAV vectors containing short-hairpin RNA targeting RBM15 and the negative control (AAV-shRBM15, AAV-shNC), AAV-HBV1.3 (carries 1.3 copies of the HBV genome and is packaged in AAV serotype 8 capsids) and the negative control AAV-NC were sourced from PackGene Biotech (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China).

The male 5–6-week-old C57BL/6 mice from Huafang Biotechnology (Beijing, China) were raised in a standard animal room under a 12-h light/dark cycle with free access to food and water. Hydrodynamic injection of the aforementioned recombinant AAV (2.5 × 1011 viral genome copies per mouse) was performed into the tail vein. The mice were divided into four groups (6 mice per group): AAV-NC, AAV-HBV1.3, AAV-HBV1.3 + AAV-shNC, and AAV-HBV1.3 + AAV-shRBM15. Four weeks later, euthanasia of the mice was conducted via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg, P3761, Sigma-Aldrich, Tulsa, OK, USA). Serum and liver samples were subsequently collected. Serum was for the detection of HBsAg and HBeAg, and liver tissue was employed to measure mRNA, protein, HBV DNA, and HBV RNAs levels.

Quantitative real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using TRIzol reagent (15596018CN, Invitrogen). cDNA synthesis was performed using the RevertAid RT Reverse Transcription kit (K1691, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). qRT-PCR was carried out on a CFX96 system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using PowerTrack SYBR Green Master Mix (A46110, Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Primer sequences listed in Table 1 were designed and synthesized through Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), with glyceraldehyde-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) serving as the internal reference. Calculation of the relative gene expression levels was performed utilizing the 2−ΔΔCt method [24].

qRT-PCR/qPCR primer sequences

Detection of HBV RNAs, HBV pregenomic RNA (HBV pgRNA) and HBV DNA

HBV RNAs, HBV pgRNA, and HBV DNA levels were examined using a previously described method [25]. Specifically, qRT-PCR was carried out for measurement of HBV RNAs and HBV pgRNA expressions in cells or liver tissues. Primers for the amplified HBV pgRNA fragment were designed based on the conserved region of HBV pgRNA, while primers for the total HBV RNA fragment were designed based on the highly conserved C gene region and S gene region of the HBV genome. HBV DNA expression in the cell supernatant or serum was quantified by qPCR. Primer sequences were detailed in Table 1.

Southern blot and Northern blot

For Southern blot DNA was extracted from cells or liver tissues. DNA samples were separated on a 1% agarose gel, and then the DNA in the gel was transferred to a nylon membrane via capillary siphon. The DNA was fixed on the membrane by UV cross-linking, followed by hybridization with digoxigenin (DIG)-labeled probes at 42°C overnight. The membrane was washed with washing buffer to remove unbound probes, blocked, and then incubated with anti-DIG antibody. Finally, the results were visualized using X-ray film.

For Northern blot RNA was extracted from cells or liver tissues. The obtained RNA samples were separated on a 1.4% formaldehyde-agarose gel and transferred to a nylon membrane. After fixation by UV cross-linking, the membrane was hybridized with DIG-labeled probes at 68°C overnight. Following washing, the membrane was blocked and incubated with anti-DIG antibody. The results were visualized by exposing the membrane to X-ray film.

Western blot (WB) assay

Extraction of total protein from cells or tissues was carried out using radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer (20–188, Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (71,285-M, Merck Millipore). Separation of proteins was conducted using 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, followed by transference onto the Immobilon®-NC nitrocellulose membrane (HATF85R, Merck Millipore). After blocking with skim milk for 2 h, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with RBM15 antibody (1:1000, 25,261, CST, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), ELAVL1 antibody (1:1000, 12,582, CST), BRD4 antibody (1:1000, 54,615, CST), and β-actin antibody (1:1000, ab8227, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA). Membrane incubation for 2 h was conducted with secondary antibody goat anti-Rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) H&L (HRP) (1:2000, ab205718, Abcam). Color development was performed using Immobilon enhanced chemiluminescence Ultra Western HRP substrate (WBULS0100, Merck Millipore). The Fuji 5 × 7-inch X-ray film (RX-N–C) was brought into contact with the nitrocellulose membrane and exposed in a darkroom for 30 s. After fixation, the film was scanned and visualized using an EPSON scanner (V19ii/V39ii). Band intensity was quantified via ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Normalization was performed with β-Actin as the control.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The cell supernatant or mice serum samples were harvested. HBsAg and HBeAg levels were detected by HBsAg ELISA kit (KA0286, Bio-Techne, Shanghai, China) and HBeAg ELISA kit (BP2-60,029, Bio-Techne). A Synergy™ H1 microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA) was employed to read the absorbance at 450 nm.

Quantitative analysis of total m6A in cells and methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) assay

Following total RNA extraction from purified cells, total m6A levels were quantified utilizing the EpiQuik m6A RNA Methylation Quantification kit (Colorimetric) (P-9005–96, Epigentek, Farmingdale, NY, USA). Measurement of the absorbance at 450 nm was performed using a microplate reader. MeRIP assay was carried out with the EpiQuik CUT&RUN m6A RNA Enrichment (MeRIP) kit (P-9018–24, Epigentek). The enrichment of HULC in the immunoprecipitated complexes was then detected by qRT-PCR.

Bioinformatics prediction and RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

Potential binding interactions between HULC and ELAVL1, as well as between ELAVL1 and BRD4 mRNA, were predicted online via the starBase database (http://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/) [26]. The EZ-Magna RIP® RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation kit (17–701, Sigma-Aldrich) was employed to conduct RIP assay. The antibodies used were ELAVL1 antibody (1:30, ab200342, Abcam) or the negative control IgG antibody (1:5000, ab6715, Abcam). Levels of HULC and BRD4 in the precipitates were subsequently quantified by qRT-PCR.

mRNA stability experiment

Cells were treated with 2 mg/mL actinomycin D (15,021, CST) for 0, 4, 8, and 12 h, followed by detection of BRD4 expression with qRT-PCR at different time points.

Statistical analysis

Statistical and graphic analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Data were initially tested for normality and homogeneity of variance, which conformed to the normal distribution and homogeneous variances. Analysis of the comparisons between two groups was performed by t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. P values were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

RBM15 is overexpressed in HBV replication

The in vitro HBV replication model was established in order to clarify the molecular mechanism of RBM15 in HBV replication. Transfection of pHBV1.3 into Huh-7 cells was carried out. qRT-PCR analysis and Northern blot demonstrated significant upregulation of HBV RNAs, HBV pgRNA, and HBx expression in Huh-7-HBV cells compared to controls (P < 0.05, Fig. 1A, 1), confirming that pHBV1.3effectively supported HBV replication in Huh-7 cells. Subsequently, RBM15 expression was examined in both Huh-7 and HepG2.2.15 cells by qRT-PCR and WB (P < 0.05, Fig. 1C, 1). Results revealed that RBM15 expression was markedly enhanced. Furthermore, RBM15 expression was detected in Huh-7 cells that were transfected with different concentrations of the pHBV1.3. RBM15 expression was elevated with the increase of pHBV1.3 plasmid concentration (P < 0.05, Fig. 1E, 1). Collectively, these results indicate that HBV replication promotes RBM15 expression in cells.

RBM15 inhibition suppresses HBV replication

To further define the effect of RBM15 in HBV replication, siRNAs targeting RBM15 were transfected into Huh-7-HBV cells for RBM15 inhibition. si-RBM15-1 exhibiting higher knockdown efficiency was selected for subsequent experiments (P < 0.05, Fig. 2A). Knockdown of RBM15 in Huh-7-HBV cells led to significant reduction in RBM15, HBV DNA, and HBV RNAs expression (P < 0.05, Fig. 2B–F). Levels of HBsAg and HBeAg were also evidently decreased (P < 0.05, Fig. 2G). Consistently, RBM15 suppression in HepG2.2.15 cells produced similar inhibitory effects (P < 0.05, Fig. 2A–G). Based on above findings, we demonstrate that RBM15 inhibition effectively suppresses HBV replication in vitro.

RBM15 promotes HULC expression by enhancing the m6A modification on HULC

To identify the downstream molecular targets of RBM15 in HBV replication, determination of total m6A modification levels, m6A modification levels on HULC, and HULC expression in Huh-7 and HepG2.2.15 cells was performed. In comparison with Huh-7-NC cells, Huh-7-HBV cells exhibited significant increases in total m6A modification levels (P < 0.05, Fig. 3A), m6A modification levels on HULC (P < 0.05, Fig. 3B), and HULC expression (P < 0.05, Fig. 3D). Furthermore, these increases occurred dose-dependently with concentrations of pHBV1.3 (P < 0.05, Fig. 3B). Conversely, total m6A modification levels, m6A modification levels on HULC, and HULC expression were evidently reduced upon knockdown of RBM15 (P < 0.05, Fig. 3A-B, D). Consistently, a similar trend of changes was observed in HepG2.2.15 cells (P < 0.05, Fig. 3A, C-D). These findings collectively suggest that RBM15 promotes HULC expression by enhancing m6A modification on HULC.

Overexpression of HULC partially reverses the inhibitory effect of RBM15 on HBV replication

For verification on functions of RBM15 on HULC, Huh-7-HBV cells were transfected by the overexpression vectors of HULC (P < 0.05, Fig. 4A), and combined with RBM15 inhibition. In comparison with Huh-7-HBV cells, the combined group showed significant increases on the expression of HULC, HBV DNA and HBV RNAs (P < 0.05, Fig. 4B-F), and contents of HBsAg and HbeAg (P < 0.05, Fig. 4G). Based on above findings, overexpression of HULC partially reverses the inhibitory effect of RBM15 on HBV replication.

HULC binds to ELAVL1 to stabilize BRD4 mRNA and promotes BRD4 expression

LncRNAs can act as molecular scaffolds to promote the binding of RNA-binding proteins to specific mRNAs, thereby affecting mRNA expression [27]. To elucidate the downstream molecular mechanism of the RBM15/lncRNA HULC axis, the binding interactions between lncRNA HULC and ELAVL1 protein, as well as between ELAVL1 protein and BRD4 mRNA, were predicted through the starBase database (P < 0.05, Fig. 5A), and verified in Huh-7-HBV cells by RIP (P < 0.05, Fig. 5B). We found that neither downregulation of RBM15 nor overexpression of HULC affects ELAVL1 expression (P< 0.05, Fig. 5C-D), indicating that HULC can directly bind to ELAVL1 without affecting its expression. Subsequently, RNA stability assay revealed that BRD4 mRNA stability in Huh-7-HBV cells was higher than that in Huh-7-NC cells. After downregulation of RBM15, BRD4 mRNA stability was decreased, while overexpression of HULC could partially restore BRD4 mRNA stability (P< 0.05, Fig. 5E), indicating that HULC can promote BRD4 mRNA stability. qRT-PCR and WB assay uncovered that compared to Huh-7-NC cells, BRD4 expression was significantly promoted in Huh-7-HBV cells, while evidently downregulated upon suppression of RBM15, and partially restored after HULC upregulation (P < 0.05, Fig. 5C-D), indicating that HULC can promote BRD4 expression. Additionally, BRD4 expression was increased dose-dependently with concentrations of pHBV1.3 (P < 0.05, Fig. 5F-G). A consistent trend of BRD4 expression was also observed in HepG2.2.15 cells (P < 0.05, Fig. 5H-I). Next, to verify the mechanism by which HULC binds to ELAVL1 and thereby affects BRD4 mRNA stability, we downregulated ELAVL1 in Huh-7-HBV cells (P < 0.05, Fig. 5J). After ELAVL1 downregulation, both BRD4 mRNA stability and BRD4 expression were significantly reduced (P < 0.05, Fig. 5E, K-L).We collectively demonstrate that lncRNA HULC binds to ELAVL1 protein, stabilizing BRD4 mRNA and thereby promoting BRD4 expression.

BRD4 overexpression partially reverses the inhibitory effect of RBM15 inhibition on HBV replication

To verify the function of the RBM15/HULC/ELAVL1/BRD4 signaling axis, the Huh-7-HBV cells were transfected by overexpression vectors of BRD4 (P < 0.05, Fig. 6A), and combined with RBM15 inhibition. Results showed that compared with RBM15 inhibition alone, the expression of BRD4, HBV DNA and HBV RNAs in the combined group was evidently enhanced (P < 0.05, Fig. 6B-G). Contents of HBsAg and HbeAg were markedly increased (P < 0.05, Fig. 6H). In conclusion, the above results manifest that BRD4 overexpression partially reverses the inhibitory effect of RBM15 inhibition on HBV replication.

RBM15 inhibition suppresses HBV replication in vivo

Finally, AAV-HBV1.3 and AAV-shRBM15 were injected into the tail vein to verify functions of RBM15 in HBV replication. Compared with the control group, after injection of AAV-HBV1.3, the expression of RBM15 (P < 0.05, Fig. 7A-B), HBV DNA (P < 0.05, Fig. 7C-D), and HBV RNAs (P < 0.05, Fig. 7E-F) in mice liver was upregulated and contents of HBsAg and HbeAg in serum were elevated significantly (P < 0.05, Fig. 7G), which were evidently diminished upon knockdown of RBM15 (P < 0.05, Fig. 7A-G). The above results suggest that RBM15 inhibition effectively suppresses HBV replication in vivo.

Discussion

Globally, HB represents a substantial chronic disease burden and induces liver failure via primary infection and reactivation events [28, 29]. As a hepatotropic virus, HBV drives severe liver disease progression from hepatitis to cirrhosis and HCC [30]. HBV replication is controlled by antivirals in most patients, however, HBsAg loss and seroconversion rarely occur during long-term therapy [31]. In this study, we revealed that RBM15 promotes HBV replication by independently elevating the m6A modification of HULC and BRD4 expression, providing new evidence for the functional division of the methyltransferase family (Fig. 8).

HBV replication promotes RBM15 expression. RBM15 stabilizes HULC expression by promoting m6A modification, promotes the binding of HULC to ELAVL1 for BRD4 mRNA stabilization, and ultimately enhances BRD4 expression and HBV replication

Abnormal m6A levels serve as key factors in liver failure [32], liver fibrosis [33], and HCC [34] caused by HBV infection. Small extracellular vesicles induce upregulation of METTL3 and IGF2BP2, and elevate m6A modification level, thereby driving HBV replication [9]. Through m6A-dependent stabilization of MCM2 and MCM5 transcripts, YTHDF2 facilitates cell cycle progression and HBV-related HCC tumorigenesis [35]. Of note, RBM15 may be involved in HBV-related HCC via m6A modification [11]. RBM15 is widely considered as a key factor in promoting HCC development. For instance, RBM15 overexpression promotes HCC progression by stabilizing YES1 epigenetically in an m6A-IGF2BP1-dependent manner [36]. In HCC xenograft models, RBM15 knockdown decreases VEGFA expression and suppresses angiogenesis, thus retarding HCC tumor growth [37]. HBx upregulates RBM15 to enhance the m6A modification of cFAM210A, thus contributing to tumorigenesis in HCC [12]. Consistently, our research elucidated that RBM15 facilitates HBV replication and enhances HULC expression by increasing the m6A modification on HULC.

LncRNAs play a pivotal role in HBV replication. For instance, overexpression of lncRNA WAC-AS1 reinforces autophagy via the miR-192-5p/ATG7 axis, thereby driving HBV replication [38]. LncRNA HOTAIR potentiates HBV promoter efficacy via SP1 recruitment to covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA), resulting in enhanced viral transcription and replication [39]. In HCC cells, lncRNA AP000253 stimulates HBV replication by elevating levels of HBsAg, HBeAg and HBV DNA [40]. Of note, overexpression of HULC aggravates HBV replication by maintaining cccDNA stability [41]. HULC elevates HBV replication by facilitating the accumulation of the HAT1/HULC/HBc complex on the cccDNA minichromosome [42]. Consistently, we manifested that HULC overexpression increases the expression of HBV DNA and HBV RNAs, and elevates HBsAg and HbeAg contents. Overexpression of HULC partially reverses the inhibitory effect of RBM15 on HBV replication.

RIP assay confirmed that HULC bound to ELAVL1, stabilized BRD4 mRNA and enhanced BRD4 expression. Recent studies have manifested the high association of BRD4 with liver [43, 44]. BRD4 is suppressed by lncRNA XIST silencing, thus alleviating sepsis-induced acute liver injury [45]. Downregulation of BRD4 mitigates alcohol-induced liver injury by enhancing the SIRT1/Beclin1 axis [46]. Of note, BRD4 expression is elevated with fibrosis severity in HBV-induced liver fibrosis [47], suggesting the close relationship between BRD4 and HBV. A prior study has proved that BRD4 binds to the HBV viral genome [22]. In HBV-infected HepG2-NTCP cells, BRD4 knockdown plays an inhibitory role in cccDNA maintenance [48]. cccDNA serves as the primary transcriptional template for HBV genes [49]. Here, combined experiment of BRD4 overexpression and RBM15 downregulation exhibited enhanced expression of BRD4, HBV DNA and HBV RNAs and increased contents of HBsAg and HBeAg. Collectively, the above findings manifested that BRD4 overexpression partially reverses the inhibitory effect of RBM15 inhibition on HBV replication.

Several limitations of our study still exist. First, we have only conducted the study based on cell and animal models. There is a lack of data on the correlation between the expression of the RBM15-HULC-BRD4 axis and viral load in tissue samples (such as liver biopsies) from HB patients. Genotype D has high infectivity and stable replicative capacity and is commonly used in in vitro models of HBV-related HCC [25, 50, 51]. Currently, we have not yet conducted relevant research on other HBV genotypes. Moreover, the targeting specificity of RBM15 and the downstream mechanism of BRD4 remain to be further explored. Experiments involving independent knockdown or overexpression of BRD4 to investigate its role in HBV replication have not yet been performed. Since m6A modification during HBV replication is a dynamically regulated and complex process, whether RBM15 is the main effector enzyme remain to be explored. The involvement of other m6A modification enzymes or inhibitors has not yet been explored. Therefore, future research should focus on: (1) deepening mechanistic studies, like mapping transcriptome-wide m6A profiles and identifying downstream effectors of BRD4 (such as transcription factors or chromatin-modifying complexes) in HBV replication; (2) promoting clinical transformation, developing RBM15-specific small-molecule inhibitors (like blocking the methyltransferase domain) or siRNA delivery systems, and assessing the antiviral effects in humanized murine models; (3) analyzing how RBM15 loss affects intrinsic immune responses within hepatocytes and modulates immune cell infiltration; (4) verifying the conclusions in the replication of other HBV genotypes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, RBM15 facilitates HBV replication by promoting the binding of HULC to ELAVL1 via m6A modification and enhancing BRD4 expression, thus offering a new therapeutic target for HB treatment.
