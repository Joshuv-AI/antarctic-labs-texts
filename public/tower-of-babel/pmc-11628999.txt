Exploring symptoms perception and barriers to medication adherence among Thai Muslim patients with non-communicable diseases in a rural community in southern Thailand: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Napakkawat Buathong, Surawut Naruephai, Worawit Wanichanon, Kananas Rodkitvitthaya, Peeraphol Thananinkul, Napat Jankaew, Phisit Wessamanon, Samatchaya Khaotrakul, Ratthakorn Boonmak, Nichamon Choo-eard, Thanakrit Nintap, Teera Sarakitphan, Paweeorn Kaewchuay, Kittiya Naruephai, Monchai Chaison
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to explore the prevalence of medication non-adherence and influence of symptom perception and various barriers on medication adherence among Thai Muslim patients in a rural subdistrict of southern Thailand.DesignExplanatory sequential mixed-methods approach.SettingA rural community in southern Thailand.MethodsData collection spanned from March to December 2023. In the quantitative phase, 138 Muslim patients diagnosed with non-communicable diseases were enrolled, and their medication adherence and associated factors were assessed. In the qualitative phase, 22 participants were selected for in-depth interviews, and their symptom perceptions and the obstacles they faced in adhering to medication were explored. A focus group discussion involving 10 healthcare workers from a subdistrict health-promoting hospital was conducted to gain insights into healthcare providers’ perspectives on patients’ perceptions of symptoms and barriers to medication adherence.Outcome measuresMedication non-adherence was assessed, and the influence of symptom perception and various barriers on medication adherence was evaluatedResultsThe prevalence of medication non-adherence in the study sample was 75.36%. The following factors were significantly associated with medication non-adherence: occupation (farmer) (adjusted OR=3.93; 95% CI 1.04 to 12.64), lack of recommendations on adjusting medication schedules/dosages during Ramadan (adjusted OR=2.65; 95% CI 1.06 to 6.61) and patients’ perception of no symptoms (adjusted OR=3.72; 95% CI 1.23 to 11.25). The qualitative analysis highlighted patient-related issues, such as lack of symptom perception, limited health literacy regarding disease information and treatment and personal reasons for non-adherence (eg, forgetfulness, occupational constraints and transportation challenges for medical appointments). Additionally, healthcare system-related factors, patient–doctor relationships and communication gaps in medication management during Ramadan were identified as factors influencing medication non-adherence.ConclusionsTo improve adherence, healthcare providers should prioritise fostering positive patient–provider relationships and promoting patient care through enhanced health literacy initiatives.Keywords: Medication Adherence, Primary Health Care, PUBLIC HEALTH, Cross-Sectional Studies

Introduction

Chronic diseases, the risk of which increases with age, are prevalent in the population, with many individuals suffering from conditions such as hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidaemia.1 Medication adherence, defined as the extent to which individuals adhere to healthcare provider recommendations regarding medication, diet and lifestyle changes, is crucial for managing chronic diseases.2 Studies indicate that approximately 50% of patients with chronic diseases do not adhere to their medication regimens.3 4 The WHO identifies five dimensions of non-adherence factors: patient-related (eg, beliefs and access), healthcare team- and system-related (eg, doctor–patient communication), therapy-related (eg, adverse effects), social and economic (eg, race, war and age), and condition-related (eg, comorbidities like depression in diabetes) dimensions.5 Age, number of concurrent medications, practitioner and economic factors are some of the factors that influence medication adherence.1

Thailand provides various healthcare coverage options, with the Universal Coverage Scheme (UCS) as the primary system for most citizens. Some residents, however, use other schemes like the Social Security Scheme for private employees, Civil Servant Medical Benefit Scheme for government workers and their families and local health coverage from Local Administrative Organisations. While these schemes aim to provide comprehensive healthcare access to all residents, ensuring equitable access and quality of care, particularly between urban and rural areas, is challenging. The UCS in Thailand is accessible to ordinary citizens with a 13-digit national identification number who do not receive other state-provided benefits. This scheme enables the users to start their healthcare journeys within the system with primary care.6 Most individuals access healthcare primarily through the UCS, often at subdistrict or primary healthcare facilities.7 However, a previous study revealed disparities in access, particularly in rural areas, where unmet health needs are more common; therefore, despite the goal of universal access, achieving equitable healthcare remains a challenge.8

Data from Thailand’s Health Data Center (HDC): 20229 show that rates of uncontrolled diabetes, hypertension and hyperlipidaemia in southern provinces with significant Muslim populations are below the benchmarks set by the Ministry of Public Health. This trend is observed in both Muslim-majority areas in three provinces bordering Malaysia and predominantly Buddhist rural subdistricts, where Muslims are a minority, particularly in other southern Thai provinces. This unexpected pattern reveals a research gap in our understanding of medication adherence and non-communicable disease (NCD) management among rural Thai Muslims. Our study addresses this gap by exploring the prevalence of medication non-adherence and investigating how patients’ symptom perceptions and various barriers affect non-adherence among Thai Muslim patients with NCDs in a rural southern Thai subdistrict. Additionally, we aimed to investigate healthcare providers’ perspectives on their relationship and communication with patients.

Methods

Study design

This study employed an explanatory sequential mixed-methods approach, integrating both quantitative and qualitative investigations. The K.M. subdistrict province was chosen owing to its diverse population composition, which includes both Thai Muslim and Thai Buddhist communities in 11 villages. Villages A, B and C were specifically selected given their demographic characteristics. These three villages have Thai Muslim populations of approximately >90%, while the majority of Thai Buddhists in the subdistrict reside in the other villages. Data collection spanned from March to May 2023, coinciding with the holy month of Ramadan. The qualitative phase, comprising in-depth interviews and focus group discussions, lasted from October to December 2023.

Study sampling

The primary eligibility criterion was being Thai Muslim. The inclusion criteria were age≥35 years, diagnosed with at least one NCD and residing in the community for more than 6 months. The exclusion criteria were Thai Buddhist individuals or Thai Muslim patients with bedridden status, confirmed diagnosis of dementia and unwillingness to participate in the study.

In the quantitative phase, according to HDC report, 167 patients with NCDs received treatment or medical services in the study area. This figure included 17 Thai Buddhist patients and 150 Thai Muslim patients. Of the 150 eligible Thai Muslim patients, 12 were unwilling to participate in our study. The 17 Thai Buddhist patients met our exclusion criteria. Of the 150 eligible Thai Muslim patients with NCDs, 138 agreed to participate in our study, yielding a response rate of 92% (138/150).

The participants for the qualitative interviews were selected from among the quantitative study participants. We employed a purposive sampling strategy to ensure a diverse range of experiences and perspectives. The selection criteria for in-depth interviews were varying levels of medication adherence between both group of participants and representation of different age groups and sexes. The sample size for the qualitative interviews was determined using the concept of data saturation. Focus group discussions involved a healthcare team comprising registered nurse and nine village health volunteers (VHVs) from the three aforementioned villages, with three volunteers from each village, each possessing a minimum of 1 year of work experience.

Study tools

In the quantitative phase of our study, all participants completed a series of standardised questionnaires. These included inquiries into sociodemographic data, symptom perception, personal health information and factors pertaining to the healthcare system. Notably, variables related to advice on medication side effects, medication adjustments during fasting periods such as Ramadan and alterations in medication regimens were assessed. Medication adherence was measured using the Modified Thai version of the Medication Adherence Assessment,10 a validated tool employed to gauge medication-taking behaviour and identify barriers to adherence. Strong correlations were observed between scores obtained from this tool and objective measures of adherence.11 12 The instrument comprised eight questions and yielded Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.90, demonstrating robust reliability. The questionnaire is scored on an 8-point scale, where high medication adherence is indicated by scores above 7.75, while scores falling between 6 and 7.75 denote moderate adherence. Scores below 6 signify low medication adherence. For the purpose of data analysis, adherence was categorised as a combination of moderate and high medication adherence, while non-adherence was defined as low medication adherence.

In the qualitative phase, in-depth interviews were conducted using a semistructured format, focusing on two primary aspects: perspectives regarding healthcare providers and personal perceptions of illness. Sample questions directed towards healthcare providers included questions such as ‘Do healthcare providers furnish guidance on medical regimens?’ and ‘Do healthcare professionals offer instructions for adjusting medication schedules during Ramadan?’ On the other hand, questions regarding personal perceptions of illness encompassed questions like ‘Can you provide examples of your illness and their impact on your approach to medical treatments?’ and ‘Do you receive any information about your illness and treatment from healthcare providers?’ Furthermore, unstructured interviews were conducted during focus group discussions to delve into healthcare providers’ perspectives on patients’ perceptions of symptoms of medication adherence and their communication with patients. These discussions addressed barriers to accessing healthcare, challenges encountered in seeking medical services and obstacles impeding medication adherence. The English-translated versions of the study questionnaire and focus group discussion and interview guides used in this study are included in the online supplemental file 1.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software, V.3.5.1. Descriptive statistics were subsequently computed. Univariate analysis of the associations between independent variables was executed employing χ2 and crude OR. Noteworthy factors exhibiting a p value<0.2 underwent further scrutiny through multiple logistic regression employing a backward stepwise regression approach to discern predictive factors of medication non-adherence. Qualitative data were subjected to content analysis.13 14 Verbatim transcriptions were categorised into key themes and subsequently subdivided into topics and subtopics. The categorised data were validated using the triangulation method. Subsequently, data from each category underwent collective analysis employing the five-dimensional framework of medication adherence of the WHO. The WHO framework offers a comprehensive comprehension of the factors influencing medication non-adherence in patients with NCDs, encompassing both individual and environmental factors, including the public healthcare system in Thailand.

Results

Quantitative study

Participant demographics

Our study achieved a high response rate of 92% (138/150 eligible participants). A cohort of 138 Thai Muslim patients, each with at least one NCD, with an average age of 65 years (SD=13.45, range 35–96 years), was included in this study. In the cohort, 104 individuals (75.4%) were classified as non-adherent. Within this subset, 67.3% were female, 70.2% had completed primary education and 67.3% were beneficiaries of the UCS. Furthermore, 59.6% were diagnosed with hypertension, 64.4% with diabetes and 66.3% with hyperlipidaemia; 50.7% had been undergoing hyperlipidaemia treatment for over 5 years. The investigation uncovered notable correlations between medication adherence and specific variables, including educational attainment, type of medical insurance scheme and duration of hyperlipidaemia medication usage. Conversely, no statistically significant relationships emerged between medication adherence and other demographic factors such as sex, marital status and employment status (p>0.05), as delineated in table 1.

Qualitative study

Participant characteristics

22 participants were interviewed, with an average age of 69.4 years. The interviewees exhibited multiple chronic conditions, predominantly hypertension (81.8%), followed by hyperlipidaemia (72.7%), diabetes (27.3%) and chronic kidney disease (13.6%). A majority of the participants (59.1%) were unemployed. Additionally, 10 healthcare professionals took part in the focus group discussions, with an average age of 48.5 years. Among them were nine VHVs and one registered nurse.

Factors influencing medication non-adherence

Factors influencing medication non-adherence are contextualised within the framework provided by the WHO, encompassing medication attributes, health conditions, patient characteristics, socioeconomic factors and healthcare system elements. From our investigation, two primary factors emerged as influential within the study population: those related to patients and those related to the healthcare system. Presented below are representative quotes for each thematic dimension.

Patient-related dimension

Within this demographic, medication non-adherence stemmed from various factors, including forgetfulness, lack of caregiving support, employment demands, personal beliefs, religious practices (particularly during Ramadan), limited healthcare access, symptom perception and health literacy deficiencies.

Forgetfulness emerged as a prevalent issue among the participants, affecting 8 out of 22 individuals, primarily attributable to age-related cognitive decline. Despite repeated reminders from healthcare providers, older adults grappled with memory lapses. Additionally, two interviewees highlighted that some elderly individuals struggled to manage their medications independently due to a lack of caregiving support, as their caregivers were already burdened with existing responsibilities and unable to provide adequate attention to patient care.

We consistently remind patients about their medication regimen, detailing the steps they need to follow, how to take the medication, and what to do in case of forgetfulness. Despite our efforts, many struggles to recall the information when asked to repeat it. This issue is particularly challenging for the elderly. (H3f)

Their child doesn’t take the patient to the hospital. They are aware of it, but they simply don’t pay attention. (H6f)

Moreover, employment in demanding sectors such as agriculture and fisheries, which commence early in the day, contributed to fatigue and adherence difficulties for some participants. Furthermore, a significant proportion of individuals (11 out of 22) exhibited limited understanding of their conditions and self-care practices due to low health literacy. Despite receiving information and education from healthcare professionals, eight participants remained unaware of the implications of their illnesses, leading to suboptimal self-care practices and subsequent health complications.

If I hold my breath or sit still for a long time, it will lower my blood pressure when it’s being measured. The doctor won’t know, but I can’t keep it up because I get too tired. (A1in)

This patient and their whole family have high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and diabetes. They’ve been given information and education from healthcare professionals about their conditions, but they just don’t care. If you ask them, they know about it, but they don’t really understand the seriousness. Now, one of them has had to have a leg amputated because of diabetes. (H9f)

Additionally, personal beliefs, including religious practices like fasting during Ramadan, and misconceptions about medication influenced medical non-adherence. Some participants chose to prioritise religious observances over medication adherence, viewing it as a means to accrue spiritual merit.

Diabetic patients, especially the elderly, often stop taking their medication to focus on religious practices because they value their faith highly and believe it brings them the most merit. (H4f)

Furthermore, challenges in accessing healthcare, stemming from physical, financial and logistical constraints, impeded overall health outcomes for many participants.

It’s hard for me to visit the doctor because I need help getting in and out of the car. I can’t do it by myself, so my child usually goes to the doctor to pick up my medication for me. (NA8in)

Symptom perception deficits were observed in six participants, while six others exhibited limited health literacy regarding medication-related information.

I am underweight yet diagnosed with high blood lipid levels. This seems contradictory to me, especially since I have never experienced symptoms like fever or headaches. (NA10in)

Taking medicine for a long time can cause kidney disease and kidney failure. Do you think it’s true? I saw a person with kidney disease needing dialysis every day. I’m scared! (NA7in)

These factors collectively contributed to the complexities of medication adherence within this population.

Healthcare system-related dimension

Healthcare teams and factors within the healthcare system significantly influence patients’ non-adherence to medical regimens. Our data reveal that effective communication between healthcare providers and patients encompasses various aspects: imparting disease knowledge, guidance on medication administration, information about medication side effects, medication reminders and regular follow-up appointments. Such communication plays a pivotal role in enhancing patients’ understanding of their illness and optimising treatment outcomes.

He will remind you not to forget your medication; failing to do so may result in a spike in your blood sugar levels. (NA4in)

Observing Ramadan, a significant Muslim tradition involving fasting, poses unique challenges in medication management. While most healthcare providers are generally aware of Ramadan, our study suggests that some may lack a thorough understanding of its implications on medication schedules. Consequently, patients might not receive adequate guidance on adjusting their medication regimen during fasting, potentially leading to medication non-adherence.

I adjust my medication myself because the doctor is unaware of the dietary practices of Muslims during Ramadan. While they understand the requirement of fasting, they may not be familiar with the medication schedule during fasting, as medications cannot be taken during daylight hours. (NA9in)

Nevertheless, our findings also highlight instances where healthcare providers proactively inform patients about modifying their medication regimen during Ramadan, empowering patients to adhere to their treatment plans effectively.

During Ramadan, I take my medication around four in the morning. After I finish my meal, I take my pills, following the recommendation of physicians. They advised me to eat first and then take the medication to ensure I do not miss a dose. (NA7in)

Adopting a patient-centred approach to treatment, which encompasses understanding patients’ individual characteristics across physical, mental, social and environmental dimensions, is crucial for fostering treatment adherence and ensuring patient convenience. Participants emphasised the significance of this approach in promoting effective collaboration in healthcare and improving treatment outcomes.

Due to the distance to the hospital, I needed to consult a doctor at a nearby hospital to reduce travel expenses. The doctor understood my situation, granted permission, provided an appointment, and advised me to promptly seek care at the main hospital if any abnormal symptoms occur. (A2in)

Furthermore, participants highlighted challenges stemming from imbalances in the public health system, such as limited resources leading to insufficient time for healthcare providers to offer personalised medication guidance. This situation often results in misunderstandings and medication non-adherence among patients.

I would appreciate it if the doctor could explain, but the hospital is consistently busy, and there is limited time for detailed discussions about medication. It is crowded throughout the day. However, if feasible, I would like the doctor to offer some guidance, particularly regarding potential side effects of the medication. Nevertheless, I understand that time constraints may make this difficult. (NA4in)

However, healthcare providers have introduced systems aimed at providing care and monitoring for patients unable to visit hospitals independently, namely, deploying community health workers to deliver medication, administer injections, and monitor vital signs at patients’ homes. While this approach ensures continuous care without necessitating hospital visits, it may inadvertently reduce patient and caregiver awareness of disease treatment.

We have implemented a patient tracking system based on their appointments. When a patient misses an appointment, local health authorities intervene. However, this approach can sometimes lead to complacency. To mitigate this, we encourage active participation from family members in patient care. (H10f)

Discussion

Our study revealed a medication non-adherence prevalence of 75.36%, notably higher than the global average of approximately 50% among patients with NCDs.3 4 15 This elevated rate in our study may be attributed to the fact that our participants were Muslim, and data were collected during the holy month of Ramadan. During this period, Thai Muslims observe fasting from sunrise to sunset, altering their lifestyle and significantly affecting medication adherence.16

Medication non-adherence is influenced by various factors such as socioeconomic status, education level, psychological factors and healthcare system barriers.15 In our study, we categorised these factors according to the WHO framework,2 dividing them into patient-related and healthcare system-related themes. We identified significant issues including an inaccurate understanding of NCD symptoms, limited patient knowledge and a lack of awareness about their medical conditions, primarily because most participants in our study were older adults with only a primary school education or no formal education. This lack of health literacy may contribute to poor medication adherence among patients with NCDs in rural communities, consistent with previous findings.17 18 Age-related cognitive decline, associated with forgetfulness, leads to poor medication adherence. Memory problems can cause individuals to forget to take prescribed medications, negatively affecting adherence.19 Subjective memory complaints are associated with reduced adherence to medication regimens among older adults with chronic conditions. Our study population primarily consisted of individuals working in agriculture and fisheries, involving irregular working hours, leading to inconsistent and untimely medication administration. The irregular work schedules of Thai rubber tappers and fishermen, dictated by nocturnal latex harvesting and tide-dependent fishing patterns, respectively, disrupt normal daily routines and meal timing, consequently impacting medication non-adherence. Personal beliefs, including religious beliefs attributing illnesses to a mysterious power rather than medical science, and misconceptions about health further contribute to misunderstandings of medication side effects. Participants living in rural areas faced limitations in public transportation availability and assistance in reaching healthcare facilities. This factor appears to be a barrier to receiving treatment and following up on their conditions, similar to the findings of Cochran et al,20 who revealed the effect of transportation barriers on care among frequent healthcare users during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Our study identified two significant predictive factors associated with medication adherence: the absence of symptoms and the perception that there are no recommendations to adjust medication schedules or doses during Ramadan. Patients who perceive no symptoms may become less aware of their medication treatment, a finding consistent with those of previous studies by Bilondii et al21 and Allugmani et al.22 Effective communication by healthcare providers is crucial to address these issues and improve medication adherence. Providing patients with disease knowledge, guidance on medication administration, information about side effects, medication reminders and regular follow-up appointments are essential components for enhancing patient awareness of their medical conditions and improving treatment effectiveness. Placing patients at the centre of their treatment is a key strategy to promote patient engagement and collaboration, leading to enhanced medication adherence.

Our qualitative data analysis revealed that family members can play a crucial role in improving patients’ medication adherence. Olagbemide et al23 reported that family support enhances medication adherence. Therefore, family physicians should assess patients’ family support systems to enhance adherence levels and management outcomes of chronic diseases.23 Another study highlighted the family as the most prevalent source of support for adherence, particularly spouses and children, indicating their significant role in improving adherence.24 25 However, the evolving roles and responsibilities of Thai VHVs have reduced caregivers’ involvement in patient care. Health education programmes could be effective in increasing knowledge, awareness and confidence in collaborative patient care between caregivers and healthcare teams.26 To assist caregivers in caring for their family members, a daily home healthcare routine must be established by prioritising the daily tasks of patients and then adapting the caregiving routine to fit the caregiver’s schedule. This approach can alleviate the burden on caregivers and enable them to play a more significant role in patient care while managing their daily lives.27

Our study boasts several strengths, including the use of a mixed-methods approach that integrates both qualitative and quantitative data, facilitated a comprehensive analysis and enhanced the overall impact of our findings. Additionally, all participants were Thai Muslims with NCDs, and this study benefited from a high response rate of 92% that enhanced the reliability of our findings in the context of the studied population. However, our study has limitations. Our participants were selected from three villages of one rural subdistrict, where Muslims were a minority in one province, and this aspect may limit the generalisability of the findings. A relatively small sample size may have led to type II errors, potentially missing the significant impact of other factors on medication adherence. These findings should be considered exploratory; larger studies are needed to confirm or refute our results. Future research should include larger, more diverse samples across various regions and demographics to confirm our exploratory findings and provide a comprehensive understanding of medication non-adherence, especially during Ramadan, among Muslim patients in Thailand. The time gap between the qualitative and quantitative data collection phases may have introduced recall bias. Additionally, the use of self-report questionnaires could lead to overestimation of adherence or socially desirable responses, introducing bias and inaccuracies. Future research should prioritise the integration of objective measurements to address these concerns. Furthermore, we did not screen for mild cognitive impairment and mental health issues, which could affect data validity. Our exclusion criteria may have omitted relevant patient experiences impacting adherence. Future studies should incorporate screening measures to identify and exclude participants with these conditions. Moreover, longitudinal studies could provide valuable insights into adherence patterns over time and their relationship with various factors, including cognitive impairments and mental health issues. By tracking participants longitudinally, researchers can better understand how these factors influence adherence behaviour and identify potential confounding variables.

Conclusion

This study elucidated several factors impacting medication adherence within the Thai Muslim rural community, with a particular focus on barriers to adherence. The findings underscore the importance of adopting a comprehensive approach to patient care among healthcare professionals, encompassing physicians, nurses and community volunteers. Such an approach prioritises a nuanced understanding of individual and societal dynamics within the community, thereby facilitating the design of tailored health education initiatives and effective strategies for medication adherence. Notably, the study underscores the significance of fostering open and constructive communication between healthcare providers and patients, while promoting client-centred care. This approach contributes to a rather optimistic perspective on disease management and self-care among patients.
