Abstract
. A restaurant dinner or a hotel stay may lead to memorable experiences when guests encounter unexpected aspects that also match their interests. For example, an origami-making station in the waiting area of a restaurant may be both surprising and enjoyable for a customer who is passionate about paper crafts. Similarly, an exhibit of 18th century harpsichords would be atypical for a hotel lobby and likely pique the interest of a guest who has a passion for Baroque music. Motivated by this insight, in this paper we introduce the new task of engineering serendipity through recommendations of items with atypical aspects. We describe an LLM-based system pipeline that extracts atypical aspects from item reviews, then estimates and aggregates their user-specific utility in a measure of serendipity potential that is used to rerank a list of items recommended to the user. To facilitate system development and evaluation, we introduce a dataset of Yelp reviews that are manually annotated with atypical aspects and a dataset of artificially generated user profiles, together with crowdsourced annotations of user-aspect utility values. Furthermore, we introduce a custom procedure for dynamic selection of in-context learning examples, which is shown to improve LLM-based judgments of atypicality and utility. Experimental evaluations show that serendipity-based rankings generated by the system are highly correlated with ground truth rankings for which serendipity scores are computed from manual annotations of atypical aspects and their user-dependent utility. Overall, we hope that the new recommendation task and the associated system presented in this paper catalyze further research into recommendation approaches that go beyond accuracy in their pursuit of enhanced user satisfaction. The datasets and the code are made publicly available at https://github.com/ramituncc49er/ATARS .



1.
Introduction and Motivation

When searching for services such as hotels or restaurants, people are often presented with an overwhelming number of high-quality options that match their search criteria, especially in large metropolitan areas. This may lead to choice overload, or overchoice
(
McGrew 1970
;
Chernev et al. 2015
)
, where an individual has to choose from a large number of items that are too difficult to compare, particularly under time constraints
(
Inbar et al. 2011
)
. Making a decision in the presence of overchoice becomes mentally exhausting and can trigger impaired self-regulation
(
Vohs et al. 2008
)
, decision paralysis, or anxiety
(
Iyengar and Lepper 2001
)
. The level of satisfaction that people experience when faced with an increasing number of choices has been observed to follow the well-known Wundt curve
(
Berlyne 1973
)
, an inverted U-shape curve originally relating stimulus intensity with its pleasantness. According to this functional dependency, as the number of choices goes up, satisfaction initially increases and then decreases
(
Shah and Wolford 2007
;
Kaimann et al. 2018
)
. Choice overload can be mitigated by reducing the number of consumer choices
(
Schwartz 2004
)
or by making one item stand out and appear better than the others
(
Scheibehenne et al. 2010
)
. In this paper, we follow the latter approach and introduce a recommender system pipeline that promotes items within the search category that possess aspects with the potential to generate serendipitous experiences for the user. Our serendipity-focused approach is justified by empirical studies showing that serendipitous encounters contribute to significant increases in satisfaction, enjoyment, and meaningfulness
(
Kim et al. 2021
)
.

The top ranked restaurant is then recommended to the user, who also has the option of choosing other top items. Importantly, the system does not disclose the extracted atypical aspects, such that, upon visiting the restaurant, its atypical aspects both surprise the user and engage his interest.

Overall, the proposed recommender system is structured as a pipeline of 3 main components:

(1)

Atypical Aspect Extraction
: For each item that satisfies the user query, process its reviews in order to identify aspects that it possesses that are atypical for its category and hence have the potential to surprise (Section
4
).

(2)

Aspect Utility Estimation
: For each atypical aspect identified above, estimate its utility to the user based on information from a user profile (Section
5
); also referred to as Utility Classification.

(3)

Serendipity-Based Re-ranking
: For each item, estimate its potential for serendipity by computing a serendipity score that aggregates utility values across all its atypical aspects. Use the serendipity score to re-rank the items from an original star-based ranking (Section
6
).

The module at Step 2 assigns the first aspect a higher utility of 3, due to the user’s interest in collecting comic books and vintage graphic novels (especially Batman), whereas the second aspect is assigned a relatively lower utility value of 1, due to the less direct relevance to the user’s interests (through the movies category). The module at Step 3 then aggregates the utility values into a user-dependent serendipity score that is used to re-rank the items. As can be seen in the pane on the right, the items that have atypical aspects are now promoted to the top of the ranking in order of their overall utility for the user, as reflected in their serendipity scores. Importantly,
the system does not disclose to the user
how the ranking was computed, in particular the roles of surprise (through atypical aspects) and utility (through relevance to the user profile). Therefore, if the user decides to go to one of the top two cafes, he is likely to experience serendipity by virtue of being surprised (due to the atypical aspects) in a positive way (due to atypical aspects matching user interests).

Surprise is an emotion caused by stimuli that confound or deviate from an individual’s expectations
(
Scherer 2005
)
. Atypical aspects have a strong potential for triggering surprise in the user. For example, a user seeking restaurant recommendations is likely to be surprised by a restaurant that has an
origami making station
in the waiting area, because this is atypical for a restaurant. The surprise-evoking potential of atypical aspects is due to their
schema-discrepancy

(
Reisenzein et al. 2019
)
, in that atypical aspects are unexpected relative to the schema associated with the corresponding domain, e.g., a comic book section relative to the restaurant schema, or a harpsichord exhibition relative to the hotel schema. In general, surprising events have the potential to increase user satisfaction because they can easily capture attention, leading to increased engagement and long dwell times
(
Horstmann 2015
)
. However, serendipity requires not only surprise, but also relevance or utility for the user, hence the importance of utility estimation. For example, users interested in paper crafts are likely to find the origami making station valuable (high utility) , whereas users with different interests may choose to ignore it (low utility). When combined with atypical aspect extraction, utility estimation will enable determining that the user who likes paper crafts is likely to experience serendipity upon visiting the restaurant, whereas other users may only be surprised by the sight of the origami making station.

The serendipity score assigned to an item aggregates the utility values of all atypical aspects across the item’s reviews, and then is used for re-ranking items in the original star-based ranking (Section
6
). However, if done repeatedly across multiple recommendation sessions, this deterministic approach can lead to diminished returns in terms of surprise, as the user can learn that the recommender system always promotes items with atypical aspects relevant to their interests. This phenomenon, where a hardwired serendipity strategy makes users start to "expect the unexpected", has been documented in HCI literature, as reviewed in
(
Makri et al. 2014
)
. Possible solutions involve inserting randomness in the serendipity engineering aspects of the recommendation process. For example, at times that are chosen in a nondeterministic fashion, the system can promote items with atypical aspects that are not necessarily known to be relevant to the user, effectively achieving a surprise-based re-ranking (Section
6
). Since the space of atypical aspects is virtually unbounded, this approach ensures that users continue encountering surprise-generating atypical aspects indefinitely. Alternatively or complementary, the system can ignore atypical aspects altogether and use different sources of surprise, or even fall back on the traditional star-based ranking. All of these techniques imply a trade-off between surprise potential and expected utility, and require maintaining a user-level history of items and their atypical aspects. These and other possible approaches for cross-session maintenance of serendipity potential are left for future work.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. In Section
2
, we summarize related work and recent progress in serendipity recommendations. In Section
3
, we introduce the overall pipeline architecture of the proposed Atypical Aspect-Based Recommender System (ATARS) and formally describe the input and output of each of the 3 processing components. In Section
4
, we describe and evaluate LLM-based approaches that identify atypical aspects in customer reviews in 3 target domains: restaurants, hotels, and hair salons. In Section
5
, we introduce a dataset of user profiles (Section
5.1
), a dataset of aspect-profile pairs that are manually annotated with utility values (Section
5.2
), as well as the LLM-based approaches for utility classification (Section
5.3
). Atypical aspect extraction and utility classification are brought together in Section
6
, where we present a method for computing the utility-based serendipity score, which is then used for the serendipity-based re-ranking of items. The paper ends with concluding remarks and ideas for future work in Section
7
.

2.
Related Work

Choice overload or overchoice in recommender systems has previously been addressed by recommending a smaller set of items
(
Bollen et al. 2010
)
that are attractive to users, based on their preferences
(
Jakob et al. 2009
;
Zhang et al. 2014
)
, ratings, item attributes, or reviews
(
Musto et al. 2017
;
Selmene and Kodia 2020
;
Li and Tuzhilin 2022
)
. Prior studies on mitigating choice overload have focused on item assortment strategies such as categorization
(
Huber and Klein 1991
)
.
Kornish and Lynch 2003
discovered that customers who had their available options ranked chose better products at lower prices than customers who had their available options in random order.

Serendipity has been recognized as an important factor for increasing user satisfaction in recommender systems, as documented in various surveys
(
Fu et al. 2023
;
Ziarani and Ravanmehr 2021
;
Kotkov et al. 2016
)
. There is a wide variety of approaches that aim to generate serendipitous recommendations. For example,
Li et al. 2020b
recommended unexpected items by modeling user interests as clusters of historical data in a latent space and calculating the weighted distance between a new item and the clusters of interests.
Kotkov et al. 2018
crowdsourced serendipity labels for a movie dataset using multiple definitions of serendipity.
Fan and Niu 2018
and
Niu et al. 2018
introduced computational approaches for serendipity and evaluated them on recommendations for online health news.
Hasan and Bunescu 2023
defined serendipity as the product between the user’s rating of an item and Bayesian surprise, calculated as the KL divergence between the user’s prior preference distribution (before consuming an item) and posterior distribution (after consuming an item) over item topics. Recommendations were then made by using collaborative filtering to find users with similar past preferences who encountered positively rated items that were surprising to them.

Serendipity-based recommendation approaches are typically organized into three stages: pre-processing, in-processing, and post-processing. The pre-processing typically involves extracting or engineering serendipity features. For example,
Li et al. 2019
constructs an asymmetric relevance network to discover weak ties between users and movies, allowing them to recommend films just beyond a user’s usual choices yet still meaningfully connected. Another pre-processing example is the task of atypical aspect extraction (ExtrAtA) from customer reviews, which was originally introduced in our work from
(
Nannaware et al. 2023
)
, together with LM-based approaches.
In-processing approaches incorporate serendipity during model training to either learn a serendipity representation or predict a serendipity score, as in
(
Fu et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2020a
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
). Post-processing methods re-rank items to generate serendipity oriented recommendation lists, as in
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
.

LLMs have been leveraged for serendipity-based recommendations in various studies.
Fu and Niu 2024
introduced SerenPrompt, a prompt-based framework to generate serendipitous recommendations. They proposed 3 types of prompts, discrete, continuous, and hybrid, and 2 prompting styles, direct and indirect, to investigate different strategies for creating serendipity.
Tokutake and Okamoto 2024
use LLMs to evaluate serendipity of items in a zero-shot setting.
Xi et al. 2025
demonstrates using LLM to compress user information and long behavior sequences to generate profiles and train an LLM-based recommender, SerenGPT, to predict serendipitous items and align its serendipity judgments with human assessments.

The serendipitous recommender system introduced in this paper leverages surprise that is triggered by encounters of atypical aspects, which are inherently unexpected. To the best of our knowledge, no prior serendipity recommendation frameworks have leveraged atypical aspects as a source of surprise.

3.
ATARS TASK DEFINITION AND SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

The primary objective of this work is to develop an Atypical Aspect-Based Recommender System (ATARS) that takes as input information about a user’s general interests together with a search query, and produces as output a list of items whose ranking is determined by two criteria:

(1)

How well the item satisfies the user query, including ratings of item quality.

(2)

How much serendipity the user is likely to experience upon consuming the item.

In this work, we consider items from three domain categories:
Restaurants
,
Hotels
, and
Hair Salons
. To illustrate the various aspects of the task, throughout most of the narrative in this paper we will primarily use examples drawn from the restaurant category.

For each domain, the input to the ATARS system will include:

•

A
user profile

u
that contains relevant information about the user, especially in terms of their interests, hobbies, passions and lifestyle.

•

A
search query

q
, be it a standalone request or a turn in a longer conversation.

•

A
set of items

i\in\mathcal{I}
, each of which is associated with a
star
rating
i.star\in[0,5]
and a number of
reviews

r\in i.R
that may mention one or more aspects of the item that are atypical for the domain.

Given the input above, the ATARS system will output a
re-ranked list of items
, where items that match the user’s search query are ranked by considering both the original star rating as well as how much serendipity the atypical aspects of the restaurant is expected to generate for the user. More formally, the recommender system first identifies a subset of items
\mathcal{I}(q)\subseteq\mathcal{I}
that are relevant to the user query
q
. Then the items in
I(q)
, their reviews, and the user profile will be processed by the 3 component pipeline as follows:

(1)

For each item
i\in\mathcal{I}(q)
, for each review
r\in i.R
, extract all aspects
AtA(r)
that are mentioned in the review and that are atypical for the target domain.

•

Collect atypical aspects across that item’s reviews in
AtA(i)=\displaystyle\bigcup_{r\in i.R}AtA(r)
.

(2)

Given a user profile
u
and an atypical aspect
a
, estimate a
utility(u,a)
measure that captures how well the atypical aspect matches the user profile in terms of being
interesting
,
appealing
, or
useful
(henceforth referred to as
useful
).

(3)

For each item
i\in\mathcal{I}(q)
, compute the user-dependent
serendipity(u,i)
by aggregating the
utility(u,a)
over all the atypical aspects
a\in AtA(i)
of that item.

•

Rank all items based on their serendipity scores and star ratings.

In the extractive annotation, only base noun phrases referring to atypical aspects are annotated. If multiple phrases refer to the same atypical aspects, we only annotate the grounding instance of the coreference chain. For example, the noun phrase "adjoining park" in the second review is not annotated, as it refers to the "Goose Creek Park" which is already annotated. However, if a review mentions an atypical category, such as "arcade games" in the first review or "local wildlife" in the third review, any category instance that is mentioned will also be annotated as atypical, such as "nfl blitz 99" in the first review or "anarchists" in the third review, respectively.
If the atypical aspect is a more complex noun phrase, we only annotate the base noun phrase that expresses the semantic core (often the syntactic head), as in "the
Goose Creek Park
behind the restaurant". The extractive annotation is meant to be used together with the original review in downstream applications, which makes it acceptable to annotate only the most important part of the phrase.

In the abstractive annotation, one or more sentences are generated that enumerate the atypical aspects mentioned in the review. The formulation is kept as close as possible to the original text while maintaining naturalness. The generated sentences are intended to be concise, usually maintaining details that are expressed in the same sentence in the review, however keeping out unimportant information about the atypical aspect that is mentioned in other sentences, or details that are vague or uncertain. Sentiment words are maintained only if it helps keep the text natural and faithful to the original. The abstractive annotation is meant to be standalone and used without the original review in downstream tasks, as such it may require some minimal rewriting of the original review formulation, e.g. adding the phrase "the restaurant", or removing opinion words such as "beats" in the first example. Sometimes reviewers use metaphors to refer to atypical aspects, in which case it is important that the abstractive version preserves the metaphorical meaning. This is the case for "local wildlife" in the third review, which refers metaphorically to types of customers that are seen relatively less often in that context.

In many aspect-based sentiment analysis approaches
(
SemEval 2014
)
, identifying
typical
aspects that are mentioned in a review is done explicitly as
aspect term extraction
. However, the task of extracting
atypical
aspects, as introduced above, cannot be solved simply by first (a) identifying all typical aspects of restaurants that are mentioned in a review, followed by (b) extracting all other noun phrases, i.e. phrases that do not refer to a typical aspect of a restaurant. In their reviews, people often mention entities or events that are not associated with the restaurant, such as "our office" in the first review, or "the farmer’s market" in the third review, and these phrases should not be extracted either. Thus, it is important that the noun phrase refers to an aspect that is
associated with the reviewed restaurant
, and that at the same time is atypical of restaurants (or unexpected for a restaurant). Finally, while Table
1
may induce the perception that atypical aspects are common, the opposite is actually true. As will be detailed in Section
4.1
below, it takes going through at least 50 reviews in order to find one review that mentions an atypical aspect. The difficulty of manually finding this "needle in a haystack" further motivates the development of automated approaches for atypical aspect extraction. In Appendix
A
, we provide further examples of atypical aspect annotations drawn from reviews of hotels (Table
15
) and hair salons (Table

).

4.1.
The ExtrAtA Dataset of Atypical Aspect Annotations

We used the Yelp dataset
(
Yelp 2023
)
as a source of reviews for the 3 target categories: Restaurant (
\sim
5M reviews), Hotel (
\sim
190K reviews), and Hair Salon (
\sim
115K reviews). Because most aspects are expressed as nouns and less frequently as verbs, we use
spaCy 2023
to collect lemmas of all nouns and verbs and compute their frequencies for each domain. We rank words in ascending order based on their counts and filter out words that appear with very low frequency, e.g., less than 10 times for the Restaurant domain, as these tend to be spelling mistakes or interjections that are purposely misspelled for extra emphasis, e.g., "amaazzing". We then consider the remaining rare words in ascending order of their frequency as candidate atypical words, extract the reviews that mention them, and read these reviews to determine which occurrences truly refer to an atypical aspect. When reading a review, all atypical aspects are annotated, not only the ones corresponding to the search word. Notwithstanding the heuristic selection of reviews based on the occurrence of rare words, overall this was still a very time-consuming process, because rare words very often appear in a review without necessarily referring to atypical aspects. For example, out of the 43 restaurant reviews that contain the lemma "poncho", in only 1 review the word "ponchos" was deemed to refer to an atypical aspect (the restaurant was selling them). The other 42 reviews contained references to ponchos that were not associated with the restaurants itself, e.g. staff helping customers put their ponchos on a rainy day, or customers describing their arrival at the restaurant on a rainy day. As we went down the list of rare words, their frequency increased, resulting in a larger number of reviews to skim through for each rare word. Overall, for the Restaurant dataset, we used as search words the rare words that appeared with a frequency of up to 187. Upon semi-automatically sifting through the
\sim
97K reviews found to contain these words, we were able to collect 114 reviews that contained atypical aspects. On average, one hour of following this process led to finding between 2 and 3 reviews containing atypical aspects for the restaurant category, whereas for the hair salon category it took on average two hours to find 1 atypical reviews. Henceforth the term
atypical review
will be used to refer to a review that contains one or more atypical aspects; analogously, the term
typical review
will be used to refer to reviews that do not contain any atypical aspect.

As illustrated in the examples from Table
1
, we organize annotations of atypical aspects on two layers:

•

A
primary
layer that contains atypical aspects that are clearly not connected to any core feature of that domain.

•

A
secondary
layer that contains atypical aspects that are related to a typical aspect, such as ambiance or location, but that stand out and are interesting on their own, separate from the core features of the domain.

For example,
’I was even encouraged to visit their
petting zoo
in the back’
would be considered a primary atypical aspect in any of the 3 categories, whereas
’There is an
interesting giant stuffed spider
that goes up and down when the door leading to the bathrooms opens and closes’
would be annotated as a secondary atypical aspect.

In
(
Nannaware et al. 2023
)
, we approached the task of atypical aspect extraction using 2 language models: Flan-T5
(
Chung et al. 2022
)
and GPT-3.5
(
OpenAI 2022
)
. FLAN-T5 is a 3 billion parameter encoder-decoder Transformer based on the T5 model
(
Raffel et al. 2020
)
that was further instruction-tuned on the FLAN dataset
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
. For atypical aspect extraction, Flan-T5 was fine-tuned and tested using a 10-fold evaluation scenario, where the Train+Test reviews dataset was partitioned into 10 folds, 9 folds were used for training and 1 fold was used for testing. This process was repeated 10 times until each fold in the dataset was used as a test fold. GPT-3.5 is a 175 billion parameters encoder Transformer from the GPT family that was used for atypical aspect extraction through a prompt that was optionally augmented with a fixed set of 5 in-context learning examples.

In this work, we developed an improved approach to atypical aspect extraction that leverages the much larger GPT-4 model, which has about 10 times the number of parameters of GPT-3.5. This new approach does extraction in 2 main steps, as follows:

•

•

: Extract atypical aspects from the list of aspect sentences, as shown below (prompt template in Table
4
):

–

Step 2.1
: Classify each aspect sentence as positive if it mentions an atypical aspect, otherwise classify it as negative.

–

Step 2.2

To evaluate the end-to-end performance of the entire atypical extraction pipeline, we compute the precision, recall, and
F_{1}
scores for the
exact
and
partial matches
of the extracted base noun phrase (BNP) with the ground truth phrase. In the exact match method, an extracted phrase is considered correct if and only if it matches exactly a ground truth (gold) phrase. Precision (P), Recall (R), and
F_{1}
measure are then computed as follows:

P=\frac{\#\mbox{\it{correct extracted BNPs}}}{\#\mbox{\it{extracted BNPs}}},\;\;\;R=\frac{\#\mbox{\it{correct extracted BNPs}}}{\#\mbox{\it{gold BNPs}}},\;\;\;F_{1}=\frac{2PR}{P+R}

In the partial match evaluation, partial credit is given to extracted phrases that overlap with ground truth phrases. For this, we use the greedy method shown in Algorithm
1
to compute a bipartite matching between gold phrases
gp
and extracted phrases
ep
that is aimed at maximizing their word overlap
|ep\cap gp|
in total. The overlaps between extracted and gold phrases are then used to compute precision, recall, and
F_{1}
.

Given that atypical item extraction generally does not require deep understanding of the text or sophisticated reasoning, we believe the much better performance of the GPT-4 model is partly explained by the decomposition into separate tasks in the two-step pipeline approach, compared to the original monolithic approach followed for GPT-3.5. The results presented in Appendix

Lastly, similar to the aspect sentence classification results, the dynamic RAG-based approach for selecting the 8-shot in-context learning examples improves performance substantially, outperforming all methods across all evaluation scenarios. MAPPING ATYPICAL ASPECTS TO USER-DEPENDENT UTILITY

The second step in the system architecture pipeline, as highlighted in Figure
5
, estimates a user-dependent utility for each of the atypical aspects extracted at the first step. More formally, given a user profile
u
and a review
r
containing a set of atypical aspects
a\in AtA(r)
, the task is to assign each atypical aspect a utility value
utility(u,a)
that reflects how useful or relevant that atypical aspect is for the user. With an eye towards both ease of manual annotation and discriminative power, we defined 4 levels of utility as
None=0.0
,
Low=0.5
,
Medium=0.75
, and
High=1.0
. With these 4 values, the utility estimation can be cast as a utility classification task.

The development and evaluation of the utility module requires 3 types of data: a set of user profile
u\in\mathcal{U}
, a set of reviews
r
annotated with atypical aspects
a\in AtA(r)
, and a set of
\langle u,a\rangle
pairs that are annotated with their
utility(u,a)
values. Publicly available user profiles, however, are very rare, especially profiles that indicate a user’s hobbies, passions, or lifestyles, as illustrated in Figure
2
. Correspondingly, in Section
5.1
we introduce an LLM-based approach for generating a diverse set of user profiles that can be used in combination with the dataset of reviews described in Section
4
. Based on this dataset of user profile and the dataset of customer reviews, in Section
5.2
we introduce a crowd-sourced dataset of
utility(u,a)
mappings. These mappings are then used to provide in-context learning examples for the LLM-based utility classification approach that will be introduced in Section
5.3
, as well as an evaluation test bed for the experimental evaluations reported in Section
5.4
.

Dataset of Utility Value Annotations

Atypical aspects, which are inherently surprising, have varying degrees of usefulness or utility for a user. For example, the fictional user Sheldon from Figures
8
and
2

In contrast, if he visited Julia’s Cafe and Books, it is likely that he would be delighted to discover the
Batman Adventures issue from 96
, due to his being a fan of vintage comic books, particularly of Batman comics, as is
directly
mentioned in his profile. Similarly, if he visited Undercurrent Coffee, he would likely appreciate the presence of
autographed memorabilia from movies such The Dark Knight
, an interest that is
indirectly
related to his interest in comic book movies which he reviews on his YouTube channel.

We refer to objects or activities stated in the profile that a user finds interesting, appealing, or useful, as
attractors
; these typically relate to the user’s hobbies, passions, or lifestyle. As described in the annotation guidelines from Section
5.2.1
below, determining the user-dependent utility of an atypical aspect is done by considering its relationship to semantically related attractors in the user profile.

5.2.1.
Utility Annotation Guidelines

In order to balance the effort and reliability of manual annotation of utility values vs. their discriminative power, we chose to define 4 levels of utility
None=0.0
,
Low=0.5
,
Medium=0.75
, and
High=1.0
, as described below.

The
None
utility value is assigned to an atypical aspect in any of the cases below:

(1)

The
atypical aspect
lacks any relevance to the
attractors
in the user profile.

(2)

The atypical aspect, despite appearing relevant, is too specific for the user’s very broad interests or hobbies in the profile. For example, an atypical aspect such as
live music
, is too specific for the very broad attractor creating a
playful and family-friendly
environment.

(3)

The atypical aspect involves materials or items that, despite appearing relevant, belong to a completely different context or are used for a completely different purpose than those in the user profile. For example:

-

In the review the atypical aspect of
high-end look
pertains to architecture or interior design, whereas the attractor
high-end look
is about fashion choices.

-

If according to the profile a user is interested in
modern plastic furniture
for home design, this does not necessarily translate to an interest in all plastic items. Therefore, the utility of an atypical aspect like
plastic ponchos
that are sold by a restaurant should be annotated as None.

The
Low
utility value is assigned to an atypical aspect in any of the cases below:

(1)

The attractor and the atypical aspect are distinct and specific concepts with only a small degree of conceptual similarity between them. For example, if a user is passionate about
surfing
, the utility of an atypical aspect such as a
sand sculpture
or a
swimming pool
should be annotated as Low, as they have only a small degree of conceptual similarity to surfing.

(2)

The attractor and the atypical aspect are very broad concepts with a very limited conceptual overlap. For example, if a user has a general interest in
visual aesthetics
, which can include
photography
,
painting
, and
architectural design
, but the atypical aspect is
art
, which can include
handicrafts
,
paintings
, and
sculptures
, then even though there is some overlap between these two very broad concepts, the overlap is small, as such the utility of the atypical aspect should be annotated as Low.

The
Medium
utility value is assigned to an atypical aspect in any of the cases below:

(1)

The attractor is a broad concept, while the atypical aspect is a more specific concept subsumed by that broad concept. For example, if a user is passionate about a broad topic such as
interior design
, an atypical aspect like a
lime green counter
would have Medium utility, as it may be very appealing to the user.

(2)

Conversely, the attractor is a specific concept that is subsumed by the broader atypical aspect. For example, if a user enjoys listening to
live pop music performances
, they might also be interested in listening to music in general, hence the atypical aspect
music
would have Medium utility for the user.

The
High
utility value is assigned to an atypical aspect if:

(1)

The atypical aspect closely matches an attractor in the user profile. Both the attractor and the atypical aspect must involve the same specific context. For example, if the highlight of a user’s year is seeing
the Fourth of July fireworks display over the ocean
, then a restaurant that offers
a vantage point over the ocean for watching fireworks
would have High utility. In this example, it was important that the fireworks happen in the same context, i.e. over the ocean.

In Appendix
F
, we provide detailed illustrative examples of utility annotations for each of the 4 utility values. Each example shows the user profile and the review containing the atypical aspect labeled with a utility value.

Crowd-sourcing Utility Annotations with Amazon Mechanical Turk

Using the annotation guidelines above, we designed a set of instructions and examples that were used for crowd-sourcing the ground truth utility annotations through the Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) platform
(
Crowston 2012
)
. MTurk is a web service that enables a requester to create data annotation tasks, referred to as Human Intelligence Tasks (HITs), which workers then complete for a predetermined reward. For the utility annotation task, each HIT provided workers with a user profile and a review in which an atypical aspect was highlighted, and they were required to select the utility of the atypical aspect according to the provided guidelines. If a review contained more than one atypical aspect, workers could move between them in order to specify a utility value for each. The overall design of the MTurk user interface is detailed in Appendix
H
.

For each HIT, namely for each user profile, review and atypical aspect triplet
\langle u,r,a\rangle
, annotations were collected from 3 independent MTurk workers. For the experimental evaluations reported in Section
5.4
, we use only HIT triplets where at least 2 annotators agreed on the utility value, e.g. accepted HITS. To estimate the inter-annotator agreement (ITA) on utility annotations, we report the mean, median and maximum standard deviation across each domain-specific dataset. Thus, for each HIT triplet
t_{n}=\langle u,r,a\rangle
, let
v^{n}_{k}
be the numerically encoded utility value provided by the
k^{th}
annotator, with
K=3
annotations per triplet. The mean
\hat{\mu}_{n}
and standard deviation
\hat{\sigma}_{n}
for the HIT
t_{n}
are then computed as:

(1)

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{n}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}v^{n}_{k}\quad\quad\hat{\sigma}_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}(v^{n}_{k}-\hat{\mu}_{n})^{2}}

An overall mean standard deviation
\hat{\sigma}
is then calculated over all
N
HITS in the dataset, together with median
\sigma_{1/2}
and maximum
\sigma_{\text{max}}
values:

(2)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}_{n}\quad\quad\sigma_{1/2}=\med_{1\leq n\leq N}\hat{\sigma}_{n}\quad\quad\sigma_{\text{max}}=\max_{1\leq n\leq N}\hat{\sigma}_{n}

SERENDIPITY-BASED RE-RANKING OF ITEMS

So far, we have described automatic procedures for the first two components of the system pipeline, as follows:

(1)

Extraction of atypical aspects
a\in AtA(i)
from the reviews
i.R
of an item
i\in\mathcal{I}(q)
that is relevant to a user query
q
(Section
4
).

(2)

Classification of the
utility(a,u)
of an atypical aspect
a
for a user profile
u
(Section
5
),

Given the output of the two modules above, the final module of the system pipeline, as highlighted in Figure
7
, performs a serendipity-based re-ranking of the items in
\mathcal{I}(q)
.

We define the user-dependent
serendipity(u,i)
of an item
i
as the sum of the utility values of all the atypical aspects found across all the reviews of that item, where each utility value is divided by the total similarity of that aspect with all atypical aspects, including itself:

(3)

serendipity(u,i)=\sum_{a\in AtA(i)}\frac{utility(u,a)}{\displaystyle\sum_{\hat{a}\in AtA(i)}sim(a,\hat{a})}

Assuming a normalized aspect to aspect similarity measure
sim(a,\hat{a})\in[0,1]
, with a value of 1 if and only if
\hat{a}=a
, this means that:

•

When all aspects are identical, each utility value in the sum above is divided by the total number of atypical aspects for that item, effectively producing a mean utility.

•

When each aspect has zero similarity with other aspects, each utility value in the sum above is divided by 1, effectively producing a sum of utilities.

Note that by summing up user-dependent utilities for atypical aspects, which by definition have strong potential for surprise, the measure computed in Equation
3
captures the two core features of serendipity, namely surprise (through atypical aspects) and relevance (through utility).

Figure 7.
ATARS system architecture: Serendipity-based Re-ranking.

We also define a user-independent measure of
surprise(i)
for item
i
by setting the utility value to
utility(u,a)=1
for all atypical aspects of that item in Equation
3
, which results in:

(4)

surprise(i)=\sum_{a\in AtA(i)}\frac{1}{\displaystyle\sum_{\hat{a}\in AtA(i)}sim(a,\hat{a})}

By setting the utility to a constant, the resulting surprise measure reflects only the aggregate surprise caused by all of the item’s atypical aspects, and ignores their potential relevance to the user.

6.2.
Discussion of Results

The results in Tables
13
and
14
show that rankings based solely on star ratings (GT
\star
) obtain a very low correlation with the ground truth serendipity-based rankings. Customers may provide high ratings to restaurants that lead to memorable experiences caused by surprising aspects, which means that theoretically star ratings may indirectly reflect the potential serendipity of an item. However, the low correlations between the star-based rankings and ground truth serendipity-based rankings show that the serendipity signal contained in star ratings is weak, and thus star ratings are insufficient for promoting items with high potential for serendipity. This demonstrates the importance of designing a ranking function that captures explicit, direct measures of surprise or serendipity.

As expected, the results also show that surprise-based system rankings (Sys
\circ
Sur and Sys
\star
Sur) are much less correlated with the ground truth serendipity-based rankings (GT
\circ
Seren and GT
\star
Seren) than the serendipity-based system rankings (Sys
\circ
Seren and Sys
\star
Seren), demonstrating the importance of estimating user-dependent utility and integrating it in the serendipity measure. The assumption behind the ground truth serendipity-based rankings is that users are not habituated with ATARS, i.e. they do not expect a recommender system that promotes items with atypical aspects that match their interests. However, as noted in Section
1
, the serendipitous effect may diminish if the approach is employed repeatedly across multiple recommendation sessions. In such cases, one solution is to insert more randomness in the process, e.g. by randomly choosing between serendipity-based and surprise-based re-rankings at each recommendation session.

Finally, re-rankings based on a combination of star ratings with serendipity scores lead to higher correlations with the ground truth rankings, as observed by looking at the Mean results in Table
14
vs. Table
13
. This is expected, as including star rating information in both system and ground truth re-rankings provides a base level of correlation between the two.

7.
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we described a new method for engineering serendipity in recommender systems by leveraging items with atypical aspects that are relevant to the user. To this aim, we introduced an Atypical Aspect-based Recommender Systems (ATARS) that is implemented as a three-stage pipeline, where the first component processes item reviews in order to identify aspects that are atypical of the item’s category, and thus have high potential for surprise. The second component estimates the user-dependent utility of an atypical aspect based on information in a user profile, whereas the third component integrates the utility values across all atypical aspects of an item into a serendipity score that is used to re-rank the list of items. We introduced datasets of customer reviews that were manually annotated with atypical aspects, and crowd-sourced datasets of utility annotations. These datasets were used to provide in-context learning (ICL) examples and evaluation benchmarks for the corresponding components. In particular, we introduced a new LLM-based approach for extracting domain-atypical aspects from customer reviews, which was shown to substantially improve over a previous fine-tuned approach. Similarly, we developed LLM-based approaches for mapping atypical aspects to user-dependent utility scores, ensuring that aspects are not only surprising, but also relevant to an individual’s hobbies and passions. A dynamic approach for selection of ICL examples was introduced and shown to improve performance. Extensive experimental evaluations of the system-generated rankings based on surprise or serendipity scores show high correlations with ground-truth rankings where serendipity is computed from manual annotations of atypical aspects and utility values.
Results also demonstrate that traditional star ratings are insufficient to promote serendipitous recommendations, thus justifying the need for the explicit modeling of surprise and utility.

In future work, we plan to explore methods for maintaining the serendipity effect across recommendation sessions that employ randomness in the serendipity generation mechanisms, such as randomly selecting between user-dependent serendipity measures and user-independent surprise measures for re-ranking.
