AI4EOSC: a Federated Cloud Platform for Artificial Intelligence in Scientific Research Journal: Future Gener. Comput. Syst.
Ignacio Heredia, Álvaro López García, Germán Moltó, Amanda Calatrava, Valentin Kozlov, Alessandro Costantini, Viet Tran, Mario David, Daniel San Martín, Marcin Płóciennik, Marta Obregón Ruiz, Saúl Fernandez, Judith Sáinz-Pardo Díaz, Miguel Caballer, Caterina Alarcón Marín, Stefan Dlugolinsky, Martin Šeleng, Lisana Berberi, Khadijeh Alibabaei, Borja Esteban Sanchis, Pedro Castro, Giacinto Donvito, Diego Aguirre, Sergio Langarita, Vicente Rodriguez, Leonhard Duda, Andrés Heredia Canales, Susana Rebolledo Ruiz, João Machado, Giang Nguyen, Fernando Aguilar Gómez, Jaime Díez

Abstract
In this paper, we describe a federated compute platform dedicated to support Artificial Intelligence in scientific workloads. Putting the effort into reproducible deployments, it delivers consistent, transparent access to a federation of physically distributed e-Infrastructures. Through a comprehensive service catalogue, the platform is able to offer an integrated user experience covering the full Machine Learning lifecycle, including model development (with dedicated interactive development environments), training (with GPU resources, annotation tools, experiment tracking, and federated learning support) and deployment (covering a wide range of deployment options all along the Cloud Continuum). The platform also provides tools for traceability and reproducibility of AI models, integrates with different Artificial Intelligence model providers, datasets and storage resources, allowing users to interact with the broader Machine Learning ecosystem. Finally, it is easily customizable to lower the adoption barrier by external communities.

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI), Machine Learning (ML), and Deep Learning (DL) are transforming the scientific and industrial research panorama. The impact of these techniques over the last years has revolutionized the field, e.g. by making it possible to tackle new scientific endeavors
[
24
,
69
,
42
]
, by automatizing manual and tedious tasks
[
73
]
or by speeding up some cost and effort expensive tasks
[
112
]
, just to cite some examples. These techniques are a reality, and they form now part of the basic set of tools for a wide set of scientists and researchers
[
56
]
.

We argue that researchers willing to exploit these state of the art techniques need not only to be able to get access to appropriate compute and storage resources, but also to more advanced platforms and tools that can broaden their adoption, lowering the entry barriers, easing the data exploration and analysis, model development, training, sharing and deployment, in an open, transparent and collaborative way. With a broader scope, the European Commission is currently engaged in the implementation of the European Open Science Cloud (EOSC)
[
21
]
, that aims to become the ”The web of FAIR data and services“
[
40
]
, as a transformative process towards the promotion of open science practices among researchers, also delivering a comprehensive environment for the sharing, processing, and analysis of research data.

In this context, in this manuscript we present the AI4EOSC platform, a federated system that provides a comprehensive cloud-based toolbox for the development, training, sharing and deployment of ML/DL/AI models also focusing on their traceability and reproducibility. The AI4EOSC federated platform enables data processing from the cloud to edge continuum, being able to mobilize different resources for different needs. Moreover, this platform is being developed in the context of the EOSC, both from the technical perspective including seamless integration and interoperability with the EOSC ecosystem, but also from the perspective of strengthening open science practices as a collaborative, transparent, and accessible research model.

The objective of the AI4EOSC platform is not only to provide scientists with the tools and functionalities needed to
develop
,
train
or
serve
models, but also to frame the development of these models with the open science practices. These include, for instance, following the well known FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable)
[
124
]
, providing fine-grained model provenance metadata or ensuring metadata interoperability across different formats. To this aim, we have implemented these functionalities in an automated and transparent way for the users, so that these principles can be granted by design, to the possible extent, without their intervention.

2
Related work and motivation

While there are many private platforms to deploy AI workloads for users, few of them have put an effort on addressing the open source and reproducibility requirements that are key to scientific workloads.

In addition, many of the solutions are fragmented. For example, a number of AI model repositories exists, including many devoted to scientific communities, like the BioImage Model Zoo
[
91
]
for microscopy images or Kipoi
[
10
]
for genomics. Even very popular and general purpose ones, like Hugging Face
[
125
]
or Replicate
[
104
]
, allow users to deploy their models. However, most model repositories are detached from the training process of the model, thus making users unable to assess the trustworthiness of the said model.

On the other side, many solutions exist to support users in the model training.
For example, there are a wide range of MLOps solutions
[
13
]
to track experiments, both open source (MLflow
[
126
]
, LabML
[
66
]
) and proprietary ones (Weights and Biases
[
29
]
, Valohai
[
118
]
, Comet
[
26
]
).
In parallel, several data annotation tools are available, both open source (LabelStudio
[
116
]
, Doccano
[
84
]
, refinery
[
71
]
) and proprietary (Prodigy
[
99
]
, Roboflow
[
106
]
). However, even open source ones are not always easy to use locally by users looking for advanced features (e.g. AI-assisted annotation) and offer paid cloud access to them (CVAT
[
31
]
).
In addition, most of the time researchers have to manually access external cloud providers to deploy and configure their training environments.
No tools seems cover the full training process in a single coherent platform, from labeling the data, to automatically deploying resources, developing the model and logging the training runs.

For the few AI platforms that do cover the ML process end-to-end, they are usually closed source (H2O.ai
[
55
]
) and restrict model deployment or the data storage to their specific cloud solutions (like Google Vertex AI
[
7
]
, Amazon Sage Maker
[
9
]
or Azure ML Studio
[
80
]
), thus defeating the open principles on which science is built. Others are partially open-source but do not offer all features in their community free versions (Polyaxon
[
98
]
).

Therefore, an open-source solution is needed to address the ML lifecycle end-to-end, from development to deployment, giving a more comprehensive overview of all the processes involved, thus fostering reproducibility. That platform should be approachable enough for non-technical scientists to use and offer a ready-to-use environment and workflows to streamline AI model creation. The resulting models should be reusable and not tied to the platform that created them.

One first approach to such solution was the Deep-Hybrid Datacloud (DEEPHDC) platform
[
50
]
. It deployed a training facility connected to storage resources, that users could leverage to train their models and share them in a catalog, monitored with a CI/CD pipeline that ensured software quality. It allowed to deploy them on a serverless architecture and query them though a standardized AI model API.

While this platform was a successful proof-of-concept, it showed many limitations in terms of scalability, extensibility and interoperability. The AI4EOSC platform presented in this paper, was created as an evolution of the DEEPHDC platform. It has advanced and redesigned its technologies in many ways, including:

1.

federating the Workload Management System, to be able to scale across many physically distributed datacenters,

2.

improving the overall architecture design, allowing for an easier integration of new components around a central platform API,

3.

an extensive offer of deployment options, from our serverless platform to external cloud providers, including the ability to create custom AI inference pipelines,

4.

adding new features and components to the platform, including annotation tools, federated learning, batch trainings, etc; all of them organized around a clean and intuitive dashboard,

5.

strengthening the integration, both among internal components (e.g. storage, secrets, Docker registry) and with external components (AI marketplaces, storages, clouds providers)

6.

redesigning many of the components to make them more robust, like the CI/CD pipeline, and updating them with state-of-the-art technologies, like the PaaS Orchestrator.

7.

focusing on interoperability and reproducibility, which are key to science, by developing an interoperable metadata schema, as well as a comprehensive AI model provenance system,

All these advances will be presented in the following section.

The AI4EOSC platform

The AI4EOSC Platform is designed to help scientists to develop, train and deploy
AI models in a user-friendly manner. By allowing transparent access to a
large set of federated computing resources, the platform ensures scalability and
efficiency of scientific workflows.

PaaS Orchestration and Provisioning

In the AI4EOSC platform, we use the
INDIGO-PaaS Orchestration system
[

to handle the deployment of platform components across the different IaaS platforms made available by the federated Cloud providers.
The system is composed of different micro-services:

1.

the Infrastructure Manager

[

submission of the deployment to the proper Cloud provider. The provider is selected according to the AI-Ranker
[

2.

the Federation-registry
[
109
]
, which is the configuration management database for dynamic cloud federation. Built using FastAPI
[
102
]
and neo4j
[
85
]
, the Federation-registry is able to efficiently manage the federation-related information and SLA agreements.

3.

the INDIGO-PaaS Orchestrator Dashboard, which provides a user-friendly interface for managing and monitoring the TOSCA-based deployments, built on Flask
[
93
]
and MySQL
[
83
]
.

With the developed TOSCA
[
94
]
templates, the PaaS Orchestration layer is able to automate the deployment of multiple platform components, including the Workload Management System (
3.3
), the AI as a Service platform (
3.4
), the Storage (
3.11
), etc.

These strong reproducible recipes make the AI4EOSC platform a suitable solution to be easily adopted by external communities wishing to profit from the technologies here developed.
Moreover, the adoption of well known technology standards allow the Cloud-enabled resource
providers to easily join the federation and contribute to the existing AI4EOSC platform.

3.3
Workload Management System

The Workload Management System (WMS) is a federation of service providers that
is used to run the platform jobs. Deployed with Ansible
[
61
]
, it uses
Consul
[
58
]
to federate the providers (following a WAN approach) and Nomad

[
59
]
to run the workloads. The current federation includes resources
from over 4 different clouds located in 4 different countries: IFCA (Spain),
IISAS (Slovakia), INCD (Portugal) and Tubitak (Turkey). It currently offers a
total of 50 GPUs, 4 CPU’s THz and 4 TB of dedicated disk.

The WMS runs both user jobs and system jobs.

User jobs include both AI modules (
3.5
) and tools (
3.6
).
AI modules can run in two modes. In standard mode, resources are allocated to users
indefinitely and users access them via an Interactive Development Environment (IDE) or an API. In batch mode, resources
are allocated to users as long as strictly needed to run the training and then
freed for other users. We have enriched the Nomad jobs with a number of side
tasks to enhance the user experience, like connecting the job with external storage
(
3.11
,
4.4
), download datasets from external
repositories at launch time (
4.3
) or notify users by email
if the jobs take long to deploy, etc. The WMS also handles short-lived try-me jobs
for users that want to test a modules functionality using a friendly Gradio UI
[
2
]
.

System jobs include Traefik
[
77
]
reverse proxies to allow users to access their
containers, Docuum
[
16
]
to manage Docker images, custom jobs to make
Docker snapshots (
3.11
), etc. System jobs are also sometimes used to deploy some of the platform components, like the Platform API (
3.14
) and the Platform Dashboard (
3.15
).

3.4
AI as a Service and AI pipelines

The AI as a Service (AIaaS) allows to deploy AI modules (
3.5
)
in the serverless inference platform, based on OSCAR
[
96
,
54
]
.
The OSCAR cluster allows to autoscale resources on demand, automatically deploying
multiple instances of the model to run predictions if needed.
The platform allows both synchronous and asynchronous (via file uploads to a MinIO object store
[

]
) calls to the endpoints.

The Dashboard (
3.15
) allows users to automatically create and manage
those serverless endpoints for any AI module in the catalog. Thanks to the
DEEPaaS API (
3.5.2
) that interfaces the module, users are able
to send inference requests to any AI module in a standardized way.

Leveraging on those serverless endpoints, the AI4EOSC Platform allows to
create AI pipelines where users can compose multiple inference requests to
different AI models, as well as data preprocessing and postprocessing steps.

Pipelines can currently be created with two different visual composition tools:
Node-Red
[
89
]
(managed via a FlowFuse
[
49
]
instance) and
Elyra
[
39
]
(integrated with EGI Jupyter Notebooks). Users can choose between
these two tools to compose their pipelines based on their working environment needs
and prior experience.

Finally, usage metrics about the serverless endpoints are generated with kube-state-metrics

[
76
]
, GoAccess
[
53
]
, and Prometheus

[
100
]
.

3.5
AI Modules

The AI modules are the main platform asset that users interact with. They are both produced

AI models are created from a module template (

3.5.2
DEEPaaS API

The AI model functionality, like training or inference, is exposed via a
common API, DEEPaaS
[
51

. This unified interaction with
all models allows the AI4EOSC Platform to:

1.

3.5.3
AI4EOSC metadata

The AI4EOSC Platform requires each AI module (
3.5
) or tool (
3.6
) that is registered in the
AI4EOSC catalog to include a valid YAML metadata file, enforcing the syntax
with JSON Schema
[
68
]
.

Our metadata schema includes both user-defined fields (some of them optional) as well
as fields automatically filled from external sources like License and
modification dates (read from GitHub). User defined-fields include title,
summary, description, DOI, external links (e.g. to dataset or model weights)
and tags (like the software libraries used or the data types used as input).

Our Platform API enables to retrieve and map the metadata into different ML-relevant formats,
using the MLDCAT Application Profile
[
111
]
. This allows for a
greater interoperability of our models with external catalogs.

to ensure that any AI module

The first set of checks use tox

3.6
Tools

Tools are a collection of assets that the AI4EOSC Platform provides to users in
order to support them during the whole ML development lifecycle.

Unlike AI modules (
3.5
), tools are developed and curated by the AI4EOSC Platform.
Once integrated, tools are easily self-deployable by users on their personal workspaces,
just like AI modules.

For technical reasons, some of these tools do not make sense to be deployed per-user
and therefore are instead offered as platform wide services, like the
Experiment Tracking (
3.12.1
) or the Drift Monitoring (
3.12.2
).
Nevertheless, to combine the strengths of both approaches, some services can be
either deployed per-user or platform-wide, like LLMs (
3.6.3
,

3.10
).

In the following, we will explain the main tools offered by the platform.

3.6.1
Development Environment

The AI4EOSC Platform allows users to deploy a ready-to-use development environment
to create their new AI modules (
3.5
). The environment can be
used out-of-the-box with multiple versions of the main Deep Learning frameworks (Tensorflow
[
1
]
,
Pytorch
[
95
]
).
Additional frameworks are also supported by starting from provided plain Ubuntu or NVIDIA CUDA flavors
and installing a custom software stack.

When configuring the deployment, users can choose their preferred IDE to edit the
code. The platform currently supports both VSCode
[
81
]
(bundled with many
useful extensions, and installed using code-server
[
25
]
), and JupyterLab

[
70
]
. All IDE endpoints are password protected to prevent
malicious actors to access user data and code.

3.6.2
Federated Learning servers

The AI4EOSC Platform allows for the creation of federated learning servers
[
87
]
,
both using Flower
[
14
]
and NVFLARE
[
107
]

frameworks. Additionally, we have extended Flower to support token-based
client-server authentication, added novel aggregation methods (like FedAvgOpt

[
34
]
) as well as incorporated server-side
metric differential privacy
[
33
]
with fixed clipping, in order to
prevent attackers from retrieving training data at inference time from the aggregated model and to defend against client inference attacks.

For running the training, federated clients can be deployed both on the AI4EOSC
Platform or connect from outside, thus easily accommodating the individual
privacy needs of the different data holders.

This flexible and distributed approach for training models allows to leverage a
wide range of resources in the Cloud continuum that could not support by
themselves a full ML training cycle due to their low capacity (e.g. edge
devices).

Still, by self-deploying the tool, users are able to leverage administrator-only
OpenWebUI features like the generation of Knowledge Bases or the use of Functions

For a rich user experience, we offer the ability to choose from a wide variety
of models from different AI providers (like Qwen

]
, Meta
[

]
, DeepSeek

3.6.4
Annotation tools

The AI4EOSC Platform allows user to deploy the Computer Vision Annotation Tool
(CVAT)
[
31

to annotate images for training Computer Vision models. By
deploying CVAT on dedicated resources (including GPUs), users can streamline the

Once deployed, users can then automatically read their data and save their annotations directly into our storage (

Automated periodic backups are in place to prevent any unexpected loss of the time-costly annotations.

3.7
Authentication

At the PaaS layer the AI4EOSC Platform adopts the
INDIGO Identity and Access Management (INDIGO-IAM)
[
63
]
service, in line
with the use of other INDIGO components at the PaaS layer (
3.2
).
It features attribute and identity management (to manage group membership, attributes assignment and account linking), as well as Multi Factor Authentication integration with OIDC, SAML and X.509.

At the service layer, the AI4EOSC Platform currently uses Keycloak
[
72
]
to manage authentication to the whole software stack. This allows to seamlessly onboard new external communities (
6
)
by transparently brokering their own authentication systems (e.g. EGI Check-In
[
43
]
, eduGAIN
[
38
]
). Based on the identity provider and the user entitlements in that provider, we assign specific roles to users.

We allow different access tiers based on the different user roles. For example,
"demo access" tier users will only be able to deploy short-lived jobs (
3.3
) with the Dashboard (
3.15
) while "full access"
tier users will be able to access the whole ecosystem.
We support additional authentication granularity to allow access to only
specific services, like the storage (
3.11
) or the AI4EOSC LLM (
3.10
).

To properly account for resource usage, we group users into Virtual Organizations
(VO). We deploy a different Dashboard (
3.15
) for each VO, leveraging
the common Platform API (
3.14
) to expose VO-finetuned services and configurations,
as well deploy jobs in specific datacenters in the WMS (
3.3
)
and the AI as a Service components (
3.4
).

3.8
Docker registry

The AI4EOSC Platform has its own Docker registry instance, based on Harbor
[
57
]
.
This allows to overcome the limitations of Docker Hub’s
[
35
]
free-tier
and a tighter integration with our authentication system (
3.7
).

This registry holds all the Docker images of the different components of the
platform, as well as the images of the user AI modules (
3.5
) that
are additionally mirrored in Docker Hub. By storing those images in a public registry
we increase the reusability of the assets generated by the platform, avoiding the
vendor lock-in usually imposed by Big Tech AI platforms.

Using this registry, we are also able to provide the users with the ability of
creating snapshots of their deployments (
3.3
)
allowing them to redeploy when needed. This avoids to keep consuming resources when they
need to pause their work.

3.11
Storage

Storage is a key component in the AI development cycle, as AI models are particularly
data hungry.

The AI4EOSC Platform features its own dedicated Nextcloud instance
[
86
]

for users to store their data and models, with current total capacity of 50 TB
and a default 500 GB quota per user (extensible).

This storage is then automatically mounted inside the user’s WMS deployments (
3.3
) as a
virtual file system using Rclone
[
103
]
so that they can transparently access their datasets while developing and training their AI modules.

Additionally, this user storage is leveraged by the platform for saving external
datasets (
4.3
) and backup information from some tool
deployments like CVAT (
3.6.4
).

The Dashboard (
3.15

subsequently generated token will be saved in the secrets management (

3.12
MLOps

Extending the DevOps approach to the ML realm, we have
deployed a series of tools to allow the users to address the key challenges
that specifically arise during the ML cycle.

3.12.1
Experiment tracking

Users deploying AI modules (
3.5

can log their training metrics into a dedicated MLflow
[

tracking server instance we have deployed, featuring a custom Authentication GUI to be able to integrate it with our authentication (

By logging their different training runs and

3.12.2
Drift monitoring

The AI4EOSC Platform has developed a drift monitoring system, DriftWatch. When
AI modules (
3.5
) are created, they can choose to train a
detector on labeled clean and faulty data. Then, at inference time, when these
models are deployed in production, the detector can be used to collect and send metrics to the DriftWatch
server. From an intuitive UI, users are able to log in and visualize whether drift has occurred,
eventually inspecting the data that caused the drift by entering their storage
(
3.11
).

Driftwatch is agnostic to the underlying drift detection framework chosen by the developer
(Alibi-detect
[
119
]
, Evidently

[
47
]
, etc).
To address the shortcomings of other popular frameworks, we also developed our own drift detection framework, Frouros
[
113
]
, supporting a large number of detectors and integrated with the AI as a Service component (
3.4
)
[
114
]

3.13
Provenance

Provenance is essential for science and the FAIR principles

[
124
]
. The AI4EOSC Platform has built a pipeline that allows
traceability of any AI module (
3.5
), thus improving their
reproducibility.

Once a module has committed changes to the GitHub code, the CI/CD pipeline triggers the provenance
fetching, gathering information from multiple sources: metadata (
3.5.3
)
and training resources (
3.3
) from the Platform API (
3.14
),
experiment tracking metrics from MLflow (
3.12.1
) and
build information from the CI/CD pipeline itself (
3.5.4
).

These non-semantic metadata fragments are stored in JSON format in a PostgreSQL database

[
115
]
managed by an API built with Spring
[
17
]
. Then,
using the RDF Mapping Language (RML) via CARML
[
22
]
, we convert the plain
JSON metadata into an RDF provenance graph (W3C PROV
[
123
]
), optionally using domain
specific ontologies. Having the provenance data into RDF allows for an easier conversion
to external formats like FAIR4ML
[
23
]
.

Finally we built an interactive user-friendly provenance graph using Typescript

[
15
]
and D3.js
[
88
]
, that can be visualized from the
Dashboard (
3.15
).

We also allow users to query the provenance graph in natural language (e.g. "What dataset was used to train this model?") by passing the RDF file as context to the AI4EOSC LLMs (
3.10

5
Usage scenarios

In this section we will explain how scientists are leveraging the AI4EOSC
Platform all along for the ML lifecycle.

5.1
Developing a model

In this case, a scientist wants to develop a new AI module (
3.5
)
not already available in AI4EOSC module catalog.

For this, they would log to the Dashboard (
3.15
) and deploy a Development
Environment (
3.6.1
). They would then create a
template (
3.5.1
) for their code by answering some
questions. Now they can start adding the Deep Learning code of their application,
interfacing it with the DEEPaaaS API (
3.5.2
), editing the metadata
(
3.5.3
) and fine-tuning the Docker deployment image.

Once ready, they will integrate it in our modules catalog, where our CI/CD pipeline
(
3.5.4
) will build the Docker image (
3.8
) and refresh the different components.
The new module is now ready to use from the Dashboard.

5.2
Training a model

In this case a scientist wants to train an existing AI module (
3.5
)
already available in AI4EOSC module catalog.

For this, they would log to the Dashboard (
3.15
) and select the
module they want to train. In the deployment configuration, they will be
careful to select the proper resources (like GPUs for faster training) and
connect their deployment with the storage (
3.11
,

4.4
), so that they can easily access their training
data from inside the deployment. Optionally, they can select an external dataset (
4.3
)
to be downloaded at launch time and used for training. If their data needs to be
annotated, they can subsequently deploy annotations tools (
3.6.4
)
to do so.

Once, they deployment is ready in the WMS (
3.3
) they can connect to it and choose to run the training
from the command line or from the DEEPaaS UI (
3.5.2
),
optionally logging the results in the experiment tracking component (
3.12.1
).

If their data is especially sensitive or their training is distributed, they can
deploy a Federated Learning server (
3.6.2
) to orchestrate
the training.

The generated trained model can then be integrated in the module catalog, as
in the model development case (
5.1
).

5.3
Deploying a module

In this case a scientist wants to deploy an existing AI module (
3.5
)
for inference.

For this, they would log to the Dashboard (
3.15
) and select the
module they want to deploy. They will be able to select among different
deployment options: (1) deploy in the AI as a Service component (
3.4
)
if they only want to consume resources on-demand, (2) deploy in the WMS (
3.3
)
if they want their module to be permanently deployed for low-latency, and (3) deploy
in external resources (
4.1
) if they want to host it outside the platform.

With any of those options, the user will be able to select the amount of
computing resources to dedicate. Depending on the deployment option, inference will be
made through a UI or via HTTPS calls (or both).

Finally, they can connect their deployment to our Drift Monitoring service (
3.12.2
)
to keep track of the inference data process and visualize when their
data starts to drift, thus avoiding a degradation in inference performance.

If their inference pipeline needs the interaction of two or more modules, they
can use our Pipeline component (
3.4
) to create their
custom workflow.

5.4
Deployment along the Computing Continuum

The AI4EOSC platform can leverage the computing continuum in a multi-faceted approach. First, by deploying pre-trained AI models for inference on OSCAR clusters deployed on arm64-based low-powered devices such as clusters of Raspberry PIs (see
[
105
]
), to perform data processing closer to where it is generated. Second, by offloading AI inference executions into HPC supercomputers via the integration of OSCAR with interLink
[
3
]
, as part of a collaboration between the AI4EOSC and interTwin
[
64
]
Horizon Europe projects. Finally, by splitting AI inference requests across replicated OSCAR services on geographically distributed OSCAR clusters, as demonstrated via the combined usage of Chameleon Cloud and EGI Federated Cloud to support the execution of fish detection on a transatlantic testbed of Kubernetes-based clusters for scalable AI inference
[
4
]
. Further work on integrating with external cloud providers has been described in Section
4.1

6
Platform Customizations and wider adoption

To foster the adoption of the platform, we have streamlined the process of integrating external communities, offering them a customized experience. For this several elements are in place:

1.

We can easily federate any external Identity provider into our Keycloak-based authentication system (
3.7
). Then creating a new Virtual Organization for that community, we can create a mapping to give them the desired roles and permissions in the platform. This means that any user from that community will have immediately access to our platform.

2.

We can deploy a customized dashboard (
3.15
) tailored to that community. The customizations can including color theme, custom links to the community documentation and a custom subset of the AI module catalog (e.g. showing only computer vision models to the iMagine community
[
62
]
, devoted to imaging in aquatic science).

3.

We can integrate the computing resources that each community might bring, either adding them to the common pool or making them exclusive for that community. This can be done by adding the resources to a dedicated Nomad namespace in the WMS (
3.3
) and by deploying over them a dedicated OSCAR cluster for inference (
3.4
), as was done with the iMagine community.

Through this process, we have successfully deployed customized versions of the platform for the iMagine
[
62
]
and AI4Life
[
5
]
communities. The interactions with those communities has led to a virtuous circle, where the platform has guided them on their adoption of AI
[
11
]
and they have in turn provided feedback for a user-driven platform development.

The platform solutions were actively verified by twenty real-life official use cases coming from AI4EOSC and iMagine projects, as well as by several other individual partners. The platform currently supports a total of 122 users from around 50 different institutions belonging to 17 countries.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

With a fragmented AI ecosystem increasingly dominated by large corporations, a scalable open-source solution putting the focus on reproducibility and interoperability is needed. To address this challenge, we have here presented the AI4EOSC platform. We provide a comprehensive set of tools that target the full ML lifecycle, backed with easy to use interfaces and dashboards, a federated workload management system to train models, options to deploy inference models along the whole Cloud Continuum, the ability to create composable inference workflows paired with our AI as a Service serverless framework, built-in AI model provenance to foster trustworthiness, an extensive CI/CD pipeline that ensures AI code quality, reproducible recipes for a consistent platform deployment, a modular extensible platform architecture that allows for the integration of many external services and catalogs, and an overall customizable system that has been adopted by many usecases from external research communities.
We hope that our contribution will help advancing the responsible adoption of AI in science.

Concerning future work, we plan to keep evolving the platform along several axes.
Specifically, we will extend the semantic support of ML assets, like datasets or models, to be able to seamlessly import, export and connect them in the platform. In addition, we plan to extend the usage of the Experiment Tracking component to serve as a model registry, enriching the models with semantic information. We will also further strengthen component integration, for example, by allowing the use of any existing AI4EOSC AI module as an annotation assistant inside the CVAT tool, using the AIaaS serverless endpoints.
Finally, we will keep adding more tools to support users in their AI model creation and will extend the integration with popular external AI catalogs (e.g. Hugging Face) to increase the service offering to users.
