Evaluation of Occlusal Stains and Surface Roughness of Prefabricated Zirconia Crowns in Pediatric Dentistry: An Observational and In Vitro Study
Authors: Hammam A Bahammam, Ahmed F Rady
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims and backgroundThe purpose of this study is to assess the alteration of surface roughness and occlusal stains in zirconia crowns produced for pediatric dental applications.Materials and methodsThis study combined clinical observation and in vitro experimentation. The clinical component involved collecting data from 20 pediatric patients under the age of 6 years who received zirconia crowns in their upper teeth and stainless steel crowns (SSCs) in their lower teeth. Introduction

Early childhood caries (ECC) is one of the most commonly chronic diseases found in children, often resulting in severe decay and loss of primary teeth early in their life.1 Untreated ECC significantly affects the oral condition of a child, creating problems in mastication, phonetics, and esthetic abilities. It may lead to certain deleterious sequelae such as edentulous spaces and occlusal problems. Conditions such as these may also predispose a child to functional disorders such as malocclusion and temporomandibular joint disorders due to abnormal masticatory forces.2,3

Given the severity of ECC and the frequent extractions that happen in preschool children, full-coverage restorations such as prefabricated crowns have become vital in pediatric restorative dentistry. These restorations not only restore oral function, integrity of the arch, and occlusal harmony but also prevent loss of space and its consequences.1,4 Among the available restorative materials, prefabricated zirconia crowns are gaining lots of interest due to better esthetic qualities, greater strength, and biocompatibility in comparison to older ones such as stainless steel crowns (SSCs).5,6

Zirconia crowns are favorable for esthetic areas or for patients who need superior esthetics, due to their different features, tooth-like color, and highly polished surfaces.7,8 Nonetheless, there are some disadvantages to zirconia crowns. Recent clinical evidence and lab findings have reported numerous black occlusal stains and changes in the surfaces of zirconia crowns especially when they come in contact with each other (with SSCs or with other zirconia crowns).9,10 Overall, it is well established that zirconia crowns show far better stain resistance as a group. However, certain clinical situations with other materials show black occlusal stains that resist even professional polishing techniques.9,11 Several hypotheses have been given to define this event, for example, material transfer from metal antagonists, discoloration from biofilms, saliva chemistry, and mechanical abrasion by the forces of masticatory repetition.12,13 An understanding of the mechanisms underlying these stains will be essential because they not only determine the restorative material of choice by a clinician but also influence patient satisfaction.

Scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDX) are analytical techniques commonly used to assess surface alteration and elemental composition change in dental restorative materials. SEM is used to visualize microstructural surface degradation by providing high resolution, wear pattern, and microscopic cracks that can be viewed, while EDX ordinarily gives the elemental composition of used materials.14 This study aims to evaluate surface roughness alterations after simulated mastication of SSCs against zirconia crowns, and zirconia crowns against zirconia crowns, additionally to assess the severity and prevalence of occlusal stains through clinical case analysis, in vitro simulation, and correlating clinical findings with these results to identify the causative factors of staining. Surface roughness changes and occlusal staining phenomena within zirconia crowns will be investigated regarding their clinical implications when zirconia crowns face either SSCs or zirconia crowns by employing clinical case analysis and in vitro simulations. The research links clinical observation results to in vitro data to determine essential material-altering elements and establish a clear insight regarding the long-term functional aspects of prefabricated zirconia restorations.

Materials and Methods

A mixed-methods approach consisting of retrospective clinical data with a controlled in vitro experimental study to evaluate occlusal color changes in prefabricated zirconia crowns used in pediatric dentistry.

Clinical Component

A review was conducted on 48 documented cases of stained zirconia crowns collecting patient information, duration of the crowns, details about opposing materials (either stainless steel or zirconia), and assessments of staining severity using a standardized shade guide, and presence of any material deformation using SEM/EDX from patients treated in the last 5 years. The included patients had full-mouth rehabilitation under general anesthesia in which zirconia crowns were placed on the upper primary molars for esthetic purposes and SSCs were placed in the mandibular primary molars due to financial and clinical challenges (Fig. 1).

Placement of zirconia crown and stainless steel denture

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was calculated for comparison of means between two independent groups using G*Power version 3.1.9.4 with an a priori power analysis. Statistical power (1-β) of 0.95 and effect size (Cohen's d) of 1.06 from pilot data were used to calculate the adequate sample size with a two-tailed independent samples t-test at alpha (α) of 0.05. An analysis based on 24 participants in each group (overall N = 48) was needed to provide adequate statistical power. Such calculations ensured the minimum risk of type II errors.

In Vitro Component

In the in vitro experimentation sample was divided into two groups: group I included zirconia crowns opposed by zirconia crowns and group II included zirconia crowns opposed by SSCs as shown in Figure 2.

Group I: zirconia crown vs zirconia crown; group II: stainless steel crown vs zirconia crown

Dual-axis dual simulator chewing simulation machine (Robota, Egypt) was used for the chewing simulation. Stainless steel styli were mounted at the force application ends of the machine to standardize the mechanical interaction. The acrylic blocks (Acrostone, Egypt) holding 48 specimens (24 zirconia crown against zirconia crown and 24 zirconia crowns against SSC) were securely positioned at the base of distilled water-filled tanks in the lower section of the machine dipped in artificial saliva to maintain hydration and simulate oral conditions (Fig. 3). The stimulation was operated in a total of 80,000 cycles representing 4 months with a standard force of children's bite from 20 to 49 N in artificial saliva used for lubrication and debris removal at 1 Hz frequency to mimic natural chewing rhythm. The profilometer was used to assess surface roughness before and after each simulation interval. SEM/EDX was used to assess wear patterns and surface topography. Spectrophotometry was used to measure stain severity and area.

Chewing simulator—ROBOTA—RCS40S

Materials and Sample Preparation

NuSmile (USA)-manufactured zirconia crowns and stainless steel crowns were used in both the in vitro experimentation and the clinical observational study.

SEM and EDX Analysis

A SEM (Model Quanta 250 FEG, Holland) was used, attached with an EDX unit, to function as the instrument for evaluating sample morphological structures. Desirable electron gun values measured 1 nm while the SEM machine worked at 30 kV of accelerating voltage during analysis. The microscope operated at two levels of magnification, which reached 200× and 100,000× to capture distinct details of crown material surface alterations and wear features, together with elemental exchanges. The set parameters delivered advanced imaging quality plus element detection capacity, which permitted complete evaluation of structural transformations and compositional changes within the samples.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 27. The Shapiro–Wilk test determined the normality distribution of continuous variables. An independent samples t-test was used to assess the differences between the mean surface roughness properties of the two experimental groups. A significance level lower than a p-value of 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Ethical Consideration

The approval for this study was obtained from the Research and Ethical Committee (REC), FD-ERU-REC(23). Moreover, there is no information (names, initials, hospital identification numbers, or photographs) in the submitted manuscript that can be used to identify patients.

Results

Our study included 48 pediatric patients aged lower than 6 years old with zirconia crowns in their maxillary molars and SSCs in their mandibular molars. Dark brown spots were observed on the surface of zirconia crowns in which multiple polishing techniques failed to remove them. After tooth exfoliation, the crown was collected from the patient to study it under SEM. The SEM analysis of the zirconia crown confirmed increased texture and displayed abundant carbon (C) deposits lodged in the abnormal areas (Fig. 4).

Scanning electron microscopy analysis of exfoliated tooth

In spectrometry, it was revealed that the exfoliated tooth had an increased quantity of C, oxygen (O), and zirconium (Zr) elements. Additionally, stains of several other elements were also observed, including sodium (Na), aluminum (Al), chlorine (Cl), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), chromium (Cr), and iron (Fe) as shown in Figure 5.

In Vitro Experimentation

The results of EDX after mastication for Zr vs Zr crowns revealed a high quantity of Zr was deposited in the opposing crown, with deposition of some other organic elements, such as C, O, and Al (Fig. 6).

Energy depressive X-ray analysis—Zr vs Zr crown

Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy analysis of the Zr crown vs SSC after mastication reveals that in addition to Zr, there were other elements such as Cr, O, and Fe on the surface of the Zr crown (Fig. 7).

Energy depressive X-ray analysis—Zr crown vs SSC

The analysis of group I (zirconia vs zirconia crown) and group II (zirconia vs SSC) showed statistically significant differences between surface roughness results, whereas group II presented higher roughness values (Table 1). Roughness average (Ra) was greater in group II [mean = 2.83 ± standard deviation (SD) = 0.21] than in group I (mean = 2.12 ± SD = 0.27) (p-value < 0.001) with a large effect size (Cohen's d = −2.984), signifying an extremely large effect size.

Discussion

The findings of our study provide knowledge regarding how zirconia crowns used in pediatric dentistry experience changes to their surface roughness and develop staining on their occlusal surfaces. The results showed that zirconia crowns observed discoloration while showing signs of material erosion primarily when they met SSCs. EDX elemental analysis results showed different inorganic components on the surface of the zirconia crown, thus confirming material exchange and biofilm accumulation are the causes of these modifications.

Our research study highlighted the development of staining along the occlusal surfaces as one of the main findings of the zirconia crowns. Dentists widely reported staining as a problem in zirconia restoration since the material permits opposite surface metallic ion transfer as well as organic deposit accumulation within the oral environment.15 Our findings align with the findings of Magalhães et al.16 that zirconia surfaces become discolored due to mastication force, resulting in a deposit of metallic oxides and organic matter. Another study by Chen et al.17 supports these findings both Fe and Cr were detected in the EDX analysis indicating metal movement from SSCs to zirconia crowns. This is supported by the EDX analysis of our study as shown in Figure 7. The presence of Fe and Cr confirms metallic transfer onto the zirconia surface.

The performance and durability of zirconia crowns may be affected by surface roughness. When zirconia was brought in contact with zirconia, it retained a higher Zr content. However, when zirconia was brought in contact with stainless steel, it left more inorganic residue on the surface. The research aligns with the findings of El Mandouh and Nassif18 who reported that the opposing materials with different hardness levels contribute to the material wear in restorative materials. Several studies have confirmed that rough materials allow more bacterial growth and plaque formation, leading to more severe staining.19 The maintenance of oral hygiene becomes more difficult in pediatric patients; so, this problem presents a significant risk. Surface roughness was measured only once after 80,000 cycles; the observed result shows an increase in roughness. These findings align with the findings of Wertz et al.20 which reports that mechanical and thermal stresses can affect zirconia surface quality, supporting our findings about roughness changes increased with the increase of masticating simulation cycles.

Our results demonstrate that zirconia–SSCs (group II) possess greater surface roughness values than zirconia–zirconia crowns (group I). The higher roughness values of group II, as measured through Ra, demonstrate an irregular surface that aligns with previous studies, which showed SSCs to possess coarser textures that increase both plaque retention and wear of opposing teeth.21,22 The increased roughness average (Ra) readings in group II effectively demonstrate the pronounced surface topography because topographies that are pronounced have a stronger potential to influence bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation.23

The results of this study have important clinical implications that affect pediatric dental practices. The selection of appropriate materials stands as the essential factor when performing full-mouth rehabilitation procedures for children. Proper evaluation of the material should take place when choosing opposing materials because the results showed that increased staining occurs when paired with SSCs. Polishing methods proved unable to eliminate surface stains so additional research on improved maintenance techniques and protective layers must be performed to remove staining and reduce surface roughness changes. Future research needs to incorporate patient-specific aspects of diet, oral hygiene, and fluoride exposure together with salivary composition data because these elements affect zirconia crowns durability independently. Longitudinal research on zirconia crowns for pediatric patients needs to be conducted because this study only focused on brief time results.

This study has several limitations. This study utilized 48 pediatric patients to generate information, even though this limited sample size may restrict the study's applied scope for a wide population. The reliability of the results would improve if future studies use large patient groups while collecting information at different Hospitals. Although the in vitro simulation created controlled conditions, it failed to reproduce the complete oral environment dynamics, which includes natural variations of pH levels as well as temperature and enzyme activity. This study has not incorporated an analysis of how saliva affects the process of occlusal staining. The oral environment depends heavily on saliva because this substance affects biofilm formation while controlling material breakdown.24 Further studies must include salivary analysis because it will help researchers gain better insights into how zirconia performs within oral environments.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the importance of resolving clinical problems associated with staining and surface roughness in commercially produced zirconia crowns which are widely used in pediatric dentistry. Results on both clinically observed evidence and in vitro experimentation show that SSCs are a cause of material degradation and discoloration possibly owing to surface interactions and material transfers. These findings illustrate the importance of material selection and long-term monitoring in optimizing the esthetic and function of zirconia crowns in children. Zirconia crowns are considered a stronger and more durable pediatric restoration with improved esthetic appearance. Using them leads to greater patient satisfaction and successful treatment outcomes.

Clinical Significance

The results of the study reveal the clinical significance of material choice in pediatric dentistry as zirconia crowns combined with SSCs have shown more surface roughness and occlusal staining as compared to zirconia–zirconia combinations. These outcomes highlight the importance of taking care when making the choices between competing restorative materials, for the sake of reducing the staining and material degradation while increasing the life and appearance of the pediatric restorations. New polishing techniques and protective coating may be required to deal with stubborn stains with enhanced patient satisfaction and treatment outputs in the end. This study supports the clinicians on how to be optimizing full-mouth rehabilitation strategies for youngsters with ECC.
