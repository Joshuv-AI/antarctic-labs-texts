William Cooperman, Gautam Iyer, Keefer Rowan, Seungjae Son

Abstract
. Consider a passive scalar which is advected by an incompressible flow u u and has small molecular diffusivity κ \kappa . Previous results show that if u u is exponentially mixing and C 1 C^{1} , then the dissipation time is O ⁡ ( | log ⁡ κ | 2 ) O(\lvert\log\kappa\rvert^{2}) . We produce a family of incompressible flows which are C 0 C^{0} and exponentially mixing, uniformly in κ \kappa ; however have a dissipation time of order 1 / κ 1/\kappa (i.e. exhibits no enhanced dissipation). We also estimate the dissipation time of mixing flows, and obtain improved bounds in terms of the mixing rate with explicit constants, and allow for a time inhomogeneous mixing rate which is typical for random constructions of mixing flows.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Main result

The aim of this paper is to produce an example of an incompressible flow which mixes exponentially but does not enhance dissipation.
We begin by stating our results.
Following this, we will survey the literature and place our work in the context of existing results.

Let
\kappa>0
,
u=u^{\kappa}
be a (possibly time and
\kappa
dependent) divergence-free vector field on the
d
-dimensional torus, and let
\theta^{\kappa}
solve the advection diffusion equation

(1.1)

\partial_{t}\theta^{\kappa}+u^{\kappa}\cdot\nabla\theta^{\kappa}-\kappa\Delta\theta^{\kappa}=0\,.

Our interest is in understanding the asymptotic behavior of the
dissipation time

t_{\mathrm{dis}}=t_{\mathrm{dis}}(u,\kappa)
as
\kappa\to 0
.
Recall the
dissipation time

[
FW03
,
FNW04
]
is a measure of the rate at which solutions to (
1.1
) converge to their equilibrium state and is defined to be the smallest time after which mean-zero solutions to (
1.1
) are guaranteed to dissipate half of their initial
L^{2}
energy.
Since the flows we consider are time-inhomogeneous,

(1.2)

t_{\mathrm{dis}}(u,\kappa)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sup_{s\geqslant 0}t_{\mathrm{dis}}^{s}(u,\kappa)\,,

where for every
s\geqslant 0
,
t_{\mathrm{dis}}^{s}(u,\kappa)
is defined by

(1.3)

t_{\mathrm{dis}}^{s}(u^{\kappa},\kappa)^{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\inf\Big\{t\geqslant 0\nonscript\>\Big|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\allowbreak\text{for every }\theta^{\kappa}_{s}\in\dot{L}^{2},\text{ we have }\lVert\theta^{\kappa}_{s+t}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\lVert\theta^{\kappa}_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}}{2}\Big\}\,.

Here the space
\dot{L}^{2}\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is the space of mean-zero square integrable functions on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
Moreover, the function
\theta^{\kappa}
in the in the infimum above is a solution to (
1.1
) with initial data
\theta^{\kappa}_{s}
, specified at time
s
.

Using the Poincaré inequality,
\nabla\cdot u^{\kappa}=0
, and that
\theta\in\dot{L}^{2}
quickly shows that the dissipation time is always bounded above by

(1.4)

t_{\mathrm{dis}}(u^{\kappa},\kappa)\leqslant\frac{\log 2}{\lambda_{1}\kappa}\,,

where
\lambda_{1}
is the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of the negative Laplacian.
In many physical situations, however, it is observed that the dissipation time is much smaller than the upper bound in (
1.4
); see
[
CZCIM24
]
for a review of the relevant literature.
This phenomenon is called
enhanced dissipation
, and occurs, for example, when
u^{\kappa}
is mixing.
In fact, if
u^{\kappa}
is
C^{1}
and
exponentially mixing
uniformly in
\kappa
then
t_{\mathrm{dis}}\leqslant C\lvert\log\kappa\rvert^{2}
(see Corollary
1.5
, below).
The aim of this paper is to produce an example of flows that are
C^{0}
and exponentially mixing uniformly in
\kappa
but
do not
exhibit enhanced dissipation – that is they saturate the bound (
1.4
).
(The flows we construct are in fact smooth, but higher norms are not bounded uniformly in
\kappa
.)
Explicitly, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every sufficiently small
\kappa>0
, there exists an incompressible velocity field
u^{\kappa}
(which will be constructed explicitly in Section
3
, below) such that the following hold:

(1)

(Uniform
L^{\infty}
boundedness)

There exists constants
C,F
that are independent of
\kappa
such that for all
\kappa>0

(1.5)

\lVert u^{\kappa}\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leqslant C\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert u^{\kappa}\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,\infty);C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}<\frac{F}{\kappa}\,,

(2)

(Exponential mixing)

There exists constants
D,\gamma_{1}>0
(independent of
\kappa
) such that for all sufficiently small
\kappa>0
, every solution to the transport equation

(1.6)

\partial_{t}\phi+u^{\kappa}\cdot\nabla\phi=0\,,

with initial data
\phi_{0}\in\dot{H}^{1}
satisfies the mixing bound for all
n,m\in 2\mathbb{N}

(1.7)

\lVert\phi_{m+n}\rVert_{H^{-1}}\leqslant D(m^{2}+1)\exp\big(-\gamma_{1}n\big)\lVert\phi_{m}\rVert_{H^{1}}\,.

(3)

(No enhanced dissipation)

For all sufficiently small
\kappa>0
, and for all
s\geqslant 0
, the dissipation time satisfies the lower bound

(1.8)

t_{\mathrm{dis}}^{s}(u^{\kappa},\kappa)\geqslant\frac{C}{\kappa}\,.

Remark 1.2
.

We note that our main mixing estimate (
1.7
) is somewhat non-standard in two distinct ways. The first is that the estimate is only given on times
s,t\in 2\mathbb{N}
. For a uniformly Lipschitz velocity field, a statement of mixing on times
s,t\in 2\mathbb{N}
is equivalent to a statement for times
s,t\in[0,\infty)
using the standard estimates for the fluctuation of the
H^{1}
and
H^{-1}
norms of the solution to (
1.6
), paying only an additional prefactor constant.

In our setting we do not have uniform-in-
\kappa
bounds on the Lipschitz norm of
u
, and so (
1.7
) does not immediately imply a similar estimate for all
s,t\in[0,\infty)
.
However, using the construction of
u^{\kappa}
(specifically, using the shear structure) and the Lipschitz norm bound in (
1.5
) and the integer time mixing bound (
1.7
), we can readily deduce the mixing bound

(1.9)

\lVert\phi_{t+s}\rVert_{H^{-1}}\leqslant\frac{D(s^{2}+1)}{\kappa^{2}}\exp\big(-\gamma_{1}t\big)\lVert\phi_{s}\rVert_{H^{1}}\quad\text{ for all }s,t\in[0,\infty),\,.

We note that (
1.9
) has a
\kappa
-dependent pre-factor, it is unimportant in the study of enhanced dissipation as it only enters logarithmically (see Corollary
1.5
, below).

The second way in which our main mixing estimate (
1.7
) differs from the usual statement of mixing is the lack of initial time homogeneity: the prefactor constant depends on the initial time
m
.
This is however a feature of all currently known smooth mixing flows and a consequence of their random construction, and is discussed further in Section
1.2
, below.

The main idea behind the construction is to alternate randomly shifted horizontal and vertical shears, inspired by the construction of Pierrehumbert
[
Pie94
]
.
In order to ensure that these flows
do not
enhance dissipation (in the sense of (
1.8
)), we choose shear profiles with amplitude
1
that oscillate on scales of order
\kappa
.
Based on energy estimates we show that any such flow can not enhance dissipation in the sense of (
1.8
).

Proving the
\kappa
-independent exponential mixing bound (
1.7
) is more delicate.
Existing methods verify Harris conditions for the two point chain
[
BBPS21
,
BCZG23
]
, and then use a Borel–Cantelli argument as in
[
DKK04
]
to prove almost sure exponential mixing.
The Harris conditions require a Lyapunov function, which is typically produced using a version of Furstenberg’s criterion
[
Fur63
]
.
This
can not
be used in our situation because this approach will only yield a
\kappa
-dependent Lyapunov function, and a
\kappa
-dependent Lyapunov exponent, resulting in an exponential mixing estimate like (
1.7
) where the constant
\gamma_{1}
depends on
\kappa
in an uncontrolled manner.
This is not suitable as it will not address sharpness of dissipation time bounds (described in Section
1.4
, below).
Moreover, if
\gamma_{1}
vanishes fast with
\kappa
the dissipation time lower bound (
1.8
) is not surprising, nullifying main point of Theorem
1.1
.

We will instead prove Theorem
1.1
by constructing a
\kappa
-independent Lyapunov function explicitly (see Section
3
, below).
Following this, existing methods
[
DKK04
]
can be used to prove almost sure exponential mixing as in (
1.7
) with a
\kappa
-independent (deterministic) rate constant
\gamma_{1}
, and a
\kappa
-dependent (random) prefactor
D_{\kappa}
.
Using the fact that the distribution of
u
is stationary, and that moments of
D_{\kappa}
are bounded uniformly in
\kappa
we will show the exponential mixing bound (
1.7
).

1.2.
Time inhomogeneous mixing

Let us further discuss the lack of time homogeneity in our main mixing result (
1.7
). We first introduce our definition of a mixing rate. Let
h\colon[0,\infty)^{2}\to(0,\infty)
be a function such that for every
s
, the function
t\mapsto h(s,t)
is decreasing and vanishes at infinity.
We say the velocity field
u
is
mixing with rate
h
if for every
s,t\geqslant 0
, and every
\phi_{s}\in\dot{H}^{1}
, the solution to (
1.6
) with
u^{\kappa}=u
and initial data
\phi_{s}
at time
s
satisfies the mixing bound

(1.10)

\lVert\phi_{s+t}\rVert_{H^{-1}}\leqslant h(s,t)\lVert\phi_{s}\rVert_{H^{1}}\,.

We say that the mixing is
time homogeneous
if
h(s,t)
is independent of
s
.
We say
u
is
exponentially mixing
if the mixing rate is of the form
h(s,t)=D(s)e^{-\gamma t}
for some function
D
that only depends on the initial time
s
.

We remark that in context of dynamical systems, the velocity field
u
is time-independent, in which case the mixing is time homogeneous “for free”.
In the time dependent setting, the mixing rate generically has a nontrivial dependence on the initial time
s
. In fact, surveying some of the myriad mixing examples so far constructed—
[
YZ17
,
ACM19
,
EZ19
,
BBPS22
,
MHSW22
,
BCZG23
,
CZNF24
,
ELM25
]
—only
[
MHSW22
,
ELM25
]
, built using deterministic tools and explicitly time periodic, have a mixing rate that is both time homogeneous, and exponentially decaying.
Interestingly, these examples are only Lipschitz regular, and consequently the following problem remains open.

Problem 1.1
.

Construct
u\in L^{\infty}([0,\infty),C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
for some
\alpha>0
, which has a time-homogeneous exponential mixing rate
h(s,t)=De^{-\gamma t}
for some constants
D<\infty
and
\gamma>0
that are independent of
s
and
t
.

We note that the random constructions of exponential mixers
[
BBPS22
,
BCZG23
,
CZNF24
]
only state exponential mixing for
s=0
: that is they only state a mixing result for initial data specified at time
s=0
.
For initial data specified at an arbitrary time
s>0
, it is natural to expect that the mixing rate
h(s,t)
has some dependence on
s
.
Indeed, on any time interval, there is a small probability that the mixing is highly ineffective, and so on large time intervals we must pick up some periods where the mixing is less effective.

However, as their random constructions are time stationary in law, one can use a Borel–Cantelli type argument to prove exponential mixing for all initial times
s\geqslant 0
, at the cost of an
s
-dependent prefactor.
We use this argument in Section
2.2
, below, to derive our mixing estimate (
1.7
).
The same argument gives a generalization of main result of
[
CIS25
]
, which we now state.

Proposition 1.3
.

Suppose the flow of
u
generates a random dynamical system that satisfies Assumptions 2.1–2.4 of
[
CIS25
]
.
Additionally, suppose the law of
u
is stationary in time.
There exists a random
D\geqslant 1
, and deterministic
\gamma>0
such that we have the almost sure mixing bound (
1.10
) with

(1.11)

h(s,t)=D(1+s^{2})e^{-\gamma t}\,.

Moreover, for every
q<\infty
,
\alpha>0
, and all sufficiently small
\kappa>0
, there exists a random
D_{q,\kappa}\geqslant 1
and finite constants
\bar{D}_{q}
(independent of
\kappa,\alpha
),
C_{\alpha}
(independent of
q,\kappa
) such that for every
s,t\geqslant 0
,
\theta_{s}\in\dot{L}^{1}
the solution to (
1.1
) with initial data
\theta_{s}
at time
s
satisfies

(1.12)

\lVert\theta^{\kappa}_{s+t}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant\frac{C_{\alpha}D_{q,\kappa}(1+s^{2})e^{-\gamma t}}{\kappa^{\frac{d}{2}+\alpha}}\lVert\theta^{\kappa}_{s}\rVert_{L^{1}}\,,

Clearly Proposition
1.3
implies the dissipation time bound

(1.13)

t_{\mathrm{dis}}^{s}\leqslant\ln\Big(\frac{D_{q,\kappa}(1+s^{2})}{\kappa}\Big)\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{E}D_{q,\kappa}^{q}\leqslant\bar{D}_{q}\,,

but does not imply a bound on
t_{\mathrm{dis}}
.

1.3.
Enhanced dissipation

Given a velocity field is mixing with time-homogeneous rate
h(s,t)=h(t)
, existing results
[
Fen19
,
FI19
,
CZDE20
]
provide an upper bound for the dissipation time. However no such bound has been given in the setting of time-inhomogeneous mixing, which appears naturally in the random mixing constructions. A refinement of these results, with explicit constants, a better dependence on the mixing rate, and accounting for non-homogeneity in the initial time is given below.

The central refinement is given by the following proposition, which gives that on a shorter time scale (
\lvert\log\kappa\rvert
for exponentially mixing flows) we get
some
dissipation (
\lvert\log\kappa\rvert^{-1/2}
much for exponentially mixing flows). It is then by iterating this result that we bound the dissipation time and deal with the time-inhomogeneity.

Proposition 1.4
.

For all
\kappa>0,\theta_{0}\in\dot{L}_{2}
, if
\theta^{\kappa}_{t}
is the solution (
1.1
) with incompressible velocity field
u
with mixing rate
h(s,t)
, then

\|\theta^{\kappa}_{\tau_{\kappa}}\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\sqrt{1-A_{\kappa}}\|\theta_{0}\|_{L^{2}},

where

\displaystyle H(T)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T/3\leqslant t\leqslant T}h(s,t)

\displaystyle\tau_{\kappa}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\inf\big\{t\in[2,\infty):t^{-2}H(t)\leqslant 2^{8}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)\kappa\big\},

\displaystyle A_{\kappa}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2^{15}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)\tau_{\kappa}}.

A careful iteration of the above gives the following after some computation.

Corollary 1.5
.

Suppose
u\in L^{\infty}([0,\infty);W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
is an incompressible velocity field with mixing rate
h(s,t)\leqslant D(1+s^{p})e^{-\gamma t}
for some
p\in[0,\infty)
. Then for all
\kappa>0
,

(1.14)

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{dis}}^{0}(u,\kappa)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{24}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)\cdot

(1.15)

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\bigg(1+2^{24}\Big(\frac{p}{\gamma}\Big)^{4}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)+\frac{(\log D)^{2}+\lvert\log\kappa\rvert^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\bigg).

The proof of these results will be given in Section
5
.

1.4.
Sharpness of dissipation time bounds.

As a consequence of Theorem
1.1
, we can address (though not completely resolve) a gap between the heuristically expected bounds on the dissipation time and the rigorously provable bounds given by Corollary
1.5
. Of the exponential mixing examples, the dissipation times for
[
BBPS21
,
CIS25
,
ELM25
]
are known.
Interestingly, in each case the dissipation time is
t_{\mathrm{dis}}^{0}\approx\lvert\log\kappa\rvert
, which is a
stronger bound
than the bound given by Corollary
1.5
:
t_{\mathrm{dis}}^{0}\leqslant C\lvert\log\kappa\rvert^{2}
.
Moreover, a compelling heuristic argument (which we explain in Section
1.5
, below) suggests that flows which are sufficiently regular (uniformly in
\kappa
) and are mixing with rate
h(s,t)=De^{-\gamma t}
should in fact generically satisfy the stronger dissipation time bound

(1.16)

t_{\mathrm{dis}}(u^{\kappa},\kappa)\leqslant\frac{1}{\gamma}\bigg\lvert\log\bigg(\frac{C_{\delta}(u)D}{\kappa^{\frac{d}{2}+\delta}}\bigg)\bigg\rvert\,,

for any
\delta>0
.

Theorem
1.1
can be used to show that the gap between the provable bound of Corollary
1.5
and the expected bound (
1.16
) may not be closable.
To explain this, let
\{u^{\kappa}\}
be the family of flows from Theorem
1.1
.
Rescale time by a factor of
\kappa
, and set

(1.17)

v^{\kappa}_{t}(x)=\varepsilon u^{\varepsilon}_{\varepsilon t}(x)\quad\text{where }\varepsilon=\sqrt{\kappa}\,.

Clearly, the velocity field
v^{\kappa}
satisfies the
\kappa
-uniform
C^{1}
bound

(1.18)

\lVert v^{\kappa}\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leqslant C\sqrt{\kappa}\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{\kappa>0}\lVert v^{\kappa}\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,\infty);C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}<F\,,

for some constants
C,F
independent of
\kappa
.
The velocity field
v^{\kappa}
is still exponentially mixing; however, due to the time rescaling the mixing rate is now
\kappa
dependent.
That is, every solution to the transport equation (
1.6
) (with drift
v^{\kappa}
, instead of
u^{\kappa}
) satisfies the mixing bound

(1.19)

\lVert\phi_{t}\rVert_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leqslant D\exp\big(-\gamma_{\kappa}t\big)\lVert\phi_{0}\rVert_{H^{1}}\quad\text{where }\gamma_{\kappa}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\gamma_{1}\sqrt{\kappa}\,.

Finally, we note that the time rescaling gives

(1.20)

t_{\mathrm{dis}}(v^{\kappa},\kappa)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}t_{\mathrm{dis}}(u^{\varepsilon},\varepsilon)\overset{\eqref{e:tdis-lowerR}}{\geqslant}\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{2}}=\frac{C}{\kappa}\,.

Using (
1.19
) we see that the right hand side of (
1.16
) is only of order
1/\sqrt{\kappa}
(up to a logarithmic factor), which is an order of magnitude smaller than the right hand side of (
1.20
).
Thus,
this gives a family of exponentially mixing flows
v^{\kappa}
which are
C^{1}
bounded (uniformly in
\kappa
) for which the stronger bound (
1.16
) can not hold.

We note that the above isn’t a proof that Corollary
1.5
is sharp as our example is a
\kappa
-dependent family.
Even though these flows are
C^{1}
bounded uniformly in
\kappa
, our construction does not allow us to find one,
\kappa
-independent, velocity field for which we have a matching lower bound to Corollary
1.5
.

1.5.
Literature review

We now survey the literature and place our results in the context of existing results.

Enhanced dissipation
is a phenomenon that can be observed in every day life:
pour some cream in your coffee.
If left alone, it will take hours to mix.
Stir it a little and it mixes right away.
This effect arises due to the interaction between the advection (stirring) and diffusion, and it plays an important role in many applications concerning hydrodynamic stability and turbulence and occurs on scales ranging from microfluidics to the meteorological and cosmological
[
LTD11
,
Thi12
,
Are84
,
SSA04
]
.

To describe this mathematically, let
u
be the velocity field of the ambient incompressible fluid and
\rho^{\kappa}
denote the concentration of a passively advected solute with molecular diffusivity
\kappa>0
.
The evolution of
\rho^{\kappa}
is governed by the advection diffusion equation

(1.21)

\partial_{t}\rho^{\kappa}+u\cdot\nabla\rho^{\kappa}-\kappa\Delta\rho^{\kappa}=0\,.

For simplicity, in this paper we only consider (
1.21
) with periodic boundary conditions on the
d
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

If the ambient fluid is incompressible, the velocity field
u
is divergence-free (i.e.
\nabla\cdot u=0
).
In this case, an elementary energy estimate and the Poincaré inequality shows that the
L^{2}
distance of the concentration from the equilibrium distribution decreases at most exponentially with a rate proportional to
\kappa
, and yields the dissipation time bound (
1.4
).

This, however, is a very crude upper bound and completely neglects the effect of the advection.
In practice, the advection term typically causes filamentation and moves mass towards small scales.
Since the diffusion term
\kappa\Delta\rho^{\kappa}
damps small scales faster, the combined effect of advection and diffusion should lead to faster convergence of
\rho^{\kappa}_{t}
to its equilibrium state as
t\to\infty
.
This phenomenon is known as
enhanced dissipation
and has been observed in many situations.

One indication of enhanced dissipation is when
t_{\mathrm{dis}}(u,\kappa)=o(1/\kappa)
, and has been studied by several authors.
Seminal work of Constantin et al.
[
CKRZ08
]
(see also
[
Zla10
,
KSZ08
]
) shows that if
u
is time independent, then
\kappa t_{\mathrm{dis}}(\kappa)\to 0
if and only if
u\cdot\nabla
has no eigenfunctions in
H^{1}
.
For shear flows, the classical work of Kelvin
[
Kel87
]
shows
t_{\mathrm{dis}}(\kappa)=\kappa^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha<1
.
There are now several results studying enhanced dissipation in more generality and for nonlinear equations (see
[
FNW04
,
BCZ17
,
Wei19
,
CZD21
,
ABN22
,
FSW22
,
FMN23
,
CZG23
,
CH23
,
FHX23
,
Sei23
,
IZ23
,
TZ25
]
).

Of particular interest is the connection between
mixing
and enhanced dissipation, which has been studied and quantified in
[
Fen19
,
FI19
,
FFIT20
,
CZDE20
,
CZCIM24
]
.
In particular, it is known that for exponentially mixing flows, we have
t_{\mathrm{dis}}(\kappa)\leqslant C\lvert\log\kappa\rvert^{2}
, as stated in Corollary
1.5
.
The main result of this paper (Theorem
1.1
) produces a family of
\kappa
-dependent incompressible vector fields
u^{\kappa}
which are
C^{0}
bounded and exponentially mixing uniformly in
\kappa
, however are not dissipation enhancing (i.e. saturate (
1.4
)).

Another point of interest is optimality of the dissipation time bound for mixing flows (Corollary
1.5
).
In particular, if
u
is
C^{1}
and exponentially mixing, it is not known if the
O(\lvert\log\kappa\rvert^{2})
bound given by Corollary
1.5
is sharp.
Indeed, to the best of our knowledge, for all known examples of exponentially mixing flows where the dissipation time can be computed, it is bounded by
O(\lvert\log\kappa\rvert)
.
These examples include
toral automorphisms
[
FNW04
,
FI19
]
,
stochastically forced Navier–Stokes equations
[
BBPS21
]
,
expanding Bernoulli maps
[
ILN24
]
,
alternating shear flows
[
ELM25
]
,
contact Anosov flows
[
TZ25
]
,
Pierrehumbert flows
[
CIS25
]
.
The last reference
[
CIS25
]
in fact shows that if
u
is generated by a random dynamical system that satisfies certain Harris conditions, then the dissipation time is bounded by
O(\lvert\log\kappa\rvert)
.
These Harris conditions also imply almost sure exponential mixing, and so
[
CIS25
]
provides a large class of flows (including Pierrehumbert flows) which are exponentially mixing and enjoy an
O(\lvert\log\kappa\rvert)
dissipation time bound.

A heuristic explanation for the
O(\lvert\log\kappa\rvert)
dissipation time bound is as follows.
The SDE associated with (
1.1
) is

(1.22)

dX^{\kappa}_{t}=u_{t}(X^{\kappa}_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2\kappa}\,dW_{t}\,,

where
W
is a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion.
Note that when
\kappa=0
, the process
X^{0}
is simply the deterministic flow of the vector field
u
.
Thus, if
u
is exponentially mixing with rate
h_{t}=De^{-\gamma t}
, then the mix norm decay (
1.10
) is equivalent to

(1.23)

\lVert\phi\circ X^{0}_{t}\rVert_{H^{-1}}\leqslant De^{-\gamma t}\lVert\phi\rVert_{H^{1}}\quad\text{for all test functions }\phi\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\,.

Since the noise is stationary, ergodic and homogeneous, it is reasonable to expect that the mixing estimate (
1.23
) persists for small
\kappa>0
.
That is, for all
\kappa>0
we still expect to have the
almost sure
mixing bound

(1.24)

This immediately implies the dissipation time bound (

).

The long time decay of solutions to (

) provides another interesting avenue for study.
In turbulence theory
[
Bat59
]
identified a length scale at which the effects of advection and diffusion balance.
At this scale
\kappa\lVert\nabla\rho^{\kappa}\rVert_{L^{2}}
becomes
independent
of
\kappa
and

at a rate that is independent of the diffusivity (see
[

,

]
).
This is reflected in (

\kappa
-independent
decay rate for large time.
Notice that this

-independent rate can not be obtained with knowledge of the dissipation time alone.
To the best of our knowledge, there are no continuous time deterministic examples that exhibit this
\kappa
-independent long time decay rate.

On a different note, if

is regular, and uniformly bounded in time, then we must have

[

,

,

When

This is known as

and was predicted in certain turbulent regimes Obukhov and Corrsin

,
Cor51
]
.
Examples of this were constructed recently in
[

,
CCS22
,
AV23
,
BCC+24
,
BJW24
,
EL24
,
JS24
,
HCR25
]
.

Finally we mention that the dissipation time

is dual to the
L^{2}

mixing time
.
The mixing time of various Markov processes has been an active area of study for decades
[
CLP99
,
Dia09
,
LP17
]
.
One relevant discrete result is
[
CD20
]
, where the authors study the mixing time of a random walk with deterministic jumps specified by a bijection.
Two other relevant are
[
DFY20
,
CFIN23
]

where the authors use a measure preserving drift to accelerate convergence of an Ornstein–Ullenbeck process.

3.
Exponential mixing of
u^{\kappa}
(Lemma
2.1
)

We will prove
u^{\kappa}
is exponentially mixing by verifying the conditions of a Harris theorem.
Once these conditions are verified, we use the Harris theorem to prove geometric ergodicity of the
two point
chain.
Then a result of
[
DKK04
]
will show that the flows
u^{\kappa}
are
almost surely
exponentially mixing.
This general principle is well-known and has been used in several recent papers
[
BBPS21
,
BCZG23
,
CIS25
]
.

The main difficulty in our situation is verifying the conditions of the Harris theorem.
This typically requires finding a small set and a Lyapunov function.
Existing methods
[
BBPS21
,
BCZG23
]
prove the existence of a Lyapunov function

using Furstenberg’s criterion.
This can not be used in our situation as it will only prove exponential mixing where the rate constants depend on
\kappa
in an uncontrolled manner.
Instead, we will provide an explicit Lyapunov function that is
independent
of
\kappa
, allowing us to obtain (
1.7
) with
\kappa
-independent constants

.

3.1.
Notation and preliminaries

We define the flow map
\Phi^{\kappa}:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{T}^{2}
, corresponding to the velocity field

(

), as the solution to the ODE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Phi^{\kappa}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=u^{\kappa}(t,\Phi^{\kappa}_{t}(x))\,,

\displaystyle\Phi^{\kappa}_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=x\,.

Notice

is a

-valued

(RDS).
Let
(P^{\kappa}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denote the

-step transition probability

(3.1)

The two-point RDS
\Phi^{\kappa,(2)}:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{2,(2)}\to\mathbb{T}^{2,(2)}
is defined by

where
\mathbb{T}^{2,(2)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{T}^{2})-\Delta

Explicitly,

(3.3)

3.2.
The Harris conditions

We first use a Harris theorem to prove geometric ergodicity of the two point chain, for which we need a Lyapunov function and a small set condition.
As mentioned earlier, the Lyapunov function is usually produced using non-constructive arguments using Furstenberg’s criterion, in a manner that the
\kappa

We avoid this issue by explicitly producing a

Explicitly, given

Here we used the convention that

Proof of Proposition

5.
The dissipation time of mixing flows

In this section we prove Proposition
1.4
and Corollary
1.5
. We start with an estimate on the closeness of the solution to a drift-diffusion equation to the solution to the associated transport equation. We use throughout this section the solution operator notation given by Definition
2.3
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
u\in L^{\infty}([0,\infty);W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
be a divergence-free velocity field and let
\theta\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Then denote
\theta^{0}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{T}_{0,t}^{0}\theta
and
\theta^{\kappa}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{T}_{0,t}^{\kappa}\theta.
We have the estimate.

(5.1)

\|\theta^{\kappa}_{t}-\theta^{0}_{t}\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant e^{3}\sqrt{\kappa}\Big(\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}}+\sqrt{\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1}\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{s}\|_{L^{2}}\,ds\Big).

Proof.

Let
\phi^{\kappa}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\theta^{\kappa}_{t}-\theta^{0}_{t}
so that

\partial_{t}\phi^{\kappa}-\kappa\Delta\phi^{\kappa}+u\cdot\nabla\phi^{\kappa}=\kappa\Delta\theta^{0}.

Then

(5.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\|\phi^{\kappa}_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant-2\kappa\|\nabla\phi^{\kappa}_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2\kappa\|\nabla\phi^{\kappa}_{t}\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla\theta^{0}_{t}\|_{L^{2}}

(5.3)

\displaystyle\leqslant\kappa\|\nabla\theta^{0}_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leqslant\kappa e^{2t\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}}\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,.

Thus we have the bound

\|\mathcal{T}_{0,t}^{\kappa}-\mathcal{T}^{0}_{0,t}\|_{H^{1}\to L^{2}}\leqslant\sqrt{\kappa t}e^{t\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}}.

Then for any
n\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle\|\theta^{\kappa}_{t}-\theta^{0}_{t}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|(\mathcal{T}^{\kappa}_{0,t}-\mathcal{T}^{0}_{0,t})\theta\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|\big((\mathcal{T}^{\kappa}_{\frac{n-1}{n}t,t}-\mathcal{T}^{0}_{\frac{n-1}{n}t,t})\mathcal{T}^{\kappa}_{0,\frac{n-1}{n}t}+\mathcal{T}^{0}_{\frac{n-1}{n}t,t}(\mathcal{T}^{\kappa}_{0,\frac{n-1}{n}t}-\mathcal{T}^{0}_{0,\frac{n-1}{n}t})\big)\theta\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\kappa}\sqrt{\frac{n}{t}}\exp\Big(\frac{t\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}}{n}\Big)\frac{t}{n}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{\frac{n-1}{n}t}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\theta^{\kappa}_{\frac{n-1}{n}t}-\theta^{0}_{\frac{n-1}{n}t}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\kappa}\sqrt{\frac{n}{t}}\exp\Big(\frac{t\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}}{n}\Big)\frac{t}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{tj/n}\|_{L^{2}}.

Then we note that for
j\geqslant 1,0\leqslant s\leqslant t/n
, we have that

\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}\|_{tj/n}\leqslant e^{s\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{tj/n-s}\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\exp\Big(\frac{t\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}}}{n}\Big)\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{tj/n-s}\|_{L^{2}},

and so

\frac{t}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{tj/n}\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\exp\Big(\frac{t\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}}}{n}\Big)\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{s}\|_{L^{2}}\,ds.

Combining the displays, we get that

\|\theta^{\kappa}_{t}-\theta^{0}_{t}\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\sqrt{\kappa}\sqrt{\frac{n}{t}}\exp\Big(\frac{2t\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}}{n}\Big)\Big(\frac{t}{n}\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{s}\|_{L^{2}}\,ds\Big).

We now choose

n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\lceil t(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)\rceil,

giving

\|\theta^{\kappa}_{t}-\theta^{0}_{t}\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant e^{2}\sqrt{\kappa}\Big(\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}}+\big(\sqrt{\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1}+t^{-1/2}\big)\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{s}\|_{L^{2}}\,ds\Big).

Then if
t\leqslant(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)^{-1}
, then

t^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{s}\|_{L^{2}}\,ds\leqslant et^{1/2}\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant e\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}},

otherwise
t\geqslant(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)^{-1}
and so
t^{-1/2}\leqslant\sqrt{\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1}
. In either case, we can conclude.
∎

We now prove Proposition
1.4
. We need to define the following orthogonal Fourier projectors. For
R\in[0,\infty)
, we define the orthogonal projection
\Pi_{\leqslant R},\Pi_{>R}:\dot{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\dot{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
so that if

f(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}e^{ik\cdot x}\hat{f}(k)\quad\text{then}\quad\Pi_{\leqslant R}f(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},|k|\leqslant R}e^{ik\cdot x}\hat{f}(k),

and
\Pi_{>R}=1-\Pi_{\leqslant R}.

The key idea of the proof is to case split according to whether the drift-diffusion solution
\theta^{\kappa}_{t}
stays close in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
to some solution to the transport equation or if it separates from it. If it stays close, then the mixing of the transport equation ensures, at the relevant time scale, that most of the
L^{2}
mass of the transport solution—and hence by the
L^{2}
closeness, most of the mass of
\theta^{\kappa}_{t}
—is on high Fourier modes, thus inducing
L^{2}
-norm dissipation by the energy identity. On the other hand, if the transport solution separates from
\theta^{\kappa}_{t}
, then (
5.1
) gives a lower bound on the
L^{1}_{t}H^{1}_{x}
norm of
\theta^{\kappa}_{t}
. The energy identity gives that
L^{2}
dissipation is however governed by the
L^{2}_{t}H^{1}_{x}
norm of the
\theta^{\kappa}_{t}
, so we need to apply Hölder’s inequality, giving an inverse time scale factor. It is this inverse time scale—coming from comparing the
L^{2}
norm in time to the
L^{1}
norm in time—that causes us to get the dissipation time of an exponentially mixing flow to be
\lvert\log\kappa\rvert^{2}
instead of
\lvert\log\kappa\rvert
.

This proof technique is largely analogous to that of
[
CZDE20
]
however uses a slightly more refined (or “less interpolated”) estimate of the distance of a transport solution and an advection-diffusion solution in the form of (
5.1
). Additionally, taking a slightly different approach gives more information on shorter times, essentially showing some linear decay of the
L^{2}
norm on the time interval
[0,t_{\mathrm{dis}}].

Proof of Proposition
1.4
.

Let
\theta_{0}\in\dot{L}^{2}
and without loss of generality suppose that
\|\theta_{0}\|_{L^{2}}=1
. Let
\theta^{\kappa}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{T}^{\kappa}_{0,t}\theta_{0}
. To conclude, it suffices to show that

\|\theta^{\kappa}_{\tau_{\kappa}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leqslant 1-A_{\kappa}.

We suppose for the sake of contradiction that
\|\theta^{\kappa}_{\tau_{\kappa}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}>1-A_{\kappa}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
. Then by the energy identity, we have that

1-A_{\kappa}<\|\theta^{\kappa}_{\tau_{\kappa}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=1-2\kappa\int_{0}^{\tau_{\kappa}}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{s}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,ds.

Thus

2\kappa\int_{0}^{\tau_{\kappa}/3}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{s}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,ds\leqslant 2\kappa\int_{0}^{\tau_{\kappa}}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{s}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,ds<A_{\kappa}.

As such, we can choose some
t_{0}\in[0,\tau_{\kappa}/3]
such that

(5.4)

\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{t_{0}}\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{3A_{\kappa}}{2\kappa\tau_{\kappa}}}.

Define
\theta^{0}_{t_{0},t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{T}^{0}_{t_{0},t}\theta^{\kappa}_{t_{0}}.
We split into two cases.

Case I: For all
t\in[2\tau_{\kappa}/3,\tau_{\kappa}],\;\|\theta^{\kappa}_{t}-\theta^{0}_{t_{0},t}\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\tfrac{1}{4}
.

By the definition of mixing rate, for all
t\in[2\tau_{\kappa}/3,\tau_{\kappa}]
, we see

\|\theta^{0}_{t_{0},t}\|_{H^{-1}}\leqslant H(\tau_{\kappa})\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{t_{0}}\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant H(\tau_{\kappa})\sqrt{\frac{3A_{\kappa}}{2\kappa\tau_{\kappa}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}B_{\kappa}^{-1}.

Then

\|\Pi_{>B_{\kappa}/4}\theta^{0}_{t_{0},t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|\theta^{0}_{t_{0},t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\|\Pi_{\leqslant B_{\kappa}/4}\theta^{0}_{t_{0},t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\geqslant\|\theta^{\kappa}_{\tau_{\kappa}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{B_{\kappa}^{2}}{16}\|\theta^{0}_{t_{0},t}\|_{H^{-1}}^{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{9}.

Then, still for
t\in[2\tau_{\kappa}/3,\tau_{\kappa}],

\displaystyle\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{t}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{B_{\kappa}}{4}\|\Pi_{>B_{\kappa}/4}\theta^{\kappa}_{t}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{B_{\kappa}}{4}\big(\|\Pi_{>B_{\kappa}/4}\theta^{0}_{t_{0},t}\|_{L^{2}}-\|\Pi_{>B_{\kappa}/4}(\theta^{\kappa}_{t}-\theta^{0}_{t_{0},t})\|_{L^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{B_{\kappa}}{4}\Big(\frac{1}{3}-\|\theta^{\kappa}-\theta^{0}_{t_{0},t}\|_{L^{2}}\Big)\geqslant\frac{B_{\kappa}}{48}.

Thus

A_{\kappa}>2\kappa\int_{0}^{\tau_{\kappa}}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{s}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,ds\geqslant 2\kappa\int_{2\tau_{\kappa}/3}^{\tau_{\kappa}}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{s}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,ds\geqslant\frac{2\kappa B_{\kappa}^{2}\tau_{\kappa}}{3\cdot 48^{2}}=\Big(\frac{\kappa\tau_{\kappa}}{72H(\tau_{\kappa})}\Big)^{2}A_{\kappa}^{-1},

or using the definitions of
\tau_{\kappa},A_{\kappa},

\frac{1}{72\cdot 2^{8}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)\tau_{\kappa}}\leqslant\frac{\kappa\tau_{\kappa}}{72H(\tau_{\kappa})}<A_{\kappa}=\frac{1}{2^{15}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)\tau_{\kappa}},

which is a contradiction as
72\cdot 2^{8}<2^{15}.

Case II: For some
t_{1}\in[2\tau_{\kappa}/3,\tau_{\kappa}],
such that
\|\theta^{\kappa}_{t_{1}}-\theta^{0}_{t_{0},t_{1}}\|_{L^{2}}\geqslant\frac{1}{4}
.

By (
5.1
) and (
5.4
), we see

\frac{1}{4}\leqslant\|\theta^{\kappa}_{t_{1}}-\theta^{0}_{t_{0},t_{1}}\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant e^{3}\sqrt{\kappa}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{3A_{\kappa}}{2\kappa\tau_{\kappa}}}+\sqrt{\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1}\int_{0}^{\tau_{\kappa}}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{s}\|_{L^{2}}\,ds\Big).

Then we note that by the definition of
A_{\kappa}
and that
\tau_{\kappa}\geqslant 2,

e^{3}\sqrt{\frac{3A_{\kappa}}{2\tau_{\kappa}}}\leqslant\frac{1}{4(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)^{1/2}\tau_{\kappa}}\leqslant\frac{1}{8}.

Combining the previous two equations, using the definition of
A_{\kappa}
, and applying Hölder’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\tau_{\kappa}A_{\kappa}=\frac{1}{2^{15}\big(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1\big)}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\kappa\Big(\int_{0}^{\tau_{\kappa}}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{s}\|_{L^{2}}\,ds\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\tau_{\kappa}\kappa\int_{0}^{\tau_{\kappa}}\|\nabla\theta^{\kappa}_{s}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,ds<\tau_{\kappa}A_{\kappa},

which is a contradiction. Thus we conclude in both the cases.
∎

We conclude by proving Corollary
1.5
as a consequence of Proposition
1.4
.

Proof of Corollary
1.5
.

Let
\theta_{0}\in\dot{L}^{2}
arbitrary, and let
\theta^{\kappa}_{t}
denote the solution to (
1.1
) with
\theta^{\kappa}_{0}=\theta_{0}.

Let

T_{\kappa}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}2^{24}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)\bigg(1+2^{24}\Big(\frac{p}{\gamma}\Big)^{4}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)+\gamma^{-2}\big((\log D)^{2}+\lvert\log\kappa\rvert^{2}\big)\bigg).

Then for any
0\leqslant r\leqslant T_{\kappa}
, we define as in Proposition
1.4
,

\displaystyle H^{r}(T)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T/3\leqslant t\leqslant T}h(r+s,t)

\displaystyle\tau^{r}_{\kappa}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\inf\big\{t\in[2,\infty):t^{-2}H^{r}(t)\leqslant 2^{8}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)\kappa\big\},

\displaystyle A^{r}_{\kappa}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2^{15}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)\tau^{r}_{\kappa}}.

so that

(5.5)

\|\theta_{r+\tau^{r}_{\kappa}}^{\kappa}\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\sqrt{1-A^{r}_{\kappa}}\|\theta^{\kappa}_{r}\|_{L^{2}}.

Note that, since
r\leqslant T
, using the definition of
h(s,t)
, we can directly verify that

\tau_{\kappa}^{r}\leqslant 2+\frac{3}{\gamma}\big(\log D+p\log(4T_{\kappa})+\lvert\log\kappa\rvert\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sigma_{\kappa}\leqslant T_{\kappa},

and thus

A^{s}_{\kappa}\geqslant\frac{1}{2^{15}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)\sigma_{\kappa}}.

Thus for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n\sigma_{\kappa}\leqslant T
, we have by iterating (
5.5
) that

\|\theta^{\kappa}_{T}\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\Big(1-\frac{1}{2^{15}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)\sigma_{\kappa}}\Big)^{n/2}\|\theta_{0}\|_{L^{2}}.

Using that
(1-x)^{x^{-1}}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
, we thus get that
\|\theta^{\kappa}_{T_{\kappa}}\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\|\theta_{0}\|_{L^{2}}
—allowing us to conclude—provided

2^{17}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)\sigma_{\kappa}^{2}\leqslant T_{\kappa}.

Using the definition of
\sigma_{\kappa}
, we see this is implied provided

(5.6)

1+\gamma^{-2}\big((\log D)^{2}+\lvert\log\kappa\rvert^{2}\big)\leqslant\frac{T_{\kappa}}{2^{23}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)}-\Big(\frac{p}{\gamma}\log(4T_{\kappa})\Big)^{2}.

We first note that since

T_{\kappa}\geqslant 2^{48}p^{4}\gamma^{-4}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)^{2},

we have that

\frac{T_{\kappa}}{2^{23}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)}-\Big(\frac{p}{\gamma}\log(4T_{\kappa})\Big)^{2}\geqslant\frac{T_{\kappa}}{2^{24}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)}.

Then using that

T_{\kappa}\geqslant 2^{24}(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}+1)(1+\gamma^{-2}((\log D)^{2}+\lvert\log\kappa\rvert^{2})),

we conclude (
5.6
) and thus the proof.
∎
