Neurofibromatosis type 1 complicated by pelvic arteriovenous fistula and pseudoaneurysm: A rare case with endovascular management
Authors: Mohammad Sahneh, Alireza Rezvani, Morteza Daraei, Mohammad Ashouri, Faeze Salahshour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We describe a case of an 80-year-old man with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) who presented with weakness, weight loss, anemia, and inflammatory symptoms, initially raising suspicion for malignancy. Further hematologic evaluation confirmed chronic lymphocytic leukemia while an adrenal mass was also identified. but further work-up revealed a right common iliac artery pseudoaneurysm with arteriovenous fistula formation to right common iliac vein. This case illustrates a rare vascular complication of NF1 and the success of endovascular management in achieving a favorable outcome.

Introduction

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is a rare autosomal dominant genetic disorder caused by mutations in the NF1 gene, with an incidence of about 1 in 2500-3000 [1]. It is characterized by neurofibromas of the skin, cafe-au-lait spots, Lisch nodules, and skeletal abnormalities. Vascular complications, including aneurysms, stenoses, and arteriovenous malformations, occur in 0.4%-6.4% of patients and are the result of NF1-associated vasculopathy with abnormal smooth muscle and endothelial function [2].

Arteriovenous fistulas (AVF) are abnormal communications between arteries and veins, and they are very rare in NF1, especially in the pelvis; only a few cases have been reported [3]. We present a case report of pelvic AVF in a patient with systemic symptoms and the diagnostic challenge and successful endovascular treatment in an elderly patient with NF1.

Additionally a left adrenal cystic lesion was depicted suggestive of an adrenal Pheochromocytoma considering the known NF1. throughout the course of patient’s hospitalization the patient’s lymphocytosis gradually decreased probably related to the therapeutic regimen prescribed for the management of CLL.

Empirical antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) reduced, CRP to 10 mg/L, and ESR to 24 mm/h within 5 days, with resolution of fever and clinical improvement of the patient, and then Endovascular stent grafting, performed by a vascular surgeon, closed the AVF.

Postprocedure imaging confirmed resolution, and his hemoglobin (12.5 g/dL), CRP, and ESR normalized. He was discharged on day 14 with outpatient follow-up.

Discussion

In neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), neurofibromas can penetrate deep tissues and affect nearby vascular structures. Despite the rarity of vascular complications associated with NF1, these complications may include stenosis, aneurysm formation, arteriovenous malformations, and vascular wall fragility related to NF1-associated vasculopathy [4]. On the other hand, pelvic arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) are extremely uncommon. This case is noteworthy because it depicts an atypical pelvic AVF with pseudoaneurysm development in an elderly man with NF1 who presented with inflammatory symptoms rather than obvious vascular signs.

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) was suspected based on persistent lymphocytosis and confirmed by flow cytometry results, which may have contributed to some of the constitutional symptoms.

Further work-up revealed an incidental adrenal lesion measuring >4 cm. In this setting, further characterization with adrenal imaging, such as MRI with adrenal protocol, and evaluation for pheochromocytoma using plasma-free metanephrines or 24 hour urinary fractionated metanephrines would be appropriate. However, acute illness and physiological stress may cause false-positive elevations in catecholamine metabolites and should therefore be considered when interpreting biochemical test results.

Despite the diagnosis of CLL, the patien’s clinical course suggested an additional underlying process. Imaging during hospitalization revealed a pseudoaneurysm of the the right common iliac artery, associated with iliac venous thrombosis, and formation of an AVF between the right common iliac artery and right comom iliac vein.

This finding highlights the difficulty in diagnosing NF1 patients, as vascular problems could be related to other systemic diseases and mask the major cause of clinical deterioration.

Several mechanisms have been proposed to contribute to vascular complications in NF1 patients, including vascular wall fragility, local inflammation, and compression by adjacent neurofibromatous tissue.

The vascular abnormalities associated with NF1 may predispose patients to aneurysm formation, thrombosis, and fistulization [5].

In our patient, however, imaging revealed no evidence of surrounding inflammatory changes or compression of the affected vessel by adjacent neurofibromatous tissue. Therefore, among the proposed mechanisms, NF1-associated vascular wall fragility appears to be the most plausible explanation for the development of the pseudoaneurysm and associated fistula in this patient, although a definitive mechanism cannot be established.

Contrast-enhanced CT angiography was pivotal for diagnosis and demonstrated an irregular saccular outpouching arising from the right common iliac artery with communication with the proximal right common iliac vein consistent with pseudoaneurysm and AVF. Deep vascular lesions in NF1 patients may be clinically silent until complications occur, thus making vascular imaging essential in patients with unexplained constitutional or inflammatory symptoms.

Deans et al. [6] were the first to report the association of neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) with arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) and emphasized that these lesions are rare but important vascular manifestations of NF1-related vasculopathy. The utility of angiography for the diagnosis of AVFs was emphasized. It was also shown that vascular involvement in NF1 may present with hemorrhagic or compressive symptoms rather than the usual cutaneous findings [6]. Similarly, Kamiyama et al. [7] reported a patient with NF1, multiple aneurysms and an extracranial vertebral AVF. They suggested that the NF1-related vascular disease represents diffuse arterial wall abnormalities rather than focal vascular defects and emphasized the rarity of the AVFs in patients with NF1 [7]. Our case differs from previously reported cases in that the patient presented with constitutional symptoms mimicking malignancy rather than with focal neurological or vascular symptoms. This underscores the extensive clinical spectrum of NF1-associated vasculopathy.

Considering the absence of prior history of trauma or vascular intervention, lack of local precipitating factors such as surrounding inflammation or compression by adjacent neurofibromatous tissue and negative work-up for infectious diseases and endocarditis, no clear acquired precipitating factor for the pseudoaneurysm and associated AVF was identified.

Therefore the pseudoaneurysm and associated fistula formation were considered likely to have developed spontaneously in the setting of NF1-associated vascular wall fragility.

Given the patient’s age and underlying vascular fragility, endovascular treatment was favored over open surgery, because of it’s less invasive nature and potentially less procedural risk. Angioplasty and stent grafting resulted in exclusion of the pseudoaneurysm and occlusion of the fistulous communication. This case highlights the need to consider vascular problems in NF1 patients presenting with unusual systemic symptoms, even when other diagnoses, such as hematologic malignancy, are present.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of being aware of vascular problems in patients with NF1 presenting with unexplained inflammatory or systemic symptoms, even when they have other diagnoses such as cancer. Timely vascular imaging and multidisciplinary care are critical for the early diagnosis and best possible outcomes in these rare but potentially catastrophic conditions.
