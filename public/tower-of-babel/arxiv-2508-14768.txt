The Fractal Goodstein Principle
David Fernández-Duque 1 and Andreas Weiermann 2

Abstract
The original Goodstein process is based on writing numbers in hereditary b b -exponential normal form: that is, each number n n is written in some base b ≥ 2 b\geq 2 as n = b e ​ a + r n=b^{e}a+r , with e e and r r iteratively being written in hereditary b b -exponential normal form. We define a new process which generalises the original by writing expressions in terms of a hierarchy of bases B B , instead of a single base b b . In particular, the ‘digit’ a a may itself be written with respect to a smaller base b ′ b^{\prime} . We show that this new process always terminates, but termination is independent of Kripke-Platek set theory, or other theories of Bachmann-Howard strength.

1
Introduction

Goodstein’s principle
[
11
]
is a classic example of a ‘purely number-theoretic’ statement independent of Peano arithmetic (
\sf PA
)
[
12
]
.
While the statement is relatively simple, the proof uses the well-foundedness of
\varepsilon_{0}

[
10
]
, the proof-theoretic ordinal of
\sf PA
.
Goodstein’s principle is based on
hereditary exponential normal forms,
whereby each natural number is written in some base
b\geq 2
in the standard way, but then each exponent appearing in the expansion is also written in base
b
.
For example, we would write
6
in base
2
as
6=_{2}2^{2}+2
.
The Goodstein process then proceeds by increasing the base by one, then subtracting one. If the first element in the sequence is
6
, we first increase its base by one, obtaining
30=_{3}3^{3}+3
, so the second element in the sequence is
29
.
Despite initially growing rather quickly, this process eventually reaches zero, a fact not provable in
\sf PA
.

Several recent results have extended Goodstein’s theorem in various ways
[
1
,
7
,
8
]
, including variants independent of Kripke-Platek set theory (
\sf KP
) or other theories of Bachmann-Howard strength

[
2
,
9
]
.
The latter are themselves based on functions not provably total in
\sf PA
, with the general philosophy being that they are fair game, as long as they are provably total in
\sf KP
.
But this begs the question: are such functions really necessary for Goodstein processes of high proof-theoretic strength?
May it be possible to squeeze some more power out of Goodstein’s original construction?
A partial answer is ‘no’, because the authors
[
8
]
have shown that no way of writing numbers in hereditary exponential normal form will lead to termination times larger than those given by Goodstein’s original progression.
However, there is a way to circumvent this restriction.

Our first observation is that there is no need to always increase the base by one; the next base may be chosen to be much larger than the previous one.
However, simply replacing the base
b
by some uniform
f(b)
will not yield a principle of higher proof-theoretic strength, since the well-foundedness of
\varepsilon_{0}
may still be applied.
Our second observation is that the new base for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
could depend on
n
.
We may consider, say, replacing the base of
n
by
n
itself, like the ouroborous eating its own tail.
Some reflection shows that this approach will not lead to a uniform base being used at the next step, since following this rule would lead us to replacing the base of
n+1
by
n+1
, and thus every natural number would be regarded as a possible base at each stage.
This naïve idea would not quite work out, but we can follow an intermediate approach and create new bases only at ‘critical’ numbers.
This will lead to a hierarchy of bases at each stage, rather than a single ‘global’ base.
One can even imagine that for a number written as
b^{e}a+r
in base
b
, the numbers
b,e,a,r
may all be written in distinct bases, with
their
digits written in terms of even smaller bases, each performing its own Goodstein process, like a self-similar fractal made up of smaller copies of itself.
As we will see, this can be implemented in a manner that yields a terminating sequence; in this way, we obtain a Goodstein principle very close to the classic version, but of much higher proof-theoretic strength.

Let us give a very brief description of
\sf KP
; see e.g.
[
3
]
for more details.
The theory
\sf KP
(with infinity) is axiomatised by all axioms of
\sf ZFC
except for powerset, but with separation restricted to
\Delta_{0}
formulas and replacement restricted to
\Delta_{0}
-collection.
Roughly, new sets may only be defined in terms of the elements of previously defined sets.
Other theories of the same proof-theoretic strength as
\sf KP
are the theory
{\sf ID}_{1}
of non-iterated inductive definitions and
{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
-
{\sf CA}^{-}_{0}
of parameter-free
{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
comprehension.
They share the proof-theoretic ordinal
\vartheta(\varepsilon_{{\Omega}+1})
, where
{\Omega}
is the first uncountable ordinal and
\vartheta\colon\varepsilon_{{\Omega}+1}\to{\Omega}
‘collapses’ possibly uncountable ordinals into countable ones
[
14
]
.
This theory is far more powerful than
\sf PA
and certainly proves Goodstein’s theorem, but not its fractal extension.

2
Fractal Goodstein processes

First we recall some notions from the classic Goodstein process.
Let
b\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0,1\}
.
For
0<n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the
b
-decomposition
of
n
to be
b^{e}a+r
, where
a,e,r
are uniquely chosen so that
0<a<b
and
r<b^{e}
, in which case we write
n=_{b}b^{e}a+r
.
Technically, we think of the
b
-decomposition of
n
as the quadruple
(b,e,a,r)\in\mathbb{N}^{4}
. Of course, one could iteratively write
b
,
e
,
a
, and
r
using their own
B
-decompositions; note that
{\rm base}_{B}(a)<b
, but
e
or
r
could have the same base as
n
.
Note also that
=_{b}
is not symmetric, i.e.
\tilde{b}^{\tilde{e}}\tilde{a}+\tilde{r}=_{B}b^{e}a+r
does not imply
b^{e}a+r=_{b}\tilde{b}^{\tilde{e}}\tilde{a}+\tilde{r}
; only the right-hand side needs to be a
b
-decomposition.
The number
a
is regarded as a ‘digit,’ since it is less than the base.
Formally, for
b\geq 2
, we say that
c
is a
b
-digit
of
n
if either
c=n<b
, or else
n=_{b}b^{e}a+r
and
c
is a
b
-digit of
a
,
e
or
r
.
The set of
b
-digits of
n
is denoted
{\rm Digit}_{b}(n)
.

The classic Goodstein process is based on the base-change operator, where we replace
b
in the
b
-decomposition of
n
by some other base
c\geq b
.
This is defined recursively as
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)n=n
if
n<b
and
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)n=c^{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)e}a+\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)r
if
n\geq b
and
n=_{b}b^{e}a+r
.
The base change has the following basic properties, which may be verified directly, but see e.g.
[
8
]
.

Lemma 2.1

Let
n,m,b,c,d\in\mathbb{N}
with
2\leq b\leq c
.

1.

If
n<m
, then
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)n<\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)m
.

2.

If
c\leq d
, then
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)n\leq\left(\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)n
.
The inequality is strict if
n\geq b
and
d>c
.

3.

If
n=_{b}b^{e}a+r
, then
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)n=_{c}c^{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)e}a+\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)r
.

Then, the classic Goodstein sequence starting on
n
is the sequence
(n_{i})_{i<I}
, where
n_{0}=n
,
n_{i+1}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}i+3\\
i+2\end{smallmatrix}\right)n_{i}-1
if
n_{i}>0
, and either
I
is finite and
n_{I-1}=0
, or else
I=\infty
and all elements of the sequence are positive.
Goodstein’s theorem then states that
I
will always be finite
[
10
]
.

We generalize this to a multi-base setting, using
base hierarchies.

Let
B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, let
S_{B}(n)
be the least
b\in B
such that
b>n
and let
S_{B}(n)=\infty
if there is no such
b
.
We regard every positive integer to divide
\infty
.

Definition 2.2

A set
B\subseteq\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0,1\}
is a base hierarchy if
B\neq\varnothing
and for every
b\in B
,
b\mid S_{B}(b)
.

If
B
is a base hierarchy, elements of
B
are bases.

Each
n\in\mathbb{N}
is assigned a base from
B
.
The general rule is that the base of
n
should be the largest base below
n
.
For example, if
B=\{5,10\}
, the number
7
would be written in base
5
, and (say)
14
in base
10
.
However, it is unclear if we wish to write
10
in base
5
or in base
10
.
Either choice is reasonable, and both will be useful, but writing
10
in base
5
will carry some essential information.
We thus assign two bases to
10
:
{\rm base}_{B}(10)=5
and
{\rm Base}_{B}(10)=10
.
On the other hand,
5
, being the least base, will only be assigned the base
5
.

Definition 2.3

Given a base hierarchy
B
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

1.

the lower base of
n
by

{\rm base}_{B}(n)=\max\{b\in B:b\leq\max\{n-1,\min B\}\};

2.

the upper base of
n
by

{\rm Base}_{B}(n)=\max\{b\in B:b\leq\max\{n,\min B\}\}.

It is not hard to check that
{\rm base}_{B}(n)={\rm Base}_{B}(n)
except when
n\in B\setminus\{\min B\}
.
For the most part,
{\rm Base}_{B}(n)
can be regarded as the ‘official’ base, but
{\rm base}_{B}(n)
will be useful in Section
4
.

Example 2.4

Every singleton
\{b\}
with
b\geq 2
is a base hierarchy, since in this case we have decreed
b\mid\infty=S_{B}(b)
.

Definition 2.5

Let
B,C
be base hierarchies with
\min B\leq\min C
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We define
{\uparrow}n={\uparrow}_{B}^{C}n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
recursively on
n
.
If
n<\min B
, then
{\uparrow}n=n
.

Otherwise,
n\geq\min B
.
Let
b={\rm Base}_{B}(n)
and assume inductively that
{\uparrow}m
is defined for all
m<n
.
We first define the deep base-change operator
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle^{C}_{B}
on
m\leq n
:
if
m<b
, then
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m={\uparrow}_{B}^{C}m
.
If
m\geq b
, write
m=_{b}b^{e}a+r
and set

\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m=c^{\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle e}{\uparrow}a+\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle r.

This has the effect of applying the upgrade operator to all the
b
-digits of
m
, while changing the base to
c
.

Then, define
{\uparrow}n=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
, where
c
is the least element of
C
such that

{\uparrow}(n-1)<\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n<S_{C}(c).

If no such
c
exists,
{\uparrow}n=\infty
.

In words, given
n\notin B
with
b={\rm Base}_{B}(n)
, in order to perform the upgrade, we first upgrade all the
b
-coefficients of
n
.
We then search for the least base
c
for which changing the base
b
to
c
will yield a ‘reasonable’ result: namely, we need
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
to be at least as large as
{\uparrow}(n-1)
to make
{\uparrow}
monotone, but we also want the result to have
c
as its base.

When
n
is a base in
B
, note that
b=n
and
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n=c
.
So, in this case, we basically jump to the next available base in
C
; for this to be a finite value,
C
should have ‘enough available bases.’
In order for the upgrade to be well-behaved, we also do not want this value to be ‘much larger’ than
{\uparrow}_{B}^{C}(n-1)
.
The precise conditions required are given by Definition
2.9
below.

Convention 2.6

If in a result or proof we state that transformations are from
B
to
C
, this indicates that
{\uparrow}
is to be understood as
{\uparrow}^{C}_{B}
and
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}\cdot\\
\cdot\end{smallmatrix}\rangle
as
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}\cdot\\
\cdot\end{smallmatrix}\rangle^{C}_{B}
.
Similarly, we may state e.g. that
{\uparrow}
is from
B
to
C
to indicate that
{\uparrow}={\uparrow}^{C}_{B}
.
More often than not, these indices can be suppressed, but there will be occasions when we do consider multiple base hierarchies in the same context.

Convention 2.7

The transformations
{\uparrow}
,
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle
are performed before addition but after multiplication and exponentiation.
Thus for example,
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)b^{e}a+r
should be understood as
(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)(b^{e}a))+r
.

Example 2.8

Consider transformations between
B=\{2,6\}
and
C=\{3,30,180\}
.
We have that
{\uparrow}0=0
and
{\uparrow}1=1
.
Then,
{\rm Base}_{B}(2)=2
and
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}3\\
2\end{smallmatrix}\rangle 2=3<30=S_{C}(3)
, so
{\uparrow}2=3
.
More generally, when
B
and
C
are base hierarchies with
\min C\geq\min B
,
{\uparrow}^{C}_{B}\min B=\min C
.

Now, let
n=6^{6}+6^{2^{2}+1}
and let us compute
{\uparrow}_{B}^{C}n
.
We first perform the upgrade on
2^{2}+1
.
Writing
{\uparrow}
instead of
{\uparrow}_{B}^{C}
for simplicity, in this case
{\uparrow}(2^{2}+1)=3^{3}+1<30
, exactly as we would expect from the original Goodstein process.

However, when we attempt to replace
6
by
30
in
n
and upgrade the digit, we obtain
30^{30}+30^{3^{3}+1}
, which is larger than the next base of
C
.
Thus, we instead use the base
180
, and

{\uparrow}n=180^{180}+180^{3^{3}+1}.

Definition 2.9

If
B,C\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and
{\uparrow}={\uparrow}_{B}^{C}
, we say that
C
is a good successor for
B
if both
B
and
C
are base hierarchies and

1.

\min B\leq\min C
,

2.

{\uparrow}n<\infty
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and

3.

if
\min B<n+1\in B
, there are no multiples of
{\rm Base}_{C}({\uparrow}n)
in the interval

({\uparrow}n,S_{C}({\uparrow}n))
.

Example 2.10

Let
B
be any base hierarchy.
We define
B_{+}=\bigcup_{n<\infty}C_{n}
, where
C_{n}
is defined by induction on
n
as follows:

1.

C_{n}=\{\min B+1\}
if
n\leq\min B
,

2.

C_{n+1}=C_{n}
if
n+1\notin B
, and

3.

if
\min B<n\in B
, then
C_{n+1}=C_{n}\cup\{k\}
, where
k
is the least multiple of
\max C_{n}
strictly above
{\uparrow}_{B}^{C_{n}}n
.

Then,
B_{+}
is always a good successor for
B
, a fact that will follow from the more general Proposition
5.9
in view of Example
5.3
.

As an example, let
B=\{2,6\}
, and let us compute
C:=B_{+}
, with
C^{n}:=B_{+}^{n}
.
We have that
C^{n}=\{\min B+1\}=\{3\}
for
n\leq 3
.
From here, we see that
{\rm base}_{C^{5}}(5)=3
and
{\uparrow}_{B}^{C^{5}}5=3^{3}+1=10
, so
C^{6}
is obtained by adding the next multiple of
3
, namely,
12
.
We thus arrive at
C=\{3,12\}
, and it is not hard to check that
C
is indeed a good successor for
B
.

For our Goodstein processes, each
i\in\mathbb{N}
will be endowed with a base hierarchy
\mathcal{B}_{i}
.
The collection of these hierarchies is a
dynamical hierarchy.

Definition 2.11

A dynamical hierarchy is a sequence
\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{B}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, where for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is a base hierarchy and
\mathcal{B}_{i+1}
is a good successor for
\mathcal{B}_{i}
.

Example 2.12

If
B_{i}=\{i+2\}
, then
\mathcal{B}=(B_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a dynamical hierarchy.
As we will see, this dynamical hierarchy is related to Goodstein’s original theorem.

Example 2.13

Let
B_{0}
be any base hierarchy and define inductively
B_{i+1}=(B_{i})_{+}
, as in Example
2.10
.
Then,
(B_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a dynamical hierarchy.
If
B_{0}=\{2\}
, we obtain the dynamical hierarchy of Example
2.12
as a special case.
Note moreover that if
B_{0}
is finite, then
B_{i}
is finite for all
i
.

If a dynamical hierarchy
\mathcal{B}
is given and clear from context, we may replace
\mathcal{B}_{i}
by
i
in our terminology and notation, e.g. we may write
=_{i}
instead of
=_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
,
{\uparrow}_{i}
instead of
{\uparrow}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}^{\mathcal{B}_{i+1}}
, etc.
With this, we are ready to define our fractal Goodstein processes.

Definition 2.14

Given a dynamical hierarchy
\mathcal{B}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we recursively define
{\mathbb{G}}^{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(n)
by letting
{\mathbb{G}}^{\mathcal{B}}_{0}(n)=n
and, if
{\mathbb{G}}^{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(n)
is defined and positive, then
{\mathbb{G}}^{\mathcal{B}}_{i+1}(n)={\uparrow}_{i}{\mathbb{G}}^{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(n)-1
; otherwise
{\mathbb{G}}^{\mathcal{B}}_{i+1}(n)
is undefined.

Example 2.15

If
\mathcal{B}
is the dynamical hierarchy of Example
2.12
, then the sequence
{\mathbb{G}}^{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(n)
is the classical Goodstein sequence for
n
.

With this, we may state our main results, proven throughout the rest of the paper.

Theorem 2.16

If
\mathcal{B}
is any dynamical hierarchy and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, then there is
i\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbb{G}^{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(m)=0
.

Theorem 2.17

There is a dynamical hierarchy
\mathcal{O}
such that
\sf KP
does not prove that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
i\in\mathbb{N}
with
{\mathbb{G}}^{\mathcal{O}}_{i}(n)=0
.

The dynamical hierarchy
\mathcal{O}
is computable and will be given by Definition
5.1
.
Thus Theorem
2.17
should be understood as follows.
Say that an arithmetical formula
\delta
is
bounded
if all quantifiers occurring in
\delta
are of the form
\forall u<t(\vec{v})
or
\exists u<t(\vec{v})
, where
t
is a polynomial with coefficients in
\mathbb{N}
, and
\varphi
is a
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-formula if it is of the form
\exists z\delta
, where
\delta
is bounded.
Arithmetical formulas, and
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-formulas in particular, can be encoded in the language of
\sf KP
in a standard way
[
3
]
.
Since
{\mathbb{G}}^{\mathcal{O}}_{y}(x)
is computable
[
16
]
, there is a
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-formula
\varphi(x,y)
such that
\varphi(x,y)
is true on
\mathbb{N}
if and only if
{\mathbb{G}}^{\mathcal{O}}_{y}(x)=0
.
Then, Theorem
2.17
states that
\sf KP
does not prove
\forall x\in\mathbb{N}\ \exists y\in\mathbb{N}\ \varphi(x,y)
.

3
Properties of the Upgrade Operator

Our first main goal is to prove that the fractal Goodstein process is always terminating.
This is based on various regularity properties of the upgrade operator on good successors, which we establish in this section.
Perhaps the most crucial property is that it is strictly increasing.

Lemma 3.1

Let all transformations be from
B
to a good successor
C
,
m<n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
b\geq 2
.

1.

{\uparrow}m<{\uparrow}n
.

2.

If
1<{\uparrow}b\leq c\leq d
, then
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m\leq\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m<\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
.

Proof 3.2
.

Note that we may assume without loss of generality that
m=n-1
, since otherwise we can see by induction on
n
that
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m\leq\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle{(n-1)}<\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
and
{\uparrow}m\leq{\uparrow}(n-1)<{\uparrow}n
.
The first claim is then immediate, as
{\uparrow}n
is explicitly selected larger than
{\uparrow}(n-1)
whenever
{\uparrow}(n-1)<\infty
, which is always the case for a good successor.

The second claim is proven by induction on
n
.
If
n<b
, we can apply the first claim to obtain
{\uparrow}m<{\uparrow}n
, and thus

\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m={\uparrow}m<{\uparrow}n=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n.

Otherwise, write
n=_{b}b^{e}a+r
and consider the following cases.

Case
1

(
r>0
). We use the induction hypothesis on
e
and
r
to see that

\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m=c^{\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle e}{\uparrow}a+\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle(r-1)\leq d^{\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle e}{\uparrow}a+\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle(r-1)<d^{\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle e}{\uparrow}a+\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle r=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n,

and since
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m=d^{\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle e}{\uparrow}a+\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle(r-1)
, the claim follows.

Case
2

(
r=0
). This case splits in two sub-cases.

Case
2.1

(
a>1
). We have that

\displaystyle\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m

\displaystyle=c^{\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle e}{\uparrow}(a-1)+\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle(b^{e}-1)

\displaystyle\leq d^{\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle e}{\uparrow}(a-1)+\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle(b^{e}-1)<d^{\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle e}({\uparrow}a-1)+\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle b^{e}=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\

Our proof of termination follows the same basic pattern as that of Goodstein’s original proof: namely, each fractal Goodstein progression is assigned a decreasing sequence of ordinals.

b^{e}-1=b^{e-1}(b-1)+b^{e-1}-1
and thus
O(b^{e}-1)={\Omega}^{O(e-1)}f(b-1)+O(b^{e-1}-1)
.
The induction hypothesis yields
O(e-1)<O(e)
and
O(b^{e-1}-1)<O(b^{e-1})
, so since
O(b-1)=f(b-1)<{\Omega}
,
O(b^{e}-1)<O(b^{e})
.

With this, we can define the ordinal interpretation we will use to prove termination of the Goodstein process.
For this particular interpretation, the lower base
{\rm base}_{B}(n)
of a number
n
will be more suitable than the upper base (see Definition
2.3
).
The reason for this is that when
n\in B
,
n
must ‘remember’ previous ordinals in order to provide the right value for
o^{*}_{B}
, so it is convenient for
n
to share a base with
n-1
.
We recall that, for non-basic elements, the upper and lower bases coincide.

Definition 4.7
.

Given a base hierarchy
B
, we define
o_{B}\colon\mathbb{N}\to\Omega
by

o_{B}(n)=\vartheta(O_{B}(n)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(n)),

where
O_{B}(n)
,
o^{*}_{B}(n)
are defined according to the following cases.

Case
1

(
n<\min B
).
Then,
O_{B}(n)=n
and
o^{*}_{B}(n)=0
.

Case
2

(
n\geq\min B
).

Let
b={\rm base}_{B}(n)
.
For
f=o_{B}\upharpoonright[0,b)
, we define an auxiliary operation given by
O^{b}_{B}(x):=O_{f}^{b}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then, consider the following sub-cases.

Case
2.1

(
n\notin B\setminus\{\min B\}
).

Set
O_{B}(n)=O^{b}_{B}(n)
.

Case
2.2

(
n\in B\setminus\{\min B\}
).
Set

O_{B}(n)=O^{b}_{B}(n-b)+o_{B}(n-b).

In either case, let
b^{*}<n
be greatest element of
B
such that
O_{B}(b^{*})+{\Omega}\geq O_{B}(n)+{\Omega}
, if such a
b^{*}
exists, and set
o^{*}_{B}(n)=o_{B}(b^{*})
.
If no such
b^{*}
exists,
o^{*}_{B}(n)=0
.

Example 4.8
.

Let us compute some values of
o_{B}
for
B=\{2,6\}
.
First we observe that for
n\leq 2
there is no
b^{*}\in B
with
b^{*}<n
as required by the definition of
o^{*}_{B}(n)
, so
o^{*}_{B}(n)=0
in this case.
Since
0,1<\min B
,
O_{B}(0)=0
and
O_{B}(1)=1
, so
o_{B}(0)=\vartheta(0)=1
and
o_{B}(1)=\vartheta(1)=\omega
.
We have that
O_{B}(2)={\Omega}
and thus
o_{B}(2)=\vartheta({\Omega})=\varepsilon_{0}
.
Similarly,
o_{B}(4)=\vartheta({\Omega}^{\Omega})
.

Now,
6\in B\setminus\{\min B\}
and
{\rm base}_{B}(6)=2
.
We thus have

O_{B}(6)=O^{2}_{B}(4)+o_{B}(4)={\Omega}^{\Omega}+\vartheta({\Omega}^{\Omega})

and
o^{*}_{B}(6)=0
since
O_{B}(2)+{\Omega}<O_{B}(6)+{\Omega}
.
It follows that
o_{B}(6)=\vartheta({\Omega}^{\Omega}+\vartheta({\Omega}^{\Omega}))
.

Next, we see that
O_{B}(7)=O_{B}(6+1)={\Omega}+\omega
, and in this case
o^{*}_{B}(7)=o_{B}(6)
, so

o_{B}(7)=\vartheta(({\Omega}+\omega)\oplus o_{B}(6))=\vartheta({\Omega}+o_{B}(6)+\omega).

Note that the natural sum is used here to avoid cancellation of
\omega
.

The next ‘interesting’ case is
o_{B}(n)
for
n=6^{6}+6
.
We have that
O_{B}(n)={\Omega}^{\Omega}+{\Omega}
.
Thus when computing
o^{*}_{B}(n)
, we see that it is no longer the case that
O_{B}(6)+{\Omega}\geq O_{B}(n)+{\Omega}
.
It is also not the case that
O_{B}(2)+{\Omega}\geq O_{B}(n)+{\Omega}
.
Hence,
o^{*}_{B}(n)=0
, and
o_{B}(n)=\vartheta({\Omega}^{\Omega}+{\Omega})
.

Example 4.9
.

In order to illustrate the behaviour of bases under
o
, let us consider the base hierarchy
C=\{2,4,8\}
.
These bases are as close as they can be to each other, given that each base must divide its successor by definition.

Since
2=\min C
, we have that
{\rm base}_{C}(2)=2
, i.e.
2
is its own base.
As in the previous example,
O_{C}(2)={\Omega}
and
o_{C}(2)=\vartheta({\Omega})
.
Let us also compute
o_{C}(3)
: here, we have that
{\rm base}_{C}(3)=2
and
3=_{2}2+1
, so
O_{C}(3)={\Omega}+o_{C}(1)={\Omega}+\omega
.
Meanwhile,
O_{C}(2)+{\Omega}={\Omega}\cdot 2=O_{C}(3)+{\Omega}
, so
o^{*}_{C}(3)=o_{C}(2)=\vartheta({\Omega})
.
It follows that

o_{C}(3)=\vartheta(O_{C}(3)\oplus o^{*}_{C}(3))=\vartheta({\Omega}+\vartheta({\Omega})+\omega).

Next, we compute
o_{C}(4)
.
Since
4\in C\setminus\{\min C\}
, the computation is a bit different from that of
o_{C}(2)
.
Note that
{\rm base}_{C}(4)=2
, so
4
is not its own base.
The definition then yields

O_{C}(4)=O^{2}_{C}(4-2)+o_{C}(4-2)
.
Since
{\rm base}_{C}(2)=2
, we will have that
O^{2}_{C}(2)=O_{C}(2)
and thus

O_{C}(4)=O_{C}(2)+o_{C}(2)={\Omega}+\vartheta({\Omega}).

Moreover,
O_{C}(2)+{\Omega}={\Omega}2=O_{C}(4)+{\Omega}
, so
o^{*}_{C}(4)=o_{C}(2)=\vartheta({\Omega})
.
We thus obtain

o_{C}(4)=\vartheta(O_{C}(4)\oplus o_{C}(2))=\vartheta({\Omega}+\vartheta({\Omega})2).

The factor
2
is not an accident, as removing it yields an ordinal below
o_{C}(3)
.

Finally, we compute
o_{C}(8)
.
We have that
{\rm base}_{C}(8)=4
, so the definition gives

O_{C}(8)=O^{4}_{C}(8-4)+o_{C}(8-4)
.
Unlike in the computation of
o_{C}(4)
,
{\rm base}_{C}(4)\neq 4
, so
O^{4}_{C}(4)\neq O_{C}(4)
.
Instead,
O^{4}_{C}(4)={\Omega}
and

O_{C}(8)={\Omega}+o_{C}(4)
.
Since
O_{C}(4)+{\Omega}\geq O_{C}(8)+{\Omega}
,
o^{*}_{C}(8)=o_{C}(4)
and

o_{C}(8)=\vartheta(O_{C}(8)\oplus o_{C}(4))=\vartheta({\Omega}+o_{C}(4)2).

Observe that, as per Definition
2.3
,
{\rm Base}_{C}(4)=4
.

The takeaway is that the base
4
, which is not
\min C
but is half of its successor, is implicitly assigned two uncountable ordinals: the standard
O_{C}(4)
, defined in terms of
{\rm base}_{C}(4)
, and
O^{4}_{C}(4)
, defined in terms of
{\rm Base}_{C}(4)
and used for computing
O_{C}(8)
.
In a sense, both ordinals provide ‘reasonable’ interpretations for
4
: in one, we are viewing
4
as the last number with base
2
, and in the other, as the first with base
4
.
This gives us a bit of wiggle room, and we selected the first option since it encodes the information needed to compute
o^{*}_{C}(n)
for
n\geq 8
.
The same situation will occur whenever there is
c\in C\setminus\{\min C\}
such that
S_{C}(c)=2c
.

Meanwhile, both of these ordinals are different from the value we would obtain if we had
4\notin C
: namely,
O^{2}_{C}(4)={\Omega}^{\Omega}
.

We have already seen how sometimes it is convenient to write numbers in the form
ba+r
with
r<b
.
Let us see how the large ordinal interpretation behaves in this case.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
B
be a base hierarchy and
n=ba+r
with
r<b
.
Then,

O^{b}_{B}(n)=O^{b}_{B}(ba)+o_{B}(r).

Proof 4.11

(sketch)
.

By induction on
n
.
Write
n=_{b}b^{e}x+y
and observe that either
y=r
and we can apply Definition
4.3
directly, or else
b<y<S_{B}(b)
and we can apply the induction hypothesis to
y
.

Following our proof plan, the next step is to prove that the ordinal interpretation
o_{B}
is monotone on
n
.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
B
be a base hierarchy and
m<n
. Then,

1.

o_{B}(m)<o_{B}(n)
, and

2.

if
{\rm base}_{B}(m)={\rm base}_{B}(n)
, then
O_{B}(m)<O_{B}(n)
.

Proof 4.13
.

By induction on
m+n
.
If
n\leq\min B
this is clear, so we assume otherwise.
Let
b={\rm base}_{B}(n)
and consider the following cases.

Case
1

(
O_{B}(m)+{\Omega}<O_{B}(n)+{\Omega}
).
Write
O_{B}(m)={\Omega}{\alpha}+\eta
and
O_{B}(n)={\Omega}\gamma+\delta
with
\eta,\delta<{\Omega}
.
Note that

{\Omega}{\alpha}+\eta\leq{\Omega}{\alpha}+(\eta\oplus o^{*}_{B}(n))<{\Omega}{\alpha}+{\Omega}\leq{\Omega}\gamma,

so

O_{B}(m)\leq O_{B}(m)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(m)<O_{B}(n)\leq O_{B}(n)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(n),

establishing the second claim when
{\rm base}_{B}(m)=b
.
We moreover note that
O_{B}(m)^{*}
is of the form
o_{B}(m^{\prime})
with
m^{\prime}<m
.
By the induction hypothesis,
o_{B}(m^{\prime})<o_{B}(n)
, so
o_{B}(m)<o_{B}(n)
by Proposition
4.2
.

Case
2

(
O_{B}(m)+{\Omega}\geq O_{B}(n)+{\Omega}
).
This case divides into the following.

Case
2.1

(
m\leq b
).
Let
b^{\prime}
be the least element of
B
greater or equal to
m
.
Since
m\leq b^{\prime}\leq b<n
, by the induction hypothesis applied to the second claim, we see that

O_{B}(m)\leq O_{B}(b^{\prime})
, hence
O_{B}(b^{\prime})+{\Omega}\geq O_{B}(n)+{\Omega}
.
It follows by the definition of
o^{*}_{B}(n)
that
o^{*}_{B}(n)=o_{B}(b^{*})
for some
b^{*}
with
m\leq b^{\prime}\leq b^{*}<n
.
Thus, the induction hypothesis yields

o_{B}(m)\leq o_{B}(b^{*})=o^{*}_{B}(n)<o_{B}(n).

Case
2.2

(
m>b
).
Note that in this case, we have that
b<m<n<S_{B}(b)
, so
{\rm base}_{B}(m)=b={\rm base}_{B}(n)
.
With this in mind, we consider two further sub-cases.

Case
2.2.1

(
n\notin B
).
Lemma
4.5
yields
O_{B}(m)<O_{B}(n)
, establishing the second claim.
For the first, write
m=ba+r

and
n=bc+s
with
r,s<b
.
If
a<c
, the second claim yields
O_{B}(m)<O_{B}(bc)
, and since the latter is a multiple of
{\Omega}
by Lemma
4.4
,

O_{B}(m)+{\Omega}\leq O_{B}(bc)<O_{B}(n)+{\Omega}.

This contradicts the assumption that
O_{B}(m)+{\Omega}\geq O_{B}(n)+{\Omega}
, and thus
a=c
.

Since
O_{B}(m)=O^{b}_{B}(m)
, we have by Lemma
4.10
that

O_{B}(m)=O^{b}_{B}(ba)+o_{B}(r)<O^{b}_{B}(ba)+o_{B}(s)

and

O_{B}(m)+{\Omega}=O^{b}_{B}(ba)+{\Omega}=O_{B}(n)+{\Omega},

so
o^{*}_{B}(m)=o^{*}_{B}(n)
and
O_{B}(m)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(m)<O_{B}(n)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(n)
.
Finally,

\displaystyle(O_{B}(m)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(m))^{*}

\displaystyle=\max\{O^{b}_{B}(ba)^{*},o_{B}(r)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(m)\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{O^{b}_{B}(ba)^{*},o_{B}(s)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(n)\}=(O_{B}(n)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(n))^{*},

so

o_{B}(m)=\vartheta(O_{B}(m)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(m))<\vartheta(O_{B}(n)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(n))=o_{B}(n).

Case
2.2.2

(
n\in B
).
We may assume that
m=n-1
, since otherwise we can apply the induction hypothesis to
m<n-1
.
Write
m=(n-b)+r
with
r=b-1
.
Since
n>b
and
n
is a multiple of
b
, we have that
r<b\leq n-b
, so the induction hypothesis yields
o_{B}(r)<o_{B}(n-b)
and thus

O_{B}(n)=O^{b}_{B}(n-b)+o_{B}(n-b)>O^{b}_{B}(n-b)+o_{B}(r)=O_{B}(m),

establishing the second claim.
Moreover,

O_{B}(m)+{\Omega}=O^{b}_{B}(n-b)+{\Omega}=O_{B}(n)+{\Omega},

so
o^{*}_{B}(m)=o^{*}_{B}(n)
,
and thus

\displaystyle(O_{B}(m)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(m))^{*}

\displaystyle=\max\{O^{b}_{B}(n-b)^{*},o_{B}(r)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(m)\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{O^{b}_{B}(n-b)^{*},o_{B}(n-b)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(n)\}

\displaystyle=(O^{b}_{B}(n-b)+o_{B}(n-b)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(n))^{*}

\displaystyle=(O^{b}_{B}(n)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(n))^{*},

so

o_{B}(m)=\vartheta(O_{B}(m)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(m))<\vartheta(O_{B}(n)\oplus o^{*}_{B}(n))=o_{B}(n).

We note that
O_{B}(n)
is computed differently depending on whether or not
n\in B
.
One intuition here is that, since we set
{\uparrow}_{B}^{C}n=S_{C}({\uparrow}(n-1))
, which is not ‘much larger’ than
{\uparrow}(n-1)
when
C
is a good successor of
B
, we should also have
O_{B}(n)
be not too much larger than
O_{B}(n-1)
.
Accordingly, the recipe for the case
n\notin B
will yield larger values than that for
n\in B
, as made precise by the following.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
B
be a base hierarchy
b\in B
and
n=S_{B}(b)<\infty
.
Then,

O^{b}_{B}(n)\geq O_{B}(n)+{\Omega}.

Proof 4.15
.

Since
n
is a multiple of
b
by the definition of base hierarchy,
O^{b}_{B}(n)
and
O^{b}_{B}(n-b)
are multiples of
{\Omega}
by Lemma
4.4
.
By definition,

O_{B}(n)=O^{b}_{B}(n-b)+o_{B}(n-b)<O^{b}_{B}(n-b)+{\Omega}.

Write
O^{b}_{B}(n-b)={\Omega}{\alpha}
and
O^{b}_{B}(n)={\Omega}{\beta}
.
Since
O^{b}_{B}(n-b)<O_{B}^{b}(n)
by Lemma
4.5
and Proposition
4.12
, we must have
{\alpha}<{\beta}
, and thus
{\alpha}+1\leq{\beta}
.
But then,

O^{b}_{B}(n-b)+{\Omega}={\Omega}({\alpha}+1)\leq{\Omega}{\beta}=O^{b}_{B}(n).

Example 4.16
.

Recall that in Example
4.9
, we had
C=\{2,4,8\}
and showed that
O^{4}_{C}(4)={\Omega}
,
O_{C}(4)={\Omega}+\vartheta({\Omega})
, and
O^{2}_{C}(4)={\Omega}^{\Omega}
.
Note that
O_{C}(4)+{\Omega}<O^{2}_{C}(4)
, so the inequality in Lemma
4.14
can be strict.
The general picture is that if
b\in C
and
c=S_{C}(b)<\infty
, then

O^{c}_{C}(c)<O_{C}(c)<O_{C}(c)+{\Omega}\leq O^{b}_{C}(c).

The final ingredient in the proof of termination is the preservation of the ordinal interpretation under the base upgrade.
Note that if we wish to prove that
o_{B}(m)=o_{C}({{\uparrow}}m)
, we will first need to show that
O_{B}(m)=O_{C}({{\uparrow}}m)
.
The following technical lemma will be a first approximation to this.

Lemma 4.17
.

Let all transformations be from
B
to a good successor
C
.
Suppose that
b\geq 2
is such that for all
m<b
,
o_{B}(m)=o_{C}({\uparrow}m)
.
Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
c\geq{\uparrow}b
,
O^{b}_{B}(n)=O^{c}_{C}(\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n)
.

Proof 4.18
.

Fix
b,c
satisfying the assumptions and proceed by induction on
n
.
The claim is trivial for
n=0
, so we assume otherwise.
Write
n=_{b}b^{e}a+r
.
Since
e,r<n
, the induction hypothesis yields

O^{b}_{B}(n)={\Omega}^{O^{b}_{B}(e)}o_{B}(a)+O^{b}_{B}(r)={\Omega}^{O^{c}_{C}(\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle e)}o_{C}({\uparrow}a)+O^{c}_{C}(\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle r)=O^{c}_{C}(\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n).

We remark that the assumption that
C
is a good successor for
B
is not used explicitly, but it ensures that
{\uparrow}
is well defined.
Lemma
4.17
can then be used in an inductive argument to show that
O_{B}
is preserved by the upgrade.

Lemma 4.19
.

Consider transformations from
B
to a good successor
C
.
Let
n\geq\min B
and
b={\rm base}_{B}(n)
.
Suppose that for all
m<n
,
o_{B}(m)=o_{C}({\uparrow}m)
.
Then,
O_{B}(n)=O_{C}({\uparrow}n)
.

Proof 4.20
.

If
n<\min B
, then
O_{B}(n)=n={\uparrow}n=O_{C}({\uparrow}n)
, and
O_{B}(\min B)={\Omega}=O_{C}(\min C)=O_{C}({\uparrow}\min B)
.
Thus we may assume
n>\min B
, so that for
1<b:={\rm base}_{B}(n)<n
.
Consider the following cases.

Case
1

(
n\notin B
).
We have by Lemma
3.7
2
that for
d={\rm base}_{C}({\uparrow}n)
,
{\uparrow}n=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
.
By Lemma
3.7
1
,
{\uparrow}n\notin C
, so Lemma
4.17
yields

O_{B}(n)=O^{b}_{B}(n)=O^{d}_{C}(\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n)=O_{C}({\uparrow}n).

Case
2

(
n\in B
).
We have that
{\uparrow}n=S_{C}({\uparrow}(n-1))\in C
.
Write
n-1=ba+r
with
r=b-1
and

{\uparrow}(n-1)=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle(n-1)=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle ba+{\uparrow}r={\uparrow}ba+{\uparrow}r,

where the second and third equalities are by items
1
and
2
of Lemma
3.5
, so in particular,
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle ba={\uparrow}ba
.
By Lemma
3.7
3
,

{\uparrow}n=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle ba+d
and
{\rm base}_{B}({\uparrow}(n-1))=d
.
Lemma
4.17
yields

O^{d}_{C}({\uparrow}ba)=O^{b}_{B}(ba)
, so

\displaystyle O_{C}({\uparrow}n)

\displaystyle=O^{d}_{C}({\uparrow}n-d)+o_{C}({\uparrow}n-d)

by Definition
2.5
,

\displaystyle=O^{d}_{C}(\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle ba)+o_{C}({\uparrow}ba)

since
{\uparrow}n-d=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle ba={\uparrow}ba
,

\displaystyle=O^{b}_{B}(ba)+o_{B}(ba)

by Lemma
4.17
and assumption,

\displaystyle=O_{B}(n)

by Definition
2.5
.

With this, we may prove that

is preserved under the base change.
Lemma
4.19
can be used inductively to show that
O
is preserved, and thus the bulk of the remaining work lies in showing that

is also preserved.

Proposition 4.21
.

If
C
is a good successor for
B
, then
o_{B}(n)=o_{C}({\uparrow}_{B}^{C}n)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof 4.22
.

Let all transformations be from
B
to
C
and proceed by induction on
n
to show that
O_{B}(n)=O_{C}({\uparrow}_{B}^{C}n)
and
o^{*}_{B}(n)=o^{*}_{C}({\uparrow}_{B}^{C}n)
, from which
o_{B}(n)=o_{C}({\uparrow}_{B}^{C}n)
follows by the way we defined
o
.
Lemma
4.19
and the induction hypothesis yield
O_{B}(n)=O_{C}({\uparrow}n)
, so it remains to show that
o^{*}_{B}(n)=o^{*}_{C}(n)
.

First, we check that
o^{*}_{B}(n)\leq o^{*}_{C}({{\uparrow}}n)
.
The case where
o^{*}_{B}(n)=0
is immediate, so assume
o^{*}_{B}(n)=o_{B}(b^{*})
for some
b^{*}\leq b
.
By Lemma

O_{C}({{\uparrow}}b^{*})+{\Omega}=O_{B}(b^{*})+{\Omega}\geq O_{B}(n)+{\Omega}=O_{C}({{\uparrow}}n)+{\Omega}.

Since

by Lemma

and
{\uparrow}b^{*}<{\uparrow}n
by Proposition

, Definition
4.3
yields
o^{*}_{C}({\uparrow}n)=o_{C}(c^{*})
for some
c^{*}\geq{\uparrow}b^{*}
.
Proposition
4.12
and the induction hypothesis then yield
o_{B}(b^{*})=o_{C}({\uparrow}b^{*})\leq o_{C}(c^{*})
.
We conclude that
o^{*}_{B}(n)\leq o^{*}_{C}({\uparrow}n)
.

Next, we check that
o^{*}_{C}({{\uparrow}}n)\leq o^{*}_{B}(n)
.
As above, we may assume that
o^{*}_{C}({{\uparrow}}n)=o_{C}(c^{*})
for some
c^{*}\leq{\rm base}_{C}({{\uparrow}}n)
.
Let
b^{*}
be greatest so that
b^{*}\in B
and
{\uparrow}b^{*}\leq c^{*}
, which exists since
{\uparrow}\min B=\min C\leq c^{*}
.
We claim that
{\uparrow}b^{*}=c^{*}
.

If not, since
{\uparrow}b^{*}\leq c^{*}
by choice of
b^{*}
, we must have that
{\uparrow}b^{*}<c^{*}<{{\uparrow}}n
.
By Lemma
3.1
1
, this implies that
b^{*}<n
.

Claim 1
.

Let
m=\min\{n,S_{B}(b^{*})\}
and let

m^{\prime}=\begin{cases}m-1&\text{if $m\in B$,}\\
m&\text{otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

Then,
{\rm base}_{B}(m^{\prime})={\rm base}_{B}(m)=b^{*}
and
{\uparrow}m^{\prime}\geq c^{*}
.

Proof 4.23

(of Claim
1
)
.

We prove the claim according to the following cases.

Case A

(
m\notin B
).
We must have that
m=n
, since
S_{B}(b^{*})\in B
.
The definition of
m^{\prime}
then yields
m^{\prime}=m=n
, and thus
c^{*}<{\uparrow}n={\uparrow}m^{\prime}
by choice of
c^{*}
.
Moreover,
b^{*}<n\leq S_{B}(b^{*})
, and the last inequality is strict since
S_{B}(b^{*})\in B
.
Since
m^{\prime}=n
,
{\rm base}_{B}(m^{\prime})={\rm base}_{B}(m)=b^{*}
follows.

Case B

(
m\in B
).
Then,
m^{\prime}=m-1\notin B
, since consecutive numbers cannot be bases.
We thus have that
b^{*}\leq m^{\prime}<m
, but
b^{*}\in B
, so
b^{*}\neq m^{\prime}
and thus
b^{*}<m^{\prime}<m\leq S_{B}(b^{*})
.
We conclude that
{\rm base}_{B}(m^{\prime})=b^{*}={\rm base}_{B}(m)
.
Moreover, since
m\in B
and
b^{*}
was chosen to be the maximum element of
B
with
{\uparrow}b^{*}\leq c^{*}
, we must have that
c^{*}<{\uparrow}m
.
By Definition
2.5
,
{\uparrow}m=S_{C}({\uparrow}(m-1))
, but
c^{*}\in C
, so
c^{*}<S_{C}({\uparrow}(m-1))
can only happen if
{\uparrow}(m-1)\geq c^{*}
.
Thus
{\uparrow}m^{\prime}\geq c^{*}
, as claimed.

Let
c={\rm base}_{C}(c^{*})
, and note that
c<c^{*}
, since
\min C\leq{\uparrow}b^{*}<c^{*}
.
We thus have that
c^{*}=S_{C}(c)
.
Moreover,
{\uparrow}b^{*}\in C
by Lemma
3.7
1
, so
{\uparrow}b^{*}<c^{*}
yields
{\uparrow}b^{*}\leq c
.

We claim that
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b^{*}\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m^{\prime}\geq c^{*}
.
Otherwise,
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b^{*}\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m^{\prime}<c^{*}=S_{C}(c)
and
{\uparrow}b^{*}\leq c
, and since
m^{\prime}\notin B
, Definition
2.5
implies that
{\uparrow}m^{\prime}=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b^{*}\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m^{\prime}
for some
d\leq c
with
{\uparrow}b^{*}\leq d
and
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b^{*}\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m^{\prime}<S_{C}(d)
.
Lemma
3.1
2
would then yield
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b^{*}\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m^{\prime}\leq\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b^{*}\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m^{\prime}<c_{*}
and thus
{\uparrow}m^{\prime}<c^{*}
, contrary to Claim
1
.
We conclude that
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b^{*}\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m^{\prime}\geq c^{*}
, as desired.

We then have that

\displaystyle O_{C}({\uparrow}m)

\displaystyle=O_{B}(m)

by Lemma
4.19
,

\displaystyle\geq O_{B}(m^{\prime})

by Proposition
4.12
and Claim
1
,

\displaystyle=O^{b^{*}}_{B}(m^{\prime})

since
m^{\prime}\notin B
and
b^{*}={\rm base}_{B}(m^{\prime})
,

\displaystyle=O^{c}_{C}(\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b^{*}\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m^{\prime})

by Lemma
4.17
,

\displaystyle\geq O^{c}_{C}(c^{*})

by Lemma
4.5
and
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b^{*}\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m^{\prime}\geq c^{*}
,

\displaystyle\geq O_{C}(c^{*})+{\Omega}

by Lemma
4.14
and
c^{*}=S_{C}(c)
.

By definition of
m
, either
m=S_{B}(b^{*})<n
or
m=n
.
If
m=S_{B}(b^{*})<n
, we have that
{\uparrow}m<{\uparrow}n
by Proposition
4.12
and
{\uparrow}m\in C
by Lemma
3.7
1
.
Moreover,

O_{C}({\uparrow}m)\geq O_{C}(c^{*})+{\Omega}\geq O_{C}({\uparrow}n),

where the second inequality is by choice of
c^{*}
.
But
O_{C}({\uparrow}m)\geq O_{C}({\uparrow}n)
implies that
O_{C}({\uparrow}m)+{\Omega}\geq O_{C}({\uparrow}n)+{\Omega}
, and thus
c^{*}\geq{\uparrow}m
by definition of
o^{*}_{C}
.
Since
m=S_{B}(b^{*})>b^{*}
, this contradicts our choice of
b^{*}
as the maximum element of
B
with
{\uparrow}b^{*}\leq c
.

If
m=n
, then

O_{C}({\uparrow}n)+{\Omega}>O_{C}({\uparrow}n)\geq O_{C}(c^{*})+{\Omega},

again contradicting our choice of
c^{*}
, since
o^{*}_{C}(n)=o_{C}(c^{*})
requires
O_{C}({\uparrow}n)+{\Omega}\leq O_{C}(c^{*})+{\Omega}
.

In all cases, we obtain a contradiction from the assumption
{\uparrow}b^{*}<c^{*}
.
Since
{\uparrow}b^{*}\leq c^{*}
, we thus conclude that
{\uparrow}b^{*}=c^{*}
.
By Lemma
4.19
,

O_{B}(b^{*})+{\Omega}=O_{C}({\uparrow}b^{*})+{\Omega}=O_{C}({\uparrow}b^{*})+{\Omega}\geq O_{C}({\uparrow}n)+{\Omega}=O_{B}(n)+{\Omega}.

Since
b^{*}\in B
and
b^{*}<n
, Definition
4.3
yields
o^{*}_{B}(n)=o_{B}(b^{+})
for some

, and Proposition
4.12
and the induction hypothesis then yield
o_{B}(b^{+})\geq o_{B}(b^{*})=o_{C}(c^{*})
.
We conclude that
o^{*}_{B}(n)\geq o^{*}_{C}({\uparrow}n)
.

We now have all the tools to prove that fractal Goodstein processes are always terminating.

Proof 4.24

(of Theorem
2.16
)
.

Let
o_{i}
denote
o_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
and
{\uparrow}_{i}
denote
{\uparrow}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}^{\mathcal{B}_{i+1}}
.
Let
I\leq\infty
be the length of the Goodstein sequence starting on
m
.
Then, if
\mathbb{G}^{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(m)>0
, we have that

o_{i+1}(\mathbb{G}^{\mathcal{B}}_{i+1}(m))=o_{i+1}({\uparrow}_{i}\mathbb{G}^{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(m)-1)<o_{i+1}({\uparrow}_{i}\mathbb{G}^{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(m))=o_{i}(\mathbb{G}^{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(m)),

where the first inequality is by Proposition
4.12
and the second by Proposition
4.21
.
It follows that
(o_{i}(\mathbb{G}^{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(m)))_{i<I}
is a decreasing sequence of ordinals, hence it is finite and we must have
o_{I-1}(\mathbb{G}^{\mathcal{B}}_{I-1}(m))=0
.

5
The Ouroboros Dynamical Hierarchy

We have shown that fractal Goodstein processes terminate, regardless of
\mathcal{B}
; in view of Example
2.12
, this can be seen as an alternative proof of Goodstein’s original theorem if we set
\mathcal{B}_{i}=\{i+2\}
.
However, for other choices of
\mathcal{B}
, the proof-theoretic strength of termination is much higher.
Our goal is to define a dynamical hierarchy which will yield a Goodstein principle independent of
\sf KP
.
This will be achieved by defining some specially tailored good successors
B_{+i}
for a given base hierarchy
B
, where
i
is a natural number parameter.
Our dynamical hierarchy, which will be denoted
\mathcal{O}
, will then be defined so that
\mathcal{O}_{i+1}=(\mathcal{O}_{i})_{+i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
The initial base hierarchy
\mathcal{O}_{0}
is not too important and we may simply let
\mathcal{O}_{0}=\{2\}
, mirroring Goodstein’s original theorem.

Fix a base hierarchy
B
; let us sketch how
B_{+i}
is to be constructed.
We hinted in the introduction about the idea of changing the base of
n\in\mathbb{N}
to
n
itself when performing the upgrade operator.
With the machinery we have developed, this would amount to finding a good successor
C
for
B
such that, for
b={\rm Base}_{B}(n)
,
{\uparrow}n=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}n\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
.
Suppose that a hypothetical such
C
is given and let all transformations be from
B
to
C
.

As per Definition
2.5
, replacing the base of
n
by
n
would require
{\uparrow}(n-1)<\langle\begin{smallmatrix}n\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n<S_{C}(n)
.
The first inequality cannot always be guaranteed to hold, since
{\uparrow}(n-1)
may be quite large.
Instead, we can let
{\uparrow}n=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
, where
d
itself is of the form
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d^{\prime}\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
.
The number
d
is not equal to
n
, but it is at least as large, and in view of properties such as Lemmas
3.3
2
and
4.17
, can be regarded as a ‘copy’ of
n
.

We need to have
d^{\prime}\geq{\rm base}_{C}({\uparrow}(n-1))
, but aside from that, we have some liberty in choosing the value of
d^{\prime}
.
In fact, we may even have
d^{\prime}=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d^{\prime\prime}\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
, and we can iterate this idea any finite number of steps.
Thus the general picture will be that, for a number
n
, we will add a sequence of bases
d_{1},\ldots,d_{i}
, with
d_{j+1}=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d_{j}\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
.
The restriction
d_{j}\geq{\rm base}_{C}({\uparrow}(n-1))
is obtained by letting
d_{0}
be the largest base previously available, then adding this as an initial value to the sequence.
Note that the value of
i
can vary; if we follow this construction for a given
i
, we obtain
B_{+i}:=C
.

Since our goal is to show that Theorem
2.16
is not provable in
\sf KP
, the dynamic hierarchy we will construct will be a sort of ‘worst case scenario,’ so we want to add the new bases
\{d_{j}\}^{i}_{j=1}
whenever possible.
Nevertheless, this cannot be done for
all

n\in\mathbb{N}
.
If
n<\min B
, then
n
must be fixed by the upgrade, and
\min B
must be mapped to
\min C\geq\min B
.
If we want
C
to be a good successor of
B
, then whenever
n\in B
,
{\uparrow}n
is determined by
{\uparrow}(n-1)
; namely, it must be the next multiple of
{\rm base}_{C}({\uparrow}(n-1))
.
Likewise, if
\min B<n\notin B
and
b\nmid n
, then for any
d\geq{\uparrow}b
, we have that
d\nmid\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
by Lemma
3.3
1
, so
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
cannot be a base if
d\in C
.
Thus we will add extra bases only in the ‘critical’ case, where
\min B<n
and
{\rm Base}_{B}(n)\mid n\notin B
.
With this, arrive at the following construction.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
B
be a base hierarchy and
i\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
.
Say that
n\in\mathbb{N}
is
B
-critical if
n>\min B
and
{\rm Base}_{B}(n)\mid n\notin B
.
Let
\partial B
denote the set of
B
-critical numbers.

We define a new base hierarchy
B_{+i}=\bigcup_{n<\infty}B_{+i}^{n}
, where
B_{+i}^{n}
is defined recursively as follows.
If
n\leq\min B
, define
B_{+i}^{n}=\{\min B+1\}
.
For
n>\min B
, assume inductively that
B_{+i}^{m}
has been defined and is finite for
m<n
, and let
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}\cdot\\
\cdot\end{smallmatrix}\rangle
be from
B
to
B^{n-1}_{+i}
.
Let
b={\rm base}_{B}(n)
and
c=\max(B_{+i}^{n-1}\cup\{1\})
.

Case
1

(
n\notin B
).
Set
B_{+i}^{n}=B_{+i}^{n-1}
.

Case
2

(
n\in B\setminus\partial B
).
Let
a
be least so that
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle(n-1)<ca
, and set

B_{+i}^{n}=B_{+i}^{n-1}\cup\big\{ca\}.

Case
3

(
n\in\partial B
).

Define
d_{j}=d_{j}(n)
recursively by
d_{0}=c
and

d_{j+1}=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d_{j}\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n.

Then, set

B_{+i}^{n}=B_{+i}^{n-1}\cup\{d_{j}\}_{j=1}^{i}.

We refer to a hierarchy of the form
B_{+i}
as an ouroboros successor.
We define the ouroboros dynamical hierarchy
\mathcal{O}=(\mathcal{O}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
to be the unique dynamical hierarchy with
\mathcal{O}_{0}=\{2\}
and
\mathcal{O}_{i+1}=(\mathcal{O}_{i})_{+i}
.

The moniker is given since in the critical case, an upgrade of
n
is used as the new base for
n
, as if it were ‘eating its own tail’.

Example 5.2
.

In Example
2.10
, we defined an operation
B\mapsto B_{+}
.
This is in fact a special case of an ouroboros successor: specifically,
B_{+}=B_{+0}
.
To see this, note that if
n
is
B
-critical, then we are to add the bases
\{d_{j}(n)\}_{j=1}^{0}
; but this set is empty, hence
B^{n}_{+0}=B^{n-1}_{+0}
.
It follows that only the clause for
n\in B
produces new bases, and then it is easily checked to produce the same new base as in the definition of
B_{+}
.

Example 5.3
.

Now, let
B=\{2\}
and let us compute
B^{6}_{+1}
.
As before,
B^{3}_{+1}=\{3\}
, but now
B^{4}_{+1}=B^{3}_{+1}\cup\{d_{1}(4)\}
, so we do add a new base for
4
.
We easily compute
d_{1}(4)=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}3\\
2\end{smallmatrix}\rangle 4=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}3\\
2\end{smallmatrix}\rangle 2^{2}=3^{3}
and hence
B^{4}_{+1}=\{2,27\}
.
Since
2\nmid 5\notin B
,
B^{5}_{+1}=B^{4}_{+1}
.
On the other hand,
B^{6}_{+1}=B^{5}_{+1}\cup\{d_{1}(6)\}
, and since
27
is the maximum element of
B^{5}_{+1}
, we have that
d_{1}(6)=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}27\\
2\end{smallmatrix}\rangle 6=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}27\\
2\end{smallmatrix}\rangle(2^{2}+2)=27^{27}+27
.

It should be observed that we will add one new base for every even number, and thus
B_{+1}
is infinite, even though
B
has only one element.

Example 5.4
.

Once again, let
B=\{2\}
and let us compute
B^{4}_{+2}
.
The construction is very similar, including the computation of
d_{1}(4)=27
.
However, in this case, we have that
B^{4}_{+2}=B^{3}_{+2}\cup\{d_{1}(4),d_{2}(4)\}
.
So, we must also compute
d_{2}(4)=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}27\\
2\end{smallmatrix}\rangle 4=27^{27}
, and hence
B^{4}_{+2}=\{3,27,27^{27}\}
.

Finally, we remark that, while the value of
d_{1}(4)
did not change compared to Example
5.3
, that is no longer the case for
d_{1}(6)
.
Instead, we have
d_{1}(6)=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}27^{27}\\
2\end{smallmatrix}\rangle 6
, a number we will not even attempt to represent, let alone the gargantuan
d_{2}(6)=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d_{1}(6)\\
2\end{smallmatrix}\rangle 6
.

The takeaway is that
B^{n}_{+i}
adds new bases only at ‘critical’ numbers, i.e. those satisfying
1<{\rm base}_{B}(n)\mid n\notin B
, in which case
i
new bases are added.
Since there are infinitely many such numbers in any base hierarchy, even a finite one,
B_{+i}
is always infinite whenever
i>0
.
Moreover,
d_{j}(n)
depends also on
B
and
i
, but we omit them from notation since they will always be fixed and clear from context.

It is useful to note that in our construction of
B_{+i}
, the new bases that are added are always larger than any previously available base, and indeed are multiples of these bases.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
B
be a base hierarchy and
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
C=B_{+i}
and
C_{n}=B_{+i}^{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

1.

For all
n>0
,
C_{n-1}\subseteq C_{n}
, and if
d\in C_{n}\setminus C_{n-1}
, then
d>\max C_{n-1}
.

2.

If
n\in B\setminus\{\min B\}
, then
c_{n-1}\mid c_{n}=S_{C}(c_{n-1})
, and there is no multiple of
c_{n-1}
between
{\uparrow}(n-1)
and
c_{n}
.

3.

If
n\in\partial B
and
d_{j}=d_{j}(n)
, then for
j<i
,
d_{j}\mid d_{j+1}=S_{C}(d_{j})
.
In particular,
c_{n-1}=d_{0}
and
c_{n}=d_{i}
.

Proof 5.6
.

Let
n>0
,
b={\rm Base}_{B}(n-1)
, and
c=\max C_{n-1}
.
We can only have
C_{n}\neq C_{n-1}
if
n\in B\setminus\{\min B\}
or
n\in\partial B
.
Then,
B^{n}_{+i}
has exactly one new element
ca>\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n-1\geq\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle b=c
, with the second inequality being by Lemma
3.1
2
, and chosen so that
a
is minimal, i.e. there are no multiples of
c
between
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n-1
and
ca=S_{C_{n}}(c)
.

In the second,
n
is
B
-critical, which means that
1<b\mid n\notin B
.
Let
d_{j}=d_{j}(n)
.
We have that
d_{0}=c
and
d_{j+1}=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d_{j}\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
.
Since
b\mid n
,
d_{j}\mid d_{j+1}
by Lemma
3.3
1
.
Moreover, we cannot have
b=n
since
n\notin B
, so
b<n
.
Thus by Lemma
3.1
2
,
d_{j}=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d_{j}\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle b<\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d_{j}\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n=d_{j+1}
.
Thus,
C_{n}\setminus C_{n-1}=\{d_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
, with
c\mid d_{1}=S_{C_{n}}(c)
and
d_{j}\mid d_{j+1}=S_{C_{n}}(d_{j})
for
1\leq j<i
.

In both cases, we obtain
1
, from which we easily deduce that if
c\in C
and
S_{C_{n}}(c)\in C_{n}
, then
S_{C}(c)=S_{C_{n}}(c)
, thus yielding
2
and
3
, since we have proven them with
S_{C_{n}}
in place of
S_{C}
.

In fact, the number
c_{n}
will always be the base of
n
after upgrade, due to the following.

Lemma 5.7
.

Consider transformations from
B
to
C
, where
B
is a base hierarchy and
C=B_{+i}
with
i>0
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
C_{n}=B^{n}_{+i}
.
Then, for all
n,c\in\mathbb{N}
, if
c=\max C_{n}
and
b={\rm Base}_{B}(n)
, then
{\uparrow}n=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n<S_{C}(c)
, and hence
{\rm Base}_{C}({\uparrow}n)=c
.
As a consequence,
{\uparrow}_{B}^{C}
is a total function.

Proof 5.8
.

Proceed by induction on
n
.
If
n\leq\min B
, then
c=\min C
and either
n<\min B
and
{\uparrow}n=n=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
, or
{\uparrow}n=\min C=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
.
Thus we may henceforth assume that
n>\min B
.

For
x\in\mathbb{N}
, let
c_{x}=\max C_{x}
, so that in particular,
c=c_{n}
.
Let
b_{x}={\rm Base}_{B}(x)
.
By the induction hypothesis, we have that for
x<n
,
{\uparrow}x=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c_{x}\\
b_{x}\end{smallmatrix}\rangle x<S_{C}(c_{x})
, and thus
{\rm Base}_{C}({\uparrow}x)=c_{x}\leq c_{n}
.
By Lemma
5.5
1
, we see that
C{\upharpoonright_{c_{n}}}=C_{n}
, since if
y\in C\setminus C_{n}
then
y\in C_{z}
for some least
z>n
and thus
y>\max c_{z-1}\geq c_{n}
.
Lemma

then yields
{\uparrow}x={\uparrow}_{B}^{C_{n}}x
, and thus
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle^{C}_{B}x=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle^{C_{b}}_{B}x
for all
c\geq{\uparrow}b
and
x
, given that
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}\cdot\\
\cdot\end{smallmatrix}\rangle_{B}^{C}
is defined in terms of
{\uparrow}_{B}^{C}\upharpoonright[0,b)
.
From this it will follow in all relevant instances that transformations could be regarded as being from
B
to either
C_{n}
or
C
, and thus we do not need to specify the target base hierarchy.

Thus,
{\uparrow}n=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
, where
d\in C
is least such that
{\uparrow}(n-1)<\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n<S_{C}(d)
, if such a

exists.

Let us first that
d=c
itself satisfies this property.
Let
m
be the least multiple of
b
strictly above
n
.
Then,
m\in B\cup\partial B
, and it is easy to see that if
n<k<m
then
C_{n}=C_{k}
, since such a
k
cannot be a base nor can it be critical.
It follows that
S_{C}(c)=d:=\min(C_{m}\setminus C_{n})
.
If
m\in B
, then this element is of the form
ca
, explicitly chosen to be greater than
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle(m-1)\geq\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
, where the inequality follows from Lemma
3.1
1
.
Otherwise,
d=d_{1}(m)=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle m>\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
, with the inequality again following from Lemma
3.1
1
.
In either case,
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n<d=S_{C}(c)
.

Next we check that no
d<c
is such that
{\uparrow}(n-1)<\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n<S_{C}(d)
.
Let
b^{\prime}={\rm Base}_{B}(n-1)
.
The induction hypothesis yields
{\uparrow}(n-1)=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c_{n-1}\\
b^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\rangle(n-1)<\infty
.
If
d<c_{n-1}
, then
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n<S_{C}(d)\leq c_{n-1}\leq\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c_{n-1}\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle(n-1)={\uparrow}(n-1)
, contrary to
{\uparrow}(n-1)<\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
.
Thus, we only need to consider
d\geq c_{n-1}
.
If
c_{n}=c_{n-1}
, there is nothing to prove, since
c
is the least possible value for
d
and thus
{\uparrow}n=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
, as claimed.
So, assume
c_{n}>c_{n-1}
and consider the following cases.

Case
1

(
n\in\partial B
).
Since we are assuming that
c_{n}\neq c_{n-1}
, we must also have
i>0
.
Letting
d_{j}=d_{j}(n)
, we have by Lemma
5.5
3
that for
j<i
,
d_{j+1}=S_{C}(d_{j})
, and moreover
c=c_{n}=d_{i}
.
So, if
d\in C
is such that
c_{n-1}\leq d<d_{i}
, we must have that
d=d_{j}
for some
j\in(0,i)
.
But then,
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d_{j}\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n=d_{j+1}=S_{C}(d_{j})
, which rules out
d=d_{j}
as this requires
\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d_{j}\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n<S_{C}(d_{j})
.

Case
2

(
n\in B
).
Let
b^{\prime}={\rm Base}_{B}(n-1)
.
In this case,
C_{n}=C_{n-1}\cup\{c\}
by definition, with
{\uparrow}(n-1)=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c_{n-1}\\
b^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\rangle(n-1)<c=S_{C}(c_{n-1})
.
By the induction hypothesis,

{\uparrow}(n-1)=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c_{n-1}\\
b^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\rangle(n-1)\geq c_{n-1}.

We then have that
{\uparrow}(n-1)<c=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\
n\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n<S_{C}(c)
, and if

then

\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
n\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n=d\leq c_{n-1}\leq\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c_{n-1}\\
b^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\rangle(n-1)={\uparrow}(n-1),

so indeed
c
is the least value for

and thus
{\uparrow}n=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\rangle n
.

With this, we are ready to prove that indeed
B\mapsto B_{+i}
produces good successors.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
B
be a base hierarchy,
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then,
B_{+i}
is a base hierarchy and a good successor for
B
.
In particular,
\mathcal{O}
is a dynamical hierarchy.

Proof 5.10
.

The second claim clearly follows from the first, as
\mathcal{O}_{0}=\{2\}
, which is a base hierarchy by Example

, so we focus on the first.

Consider transformations from
B
to
{B_{+i}}
.
We check that
{B_{+i}}
is a base hierarchy as follows: let
c\in C
and let
n
be least so that

.
Then, we have that either

and

by Lemma
5.5
2
, or else

, so

for some

and

by Lemma
5.5
3
.

To check that
C
is a good successor for
B
, we first note that

is total by Lemma
5.7
.
If
n\in B
, let

,

, and

.
Also by Lemma

,
{\uparrow}(n-1)=\langle\begin{smallmatrix}c\\

.
But we chose

It then follows from Theorem

that the fractal Goodstein process for

is always terminating; however, this fact is independent of
\sf KP
.
In order to prove this, we need to review fundamental sequences for the Bachmann-Howard ordinal.

6
Fundamental Sequences

In most, if not all, past studies on Goodstein processes, one ordinal interpretation is used to prove both termination and independence.
However, in our case, the ordinal interpretation
o_{B}
only provides an upper bound on the order-types of numbers in a Goodstein sequence; it is not ‘tight’ enough to provide a lower bound.
While it is possible to find a tight ordinal interpretation, it becomes rather cumbersome.
Instead, our lower bound will be based on the
\psi
-function, which grows much more slowly than
\vartheta
(see e.g.
[
5
]
).

Definition 6.1
.

Set
\sup\varnothing=0
and define

\psi\colon\varepsilon_{{\Omega}+1}\to{\Omega}
by

\psi(\xi)=\sup\{\psi(\zeta)+1:\zeta<\xi\text{ and }\zeta^{*}<\psi(\zeta)\}.

Despite their different growth rates,
\psi
and
\vartheta
both provide notations for the Bachmann-Howard ordinal, defined equivalently as
\sup_{n<\omega}\psi(\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44333pt\mathopen{\vphantom{I}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{n}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44333pt\mathopen{\vphantom{I}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{n}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.45374pt\mathopen{\vphantom{I}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{n}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.12625pt\mathopen{\vphantom{I}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{n}}}}{\Omega})
or
\sup_{n<\omega}\vartheta(\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44333pt\mathopen{\vphantom{I}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{n}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44333pt\mathopen{\vphantom{I}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{n}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.45374pt\mathopen{\vphantom{I}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{n}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.12625pt\mathopen{\vphantom{I}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{n}}}}{\Omega})
.
Recall that
\vartheta
only took additively indecomposable ordinals as values; this was helpful to ease computations, but leaves many ‘gaps.’
In contrast, it is not hard to see that if
\xi<{\Omega}
, then
\psi(\xi)\leq\xi
.
In fact, the range of
\psi
is all of
\psi(\varepsilon_{{\Omega}+1})
, so we can represent any relevant ordinal in terms of
0
,
\psi
, and the function
({\alpha},{\beta},\gamma)\mapsto{\Omega}^{\alpha}{\beta}+\gamma
.

Fundamental sequences are sequences
(\xi[n])_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, such that when
\xi
is a limit,
\xi[n]\to\xi
.
They are defined for ordinals written in
\psi
-normal form.

Definition 6.2
.

Given
\xi<\varepsilon_{{\Omega}+1}
, we say that
\psi(\xi)
is in
\psi
-normal form if
\xi^{*}<\psi(\xi)
.

Note in particular that
\psi({\Omega}_{n})
is always in
\psi
-normal form.
Fundamental sequences also depend on the cofinality of
\xi
, as given by the following definition.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
\xi<\varepsilon_{{\Omega}+1}
.
The cofinality of
\xi
, denoted
\tau(\xi)
, is given recursively by

1.

\tau(\xi)=\omega
if
\xi<{\Omega}
,

2.

\tau({\Omega}^{\alpha}{\beta}+\gamma)=\tau(\gamma)
if
0<\gamma<{\Omega}^{\alpha}
,

3.

\tau({\Omega}^{\alpha}{\beta})=\omega
if
{\beta}<{\Omega}
is a limit,

4.

\tau({\Omega}^{\alpha}({\beta}+1))=\tau({\alpha})
if
{\alpha}
is a limit, and

5.

\tau({\Omega}^{{\alpha}+1}({\beta}+1))={\Omega}
.

Note that successors are assigned cofinality
\omega
, so this is not a ‘true’ cofinality, but this definition is convenient for the fundamental sequences below.

Definition 6.4
.

Let

\psi[\varepsilon_{{\Omega}+1}]=\{\xi<\varepsilon_{{\Omega}+1}:\xi^{*}<1+\psi(\varepsilon_{{\Omega}+1})\}.

Then, for
\xi\in\psi[\varepsilon_{{\Omega}+1}]
and
\theta<\tau(\xi)
, we define
\xi[\theta]
inductively by

1.

0[\theta]=1[\theta]=0
,

2.

({\Omega}^{\alpha}{\beta}+\gamma)[\theta]={\Omega}^{\alpha}{\beta}+\gamma[\theta]
if
\gamma>0
,

3.

({\Omega}^{\alpha}{\beta})[\theta]={\Omega}^{\alpha}({\beta}[\theta])
if
{\beta}
is a limit,

4.

{\Omega}^{{\alpha}+1}[\theta]={\Omega}^{\alpha}\theta
,

5.

({\Omega}^{\alpha}({\beta}+1))[\theta]={\Omega}^{\alpha}{\beta}+{{\Omega}^{\alpha}[\theta]}
if
{\beta}>0
,

6.

{\Omega}^{\alpha}[\theta]={\Omega}^{{\alpha}[\theta]}
if
{\alpha}
is a limit,

7.

\psi(\zeta)[\theta]=\psi(\zeta[\theta])
if
\tau(\zeta)=\omega
,

8.

if
\tau(\zeta)={\Omega}
and
\xi=\psi(\zeta)
, then
\xi[0]=0

and

\xi[\theta+1]=\psi(\zeta[\xi[\theta]])
,

where all expressions are written in either
{\Omega}
-decomposition or
\psi
-normal form.

These fundamental sequences converge and preserve
\psi
-normal forms, as shown in
[
??
]
.

Lemma 6.5
.

If
\lambda\in\psi[\varepsilon_{{\Omega}+1}]
is a limit, we have that
(\lambda[\iota])_{\iota<\tau(\lambda)}
is an increasing sequence which converges to
\lambda
.

Moreover, if
\psi(\zeta)
is in
\psi
-normal form and
i>0
, then:

1.

If
\tau(\zeta)=\omega
, then
\psi(\zeta[i])
is in
\psi
-normal form.

2.

If
\tau(\zeta)={\Omega}
and
\xi=\psi(\zeta)
, then
\psi(\zeta[\xi[i]])
is in
\psi
-normal form.

We also note that fundamental sequences mostly map uncountable ordinals to uncountable ones.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\zeta\in\psi[\varepsilon_{{\Omega}+1}]
and
\iota<\tau(\zeta)
.
If
\zeta>{\Omega}
, then either
\zeta[\iota]\in\{0,1\}
and
\iota=0
, or else
\xi[\iota]
is uncountable.

Proof 6.7
.

Write
\zeta={\Omega}^{\eta}{\alpha}+\gamma
and proceed by induction on
\zeta
.
Since
\zeta>{\Omega}
,
\zeta[\iota]
is countable only if
\gamma=0
.
If
{\alpha}
is a limit then
\zeta[\iota]={\Omega}^{\eta}({\alpha}[\iota])
, which is either uncountable or zero.
If
{\alpha}
is a successor, then
\zeta[\iota]
is uncountable.

Otherwise,
{\alpha}=1
.
If
\eta
is a limit, then
\zeta[\iota]
can only be countable when
\eta[\iota]=0
, whence
\zeta[\iota]={\Omega}^{0}=1
.
Otherwise,
\eta=\delta+1
is a successor and

since

, so
\zeta[\iota]
is uncountable.

Definition 6.8
.

For

and
{\alpha}<\psi(\varepsilon_{{\Omega}+1})
, define
{{\alpha}}\llbracket{i}\rrbracket
recursively by
{{\alpha}}\llbracket{0}\rrbracket={\alpha}
and
{{\alpha}}\llbracket{i+1}\rrbracket={{\alpha}}\llbracket{i}\rrbracket[i+1]
.
Define

to be the least

.

A sequence

is ‘stepping down the fundamental sequences.’
The function

is total since
{\psi(\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44333pt\mathopen{\vphantom{I}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{n}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44333pt\mathopen{\vphantom{I}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{n}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.45374pt\mathopen{\vphantom{I}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{n}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.12625pt\mathopen{\vphantom{I}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{n}}}}{\Omega})}\llbracket{i+1}\rrbracket<{\psi(\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 5.44333pt\mathopen{\vphantom{I}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{n}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 5.44333pt\mathopen{\vphantom{I}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{n}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.45374pt\mathopen{\vphantom{I}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{n}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.12625pt\mathopen{\vphantom{I}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{n}}}}{\Omega})}\llbracket{i}\rrbracket
whenever the right-hand side is not zero, but totality is not provable in

.
In fact, a more general claim holds: it is a special case of a general principle of Cichon et al.
[
6
]
, which applies to

due to results of Buchholz
[

]
.

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
f
be a computable function and suppose that
{\sf KP}\vdash\forall x\exists y(y=f(x))
.
Then,
\exists m\ \forall n>m\ \big(f(n)<F(n)\big)
.

Here, the expression

should be represented according to the conventions of Theorem
2.17
.

7
Independence

Given Theorem
6.9
, in order to to prove independence of Theorem
2.16
, it suffices to compare our Goodstein process to the process of stepping down the fundamental sequences.
As pointed out above, the ordinal interpretation
o_{B}
is not suitable here.
Instead, we will introduce a second ordinal interpretation,
u_{B}
, based on
\psi
.
The issue here is that
\psi
does not have some of the key properties of
\vartheta
which allowed us to define a monotone ordinal assignment, and thus
u_{B}
will not be monotone.
This, however, will not be a problem.

To illustrate this, let us turn to the proof of independence of the original Goodstein principle.
In this setting, one typically shows that for
b\geq 2
,

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\omega\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)(n-1)\geq\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\omega\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)n[b-1]

(where
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\omega\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)n[x]
should be read as
(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\omega\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)n)[x]
).
Using properties of the fundamental sequences and the ordinal assignment, including monotonicity, this suffices for concluding that the Goodstein process takes longer than the process of stepping down the fundamental sequences below
\varepsilon_{0}
, which is not provably terminating in
\sf PA
.
But there is a different argument which avoids the use of monotonicity of the ordinal assignment.

Suppose that we show that for every
n
and
b\geq 2
there is
n^{\prime}<\left(\begin{smallmatrix}b+1\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)n
such that
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\omega\\
b+1\end{smallmatrix}\right)n^{\prime}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\omega\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)n[b-1]
.
For example, if
b=3
and
n=3^{3}
, we could take
n^{\prime}=4^{2}
and observe that

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\omega\\
4\end{smallmatrix}\right)n^{\prime}=\omega^{2}=\omega^{\omega}[2]=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\omega\\
3\end{smallmatrix}\right)n[2].

It is not too hard to check that such an
n^{\prime}
can always be found.

Then, reason as follows.
Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\xi=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\omega\\
2\end{smallmatrix}\right)m
.
Inductively define a sequence
(n_{i})
with
n_{0}=m
,
n_{i+1}<\left(\begin{smallmatrix}i+3\\
i+2\end{smallmatrix}\right)n_{i}
, and such that
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\omega\\
i+3\end{smallmatrix}\right)n_{i+1}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\omega\\
i+2\end{smallmatrix}\right)n_{i}[i+1]
.
By induction on
i
,
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\omega\\
i+2\end{smallmatrix}\right)n_{i}={\xi}\llbracket{i}\rrbracket
.
Moreover, letting
m_{i}={\mathbb{G}}^{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(n)
, we inductively see that

m_{i+1}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}i+3\\
i+2\end{smallmatrix}\right)m_{i}-1\geq\left(\begin{smallmatrix}i+3\\
i+2\end{smallmatrix}\right)n_{i}-1\geq n_{i+1};

note that the first inequality requires monotonicity of the
finitary
base change, but not the
in
finitary one.
Thus
m_{i}>0
as long as
{\xi}\llbracket{i}\rrbracket>0
, and we have avoided using the monotonicity of the ordinal assignment.
We only need for the finitary base change to be monotone, but this corresponds to the monotonicity of the upgrade operator, which will still be available.

Thus our function
u_{B}
will be such that, letting
u_{i}=u_{\mathcal{O}_{i}}
and
{\uparrow}_{i}={\uparrow}_{\mathcal{O}_{i}}^{\mathcal{O}_{i+1}}
, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
then there is
n^{\prime}<{\uparrow}_{i}n
such that
u_{i+1}(n^{\prime})=u_{i}(n)[i+1]
.
We can then replicate the above argument to show that the Goodstein process majorizes the function
F
of Definition
6.8
.
The function
u_{B}
follows a similar pattern to
o_{B}
, where a ‘large’ ordinal assignment
U_{B}
is first defined, and then ‘collapsed’ to
u_{B}
.

Definition 7.1
.

Given a base hierarchy
B
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
u_{B}(n)<\psi(\varepsilon_{{\Omega}+1})
inductively on
n
.
If
n<\min B
, then
u_{B}(n)=n
.

Otherwise, let
b={\rm Base}_{B}(n)
.
In this case, we also define
U_{B}(n)\in\psi[\varepsilon_{{\Omega}+1}]
.
Let
f=u_{B}\upharpoonright[0,b)
and define
U^{b}_{B}(x)=O_{f}^{b}(x)
, where
O_{f}^{b}
is as in Definition
4.3
, and set
U_{B}(n)=U_{B}^{b}(n)
.
In both cases,
u_{B}(n)=\psi(U_{B}(n))
.

Despite being simpler to work with in many ways, the main drawback of the
\psi
function over
\vartheta
is the need to check whether ordinals are in normal form before applying standard operations to them.
Likewise, the
u_{B}
and

Definition 7.2
.

Let
B
be a base hierarchy and
b\in B
.
Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

, we simultaneously define

to be in

-normal form,
U_{B}
-normal form, and
U^{b}_{B}
-normal form inductively on

1.

If

n

u_{B}
-normal form,
U^{b}_{B}
-normal form, and
U_{B}
-normal form.

2.

If

u_{B}
-normal form,

-decomposition.

9
Concluding remarks

We have exhibited a Goodstein process based only on exponential notation, yet independent of very strong theories.
This opens a new pathway to obtaining powerful extensions of Goodstein’s classic result.
A somewhat less powerful Goodstein principle is obtained by using a version of the Ackermann function instead of the exponential
[
1
]
; it would be interesting to see if the combination of the two approaches leads to substantially more powerful principles, thus avoiding the use of Hardy functions in the search for even longer Goodstein processes.

Another interesting question we have not addressed is whether our processes retain the maximality condition of the Goodstein’s original, where the normal forms employed lead to the longest possible termination time among all processes using the same bases
[
8
]
.
Here one may ask what ‘all processes’ should be considered for a maximality principle: should we consider variations of the dynamical hierarchy?
Or, simply variations of notation within a fixed dynamical hierarchy?

Finally, we note a peculiarity of our proofs of termination and independence.
We have used a different ordinal interpretation for each, but normally one employs a single ‘optimal’ ordinal interpretation.
In fact, it is possible to give such an interpretation, at the expense of computations becoming substantially more cumbersome.
The crucial difference is that for
\min B<n\notin B
, if
O_{B}(n)={\Omega}{\alpha}+\gamma
with
\gamma<{\Omega}
, we must set

o_{B}(n)=\vartheta({\alpha}\oplus{\beta}(n))+\gamma.

The proof of termination is not too different from the one presented here, but independence uses the fundamental sequences for
\vartheta

[
4
]
, which are much less straightforward than those for
\psi
.
Overall, we felt that the simplifications afforded by using two interpretations outweighed the cost of introducing a second collapsing function.
While this was mostly a stylistic choice, a dual-interpretation approach may prove indispensable in devising Goodstein processes independent of increasingly strong subsystems of second order arithmetic, perhaps one day including second order arithmetic itself.
