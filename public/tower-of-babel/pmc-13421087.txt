Demographic Differences in Otolaryngology Applicants Compared with Other Specialties: Analysis of 2022‐2024 Match Data
Authors: Maria Armache, Angela Renne, Francis Deng, Jenny X. Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe analyzed publicly available data from the National Resident Matching Program to compare the demographics of applicants who ranked otolaryngology–head and neck surgery (OHNS) programs first with those who ranked other specialties first in the 2022 to 2024 Match cycles. Among 1337 applicants preferring OHNS and 110,677 applicants preferring non‐OHNS specialties, significant differences emerged. OHNS applicants were more likely to be male, White, and US citizens, and less likely to identify as Asian, Black/African American, or Hispanic/Latino. They were also less likely to report indicators of socioeconomic disadvantage, including first‐generation college or medical graduate status, or receipt of government assistance during childhood. No significant differences were observed in sexual orientation or disability status. These findings suggest that applicants to OHNS differ demographically from those to other specialties, with evidence of underrepresentation of individuals from historically marginalized and lower socioeconomic backgrounds. Similar trends have been observed in other competitive specialties, underscoring the need for targeted efforts to improve equity in recruitment strategies.Level of EvidenceLevel IV.

Otolaryngology–head and neck surgery (OHNS) is one of the most competitive specialties with an average match rate of 77.6% for US medical student seniors from 2022 to 2024. 1 Prior studies have identified persistent gender and racial disparities among OHNS trainees, which do not mirror the demographics of the United States. 1 We compared the demographics of OHNS applicants in the National Resident Matching Program (NRMP) Main Residency Match™ with those of applicants to other specialties to explore recent trends in recruitment.

Materials and Methods

We analyzed publicly available demographic data of Match applicants in 2022, 2023, and 2024 that includes survey responses from ~85% of applicants. 2 Applicants to the Supplemental Offer and Acceptance Program were excluded. This study was deemed exempt from review by the Johns Hopkins Institutional Review Board.

We examined sex assigned at birth, sexual orientation, race, ethnicity, disability, first‐generation college and medical graduate status, childhood urbanicity, contributions to household income during childhood, receipt of government assistance during childhood. Since only US citizens were asked about goverment assistance, US citizenship status was inferred based on this assumption. “Do not know” or “Prefer not to answer” responses were excluded. The significance of differences in demographic distributions were determined using Chi‐squared tests. Percentage differences between demographic characteristics among applicants preferring OHNS (ie, submitting a rank order list in which the first rank was an OHNS program) and those preferring other specialties were determined and expressed with Wald binomial proportion confidence intervals (CI). Statistical analyses were performed using Excel (Microsoft). Detailed data collection and analysis methods have been previously described. 3

Results

From 2022 to 2024, 1337 applicants preferentially ranked OHNS, while 110,677 applicants preferentially ranked other specialties. There were differences in the demographic distributions of applicants to OHNS as compared to applicants to other specialties for sex (P < .001), race (P < .001), ethnicity (P = .04), childhood urbanicity (P < .001), parental education (P = .01), childhood income contribution (P < .001), childhood government assistance (P < .001), and US citizenship status (P < .001). OHNS had a higher proportion of male applicants (OHNS minus other specialties = +5.71%, 95% CI 3.01%‐8.40%) and White applicants (+7.64%, 95% CI 4.95%‐10.32%) as compared to non‐OHNS specialties. In contrast, lower proportions of applicants preferring OHNS were Asian (−3.62%, 95% CI −6.09% to −1.15%), Black/African American (−2.8%, 95% CI −4.14% to −1.50%), as well as Hispanic/Latino (−1.81%, 95% CI −3.43% to −0.19%) as compared to applicants preferring other specialties. Non‐US citizens also applied to OHNS at a lower rate (−17.05%, 95% CI −18.17% to −15.92%).

OHNS applicants were more likely to report childhoods in suburban areas (+12.36%, 95% CI 9.80%–14.93%) and less likely to report childhoods in urban areas (−10.4%, 95% CI −12.49% to −8.33%), with no significant difference for rural areas as compared to non‐OHNS applicants. OHNS applicants were also less likely to have been first‐generation college graduates (−4.66%, 95% CI −6.45% to −2.86%) or first‐generation medical school graduates (−3.98%, 95% CI −6.47% to −1.49%). A lower proportion of OHNS applicants contributed to family incomes (−2.62%, 95% CI −4.45 to −0.79%) or received government assistance (−4.12%, 95% CI −6.14 to −2.11%) during childhood compared to applicants to other specialties. No differences were observed in sexual orientation or disability status (Table 1).

Discussion

We identified demographic differences between OHNS applicants and those applying to other specialties in recent Match years. Applicants preferring OHNS were more likely to be male, White, non‐Hispanic/Latino, and US citizens and less likely to be first‐generation college or medical school graduates or to report socioeconomic disadvantage during childhood. These findings suggest that OHNS attracts applicants from more privileged backgrounds, which has been reported in other competitive specialities including orthopedic surgery 4 and dermatology. 5 Prior studies in OHNS have explored gender and racial differences among applicants and residents, demonstrating persistent underrepresentation of groups historically underrepresented in medicine (URiM). 6 , 7 , 8 Our analysis extends this work by examining not only race and gender but also indicators of socioeconomic status using an applicant survey with a high response rate.

Several underlying mechanisms may be postulated. First, access to mentorship and specialty exposure differs across groups. 9 , 10 Prior cross‐sectional studies found that Black/African‐American and Hispanic/Latino students perceive limited mentorship and cultural exclusion as barriers to pursuing OHNS. 11 , 12 , 13 Second, financial barriers may also dissuade students from lower socioeconomic statuses from applying into competitive fields that require money for research years, away rotations, and applications. 14 , 15 Further qualitative research is needed to examine the experiences of each group.

Based on this study's findings, a variety of potential interventions could be considered. As mentorship is a key determinant of specialty choice and programs with more diverse faculty have been found to have higher trainee diversity, 10 , 11 , 16 , 17 inclusive recruitment at all stages is vital. 8 , 10 , 11 , 16 , 17 , 18 Additionally, financial support for students such as paid research experiences or funded visiting clerkships may be impactful. Further research is needed to better understand the impact of targeted interventions.

This study is limited by its reliance on self‐reported survey data susceptible to reporting biases. The data analyzed also excludes applicants may have preferred OHNS but did not have any programs to rank. Moreover, uncaptured variables may also influence match outcomes, such as indicators of academic performance. Additionally, the absence of individual‐level data prevents analysis of intersectional variables. Lastly, with only 3 match cycles analyzed, the sample size may not detect small differences.

Conclusion

Demographic differences exist between applicants to OHNS and applicants to other specialties in recent Match years.
