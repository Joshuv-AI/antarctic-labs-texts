Green synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles using bioactive compounds from the Amycolatopsis roodepoortensis strain EA7 and their effects in the HT-29 cell line
Authors: Elham Amiri, Mirsasan Mirpour, Khosro Issazadeh, Behnam Rasti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The biological synthesis of nanoparticles (NPs) has attracted because of their non-toxic abilities. We reported the synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) via an extract of the Amycolatopsis roodepoortensis strain EA7. Their physical characteristics were investigated with XRD, FT-IR, SEM, TEM, DLS, EDX, and zeta potential analyses. The toxicity of nanoparticles (NPs) was investigated via antimicrobial and the MTT assays in the HT-29, HEK293, and HDF1BOM cell lines.The expression levels of genes related to apoptosis (ATM, ATR, CHK1, and CHK2), anti apoptose (MMP-9 and Bcl-2), cell death, and cell cycle distribution were determined in the HT-29 cell line. ZnO nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) exhibited stronger antibacterial efficacy against clinical and standard Staphylococcus aureus (250 and 125 μg/mL) than Pseudomonas aeruginosa (500 μg/mL) by the MIC assay. The anticancer effects of these nanoparticles (NPs) demonstrated a dose‒dependent relationship against HT-29, HEK-293, and HDF1BOM cell lines. The IC50 values were determined to be 47 , 32.88, and 81.37 μg/mL for the HT-29, HEK293, and HDF1BOM cell lines, respectively. The levels of genes related to apoptosis (ATM = 2.35 ± 0.293, ATR = 2.87 ± 0.280, CHK1 = 3.67 ± 0.378, and CHK2 = 5.86 ± 0.495) were significantly increased compared to the control (P ≤ 0.0001). However, no significant decrease was observed in the expression of genes associated with cancer development. Flow-cytometric analysis revealed apoptosis induction (P ≤ 0.001) and cell cycle arrest in G0/G1 phase in the HT-29 cells. Given the results presented, this research could be an important step towards further investigations and the development of novel antimicrobial and anticancer therapeutics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45333-3.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer ranks as most the common type of cancer in women and the third most frequent cancer in men1. Predisposing factors that can cause colon cancer include three important factors: epigenetic, genetic, nutritional, and lifestyle factors2,3. Chemotherapy is a well-known treatment method for colon cancer patients1,4. However, chemotherapeutic agents, in addition to targeting cancer cells, also cause side effects4–6. Therefore, there is a necessity for drug carriers that help reduce these side effects when delivering antitumor medications1. Nanoparticles (NPs) show promise as pharmaceutical agents, with zinc oxide (ZnO) being notable among metal oxide NPs7,8. ZnO nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) possess characteristic physicochemical properties and exhibit fewer side effects compared to other metal NPs.They demonstrate good catalytic activity, remarkable coating, and good bonding ability9,10. Also, one of the important benefits of ZnO NPs is their biodegradability and environmental friendliness. Additionally, there are no mentions of toxicity related to these NPs11,12. Nowadays, due to the widespread use of NPs in various industries, their synthesis has attracted the attention of researchers. These methods are divided into three general categories: physical, chemical, and biological5. However, their biomedical applications are often restricted by synthetic conditions, which involve harmful chemicals, acidic pH, and high temperatures. As a result, green synthesis has attracted notable attention because of its environmental friendliness, cost-effectiveness, and minimal toxicity1,10. Different research works have looked into how NPs are made using plants and microorganisms such as fungi and bacteria, which provide nucleation cores for nanoparticle production13.

ZnO NPs have been prepared using various plants, bacteria and fungi, which show catalytic activities and therapeutic potential against colon cancer cell lines9,11.

Among the various synthesis methods of ZnO NPs, biosynthesis using actinomycetes extract has attracted attention due to the diverse potential of metabolites. This method has attracted attention due to its low cost, environmental friendliness, and the use of a single-step method for the treatment of cancers, infections and degradation of organic pollutants. The actinomycetes extract acts as a capping and reducing agent13.

Bacteria-mediated NPs synthesis can occur either intracellularly or extracellularly. Actinobacteria stand out as promising options for both the extracellular and intracellular synthesis of metallic NPs. Because of their ability to produce potent bioactive secondary metabolites, ease of handling, low cost, and minimal experimental requirements5.

In recent years, extracellular synthesis of NPs has attracted attention due to its simplicity and large-scale production. In this method, microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi secrete biomolecules into the culture medium that reduce metal ions and stabilize the formed NPs. This method has advantages over intracellular synthesis, including easier separation of NPs from biomass, reduced processing time, and better control over particle size and shape. For this reason, extracellular synthesis is considered an economical and environmentally friendly method for producing NPs for medical and industrial applications5,13.

In this study, we selected extracellular synthesis using rare actinobacteria, as it allows efficient production of stable NPs with consistent properties. However, working with rare actinobacteria presents several challenges, including limited availability of strains, variable NPs yields, and the need for specialized growth conditions or equipment13. Controlling particle size, shape, and aggregation is also difficult, which can affect reproducibility and practical applications. Despite these challenges, rare actinobacteria remain an important resource for discovering novel bioactive NPs6.

NPs derived from actinobacteria are particularly suitable because of their potential for genetic manipulation and stability4,13. In recent years, several studies have been conducted to synthesize nanoparticles from Streptomyces, bacteria (Klebsilla pneumoniae), plants, and also to investigate their physicochemical properties9,11.

ZnO NPs synthesized using Pleurotus sago-caju mushroom extract have shown potential medical and environmental applications14.

Previous studies have shown that ZnO and Ag nanoparticles synthesized using bioactive compounds from actinomycetes have good antimicrobial potential and biocompatibility9–11. Recent studies have demonstrated the green synthesis of ZnO and Ag NPs using Ocimum tenuiflorum and Valeriana jatamansi extract.These nanoparticles have antibacterial and antibiofilm activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria15,16

However, simultaneous investigation of the biological effects and cytotoxicity of these NPs and their pharmaceutical applications is still limited. These studies have focused mostly on common bacteria and plants and have used traditional methods9,11,14.

A review of the reported studies revealed that no attention had been given to the synthesis of NPs using rare actinomycetes. This indicates the existence of a specific research gap in this regard. To address this gap, the present study investigates the extracellular synthesis of NPs using rare actinobacteria. This study aimed to explore the biosynthesis of ZnO NPs via the extraction of the rare and scarce actinomycete Amycolatopsis roodepoortensis strain EA7, which was extracted and characterized from the soils of native areas located in northern Iran (southwestern Guilan Province) according to a previous study17. In this study, the synthesized NPs were analyzed using XRD, FT-IR, SEM, TEM, DLS, EDX, and zeta potential techniques. The antimicrobial effects of the synthesized NPs on the bacteria studied were investigated via three methods: agar well diffusion, MIC, and MBC. Additionally, we examined the cytotoxicity of the ZnO NPs synthesized with the extract strain EA7 in the HT-29 colon cancer cells, HEK-293, and HDF1BOM cell lines. This study also demonstrated the impact of these NPs on the expression of antiapoptotic, proapoptotic genes, apoptosis/necrosis levels, and cell cycle analysis.

In addition, comprehensive investigations into the synthesis of NPs using bioactive compounds obtained from rare actinomycetes remain limited. To our knowledge, this study provides the first detailed report on the synthesis of ZnO NPs utilizing bioactive metabolites isolated from these strains in this region.”

Materials and methods

Actinomycete isolate

The actinobacterial species Amycolatopsis roodepoortensis strain EA7 was extracted from agricultural soil samples collected in the Guilan Province of northern Iran. The strain was identified through 16S rRNA gene sequencing, as outlined by17.

Fermentation process and cell-free extract preparation

The process of the cell-free extract followed the established submerged fermentation technique outlined by14. 1000 mL of microbial suspension prepared from pure actinomycete colonies cultured on ISP2 agar media was inoculated into ISP2 broth media (100 mL) and adjusted to a pH of 7 with sodium chloride (1 M). The cultures were incubated under dark conditions for 7 days at 30 °C with continuous shaking (120–150 rpm) to facilitate optimal growth and metabolite production. For the extraction process, the fermented broth was centrifuged (4000 rpm, 20 min, 40 °C) to separate the cellular biomass from the supernatant. The clarified supernatant was then meticulously collected and utilized as the bioactive extract for subsequent NPs synthesis experiments. The antibacterial properties of the culture medium were determined from 5 to 7 days, as described in a previous study18.

Biosynthesis and purification of ZnO NPs

Zinc acetate salt solution (Zn(CH₃COO)₂·2H₂O, 1% w/v ) was incrementally introduced into the cell-free extract of strain EA7 and was subjected to continuous magnetic stirring for 30 min. Sodium hydroxide was used to adjust the pH to 8. Following 1 h of stirring, the solution was incubated overnight. The addition of the bacterial extract to the zinc salt solution resulted in a color change of the solution, indicating the formation of ZnO NPs. The suspension was subsequently centrifuged for 30 min at 5000 rpm and 4 °C. After purification with distilled water and 70% ethanol, NPs were dried in an oven and subsequently calcined at 350 °C for 2 h to increase crystallinity19,20.

Properties of the biosynthesized ZnO NPs

The morphology and dimensions of the NPs were characterized via transmission and scanning electron microscopy (TEM and SEM)9. To evaluate the crystallinity of the synthesized nanoparticles, X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed at 40 kV14. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was applied to determine the elemental composition of the samples15. The functional groups were identified via Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy in the 400–4000 cm⁻1 range14. Additionally, dynamic light scattering (DLS) was used to evaluate the hydrodynamic size and distribution in the suspension. Zeta potential was used to determine surface charge of the NPs in solution15.

Preparation and identification of multidrug-resistant bacteria

Multidrug-resistant strains, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aeruginosa, were selected as target strains for the antimicrobial assay of ZnO NPs from sourced hospitals in Rasht city, Iran. These strains were characterized and verified in our laboratory according to previous research18. We subsequently conducted an antibiotic sensitivity test via the disc diffusion method to evaluate their responses to eight antibiotics: chloramphenicol (C-30 mg), tetracycline (TE-30 mg), vancomycin (V-30 mg), ampicillin (AMP-10 mg), imipenem (IPM-10 mg), ciprofloxacin (CIP-5 mg), ceftazidime (CAZ-30 mg), and piperacillin (PIP-100 mg)19. In this study, the standard strains P. aeruginosa PTCC 1565 and S. aureus PTCC 1112 were utilized.

Assessment of the antibacterial properties of the biosynthesized ZnO NPs

Agar well diffusion assay

The NPs were mixed into 1 mL of DMSO. The NPs were made in different concentrations: 5000, 4000, 3000, and 2000 μg/mL. The test bacteria were adjusted to a standard 0.5 McFarland. Mueller‒Hinton agar (MHA) plates were uniformly swabbed with the bacterial suspension via a sterile cotton swab. Each well was filled with 50 µL of the respective NPs suspension at different concentrations and was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h14,20.

Determination of the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) 15 9 20

MIC of the nanoparticles (NPs) was assessed against the target bacterial strains via the broth microdilution technique in 96-well microplates in accordance with the CLSI (2021) guidelines15,21. The procedure was as follows: Each well was filled with 100 µL of Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB). Then, A 100 µL aliquot of the NPs stock solution was added to the first well and a two-fold serial dilution was performed to achieve a concentration range of 2000 to 62.5 µg/mL. The bacterial suspension was diluted (1:100) to obtain a final concentration of 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL. 100 µL of the diluted bacterial suspension was added to each well. The bacterial suspension served as a positive control and sterilized culture medium (MHB) was used as a negative control. The microplate was incubated at 37 °C overnight. The MIC was recorded as the lowest NPs concentration that completely inhibited visible bacterial growth (no turbidity). 100 µL from each clear well (no growth) was spread onto Mueller–Hinton Agar (MHA) plates. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for overnight. Wells without turbidity were used to determine MBC.

Cytotoxic potential of the biosynthesized ZnO NPs in the HT-29, HEK-293, and HDF1BOM cell lines

The cell lines (HT-29, HEK-293, and HDF1BOM cell lines) were used from the cell linescollection of Mahamax Laboratory, Iran, and were maintained and cultured in Mahamax Laboratory. The HDF cell line was served as a normal cell. This study used the MTT assay to evaluate the efficacy of ZnO NPs against the HT-29 colorectal adenocarcinoma, HEK-293, and HDF1BOM cells. The cells were grown in DMEM with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and a 1% penicillin–streptomycin mixture under controlled conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). A 100 μL cell suspension was added to plates and incubated overnight. Then the cells were treated with different quantities of ZnO NP: 500, 250, 125, 62.5, 31.25, and 15.625 μg/mL. After 24 h, 10 μL of MTT solution was added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere to let the viable cells form formazan crystals. Next, 100 μL of DMSO was added to each well and the plates were protected from light and gently agitated for 30 min at 25 ± 2 °C. The optical absorbance was assessed via ELISA reader at 570 nm14,22,23. The percentage of viable cells was determined as below:

% cell viability = (OD value of treated samples/OD value of untreated samples) × 100.

Additionally, the concentration required for 50% cell death (IC50) was assessed24,25.

Gene expression level determination via real-time PCR in the HT29 cell line

To examine gene expression, we analyzed the antiapoptotic genes Bcl2 and MMP9, as well as genes that effectively reduce cancer cell growth including, ATR-CHK1 and ATM-CHK2. Additionally, the GAPDH gene was addicted as a reference gene. Initially, total RNA was extracted from both treated and untreated cells that had been exposed to nanoparticles via an RNA extraction kit. The RNA concentration was determined with a vortex nanodrop device. cDNA synthesis was subsequently conducted with a Sina clone kit. Real-time PCR was performed with a light cycler device under the following temperature conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, secondary denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 55–60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s; this process was repeated for a total of 40 cycles 24,26. In this study, the difference in threshold cycles was analyzed, and the gene expression level was determined with the formula 2-∆∆Ct24. Investigating the type of cell death via Annexin V/PI staining in the HT-29 cell line

The percentages of apoptotic and necrotic cells were assessed in the HT-29 cells that were treated with ZnO NPs, followed by staining with FITC annexin V and Propidium iodide (PI). The cells (at a density of 5 × 105) were treated with ZnO NPs at the IC₅₀ concentration for 24 h. Following treatment, both adherent and floating cells were washed with cold Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS), and resuspended in binding buffer. Then, the cells were stained with FITC annexin V and PI (5 μL each) for 15 min in the dark at room temperature. Flow cytometric analysis was performed to distinguish four distinct cell populations: viable cells, early apoptotic cells, late apoptotic cells, and necrotic cells23,28.

Cell cycle analysis in the HT-29 cell line

The impact of ZnO NPs was evaluated in human colon cancer HT-29 cells via PI staining. The cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well, incubated for 24 h, and allowed to adhere. After treatment with ZnO NPs at predetermined IC₅₀ concentrations for 24 h, the cells were harvested, washed twice with PBS, and fixed in 70% ethanol at -20 °C overnight. Fixed cells were resuspended in 250 μL of PI staining solution containing RNase A for cell cycle analysis. The DNA content distribution was measured via flow cytometry (BD FACSCalibur), with untreated HT-29 cells serving as the negative control23,28.

Statistical analysis

All experimental results were analyzed via both the SPSS-16 and GraphPad Prism-9 software packages. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for data evaluation. The quantitative results are expressed as the means ± standard deviations. We used t-test with P ≤ 0.0001, P ≤ 0.001, and P ≤ 0.01 to determine the significance or nonsignificance of the results14,28.

Preparation and identification of multidrug-resistant bacteria

In accordance with diagnostic microbiology, the target strains were validated in our laboratory. A total of six bacterial strains were identified, which included three strains of Staphylococcus aureus and three strains of Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Additionally, among these clinical samples, four samples were from wounds (isolated from male patients), one sample was from respiratory secretions (isolated from a female), and one sample was from blood (isolated from a female). The main characteristic of the Staphylococcus aureus strains was coagulase positivity, and the main characteristic of the Pseudomonas aeruginosa strains was pyocyanin production. Following CLSI guidelines, antibiotic susceptibility testing was reported over a 24 h incubation period at 37 °C. The standard strains of Staphylococcus aureus PTCC 1112, Pseudomonas aeruginosa PTCC 1565, and the clinical strains were resistant to the antibiotic disks (Figs. 7, 8).Fig. 7Antibiotic susceptibility testing of standard strains of Staphylococcus aureus PTCC 1112 and Pseudomonas aeruginosa PTCC 1565.Fig. 8Antibiotic susceptibility testing of clinical specimens of Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.

In contrast, the inhibition zones of 22, 20, 19, and 15 mm were observed at the same concentrations against P. aeruginosa (Fig. 9b). Cytotoxic potential of biosynthesized ZnO NPs in the HT-29, HEK-293, and HDF1BOM cell lines

The cytotoxic effects of the ZnO NPs were assessed in the HT-29, HEK-293, and HDF1BOM cell lines via the MTT test. The cytotoxicity of ZnO NPs was dose-dependent, with cell viability decreasing as the concentration of NPs increased. The greatest inhibition of cell proliferation was observed in the HT-29 cell line at a concentration of 500 μg/mL (P ≤ 0.0001), followed by 250, 125, 62.5, and 31.250 μg/mL (P ≤ 0.001) compared with the untreated control. ZnO NPs reduced cell viability at concentrations of 500, 250, and 125 μg/m by 3.40, 5.04, and 10.26% (P ≤ 0.0001), respectively, in the HEK-293 cell line. At concentrations of 62.5 and 31.25 μg/mL, the reductions were 17.21% and 30.22% (P ≤ 0.001), respectively, compared with those of the control sample. In this cell line, ZnO NPs had no significant effect compared with the control sample at concentrations of 15.62 and 7.813 μg/mL (Fig. 11b).

ZnO NPs reduced cell viability at concentrations of 500 and 250 μg/mL, by 10.80 and 18.27% (P ≤ 0.0001), respectively, in HDF1BOM cell line. The IC50 values, indicating the concentration required for 50% inhibition of cell growth, were 47 μg/mL, 32.88, and 81.37 μg/mL for the HT-29, HEK-293, and HDF1BOM cell lines, respectively.

Discussion

Cancer is recognized as one of the most significant global health issues1. Despite advancements in treatment methods, common cancer therapies often have various side effects4,6. The development of nanotechnology, owing to its distinct chemical and physical properties, has led to valuable studies in the diagnosis of numerous diseases, including cancer11. Among the various methods of NPs synthesis, biological synthesis (green synthesis) stands out in nanotechnology.This method is preferred because of the environmental friendliness, cost-effectiveness, and minimal toxicity of the resulting NPs19,29. NPs are synthesized biologically using various catalysts, including plants, algae, fungi, yeasts, and bacteria9,12,14,20 reported that the use of extracts from P. aeruginosa enhances the antibacterial properties of the resulting NPs. However, most studies have concentrated on the chemical synthesis of NPs, with little research focused on the biological synthesis of ZnO NPs using rare actinomycetes. This study aimed to biosynthesize ZnO NPs using extracts from the Amycolatopsis roodepoortensis strain EA7. Additionally, bacterial extract to the zinc salt solution resulted in a color change of the solution, indicating the formation of ZnO NPs. This phenomenon is due to the reduction and nucleation of Zn2 + ions by the biomolecules present in the extract. Due to the nano-size and semiconducting nature of ZnO, the resulting NPs exhibit different light absorption and scattering, which causes the color change to be observed14,20.

The characteristics of the synthesized ZnO NPs were confirmed by different analyses. The FT-IR spectrum revealed various functional groups, such as phenolic compounds, proteins, carboxylic acids, aromatics, alkenes, amines, amides, and carbohydrates, which are crucial for the biosynthesis and stabilization of ZnO NPs. Also, the absorption peak between 509 and 449 cm–1 indicates that these NPs are coated with the extract and stabilized. TEM images revealed that the ZnO NPs were spherical, confirming 2.98 ± 0.69 nm size and their spherical structure. In the SEM analysis, spherical NPs with a size of 140 ± 0.36 nm were determined. Previous studies have reported different ranges of absorption peaks for ZnO NPs synthesized from microbes and plants9,12,23. Kalaba et al.9 reported analysis of Streptomyces-synthesized ZnO NPs at 349–351 cm–1, with 21.72 ± 4.27 nm particle size. The variations in the absorption spectra may be attributed to the shape type, size, and dielectric constant of the NPs11,18. Additionally, the size and absorption peak of the synthesized NPs in our study differed from the results of similar studies conducted by11,12. DLS analysis revealed that the NPs had sizes below 390 nm (< 390 nm). The average zeta potential was calculated to be -19.1 mV. Zeta potential analysis demonstrated that the NPs had moderate colloidal stability. Generally, when the zeta potential approaches -30 mV, metal NPs exhibit greater stability and a lower toxic impact on biological systems14,30. The absorption peak, SEM, DLS, and zeta potential results in this study align closely with findings from similar studies14,16,18. Additionally, EDX analysis confirmed Zn and O atoms in NPs structure. The observed size difference between ZnO NPs obtained via DLS and those obtained via TEM and SEM can be attributed to multiple scattering and the measurement principles of these techniques of NPs11. There is a possibility that in our research, several 2 nm single cores were stacked on top of each other via SEM. The DLS size of ZnO NPs, along with the core of the metal NPs, is influenced by materials adsorbed on their surfaces11,23.

The well diffusion agar, MIC, and MBC methods were used to investigate the antimicrobial properties of the ZnO NPs. In this study, the synthesized ZnO NPs demonstrated concentration-dependent antibacterial effects against target strains. In the agar well diffusion test, antibacterial properties were effective at concentrations ranging from 2000–5000 μg/mL for both strain groups. The antimicrobial effect of the synthesized NPs in the present study was greater than that reported by20 and similar studies by14. This can be attributed to the small size of the NPs synthesized in the present research compared with those in similar studies11.

The lowest amounts needed to stop the growth of multidrug-resistant bacteria and the standard S. aureus strain were 250 and 125 μg/mL. For the multidrug-resistant bacteria and the standard P. aeruginosa strain, the amount used was 500 μg/mL. The minimum bactericidal concentrations for both groups of P. aeruginosa and S. aureus strains were 2000 and 1000 μg/mL, respectively. Owing to the identical antimicrobial results of the ZnO NPs, statistical analysis could not be used for this part of the study.

Ref15 demonstrated the antimicrobial activity of ZnO NPs prepared by Ocimum tenuiflorum plant extract. Their results (MIC: 25 μg/mL for Gram- positive bacteria and 15 μg/mL for Gram-nagative bacteria) do not align with the MIC results from our study21 investigated the preparation of ZnO NPs using Arthrospira platensis algae and their results showed the MIC: 50 mg/L for S . aureus and 25 mg/L for P. aeruginosa. Our study results demonstrated that the antimicrobial properties of synthesized ZnO NPs were greater than that reported by21. This difference in the results of different studies could be due to the density of cells per well, incubation conditions, and physiological and genetic conditions of the strains15,21. The ZnO NPs synthesized in the present study had a greater effect on Gram-positive bacteria than on Gram-negative bacteria, which can be explained by the reason that the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria contains lipopolysaccharide compounds9,15.

The differences observed between MIC and well-plating methods can be attributed to the underlying mechanisms of each method15,21. In MIC, NPs are in direct contact with bacterial cells, allowing immediate interaction, whereas in well plating, their effect depends on gradual diffusion through the agar medium31,32. Consequently, the concentration of nanoparticles decreases with increasing distance from the well. It is also worth noting that, in the MIC method, the NPs concentration within the wells remains uniform, which may contribute to the consistency of the results33.

The ZnO NPs exhibited peak cytotoxicity at concentrations of 500 μg/mL (P ≤ 0.0001) in the HT-29 and HEK-293 cell lines. Lower cytotoxicity was observed in the human skin fibroblast cell line (HDF1BOM) compared with the other two cell lines (HT-29 and HEK-293). At concentration of 15.62 μg/mL, no significant difference was observed in the HT-29 and HDF1BOM cell lines. Additionally, at concentrations of 15.62 and 7.813 μg/mL, no significant difference was observed compared to the control sample in the HEK-293 cell line. The IC50 values of the ZnO-NPs for the HT-29, HEK-293, and HDF1BOM were determined to be 47, 32.88, and 81.31 μg/mL, respectively5,24 were reported the cytotoxicity of ZnO and CuO NPs in the HT-29 cell line. The results revealed that apoptosis was induced and was observed a large increase in the number of cells in the sub-G1 phase. This finding aligns closely with findings from similar studies.

Ref22 reported that the IC50 for ZnO NPs synthesized from leaf plants was 194.3 μg/mL in the HT-29 cell line. This finding does not align with our results. In our study, ZnO NPs exhibited greater cytotoxicity against the HEK-293 than against the HT-29 and HDF. These findings are compatible with the previous study24,25,35. The reason can be the different nature of the cell lines, size, and physicochemical properties of the NPs25,34. The higher growth rate, proliferative capacity, and sensitivity to ROS observed in the HEK-293 cell line compared with the other two cell lines may explain the increased drug uptake in this line35. HDF cells are more resistant than HT-29 and HEK-293 cell lines, likely due to their normal cellular nature, more efficient antioxidant, DNA repair systems, and lower proliferation and uptake rates23,35.

Here, the anticancer properties of the ZnO NPs were stronger than their antimicrobial effects, according to the report by33. The reason can be attributed to the difference between the cell wall structure, physiological variations, and oxidative stress in bacteria and humans25,33. Bacteria possess metal resistance mechanisms, including metal-binding proteins, efflux transporters, and redox-active enzymes, which can reduce metal ion toxicity and limit NPs-induced damage. Another reason is that they can form biofilms23,25. In contrast, cancer cells are more susceptible to oxidative stress and intracellular NPs interactions, leading to enhanced cytotoxic effects28,32. Bacteria have metal-transforming enzymes and metal-efflux systems. Additionally, the ability of bacteria to form biofilms contributes to their tolerance against metal ions and NPs32,33. In our study, the expression levels of the ATR, ATM, CHK1, and CHK2 genes were found to be higher than of the reference gene GAPDH, which suggests that apoptosis was induced in the HT-29 cells. The NPs that were synthesized did not significantly reduce the expression of the MMP-9 and Bcl-2 genes or stop the movement of the cells. The results of our study regarding the reduction of Bcl-2 and MMP-9 genes are not consistent with the findings of29. This difference is related to the different natures of the two genes, the duration of treatment of cells with the NPs, and the concentration of the NPs29. In flow cytometry analysis, 46.74% (P ≤ 0.0001) of the HT-29 cells underwent apoptosis-inducing. Also, these NPs stop the cell cycle at the G0/G1 stage. These findings match the results of Baskabadi et al.’s study24.

Our results indicate that NPs exerted a more pronounced influence on apoptosis-related genes than on genes linked to carcinogenesis. This pattern is likely related to differences in regulatory mechanisms, NPs concentration and exposure time, physicochemical features, and variations in how different cell lines respond. In addition, differences in cellular uptake (Differences in cellular sensitivity) and intracellular handling of NPs may also play a role in the observed gene expression changes.The expression of genes effective in carcinogenesis (MMP-9 and Bcl-2) is higher than genes effective in apoptosis in HT-29 cell line35,36.

ATR and ATM are known to phosphorylate CHK1 and CHK2. When the levels of these genes increase, the cell cycle checkpoint response (CHK1 and CHK2 ) is activated, which causes the cell cycle to stop, DNA to be repaired, and in some cases, the cell to die27,28. These studies have shown that the DNA Damage Response (DDR) is important and can be a new target for cancer treatment5.

The toxicity of NPs arises from their interaction with the body’s cells. NPs that are smaller than 10 nm can easily pass the cell membrane and cause damage23. Because of these interactions, ZnO NPs enter the cell, disrupt the plasma membrane, and weaken the cell wall22,36.

The toxicity is related to the formation of O2–, OH–, and H2O2. Only H2O2 can pass through the cell to react with lipids, proteins, and DNA, which can induce cell death11. Since cancer cells have more active mitochondria during respiration than normal cells do, ZnO NPs find it easy to target them completely37,38. Apoptosis, or programmed cell death, has been explored as a method to inhibit cancer growth, with a primary focus on creating cancer treatments that target the Bcl-2 family of proteins28. Research indicates that utilizing DNA damage response pathways in cancer treatment could be a promising approach for therapeutic intervention. As a result, it is crucial to maintain adequate levels of antioxidants in the body to effectively counteract the harmful effects of oxidants and ensure a balanced cellular environment, which can help prevent cancer38,39. Our flow cytometry and real-time PCR results confirmed that NPs exert toxic effects in the HT-29 cells and lead to apoptosis. Our results suggest that further research should focus on the anti-cancer potential of metal NPs to better understand their possible applications in medicine.

Conclusions

In our study, ZnO NPs were synthesized for the first time via the Amycolatopsis roodepoortensis strain EA7. Although this group of actinomycetes exhibits significant chemical diversity, their applications have been limited. The physical characterization of the synthesized ZnO NPs were confirmed, and cytotoxicity was observed in the HT-29 and HEK-293 cell lines. The synthesized NPs displayed notable antibacterial effects against the tested bacteria. In real-time PCR analysis, the expression levels of apoptosis-related genes were found to be elevated in the HT-29 cell line compared with those in the control sample, suggesting the induction of apoptosis. According to the strong antibacterial and anticancer activities of the ZnO NPs, these findings support further development and research into the application of these ZnO NPs for the preparation of anticancer agents. These NPs have anticancer drug potential but cannot be used directly as drugs. In other words, they can be drug candidates. To be introduced as drugs, NPs optimization, appropriate NPs surface coating, invivo tests, systemic toxicity, and tissue distribution are necessary.
