In situ generation of proinflammatory CAR macrophages via mRNA-TLR agonist co-delivery for triple-negative breast cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Jing-E Zhou, Yiyan Hu, Yajuan Chen, Jiahui Liu, Yujie Gao, Xueqin Dai, Haiyan Wang, Lei Yu, Nan Xu, Ji Xiao, Zhiqiang Yan, Lei Sun, Fubing Li, Ceshi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChimeric antigen receptor (CAR) macrophage therapy shows significant potential for solid tumors owing to the intrinsic tumor infiltration and phagocytic capacity of macrophages. However, its clinical translation is limited by macrophage phenotypic plasticity within the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and the complexity of ex vivo cell manufacturing. It is essential to develop techniques that enable macrophages to be activated specifically by antigens while sustaining their proinflammatory activity in vivo.MethodsHere, we report a mannose-modified lipid nanoparticle (LNP) platform for the co-delivery of CAR-encoding messenger RNA (mRNA) and the Toll-like receptor (TLR) 7/8 agonist resiquimod (R848), enabling in situ generation of proinflammatory CAR macrophages. In vitro, we assessed macrophage-preferential uptake, CAR expression efficiency, TLR7/8 agonist-mediated macrophage polarization, and immune activation. In vivo efficacy was assessed in syngeneic and humanized mouse models of triple-negative breast cancer, including postoperative recurrence and lung metastasis models.ResultsSystemic administration of M-LNP/CAR+R848 induced robust CAR expression in tumor-associated macrophages and promoted sustained M1 polarization. Engineered macrophages exhibited enhanced antigen-specific phagocytic activity and tumor cell clearance, and promoted CD8+ T cell proliferation and NK cell infiltration, thus coordinating innate and adaptive immune responses. Functional macrophage depletion experiments demonstrated that tumor control was dependent on macrophages. In vivo treatment significantly reduced the growth of primary tumors, prevented postoperative recurrence, and prolonged survival in mice with lung metastases in both syngeneic and humanized models.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that M-LNPs enabling co-delivery of mRNA and an innate immune agonist enable in situ generation of proinflammatory CAR macrophages and induce durable antitumor immunity. This controllable and non-integrative strategy allows tunable immune activation, provides a flexible platform for CAR macrophage-based immunotherapy in triple-negative breast cancer.

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has transformed the treatment landscape of hematologic malignancies.1–3 However, its therapeutic efficacy in solid tumors remains limited by insufficient tumor infiltration, antigen heterogeneity, and considerable toxicities.4 Macrophages are abundant innate immune cells that naturally infiltrate solid tumors, exhibit intrinsic phagocytic capacity, and function as professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs). These properties have prompted the development of CAR-macrophage (CAR-M) strategies, including in situ CAR-M programming approaches designed to circumvent the limitations of ex vivo cell manufacturing, including plasmid DNA/messenger RNA (mRNA)-based CAR delivery treatment.5–14 Yet, within the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), proinflammatory M1-like macrophages readily revert to tumor-promoting M2-like phenotypes, thereby restricting durable antitumor activity.7 8 15 16 Consequently, effective in situ CAR-M therapy requires not only antigen-specific activation but also coordinated modulation of tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) polarization to maintain a proinflammatory functional state.

To overcome this challenge, various strategies have been explored to reprogram TAMs, such as immune checkpoint blockade or inhibition of the CD47–SIRPα “don’t eat me” pathway.17–20 However, prolonged survival of macrophages in the TME may contribute to their passive reprogramming into a non-phagocytic phenotype with elevated SIRPα expression, thereby limiting the effectiveness of inhibitory receptor blockade. Therefore, regulating macrophage plasticity to reprogram TAMs toward an M1 phenotype and reverse the immunosuppressive TME is critical for improving the efficacy of CAR-M therapy. Nevertheless, the use of CAR-M therapy alone is inadequate to overcome the immunosuppressive effects of TAMs, indicating the importance of combined strategies to enhance the antitumor capabilities of M1 macrophages. This issue is particularly relevant in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), one of the most immunologically active subtypes of breast cancer, which is characterized by extensive TAM infiltration and a profoundly suppressive TME associated with poor clinical outcomes.21

The Toll-like receptor (TLR) family is crucial for immune control and is widely expressed in several immune cell types. Activation of TLR pathways promotes M1-like polarization, induces proinflammatory cytokine production, and coordination of innate and adaptive immune responses.22–27 Among the various TLR agonists, resiquimod (R848), a small-molecule agonist that activates TLR7/TLR8, which are innate immune receptors expressed on macrophages. However, Free R848 is poorly suited for systemic delivery because of rapid systemic distribution, limited tumor-localized exposure and dose-limiting inflammatory toxicity. To improve its compatibility with lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-based co-delivery, we conjugated R848 to cholesterol to generate a lipid-compatible prodrug-like component. Cholesterol conjugation increased the lipophilicity and lipid-phase affinity of R848, thereby promoting its stable incorporation into LNPs and enabling co-delivery with CAR-encoding mRNA.

mRNA, eliminates the risk of insertional mutagenesis and has consequently gained prominence as a therapeutic platform across a wide range of clinical applications. It has been shown that mRNA enables transient,28 non-integrative29 and dose-adjustable immune-cell programming, with CAR expression typically persisting for several days depending on the mRNA design,28 30–32 delivery system and target cell type. This transient expression profile may reduce the risk of sustained immune activation and provides a pharmacologically controllable strategy for macrophage engineering. On that, mRNA without any protection is inherently unstable and rapidly degraded by nucleases, making in situ CAR-M generation challenging.

To address these limitations, we designed a macrophage-targeted LNP platform capable of co-delivering CAR-encoding mRNA and the TLR7/8 agonist R848. Intravenous administration enables in situ generation of CAR-Ms while simultaneously enforcing M1 polarization, thereby synergistically augmenting antitumor immunity. Mannose-functionalized LNPs preferentially accumulate in macrophages following systemic administration.13 33–37 We further selected the CAR antigen, trophoblast cell-surface antigen 2 (Trop2), which is a clinically validated target that is high in expression in TNBC as well as multiple epithelial cancers.38–40 We demonstrate that this combinatorial approach promotes antigen-specific phagocytosis, reprograms TAMs toward an M1-like phenotype, and remodels the immunosuppressive TME. Together, these findings establish a modular and translatable framework for in vivo macrophage engineering in solid tumor immunotherapy.

Results

Mannose-modified lipid nanoparticles promote preferential macrophage uptake and efficient mRNA delivery

Preparation and characterization of mannose-modified LNPs

To enable in situ macrophage programming, we first engineered mannose-modified LNPs (M-LNPs) designed for preferential macrophage uptake and efficient mRNA delivery. Cholesterol-R848 was synthesized using a straightforward reaction between amino and carboxyl groups (figure 1A). Its molecular structure was verified by 1H-NMR analysis (online supplemental figure S1A), and its purity, determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (online supplemental figure S1B), was approximately 85%. Subsequently, LNPs encapsulating GFP-mRNA were generated via rapid mixing of ethanol and aqueous phases using a microfluidic system (figure 1B), and their formulation was further optimized. As shown in figure 1B and online supplemental table S1, the composition consisting of 45% ionizable lipid, 38.5% cholesterol-R848, 1.5% PEGylated lipid, 10% 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC), and 2% 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-polyethylene glycol-Mannose (DSPE-PEG-Mannose) exhibited the most favorable encapsulation efficiency and GFP expression level. Therefore, we used this formulation for subsequent experiments.

As shown in figure 1C,D, the LNPs exhibited a spherical morphology with an average diameter of 108 nm. The mRNA encapsulation efficiency reached approximately 97%, and the particles exhibited a slightly positive surface charge. Encapsulation efficiencies were 97.2±3.1% for mRNA and 33.5±4.1% for R848. In addition, no significant cytotoxic effects of LNPs were observed at concentrations treated with increasing doses of LNPs (online supplemental figure S2).

To induce macrophage reprogramming, CAR mRNA and Chol-R848 were coformulated into mannose-functionalized LNPs. Mannose modification enhanced reporter mRNA expression in macrophages in vitro (online supplemental figure S3A). After repeated intravenous administration, CAR expression was preferentially detected in peripheral blood CD45+CD11b+F4/80+ macrophage-lineage myeloid cells, whereas CAR positivity in CD45+CD3+ T cells remained low (online supplemental figure S3B–D). To further define the cellular distribution of M-LNPs after systemic delivery, we profiled DiD-labeled LNP uptake and functional CAR expression across major immune-cell populations in the tumor, liver and spleen. Within the TME, M-LNPs were preferentially taken up by TAMs and mediated predominant surface CAR expression in this population (online supplemental figure S4A,B). Although LNP uptake was also detected in liver-resident Kupffer cells and dendritic cells (DCs), consistent with the hepatic clearance of systemically administered LNPs, CAR expression in these off-tumor myeloid populations remained lower than that observed in TAMs (online supplemental figure S4C–F). Thus, mannose modification confers preferential macrophage uptake and functional mRNA delivery within the TME.

We next constructed Trop2-specific CAR mRNA to generate engineered macrophages. LNPs were incubated with murine bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) and phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA)-induced THP-1 cells for 48 hours. Based on transfection kinetics (figure 1E,F), we assessed transfection efficiency at 24 hours. As shown in figure 1G–1H, LNP-mediated transfection achieved efficiencies of 13.3±2.6% in BMDMs and 19.7±3.2% in THP-1 cells. Because innate immune activation may affect mRNA-driven protein expression, we examined whether R848 co-delivery altered CAR expression kinetics. BMDMs were treated with M-LNP/CAR or M-LNP/CAR+R848 at matched CAR mRNA doses, and surface CAR expression was measured at 12, 24 and 48 hours. R848 did not significantly reduce the frequency of CAR+ macrophages or alter CAR expression kinetics, indicating that R848 co-delivery did not measurably impair productive CAR mRNA expression under these conditions (online supplemental figure S5A,B). To further evaluate mRNA delivery, internalization assays were performed in both cell types (figure 1J). Moreover, DiO-labeled M-LNP/CAR+R848 was initially localized within lysosomes at 1 hour but progressively redistributed into the cytoplasm by 4 hours (figure 1I), indicating that LNPs facilitated lysosomal escape. Together, these results demonstrate that mannose modification confers preferential macrophage uptake and enables efficient intracellular delivery of CAR mRNA.

LNP-mediated generation of CAR-M1 and effector activity evaluation in vitro

Phagocytosis by CAR-macrophages in vitro

Having established efficient macrophage targeting, we next investigated whether M-LNP-mediated co-delivery of CAR mRNA and the TLR7/8 agonist R848 could functionally reprogram macrophages in vitro. To evaluate the Trop2-specific phagocytosis, we established a 4T1-Trop2 cells with high Trop2 expression (online supplemental figure S6). Macrophages were identified by anti-mouse F4/80-BV421 antibody, and tumor cells were stained with DiD. Phagocytosed tumor cells were quantified by flow cytometry as DiD+events within macrophages gate (gating strategy shown in online supplemental figure S7A). After 12 hours of co-culture, BMDMs treated with M-LNP/CAR exhibited significantly enhanced phagocytosis of 4T1-Trop2 cells compared with untransduced controls, whereas no differences were observed when Trop2-negative 4T1 cells were used (figure 2A). Similar results were obtained in a human system: carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE)-labeled THP-1 derived macrophages exhibited increased phagocytosis of DiD-labeled MDA-MB-231 cells after M-LNP/CAR treatment, while no enhancement was detected against Trop2-negative A549 cells (figure 2B, gating strategy shown in online supplemental figure S7B). To exclude non-specific effects of the CAR construct itself or R848-driven activation, we generated a non-targeting anti-CD19 CAR control using the same LNP formulation. CAR surface expression levels were comparable between Trop2-CAR and CD19-CAR groups (online supplemental figure S8A). In co-culture with Trop2+ tumor cells, M-LNP/CAR19+R848 did not increase phagocytosis (online supplemental figure S8B), confirming antigen-dependent activity.

The cytotoxic activity of CAR-Ms against Trop2 high-expressing tumor cells was evaluated using a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay. BMDMs treated with different LNP formulations served as effector cells, while 4T1-Trop2 cells were used as targets. After 24 hours of co-culture at different effector-to-target (E:T) ratios, the M-LNP/CAR+R848 group exhibited the highest LDH release, with cytotoxicity peaking at an E:T ratio of 6:1 (figure 2C). These results confirm the superior tumor-killing capacity of BMDMs engineered with M-LNP/CAR+R848.

To assess whether CAR-Ms could activate T-cell proliferation through antigen presentation, CFSE-labeled murine T cells were co-cultured with LNP-treated BMDMs and 4T1-Trop2 cells, and proliferation was quantified by flow cytometry on day 3. T-cell expansion was significantly higher in the M-LNP/CAR+R848 group compared with M-LNP/CAR, Cholesterol-R848, or untransduced controls (figure 2D). These results indicate that M-LNP/CAR+R848 treated macrophages not only enhance phagocytosis but also promote T-cell proliferation in vitro. To determine whether the T cells expanded in the BMDM and tumor cells co-culture system acquired functional antitumor activity, we performed a tumor rechallenge cytotoxicity assay. T cells were first primed by co-culture with LNP-treated BMDMs and 4T1-Trop2 cells, and the non-adherent T-cell fraction was then rechallenged with freshly prepared CFSE-labeled 4T1-Trop2 cells. Tumor-cell death was quantified as the percentage of PI+ cells within the CFSE+ tumor-cell gate. T cells primed in the M-LNP/CAR and M-LNP/CAR+R848 groups induced stronger tumor-cell killing than naïve T cells or T cells from phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)-treated co-cultures, with the M-LNP/CAR+R848 group showing a further trend toward enhanced cytotoxicity (p=0.06; online supplemental figure S9A–C).

We next investigated whether incorporation of R848 into LNPs further potentiated CAR-M phagocytic activity in human monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs). As shown in figure 2E, human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from healthy donors and differentiated into MDMs using granulocyte colony-stimulating factor. Following treatment with M-LNP/CAR+R848, CAR expression was detected in approximately 20% of the MDM population (figure 2G). This result indicates that the in vitro transfection efficiency of the mRNA-loaded nanoparticles was sufficient to support subsequent functional and mechanistic studies. Using dynamic live-cell imaging within 16 hours, we further confirmed that M-LNP/CAR+R848-programmed macrophages efficiently engulfed Trop2-overexpressing tumor cells, whereas untransduced macrophages displayed minimal activity (figure 2F; online supplemental videos 1–4). Overall, these results indicate that Trop2-specific CAR expression effectively redirected macrophage phagocytosis toward Trop2 high expression TNBC cells, which protects normal cells without upregulating Trop2 expression from damage and ensures the efficacy and safety of CAR-Ms therapy.

R848 induces M1 polarization of macrophages

Based on their effects on tumors, macrophages can be divided into tumor-promoting M2 and tumor-suppressing M1 types. We found that the groups treated with M-LNP/CAR+R848 showed higher protein levels of the iNOS (M1 marker) and lower protein levels of the Arg-1 (M2 marker) in BMDMs, as determined by Western blot after treatment (figure 3A). Flow cytometry showed that treated macrophages expressed higher levels of the M1 marker CD80 and lower levels of the M2 marker CD206 (figure 3B,C). We then performed RNA-seq on macrophages treated with UTD and M-LNP/CAR+R848. Differential gene expression patterns between UTD and M-LNP/CAR+R848 treated cells were revealed by RNA-Seq. With the combination of CAR-M and a TLR agonist, there was an increase in the expression of genes for classically activated (M1) macrophages and a decrease in the expression of genes for alternatively activated (M2) macrophages (figure 3D,E). We also observed activation of the TLR7/8 pathway in the M-LNP/CAR+R848 treated group, demonstrating that TLR activation leads to macrophage polarization toward the M1 type by inducing the expression of proinflammatory factors. To access the molecular mechanisms underlying macrophage activation, we examined intracellular signaling pathways following transduction with M-LNP/CAR and M-LNP/CAR+R848. The p-STAT1 and total STAT1 protein levels were significantly up-regulated in the M-LNP/CAR and M-LNP/CAR+R848 groups by Western blot analysis, indicating the activation of the classical M1 polarization pathway. Notably, treatment with M-LNP/CAR+R848 further induced robust activation of the NF-κB pathway, consistent with stimulation of the TLR7/8 signaling cascade by R848 (figure 3F). These results suggest that the combined delivery of CAR mRNA and TLR agonist effectively activates proinflammatory signaling pathways, thereby driving macrophage polarization toward an antitumor M1 phenotype.

In situ macrophage programming by M-LNP/CAR+R848 remodels the tumor microenvironment and suppresses TNBC progression

In vivo generation of CAR-macrophages and antitumor efficacy

We next evaluated whether systemic administration of M-LNP/CAR+R848 could achieve in vivo macrophage programming and exert therapeutic effects in 4T1-Trop2 murine models of TNBC. 7 days after tumor inoculation, mice were randomly divided into four groups. In vivo CAR-M efficacy was evaluated in groups treated with LNP vehicle (Control, loaded with GFP-encoding mRNA), M-LNP/CAR, M-LNP/CAR+R848, or Chol-R848 (figure 4A). 24 hours after intravenous administration of DiR-labeled M-LNP/mRNA+R848, ex vivo fluorescence imaging revealed DiR fluorescence signals within the tumors, confirming effective intratumoral delivery and retention of LNPs (online supplemental figure S10A,B). As shown in figure 4B,C, both M-LNP/CAR and M-LNP/CAR+R848 reduced tumor burden to varying degrees, with mice in the M-LNP/CAR+R848 group exhibiting the lowest tumor burden at day 20 (figure 4B–D). Notably, one mouse in the M-LNP/CAR+R848 group showed complete tumor regression following treatment (figure 4C).

Next, we further investigated the antitumor mechanism of M-LNP/CAR+R848. PBMCs were harvested from mice receiving various treatments on days 2, 5, 9, and 16 for flow cytometric analysis. CAR expression in peripheral blood CD45+CD11b+F4/80+ macrophage-lineage myeloid cells remained detectable for at least 14 days after systemic administration (figure 4E). There was a significant increase in mature DCs (CD80+CD86+CD11c+ within CD45+ cells) in the M-LNP/CAR+R848 group compared with other groups, indicating that R848 promotes the maturation of DCs (figure 4F). Additionally, as professional APCs, activated macrophages show significant tumor phagocytic activity, presenting antigens associated with tumors to T cells and activating them. Following four intravenous administrations of M-LNP/CAR+R848, the percentage of CD8+cytotoxic T lymphocytes in the blood increased to approximately 31.2%, as expected (figure 4H). Importantly, mice treated with M-LNP/CAR+R848 also exhibited higher levels of effector memory CD8+ T cells (TEM) compared with both control and Chol-R848 groups (figure 4I). In contrast, no significant differences were observed in central memory CD8+ T cells (TCM) across groups (figure 4J).

We next analyzed LNP-induced polarization of macrophages in vivo. PBMCs were harvested from mice receiving various treatments on days 5, 10, and 15 for flow cytometric analysis. The populations of M1 macrophages increased over time in M-LNP/CAR and M-LNP/CAR+R848 treatment groups (gating strategy shown in online supplemental figure S11). compared with the Control group, the M1/M2 macrophage ratio in the M-LNP/CAR+R848 group significantly increased to approximately 5 (figure 4G). Proinflammatory cytokines, including interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), interleukin (IL)-10, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), were found to be significantly increased at the tumor site (figure 4L–O). This suggests that M-LNP/CAR+R848 exhibits strong immunomodulatory effects, including the potential to polarize macrophages into the M1 phenotype.

We further examined the pharmacokinetics of R848 in vivo. Due to its low molecular weight (314.4 Da), it is rapidly distributed and systemically cleared following injection. To compare its metabolic profile with that of Chol-R848-loaded LNPs (M-LNP/CAR+R848), serum R848 concentrations were quantified by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) after administration of 15 µg R848 per mouse, a dose previously shown to be well tolerated without adverse effects. As shown in figure 4K, free R848 quickly peaked at around 100 ng/mL and then became almost undetectable. In contrast, serum R848 concentrations in the M-LNP/CAR+R848 group remained significantly lower, below 30 ng/mL, but persisted for a longer duration. These results indicate that R848 loaded in LNP had a sustained and controlled release rather than a rapid burst.

Remodeling of tumor immune microenvironment

To investigate the effects of LNPs on the composition of the tumor-infiltrating immune cells, mice receiving various treatments were sacrificed on day 10, and tumor tissues were analyzed by flow cytometry. As shown in figure 5A, approximately 70% of TAMs expressed CAR Trop2 on day 10 after administration (gating strategy shown in online supplemental figure S12), and Chol-R848 encapsulated in LNPs did not impact CAR expression. To further compare CAR expression between peripheral and tumor-resident myeloid populations, we analyzed CAR expression kinetics in peripheral blood CD45+CD11b+F4/80+ macrophage-lineage myeloid cells and TAMs. CAR expression was preferentially enriched and more sustained in TAMs, whereas peripheral blood myeloid cells showed lower and more transient CAR positivity over the examined time course (online supplemental figure S13). Notably, macrophages from the M-LNP/CAR+R848 group predominantly displayed an M1 phenotype (figure 5B,C, gating strategy shown in online supplemental figure S14). The M1/M2 ratio increased to 6.0, compared with 0.87 in the PBS group, 2.58 in the M-LNP/CAR group, and 2.55 in the Cholesterol-R848 group. While Chol-R848 alone led to a moderate rise in the M1/M2 ratio, it did not significantly elevate the absolute frequency of CD80+ M1-like TAMs, suggesting that R848-induced TLR7/8 activation alone only partially remodeled TAMs. Immunofluorescence staining of tumor tissue, labeling macrophages and CD80, CD206, also confirmed the predominance of M1 macrophages (online supplemental figure S15A,B).

Furthermore, the enhanced T-cell activity was also evident. By measuring the number of CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues, we found that the CD8+ T cell count in the M-LNP/CAR+R848 group was significantly higher than that in the control group (figure 5D, online supplemental figure S15C), while the number of Treg cells was significantly reduced (figure 5E). The proportion of granzyme B+ and perforin+ cells among CD8+ T cells increased significantly in the M-LNP/CAR+R848 group (online supplemental figure S16). The proportion of TEM cells in the spleen did not show a significant increase (figure 5F), but the proportion of CD8+ TCM cells was significantly increased (figure 5G). Consistently, splenocytes isolated from 4T1-Trop2 tumor-bearing mice after repeated M-LNP/CAR+R848 treatment displayed significantly increased killing of CFSE-labeled 4T1-Trop2 cells on ex vivo rechallenge (online supplemental figure S9D–F). These results demonstrate that the M-LNP/CAR+R848 treatment not only stimulated the antitumor activity of macrophages but also improved the performance of adaptive immune cells, thus enhancing the overall antitumor effect.

To evaluate the activation of innate immunity, natural killer (NK) cells (CD3−NK1.1+ within CD45+ cells) were analyzed in tumors by flow cytometry. A significant increase in NK cell numbers was observed in the M-LNP/CAR+R848 group, whereas no appreciable change was detected in the control group and Chol-R848 group (figure 5H). Overall, CAR-M1 macrophages were enriched at the tumor site, while CD8+ T cells and NK cells accumulated intratumorally after M-LNP/CAR+R848 administration. At the same time, infiltration of immunosuppressive cells was markedly reduced. These coordinated immune responses elicited a potent antitumor effect that effectively inhibited TNBC progression.

To access the specificity and safety of our CAR toward macrophages in vivo, flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that macrophages were the primary immune cell population infiltrating tumors (online supplemental figure S17A). Following M-LNP/CAR+R848 administration, CAR was mostly expressed in TAMs, while expression in other immune cells such as T, B, NK, or neutrophil cells was negligible (online supplemental figure S17B,C).

We next investigated the contribution of macrophages and adaptive immune cells to the observed antitumor effects. Depletion of macrophages markedly impaired therapeutic efficacy, indicating the key role of CAR-engineered macrophages in the suppression of tumors (online supplemental figure S18). In NSG mice deficient in T, B, and NK cells, M-LNP treatment resulted in substantially reduced tumor control compared with immunocompetent mice, suggesting that adaptive immunity is crucial for optimal therapeutic outcomes (online supplemental figure S19).

Therapeutic effects on lung metastasis

Lung metastasis is a common occurrence in breast cancer owing to its abundant vascularization, contributing to the high mortality rate associated with tumor metastasis.

To evaluate therapeutic efficacy in metastatic disease, a lung metastasis model was established via intravenous injection of 4T1-Trop2 cells. 7 days after tumor inoculation, H&E staining of lung tissue revealed the establishment of pulmonary metastatic lesions (online supplemental figure S20). Mice were subsequently randomized into four groups and treated with PBS, M-LNP/CAR, M-LNP/CAR+R848 and Chol-R848, respectively (figure 5I). Flow cytometry confirmed CAR expression in lung-resident macrophages following M-LNP/CAR+R848 administration (figure 5J). Histological analysis revealed that M-LNP/CAR+R848 treatment not only significantly extended survival in tumor-bearing mice (figure 5K) but also produced the greatest reduction in lung metastatic burden compared with the other groups (figure 5L,M). These data suggest that combining CAR-M therapy with TLR agonists leads to strong antitumor activity, effectively inhibiting both primary tumor growth and metastatic progression.

Antitumor efficacy and safety of in situ generated CAR-macrophages in a humanized TNBC model

To evaluate the clinical efficacy of our macrophage-engineered therapies, we employed a humanized mouse model genetically modified to express human granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor and IL-3, facilitating the engraftment of human myeloid and T cells. Following the transplantation of human hematopoietic stem cells into irradiated NCG-M mice that lack NK, T, and B cells, those mice with over 40% huCD45+ cells in their peripheral blood by the 7th week were selected. A humanized model of TNBC was developed by week 12 using fully differentiated macrophages (figure 6A). In the humanized TNBC model, M-LNP/CAR+R848 significantly inhibited tumor growth compared with the control group and produced the lowest mean tumor burden among the treatment groups (figure 6B–D). We also found CAR Trop2 expression on human macrophages in the PBMCs of humanized mice (figure 6E). Compared with other groups, the M-LNP/CAR+R848 group exhibited a notable rise in human CD8+ T cells (figure 6F) and a considerable reduction in Ki67-positive cells (figure 6G). Our results indicate that M-LNP/CAR+R848 treatment has a favorable antitumor effect. Following treatment, we excised the tumors surgically and observed that the mice treated with M-LNP/CAR+R848 had no recurrence 20 days post-tumor removal (figure 6H). Furthermore, the quantity of human CD8+T and TEM cells in the mice without recurrence was markedly greater than that in the mice with recurrence (figure 6I,J). These findings demonstrate the potential of our treatment in clinically relevant settings, highlighting its potential for future clinical applications.

In addition, we determined the in vivo biosafety of LNPs administered intravenously. No evident systemic toxicity was detected in the serum liver and kidney function levels or major organ sections of healthy mice with different treatments, demonstrating the feasibility of repeated LNP administration (online supplemental figure S21). These findings clearly show that reprogramming macrophages in situ with Trop2-specific CAR mRNA and R848 boosts strong innate and adaptive immune responses against TNBC without overt systemic toxicity under the tested dosing regimen.

Discussion

TNBC, particularly its aggressive subtypes, presents formidable challenges for therapeutic intervention and long-term disease control. Modulating immunosuppressed TAMs to remodel the TME may be a complementary immunotherapeutic approach. Recent advances in in situ immune cell engineering offer a new alternative to traditional ex vivo cell production. Among available delivery platforms, LNPs have become the most widely adopted system for nucleic acid therapeutics owing to their favorable physicochemical properties and established clinical track record. In this study, we describe a combinatorial strategy that integrates in situ CAR expression with innate immune reprogramming to enhance CAR macrophage-based immunotherapy. The M-LNP platform was designed to overcome several barriers associated with macrophage engineering. Mannose-modified nanoparticles enhance macrophage-preferential uptake, whereas the optimized ionizable lipid formulation promotes mRNA encapsulation and lysosomal escape. Coordinated delivery of CAR-encoding mRNA and localized activation of innate immunity promote APC maturation and polarize TAMs toward a proinflammatory, M1-like phenotype. These allow antigen-specific phagocytosis and help macrophages keep their inflammatory function in preclinical TNBC models.

In recent years, a variety of strategies have been explored to modulate TAMs, including the incorporation of activating intracellular domains or cytokine-encoding sequences into CAR constructs to reinforce proinflammatory phenotypes.41 42 However, activation signals encoded as sequences are limited in their ability to be controlled. Once they are genetically encoded, they remain active and cannot easily be adjusted to suit changing environments. Excessive or sustained activation of innate signaling pathways has been reported to impair CAR functionality and cellular persistence. We employed a strategy of co-delivering CAR mRNA and TLR agonists to achieve a transient activation of the TLR system concurrent with CAR expression. R848 was selected because TLR7/8 activation directly promotes macrophage and dendritic-cell activation, while cholesterol conjugation improves its compatibility with LNP formulation, enabling coordinated co-delivery with CAR mRNA and reducing the limitations associated with systemic free R848 exposure. This approach uses pre-existing and conserved immunological pathways to attain coordinated functionality and achieves spatiotemporal controllability via targeted nanoparticle delivery. Transient mRNA-mediated CAR expression allows pharmacological control; repeated dosing enables sustained therapeutic activity.

Cell type-specific targeting remains a fundamental challenge for LNP-based in situ cell engineering. In this study, mannose receptor-mediated targeting was employed to enhance macrophage-preferential delivery. Although a subset of LNPs was internalized by non-macrophage immune cells, functional CAR expression was preferentially enriched in TAMs relative to non-macrophage immune-cell populations. Similarly, CAR expression in non-immune cells was minimal. Given the high enrichment of Trop2 in TNBC, such low-level off-target expression did not compromise antigen specificity.

In the 4T1-Trop2 tumor model, splenic CD8+ TCM cells were increased after M-LNP/CAR+R848 treatment, whereas TEM cells did not show a significant increase. This pattern is consistent with the establishment of a systemic memory reservoir after tumor control. By contrast, in the humanized postoperative recurrence model, mice without recurrence showed a higher proportion of effector memory T cells, consistent with a more differentiated memory response associated with peripheral immune surveillance after tumor resection. These findings suggest that M-LNP/CAR+R848 may support distinct stages of antitumor T-cell memory.

This work still has a few limitations to consider. First, CAR expression mediated by mRNA delivery is inherently transient, and repeated administration may be required to sustain therapeutic activity. Second, even though the TLR7/8 agonist preferentially enriched immune activation within macrophage-rich tumor compartments, the balance of immune activation versus safety must be optimized, especially under repeated dosing. Nevertheless, hepatic accumulation remains a key translational consideration for systemically administered LNPs, and further pharmacokinetic and long-term toxicological studies will be required to fully define the safety window of M-LNP/CAR+R848.

Despite these limitations, the modular design of the platform provides substantial flexibility. The LNP formulation can be readily adapted to different CAR targets or immunomodulatory cargos, allowing customization for distinct tumor types and immune microenvironments. While this study focuses on TLR-based stimulation, other immunostimulatory agents may offer complementary or synergistic effects. This platform offers a versatile framework to screen and optimize adjuvant combinations.

In conclusion, we present an innovative strategy that integrates macrophage editing with immune adjuvant therapy, offering a promising approach for the treatment of TNBC. Specifically targeting and reprogramming TAMs markedly suppressed breast tumor growth and pulmonary metastasis in tumor-bearing mice. This strategy directly overcomes macrophage-driven immune dysregulation and holds potential to improve outcomes for patients with TNBC.

Methods

Synthesis and characterization of cholesterol-R848

R848 was solubilized in 2 mL of anhydrous dichloromethane (DCM), then including 1.1 molar equivalents of cholesterol hemisuccinate, 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP), and 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC). The amalgamation was agitated at ambient temperature for 24 hours. The crude product was purified by column chromatography with a 10% methanol: DCM gradient, resulting in the Chol-R848 agonist amphiphile as a white solid. The structure of Chol-R848 was characterized by 1H-NMR (online supplemental figure S1A). The purity testing of the cholesterol-R848 was determined by HPLC (online supplemental figure S1B).

mRNA construct

The Trop2-specific CAR construct consisted of a CD8α leader sequence, an anti-Trop2 single-chain variable fragment, a CD8α hinge region, a CD8 transmembrane domain, and an intracellular CD3ζ signaling domain. A DNA template encoding the Trop2-specific CAR followed by a poly(A) sequence was cloned downstream of a T7 promoter. The plasmid was linearized with the restriction enzyme Mlu I and purified before in vitro transcription. CAR mRNA synthesis was performed by Kaituo Biotechnology (Suzhou). Briefly, CAR mRNA was synthesized using a T7 High Yield RNA Transcription Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by Cap 1 capping. The resulting Trop2-specific CAR mRNA was purified and its integrity was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis.

Formula optimization of LNP

GFP-mRNA and chol-R848 encapsulated in LNPs was created using various formulas listed in online supplemental table S1 through a microfluidic device. Candidate formulations were selected based on the following criteria: a hydrodynamic diameter below 200 nm, PDI no greater than 0.2, a slightly positive surface charge, a clear and transparent appearance without visible precipitation after standing, and effective in vitro transfection of primary macrophages. The formulation yielding the highest transfection efficiency was selected on the basis of this performance for follow studies.

Construction and characterization of M-LNP/CAR+R848

Ionizable lipid, cholesterol-R848, DMG-PEG2000, DSPC, and DSPE-PEG-MAN (purchased from Xi’an RuiXi Biotechnology) were dissolved in 1 mL of ethanol according to a molar ratio of 45:38.5:1.5:10:2 to obtain the mixed lipid solution. Then CAR mRNA (1 mg/mL) was diluted in sodium citrate buffer at pH=4 to obtain the mRNA mix solution. Specifically, mRNA mix solution and mixed lipid solution were combined in a microfluidic device. The mass ratio between the mRNA in the mRNA mixture and the ionizable lipid in the lipid solution was maintained at 1:14.5. The amount of mRNA present in each formulation of LNPs was quantified by absorbance and RiboGreen fluorescence assays. The encapsulation efficiency (EE) was calculated as follows: EE (%) = (m₂ − m₁) / m₂ × 100%.

LNPs size were determined by dynamic light scattering with a Zetasizer 3000 (Malvern Instruments, U.K.) after dilution in PBS. NP morphology was determined by transmission electron microscopy using a microscope.

R848 drug loading

R848 in LNPs was quantified via HPLC. R848-loaded LNP samples, both with and without burst release, were diluted in acetonitrile, and analyzed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity HPLC system. The column temperature was established at 25°C, the flow rate was 0.5 mL/min, and the mobile phase comprised a 70% methanol solution. The peak region at 320 nm was quantified using HPLC to assess the R848 concentration.

Quantification of released R848 in vivo

R848 concentrations in murine serum were quantified using the LC-MS/MS technique. The mass spectrometer operated in multiple reaction monitoring mode using positive electrospray ionization. A C18 analytical column (50 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.6 µm) was employed for determination. The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min with a gradient of 0.1% formic acid in aqueous solution. A 100 µL plasma sample was combined with 50 µL of an internal standard (50 ng/mL midazolam-d4), followed by the addition of 100 µL of 0.5 M NaOH to achieve a basic pH.

Lysosomal escape experiments of LNPs

BMDMs (2.5×105 cells) were plated on sterile glass-bottom culture dishes and incubated with Dio-labeled M-LNP/CAR+R848 for 1, 2, and 4 hours, respectively. Subsequently, the lysosomes were labeled with LysoTracker Red (100 nM) and the nuclei with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (10 µg/mL). Images were acquired using confocal microscopy.

In vitro macrophage transfection

BMDM cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 100,000 cells per well and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. The formulated LNPs were incubated with macrophages for 48 hours. CAR expression was detected using biotinylated human Trop2 protein (ACRO Biosystems) followed by streptavidin-PE (ACRO Biosystems).

CAR-macrophage phagocytosis and cytotoxicity analysis in vitro

BMDM cells (1×105) or THP-1 cells were inoculated on a 24-well plate and cultured for 24 hours, after which LNPs were introduced for a subsequent 24-hour incubation to transfect the macrophages. DiD-labeled tumor cells were subsequently introduced into the culture medium and cultured for 24 hours. Phagocytosis was evaluated via flow cytometry by determining the percentage of DiD+ cells within the macrophage gate.

BMDMs (2.5×105 cells per well) were seeded and treated with the indicated LNP formulations for 24 hours. After treatment, cells were washed and used directly as effector cells without CAR+ cell sorting. The effector population consisted of unsorted bulk LNP-treated macrophages. 4T1-Trop2 cells were used as target cells. LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit was used to measure the cytoplasmic LDH released into the medium by detecting the optical density of the supernatant at 490 nm.

Tumor rechallenge cytotoxicity assay

For the in vitro T-cell priming assay, BMDMs were treated with the indicated LNP formulations for 24 hours and then co-cultured with murine T cells and 4T1-Trop2 tumor cells for 48 hours. The non-adherent T-cell fraction was collected, washed, and re-seeded with freshly prepared CFSE-labeled 4T1-Trop2 cells at an E:T ratio of 10:1. After 48 hours, cells were harvested and stained with propidium iodide (PI). Tumor-cell death was quantified by flow cytometry as the percentage of PI+ cells within the CFSE+ tumor-cell population.

For the ex vivo splenocyte rechallenge assay, splenocytes were isolated from 4T1-Trop2 tumor-bearing mice after four rounds of treatment with the indicated LNP formulations using a mouse lymphocyte separation medium. Splenocytes were co-cultured with CFSE-labeled 4T1-Trop2 cells at an E:T ratio of 10:1 for 48 hours. Cells were then collected, stained with PI, and analyzed by flow cytometry. Cytotoxicity was calculated as the percentage of PI+ cells among CFSE+ tumor cells.

Biodistribution

DiR was incorporated into the ethanol-phase lipid mixture during LNP preparation to generate DiR-labeled M-LNPs. These DiR-labeled LNPs were administered intravenously to 4T1-Trop2 tumor-bearing mice to evaluate the ex vivo biodistribution of the LNP carrier. At 24 hours after systemic administration, mice were sacrificed and major organs and tumors were collected for IVIS fluorescence imaging. The DiR fluorescence signal was measured using an excitation wavelength of 750 nm and an emission wavelength of 780 nm with an IVIS Spectrum imaging system.

Triple negative breast cancer mouse models for antitumor efficacy study

All animal procedures received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Kunming Medical University (approval no. kmmu20230287). 5-week-old female BALB/c mice received an injection of 1×106 4T1-Trop2 cells into the fourth pair of mammary glands. Following tumor inoculation, when the tumors were palpable (approximately 15 mm3), the mice were randomized and divided into the following four groups: LNP vehicle (Control, n=5); M-LNP/CAR (n=5); M-LNP/CAR+R848 (n=5); LNP encapsulated Chol-R848 but did not contain CAR mRNA (n=5).

Quantification of CAR-M produced by LNP

PBMCs were separated using a density gradient separation solution, succeeded by red blood cell lysis to acquire leukocytes. CAR-M populations were examined via flow cytometry employing the following antibodies: APC-CY7-anti-mouse CD45, APC-anti-mouse CD11b, BV421-anti-mouse F4/80, PE/Dazzle-anti-mouse CD206, and FITC-anti-mouse CD80.

Cytokine assay

Cytokine synthesis by macrophages was evaluated using ELISA kits (IL-10, TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6; Multi Science). Tumor-bearing mice were administered the specified formulations. Mice serum was collected, and cytokine concentrations were measured following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Absorbance at 450 nm was quantified via an automated microplate reader, with values derived from standard curves.

Immunofluorescence analysis of tumor sections

Following a 20-day medication regimen, the mice in each cohort were euthanized, and their tumor tissues were harvested and scanned. F4/80 antibodies delineate macrophages (red), CD80 marks M1 macrophages (pink), and CD206 designates M2 macrophages. Cells that co-express F4/80 and CD80 were designated as M1 macrophages, whereas those co-expressing F4/80 and CD206 were designated as M2 macrophages.

Flow cytometry for detecting immune cells

The tumor-bearing mice were treated with LNPs after tumors were palpable. The blood samples were treated with 1 µL of each fluorescently tagged antibody listed below:

APC-Cy7 Rat Anti-Mouse CD45, PerCP-Cy5.5 Hamster Anti-Mouse CD3, APC Rat Anti-CD11b, BV421 Rat Anti-Mouse F4/80, BV605 Rat Anti-Mouse CD19, PE Rat Anti-Mouse Ly-6G, PE-Cy7 Mouse Anti-Mouse NK-1.1 for 20 min. Immune cells were subsequently detected by flow cytometry.

Establishment of humanized mouse tumor models

To establish the humanized mouse tumor models, hu-NSG-M mice (huCD34+NOD/ShiLtJGpt-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22Il15em1Cin(hIL15)Rosa26em1Cin(hCSF2&IL3&KITLG)/Gpt) were employed and maintained in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment within the animal facility. The mice were initially subjected to irradiation, followed by the injection of hematopoietic stem cells via the tail vein the subsequent day. 7 weeks post-transplantation, mice with above 40% huCD45+ cells in their peripheral blood were chosen for further studies. By week 12, when both macrophages and T cells had achieved full differentiation, MDA-MB-231 cells were directly injected into the mammary fat pads, then a humanized mouse model of TNBC with high Trop2 expression was successfully constructed. 1 week following tumor implantation, the various LNPs were administered.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses of raw data were performed using GraphPad Prism software. The statistical significance between two groups was analyzed by Student’s t-test, and statistical significance among multiple groups was analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Tumor growth curves were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple-comparisons test. The statistical significance of differences for survival analysis was analyzed by the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Significant differences are indicated as *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, and ****p<0.0001.
