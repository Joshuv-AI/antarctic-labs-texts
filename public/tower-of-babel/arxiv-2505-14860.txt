Optimization and the Topology of Spaces of Parseval Frames
Anthony Caine, Tom Needham, Clayton Shonkwiler

Abstract
A Parseval frame is a spanning set for a Hilbert space which satisfies the Parseval identity: a vector can be expressed as a linear combination of the frame whose coefficients are inner products with the frame vectors. There is considerable interest within the signal processing community in the structural properties of the space of finite-dimensional Parseval frames whose vectors all have the same norm, or which satisfy more general prescribed norm constraints. In this paper, we introduce a function on the space of arbitrary spanning sets that jointly measures the failure of a spanning set to satisfy both the Parseval identity and given norm constraints. We show that, despite its nonconvexity, this function has no spurious local minimizers, thereby extending the Benedetto–Fickus theorem to this non-compact setting. In particular, this shows that gradient descent converges to an equal norm Parseval frame when initialized within a dense open set in the associated matrix space. We then apply this result to study the topology of frame spaces. Using our Benedetto–Fickus-type result, we realize spaces of Parseval frames with prescribed norms as deformation retracts of simpler spaces, leading to explicit conditions which guarantee the vanishing of their homotopy groups. These conditions yield new path-connectedness results for spaces of real Parseval frames, generalizing the Frame Homotopy Theorem, which has seen significant interest in recent years.

Introduction

A
Parseval frame
for
\mathbb{K}^{d}
(with
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
) is a spanning set of vectors
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}
with the property that, when representing a vector
v\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
as a linear combination with respect to this collection, the coefficients can be taken to be (standard) inner products:

v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle v,f_{i}\rangle f_{i}.

(1)

This property (known as the
Parseval identity
) obviously holds for orthonormal bases, so that Parseval frames generalize the notion of orthonormal bases to allow for
n>d
; indeed, a Parseval frame is just an orthonormal basis when
n=d
.

The generalization from orthonormal bases to “overcomplete” spanning sets is motivated by the desire for robust representations of signals: considering
\mathbb{K}^{d}
as a (finite-dimensional approximation of a) space of signals, one can model measurements of a signal
v\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
as inner products with the frame vectors,
\langle v,f_{i}\rangle
. Property (
1
) then intuitively says that the process of reconstructing a signal from its measurements is entirely straightforward. Of course, (
1
) holds if the frame is an orthonormal basis (i.e., if
n=d
), but Parseval frames with
n\gg d
offer more accurate reconstruction when measurements are contaminated with noise or when some are lost in transmission
[
20
,
11
,
24
]
. This robustness is an important byproduct of the inherent redundancy in the measurement acquisition process.

One frequently considers Parseval frames with the additional constraint that the frame vectors all have the same norm. Such frames are referred to as
equal norm Parseval frames
. This is a natural restriction both from a practical perspective—this provides uniformity in the “power” of the respective measurements—and from a mathematical one—for instance, the space of equal norm frames is compact. Properties of Parseval frames are a prominent topic within the larger field of
frame theory
; see
[
45
]
for a recent textbook on the topic.

Fixing dimension parameters
d
and
n
, the collection of equal norm Parseval frames with this data forms a complicated algebraic variety
[
15
,
41
]
. The geometric and topological properties of this space have been thoroughly studied
[
10
,
38
,
39
,
9
,
8
,
31
]
, frequently with a view toward associated optimization problems. This paper extends the study of the space of equal norm Parseval frames (and related spaces) through techniques of Geometric Invariant Theory (GIT)
[
34
,
42
]
, which have been shown to be effective in this domain in the authors’ previous work
[
33
,
36
,
37
]
. We now explain more details about our main contributions and give an outline of the paper.

Extension of the Benedetto–Fickus theorem (
2.8
).

Consider the problem of constructing an equal norm Parseval frame: this is a nontrivial task, due to the complicated structure of the space of frames, an algebraic variety of high codimension in the ambient matrix space. One natural idea would be to start with a collection of equal norm vectors (easy to generate, uniformly at random) and to then evolve them towards a Parseval frame while maintaining the equal norm condition. This could be accomplished, say, via gradient descent with respect to some loss function measuring the failure of property (
1
) for a given collection. An immediate concern about the viability of this plan is that the gradient descent process could converge to a spurious local minimum which is not actually a Parseval frame. This is addressed by making a principled choice of loss function, as is shown in the celebrated
Benedetto–Fickus Theorem

[
4
]
, which serves as a main inspiration for the present paper. Roughly,
[
4
]
introduces a certain loss function (the
frame potential
) on the space of equal norm frames whose global minima are equal norm Parseval frames, and then shows that this loss function has no spurious local minima. This result has been generalized, refined, and extended in several recent articles
[
33
,
10
,
36
,
37
]
.

In this paper, we define a loss function on
\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
(a point in which is identified with a collection of
n
vectors in
\mathbb{K}^{d}
) that measures, roughly, how far an arbitrary collection of vectors is from being an equal norm Parseval frame. Although this loss function is non-convex, we show in
2.8
and
2.9
that it has no spurious local minimizers. Our result, in particular, implies that one can construct an equal norm Parseval frame by starting from a random collection of vectors and gradient descending via the loss function. This extension of the Benedetto–Fickus theorem is complicated by the lack of compactness in the domain of the loss function, and requires a different proof technique, based on ideas from GIT. In fact, our result applies more generally to the construction of Parseval frames with arbitrary prescribed norms, as was previously studied in
[
7
]
. We note that the construction of
d\times n
matrices with constant spectrum and prescribed column norms—which can be achieved by gradient descent, according to our result—has important applications in wireless communication
[
44
,
43
]
.

Topology of frame spaces (
4.5
).

We apply our Benedetto–Fickus-type theorem to describe the topology of spaces of Parseval frames with prescribed norms; in particular, we use
2.8
to realize frame spaces as deformation retracts of spaces whose homotopy groups are easier to understand.
4.5
gives conditions (depending on coefficient field, dimension, number of frames, and choice of prescribed norms) which guarantee that the homotopy groups (of various dimensions) of these frame spaces vanish.

Our results expand on a series of recent results in the literature stemming from Larson’s
Frame Homotopy Conjecture

[
15
]
, which states that the space of equal norm Parseval frames is path-connected. This conjecture was resolved by Cahill, Mixon and Strawn in
[
8
]
, and there have since been several subsequent generalizations and extensions
[
36
,
38
,
31
,
35
]
.
4.5
and its corollaries give new examples of connected frame spaces over
\mathbb{R}
(
4.9
) and the first (to our knowledge) general results about the higher-dimensional topology of frame spaces (
4.7
). We note that our results may be of more general interest: the frame spaces we consider arise in pure mathematical contexts as moduli spaces of polygons
[
18
,
17
,
26
]
and, in the complex case, as level sets of momentum maps of Hamiltonian actions on Grassmann and flag manifolds
[
31
,
38
]
; moreover, the techniques we use to demonstrate vanishing homotopy groups may be more broadly applicable to other spaces.

,

,

, which is based on establishing a few claims about the structure of the total frame energy; these claims are then proved in

2
The Total Frame Energy

In this section, we introduce a function on the space of arbitrary collections of vectors whose global minima are equal norm Parseval frames, and formulate the main result of the paper. We begin by fixing notation and recording some basic results of frame theory.

2.1
Background and Notation

Let
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
and let
d
be a positive integer. A
frame
for
\mathbb{K}^{d}
is simply a spanning set of vectors
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}
, for some
n\geq d
. Let
\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
denote the space of
d\times n
matrices. If a matrix
F=\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}&f_{2}&\dots&f_{n}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
has full rank, then the column vectors
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
for
F
define a frame for
\mathbb{K}^{d}
. Based on this correspondence, we identify the space of
n
-frames for
\mathbb{K}^{d}
with the space of full-rank matrices in
\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
.

A matrix
F\in\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
is a
Parseval frame
if
FF^{\ast}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix. Here,
F^{\ast}
denotes the adjoint of
F
(i.e., transpose or conjugate transpose, depending on
\mathbb{K}
). The matrix
FF^{\ast}
is commonly referred to as the
frame operator
of a frame
F
. We note that this definition agrees with the characterization (
1
) given in the introduction.

We are interested in Parseval frames with prescribed frame vector norms, so we introduce some notation. If
\vec{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
is an
n
-tuple of positive numbers, let
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
be the collection of
d\times n
Parseval frames
F
with
\|f_{i}\|^{2}=r_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
. In general there may not be any such frames, but we can characterize precisely when
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
is non-empty. Let
r_{(1)}\geq\dots\geq r_{(n)}>0
be the reverse-ordering of
(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
; that is,
r_{(k)}
is the
k
th largest of the
r_{i}
’s.

Definition 2.1
.

Suppose
\vec{r}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and
\vec{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
. Say that
\vec{r}
is
\vec{\lambda}
-admissible
if

\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}

and

\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{(i)}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\quad\text{for all $1\leq k\leq d-1$}.

(2)

In the language of
[
32
]
, the partial sum conditions in (
2
) say that
\vec{\lambda}

majorizes

\vec{r}
. As an easy consequence of the Schur–Horn theorem
[
25
,
40
]
(see also
[
12
,
3
,
7
]
), we have the following characterization of when Parseval frames of prescribed norms exist:

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose
\vec{r}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
. Then
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
is non-empty precisely when
\vec{r}
is
(\overbrace{1,\dots,1}^{d})
-admissible; that is, when

\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}=d\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{(i)}\leq k\quad\text{for all $1\leq k\leq d-1$}.

(3)

Terminology 2.3
.

For the remainder of this paper, we will only be interested in
\vec{\lambda}
-admissibility when
\vec{\lambda}=(1,\dots,1)
. Therefore, we will use
admissible
as a synonym for
(1,\dots,1)
-admissible
. In other words,
\vec{r}
will be admissible precisely when
\vec{r}
satisfies (
3
).

2.2
Defining the Total Frame Energy

As was described in the introduction, the main result of the paper shows that Parseval frames with presecribed vector norms can be constructed via gradient descent for a particular objective function. We now give a precise definition of this function.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a vector
\vec{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, we define the
total frame energy

{{E_{\vec{r}}}}:\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
by

{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(F)\coloneqq\|FF^{\ast}-\mathbb{I}_{d}\|_{\text{Fr}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\|f_{i}\|^{2}}{r_{i}}-1\right)^{2},

where
\mathbb{I}_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix and
\|\cdot\|_{\text{Fr}}
is the Frobenius norm.

Remark 2.5
.

The definition of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
may seem arbitrary, but it is very natural from the perspective of symplectic geometry. Specifically, there is a Hamiltonian action of
\operatorname{U}(d)\times\operatorname{U}(1)^{n}
on
\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
defined as in (
4
) below and
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
is the squared norm of a momentum map for this action. Our motivation for defining
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
in this way is heavily inspired by Kirwan’s pioneering work on such maps
[
27
]
(see also Lerman’s survey
[
29
]
), though our goal is to make our arguments as elementary as possible, so we mostly avoid appealing to this formalism. See our previous work
[
16
,
39
,
36
]
for more on momentum maps arising in frame theory.

Remark 2.6
.

When
F
is an equal-norm frame (i.e., there is some
r>0
so that
\|f_{i}\|^{2}=r
for all
i=1,\dots,n
), then

{{E_{(r,\dots,r)}}}(F)=\|FF^{\ast}-\mathbb{I}_{d}\|_{\text{Fr}}^{2}=\|F^{\ast}F\|_{\text{Fr}}^{2}-2nr+d

agrees, up to an additive constant, with the
frame potential

F\mapsto\|F^{\ast}F\|_{\text{Fr}}^{2}
defined by Benedetto and Fickus
[
4
]
.

Alternatively, when
F
is a Parseval frame,

{{E_{\left(\frac{d}{n},\dots,\frac{d}{n}\right)}}}(F)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{n}{d}\|f_{i}\|^{2}-1\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\frac{n^{2}}{d^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|f_{i}\|^{4}-\frac{n}{4}

agrees, up to additive and multiplicative constants, with Bodmann and Casazza’s
frame energy

F\mapsto\sum_{j,k}\left(\|f_{j}\|^{2}-\|f_{k}\|^{2}\right)^{2},

which they showed
[
6
, Proposition 3.12]
is equal to
2n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|f_{i}\|^{4}-2d^{2}
.

In other words, the total frame energy
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
contains elements of both the frame potential and the frame energy, so it is also reasonably natural from a frame theory perspective.

Elements of
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
are precisely the zeros of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
, so
2.2
implies:

Corollary 2.7
.

If
\vec{r}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
is admissible, then the elements of
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
are the global minima of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
.

In particular,
\left(\frac{d}{n},\dots,\frac{d}{n}\right)
is admissible, so the equal-norm Parseval frames are the global minima of
{{E_{\left(\frac{d}{n},\dots,\frac{d}{n}\right)}}}
.

2.3
Optimizing the Total Frame Energy

Since
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
consists precisely of the global minima of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
, we might hope to be able to flow a random frame to an element of
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
by negative gradient flow of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
. Indeed, this will turn out to be the case, at least when
\vec{r}
is rational. The restriction to rational
\vec{r}
is due to our method of proving the following theorem, which uses tools from algebraic geometry: we don’t expect that this is an essential hypothesis.

In order to state the theorem, we recall one definition: a frame
F\in\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
is
full spark
if every
d\times d
minor of
F
is non-singular. Full spark frames provide optimal reconstructions of the largest possible class of sparse signals
[
2
,
14
]
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Suppose
\vec{r}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{n}
is admissible, so that
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})\neq\emptyset
. Consider the negative gradient flow
\Gamma_{\vec{r}}:\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
defined by the differential equation

\Gamma_{\vec{r}}(F_{0},0)=F_{0},\qquad\frac{d}{dt}\Gamma_{\vec{r}}(F_{0},t)=-\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(\Gamma_{\vec{r}}(F_{0},t)),

where
F_{0}\in\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
is some initial matrix.

If
F_{0}
is full spark, then
\lim_{t\to\infty}\Gamma_{\vec{r}}(F_{0},t)
is an element of
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
.

The assumption that
F_{0}
is full spark can be relaxed slightly: it is enough for
F_{0}
to have property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
, defined below in
Definition

2.13
and fully characterized in
4.1
. As we’ll see in
3.1
, having property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
is a weaker condition than being full spark.

Note that
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
is a real polynomial on the real vector space
\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
, so the gradient flow from any point has a well-defined limit
[
30
]
and hence
F_{\infty}\coloneqq\lim_{t\to\infty}\Gamma_{\vec{r}}(F_{0},t)
exists.

It is well-known that almost every element of
\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
has full spark; see, for example,
[
2
, Theorem 13]
and
[
5
, Theorem 2.6]
. Indeed, the collection of spark-deficient matrices in
\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
is the zero locus of the product of determinants of
d\times d
minors, so it has positive codimension in
\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
, and hence is a null set with respect to any measure which is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. Therefore,
2.8
tells us that almost every
d\times n
matrix will flow to an element of
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
under the negative gradient flow of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
.

In turn, this implies that there are points in
\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
arbitrarily close to any non-minimal critical point of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
which flow to a global minimum, so non-minimal critical points of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
cannot be basins of attraction of the gradient flow. Since
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
is a polynomial defined on a Euclidean space, local minima must be basins of attraction
[
1
, Theorem 3]
, so
2.8
implies that
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
has no spurious local minima:

Corollary 2.9
.

When
\vec{r}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{n}
is admissible, all local minima of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
are global minima.

This is a direct analog of the classical Benedetto–Fickus theorem
[
4
]
in this setting.

As in our previous work
[
33
,
36
,
10
]
, the strategy for proving
2.8
is to identify a property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
satisfying the following conditions:

Conditions 2.10
.

1.

Every full spark frame in
\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
satisfies
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
and

2.

gradient flow (and its limit) preserves
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
, but

3.

non-minimizing critical points of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
do not satisfy
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
.

2.4
Geometric Invariant Theory and Semi-Stability

Property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
will be semi-stability with respect to a certain algebraic group action on
\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
. This idea is based on Mumford’s
geometric invariant theory
(GIT)
[
34
]
, so we give some general background (which is basically a gloss of Thomas’s explanation
[
42
]
) before specifying to our problem.

Let
G
be a reductive algebraic group that acts linearly on a finite-dimensional complex vector space
V
; for example,
\operatorname{GL}(V)
or
\operatorname{SL}(V)
. A non-zero vector
v\in V
is
unstable
under the action of
G
if the closure
\overline{G\cdot v}
of the
G
-orbit of
v
contains the origin. If
v
is not unstable, it is called
semi-stable
. From an algebraic geometry perspective, the point is that the unstable points are precisely those on which every
G
-invariant homogeneous polynomial evaluates to zero, so the semi-stable points are those which can potentially be distinguished by
G
-invariant homogeneous polynomials.

All orbits consist either entirely of semi-stable points or entirely of unstable points:

Proposition 2.11

(see, e.g.,
[
33
, Proposition 6]
)
.

Given
v\in V
that is semi-stable, every point in
\overline{G\cdot v}
is also semi-stable.

Likewise, semi-stability is really a feature of the line containing
v
:
v
is semi-stable if and only if
\lambda v
is semi-stable for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
.

A
one-parameter subgroup
of
G
is a homomorphism of algebraic groups
\lambda:\mathbb{C}^{\times}\to G
, which induces a decomposition
V=\bigoplus_{i\in I}V_{i}
and integer weights
w:I\to\mathbb{Z}
so that, for every
i\in I
,
v\in V_{i}
, and
t\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
,

\lambda(t)\cdot v=t^{w(i)}v.

It follows immediately that a nonzero vector
v\in V
is unstable under the action of
G
if there exists a one-parameter subgroup
\lambda
so that

\lim_{t\to 0}\lambda(t)\cdot v=0.

Much less obvious is that the converse holds:

Theorem 2.12

(Hilbert–Mumford criterion
[
22
,
34
]
)
.

v\in V\backslash\{0\}
is unstable under the action of
G
if and only if there exists a one-parameter subgroup
\lambda
of
G
so that

\lim_{t\to 0}\lambda(t)\cdot v=0.

With this setup, we now specify to our problem. The complex vector space will be
\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
and the algebraic group will be
G=\operatorname{SL}(d)\times S((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n})
, where
S((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n})
is the group of diagonal
n\times n
matrices with determinant 1. The action of
G
on
\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
will be determined by
\vec{r}
: while the
\operatorname{SL}(d)
action will always be by left multiplication, the
S((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n})
action will depend on
\vec{r}
.

To get an algebraic action, we need to convert
\vec{r}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{n}
into a list of positive integers, which we can do by clearing denominators. More precisely, if
r_{i}=\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
, then define
s_{i}\coloneqq r_{i}\frac{\operatorname{lcm}\{b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\}}{\gcd\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}}
. Then
\vec{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and
\frac{s_{i}}{s_{j}}=\frac{r_{i}}{r_{j}}
for all
i
and
j
. Also, let
S\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}
be the product of the
s_{i}
.

Then we define the action of
G
as follows: if
A\in\operatorname{SL}(d)
and
D=\operatorname{diag}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\in S((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n})
, then define

(A,D)\cdot F\coloneqq AF\left(\operatorname{diag}\left(t_{1}^{S/s_{1}},\dots,t_{n}^{S/s_{n}}\right)\right)^{-1}=AF\operatorname{diag}\left(t_{1}^{-S/s_{1}},\dots,t_{n}^{-S/s_{n}}\right).

(4)

Definition 2.13
.

We say that
F\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}

has property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
if it is semi-stable under the
G
-action defined in (
4
).

We will eventually build up to a full characterization of property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
in
4.1
, but for now we just give an easy special case:

Proposition 2.14
.

If
F\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
contains zero vectors, then
F
does not have property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
.

Proof.

Up to re-ordering, we may as well assume the last column of
F
is the zero vector; i.e.,
F=\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}&\dots&f_{n-1}&\vec{0}\end{bmatrix}
. Define the one-parameter subgroup
\lambda:\mathbb{C}^{\times}\to G
by

\lambda(t)\coloneqq\left(I,\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{1}{t},\dots,\frac{1}{t},t^{n-1}\right)\right).

Then

\lim_{t\to 0}\lambda(t)\cdot F=\lim_{t\to 0}I\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}&\dots&f_{n-1}&\vec{0}\end{bmatrix}\operatorname{diag}\left(t^{S/s_{1}},\dots,t^{S/s_{n-1}},t^{-(n-1)S/s_{n}}\right)=\lim_{t\to 0}\begin{bmatrix}t^{S/s_{1}}f_{1}&\dots&t^{S/s_{n-1}}f_{n-1}&\vec{0}\end{bmatrix}=0,

so
F
is unstable by the easy direction of
2.12
.
∎

3
Proof of
2.8

In the following subsections, we verify that property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
, from
Definition

2.13
, satisfies each of the three conditions stated in
2.10
; combining these then leads to the proof of
2.8
.

3.1
Full Spark Frames Have Property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}

The next result establishes the first item from
2.10
.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
F\in\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
has full spark, then it has property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
.

Proof.

Suppose
F\in\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
. Whether
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
, we can interpret
F
as an element of
\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
, so the notion of semi-stability with respect to the
G
-action on
\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
makes sense.

Our goal is to use
2.12
to show that
F
is semi-stable, and hence has property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
. So let
\lambda:\mathbb{C}^{\times}\to G
be a one-parameter subgroup. Then
\lambda(t)=(A(t),D(t))\in\operatorname{SL}(d)\times S((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n})
and, up to a change of basis, we may as well assume
A(t)
is diagonal. There are integer weights
u(1),\dots,u(d),w(1),\dots,w(n)\in\mathbb{Z}
with
u(1)+\dots+u(d)=0
and
w(1)+\dots+w(n)=0
so that

\lambda(t)\cdot F=(A(t),D(t))\cdot F=\operatorname{diag}(t^{u(1)},\dots,t^{u(d)})F\operatorname{diag}(t^{-w(1)S/s_{1}},\dots,t^{-w(n)S/s_{n}})=\begin{bmatrix}t^{u(i)-w(j)S/s_{j}}f_{ij}\end{bmatrix}_{i,j},

where
f_{ij}
is the
(i,j)
entry of
F
.

Let
J\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
with
|J|=d
and let
F_{J}
be the
d\times d
minor of
F
whose columns have indices in
J
, and let
(\lambda(t)\cdot F)_{J}
be the corresponding minor of
\lambda(t)\cdot F
. Then

\det(\lambda(t)\cdot F)_{J}=\det\begin{bmatrix}t^{u(i)-w(j)S/s_{j}}f_{ij}\end{bmatrix}_{1\leq i\leq d,j\in J}=t^{\sum_{i=1}^{d}u(i)-\sum_{j\in J}w(j)S/s_{j}}\det F_{J}=t^{-\sum_{j\in J}w(j)S/s_{j}}\det F_{J},

(5)

since
\sum_{i=1}^{d}u(i)=0
. As
F
is full spark,
\det F_{J}\neq 0
for all
J
, so (
5
) goes to zero as
t\to 0
if and only if
\sum_{j\in J}w(j)\frac{S}{s_{j}}<0
.

Up to reindexing, we may as well assume that
w(1)\geq\dots\geq w(n)
. Since
\sum_{j=1}^{n}w(j)=0
, either we have
w(1)=\dots=w(n)=0
, in which case we always have
\sum_{j\in J}w(j)\frac{S}{s_{j}}=0
, so
\lim_{t\to 0}\det(\lambda(t)\cdot F)_{J}\neq 0
, or we have
w(1)>0>w(n)
. In this latter case, let
J=\{1,\dots,d\}
, let
\overline{s}=\min\left\{\frac{S}{s_{j}}:j\in J\right\}
and consider

\sum_{j\in J}w(j)\frac{S}{s_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}w(j)\frac{S}{s_{j}}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{d}w(j)\overline{s}=\overline{s}\sum_{j=1}^{d}w(j)>0

since
\sum_{j=1}^{d}w(j)>\sum_{j=1}^{n}w(j)=0
and
\overline{r}>0
. Hence, for this choice of
J
we have

\lim_{t\to 0}\det(\lambda(t)\cdot F)_{J}=\lim_{t\to 0}t^{-\sum_{j\in J}w(j)S/s_{j}}\det F_{J}\neq 0.

Of course, this implies that
\lim_{t\to 0}\lambda(t)\cdot F\neq 0
. Since our initial choice of one-parameter subgroup
\lambda
was arbitrary,
2.12
implies that
F
has property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
.
∎

3.2
Gradient Flow Preserves
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}

Our next goal is to verify the second condition from
2.10
. A straightforward computation gives the gradient of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
:

Proposition 3.2
.

The gradient of the total frame energy is given by

\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(F)=F\left[4F^{\ast}F+\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{\|f_{1}\|^{2}}{r_{1}^{2}}-\frac{1}{r_{1}}-4,\dots,\frac{\|f_{n}\|^{2}}{r_{n}^{2}}-\frac{1}{r_{n}}-4\right)\right].

Define

a_{F}\coloneqq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\|f_{i}\|^{2}}{r_{i}^{2}}-\frac{1}{r_{i}}-4\right)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}}.

If we let
\Delta=-\frac{1}{S}\operatorname{diag}\left(s_{1}\left(\frac{\|f_{1}\|^{2}}{r_{1}^{2}}-\frac{1}{r_{1}}-4-a_{F}\right),\dots,s_{n}\left(\frac{\|f_{n}\|^{2}}{r_{n}^{2}}-\frac{1}{r_{n}}-4-a_{F}\right)\right)
, then
\operatorname{tr}\Delta=0
and

\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(F)=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\left(\exp\left(t\left(4FF^{\ast}+a_{F}\mathbb{I}_{d}\right)\right),\exp(t\Delta)\right)\cdot F.

Since exponentiating any square matrix gives an invertible matrix, we know that
\exp\left(t\left(4FF^{\ast}+a_{F}\mathbb{I}_{d}\right)\right)\in\operatorname{GL}(d)
. Also,
\operatorname{tr}\Delta=0
implies that
\exp(t\Delta)\in S((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n})
, so the above equation shows that
\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(F)
is tangent to the
\left(\operatorname{GL}(d)\times S((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n})\right)
-orbit of
F
. In general, for
(A,D)\in\operatorname{GL}(d)\times S((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n})
with
D=(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
, we see that

\left((\det A)^{-1/d}A,D\right)\cdot F=(\det A)^{-1/d}AF\operatorname{diag}\left(t_{1}^{-S/s_{1}},\dots,t_{n}^{-S/s_{n}}\right)=(\det A)^{-1/d}(A,D)\cdot F,

so the
\left(\operatorname{GL}(d)\times S((\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n})\right)
-orbit of
F
is contained in the
\left(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times G\right)
-orbit of
F
, and we conclude that
\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(F)
is tangent to the
\left(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times G\right)
-orbit of
F
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose
F_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
has property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
. Then so does
F_{t}\coloneqq\Gamma_{\vec{r}}(F_{0},t)
for all
t\in[0,\infty)
and so does
F_{\infty}\coloneqq\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{t}
.

Proof.

We just saw that the gradient flow of
F_{0}
is tangent to the
\left(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times G\right)
-orbit of
F_{0}
, so we see that
F_{t}\in\left(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times G\right)\cdot F_{0}
for all
t\in[0,\infty)
. Since scaling by a nonzero number doesn’t affect semistability,
2.11
implies that
F_{t}
is semi-stable for all
t\in[0,\infty)
.

As long as the scalar term stays away from zero,
F_{\infty}
is a non-zero scalar multiple of an element of
\overline{G\cdot F_{0}}
, and hence
2.11
implies that
F_{\infty}
is also semi-stable.

Therefore, we need only rule out the possibility that the scalar term goes to zero. In that case,
F_{\infty}
is the zero matrix. As we show in
3.4
below, the zero matrix is a local maximum of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
, so nothing flows to it except the zero matrix itself. But
F_{0}
has property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
, so cannot be the zero matrix by
2.14
. Therefore, the scalar term cannot go to zero, and the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

The zero matrix is a local maximum of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
.

Proof.

We prove this using the second derivative test. If we write
F=\begin{bmatrix}x_{ij}+\sqrt{-1}\,y_{ij}\end{bmatrix}_{ij}
and we write the Hessian of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
as a square matrix with respect to the
x_{ij}
and
y_{ij}
real coordinates, then by inspection of the gradient formula from
3.2
it is clear that the Hessian at
0
of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
is a diagonal matrix with entries of the form
-4-\frac{1}{r_{i}}
on the diagonal. This is certainly negative definite, so
0
is a local maximum of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
.
∎

In fact, we can leverage this result to show that the negative gradient flow of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
cannot shrink any columns to the zero vector. We will prove this by showing that the negative gradient flow of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
causes the norm of the
i
th column to increase in a small enough neighborhood of any critical point of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
which has zero
i
th column.

To that end, define
g_{i}:\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
by
F\mapsto\|f_{i}\|^{2}
, where
f_{i}
is the
i
th column of
F
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Suppose
\widehat{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
is a critical point of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
whose
i
th column is the zero vector. Then there exists
\epsilon>0
so that, for any
F
in an
\epsilon
-neighborhood of
\widehat{F}
,

\langle\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(F),\nabla g_{i}(F)\rangle\leq 0,

with equality if and only if the
i
th column of
F
is zero.

Proof.

First,
\nabla g_{i}(F)
is the
d\times n
matrix with all zero columns except the
i
th, which is
2f_{i}
. Therefore, for any
Y\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
, the (real) Frobenius inner product

\langle Y,\nabla g_{i}(F)\rangle=\operatorname{Re}(Y^{\ast}\nabla g_{i}(F))=2\operatorname{Re}(y_{i}^{\ast}f_{i})=2\langle y_{i},f_{i}\rangle.

(6)

This implies
\langle\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(F),\nabla g_{i}(F)\rangle=0
whenever the
i
th column of
F
is zero.

Now, we consider
F
in a neighborhood of
\widehat{F}
and write
F=\widehat{F}+X
for small
X\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
. Then, since
\widehat{F}
is a critical point of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
,

\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(F)=\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(\widehat{F}+X)=\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(X).

Since
X
is in a small neighborhood of the zero matrix, expanding
\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
at 0 yields

\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(X)=\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(0+X)=\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(0)+H(0)X+O(\|X\|^{2})=H(0)X+O(\|X\|^{2}),

where
H(0)
is the Hessian of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
at 0. We know from the proof of
3.5
that, focusing on the
i
th column,
H(0)
simply scales the
i
th column of
X
by
-4-\frac{1}{r_{i}}
. Since
F=\widehat{F}+X
and the
i
th column of
\widehat{F}
is zero, the
i
th column of
X
is the same as the
i
th column of
F
. Therefore,

\left(\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(F)\right)_{i}=\left(\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(X)\right)_{i}\approx\left(-4-\frac{1}{r_{i}}\right)x_{i}=\left(-4-\frac{1}{r_{i}}\right)f_{i}

to first order.

Therefore, substituting
\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(F)
into (
6
), we see that

\langle\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(F),\nabla g_{i}(F)\rangle=2\langle\left(\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(F)\right)_{i},f_{i}\rangle\approx 2\left(-4-\frac{1}{r_{i}}\right)\|f_{i}\|^{2}.

This is strictly negative whenever
f_{i}
is nonzero and
F
is close enough to
\widehat{F}
.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Suppose
F_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
and define
F_{t}
for
t\in[0,\infty]
as in
3.3
. If the
i
th column of
F_{t}
is zero for any
t\in[0,\infty]
, then the
i
th column of
F_{0}
must also be zero.

Proof.

By inspection of the formula from
3.2
, observe that the
i
th column of
\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(F)
is

\left(4FF^{\ast}+\left(\frac{\|f_{i}\|^{2}}{r_{i}^{2}}-\frac{1}{r_{i}}-4\right)\mathbb{I}_{d}\right)f_{i}.

Hence, the
positive
gradient flow of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
preserves zero columns, so the
negative
gradient flow cannot introduce new zero columns in finite time. This proves the result for
t\in[0,\infty)
.

Now, suppose
F_{\infty}
has a zero column and choose
t_{0}
large enough so that
F_{t}
satisfies the hypotheses of
3.5
for all
t\geq t_{0}
. Then
3.5
implies that

\langle-\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(F_{t}),\nabla g_{i}(F_{t})\rangle\geq 0

for all
t\geq t_{0}
, so
g_{i}(F_{t})
must be non-decreasing with
t
. But we know that
g_{i}(F_{t})\geq 0
and
0=g_{i}(F_{\infty})=\lim_{t\to\infty}g_{i}(F_{t})
, so it must be the case that the
i
th column of
F_{t}
is zero for all
t\geq t_{0}
, and hence (by the first part of the proof) that the
i
th column of
F_{0}
was already zero.
∎

3.3
Non-Minimizing Critical Points Do Not Have Property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}

Finally, we establish Condition 3 in
2.10
. We begin with some necessary definitions.

Definition 3.7
.

A frame
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
is
orthodecomposable
if there exists a nontrivial partition
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}
of
\{1,\dots,n\}
and pairwise orthogonal subspaces
V_{1},\dots,V_{k}\subset\mathbb{K}^{d}
with
\mathbb{K}^{d}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}V_{i}
so that, for all
j=1,\dots,k
,
\{f_{i}\}_{i\in P_{j}}
is a frame for
V_{j}
.

Definition 3.8
.

An orthodecomposable frame
F
with partition
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}
is a
blockwise (equal-norm) tight frame
if each
\{f_{i}\}_{i\in P_{j}}
is a (equal-norm) tight frame for its span.

Proposition 3.9
.

F
is a critical point of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
if and only if one of the following conditions holds:

1.

F\in\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
,

2.

F
is a blockwise tight frame so that
\frac{1}{r_{i}}-\frac{\|f_{i}\|^{2}}{r_{i}^{2}}
is constant on each block, or

3.

F
is the union of a frame satisfying
1
or
2
with some zero vectors.

In particular, the critical points of
{{E_{\left(\frac{d}{n},\dots,\frac{d}{n}\right)}}}
are precisely the ENP frames, the blockwise equal-norm tight frames, and the unions of ENP frames or blockwise ENT frames with some zero vectors.

Proof.

From
3.2
,
F
is a critical point of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
if and only if

4FF^{\ast}F=F\operatorname{diag}\left(4+\frac{1}{r_{1}}-\frac{\|f_{1}\|^{2}}{r_{1}^{2}},\dots,4+\frac{1}{r_{n}}-\frac{\|f_{n}\|^{2}}{r_{n}^{2}}\right).

Considered column-wise, this says that

4FF^{\ast}f_{i}=\left(4+\frac{1}{r_{i}}-\frac{\|f_{i}\|^{2}}{r_{i}^{2}}\right)f_{i},

so each non-zero
f_{i}
is an eigenvector of the frame operator
FF^{\ast}
with eigenvalue
\frac{1}{4}\left(4+\frac{1}{r_{i}}-\frac{\|f_{i}\|^{2}}{r_{i}^{2}}\right)
. If the frame operator has distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}>\dots>\lambda_{k}>0
with associated eigenspaces
E_{1},\dots,E_{k}
, then we get a partition
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}
of
\{1,\dots,n\}
with
i\in P_{j}
if and only if
FF^{\ast}f_{i}=\lambda_{j}f_{i}
.

Since eigenspaces of Hermitian matrices like
FF^{\ast}
are pairwise orthogonal, we see that
F
must be orthodecomposable with partition
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}
. Moreover, since

\lambda_{j}=\frac{1}{4}\left(4+\frac{1}{r_{i}}-\frac{\|f_{i}\|^{2}}{r_{i}^{2}}\right)

(7)

for any
i\in P_{j}
, it follows that
\frac{1}{r_{i}}-\frac{\|f_{i}\|^{2}}{r_{i}^{2}}
is constant on
\{f_{i}\}_{i\in P_{j}}
.
∎

We want to see that non-minimizing critical points of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
do not have property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
. When a critical point has some zero vectors, we can use
2.14
to see that it doesn’t have property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
, so we only need to worry about the case when the critical point
F
is blockwise tight with
\frac{1}{r_{i}}-\frac{\|f_{i}\|^{2}}{r_{i}^{2}}
constant on each block and
F\notin\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
. First, we find an alternative characterization of such frames:

Lemma 3.10
.

Suppose
F
is a critical point of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
which is blockwise tight with
\frac{1}{r_{i}}-\frac{\|f_{i}\|^{2}}{r_{i}^{2}}
constant on each block and
F\notin\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
. Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}
be the partition of
\{1,\dots,n\}
and
V_{1},\dots,V_{k}\subset\mathbb{K}^{d}
the pairwise orthogonal subspaces coming from the orthodecomposability of
F
. Then there exists
\ell
so that

\sum_{i\in P_{\ell}}r_{i}>\dim(V_{\ell}).

Proof.

For each
j=1,\dots,k
, let
d_{j}=\dim(V_{j})
. Up to permuting columns and changing basis, we can write
F
in the block-diagonal form

\begin{bmatrix}F_{1}&0&\dots&0\\
0&F_{2}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&F_{k}\end{bmatrix},

where
F_{j}
is
d_{j}\times n_{j}
. Note that
d_{1}+\dots+d_{k}=d
and
n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}=n
.

Since
\vec{r}
satisfies (
3
),

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i\in P_{j}}r_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}=d=\sum_{j=1}^{k}d_{j},

so either
\sum_{i\in P_{j}}r_{i}=d_{j}
for all
j=1,\dots,k
, or there exists some
\ell
so that
\sum_{i\in P_{\ell}}r_{i}>d_{\ell}
. The latter is our desired conclusion, so we just need to rule out the former.

To that end, assume
\sum_{i\in P_{j}}r_{i}=d_{j}
for all
j=1,\dots,k
. Pick an arbitrary
j
.
F
is a blockwise tight critical point with
\frac{1}{r_{i}}-\frac{\|f_{i}\|^{2}}{r_{i}^{2}}
constant on each block, so we know from (
7
) that, for each
i\in P_{j}
,

\|f_{i}\|^{2}=r_{i}\left(1+4r_{i}(1-\lambda_{j})\right).

(8)

Also, the proof of
3.9
implies that

F_{j}F_{j}^{\ast}=\lambda_{j}\mathbb{I}_{d_{j}}.

Hence,

d_{j}\lambda_{j}=\operatorname{tr}(\lambda_{j}\mathbb{I}_{d_{j}})=\operatorname{tr}(F_{j}F_{j}^{\ast})=\operatorname{tr}(F_{j}^{\ast}F_{j})=\sum_{i\in P_{j}}\|f_{i}\|^{2}=\sum_{i\in P_{j}}r_{i}\left(1+4r_{i}(1-\lambda_{j})\right)=d_{j}+4(1-\lambda_{j})\sum_{i\in P_{j}}r_{i}^{2},

or, after simplification

d_{j}(1-\lambda_{j})=-4(1-\lambda_{j})\sum_{i\in P_{j}}r_{i}^{2}.

Since
d_{j}>0
and
\sum_{i\in P_{j}}r_{i}^{2}>0
, this is impossible unless
\lambda_{j}=1
. Since our choice of
j
was arbitrary, this is true for all
j
, meaning that
FF^{\ast}=\mathbb{I}_{d}
and, from (
8
),
\|f_{i}\|^{2}=r_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
. In other words,
F\in\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
, which is precisely the condition ruled out in the hypotheses of the lemma.

So we conclude that
\sum_{i\in P_{j}}r_{i}
cannot be equal to
d_{j}
for all
j=1,\dots,k
, so there exists
\ell
with
\sum_{i\in P_{\ell}}r_{i}>d_{\ell}
.
∎

With this characterization of the non-minimizing critical points of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
, we can now show that they do not have property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
since they satisfy the hypotheses of the following proposition.

Proposition 3.11
.

Suppose
F\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
so that there is some subspace
V\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
so that
f_{i_{1}},\dots,f_{i_{n_{1}}}\in V
and
\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}r_{i_{j}}>\dim(V)
. Then
F
does not have property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
.

Proof.

Suppose
F
is such a frame. Let
d_{1}=\dim(V)
. The hypothesis

\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}r_{i_{j}}>d_{1}=\frac{d_{1}}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}

is equivalent to

\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}s_{i_{j}}>\frac{d_{1}}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}.

(9)

Up to permuting columns, we may as well assume that
i_{j}=j
for each
j=1,\dots,n_{1}
, and hence, up to a change of basis, that we can represent
F
by the block upper-triangular matrix

F=\begin{bmatrix}F_{1}&F_{2}\\
0&F_{3}\end{bmatrix},

where
F_{1}
is
d_{1}\times n_{1}
,
F_{2}
is
d_{1}\times(n-n_{1})
, and
F_{3}
is
(d-d_{1})\times(n-n_{1})
.

Note that
\|\vec{s}\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}
and define

S_{1}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}s_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{S}_{1}\coloneqq\sum_{i=d_{1}+1}^{n}s_{i}=\|\vec{s}\|_{1}-S_{1}.

Using this notation and multiplying both sides of (
9
) by
d
yields

dS_{1}>d_{1}\|\vec{s}\|_{1}.

(10)

Consider the 1-parameter subgroup
\lambda:\mathbb{C}^{\times}\to G
given by

\lambda(t)=\left(\begin{bmatrix}t^{(d-d_{1})\|\vec{s}\|_{1}S}\mathbb{I}_{d_{1}}&0\\
0&t^{-d_{1}\|\vec{s}\|_{1}S}\end{bmatrix},\operatorname{diag}\left(t^{d\widehat{S}_{1}s_{1}},\dots,t^{d\widehat{S}_{1}s_{d_{1}}},t^{-dS_{1}s_{d_{1}+1}},\dots,t^{-dS_{1}s_{n}}\right)\right).

This really is in
G
since

\det\begin{bmatrix}t^{(d-d_{1})\|\vec{s}\|_{1}S}\mathbb{I}_{d_{1}}&0\\
0&t^{-d_{1}\|\vec{s}\|_{1}S}\mathbb{I}_{d-d_{1}}\end{bmatrix}=t^{d_{1}(d-d_{1})\|\vec{s}\|_{1}S-(d-d_{1})d_{1}\|\vec{s}\|_{1}S}=t^{0}=1

and

\det\left(\operatorname{diag}\left(t^{d\widehat{S}_{1}s_{1}},\dots,t^{d\widehat{S}_{1}s_{d_{1}}},t^{-dS_{1}s_{d_{1}+1}},\dots,t^{-dS_{1}s_{n}}\right)\right)=t^{d\widehat{S}_{1}S_{1}-dS_{1}\widehat{S}_{1}}=t^{0}=1.

Now,

\lambda(t)\cdot F=\begin{bmatrix}t^{(d-d_{1})\|\vec{s}\|_{1}S}\mathbb{I}_{d_{1}}&0\\
0&t^{-d_{1}\|\vec{s}\|_{1}S}\mathbb{I}_{d-d_{1}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}F_{1}&F_{2}\\
0&F_{3}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}t^{-dS\widehat{S}_{1}}\mathbb{I}_{n_{1}}&0\\
0&t^{dSS_{1}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}t^{S((d-d_{1})\|\vec{s}\|_{1}-d\widehat{S}_{1})}F_{1}&t^{S(dS_{1}+(d-d_{1})\|\vec{s}\|_{1})}F_{2}\\
0&t^{S(dS_{1}-d_{1}\|\vec{s}\|_{1})}F_{3}\end{bmatrix}.

But now (
10
) implies that
dS_{1}-d_{1}\|\vec{s}\|_{1}>0
and

(d-d_{1})\|\vec{s}\|_{1}-d\widehat{S}_{1}=(d-d_{1})\|\vec{s}\|_{1}-d(\|\vec{s}\|_{1}-S_{1})=dS_{1}-d_{1}\|\vec{s}\|_{1}>0,

so we conclude that

\lim_{t\to 0}\lambda(t)\cdot F=0,

and hence
F
does not have property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
by
2.12
.
∎

Corollary 3.12
.

The non-minimizing critical points of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
do not have property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
.

Proof.

If
F
is a non-minimizing critical point of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
, then it is not in
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
, so it must either be blockwise tight or contain one or more zero vectors. If
F
is blockwise tight,
3.10
and
3.11
imply that
F
does not have property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
. On the other hand, if
F
contains a zero vector, then
2.14
implies it does not have property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
.

Either way, we see that
F
does not have property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
.
∎

3.4
Completing the Proof

Finally, we use the established fact that
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
satisfies
2.10
to prove our main result.

Proof of
2.8
.

Suppose
F_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
has full spark. Then, by
3.1
,
F_{0}
has property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
. By
3.3
, this implies that
F_{\infty}\coloneqq\lim_{t\to\infty}\Gamma_{\vec{r}}(F_{0},t)
also has property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
.

Since
F_{\infty}
is the limit point of the gradient flow, it must be a critical point; since it has property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
,
3.12
implies that it must be a global minimizer, and hence
F_{\infty}\in\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
by
2.7
.

This proves
2.8
when
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
. Since the gradient of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
at a real matrix is real,
\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
is invariant under the gradient flow, and the
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
case follows.
∎

4
Topological Applications

We now turn to some topological consequences of
2.8
. The proof of that theorem showed that the semistable points in
\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
flow to the Parseval frames with prescribed frame vector norms; in other words, the gradient flow realizes a deformation retraction of the semistable points to
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
(see
4.6
below for a formal argument). Therefore, if we can show that the set of semistable points is highly connected, then
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
will be as well; this is the content of
4.5
, which we prove as follows. The semistable points are the complement in
\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
of the variety of unstable points, so our strategy is to give a lower bound on the codimension of the unstable locus. The result then follows because the complement in a contractible set like
\mathbb{K}^{d\times n}
of a subvariety of high codimension remains highly connected.

4.1
Characterization of the Unstable Set

We know from
3.1
that the unstable points are a subset of the matrices that do not have full spark. Unfortunately, the set of spark-deficient matrices is defined by a single equation, namely that the product of the determinants of all
d\times d
minors is zero, so we generally expect this set to have codimension 1.

However, there are many spark-deficient matrices which nonetheless have property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
; indeed, the locus of unstable points generally has codimension much bigger than 1. To see this, we need a more precise characterization of the unstable points.

Theorem 4.1
.

A matrix
F\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
fails to have property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
if and only if there exists a subspace
V\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
and indices
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}
so that
f_{i_{1}},\dots,f_{i_{k}}\in V
and

\sum_{j=1}^{k}r_{i_{j}}>\dim(V).

Notice that this is a generalization of
2.14
, which covers the case when
\dim(V)=0
.

Proof.

We already proved the backward direction in
3.11
. As for the forward direction, assume
F
is unstable. If the
i
th column of
F
is the zero vector, then that column lives in the trivial subspace
\{0\}
and
r_{i}>0=\dim\{0\}
, so the result follows.

Hence, we may as well assume that all columns of
F
are nonzero. Since
F
is unstable,
2.12
implies there exists a one-parameter subgroup
\lambda:\mathbb{C}^{\times}\to G
so that
\lim_{t\to 0}\lambda(t)\cdot F=0
. We may as well assume the first term in
\lambda(t)
has been diagonalized, so that

\lambda(t)=\left(\operatorname{diag}\left(t^{u_{1}},\dots,t^{u_{d}}\right),\operatorname{diag}\left(t^{w_{1}},\dots,t^{w_{n}}\right)\right),

where
\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}=0
and
\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}=0
. Then

\lambda(t)\cdot F=\operatorname{diag}\left(t^{u_{1}},\dots,t^{u_{d}}\right)\begin{bmatrix}f_{ij}\end{bmatrix}_{i,j}\operatorname{diag}\left(t^{-w_{1}S/s_{1}},\dots,t^{-w_{n}S/s_{n}}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}t^{u_{i}-w_{j}S/s_{j}}f_{ij}\end{bmatrix}_{i,j},

so
\lim_{t\to 0}\lambda(t)\cdot F=0
implies that

u_{i}-w_{j}S/s_{j}>0\quad\text{for all $i,j$ with $f_{ij}\neq 0$}.

(11)

We now break this into two cases:

Case 1.
If
u_{1}=\dots=u_{d}=0
, then (
11
) implies that
w_{j}<0
whenever the
j
th column of
F
is not the zero vector. Since
\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}=0
, not all of the
w_{j}
can be negative, so
F
must have at least one zero column. We’ve already proved the result in this case.

Case 2.
On the other hand, suppose not all
u_{i}
are zero. We can assume
u_{1}\geq\dots\geq u_{d}
and
w_{1}\geq\dots\geq w_{n}
, so we know that
u_{1}>0>u_{d}
. Let
k
be the largest index so that
u_{k}>0
and let
\ell
be the largest index so that
w_{\ell}\geq 0
. Then for
i>k
and
j\leq\ell
, we have that
u_{i}-w_{j}S/s_{j}\leq 0
, and hence (
11
) implies
f_{ij}=0
.

Therefore,
f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
, where we think of
\mathbb{C}^{k}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
as the span of the first
k
coordinate directions. This means we can write
F
in the block upper-triangular form

F=\begin{bmatrix}F_{1}&F_{2}\\
0&F_{3}\end{bmatrix}.

If some columns of
F_{3}
are zero, then we can rearrange and absorb them into
F_{1}
, so we may as well assume
F
is in this form with each column of
F_{3}
non-zero. Since all columns of
F_{3}
contains at least one non-zero entry, we can transform
F
into a block-diagonal form

\widetilde{F}=\begin{bmatrix}F_{1}&0\\
0&\widetilde{F}_{3}\end{bmatrix}

using only elementary row operations with determinant 1 (i.e., adding a scalar multiple of one row to another). The row operations can be achieved by left-multiplication by a matrix in
\operatorname{SL}(d)
, so
\widetilde{F}
is in the same
G
-orbit as
F
, and hence must also be unstable by
2.11
. Since all columns of
F
are nonzero, all the columns of
\widetilde{F}
are also nonzero.

Since
\widetilde{F}^{\ast}\widetilde{F}
is block-diagonal, we see from
3.2
that
\nabla{{E_{\vec{r}}}}(F)
is also block-diagonal, so gradient flow will preserve orthodecomposability, and hence
\widetilde{F}_{\infty}\coloneqq\lim_{t\to\infty}\Gamma_{\vec{r}}(\widetilde{F},t)
must be an orthodecomposable critical point of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
with the same block structure as
\widetilde{F}
. Since
\widetilde{F}
is unstable,
3.3
implies that
\widetilde{F}_{\infty}
must be as well. Since
\widetilde{F}
has no zero columns,
3.6
implies that
\widetilde{F}_{\infty}
can’t have any zero columns, either, and hence
3.9
tells us that
\widetilde{F}_{\infty}
must be a blockwise tight critical point which is not in
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
.

But then
3.10
implies that either
\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}r_{j}>k
or
\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{n}r_{j}>d-k
; either way,
\widetilde{F}
satisfies the conclusion of the theorem since its first
\ell
columns lie in a
k
-dimensional subspace and its last
n-\ell
columns lie in a
(d-k)
-dimensional subspace.

Finally, the first
\ell
columns of
F
lie in a
k
-dimensional subspace and, since the columns of
F
are related to the columns of
\widetilde{F}
by an invertible linear transformation, the last
n-\ell
columns of
F
must lie in some
(d-k)
-dimensional subspace since the last
n-\ell
columns of
\widetilde{F}
do. Therefore,
F
also satisfies the conclusion of the theorem.
∎

4.2
Codimension Bound

Let
\vec{r}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{n}
be admissible. We use
\mathcal{U}(\vec{r})
to denote the
set of unstable frames
, as is characterized in
4.1
; that is,

\mathcal{U}(\vec{r})\coloneqq\left\{F\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}\mid\,\exists\,i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\,\mbox{ and }V\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}\,\mbox{s.t.}\,f_{i_{1}},\ldots,f_{i_{k}}\in V\,\mbox{ and }\sum_{j=1}^{k}r_{i_{j}}>\mathrm{dim}(V)\right\}.

Our goal now is to get a lower bound on the codimension of
\mathcal{U}(\vec{r})
.

Given
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
, choose an indexing set
I=\{i_{i},\ldots,i_{k}\}\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
with the property that
\sum_{i\in I}r_{i}>\ell
. We note that, by admissibility, there is always at least one such indexing set: namely,
I=\{1,\ldots,n\}
satisfies
\sum_{i\in I}r_{i}=d>\ell
. The collection of available indexing sets with this property depends, in general, on the choice of
\vec{r}
and
\ell
. We then define

\mathcal{U}_{\ell,I}(\vec{r})\coloneqq\left\{F\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}\mid\,\exists\,V\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}\,\mbox{s.t.}\,f_{i_{1}},\ldots,f_{i_{k}}\in V\mbox{ and }\mathrm{dim}(V)=\ell\right\},

so that

\mathcal{U}(\vec{r})=\bigcup_{\ell,I}\mathcal{U}_{\ell,I}(\vec{r}),

where the union is over all
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
and all corresponding indexing sets.

Each
\mathcal{U}_{\ell,I}(\vec{r})
is an algebraic variety, and our first goal is to bound the dimension of these varieties. In particular,
\mathcal{U}_{\ell,I}(\vec{r})
is essentially a product of a
determinantal variety
, whose dimensions are well known
[
28
,
23
]
, with a vector space of rectangular matrices. We derive explicit dimension estimates for the sake of completeness, focusing on “worst case” upper bounds, depending on the parameters
\ell
and
I
. In the following, mentions of
dimension
refer to dimension over the field
\mathbb{C}
.

Fix
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
and
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
with
\sum_{i\in I}r_{i}>\ell
. To build a frame
F
in
\mathcal{U}_{\ell,I}(\vec{r})
, one could follow three steps:

1.

choose an
\ell
-dimensional subspace
V
of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
;

2.

choose
k
vectors
f_{i_{1}},\ldots,f_{i_{k}}
from
V
;

3.

fill out the rest of the frame with
n-k
vectors
f_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
,
j\not\in I
.

The space of
\ell
-dimensional subspaces is the Grassmann manifold, which has dimension
\ell(d-\ell)
, the selection of a single vector
f_{i_{j}}
from a given subspace
V
is an
\ell
-dimensional choice, and each choice of vector to fill the rest of the frame is a
d
-dimensional choice. Adding the dimensions involved in the construction yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{U}_{\ell,I}(\vec{r}))

\displaystyle=\ell(d-\ell)+k\cdot\ell+(n-k)\cdot d

\displaystyle=\ell\cdot d-\ell^{2}+n\cdot d-k(d-\ell).

(12)

The expression (
12
) is intended to emphasize the fact that the dimension is decreasing in
k
. The worst case for the upper bound is realized when
k
is as small as possible; that is, when the chosen weights are as large as possible,
r_{(1)},\ldots,r_{(k)}
(recall this notational convention, introduced just prior to
Definition

2.1
), which is the case where the indexing set
I
consists of the indices of the first
k
largest entries in
\vec{r}
. Let

k_{\ell}(\vec{r})\coloneqq\min\left\{k\mid\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{(i)}>\ell\right\}.

(13)

Together with (
12
), this gives

\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{U}_{\ell,I}(\vec{r}))\leq\ell\cdot d-\ell^{2}+n\cdot d-k_{\ell}(\vec{r})\cdot(d-\ell).

(14)

Next, we introduce the ansatz that there exists a universal (i.e., independent of
\ell
) constant
c>\frac{d}{n}
satisfying

k_{\ell}(\vec{r})\geq c\cdot\frac{n\ell}{d},\qquad\forall\;\ell\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}.

(15)

Such a
c
must exist. Indeed, by the admissibility condition, we must have
k_{\ell}(\vec{r})>\ell
, as we would otherwise obtain the contradiction

\ell<\sum_{i=1}^{k_{\ell}(\vec{r})}r_{(i)}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}r_{(i)}\leq\ell.

It follows that (assuming
d>1
)

k_{\ell}(\vec{r})\geq\ell+1=\frac{d(\ell+1)}{n\ell}\frac{n\ell}{d}\geq\frac{d^{2}}{n(d-1)}\frac{n\ell}{d},

so that
c=\frac{d^{2}}{n(d-1)}>\frac{d}{n}
has the desired properties. Depending on the choice of
\vec{r}
,
c
could be chosen to be much larger, so we leave it as a parameter to be manipulated later on.

Incorporating (
15
) into the dimension estimate (
16
), we have the following bound whenever
\ell>0
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{U}_{\ell,I}(\vec{r}))

\displaystyle\leq\ell\cdot d-\ell^{2}+n\cdot d-cn\ell+c\frac{n}{d}\ell^{2}

\displaystyle=\big(c\frac{n}{d}-1\big)\ell^{2}-(cn-d)\ell+nd.

(16)

The coefficient of
\ell^{2}
is positive, by our assumption on
c
, so that the worst case upper bound occurs at the extremal points of the domain of
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
. At both of these points (i.e.,
\ell=1
and
\ell=d-1
), (
16
) simplifies to

-(cn-d)\frac{d-1}{d}+nd.

On the other hand, if
\ell=0
, then
k_{\ell}(\vec{r})=1
, so that (
14
) immediately simplifies to

\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{U}_{0,I}(\vec{r}))\leq nd-d.

Putting these dimension estimates together, we have proved the following result. In the following, we emphasize the field of coefficients by writing
\mathcal{U}_{\ell,I}^{\mathbb{C}}(\vec{r})=\mathcal{U}_{\ell,I}(\vec{r})
,
\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{C}}(\vec{r})=\mathcal{U}(\vec{r})
and
\mathrm{codim}_{\mathbb{C}}
for codimension over
\mathbb{C}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Every stratum
\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{C}}_{\ell,I}(\vec{r})
of the unstable set
\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{C}}(\vec{r})
satisfies the codimension bound

\mathrm{codim}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{C}}_{\ell,I}(\vec{r}))\geq\min\left\{d,(cn-d)\frac{d-1}{d}\right\},

with
c
as in the ansatz (
15
).

The reason for emphasizing the field
\mathbb{C}
above is that we would like to extend our results to frames over
\mathbb{R}
. Accordingly, let

\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{R}}(\vec{r})\coloneqq\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{C}}(\vec{r})\cap\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},

where
\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
is included in the obvious way. It is straightforward to show that

\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{R}}(\vec{r})=\left\{F\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\mid\,\exists\,i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\,\mbox{ and }V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\mbox{s.t.}\,f_{i_{1}},\ldots,f_{i_{k}}\in V\,\mbox{ and }\sum_{j=1}^{k}r_{i_{j}}>\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}(V)\right\}.

Letting
\mathcal{U}_{\ell,I}^{\mathbb{R}}(\vec{r})
denote the associated strata, the codimension bound derivations above go through mutatis mutandis. Combining this observation with
4.2
leads to the following codimension bounds over the reals (treating
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
as real vector spaces).

Lemma 4.3
.

The strata of the unstable sets satisfy the codimension bounds

\mathrm{codim}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{C}}_{\ell,I}(\vec{r}))\geq 2\cdot\min\left\{d,(cn-d)\frac{d-1}{d}\right\},

(17)

and

\mathrm{codim}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{R}}_{\ell,I}(\vec{r}))\geq\min\left\{d,(cn-d)\frac{d-1}{d}\right\},

(18)

with
c
as in (
15
).

Remark 4.4
.

Either possibility on the right hand side of (
18
) can realize the minimum, depending on the choice of
\vec{r}
. We now show this explicitly.

Let
\vec{r}=(d/n,d/n,\ldots,d/n)
. Then we can take
c=1
in (
15
), and this choice is essentially optimal, so that

(cn-d)\frac{d-1}{d}=(n-d)\frac{d-1}{d}\geq d

holds whenever

n\geq\frac{d(2d-1)}{d-1}.

In this case, the bound (
17
) becomes
\mathrm{codim}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{R}}_{\ell,I}(\vec{r}))\geq d
.

On the other hand, let
r_{1}=\cdots=r_{d-1}=1
,
r_{d}=1-\epsilon
,
r_{d+1}=\cdots=r_{n}=\frac{\epsilon}{n-d}
, for some small
\epsilon
. Then
\vec{r}
is admissible, and (assuming
d>1
)

c=\frac{d^{2}}{n(d-1)}

is an essentially optimal choice of constant in (
15
) (see the discussion following (
15
)). In this case,

(cn-d)\frac{d-1}{d}=\left(\frac{d^{2}}{n(d-1)}n-d\right)\frac{d-1}{d}=1\leq d,

so that (
17
) becomes
\mathrm{codim}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{R}}_{\ell,I}(\vec{r}))\geq(cn-d)\frac{d-1}{d}
.

4.3
Vanishing Homotopy Groups for
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})

We’re now ready to state and prove our result on the topology of the
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
spaces, which is stated in terms of its homotopy groups
\pi_{q}(\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r}))
. In particular, we give conditions which imply that this is the trivial group—if this is the case, we say that
\operatorname{PF}_{d}(\vec{r})
is
q
-connected
. As special cases,
0
-connected is the same as being connected in the usual sense from point-set topology (which is the same as being path connected for algebraic varieties such as the frame spaces), and
1
-connected is typically referred to as being simply connected.

In this subsection, we distinguish between Parseval frames over different fields by writing
\mathrm{PF}_{d}^{\mathbb{K}}(\vec{r})
for the space of frames over the field
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
q
be a non-negative integer, let
\vec{r}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}_{+}
be admissible, and let
c
be a choice of constant satisfying (
15
) for
\vec{r}
.

1.

For real frames, suppose that

c\geq\frac{d}{n}\left(\frac{q+d+1}{d-1}\right)\quad\mbox{and}\quad d\geq q+2.

Then
\mathrm{PF}^{\mathbb{R}}_{d}(\vec{r})
is
q
-connected.

2.

For complex frames, suppose that

c\geq\frac{d}{2n}\left(\frac{q+d}{d-1}\right)\quad\mbox{and}\quad d\geq\frac{q+2}{2}.

Then
\mathrm{PF}^{\mathbb{C}}_{d}(\vec{r})
is
q
-connected.

Let
\mathcal{S}^{\mathbb{C}}(\vec{r})\subset\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
denote the
semistable set
, i.e., the complement of the unstable set
\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{C}}(\vec{r})
that was discussed in the previous subsections,

\mathcal{S}^{\mathbb{C}}(\vec{r})\coloneqq\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}\setminus\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{C}}(\vec{r}).

Equivalently,
\mathcal{S}^{\mathbb{C}}(\vec{r})
is the set of frames having property
\mathscr{S}_{\vec{r}}
. Let
\mathcal{S}^{\mathbb{R}}(\vec{r})
denote the subset of the semistable set consisting of real matrices, i.e.,

\mathcal{S}^{\mathbb{R}}(\vec{r})\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\setminus\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{R}}(\vec{r}).

The proof of
4.5
relies on the following lemma.

Lemma 4.6
.

Gradient descent of the total frame energy
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
(i.e., the flow of the differential equation in
2.8
) defines a strong deformation retract from
\mathcal{S}^{\mathbb{K}}(\vec{r})
onto the space of Parseval frames
\mathrm{PF}_{d}^{\mathbb{K}}(\vec{r})
. In particular,
\mathcal{S}^{\mathbb{K}}(\vec{r})
and
\mathrm{PF}_{d}^{\mathbb{K}}(\vec{r})
are homotopy equivalent.

Proof.

The complex case follows from the main result of
[
29
]
(an exposition of unpublished work of Duistermaat), which characterizes the gradient descent dynamics of squared norms of momentum maps on symplectic manifolds. This applies to the total frame energy, according to
Remark

2.5
. However, the result follows in our special case by an essentially elementary argument: the fact that gradient descent defines a well-defined map from
\mathcal{S}^{\mathbb{C}}(\vec{r})\times[0,\infty]
onto
\mathrm{PF}_{d}^{\mathbb{C}}(\vec{r})
, which fixes
\mathrm{PF}_{d}^{\mathbb{C}}(\vec{r})
, follows from
2.8
, and the continuity of this map then follows from the analytical arguments in
[
29
]
(see page 124 therein), which do not require any machinery from symplectic geometry. The real case follows by considering
\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
and observing that the gradient flow preserves this subspace.
∎

Proof of
4.5
.

Consider the real case of the theorem. As homotopy groups are invariant under homotopy equivalence,
4.6
tells us that it suffices to prove that the assumptions on
c
,
d
and
q
imply triviality of the homotopy groups of
\mathcal{S}^{\mathbb{R}}(\vec{r})
. We have that
\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{R}}(\vec{r})
is a union of algebraic varieties with codimensions bounded by the minimum of
d
and
(cn-d)\frac{d-1}{d}
. Our assumptions on
c
and
d
imply that the minimum is bounded below by
q+2
. By a general transversality argument (see
[
37
, Lemma 3.12]
for details), it follows that, for
d\geq q+2
,

\pi_{q}(\mathcal{S}^{\mathbb{R}}(\vec{r}))=\pi_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d\times n})=0.

The proof in the complex case is similar.
∎

4.4
Consequences

While
4.5
gives precise conditions for vanishing homotopy groups, the conditions themselves are not immediately easy to interpret. We now provide some direct corollaries of this result with more straightforward sufficient conditions.

We first show that spaces of equal norm Parseval frames can be forced to have trivial topology in any dimension by taking a large number of frame vectors.

Corollary 4.7
.

Fix a field
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
, a positive integer
q
and a dimension
d
with

d\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}q+2&\mbox{if }\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R},\\
\frac{q+2}{2}&\mbox{if }\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}.\end{array}\right.

The space of equal norm Parseval frames with
n
vectors in

The next result establishes the existence of several infinite families of vectors

such that the corresponding space of frames
\mathrm{PF}_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(\vec{r})

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
d\geq 2
and let
\vec{r}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{n}
be an admissible vector, such that the constant
c
from (
15

c\geq\frac{d}{n}\left(\frac{2d}{d-1}\right).

(19)

Then there exists
\epsilon>0
such that, for any admissible vector
\vec{s}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{n}
with
|s_{i}-r_{i}|<\epsilon
for all
i
, the space
\operatorname{PF}_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(\vec{s})
is path-connected.

The condition (
19
) holds for equal norm Parseval frames (i.e., where
r_{i}=\frac{d}{n}
, in which case we can take
c=1
) whenever
n\geq\frac{2d^{2}}{d-1}
. So this result, in particular, implies that there is an open (with respect to the subspace topology on
\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
) set of admissible vectors around the constant vector so that the associated frame space is path-connected.

Proof.

Let
\vec{s}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{n}
be admissible. We claim that, for each
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
, there exists
\epsilon_{\ell}>0
such that
|s_{i}-r_{i}|<\epsilon_{\ell}
, for all
i
, implies that

k_{\ell}(\vec{s})\geq k_{\ell}(\vec{r})-1,

with
k_{\ell}
defined as in (
13
). Indeed, supposing that
|s_{i}-r_{i}|<\epsilon_{\ell}
for some
\epsilon_{\ell}>0
, and that
k_{\ell}(\vec{s})<k_{\ell}(\vec{r})-1
, leads to

\ell<\sum_{i=1}^{k_{\ell}(\vec{r})-2}s_{(i)}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k_{\ell}(\vec{r})-2}r_{(i)}+(k_{\ell}(\vec{r})-2)\epsilon_{\ell},

which implies

\ell-(k_{\ell}(\vec{r})-2)\epsilon_{\ell}<\sum_{i=1}^{k_{\ell}(\vec{r})-2}r_{(i)}.

If such a vector
\vec{s}
existed for every choice of
\epsilon_{\ell}>0
, then we would deduce that

\ell\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k_{\ell}(\vec{r})-2}r_{(i)}<\sum_{i=1}^{k_{\ell}(\vec{r})-1}r_{(i)},

which gives a contradiction to the minimality of
k_{\ell}(\vec{r})
, so that our claim is established.

Now let
\epsilon>0
such that
|s_{i}-r_{i}|<\epsilon
for all
i
implies that
k_{\ell}(\vec{s})\geq k_{\ell}(\vec{r})-1
holds for all
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
(e.g., take
\epsilon
to be the minimum of the
\epsilon_{\ell}
’s whose existence was demonstrated above). Setting

c^{\prime}=c-\frac{d}{n},

we have, for any
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
,

c^{\prime}\cdot\frac{n\ell}{d}=\left(c-\frac{d}{n}\right)\cdot\frac{n\ell}{d}\leq\left(c-\frac{d}{n\ell}\right)\cdot\frac{n\ell}{d}=c\cdot\frac{n\ell}{d}-1\leq k_{\ell}(\vec{r})-1\leq k_{\ell}(\vec{s}).

Moreover, we have

c^{\prime}=c-\frac{d}{n}\geq\frac{d}{n}\left(\frac{2d}{d-1}\right)-\frac{d}{n}=\frac{d}{n}\left(\frac{d+1}{d-1}\right).

Therefore,
c^{\prime}
satisfies the ansatz (
15
) for the vector
\vec{s}
, as well as the bound in Theorem
4.5
which is sufficient for
\mathrm{PF}_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(\vec{s})
to be 0-connected, hence
\mathrm{PF}_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(\vec{s})
is path-connected.
∎

5
Discussion

An obvious avenue for improving
2.8
would be to show that the conclusion holds for all admissible
\vec{r}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
, not just the rational ones. This will require a completely different method of proof, though, since the
G
-action we’ve defined here is not algebraic when
\vec{r}
is not rational. That said, it seems likely that
2.9
(our version of the Benedetto–Fickus theorem) can be proved for irrational
\vec{r}
using approaches similar to those employed in Sections 2 and 3 of
[
33
]
.

Another way of generalizing
2.8
would be to characterize the limiting behavior of the negative gradient flow of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
when
\vec{r}
is
not
admissible. The dependence on
\vec{r}
seems to be non-trivial, and we have not been able to formulate a coherent conjecture.

In our definition of
{{E_{\vec{r}}}}
, we could have replaced
\mathbb{I}_{d}
in the term
\|FF^{\ast}-\mathbb{I}_{d}\|_{\text{Fr}}^{2}
by any desired positive-definite matrix
S
. Under appropriate admissibility assumptions, does the negative gradient flow of such a modified energy function limit to frames
F
with frame operator
FF^{\ast}=S
and squared frame vector norms
\|f_{i}\|^{2}=r_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
? The answer seems to be yes in small-scale experiments, but there are some technical challenges in extending our proof to this case.

As with the original Frame Homotopy Conjecture,
4.8
and
4.9
are potentially useful in that they imply that there is a way of continuously interpolating between any two Parseval frames with the same frame vector norms. With an eye to
4.5
and
4.7
, are there any practical consequences of
q
-connectedness of
\operatorname{PF}_{d}^{\mathbb{K}}(\vec{r})
when
q>0
? For example, simple connectedness implies that if we have two different interpolations between Parseval frames, we can continuously interpolate between these interpolations. Also, simple connectedness implies that all closed 1-forms are exact, so line integrals involving closed 1-forms are automatically path-independent.
