The early-onset Alzheimer’s disease MRI signature: a replication and extension analysis in early-stage AD
Authors: Rashi I Mehta, Cierra M Keith, Camila Vieira Ligo Teixeira, Patrick D Worhunsky, Holly E Phelps, Melanie Ward, Mark Miller, R Osvaldo Navia, Stephanie Pockl, Nafiisah Rajabalee, Michelle M Coleman, Pierre-François D’Haese, Ali R Rezai, Kirk C Wilhelmsen, Marc W Haut
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly-onset Alzheimer’s disease (EOAD) is less investigated than the more common late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (LOAD) despite its more aggressive course. A cortical signature of EOAD was recently proposed and may facilitate EOAD investigation. Here, we aimed to validate this proposed MRI biomarker of EOAD neurodegeneration in an Appalachian clinical cohort. We also compared differences in EOAD signature atrophy in participants with biomarker-positive EOAD, LOAD, early-onset non-AD pathologies, and cognitively normal individuals. Cortical thinning was reliably detected in eight of nine signature areas of persons with EOAD relative to cognitively normal individuals despite very early disease stage. Additionally, individuals with EOAD showed thinner cortex in most signature regions relative to those with early-onset non-AD pathologies. EOAD and LOAD showed similar cortical atrophy within most EOAD signature regions. Whole-brain vertex-wise cortical analyses supported these findings. Furthermore, signature cortical atrophy showed expected relationships with measures of global and specific cognitive and functional status. This investigation further validates and expands upon the recently defined EOAD signature and suggests its robustness within a rural population, even at early disease stage. Larger scale and longitudinal studies employing this marker of EOAD neurodegeneration are needed to further understand clinical effects and appropriate management of persons with EOAD.Keywords: MRI, early-onset Alzheimer’s disease, cortical thickness, amyloid, neuroimaging

Introduction

Early-onset Alzheimer’s disease (EOAD), defined as symptomatic onset before age 65 years, accounts for ~5% of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) cases (Zhu et al. 2015). Despite that individuals with EOAD suffer a particularly devastating form of the disorder (Jacobs et al. 1994; Koss et al. 1996; Mendez, 2017), EOAD is less well investigated and understood than the more typical late-onset form of AD (LOAD). Five to 10 percent of individuals with EOAD carry a dominantly inherited mutation (i.e. APP, PSEN1, or PSEN2) that is known to cause early aggregation of amyloid-β (Aβ), while greater than 90% of EOAD cases are presumed to be sporadic. Numerous barriers to EOAD research exist, and investigation of sporadic EOAD is particularly lacking (Reitz et al. 2020). In addition to younger age at onset, persons with EOAD exhibit a more aggressive disease course and more commonly present with nonmemory impairments such as attention, language, visuospatial, and executive dysfunction (Mendez, 2017; Mendez, 2019). Further, individuals with EOAD generally harbor a greater pathologic burden when compared to those with LOAD (Bigio et al. 2002; Marshall et al. 2007; Cho et al. 2017; Schöll et al. 2017). Given known phenotypic and pathogenic distinctions from LOAD and genetically inherited forms of AD (Llibre-Guerra et al. 2023), persons with sporadic EOAD are commonly excluded from large-scale observational, longitudinal, and therapeutic investigations such as the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative and the Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer’s Network studies (Reitz et al. 2020). Considering these factors, it is imperative to conduct clinical research and trial investigations, specifically among sporadic EOAD cohorts. However, limited knowledge regarding this AD subtype and the absence of specific diagnostic biomarkers for EOAD have been limitations to this end.

In seminal work by Dickerson et al. a “cortical signature” of LOAD was put forth as an MRI biomarker of AD neurodegeneration (Dickerson et al. 2009). Regional cortical atrophy within this MRI signature has been shown to reliably detect neurodegeneration even at very early LOAD disease stage and has proven to be impactful in predicting progressive decline to dementia in persons with prodromal and preclinical LOAD (Bakkour et al. 2009; Dickerson et al. 2009; Dickerson et al. 2011; Dickerson et al. 2012). Subsequent studies have aimed to understand differences in spatial topography and temporal progression of structural neurodegeneration between EOAD and LOAD (Cho et al. 2013; Möller et al. 2013; Migliaccio et al. 2015; Eckerström et al. 2018). These works provide evidence to suggest that EOAD and LOAD may not necessarily reflect distinct nosological entities (Migliaccio et al. 2015; Eckerström et al. 2018). Eckerström et al. showed that while both groups are characterized by smaller hippocampal volumes relative to healthy controls, no significant differences in regional atrophy between EOAD and LOAD were found after correcting for age. While hippocampal volumes differ between groups, being more diminutive in LOAD, this was attributable to greater age-related atrophy in subjects with LOAD (Eckerström et al. 2018). Migliaccio et al. showed that while distinctions in baseline atrophy are present between EOAD and LOAD, the progression of structural atrophy between groups was similar, predominantly affecting regions involved in the default mode network, primarily the precuneus and dorsal cingulate gyrus (Migliaccio et al. 2015). More rapid neurodegeneration and clinical decline in EOAD (Cho et al. 2013) have been hypothesized to reflect faster disease progression in a subset of diseased individuals with greater cognitive reserve (Bigio et al. 2002; Migliaccio et al. 2015).

To further assess neurodegeneration among individuals with sporadic EOAD, cortical morphometric analysis was recently performed by the longitudinal early-onset Alzheimer’s disease study (LEADS) consortium. Touroutoglou et al. systematically evaluated the MRI pattern of cortical atrophy among two separate cohorts, the Massachusetts General Hospital cohort (MGH; n = 25) and the larger, ongoing multicenter LEADS cohort (n = 211)—notably the largest United States EOAD sample to date (Touroutoglou et al. 2023). In this recent study, investigators created whole-cortex vertex-wise two-class general linear models in FreeSurfer to identify areas of cerebral cortical atrophy in biomarker-positive EOAD subjects relative to cognitively normal (CN) controls. Using a high statistical significance threshold (i.e. P < 0.0001) and a 0.2 mm threshold difference in cortical thickness relative to control subjects, EOAD-signature maps were then created for both cohorts. The investigators found that the EOAD signature, defined by nine anatomic regions of interest (ROIs) demarcating regionally advanced cortical atrophy in the left cerebral hemisphere, was reproducible across the two independent patient cohorts. Additionally, the EOAD signature was associated with the severity of cognitive impairment, measured by the clinical dementia rating-sum of boxes (i.e. CDR-SB) and Mini-Mental State Exam (i.e. MMSE). The authors found that the EOAD signature was superior in detecting EOAD-related neurodegeneration compared to established MRI biomarkers of AD, including volumetric measures of the hippocampus (Touroutoglou et al. 2023).

In the current study, we aim to validate the utility of the proposed EOAD signature by replicating analysis in our clinic population. In our distinct cohort, we will examine the EOAD cortical signature among persons with EOAD, other early-onset non-AD pathologies (EOnonAD), and CN individuals. In addition, we will test the novel hypothesis that EOAD MRI signature atrophy overlaps between individuals with EOAD and LOAD. Thus, we aim to extend upon the mission of the LEADS consortium by further defining the clinical, imaging, and biomarker characteristics of EOAD by including comparisons with LOAD, EOnonAD, and CN participants of a well-characterized rural multidisciplinary outpatient memory clinic cohort. We also aim to provide further clinically valid evidence of relationships between EOAD MRI biomarkers and global and specific cognitive impairment, as well as functional status.

Materials and methods

Participants

We retrospectively reviewed the records of patients seen consecutively in the Memory Health Clinic at the Rockefeller Neuroscience Institute (Morgantown, West Virginia) from June of 2020 through March 2024 to identify individuals with biomarker testing for the presence of Aβ by cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) or 18F-florbetaben amyloid PET analysis. We included all individuals who were Aβ biomarker positive and underwent 3 T MRI scanning that included a high-resolution 3D T1-weighted sequence. Persons with any prior cortical infarction, a primary psychiatric diagnosis, or another neurologic diagnosis driving the symptomatology were excluded. Aβ biomarker positivity was determined by SUVr ≥1.18 (Villeneuve et al. 2015) using the anatomically parcellated average of the LOAD signature areas on amyloid PET (Dickerson et al. 2009). This is the cutoff utilized by the LEADS study (Touroutoglou et al. 2023). CSF values of Aβ42 < 1026 before May 2023 or < 834 afterwards, or a pTau/Aβ ratio of > 0.026 before May 2023 or > 0.028 after also defined Aβ positivity. Individuals who were Aβ biomarker positive and had symptom onset before the age of 65 years were included in the EOAD group (n = 64). For comparison purposes, we also included individuals who were Aβ biomarker negative and had symptom onset before age 65 into the EOnonAD group (n = 40) and those who were Aβ positive with symptom onset after the age of 65 into the LOAD group (n = 85). All clinical participants had evidence of cognitive symptoms on standard neuropsychological examination and were diagnosed by multidisciplinary consensus with either mild cognitive impairment or dementia (Albert et al. 2011; McKhann et al. 2011). We did not screen for known genetic mutations for dominantly inherited EOAD. We also included a group of CN participants aged 65 or younger (n = 37) with a normal neuropsychological examination and normal MRI. All participants signed an informed consent form approved by the Institutional Review Board at West Virginia University.

Cognitive and functional measures

We used the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE; Folstein et al. 1975) as a measure of overall cognition and the Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ; Pfeffer et al. 1982) as a measure of functional status as reported by an informant. Both raw scores are out of 30, but lower scores on the MMSE indicate greater impairment, while higher scores on the FAQ indicate greater impairment. We included three additional cognitive measures: total number of words recalled after 20 min of learning a set of words in four trials on the California Verbal Learning Test-Short Form (CVLT-SF long delay free recall; Delis et al. 2000), total number of animals named in 60 s (Animal Naming; Henry et al. 2004) and time to complete Trail Making Test part B (Reitan, 1985) with a maximum time allowed of 300 s unless they were within 2 items of completing the task. Each measure represents a specific aspect of cognitive functioning typically impaired in AD—delayed memory recall, semantic language, and executive functions. These single measures robustly correlate with the LOAD signature areas in our sample (Coleman et al. 2023). For descriptive purposes, we also used the Wide Range Achievement Test-4 (WRAT-4; Wilkinson and Robertson, 2006) reading subtest standard score as an estimate of premorbid intelligence and the Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire (NPI-Q; Kaufer et al. 2000) as an indicator of neuropsychiatric symptoms as reported by an informant.

MRI acquisition

All participants completed MRI scanning that included a structural 3D T1-weighted MRI sequence for measurement of cortical thickness. MRI data were obtained on either a Siemens Magnetom Prisma 3 T scanner with a 20-channel head coil (MPRAGE; spatial resolution: at least 1 × 1 × 1 mm; TR = 2300 ms; TE = 2.26 ms; n = 204) or a GE Architect 3 T scanner with a 48-channel head coil using a comparable 3D T1 sequence (BRAVO SPGR; spatial resolution: at least 1 × 1 × 1 mm; TR = 8.5 ms; TE = 3.3 ms; n = 22). We have previously shown no differences in cortical thickness measurements between these MRI systems (Coleman et al. 2023); however, we included scanner type as a covariate in all analyses.

MRI processing

Cortical thickness was measured using FreeSurfer v6.0 (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu). All images were manually inspected for motion and artifact. High resolution T1-weighted images passing quality checks were processed following the methods described by Touroutoglou et al. Images underwent intensity normalization, skull stripping, and automated segmentation to demarcate the gray and white matter boundaries and pial surfaces. Each hemisphere was visually inspected for errors, and manual corrections were performed as needed. Cortical thickness was calculated as the closest distance from the gray/white matter boundary to the gray matter/CSF boundary at each surface vertex. The Desikan Killiany atlas (Desikan et al. 2006) was used to identify ROIs corresponding to the cortical signature areas identified by Touroutoglou et al.: middle temporal, inferior temporal, fusiform, superior frontal, middle frontal, posterior cingulate, precuneus, superior parietal lobule, and inferior parietal gyri. The pericalcarine region was assessed as a control region. We examined the left, right, and the average of each of the individual signature ROIs across hemispheres. In addition to cortical analysis of each ROI, we calculated the average cortical thickness across all nine signature areas (i.e. the EOAD signature composite).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). We used ANOVA for continuous demographic and clinical variables and Chi-square for categorical data (sex, APOE4). We used ANOVA to examine group differences for cortical thickness with significant main effects, followed by post hoc least significant difference testing. We calculated all comparisons, but in this text, we report on the following specific group comparisons: EOAD vs. CN, EOAD vs. LOAD, and EOAD vs. EOnonAD. Significance was set at P < 0.002 using Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (0.05/27 = 0.0019). Pearson’s product–moment correlations were used to examine the relationship between signature cortical thickness and overall cognition, delayed recall, semantic language, executive functions, and functional status. The significance threshold for these correlations was set at P < 0.012 using Bonferroni correction for multiple correlations.

Whole-cortex vertex-wise analysis

A whole-cortex vertex-wise general linear model was generated in FreeSurfer to analyze topographical cortical thickness differences between the four groups of participants. Each participant’s cortical thickness map was first registered to a spatial template (fsaverage) and smoothed at a 15 mm full-width half-maximum. Between-group comparisons were performed to detect differences in cortical thickness at a statistical significance threshold of P < 0.0001 and a Monte Carlo z-field simulation cluster threshold of P < 0.05. Post hoc examination was then performed at a vertex significance threshold of P < 0.01 to explore group differences at a less conservative threshold.

Results

Participants

Group demographics are displayed in Table 1. Among the EOAD group, four individuals were found to have primary progressive aphasia (PPA), eight had executive subtype, two had executive subtype with PPA, eight had posterior cortical atrophy (PCA), and 42 had typical (classic) memory presentation of EOAD. As expected, a large age difference between EOAD and LOAD groups was observed. There was no difference in sex distribution between groups. The groups were also well-matched for education and estimated premorbid intelligence. There was no difference between EOAD and EOnonAD for neuropsychiatric symptoms, and both groups presented more neuropsychiatric symptoms compared to LOAD. The clinical groups were not different in terms of the frequency of MCI versus dementia. The presence of apolipoprotein E ε4 allele (APOE4) was higher in both AD groups relative to EOnonAD but did not differ between EOAD and LOAD. There was a small difference in overall cognition, measured by MMSE, between EOAD and EOnonAD, but no difference between the AD groups (Table 1). CVLT-SF delayed recall was similar between EOAD and LOAD, but EOAD performed worse on this measure than EOnonAD. For Animal Naming and Trail Making Test part B, EOAD and EOnonAD showed similar performance, different from the LOAD group, which displayed worse performance in both tests. Functional status did not differ among the clinical groups. Time intervals from symptom onset to clinical evaluation, symptom onset to MRI, and symptom onset to biomarker testing are listed in Table 1. There was no difference between EOAD and LOAD groups for any of the CSF and Aβ PET biomarkers (see Table 2 for specific comparisons).

EOAD vs. CN

Signature region comparison: EOAD had a thinner cortex relative to CN participants in the EOAD signature area composite and in all signature ROIs except the middle frontal gyrus (Fig. 1; Table 3). Bihemispheric signature ROI analysis revealed the greatest degree of gyral thinning in the precuneus and inferior parietal regions, where the cortical thickness differed by 0.12 and 0.11 mm, respectively, (5%) between groups (Table 3). The greatest magnitudes of cortical thinning in EOAD relative to CN, detected by unilateral signature ROI analysis, were in the precuneus and inferior parietal cortical regions of the right cerebral hemisphere where average cortical thickness differed by 0.13 mm (5%) and 0.14 mm (6%), respectively, between the groups (Supplemental Table 1).

EOAD vs. LOAD

Signature region comparison: EOAD and LOAD showed no difference in cortical thickness of the signature area composite and for all individual signature ROIs except the middle temporal gyrus, where LOAD showed a thinner cortex than EOAD (Table 3).

Whole-brain vertex-wise comparison: As shown in Fig. 3, vertex-wise comparison revealed similar patterns of cortical atrophy in EOAD and LOAD, with greater cortical thinning involving the temporal poles, specifically the rostral-most aspect of the superior temporal gyri of both the right and left hemispheres, in LOAD compared to EOAD. EOAD showed slightly greater cortical atrophy in the rostral middle frontal gyrus of the right hemisphere (Fig. 3).

Cortical thickness relationships with cognition and functional status

There was a statistically significant relationship between the cortical thickness of the EOAD signature composite and overall cognition, as measured by MMSE, with a thicker cortex being associated with better cognition (r = 0.33, P < 0.001). Similarly, each of the specific cognitive measures (CVLT-SF delayed recall, Animal Naming, and Trail Making Test part B) had a statistically significant relationship with EOAD signature composite cortical thickness, with thicker cortex associated with better performance (CVLT-SF delayed recall r = 0.35, P < 0.001, Animal Naming r = 0.42, P < 0.001, and Trail Making Test part B r = −0.48, P < 0.001). The correlation with Trail Making Test part B is inverse, as a shorter duration to complete the task (e.g. the raw score of the number of seconds) indicates better performance. There was also a significant relationship between EOAD signature composite atrophy and functional status, as measured by FAQ (r = −0.26, P = 0.001; see Fig. 5).

Discussion

The cortical EOAD signature, an MRI-based biomarker of neurodegeneration in EOAD, was recently proposed by the LEADS consortium (Touroutoglou et al. 2023). This MRI biomarker consists of cortical atrophy involving parietal and posterior temporal cortices with relative sparing of the medial temporal lobes (Touroutoglou et al. 2023). In the present study, we aimed to replicate and further validate the utility of this signature as a diagnostic indicator of EOAD by analysis in a rural Appalachian memory clinic population. Despite the mild degree of cognitive deficits, we show that the cortical EOAD signature reliably differentiated biomarker-positive EOAD (n = 64) from healthy control participants (n = 37) among our cohort. We further demonstrate that EOAD signature atrophy correlates with the level of cognitive impairment as measured by MMSE, concordant with previous findings (Touroutoglou et al. 2023), and with specific measures of memory (CVLT-SF delayed recall), semantic language (Animal Naming), and executive function (Trail Making Test part B), as well as with functional status (FAQ). Thus, this study further supports the validity and robustness of the EOAD signature as an MRI biomarker for EOAD diagnosis, even at early AD disease stage.

Similar to the analysis by Touroutoglou et al. cortical atrophy in EOAD relative to healthy participants was greatest in parietal and lateral temporal regions, with gyral thinning of the precuneus and inferior parietal lobule more advanced than that of middle and inferior temporal gyri. Less pronounced cortical atrophy was observed in the prefrontal regions compared to healthy participants, as was also noted in the analysis by the LEADS consortium. Nevertheless, compared to controls, cortical atrophy was reliably detected in eight of the nine EOAD signature regions of our EOAD group. This sensitivity was maintained with no differences between the groups for sex, education, and estimated premorbid intelligence. As was also seen in the prior LEADS study, no difference in cortical thickness was found between EOAD and healthy subjects in the control anatomic site (i.e. pericalcarine cortex).

Differing from the results of the LEADS study, we report cortical thickness differences between EOAD and healthy controls when evaluating the EOAD signature composite over both the right and left hemispheres. Touroutoglou et al. defined the EOAD signature within the left cerebral hemisphere only due to overall greater regional cortical atrophy detection in the left cerebrum. Our data support that EOAD signature atrophy is detectable and similar in degree when evaluated in aggregate in each hemisphere. This is consistent with prior literature suggesting that neurodegeneration in EOAD may also predominantly involve the right cerebral hemisphere (Kim et al. 2005); however, variation in unilateral hemispheric involvement has been reported between variant AD phenotypes (Mendez, 2017). It may be that with larger samples that seek to compare structural neurodegeneration between different AD subtypes, for example, language variant versus posterior cortical atrophy, hemispheric differences could emerge.

It is important to note that our study population consisted of individuals with an overall lesser magnitude of cognitive impairment than the MGH and LEADS cohorts. The average MMSE was 26.7 among our Appalachian EOAD participants versus 21.9 among the LEADS participants and 19.7 among the MGH participants (Touroutoglou et al. 2023). Thus, our analysis suggests that the EOAD MRI signature pattern of neurodegeneration manifests early in EOAD disease course. This is concordant with prior investigations of the MRI signature of neurodegeneration in persons with LOAD, which have demonstrated similar but milder regional cortical thinning effects in signature regions of persons with clinically milder forms of AD (Dickerson et al. 2009). In fact, a 10–20% loss of signature cortical thickness was detected at very early clinical stages of LOAD and even in asymptomatic individuals nearly a decade prior to a dementia diagnosis (Dickerson et al. 2011). Thus, while we found a lower magnitude of cortical thinning (i.e. generally less than 0.1 mm cortical thickness loss relative to controls among our study population compared to at least 0.2 mm cortical thinning identified in LEADS participants), this cortical thinning effect was reliably detectable in our cohort throughout the EOAD signature regions. This difference in magnitude is suspected to reflect an earlier stage of disease in our sample. The greatest magnitude of cortical thinning in EOAD detected by bihemispheric ROI analysis in our cohort was in the precuneus and inferior parietal cortical regions, where the gray matter was regionally thinned by 5% relative to healthy controls, which was similar in degree compared to the LOAD group despite expected differences in age.

Distinct neurobiological differences between EOAD and LOAD are known to exist; however, the factors driving neurodegeneration in EOAD are poorly understood (Sakamoto et al. 2002; Rabinovici et al. 2010; Ossenkoppele et al. 2012; Frontzkowski et al. 2022). Our analysis showed minimal difference in regional cortical atrophy between persons with EOAD (n = 64) versus LOAD (n = 85) within each of the EOAD signature ROIs except in the middle temporal gyrus, which showed mildly greater regional thinning among persons with LOAD. Whole brain vertex-wise comparisons also revealed similar patterns of cortical atrophy. However, LOAD showed greater cortical thinning focally in the bilateral temporal poles whereas EOAD showed slightly greater atrophy in the rostral middle frontal gyrus of the right hemisphere. In addition to similar patterns of structural neurodegeneration, the older and younger onset AD groups also showed similar performances (using raw scores not corrected for age) on tests of memory and executive functioning, though LOAD performed worse than EOAD in semantic fluency. Furthermore, the early and late-onset AD groups did not differ in CSF or amyloid PET biomarkers. Thus, on our analysis, the LOAD and EOAD groups showed more similarities than differences in cognitive performances, cortical atrophy, and amyloid and tau biomarkers despite differing ages of onset. Given that aging effects in LOAD have previously been examined, in addition to our limited sample size, we did not control for age and it is noteworthy that these groups appeared similar within that context. Our cortical morphometric analysis further supports the concept that EOAD and LOAD may reflect similar underlying driving pathologic mechanisms (Eckerström et al. 2018; Migliaccio et al. 2015) despite differences in timing of disease onset.

We here also compared cortical atrophy in individuals with EOAD versus those with early-onset non-AD pathologies (EOnonAD) and found thinner cortex in EOAD within all EOAD signature regions except for the middle temporal gyrus. Whole brain vertex-wise comparisons showed a thinner cortex primarily in parietal regions (precuneus and inferior parietal lobule) and in a few frontal regions (left medial orbitofrontal and right rostral middle frontal gyri) in EOAD relative to EOnonAD. It is of interest that EOAD showed a thinner parietal cortex than EOnonAD, similar to the findings of our previous analysis comparing AD and behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (Keith et al. 2024). However, in the current sample, the distribution of cortical thinning in EOAD also differed from that in EOnonAD in most EOAD signature regions, further supporting the specificity of this regional atrophy pattern in differentiating AD from non-AD pathology. The EOnonAD group unexpectedly showed no significant differences upon comparison with healthy controls. This finding could be impacted by the heterogeneity within the EOnonAD group as well as the relatively milder degree of cognitive impairment (the EOnonAD group showed higher MMSE scores compared to EOAD). Nonetheless, participants in the EOnonAD group all displayed clinical features and aspects of biomarker testing concerning for developing neurodegenerative conditions such as frontotemporal dementia and Lewy body dementia. Clearly, further work is needed to understand the pathological processes within the EOnonAD group.

The spatial topography of cortical neurodegeneration in our sample adds to the growing literature suggesting that AD pathophysiology occurs primarily along the default mode network (Palmqvist et al. 2017; Tanner et al. 2022; Touroutoglou et al. 2023; Katsumi et al. 2024). Tanner et al. recently demonstrated that tau accumulation (measured by 18F-flortaucipir PET) and neurodegeneration (measured by FDG-PET hypometabolism) similarly localize and correlate with cognitive dysfunction in EOAD and LOAD. EOAD patients had greater tau and neurodegeneration in EOAD signature regions than LOAD (Tanner et al. 2022). More recently, Katsumi et al. evaluated the relationship between tau PET and the rate of clinical decline in individuals with atypical AD phenotypes with early-stage AD. The authors found that tau burden in the major nodes of the default mode network, including the angular gyrus, precuneus, and posterior cingulate cortex, predicted faster clinical decline, outperforming the magnitude of cortical atrophy in predictive value. However, baseline cortical thickness of the default mode network combined with tau accumulation within this network most strongly predicted cognitive deterioration (Katsumi et al. 2024). Overall, the literature supports that temporal progression summarized by the A/T/N framework (Jack et al. 2016) occurs predominantly along this default mode functional network, with recent work also supporting early inflammation along the network (Appleton et al. 2024). Although regional tau accumulation has been demonstrated to be a more powerful predictor in early-stage disease (Katsumi et al. 2024) and is suggested to be the main contributor to cognitive decline in EOAD (Cho et al. 2024), our findings suggest that EOAD signature cortical thinning is also a detectable imaging feature of early-stage EOAD. Future longitudinal studies employing this MRI biomarker for assessment of EOAD neurodegeneration may be useful for clarifying the risk of AD dementia and predicting the time to onset. Additionally, studies incorporating this imaging biomarker may clarify appropriate patient selection for potential interventions and elucidate distinct variations in the safety and efficacy of current and future therapeutic options among persons with EOAD.

Several limitations of our study should be noted. Small sample size limits our analysis; however, our EOAD sample of 64 patients is more than double the size of the single-institution cohort (i.e. the MGH cohort) examined previously (Touroutoglou et al. 2023). Our cohort consisted almost entirely of White individuals representing the Appalachian rural population; analysis of the EOAD signature MRI biomarker in diverse cohorts is needed. Additionally, our CN group was defined without the acquisition of CSF or PET biomarkers for Aβ and tau, and thus, some of the healthy controls may have been biomarker-positive. Despite this limitation, we did observe significant differences between EOAD and CN individuals. Our EOAD cohort was not screened for genetic mutations; therefore, few persons with specific predisposing genetic mutations may have been included. Future studies are needed to evaluate the utility of the EOAD signature, specifically among persons with genetic predisposing factors for EOAD. Finally, longitudinal studies evaluating regional cortical atrophy over the disease course of EOAD, with reference to normal aging, are needed to better understand temporal biologic changes of EOAD-related neurodegeneration.

Conclusion

A structural MRI biomarker of LOAD was previously defined (Dickerson et al. 2009) and has established reliability in predicting progressive decline in persons with prodromal and preclinical AD. A regional atrophy signature of EOAD was recently proposed and may serve a similar important role in patients with EOAD. Our analysis supports the validity of EOAD signature atrophy as a diagnostic indicator of EOAD-related neurodegeneration and supports the utility of this biomarker in persons with early-stage EOAD. This work also further supports the robustness of this signature against site differences and in an aging rural population. Furthermore, the current study expands on prior research by evaluating EOAD signature associations with measures of specific cognitive function and by comparing EOAD with LOAD and EOnonAD. Future studies that assess EOAD using this feasible and automated approach may enable larger-scale and longitudinal analyses and clarify the appropriateness of this MRI biomarker in patient counseling, selection for putative therapies, and monitoring of disease course.
