Food allergy knowledge and attitudes among university students in Palestine: a cross-sectional study of determinants and educational needs
Authors: Ruaa Sleimieh, Fadwa Nasri, Hidaya Ibrahim, Dania Abuhalima, Muna Shakhshir, Sari Taha, Banan M Aiesh, Samah W Al-Jabi, Sa’ed H Zyoud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFood allergy (FA) is an immune-mediated reaction that can cause anything from minor symptoms such as skin rashes to severe, potentially fatal anaphylaxis. Little is known about FA knowledge and management among Palestinian university students, despite the abundance of studies on the condition in pediatric populations. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is one of the first studies to evaluate FA knowledge and attitudes, particularly among Palestinian university students, with the goal of identifying important gaps and investigating behavioral and sociodemographic factors that affect their understanding.MethodsFrom September 2024 to May 2025, a cross-sectional, single-center study was conducted at An-Najah National University in Nablus, Palestine. A total of 424 students from a variety of academic fields were recruited via a convenience sampling technique, and the sample size was determined on the basis of feasibility and the population that was accessible. A questionnaire that was delivered by the interviewer and modified for cultural relevance was taken from the Chicago Food Allergy Research Survey. Demographic characteristics, FA knowledge (18 items), attitudes (9 Likert scale items), and relevant clinical history were all evaluated in four sections of the measure. Both descriptive and inferential statistical analyses were conducted, and a composite knowledge score was produced.ResultsAmong the 424 participants (mean age 21 ± 2 years), 68.6% (n = 291) were female. The overall median knowledge score was 52.4% (IQR = 42.9–61.9%). The knowledge of female students was much greater than that of male students (p < 0.001). Additionally, the knowledge scores varied by field of study, with students in the Doctor of Pharmacy (median rank = 243.91) and Medicine (median rank = 240.68) programs demonstrating greater knowledge (p = 0.034). The knowledge scores were significantly higher for those who ate more meals outside the home (p = 0.007). Other variables, including marital status, income, residence, type of residence, and prior training, were not significantly associated with knowledge scores.ConclusionsUniversity students demonstrated multiple knowledge gaps and positive attitudes toward food allergies. Targeted interventions are needed to address these knowledge gaps by adopting a peer-led approach for better outreach strengthened through collaboration with university-based public health institutions and student associations. These interventions should be complemented by institutional safety measures and management protocols implemented in universities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25637-1.Keywords: Food allergy, Health literacy, Public health, Health belief model, Palestine, University students

Background

Food allergy (FA) is an immune response usually caused by specific food proteins [1]. Symptoms can occur from limited amounts of allergenic foods, which can lead to symptoms ranging from mild skin reactions to severe, life-threatening anaphylaxis [2, 3]. Acute management mainly involves recognizing the allergic reaction and quickly administering epinephrine [4].

FA is known to affect pediatric patients, as most cases appear and disappear during childhood [5, 6]. However, in some cases, FA can continue or appear during adulthood. This requires different approaches than those provided to children, especially university students, who constitute a considerable proportion of young adults and usually have new lifestyles that may affect the management of FAs [7]. Globally, the prevalence rates of FAs among university students’ range between 9.6% and 15.7% [8, 9].

Assessing FA knowledge and attitudes among university students is particularly important for several reasons. First, at this time, students frequently move from being teenagers, when their family takes care of them, to being young adults, when they are in charge of their own health and everyday decisions. Additionally, An-Najah students who live away from home or spend most of their time on campus must also be able to handle possible allergic reactions on their own, notice early signs, and know how to act in an emergency [10, 11]. Additionally, there are many social events in college, which makes it more likely that you will come into contact with food allergies. Improper treatment of FA at university can lead to more than just physical problems; it can also damage academic performance, mental health, and overall quality of life because of the stress of academic life [12, 13].

Given these difficulties, increasing health literacy is essential for helping students with FAs make educated decisions, lower risks, and create a safer campus environment. However, to improve health literacy among a particular population, the current level of knowledge and prevailing attitudes should be explored to optimize health interventions to address existing knowledge gaps on the basis of learning preferences. Previous studies have identified determinants such as gender, field of study, and prior FA training as significant factors influencing knowledge and attitudes toward food allergies [8, 14]. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, only one study has assessed knowledge and attitudes related to FA in Palestine, which targeted parents in particular and revealed multiple knowledge gaps [14]. This study aimed to assess students’ knowledge and attitudes toward food allergies and to identify sociodemographic and behavioral factors associated with these outcomes.

Methods

Study design, setting and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted at An-Najah National University (ANNU) in Nablus, Palestine, from September 2024 to May 2025 via an interviewer-administered questionnaire. The study included students who enrolled in any academic program during the abovementioned period. The authors have reviewed the STROBE Statement checklist and ensured that the manuscript was prepared and revised in accordance with its guidelines (Additional File 1).

Sample size and calculation

Because no published data are available on the prevalence of food allergies among Palestinian university students, the prevalence reported in earlier studies of Palestinian children (12.3%) was the best available estimate for sample size calculation [14]. The calculation was based on the total student population of the ANNU, which is approximately 25,000 students [15]. The standard formula for calculating sample size was applied: n = (Z² × P × (1 − P))/E², where n = the required sample size, Z = the standard score (1.96 for 95% confidence), P = the expected prevalence (0.123), and E = the margin of error (0.05) [16]. A minimum of 165 students was required for the sample. As many people as possible were recruited to maximize precision, reduce the impact of missing data, and strengthen the study’s power. The total sample size was 380 participants.

The participants were recruited via a convenience sampling method. Although this approach may limit generalizability to all university students, the final sample exceeded the minimum calculated sample size and included students from diverse academic programs and years, improving its representativeness within ANNU.

Measures and data collection

Data were collected via the Chicago Food Allergy Research Survey for the General Public [17], which was originally developed on the basis of the health belief model by Gupta et al. [17, 18]. An additional file has been provided that contains a detailed description of the study data collection form (instrument) in English (Additional File 2). A group of academic and professional experts reviewed, cross-checked, and improved the language and cultural appropriateness of the instrument before it was tested with 30 students to assess its clarity, cultural relevance, and comprehension and improve readability and understanding. Only minor modifications in the wording of the items were made after the content and face validity evaluation. While the Cronbach’s alpha values for the knowledge (α = 0.507) and attitude (α = 0.566) sections suggest moderate internal consistency, they are still considered acceptable for exploratory research. This is particularly true when the questionnaire includes a range of items designed to capture various aspects of a broad issue, such as attitudes or knowledge about FA. The multidimensional structure of the scales, which address a range of areas such as symptoms, management, prevention, and policy perceptions rather than a single underlying concept, may be the cause of the somewhat lower alpha values. Additionally, the questionnaire was adapted from a previously validated instrument and underwent cultural and language modifications, which can affect the internal consistency scores. As such, these alpha levels are appropriate for the study’s objective of uncovering general trends and educational needs rather than developing a scale with a singular psychometric focus [19, 20]. The final questionnaire took an average of 15 min to complete and consisted of four parts:

Part 1: Personal, demographic, and socioeconomic characteristics, including age (in years), marital status (married/engaged, single), academic year, residence (city, camp, village), type of residence (family home, dormitory), household income level (< 2,000, 2,000–5,000, 5,000–10,000, > 10,000 NIS), the person who usually prepares their meals (mother, father, grandmother, others), number of meals eaten outdoors (none, 1–3 per month, 1–2 per week, > 2 per week, daily), and whether they had received any formal training on FAs in the form of educational lectures, workshops, or a structured course prior to participation.

Part 2: The knowledge assessment section comprises 19 items designed to assess knowledge about FAs. One of the questions in the original questionnaire was removed because of its contextual irrelevance, as it addresses the epidemiology of FAs in the U.S. (Question 12). The 18 remaining item questions were formatted as 15 true-or-false and 3 multiple-response questions. One point was assigned to each correct answer, with two multiple-response questions having more than one correct answer (one question has two correct answers, while another question has three correct answers). The knowledge score was calculated by summing the number of correct answers, with a maximum of 21 points, and then reported as a percentage. Incorrect and “I don’t know” responses were scored as zero.

Part 3: Nine items measuring attitudes toward FAs, including opinions on treatment, quality of life, stigma, policies, and measures addressing FAs, constitute the attitudes component. Every item was scored on a five-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” The original validation study did not detail a definitive, explicit scoring rubric for the attitude items, nor did it perform a factor analysis for construct validity. Calculating a composite score instead of descriptive item-level frequency may result in findings with limited construct validity, as the items may not conform to a single construct [17].

Part 4: Perceptions and experiences. Opinions on topics such as family support, school policy, and awareness efforts pertaining to helping people with FAs were evaluated in this area.

Data management and statistical analysis

IBM’s Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software, version 25.0, which uses both descriptive and inferential statistics, was used to analyse the data. Frequencies and percentages were used to summarize the responses pertaining to students’ knowledge and opinions, as well as demographic and socioeconomic data. The responses on the Likert scale for the attitude items were also displayed as frequencies and percentages, while the knowledge items were summarized according to their respective formats. Prior to inferential analysis, the normality of the knowledge and attitude scores was assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test, with a p value less than 0.01 indicating a nonnormal distribution. As the knowledge scores were nonnormally distributed, the Mann‒Whitney U test and the Kruskal‒Wallis H test were used to examine differences across groups, as appropriate. Spearman’s correlation was used to assess the correlation between knowledge scores and age and year of study, as this correlation is better suited for nonnormally distributed data. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at ANNU [IRB Reference #: Pharm.July. 2025/19]. Permission to conduct research on campuses was also obtained from the appropriate university authorities. Before taking part, the students gave their oral informed consent after being fully informed about the study’s goals and methods. The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki were followed in this research. All of the data were safely stored, their anonymity was rigorously upheld, and they were only used for study.

Results

Demographic, socioeconomic, and personal characteristics

The final sample consisted of 424 participants (mean age = 21 years, SD = ± 2), most of whom were females (68.6%, n = 291) and single individuals (97.2%, n = 412). The largest proportion of participants were enrolled in Doctor of Medicine (19.1%, n = 81) or Doctor of Pharmacy (20.3%, n = 86) programs and were in their fourth (24.5%, n = 104) or third (22.6%, n = 96) academic years of study. A majority lived in urban areas (57.5%, n = 244) and with their families (69.6%, n = 295), while 39.4% (n = 167) lived in villages and 30.4% (n = 129) lived in dormitories. Over one-third had a monthly household income ranging between 5,000 and 10,000 NISs (37.5%, n = 159), and nearly another third had a monthly household income ranging between 2,000 and 5,000 NISs (34.9%, n = 148).

Most participants said that their mothers were the main people who prepared their meals (80.2%, n = 340). The largest proportion of participants said that they dined out once to twice weekly on average (39.4%, n = 167), and fewer than one-third said they dined out once to three times monthly (27.8%, n = 118). Furthermore, only 13.9% (n = 59) had received formal food allergy training. The internet was the most frequently reported source of information about FAs (80.0%, n = 339), followed by artificial intelligence tools (50.0%, n = 212) and brochures (34.7%, n = 147) (Table 1).

Knowledge of food allergies

The median knowledge score was 52.4% (IQR = 42.9–57.1%), with a range of 0–95.2. The answers to the knowledge items were variable. Most participants correctly identified the mechanism causing FAs (75.9%) and that FAs are more common in children than in adults (62.0%), commonly manifested as urticaria (80.9%), can be fatal (77.8%), prevented through avoidance of triggering food (65.8%), and caused by food transmitted through maternal milk (69.3%). Most also provided correct answers when asked about the development of FA symptoms. On the other hand, the majority provided incorrect answers indicating misconceptions about FAs, including that FAs and lactose intolerance are the same (28.5%), FAs have a permanent cure (45.3%), can be prevented by medications (39.4%), and may not decrease with age (27.4%). The full results are detailed in Supplementary Table S1 (Additional File 3).

The results of the correlation analysis revealed that the knowledge scores were correlated with age (ρ = 0.231; p < 0.001) and year of study (ρ = 0.255; p < 0.001). The knowledge scores were also associated with gender (p < 0.001), field of study (p = 0.034) and the number of meals consumed outdoors (p = 0.007). All other variables did not significantly differ from the knowledge scores (Table 2).

Attitudes towards food allergies

Attitudes towards people living with FAs were generally positive. The vast majority agreed or strongly agreed that FA is a serious health issue (90.3%), can cause anxiety among patients (66.3%), and makes eating at restaurants difficult (71.2%). Most also encouraged the implementation of safety plans in schools (90.3%) and recognized the importance of carrying EpiPen (75.7%). However, most agreed that people living with FAs should receive different treatments (71.9%) and that their parents are overprotective (73.3%). The full results are detailed in Supplementary Table S2 (Additional File 3).

Opinions on the most important factor in supporting individuals with food allergies

When asked about the most important factor in supporting individuals with food allergies, the largest proportion of participants responded with ‘development of a definitive and curative treatment’ (30.4%, n = 129), followed by ‘identification of the causes of allergies’ (26.9%, n = 114) and ‘strengthening of community awareness campaigns’ (17.0%, n = 72) (Fig. 1). The participants’ preference for ‘definitive and curative treatment’ reflects their perception of ideal management, not established medical cures.

Discussion

This study is the first to explore knowledge about and attitudes towards FAs among university students in Palestine. The findings revealed overall inadequate knowledge levels, despite generally positive attitudes toward individuals living with FAs. The knowledge scores were significantly associated with age, year of study, gender, field of study, and the number of meals consumed outdoors. These results point to significant gaps in education and offer prospects for focused awareness campaigns in Palestinian academic institutions. Numerous knowledge gaps regarding FA mechanisms, prevention, and management were identified by this investigation. The participants expressed misconceptions about the importance of drugs and avoidance strategies, believed that there was permanent treatment for FAs, and confused FAs with lactose intolerance. These findings share similar patterns with those of regional and global studies, where misconceptions, such as confusing FAs with lactose intolerance, are common despite acceptable basic knowledge related to the recognition of symptoms and general FA concepts [14, 17, 21–23]. For example, two studies conducted among adults in Saudi Arabia and Kuwait used the same questionnaire and revealed knowledge levels, gaps, and misconceptions similar to those reported in the present study, including those related to FA management and confusion with intolerance [21, 23].

Locally, these results are comparable to those from a previous study carried out among parents of children with FAs, which revealed even more knowledge gaps in identification and management despite their expected need to be more knowledgeable about allergy management in their capacity as parents [14]. The greater knowledge level shown in this study compared with that of parents can be attributed to the characteristics of the target population, such as health science students, who are positioned to have greater knowledge by virtue of their study fields and consistent exposure to related information.

Although the vast majority of the participants in the current study were single university students, awareness of FAs is essential and relevant among this population, as most will potentially become parents in early adulthood. This is in addition to the role of improved health literacy in optimizing the use and benefits of implementing university-based safety measures. However, the multiple knowledge gaps revealed among the two different populations suggest a lack of structured education and awareness campaigns addressing FAs in Palestine, which limits exposure to evidence-based information. This could be due to the lack of public health attention given to allergic diseases, as evidenced by the absence of institutional rules on allergen labelling and school-based management protocols. Furthermore, the local food culture and social eating habits, which rely primarily on shared meals and traditional cooking methods, may reduce personal awareness of allergen exposure and cross-contamination hazards.

These difficulties are indicative of more general limitations unique to low- and middle-income nations, such as restricted availability of epinephrine and qualified medical staff, fewer organized public health campaigns than in high-income nations, and restricted access to standardized allergy management resources. As a result, in environments with limited resources, such as Palestine, the knowledge gaps may be exacerbated, highlighting the necessity of workable, affordable solutions such as institution-based awareness campaigns and peer-led education.

The study revealed favourable attitudes toward those who live with FAs, despite low levels of knowledge. This is consistent with the results of local research by parents in Palestine, who stated that society has good views and does not stigmatize those who live with FAs. Given that participants in both studies stressed the importance of educational interventions and the institutionalization of safety measures, such positive attitudes may make it easier to implement these interventions. Positive attitudes are a driving force behind public health improvement, resulting in improved health outcomes and a stronger propensity to take proactive measures to promote health [24].

Gender and field of study were found to be significantly associated with knowledge levels; students who were female and enrolled in programs involving medicine and pharmacy presented higher levels of knowledge. This pattern is in line with earlier studies that reported that more academic exposure to health sciences had a positive effect on FA awareness [8, 25–27]. Higher scores among medical and pharmacy students indicate that they are more engaged in subjects that deal with FAs, such as immunology and patient management. There may be less exposure to FA-related course material and hands-on training for other health-related disciplines. Formal food allergy training, on the other hand, did not substantially correlate with knowledge ratings, indicating that the training that is now provided may be insufficiently practical, brief, or nonstandardized. These results underline the necessity for focused educational tactics and university-level awareness initiatives by indicating that knowledge may be influenced more by academic exposure and environmental interactions than by isolated training experiences [28, 29].

Educational interventions must be planned to be practical, affordable, and implementable within the current university and community institutions in Palestine due to the country’s inadequate healthcare resources, lack of standardized allergy management regulations, and institutional constraints. The necessity of creating and implementing educational interventions is highlighted by inadequate knowledge levels. Because university students are able to comprehend these concepts and the mechanisms behind them, these interventions should be based on targeted modules that emphasize the misconceptions identified by this study, such as the role of avoidance, the belief in a permanent cure, and the confusion of allergies with intolerance. For efficiency, these focused modules can be distributed through peer-led outreach via a training-of-trainers methodology, with an emphasis on the study’s identified lower-knowledge subgroups. By making use of current student networks, such as well-known online platforms, peer-based education may increase reach.

Active university-based organizations, such as student health associations and public health organizations such as An-Najah Global Health Institution (GHI), can lead, develop, and carry out these educational interventions. These organizations have experience creating and implementing community campaigns meant to raise public awareness. These educational interventions can increase their reach beyond the target demographic of university students to the larger community by making use of the positive attitudes this study revealed as a ready platform for behavioral change [30, 31]. Stakeholders such as healthcare professionals, university health service platforms, student unions, local organizations, the Ministry of Health, campus food vendors, and university hospital clinics can help public health institutions become more effective. Proposals for institutional safety measures, such as required allergen labelling in university cafeterias, basic epinephrine availability, and a student orientation module, can be paired with educational initiatives.

Strengths and limitations

There are multiple limitations to this study. Convenience sampling might have resulted in selection bias, which would have limited the applicability of the findings to the larger group of Palestinian university students. Additionally, the results may not be entirely generalizable to students from other fields because the sample was not stratified by faculty, and the overrepresentation of medical and pharmacy students may have skewed the results in terms of knowledge and attitudes. The cross-sectional design of the study prevents inferences from being drawn about causality. Furthermore, the use of self-reported data increases socially desired responses and recall bias. Bias may be introduced by the interviewer, which could affect participant responses. Given the descriptive and exploratory nature of the study, only bivariate analyses were conducted to identify general patterns and associations rather than establish causality. However, no statistical corrections for multiple comparisons (such as Bonferroni or Holm adjustments) were applied, which increases the risk of Type I error and the likelihood of finding significant results by chance. Moreover, the large sample size may have produced smaller p values, potentially exaggerating statistical significance for minor differences. As effect estimates were not computed, the findings should be interpreted cautiously in terms of both statistical and practical significance. Nonetheless, the survey was the first to be carried out among Palestinian university students, offering new insights and establishing a standard for future research. The questionnaire was carefully adapted from the validated Chicago Food Allergy Research Survey to guarantee cultural relevance and improve the tool’s usefulness in the local context. The study’s comprehensive examination, which examined a variety of sociodemographic factors in addition to knowledge and attitudes, also allowed for a deeper understanding of FA management in this community. The use of an interviewer-administered format improved the correctness of the data since the interviewer cleared up any confusion during data collection.

Conclusions

University students in Palestine showed multiple misconceptions and gaps in knowledge about FAs. Age, year of study, gender, field of study, and the number of meals eaten outside were associated with knowledge scores. Additionally, the participants demonstrated positive attitudes toward those who live with FAs. To address the knowledge gaps and misconceptions revealed by this study, educational initiatives should be targeted, practical, and feasible by capitalizing on established university structures. Peer-led modules is an effective method to disseminate targeted information, especially among groups with low knowledge levels. Importantly, collaboration with student associations and public health organizations can improve the reach of these programs. Furthermore, university-implemented institutional safety measures such as management protocols, allergen labelling, and quick orientation modules can support education. However, its implementation in Palestine can be impacted by institutional limitations, a lack of standardized allergy management protocols, and scarcity of health resources.
