Eric E. Martínez-García

Abstract
Recent studies have reported a non evolution of galaxy ages at redshifts higher than z ∼ z\sim 2.5, as well as galaxies older than the Universe. In this work, a sample of galaxies from JWST and HST was analysed via photometry to further understand this astronomical phenomenon. No prior cosmological parameters were assumed in the analysis, but the spectroscopic redshift. When compared to stellar population synthesis models, the results for mass-weighted galaxy ages indicate that the analysed objects seem to be divided into two subsets. The results for the subset with the majority of objects (60% assuming a flat- Λ \Lambda CDM cosmology) indicate an evolution of galaxy ages within the redshift range z z =0.1-7.0, in the sense that higher redshift galaxies are younger than the Universe. Sources of systematic errors were discussed drawing into conclusion that degeneracies between reddening-age-metallicity, and/or AGN emission may explain the rest 40% of the galaxies with ages older than expected from a flat- Λ \Lambda CDM cosmology.

Introduction

Understanding the origin and evolution of the Universe is an important aspect of modern astronomy.
The Big Bang model had led to what is known as the standard cosmology,
aka concordance model, or flat-
\Lambda
CDM model
(
Peebles & Ratra 2003
, e.g.,)
.
The Big Bang model explains most of the observations of the Universe,
for instance, the discovery of the Hubble flow,
the discovery of the Cosmic microwave background (CMB) radiation,
the prediction of the abundance of light elements, and the baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO) measurements.
However, there are still some observational tests that are taken for granted but have not been fully proven.
For instance, if all galaxies have a common origin,
their stellar populations should be younger than the age of the Universe at a certain redshift.
In this regard, most studies pertain to a limited redshift (
z
) range, and/or a forced restriction
of the ages allowed by the standard cosmological model
(
Mignoli et al. 2005
;
Thomas et al. 2017
;
Whitler et al. 2023a
;
Whitler et al. 2023b
;
Damjanov, Geller, & Sohn 2024
, e.g.,)
.
There are also studies with no cosmological restrictions
that point to a non evolution of galaxy ages at
z\gtrsim 2.5

(
Gao, López-Corredoira, & Wei 2024
)
,
or galaxy ages with values greater than those allowed for a flat-
\Lambda
CDM cosmology at
z\sim 8

(
López-Corredoira et al. 2024
)
.
In these works, the analysed galaxies present a “v-shaped” signature in their spectral energy distributions (SEDs),
where the rest-frame ultraviolet (UV) has a slope
\beta_{\rm UV}\la 0
and the rest-frame optical slope is
\beta_{\rm opt}>0
.
The “v-shape” was interpreted as a mixture of a young stellar population in the UV, and an old stellar population
dominating rest-frame optical wavelengths, giving rise to a Balmer break.

On the other hand, “Little red dots”
(
Matthee et al. 2024
;
Kocevski et al. 2024
, LRDs, e.g.,)
have been recently uncovered by JWST observations.
These are distant point-like sources with a “v-shaped” SED confirmed by spectroscopy
(
Furtak et al. 2024
;
Greene et al. 2024
;
Labbe et al. 2025
)
.
Stellar light dominating the continuum has been speculated to explain LRDs
(
Labbé et al. 2023
;
Pérez-González et al. 2024
;
Baggen et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
,
with the possibility of having very massive and extremely dense galaxies at early times.
Due to the hosting of broad permitted lines with FWHM
\ga 1000
km/s,
AGN-dominated SEDs have also been proposed
(
Matthee et al. 2024
;
Harikane et al. 2023
;
Maiolino et al. 2024
;
Kokorev et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Ma et al. 2025
;
Labbe et al. 2025
;
Hainline et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.
Recent LRDs developments demonstrate a non-stellar origin for the continuum
including a Balmer break produced by very dense gas near an
accreting supermassive black hole
(
Inayoshi & Maiolino 2025
;
Ji et al. 2025
;
D’Eugenio et al. 2025
;
de Graaff et al. 2025
;
Naidu et al. 2025
)
,
which also explains the presence of Balmer absorption lines sometimes detected in LRDs.

The main objective of this work is to further investigate the evolution of galaxy ages
through an analysis unconstrained by any cosmological model, and assess the possible impact of “v-shape” objects
in the results.

Building the SPS libraries

In order to analyse the photometry of the complete galaxy sample,
libraries of stellar population synthesis (SPS) models were built,
without acknowledging any cosmological parameters, but the redshift.
The SPS models of
Bruzual & Charlot 2003
,
2

2

https://www.bruzual.org/bc03/
.

with the BaSeL stellar library, and the
Chabrier 2003

function (IMF, see also Section
5.1.2
), were used for this purpose.
The libraries were built by randomly selecting the model parameters for the
metallicity, dust attenuation, and star formation history (SFH).
The main parameter of interest for each model is the mass-weighted age (see Section
3.3
),
which was used for age dating the objects in the galaxy sample.

GALAXEV

3.1
Metallicity

The stellar metallicity was distributed according to a
uniform probability density function (PDF) using six values:
0.0001, 0.0004, 0.004, 0.008, 0.02 (
\sim~Z_{☉}
), and 0.05.

3.2
Dust attenuation

For the dust attenuation, the two component model of
Charlot & Fall 2000
was adopted.
In this model the stellar radiation is attenuated by dust in both stellar birth clouds (BC) and the ambient interstellar medium (ISM).
Only dust in the ambient ISM is considered for stellar ages older the typical lifetime of a molecular cloud
\sim 1\times 10^{7}
yr.
The absorption optical depth follows the expression
\hat{\tau}_{\lambda}\propto\lambda^{\delta}
,
where
\delta_{\rm{ISM}}=-0.7
and
\delta_{\rm{BC}}=-1.3
.
The parameters needed to compute the attenuation by dust are the total effective attenuation optical depth
in the
V
band,
\hat{\tau}_{V}
, and the fraction of
\hat{\tau}_{V}
originating from the ambient ISM,
\mu
.
The
\hat{\tau}_{V}
and
\mu
values were randomly distributed according to the PDFs of
da Cunha, Charlot, & Elbaz 2008
.

3.3
Star formation history

For the SFH, the delayed model, or à la
Sandage 1986
,
with an additional burst of star formation
(
Małek et al. 2018
;
Boquien et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
, was used.
This form of the SFH produces a smooth rise and then a smooth decline for the star formation rate, SFR(t):

~\Psi(t)=\Psi_{\rm delayed}(t)+\Psi_{\rm burst}(t^{\prime}),

(1)

where
\Psi
in the star formation rate,
t
is the time since the onset of star formation
with an upper limit of T
form
,
t^{\prime}=(t-\rm{T}_{\rm form}+\rm{B}_{\rm form})
,

\rm{B}_{\rm form}
represents the age of the added star formation burst, and

\displaystyle\Psi_{\rm delayed}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(t/\tau_{0}^{2})\exp(-t/\tau_{0}),

(2)

\displaystyle\Psi_{\rm burst}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(k)\exp(-t^{\prime}/\tau_{1}).

(3)

The parameter
\tau_{0}
is the e-folding timescale
of the main stellar population and
\tau_{1}
is the e-folding timescale
of the added burst of star formation.
The factor
k
is defined as
k\propto\frac{f}{1-f}

(
Boquien et al. 2019
)
,

k=0
when
t<(\rm{T}_{\rm form}
-
\rm{B}_{\rm form})
,
where

f=\frac{\int_{0}^{(\rm{B}_{\rm form})}\Psi_{\rm burst}(t^{\prime}){\rm d}t^{\prime}}{\int_{0}^{(\rm{T}_{\rm form})}\Psi(t){\rm d}t},

(4)

i.e., the fraction of stellar mass formed in the added burst
relative to the total stellar mass ever formed.

In line with the above-mentioned, there are five parameters
that define the SFH for each model:
\tau_{0}
,
\tau_{1}
,
f
, T
form
, and B
form
.
The choice of these parameters will define the mass-weighted age:

~{\rm age}_{\rm mw}=\frac{\int_{0}^{\rm{T}_{\rm form}}t\Psi(t){\rm d}t}{\int_{0}^{\rm{T}_{\rm form}}\Psi(t){\rm d}t},

(5)

which represents the epoch when most of the stellar mass of a galaxy was assembled
(
da Cunha et al. 2015
;
Lofaro et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
,
i.e., an estimate for the age of the object.
A nearly uniform PDF for
{\rm age}_{\rm mw}
, i.e., unrestricted by cosmological parameters, was adopted.
For this purpose, the SFH parameters were randomly selected with the following constraints:

•

0<\tau_{0}^{-1}<1
(Gyr
-1
),

•

10<\tau_{1}<100
(Myr),

•

0<f<1
,

•

100<{\rm{T}}_{\rm form}<19990
(Myr),

•

1<{\rm{B}}_{\rm form}<{\rm{T}}_{\rm form}
(Myr).

The T
form
parameter upper limit (
\sim 20
Gyr) is given by
Bruzual & Charlot 2003
models.
With each array of parameters (
\tau_{0}
,
\tau_{1}
,
f
, T
form
, and B
form
) we
gradually complete a nearly uniform PDF for
{\rm age}_{\rm mw}
.
This PDF has a smooth decline for ages older than
\ga 16
Gyr, resulting from the fewer parameter
combinations to fill the histogram bins.

3.4
Redshifting and IGM absorption

Given the above conditions, a fiducial SPS library can be constructed.
During the process it is necessary to rectify each spectrum for the
redshift of the observed object to be analysed,

z=(\lambda_{\rm observed}-\lambda_{\rm emitted})/\lambda_{\rm emitted}
,
and to correct for the attenuation in the IGM
(
Madau 1995
)
.
The IGM correction was made following the algorithm of
Meiksin 2006
,
provided in the software “Code Investigating GALaxy Emission”
(
Boquien et al. 2019
,
CIGALE
,)
.

In this manner, for every object in the galaxy sample a library of
\sim

4.3\times 10^{4}
models
was built, being the total number of computed models
\sim

2.9\times 10^{7}
.

4
Fits to the observed photometry

The corresponding libraries were used to fit colours of the individual objects in the galaxy sample.
A maximum likelihood approach was applied by computing the probability:

~P\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\chi^{2}}{2}\right),

(6)

~\chi^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{C_{i}^{\rm obs}-C_{i}^{\rm model}}{\sigma^{\rm obs}_{i}}\right)^{2},

(7)

where
C_{i}^{\rm obs}
is the observed
i_{\rm th}
colour with
\sigma^{\rm obs}_{i}
photometric error,
and
C_{i}^{\rm model}
is the colour of the SPS model.
A total of
n=10
, and
n=11
colours, with adjacent bandpasses (e.g., F140W-F160W),
were used for the HST and JWST samples, respectively (see Section
2
).
The use of colours obviates the need to know the distance to the object.
The best fitting model is the one with the maximum probability,
P
, in equation
6
.
A likelihood distribution for
{\rm age}_{\rm mw}
can be obtained from the
P
values,
where the percentiles, P
16
and P
84
, were computed to estimate the 1-
\sigma
error.

5.1
Systematic errors

The dispersion of points in Figure
2
suggests that systematic errors
may deviate some of the age
mw
results from the true value.
Cosmic variance is assumed to be negligible.
Systematic errors may arise from, e.g.,
degeneracies in the SPS models,
a different stellar IMF,
emission from thermally pulsing asymptotic giant branch stars,
nebular line and continuum emission, or
sample contamination by “little red dots” (and/or AGNs).

5.1.1
Degeneracies

Degeneracies between age-metallicity-reddening can give similar colours in the SPS models
causing uncertainty in the results
(see, e.g.,
Cheng et al. 2025
, for
z\sim 1
galaxies)
.
For instance, if degeneracies are not tackled properly
in studies of resolved maps of stellar mass (at
z\sim 0
), this may result in biased spatial structures
(
Martínez-García et al. 2017
)
.
This may also have an impact on the retrieved SFH of a galaxy
(
Martínez-García et al. 2018
)
.
Bayesian methods may help to hinder degeneracies, although this kind of
studies are outside the scope of the present investigation.
For the current work, it is assumed that degeneracies can be reduced with a statistically large sample.

5.1.2
IMF

A distinct IMF at different cosmic times may also be a source of systematic error.
It has been suggested that the IMF may evolve with redshift
(
van Dokkum 2008
;
Sneppen et al. 2022
;
Steinhardt et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
.
The
Bruzual & Charlot 2003
models provide the alternative to use the
Kroupa 2001
,
Salpeter 1955
,
or a top-heavy IMF, besides
Chabrier 2003
.
As a means of testing the impact of the IMF choice on the inferred ages,
additional SPS libraries were computed with the
Salpeter 1955
IMF, with selfsame parameters
as described in Section
3
.
The outcome of this exercise results in no significant difference from the
Chabrier 2003
IMF case.
In this instance the amount of data points with ages younger than the age of the Universe
is
58\%
, and the trend of younger galaxies at higher redshifts is still verified.

5.1.3
TP-AGB stars

Thermally pulsing asymptotic giant branch (TP-AGB) stars are cool luminous giants with stellar masses
\sim
0.5-6.4
M_{☉}

(
Marigo et al. 2017
)
.
Their main contribution to the luminosity of stellar populations lies at rest-frame NIR wavelengths
for ages
\sim 0.2-2
Gyr
(
Maraston et al. 2006
;
Bruzual 2007
)
.
The consideration of TP-AGB stars may affect the determination of stellar masses and ages with differences as high as
\sim 50\%
.
The inclusion of TP-AGB stars has been favored in some investigations
(
Maraston et al. 2006
;
MacArthur et al. 2010
;
Capozzi et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
,
and disfavoured in others
(
Kriek et al. 2010
;
Melbourne et al. 2012
;
Zibetti et al. 2013
, e.g.,)
.
There is also evidence of a correlation between the presence of TP-AGB stars in nearby disk galaxies and metallicity
(
Martínez-García et al. 2021
, or Hubble type,)
.
For the purpose of testing the effect of the inclusion of TP-AGB stars in the results,
supplementary SPS libraries were constructed.
The
Bruzual & Charlot 2003
models include a different variation that takes into account
TP-AGB stars with the prescription of
Marigo & Girardi 2007
and
Marigo et al. 2008
,
4
4

4

https://www.bruzual.org/cb07/
.

leading to significantly redder NIR colours.
After fitting these libraries to the observed photometry, a similar result to the previous case (Figure
2
) was obtained.
So, although some clear cases of strong TP-AGB signatures have been found in individual galaxies at
z\sim 1-2

(
Lu et al. 2025
)
,
overall the contribution of TP-AGB stars may not be relevant for
z\gtrsim 1-2
galaxies
(
Bevacqua et al. 2025
, see also)
.

5.1.4
Nebular line and continuum emission

The vast majority of galaxies at
z>3
are star forming with the implication of
high equivalent widths EW of nebular lines
(
Endsley et al. 2023
;
Topping et al. 2024
, 100 Å
\lesssim

\lesssim
1000 Å, e.g.,)
.
Emission lines may bias the results if the flux is boosted in individual filters.
Line-sensitive filters
(
Roberts-Borsani et al. 2021
)
can be used to
test the effect of line boosting of the flux by including in the computations the
JWST medium-band F335M and F410M filters (“M” filters, which are part of JADES).
In certain cases, these filters trace better the continuum because emission lines will only boost the flux at specific redshifts.
For this purpose, another set of fits was done by including the JWST “M” filters. From these fits the percentage of
objects with
{\rm age}_{\rm mw}
<
{\rm age}_{\rm Universe}
is
63\%
,
and
61\%
, for the
Chabrier 2003
and
Salpeter 1955
IMF’s, respectively.
The percentages are slightly higher than the case where no “M” filters were adopted (see Figure
2
).
Additionally, continuum nebular emission may become negligible when adopting colours for the fits to the stellar photometry,
as long as this emission remains nearly constant for adjacent filters.
Therefore, for this work it is assumed that nebular emission may bias the results
for some of the objects in the sample but not for the overall outcome.

Conclusions

Based on a sample of 668 galaxies with photometric data from HST and JWST,
spectroscopic redshifts from MUSE and NIRSpec,
and SPS models that assume no prior cosmological model,
the results demonstrate that for
\sim 60\%
of the analysed objects
the mass-weighted galaxy ages evolve with cosmological redshift,
in accordance to a flat-
\Lambda
CDM cosmology.
The results hold within the redshift range
0.1\lesssim z\lesssim 7
.
Possible reasons for retrieving
\sim 40\%
of galaxies with ages older than the age of the Universe
may include: degeneracies between age-metallicity-reddening of the SPS models used for the fits,
a different stellar IMF at different redshifts, emission from TP-AGB stars
in the stellar populations, nebular line and continuum emission,
or intruders in the sample due to the presence of LRDs and/or AGNs.
It was discussed that among these, only degeneracies or AGN emission may have a significant effect on the outcome for
z\lesssim 7
galaxies.
Future efforts should focus on eliminating all sources of systematic errors in order to better constrain
galaxy evolution based on observations of galaxy ages.
