Shell energies derived from three-dimensional isotropic strain-gradient elasticity
C. Balitactac, Y. Canzani, R. S. Hallyburton, J. Mott, C. Rodriguez

Abstract
. We derive a class of two-dimensional shell energies for thin elastic bodies exhibiting small-length scale effects modeled via strain-gradient elasticity. Building on the final author’s earlier work on plate models, the kinetic and stored surface energies arise as the leading cubic order-in-thickness expressions for three-dimensional kinetic energies with velocity-gradient effects and a broad class of isotropic stored energies, each possessing an intrinsic length scale ℓ \ell . These include both classical Toupin–Mindlin and more recent dilatational strain-gradient elastic stored energies. A key insight of this work is that consistent asymptotic reductions of strain-gradient theories necessarily begin at cubic order-in-thickness due to the natural scaling assumption ℓ = O ⁡ ( h ) \ell=O(h) where h h is the thickness of the body. In the limit as the intrinsic length scales vanish, the theory reduces to Koiter’s classical shell energy. We illustrate the theory using the shell energy derived from dilatational strain-gradient elasticity, computing the body force, edge tractions and edge double force densities required to support a variety of finite deformations.

1.2.
Outline

We now provide a brief outline of this study. Section 2 introduces the geometric and kinematic quantities used to describe the deformation of thin elastic bodies and shells. It also outlines the structure of the stored and kinetic energies, which are assumed to decompose into a sum of a classical contribution (with no additional gradients) and strain-gradient/velocity gradient part. A key assumption is that the intrinsic length scales associated to the strain-gradient/velocity gradient contributions scale with the shell thickness
h
. This implies that the stress vectors on the top and bottom lateral surfaces are approximated up to
O(h^{2})
by those derived from the classical energy contributions alone (see (
2.26
)).

Section 3 uses the previous implications for the stress vectors to motivate the choice of director fields in the Taylor expansion (normal to
\Omega
) of the motion, following the methods of Hilgers and Pipkin
[
20
]
and Steigmann et al.
[
42
]
. Assuming small midsurface strain and a quadratic isotropic classical energy contribution, we show that the stored surface energy satisfies

\displaystyle U=W_{\mathrm{Koiter}}+W_{4}+O(h^{4}),

(1.4)

where
W_{4}
incorporates strain-gradient effects from the three-dimensional stored energy.

In Section 4, we derive the explicit form of
W_{4}
for isotropic strain-gradient stored energies of both Toupin–Mindlin and dilatational type, completing the explicit computation of the leading cubic order-in-thickness in (
1.4
). Section 5 then derives the explicit leading cubic order-in-thickness expression for the body’s kinetic energy. In both sections, we assume small midsurface strain and a quadratic isotropic classical energy contribution.

Finally, Section 6 illustrates the theory using the simplest strain-gradient model, one derived from dilatational elasticity. We compute the edge tractions and edge double force densities needed to support various equilibrium states including deforming a finite plate into a cylinder, combined extension/compression and radial expansion/contraction of a finite cylinder, and pure torsion of a finite cylinder.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the geometric and kinematic setup for thin bodies and shells, and we specify the forms of the three-dimensional stored and kinetic energies considered in this work.

\eta,\zeta>0
. It follows from above that
\boldsymbol{A}_{1}=\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta},\boldsymbol{A}_{2}=\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{N}=\boldsymbol{e}_{r},
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{1}=\eta\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta},\boldsymbol{a}_{2}=\zeta\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{e}_{r}.
We compute

\displaystyle(a_{\alpha\beta})=\begin{pmatrix}\eta^{2}&0\\
0&\zeta^{2}\end{pmatrix},\hskip 10.00002pt(b_{\alpha\beta})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{-\eta}{R}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\hskip 10.00002pt\Gamma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}=\bar{\Gamma}^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}=0,

(6.40)

which implies that

\displaystyle(\epsilon_{\alpha\beta})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\eta^{2}-1}{2}&0\\
0&\frac{\zeta^{2}-1}{2}\end{pmatrix},\hskip 10.00002pt(\rho_{\alpha\beta})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1-\eta}{R}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(6.41)

We compute that

\displaystyle N^{11}

\displaystyle=2h\mu\frac{\lambda+\mu}{\lambda+2\mu}\left(\eta^{2}-1\right)+h\frac{\lambda\mu}{\lambda+2\mu}\left(\zeta^{2}-1\right),

(6.42)

\displaystyle N^{22}

\displaystyle=h\frac{\lambda\mu}{\lambda+2\mu}\left(\eta^{2}-1\right)+2h\mu\frac{\lambda+\mu}{\lambda+2\mu}\left(\zeta^{2}-1\right),

(6.43)

\displaystyle M^{11}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{h^{3}\mu}{6}\frac{\lambda+\mu}{\lambda+2\mu}+hl_{s}^{2}\mu\frac{4\mu^{2}}{(\lambda+2\mu)^{2}}\right)\frac{1-\eta}{R},

(6.44)

\displaystyle M^{22}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{h^{3}}{12}\frac{\lambda\mu}{\lambda+2\mu}+hl_{s}^{2}\mu\frac{4\mu^{2}}{(\lambda+2\mu)^{2}}\right)\frac{1-\eta}{R},

(6.45)

with the off-diagonal components of
\sigma
and
M
vanishing. Additionally
M^{\lambda\alpha\beta}=0
for all
\lambda,\alpha,\beta
. From here, we find

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}^{1}=\left(\eta N^{11}-\frac{M^{11}}{R}\right)\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta},\hskip 10.00002pt\boldsymbol{T}^{2}=\zeta N^{22}\boldsymbol{k},

(6.46)

which immediately implies that the required body force is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}=-\boldsymbol{T}^{\alpha}_{,\alpha}=\left(\frac{\eta N^{11}}{R}-\frac{M^{11}}{R^{2}}\right)\boldsymbol{e}_{r}.

When
z=0
,
\boldsymbol{\nu}=-\boldsymbol{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta}
, so
\boldsymbol{t}|_{z=0}=-\boldsymbol{T}^{2}=-N^{22}\zeta\boldsymbol{k}
. On the other edge, where
z=L
,
\boldsymbol{\nu}=\boldsymbol{k}
, so
\boldsymbol{t}|_{z=L}=\boldsymbol{T}^{2}=N^{22}\zeta\boldsymbol{k}
. On both edges,
\boldsymbol{m}=M^{22}\boldsymbol{e}_{r}
, which implies that
\boldsymbol{c}|_{z=0}=-\zeta\boldsymbol{k}\times M^{22}\boldsymbol{e}_{r}=-\zeta M^{22}\boldsymbol{e_{\theta}}
and
\boldsymbol{c}|_{z=L}=\zeta M^{22}\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta}
.

In the case of materials with
\lambda,\mu>0
(i.e., Poisson’s ratio is positive), we can consider the semi-inverse problem of supposing
\eta<1
and
\zeta>1
with
\boldsymbol{g}=\boldsymbol{0}
. This corresponds to increasing the height of the cylinder and shrinking its radius while applying no body forces. For any
\eta<1
, a unique
\zeta>1
satisfying this requirement always exists. On the
z=L
edge of the cylinder, we may interpret the traction as a sum of two forces: one depending on
\zeta
in the
+\boldsymbol{k}
direction that pulls the edges apart to increase the height and one depending on
\eta
that applies a slight compression in the
-\boldsymbol{k}
direction to counteract the natural stretching of the cylinder as the radius decreases. Furthermore, we may interpret
\boldsymbol{c}
as a density of edge couples that prevents the edges of the cylinder from bowing inwards during the deformation.

6.4.
Pure torsion

For
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, set
\vartheta:=\theta+\frac{\gamma}{R}z
. We now consider the deformation of
\boldsymbol{x}=R\boldsymbol{e}_{r}(\theta)+z\boldsymbol{k}
to
\boldsymbol{y}=R\boldsymbol{e}_{r}(\vartheta)+z\boldsymbol{k}
. It follows that
\boldsymbol{a}_{1}=\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta}(\vartheta),\boldsymbol{a}_{2}=\gamma\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta}(\vartheta)+\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{e}_{r}(\vartheta).
Then

\displaystyle(a_{\alpha\beta})=\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma\\
\gamma&\gamma^{2}+1\end{pmatrix},\hskip 10.00002pt(b_{\alpha\beta})=-\frac{1}{R}\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma\\
\gamma&\gamma^{2}\end{pmatrix},\hskip 10.00002pt\Gamma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}=\bar{\Gamma}^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}=0,

(6.47)

which implies that

\displaystyle(\epsilon_{\alpha\beta})=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&\gamma\\
\gamma&\gamma^{2}\end{pmatrix},\hskip 10.00002pt(\rho_{\alpha\beta})=-\frac{1}{R}\begin{pmatrix}0&\gamma\\
\gamma&\gamma^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(6.48)

We can now compute
\boldsymbol{N}^{\alpha}=N^{\alpha\beta}\boldsymbol{a}_{\beta}
with

\displaystyle N^{11}

\displaystyle=h\gamma^{2}\frac{\lambda\mu}{\lambda+2\mu},

\displaystyle N^{12}

\displaystyle=N^{21}=h\gamma\mu,

\displaystyle N^{22}

\displaystyle=h\gamma^{2}\left[\frac{\lambda\mu}{\lambda+2\mu}+\mu\right],

and
\boldsymbol{M}^{\alpha\beta}=M^{\alpha\beta}\boldsymbol{e}_{r}(\vartheta)
with

\displaystyle M^{11}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{R}\left(\frac{h^{3}}{12}\frac{\lambda\mu}{\lambda+2\mu}+hl_{s}^{2}\mu\frac{4\mu^{2}}{(\lambda+2\mu)^{2}}\right),

(6.49)

\displaystyle M^{12}

\displaystyle=M^{21}=-\frac{\gamma}{R}\frac{h^{3}}{12}\mu,

(6.50)

\displaystyle M^{22}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{R}\left(\frac{h^{3}}{12}\left[\frac{\lambda\mu}{\lambda+2\mu}+\mu\right]+hl_{s}^{2}\mu\frac{4\mu^{2}}{(\lambda+2\mu)^{2}}\right).

(6.51)

From here we see that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}^{1}

\displaystyle=\left(N^{11}+\gamma N^{21}-\frac{1}{R}(M^{11}+\gamma M^{12})\right)\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta}(\vartheta)+N^{21}\boldsymbol{k},

(6.52)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(N^{12}+\gamma N^{22}-\frac{1}{R}(M^{21}+\gamma M^{22}\right)\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta}(\vartheta)+N^{22}\boldsymbol{k}.

(6.53)

It follows from this that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}

\displaystyle=\left[-\frac{1}{R^{2}}\left(M^{11}+2\gamma M^{12}+\gamma^{2}M^{22}\right)+\frac{1}{R}\left(N^{11}+2\gamma N^{12}+\gamma^{2}N^{22}\right)\right]\boldsymbol{e}_{r}(\vartheta).

(6.54)

On the boundaries
\{z=0\}
and
\{z=L\}
, as in the previous example we have that
\boldsymbol{t}|_{z=0}=-\boldsymbol{T}^{2},

\boldsymbol{t}|_{z=L}=\boldsymbol{T}^{2}
and on both edges,
\boldsymbol{m}=M^{22}\boldsymbol{e}_{r}(\vartheta)
. This implies that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}|_{z=0}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{a}_{2}\times\boldsymbol{m}=-(\gamma\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta}+\boldsymbol{k})\times M^{22}\boldsymbol{e}_{r}=\gamma M^{22}\boldsymbol{k}+-M^{22}\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta}

(6.55)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}|_{z=L}

\displaystyle=-\gamma M^{22}\boldsymbol{k}+M^{22}\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta}.

(6.56)

Thus, when
\lambda,\mu>0
(i.e., Poisson’s ratio is positive), we must apply an outward radial force to the cylinder which prevents the cylinder from crushing inward during torsion. We see also that we must twist in the direction of the torsion and pull outward on the cylinder to prevent the cylinder from twisting back or crushing along
\boldsymbol{k}
under the twist. Finally, the value of
\boldsymbol{c}
shows that density of edge couples has components in both the
\boldsymbol{k}
and
\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta}
directions that prevents the cylinder lip from bowing out in a skew direction.
