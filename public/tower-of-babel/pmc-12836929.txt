Effects of predatory mite biocontrols on the dispersal of antibiotic resistomes and virulence factors in tea garden soils
Authors: Da Lin, Gong-Yu Lin, Jin-Ting Zheng, Zhi-Hong Qiao, Hua-Yuan Shangguan, Hai-Feng Yao, Tian-Lun Zhang, Jia-Yang Xu, Hongjie Li, Martin F Breed, Xin Sun, Yong-Guan Zhu, Dong Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBiocontrols can be used to manage pests while supporting soil health. However, the effects of the biocontrols such as predatory mites on antibiotic resistance in bacterial communities remains largely unknown. Here, we examined long-term field experiments in tea garden soils, combined with global datasets and soil microcosm experiments, to explore the effects and underlying mechanisms of predatory mite treatment on the abundance of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) and virulence factor genes (VFGs).ResultsPredatory mite treatment may intensify the predation pressure exerted by bacterial predators on bacterial communities through trophic cascades. This led to genome streamlining and alterations in microdiversity and functions within the bacterial communities. In this process, members of the phylum Actinobacteriota, especially the family Pseudonocardiaceae, demonstrated greater adaptability, with their relative abundance increasing from 25.0 to 41.8%, due to their higher nucleotide diversity and growth rates compared to other bacterial taxa. These taxa served as the primary hosts for ARGs and VFGs, which were also identified in global datasets, playing a key role in promoting the abundance of ARGs and VFGs in soil ecosystems. The possibility of trophic cascade effects of predatory mites on the dispersal of ARGs and VFGs were further validated through soil microcosm experiments.ConclusionsThese findings advance our understanding of bacterial evolutionary trajectories under biocontrols, which is crucial for slowing the spread of antibiotic resistance and promoting sustainable agriculture. Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance poses an enormous threat to both society and the environment [1, 2]. Soils serve as major reservoirs of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) [3, 4]. Virulence factor genes (VFGs) have also gained widespread attention due to their links to pathogenicity, enabling bacterial hosts to evade defense mechanisms and infect animals, including humans [5]. Exposure to bacteria that carry ARGs and/or VFGs can lead to ineffective antibiotic therapy and prolonged bacterial infections [6]. Furthermore, previous studies have found that ARGs such as aac(6′)-lb, yidY/mdtL, and tolC were transmitted to the microbiome of predatory mites via their collembola prey [7], highlighting the potential spread of these microbial contaminants in soils via ecological food web processes. Thus, revealing the fate of ARGs, VFGs, and their potential bacterial hosts in soils is critical for better understanding antibiotic resistance and related risks under the One Health framework [8].

Pest control is an important strategy for sustaining agricultural productivity [9]. Growing concerns over the environmental impacts of pesticides, particularly their potential to select for ARGs in soils [10], have increased interest in non-chemical biological control methods [11]. The biological controls involve leveraging natural predators and beneficial organisms, such as predatory mites, to reduce pest populations and suppress plant diseases [12]. This approach has been shown to benefit soil health, pest management, and crop productivity [13], and thus being increasingly adopted in agriculture systems.

Nonetheless, biological controls are not without ecological risks. Trophic cascade effects of predatory mites may, for example, exert predation pressure on soil bacterial communities via bacterial predators, such as protists, nematodes and collembola, which may lead to strong and non-target ecological responses (e.g., via changes to competition and/or predation) [14]. These indirect effects may lead to the evolution of bacterial defense strategies, including the production of antibiotics and resistance genes [15]. Competition between bacteria and other microorganisms can stimulate the production of antibacterial compounds, such as antibiotics, which are used to inhibit or kill their opponents [16]. Furthermore, predation by soil bacterial predators introduces a selection pressure that drives the evolution of bacterial antibiotic resistance. These predators tend to avoid antibiotic-producing bacteria and selectively consume those that do not, resulting in a bacterial community that is dominated by bacteria with strong resistance and maintaining high levels of antibiotic resistance [17, 18]. Despite this knowledge, the mechanisms of interactions among these factors remain unclear.

Tea gardens represent a common agricultural system in many countries [19]. Unlike cereal crops, which are primarily harvested for grain yield, tea trees are cultivated for their buds and leaves, placing higher emphasis on pest control. To this end, biocontrol agents such as Stratiolaelaps scimitus, a widely used predatory mite in soil ecosystems are introduced to manage pests on tea leaves, aiming to ensure high yields and tea quality. Given the potential predation pressure on bacteria caused by the trophic cascade effects of predatory mites mentioned above, we hypothesize that predatory mites cause increases in the abundance and diversity of ARGs and associated risks (e.g., VFGs) within soil bacterial communities.

Here, we employed a metagenomic approach combined with global datasets to investigate how predatory mites influence the dispersal of ARGs and VFGs in tea gardens. We conducted long-term field experiments and validated our findings through soil microcosm experiments. The main objectives of our study were to (1) investigate the trophic cascade effects of predatory mites on the dispersal of ARGs and VFGs in soil bacterial communities; (2) identify the key factors driving ARGs and VFGs variation; and (3) determine the key driving bacterial taxa responsible for the dispersal of ARGs and VFGs and explore their global distribution patterns. Together, our findings provide new insights into trophic cascade effects on the evolution and development of ARGs and their health risks in soil ecosystems.

Results

Effect of predatory mites on soil ARGs and VFGs

A total of 503 ARGs and 2132 VFGs were detected across 33 samples. The detected ARG types primarily included resistance to multidrug (21.1%), rifamycin (17.0%), and novobiocin (15.4%), while VFG types were predominantly composed of adherence (19.8%), immune modulation (18.9%), and nutritional/metabolic factors (18.3%) (Fig. 1a, b). PCoA analysis of ARGs and VFGs revealed clear separation among control, pesticide, and predatory mite treatments (Fig. S1). PERMANOVA further confirmed that treatment significantly contributed to the differentiation of ARG and VFG profiles (P = 0.005), explaining 23.7% and 23.0% of the variation, respectively, rather than being driven by site (PERMANOVA test, P > 0.05) (Table S1). Overall, similar to the pesticide treatment, the release of predatory mites significantly increased the abundance of ARGs and VFGs compared to controls (P = 0.02; Fig. 1a, b).

The number of detected ARGs and VFGs differed among control, pesticide, and predatory mite treatments (Fig. 1c). More ARGs and VFGs were uniquely shared within the pesticide (ARGs: 73; VFGs: 169) and predatory mite (ARGs: 72; VFGs: 257) treatments than in controls (ARGs: 23; VFGs: 80), while the predatory mite treatment exhibited the highest number of ARGs (395) and VFGs (1837) (Fig. 1c). The representative co-occurrence patterns between ARGs and VFGs in the predatory mite treatment were identified. The multidrug resistance genes mexF and rgt1438 co-located on the same contig along with some VFGs (Fig. S2). The variation in mobile genetic elements (MGEs) across different sites and treatments did not follow a consistent pattern (cf. ARGs and VFGs) (Fig. S3).

Shifts in soil bacterial communities affect ARGs and VFGs

The pesticide (P = 0.04) and predatory mite (P = 2e−6) treatments led to an increase in the total organic carbon (TOC) content compared to controls (Table S2). Additionally, the total phosphorus (TP) content significantly decreased in pesticide (P = 0.015) and predatory mite (P = 0.000173) treatments (Table S2). pH did not vary across treatments (P > 0.05; Table S2). Furthermore, high concentrations of pesticide residues, specifically imidacloprid, clothianidin and thiamethoxam, were detected in soils treated with pesticides, whereas they were rarely detected in the control and predatory mite treatments (Table S3). The presence of small amounts of pesticide residues in control and predatory mite treatments may result from the migration of pesticides from nearby contaminated soil through natural processes such as wind or rain.

Bacterial taxa in pesticide and predatory mite treatments were also distinct from those in control. The abundant phyla were Proteobacteria (36.1%), Actinobacteriota (25.0%), and Acidobacteriota (19.0%) in control; Actinobacteriota (45.1%), Proteobacteria (24.9%), and Acidobacteriota (12.0%) in pesticide treatment; and Actinobacteriota (41.8%), Proteobacteria (27.9%), and Acidobacteriota (12.0%) in predatory mite treatment (Fig. S4). The proportion of Actinobacteriota in the pesticide (P = 2.3e−7) and predatory mite (P = 5.0e−6) treatments was markedly higher compared to the controls.

Shannon’s index of bacteria in both pesticide (P = 0.001) and predatory mite (P = 0.010) treatments was significantly lower than those in controls (P < 0.05; Fig. S5). In addition, the application of pesticides and predatory mites also led to a substantial increase in the abundance of potential and dangerous human bacterial pathogens (HBPs) (P < 0.05), which potential HBPs were mainly comprised of Actinobacteriota accounted for 71.3% and 74.9%, respectively (Fig. S6). The neutral community model based on bacterial communities revealed that a higher proportion of bacteria (79.8%) and HBPs (82.5%) in the predatory mite treatment were affected by deterministic processes, compared to controls (bacteria 72.1%; HBPs 75.6%) (Fig. S7). Actinobacteriota was strongly influenced by deterministic process within the pesticide (bacteria: 87.7%; HBPs: 88.5%) and predatory mite treatments (bacteria 91.6%; HBPs 90.3%) compared to other phyla (Fig. S8).

Variation partitioning analysis was conducted to investigate the impact of soil properties (including TOC, total nitrogen (TN), TP, pH and moisture), treatments (control, pesticides and predatory mites), bacterial communities (bacterial abundance and bacterial diversity) and MGEs on ARGs and VFGs. This analysis showed that bacterial communities were the most important predictor of ARGs and VFGs (Fig. 2a and Fig. S9a). And procrustes tests also revealed that the bacterial communities were strongly associated with both ARGs (M2 = 0.2891, P = 0.001) and VFGs (M2 = 0.147, P = 0.001) (Fig. 2b and Fig. S9b).

Predatory mites affect the soil bacterial communities via trophic cascades

Pesticide and predatory mite treatments strongly impacted the interaction network structures of co-occurring taxa in the soil food web, including three trophic levels: bacteria, protists (predators of bacteria), and nematoda/collembola (predators of bacteria and protists) (Fig. S10). After the application of pesticides and predatory mites, there was an observed increase in niche differentiation within the soil food web, with higher modularity and a lower proportion of bacteria (node and edge), and the cohesion of the soil food web (as reflected by cohesion metrics in the network) decreased (Fig. 2c, d). However, in contrast to the overall decrease in cohesion observed in all organisms and the reduced predation effect (negative correlation effect) on bacteria by predators, such as protists and nematodes/collembola, following pesticide treatment, treatment with predatory mites increased both the cohesion of bacterial predators and their predation effect on bacteria, while Actinobacteriota were less susceptible to predation compared to other bacteria (Fig. 2d). Genetic structural changes of the soil bacterial communities

Next, the genetic structural changes of bacterial populations were analyzed. The results showed that average genome size and average nucleotide diversity of bacteria decreased, while GC content increased after treatment with pesticides and predatory mites (P < 0.05; Fig. 3a, b, c) and the degree of homologous recombination increased under both treatments (quantified using the linkage disequilibrium between the alleles of the two loci, D’, where the closer the value D’ is to 0, the higher the degree of recombination [23]) (P < 0.05; Fig. 3d).

In our strain analysis, the results revealed that Actinobacteriota demonstrated greater nucleotide diversity in comparison to other bacterial taxa (P = 3.8e−12; Fig. 3e). Furthermore, there was notable positive correlation between the nucleotide diversity of bacterial communities and their relative abundance (P = 0.018; Fig. 3f). Indeed, the maximum growth rate of Actinobacteriota was markedly higher compared to other bacterial subpopulations (P = 0.001; Fig. 3g). Subsequently, the average gene pN/pS value was used to quantify the ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous mutations. A pN/pS > 1 indicates gene under positive selection, while pN/pS < 1 represents gene under purification selection [23]. Actinobacteriota was more prone to non-synonymous mutations and positive selection, and had higher GC content compared to other bacterial taxa (P < 0.05; Fig. 3h–j).

The functional abundance of secondary metabolite synthesis pathways related to antibiotics, indole, and phenazine (each known to contribute to antibiotic resistance in bacteria [24]) in the pesticide and predatory mite treatments were higher than in the controls (P < 0.05; Fig. 3k). Additionally, the pathways related to cofactor biosynthesis such as ubiquinone and folate biosynthesis (involved in regulating oxidative stress in bacteria, DNA replication and repair pathways [25]) also exhibited a significant increase after pesticide and predatory mite treatments (P < 0.05; Fig. 3l, m).

Key bacterial strains driving soil ARGs and VFGs

To investigate the shifts in bacterial communities, particularly the correlation between the enrichment of Actinobacteriota and the proliferation of ARGs and VFGs, an analysis of their interrelationships was conducted. The abundance of ARG hosts in pesticide (P = 2.8e−4) and predatory mite (P = 3.3e−4) treatments was significantly higher than in the controls (Fig. S11a). Most ARG hosts were affiliated with Actinobacteriota (56.2%) and Proteobacteria (33.0%) in controls; Actinobacteriota (76.8%) and Proteobacteria (17.9%) in the pesticide treatment; and Actinobacteriota (78.2%) and Proteobacteria (17.1%) in the predatory mite treatment (Fig. 4). The abundance of Actinobacteriota in ARG hosts treated with pesticides and predatory mites was significantly higher than in controls, consistent with the trend of bacterial community changes (Fig. 4). The relative abundance of most metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), recovered from these metagenomic samples, was higher in the pesticide and predatory mite treatments than in controls, with a majority of these MAGs containing ARGs, VFGs and MGEs (Fig. 5a). Over 60% of the MAGs carried ARGs, while 27.3% of the MAGs were identified to encode resistance to multiple classes, which conferred resistance to > 1 class of antibiotics (Fig. 5a). Taxonomically, 58.3% of MAGs with multi-resistance came from the phyla Actinobacteriota, with Pseudonocardiaceae being the predominant family (Fig. 5b). These MAGs encoded high numbers of ARGs associated with multidrug, rifamycin, novobiocin, vancomycin and tetracycline resistance, and also possess a high number of VFGs linked to regulation, nutritional/Metabolic factor, immune modulation, stress survival and adherence, as well as a higher number of MGEs (Fig. 5b). Meanwhile, a significant enhancement in the abundance of Pseudonocardiaceae family within both the total bacteria and ARG host bacteria was observed following pesticide and predatory mite treatments (P < 0.01; Fig. S11b, c). Pseudonocardiaceae family in total bacteria or ARG host bacteria were in strongly positive correlation with ARGs and VFGs (P < 0.01; Fig. S11d–g).

The network analysis revealed that bacterial taxa in the predatory mite treatment exhibited stronger correlations with ARGs and VFGs than those in the control group, which tended to have a larger number of nodes, edges, average degrees, and modularity (Fig. S12a). Four clusters of ARGs and VFGs exhibited strong coexistence with bacteria in the networks of control, pesticide and predatory mite treatments (Fig. S12b). The main co-existence modules in the network of control, pesticide and predatory mite treatments were identified as module 2, module 1 and module 2, respectively, due to their higher degrees within their respective networks (Fig. S12c). After comprehensive analysis of the bacterial composition within these three modules, it was found that they were primarily composed of Actinobacteriota (Fig. S13). Additionally, the Pseudonocardiaceae family, which was prominently detected in the previous binning analysis, was also present in all three modules (Fig. S13). The degree of connections between genera within the ARG, VFG and Pseudonocardiaceae family, as well as their abundance proportion within the module, were higher in both pesticide and predatory mite treatments than in controls (Figs. S12 and S13).

Characterization of ARGs and VFGs in key strains at a global scale

To examine the worldwide distribution of Pseudonocardiaceae family, and the presence of ARGs and VFGs, information from the NCBI regarding the Pseudonocardiaceae family was collected. Apart from our study, Pseudonocardiaceae from various regions and habitats across the world also harbor a diverse range of ARGs and VFGs, with an average of 77 ARG subtypes and 320 VFGs (Fig. 6a). Compared to the dominant soil bacterial family Bradyrhizobium, the abundance of ARGs (P = 0.001) and VFGs (P = 0.001) in the Pseudonocardiaceae family was significantly higher, with average increases of 1.9 times and 2.1 times, respectively (Fig. 6b). The Bradyrhizobium family had the highest Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) score in the control group of bacterial family-level linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis, accounting for 7.1% of the non-treated soil bacteria (Fig. S14). However, its relative abundance decreased to 4.1% and 5.4% after the application of pesticides and predatory mites, respectively (Fig. S14b). Moreover, Pseudonocardiaceae exhibits the highest abundance of ARGs and VFGs in “human related” habitats (Fig. 6b). In Pseudonocardiaceae, the abundance of ARGs was dominated by genes conferring resistance to rifamycin, tetracycline, MLS, novobiocin and vancomycin, accounting for 24.7%, 14.8%, 13.2%, 7.9% and 7.9% of the total abundance of ARGs, respectively (Fig. 6c). The abundance of VFGs in Pseudonocardiaceae is primarily associated with nutritional/metabolic factors (25.6%), immune modulation (21.4%), regulation (20.8%), stress survival (12.5%), and adherence (12.0%) (Fig. 6d). The types of genes were identical to the ARGs and VFGs encoded in MAGs belonging to the Pseudonocardiaceae family in our study, as well as the dominant ARG and VFG types in soils of our experiments.

Validation experiments on the trophic cascades of predatory mites

Soil microcosm experiments were further conducted to verify the trophic cascade effects of predatory mites on bacterial communities and their impact on the abundance of ARGs and VFGs, by separately and simultaneously adding collembola and predatory mites (Fig. 7a). The addition of predatory mites significantly reduced the number of collembola by approximately 29.8% (P = 0.029; Fig. 7b), indicating their predation. Compared to no treatment, only when collembola and predatory mites were added simultaneously, there was a significant increase in the relative abundance of Actinobacteriota (P = 0.037; Fig. 7c) and higher proportion of consumer within protists (P = 3.8e−4; Fig. 7d). Meanwhile, the network connectance between protists and bacteria notably increased (P = 0.001; Fig. 7e), while the negative correlation between protists and Actinobacteriota markedly decreased (P = 0.07; Fig. 7f). Ultimately, the simultaneous application of collembola and predatory mites significantly enhanced ARG (P = 0.017) and VFG (P = 0.040) abundance, whereas the separate addition of collembola and predatory mites had no such effect (P > 0.05; Fig. 7g, h).

Discussion

The work here demonstrated that predatory mites used as a biological control in tea gardens promote the proliferation of ARGs and VFGs to a similar degree as pesticide use. The predatory mite treatment also led to an increased risk of exposure via ARGs, such as mexF, which is frequently identified to be enriched in areas with long-term anthropogenic pollution [26]. However, MGEs did not exhibit similar changes in ARGs and VFGs after the pesticide and predatory mite treatments. This suggests that MGEs may not be the primary factor driving the changes in ARGs and VFGs observed in our study, which is consistent with findings reported in previous studies [27, 28].

The analysis of the effects of pesticide and predatory mite treatments on bacterial communities found a notable shift in the dominant bacteria and human bacterial pathogens, with increases in the abundance of Actinobacteriota in particular. Neutral community modelling indicated that both bacteria and human bacterial pathogens belonging to Actinobacteriota treated with predatory mites were subjected to more deterministic processes compared to other phyla. Results also reported that the bacterial communities were the most important factors impacting the occurrence of ARGs and VFGs across treatments. Therefore, Actinobacteriota was more likely to survive compared to other bacterial taxa under both treatments, thereby playing a crucial role in ARG and VFG pollution.

Based on our exploration, the pesticide treatment impacted bacterial communities primarily via chemical residues, whereas the predatory mite treatment affected bacterial communities by trophic cascades. A large amount of pesticide residues persisted in soils after the pesticide treatment, mainly containing imidacloprid, clothianidin, and thiamethoxam. This non-antibiotic chemical has garnered attention for its impact on bacteria, and subsequent promotion of ARGs and VFGs pollution [29, 30]. The predatory mite treatment increased bacterial predation by predators via trophic cascade effects. Therefore, introducing predatory mites as biocontrols plays a key role in the evolution of bacterial antibiotic resistance and virulence. Previous studies have also demonstrated trophic cascade effects increase along with the level of predation suppression by predators [31]. There is also evidence of behavioral shifts in soil microorganism consumers due to top predators [32–34]. These shifts can substantially change soil microbial processes, such as soil respiration and N2O emissions, and recent research has also revealed a close link between the abundance and diversity of soil ARGs and an increase in predation pressure on protists, which are vital consumers of bacteria [15].

Bacteria undergo pronounced changes in genetic structure and metabolic function when adapting to the pressures imposed by pesticide and predatory mite treatments. These genetic changes were characterized primarily by a decrease in average genome size and nucleotide diversity, coupled with an increase in homologous recombination. The genome decrease aligns with recent studies showing that bacteria streamline their genomes under environmental stress [35, 36]. In contrast, the elevated homologous recombination likely facilitates adaptation by increasing fixation probability of beneficial mutations and reducing clonal interference, i.e., competition between beneficial mutations within bacterial populations [37].

Compared to other bacteria, Actinobacteriota had a distinct response to pesticide and predatory mite treatments. They showed higher nucleotide diversity and growth rates, were more prone to non-synonymous mutations and positive selection, and had higher GC content. These genetic changes likely confer an adaptive advantage under environmental changes such as pesticide and predatory mite treatments [37–39]. In addition, Actinobacteriota can produce a wide range of antibiotics and other secondary metabolites. When faced with pressure, Actinobacteriota tend to increase the production of these compounds, thereby inhibiting competing bacteria and securing a survival advantage [40]. Consistent with this, there was a significant increase in the synthesis pathways of antibiotics, indole and phenazine metabolites known to induce bacterial resistance to common antibiotics by mediating the expression of efflux pumps and to enhance pathogen infection levels [24]. Furthermore, pathways related to the SOS response and to DNA recombination and repair, which facilitate the acquisition of new genetic information for environmental adaptation [41], also increased after pesticide and predatory mite treatments.

An important‌ impact of Actinobacteriota, specifically the Pseudonocardiaceae, on the abundance of ARGs and VFGs in soils was demonstrated under the pesticide and predatory mite treatments. Contig and binning analyses verified that Actinobacteriota served as the primary carriers of ARGs and VFGs in this study, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [26, 42]. The Pseudonocardiaceae family stood out as it encoded multiple categories of resistance genes, including rifamycin, novobiocin, vancomycin, and tetracycline. These antibiotics are primarily produced by Actinobacteriota [43], and they also represent the dominant types of resistance genes detected in soils in our study. In the modular analysis of the network, Pseudonocardiaceae was found in both the key modules associated with pesticide and predatory mite treatments, and showed selective enrichment and a closer association with ARGs and VFGs than the control. In other studies, Pseudonocardiaceae has also been widely found in plant roots, marine sediments, insect guts, and soils with high heavy metal pollution [44–46]. Its global distribution and possession of ARGs and VFGs were further confirmed, and Pseudonocardiaceae still exhibited a high relative abundance of ARGs and VFGs in different regions and habitats. After analyzing the composition of the main ARGs and VFGs, the types of these genes were identical to the dominant ARGs and VFGs types in Pseudonocardiaceae family and soils in our study, highlighting the crucial role of these bacteria in ARG and VFG pollution. Finally, the trophic cascades of predatory mites in increasing ARGs and VFGs, and the enhanced survival of Actinobacteriota under this effect were confirmed by soil microcosm experiments.

Taken together, the application of predatory mites to tea garden soils resulted in an increase in both the abundance and number of ARGs and VFGs, which was largely attributed to alterations in bacterial communities. The introduction of predatory mites alters the predatory interactions in soils, and leads to changes in the genetic structure and bacterial functions in the soil bacterial communities—together, this treatment resulted in an increased abundance of antibiotic-resistant and pathogenic bacteria, particularly those belonging to Actinobacteriota. Among these, the Pseudonocardiaceae family in the Actinobacteriota possesses high number and abundance of ARGs and VFGs in global databases, which is consistent with the ARG and VFG types carried by these bacterial taxa in our soil samples. Our study provides a comprehensive understanding of a biological driving force behind the evolution and proliferation of antibiotic resistance and associated health risks in a productive soil ecosystem.

Although this research provides an in-depth analysis of the trophic cascade effects of predatory mites on the evolution and proliferation of ARGs and their health risks in soil ecosystems, it is important to note the potential caveats regarding our research and suggest possible directions for future studies. First, despite collecting more than three replicate samples from three experimental sites in this study, only a single round of sampling and measurement was conducted. Future research should incorporate time series and large-scale sampling to overcome this potential limitation. Second, while we have demonstrated that predatory mite treatment can increase ARGs and VFGs in soil ecosystems through trophic cascade effects, future multidisciplinary and multiscale studies are needed to fully understand and assess the ecological impacts of introducing predatory mites. These studies should focus on the changes in the predatory mite population size during the process, the bacterivores (such as protists, nematodes, and collembola) present in the guts of predatory mites, the specific predation efficiency of predatory mites on bacterivores and the subsequent impact on bacterial communities. The types and quantities of predatory mites used in biocontrols, and the potential risks of food chain transmission associated to the rise of ARGs and VFGs in bacterial communities, should be further evaluated to help mitigate the risks associated with their trophic cascade effects and the subsequent increases in ARG and VFG abundance. Third, the trophic cascade effects of predatory mites could be partially revealed by our 1-month incubation of the simple soil microcosm experiments, but long-term and large-scale experiments are encouraged for future studies to examine changes across various trophic levels and their long-term adaptive responses. Finally, since the trophic cascade may represent just one of the complex pathways through which predatory mites influence the exposure of ARGs and VFGs, further research should explore other possible mechanisms that could affect the abundance of soil ARGs and VFGs, such as plant growth or root exudates.

Materials and methods

Field experiments setup

The experiments began in 2009 at Zhangping Tea Garden (25°46' N, 117°34' E) in Fujian Province, China, a renowned tea-producing region with a long history of promoting biological control technologies. We included three different experimental sites, each containing three different treatments: controls (no treatment), pesticides (600 g/ha of composite pesticide mainly containing imidacloprid, clothianidin and thiamethoxam), and predatory mites (900,000 Stratiolaelaps scimitus/ha). Stratiolaelaps scimitus (S. scimitus) is a generalist soil predatory mite widely used to control various pests [47]). The experimental sites are located at the Institute of Plant Protection, Fujian Academy of Agricultural Sciences, with a research base dedicated to advancing and commercializing predatory mites as an eco-friendly pest control solution for tea garden management. Therefore, strict management was implemented at the experimental sites to ensure the independent use of predatory mites. The pesticide and predatory mite treatment reflected common agricultural practices to eliminate pests in tea gardens. These amounts of pesticides and predatory mites correspond to normal agricultural application rates. The experimental station applied 2–3 insecticide treatments annually and released predatory mites once a year in spring.

According to our long-term field experiment, the introduction of predatory mites effectively controls Brevipalpus obovatus Donnadieu (average control efficacy 81% over 50 days) and Calacarus carinatus (average control efficacy 52.3% after 50 days), both common mites in tea gardens. This intervention reduced the loss of tea buds by approximately 42.2%, crude tea green weight by about 34.7%, fine tea green weight by around 54.1%, tannin by 6.8–4.1%, and tea polyphenol by approximately 6.5%. We divided each sampling site into four sub-sites based on equal area proportion.

Each sampling site was divided into four equal sub-sites. After removing litter, five topsoil samples (0–20 cm depth) were collected from the four corners and the center of each sub-site, with each sampling points located 30–50 cm away from the tea trees. The five samples were thoroughly mixed to form one composite sample per sub-site. Due to sample loss, only three soil samples were collected for each control (CK) treatment. In total, 33 soil samples were collected from the nine sampling sites. Each soil sample was divided into two parts: one stored at 4 °C for soil property analysis, and the other stored at − 80 °C for DNA extraction for microbial communities and functional analyses.

Determination of soil physicochemical properties and pesticide concentration

Soil moisture content was determined by weighing after drying in 105 ℃ oven for 48 h to ensure they reached constant weight. After air-drying and homogenizing the soils through 2 mm mesh sieve, soil pH, TN, TP, and TOC were measured using conventional methods. Soil pH was measured using a glass electrode calibrated with a standard buffer solution, with a soil-to-distilled water ratio of 1:2.5. Soil TOC and TN were determined using an elemental analyzer (Vario PYRO Cube, Elementar, Germany) [48], while soil TP was quantified using the alkali fusion-molybdenum antimony anti-spectrophotometric method [48]. Blank samples were included, and duplicate measurements of each sample were conducted to ensure precision.

Soil pesticides were extracted based on the previous QuEChERS method, with relative standard deviations < 20% [49]. 5 g soil samples were accurately weighed into 50 mL centrifuge tube, and 10 mL of water was added. The tube was shaken manually for several seconds to hydrate the sample, then left to stand for 30 min. Subsequently, 10 mL of acetonitrile containing 1% acetic acid was added. The tube was sealed and vortexed for 2 min using a multi-position vortexer, then centrifuged at 1600 g for 5 min. The supernatant was transferred to 15 mL centrifuge tube containing dispersive solid-phase extraction adsorbents (900 mg anhydrous MgSO4, 150 mg C18, and 150 mg PSA). The extract was vortexed for 1 min and centrifuged at 4200 rpm for 5 min. Finally, 1 mL of the resulting supernatant was filtered through 0.22 μm PTFE filter. Pesticide concentrations in all samples were determined using high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (Waters, USA).

Metagenomic sequencing and analysis

Soil DNA of each sample was extracted using a Fast DNA spin kit (MP Biomedicals, Cleveland, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Metagenomic sequencing of each DNA sample was performed on an Illumina HiSeq platform operated by Majorbio Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The raw reads of metagenomes were trimmed using Fastp to eliminate low-quality reads with Q scores ≤ 20, lengths < 50 and those containing N base [50]. After quality control, we obtained 414.4 GB of data from 33 samples, which were assembled using MEGAHIT (Table S4) [51]. Contigs with overlap lengths > 300 bp were selected for further analysis. The Prodigal software was used to predict open reading frames (ORFs) [52], and the CD-HIT algorithm was utilized to establish a non-redundant gene catalog [53].

Taxonomic annotation of bacteria, protists, nematodes, and collembola, sequences from the non-redundant gene set were compared with those in the NCBI Non-Redundant (NR) database. Bacteria were classified as potential and dangerous HBPs by referencing a compilation of HBPs reported in previous studies [54]. Bacterivorous nematodes were identified based on reported feeding habits of soil nematode families and genera [22]. Alterations in the ARGs, VFGs and MGEs were analyzed using local ARG-OAP (version 3.2) [55] based on structured ARG databases (SARG) [55], MGE databases [56], and VFDB databases [57] with criteria of E-value ≤ 10−7, identity ≥ 80%, and hit length ≥ 75%. ARG-carrying ORFs were identified as those ORFs with the best hit in the SARG database over 80% identity. Taxonomic assignment of each ARG-carrying contig was determined by examining the classification of the ARG-like ORFs within the contig. If more than 50% of the ARG-like ORFs were classified to the same species, the contig was annotated to this species [58]. Consequently, this species was regarded as a potential ARG host. ARGs, VFGs and MGEs were scored as co-contig when they were found to be located on the same contigs [59].

To focus on bacteria, we identified and removed potential eukaryotic contigs using EukRep [60]. Average genome size per sample was estimated using MicrobeCensus pipeline based on contigs > 500 bp, as contig length could impact the accuracy [61]. GC content (%) was calculated by using Quast v5.2.0 [62]. To infer the metabolic pathways, sequences from the non-redundant gene set were aligned with the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database (KEGG) by BLASTP software (version 2.2.28 +) with an E-value of ≤ 10−5.

Metagenome binning, microdiversity profiling and growth rate estimation

Metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) were recovered from each metagenome via MetaBAT2 [63], MaxBin2 [64], and CONCOCT [65]. MAG completeness and contamination were assessed using CheckM [66], and only MAGs with completeness > 50% and contamination < 10% were kept for downstream analyses. MAG relative abundance was calculated by normalizing read coverage to total sample reads. Taxonomy assignment of the recovered MAGs was determined by GTDB-Tk [67]. ORFs predicted from MAGs by Prodigal were searched against SARG, VFDB, and mobileOG databases [68]. ORF with an amino acid identity > 60% to reference sequence were classified as ARGs, VFGs and MGEs. Population microdiversity metrics such as genome-wide nucleotide diversity, selection measures (pN/pS) and linkage disequilibrium measures (D’), were calculated using InStrain v1.0.0 with the representative MAGs as the reference database [69]. The maximal growth rate for each MAG was estimated using gRodon v.1 [70].

Collection and analysis of sequencing data from Pseudonocardiaceae and Bradyrhizobium families

According to our experimental results, Pseudonocardiaceae family is the important host of ARGs, while Bradyrhizobium family serves as the representative control bacterial subpopulation that possess high abundance but decreased following predatory mite treatment. We retrieved 187 valid sequencing datasets of Pseudonocardiaceae family and 35 datasets of Bradyrhizobium family from the NCBI SRA. All retrieved data was subjected to quality control and successfully annotated ARGs and VFGs information as mentioned above, as well as relatively accurate location and habitat information. To compare the relative abundances of ARGs and VFGs across different sequencing data, values was normalized relative to the total number of bases in each sequencing data. More detailed information is found in Table S5 and S6.

Habitat information for the Pseudonocardiaceae family was classified into five groups based on the intrinsic characteristics of their environments. Briefly, different types of soil samples and root systems were classified as “soil”; pure culture in the laboratory, atmospheric dust and bronchoalveolar lavage were categorized as “human relate”; deep sea and pond sediments, as well as hyporheic zone, are classified as sediment, while “caves” are classified as a single type. More specific information about the classification refers to Table S7. The sequencing data of Bradyrhizobium family were all from soils (forest soils and groundnut field).

Microcosm setup and performance

Soils were collected from the field experiment soil of predatory mite treatment. Moisture content was adjusted to 60% of the maximum field water holding capacity and incubated for 7 days for stabilization. Based on the soils from the predatory mite treatment, we selected collembola Folsomia candida (F. candida), a key bacterial predator, and predatory mites S. scimitus to construct a model soil food chain, aiming to verify the role of trophic cascades of predatory mites. Before the experiments, animals of the same age were obtained according to the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) guidelines. Specifically, we transferred F. candida and S. scimitus of similar body size and capable of spawning to a new Petri dish containing clean gypsum-activated carbon culture medium (8:1 w/w), and marked the dish with the laying date. Fresh eggs (< 24 h) were collected and transferred to a new Petri dish. The hatched larvae (< 24 h) were defined as synchronized F. candida and S. scimitus.

Subsequently, we added 50 g of soils to sterilized glass beakers to establish the soil microcosms (n = 16). The experiments included four treatments, each with four replicates: (1) CK: no treatment; (2) MC: addition of 30 synchronized F. candida; (3) MPM: addition of 6 synchronized S. scimitus; (4) MCPM: simultaneous addition of 30 synchronized F. candida and 6 synchronized S. scimitus. Microcosms were maintained under controlled conditions (800 lx light, 8 h:16 h light–dark cycle, 20 ± 2 °C, 75% humidity) for 30 days. Yeast (typical food source for F. candida) and water were replenished twice a week to maintain the microcosms. After 30 days, F. candida were extracted from the soils by improved Berlese dry extraction [71]. Due to their heat sensitivity, a temperature-controlled gradient extractor (HG-KWHC100, Jiangsu Zhuoheng Measurement and Control Technology Co., Ltd.) was used. Soil samples were placed atop the exactor, with collection container below containing 75% ethanol to preserve the F. candida. Temperature started at 25 °C, and increased by 5 °C every 12 h until 45 °C, terminating the process after approximately 48 h to ensure sufficient migration of the F. candida. Humidity was maintained between 40 and 60% to prevent rapid soil drying and maintain the F. candida activity. The number of F. candida was photographed under a stereomicroscope and subsequently counted using ImageJ software.

Soil samples were subjected to DNA extraction and metagenomic sequencing, followed by annotation of ARGs and VFGs as described above. Furthermore, the bacterial 16S rRNA genes were amplified using the primer sets 515 F (GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGG) and 806R (GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT), while the 18S rRNA genes were amplified using the primers Euk1391f (GTACACACCGCCCGTC) and EukBr (TGATCCTTCYGCAGGTTCACCTAC) [72]. The purified amplicons were sequenced using the Illumina MiSeq PE 300 at Magigene Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangdong, China). 16S/18S rRNA sequence analysis was performed using Qiime2 v2022.06 [73]. Raw paired-end reads were filtered, and chimeras were removed with the DADA2 plugin. Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) for bacteria and protists were clustered at 100% similarity based on representative sequence comparisons with the Silva v138 [74] and Pr2 v4.14 [75] databases, respectively. The putative functional groups of protists, i.e., consumers, parasites, and phototrophs, were assigned based on the broad life history strategy of the major protistan taxonomic groups as described previously [76].

Statistical analysis

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) cluster analysis and PERMANOVA tests based on the Bray–Curtis distance were conducted to display the dissimilarity in ARGs, VFGs and bacterial communities between treatments and site, using the “vegan” package in R (4.0.5) [77]. The visualized of bar graph, box graph, pie graph, bubble graph, violin plot and heatmaps and procrustes analysis were employed using the packages “vegan” and “ggplot2” in R [77]. The neutral community model was used to analyze the assembly mechanism of the bacterial communities while the extent of stochastic and deterministic processes contributing to the bacterial assembly was assessed by the R code from Burns et al. [78]. Phylogenetic trees were visualized using the iTOL online server. A Sankey diagram and packing bubble plot were visualized using the package “networkD3” and “packcircles” in R, respectively [77]. The top 100 bacterial genera, top 100 ARG subtypes and top 100 VFGs in control, pesticide and predatory mite treatments were selected for co-occurrence analysis to evaluate the relationships between bacterial genera, ARGs and VFGs. The correlation between bacterial genera, ARGs and VFGs and the correlation between bacterial genera, protists, nematodes and collembola in field experiments, and the correlation between bacterial ASV and protist ASV in soil microcosm experiments was calculated using the “WGCNA” package in R. Subsequently, the generation of network maps based on these correlation coefficients, calculation of topological parameters and modular analysis were performed using Gephi. Followed the method imposed by Yuan et al. [79]. and calculated two metrics, vulnerability and cohesion, to describe the topological structure of the meta-food web (co-network of bacteria, protists, nematodes, and collembola). The measure of network vulnerability assesses the strength of associations among taxa in a network and offers insights into how quickly the ecological impact of losing taxon spreads to parts or the entire network [80]. Network cohesion quantifies the degree of connectivity resulting from both positive and negative associations among taxa within communities [81]. Network connectance (C = E/N2 where E is the number of edges, and N is the number of nodes) were calculated [82]. The variation partitioning analysis was conducted using the “rdacca.hp” package in R [83]. The visualized of UpSet graph was employed using the “UpSet” package in R [84]. The global distribution map and analysis data of the collected sequencing data were plotted using ArcGIS 10.2 (ESRI, Berkeley, CA, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with LSD test and the Kruskal–Wallis test were used to evaluate statistical significance of three or more groups in SPSS V26 software (IBM Inc., Chicago, USA), while Mann–Whitney U test was employed to assess the statistical significance of two groups in the same software.
