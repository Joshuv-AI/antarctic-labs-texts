Time-integration of Gaussian variational approximation for the magnetic Schrödinger equation
Malik Scheifinger, Kurt Busch, Marlis Hochbruck, Caroline Lasser

Abstract
In the present paper we consider the semiclassical magnetic Schrödinger equation, which describes the dynamics of charged particles under the influence of a electro-magnetic field. The solution of the time-dependent Schrödinger equation is approximated by a single Gaussian wave packet via the time-dependent Dirac–Frenkel variational principle. For the approximation we use ordinary differential equations of motion for the parameters of the variational solution and extend the second-order Boris algorithm for classical mechanics to the quantum mechanical case. In addition, we propose a modified version of the classical fourth order Runge–Kutta method. Numerical experiments explore parameter convergence and geometric properties. Moreover, we benchmark against the analytical solution of the Penning trap.

Introduction

In the present paper we study the numerical time-integration for
charged quantum particles that are
subjected to external magnetic and electric fields. The dynamics is governed by
the semiclassical magnetic Schrödinger equation

\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\partial_{t}\psi(t)=H(t)\psi(t),\hskip 10.00002pt\psi(0)=\psi_{0},\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{R},

(1.1a)

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with magnetic Hamiltonian

H(t)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\nabla_{x}-{A}(t,\cdot)\bigr)^{2}+\phi(t,\cdot),

(1.1b)

and initial value
\psi_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with semiclassical parameter
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
. Here,
{A}
is a divergence-free magnetic vector potential, and
\phi
is the electric potential.
From a numerical point of view, solving this time-dependent partial differential equation raises three major problems. First, it is a high-dimensional problem, since the space dimension is typically given by
d=3N
, where

N
is the number of quantum particles in the system. Further, the computational domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is naturally unbounded, and thus most numerical methods require truncation before discretization. For the method of lines (first discretize space, then time), high dimension combined with an unbounded domain leads to inadequately if not intractably large systems that have to be integrated in time.
Another challenge is given by the high oscillations induced by the small semiclassical parameter
\varepsilon
. For standard time integration schemes severe step-size restrictions have to be imposed and leave these methods impracticable.

We consider the case that the initial condition
\psi_{0}
is strongly localized and given by a Gaussian wave packet, and investigate the numerical time-integration of an approximate solution
{u}(t)\approx\psi(t)
, which is a Gaussian wave packet

{u}(t,x)=\exp{\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}(x-{q}_{t})^{\top}{\mathcal{C}}_{t}(x-{q}_{t})+(x-{q}_{t})^{\top}{p}_{t}+\zeta_{t}\bigr)\Bigr)}.

(1.2)

The parameters to be computed are the packet’s position and momentum center
{q}_{t},{p}_{t}
, the complex width matrix
{\mathcal{C}}_{t}
of the envelope, and the complex phase and weight parameter
\zeta_{t}
. These parameters evolve according to ordinary differential equations, which are systematically derived by the Dirac–Frenkel time-dependent variational principle.
By the approximation ansatz, high oscillations in time and space are captured and thus eliminated for the numerical time integration.
For
{A}=0
it is well established that variational Gaussian wave packets offer reasonable mesh-free approximations at low
computational cost, see for example
[
1
,
2
]
. More recently, they have also been proposed for magnetic quantum dynamics
[
3
]
.

3
Variational approximation

In the semiclassical regime, the solution of the Schrödinger equation (
1.1a
) is highly oscillatory and well localized. We thus seek approximations within the manifold of complex Gaussian wave packets

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\Bigl\{

\displaystyle{u}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\bigm|{u}(x)=\exp{\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}(x-{q})^{\top}{\mathcal{C}}(x-{q})+(x-{q})^{\top}{p}+\zeta\bigr)\Bigr)},

\displaystyle{q},\,{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}^{\top}}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d},\,\mathrm{Im}\,{\mathcal{C}}\text{ positive definite},\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\Bigr\}.

(3.1)

We construct the optimal approximation
{u}(t)\approx\psi(t)
,
{u}(t)\in\mathcal{M}
, in the sense that the time-derivative
\partial_{t}{u}(t)
minimizes the residual,

\left\lVert\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\partial_{t}{u}(t)-H(t){u}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=\min_{\partial_{t}{u}(t)}!

(3.2)

We consider initial data with

\psi_{0}={u}_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
and
\left\lVert{u}_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=1
, and mention in passing, that the variational approximation (
3.2
) is norm preserving in any case and energy preserving for time-independent Hamiltonians,

\displaystyle\left\lVert{u}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=\left\lVert{u}_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}},\hskip 10.00002pt\langle H\rangle_{{u}(t)}=\langle H\rangle_{{u}_{0}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all}\ t,

(3.3)

see
[
8
, §II.1.5]
.

3.1
Variational equations of motion

In earlier work
[
3
]
, we derived ordinary differential equations for the parameters
{q}_{t}
,
{p}_{t}
,
{\mathcal{C}}_{t}
,
\zeta_{t}
of the approximating Gaussian wave packet, which we reproduce here. We denote the classical Hamiltonian function for charged particles in a magnetic field by

h(t,x,\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\xi-{A}(t,x)\right)^{2}+\phi(t,x),\hskip 10.00002pt(t,x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(3.4)

Then, the residual minimization in (
3.2
) implies that

\displaystyle\dot{q}

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{\xi}h\rangle_{{u}},\hskip 10.00002pt\dot{p}=-\langle\partial_{x}h\rangle_{{u}},\hskip 10.00002pt\dot{\mathcal{C}}=-\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{C}}),

(3.5a)

\displaystyle\dot{\zeta}

\displaystyle=-\langle h\rangle_{{u}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\mathrm{tr}\>\!\left(\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{C}}){{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}\right)+p^{\top}\langle\partial_{\xi}h\rangle_{{u}},

(3.5b)

where the complex matrix
\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{C}})\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
, that depends on a Gaussian average, is given by

\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{C}})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&{\mathcal{C}}\end{pmatrix}\langle\nabla^{2}h\rangle_{{u}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}\\
{\mathcal{C}}\end{pmatrix}.

(3.5c)

The averages

\langle\partial^{\alpha}h\rangle_{{u}}=\langle\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(\partial^{\alpha}h)\rangle_{{u}}
use the standard Weyl quantization of the derivatives of the Hamiltonian function.
In
Section

4
, we reformulate these averages such that the equations of motion become amenable to a Boris-type time discretization.

3.2
Asymptotic accuracy

The variational Gaussian wave packet
{u}(t)
determined by (
3.2
) is the exact Schrödinger solution
\psi(t)
, if the magnetic potential
{A}(t,\cdot)
is
linear and the electric potential
\phi(t,\cdot)
quadratic with respect to position. In particular, the dynamics in a Penning trap are exactly described. More generally, if
{A}
and
\phi
are sub-linear and sub-quadratic in the following sense,

\displaystyle\partial^{\alpha}_{x}{A}(t,\cdot),\hskip 10.00002pt\partial^{\beta}_{x}\phi(t,\cdot)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{bounded for all}\ |\alpha|\geq 1,\ |\beta|\geq 2,

(3.6)

then the following bounds for the norm and the observable error can be proven, see
[
3
, Theorems 3.8 & 3.10]
. Given a finite time horizon
[0,T]
, the norm error and the observable
error for expectation values with respect to a linear operator
\mathbf{A}
satisfy

\displaystyle\left\lVert\psi(t)-{u}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq c\,t\,\sqrt{\varepsilon},\hskip 20.00003pt\left|\langle\mathbf{A}\rangle_{\psi(t)}-\langle\mathbf{A}\rangle_{{u}(t)}\right|\leq C\,t\,\varepsilon^{2}

(3.7)

for all
t\in[0,T]
,
where the constants
c,C>0
are independent from
\varepsilon
and
t
, but depend on a lower bound for the eigenvalues of
{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}}
on
[0,T]
. It is worth pointing out the high observable accuracy.

3.3
Classical versus variational approximation

Traditional Gaussian wave packet approximation evolves the centers
according to the purely classical equations of motion

\dot{q}=\partial_{p}h,\hskip 20.00003pt\dot{p}=-\partial_{q}h,

(3.8)

see
[
9
, §4]
. If the magnetic and the electric potential are linear respectively quadratic in position (as they are for a Penning trap), then the classical and the variational approximation coincide and yield the exact quantum solution. For general Hamiltonians, however, the approximations differ. The variational equations of motion (
3.5
) contain averages with respect to the approximating Gaussian wave packet, which are computationally more costly than the pure point evaluations of the traditional approach. However, a traditional Gaussian approximation is neither energy preserving nor as accurate as a variational one, since the classical observable error is only first order in
\varepsilon
, while the variational one is second order.

3.4
Hagedorn parametrization

It is convenient to write the complex symmetric width matrix
{\mathcal{C}}
in Hagedorn’s parametrisation as

{\mathcal{C}}={{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{R}}}+\mathrm{i}{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}}={P}{Q}^{-1}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{and}\hskip 10.00002pt{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}}=({Q}{Q}^{*})^{-1},

(3.9)

with two real symmetric matrices
{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{R}}},{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}}
and two invertible complex matrices
{Q},{P}
that satisfy the symplecticity condition
{Q}^{\top}{P}-{P}^{\top}{Q}=0
,
{Q}^{*}{P}-{P}^{*}{Q}=2\mathrm{i}\mathrm{Id}
. Such a decomposition of complex symmetric matrices with positive definite imaginary part is unique up to unitary factors, see
[
8
, Chapter V]
. The Riccati-type equation (
3.5a
) for the evolution of the width matrix
{\mathcal{C}}
takes in Hagedorn’s parametrisation the form

\dot{Q}=\langle\partial_{pq}h\rangle_{{u}}Q+\langle\partial_{pp}h\rangle_{{u}}P,\hskip 10.00002pt\dot{P}=-\langle\partial_{qq}h\rangle_{{u}}Q-\langle\partial_{qp}h\rangle_{{u}}P.

(3.10)

Unfortunately, we can express
{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{R}}}=\frac{1}{2}({P}{Q}^{-1}+{Q}^{-*}{P}^{*})
only in terms of both
{Q}
and
{P}
, which will have implications for the integrators to be developed.

4
Transformation of equations of motion

We transform the variational equations of motion in such a way that they structurally mimic the classical equations
for a charged particle.

4.1
Averaged magnetic momenta

In a first step, we rewrite the variational equations of motion such that an averaged version of the usual magnetic momenta becomes visible.

Lemma 4.1
.

The variational equations of motion (
3.5
) for the parameters of a Gaussian wave packet are equivalent to

\displaystyle\dot{{q}}

\displaystyle={p}-\langle{A}\rangle_{{u}},\hskip 10.00002pt\dot{{p}}=\langle J_{{A}}^{\top}(\xi-{A})-\nabla\phi\rangle_{{u}},

(4.1a)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle=-\langle\partial^{2}_{x}h\rangle_{{u}}+\langle J_{{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{{u}}{\mathcal{C}}+{\mathcal{C}}\langle J_{{A}}\rangle_{{u}}-{\mathcal{C}}^{2},

(4.1b)

\displaystyle\dot{\zeta}

\displaystyle=-\langle h\rangle_{{u}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\mathrm{tr}\>(\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{C}}){{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})+{p}^{\top}({p}-\langle{A}\rangle_{{u}}),

(4.1c)

where
\langle a\rangle_{{u}}=\langle\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(a)\rangle_{{u}}
for any smooth
a:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}
,
(x,\xi)\mapsto a(x,\xi)
. In Hagedorn’s parametrisation, the matrix evolution (
4.1b
) satisfies

\displaystyle\dot{Q}

\displaystyle={P}-\langle J_{{A}}\rangle_{{u}}{Q},

(4.2a)

\displaystyle\dot{P}

\displaystyle=\langle J_{{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{{u}}{P}-\big\langle J_{{A}}^{\top}J_{{A}}-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\nabla^{2}{A}_{k}(\xi_{k}-{A}_{k})+\nabla^{2}\phi\big\rangle_{{u}}{Q}.

(4.2b)

Proof.

Since the partial derivatives of the classical Hamiltonian function
h(x,\xi)=\frac{1}{2}(\xi-{A}(x))^{2}+\phi(x)
satisfy

\displaystyle\partial_{\xi}h(x,\xi)=\xi-{A}(x),\hskip 20.00003pt\partial_{x}h(x,\xi)=-J_{{A}}(x)^{\top}(\xi-{A}(x))+\nabla\phi(x),

(4.3)

we have (
4.1a
). We next turn to the second order derivatives of
h
. Denoting the partial derivatives of the magnetic and the electric potential shortly by
\partial_{1},\ldots,\partial_{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{m}}\partial_{x_{\ell}}h

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\partial_{m}{A}_{k}\partial_{\ell}{A}_{k}-(\xi_{k}-{A}_{k})\partial_{m}\partial_{\ell}{A}_{k}\right)+\partial_{m}\partial_{\ell}\phi,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{m}}\partial_{\xi_{\ell}}h

\displaystyle=-\partial_{m}{A}_{\ell},\hskip 20.00003pt\partial_{\xi_{m}}\partial_{x_{\ell}}h=-\partial_{\ell}{A}_{m},

(4.5)

which gives a Hessian matrix

\nabla^{2}h=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}^{2}h&\partial_{x\xi}h\\
\partial_{\xi x}h&\partial_{\xi}^{2}h\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}^{2}h&-J_{{A}}^{\top}\\
-J_{{A}}&\mathrm{Id}\end{pmatrix}

(4.6)

with

\partial_{x}^{2}h=J_{{A}}^{\top}J_{{A}}-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\nabla^{2}{A}_{k}(\xi_{k}-{A}_{k})+\nabla^{2}\phi
.
By (
3.5c
) we then infer that

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{C}})

\displaystyle=\langle\partial^{2}_{x}h\rangle_{{u}}+\langle\partial_{x\xi}h\rangle_{{u}}{\mathcal{C}}+{\mathcal{C}}\langle\partial_{\xi x}h\rangle_{{u}}+{\mathcal{C}}\langle\partial^{2}_{\xi}h\rangle_{{u}}{\mathcal{C}}

(4.7)

\displaystyle=\langle\partial^{2}_{x}h\rangle_{{u}}-\langle J_{{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{{u}}{\mathcal{C}}-{\mathcal{C}}\langle J_{{A}}\rangle_{{u}}+{\mathcal{C}}^{2}.

(4.8)

Hence, the variational equations of motion (
3.5
) are equivalent to (
4.1
).
∎

This formulation of the equations of motion prominently features averaged magnetic momenta

v\coloneqq{p}-\langle{A}\rangle_{{u}},\hskip 20.00003pt\Upsilon\coloneqq{P}-\langle J_{{A}}\rangle_{{u}}{Q}.

(4.9)

We aim at rewriting them in terms of the real vector
v(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the complex matrix
\Upsilon(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
.

4.2
Equations for the center and the width

Since the averaged magnetic momenta contain averages of the magnetic field and of its Jacobian, we need a compact way of assessing the time derivative of averages in the spirit of a magnetic Ehrenfest-type theorem.

Proposition 4.2
.

For any smooth function
w\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, its average with respect to the variational Gaussian wave packet
{u}={u}(t)
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle w\rangle_{{u}}

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla w\rangle_{{u}}^{\top}v+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathrm{tr}\>\!\left(\langle\nabla^{2}w\rangle_{{u}}(\Upsilon{Q}^{*}-\mathrm{i}\mathrm{Id})\right),

(4.10)

where
\Upsilon{Q}^{*}-\mathrm{i}\mathrm{Id}
is a real matrix.

Proof.

We differentiate the position density with respect to time,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left\lvert{u}(t,x)\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(x-{q})^{\top}{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}(x-{q})-\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\zeta_{\textup{I}}\right)

(4.11)

\displaystyle=\left\lvert{u}(t,x)\right\rvert^{2}\bigg(-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(x-{q})^{\top}\dot{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}(x-{q})+\frac{2}{\varepsilon}(x-{q})^{\top}{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}\dot{q}-\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\dot{\zeta}_{\textup{I}}\bigg)

(4.12)

\displaystyle=\left\lvert{u}(t,x)\right\rvert^{2}\bigg(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}(x-{q})^{\top}\big({{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}-\langle J_{{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{{u}}\big){{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}(x-{q})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 10.00002pt+\frac{2}{\varepsilon}(x-{q})^{\top}{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}v-\mathrm{tr}\>({{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}})\bigg),

(4.13)

where we have used that, by Lemma
4.1
, the symmetry of
{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}
and
{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}
, and
\mathrm{tr}\>(\langle J_{{A}}\rangle_{{u}})=0
since
\nabla\cdot A=0
,

\dot{q}=v,\hskip 10.00002pt\dot{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}={{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}(\langle J_{{A}}\rangle_{{u}}-{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}})+(\langle J_{{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{{u}}-{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}){{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}},\hskip 10.00002pt\dot{\zeta}_{\textup{I}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathrm{tr}\>({{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}).

(4.14)

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle w\rangle_{{u}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\Big\langle w(x-{q})^{\top}\big({{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}-\langle J_{{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{{u}}\big){{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}(x-{q})\Big\rangle_{{u}}

(4.15)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\big\langle w{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}(x-{q})\big\rangle_{{u}}^{\top}v-\langle w\rangle_{{u}}\mathrm{tr}\>({{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}})

Lemma 4.5

For the real part

Proof.

We start with the normalization formula.
Since
\mathrm{Im}\,\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{C}})=-\langle J_{{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{{u}}{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}-{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}\langle J_{{A}}\rangle_{{u}}+{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}+{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\,\left(\mathrm{tr}\>(\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{C}}){{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})\right)

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{tr}\>(\mathrm{Re}\,((\dot{Q}+\langle J_{{A}}\rangle_{{u}}{Q}){Q}^{-1}))

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{tr}\>(\mathrm{Re}\,(\dot{Q}{Q}^{-1})).

(4.50)

We thus obtain from (
4.1c
) with Jacobi’s formula

\displaystyle\dot{\zeta_{\textup{I}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathrm{tr}\>(\mathrm{Re}\,(\dot{{Q}}{Q}^{-1}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\frac{1}{\left\lvert\det{Q}\right\rvert^{2}}\left(2\mathrm{Re}\,\left(\overline{\det{Q}}\det{Q}\penalty\ \mathrm{tr}\>\big(\dot{{Q}}{Q}^{-1}\big)\right)\right)

(4.51)

leads

\mathrm{Re}\,\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{C}})=\langle\partial_{x}^{2}h\rangle_{{u}}-\langle J_{{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{{u}}{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}-{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}\langle J_{{A}}\rangle_{{u}}+{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}^{2}-{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}^{2}

(4.52)

Combining this with

, we use
Lemma

A.2
and obtain

\displaystyle-\langle h\rangle_{{u}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\mathrm{tr}\>(\mathrm{Re}\,\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{C}}){{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})

(4.53)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\langle(p-{A})^{2}\rangle_{{u}}-\langle\phi\rangle_{{u}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\mathrm{tr}\>(\langle\partial_{x}^{2}h\rangle_{{u}}{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}-2{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}).

Next we observe that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\langle(p-{A})^{2}\rangle_{{u}}+{p}^{\top}\langle{p}-{A}\rangle_{{u}}

5
Time integration for the equations of motion

In this section we first briefly review the classical Boris algorithm. Afterwards, we present the new Boris-type algorithm and a modification of the classical Runge–Kutta method to solve (
4.17
) and (
4.47
).

5.1
Boris algorithm for classical equations of motion

The Boris algorithm was originally proposed in
[
4
]
for solving the classical equations of motion

\dot{{q}}=v,\hskip 20.00003pt\dot{v}=v\times{{B}}+{{E}}.

(5.1)

for charged particles in an electro-magnetic field.
We consider a time-grid
t^{n}
,
n\geq 0
, with step size

\tau>0
.
Given approximations
{q^{n}}\approx{q}(t^{n})
and
v^{n-\frac{1}{2}}\approx v(t^{n-\frac{1}{2}})
, the algorithm can be written as

\displaystyle v^{-}

\displaystyle=v^{n-\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{\tau}{2}{{E}^{n}},

\displaystyle{{E}}^{n}

\displaystyle={{E}}(t^{n},{q^{n}}),

(5.2a)

\displaystyle v^{+}-v^{-}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau}{2}\bigl(v^{+}+v^{-}\bigr)\times{{B}^{n}},

\displaystyle{{B}^{n}}

\displaystyle={{B}}(t^{n},{q^{n}}),

(5.2b)

\displaystyle v^{n+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=v^{+}+\frac{\tau}{2}{{E}^{n}},

(5.2c)

\displaystyle{q^{n+1}}

\displaystyle={q^{n}}+\tau v^{n+\frac{1}{2}}.

(5.2d)

Note that the algorithm provides approximations on a staggered grid, where the velocities are only given at half time-steps. Approximations at
t^{n}
can be obtained by averaging

\displaystyle v^{n}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(v^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+v^{n-\frac{1}{2}}\bigl).

(5.2e)

Moreover, the scheme is explicit, since one can replace (
5.2b
) by

\displaystyle v^{+}=v^{-}+\left(v^{-}+v^{-}\times\frac{\tau}{2}{{B}}^{n}\right)\times\frac{\tau{{B}}^{n}}{1+\left\lvert\frac{\tau}{2}{{B}}^{n}\right\rvert^{2}},

(5.2f)

see, e.g.,
[
5
]
.
The Boris algorithm is a second-order method which is not symplectic but conserves the phase-space volume as shown in
[
10
]
. A recent analysis was presented in
[
11
]
.

5.2
Boris-type algorithm

We aim at solving the
Euler-Lagrange system (
4.17
) together with the two phase equations
(
4.47a
) and (
4.47b
). The former are closely related to the classical equations of motion of a charged particle (
5.1
) except that
{{B}}
is replaced by an averaged field
\langle{{B}}\rangle_{{u}}
and that the fields

and
{S}{Q}
do not only depend on
{q}
and
{Q}
but also in a nontrivial way on
v
and
\Upsilon
.
While the means
\langle{{B}}\rangle_{{u}}
can be approximated by a suitable Gauß-Hermite quadrature formula, evaluating the fields
{{E}}
and
{S}
is more involved since they require approximations to
v
and
\Upsilon

t^{n}

and even worse, the update of
\Upsilon
is coupled to the evaluation of

To be more precise, it is the matrix

\Upsilon
which is necessary to compute the fields
{{E}}
and
{S}
in
Theorem

.
Averaging

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{n}=\frac{3}{2}\Upsilon^{n-\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\Upsilon^{n-\frac{3}{2}},

(5.3)

and compute also
{E^{n}}
and
{S^{n}}
from it.
In our numerical experiments we saw that using fixed-point iterations to improve the accuracy of
\Upsilon^{n}
did not change the errors.

5.3
Discretization of the phase

Motivated by (

), we define the approximation to

\displaystyle\zeta^{n+1}_{\textup{I}}=\zeta^{n}_{\textup{I}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\ln\left\lvert\det{Q^{n+1}}\right\rvert-\ln\left\lvert\det{Q^{n}}\right\rvert\right).

This update formula ensures norm preservation of the wave packet.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
{u^{n}}
and
{u^{n+1}}
denote two Gaussian wave packets with parameters
({q^{n}},v^{n},{Q^{n}},\Upsilon^{n},\zeta^{n})
and

5.4
) is equivalent to

\left\lVert{u^{n}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=\left\lVert{u^{n+1}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}.

(5.5)

Proof.

Because of

\displaystyle\left\lVert{u^{n}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{2}{\varepsilon}\zeta_{I}^{n}\bigr)(\varepsilon\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}\left\lvert\det Q^{n}\right\rvert,

(5.6)

the norm preservation is equivalent to

\exp\bigl(\tfrac{2}{\varepsilon}(\zeta_{I}^{n}-\zeta_{I}^{n+1})\bigr)=\frac{\left\lvert\det{Q}^{n}\right\rvert}{\left\lvert\det{Q}^{n+1}\right\rvert}.

(5.7)

This proves the statement.
∎

Furthermore, for the integration of (

(5.8)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\frac{\varepsilon\tau}{2}\,\mathrm{tr}\>\!\left(\langle\partial_{x}^{2}h\rangle_{{u}}^{n}{Q^{n}}({Q^{n}})^{*}-2({Q^{n}}{Q^{n}}^{*})^{-1}\right)

(5.9)

In general, one has to apply Gauss-Hermite quadrature rule to approximate the averages, see

5.5
Modified classical Runge–Kutta method

As an alternative to the Boris-type algorithm, we propose a modification of the classical Runge–Kutta method (RK4) of order 4. The modification consists of updating the component
\zeta_{\textup{I}}
in each intermediate step by using (
5.4
) to get the approximations to
\zeta_{\textup{I}}(t_{n+1/2})
and
\zeta_{\textup{I}}(t_{n+1})
. All other components are updated by the standard RK4 procedure. In contrast to the original RK4 scheme, the modification automatically conserves the
L^{2}
-norm of a Gaussian wave packet.

Appendix: Gaussian calculus and energy formula

Lemma A.1
.

Consider smooth functions
W\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
and
w\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
, and an arbitrary matrix
M\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
.
Let
{u}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a Gaussian wave packet with position
{q}
and width
{\mathcal{C}}={{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}+\mathrm{i}{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\langle W(x-{q})\rangle_{{u}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\left((\langle\partial_{\ell}W\rangle_{{u}}{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})_{k\ell}\right)_{k=1}^{d},

(A.1)

\displaystyle\langle(x-{q})^{\top}wM(x-{q})\rangle_{{u}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\langle w\rangle_{{u}}\mathrm{tr}\>(M{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\mathrm{tr}\>(\langle\nabla^{2}w\rangle_{{u}}{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}M{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}).

(A.2)

In particular,

\langle w(x-{q})\rangle_{{u}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}\langle\nabla w\rangle_{{u}}
.

Proof.

The position density

\left\lvert{u}(t,x)\right\rvert^{2}=\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(x-{q})^{\top}{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}(x-{q})-\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\zeta_{\textup{I}}\right)

(A.3)

satisfies

\nabla\left\lvert{u}(t,x)\right\rvert^{2}=-\frac{2}{\varepsilon}{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}(x-{q})\left\lvert{u}(t,x)\right\rvert^{2}.

Therefore, partial integration yields that

\displaystyle\langle W(x-{q})\rangle_{{u}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}W(x)\,{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}\nabla\left\lvert{u}(t,x)\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

(A.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\left(\langle\partial_{\ell}(W{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})_{k,\ell}\rangle_{{u}}\right)_{k=1}^{d}.

(A.5)

Similarly, two partial integrations imply that

\displaystyle\langle w(x-{q})^{\top}M(x-{q})\rangle_{{u}}=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{d}\Big\langle w({{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}(x-{q}))_{k}({{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}M{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})_{k\ell}({{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}(x-{q}))_{\ell}\Big\rangle_{{u}}

(A.6)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{d}\left(\langle w\rangle_{{u}}({{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}})_{k\ell}+\big\langle\partial_{\ell}w({{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}(x-{q}))_{k}\big\rangle_{{u}}\right)({{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}M{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})_{k\ell}

(A.7)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\langle w\rangle_{{u}}({{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}})_{k\ell}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\big\langle\partial_{k}\partial_{\ell}w\big\rangle_{{u}}\right)({{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}M{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})_{k\ell}

(A.8)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\langle w\rangle_{{u}}\,\mathrm{tr}\>(M{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\mathrm{tr}\>(\langle\nabla^{2}w\rangle_{{u}}{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}M{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}).

(A.9)

∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
{u}
be a normalized Gaussian wave packet with center
({q},{p})
and width
{\mathcal{C}}={{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}+\mathrm{i}{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}
. Then it holds for the energy

\displaystyle\langle H\rangle_{{u}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{p}^{2}-{p}^{\top}\langle{A}\rangle_{{u}}+\frac{1}{2}\langle{A}^{2}\rangle_{{u}}+\langle\phi\rangle_{{u}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}R_{H}

(A.10)

with remainder

R_{H}=\mathrm{tr}\>\left(\left({{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}^{2}+{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}^{2}-2\langle J_{{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{{u}}{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}\right){{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}\right).

(A.11)

If the magnetic potential
{A}
is linear in
x
, then
\langle{A}\rangle_{{u}}={A}({q})
and

\displaystyle\langle{A}^{2}\rangle_{{u}}

\displaystyle={A}({q})^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathrm{tr}\>\left(J_{{A}}({q})^{\top}J_{{A}}({q}){{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}\right)

(A.12)

Proof.

In the following we ignore the dependence on
t
to simplify the notation. Since
\langle H\rangle_{{u}}=\langle h\rangle_{{u}}=\frac{1}{2}\langle(\xi-{A})^{2}\rangle_{{u}}+\langle\phi\rangle_{{u}}
, we only need to work on the mean of
\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}((\xi-{A})^{2})=(\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(\xi-{A}))^{2}
. We have

\displaystyle\langle(\xi-{A})^{2}\rangle_{{u}}=\langle(\xi-{A}){u},(\xi-{A}){u}\rangle

(A.13)

\displaystyle=\langle({\mathcal{C}}(x-{q})+({p}-{A})){u},({\mathcal{C}}(x-{q})+({p}-{A})){u}\rangle

(A.14)

\displaystyle=\langle(x-{q})^{\top}{\mathcal{C}}^{*}{\mathcal{C}}(x-{q})\rangle_{{u}}+\langle({p}-{A})^{\top}({\mathcal{C}}+{\mathcal{C}}^{*})(x-{q})\rangle_{{u}}+\langle({p}-{A})^{2}\rangle_{{u}}

(A.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathrm{tr}\>({\mathcal{C}}^{*}{\mathcal{C}}{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathrm{tr}\>(\langle J_{{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{{u}}({\mathcal{C}}+{\mathcal{C}}^{*}){{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})+\langle({p}-{A})^{2}\rangle_{{u}}

(A.16)

due to
Lemma

A.1
.
For the traces, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\>({\mathcal{C}}^{*}{\mathcal{C}}{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})=\mathrm{tr}\>(({{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}-\mathrm{i}{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}})({{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}+\mathrm{i}{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}){{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})=\mathrm{tr}\>(({{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}^{2}+{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{I}}}^{2}){{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})

(A.17)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\>(\langle J_{{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{{u}}({\mathcal{C}}+{\mathcal{C}}^{*}){{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})=2\,\mathrm{tr}\>(\langle J_{{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{{u}}{{\mathcal{C}}_{\textup{R}}}{{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}).

(A.18)

Combining the terms, we obtain (
A.10
). In the linear case, we expand
{A}(x)={A}({q})+J_{{A}}({q})^{\top}(x-{q})
and use
Lemma

A.1
to prove (
A.12
).
∎
