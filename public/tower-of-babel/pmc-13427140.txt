A Crystalline and Thermally Stable Selenocysteine Selenenic Acid
Authors: Ryosuke Masuda, Satoru Kuwano, Kei Goto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite more than half a century of research on selenoproteins, the central catalytic intermediate, selenocysteine selenenic acid (Sec–SeOH), has remained experimentally elusive. Its isolation has long been impeded by its presumed instability and propensity for thermal deselenation. Here, we report the first isolable Sec–SeOH at ambient temperature. This relies on a bioinspired design of a selenopeptide sequence encapsulated within a protective cradle, together with an oxidant‐free route from the corresponding selenenyl iodides (Sec–SeI), enabling x‐ray structural analysis and chemical characterization. The isolated Sec–SeOH shows unexpected resistance to β‐elimination to dehydroalanine (DHA). Oxidation experiments combined with theoretical calculations demonstrate that conversion to DHA proceeds preferentially via overoxidation to the seleninic acid (Sec–SeO2H), for which β‐elimination is substantially more favorable. Reactivity profiling further highlights the pronounced electrophilicity of Sec–SeOH toward biologically and pharmacologically relevant nucleophiles. These findings redefine the stability–reactivity landscape of Sec–SeOH and provide a foundation for strategies aimed at preventing selenoprotein inactivation. Beyond defining an isolable Sec–SeOH model, the work provides a molecular‐level rationale for how selenoproteins can combine high selenium‐centered reactivity with resistance to irreversible oxidative self‐inactivation.

Introduction

Since the identification of selenocysteine (Sec) as the 21st amino acid in 1973 [1, 2], the catalytic cycle of glutathione peroxidase (GPx) [3, 4] has served as a foundational model for redox regulation [5, 6, 7, 8]. At the heart of this cycle lies the transient selenocysteine selenenic acid (Sec–SeOH) intermediate [4], formed via oxidation of Sec by reactive oxygen species (ROS) (“Catalytic processes” in Figure 1A). Regeneration of the reduced selenol (Sec–SeH) is generally proposed to proceed through a selenenyl sulfide (Sec–SeSR) form upon reduction by glutathione (GSH) [9]. Despite this central role, Sec–SeOH has remained experimentally elusive, in clear contrast to its well‐characterized cysteine analogues [10, 11]. Even efforts to trap Sec–SeOH within proteins have met with limited success [12, 13, 14, 15]. This difficulty arises from its notorious thermal instability and irreversible degradation to dehydroalanine (DHA) derivatives (“Inactivation processes” in Figure 1A) [16, 17, 18, 19]. Such inactivation is thought to occur either via overoxidation to seleninic acid (Sec–SeO2H) followed by deselenation (Pathway I in Figure 1A) [16, 17, 18] or through direct β‐elimination of Sec–SeOH (Pathway II) [19]. Although a cyclic selenenyl amide [20] has been proposed as a protective resting state that suppresses these pathways [21], the intrinsic resistance of Sec–SeOH toward β‐elimination remains a fundamental mechanistic mystery in selenocysteine chemistry. The chemical basis for this balance between catalytic proficiency and resistance to inactivation is a central question in selenoprotein chemistry. In seminal studies by Chaudière and co‐workers, a sulfur analogue of murine GPx, in which the active‐site Sec was replaced with Cys, exhibited markedly attenuated activity compared with the native enzyme and was readily inactivated by hydroperoxides [22]. This contrast suggests that selenium is not merely a more reactive congener of sulfur, but also contributes to resistance against irreversible oxidative damage during peroxide reduction. Direct experimental information on the stability and degradation pathways of Sec–SeOH is therefore essential for understanding the selective advantage of Sec in redox enzymes.

Discriminating between pathways I and II therefore requires two stringent criteria: (1) the oxidant‐free formation of Sec–SeOH and (2) a molecular framework that precludes intramolecular cyclization (Figure 1A). To resolve these mechanistic ambiguities, isolable model compounds are indispensable. We recently developed a meta‐phenylene‐based protective cradle (Bpsc group) [12, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27] that encapsulates a selenopeptide, enabling the first NMR characterization of Sec–SeOH (Figure 1C) [23]. However, because those experiments relied on excess H2O2, it remained impossible to determine whether DHA formation proceeded through pathway I or II. A compelling biological precedent exists in type‐3 iodothyronine deiodinase (DIO3), in which a –Sec–Pro– sequence prevents the formation of a cyclic selenenyl amide. Although it has been postulated that Sec–SeOH in DIO3 arises from hydrolysis of a selenenyl iodide (Sec–SeI) form, direct spectroscopic evidence for this process remains lacking (Figure 1B) [28].

Guided by this enzymatic precedent, we designed a DIO3‐inspired model incorporating the –Sec–Pro– motif within the Bpsc protective cradle. This platform enabled the generation of Sec–SeOH through a non‐oxidative hydrolytic route, thereby circumventing the use of excess H2O2 (Figure 1D). Herein, we report the first isolation and X‐ray structural characterization of Sec–SeOH. Our results provide experimental and computational evidence that (i) Sec–SeOH is thermally stable at ambient temperature in the absence of oxidants such as H2O2 and (ii) DHA formation proceeds preferentially via overoxidation to Sec–SeO2H (Pathway I) rather than via direct β‐elimination (Pathway II). These findings provide a new structural foundation for understanding and potentially preventing selenoprotein inactivation. More broadly, these results address how oxidized Sec intermediates can retain sufficient persistence to undergo productive thiol‐mediated resolution, while simultaneously remaining susceptible to irreversible inactivation upon overoxidation.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis, Structure, and Stability of Sec–SeOH

We devised N‐Bpsc‐protected Sec–SeI 2a, incorporating the –Sec–Pro–Pro– motif, as a precursor to Sec–SeOH (see Schemes S1‒S3 in the Supporting Information for the experimental details). Guided by our previous findings [23, 25], the oxidation of selenopeptide 3a with I2 (1.0 equiv) furnished 2a in the form of reddish‐purple crystals in 90% yield (Figure 2A). 1H NMR monitoring of the reaction of 2a with NaOH (10 equiv) in [D8]tetrahydrofuran ([D8]THF)/D2O at −20°C revealed the quantitative formation of Sec–SeOH 1a (Figure S1), as evidenced by the diagnostic 77Se NMR signal at 1063 parts per million (ppm) (Figure S2). Prompted by this result, 2a was hydrolyzed at −20°C, followed by quenching with phosphate buffer (pH 7) (Figure 2A). Slow recrystallization from MeCN at −20°C allowed the isolation of the target Sec–SeOH 1a as colorless crystals in 76% yield. This stands in stark contrast to our previous attempts to isolate Sec–SeOH species generated by H2O2 oxidation, where isolation was possibly hampered by excess oxidant and/or thermal cyclization of Sec–SeOH to the cyclic selenenyl amide [23, 25]. Sec–SeOH 1b, bearing a –Sec–Pro– moiety, was also successfully isolated in crystalline form.

Slow evaporation of a concentrated solution of Sec–SeOH 1b in Et2O/MeCN at −20°C furnished single crystals suitable for single‐crystal x‐ray diffraction (SXRD) analysis, which unequivocally confirmed the structure of 1b (Figures 2B, S27, and S28) [29]. The SeOH moiety of 1b is securely cradled within the Bpsc framework, effectively shielding it from bimolecular reactions [30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37]. The Se1–O1 bond length [1.829(5) Å] is longer than those of previously reported areneselenenic acid encapsulated by a calix[6]arene [1.763(7) Å] [34] and BmtSeOH [1.808(3) Å] (Bmt = 4‐tert‐butyl‐2,6‐bis[(2,2′′,6,6′′‐tetramethyl‐m‐terphenyl‐2′‐yl)] [36]. Taken together with the C1–Se1–O1 bond angle [97.7(3)°], these structural parameters support the assignment of 1b as the selenenyl form (R–Se–O–H) rather than the selenoxide form (R–Se(O)–H). To date, crystal structures of oxidized glutathione peroxidases have been reported only for Sec–SeO2H forms (GPx1 at 0.2 nm resolution [38] and GPx4 at 1.0 nm resolution [39]). Accordingly, the present crystallographic characterization represents the first unambiguous structural definition of Sec–SeOH and provides a long‐sought structural blueprint for refining oxidized selenoprotein architectures.

The 77Se{1H} NMR spectra (C6D6) of isolated Sec–SeOHs 1a and 1b exhibited resonances at 1063 ppm (Figure 3B) and 1087 ppm (Figure S10), respectively, in good agreement with that of Sec–SeOH bearing the Sec‒Gly‒Gly sequence, generated in situ by H2O2 oxidation of the corresponding Sec–SeH precursor [1082 ppm] [23]. The 1H NMR spectra of 1a and 1b displayed singlet signals at 6.25 and 4.79 ppm (C6D6), respectively (Figures 3A and S8), which were unambiguously assigned to the Se–OH protons based on 1H–77Se heteronuclear multiple bond coherence experiments (Figures S6 and S11). Broad absorption bands at ṽ = 3268 cm−1 (1a) and 3352 cm−1 (1b) in the infrared (IR) spectra further corroborated the presence of Se–OH moieties (Figures 3C and S19).

Spectra of Sec–SeOH 1a. (A) 1H NMR spectrum (400 MHz, C6D6), (B) 77Se NMR spectrum (76 MHz, C6D6), and (C) FT‐IR spectrum (CCl4) of isolated 1a.

Contrary to the long‐standing belief that Sec–SeOHs are thermally labile and inherently unisolable, both 1a and 1b proved remarkably robust. These compounds can be handled under an argon atmosphere even at ambient temperature and can be stored at −20°C for at least 3 months without detectable decomposition. Notably, no degradation of 1a was observed upon heating in C6D6 at 70°C for 7 h (Figure S21). Furthermore, 1a remained chemically intact in solution at ambient temperature even after 1 week, underscoring the substantial resistance of Sec–SeOH toward β‐elimination.

Oxidation of Sec–SeOH

Under conditions of high oxidative stress, the Sec–SeOH forms of GPx1 [40, 41] and GPx4 [42] are believed to undergo overoxidation to the corresponding Sec–SeO2H species. Motivated by the successful isolation of Sec–SeOHs, we next sought to explore the origins of the apparent lability of Sec residues in selenoproteins. To eliminate potential steric or electronic influences from the proline residue, we prepared a simplified model compound, Sec–SeOH 1c, based on a Sec methyl ester scaffold, via hydrolysis of the corresponding Sec–SeI [43] (Scheme S9). Quenching and recrystallization afforded colorless crystals containing 1c as the major component, accompanied by DHA 4c [1c:4c = 86:14] (Figure S13). A distinct resonance at 1122 ppm in the 77Se{1H} NMR spectrum (C6D6) (Figure S15) confirmed the identity of 1c. In addition, the 1H NMR spectrum ([D8]THF, −20°C) of recrystallized 1c was identical to that recorded for 1c generated by H2O2 oxidation of the corresponding Sec–SeH (Figure S17) [23], firmly establishing that Sec–SeOHs can be accessed through both oxidative and hydrolytic pathways.

The isolated 1c exhibited substantial persistence under oxidant‐free conditions. Even after 52 h under argon atmosphere at room temperature, the composition of a C6D6 solution of recrystallized 1c changed only minimally (1c:4c = 86:14 to 74:16) (Figure 4A, the top, and Figure S22). In contrast, exposure of 1c to strongly oxidizing conditions (1 equiv H2O2) led to complete consumption of 1c within 40 min, affording DHA 4c in 64% NMR yield (Figure 4A, the bottom, and Figure S25). Notably, during each 1H NMR monitoring, the Sec–SeO2H form was not observed. These findings are consistent with our previous observation that 1c generated in situ via oxidation with excess H2O2 was stable at −20°C but underwent conversion to 4c upon gentle warming to 5°C [23]. Accordingly, β‐elimination of Sec–SeOH appears to be very slow, if it occurs at all, while that of Sec–SeO2H proceeds rapidly (Figure 4B). Density functional theory calculations (SMD(THF)/ZORA‐M06‐2X/ZORA‐def2‐TZVPP//ZORA‐OPBE/ZORA‐def2‐TZVPP) [44, 45] support this interpretation. Concerted β‐elimination of the Sec–SeO2H form of N‐acetyl‐Sec‐Pro‐OMe proceeds with a significantly lower activation barrier (24.4 kcal mol−1) than that of the Sec–SeOH form (42.2 kcal mol−1) (Figure 4C), which is consistent with the absence of observable Sec–SeO2H signals during NMR monitoring (Figure S25). These results demonstrate the preferential β‐elimination of Sec–SeO2Hs over Sec–SeOHs.

This mechanistic distinction is relevant to native selenoproteins because it separates two processes that have often been conflated: the intrinsic thermal lability of Sec–SeOH and the oxidative inactivation of Sec residues under peroxide stress. Our results indicate that Sec–SeOH itself can persist long enough to undergo productive resolution by thiol‐based reductants, whereas irreversible DHA formation becomes kinetically favored after overoxidation to Sec–SeO2H. Thus, the principal vulnerability of oxidized Sec is not the immediate β‐elimination of Sec–SeOH, but the competition between reductive resolution and further oxidation.

Oxidation of Sec–SeOH by molecular oxygen also appears to be slow. Exposure of 1c to air at ambient temperature for 19 h resulted in only a limited compositional change (1c:4c = 86:14 to 71:29) (Figure S23). Accordingly, protected Sec–SeOH species can possess finite lifetimes under aerobic, GSH‐deficient conditions (Figure 1A). Taken together with our previous findings that a Sec–SeOH bearing the Sec‒Gly‒Gly sequence resists rapid overoxidation at −20°C even in the presence of excess H2O2 [23], these results indicate that overoxidation of Sec–SeOHs to Sec–SeO2Hs is not necessarily rapid under oxidative conditions at low temperature. In contrast, once overoxidation does occur, Sec–SeO2Hs are rapidly converted into DHA via the β‐elimination. This should not be interpreted to imply that Sec–SeOH is intrinsically resistant to overoxidation at elevated or physiological temperatures under sustained peroxide stress. Rather, our data indicate that the fate of Sec–SeOH is governed by the competition between productive thiol‐mediated resolution and further oxidation to Sec–SeO2H, the latter leading rapidly to DHA formation. Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that the irreversible inactivation of selenoproteins under oxidative conditions arises from the overoxidation of Sec–SeOH (Pathway I in Figure 1A). Given our previous demonstration of the high resistance of cyclic selenenyl amides toward H2O2 oxidation [23, 25], the principal role of the proposed bypass mechanism in the catalytic cycle of GPx (Figure 1A) [21] is therefore likely to prevent overoxidation of Sec–SeOH rather than merely suppress thermal β‐elimination. Chaudière and co‐workers previously proposed that marked hydroperoxide‐induced inactivation of the [Cys]GPx mutant arises from overoxidation of cysteine sulfenic acid (Cys–SOH) followed by β‐elimination to DHA [22]. Viewed in this context, the present findings provide a chemical rationale for the use of Sec as a catalytic redox center: the high nucleophilicity of Sec–SeH enables rapid peroxide reduction, whereas the finite persistence of Sec–SeOH, together with protective pathways that suppress further oxidation to the β‐elimination‐prone Sec–SeO2H state, helps prevent irreversible deselenation during catalysis. Together, these properties explain how Sec can combine exceptional catalytic reactivity with resistance to oxidative self‐inactivation, a balance that is difficult to achieve with cysteine‐based active sites.

Reactions of Sec–SeOH

The reaction of Sec–SeOH with cysteine thiols (Cys–SHs) to form selenenyl sulfides (Sec–SeSR) is recognized as a pivotal process in the catalytic cycle of GPx and thioredoxin reductase (TrxR) [46, 47]. Treatment of Sec–SeOH 1a with N‐acetyl cysteine methyl ester (1.2 equiv) led to smooth conversion to the corresponding Sec–SeSR 5, reaching >99% NMR yield within 23 min (Figures 5A and S24). The pronounced electrophilicity of 1a toward the cysteine thiol contrasts sharply with that of N‐Bpsc‐Cys(SOH)‐OMe, the sulfur analogue of 1c, which requires triethylamine as an external base [11]. These results suggest that the substantial electrophilicity of Sec–SeOH in selenoproteins enables its rapid capture by Cys–SH species. In the context of iodothyronine deiodinases, a hydrolytically generated Sec–SeOH species could be intercepted by biological thiol‐containing species, such as GSH, a resolving Cys residue of thioredoxin, or a nearby proteinaceous Cys residue in the deiodinase active‐site environment, forming Sec–SeSR‐type species that ultimately regenerate Sec–SeH before overoxidation. Although direct observation in native deiodinases remains an important future goal [28, 48], the present model establishes the intrinsic feasibility of this resolution pathway.

We next evaluated reactivity toward pharmacologically relevant soft nucleophiles. The antithyroid drug 6‐n‐propylthiouracil (PTU) is known to inhibit the deiodination activity of DIO [49]. This inhibition plausibly involves the reaction of Sec–SeI with PTU, as demonstrated by du Mont and co‐workers in a small‐molecule system [50]. In light of the hypothesis in Figure 1B [28], the Sec–SeOH form of DIO may likewise be relevant to this inhibition pathway. Indeed, treatment of 1a with PTU (3.0 equiv) quantitatively afforded the corresponding Se‐PTU product 6, the structure of which was confirmed by SXRD analysis (Figure 5B). These results indicate that Sec–SeOH 1a readily reacts with soft nucleophiles, e.g., Cys–SH and PTU, even in the absence of an external base.

2‐Iodoacetamide (IAM) is a common alkylation agent for Cys and Sec residues, delivering the corresponding S‐ and Se‐ alkylated compounds owing to the nucleophilic character of the chalcogens. Strikingly, however, reaction of 1a with IAM (3.0 equiv) in the presence of triethylamine (3.5 equiv) furnished selenenamide 7 in 66% isolated yield, while the alkyl iodide moiety remained intact, indicating that IAM acts as a nucleophile toward Sec–SeOHs (Figure 5A). The unusual formation of selenenamide 7 from IAM further underscores the exceptional electrophilicity of the Se center in Sec–SeOH, because the carboxamide nitrogen is only weakly nucleophilic. This observation may also provide a useful perspective on earlier studies of iodine‐mediated oxidation of Sec‐containing peptides, in which complex product mixtures, including nucleophile‐incorporated products, were observed [51]. In retrospect, some of these products may reflect trapping of transient Sec–SeOH‐type intermediates by external nucleophiles.

Finally, treatment of 1a with excess methanol quantitatively yielded the corresponding methyl selenenate 8 (Figure 5A). A signal appeared at 1233 ppm (C6D6) in the 77Se{1H} NMR spectrum of 8, consistent with that of transiently generated 2,4,6‐(t‐Bu)3C6H4SeOMe (1269 ppm, CDCl3) [31]. Collectively, these findings highlight the distinctive electrophilic character of Sec–SeOH 1a and its broad reactivity toward biologically and pharmacologically relevant nucleophiles.

Conclusion

More than five decades have passed since Ganther [1] and Flohé [2] first identified selenium in natural proteins, laying the foundations of selenoprotein biology. We herein achieved the first isolation and single‐crystal x‐ray structure determination of a selenocysteine selenenic acid (Sec–SeOH) using a selenopeptide model encapsulated within a protective molecular cradle and accessed through an oxidant‐free route via hydrolysis of the corresponding selenenyl iodide (Sec–SeI). The isolated Sec–SeOH exhibits pronounced resistance toward β‐elimination at ambient temperature. Combined oxidation experiments and density functional theory calculations demonstrate that dehydroalanine (DHA) formation proceeds predominantly after overoxidation to the seleninic acid (Sec–SeO2H), for which β‐elimination is kinetically accessible, rather than directly from Sec–SeOH. Reactivity profiling further establishes Sec–SeOH as a highly electrophilic selenium intermediate that is readily trapped by biologically and pharmacologically relevant nucleophiles. Collectively, these results redefine the stability–reactivity landscape of Sec–SeOH and provide a basis for clarifying oxidative deselenation pathways in selenocysteine chemistry. In this context, our findings indicate that the GPx bypass pathway serves predominantly to suppress overoxidation of Sec–SeOH. More broadly, this study provides a molecular‐level framework for understanding why Sec can serve as a highly reactive redox center without undergoing immediate irreversible deselenation: Sec–SeOH is intrinsically more persistent than previously assumed, but becomes vulnerable upon overoxidation to Sec–SeO2H. Thus, the crystalline model transforms Sec–SeOH from a long‐postulated but elusive intermediate into an experimentally defined platform for understanding the interplay between catalysis, repair, and inactivation in selenium‐dependent enzymes.
