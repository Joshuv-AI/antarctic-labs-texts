Phase-selective growth of GaS films by chemical vapor deposition
Authors: Stefano Dicorato, Maria Losurdo, Fabio Palumbo, Maria M. Giangregorio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gallium sulfide (GaS) is a layered semiconductor that exhibits polymorphism, making the controlled synthesis of specific crystalline phases essential for tailoring properties and potential applications. In this work, we develop a phase-selective chemical vapor deposition (CVD) strategy for controlling the polymorphism and growth mode of GaS thin films using Ga2S3 as a single precursor and pure H2 as the carrier gas. The effects of pressure, flux, temperature, and growth rate on the phase, crystallinity, and morphology are systematically investigated by Raman spectroscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and atomic force microscopy (AFM). By controlling the growth conditions, the GaS films are selectively tuned from the 2H to the 3R phase, and their growth mode from island-like to a layer-by-layer. Highly crystalline and uniform 3R GaS films are obtained at 100 mbar, 50 sccm, and 550 °C, with a growth rate of approximately 10 nm min−1. Different experimental conditions result in a more island-growth mode and worsening of structural and morphological properties. We also discuss the synergetic effects of H2 in the improvement of the precursor reduction and the film stoichiometry allowing the deposition of 3R films with a high uniform coverage of ∼3 cm2. 2H GaS films are deposited at pressure < 3 mbar and growth rate < 2.5 nm min−1. These results establish a direct process–structure relationship for phase-selective GaS growth and provide a scalable route to centimeter-scale 3R GaS films.

Introduction

Polymorphism has emerged as a pivotal research area in the rapidly expanding field of two-dimensional (2D) materials because it provides unique opportunities to tailor their electronic and optical properties through structural engineering without changing their chemical composition.1 While significant progress has been made in understanding and controlling polymorphism in transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), post-transition metal chalcogenides, such as gallium sulfide (GaS), remain relatively underexplored despite their promising properties.2–5

GaS exhibits two main crystalline phases: the thermodynamically stable 2H (hexagonal) phase and metastable 3R (rhombohedral) phase. In its stable 2H phase, GaS crystallizes in a hexagonal lattice with space group P63/mmc (Fig. 1a).

Crystalline structures of (a) 2H and (b) 3R GaS. The 2H unit cell, indicated with a solid line, has two layers, whereas in the case of the 3R cell, it has three layers. A side and top view sketch of S-Ga-Ga-S layer and the tripe Ga–S bonds of each GaS phase are shown at the side. The purple lines are plotted as a reference of the orientation and symmetry of the layers in each crystalline structure.

In this phase, the S-Ga-Ga-S layers are non-centrosymmetric, and adjacent layers are stacked with alternating 180° rotations around the c-axis. In contrast, the metastable 3R phase crystallizes in the R̄3m space group (Fig. 1b)3 and exhibits a different stacking sequence characterized by a lateral displacement of 1/3 of a unit cell along the [1010] direction.

This difference in layer stacking results in distinct electronic properties: 2H GaS exhibits an indirect bandgap, whereas 3R has been reported to exhibit a direct bandgap of approximately 2.55 eV with near-blue light emission at room temperature, making the latter particularly attractive for optoelectronic applications such as light-emitting devices and photodetectors.6

Several approaches have been employed to synthesize layered GaS, including mechanical exfoliation, liquid phase exfoliation, screen printing deposition, atomic layer deposition, pulsed laser deposition, physical vapour deposition and chemical vapor deposition (CVD).7–15

Among these methods, CVD is particularly promising for the scalable growth of large-area 2D GaS films.3,5,16–18

However, previous studies have predominantly focused on the stable 2H phase, whereas the controlled synthesis of the metastable 3R phase remains challenging. The metastable 3R phase was first reported in 1973 (ref. 19–21) following the laser-induced destabilization of GaS crystals. More recently, in 2024, we reported the first successful CVD growth of 3R GaS.3 Therefore, the relationship between the CVD growth parameters, phase selection, nucleation behavior, and film morphology has not yet been systematically established because, in addition to the feasibility of obtaining the metastable phase, it is necessary to improve the control over its nucleation and large-area growth. In this regard, previous CVD approaches based on Ga2S3 as single precursor, typically employing H2/Ar carrier-gas mixtures, have reported film uniformity, with a maximum uniform coverage of approximately 0.7 cm2.5,16

To address these challenges, we investigate a single-precursor CVD strategy based on Ga2S3 and pure H2, exploiting H2 not only as a carrier gas but also as a reducing atmosphere. We hypothesize that the use of pure H2 can promote a more favorable chemical environment for GaS formation and improve film uniformity and stoichiometric control.5 The specific objective of this study is to establish the processing conditions to selectively obtain the 2H or 3R phase while controlling the transition between layer-by-layer and island growth modes. We systematically investigate the effects of pressure, H2 flux, and growth temperature on the crystal phase, growth rate, crystallinity, surface morphology, and the evolution of the growth mode has been rationalized in terms of nucleation and growth kinetics.

We discuss the role of pure H2 as reducing agent in improving the stoichiometry and uniformity of the films.

This work advances the field of phase engineering in 2D materials and paves the way for the scalable synthesis of GaS-based devices for optoelectronic applications.

Experimental section

Materials synthesis

The growth of GaS thin films was performed using a single-zone horizontal tube furnace, as schematized in Fig. 2, equipped with a 40 mm in diameter quartz tube. Al2O3c-plane (0001) substrates were cleaned using high purity acetone, isopropyl alcohol, and ethanol by sequential sonication for 10 minutes followed by drying under nitrogen flow.

Schematic process for the CVD synthesis of GaS from Ga2S3 powder and pure H2.

High purity (99.99%) Ga2S3 powder was used as the sole precursor. The substrates were strategically positioned downstream within the furnace to set their temperature.

Due to the single-zone nature of the furnace, a stable, reproducible temperature gradient naturally establishes along the downstream region. By placing the substrates at specific distances from the hot center zone, the corresponding deposition temperatures ranging from 425 °C to 660 °C were calibrated.

Prior to deposition, the tube was evacuated to a base pressure of 8 × 10−3 mbar and purged with Ar gas to eliminate residual oxygen. Subsequently, the furnace was heated to the reaction temperature of 800 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1, while hydrogen (H2) gas was introduced at three distinct flow rates: 50 sccm, 100 sccm, and 200 sccm. Upon reaching the reaction temperature, the furnace was shifted from the precursor to the substrate zone. Deposition conditions, including temperature (ranging from 425 °C to 660 °C), flux (ranging from 50 sccm to 200 sccm), and pressure (ranging from 3 mbar to 300 mbar), were meticulously controlled throughout the process. The deposition time is 90 min. After deposition, the furnace was repositioned away from the precursor and substrate and naturally cooled down while H2 gas continued to flow at a rate of 50 sccm (equivalent to 3.5 × 10−1 mbar).

Characterization

To establish a direct correlation between the growth parameters, phases, crystallinity, morphological features and growth modes, GaS films were comprehensively characterized using a combination of Raman spectroscopy, Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) and Spectroscopic Ellipsometry.

Structural characterization was performed by Raman spectroscopy in backscattering configuration with a LabRam Horiba using a 532 nm wavelength laser (20 mW) with a ×100 microscope objective (NA = 0.9). Raman spectroscopy was used to assess the structural quality, phase of the samples. Morphological analysis was performed using atomic force microscopy (AFM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). AFM analysis was performed using the Autoprobe CP (Thermomicroscope). The sample topography and phase were recorded in a single-pass mode using a gold-coated Si tip (frequency of 80 Hz) in non-contact mode. AFM was employed to obtain high-resolution topographical maps and surface roughness (RMS) measurements, providing nanoscale information about the sample morphology and surface features, like grain size and shape and height of step edges. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was carried out with a Zeiss Supra 40 FEG SEM equipped with a Gemini field emission gun. Analyses were carried out at an extraction voltage of 3 kV and a 30-µm aperture. Spectroscopic Ellipsometry (HORIBA Ltd, Palaiseau, France) was used to measure spectra of the pseudodielectric functions <ε> = <ε1> + i<ε2> at the incident angle of 70 °in the range 0.75–6.50 eV. Through the modelling of the ellipsometric spectra as discussed in our previous work,17 the film thicknesses and hence the growth rate (rG) were derived. In Fig. S1 two representative ellipsometric spectra of the imaginary part, <ε2>, of the pseudodielectic functions are reported, showing a different number of interference fringes below 3 eV, that correlates to a different film thickness. Values in the range of 77–963 nm were determined.

Results

Fig. 3l shows two representative Raman spectra of 2H and 3R films where three main peaks are present: two intense A modes, A1g1 at ∼188 cm−1, and A1g2 at ∼363 cm−1 and a lower intense band, E mode, in the range 290–295 cm−1, whose position is correlated with the GaS phase.3

Fig. 3m shows the evolution of the E mode as a function of pressure and flux and the corresponding Raman spectra are reported in Fig. S2.

At low pressure (3 mbar), the E mode is at 295.0 ± 0.5 cm−1 indicative that GaS films exclusively crystallize in the 2H phase, regardless of flow rate.

Increasing the pressure to 100 mbar and 300 mbar and flux to 100 sccm and 200 sccm, the E mode shifts to lower cm−1, ∼290.0 ± 0.5 cm−1 as an indication that the 3R phase dominates at high pressures across all flow rates.

The E-mode shift of 5 ± 1 cm−1 between the 2H and 3R phases is structural and symmetry-driven. This assignment based on the E-mode position is supported by our previous work,3 where the Raman fingerprints were independently validated by XRD measurements and density functional perturbation theory, DFPT, calculations. Similarly, this shift was also found in GaSe with analogous symmetry.22

The statistical distribution of the Raman spectra measured over more than 10 samples in various random points is shown in Fig. S3, corroborating the Raman accuracy to the position of the E mode.

SEM images of both 2H and 3R films in Fig. 3 show that the surface coverage and grain size decrease increasing flux and/or pressure. However, the grains of 3R films are more ordered and regular than 2H films. At 100 mbar and 50 sccm (Fig. 3b), 3R GaS films show a layer-by-layer growth with a uniform and flat surface, large grains (2–3µm) and sharp edges while, increasing flux and/or pressure, they move to an island growth mode, grains are smaller (in the range 150–300 nm) and more isolated with a mixed shape of triangles, truncated triangles and hexagons, and tower-like structures, with a stronger effect changing the flux with respect to the pressure.

The morphology can be well correlated with the evolution of the A1g1/A1g2 intensity ratio in Raman spectra and with RMS data from AFM measurements. Specifically, Raman spectra provide a sensitive probe of crystalline quality and crystal orientation: values of the A1g1/A1g2 close to 1 are indicative of high-quality, c-axis-oriented and basal plane exposed films, while higher values reflect degradation in crystalline order with other planes exposed.23 Analogously, RMS data are indicative of the surface homogeneity and coverage. Fig. 4a and b summarize the evolution of the A1g1/A1g2 ratio and RMS data calculated on 2 µm × 2 µm areas, respectively, as a function of pressure and flux at 550 °C. 3R GaS film deposited at a flux of 50 sccm and 100 sccm (Fig. 3b) has a A1g1/A1g2 ratio ≈1 and a RMS of 1.2 nm confirming, in addition of a very uniform surface, a high crystallinity. Moving away from these experimental conditions, A1g1/A1g2 ratio and RMS increase, both for 2H and 3R films, indicative of a worsening of the film crystallinity and increase in the film roughness consistently with a change in the film growth mode.

Evolution of the (a) A1g1/A1g2 ratios and (b) RMS as a function of pressure and flux at 550 °C.

A deeper view of the morphology and structural features is reported in Fig. S4, that shows some representative 2 µm × 2 µm topographical images of 2H and 3R GaS films with different growth mode and morphology.

Starting from the experimental conditions of 100 mbar, 50 sccm and 550 °C that yielded 3R films with uniform surface and high crystallinity (layer-by-layer growth), we investigated the effect of the growth temperature in the range 425–660 °C.

Fig. 5 shows that for temperatures below 550 °C films grow 3R but their structural and morphological properties worsen. Moreover, for temperatures higher that 550 °C, 3R phase is lost favouring the formation of a mixed 3R+2H one.

Data discussed in Fig. 3–5 show that in our experimental conditions, a continuous, compact, crystalline and layered 3R GaS films can be deposited at 100 mbar, 50 sccm and 550 °C. A deeper and wider investigation of this film is shown in Fig. 6.

This film also exhibits considerable uniformity over a cm-scale area (∼3 cm2 coverage), suggesting that the experimental conditions employed may help to address some of the uniformity challenges typically associated with the CVD process.5

AFM measurements show that the edges between grains are very sharp with step heights of ∼12–17 nm (Fig. 6e–g) and that grains are not flat but present monolayer terraces with vertical spacing of ∼0.4–1 nm, consistent with the expected interlayer spacing of the 3R phase (0.69–0.79 nm).1

Raman analysis confirms that spectra remain unchanged over 12 months, demonstrating the long-term stability of this 3R film. Notably, the robustness of the synthesis conditions has been further validated by the successful growth of 3R GaS on alternative substrates, including Si (100), SiO2, and glass (Fig. S5).

Discussion

Effect of pure H2 in the CVD growth from Ga2S3 single precursor

Researchers have developed several CVD processes to deposit GaS films starting from different precursors, double-source11,18,24 and single-source5,16,25–28 precursors.

The use of single precursor has some advantages like a superior control over stoichiometry, lower deposition temperatures, minimization of toxic waste and simplification of the precursor delivery system compared to dual-source methods.29 In this field, a simple Ga2S3 precursor is preferred to metalorganic sources because it is (i) a simple inorganic compound avoiding contamination of carbon and oxygen impurities, (ii) not air-sensitive and pyrophoric, (iii) less expensive and (iv) easier to produce in high purity, making it ideal for scalable industrial processes.

Moreover, the lower vapor pressure of single precursors avoids gas-phase pre-reactions, toxicity hazards, and phase impurities.

The CVD deposition of GaS films from Ga2S3 single precursor was investigated in literature using as gas carrier H2 and Ar in a ratio 1 : 2.5,16 In these conditions, the best uniform coverage is reported to be 0.7 cm2.5

The enhanced uniformity observed in our CVD approach, ∼3 cm2, compared to values reported in the literature, may be attributed to the synergetic effects of H2.

Firstly, pure hydrogen acts as a relatively strong reducing agent, potentially offering more effective reduction of the precursors and contributing to improved control over film stoichiometry.

Indeed, the GaS deposition involves thermal–decomposition reaction at 800 °C of the solid source precursor by the reaction:301Ga2S3 → Ga2S(g) + S2(g)

Due to the preferential loss of volatile sulfur components, the thermal decomposition of Ga2S3 can cause the formation of lower-sulfur phases.2,31

In presence of H2, more reactions can be involved2Ga2S3 + H2 → Ga2S2 + H2S3Ga2S2 + H2 → Ga2S + H2S4Ga2S + H2 → Ga2 + H2S5(1/2)S2 + H2 → H2Swith the formation of H2S that favours further reactions, as follow:6Ga2 + H2S(g) ⇆ Ga2S(g) + H2(g)7Ga2S + H2S(g) ⇆ Ga2S2 + H2(g)In this way, the deposition of stoichiometric GaS films is favoured in presence of H2, as we discussed in our previous work.3

Moreover, H2 is also effective in the cleaning of the substrate surfaces, promote the etching of amorphous phases and the passivation of dangling bonds crucial for reducing defects density and remove unwanted surface oxygen species and impurities, ensuring the synthesis of phase-pure materials.

Control of phase and growth mode of GaS films

The experimental results demonstrated that by tailoring CVD parameters—specifically pressure, carrier gas flux, and temperature—it is possible to selectively control the crystalline phase (2H vs. 3R) and the growth mode of GaS films.

Fig. 7a and b illustrate, respectively, the evolution of the E mode and growth rate as a function of pressure and flux. We find that the GaS phase can be tailored controlling the pressure and growth rate, while the GaS growth mode is mainly affected by flux and growth rate, during our CVD process. Specifically, the 3R phase (red region) is selectively obtained at pressures ≥ 100 mbar and growth rate > 2.5 nm min−1, while pressures < 100 mbar and growth rate < 2.5 nm min−1 favor the formation of the 2H phase (blue region), independently on the flux.

(a) E Raman mode and (b) growth rate contour plots as flow and pressure function. Experimental points are highlighted by red x.

On the contrary, the growth mode of 3R GaS is affected by flux and growth rate as shown in Fig. 7b. At fluxes ≥ 100 sccm, independently from pressures, the growth rate is in the range 2.5–3.9 nm min−1, yielding an island growth and films with lower structural properties, as suggested by the Fig. 3e, f, h, and i. At fluxes < 100 sccm, the growth rate tends to be elevated (∼10 nm min−1), and a layer-by-layer growth mode is observed, shown in Fig. 3b and c.

High-pressure and high-growth-rate CVD processes were demonstrated to be critical for the synthesis of metastable metal dichalcogenides by modulating kinetic and thermodynamic factors to bypass the stable 2H phase and stabilize the metastable phases.32,33 Elevated pressures in the CVD chamber help the formation of metastable 3R phases and the rapid deposition rates (high growth rates) are essential to “freeze” or “trap” the atoms in a metastable structure before they can reorganize into the thermodynamically stable 2H phase.

Conversely, the change in deposition temperature, discussed in Fig. 5, does not affect the growth rate that, in the range 425–550 °C, is ∼10 nm min−1.

Eqn (8)–(10) suggest that an increase in both flux, F, and pressure, P, leads to a reduction in lg, and consequently to a smaller final grain size. Due to the square dependence of lg on F, the flux appears to exert a stronger influence on the growth mode than pressure, which is consistent with the trend observed in Fig. 7b.

Similarly, a decrease of temperature from 550 °C to 425 °C does not affect the film phase but only reduces lg reducing grains and the substrate coverage, as shown in Fig. 5. However, increasing temperature to 660 °C the film phase evolves from 3R to a mixture of 3R+2H phases because the elevated temperatures induce a transition to the thermodynamically stable phases. Moreover, the film coverage decreases, because high temperature can lead to excessive surface migration of atoms, often resulting in higher roughness, irregular growth, or increased grain boundaries that act as defect sites.

Conclusions

In this work, we demonstrated a scalable single-precursor CVD approach for the selective growth of 2H and 3R GaS films using Ga2S3 and pure H2. By systematically varying pressure, H2 flow, temperature, and growth rate, we established a clear relationship between the CVD parameters, crystalline phase, growth kinetics, and surface morphology.

The GaS phase was primarily controlled by pressure and growth rate: the stable 2H phase was obtained at low pressure (<3 mbar) and growth rates below ∼2.5 nm min−1, whereas the metastable 3R phase was selectively stabilized at pressures ≥100 mbar and growth rates above ∼3 nm min−1. Within the 3R growth regime, the growth mode was strongly affected by the H2 flow and growth rate. Lower H2 flow and higher growth rates promoted a uniform morphology consistent with layer-by-layer growth, while increasing the flow resulted in smaller grains, reduced surface coverage, and island-like growth.

The optimal conditions, namely 100 mbar, 50 sccm H2, and 550 °C, yielded highly crystalline and uniform 3R GaS films with a growth rate of approximately 10 nm min−1 and a coverage of ∼3 cm2. AFM analysis revealed well-defined terraces with step heights consistent with the interlayer spacing of layered 3R GaS. The 3R phase was also stable over at least 12 months and could be grown on different substrates, demonstrating the robustness of the process.

The improved film uniformity and stoichiometric control observed using pure H2 suggest a beneficial role of the reducing atmosphere in the Ga2S3-to-GaS conversion, although the contribution of the individual chemical pathways requires further investigation. Overall, these results establish a direct process–structure relationship for GaS CVD growth and provide a viable route toward the large-area fabrication of phase-controlled 3R GaS films for future optoelectronic applications.
