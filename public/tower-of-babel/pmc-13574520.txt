Learning curve analysis of laparoscopic low anterior resection using the ArtiSential multi-articulating device: a multicenter retrospective cohort study with pooled analysis (STARC trial)
Authors: Seijong Kim, Dae Hee Pyo, Jaeim Lee, Chang Hyun Kim, Heung-Kwon Oh, Yoon Suk Lee, Yong Sik Yoon, Dong Hwan Bae, Byung Soh Min, Jung Wook Huh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRobotic surgery has shown promising outcomes in colorectal cancer treatment but is limited by its high cost. In response, the ArtiSential system (LivsMed Inc.), an articulated handheld laparoscopic instrument, has emerged as an alternative. This study aimed to assess the learning curve associated with ArtiSential during low anterior resection (LAR) of colorectal cancer.MethodsA multicenter retrospective cohort study enrolled 208 patients who underwent LAR with ArtiSential between January 2021 and October 2022. A cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis was conducted for operation time. Three experienced surgeons, each with over 30 completed ArtiSential-assisted LAR procedures, were included in the analysis.ResultsThe median operation time was 129 minutes; it was accompanied by minimal blood loss and no open conversions. Pathological analysis revealed favorable outcomes with high-quality total mesorectal excision and a circumferential resection margin. Surgeons A and B followed a conventional 4-stage learning curve, achieving mastery after 25 and 24 cases, respectively. In contrast, surgeon C demonstrated a unique 2-stage learning curve, achieving mastery after 19 cases.ConclusionThe CUSUM analysis suggests a relatively manageable learning curve, with the initial phase being completed after approximately 10 cases and proficiency attained after 20–30 cases. In conclusion, ArtiSential appears to be a promising tool for colorectal surgeons, offering a favorable learning curve and encouraging outcomes in LAR for rectal cancer.Keywords: Learning curve, Proctectomy, Rectal neoplasms, Surgical instrument

INTRODUCTION

In recent years, there has been a rapid increase in the utilization of robotic surgery for the treatment of colorectal cancer. Numerous studies have demonstrated that robotic surgery offers perioperative and oncologic outcomes comparable to those achieved with laparoscopic surgery while maintaining low conversion rates [1,2,3,4]. Total mesorectal excision (TME), a cornerstone of rectal cancer surgery that was established by Heald [5] in 1979, involves meticulous sharp dissection of the avascular plane between the presacral fascia and the fascia propria of the rectum, ensuring en bloc removal of the tumor within an intact fascial envelope. Laparoscopic TME presents challenges that are primarily related to difficulties in adequate pelvic exposure and that result in a higher conversion rate to open surgery and increased morbidity [6,7,8].

In contrast, robotic surgery offers advantages such as enhanced maneuverability and visibility, which have translated into favorable outcomes, including improved circumferential resection margins (CRM), reduced open conversion rates, shorter hospital stays, and faster patient recovery, particularly in cases of rectal cancer [9,10,11]. However, the widespread adoption of robotic surgery is hindered by the substantial cost associated with such systems [12,13,14].

To address this issue, the ArtiSential system (LivsMed Inc.) was developed as an alternative to robotic surgery. ArtiSential is an articulated handheld laparoscopic instrument with a multi-joint structure that operates using a pulley mechanism. This innovative instrument allows 90[Symbol - °] movements in all directions (up, down, right, and left), providing surgeons with intuitive control that closely mimics the natural movements of their fingers and wrists [15]. The benefits of this articulation become particularly evident when operating on the left side of the pelvic cavity during low anterior resection (LAR). Nevertheless, the adoption of any new surgical device raises questions about the learning curve. Consequently, this study was undertaken to assess the learning curve associated with the ArtiSential system during LAR of rectal cancer.

METHODS

This was a multicenter retrospective cohort study with prospectively collected data. It was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Samsung Medical Center (No. 2022-01-174), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design. The study protocol was also reviewed and approved by the local IRBs of the other participating institutions, and data were shared in accordance with each institution’s data governance policies and applicable regulations.

The study period lasted from January 2021 to October 2022, during which time patients who underwent LAR with double-stapled anastomosis for rectal cancer using the ArtiSential device were enrolled. The choice to perform ArtiSential-assisted LAR was primarily determined by surgeon preference and device availability at each participating institution; no predefined allocation algorithm across modalities was applied. Patients were identified from the registry based on operative records confirming the use of ArtiSential instruments during rectal dissection. Among rectal cancer patients eligible for minimally invasive surgery, those who did not consent to a robotic surgery underwent multi-port laparoscopic LAR using the ArtiSential device. All such consecutive patients were included without selective exclusion, ensuring a comprehensive and unselected series of articulating laparoscopic procedures during the learning period. Patients with stage IV disease or familial cancer were excluded.

The procedures were performed by 3 experienced laparoscopic colorectal surgeons in tertiary care centers. All participating surgeons utilized a standardized setup: a monopolar spatula-type ArtiSential in the right hand, which was dynamically interchanged with advanced bipolar energy devices. For the left hand, a bipolar grasper-type ArtiSential was used, alternating with conventional rigid graspers as required by the operative context.

The following patient information and surgical parameters were systematically recorded: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, history of neoadjuvant concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CRT), tumor location, surgical approach, operation time, estimated blood loss, level of anastomosis from the anal verge, number of port sites (excluding the camera port), diverting stoma status, open conversion rate, intraoperative transfusion requirements, specific details regarding the usage of the ArtiSential device, pathologic TNM stage, the number of harvested lymph nodes, and the quality of TME and CRM. Data from the 3 participating centers were consolidated into a single database, and any entries with missing or erroneous values were excluded. Operating the LAR with the ArtiSential device primarily involved using 2 devices simultaneously, with a 2-handed approach [16].

To assess the learning curve associated with the use of ArtiSential in LAR, a cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis was conducted for operation time. CUSUM is a statistical method used to evaluate trends in performance over time by continuously summing deviations from a predefined benchmark. It is widely applied in surgical learning curve assessments to identify the point at which a surgeon achieves proficiency. In this study, CUSUM analysis was performed on 3 surgeons who had completed more than 30 ArtiSential-assisted LAR procedures, measuring operation time from skin incision initiation to skin closure completion.

RESULTS

This study included 208 patients, and their baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Among these patients, 55.3% were male, the median age was 62 years, and the median BMI was 24.3 kg/m2. Approximately 36.5% of the patients underwent neoadjuvant CRT. The tumor was located in the upper rectum in 42.3%, the mid rectum in 43.8%, and the lower rectum in 13.9% of total patients.

Intraoperative outcomes are presented in Table 2. LAR was performed in 82.2%, while ultra-LAR (anastomosis level <5 cm from the anal verge) was conducted in the remaining 17.8%. The median total operation time was 129 minutes (range, 114–134 minutes) with a median estimated blood loss of 30 mL (range, 20–100 mL). DISCUSSION

Considering the rapid integration of robotics into colorectal surgery, it is essential to assess the adaptability of surgeons to articulated devices that may serve as viable alternatives to robotic surgery in the context of colorectal cancer. ArtiSential, an articulated handheld laparoscopic instrument with a multi-joint structure, has demonstrated its utility across a spectrum of surgical procedures, encompassing gastrectomy, appendectomy, colectomy, and more [17,18,19,20]. In the realm of colorectal surgery, LAR stands out as a prime candidate for robotic assistance, primarily owing to the challenges inherent in adequate pelvic access [21,22].

ArtiSential has emerged as an intriguing option for LAR, as its articulating design augments dexterity and enhances surgical precision, particularly in cases with narrow pelvic anatomy or tumors located in the lower rectum. Therefore, analysis of the learning curve for the ArtiSential is imperative for its effective utilization in clinical practice. The CUSUM method has revealed that the initial learning phase is typically completed after approximately 10 cases, with proficiency achieved after 20–30 cases. In contrast, robotic LAR in previous studies has required 25–44 cases to achieve the proficiency phase [23,24,25]. After the peak, the operation times decreased and became stable in all 3 operators. Lee et al. [26] conducted a multicenter study on the learning curve for single-port laparoscopic colon resection. They found that experienced surgeons achieved mastery after 13–36 cases in anterior resection and 6–15 cases in right hemicolectomy. Based on these study findings, the ArtiSential device may offer a more expedited learning curve, making it a potentially more accessible option for surgeons to master.

In the present study, participating surgeons generally adopted a standardized bimanual setup with a spatula-type instrument in the dominant hand and a grasper-type instrument in the nondominant hand, with selective interchange according to operative context. In our experience, articulation was particularly advantageous during left-sided mobilization around a tortuous aorta and during left pelvic wall dissection during TME, which is consistent with our previous reports. However, the exact frequency and procedural context of each instrument exchange were not prospectively recorded, limiting a more granular surgeon-specific usage analysis [27,28].

The median total operative time in this study was 129 minutes, which compares favorably with previous reports on robotic and laparoscopic LAR [4,29,30]. Furthermore, the estimated blood loss and intraoperative transfusion rates were consistent with findings from other studies that investigated laparoscopic and robotic LAR [13,31]. A notable observation was the absence of cases that required conversion to open surgery, a remarkable outcome considering previous reports indicating open conversion rates of 7.9% in laparoscopic LAR [4,13,32]. Additionally, the quality of TME and CRM was comparable with outcomes in laparoscopic and robotic LAR [18,33,34,35]. These findings suggest that ArtiSential may indeed offer a viable alternative for LAR in the treatment of rectal cancer.

Nonetheless, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, although this analysis was retrospective in nature, the data were prospectively collected; however, the retrospective design still limits causal inference. Second, because ArtiSential use was not randomized and was influenced by surgeon and institutional factors, selection bias and differences in case-mix cannot be completely excluded; thus, the learning-curve estimates should be interpreted as reflecting real-world adoption in experienced surgeons. Third, the learning-curve analysis included only 3 surgeons who met the prespecified inclusion criterion (≥30 ArtiSential-assisted LAR cases per surgeon). All surgeons had extensive laparoscopic expertise and at least 20 years of practice, and therefore, the observed learning curve may underestimate the learning phase required for less-experienced or lower-volume surgeons, limiting generalizability to early-career operators. Future studies including a broader range of surgeons and institutional volumes are warranted. In addition, we are conducting a prospective cohort study comparing ArtiSential-assisted and robotic LAR to further validate these findings.

However, this study was the first multicenter study to analyze the learning curve for ArtiSential LAR; the initial learning phase was achieved after 10 cases, and the mastery phase was attained between 19 and 25 cases.

In conclusion, ArtiSential-assisted LAR is a safe and feasible procedure. For experienced laparoscopic colorectal surgeons, the learning curve associated with ArtiSential LAR appears to be manageable and not overly steep.
