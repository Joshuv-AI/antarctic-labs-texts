Modular vector bundles with and without moduli
Enrico Fatighenti, Claudio Onorati

Abstract
. If X ⊂ Gr ⁡ ( 2 , 6 ) X\subset\Gr(2,6) is the Fano variety of lines of a smooth cubic fourfold, then we show that the restriction to X X of any Schur functor of the tautological quotient bundle is modular and slope polystable. Moreover it is atomic if and only if it is rigid, in which case it is also slope stable. We further compute the Ext-groups of such bundles in infinitely many cases, showing in particular the existence of new modular vector bundles on manifolds of type K3 [ 2 ] \operatorname{K3}^{[2]} that are slope stable and whose Ext 1 \operatorname{Ext}^{1} -group is 40-dimensional.



Introduction

In the recent years there has been a lot of interest around the study of special sheaves on irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifolds. Three different definitions are present in literature, each generalising some aspects of the moduli theory of sheaves on K3 surfaces:

•

in
[
Ver96b
]
, Verbitsky introduced
projectively hyper-holomorphic
vector bundles, and proved that the smooth locus of (an irreducible component of) the moduli space of stable hyper-holomorphic vector bundles (with fixed numerical invariants) is hyper-Kähler. On the same lines, in
[
MO23
]
it is shown that the infinitesimal deformations of a projectively hyper-holomorphic vector bundle are controlled by a formal DG Lie algebra;

•

in
[
O’Gr22
]
, O’Grady introduced
modular
torsion free sheaves, and proved that the (slope) stability problem of such sheaves decomposes the ample cone as in the surface case;

•

finally in
[
Bec22
]
(see also
[
Mar21
]
), Beckmann defined
atomic
objects in the derived category, and proved that they admits an
extended Mukai vector
.

It is known that atomic torsion free sheaves and stable projectively hyper-holomorphic vector bundles are modular. When the irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifold
X
is of type
\operatorname{K3}^{[2]}
, then a stable modular vector bundle is projectively hyper-holomorphic. Nevertheless, there are examples of stable modular sheaves that are not atomic already for this kind of manifolds (as the present paper contributes to show).

The brief discussion above shows many important features shared by these special vector bundles, but leaves open a fundamental question, namely the existence of such objects. Very few examples exist in literature, and most of them are rigid.

When
X
is of type
\operatorname{K3}^{[2]}
, then
[
O’Gr22
]
exhibits many rigid modular vector bundles on
X=S^{[2]}
, all coming from the K3 surface
S
; with a similar technique, in
[
O’Gr24
]
the author constructs infinitely many examples with
2(a^{2}+1)
parameters for any integer
a\geq 1
. In
[
Bot22
]
, Bottini studies a stable atomic vector bundle on
X=S^{[2]}
with
10
parameters, and shows that an irreducible component of the corresponding moduli space is birational to a manifold of type OG10. In
[
Mar21
,
Bec22
]
other examples of torsion sheaves are exhibited. Finally, in
[
Fat24
]
two more examples of modular vector bundles on the Fano varieties of lines of a smooth cubic fourfold are produced, both with
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}
-group
20
-dimensional.

Recall that if
F
is a torsion free sheaf on a smooth and projective variety
X
, its
discriminant
is defined as

\Delta(F)=c_{1}(F)^{2}-2\operatorname{rk}(F)\operatorname{ch}_{2}(F).

If
X
is an irreducible holomorphic symplectic variety of type
\operatorname{K3}^{[2]}
, then a torsion free sheaf is modular if
\Delta(F)\in{\mathbb{Q}}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)
, where
\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)=c_{2}(T_{X})
is the second Chern class of
X
.

The first aim of this work is to expand the list of examples above by proving the following result.

Theorem A
.

Let
X\subset\Gr(2,V_{6})
be the Fano variety of lines of a very general cubic fourfold. For any partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})
, the restricted Schur functor
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
on
X
is (slope) polystable and modular. More precisely,

\Delta(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})=\frac{\delta(\lambda)}{4}\operatorname{rk}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})^{2}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X),

where

\delta(\lambda)=\frac{3(\sum_{i=1}^{4}\lambda_{i}^{2})-2(\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j})+12\lambda_{1}+4\lambda_{2}-4\lambda_{3}-12\lambda_{4}}{60}.

As remarked before, it is very interesting to understand the relation between modular and atomic sheaves. Roughly speaking atomic sheaves are those for which we can define a surface-like Mukai vector, and conjecturally they deform with
X
over all its directions, included the non-commutative ones (see Section
1.2.4
).

Since we have just seen that the Schur functors of the tautological quotient bundle are modular, it is natural to ask weather they are also atomic. The answer is negative in general, but it is interesting to notice that only those that are rigid are in fact atomic. Our second main result is the following.

Theorem B
.

Let
X\subset\Gr(2,V_{6})
be the Fano variety of lines of a very general cubic fourfold. For any partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})
, the associated vector bundle
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
on
X
is atomic if and only if
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}
or
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}=\lambda_{4}
.

Moreover, these bundles are all slope stable and rigid.

We wish to remark that up to taking dual and twisting with a line bundle, Theorem
B
is saying that the only atomic bundles are the symmetric bundles
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
, which we fully describe in Section
2
. Unless the case
m=1
, the rigid and stable atomic vector bundles
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
do not satisfy the hypothesis of
[
O’Gr22
, Theorem 1.4]
, therefore they are essentially new.

The proof of Theorem
B
is based on Theorem
4.1
, where we explicitly compute the Chern character of the bundles
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
in function of
\lambda
and the Chern classes of
\mathcal{Q}
. To the best of our knowledge this is the first time that Chern classes of Schur functors of a bundle are shown to be related to Chern classes of the bundle itself via functions depending only on the partition. Even though all our computations take place on the Fano variety of lines, thus heavily relying on non-trivial relations in the cohomology ring, one can reproduce the argument in general for any bundle of rank
r
on a smooth and projective variety. We will face this problem elsewhere.

Next we move to study the Ext-algebra of the bundles
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
.
The strategy to compute the groups
\operatorname{Ext}^{i}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q},\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})
is to use the famous Borel–Weil–Bott formula combined with the Koszul complex of
X\subset\Gr(2,6)
. This machinery can be encoded in the spectral sequence (
1.15
), whose degeneracy is hard to control in general.

Our third main result is an implementation of this strategy for infinitely many partitions.

The proof of Theorem
C
led us to believe that in general a rigid pattern underlines the structure of
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q},\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})
. We set the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

Let
X\subset\Gr(2,V_{6})
be the Fano variety of lines of a smooth cubic fourfold, and let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})
be a partition. Then

(1)

\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
is simple, i.e.
\operatorname{Hom}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q},\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})={\mathbb{C}}
;

(2)

if
\lambda
has at most two different entries (i.e.
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
is rigid), then

\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q},\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\bigwedge^{3}V_{6}^{\vee}&\mbox{if}\quad\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}>\lambda_{3}\,\mbox{or}\,\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}>\lambda_{4}\,\mbox{or}\,\lambda_{2}>\lambda_{3}=\lambda_{4}\\
(\bigwedge^{3}V_{6}^{\vee})^{\oplus 2}&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Notice also that the simplicity claim, together with Theorem
A
, implies that the vector bundles
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
are slope stable.

Another interesting aspect to notice is the fact that
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q},\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})
is isomorphic to a
\SL(6)
-representation: this is the content of the Borel–Weil–Bott Theorem for Schur functors on
\Gr(2,6)
, but the fact that it descends to the variety
X
is quite surprising. (We note that this is not true in general for the group
\operatorname{Ext}^{2}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q},\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})
.)

We wish to remark once more that Conjecture
1
is confirmed in infinitely many cases by Theorem
C
.

Finally, an important aspect of moduli theory is to determine whether or not the Kuranishi space of a polystable vector bundle is smooth. The obstructions to the smoothness are contained in the
\operatorname{Ext}^{2}
-group, which in the cases considered by us grows indefinitely with
\lambda
(see Proposition
6.1
).

We set an optimistic conjecture about smoothness of the Kuranishi space of the vector bundles
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
on
X
.

Conjecture 2
.

Let
X\subset\Gr(2,V_{6})
be the Fano variety of lines of a smooth cubic fourfold. For any partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})
, let us consider the associated vector bundle
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
on
X
. Then the Kuranishi space

\operatorname{Def}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})

of infinitesimal deformations of
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
is smooth.

We collect in Section
9
some little evidences for Conjecture
2
.

Plan of the paper.

In Section
1
we state the main definitions, notations and preliminary results we will need in the rest of the paper. In particular, Section
1.1
contains a quick recap of the homogeneous vector bundles on the Grassmannian
\Gr(2,6)
we will consider and the recipes to perform Pieri and Littlewood–Richardson decompositions, and the algorithm for the Borel–Weil–Bott Theorem.
Section
1.2
is instead a summary of results on irreducible holomorphic symplectic fourfolds, focusing on modular and atomic sheaves. Here we also explicitly determine relations on the cohomology ring of a manifold of type
\operatorname{K3}^{[2]}
which are probably known to experts, but for which a reference was missing in literature.

Section
2
contains the first original results of the paper: we prove here that the symmetric vector bundles
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
are slope stable, atomic and rigid, and we compute the Ext-algebra explicitly. This will be the starting point of our investigations of more general Schur functors.

Before tackling this more general problem though, we collect in Section
3
two worked-out “easy” examples. We hope that this section will help the reader to navigate the rest of the paper. We prove here that the vector bundles
\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}
and
\Sigma_{(3,2,1,0)}\mathcal{Q}
are slope stable and modular, and we determine their Ext-algebra. In particular it is important to notice that
\dim\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(\Sigma_{(3,2,1,0)}\mathcal{Q},\Sigma_{(3,2,1,0)}\mathcal{Q})=40
, providing the first example of a stable modular sheaf on a variety of type
\operatorname{K3}^{[2]}
with
40
moduli.

Section
4
contains the long computation of the Chern character of
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
in terms of
\lambda
and the Chern classes of
\mathcal{Q}
. This is a technical, but not difficult, section based on several inductive steps on the rows and columns of
\lambda
.

Section
5
and Section
6
are the first two main sections, in which we prove Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
, respectively. In particular, in Section
6.1
we compute the Euler characteristic of
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
as a function of
\lambda
.

In Section
7
we state Conjecture
1
and prove some general facts in its support; in Section
8
we prove Theorem
C
via a case-by-case analysis of the several cases.

Finally, in Section
9
we make some speculations about the smoothness of the Kuranishi space of
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
and set Conjecture
2
.

To conclude the paper we also include two appendixes. In Appendix
A
we quickly prove that the vector bundles
\Sigma_{\mu}\mathcal{U}
are not modular, thus justifying our choice to concentrate on Schur functors of the tautological quotient bundle. In Appendix
B
we collect several combinatorial questions and remarks about the Littlewood–Richardson decomposition of endomorphism bundles. These facts have been noticed while performing the computations in the paper, especially in Section
8
, but a general proof is lacking, so that we leave them as open questions.

1.
Preliminaries

1.1.
Homogeneous vector bundles on
\operatorname{Gr}(2,6)

The grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(2,6)
is the variety of two dimensional subspaces in
V_{6}
, a six dimensional complex vector space. It is a homogeneous variety, being the quotient of
\SL(6)
by a parabolic subgroup (containing
\SL(4)\times\SL(2)
).
The homogeneous vector bundles we consider in this manuscript will come from representations of
\SL(4)\times\SL(2)
.

Recall that irreducible representations of
\SL(N)
are indexed by
dominant weights

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{N})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}
(recall that dominant means that
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{N}
). If
\lambda_{N}\geq 0
, then
\lambda
will be called a partition. The integer number
N
is the
length
of the weight. If
\lambda
is a partition of length
N
, then its
size
is

|\lambda|=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{N}.

Now, if
V_{N}
is a vector space of rank
N
where
\SL(N)
acts naturally, then we denote by

\Sigma_{\lambda}V_{N}

the
Schur
module corresponding to the irreducible representation with maximal weight
\lambda
.

Example 1.1
.

(1)

If
\lambda=(m,0,\cdots,0)
, then
\Sigma_{\lambda}V_{N}=\Sym^{m}V_{N}
is the symmetric product representation;

(2)

if
\lambda=(1,\cdots,1,0,\cdots,0)
, where the number of
1
’s is
n
, then
\Sigma_{\lambda}V_{N}=\bigwedge^{n}V_{N}
is the wedge product representation.

With this notation, we denote by

\Sigma_{\lambda|\mu}:=\Sigma_{\lambda}V_{4}\otimes\Sigma_{\mu}V_{2}

the irreducible representation of
\SL(4)\times\SL(2)
with maximal weights
\lambda
and
\mu
.

In the rest of the paper we use the following notation.

Notation
.

Working on
\Gr(2,6)
, we have:

•

the partition
\lambda=(1,0,0,0)
, corresponding to the tautological quotient vector bundle, is denoted by
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}
;

•

for any dominant weight
\lambda
of length
4
, we denote by

\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}

the irreducible homogeneous vector bundles corresponding to the Schur module
\Sigma_{\lambda|(0,0)}
;

•

the partition
\mu=(1,0)
, corresponding to the tautological subspace vector bundle, is denoted by
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
;

•

for any dominant weight
\mu
of length
2
, we denote by

\Sigma_{\mu}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}

the irreducible homogeneous vector bundles corresponding to the Schur module
\Sigma_{(0,0,0,0)|\mu}
;

•

finally, with an abuse of notation we keep denoting by

\Sigma_{\lambda|\mu}:=\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\Sigma_{\mu}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}

the irreducible homogeneous vector bundle associated to the corresponding Schur module.

If
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{N})
is a dominant weight of length
N
, then we define its
dual
as

\lambda^{*}:=(-\lambda_{N},\cdots,-\lambda_{1}).

Then for any Schur module
\Sigma_{\lambda|\mu}
on
\Gr(2,6)
we have

(1.1)

(\Sigma_{\lambda|\mu})^{\vee}=\Sigma_{\lambda^{*}|\mu^{*}}.

If we put

\lambda+n:=(\lambda_{1}+n,\cdots,\lambda_{N}+n)

for an integer
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
then

(1.2)

\Sigma_{\lambda+n}=\Sigma_{\lambda}V_{N}\otimes\left(\bigwedge^{N}V_{N}\right)^{\otimes n}.

Since

\Sigma_{(1,1,1,1)}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}=\det\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}=\mathcal{O}_{\Gr(2,6)}(1)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\Sigma_{(1,1)}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}=\det\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}=\mathcal{O}_{\Gr(2,6)}(-1),

from (
1.2
) it follows that

(1.3)

\Sigma_{\lambda+n|\mu+n}=\Sigma_{\lambda|\mu}

for every
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Example 1.2
.

Let
\lambda
be a partition of length
4
. Then

\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}=\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Gr(2,6)}(-\lambda_{1}),

where
\lambda^{\prime}=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{4},\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{3},\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2},0)
.
In fact, combining (
1.1
) with (
1.3
) it follows that
\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}=\Sigma_{\lambda^{*}}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}=\Sigma_{\lambda^{*}+\lambda_{1}|(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1})}
.

Remark 1.3
.

As a consequence of (
1.3
), up to tensoring with a suitable ample line bundle
\mathcal{O}_{\Gr(2,6)}(n)
we can restrict ourselves to considering only the bundles
\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}
where
\lambda
is a partition.

Because of this remark, from now on we only work with homogeneous vector bundles
\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}
where
\lambda
is a partition. In particular we can picture
\lambda
as a Young diagram.

We state in the following the main technical results we will need to decompose plethysms and compute cohomology of irreducible homogeneous vector bundles on
\Gr(2,6)
. These are standard facts and can be found for example in
[
Wey03
]
.

Pieri’s decomposition.

Let
\lambda
be a partition of length
N
, which we think as a Young diagram. Pieri’s formula says that

(1.4)

\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\Sym^{m}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}=\bigoplus_{\nu}\Sigma_{\nu}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}},

where the direct sum runs over all the Young diagrams
\nu
obtained from
\lambda
by adding
m
blocks subject to the following rule.

\bullet

Pieri rule
: no two blocks are added on the same column.

The same formula holds if we replace
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}
with
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
.

Littlewood–Richardson’s decomposition.

As before, let
\lambda
and
\mu
be two partitions of length
N
, which we think as Young diagrams. Littlewood–Richardson’s formula says that

(1.5)

\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\Sigma_{\mu}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}=\bigoplus_{\nu}(\Sigma_{\nu}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}})^{\oplus m^{\nu}_{\lambda,\mu}},

where the direct sum runs over all the Young diagrams
\nu
such that
|\nu|=|\lambda|+|\mu|
, and the multiplicities
m^{\nu}_{\lambda,\mu}
are determined according to the following algorithm. Let us write
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3},\mu_{4})
and
|\mu|=\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\mu_{4}
. Starting with the Young diagram of
\lambda
, we want to arrive to the Young diagram of
\nu
using the following steps:

\bullet

Step i
: add
\mu_{i}
boxes labelled with the number
i
, following the Pieri rule;

for
i=1,\cdots,N
.

At the end of this process, let us list the numbers inserted starting from the top-right box and proceeding right-to-left and top-to-bottom. Let us call
S
this sequence. Then the following rule must be satisfied.

\bullet

Littlewood–Richardson rule
: for every
0\leq t\leq|\mu|
, we look at the first
t
elements of the sequence
S
and for every
p=1,2,3,4
we must have that the number of
p
’s is bigger or equal to the number of
(p+1)
’s.

The multiplicity
m^{\nu}_{\lambda,\mu}
is then the number of admissible sequences
S
satisfying the Littlewood–Richardson rule.

Remark 1.4
.

The rule in Step 5 in particular says that in the first row we must insert only boxes labelled with
1
.

Remark 1.5
.

Since we add boxes labelled progressively at each step following the Pieri rule, it follows that at end on each column we must read a (strictly) increasing sequence of integers.

1.1.1.
The Borel–Weil–Bott Theorem

Let
\Sigma_{\lambda|\mu}
be an irreducible homogeneous vector bundle on
\Gr(2,6)
, with
\lambda
and
\mu
two partitions. In the following we abuse notation and consider
\lambda|\mu
as a
6
-tuple of positive integers.

Let
\delta=(5,4,3,2,1,0)
be the
fundamental dominant weight
for
\SL(6)
. If
v
is a
6
-tuple of integral numbers, then the
l
-function of
v
is defined as

(1.6)

l(v):=\mbox{minimum number of transposition needed to bring $l(v)$ to a dominant partition}.

Then the Borel–Weil–Bott Theorem is based on the following algorithm.

•

If
\lambda|\mu+\delta
has repeated entries, then
\Sigma_{\lambda|\mu}
is acyclic, i.e.
\oH^{p}(\Sigma_{\lambda|\mu})=0
for every
p
.

•

If
\lambda|\mu+\delta
has no repeated entries, then

\oH^{p}(\Sigma_{\lambda|\mu})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\Sigma_{\widetilde{\lambda|\mu}}V_{6}&\mbox{if}\;p=l(\lambda|\mu+\delta)\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

where
\widetilde{\lambda|\mu}:=\operatorname{sort}(\lambda|\mu+\delta)-\delta
.

1.1.2.
The reduction trick

In this paper we will work with homogeneous vector bundles of the form
\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}
, where
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})
is a partition.

In we apply Example
1.2
twice, we get the following identity

(1.7)

\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}=\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Gr(2,6)}(\lambda_{4}),

where
\lambda^{\prime}=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{4},\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{4},\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{4},0)
.

This simple observation will make us able to reduce most of our computations (for example of discriminants or Euler characteristic) to partitions of the form
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},0)
, thus shortening or simplifying the proofs.

1.2.
Irreducible holomorphic symplectic fourfolds

The main character of this work is the Fano variety of lines of a smooth cubic fourfold. As it is well known, such a variety is an irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifold of dimension
4

1.2.2.
LLV algebra and Mukai lattice

Let
X
be an irreducible holomorphic symplectic fourfold.

Let us start by recalling the definition of the Looijenga–Lunts–Verbitsky (LLV) algebra. First of all, we denote by
h\in\operatorname{End}(\oH^{\ast}(X,{\mathbb{Q}}))
the grading endomorphism, i.e.
h(y)=(k-4)\operatorname{id}
for any
y\in\oH^{k}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
. Also, for any element
x\in\oH^{2}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
we denote by
e_{x}\in\operatorname{End}(\oH^{\ast}(X,{\mathbb{Q}}))
the operator
e_{x}(y)=x\cup y
. Then one says that
x
satisfies the Hard Lefschetz property if there exists another operator
f_{x}\in\operatorname{End}(\oH^{\ast}(X,{\mathbb{Q}}))
such that
(e_{x},h,f_{x})
is a basis for the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
. One usually refers to a such triple
(e_{x},h,f_{x})
as an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple.

The
LLV algebra
is the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}(X)\subset\operatorname{End}(\oH^{\ast}(X,{\mathbb{Q}}))
generated by all the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triples associated to elements of
\oH^{2}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
with the strong Lefschetz property (see
[
LL97
]
).

Let us denote by
\operatorname{SH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})\subset\oH^{\ast}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
the image of
\Sym\oH^{2}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
via cup product. By
[
Bog96
,
Ver96a
]
we have that
\operatorname{SH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
is an irreducible
\mathfrak{g}(X)
-module of
\oH^{\ast}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
, called the
Verbistky component
, appearing with multiplicity
1
.

Finally, let us also recall the other useful description of the LLV algebra. The
extended Mukai (rational) lattice
is

\widetilde{\oH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})=\left({\mathbb{Q}}\alpha\oplus\oH^{2}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})\oplus{\mathbb{Q}}\beta,\;\tilde{q}\right),

where
\tilde{q}(\alpha)=\tilde{q}(\beta)=0
,
\tilde{q}(\alpha,\beta)=-1
,
\tilde{q}|_{\oH^{2}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})}
is the Beauville–Bogomolov–Fujiki form and
\oH^{2}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
is
\tilde{q}
-orthogonal to
{\mathbb{Q}}\alpha\oplus{\mathbb{Q}}\beta
.

For any
x\in\oH^{2}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
with the strong Lefschetz property, putting

(1.11)

e_{x}(\alpha)=x,\quad e_{x}(y)=q_{X}(x,y)\beta\quad\mbox{ and }\quad e_{x}(\beta)=0

defines a structure of
\mathfrak{g}(X)
-module on
\widetilde{\oH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
and moreover (
[
Ver96a
]
)

\mathfrak{g}(X)\cong\mathfrak{so}(\widetilde{\oH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})).

1.2.3.
Modular sheaves

Recall that the Verbitsky component
\operatorname{SH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
is the irreducible
\mathfrak{g}(X)
-submodule of
\oH^{\ast}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
generated by
\oH^{2}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
.

Definition 1.8
.

Let
X
be an irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifold.
We denote by
\mathsf{q}_{X}\in\operatorname{SH}^{4}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
the BBF-class of
X
, i.e.
\mathsf{q}_{X}
is uniquely determined by the property that

\int_{X}\gamma^{2}\mathsf{q}_{X}=q_{X}(\gamma)

for every
\gamma\in\oH^{2}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})
.

Definition 1.9

(O’Grady)
.

Let
F
be a torsion free coherent sheaf on
X
.
Then
F
is
modular
if

\Delta(F)=c_{1}(F)^{2}-2\operatorname{rk}(F)\operatorname{ch}_{2}(F)\in{\mathbb{Q}}\,\mathsf{q}_{X}.

Remark 1.10
.

In
[
O’Gr22
]
, O’Grady defines a modular sheaf as a torsion free sheaf
F
such that the projection of
\Delta(F)
into the Verbitsky component is a multiple of
\mathsf{q}_{X}
. Our definition implies his definition, but the vice versa is not true in general. On the other hand,if
X
is of type
\operatorname{K3}^{[2]}
, then the Verbitsky component coincides with the rational cohomology ring. Since we will only work with fourfolds of type
\operatorname{K3}^{[2]}
, we choose to state the definition in this weaker form.

Let us recall the following known fact.

Lemma 1.11
.

If
X
is of type
\operatorname{K3}^{[2]}
, then
\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)=30\mathsf{q}_{X}
.

In particular a torsion free sheaf
F
on
X
is modular if and only if
\Delta(F)\in{\mathbb{Q}}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)
.

Proof.

First of all, by
[
Mar20
, Lemma 1.5]
we know that
\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)
is proportional to
\mathsf{q}_{X}
. Now, by a result of Fujiki (
[
Fuj87
]
) there is a constant
C(\mathsf{c}_{2}(X))
such that

\int_{X}\gamma^{2}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)=C(\mathsf{c}_{2}(X))q_{X}(\alpha)

for every
\gamma\in\oH^{2}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})
. From
[
BS22
, Corollary 2.7, Example 2.12]
it follows that
C(\mathsf{c}_{2}(X))=30
. The claim follows.
∎

1.2.4.
Atomic sheaves

Recall from Section
1.2.2
the definition of the LLV algebra
\mathfrak{g}(X)
and its action on the cohomology ring
\oH^{\ast}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
, the Verbitsky component
\operatorname{SH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
and the extended Mukai lattice
\widetilde{\oH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}}))
.

To these actions we also add the action of
\mathfrak{g}(X)
on
\Sym^{2}\widetilde{\oH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
by derivation. Then by
[
Bog96
,
Ver96a
]

there is a short exact sequence of
\mathfrak{g}(X)
-modules

0\longrightarrow\operatorname{SH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\psi}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Sym^{2}\widetilde{\oH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tilde{q}}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbb{Q}}\longrightarrow 0,

where
\psi
is normalised as
\psi(1)=\alpha^{(2)}/2
.

The
{\mathbb{Q}}
-vector space
\operatorname{SH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
has the natural bilinear product defined by cup-product and the property that
\operatorname{SH}^{2p}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
is orthogonal to
\operatorname{SH}^{2q}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
if
p+q\neq 2
. On the other hand
one can define a bilinear non-degenerate pairing on
\Sym^{2}\widetilde{\oH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
by the rule

\tilde{q}^{(2)}((v_{1},v_{2}),(w_{1},w_{2}))=\tilde{q}(v_{1},w_{1})\tilde{q}(v_{2},w_{2})+\tilde{q}(v_{1},w_{2})\tilde{q}(v_{2},w_{1}).

The morphism
\psi
is then an isometry with respect to these products (
[
Tae23
,
Bec23
]
) and we denote by

\mathsf{T}\colon\Sym^{2}\widetilde{\oH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})\longrightarrow\operatorname{SH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})

the corresponding orthogonal projection.
The morphism
\mathsf{T}
is a morphism of
\mathfrak{g}(X)
-modules.

Recall that the Mukai vector of a coherent sheaf
F
is

v(F)=\operatorname{ch}(F)\sqrt{\operatorname{td}_{X}}\in\oH^{\ast}(X,{\mathbb{Q}}).

Definition 1.13

(Beckmann, Markman)
.

Let
F
be a coherent sheaf on
X
.

(1)

(
[
Bec23
, Definition 4.15]
) An
extended Mukai vector
of
F
is an element
\tilde{v}\in\widetilde{\oH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
such that

\mathsf{T}(\tilde{v}^{(2)})\in{\mathbb{Q}}\,\bar{v}(F),

where
\bar{v}(F)
is the projection of the Mukai vector
v(F)
to the irreducible component
\operatorname{SH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
.

(2)

(
[
Bec22
, Definition 1.1]
)
F
is
atomic
if there is a vector
0\neq\tilde{v}
such that

\operatorname{Ann}(\tilde{v})=\operatorname{Ann}(v(F)).

By
[
Bec22
, Proposition 3.3]
(see also
[
Mar21
, Theorem 1.7]
), if
F
is an atomic sheaf and
\tilde{v}\in\widetilde{\oH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})
is an element such that
\operatorname{Ann}(\tilde{v})=\operatorname{Ann}(v(F))
, then
\tilde{v}
is an extended Mukai vector for
F
.

Remark 1.14
.

By
[
Bec22
, Theorem 1.2]
, a sheaf
F
is atomic if and only if it has a rank
1

\operatorname{HT}^{2}(X)\longrightarrow\oH^{\ast}(\Omega_{X}^{\ast}),\qquad\mu\mapsto\mu\lrcorner\,v(F).

Conjecturally, if the sheaf
F
is simple, this should be related to
F
deforming with
X
along any directions, included the gerby and non-commutative ones, see
[
Bec22
, Theorem 1.4]
.

Let us remark that the extended Mukai vector is only defined up to a (rational) constant. Nevertheless if the rank of
F
does not vanish, then by
[
Bec22
, Proposition 3.8]
(see also
[
Mar21
, Theorem 6.13]
) there is a natural normalisation given by

(1.12)

\tilde{v}(F)=\left(\operatorname{rk}(F),c_{1}(F),s(F)\right),

for some

.

The following result is
[
Bec22
, Proposition 3.1, Proposition 3.3]
in the particular case of an irreducible holomorphic symplectic fourfold of type
\operatorname{K3}^{[2]}
.

Proposition 1.15
.

Let
X
be an irreducible holomorphic symplectic fourfold of type
\operatorname{K3}^{[2]}
, and
F
a torsion free coherent sheaf on
X
. If
F
has an extended Mukai vector
\tilde{v}(F)
, then it is atomic.

Proof.

First of all, by
[
Bec22
, Proposition 3.1, Proposition 3.3]
the following equivalence holds,

T(\tilde{v}^{(2)})\in{\mathbb{Q}}\,\bar{v}(E)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\operatorname{Ann}(\mathsf{T}(\tilde{v}^{(2)}))=\operatorname{Ann}(\bar{v}(E)),

and
\operatorname{Ann}(\mathsf{T}(\tilde{v}^{(2)}))=\operatorname{Ann}(\tilde{v})
.

Now, since
X
is of type
\operatorname{K3}^{[2]}
, there is a natural isomorphism

\operatorname{SH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})\cong\oH^{\ast}(X,{\mathbb{Q}}),

therefore
\bar{v}(E)=v(E)
and the claim follows.
∎

X\subset\Gr(2,6)
be the Fano variety of lines of a cubic fourfold.

Let us first look at the grassmannian side. As usual, we denote by
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}
the tautological vector bundles on
\Gr(2,6)
. For any partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})
we consider the Schur module
\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}
, and we denote by

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}:=\mathcal{E}nd(\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}})=\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}\otimes\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}

the associated endomorphism bundle.
Notice that by Example
1.2
we can write

(1.13)

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}=\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Gr(2,6)}(-\lambda_{1}),

where
\lambda^{\prime}=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{,}\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{3},\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2},0)
.

Let us now pass to the Fano variety of lines side, so that we put

\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}:=(\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}})|_{X}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}:=(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})|_{X}.

One of the main purposes of this work is to compute the Ext-groups of
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
on
X
, or equivalently the cohomology groups of
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
. This will be achieved by using the Borel–Bott–Weil formula and the Koszul resolution of
X
.

Recalling that
X
is described as the zero locus of a general section of
\Sym^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}
, the Koszul complex of
X
is

(1.14)

0\to\bigwedge^{4}\Sym^{3}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\to\bigwedge^{3}\Sym^{3}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\to\bigwedge^{2}\Sym^{3}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\to\Sym^{3}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\to\mathcal{O}_{\Gr(2,6)}\to\mathcal{O}_{X}\to 0.

Since all the bundles appearing in the sequence above are homogeneous, we can decompose them in irreducible ones, see Section
1.1
. It turns out that:

•

\Sym^{3}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}=\Sigma_{(3,0)}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}

•

\bigwedge^{2}\Sym^{3}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}=\Sigma_{(5,1)}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\oplus\Sigma_{(3,3)}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
;

•

\bigwedge^{3}\Sym^{3}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}=\Sigma_{(6,3)}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
;

•

\bigwedge^{4}\Sym^{3}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}=\Sigma_{(6,6)}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
.

Tensoring (
1.14
) with
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}
allows us to compute the cohomology of
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
via the induced spectral sequence

(1.15)

\operatorname{E}^{-p,q}_{1}=\oH^{q}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}\otimes\bigwedge^{p}\Sym^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\right)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\oH^{q-p}(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}).

Let us spell out the decomposition of (
1.14
)
\otimes\,\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}
according to (
1.13
). First of all, by the Littlewood–Richardson formula we can write

\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\Sigma_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}=\bigoplus_{\nu}(\Sigma_{\nu}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}})^{\oplus m^{\nu}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda}},

from which we get

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}=\bigoplus_{\nu}\left(\Sigma_{\nu|(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1})}\right)^{\oplus m^{\nu}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda}}.

Eventually we have:

•

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}\otimes\Sym^{3}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}=\bigoplus_{\nu}\left(\Sigma_{\nu|(\lambda_{1}+3,\lambda_{1})}\right)^{\oplus m^{\nu}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda}}
;

•

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}\otimes\bigwedge^{2}\Sym^{3}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}=\bigoplus_{\nu}\left(\Sigma_{\nu|(\lambda_{1}+5,\lambda_{1}+1)}\right)^{\oplus m^{\nu}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda}}\oplus\bigoplus_{\nu}\left(\Sigma_{\nu|(\lambda_{1}+3,\lambda_{1}+3)}\right)^{\oplus m^{\nu}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda}}
;

•

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}\otimes\bigwedge^{3}\Sym^{3}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}=\bigoplus_{\nu}\left(\Sigma_{\nu|(\lambda_{1}+6,\lambda_{1}+3)}\right)^{\oplus m^{\nu}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda}}
;

•

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}\otimes\bigwedge^{4}\Sym^{3}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}=\bigoplus_{\nu}\left(\Sigma_{\nu|(\lambda_{1}+6,\lambda_{1}+6)}\right)^{\oplus m^{\nu}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda}}
.

The
(p+i)
-th cohomology of each irreducible summand in
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}\otimes\bigwedge^{p}\Sym^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
contributes to the
i
-th cohomology of
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
.

More explicitly, the spectral sequence (
1.15
) can be read out by splitting the Koszul exact sequence (
1.14
)
\otimes\,\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}
into short exact sequences:

(1.16)

\displaystyle 0\to\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}\otimes\bigwedge^{4}\Sym^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}\otimes\bigwedge^{3}\Sym^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\to K_{1}\to 0

\displaystyle 0\to K_{1}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}\otimes\bigwedge^{2}\Sym^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\to K_{2}\to 0

\displaystyle 0\to K_{2}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}\otimes\Sym^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\to K_{3}\to 0

\displaystyle 0\to K_{3}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}\to\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}\to 0.

2.
The symmetric bundle
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}

The aim of this section is to prove Theorems
A
,
B
and
C
in the special case when
\lambda=(m,0,0,0)
. Recall that in this case
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}=\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
.

More precisely, we will prove the following result. Let us put

r_{m}=\binom{m+3}{3}.

Theorem 2.1
.

For any
m\geq 0
the vector bundle
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
is slope stable, atomic and rigid.

Moreover we have

\operatorname{ch}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=r_{m}\left(1,\frac{m}{4}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q}),\frac{m(m-1)}{40}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{2}+\frac{m(m+4)}{20}\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\mathcal{Q}),\frac{m^{2}}{4}\operatorname{ch}_{3}(\mathcal{Q}),\frac{m(7m-2)}{20}\operatorname{ch}_{4}(\mathcal{Q})\right)

from which we get:

\displaystyle\Delta(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\frac{m(m+4)}{20}r_{m}^{2}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X),

\displaystyle\chi(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q},\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle=3\left(\frac{3m^{2}+12m-20}{20}\right)^{2}r_{m}^{2}\quad\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle\tilde{v}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle=r_{m}\left(1,\frac{m}{4}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q}),-\frac{3m-5}{4}\right).

In Proposition
2.3
we give a deeper description of the Ext-groups of
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Let us put

\tilde{\delta}(m):=\frac{1}{4}\frac{m(m+4)}{20}r_{m}^{2}\in{\mathbb{Q}}[m].

The same proof of Theorem
2.1
shows that if
E
is a vector bundle of rank
4
on a smooth and projective variety
Z
(of dimension at leastt
2
), then

\Delta(\Sym^{m}E)=\tilde{\delta}(m)\Delta(E).

What is remarkable about the polynomial
\tilde{\delta}(m)
is that it takes integral values when
m
in an integer. This suggests that
\tilde{\delta}(m)
may be counting some combinatorial quantity.

In fact this is the case: the function
\delta(m)
appears as a special case of
[
Muk03
, Lemma 8.14]
(more precisely, the case
r=4
in loc. cit.) and counts the number of standard tableaux of size
(m-1,m-1)
for
\textrm{Mat}(2,6)
. These form a basis for the degree
m-1
component of the homogeneous coordinate ring of the Grassmannian
\Gr(2,6)
(see e.g. Remark 8.16 in loc. cit.), i.e.

\tilde{\delta}(m)=h^{0}(\Gr(2,6),\mathcal{O}(m-1)).

We point out that
\delta(m)
has another interesting Lie-theoretical connection, being the dimension of
V^{(k)}
, the distinguished module in the Severi variety, see the case
a=4
of
[
LM06
, Theorem 7.3]
.

As we will see later, the function
\delta(m)
extends to a function
\delta(\lambda)
such that
\Delta(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})=\delta(\lambda)\Delta(\mathcal{Q})
. The values of
\delta(\lambda)
are still integral (when valuated on integral entries), but it is not clear what is that they count.

The rest of this section is dedicated to the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Since the rank of
\mathcal{Q}
is
4
, it is well known that

\operatorname{rk}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=r_{m}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad c_{1}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=\frac{m}{4}r_{m}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q}).

The remaining Chern classes can be computed combinatorially via the splitting principle. The following formula can be found in
[
Svr93
, Theorem 4.8]
:

(2.1)

\operatorname{ch}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=\sum_{\lambda}\frac{1}{\lVert\lambda\rVert}\sum_{\mu\leq\lambda}\binom{m+3}{m-|\mu|}\overleftarrow{\mu}!S(\lambda,\mu)\operatorname{ch}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{Q}).

Let us explain the formula above. The first summation is taken over all partitions
\lambda
such that
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
for every
i
, and
\operatorname{ch}_{\lambda}(\tilde{\mathcal{Q}})
stands for the product
\prod_{i}\operatorname{ch}_{\lambda_{i}}(\tilde{\mathcal{Q}})
. The norm
\lVert\lambda\rVert
is the product
a_{1}!a_{2}!\cdots
, where
a_{i}=\sharp\{j\mid\lambda_{j}=i\}
. The second summation is taken over all the sub-partitions
\mu\leq\lambda
such that
\ell(\mu)=\ell(\lambda)
(in particular also
\mu_{i}\neq 0
for every
i
). The symbol
\overleftarrow{\mu}
stands for the partition
(\mu_{1}-1,\mu_{2}-1,\cdots)
and
\overleftarrow{\mu}!=\prod_{i}(\mu_{i}-1)!
.
Finally,
S(\lambda,\mu)=\prod_{i}S(\lambda_{i},\mu_{i})
, where
S(x,y)
is the Stirling number of the second kind.

It follows from the formula that
\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})
has only contributes from
c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{2}
(corresponding to
\lambda=(1,1)
) and
\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\mathcal{Q})
(corresponding to
\lambda=(2)
). An explicit computation gives

\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=\frac{m^{2}-m}{40}r_{m}c_{1}(Q)^{2}+\frac{m^{2}+4m}{20}r_{m}\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\mathcal{Q}).

Using the definition, we get

\Delta(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{2}-2\operatorname{rk}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\mathcal{Q})=\frac{1}{4}\frac{m^{2}+4m}{20}r_{m}^{2}\Delta(\mathcal{Q}).

Since
\Delta(\mathcal{Q})=c_{2}(X)
(item
(4)
of Lemma
1.6
), it follows that
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
is modular.

Using again the formula (
2.1
) and the identities in Lemma
1.7
, we further compute

\operatorname{ch}_{3}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=\frac{m^{2}}{4}r_{m}\operatorname{ch}_{3}(\mathcal{Q})

and

\operatorname{ch}_{4}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=\frac{7m^{2}-2m}{20}r_{m}\operatorname{ch}_{4}(\mathcal{Q})

Now, to simplify the notation, for a vector bundle
F
let us put

\Xi(F):=\operatorname{ch}_{2}(F)^{2}-2c_{1}(F)\operatorname{ch}_{3}(F)+2\operatorname{rk}(F)\operatorname{ch}_{4}(F).

This is justified by the fact that in this way we get

\operatorname{ch}(\mathcal{E}nd(F))=\left(\operatorname{rk}(F)^{2},0,-\Delta(F),0,\Xi(F)\right).

Using the computations of
\operatorname{ch}_{j}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})
above,
Lemma
1.7
and the fact that
\operatorname{ch}_{4}(\mathcal{Q})=\frac{1}{1104}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)^{2}
(item
(3.e)
of Lemma
1.7
), we get

\Xi(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=\frac{m(m+4)}{110400}(9m^{2}+36m-5)r_{m}^{2}c_{2}(X)^{2}.

Since
\int_{X}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)^{2}=828
(item (1) of Lemma
1.6
), we have that

\int_{X}\Xi(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=\frac{3m(m+4)}{20}\frac{9m^{2}+36m-5}{20}r_{m}^{2}

Now, by item (2) of Lemma
1.6

\operatorname{td}_{X}=\left(1,0,\frac{1}{12}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X),0,3\mathsf{p}\right)

so that by Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch formula we get

\displaystyle\chi(\Sym^{m}(Q),\Sym^{m}(Q))

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\operatorname{ch}(\mathcal{E}nd(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}))\operatorname{td}_{X}

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\left(3r_{m}^{2}\mathsf{p}-\frac{1}{12}\Delta(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}).c_{2}(X)+\Xi(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})\right)

\displaystyle=3r_{m}^{2}-\frac{69}{4}\frac{m(m+4)}{20}r_{m}^{2}+\frac{3}{20}\frac{m(m+4)}{20}(9m^{2}+36m-5)r_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(3+9\frac{m(m+4)}{20}\frac{3m^{2}+12m-40}{20}\right)\binom{m+3}{3}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{400}(9m^{4}+72m^{3}+24m^{2}-480m+400)r_{m}^{2}

(2.2)

\displaystyle=3\left(\frac{3m^{2}+12m-20}{20}\right)^{2}r_{m}^{2}.

Let us now compute the dimensions of the Ext-groups of
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

We have

\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Ext}^{p}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q},\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\mbox{if }p=0,4\\
0&\mbox{if }p=1,3\\
3\left(\frac{3m^{2}+12m-20}{20}\right)^{2}r_{m}^{2}-2&\mbox{if }p=2.\end{array}\right.

More precisely,

\operatorname{Ext}^{2}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q},\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathbb{C}}&m=0\\
{\mathbb{C}}&m=1\\
{\mathbb{C}}\oplus\Sigma_{(2,2,1,1,0,0)}&n=2\\
{\mathbb{C}}\oplus\Sigma_{(2,2,1,1,0,0)}\oplus\bigoplus_{n=3}^{m}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\Sigma_{(2n-2,n-2,n-2,n-3,0)}\\
\oplus\\
\Sigma_{(2n-2,n,n-1,n-1,n-2,0)}\\
\oplus\\
\Sigma_{(2n-2,n,n-1,n-1,n-2)}\end{array}\right)&n\geq 3\,.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

We use the Koszul resolution (
1.14
) and the spectral sequence (
1.15
) applied to the vector bundle
\mathcal{E}nd(\Sym^{m}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}})
.

First of all, let us observe that by Example
1.2
we have

\mathcal{E}nd(\Sym^{m}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}})=\left(\Sigma_{(m,m,m,0)}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\Sym^{m}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\right)\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(-m),

and that, by Pieri’s formula, we have

\Sigma_{(m,m,m,0)}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\Sym^{m}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{m}\Sigma_{(2m-i,m,m,i)}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}.

Therefore we get

(2.3)

\mathcal{E}nd(\Sym^{m}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}})=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{m}\Sigma_{(2m-i,m,m,i)|(m,m)}=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{m}\Sigma_{(2n,n,n,0|n,n)},

where in the last equality we used (
1.3
) and the substitution
n=m-i
.

In Lemma
2.5
below we compute explicitly the cohomology groups of the bundles
\Sigma_{(2n,n,n,0|n,n)}
, from which the claim follows at once.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

The claim about the dimensions of the Ext-groups of
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
does not need the whole analysis done in the proof of Lemma
2.5
. In fact it would have been enough to remark that for odd
p
the bundles
\Sigma_{(2n,n,n,0|n,n)}\otimes\bigwedge^{p}\Sym^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
are acyclic and that for even
p
the same bundles only contribute to
\operatorname{Ext}^{2}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q},\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})
(apart from the case
n=0
, which corresponds to the trivial line bundle). Then the claim would follow from the computation (
2.2
).

Lemma 2.5
.

For any
n\geq 0
we have

\oH^{0}(\Sigma_{(2n,n,n,0|n,n)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathbb{C}}&\mbox{if }n=0\\
0&\mbox{if }n\neq 0\end{array}\right.

\oH^{1}(\Sigma_{(2n,n,n,0|n,n)}=0

\oH^{2}(\Sigma_{(2n,n,n,0|n,n)})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathbb{C}}&\mbox{if }n=0\\
0&\mbox{if }n=1\\
\Sigma_{(2,2,1,1,0,0)}&\mbox{if }n=2\\
\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\Sigma_{(2n-2,n-2,n-2,n-3,n-3,0)}\\
\oplus\\
\Sigma_{(2n-2,n,n-1,n-1,n-2,0)}\\
\oplus\\
\Sigma_{(2n-2,n+1,n+1,n,n,0)}\end{array}\right)&\mbox{if }n\geq 3\end{array}\right.

Proof.

First of all, we need to compute the cohomology groups of the homogeneous vector bundles on
\Gr(2,6)
of the form

\Sigma_{(2n,n,n,0|n,n)}\otimes\bigwedge^{p}\Sym^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}.

We divide this computation in several steps labelled by the exponent
p
of the wedge product. We remind to Section
1.3
for the decomposition of
\bigwedge^{p}\Sym^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
into irreducible factors, which we will tacitly use.

•

(
p=0
). In this case we need to compute the cohomology of
\Sigma_{(2n,n,n,0|n,n)}
. Let us put
v=(2n,n,n,0,n,n)
. According to the Borel–Weil–Bott algorithm (Section
1.1.1
), we first need to look at

v+\delta=(2n+5,n+4,n+3,2,n+1,n).

It is then clear that for
n=0
we have only cohomology in degree
0
, given by the trivial partition, while for
n=1,2
the bundle
\Sigma_{(2n,n,n,0|n,n)}
is acyclic. Finally, for
n\geq 3
we always have
\ell(v+\delta)=2
, therefore in this case we have

\oH^{2}(\Sigma_{(2n,n,n,0|n,n)})=\Sigma_{(2n-2,n-2,n-2,n-3,n-3,0)}.

•

(p=1). In this case we need to compute the cohomology of
\Sigma_{(2n,n,n,0|n+3,n)}
, so that we put
v=(2n,n,n,0,n+3,n)
and hence we need to look at

v+\delta=(2n+5,n+4,n+3,2,n+4,n).

Since there are repeated entries, the bundle is acyclic for every
n\geq 0
.

•

(p=2). Here the bundle
\bigwedge^{2}\Sym^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
has two irreducible factors, hence we divide the analysis in two sub-cases.

(1)

Let us first consider the vector
v=(2n,n,n,0,n+3,n+3)
.
In this case

v+\delta=(2n+5,n+4,n+3,2,n+4,n+3)

has repeated entries, therefore
\Sigma_{(2n,n,n,0|n,n)}\otimes\Sigma_{(3,3)}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
is acyclic.

(2)

Let us then consider the vector
v=(2n,n,n,0,n+5,n+1)
. Then

v+\delta=(2n+5,n+4,n+3,2,n+6,n+1).

For
n=0
we have
\ell(v+\delta)=4
, so that there is only one non-trivial cohomology group in degree
4
, given by the trivial partition. For
n=1
we have repeated entries, so that the bundle is acyclic. Finally, for
n\geq 2
we have
\ell(v+\delta)=4
, so that we have only one non trivial cohomology group in degree
4
, more precisely

\oH^{4}(\Sigma_{(2n,n,n,0|n,n)}\otimes\Sigma_{(5,1)}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}})=\Sigma_{(2n-2,n,n-1,n-1,n-2,0)}.

•

(
p=3
). In this case we have to consider the vector
v=(2n,n,n,0,n+6,n+3)
. Since

v+\delta=(2n+5,n+4,n+3,2,n+7,n+3),

has repeated entries, the bundle
\Sigma_{(2n,n,n,0|n+6,n+3)}
is acyclic for every
n\geq 0
.

•

(
p=4
). Let us finally consider the vector
v=(2n,n,n,0,n+6,n+6)
, and hence the vector

v+\delta=(2n+5,n+4,n+3,2,n+7,n+6).

For
n=0
we have
\ell(v+\delta)=8
and again we have only one non-trivial cohomology group in degree
8
given by the trivial partition. If
n=1,2
, then the bundle
\Sigma_{(2n,n,n,0|n+6,n+6)}
is acyclic. For
n\geq 3
we have
\ell(v+\delta)=6
so that

\oH^{6}(\Sigma_{(2n,n,n,0|n+6,n+6)})=\Sigma_{(2n-2,n+1,n+1,n,n,0)}.

To get the claim is it enough to plug these results into the long exact sequences associated to the short exact sequences (
1.16
).
∎

As a corollary we get the stability of
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
.

Corollary 2.6
.

The vector bundles
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
are slope stable.

Proof.

First of all, by
[
O’Gr22
, Remark 8.6]
the vector bundle
\mathcal{Q}
on
X
is slope stable, hence by
[
HL10
, Theorem 3.2.11]
the vector bundles
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
are polystable. Since by Proposition
2.3
they are simple, the claim follows.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

We have seen so far that
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
is a slope stable, modular and rigid vector bundle on a fourfold of type
\operatorname{K3}^{[2]}
. We point out that, unless
m=1
, these bundles do not satisfy the hypothesis of
[
O’Gr22
, Theorem 1.4]
, for example because the ranks do not match, and are therefore new in literature.

Since they are rigid, in analogy with the case of stable sheaves on K3 surfaces it is natural to expect that the uniqueness statement of
[
O’Gr22
, Theorem 1.4]
also applies to them.

Finally, we prove that
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
are atomic.

Proposition 2.8
.

The bundles
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
are atomic with extended Mukai vector

\tilde{v}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=\left(r_{m},\frac{m}{4}r_{m}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q}),-\frac{3m-5}{4}r_{m}\right).

Proof.

First of all, let us remark that the equalities

\Delta(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=\frac{1}{4}\frac{m^{2}+4m}{20}r_{m}^{2}\Delta(\mathcal{Q})\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\chi(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q},\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=3\left(\frac{3m^{2}+12m-20}{20}\right)^{2}r_{m}^{2}

from Section
2
, together with items
(1)
and
(2)
of Lemma
1.19
forces the quantity
s(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})
in the normalisation (
1.12
) to be
s(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=-\frac{3m-5}{4}r_{m}
. Therefore an extended Mukai vector for
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
, if it exists, must be of the form

\tilde{v}=\left(r_{m},\frac{m}{4}r_{m}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q}),-\frac{3m-5}{4}r_{m}\right)\in\widetilde{\oH}(X,{\mathbb{Q}}).

On the other hand, we claim that

(2.4)

\operatorname{T}(\tilde{v}^{(2)})=v(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}),

from which it would follow that
\tilde{v}
is indeed the extended Mukai vector of
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
, and therefore, by Proposition
1.15
,
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
would be atomic.

Let us prove the claim by using item
(3)
of Lemma
1.19
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{T}(\tilde{v}^{(2)})=

\displaystyle\left(r_{m},\frac{m}{4}r_{m}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q}),\right.

\displaystyle\frac{3m^{2}-3m+5}{120}r_{m}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{2}+\frac{3m^{2}+12m-20}{60}r_{m}\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\mathcal{Q}),

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{m(3m-5)}{288}r_{m}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{3},\frac{(3m-5)^{2}}{32}r_{m}\right).

Now, by item
(3)
of Lemma
1.6
, we have that

\sqrt{\operatorname{td}_{X}}=\left(1,0,\frac{1}{24}\operatorname{c}_{2}(X),0,\frac{25}{32}\mathsf{p}\right).

The Mukai vector
v(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=\operatorname{ch}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})\sqrt{\operatorname{td}_{X}}
can then be explicitly computed as

\displaystyle v(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})=

\displaystyle\left(r_{m},\frac{m}{4}r_{m}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q}),\right.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{24}r_{m}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)+\frac{m(m-1)}{40}r_{m}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{2}+\frac{m(m+4)}{20}r_{m}\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\mathcal{Q}),

\displaystyle\frac{m}{96}r_{m}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q}).\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)+\frac{m^{2}}{4}r_{m}\operatorname{ch}_{3}(\mathcal{Q}),

\displaystyle\left.\frac{25}{32}r_{m}+\frac{m(m-1)}{960}r_{m}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{2}.\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)+\frac{m(m+4)}{480}r_{m}\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\mathcal{Q}).\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)+\frac{m(7m-2)}{20}r_{m}\operatorname{ch}_{4}(\mathcal{Q})\right)

Let us now recall that

\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)=\Delta(\mathcal{Q})=c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{2}-8\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\mathcal{Q}).

Using the identities in Lemma
1.7
, it follows from this that

c_{1}(\mathcal{Q}).\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)=\frac{5}{3}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{3},\quad\int_{X}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{2}.\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)=180\quad\mbox{and}\quad\int_{X}\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\mathcal{Q}).\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)=-81.

Substituting these equalities in the expression of
v(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})
above gives the equality (
2.4
), thus completing the proof.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

Notice that

\tilde{v}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q})^{2}=\frac{3m^{2}+12m-20}{8}r_{m}^{2}.

This number is negative for
m=1
, but it is positive for
m>1
. Since
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
is rigid for every
m\geq 1
, this is in contrast with the case of K3 surfaces, where rigidity is equivalent to having a Mukai vector with negative square.

3.
Two worked-out examples

Before diving into the proof of the main theorems, we wish to work out by hand two examples, in which some technical details are exploited: this will also serve as a leading guide for the rest of the paper.

3.1.
The vector bundle
\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}

Let us first consider the vector bundle
\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}
on
\Gr(2,V_{6})
. By Pieri’s formula (see decomposition (
1.4
)) we have that

\Sym^{2}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}=\Sym^{3}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}\oplus\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}

and hence, by restriction,

\Sym^{2}\mathcal{Q}\otimes\mathcal{Q}=\Sym^{3}\mathcal{Q}\oplus\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}.

In particular we can interpret
\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}
as the kernel of the natural map of homogeneous bundles
\Sym^{2}\mathcal{Q}\otimes\mathcal{Q}\to\Sym^{3}\mathcal{Q}
.

We start by computing the Chern character of
\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}
using the decomposition above:

\operatorname{ch}(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})=\operatorname{ch}(\Sym^{2}\mathcal{Q}\otimes\mathcal{Q})-\operatorname{ch}(\Sym^{3}\mathcal{Q}).

By the computations in Section
2
we know that

\operatorname{ch}(\Sym^{2}\mathcal{Q})=\left(10,5c_{1}(\mathcal{Q}),\frac{1}{2}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{2}+6\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\mathcal{Q}),10\operatorname{ch}_{3}(\mathcal{Q}),12\operatorname{ch}_{4}(\mathcal{Q})\right)

and

\operatorname{ch}(\Sym^{3}\mathcal{Q})=\left(20,15c_{1}(\mathcal{Q}),3c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{2}+21\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\mathcal{Q}),45\operatorname{ch}_{3}(\mathcal{Q}),57\operatorname{ch}_{4}(\mathcal{Q})\right),

from which we deduce that (we use Lemma
1.7
to simplify the expressions)

\operatorname{ch}(\Sym^{2}\mathcal{Q}\otimes\mathcal{Q})=\left(40,30c_{1}(\mathcal{Q}),7c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{2}+34\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\mathcal{Q}),60\operatorname{ch}_{3}(\mathcal{Q}),34\operatorname{ch}_{4}(\mathcal{Q})\right).

We eventually get

\operatorname{ch}(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})=\left(20,15c_{1}(\mathcal{Q}),4c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{2}+13\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\mathcal{Q}),15\operatorname{ch}_{3}(\mathcal{Q}),-23\operatorname{ch}_{4}(\mathcal{Q})\right).

From this, it is easy to compute the discriminant and the Euler characteristic of
\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}
, which we collect in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

The following equality hold:

\Delta(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})=65\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\chi(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q},\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})=363.

Proof.

The first equality follows directly from the definition of the discriminant.

Let us prove the second equality. First of all, as in Section
2
we define the quantity

\Xi(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})=\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})-2c_{1}(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}).\operatorname{ch}_{3}(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})+2\operatorname{rk}(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})\operatorname{ch}_{4}(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})

so we have

\operatorname{ch}(\mathcal{E}nd(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}))=\left(400,0,-\Delta(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}),0,\Xi(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})\right).

By an explicit computation (and the help of Lemma
1.7
to simplify the expressions) we get

\Xi(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})=4864\operatorname{ch}_{4}(\mathcal{Q}).

Now, using again the expression

\operatorname{td}_{X}=\left(1,0,\frac{1}{12}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X),0,3\mathsf{p}\right)

and the Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch formula, we eventually get

\chi(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q},\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})=\int_{X}\left(1200\mathsf{p}-\frac{65}{12}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)^{2}+4864\operatorname{ch}_{4}(\mathcal{Q})\right)=363,

where we used again items (1) and (4) of Lemma
1.6
.
∎

\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}

.

4.
Chern character of
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}

In this section we will provide the preliminary computations needed for the proof of
Theorem A
. In particular we will compute the Chern character of the vector bundles
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
on the irreducible symplectic fourfold
X
.
Before stating the main result, let us introduce the following notation.

For a partition
\lambda=(m,t,s,0)
, we define the following functions:

(4.1)

\displaystyle r_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{(m+3)(t+2)(s+1)(m-t+1)(m-s+2)(t-s+1)}{12}

(4.2)

\displaystyle\ell(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{m+t+s}{4}

(4.3)

\displaystyle\delta(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{5}\left(4\ell(\lambda)^{2}+\tau(\lambda)\right)

(4.4)

\displaystyle\tau(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{-2(mt+ms+ts)+3m+t-s}{3}

(4.5)

\displaystyle\xi(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(t,s)m^{2}+\beta(t,s)m+\gamma(t,s)}{60}

where

\displaystyle\alpha(t,s)

\displaystyle=9t^{2}+21ts+9s^{2}-33t-30s+21

\displaystyle\beta(t,s)

\displaystyle=21t^{2}s+21ts^{2}-15t^{2}-18ts+6s^{2}+t-8s-6

\displaystyle\gamma(t,s)

\displaystyle=9t^{2}s^{2}-9t^{2}s+9ts^{2}-3t^{2}+13ts-3s^{2}-14t+14s.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\lambda=(m,t,s,0)
be a partition as above. Then

(4.6)

\displaystyle\operatorname{rk}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})=

\displaystyle r_{\lambda}

(4.7)

\displaystyle c_{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})=

\displaystyle\ell(\lambda)r_{\lambda}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})

(4.8)

\displaystyle\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\ell(\lambda)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\delta(\lambda)\right)r_{\lambda}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{2}+\delta(\lambda)r_{\lambda}\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\mathcal{Q})

(4.9)

\displaystyle\operatorname{ch}_{3}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})=

\displaystyle\tau(\lambda)\ell(\lambda)r_{\lambda}\operatorname{ch}_{3}(\mathcal{Q})

(4.10)

\displaystyle\operatorname{ch}_{4}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})=

\displaystyle\xi(\lambda)r_{\lambda}\operatorname{ch}_{4}(\mathcal{Q}).

The rank formula is the very well-known Weyl formula, and the formula for the first Chern class is a particular case of
[
Rub13
, Theorem 2]
; the formulas for the higher Chern classes seems to be new in literature, even in this very special case.

Remark 4.2
.

Notice that one can also write

\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})=\frac{1}{10}\left(4\ell(\lambda)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\tau(\lambda)\right)r_{\lambda}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{2}+\frac{1}{5}\left(4\ell(\lambda)^{2}+\tau(\lambda)\right)r_{\lambda}\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\mathcal{Q}).

The reason to state the result only for partitions with last entry equal to zero is motivated by the fact that we can always reduce to this case after a twist by a suitable line bundle. We will explain this in Section
1.1.2
. Moreover we will explain in Section
4.1
the strategy to prove the theorem and we will perform the proof in Section
4.2
.

4.1.

The main idea is to use several strong inductions on the entries of
\lambda
. The starting point of the argument is the case
\lambda=(m,0,0,0)
: in this case
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}=\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
and the computations have been performed in Section
2
thanks to the work of
[

Next we will use Pieri’s rule to perform the inductive steps. We will divide the proof in three cases.

\bullet

Case

Here we will deal with partitions of the form

5.
Proof of Theorem
A

Here
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})
is a partition and
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
is the associated vector bundle on
X
. From now on we will always assume that
X
is very general, i.e.
\operatorname{Pic}(X)
has rank
1
and is generated by the restriction of the Plücker class.

Proposition 5.1
.

The vector bundle
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
is slope polystable.

Proof.

The proof is divided in a few steps, but before proceeding, let us simplify a bit our analysis. Since tensoring with a multiple of the polarisation does not change the stability, using the reduction trick explained in Section
1.1.2
it will be enough to prove the proposition for partitions of the form
\lambda=(m,t,s,0)
.

First of all we have already noticed in Corollary
2.6
that the symmetric bundles
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
are slope stable.

By
[
HL10
, Theorem 3.2.11]
we then have that
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}\otimes\Sym^{t}\mathcal{Q}
is slope polystable. In particular every factor in the decomposition (
4.11
) is also slope polystable.

The rest of proof proceeds in the same way, where the decompositions (
4.12
) and (
4.13
) are used. We leave the details to the reader.
∎

What prevents us to claim the slope stability of
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
is the fact that we do not have an a priori control of
\operatorname{Hom}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q},\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})
. Conjecture
1
would imply that the
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
’s are indeed stable. We exhibit in Section
8
many cases for which the simplicty of
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
can be deduced.

Next we prove the modularity.

Proposition 5.2
.

The vector bundle
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
is modular. More precisely,

\Delta(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})=\frac{\delta(\lambda)}{4}\operatorname{rk}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})^{2}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X),

where

\delta(\lambda)=\frac{3(\sum_{i=1}^{4}\lambda_{i}^{2})-2(\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j})+12\lambda_{1}+4\lambda_{2}-4\lambda_{3}-12\lambda_{4}}{60}.

Proof.

First of all, as we recalled in Section
1.1.2
, there is an equality

\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}=\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(\lambda_{4}),

where
\lambda^{\prime}=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{4},\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{4},\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{4},0)
, and clearly

\Delta(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})=\Delta(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}).

Since
\lambda^{\prime}_{4}=0
, we can apply Theorem
4.1
to explicitly compute the discriminant:

\Delta(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q})=\frac{\delta(\lambda^{\prime})}{4}\operatorname{rk}(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q})^{2}\Delta(\mathcal{Q}).

Finally it is enough to define

\delta(\lambda):=\delta(\lambda^{\prime}),

notice that
\operatorname{rk}(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q})=\operatorname{rk}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})
and recall that
\Delta(\mathcal{Q})=\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)
(see for example
[
O’Gr22
, Section 2.1]
).
∎

Proof of Theorem

In this section we want to study which of the modular vector bundles
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
are atomic.

We start by computing the Euler characteristic of the bundles
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
via Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch.

6.1.
The Euler characteristic of
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}

We have already explicitly computed the discriminant of
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
in Proposition
5.2
, so that we compute now the Euler characteristic of
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
in order to compare it with their values in case they were atomic.

Proposition 6.1
.

There exists a degree
4
polynomial
\mathsf{P}\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}]
such that the Euler characteristic of the vector bundle
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
is

\chi(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q},\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})=3\mathsf{P}(\lambda)r_{\lambda}^{2}.

The polynomial
\mathsf{P}(\lambda)
is explicitly determined at the end of the proof as a combination of the polynomials
\ell
,
\tau
and
\xi
introduced at the beginning of Section
4
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Proposition
5.2
, we use the reduction trick of Section
1.1.2
to get

\chi(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q},\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})=\chi(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q},\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}),

where
\lambda^{\prime}=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{4},\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{4},\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{4},0)
.

As usual, if we put

\Xi(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q})=\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q})^{2}-2c_{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q})\operatorname{ch}_{3}(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q})+2\operatorname{rk}(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q})\operatorname{ch}_{4}(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q})

then

\operatorname{ch}(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q})=\left(r_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{2},0,-\Delta(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}),0,\Xi(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q})\right).

Since

\operatorname{td}_{X}=\left(1,0,\frac{1}{12}c_{2}(X),0,3\mathsf{p}\right)

(where
\mathsf{p}
is the class of a point)
by Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch formula we get

\displaystyle\chi(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q},\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\operatorname{ch}(\mathcal{E}nd(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q})\operatorname{td}_{X}

\displaystyle=3r_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{2}-\frac{1}{12}\int_{X}\Delta(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}).\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)+\int_{X}\Xi(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}).

By Proposition
5.2
we have that

\Delta(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q})=\frac{\delta(\lambda^{\prime})}{4}r_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{2}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X).

Using the equality
\int_{X}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)^{2}=828
(see
[
O’Gr22
, Equality (5.3.4)]
), we get

-\frac{1}{12}\int_{X}\Delta(\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}).\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)=-\frac{69}{4}\delta(\lambda^{\prime})r_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{2}=-\frac{69}{20}(4\ell(\lambda^{\prime})^{2}+\tau(\lambda^{\prime}))r_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{2},

where the last equality is the definition

.

Proof of Theorem

Let us recall the statement of Theorem

Theorem 6.3

(Theorem
B
)
.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})
be a partition. Then
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
is atomic if and only if
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}
or
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}=\lambda_{4}
.

We will prove each implication in a different proposition.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})
be a partition. If at most two entries are equal, then
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
is not atomic.

Proof.

First of all, by Proposition
6.1
and Lemma
1.19
we get that if
\mathsf{P}(\lambda)
is not the square of a rational number, then
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
cannot be atomic.

Let us then consider those partitions
\lambda
for which
\mathsf{P}(\lambda)
is of the form
q_{\lambda}^{2}
for some
q_{\lambda}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
. If
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
were atomic, then let us denote by
\tilde{v}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})
its extended Mukai vector. By Lemma
1.19
.(1) and Theorem
A
then we must have

\tilde{q}(\tilde{v}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}))=\frac{5}{2}q_{\lambda}r_{\lambda}^{2}.

On the other hand, by Lemma
1.19
.(2) and Proposition
5.2
we must also have

\tilde{q}(\tilde{v}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}))=\frac{5}{2}(3\delta(\lambda)-1)r_{\lambda}^{2}.

Putting these two equality together we get that

q_{\lambda}=3\delta(\lambda)-1.

One can directly see that the equation

\left(3\delta(\lambda)-1\right)^{2}-\mathsf{P}(\lambda)=0

has no integral solutions as soon as at most two entries are equal.
∎

Let us then prove the reverse implication.

Proposition 6.5
.

If
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}
or
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}=\lambda_{4}
, then
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}
is atomic.

Proof.

Let us first reduce the proof to the case of symmetric bundles. Of course if
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}=\lambda_{4}
, then
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{O}_{X}
is atomic, therefore we can suppose that at least two entries are different.

Under this assumption we have only two cases, namely
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{2})
and
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{4})
.

In the first case, by the reduction trick of Section
1.7
, we have

\Sigma_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{2})}\mathcal{Q}=\Sym^{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}\mathcal{Q}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(\lambda_{2}).

In the second case, by Example
1.2
we have

\Sigma_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{4})}\mathcal{Q}=\Sym^{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{4}}\mathcal{Q}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(\lambda_{1}).

Since tensoring with a line bundle and passing to the dual are operations that preserve the atomicity (
[
Bec22
, Proposition 3.10]
), without loss of generality we can reduce to the case
\lambda=(m,0,0,0)
, so that
\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}=\Sym^{m}\mathcal{Q}
and the claim follows at once from Proposition
2.8
.
∎

Computing the Ext-groups of the vector bundles

can be very difficult. In fact the spectral sequence (

Proposition 7.1

If

∎

8.11.
An example of resolution of the indeterminacy

The examples analysed in the sections before have a common feature: for all of them the Koszul spectral sequence determines
\operatorname{Hom}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q},\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})
without indeterminacy. This is enough for item
(1)
of Conjecture
1
. On the other hand in many cases the Koszul spectral sequence has non-trivial differentials contributing to
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q},\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q})
. An example is the series
\lambda=(m,3,0,0)
, which has indeterminacy in degree
1
from
m\geq 5
.

In this section we resolve by brute force this indeterminacy in the first case, namely for
\lambda=(5,3,0,0)
. This computation also shows the typical problems that one encounters in these cases.

Lemma 8.52
.

Conjecture
1
holds for
\Sigma_{(5,3,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}
.

Proof.

By Section
8.7
,
\Sigma_{(5,3,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}
is simple, hence stable. Therefore, we only need to check that the first extension space is indeed 20 dimensional. As a matter of fact,
\Sigma_{(5,3,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}
corresponds to the value
m=5
in
\Sigma_{(m,3,0,0)}
, which is the first one in the series for which we find some indeterminacy.
If we decompose
\mathcal{E}nd(\Sigma_{(5,3,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})
in irreducible factors, we get a direct sum of 60 factors. Of these, only two create problems (and they are dual to each other). These are
\Sigma_{(10,6,2,2)}\mathcal{Q}\otimes\mathcal{O}(-5)
and
\Sigma_{(8,8,4,0)}\mathcal{Q}\otimes\mathcal{O}(-5)
. We analyze here the first one, since the second one will completely analogous.

We can easily compute that

\oH^{k}\left(\Gr(2,6),\bigwedge^{4}\Sym^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\otimes\Sigma_{(10,6,2,2)}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\mathcal{O}(-5)\right)=\begin{cases}0\ \textrm{ if }k\neq 6\\
\Sigma_{(6,4,4,4,0,0)}V_{6}^{\vee}\textrm{ if }k=6\,,\end{cases}

\oH^{k}\left(\Gr(2,6),\bigwedge^{3}\Sym^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\otimes\Sigma_{(10,6,2,2)}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\mathcal{O}(-5)\right)=\begin{cases}0\ \textrm{ if }k\neq 5\\
\Sigma_{(6,4,3,2,0,0)}V_{6}^{\vee}\textrm{ if }k=5\end{cases}

and

\oH^{k}\left(\Gr(2,6),\bigwedge^{2}\Sym^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\otimes\Sigma_{(10,6,2,2)}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\mathcal{O}(-5)\right)=\begin{cases}0\ \textrm{ if }k\neq 4,5\\
\Sigma_{(6,3,3,0,0,0)}V_{6}^{\vee}\textrm{ if }k=5\\
\Sigma_{(6,2,2,2,0,0)}V_{6}^{\vee}\textrm{ if }k=4\,.\\
\end{cases}

Denoting with
C_{3}
the last kernel bundle coming from the splitting of the Koszul sequence (and similarly with
C_{2}
), we therefore obtain a sequence

0\to\Sigma_{(6,4,3,2,0,0)}V_{6}^{\vee}\to\oH^{5}(C_{3})\to\Sigma_{(6,4,4,4,0,0)}V_{6}^{\vee}\to 0,

and moreover
\oH^{i}(C_{3})=0
for
i\neq 5
.

Putting all these information together, we obtain a sequence in cohomology as follows:

0\to\Sigma_{(6,2,2,2,0,0)}V_{6}^{\vee}\to\oH^{4}(C_{2})\to\Sigma_{(6,4,4,4,0,0)}V_{6}^{\vee}\oplus\Sigma_{(6,4,3,2,0,0)}V_{6}^{\vee}\to\Sigma_{(6,3,3,0,0,0)}V_{6}^{\vee}\to\oH^{5}(C_{2})\to 0.

The indeterminacy is therefore resolved (and with it, the Lemma proved) if we are able to show that the map

\Sigma_{(6,4,4,4,0,0)}V_{6}^{\vee}\oplus\Sigma_{(6,4,3,2,0,0)}V_{6}^{\vee}\to\Sigma_{(6,3,3,0,0,0)}V_{6}^{\vee}

is surjective, which is possible if we look at the dimensions (
134750
vs
28875
). Equivalently, we are looking at the injectivity of the dual map

\varphi\colon\Sigma_{(6,6,6,3,3,0)}V_{6}\to\Sigma_{(6,6,2,2,2,0)}V_{6}\oplus\Sigma_{(6,6,4,3,2,0)}V_{6}.

We claim that the projection of the above map to the second component is in fact already injective, which will conclude the proof.

We first map
u\in\Sigma_{(6,6,6,3,3,0)}V_{6}

to
\phi(u)=u\otimes\sigma^{t}\in\Sigma_{(6,6,6,3,3,0)}V_{6}\otimes\Sym^{3}V_{6}
. This map is injective for a general
\sigma^{t}
, where we are reasoning as in
[
KMM10
, Appendix B]
. Moreover, again by generality of
\sigma
, the map
\phi
is non-zero on any irreducible component of
\Sigma_{(6,6,6,3,3,0)}V_{6}\otimes\Sym^{3}V_{6}
.

If we decompose
\Sigma_{(6,6,6,3,3,0)}V_{6}\otimes\Sym^{3}V_{6}
in irreducibles factors, then we observe that
\Sigma_{(6,6,4,3,2,0)}V_{6}
appears with multiplicity 1. Let us denote by
\pi
the projection from
\Sigma_{(6,6,6,3,3,0)}V_{6}\otimes\Sym^{3}V_{6}
to
\Sigma_{(6,6,4,3,2,0)}V_{6}
. Then the map
\varphi
must be obtained as the composition
\varphi=\pi\circ\phi
, which is injective since
\pi
is non-zero on the image of
\phi
.

∎

The proof above solves completely the case
\Sigma_{(5,3,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}
, corroborating our claim leading to Conjecture

. However this proof relies on a sophisticated analysis on the map involved, which cannot be transformed into a general argument for any partition. Moreover, in general the sequences arising from the Koszul complex are longer, and the maps involved are way more complicated to analyze, as already the case
\Sigma_{(4,4,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}
shows.

Appendix A

\Sigma_{\mu}\mathcal{U}

In the main part of the manuscript we only considered vector bundles of the form

, but the reader could ask the reason for this choice. This section provide a simple answer to this question.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})
and
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
be two partitions. Then the vector bundle

\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}\otimes\Sigma_{\mu}\mathcal{U}

on the Fano variety of lines
X
is modular if and only if
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
.

When
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=k
we have
\Sigma_{\mu}\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{O}_{X}(k)
and the modularity follows from Theorem
A
. Therefore the proposition is saying that the bundles considered in Theorem
A
are essentially the only modular vector bundles (among the possible plethysms on a general

).

Proof of Proposition
A.1
.

Let us proceed in steps. First of all, we look at the case when
\lambda=(0,0,0,0)
.

Lemma A.2
.

If
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
is a partition, then

\Delta(\Sigma_{\mu}\mathcal{U})=\frac{\rho(\mu)}{2}\operatorname{rk}(\Sigma_{\mu}\mathcal{U})^{2}\Delta(\mathcal{U}),

where

\rho(\mu)=\frac{\mu_{1}^{2}+\mu_{2}^{2}-2\mu_{1}\mu_{2}+2\mu_{1}-2\mu_{2}}{6}.

In particular,
\Sigma_{\mu}\mathcal{U}
is not modular.

Proof.

Using the same reduction trick as in Section
1.1.2
we have

\Sigma_{\mu}\mathcal{U}=\Sym^{\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}}\mathcal{U}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(\mu_{2}).

Since
\Delta(\Sym^{\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}}\mathcal{U}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(\mu_{2}))=\Delta(\Sym^{\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}}\mathcal{U})
, we can reduce the proof to the symmetric case.

As in Section
2
, we can use
[
Svr93
, Theorem 4.8]
to compute the first two Chern classes of
\Sym^{m}\mathcal{U}
:

\displaystyle c_{1}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{U})=

\displaystyle\frac{m}{2}(m+1)c_{1}(\mathcal{U})

\displaystyle\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{U})=

\displaystyle\frac{m(m-1)}{12}(m+1)c_{1}(\mathcal{U})^{2}+\frac{m(m+2)}{6}(m+1)\operatorname{ch}_{2}(\mathcal{U}).

It is then easy to see that

\Delta(\Sym^{m}\mathcal{U})=\frac{m(m+2)}{12}(m+1)^{2}\Delta(\mathcal{U}).

Putting

\rho(\mu):=\frac{(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}+2)}{6}

proves the first statement of the proof.

Finally, as it is easy to check, one has
\Delta(\mathcal{U})=\frac{3}{2}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)
, from which it follows that
\Sigma_{\mu}\mathcal{U}
is not modular, unless
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{1})
.
∎

To conclude the proof, we use the following known result.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
F
and
G
be two torsion free sheaves on a smooth and projective variety
X
. Then

\Delta(E\otimes F)=\operatorname{rk}(F)^{2}\Delta(E)+\operatorname{rk}(E)^{2}\Delta(F).

Proof.

This is an easy computation.
∎

Putting all together, we eventually get

\displaystyle\Delta(\Sigma_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}\otimes\Sigma_{\mu}\mathcal{U})

\displaystyle=r_{\mu}r_{\lambda}\left(\frac{\delta(\lambda)}{4}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)+\frac{\rho(\mu)}{2}\left(\frac{3}{2}c_{1}(\mathcal{Q})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=r_{\mu}r_{\lambda}\frac{3\rho(\mu)}{2}c_{1}(Q)^{2}+\frac{r_{\mu}r_{\lambda}}{4}\left(\delta(\lambda)+\rho(\mu)\right)\mathsf{c}_{2}(X)

which is not modular unless
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{1})
.

∎

Appendix B
Combinatorial remarks and questions

In this section we work on the Grassmannian
\Gr(2,6)
and we consider the Schur functors
\Sigma_{\lambda}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}
of the tautological quotient bundle. We are interested in uderstanding the Littlewood–Richardson decomposition of

\mathcal{E}nd(\Sigma_{\lambda}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}).

In particular our starting point is Proposition
8.3
, which claims that there is an abstract inclusion

\mathcal{E}nd(\Sigma_{\lambda}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}})\subset\mathcal{E}nd(\Sigma_{\lambda+\delta_{1}}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}),

where
\delta_{1}=(1,0,0,0)
is the first fundamental weight. In particular there exists a finite set of partitions
\mathsf{K}_{\lambda}
such that

(B.1)

\mathcal{E}nd(\Sigma_{\lambda+\delta_{1}}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}})=\mathcal{E}nd(\Sigma_{\lambda}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}})\oplus\bigoplus_{\mu\in\mathsf{K}_{\lambda}}\Sigma_{\mu}.

Moreover, in Section
8
we noticed that the cardinality of
\mathsf{K}_{\lambda}
becomes constant as the first entry
\lambda_{1}
of
\lambda
becomes bigger.

The aim of this appendix is to formalise this remark through a series a conjectures.

From now on, as usual, without loss of generality we suppose that
\lambda=(m,t,s,0)
.

Starting from
\lambda
we can repeatedly apply Proposition
8.3
and hence consider the sequence of partitions
\{\lambda+n\delta_{1}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. To avoid repetitions, when considering a sequence we always think it as starting from
(t,t,s,0)
. This is implicitly what we have done in Section
2
and Section
8
when considering at once partitions of the form
(m,0,0,0)
or
(m,2,1,0)
etc…

It turns out that to better express the phenomena we observed, it is more convenient to write a partition
\lambda
as a combination of the fundamental weights
\delta_{1}=(1,0,0,0)
,
\delta_{2}=(1,1,0,0)
and
\delta_{3}=(1,1,1,0)
. (We do not consider
\delta_{4}
since we only consider partitions whose last entry vanishes.) In other words, we write

\lambda=a\delta_{1}+b\delta_{2}+c\delta_{3},

where

m=a+b+c,\qquad t=b+c\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad s=c.

With this notations it is easier, for example, to express the sequence of partitions associated to Proposition
8.3
: once two positive integers
b
and
c
are fixed, we start with the partition
\lambda=b\delta_{2}+c\delta_{3}
and then we repeatedly add
\delta_{1}
to it.

Our first remark is the following conjecture about the cardinality (counted with multiplicity) of the set
\mathsf{K}_{\lambda}
defined in (
B.1
).

Conjecture 3
.

Fix two positive integers
b
and
c
. Then the cardinality (with multiplicity) of
\mathsf{K}_{a\delta_{1}+b\delta_{2}+c\delta_{3}}
is constant for every
a\geq b+c-1
.

This conjecture determines the threshold after which the number of “new” partitions needed in the Littlewood–Richardson decomposition of
\mathcal{E}nd(\Sigma_{\lambda}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}})
is constant.

This motivates the following definition.

Definition B.1
.

For any positive integers
b
and
c
, we define the natural number
\mathsf{k}_{b,c}
as the cardinality of
\mathsf{K}_{b+c-1\delta_{1}+b\delta_{2}+c\delta_{3}}
, i.e.

\mathsf{k}_{b,c}=|\mathsf{K}_{b+c-1\delta_{1}+b\delta_{2}+c\delta_{3}}|.

Before continuing, let us pause on three examples.

Example B.2
.

Let us point out three explicit examples.

(1)

Let
\lambda=(m,0,0,0)=m\delta_{1}
. We saw in Section
2
that for any
m\geq 1
,

\mathcal{E}nd(\Sym^{m+1}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}})=\mathcal{E}nd(\Sym^{m}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}})\oplus\Sigma_{(2m,m,m,0\,|m,m)}.

Since in this case we have
b=c=0
, Conjecture
3
holds and
\mathsf{k}_{0,0}=1
.

(2)

Let
\lambda=(m,1,0)=(m-1)\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}
, so that
b=1
and
c=0
.
The case
m=1
was described in Example
8.6
and we have

\mathcal{E}nd\left(\Sigma_{(1,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}\right)=\Sigma_{(2,2,0,0\mid 1,1)}\oplus\Sigma_{(2,1,1,0\mid 1,1)}\oplus\Sigma_{(1,1,1,1\mid 1,1)}.

Moreover, we saw in Section
3.1
that for
m=2
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}nd(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}nd\left(\Sigma_{(1,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}\right)\oplus\Sigma_{(4,3,1,0\mid 2,2)}\oplus\Sigma_{(4,2,2,0\mid 2,2)}\oplus

\displaystyle\Sigma_{(4,2,1,1\mid 2,2)}\oplus\Sigma_{(3,3,2,0\mid 2,2)}\oplus\Sigma_{(3,2,2,1\mid 2,2)}

We can easily reproduce similar computations for
m=3
, which gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}nd(\Sigma_{(3,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}nd\left(\Sigma_{(2,1,0,0)}\mathcal{Q}\right)\oplus\Sigma_{(6,4,2,0\mid 3,3)}\oplus\Sigma_{(6,3,3,0\mid 3,3)}\oplus

\displaystyle\Sigma_{(6,3,2,1\mid 3,3)}\oplus\Sigma_{(5,4,3,0\mid 3,3)}\oplus\Sigma_{(5,3,3,1\mid 3,3)}

In each case we add
5
more partitions. In fact, Conjecture
3
holds by Proposition
8.5
and
\mathsf{k}_{1,0}=5
.

(3)

In both the two previous examples, we had
b+c-1\leq 0
. To have a more interesting example, let us consider the sequence
(m,2,0,0)=(m-2)\delta_{1}+2\delta_{2}
. In Example
8.17

\mathcal{E}nd(\Sigma_{(2,2,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})
has 6 irreducible factors, while
\mathcal{E}nd(\Sigma_{(3,2,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})
has 18 irreducible factors: we need to add
12
partitions. Similarly we can see that
\mathcal{E}nd(\Sigma_{(4,2,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})
has 32 components and
\mathcal{E}nd(\Sigma_{(5,2,0,0)}\mathcal{Q})
has 46 components: in both these cases we need to add
14
partitions. In fact Conjecture
3
holds by Proposition
8.16
and
\mathsf{k}_{2,0}=14
.

Remark B.3

Proposition

Working out explicit cases with the help of computer algebra, it seems that an analog of Conjecture

Remark B.4

So far we worked in the grassmannian
