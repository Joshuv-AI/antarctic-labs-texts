Higher discriminants of vector bundles and Schur functors
Alessandro D’Andrea, Enrico Fatighenti, Claudio Onorati

Abstract
. We prove some closed formulas for the logarithmic Chern character of a locally free sheaf. The argument used is representation-theoretic and we connect these formulas with the actions of some Casimir elements of 𝔰 ​ l r \ssl_{r} . As an application, we give a recipe to construct slope polystable modular bundles on hyper-Kähler manifolds from old ones.



Introduction

If
X
is a compact Kähler manifold and
E
is a holomorphic vector bundle on
X
, it is in general an important and difficult problem the computation of the Chern classes
\cc_{i}(E)\in\oH^{2i}(X,\mathbb{Z})
.

In a similar way, if
E
is a vector bundle obtained from another vector bundle
E^{\prime}
via tensor operations, it is important and difficult to write Chern classes of
E
in terms of Chern classes of
E^{\prime}
. Examples are given by symmetric and exterior powers: in
[
Svr93
]
the author give a combinatorial expression of the Chern character of these operations.
Extracting general formulas can be quite difficult even in the case of tensor product of two bundles (see
[
Man16
]
).

Our main result is the following explicit formula for the first three
logarithmic Chern characters
of the Schur functor a vector bundle
E
. As a consequence of this result, we have explicit formulas for the first three Chern characters of Schur functors (see Appendix
B.3
).

Let us recall the setting. If
E
is a vector bundle of rank
r
, then we set

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}(E)=

\displaystyle\cc_{1}(E)

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}(E)=

\displaystyle\cc_{1}(E)^{2}-2r\ch_{2}(E)

\displaystyle\Delta_{3}(E)=

\displaystyle\cc_{1}(E)^{3}-3r\cc_{1}(E)\ch_{2}(E)+3r^{2}\ch_{3}(E)\,.

These classes arise naturally from the logarithm expansion of the Chern character (see Section
2.2
).

Theorem A

(Theorem
4.2
)
.

Let
X
be a compact Kähler manifold or a proper smooth complex variety, and let
E
be a vector bundle of rank
r
on
X
. Denote by
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E
the Schur functor associated to a partition
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
, and denote by
r_{\alpha}
its rank. Then

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)=

\displaystyle\,|\alpha|\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\Delta_{1}(E)

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)=

\displaystyle\,\frac{\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}(\alpha)}{(r-1)(r+1)}\left(\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\right)^{2}\Delta_{2}(E)

\displaystyle\Delta_{3}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)=

\displaystyle\,\frac{\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(3)}(\alpha)}{(r-2)(r-1)(r+1)(r+2)}\left(\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\right)^{3}\Delta_{2}(E)\,,

where
\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}(\alpha)
and
\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(3)}(\alpha)
are polynomials in
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
as introduced in Theorem
3.1
.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time explicit formulas for Chern classes of Schur functors are exhibited in this generality. Notice also that from the classes
\Delta_{1}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)
,
\Delta_{2}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)
and
\Delta_{3}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)
one can recursively also find the classes
c_{1}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)
,
\ch_{2}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)
and
\ch_{3}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)
. For book-keeping reasons, we collect in Section
B.3
these expressions.

The polynomials
\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}(\alpha)
and
\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(3)}(\alpha)
are explicit. In fact we have a purely algebraic interpretation of
\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(k)}
as characters of two explicit Casimir elements of
\ssl_{r}
(see Section
3
).

The proof of Theorem
A
is representation-theoretic. Using the splitting principle, we can reduce to work formally with a complex vector space
V
of dimension
r
and its Schur modules
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}V
, i.e. the corresponding irreducible polynomial representations of
\GL(V)
. We review in Section
1
some basic definitions and facts about polynomial representations.
The two main results in this setting are Theorem
2.7
and Theorem
3.1
, proved respectively in Section
2
and Section
3
. The combination of the two gives Theorem
A
.

Extending these results to degrees higher than
3
seems to be difficult. As we point out in Remark
2.5
, one can hope to achieve such a generalisation by rigidifying the situation, i.e. fixing some numerical invariants. In Section
2.3
we discuss a bit more about this higher degree situation, leaving two open questions.

Beside being an interesting computational and combinatorial problem, there are other geometric motivations for considering (higher) discriminants. In fact we wish to point out that they are strictly connected to the notion of stability. For example, the class
\Delta_{2}
is the usual discriminant, and the famous Bogomolov Theorem states that if a vector bundle
E
is
\omega
-polystable, then
\Delta_{2}(E).\omega^{\dim X-2}\geq 0
. (Here
\omega
is a Kähler class.) Other connections can be found in
[
Dre95
,
Ful20
]
.

From our point of view, another important motivation to consider this problem comes from the geometry of compact hyper-Kähler manifolds. Recently O’Grady have introduced the notion of
modular
vector bundles on compact hyper-Kähler manifolds as an attempt to generalise to higher dimensions the theory of moduli spaces of bundles on K3 surfaces (see
[
O’Gr22
]
). In this context, a vector bundle
E
is called modular if
\Delta_{2}(E)
belongs to a line in
\oH^{4}(X,\mathbb{Q})
intrinsically attached to
X
(see Definition
4.8
).

Because of the numerical nature of this definition, it seems to be particularly difficult to construct examples of modular vector bundles. Recent works in this direction include
[
Bot24
,
Fat24
,
FO24
,
FT25
,
O’Gr24
]
.

A straightforward corollary of Theorem
A
yields the following result, which allows us to construct infinitely many polystable modular bundles starting from old ones.

Theorem B

(Theorem
4.9
)
.

Let
X
be a compact hyper-Kähler manifold. If
E
is a slope polystable modular vector bundle, then for every
\alpha
the Schur functor
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E
is a slope polystable modular vector bundle.

Even if
E
is slope stable, in general
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E
would not be stable (cf. Remark
4.7
).

We conclude the article with two appendices. In the first one, Appendix
A
, we comment on an interesting property of discriminants that stops to work again from degree
4
on-wards. This gives us the opportunity to explore higher discriminants from a different point of view. In Appendix
B
we list useful formulas for the Chern character (in degree up to three) of symmetric and exterior powers bundles, and for Schur bundles, which are obtained as a consequence of Theorem
A
.

1.
Review of polynomial representations of
\GL_{r}

Let
V
be a complex vector space of dimension
r
and
\GL_{r}=\GL(V)
the corresponding algebraic group.

Choosing a basis of
V
provides a choice of a maximal sub-torus
T\subset\GL_{r}
of diagonal matrices. Every polynomial group homomorphism
T\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
is of the form

\lambda\colon\operatorname{diag}(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})\mapsto t_{1}^{\lambda_{1}}\dots t_{r}^{\lambda_{r}},

so that the set
\widehat{T}
of all polynomial group homomorphisms
T\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
can be identified with
\mathbb{N}^{r}
via
\lambda\mapsto(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r})
.
Restricting the action of
\GL_{r}
on a finite-dimensional polynomial representation to the torus
T
provides
a direct sum decomposition

W=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\widehat{T}}W(\lambda),

where
W(\lambda)=\{v\in W\mid t.v=\lambda(t)v\}
is the
weight space
corresponding to
\lambda
.

Polynomial actions of
\GL_{r}
are completely reducible and all irreducible polynomial representations, called
Schur representations
, arise as direct summands of some
V^{\otimes d}
.
Recall that Schur representations are indexed by partitions
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
and denoted by
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}V
. The size of
\alpha
is
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{r}
and if
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}V\subset V^{\otimes d}
, then
|\alpha|=d
and we will say that
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}V
is a Schur representation of degree
d
.

With each polynomial representation
W
of
\GL_{r}
, one may associate its character

\Char(W)=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}^{r}}\dim W(\lambda)\,q^{\lambda}\in\mathbb{Z}[q_{1},\dots,q_{r}],

where
q^{\lambda}=q_{1}^{\lambda_{1}}\cdots q_{r}^{\lambda r}
.
It is immediate to see that

\Char(W_{1}\oplus W_{2})=\Char(W_{1})+\Char(W_{2})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Char(W_{1}\otimes W_{2})=\Char(W_{1})\cdot\Char(W_{2}).

The character of a polynomial representation is always a symmetric polynomial in
q_{1},\dots,q_{r}
, and the character of a Schur representation of degree
d
is homogeneous of degree
d
.

Example 1.1
.

Among Schur functors we have the symmetric powers
S^{k}V
and the exterior powers
\bigwedge^{k}V
. This defines three famous sets of symmetric polynomials:

•

elementary symmetric polynomials
\sigma_{k}=\Char(\bigwedge^{k}V)
;

•

complete symmetric polynomials
h_{k}=\Char(S^{k}V)
;

•

Schur polynomials
s_{\alpha}=\Char(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}V)
.

Finally, let us also recall that every action of
\GL_{r}
induces corresponding representations of the Lie algebra
\gl_{r}
and of its universal enveloping algebra
\mathfrak{U}(\gl_{r})
, which has a canonical cocommutative Hopf algebra structure where elements of
\gl_{r}
are all primitive. This will be used in Section
3
to study some special characters.

1.1.
The Grothendieck group of representations

Let
\Rep=\Rep(\GL_{r})
be the Grothendieck ring of polynomial representations of
\GL_{r}
, i.e. the abelian group of formal
\mathbb{Z}
-linear combinations of isomorphism classes of finite-dimensional polynomial representations of
\GL_{r}
endowed with the unique multiplication extending tensor product of representations. We also consider the
\mathbb{Q}
-linear extension of
\Rep
, i.e. the
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra
\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}=\Rep\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}
.
(Non-zero) Schur representations provide a free basis of both algebras.

One may extend
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly
\Char
to a
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra homomorphism

(1.1)

\Char\colon\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}\to\mathbb{Q}[q_{1},\dots,q_{r}]

which indeed defines an isomorphism between
\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}
and the sub-algebra of symmetric polynomials.

Moreover
\Char(U)
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
if and only if
U
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear combination of Schur representations of degree
d
. We thus endow
\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}
with the grading corresponding to the degree, i.e.

\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}=\bigoplus_{d\geq 0}\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}^{d},

thus making
\Char
into a homogeneous homomorphism.

It is a standard fact that elementary symmetric polynomials
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{r}
are
\mathbb{Z}
-algebra generators of the ring
\mathbb{Z}[q_{1},\dots,q_{r}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{r}}
of symmetric polynomials with integral coefficients, so that wedge powers
\wedge^{k}V
for
k=1,\dots,r
generate
\Rep
over
\mathbb{Z}
(hence also
\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}
over
\mathbb{Q}
). The same holds for symmetric powers
S^{k}V
for
k=1,\dots,r
, as complete homogeneous symmetric polynomials
h_{1},\dots,h_{r}
are also known to generate
\mathbb{Z}[q_{1},\dots,q_{r}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{r}}
. In particular, the symmetric power representations
S^{1}V,\dots,S^{r}V
are
\mathbb{Z}
-algebra generators of
\Rep
, and similarly
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra generators of
\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Remark 1.2
.

Each
S^{k}V
is a representation of degree
k
. The homogeneous component
\Rep^{d}
is generated by those monomials of symmetric power representations which have degree
d
.
To introduce a notation we will use later, with any
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{t})\in\mathbb{N}^{t}
we associate the representation
\Sym^{\alpha}V:=S^{\alpha_{1}}V\cdots S^{\alpha_{t}}V\in\Rep
. Then
\Rep^{d}
is
\mathbb{Z}
-linearly generated by all
\Sym^{\alpha}V
such that
|\alpha|=d
.

Remark 1.3
.

Power sums polynomials

(1.2)

p_{k}=q_{1}^{k}+\dots+q_{r}^{k},

for
k=1,\dots,r
, do not generate
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{r}]
but are
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra generators of
\mathbb{Q}[\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{r}]
. If we define
P_{k}:=\Char^{-1}(p_{k})\in\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}
, then the
P_{k}
, for
k=1,\dots,r
generate
\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}
as a
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra. As before, if we set
P_{\alpha}:=P_{\alpha_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot P_{\alpha_{t}}
then
\Rep^{d}
is
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly generated by all
P_{\alpha}
such that
|\alpha|=d
.

For instance,
P_{1}=V
and
P_{2}=S^{2}V-\wedge^{2}V
. In general
P_{k}
is not an honest representation of
\GL_{r}
: it is known that
P_{n}
is the alternating sum of all Schur
hook representations
1
1

1

i.e. those corresponding to a hook partition
(k,1,1,\dots,1,0,\dots,0)
.
of degree
n
, but we will not need this fact. Notice that
P_{0}
equals
r
times the trivial one-dimensional representation.

1.2.
Chern character of a representation

In analogy with bundles, we define now the Chern character
\ch(W)
of a polynomial representation
W
.

First of all, denote by

e\colon\mathbb{Q}[q_{1},\dots,q_{r}]\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}[[a_{1},\dots,a_{r}]]

the evaluation homomorphism obtained by setting
q_{i}\mapsto e^{a_{i}}\in\mathbb{Q}[[a_{i}]]\subset\mathbb{Q}[[a_{1},\dots,a_{r}]]
. The indeterminates
a_{1},\dots,a_{r}
are called
Chern roots
(of
V
).

Definition 1.4
.

The
Chern character
morphism is

\ch:=e\circ\Char\colon\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}\to\mathbb{Q}[[a_{1},\dots,a_{r}]].

Given
U\in\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}
, we group terms in
\ch(U)
by degree, so that

\ch(U)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\ch_{k}(U),

where
\ch_{k}(U)
is a homogeneous symmetric polynomial in
a_{1},\dots,a_{r}
of degree
k
.

Example 1.5
.

Let
P_{d}
be the elements defined in Remark
1.3
. By definition, we have

\ch_{k}(P_{d})=\frac{(da_{1})^{k}+\dots+(da_{r})^{k}}{k!}=d^{k}\frac{p_{k}(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})}{k!}.

Notice that in particular
\ch_{0}(P_{d})=r
for every
d\geq 1
.
As a consequence,

(1.3)

\ch_{k}(P_{d})=d^{k}\ch_{k}(P_{1}).

More in general, if
\mathsf{c}=\ch_{k_{1}}\dots\ch_{k_{s}}
, then

\mathsf{c}(P_{d})=d^{k_{1}+\dots+k_{s}}\mathsf{c}(P_{1}).

Example 1.6

(Svrtan)
.

In
[
Svr93
]
, closed formulas are given for
\ch(S^{m}V)
and
\ch(\bigwedge^{m}V)
(see Sections
B.1
and
B.2
). The statements of such formulas are in terms of Chern characters of vector bundles, but the proofs are purely combinatorial and based on the splitting principle, so that they are indeed set in this algebraic framework.

As an example, let us state here the formula for the symmetric power (
[
Svr93
, Theorem 4.8]
):

(1.4)

\ch(S^{m}V)=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{1}{\lVert\alpha\rVert}\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha}\binom{m+r-1}{m-|\beta|}\overleftarrow{\beta}!S(\alpha,\beta)\ch_{\alpha}(V).

The first summation is taken over all partitions
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{t})
such that
\alpha_{i}\neq 0
for every
i=1,\dots,t
, and
\ch_{\alpha}(\tilde{V})
stands for the product
\prod_{i}\ch_{\alpha_{i}}(V)
. The norm
\lVert\alpha\rVert
is the product
a_{1}!a_{2}!\cdots a_{t}!
, where
a_{i}=\sharp\{j\mid\alpha_{j}=i\}
. The second summation is taken over all
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
such that
\beta\leq\alpha
and
\ell(\beta)=\ell(\alpha)
(in particular also
\beta_{i}\neq 0
for every
i
). The symbol
\overleftarrow{\beta}
stands for the partition
(\beta_{1}-1,\dots,\beta_{t}-1)
and
\overleftarrow{\beta}!=\prod_{i}(\beta_{i}-1)!
.
Finally,
S(\alpha,\beta)=\prod_{i}S(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
, where
S(x,y)
is the Stirling number of the second kind.

Remark 1.7
.

If
U
is a representation of
\GL_{r}
, then

\ch_{0}(U)=\dim U.

The same follows when
U\in\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}
(alternatively one can define the dimension of
U
in this way).

Finally, let us remark that for any
U_{1},U_{2}\in\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}
we have

(1.5)

\ch(U_{1}+U_{2})=\ch(U_{1})+\ch(U_{2})\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\ch(U_{1}\cdot U_{2})=\ch(U_{1})\ch(U_{2}).

2.
Discriminants

2.1.
Slope

From (
1.5
) we obtain additivity

\ch_{1}(U_{1}+U_{2})=\ch_{1}(U_{1})+\ch_{1}(U_{2})

and the equality

\ch_{1}(U_{1}\cdot U_{2})=\ch_{0}(U_{2})\ch_{1}(U_{1})+\ch_{0}(U_{1})\ch_{1}(U_{2}),

that we call
log-multiplicativity
as it may be rephrased as

(2.1)

\frac{\ch_{1}}{\ch_{0}}(U_{1}\cdot U_{2})=\frac{\ch_{1}}{\ch_{0}}(U_{1})+\frac{\ch_{1}}{\ch_{0}}(U_{2}).

Proposition 2.1
.

If
U\in\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
, then

\frac{\ch_{1}}{\ch_{0}}(U)=d\,\frac{\ch_{1}}{\ch_{0}}(V).

Proof.

By Example
1.5
, we already know that
\ch_{1}(P_{d})=d\ch_{1}(P_{1})
and
\ch_{0}(P_{d})=r=\ch_{0}(P_{1})
, thus showing the claim when
U=P_{d}
(recall that
P_{1}=V
).

If now
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{t})
with
|\alpha|=d
, then by equality (
2.1
) it also follows that

(2.2)

\frac{\ch_{1}}{\ch_{0}}(P_{\alpha})=d\,\frac{\ch_{1}}{\ch_{0}}(P_{1}),

where
P_{\alpha}=P_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots P_{\alpha_{t}}
.
The statement then follows from the fact that the
P_{\alpha}
’s, for
|\alpha|=d
, are
\mathbb{Q}
-linear generators of
\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
.
∎

The ratio
\frac{\ch_{1}}{\ch_{0}}
is known as
slope
, and Proposition
2.1
shows in particular that the slope of each Schur representation
S^{\alpha}V
is
|\alpha|
times the slope of
V
. Therefore we see that
\Rep
is (essentially) graded by the slope.

2.2.
Logarithmic Chern character

What makes Proposition
2.1
to work is the logarithmic equality (
2.1
). In order to extend it to higher degrees, let us then introduce the logarithmic Chern character.

Definition 2.2
.

For any
U\in\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}
, define the
logarithmic Chern character
as

\Log_{+}(U)=\log\frac{\ch(U)}{\ch_{0}(U)}=\log\left(1+\frac{\ch_{1}(U)}{\ch_{0}(U)}+\frac{\ch_{2}(U)}{\ch_{0}(U)}+\cdots\right)=\sum_{k>0}(-1)^{k+1}\frac{\Delta_{k}(U)}{k\ch_{0}(U)^{k}}.

As an example, let us expand the first values of
\Delta_{k}
:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta_{1}(U)=&\ch_{1}(U)\\
\Delta_{2}(U)=&\ch_{1}(U)^{2}-2\ch_{0}(U)\ch_{2}(U)\\
\Delta_{3}(U)=&\ch_{1}(U)^{3}-3\ch_{0}(U)\ch_{1}(U)\ch_{2}(U)+3\ch_{0}(U)^{2}\ch_{3}(U)\,.\end{split}

The multiplicativity of
\ch
translates into
log-multiplicativity
of
\Log_{+}
,

(2.4)

\Log_{+}(U_{1}\cdot U_{2})=\Log_{+}(U_{1})+\Log_{+}(U_{2})\,.

Before continuing towards the main result of the section, let us state another useful definition.

Definition 2.3
.

For any
U\in\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}
, define

\dd_{k}=\frac{\Delta_{k}(U)}{k\ch_{0}(U)^{k-1}}.

Equality (
2.4
) translates in

(2.5)

\frac{\dd_{k}}{\ch_{0}}(U_{1}\cdot U_{2})=\frac{\dd_{k}}{\ch_{0}}(U_{1})+\frac{\dd_{k}}{\ch_{0}}(U_{2})

or, equivalently, in

(2.6)

\dd_{k}(U_{1}\cdot U_{2})=\ch_{0}(U_{2})\dd_{k}(U_{1})+\ch_{0}(U_{1})\dd_{k}(U_{2}).

Moreover,
\dd_{1}(U)=\ch_{1}(U)
is linear with respect to the sum operation. On the other hand, it is easy to see that in general
\dd_{k}
is not additive for
k\geq 2
(see Example
2.6
).

However, a
weak additivity
holds for
\dd_{2},\dd_{3}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Fix
d\geq 0
. If
U_{1},U_{2}\in\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
, then

\dd_{k}(U_{1}+U_{2})=\dd_{k}(U_{1})+\dd_{k}(U_{2})

for
k=2,3
.

Proof.

As

\Log_{+}=\sum_{k>0}\frac{\dd_{k}}{\ch_{0}}

satisfies

\ch_{0}\cdot\exp(\Log_{+})=\ch
, then each coefficient of

(2.7)

\ch_{0}\cdot\exp(\Log_{+})=\ch_{0}\cdot\left(1+\frac{\dd_{1}}{\ch_{0}}+\left(\frac{\dd_{2}}{\ch_{0}}+\frac{\dd_{1}^{2}}{2\ch_{0}^{2}}\right)+\left(\frac{\dd_{3}}{\ch_{0}}+\frac{\dd_{1}\dd_{2}}{\ch_{0}^{2}}+\frac{\dd_{1}^{3}}{6\ch_{0}^{3}}\right)+\dots\right)

must be additive. In particular, both

\dd_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\dd_{1}}{\ch_{0}}\cdot\dd_{1}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\dd_{3}+\frac{\dd_{1}}{\ch_{0}}\cdot\dd_{2}+\left(\frac{\dd_{1}}{\ch_{0}}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{\dd_{1}}{6}

are additive on
\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

However, when restricting to
\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
, the first expression equals
\dd_{2}+d/2\cdot\dd_{1}
(see Proposition
2.1
); as
\dd_{1}=\ch_{1}
is additive, then also
\dd_{2}
must be additive.

Similarly, on
\Rep^{d}_{\mathbb{Q}}
the second expression equals
\dd_{3}+d\cdot\dd_{2}+d^{2}/6\cdot\dd_{1}
and we already know that
\dd_{1}
and
\dd_{2}
are additive on
\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
. Thus
\dd_{3}
must also be additive and the proof is concluded.
∎

Recall that additivity on a rational vector space, such as
\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
, is equivalent to
\mathbb{Q}
-linearity.

Remark 2.5
.

Proposition
2.4
stops to work from degree
4
on: it is not difficult to find counter-examples. We digress a bit more about higher discriminants in Section
2.3
.

Example 2.6
.

Proposition
2.4
is sharp: it is easy to find a counter-example to additivity. Let us take
r=2
for simplicity. Then by Example
1.6
(see also Section
B.1
) we have that

\ch(S^{2}V)=(3,3\ch_{1}(V),\frac{1}{2}\ch_{1}(V)^{2}+4\ch_{2}(V),\cdots),

so that in particular
\dd_{2}(S^{2}V)=\dd_{k}(V)
.

Therefore, by additivity of Chern character, we have

\ch(V\oplus S^{2}V)=(5,4\ch_{1}(V),\frac{1}{2}\ch_{1}(V)^{2}+5\ch_{2}(V),\cdots).

By an explicit computation,

\dd_{2}(V\oplus S^{2}V)=5\ch_{2}(V)-\frac{11}{10}\ch_{1}(V)^{2}\neq 8\ch_{2}(V)-2\ch_{1}(V)^{2}=\dd_{2}(V)+\dd_{2}(S^{2}V).

Our main result is the following generalisation of Proposition
2.1
.

Theorem 2.7
.

If
U\in\Rep^{d}_{\mathbb{Q}}
, then for
k=2,3

\dd_{k}(U)=f^{(k)}_{U}(r)\dd_{k}(V)

for opportunely chosen polynomials
f^{(k)}_{U}\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
of degree at most
d-1
.

Proof.

By Example

Example 2.8
.

We have
\frac{\dd_{2}}{\ch_{0}}(P_{2})=4\frac{\dd_{2}}{\ch_{0}}(V)
. As
\ch_{0}(V)=\ch_{0}(P_{2})

3.1.
Universal enveloping algebra and Casimir elements

We refer to
[
Hum73
]
for the background about Lie algebras and their universal enveloping algebras.

If
\mathfrak{g}
is a Lie algebra, we denote by
\mathfrak{U}(\mathfrak{g})
its universal enveloping algebra.
Recall that
\mathfrak{U}(\mathfrak{g})
is constructed by quotienting the tensor algebra
T(\mathfrak{g})
via the bracket relations. This endows
\mathfrak{U}(\mathfrak{g})
both with a natural filtration

\mathfrak{U}_{0}(\mathfrak{g})\subset\mathfrak{U}_{1}(\mathfrak{g})\subset\mathfrak{U}_{2}(\mathfrak{g})\subset\cdots

and with a natural structure of Hopf algebra,

\epsilon\colon\mathfrak{U}(\mathfrak{g})\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\Delta\colon\mathfrak{U}(\mathfrak{g})\longrightarrow\mathfrak{U}(\mathfrak{g})\otimes\mathfrak{U}(\mathfrak{g}).

Elements in
\mathfrak{U}_{m}(\mathfrak{g})/\mathfrak{U}_{m-1}(\mathfrak{g})
correspond, via the Poincaré-Birkhoff–Witt Theorem, to symmetric
m
-tensors, and the maps
\epsilon
and
\Delta
are the counit and coproduct maps, respectively.
The coproduct
\Delta
describes the action on the tensor product of representations.

Our first remark is that the trace of the action of certain elements of
\mathfrak{U}(\ssl_{r})
satisfies additivity and log-multiplicativity identities.

If
C\in\mathfrak{U}(\gl_{r})
and
W
is a
\gl_{r}
-representation, then we denote by
\Tr_{W}C
the trace of the action on
C
on
W
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
C\in\mathfrak{U}(\ssl_{r})\subset\mathfrak{U}(\gl_{r})
be an element such that
C\in\ker(\epsilon)\cap\mathfrak{U}_{3}(\ssl_{r})
.

For every
U_{1},U_{2}\in\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}
, the trace of the action of
C
satisfies

\Tr_{U_{1}+U_{2}}C=\Tr_{U_{1}}C+\Tr_{U_{2}}C\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\Tr_{U_{1}\cdot U_{2}}C=\Tr_{U_{1}}C\cdot\dim(U_{2})+\dim(U_{1})\cdot\Tr_{U_{2}}C.

Proof.

Additivity always holds.
Let us then look at log-multiplicativity.

First of all, let us notice that if
C
has degree
1
, i.e.
C\in\ssl_{r}\subset\mathfrak{U}(\ssl_{r})
, then it is primitive. Primitive here means that
\Delta(C)=C\otimes 1+1\otimes C
, so that the claim is straightforward.

Let us now assume that
C\in\ker(\epsilon)\cap\mathfrak{U}_{2}(\ssl_{r})
is quadratic. Let
U_{1},U_{2}
be polynomial representations of
\GL_{r}
. If
C
is of the form
C=\sum_{i}x_{i}y_{i}
, where
x_{i},y_{i}\in\ssl_{r}
, then

\Delta(C)=C\otimes 1+1\otimes C+\sum_{i}(x_{i}\otimes y_{i}+y_{i}\otimes x_{i}).

As a consequence, the trace of the action of
C
on
U_{1}\otimes U_{2}
, which is prescribed by
\Delta(C)
, equals the trace of
C\otimes 1+1\otimes C
, as
\Tr(x_{i})=\Tr(y_{i})=0
since every element in
\ssl_{r}
is a commutator. However
\Tr_{U_{1}\otimes U_{2}}(C\otimes 1)=(\Tr_{U_{1}}C)\cdot\dim(U_{2})
and similarly
\Tr_{U_{1}\otimes U_{2}}(1\otimes C)=\dim(U_{1})\cdot(\Tr_{U_{2}}C)
. We thus obtain log-multiplicativity.

A similar proof works for cubic elements.
∎

\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}V
we have

\phi^{(2)}_{\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}V}(r)=\frac{\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}(\alpha)}{(r-1)(r+1)}\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\phi^{(3)}_{\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}V}(r)=\frac{\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(3)}(\alpha)}{(r-2)(r-1)(r+1)(r+2)}\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}.

Proof.

First of all, by the proof of Lemma
3.8
and by a direct check using Lemma
3.11
, it follows that both equalities hold for symmetric representations
S^{m}V
.

Now, by Lemma
3.2
(which can be applied because of Lemma
3.12
), both
\phi^{(k)}_{\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}V}(r)
and the functions
\frac{\dot{\delta}_{2}(\alpha)}{(r-1)(r+1)}\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}
and
\frac{\dot{\delta}_{3}(\alpha)}{(r-2)(r-1)(r+1)(r+2)}\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}
are additive and log-multiplicative. Therefore they agree on
\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}
if and only if they agree on a set of generators. Since symmetric representations generate
\Rep_{\mathbb{Q}}
, the claim follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

The proof follows at once from Proposition
3.7
and Proposition
3.13
.
∎

Remark 3.14
.

Let us consider the central element
I=\sum E_{ii}\in\mathfrak{U}(\gl_{r})
of degree
1
. It is not an element in
\mathfrak{U}(\ssl_{r})
, but by Remark
3.3
the proof of Lemma
3.2
continues to work for it. Therefore if we define

\phi_{U}^{(1)}(r)=\Tr_{U}\left(\frac{1}{r}I\right),

then
\phi^{(1)}(r)
is additive and log-multiplicative. Explicitly we have

\phi_{\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}V}^{(1)}(r)=|\alpha|\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}.

In this way we understand Proposition
2.1
as a Casimir action. In fact, it is this observation that led us to think at the characters
f^{(k)}(r)
as Casimir actions for
k=2
and
3
.

Remark 3.15
.

The function

f^{(2)}_{S^{m}V}(r)\cdot\left(\frac{r_{m}}{r}\right)=\frac{m(m+r)}{(r+1)}\left(\frac{r_{m}}{r}\right)^{2}

counts the dimension of the representation
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{m-1,m-1}V_{r+2}
, as it can be directly seen from Weyl’s formula. Here
V_{r+2}
is a vector space of dimension
r+2
.

Equivalently, it counts the number of standard tableaux of size
(m-1,m-1)
for
\textrm{Mat}(2,r+2)
. These form a basis for the degree
m-1
component of the homogeneous coordinate ring of the Grassmannian
\Gr(2,r+2)
(see
[
Muk03
, Lemma 8.14, Remark 8.16]
), i.e.

f(m)=h^{0}(\Gr(2,r+2),\mathcal{O}(m-1)).

We point out that
f^{(2)}_{S^{m}V}(r)
has another interesting Lie-theoretical connection when
r=4
, being the dimension of
V^{(k)}
, the distinguished module in the Severi variety with
a=4
in
[
LM06
, Theorem 7.3]
. We have not explored a connection between these two facts yet.

On the other hand, for
f^{(3)}_{S^{m}V}(r)
we haven’t found an interpretation valid for every
r
. However, for
r=3
, we can see that
\frac{10}{3}\cdot f^{(3)}_{S^{m}V}(3)
is the dimension of the Cartan power
\mathfrak{g}^{(m)}
for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{so}_{8}
, see
[
LM06
, Theorem 7.1]
.

We do not have an explanation for such phenomenon, but a natural question is to understand if the polynomials appearing in Theorem
3.1
admit in general a geometrical interpretation.

4.
Geometric applications

4.1.
Schur functors of locally free sheaves

In this section
X
is a compact complex manifold (or a proper smooth algebraic variety over
\mathbb{C}
).

Let
E
be a vector bundle of rank
r
on
X
. Define the following characteristic classes:

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}(E)=

\displaystyle\cc_{1}(E)

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}(E)=

\displaystyle\cc_{1}(E)^{2}-2r\ch_{2}(E)

\displaystyle\Delta_{3}(E)=

\displaystyle\cc_{1}(E)^{3}-3r\cc_{1}(E)\ch_{2}(E)+3r^{2}\ch_{3}(E).

Remark 4.1
.

These classes are the same as defined in (
2.3
). We refer to
[
Dre95
,
Ful20
]
for other appearances of logarithmic Chern classes.

For any partition
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
, we denote by
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E
the
Schur bundle
associated to
E
. It is the vector bundle of rank
r_{\alpha}
such that

(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)_{x}=\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E_{x}\qquad\forall x\in X.

As a consequence of the results in the previous section, we get the following statement.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
X
and
E
be as above. Then

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)=

\displaystyle\,|\alpha|\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\Delta_{1}(E)

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)=

\displaystyle\,\frac{\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}(\alpha)}{(r-1)(r+1)}\left(\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\right)^{2}\Delta_{2}(E)

\displaystyle\Delta_{3}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)=

\displaystyle\,\frac{\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(3)}(\alpha)}{(r-2)(r-1)(r+1)(r+2)}\left(\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\right)^{3}\Delta_{2}(E)\,,

where
\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}(\alpha)
and
\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(3)}(\alpha)
are as in Theorem
3.1
.

Proof.

Let us assume that
E=L_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus L_{r}
, where the
L_{i}
’s are line bundle with
\cc(L_{i})=a_{i}
, i.e. the
a_{i}
’s are the Chern roots of
E
. Then by definition

\ch(E)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}e^{a_{i}},

which is the same expression as in Definition
1.4
.
In particular every expression involving the Chern character of
E
and
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E
is equivalent to the corresponding expression for the vector spaces
E_{x}
and
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E_{x}
, for any point
x\in X
.

The claim then follows at once from Proposition
2.1
, Theorem
2.7
and Theorem
3.1
.

If
E
is any vector bundle, then by the splitting principle we can formally reduce to the case above, thus concluding the proof.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

The case
\Delta_{1}=\cc_{1}
was already proved in
[
Rub13
]
. Rubei’s proof uses a double-induction argument to reduce to the symmetric case, where the result is easy to prove. Also our proof is deduced from the symmetric case, but our reduction argument is not inductive and is instead based on representation-theoretic arguments.

Remark 4.4
.

The expressions in Theorem
4.2
can be used to deduce closed expressions for the first three Chern characters of a Schur functor, see Section
B.3
. Similar expressions have been investigated in
[
Iso15
, Section 4]
. More precisely, in
[
Iso15
, Theorem 4.3]
there is an asymptotic version of the expression for
\ch_{2}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)
under some (strong) assumptions on the partition
\alpha
.

Remark 4.5
.

It is interesting to notice that for any partition
\alpha
, the polynomial
\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}(\alpha)
is non-negative. It follows that if
X
is a Kähler manifold of dimension
N
, then

\Delta_{2}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E).\omega^{N-2}\geq 0\qquad\iff\qquad\Delta_{2}(E).\omega^{N-2}\geq 0.

Here
\omega
is a Kähler class on
X
.

The comment above should be read with an eye on the Bogomolov inequality, which says that if a vector bundle
E
is slope
\omega
-semistable on a compact Kähler manifold, then
\Delta_{2}(E).\omega^{N-2}\geq 0
. The vice versa is not true, but it is known that we can still deduce polystability of
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E
from that of
E
. We state here the following known result for lack of a better reference.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
X
be a compact Kähler manifold,
\omega
a Kähler class and
E
a slope
\omega
-polystable vector bundle on
X
. Then for any partition
\alpha
the Schur functor
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E
is slope
\omega
-polystable.

Proof.

If we put
\ell=|\alpha|
, then
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E\subset E^{\otimes\ell}
as a direct summand. It follows from the Donaldson–Uhlenbeck–Yau Theorem (see for example
[
LT95
]
) that
E^{\otimes\ell}
is slope
\omega
-polystable, so that
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E
is also
\omega
-polystable.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

In Proposition
4.6
, even if
E
is slope stable, the polystability of
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E
cannot in general be improved. As an example, consider
E=S^{2}Q
, where
Q
is the quotient tautological bundle on a grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(k,n)
. Then
E
is slope stable, but
S^{2}E=S^{2}(S^{2}Q))
is not indecomposable, hence it is strictly polystable.
On the other hand, indecomposability of
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E
is the only obstruction to stability: if
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E
is indecomposable, then it is stable.

4.2.
Modular bundles on hyper-Kähler manifolds

Let
X
be a compact hyper-Kähler manifold. Recall that the second integral cohomology group
\oH^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})
is torsion free and endowed with a non-degenerate quadratic form
\mathsf{q}_{X}
. With an abuse of notation, we keep denoting by
\mathsf{q}_{X}

the class in
\oH^{4}(X,\mathbb{Q})
dual to this quadratic form.

We denote by
\operatorname{SH}(X)\subset\oH(X,\mathbb{Q})
the Verbitsky component, namely the submodule generated by
\oH^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})
via cup product. Notice that
\mathsf{q}_{X}\in\operatorname{SH}^{4}(X)
.

Definition 4.8

(
[
O’Gr22
, Definition 1.1]
)
.

A vector bundle
E
on
X
is called
modular
if the projection of
\Delta_{2}(E)
onto
\operatorname{SH}^{4}(X)
is a multiple of
\mathsf{q}_{X}
.

As a corollary of Theorem
4.2
we obtain the following result that allows to construct new modular bundles on hyper-Kähler manifolds from old ones.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
X
be a compact hyper-Kähler manifold. If
E
is a slope polystable modular vector bundle, then
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E
is a slope polystable modular vector bundle.

Proof.

The slope polystability follows from Proposition
4.6
. The modularity follows from Theorem
4.2
: in fact the projection into
\operatorname{SH}^{4}(X)
of
\Delta_{2}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)
is a multiple of the projection of
\Delta_{2}(E)
, which is a multiple of
\mathsf{q}_{X}
by hypothesis.
∎

Example 4.10
.

Let
S
be a K3 surface, i.e. a hyper-Kähler surface, and let
E
be a stable vector bundle with Mukai vector
v(E)=(r,\cc,s)
, where
r
is the rank of
E
. By dimension reasons, the bundle
E
is modular.

For the same dimension reasons, any Schur functor
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E
is also modular. The Mukai vector of
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E
is

v(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)=\left(r_{\alpha},|\alpha|\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\cc,\frac{(|\alpha|^{2}-\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}(\alpha))}{r}\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\frac{\cc^{2}}{2}+\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}(\alpha)(s-r)\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}+r_{\alpha}\right),

where we have used the same notations as in Section
B.3
, so that
\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}(\alpha)=\frac{\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}(\alpha)}{(r-1)(r+1)}
.

The Mukai vector
v(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)\in\oH^{*}(S,\mathbb{Z})
can be primitive or not depending on
\alpha
,
r
and the bundle
E
itself. For example, let us suppose that
\operatorname{Pic}(S)=\mathbb{Z}\cdot H
, where
H
is ample and
H^{2}=2d\geq 6
. Then there exists a
H
-stable vector bundle
E
of rank
2
with
\cc_{1}(E)=H
and
\ch_{2}(E)=0
. Such a vector bundle has a primitive Mukai vector
v(E)=(2,H,2)
. The symmetric power
S^{2}E
has Mukai vector

v(S^{2}E)=(3,3H,d+3),

therefore it is primitive for all
d\nmid 3
, but it is not primitive for
d\in 3\mathbb{Z}
.

Example 4.11
.

Examples of stable modular vector bundles on hyper-Kähler fourfolds of type
\operatorname{K3}^{[2]}
have been constructed in
[
O’Gr24
,
Bot24
,
Fat24
,
FO24
,
FT25
]
. By Theorem
4.9
, each of these examples gives rise to many more (polystable) examples.

Appendix A
Discriminants for low-rank bundles

Let us consider the logarithmic classes defined in (
2.3
). As already remarked in the introduction, these logarithmic classes
\Delta_{k}(F)
for a coherent sheaf
F
are linked to the the stability of
F
itself. This is well known for
k=1,2
, and there are evidences starting to build up for
k=3
, see
[
Ful20
]
.
It is therefore interesting to study not only the first logarithmic classes, but also the higher-degree terms.
As a first observation, notice that
all
logarithmic classes behave well with respect to twist with line bundles: for example (see
[
Dre95
, Proposition 2.1]
) we have for
E
a vector bundle and
L
a line bundle

\Delta_{k}(E\otimes L)=\Delta_{k}(E),\ k\geq 2

The first three logarithmic classes satisfies another nice property.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
E
be a locally free sheaf of rank
r<k
, for
k=1,2,3
. Then
\Delta_{k}(E)=0
.

Proof.

For
k=1
the claim is trivially true. For
k=2,3
this is not evident from the definition. However, if we write
\Delta_{k}
in terms of the Chern classes, we find that

\Delta_{2}(E)=-(r-1)\cc_{1}^{2}(E)+2r\cc_{2}(E)

and

\Delta_{3}(E)=\frac{1}{2}\left((r-1)(r-2)\cc_{1}(E)^{3}-3r(r-2)\cc_{1}(E)\cc_{2}(E)+3r^{2}\cc_{3}(E)\right).

The result then follows by simply noting that
\cc_{i}(E)=0
for
i>r
.
∎

The above result does not hold for
k\geq 4
, highlighting another difference between lower and higher degree discriminants. In fact,
\Delta_{4}(E)
is already not zero on rank 2 bundles with
\cc_{2}\neq 0
. However, it is possible to find suitable modifications for both
\Delta_{4}
and
\Delta_{5}
that behave well with respect to this property. For this, let us expand the degree
k=4,5
the expression in Definition
2.2
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{4}(E)=

\displaystyle\,\ch_{1}(E)^{4}-4r\ch_{1}(E)^{2}\ch_{2}(E)+2r^{2}(\ch_{2}(E)^{2}+2\ch_{1}(E)\ch_{3}(E))-4r^{3}\ch_{4}(E)

\displaystyle\Delta_{5}(E)=

\displaystyle\,\ch_{1}(E)^{5}-5r\ch_{1}(E)^{3}\ch_{2}(E)+5r^{2}\ch_{1}(E)(\ch_{2}(E)^{2}+\ch_{1}(E)\ch_{3}(E))

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\,5r^{3}(\ch_{2}(E)\ch_{3}(E)+\ch_{1}(E)\ch_{4}(E))+5r^{4}\ch_{5}(E)r^{4}\,.

We have the following result.

Proposition A.2
.

Let
E
be a locally free sheaf of rank
r
and let us consider the two modified classes

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}_{4}(E)=

\displaystyle\,(r+1)\Delta_{4}(E)-\Delta_{2}(E)^{2}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}_{5}(E)=

\displaystyle\,(r+5)\Delta_{5}(E)-5\Delta_{2}(E)\Delta_{3}(E).

Then
\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}(E)=0
for
r<k
and
k=4,5
.

Proof.

Once again, it is enough to expand the two classes in term of the Chern classes. We have:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}_{4}=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{6}r(-\cc_{1}^{4}(-3+r)(-2+r)(-1+r)+4\cc_{1}^{2}\cc_{2}(-3+r)(-2+r)r

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\,2\cc_{2}^{2}(-3+r)(-2+r)r-4\cc_{1}\cc_{3}(-3+r)r(1+r)+4\cc_{4}r^{2}(1+r))

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}_{5}=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{24}r(\cc_{1}^{5}(-4+r)(-3+r)(-2+r)(-1+r)-5\cc_{1}^{3}\cc_{2}(-4+r)(-3+r)(-2+r)r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\,5\cc_{1}^{2}\cc_{3}(-4+r)(-3+r)r(2+r)+5\cc_{1}(-4+r)r(\cc_{2}^{2}(-3+r)(-2+r)-\cc_{4}r(5+r))

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\,5r^{2}(-\cc_{2}\cc_{3}(-4+r)(-3+r)+\cc_{5}r(5+r))).

The result then follows.
∎

One can of course try to generalise this to higher discriminants, but we could not find any rigid pattern. It would be anyway very interesting to have an explicit expression for classes
\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}
, for any
k\geq 1
, with the property that
\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}(E)=0
for every locally free sheaf
E
of rank
r<k
.

Explicit formulas

In this appendix we collect some explicit formulas for the computation of Chern classes of Schur bundles. Sections
B.1
and
B.2
follows from
[
Svr93
]
, while Section
B.3
is a consequence of Theorem
4.2
.

From now on
E
is a locally free sheaf of rank
r
,
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
a partition of size
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{r}
and
r_{\alpha}
the rank of the Schur bundle
\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E
.

\cc_{1}:=\cc_{1}(E)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\ch_{i}:=\ch_{i}(E).

The symmetric bundle

The following is an expansion of
[
Svr93

1.4
).

\displaystyle\rk(S^{m}E)=

\displaystyle\,\,m\frac{r_{m}}{r}\cc_{1}

\displaystyle\ch_{2}(S^{m}E)=

\displaystyle\,\,\frac{1}{2}\frac{(m-1)m}{r+1}\frac{r_{m}}{r}\cc_{1}^{2}+\frac{m(m+r)}{r+1}\frac{r_{m}}{r}\ch_{2}

\displaystyle\,\,\frac{1}{6}\frac{(m-2)(m-1)m}{(r+1)(r+2)}\frac{r_{m}}{r}\cc_{1}^{3}+\frac{(m-1)m(m+1)}{(r+1)(r+2)}\frac{r_{m}}{r}\cc_{1}\ch_{2}+\frac{m(m+r)(2m+r)}{(r+1)(r+2)}\frac{r_{m}}{r}\ch_{3}

\displaystyle\,\,\frac{m(m+r)}{r+1}\left(\frac{r_{m}}{r}\right)^{2}\Delta_{2}(E)

\displaystyle\Delta_{3}(S^{m}E)=

\displaystyle\,\,\frac{m(m+r)(2m+r)}{(r+1)(r+2)}\left(\frac{r_{m}}{r}\right)^{3}\Delta_{3}(E)

The exterior bundle

The following is an expansion of

.

\displaystyle\rk(\bigwedge^{n}E)=

\displaystyle\,\ n\frac{r_{n}}{r}\cc_{1}

\displaystyle\ch_{2}(\bigwedge^{n}E)=

\displaystyle\,\,\frac{1}{2}\frac{(n-1)n}{r-1}\frac{r_{n}}{r}\cc_{1}^{2}+\frac{n(r-n)}{r-1}\frac{r_{n}}{r}\ch_{2}

\displaystyle\,\,\frac{n(2n^{2}-3nr+r^{2})}{(r-2)(r-1)}\frac{r_{n}}{r}\Delta_{3}(E)

B.3.
The Schur bundles

The functions
\dot{\delta}_{2}(\alpha,r)
and
\dot{\delta}_{3}(\alpha,r)
are the same as defined in Theorem
3.1
. For sake of readability, let us introduce this notation:

\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}:=\frac{\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}(\alpha)}{(r-1)(r+1)}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{(3)}:=\frac{\dot{\delta}_{r}^{(3)}(\alpha)}{(r-2)(r-1)(r+1)(r+2)}.

\displaystyle\rk(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)=

\displaystyle\,\,r_{\alpha}:=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq r}\frac{\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}+j-i}{j-i}

\displaystyle\cc_{1}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)=

\displaystyle\,\,|\alpha|\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\cc_{1}

\displaystyle\ch_{2}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)=

\displaystyle\,\,\frac{1}{2r}\left(|\alpha|^{2}-\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}\right)\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\cc_{1}^{2}+\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\ch_{2}

\displaystyle\ch_{3}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)=

\displaystyle\,\,\frac{1}{6r^{2}}\left(|\alpha|^{3}-3|\alpha|\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}+2\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{(3)}\right)\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\cc_{1}^{3}+\frac{1}{r}\left(|\alpha|\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}-\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{(3)}\right)\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\cc_{1}\ch_{2}+\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{(3)}\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\ch_{3}

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)=

\displaystyle\,\,\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{(2)}\left(\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\right)^{2}\Delta_{2}(E)

\displaystyle\Delta_{3}(\mathsf{\mathbf{S}}^{\alpha}E)=

\displaystyle\,\,\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{(3)}\left(\frac{r_{\alpha}}{r}\right)^{3}\Delta_{3}(E)
