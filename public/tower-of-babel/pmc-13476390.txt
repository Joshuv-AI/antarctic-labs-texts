G6PD activity patterns and a stable adjusted male median derived from 16 years of clinical laboratory data
Authors: Muhammad Shariq Shaikh, Zeeshan Ansar Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeGlucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, the most common enzymopathy worldwide, poses significant clinical and public health challenges, particularly in malaria-endemic regions. Despite the importance of quantitative G6PD activity assessments, clinically meaningful deficiency thresholds remain difficult to define owing to biological variability related to age, sex, and X-linked inheritance. The adjusted male median (AMM) is a recommended framework for defining 100% activity and deriving assay- and region-specific thresholds. However, large-scale evaluations of AMM stability and G6PD activity patterns in Pakistan are limited.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed all quantitative G6PD assays performed at a national reference laboratory in Pakistan between January 2009 and December 2024. Records with valid G6PD activity (U/g hemoglobin (Hb)), age, and sex information were included. Age- and sex-stratified distributions were examined across predefined clinical age groups. The calculated AMM was based on adult males aged 18–45 years (excluding those with ≤ 10% of the initial median). Sensitivity analyses were used to assess AMM stability across alternative adult male age windows (15–50 and 18–60 years). The G6PD activity categories were defined as severe (< 10%), moderate (10–30%), intermediate (30–60%), and normal (> 60%) relative to the AMM.ResultsIn total, 51,748 specimens (41,397 from males and 10,351 from females) were analyzed. The median (interquartile range) G6PD activity was 10.3 (7.8–12.6) U/g Hb among males and 10.7 (8.5–12.9) U/g Hb among females. The AMM derived from 7,436 adult males was 9.2 U/g Hb and remained highly stable across alternative age windows (9.2–9.3 U/g Hb; ~ 1% variation). Based on AMM thresholds, 15.4% (95% CI: 15.1–15.7%) of the tested population had < 60% activity, including 6.8% with severe and 4.9% with moderate deficiency. G6PD activity was highest in neonates and infants and declined progressively with age.ConclusionsThe locally derived AMM provides a robust reference standard that reflects the enzymatic capacity within the specific clinical catchment area and serves as a foundation for future clinical validation studies. Age- and sex-specific activity patterns aligned with known physiological and genetic determinants. Although this study provided a locally validated laboratory reference framework, prospective studies correlating the enzymatic categories with clinical outcomes and genotypic data are needed before its broader clinical or public health implementation.Keywords: Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, Adjusted male median, G6PD activity, Reference intervals, Pakistan

Introduction

Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency is the most prevalent enzymopathy worldwide, affecting an estimated 400 million individuals and exhibiting substantial geographic variation driven by historical and prehistorical selective pressure from malaria [1]. As the key rate-limiting enzyme in the pentose phosphate pathway, G6PD maintains red cell integrity by reducing nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) production. Its deficiency predisposes individuals to hemolysis in the presence of various oxidative triggers, including infections, medications, and certain foods. Therefore, accurate enzymatic assessment is essential for clinical management of the disorder, newborn screening, blood transfusion safety, and safe administration of antimalarial agents such as primaquine and tafenoquine [2].

Currently, quantitative spectrophotometry is the laboratory gold standard method for measuring G6PD activity. The classification of G6PD deficiency is generally more straightforward in males because of their hemizygous state, whereas interpretation remains challenging in females owing to X chromosome inactivation and the resulting mosaic enzyme expression levels. Additionally, biological factors such as reticulocytosis can influence enzyme activity measurements in both sexes [3]. Traditional quantitation approaches rely on manufacturer-provided reference intervals or fixed international cutoffs. However, these may not reflect local population variations or assay-specific performance. To address this issue, Domingo et al. [4] recommended using the adjusted male median (AMM) as a reference for defining 100% activity and deriving region-specific deficiency thresholds. Although a recent World Health Organization (WHO) publication on the revised G6PD variant classification provides an updated variant nomenclature, it does not specifically recommend the AMM methodology [5]. Despite this guideline, many laboratories, particularly in regions with a high prevalence of G6PD deficiency, lack large-scale data to robustly implement AMM-based classifications.

Pakistan, which is situated in a region with substantial ethnic heterogeneity, has a moderate-to-high prevalence of G6PD deficiency, yet comprehensive evaluations of enzymatic activity patterns from clinical laboratories in the country remain limited [6]. Ali et al. [7] reported G6PD deficiency in 1.8% of 3,000 healthy males across different ethnic groups in Pakistan, with the prevalence varying from 1.07% in Kashmiris to 3.17% in Pathans. Existing studies are often constrained by small sample sizes, narrow age ranges, or lack of sex stratification [8–10]. Furthermore, the stability of the AMM value across different adult male age windows has not been assessed locally.

To characterize the patterns observed in routine laboratory practice, we conducted a large-scale quantitative evaluation of G6PD activity using long-term data obtained at a clinical reference laboratory. This study provides a detailed characterization of clinical laboratory-based G6PD activity patterns, including age- and sex-specific distributions; reveals the stability of the AMM; and establishes locally relevant deficiency thresholds.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective clinical laboratory study was conducted at the Department of Pathology and Laboratory Medicine, Aga Khan University Hospital (AKUH), Karachi, Pakistan. The AKUH Clinical Laboratories constitute a high-volume tertiary-care academic center that undertakes College of American Pathologists-accredited testing of specimens received from across the country. For this study, nationwide sample coverage was facilitated through an extensive network that primarily collected samples from patients for whom G6PD testing was clinically requested. Therefore, the specimens are enriched for individuals with clinically suspected G6PD deficiency. All quantitative G6PD assays performed between January 2009 and December 2024 were included in the study. G6PD activity was measured using a standardized spectrophotometric enzymatic assay based on the reduction of NADP+ to NADPH, with the absorbance measured at 340 nm. All assays were performed using the Trinity Biotech G6PD Assay Kit (Trinity Biotech, Bray, Ireland). The laboratory participates in proficiency testing surveys conducted by the College of American Pathologists and maintains this accreditation. Internal quality control was performed using two levels of controls for each analytical batch.

Data extraction and cleaning

All available entries containing quantitative G6PD activity (U/g Hb), birth date, sex, and specimen collection date information were extracted. The age at testing was calculated as the difference between the specimen collection and birth dates. Records were excluded if they had missing date, age, or G6PD activity data as well as implausible values identified upon a review of the specimen integrity, quality control data, and assay measurement limitations. Patient-level deduplication was not performed because the dataset contained specimen-level rather than patient-level identifiers. For the age-stratified analyses, clinically meaningful bands were used to group the data as follows: neonate (< 28 days), infant (28 days to < 1 year), early childhood (1–5 years), late childhood (6–12 years), adolescence (13–17 years), early adulthood (18–45 years), middle-aged adult (46–60 years), and older adult (> 60 years).

Determination of the adjusted male median

The estimation of 100% G6PD activity was performed using the AMM approach described by Domingo et al. [4]. The dataset was filtered to include adult males in the age window of 18–45 years, which was selected to minimize confounding from neonatal and infantile reticulocytosis, developmental physiology, and age-related erythrocyte turnover decline in older adults, consistent with the rationale described by Domingo et al. [4]. The initial median (M₀) G6PD activity was calculated for all males in this group. Individuals with an enzyme activity of 10% or less of the M₀ were classified as markedly G6PD deficient and excluded from the study. The median of the remaining non-deficient adult males was computed to derive the AMM, which served as the 100% activity reference.

Sensitivity testing of the stability of the adjusted male median

To assess the robustness of the AMM, sensitivity testing was performed using alternative adult male age windows: the primary window of 18–45 years and two additional windows of 15–50 and 18–60 years.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3 software (NumPy, Pandas, SciPy, and Matplotlib). Descriptive statistics included the median and interquartile range (IQR) and the mean and standard deviation. Prevalence estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Histograms, density plots, and distribution overlays were generated to visualize the data structure. Imputation was not applied for missing values.

Results

Specimens and demographic profile

This study included 51,748 specimens (from 41,397 males and 10,351 males) with valid quantitative G6PD activity results, collected over a 16-year period. Among the males and females, the mean G6PD activities were 10.59 ± 5.23 and 10.90 ± 4.31 U/g Hb and the median (IQR) values were 10.3 (7.8–12.6) and 10.7 (8.5–12.9) U/g Hb, respectively. The mean age of the tested individuals was 10.70 ± 17.07 years for males and 17.42 ± 20.40 years for females.

Adjusted male median

The data on 7,436 adult males aged 18–45 years were used for the AMM calculation. The M₀ G6PD activity was 9.0 U/g Hb. After excluding individuals with activity values of 0.9 U/g Hb or less (≤ 10% of M₀), the AMM was determined to be 9.2 U/g Hb (Fig. 1, Table 1). The small difference between the M₀ (9.0 U/g Hb) and AMM (9.2 U/g Hb) confirms that the tested population is predominantly G6PD normal and that the small proportion of individuals severely deficient for the enzyme did not materially bias the derivation of the 100% activity reference. On the basis of this value, the following deficiency thresholds were defined: severe deficiency (< 10% AMM), less than 0.92 U/g Hb; moderate deficiency (10–30%), 0.92–2.76 U/g Hb; intermediate activity (30–60%), 2.76–5.52 U/g Hb; and normal activity (> 60%), greater than 5.52 U/g Hb (Table 2). The stability of the AMM across alternative age windows is shown in Table 1 (range 9.2–9.3 U/g Hb, ~ 1% variation). An alternative empirical approach to defining the male G6PD deficiency threshold is visual inspection of the bimodal male distribution, which showed a natural dip at approximately 4 U/g Hb in this study cohort (Fig. 1). This empirical approach complements the AMM-based methodology, and both were consistent in identifying the predominantly G6PD-normal male population.

Distribution of G6PD activity data

Among the adult males, the G6PD activity data exhibited a right-skewed distribution, with most values clustering between 7 and 12 U/g Hb and a long tail extending toward higher activities (Fig. 1). A bimodal distribution was evident, with a dip at approximately 4 U/g Hb separating the G6PD-deficient from the G6PD-normal subpopulation. This natural dip provides empirical validation of the AMM-derived deficiency threshold.

Age- and sex-stratified patterns

Across the full dataset, groups of a younger age (particularly neonates and infants) displayed higher median G6PD activity values, which gradually declined through adulthood and older age (Fig. 2). The female activity distributions were consistently broader than those of the males, reflecting the biological variability associated with X-chromosome inactivation (Fig. 3). Discussion

This large clinical laboratory-based analysis provides one of the most comprehensive evaluations of G6PD activity patterns in Pakistan to date and offers evidence supporting the reliability of a population-specific AMM. By leveraging 16 years of quantitative testing data from a national reference laboratory, we demonstrated that the AMM is highly stable across plausible adult male age windows and provides a reliable benchmark for defining G6PD deficiency categories. Moreover, the size of this dataset (> 50,000 specimens) adds considerable statistical strength.

The stability of the AMM across alternative adult age windows (18–45, 15–50, and 18–60 years), with only ~1% variation, indicated that the male G6PD activity distribution in this population was minimally influenced by age-related physiological variability (Table 1; Fig. 1). The minimal difference between the M₀ (9.0 U/g Hb) and AMM values (9.2 U/g Hb) confirmed that the tested population was predominantly G6PD normal and that excluding severely deficient outliers did not materially shift the 100% activity estimate, which is consistent with the findings in other studies [4, 11]. Attributing this methodology to Domingo et al. [4] rather than a formal WHO document more accurately reflects the evidence base; the 2024 WHO Bulletin on G6PD variant classification does not specifically address the AMM methodology [5].

Age- and sex-stratified analyses revealed patterns consistent with the known physiological and genetic determinants of G6PD expression. The markedly higher enzyme activity in neonates and infants (Fig. 2) aligns with their established developmental physiology, where reticulocytosis and immature erythrocyte populations contribute to elevated measured activity [12]. The substantially higher rates of apparent severe deficiency in young children in our dataset (12.4% in early childhood vs. 3.7% in middle-aged males, Table 3) should be interpreted with caution: this pattern is expected in a clinical referral sample, as neonatal jaundice and acute hemolytic anemia are the most common indications for G6PD testing in children, producing a clinically enriched subset. By contrast, G6PD testing in adults is often performed in a lower acuity context. This enrichment effect means that the age-stratified prevalence estimates from this dataset cannot be extrapolated to the general population. This gradual decline in G6PD activity with advancing age parallels previous reports on age-related erythrocyte turnover [13].

The G6PD activity distributions among females were characteristically broader and more heterogeneous than those among males (Fig. 3), reflecting mosaic expression due to X-chromosome inactivation [14, 15]. Importantly, the enzyme activity in heterozygous females could not reliably predict clinical phenotypes. The variable expression level due to X-chromosome inactivation means that a female with intermediate enzymatic activity may either carry one copy of a severely deficient allele or have a normal genotype. Therefore, activity-based thresholds in females serve as screening tools only, and clinical decisions, particularly regarding the safe administration of radical cure therapies, require molecular confirmation in this group [14].

The prevalence of severe and moderate deficiency together (11.7%; 95% CI 11.4–12.0%) is consistent with the estimates from South Asia reported by Kalnoky et al. [15], who described similar overall deficiency rates across the region. These distributions have practical significance for malaria-endemic regions, where G6PD deficiency screening results can inform the safe administration of primaquine and tafenoquine.

An important strength of this study is its methodological consistency. All assays were performed on standardized spectrophotometric platforms with rigorous quality assurance [16]. Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, because this was a clinical laboratory-based dataset rather than a population-representative sample, the prevalence estimates reflect rates among the tested individuals and not the general population. Second, patient-level deduplication was not performed owing to the specimen-level nature of the database. Hence, repeat testing in individuals with chronic hemolysis or follow-up monitoring may have introduced bias, particularly in extreme activity categories. Third, the absence of clinical covariates, including hemoglobin, reticulocyte counts, transfusion history, and clinical indications, precluded phenotypic validation of the AMM-derived categories and prevented us from confirming whether intermediate and moderate categories in males reflect true partial deficiency, reticulocytosis, or high-residual-activity variants such as G6PD Kalyan-Kerala [17]. Fourth, the absence of genotypic data for any specimen means that the molecular variants underlying G6PD deficiency in this population remain uncharacterized. Therefore, future studies should incorporate molecular characterization along with enzymatic testing. Fifth, the unavailability of clinical data meant a lack of important insights into the clinical impact of G6PD deficiency in this population, such as the proportion of patients with neonatal jaundice and acute hemolytic anemia from drugs, fava beans, or infections. Such information would have substantially strengthened the clinical relevance of our findings.

In summary, the locally derived AMM provides a robust reference standard that reflects the enzymatic capacity within this clinical catchment area. These findings provide a foundation for future prospective clinical validation studies. However, until they can be correlated with clinical outcomes and genotypic data, the AMM-derived thresholds in this study should be interpreted as laboratory reference values rather than validated clinical decision cutoffs.
