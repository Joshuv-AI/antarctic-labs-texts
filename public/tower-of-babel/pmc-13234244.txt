An active and multifunctional curcumin-zein composite film for shelf-life extension in sustainable food packaging
Authors: Zhihua Liu, Lingying Chen, Xiaozhi Zheng, Junlin Li, Jiankun Ding, Chen Cai, Xiaofang Chen, Haiming Yu, Qunlong Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conventional plastic packaging faces significant challenges due to its non-biodegradable nature and lack of active preservation functionality, leading to environmental pollution and food waste. To address these issues, this study developed a curcumin-zein composite film as a multifunctional active packaging material based on natural polymers. Systematic characterization confirmed that curcumin was incorporated into the zein matrix through intermolecular interactions. Functional evaluations demonstrated that the composite film exhibits excellent UV-shielding performance, remarkable antioxidant and antibacterial activities, as well as favorable biodegradability and biosafety. In preservation tests on cherry tomatoes and black grapes, the 5% Cur-Zein composite film effectively delayed fruit quality deterioration, reducing weight loss, color changes, and nutrient consumption. This study demonstrates that the Cur-Zein composite film represents a green and high-performance active packaging material with good potential for reducing food loss and extending the shelf life of fruits.

Introduction

Food packaging plays an indispensable role in the modern food supply chain. With growing consumer demand for fresh and minimally processed foods, the development of effective packaging materials that can extend shelf life while maintaining food quality and safety has become a top priority (Chen, Zhang, et al., 2025; Mao et al., 2024; Rahman et al., 2025; Huan Zhang et al., 2025). Although conventional plastic films are widely used due to their low cost and favorable mechanical properties, they pose serious environmental concerns because of their non-biodegradable nature(Chen, Huang, et al., 2025; Wei, Zhu, et al., 2025). In response, biodegradable films derived from natural polymers (e.g., proteins, polysaccharides, and lipids) have gained increasing attention for their sustainability (Li, Su, et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2024; Panda et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2024). However, they often have some limitations, such as a lack of antibacterial properties, no antioxidant capacity, and poor barrier performance, among others (Jat et al., 2025; Pawde et al., 2025).Therefore, enhancing the functional performance of biodegradable films through the incorporation of bioactive compounds or functional additives has become a key research direction.

Curcumin, a natural polyphenolic compound derived from Curcuma longa, has attracted considerable interest for food packaging applications due to its well-documented antioxidant and antimicrobial properties (Alavi & Ciftci, 2025; Aliabbasi et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2024; Roy et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2025; Zhai et al., 2025). In the food industry, it serves as a natural colorant and preservative. The antioxidant activity of curcumin is primarily attributed to its ability to scavenge free radicals (e.g., DPPH• and ABTS•+) by donating hydrogen atoms from its phenolic hydroxyl groups, as well as to chelate metal ions, thereby inhibiting lipid peroxidation (Tan et al., 2025). Its anti-inflammatory effects are associated with the downregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators (such as TNF-α, IL-6, and COX-2) via the modulation of NF-κB and other signaling pathways (Wu et al., 2026). Furthermore, the antimicrobial properties of curcumin have been extensively studied (Huang et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025; Li, Wei, et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025); it is believed to disrupt bacterial cell membranes and interfere with cell division, demonstrating efficacy against a range of foodborne pathogens, thereby enhancing food safety and preservation (Ibeogu et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2025). Compared with other natural antioxidants such as tea polyphenols or essential oils, curcumin offers distinct advantages for food packaging, including strong UV-shielding capability (due to its conjugated diketone structure), thermostability, and compatibility with hydrophobic biopolymers like zein (Hashimoto et al., 2024). However, its application is often limited by poor water solubility and low bioavailability. Consequently, encapsulation within biopolymer matrices has emerged as a promising strategy to improve its stability and functionality in food systems (Tian et al., 2024).

Zein, a prolamine protein derived from corn, has emerged as a promising food packaging material due to its biodegradability, non-toxicity, and excellent film-forming properties (Ashraf et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). Zein exhibits good mechanical strength, flexibility, and barrier properties against oxygen and water vapor, making them suitable for food preservation (Ashraf et al., 2026). More importantly, the amphiphilic nature of zein enables it to form stable nanoparticles that encapsulate hydrophobic bioactive compounds such as curcumin, thereby improving their solubility and bioavailability (Wei, Shi, et al., 2025). Accordingly, the combination of curcumin with zein offers a promising strategy for developing an efficient and sustainable food packaging system.

In this study, we developed a curcumin-zein (Cur-Zein) composite film and systematically evaluated its structural, optical, antioxidant, antibacterial, and biodegradable properties. The application potential of this film was further assessed through a cherry tomato preservation experiment, as illustrated in Scheme 1. Our results demonstrate that the incorporation of curcumin significantly enhances the functional performance of zein-based films, offering a green and multifunctional packaging material for extending the shelf life of perishable foods.Scheme 1The design roadmap and application of the materials.Scheme 1

Materials and methods

Materials

Zein powder (purity≥99%) and Curcumin (purity≥95%) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., LTD. Glycerol and 1,3-propanediol were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Ethanol (99.5%) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., LTD. ABTS (98%) and DPPH (96%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich GMBH and Sinopharm Chemical Reagents Co., LTD., respectively. Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), high-quality fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin-streptomycin, and PBS reagents were purchased from Thermofisher Scientific Inc. The Escherichia coli (E. coli), Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), Listeria monocytogenes (LM), and Salmonella were obtained from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University. All other chemicals used were of analytical grade and used as received.

Preparation of Cur-Zein composite films

Cur-Zein films were prepared by dissolving 1 g of zein in 9 mL of an ethanol/water solution (75% v/v) to form a 10% (w/v) zein solution. The solution was stirred continuously at 50–60 °C for at least 30 min to induce zein self-assembly. The stirring speed was optimized to ensure uniform dissolution without disrupting the self-assembly process. Subsequently, a plasticizer mixture (30% by weight of zein, composed of glycerol and 1,3-propanediol in a 1,5 ratio) was added and stirred for an additional 30 min. Curcumin was then incorporated into the solution at concentrations of 1%, 3%, and 5% (w/w, relative to zein). The mixture was stirred for another 30 min and then cooled to room temperature. The resulting film-forming solution was cast onto a 9 cm diameter Petri dish and dried in an oven at 40 °C for 1–2 h, followed by further drying at room temperature. Finally, the prepared films were conditioned for 72 h in an artificial climate chamber at 25 °C and 53% relative humidity. A blank composite film without curcumin was also prepared. The plasticizers (glycerol and 1,3-propanediol) used in this research are both biobased and biodegradable. The casting method using this solution only requires a regular oven to complete, without the need for energy-consuming equipment or special facilities. The above proves that this research has good potential in terms of environmental friendliness and cost-effectiveness.

Characterization of Cur-Zein composite films

The crystalline structure of the Cur-Zein films were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer (Germany) equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å). The measurements were performed over a 2θ range of 5° to 80° at a scanning rate of 0.02°/s. The functional groups and molecular interactions in the Cur-Zein films were characterized using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) with a Thermo Fisher Nicolet iS50 spectrometer (Waltham, MA, USA). The spectra were recorded in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1. The surface elemental composition and chemical states of the Cur-Zein films were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Scientific, USA). The measurements were performed under ultra-high vacuum conditions with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source. The surface morphology and microstructure of the Cur-Zein films were observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with a JEOL JSM-7600F microscope (Tokyo, Japan). The samples were coated with a thin layer of gold to enhance conductivity before imaging.

Optical properties

The optical properties of the Cur-Zein films, including transparency and UV–Vis absorption, were evaluated using a Shimadzu UV-3600 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Kyoto, Japan). The absorbance spectra were recorded in the wavelength range of 200–800 nm.

Water resistance, water vapor permeability (WVP), water contact angle (WCA)

The moisture content (MC) and water solubility (WS) of the films were evaluated following a gravimetric method. Briefly, film samples (20 mm × 20 mm) were precisely weighed (W0) and dried to a constant weight (W1) in a vacuum oven at 105 °C. The dried samples were then immersed in 25 mL of distilled water and maintained at room temperature for 48 h. Subsequently, the undissolved film fragments were collected, dried at 50 °C for 24 h, and weighed again (W2). The WC and WS were calculated according to Eqs. (1-1), (1-2), respectively.(1-1)MC%=W0−W1W1×100%(1-2)WS%=W1−W2W2×100%

The hydrophilicity and hydrophobicity of the Cur-Zein films were assessed by measuring the water contact angle using a Krüss DSA30S goniometer (Hamburg, Germany). A droplet of deionized water was placed on the film surface, and the contact angle was measured at room temperature.

The WVP of the composite films was measured according to a modified ASTM E96–90 method (Ruggeri et al., 2020). In this procedure, film samples were cut into circular discs with a diameter of 15 mm and securely sealed over glass vials containing 10 mL of deionized water. The assemblies were then placed in a controlled environment chamber maintained at 34 °C and 47 ± 2% relative humidity. The weight loss of the vials was monitored at 1-h intervals until a steady state was achieved, as indicated by a linear weight change over time. The WVP was subsequently calculated using Eq. (1-3):(1-3)WVP%=w×dA×t×100%where w is the weight loss (g) over time t (h), A is the effective film area (m2), and d is the film thickness (mm).

Antioxidant and antibacterial activities of Cur-Zein composite films

To evaluate the antioxidant activity of the Cur-Zein composite films, DPPH• radical scavenging, ABTS•+ radical scavenging, hydroxyl radical (•OH) scavenging, and superoxide anion radical (•O2−) scavenging assays were performed. The detailed procedures are as follows: Specifically, for the DPPH• assay: 50 mg of the composite film was mixed with 5 mL of DPPH ethanol solution and incubated in the dark for 30 min. For the ABTS•+ assay: 50 mg of the composite film was reacted with 5 mL of ABTS•+ solution under the same conditions. Subsequently, the absorbance of the DPPH mixture was measured at 517 nm, and that of the ABTS•+ mixture was measured at 734 nm using a microplate reader. The •OH and•O2− assays were performed following the same procedure, with absorbance measured at 510 nm and 560 nm, respectively. Each experiment was performed in triplicate (n = 3). In all assays, a control consisting of the corresponding radical solution without any film sample was used to assess radical scavenging activity.

To quantitatively evaluate the antibacterial activity, the plate colony counting method was performed against E. coli, S. aureus, LM, and Salmonella. The detailed procedure is as follows: The bacterial suspension was diluted with normal saline to a concentration of approximately 107 CFU/mL. Then, 1 mg of each composite film was co-incubated with the bacterial suspension for 30 min. After incubation, 200 μL of the upper bacterial suspension was mixed with agar solution, poured onto a culture plate, and gently shaken to ensure uniform distribution. The plates were then incubated in an incubator for 18 h, and the number of colonies on each plate was observed and counted.

The antibacterial rate is calculated according to the following formula (1-4):(1-4)Antibacterial ratio%=Aa−AbAa×100%where Aa and Ab represent the number of bacterial colonies in the control and sample groups, respectively.

To present the antibacterial effect more intuitively, we also conducted supplementary experiments on E. coli and S. aureus using the inhibition zone method. We added 30 mL of agar medium to each plate, allowed it to cool and solidify, and then added 30 μL of bacterial solution (106 CFU/mL) to each. Subsequently, we used a 1 mL pipette to make four holes on the agar medium, and placed an appropriate-sized Cur/Zein composite film of each ratio in each hole. Then, we placed it in a CO2 incubator for 18 h (at 37 °C). Finally, we removed it, observed it, and recorded the antibacterial effect.

In addition, an antibacterial aging test was conducted to evaluate the long-term antibacterial stability of the composite films. Specifically, the prepared Zein-curcumin composite films were placed in a constant temperature and humidity chamber at 40 °C and 53% relative humidity for accelerated aging for 7 days. Samples were taken at day 0 (initial control) and day 7 of aging. The antibacterial activity of the films against E. coli and S. aureus was evaluated using the shake flask method. The initial bacterial concentration was adjusted to 1 × 106 CFU/mL. After shaking incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, the number of viable colonies was counted, and the antibacterial rate was calculated according to the formula.

Biodegradability and biocompatibility tests of Cur-Zein composite films

To evaluate the biodegradability of the Cur-Zein composite films, a soil burial degradation experiment was conducted. To minimize test errors caused by soil humidity, the film samples were buried at a depth of approximately 10 cm in a sunny environment. Every 3 days, the samples were carefully retrieved, and the soil adhering to the film surface was gently removed with a soft brush; the removed soil was then returned to the culture container. The degradation status of each film sample was observed and photographed, and the samples were weighed to calculate the residual weight percentage. Additionally, the mass and pH of the soil were measured before and after the degradation test to assess the impact of film degradation on the soil environment. The following formula is used to calculate the percentage of residual mass (1-5):(1-5)Residual mass%=ARA0×100%

Here, AR represents the total mass of the composite film at each time point. A0 represents the initial mass of the composite film.

The cytotoxicity of the Cur-Zein composite films was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. L929 cells were cultured in a complete medium consisting of high-glucose DMEM, 10% FBS, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 24 h. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with 100 μL of fresh medium containing extracts of the 5% Cur-Zein films at various concentrations, and the cells were incubated for another 24 h. After this treatment, the medium was removed, and each well was supplied with 100 μL of fresh medium and 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent. Following 1.5 h of incubation, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Cell vitality is calculated by the following formula (1-6):(1-6)Cell viability%=At−AbAc−Ab×100%where At is the absorption of solutions containing different concentrations of Cur-Zein. Ac is the absorption of the control sample and Ab is the absorption of cell-free solutions.

Furthermore, to assess the biological safety of the degraded material, a soybean growth experiment was conducted. Soybean seeds were germinated in water and then transplanted into pots containing 90 g of soil that had been mixed with the degraded Cur-Zein composite film. The growth of these seedlings was monitored and compared with that of control seedlings grown in untreated natural soil.

Fruit storage experiments

Small tomatoes were purchased from a local market. A batch of tomatoes uniform in size, color, and free from physical damage was selected. These tomatoes were randomly divided into three groups and placed in circular PET plastic cups (diameter: 3.5 cm; depth: 7.5 cm). The cups of the experimental group were sealed with the 5% Cur-Zein composite film. Unpackaged tomatoes served as the blank control, and tomatoes sealed with the 0% Cur-Zein composite film were used as the negative control. All samples were stored at room temperature for 12 days. Every 72 h, the color, weight loss, pH, and total soluble solid (TSS) content of the tomatoes in each group were evaluated using standard methods.

Following the aforementioned experimental method, a 12-day preservation experiment was conducted on black grapes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM, USA). All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD post-hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are indicated in the figures as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. Each group has a data volume greater than 3 (n ≥ 3).

Results and discussion

Characterization of Cur-Zein composite films

Fig. 1a presents the XRD spectra of pure Curcumin material and the composite films containing 0%, 1%, 3%, and 5% Cur-Zein. The pure Zein film shows a typical amorphous broad peak, located in the range of 2θ approximately 15°–25°, which is a characteristic of protein-based materials (Bahadori et al., 2026). As the curcumin content increases, the crystalline diffraction peaks at 17.3° and 24.7° gradually intensify, which match those of pure curcumin (Luo et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). These results indicate that curcumin exists in a crystalline state within the composite film, and its abundance in the zein matrix increases with higher curcumin loading. At the same time, the amorphous broad peak of Zein remains basically unchanged, indicating that the matrix structure of Zein has not been damaged. These results confirm that the Cur-Zein composite films have been successfully prepared.Fig. 1Structural characterization of Cur-Zein composite films. As the curcumin content increases, the positions of these two key peaks show no significant shifts, indicating that the secondary structure of zein remains largely intact and is not significantly disrupted. However, the broad band at approximately 3297 cm−1 (O—H stretching vibration) gradually intensifies and exhibits a distinct shift to a lower wavenumber with the incorporation of curcumin (Guo et al., 2023). This “red-shifted” and intensified profile is a classic signature of hydrogen bond formation, providing strong evidence for the establishment of intermolecular hydrogen bonds between the phenolic hydroxyl groups of curcumin and the hydroxy groups of zein (Luo et al., 2021).

TGA was performed to evaluate the thermal stability of the composite films. As shown in Fig. 1c, all films exhibit a similar thermal degradation profile. The initial weight loss below approximately 150 °C corresponds to the evaporation of absorbed moisture. The major weight loss occurs between 268 °C and 330 °C, attributed to the thermal decomposition of zein and curcumin. Above 330 °C, the weight loss gradually stabilizes, leaving a residual char of about 8% at 800 °C. The incorporation of curcumin does not cause a significant shift in the degradation temperature, indicating that curcumin has no pronounced effect on the thermal stability of the zein matrix.

The XPS spectra of the Cur-Zein composite are shown in Figs. 1d, e, and f. In the C1s spectrum (Fig. 1d), the peak intensity of the C—O/C—N bond at 286.3 eV is notably higher than that of the C—C/C—H bond at 284.8 eV. This reflects a higher proportion of polar functional groups (C—O/C—N) compared to non-polar carbon species (C—C/C—H) on the film surface (Wang et al., 2019). The O1s spectrum (Fig. 1e) supports this conclusion, collectively revealing that the film surface is dominated by polar oxygen-containing functional groups. These primarily originate from the methoxy (—OCH3) and phenolic hydroxyl (—OH) groups of curcumin, as well as the peptide bonds (C=O) and polar side chains of zein, making C—O and C—OH the predominant chemical environments (Geng et al., 2023). The distinct signals for CO and O—H further confirm the successful incorporation of curcumin. Furthermore, the N1s spectrum (Fig. 1f) shows a clear peak for the amide bond (—NH—C=O) at 400.3 eV, confirming the formation and surface presence of the zein matrix. An additional signal from free amino groups (—NH2) at around 399.5 eV provides further evidence for the migration and enrichment of polar components at the surface during the film formation process.

The surface morphology of the composite films was analyzed using a SEM. The SEM images in Fig. 1g show the surface morphology of the films with different curcumin contents. The surface of the pure zein composite film is relatively smooth, with only a few visible pores and lumps. As the curcumin content increases, the surface becomes progressively rougher, developing larger pores and aggregates. This observed morphology is consistent with the aggregation of curcumin nanoparticles within the zein matrix (Liang et al., 2025). Moreover, as the curcumin loading increases, the surface roughness of the composite film also increases, which may affect its water vapor barrier performance. A rougher surface typically provides a larger effective surface area for water vapor diffusion, which generally leads to an increase in WVP. This is consistent with the WVP results shown in Fig. 4c.

Color difference and thickness analysis

The color of food packaging can indirectly influence consumers' purchasing decisions. The color and thickness of the Cur-Zein composite films with different curcumin concentrations are presented in Fig. 2. As the curcumin content increased, the L* value (Fig. 2a) rose from 36.6 to 43.4, suggesting enhanced lightness and uniform dispersion of curcumin within the film matrix. Correspondingly, the a* value (Fig. 2b) increased from −0.5 to 6.3, indicating a color shift towards orange-yellow. A more pronounced change was observed in the b* value (Fig. 2c), which surged from 7.4 to 31.3, providing direct evidence for the successful incorporation of curcumin and confirming a strong positive correlation with its concentration. Furthermore, the film thickness (Fig. 2d) increased from 0.056 mm to 0.077 mm, a change attributable to the incorporation of curcumin. It is worth noting that the yellowish color of the 5% Cur-Zein film remains within an acceptable range for natural food packaging applications (Roy & Rhim, 2020). UV–Vis light transmittance of the four Cur-Zein composite films

The optical properties and color dispersion performance of the film are the core evaluation indicators for measuring its performance. These not only directly determine the visual presentation effect of the product, but also, by influencing consumers' perception of the freshness and quality of food, become the key factors determining the market acceptance. Fig. 3a shows the macroscopic photos of the four composite films, and the color of these films is closely related to the concentration of Curcumin.Fig. 3(a) Photographs, (b) UV–Vis light transmittance and (c) absorbance of the 0%, 1%, 3%, 5% Cur-Zein composite films.Fig. 3

(a) Photographs, (b) UV–Vis light transmittance and (c) absorbance of the 0%, 1%, 3%, 5% Cur-Zein composite films.

As illustrated in Fig. 3b, the UV–Vis transmittance spectra of the composite films demonstrate their distinct light-management capabilities. The pure zein film exhibits high transparency across both the UV and visible regions, confirming its poor UV-blocking ability. In contrast, the incorporation of curcumin drastically reduces transmittance in the ultraviolet range (200–400 nm). The 5% Cur-Zein film achieves near-complete UV shielding, which is attributed to the strong intrinsic UV absorption of curcumin molecules due to their conjugated structure. A distinct absorption peak is observed around 420 nm, further affirming the presence and function of curcumin as a natural UV absorber. Concurrently, as shown in the absorbance spectra (Fig. 3c), the absorption intensity at 420 nm increases progressively with curcumin concentration, a trend that aligns well with the Beer-Lambert law. In the visible region (400–800 nm), the transmittance decreases with increasing curcumin content, particularly in the blue-light range, imparting a yellow tint to the films and reducing their overall transparency. The significantly enhanced UV-shielding performance of the 5% Cur-Zein film underscores its great potential as an active food packaging material to prevent photo-oxidation and extend the shelf life of light-sensitive foods.

Water resistance, WVP and water contact angle

The physicochemical properties related to water interaction of the Cur-Zein composite films are summarized in Fig. 4. The MC of the films exhibited a significant decrease with increasing curcumin loading (Fig. 4a). This trend can be attributed to the intrinsic hydrophobicity of curcumin, which reduces the number of hydrophilic sites available for water binding within the zein matrix (Mehrabi-Khozani et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2023). In contrast to MC, the WS of all films remained high (75–80%) and showed no significant variation with curcumin concentration (Fig. 4b), indicating that the fundamental hydrophilic-hydrophobic balance of the zein matrix was not substantially altered. A critical functional property, the WVP demonstrated a concentration-dependent increase, with the 5% Cur-Zein film showing the highest value (Fig. 4c). This suggests that the incorporation of curcumin may introduce structural discontinuities or increase the free volume within the film, thereby facilitating water vapor diffusion. The surface hydrophobicity, assessed by WCA measurements, revealed a non-monotonic trend (Fig. 4d). The WCA increased up to a curcumin concentration of 3%, likely due to the surface presentation of non-polar curcumin groups, but decreased at 5% Cur, possibly owing to aggregation-induced surface roughness or phase separation.Fig. 4(a) The MC of Cur-Zein composite films at 0%, 1%, 3%, and 5%. The antibacterial results of the four composite films are shown in Fig. 5e–i. The control group and the 0% Cur-Zein composite film exhibited negligible antibacterial activity against all four tested bacterial strains. In contrast, the 3% Cur-Zein and 5% Cur-Zein composite films demonstrated excellent antibacterial effects against E. coli, S. aureus, LM, and Salmonella. Notably, the 5% Cur-Zein composite film achieved antibacterial rates exceeding 95% against all four strains. Furthermore, its significant inhibitory effect against the two common foodborne pathogens LM and Salmonella highlights its potential to suppress bacterial proliferation in food safety applications.

The antibacterial results of the inhibition zones of E. coli and S. aureus are shown in Fig. S1a and S1b. Overall, as the curcumin content in the composite films increase, the size of the inhibition zone gradually increases. For S. aureus, the inhibition zone is the largest in the 5% Cur/Zein composite film, with a maximum size of 1.3–1.6 cm. Comparatively, the antibacterial effect of the Cur/Zein composite film on E. coli is poorer, but the 5% Cur/Zein composite film still has the best antibacterial effect, with an inhibition zone size of 1.2–1.5 cm.

The antibacterial activity of the four composite films after accelerated aging is shown in Fig. S2. After 7 days of accelerated aging, the antibacterial rates of the 5% Cur-Zein composite film against E. coli and S. aureus were 84.4% and 85.0%, respectively. Although slightly decreased compared to the initial rates of approximately 95% before aging, they remained at a high level, indicating good long-term antibacterial stability of the composite film. Overall, the Cur-Zein composite film exhibits excellent antibacterial properties and holds potential for antibacterial applications in food preservation.

Biosafety evaluation and biodegradation of the Cur/Zein composite films

The biocompatibility of the Cur-Zein composite films was evaluated through both plant growth and cytotoxicity assays. In the mung bean growth experiment (Fig. 6a), plants were cultivated in soil amended with the degraded films. No significant differences in leaf color or appearance were observed between the treatment and control groups. According to Fig. 6b, the seedling height in the Cur-Zein groups was generally greater than that of the control. Fig. 6c shows the total number of leaves for each group of plants over a 12-day growth period. By the 12th day, all groups had reached the same total leaf count of 12 leaves. On the 3rd day, the leaf count of the control group was slightly higher than that of the film-treated groups. This early-stage difference is attributed to normal variation in seed germination rates among individual seeds, rather than any cytotoxic effect of the composite films. By the 12th day, the leaf counts for all groups tended to be consistent, and the height of the seedlings in the Cur-Zein groups also increased (Fig. 6b). These results confirm the excellent biocompatibility of the Cur-Zein composite films. Furthermore, cytotoxicity tests on L929 cells (Fig. 6d) revealed no adverse effects; cell viability remained at or above 100% (with a peak of 109% at 10 μg/mL) across a wide concentration range (1–1000 μg/mL). This indicates the absence of cytotoxicity and suggests a potential stimulatory effect on cell metabolism, which might be attributed to bioactive peptides derived from zein. Application for fruit preservation

To evaluate the fresh-keeping efficacy of the Cur-Zein composite film, preservation studies were conducted using cherry tomatoes and black grapes. As shown in Fig. 7a, the cherry tomatoes in the blank control group exhibited pronounced shriveling after 12 days of storage. The tomatoes packaged with the 0% Cur-Zein film also showed varying degrees of dehydration and shrinkage, whereas those packaged with the 5% Cur-Zein film remained relatively plump and fresh. This preservation outcome appears more favorable than that reported by Mao et al. (2024), where control fruits showed shriveling or spoilage as early as day 4 or 8. The observed discrepancy could be attributed to the different cherry tomato cultivars used in the studies. The variety selected for our work likely possesses a thicker and more robust peel, which inherently confers greater resistance to water loss and physical deterioration during storage. In addition, black grapes in the control group also exhibited varying degrees of shriveling and damage by day 12 of storage (Fig. S4), whereas those in the experimental group (packaged with the 5% Cur-Zein film) remained relatively intact. During storage, cherry tomatoes and black grapes undergo quality deterioration due to factors such as respiration, oxidation, and microbial activity, leading to weight loss, color changes, and spoilage. These changes significantly reduce their edible and commercial value. Treatment with the 5% Cur-Zein composite film effectively mitigated this decline. As shown in Fig. 7b–d and Fig. S5a-c, the color parameters (L*, a*, b*) of both cherry tomatoes and black grapes remained stable throughout the storage period, and the values of the 5% Cur-Zein treated group were consistently better than those of the control group. Although the differences were not statistically significant, they still provide useful insights for evaluating the preservation efficacy of the composite film. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 7e and Fig. S5d, the weight loss of the treated cherry tomatoes and black grapes at day 12 was less than 6% and 9% of their respective initial weights, indicating that the composite film inhibited water vapor transfer to some extent, thereby reducing moisture loss. It is worth noting that although the water vapor permeability (WVP) of the Cur-Zein films increased with increasing curcumin loading (Fig. 4c), the weight loss of fruits packaged with the 5% Cur-Zein film was still significantly lower than that of the control group. This apparent discrepancy can be explained by two factors. First, the increased film thickness (from 0.056 mm to 0.077 mm, Fig. 2d) may offset the higher WVP, resulting in a comparable or even lower actual water vapor transmission rate. Second, weight loss of fresh produce is not solely caused by water evaporation; respiratory metabolism also plays an important role. The antioxidant and antibacterial activities of the curcumin-containing film likely suppressed fruit respiration, thereby reducing overall weight loss.

Meanwhile, pH and TSS content are key indicators of fruit flavor and ripeness. During fruit storage, organic acids (e.g., citric acid and malic acid) are gradually consumed as a result of respiratory metabolism, leading to an increase in fruit pH. Therefore, the rate of pH increase indirectly reflects the metabolic activity and senescence degree of the fruit (Mao et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 7f and Fig. S5e, the pH of all tomato groups gradually increased from an initial value of 4.25 to 4.6 during storage, while the pH of black grapes increased from 3.0 to 3.6. Notably, the pH of the 5% Cur-Zein group was consistently lower than that of the control, suggesting that the film delayed the metabolic degradation of organic acids and helped maintain a better flavor profile. Concurrently, fruits consume nutrients like soluble sugars for respiration, leading to a continuous decrease in TSS. As shown in Fig. 7g and Fig. S5f, the TSS content of cherry tomatoes in the 5% Cur-Zein treated group decreased by only 2% over 12 days, whereas that of the control group decreased by 5%. For black grapes, the TSS content decreased by 3% in the treated group and by 7% in the control group. This demonstrates that the Cur-Zein composite film effectively suppressed the respiration rate of the cherry tomatoes, thereby extending their shelf life.

Conclusion

In this study, a curcumin-loaded zein composite film was successfully prepared. Structural characterization revealed that curcumin was uniformly dispersed in the zein matrix primarily through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. Performance tests showed that the 5% Cur-Zein composite film achieved near-complete UV shielding in the 200–400 nm range. The scavenging rates against DPPH•, ABTS•+, •OH, and •O2− radicals were 78.9%, 56.5%, 75.5%, and 66.5%, respectively. The antibacterial rates against E. coli, S. aureus, LM, and Salmonella all exceeded 95%. The film also exhibited good biodegradability and biosafety. In preservation tests on cherry tomatoes and black grapes, the composite film effectively retarded weight loss, color deterioration, and nutrient consumption, maintaining the sensory and nutritional quality of the fruits during 12 days of storage. Future work should also pay attention to the mechanical properties of the Cur-Zein composite films. Collectively, the Cur-Zein composite film integrates UV-shielding, antioxidant, antibacterial, and environmentally friendly characteristics, demonstrating good application prospects in the field of sustainable food packaging.
