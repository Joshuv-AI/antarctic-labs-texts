Benchmarking large language models for question answering on German clinical practice guidelines
Authors: Johannes Schwietering, Gregor Lichtner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesClinical practice guidelines are a cornerstone of evidence-based medicine, yet their implementation in routine care remains inconsistent. Large language models (LLMs), particularly with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), have shown strong performance in medical question answering, but their ability to use knowledge from German-language guidelines has not been systematically evaluated due to a lack of a dedicated benchmark. We therefore developed such a benchmark and evaluated guideline-based question answering with different LLMs and retriever configurations.MethodsWe developed cpgQA-DE, an expert-validated benchmark dataset of 200 multiple-choice questions derived from 10 current German clinical practice guidelines across five specialties. All questions were reviewed for correctness, relevance and complexity. The dataset includes case-based and knowledge-based questions with metadata on guideline source, specialty, relevance and difficulty. We evaluated LLM performance using a RAG-based pipeline built on a corpus of German guidelines, comparing multiple model–retriever combinations.ResultsRAG integration substantially improved accuracy across all tested models and for most retrievers. The best-performing configuration, GPT-5 combined with the multilingual-e5-large retriever, achieved an accuracy of 95%. Notably, the open-weight model gpt-oss-120b reached 90% accuracy when used with RAG.ConclusionscpgQA-DE enables systematic and reproducible offline evaluation of guideline-aware question answering systems in the German healthcare context. The observed performance gains with RAG support the use of LLMs augmented with quality-assured external knowledge. Such systems may help bridge the evidence-practice gap by providing guideline-based recommendations at the point of care, while strong performance of open-weight models suggests potential for on-premises deployment in privacy-sensitive clinical environments.

Introduction

The implementation of clinical practice guideline recommendations in routine healthcare remains suboptimal,1–3 contributing to a persistent evidence-practice gap.4–7 Physicians must navigate large volumes of text-based recommendations, which can be time-consuming and cognitively demanding, increasing the risk that relevant evidence is not applied at the point of care.8 9

Large language models (LLMs) have raised expectations for supporting medical professionals by providing access to evidence-based knowledge. They encode substantial clinical knowledge and perform strongly on standardised multiple-choice examinations.10–12 However, these successes are tempered by critical limitations: LLMs lack metacognitive abilities and are prone to hallucinating information.13 They also rely on static training data, hindering incorporation of newly published evidence.14 Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) addresses these limitations by retrieving authoritative external knowledge and providing it as context for answer generation.15

A large body of research evaluates language models as clinical decision-support systems, primarily focusing on general medical knowledge.16 However, most benchmarks do not assess whether models can apply information from clinical practice guidelines. Recent initiatives have begun to address this limitation: The cpgQA dataset17 was developed from a single opioid therapy guideline and includes over 1000 questions linked to contextual passages. A synthetic dataset of 39 patient cases based on a COVID-19 guideline18 evaluates adherence to flowchart-based recommendations. The AMEGA benchmark19 assesses adherence to multiple clinical practice guidelines through 20 simulated physician-authored cases across specialties. The neurovascular-qa dataset includes 85 open-ended questions derived from two German neurovascular guidelines and 384 automatically generated questions to evaluate RAG retrieval accuracy.20

These efforts demonstrate the feasibility of guideline-based evaluation but are limited by narrow scope (single guideline or specialty) or focus on English language resources. No benchmark currently evaluates whether LLMs, with or without retrieval, can apply knowledge from multiple German clinical practice guidelines, despite their central role in evidence-based care within the German healthcare system.

We address this gap by developing and validating a benchmark to evaluate how effectively LLMs retrieve and apply knowledge from German guidelines across specialties. Specifically, we (1) construct a high-quality multiple-choice dataset; (2) validate the questions through expert review; and (3) assess LLM performance with and without retrieval.

Methods

Study design, setting and data sources

We conducted a methodological benchmarking study to develop a multiple-choice question dataset from German clinical practice guidelines and evaluate LLM performance under different configurations.

Data were obtained from publicly available guidelines in the German national guideline registry maintained by the Association of Scientific Medical Societies (Arbeitsgemeinschaft der Wissenschaftlichen Medizinischen Fachgesellschaften, AWMF). In total, 810 PDF guidelines were downloaded for use as the retrieval corpus. The clinical practice guidelines forming the basis for question development were selected based on three criteria: (1) highest methodological level (S3 classification) of the AWMF classification system; (2) coverage of diverse clinical specialties to ensure thematic breadth; and (3) publication after 1 January 2021.

Question development

Multiple-choice questions were developed following established principles21 22: Each question was either a short clinical vignette or a direct question with four response options (one correct answer and three distractors). Questions were phrased positively, while absolute or negatively worded expressions were avoided. Distractors were designed to be grammatically and semantically parallel to the correct answer and to appear plausible while being unambiguously incorrect according to the guideline evidence. The questions were constructed to be answerable without the response options, allowing questions to be used in an open setting without providing the answers.23

The questions were authored by a physician (JS) based on selected guideline recommendations following a standardised procedure. First, one of five predefined question types was chosen: (1) case-based “what’s next?” questions, which were embedded in short patient vignettes; (2) knowledge recall questions on specific factual details from the guideline; (3) classification questions derived from diagnostic or staging schemes; (4) evidence and recommendation questions targeting recommendation strength or evidence grading; and (5) error detection questions, introducing a subtle but clinically meaningful error. Recommendations were selected pragmatically for clinical relevance, specificity and suitability for the predefined question type. Questions and correct answers were formulated to reflect realistic clinical decision-making, with three plausible but incorrect distractors. A locally executed language model (Llama 3 8B) was occasionally used to refine phrasing, but not to contribute to or alter any medical content. All questions were reviewed and refined to ensure clinical accuracy and adherence to the source guideline, and corresponding guideline passages were documented for traceability.

Expert validation

To ensure accuracy, complexity and clinical relevance, questions were validated through expert assessment along three dimensions: (1) content accuracy (correct response demonstrably supported by the guideline evidence, distractors clearly incorrect); (2) complexity (question difficulty for a second-year resident and for a board-certified specialist); (3) clinical relevance (importance for patient care, whether a physician should know the answer without consulting external resources, how frequently the topic occurs in routine clinical work). Error detection questions as well as evidence grading questions were excluded, as their correctness could be verified directly from the guideline text.

Expert validation was conducted via a web-based survey,24 with physicians having at least 3 years of clinical experience in the corresponding specialty. Each expert reviewed the full set of ~40 questions from their field. The survey included structured ratings and optional free-text fields for qualitative feedback (online supplemental appendix A). A pilot test involving two experts was used to assess clarity and usability, leading to minor refinements in wording. Two experts per specialty were invited, and each question was reviewed by at least one expert. Data collection took place between 1 May 2025 and 1 June 2025.

Free-text feedback was reviewed, and resulting question revisions were documented with justifications in a change protocol. For quantitative synthesis, ordinal ratings on four-point verbally anchored response scales were mapped to numeric scores and collapsed into three ordered categories. Mean scores were computed across raters, and the corresponding category labels are reported. Data processing and analysis were performed in R (V.4.5.1).

Benchmark utilisation for LLM assessment

We used the MedRAG toolkit23 to implement a modular RAG workflow (figure 1). In the direct (non-RAG) setting, questions are submitted directly to the model without additional context. In the RAG configuration, a retriever first identifies relevant passages from a predefined corpus, which are then concatenated and provided to the LLM.

We systematically evaluated the impact of prompt design, model choice, and, for RAG-enhanced inference, corpus composition and retrieval strategy on the question-answering performance of LLMs. Baseline performance was established through direct (non-RAG) inference using GPT-4o mini with multiple prompt variants, all incorporating chain-of-thought-style stepwise reasoning and requiring the model to return a brief rationale (online supplementary appendix). We then tested different commercial LLMs (GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-4o mini, GPT-4o, o4-mini, GPT-4.1, GPT-5, Mistral Small, Mistral Large, Claude 3.5 Haiku) and open-weight LLMs (Gemma 3 27B, MedGemma 27B Instruct, gpt-oss-120b and DeepSeek R1) using the optimised prompt.

For RAG, the knowledge base consisted of 810 German clinical practice guidelines retrieved from the German national guideline registry. PDFs were converted to text using Docling,25 split into 500-token chunks with a 50-token overlap. This chunking strategy was chosen a priori to preserve sufficient local guideline context. To explore the influence of different chunking configurations, we performed an additional chunk-size and overlap sensitivity analyses (online supplementary appendix). Chunks were embedded using two different models (multilingual-e5-large26 and MedCPT27) and indexed in a vector store (FAISS).28

To explore corpus effects, we created three nested corpora: the full set (‘All CPG’) reflected a realistic open retrieval setting over the entire national guideline registry. The subset of 224 guidelines with the highest methodological standard (S3) of the AWMF classification system (‘S3 CPG’) represented a quality-filtered setting. The 10-guideline subset represented a focused dataset, aligned with the benchmarking dataset (‘10 CPG’).

Retrieval was implemented using complementary approaches: (1) BM25, a lexical baseline applied to plain-text chunks to capture exact matches of clinically relevant terms; (2) embedding-based k-nearest-neighbour search in a FAISS vector store, using multilingual-e5-large as a dense multilingual retriever suitable for German text and MedCPT to assess whether biomedical-domain pretraining improved retrieval in this setting; and (3) reciprocal rank fusion (RRF), combining lexical and dense retrieval as a hybrid method. For all RAG runs, the top 32 retrieved chunks were appended to the model prompt, and deterministic decoding was used (temperature=0, top-p=1).

The primary outcome measure was accuracy, defined as the proportion of correctly answered items among all questions, with 95% CIs based on Wilson binomial intervals. Subgroup analyses examined performance by question type and specialty.

Results

Dataset characteristics

The primary objective was to develop a benchmark for evaluating medical question-answering systems on medical knowledge from German clinical practice guidelines. To this end, we created the German Clinical Practice Guideline Question Answering–Benchmark (cpgQA-DE).

The cpgQA-DE dataset comprises 200 multiple-choice questions from 10 German clinical practice guidelines covering five specialties (table 1). Each question includes metadata on source guideline, contextual guideline passages, chapter and question type. Expert-validated questions additionally include complexity ratings (differentiated for residents and specialists), clinical relevance, answerability without consulting external aids and estimated frequency of the question’s topic in clinical practice.

Five question types capture different reasoning patterns in guideline understanding: (1) case-based “What’s next?” questions (39% of all questions) present a clinical vignette and ask for the appropriate next diagnostic, therapeutic or monitoring step; (2) knowledge recall questions (34%) require retrieval of specific factual information from the guideline; (3) classification questions (9%) involve the assignment of findings to predefined categories (eg, risk classes); (4) evidence and recommendation questions (8%) target levels of evidence or recommendation grades; and (5) error detection questions (10%) require recognising that a question is unanswerable due to missing, inconsistent, or contradictory information.

Expert validation

Of 206 benchmark questions, error detection (n=20) and evidence and recommendation (n=16) questions were excluded from validation, as correctness could be directly verified from the guideline. Based on expert feedback, six questions were removed (online supplemental figure S1), yielding a final dataset of 200 questions, of which 164 underwent clinical validation.

Among the validated questions, most (n=119 (73%)) were rated medium or high difficulty for residents, compared with n=78 (48%) for board-certified specialists. Clinical relevance was rated medium or high for 127 questions (77%). Seventy questions (43%) covered knowledge that physicians are not expected to recall from memory but may consult external resources for. Most questions (n=125 (76%)) were considered to arise only occasionally in clinical practice, with 25 (15%) frequent and 14 (9%) never occurring (figure 2).

Benchmarking LLMs

Direct LLM versus RAG-enhanced LLM

We evaluated nine commercial LLMs from three providers (OpenAI, Mistral and Anthropic) and four open-weight LLMs from three providers (Google, OpenAI, DeepSeek) on cpgQA-DE using direct querying and RAG (table 2). RAG substantially improved performance across all models: The best-performing model (GPT-5) improved from 72% to 95% accuracy, while the largest relative improvement was observed for Claude 3.5 Haiku (50% to 83%).

Comparing different corpora and retrievers

To assess the impact of different retrieval settings on guideline-based question answering, we compared multiple corpora and retriever configurations (online supplemental table S2). Retriever choice strongly affected performance: MedCPT showed marginal improvements over the direct querying baseline, while multilingual-e5-large substantially improved results. BM25 also improved performance and achieved its highest accuracy on the 10-guideline corpus. The hybrid RRF approach showed better performance than BM25 but not multilingual-e5-large. Additional sensitivity analyses showed that retrieval depth affected accuracy, with performance increasing as more snippets were retrieved (online supplemental table S3). In contrast, different chunk-size and overlap settings yielded broadly similar accuracies (online supplemental table S4).

Comparing question characteristics

cpgQA-DE includes metadata on question type, originating guideline and associated specialty, enabling subgroup analyses. We compared accuracy by question type and specialty for the direct (‘No-RAG’) and RAG inference using gpt-oss-120b (table 3). RAG effects varied by question type, with the largest gains for evidence grading and classification, strong gains for knowledge questions, more modest but still substantial improvements for case-based ‘What’s next?’ questions and error-detection items.

Discussion

We developed cpgQA-DE, a benchmark for evaluating LLMs on guideline-based question answering in the German healthcare context. Using 200 expert-validated questions, we assessed multiple LLMs and examined the effect of retrieval augmentation. RAG consistently improved accuracy, with performance varying by question type, retriever and corpus.

cpgQA-DE addresses an important gap: Existing medical benchmarks are predominantly based on English-language sources,30 whereas cpgQA-DE focuses on knowledge drawn from German guidelines. Each question is linked to its source including guideline page and content format (text, figure or table), enabling independent evaluation of retriever performance and granular analyses, such as examining whether questions derived from figures or tables present greater difficulty than those based on narrative text. Questions were designed to allow question-only retrieval, reflecting a more realistic clinical use than approaches incorporating answer options.31 Finally, the classification into five distinct question types facilitates analysis of performance across different cognitive demands.

Expert validation supports clinical relevance of the benchmark

Expert ratings indicate that the benchmark captures clinically meaningful and realistically challenging content (figure 2). Question difficulty was balanced for residents, with a shift toward easier ratings for specialists while retaining a meaningful proportion of hard questions (16%). Clinical relevance was rated medium to high for most items (77%), underscoring that the questions reflect meaningful guideline knowledge rather than contrived edge cases. Only about one-third of questions were considered answerable from memory, indicating that the benchmark captures guideline knowledge that clinicians may need to consult in practice. Most questions were judged to occur at least occasionally in clinical practice (91%), further reinforcing the benchmark’s practical utility.

RAG substantially improves performance of LLMs

RAG integration substantially improved performance across all models, with the best configuration reaching 95% accuracy (table 2). This supports the view that LLMs benefit from high-quality external knowledge rather than relying solely on training data.32 However, these findings are based on an offline benchmark evaluation and therefore do not provide evidence of direct clinical applicability. Clinical implications of such accuracy levels would depend on the use case and risk profile: more tightly workflow-integrated or higher-autonomy decision-support applications require higher performance and stronger safety measures than tools intended primarily for information retrieval or navigation.

Notably, smaller models enhanced with RAG substantially outperformed frontier models without retrieval. We observed similar effects for open-weight models: gpt-oss-120b achieved 0.90 (95% CI 0.85 to 0.93) with RAG, on par with GPT-4o and GPT-4.1 under RAG, although below GPT-5 with RAG (0.95 (95% CI 0.91 to 0.97)). These results suggest that once a relevant passage is retrieved, even moderately capable models can generate accurate responses. Open-weight models with RAG may therefore offer a practical route toward guideline-grounded decision support in settings where local deployment is preferred, such as in hospital information technology infrastructure.

Retriever choice and corpus composition influence RAG performance

Both retriever architecture and corpus design strongly affected guideline-based question answering performance (online supplemental table S2). Embedding-based retrieval (multilingual-e5-large) achieved consistently strong performance, while BM25 achieved its highest accuracy on the curated 10-guideline corpus. The hybrid RRF approach showed no consistent advantage, and MedCPT provided only marginal gains over the non-RAG baseline, which may reflect a mismatch between its embedding space and German guideline texts.

Practically, these findings support embedding-based retrieval as a robust default, with lexical/hybrid methods beneficial for curated subsets. Performance also depended on corpus structure: PDF-based chunking may introduce artefacts, whereas structured representations (eg, recommendation/section-level passages) could improve retrieval. Ongoing efforts to digitise and structure the German national guideline repository in FHIR33 are therefore particularly promising, as they could support systematic corpus construction and more targeted content retrieval.

Error-detection remains challenging despite RAG

Performance varied more by question type than by specialty (table 3). ‘What’s next’ questions were already answered well without retrieval but improved further with RAG, suggesting that these items can be addressed from general clinical knowledge encoded in the models. Classification and evidence grading questions demonstrated the largest gains from retrieval, indicating the need for precise guideline context. Error-detection questions also improved with retrieval, but remained the lowest performing category, indicating that identifying implausible or contradictory statements remains comparatively challenging for LLMs and is not easily remedied by simply providing the correct source text. Across specialties, performance was broadly similar. Results discussed here are based on an open-weight model (gpt-oss-120b), selected to reflect realistic deployment settings with locally deployed models.

Prompt-based strategies improve performance but do not replace retrieval

Because multiple-choice accuracy alone does not distinguish retrieval failure from generation failure, we added a RAGAS-based supplementary evaluation of the main RAG configuration34 (online supplemental table S5). Answer relevance was high (0.89 (95% CI 0.89 to 0.90)), whereas faithfulness was lower (0.78 (95% CI 0.74 to 0.81)). This suggests that generated answers generally addressed the questions, but some explanatory statements were not fully supported by the retrieved context. However, lower faithfulness ratings may also reflect that answer-option content was not provided to the judge model and may therefore have been classified as unsupported despite being consistent with the selected answer. Chunk-level context relevance was low (0.05 (95% CI 0.04 to 0.05)), but consistent with the expectation that usually only 1–2 out of the 32 retrieved chunks contain the relevant guideline knowledge.

To assess whether prompt-based performance-improvement strategies could further improve guideline question answering, we performed a Medprompt-style analysis including few-shot exemplar selection and answer-choice shuffling with majority voting35 (online supplemental table S6). These strategies improved performance in the No-RAG setting, but in the RAG setting baseline performance was already high, and Medprompt-style prompting did not yield a clear additional benefit. Thus, prompt-based strategies improved direct LLM answering but did not replace the larger benefit of retrieval augmentation.

Fine-tuning may also improve domain-specific LLM performance, but was outside the scope of this study. cpgQA-DE was designed as an independent evaluation benchmark, and fine-tuning on the underlying guideline corpus would address model adaptation rather than retrieval-grounded access to verifiable and updateable guideline knowledge.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Regarding internal validity, we evaluated models using multiple-choice accuracy on a 200-question dataset. While accuracy is important, it captures only one dimension of trustworthy clinical decision support and future work should incorporate multi-dimensional competency evaluation frameworks (eg, multi-axis human evaluation of factual accuracy, comprehension, reasoning quality, and potential for harm or bias10 or medical domain-specific frameworks).36

Regarding external validity, the benchmark includes ten guidelines across five specialties, representing only a subset of German guidelines. Although all guidelines follow common structural principles, presentation formats vary considerably across specialties. These variations (eg, predominant use of flowcharts and tables vs narrative text) may affect retrieval effectiveness and limit generalisability. Moreover, all items are expert-constructed multiple-choice questions derived from guideline content rather than real clinical queries and the evaluation was conducted in an offline, vignette-style setting. It therefore remains unclear how well results translate to real-world clinical use or interactive decision support.

Conclusion

We developed and expert-validated cpgQA-DE, a benchmark targeting guideline-based question answering for German clinical practice guidelines. RAG substantially improved performance across all question types and model families, while error-detection tasks remain the lowest-performing category even under RAG. These findings support the use of high-quality external knowledge sources in LLM-based guideline question-answering systems, as models without retrieval showed lower accuracy in our benchmark. Especially in the context of living guidelines, newly released recommendations or institution-specific standard operating procedures, RAG-enabled systems should therefore be understood primarily as language interfaces to structured, authoritative information rather than as standalone knowledge engines. By providing a clinically grounded, systematically validated benchmark, our work offers a practical tool for offline evaluation of guideline-aware language models and may contribute to the development of safe, transparent and guideline-aware clinical decision-support technologies.
