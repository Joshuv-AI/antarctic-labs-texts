The cellular basis of locomotor evolution: insights from larval insects
Authors: Annika Sharma, Samantha Swank, Elizabeth S Heckscher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnimals move through their environments using remarkably diverse locomotor behaviors, which are critical for their survival and success. All movement, including locomotion, requires the coordinated function of three components: the central nervous system (CNS), the peripheral nervous system (PNS), and the musculature. Although evolutionary change in any one of these components can alter locomotion, how these changes arise and combine to generate behavioral diversity remains poorly understood. Larval insects are an exceptional system for addressing this question: they combine extensive behavioral and morphological diversity with relatively simple, stereotyped anatomy, enabling cell-level homology inferences and quantitative cross-tissue comparisons. We first review anatomical evidence for the long-standing peripheral change hypothesis, which posits that locomotor diversity is primarily driven by modifications to peripheral structures, such as muscles, while central circuits remain conserved. We then propose an alternative hypothesis, the motor neuron bottleneck hypothesis, which draws on comparative neurodevelopmental data to suggest that motor neurons are disproportionately conserved relative to both upstream sensory neurons and interneurons and downstream muscles. Finally, we consider how connections are maintained between the CNS, PNS, and musculature when these components change in number. Throughout, we assess relative rates of evolutionary change in cell number across nested phylogenetic scales, from the Drosophila genus to the Diptera order to the Holometabola supraorder. By integrating anatomical, developmental, and functional perspectives, larval insects emerge as a powerful comparative model for uncovering general evolutionary principles.

Introduction

Animals move through their environments using remarkably diverse locomotor behaviors. All movement requires the coordinated function of three components: the central nervous system (CNS), the peripheral nervous system (PNS), and the musculature (Figure 1a) [1,81]. A core question in evolutionary neuroscience is to what extent locomotor diversity reflects changes in the periphery or CNS [2,3]. A long-standing ‘peripheral change hypothesis’ posits that locomotor diversity arises from changes in peripheral structures, such as muscles, while circuits within the CNS remain relatively conserved (Figure 1b) [82–84]. Evaluating this hypothesis is challenging in most animals as it requires comparing the extent of change across the CNS, PNS, and muscles. However, larval insects enable such comparisons because their numerically simple and stereotyped anatomy facilitates counting individual cells and inferring cellular homologies [85–87]. Herein, we use larval insects to evaluate the peripheral change hypothesis and to propose an alternative. The alternative ‘motor neuron bottleneck hypothesis’ posits that motor neurons are relatively conserved compared to other neurons and muscles (Figure 1b). We provide developmental evidence to support this hypothesis. Finally, we describe variations in morphology and synaptic connectivity when data are available. Although this review focuses on larval insects, we expect that the principles discussed herein apply broadly.

Larval insect ecological, morphological, and behavioral diversity suggests locomotor system evolution

Ecological diversity

Larval insects inhabit an extraordinary range of environments, including water, soil, decaying matter, plant tissues, and animal hosts [88–91]. Each environment imposes distinct mechanical and sensory constraints that influence locomotor strategies [4–6].

Morphological diversity

All larval insects share a body plan — cylindrical, segmented, and bilaterally symmetric — with half-segments, or hemisegments, considered the repeating units of the larval body [92–95]. Yet, this body plan demonstrates considerable diversity in size and shape [7–9,88–91,96]. Additionally, some larvae possess external locomotor specializations, like appendages [10,97–99] and denticles [11,100–101]. These differences likely necessitate modifications in musculature and neural control.

Locomotor diversity

Larval insects locomote using waves of segmental contraction that propagate along the body axis; however, the direction of these waves, bilateral symmetry, and intersegmental coordination can vary [12–18,102–114]. Escape behaviors are particularly diverse, with some taxa exhibiting movements completely distinct from standard peristalsis [15,19–22,107,109,115–117]. Even conserved movements can vary in frequency and context dependence [23,24]. These differences underscore that larval locomotion, while built on a shared foundation, has diversified extensively.

In summary, ecological, morphological, and behavioral comparisons indicate that at least some components — CNS, PNS, or muscles — have evolved. In theory, a change in any component could lead to a profound difference in locomotion. Our knowledge of which components change, when, and how is reviewed below.

Larval insects are a tractable model to investigate locomotor evolution

Herein, we use larval insects to investigate locomotor evolution. First, we address the existing peripheral change hypothesis. Second, we raise an alternative motor neuron bottleneck hypothesis. To evaluate these hypotheses, we focus on cell number and type. Third, we address the connections between the CNS, PNS, and muscles by focusing on neuron morphology and synaptic connectivity.

We approximate the rate of evolutionary change using nested phylogenetic scales (Figure 2). Only a subset of insects possesses true larval stages, the supraorder Holometabola [25,26]. Holometabola represent approximately 80% of all insect species and are concentrated in four major orders: Coleoptera (beetles), Hymenoptera (bees, wasps, and ants), Lepidoptera (butterflies and moths), and Diptera (two-winged flies) [90]. Among these, Drosophila melanogaster (Diptera) is a key model with well-characterized neurons and muscles. We compare traits across the following scales: (1) within the Drosophila genus (~70 million years of divergence [my]), (2) within the Diptera order (~255 my), (3) within the Holometabola supraorder (~345 my), and (4) within the Insecta class (~435 my) [27]. Comparisons beyond Holometabola are made when available, but must be interpreted cautiously, as matching developmental stages is challenging.

In contrast, head and tail segments are specialized for feeding and respiration [13] and have been reviewed elsewhere (e.g. morphology [13,28,118]; PNS [119–122]; musculature [28,29]; CNS [123–125]).

Finally, we do not discuss larval size or stage because cell number and connectivity within larval circuits are generally stable once embryonic development is complete, even as larvae grow [30–32,126]. Additionally, published cell counts and our own observations across taxa reveal no systematic correlation between body size and cell number per hemisegment, suggesting size variation is unlikely to confound cross-taxa comparisons.

The peripheral change hypothesis has been used to explain locomotor evolution

The peripheral change hypothesis suggests that not all components — CNS, PNS, and muscles — evolve at the same rate. CNS neurons are embedded within complex, multifunctional networks, making neuroanatomical changes likely to be deleterious [82–84]. In contrast, peripheral structures, like muscles, have more specialized roles and thus can likely change with fewer negative effects [82–84]. In this review, we extend the meaning of periphery to encompass both muscles and sensory neurons (Figure 1a). Traditionally, the peripheral change hypothesis focused on comparing muscles to the CNS, but peripheral sensory systems — particularly chemosensory systems — provide additional and well- characterized examples of rapid peripheral evolution [3]. These data raise the possibility that similar principles apply to somatosensory neurons in the locomotor system.

The peripheral change hypothesis is also supported by developmental considerations. CNS neurons, PNS sensory neurons, and muscles arise from distinct precursors [33–36,127–128] that initially develop independently, facilitating their independent evolution [127,129–130]. In the developing CNS, neural stem cells generate large lineages of cells, while in the developing periphery, PNS and muscle progenitors generate much smaller sets of cells [33–36,128]. Therefore, compared to the CNS’s precursors, changes to peripheral precursors may be better tolerated as smaller numbers of cells would be disrupted (Figure 1b) [131–133].

In the following subsections, we describe the anatomical evidence in larval insects supporting the peripheral change hypothesis (Figure 1c): First, the PNS varies across insects, with differences even between species within the Drosophila genus. Second, muscles vary within the Diptera order and beyond. Third, in the CNS, motor neurons are relatively conserved within the Holometabola supraorder. Together, these data support the peripheral change hypothesis. However, the total number of neurons varies considerably within Diptera, implying that the CNS as a whole may not be constrained as predicted by the hypothesis.

Within the Drosophila genus, the number of sensory neurons varies

Because locomotion is shaped by environmental stimuli, differences in sensory neurons can significantly influence locomotor behavior. Both the number and morphology of larval sensory neurons vary within the Drosophila genus, Diptera order, and Holometabola supraorder.

Two major types of sensory neurons are found in larval insects. Type I sensory neurons are unipolar cells associated with external sense organs (ESOs) [32] and internal chordotonal organs (CHOs). The neuron-to-sense organ ratio is typically one-to-one, but can be higher [32,36,128]. In most Drosophila species, including D. melanogaster, each abdominal hemisegment comprises 12 ESOs and eight CHOs (Figure 3a) [134]; however, we know of one species that has lost an ESO [24,135]. Within Diptera, ESO numbers range from three to 16, while CHO numbers remain fixed per hemisegment [135–141]. Within Holometabola, even greater variation is observed, with ESO counts ranging from 3 to 26 and CHOs from 2 to 8 per hemisegment [37–40,119,121,138–140,142–145].

Type II sensory neurons lie beneath the cuticle with naked, branching dendrites [36,128]. These neurons have been characterized in fewer taxa, but variation is still evident. Within the Drosophila genus, all 22 Type II neurons per hemisegment appear to be conserved (Figure 3b) [24,135,146]. Within Diptera, counts range from 18 to 33 [135,140,147] and within Holometabola from 13 to 33 [140,148]. In Manduca (Lepidoptera), a second proliferative period of sensory neurogenesis adds a variable number of additional Type II neurons [143].

Even when the number of sensory neurons is the same, subtypes and positions can vary. ESOs are classified into morphological subtypes. These subtypes and relative positions are conserved within the Drosophila genus but vary at broader phylogenetic scales [24,135]. Most larval insect taxa possess mainly hair-like ESOs, but these have been largely replaced by other morphological subtypes, such as papilla, in many dipterans [139]. ESO subtype diversity may reflect ecological pressures for enhanced sensory discrimination [136–137]. Type II sensory neurons also have morphological subtypes [146]. These have rarely been homologized between taxa; however, the Manduca gamma neurons resemble Drosophila class IV neurons in morphology and nociceptive function [41], and the stretch receptor neuron dorsal bipolar dendrite (dbd) in Drosophila has clear homologs across Insecta [42–43,138,140,147,149–157].

Within the Diptera order, the number of muscle cells varies

Muscle contractions drive body movements, forming the mechanical basis of locomotor behavior [44–46,110]. Consequently, any change in musculature could alter locomotion. Muscles exhibit substantial variation within the Diptera order and the Holometabola supraorder. Complicating direct comparisons, counts inconsistently differentiate individual muscle cells from groups of cells. Nonetheless, variability in muscle number is evident. Drosophila melanogaster larvae have a stereotyped pattern of 30 muscles per abdominal hemisegment, each a single cell (Figure 3c) [127]. Within the Drosophila genus, muscles are conserved in position and number [158–159]. Within Diptera, muscle counts range from 23 to 43 per hemisegment [20,47–49,97–98,140,160–166] and within Holometabola from 23 to 148 [19,37,39,50,97,120,142,157,167–184]. Muscle morphology also varies substantially, but homologies have rarely been inferred.

Within the Holometabola supraorder, the number of motor neurons is conserved; however, within the Diptera order, the number of CNS neurons varies

The insect CNS comprises the central brain, analogous to the cerebrum, and the nerve cord, analogous to the spinal cord. Below, we focus on nerve cord neurons, which are responsible for processing sensory information from midbody sensory neurons and driving midbody muscle contractions. We discuss two types of neurons, defined anatomically as motor neurons with axons innervating muscles and interneurons with axons remaining within the CNS. The peripheral change hypothesis does not distinguish between motor neurons and interneurons (Figure 1b). However, available data suggest that not all nerve cord neurons are equally constrained.

Motor neurons seem to be conserved, with relatively small differences in number within the Holometabola supraorder compared to changes in the musculature. For example, when comparing hemisegments in Drosophila and Manduca (Lepidoptera), there is a difference of five motor neurons, compared to a difference of 20 muscles [51,93–94,170,185–186]. Additionally, some motor neurons, including ventral unpaired median (VUMs), anterior corner cell (aCC), raw prawn (RP) 1–2, and U 1–5 motor neurons, have been homologized across Insecta based on cell body position, marker gene expression, or a combination of both (Figure 3e) [87,94–95,186–193]. A technical caveat is that marker gene expression may overestimate the extent of conservation by overlooking novel or divergent cells; newly accessible genetic and molecular tools can use a larger set of marker genes to identify all motor neuron somata and infer homology [52].

Compared to motor neurons, the CNS comprises many more interneurons about which far less is known. We can infer the number of interneurons by subtracting the number of motor neurons from the total nerve cord neurons. For example, within the Diptera order, Hermetia is estimated to have roughly a third as many total nerve cord neurons as Drosophila, while Aedes has half as many [53–55,194]. If motor neuron counts are similar between these taxa, interneurons must account for this significant difference. Similarly, across Insecta, estimated neuron number for a hemisegment ranges from ~220 in Schistocerca (Orthoptera) to ~350 in Drosophila [95,195–196]. These observations suggest that some interneurons vary significantly, likely an underappreciated source of evolutionary flexibility. There are myriad types of interneurons [51,56], and whether the flexibility in interneuron number owes to more neurons of a given type or more types is an open question. Comprehensively assessing interneuron variability is now possible through techniques like comparative connectomics (e.g. [24,57]).

The motor neuron bottleneck hypothesis is an alternative explanation for the available data

The observation that motor neuron numbers are likely more conserved than interneurons raises an alternative framework for understanding locomotor evolution. Under the motor neuron bottleneck hypothesis (Figure 1b), CNS cell types do not evolve at equal rates. Instead, motor neurons are disproportionately conserved, creating an evolutionary bottleneck that allows sensory neurons and interneurons to evolve semi-independently from muscles. Compared to interneurons, motor neurons are few in number, with minimal redundancy in their outputs. Thus, changes to motor neurons may be particularly deleterious [129], providing a functional justification for their constraint.

The subsections below provide additional support for the motor neuron bottleneck hypothesis based on development. The number of neural stem cells (hereafter, neuroblasts) is conserved across Insecta (Figure 4a). Thus, variation in neuron number must result from differences in neurogenesis or cell death. Neuroblasts generate conserved sets of early-born neurons, including motor neurons, whereas later-born neurons may vary in number or type (Figure 4b). While we present evidence from larval insects, the motor neuron bottleneck hypothesis may apply broadly, as motor neurons are among the earliest-born neurons in both insects and vertebrates [58,87,197–201].

Across Insecta, the number and arrangement of neuroblasts are conserved

During CNS development, neuroblast specification is the earliest stage at which evolutionary differences could occur; yet, neuroblast number and arrangement are remarkably conserved across Insecta. Homologous neuroblasts have been identified by cellular position in Drosophila, Tribolium (Coleoptera), Schistocerca (Orthoptera), and Ctenolepisma (Zygentoma) [59–60,92,197,202–203]. Neuroblast conservation suggests a developmental constraint, or a limitation on the production of phenotypic variation [61,130,204]. Indeed, in Drosophila, mutants that alter large numbers of neuroblasts are lethal [131–133], demonstrating deleterious effects of such a developmental change. Furthermore, in Schistocerca, individually ablated neuroblasts are replaced [203,205], showing that developmental mechanisms buffer the system from change.

Across Insecta, early-born neurons are conserved; however, later-born neurons may vary

As similar neuroblasts generate all nerve cord neurons across Insecta, variation in CNS size and composition must result from differences in neurogenesis or neuronal cell death rather than the addition or elimination of neuroblasts themselves.

During neurogenesis, each neuroblast divides repeatedly to produce a unique lineage of neurons, stereotyped between individuals. Neurons within a lineage each possess a distinct temporal identity (e.g. first-born, second-born, etc), and neurons can be roughly split into early-born and late-born sets [62–65]. Comparative studies suggest that early-born neurons are highly conserved across Insecta. As mentioned above, motor neurons are early-born. Early-born neurons also include conserved interneurons, like those labeled by serotonin or Engrailed [85,94,189,191,197,206–208]. Conservation of early- born neurons may be explained by developmental constraint: Many early-born neurons act as pioneers, providing a scaffold along which later-born neurons project [191,193,205,209–214]. Thus, changes to early- born neurons may be particularly harmful.

Compared to early-born neurons, indirect evidence suggests that later-born neurons may be evolutionarily labile. First, as mentioned above, the total neuron number varies, but early- born neurons are conserved, necessitating changes among late-born neurons. Second, neuroblast temporal and spatial patterning genes, which determine many lineage properties including the number and type of neurons produced [62–63,66–70,92,215–224], differ in expression for approximately half of neuroblasts within the Holometabola supraorder (i.e. between Drosophila and Tribolium (Coleoptera)) [59–60,225]. Because early-born neurons are conserved, such molecular differences likely preferentially affect later-born neurons. Characterizing how patterning gene variation drives neuronal diversity across taxa will require approaches such as comparative single-cell sequencing [53,71].

How relatively conserved motor neurons could connect to more variable cell types

Above, we discussed two hypotheses focused on the number and type of neurons and muscles. However, a key challenge for both hypotheses is understanding how relatively conserved motor neurons connect to variable cell types across taxa. In the following subsections, we address the connections between the CNS, PNS, and musculature, focusing on neuron morphology and synaptic connectivity. Neuronal morphology and synaptic connectivity are traits that are acquired sequentially as neurons mature, with morphogenesis occurring before synaptic connectivity is established [72,226]. Morphology and connectivity can evolve independently of cell number, and of each other to some extent, enabling functional diversification even when neurogenesis is conserved. We review evidence that traits arising during early neuronal maturation — such as major nerves and neuropil regionalization — are constrained, whereas traits arising during later neuronal maturation — like synaptic connectivity — vary. Thus, flexibility in late neuronal maturation may enable conserved motor neurons to connect with more variable cell types.

Across Insecta, morphological traits established during early maturation are conserved

Collectively, the morphogenetic choices made by each neuron shape the organization of nerve cord segments, including nerve routes and neuropil regionalization. Across Insecta, connections between the nerve cord and periphery are funneled through the conserved intersegmental and segmental nerves (Figure 3d) [174,191,209,211,214,227]. These nerves are pioneered by early-born motor neurons and sensory neurons [128,174,191,193,209–214,214]. Within the nerve cord, sensory and motor projections localize to discrete neuropil regions reflecting their peripheral positions and functional modality, a pattern that is conserved across Insecta (Figure 3f) [156,211,228–237].

Within the Diptera order, connectivity established during later maturation can vary

After morphogenesis, each neuron selects synaptic partners from among the available nearby targets and refines synaptic architecture and physiology. Below, we describe what is known about variation in sensory neuron-to-interneuron, motor neuron-to-muscle, and sensory neuron-to-motor neuron connectivity. Comparative data on interneuron-to-motor neuron connectivity are absent. Together, the data are too sparse to draw general conclusions, except to suggest that connectivity can vary across taxa.

Sensory neuron-to-interneuron connectivity varies even within the Drosophila genus: D. santomea class IV sensory neurons synapse onto an additional interneuron compared to D. melanogaster, altering the frequency of an escape behavior in response to noxious stimuli (Figure 4c) [24].

Motor neuron-to-muscle connectivity can also vary. Within the Diptera order, individual neuromuscular synapses onto conserved muscles are present on the same area of the muscle surface, but vary in size, shape, and physiology [159,238–242]. In one case, in Megaselia, there is an additional neuromuscular synapse onto a conserved muscle [240].

Finally, direct connectivity between sensory neurons and motor neurons forms the basis of reflex arcs, which trigger rapid, involuntary movements. Within the Holometabola supraorder, the reflex arc between the sensory neuron dbd and the motor neuron RP2 is conserved (Figure 4c) [73,155,157,243], suggesting reflex arcs may be immutable.

Outlook

Our current opinion is that larval insects are an exceptional model for identifying fundamental principles of locomotor evolution. Larval insects have been instrumental in the discovery of generalizable principles in neuroscience and neurodevelopment [64–66,68,74–75,244]. Herein, we explain how they are uniquely positioned to generate and test hypotheses for the evolution of locomotion, in part because they provide access to development [76]. We articulated two reductionist hypotheses. The peripheral change hypothesis, motivated by the complexity of the CNS, proposes that behavioral diversity arises primarily from changes in muscles and sensory structures. The motor neuron bottleneck hypothesis, informed by neurodevelopment, suggests that motor neurons are more conserved than other cell types. Each hypothesis provides a lens through which to interpret existing data and design future experiments that explore locomotor diversity. Uncovering new principles of locomotor evolution will require systematic efforts to: (1) characterize complete locomotor circuits, including connectivity, within more taxa to allow for direct comparisons across cell types; (2) distinguish interneuron subclasses that may follow distinct evolutionary trajectories; (3) apply sampling strategies that account for evolutionary relatedness and sample the full breadth of larval insect diversity; and (4) integrate development with functional consequences.
