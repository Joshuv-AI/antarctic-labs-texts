Robust and efficient estimation in the presence of a randomly censored covariate
Seong-ho Lee, Brian D. Richardson, Yanyuan Ma, Karen S. Marder, Tanya P. Garcia

Abstract
In Huntington’s disease research, a current goal is to understand how symptoms change prior to a clinical diagnosis. Statistically, this entails modeling symptom severity as a function of the covariate ‘time until diagnosis’, which is often heavily right-censored in observational studies. Existing estimators that handle right-censored covariates have varying statistical efficiency and robustness to misspecified models for nuisance distributions (those of the censored covariate and censoring variable). On one extreme, complete case estimation, which utilizes uncensored data only, is free of nuisance distribution models but discards informative censored observations. On the other extreme, maximum likelihood estimation is maximally efficient but inconsistent when the covariate’s distribution is misspecified. We propose a semiparametric estimator that is robust and efficient. When the nuisance distributions are modeled parametrically, the estimator is doubly robust, i.e., consistent if at least one distribution is correctly specified, and semiparametric efficient if both models are correctly specified. When the nuisance distributions are estimated via nonparametric or machine learning methods, the estimator is consistent and semiparametric efficient. We show empirically that the proposed estimator, implemented in the R package sparcc , has its claimed properties, and we apply it to study Huntington’s disease symptom trajectories using data from the Enroll-HD study.

1
Introduction

Huntington’s disease is a genetic neurodegenerative disease with
progressive cognitive, motor, and functional symptoms

(
Dutra et al., 2020
)
. Unlike most neurodegenerative diseases, its exact
cause is known: a mutation in the Huntingtin gene that contains an
abnormally large number of CAG (cytosine, adenine, guanine) repeats. A
genetic blood test can detect this mutation, and individuals with
sufficient CAG repeats are virtually guaranteed to exhibit symptoms
during their lifetime. This ability to identify, with near certainty, who will develop
Huntington’s disease symptoms offers a scientific
opportunity: in observational studies, individuals with the
gene mutation can be tracked to see how symptom severity changes over time (i.e.,
the symptom trajectory) in the period
before
a clinical
diagnosis. If the symptom trajectory can be accurately estimated
during this period, researchers can test interventions to slow or halt
disease progression before irreparable damage is done

(
Scahill et al., 2020
)
.

However, estimating the symptom trajectory in
the period before a diagnosis is challenging because Huntington’s
disease progresses slowly. Patient decline spans decades

(
Dutra et al., 2020
)
, so observational studies often end before all
at-risk individuals are diagnosed. In such cases, an individual’s time
of diagnosis is not observed, but is known to be some time after they exit the
study, and thus their time until diagnosis is
right-censored, i.e., known to be greater than some observed
value. The challenge then is estimating the symptom trajectory as a
function of time until diagnosis, when that
time until diagnosis
is a right-censored covariate
.

Currently, there are a range of estimators that address the right-censored
covariate problem
(
Lotspeich et al., 2024
)
. These estimators target
parameters that define the symptom trajectory, and each estimator
depends to varying degrees on the distributions of the right-censored
covariate (e.g., time until diagnosis) and censoring variable (e.g.,
time until study exit). These distributions are not of direct
interest—they are nuisance distributions—but they impact the
estimators’ consistency and statistical efficiency (i.e.,
variance). The complete case estimator only uses data from uncensored
individuals and the inverse probability weighting estimator assigns
weights to those individuals to make the sample representative of the
entire population–censored or not
(
Ashner & Garcia, 2023
;
Matsouaka & Atem, 2020
)
. Both estimators are maximally robust in the
sense that their consistency does not depend on correctly specified models for
nuisance distributions
(
Vazquez et al., 2024
)
, but relatively
inefficient because they discard data from censored individuals.

Estimators that make more direct use of censored observations are: (i) the augmented complete
case and augmented inverse probability weighting estimators, which
modify their respective original estimators to increase efficiency
(
Ahn et al., 2018
;
Vazquez et al., 2024
)
; (ii)
the imputation estimator, which replaces censored covariate values
with imputed values
(
Bernhardt et al., 2015
;
Atem & Matsouaka, 2017
)
;
and (iii) the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE), which maximizes the
likelihood of the observed data accounting for the right-censored
covariate
(
Kong et al., 2018
;
Chen et al., 2022
)
. By making more direct use of
available data, these estimators gain efficiency over the complete
case and inverse probability weighting estimators, with the MLE
achieving maximal efficiency. That efficiency gain, however, requires nuisance distributions to be correctly specified to ensure
consistency, making the estimators less robust. The two augmented
estimators require correct specification of the censoring
distribution, while the imputation estimator and MLE require correct
specification of the censored covariate distribution; under incorrect specifications, these estimators are inconsistent.
(
Bernhardt et al., 2015
;
Vazquez et al., 2024
)
.

Existing solutions that aim to compromise less efficiency and robustness include adaptations to these estimators, where the nuisance distributions are modeled flexibly, e.g., with a semiparametric model (as in
Kong et al. (2018)
;
Atem et al. (2019)
;
Chen et al. (2022)
) or a nonparametric model (as in

Calle & Gómez (2005)
). Yet, semiparametric and nonparametric models
can still be wrong. These models are not free of assumptions; e.g.,
the semiparametric Cox model imposes a proportional hazards
assumption, and nonparametric models require unknown functions to be
sufficiently smooth. When these assumptions do not hold, even flexible
semiparametric or nonparametric models will be wrong, and, if wrong,
will result in inconsistent estimators of the symptom trajectory.

These points highlight the opportunity for an estimator that strikes a
better balance between robustness and efficiency. In this paper, we address
that need by proposing the “
S
emi
pa
rametric

R
ight-
C
ensored
C
ovariate” (SPARCC) estimator. We develop this estimator by applying the general theory of semiparametric models to the specific problem of randomly right-censored covariates and using a geometric approach to construct influence functions
(
Bickel et al., 1993
;
Tsiatis, 2006
)
. The SPARCC
estimator is doubly robust: it remains consistent if we
misspecify parametric models for the distribution of either the censored covariate
or the censoring variable. If we are not willing to specify parametric models for the
nuisance distributions, we can instead use standard nonparametric
or machine learning methods for estimating the nuisance
distributions, and the SPARCC estimator
remains consistent. Under correct parametric or nonparametric models
for the nuisance distributions, the SPARCC estimator achieves the
semiparametric efficiency bound, making it maximally efficient among
all consistent estimators under the same semiparametric model assumptions.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. The SPARCC estimator
is derived in Section
2
and its implementation is
shown in Section
3
. The empirical performance of the
SPARCC estimator, which is available in the R package

sparcc
, is compared to that of existing estimators in
Section
4
. In
Section
5
, the estimator is then applied to data from Enroll-HD, an
observational study of Huntington’s disease, to assess symptom
trajectories prior to diagnosis. Finally, a discussion is provided in
Section
6
.

2
Deriving the SPARCC estimator

2.1
Notation and framework

Consider a general regression model
f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})
that describes
the relationship between an outcome
Y
and covariates
X
and
{\mathbf{Z}}
,
using the
p
-dimensional parameter
{\bm{\beta}}
. Here and throughout,
f

denotes (conditional) probability density (or mass) functions
described by the subindices. The main challenge when estimating
{\bm{\beta}}

is that, whereas
{\mathbf{Z}}
is fully observed,
X
is randomly
right-censored. Instead of observing
X
directly, we observe
W=\min(X,C)
and
\Delta=I(X\leq C)
, where
C
is a random
censoring variable and
\Delta
indicates whether
X
is observed. Thus,
the observed data are
(Y,W,\Delta,{\mathbf{Z}})
. In the context of
Huntington’s disease, for example,
Y
is symptom severity,
X
is
time until diagnosis,
{\mathbf{Z}}
contains other patient variables, and
C
is
time until study exit.

We will derive the SPARCC estimator under a common assumption known as
noninformative covariate censoring, meaning
C
is independent of

(Y,X)
given
{\mathbf{Z}}
, or, equivalently, that
C\perp\!\!\!\perp Y\mid{\mathbf{Z}}
and

C\perp\!\!\!\perp X\mid{\mathbf{Z}}
, where
\perp\!\!\!\perp
denotes independence. This
assumption may be valid in Huntington’s disease studies where time
until diagnosis is censored by time until study exit, provided participants exit the study for administrative reasons not related to their health outcomes. Under the noninformative censoring assumption, the likelihood for the observed data

(Y,W,\Delta,{\mathbf{Z}})
can be constructed by integrating over the support
of the full data
(Y,X,C,{\mathbf{Z}})
, noting that when
\Delta=1
, we have

X\leq C
, and when
\Delta=0
, we have
X>C
. This likelihood is

\displaystyle f_{Y,W,\Delta,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,w,\delta,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left\{f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,w,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})f_{X|{\mathbf{Z}}}(w,{\mathbf{z}})\int_{w}^{\infty}f_{C|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c,{\mathbf{z}})dc\right\}^{\delta}

(1)

\displaystyle\times\left\{\int_{w}^{\infty}f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})f_{X|{\mathbf{Z}}}(x,{\mathbf{z}})dxf_{C|{\mathbf{Z}}}(w,{\mathbf{z}})\right\}^{1-\delta}f_{{\mathbf{Z}}}({\mathbf{z}}).

Lemma
1

All unknown components in (
1
), i.e.,
f_{X|{\mathbf{Z}}}
,
f_{C|{\mathbf{Z}}}
,
f_{{\mathbf{Z}}}
, and
{\bm{\beta}}
, are identifiable.

The proof of identifiability in Lemma
1
is in
Section
S.1
(Supplementary
Material). Having identifiability ensures there is a well-defined and
unique estimator for
{\bm{\beta}}
even without specifying the distributions

f_{X|{\mathbf{Z}}}
,
f_{C|{\mathbf{Z}}}
, or
f_{{\mathbf{Z}}}
a priori. This result is especially
appealing, as these distributions are often unknown in practice.

2.2
The semiparametric method

We treat the distributions
f_{X|{\mathbf{Z}}}
,
f_{C|{\mathbf{Z}}}
, and
f_{{\mathbf{Z}}}
as
infinite-dimensional nuisance parameters that are not restricted to a
specific parametric form. To reflect this fact, we write them as

\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})\equiv f_{X|{\mathbf{Z}}}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
,
\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})\equiv f_{C|{\mathbf{Z}}}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
, and
\eta_{3}({\mathbf{z}})\equiv f_{{\mathbf{Z}}}({\mathbf{z}})
. We then aim
to construct the robust and efficient
SPARCC estimator for
{\bm{\beta}}
.

Under this framework, we consider estimators that are roots of
p
-dimensional and mean-zero estimating functions of
(Y,W,\Delta,{\mathbf{Z}})
and
{\bm{\beta}}
.
Since any given estimating function
{\mathbf{g}}
has mean zero, the estimator
of
{\bm{\beta}}
obtained by solving
\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{g}}(y_{i},w_{i},\delta_{i},{\mathbf{z}}_{i},{\bm{\beta}},\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3})={\mathbf{0}}
is guaranteed
to be consistent under mild regularity conditions
(
Foutz, 1977
)
.
The set of all estimating functions makes up a Hilbert space
{\cal H}
,
with the covariance inner product defined as

{\langle}{\mathbf{h}}_{1},{\mathbf{h}}_{2}{\rangle}=\mathrm{E}({\mathbf{h}}_{1}^{\rm T}{\mathbf{h}}_{2})
. The corresponding notion of
orthogonality is then
{\mathbf{h}}_{1}\perp{\mathbf{h}}_{2}
if and only if
{\langle}{\mathbf{h}}_{1},{\mathbf{h}}_{2}{\rangle}=0
.
Using this definition of orthogonality, we can decompose
{\cal H}
into two
key orthogonal subspaces that lead us to the SPARCC estimator. The first is the
nuisance tangent space,
\Lambda
, which is formed by considering all
possible parametric models for the nuisance distributions, deriving
their associated score vectors, then taking the span of those score
vectors and their limits. The second subspace is the orthogonal
complement,
\Lambda^{\perp}
, which is the space of estimating
functions orthogonal to
\Lambda
. We prove in Sections

S.2
and
S.3

(Supplementary Material) that for our model in (
1
), the
nuisance tangent space and its orthogonal complement are as given
in Proposition
1
.

Proposition
1

The nuisance tangent space is
\Lambda\equiv\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{2}\oplus\Lambda_{3}
, where
\Lambda_{1}
,
\Lambda_{2}
,
and
\Lambda_{3}
are the nuisance tangent spaces for
\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
,

\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
, and
\eta_{3}({\mathbf{z}})
, respectively. More exactly,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left[\delta{\mathbf{a}}_{1}(w,{\mathbf{z}})+(1-\delta)\frac{\mathrm{E}\{\mathrm{I}(X>w){\mathbf{a}}_{1}(X,{\mathbf{z}})\mid y,{\mathbf{z}}\}}{\mathrm{E}\{\mathrm{I}(X>w)\mid y,{\mathbf{z}}\}}:\mathrm{E}\{{\mathbf{a}}_{1}(X,{\mathbf{z}})\mid{\mathbf{z}}\}={\mathbf{0}}\right],

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left[\delta\frac{\mathrm{E}\{\mathrm{I}(C\geq w){\mathbf{a}}_{2}(C,{\mathbf{z}})\mid{\mathbf{z}}\}}{\mathrm{E}\{\mathrm{I}(C\geq w)\mid{\mathbf{z}}\}}+(1-\delta){\mathbf{a}}_{2}(w,{\mathbf{z}}):\mathrm{E}\{{\mathbf{a}}_{2}(C,{\mathbf{z}})\mid{\mathbf{z}}\}={\mathbf{0}}\right],

\displaystyle\Lambda_{3}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle[{\mathbf{a}}_{3}({\mathbf{z}}):\mathrm{E}\{{\mathbf{a}}_{3}({\mathbf{Z}})\}={\mathbf{0}}].

Proposition
2

The orthogonal complement is the intersection of
\Lambda_{1}^{\perp}
,
\Lambda_{2}^{\perp}
,
\Lambda_{3}^{\perp}
, which can be explicitly written as

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\perp}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left[{\mathbf{g}}(y,w,\delta,{\mathbf{z}})=\delta{\mathbf{g}}_{1}(y,w,{\mathbf{z}})+(1-\delta){\mathbf{g}}_{0}(y,w,{\mathbf{z}}):\right.

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\{\mathrm{I}(X\leq c){\mathbf{g}}_{1}(Y,X,{\mathbf{z}})+\mathrm{I}(X>c){\mathbf{g}}_{0}(Y,c,{\mathbf{z}})\mid c,{\mathbf{z}}\}={\mathbf{0}},

\displaystyle\left.\mathrm{E}\{\mathrm{I}(x\leq C){\mathbf{g}}_{1}(Y,x,{\mathbf{z}})+\mathrm{I}(x>C){\mathbf{g}}_{0}(Y,C,{\mathbf{z}})\mid x,{\mathbf{z}}\}={\mathbf{0}}\right].

Intuitively, since
\Lambda
includes estimating functions
correlated with nuisance distribution score vectors, estimators based
on these functions will generally be affected by misspecification of
nuisance distributions.
In contrast, the orthogonality of
\Lambda^{\perp}
to
\Lambda

implies that nuisance distributions minimally impact the estimating
functions in
\Lambda^{\perp}

(
Bickel et al., 1993
;
Tsiatis, 2006
)
. We thus build our SPARCC estimator from
an estimating function in
\Lambda^{\perp}
, in particular, the
efficient score vector,
{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}\in\Lambda^{\perp}
, which is the orthogonal projection of the score vector

{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{\beta}}(y,w,\delta,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})=\partial\log f_{Y,W,\Delta,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,w,\delta,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})/\partial{\bm{\beta}}
onto

\Lambda^{\perp}
. The SPARCC estimator is then the solution to

\sum_{i}{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}(y_{i},w_{i},\delta_{i},{\mathbf{z}}_{i},{\bm{\beta}})={\mathbf{0}}
. While all
functions in
\Lambda^{\perp}
are minimally impacted by
misspecification of nuisance distributions,
{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}
results in an
estimator that is maximally efficient. The explicit form of
{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}
is given in
Proposition
3
.

Proposition
3

The efficient score vector for
{\bm{\beta}}
is

\displaystyle{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}(y,w,\delta,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\delta\{{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{\beta}}^{\rm F}(y,w,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})-{\mathbf{a}}(w,z,{\bm{\beta}})\}

(2)

\displaystyle+(1-\delta)\frac{\mathrm{E}_{1}[\mathrm{I}(X>w)\{{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{\beta}}^{\rm F}(y,X,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})-{\mathbf{a}}(X,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\}\mid y,{\mathbf{z}}]}{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>w)\mid y,{\mathbf{z}}\}},

where
{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{\beta}}^{\rm F}(y,x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\equiv\partial\log f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})/\partial{\bm{\beta}}
, and

{\mathbf{a}}(x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{2}\{\mathrm{I}(x\leq C)\mid{\mathbf{z}}\}{\mathbf{a}}(x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})+\mathrm{E}_{2}\left[\mathrm{I}(x>C)\frac{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>C){\mathbf{a}}(X,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\mid Y,C,{\mathbf{z}}\}}{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>C)\mid Y,C,{\mathbf{z}}\}}\mid x,{\mathbf{z}}\right]

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{2}\left[\mathrm{I}(x>C)\frac{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>C){\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{\beta}}^{\rm F}(Y,X,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\mid Y,C,{\mathbf{z}}\}}{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>C)\mid Y,C,{\mathbf{z}}\}}\mid x,{\mathbf{z}}\right].

Here,
\mathrm{E}_{1},\mathrm{E}_{2}
denote expectations with respect to

\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}}),\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
, respectively.

The proof of Proposition
3
is in Section

S.4
(Supplementary Material).
There are two important observations we can make about
{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}
.
The first is that computing
{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}
requires solving the integral equation in
(
3
) for
{\mathbf{a}}(x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})
and evaluating multiple
integrals that may have no closed form:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>c){\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{\beta}}^{\rm F}(y,X,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\mid y,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}\}}{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>c)\mid y,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int\mathrm{I}(x>c){\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{\beta}}^{\rm F}(y,x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})dx}{\int\mathrm{I}(x>c)f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})dx},

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>c){\mathbf{a}}(X,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\mid y,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}\}}{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>c)\mid y,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int\mathrm{I}(x>c){\mathbf{a}}(x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})dx}{\int\mathrm{I}(x>c)f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})dx},

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{2}\{\mathrm{I}(x\leq C)\mid{\mathbf{z}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{I}(x\leq c)\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})dc,

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{2}\left\{\mathrm{I}(x>C)\frac{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>C){\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{\beta}}^{\rm F}(Y,X,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\mid Y,C,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}\}}{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>C)\mid Y,C,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}\}}\mid x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{I}(x>c)\frac{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>c){\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{\beta}}^{\rm F}(y,X,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\mid y,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}\}}{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>c)\mid y,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}\}}f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})dydc,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{2}\left\{\mathrm{I}(x>C)\frac{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>C){\mathbf{a}}(X,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\mid Y,C,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}\}}{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>C)\mid Y,C,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}\}}\mid x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{I}(x>c)\frac{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>c){\mathbf{a}}(X,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\mid y,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}\}}{\mathrm{E}_{1}\{\mathrm{I}(X>c)\mid y,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}\}}f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})dydc.

These challenges can be resolved with a numerical approach. Since we
ultimately solve for
{\bm{\beta}}
using
\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}(y_{i},w_{i},\delta_{i},{\mathbf{z}}_{i},{\bm{\beta}})={\mathbf{0}}
, we only need to construct the
function
{\mathbf{a}}(x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})
at observed values

{\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{z}}_{i}~(i=1,\cdots,n)
. For each fixed
{\mathbf{z}}_{i}
, the integral equation
in (
3
) is a univariate Fredholm integral equation of
the second kind, which we solve using an adaptation of the algorithm
from
Atkinson (1976)
. This adaptation involves solving a
linear system through discretizing (
3
) at chosen
values for
x
and numerically approximating the integrals through
quadrature rules, e.g., Gauss-Hermite quadrature or the trapezoidal
rule.
Details of this numerical procedure are given in Section

S.5
(Supplementary Material). All other integrals
in (
3
) can be similarly approximated using quadrature
rules.

The second observation is that
{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}
involves
\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and

\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
, but not
\eta_{3}({\mathbf{z}})
. Therefore, to estimate
{\bm{\beta}}
, we
need to specify
\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and
\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
, but not

\eta_{3}({\mathbf{z}})
. In the next section, we demonstrate how
{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}
, and
the consistency and efficiency of the resulting SPARCC estimator, are
impacted by modeling choices for
\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and
\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
.

3
Implementing the SPARCC estimator

We propose and analyze two options for specifying the nuisance
distributions
\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and
\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
: parametric working
models and nonparametric/machine learning methods.

3.1
Parametric working models

One way to specify
\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and
\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
is with
parametric working models,
\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and

\eta_{2}^{\dagger}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
, which depend on finite-dimensional parameters

{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},{\bm{\alpha}}_{2}
that can be known or unknown. The name ‘working model’
reflects the fact that these models may not capture the true
data-generating process. We are free to choose any working models, so
long as they are valid conditional density functions given
{\mathbf{z}}
(i.e.,
are nonnegative and integrate to one). With working models

\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and
\eta_{2}^{\dagger}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
, the functions in
Proposition
3
are changed to
\mathrm{E}_{1}^{*},\mathrm{E}_{2}^{\dagger}
,

{\mathbf{a}}^{*\dagger}
, and
{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}^{*\dagger}
to reflect their dependency
on those working models. In short, using parametric working models to
obtain the SPARCC estimator involves the following steps in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

SPARCC estimator with parametric working models

Input
: data
\{(y_{i},w_{i},\delta_{i},{\mathbf{z}}_{i}):i=1,\cdots,n\}
.

do

(a) adopt working models for
\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}}),\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
, denoted by
\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}),\eta_{2}^{\dagger}(c,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\alpha}}_{2})
and, if needed, estimate the unknown parameters
{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},{\bm{\alpha}}_{2}
,

(b) compute
\mathrm{E}_{1}^{*}
and
\mathrm{E}_{2}^{\dagger}
with respect to
\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and
\eta_{2}^{\dagger}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
in Proposition
3
,

(c) obtain
{\mathbf{a}}^{*\dagger}(x,{\mathbf{z}}_{i},{\bm{\beta}})
by solving (
3
) at
{\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{z}}_{i}~(i=1,\cdots,n)
,

(d) obtain
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
by solving
\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}^{*\dagger}(y_{i},w_{i},\delta_{i},{\mathbf{z}}_{i},{\bm{\beta}})={\mathbf{0}}
with respect to
{\bm{\beta}}
.

Output
:
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
.

3.1.1
Constructing parametric working models

There are many possible parametric working models we can use for

\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and
\eta_{2}^{\dagger}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
. For example, because
X

(or
C
) is right-censored, we can posit
\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\alpha}}_{1})
(or

\eta_{2}^{\dagger}(c,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\alpha}}_{2})
) as a log-normal distribution
corresponding to an accelerated failure time model, where
X
(or
C
)
is the response and
{\mathbf{Z}}
is the predictor. Another option is a
generalized linear model where
X
(or
C
) is the response and
{\mathbf{Z}}

is the predictor.

If the parametric working models contain unknown parameters

{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},{\bm{\alpha}}_{2}
, the next step is to estimate those parameters and
substitute them with their estimators
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}
and
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{2}
. In
general, these estimators may depend on
{\bm{\beta}}
as well. For example,
the MLEs for
{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}({\bm{\beta}})

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{2}

\displaystyle\argmax_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\delta_{i}\log\int_{w_{i}}^{\infty}\eta_{2}^{\dagger}(c,{\mathbf{z}}_{i},{\bm{\alpha}}_{2})dc+(1-\delta_{i})\log\eta_{2}^{\dagger}(w_{i},{\mathbf{z}}_{i},{\bm{\alpha}}_{2})\right\}.

(4)

When we estimate

and

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\argmax_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\delta_{i}\log\eta_{1}^{*}(w_{i},{\mathbf{z}}_{i},{\bm{\alpha}}_{1})+(1-\delta_{i})\log\int_{w_{i}}^{\infty}\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\mathbf{z}}_{i},{\bm{\alpha}}_{1})dx\right\},

Standard statistical results show that whether or not the parametric
working models are correctly specified,
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}({\bm{\beta}})

\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{2}

and

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
when the working models are possibly
misspecified.

3.1.2
Theoretical properties with parametric working models

question is: how does our choice of working models impact the

SPARCC estimator is doubly robust: it remains consistent when either

\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})

These efficiency results combined with double robustness show that the

\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\mathbf{z}})

\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
or
\eta_{2}^{\dagger}(c,{\mathbf{z}})

3.2
Nonparametric/machine learning methods

While the double robustness of the SPARCC estimator provides two
safety nets, we still require at least one of the parametric working
models
\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\mathbf{z}}),\eta_{2}^{\dagger}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
to be correctly
specified. There may be scenarios where neither can be correctly
specified, and, for these scenarios, we propose an implementation that
uses nonparametric/machine learning methods, completely bypassing
the challenge of correct parametric specification.

The implementation generally proceeds in the same way as with
parametric working models (Section
3.1.1
), except that now
there is dependency on some potentially data-driven

\widehat{\eta}_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}}),\widehat{\eta}_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
. To emphasize this dependency, we
write
\mathrm{E}_{1}
,
\mathrm{E}_{2}
,
{\mathbf{a}}
, and
{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}
as
\widehat{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
,
\widehat{\mathrm{E}}_{2}
,

\widehat{\mathbf{a}}(x,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})
, and
{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}(y,w,\delta,{\mathbf{z}},\widehat{\mathrm{E}}_{1},\widehat{\mathrm{E}}_{2},{\bm{\beta}})
,
respectively. The use of nonparametric/machine learning methods to obtain the SPARCC estimator is summarized in Algorithm
2
.

Algorithm 2

SPARCC estimator with nonparametric/machine learning methods

Input
: data
\{(y_{i},w_{i},\delta_{i},{\mathbf{z}}_{i}):i=1,\cdots,n\}
.

do

(a) compute
\widehat{\mathrm{E}}_{1},\widehat{\mathrm{E}}_{2}
with respect to

\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}}),\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})

via nonparametric/machine learning methods,

(b) obtain
\widehat{\mathbf{a}}(x,{\mathbf{z}}_{i},{\bm{\beta}})
by solving (
3
) at
{\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{z}}_{i}~(i=1,\cdots,n)
,

(c) obtain
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
by solving
\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}(y_{i},w_{i},\delta_{i},{\mathbf{z}}_{i},\widehat{\mathrm{E}}_{1},\widehat{\mathrm{E}}_{2},{\bm{\beta}})={\mathbf{0}}
with respect to
{\bm{\beta}}
.

Output
:
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
.

3.2.1
Constructing nonparametric/machine learning estimators

Since
X
and
C
are right-censored variables, candidate
estimators should adjust for this censoring. Many such
estimators exist, among which we highlight a few. When

{\mathbf{z}}
is low-dimensional
(i.e., the number of variables is small), we can use nonparametric
kernel-based estimators like the extension of the Kaplan-Meier
estimator from
Beran (1981)
and

Dabrowska (1987)
, a recursive kernel-based estimator from

Khardani & Semmar (2014)
, and a presmoothed kernel density
estimator from
Cao & Jácome (2004)
. When
{\mathbf{z}}
is
high-dimensional, possible methods include a
regression-tree estimator
(
Molinaro et al., 2004
)
, a neural network-based estimator
(
Ren et al., 2019
)
, and a Laplace approximation for
nonparametric double additive location-scale models

(
Lambert, 2021
)
, among others. Regardless of the estimator adopted,
the estimators
\widehat{\eta}_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and

\widehat{\eta}_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
need to be consistent at the rate of
o_{p}(n^{-1/4})

to meet the assumptions of Theorem
2

(Section
3.2.2
). The required convergence rate can be
achieved by diverse nonparametric and machine learning estimators

(
Chernozhukov et al., 2018
)
, shown by, e.g.,

Wager & Walther (2015)
for a class of regression trees and random
forests, and
Chen & White (1999)
for a class of neural network
models.

For the simulation study and real data application in this paper,
where
{\mathbf{z}}
is low-dimensional, we model
\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and

\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
using a B-spline density estimator that accounts for
right-censored data. We model
\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})={\bm{\alpha}}_{1}({\mathbf{z}})^{{}^{\rm T}}\mathbf{B}(x)
,
where
\mathbf{B}(\cdot)=\{B_{1}(\cdot),\dots,B_{m}(\cdot)\}^{{}^{\rm T}}
are
positive, smooth, piece-wise polynomial functions and
{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}({\mathbf{z}})=\{\alpha_{11}({\mathbf{z}}),\dots,\alpha_{1m}({\mathbf{z}})\}^{{}^{\rm T}}
are unknown
coefficients. A similar model is used for
\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
. To ensure
that these coefficients lead to a valid density, i.e., a non-negative
function that integrates to one,
{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}({\mathbf{z}})
is constrained to have
positive components satisfying
{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}({\mathbf{z}})^{{}^{\rm T}}\int\mathbf{B}(x)dx=1
.
The coefficients
{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}({\mathbf{z}})
are then chosen to maximize the
log-likelihood of
(W,\Delta)
given
{\mathbf{Z}}={\mathbf{z}}
. That is,

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}({\mathbf{z}})=\argmax_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}({\mathbf{z}})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I({\mathbf{z}}_{i}={\mathbf{z}})\left[\delta_{i}\log\{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}({\mathbf{z}})^{{}^{\rm T}}\mathbf{B}(w_{i})\}+(1-\delta_{i})\log\int_{w_{i}}^{\infty}{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}({\mathbf{z}})^{{}^{\rm T}}\mathbf{B}(x)dx\right].

3.2.2
Theoretical properties with nonparametric/machine
learning estimators

Whereas parametric working models
\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and

\eta_{2}^{\dagger}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
will not converge to the truth if the models
are misspecified,
nonparametric estimators
\widehat{\eta}_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and
\widehat{\eta}_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
are
guaranteed to converge to the truth under mild conditions. Assuming
the convergence happens at the rate of
o_{p}(n^{-1/4})
, we have
the following asymptotic results for
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
, the SPARCC estimator
from Algorithm
2
. We assume regularity conditions

(B1)
-
(B4)
, which are listed and justified in
Section
S.10
(Supplementary Material).

Theorem
2

Assume conditions
(B1)
-
(B4)
,

\sup_{x,{\mathbf{z}}}|(\widehat{\eta}_{1}-\eta_{1})(x,{\mathbf{z}})|=o_{p}(n^{-1/4})
, and

\sup_{c,{\mathbf{z}}}|(\widehat{\eta}_{2}-\eta_{2})(c,{\mathbf{z}})|=o_{p}(n^{-1/4})
. Then,
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}

is a semiparametric efficient estimator of
{\bm{\beta}}_{0}
, i.e.,

\sqrt{n}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-{\bm{\beta}}_{0})\to\hbox{Normal}({\mathbf{0}},[\mathrm{E}\{{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}^{\otimes 2}(Y,W,\Delta,{\mathbf{Z}},{\bm{\beta}}_{0})\}]^{-1})
in
distribution as
n\to\infty
.

The proof of Theorem
2
is in Section

S.12
(Supplementary Material). The asymptotic
properties in Theorem
2
establish the consistency and
efficiency of the SPARCC estimator
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
when using nonparametric
models
\widehat{\eta}_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and
\widehat{\eta}_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
. Even though we allow

\widehat{\eta}_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}}),\widehat{\eta}_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
to converge at the slower
convergence rate of
o_{p}(n^{-1/4})
, the SPARCC estimator
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
is
consistent at the faster root-
n
convergence rate.
Intuitively, the convergence rate follows because the SPARCC estimator is built
using the efficient score vector
{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}
, which diminishes the
leading order error from
\widehat{\eta}_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and
\widehat{\eta}_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
.

The SPARCC estimator
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
is also semiparametric efficient. Recall
that the SPARCC estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
using parametric working models
(Section
3.1.2
) achieves semiparametric efficiency, but
only when both nuisance distribution models are correctly
specified. Theorem
2
presents a stronger result: using
nonparametric models does not impact the asymptotic variance of

\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
. In other words, the asymptotic variance of
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
is
equivalent to that of an estimator constructed with the true but
unknown nuisance distributions. This result eliminates the need to
choose a correct parametric working model for
\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
or

\eta_{2}^{\dagger}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
. As long as the nonparametric models converge
at a rate of
o_{p}(n^{-1/4})
, which many do (Section

3.2.1
), the SPARCC estimator is both consistent and
semiparametric efficient.

4
Simulation study

We assessed the finite sample robustness and efficiency of the SPARCC
estimator and compared the results against those of other estimators
through a simulation study. For this study,
n=8000
independent
copies of the complete data
(Y,X,C,Z)
were generated. Here,
Z\sim\textrm{Bernoulli}(0.5)
is a binary fully observed covariate,

X
is a censored covariate with
X|Z\sim\textrm{beta}(\alpha_{11}+\alpha_{12}Z,\alpha_{13}+\alpha_{14}Z)
,
C
is the censoring
variable with
C|Z\sim\textrm{beta}(\alpha_{21}+\alpha_{22}Z,\alpha_{23}+\alpha_{24}Z)
, and
Y
is the outcome with
Y|X,Z\sim\hbox{Normal}(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}X+\beta_{2}Z,\sigma^{2})
. From the complete
data
(Y,X,C,Z)
, the observed data
(Y,W,\Delta,Z)
were
constructed using
W=\min(X,C)
and
\Delta=I(X\leq C)
. The
parameter
{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}=(\alpha_{11},\alpha_{12},\alpha_{13},\alpha_{14})^{\rm T}=(1.5,1,2.5,-1)^{\rm T}
characterizing
f_{X|Z}

was held constant across all simulations, whereas the parameter

{\bm{\alpha}}_{2}=(\alpha_{21},\alpha_{22},\alpha_{23},\alpha_{24})^{\rm T}

for
f_{C|Z}
was varied to yield a desired censoring
proportion
q=\textrm{P}(X>C)\in(0,1)
. The parameter of interest
was fixed at
{\bm{\beta}}=(\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\log\sigma^{2})^{\rm T}=(1,10,2,0)^{\rm T}
.

Four estimators were considered in our study: (i) the SPARCC
estimator, (ii) the MLE, (iii) the complete case estimator, and (iv)
the oracle estimator (which makes use of the unobserved
X
for each
subject). We did not consider the inverse probability weighting
estimator because though it is as robust as the complete case estimator, it
is less efficient
(
Vazquez et al., 2024
)
. We also did not consider an
imputation estimator, as it would be no more robust than the MLE and
less efficient.

The SPARCC estimator uses both nuisance distributions
\eta_{1}
and

\eta_{2}
, which were specified in one of five ways: using parametric
working models with (i) both
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
correctly
specified, (ii) only
\eta_{1}
correctly specified, (iii) only
\eta_{2}

correctly specified, or (iv) neither
\eta_{1}
nor
\eta_{2}
correctly
specified; or (v) using nonparametric models. Scenarios (i), (ii),
(iii), and (v) appropriately model nuisance distributions in the sense
that they lead to consistent estimation of
{\bm{\beta}}
. The MLE only
uses the nuisance distribution
\eta_{1}
, which was specified in
one of three ways: using (i) a correct parametric working model, (ii)
an incorrect parametric working model, or (iii) a nonparametric
model. The complete case and oracle estimators do not use nuisance
distributions. To misspecify
\eta_{1}
, we assumed
X|Z\sim\textrm{beta}(\alpha_{11}^{*},\alpha_{12}^{*})
; the other nuisance
distribution
f_{C|Z}
was similarly misspecified as

\textrm{beta}(\alpha_{21}^{\dagger},\alpha_{22}^{\dagger})
. Here, the
superscripts
∗
and
†
indicate that these posited
distributions are incorrect. Nonparametric models for
\eta_{1}
and

\eta_{2}
used the B-spline estimator described in Section

3.2.1
. The MLE with the nonparametric model for
\eta_{1}

can be viewed as a more robust version of the estimators by

Kong & Nan (2016)
and
Chen et al. (2022)
that model

\eta_{1}
semiparametrically. The oracle estimator was implemented only
as a benchmark, as it utilizes the censored
X
, which is not available
in practice.

The variance of each estimator was estimated using the empirical
sandwich technique
(
Stefanski & Boos, 2002
)
. For the MLE with the
parametric or B-spline
\eta_{1}
model, its asymptotic distribution
depends on uncertainty in the estimation of
{\bm{\alpha}}
. To account for this
dependence, the sandwich variance estimator used the stacked
estimating functions
\partial\log f_{W,\Delta|Z}(w,\delta,z,{\bm{\alpha}})/\partial{\bm{\alpha}}
for the
X|Z
model and

{\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{\beta}}(y,w,\delta,z,{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\alpha}})
for the outcome model.

When we used correct parametric working models for the nuisance
distributions, all estimators had minimal bias (
\leq 0.021
in
absolute value; Table

), but varying efficiency. The
oracle estimator, which is by
definition unaffected by censoring, had the lowest empirical standard
errors among all estimators regardless of censoring proportion

q
. The MLE followed with the next lowest empirical standard errors,
then the SPARCC estimator, and finally the complete case estimator
(Figure
1
). This ordering highlights the SPARCC
estimator’s semiparametric efficiency: it is higher than that of the
complete case estimator, which loses efficiency by discarding
incomplete cases, but somewhat lower than the MLE’s maximal parametric
efficiency.

When we used an incorrect parametric working model for
\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
,
the MLE had extreme bias and coverage probabilities of
zero. Modeling
\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
nonparametrically still resulted in
moderate bias of the MLE and coverage probability of the corresponding confidence interval significantly below the 95% nominal level. Also
in this setting, the variance estimator for the MLE had several large
outliers, leading to an average estimated standard error much larger
than the empirical standard error (for
q=0.8
, the median estimated standard error of 2.64 was closer than the mean estimated standard error of 4.14 to the empirical mean of 2.79). In contrast, the SPARCC estimator
had low bias (
\leq 0.045
in absolute value), had smaller empirical
standard errors than the complete case estimator, had near-matching
empirical and average estimated standard errors, and its confidence
interval achieved close to the nominal 95% coverage probability in a
wide range of settings. These settings include when either one of

\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
or
\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
had a correct parametric
specification, or when both
\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and
\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
were

performed reasonably well even with incorrect parametric working
models for both
\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
and
\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
. However, there are
no theoretical guarantees for such performance in general.

All results suggest that the SPARCC estimator strikes a balance between
robustness and efficiency. It is more efficient than the complete case estimator and
remains consistent across a wider range of modeling scenarios than the
MLE.

5
Application to Enroll-HD

The SPARCC estimator was applied to data from Enroll-HD, a large
observational study of people living with, or at risk of developing,
Huntington’s disease
(
Sathe et al., 2021
)
. We modeled symptom
severity as a function of time until diagnosis to learn how symptoms of Huntington’s disease progress during the period leading up to a
diagnosis. During this pre-manifest period, symptoms can progress
rapidly, but have not yet significantly impacted patient function,
making this period a promising target for candidate interventions in
future clinical trials
(
Scahill et al., 2020
)
.

In this analysis, we considered individuals who have at least 36 CAG repeats in their Huntingtin gene, making them genetically predisposed to Huntington’s disease. We analyzed symptom severity based on three common outcome metrics: (i) total motor score (TMS), (ii) symbol digit
modalities test (SDMT) score, and (iii) the composite Unified
Huntington Disease Rating Scale (cUHDRS) score. TMS is a measure of
motor function based on a battery of assessments, with a higher TMS
indicating more severe motor impairment

(
Mestre et al., 2018
)
. The SDMT measures cognitive function based
on a participant’s ability to match symbols and digits, with low SDMT
scores indicating high impairment
(
Ryan et al., 2020
)
. The
cUHDRS is a combined measure of cognitive, motor, and functional
symptoms based on several assessment scores, with a low cUHDRS score
corresponding to high impairment
(
Schobel et al., 2017
)
. For each
analysis, we adjusted for the genetic component of Huntington’s
disease using the CAG-Age-product (CAP) score. This score, defined as
(age at study entry)
\times
(CAG - 33.66), combines age and CAG
repeat length to measure the cumulative toxicity of mutant Huntingtin protein,
with higher CAP scores corresponding to older age and longer CAG
repeat length
(
Long et al., 2014
)
. In this application, we
dichotomized the CAP score at 368—the cutoff for high vs. low-medium
CAP scores in
Long et al. (2014)
—to create a binary
uncensored covariate included in both the outcome model and the
nuisance models. Diagnosis of Huntington’s disease was defined as the
first visit where a patient received a diagnostic confidence level
(DCL) of 4. DCL is a measure of how confident a clinician is that a
patient’s motor symptoms represent Huntington’s disease, rated on a
scale of 1-4, with a DCL of 4 corresponding to unequivocal confidence

(
Hogarth et al., 2005
)
. Using this definition, patients who
exit the study before receiving a DCL of 4 have a right-censored time
until diagnosis.

At annual Enroll-HD visits, DCL, TMS, and SDMT data, as well as
data used to derive the cUHDRS score, are collected from study
participants. Since the SPARCC estimator is for cross-sectional data,
only the baseline visit data were used for model fitting. Note, however,
that subsequent visits were used to determine time of diagnosis or
study exit. Our sample was restricted to individuals with CAG repeat length
\geq 36
who
were at least 18 years old, had not been diagnosed upon study entry
(i.e., had a DCL less than 4 at baseline), and had at least two annual
study visits. Based on these filters, the analysis data set had a
sample size of
n=4530
; among these,
q=81.9\%
of participants had a
censored time until diagnosis (
q=63.7\%
in the low-medium CAP group
and
q=93.0\%
in the high CAP group). For each analysis, we excluded
participants with missing outcome values (0.7% for TMS and SDMT, and
2.1% for cUHDRS). The model used for each of the three outcomes was

\displaystyle Y\sim N(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}X+\beta_{2}Z+\beta_{3}XZ,\sigma^{2}),

where
Y
is a given outcome,
X
is time until diagnosis censored by
time until study exit
C
, and
Z
is an indicator for a participant
having a high CAP score.

We fit the model for each outcome using the SPARCC estimator with
parametric working models and nonparametric models for

\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}}),\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
. We compared the results to those from the
complete case estimator and the MLE with a parametric working model
for
\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
. For parametric working models, we used the beta
distributions specified in Section
4
. For nonparametric
models, we used the B-spline density estimator from
Section
3.2.1
. To facilitate nuisance modeling,
X
and

C
were scaled based on the sample maximum of
W=\min(X,C)
to be
within
(0,1)
. Since the TMS outcome is right-skewed, we transformed
it using
Y\mapsto\log(Y+1)
; results are presented on the
original TMS scale. Estimated curves of mean outcome over time until
diagnosis and stratified by CAP group are plotted in
Figure

.

All estimators showed a clear pattern of worsening symptom severity over
time in the period leading up to diagnosis (accounting for the fact
that increased TMS corresponds to increased impairment;
Figure

). This result is consistent with earlier studies (e.g.,
Long et al. (2014)
)
that showed the progressive nature of Huntington’s disease, which
based their analyses on the complete case estimator. While estimates the complete case and
SPARCC estimates from our analyses were similar, the SPARCC estimator offered smaller
standard errors (Table
2
), and thus more precise
inference. In the context of Huntington’s disease, more precise
inference means that studies can make more efficient use of data and
have more power to identify candidate interventions as effective. This
is particularly important given the difficulty in recruiting patients
for long-term observational studies and the burden placed on those
patients.

Other symptom trajectory analyses in the Huntington’s disease
literature have relied on an imputation estimator, plugging in an

estimated
time until diagnosis
(
Scahill et al., 2020
;
Zhang et al., 2021
)
. Since these imputation approaches rely on a
correctly specified model for
\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
, they are no more robust
than the MLE, and, moreover, they are generally less efficient than the
MLE. For these reasons, in our analysis we included the MLE—and not
an imputation estimator—as a comparator. If the model for

\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
was in fact misspecified, then the MLE, with its low
standard error, gives a false sense of confidence of the symptom
trajectories in Figure
2
. In Huntington’s disease
research, this false sense of confidence could lead to erroneously
concluding that a null intervention is effective, or that an effective
intervention is not effective. Because the SPARCC estimator is either
doubly robust to misspecification of parametric models for nuisance
distributions, or consistent with nonparametric nuisance models, it is
an overall more reliable choice than the MLE.

6
Conclusion

We proposed a semiparametric estimator that handles randomly
right-censored covariates robustly and efficiently. This SPARCC
estimator is the root of the efficient score vector
{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}
, which is derived using
semiparametric theory and is constructed by substituting models for
the nuisance distributions of the right-censored covariate,

\eta_{1}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
, and the censoring variable,
\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})
. These
nuisance distributions can be estimated with parametric working models
or using nonparametric/machine learning methods. The SPARCC estimator promises consistent
estimation that is doubly robust to misspecification of parametric working
models, and achieves semiparametric efficient estimation as long as
the nuisance distributions are estimated consistently and with a
sufficient convergence rate. The efficiency enables more precise
inference compared to existing semiparametric estimators in the
censored covariate literature. We also rigorously investigated large
sample properties under mild conditions and demonstrated asymptotic
normality, which can be used for inference on parameters of interest. Finally, we showed
how the SPARCC estimator can be applied to Huntington’s disease data to
learn the progression patterns of impairments for different patient
groups without needing to accurately model both the time until
diagnosis and censoring distributions. By protecting against model
misspecification, the SPARCC estimator can help researchers better
understand progression patterns in other neurodegenerative diseases,
like sporadic Alzheimer’s, where incomplete penetrance
makes it even more difficult to appropriately model time until
diagnosis and censoring distributions.

Another doubly robust estimator in the censored covariate literature
is that from
Ahn et al. (2018)
, yet that estimator is designed for a
different setting. The
Ahn et al. (2018)
estimator is for a Cox
proportional hazards model analyzing a time-to-event outcome, while
the SPARCC estimator is for a cross-sectional regression
model. Moreover,
Ahn et al. (2018)
consider a binary covariate that
may change from 0 to 1, with time of change subject to censoring. This
is in contrast to the SPARCC estimator, which is for a continuous
censored covariate.
Lastly, their estimator is doubly robust to misspecification of

f_{\Delta|Y,{\mathbf{Z}}}
or
f_{X|Y,{\mathbf{Z}}}
, whereas the SPARCC estimator
is doubly robust to misspecification of
f_{X|{\mathbf{Z}}}
or
f_{C|{\mathbf{Z}}}
. As
there is no one-to-one correspondence between these sets of
distributions, the SPARCC estimator and its double robustness
property are unique.

There are several avenues for future work to build on the SPARCC estimator. One is to
investigate whether the double robustness property is also valid
when both nuisance models are estimated at a slower convergence rate. Similar investigations of double robustness with
nonparametrically estimated nuisance distributions have been done in
different problem contexts
(
Kennedy et al., 2017
;
Bennett et al., 2023
)
.
A second is to extend the SPARCC estimator to the case where multiple
covariates are right-censored, or where one or more covariates is
interval-censored. A third is extending this cross-sectional regression model
to a longitudinal model. These extensions would improve the versatility of the SPARCC estimator and allow for robust and efficient estimation in a broader range of applications.

S.5
Numerical Solution for the Efficient Score Vector

We show how to numerically solve the efficient score vector

{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}^{*\dagger}(y,w,\delta,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})
given in Proposition

3
. The first step of this procedure is to find
the solution
{\mathbf{a}}(x,z,{\bm{\beta}})
to the integral equation (
3
). Roughly, this solution is found by discretizing the integrals with respect to
x
, which converts the integral equation into a system of linear equations that can be directly solved. This discretization is performed separately at each unique observed value of
{\mathbf{z}}
.

More formally, consider a quadrature rule
\{(x_{j},r_{j}):j=1,\dots,m\}
that assigns point mass
r_{j}({\mathbf{z}})=\eta_{1}(x_{j},{\mathbf{z}})/\sum_{k=1}^{m}\eta_{1}(x_{k},{\mathbf{z}})
to each of
m
nodes
x_{j}
in the support of
X|{\mathbf{z}}
. In practice, we can choose the support based on some combination of the observed
W|{\mathbf{z}}
, the estimated distribution of
X|{\mathbf{z}}
, and prior knowledge about
X
. Define the
p\times m
matrix
{\mathbf{A}}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})=\{{\mathbf{a}}(x_{1},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})r_{1}({\mathbf{z}}),\dots,{\mathbf{a}}(x_{m},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})r_{m}({\mathbf{z}})\}
, with columns corresponding to the values of
{\mathbf{a}}(x_{j},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})r_{j}({\mathbf{z}})
evaluated at each node
x_{j}
. Then, using this quadrature rule, we can rewrite the left-hand side (
\textrm{LHS}_{j}
) of (
3
) evaluated at
x=x_{j}
as follows:

\displaystyle\rm{LHS}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\{\mathrm{I}(x_{j}\leq C)\mid{\mathbf{z}}\}{\mathbf{a}}(x_{j},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})+\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{I}(x_{j}>C)\frac{\mathrm{E}\{\mathrm{I}(X>C){\mathbf{a}}(X,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\mid Y,C,{\mathbf{z}}\}}{\mathrm{E}\{\mathrm{I}(X>C)\mid Y,C,{\mathbf{z}}\}}\mid x_{j},{\mathbf{z}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{a}}(x_{j},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\int_{x_{j}}^{\infty}\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})dc+\int\int\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathrm{I}(x_{k}>c){\mathbf{a}}(x_{k},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})r_{k}({\mathbf{z}})f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x_{k},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})}{\sum_{l=1}^{m}\mathrm{I}(x_{l}>c)r_{l}({\mathbf{z}})f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x_{l},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})}

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{I}(x_{j}>c)f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x_{j},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})dydc

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{a}}(x_{j},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})r_{j}({\mathbf{z}})\underbrace{\frac{1}{r_{j}({\mathbf{z}})}\int_{x_{j}}^{\infty}\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})dc}_{\equiv d_{j}({\mathbf{z}})}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}{\mathbf{a}}(x_{k},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})r_{k}({\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle\times\underbrace{\int\int\frac{\mathrm{I}(x_{j}\wedge x_{k}>c)f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x_{k},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x_{j},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})}{\sum_{l=1}^{m}\mathrm{I}(x_{l}>c)r_{l}({\mathbf{z}})f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x_{l},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})}\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})dydc}_{\equiv M_{jk}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{a}}(x_{j},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})r_{j}({\mathbf{z}})d_{j}({\mathbf{z}})+\sum_{k=1}^{m}{\mathbf{a}}(x_{k},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})r_{k}({\mathbf{z}})M_{jk}({\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{a}}(x_{j},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})r_{j}({\mathbf{z}})d_{j}({\mathbf{z}})+{\mathbf{A}}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\boldsymbol{M}_{j}({\mathbf{z}}),

where
x_{j}\wedge x_{k}
denotes the minimum of
x_{j}
and
x_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{M}_{j}({\mathbf{z}})=\{M_{j1}({\mathbf{z}}),\dots,M_{jm}({\mathbf{z}})\}^{\rm T}
is an
m\times 1
vector. Then the right-hand side (
\textrm{RHS}_{j}
) of (
3
) evaluated at
x=x_{j}
is

\displaystyle\rm{RHS}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{I}(x_{j}>C)\frac{\mathrm{E}\{\mathrm{I}(X>C){\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{\beta}}^{\rm F}(Y,X,{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\mid Y,C,{\mathbf{z}}\}}{\mathrm{E}\{\mathrm{I}(X>C)\mid Y,C,{\mathbf{z}}\}}\mid x_{j},{\mathbf{z}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\int\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathrm{I}(x_{k}>c){\mathbf{S}}_{\bm{\beta}}^{\rm F}(y,x_{k},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})r_{k}({\mathbf{z}})f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x_{k},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})}{\sum_{l=1}^{m}\mathrm{I}(x_{l}>c)r_{l}({\mathbf{z}})f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x_{l},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})}

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{I}(x_{j}>c)f_{Y|X,{\mathbf{Z}}}(y,x_{j},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\eta_{2}(c,{\mathbf{z}})dydc

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b}_{j}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}).

Then, setting
\rm{LHS}_{j}=\rm{RHS}_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,m
gives

\displaystyle{\mathbf{A}}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\left(\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}({\mathbf{z}})&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&d_{m}({\mathbf{z}})\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}|&&|\\
\boldsymbol{M}_{1}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})&\dots&\boldsymbol{M}_{m}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\\
|&&|\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}|&&|\\
\boldsymbol{b}_{1}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})&\dots&\boldsymbol{b}_{m}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\\
|&&|\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\iff{\mathbf{A}}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\left\{\boldsymbol{D}({\mathbf{z}})+\boldsymbol{M}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\right\}=\boldsymbol{B}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}),

\displaystyle\iff{\mathbf{A}}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})=\boldsymbol{B}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\left\{\boldsymbol{D}({\mathbf{z}})+\boldsymbol{M}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\right\}^{-1},

where
\boldsymbol{D}({\mathbf{z}})
is a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries

,
\boldsymbol{M}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})=\{\boldsymbol{M}_{1}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\dots\boldsymbol{M}_{m}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\}
is a symmetric matrix, and
\boldsymbol{B}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})=\{\boldsymbol{b}_{1}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\dots\boldsymbol{b}_{m}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\}
. Since

and
\boldsymbol{M}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})
are both symmetric, their sum is also symmetric and can thus be inverted stably and efficiently using the Cholesky decomposition.

The solution
{\mathbf{A}}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})
provides approximations for
{\mathbf{a}}(x_{j},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})r_{j}({\mathbf{z}})
evaluated at the nodes
x_{j}
(
j=1,\dots,m
), from which an approximation for
{\mathbf{a}}(x_{j},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})
can be recovered by dividing by

. Recall, however, that
{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm eff}(y_{i},w_{i},\delta_{i},{\mathbf{z}}_{i},{\bm{\beta}})
involves
{\mathbf{a}}(x_{i},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})
evaluated at each
x_{i}
with
\delta_{i}=1
. For such
x_{i}
that are not nodes, we can interpolate
{\mathbf{a}}(x_{i},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})
using the closest nodes
x_{j},x_{k}
on either side of
x_{i}
. In particular,

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbf{a}}(x_{i},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})\eta_{1}(x_{i},{\mathbf{z}})}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\eta_{1}(x_{k},{\mathbf{z}})}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbf{a}}(x_{j},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})r_{j}({\mathbf{z}})(x_{k}-x_{i})+{\mathbf{a}}(x_{k},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})r_{k}({\mathbf{z}})(x_{j}-x_{i})}{x_{k}-x_{j}}

\displaystyle\implies{\mathbf{a}}(x_{i},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\eta_{1}(x_{k},{\mathbf{z}})}{\eta_{1}(x_{i},{\mathbf{z}})}\left\{\frac{{\mathbf{a}}(x_{j},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})r_{j}({\mathbf{z}})(x_{k}-x_{i})+{\mathbf{a}}(x_{k},{\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})r_{k}({\mathbf{z}})(x_{j}-x_{i})}{x_{k}-x_{j}}\right\}.

Finally, note that the components of
\boldsymbol{B}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}}),\boldsymbol{D}({\mathbf{z}})
, and
\boldsymbol{M}({\mathbf{z}},{\bm{\beta}})
involve integrals with respect to

or

follow particular distributions (e.g., normal), then these integrals can be computed more efficiently using other quadrature rules (e.g., Guass-Hermite).
