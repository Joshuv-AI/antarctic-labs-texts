Comparison of anterior middle superior alveolar (AMSA) injection and buccal infiltration as single-injection anesthetic techniques for orthodontic extraction of maxillary first premolars: a randomized split-mouth clinical trial
Authors: Abdul Ahad, Ali Asger Nakib, Manjula Marandi, Mrinalini, Rajat Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPain and anxiety regarding local anesthetic injections are common concerns during orthodontic tooth extraction. Single-injection anesthetic techniques may reduce patient discomfort while maintaining adequate pain control by eliminating the need for separate buccal and palatal injections. This study compared the anesthetic efficacy, pain perception, onset and duration of anesthesia, and postoperative analgesia linked to anterior middle superior alveolar (AMSA) injection versus buccal infiltration for maxillary first premolar extraction.MethodsThis assessor-blinded randomized split-mouth clinical trial enrolled 42 orthodontic patients (mean age, 18.5 ± 3.12 years; range, 13–24 years) requiring bilateral extraction of maxillary first premolars. The participants received an AMSA injection and buccal infiltration in the contralateral quadrant during two visits, spaced 10 days apart. Both techniques utilized 1.2 mL of 4% articaine with adrenaline (1:100,000) via a 30-gauge needle. The outcomes included anesthetic success, pain during injection and extraction assessed on a visual analog scale (VAS; 0–100 mm), onset and duration of anesthesia, and postoperative analgesia. Anesthetic success was defined as completion of extraction without supplemental anesthesia. Supplemental anesthesia was administered when intraoperative pain exceeded 44 mm on the VAS, and the primary anesthetic technique was classified as unsuccessful. McNemar’s test and the paired Student’s t-test were used for statistical comparisons.ResultsAnesthetic success rates were 85.7% for AMSA injection and 78.6% for buccal infiltration, with no significant difference (P = 0.58; matched OR 1.60, 95% CI 0.52–4.92). AMSA injection produced a significantly faster onset and longer duration of palatal anesthesia, whereas buccal infiltration produced a significantly faster onset and longer duration of buccal anesthesia (P < 0.001). AMSA injection caused greater injection-related pain (P < 0.001; Cohen’s dz = 0.68), but lower extraction-related pain (P < 0.001; Cohen’s dz = 0.82). Buccal infiltration achieved a longer postoperative analgesia duration (P = 0.006; Cohen’s dz = 0.57).ConclusionsBoth techniques are effective single-injection options for maxillary first premolar extraction. AMSA provided superior intraoperative comfort and palatal anesthesia, but inflicts greater injection discomfort, while buccal infiltration offered greater injection comfort and longer postoperative analgesia.Keywords: Anesthesia, Dental; Anesthesia, Local; Anterior Middle Superior Alveolar Injection; Articaine; Nerve Block; Tooth Extraction

INTRODUCTION

Orthodontic patients, particularly younger individuals, commonly experience anxiety when undergoing treatments involving tooth extraction. For many patients, extraction procedures represent their first exposure to exodontia and local anesthetic administration, which are often perceived as more traumatic than the extraction itself. This anxiety is not limited to adolescents; adult patients also report experiencing considerable fear related to dental anesthesia and intraoperative pain [1,2]. Several advances in local anesthetic techniques have aimed to minimize pain and reduce the number of injections required, while maintaining effective anesthesia during dental procedures. Pain during local anesthetic administration is influenced by multiple factors, including the injection site, needle gauge, tissue characteristics, rate of solution deposition, and physicochemical properties of the anesthetic agent [3].

Extraction of the maxillary premolars is a routine orthodontic procedure commonly performed to create space for alignment and retraction of the anterior teeth [4]. Conventionally, the extraction of maxillary premolars requires both buccal infiltration targeting the anterior and middle superior alveolar nerves, and a greater palatine nerve block to achieve palatal anesthesia. However, palatal injections are considered among the most painful intraoral injections because of the dense and tightly adherent palatal mucosa [5,6].

Recent studies have investigated the feasibility of achieving adequate anesthesia for maxillary extractions with a single injection, either via buccal infiltration or anterior middle superior alveolar (AMSA) injection, particularly using articaine. Owing to its high lipid solubility and enhanced tissue diffusibility, articaine demonstrates superior penetration through the cortical bone compared with conventional local anesthetics [7]. The anatomical rationale for performing single-injection anesthesia in the maxillary premolar region is based on the presence of nutrient canals and relatively porous alveolar bone, which permit diffusion of the anesthetic solution across the alveolar process and facilitate anesthesia of the terminal alveolar nerve branches and the subneural dental plexus.

Several investigators have reported favorable outcomes with single buccal infiltration of articaine for maxillary tooth extraction [8,9,10,11]. Somuri et al. previously demonstrated a 90% success rate for orthodontic maxillary premolar extraction using buccal infiltration alone with minimal discomfort during extraction [12]. Similarly, Fan et al. reported that routine palatal injections may not be necessary when 4% articaine is administered buccally, because none of the patients in their study required supplemental palatal anesthesia [13]. Other studies have also shown improved anesthetic efficacy and pain control with articaine compared to lignocaine when used as a single buccal injection for premolar extraction [14,15]. However, evidence regarding the reliability of buccal infiltration alone remains inconsistent. Some studies have demonstrated inadequate palatal anesthesia in certain patients despite the use of larger volumes of articaine, suggesting that anatomical variability and cortical bone characteristics may influence the diffusion of anesthetic solutions and their clinical effectiveness [16]. Therefore, alternative single-injection approaches have been proposed. The AMSA injection, administered from the palatal aspect, was designed to anesthetize multiple maxillary teeth with a single injection while minimizing collateral soft tissue anesthesia of the lips and facial muscles [17,18]. Previous studies have suggested that AMSA injections are particularly effective in the canines, premolars, and first molars. Tomić et al. previously reported that AMSA injection alone provided adequate anesthesia for maxillary premolar extraction, regardless of the anesthetic solution or injection system used [17]. Despite these potential advantages, AMSA injection remains technique-sensitive and may cause discomfort if not administered properly. Although it has been suggested that controlled slow deposition can reduce injection pain [18], evidence regarding patient comfort and overall clinical superiority remains limited and inconclusive.

Currently, there is no consensus regarding the most effective single-injection technique for maxillary premolar extraction. An ideal approach should minimize injection discomfort, provide adequate intraoperative anesthesia, and reduce unnecessary postoperative soft tissue numbness.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous split-mouth randomized clinical trial has directly compared AMSA injections and buccal infiltration. Accordingly, the present assessor-blinded, randomized, split-mouth clinical trial was conducted to compare AMSA injection and buccal infiltration using articaine for the orthodontic extraction of maxillary first premolars. The null hypothesis was that there would be no significant differences between the AMSA injection and buccal infiltration with respect to anesthetic success, onset time, duration of anesthesia, postoperative analgesia, or pain experienced during anesthetic administration for maxillary premolar extraction. The primary objective was to compare the anesthetic success between the two techniques. Secondary objectives included comparisons of pain during injection and extraction, onset and duration of anesthesia, and duration of postoperative analgesia.

METHODS

This assessor-blinded, split-mouth clinical trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines (ICH-E6), after obtaining approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (Memo No. 503/Ethics, dated 14th May 2025). This trial was prospectively registered in the Clinical Trial Registry of India (CTRI/2025/06/089285) and was conducted in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 extension for randomized crossover and split-mouth clinical trials.

3. Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was performed a priori using G*Power (version 3.1.9.2; Düsseldorf, Germany), based on McNemar’s test for paired binary outcomes, which is appropriate for a split-mouth study design. The primary outcome was successful anesthesia without the need for supplemental injections. A two-sided alpha level of 0.05 and statistical power of 80% were specified. In the absence of published split-mouth data directly comparing the two anesthetic techniques for the primary outcome, a discordant proportion of 40% and a minimum clinically important paired difference of 28% were pre-specified as the planning assumptions for sample size calculation. The 40% discordance reflected the expectation that the two techniques, which rely on distinct anatomical diffusion pathways, could yield discordant outcomes in a clinically meaningful minority of participants, rather than an estimate derived from directly comparable prior data. Based on these assumptions, the minimum sample size required was estimated to be 40 participants. To accommodate a potential dropout rate of approximately 5%, the target sample size was increased to 42.

4. Patient recruitment

Sixty-three patients who were advised to undergo extraction of bilateral maxillary premolars were assessed for eligibility and screened against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Of these, 46 patients were found eligible for enrollment. However, 4 eligible patients declined to provide informed consent and were therefore excluded from the study; consequently, 42 patients were recruited, as depicted in the CONSORT flowchart (Fig. 1). Written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants (≥18 years). For participants aged < 18 years, written informed consent was obtained from both parents/legal guardians, and written assent from the participant was obtained prior to enrollment.

5. Randomization and allocation concealment

Each participant received both interventions (AMSA injection and buccal infiltration) in two quadrants during two separate treatment visits over 10 days to allow adequate healing and minimize potential carryover effects. The sequence of intervention administration and quadrant allocation was randomized using sequential numbering, with numbers sealed in opaque envelopes prepared by an independent assistant to ensure allocation concealment.

6. Intervention and outcome assessment

Owing to the nature of the intervention, blinding of the operator and participants was not feasible. However, the outcome assessors were blinded to treatment allocation. All injections were administered by a single operator using a sterile, single-use 30-gauge, 25-mm dental needle (Septoject®, Septodont, France) at a standardized deposition rate of approximately 1 mL/min. Uniform needle type and injection rate were maintained for all participants to ensure consistency across both anesthetic techniques.

Each participant received both AMSA injections and buccal infiltration at two treatment visits, separated by a 10-day interval. Both techniques involved the administration of 1.2 mL of 4% articaine with adrenaline 1:100,000 (Septanest®, Septodont, France). Atraumatic extraction was initiated following a standardized 10-minute latency period using a periotome and luxator and was completed with extraction forceps.

The primary outcome was anesthetic success, defined as completion of the extraction without the need for supplemental anesthesia. If a participant reported intolerable intraoperative pain, extraction was briefly interrupted, and pain intensity was immediately recorded on a 100-mm visual analog scale (VAS), as described below. The threshold for supplemental anesthesia was set at a VAS score > 44 mm, based on the pain intensity categorization proposed by Jensen et al., in which 100-mm VAS ratings of 0–4 mm represented no pain, 5–44 mm mild pain, 45–74 mm moderate pain, and 75–100 mm severe pain [19]. A pain score exceeding 44 mm, corresponding to moderate or greater pain intensity, prompted the administration of supplemental anesthesia, and the primary anesthetic technique was classified as unsuccessful. Procedures where extraction was completed without supplemental anesthesia were classified as successful extractions.

The injection-related pain was recorded 1 min after anesthetic administration. Before extraction, the participants were instructed to raise their hands if they experienced intolerable pain. For participants requiring supplemental anesthesia, extraction-related pain was recorded immediately when the procedure was interrupted. For those who completed the extraction without interruption, the overall extraction-related pain was recorded approximately 2 min after completion. These measurements were analyzed together as secondary extraction- related pain outcomes and were independent of the primary anesthetic success outcome. Consequently, some participants who completed the extraction without supplemental anesthesia reported post-extraction VAS scores exceeding 44 mm; because these measurements were obtained only after completion of the procedure, they did not alter the primary success classification.

The onset of anesthesia was assessed separately on the buccal and palatal aspects using a blunt periodontal probe (UNC-15; Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA) for standardized soft-tissue probing and bone sounding at predetermined midbuccal and midpalatal sites. Assessments were conducted at 1-minute intervals. If a participant reported any subjective numbness before the next scheduled assessment, an additional assessment was immediately performed. However, anesthetic onset was confirmed only when objective probing and bone sounding at the corresponding site elicited no pain responses.

The duration of anesthesia was subsequently assessed at the same predetermined buccal and palatal sites using the same probing protocol at 5-minute intervals after extraction or earlier if the participant reported loss of numbness or onset of pain, whichever occurred first. For each aspect, the anesthetic duration was defined as the interval from the confirmed anesthetic onset to the first positive pain response at the corresponding assessment site.

Postoperative analgesia was evaluated only for procedures completed without any supplemental anesthesia. The participants remained under observation for 2 h after extraction and were instructed to notify the blinded assessor when the postoperative pain first required rescue analgesia (ibuprofen, 400 mg). The interval between anesthetic administration and rescue analgesic intake was recorded as the duration of postoperative analgesia. All outcome assessments were conducted by a single assessor blinded to the anesthetic technique used.

7. Data analysis

Data were entered into a spreadsheet (Microsoft Excel) and analyzed using Jamovi (Version 2.6, The Jamovi Project, Sydney, Australia). The success rate of anesthesia and requirements for supplemental anesthesia are expressed as percentages. The proportion of successful anesthesia between the two interventions was compared using continuity-corrected McNemar’s test, as the study followed a split-mouth design with paired binary outcomes. Continuous data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For continuous outcomes, normality was assessed on the paired differences using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of the distribution plots. As the durations of anesthesia and postoperative analgesia were recorded only in cases of successful primary anesthesia, per-protocol analyses of these outcomes were restricted to participants in whom both interventions were successful (n = 28), thereby preserving the paired nature of the split-mouth design. Statistical significance was set at P-value < 0.05. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, as the primary outcome (anesthetic success) was the sole outcome powered a priori, and the remaining comparisons were pre-specified secondary outcomes addressing distinct clinical domains (onset, duration, pain, and postoperative analgesia), and were therefore reported as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

DISCUSSION

Both AMSA injection (85.7% success) and buccal infiltration (78.6% success) demonstrated high anesthetic success rates without any significant difference in supplemental anesthesia requirements, supporting both techniques as clinically acceptable single-injection alternatives for orthodontic maxillary premolar extractions. The marginally higher success rate of AMSA may reflect the greater porosity and lower cancellous bone density of the palatal aspect of the maxillary alveolar process, facilitating transalveolar diffusion of the anesthetic solution toward the subneural dental plexus [20]. Cetkovic et al. reported that the AMSA injection site had greater cortical porosity and significantly more microcanals fully penetrating the palatal cortex than into the buccal cortex [21].

The study findings are consistent with those of Tomić et al., who reported high success rates for AMSA injection during maxillary premolar extraction irrespective of the anesthetic solution or delivery system used [17]. Jovanovic et al. additionally reported a success rate > 83% with AMSA injection using 4% articaine for the extraction of incisors, canines, and premolars [22]. Similarly, Chourasia et al. reported an overall AMSA success rate of 71.5%, with success as high as 82.2% for the first premolars, even with 2% lignocaine [23].

Similarly, studies of single buccal infiltration with articaine have reported favorable outcomes, with success rates ranging from approximately 75% to 95%, depending on patient age, tooth type, and anatomical variation. Somuri et al. reported nearly 90% success rate with orthodontic premolar extraction using buccal infiltration alone, which is higher than the success rate observed in the present study [12]. However, other investigators have reported inconsistent palatal anesthesia with buccal infiltration alone, even when larger volumes of articaine are used [15,16]. Woo et al. found a lower success rate (20-32%) and high variability of the anesthetized area, concluding that the clinical efficacy of both 1.8 mL and 3.6 mL of articaine as buccal infiltration is of questionable value for producing palatal anesthesia [15]. These variations may reflect the differences in cortical bone thickness, cancellous bone density, nutrient canal distribution, and palatal soft tissue innervation. Articaine was chosen because it exhibits superior lipid solubility and enhanced diffusion through the maxillary cortical bone compared with conventional local anesthetics, such as lignocaine. These characteristics make it well suited for evaluating single-injection techniques that rely on transalveolar diffusion for palatal and pulpal anesthesia [24].

A noteworthy observation of our study was that all cases requiring supplemental anesthesia in both intervention groups occurred in participants aged > 20 years. Although this study was not designed to evaluate age as a determinant of anesthetic success, the observed pattern indicates that age-related increases in cortical bone density and mineralization may reduce transalveolar diffusion and compromise anesthetic efficacy. This represents a practical clinical takeaway: in patients beyond their early twenties, clinicians should anticipate a higher likelihood of needing supplemental anesthesia with either single-injection technique, and should be prepared in advance to administer it, rather than treating supplementation as an unexpected deviation from the plan. Further adequately powered studies are required to confirm whether age is an independent predictor. Occasional separate innervation of the buccal and palatal roots of the maxillary first premolar may also reduce the predictability of single-injection techniques [18,22].

A key finding of this study was the site-specific differences in the onset and duration of anesthesia. AMSA injection produced significantly faster onset and longer durations of palatal anesthesia, whereas buccal infiltration produced significantly faster onset and longer duration on the buccal aspect, reflecting the anatomical site of deposition. The AMSA solution is deposited adjacent to the palatal subneural plexus, while buccal infiltration is deposited closer to the buccal cortical plate and terminal nerve branches supplying the buccal gingiva [20]. The dense, tightly adherent palatal mucosa may additionally promote diffusion toward the buccal tissues under tissue pressure. In contrast, the more compliant buccal soft tissue does not appear to promote diffusion toward the palate to the same extent [20].

Buccal infiltration produced significantly longer postoperative analgesia than AMSA injection, an effect likely attributable to the more effective diffusion of the anesthetic into the periodontal ligament and buccal alveolar bone, the tissues most manipulated during extraction. Although statistically significant, the absolute difference in postoperative analgesia was modest (98.9 vs. 91.1 minutes; approximately 8 minutes). Whether this difference is clinically meaningful likely depends on the clinical setting and patient circumstances, particularly the timing and accessibility of rescue analgesia.

Pain perception during injection differed significantly between techniques, with patients reporting higher VAS scores during AMSA injection. This is consistent with the anatomical characteristics of the palatal mucosa, which is tightly bound to the underlying periosteum, exhibits limited tissue compliance, and is highly innervated. Moreover, AMSA injections require slow pressure-controlled deposition, which may further contribute to discomfort when administered using conventional syringes [18].

Despite greater injection discomfort, AMSA was associated with significantly lower extraction-related pain, likely reflecting a more uniform anesthesia of the tissues involved in the extraction and a more comprehensive blockade of the sub-neural dental plexus, as suggested in previous studies [23,25]. Clinically, this represents an important trade-off—a more uncomfortable injection for a more comfortable extraction—that may be especially relevant for adolescent orthodontic patients undergoing their first extraction, since fear of pain can shape future dental anxiety and treatment acceptance [1,2]. Therefore, technique selection should be individualized based on whether minimizing injection discomfort, maximizing intraoperative comfort, or prolonging postoperative analgesia is the primary clinical priority.

Another consideration is the method of anesthetic delivery. In the present study, all injections were administered using conventional dental syringes to standardize the intervention. Computer-controlled local anesthetic delivery (CCLAD) systems provide a constant and controlled injection rate and pressure, and have been reported to reduce discomfort during palatal injections [25]. Therefore, future studies should investigate whether administering AMSA injections using a CCLAD system can reduce injection pain while preserving anesthetic efficacy.

These findings highlight the importance of an adequate latency period when using buccal infiltration as the sole anesthetic technique for maxillary premolar extraction. Given that the mean onset of palatal anesthesia following buccal infiltration was approximately 7 min, with significant inter-individual variability, clinicians should observe a waiting period of at least 10 min before commencing the procedure to allow sufficient transalveolar diffusion of the anesthetic solution and maximize the likelihood of achieving complete palatal anesthesia.

This study has several methodological strengths: Notably, the randomized split-mouth design allowed each participant to serve as their own control, and standardized anesthetic administration combined with assessor blinding further strengthened the internal validity of the findings.

This study has several important limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the study population consisted exclusively of young orthodontic patients undergoing elective extraction of healthy premolars. Therefore, the findings may not extend to older patients, teeth with acute pulpal or periodontal inflammation, or more complex extractions, where anesthetic efficacy and pain perception may differ. Pain intensity was assessed using the self-reported VAS, which, although widely validated, is inherently influenced by individual pain thresholds, anxiety, and prior dental experience. Therefore, the observed differences in pain scores should be interpreted with caution. A further methodological weakness is the reliance on participants’ subjective perception of numbness to trigger additional onset assessments. Although onset was always confirmed objectively via standardized soft-tissue probing and bone sounding, using subjective reports rather than fixed-interval objective testing alone to time these assessments may have introduced imprecision into the recorded onset times. Minor operator-dependent variations in needle positioning or tissue characteristics, despite standardized injection volumes and rates, could also have affected anesthetic diffusion. Finally, although the study was adequately powered for the primary outcome, analyses of anesthetic duration and postoperative analgesia were restricted to the 28 participants in whom both techniques achieved successful anesthesia, reducing the statistical power and increasing the risk of type II error for these secondary outcomes; therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory pending confirmation in adequately powered studies.

In conclusion, both AMSA injection and buccal infiltration with articaine are effective single-injection techniques for maxillary first premolar extraction, with each technique offering distinct advantages. Notably, AMSA injection provided superior intraoperative comfort and more effective palatal anesthesia, although it was associated with greater pain during injection. Conversely, buccal infiltration resulted in less pain during injection and longer postoperative analgesia. Clinicians should consider whether maximizing intraoperative and palatal anesthesia (favoring AMSA) or minimizing injection discomfort and prolonging postoperative pain relief (favoring buccal infiltration) better suits a given patient’s needs and anatomical factors. Future multicenter randomized controlled trials with larger and more diverse populations are needed to confirm these findings and to evaluate patient-centered outcomes such as anxiety reduction, treatment preference, treatment acceptance, and long-term satisfaction with single-injection anesthetic techniques.
