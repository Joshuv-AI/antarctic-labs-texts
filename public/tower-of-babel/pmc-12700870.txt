Linking walkability and pedestrian crossing safety using simulated urban environments
Authors: Anat Meir, Tal Oron-Gilad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWalking is the most environmentally sustainable mode of transport, offering wide-ranging physical, mental, and ecological benefits. Yet, pedestrian safety remains a critical concern in urban environments. We examined the relationship between pedestrian crossing behaviour and walkability through two complementary stages. In the first stage, we developed a localised Walkability Index by integrating environmental and street design features, weighted by 52 subject matter experts (SMEs). In the experimental stage, we empirically examined 47 participants for road-crossing decisions under controlled conditions by exposing them to real-world panoramic traffic video scenes in a semi-immersive dome environment. Pedestrians’ crossing-performance differences among scenarios were pronounced in missed crossing opportunities and response times, demonstrating walkability effects on pedestrian behaviour and safety. Further, participants rated the street images for perceived pleasantness. Ratings were compatible with the walkability index scores, supporting its internal validity. Our findings suggest that targeted urban design may reduce decision latency and exposure to traffic hazards. We propose a preliminary theoretical model, contextualising how walkability constructs impact pedestrians’ decisions within a local road crossing context. This study advances understanding of the built environment’s role in shaping pedestrian safety behaviour and strengthens the need for evidence-based urban design policy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27558-w.Keywords: Walkability, Pedestrian safety, Road-crossing behaviour, Built environment, Simulated pedestrian crossingSubject terms: Human behaviour, Psychology and behaviour

Introduction

Background

Walking, the most fundamental and sustainable mode of transport, offers a multitude of physical, mental, and ecological benefits1–5. The significance of walking is increasingly acknowledged, considering growing health and environmental sustainability concerns6, spurring global initiatives promoting walking. Walking, as part of a broader modal shift from motorised to active, low-carbon transport modes7, can alleviate pressure on urban transport systems and support sustainable urban transformations8. Walkability refers to the extent to which an environment supports safe, comfortable, and convenient walking for pedestrians9–11, but it is acknowledged that a more comprehensive understanding of how built, natural, and social environments are necessary to improve active living environments12,13. Walkability is an integral concept to urban design and transportation planning, emphasising the creation of pedestrian-friendly environments14,15 to encourage walking as a primary mode of transport16–20, with well-documented health benefits21.

While walkability typically captures pedestrian comfort and access, its role in pedestrian safety remains underexplored, especially during road crossings. To advance understanding of the rarely examined empirical link between walkability and pedestrian safety, this study introduces a walkability metric and applies it within simulated road-crossing scenarios to assess its association with safety-related pedestrian behaviours.

The World Health Organization22,23 estimates that approximately 1.3 million individuals lose their lives each year as a result of road traffic crashes, with vulnerable road users accounting for over half of these casualties. Vulnerable road users’ road safety has been extensively researched24–32. Specifically, extensive research on pedestrian safety33–50 has provided valuable insights into pedestrian behaviour and the challenges they face in urban environments. However, smart city technologies could further prioritise pedestrian safety by emphasising walkability. As noted by13, cross-disciplinary collaboration can deepen the understanding of how transportation investments and land use decisions impact public health, the environment, and overall quality of life51. Creating a walkable setting is not only essential for promoting walking but also for achieving broader goals of sustainable urban environments52.

Walkability indices and crossing safety in the literature

Numerous methods exist for assessing street walkability53, ranging from straightforward approaches such as pedestrian counts conducted at specific locations54,55 to more intricate models incorporating demographic data, such as income levels, building age, and land use (e.g., commercial, industrial, residential). It has been suggested that physical features impact the quality of the walking environment by interacting with individuals’ perceptions and sensitivities54, and that characteristics of the pedestrian environment substantially influence walking experience and route utility56. Several studies have explored the influence of physical attributes and urban landscape elements, aiming to gain a deeper understanding of walking and walkability57–61. For instance59, examined urban design qualities and key physical features, while61 studied the impact of built environment characteristics, such as density, diversity, design, distance to transit, and destination accessibility on pedestrian-vehicle crashes. However, most of these studies emphasise qualitative observations over quantitative measures54,61.

Existing urban planning literature identifies several urban qualities (e.g., imageability, enclosure, human scale), leading to multiple urban design qualities and features54,59. Nonetheless, there remains a critical gap in walkability research, particularly regarding the role of road-crossing complexity and environmental settings in shaping Walkability indices. Walkability studies prioritise personal safety and well-being factors, such as adequate lighting and unobstructed sidewalks. However, urban features and factors impact road crossing safety, as supported by prior findings41,42,49,62. By bridging this gap through the comprehensive evaluation of urban design attributes, this research seeks to provide a robust and quantitative foundation for enhancing pedestrian safety and walkability.

Due to the unique attributes of local urban environments, which may differ substantially from those in other countries and settings, this research necessitated a comprehensive examination of locally specific street attributes. Prior studies on young drivers have emphasised the importance of incorporating contextual scenery and regionally calibrated weightings48,63,64. To account for the spatial and infrastructural complexity of the urban landscape, the assessed attributes were tailored to reflect its distinctive features. Accordingly, the methodological framework was critically examined and refined to ensure its suitability for the specific configuration of local street environments.

To summarise, while some research has explored the relationship between walkability and pedestrian safety, several critical questions remain. One key issue is determining how effectively walkability indices translate into improved safety outcomes for pedestrians. Some studies, such as those by65, have shown that neighbourhoods with higher walkability - characterised by well-designed sidewalks and crosswalks - are associated with lower rates of pedestrian injuries. Yet, the ties between urban features and safety outcomes are complex. Other studies, such as those by66, found no direct link between walkability indices and road crossing safety, indicating that a walkable area may not necessarily guarantee a safer crossing environment. Although promoting walkability is crucial for encouraging walking, safety considerations have not always kept pace with these developments, as evident by persistently elevated crash rates. This research seeks to address the walkability–road-crossing safety gap by developing metrics that better align walkability with safety, aiming to model the relationship between walkability, safety, and pedestrian behaviour. Importantly, current literature often examines the constructs of walkability and pedestrian safety independently67, with research typically emphasising either walkability and physical environment attributes or pedestrian safety and road users’ attributes. For instance68, emphasised the need to account for established individual pedestrian collision risk and individual factors in the planning of school travel, cautioning against presuming that a highly walkable environment inherently ensures safe pedestrian settings.

While walkability is associated with multiple health and environmental benefits, its direct influence on pedestrian safety, particularly at the point of crossing decisions, remains underexplored. This study addresses that gap by adopting a two-phase approach: first, developing a localised Walkability Index, tailored to the unique characteristics of local settings, landscapes, and road users, and second, applying it to assess pedestrian safety outcomes in simulated road-crossing scenarios. This approach seeks to address existing knowledge gaps in the literature by promoting a more comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing pedestrian safety within the local context and by capturing the linkage between walkability and pedestrian safety. Accordingly, the present manuscript outlines a two-stage analysis: the first stage focuses on developing a designated crossing Walkability Index; the second applies this index to evaluate its relationship with pedestrian safety. The following sections provide a detailed examination of each stage.

Stage I - development of a designated crossing walkability index

Background

The objective of this stage was to develop a crossing Walkability Index tailored to the unique characteristics of local settings, landscapes, and road users, which was subsequently evaluated against safety measurements. Accordingly, the focus extends beyond the formulation itself to encompass methodological considerations. While the components and weightings of the index may vary across localities, the underlying development process is systematic and generalisable.

The formulation of the local crossing Walkability Index required the establishment of a comprehensive list of factors and corresponding weights for each crossing context. For instance, in the work of60, a set of five factors with designated weights was employed. Transposing research findings from one city to another, and from one country to another, especially from a vastly different context, such as Hong Kong to Israel, required meticulous adjustments. Indeed, as suggested by69 after conducting a systematic review of walkability indices, future studies should enhance urban form delineation, optimise the balance between metric comprehensiveness and data availability, apply rigorous methodologies for metric selection, and explore alternative weighting techniques that account for cognitive and emotional responses to the built environment. These advancements are essential for advancing walkability metrics within the compact city development and placemaking paradigms.

It was imperative to clarify the extent of disparities in pertinent variables and weightings. Thus, it became essential to ascertain the specific influence of factors on distinct crossing environments to create a local crossing Walkability Index. This involved the incorporation and refinement of factors and a meticulous evaluation of the weightings within the index. Thus, an initial step involved defining a scaling mechanism for each pertinent factor. Elements such as traffic density, accessibility, sidewalk quality, and visual aspects of streets were integral considerations, at least in the initial stages. Subsequently, the assignment of weights was guided by questionnaires and evaluations from subject matter experts (SMEs) in urban design, traffic management, and pedestrian safety.

Method

Participants

A structured questionnaire was administered to assess the relative influence of various environmental features. The experiment was conducted on 52 experts from a local firm with extensive experience in transportation engineering, architecture, urban design, and planning, spanning 35 years. Utilising a structured questionnaire, these professionals assessed the influence of environmental features. All participants were affiliated with the same firm, noted for its comprehensive portfolio and integrated divisions, highlighting the firm’s specialization and sustained impact in the field.

Participants evaluated the extent to which each feature affects pedestrian behaviour in proximity to crossing locations. The purpose of this questionnaire was to calculate the coefficients of environmental characteristics - specifically, the degree of influence of each characteristic on pedestrian behaviour near crossings. Participants were asked to rate, according to their opinion, the degree of influence of each of 22 environmental characteristics on pedestrian behaviour near the crossing. Ratings ranged from 1, indicating no effect, to 5, indicating a significant impact.

Apparatus

Questionnaires to subject matter experts (SMEs) in relevant fields

The determination of coefficients for environmental characteristics, reflecting the extent of each characteristic’s impact on pedestrian behaviour near crossings, was achieved through questionnaires distributed to subject-matter experts (SMEs) in urban planning, design, and related fields. Experts were asked to rate the influence of each characteristic on pedestrian behaviour using a scale from ‘1’ (denoting no effect) to ‘5’ (signifying a significant impact). These responses, along with insights from the literature, informed the selection and weighting of factors in the crossing Walkability Index. The initial item of the questionnaire assessed the influence of pedestrian crossings on nearby behaviour and was intended to ensure respondent attentiveness and engagement. The remaining 22 items were based on urban characteristics defined in the studies by60,61 (see Supplementary Table S1).

Compiling a set of urban locations for analysis

Crossing geometry has been shown to influence pedestrian behaviour41,70. Accordingly42, advocate for further investigation into the relationship between pedestrian behaviour and performance and the physical characteristics of crossings. In line with this perspective, a set of nine crossing scenarios featuring real-life filmed footage from various local urban settings71 was employed. These scenarios represent typical local traffic conditions and urban features. The locations were diverse; each was selected to capture specific environmental characteristics relevant to pedestrian safety and road-crossing behaviour (see Table 1).

Development of the local crossing walkability index

The calculation of the crossing walkability at each location followed a multi-step process, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The items addressed specific urban characteristics, adapted from the frameworks proposed by60,61. Several attributes were adapted from61, with specific measurable indicators defined for each, assessed via Google Maps (map data ©2025 Google). Additionally, drawing on the methodology outlined by60, several attributes were physically measured within a 200-square-metre area surrounding each crossing. Each attribute was assigned a value of 0, 0.5, or 1, in accordance with predefined criteria. The coefficient step is based on the ratings of the SMEs, while the mapping of the urban features in each crossing location is based on analysis and extraction of features from Google maps (map data ©2025 Google) and observations at each location.

Results

Coefficients - calculating the weighted average and importance coefficients

Data were collected from 52 respondents on 22 environmental features rated on a 5-point Likert scale (‘1’=not important at all, ‘5’=very important). A weighted average was calculated for each item. Supplementary Table S2 (see Supplementary Information) presents the derived average weight and coefficient of each environmental feature. Then, a coefficient is calculated using Supplementary Equation S2 (see Supplementary Information). Supplementary Equation S2 looks at the minimum and maximum average weights and the specific value of the feature. To exemplify, Supplementary Equation S3 showcases the coefficient calculation for one item, where the maximum and minimum values were derived from the weighted averages (see Supplementary Table S2).

Features - calculating the environmental features of each location

To quantify the characteristics of each crossing location (see Supplementary Table S3), Google Maps (map data ©2025 Google), a web-based mapping application developed by Google, was utilised. The platform overlays maps onto satellite imagery and aerial photographs and offers four display modes: standard map view, satellite imagery, 3D surface visualisation, and street-level photographs. Based on the 22 items, the area surrounding each crossing - extending up to three city blocks or approximately 200 square metres - was delineated within the application, and the environmental characteristics of each designated area were recorded. The Google Maps view of each crossing facilitated the systematic quantification of its features. Some attributes were assessed through direct on-site measurement, which entailed visiting each street and directly measuring the values on-site.

Next, each urban feature, comprising multiple environmental characteristics and their impact, was quantified for the locations included in the experiment (Supplementary Table S3). Notably, the urban features adapted from60 were pre-standardised on a 0/0.5/1 scale according to their predefined criteria. Moreover, for certain variables, our approach was to utilise a simplified and easy to measure method in place of aggregating extensive raw datasets, thereby enabling efficient data collection via Google Maps. For instance, rather than collecting data on the total floor area of single-family housing, the total number of single-family housing units was gathered (Fig. 2).

Road views of ‘44 Bnei Dan, Tel Aviv’: the top panel presents a participant’s perspective, the middle panel depicts a street-level photograph, and the bottom panel shows a Google Maps (map data ©2025 Google) view. These visualisations support the quantification of route characteristics and attributes, thereby enhancing understanding of the complexities faced by pedestrians and informing improvements in urban crossing safety and design. The road-crosser’s perspective demonstrates that a single viewpoint is insufficient to capture the full context, highlighting the importance of a comprehensive index. Factor analysis and weighting were applied to calculate walkability scores for the index.

Given the variability in the number of environmental characteristics per feature and their differing units of measurement, as identified by61, normalisation was performed by dividing values into quartiles relative to the corresponding characteristic across all streets. This approach aligned each measured value with quartile benchmarks, assigning scores of 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, or 1 based on quartile classification. Since density-related items and the number of bus and train stops were not categorised, an additional step was required in the index formulation. For these items, values were normalised relative to the nine locations (Table 1) using Supplementary Equation S4 (see Supplementary Information). For instance, the number of bus routes serving each crossing varied across sites, ranging from 1 (Avia-Hashofet 9, Beer-Sheva) to 44 (Bar-Cochva 31, Petach Tikva). Each location was assigned a score ranging from 0 to 1 based on its quartile relative to the distribution, with the lowest and highest values receiving scores of 0 and 1, respectively.

Deriving the walkability index score of each location

The Walkability Index was formulated using weights derived from expert evaluations of environmental characteristics influencing pedestrian behaviour. Subject matter experts (SMEs) ranked each feature, and weighted averages of responses were calculated. Due to the varying number of characteristics per feature, min-max normalisation was applied to produce standardised coefficients. These coefficients (βi) were then used in the index formula (Supplementary Equation S5, Supplementary Information). Scores ranged from 2.76 (Hashfela 6) to 9.13 (Bnei Dan 44), providing a relative walkability scale (see Table 1).

Summary

This stage yielded a context-specific crossing Walkability Index, making a valuable methodological contribution to the evaluation of pedestrian environments. Grounded in systematically audited urban features and coefficients elicited from subject-matter experts (SMEs), a well‑established approach within pedestrian walkability and urban design literature9,12,60,61, the index ensures variable selection and weighting reflect localised conditions. The lowest score (2.76) was associated with a peripheral area lacking pedestrian-oriented destinations or services, whereas the highest score (9.13) was attributed to a location adjacent to a public park and extensive green space7,72. While methodologically robust, the index primarily serves as a tool to facilitate the study’s central investigation into the relationship between walkability and safety, which is addressed in the next stage through simulated crossing experiments.

Stage II - Application of the designated crossing walkability index to assess the relationship between walkability and pedestrian safety

Background

The second stage applied the developed crossing Walkability Index to assess the relationship between walkability and pedestrian safety using a simulated crossing task based on high-resolution panoramic video recordings of real-world urban traffic environments. While virtual environments are commonly used in road safety research to study behavioural responses under controlled, risk-free conditions46,73,74, they often rely on computer-generated scenes that simplify the environmental complexity. In contrast, the current study employed a dome projection system that presented recorded, real-life traffic scenarios. This approach preserved the visual richness, unpredictability, and contextual depth of actual urban settings, thereby enhancing ecological validity. The dome-based system provided a controlled yet immersive platform for capturing pedestrian decision-making in response to authentic environmental dynamics. This technique builds on prior research using virtual reality settings to analyse pedestrian preferences and behaviour24,41,75–83. Notably, it aligns with efforts to replicate naturalistic conditions in transport safety studies by projecting unaltered urban scenes.

Method

Archival data from71 were used in the second phase of the study. The dataset originated from a dome pedestrian simulator projecting real-life high-resolution videos of traffic scenarios of nine locations listed in Table 1. Forty-seven participants each conducted nine crossing decisions, corresponding to the locations listed in Table 1. These data were analysed in relation to the Walkability Index developed in phase I. Relevant methodological details and analytical metrics are provided to support the current analysis.

Participants

The experiment involved 47 participants, students from the Department of Industrial Engineering and Management, aged between 23 and 29 years, all of whom had normal vision with static acuity of 6/12 or better. Participants volunteered to take part in the study and were compensated with course credit. Study protocols were approved by Ben-Gurion University of the Negev’s ethics board, informed consent was obtained from all participants involved in the study, and the study was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Apparatus and stimuli

The dome projection pedestrian laboratory facility is a semi-immersive simulator designed for controlled pedestrian behaviour experiments in a laboratory setting62,80–82. The simulator features a dome-shaped screen with a 180-degree horizontal and 45-degree vertical arc, measuring 7 m in diameter and 2.5 m in height, elevated 20 centimetres above the ground (Fig. 3). A high-precision projection system comprising three projectors (1400 × 1050 resolution each) produces a seamless 4200 × 1050 total resolution. This setup enables safe measurement of participants’ behaviour while viewing pre-designed virtual reality simulations of real-life pedestrian scenarios. An advanced surround sound system enhances immersion and realism. The simulator’s validity in replicating perceived distance and object speed has been established. The dome projection facility was further refined by integrating the ability to project real street videos, thereby enhancing its capacity to accurately replicate urban environments for pedestrian crossing experiments71.

Street view videos were captured by four Go-Pro cameras strategically positioned to offer a comprehensive panoramic field of view spanning approximately 205 degrees. The figure illustrates both the filming apparatus (on the right side) and the laboratory experimental setup showcasing the crossing scene scenario and the participant’s apparatus as conducted in the dome pedestrian laboratory facility (on the left side).

Each of the participants was presented with an array of nine scenarios of typical pedestrian crossings, from a pedestrian point of view71. Streets were selected to ensure an equal distribution of high and low walkability scores. Real-time traffic recordings were conducted in three Israeli cities (Beer-Sheva, Petach Tikva, and Tel Aviv) using four GoPro cameras on a specialised rig, capturing a 205-degree field of view (Fig. 3). The videos were stitched into a single panoramic sequence using PTGui and Video Stitch software.

The selected locations were deliberately distributed across cities that differ in their urban morphology, demographic composition, and transportation context. Tel Aviv, representing a dense metropolitan core, included three sites characterised by high pedestrian activity, complex intersection geometry, and heavy traffic flows. These encompassed both central arterial roads (e.g., Remez Street) and mixed-use urban edges (e.g., Bnei Dan Street near the Yarkon Park), where recreational and commuting pedestrians interact with varied traffic conditions. Petach Tikva, a mid-sized city with six study sites, reflects a more suburban and industrial character, combining residential and commercial areas. Its crossings mainly comprised residential or local connector streets (e.g., A.D. Gordon 9 and Sokolov 21) with moderate traffic volumes, alongside several larger arterials (e.g., Bar-Cochva 31) dominated by vehicular flow. Beer-Sheva offered a contrasting peripheral setting - a southern desert city combining newer planned roads and an older urban fabric.

This geographical spread ensured representation of central, metropolitan, and peripheral urban environments, encompassing a broad spectrum of intersection types, traffic volumes, and pedestrian behaviours. The sites vary not only in built form but also in socio-demographic and functional contexts. Tel Aviv locations serve diverse pedestrian populations, including commuters, families, tourists, and recreational walkers, within dense, walkable environments. Petach Tikva sites cater mainly to local residents and workers, featuring a mix of young families and older adults but limited transient users. Beer-Sheva contributes a university-town demographic, combining a younger population with older suburban residents. Collectively, these sites capture variation in crossing environments - from high-density, multi-lane arterials to quieter residential streets - and in traffic density, crossing complexity, and pedestrian demand. This diversity enhances the ecological validity and representativeness of the stimuli.

At the same time, to ensure experimental comparability, the street locations were carefully selected for their similar visual and structural characteristics, all representing typical two-lane, bi-directional urban streets. The crossings took place at unmarked sites lacking designated pedestrian infrastructure, with no bicycle lanes, traffic lights, or crossing islands present. Each recorded traffic scene lasted up to one minute (Figs. 4 and 5) and portrayed a comparable urban environment - featuring open fields of view41,80,81, a low and steady level of visual load48, and traffic composed mainly of private vehicles (on average 11 per scenario). Importantly, the number of vehicles did not vary significantly between scenes. To minimise order effects, all scenes were presented in a randomised sequence.

Procedure

Participants arrived at the laboratory for an orientation session where they reviewed and signed consent forms. Each was assigned a unique identifier linked to their data. Visual acuity was assessed using the Snellen chart84,85. They each walked 8.45 m three times to calculate their average walking speed for estimating the time needed to cross a designated road segment at their natural crossing speed. First, participants familiarised themselves with the dome facility, tasks, and decision button through a one-minute trial. They were then exposed to nine randomised scenarios that depicted typical street-crossing environments. Participants registered their perceived safety for crossing by pressing a decision button, i.e., they indicated their decision when to cross by pressing a button, with non-presses within a minute interpreted as decisions not to cross (in accordance with prior research, e.g41,42,49,71,80–82,86.,. Scenarios were conducted on two-lane streets, each with a single lane per direction, and were not located at regulated crosswalks. Participant position and viewpoint reflected real-world eye height and distance from the curb. Next, participants completed the online questionnaires, including the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ87;, immersion level assessments, and demographic surveys (aligned with findings on sociodemographic variations in walkability impacts by88–90. Participants also reported their subjective preferences for street crossings. The experimental session lasted approximately 25–30 min.

Crossing and safety metrics

The following metrics were used to assess participants’ performance in pedestrian crossing tasks.

Safety margin. The temporal gap, in seconds, between the completion of the participant’s road crossing and the arrival of the subsequent vehicle in the same lane91.

Crossing performance. A categorical variable derived from the safety margin represents the level of safety during road crossing. Consistent with prior research91–93, it was classified into five ordinal levels:

Level 0 - Safe crossing: Safety margin exceeds 2 s.

Level 1 - Slightly unsafe crossing: Although the safety margin exceeded 2 s and the participant successfully completed the crossing, a vehicle passed through the adjacent lane during the crossing event.

Level 2 - Unsafe crossing: The safety margin is below 2 s.

Level 3 - Highly unsafe crossing: The safety margin is less than one second.

Level 4 - Collision: Safety margin is less than 0 s.

Missed crossing opportunities. The total count of potential crossing opportunities that were not utilised by a participant within a given scenario. A crossing opportunity was defined as a temporal gap of at least four seconds between two successive vehicles, which would allow for a safe pedestrian crossing.

Crossing response time. The time interval, in seconds, from the onset of a crossing opportunity to the participant’s decision to cross, indicated by pressing a response button.

Subjective preference ranking. The perceived pleasantness and aesthetic appeal of static street images, rated by participants on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all pleasant/appealing, 5 = extremely pleasant/appealing), assuming equal walking time to a fixed destination. This approach aligns with94, who demonstrated that subjective perceptions reflect cognitive processes associated with measurable environmental features and argued that perceptual models based on such stimuli offer meaningful data on urban qualities and help explain road users’ behaviours, such as route choice and walkability. While two-dimensional images do not fully capture the complexity and dynamics of public spaces41,42, they may serve as effective proxies for eliciting such perceptions.

Results

Subjective preference ranking. To further assess the validity of the Walkability Index, its correspondence with participants’ subjective street preferences was examined. Specifically, participants were shown four static images of street locations from Table 1 and were requested to rate each on a 5-point Likert scale based on perceived pleasantness or visual appeal. To control for confounding variables, all participants were instructed to imagine walking to the same destination, with each street option requiring an equal walking time. They were then asked to indicate which street they perceived as the most pleasant and aesthetically appealing. These subjective preferences were compared with the corresponding Walkability Index scores (Table 1). Participants consistently favoured streets with higher Walkability Index scores - Bnei Dan (score = 9.13) was rated more favourably than Remez (7.30), which in turn was preferred over Sokolov (6.13) and A.D. Gordon (5.50). This pattern suggests alignment between subjective evaluations and objective walkability assessments, supporting internal consistency in participant responses.

SSQ. To examine the relationships between walkability and safety, the SSQ was examined. Results indicated that the simulator rarely induced physical symptoms, with no significant health effects in most participants. As no cases of simulator sickness occurred, no participants were excluded from the analysis. The absence of symptoms may be attributed to the relatively brief duration of each experimental session, in line with evidence suggesting that simulator-induced discomfort is time-dependent71,95 and to the characteristics of the sample, which comprised a young population of students96.

Objective crossing measures.

Crossing opportunities. Analysis of participants’ crossing performance revealed that 92.9% of crossing decisions (393 out of 423) were made with a safety margin exceeding two seconds, while only 7.1% (30 out of 423) fell below this threshold. This restricted distribution of responses suggests that this measure lacks sufficient variability to effectively differentiate between scenarios.

Missed opportunities. In 29.3% of the crossings (n = 124), participants utilised the first available crossing opportunity. In the remaining cases, participants missed the initial crossing opportunity - once (47.8%), twice (12.8%), or three or more times (10.2%), see Fig. 6. A binary variable (0 = no missed opportunity, 1 = one or more missed opportunities) was utilised to analyse the differences among scenarios. A Generalised Estimating Equations (GEE) model was employed, with participants as a random effect to account for individual differences among participants, and scenarios included as the independent variable. The model yielded a significant effect for scenario (Wald χ²(6) = 81.42, p < 0.001). Parameter estimates and scenario-specific estimates are provided in Table 2.

Interim discussion

The analysis demonstrates the relationship between scenario conditions, missed crossing opportunities, and response times, supporting the notion that enhanced walkability may positively affect pedestrian responsiveness and behaviour. Findings hold the potential to enhance safety and the road users’ experience within urban landscapes. The empirical evidence presented highlights the imperative for policymakers and urban planners to integrate walkability considerations into comprehensive mobility frameworks, recognizing their quantifiable impact on pedestrian behaviour. Such integration is essential for the development of safer, more pedestrian-oriented urban environments.

General discussion

Walking, one of the most ancient and environmentally sustainable modes of transport, offers a range of well-documented health benefits1–5. Nevertheless, pedestrian safety in urban environments remains a global concern22,23. While safety is fundamental to promoting walking, incorporating the broader concept of walkability is also essential. Walkability is an urban design and transportation concept that emphasises the creation of pedestrian-friendly environments to encourage walking as a primary mode of travel16–20,53,97.

This study examined the relationship between urban walkability and pedestrian road-crossing behaviour using a semi-immersive, real-world traffic simulation. The findings indicate that a context-specific Walkability Index is associated with both perceived street quality and observed crossing performance, including decision latency and missed crossing opportunities.

The relationship between walkability and pedestrian safety is multifaceted, requiring attention to both trip purpose and behavioural decision-making across multiple levels. Michon’s98 hierarchical model provides a structured framework by distinguishing between strategic (e.g., route selection), tactical (e.g., real-time path adjustments), and operational (e.g., immediate motor responses) levels of behaviour. In pedestrian contexts, strategic assessments of perceived walkability often guide initial route choices, while safety-related behaviours emerge through tactical and operational adaptations to situational demands. This study found that Walkability Index scores aligned closely with participants’ subjective street preferences, whereas decisions related to crossing safety were primarily influenced by the real-world traffic dynamics presented in the dome simulation. Thus, while walkability shapes strategic mobility choices, immediate crossing behaviour is more responsive to localised, dynamic environmental factors.

Walkability should therefore be conceptualised as a broader contextual construct reflecting area-wide mobility orientation, while site-specific crossing safety is determined by immediate conditions. A holistic examination of entire journeys is essential for effective urban design, while immediate crossing events and dynamic site-specific features are more directly linked to crossing safety. Future research incorporating naturalistic observation may yield deeper insights into these interactions, as real-world settings provide greater contextual fidelity and experiential richness than simulated environments.

The semi-immersive simulation was demonstrated as a valuable method for assessing pedestrian behaviour under ecologically valid yet controlled conditions. Unlike traditional laboratory setups, the use of panoramic video enabled realistic and dynamic evaluation of real streetscapes, offering a practical approach for modelling the interaction between environmental features and pedestrian behaviour.

Notably, while no participants reported simulator sickness in the present study, simulator-related discomfort is time-dependent95 and age-related96. Indeed, simulator studies should be aware of and monitor these aspects.

From a policy perspective, the Walkability Index provides a useful instrument for identifying and prioritising urban segments that require safety-oriented interventions. By capturing both environmental characteristics and behavioural responses, the index can guide improvements to street design, crossing infrastructure, and the overall pedestrian experience. Although preliminary, the findings establish a foundation for future research and targeted interventions aimed at reducing pedestrian risk and enhancing walkability in urban areas.

While the findings offer promising insights into the relationship between walkability, safety, and pedestrian decision-making, it is important to acknowledge the study’s limitations.

First, while our study identified significant differences in pedestrian crossing behaviour across the nine crossing locations, nine sites constitute a relatively small sample and may limit the strength of statistical generalisation. Future work should therefore expand both the range and number of intersections examined and may benefit from complementary approaches, such as additional field experiments and application of machine learning models, to assess the robustness of these findings. Nonetheless, it is important to note that the selected locations encompass a diverse set of urban contexts - including central and peripheral cities, dense and suburban neighbourhoods, residential and arterial roads, and varied pedestrian demographics. This diversity enhances the representativeness of the study despite the relatively small number of sites. However, it also constrains the ability to isolate the influence of specific factors, such as sociodemographic context, crossing density, or traffic complexity, which should be explored in greater depth in future research.

Moreover, as pedestrian decision-making, including risk perception and reaction time, is known to vary substantially across age groups, physical abilities, and levels of environmental familiarity42,80, findings related to missed crossing opportunities and latency may not fully generalise to vulnerable populations, such as children, older adults, or individuals with mobility impairments. Given that the current model was developed using a relatively homogeneous student sample, further testing with broader populations and demographic groups is warranted, as this cohort may not reflect the full diversity of pedestrian populations. Factors such as age, physical condition, and familiarity with the environment can influence crossing behaviour, perceptual sensitivity, and responsiveness to environmental cues38,40,42,80. Consequently, the generalisability of the findings regarding missed opportunities and reaction times is constrained. Nevertheless, it should be recognised that when effects are identified within a relatively homogeneous cohort, such as the present sample, they are likely to also manifest - and potentially with greater magnitude - in more heterogeneous populations. The principal concern, therefore, does not lie in the inflation of observed effects, but rather in the potential for a Type II error, whereby meaningful effects that did not reach statistical significance within the restricted sample might emerge as significant in a broader and more demographically varied population. Future research should thus aim to include participants spanning a wider age range and exhibiting greater diversity in mobility and perceptual profiles, so as to more comprehensively evaluate the generalisability and ecological validity of the behavioural patterns observed.

Additionally, while the semi-immersive simulation environment was designed to balance experimental control with ecological relevance, the absence of physical movement limits the operational realism of the task. The use of a button-press to indicate crossing decisions, though less naturalistic, has been validated in prior research80–82 as an effective method for isolating perceptual and cognitive processes from motor execution. As shown in pilot testing, button-press responses were more intuitive and engaging for participants, supporting their use in controlled simulations80, p. 94). This approach offers consistent measurement of decision latency while reducing motor-related variability. Nonetheless, it does not fully capture the embodied nature of real-world pedestrian behaviour.

Future studies may consider more immersive platforms, such as motion-tracked99 or treadmill-based simulations, that complement this approach by enhancing ecological validity and more closely approximating naturalistic crossing dynamics.

Additionally, integrating GPS-based travel data and wearable sensors may provide more detailed and real-time insights into walkability and mobility experiences in diverse urban contexts.

As most studies on pedestrian crossing behaviour lack objective travel data100 and often overlook the link between crossing decisions and overall travel behaviour101, advances in GPS technology present a valuable methodological opportunity. By enabling the collection and segmentation of non-motorised trip data100,102,103, GPS allows integration of discrete crossing points within broader mobility patterns. Moreover, wearable technologies, such as inertial measurement units (IMUs) and in-shoe motion sensor systems (IMS), support the assessment of walking in ecologically valid, real-world environments104,105. Incorporating such tools will support the objective validation of the Walkability Index and further clarify its relationship with pedestrian safety.

Conclusions

Overall, our study reframes walkability as a predictor of pedestrian safety, introducing a locally calibrated Walkability Index that integrates expert-weighted urban features with behavioural validation. By empirically linking walkability with observed pedestrian behaviour in a dome-based simulation using real-world traffic footage, the research bridges the long-standing gap between environmental walkability metrics and safety outcomes. Findings confirm that walkability scores reflect pedestrian perceptions and preferences of street environments and predict key behavioural indicators of risk, such as missed crossing opportunities and decision latency. To contextualise these findings, we propose a multilevel model (Fig. 7) grounded in Michon’s98 framework, linking strategic, tactical, and operational decisions to environmental and individual-level factors. This model provides a structured basis for assessing pedestrian behaviour, with implications for safety-oriented, human-centred urban design and transport planning.

A preliminary multilevel conceptual model for understanding pedestrian walking and crossing behaviour through the lens of perceived walkability and safety. The model is grounded in Michon’s [93] hierarchical framework of road-user behaviour and distinguishes between the strategic level (route planning), tactical level (road crossing), and operational level (crossing behaviour). It incorporates objective environmental factors, such as walkability scores based on land use and physical characteristics, and immediate traffic elements at crossing points, alongside internal and external non-traffic-related influences. These inputs shape pedestrians’ perceived walkability and crossing safety, which in turn affect walking behaviour and, ultimately, crossing decisions.
