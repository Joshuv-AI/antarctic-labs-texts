Phase separation drives cortical enrichment of the F-BAR proteins Rga7 and Rga8 to maintain cell integrity
Authors: Biyu Zheng, Wenfan Wei, Xin Zhang, Lijin Wang, Xing Liu, Xiaoyu Song, Shengqi Xiang, Chao Xu, Chao Wang, Yuan Lin, Shengnan Zheng, Chuanhai Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolarized cell growth necessitates the dynamic remodeling of the plasma membrane, a process requiring BAR domain-containing proteins. While classical BAR proteins, with their crescent-shaped structure, are well characterized, the mechanisms underlying the localization and function of elongated F-BAR proteins remain unclear. Here, we demonstrate that the F-BAR domains of the fission yeast proteins Rga7 and Rga8 undergo liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS). These domains form oligomers via hydrophobic interactions and assemble into condensates through electrostatic interactions mediated by the charged residues at their tips. Mutants deficient in phase separation fail to localize properly at cell poles, leading to defective polar distribution of key regulators, including the Rho GTPases, the exocyst complex, and glucan synthases, all crucial for maintaining cell integrity. We further show that Rga8 requires the actin transport system for tip localization, whereas Rga7 accumulates at cell tips via diffusion. The absence of both Rga7 and Rga8 causes cell lysis. Hence, our findings establish LLPS as a fundamental mechanism for the cortical accumulation and function of F-BAR proteins, providing new insights into their role in membrane dynamics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05977-2.Keywords: Cell polarity, F-BAR, RhoGAP, Phase separation, Schizosaccharomyces pombe

Introduction

Membrane remodeling is a fundamental cellular process that requires a synergistic effort of various families of proteins, including the Bin1/Amphiphysin/Rvs167 (BAR) superfamily [1]. Mammalian proteins Bin1 [2] and Amphiphysin [3], as well as yeast protein Rvs167 [4], are the prototypes of the BAR domain-containing proteins [5]. The BAR domain is composed of three α-helices organized in an antiparallel configuration and has the propensity to form dimers by interacting with each other in a handshake manner [5, 6]. Based on the structural characteristics of the dimeric organization, three subfamilies of BAR proteins are defined: the classical crescent-shaped BAR proteins [7], the elongated (less-curved but more extended) Fes/CIP4 homology-BAR proteins (F-BAR) [8–11], and the inverse-BAR proteins (I-BAR) [5, 12]. The concave face of both classical BAR and F-BAR proteins, but the convex face of the I-BAR proteins, is rich in positively charged amino acid residues, which confer a specific affinity for membrane structures [5]. This inherent membrane-binding affinity enables BAR proteins to perform a wide range of membrane-related functions such as polarized cell growth, cell migration, cytokinesis, and endocytosis [13–18].

Extensive research on classical BAR proteins has established their roles in the generation, sensing, and/or stabilization of membrane curvature [19]. The BAR domains of many BAR proteins are connected to other modules, particularly the Src homology 3 (SH3) domain, and the GTPase-activating protein (GAP) domain, which are implicated in the regulation of the actin cytoskeleton and polarized cell growth [5, 6, 20]. Despite significant advancements in the study of classical BAR proteins, F-BAR proteins have not been well characterized. It remains to be elucidated how the elongated F-BAR domains are spatiotemporally organized and how the characteristic structural organization of F-BAR proteins dictates their subcellular localization and informs their functions.

The human genome encodes approximately twenty F-BAR proteins [20]. However, the fission yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe, which is an excellent model organism for the study of polarized cell growth, cell polarity, cytokinesis, and the cytoskeleton, encodes only seven, namely Cdc15 [21], Imp2 [22], Bzz1 [23], Rga7 [24], Rga8 [25], Rga9 [25], and Syp1 [26]. In addition to a F-BAR domain, Cdc15, Imp2, and Bzz1 carry an additional SH3 domain (two in Bzz1) while Rga7, Rga8, and Rga9 carry an additional RhoGAP domain [25–30]. In line with the organization of their functional domains, Cdc15, Imp2, and Rga7 have been established as key regulators of the contractile ring during cytokinesis [14, 28, 31, 32]. In addition, Cdc15 and Bzz1 play crucial roles in promoting endocytosis [26]. The fission yeast F-BAR proteins, except Imp2, Syp1, and Rga9, localize to both the contractile ring region and cell tips. While the majority of research has focused on the role of F-BAR proteins in cytokinesis, the function of the GAP-containing F-BAR proteins (e.g., Rga7, Rga8, and Rga9) at the cell tips remains less understood. Cdc15 and Imp2 F-BAR domains can oligomerize in a tip-to-tip or tip-to-core fashion [6, 29, 33]. Therefore, the oligomerization potential of the F-BAR domains of Rga7 and Rga8 and its implications for localization and function warrant further investigation.

In the present study, we reveal that the F-BAR domains of Rga7 and Rga8 are capable of undergoing phase separation, a process dependent on specific charged amino acid residues located at the tips of the F-BAR domain. Our microscopic analysis of phase-separation mutants indicates that the phase separation of Rga7 and Rga8 is crucial for the enrichment of Rga7 and Rga8 at cell tips. There, Rga7 and Rga8 work in concert to maintain cell integrity by facilitating the precise localization of Rho GTPases, the exocyst complex, and glucan synthases.

Results

Rga7 is synthetically lethal with rga8

The fission yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe encodes a total of ten RhoGAP proteins [34]. Among them, Rga7, Rga8, and Rga9 are notable for the presence of an F-BAR domain [25, 27, 28]. This domain is characterized by three antiparallel helices that facilitate self-interaction. However, the reason why cells require three structurally similar F-BAR-GAP proteins remains unclear. To tackle the question, we conducted an epistasis analysis of the individual deletions of rga7 (referred to as rga7∆), rga8, and rga9. Tetrad analysis from crosses between these deletions revealed that rga7∆ was synthetically lethal when combined with rga8∆, but not with rga9∆, and that rga8∆ was not synthetically lethal when combined with rga9∆ (Fig. 1A). This synthetic lethality between rga7∆ and rga8∆ has also been previously reported [35]. These findings imply that Rga7 and Rga8 perform parallel functions required to maintain cell viability.

To visualize the impact of depleting both rga7 and rga8 on cell viability, we used the auxin-inducible degron (AID) system [36] to control Rga7 protein levels in rga8∆ cells. We monitored the degradation of Rga7-AID in cells treated with the auxin indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) by western blotting. Rga7-AID was undetectable after half an hour following IAA treatment, indicating efficient degradation of Rga7-AID (Fig. 1B). Microscopic observation revealed that cell lysis took place after half an hour following IAA treatment, with the percentage of lysed cells increasing over time (Fig. 1, C and D). Consistently, rga8∆ cells expressing Rga7-AID (referred to as rga8∆ Rga7-AID) exhibited poor growth on YE medium plates supplemented with 300 nM IAA (Fig. 1E). Collectively, these results suggest that the F-BAR RhoGAPs Rga7 and Rga8 function in parallel to maintain cell integrity.

Rga7 and Rga8 are enriched at cell tips during interphase

Rga7 has been shown to localize to cell tips during interphase and to the contractile ring region during cytokinesis [28, 35]. In addition, Rga8 displays a similar localization pattern as Rga7 [25]. In the present work, we compared the localization and expression of the three F-BAR RhoGAP proteins Rga7, Rga8, and Rga9. According to PomBase (www.pombase.org), the F-BAR domain varies in length among Rga7, Rga8, and Rga9 (Fig. 2A). To assess protein levels, we tagged the three proteins with 2mNeonGreen at their C-termini and expressed them from their native promoters. We found that Rga7-2mNeonGreen and Rga9-2mNeonGreen exhibited comparable protein levels, while Rga8-2mNeonGreen exhibited the highest protein level (Fig. 2B). Microscopic observation confirmed the enrichment of Rga7 and Rga8 at cell tips (Fig. 2C). Rga9 displayed only faint amorphous signals in the cytoplasm (Fig. 2C). Notably, Rga8 also displayed additional punctum-like signals in the cytoplasm and on the cell cortex (Fig. 2C), indicative of the additional localization of Rga8 to endomembrane compartments. Dual color imaging further confirmed the enrichment and colocalization of Rga7 and Rga8 at cell tips and highlighted the additional punctum-like signals of Rga8 in the cytoplasm (Fig. 2D). These data indicate a collaborative role of Rga7 and Rga8 at cell tips in maintaining cell integrity.

Rga7 and Rga8 work in concert to orchestrate the localization of RhoGTPases, the exocyst complex, and glucan synthases to cell tips

To elucidate the synergistic action of Rga7 and Rga8, we first assessed their interdependency of localization to cell tips. The absence of either Rga7 or Rga8 impaired the cell-tip enrichment of the other, indicating partial interdependence of their localization (Fig. 3, A and B). However, further microscopic analysis revealed that their localization at the division site is independent of each other (Fig. S1, A and B). Additionally, the protein levels of Rga7 and Rga8 were found to be independent of each other (Fig. 3, A and B). Since Rga7 and Rga8 function as the GAP proteins for GTPases Rho2 and Rho1 [25, 37], respectively, which play crucial roles in cell wall synthesis [34], we next examined whether their loss affected the localization of GFP-Rho1 and GFP-Rho2. In rga7∆ and rga8∆ cells, GFP-Rho1 and GFP-Rho2 retained normal cortical distribution (Fig. 3, C and D). However, upon simultaneous depletion of both Rga7 and Rga8 using the AID system (as in Fig. 1), the cortical localization of GFP-Rho1 and GFP-Rho2 was significantly disrupted (Fig. 3, E and F), which was accompanied by cell shrinkage. To ensure that the reduction in membrane-bound GFP-Rho1 and GFP-Rho2 is specific and not due to a general loss of cell viability, we included a t-SNARE GFP-Psy1, which localizes to the plasma membrane [38], as a negative control. Our results showed that the intensity of GFP-Psy1 decreased only slightly with or without IAA treatment, primarily due to photobleaching (Fig. S1C). This indicates that the observed changes in GFP-Rho1 and GFP-Rho2 localization are specific and not a result of general cell viability issues. To further explore the localization of active Rho1, we constructed an active Rho1 probe as previously described [39–41]. We found that the localization of active Rho1 at the cell tip was lost upon simultaneous depletion of Rga7 and Rga8 (Fig. S1D), consistent with the loss of overall GFP-Rho1 localization observed in Fig. 3E. Collectively, these results suggest that Rga7 and Rga8 collaborate to regulate the cortical localization of Rho1 and Rho2.

Given the cell shrinkage and lysis observed upon deletion of both Rga7 and Rga8 (Figs. 1, C and D and 3, E and F), we hypothesized that Rga7 and Rga8 might play a role in maintaining cell wall integrity. Beyond Rho GTPases, cell wall integrity depends on the exocyst complex and glucan synthases [42, 43]. To determine whether Rga7 and Rga8 influence these components, we used the AID system to assess their localization in rga8∆ Rga7-AID cells following auxin-induced degradation of Rga7-AID. Live-cell imaging experiments revealed progressive loss of cell-tip enrichment for the exocyst subunits Sec3-GFP and Exo70-GFP, as well as the glucan synthases GFP-Ags1 (Fig. 3, G–I) and GFP-Bgs1 (Fig. S1E). This mislocalization coincided with cellular shrinkage, further supporting a role for Rga7 and Rga8 in maintaining proper membrane trafficking and cell wall synthesis. Thus, our results demonstrate that Rga7 and Rga8 act synergistically to orchestrate the localization of Rho GTPases, exocyst components, and glucan synthases at cell tips, ensuring cell wall integrity.

The F-BAR domains of Rga7 and Rga8 undergo liquid-liquid phase separation

During the purification of the recombinant F-BAR truncated proteins of Rga7 and Rga8, we observed that these truncated proteins were capable of undergoing liquid-liquid phase separation (Fig. 4, A and B). To test that these proteins were functional, we first performed circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy on the purified proteins expressed in E. coli (Fig. S2A). The results showed specific absorption peaks at 208 nm and 222 nm, characteristic of alpha helices, indicating that the Rga7 and Rga8 F-BAR domains were correctly folded. Given that most F-BAR proteins preferentially bind to phosphatidylserine (PS) or various phosphorylated phosphatidylinositol species [44], we examined the functionality of the recombinant proteins using liposome reconstitution assays as previously described [45–47]. For these assays, silica microspheres were coated with lipid mixtures containing phosphatidylcholine (PC) alone or in combination with phosphatidylserine (PS). The recombinant Rga7 F-BAR-GFP and Rga8 F-BAR-GFP proteins were then incubated with these coated microspheres. The results indicated that both Rga7 F-BAR-GFP and Rga8 F-BAR-GFP exhibited significantly enhanced binding to silica microspheres coated with PC plus PS compared to those coated with PC alone (Fig. S2B). Therefore, the Rga7 and Rga8 F-BAR domains are functional.

Next, we observed the formation of droplets of the recombinant F-BAR truncated proteins under various protein and NaCl concentrations, as previously described [48]. Our observations showed that the liquid-liquid phase separation of Rga7 F-BAR and Rga8 F-BAR is influenced by protein and NaCl concentrations (Fig. 4, A and B). Notably, the F-BAR domain of Rga8 appeared to have a stronger ability to undergo phase separation, as evidenced by the requirement of higher NaCl concentration to disrupt the phase separation process (Fig. 4, A and B). The phase separation ability was enhanced by co-incubation of Rga7 F-BAR and Rga8 F-BAR (Fig. 4, C and D). To determine whether this synergy reflected direct physical interaction, we further performed GST pull-down assays, which confirmed binding between the two F-BAR domains (Fig. 4E). Additionally, co-immunoprecipitation experiments confirmed the interaction between Rga7 and Rga8 (Fig. 4F). Since directly fluorescently labeled proteins impaired phase separation, we introduced color by mixing Rga7 F-BAR with Rga7 F-BAR-mCherry at a molar ratio of 100:1 and Rga8 F-BAR with Rga8 F-BAR-GFP at a molar ratio of 100:1. Co-phase separation of the two proteins was then demonstrated at various concentrations (Fig. 4G).

Further microscopic analysis revealed that endogenous Rga7-GFP and Rga8-GFP concentrated and formed fusion-fission puncta at the cell cortex, likely driven by phase separation (Fig. S3A). To assess the liquid-like behavior of Rga7 and Rga8 in cells, we overexpressed GFP-Rga8, which formed large puncta that colocalized with Rga7-tdTomato (Fig. 4H). Moreover, Cdc15-tdTomato (Fig. 4H) and Imp2-tdTomato (Fig. S3B), another F-BAR proteins in fission yeast, remained localized to the division site and/or distinct cortical foci, indicating that GFP-Rga8 specifically triggers Rga7 F-BAR to form puncta. Rga8 was overexpressed to enhance the phase separation phenomenon, which is crucial for observing the dynamic behavior of Rga8. Live-cell imaging experiments confirmed that these GFP-Rga8 condensates, which colocalized with Rga7-tdTomato, dynamically fused and divided (Fig. 4I). This dynamic nature aligns with their capability of liquid-liquid phase separation and forming condensates.

To characterize the material properties of these condensates, we conducted fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) experiments, showing that the signal recovery of Rga7-F-BAR-GFP droplets was slower than that of Rga8-F-BAR-GFP droplets (Fig. 5A). This finding suggests that Rga8 F-BAR condensates have a higher liquidity property than Rga7 F-BAR condensates. We next investigated the cortical dynamics of Rga7-GFP and Rga8-GFP in vivo. FRAP experiments revealed that both Rga7-GFP and Rga8-GFP were highly dynamic at the cell cortex (Fig. 5B) and that these proteins have only a minor influence on each other’s cortical dynamics (Fig. 5C).

The phase-separation property promotes the enrichment of Rga7 and Rga8 at cell tips

It has been demonstrated that F-BAR domains can oligomerize via tip-to-tip or tip-to-core interactions [6, 29, 33]. Therefore, we reasoned that the residues located at the tip region are pivotal for the self-interaction and phase-separation processes of F-BAR domain-containing proteins. Consequently, we directed our efforts towards the charged amino acid residues within the loop region at the apical tips of both Rga7 F-BAR and Rga8 F-BAR domains (Fig. 6A). Multiple attempts of mutagenesis identified four lysine residues (K193, K194, K199, and K201) in the Rga7 F-BAR domain and six lysine residues (K62, K64, K65, K141, K142, and K144) in the Rga8 F-BAR domain that are responsible for Rga7 F-BAR and Rga8 F-BAR phase-separation (Fig. 6A). Specifically, lysine was replaced with alanine, yielding two phase-separation deficient mutants: Rga7 F-BAR-4A and Rga8 F-BAR-6A. Moreover, pull-down assay showed that these mutation variants of Rga7 F-BAR and Rga8 F-BAR domains had reduced binding affinities (Fig. S4 A-C).

To assess the oligomerization behavior of the phase-separation mutants of Rga7 F-BAR and Rga8 F-BAR, we conducted a comparative purification study of Rga7 F-BAR-4A, Rga8 F-BAR-6A, and their respective wild-type proteins using gel filtration chromatography (Fig. 6, B and C). Notably, the chromatographic elution profiles of both the mutants and their wild-type counterparts were strikingly similar, each exhibiting a distinct peak that corresponds to the oligomeric forms of Rga7-F-BAR and Rga8-F-BAR. These findings suggest that the mutations introduced do not significantly perturb the inherent oligomerization propensity of these proteins.

To further test the above result, we employed a biochemical crosslinking assay using ethylene glycol bis (succinimidyl succinate) (EGS) [49, 50]. The results demonstrated that Rga7 F-BAR predominantly formed dimers, as well as trimers, and tetramers, in the presence of 4 mM EGS (Fig. 6D). The oligomerization process was disrupted by the addition of 30% 1,6-Hexanediol, but not by the addition of 400 mM NaCl. Similar results were observed for the Rga7 F-BAR-4A mutant (Fig. 6D). Likewise, both Rga8 F-BAR and Rga8 F-BAR-6A predominantly formed dimers in the presence of 4 mM EGS, with dimerization being sensitive to the addition of 40% 1,6-Hexanediol, but not to 400 mM NaCl (Fig. 6E). The higher concentration of 1,6-Hexanediol (40%) was required to disrupt the multimerization of Rga8 F-BAR and Rga8 F-BAR-6A, indicating that the hydrophobic interactions within the Rga8 F-BAR domain are stronger compared to those in the Rga7 F-BAR domain. The application of 1,6-Hexanediol significantly impaired the phase-separation capability of both Rga7 F-BAR and Rga8 F-BAR (Fig. S4D), and a higher concentration of 1,6-Hexanediol was necessary to disrupt the formation of Rga8 F-BAR droplets. Therefore, these results suggest that Rga7 F-BAR and Rga8 F-BAR form oligomers through hydrophobic interactions. It is plausible that these oligomeric structures facilitate phase separation via multivalent interactions that are mediated by the charged tip regions of the respective F-BAR domains. Consequently, Rga7 F-BAR and Rga8 F-BAR use their oligomeric forms as building blocks to construct a more intricate network via phase separation.

To study the functional significance of Rga7 and Rga8 phase separation, Rga7-4A and Rga8-6A, along with their wild-type counterparts, were expressed from their native promoters at endogenous levels. Western blotting analysis confirmed comparable expression between wild-type and mutant proteins (Fig. 6, F and G). Microscopic observations further revealed that both Rga7-4A-GFP and Rga8-6A-GFP exhibited significantly reduced enrichment at cell tips and the division site compared with their wild-type counterparts (Fig. 6, F and G; Fig. S4, E and F), underscoring a crucial role of Rga7 and Rga8 phase-separation in localization. In consistence with the finding that the cell-tip localization of Rga7 and Rga8 is only partially interdependent (Fig. 3, A and B), Rga7-4A-GFP and Rga8-6A-GFP did not appear to affect the enrichment of Rga8-tdTomato and Rga7-tdTomato at cell tips, respectively (Fig. S5, A and B). These data suggest that phase separation is required to enrich both Rga7 and Rga8 at cell tips.

Given that the localization of Rga7 depends on Rng10 [14, 35], we investigated whether the residual localization of Rga7-4A at the cell cortex is mediated by Rng10. We examined the localization of Rga7-4A in wild-type and rng10∆ cells and found that Rga7-4A-GFP completely lacked localization in rng10∆ cells (Fig. S5C), validating our hypothesis that the residual localization of Rga7-4A at the cell cortex is mediated by Rng10. Notably, Rga7-GFP retained a little localization at the cell cortex in rng10∆ cells, likely due to the presence of Rga8 (Fig. S5C). Additionally, rng10 deletion did not affect the protein levels of Rga7-GFP and Rga7-4A-GFP. Co-immunoprecipitation assays further revealed that both Rga7 and Rga7-4A interacted with Rng10. Collectively, these findings suggest that both Rng10 and phase separation play crucial roles in enriching Rga7 at cell tips.

To further assess the phase separation dependence of tip enrichment, we treated cells expressing Rga7-GFP and Rga8-GFP with 5% 1,6-Hexanediol (for 15 min) (Fig. 6H) or 400 mM NaCl (for 30 min) (Fig. 6I) [51, 52]. In parallel experiments, Tea1, a cell tip-localized protein delivered by growing microtubule plus ends [53], and Opy1, a pleckstrin homology (PH) domain-containing protein localizing on the cell cortex [46, 54], were used as negative controls. Treatment of Tea1-GFP-expressing or Opy1-2mNeonGreen cells with 5% 1,6-Hexanediol did not significantly affect their cortical localization (Fig. S5, E and F). Moreover, Psy1, a t-SNARE localizing to the cell cortex [38, 55], was used as a negative control (Fig. S5G). Treatment of GFP-Psy1-expressing cells with 5% 1,6-Hexanediol (for 15 min) or 400 mM NaCl (for 30 min) did not influence the cortical localization of GFP-Psy1 (Fig. S5G). By contrast, treatment with 1,6-Hexanediol effectively disrupted the cortical localization of Rga7 and Rga8 (Fig. 6H and Fig. S5H), whereas 400 mM NaCl selectively affected their enrichment at cell tips without significantly impairing overall cortical localization (Fig. 6I and Fig. S5I). Given our previous demonstration that high salt concentrations disrupt Rga7/Rga8 phase separation in vitro (Fig. 4, A and B), these in vivo results support a model in which the phase-separation capability of Rga7-GFP and Rga8-GFP is crucial for their enrichment at cell tips.

Targeted delivery of Rga8 to cell tips requires the actin transport system but not the exocyst complex.

In fission yeast, actin cables and motor Myo52 form a long-range transport system that functions in parallel with the exocyst-dependent tethering system to mediate polarized exocytosis and cell growth [56]. To determine whether the two systems influence the cortical localization of Rga7 and Rga8, we examined Rga7-tdTomato and Rga8-tdTomato in wild-type, for3∆ (lacking actin cables), myo52∆ (lacking the Myo52 motor), and exo70∆ (lacking the exocyst component Exo70) cells. Microscopic analysis revealed that both actin cables and Myo52, but not Exo70, were required for Rga8-tdTomato enrichment at growing cell tips (Fig. 7, A-C). By contrast, none of these components was necessary for Rga7-tdTomato enrichment at growing cell tips (Fig. 7, A-C). These results demonstrate that the cell-tip enrichment of Rga8 depends on the actin transport system, but not the exocyst tethering system, while Rga7 localization is independent of both systems.

To directly characterize protein dynamics, we employed live-cell microscopy to observe wild-type and for3∆ cells co-expressing Rga7-mNeonGreen and Rga8-tdTomato at 2-second intervals. In wild-type cells, cytoplasmic Rga8-tdTomato puncta exhibited rapid, directed movement toward cell poles, whereas in for3∆ cells, Rga8-tdTomato exhibited random, non-directional motility (Fig. 7D). Rga7-mNeonGreen showed cortical enrichment in both genetic backgrounds, with similar mobility patterns (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, co-visualization of Myo52-tdTomato with either Rga8-GFP or its phase separation-deficient mutant (Rga8-6A-GFP) revealed that wild-type Rga8-GFP colocalized with Myo52-tdTomato during poleward transport (Fig. 7E and Fig. S6, A and B), while Rga8-6A-GFP remained diffusely cytoplasmic with minimal Myo52-tdTomato association (Fig. 7E and Fig. S6, A and B). Additionally, the cortical localization of Myo52 was unaffected by the phase-separation-deficient mutant of Rga8 (Fig. S6A). These findings establish that Rga8 phase separation plays a crucial role in its transport and enrichment to growing cell tips. Collectively, these results support a model in which Rga8 relies on the actin transport system to target cell tips, while Rga7 is targeted to the cell tip by its interaction with Rng10, independent of the actin transport system and the exocyst complex, and is further enriched via phase separation (Fig. 7E, right).

Phase-separation of Rga7 and Rga8 is required to prevent cell Lysis

Cells lacking both Rga7 and Rga8 are susceptible to lysis (Fig. 1, B-D). To elucidate the biological importance of the phase separation characteristics of Rga7 and Rga8, we expressed Rga7-4A-GFP, Rga8-6A-GFP, and their respective wild-type forms in rga8Δ Rga7-AID cells. We then quantified the lysed cell percentage under conditions both lacking and containing IAA, which induces the degradation of Rga7-AID (Fig. 8A). The quantification indicated that the simultaneous absence of Rga8 and Rga7 led to significant cell lysis, approximately 60%. The presence of either Rga7-GFP or Rga8-GFP, but not their phase-separation-deficient variants Rga7-4A-GFP or Rga8-6A-GFP, was sufficient to counteract the lysis phenotype (Fig. 8B). These findings suggest that the phase-separation properties of Rga7 and Rga8 are essential for preventing cell lysis.

Discussion

Due to the abundance of positively charged amino acid residues on their concave or convex surfaces, BAR domain-containing proteins exhibit a natural affinity for membranes and are pivotal in various membrane-related functions [5, 6]. To efficiently mediate cellular functions, particularly F-BAR domain-containing proteins are often found concentrated at specific cortical regions, such as the growing tips and the contractile ring region [26]. Despite this inherent membrane affinity, the precise mechanism by which F-BAR domain-containing proteins concentrate in these compartments remains unclear. Furthermore, the role of multiple F-BAR domain-containing proteins coexisting in the same membrane compartment has been unclear. In this study, we reveal that the F-BAR domains of Rga7 and Rga8 are capable of undergoing liquid-liquid phase separation, a property essential for their enrichment at cell tips, where they collaborate to maintain cell integrity (Fig. 8C).

To our knowledge, this study provides the first evidence that the structural F-BAR domain of BAR-domain proteins possesses liquid-liquid phase separation properties. Notably, Cdc15, an F-BAR domain-containing protein in Schizosaccharomyces pombe, was also reported to undergo phase separation in vitro [57]. However, the phase separation of Cdc15 is dependent on an adjacent intrinsic disorder region (IDR), not the F-BAR domain itself. Thus, our research establishes the novel concept that the structural F-BAR domains are inherently capable of undergoing liquid-liquid phase separation.

The concept of the phase-separation properties of Rga7 and Rga8 F-BAR domains is supported by biochemical and cell biological evidence. First, the F-BAR domains of the Rga7 and Rga8 truncation proteins undergo liquid-liquid phase separation, influenced by protein and salt concentrations in vitro (Fig. 4, A and B). Notably, Rga8-F-BAR demonstrates a stronger phase-separation propensity than Rga7-F-BAR, requiring a higher NaCl concentration to disrupt its droplet formation. Second, specific lysine residues—four in Rga7 and six in Rga8—are identified to be responsible for the phase separation of their respective F-BAR domains (Fig. 6A). Third, endogenous Rga7-GFP and Rga8-GFP are observed to concentrate at the growth tips of the cell cortex, forming foci-like structures that exhibit continuous fusion and fission (Fig. 5B and Fig. S3A). Fourth, upon overexpression, GFP-Rga8 forms condensates, which colocalized with Rga7, and dynamically fuses and divides (Fig. 4, H and I). Fifth, the enrichment of Rga7 and Rga8 at cell tips is disrupted by 1,6-Hexanediol (Fig. 6H; Fig. S5H).

Mechanistic studies suggest that Rga7 F-BAR and Rga8 F-BAR form condensates in a stepwise fashion (Fig. 8C). Initially, they oligomerize, predominantly into dimers, trimers, and tetramers for Rga7 F-BAR, and mainly into dimers for Rga8 F-BAR, through hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 6, B-E). This oligomerization is facilitated by the characteristic tripartite helices within the BAR domains, which mediate self-interactions primarily through hydrophobic forces, as previously reported [5]. These oligomers then act as building blocks, each equipped with multiple electrostatic interaction sites, to assemble into condensates. The stepwise model highlights the crucial role of both hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions in the formation of Rga7 F-BAR/Rga8 F-BAR condensates.

Our findings reveal that specific mutations of lysine to alanine in the F-BAR domains significantly impact the homotypic interactions between these domains (Fig. S4, A-C), but have only a minimal effect on their oligomerization capabilities (Fig. 6, B-E). The minimal effect on oligomerization is consistent with the minor effect that NaCl has on the oligomerization properties of the F-BAR domains (Fig. 6, D and E), suggesting that the inherent oligomerization propensity is primarily driven by hydrophobic interactions rather than electrostatic interactions. Given the substantial effect of 1,6-hexanediol on the oligomerization and phase-separation capabilities of these F-BAR domains (Figs. 6, D-E, and S4D), it is evident that hydrophobic interactions play a dominant role in these processes. Nonetheless, in vitro LLPS assays show a clear relevance of the mutated lysine residues to phase separation (Fig. 6A), indicating that while the mutations minimally affect the oligomerization capabilities, they significantly impact homotypic interactions and phase separation. These findings underscore the importance of considering both electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions in understanding the behavior of these domains in phase separation and oligomerization.

Our in vitro experiments provide clear evidence that the F-BAR domains of Rga7 and Rga8 co-localize and enhance the stability and properties of these condensates (Figs. 4, C-E, and G). However, in vivo experiments reveal that while Rga7 and Rga8 do interact, their dynamics at the cell cortex are only minimally affected by the absence of the other protein (Figs. 4F and 5 and B-C). This suggests that although the collaborative LLPS properties of the Rga7 and Rga8 F-BAR domains are evident in vitro, their collaborative role in driving cortical enrichment in vivo awaits further investigation. It is worth noting that the localization of Rga7 and Rga8 at the cell tip shows partial interdependence (Fig. 3, A and B). However, their localization at the division site is independent of each other (Fig. S1, A and B). This suggests that their localization at the division site is not related to the synergistic liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) properties of the F-BAR domains. Instead, it may be regulated by other mechanisms or interactions specific to the division site.

The formation of Rga7 and Rga8 condensates likely stabilizes and concentrates the F-BAR proteins at the cell tips. Despite the phase-separation property of these proteins, it does not seem to directly influence their binding to the plasma membrane. This is evidenced by the finding that the phase-separation mutants, Rga7-4A and Rga8-6A, still localize to the plasma membrane (Fig. 6, F and G). However, the amount of these mutant proteins on the membrane is significantly reduced (Fig. 6, F and G). Consistent with this model, it has been reported that the F-BAR domain of Rga7 interacts with Rng10, and this interaction enhances the membrane-binding ability of Rga7 [27, 35]. Nevertheless, the phase-separation property of Rga7 and Rga8 is required to direct the enrichment of both proteins to the cell tips (Fig. 6, F and G). This study further demonstrates that Rga8 relies on the actin transport system to be enriched at cell tips, while Rga7 is enriched at cell tips by Rng10 and phase separation (Fig. 7, A-E), which is independent of the actin transport system and the exocyst complex. Similar to the Cdc15 F-BAR domain, which acts as a platform to organize the actin cytoskeleton by recruiting various proteins such as Cdc12 and Pxl1 [32], the Rga7 F-BAR domain interacts not only with Rng10 (Fig. S5D) [27, 35], but also with the Rga8 F-BAR domain (Fig. 4E). It is plausible that Rga7 and Rga8 form a compartment at the cortical tips through F-BAR domain-mediated phase separation, followed by the recruitment of multiple factors to regulate specific functions. Identifying and characterizing these recruiting factors systematically is an interesting direction for future research.

We also recognize the limitations of our study. For example, the use of overexpression systems and in vitro assays may not fully recapitulate the complex cellular environment. Future work will focus on exploring the molecular mechanisms of the phase separation of Rga7 and Rga8 in vivo and elucidating their roles in different cellular processes.

The synergistic relationship between Rga7 and Rga8 is crucial for maintaining cell viability and integrity. Our epistasis analysis revealed that cells lacking both proteins are lethal (Fig. 1A), and the simultaneous absence of Rga7 and Rga8 results in cell shrinkage and lysis (Fig. 1, B-D). Additionally, we found that the absence of Rga7 and Rga8 impairs the cortical localization of RhoGTPase Rho1/2, the exocyst component Sect. 3 and Exo70, and the glucan synthase Ags1 (Fig. 3, C-I). Notably, ectopic expression of Rga7 or Rga8, but not their phase-separation-deficient mutants Rga7-4A and Rga8-6A, can rescue the lytic phenotype caused by the loss of both proteins. While this finding highlights the importance of the phase separation of Rga7 and Rga8 in maintaining cell integrity, it remains unclear whether the phase separation of Rga7 and Rga8 directly mediates the localization of the exocyst complex and glucan synthases. A more plausible explanation is that Rga7 and Rga8 form a scaffold through phase separation, which ensures the proper Rho1 localization at the cell tip. This localization is crucial for actin polarization, which facilitates the transport of Sec3 and Exo70 to the cell tip via actin cables [34, 56, 58]. As a result, efficient membrane trafficking, supported by this scaffold and actin polarization, maintains the proper localization of Ags1 and Bgs1, preserving cell integrity.

Both Rga7-GFP and Rga8-GFP are enriched at the cell tips during interphase and at the division site during cytokinesis (Fig. 2C). Our co-localization experiments further support the notion that Rga7 and Rga8 function collaboratively at the cell tips (Fig. 2D). However, despite the genetic evidence suggesting a collaborative role for Rga7 and Rga8 in maintaining cell integrity, their localization patterns are partially different (Fig. 2D). This difference in localization suggests that Rga7 and Rga8 may also function within distinct complexes and have separate roles. Future research is needed to further explore the specific mechanisms by which Rga7 and Rga8 function, as well as how they work together to ensure cell integrity.

In conclusion, our study establishes that the F-BAR domains of Rga7 and Rga8 exhibit liquid-liquid phase separation properties, which are necessary for concentrating these proteins at cell tips. At cell tips, Rga7 and Rga8 collaborate to ensure the correct localization of various factors, including RhoGTPase, the exocyst complex, and glucan synthases, all of which are vital for maintaining cell integrity.

Materials and methods

Yeast genetics and strain construction

The yeast strains used in this study are detailed in Table S1. These strains were derived through either tetrad dissection or random spore analysis. For gene tagging and deletion, a PCR-based homologous recombination approach was employed, utilizing the pFA6a plasmid. Yeast transformation was carried out by the lithium acetate method [59].

The fluorescently tagged proteins shown in Figs. 2, 3, 4, 6 and 7, S1, S3, S4, S5 and S6 were expressed from their endogenous promoters, with the following exceptions: GFP-Rho1, GFP-Rho2, GFP-Ags1 in Fig. 3 and GFP-Bgs1 in Fig. S1, which were expressed from the ase1 promoter, GFP-Rga8 (in Figs. 4 and S3), which was expressed from the nmt promoter, and RBD-tdTomato (in Fig. S1), which was expressed from the pck2 promoter. The fluorescently tagged proteins shown in Figs. 5 and 8 were expressed from the ase1 promoter.

Yeast cell culture

For live-cell imaging, strains were cultured in Edinburgh minimal medium (EMM) supplemented with adenine, leucine, uracil, histidine, and lysine (0.225 g/L each), referred to as EMM5S. Cultures were incubated at 30 °C with continuous shaking at 220 rpm overnight. Cells at the exponential growth phase were harvested for subsequent analysis. For biochemical assays, strains were grown in yeast extract medium with the aforementioned supplements (referred to as YE 5 S).

Molecular cloning

The plasmids used in this work are listed in Table S2. Plasmid construction was achieved either by the traditional digestion-ligation method using enzymes from New England Biolabs (NEB) or by a one-step cloning technique based on homologous recombination, provided by Vazyme Biotech.

To obtain the full-length rga7 and its truncations, the coding sequence was amplified by PCR from yeast genomic DNA. The PCR products were digested with BglII and NotI and subsequently cloned into the pJK148 vector using T4 DNA ligase. To obtain the full-length rga8 and its truncations, the coding sequence was amplified by PCR from a yeast cDNA library, and BamHI and NotI were used for restriction digestion. Specific lysine residues in Rga7 (Lys193, 194, 199, and 201) and Rga8 (Lys62, 64, 65, 141, 142, and 144) were mutated to alanine using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (www.vazymebiotech.com).

The Rho probe was designed using the rho-binding domain of the budding yeast protein kinase C (PKC1) as before [39, 40, 60]. We employed a two-step homologous recombination strategy. First, the budding yeast PKC1 fragment, spanning base pairs 1–1173, was amplified and inserted into a tdTomato-pjk148 vector, replacing the original ase1 promoter downstream of the multiple cloning site (MCS). This construct was then further modified by replacing the ase1 promoter with the fission yeast pkc2 promoter, which was amplified from − 464 to − 1 upstream of the start codon of pkc2. The plasmid was verified by DNA sequencing to ensure the correct insertion and orientation of all components.

Live-cell microscopy and data analysis

Cells without IAA treatment were immobilized between a 3% EMM5S agarose pad and a glass coverslip. Imaging was performed using a PerkinElmer UltraVIEW VoX spinning disk confocal microscope. This system was equipped with a Hamamatsu C9100-23B electron-multiplying charge-coupled device (EMCCD) camera and a Nikon CFI Apochromat TIRF 100X objective lens (NA = 1.49). The microscope was controlled using Volocity software from PerkinElmer. All imaging was performed at ambient temperature.

For acquiring images in Figs. 1C and 8B, cultures were treated with 3 µM IAA and incubated at 30 °C for varying durations before imaging. An Olympus IX73 microscope was employed at ambient temperature. This system is equipped with a 100X objective lens (NA = 1.46) and a cooled digital color camera (model DP73-1–51) and is controlled by Cell Sense software from Olympus. In Fig. 3 and S1, to detect rapid changes in related proteins following the double deletion of Rga7 and Rga8 functions, cell cultures were quickly treated with 6 µM IAA. Subsequently, the collected cells were added to agar containing 6 µM IAA for imaging.

For high-temporal resolution time-lapse microscopy, images were acquired every second. For other microscopy, stack images consisting of 11 planes with a 0.5 μm interval between the planes were acquired every 30 s.

To measure the fluorescence intensity changes of proteins along the cell membrane, we employed line-scan analysis. To ensure data comparability, we selected cells with lengths of 10–15 μm from each field of view and analyzed their fluorescence intensity profiles along the cell membrane. Data analysis was performed using MetaMorph software version 7.7 (Molecular Devices) and Fiji ImageJ (National Institutes of Health). Graphs and plots were generated using KaleidaGraph 4.5 (www.synergy.com).

Propidium iodide staining for the detection of cell death

Cells were cultured in YE5S medium overnight. Experimental groups were treated with 3 µM IAA for 4 h, while control groups were treated with an equivalent volume of DMSO. After treatment, cell death was assessed using Propidium Iodide (PI) staining. Cells were stained with 10 µg/mL PI for 15 min at 30℃. Following staining, cells were washed once with YE5S medium to remove excess PI. Imaging was performed using an Olympus microscope.

Protein expression and purification

Plasmids encoding pET28m-His-Rga7-F-BAR, pET28m-His-Rga7-F-BAR-4A, pET28m-His-Rga8-F-BAR, and pET28m-His-Rga8-F-BAR-6A were individually transformed into BL21(DE3) Escherichia coli cells for the expression of His-tagged fusion proteins. Similarly, plasmids encoding pGEX-6P-Rga7-F-BAR and pGEX-6P-Rga8-F-BAR were transformed into Rosetta Escherichia coli cells for the expression of GST-tagged fusion proteins. Protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG at 16 °C when the optical density (OD600) of the culture reached between 0.8 and 1.0.

For the purification of His-tagged proteins, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5,000 g for 25 min. The resulting cell pellets were resuspended in 30 mL wash/lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 300 mM NaCl, and 30 mM imidazole, pH 8.0) containing cocktail protease inhibitors and 1 mM PMSF. Cell lysis was performed using a high-pressure homogenizer at pressures ranging from 600 to 800 MPa, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C for 40 min at 16,200 g to separate the soluble protein fraction. The supernatant was incubated with HisSep Ni-NTA Agarose Resin 6FF (QIAGEN) to bind His-tagged proteins. After incubation, the resin was washed with 50 mL of the wash/lysis buffer, and His-tagged proteins were eluted with 5 mL of elution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 300 mM NaCl, and 500 mM imidazole, pH 8.0). Purified proteins were further processed using an AKTA Prime system with a size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 300 mM NaCl, pH 7.8).

For GST-tagged proteins, the cell pellets were resuspended in 30 mL lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, and 300 mM NaCl) containing cocktail protease inhibitors and 1 mM PMSF. Cell lysis and centrifugation steps were performed as described for His-tagged proteins. The supernatant was incubated with 500 µl glutathione–Sepharose resins (GE Healthcare) to bind GST-tagged proteins. The resin was washed with 20 mL lysis buffer containing 0.1% Triton X-100 to remove unbound proteins. Purified GST-fused proteins were stored in a storage buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 20% glycerol) at −20 °C.

Gel filtration chromatography

Equal concentrations of wild-type and mutant proteins were prepared for analysis. Gel filtration chromatography was performed using a HiLoadTM 16/600 SuperdexTM 200 pg column from GE Healthcare. The column was pre-equilibrated with a size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) buffer consisting of 50 mM Tris-HCl and 300 mM NaCl at a pH of 7.8.

Protein expression analysis

The expression levels of proteins in fission yeast cells were evaluated using trichloroacetic acid (TCA) lysis [61]. Fission yeast cells in the exponential growth phase were harvested and washed with 800 µL of distilled deionized water (ddH₂O). Cells were then resuspended in 50 µL of 20% TCA (www.sinopharm.com) for lysis. To disrupt the cells, 200 µL of glass beads (www.biospec.com; catalog number 11079105) were added, and cells were disrupted using a Retsch MM400 mechanical disruptor. Following cell lysis, an additional 50 µL of 20% TCA was added, and the mixture was vortexed to ensure homogeneity. Subsequently, 400 µL of 5% TCA was introduced, and the mixture was vortexed again. After removing the glass beads, the samples were centrifuged at 16,200 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting precipitates were then treated with 175 µL of 1X SDS sample buffer and 25 µL of 1.5 M Tris-HCl at pH 8.0, and the mixture was boiled at 100 °C for 5 min. The protein samples were subsequently analyzed by western blotting using specific antibodies. Primary antibodies included those against GFP (catalog number 600-101-215; Rockland; 1:2000 dilution), tdTomato (home-made; GenScript; 1:2000 dilution), anti-mNeonGreen (home-made; GenScript; 1:2000 dilution), anti-mini-AID (catalog number M214-3; MBL; 1:2000 dilution), and an anti-tubulin antibody (catalog number 63–160; Bio Academia; 1:10000 dilution).

GST pull-down assay

His-fused proteins were pre-cleaned with glutathione resins at 4 °C for 10 min to reduce non-specific background interactions. The pre-cleaned His-fused proteins were incubated with GST-proteins bound to glutathione resins in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, and 150 mM NaCl) supplemented with 0.1% Triton X-100 at 4 °C for 90 min. After incubation, the resins were subjected to a series of washes: five times with TBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100, and once with TBS to remove any residual detergent. The resins were then resuspended in 30 µL of SDS sample buffer and boiled at 100 °C for 5 min to elute the bound proteins. The pull-down samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE followed by western blotting using antibodies against His (WH080027/AE003; Abclonal; diluted 2000-fold) and GST (WH080027/AE006; Abclonal; diluted 1:2000).

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

To prepare the cell lysate, 1 L of strains expressing GFP and tdTomato-tagged proteins were cultured overnight until the OD600 reached approximately 0.8. The cells were then harvested and ground in liquid nitrogen using a mortar grinder RM 200 (Retsch). The resulting cell lysate was dissolved in TBS lysis buffer containing 1% Triton X-100 and a cocktail of protease inhibitors and then incubated at 4 °C for 30 min. The lysate was centrifuged at 4 °C and 13,000 rpm for 20 min to obtain the supernatant, which was designated as the input. This supernatant was transferred to a new EP tube and centrifuged again for 10 min under the same conditions. Dynabeads Protein G beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added to the supernatant, followed by the addition of either tdTomato antibody (homemade; GenScript; 5 µg) or GFP antibody (homemade; GenScript; 5 µg). The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min and then transferred to 4 °C for 1 h to allow the antibodies to bind to the beads. The supernatant containing the antibody-bound beads was incubated at 4 °C for an additional hour to facilitate immunoprecipitation. The beads were then washed five times with 1× TBS buffer containing 0.1% Triton X-100, followed by a final wash with 1× TBS buffer to remove any unbound proteins.

Finally, the immunoprecipitated proteins were analyzed by western blotting using the following antibodies: GFP antibody (600-101-215; Rockland; 1:2000 dilution) and tdTomato antibody (homemade; GenScript; 1:2000 dilution).

In vitro phase separation assays

His-Rga7-F-BAR, His-Rga7-F-BAR-4A, His-Rga8-F-BAR, and His-Rga8-F-BAR-6A proteins were purified using gel filtration chromatography. The purified proteins were then concentrated to a range of 0.4 to 0.6 mM. Before further experimentation, the protein samples were pre-cleaned by centrifugation at 16,200 g for 20 min at 4 °C to ensure purity. After cleaning, the proteins were diluted to the specific concentrations required for the assay, using a range of NaCl concentrations from 100 mM to 500 mM or 1,6-Hexanediol (1,6-HD) at concentrations varying from 0.1% to 10%. The dilution process was conducted on ice to maintain optimal protein stability. The final volume of each protein mixture was standardized to 5 µL. Samples were equilibrated at room temperature for 5 min to facilitate phase separation. For the combination of Rga7 F-BAR and Rga8 F-BAR domains, the total concentration refers to the combined concentrations of Rga7 F-BAR and Rga8 F-BAR. For example, a total concentration of 150 µM indicates a mixture containing 75 µM Rga7 F-BAR protein and 75 µM Rga8 F-BAR protein. For fluorescence microscopic imaging of droplets, Rga7 F-BAR and Rga7 F-BAR-mCherry were mixed at a molar ratio of 100:1, and similarly, Rga8 F-BAR and Rga8 F-BAR-GFP were mixed at the same ratio. For imaging, protein mixtures were introduced into a home-made flow chamber. This chamber was fabricated by securely affixing a glass coverslip to a glass slide using double-sided adhesive tape. The resulting images were obtained using a spinning disk confocal microscope.

Biochemical cross-linking assays

His-Rga7-F-BAR, His-Rga7-F-BAR-4A, His-Rga8-F-BAR, and His-Rga8-F-BAR-6A proteins were diluted to a final concentration of 400 ng/µL using their respective purification buffers. The cross-linking agent, ethylene glycol bis(succinimidyl succinate) (EGS, Sigma-Aldrich), was added to a final concentration of 4 mM, with a control group containing 0 mM EGS. To assess the impact of 1,6-Hexanediol (1,6-HD) and sodium chloride (NaCl) on protein oligomerization, reaction mixtures were supplemented with 30% 1,6-HD or 40% 1,6-HD, and 400 mM NaCl, respectively. These mixtures were incubated for five minutes on ice. Following pre-incubation, the samples were allowed to equilibrate for an additional 30 min at ambient temperature. The cross-linking reaction was terminated by the addition of a 10% glycine buffer solution, prepared from a 1 M Tris and 1 M glycine mixture adjusted to a pH of 7.5. Proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE using Tris-acetate polyacrylamide gradient gels ranging from 3% to 15% in concentration. A HiMark pre-stained protein standard (Thermo Scientific) was used to ensure accurate molecular weight determination. Gels were visualized by silver staining.

FRAP experiment

For the in vitro FRAP assay, Rga7 F-BAR and Rga7 F-BAR-GFP were mixed at a molar ratio of 100:1. Similarly, Rga8 F-BAR and Rga8 F-BAR-GFP were mixed at a molar ratio of 100:1 to assess their respective dynamics within the droplets. The 488-nm signal was bleached with a 488-nm laser beam using a Zeiss LSM 980 confocal microscope.

For the in vivo FRAP assay, strains were cultured in EMM5s liquid and imaged at an optical density (OD600) of 0.6 to 0.8. In the FRAP experiments, Rga7 and Rga8 were expressed under the control of the ase1 promoter. This was necessary because the fluorescence of endogenously expressed Rga7 is prone to photobleaching, and even the higher expression levels of Rga8 fluorescence are also susceptible to photobleaching, which could affect the experimental results. Puncta with a diameter of approximately 1.0 μm were selected for analysis. The GFP signal was bleached with a 488-nm laser beam.

Images of the FRAP experiment were acquired at a single time point before bleaching, followed by 50 cycles of a 488-nm laser at 50% power with imaging every 2.51 s for 3 min. The fluorescence intensity of the bleached area was normalized to the intensity of the same area before bleaching.

Liposome reconstitution assay

The liposome reconstitution assays were performed following the previously established methods [45, 47]. To prepare small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs), we used a lipid composition consisting of either phosphatidylcholine (PC) alone or PC with 10% phosphatidylserine (PS) (www.avantilipids.com). Specifically, lipids were mixed in chloroform solvent (100 µl of 10 mg/ml PC for PC alone; 90 µL of 10 mg/ml PC plus 10 µL of 10 mg/ml PS for PC plus 10% PS), dried under a stream of nitrogen for 5 min, and then hydrated with 1 mL of liposome buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 300 mM KCl, and 1 mM MgCl2) for 30 min at 37 °C. The lipid mixtures were subsequently sonicated for 5 min to form SUVs. Next, 75 µL of the SUVs were mixed with 0.002 g of silica microsphere beads (mean diameter, 3.17 μm) (www.bangslabs.com; SSD5001), and gently rotated for 1 h at room temperature. After rotation, excess SUVs were removed by centrifugation at 900 g for 1 min, followed by three washes with wash buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, and 100 mM KCl). 25 µL of protein samples at a concentration of 1 µM were added to 75 µL of microsphere solution (100 mM KCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 0.1% BSA, and 1 mM DTT), and the mixture was incubated for 60–90 min at room temperature. After incubation, the mixture was gently mixed by pipetting, and 10 µL of the sample was added to a glass perfusion chamber for imaging with a spinning-disk confocal microscope.
