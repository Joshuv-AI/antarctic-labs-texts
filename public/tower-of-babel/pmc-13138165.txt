Cardiopulmonary resuscitation in blind and visually impaired individuals: a pilot study of skill acquisition and public attitudes
Authors: Dóra Takács, Henrietta Bánfai-Csonka, Bálint Bánfai
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, and survival depends critically on early bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR).1 International guidelines emphasize that basic life support (BLS) skills should be disseminated broadly across society. If this objective is taken seriously, people living with disabilities – including individuals who are blind or visually impaired – should be actively included in training initiatives.2, 3

According to data from the Hungarian Central Statistical Office, approximately 42,211 individuals in Hungary self-reported being blind, partially sighted, or severely visually impaired, representing a substantial population that may be affected by limited access to first aid education.4 Although previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility of adapted CPR training methods for visually impaired learners and highlighted the need for further empirical validation of practical skills acquisition, objective, performance-based data remain scarce.5, 6, 7

In this context, we conducted a pilot study in two steps. First, we assessed CPR skills among 8 blind or visually impaired adults (mean age 49.6 years; 75% female) using a pre–post design, with two 2-hour training sessions delivered between the assessments (small groups, 1:3 instructor-participant ratio). Performance was evaluated using an 8-point BLS checklist (check the safety, check the response, check the breathing, call the ambulance, chest compressions, ventilation, CPR ratio, AED use). Mean scores increased significantly from 2.00 (SD = 1.85) before training to 6.63 (SD = 1.30) after training (p < 0.001). Performance of individual steps is presented as proportions in Table 1. All participants reported the training as useful and relevant.

Second, we conducted a questionnaire survey among 98 laypersons without visual impairment, exploring their views on the role of blind individuals in CPR. Overall, 95.9% agreed that blind and visually impaired people should learn CPR, and 96.9% supported providing them with training opportunities. While 65.3% believed that CPR initiated by a blind person would not pose additional danger, 34.7% perceived potential risks. Notably, 45.9% considered it likely that blind individuals could acquire CPR skills at a high level.

This pilot study has limitations, including the small sample size, simulation-based assessment, and the short-term nature of the follow-up. The use of a binary (0/1) scoring system for assessing performance may have introduced measurement bias, particularly for complex skills requiring multiple components (e.g., chest compressions), where full credit was awarded only if all elements were performed correctly; however, accurate execution of each component is essential for effective performance, and similar considerations apply across other composite skills. The attitude survey reflects perceptions rather than observed behavior and may be influenced by social desirability bias.

Nevertheless, our findings align with previous feasibility-focused research and add a complementary perspective by examining societal attitudes alongside objective skill acquisition. This dual approach adds to the limited evidence base by combining objective skill assessment with societal perspectives, and may inform future efforts aimed at making CPR training more inclusive.
