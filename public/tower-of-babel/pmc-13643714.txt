Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and gestational diabetes mellitus by birth order: A descriptive study across lifetime reproductive history
Authors: Sage Wyatt, Linn Marie Sørbye, Liv Grimstvedt Kvalvik, Aditi Singh, Rolv Skjærven
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHypertensive disorders of pregnancy and gestational diabetes mellitus are known to be strongly related, and underlying maternal cardiometabolic dysfunction has been proposed as a mechanism for both complications. A comparison of the trends by birth order for the two complications may reveal insights into their shared or different causes.Material and MethodsWe used data from the Medical Birth Registry of Norway from 1999 to 2020 with an aim to compare risk by birth order for hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and gestational diabetes mellitus. Visualizations of cross‐sectional risks by birth order are stratified by mother's age, calendar year, and mother's body mass index.ResultsThe risk of gestational diabetes mellitus appeared to increase by birth order, while hypertensive disorders of pregnancy were most common in first birth. GDM was more common in one‐child mothers than mothers with 2 to 4 lifetime births (OR 1.96, 95%CI 1.83, 2.10). For hypertensive disorders, we observed above 50 per 1000 births in first birth, but below 50 per 1000 births for later births, regardless of covariates. We no longer observed this trend by birth order for gestational diabetes (with risk around 60 per 1000 births for 40 year old mothers at any parity, for instance). Higher risk of diabetes in subsequent pregnancies reflects the older age of mothers. Mothers' body mass index is a known risk factor for both complications.ConclusionsThe increase in gestational diabetes mellitus by birth order closely reflects trends in other chronological variables like year and age. One explanation for the distinctive rise in gestational diabetes prevalence in later births is secular trends, while secular trends do not fully explain trends by birth order for hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. Different relationships to birth order and secular trends indicate different contributing mechanisms besides maternal cardiometabolic dysfunction in these two complications.

INTRODUCTION

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) is a set of pregnancy complications characterized by high blood pressure and organ dysfunction that can lead to severe morbidity and death during the perinatal period for both mother and infant. 1 The exact causes of HDP are still being investigated, with strong evidence for involvement of the maternal cardiovascular system due to a link with cardiometabolic disease outside of pregnancy. 2

Roberts and colleagues published an article 3 suggesting that HDP may be a common set of symptoms with multiple underlying causes. They propose that “certain subtypes of preeclampsia might be better grouped with other pregnancy complications predictive of maternal cardiovascular disease.” 3 While not mentioned explicitly in Roberts' paper, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is one possible candidate.

GDM is defined as hyperglycemia with onset or first diagnosis during pregnancy, 4 and is the most common pregnancy complication. 5 Mothers with HDP are more likely to have GDM, and the two complications have many shared risk factors. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Paradoxically, a prior study from 2002 identified that GDM risk increased by birth order while HDP risk dropped after the first birth. 12

Maternal immune reaction, selective fertility, and chronic maternal risk factors have all been hypothesized as explanations for the higher risk of HDP in first births, contributing to a more advanced understanding of the disease's multiple etiologies. 13 Studies that treat pregnancy complications as cross‐sectional events, not considering birth order, miss information concerning severity and etiology. For example, details of birth order and the mother's lifetime number of pregnancies can alter predicted maternal risk of long‐term cardiovascular disease in mothers with HDP, with increased risk for mothers with HDP in second or later pregnancies but not in first pregnancies for some mothers. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 GDM is linked to higher maternal age and BMI, but knowledge on how GDM risk varies by birth order is scarce.

The aim of this study is to compare the patterns in birth order for GDM and HDP, in light of increasing evidence supporting their association with each other 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 This is a descriptive study defining the risk of each complication across birth order and other factors related to birth order. Ultimately, this study describes the relationship between HDP and GDM from a new angle.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study population

This study used longitudinal data from the Medical Birth Registry of Norway (MBRN), a compulsory national registry of all live births, stillbirths, and spontaneous abortions starting from 16 gestational weeks. Data are reported on disease, demographics, complications, and interventions during the perinatal period. While the registry began data collection in 1967, we elected to only use data from 1999 and after, when the input forms were updated with check boxes rather than free text. 17 Our study period was January 1999 to June 2020. We therefore excluded mothers whose first birth was before 1999, due to sparse reporting of GDM. To ensure ease of data processing we further exclude mothers who had more than 4 births, whose first birth was not reported in the registry, or who had any multi‐fetal births.

Variable definitions

The complications of interest were HDP and GDM. HDP included gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, eclampsia, and HELLP (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets) syndrome. Clinicians diagnose gestational hypertension following national guidelines on elevated blood pressure (140/90 mmHg) after 20 weeks gestation. Preeclampsia is gestational hypertension with proteinuria. 18 Eclampsia is characterized by general convulsions and HELLP syndrome is characterized by hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets. 19 We initially considered analysis of preeclampsia and gestational hypertension separately. However, given the similar patterns in both complications we combined them into “hypertensive disorders of pregnancy” in the final analysis.

GDM in our study was registered as check boxes in the MBRN and included women registered with GDM or registered antidiabetic medication during pregnancy. Mothers with pregestational type 1 or type 2 diabetes were excluded. During the first study period, diagnostic criteria for GDM have been fasting plasma glucose <7.0 mmol/liter and plasma glucose ≥7.8 mmol/liter after an oral glucose tolerance test. From 2017, diagnostic criteria for GDM were changed to fasting plasma glucose ≥5.3–6.9 mmol/liter or plasma glucose ≥9.0–11.00 mmol/liter after an oral glucose tolerance test. Screening criteria for GDM in the first study period were age > 38 years, body mass index (BMI) >27 kg/m2, immigrants from North‐Africa and the Indian subcontinent, or close relatives with diabetes. However, from 2017, screening criteria were revised to also include nulliparous women ≥25 years, multiparous women ≥40 years, pre‐pregnancy BMI ≥25 kg/m2 and immigrants from Asia and Africa in general rather than region‐specific criteria. 20 , 21

With a descriptive aim, this study focused on the frequency of pregnancy complications across mothers' lifetime reproductive history. We framed this order within the context of total number of pregnancies, birth order, year of childbirth, maternal age at birth, and maternal BMI. Mother's BMI recorded before pregnancy is a known risk factor for both complications. 9 BMI data was only available after 2006, with initial limited coverage gradually increasing to 90% after 2020. 22 Maternal age and calendar year have been registered since the start of the MBRN. Older maternal age is associated with higher risk of pregnancy complications. 23 GDM has increased in Norway by calendar year, possibly related to the diagnostic criteria, 24 but the reasons for the increase are not fully understood.

Statistical analyses

The prevalence of pregnancy complications by total number of births is reported with a sibship approach 25 per 1000 mothers (Figure 1). We also report risks of pregnancy complications per 1000 births, the cross‐sectional approach, stratified by birth order across a mother's lifetime reproductive history (Figure 2). In Figure 2, the visualization shows a range of maternal age from 24 to 40 and BMI from 20 to 41 due to patient counts of less than 10 cases per age or BMI value outside of this range at any parity. In a sensitivity analysis, we also examined the lifetime number of pregnancies by first pregnancy outcomes. Births in this analysis were restricted to before 2013, allowing 7 years of follow‐up to observe second or later births. Binomial logistic regressions were used to calculate crude odds ratios. Trends of absolute risks by birth order are presented visually. All analyses were conducted in R.

RESULTS

Differences in risk by birth order

There were 473 208 mothers included in the study. Figure 1 shows apparent opposing trends by birth order for the risk of HDP and GDM in mothers with 1 to 4 births. GDM in the first birth was twice as common for one‐child mothers compared to mothers with 2 to 4 lifetime births (OR 1.96, 95% CI 1.83, 2.10). Among women who had further pregnancies, there was a trend of increasing risk of GDM by birth order. Meanwhile, there was a lower risk of HDP in later births compared to the first and no increase in risk from the second birth onwards.

Trends by year, age, and BMI

The cross‐sectional analysis in Figure 2 reports risks of each complication by maternal age and year, stratified by birth order (first, second, and third). Mothers had the highest risk of HDP in first births regardless of year or age. The risk of HDP was above 50 per 1000 births across all years and ages in mothers' first birth, but for second and third births, risk was consistently below 50 per 1000 births across all years and ages. Risk of HDP was also lower in later births, regardless of BMI. In contrast, GDM risk increased across maternal age, year, and BMI regardless of birth order, with lower risk at younger ages, earlier years, and lower BMI. For example, GDM occurred in 60 per 1000 births for 40 year old mothers regardless of if it was their first, second, or third birth. We do not show risk of complications in fourth births across these continuous scales of age, year, and BMI due to low numbers that yield visual results that are difficult to interpret (625 mothers total with GDM, 347 mothers total with HDP). Maternal age and BMI increased yearly across the study period (See supplementary material).

The majority of mothers in our population had more than one birth (Table 1). For those without GDM, 79.67% of mothers with neither complication had a second birth. Among mothers with GDM in their first pregnancy 67.24% had another pregnancy. Mothers with co‐occurring HDP had lower odds of another pregnancy than mothers with complicated pregnancies (OR 0.40, 95% CI 0.33, 0.48), even lower than mothers with GDM alone (OR 0.52, 95% CI 0.49, 0.56). However, less than 1% of women had both HDP and GDM in their first pregnancy.

DISCUSSION

Initial observations showed that GDM and HDP had opposite trends in incidence by birth order, with HDP most common in first births, and GDM more common in later births. Further analysis revealed that GDM risk increased with maternal age, calendar year, and BMI, rather than birth order. For HDP, meanwhile, we confirm previous findings when showing an increased risk in first birth even when accounting for secular trends. 13

One important risk factor that is shared between the two complications is high BMI. Pre‐pregnancy BMI has increased in Norway during the study period. 26 Sun and colleagues have suggested that the underlying metabolic dysfunction connecting GDM and HDP is mostly confounded by BMI. 9 Based on our results showing a link between both complications and high BMI, we agree that co‐occurring complications may be manifestations of underlying metabolic dysfunction and maternal cardiovascular maladaptation. 27

In addition to shared mechanisms, we propose that it is important to not overlook additional mechanisms that differ between the complications. Co‐occurring HDP and GDM was rare. While a subtype of HDP may be more likely in later pregnancies, perhaps due to maternal cardiovascular maladaptation, there remains a subtype that is most common in first pregnancies, and likely has different risk factors from GDM. 13

In Norway, the typical family size has historically been two to three births, 28 and mothers with only one lifetime birth comprise a unique group with typically poorer health outcomes. 29 We confirmed previous findings that mothers with HDP in their first pregnancy are more often one‐child mothers, 13 and our results also showed fewer women having a second pregnancy after GDM in their first pregnancy.

Possible explanations behind the higher risk of GDM in one‐child mothers still remain to be explored. Previous studies have not supported the hypothesis that parity itself influences insulin resistance. 30 GDM may have some links to lower fertility, 31 especially when co‐occurring with HDP, 8 but our findings add further evidence for the stronger role of maternal age in the risk of GDM rather than birth order. Incidence of GDM was higher in older ages, and women who begin their reproduction at an older age may also have fewer children, simply due to the natural end of fertility. Selective fertility, 32 , 33 or the conscious decision to not pursue another pregnancy after the experience of a very complicated pregnancy, may also explain why women do not have another child.

This study's access to high‐quality data in a large population‐based birth registry allows us to study perinatal outcomes with relatively low prevalence. The dependencies between complications in one mother's births can only be fully explored using a successive pregnancy approach. Registry data is a rich data resource allowing family linkage of consecutive pregnancies. Reproductive histories may not be complete for women whose first births were toward the end of the study period. 24 , 34 The increase in GDM before 2017 may indicate underdiagnosis of GDM pre‐2017, so the true risk of GDM may be inaccurate during this time period. Lastly, the registry does not include time of onset of complications, and this information could have been important in grading the severity of the complications.

CONCLUSION

We observed a higher risk of HDP in first pregnancies. For GDM, increased prevalence in later births can be explained by increased maternal age and secular changes across time period, including increasing BMI. The differences in risk by birth order and differing relationships to secular trends may suggest some different etiological pathways for the two complications.
