Enhancing capacity in faith-based organizations to implement and sustain multilevel innovations to improve physical activity: study protocol for the faith in action type II hybrid implementation-effectiveness trial

Background and Rationale

Promoting physical activity (PA) is crucial for reducing the burden of obesity and chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and cancer [1]. Yet, Latinas in the U.S. continue to engage in lower levels of PA than Latino men and other racial/ethnic groups, due in part to structural barriers such as limited access to safe PA opportunities, compounded by family responsibilities and cultural gender role expectations [2–4]. While expanding access to evidence-based PA programs is a promising strategy for addressing health disparities [5–7], efforts to optimize their implementation and sustainment are needed in community-based settings serving Latinas [8].

Faith-based organizations (FBOs) are uniquely positioned to deliver and sustain health promotion programs among Latino/a communities. FBOs have a broad community reach—43% of Latinos identify as Catholic and 22% as Protestant, with 22% of Latino Catholics and 53% of Latino Protestants attending church weekly [9]. Furthermore, women are more likely than men to attend services regularly and report stronger religious affiliation and involvement in church activities [9]. FBOs often possess the social capital, organizational infrastructure, and support the physical, emotional, and spiritual needs of parishioners [10]. Despite the potential to influence the health of marginalized communities, the limited implementation of evidence-based interventions (EBIs) in FBOs highlights a critical need to identify, test, and refine implementation strategies that support the integration and sustainment of physical activity programs [7].

A persistent gap exists between the availability of EBIs and their successful implementation in community settings such as FBOs. Research has identified barriers to implementing and sustaining EBIs in FBOs [7, 11, 12]. These barriers include limited availability of trained staff or volunteers, competing organizational priorities, insufficient leadership support, and a lack of culturally or religiously relevant program adaptations. Other factors, such as inadequate space for PA programming and limited time among implementers, can influence the implementation of EBIs in FBOs. Few have tested organizational-level implementation strategies that may enhance the implementation and sustainment of physical activity programs in FBOs. Moreover, most research has been conducted in African American church settings [13, 14], which may differ in organizational structure, leadership dynamics, and decision-making processes compared to Latino-focused churches. This underscores the need for implementation strategies that are both theory-driven and adaptable—strategies that address implementation barriers while accounting for the unique organizational structures, cultural values, and contextual realities of Latino-focused FBOs.

Optimizing the implementation and sustainment of EBIs in FBOs requires targeted strategies that strengthen the inner setting, such as leadership engagement, effective communication, and organizational capacity [15, 16]. Church leaders are instrumental in shaping policies, allocating resources, and establishing congregational norms; therefore, actively involving them can foster an implementation climate supportive of EBI adoption and integration [17]. Aligning programs with the church’s core values and embedding them into existing structures and routines further enhances both implementation and long-term sustainability. Also, strategies such as staff training and the development of feedback mechanisms can enhance an organization’s capacity to sustain health programs beyond the initial funding period [18].

This study addresses critical implementation gaps by focusing on Latino-serving churches—trusted institutions uniquely positioned to promote physical activity among Latinas through culturally and contextually grounded approaches. It evaluates strategies to improve the implementation and sustainment of Faith in Action, an EBI shown in a cluster randomized trial to increase accelerometer-assessed PA in this population through PA classes and motivational-interview style phone calls [19, 20]. Central to this effort is the construct of innovation-values fit, which captures the extent to which an intervention aligns with an organization’s core mission, values, and practices. In faith-based settings, such alignment is crucial for facilitating leadership buy-in, promoting community engagement, and supporting sustainability. The current trial builds on the effectiveness trial by experimentally testing implementation strategies that engage organizational leadership and infrastructure, with the goal of optimizing both implementation outcomes and long-term sustainment.

Design & methods

Study design and aims

The protocol of the hybrid trial has been written according to the Standards Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) [21] (Supplemental Table 1) and the Standards for Reporting Implementation studies (StaRI) [22]. This hybrid type 2 implementation-effectiveness study tests the implementation and effectiveness of several implementation strategies using a clustered randomized controlled trial (parallel group). Participants include Latina church members, promotoras, church staff, and church leaders, followed for 18 months, with assessments conducted at baseline (Month 1, M1), six months (M2), 12 months (M3), and 18 months (M4). Before implementation, the team conducts pre-implementation interviews with church leaders and staff to assess the church context and tailor implementation strategies. Churches (n = 32) are randomized into three arms: (1) standard (n = 8), (2) enhanced implementation (n = 12), and (3) enhanced implementation + sustainment (n = 12). The standard condition is Faith in Action as implemented in the original effectiveness trial. The rationale for testing the comparator is to experimentally test the impact of the additional implementation and sustainment strategies on effectiveness, implementation, and sustainment of Faith in Action. The enhanced condition includes two strategies: a promotora empowerment workshop and a session with church leadership about their support of the program. The enhanced + sustainment condition includes the strategies in the enhanced condition plus five additional sustainment strategies (please refer to Fig. 1 for study design). As such, Aim 1 tests the short- and long-term impacts of organization-level implementation strategies at 6- and 12-month post-baseline. Aim 2 evaluates individual-level reach and effectiveness (physical activity). Secondary aims assess the mechanisms of change and sustainment strategies at 18 months.

Overview of study design describing the three conditions

Randomization and masking

Simple randomization using a central computer is conducted at the group level to reduce contamination among participants within the same setting and to facilitate consistent delivery of the intervention across group members. Personnel do not have access to the random allocation sequence. Participants (church leaders, church staff, and church members), promotoras, outcome assessors and are blinded to treatment allocations during the intervention trial, including during outcome data collection and analysis. Surveys and PA assessments (e.g., accelerometry) are being collected using standardized protocols that do not reference the intervention group.

Theoretical frameworks

The study is guided by two implementation frameworks: the Practical, Robust Implementation and Sustainability Model (PRISM) and the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research 2.0 (CFIR). PRISM builds on the widely used RE-AIM framework—reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, and maintenance—by incorporating contextual factors relevant to implementation and sustainability [23–25] CFIR provides a comprehensive taxonomy of constructs influencing implementation across five domains [26]. Both Dissemination and Implementation frameworks consider inner and outer settings. The inner setting includes organizational characteristics such as implementation climate, culture, and the fit between the EBI and organizational values (e.g., the compatibility of Faith in Action with FBO values). The outer setting encompasses broader contextual influences, such as funding availability and external partnerships. We use CFIR to examine organizational factors that may affect implementation. PRISM/RE-AIM complements CFIR by explicitly addressing sustainability and helping tailor implementation strategies to align with FBO priorities (Fig. 2).

Theoretical framework

Community Advisory Board (CAB)

The CAB plays a key role in shaping and guiding the development and implementation of organizational-level strategies. CAB members include leaders from the Catholic and Protestant churches as well as community organizations such as Catholic Charities, the Promotora Coalition, and local health clinics. CAB members also provide valuable feedback on measurement tools, support for the church and participant recruitment, data interpretation, and help to strengthen community-research team connections.

Setting and participants

Church and church leader recruitment

The Principal Investigator (PI) and Project Manager (PM) recruit Catholic and Protestant churches in San Diego County that offer at least one weekly Spanish-language service and agree to participate in the study, including randomization. Church leaders (e.g., pastors, associate pastors) are involved in program decision-making and invited to participate in a brief workshop and evaluation activities.

Church staff recruitment

Church staff (e.g., administrative assistants, business managers) are invited to support implementation by scheduling, making announcements, and coordinating logistics. They are invited to participate in a brief workshop and evaluation activities.

Promotora recruitment

Promotoras are eligible if they self-identify as Latina, speak Spanish, regularly attend church, and are interested in leading PA classes. Candidates are identified through word of mouth, approved by pastors, and interviewed to assess leadership potential, study commitment, and familiarity with the church and community. They are trained to implement the PA program and invited to participate in evaluation activities.

Church member recruitment and eligibility screening

Recruitment occurs through announcements and tabling at church services, advertisements in the church bulletin or communication channels, and word of mouth. Church members complete the eligibility screening in Spanish or English. Eligibility criteria include (1) self-identifying as Latina, (2) aged 18–65 years old, (3) living within 15-minute driving distance from the church, or attending the church site regularly (at least two times per week) and planning to attend the same church site for the next 18 months, (4) not meeting the PA guideline of 150 min/week of travel and leisure time MVPA and (5) having no current health factors that could interfere with PA. The exclusion criteria are any of the following: (1) a health condition precluding engagement in PA, (2) inability to complete informed consent in English or Spanish, and (3) attending other churches besides the current church of recruitment.

Informed consent

Informed consent is obtained by trained research staff who are fluent in both English and Spanish and have experience working with the target population. Consent is obtained in person at participating churches prior to any data collection. During the consent process, the team collects information for two contacts per participant to facilitate follow-up. Research staff use IRB-approved scripts and documents. Personal and identifiable information is collected and stored using secure, password-protected systems such as REDCap or encrypted drives. Each participant is assigned a unique study ID, and identifying information will be stored separately from research data.

The PI, PM, and the study biostatistician have access only to de-identified data linked to participant identification numbers, with no access to names or other personal identifiers. All study documents—including consent forms and participant information sheets—are securely stored in a locked cabinet. Only the PI and PM have access to identifiable information. To protect participant confidentiality, data will be reported in aggregate form, and these protections will be maintained throughout all phases of the trial: before, during, and after implementation.

For retention, contact information for two alternate contacts per participant is collected to facilitate follow-up. Also, birthday and holiday cards are sent as part of ongoing engagement efforts. All study procedures comply with San Diego State University’s data protection policies and, where applicable, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA).

Evidence-based physical activity intervention

Faith in Action

All churches, regardless of their assigned condition, receive all elements of the Faith in Action as was delivered in the effectiveness trial [19, 20]. Key elements include recruiting and training one to two promotoras to each lead three group exercise classes per week and make motivational support calls to enrolled participants (2x/year). Faith in Action is available at the Evidence-Based Cancer Control Programs (EBCCP) website.[27].

Group exercise classes

Each of the two promotoras leads culturally relevant cardio dance, strength training, and walking groups each week. Cardio dance and strength training classes last 1 h and include an opening prayer, warm-up, workout, cool-down, closing prayer, and a 5-minute talk on a health topic of the week (e.g., hydration, balanced plate, benefits of strength training). Walking groups last 1.5 h. Strength training classes include circuit and interval training and use body weight and commonly accessible objects (e.g., water bottles).

Motivational support calls

During program enrollment, participants complete a questionnaire assessing their motivation for PA (motivated vs. not motivated). These motivation levels inform two tailored call guides developed by the research team. The first call, made 2–3 months after PA classes start in their church, focuses on reiterating an invitation to attend classes, getting to know the participants, and building rapport by discussing their values and how they relate to PA. The second call, scheduled six months later, explores PA barriers and helps participants develop PA plans based on SMART goals (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound).

Implementation strategies

All conditions

Promotora training and support

Promotoras undergo 40 h of training, which includes virtual and in-person cardio dance sessions, PA safety, techniques for leading group exercise classes, strategies for healthy eating, and CPR training. The PA specialist meets with the promotoras weekly and biweekly to help troubleshoot implementation challenges and ensure adherence to intervention protocols. Promotoras are asked to follow established protocols, report adverse events, and document them in their logs.

Enhanced implementation condition

Churches randomized to the enhanced implementation condition receive additional training at both the implementer and organizational levels to strengthen capacity for health promotion programming. The two additional strategies include tailored empowerment training for promotoras and a leadership workshop for FBO leaders. Based on qualitative findings from focus groups and feedback from the CAB, the Promotora Empowerment Training was designed to enhance promotoras’ competencies in implementing community-based health programs. Similarly, the FBO Leadership Workshop guides leaders in creating an action plan to support the program by addressing their responsibilities and competing priorities (Table 1).

Data safety and monitoring

To ensure participant safety and data integrity, an independent Data Safety and Monitoring Board (DSMB) will be convened for this trial. The DSMB serves in an advisory capacity, reviewing trial data and operations and providing recommendations to the study team. The board comprises three members, including a chair, with expertise in data monitoring, PA, and clinical trial methodology. The DSMB functions independently of both the study sponsor and the research investigators and meets once a year during the trial to review the study protocol, accumulated data, and operational performance.

In parallel, the research team conducts ongoing monitoring. The intervention is considered to have minimal risk, and any adverse or unanticipated events are reported to the PI. The PIs review these events in consultation with relevant team members, and any incidents determined to be reportable will be submitted to the San Diego State University Institutional Review Board (IRB) in accordance with established reporting guidelines. We do not anticipate major harms related to the PA intervention. However, adverse events related to PA (e.g., injuries, falls, overexertion) are systematically tracked and summarized descriptively by study arm. Serious adverse events will be reported to the IRB and the DSMB in accordance with institutional and federal guidelines.

Data collection methods

Survey and anthropometric data collection

Church members, promotoras, church leaders, and staff across all three conditions are measured at four timepoints: baseline (M1) to (M4). Of the four participant categories, only church members undergo a screening process to determine eligibility. Eligible church members are scheduled for an M1 measurement event to complete a survey and have their anthropometric measurements recorded by trained staff. Trained research personnel verbally administer the initial section of the survey, which includes the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ). Promotoras complete surveys with research staff via Zoom and do not complete anthropometric measures. Church staff and leaders can elect to take anthropometric measurements after completing the survey. Participant data are collected and stored in a secure, local REDCap platform. Range checks and required fields ensure data integrity.

Qualitative data collection methods

Church leaders and staff are invited to individual interviews at each time point (M1-M4). Promotoras participate in paired-group interviews conducted by the church at the M2 and M4 measurement points. During interviews, church leaders, staff, and promotoras provide data about program implementation and organizational-level constructs specific to their church.

Primary study aims and relevant measures

Aim 1: implementation and mechanisms of action

We assess the impact of implementation strategies on implementation in faith-based settings, such as innovation-values fit. Innovation-values fit refers to how well the intervention aligns with organizational values, and implementation fidelity is the extent to which the intervention is delivered as reported by the parishioners.

Aim 2: reach and effectiveness

Consistent with the RE-AIM framework, Reach is defined as the proportion of eligible Latina church members who enroll in and participate in Faith in Action intervention activities, relative to the number invited or eligible.[30] Effectiveness is measured using self-reported PA, as assessed by the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ).

Secondary aims and relevant measures

Secondary measures

This aim examines the mechanisms of change potentially influenced by the proposed implementation strategies, including FBO leadership support, promotora empowerment, implementation climate, and organizational culture.

Ethnographic fieldnotes

Research staff document implementation of climate, culture, and factors affecting program uptake. This includes capturing community interest and responses after MOU signings and the initial Faith in Action event, observations after the first outreach or measurement day, and notes from unique church events.

EBI and implementation adaptations

We incorporate the Reach, Effectiveness, Implementation, and Maintenance outcomes into the Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications-Expanded (FRAME) to systematically document real-time, iterative adaptations [31–33]. Implementation challenges and proposed adaptations are regularly discussed and reviewed by the research team for inclusion in the database. Examples of adaptation categories that are captured in the tool include: locations occurred, who informed it, method documented, elements changed (e.g., format, content), nature of modifications, timepoint, reasons for modification, and changes seen as a result. Adaptation decisions are informed by direct observation, interviews, and informal discussions with individuals on various roles across different levels of the project: (1) Program implementers (i.e., promotoras and coordinators); (2) Research personnel (i.e., staff, co-investigators); (3) Trial participants (i.e., FBO leaders/staff and parishioners), and (4) CAB.

Sample size calculations

Aim 1

The sample size calculation used innovation-values fit to compare the three conditions (Standard, Enhanced, and Enhanced + Sustainment) at 6- and 12-months. We considered repeated-measures correlations, desired power, type I error rate, and mean score differences to assess whether the two Enhanced condition groups would improve fit to innovation values relative to the Standard condition group. Based on a two-sided type I error rate of 0.05, an 80% power, a mean score difference of 0.93, and a repeated measures correlation of 0.3, a total of 29 FBOs needed to be included (seven in the Standard condition, 11 in the Enhanced condition, and 11 in the Enhanced + Sustainment condition). Additionally, we included an additional FBO in each group to account for potential dropouts. Therefore, the required sample of churches was 32 after the adjustment.

Aim 2

Sample size calculation at the individual level was based on comparing the Standard, Enhanced, and Enhanced + Sustainment conditions on the changes in daily minutes of MVPA from baseline to 6, 12, and 18 months. A small-to-medium standardized effect size of 0.29, based on Cohen’s definition, was reported at 12 months. We also considered a 0.03 clustering effect ICC by the church for PA, in addition to a type I error rate of 0.05 (two-sided), 80% power, and repeated correlations. The planned sample size for this aim is 677 participants (with an anticipated 20% attrition rate, yielding a final analytic sample of 522 participants). This estimate is powered to detect a standardized effect size of 0.29 at the M2 and M3 assessments. The results of the trial will be shared through open-access scientific journals, regardless of the findings’ size or direction. A fully de-identified dataset will also be made publicly accessible in a suitable data repository.

Planned analyses

Aim 1: implementation

To evaluate the short and long-term impact of organization-focused implementation strategies in the two enhanced conditions on innovation-values fit, program promotion and support by church leaders, as well as participant engagement, and program fidelity compared to the standard condition, we will use linear mixed models (for continuous outcomes) to account the repeated measure dependence at each time point and perform a contrast between the standard condition group and the two enhanced condition groups based on the change scores from baseline as an initial assessment. We will also apply linear mixed models and nonlinear mixed models or continuous and categorical outcomes, respectively. Transformations will be applied as needed to meet the model’s assumptions. Finally, we will use a configurational comparative method, known as coincidence analysis (CNA), to identify factors that differentiate churches with higher vs. lower implementation [34]. Program participation, fidelity, and factors hypothesized to influence implementation (e.g., innovation-values fit, church leader support, promotora communication skills) will be calibrated for use in CNA.

Aim 2: reach and effectiveness

Reach represents the proportion of eligible individuals who receive the intervention [35]. Differences will be compared between the Enhanced and Standard conditions. For MVPA effectiveness among participants, we will consider dependence for repeated measures at baseline, 6, and 12 months, and dependence within the same church. Linear and nonlinear mixed models will be used to evaluate outcomes at different time points simultaneously (time*condition). The models will be adjusted for the two conditions (standard vs. enhanced), the time periods (six and 12 months), a condition by time interaction, the baseline MVPA level, a random effect for churches, a random effect for participants nested within churches, and participants’ demographics at baseline.

Analyses will follow an intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. All randomized churches and all enrolled participants from those churches will be included in the analysis according to their original randomized group assignment, regardless of the level of participation or adherence. For individual-level outcomes, all participants with baseline and at least one follow-up measurement will be included. Sensitivity analyses using per-protocol or as-treated populations may be conducted to examine the robustness of the findings. Missing data will be addressed using multiple imputation under the assumption of missing at random (MAR), and sensitivity analyses will explore the impact of departures from this assumption. Analysis results will be compared with and without the imputations.

Analyses of exploratory secondary aims

Due to the sample size, formal mediation analysis will not be conducted. Instead, correlations of process evaluation data will link implementation strategies, mechanisms of action, and organizational factors, such as innovation-values fit and FBO engagement. We will employ methods similar to those in Aims 1 and 2 to analyze data from Secondary Aim 2, focusing on the differences between the two Enhanced conditions and the 12- to 18-month time period.

Qualitative analyses

Formative data collection will be analyzed using Rapid Qualitative Analysis [36]. Pairs of trained research assistants use a template to summarize interview transcripts by construct. Each summary is limited to 2 pages and consists of bullet points organized by construct. Two research assistants independently summarize each transcript, then meet to consolidate and discuss any discrepancies. All summaries are entered into a matrix by construct, and the PI, PM, and senior research assistants conduct topic monitoring. The goals are to complement the quantitative measures and inform the analyses.

Discussion

This study addresses a critical public health challenge - the significant disparities in PA participation among Latinas, which contribute to the disproportionate burden of obesity, diabetes, cancer, and cardiovascular disease in this population [37–40]. FBOs offer unique opportunities to implement EBIs, as they have existing infrastructure, consistent membership, trusted leadership, and established community networks. Faith in Action has demonstrated its potential to increase MVPA among Latina churchgoers by involving promotoras. Capacity to implement EBIs can be enhanced through a range of strategies, including targeted training, use of implementation tools, technical assistance, ongoing assessment and feedback, and appropriate incentives.[41] To effectively implement EBIs, it is essential to build FBO capacity by providing training for church leaders, promotoras, and staff. Even modest investments in building capacity for evidence-based public health within these community settings can lead to more impactful and sustainable public health outcomes.

This study uses an experimental design to evaluate the effects of implementation strategies on the implementation of an EBI. The study contributes critical insights into the effective and sustainable delivery of EBIs in real-world settings. A key innovation lies in examining proposed mechanisms of change and the preliminary impact of sustainment strategies. Importantly, the study also addresses a significant gap in the literature by assessing implementation and sustainment strategies and identifying the factors necessary to maintain interventions beyond the initial implementation phase—insights essential for long-term scalability and public health impact. Given that Latinos currently comprise 19% of the U.S. population, with projections reaching 28% by 2060, developing effective and sustainable health interventions in this context is critical for advancing health equity.

Conclusion

Findings from the proposed study will provide a blueprint for leveraging and scaling community-based settings to advance health equity by integrating culturally tailored strategies with comprehensive implementation science frameworks. The findings will highlight the potential of FBOs as critical partners in public health initiatives, offering sustainable solutions to some of the most pressing health disparities in underserved communities.
