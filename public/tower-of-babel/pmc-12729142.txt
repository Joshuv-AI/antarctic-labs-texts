Cardiovascular outcomes associated with incident diabetes during statin therapy: a nationwide propensity-matched cohort study
Authors: Ki-Chul Sung, Dongwoo Kang, Jungkuk Lee, Seung-Jae Lee, Eun Joo Cho, Jin-Ok Jeong, Moo-Yong Rhee, Da-Eun Sung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStatins are a cornerstone of cardiovascular disease prevention, but growing evidence suggests they may increase the risk of new-onset type 2 diabetes. The cardiovascular implications of diabetes that develops during statin therapy, however, remain unclear. This study aimed to evaluate whether incident diabetes during statin use is associated with increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) or all-cause mortality, compared with individuals with preexisting diabetes and statin users who remained free of diabetes.MethodsWe conducted two retrospective cohort studies using data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (KNHIS) database between 2009 and 2020, which includes nationwide, population-based data from both primary and secondary care settings. From a source population of 1,500,959 adults, Study 1 identified 4371 individuals who had used statins continuously for ≥ 730 days before receiving a new diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). These individuals were propensity score–matched 1:1 to individuals who initiated statin therapy after a prior diagnosis of T2DM, matched by age, sex, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels. In Study 2, 4191 statin users who developed T2DM during follow-up were matched 1:1 with statin users who remained diabetes-free during the same period. The primary outcome was MACE, defined as a composite of myocardial infarction and stroke. All-cause mortality was assessed as a secondary outcome.ResultsIn Study 1, individuals who developed diabetes during statin therapy had a significantly lower risk of MACE compared with those who initiated statins after diabetes diagnosis (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 0.607; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.458–0.805; p = 0.0005), primarily driven by a reduced stroke risk (aHR 0.504; 95% CI, 0.357–0.711; p < 0.001). In Study 2, incident diabetes during statin use was not associated with increased risk of MACE (aHR 1.122; 95% CI, 0.784–1.605; p = 0.53) or all-cause mortality (aHR 0.555; 95% CI, 0.290–1.063; p = 0.0758), compared with remaining diabetes-free.ConclusionsDiabetes that develops during statin therapy was associated with a lower risk of MACE compared with initiating statins after diabetes diagnosis and was not linked to increased risk of MACE or all-cause mortality compared with remaining diabetes-free.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-025-03003-0.

Background

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide. Elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) significantly contributes to ASCVD risk, and the presence of diabetes mellitus further amplifies this susceptibility, leading to earlier disease onset and an increased burden of cardiovascular events compared with individuals without diabetes [1]. Given this compounded risk, current guidelines emphasize intensive LDL-C lowering with statin therapy as a cornerstone in cardiovascular risk management for patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [2–10]. The 2023 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines specify that patients with T2DM who are at very high cardiovascular risk—defined as having established ASCVD, severe target-organ damage, or a calculated 10-year risk of ≥ 20% based on SCORE2-Diabetes—should achieve an LDL-C target below 55 mg/dL through maximally tolerated statin therapy, with sequential addition of ezetimibe and PCSK9 inhibitors as needed. However, reaching these aggressive LDL-C targets often necessitates high-intensity statin use, raising concerns regarding potential adverse effects, including new-onset diabetes.

Despite their established cardiovascular benefits, statins have been associated with a modestly increased risk of new-onset diabetes, as demonstrated in randomized controlled trials and observational studies Da-Eun Sung [11–19]. This risk appears to be dose-dependent and more pronounced among individuals with multiple metabolic risk factors. Nonetheless, the clinical implications of statin-associated diabetes remain uncertain. Specifically, it is unclear whether diabetes developing during statin therapy confers cardiovascular risks comparable to spontaneous T2DM or if it diminishes the cardioprotective benefits provided by statins.

To address this uncertainty, we conducted two complementary analyses using a nationwide Korean cohort. First, we compared cardiovascular outcomes between individuals who developed diabetes while on statin therapy and those who initiated statin therapy after a diagnosis of preexisting T2DM. Second, we evaluated cardiovascular risk among statin users who developed diabetes during follow-up compared to those who remained diabetes-free. These analyses aimed to clarify whether diabetes developing during statin therapy confers a similar cardiovascular risk as preexisting T2DM and to inform clinical decision-making regarding the long-term use of statins for cardiovascular prevention.

Methods

Data source and study design

We conducted two retrospective cohort studies using data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (KNHIS), a mandatory universal health insurance program that covers approximately 97% of the South Korean population. The KNHIS database includes anonymized records of outpatient visits, hospital admissions, diagnoses coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), prescription claims, sociodemographic data (e.g., income level and residential area), and biennial health screenings, including blood pressure, anthropometric measurements, fasting glucose, and lipid profiles. The database is also linked to the national death registry.

We used data from 2009 to 2020 to identify study populations and define exposures, covariates, and outcomes. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Kangbuk Medical Center (IRB No. 2024–07-006), which waived the requirement for informed consent owing to the use of deidentified data.

Study populations

Study 1: statin use before versus after diabetes diagnosis

We identified 1,500,959 adults who underwent at least two health screenings through the KNHIS between 2009 and 2012. From this population, 117,011 individuals who developed new-onset type 2 diabetes between 2012 and 2020 were included (Fig. 1). Type 2 diabetes was defined as the presence of ICD-10 codes E10–E14 and at least one prescription for an antidiabetic medication. The index date was defined as the date of the first recorded diagnosis of type 2 diabetes. To ensure comparability between groups, individuals with any history of diabetes prior to the index date were excluded. All participants developed diabetes after the index date and differed only in the timing of statin initiation.

Study flow for Study 1: Statin initiation before vs after diabetes diagnosis Flow diagram showing cohort selection and matching of individuals with sustained statin use before diabetes diagnosis and those who initiated statins after diabetes diagnosis

Participants were excluded if they had missing laboratory data (n = 15,761) or demographic information (n = 2177); no statin use after diabetes diagnosis (n = 22,728); or a history of myocardial infarction (ICD-10 codes I21–I23) or stroke (I60–I64) prior to the index date, defined as at least one inpatient or outpatient encounter (n = 5283). To further reduce confounding, we excluded individuals with prior diagnoses of chronic ischemic heart disease (I20), other acute ischemic heart diseases (I24), and other cerebrovascular diseases (I65–I67), which were considered intermediate conditions potentially related to the study outcomes (n = 28,411). Additionally, individuals with fewer than 730 days of statin prescriptions within the three years before the index date were excluded.

After applying all criteria, 5258 individuals were classified as having sustained statin use before diabetes onset (statin-before-diabetes group), and 18,067 as initiating statins after diabetes diagnosis (statin-after-diabetes group). A 1:1 propensity score matching based on age, sex, and LDL-C levels yielded two matched cohorts of 4371 participants each.

Study 2: statin users with versus without incident diabetes

From the same screening population, we identified 626,397 individuals who were prescribed statins and did not have baseline diabetes, defined as fasting glucose < 126 mg/dL, absence of ICD-10 codes E10–E14, and no use of antidiabetic medications (Fig. 2). The index date was set as January 1, 2012.

Study flow for Study 2: Statin users with vs without incident diabetes Flow diagram illustrating cohort selection and matching of statin users who developed diabetes during follow-up and those who remained diabetes-free

Participants were excluded if they had missing laboratory (n = 2463) or demographic data (n = 31,208); no statin prescriptions before or after the index date (n = 562,918), including those with fewer than 730 days of prescriptions during the three years preceding the index date (n = 3977, included within the 562,918); or a history of myocardial infarction (I21–I23) or stroke (I60–I64) prior to the index date (n = 1164). To further minimize confounding, individuals with prior diagnoses of chronic ischemic heart disease (I20), other acute ischemic heart diseases (I24), and other cerebrovascular diseases (I65–I67) were also excluded (n = 7074).

After applying these criteria, 4191 individuals developed incident diabetes during follow-up (statin–diabetes group). Using 1:1 propensity score matching based on age, sex, and LDL-C levels, these participants were matched to 4191 statin users who remained diabetes-free (statin–non-diabetes group).

Variables and definitions

Sustained statin exposure was defined as at least 730 days of statin prescriptions within the 3 years preceding the index date. Comorbidities, including hypertension (ICD-10 codes I10–I11) and dyslipidemia (E78), were identified based on diagnostic codes recorded within 1 year prior to the index date. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated using the Quan ICD-10 coding algorithm. Clinical and laboratory variables, including blood pressure, anthropometric measurements, fasting glucose, and lipid profiles, were obtained from the most recent KNHIS health screening conducted within 3 years before the index date. The same 730-day criterion was applied to define sustained use of antihypertensive or antidiabetic medications, where applicable.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), defined as a composite of myocardial infarction (ICD-10 codes I21–I23) and stroke (I60–I64).

Events were identified based on the presence of at least one inpatient admission or outpatient visit with the corresponding diagnostic codes. Myocardial infarction and stroke were also analyzed separately. All-cause mortality was assessed as a secondary outcome, calculated from the index date forward. To minimize reverse causality, only myocardial infarction and stroke events occurring at least 180 days after the index date were included in the analysis.

Statistical analyses

Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for both unadjusted and adjusted analyses.

The adjusted models included covariates of age, sex, income level, residential area, history of hypertension and dyslipidemia, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, body mass index (BMI), systolic and diastolic blood pressure, use of antihypertensive medications, LDL-C, fasting blood glucose, and CCI.

In all outcomes analysis, prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted according to sex (male vs. female), age (≥ 60 years vs. < 60 years), history of hypertension (yes vs. no), income quintile (≥ 50% vs. < 50%), residential area (urban vs. rural), smoking status (never vs. ever), alcohol consumption (≥ 3 times/week vs. < 3 times/week), physical activity (≥ 3 times/week vs. < 3 times/week), and body mass index (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 vs. < 25 kg/m2).

Supplementary analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. In Study 1, propensity-score matching and adjustment were expanded to include additional demographic (age, sex, income level, residential area), clinical (hypertension, dyslipidemia, Charlson Comorbidity Index, use of antihypertensive medication), lifestyle (smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity), and metabolic variables (body-mass index, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol). Myocardial infarction and stroke were redefined to require both inpatient and outpatient diagnostic codes to improve outcome specificity.

The proportional-hazards assumption was examined using Schoenfeld residuals and log–log survival plots. When minor deviations were detected, time-dependent Cox regression models were applied. E-values were calculated to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding. Interaction p-values and false-discovery-rate (FDR)–adjusted q-values were calculated for subgroup analyses to account for multiple comparisons.

We set the threshold for statistical significance at a p-value of < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

In Study 1, participants who developed new-onset diabetes while receiving statins had a higher baseline prevalence of hypertension than those who initiated statin therapy after a prior diagnosis of diabetes. In Study 2, individuals who developed new-onset diabetes during follow-up had higher baseline rates of hypertension and higher Charlson Comorbidity Index scores compared with those who remained diabetes-free. No significant differences were observed between the groups in other baseline characteristics, including body mass index, cardiovascular risk factors, and lifestyle behaviors.

The adjusted HR for MACE was 0.607 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.458 to 0.805; p = 0.0005). This association was primarily driven by a lower risk of stroke (adjusted HR, 0.504; 95% CI, 0.357 to 0.711; p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in the risk of myocardial infarction (adjusted HR, 0.715; 95% CI, 0.433 to 1.181; p = 0.1898) or all-cause mortality (adjusted HR, 1.097; 95% CI, 0.596 to 2.020; p = 0.7653) between the groups.

Because the proportional-hazards assumption for all-cause mortality showed a minor deviation, a time-dependent Cox regression analysis was performed, yielding consistent and nonsignificant results (Supplementary Table 1). In addition, supplementary analyses using expanded propensity-score models and stricter outcome definitions produced results consistent with the main analysis, further confirming the robustness of the primary findings (Supplementary Table 2).

Subgroup analyses

Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted to assess whether the associations differed across clinical subgroups in each cohort.

In Study 1, all-cause mortality did not differ significantly between individuals who developed diabetes during statin therapy and those who initiated statins after a prior diabetes diagnosis, except among women, in whom lower risk was observed (Fig. 3). For MACE, risk was lower in the former group across most subgroups, excluding women, younger individuals, and those without hypertension (Fig. 4). For myocardial infarction, no meaningful differences were observed in most subgroups (Fig. 5). For stroke, individuals who developed diabetes during statin therapy consistently showed lower risk across subgroups (Fig. 6).

Discussion

In this large nationwide cohort of East Asian adults, individuals who developed diabetes during statin therapy had lower rates of MACE compared with those who initiated statins after a diagnosis of pre-existing type 2 diabetes. Among statin users without diabetes at baseline, the development of diabetes during follow-up was not associated with increased risks of cardiovascular events or all-cause mortality compared with remaining diabetes-free. These findings suggest that the development of diabetes during statin use does not attenuate the cardiovascular benefits of statin therapy.

Previous studies have consistently shown that statin use modestly increases the risk of new-onset diabetes. The Cholesterol Treatment Trialists’ Collaboration reported a dose-dependent association, with low- to moderate-intensity statins increasing diabetes incidence by approximately 10% and high-intensity statins by 36%. [13, 20, 21] A retrospective cohort study of US veterans also found that statin initiation was associated with both new-onset diabetes and progression of pre-existing diabetes, including increased insulin requirements and worsening glycemic control [12, 22]. However, few studies have directly compared cardiovascular outcomes among patients with statin-associated diabetes, those with spontaneous T2DM, and statin users who remain diabetes-free. Our study adds real-world evidence suggesting that although statin-induced dysglycemia warrants clinical consideration, it may not confer the same macrovascular risk as conventional T2DM. These findings support continued statin use in appropriate patients, even when diabetes develops during treatment.

Although statins are central to the prevention of cardiovascular disease, concerns about adverse effects, including new-onset diabetes continue to affect treatment decisions and long-term adherence.[23–25] While the absolute increase in risk is modest, media coverage and the lack of immediate symptomatic benefit in primary prevention settings contribute to poor adherence and high early discontinuation rates, exceeding 30% within two years of treatment.[26–28] These trends are especially pronounced among lower-risk patients and those prescribed high-intensity statins. Discontinuation, in turn, is associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [29, 30]. Clinical guidelines consistently emphasize that the cardiovascular benefits of statins outweigh the risk of new-onset diabetes. Nonetheless, it remains unclear whether statin-associated diabetes carries the same clinical implications as spontaneous T2DM, even today. Distinguishing between these two forms of diabetes in terms of disease progression and prognosis could enhance risk communication, support shared decision making, and help prevent unnecessary discontinuation among patients who stand to benefit most from ongoing statin therapy.

The mechanisms underlying statin-associated diabetes are not yet fully understood. Most evidence implicates both systemic insulin resistance and pancreatic β-cell dysfunction, which are central to the pathophysiology of type 2 diabetes [31]. However, more recent experimental data suggest that hepatic insulin signaling may play a primary role [32]. In hyperlipidemic mouse models, short-term statin exposure suppressed hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 alpha (HNF4α) activity, triggering a cascade involving PAQR9, proteasomal degradation of the phosphatase PPM1α via STUB1, and downstream activation of the ERK signaling pathway. This sequence impaired hepatic insulin signaling and may underlie the hyperglycemic effects observed with statin use. While these findings are preliminary and derived from animal models, they raise important questions about whether statin-associated diabetes differs mechanistically—and potentially prognostically—from spontaneous T2DM.

Clinical implications

Our findings reinforce the substantial cardioprotective benefits of statin therapy, even in individuals who develop diabetes during treatment. In prespecified subgroup analyses, the associations observed in the primary analyses were consistent across major clinical subgroups, with no significant heterogeneity by sex, age, hypertension status, income level, residential area, smoking status, alcohol use, physical activity, or body mass index. Thus, the development of diabetes during statin therapy should not warrant dose reduction or discontinuation, [5, 33] particularly in high-risk populations for ASCVD, as current guidelines recommend.

Although the adjusted hazard ratio for MACE did not reach statistical significance, the higher incidence rate among individuals who developed diabetes during statin therapy suggests a potential signal that merits longer-term follow-up. Furthermore, the potential impact of diabetes developing during statin therapy on microvascular complications, including retinopathy and nephropathy, remains to be fully elucidated. Clinicians should therefore remain vigilant in monitoring glycemic control, particularly in patients with multiple metabolic risk factors.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as this was a retrospective observational study, some residual confounding may persist despite extensive propensity-score matching and multivariable adjustment. Advanced analytic approaches, such as instrumental-variable or negative-control analyses, were not feasible with the available data. Second, the diagnosis of diabetes was based on claims data and prescription records, which may have missed subclinical or undiagnosed cases. Third, laboratory variables such as HbA1c were not available in the KNHIS database, and even if HbA1c data had been accessible from the health screening dataset, their biennial measurement intervals would not coincide with the timing of diabetes diagnosis. Fourth, information on statin type, dose, and intensity was unavailable in the KNHIS claims data; therefore, dose–response or potency-stratified analyses could not be performed. Fifth, information on microvascular outcomes—such as nephropathy, retinopathy, and renal function parameters—was not available, precluding assessment of these endpoints. Finally, because our cohort consisted exclusively of East Asian individuals, the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups may be limited.

Future directions

Further research is needed to clarify how variations in statin dose, intensity, and patient demographics influence the risk of dysglycemia. Although our findings suggest limited macrovascular risk associated with diabetes developing during statin therapy, additional studies are necessary to assess potential microvascular outcomes [34]. Prospective, multicenter trials across diverse populations could provide more definitive insights into the long-term metabolic effects of statins.

Conclusion

In this large, population-based cohort of East Asian adults, individuals who developed diabetes during statin therapy had a significantly lower risk of MACE compared with those who initiated statins after a diagnosis of pre-existing type 2 diabetes. Among statin users without diabetes at baseline, the development of diabetes during follow-up was not associated with an increased risk of MACE or all-cause mortality compared with those who remained diabetes-free. These findings suggest that the onset of diabetes during statin therapy does not diminish the cardioprotective benefits of statins. Therefore, the occurrence of diabetes during statin treatment should not justify discontinuation or avoidance of statin therapy in appropriate patients.
