Detecting Ultralight Dark Matter with Matter Effect
Xucheng Gan, Da Liu, Di Liu, Xuheng Luo, Bingrong Yu

Abstract
Ultralight particles, with a mass below the electronvolt scale, exhibit wave-like behavior and have arisen as a compelling dark matter candidate. A particularly intriguing subclass is scalar dark matter, which induces variations in fundamental physical constants. However, detecting such particles becomes highly challenging in the mass range above 10 − 6 ​ eV 10^{-6}\,\text{eV} , as traditional experiments face severe limitations in response time. In contrast, the matter effect becomes significant in a vast and unexplored parameter space. These effects include (i) a force arising from scattering between ordinary matter and the dark matter wind and (ii) a fifth force between ordinary matter induced by the dark matter background. Using the repulsive quadratic scalar-photon interaction as a case study, we develop a unified framework based on quantum mechanical scattering theory to systematically investigate these phenomena across both perturbative and non-perturbative regimes. Our approach not only reproduces prior results obtained through other methodologies but also covers novel regimes with nontrivial features, such as decoherence effects, screening effects, and their combinations. In particular, we highlight one finding related to both scattering and background-induced forces: the descreening effect observed in the non-perturbative region with large incident momentum, which alleviates the decoherence suppression. Furthermore, we discuss current and proposed experiments, including inverse-square-law tests, equivalence principle tests, and deep-space acceleration measurements. Notably, we go beyond the spherical approximation and revisit the MICROSCOPE constraint on the background-induced force in the large-momentum regime, where the decoherence and screening effects interplay. The ultraviolet models realizing the quadratic scalar-photon interaction are also discussed.

Summary

For the scattering force, Refs.

Although the partial wave analysis inherently includes some aspects of decoherence and descreening effects, this study does not comprehensively address these effects, nor does it thoroughly discuss the breakdown of the perturbative method in the non-perturbative regime. In our work, we conduct a systematic exploration of both the perturbative and non-perturbative regions by combining the Born approximation with partial wave analysis. We also address the decoherence effect in detail and investigate the optimal geometry of the test mass to maximize sensitivity. In addition, we perform a comparison between the Born approximation and partial wave analysis, identifying regions where the previously employed Born approximation breaks down. Moreover, we investigate the descreening effect in the scattering force. Furthermore, we compare the scattering and background-induced forces, identifying the regions where one effect dominates over the other.

11
) into Eq. (
9
), we obtain the Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} -\frac{1}{2m_{0}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}\psi+V_{\text{eff}}(\mathbf{r})\psi=E_{\text{eff}}(\mathbf{k})\psi\,.\end{aligned}

(12)

In the above equation,
V_{\text{eff}}
is the effective potential, and
E_{\text{eff}}
is the effective energy, given by:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} V_{\text{eff}}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{m_{\text{M}}^{2}(\mathbf{r})}{2m_{0}}\,,\qquad E_{\text{eff}}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{\mathbf{k}^{2}}{2m_{0}}\,.\end{aligned}

(13)

From these expressions, we see that the significance of the matter effect on the scalar field relies on the relative magnitudes of
V_{\text{eff}}
and
E_{\text{eff}}
, or equivalently speaking, the ratio of
m_{\text{M}}
to
\mathbf{k}
. Another key factor determining the significance of the matter effect is the geometry of scattered ordinary matter, characterized by
R
. However, this parameter does not appear explicitly in the Schrödinger equation above but in matching the boundary conditions. Since we are studying dark matter scattering with ordinary matter, we write
\psi
as the sum of an incident wave and a scattered wave given a specific momentum
\mathbf{k}
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \psi(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k})=\psi_{\text{inc}}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k})+\psi_{\text{sc}}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k}).\end{aligned}

(14)

Here,
\psi
represents the total wave outside the scattered object.
\psi_{\text{inc}}
and
\psi_{\text{sc}}
represent the incident and scattered wave functions, respectively, and are given by:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\{\begin{aligned} &\text{Incident Wave:}&&\quad\quad\psi_{\text{inc}}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k})=\absolutevalue{\psi_0}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}&\\
&\text{Scattered Wave:}&&\quad\quad\psi_{\text{sc}}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k})\simeq\absolutevalue{\psi_0}f(\theta;k)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}\quad\quad\text{(Far-Field)}\end{aligned}\right..\end{aligned}

(15)

The scattered wave function
\psi_{\text{sc}}
is presented in the asymptotic form, which is valid in the far-field limit.
\absolutevalue{\psi_0}=\absolutevalue{\phi_0}
is the amplitude of the incident dark matter wave.
f(\theta;k)
is the scattering amplitude.
\theta
is the deflection angle of the scattered wave, defined as the angle between
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{k}
. For simplicity, we consider only cylindrically symmetric collisions along the
\mathbf{k}
-axis, making the calculations independent of the azimuthal angle. In
\psi_{\text{sc}}
, there is a factor
e^{ikr}/r
, which corresponds to an outgoing spherical wave. From the Lippmann-Schwinger equation
[
122
]
, this is associated with the retarded Green’s function, reflecting the causal structure of the scattering process.

IV.1
Scattering Force

The scattering amplitude
f(\theta;k)
describes the asymptotic behavior of the scattered wave. Armed with this, we can get the scattering force on the ordinary object from the scalar dark matter with the momentum
\mathbf{k}
. Specifically, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{F}_{\text{sc}}(\mathbf{k})=\rho_{\phi}v^{2}\times\sigma_{\text{T}}(k)\times\hat{\mathbf{k}}.\end{aligned}

(16)

\sigma_{\text{T}}
is the momentum-transfer cross section, which is represented as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\text{T}}(k)=\int d\Omega\,\absolutevalue{f(\theta;k)}^{2}(1-\cos\theta).\end{aligned}

(17)

The factor
(1-\cos\theta)
is the
\mathbf{q}
’s projection on the
\mathbf{k}
-direction, and it suppresses the scattering amplitude
contribution from the forward direction. The force contributions being perpendicular to
\mathbf{k}
are canceled out due to the cylindrical symmetry of the system with respect to the
\mathbf{k}
-axis. In the meantime, using
f(\theta;k)
we could acquire the total scattering cross section

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sigma(k)=\int d\Omega\,\absolutevalue{f(\theta;k)}^{2}.\end{aligned}

(18)

IV.2
Background-Induced Force

In the meantime, the scattering process induces the spatial inhomogeneity of the scalar distribution. When a test mass
\mathcal{T}
moves inside such a scalar background, its mass varies with the spatial coordinate. Therefore, the test mass experiences an extra force

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{F}_{\text{bg}}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}V_{\text{bg}}.\end{aligned}

(19)

In the above formula,
V_{\text{bg}}
represents the potential induced by the spatial variation of the ultralight scalar, and
\mathbf{F}_{\text{bg}}
is the corresponding background-induced force.
V_{\text{bg}}
is represented as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} V_{\text{bg}}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k})&=\frac{m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}{4}\big(\absolutevalue{\psi(\vecr;\veck)}^{2}-\absolutevalue{\psi_0}^{2}\big)&&\quad\text{(Exact)}\\
&\simeq\frac{m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}{2}\real\left[\psi_{\text{inc}}^{*}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k})\,\psi_{\text{sc}}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k})\right]&&\quad(\absolutevalue{\psisc}<\absolutevalue{\psiinc}).\end{aligned}

(20)

Here,
m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}
denotes the effective mass induced by the test mass, and
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
is its volume. In the parameter space we are interested in, the oscillation frequency of the ultralight scalar is much higher than the response frequencies of the experiments. For this reason, in Eq. (
20
), we do the time average over the background-induced potential. “
\simeq
” holds in the region satisfying
\absolutevalue{\psisc}<\absolutevalue{\psiinc}
, otherwise the contribution from
\absolutevalue{\psisc}^{2}
should be included as shown in Eq. (
113
). For more detailed and quantitative discussions on the movement of the test mass in the scalar background, one can refer to Appendix
A
.

Substituting Eq. (
15
) into Eq. (
20
), we get the potential of
\mathcal{T}
induced by the scalar scattering with
\mathcal{S}
in the far-field limit, which is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} V_{\text{bg}}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k})\simeq\frac{\rho_{\phi}}{m_{0}^{2}}\,m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}\absolutevalue{f(\theta;k)}\frac{\cos(k r - \veck\cdot\vecr+ \arg f(\theta;k))}{r}\quad\quad\text{(Far-Field)}.\end{aligned}

(21)

From the above equation, we see that the strength of the background-induced potential is determined by the factor
f(\theta;k)
and
m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
. The information about the source mass
\mathcal{S}
, including its size and the magnitude of the matter effect, is encoded in the scattering amplitude
f(\theta;k)
, while the information about the test mass is encoded in the factor
m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
. The factor
\rho_{\phi}/m_{0}^{2}
arises from replacing
\absolutevalue{\psi_0}
with
\sqrt{2\rho_{\phi}}/m_{0}
. Given that
V_{\text{bg}}\propto 1/m_{0}^{2}
, the background-induced force becomes more sensitive when the scalar mass becomes smaller. Notably, Eq. (
21
) includes the term
\cos(kr - \veck\cdot\vecr+ \arg f(\theta;k))
. Therefore, the background-induced potential experiences rapid oscillation when the large-distance or large-momentum condition
kr>1
is satisfied. More detailed discussion and comparisons with previous works will be provided in Sec.
VI
.

V.1
Perturbative Region

In this subsection, we discuss the scattering force in the perturbative regions, including Region A (

In the rest of the subsection, we will introduce the Born approximation, provide a detailed discussion of Regions A and B, and determine the perturbative condition.

Because the entire calculation in this section is performed in the perturbative regime, the use of the Born approximation is well justified. As we are only focusing on the momentum distribution in the asymptotically far region, we directly compute the scattering amplitude using

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f(\theta;k)=-\frac{m_{\text{M}}^{2}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{V}}d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}\,\,e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\delta\mathbf{r}}\end{aligned}

In Region A,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{Region A}:\quad\sigma_{\text{T}}=\frac{(m_{\text{M}}^{2}\mathcal{V})^{2}}{4\pi}.\end{aligned}

is the test particle number, and
f_{\text{single}}
is the scattering amplitude of a single test particle. In the long-wavelength perturbative limit, the scattering force is scaled as
F_{\text{sc}}\propto\mathcal{V}^{2}\propto R^{6}
. As the total test mass also increases as
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{T}}\propto R^{3}
, the acceleration induced by the scattering force obeys
a_{\text{sc}}\propto\mathcal{V}^{2}/\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{T}}\propto R^{3}
, explaining the scaling law in the small-
R
limit in Fig.
5
.

In Region B, because
e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\delta\mathbf{r}}
is highly oscillatory when computing Eq. (
35
), we need to integrate over the concrete geometric shape of the test mass. Let us consider a uniform sphere with radius
R
, then Eq. (
35
) can be analytically expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f(\theta;k)=-m_{\text{M}}^{2}R^{3}\times\frac{\sin(qR)-qR\cos(qR)}{(qR)^{3}}.\end{aligned}

(37)

In the long-wavelength regime
kR<1
(Region A), the above expression returns to Eq. (
35
) as expected. Although Eq. (
37
) is derived for a uniform spherical potential, we expect an
\mathcal{O}(1)
factor difference when the detailed shape of the bulk matter is considered. Substituting Eq. (
35
) into Eq. (
17
) and expanding
\sigma_{\text{T}}
in the large-
R
limit, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{Region B}:\quad\sigma_{\text{T}}=\pi R^{2}\times\left(\frac{m_{\text{M}}}{k}\right)^{4}\times\frac{\log(4kR)}{2},\end{aligned}

(38)

which is written in a form suitable for comparison with the geometric cross section
\pi R^{2}
. Knowing that the total test mass scales as
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{T}}\propto R^{3}
, we have
a\propto\sigma_{\text{T}}/\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{T}}\propto R^{-1}
scaling in the large-
R
limit. The variation in the
\log(4kR)
is ignored, as it introduces only an
\mathcal{O}(1)
difference within the relevant parameter range. Equivalently, we can also write the momentum-transfer cross section as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{Region B}:\quad\sigma_{\text{T}}=\frac{(m_{\text{M}}^{2}\mathcal{V})^{2}}{4\pi}\times\frac{9}{8}\frac{\log(4kR)}{(kR)^{4}},\end{aligned}

(39)

which is written in a form that enables comparison with Eq. (
36
), the momentum-transfer cross section in Region A. In Region B, coherent enhancement is terminated as the dark matter wind begins to resolve the finite size of the test mass. This breakdown of coherent enhancement is referred to as the finite-size decoherence effect on the scattering force. For detailed derivations of the scattering cross section
\sigma
and momentum-transfer cross section
\sigma_{\text{T}}
in the perturbative region (Regions A and B), one can refer to Appendix
C.2
.

Before concluding the discussion on the perturbative region and transitioning to the non-perturbative regime, we must determine the conditions under which perturbativity breaks down. The key criterion is when the scattering cross-section in Eq. (
134
) saturates
\sim\pi R^{2}
. We avoid using the momentum-transfer cross section for this comparison because, as seen in Eq. (
17
), the non-perturbative wave function contributions in the forward direction are suppressed, making the condition weaker. For
kR<1
, Born approximation gives
\sigma\sim\pi R^{2}\times(m_{\text{M}}R)^{4}
. Thus, perturbativity holds as long as
R^{-1}>m_{\text{M}}
. For
kR>1
, Born approximation gives
\sigma\sim\pi R^{2}\times(m_{\text{M}}^{4}R^{2}/k^{2})
, giving the perturbativity condition
(kR^{-1})^{1/2}>m_{\text{M}}
. To derive the aforementioned perturbativity condition directly from comparing the wave functions, one can refer to Appendix
C.1
.

V.2
Non-Perturbative Region

As the interaction strength of the quadratic scalar-photon interaction increases, we eventually reach a non-perturbative regime where
\absolutevalue{\psisc}\sim\absolutevalue{\psiinc}
in the vicinity of the scattered object. In this situation, the general solution in Eq. (
128
) is invalid. Therefore, the partial wave analysis becomes essential, enabling us to effectively explore the non-perturbative regions and provide us with physical intuition as well. In the following subsection, we will utilize the partial wave analysis to explore Region C
(m_{\text{M}}>R^{-1}>k)
and Region D
(m_{\text{M}}>k>R^{-1})
, which is non-perturbative. In these regions, the scattered object can be approximated as a hard sphere with an infinite potential. We will also discuss the non-perturbative part of the Region E (
k>m_{\text{M}}>(kR^{-1})^{1/2}
), which is treated as the solid sphere that is penetrated by the incident scalar. We summarize that in the non-perturbative region there is an upper bound for the acceleration

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\text{T}}\lesssim\xi\times\pi R^{2},\end{aligned}

(40)

where the momentum-transfer cross section saturates as the coupling strength increases. Here
\xi
is a dimensionless factor that varies in the
kR\text{-}m_{\text{M}}
plane. In Region C (long-wavelength limit, hard sphere),
\xi=4
for the sphere, meaning that the whole surface of the sphere takes part in the scattering process when saturating the upper bound. In Region D (short-wavelength limit, hard sphere), there is
\xi=1
, which means only the dark matter flux within the geometric cross section is reflected when saturating the upper bound. In Region E
\setminus
B (short-wavelength limit, solid sphere),
\xi=\mathcal{O}(1)\times(m_{\text{M}}/k)^{4}
, which becomes similar to the perturbative result in Eq. (
38
). Based on the above discussion, we find that the upper bound of the acceleration of the scattered test mass
\mathcal{T}
is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} a_{\mathcal{T}}\lesssim\frac{\rho_{\phi}v^{2}_{0}\times\mathcal{O}(1)\times\pi R^{2}}{\rho_{\mathcal{T}}\times\mathcal{V}}\sim 10^{-13}\text{ m}/\text{s}^{2}\times\left(\frac{\rho_{\text{Pt}}}{\rho_{\mathcal{T}}}\right)\left(\frac{1\text{cm}}{R}\right),\end{aligned}

(41)

where
\rho_{\mathcal{T}}
is the density of the scattered test mass. To numerically evaluate the abovementioned maximal acceleration, we choose a
1\text{cm}
Platinum (Pt) sphere to set the benchmark values. The density of Platinum is
\rho_{\text{Pt}}=21.45\,\text{g}/\text{cm}^{3}
. For materials with lower densities, such as Aluminum (Al), Titanium (Ti), or Beryllium (Be), the maximum acceleration can be an order of magnitude higher. From the above discussion, we can find that the non-perturbative region actually provides the upper cutoff for the induced acceleration in the strong coupling region. That is to say, when the induced acceleration saturates Eq. (
41
), no matter how large the coupling is, the acceleration will never become larger.

Even though the back-of-envelope estimation gives similar results on the maximum value of
\sigma_{\text{T}}
, merely differing by
\mathcal{O}(0.1)
-
\mathcal{O}(1)
, the underlying physics varies in different regions. In the rest of this subsection, we will show the concrete calculation based on the partial wave analysis. To begin with, we list the momentum-transfer cross section under the framework of the partial wave analysis as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\text{T}}=\frac{4\pi}{k^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}(l+1)\left[\absolutevalue{A_l}^{2}+\absolutevalue{A_{l+1}}^{2}-A_{l}^{*}A_{l+1}-A_{l}A_{l+1}^{*}\right].\end{aligned}

(42)

A_{l}
is the amplitude of the
l
-th component of the scattered wave, and it can be represented as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} A_{l}=\frac{S_{l}-1}{2}=\frac{e^{2i\delta_{l}}-1}{2}.\end{aligned}

(43)

S_{l}
is the S-matrix for the
l
-th component and
\delta_{l}
is the phase shift. For the repulsive potential discussed in this paper, we generically have
\delta_{l}<0
. Since we are dealing with elastic scattering, the S-matrix satisfies the unitarity condition
\absolutevalue{S_l}=1
, being equivalent to the probability current conservation. The momentum-transfer cross section listed in Eq. (
42
) has two parts. The first part contains the diagonal terms, and it is the contribution from the integral
\int d\Omega\,\absolutevalue{f(\theta)}^{2}
, which is the ordinary cross section. The second part is the one containing the off-diagonal terms, and it is the contribution from the integral
-\int d\Omega\,\cos\theta\absolutevalue{f(\theta)}^{2}
, which suppresses the forward direction’s contribution. By matching the boundary condition at the spherical surface, we can exactly solve the phase shift of the uniform solid sphere as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \tan\delta_{l}=\frac{kj_{l}(k^{\prime}R)j_{l+1}(kR)-k^{\prime}j_{l}(kR)j_{l+1}(k^{\prime}R)}{kj_{l}(k^{\prime}R)y_{l+1}(kR)-k^{\prime}y_{l}(kR)j_{l+1}(k^{\prime}R)}.\end{aligned}

(44)

j_{l}
is
l
-th spherical Bessel function,
y_{l}
is
l
-th spherical Neumann function,
k
is the momentum of the incident scalar dark matter,
k^{\prime}=(k^{2}-m_{\text{M}}^{2})^{1/2}
is the scalar’s momentum inside the solid sphere.
R
is the radius of the sphere. For a more detailed discussion of scattering theory in the framework of the partial wave analysis, one can refer to Appendix
E
. For the derivation of Eq. (
42
), one can refer to Appendix
E.3
. The derivations of the outgoing partial wave amplitude Eq. (
43
) and the phase shift Eq. (
44
) can be found in Appendix
E.2
.

In Region C, we are discussing the low-momentum hard sphere scattering. Because the incident momentum
k
is much smaller than
R^{-1}
, merely the s-wave component contributes to the wave function. Therefore, the scattering force is calculated as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{Region C}:\quad\sigma_{\text{T}}=\frac{4\pi}{k^{2}}\absolutevalue{A_0}^{2}=4\pi R^{2}.\end{aligned}

(45)

A_{0}=(e^{2i\delta_{0}}-1)/2
and
\delta_{0}
is the phase-shift of the s-wave component. For the hard sphere low-
k
scattering,
\delta_{0}\simeq-kR
, therefore we can derive the above formula. To understand this formula more intuitively, the scattering process is in the long-wavelength limit. Therefore, the whole area of the scattered mass can participate in the scattering, and the momentum-transfer cross section is
4\pi R^{2}
. We can also equivalently express Eq. (
45
) as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{Region C}:\quad\sigma_{\text{T}}=\frac{(m_{\text{M}}^{2}\mathcal{V})^{2}}{4\pi}\times\left[\frac{3}{(m_{\text{M}}R)^{2}}\right]^{2},\end{aligned}

(46)

where the factor
3/(m_{\text{M}}R)^{2}
represents the characteristic suppression in Region C. Compared to the momentum-transfer cross section in Region A (see Eq. (
36
)), effectively only a fraction of the test mass participates in the scattering in Region C. Therefore, the suppressed cross section in Region C relative to Region A can be interpreted as the screening effect. This screening factor also appears in the discussion of the background-induced force in the far-field limit. The relevant discussion is provided in Sec.
VI.2
and explicitly illustrated in Eq. (
78
). This similarity arises from their shared mathematical origin: the ratio of the scattering amplitude
f(\theta;k)
between Regions C and A.

In Region D, we are dealing with the high-momentum hard sphere scattering. Because the incident momentum
k
is much larger than
R^{-1}
, high-
l
components of the wave function are excited. To acquire the correct result, we must sum over all the partial waves from
l=0
to
l_{\max}\sim kR
. From the numerical calculation, we find that

\sigma=2\pi R^{2}
in this region, which is different from
\sigma_{\text{T}}=\pi R^{2}
. This is because the cross section
\sigma
contains the classical reflection part (
\sigma_{\text{refl}}=\pi R^{2}
) and the shadow part (
\sigma_{\text{shadow}}=\pi R^{2}
). Only the classical part of the wave function contributes to the scattering force.

The
l

. To explain the above formula, the scalar dark matter behaves more particle-like in this region. Therefore, the scattering process can be treated as classical hard-sphere scattering. ) as

compared to the result in Region C, as shown in Eq. (

VI.2
Non-Perturbative Region: Low Momentum

Now, we move to the non-perturbative region where the screening effect becomes important. The non-perturbative background-induced force has previously been explored by Refs.
[
65
,
27
,
102
,
13
]
using the spherical symmetric ansatz. However, these studies only cover the low-momentum and near-field region (Regions A and C with
kr<1
) rather than other regions with relatively high momentum of the incident dark matter. In this subsection, we review their treatment but adapt to the notation and formalism used in this paper.

When the ultralight scalar forms a spherically symmetric configuration around the source, which is a uniform sphere, it has zero momentum in Eq. (
12
), which gives

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}\psi_{\text{sph}}=m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\,\theta(R_{\mathcal{S}}-r)\,\psi_{\text{sph}}.\end{aligned}

(69)

Using the spherical symmetric ansatz and imposing the boundary conditions that
\psi_{\text{sph}}=\absolutevalue{\psi_0}
when
r\rightarrow\infty
and
d\psi_{\text{sph}}/dr=0
at
r=0
, we know the wave function inside and outside the sphere are

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \psi_{\text{sph}}(r)=\absolutevalue{\psi_0}\times\left\{\begin{aligned} &C_{1}\frac{\sinh(\mMsource r)}{r}\quad&(r\leq R_{\mathcal{S}})\\
&1+\frac{C_{2}}{r}\quad&(r\geq R_{\mathcal{S}})\end{aligned}\right..\end{aligned}

(70)

Matching the boundary conditions of the wavefunctions inside and outside the spheres, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} C_{1}=\frac{1}{m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}\cosh(\mMsource R_\sourcemass)},\quad\quad C_{2}=-\frac{m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}}{4\pi}J_{+}(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}R_{\mathcal{S}}).\end{aligned}

(71)

Therefore, the function outside the sphere is represented as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} r\geq R_{\mathcal{S}}:\quad\quad\psi_{\text{sph}}(r)=\absolutevalue{\psi_0}\bigg[1-\frac{m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}}{4\pi r}J_{+}(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}R_{\mathcal{S}})\bigg].\end{aligned}

(72)

Here,
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}=4\pi R_{\mathcal{S}}^{3}/3
is the volume of the source. The function
J_{+}
is defined as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} J_{+}(x)=\frac{3(x-\tanh(x))}{x^{3}}.\end{aligned}

(73)

In the region where
x\ll 1
limit, there is
J_{+}\rightarrow 1
, while when
x\gg 1
, there is
J_{+}\rightarrow 3/x^{2}
and
1-(x^{2}/3)J_{+}\rightarrow 1/x
. Therefore, in the perturbative region,
C_{2}\rightarrow m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}/4\pi
, while in the highly non-perturbative region,
C_{2}\rightarrow 1
.

Substituting the wave function outside the sphere in Eq. (
72
) into Eq. (
20
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} V_{\text{bg},\,\text{sph}}=-\frac{\rho_{\phi}}{m_{0}^{2}}\frac{(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}})(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}})}{4\pi r}\times\mathcal{F}_{\text{sph}}(r).\end{aligned}

(74)

\mathcal{F}_{\text{sph}}
is the form factor incorporating the screening effect of the spherically symmetric configuration, and it is represented as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}_{\text{sph}}(r)=J_{+}(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}R_{\mathcal{S}})\times\left[1-\frac{1}{2}\frac{m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}}{4\pi r}J_{+}(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}R_{\mathcal{S}})\right].\end{aligned}

(75)

When
m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}R_{\mathcal{S}}<1
, the matter effect of the source is perturbative. In this region, we have
\mathcal{F}_{\text{sph}}\simeq 1
. When deeply going inside the strong-coupling region, where
m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}R_{\mathcal{S}}>1
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}_{\text{sph}}\simeq\frac{3}{(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}R_{\mathcal{S}})^{2}}\quad\quad\quad\text{(Strongly-Coupled + Far Field)}\end{aligned}

(76)

in the far-field region, which is understood as the screening effect. The suppression factor in Eq. (
76
) has two contributions:
1. When the potential barrier is sufficiently high, the scalar field cannot penetrate the solid sphere
\mathcal{S}
and instead decays exponentially inside it. As a result, only a thin spherical shell with thickness set by the penetration depth,
\Delta R_{\mathcal{S}}\simeq 1/m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}
, contributes effectively to the background-induced force, leading to a geometric suppression factor
\frac{4\pi R_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}\Delta R_{\mathcal{S}}}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}}\simeq\frac{3}{m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}R_{\mathcal{S}}}
. 2. According to Eq. (
72
), the scalar field at the surface of
\mathcal{S}
is further suppressed by an additional factor
\frac{1}{m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}R_{\mathcal{S}}}
. This reduces the scalar background near the source
\mathcal{S}
and hence the background-induced potential. Combining these two effects gives the form factor
\mathcal{F}_{\text{sph}}
in Eq. (
76
).

Apart from the method described above, which utilizes the spherically symmetric ansatz to derive the form factor of the background-induced force, we introduce an alternative approach based on partial wave analysis in the s-wave limit. The detailed calculation for arbitrary distances from the source can be found in Appendix
E.4
. Here, we choose the far-field limit to simplify the discussion. In the strong coupling regime (Region C), the source can be treated as a hard sphere. Because we focus on the region where the incident momentum is negligible (
k_{0}R_{\mathcal{S}}<1
), we only need to keep the s-wave component. Based on this, we substitute the s-wave phase shift

\delta_{0}=-kR_{\mathcal{S}}
into the scattering amplitude given in Eq. (
170
), and have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f_{\text{C}}(\theta;k)=\frac{\delta_{0}}{k}=-R_{\mathcal{S}}.\end{aligned}

(77)

Substituting Eq. (
77
) into Eq. (
21
), we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}_{\text{sph}}\simeq\frac{f_{\text{C}}(\theta;k)}{f_{\text{A}}(\theta;k)}=\frac{-R_{\mathcal{S}}}{-\frac{m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}}{4\pi}}=\frac{3}{(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}R_{\mathcal{S}})^{2}}\quad\quad\quad\text{(Strongly-Coupled + Far Field)},\end{aligned}

(78)

which gives the same form factor for the screening effect as Eq. (
75
) in the strongly coupled and far-field limit, but with a new physical interpretation. From Eq. (
77
), we see that the scattering amplitude in the strongly coupled Region C is independent of
m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}
or, equivalently, independent of the strength of the matter effect induced by the source. In contrast, the scattering amplitude in Region A follows the relation
f_{\text{A}}\propto m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}
. This indicates that when the source becomes sufficiently “hard”, the scattering of the incident scalar reaches saturation, regardless of further increasing the coupling constant. However, when computing the form factor, one might still expect that increasing the coupling constant would enhance scalar scattering, as inferred from the perturbative Region A. Therefore, the screening effect can also be interpreted as the gap between the expected scattering behavior derived from perturbative calculations in Region A and the reality of the non-perturbative saturation in Region C. Notably, increasing the incident momentum can enhance the scattering and consequently increase the background-induced force. This momentum-dependent enhancement of the background-induced force is the basic characteristic of the descreening effect discussed in Sec.
VI.3
.

For an intuitive illustration of the screening effect, one can refer to the left panel of Fig.
12
, which is computed through the partial wave analysis and consistent with the field configuration shown in Eq. (
70
). This figure shows the spherically symmetric configuration of
\absolutevalue{\psi}^{2}/\absolutevalue{\psi_0}^{2}-1
, the dimensionless and test-mass-independent part of the background-induced potential. We can find that near or inside the sphere, the field value of the scalar field is highly suppressed, while in the large distance, the scalar field tends to
\absolutevalue{\psi}\simeq\absolutevalue{\psi_0}
, which is consistent with the imposed boundary condition for the spherically symmetric solution covered in this section.

However, as one can see in the right panel of Fig.
12
, such a spherical ansatz breaks down when
kR>1
. In this region, two additional effects arise compared with the spherical symmetric ansatz. The first effect is that the high momentum of the dark matter wind squeezes the dark matter to the near-surface of the source mass, alleviating the screening effect mentioned above. The second effect is the decoherence suppression from the finite-phase-space and finite-size effects when
kr>1
, which is discussed in Sec.
VI.1
for the perturbative case. We stress that even in Regions A and C, where the s-wave approximation is legitimate, the spherical symmetric ansatz still cannot capture the complete information, as long as
k_{0}\neq 0
. The reason is that we can always find the large distance region satisfying
k_{0}r\gtrsim 1
as long as there is a hierarchy between
r
and
R
, where the finite-phase-space decoherence suppression shown in Fig.
10
or Fig.
11
becomes significant. We attach more detailed discussions in Appendix
E.4
.

The plots show
\absolutevalue{\psi}^{2}/\absolutevalue{\psi_0}^{2}-1

VI.3
Non-Perturbative Region: High Momentum

In this section, we discuss the non-perturbative region characterized by high incident mean momentum
k_{0}
of the dark matter. At the beginning of this section, we decompose the form factor in Eq. (
57
) as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k}_{0})=\mathcal{F}_{\text{sph}}(r)\times\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k}_{0}).\end{aligned}

(79)

Here,
\mathcal{F}_{\text{sph}}
is the spherically symmetric form factor described in Eq. (
75
), incorporating the screening effect in the zero-momentum limit. In Eq. (
79
), we define the reduced factor as
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\equiv\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{F}_{\text{sph}}
, which captures the interplay between the decoherence and screening effects in the strongly-coupled regime when the incident scalar momentum is significant. The deviation of the reduced form factor
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
from
1
quantifies the deviation of the real background-induced force from the one assuming the spherically symmetric ansatz, as shown in Eq. (
75
). In the strongly-coupled region where
\mathcal{F}
is highly suppressed by the screening effect, the definition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
normalizes the value to one when
k_{0}r<1
, which helps the visualization.

Notably, we discover the descreening (or deshielding) effect in the background-induced force, which substantially alleviates the screening effect and compensates for decoherence suppression when the incident momentum increases. Moreover, in contrast to the perturbative finite-size effect discussed in Fig.
11
, which at most changes the form factor by an order of magnitude, the finite size of the source mass becomes crucial in the non-perturbative region, leading to changes in the form factor by several orders of magnitude.

To numerically compute the values of
\mathcal{F}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
crossing the perturbative and non-perturbative regions, we use the partial wave analysis introduced in Appendix
E
. First, we calculate the incident wave function
\psi_{\text{inc}}
using Eq. (
165
) and the scattered wave function
\psi_{\text{sc}}
using Eq. (
166
). We utilize the complete expression of the incident plane wave
\psi_{\text{inc}}
and keep the partial wave components of the scattered wave
\psi_{\text{sc}}
up to
l_{\max}\sim kR_{\mathcal{S}}
. The specific choice of
l_{\max}
is listed in Appendix
E.2
. Next, we compute the background-induced potential using the full expression in Eq. (
20
). Finally, we perform the phase space integration using Eq. (
29
). To optimize computational efficiency on the cluster, we evaluate the form factors in the forward and backward directions as a benchmark. This approach reduces the 3D integral in Eq. (
29
) to a 2D integral over

The background-induced force between two objects leads to deviations from the inverse square law of gravity between two objects, which have been searched extensively in various types of experimental setups and on many different physical scales (see

While background-induced, scattering, vacuum, and Yukawa forces are all capable of generating anomalous accelerations, the background-induced force is distinguishable from the other forces under appropriate experimental conditions. First, we discuss the distinguishability of the background-induced force from static vacuum (see Appendix
B
) or Yukawa force (from linear scalar-SM coupling). Because of screening effects from the Earth, the density of the scalar may vary over time. Since the background-induced force is proportional to the dark matter energy density, its signal would exhibit periodic modulation, making it distinguishable from static forces. Second, we discuss the distinction between the background-induced force and the scattering force. The background-induced force acts between two objects, allowing its direction to be manipulated by adjusting the experimental setup. In contrast, the scattering force always points along the dark matter velocity vector. This directional difference leads to distinct modulation patterns, enabling experimental distinction between the two forces. Moreover, the detection of quadratic scalar-SM couplings is easier when both background-induced and scattering forces are present. Recasting experimental sensitivities separately for each force is therefore a conservative approach.

VIII
Conclusion

In this work, we systematically investigate the scattering force and background-induced force arising from the matter effect generated by a quadratic coupling between light scalar dark matter and Standard Model fields. By utilizing quantum mechanical scattering theory, we develop a unified framework to analyze both forces, perform a systematic classification of the parameter space (as illustrated in Fig.
1
), and explore all regimes—crossing perturbative and non-perturbative, low-momentum and high-momentum regions. Moreover, we reconcile previous discussions based on different methodologies.

For the scattering force, we unify the perturbative and non-perturbative analyses scattered across previous works, identifying regions where decoherence effects become significant and where perturbative calculations break down. We also discuss experimental sensitivities based on acceleration tests, as well as theoretical sensitivities by comparing signals with thermal and quantum noise. In addition, we provide a comparison of the scattering force and the background-induced force and identify the regions where each force dominates.

For the background-induced force in the perturbative region, we not only reproduce the perturbative results derived from finite-density field theory using the Born approximation, but also extend the analysis of decoherence effects to scenarios involving sources of finite size. Furthermore, we conduct a thorough investigation of the non-perturbative region, where the screening effect becomes important. We revisit the previously employed spherically symmetric ansatz, which is suitable for the low-momentum near-field region, and also analyze its limitations in the high-momentum or far-field region. Additionally, we highlight the descreening effect, which serves as a transition from the spherically screening ansatz to the perturbative treatment. We discuss the experimental constraints, including inverse square law tests and short-range E
\ddot{\rm o}
t-Wash equivalence principle tests. Sensitivities associated with the background-induced force are evaluated using acceleration tests and by comparing predicted signals with thermal and quantum noise. Moreover, we go beyond the spherically symmetric ansatz and revisit the MICROSCOPE constraint in the high-momentum region, where the descreening and decoherence effects reach a balance.

Finally, we present UV models that give rise to the quadratic scalar-photon coupling, which induces variations in the fine-structure constant. We consider models involving heavy
U(1)_{Y}
-charged fermions, heavy
U(1)_{Y}
-charged scalars, and dark QCD axions, and clarify the signs of the effective mass-squared contributions from matter effects in each case through a renormalization group analysis.

In the second equal sign, we take the NR limit of the test mass.
x_{\mathcal{T}}
is the four-coordinate of the test mass, and
\mathbf{v}_{\mathcal{T}}
is the three-velocity of the test mass. Throughout this paper, we impose a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry under the transformation
\phi\rightarrow-\phi
, which forbids linear terms in the Lagrangian. Therefore, after expanding
M_{\mathcal{T}}
over
\phi
, we write the Lagrangian as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} L_{\mathcal{T}}=\int d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}\Big[\rho_{\mathcal{T}}\frac{\mathbf{v}_{\mathcal{T}}^{2}}{2}-\frac{d\rho_{\mathcal{T}}}{d\phi^{2}}\bigg|_{\phi=0}\phi^{2}+\cdots\Big],\end{aligned}

(108)

where

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{d\rho_{\mathcal{T}}}{d\phi^{2}}\bigg|_{\phi=0}=\frac{m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}}{2}.\end{aligned}

(109)

Because the velocity of the test mass is negligible, we can neglect the cross-term between
\phi^{2}
and the kinetic term of the test mass. From Eq. (
108
), we can find that the term denoting the variation of the test mass
\mathcal{T}
sourced by the scalar field serves as the effective potential,
V_{\text{bg}}
. Utilizing the Euler-Lagrange equation, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{F}_{\text{bg}}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}V_{\text{bg}},\end{aligned}

(110)

where
V_{\text{bg}}
is the potential induced by the quadratic scalar-SM couplings. This potential is written as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} V_{\text{bg}}(t)=\frac{m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}}{2}\int d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}\,\phi^{2}(t,\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}).\end{aligned}

(111)

Here, we factor out the spatial gradient operator
\vnabla
from
V_{\text{bg}}
, which applies to the arbitrary shape in the perturbative region when there is no strong backreaction. In the strongly coupled regime, where screening effects are significant to the test mass, this factorization is no longer valid. The background-induced force experienced by the test mass in this regime will be discussed in Ref.
[
17
]
. In the equation above, the effective potential
V_{\text{bg}}
induced by the scalar background contains a time-modulating component. However, within the mass range considered in this work, the scalar field oscillates much faster than the detector response time. As a result, we can directly average over time and treat the effective potential as time-independent. For the monochromatic incident wave, substituting the scalar’s non-relativistic approximation
\phi=\real[e^{-i\omega t}\psi]
and doing time average over the background-induced potential, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle V_{\text{bg}}\rangle_{t}&=\frac{m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}}{2}\int d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}\,\frac{1}{2\pi/\omega}\int^{2\pi/\omega}_{0}dt\,\absolutevalue{\psi(\delta\vecr_\testmass)}^{2}\cos^{2}(\omega t-\arg\psi(\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}))\\
&=\frac{m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}}{4}\int d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}\,\absolutevalue{\psi(\delta\vecr_\testmass)}^{2}.\end{aligned}

(112)

From the above formula, we find that the background-induced potential is proportional to the probability amplitude of the scalar wave function. For the situation in which the incident scalar wave function is the plane wave, the
\absolutevalue{\psiinc}^{2}
contribution is the constant of the spatial coordinate and thus does not induce the extra force on the test mass. For this reason, we drop the plane wave probability amplitude (replace
\absolutevalue{\psi}^{2}
with
\absolutevalue{\psi}^{2}-\absolutevalue{\psi_0}^{2}
) in Eq. (
112
) and write the background-induced potential as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle V_{\text{bg}}\rangle_{t}=\frac{m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}}{2}\int d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}\left\{\real\left[\psi_{\text{inc}}^{*}(\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}})\psi_{\text{sc}}(\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}})\right]+\frac{\absolutevalue{\psi_\sct(\delta\vecr_\testmass)}^{2}}{2}\right\}.\end{aligned}

(113)

In the spatial region where
\absolutevalue{\psisc}<\absolutevalue{\psiinc}
, we keep only
\real\left[\psi_{\text{inc}}^{*}\psi_{\text{sc}}\right]
, the interference term between the incident wave and the scattered wave, in the above formula. However, when the scattering process is non-perturbative, we have
\absolutevalue{\psisc}\sim\absolutevalue{\psiinc}
in the scattering region. In this case, both terms in Eq. (
113
) must be kept. Throughout the rest of the paper, we use Eq. (
112
) to compute the additional force experienced by the test mass
\mathcal{T}
moving in the scalar background. For notational simplicity, we omit the sign
\langle\cdots\rangle_{t}
. Unless specified, all background-induced potentials discussed in the rest of the paper are time-averaged.

Appendix B
Forces in Quantum Field Theory

In this section, we provide a comprehensive derivation of scalar-mediated forces within the framework of quantum field theory for completeness. Using this formalism, we can simultaneously derive both the vacuum force and the background-induced force in the same framework. Such derivations help us to get the equivalent result as the method utilizing non-relativistic quantum mechanics in the weak-coupling region where the Born approximation is applicable, and the size of the source mass is small compared with the inverse of the incident momentum (Region A,
m_{\text{M}},k<R^{-1}
). Similar derivations can be found in Refs.
[
136
,
104
,
76
,
77
,

,

,
35
,
90
,
170
,
14
]
, which studied the background-induced effects in neutrino physics and other new physics scenarios.

Here, we focus on the scalar-induced forces acting on the test mass
\mathcal{T}
due to the scalar potential generated by the source mass
\mathcal{S}
. As discussed in Sec.
III
, in an environment composed of ordinary matter, we can do the replacement
\langle F^{2}/4\rangle\rightarrow-Q_{\gamma}\rho
. As a result, The vertex coupling between the test mass
\mathcal{T}
and the scalar field is
Q_{\gamma,\mathcal{T}}M_{\mathcal{T}}/\Lambda_{\gamma}^{2}=m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
, and the vertex coupling between the source mass
\mathcal{S}
and the scalar field is
Q_{\gamma,\mathcal{S}}M_{\mathcal{S}}/\Lambda_{\gamma}^{2}=m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}
. Using these expressions, we can compute the one-loop scattering amplitude in a dense environment utilizing

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} i{\cal M}=(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}})(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}})\int{\mathchar 22\mkern-12.0mud}^{4}k\,D_{\rm mod}(k)D_{\rm mod}(k+q)\;,\end{aligned}

(114)

where
q
is the momentum transfer. To include the background effect, we introduce the modified propagator

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} D_{\text{mod}}(k)\equiv D_{\text{vac}}(k)+D_{\text{bg}}(k),\end{aligned}

(115)

which contains the vacuum propagator
D_{\text{vac}}
and the background propagator
D_{\text{bg}}

D_{\text{vac}}
and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} D_{\text{vac}}(k)=\frac{1}{k^{2}-m^{2}_{0}+i\epsilon},\quad\quad D_{\text{bg}}(k)=-2\pi i\delta(k^{2}-m^{2}_{0})f_{\phi}({\mathbf{k}}),\end{aligned}

(116)

where
f_{\phi}(\mathbf{k})
is the dimensionless phase space distribution of the background particles. The
\delta

When both propagators in Eq. (
114

D_{\text{vac}}
term, it gives the amplitude from the exchange of two virtual scalars:

\displaystyle i{\cal M}_{\text{vac}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}})(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}})}{2}\int{\mathchar 22\mkern-12.0mud}^{4}k\,D_{\text{vac}}(k)D_{\text{vac}}(k+q)=i\frac{(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}})(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}})}{2}\times\frac{B_{0}(q^{2};m_{0},m_{0})}{16\pi^{2}},

(117)

where a symmetry factor of 1/2 has been included, and
B_{0}
is the standard Passarino-Veltman function
[
139
]
simplified to be

\displaystyle B_{0}(q^{2};m_{0},m_{0})=\frac{\sqrt{q^{2}\left(q^{2}-4m^{2}_{0}\right)}}{q^{2}}\log\left[\frac{2m^{2}_{0}-q^{2}+\sqrt{q^{2}\left(q^{2}-4m^{2}_{0}\right)}}{2m^{2}_{0}}\right]+\cdots.

(118)

Here, “
\cdots
” denotes the terms that have no branch cuts in the
q^{2}
-plane, and thus do not contribute to the vacuum force. The vacuum force only depends on the imaginary part of
{\cal M}_{\text{vac}}
, which, by definition, is connected to the discontinuity of the amplitude:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\rm Disc}\left[i{\cal M}_{\text{vac}}(q^{2})\right]&\equiv i{\cal M}_{\text{vac}}\left(q^{2}+i\epsilon\right)-i{\cal M}_{\text{vac}}\left(q^{2}-i\epsilon\right)\\
&=-2{\rm Im}\left[{\cal M}_{\text{vac}}(q^{2})\right]\\
&=-\Theta\left(q^{2}-4m^{2}_{0}\right)\frac{(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}})(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}})}{2}\frac{1}{8\pi q^{2}}\sqrt{q^{2}\left(q^{2}-4m^{2}_{0}\right)}\;,\end{aligned}

(119)

where
\Theta
is the step function. On the other hand, the background effect is captured by the “crossing terms” (namely, one of the propagators is on-shell while the other is off-shell):

\displaystyle i{\cal M}_{\text{bg}}=\frac{(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}})(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}})}{2}\int{\mathchar 22\mkern-12.0mud}^{4}k\left[D_{\text{vac}}(k)D_{\text{bg}}(k+q)+D_{\text{bg}}(k)D_{\text{vac}}(k+q)\right].

(120)

By integrating out the
k^{0}
component first and using the non-relativistic approximation
q^{\mu}\simeq(0,\mathbf{q})
, one obtains

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{\text{bg}}=(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}})(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}})\int\frac{{\mathchar 22\mkern-12.0mud}^{3}\mathbf{k}}{2\omega_{\mathbf{k}}}f_{\phi}(\mathbf{k})\left(\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}^{2}+2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{q}-i\epsilon}+\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}^{2}-2\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{q}+i\epsilon}\right).

(121)

Since the source mass
\mathcal{S}
is fixed while we measure the force experienced by the test mass
\mathcal{T}
, we express Eq. (
121
) in terms of the retarded Green’s function. This formulation is also consistent with causality in quantum mechanical scattering theory, as shown in Eq. (
15
), where the source mass generates only an outgoing wave without contributing to the incoming wave.

The potential originating from scattering is extracted by matching the scattering amplitude in the momentum space to the position space using
V(\mathbf{r})=-\int{\mathchar 22\mkern-12.0mud}^{3}\mathbf{q}\,e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{q})
, where
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{q})
can be amplitudes from both vacuum and background effects. For the vacuum amplitude, we use the dispersion technique
[
75
]

\displaystyle\int{\mathchar 22\mkern-12.0mud}^{3}\mathbf{q}\,e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}{\cal M}_{\text{vac}}(\mathbf{q})=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}r}\int_{4m^{2}_{0}}^{\infty}\text{Disc}\left[i\mathcal{M}_{\text{vac}}\left(t\right)\right]e^{-\sqrt{t}r}\text{d}t,

(122)

and then have

\displaystyle\text{Vacuum Potential:}\quad V_{\text{vac}}(\mathbf{r})=-(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}})(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}})\frac{m_{0}}{32\pi^{3}r^{2}}K_{1}\left(2m_{0}r\right),

(123)

where
K_{1}
is the modified Bessel function. In the near-field region where
r<m_{0}^{-1}
, the vacuum force follows the power law
V_{\text{vac}}\propto 1/r^{3}
.
In the far-field region where
r>m_{0}^{-1}
, the vacuum force follows
V_{\text{vac}}\propto e^{-2m_{0}r}/r^{5/2}
, which means that the vacuum force is exponentially suppressed at large distances because of the nonzero mass of the mediator. These characteristic behaviors at short and long distances arise from the shift-symmetry-breaking operators in Eq. (
1
), as shown by Refs.
[
75
,
78
,
79
,
22
]
.

On the other hand, the background-induced force is essentially a classical effect since there is only one off-shell propagator in the loop calculation. By performing the Fourier transform, we obtain

\text{Background-Induced Potential}:\quad V_{\text{bg}}(\mathbf{r})=-(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}})(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}})\frac{n_{\phi}}{4\pi r}\int\frac{{\mathchar 22\mkern-12.0mud}^{3}\mathbf{k}}{\omega_{\mathbf{k}}}\frac{f_{\phi}(\mathbf{k})}{n_{\phi}}\cos\left(kr-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}\right).

(124)

This is the relativistic form of the background-induced force, which is consistent with the previous work calculating the tree-level Compton scattering amplitude
[
170

D_{\text{vac}}
and
D_{\text{bg}}
, while the Ref.
[
170

D_{\text{bg}}
and sets the scalars to be the external legs. Because we focus on the ultralight dark matter, we take the non-relativistic limit
\omega_{\mathbf{k}}\simeq m_{0}
and recover the scalar-background-induced potential derived in Eq. (
59

Before ending this section, we compare the effects of the vacuum and background-induced potentials. For the perturbative region which applies for most of the terrestrial experiments, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \absolutevalue{\frac{V_\vac}{V_\bg}}\sim\frac{m_{0}^{3}}{\sigma_{k}^{3}\,N_{\phi}}\times\frac{K_{1}(2m_{0}r)}{m_{0}r}\times\max\big[1,(\sigma_{k}r)^{2}\big].\end{aligned}

(125)

Here
N_{\phi}\sim n_{\phi}/\sigma_{k}^{3}
is the occupation number of the ultralight dark matter, and
\max\big[1,(\sigma_{k}r)^{2}\big]
arises from the finite-phase-space effect of
V_{\text{bg}}
as derived in Eq. (
60
). For example, when
m_{0}\sim 0.1\,\text{eV}
, we have
N_{\phi}\sim 10^{8}
, which implies that
\absolutevalue{V_\vac/V_\bg}\ll 1
. In the non-perturbative region where the equivalence principle test on the MICROSCOPE satellite becomes relevant, there is an
\sim 1/(m_{\text{M}}R)^{2}
suppression (s-wave limit) on the background-induced force due to the screening effect as shown in Eq. (
76

V_{\text{bg}}
from the large occupation number (
N_{\phi}\sim 10^{44}
for
m_{0}\sim 10^{-10}\,\text{eV}
) given that the MICROSCOPE experiment constrains a much lighter region. (2) The exponential suppression of
V_{\text{vac}}
(
m_{0}R_{\oplus}\sim 10^{3}
for
m_{0}\sim 10^{-10}\,\text{eV}
). Therefore,
\absolutevalue{V_\vac/V_\bg}\ll 1
remains valid for the MICROSCOPE experiment. Based on the discussion in this subsection, we conclude that the force from the vacuum potential is negligible compared to the background-induced potential in the macroscopic experiments discussed in this paper.

Appendix C
Born Approximation

The Born approximation is the standard approach for dealing with the quantum mechanical scattering in the perturbative regime, where
|\psi_{\text{sc}}|<|\psi_{0}|

[
37
]
. In this section, we begin by introducing the solution to the Lippmann-Schwinger equation
[
122
]
. We then derive the scattering amplitude, which describes the wave function’s behavior in the asymptotic far-field limit. Next, we present analytical results for both the scattering cross section and the momentum-transfer cross section of a solid sphere. Finally, we prove that in the perturbative region where the finite-size effects become significant, the background-induced potential can be obtained by directly integrating the background-induced potential from point-to-point interaction.

C.1
Wave Function

In this subsection, we discuss the wave function in the perturbative limit. Substituting
\psi=\psi_{\text{inc}}+\psi_{\text{sc}}
into the Schrödinger equation Eq. (
12
), we have the exact representation

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \psi_{\text{sc}}(\mathbf{r})=\int_{\mathcal{V}}d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}\,G^{(\pm)}(\mathbf{r},\delta\mathbf{r})\,V_{\text{eff}}(\delta\mathbf{r})\,\psi(\delta\mathbf{r}),\end{aligned}

(126)

which is known as the Lippmann-Schwinger equation
[
122
]
.
G^{(\pm)}(\mathbf{r},\delta\mathbf{r})
are the Green’s functions represented as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} G^{(\pm)}(\mathbf{r},\delta\mathbf{r})=\Big\langle\mathbf{r}\Big|\frac{1}{E-H_{0}\pm i\epsilon}\Big|\delta\mathbf{r}\Big\rangle=-\frac{m_{0}}{2\pi}\,\frac{\exp(\pm i \abs{\veck}\abs{\vecr-\delta\vecr})}{\absolutevalue{\vecr- \delta\vecr}},\end{aligned}

(127)

where
(+)
represents the retarded Green’s function, and
(-)
represents the advanced Green’s function.
E=k^{2}/2m_{0}
is the energy of the incident wave.
H_{0}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}/2m_{0}
is the free Hamiltonian. Because we focus on the outgoing scattered wave, we merely choose the retarded Green’s function because of the causality. Substituting
\psi
with zeroth order wave function
\psi_{\text{inc}}
on the right-hand-side of Eq. (
126
), we have the first-order scattered wave function

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \psi_{\text{sc}}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k})=-\absolutevalue{\psi_0}\frac{m_{0}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathcal{V}}d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}\frac{\exp(i \abs{\veck} \abs{\vecr- \delta\vecr})}{\absolutevalue{\vecr- \delta\vecr}}V_{\text{eff}}(\delta\mathbf{r})\exp(i \veck\cdot\delta\vecr).\end{aligned}

(128)

For the perturbativity condition to hold, we want
\absolutevalue{\psisc}_{r\sim R}\lesssim\absolutevalue{\psiinc}
. In the short-wavelength limit where
kR\lesssim 1
, the entire spherical volume contributes to the integration in Eq. (
128
). So we have
\absolutevalue{\psisc}_{r\sim R}\sim\absolutevalue{\psi_0}m_{\text{M}}^{2}R^{3}/R\lesssim\absolutevalue{\psi_0}
, which leads to
m_{\text{M}}\lesssim R^{-1}
. In the long-wavelength limit where
kR\gtrsim 1
, the exponential term inside Eq. (
128
) is highly oscillatory, so only a shell of thickness
k^{-1}
effectively contributes to the integration. This gives
\absolutevalue{\psisc}_{r\sim R}\sim\absolutevalue{\psi_0}m_{\text{M}}^{2}R^{2}k^{-1}/R\lesssim\absolutevalue{\psi_0}
, leading to
m_{\text{M}}\lesssim(kR^{-1})^{1/2}
. To summarize, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{Perturbativity Condition:\quad}\left\{\begin{aligned} \text{Region A}:\quad&kR\lesssim 1,\,\,m_{\text{M}}\lesssim R^{-1}\\
\text{Region B}:\quad&kR\gtrsim 1,\,\,m_{\text{M}}\lesssim(kR^{-1})^{1/2}\end{aligned}\right.,\end{aligned}

(129)

which can be utilized to divide the perturbative region of the parameter space shown in Fig.
1
.

In the far-field region which satisfies
r>\max(kR^{2},R)
, we can neglect the oscillating term, factor out the spherical wave
e^{ikr}/r
, and approximately write
\psi_{\text{sc}}
as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{Far-Field}:\quad\psi_{\text{sc}}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k})\simeq\underbrace{-\frac{m_{0}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathcal{V}}d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}\,V_{\text{eff}}(\delta\mathbf{r})e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\delta\mathbf{r}}}_{f(\hat{\mathbf{r}};\mathbf{k})}\times\absolutevalue{\psi_0}\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}.\end{aligned}

(130)

Here we get the scattering amplitude
f(\hat{\mathbf{r}};\mathbf{k})
listed in Eq. (
35
), which describes the asymptotic behavior of the wave function in the direction labeled by
\hat{\mathbf{r}}
. When discussing the system with the azimuthal symmetry, we can write
f(\hat{\mathbf{r}},\mathbf{k})
as
f(\theta;k)
. For the solid sphere with a radius of
R
, we use the formula

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\int_{\mathcal{V}}d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}\,e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\delta\mathbf{r}}=\frac{3(\sin(q R)-qR\cos(q R))}{(qR)^{3}},\end{aligned}

(131)

and get the analytical representation, which is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{Solid Sphere}:\quad f(\theta;k)=-m_{\text{M}}^{2}R^{3}\times\frac{\sin(qR)-qR\cos(qR)}{(qR)^{3}}\simeq\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\frac{m_{\text{M}}^{2}\mathcal{V}}{4\pi}&\quad(qR<1)\\
&\frac{m_{\text{M}}^{2}\mathcal{V}}{4\pi}\times\frac{3\cos(qR)}{(qR)^{2}}&\quad(qR>1)\end{aligned}\right..\end{aligned}

(132)

Here,
\mathcal{V}=4\pi R^{3}/3
is the volume of the solid sphere,
q=2k\sin(\theta/2)
is the magnitude of the momentum transfer. In the above formula, “
=
” denotes the exact integration, and “
\simeq
” denotes the scattering amplitude in different limits of
qR
.

In the long-wavelength limit (
kR<1
), we have:

•

All Direction:

f(\theta;k)\propto\mathcal{V}
.

In the short-wavelength limit (
kR>1
), we have:

•

Forward Direction (
\absolutevalue{\theta}<(kR)^{-1}
):
f(\theta;k)\propto\mathcal{V}
.

•

Non-Forward Direction (
\absolutevalue{\theta}>(kR)^{-1}
):

f(\theta;k)\propto\mathcal{V}\times\cos(qR)/(qR)^{2}
.

The relation
f(\theta;k)\propto\mathcal{V}\propto\mathcal{N}
represents the coherent enhancement of the scattering amplitude, where
\mathcal{N}
is the number of particles in the scattered object. In the long-wavelength limit, this enhancement occurs in all directions. However, in the short-wavelength limit, coherent enhancement persists only in the forward direction, while in non-forward directions, it is suppressed due to finite-size decoherence effects.

C.2
Cross Sections

To calculate the scattering cross section and the momentum transfer before or after the collision, one only needs to focus on the asymptotic behavior of the scattered wave. More specifically, we use the formulas of the cross section and momentum-transfer cross section

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sigma(k)=\int d\Omega\absolutevalue{f(\theta;k)}^{2},\quad\quad\sigma_{\text{T}}(k)=\int d\Omega\absolutevalue{f(\theta;k)}^{2}(1-\cos\theta).\end{aligned}

(133)

To calculate the cross section of the solid sphere, we substitute Eq. (
132
) into Eq. (
133
). Then we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sigma(k)=\pi R^{2}\times\left(\frac{2m_{\text{M}}^{4}R^{2}}{k^{2}}\right)\times F_{\sigma}(2kR),\end{aligned}

(134)

where the dimensionless function
F_{\sigma}
is defined as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} F_{\sigma}(x)&=\int^{x}_{0}d\xi\,\frac{(\sin\xi-\xi\cos\xi)^{2}}{\xi^{5}}\\
&=\frac{\cos(2x)-1+2x\sin(2x)-2x^{2}+2x^{4}}{8x^{4}}.\end{aligned}

(135)

The approximate form in different limits of
kR
is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sigma(k)\simeq\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{(m_{\text{M}}^{2}\mathcal{V})^{2}}{4\pi}&\quad(kR<1)\\
&\frac{(m_{\text{M}}^{2}\mathcal{V})^{2}}{4\pi}\times\frac{9}{8}\frac{1}{(kR)^{2}}&\quad(kR>1)\end{aligned}\right..\end{aligned}

(136)

In the region where
kR<1
, we approximately have
\sigma/\pi R^{2}\sim(m_{\text{M}}R)^{4}
, which gives the perturbativity bound
m_{\text{M}}<R^{-1}
. In the region where
kR>1
, we have
\sigma/\pi R^{2}\sim m_{\text{M}}^{4}R^{2}/k^{2}
, which gives the perturbativity bound
m_{\text{M}}<(kR^{-1})^{1/2}
. In this region, because
\sigma(k)\sim f(\theta=0)\times(1/kR)^{2}
, we know that the major contribution comes from the wave function in the forward direction.

We also calculate the momentum-transfer cross section utilizing

\begin{split}\sigma_{\text{T}}&=\pi R^{2}\times\frac{m_{\rm M}^{4}}{k^{4}}\times F_{\text{T}}(2kR).\\
\end{split}

(137)

The dimensionless function
F_{\text{T}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} F_{\text{T}}(x)&=\int_{0}^{x}d\xi\,\frac{(\sin\xi-\xi\cos\xi)^{2}}{\xi^{3}}\\
&=\frac{-1+\cos 2x}{4x^{2}}+\frac{\sin 2x}{2x}+\frac{-1-\text{Ci}(2x)+\gamma_{\text{E}}+\log 2x}{2}.\\
\end{aligned}

(138)

Here
\text{Ci}(x)=-\int_{x}^{\infty}d\xi\cos\xi/\xi
is cosine integral function, and
\gamma_{\text{E}}\simeq 0.57721
is the Euler’s constant. In long-wavelength and short-wavelength limits, the momentum-transfer cross section is approximately

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\text{T}}(k)\simeq\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{(m_{\text{M}}^{2}\mathcal{V})^{2}}{4\pi}&\quad(kR<1)\\
&\frac{(m_{\text{M}}^{2}\mathcal{V})^{2}}{4\pi}\times\frac{9}{8}\frac{\log(4kR)}{(kR)^{4}}&\quad(kR>1)\end{aligned}\right.,\end{aligned}

(139)

which appears in Eq. (
36
) and Eq. (
38
) in the main text. The result in the long-wavelength limit (
kR<1
), is derived using the Taylor expansion
\text{Ci}(2x)\simeq\gamma_{E}+\log(2x)-x^{2}+x^{4}/6+\mathcal{O}(x^{6})
. The result in the short-wavelength limit (
kR>1
) is derived using the asymptotic expansion
\text{Ci}(2x)\simeq\sin(2x)/2x-\cos(2x)/4x^{2}
. Here, we find that
\sigma_{\text{T}}
increases much slower than
\sigma
as
R
increases. This is because when calculating
\sigma_{\text{T}}
, the extra factor
(1-\cos\theta)
suppresses the contribution of the wave function in the forward direction (
\absolutevalue{\theta}<(kR)^{-1}
).

C.3
Background-Induced Force

In this subsection, we derive the general expression for the background-induced force under the first-order Born approximation. Combining Eq. (
128
) and Eq. (
113
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} V_{\text{bg}}(\mathbf{k})&=-\frac{\rho_{\phi}}{m_{0}^{2}}\,\frac{(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}})\,(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}})}{4\pi r}\\
&\quad\times\frac{r}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}}d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{S}}\frac{\cos(\abs{\veck}\abs{\vecr+ \delta\vecr_\testmass- \delta\vecr_\sourcemass}-\veck\cdot(\vecr+ \delta\vecr_\testmass- \delta\vecr_\sourcemass))}{\absolutevalue{\vecr+ \delta\vecr_\testmass- \delta\vecr_\sourcemass}},\end{aligned}

(140)

which is the full form of the background-induced potential in the perturbative region when both the test and source masses have a finite geometric shape. Here,
\mathbf{r}
is the position vector pointing from
O_{\mathcal{S}}
, the reference point on the source mass, to
O_{\mathcal{T}}
, the reference point on the test mass.
\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{S}}
denotes the position vector pointing from the
O_{\mathcal{S}}
to a point within the source mass, and
\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}
is the position vector pointing from
O_{\mathcal{T}}
to a point within the test mass. Thus,
\mathbf{r}+\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}-\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{S}}
gives the relative position vector from an infinitesimal volume element in the source to one in the test mass. The specific choices of
O_{\mathcal{S}}
and
O_{\mathcal{T}}
are flexible as long as they are fixed points attached to the respective experimental objects. For spherically symmetric objects, it is conventional and convenient to place these reference points at the centers of the spheres to simplify the computation.

In Eq. (
140
), we prove strictly from quantum mechanics that in the perturbative limit, the total background-induced potential is the linear combination of the point-to-point potential between different volume elements. For this equation to hold, both the test and source masses must satisfy the perturbativity conditions, which are

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{T}\in\text{Region A}_{\mathcal{T}}\cup\text{Region B}_{\mathcal{T}},\quad\mathcal{S}\in\text{Region A}_{\mathcal{S}}\cup\text{Region B}_{\mathcal{S}}.\end{aligned}

(141)

Given Eq. (
140
), we find an interesting phenomenon: When either the source mass or the test mass has a size larger than the de Broglie wavelength (
kR_{\mathcal{T}}>1
or
kR_{\mathcal{S}}>1
), the cosine term in Eq. (
140
) undergoes rapid oscillations during spatial integration. Such a spatial oscillation leads to the strong cancellation of the background-induced potential when the phase space configuration is monochromatic characterized by momentum
\mathbf{k}
. When the phase space also has a finite width, things are more complicated. More detailed discussions are provided in Appendix
D
and in Sec.
VI.1
.

Appendix D
Perturbative Decoherence Effects

In this appendix, we discuss the decoherence effects coming from the finite phase space and the finite size in the perturbative region, where the Born approximation introduced in Appendix
C
is valid. When the phase space distribution has a finite width, such as the boosted Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution listed in Eq. (
22
), it is necessary to average Eq. (
140
) over the phase space. After performing this averaging, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}=\frac{r}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}}d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{S}}\int{\mathchar 22\mkern-12.0mud}^{3}\mathbf{k}\frac{f_{\phi}(\mathbf{k})}{n_{\phi}}\frac{\cos(\abs{\veck}\abs{\vecr+ \delta\vecr_\testmass- \delta\vecr_\sourcemass}-\veck\cdot(\vecr+ \delta\vecr_\testmass- \delta\vecr_\sourcemass))}{\absolutevalue{\vecr+ \delta\vecr_\testmass- \delta\vecr_\sourcemass}}.\end{aligned}

(142)

To evaluate Eq. (
142
), one needs to perform a multidimensional integration over the source, test mass, and phase space. In this appendix, we consider several simplified scenarios where exact or approximate analytical results can be obtained. First, we discuss the background-induced force between two point-like masses using the phase space distribution in Eq. (
22
). In this case, the form factor reduces to a 2D integral, and we derive exact analytical expressions in the forward and backward directions. Next, we investigate the finite-size effect of a solid spherical source. To simplify the analysis, we assume a monochromatic phase-space distribution with the momentum
\mathbf{k}
, i.e.
f_{\phi}(\mathbf{p})=(2\pi)^{3}n_{\phi}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k})
, which allows for exact analytical expressions in both the forward and backward directions. Finally, we discuss the finite-size effects of both the source and test masses in the far-field limit. The combined analysis incorporating finite-source and finite-phase-space effects in the perturbative region is presented in the main text, as shown in detail in Sec.
VI.1
.

D.1
Finite-Phase-Space Effect

We begin with the situation in Region A, where both the source and test masses are point-like. In this region, the form factor is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}_{\text{A}}(\mathbf{r})=\int{\mathchar 22\mkern-12.0mud}^{3}\mathbf{k}\,\frac{f_{\phi}(\mathbf{k})}{n_{\phi}}\cos(k r-\veck\cdot\vecr).\end{aligned}

(143)

Substituting the boosted Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution in Eq. (
22
) into
\mathcal{F}_{\text{A}}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{A}}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left(2\pi\sigma_{k}^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\sin\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi_{\mathbf{k}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,k^{2}\,e^{-\frac{k^{2}+k_{0}^{2}}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}}}e^{\frac{kk_{0}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}}\cos\left(kr\left(1-\cos\theta\right)\right),

(144)

where the deflection angle
\theta

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos\left(\xi\cos\phi_{\mathbf{k}}\right)\,d\phi_{\mathbf{k}}=2\pi J_{0}(\xi)\,,\quad\quad\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin\left(\xi\cos\phi_{\mathbf{k}}\right)\,d\phi_{\mathbf{k}}=0\,,\end{aligned}

(145)

then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{A}}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{2\pi}{\left(2\pi\sigma_{k}^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,k^{2}e^{-\frac{k^{2}+k_{0}^{2}}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}}}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\sin\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\,e^{\frac{kk_{0}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}}\,J_{0}\left(kr\sin\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\sin\theta_{\mathbf{r}}\right)\cos\left[kr\left(1-\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{r}}\right)\right],

(146)

where
J_{0}

\theta_{\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{F}_{\text{A}}(\theta_{\mathbf{r}}=0)=\frac{k_{0}^{2}\left[1+\erf\left(\frac{k_{0}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{k}}\right)\right]}{2\left(k_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{4}\,r^{2}\right)}+\frac{e^{-2\sigma_{k}^{2}r^{2}}}{2\left(k_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{4}\,r^{2}\right)}\real\left[e^{-2ik_{0}r}(k_{0}^{2}-ik_{0}\,\sigma_{k}^{2}\,r+2\sigma_{k}^{4}\,r^{2})\,\,\text{erfc}\left(\frac{k_{0}-2i\sigma_{k}^{2}\,r}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{k}}\right)\right]\end{aligned}

(147)

and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}_{\text{A}}(\theta_{\mathbf{r}}=\pi)&=\frac{k_{0}^{2}\,\text{erfc}\left(\frac{k_{0}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{k}}\right)}{2(k_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{4}\,r^{2})}+\frac{e^{-2\sigma_{k}^{2}r^{2}}}{2(k_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{4}\,r^{2})}\real\left[e^{-2ik_{0}r}(k_{0}^{2}-ik_{0}\,\sigma_{k}^{2}\,r+2\sigma_{k}^{4}\,r^{2})\erf\left(\frac{k_{0}-2i\sigma_{k}^{2}\,r}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{k}}\right)\right]\\

(148)

Here
\erf(x)\equiv\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-t^{2}}{\rm d}t
is the error function and
\text{erfc}(x)\equiv 1-\erf(x)
is the complementary error function. In Fig.
10
, we present our numerical results, including the form factors in the forward and backward directions, computed using Eq. (

\mathbf{r}

In the short-distance limit, the forward and backward form factors listed in Eq. (

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} k_{0}r<1:\quad\mathcal{F}_{\text{A}}(\theta_{\mathbf{r}}=0)\simeq 1-\left[2+\frac{k_{0}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}e^{-\frac{k_{0}^{2}}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}}}\left(\frac{k_{0}}{\sigma_{k}}+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{k_{0}}\right)-\left(\frac{k_{0}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}-\frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{k_{0}^{2}}+2\right)\text{erf}\left(\frac{k_{0}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{k}}\right)\right]\sigma_{k}^{2}\,r^{2}\end{aligned}

(149)

From Eq. (

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} k_{0}r>1:\quad\mathcal{F}_{\text{A}}(\theta_{\mathbf{r}}=0)\simeq\frac{k_{0}^{2}}{2k_{0}^{2}+2\sigma_{k}^{4}r^{2}}\left[1+\text{erf}\left(\frac{k_{0}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{k}}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{\sigma_{k}}{k_{0}}e^{-\frac{k_{0}^{2}}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}}}\right]\end{aligned}

(151)

and

(152)

We find that both form factors experience suppression due to the finite-phase-space effect when
k_{0}r>1

becomes highly oscillatory in this region. Moreover, from Eq. (
151
) and Eq. (
152
) we can find that the backward direction is suppressed more than the forward direction. This suppression arises because of two reasons. First, the boosted Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution gives more weight in the phase-space integration around
\mathbf{k}\sim\mathbf{k}_{0}
. Second, the term
\cos(kr - \veck\cdot\vecr)
oscillates more rapidly when
\mathbf{k}
is anti-parallel to
\mathbf{r}
. Therefore, when
\mathbf{r}
is anti-parallel to
\mathbf{k}_{0}
, which means the test mass is located in the backward direction, the cancellation during the phase space integration becomes the strongest.

D.2
Monochromatic Finite-Size Effect

We now compute the finite-size effect of the experimental objects, assuming a monochromatic phase space distribution with momentum
\mathbf{k}
. For simplicity, we consider a source as a uniform sphere of radius
R
and begin with the case where the test mass is point-like. Under these conditions, the form factor in Eq. (
142
) reduces to

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{k})\equiv\frac{r}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{S}}\,\frac{\cos(\abs{\veck} \abs{\vecr-\delta\vecr_\sourcemass}-\veck\cdot\left(\vecr-\delta\vecr_\sourcemass\right))}{\left|\mathbf{r}-\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{S}}\right|}\;,

(153)

where
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}=4\pi R^{3}_{\mathcal{S}}/3
is the volume of the source, and the integral is performed over the that. For the forward and backward directions, we find the analytical expression of the forward and backward form factors, which are

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{Forward:}\quad\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\theta_{\mathbf{r}}=0;\mathbf{k}\right)&=3\sqrt{\pi}\left(\frac{r}{kR^{2}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\left[\mathscr{C}\left(R_{\mathcal{S}}\sqrt{\frac{k}{\pi r}}\right)\sin\left(\frac{kR^{2}_{\mathcal{S}}}{2r}\right)-\mathscr{S}\left(R_{\mathcal{S}}\sqrt{\frac{k}{\pi r}}\right)\cos\left(\frac{kR^{2}_{\mathcal{S}}}{2r}\right)\right],\end{aligned}

(154)

and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{Backward:}\quad\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\theta_{\mathbf{r}}=\pi;\mathbf{k}\right)&=\frac{3\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\left(\frac{r}{kR^{2}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\bigg[\frac{\sin\left(2kr\right)\sin\left(2kR_{\mathcal{S}}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi kr}}\\
&\quad\quad+\left(\mathscr{C}\left(\xi_{+}\right)-\mathscr{C}\left(\xi_{-}\right)\right)\sin\left(\frac{kR^{2}_{\mathcal{S}}}{2r}\right)-\left(\mathscr{S}\left(\xi_{+}\right)-\mathscr{S}\left(\xi_{-}\right)\right)\cos\left(\frac{kR^{2}_{\mathcal{S}}}{2r}\right)\bigg].\end{aligned}

(155)

In the above formulas,
\xi_{\pm}\equiv\left(2r\pm R_{\mathcal{S}}\right)\sqrt{k/(\pi r)}
.

\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{S}
are Fresnel integrals defined as

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}(z)\equiv\int_{0}^{z}\cos\left(\frac{\pi t^{2}}{2}\right)dt\;,\qquad\mathscr{S}(z)\equiv\int_{0}^{z}\sin\left(\frac{\pi t^{2}}{2}\right)dt\;.

(156)

In the far-field region (
r>kR_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}
), they are reduced to

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{Forward:}\quad\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\theta_{\mathbf{r}}=0;\mathbf{k}\right)&\simeq 1\quad\quad\text{(Far-Field)},\end{aligned}

(157)

and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{Backward:}\quad\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\theta_{\mathbf{r}}=\pi;\mathbf{k}\right)&\simeq\frac{3(\sin\left(2kR_{\mathcal{S}}\right)-2kR_{\mathcal{S}}\cos\left(2kR_{\mathcal{S}}\right))}{(2kR_{\mathcal{S}})^{3}}\times\cos\left(2kr\right)\quad\quad\text{(Far-Field)}.\end{aligned}

(158)

The expansions of the form factors listed in Eq. (
157
) and Eq. (
158
) agree with the results derived from the Born approximation after the far-field expansion.

Now, let us consider the case where both the source and test masses have finite sizes. Even though a complete investigation needs numerical evaluation, as shown in Eq. (
142
), we can obtain the analytical expression in the far field region, where
r>kR_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}
and
r>kR_{\mathcal{T}}^{2}
. We express the cosine term in Eq. (
140
) as the real part of an exponential function, apply a similar technique used to derive Eq. (
130
), and obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} V_{\text{bg}}(\mathbf{k})\simeq\frac{\rho_{\phi}}{m_{0}^{2}}\,m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}\,\absolutevalue{f_{\sourcemass\testmass}}\frac{\cos(kr - \veck\cdot\vecr+ \arg f_{\sourcemass\testmass})}{r}\quad\quad\text{(Far-Field)}.\end{aligned}

(159)

As we have known in the previous text,
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{k}
is the momentum transfer vector, and it obeys
q=2k\sin(\theta/2)
.
f_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{T}}
represents the effective scattering amplitude, accounting for the finite-size effect of both the test and source masses. It is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{T}}=-\frac{m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}}{4\pi}\times\underbrace{\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}\int_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}\,e^{+i\mathbf{q}\cdot\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}}}_{\text{Finite Test Mass}}\times\underbrace{\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}}d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{S}}\,e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{S}}}}_{\text{Finite Source Mass}}.\end{aligned}

(160)

When the size of the test mass is negligible (
kR_{\mathcal{T}}<1
),
f_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{T}}
in the above formula goes back to the standard quantum mechanical scattering amplitude. Therefore, we have the generic expression of the form factor in the far-field region, which is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{k})=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}\int_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}\,e^{+i\mathbf{q}\cdot\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}}}_{\text{Finite Test Mass}}\times\underbrace{\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}}}d^{3}\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{S}}\,e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{S}}}}_{\text{Finite Source Mass}}\times\cos(kr - \veck\cdot\vecr+ \arg f_{\sourcemass\testmass})\quad\quad\text{(Far-Field)}.\end{aligned}

(161)

From the above formula, we find that the finite-size effects of the source and the test masses can be factorized in the far-field limit. When the source and test masses are both spheres, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{k})=\underbrace{\frac{3(\sin(q R_\testmass)-qR_{\mathcal{T}}\cos(q R_\testmass))}{(qR_{\mathcal{T}})^{3}}}_{\text{Finite Test Mass}}\times\underbrace{\frac{3(\sin(q R_\sourcemass)-qR_{\mathcal{S}}\cos(q R_\sourcemass))}{(qR_{\mathcal{S}})^{3}}}_{\text{Finite Source Mass}}\times\cos(kr - \veck\cdot\vecr)\quad\quad\text{(Far-Field)}.\end{aligned}

(162)

From the above formula, we find that given the conditions that
kR_{\mathcal{S}}>1
and
kR_{\mathcal{T}}>1
and
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathbf{r}
are not parallel, there is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{k})\simeq\underbrace{\frac{3\cos(qR_\testmass)}{(qR_{\mathcal{T}})^{2}}}_{\text{Finite Test Mass}}\times\underbrace{\frac{3\cos(qR_\sourcemass)}{(qR_{\mathcal{S}})^{2}}}_{\text{Finite Source Mass}}\times\cos(kr - \veck\cdot\vecr)\quad\quad\quad\text{(Far-Field $+$ $\mathbf{k}$ and $\mathbf{r}$ Not Parallel)}.\end{aligned}

(163)

Therefore, the form factor has
(kR_{\mathcal{T}})^{-2}\times(kR_{\mathcal{S}})^{-2}
suppression because of the finite size effect if the phase space distribution is monochromatic and
\mathbf{k}
is not parallel to
\mathbf{r}
. When
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathbf{r}
are parallel (
\absolutevalue{\theta}<\min\left[(kR_{\mathcal{T}})^{-1},\,(kR_{\mathcal{S}})^{-1}\right]
), the finite-size suppression in the far-field limit vanishes. More specifically, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{k})\simeq\cos(kr - \veck\cdot\vecr)\quad\quad\quad\text{(Far-Field $+$ $\mathbf{k}$ and $\mathbf{r}$ Parallel)}.\end{aligned}

(164)

We stress that the above discussions are all based upon the assumption that the phase space is monochromatic, i.e.,
f_{\phi}(\mathbf{p})=(2\pi)^{3}n_{\phi}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k})
. When considering the finite-size and finite-phase-space effects jointly, the suppressions from these two effects are not simply multiplicative. Specifically, when performing phase-space integration for point-to-point interactions, the oscillatory features of the background-induced potential are already significantly smoothed out due to the finite width of the phase space. Further integrating over the finite volumes of the source and test masses does not lead to significant additional cancellation. In fact, given the boosted Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution shown in Eq. (
22
), we have numerically verified in Sec.
VI.1
that in the perturbative region satisfying
\absolutevalue{r-R}/R\gtrsim\mathcal{O}(1)
, accounting for only the finite-phase-space suppression is sufficient to obtain a correct result with deviations of order
\mathcal{O}(10\%)
.

In the perturbative region, the finite-size effect becomes important only when
\absolutevalue{r-R}/R<1
. It is worth noting that the analysis in this section cannot be directly applied to this case because the far-field condition is violated. The numerical approach for studying the
\absolutevalue{r-R}/R<1
case can be found in Sec.
VI.1
. In the non-perturbative region where the screening effect becomes important, the finite-size effect is relevant even when
\absolutevalue{r-R}/R\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
. However, the analysis based on the Born approximation is also invalid in that regime, as the perturbative condition breaks down when the screening effect becomes important. The detailed exploration of the non-perturbative region based on the partial wave analysis is shown in Sec.
VI.3
.

Appendix E
Partial Wave Analysis

In this section, we introduce the partial wave analysis used in this paper. Unlike the Born approximation introduced in Appendix
C
, which is applicable only in the perturbative regions (Regions A and B), the partial wave analysis allows us to investigate both perturbative and non-perturbative regions, as outlined in Fig.
1
. In this section, we focus on the uniform solid sphere. The structure of this section is as follows. First, we introduce the decomposition of wave functions, the phase shift of the solid sphere, and the far-field limit of the wave function. Next, we introduce the computations of the cross section and the momentum-transfer cross section through the summation of the components of different partial waves. Finally, we compute the field configuration in the low-momentum limit. We reproduce the field configuration in Sec.
VI.2
, which is derived from the ansatz assuming a spherical symmetry.

E.1
Wave Functions

For the solid sphere with radius
R
, we have the incident wave function

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \psi_{\text{inc}}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k})=\absolutevalue{\psi_0}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}=\absolutevalue{\psi_0}\,\sum^{\infty}_{l=0}(2l+1)\,i^{l}\,j_{l}(kr)\,P_{l}(\cos\theta),\end{aligned}

(165)

and the scattered wave function

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \psi_{\text{sc}}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k})=\absolutevalue{\psi_0}\,\sum^{\infty}_{l=0}(2l+1)\,i^{l}\,A_{l}\,h_{l}(kr)\,P_{l}(\cos\theta),\end{aligned}

(166)

where
\theta
is the reflection angle as shown in Fig.
2
and Fig.
3
.
h_{l}=j_{l}+iy_{l}
are the spherical Hankel functions of the first kind, where
j_{l}
are the spherical Bessel functions, and
y_{l}
are the spherical Neumann functions.
A_{l}
is the amplitude of the scattered wave, which can also be represented as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} A_{l}=\frac{S_{l}-1}{2}=\frac{e^{2i\delta_{l}}-1}{2},\end{aligned}

(167)

where
S_{l}
is the S-matrix for the
l
-th component of the partial wave, and
\delta_{l}
is the corresponding phase shift of the
l
-th partial wave. To understand the physical meaning of the phase shift, we expand
j_{l}
,
y_{l}
, and
h_{l}
in the far-field region as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} j_{l}(kr)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle r\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{\sin{(kr-\frac{l\pi}{2})}}{kr},\quad\quad h_{l}(kr)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle r\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{\exp\left(i(kr-\frac{l\pi}{2})\right)}{ikr},\end{aligned}

(168)

and have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \psi_{\text{out}}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k})&=\psi_{\text{inc}}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k})+\psi_{\text{sc}}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k})\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle r\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\absolutevalue{\psi_0}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)\,i^{l}\,e^{i\delta_{l}}\,\frac{\sin\left(kr-\frac{l\pi}{2}+\delta_{l}\right)}{kr}\,P_{l}(\cos\theta).\end{aligned}

(169)

For the repulsive potential discussed in this paper, the phase shift obeys
\delta_{l}<0
, which means that the radial wave function is pushed outwards. In contrast, for the attractive potential, the phase shift obeys
\delta_{l}>0
, which means that the radial wave function is pulled inwards. We can also expand the scattered wave function Eq. (
166
) in the far-field region and have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{ik}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)\,A_{l}\,P_{l}(\cos\theta)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)\,e^{i\delta_{l}}\,\sin\delta_{l}\,P_{l}(\cos\theta),\end{aligned}

(170)

which is the scattering amplitude shown in Eq. (
15
).

E.2
Phase Shifts of Solid Sphere

From Eq. (
166
) and Eq. (
167
), we know that as long as the phase shifts are determined, the scattered wave function in Eq. (
166
) is determined. Generally speaking, the phase shifts are determined by the potential shape and need to be solved numerically, as shown in Refs.
[
44
,
165
,
181
,
180
]
. However, for the solid sphere potential (as shown in Eq. (
13
)) we are focusing on in this paper, we can determine the phase shift by matching the boundary condition between the wave function inside the sphere and the wave function outside the sphere. For a solid sphere with radius
R
and e, the total wave function in the interior of the sphere is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \psi_{\text{int}}(\mathbf{r};k)=\absolutevalue{\psi_0}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)\,i^{l}\,P_{l}(\cos\theta)B_{l}\,j_{l}(k^{\prime}r),\end{aligned}

(171)

where
k^{\prime}=\sqrt{k^{2}-m_{\text{M}}^{2}}
is the momentum inside the solid sphere. When
m_{\text{M}}>k
, which means the barrier is larger than the incident kinetic energy, we do the Wick rotation
k^{\prime}=i\kappa^{\prime}
, where
\kappa^{\prime}=\sqrt{m_{\text{M}}^{2}-k^{2}}
.
B_{l}
is the amplitude of the interior wave function. By matching the boundary conditions at
r=R
, we find
A_{l}
and
B_{l}
satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} A_{l}=-\frac{kj_{l}(k^{\prime}R)j_{l+1}(kR)-k^{\prime}j_{l}(kR)j_{l+1}(k^{\prime}R)}{kj_{l}(k^{\prime}R)h_{l+1}(kR)-k^{\prime}h_{l}(kR)j_{l+1}(k^{\prime}R)},\quad\quad B_{l}=\frac{kj_{l}(kR)h_{l+1}(kR)-kh_{l}(kR)j_{l+1}(kR)}{kj_{l}(k^{\prime}R)h_{l+1}(kR)-k^{\prime}h_{l}(kR)j_{l+1}(k^{\prime}R)}.\end{aligned}

(172)

Therefore, the phase shift satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \tan\delta_{l}=\frac{A_{l}/i}{A_{l}+1}=\frac{kj_{l}(k^{\prime}R)j_{l+1}(kR)-k^{\prime}j_{l}(kR)j_{l+1}(k^{\prime}R)}{kj_{l}(k^{\prime}R)y_{l+1}(kR)-k^{\prime}y_{l}(kR)j_{l+1}(k^{\prime}R)}.\end{aligned}

(173)

In the strongly coupled region that satisfies
m_{\text{M}}\gg k
, the potential can be treated as the hard sphere. In this situation, we use

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \kappa^{\prime}R\gg 1:\quad j_{l}(k^{\prime}R)\simeq i^{l}\frac{e^{\kappa^{\prime}R}}{2\kappa^{\prime}R},\end{aligned}

(174)

and have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \kappa^{\prime}R\gg 1:\quad\tan\delta_{l}=\frac{kj_{l+1}(kR)-ik^{\prime}j_{l}(kR)}{ky_{l+1}(kR)-ik^{\prime}y_{l}(kR)}\simeq\frac{j_{l}(kR)}{y_{l}(kR)}\quad\quad\quad\text{(Hard Sphere)}.\end{aligned}

(175)

For the low-momentum hard-sphere scattering satisfying
kR<1
, which is related to Region C as shown in Fig.
1
, we have the phase shift for the s-wave component, which is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} kR<1:\quad\delta_{0}\simeq\tan\delta_{0}\simeq-kR\quad\quad\quad\text{(s-Wave)}.\end{aligned}

(176)

For high-momentum scattering satisfying
kR\gtrsim 1
, corresponding to Region D or E, we sum the partial wave components up to
l_{\max}\sim kR
to ensure sufficient resolution for capturing the excited higher-moment components of the partial waves. Particularly, throughout our numerical computation, we adjust the value of
l_{\max}
to balance precision, computational speed, and numerical stability. Specifically, we set
l_{\max}=0
when
kR\leq 10^{-3}
,
l_{\max}=\min([2kR]+3,[kR]+300)
when
kR>10^{-3}
. Although a larger
l_{\max}
provides higher resolution in principle, we avoid selecting an excessively large
l_{\max}
to prevent numerical singularities. To ensure the reliability of our results, we varied the number of partial wave summation terms and confirmed the stability of our numerical outcomes following the above method for choosing
l_{\max}
for the region satisfying
kR\lesssim 10^{4}
.

E.3
Cross Sections

In this subsection, we list the formulas for computing the cross section
\sigma
and the momentum-transfer cross-section
\sigma_{\text{T}}
for the elastic scattering, where the unitarity condition
\absolutevalue{S_l}=1
is satisfied, and the probability current is conserved. To calculate the cross section
\sigma
, we substitute Eq. (
170
) into Eq. (
18
). Then, we utilize the orthogonality relation of the Legendre polynomials, which is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{-1}^{+1}P_{l}(\cos{\theta})P_{l^{\prime}}(\cos{\theta})\,d\cos{\theta}=\frac{2}{2l+1}\delta_{ll^{\prime}}.\end{aligned}

(177)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sigma=\frac{4\pi}{k^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)\absolutevalue{A_l}^{2}=\frac{4\pi}{k^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)\sin^{2}\delta_{l}.\end{aligned}

(178)

Equivalently, we can write
\sigma
in a more symmetric formalism as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sigma=\frac{4\pi}{k^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(l+1)(\absolutevalue{A_l}^{2}+\absolutevalue{A_{l+1}}^{2})=\frac{4\pi}{k^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(l+1)(\sin^{2}\delta_{l}+\sin^{2}\delta_{l+1}).\end{aligned}

(179)

To derive Eq. (
179
) from Eq. (
178
), we use the relation
\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}l\absolutevalue{A_l}^{2}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(l+1)\absolutevalue{A_{l+1}}^{2}
.

To compute the momentum-transfer cross section, we substitute Eq. (
170
) into Eq. (
17
). Because there is a
\cos\theta
term inside the integral in Eq. (
17
), we utilize the equation

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{-1}^{+1}P_{l}(\cos{\theta})P_{l^{\prime}}(\cos{\theta})\cos{\theta}\,d\cos{\theta}=\frac{2\max(l,l^{\prime})}{(l+l^{\prime})(l+l^{\prime}+2)}\delta_{\absolutevalue{l-l'},1},\end{aligned}

(180)

which is derived from Eq. (
177
) by using the Bonnet’s recursion formula

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (2l+1)\,\cos\theta\,P_{l}(\cos\theta)=(l+1)\,P_{l+1}(\cos\theta)+l\,P_{l-1}(\cos\theta).\end{aligned}

(181)

Then we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\text{T}}=\frac{4\pi}{k^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}[(2l+1)\absolutevalue{A_l}^{2}-(l+1)A_{l}^{*}A_{l+1}-(l+1)A_{l}A_{l+1}^{*}].\end{aligned}

(182)

Similarly, we write the momentum-transfer cross section in a more symmetric form as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\text{T}}=\frac{4\pi}{k^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(l+1)(\absolutevalue{A_l}^{2}+\absolutevalue{A_{l+1}}^{2}-A_{l}^{*}A_{l+1}-A_{l}A_{l+1}^{*}).\end{aligned}

(183)

In Eq. (
183
), the first two terms are exactly the same as the terms in Eq. (
179
), which is the symmetric form of the scattering cross section. These terms come from the integral
\int d\Omega\absolutevalue{f(\theta;k)}^{2}
in Eq. (
17
). The last two terms are conjugate mixing between
A_{l}
and
A_{l+1}
. These terms come from the integral
-\int d\Omega\absolutevalue{f(\theta;k)}^{2}\cos\theta
in Eq. (
17
).

Equivalently, we can write the momentum-transfer cross section as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\text{T}}=\frac{\pi}{k^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(l+1)(2-S_{l}^{*}S_{l+1}-S^{*}_{l+1}S_{l}).\end{aligned}

(184)

To derive Eq. (
184
) from Eq. (
183
), we use
\absolutevalue{S_l}^{2}=1
, the unitarity condition for the elastic scattering. Using Eq. (
167
), we can further simplify Eq. (
184
) to be

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\text{T}}=\frac{\pi}{k^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(l+1)\sin^{2}(\delta_{l+1}-\delta_{l}).\end{aligned}

(185)

In the end, we summarize the mathematical formalisms of the scattering cross section and momentum-transfer cross section presented above for elastic scattering because they appear in various literature. Eq. (
179
) and Eq. (
182
) are the scattering cross section and momentum-transfer cross section used in this work. Eq. (
178
) is the scattering cross section appearing in standard literature
[
121
,
147
]
. Eq. (
184
) is the momentum-transfer cross section appearing in Ref.
[
64
]
. Eq. (
185
) is the momentum-transfer cross section appearing in Ref.
[
41
]
.

E.4
s-Wave Approximation

In this section, we reproduce the scalar profile of the spherically symmetric solution as discussed in Sec.
VI.2
by taking the low-momentum limit of the scattered wave. We show that the spherically symmetric ansatz discussed in Refs.
[
138
,
65
,
27
,
102
,
13
]
comes from the s-wave approximation, which motivates us to further include the higher moment into the analysis as we have discussed in Sec.
VI.3
. Expanding the amplitude of the scattered wave and interior wave shown in Eq. (
172
) in the region where
kR<1
, we obtain the amplitude of the s-wave component of the scattered wave as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} A_{0}=-i\frac{m_{\text{M}}R-\tanh(m_\text{M} R)}{m_{\text{M}}R}\,kR-\left(\frac{m_{\text{M}}R-\tanh(\mM R)}{m_{\text{M}}R}\right)^{2}\,(kR)^{2}+\cdots,\end{aligned}

(186)

and the amplitude of the s-wave component of the interior wave as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} B_{0}=\frac{1}{\cosh(m_\text{M} R)}+\frac{-i}{\cosh(m_\text{M} R)}\left(\frac{m_{\text{M}}R-\tanh(\mM R)}{m_{\text{M}}R}\right)(kR)+\cdots.\end{aligned}

(187)

In Eq. (
186
) and Eq. (
187
), “
\cdots
” means the higher order terms in terms of
kR
.

The spherical Hankel and Bessel functions for the s-wave partial wave (
l=0
) are given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} h_{0}(kr)=-\frac{i}{kr}e^{ikr},\quad\quad\quad\quad j_{0}(k^{\prime}r)\simeq\frac{\sinh(\mM r)}{m_{\text{M}}r}.\end{aligned}

(188)

Keeping only the leading-order term in
kR
from Eq. (
186
), the scattered wave is written as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \psi_{\text{sc}}(r)=\absolutevalue{\psi_0}A_{0}\,h_{0}(kr)\simeq-\absolutevalue{\psi_0}\,\,\frac{m_{\text{M}}R-\tanh(\mM R)}{m_{\text{M}}}\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}.\end{aligned}

(189)

The total wave outside the sphere is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \psi_{\text{out}}(r)=\psi_{\text{inc}}(r)+\psi_{\text{sc}}(r)\simeq\absolutevalue{\psi_0}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}-\absolutevalue{\psi_0}\frac{m_{\text{M}}R-\tanh(\mM R)}{m_{\text{M}}}\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}.\end{aligned}

(190)

Keeping only the leading-order term in
kR
from Eq. (
187
), the wave inside the sphere is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \psi_{\text{int}}(r)\simeq\absolutevalue{\psi_0}\,\frac{\sinh(\mM r)}{m_{\text{M}}r\cosh(\mM R)}.\end{aligned}

(191)

In the near-field region where
r/R\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
, the
e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
and
e^{ikr}
terms in Eq. (
190
) are replaced by
1
because
kr\sim kR\lesssim 1
, and Eq. (
190
) comes back to the spherically symmetric solution listed in Eq. (
70
), which is explored in Refs.
[
138
,
65
,
27
,
102
,
13
]
. In the perturbative Region A where
m_{\text{M}}<R^{-1}
, Eq. (
190
) gives the background-induced potential as shown in Eq. (
59
) by substituting Eq. (
190
) into Eq. (
20
), whose analytical formalism is given by Refs.
[
104
,
76
,
77
,
78
,
89
,
35
,
90
,
170
,
14
]
.

In the long-wavelength region which is strongly coupled (
m_{\text{M}}>R^{-1}
,
kR\ll 1
), we substitute Eq. (
190
) into Eq. (
20
), and have the monochromatic background-induced potential given momentum
\mathbf{k}
, which is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} V_{\text{bg}}(\mathbf{k})\simeq-\frac{\rho_{\phi}}{m_{0}^{2}}\frac{(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}})(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}})}{4\pi r}\times\frac{3}{(m_{\text{M}}R)^{2}}\times\cos(kr -\veck\cdot\vecr).\end{aligned}

(192)

Then we do the phase-space average over Eq. (
192
) to take the decoherence effect into account and have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle V_{\text{bg}}\rangle_{\mathbf{k}}\simeq-\frac{\rho_{\phi}}{m_{0}^{2}}\frac{(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{S}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{S}})(m_{\text{M},\mathcal{T}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}})}{4\pi r}\times\frac{3}{(m_{\text{M}}R)^{2}}\times\mathcal{F}_{\text{A}}.\end{aligned}

(193)

In Eq. (
192
) and Eq. (
193
),
3/(m_{\text{M}}R)^{2}
is the form factor of the screening effect of the spherically symmetric ansatz in the hard sphere region, as shown in Eq. (
76
) and Eq. (
78
).
\mathcal{F}_{\text{A}}
is the form factor describing the decoherence effect from the finite-phase-space, as shown in Eq. (
60
) and plotted in Fig.

kr\gtrsim 1
where the finite-phase-space induced decoherence suppression becomes important.

Appendix F
Astrophysical Constraints

In this section, we estimate the energy loss constraint from the supernova, horizontal branch stars, and red giants following Refs.
[
144
,
54
]
. The light
\phi
particle can be on-shell produced in the stellar plasma. It will dissipate the energy of the hot environment if the produced light particle can escape freely. The dominant stellar energy loss contribution for the quadratic scalar-photon interaction comes from the
\gamma\gamma\rightarrow\phi\phi
channel. Other channels, such as
e^{-}e^{+}\rightarrow\phi\phi
,
e^{-}e^{+}\gamma\rightarrow\phi\phi
, and
e\gamma\rightarrow e\phi\phi
, are either suppressed by the one-loop or the multi-body phase space. Here, the emission rate from
\gamma(k_{1})\gamma(k_{2})\rightarrow\phi(p_{1})\phi(p_{2})
is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma_{\gamma\gamma\rightarrow\phi\phi}=\frac{1}{4}\int\frac{{\mathchar 22\mkern-12.0mud}^{3}\mathbf{k}_{1}}{2E_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}}\frac{{\mathchar 22\mkern-12.0mud}^{3}\mathbf{k}_{2}}{2E_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}}\frac{{\mathchar 22\mkern-12.0mud}^{3}\mathbf{p}_{1}}{2E_{\mathbf{p}_{1}}}\frac{{\mathchar 22\mkern-12.0mud}^{3}\mathbf{p}_{2}}{2E_{\mathbf{p}_{2}}}f_{\gamma}(\mathbf{k}_{1})f_{\gamma}(\mathbf{k}_{2})(E_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}+E_{\mathbf{k}_{2}})(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(k_{1}+k_{2}-p_{1}-p_{2})\sum_{\rm d.o.f}\left|\mathcal{M}_{\gamma\gamma\rightarrow\phi\phi}\right|^{2}\;,\end{aligned}

(194)

where the factor of
1/4
accounts for the symmetry factors from the real scalars (
1/2
) and the photon fields (
1/2
).
f_{\gamma}(\mathbf{k})\simeq\exp(-E_\veck/T)
is the phase space density of the photons in the stellar’s thermal bath. The unpolarized amplitude square and the polarization averaged cross section of
\gamma\gamma\rightarrow\phi\phi
is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sum_{\rm d.o.f}\left|\mathcal{M}_{\gamma\gamma\rightarrow\phi\phi}\right|^{2}=\frac{s^{2}}{2\Lambda^{2}_{\gamma}}\;,\quad\sigma_{\gamma\gamma\rightarrow\phi\phi}=\frac{s}{128\pi\Lambda_{\gamma}^{4}}\sqrt{1-\frac{4m_{0}^{2}}{s}}\;,\quad s=(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}.\end{aligned}

(195)

Focusing on the
m_{0}\ll E
region and substituting Eq. (
195
) into Eq. (
194
),
we have the
\phi
’s emission rate, which is written as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma_{\gamma\gamma\rightarrow\phi\phi}\simeq\frac{3}{2\pi^{5}}\frac{T^{9}}{\Lambda_{\gamma}^{4}}.\end{aligned}

(196)

This result is consistent with the result of Ref.
[
54
]
but differs from that of Ref.
[
138
]
. The discrepancy arises because Ref.
[
138
]
oversimplifies the phase space integration when computing the emission rate in Eq. (
194
). We define that
\Gamma_{\gamma\gamma\rightarrow\phi\phi}=\epsilon_{\phi}\,\rho_{\text{core}}
, where
\epsilon_{\phi}
is the rate of energy loss to
\phi
per unit stellar mass, and
\rho_{\text{core}}
is the stellar core density. For SN1987A, we have
\rho_{\text{core},\text{SN}}\simeq 3\times 10^{14}\,\text{g}/\text{cm}^{3}
,
\epsilon_{\phi,\text{SN}}\lesssim 10^{19}\,\text{erg}\,/\text{g}\cdot\text{s}
, and the core temperature
T_{\text{SN}}\simeq 30\,\text{MeV}

[
54
]
. For HB, we have
\rho_{\text{core},\text{HB}}\simeq 10^{4}\,\text{g}/\text{cm}^{3}
,
\epsilon_{\phi,\text{HB}}\lesssim 10\,\text{erg}\,/\text{g}\cdot\text{s}
, and
T_{\text{HB}}\simeq 10^{8}\,\text{K}\simeq 8.6\,\text{keV}

[
54
]
. For RG, we have
\rho_{\text{core},\text{RG}}\simeq 10^{6}\,\text{g}/\text{cm}^{3}
,
\epsilon_{\phi,\text{RG}}\lesssim 10\,\text{erg}\,/\text{g}\cdot\text{s}
, and
T_{\text{RG}}\simeq 10^{8}\,\text{K}\simeq 8.6\,\text{keV}

[
144
]
. Finally, we derive the constraints for
\Lambda_{\gamma}
which are

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{SN1987A}:\,\,\Lambda_{\gamma}\gtrsim 1.8\,\text{TeV},\quad\quad\text{HB}:\,\,\Lambda_{\gamma}\gtrsim 2.5\times 10^{2}\,\text{GeV},\quad\quad\text{RG}:\,\,\Lambda_{\gamma}\gtrsim 80\,\text{GeV}.\end{aligned}

(197)

Given that the constraint from SN1987A is stronger than the one from HB, we utilize the SN1987A constraint as the benchmark astrophysical constraint in the rest of the paper. One should note that
\phi
has scattering with photons and other particles in the hot dense supernova environment. As
\Lambda_{\gamma}
increases, its effective mean free path
\lambda_{\text{free},\phi}
for energy exchanging scattering becomes smaller. When
\lambda_{\text{free},\phi}
becomes smaller than the supernova radius,
\phi
is trapped inside, which effectively reduces the supernova energy loss constraint. We do not show it in the plots of this work for two reasons: 1. The upper boundary of the SN1987A constraint caused by the trapping effect in the
m_{0}\text{-}\Lambda_{\gamma}^{-1}
plane is
\sim 10^{2}
times higher than the lower boundary
[
54
]
, which lies out of the region we are interested in. 2. For HB and RG, the density and temperature of the core are much smaller than SN, which reduces the trapping effects and gives stronger constraints in the strongly-coupled regions.

Appendix G
Alternative Dark Axion UV Models

In this section, we present the KSVZ-like UV-complete model for Eq. (
101
).
For simplicity, we set
x=1/2
in Eq. (
100
) and assume that the dark quarks
q^{\prime}
are invariant under
U(1)_{\text{PQ}}
. In this scenario, we introduce a neutral scalar
\Phi
and a pair of fermions
\Xi_{L,R}
. The charge assignments under
SU(N_{c})\times U(1)^{\prime}\times U(1)_{\text{PQ}}
for the aforementioned particles are given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} u^{\prime}_{L,R}:(N_{c},1,0)\;,\quad d^{\prime}_{L,R}:(N_{c},0,0)\;,\quad\Phi:(1,0,-2)\;,\quad\Xi_{L},\Xi^{\dagger}_{R}:(N_{c},1,1)\end{aligned}

(198)

The corresponding Lagrangian takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} -\mathcal{L}\supset\mathcal{Y}_{\Xi}\Phi\Xi_{L}^{\dagger}\Xi_{R}+\bar{q}_{L}^{\prime}\hat{m}_{q^{\prime}}q^{\prime}_{R}+h.c.+\kappa\left(|\Phi|^{2}-\frac{\mathscr{F}_{a}}{2}\right)^{2}\end{aligned}

(199)

In the ground state, the global
U(1)_{\text{PQ}}

\Phi
, which also generates the mass term in the UV model,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} M_{\Xi}=\frac{{\cal Y}_{\Xi}\mathscr{F}_{a}}{\sqrt{2}}e^{i\frac{a}{\mathscr{F}_{a}}}\;.\end{aligned}

(200)

Since the fermion fields
\Xi

|M_{\Xi}|>\Lambda_{c}
, they can be integrated out. The resulting effective Lagrangian at energies below
|M_{\Xi}|
reads,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} -{\cal L}\supset\bar{q}_{L}^{\prime}\hat{m}_{q^{\prime}}q^{\prime}_{R}+h.c.+\frac{a}{8\pi\mathscr{F}_{a}}\left(\alpha_{s}^{\prime}G^{a\prime}_{\mu\nu}\tilde{G}^{a\prime\mu\nu}+2N_{c}\alpha^{\prime}F_{\mu\nu}^{\prime}\tilde{F}^{\prime\mu\nu}\right)\end{aligned}

(201)

which reduces to Eq. (
101
) with,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \hat{Q}_{A}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{1}\;,\quad E=N_{c}\;,\quad N=\frac{1}{2}\;,\quad\hat{C}_{q}^{0}=0\;.\end{aligned}

(202)
