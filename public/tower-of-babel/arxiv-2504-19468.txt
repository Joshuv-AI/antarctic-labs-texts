Characteristic polynomials and some combinatorics for finite Coxeter groups
Shoumin Liu, Yuxiang Wang

Abstract
Let W W be a finite Coxeter group with Coxeter generating set S = { s 1 , … , s n } S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\} , and ρ \rho be a complex finite dimensional representation of W W . The characteristic polynomial of ρ \rho is defined as d ⁡ ( S , ρ ) = det [ x 0 ​ I + x 1 ​ ρ ​ ( s 1 ) + ⋯ + x n ​ ρ ​ ( s n ) ] , d(S,\rho)=\det[x_{0}I+x_{1}\rho(s_{1})+\cdots+x_{n}\rho(s_{n})], where I I is the identity operator. In this paper, we show the existence of a combinatorics structure within W W , and thereby prove that for any two complex finite dimensional representations ρ 1 \rho_{1} and ρ 2 \rho_{2} of W W , d ⁡ ( S , ρ 1 ) = d ⁡ ( S , ρ 2 ) d(S,\rho_{1})=d(S,\rho_{2}) if and only if ρ 1 ≅ ρ 2 \rho_{1}\cong\rho_{2} .

Introduction

Suppose
A_{0},\ldots,A_{n}
are bounded linear operators on a Hilbert space
V
. Their
projective joint spectrum
, introduced by R. Yang in
[
25
]
, is the set

\sigma(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})=\{[x_{1}:\cdots:x_{n}]\in\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n}:x_{1}A_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}A_{n}\ {\rm is\ not\ invertible}\}.

Projective spectrum has played an important role in the study of functional analysis, representation theory and spectral dynamical systems. Fruitful results have been obtained in
[
1
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
.

When V is finite dimensional,
\sigma(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
is determined by the multivariable homogeneous characteristic polynomial

f_{A}(x)=\det[x_{1}A_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}A_{n}].

In recent years, the spectrum
\sigma(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
and the polynomial
f_{A}(x)
were intensely investigated in representation theory of groups and Lie algebras, see
[
2
,
9
,
16
,
24
]
. Given a finite group
G
with a generating set
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
, let
{\mathcal{R}}(G)
denote the set of all finite dimensional complex linear representations of
G
. For any
\rho\in{\mathcal{R}}(G)
,
\rho:G\rightarrow GL(V)
, the projective joint spectrum of
\rho
is defined as

D(S,\rho)=\sigma(I,\rho(s_{1}),\ldots,\rho(s_{n})),

where
I
is the identity operator on
V
. The characteristic polynomial of
\rho
is

d(S,\rho)=\det[x_{0}I+x_{1}\rho(s_{1})+\cdots+x_{n}\rho(s_{n})].

Let
\hat{G}=G\setminus\{1\}
, where 1 is the identity element of
G
. Let
\lambda_{G}
denote the left regular representation of
G
, the polynomial
d(\hat{G},\lambda_{G})
was studied by Dedekind and Frobenius in the early research of representation theory. Let
{\it Irr}(G)
be the set of equivalence classes of irreducible representations of
G
, and
{\it deg}\,\rho
be the degree of
\rho
. In 1896 Frobenius proved that

d(\hat{G},\lambda_{G})=\prod_{\rho\in{\it Irr}(G)}(d(\hat{G},\rho))^{{\it deg}\,\rho}.

Besides,
d(\hat{G},\rho)
is an irreducible polynomial for each
\rho\in{\it Irr}(G)
. The polynomial
d(\hat{G},\lambda_{G})
was later called the
group determinant
of
G
. For more information about it, we refer the reader to
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
17
]
. Although so many good properties hold for the group determinant and the general
d(\hat{G},\rho)
, as the order of
G
increases, the calculation and application of the polynomials become essentially inaccessible. So one tends to take a small enough generating set
S
and expects that
D(S,\rho)
and
d(S,\rho)
could retain all the salient features of
D(\hat{G},\rho)
and
d(\hat{G},\rho)
.

Basically, given a finite group
G
and a generating set
S
, one wonders whether the following properties hold:

(a)

D(S,\lambda_{G})
determines
G
.

(b)

D(S,\rho_{1})=D(S,\rho_{2})
as subschemes of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n}
implies
\rho_{1}\cong\rho_{2}
.

(c)

D(S,\rho)
is reduced and irreducible whenever
\rho
is finite dimensional and irreducible.

In general, the answers are negative. Counterexamples can be found in
[
18
,
19
,
21
]
.

However, when considering
G
as a Coxeter group and
S
as a Coxeter generating set of
G
, some positive answer were obtained in recent studies. In
[
2
]
, authors show that, considering
D(S,\rho)
as algebraic subschemes of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n}
, (a) holds within all Coxeter groups, (b) holds for any finite Coxeter groups of classical types. Later the paper
[
20
]
shows a generalization of (a). Suppose
G
is taken from all Coxeter groups without infinite bonds in their Coxeter diagrams, and
D(S,\rho)
is considered as a set, the results in
[
20
]
indicates that
D(S,\rho)
determines
G
for any faithful representation
\rho
of
G
. In
[
2
,
22
]
, positive answers to some generalizations of (b) for certain infinite Coxeter groups are obtained.

In this paper, we first present a sufficient condition for (b), which is the existence of a combinatorics structure in
G
. Then we show this structure exists for any finite Coxeter groups. Finally we show (b) holds for any finite Coxeter groups.

It is easily seen that (b) is equivalent to the property (
{\rm b}^{\prime}
):
d(S,\rho_{1})=d(S,\rho_{2})
implies
\rho_{1}\cong\rho_{2}
. So we mainly use statement (
{\rm b}^{\prime}
) so as to study the relation between polynomials and representations. Before we present the main theorem of this paper, let’s define some notations. Let

M(S)\coloneqq\{t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{m}:t_{i}\in S,m\in\mathbb{N}\},

which is a free monoid generated by alphabet
S
. Let a word
w\in M(S)
, the
signature
of
w
is
{\rm sig}(w)\coloneqq(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
where
a_{i}
is the number of times that
s_{i}
appears in
w
. There is a natural homomorphism

\phi:M(S)\rightarrow G

where
\phi(s_{i})=s_{i}
for each
i
. We use
[n]
to denote the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

As the main result of this paper, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
W
be a finite Coxeter group with
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
as its Coxeter generating set.

(a)

There is an order of all conjugacy classes of
W
:
C_{1},\ldots,C_{r}
and a sequence
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle\{g=\phi(w):{\rm sig}(w)=\alpha_{i}\}\cap C_{i}\neq\varnothing\quad\text{for}\ i\in[r],

\displaystyle\{g=\phi(w):{\rm sig}(w)=\alpha_{i}\}\cap C_{j}=\varnothing\quad\text{for}\ i,j\in[r]\ \text{and}\ j>i.

(b)

For two finite dimensional complex linear representations
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
for
W
,
d(S,\rho_{1})=d(S,\rho_{2})
if and only if
\rho_{1}\cong\rho_{2}
.

In this paper, such a sequence
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r}
in (a) is called an
independent signature sequence
(ISS) with a lower triangular
independent signature matrix
(ISM). Basically, the main idea of the proof is to show the existence of such sequence. In
[
2
]
, authors provided a key theorem
[
2
, Theorem 7.5]
, cited as Theorem
3.1
in this paper, which provides a relation between the characters of the representations and combinatorics of words for a finitely generated group
G
. Based on this, it can be proven that if
G
has such a sequence, then for any
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{R}(G)
, the equation
d(S,\rho_{1})=d(S,\rho_{2})
implies
\chi_{\rho_{1}}=\chi_{\rho_{2}}
where
\chi
denotes the character, see Proposition
3.4
. Besides, we prove that if each of
G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}
has such a sequence, then so does
G_{1}\times\cdots\times G_{k}
, see Corollary
3.9
. Thus the crucial step is to show that each irreducible finite Coxeter group has an independent signature sequence with a lower triangular independent signature matrix, which is one of the core components of this paper.

The structure of the paper is sketched as follows. In Section 2, we recall some necessary conceptions and basic results about the Coxeter system. In Section 3, we define independent signature sequence and present some properties about it. In Section 4 and 5, we show that each irreducible finite Coxeter group has an independent signature sequence with a lower triangular independent signature matrix. For classical types, our proof is inspired by the proof in
[
2
, Section 7]
where the authors proved that for types
{\rm A,B\ \text{and}\ D}
,
d(S,\rho_{1})=d(S,\rho_{2})
if and only if
\rho_{1}\cong\rho_{2}
. We refine its proof and obtain the existence of an independent signature sequence with a lower triangular independent signature matrix for each classical type. For exceptional types, we start with their conjugacy classes and reduce the problem to a theorem about cuspidal classes (a special type of conjugacy classes), namely Theorem
5.6
. Cuspidal classes of exceptional types were listed by M. Geck and G. Pfeiffer in
[
8
, Appendix B]
. The proof of Theorem
5.6
is presented in Section 6 and is largely based on the results obtained by running the algorithm we developed in MATLAB. In Section 7, we complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
, establish a semigroup isomorphism between representations and their characteristic polynomials as a corollary, and provide two examples for
W({\rm H_{3}})
and
W({\rm A_{n}})
.

2
The Coxeter system

We first recall some basic results about the Coxeter system, which
can be found in
[
15
]
or any other books about it.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
M=(m_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
be a symmetric
n\times n
matrix with entries from
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
such that
m_{ii}=1
for all
i\in[n]
and
m_{ij}>1
whenever
i\neq j
. The
Coxeter group
of type
M
is the group

W(M)=\langle\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}\ |\ \{(s_{i}s_{j})^{m_{ij}}=1,\ i,j\in[n],m_{ij}<\infty\}\rangle.

S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
is called the
Coxeter generating set
. If no confusion is imminent, we write
W
instead of
W(M)
. The pair
(W,S)
is called the
Coxeter system
of type
M
.

Fix a Coxeter matrix
M=(m_{ij})_{i,j\in[n]}
. Let
V
be a real vector space with basis
(e_{i})_{i\in[n]}
. Denote by
B_{M}
, or just
B
if
M
is clear from the context, the symmetric bilinear form on
V
determined by

B(e_{i},e_{j})=-2\cos(\pi/m_{ij}).

The linear transformations
\sigma_{i}
(
i\in[n]
) is given by

\sigma_{i}(x)=x-B(x,e_{i})e_{i}\quad(x\in V).

Theorem 2.2

(
[
15
, Section 5.3, 5.4]
)
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system.

(a)

The mapping
w\mapsto\sigma_{w}
given by
\sigma_{w}=\sigma_{r_{1}}\cdots\sigma_{r_{q}}
whenever
w=s_{r_{1}}\cdots s_{r_{q}}\\
\in W
with
r_{j}\in[n]
(
j\in[q]
) defines a linear representation of
W
on
V
, which is called the reflection representation (or the geometric representation) of
W
, denoted by
\rho_{\it ref}
.

(b)

The reflection representation of
W
is faithful.

Definition 2.3
.

The Coxeter matrix
M=(m_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
is often described by a labelled graph
\Gamma(M)
whose vertex set is
[n]
and in which two nodes
i
and
j
are joined by an edge labelled
m_{ij}
if
m_{ij}>2
. If
m_{ij}=3
, then the label 3 of of the edge
\{i,j\}
is often omitted. If
m_{ij}=4
, then instead of the label 4 at the edge
\{i,j\}
one often draws a double bond. This labelled graph is called the
Coxeter diagram
of
M
. We say that the Coxeter system
(W,S)
is irreducible if the Coxeter diagram
\Gamma
is connected.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system,
J\subseteq S
be any subset and consider the subgroup
W_{J}\coloneqq\langle J\rangle\leqslant W
. Then
W_{J}
is called a parabolic subgroup of
W
. We call
W_{J}
a proper parabolic subgroup if
J\neq S
.

Proposition 2.5

(
[
15
, Section 2.2]
)
.

Let Coxeter system
(W,S)
have Coxeter diagram
\Gamma
, with connected components
\Gamma_{1},\ldots,\Gamma_{r}
, and let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{r}
be the corresponding subsets of
S
. Then
W
is the direct product of the parabolic subgroups
W_{S_{1}},\ldots,W_{S_{r}}
, and each Coxeter system
(W_{S_{i}},S_{i})
is irreducible.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
15
, Section 2.4, 2.7]
)
.

An irreducible Coxeter group is finite if and only if its Coxeter diagram occurs in Table
1
(see Section 4) or Table
2
(see Section 5), where the types in the former are called classical types while the types in the latter are called exceptional types.

Corollary 2.7
.

A Coxeter group
G
is finite if and only if
G\cong W(M_{1})\times W(M_{2})\times\cdots\times W(M_{k})
where each
M_{i}
occurs in Table
1
or Table
2
.

3
Independent signature sequences and their properties

Suppose
G
is a finitely generated group with a generating set
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,\\
s_{n}\}
. The unit element of
M(S)
is denoted by
\varepsilon
. Given a word
w\in M(S)
, the length of
w
is the number of letters in
w
, denoted by
|w|
. Given a signature
\alpha=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, the length of
\alpha
is
|\alpha|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
.

For the finite dimensional complex linear representations of
G
, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
2
, Theorem 7.5]
)
.

Let
G
be any finitely generated group with generating set
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
, and
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in{\mathcal{R}}(G)
. If
d(S,\rho_{1})=d(S,\rho_{2})
, then for each given
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we have

\sum_{{\rm sig}(w)=\alpha}\chi_{\rho_{1}}(w)=\sum_{{\rm sig}(w)=\alpha}\chi_{\rho_{2}}(w)

(3.1)

where
\chi_{\rho_{i}}
denotes the character of
\rho_{i}
.

Equation (
3.1
) contains a lot of information for relation between two given representations. Take
\alpha={\rm sig}(s_{i})
, it follows that
\chi_{\rho_{1}}(s_{i})=\chi_{\rho_{2}}(s_{i})
; take
\alpha={\rm sig}(s_{i}s_{j})
, it follows that
\chi_{\rho_{1}}(s_{i}s_{j})=\chi_{\rho_{2}}(s_{i}s_{j})
. We aim to show that the equality
\chi_{\rho_{1}}(w)=\chi_{\rho_{2}}(w)
holds for any word
w
, but this is challenging to establish directly. Therefore, we develop a method to obtain the information from equation (
3.1
).

Definition 3.2
.

Let
G
be any finitely generated group with finite number of conjugacy classes
C_{1},\ldots,C_{r}
, and
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
be the generating set. Given a signature
\alpha=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
,
E_{\alpha}\coloneqq\{u\in M(S):{\rm sig}(u)=\alpha\}
. The
signature vector
of
\alpha
is

V_{\alpha}=(|E_{\alpha}\cap\phi^{-1}(C_{1})|,\ldots,|E_{\alpha}\cap\phi^{-1}(C_{r})|).

Let
\mathcal{C}_{i}=(\chi_{\rho_{i}}(C_{1}),\ldots,\chi_{\rho_{i}}(C_{r}))^{\rm T}
for
i=1,2
where
\chi_{\rho_{i}}(C_{j})\coloneqq\chi_{\rho_{i}}(g)
for an arbitrary
g\in C_{j}
, then we can express equation (
3.1
) as

V_{\alpha}\mathcal{C}_{1}=V_{\alpha}\mathcal{C}_{2}.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
G,S
and
C_{i}
be as in Definition
3.2
. A sequence
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\\
\alpha_{r}
of signatures is called an
independent signature sequence
(abbreviated as ISS) if
V_{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,V_{\alpha_{r}}
is linearly independent. Suppose
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r}
is an ISS, the corresponding
independent signature matrix
(abbreviated as ISM) is the matrix

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}V_{\alpha_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
V_{\alpha_{r}}\\
\end{array}\right).

When we talk about ISM, there are usually a clear ISS and a clear order of conjugacy classes in the context.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
G,S
and
C_{i}
be as in Definition
3.2
. If
G
has an
ISS
, then for any
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in{\mathcal{R}}(G)
,
d(S,\rho_{1})=d(S,\rho_{2})
implies
\chi_{\rho_{1}}(g)=\chi_{\rho_{2}}(g)
for each
g\in G
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r}
be an ISS of
G
. By Theorem
3.1
,
V_{\alpha}\mathcal{C}_{1}=V_{\alpha}\mathcal{C}_{2}
for any signature
\alpha
. Therefore

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}V_{\alpha_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
V_{\alpha_{r}}\\
\end{array}\right)\mathcal{C}_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}V_{\alpha_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
V_{\alpha_{r}}\\
\end{array}\right)\mathcal{C}_{2}.

Since the ISM is invertible, we have
\mathcal{C}_{1}=\mathcal{C}_{2}
, i.e.
\chi_{\rho_{1}}(C_{i})=\chi_{\rho_{2}}(C_{i})
for each
i\in[r]
. Thus
\chi_{\rho_{1}}(g)=\chi_{\rho_{2}}(g)
for each
g\in G
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Suppose
G
is a finite group. It is well known that for
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in{\mathcal{R}}(G)
,
\rho_{1}\cong\rho_{2}
if and only if
\chi_{\rho_{1}}(g)=\chi_{\rho_{2}}(g)
for each
g\in G
. Therefore, if
G
has an ISS, then
d(S,\rho_{1})=d(S,\rho_{2})
implies
\rho_{1}\cong\rho_{2}
.

The rest of this section focuses on the signatures for the direct product of groups. The following lemma is an elementary conclusion in group theory.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
G=G_{1}\times G_{2}
where
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are any groups. Let
\{C_{1,\alpha}:\alpha\in\Omega_{1}\}
and
\{C_{2,\beta}:\beta\in\Omega_{2}\}
be the sets of all conjugacy classes of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, respectively. Then
\{C_{1,\alpha}\times C_{2,\beta}:(\alpha,\beta)\in\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}\}
is the set of all conjugacy classes of
G
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
G=G_{1}\times G_{2}
, where
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are finitely generated groups. Generating sets of
G_{1},G_{2}
and
G
are
S,T
and
S\sqcup T
, respectively. Given
w=r_{1}\cdots r_{n}\in M(S\sqcup T)
and
r_{i}\in S\sqcup T
, let

r_{i}^{(1)}=\begin{cases}r_{i}&r_{i}\in S\\
\varepsilon&r_{i}\in T\end{cases},\quad r_{i}^{(2)}=\begin{cases}\varepsilon&r_{i}\in S\\
r_{i}&r_{i}\in T\end{cases}.

Let
w^{(1)}=r_{1}^{(1)}r_{2}^{(1)}\cdots r_{n}^{(1)}
,
w^{(2)}=r_{1}^{(2)}r_{2}^{(2)}\cdots r_{n}^{(2)}
, and
f
denote the map
w\mapsto w^{(1)}w^{(2)}
, then
|f^{-1}(w_{1}w_{2})|=|w_{1}w_{2}|!/(|w_{1}|!|w_{2}|!)
for any
w_{1}\in M(S)
,
w_{2}\in M(T)
.

Proof.

Since
|f^{-1}(w_{1}w_{2})|
is equal to the number of all the methods for selecting
|w_{1}|
positions from
|w_{1}w_{2}|
different positions. we have
|f^{-1}(w_{1}w_{2})|=|w_{1}w_{2}|!/(|w_{1}|!|w_{2}|!)
.
∎

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
G=G_{1}\times G_{2}
, where
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are any finitely generated groups with finite number of conjugacy classes. Generating sets of
G_{1},G_{2}
and
G
are
S,T
and
S\sqcup T
respectively.

(a)

If both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
have an
ISS
, then
G
has an
ISS
.

(b)

If both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
have a lower triangular
ISM
, then
G
has a lower triangular
ISM
.

Proof.

Let
C_{i,1},\ldots,C_{i,r_{i}}
be all conjugacy classes of
G_{i}
for
i=1,2
. Suppose
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r_{1}}
is an ISS of
G_{1}
;
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{r_{2}}
is an ISS of
G_{2}
. Let
\alpha_{i}||\beta_{j}
denote the signature spliced by
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{j}
, for example,
(1,2)||(3,4)\coloneqq(1,2,3,4)
. Consider signature sequence

\alpha_{1}||\beta_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{1}||\beta_{r_{2}},\ldots,\alpha_{r_{1}}||\beta_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r_{1}}||\beta_{r_{2}}.

(3.2)

We will prove that it is an ISS of
G
. For each
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{j}
, we have

E_{\alpha_{i}||\beta_{j}}=\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{1}\in E_{\alpha_{i}}\\
u_{2}\in E_{\beta_{j}}\end{subarray}}f^{-1}(u_{1}u_{2})

where
f
is as defined in Lemma
3.7
. Let
V_{\alpha_{i}}=(x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,r_{1}})
and
V_{\beta_{j}}=(y_{j,1},\ldots,y_{j,r_{2}})
. For each conjugacy class
C_{1,k}\times C_{2,l}
, it follows that

\displaystyle|E_{\alpha_{i}||\beta_{j}}\cap\phi^{-1}(C_{1,k}\times C_{2,l})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{1}\in E_{\alpha_{i}}\\
u_{2}\in E_{\beta_{j}}\end{subarray}}(f^{-1}(u_{1}u_{2})\cap\phi^{-1}(C_{1,k}\times C_{2,l}))|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{1}\in E_{\alpha_{i}}\\
u_{2}\in E_{\beta_{j}}\end{subarray}}|f^{-1}(u_{1}u_{2})\cap\phi^{-1}(C_{1,k}\times C_{2,l})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{1}\in E_{\alpha_{i}}\cap\phi^{-1}(C_{1,k})\\
u_{2}\in E_{\beta_{j}}\cap\phi^{-1}(C_{2,l})\end{subarray}}|f^{-1}(u_{1}u_{2})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{1}\in E_{\alpha_{i}}\cap\phi^{-1}(C_{1,k})\\
u_{2}\in E_{\beta_{j}}\cap\phi^{-1}(C_{2,l})\end{subarray}}\frac{(|\alpha_{i}|+|\beta_{j}|)!}{|\alpha_{i}|!|\beta_{j}|!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|E_{\alpha_{i}}\cap\phi^{-1}(C_{1,k})|\cdot|E_{\beta_{j}}\cap\phi^{-1}(C_{2,l})|\frac{(|\alpha_{i}|+|\beta_{j}|)!}{|\alpha_{i}|!|\beta_{j}|!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(|\alpha_{i}|+|\beta_{j}|)!}{|\alpha_{i}|!|\beta_{j}|!}x_{i,k}y_{j,l}.

Arrange all the conjugacy classes in
G
as

C_{1,1}\times C_{2,1},\ldots,C_{1,1}\times C_{2,r_{2}},\ldots,C_{1,r_{1}}\times C_{2,1},\ldots,C_{1,r_{1}}\times C_{2,r_{2}}.

Then we have

V_{\alpha_{i}||\beta_{j}}=\frac{(|\alpha_{i}|+|\beta_{j}|)!}{|\alpha_{i}|!|\beta_{j}|!}(x_{i,1}y_{j,1},\ldots,x_{i,1}y_{j,r_{2}},\ldots,x_{i,r_{1}}y_{j,1},\ldots,x_{i,r_{1}}y_{j,r_{2}}).

Let
M_{i,j}\coloneqq(|\alpha_{i}|+|\beta_{j}|)!/(|\alpha_{i}|!|\beta_{j}|!)
, we have

\begin{gathered}\begin{pmatrix}V_{\alpha_{1}||\beta_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
V_{\alpha_{1}||\beta_{r_{2}}}\\
\vdots\\
V_{\alpha_{r_{1}}||\beta_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
V_{\alpha_{r_{1}}||\beta_{r_{2}}}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}M_{1,1}&~&~&~&~&~&~\\
~&\ddots&~&~&~&~&~\\
~&~&M_{1,r_{2}}&~&~&~&~\\
~&~&~&\ddots&~&~&~\\
~&~&~&~&M_{r_{1},1}&~&~\\
~&~&~&~&~&\ddots&~\\
~&~&~&~&~&~&M_{r_{1},r_{2}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\\
\begin{pmatrix}V_{\alpha_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
V_{\alpha_{r_{1}}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\bigotimes\begin{pmatrix}V_{\beta_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
V_{\beta_{r_{2}}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.\end{gathered}

(3.3)

Then

{\rm det}\begin{pmatrix}V_{\alpha_{1}||\beta_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
V_{\alpha_{r_{1}}||\beta_{r_{2}}}\\
\end{pmatrix}=(\prod_{i=1}^{r_{1}}\prod_{j=1}^{r_{2}}M_{i,j}){\rm det}^{r_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}V_{\alpha_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
V_{\alpha_{r_{1}}}\\
\end{pmatrix}{\rm det}^{r_{1}}\begin{pmatrix}V_{\beta_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
V_{\beta_{r_{2}}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\neq 0.

Hence sequence (
3.2
) is an ISS of
G
.

If the corresponding ISMs of
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r_{1}}
and
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{r_{2}}
are both lower triangular matrices, then the corresponding ISM of (
3.2
) is also a lower triangular matrix by equation (

).
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
W
be a finite Coxeter group of type
{\rm A_{n}}
,
{\rm B_{n}}
or
{\rm D_{n+1}}

Proof.

Let
C_{1},\ldots,C_{r}

W

. Let

Since

Let

For each

and

. Since

W
. Besides, with proper reordering of the conjugacy classes

\alpha_{p_{1}},\ldots,\alpha_{p_{r}}

The proof of Theorem
1.1
and examples

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By Theorem
4.2
,
4.4
and
5.7
, any finite irreducible Coxeter group has an ISS with a lower triangular ISM. Thus any finite Coxeter group has an ISS with a lower triangular ISM by Corollary
3.9
.

Let
W
be a finite Coxeter group,
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
are two finite dimensional complex linear representations of
W
. It is obvious that
\rho_{1}\cong\rho_{2}
implies
d(S,\rho_{1})=d(S,\rho_{2})
. Conversely, by Proposition
3.4
,
d(S,\rho_{1})=d(S,\rho_{2})
implies
\rho_{1}\cong\rho_{2}
.
∎

Suppose
(W,S)
is a finite Coxeter system. Recall that
{\mathcal{R}}(W)
is the set of all finite dimensional complex linear representations of
W
. Let

\mathscr{D}(W,S)\coloneqq\{d(S,\rho):\rho\in{\mathcal{R}}(W)\}.

\mathscr{D}(W,S)
is a semigroup with respect to polynomials multiplication. Let
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(W)
denote the semigroup of equivalence classes of
{\mathcal{R}}(W)
, with the semigroup operation defined by the direct sum of representations. By Theorem
1.1
and the equation

d(S,\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}\rho_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{k}d(S,\rho_{i}),

the following corollary holds.

Corollary 7.1
.

Let
(W,S)
be a finite Coxeter system. Let
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{r}
be a complete list of irreducible representations of
W
.

(a)

For
\rho\in\mathcal{R}(W)
,
\rho\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}k_{i}\rho_{i}
if and only if
d(S,\rho)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}d^{k_{i}}(S,\rho_{i})
.

(b)

There is a semigroup isomorphism

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(W)\cong\mathscr{D}(W,S).

In particular,
\mathscr{D}(W,S)=\langle d(S,\rho_{1}),\ldots,d(S,\rho_{r})\rangle
is a free commutative semigroup.

Remark 7.2
.

Let

\tilde{d}(S,\rho)\coloneqq\det[I+x_{1}\rho(s_{1})+\cdots+x_{n}\rho(s_{n})]

and

\widetilde{\mathscr{D}}(W,S)\coloneqq\{\tilde{d}(S,\rho):\rho\in{\mathcal{R}}(W)\}.

Replacing
d
and
\mathscr{D}
by
\tilde{d}
and
\widetilde{\mathscr{D}}
, Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
7.1
still hold.

\displaystyle d(T_{3},\rho^{(3,1)}\big\downarrow_{S_{3}}^{S_{4}})

Remark 7.5
.

In this paper, by studying the existence of the independent signature sequence, we generalize the main result of
[
2
]
to any finite Coxeter groups. A semigroup isomorphism between representations and their characteristic polynomials for finite Coxeter groups is established. We conjecture such sequence may exist for a larger category of finitely generated groups so that the result can be further generalized. On the other hand, there are many interesting topics about these polynomials, such as whether the characteristic polynomial for an irreducible representation is irreducible, how to calculate the characters from the polynomials directly. Therefore, we need more efforts to work on these topics.
