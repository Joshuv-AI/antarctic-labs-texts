Abstract
This paper addresses the fundamental performance limits of Integrated Sensing and Communication (ISAC) systems by introducing a novel converse bound based on rate-distortion theory. This rate-distortion bound (RDB) overcomes the restrictive regularity conditions of classical estimation theory, such as the Bayesian Cramér-Rao Bound (BCRB). The proposed framework is broadly applicable, holding for arbitrary parameter distributions and distortion measures, including mean-squared error and probability of error. The bound is proved to be tight in the high sensing noise regime and can be strictly tighter than the BCRB in the low sensing noise regime. The RDB’s utility is demonstrated on two challenging scenarios: Nakagami fading channel estimation, where it provides a valid bound even when the BCRB is inapplicable, and a binary occupancy detection task, showcasing its versatility for discrete sensing problems. This work provides a powerful and general tool for characterizing the ultimate performance tradeoffs in ISAC systems.

Introduction

Integrated Sensing and Communications (ISAC)—the joint design of wireless systems for both sensing and data transmission—has become a key technology for 6G networks. By sharing hardware and spectrum, ISAC improves energy, spectral, and hardware efficiency
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. These gains support simultaneous sensing and communication in automotive (e.g., 6G V2X systems
[
4
]
), industrial (e.g., smart manufacturing), and urban (e.g., smart cities, transportation, homes
[
5
]
) environments. Recent surveys highlight a growing range of system designs and applications, underscoring the need for principled performance limits
[
6
]
.

From the sensing perspective, the goal is to estimate channel parameters from the transmitted waveform; from the communication side, the channel is assumed known and the aim is to design signals that maximize information rate. These differing objectives lead to distinct metrics and design criteria. Rigorous analysis thus requires combining information- and estimation-theoretic tools to characterize meaningful tradeoffs in ISAC systems
[
1
]
.

I-A

Prior Work

The approach in
[
1
]
uses a version of the Bayesian Cramér–Rao bound (BCRB)
[
7
]
. However, estimation theory offers several other bounds with distinct advantages, including the Weiss–Weinstein family
[
8
]
(which generalizes the Cramér–Rao bound), the Ziv–Zakai family
[
9
,
10
]
, bounds from functional inequalities (e.g., log-Sobolev)
[
11
,
12
]
, and those based on variational divergence representations
[
13
]
.

In this work, we rely on a bound that is well known in the information-theoretic literature but has received limited attention in estimation theory. This approach lower-bounds the Bayesian risk via a rate-distortion function and, to the best of our knowledge, was first introduced in
[
7
]
.

There are several advantages to this approach. First, it applies to any well-defined loss function, which allows us to study ISAC problems beyond mean-squared error, including error criteria such as probability of error. Second, unlike the BCRB, it does not require regularity conditions on the prior distribution, enabling us to derive true converses on performance on a wide range of problems. Third, the bound is expressed in terms of the rate-distortion function, which admits closed-form expressions in many case of practical interest. When such expressions are unavailable, the bound can be further relaxed using the Shannon lower bound, which is known to be tight in the low-distortion regime
[
14
,
15
]
. Finally, as we will show, unlike the BCRB, typically tight only in the low sensing noise regime, the rate-distortion-based bound can be tight in both high- and low-noise regimes.

Definition 2

((Distortion or Estimation Error))

Fix a distortion function

\displaystyle R\leq\frac{1}{T}{I}\left(\left.\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y}_{\rm c}\right|\mathbf{H}_{\rm c}\right),\qquad D\geq D(P_{\mathbf{X}},P_{\mathbf{Y}_{s}|\mathbf{X}}).

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\triangleq\overline{\rm conv}\bigcup_{P_{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathcal{F}}\left\{(D,R):\text{\rm the pair is achievable}\right\}.

Generic Converse

In this section, we present the
rate-distortion bound
(RDB), a generic lower bound on the ISAC model of Sec.

III-A

Rate-Distortion Preliminaries

Definition 5

((Rate-distortion function))

For
D>0
, the rate-distortion function is defined as

\displaystyle R_{\mathsf{X},d}(D)=\inf_{P_{\hat{\mathsf{X}}\mid\mathsf{X}}:\mathbb{E}\left[d(\mathsf{X},\hat{\mathsf{X}})\right]\leq D}I(\mathsf{X};\hat{\mathsf{X}}).

(8)

Next, we list properties of the rate-distortion function that would be useful in this work (an interested reader is referred to
[
16
]
for a comprehensive review):

•

D\mapsto R_{\mathsf{X},{d}}(D)
is non-decreasing, with inverse function

R_{\mathsf{X},{d}}^{-1}(r)=\inf\{D:R_{\mathsf{X},{d}}(D)\leq r\};

(9)

•

r\mapsto R_{\mathsf{X},{d}}^{-1}(r)
is convex;

•

Low Rate-Behavior:

R_{\mathsf{X},{d}}^{-1}(0^{+})=\inf_{c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbb{E}[d(\mathsf{X},c)];

(10)

•

Dual representation of the rate-distortion function:

R_{\mathsf{X},{d}}(D)=\sup_{\lambda\geq 0,\,P_{\hat{\mathsf{X}}}}\left(-\lambda D-\int\log\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda d(\mathsf{X},\hat{\mathsf{X}})}\right]\right)\mathrm{d}P_{\mathsf{X}}(\mathsf{x})\right),

(11)

where the optimization is taken over the reconstruction distributions
P_{\hat{\mathsf{X}}}
such that

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda d(\mathsf{x},\hat{\mathsf{x}})}}{{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda d(\mathsf{x},\hat{\mathsf{X}})}\right]}\mathrm{d}P_{\mathsf{X}}(\mathsf{x})

\displaystyle=1\qquad\hat{\mathsf{x}}\in{\mathsf{supp}}(P_{\hat{\mathsf{X}}}),

(12)

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda d(\mathsf{x},\hat{\mathsf{x}})}}{{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda d(\mathsf{x},\hat{\mathsf{X}})}\right]}\mathrm{d}P_{\mathsf{X}}(\mathsf{x})

\displaystyle\leq 1\qquad\hat{\mathsf{x}}\not\in{\mathsf{supp}}(P_{\hat{\mathsf{X}}}).

(13)

The next theorem states a general lower bound on the estimation error. While the theorem is well-known in information theory
[
17
]
, it has received limited application in communication or signal processing.

Theorem 1

Let
\mathsf{V}\in\mathcal{V}
and
\mathsf{W}\in\mathcal{W}
. Then, for any
g:\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[d(\mathsf{V},{g(}\mathsf{W}))\right]\geq R_{\mathsf{V},{d}}^{-1}\left(I(\mathsf{V};g(\mathsf{W})\right)\geq R_{\mathsf{V},{d}}^{-1}\left(I(\mathsf{V};\mathsf{W})\right).

(14)

Proof.

Let
D={\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathsf{V},g(\mathsf{W}))\right]
, and consider the test channel
\mathsf{V}\to\hat{\mathsf{V}}=g(\mathsf{W})
.
Then, by the definition of the rate-distortion function, we obtain

\displaystyle R_{\mathsf{V},{d}}(D)=\inf_{P_{\hat{\mathsf{V}}|\mathsf{V}}:\,\mathbb{E}[d(\mathsf{V},\hat{\mathsf{V}})]\leq D}I(\mathsf{V};\hat{\mathsf{V}})\leq I(\mathsf{V};g(\mathsf{W}))\leq I(\mathsf{V};\mathsf{W}),

(15)

where the first bound holds since we assumed
D=\mathbb{E}[d(\mathsf{V},g(\mathsf{W}))]
, so the chosen
g(\cdot)
satisfies the average distortion constraint. The last bound follows from the data-processing inequality, as
\mathsf{V}\to\mathsf{W}\to g(\mathsf{W})
. Taking the inverse of (
15
) yields (
14
)..
This concludes the proof.
∎

Next, we state a conditional version of Theorem
1
.

Corollary 1

((Rate-distortion bound))

Suppose that
(\mathsf{U},\mathsf{V},\mathsf{W})\sim P_{\mathsf{U}}P_{\mathsf{V}}P_{\mathsf{W}|\mathsf{V},\mathsf{U}}
. Then,

\mathbb{E}\left[d(\mathsf{V},g(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{U}))\right]\geq\int R_{\mathsf{V},{d}}^{-1}\left(I(\mathsf{V};\mathsf{W}\mid\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{u})\right)\mathrm{d}P_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathsf{u}).

(16)

Proof.

First note that by the law of total expectation

\mathbb{E}\left[d(\mathsf{V},g(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{U}))\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[d(\mathsf{V},g(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{U}))\mid\mathsf{U}\right]\right].

(17)

Second, the proof is concluded by applying the bound in Theorem
1
to the inner expectation in (
17
)
for a fixed
\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{u}.

∎

III-B

Generic Converse

Theorem 2

The sensing-information rate capacity region is outer-bounded as
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}=\overline{\rm conv}\bigcup_{P_{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathcal{F}}\left\{(D,R):D\geq\int R^{-1}_{\mathbf{A},\,d}(I(\mathbf{A};\mathbf{Y}_{s}|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}P_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}),\,R\leq T^{-1}{I}\left(\left.\mathbf{Y}_{\rm c};\mathbf{X}\right|\mathbf{H}_{\rm c}\right)\right\}.

A few remarks are in order:

•

The converse of Theorem
2
requires no regularity conditions on the joint distribution. This should be contrasted with bounds like the BCRB, which require both smoothness and certain behavior at the boundary of the support. Sections
IV
and
V
will showcase examples where the BCRB cannot be applied, while the RDB presented in this section can.

•

The bound is fairly general, and as long as
R_{\mathbf{A},\,d}
can be computed or estimated, and it relies only on mutual information terms. Section
IV
and
V
present such examples. Additionally, Sec.
III-D
will show how the RDB can be further relaxed using Shannon lower bound.

The Pareto frontier
\partial\mathcal{R}
of the outer bound of Theorem
2
is characterized as

\partial\mathcal{R}=\bigcup_{\lambda\geq 0}\left\{\left(D(P_{\mathbf{X}}^{(\lambda)},P_{\mathbf{Y}_{s}|\mathbf{X}}),\,\frac{1}{T}{I}\left(\left.\mathbf{Y}_{\rm c};\mathbf{X}^{(\lambda)}\right|\mathbf{H}_{\rm c}\right)\right)\right\}

(20)

where
P_{\mathbf{X}}^{(\lambda)}
is given by the optimization problem

\displaystyle P_{\mathbf{X}}^{(\lambda)}=\arginf_{P_{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathcal{F}}\,D(P_{\mathbf{X}},P_{\mathbf{Y}_{s}|\mathbf{X}})-\lambda T^{-1}{I}\left(\left.\mathbf{Y}_{\rm c};\mathbf{X}\right|\mathbf{H}_{\rm c}\right),

(21)

with
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
balancing sensing and the communication performance.

In this work, we primarily focus on the converse result for the ISAC problem. That said, it’s important to briefly address achievability and the tightness of our converse. We show that even in our general setting, tightness can be claimed in both high- and low-noise regimes.

III-C

Tightness of Generic Converse in the High Sensing-Noise Regime

To argue about tightness of the bound, we need to define a notion of sensing noise parameter. In order to express such regime in limit expression, we adopt the following assumptions:

•

A1:

P_{\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{s}}}
can be parametrized by
\eta\geq 0
, which refers to as the
noise level
, i.e.,
P_{\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{s}}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h};\eta)
for all
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{h})\in\mathcal{X}^{T}\times\mathcal{H}_{s}\times\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}^{T}
.

•

A2:
High Sensing-Noise Regime: For all
P_{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathcal{F}
, we have

\lim_{\eta\to\infty}I(P_{\mathbf{X}};P_{\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{s}}}(\cdot|\cdot,\cdot;\eta))=0.

(22)

We now show that bound is tight in high noise regime.

Proposition 1

((Tightness of the generic converse in the high sensing-noise regime))

For any
P_{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathcal{F}
, the generic converse of Theorem
2
is tight in the
\eta\to\infty
limit, i.e.,

\lim_{\eta\to\infty}\int R^{-1}_{\mathbf{A},d}(I(\mathbf{A};\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}P_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})=\inf_{c}\mathbb{E}\left[d\left(\mathbf{A},c\right)\right]=\lim_{\eta\to\infty}D(P_{\mathbf{X}},P_{\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{s}}}(\cdot|\cdot,\cdot;\eta)).

(23)

Proof.

On one hand, write

\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\to\infty}D(P_{\mathbf{X}},P_{\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{s}}}(\cdot|\cdot,\cdot;\eta))

\displaystyle=\lim_{\eta\to\infty}\inf_{\psi_{\mathrm{s}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\psi_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{s}},\mathbf{X}))\right]=\inf_{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{c})\right].

(24)

On the other hand, write

\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\to\infty}\int R^{-1}_{\mathbf{A},\,d}(I(\mathbf{A};\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}P_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int R^{-1}_{\mathbf{A},\,d}\left(\lim_{\eta\to\infty}I(\mathbf{A};\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})\right)\mathrm{d}P_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})

(25)

\displaystyle=\int R^{-1}_{\mathbf{A},\,d}(0^{+})\mathrm{d}P_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})

(26)

\displaystyle=R^{-1}_{\mathbf{A},\,d}(0^{+})

(27)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{c})\right],

(28)

where (
25
) follows from the continuity and boundedness of
R_{\mathbf{A},\,d}^{-1}(r)\in[0,\inf_{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{c})\right]]
; (
26
) follows from using assumption A2; and (
28
) follows from (
10
).
∎

Proposition 2

((A second-order approximation of the generic converse))

Let
\mathbf{c}=\arg\inf_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{a})\right]
and suppose
{\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{c})^{2}\right]<\infty
. Then, for any
P_{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathcal{F}
we have

D(P_{\mathbf{X}},P_{\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{H}_{\rm s}})\geq{\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{c})\right]-\int\sqrt{2I(\mathbf{A};\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}){\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{c})^{2}\right]}\mathrm{d}P_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}).

(29)

Proof.

First, we get a lower bound on the RD function by fixing a reconstruction distribution
P_{\hat{\mathbf{A}}}
in (
11
). Specifically, let us choose
\hat{\mathbf{A}}\sim\delta(\hat{\mathbf{a}}-\mathbf{c})
where
\mathbf{c}=\arg\inf_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{a})\right]
.
Note that this choice of
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
satisfies both conditions (
12
) and (

). After the substitution in (

0\leq D\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{c})\right]
we have

\displaystyle R_{\mathbf{A},\,d}(D)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\lambda\geq 0}\left(-\lambda D-\int\log\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda d(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{c})}\right)\mathrm{d}P_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{a})\right)

(30)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\lambda\geq 0}\left(-\lambda D-\int\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda d(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{c})}-1\right)\mathrm{d}P_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{a})\right)

(31)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\lambda\geq 0}\left(-\lambda D-\int\left(-\lambda d(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{c})+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}d(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{c})^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}P_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{a})\right)

(32)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lambda\geq 0}\left(-\lambda D+\lambda{\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{c})\right]-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{c})^{2}\right]\right)

(33)

\displaystyle=\frac{({\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{c})\right]-D)^{2}}{2{\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{c})^{2}\right]}

(34)

where (

) follows from
\log(x)\leq x-1
for
x>0
; (

) follows from
\mathrm{e}^{x}\leq 1-x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
; (
33
) holds by the assumption
{\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{c})^{2}\right]<\infty
; and the final step is obtained by finding the optimal
\lambda=\frac{({\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{c})\right]-D)^{2}}{{\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{c})^{2}\right]}
. Thus, a lower bound on the inverse of the RD function is

R_{\mathbf{A},\,d}^{-1}(r)\geq{\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{c})\right]-\sqrt{2r{\mathbb{E}}\left[d(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{c})^{2}\right]},\quad r\geq 0.

(35)

Finally, the application of (
19
) concludes the proof.
∎

III-D

Applying Shannon Lower Bound

In many cases,
R_{\mathbf{A},\,d}
may not be available in closed form. Therefore, it is important to have way to ‘estimate’
R_{\mathbf{A},\,d}
. One such way is to further lower bound
R_{\mathbf{A},\,d}
. A classical way to lower bound the rate-distortion function is to use Shannon lower bound, which we review and provide in this section.

We first consider the case of continuous random variables.

Lemma 1

((Shannon lower bound, continuous case))

Consider a distortion measure
d(\mathsf{v},\hat{\mathsf{v}})=\|\mathsf{v}-\hat{\mathsf{v}}\|^{k}_{\ell}
. Then, for any real-valued
\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with continuous distribution, it holds that

R_{\mathsf{V},\,d}(D)\geq{R_{\rm SLB}(D)}\triangleq h(\mathsf{V})+\log\left(\frac{1}{V_{n}\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{k}+1\right)}\right)+\frac{n}{k}\log\left(\frac{n}{kD\mathrm{e}}\right),\quad D\geq 0,

(36)

where
V_{n}
is the volume of the
n
-dimensional unit ball under the
\|\cdot\|_{\ell}
norm, and
k>0
. Consequently, for
0\leq r\leq h(\mathsf{V})

R_{\mathsf{V}}^{-1}(r)\geq\frac{n}{k\mathrm{e}(V_{n}\Gamma(\frac{n}{k}+1))^{\frac{k}{n}}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{k}{n}\left(h(\mathsf{V})-r\right)}.

(37)

For the practically relevant complex-valued source alphabet, the above can be adapted to: If
\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
with continuous distribution and the Euclidean norm (i.e.,
\ell=2
) over
\mathbb{C}
, then

R_{\mathsf{V},\,d}^{-1}(r)\geq\frac{n}{\pi\mathrm{e}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{n}\left(h(\mathsf{V})-r\right)}.

(38)

The Shannon lower bound is also available for discrete cases as shown next.

Lemma 2

((Shannon lower bound, discrete case))

Consider a discrete source
\mathsf{V}
with a distortion measure
d(\mathsf{v},\hat{\mathsf{v}})
that satisfies the following condition: all columns of the distortion matrix are permutation of the set
\{d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{m}\}
. Define the function

\phi(D)=\max_{\mathbf{p}:\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}d_{i}\leq D}H(\mathbf{p})

(39)

which is non-decreasing and concave. Then, for
D\geq 0

R_{\mathsf{V},\,d}(D)\geq H(\mathsf{V})-\phi(D).

(40)

Consequently, for
0\leq r\leq H(\mathsf{V})
2
2

2

Where we define
\phi^{r}=\inf\{D:\phi(D)\leq r\}
.

R_{\mathsf{V}}^{-1}(r)\geq\phi^{-1}\left(H(\mathsf{V})-r\right).

(41)

To the best of our knowledge, the most general Shannon lower bound that holds for arbitrary source distribution and large class of distortion measure was provided in
[
18
]
.

III-E

Comparison to the Cramér-Rao Bound

In this section, we show that if
\mathbf{A}
has a density with finite Fisher information, the RDB from Corollary
1
, under the MMSE criterion, can be strictly tighter than the BCRB. We establish this result in the scalar case, though certain generalizations to the vector case may be possible.

For clarity, we consider a generic estimation problem, similar to Section
III-A
, where the goal is to estimate
\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with a continuous distribution, from an observation
\mathsf{W}
. In this setting, the MMSE is given by

\displaystyle\inf_{f}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathsf{V}-f(\mathsf{W})\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\text{MMSE}(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W})\geq R_{\mathsf{V}}^{-1}\left(I(\mathsf{V};\mathsf{W})\right)

(42)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n}{2\pi e}\exp\left(\tfrac{2}{n}\big(h(\mathsf{V})-I(\mathsf{V};\mathsf{W})\big)\right)

(43)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2\pi e}\exp\left(\tfrac{2}{n}h(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W})\right)=nN(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W}),

(44)

where
N(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W})
denotes the conditional entropy power.
On the other hand, the BCRB for
\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathsf{W}\in\mathcal{W}
is given by
[
7
]

\text{MMSE}\geq{\rm tr}\left(\mathbf{J}^{-1}(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W})\right)
,
where
\mathbf{J}(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W})
is the conditional Fisher information matrix. We now show that, in the scalar case, the RDB dominates by proving a conditional version of Stam’s inequality
[
19
]
.

Lemma 3

((Conditional Stam’s inequality))

For any continuous scalar random variable
\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{R}
and a random vector
\mathsf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(with an arbitrary support topology), we have

N(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W})\geq J^{-1}(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W}).

(45)

Proof.

Conditioned to
\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{w}
, by Stam’s inequality we have that

N(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{w})J(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{w})\geq 1.

(46)

By taking logarithms and the averages over
P_{\mathsf{W}}
, we get

\int\left(\log(N(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{w}))+\log(J(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{w}))\right)\mathrm{d}P_{\mathsf{W}}(\mathsf{w})\geq 0.

(47)

By re-exponentiating, we have

\exp\left(\int\log(N(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{w}))\mathrm{d}P_{\mathsf{W}}(\mathsf{w})\right)\geq\exp\left(-\int\log(J(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{w}))\mathrm{d}P_{\mathsf{W}}(\mathsf{w})\right).

(48)

Now notice that

\exp\left(\int\log(N(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{w}))\mathrm{d}P_{\mathsf{W}}(\mathsf{w})\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{e}}\exp\left(2h(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W})\right)=N(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W}),

(49)

and that

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\int\log(J(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{w}))\mathrm{d}P_{\mathsf{W}}(\mathsf{w})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-\log\left(\int J(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{w})\mathrm{d}P_{\mathsf{W}}(\mathsf{w})\right)\right)

(50)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\log\left(J(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W})\right)\right)=J^{-1}(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W}).

(51)

Finally, we have
N(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W})\geq J^{-1}(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W})
.
∎

Remark 1

We do not have a proof of Lemma
3
for the vector case, nor are there indications that it should hold. Thus, a different approach is needed. Since we’ve already shown tightness in the high-noise regime, it remains to do so for the low-noise case.

The potential tightness of the bound in the low-noise regime can be argued via the following example. Suppose that
\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathsf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
are independent, and consider the output

\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{V}+\sigma\mathsf{Z}.

(52)

It is known that for every continuous random vector
\mathsf{V}
, in the low-noise regime we have

\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{MMSE}(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W})}{\sigma^{2}}=n.

(53)

We now show that our lower bound also achieves this rate, hence it is tight in the low-noise regime. Starting with (
43
), we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{MMSE}(\mathsf{V}|\mathsf{W})}{\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{n}{2\pi\mathrm{e}}\exp\left(\frac{-2I(\mathsf{V};\mathsf{W})+2h(\mathsf{V})}{n}\right)

(54)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\sigma\to 0}n\exp\left(\frac{2h(\mathsf{V})-2h(\mathsf{V}+\sigma\mathsf{Z})}{n}\right)

(55)

\displaystyle=n\exp\left(-\tfrac{2\lim_{\sigma\to 0}I(\mathsf{Z};\mathsf{V}+\sigma\mathsf{Z})}{n}\right)=n,

(56)

where in the last step we used the fact that
\lim_{\sigma\to 0}I(\mathsf{Z};\mathsf{V}+\sigma\mathsf{Z})=0
(see, for example,
[
20
, Lemma 3]
).
The above together with the tightness of the Shannon lower bound in the small distortion regime
[
14
]
show that for the channel in (
52
), the RDB is tight for all continuous random vectors
\mathsf{V}
as
\sigma\to 0
.

IV
Case Study: Nakagami Fading

The Nakagami distribution is commonly used to model signal amplitude fluctuations in wireless channels affected by multipath fading. Parameterized by a shape factor
m>0
and scale
\omega>0
, it flexibly captures fading severity: smaller
m
implies more severe fading. Notably, for
m=1
, it reduces to the Rayleigh distribution, the standard model for rich-scattering environments without line-of-sight. This versatility makes Nakagami a key tool in analyzing wireless systems under multiplicative fading.

Next, we show that the Fisher information of the Nakagami distribution diverges for
m<1
, due to heavy concentration of probability mass near the origin. As a result, the BCRB cannot be established when the channel prior follows a Nakagami distribution with
m<1
indicating that in severe fading conditions, the prior is too singular to yield a finite estimation error bound.

Theorem 3

((Fisher information of a complex Nakagami random variable))

Let
H=N\mathrm{e}^{j\Theta}

where
N\sim\mathrm{Nakagami}(m,\omega)
and

\Theta\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,2\pi)
is independent of
N
.
Define the Fisher information of
H
through the Wirtinger derivative as
J_{H}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\bigl|\tfrac{\partial}{\partial H^{*}}\log f_{H}(H)\bigr|^{2}\right]
,
where
f_{H}
is the density of
H
. Then,

J_{H}=\frac{m}{\omega}\text{ for $m\geq 1$, while $J_{H}=+\infty$ for $m<1$.}

(57)

Proof.

By rotational invariance the density of
H
depends only on
r=|h|
:

f_{H}(h)=\frac{m^{m}}{\pi\Gamma(m)\omega^{m}}r^{2m-2}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{m}{\omega}r^{2}\right),\qquad r>0.

(58)

Differentiating with respect to
h^{*}
using Wirtinger calculus,

\partial_{h^{*}}\log f_{H}(h)=(2m-2)\frac{h}{2r^{2}}-\frac{m}{\omega}h=h\!\left(\frac{m-1}{r^{2}}-\frac{m}{\omega}\right).

(59)

Therefore, with
N=|H|\sim\mathrm{Nakagami}(m,\omega)
,

\bigl|\partial_{H^{*}}\log f_{H}(H)\bigr|^{2}=\frac{(m-1)^{2}}{N^{2}}-\frac{2m(m-1)}{\omega}+\frac{m^{2}}{\omega^{2}}N^{2}.

(60)

The expectations are
\mathbb{E}[N^{2}]=\omega
and

\mathbb{E}[N^{-2}]=\tfrac{m}{\omega(m-1)}
(the latter valid for
m>1
).
Substituting,

J_{H}=(m-1)^{2}\frac{m}{\omega(m-1)}-\frac{2m(m-1)}{\omega}+\frac{m^{2}}{\omega}=\frac{m}{\omega}.

(61)

At
m=1
the singular term vanishes, and the same formula holds.
For
0<m<1
the expectation
\mathbb{E}[N^{-2}]
diverges, so
J_{H}=+\infty
.
∎

Proposition 3

If
\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
has independent and identically distributed (iid)
\text{Nakagami}(m,\omega)
random entries, then

R^{-1}_{\mathsf{V}}(t)=n\omega\mathrm{e}^{c_{m}-\frac{1}{n}t},\quad t\geq 0,

(62)

where
c_{m}=(m-1)(1-\psi(m))+\log\left(\frac{\Gamma(m)}{m}\right)
, and
\psi(m)
is the digamma function.

Proof.

The differential entropy of
V_{i}
is

\displaystyle h(V_{i})

\displaystyle=\log(\pi)+h(|V_{i}|^{2})

(63)

\displaystyle=\log(\pi)+(m+(1-m)\psi(m))\log(\mathrm{e})+\log\left(\Gamma(m)\frac{\omega}{m}\right),

(64)

where (
63
) follows from the circular symmetry of
V_{i}
, and (
64
) uses the differential entropy of
|V_{i}|^{2}\sim\text{Gamma}(m,\frac{\omega}{m})
. Next, by using Shannon lower bound (Lemma
1
), we have

R_{\mathsf{V}}^{-1}(t)\geq\frac{n}{\pi\mathrm{e}}\mathrm{e}^{\log(\pi)+(m+(1-m)\psi(m))\log(\mathrm{e})+\log\left(\Gamma(m)\frac{\omega}{m}\right)-\frac{1}{n}t}=n\omega\mathrm{e}^{c_{m}-\frac{1}{n}t}.

(65)

∎

IV-A

ISAC Model

The input-output relationships are

\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{c}}=\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{c}}\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{c}},\quad\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}=\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{s}}\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{s}},

(66)

where we have the following assumption.
The communication receiver knows
(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{c}},\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{c}})
.
The sensing receiver knows
(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{s}},\mathbf{X})
.
The transmit signal
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times T}
is subject to the per-antenna average power constraint

\mathcal{F}=\{P_{\mathbf{X}}:\>{\text{tr}}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\right]\right)\leq TMP_{0}\}

(67)

for some
P_{0}>0
. Here,
M
is the number of transmit antennas and
T
is the channel coherence time.
The noise matrices
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{c}}\times T}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{s}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{s}}\times T}
have iid circularly symmetric complex-valued zero-mean Gaussian entries with variances
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{c}}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{s}}
, respectively.
Here,
N_{\mathrm{c}}
and
N_{\mathrm{s}}
are the number of antennas at the communication and sensing receiver, respectively.

\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{c}}\times M}
is the communication channel response matrix.

\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{c}}
varies every
T
symbols in an iid manner.

\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{c}}
is known at the communication receiver – i.e., perfect CommRxCSI.

\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{s}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{s}}\times M}
is the target response matrix that varies in an iid manner every
T
symbols.

\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{s}}
is not known at the sensing receiver – i.e., absent SenseRxCSI, and it must be estimated.
The entries of
\mathbf{H}_{\star}
are iid complex-valued Nakagami
(m_{\star},\omega_{\star})
for

Finally, the three random matrices

\star\in\{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{s}\}

(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{c}},\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{c}})

In terms of performance, we have the following.

•

Theorem 5

((Capacity of fading channels with perfect CSIR))

Assume that
\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{c}}

B1)

B2)

C_{\text{CSIR}}={\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\det\left(\mathbf{I}_{N_{\mathrm{c}}}+\frac{P_{0}}{\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{c}}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{c}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\dagger}\right)\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\det\left(\mathbf{I}_{M}+\frac{P_{0}}{\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{c}}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{c}}\right)\right]

\,\sim{\cal CN}(\mathbf{0},P_{0}\mathbf{I}_{M})

By condition B1 and the memoryless property of the channel, we can focus on a single MIMO channel use, i.e., on finding the optimal distribution for one column of

P_{\mathbf{X}}=\prod_{i=1}^{T}P_{\mathbf{X}_{:,i}}.

\displaystyle\Phi(\Pi Q\Pi^{\dagger})

.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of all
M\times M
permutation matrices, and define the permutation-average

\overline{Q}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}}\Pi Q\Pi^{\dagger}.

By symmetry,
\overline{Q}=\frac{\mathrm{tr}(Q)}{M}\mathbf{I}_{M}
, since only scalar multiples of the identity are invariant under all permutations. By concavity and Jensen,

\Phi(\overline{Q})\geq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}}\Phi(\Pi Q\Pi^{\dagger})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}}\Phi(Q)=\Phi(Q).

(86)

Thus
\overline{Q}
attains an objective value at least as large as
Q
. Because this holds for every feasible
Q
, an optimizer can be chosen as
\overline{Q}
, i.e. a scalar matrix with trace
MP_{0}
. Under the trace constraint the scalar matrix is
Q^{\star}=P_{0}\mathbf{I}_{M}
.
∎

Note that all fading matrices
\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{c}}
with i.i.d. circularly symmetric entries satisfy B2 in Theorem
5
. Examples that also meet assumption B3 include Rayleigh and Nakagami fading
[
21
]
.

Theorem 6

((Converse RDB for Nakagami channel))

When
\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{c}}
is known at the receiver, and the sensing channel is iid Nakagami, an outer bound to the sensing-information rate capacity region is

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\overline{\rm conv}\bigcup_{P_{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathcal{F}}\left\{(D,R):\quad\begin{array}[]{c}D\geq MN_{s}\omega_{\mathrm{s}}\mathrm{e}^{c_{m}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\det\left(\mathbf{I}_{M}+\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{s}}}{\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{s}}}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\right)\right)^{-\frac{1}{M}}\right]\\

(87)

The outer bound can be further relaxed as

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\overline{\rm conv}\bigcup_{\lambda\geq 0}\left\{(D,R):\quad\begin{array}[]{c}D\geq MN_{s}\omega_{\mathrm{s}}\mathrm{e}^{c_{m}}{\left(\det\left(\mathbf{I}_{M}+\frac{\omega_{s}}{\sigma^{2}_{s}}Q_{\lambda}\right)\right)^{-\frac{1}{M}}}\\

(88)

where
Q_{\lambda}
is the covariace shaping solution of

\displaystyle Q_{\lambda}=\argmin_{Q:\>{\text{tr}}\left(Q\right)=MP_{0}T}MN_{s}\omega_{\mathrm{s}}\mathrm{e}^{c_{m}}{\left(\det\left(\mathbf{I}_{M}+\frac{\omega_{s}}{\sigma^{2}_{s}}Q\right)\right)^{-\frac{1}{M}}}-\lambda{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\det\left(\mathbf{I}_{N_{\mathrm{c}}}+\frac{1}{T\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{c}}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{c}}Q\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\dagger}\right)\right].

(89)

Proof.

We apply the converse bound of Theorem
2
. For the MMSE sensing bound we use Theorem
4
, while from Theorem
5
we have

\frac{1}{T}{I}\left(\left.\mathbf{Y}_{c};\mathbf{X}\right|\mathbf{H}_{c}\right)\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\det\left(\mathbf{I}_{N_{\mathrm{c}}}+\frac{1}{T\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{c}}}\mathbf{H}_{c}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\right]\mathbf{H}_{c}^{\dagger}\right)\right].

(90)

To show (
88
), note that
\det(\mathbf{B})^{-1/M}
is convex over

\mathbb{E}[\det(\mathbf{B})^{-1/M}]\geq\det(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{B}])^{-1/M}

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}]
under the average power constraint
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}]=MP_{0}T

\lambda

V
Case Study: Occupancy Detection

To illustrate the generality of the proposed converse bound, we consider a
binary occupancy detection
task, where the sensing system must decide whether a person is present in a monitored environment.

Let the hidden state be
A\in\{0,1\}
, where
A=0
corresponds to an empty room and
A=1
corresponds to an occupied room. The presence of a person affects the multipath environment and thereby modifies the effective sensing channel matrix. Specifically, we model the sensing channel
\mathbf{H}_{\rm s}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{s}}\times M}
as a deterministic baseline response plus a low-rank perturbation:

\mathbf{H}_{\rm s}=\mathbf{H}_{0}+\mathbbm{1}\{A=1\}\cdot(\alpha\,\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}+\mathbf{W})

(94)

where we assume the following:

\mathbf{H}_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{s}}\times M}
is the static empty-room response (e.g., reflections from walls and ceiling);

\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
and
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
are unit-norm steering vectors for the additional scatterer (e.g., human body);

\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
is the associated scattering amplitude;

\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{s}}\times M}
models diffuse multipath via iid
\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma_{W}^{2})
entries.

The rank-one term
\alpha\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}
captures the dominant reflection due to human presence.
The received sensing signal is
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}=\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{s}}\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{s}}
, where
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{s}}
has iid
\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}^{2})
entries.
The communication signal
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{c}}
follows the model in Sec.
IV
with
m_{\mathrm{c}}=1
and
\omega_{\mathrm{c}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}
(Rayleigh fading).

The sensing receiver aims to estimate
A
from
(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{s}},\mathbf{X})
, using the indicator distortion function

d(a,\hat{a})=\mathbbm{1}\{a\neq\hat{a}\}
,
so that expected distortion equals the detection error probability
\mathbb{P}[\hat{A}\neq A]
.

Converse Bound for Integrated Detection and Communication

Applying Theorem

R_{A}(D)=H_{2}(p_{A}(1))-H_{2}(D),\qquad 0\leq D\leq\min(p_{A}(0),p_{A}(1)),

(96)

Proposition 4

\displaystyle D_{\text{avg}}

Proof.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we present a novel, general converse bound on the fundamental limits of integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) systems using rate-distortion theory. The rate-distortion bound (RDB) is robust—applicable to arbitrary parameter distributions and distortion measures—and overcomes the restrictive regularity conditions of the Bayesian Cramér-Rao Bound (BCRB). We formally prove that the RDB can be strictly tighter than the BCRB under the squared-error criterion via a conditional version of Stam’s inequality. The framework’s utility is demonstrated across diverse scenarios, including valid performance limits under severe Nakagami fading (where the BCRB fails) and analysis of a discrete occupancy detection problem. This work offers a principled tool for characterizing the sensing-rate tradeoff, providing key guidance for future 6G system design. Future work will focus on achievability schemes and extending the RDB to multi-user and multi-target ISAC models.
