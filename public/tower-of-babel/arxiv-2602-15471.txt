Abstract
. We study the problem of prescribing the Gaussian curvature on the disk and the geodesic curvature on its boundary via a conformal change of the metric. In this paper the case of negative Gaussian curvature is treated, a regime for which the bubbling behavior of approximate solutions is not so well understood. This is due to the possible appearance of blow-up solutions with diverging length and area. We give an existence result under assumptions on the curvatures which are somewhat natural, in view of some obstructions inherent to the problem. Our strategy is variational and relies on the study of certain families of approximated problems. By performing a refined blow-up analysis for solutions with bounded Morse index, we conclude compactness.

1.
Introduction

Let
(\Sigma,\tilde{g})
be a compact Riemannian surface with smooth boundary
\partial\Sigma
. A metric
g
is said to be conformal to
\tilde{g}
if there exists a smooth positive function
\rho\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
, called the conformal factor, such that
g=\rho\,\tilde{g}
.
The classical problem of prescribing the Gaussian curvature on
\Sigma
via a conformal change of the metric was initiated by Berger
[
9
]
and Kazdan-Warner
[
31
]
, and has attracted the attention of a large amount of work since then. The case
\Sigma=\mathbb{S}^{2}
(the so called Nirenberg problem) turns out to be particularly delicate due to the noncompact effect of the group of conformal maps of the sphere, see
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
27
,
30
,
41
]
.

When the boundary is not empty, a natural extension is to simultaneously prescribe the geodesic curvature along
\partial\Sigma
. Writing the conformal factor as
\rho=e^{u}
for some smooth function
u
, the Gaussian curvature
K
and the geodesic curvature
h
of the perturbed metric
g=e^{u}\tilde{g}
are related to the initial curvatures
\tilde{K}
and
\tilde{h}
through the transformation laws:

\begin{cases}-\Delta u+2\tilde{K}=2Ke^{u}&\text{in }\Sigma,\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}+2\tilde{h}=2he^{u/2}&\text{on }\partial\Sigma,\end{cases}

where
\Delta
denotes the Laplace–Beltrami operator associated with
\tilde{g}
, and
\nu
is the outward unit normal vector with respect to
\tilde{g}
.

The case of prescribing constant curvatures
K
and
h
was treated by Brendle
[
12
]
, who employed a parabolic flow to obtain solutions in the limit. Classification results for related Liouville-type problems in the half-plane are also available in
[
23
,
33
,
43
]
. Regarding nonconstant curvatures, the problem was first studied by Cherrier
[
19
]
, and later extended in
[
4
,
35
]
. In particular, the negative curvature regime
K<0
has been investigated in depth for multiply connected surfaces in
[
35
]
, while more recent advances for arbitrary genus have been obtained through a mean-field approach in
[
4
]
. For other results in this framework, see
[
7
,
13
]
.

When
\Sigma
is the unit disk, the problem becomes the natural boundary counterpart to the classical Nirenberg problem on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and several analogies have been observed in the literature. Observe that by the Riemann mapping theorem we can assume that
\tilde{g}
is the standard Euclidean metric, so that our problem becomes:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u=2K(x)e^{u}&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}+2=2h(x)e^{u/2}&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{D}.\end{array}\right.

Integrating (
1.1
) yields a constraint which corresponds to the Gauss-Bonnet identity:

(1.2)

\int_{\mathbb{D}}K(x)e^{u}+\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}h(x)e^{u/2}=2\pi.

Most of the available results in the literature address the problem where at least one of the curvatures to be prescribed is constant (or zero). For instance, the case
h=0
was studied in
[
16
]
via reflection to a Nirenberg problem on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The zero Gaussian curvature case (
K=0
) has been extensively investigated, see
[
14
,
22
,
24
,
26
,
32
,
34
]
.

When both
K
and
h
are nonconstant functions, the problem becomes more intricate, as the interaction of the two curvatures has an effect on the problem that has no counterpart in the classical Nirenberg problem. In
[
21
]
a new variational approach is developed when both
K
and
h
are positive. This variational formulation has been used by Struwe in
[
42
]
to define a new geometric parabolic flow from which general existence results are derived. On the other hand, in
[
39
]
the Leray-Schauder degree of the problem is computed in a compact regime. This last result is based on the blow-up analysis of
[
29
]
, that we briefly describe below. Assume that there exists a blow-up sequence of solutions
u_{n}
to the problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u_{n}=2K_{n}(x)e^{u_{n}}&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\
\frac{\partial u_{n}}{\partial\nu}+2=2h_{n}(x)e^{{u_{n}}/2}&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{D},\end{array}\right.

with
K_{n}\to K
,
h_{n}\to h
in
\mathcal{C}^{2}
sense, and with bounded area and length, i.e.,

(1.3)

\int_{\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{n}}+\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{{u_{n}}/2}<C.

Then there is a unique blow-up point
p\in\partial\mathbb{D}
such that
h^{2}(p)+K(p)>0
and
p
is a critical point of the auxiliary function

\Phi(x)=H(x)+\sqrt{H(x)^{2}+K(x)},

defined in a neighborhood of
p
, where
H
denotes the harmonic extension of
h
to
\mathbb{D}
. In fact, these blow-up solutions have been constructed, see
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
6
,
20
]
. It is worth pointing out that in both articles
[
39
,
42
]
, the behavior of the function
\Phi
turns out to be crucial.

Observe that if both
K
,
h
are positive, condition (
1.3
) is automatically satisfied due to (
1.2
). In contrast, in this paper we consider the case of negative Gaussian curvature. Being more specific, we assume:

(H)

K\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\overline{\mathbb{D}}),\ h\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\partial\mathbb{D})\ \,\text{and}\ K<0\,\text{ in }\overline{\mathbb{D}}.

A first step toward the existence of solutions in this setting was given in
[
36
]
, which deals with curvatures
K
,
h
with some kind of symmetry. Restricting to a space of symmetric functions leads to a significant simplification of the problem, as previously observed in
[
37
]
for the Nirenberg problem. The main purpose of this paper is to remove the symmetry assumptions and give general existence results for negative
K
.

It is worth pointing out that the techniques of
[
39
,
42
]
do not apply to the case
K<0
. First, the parabolic flow defined in
[
42
]
is intrinsically restricted to positive curvatures due to the variational formulation used. Moreover, if
K<0
, the behavior of blow-up solutions is much less understood since (
1.3
) need not be satisfied; indeed, there are examples showing solutions with diverging area and length, see
[
35
]
. Then, the arguments of
[
39
]
(which rely on a precise knowledge of the behavior of all possible blow-up solutions) cannot be applied to this framework.

A satisfactory description of blow-up sequences of solutions without (
1.3
) was given in
[
35
]
under the additional assumption that the Morse index of the solutions remains bounded. This analysis is useful to deal with solutions that are obtained via variational methods, since their Morse index can be controlled by the min-max scheme used. This is the strategy that we follow in this paper to obtain the following existence result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume (
H
), and suppose that
(a)
and
(b^{-})
or
(b^{+})
are satisfied, where:

(a)

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\partial\mathbb{D}}h(x)>\max_{x\in\partial\mathbb{D}}\sqrt{|K(x)|}
;

(
b^{-}
)

\partial_{\nu}\Phi(x)<0
for all
x\in\partial\mathbb{D}
such that
\partial_{\tau}\Phi(x)=0
;

(
b^{+}
)

\partial_{\nu}\Phi(x)>0
for all
x\in\partial\mathbb{D}
such that
\partial_{\tau}\Phi(x)=0
.

Then problem (
1.1
) admits at least one solution.

Here,
\partial_{\tau}
and
\partial_{\nu}
denote the tangential and outward normal derivatives on the boundary, associated with the unit vectors

\nu(x)=x=(x_{1},x_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad\tau(x)=(-x_{2},x_{1}).

Remark 1.2
.

It is worth noting that the assumptions
(a)
and
(b^{\pm})
are somewhat necessary, in view of some available obstructions to existence. For instance, if
K=-1
and
h<1
is a constant, non-existence of solutions follows from the behavior of horocycles in the hyperbolic plane. This idea has been pushed further in
[
36
, Theorem 1.2]
, where nonexistence is shown if

(1.4)

\min_{x\in\partial\mathbb{D}}h(x)<\max_{x\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}}\sqrt{|K(x)|}.

We can easily find examples of
K
and
h
satisfying (
1.4
) and
(b^{+})
(or
(b^{-})
); hence, assumption
(a)
is required in Theorem
1.1
. At this point, let us mention that
(a)
can be relaxed under condition
(b^{-})
, see Theorem
2.1
for details.

But also
(b^{\pm})
is necessary in Theorem
1.1
. To see this, consider
K(x)=-1
and
h(x)=3+x_{1}
. Although (
H
) and
(a)
are satisfied, (
1.1
) does not admit a solution. This comes from the Kazdan-Warner identity derived in
[
29
, Proposition 2.7]
: any solution to (
1.1
) satisfies the following:

\int_{\mathbb{D}}e^{u}\nabla K\cdot F=4\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\partial_{\tau}h\,e^{u/2}x_{2},

where
F(x_{1},x_{2}):=(1-x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2},-2x_{1}x_{2})
. In our case,

\int_{\mathbb{D}}e^{u}\nabla K\cdot F=0,\ \ \int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\partial_{\tau}h\,e^{u/2}x_{2}=-\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}x_{2}^{2}e^{u/2}<0,

yielding a contradiction.

Observe that here
H(x)=3+x_{1}
and the equation
\partial_{\tau}\Phi(p)=0
(
p\in\partial\mathbb{D}
) has solutions
p_{1}=(1,0)
and
p_{2}=(-1,0)
. A direct computation gives
\partial_{\nu}\Phi(p_{1})>0
while
\partial_{\nu}\Phi(p_{2})<0
, so that neither
(b^{-})
nor
(b^{+})
is satisfied.

As previously mentioned, our proofs use variational methods; solutions to problem (
1.1
) are searched as critical points of the energy functional
\mathcal{I}:H^{1}(\mathbb{D})\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\mathcal{I}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}-2K(x)e^{u}\right)+\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\left(2u-4h(x)e^{u/2}\right).

The geometric properties of
\mathcal{I}
are far from evident, due to the presence of competing inner terms and boundary terms. Moreover, the possibility of blow-up solutions makes the Palais-Smale condition unlikely to hold. Because of that, we first use a perturbation argument, considering a one-parameter family of functionals

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}:H^{1}(\mathbb{D})\to\mathbb{R}
(see (
2.1
)), where
\varepsilon
is a small parameter, not necessarily positive. The basic idea of the proof is to first find critical points of
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
via variational methods, and then pass to the limit to get a solution to (
1.1
). Let us elaborate on this procedure.

It turns out that the behavior of the functional
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
changes dramatically depending on the sign of
\varepsilon
. If
\varepsilon\geqslant 0
the functional is of mountain-pass type, as low sublevel sets are not path connected. This is not the case for
\varepsilon<0
; instead, a more sophisticated 3-dimensional linking structure is found in this case. This linking structure is completely new, and its description uses in an essential way a comparison argument with the constant coefficient case. We consider this construction as one of the primary contributions of our work, and we think that this idea could be useful in other geometric problems of this sort.

The (PS) property seems out of reach even for the perturbed functionals
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
, as (PS) sequences could eventually diverge in norm. We address this difficulty by using Struwe’s monotonicity trick
[
28
,
40
]
, which allows us to find bounded (PS) sequences
v_{\varepsilon}^{k}
for almost every
\varepsilon
small. Passing to the limit as
k\to\infty
, solutions are obtained for a family of perturbed problems. Moreover, this procedure can be done controlling the Morse index of the solutions, see
[
8
,
10
]
.

At this point, we have obtained the existence of sequences
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
(where
\varepsilon_{n}
can be chosen either positive or negative), and
u_{n}
critical points of
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon_{n}}
with bounded Morse index. We can now use
[
35
]
to show that the length and area are uniformly bounded, i.e., (
1.3
) holds. At this point, we address the compactness of
u_{n}
by contradiction, employing the arguments of
[
29
]
. In contrast to
[
29
]
, the perturbed problems we are working with are not genuinely geometric (i.e., their solutions do not correspond to metrics with prescribed curvatures). Therefore, the study of
[
29
]
must be suitably adapted to our setting. Both in the estimate of the error terms, and in the resulting asymptotic expansion, the perturbation plays a significant role in our analysis. In sum, we conclude that if
u_{n}
is unbounded, then there exists a unique blow-up point
p\in\partial\mathbb{D}
with
\partial_{\tau}\Phi(p)=0
and
\varepsilon_{n}\,\partial_{\nu}\Phi(p)\geqslant 0
. This contradicts either condition
(b^{-})
or
(b^{+})
, so that
u_{n}
is bounded and we can pass to the limit to obtain a solution to (
1.1
).

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the variational framework and presents two auxiliary existence results which, combined, yield Theorem
1.1
. This section also collects necessary preliminary results. Section
3
is devoted to the geometric construction of the mountain-pass and linking structures, from which a sequence of approximate solutions is found. In Section
4
we perform a refined blow-up analysis that provides the crucial compactness criterion. With all these ingredients at hand, we conclude the proof of the main results in Section
5
. Finally, the Appendix is devoted to an explicit computation of a couple of integral terms appearing in our arguments.

Notation:
Throughout the paper, the letter
C
denotes a generic constant, whose value may change from line to line. We also use the standard Landau notation:
O(\varepsilon)
denotes a quantity bounded by a constant multiple of
\varepsilon
, while
o(\varepsilon)
denotes a quantity negligible with respect to
\varepsilon
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

2.
Statement of the Main Results and Preliminaries

In order to give a precise statement of our main results, it is convenient to define the following scale-invariant function, introduced in
[
35
]
:

\mathfrak{D}:\partial\mathbb{D}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},\qquad\mathfrak{D}(x)=\frac{h(x)}{\sqrt{|K(x)|}}.

We point out that, when assumption
(b^{-})
of
Theorem
1.1
is satisfied, hypothesis
(a)
can be relaxed significantly. Indeed, we will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
K
and
h
satisfy (
H
) and assume that the following hypotheses hold:

(i)

There is a point
x_{0}\in\partial\mathbb{D}
with
\mathfrak{D}(x_{0})>1
;

(ii)

\partial_{\tau}\mathfrak{D}(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in\partial\mathbb{D}
where
\mathfrak{D}(x)=1
;

(iii)

\partial_{\nu}\Phi(x)<0
for all
x\in\partial\mathbb{D}
where
\partial_{\tau}\Phi(x)=0
.

Then, problem (
1.1
) admits at least one solution.

Instead, when assumption
(b^{+})
of Theorem
1.1
is satisfied, we do need hypothesis
(a)
:

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
K
and
h
satisfy (
H
) and assume that the following hypotheses hold:

(i)

\displaystyle{\min_{x\in\partial\mathbb{D}}h(x)>\max_{x\in\partial\mathbb{D}}\sqrt{|K(x)|}}
;

(ii)

\partial_{\nu}\Phi(x)>0
for all
x\in\partial\mathbb{D}
where
\partial_{\tau}\Phi(x)=0
.

Then, problem (
1.1
) admits at least one solution.

2.1.
The Perturbation Framework and Variational Settings

As outlined in the introduction, the functional
\mathcal{I}
does not seem to satisfy the (PS) condition globally, preventing a direct application of standard critical point theory. To overcome this difficulty, we first employ the monotonicity trick to a family of perturbed functionals
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
, finding critical points
u_{\varepsilon}
. Then, a solution to (
1.1
) is found by passing to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Being more specific, recall that:

(2.1)

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\frac{\mathcal{I}(u)+\varepsilon\,\mathcal{T}(u)}{1+\varepsilon},

where
\varepsilon
is a parameter (not necessarily positive) with
|\varepsilon|
small, and
\mathcal{T}:H^{1}(\mathbb{D})\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is given by

\mathcal{T}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+e^{u}-u\right).

The normalization factor
(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}
in the definition of
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
is chosen for notational convenience, to keep the coefficient
1/2
for the Dirichlet energy term. Substituting the expressions for
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{T}
, the perturbed functional takes the form:

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+2\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}u-2K_{\varepsilon}(x)e^{u}\right)+\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\left(2\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}u-4h_{\varepsilon}(x)e^{u/2}\right),

with coefficients given by:

(2.2)

\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2(1+\varepsilon)},\ \ K_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{K(x)-\varepsilon/2}{1+\varepsilon},\ \ \tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}=1-\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon},\ \ h_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{h(x)}{1+\varepsilon}.

Let us point out that the sign of the perturbation terms,
\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}
and
\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}-1
, is determined by the sign of
\varepsilon
:

\mathrm{sign}(\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon})=\mathrm{sign}(\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}-1)=-\mathrm{sign}(\varepsilon).

The Euler-Lagrange equation associated with this functional
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
is precisely the perturbed problem:

(2.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+2\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}=2K_{\varepsilon}(x)e^{u}&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}+2\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}=2h_{\varepsilon}(x)e^{u/2}&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{D}.\end{array}\right.

2.2.
Analytic tools

In order to perform a variational study of the energy functional
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
, we need some preliminary results. We start by revisiting a weak version of the Moser–Trudinger inequality for the boundary term, that will help us to prove that under the assumptions of Theorem
2.1
, in a certain region, the functional is bounded from below.

Lemma 2.3

(Lebedev-Milin Inequality,
[
38
]
)
.

For any function
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{D})
, the following trace inequality holds:

16\pi\log\left(\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{u/2}\right)\leq\int_{\mathbb{D}}|\nabla u|^{2}+4\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}u.

In the framework of Theorem
2.2
, the preceding inequality does not suffice. Instead, we require an improved version applicable to functions whose mass is spread in several separated regions on the boundary. Such results are known as Chen-Li type inequalities, and the specific version used here follows from
[
21
, Corollary 2.11]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
l\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\ldots,\Gamma_{l}\subset\partial\mathbb{D}
for which there exists
r>0
such that
(\Gamma_{i})^{r}\cap(\Gamma_{j})^{r}=\emptyset
if
i\neq j
, and
\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{l})
in such a way that

\frac{\int_{\Gamma_{i}}e^{u/2}}{\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{u/2}}\geq\gamma,\,\forall i=1,\ldots,l,

where
(\Gamma)^{r}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{D}\,:\,dist(x,\Gamma)<r\right\}
.

Then, for every
\delta>0
there exists a constant
C\in\mathbb{R}
, depending on
r
,
\gamma
and
\delta
, such that

16\pi l\log\left(\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{u/2}\right)\leq(1+\delta)\int_{\mathbb{D}}|\nabla u|^{2}+8l\int_{\mathbb{D}}u+C,\quad\forall u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{D}).

With Lemmas
2.3
,
2.4
at hand, we will be able to prove the existence of solutions to (
2.3
) for some
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
. In order to prove convergence of those solutions, a blow-up analysis is required. For this, we need Kazdan-Warner identities for problem (
2.3
). These conditions are an extension of those established in
[
29
, Section 2]
to our context. First, let us recall the following Pohozaev-type identity, depending on an arbitrary field
F
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
2.3
). Then given any vector field
F:\overline{\mathbb{D}}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\begin{split}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}&\left[2K_{\varepsilon}e^{u}(F\cdot\nu)+(2h_{\varepsilon}e^{u/2}-2\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon})(\nabla u\cdot F)-\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{2}F\cdot\nu\right]=\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left[2\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}\nabla u\cdot F+2e^{u}(\nabla K_{\varepsilon}\cdot F+K_{\varepsilon}\,\nabla\cdot F)+DF(\nabla u,\nabla u)-\nabla\cdot F\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{2}\right].\end{split}

Proof.

The proof follows by multiplying (
2.3
) by
\nabla u\cdot F
and integrating by parts; see for instance
[
35
, Lemma 5.5]
or
[
29
, Lemma 2.6]
.
∎

From this we can obtain the following Kazdan-Warner identity.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
2.3
) then

\begin{split}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}&\left[4x_{2}\partial_{\tau}h_{\varepsilon}e^{u/2}-2x_{1}(\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}-1)u-2x_{2}\partial_{\tau}\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}u+4x_{1}\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}\right]=\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left[4x_{1}\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}u-u\nabla\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}\cdot F+e^{u}\nabla K_{\varepsilon}\cdot F-4x_{1}\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}\right]\end{split}

where
F(x_{1},x_{2}):=(1-x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2},-2x_{1}x_{2})
. Note that, if
\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}
and
\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}
are constants, the above expression reduces to

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\left[4x_{2}\partial_{\tau}h_{\varepsilon}e^{u/2}-2x_{1}u(\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}-1)\right]=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left[4x_{1}u\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}+e^{u}\nabla K_{\varepsilon}\cdot F\right].

Proof.

The idea is to consider the variation along the conformal transformations that keep fixed the point
p=(1,0)
. Using Lemma
2.5
and following the same ideas as Proposition 2.7 of
[
29
]
, and noting that on
\partial\mathbb{D}
one has that
F\cdot\nu=0
and
F=-2x_{2}\tau
, we obtain

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}-2x_{2}\,\partial_{\tau}u\left(h_{\varepsilon}e^{u/2}-\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}\nabla u\cdot F+e^{u}\nabla K_{\varepsilon}\cdot F-4x_{1}e^{u}K_{\varepsilon}\right).

We now multiply (
2.3
) by
2x_{1}
and integrate to obtain

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{D}}4x_{1}K_{\varepsilon}e^{u}&=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(2x_{1}(-\Delta u)+4x_{1}\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{D}}2\partial_{x_{1}}u-\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}2x_{1}\nabla u\cdot\nu+\int_{\mathbb{D}}4x_{1}\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{D}}2\partial_{x_{1}}u+\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}4x_{1}\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}-\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}4x_{1}h_{\varepsilon}e^{u/2}+\int_{\mathbb{D}}4x_{1}\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}.\end{split}

Observe also that, integrating by parts,

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}x_{2}\partial_{\tau}u=-\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}x_{1}u=-\int_{\mathbb{D}}\partial_{x_{1}}u.

Putting together

\begin{split}&\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}-2x_{2}\,\partial_{\tau}u\left(h_{\varepsilon}e^{u/2}-\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}\right)=\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}\nabla u\cdot F+e^{u}\nabla K_{\varepsilon}\cdot F\right)-\int_{\mathbb{D}}2\partial_{x_{1}}u-\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}4x_{1}\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}+\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}4x_{1}h_{\varepsilon}e^{u/2}-\int_{\mathbb{D}}4x_{1}\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}.\end{split}

Integrating by parts

\begin{split}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}&\left(4x_{2}\partial_{\tau}h_{\varepsilon}e^{u/2}+4x_{1}h_{\varepsilon}e^{u/2}-2x_{1}\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}u-2x_{2}\partial_{\tau}\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}u-4x_{1}h_{\varepsilon}e^{u/2}+4x_{1}\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}+2x_{1}u\right)=\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(-u\nabla\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}\cdot F+4x_{1}\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}u+e^{u}\nabla K_{\varepsilon}\cdot F-4x_{1}\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}\right).\end{split}

Then

\begin{split}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}&\left(4x_{2}\partial_{\tau}h_{\varepsilon}e^{u/2}-2x_{1}(\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}-1)u-2x_{2}\partial_{\tau}\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}u+4x_{1}\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}\right)=\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(4x_{1}\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}u-u\nabla\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}\cdot F+e^{u}\nabla K_{\varepsilon}\cdot F-4x_{1}\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}\right).\end{split}

∎

3.
Existence of solutions of the perturbed problems

This section is devoted to the first part of our arguments, namely, the construction of solutions to a family the perturbed problems (

sufficiently small, the associated functional

3.1.

Proposition 3.3

Lemma 3.4
.

The functional
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}

M=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{D}):\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{u/2}=1\right\}.

For a fixed

Let

First, since

{\mathcal{H}=\max_{\partial\mathbb{D}}h(x)}

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}h(x)e^{u/2}\leq\mathcal{H}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{u/2}=\mathcal{H}.

Finally, using Lemma

\mathcal{I}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}-2K(x)e^{u}\right)+\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\left(2u-4he^{u/2}\right)\geqslant-4\mathcal{H}.

∎

Lemma 3.5

For

Proof.

The proof immediately follows from the estimates given in the Appendix of
[
35
]
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

First, let us evaluate the functional on the constant functions

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(-n)&=-\int_{\mathbb{D}}2\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}n-\int_{\mathbb{D}}2K_{\varepsilon}(x)e^{-n}-\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}2\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}n-\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}4h_{\varepsilon}(x)e^{-n/2}\\

Then, choose

As a consequence,

∎

3.2.
Higher dimensional linking geometry for

In this section, we establish a three-dimensional linking geometry for the

Proposition 3.6
.

Under the assumption of Theorem
3.2
, there exists
\delta_{0}>0

(iii)

For all

Remark 3.7

As the proof will show, the set

(not on

Proof.

First of all, observe that by addition of a constant

h_{\varepsilon}(x)>\mathfrak{h}_{0}+r\quad\forall x\in\partial\mathbb{D},\qquad\ K_{\varepsilon}(x)+1>r\ \quad\mbox{ for }|x|>1-l.

The proof is divided into four steps.

Step 1: Definition of

We first show that there exists a subset

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(u)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{D}}|\nabla u|^{2}+2\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{D}}u+2\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}u+C,\quad\text{for all }u\in M.

We can rewrite this expression as:

(3.4)

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(u)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{D}}|\nabla u|^{2}+2(\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}+2\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon})\int_{\mathbb{D}}u+4\pi\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}u-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{D}}u\right]+C.

The difference between the boundary mean and the interior mean can be controlled by a Poincaré-type inequality:

\left|4\pi\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}u-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{D}}u\right]\right|\leq C\left(\int_{\mathbb{D}}|\nabla u|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

We now employ the Chen-Li type inequality. For any

Plugging these inequalities into (

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(u)&\geqslant\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{(\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}+2\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon})(1+\delta)}{8}\right)\int_{\mathbb{D}}|\nabla u|^{2}-C\left(\int_{\mathbb{D}}|\nabla u|^{2}\right)^{1/2}+C\\
&\geqslant\frac{1}{5}\int_{\mathbb{D}}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.\end{split}

Step 2: Definition of the map

\gamma_{p}(\tau)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\gamma_{1,p}(3\tau)&\text{for }\tau\in[0,1/3],\\

We will make use of a family of test functions derived from an auxiliary problem. This problem consists in prescribing constant Gaussian curvature

\begin{split}u_{0}&=3\log\sigma;\\

Step 3: Proof of (ii).
Our objective now is to prove that

This motivates us to decompose the perturbed functional

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\mathcal{J}_{\mathfrak{h}}(u)+\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\mathfrak{h}}(u),\quad\text{for all }u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{D}),

\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\mathfrak{h}}(u)=2\tilde{K}_{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{D}}u-2\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(K_{\varepsilon}(x)+1\right)e^{u}+2\left(\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}-1\right)\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}u-4\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\left(h_{\varepsilon}(x)-\mathfrak{h}\right)e^{u/2}.

We now give estimates on

(3.6)

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{\Psi(x,\phi,\lambda)/2}=\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{\Psi(x,\phi,0)/2}=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{\mathfrak{h}^{2}-1}},

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{D}}(K_{\varepsilon}(x)+1)e^{\Psi}&=\int_{1-l<|x|\leq 1}(K_{\varepsilon}(x)+1)e^{\Psi}+\int_{|x|\leq 1-l}(K_{\varepsilon}(x)+1)e^{\Psi}\\

On the other hand:

(3.9)

\begin{split}2\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\Psi(x,\phi,\lambda)&=4\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\log\frac{2(1-\lambda^{2})\phi}{\phi^{2}\left[1-2\lambda x_{1}+\lambda^{2}\right]-\left[1-2\lambda x_{1}+\lambda^{2}\right]}\\

By (

), (
3.8

(3.10)

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\mathfrak{h}}(\Psi(x,\phi,\lambda))&\leq\alpha_{\varepsilon}\log(1-\lambda^{2})-\frac{\beta}{\phi^{2}-1}+O\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\phi^{2}-1}\right)\right)\\

where

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=8\pi r.

Observe that

and

We are now ready to estimate

on

- The Segment

where we have used that

Then we can apply (

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(\gamma_{1})\leq\alpha\log(1-(1-\sigma)^{2})-\frac{\beta}{\phi_{0}^{2}-1}+O\left(1\right)

We can conclude by taking

and

- The second curve

Recall that in this curve we use test functions

and

to

The energy associated with the auxiliary problem is

\mathcal{J}_{\mathfrak{h}}(\gamma_{2}(t))=-8\pi(1+\log(\phi(t)/2)).

Since

is continuous and remains within the compact interval

For the perturbation term
\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\mathfrak{h}}
, we apply the upper bound (
3.10
):

\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\mathfrak{h}}(\gamma_{2}(t))\leq\alpha\log(1-(1-\sigma)^{2})-\frac{\beta}{\phi^{2}(t)-1}+O\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\phi(t)^{2}-1}\right)\right).

We conclude again by taking
\sigma
sufficiently small.

- The third curve
\gamma_{3}
.
Observe that now
\phi=1+\sigma
and
\lambda
takes values between
1-\sigma
and
0
. Reasoning as above and applying again(
3.10
), we obtain:

\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\mathfrak{h}}(\gamma_{3}(t))\leq\alpha\log(1-\lambda(t)^{2})-\frac{\beta}{(1+\sigma)^{2}-1}+O\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{(1+\sigma)^{2}-1}\right)\right),

so that we can conclude if
\sigma
is chosen appropriately.

- Conclusion.
The analysis above shows that, given
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
and any real number
R
, we can choose
\sigma>0
small enough so that:

\sup_{\tau}\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}\left(\gamma_{p}(\tau)\right)<R,

for any
\varepsilon\in(-\delta,-\delta/2)
. Since
p\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
can be chosen arbitrarily, we conclude the proof of (ii).

Step 4: Proof of (iii).

The final step is the verification of the topological linking condition. We employ a degree theory argument. Let us define the map
\chi:H^{1}(\mathbb{D})\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
by

\chi(u)=\left(A(u)-1,B_{1}(u),B_{2}(u)\right),

where
B(u)
is the barycenter vector defined as in (
3.3
).

By definition,
u\in M\iff\chi(u)=0
. The linking condition is therefore proved if we show
\deg(\chi\circ\eta,B,0)\neq 0
. By (ii),
\chi(\Lambda(y))\neq 0
for all
y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, so the Brouwer degree
\deg(\chi\circ\Lambda,\mathbb{S}^{2},0)
is well defined. We claim that this degree is equal to 1.

To prove this, we show that
\chi\circ\Lambda
is homotopic to the identity map. Consider the standard linear homotopy:

H:\mathbb{S}^{2}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad H(y,s)=(1-s)\chi(\Lambda(y))+sy.

We show
H(y,s)\neq 0
for all
(y,s)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\times[0,1]
. The cases
s=0
and
s=1
are clear. We proceed by contradiction, assuming
H(y_{0},s_{0})=0
for some
y_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
s_{0}\in(0,1)
. This implies

(3.11)

\chi(\Lambda(y_{0}))=-\rho\,y_{0},

for some
\rho=s_{0}/(1-s_{0})>0
. We distinguish two cases.

- Case 1:
We consider the poles
y_{0}=(\pm 1,0,0)
. The function
\Lambda(y_{0})
is either
u_{0}
or
u_{3}
, in both cases
B(u_{0})=B(u_{3})=(0,0)
because they are radially symmetric. Furthermore:

\begin{split}A(u_{0})&=\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{0}/2}=2\pi\sqrt{\sigma^{3}}\to 0,\quad\text{as }\sigma\to 0,\\
A(u_{3})&=\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{3}/2}=2\pi\frac{2(1+\sigma)}{(1+\sigma)^{2}-1}\to+\infty,\quad\text{as }\sigma\to 0.\end{split}

This implies that
\chi(\Lambda(y_{0}))=(c,0,0)
with
c=A(\Lambda(y_{0}))-1
. In both cases we get a contradiction because
\rho>0
and
(c,0,0)
is proportional to
y_{0}
.

- Case 2:
We now consider
y_{0}=\gamma(\tau_{0})
different from a pole, which can be described via cylindrical coordinates by
(p_{0},\tau_{0})\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\times(0,1)
. We claim that
B\left(\gamma_{p}(\tau)\right)=\kappa p
for all
p\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
\tau\in(0,1)
, and for some choice of
\kappa>0
. By rotational invariance, it suffices to analyze the case
p=(1,0)
.

Observe that the component
B_{2}=\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}x_{2}e^{\gamma(\tau_{0})/2}ds
vanishes by odd symmetry. In order to estimate
B_{1}=\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}x_{1}e^{\gamma(\tau_{0})/2}ds
, we split the integral over the right and left half-circles,
\partial\mathbb{D}^{+}=\{x\in\partial\mathbb{D}:x_{1}>0\}
and
\partial\mathbb{D}^{-}=\{x\in\partial\mathbb{D}:x_{1}<0\}
. Applying the change of variables
\tilde{x}=(-x_{1},x_{2})
to the integral over
\partial\mathbb{D}^{-}
, we can write:

B_{1}=\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}^{+}}x_{1}\left[e^{\gamma(\tau_{0})(x))/2}-e^{\gamma(\tau_{0})(\tilde{x})/2}\right].

We claim that
\gamma(\tau_{0})(x)>\gamma(\tau_{0})(\tilde{x})
for
x\in\partial\mathbb{D}^{+}
. Note that, for
x\in\partial\mathbb{D}
we have the strict monotonicity in
x_{1}
:

\begin{split}\Psi\left(x,\phi,\lambda\right)&=2\log\frac{2(1-\lambda^{2})\phi}{(\phi^{2}-1)(1-2\lambda x_{1}+\lambda^{2})}\\
&>2\log\frac{2(1-\lambda^{2})\phi}{(\phi^{2}-1)(1+2\lambda x_{1}+\lambda^{2})}\\
&=\Psi\left(\tilde{x},\phi,\lambda\right)\quad\text{if }x_{1}>0\end{split}

Hence an analogous inequality holds for
u_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}(t)
, and
\gamma_{3}(t)
. It is also inherited by the convex combination
\gamma_{1}(t)=(1-t)u_{0}+tu_{1}
, since
u_{0}
is constant. Thus, for any
\tau_{0}\in(0,1)
,
\gamma(\tau_{0})(x)>\gamma(\tau_{0})(\tilde{x})
, which implies
B_{1}>0
.

Coming back to equality (
3.11
), we compare the barycenter components:

\kappa\,p_{0}=-\hat{\rho}\,p_{0},

but this is a contradiction, as
\kappa>0
,
\hat{\rho}>0
.

Since the assumption
H(y_{0},s_{0})=0
leads to a contradiction in all cases,
H
is a valid homotopy and then
\deg(\chi\circ\Lambda,\mathbb{S}^{2},0)=\deg(\Id,\mathbb{S}^{2},0)=1
. As a consequence
\deg(\chi\circ\eta,\mathbb{S}^{2},0)=1
for any
\eta\in\Gamma
, which guarantees the existence of a point
y_{0}\in\overline{B}
such that
\chi(\eta(y_{0}))=0
. This implies
\eta(y_{0})\in M
, completing the proof of (iii).
∎

3.3.
Existence of a Critical Value

In this subsection we complete the proofs of Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be as in Proposition
3.3
. We recall the definition of the perturbed functionals
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}:H^{1}(\mathbb{D})\to\mathbb{R}
for
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
:

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\frac{\mathcal{I}(u)+\varepsilon\,\mathcal{T}(u)}{1+\varepsilon},\ \mbox{where }\ \mathcal{T}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+e^{u}-u\right).

As established in the previous sections, this functional
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
possesses the required min-max geometry (either mountain-pass or higher dimensional linking), the nature of which depends on the sign of
\varepsilon
.

Let
c_{\varepsilon}
denote the corresponding min-max level for
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
. The term
\mathcal{T}
is coercive and strictly positive, and the derivatives
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}
,
\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon}
are uniformly Hölder continuous on bounded sets. We may therefore apply to
\mathcal{I}+\varepsilon\mathcal{T}
the monotonicity trick of Struwe (
[
40
,
28
]
). In our case, we make use of the abstract results from
[
8
, Proposition 4.2]
or
[
11
, Theorem 1]
, which allow us to keep control on the Morse index of the solutions. We obtain the existence of a bounded Palais–Smale sequence
\{v_{k}^{\varepsilon}\}
for
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
at the level
c_{\varepsilon}
for almost every
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
. Then, up to a subsequence, we may assume that
v_{k}^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup u_{\varepsilon}
weakly in
H^{1}(\mathbb{D})
. By standard regularity arguments,
u_{\varepsilon}
is a classical solution of the perturbed problem (
2.3
) with the coefficients given in (
2.2
). In particular, one can take a sequence
\varepsilon_{n}>0
converging to
0
with that property.

Furthermore, the solutions obtained via these min-max procedures have a Morse index controlled by the dimension of the underlying geometric structure. In this case, since we are using a mountain-pass argument,

\mathrm{ind}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq 1.

∎

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

We reason analogously, with only one technical detail. Let
\delta_{0}
as given by using Proposition
3.6
, and fix
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
. We consider again the family of functionals
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
, with
\varepsilon\in(-\delta,-\delta/2)
. Taking into account the linking structure proved in Proposition
3.6
(which is independent of
\varepsilon
, although it depends on
\delta
), we can argue as above to find
u_{\varepsilon}
solutions to (
2.3
) for almost every
\varepsilon\in(-\delta,-\delta/2)
. Since
\delta>0
is arbitrary, we obtain the desired sequence of solutions.

Since our min-max scheme is three-dimensional in this case, we obtain the Morse index bound:

\mathrm{ind}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq 3.

∎

4.
Blow-up analysis

In this section, we analyze the properties of blow-up solutions to the perturbed problem

(4.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u_{n}+2\tilde{K}_{n}=2K_{n}(x)e^{u_{n}}&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\
\frac{\partial u_{n}}{\partial\nu}+2\tilde{h}_{n}=2h_{n}(x)e^{u_{n}/2}&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{D},\end{cases}

where the coefficients depend on a sequence
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
according to

(4.2)

\tilde{K}_{n}=-\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{2(1+\varepsilon_{n})},\quad K_{n}(x)=\frac{K(x)-\varepsilon_{n}/2}{1+\varepsilon_{n}},\quad\tilde{h}_{n}=\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon_{n}},\quad h_{n}(x)=\frac{h(x)}{1+\varepsilon_{n}}.

Most of the related literature on this kind of problems imposes a uniform bounded mass condition in the form:

(4.3)

\int_{\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{n}}+\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{n}/2}<C,

see for instance
[
3
,
29
]
. However, in general this condition need not be satisfied, and the blow-up analysis available in literature is much less complete without (
4.3
). However, more is known if the solutions have bounded Morse index, as happens in our setting.

Following the classical approach, we define the singular set
S
by

S=\left\{x\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}:\exists\,x_{n}\to x\text{ such that }u_{n}(x_{n})\mbox{ is unbounded from above}\right\}.

The next theorem encompasses the blow-up analysis of
[
35
]
(which treats the case of a general surface, and possibly unbounded mass) and
[
29
]
(which is concerned with the case of the disk and the localization of the blow-up point).

Theorem 4.1

(Theorem 1.1 of
[
29
]
and Theorem 1.4 of
[
35
]
)
.

Assume (
H
), and let
u_{n}
be a sequence of solutions to (
4.1
), where the coefficients are given by (
4.2
), such that
\sup u_{n}\to+\infty
. Assume also that the Morse index of such solutions is bounded (see (
3.1
)), i.e.,
ind(u_{n})\leq m
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Then, passing to a subsequence if necessary, we have that
the singular set
S
lies on the boundary
\partial\mathbb{D}
and admits the decomposition
S=S_{0}\cup S_{1}
, where:

\begin{split}S_{0}&\subset\left\{x\in\partial\mathbb{D}:\mathfrak{D}(x)=1,\quad\partial_{\tau}\mathfrak{D}(x)=0\right\},\\
S_{1}&=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{j}\}\subset\left\{x\in\partial\mathbb{D}:\mathfrak{D}(x)>1\right\},\quad\text{with }j\leq m.\end{split}

Moreover, if
S_{0}=\emptyset
, then (
4.3
) is satisfied, and
S=S_{1}=\{p\}
, for some
p\in\partial\mathbb{D}
. In this case, there exists a sequence
a_{n}\in\mathbb{D}
,
a_{n}\to p
such that:

u_{n}(x)=u_{a_{n}}(x)+\psi_{n}(x),\quad u_{a_{n}}(x)=2\log\left(\frac{2\hat{\phi}_{n}(1-|a_{n}|^{2})}{\hat{\phi}_{n}^{2}|1-\overline{a}_{n}x|^{2}+\hat{k}_{n}|x-a_{n}|^{2}}\right),

where the error term
\psi_{n}
is uniformly bounded. The coefficients above are given by:

(4.4)

\hat{\phi}_{n}=\phi_{n}\left(\frac{a_{n}}{|a_{n}|}\right),\quad\hat{k}_{n}=K_{n}\left(\frac{a_{n}}{|a_{n}|}\right),\quad\hat{h}_{n}=h_{n}\left(\frac{a_{n}}{|a_{n}|}\right),

with the auxiliary function defined as:

\phi_{n}(x)=h_{n}(x)+\sqrt{h_{n}^{2}(x)+K_{n}(x)}.

The remainder of this section focuses on the bounded mass regime (
4.3
). Our main result establishes the following necessary conditions concerning the localization of the blow-up point:

Theorem 4.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
, suppose that
S_{0}=\emptyset
and let
p\in\partial\mathbb{D}
be the unique blow-up point. Assume also that either
\varepsilon_{n}>0
or
\varepsilon_{n}<0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, Then,
\partial_{\tau}\Phi(p)=0
and:

\mbox{ If }\varepsilon_{n}>0,\mbox{ then }\partial_{\nu}\Phi(p)\geqslant 0;\ \mbox{ instead, if }\varepsilon_{n}<0,\mbox{ then }\partial_{\nu}\Phi(p)\leq 0

The proof of Theorem
4.2
requires a refined analysis of the blow-up profile. As a first step, we improve the control on the error term
\psi_{n}
. Although Theorem
4.1
ensures that
\psi_{n}
is uniformly bounded, the following proposition establishes the sharper quantitative estimate necessary for our argument.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\psi_{n}
be the error term given in Theorem
4.1
. Then, for any
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
, the following bound holds:

(4.5)

\|\psi_{n}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}}\leq C(1-|a_{n}|)^{-2\alpha}\left(1-|a_{n}|+|\varepsilon_{n}|\right),

where
C>0
is a constant independent of
n
.

Proof.

We define:

v_{n}(x)=u_{n}(f_{a_{n}}(x))+2\log|f^{\prime}_{a_{n}}(x)|

with
f_{a}(x)=\frac{a+x}{1+\overline{a}x}
. Invoking
[
29
, Proposition 4.1]
, the following properties hold:

(a)

v_{n}
solves the problem:

(4.6)

\begin{cases}-\Delta v_{n}+2\tilde{K}_{n}|f^{\prime}_{a_{n}}(x)|^{2}=2K_{n}(f_{a_{n}}(x))e^{v_{n}}&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\
\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial\nu}+2\left(|f^{\prime}_{a_{n}}(x)|(\tilde{h}_{n}-1)+1\right)=2h_{n}(f_{a_{n}}(x))e^{v_{n}/2}&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{D}.\end{cases}

(b)

\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{1}e^{v_{n}}=\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{2}e^{v_{n}}=0
.

(c)

a_{n}\to p
, where
p\in\partial\mathbb{D}
is the blow-up point.

(d)

v_{n}
is uniformly bounded.

In view of
(d)
, the nonlinear terms in (
4.6
), namely
2K_{n}(f_{a_{n}})e^{v_{n}}
and
2h_{n}(f_{a_{n}})e^{v_{n}/2}
, are uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
. Moreover, since
f_{a_{n}}
is a conformal map, we have the identities:

(4.7)

\int_{\mathbb{D}}|f^{\prime}_{a_{n}}(x)|^{2}=\pi\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}|f^{\prime}_{a_{n}}(x)|=2\pi.

Consequently, all terms involved in problem (
4.6
) are uniformly bounded in
L^{1}(\mathbb{D})
and
L^{1}(\partial\mathbb{D})
, respectively. Here and in what follows, let us fix
q\in(1,2)
. Standard elliptic regularity ensures that
v_{n}
is bounded in
W^{1,q}(\mathbb{D})
for any
q<2
. Therefore, up to a subsequence,
v_{n}\rightharpoonup v_{0}
weakly in
W^{1,q}(\mathbb{D})
, strongly in
L^{q}(\mathbb{D})
, and pointwise almost everywhere.

Crucially, recalling property
(d)
, the sequence
v_{n}
is uniformly bounded. This ensures that the exponential terms
e^{v_{n}}
and
e^{v_{n}/2}
are uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
. Then, by the Dominated Convergence Theorem,
e^{v_{n}}\to e^{v_{0}}
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{D})
for any
p\geqslant 1
. Passing to the limit,
v_{0}
satisfies:

\begin{cases}-\Delta v_{0}=2K(p)e^{v_{0}}&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\
\frac{\partial v_{0}}{\partial\nu}+2=2h(p)e^{v_{0}/2}&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{D}.\end{cases}

Furthermore, the barycenter condition implies
\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{1}e^{v_{0}}=\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{2}e^{v_{0}}=0
, which identifies
v_{0}
, following
[
29
, Lemma 2.2]
, as:

v_{0}(x)=2\log\frac{2\phi(p)}{\phi^{2}(p)+K(p)|x|^{2}}.

Motivated by this limiting profile, we introduce the auxiliary sequence

\tilde{v}_{n}(x)=2\log\frac{2\hat{\phi}_{n}}{\hat{\phi}^{2}_{n}+\hat{k}_{n}|x|^{2}},

which corresponds to the exact solution of the Liouville problem with frozen coefficients (defined in (
4.4
)) at the concentration point:

\begin{cases}-\Delta\tilde{v}_{n}=2\hat{k}_{n}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}}&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\
\frac{\partial\tilde{v}_{n}}{\partial\nu}+2=2\hat{h}_{n}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}/2}&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{D}.\end{cases}

Observe that
\tilde{v}_{n}\to v_{0}
in
C^{k}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We also introduce the singular correction
s_{n}(x)
as the unique solution with zero mean to:

\begin{cases}-\Delta s_{n}=-2\tilde{K}_{n}(|f^{\prime}_{a_{n}}(x)|^{2}-1)&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\
\frac{\partial s_{n}}{\partial\nu}=-2(\tilde{h}_{n}-1)(|f^{\prime}_{a_{n}}(x)|-1)&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{D}.\end{cases}

Note that the compatibility condition for this Neumann problem is satisfied because the right-hand sides integrate to zero. Additionally, observe that, by (
4.7
), standard elliptic estimates yield the quantitative bound

(4.8)

\|s_{n}\|_{W^{1,q}}\leq C|\varepsilon_{n}|,

which implies
s_{n}\to 0
strongly in
W^{1,q}(\mathbb{D})
for any
q<2
.

Defining the error term as
\xi_{n}(x)=v_{n}(x)-(\tilde{v}_{n}(x)+s_{n}(x))
, it follows from the convergence results above that
\xi_{n}\to 0
in
W^{1,q}(\mathbb{D})
. Moreover, a direct computation shows that
\xi_{n}
satisfies the linearized equation:

(4.9)

\begin{cases}-\Delta\xi_{n}=2\hat{k}_{n}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}}\xi_{n}+c_{n}(x)&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\
\frac{\partial\xi_{n}}{\partial\nu}=\hat{h}_{n}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}/2}\xi_{n}+d_{n}(x)&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{D},\end{cases}

where the residuals are given by:

\begin{split}c_{n}(x)&=2\left[K_{n}(f_{a_{n}}(x))-\hat{k}_{n}\right]e^{v_{n}}+2\hat{k}_{n}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}}\left(e^{v_{n}-\tilde{v}_{n}}-1-\xi_{n}\right)-2\tilde{K}_{n},\\
d_{n}(x)&=2\left[h_{n}(f_{a_{n}}(x))-\hat{h}_{n}\right]e^{v_{n}/2}+2\hat{h}_{n}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}/2}\left(e^{(v_{n}-\tilde{v}_{n})/2}-1-\frac{\xi_{n}}{2}\right)-2(\tilde{h}_{n}-1).\end{split}

The objective is to estimate the norm
\|\xi_{n}\|_{W^{1+1/q,q}}
. To do so, we decompose the error as
\xi_{n}=\hat{\xi}_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{2}c_{n,j}Z_{n,j}
, where the component
\hat{\xi}_{n}
satisfies:

(4.10)

\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{j}\hat{\xi}_{n}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}}\,=0,\quad\text{for }j=1,2,

and
Z_{n,j}
are defined by:

Z_{n,j}(x)=\frac{x_{j}}{\hat{\phi}_{n}^{2}+\hat{k}_{n}|x|^{2}},\quad\text{for }j=1,2.

See
[
29
, Lemma 2.3]
for further details.

Our strategy consists of analyzing
\hat{\xi}_{n}
and
\sum_{j=1}^{2}c_{n,j}Z_{n,j}
separately. First, since
\hat{\xi}_{n}
solves (
4.9
) and satisfies the orthogonality condition (
4.10
), the linear estimates from
[
29
, Lemma 2.5]
apply, yielding:

\|\hat{\xi}_{n}\|_{W^{1+1/q,q}}\leq C(\|c_{n}\|_{L^{q}}+\|d_{n}\|_{L^{q}}).

To estimate the norms on the right-hand side, we first observe that

\|\tilde{K}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\tilde{h}_{n}-1\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C|\varepsilon_{n}|.

Furthermore, following the computations in
[
29
, Proposition 4.1 (e)]
, we obtain:

\left\|2\left[K_{n}(f_{a_{n}}(x))-\hat{k}_{n}\right]e^{v_{n}}+2\hat{k}_{n}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}}\left(e^{v_{n}-\tilde{v}_{n}}-1-\xi_{n}\right)\right\|_{L^{q}}\leq C(1-|a_{n}|)^{2/q},

and

\left\|2\left[h_{n}(f_{a_{n}}(x))-\hat{h}_{n}\right]e^{v_{n}/2}+2\hat{h}_{n}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}/2}\left(e^{(v_{n}-\tilde{v}_{n})/2}-1-\frac{\xi_{n}}{2}\right)\right\|_{L^{q}}\leq C(1-|a_{n}|)^{1/q}.

Combining these estimates allows us to conclude:

(4.11)

\|\hat{\xi}_{n}\|_{W^{1+1/q,q}}\leq C(1-|a_{n}|)^{1/q}+|\varepsilon_{n}|.

In order to control the coefficients
c_{n,j}
, we expand the barycenter condition
(b)
for each coordinate
x_{k}
, with
k\in\{1,2\}
:

(4.12)

\begin{split}0&=\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{k}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}}e^{s_{n}+\xi_{n}}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{k}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}}\left(1+s_{n}+\hat{\xi}_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{2}c_{n,j}Z_{n,j}+O((|s_{n}|+|\xi_{n}|)^{2})\right).\end{split}

Here we have performed a Taylor expansion of
e^{s_{n}+\xi_{n}}
. The validity of this expansion relies on the fact that the exponent
s_{n}+\xi_{n}=v_{n}-\tilde{v}_{n}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
.

In equation (
4.12
) we have that
\int x_{k}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}}=0
by symmetry. Moreover, thanks to the orthogonality condition imposed on
\hat{\xi}_{n}
,
\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{k}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}}\hat{\xi}_{n}=0
. Finally,
\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{k}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}}Z_{n,j}=0
if
j\neq k
, again by symmetry. Consequently, we are led with:

(4.13)

c_{n,k}\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{k}Z_{n,k}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}}=-\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{k}s_{n}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}}+O(\|\xi_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\varepsilon_{n}^{2}),\quad\text{for }k\in\{1,2\}.

Substituting the explicit expression of
Z_{n,k}
, the integral coefficient on the left-hand side satisfies the nondegeneracy condition:

(4.14)

\left|\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{k}Z_{n,k}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}}\right|=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\frac{x_{k}^{2}}{\hat{\phi}_{n}^{2}+\hat{k}_{n}|x|^{2}}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}}\geqslant C>0,\quad\text{for }k\in\{1,2\}.

On the other hand, since
x_{k}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}}
is uniformly bounded, we can estimate:

\left|\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{k}s_{n}e^{\tilde{v}_{n}}\right|\leq C\|s_{n}\|_{L^{1}}\leq C|\varepsilon_{n}|.

By (
4.13
) and (
4.14
), we can isolate
c_{n,k}
to obtain:

|c_{n,k}|\leq C\left(|\varepsilon_{n}|+\|\xi_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right).

Combining these bounds with (
4.11
) we obtain:

\|\xi_{n}\|_{W^{1+1/q,q}}\leq\|\hat{\xi}_{n}\|_{W^{1+1/q,q}}+C\sum_{j=1}^{2}|c_{n,j}|\leq C\left((1-|a_{n}|)^{1/q}+|\varepsilon_{n}|+\|\xi_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right).

Using the embedding
\|\xi_{n}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\xi_{n}\|_{W^{1+1/q,q}}
together with the fact that
\|\xi_{n}\|_{L^{2}}\to 0
, we get:

\|\xi_{n}\|_{W^{1+1/q,q}}\leq C\left[(1-|a_{n}|)^{1/q}+|\varepsilon_{n}|\right].

By the Sobolev embedding
W^{1+1/q,q}(\mathbb{D})\hookrightarrow C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
with
\alpha=1-1/q
, the error term satisfies:

(4.15)

\|\xi_{n}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}}\leq C\left[(1-|a_{n}|)^{1-\alpha}+|\varepsilon_{n}|\right],

valid for any
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
.

Finally, we transfer the estimates (
4.8
) and (
4.15
) to the original coordinates via
\psi_{n}=(\xi_{n}+s_{n})\circ f_{-a_{n}}
. First, the correction term
\tilde{s}_{n}=s_{n}\circ f_{-a_{n}}
satisfies:

\begin{cases}-\Delta\tilde{s}_{n}(x)=-2\tilde{K}_{n}\left(1-|f^{\prime}_{-a_{n}}(x)|^{2}\right)&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\
\frac{\partial\tilde{s}_{n}}{\partial\nu}(x)=-2(\tilde{h}_{n}-1)\left(1-|f^{\prime}_{-a_{n}}(x)|\right)&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{D}.\end{cases}

Again, by the Sobolev embedding
W^{1+1/q,q}(\mathbb{D})\hookrightarrow C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
with
\alpha=1-1/q
, combined with the linear estimates from
[
29
, Lemma 2.4]
, we have:

\|\tilde{s}_{n}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}}\leq C\|\tilde{s}_{n}\|_{W^{1+1/q,q}}\leq C|\varepsilon_{n}|\left(1+\left\||f^{\prime}_{-a_{n}}|^{2}\right\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{D})}+\|f^{\prime}_{-a_{n}}\|_{L^{q}(\partial\mathbb{D})}\right).

Observe that
\|f_{a_{n}}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}=C(1-|a_{n}|)^{-1}
. Now, interpolating between the
L^{1}
and
L^{\infty}
norms we get:

\begin{split}\left\||f^{\prime}_{-a_{n}}|^{2}\right\|_{L^{q}}&\leq\left\||f^{\prime}_{-a_{n}}|^{2}\right\|_{L^{1}}^{1/q}\cdot\left\||f^{\prime}_{-a_{n}}|^{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1-1/q}\\
&\leq C(1-|a_{n}|)^{-2\alpha}.\end{split}

Similarly
\|f^{\prime}_{-a_{n}}\|_{L^{q}(\partial\mathbb{D})}\leq C(1-|a_{n}|)^{-\alpha}
, so we conclude:

(4.16)

\|\tilde{s}_{n}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}}\leq C|\varepsilon_{n}|(1-|a_{n}|)^{-2\alpha}.

In contrast, for
\tilde{\xi}_{n}=\xi_{n}\circ f_{-a_{n}}
we can use (
4.15
) to derive:

[\tilde{\xi}_{n}]_{C^{0,\alpha}}\leq[\xi_{n}]_{C^{0,\alpha}}\,\|f^{\prime}_{-a_{n}}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\alpha}\leq C\|\xi_{n}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}}(1-|a_{n}|)^{-\alpha}.

Combining these bounds we can conclude

[\psi_{n}]_{C^{0,\alpha}}\leq C(1-|a_{n}|)^{-2\alpha}\left[(1-|a_{n}|)+|\varepsilon_{n}|\right].

To conclude the proof of (
4.5
), we only need to control the norm
\|\psi_{n}\|_{C^{0}}
, which can be bounded by
\|\psi_{n}\|_{C^{0}}\leq\|\xi_{n}\|_{C^{0}}+\|\tilde{s}_{n}\|_{C^{0}}
.
From (
4.15
) and (
4.16
), we conclude that:

\|\psi_{n}\|_{C^{0}}\leq C[(1-|a_{n}|)^{1-\alpha}+|\varepsilon_{n}|].

Therefore, the estimate for
[\psi_{n}]_{C^{0,\alpha}}
controls the full norm, yielding the desired result.
∎

Once we have an appropriate estimate on the error term
\psi_{n}
, we now plan to use Kazdan-Warner identities to obtain information on the derivatives of
\Phi
at the blow-up point
p
. The proof of Theorem
4.2
relies on the following three lemmas.

Lemma 4.4
.

\partial_{\tau}\Phi(p)=0
.

Proof.

Define the vector field
F:\overline{\mathbb{D}}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
by
F(x_{1},x_{2})=(-x_{2},x_{1})
. Observe that
F
is tangential to
\partial\mathbb{D}
and oriented in the same direction as the tangential vector
\tau
.

Applying Lemma
2.5
, and using the identities
\nabla\cdot F=0
and
DF(\nabla u_{n},\nabla u_{n})=0
, we obtain the following expression:

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\left(h_{n}e^{u_{n}/2}-\tilde{h}_{n}\right)\partial_{\tau}u_{n}=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left[\tilde{K}_{n}\nabla u_{n}\cdot F+e^{u_{n}}\nabla K_{n}\cdot F\right].

Integrating by parts and rearranging terms yields

(4.17)

-\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\tilde{h}_{n}\,\partial_{\tau}u_{n}=\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}2\,\partial_{\tau}h_{n}\,e^{u_{n}/2}+\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(\tilde{K}_{n}\nabla u_{n}\cdot F+e^{u_{n}}\nabla K_{n}\cdot F\right).

From Proposition 3.1 in
[
29
]
, the following

convergences hold:

\displaystyle u_{n}

\displaystyle\to-\infty,\quad\text{uniformly in compact sets of }\overline{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{p\},

\displaystyle h_{n}e^{u_{n}/2}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup 2\pi\frac{h(p)}{\sqrt{h^{2}(p)+K(p)}}\delta_{p},\quad\text{in the sense of measure supported on }\partial\mathbb{D},

\displaystyle K_{n}e^{u_{n}}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup 2\pi\left(1-\frac{h(p)}{\sqrt{h^{2}(p)+K(p)}}\right)\delta_{p},\quad\text{in the sense of measure supported on }\mathbb{D}.

Since
\tilde{K}_{n}
and
\tilde{h}_{n}
are constant functions, we obtain:

\tilde{K}_{n}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\nabla u_{n}\cdot F=-\tilde{K}_{n}\int_{\mathbb{D}}u_{n}\nabla\cdot F=0,\quad\tilde{h}_{n}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\partial_{\tau}u_{n}=0.

Substituting these limits into (
4.17
) and passing to the limit as
n\to\infty
, we obtain:

\frac{4\pi\,\partial_{\tau}h(p)}{\sqrt{h^{2}(p)+K(p)}}+\frac{2\pi\,\partial_{\tau}K(p)}{\Phi(p)\sqrt{h^{2}(p)+K(p)}}=0,

which is equivalent to
\partial_{\tau}\Phi(p)=0
.
∎

The estimate of the normal derivative of
\Phi
requires a more accurate description of the behavior of
u_{n}
. By using convenient rotations we can assume, without loss of generality, that
p=(1,0)
and
a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
and
a_{n}\to 1
. Then the profile given in Theorem
4.1
reduces to

(4.18)

u_{a_{n}}(x_{1},x_{2}):=2\log\left\{\frac{2(1-a_{n}^{2})\hat{\phi}_{n}}{\hat{\phi}_{n}^{2}(1-a_{n}x_{1})^{2}+\hat{\phi}_{n}^{2}a_{n}^{2}x_{2}^{2}+\hat{k}_{n}(x_{1}-a_{n})^{2}+\hat{k}_{n}x_{2}^{2}}\right\}.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
u_{n}=u_{a_{n}}+\psi_{n}
, where
u_{a_{n}}
is given by (
4.18
), then:

(4.19)

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\partial_{\tau}h_{n}e^{u_{n}/2}x_{2}=e^{\psi_{n}(p)/2}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\partial_{\tau}h_{n}e^{u_{a_{n}}/2}x_{2}+O(1-a_{n})^{1-2\alpha}\left(1-a_{n}+|\varepsilon_{n}|\right),

(4.20)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(\partial_{x_{2}}K_{n}\,x_{1}x_{2}-\partial_{x_{1}}K_{n}(1-x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})\right)e^{u_{n}}=&\\
e^{\psi_{n}(p)}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(\partial_{x_{2}}K_{n}\,x_{1}x_{2}-\partial_{x_{1}}K_{n}(1-x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})\right)e^{u_{a_{n}}}&+O(1-a_{n})^{1-2\alpha}\left(1-a_{n}+|\varepsilon_{n}|\right),\end{split}

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}x_{1}u_{n}(\tilde{h}_{n}-1)=\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}x_{1}u_{a_{n}}(\tilde{h}_{n}-1)+O(1-a_{n})^{1-2\alpha}\left(1-a_{n}+|\varepsilon_{n}|\right),

and

\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{1}u_{n}\tilde{K}_{n}=\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{1}u_{a_{n}}\tilde{K}_{n}+O(1-a_{n})^{1-2\alpha}\left(1-a_{n}+|\varepsilon_{n}|\right).

Proof.

The estimates in (
4.19
) and (
4.20
) can be obtained following the same computations of
[
29
, Lemma 5.2]
and making use of the
C^{0,\alpha}
bound for
\psi_{n}
provided in (
4.5
). The last two estimates are also immediate consequences of this bound.
∎

The following result completes the proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Lemma 4.6
.

If
\varepsilon_{n}>0
, then
\partial_{\nu}\Phi(p)\geqslant 0
. Instead, if
\varepsilon_{n}<0
, then
\partial_{\nu}\Phi(p)\leq 0

Proof.

We start from the integral identity derived in Proposition
2.6
:

(4.21)

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\left[4x_{2}\partial_{\tau}h_{n}e^{u_{n}/2}-2x_{1}u_{n}(\tilde{h}_{n}-1)\right]=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left[4x_{1}u_{n}\tilde{K}_{n}+e^{u_{n}}\nabla K_{n}\cdot F\right],

where
F(x_{1},x_{2})=(1-x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2},-2x_{1}x_{2})
. Applying the expansions obtained in Lemma
4.5
to each term and grouping the integrals involving the profile
u_{a_{n}}
, equation (
4.21
) becomes:

(4.22)

\begin{split}4e^{\psi_{n}(p)/2}&\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\partial_{\tau}h_{n}e^{u_{a_{n}}/2}x_{2}-e^{\psi_{n}(p)}\int_{\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{a_{n}}}\nabla K_{n}\cdot F\\
&=4\tilde{K}_{n}\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{1}u_{a_{n}}+2(\tilde{h}_{n}-1)\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}x_{1}u_{a_{n}}+\mathcal{R}_{n},\end{split}

where the remainder satisfies
|\mathcal{R}_{n}|\leq C(1-a_{n})^{1-2\alpha}\left[1-a_{n}+|\varepsilon_{n}|\right]
.

We analyze the asymptotic behavior of the leading terms. For the right-hand side of (
4.22
), as justified in Appendix
A
, we have the convergence:

4\tilde{K}_{n}\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{1}u_{a_{n}}+2(\tilde{h}_{n}-1)\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}x_{1}u_{a_{n}}=-\frac{10\pi\varepsilon_{n}}{1+\varepsilon_{n}}(1+o(1)).

For the left-hand side, following the computations in
[
29
, Prop. 5.3]
and identifying the boundary term resulting with the half-Laplacian (see
[
22
, Appendix]
), we obtain:

\begin{split}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\partial_{\tau}h_{n}e^{u_{a_{n}}/2}x_{2}&=2\pi(1-a_{n}^{2})\frac{\hat{\phi}_{n}}{\hat{\phi}_{n}^{2}+\hat{k}_{n}}(-\Delta)^{1/2}h(p)+o(1-a_{n}),\\
\int_{\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{a_{n}}}\nabla K_{n}\cdot F&=-2\pi(1-a_{n}^{2})\frac{\partial_{\nu}K(p)}{\hat{\phi}_{n}^{2}+\hat{k}_{n}}+o(1-a_{n}).\end{split}

Substituting these expansions into (
4.22
), we get:

(4.23)

\begin{split}\frac{2\pi(1-a_{n}^{2})}{\hat{\phi}_{n}^{2}+\hat{k}_{n}}&\Bigg(4\hat{\phi}_{n}e^{\psi_{n}(p)/2}(-\Delta)^{1/2}h(p)+e^{\psi_{n}(p)}\partial_{\nu}K(p)\Bigg)+o(1-a_{n})\\
&=-\frac{10\pi\varepsilon_{n}}{1+\varepsilon_{n}}(1+o(1))+\mathcal{R}_{n}.\end{split}

Since
\psi_{n}
is bounded, we obtain
|\varepsilon_{n}|\leq C(1-a_{n})
. With this information we come back to the norm estimate (
4.5
):

\|\psi_{n}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}}\leq C(1-a_{n})^{-2\alpha}\left(1-a_{n}+|\varepsilon_{n}|\right)\leq C(1-a_{n})^{1-2\alpha}.

Since
\alpha<1/2
, this implies
\|\psi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, and in particular
e^{\psi_{n}(p)}\to 1
. Using again (
4.23
) and recalling the definition of
\Phi
we have:

\sqrt{h(p)^{2}+K(p)}\,\partial_{\nu}\Phi(p)=\Phi(p)(-\Delta)^{1/2}h(p)+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\nu}K(p).

Thus, the equation (
4.23
) simplifies to:

-\frac{\hat{\phi}_{n}}{\hat{\phi}_{n}^{2}+\hat{k}_{n}}(1-a_{n}^{2})\partial_{\nu}\Phi(p)=-10\pi\varepsilon_{n}+o(1-a_{n}).

This concludes the proof.
∎

5.
Proof of the Main Theorems

Proof of Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
.

The proofs of both theorems follow a unified strategy. Let
(u_{n})
be the sequence of solutions to the perturbed problems obtained in Theorem
3.1
(with
\varepsilon_{n}>0
) or in Theorem
3.2
(with
\varepsilon_{n}<0
). Recall that, by construction, this sequence has uniformly bounded Morse index. We claim that it is also uniformly bounded from above. Once this uniform bound is established, standard elliptic regularity and compactness arguments guarantee that, up to a subsequence,
u_{n}
converges to a solution of the original problem (
1.1
).

Reasoning by contradiction, assume that
\max_{\overline{\mathbb{D}}}u_{n}\to+\infty
. According to Theorem
4.1
, the singular set
S
decomposes as
S_{0}\cup S_{1}
. Moreover,

•

In Theorem
2.1
, condition (ii) explicitly assumes that
\partial_{\tau}\mathfrak{D}(x)\neq 0
whenever
\mathfrak{D}(x)=1
. This implies that
S_{0}=\emptyset
.

•

In Theorem
2.2
, condition (i) implies that
\mathfrak{D}(x)>1
everywhere on
\partial\mathbb{D}
, so again
S_{0}=\emptyset
.

Therefore, in virtue of Theorem
4.1
, the singular set consists of a single boundary point
S=\{p\}
. Applying Theorem
4.2
, we have that
\partial_{\tau}\Phi(p)=0
and the sign relation:

\varepsilon_{n}\,\partial_{\nu}\Phi(p)\geqslant 0

This contradicts the geometric hypotheses in both cases: condition (iii) of Theorem
2.1
(if we choose
\varepsilon_{n}>0
) and condition (ii) of Theorem
2.2
(if we choose
\varepsilon_{n}<0
).

∎

Appendix A

This appendix is devoted to the computation of the integral limits used in the proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
u_{a_{n}}
be the profile defined in (
4.18
), where
a_{n}\in(0,1)
and
a_{n}\to 1
. Then

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{1}u_{a_{n}}=\pi\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}x_{1}u_{a_{n}}=4\pi.

Proof.

Using the explicit expression of the profile, we rewrite the integrand as:

\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{1}u_{a_{n}}=-2\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{1}\log{\left[\hat{\phi}_{n}^{2}|1-a_{n}x|^{2}+\hat{k}_{n}|x-a_{n}|^{2}\right]}.

Define the function
\tilde{u}_{a_{n}}(x)=-2\log{\left[\hat{\phi}_{n}^{2}|1-a_{n}x|^{2}+\hat{k}_{n}|x-a_{n}|^{2}\right]}
. As
n\to\infty
, the coefficients satisfy
\hat{\phi}_{n}^{2}+\hat{k}_{n}\to\delta
, where
\delta=\hat{\phi}_{0}^{2}+\hat{k}_{0}
.

Consequently,

\tilde{u}_{a_{n}}(x)\to-2\log{\left(\delta|x-1|^{2}\right)},

pointwise for all
x\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\{1\}
.

To apply the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we need to find an integrable majorant. Fix
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
large enough such that for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
,
\hat{\phi}_{n}^{2}+\hat{k}_{n}\geqslant\overline{\delta}>0
for some constant
\overline{\delta}<\delta
. Noticing that
\hat{k}_{n}
is negative, we can write
\hat{\phi}_{n}^{2}\geqslant\overline{\delta}+|\hat{k}_{n}|
. Combining this with the inequality
|1-a_{n}x|^{2}\geqslant|x-a_{n}|^{2}
(valid for
|x|\leq 1
), we observe:

\begin{split}-2\log{\left[\hat{\phi}_{n}^{2}|1-a_{n}x|^{2}+\hat{k}_{n}|x-a_{n}|^{2}\right]}&\leq-2\log{\left[\overline{\delta}|1-a_{n}x|^{2}+|\hat{k}_{n}|(|1-a_{n}x|^{2}-|x-a_{n}|^{2})\right]}\\
&\leq-2\log{\left[\overline{\delta}|1-a_{n}x|^{2}\right]}.\end{split}

Furthermore, employing the estimate
|1-a_{n}x|^{2}\geqslant a_{n}|x-1|^{2}
(valid since
|x|\leq 1
and
a_{n}\in(0,1)
), we obtain:

\begin{split}-2\log{\left[\overline{\delta}|1-a_{n}x|^{2}\right]}&\leq-2\log{\left[a_{n}\overline{\delta}|1-x|^{2}\right]}\\
&\leq-2\log{\left[\frac{\overline{\delta}}{2}|1-x|^{2}\right]},\end{split}

where we assumed
a_{n}\geqslant 1/2
for sufficiently large
n
. Since the function
\left|\log|1-x|\right|
is integrable over
\mathbb{D}
, this majorant allows us to pass the limit inside the integral.

We proceed to compute the limit integral. By symmetry, we have:

\begin{split}-2\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{1}\left[\log\delta+\log(|1-x|^{2})\right]&=-2\int_{\mathbb{D}}x_{1}\log{|1-x|^{2}}\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\rho^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos\theta\log(1-2\rho\cos\theta+\rho^{2})d\theta\right)d\rho=\pi,\end{split}

where we have used the identity from
[
25
, Equation 4.224(9)]
:

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos(\theta)\log(1-2\rho\cos\theta+\rho^{2})d\theta=-2\pi\rho\quad(\text{for }\rho^{2}<1).

The boundary term is analyzed analogously. Following the same dominance argument established above, we obtain:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}x_{1}u_{a_{n}}=-2\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}x_{1}\log|1-x|^{2}=-2\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos\theta\log(2-2\cos\theta)\,d\theta=4\pi.

∎
