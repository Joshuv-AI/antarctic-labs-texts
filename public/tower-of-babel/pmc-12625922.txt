Beyond capacity: an EAST multicenter mixed-methods study exploring surgeon perceptions on patient ratios in acute care surgery
Authors: Danielle J Wilson, Jaclyn A Gellings, Jamie Coleman, Kaushik Mukherjee, Stephanie Bonne, Melissa Boltz, Jennifer L Hartwell, Brandon Bruns, Jason Kurle, Moustafa Hassan, Samuel Rob Todd, Baila Maqbool, Bryan C Morse, Michael W Cripps, Mayur Patel, Daniel R Margulies, Jordan T Lilienstein, Negaar Aryan, Ben L Zarzaur, Charles V Bayouth, John Porter, Kristan Staudenmayer, Dalier R Mederos, Charles Fasanya, Kyle Leneweaver, Lewis E Jacobson, Michael Steven Farrell, Scott Norwood, John David Cull, Jason Hoth, Tovy Kamine, Kartik Prabhakaran, Ilya Rakitin, Michael S Englehart, Taylor Fusco-Ruiz, Benoit Blondeau, Charles A Adams Jr, Katherine McKenzie, Gerrit Holleman, Marjorie R Liggett
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOptimal provider-to-patient (PtP) ratios in acute care surgery (ACS) remain undefined despite their importance for care quality and provider sustainability. This study aimed to understand surgeon perspectives on maximum ideal ratios across trauma, emergency general surgery (EGS) and surgical intensive care unit (SICU) services.MethodsThis multicenter mixed-methods study combined quantitative surveys and semistructured interviews with ACS surgeons at level I/II trauma centers across the USA (1 August 2023–19 April 2024). Service line census data were also collected. Interviews were recorded, transcribed and qualitative analysis performed; surveys were analyzed with descriptive statistics.ResultsFifty-two interviews were completed. Survey response rate was 50.3% (212/421 eligible division leadership and faculty) from 40 centers across 24 states. The perceived maximum safe patient load for trauma and EGS was <20 patients when working independently, and up to 40 patients with full team support. SICU ratios were lower with most reporting ≤10 patients for independent coverage and ≤20 with team support. Regarding appropriate patient loads for junior residents and advanced practice providers, most respondents recommended ≤10 patients for trauma/EGS and ≤7 for SICU. For senior residents, most recommended ≤13 patients for trauma/EGS and ≤7 for SICU. Notably, 72% of centers exceeded their own leadership-recommended maximums for at least one service line. Qualitative analysis revealed patient acuity, team experience and competing demands as key workload modulators, with concerns about care quality degradation and burnout at higher ratios.ConclusionsThis study establishes potential upper threshold benchmarks for ACS PtP ratios with strong agreement across institutions. Division leadership should consider developing staffing models that account for patient acuity and service complexity while implementing escalation protocols for sustained high workloads. Current practices frequently exceed maximum ideal ratios, highlighting the need for evidence-based staffing guidelines that balance financial constraints with mounting evidence linking workload intensity and density to adverse outcomes.Level of evidenceIV.Keywords: workload; quality improvement; Practice Patterns, Physicians'; Delivery of Health Care

Introduction

Acute care surgery (ACS) integrates trauma surgery, emergency general surgery (EGS) and the surgical intensive care unit (SICU), which encompasses care for both injured and non-injured surgical patients. Given the urgent and emergent presentation of ACS patients, the specialty functions 24/7 and requires appropriate staffing at all times to deliver effective care. This necessitates well-structured teams capable of managing the inherent unpredictability of patient volumes, surgical case complexity and time-critical clinical scenarios.1 2 Although effective team performance depends on multiple factors, the provider-to-patient (PtP) ratio across all levels, including surgeons, advanced practice providers (APPs) and surgical trainees, is a fundamental component of adequate staffing. Despite the recognition of its importance, there have been no studies exploring optimal PtP ratios in ACS.

Staffing ratios have been explored in other healthcare professions, particularly nursing, where society guidelines and even legislation in several states mandate maximum PtP ratios.3 4 These guidelines are supported by nursing literature demonstrating that higher patient loads correlate with increased mortality, healthcare costs and burnout.5,7 However, research examining PtP ratios for physicians remains limited and primarily focuses on critical care settings where higher patient loads have been similarly associated with poor patient outcomes.8 9 Recognizing the importance of appropriate staffing for effective patient care, the Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM) has established recommendations, which also take into account length of stay, physician burnout and trainee education.10,12 The SCCM recommends that intensivist-to-patient ratios should not exceed 1:14 in closed academic medical intensive care units (ICUs).10

The need for staffing guidelines in ACS is particularly pressing, given that these services account for 20% of all inpatient hospital admissions.13 Similar to other critical care specialties, acute care surgeons are at high risk of burnout and depression. Risk factors associated with burnout, such as managing large numbers of complex patients and working long hours, nights, holidays and weekends, are common in ACS practices.14,17 This suggests that ACS, like critical care medicine and nursing, should explore the role of staffing ratios to optimize care and ensure workforce stability.

This study aims to address this gap by examining provider perspectives on optimal PtP ratios across ACS services. Through a mixed-methods approach combining quantitative assessments of perceived ideal workloads with qualitative insights, we sought to develop comprehensive staffing recommendations. We hypothesized that ideal PtP ratios would vary based on team composition, such as the degree of resident or APP support. This research represents a significant step toward establishing evidence-based staffing guidelines tailored to the demands of ACS practices.

Methods

A mixed-methods, multicenter study was conducted to evaluate the perceptions of ideal PtP ratios for trauma, EGS and SICU. This study was part of a prospective, multicenter trial examining the national ACS workforce.18 Participation was voluntary and included level I and II trauma centers recruited through the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma. Participants included acute care surgeons, trauma medical directors and division chiefs. The study was approved by the Medical College of Wisconsin Institutional Review Board (IRB) (PRO00036985) and informed consent was obtained for interviews. Participation in the survey implied consent. Data were collected between 1 August 2023 and 19 April 2024.

Quantitative data

A 12-question survey was iteratively developed for this study to assess acute care surgeons’ perceptions of appropriate PtP ratios across trauma, EGS and SICU services (online supplemental file 1). For this survey, trauma, EGS and SICU were treated as distinct service lines with separate patient counts and staffing considerations. This survey has not undergone prior reliability or validity testing; however, acute care surgeons piloted the survey before broader distribution to assess clarity and relevance. The survey was only open to acute care surgeons working at centers enrolled in the trial and was disseminated through leadership at each site. No sample size was precalculated. The survey was distributed via Qualtrics (Qualtrics, Provo, UT), with each participating center receiving a unique survey link. The number of surgeons eligible to take the survey at each site was known, and no responses exceeded the number of eligible participants. If no responses were received from a center, a reminder email was sent; however, individual participation was not monitored.

After collecting demographic data, participants were asked for their opinion on the maximum, safe number of patients for surgeons working with different team configurations. Patient load was defined as the total number of patients for whom a surgeon is responsible for during a shift, regardless of other concurrent clinical or non-clinical duties (ie, operating, clinic, teaching or administrative tasks). While only acute care surgeons were eligible to complete the survey, the term ‘provider’ was used as the study explored the maximum safe number of patients for APPs and residents as well. The different team configurations included a single surgeon without support staff, a surgeon with one junior and one senior resident, a surgeon with two APPs and a surgeon with one junior and one senior resident and two APPs. Additional questions assessed the maximum number of patients that a junior resident, senior resident or APP should manage on each service.

Participants had the option to provide their email address to volunteer for a follow-up semistructured interview. Surveys remained anonymous unless an email address was submitted. All questions were optional, allowing respondents to skip questions for services they did not participate in. However, surveys missing responses to an entire section were classified as incomplete and excluded from the analysis. Additionally, all centers completed a standardized data collection form, which included data on each center’s average patient census for trauma, EGS and SICU services.

Qualitative data

Semistructured interviews were conducted via a cloud-based secure audio/visual platform (Zoom; Zoom Video Communications, San Jose, California). Interviews were conducted by physician team members experienced in conducting qualitative interviews (PBM, JC, DJW, JAG). Interview participants consisted of two groups: (1) division leadership (eg, division chiefs or trauma medical directors) and (2) non-leadership faculty who volunteered through completion of the survey. If multiple non-leadership faculty from a single institution volunteered, purposeful sampling was used to select one participant to ensure diverse perspectives. Some of the participants may have known the interviewers prior to the interview.

Interview topics included ideal PtP ratios for various staffing configurations and factors influencing these perceptions. Interviews were transcribed verbatim using the video platform’s tool, with manual corrections made as needed. Transcripts were coded in duplicate by the study team (DJW, JAG) using a combination of deductive coding (based on predefined categories) and inductive coding (to identify emerging themes).

Analysis

Survey data were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables are reported as medians with IQRs. Nominal variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. The percentage of respondents selecting each predefined maximum patient range was calculated for each ACS service and team configuration. The ideal maximum patient load was defined as the highest number of patients endorsed by ≥50% of participants (ie, cumulative percentage of respondents selecting that load or fewer). Survey responses were unweighted, and no sensitivity analysis was performed. Comparisons by respondent age (<55 and ≥55) were conducted using a t-test, with statistical significance defined as p<0.05. Analysis was conducted with Stata (V.13; StataCorp, College Station, Texas).

The average census for each participating center’s ACS services was compared with the ideal staffing ratio provided by reported by division leadership during interviews.

Qualitative data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s deductive–inductive thematic analysis framework.19 20 Two researchers independently coded five initial transcripts to develop the preliminary codebook. Through collaborative meetings, they aligned their interpretations and established a consensus-based coding framework. The codebook evolved iteratively, and coding discrepancies were resolved through team discussion. Both deductive and inductive approaches were applied until thematic saturation was reached. Coding was conducted using MaxQDA (VERBI Software, Berlin, Germany).

Themes were systematically organized based on observed patterns. Regular analytical meetings facilitated collective review and refinement of emerging themes. All team members validated the final themes and representative quotes to ensure consistency with the original coding framework.

Results

Of the 51 centers that expressed interest in the study, 40 level I and level II trauma centers were included. Eleven centers were excluded for the following reasons: five division chiefs declined participation, five did not respond to follow-up communications and one was a level III trauma center. The included centers spanned 24 states and all five US geographic regions.

Survey response

The staffing survey was distributed to 40 participating centers, comprising 421 acute care surgeons. After excluding incomplete surveys (n=16), the response rate was 50.3% (n=212), including responses for all 40 centers. Respondents represented diverse demographics, including various age ranges and clinical ranks (table 1). Full survey results are available as onlinesupplemental files 2 3.

PtP ratios for the SICU differed from those for trauma and EGS services. When working alone in the SICU, most respondents (65%) endorsed an ideal maximum of 10 patients. When the team consisted of one SICU attending with either resident support (one junior, one senior) or two APPs, the maximum increased to 15 patients, as reported by 70.1% and 68.4%, respectively. For a surgeon with a full team (one junior resident, one senior resident and two APPs), 63.7% identified ≤20 as an appropriate maximum.

Regarding individual workload, we asked respondents about the optimal number of patients that should be directly managed (eg, physical exam, progress notes) by residents and APPs. Most respondents (73.5%) recommended junior residents handle 10 or fewer patients for both trauma and EGS services (table 3). For the SICU, 85.7% recommended seven or fewer patients per junior resident.

For senior residents, most respondents recommended maximum loads of 13 or fewer patients for trauma (71.7%) and EGS (70.3%). For APPs, the ideal cap was lower with most respondents indicated a maximum of 10 patients on trauma and EGS (59.4% and 58.3%, respectively). The recommended patient loads in the SICU were the lowest, with most respondents endorsing a maximum of seven patients for senior residents (59.2%) and APPs (65.9%).

When stratified by respondent age, those over 55 years were significantly more likely to report a lower ideal maximum PtP ratio for a surgeon with residents on both trauma and EGS services compared with those under 55 (p=0.03 and p=0.04, respectively). This was similar for a surgeon with a full team on both services (p=0.01 for both). Despite statistical significance, the mean responses in both age groups fell within the same ordinal category (eg, 11–20 patients). Conversely, respondents over 55 reported significantly higher PtP ratios in the SICU for junior residents (4–7 patients vs 1–3, p=0.01), senior residents (8–10 vs 4–7, p=0.02) and APPs (both 4–7, p=0.02). No other team configurations showed statistically significant differences by age group.

During interviews with ACS division leadership at 36 of the 40 centers, ideal maximum PtP ratios were discussed. Comparison of these recommended ratios to actual self-reported average census data at each center revealed that 72% of centers exceeded their leadership’s recommended maximum for at least one service line.

Maintaining quality of care

Ensuring high-quality patient care amid high patient censuses emerged as the paramount concern. Providers expressed that exceeding certain patient thresholds compromised care quality. One participant noted, ‘You start feeling like you’re rushing through stuff. You’re not paying enough attention to the things you need to’ (ST45). The impact was described as progressive, with later patients receiving less thorough attention than those seen earlier in rounds. This concern was particularly pronounced in critical care settings, where providers reported difficulty balancing quality of care with essential tasks such as family meetings and quality checklists. Similarly, nighttime care was identified as problematic, with single providers often managing all ACS patients, emergency operations and trauma activations.

Administrative and systemic constraints

Despite recognizing the importance of appropriate PtP ratios, surgeons identified several barriers to increasing staffing. A primary challenge was financial constraints, particularly justifying additional surgeons, especially for overnight coverage, when relative value unit (RVU) increases may not sufficiently offset salaries. Division leadership acknowledged the delicate balance of staffing decisions, with one leader candidly stating, ‘I always think that it’s better to be too thin than too fat. Exceeding our RVUs gives me leverage to ask for things. If I overstaff, I’m not prepared for a rainy day’ (ST52).

Additionally, surgeons expressed concerns about administration understanding the dynamic between surgeons and support staff. While APPs and trainees help offload patient care responsibilities, surgeons felt accountable for all patients, resulting in a practical upper limit to the number of patients they could effectively manage, even with increased support staff. In contrast, administration viewed each additional provider as increasing the total number of patients the team could manage, leading to a disconnect in expectation regarding workload capacity. There was additional nuance and contrast depending on billing practices in the group.

Acuity-dependent staffing

Participants emphasized that patient acuity played a critical role in determining ideal ratios. Many noted that raw patient numbers failed to capture workload accurately, as acutely ill patients could demand a disproportionate amount of a provider’s time, potentially diverting care from other patients.

This variability extended to system-level differences in patient populations. As one participant explained, ‘We have all ground-level fall traumas here so the acuity, once they are admitted, isn’t huge’, contrasting this with facilities seeing ‘high penetrating trauma, patients with high ISS (injury severity scores)’ (ST34), which necessitate different staffing models. Despite these differences, many agreed that PtP ratios and services should be structured with the needs of acutely ill patients in mind to ensure appropriate resource allocation and care quality.

Service-specific considerations

Participants highlighted key differences between EGS and trauma services that necessitate distinct staffing approaches. Many noted that EGS patients generally require a lower PtP ratio due to their higher acuity and complexity, whereas patients with trauma, once on the floor, often have more rehabilitation and social needs than ongoing medical concerns. However, some observed overlapping complexity explaining that ‘[On trauma] you have a lot of falls and things that are not that complex. But [on EGS] you also have appe[ndectomie]s and chole[cystectomie]s that are going home the same day’ (ST18). This variability underscored the need for flexible staffing models that can be tailored to each institution’s specific needs.

Balancing competing interests

Providers described challenges balancing operative and non-operative responsibilities, with one participant explaining, ‘Rounding on EGS with 30 patients, I can see them in a couple hours. It’s no big deal. But if I have 2 ex-laps and 3 chole[cystectomie]s, that’s a totally different ball game. Or if it’s a clinic day’ (ST11). This challenge was reported to be especially pronounced when surgeons are covering multiple services or responsibilities, requiring them to interrupt rounds for operations or trauma activations. Participants felt these competing demands led to inefficiencies and increased provider stress, emphasizing the need for staffing models that account for both clinical and operative workloads.

Support staff experience and availability

The experience level of support staff was identified as a key modifier of appropriate patient ratios. Experienced APPs were reported to significantly enhance efficiency, allowing for more manageable workloads. However, others maintained that attending surgeon presence remained crucial for each patient, regardless of APP experience. Additionally, participants highlighted that appropriate patient loads should be tailored to the experience and competence levels of both APPs and trainees.

Provider sustainability and burnout

Provider sustainability and burnout emerged as significant concerns when determining ideal PtP ratios. Some providers reported temporarily maintaining quality care at the expense of their own well-being, noting ‘Did we miss anything? No. Did I come home at seven o’clock, sit on my couch, and fall asleep? Yes, I did’ (ST9). Many expressed concerns about long-term sustainability, with consistently high workloads leading to exhaustion. Many felt that excessive patient loads created unnecessary stress for surgeons, ultimately impacting job satisfaction and overall well-being.

These themes underscore the complex interplay of factors affecting appropriate staffing ratios in ACS. While quality of care remains paramount, achieving optimal staffing requires balancing multiple competing demands within systemic constraints while maintaining provider wellness and sustainability.

Discussion

This multicenter, mixed-methods study provides the first assessment of acute care surgeons’ perspectives on what constitutes ideal maximum PtP ratios for ACS services. Consistent with our hypothesis, ideal PtP ratios depended on the presence and number of residents and APPs. We also found that, based on team composition, there was consistency in perceived maximum workloads across trauma, EGS and SICU services, despite variations in institutional characteristics and geography. For trauma and EGS services, most surgeons indicated that fewer than 20 patients were ideal when working independently, increasing to a maximum of 30–40 patients when supported by residents and APPs. The SICU ratios were lower, with the majority reporting an independent surgeon should ideally manage 10 or fewer patients and a surgeon with a team should manage no more than 20 patients.

Our prior work has focused on elucidating the workload in a full-time job for acute care surgeons.2 21 22 The complex interplay of work intensity and census loads in ACS remains understudied, despite its impact on quality of care as well as surgeon’s well-being and professional sustainability.89 14,16 Although electronic medical records readily capture patient volume, this measure fails to take into consideration patient complexity and the demands of clinical care. Our qualitative analysis revealed that patient acuity, APP and trainee experience, and competing clinical demands all significantly modulate workload. These findings parallel observations in emergency medicine, where factors such as acuity, documentation requirements, provider experience, ancillary staffing and the supervision of APPs and trainees impact physician productivity.23 The complexity of these factors is exemplified by patient acuity, which has resulted in the development of multiple scoring systems designed to capture and facilitate workload for critical care nurses.24

Several findings in this study warrant particular attention. First, participants often identified maximum SICU ratios exceeding existing critical care guidelines, with 30% of respondents indicating an ideal maximum PtP ratio of 1:16 or greater for a surgeon supported by residents or APPs. This rose to 67% of respondents who responded 1:16 or higher when surgeons had a full team complement. These perspectives exceed the SCCM recommended intensivist-to-patient ratio of 1:14 or less, which raises questions about whether inherent differences exist in surgical critical care patient management or acuity, or if acute care surgeons overestimate the sustainable workload based on their current staffing models.10 Another possible explanation for these differences is that the SCCM recommendations apply specifically to ‘closed’ ICUs, while participant responses may reflect experiences in either ‘open’ or ‘closed’ ICU settings.

Second, most ACS leaders believed their services were understaffed. A total of 72% of leaders reported that their PtP ratios exceeded what they thought was an appropriate maximum. Financial constraints were frequently cited as barriers to additional hiring. However, evidence from hospitalist and critical care medicine suggests that excessive workloads can decrease productivity, ultimately increasing costs by prolonging hospital stays.11 25 For example, increased APP staffing has been shown to decrease length of stay and hospital charges.26 27 Furthermore, the financial impact of burnout and the associated turnover is substantial, with replacement costs estimated at two to three times a physician’s annual salary due to recruitment costs, lost revenue and onboarding.28 Organizations may overlook this issue due to fragmented cost burdens. Medical schools often see only staffing expenses, while hospitals may not account for turnover costs. This siloed perspective leads to a narrow view of staffing decisions.

Our findings on maximum workload for APPs align with previous workload studies.29 Kapu et al reported that 57.4% of critical care APPs considered a workload of 4–8 patients to be reasonable, which is comparable to the 53.1% of surgeons in our study who identified an ideal workload of 4–7 patients for APPs in the SICU. Additionally, 51.4% of general surgery APPs in the study by Kapu et al reported a reasonable workload of 8–12 patients, though responses varied widely for trauma APPs.29 These findings provide valuable guidance for support staff workforce planning.

Quality metrics emerged as a major concern for respondents. At higher workloads, surgeons reported worsening quality. This concern is well-founded in other areas of medicine, particularly in nursing and critical care where increased mortality and failure-to-rescue are associated with increased volume of work.5,930 While some respondents reported maintaining quality under high workloads, they acknowledged the long-term unsustainability of such models. This suggests that short periods of ‘flexing’ to increased volume or acuity can be accomplished while maintaining quality, but that overextension leads to poor quality over time. Related, provider sustainability and burnout remain pressing issues in ACS with a known shortage of surgeons that is predicted to worsen in the coming decade.31 32 Physician burnout has frequently been linked to excessive workload, typically measured by hours worked.33 However, a study investigating post-traumatic stress disorder in surgeons found that overwhelming work responsibility was the most common traumatic stressor.34 These findings highlight the critical need for sustainable staffing models that balance workload demands with quality of care and provider well-being, ensuring both patient outcomes and surgeon retention in the long term.

Balancing patient safety and physician well-being with the financial realities of staffing an ACS Division represents one of the greatest challenges. One potential strategy is to have a ‘flex’ role for a surgeon who is available to help offload patient care during times of high census or acuity, or to offload operative/consult responsibilities to allow for an increased focus on the management of admitted inpatients. Divisions might also consider setting predefined census thresholds that trigger splitting the service into two teams when volumes remain at or above those levels for a sustained period (eg, a SICU census ≥15 for 3 months). Another approach is to use APPs practicing at the top of their license. Structured, team-based care models involving APPs have been shown to improve patient throughput, enhance continuity of care, reduce length of stay and lower readmission rates.35,37 While these strategies may require additional staffing expenditures, their potential to improve patient outcomes, reduce length of stay and decrease provider turnover may prove cost-effective in the long term.

Our study has several limitations. The identified ratios are based on expert opinion of acute care surgeons rather than patient outcome data, and numerous factors can affect each respondent’s perceptions of appropriate staffing. Relatedly, consideration should be given to staffing choices that emphasize long-term system sustainability. This survey was designed to explore perceived maximum safe workloads to help define an ideal upper threshold. We recognize that routinely working at this upper limit may negatively affect provider well-being. Therefore, future work focused on median daily census could better inform sustainable baseline staffing models, with flexibility to accommodate patient surges. Additionally, while we were able to stratify responses by surgeon age, we did not collect detailed information on years in practice or the proportion of clinical FTE, which may influence perceptions of safe or acceptable patient loads.

Selection bias may have influenced our findings at multiple levels: centers voluntarily participated in the trial, survey participation was voluntary, and interview participants were selected through purposive sampling. Additionally, self-serving responses may introduce bias as providers may advocate for more resources. Our focus on level I and II trauma centers may limit the generalizability to other practice settings, and large geographic areas remain unrepresented although all major regions of the USA were included. Local factors such as institutional resources, service model (eg, combined services), patient population and geographic considerations should be evaluated on an institutional basis. Our survey did not differentiate APP practice models, such as independent patient management and billing, which may affect ideal PtP ratios and workload distribution. Furthermore, the absence of perspectives from hospital administrators, APPs and residents leaves gaps in understanding broader systemic and trainee-related perspectives. When defining appropriate workloads for residents and APPs, participants were asked about the number of patients one could reasonably assess and write a note for daily, even though many senior residents take a more supervisory role. Finally, the presence and role of ACS fellows likely impact these ratios and warrant further investigation.

Despite its limitations, this study represents an important first step in understanding what providers consider ideal PtP ratios in ACS. The complexity of these ratios should not deter efforts to improve ACS staffing models, especially given the concern surrounding care quality and provider burnout. The strong agreement among respondents regarding appropriate ratios, along with evaluations of various team structures, offers preliminary insight. Still, critical questions remain and require further investigation. Notably, nearly three-quarters of ACS divisions exceed the perceived maximum ratios. Future research should aim to validate these ratios through outcome-based studies examining their impact on patient outcomes, length of stay and provider burnout. In the interim, division leadership should consider developing staffing models that consider patient acuity, service complexity and escalation protocols for sustained high workloads. Furthermore, professional societies could lead efforts to develop evidence-based staffing guidelines and quality care metrics tailored to ACS, with flexibility for local system differences.

Conclusion

This multicenter study establishes a framework for PtP ratios in ACS. Our findings demonstrate strong agreement across diverse settings: maximum ratios of 20 patients for independent surgeons and 40 patients for fully staffed teams in trauma/EGS services, with lower thresholds for SICU coverage. The stark contrast between these ideals and current practices—with 72% of centers exceeding their own recommended maximums—highlights an urgent need for systemic change. While patient volume provides a starting point, our qualitative analysis reveals that optimal staffing must account for patient acuity, team experience and competing clinical demands. Future research must validate these ratios through outcome-based studies while accounting for the complex interplay of factors that influence workload intensity.
