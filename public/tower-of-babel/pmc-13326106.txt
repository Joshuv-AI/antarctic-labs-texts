Protective and risk factors for caregiver mental health: a mediation analysis in a rural Chinese cohort of mothers and grandmothers caring for young children
Authors: Shanshan Li, Dorien Emmers, Boya Wang, Hanwen Zhang, Scott Rozelle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesConsidering the importance of caregiver mental health for early childhood development, this study investigates risk and protective factors of mental health of mothers and grandmothers caring for infants and toddlers in rural China.MethodsUsing survey data from 777 primary caregivers of children aged 5 to 25 months, we apply regression analysis and structural equation modeling to examine associations between social support, mental health literacy, parenting-related hardships, and mental health among mothers and grandmothers.ResultsThe study finds that 33% of the caregivers report symptoms of mental health problems, with grandmothers experiencing more severe symptoms. Poor caregiver mental health is associated with lower child language (p < 0.05) and social-emotional development (p < 0.001). Social support and mental health literacy are associated with better mental health, but this association was not statistically significant among either the mothers or the grandmothers alone.ConclusionsEnhancing caregiver mental health is crucial for children’s development. Social support and mental health literacy are predictors of mental health. Future research should examine the effect of improving social support and mental health literacy on the mental health of caregivers for young children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04622-2.Keywords: Mental health, Mental health literacy, Parenting self-efficacy, Social support, Parental stress, Chinese grandmothers, Grandmother caregiving

Introduction

Mental health issues are prevalent among caregivers of young children in low- and middle-income countries (henceforth, LMICs) [1, 2]. About 25% of the postpartum mothers in LMICs experience depression [3], often due to hormonal changes after childbirth. Parenting responsibilities can also lead to heightened stress and worse mental health, particularly when caregivers lack the adequate resources and support for parenting [4]. Mental health issues related to parenting affect mothers and other caregivers, including grandmothers who are primary caregivers for their grandchildren (henceforth “grandmother caregivers”) [5]).

Poor caregiver mental health also has profound implications for early childhood development (henceforth, ECD) [2, 6]. Research has found that caregivers with poorer mental health are less responsive to their children and, in turn, engage in fewer stimulating interactions with their children [6, 7]. Developmental delays due to insufficient stimulation in the first thousand days of life have been associated with lifelong differences in mental health and cognitive functioning [8–11]. Considering this, improving caregiver mental health is crucial for breaking the cycle of intergenerational disadvantage [6] in LMICs, where an estimated 43% (250 million) of the children under five years old are at risk of developmental delay [12].

A growing body of research has examined risk and protective factors of caregiver mental health in LMICs [1, 13]. For example, social support have been found to improve caregiver mental health [1], while perceived difficulties with parenting [14], parenting hassles [15], and low self-efficacy [13, 16] have been identified as risk factors. Additionally, mental health literacy, defined as one’s knowledge about and awareness of mental health, is a protective factor of mental health [17, 18]. However, interactions between mental health literacy and social support remains contested: While some studies suggest mental health literacy increases caregivers’ intention and ability to seek help from others and enhances their social support [7, 19, 20], other studies suggest that social support improves mental health literacy [21].

Grandmothers caring for young children are particularly at risk of mental health issues, yet it remains unclear whether this is associated with lower social support. A previous analysis of large-scale field data estimated that 40% of all caregivers of young children in China experienced depressive symptoms, of whom 38% were mothers and 52% were grandmothers [22]. Another study of 1787 caregivers in northwestern China found that grandmothers were 9.8% points more likely than mothers to experience depression [23]. In rural China, grandmothers often assume the primary caregiver role for children whose parents emigrate to cities for work. When compared to mothers, grandmothers’ social networks can be more concentrated within the family and the local community, limiting the number and diversity of supportive connections. Additionally, although many parents welcome the support from grandmothers [24, 25], grandmothers’ beliefs and practices related to childrearing can be inconsistent with the parents’ [26]. Intergenerational disagreements over caregiving can make grandmothers feel less supported by their family members.

Mental health literacy may also explain the disproportionately prevalent mental health issues among grandmothers caring for young children in rural China. While there is little empirical research on intergenerational differences in mental health literacy in China, mental health literacy often increases with experience: research conducted in the U.S. found that grandmothers’ mental health is buffered by their prior experience of raising children under financial strain and seeking emotional support [27, 28]. However, mental health literacy is also associated with education [29]. Considering the improved access to secondary and higher education in rural China in the past decades, mothers of young children today tend to be more educated than the children’s grandmothers, and they are more likely to have learned how to recognize mental health issues and seek help in schools. Collecting empirical evidence on this question helps illustrate whether the association between mental health literacy and mental health varies across generations.

Examining caregiver mental health in rural China will also inform policies to support mental health and ECD in low-resource contexts. Recent research found that 32% of all caregivers of young children in rural China experienced symptoms of depression, 42% symptoms of anxiety, and 30% symptoms of stress [30]. Meanwhile, approximately 40% of the children under age three experience delays in early cognitive, language, or social-emotional development [31]. Consistent with the international literature, poor caregiver mental health has been associated with poor ECD outcomes in rural China [23, 30]. While prior empirical studies [23, 30] have explored associations between caregiver mental health and demographic characteristics, little is known about whether parenting stress and parenting hassles are indicators of more generalized mental health issues. Similarly, examining how mental health is associated with social support, parenting self-efficacy, and mental health literacy can help public health practitioners identify caregivers at elevated risk of mental health issues and direct services to them.

The present study aims to identify protective and risk factors of caregiver mental health in rural China and describe their associations with mental health. We pursue four objectives. First, we describe the prevalence of caregiver mental health issues and the level of ECD in our sample from rural China. Second, we examine associations between mental health, parenting stress, parenting hassles, self-efficacy, social support, and mental health literacy. Third, we use structural equation modeling (SEM) to examine the pathways through which these factors may predict caregiver mental health. Fourth, we compare how the pathways differ between mothers and grandmothers, who together constitute most primary caregivers of children in rural China.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Stanford University (IRB Protocol No. 63680) and the Southwestern University of Finance and Economics in Chengdu, Sichuan, China. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Before conducting the survey, enumerators trained in the informed consent process introduced the contents of the survey to participants and obtain written informed consent. They explained the study risks and benefits to participants and answered any questions. Written informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants.

Study location and sampling

The data presented in this study were collected from six counties located in a mostly rural province in southwestern China. In 2022, this province ranked in the bottom half of China’s provinces in terms of per capita disposable income. After randomly selecting eight counties, we excluded two counties that were in mountainous areas and had low population densities. In the remaining six counties, we randomly selected 120 rural villages that met the following criteria: The village was in a township with a predominantly Han Chinese population (to avoid language barriers to survey administration), and it had more than 1,000 inhabitants (to ensure sufficient infrastructure and human capital for a planned intervention study). From the selected villages, we enrolled all children in the target age range (5–25 months) and their primary caregivers, identified by the household as the person most responsible for the child’s daily care. In all 777 eligible households we enrolled, the primary caregiver was either the grandmother or the mother. Of them, 690 completed the survey. Enumerators completed observational assessment of ECD in 607 of the 777 households.

Data collection

Data were collected for 777 caregiver-child dyads by trained enumerators in May and October 2023. From each child-caregiver dyad, we collected data on caregiver mental health, factors affecting mental health, ECD outcomes, and demographic characteristics.

Caregiver mental health

Caregiver mental health was measured using the 21-item version of the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21) [32], which consists of three seven-item subscales assessing depression, anxiety, and stress. The DASS-21 measures the severity of symptoms and is not intended for clinical diagnosis. The scale has demonstrated good validity and reliability, with a previous study in China reporting a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83 for depression, 0.80 for anxiety, 0.82 for stress, and 0.92 for the whole scale [33]. In each subscale, a higher score corresponds to worse mental health, and cutoff scores are established for mild and severe mental health issues [32]. We additionally construct binary variables for whether the caregiver experienced symptoms of depression, anxiety, or stress. For each subscale, the binary indicator takes on the value of 1 if the caregiver’s score indicates mild or more severe symptoms and 0 if otherwise.

Protective and risk factors of mental health problems

We collected information on five protective or risk factors for caregiver mental health: parental stress, parental hassles, parental self-efficacy, mental health literacy, and social support. First, we assessed parental stress using the Parental Stress Index short form (PSI-SF). The PSI-SF has widely been used to identify parental stress due to parenting roles [34, 35], including in China [36]. A higher total score corresponds to higher levels of parental stress. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha of the PSI-SF is 0.92.

Parenting hassles were measured with the Parenting Daily Hassle (PDH) scale. Caregivers indicated the extent to which they experienced a series of parenting-related hassles on a five-point Likert scale. A higher total score corresponds to more parental hassles. The PDH scale has been found to have good concurrent validity [37] and has been widely used in China [38]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha of the PDH scale is 0.93.

Parenting self-efficacy was measured using the “Tool to measure Parental Self-Efficacy” (TOPSE). Caregivers indicated the extent to which they agreed with a series of statements about self-efficacy on a scale of 0 (completely disagree) to 10 (completely agree). A higher score corresponds to a higher level of self-efficacy. The TOPSE has been found to have good reliability and validity [39, 40] and has been translated and validated for use in China [41]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha of the TOPSE is 0.92.

Mental health literacy was assessed using an adapted version of the Mental Health Literacy Scale (MHLS, see Appendix Part E.1 and E.2). Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale. A higher total score indicates better mental health literacy. Cronbach’s alpha for the subset of items used in this study is 0.73.

Perceived social support was measured using the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS). The MSPSS has been demonstrated to be valid and reliable across different cultural contexts, including in China [42]. All caregivers rated the level of social support they perceived from friends and family on a seven-point Likert scale. A higher total score corresponds to higher levels of perceived social support. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha for the MSPSS is 0.92.

Additionally, parental stress, parenting hassles, and parenting self-efficacy were modeled as indicators of a broader latent construct of perceived parenting hardship. Within this construct, parenting self-efficacy was reversely coded so that higher values reflected lower efficacy and thus greater perceived parenting difficulty. We estimated a dedicated measurement system to relate the observed measures to this underlying latent factor (see Appendix Part C). Because low self-efficacy is conceptually related to, but not identical with, parenting stress and parenting hassles, we also conducted a sensitivity analysis using parenting hassles alone as the mediator (see Appendix Figure D). To further assess whether perceived parenting hardship and caregiver mental health were empirically distinguishable constructs, we compared a one-factor measurement model with a two-factor measurement model separating hardship and mental health (see Appendix Table D.1). To facilitate interpreting and comparing scores from the different measures, we internally standardized outcome scores using the sample mean and standard deviation to obtain standard normally distributed z-scores.

ECD outcomes

To measure ECD, enumerators administered the cognition, language, and motor scales of the third edition of the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development (Bayley-III) test [43] to sample children, and the Brief Infant-Toddler Social and Emotional Assessment (BITSEA) [44] to their caregivers.

Children’s cognition, language, and motor development were assessed using the Bayley-III test. To reduce field survey costs, children were stratified by sex and age and randomly assigned to complete either the full Bayley-III test (n = 324) or a short form of it (n = 283, see Appendix Part B for details on the shortened test). The Chinese version of the Bayley-III test has been shown to have good reliability and validity and has been used extensively, including in rural settings [45–47]. The tests were administered by trained enumerators using standardized toy kits and detailed record forms. Prior to data collection, each of the enumerators attended a one-week training course on test administration, including 2.5 days of practical training in the field. We computed two aggregate scores: First, we constructed continuous factor z-scores using item response theory (see Appendix Part B). Second, in line with Bayley-III guidelines, we constructed binary indicators of developmental delay such that an outcome score of one or more SDs below the mean of a reference population.

Children’s social-emotional development was measured with the BITSEA. The BITSEA measures delay in competence as well as social-emotional problems. Primary caregivers were asked to indicate whether the child exhibited a series of behaviors often, sometimes, or rarely, or whether a series of statements about the child’s behavior were very true, somewhat true, or not true. Answers were scored 0, 1, or 2 points, with a higher score indicating lower competence or more social-emotional problems. We additionally define dummy indicators for delays in competence and social-emotional problems using cutoff values identified in a previous study in China [48]. The Chinese version of BITSEA has demonstrated adequate reliability and validity [49]. But since the scale was original for children between 12 and 36 months old, we conducted robustness tests to determine whether the BITSEA scores of children younger than 12 months were valid (see Appendix Figure A.2.1 and Table A.2.3).

Demographic characteristics

We collected information on child, caregiver, and household characteristics. Child characteristics include sex, age, whether the child was born premature, and whether the child was firstborn. Caregiver characteristics include the primary caregiver’s relationship to the child (i.e., mother or grandmother), educational attainment, age, and physical health status. To construct the family asset index, enumerators recorded whether the household owned each of the following 13 household assets (1 = yes; 0 = no): a flush toilet, a water heater, a range hood, a desktop computer, a laptop, a tablet, wireless internet, a LCD television, a refrigerator, an air conditioner, a motorcycle or electric bicycle, a sedan, and a van. The index was then constructed based on the records using polychoric principal component analysis and standardized as a z-score within the analytic sample. A higher score indicates higher household assets relative to the analytic sample.

Empirical estimation strategy

We conducted four steps of analysis. First, we describe the sample and compare differences in demographic characteristics between the mothers and grandmothers using two-tailed t-tests. Second, we examine whether caregiver mental health is associated with ECD outcomes using ordinary least squares (OLS), adjusting for child and caregiver characteristics as covariates. Standard errors were clustered at the village level. Third, we examine factors associated with caregiver mental health using OLS with clustered standard errors. Finally, we conducted structural equation modeling (SEM) in Stata 19 to examine the associations between caregiver mental health and the potential risk and protective factors. Specifically, we examined parenting hardship, social support, mental health literacy, and caregiver mental health within a path model. To assess whether perceived parenting hardship and caregiver mental health were empirically distinguishable constructs, we compared a one-factor measurement model with a two-factor model separating parenting hardship and caregiver mental health. To further address concerns about conceptual overlap, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using parenting hassles alone, rather than the broader parenting hardship construct, as the mediator.

Model fit was evaluated using standard indices, including CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR. The path coefficients shown in Figs. 1 and 2 are standardized estimates from SEM models adjusted for the child- and household-level covariates listed in Table 1. Details are provided in Appendix A.1. Sample sizes varied across child development analyses because some children did not complete the relevant developmental assessments.

Results

Socioeconomic and demographic characteristics

Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics of all sample primary caregivers and, separately, of those who were mothers of the child and of those who were grandmothers. In the overall analytic sample, the average age of the children was 14.9 months (range: 5–25 months). Half (52%) of the children were male. Just under half (45%) of the children were firstborn, and only 8% were born prematurely. A majority (81%) of primary caregivers were mothers; the remaining 19% were grandmothers. In total, 42% of the caregivers had completed senior high school or above, and about 65% reported being in good health. On average, the mothers had higher educational attainment and reported being in good physical health more often than the grandmothers. Some differences in household structure were also observed between the two groups (Table 1).

Prevalence of mental health problems

Our data show that 33% of caregivers had symptoms of at least one mental health issue (i.e., depression, anxiety, or stress, see Table 1). Specifically, 22% experience symptoms of depression, 25% symptoms of anxiety, and 15% symptoms of stress. Grandmothers experience depression (27%, versus 21% among mothers), anxiety (33%, versus 23% among mothers), and stress (20%, versus 14% among mothers) more often than mothers. Furthermore, grandmothers experience more severe symptoms than mothers (Appendix Table A.2.1).

In addition, we find that grandmother caregivers exhibit significantly higher levels of risk factors for poor mental health than mothers (see Appendix Table A.2.2). Specifically, grandmother caregivers have significantly lower mental health literacy than mothers (p < 0.001), and they report lower levels of social support (p < 0.05) and higher levels of parental stress (p < 0.001).

ECD outcomes

The results showed that 39% of the children experienced delay in cognitive development. A third (34%) experienced language developmental delay, and 21% motor developmental delay. In total, 58% of the children had at least one of the three types of delay. Among the 491 children over 12 months old, the BITSEA scores show that 28% experienced delay in development of social-emotional competence, and 18% displayed social-emotional problems.

Associations between caregiver mental health and ECD outcomes

Table 2 shows the associations between caregiver mental health and ECD. Overall, we found that children of caregivers with symptoms of mental health issues had worse ECD outcomes. Specifically, caregiver depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms were associated with an elevated risk of social-emotional problems and lower language development in children. A one-SD increase in the caregiver’s depression z-score was associated with an increase in child social-emotional problems by 0.27 SD (p < 0.01) and a decrease in child language development by 0.12 SD (p < 0.05).

Protective and risk factors for caregiver mental health

We explored the associations between parenting-related concerns, perceived social support, and caregiver mental health (Table 3). We found that lower social support was associated with significantly higher levels of depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms: A one-SD decrease in social support was associated with an increase in depression symptoms by 9% points, anxiety by 6% points, and stress by 5% points (ps < 0.05). Similarly, a one-SD decrease in parental self-efficacy was associated with an increase in depression by 9% points, anxiety by 7% points, and stress by 4% points (ps < 0.05). Parental stress and hassles were also associated with significantly higher levels of depressive, anxiety, and stress symptoms (ps < 0.001). Finally, better mental health literacy was associated with lower levels of depressive, anxiety, and stress symptoms (ps < 0.05). A one-SD increase in mental health literacy score was associated with a 4-percentage-point decrease in depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms, respectively.

Pathway analysis

Figure 1 presents the associations between social support, mental health literacy, and mental health. We used SEM to examine the extent to which their associations can be explained by the associations between social support, mental health literacy, and parenting hardships. The model fit measures indicate an acceptable fit (CFI = 0.943, TLI = 0.912, RMSEA = 0.056, SRMR = 0.032).

Figure 1 presents the estimated path model. Results indicate social support and mental health literacy are positively correlated (p < 0.001). Furthermore, we observe that a one-SD increase in social support is associated with a 0.32 SD decrease in perceived parenting hardship (p < 0.001), while a one-SD increase in mental health literacy is associated with a 0.16 SD decrease (p < 0.001). In turn, a one-SD decrease in perceived parenting hardship is associated with a 0.76-SD decrease in caregiver’s symptoms of mental health issues.

We separately estimated path models for grandmother caregivers and mother caregivers (see Fig. 2). Model fit was acceptable for both subgroup models. For grandmother caregivers, the corresponding values were CFI = 0.970, TLI = 0.954, RMSEA = 0.045, and SRMR = 0.035; for mother caregivers, the fit indices were CFI = 0.948, TLI = 0.919, RMSEA = 0.054, and SRMR = 0.029. In both subgroup models, greater social support was associated with lower perceived parenting hardship (=-0.25, p < 0.05 for grandmother; = -0.21, p < 0.001 for mother), and greater parenting hardship was associated with worse mental health (=0.58, p < 0.001 for grandmother; = 0.83, p < 0.001 for mother). By contrast, the paths involving mental health literacy were not statistically significant in either subgroup model. Although the coefficients suggested a somewhat stronger positive association between social support and mental health literacy among mothers than among grandmothers, this pattern should be interpreted cautiously. Notably, grandmothers on average had lower mental health literacy and perceived less social support than mothers (ps < 0.05, see Appendix Table A.2.2).

Because perceived parenting hardship and caregiver mental health may be conceptually related, we conducted a discriminant validity analysis (see Appendix Table C.3). We compared a one-factor measurement model in which hardship and mental health indicators loaded on a single latent factor to a two-factor model which separated parenting hardship and caregiver mental health into two correlated latent factors. The two-factor model fit the data substantially better than the one-factor model. Specifically, the one-factor model showed only moderate fit (χ²(9) = 60.17, p < 0.001; RMSEA = 0.086; CFI = 0.949; TLI = 0.915; SRMR = 0.044; AIC = 23899.19; BIC = 23982.99), whereas the two-factor model showed excellent fit (χ²(8) = 7.79, p = 0.454; RMSEA = 0.000; CFI = 1.000; TLI = 1.000; SRMR = 0.015; AIC = 23848.81; BIC = 23937.27). The two latent constructs showed a strong positive correlation (r = 0.739), indicating that they are closely related. However, the superior fit of the two-factor model suggests that they are empirically distinguishable.

We also conducted a sensitivity analysis using parenting hassles alone as the mediator between social support, mental health literacy, and mental health outcomes (see Appendix Figs. 1 and 2). The pattern of associations was similar to when we used the broader parenting hardship construct as the mediator: greater social support and higher mental health literacy were associated with lower parenting hassles, and lower parenting hassles was associated with better caregiver mental health. Notably, greater social support was directly associated with a 0.14 SD decrease in caregiver mental health (p < 0.001). Although conceptual overlap between parenting hardship and caregiver mental health cannot be fully ruled out, the results suggest that their association is not solely driven by conceptual overlap.

Discussion

Efforts to understand the factors contributing to caregiver mental health issues in LMICs can directly inform future interventions to promote engaged caregiving and combat developmental delay among young children. In this study, we analyzed the associations between several factors of caregiver mental health, including social support, mental health literacy, parenting self-efficacy, parental stress, and parental hassles. The study supports past research on caregiver mental health and ECD and illustrates how risk factors of mental health problems differ between mothers and grandmothers. Below, we discuss each of our main findings and their implications for future research.

First, our findings reveal a high prevalence of caregiver mental health problems, with more grandmothers at risk of severe mental health symptoms than mothers. In the full sample, we found that 22% of caregivers exhibit symptoms of depression, 25% exhibit symptoms of anxiety, and 15% exhibit symptoms of stress. Outside of the present study, few data currently exist on mental health among caregivers of young children in rural China after the COVID-19 pandemic. However, the depression rate among caregivers in our sample is comparable to that found among caregivers in a different part of rural China before COVID-19 (23.5%) [23]. The rates of caregiver anxiety and stress symptoms in our study are lower than those found by Zhang S et al. [23] in rural southwestern China before COVID-19 (42% for anxiety and 30% for stress); this may be because that earlier study was conducted in a region where natural disasters are common, which may have contributed to elevated rates of mental health problems.

Notably, caregivers who are grandmothers appear to face a higher risk of severe mental health symptoms than those who are mothers. The rate of mental health issues (depression, stress, and anxiety symptoms) was higher among grandmothers than mothers. Furthermore, within the subsample of caregivers exhibiting symptoms of depression, stress, or anxiety, grandmothers were much more likely than mothers to experience symptoms that were moderate or above. In terms of depression, this finding resembles Yue A et al. [23], which also showed that grandmothers in rural China were more likely to experience a greater severity of depressive symptoms than mothers. However, our study is the first to find that, among primary caregivers of young children in rural China, grandmothers experience a higher severity of stress and anxiety symptoms than mothers. Although grandmothers are primary caregivers in many households in rural China and other low- and middle-income countries, research on caregiving arrangements often only focused on the effect of their support on mothers. Our findings highlight the importance of understanding the experience and challenges faced by grandmothers caring for young children.

Second, our findings offer new insights into correlates of caregiver mental health problems, including social support, parenting self-efficacy, parental stress, parental hassles, and mental health literacy. Echoing past findings from rural China, we found that lower caregiver social support and higher parental hassles were associated with symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress [21, 23]. We also found that parental stress was correlated with a higher incidence of mental health problems, and parenting self-efficacy was correlated with a lower incidence of mental health problems. These findings are consistent with the international literature [14, 50–52] and complement the findings of Hong X and Liu Q [53] in rural China, which found that higher parenting self-efficacy was correlated with lower parental stress, resulting in fewer mental health issues.

We identified mental health literacy, which has rarely been included in past studies of mental health and ECD in LMICs, as a significant correlate of caregiver mental health. Our results showed that better mental health literacy predicted fewer depressive, anxiety, and stress symptoms. This finding aligns with the results of a study conducted by Ding KR et al. [54], which found that low mental health literacy is correlated to poor mental health among older Chinese people. Previous studies have highlighted the role of mental health literacy in mitigating the adverse impact of stigma on care-seeking [54] and that lower education results in lower mental health literacy [55]. More research is needed to examine the importance of mental health literacy and possible strategies to enhance it, particularly among rural caregivers.

Finally, our study is among the first to examine the pathways between protective and risk factors of mental health among caregivers of young children in rural China. We found that stronger social support was indirectly associated with better mental health through less parenting hardship, suggesting that strengthening social support systems could help caregivers respond to parenting hassles and reduce stress related to parenting. We also found that higher mental health literacy was associated with significantly less parenting hardship in the overall analytic sample, but this association was not statistically significant among either the mothers or the grandmothers alone. Considering the reduced sample sizes in these subgroup analyses, especially in the analysis of the grandmothers, the nonsignificant results may reflect reduced statistical power rather than substantive differences in this association across subgroups. Based on our findings, the positive association between social support and mental health is consistent across mothers and grandmothers in rural China, while the role of mental health literacy warrants more robust examination. Future research could use information interventions to establish the causal association between mental health literacy and mental health outcomes and determine whether this association varies by the caregivers’ relationship to the child.

Despite the contributions of this study, we also acknowledge four limitations of our analysis. First, as the data used in this study are cross-sectional, we could not draw causal conclusions on the relationship between caregiver mental health problems and ECD outcomes until longitudinal data are available. Second, the sample data were collected from only one underdeveloped area in rural China. While this area is broadly representative of rural China as a whole in terms of income and population, not all results may be fully generalizable to other settings. Third, our analysis only considered data from households in which the primary caregiver is a mother or grandmother and cannot offer insight into households where the father, a neighbor, or a different relative serve as the primary caregiver. Fourth, the association between parenting hardship and caregiver mental health should be interpreted with caution. Although our discriminant validity testing and sensitivity analysis show that parenting hardship and caregiver mental health were empirically distinguishable in our data, the two constructs were strongly correlated. The limited number of items measuring objective caregiving burden prevented us from fully separating it from subjective perceptions of parenting hardship. To avoid conceptual overlap, future studies could incorporate theories like the transactional model of stress to distinguish external stressors associated with parenting from symptoms of stress [56]. Practically, this involves designing more objective measures of stressors and distinguishing them from stress responses.

The prevalence of mental health problems among caregivers of young children poses a key problem for ECD and human capital accumulation in rural China. While the links between mental health and ECD outcomes are well known, our research reveals the importance of taking risk factors like low social support, perceived parenting hardships, and low mental health literacy into account when screening for mental health problems and designing interventions. Furthermore, we find that caregivers who are grandmothers may be at risk of more severe mental health symptoms than those who are mothers. However, the subgroup analyses did not provide strong evidence that the main pathways differed substantially between mothers and grandmothers. Well-being assessments for caregivers of young children could include measures of mental health literacy and social support as indicators of mental health.

Future research should examine the effect of improving mental health literacy and social support on mental health, particularly among grandmothers. In rural China and other low-income contexts, these interventions may strengthen caregiver social support through low-cost, community-based approaches, such as village-level caregiver support groups, peer discussion groups, or integration with existing maternal-child health and community service platforms. Although our results do not provide strong evidence that completely different intervention mechanisms are needed for mothers and grandmothers, they do suggest that implementation strategies may need to account for the primary caregiver’s relationship to the child. For example, interventions for grandmother caregivers may need to rely more on face-to-face and family- or community-based delivery, whereas mothers may be more easily reached through peer-based and parenting information channels.
