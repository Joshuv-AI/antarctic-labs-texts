Background

Synovial chondromatosis (SC) is a proliferative disease of the synovium and stands as a curious and often misunderstood anomaly. Despite its rarity with a reported incidence of 1 in 100,000, this condition, characterized by the formation of cartilage nodules within the synovial membrane of joints, can significantly impact joint function and quality of life, making accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment essential [1]. In this article, we present a case of unusual SC.

Presentation

A 63-year-old white woman presented to the rheumatology department with a 10-year history of mechanical right hip pain, progressively worsening over time, significantly impacting her mobility and diminishing her ability to walk. Despite multiple treatment modalities including analgesics, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and rehabilitation exercises, her symptoms persisted. Past medical history was notable for controlled hypertension, dyslipidemia, and a previous diagnosis of greater trochanteric pain syndrome, for which she had undergone multiple trochanteric bursa steroid injections. Her daily medication regimen comprised lisinopril, simvastatin, and naproxen (ranging from 500 to 1500 mg daily, on demand).

On physical examination, she had pain and restricted abduction and internal/external rotations of the right hip. No other abnormalities were presented. Pelvic radiography revealed signs consistent with right hip osteoarthritis, along with periacetabular opacities on the ipsilateral side (Fig. 1). Further investigation through hip computed tomography (CT) confirmed the diagnosis of SC (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Synovial chondromatosis (SC) is a benign, rare condition that manifests between the third and fifth decades of life [1]. It affects the synovial joints and is characterized by the presence of free cartilaginous bodies within the joint capsule. Although the etiology is not established, it is known that synovial membrane metaplasia leads to the formation and proliferation of cartilaginous nodules [1, 2]. The knee is the most affected joint; however, CS may occur in the shoulder, elbow, or, rarely, the hip [1, 3]. Diagnosis can be made using radiography. CT or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan are indicated if diagnosis remains unclear. Management strategies for SC vary depending on the extent of joint involvement and symptom severity. Surgical intervention, including synovectomy and removal of loose bodies, remains the mainstay of treatment for symptomatic cases. In cases where surgery is not feasible, symptomatic relief can be achieved through pharmacological management and physical therapy [1, 2, 4, 5]. Of note, malignant transformation rarely occurs [6]. Close monitoring and long-term follow-up are essential to detect disease recurrence and ensure optimal outcomes.

This case underscores the importance of thorough clinical evaluation and appropriate basic imaging studies in elucidating the underlying pathology. By raising awareness of this condition, clinicians can expedite diagnosis and implement timely interventions to alleviate symptoms and improve patients’ quality of life.

Conclusion

The patient exhibited multiple synovial chondromas, a condition uncommonly encountered. Synovial chondromatosis, although benign, may cause pain and functional disability with potential deterioration of the articular cartilage. Pain relief is the primary treatment, but if it does not lead to improvement, surgical removal becomes necessary.
