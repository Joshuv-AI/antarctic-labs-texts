Preoperative Urine Albumin-to-Creatinine Ratio Predicts Acute Kidney Injury After Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting
Authors: Ahmed M. Alkhunaizi, Jumana A. Al Naser, Khaled D. Algarni, Suhaib M. Ahmed, Amro M. Moubarak, Haralabos D. Parissis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjective:To prospectively evaluate whether baseline urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR) predicts acute kidney injury (AKI) and postoperative decline in kidney function in elective, isolated coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) patients and explore the magnitude of effect across clinically relevant uACR strata. Preoperative uACR is a marker of glomerular endothelial dysfunction and systemic microvascular injury that may identify patients at increased risk for AKI after CABG.Methods:In this single-center prospective cohort study, 111 adults undergoing elective, isolated CABG between January 2020 and December 2024 were stratified by baseline uACR (< 30, 30–300, > 300 milligram/gram[mg/g]). The primary outcome was AKI defined by Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes creatinine criteria. Secondary outcomes included peak postoperative creatinine, 6-month estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), ventilator duration, and hospital length of stay. Regression models were adjusted for diabetes, hypertension, body mass index, and baseline eGFR. Log-transformations were applied to skewed variables to improve model performance.Results:The AKI incidence increased across uACR strata: 14.1% (< 30 mg/g), 23.5% (30–300 mg/g), and 66.7% (> 300 mg/g). In adjusted logistic regression (n = 109), each doubling of log-transformed uACR was associated with higher AKI odds (odds ratio 1.44; 95% confidence level 1.03–2.01). Macroalbuminuria (>300 mg/g) conferred a sixfold increase in AKI risk compared with <30 mg/g. Macroalbuminuric patients experienced a 0.75 mg/dL greater rise in postoperative creatinine and a 16.2 mL/min/1.73 m2 larger decline in 6-month eGFR. No significant associations were observed with ventilator duration or hospital stay.Conclusion:In this single center cohort, elevated preoperative uACR, particularly in the macroalbuminuric range, was associated with AKI and early renal dysfunction after CABG and remained an independent predictor after adjustment. Routine preoperative uACR measurement may aid risk stratification and identify patients for nephroprotective strategies; however, these findings require confirmation in larger, adequately powered studies.

Introduction

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) remains a cornerstone intervention for patients with advanced coronary artery disease, offering significant improvements in survival and quality of life [1]. However, postoperative acute kidney injury (AKI) occurs in up to 30% of CABG patients and is associated with increased short- and long-term mortality, prolonged intensive care stays, and greater healthcare costs [2,3,4,5].

Traditional clinical risk factors such as advanced age, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and reduced baseline glomerular filtration rate (GFR) have been incorporated into predictive models for AKI, yet their sensitivity and specificity remain suboptimal for early risk stratification in the perioperative setting [6,7,8,9].

Albuminuria, quantified by the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR), has emerged as a sensitive marker of glomerular endothelial dysfunction and systemic microvascular injury [10,11]. Elevated uACR levels, even within the microalbuminuric range, independently predict cardiovascular events, progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD), and all-cause mortality in diverse populations with preserved or reduced renal function [10,12,13]. A growing body of evidence suggests that preoperative albuminuria is associated with heightened risk of AKI following major surgery [14,15].

The pathophysiological link between albuminuria and AKI likely reflects shared mechanisms of endothelial injury, oxidative stress, and impaired microcirculatory autoregulation during ischemia reperfusion.

The prognostic importance of AKI in cardiac surgery has been highlighted in recent publications. Luckraz et al. [16] demonstrated that balanced forced diuresis reduced AKI incidence in patients undergoing cardiopulmonary bypass, while Kalisnik et al. [17] applied artificial intelligence algorithms for early detection of AKI following cardiac surgery. Our study adds to this growing body of work by evaluating preoperative uACR as a simple, noninvasive biomarker to identify CABG patients at heightened risk of postoperative AKI. We hypothesize that elevated preoperative uACR, particularly in the macroalbuminuric range (>300 milligram/gram [mg/g]), independently predicts postoperative AKI and sustained declines in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).

Methods

This was a prospective, single-center cohort study involving consecutive patients undergoing elective, isolated CABG between January 2020 and December 2024. Patients were followed from the time of surgery through postoperative recovery and up to 6 months after discharge. Survival status and attendance at the 6-month follow-up visit were recorded. Of the 111 patients enrolled, 109 patients had complete covariate data, and 107 patients had complete 6-month follow-up data; 4 patients did not have follow-up values recorded, either due to loss to follow-up or death, and were excluded from analyses of secondary outcomes.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Johns Hopkins Aramco Healthcare (Protocol #19-41, 2019). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection and measurements. Data were collected prospectively using standardized case report forms. Baseline demographics (age, sex, body mass index, smoking status) and comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension) were documented. Preoperative laboratory assessments included serum creatinine, urine albumin, and urine creatinine for calculation of the uACR, as well as eGFR, calculated using the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology (CKD-EPI) creatinine equation [18].

Spot urine samples were collected preoperatively (within 7 days of surgery) and processed per institutional laboratory standards. Urine albumin was measured by immunoturbidimetric assay and urine creatinine by enzymatic method; uACR was calculated as mg albumin per gram g creatinine. Laboratory quality control procedures and assay coefficients of variation were consistent with routine clinical practice.

Serum creatinine was measured at predefined intervals: preoperatively (baseline), 48 hours after surgery, at hospital discharge, and at 6 months follow-up. Additional measurements during hospitalization were obtained at the discretion of the treating clinical team.

Peak postoperative serum creatinine was defined as the highest serum creatinine measured during the index hospitalization among the preoperative baseline, the 48-hour postoperative sample, and the discharge sample. For primary AKI classification, we applied Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) creatinine criteria using the 48-hour and 7-day windows as appropriate. Sensitivity analyses used: i) the 48-hour postoperative creatinine only ii) and the discharge creatinine only to assess the robustness of associations.

At hospital discharge, in addition to serum creatinine, perioperative outcomes including duration of mechanical ventilation, intensive care unit length of stay, and total hospital length of stay were recorded. Because eGFR equations are unreliable during acute changes in serum creatinine, eGFR was calculated using the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation for baseline and 6-month follow-up assessments to evaluate long-term renal function.

The primary outcome was AKI, defined according to KDIGO criteria as either i) an increase in serum creatinine ≥ 0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours of surgery compared to baseline, ii) or an increase to ≥ 1.5 times baseline within 7 days postoperatively [19]. Secondary outcomes included peak postoperative serum creatinine, follow-up eGFR value at 6 months, duration of mechanical ventilation, and total hospital length of stay. Patients without 6-month follow-up data were excluded from analyses of secondary outcomes.

Statistical analysis. Baseline characteristics were stratified by uACR categories (< 30, 30–300, > 300 mg/g) for descriptive purposes. Continuous variables were summarized using means ± standard deviation (SD) when approximately normally distributed (age, body mass index [BMI]), and as medians [min–max] when skewed (serum creatinine, eGFR, uACR). Categorical variables were presented as counts (%). Differences across uACR categories were assessed using Analysis of Variance for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

For regression analyses, uACR was treated primarily as a continuous variable after log-transformation to account for skewed distribution and to maximize statistical power. Logistic regression was used to evaluate associations between log-transformed uACR and AKI, while linear regression was applied to postoperative outcomes (peak postoperative creatinine, follow-up eGFR value at 6 months, log-transformed ventilator hours, and log-transformed hospital length of stay). Categorical uACR strata (< 30, 30–300, > 300 mg/g) were additionally analyzed in regression models to illustrate dose-response relationships, though we acknowledge the limited sample size in the macroalbuminuria group. We used log-transformations for uACR and other skewed outcomes because a few patients had extremely high values; transforming them made the data more balanced and allowed the statistical models to work properly, while results are still presented in the original units for easier clinical interpretation. Analyses were restricted to patients with complete covariate data, resulting in n = 109 for logistic regression models.

All models were adjusted for diabetes, hypertension, body mass index, and baseline eGFR. Model fit was assessed using McFadden's Pseudo-R2 for logistic models and adjusted R2 for linear models. All analyses were performed using RStudio Version 2023.06.0+421.

Of 111 enrolled patients, 107 had complete 6-month follow-up; 4 were excluded from secondary outcome analyses due to loss to follow-up or death. Analyses of the primary outcome included patients with complete covariate data (n = 109). Missing covariate data were minimal.

Intraoperative metrics including cardiopulmonary bypass time, aortic cross-clamp time and intraoperative hemodynamic measures were not consistently recorded in the electronic dataset, and therefore were not available for inclusion in multivariable models.

Results

A total of 111 consecutive patients undergoing elective, isolated CABG were included in the analysis. Baseline demographics were broadly similar across uACR categories, while comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension tended to be more frequent in patients with higher uACR values. Kidney function parameters showed clear differences across groups (Table 1). Mean age ranged from 61.9 to 63.2 years and mean BMI from 28.6 to 29.4 kg/m2, with no significant differences. The prevalence of diabetes was 67.1% in the < 30 mg/g group, 82.4% in the 30–300 mg/g group, and 77.8% in the > 300 mg/g group. Hypertension was present in 81.2%, 94.1%, and 100% of patients in the < 30, 30–300, and > 300 mg/g groups. Baseline serum creatinine was significantly higher in the > 300 mg/g group (1.34 ± 0.67 mg/dL) compared with the < 30 mg/g group (0.83 ± 0.23 mg/dL; p = 0.0423).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study of 111 patients undergoing elective, isolated CABG, we found that elevated preoperative uACR, particularly macroalbuminuria, was an independent predictor of postoperative kidney dysfunction and early decline in renal function. Patients with macroalbuminuria (uACR > 300 mg/g) experienced a 6-fold higher adjusted odds of AKI, significantly higher peak postoperative creatinine, and a greater decline in eGFR at 6 months. In contrast, uACR in the microalbuminuric range (30–300 mg/g) showed only a nonsignificant trend toward worsened renal outcomes.

The absence of association between preoperative uACR and ventilator hours or hospital length of stay likely reflects the multifactorial determinants of these outcomes (cardiac and pulmonary status, intraoperative events, and discharge logistics) and the limited sample size in the macroalbuminuria subgroup.

Because of the relatively small number of patients with macroalbuminuria (> 300 mg/g), categorical analyses should be interpreted with caution. We therefore emphasize the continuous log-transformed analysis as the primary and more statistically robust finding, with categorical stratification used to illustrate dose-response trends. Our findings support and extend earlier work linking preoperative albuminuria to AKI risk in cardiac and non-cardiac surgical populations.

There is a growing body of evidence supporting an association between pre-existing proteinuria and the development of AKI. Recent observational, cohort, and registry studies consistently showed a graded, independent association between preoperative or preexisting proteinuria (from trace dipstick findings to macroalbuminuria) and subsequent AKI risk, including hospital-acquired and post-cardiac surgery AKI, even after adjusting for baseline renal function and comorbidities [14,20,21].

Grams et al. [22] reported that albuminuria was associated with AKI after adjusting for kidney function and comorbid conditions. In that study there was a dose-response relation with an adjusted relative hazard of 1.9, 2.2, and 4.8 for subclinical albuminuria, microalbuminuria, and macroalbuminuria compared with no albuminuria.

In a prospective cohort of 616 cardiac surgery patients, higher preoperative serum cystatin C and dipstick-detected heavy proteinuria each independently predicted postoperative AKI [23].

A meta-analysis of 11 observational studies totaling 203,987 patients found that preoperative proteinuria independently predicts postoperative AKI (adjusted OR 1.65), with both mild (OR 1.30) and heavy proteinuria (OR 1.93) conferring increased risk. Dipstick-detected proteinuria (OR 1.48) and its presence in both cardiac (OR 1.69) and non-cardiac (OR 2.06) surgery cohorts further confirmed its prognostic value [24].

From a pathophysiological standpoint, albuminuria reflects both increased glomerular permeability and widespread microvascular injury. Endothelial glycocalyx disruption, oxidative stress, and inflammatory cytokine release during cardiopulmonary bypass may accentuate this vulnerability, leading to greater tubular injury and sustained eGFR decline [25]. Moreover, albuminuria itself can promote tubular toxicity and interstitial fibrosis, potentially compounding the impact of perioperative hemodynamic perturbations [26].

While our study provides valuable insights into the predictive role of preoperative uACR for AKI following CABG, we acknowledge the relatively small sample size and the single-center design as a limitation. The small cohort size, particularly in the macroalbuminuria subgroup, may reduce the precision of effect estimates and limit generalizability. However, the prospective design, rigorous data collection, and comprehensive statistical adjustments, including log transformations and multivariable regression models, strengthen the validity of our findings despite this constraint.

Additional limitation of our study is the absence of detailed intraoperative data (cardiopulmonary bypass time, aortic cross-clamp time, and continuous intraoperative hemodynamics) for the full cohort. These intraoperative exposures are established contributors to cardiac surgery-associated AKI and could confound the observed associations. We adjusted for baseline kidney function and major comorbidities to mitigate confounding, and we acknowledge residual confounding by unmeasured intraoperative factors remains possible. Our findings are hypothesis-generating and indicate that preoperative uACR could be a useful biomarker for perioperative risk stratification; however, larger, multicenter studies are needed to confirm this potential Future research directions and clinical recommendations. To translate our findings into practice, larger multicenter studies should validate uACR thresholds and integrate uACR into refined risk models while randomized interventional trials test whether targeted nephroprotective bundles (optimized hemodynamic protocols, avoidance of nephrotoxins, and perioperative renoprotective strategies) reduce AKI in albuminuric patients; parallel work should evaluate multimarker panels combining uACR with tubular injury and inflammatory biomarkers such as Neutrophil Gelatinase-associated Lipocalin and Kidney Injury Molecule-1, pursue mechanistic studies of endothelial and tubular pathways, and extend follow-up to assess progression to CKD and cardiovascular outcomes. Clinically, routine measurement of uACR preoperatively may be recommended to identify at-risk patients, with macroalbuminuria (> 300 mg/g) flagged for enhanced perioperative vigilance, early nephrology involvement, and optimization of modifiable factors such as blood pressure, glycemic control, and volume status. Embedding uACR in surgical registries will facilitate larger observational and interventional efforts and help determine whether uACR-guided care improves renal and clinical outcomes.

In conclusion, elevated preoperative uACR was associated with AKI and early renal function decline after elective, isolated CABG in our cohort; however, given the small sample size, these results are preliminary. After adjustment for clinical covariates, uACR remained an independent predictor. Incorporating uACR into preoperative assessment may improve risk stratification and help identify patients for targeted nephroprotective strategies. Larger, adequately powered observational studies and randomized interventional trials are warranted to validate these findings and to determine whether perioperative management guided by uACR improves renal and clinical outcomes.
