A Variational Framework for the Algorithmic Complexity of PDE Solutions
Juan Esteban Suarez Cardona, Holger Boche, Gitta Kutyniok

Abstract
Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) are fundamental tools for modeling physical phenomena, yet most PDEs of practical interest cannot be solved analytically and require numerical approximations. The feasibility of such numerical methods, however, is ultimately constrained by the limitations of existing computation models. Since digital computers constitute the primary physical realizations of numerical computation, and Turing machines define their theoretical limits, the question of Turing computability of PDE solutions arises as a problem of fundamental theoretical significance. The Turing computability of PDE solutions, provides a rigorous framework to distinguish equations that are, in principle, algorithmically solvable from those that inherently encode undecidable or non-computable behavior. Once computability is established, complexity theory extends the analysis by quantifying the computational resources required to approximate the corresponding PDE solutions. In this work, we present a novel framework based on least-squares variational formulations and their associated gradient flows to study the computability and complexity of PDE solutions from an optimization perspective. Our approach enables the approximation of PDE solution operators via discrete gradient flows, linking structural properties of the PDE—such as coercivity, ellipticity, and convexity—to the inherent complexity of their solutions. This framework characterizes both regimes where PDEs admit effective numerical solvers in polynomial-time and those exhibiting complexity blowup, where the input data possess polynomial-time complexity, yet the solution itself scales super-polynomially. In summary, this paper establishes a general theoretical framework for understanding the limits of digital computation in solving both linear and nonlinear PDEs and provides constructive insights for the development of effective numerical methods.

Introduction

For centuries, the modeling and description of physical phenomena have relied on the formulation and analysis of Partial Differential Equations (PDEs). While classical analytical methods have provided closed-form solutions for a limited number of cases, the vast majority of physically relevant PDEs require numerical methods to approximate their solutions and simulate the underlying systems. Entire areas within scientific computing and numerical analysis focus on designing and analyzing these numerical methods, ensuring the well-posedness of the problem, and the accuracy and efficiency of the resulting algorithms. Yet, the fundamental question of Turing computability
Church1937-sr
of PDE solutions has only been addressed to a limited extent. In this context, the computability question is, however, relevant because digital computers are the only hardware platforms for numerical calculations, with Turing machines defining their theoretical limits. Thus, tasks that are intractable for Turing machines are necessarily more challenging for digital computers in practice. The central problem is to determine whether the infinite-dimensional solutions of PDEs can, in principle, be approximated effectively on a digital machine and, if so, what computational resources are inherently required. Within the framework of computability analysis and complexity theory, we can distinguish PDEs that are effectively solvable from those that may encode undecidable behavior and quantify the efficiency of approximation when computability is assured. Therefore, addressing the computability and complexity of PDE solutions not only clarifies the limits of current digital hardware for solving PDEs but also highlights classes of equations where novel computational architectures
Handler1967-ty
and new notions of computability may be necessary to ensure feasible algorithms for solving such PDEs.

Classical analyzes of computability and complexity of PDEs have primarily addressed equations admitting closed-form solutions
Pour-El1997-br
;
Weihrauch2006-cp
;
bacho2023complexityblowupsolutionslaplace
. However, for most PDEs arising in real-world applications, such explicit solutions are unavailable
Evans2022-ig
;
Trefethen2000-su
, motivating the development of new frameworks to study their computability and complexity. Recent advances in variational methods, especially least-squares formulations
Los2023-jo
;
Cai2020-js
, offer a flexible framework that has already shown success in nonlinear, high-dimensional, and even discontinuous problems
Hu2024-sy
;
Cardona2023-ma
;
Suarez2025
.

In this work, we leverage the gradient flow associated with least-squares variational formulations
Ghoussoub2004-ou
;
Ambrosio2008-qd
to develop a novel framework for analyzing the computability and complexity of PDE solutions. Our approach establishes a direct link between the complexity of solving a PDE and the convergence rates of its discrete gradient flow. This connection relates structural properties of the PDE operator—such as coercivity, ellipticity, and convexity—to the complexity class of its solutions, thereby extending existing results to a much broader class of PDEs. Moreover, it establishes a relation between the loss of regularity of the PDE solutions with the complexity blowup, where polynomial-time input data produce solutions with super-polynomial complexity
bacho2023complexityblowupsolutionslaplace
.

This framework contributes to the development of a more general computability and complexity theory for PDEs, extending to both linear and nonlinear equations without closed-form solutions. Related Work

The literature on complexity theory for PDEs focuses mainly on four complexity classes
bacho2023complexityblowupsolutionslaplace
. The class
P
consists of all decision problems that can be solved in polynomial time by a deterministic Turing machine. The class
NP
contains those problems for which a proposed solution can be verified in polynomial time. The class
\#P
encompasses counting problems, namely, those for which the number of solutions to an
NP
problem can be computed. Finally, the class
FP
includes all the counting problems that can be solved by a function-oracle Turing machine in polynomial time
Arora2012-bh
.

A central question in the complexity theory of PDEs is determining whether solutions lie in a different complexity function class than that of the input data (e.g., boundary conditions or source terms). Under the conjecture that
FP\neq\#P
, the notion of complexity blowup formalizes settings in which the solution of a PDE belongs to a strictly bigger complexity class than its input data. We now review foundational results on complexity blowup in PDEs.

The arguably first study on the computability of PDE solutions, presented in
Pour-El1983-ls
, examined a wave equation of the form

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u+\Delta u=0,\textrm{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
&u|_{t=0}=u_{0},\textrm{ in }\Omega,\\
&\partial_{t}u\bigr|_{t=0}=0\textrm{ in }\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(5)

and demonstrated that for a certain domain
\Omega\times(0,T)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,\infty)
, there exists a computable continuous function
u_{0}
such that the solution
u
is continuous but non-computable. This result was extended in
Pour-El1997-br
, where it was established that there exists a computable
C^{1}
function
u_{0}
such that the solution
u
of (
5
) is nowhere computable on any given compact subset
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,\infty)
. This result was further generalized in
Boche2020-uu
, where it was shown that
u_{0}
can be chosen as a computable
C^{1}
function with compact support in an annulus
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:r\leq||x||_{2}\leq R\}
for some
r,R\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, such that its first derivative is absolutely continuous.

Additional work in
Weihrauch2001-rm
addressed the inhomogeneous linear Schrödinger equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u=i\Delta u+\phi\textrm{, in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty),\\
&u|_{t=0}=u_{0}\textrm{, in }\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{aligned}\right.

(6)

showing that the solution
u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty))
is computable for all
\phi\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty))
. Moreover, in the homogeneous case (
\phi=0
), it was shown that the solution
u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty))
is computable if and only if
p=2
. Extensions to the nonlinear setting are presented in
Weihrauch2006-cp
, establishing the computability of solutions in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty))
.

Another prominent PDE that has been studied in the context of computability and complexity analysis is the Poisson equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta u=f\textrm{ in }\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
&u=g\textrm{, on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(7)

It was shown in
Kawamura2017-dv
that if the complexity classes
FP
and
\#P
coincide (a stronger assumption than
P=NP
), then any solution
u\in C^{2}(B_{1})
over the unit ball
B_{1}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|_{2}\leq 1\}
is polynomial time computable, as formalized in Definition
6.4
. This holds whenever
f
and
g
are smooth and polynomial time computable on
B_{1}
and
\partial B_{1}
, respectively.

The results for the Laplace and diffusion equations were recently improved in
bacho2023complexityblowupsolutionslaplace
, showing that the solution in each case is
\#P
-complete when the initial and boundary data are polynomial time computable. Moreover, it was shown in
bacho2023complexityblowupsolutionslaplace
that these equations exhibit a complexity blowup, implying that no numerical algorithm can compute their solutions in polynomial time for arbitrary initial and boundary data, unless
FP=\#P
.

More recently, a general result was established for linear PDEs of the form

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u=\mathcal{A}u,\textrm{ in }\Omega\times(0,1),\\
&u|_{t=0}=\phi(x),\textrm{ in }\Omega,\\
&\mathcal{L}u=0\textrm{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,1),\end{aligned}\right.

(8)

with
\mathcal{A}
is a linear differential operator,
\phi
the initial condition and
\mathcal{L}
is a linear operator describing the boundary conditions. In
Koswara2021-sr
, it is shown that the solution
u
of (
8
) is computable in
PSPACE

Arora2009-ji
under certain conditions on the initial and boundary data. In particular, the differential operator
\mathcal{A}
must be computable in polynomial time, with the solution admitting a finite-difference approximation
u_{n}
converging to the solution
u
in the supremum norm.

The contributions of the present paper follow a similar philosophy to
Koswara2021-sr
, where we estimate the complexity of a numerical algorithm approximating the solution of the PDE to characterize the intrinsic complexity of the solution itself. Our approach, however, introduces a novel and more general framework based on discrete gradient flows, offering a powerful characterization of the complexity of broad classes of PDEs and identifying regimes in which their solutions exhibit complexity blowup.

Contributions

In this paper, we present a novel variational framework for analyzing the complexity of different classes of PDEs by considering a variational formulation whose minimizer solves the PDE, and estimating the complexity of its discrete gradient flow. The theoretical framework developed here builds upon previous work on Sobolev cubatures
Cardona2023-ma
and polynomial surrogate models for PDE learning problems
Cardona2024-sr
. The main contributions on this paper are summarized as follows:

•

Sufficient conditions for computability
: We provide sufficient conditions under which the solution of a (possibly nonlinear) PDE is
H^{k}
-computable for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, expressed in terms of structural properties of the variational losses (e.g., convexity, coercivity, continuity). Specifically, we present in Theorem
6.13
sufficient conditions for a loss of the form

\mathcal{L}[u]:=\|\mathcal{N}[u]-f\|_{H^{a}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|u-g\|_{H^{b}(\partial\Omega)}^{2},

(9)

to have an
H^{k}
-computable solution
provided that the input data and its solution belong to the space
AC^{k-1}(\Omega)\cap BV^{k}(\Omega)
, for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

•

Polynomial-time computability of PDE Solutions
: We prove in Theorem
6.13
that a class of PDEs has
polynomial-time
H^{k}
-computable solutions
, provided the cubature loss is polynomial-time computable, the input data is sufficiently regular, and the solution operator preserves this regularity.

•

Lower Bounds and Complexity Blow-Up
: We provide lower bounds for the complexity of non-analytic solutions in Corollary
6.16
, complementing the results from Theorem
6.13
.
Consequently, we provide sufficient conditions for a PDE to exhibit
complexity blowup
whenever the solution has a super-polynomial convergence rate.

•

Illustrative PDE Examples – Polynomial-Time Computability and Complexity Blow-Up
: We present two examples demonstrating the applicability of our variational framework: a linear case with a polynomial-time computable solution and a nonlinear case exhibiting complexity blowup. Specifically, Theorem
7.3
shows that the Poisson equation admits
H^{1}
-computable
solutions with
polynomial-time complexity
under additional regularity assumptions, whereas Theorem
7.8
demonstrates that the Eikonal equation also has
H^{1}
-computable
solutions but exhibits
complexity blowup
regardless of the regularity of the boundary data.

These results build a rigorous bridge between computability, complexity theory, and variational formulations of PDEs. By establishing upper and lower bounds on solution complexity, the framework determines whether a PDE admits effective numerical algorithms and when it exhibits complexity blowup. In particular, it links structural properties of PDE learning losses, such as convexity and coercivity, and the regularity of the solution operator to the inherent complexity of the infinite-dimensional solution. Thus, the framework not only advances the theoretical foundations of computability and complexity of PDEs but also provides constructive insights for designing effective numerical schemes.

Variational Framework

This section presents a variational framework that will allow us to characterize the complexity of solutions to a class of PDEs by leveraging the structural properties of their governing operators.

For this, let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open, bounded domain. We start by considering the following general PDE given by

\biggr\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{N}[u]=f&\textrm{ in }\Omega,\\
u|_{\partial\Omega}=g&\textrm{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}

(10)

where
\mathcal{N}:X(\Omega)\rightarrow Y(\Omega)
is a (possibly non-linear) differential operator,
f\in Y(\Omega)
the right-hand side and
g\in W(\partial\Omega)
the Dirichlet boundary conditions, with
X,Y,W
some Banach spaces. Furthermore, we consider the following variational forward problem.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{N}:X(\Omega)\rightarrow Y(\Omega)
be a PDE operator,
f\in Y(\Omega)
the right-hand side and
g\in W(\partial\Omega)
the Dirichlet boundary condition. The
forward PDE learning problem
consists of finding a function
u^{*}\in X(\Omega)
solving

\inf\limits_{u\in X}\mathcal{L}[u]:=\|\mathcal{N}[u]-f\|_{Y(\Omega)}^{2}+\|u|_{\partial\Omega}-g\|_{W(\partial\Omega)}^{2}.

(11)

The functional
\mathcal{L}:X(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is called the
forward problem loss
.

To solve the forward problem in (
11

Definition 4.2

Let

11

(12)

Using Definitions

Let

Next, we recall an important condition characterizing the asymptotic growth behavior of functionals.

Definition 4.10
.

A loss functional
\mathcal{L}:H(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
, defined on a Hilbert space

\mathcal{L}[u]-\mathcal{L}[u^{*}]\geq\mu\|u-u^{*}\|_{H(\Omega)}^{2}.

The next result shows the equivalence between the QGC and the RSI condition for convex functionals.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
\mathcal{L}:H(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a convex loss functional, with a minimizer

Proof..

Let

for all

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(\Omega)\subseteq H(\Omega)

Finite-Dimensional Approximations

This section reviews the approximation-theoretical foundation for our framework for estimating the PDE solution’s computational complexity. We examine the convergence rates at which polynomial spaces can approximate ground-truth solutions, as well as their associated loss functionals. These approximation rates are intrinsically linked to computability and complexity classes, as we elaborate on in Section

Let
\Omega:=(-1,1)^{d}

In this section, we introduce the notion of Turing computability in Banach spaces, following

is lower semi-continuous, convex, and coercive, hence the existence of a minimizer
u^{*}\in H^{k}(\Omega)
is guaranteed by applying Tonelli’s direct method
Maso1993-ss
.

Let
\hat{u}_{\theta^{j}}\in\Pi_{n}(\Omega)
be a polynomial surrogate model obtained after
j
-iterations of the explicit Euler gradient flow of the Sobolev cubature approximation
\mathcal{L}^{n}:\Pi_{n}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
of the loss in (
49
). Since
\mathcal{L}
satisfies the QGC, by Theorem
4.12
we have

\|\hat{u}_{\theta^{j}}-u^{*}\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\epsilon_{\textrm{app}}(n)+\epsilon_{\textrm{int}}(n)+\epsilon_{\textrm{opt}}(j).

(52)

Moreover, since
\mathcal{L}
is convex and satisfies the QGC, the map
\theta\mapsto\mathcal{L}^{n}[\hat{u}_{\theta}]
is convex and satisfies the RSI condition by Lemma
4.11
, with modulus
\mu\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, where
\sigma
is the smallest eigenvalue of the matrix
\mathbb{V}^{T}\mathbb{W}_{k}\mathbb{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{|A_{n,d}|\times|A_{n,d}|}
as in the proof of Lemma
6.15
. Therefore, by Theorem
4.12
, the optimization error converges with the rate

\epsilon_{\textrm{opt}}(j)=\mathcal{O}\left((1-\mu\sigma/L)^{j}\right),

(53)

where we used the Lipschitz continuity of
\theta\mapsto\nabla\mathcal{L}^{n}[\hat{u}_{\theta}]
.
Moreover, by Propositions
5.6
and the regularity assumptions on
f
and
g
, we know that

\epsilon_{\textrm{app}}(n)+\epsilon_{\textrm{int}}(n)=\mathcal{O}(n^{-k^{\prime}+k}+n^{-k^{*}}),

where
k^{*}:=\min\{k^{\prime}-k-2a,k^{\prime}-k-2b\}
.
To conclude the proof, we apply Lemma
6.15
to ensure the computability of
f
and
g
, and Corollary
6.8
to establish the computability of the explicit Euler gradient flow. This implies that for all
n\geq e_{n}(N)
and
j\geq e_{j}(N)
, where
e_{n}(N):=\max\{2^{N/(k-k^{\prime})},2^{N/k^{*}}\}
and
e_{j}(N)=N\log(1-\mu\sigma/L)
, the convergence error achieves fast convergence, i.e.,

\|\hat{u}_{\theta^{j}}-u^{*}\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 2^{-N}.

(54)

Equivalently, in order to obtain an error of order
2^{-N}
we need to compute
j=\mathcal{O}(N\log(1-\mu\sigma/L))
iterations with
\mathcal{O}(F(\max\{2^{dN/(k-k^{\prime})},2^{dN/k^{*}}\}))
computations each, where
F:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is a function proportional to the complexity of
\theta^{j}-\delta\tau\nabla\mathcal{L}^{n}[\hat{u}_{\theta^{j}}]
.
∎

Note that Theorem
6.13
provides only a qualitative characterization of the complexity of the solution to the PDE learning problem. The following corollary refines this by linking the complexity class of the solution with its regularity.

Corollary 6.16
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
6.13
hold, and suppose that the source term
f
and the boundary condition
g
are
\rho
-analytic functions. Assume further that the PDE operator is regularity-preserving, i.e.,
u^{*}
is
\rho^{*}
-analytic for some
\rho^{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. If, in addition
\mathcal{N}[u_{n}]
and
\nabla\mathcal{N}[u_{n}]
are
C^{0}
-polynomial time computable functions for all
u_{n}\in\Pi_{n}(\Omega)
, then the solution
u^{*}
is
H^{k}
-computable in polynomial time.

Conversely, if the PDE operator fails to preserve the regularity of the data, that is, if the solution
u^{*}
is not
\rho
-analytic for any
\rho\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
but only satisfies
u^{*}\in AC^{k-1}(\Omega)\cap H^{k}(\Omega)
for some
k>\frac{d}{2}
, then
u^{*}
exhibits a complexity blowup and is
H^{k}
-computable in at least super-polynomial time.

Proof..

For the first part of the statement, we follow the proof of Theorem
6.13
, and apply Proposition
5.5
yielding

\|\hat{u}_{\theta^{j}}-u^{*}\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\rho^{-n}+(1-\mu\sigma/L)^{-j}\right).

(55)

This implies that the error is of order
\mathcal{O}(2^{-N})
in
\mathcal{O}(N\log(1-\mu\sigma/L))
iterations with

\mathcal{O}\left(F_{\mathcal{N}}(N\log(2)/\log(\rho)+1)^{d}\right)
operations. Here
F_{\mathcal{N}}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is a polynomial function proportional to the complexity of the operation
\theta^{j}-\delta\tau\nabla\mathcal{L}^{n}[\hat{u}_{\theta^{j}}]
, which has polynomial complexity due to the assumptions on the operator
\mathcal{N}
, yielding the claim.

For the second part of the statement, we apply Lemma
6.15
, which ensures that the Dirichlet boundary condition
g
and the source term
f
are computable in polynomial time. Furthermore, by Theorem
6.13
the solution is
H^{k}
-computable. However, by Theorem
5.7
together with the Sobolev embedding theorem
brezis2011
for
k>d/2
, there exists a sub-exponential function
\mathcal{Q}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}+
such that

\|\hat{u}_{\theta^{j}}-u^{*}\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}^{2}\geq\|\hat{u}_{\theta^{j}}-u^{*}\|_{C^{0}(\Omega)}^{2}\geq\mathcal{Q}(n).

(56)

Therefore, achieving an error of order

\|\hat{u}_{\theta^{j}}-u^{*}\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 2^{-N},

requires at least
\mathcal{S}(N)
operations, where
\mathcal{S}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is a super-polynomial function. In particular, this shows that the solution is not computable in polynomial time.
∎

Complexity of solutions to the Poisson Equation

Proof..

The proof of Theorem

is included in Appendix

Next, we show how under certain assumptions the Poisson forward loss from Definition

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
\mathcal{L}:H^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}+

) in the hypercube

Before proving the theorem, we provide the following lemma.

Lemma 7.5
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
g\in H^{1/2}(\Omega)
be polynomial time computable functions and
\mathcal{L}^{n}:\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a Sobolev cubature loss, defined as

\mathcal{L}^{n}[\hat{u}_{\theta}]:=\|\Delta\hat{u}_{\theta}-f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+n\|\hat{u}_{\theta}|_{\partial\Omega}-g_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2},\textrm{ }\forall\hat{u}_{\theta}\in\Pi_{n}(\Omega),

(59)

where
f_{n}:=\mathcal{I}_{n}[f]
and
g_{n}:=\mathcal{I}_{n}[g]
. If the coefficients
\theta\in\Theta
are computable in polynomial time, then the
L^{2}
-gradient of the loss
\nabla\mathcal{L}^{n}[\hat{u}_{\theta}]\in\Pi_{n}(\Omega)
is computable in polynomial time for all

Proof..

Let
\mathbb{D}_{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{|A_{n,d}|\times|A_{n,d}|}
be the polynomial differentiation matrix associated with the operator
\Delta:\Pi_{n}(\Omega)\rightarrow\Pi_{n}(\Omega)
. Following Proposition

, we know that
\partial^{\beta}L_{\alpha}\in\Pi_{n}(\Omega)
is computable in polynomial time and therefore

(\mathbb{D}^{\beta})_{i,j}:=\partial^{\beta}L_{i}(p_{j}),

(60)

is computable in polynomial time for all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,|A_{n,d}|\}
.

We compute the
L^{2}
-gradient of the loss
\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\theta}[\theta]:=\mathcal{L}^{n}[\hat{u}_{\theta}]
, by evaluating its first variation in the direction of a perturbation
\delta\theta\in\Theta
, yielding

d\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\theta}[\theta](\delta\theta)=2\int\limits_{\Omega}\Delta^{*}(\Delta\hat{u}_{\theta}-f_{n})\hat{u}_{\delta\theta}dx+2n\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}(\hat{u}_{\theta}|_{\partial\Omega}-g_{n})\hat{u}_{\delta\theta}|_{\partial\Omega}ds,

(61)

where

is the adjoint of the Laplacian, restricted to polynomial spaces.
Consequently, by applying the Sobolev cubatures to the variation in (

), the

-gradient is equal to

\nabla\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\theta}[\theta]=2\mathbb{T}_{\Omega}^{T}\mathbb{D}^{T}_{\Delta}\mathbb{W}_{\Omega}(\mathbb{D}_{\Delta}\hat{\mathfrak{u}}_{\theta}-\mathfrak{f})+2\mathbb{T}^{T}_{\partial\Omega}\mathbb{W}_{\partial\Omega}(\hat{\mathfrak{u}}_{\theta}|_{\partial\Omega}-\mathfrak{g}),

(62)

where
\mathfrak{f}=f(P_{n}(\Omega))
,
\mathfrak{g}=g(P_{n}(\partial\Omega))
,
\mathbb{W}_{\Omega}:=\textrm{diag}(\{\omega_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A_{n,d}})
,
\mathbb{W}_{\Omega}:=\textrm{diag}(\{\omega_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A_{n,d}})
,
\mathbb{W}_{\partial\Omega}:=\textrm{diag}(\{\omega_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A_{n,d-1}})
,
\mathbb{T}_{\Omega}:=(T_{\alpha}(P_{n}(\Omega)))_{\alpha\in A_{n,d}}
,
\mathbb{T}_{\partial\Omega}:=(T_{\alpha}(P_{n}(\partial\Omega)))_{\alpha\in A_{n}}
,
\hat{\mathfrak{u}}_{\theta}:=\mathbb{T}_{\Omega}\theta
,
\hat{\mathfrak{u}}_{\theta}:=\mathbb{T}_{\partial\Omega}\theta
.

The rest of the proof is a consequence of Proposition
6.7
, which implies that the matrices and vectors from (

) are computable in polynomial time. Hence, the gradient is computable in polynomial time.
∎

Building on the previous result, we now proceed to prove the computability of the solution

.

Proof of Theorem

.

We start by noting that Lemma

implies that the implicit Euler iterate

\theta^{k+1}=\theta^{k}-\delta\tau\nabla\mathcal{L}^{n}[\theta^{k}]

can be computed in polynomial time, assuming that the initial guess
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
and the time-step
\delta\tau
are computable in polynomial time.

Next, we show that the functional
\theta\mapsto\mathcal{L}^{n}[\hat{u}_{\theta}]
is
\lambda
-convex. Using that

\|Q_{n}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}\leq n\|Q_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2},\,\,\forall Q_{n}\in\Pi_{n}(\Omega),

as shown in
Diethelm2002-kr

\|u\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
by definition of the negative order norm, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\Delta\hat{u}_{\theta}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+n\|\hat{u}_{\theta}|_{\partial\Omega}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}\geq\|\Delta\hat{u}_{\theta}\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\hat{u}_{\theta}|_{\partial\Omega}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2},

for all
\hat{u}_{\theta}\in\Pi_{n}(\Omega)
. Therefore, by Theorem
7.2
, we have that

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\theta}[\theta_{2}]-\nabla\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\theta}[\theta_{1}],\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\|\Delta(\hat{u}_{\theta_{2}}-\hat{u}_{\theta_{1}})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+n\|\hat{u}_{\theta_{2}}|_{\partial\Omega}-\hat{u}_{\theta_{1}}|_{\partial\Omega}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}

(63)

\displaystyle\geq\|\Delta(\hat{u}_{\theta_{2}}-\hat{u}_{\theta_{1}})\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\hat{u}_{\theta_{2}}|_{\partial\Omega}-\hat{u}_{\theta_{1}}|_{\partial\Omega}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\|\hat{u}_{\theta_{2}}-\hat{u}_{\theta_{1}}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

\lambda
-convexity follows by noting that the map
\theta\mapsto\|\hat{u}_{\theta}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}
is coercive for all

\mathbb{T}_{\Omega}:=\left(T_{i}(P_{n}(\Omega))\right)_{i\in A_{n,d}}
, be the Vandermonde matrix of the Lagrange basis of degree

in the Legendre grid of degree

\mathbb{T}_{\Omega}^{T}\mathbb{W}\mathbb{T}_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{|A_{n,d}|\times|A_{n,d}|}

\|\hat{u}_{\theta}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\geq\|\hat{u}_{\theta}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\gtrsim\sigma\|\theta\|_{1}^{2},

(64)

where

is the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathbb{T}_{\Omega}^{T}\mathbb{W}\mathbb{T}_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{|A_{n,d}|\times|A_{n,d}|}

\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\theta}[\theta_{2}]-\nabla\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\theta}[\theta_{1}],\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\gtrsim\sigma\|\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}\|_{1}^{2},

(65)

as claimed.
Next we show that the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{L}^{n}:\Pi_{n}(\Omega)\rightarrow\Pi_{n}(\Omega)
is a Lipschitz continuous map. Let
u_{n},v_{n}\in\Pi_{n}(\Omega)
, then by definition of the gradient and the Sobolev cubatures, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\theta}[\theta_{1}]-\nabla\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\theta}[\theta_{2}]\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\biggr\|\int\limits_{\Omega}(\Delta^{*}\Delta\hat{u}_{\theta_{1}}\nabla_{\theta}\hat{u}_{\theta_{1}}-\Delta^{*}\Delta\hat{u}_{\theta_{2}}\nabla_{\theta}\hat{u}_{\theta_{2}})dx

(66)

\displaystyle+n\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}\hat{u}_{\theta_{1}}\nabla_{\theta}\hat{u}_{\theta_{1}}-\hat{u}_{\theta_{1}}\nabla_{\theta}\hat{u}_{\theta_{2}}ds\biggr\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|(\mathbb{T}_{\Omega}^{T}\mathbb{D}_{\Delta}^{T}\mathbb{W}\mathbb{D}_{\Delta}\mathbb{T}_{\Omega}+n\mathbb{T}_{\partial\Omega}^{T}\mathbb{W}\mathbb{T}_{\partial\Omega})(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})\|_{1}

where
\mathbb{D}_{\Delta}:=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{D}_{x_{i}}^{2}
, and

.

Let
\theta^{j}\in\Theta
be the

-th iterate of the explicit Euler gradient flow with time-step
\delta\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Since
\mathcal{L}^{n}
is a
\lambda
-convex and

-Lipschitz smooth functional, then by Theorem

\|\hat{u}_{\theta^{j}}-u^{*}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\epsilon_{\textrm{app}}(n)+\epsilon_{\textrm{int}}(n)+\epsilon_{\textrm{opt}}(j),

(67)

with
\epsilon_{\textrm{opt}}(j)=(1-\sigma/L)^{j}
.

To finish the proof, we use that the Laplace operator is a hypo-elliptic operator

, hence, the solution

inherits the regularity of the domain, boundary condition and right hand-side. Therefore, as a result of Corollary

, we can characterize the complexity of the solution as follows:

•

If

and

Moreover, if

7.2
Complexity of Solutions to the Eikonal Equation

This subsection presents the Eikonal equation
Alimov2019-np
as a representative example of a PDE whose solution is
H^{1}
-computable but exhibits a loss of regularity, resulting in a complexity blowup. We begin by formulating the corresponding variational problem.

Definition 7.6
.

Let
S\subseteq\Omega
be a smooth manifold of co-dimension one, then the
Eikonal forward loss

\mathcal{L}:H^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is defined as

\mathcal{L}[u]:=\bigr\|\|\nabla u\|_{1}-1\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u|_{S}\|_{H^{1/2}(S)}.

(68)

Next, we present some of the analytical properties of the Eikonal loss.

Theorem 7.7
.

The Eikonal loss
\mathcal{L}:H^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
from (
68
) is a convex, continuous functional,
satisfying the apriori coercivity estimate

\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\mathcal{L}[u]+1,

(69)

and the QGC.
Therefore, there exists a solution
u^{*}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
minimizing
\mathcal{L}
.

Proof..

We start by proving the convexity of the loss. Let
u,v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and
t\in(0,1)
, then by the monotonicity and convexity of the norms and the linearity of the gradient, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[tu+(1-t)v]

\displaystyle\leq\bigr\|t\|\nabla u\|_{1}+(1-t)\|\nabla v\|_{1}-1\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+t\|u|_{S}\|_{H^{1/2}(S)}+(1-t)\|v|_{S}\bigr\|_{H^{1/2}(S)}

\displaystyle\leq t\mathcal{L}[u]+(1-t)\mathcal{L}[v].

Next, we show the Hölder continuity of the loss. Using the reverse triangle inequality, the following holds

\displaystyle|\mathcal{L}[u]-\mathcal{L}[v]|

\displaystyle\leq\bigr\|\|\nabla u-\nabla v\|_{1}\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u|_{S}-v|_{S}\|_{H^{1/2}(S)}

\displaystyle\leq\|u-v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)},

where we used the triangle inequality and the trace theorem for the last inequality.
To prove the coercivity estimate, we define
u_{S}=\mathcal{E}[u|_{S}]\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and
u_{0}:=(u-u_{S})\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Then, by the extension theorem, the triangle and Poincaré inequalities, the following holds

\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u_{S}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u|_{S}\|_{H^{1/2}(S)}.

(70)

Moreover, by definition of the PDE term and the reverse triangle inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\partial}[u]

\displaystyle:=\bigr\|\|\nabla u\|_{1}-1\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\geq\left(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\sum\limits_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i,j=1}}{{i\neq j}}}^{d}\int\limits_{\Omega}|\partial_{x_{i}}u||\partial_{x_{j}}u|dx\right)^{1/2}-|\Omega|

(71)

\displaystyle\geq\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-|\Omega|.

(72)

Hence, combining (
70
) and (
70
), the following holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[u]+1\gtrsim\mathcal{L}[u]+|\Omega|

\displaystyle\geq\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u|_{S}\|_{H^{1/2}(S)}

(73)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

(74)

It remains to prove the quadratic growth condition of the functional. Let
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, and
u^{*}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
the minimizer of the loss
\mathcal{L}
. Applying the reverse triangle inequality, the estimate in (
70
), and assuming without loss of generality that
\mathcal{L}[u^{*}]=0
, the following holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[u]-\mathcal{L}[u^{*}]

\displaystyle=\bigr\|\|\nabla u\|_{1}-1\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u|_{S}\|_{H^{1/2}(S)}

(75)

\displaystyle\geq\bigr\|\|\nabla u-\nabla u^{*}\|_{1}-\|\nabla u^{*}\|_{1}-1\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u|_{S}\|_{H^{1/2}(S)}

(76)

\displaystyle=\bigr\|\|\nabla u-\nabla u^{*}\|_{1}\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u|_{S}\|_{H^{1/2}(S)}

(77)

\displaystyle=\bigr\|\|\nabla u-\nabla u^{*}\|_{1}\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u|_{S}-u^{*}|_{S}\|_{H^{1/2}(S)}

(78)

\displaystyle\geq\bigr\|\nabla u-\nabla u^{*}\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u|_{S}-u^{*}|_{S}\|_{H^{1/2}(S)}

(79)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|u-u^{*}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

(80)

For the third inequality we used that since
\mathcal{L}[u^{*}]=0
, then
\|\nabla u^{*}\|_{1}-1=0
almost everywhere in
\Omega
.
∎

Theorem 7.8
.

Let
S\subseteq\Omega
be a closed smooth manifold of co-dimension one, and
\Psi:U\rightarrow\Omega
a parametrization of the manifold, for some open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

\psi

Before proving the theorem, we present the following result that characterizes the loss of regularity in solutions of the Eikonal equation.

Lemma 7.9
.

Let
S\subseteq\Omega:=(-1,1)^{2}
be a smooth manifold of co-dimension one, and
\phi_{S}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the signed distance function of

, given by

\phi_{S}(x):=\min\limits_{x_{s}\in S}\|x-x_{s}\|_{1},

(81)

for some

S^{+}:=\{\phi_{S}\geq 0\}
and

be the medial axis of the manifold
S

M_{S}:=\{c\in S^{+}:\exists x_{l},x_{r}\in S,x_{l}\neq x_{r},\|c-x_{l}\|_{1}=\|c-x_{r}\|_{1}\}.

(82)

Then, the signed distance function
\phi_{S}
is non-differentiable on

,

(83)

Proof..

This is a well-known result in the signed distance function literature
Alimov2019-gh
, and we include a proof in Appendix

.

Proof of Theorem
7.8

We start by showing the existence of the solution

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a variational framework for analyzing the computational complexity of partial differential equations (PDEs) without requiring closed-form solutions, thereby generalizing results from previous works
bacho2023complexityblowupsolutionslaplace
;
Pour-El1983-ls
. Specifically, the variational framework allows to estimate the complexity of a discrete gradient flow, approximation the solution operator of the variational problem, and uses the convergence rates of these discretizations to estimate the complexity of the infinite-dimensional solution of the PDE. This approach enabled us to relate structural properties of PDE operators and their associated variational losses to the computational complexity of the corresponding solutions. Moreover, we introduce a method for proving complexity blowup of the solution based on the regularity-preserving properties of the solution operators. Specifically, if the solution loses regularity relative to analytical boundary and/or initial data, then the solution exhibits a complexity blowup. This result gives a direct link between regularity and approximation theory of PDEs and complexity theory.

In particular, Theorem
6.13
establishes
H^{k}
-computability for the solutions of a class of (possibly nonlinear) PDEs, under the assumptions that the associated variational loss is coercive, convex, satisfies the quadratic growth condition, and is Lipschitz smooth. Furthermore, Corollary
6.16
provides a quantitative characterization of the complexity of the solution, identifying sufficient conditions under which the solution is computable in polynomial time, and conditions under which it exhibits complexity blowup. We illustrate these results with two examples: (i) the Poisson equation, whose solution—assuming analytic boundary data—is
H^{1}
-computable in polynomial time; and (ii) the Eikonal equation, which, despite being
H^{1}
-computable under analytic boundary conditions, exhibits complexity blowup due to loss of regularity in the solution.

To summarize, we have developed a framework that builds a rigorous bridge between computability, complexity theory, and variational formulations of PDEs. By providing upper and lower bounds on solution complexity, the framework delineates the regimes in which PDEs admit effective numerical algorithms and those in which complexity blowup is inevitable. Moreover, it establishes a direct connection between structural properties of PDE learning losses—such as convexity and coercivity—and the regularity of the solution operator with the inherent complexity of the infinite-dimensional solution. Thus, the framework not only strengthens the theoretical foundations of PDE computability and complexity but also provides constructive guidance for the design of efficient numerical schemes.

The main limitation of our framework lies in the fact that the error decomposition in Theorem
4.12
provides only an upper bound on the solution’s complexity. Consequently, with the tools presented in this paper, we can only show complexity blowup by identifying a loss of regularity in the solution
u^{*}\in H^{k}(\Omega)
, restricted to the case
k>d/2
. Further research is required to extend the applicability of the lower bounds, possibly for higher dimensions and to include lower bounds on the convergence of the gradient flow, thereby providing alternative characterizations of the complexity blowup.

\displaystyle=2\|u_{2}|_{\partial\Omega}-u_{1}|_{\partial\Omega}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}.

(110)

Therefore, applying the estimates in (
105
) and
101
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}[u_{2}-u_{1}],u_{2}-u_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\|\Delta(u_{2}-u_{1})\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|u_{2}|_{\partial\Omega}-u_{1}|_{\partial\Omega}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}

(111)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\|u_{2}-u_{1}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2},

yielding the claim that
\mathcal{L}
is
\lambda
-convex for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

To finish the proof we apply Tonelli’s direct method
Maso1993
to a convex, coercive and lower-semi-continuous functional, yielding the existence of the solution. The uniqueness holds since
\mathcal{L}
is
\lambda
-convex and therefore strictly convex.
∎

Next we show the non-differentiability of the signed distance function.

7.9
.

(113)

where we used the reverse triangle inequality and the fact that
