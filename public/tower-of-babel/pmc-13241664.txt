Long-segment cervical brucellar epidural abscess limited by a disc barrier presenting with tetraparesis: A case report
Authors: Timur Yildirim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spinal epidural abscess caused by Brucella species is an uncommon but potentially devastating complication of osteoarticular brucellosis. Cervical involvement is exceedingly rare, and long-segment cervical brucellar epidural abscess presenting with tetraparesis represents an uncommonly described clinical scenario. A 42-year-old female presented with a two-week history of progressive tetraparesis. Neurological examination revealed MRC grade 3/5 weakness in the upper extremities and 4/5 in the lower extremities, with bilateral sensory disturbance and upper motor neuron signs, classified as ASIA grade D. Gadolinium-enhanced MRI demonstrated a long-segment anterior epidural collection from C2 to C6 with significant spinal cord compression, associated with contrast enhancement of the C5–6 disc space consistent with spondylodiscitis. Laboratory investigations revealed ESR 68 mm/h, CRP 48 mg/L, WBC 11.2 00d7 1000b3/03bcL, and Brucella standard tube agglutination titre of 1/320. Blood cultures were negative. The patient underwent three-level anterior cervical microdiscectomy (C3–4, C5–6, and C6–7) with complete evacuation of the epidural abscess and intraoperative rifampicin irrigation. Intraoperative cultures confirmed Brucella melitensis. Targeted antimicrobial therapy consisted of doxycycline 100 mg twice daily combined with rifampicin 600 mg once daily for 12 weeks. Neurological recovery was substantial; lower extremity strength normalised to 5/5 at one month and upper extremity strength at three months. At 12-month follow-up, complete neurological recovery was confirmed with no evidence of recurrence on MRI. This case raises the hypothesis that a pathological intervertebral disc may act as an anatomical barrier limiting caudal spread of epidural infection, warranting further investigation.

Introduction

Brucellosis is the most prevalent zoonotic infection worldwide, with an estimated 500,000 new cases reported annually, predominantly in the Mediterranean basin, Middle East, Central Asia, and Latin America [1]. The disease is caused by facultative intracellular gram-negative bacteria of the genus Brucella, most commonly transmitted through the ingestion of unpasteurized dairy products or direct contact with infected animals [2]. Osteoarticular involvement represents the most frequent focal complication, occurring in up to 40% of cases, with spondylodiscitis being the most common spinal manifestation [3].

Spinal epidural abscess (SEA) formation is a rare but serious complication of brucellar spondylitis, reported in less than 1.5% of neurological complications of brucellosis [4]. The lumbar spine is most frequently affected, followed by the thoracic spine; cervical involvement is uncommon and carries a particularly high risk of neurological morbidity and mortality due to the narrow spinal canal and proximity to critical neural structures [5], [6]. The incidence of spinal epidural abscess has been reported at approximately 0.2–2.8 per 10,000 hospital admissions [7].

Long-segment cervical brucellar epidural abscess causing tetraparesis is exceedingly uncommon, with only a handful of cases reported in the literature [8], [9], [10]. We describe a case of a 42-year-old woman presenting with tetraparesis due to a C2-C6 brucellar epidural abscess, in whom a pathological disc at C6–7 appeared to limit caudal extension of the abscess. We discuss the surgical management, antimicrobial therapy, and the potential anatomical role of the intervertebral disc as a barrier to infection spread.

Case report

A 42-year-old female presented to the neurosurgery department with a two-week history of progressive weakness in all four extremities. She reported no fever but noted a recent history of neck pain and generalised malaise. She resided in a rural, endemic region and reported regular consumption of unpasteurized dairy products.

On neurological examination, the patient demonstrated tetraparesis. Upper extremity motor strength was 3/5 bilaterally on the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale, and lower extremity strength was 4/5 bilaterally. Bilateral numbness, paraesthesia, and reduced sensation were present in all four extremities. Deep tendon reflexes were hyperreflexic in the lower extremities with bilateral Babinski signs, consistent with an upper motor neuron lesion. According to the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment Scale, the patient was classified as ASIA grade D (incomplete motor function preserved below the neurological level, with most key muscles grading below 3). No bowel or bladder dysfunction was noted. Preoperatively, she was ambulatory with assistance only.

Laboratory investigations revealed elevated inflammatory markers: ESR 68 mm/h (reference <20 mm/h), CRP 48 mg/L (reference <5 mg/L), and a white blood cell count of 11.2 × 10 ³ /μL (reference 4.5–11.0 ×10 ³/μL). Given the epidemiological context, Brucella serology was obtained: the Rose Bengal test was positive, and Brucella standard tube agglutination (STA) was positive at a titre of 1/320 (reference <1/160). Blood cultures were obtained and yielded no growth. Alternative diagnoses including tuberculosis, pyogenic spondylodiscitis, and fungal infection were considered at presentation. The absence of risk factors for tuberculosis, negative tuberculin skin test, and the strongly positive Brucella serology in an epidemiologically appropriate context supported a diagnosis of brucellar infection. Empirical antimicrobial therapy was initiated prior to surgical intervention pending culture confirmation.

Gadolinium-enhanced MRI of the cervical spine demonstrated a long-segment anterior epidural collection extending from C2 to C6. On T2-weighted sequences, the collection appeared hyperintense, consistent with fluid and inflammatory tissue. T1-weighted post-contrast sequences demonstrated peripheral rim enhancement of the epidural collection and significant contrast enhancement of the C5–6 disc space and adjacent vertebral endplates, consistent with spondylodiscitis at this level. Anterior cord compression was most pronounced at the C3–4 level on both sagittal and axial sequences, where the epidural collection was thickest (Fig. 1). The collection did not extend below the C6–7 disc level. A midline disc protrusion at C6–7 with posterior longitudinal ligament thickening was identified; this structural configuration was postulated to represent an anatomical barrier limiting caudal spread of the infection [12]. Given the severity of the neurological deficit and the extent of cord compression, the patient underwent emergency anterior cervical surgery. The anterior surgical approach was selected for several reasons: the abscess had a predominantly anterior epidural location compressing the anterior surface of the spinal cord; a posterior approach would not have permitted direct access to the C5–6 disc space with spondylodiscitis, which required debridement; and the anterior column required reconstruction to restore stability.

A three-level anterior cervical microdiscectomy was performed. The initial discectomy was performed at C3–4, the level of greatest epidural compression as determined on preoperative axial and sagittal MRI, to achieve early cord decompression and abscess evacuation. Abscess material was carefully evacuated using a hook instrument with a gentle pushing manoeuvre to avoid anterior traction on the dural sac, thereby minimising the risk of injury to the anterior arachnoid membrane. Discectomy was then extended to C5–6, the primary spondylodiscitis level with significant contrast enhancement, for complete debridement. Finally, C6–7 discectomy was performed due to pre-existing significant disc herniation at this level causing independent cord compression, which required decompression regardless of its hypothesised role as an infection barrier. Intraoperatively, turbid fluid and granulation tissue were encountered within the epidural space. The epidural space was copiously irrigated with rifampicin-containing saline solution (rifampicin 600 mg/500 mL normal saline), consistent with reported local antibiotic applications in infected spinal surgery [11]. Intraoperative cultures of abscess material subsequently confirmed Brucella melitensis. Species identification was performed by standard microbiological culture methods. Empirical antibiotic therapy was transitioned to targeted Brucella-specific treatment: doxycycline 100 mg twice daily combined with rifampicin 600 mg once daily, continued for a total of 12 weeks, in keeping with established treatment principles for complicated brucellar spondylitis [13], [14].

Neurological recovery was substantial and progressive. At one month postoperatively, lower extremity strength had normalised to 5/5 MRC, and upper extremity strength had improved to 4/5 MRC. By three months, upper extremity strength had also normalised to 5/5 MRC. The patient received adjunctive physiotherapy and rehabilitation throughout this period. By the one-month follow-up, she was ambulatory independently without assistive devices. At 12-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated complete neurological recovery, with 5/5 strength in all extremities, no residual sensory deficits, and no evidence of recurrence on MRI (Fig. 3). Discussion

Brucellar spinal epidural abscess is a recognised but infrequent complication of osteoarticular brucellosis. The cervical region accounts for only a minority of cases, with available literature largely confined to case reports and small series [8], [9], [10], [15]. Long-segment cervical involvement causing tetraparesis, as observed in our patient, is particularly unusual and underscores the importance of early diagnosis.

The pathogenesis of spinal epidural abscess in brucellosis most commonly involves contiguous spread from vertebral osteomyelitis or spondylodiscitis, although direct haematogenous seeding of the epidural space has also been described [4], [16]. Brucella species are facultative intracellular organisms with a predilection for highly vascular tissues; the relative avascularity of the nucleus pulposus in adults may contribute to the disc's resistance to direct bacterial invasion [17].

An intriguing observation in this case is that the epidural collection did not extend below C6–7, where a pre-existing midline disc protrusion with posterior longitudinal ligament thickening was identified. We hypothesise that this structural configuration may have acted as a mechanical barrier limiting the caudal spread of the epidural collection. However, this interpretation must be framed cautiously as a hypothesis-generating observation derived from a single case. Alternative explanations for the limited caudal spread should be acknowledged, including the constraining effect of posterior longitudinal ligament integrity, local pressure gradients within the epidural space, regional differences in vascular supply, and the possibility that the observed boundary reflected the natural extent of haematogenous seeding rather than a true anatomical barrier. While the concept of epidural space compartmentalisation by connective tissue structures is supported by anatomical literature [12], [18], we cannot exclude these alternative mechanisms, and no definitive conclusion regarding the disc barrier hypothesis can be drawn from this single observation.

From a clinical perspective, cervical epidural abscesses pose particular danger due to the limited epidural space and the vulnerability of the cervical spinal cord. The cervical spine accounts for approximately 18–36% of spinal epidural abscess cases and is consistently associated with worse neurological outcomes despite its lower incidence compared to lumbar involvement [6]. In our patient, the long-segment nature of the abscess mandated urgent surgical decompression.

Regarding the differential diagnosis at presentation, the combination of cervical myelopathy, elevated inflammatory markers, strong Brucella serology (STA 1/320), and epidemiological exposure to unpasteurized dairy products strongly favoured a brucellar aetiology. Tuberculosis was considered but judged less likely given the absence of systemic features, negative tuberculin test, and the pattern of serological positivity. Pyogenic bacterial spondylodiscitis and fungal infection were also considered; negative blood cultures and the epidemiological context further supported a brucellar diagnosis, which was ultimately confirmed by intraoperative culture.

Surgical management of cervical brucellar epidural abscess remains the treatment of choice when neurological deficits are present [9], [15]. The anterior approach was preferred in this case because the abscess was predominantly anterior in location, direct access to the spondylodiscitis at C5–6 was required for adequate debridement, and anterior column reconstruction was necessary. A posterior approach would have offered decompression but would not have permitted complete anterior abscess evacuation or debridement of the infected disc space. Three-level surgery was performed: C3–4 was approached first as the level of maximum cord compression; C5–6 was debrided as the primary spondylodiscitis level; and C6–7 was included due to clinically significant pre-existing disc herniation causing independent cord compression at that level. Interbody fusion was achieved using zero-profile cages with integrated fixation, allowing anterior column stabilisation without a separate anterior plate.

Local intraoperative irrigation with rifampicin-containing saline has been employed as an adjunctive measure in infected spinal surgery. While definitive evidence for this practice remains limited, its use is supported by rifampicin's known excellent tissue penetration and reports of local antibiotic application in spinal infections [13], [11]. Abscess material was evacuated using a hook instrument with a careful pushing manoeuvre to avoid traction on the dural membrane.

Regarding antimicrobial therapy, the World Health Organization has historically recommended doxycycline combined with either rifampicin or streptomycin for uncomplicated brucellosis [13]. For focal complications such as spondylitis and epidural abscess, longer treatment durations of at least 12 weeks and combination regimens are generally advocated [14], [19]. Doxycycline plus streptomycin has been suggested to be superior to doxycycline plus rifampicin in preventing relapse in spondylitis, although ciprofloxacin-based combinations have shown comparable efficacy [19]. In our patient, doxycycline plus rifampicin was selected based on availability and clinical response, with a favourable outcome at 12-month follow-up. Serological monitoring with STA titre confirmed progressive decline and normalisation at six months, with no evidence of relapse.

This case illustrates that long-segment cervical brucellar epidural abscess presenting with tetraparesis, while rare, can be managed successfully with emergency anterior decompression and targeted antimicrobial therapy. The observation that a pathological disc at C6–7 may have limited caudal spread is an hypothesis that merits attention in future anatomical and radiological studies examining the pattern of epidural infection propagation. Furthermore, prolonged clinical and serological follow-up is essential in brucellar spinal infections, as the organism may relapse or manifest at new osteoarticular sites despite initial apparent recovery, underscoring the need for extended surveillance beyond the completion of antimicrobial therapy.

Conclusion

Long-segment cervical brucellar epidural abscess causing tetraparesis is an uncommonly described and neurosurgically challenging presentation. This case raises the hypothesis that pathological intervertebral disc structures may serve as anatomical barriers limiting the longitudinal spread of epidural infection, though alternative explanations cannot be excluded. A high index of clinical suspicion is essential for early diagnosis in endemic regions. Emergency surgical decompression via an anterior approach, combined with targeted prolonged antimicrobial therapy, can yield excellent neurological recovery even in patients presenting with severe deficits. Serological follow-up should be performed to confirm treatment response and monitor for relapse.
