In-vitro pharmacological and cytotoxic evaluation of genipin-crosslinked human serum albumin nanoparticles loaded with methyltestosterone in MCF-7 breast cancer cells
Authors: Elena Ganji, Maryam Heydari, Aref Arminfar, Midiya Mirzaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide. Although methyltestosterone (MT) has demonstrated therapeutic potential in hormone-responsive breast cancer, its clinical application may be limited by poor aqueous solubility and non-specific distribution. Albumin-based nanocarriers may enhance drug stability and intracellular availability. This study aimed to develop genipin-crosslinked human serum albumin (HSA) nanoparticles loaded with MT and to evaluate their physicochemical properties, release behavior, and in-vitro pharmacological and cytotoxic effects in MCF-7 breast cancer cells.MethodsMT-loaded HSA nanoparticles were prepared using the desolvation method followed by genipin crosslinking. Particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS), while morphology and crystallinity were evaluated using SEM and XRD analysis. Drug loading (DL) and encapsulation efficiency (EE) were quantified spectrophotometrically. In-vitro release was assessed under different pH conditions (5.5, 6.8, and 7.4). Cytotoxicity and pharmacological activity were evaluated in MCF-7 cells using MTT, LDH, and TUNEL assays. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA (p < 0.05).ResultsThe formulated MT-HSA nanoparticles exhibited a mean diameter of 83 nm (PDI 0.25) and a zeta potential of − 16.3 mV, indicating uniform nanoscale distribution and moderate colloidal stability. Encapsulation efficiency and drug loading were 77% and 11%, respectively. Sustained and pH-dependent drug release was observed over 100 h, with higher release under acidic conditions (83% at pH 5.5). MT-HSA nanoparticles significantly reduced MCF-7 cell viability compared with free MT (p < 0.05), accompanied by increased LDH release and higher apoptotic index in TUNEL assays. Unloaded HSA nanoparticles showed negligible cytotoxicity. The formulation remained physically stable for two months at 4 °C.ConclusionsGenipin-crosslinked MT-HSA nanoparticles demonstrated improved in-vitro pharmacological efficacy, enhanced cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic activity compared with free methyltestosterone, and favorable carrier biocompatibility. These findings support albumin-based nanoencapsulation as a promising strategy for optimizing steroid-based therapy in hormone-responsive breast cancer, although further in-vivo studies are required to confirm translational potential.Keywords: Breast cancer, Human serum albumin nanoparticles, Methyltestosterone, Drug delivery, Controlled release, Cytotoxicity, Apoptosis, MCF-7 cells, Genipin crosslinking

Introduction

Cancer remains the second leading cause of mortality worldwide. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), cancer-related deaths are projected to increase by approximately 45% between 2008 and 2030 [1]. The disease results from uncontrolled cell division and metastasis to healthy tissues. Among malignancies, breast cancer is the most prevalent neoplastic disease in women and the primary cause of cancer-associated death [2]. Due to the increase in the prevalence of cancer, the resulting mortality, and the low performance of chemotherapy and radiation therapy methods in the treatment of advanced cancer, there is a need for new ways to fight cancer [3]. The major challenge in chemotherapy is the lack of drug selectivity toward cancer cells, leading to systemic toxicity and the development of drug resistance [4]. To reduce these side effects and improve the performance of existing drugs, various drug delivery systems have been developed [4]. In recent years, nanotechnology has been the newest solution in cancer treatment [1, 5]. Nanoscale drug delivery systems (DDS) allow for controlled and targeted delivery of therapeutic agents, improving pharmacokinetics and therapeutic index. Key advantages of nanocarriers include ease of synthesis, biodegradability, prolonged circulation time and preferential accumulation in tumor tissues via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect [6]. A Biodegradable and biocompatible polymeric NPs are widely used in drug delivery systems. Among these polymer systems, those based on protein are more widely used. Proteins are a group of large natural molecules that have a unique structure and have potential applications in the field of biology. Nanomaterials obtained from proteins are biodegradable, non-toxic, do not stimulate the immune system, and have high [7, 8]. Nanoparticles (NPs) made from serum albumin have high drug loading capacity and very few side effects on non-cancerous tissues [9]. Also, these NPs are biodegradable, and their preparation method is simple. Human serum albumin (HSA) is a suitable carrier for medicine and the NPs obtained from it are also compatible with the human body. Due to the negative charge of albumin in the natural pH of the body, there are no problems such as rapid removal from the bloodstream, which is seen in particles with a positive charge. In addition, the surface of albumin NPs has many amine and carboxyl groups, which is suitable for the covalent connection of drugs or proteins [8, 10–12]. In protein-based NPs, cross-linkers such as glutaraldehyde are used to stabilize NPs, but their toxicity is high. Therefore, the use of natural cross-linkers such as Genipin (Gnp) (an aglycone of geniposide obtained from gardenia fruit) is essential due to its very low toxicity. In previous studies, genipin has been used for protein-mediated drug loading [13]. Gnp is used as a cross-linking agent for proteins, collagen, and chitosan, which is much less toxic and more biocompatible compared to GA [14, 15]. Methyltestosterone (MT) is a steroid hormone with trade names Testred, Metandren, and Android [16, 17]. This drug is prescribed as an alternative treatment for men whose male testosterone levels are not normal and also for adolescent boys who have delayed puberty [18]. MT, when combined with estrogen, is also used in the treatment of menopausal symptoms, pregnancy-related edema, and breast discomfort [19]. In addition to the above, MT was approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1973 for breast cancer treatment [20]. The anticancer effect of methyltestosterone in hormone-responsive breast cancer cells is thought to be mediated through the androgen receptor (AR). Although MCF-7 cells are classically estrogen receptor-positive (ER+), they also express low to moderate levels of functional AR. Emerging evidence indicates that AR activation in ER+ breast cancer cells can exert anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects [21, 22]. Therefore, delivering MT via a targeted nanocarrier may enhance its AR-mediated therapeutic activity while minimizing off-target effects.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report the encapsulation of methyltestosterone in genipin-crosslinked human serum albumin nanoparticles. While albumin-based nanocarriers have been widely investigated for various anticancer agents, the application of this system for methyltestosterone delivery has not been previously explored. Moreover, our study provides the first comparative in-vitro evaluation of the cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic activity of MT-HSA nanoparticles versus free MT in MCF-7 breast cancer cells.

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the pharmacological efficacy and in-vitro toxicological behavior of genipin-crosslinked MT-HSA nanoparticles compared with free methyltestosterone in a hormone-responsive breast cancer model.

Materials and methods

Methyltestosterone (MT) was purchased from Aburaihan Pharmaceutical Company (Iran). Genipin (Gnp) was obtained from Challenge Bioproducts Co., Ltd. (Taiwan). Human serum albumin (HSA) and dialysis membranes (12 kDa cutoff) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). All other reagents were of analytical grade and used as received.

Preparation of albumin nanoparticles containing drug by desolvation method

Albumin NPs were made using the desolvation method [23]. A 5 mg Albumin solution was first prepared in 10 mM NaCl. 1 mg of MT was added and ultrasonicated using a probe-type sonicator at 20 W for 30 min [24]. Then The pH of the solution was adjusted to 8.0 using NaOH and the mixture was stirred magnetically at 550 rpm.

8 mL of Ethanol was added dropwise (1 mL/min) using a syringe pump until the solution turned cloudy, indicating nanoparticle formation. To stabilize the formed nanoparticles, 200 µL of genipin (Gnp) solution (0.5% w/v) was added to solution and the reaction mixture was stirred for 24 h at room temperature (550 rpm). The resulting nanoparticles were centrifuged (8000 rpm, 30 min) three times to remove unreacted components and unloaded drug.

Determination of zeta potential and size of nanoparticles

Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) (Malvern Instruments Ltd. Worcestershire, UK) was used to measure the average diameter of NPs. 1 ml of the synthesized NPs were poured into the cuvette and placed in the device, placed that the sample is read, and PDI and average size of NPs were measured. DLS was also used to assess the zeta potential of HSA-NPs containing MT. To measure the surface charge of nanoparticles, the solution (1 ml) was poured into the cuvette and placed in the device, place that the sample is read. All measurements were performed in triplicate (n = 3).

Characterization of NPs

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The morphology and microstructure of MT-loaded HSA nanoparticles were examined using a scanning electron microscope (KYKY EM3200, China) operated at an accelerating voltage of 10–15 kV. The nanoparticle suspension was diluted fivefold with distilled water and sonicated for 3 min to ensure uniformity. A few drops of the sample were placed on a clean glass slide and dried at room temperature. To improve image quality and prevent charging effects, the dried sample was coated with a thin layer of gold before imaging.

X-ray powder diffraction (XRD)

The crystalline structures of pure MT, HSA and MT-loaded HSA nanoparticles were analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD Laboratory, University of Kashan). The diffraction scans were recorded between 10° and 40° (2θ) to evaluate the crystalline and amorphous nature of the samples.

Calibration curve of MT

A calibration curve of MT was constructed to determine the drug concentration. MT solutions with known concentrations (0.09–0.9 mg/mL) were scanned using a UV-visible spectrophotometer in the wavelength range of 200–400 nm. Sharp peaks were observed at 241 nm. The absorbance values obtained with related concentrations were recorded and drawn the calibration equation was obtained as y = 1.254x + 0.009.

Drug loading efficiency and encapsulation efficiency calculation of NPs

To determine the drug loading efficiency (LE) and encapsulation efficiency (EE), the amount of free MT in the supernatant was quantified spectrophotometrically at 241 nm. In vitro drug release

In-Vitro drug release was studied by the dialysis bag diffusion technique. Two milliliters of the MT-HSA nanoparticle suspension were transferred into the dialysis bag (12 kDa cutoff) and placed in 100 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at different pH values (5.5, 6.8 and 7.4). The system was kept at 37 °C with continuous stirring at 550 rpm for 100 h. At predetermined intervals, 2 mL of the external medium was withdrawn and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS. The concentration of released MT was measured by UV-Vis spectroscopy at 241 nm and cumulative release was calculated. Release studies were performed in triplicate (n = 3).

Stability studies

HSA nanoparticles was assessed by monitoring changes in hydrodynamic size and zeta potential after storage at 4 °C for two months. DLS measurements were performed as described in section “Determination of zeta potential and size of nanoparticles”. Stability was assessed in triplicate (n = 3).

Cell culture

The human breast cancer cell line MCF-7 was cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA, Sigma), 2 mM L-glutamine (Sigma), 100 IU/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. The culture medium was changed every other day. Subculturing was performed at 70–80% confluence using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA (Sigma).

MCF-7 cells were selected as a hormone-responsive breast cancer model due to their estrogen receptor positivity, which makes them suitable for evaluating steroid-based therapeutic agents.

Cell treatment

After seeding at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well in 24-well plates, cells were incubated for 24 h. After washing with PBS at pH 7.4, the cells were divided into three groups:

Group I: treated with MT.

Group II: treated with HSA nanoparticles.

Group III: treated with MT loaded HSA nanoparticles.

Each group was exposed to six concentrations: 5 nM, 10 nM, 20 nM, 30 nM, 40 nM and 60 nM. The cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 48 h in DMEM medium containing 0.2% BSA. All experiments were repeated three times (n = 3).

Cell viability assay (MTT)

Cell viability was determined using the MTT assay according to standard procedures [26]. MCF-7 cells at density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated in 200 µL DMEM medium containing 0.2% BSA. After 24 h, treatment media were added and incubated for 48 h. The results were spectrophotometrically measured for absorbance, and the cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to control cells. All experiments were repeated three times (n = 3).

Cell cytotoxicity measurement (LDH assay)

The extent of cytotoxicity was determined by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release using the LDH Cytotoxicity Detection Kit (Roche, Germany) [27]. Cells were plated at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/mL in 24-well plates and incubated for 12 h prior to treatment. After 48 h of exposure, LDH activity was measured at 490 nm (reference ≥ 600 nm) using an ELISA reader (EL800, USA). All experiments were repeated three times.

Quantification of apoptosis incidence

Apoptotic cells were detected using the TUNEL (Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP Nick End Labeling) assay with an In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Roche)[28]. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (pH 7.4) for 10 min, permeabilized and incubated with fluorescein-labeled nucleotides and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT). Following counterstaining with propidium iodide (10 µg/mL) for 15 min, samples were analyzed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus AX-70). The apoptotic index was determined by counting the TUNEL-positive cells in eight randomly selected fields and calculating the ratio of apoptotic to total cells.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data are presented as mean ± SEM. Data analysis was conducted by SPSS software, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Multiple groups were compared using one-way ANOVA analysis, followed by a post hoc multiple comparisons test (Tukey’s test). The value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Overview

Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women and the second most common cause of death [29]. Due to the complexity of the mechanism and the presence of multiple factors in causing cancer, no definitive treatment has been found for this disease, but today, various treatment methods are used to treat breast cancer. That is why, new targeted cancer treatments that have led to the specificity of tumor treatment and reduced toxicity have been investigated [30]. In this study, methyltestosterone-loaded human serum albumin nanoparticles (MT-HSA NPs) were successfully synthesized using the desolvation method and extensively characterized in terms of their physicochemical properties, in vitro drug release, stability and cytotoxicity against the MCF-7 cell line.

Overall, the results demonstrate the successful preparation and comprehensive evaluation of a stable MT-HSA nanoparticle system designed for breast cancer therapy.

Physicochemical characterization of MT-HSA nanoparticles

Particle size and zeta potential

One of the key physical properties that determine the behavior of nanoparticles in colloidal solutions is zeta potential [31]. For the determination of the size and surface charge, Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) was used to analyze the synthesized nanoparticles. A mean hydrodynamic diameter of 83 ± 4.2 nm with a PDI of 0.25 ± 0.03 and a zeta potential of -16.3 ± 1.8 mV was obtained (n = 3), which indicates good uniformity and moderate colloidal stability (Table 1). These results confirm that monodisperse nanoparticles suitable for biological applications have been fabricated [32].

In summary, these findings demonstrate that the fabricated MT-HSA NPs possess uniform nanoscale dimensions and appropriate surface charge for efficient cellular uptake and stability in biological media.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

SEM analysis demonstrated that the synthesized MT-HSA nanoparticles exhibited a spherical morphology with smooth surfaces and uniform distribution (Fig. 1). The observed average size of 83 nm was consistent with the DLS results. In this regard, Kimura et al. using the desolvation method, synthesized spherical HSA-DOX NPs with a size of 108 [33]. These observations confirm that the desolvation process yielded uniformly shaped and well-dispersed MT-HSA nanoparticles, supporting their suitability as nanocarriers for drug delivery.

X-ray powder diffraction (XRD)

The XRD patterns of pure MT, pure HSA and MT-HSA nanoparticles are presented in Fig. 2. Pure MT exhibited sharp diffraction peaks at 2θ = 14.475°, 15.775°, and 16.825°, indicating its crystalline nature. In contrast, MT-HSA nanoparticles showed a broad and diffused pattern, suggesting that MT was present in an amorphous or molecularly dispersed form within the albumin matrix. This reduction in crystallinity confirms successful encapsulation and uniform drug distribution [34, 35]. Therefore, XRD data clearly indicate that the incorporation of MT into the albumin matrix converted it from a crystalline to an amorphous state, enhancing its solubility and potential bioavailability.

Drug entrapment efficiency and loading capacity

Using the UV–Vis calibration curve (λmax = 241 nm, y = 1.254x + 0.009), the encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL) of MT-HSA nanoparticles were calculated as 77% and 11%, respectively. These values indicate efficient drug incorporation into the HSA matrix. Subia and colleagues loaded MTX into NPs composed of a combination of albumin and silk fibroin. The DEE ranged from 14% to 18% and its EE was about 83–87%. Of course, desolvation was the method they used to prepare these nanoparticles [36].

In summary, the high EE and moderate DL values demonstrate that the HSA-based formulation efficiently entraps MT, minimizing drug loss during synthesis and ensuring controlled release.

In vitro drug release studies

The release profile of MT from MT-HSA nanoparticles was investigated varying pH conditions (pH 5.5, pH 6.8, and pH 7.4) for a period of 100 h (Fig. 3). Results demonstrated a sustained and pH-dependent release pattern, with higher release observed at lower pH. Cumulative drug releases of about 83% at pH 5.5, 74% at pH 6.8, and 60% at pH 7.4 were observed after 80 h of study period.This behavior reflects enhanced drug diffusion under acidic conditions, mimicking the tumor microenvironment. The sustained release profile confirms the suitability of HSA nanoparticles as carriers for prolonged MT delivery [37]. These results prove that potential of MT-HSA NPs for controlled and pH-responsive drug delivery in tumor-like environments.

Physical stability of NPs

Investigation of NPs stability was assessed as it could be vital for future applications [38]. Stability was evaluated by measuring particle size after two months of storage at 4 °C. Only a slight increase in size from 83 nm to 90–95 nm was observed, with no visible aggregation, confirming good physical stability of the nanoparticles over time. These results are in agreement with previous studies reporting stable albumin nanoparticle formulations [38, 39].This observation suggests that the fabricated MT-HSA NPs maintain their colloidal integrity during storage, an important prerequisite for future in-vivo applications.

Summary of findings

Collectively, these results indicate that MT-HSA nanoparticles were successfully synthesized with optimal physicochemical properties, high encapsulation efficiency, sustained drug release, and excellent physical stability.

Taken together, the findings validate MT-HSA nanoparticles as a promising platform for targeted, controlled, and biocompatible delivery of methyltestosterone in breast cancer treatment.

Discussion

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy among women and remains a major cause of cancer-related death [40]. The lack of selectivity and systemic toxicity associated with conventional therapies underscores the need for advanced drug delivery strategies. In fact, Albumin-based Nano systems gained attention because of their biocompatibility but also for their well-defined physicochemical behavior, structural stability and favorable ligand-binding properties that make them attractive platforms for chemical modification and controlled drug release [41–43]. In this study, genipin-crosslinked human serum albumin nanoparticles were engineered as a nanocarrier for methyltestosterone that could provide improved solubility, structural stabilization and sustained delivery of this hydrophobic anticancer agent.

The physicochemical evaluation of the synthesized MT-HSA nanoparticles revealed an average size of 83 nm, a uniform distribution (PDI 0.25) and a moderately negative zeta potential. From a pharmacological perspective, nanoparticles within this nanoscale range are more likely to facilitate enhanced cellular uptake and sustain intracellular drug availability, thereby improving therapeutic response. The negative surface charge also reflects the ionization state of albumin at physiological pH and contributes to electrostatic stabilization, reducing the likelihood of protein–protein bridging in biological fluids [41–45].

SEM imaging confirmed the formation of spherical and homogeneously distributed nanoparticles, consistent with the desolvation-driven nucleation and growth process of albumin matrices. XRD further analysis revealed that MT transitioned from a crystalline to an amorphous state after encapsulation. This amorphization is chemically important: the disruption of MT’s crystalline lattice decreases thermodynamic stability and enhances dissolution and molecular dispersion inside the albumin matrix, which ultimately improves bioavailability. Such transitions have been similarly reported for hydrophobic compounds incorporated into protein-based nanocarriers [43].

The use of genipin as a natural crosslinker played a central role in the developed system’s structural chemistry. The Genipin forms stable covalent bridges with lysine residues on albumin, creating a three-dimensional network that enhances rigidity, reduces premature swelling and controls drug diffusion kinetics. Compared with aldehyde-based agents, genipin offers lower toxicity and more predictable crosslink density, making it advantageous for biomedical formulations [46, 47]. This structural reinforcement directly influenced the pH-responsive release pattern observed: MT release was significantly faster at acidic pH (5.5) than under neutral or physiological conditions. Acid-sensitive release behavior is driven by partial loosening of albumin–genipin interactions and enhanced protonation of functional groups in acidic environments, resulting in higher diffusivity of MT. This can be quite useful for tumor-targeted delivery, as the extracellular tumor matrix and intracellular endo-lysosomal compartments are typically acidic [15, 45].

The biological assays complemented the physicochemical findings. MT-HSA nanoparticles exhibited stronger cytotoxicity and apoptosis induction in MCF-7 cells compared with the free drug. From a pharmacodynamic standpoint, the enhanced activity of MT-HSA nanoparticles may result from improved intracellular drug accumulation, sustained release kinetics, and albumin-mediated cellular uptake pathways. It was reported that albumin nanoparticlesgp60-mediated transcytosis and SPARC binding, which enhance intracellular drug accumulation and may partially account for the heightened therapeutic response observed [41, 47]. Additionally, the observed anti-proliferative effect of MT in MCF-7 cells may involve androgen receptor (AR) signaling. Although MCF-7 cells are classically ER-positive, they also express functional AR. Previous studies have shown that AR activation in ER-positive breast cancer cells can lead to growth inhibition and apoptosis [21, 22]. Therefore, the sustained release of MT from HSA nanoparticles may provide prolonged AR occupancy, potentially contributing to the enhanced cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic activity observed in our study. The minimal toxicity of unloaded HSA nanoparticles further confirms the intrinsic biocompatibility of the albumin–genipin matrix and supports its suitability as a delivery scaffold [48].

The two-month stability assessment at 4 °C showed only minimal size variation, indicating robust structural integrity of the crosslinked network. This aspect is vital from a formulation chemistry perspective because aggregation or instability can compromise therapeutic performance and shelf-life.

Overall, the present findings indicate that genipin-crosslinked HSA nanoparticles significantly enhance the in-vitro pharmacological performance of methyltestosterone compared with the free drug. The nanoformulation promoted greater cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic responses in MCF-7 cells, which may be attributed to sustained drug release and improved intracellular availability mediated by the albumin carrier system. Importantly, unloaded HSA nanoparticles demonstrated negligible cytotoxicity, supporting the biocompatibility of the delivery platform. These results suggest that albumin-based nanoencapsulation may improve the therapeutic index of steroid-based agents by enhancing pharmacodynamic activity while maintaining a favorable in-vitro safety profile. Although further in-vivo pharmacokinetic and systemic toxicity studies are required to confirm translational potential, the present data provides a pharmacological basis for considering MT-HSA nanoparticles as a promising strategy for optimizing hormone-responsive breast cancer therapy.

Conclusion

In this study, a genipin-crosslinked human serum albumin nanocarrier was successfully developed to improve the delivery profile of methyltestosterone. The physicochemical analyses confirm the formation of uniform nanoparticles, spherical in shape, with a mean size of 83 nm and a negative zeta potential and a spherical morphology, which collectively support colloidal stability and suitability for biological interaction. The XRD results showed the transition of MT from crystalline to amorphous phase within the albumin matrix, indicating enhanced solubility and molecular dispersion. High encapsulation efficiency and the pH-responsive, sustained release behavior highlight the favorable chemical interactions between MT, albumin and the genipin-stabilized network.

Biological assessments further showed that MT-HSA nanoparticles significantly increased cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic activity in MCF-7 cells compared with free MT, whereas unloaded HSA nanoparticles exhibited minimal toxicity. These findings further establish the fact that the albumin–genipin matrix not only stabilizes MT and also enhances its therapeutic performance. Overall, the formulation integrates controlled release behavior with enhanced pharmacological efficacy and improved apoptotic induction, supporting MT-HSA nanoparticles as a promising strategy for optimizing steroid-based breast cancer therapy.

Limitations

Despite the promising results, this study has several limitations. First, all biological evaluations were restricted to a single breast cancer cell line (MCF-7), which cannot fully represent the heterogeneity of breast tumors. Second, the experiments were conducted exclusively in vitro; therefore, the pharmacokinetic behavior, biodistribution, metabolic stability, and potential systemic toxicity of the MT-HSA nanoparticles have not been characterized. Third, although physicochemical stability was assessed over a two-month period, long-term stability under physiological or simulated physiological conditions was not examined. Fourth, the raw data from the drug release experiments were not available for calculation of standard deviation values; consequently, error bars could not be included in Fig. 3. This limitation has been acknowledged, and complete raw data retention will be ensured in future studies.

Furthermore, the molecular mechanisms underlying the observed apoptosis such as caspase activation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, or mitochondrial pathway involvement were not investigated in this study. Cellular uptake studies (e.g., fluorescence imaging of labeled nanoparticles) were also beyond the scope of this initial in-vitro characterization. Additionally, the colloidal stability of MT-HSA nanoparticles in serum-containing biological media (e.g., DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS) was not assessed. This parameter is critical for predicting nanoparticle behavior under physiological conditions, as serum proteins can adsorb onto nanoparticle surfaces and alter their size, surface charge, and aggregation state.

Finally, mechanistic aspects related to nanoparticle-cell interactions, cellular uptake pathways, and intracellular trafficking were beyond the scope of this study. The absence of in-vivo pharmacokinetic and systemic toxicity evaluation also limits direct translational conclusions. Addressing these limitations through comprehensive in-vivo and mechanistic studies is necessary to move forward toward the clinical applicability of this nanocarrier.

Future perspectives

For future research, in-vivo studies are required to evaluate the biodistribution, tumor-targeting ability, and pharmacodynamic behavior of MT-HSA nanoparticles. Investigating the interaction of these nanoparticles with immune cells, plasma proteins, and tumor vasculature will provide deeper insight into their biocompatibility and clinical potential.

Furthermore, the decoration of targeting ligands such as folic acid, peptides, or antibodies on the surface of HSA nanoparticles may enhance selective uptake by cancer cells and further improve therapeutic efficacy. The integration of such strategies could lead to the development of a next-generation HSA-based delivery platform not only for hormone-related therapies but also for other chemotherapeutic agents.

Future studies should also focus on elucidating the apoptotic signaling pathways involved specifically, caspase-3/9 activation, Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, and mitochondrial membrane potential as well as visualizing cellular uptake using fluorescently labeled HSA nanoparticles to better understand the intracellular fate of MT-HSA nanoparticles.
