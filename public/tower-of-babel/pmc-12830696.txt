A PIKAN-based model for the prediction of the temperature fields of castings
Authors: Qichao Zhao, Baiqiao Wang, Jinwu Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the casting process, solving various physical quantities centered on the temperature field through numerical simulation is very helpful for optimizing the process. The rapid development of deep learning technology offers potential for industrial applications and can represent a new simulation methodology. This study proposes a PIKAN model that groups spatiotemporal parameters and inputs them through an MLP model. During the training process, this study used a loss function that combines physical loss and data loss, and identified the optimal weight parameters through Bayesian optimization. Pre-training with multiple casting geometries as labels has improved the efficiency of model prediction. With an absolute error of 10 K as the critical value, the average temperature error of this study is 5.62 K, and the average accuracy reaches 88.74%. This study predicts the evolution of the two-dimensional temperature field during the casting solidification process using deep learning, supporting the application of new technologies in engineering.Keywords: Deep learning, PIKAN, Casting, Simulation, Heat transferSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Artificial intelligence has currently demonstrated its significant potential across various domains. Notable examples attracting significant attention include large-scale intelligent models represented by ChatGPT1 and DeepSeek2. Beyond conversational functionalities, AI has been extensively applied in computer science3, healthcare4, industrial sectors5, and transportation systems6. Compared with traditional numerical computation methods, the open-source ecosystem provides diverse encapsulated libraries for constructing AI models. These models can be efficiently computed on high-performance computing clusters with parallel GPU architectures, while optimized learning algorithms ensure rapid convergence during the training process. Consequently, AI technology exhibits broad application prospects in engineering fields requiring numerical computations.

The most prominent area in artificial intelligence is currently deep learning technology, which performs computations based on neural network models. Traditional deep learning methods train networks on datasets to obtain appropriate fitting relationships. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) such as U-net7 can address image-related problems, while recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and LSTM8 are capable of handling time-dependent data. Generative models such as Generative Adversarial Nets (GANs)9 learn data distributions to synthesize new samples. Data-driven neural network models typically require large datasets and often suffer from poor interpretability. Consequently, novel approaches incorporating physical principles, such as physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) and neural operators, have been proposed. The DeepONet10 operator learns functions and spatial variables in two separate branch networks and computes their inner product to produce the final output. The PINN11 algorithm introduces the residual of physical equations as part of the loss function, enabling label-free learning while improving the interpretability of the training process. These methods have expanded the applicability of deep learning under various conditions.

Deep learning has demonstrated significant maturity in solving partial differential equations (PDEs), prompting several researchers to explore its specific applications in this domain. Jiang et al.12 designed a GAN-based network model to solve laminar convection heat transfer problems in two-dimensional square cavities. They designed multiple equally sized images to represent speed, pressure, temperature, time, and each pixel in the image represents a different value at a different location. The initial values of these physical quantities and their values at a certain moment are used as inputs and labels for training. Cheng et al.13 predicted the velocity field and pressure field of the flow process through a PINN network composed of multiple residual blocks. They use time, spatial coordinates, and fluid parameters as inputs to simulate the flow field by optimizing data loss and physical loss. Kushwaha et al. applied the DeepONet model to solve the temperature and stress fields in continuous casting and additive manufacturing processes14. The model uses a main network and a sub network to process position information and process parameters, respectively, and multiplies the outputs of both networks to obtain the total output. These studies provide valuable references for implementing deep learning techniques in casting process simulations.

The aforementioned studies have demonstrated the potential of deep learning, yet further in-depth exploration remains necessary. In continuous casting processes, the morphology of the pouring system remains fixed, while additive manufacturing involves only the physical transformation of a single material. Both processes control the casting solely through adjustments to process parameters. In contrast, for manufacturing processes such as sand casting, the shape of the casting, thermophysical parameters, and initial and boundary conditions collectively influence the evolution of the physical field, necessitating the development of more suitable network models. The core idea of PINN is to incorporate the residuals of PDEs along with data errors into the loss function, thereby enabling simultaneous supervision of the training process by both empirical data and physical principles. However, conventional PINN models, which typically employ MLPs (Multi-Layer Perceptron), exhibit significant computational errors. The objective of this study is to enhance the network model to accurately simulate the two-dimensional temperature field evolution in sand casting processes.

Model

PIKAN (physics-informed kolmogorov–arnold network) model

PINNs can be categorized into supervised and unsupervised learning based on the availability of real-world data. Supervised learning incorporates both data loss, which represents the error between predicted values and actual measurements, and physics loss, which denotes the residuals of governing physical equations. In contrast, unsupervised learning solely employs physics loss. Regarding network architecture selection, PINNs can be integrated with various neural network models, among which the fully connected feedforward neural network is the most commonly adopted. However, the computational accuracy of the network structure used by PINN needs to be improved.

The research problem addressed in this study concerns the temperature field variation in two-dimensional castings. Consequently, the model is designed to accept different spatiotemporal coordinates (t, x, y) and the corresponding temperature values as inputs and outputs. The traditional PINN is based on the MLP network structure, and its calculation accuracy is relatively low. Inspired by the KAN model15, this study draws on the KAN model and makes certain simplification modifications to the model. At the same time, the PINN algorithm is used as a reference for the loss function, resulting in the PIKAN model.

The KAN model is generally better at approximating PDE than MLP, primarily because the solutions of many physical processes can naturally be expressed as a composite of multiple simple functions. Moreover, Kolmogorov-Arnold’s theorem, under strict mathematical conditions, states that if f is a multivariate continuous function, then f can be expressed as a finite composition of univariate continuous functions through a two-layer nested superposition, as shown in (1). In contrast, the MLP model relies on the general approximation theorem, which approximates functions through linear combinations and nonlinear activations of neurons. The model does not explicitly construct a composite structure of functions, and learning a complex function often requires more parameter fitting.

Therefore, the network initially processes each input dimension through multiple sets of univariate function networks (φi). Subsequently, the outputs from each φ network undergo nonlinear transformations via multiple G networks, with all G network outputs ultimately being integrated, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The φi network models univariate functions, while the G network captures interactions between different inputs. In order to balance the accuracy and efficiency of calculations, each part of the network is composed of basic modules, each of which uses a combination of linear transformations and the SiLU activation function, as shown in (2). The modules of the φi network have added residual modules at the end.

Loss function

The loss function L can be regarded as a function with network parameter as variable. According to the PINN approach, the loss function in this study can be written as (3) to (5):

where LPDE and Ldata represent partial differential equation loss and data loss.

The loss LPDE represents the physical constraint of the equation, and its value is equal to the residual sum of the network predicted values substituted into the physical equation. To prevent excessive temperature gradient at the boundary from causing calculation loss, the physical equation is a discretized Fourier heat transfer equation. LPDE is the mean square root of the PDE residuals for each data point.

For all Ndata pixels on the temperature field image at each supervision moment, Ldata is the average of the mean square root between the predicted values and the true values for each pixel.

For the weight parameter of the loss function, this study uses Bayesian search to find its optimal solutions. The principle of this algorithm is Bayes’ theorem of probability theory, and the core idea is to construct an approximate probability model through a Gaussian process to fit the unknown objective function. Through historical information and collection functions, the unknown area is explored, and the next point with the most promising improvement is selected for evaluation. This algorithm can dynamically adjust the search path and significantly reduce the number of evaluations.

Parameter setting

Sampling point parameters

The number of sampling points Np is primarily employed in computing the loss function LPDE. Np data points were randomly selected throughout the entire predicted time range and in space, with each data point representing an array composed of three spatiotemporal coordinates t, x, y. To prevent numerical instability caused by excessively large gradients, Np points are chosen to be positions that are not too close to the inner and outer boundaries. Insufficient sampling points may result in inadequate coverage, whereas an excessive number increases computational and memory overhead. Experimental verification has shown that setting the number of sampling points to 4096 is appropriate.

Optimizer parameters

The optimizer chosen for this study is Adam, which is the culmination of deep learning optimization algorithms and integrates ideas such as stochastic gradient descent, momentum, and adaptive step size.

For the setting of the learning rate, a larger learning rate is needed in the early stage of optimization to quickly approach the extremum point and accelerate convergence. When the parameter optimization approaches the extremum point, a smaller learning rate is used to avoid oscillation. Therefore, the configuration of the learning rate should follow the decay strategy. The commonly used linear preheating with cosine decay was chosen. Considering the number of parameters in the network model, is set to 0.01 in order to balance learning efficiency and model convergence.

Model structure parameters

The number of layers La in research refers to the number of fully connected layers, while the number of hidden dims Hd refers to the number of neurons in each layer, representing the complexity of the model, as shown in Fig. 1. The increase in this quantity can improve the ability of feature extraction, but it can also lead to an increase in parameter count and overfitting. Considering the number of datasets and experimental hardware configuration, La and Hd are set to 3 and 24.

The number of φ-functions within a single group Phi represents how many functions are used to represent the original function at each step, which determines how many nonlinear transformations are provided for each input variable, as shown in Fig. 1. In the standard KAN model, it is a part of mathematical expressions with a fixed number and is related to the number of network layers. In our study, the univariate function is the MLP model, and its learning process is an approximate process. Therefore, this parameter can be modified. Meanwhile, this parameter represents a positive correlation between the number of G1 networks and φ networks, which has a significant impact on the number of trainable parameters for the entire network. Therefore, in order to balance the number of parameters and learning accuracy, Phi was set to 5.

Evaluation criteria

In order to evaluate the effectiveness of the model, referring to the results of our research group16, two indicators, mean error loss (diff) and accuracy (acc), were used.

Mean error (diff) is defined as the mean square error between the predicted results of the model and the actual temperature at any testing moment, as shown in (7).

The pouring temperature of the casting is 973.15 K, so the error range is located at about 1% of the pouring temperature, which is 10 K. Therefore, the accuracy (acc) is defined as the percentage of the total area of the casting system where the absolute error between the predicted result and the actual temperature at any testing moment is less than 10 K, as shown in (8).

To test the predictive stability of the model, uncertainty is quantified through deep integration17. This study randomly initialized the model parameters by adding random seeds. When training and predicting each casting model, except for the model that do not use random seeds, the model was trained three times using 10, 50, and 100 random seeds, respectively. For each casting model, the standard deviation of the predicted result at each pixel position of the above four models was calculated, and the average standard deviation of all data points was used as an indicator to measure uncertainty, as shown in (9).

Training and predicting results

The PIKAN model was used to predict the temporal variation of temperature fields in two-dimensional castings and molds. The mold used in this study is a square with a side length of 0.64 m. The predicted time period is the temperature field from 0 to 300 s (seconds) after pouring, and the predicted time interval is 25s. Five casting models with different shapes were selected from the dataset, and each model selected the data from the 13 time periods mentioned above as the standard for testing the simulation effect. In order to monitor the temperature field changes of castings through data, a multi-time temperature field every 50 s within the time range of 0 to 300 s was selected as the dataset for Ldata.

Pre-training and training for the model

Simulating with the PIKAN model can achieve high accuracy, but the computational efficiency is relatively low. Therefore, this study adopts a pre-training method to improve computational efficiency. The pre-training process is shown in Fig. 7.

The process of the simulation method combining pre-training and training.

In the pre-training stage, this study used 830 casting models of different shapes and their temperature fields at 13 time instants as the training data set. The validation set accounted for 10% of all data, and the loss function of the network model during pre-training is only Ldata. The epoch is set to 10. After pre-training, the average accuracy of the test set was 32.69%. The pre-training time is 3316 s.

During the training phase, the parameters of the pre-trained network model are first read. On this basis, the parameters were optimized, with 25 warm-up steps and the same parameters as the PIKAN model. The casting models 1 to 5 were used for prediction, and their average accuracy and calculation time were recorded. Under the same prediction accuracy, pre-trained models have fewer training steps. For example, as shown in Table 4, when the average accuracy is approximately the same, the pre-trained model and the non-pre-trained model were optimized 2500 and 5000 times, with average training times of 910 s and 1620 s, respectively.

Comparison between different models

To evaluate the performance of the model in this study, we compared the PIKAN model with the PINN model based on MLP and the standard KAN model. In order to prevent gradient explosion in the prediction results, the maximum learning rate of the MLP model is 0.001, and the of the two models is chosen as 4.02 × 10− 8. At the end of the output layer, a Sigmoid activation function is added. The loss functions of PINN and the of PIKAN are physics equation residuals and data-driven, while the standard KAN is only data-driven.

The standard KAN model uses the parameters of the B-line function as the learnable parameters of the model, which is essentially an interpolation function that requires setting some parameters during training. Grid represents the number of intervals that divide the value range of each input dimension. To improve the accuracy, the grid requires a larger value, so it is set to 30. K represents the polynomial order of the interpolation basis function. The calculation equation for heat transfer problems is not complex, so k is set to 3. Based on KAN literatures, when the input dimension is 3, Hd and La can be set to 5 and 2, respectively. The network model structure of the standard KAN is shown in Fig. 9. The PIKAN used in this study adopts the KAN grouping strategy, which can more accurately learn the relationships between different parameters. Compared with MLP networks, it can approach the solution of PDE equations using fewer parameters. Compared to the standard KAN model, due to the clear heat transfer calculation PDE equation in this study, linear transformations and nonlinear activation functions are more suitable for this task compared to high-order polynomials.

The primary distinction between the PIKAN model and PINNs and their variants lies in the fact that improvements in PINN models primarily focus on training mechanisms and loss functions, while research on structural modifications to the model is relatively scarce. The PIKAN model replaces the original learnable linear combination weights with multiple learnable univariate functions, thereby enabling more effective learning when dealing with equations possessing complex structures or sharp gradient variations. Under comparable parameter conditions, PIKAN generally exhibits higher learning efficiency and faster convergence rates.

Limitations and future research

The PIKAN model proposed in this paper is currently applied only to two-dimensional castings. For real three-dimensional castings, which involve more complex geometries, initial conditions, and boundary conditions, its application presents significantly greater challenges. Furthermore, the PDEs differ under various process conditions, and the generalization capability of the model itself requires further enhancement.

Currently, the PDE residual only serves as one component of the loss function to guide the model. Relying solely on the deep learning model without sufficient physical constraints may lead to solutions that violate physical laws. Consequently, the model’s learning still depends on a certain amount of simulated data, and these aspects necessitate further research.

Conclusions

This study investigated the prediction of temperature field evolution in castings using the PIKAN model, compared it with other network architectures. The results indicate that the predictive performance of the PIKAN model is acceptable.

(1) A neural network model named PIKAN was established to simulate the temperature field changes during the casting process of two-dimensional casting. PIKAN is a model based on PINN and KAN that faces multiple input parameters. This model establishes a composite MLP structure, including φ networks and G networks. The φ networks learn the mapping relationships between different single inputs in groups, and the G networks learn the mapping relationships between different inputs. The loss function is set as a combination of PDE equation residuals and data errors.

(2) Using the temperature fields of 830 casting models with different shapes as the training set for pre-training improved the computational efficiency of the PIKAN model. The pre-trained PIKAN model was used to simulate the temperature field of 5 casting models with different shapes. Each model was selected to detect 13 time points, and the average temperature error obtained was 5.62 K, with an average accuracy of 88.74%.

(3) This study compared the predictive performance of the normal PINN model, the standard KAN model, and the PIKAN model. The computational efficiency of the PIKAN model is between the other two models, while maintaining relatively high prediction accuracy. It is shown that the PIKAN model can balance the relationship between accuracy and efficiency under certain circumstances.

(4) The simulation results of this study are two-dimensional and cannot currently be directly applied to real-world engineering. After adding the number of input parameters, the PIKAN model has the potential to simulate the temperature field of three-dimensional castings. In addition, by providing simulated or real data and PDE for a certain problem, the simulation capability of this model can be extended to other physical fields. In an industrial factory, a deep learning model based on PIKAN can be used to build a digital twin system in the future, improving production efficiency and making the production process more intelligent.
