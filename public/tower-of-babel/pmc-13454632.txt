Optimization the synergy of magnetic whole cell technology and microfluidic reactor for the production of epoxidized fatty acids from vegetable oil refinery by-product
Authors: Elham Tohfegar, Alireza Habibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This research investigates the epoxidation of unsaturated free fatty acids obtained from soap stock after treating through the degumming process. The Fe3O4-loaded whole-cells of Candida catenulata were used as the biocatalyst for the synthesis of epoxy fatty acids within serpentine and spiral microfluidic reactors under an electrically induced magnetic field (IMF). The magnetic properties of the biocatalyst particles were applied to improve the mixing behaviors of the immiscible reactants within the reactors. The syringe of the spiral microfluidic reactor and magnetic biocatalyst motions provide advantages to obtain a high epoxidation yield at low retention time. Also, the biocatalyst frequency under the IMF system showed a significant role on the epoxidation of fatty acids in this system. Therefore, optimization of the epoxidation reaction was evaluated by analyzing the effects of the reactant flow rate (X1), the biocatalyst loading (X2), and the frequency of the induced magnetic field (X3) in the microfluidic reactor. The optimal conditions were determined at X1 = 0.229 ml min− 1, X2 = 100 wv− 1, and X3 = 2.5 Hz with an epoxidation yield of 47.67% after 1.53 min as the hydrodynamic retention time. The cascading of microreactors for achieving high conversion efficiency was also studied and the results showed a cascade consisting of six spiral reactors gained an epoxy fatty acid conversion of about 90.17%. The epoxidation reaction at the continuous operation and 80.66% of the initial conversion yield was saved after 12 h.

Introduction

Epoxidized fatty acids (EFAs), often obtained from epoxidation of unsaturated vegetable fatty acids, are interesting industrial products due to the economic value, low-toxicity, and sustainability with an extensive application in the polymer industries as stabilizers, plasticizers, lubricants, or intermediates1,2. They enhance the flexibility of epoxy resins and polyvinyl chloride (PVC), and are also used in surface coatings and polyurethane foam synthesis as a viable alternative to crude oil-derived products3,4. A main parameter affecting in selection of fatty acids as the feed of the epoxidation reaction is iodine value. The iodine value is a measure of the number of unsaturated bonds in fatty acids. Therefore, a higher iodine value is favorable for the synthesis of epoxy fatty acids that are used in polymer applications due to the higher structural cross-connections and mechanical strength. Soybean and linseed oils are common vegetable oils that are utilized for the production of high-performance epoxies4. However, the rising costs of edible oils are prompting the exploration of alternative sources of unsaturated fatty acids in the last decades. Soap stock, which is an inherent by-product of the refining of edible oils, is rich in free fatty acids (FFAs) such as linolenic oleic, linoleic, and oleic acids, rendering it as a cost-effective feedstock for EFAs production. This approach has advantages in conversion of the industrial waste into a valuable product, and it also mitigating environmental impacts. Nevertheless, it is essential to treat soap stock for isolation of FFAs before epoxidation reactions5,6.

Catalytic techniques including Prilezhaev reaction, metal-catalyzed, and enzymatic epoxidation are often reported for the oxirane ring formation from the reaction between a double bond in FFAs and active oxygen6,7. Enzymatic methods offer an environmentally-friendly alternative to chemical epoxidation, owing to their selectivity, the prevention of oxirane ring opening, and the absence of acidic reagents8. However, the inactivation of enzymes in the presence of hydrogen peroxide as the inhibitory substrate poses a significant challenge for the enzymatic epoxidation processes. Previous findings showed that the immobilization of enzymes, especially through cross-linking, enhances stability and reusability of biocatalysts during the epoxidation reactions9. For this purpose, the application of magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) of Fe3O4 for enzyme immobilization was attractive for numerous researchers, because the obtained biocatalyst could be easily recovered from the reaction mixture by applying an external magnetic field and also the magnetic properties could be applied to facilitate the mixing of reactants and also temperature regulation10. Nevertheless, the enzymatic epoxidation due to the expensive cost of enzymes is restricted in the research stage and rarely applied at industrial scale11.

Promising findings in the application of whole-cell biocatalysts in the last decade offer a new insight for development of the robust and efficient biocatalysts for the epoxidation reactions. In fact, the intracellular lipases in certain microbial cells could be used as the catalyst of biological origin without needing to pass the expensive purification steps in usual enzymatic systems12. In 2023, the whole-cells of Rhizopus oryzae immobilized through glutaraldehyde cross-linking in a polyurethane foam were applied as an intracellular lipase system in the epoxidation of α-pinene and the results exhibited a notable improvement in catalytic efficiency when compared to the untreated cells where the epoxidation rate rising from 126.71 to 234.72 mmol L−1 h−1, and maintaining 83% of original efficiency after four cycles13. Recently, an efficient magnetic whole-cell biocatalyst (MWCB) based on Fe3O4 loaded Candida catenulata was reported for the epoxidation of fatty acids14. Alongside the importance of the biocatalyst type used in the epoxidation process, selecting the appropriate reactor type is also crucial, as it can significantly enhance the production efficiency of epoxy compounds, as discussed in greater detail below. Various reactors have been explored for this purpose, including stirred tanks operated in batch and semi-batch modes15, as well as continuous reactors such as tubular16, fluidized bed17, and rotating bed reactors18. For instance, in 2021, Perez et al. examined the differences between batch and semi-batch reactors in terms of their efficiency in the epoxidation of methyl oleate using H2O2 with aluminum oxide as a catalyst. They found that the semi-batch system increased the oxirane yield by 30% compared to the batch process15. Because the concentration of hydrogen peroxide in the reactor is consistently higher in semi-batch mode than in batch mode, it is entirely feasible to maintain a low flow rate of hydrogen peroxide in a semi-batch reactor15. In another study, a continuously rotating bed reactor system was evaluated against a batch reactor for the epoxidation of oleic acid, using immobilized lipase (Novozym 435) as the catalyst18. The results showed that the rotating reactor yield achieved a 14.30% improvement compared to the batch reactor. This enhancement is attributed to maintaining a low hydrogen peroxide concentration during the rotating bed reactor trials, which promoted the formation of a more stable emulsion and created milder reaction conditions in the presence of the catalyst. Furthermore, the incremental addition of hydrogen peroxide led to increased epoxidation yields due to the enhanced selectivity of the catalyst18. Also, Olivieri et al. employed a continuous tubular reactor for a rapid epoxidation of soybean oil with a net product output of 79.1% during a residence time of 120 min16.

Another aspect in the epoxidation of fatty acids is the the immiscible nature of reactants that limited the overall reaction rate in the system16. To overcome the mass transfer limitations and improve the mixing behaviors in these systems, application of magnetic biocatalysts and the movement of MWCBs under a magnetic field has shown promising approach19–21. Another innovative approach to enhancing the mixing of immiscible phases is the use of microreactors. Integrating these devices with magnetic processes enables the continuous production of biological products while reducing costs, energy consumption, and processing time. Microreactors also improve efficiency through enhanced micromixing and mass transfer, minimize secondary reactions, and optimize heat management. Their scalability and effective micromixing capabilities make them particularly well-suited for biological reactions22,23. Microreactors are classified into four types: microchannel reactors, slit channel reactors, fixed-bed microreactors, and membrane microreactors, with microchannels being the most critical and commonly utilized category. Direct-path microchannels were the initial microscale reactors documented for the production of organic products22. Microchannel reactors employ meso- or micro-scale geometries for their reaction pathways, which may exhibit varying hydraulic diameters ranging from 10 to 1000 μm. Typically, microreactors possess exceptionally high surface-to-volume ratios that far exceed those of traditional laboratory vessels, being approximately 100 to 500 times greater24. Magnetic biocatalysts or iron nanoparticles in microchannels, along with a magnetic field, offer a new approach to microreactor technology. For example, in immiscible reactions, iron nanoparticles in the oil phase can be moved via a magnetic field to improve mixing14. These particles can also be held in place by a magnetic field, without needing an external magnet, and released by removing the field25. However, to date, no studies have been performed that employ magnetic enzymes or magnetic whole cells for the synthesis of epoxy compounds in microchannels with different oscillation frequencies and amplitudes. The only microreactors utilized for enzymatic epoxidation have featured free or non-magnetic immobilized enzymes. For example, Casali et al. conducted enzymatic epoxidation of sunflower oil soap stock in a microchannel reactor using a lipase system from Thermomyces lanuginosus (Eversa Transform 2.0), achieving a yield of 83%26. In a separate investigation, Novozym 435 immobilized on silica was applied for the enzymatic epoxidation of soybean and palm oils within a fibrous microchannel, yielding 72.59%27. In another investigation, Mashhadi et al. employed a microchannel reactor to facilitate the enzymatic epoxidation of soybean oil soap stock utilizing a commercial lipase from Candida rugosa, achieving an efficiency of 85%6.

This study aims to present a MWCB for the production of epoxy compounds using fatty acids derived from industrial waste, employing various microchannel configurations under a frequency-controlled magnetic field. Based on our investigations, the enzymatic epoxidation reaction utilizing MWBC systems in a frequency-controlled electric-induced magnetic field has yet to be documented in microchannel reactors. Thus, the impact of varying frequencies, flow rates, and MWCB loading as operational parameters was examined by optimizing the experimental design in conjunction with different geometric arrangements of the microchannels. The stability of the microchannel reactor under the optimal configuration and operating conditions was assessed through a series of repeated experiments.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Soap stock (a by-product of soybean oil refining process) gained from Nazgol oil Factory (Kermanshah province, Iran). All nutrients utilized for microbial cell cultivation, as well as solvents, were of analytical grade and procured from Merck. Magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles (20–25 nm) were sourced from Armine Engineering of Sustainable and Initiative Company (Tehran, Iran), exhibiting an average particle size ranging from. The solvents employed were of analytical grade and obtained from Merck. The polyethylene imine solution, which was utilized for immobilization, was acquired from Sigma-Aldrich.

Microorganisms’ cultivation and magnetic immobilization

A pure culture of C. catenulata, identified by accession number KP324968, was obtained from the Faculty of Agricultural Engineering at Razi University. A culture of C. catenulata was maintained for 24 h at 30 °C on potato dextrose agar. Subsequently, a liquid culture medium was formulated with the following components: the liquid culture contained the following elements (gL−1) as referenced28: soybean oil, 4; yeast extract, 4; MgSO4.7H2O, 4; KH2PO4, 3; and K2HPO4, 3. The initial pH was adjusted to 5.6. The sterilization of this culture medium was performed at 121 °C through autoclaving for 20 min29. The cultivation of the cells conducted in an incubator-shaker at 150 rpm and 30 °C for a duration of 60 h29. Cells were subsequently harvested from the culture medium via centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min, washed with a phosphate buffer solution (pH = 7.0), and dried via freeze-drying. In order to magnetize C. catenulata cells, Fe3O4 nanoparticles were initially functionalized with polyethyleneimine (PEI) following a previously established method 30. For this process, 0.4 mg of PEI was dissolved in a phosphate buffer (pH = 5.8, 0.5 M), and 3 mg of Fe3O4 nanoparticles were incorporated and sonicated for 20 min. To confirm the functionalization of Fe3O4, the resultant mixture was stirred for 24 h, after which 1 g of dried cells was added to the mixture and stirred at 25 °C for 3 h. The MWCB was then isolated using a magnetic field, washed with phosphate buffer, and dried through freeze-drying. The characteristics of the obtained MWCBs are thoroughly reported in our previous publication29.

Isolation of FFAs from soap stock

To treat and subsequently degumming the soap stock (SS) to generate a precursor material that exhibits minimal steric hindrance for the epoxidation process, the following procedures were executed31: Initially, 100 g of SS was combined with 200 g of distilled water and stirred for a duration of 10 min at a temperature of 60 °C. Subsequently, 16 ml of sulfuric acid was introduced, and stirring continued for an additional 30 min at 60 °C. The temperature of the mixture was then lowered to 50 °C before transferring into the sedimentation tank. After a period of 5 h, the reaction mixture separated into two distinct phases, with the upper phase, identified as the degummed soap stock (DSS), being washed with hot water to ensure the complete removal of any residual sulfuric acid. Following this, the remaining mixture was combined with an equal volume of n-hexane and stirred for 5 min at 200 rpm, after which it was centrifuged for 10 min at 6000 × g. The final separation of DSS from n-hexane was achieved using a rotary evaporator under vacuum conditions (at a temperature of 60 °C and a vacuum pressure of 300 mbar). This process not only facilitated the recovery of n-hexane but also ensured the complete separation of DSS.

Microchannel enzymatic epoxidation and optimization

The enzymatic epoxidation of DSS was conducted using two varieties of microchannel reactors (spiral and serpentine) in a continuous mode. To this end, 3 g of feedstock containing unsaturated fatty acids and 4 ml of hydrogen peroxide (molar ratio of H2O2 to C=C of 4) were introduced into the microchannels. The microchannels measured 60 cm in length and had an inner diameter of 800 nm. To leverage the magnetic characteristics of MWCB, a frequency-controlled induced magnetic field was utilized within the microchannels. To compare the microchannel reactor’s performance, the enzymatic epoxidation was replicated in a stirred tank reactor under identical conditions. The reaction was carried out in a stirred system with 50 ml volume at 250 rpm for 3 h, with hydrogen peroxide added at 0.04 ml min−132. All reactions were maintained at 43°C.

Among the various factors influencing the operational conditions of the microchannel, the reactant flow rate (X1), the amount of MWCBs loading (X2), and the frequency of the induced magnetic field (X3) significantly impacted the conversion yield of C=C to oxirane (Y1), and the specific epoxidation activity (Y2). Experimental setup

Fabrication of spiral and serpentine microchannels

The microchannel configurations, including spiral and serpentine designs, were created using Adobe Illustrator. Subsequently, the design was etched onto a 60 cm long sheet of acrylic plexiglass utilizing a nanosecond fiber laser that featured a 400 μm focused laser beam (with a pulse duration of 250 ns and a wavelength of 1062 nm). Following the etching process, the sheets were bonded together with chloroform and fastened using screws. Generating a frequency-controlled-induced magnetic field (FC-IMF)

To utilize the magnetic characteristics of MWCBs within spiral and serpentine microchannel reactors and to examine the influence of MWCBs oscillation intensity on the enhanced mixing of the reactant mixture, a system comprising an electric induction magnetic field with a controlled frequency was established. For this objective, a variable voltage power supply (0–100 V) operating at a constant frequency was employed. Subsequently, an oscillating circuit with variable frequencies was used for controlling the particle pulses. The system incorporates a MOSFET transformer, relay, contactor, H-bridge, and frequency meter. The microreactors were analyzed under varying magnetic intensities ranging from 20 to 60 mT and across different frequencies from 0.5 to 4.5 Hz. Additionally, a magnetic field with a fixed frequency (without changing the orientation of the poles over time) was used to examine consistent magnetic polarity.

Microreactor system

The diagram of the FC-IMF microchannel system utilized for enzymatic epoxidation is illustrated in Fig. 2. This setup incorporated two syringe pumps: First pump transferred the mixture of t-butanol and DSS, and second pump controlled the flow of hydrogen peroxide. The T-mixer has an inlet length of 10 mm and a mixing length of 30 mm, which facilitates the combination of the two flows into the microchannels via this T-mixer. The enzymatic epoxidation of treated stock soap was conducted under FC-IMF conditions within both spiral and serpentine microchannels, each measuring 60 cm in length, with an injection volume of 20 ml for the reaction mixture. The temperature of the microchannel reactor was set on 43 °C through the circulation of cooling water in a chamber around the microchannel. The resulting EDSS was gathered in a tank.

Analytical methods

Microreactor parameter measurement (field intensity and frequency)

The intensity of the magnetic field was assessed utilizing a Gauss Tesla magnetometer testing apparatus (Sypris F W Bell Model 5170, USA) that featured an ultrathin transverse probe (STB1X-0201). Measurements were conducted at the inlet, center, and outlet of the microchannel, with the average value expressed in mT. To guarantee consistent probe positioning during the measurements, the microchannel was secured in a custom-designed fixture. Before each measurement session, the magnetometer underwent calibration in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines to ensure both accuracy and reliability. The values reported represent the average of three independent measurements taken at each location. Additionally, the standard deviation of these measurements was calculated to evaluate the variability in magnetic field strength. The frequency (0.5–4.5 Hz) was measured using a digital frequency meter (ERH-India) positioned between the magnetic coil and the frequency change circuit.

Characterization of FFAs and the produced EFAs

The fatty acid composition was analyzed using gas chromatography with flame ionization detection (GC-FID, TRACE GC Thermo Finnigan gas chromatograph, manufactured in Italy) according to the established standard method31. This instrument was equipped with a BPX70 column and used high-purity helium as the carrier gas. The helium flow rate was consistently maintained at 1.5 mL min−1, while the injector and detector temperatures were set at 220 °C and 250 °C, respectively. For the gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis, negative ionization mode was employed using an Agilent Technologies 5973N system to detect the essential fatty acids produced. This study was conducted using helium at a flow rate of 1.0 mL min−1, employing an Agilent HP-1 capillary column with dimensions of 30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.5 μm. The oven temperature program was as follows: it started at 60 °C for 2 min, then increased at a rate of 10 °C per min until reaching a final temperature of 280 °C, which was held for 30 min. Peak identification was performed based on mass spectra obtained from the NIST17 and Wiley Mass Spectral Libraries, provided by the National Institute of Standards and Technology, USA. In addition to analyzing the raw materials composed of treated soap stocks, a thorough investigation was conducted on the epoxy compounds produced during the experiment. This investigation utilized Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy (Shimadzu IRPrestige 21 FT-IR spectrophotometer), a method that yields significant insights into the molecular composition and structure of the materials.

Cell viability assessment

The capacity to differentiate between living and dead cells is essential for assessing the stability of whole-cell biocatalysts in biological processes. To ascertain the proportion of dead cells following an enzymatic epoxidation reaction, the initial step involves counting the cells in a small volume, which is then extrapolated to the total cell volume. The difference between these two counts serves as an indicator of the percentage of dead cells. The protocol employed for this assessment is as follows: Initially, 10 μl of the cell mixture was combined with 0.4% trypan blue at a 1:1 dilution in an Eppendorf tube, and subsequently, the mixture was transferred to a hemacytometer for cell counting under a microscope. The trypan blue dye selectively penetrates non-viable cells that exhibit compromised or ruptured cell walls. Notably, viable cells display a darker “halo,” whereas non-viable cells appear blue. Comparison of the microreactors in the epoxidation of FFAs

Figure 4 illustrates the impact of various types of magnetic microchannels, including spiral and serpentine designs, as well as different magnetic field types (fixed frequency and controlled frequency), on specific enzyme activity and epoxy production yield. As depicted in Fig. 4 for the serpentine and spiral microchannel, the efficiency of epoxidation and the specific activity within the constant frequency induced magnetic field (FF-IMF) were found to be lower than the corresponding values observed in the frequency controlled induced magnetic field (FC-IMF). This phenomenon can be explained by the optimal blending of immiscible phases at a controlled frequency, along with the increased contact area of MCWBs with the reaction mixture, which subsequently accelerates the reaction rate. In FF-IMF, the force exerted on the magnetic particle is directly proportional to the impulse force generated by the fluid flow37, which facilitates the optimal stability of MCWBs within the microchannel. However, the in-situ turbulence of MCWBs is attained by preserving stability in FC-IMF. In this case, the establishment of periodicity promotes the lateral migration of magnetized particles, and this lateral movement contributes to an increase in turbulence, thereby enabling mixing to occur in the most optimal state possible38.

Fig. 4The bio-epoxidation performance observed by (a) serpentine and (b) spiral configurations under the FC-IMF and FF-IMF conditions.

The bio-epoxidation performance observed by (a) serpentine and (b) spiral configurations under the FC-IMF and FF-IMF conditions.

As shown in Fig. 4, the EDSS yield is 36.45% in the serpentine microchannel (3a) and 47.91% in the spiral reactor (3b) under FC-IMF. This enhancement in efficiency can be attributed to the curved length and its influence on the mixing rate. In configurations of curved microchannels, an increase in curved length results in a greater formation of Dean vortices, thereby enhancing the homogeneity of passive mixing39. The curved length in the serpentine microchannel measures 28.68%, in contrast to the spiral reactor, which has a value of 88.10%. Consequently, the production yield of EDSS in the spiral reactor surpasses that of the serpentine microchannel.

Besides the effect of curvature length, sufficient residence time in the microchannels reduces the impact of inertial forces, allowing molecular diffusion to mix phases within the microreactor40 effectively. Moreover, the utilization of a magnetic field as an active mixing agent generates a slab disk of MWCBs that is oriented perpendicular to the direction of fluid flow. This arrangement enhances the variance in concentration and improves mixing efficiency. Consequently, the force acting on the magnetic particles becomes proportional to the momentum force applied by the fluid flow37, which also aids in stabilizing the particles. Thus, an increase in the curvature percentage amplifies the intensity of this concentration variance, ultimately leading to a rise in overall efficiency.

Interaction plots (Figs. 5 and 7) illustrate the interactions among variables. In Fig. 5a, the effect of reactant flow rate (X1) on conversion yield of C=C to oxirane (Y1) is contingent upon the amount of MWCBs loading (X2). At a low flow rate, an increase in MWCBs loading leads to a rise in Y1. This is because, initially, the increasing flow rate enhances fluid momentum, which facilitates phase micro-mixing. Figure 5b showed the dependency of the conversion yield of C=C to oxirane (Y1) as a function of reactant flow rate (X1) and frequency of the induced magnetic field (X3). It has been noted that the peak value of the response Y1 is achieved at an intermediate reactant flow rate and frequency of the MWCBs, with both parameters X1 and X3 influencing the production yield. This phenomenon occurs because the flow rate and frequency determine the placement pattern of the MWCBs within the magnetic field, which directly affects phase mixing and, consequently, the product yield. Conversely, excessively increasing the frequency leads to a reduction in the effect on Y1, as higher particle displacement at frequencies exceeding 2.5 Hz results in a decrease in the magnetic force acting on the magnetic dipoles. This causes the orientation of the field lines to become non-uniform41, subsequently disturbing the orientation of the MWCBs within the channel cross-section and reducing the contact area between the magnetic biocatalyst and the reactants.

The effects of the amount of MWCB loading and the frequency of the induced magnetic field are shown in Fig. 5c. As illustrated in the figure, at the maximum biocatalyst loading and a frequency of 2.5 Hz, the value of Y1 reaches its peak. Generally, the movement of particles within a magnetic field can be categorized into two types: lateral and rotational motions. Lateral motion arises from the influence of an external magnetic field and the excitation of particles possessing magnetic characteristics. In contrast, rotational motion is induced by the secondary magnetic field generated by the magnetized particles in relation to one another.

Figure 6 illustrates the variation in the configuration and alignment of MWCBs across different frequencies. Generally, magnetic particles are sensitive to magnetic fields and can interact with an external magnetic field at any frequency42. Following the process of magnetization and the establishment of dipoles, these particles align themselves in relation to the field43, and the creation of a particle chain is facilitated by their dipole interactions44. As shown in Fig. 6a, at lower frequencies, the reduced frequency and diminished MWCBs excitation result in a broader chain of magnetic particle connections, and the emergence of distinct disks within the current cross-section is not evident. Conversely, at higher frequencies, as shown in Fig. 6c, the magnetic particles tend to move away from the wall, preventing the formation of complete disks and causing accumulation on one side of the wall. The optimal frequency for achieving the formation of complete discrete disks throughout the entire microchannel is identified as 2.5 Hz.

Operational stability of the spiral microreactor

Figure 8a illustrates the time-dependent values of epoxidation yield. The FC-IMF method demonstrates enhanced stability compared to FF-IMF, as FC-IMF inhibits the clustering of MWCB particles. After 12 h, the yield decreased by 19.33% for FC-IMF and 41.73% for FF-IMF. According to first-order deactivation kinetics, the half-deactivation times of the biocatalyst in FF-IMF and FC-IMF were 14.82 and 29.91 h, respectively. Given that the microchannel reactor utilizing FC-IMF demonstrated superior efficiency compared to the FF-IMF system, this system was employed to assess operational stability over time for the production of EDSS. Additionally, the yield and specific epoxidation activity of an innovative six-stage cascade microreactor system are shown in Fig. 8b. The figure shows that the EDSS efficiency increased from 47.91% to 90.17% after passing through six cascaded microchannels. These results suggest that introducing fresh hydrogen peroxide at six distinct intervals, each lasting 1.53 min along the six spiral microchannels, which collectively measure 360 cm in length, significantly enhances the EDSS efficiency within the microchannel system. Conversely, the performance of the microchannel system was compared to that of a stirred tank system operating in batch mode, revealing that the EDSS efficiency after 3 h in the stirred system was 44.63%. This underscores that implementing a cascaded microchannel reactor markedly improves the epoxidation rate, attributed to the micromixing of immiscible reactants facilitated by the movement of the biocatalyst under a magnetic field, in contrast to the bulk mixing achieved through mechanical stirring in the stirred tank system. Consequently, relatively high EDSS yields can be attained in the microchannel reactor within short hydrodynamic residence times. The total hydrodynamic residence time required to reach a yield of 90.17% was approximately 9.18 ± 0.2 min. This phenomenon can be attributed to the minimized magnetic excitation of MWCBs, facilitated by the application of a frequency-controlled magnetic field43. Furthermore, unlike macroscale processes, magnetic microchannels enable the micromixing of immiscible phases through the application of a magnetic field, which enhances mixing and increases the contact surface area. In addition, the proportion of viable cells in the enzymatic oxidation reaction was assessed following their passage through six cascade microchannels. The viability percentage recorded after traversing these six microchannels was 93.86%, indicating a cell death rate of under 10%, a figure that is typically deemed acceptable for biological experiments45. These findings suggest that the cell death rate within magnetic spiral microchannels continues to be minimal. This phenomenon can be attributed to the minimized magnetic excitation of MWCBs, facilitated by the implementation of a frequency-controlled magnetic field43. Furthermore, in contrast to macroscale processes, magnetic microchannels enable micromixing of immiscible phases, and the application of a magnetic field for mixing and enhancing the contact surface22 significantly mitigates cell death, allowing MWCBs to interact with the reaction mixture in a very brief contact duration. On the other hand, kim et al. examined the impact of shear stress on cell viability within spiral-shaped microchannels and discovered that cell viability was effectively preserved in cascade spiral microchannels46. This occurrence can be attributed to the fact that cells within microchannels are subjected to reduced shear stress levels and shorter durations of exposure, leading to cell viability rates surpassing 90%45.

Conclusion

The findings of the current study indicate that advancing the MWCB system, achieved by omitting the enzyme purification steps, utilizing it within a microchannel exposed to a magnetic field, and enhancing the mixing of immiscible phases during the epoxidation reaction combined with the use of waste from vegetable oil factories, offers a novel approach for the environmentally friendly synthesis of epoxy compounds. Among the microchannels utilizing FC-IMF and FF-IMF, the FC-IMF system demonstrated superior effectiveness due to enhanced stimulation of MWCB mixing resulting from frequency variations caused by changes in the direction of the field polarity. This led to an increase in the production efficiency of epoxy compounds by 20.74% compared to FF-IMF. The optimization results concerning the operational conditions further revealed that the effects of the reactant flow rate and the frequency of the induced magnetic field on the responses were more significant than the influence of MWCB loading. These two critical parameters exerted the most substantial impact on the responses at their median values. Additionally, the study thoroughly examined the influence of frequency variations on the orientation of MWCB within the microchannel cross-sections, identifying 2.5 Hz as the optimal frequency. Furthermore, the results demonstrated that Candida catenulata magnetic cells effectively converted double bonds into oxirane rings, showing greater efficiency with fatty acids containing a higher number of double bonds, such as linolenic acid. The operational stability of MWCB was evaluated over time, revealing that MWCBs largely retained their activity after 12 h of use, with only a 19.33% reduction in their initial yield.
