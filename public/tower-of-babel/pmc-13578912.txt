Real‐World Management of Chronic Myelogenous Leukemia in an Underserved Patient Cohort at a Los Angeles Safety‐Net Health System: Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitor Treatment Patterns and Outcomes From 2015 to 2024
Authors: Dominick Zheng, Timothy Nold, Neeraj Ramakrishnan, Kailyn Kim, Sarah Tomassetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWhile overall survival for chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) at 5 years now exceeds 90% with modern tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapies, disparities in management and outcomes persist among underserved populations. This study describes TKI treatment patterns and outcomes in a predominantly Hispanic, underserved CML cohort treated at Los Angeles County safety‐net hospitals.MethodsWe performed a retrospective chart review of 108 adult patients with CML who presented to Los Angeles County hospitals between 2015 and 2024. Data collected included demographics, comorbidities, treatment selection per line of therapy (LOT), and major molecular response (MMR; > 3‐log reduction) rates.ResultsThe majority (58.3%) of patients were Hispanic, and most (59.2%) lived in zip codes with household incomes below the national average. The median age at diagnosis was 46 years old, and 82.4% of patients had comorbid conditions at diagnosis, including elevated BMI (67.5%), hypertension (28.7%), and diabetes (20.4%). By EUTOS score, 55.6% of patients were low‐risk, and 11.1% were high‐risk. Imatinib was the most common first‐line treatment for 64.8% of patients, followed by 31.5% with dasatinib. MMR was achieved in 44.4% of patients, while 43.5% of patients discontinued first‐line LOT due to medication intolerance. Overall, 56.5% of patients progressed to second LOT and 27% required third LOT.ConclusionsDespite a younger median age at diagnosis, our patients had less favorable outcomes compared to previously published cohorts including lower rates of MMR with first‐line therapy, higher rates of toxicity, and needing more lines of therapy overall. These findings highlight the need for further investigation into the socioeconomic, biologic, and treatment‐access factors impacting outcomes in underserved patients with CML.

Introduction

Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) is a myeloproliferative neoplasm representing approximately 15% to 20% of adult leukemias. The hallmark of CML is the Philadelphia chromosome, a balanced reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22 designated t(9;22)(q34;q11), which generates the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene and results in constitutively active tyrosine kinase signaling. Prior to the advent of the first tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), imatinib, in the early 2000s, the prognosis of CML was poor. Since then, the development of multiple TKIs has transformed the disease's management, with patients now typically having a life expectancy comparable to that of age‐matched individuals without leukemia. With the availability of newer generations of TKIs, the goals of therapy have evolved beyond disease control toward treatment‐free remission (TFR), in which patients with sustained deep molecular response are able to discontinue TKI therapy without experiencing relapse. Discontinuation of TKI therapy ultimately has significant benefits due to minimizing adverse effects, including long‐term cardiovascular toxicity and improving patient quality of life [1, 2]. Understanding TKI treatment patterns and outcomes at each line of therapy (LOT) is an important step toward individualizing treatment selection according to patient‐specific factors in pursuit of this milestone [3, 4].

While overall survival for CML at 5 years now exceeds 90% with targeted therapy [5], disparities in management and outcomes persist among minority and underserved populations due to financial/insurance barriers, social factors, and medical comorbidities [5, 6, 7]. As minority populations have historically been underrepresented in hematologic clinical trials [8, 9], further investigation is needed to assess the applicability of these results in a diverse population. For the purposes of this study, “underserved” is defined as a patient with at least one of the following criteria: racial/ethnic minority status; low socioeconomic status; and reliance on safety‐net health systems serving a disproportionately high share of Medicaid, uninsured, and low‐income patients for medical follow‐up. This study aims to describe demographics, TKI treatment patterns, molecular response rates, and reasons for treatment discontinuation across multiple lines of therapy in our predominantly underserved cohort, as a step toward identifying the contributors to outcome disparities in this population.

Methods

Patient Data

We conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study of all patients aged greater than 18 years who presented to Harbor‐UCLA Medical Center, Olive View‐UCLA Medical Center, and Los Angeles General Medical Center between January 2015 through December 2024. All patients had a confirmed diagnosis of CML based on detection of the BCR‐ABL1 fusion gene using reverse‐transcription real‐time polymerase chain reaction standardized to the International Scale (IS). Data collected from the electronic health record (EHR) included patient demographics, clinical parameters at diagnosis, laboratory values, and comorbid conditions. Treatment data was collected from first to fourth LOT, including treatment response, complications, discontinuation rates, and reasons for discontinuation. Data entry was completed via a standardized REDCap database.

Prognostic risk stratification at diagnosis was performed using the Sokal score and the European Treatment and Outcome Society (EUTOS) score, both of which are validated tools for predicting outcomes in CML. The Sokal score incorporates age, spleen size, platelet count, and blast percentage to stratify patients into low‐, intermediate‐, and high‐risk categories [10]. The EUTOS score uses spleen size and basophil percentage to classify patients as low‐ or high‐risk and has demonstrated superior predictive value for achievement of complete cytogenetic response in the TKI era [11].

Socioeconomic data was extracted either from structured demographic fields in the EHR or free text within notes. Low socioeconomic status was operationalized as residence in a zip code with a median household income below the national median reported by the US Census Bureau American Community Survey. Comorbidity burden was characterized descriptively using data extracted from the EHR.

This study was reviewed and granted exempt status by the Lundquist Institute/Harbor UCLA Institutional Review Board (IRB #2025–33,507‐01), and the study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards described in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and subsequent amendments.

Outcomes of Interest

The primary outcome of this study was achievement of major molecular response (MMR3), defined as a ≥ 3‐log reduction in BCR‐ABL1 transcript levels or a BCR::ABL1 IS ≤ 0.1%, at any timepoint during each line of therapy. MMR was utilized as an endpoint because it was reliably and comprehensively available in our dataset and is closely associated with both cytogenic response and prognostic outcomes [12]. Secondary outcomes included: time to MMR from initiation of each line of therapy; rates of TKI discontinuation per line of therapy; reasons for discontinuation, categorized as inadequate treatment response, drug intolerance, or both; rates of disease progression to accelerated or blast phase; and rates of loss to follow‐up.

Our definition of inadequate treatment response adhered to NCCN's early treatment response milestones for the first 12 months of therapy, with BCR::ABL1 IS > 1% at 12 months suggesting TKI resistance and consideration for discontinuation. Beyond 12 months, we utilized the relapse criteria in the NCCN guidelines, which includes a 1‐log increase in BCR::ABL1 IS with a loss of MMR. Mutational testing dates and results were recorded, with a particular focus on T315I mutations conferring TKI resistance.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 108 patients with a diagnosis of CML met the inclusion criteria between 2015 and 2024 (Table 1). Our cohort consisted of 43 (40%) female and 65 (60%) male patients, with a median age at diagnosis of 46 years old (IQR = 38–59). Sixty‐three (58.3%) patients were Hispanic, 15 (13.9%) were African American, 5 (4.5%) were Asian/Pacific Islander, 4 (3.7%) were White/Caucasian, and 21 (19.4%) were labeled as other or unknown. Sixty‐four (59.2%) patients had addresses in zip codes with average household incomes below the national average. Comorbid conditions were found in 82.4% of our patients at diagnosis, including a majority (67.6%) with body mass index (BMI) > 25, 28.7% with hypertension, and 20.4% with diabetes (Table 2).

Clinical characteristics at diagnosis.

Diagnosis of CML was made during chronic and accelerated phases in 91.7% and 5.6% of cases, respectively. Sokal scores at diagnosis were found to be 21.3% low‐risk, 24.1% intermediate‐risk, 25.9% high‐risk, and the remaining 28.7% unknown. EUTOS scores at diagnosis were 55.6% low‐risk, 11.1% high‐risk, and 33.3% unknown. For lab parameters at diagnosis, the mean BCR‐ABL percentage was 87.1% (SD = 45.9), mean white blood cell (WBC) count was 145.6 × 109/L (SD = 124.4 × 109), and mean platelet count was 503 × 109/L (SD = 427.4 × 109) (Table 2). A total of 4 (3.7%) patients tested positive for T315I mutations. Hence, our population reflects a majority of patients in chronic phase at time of diagnosis, with a heterogenous risk profile based on Sokal and EUTOS scores.

Treatment Characteristics and Molecular Response

Imatinib was selected as first‐line treatment for 64.8% patients, followed by 31.5% dasatinib, 0.9% nilotinib, and 0.9% interferon for one pregnant patient (Table 3). Only 48 (44.4%) patients achieved MMR3 or better at any timepoint during first LOT, with a mean time to MMR3 of 26.5 months (SD = 21.2). Side effects were reported in 37 (34.3%) patients who received first LOT, and issues with adherence to first LOT were reported in 15 (13.9%) patients, and 4 (3.7%) patients with both. First LOT was discontinued in 62 (57.4%) patients, with the mean time to discontinuation at 14 months (SD = 17). Of those who experienced first LOT discontinuation, 45.1% was due to inadequate response only, 27.4% was due to intolerance only, and 16.1% was due to both. Adverse effects leading to discontinuation were inconsistently captured in the EHR. However, reasons for imatinib discontinuation included musculoskeletal pain, gastrointestinal toxicity, cardiotoxicity, significant fatigue, and renal injury. Reasons for dasatinib discontinuation included cytopenias, effusions, and rash, whereas nilotinib was discontinued for cardiotoxicity. Thus, imatinib was used in a majority of patients as first line treatment, but there were high rates of discontinuation primarily due to inadequate response and to a lesser degree intolerance.

Sixty‐one (56.5%) patients progressed to second LOT, with most patients receiving dasatinib (60.7%), followed by 11.3% imatinib, 9.7% asciminib, 8.1% nilotinib, 4.8% ponatinib, 3.2% interferon, and 1 (1.6%) patient on bosutinib (Table 3). Twenty (32.8%) patients were able to achieve MMR3 or better, with mean time to MMR3 of 13 months (SD = 14). Side effects were documented in 21 (34.4%) patients on second LOT, and 4 (6.6%) patients were non‐adherent. Second LOT was discontinued in 34 (55.8%) patients, with 38.2% attributed to inadequate response, 41.1% to intolerance, and 8.8% to both reasons. Reasons for discontinuation of dasatinib in second LOT included edema and/or effusions, significant fatigue, cytopenia, and onset of pulmonary hypertension, whereas imatinib was discontinued for renal injury. Dasatinib was the predominant second‐line therapy and was associated with a notable burden of adverse effects. Discontinuation rates were high, with intolerance representing a more frequent driver of discontinuation compared to first LOT.

Thirty patients (27% of the original cohort) progressed to third LOT (Table 3). TKI selection was more evenly distributed at this LOT, with 26.7% receiving nilotinib, 20% on dasatinib, 16.7% on imatinib, 16.7% on ponatinib, 13.3% on bosutinib, and 6.7% on asciminib. Eight patients (26.7%) on third LOT achieved MMR3 or better, with a mean time to MMR3 of 11 months (SD = 16). Eleven patients (36.7%) reportedly experienced side effects, 2 (6.7%) experienced non‐adherence, and 6 (20%) experienced both. Of the 16 (53.3%) patients in the third LOT who discontinued treatment, 37.5% had an inadequate response, 25% could not tolerate the TKI, and 31.3% discontinued due to both reasons.

Finally, 15 patients (13.8% of the original cohort) were placed on fourth LOT (Table 3). The majority (40%) of patients received bosutinib. Imatinib, dasatinib, nilotinib, and asciminib were given to 2 (13.3%) patients each, and 1 (6.7%) patient received ponatinib. Seven (46.7%) patients achieved MMR3 or better, with mean time to MMR3 of 18 months (SD = 14). Two patients discontinued fourth LOT, one at 2.8 months and the other at 13.8 months. One patient was discontinued due to intolerance, whereas the reason for discontinuation was undocumented for the second patient. A total of 3 patients were placed on a clinical trial for treatment with asciminib.

A total of 6 (5.6%) patients progressed to blast phase. Three (2.8%) patients progressed to blast phase during first LOT with a mean time to progression of 20 months (SD 24.3). Two (1.9%) patients progressed to blast phase during second LOT with times to progression of 6 and 49 months. One patient progressed to blast phase during third LOT, with time to progression of 31 months. Ultimately, 17 (15.7%) patients were lost to follow‐up.

Discussion

Cohort Characteristics

To the authors' knowledge, this is one of few studies in the literature describing CML treatment patterns and outcomes in a significantly underserved population. To characterize the degree of social disadvantage, we examined two indicators of healthcare vulnerability. Regarding socioeconomic status, individual‐level financial measures were unavailable, but we found that the majority (59.2%) of patients lived in zip codes with household incomes below the national average. Regarding racial/ethnic minority status, a total of 72.2% of patients were Hispanic or African American, groups historically underrepresented in hematologic malignancy trials. In contrast, a study of 2857 patients based on Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) data from the 2000 to 2019 TKI therapy era was found to be comprised of 79% Caucasian patients, with an unreported proportion of Hispanic patients [5].

The median age at diagnosis in our cohort was 46 years old with a range of 17–74 years old, notably lower than the median age of 65 years old reported in SEER CML data [13]. This more closely mirrors the median age at diagnosis reported in studies of low‐ and middle‐income countries, including the Glivec International Patient Assistance Program (GIPAP) cohort study that demonstrates a mean age of diagnosis of 41 in patients from Latin American countries [14]. A retrospective study of CML patients at Los Angeles General Medical Center, a center included in our study, reported an even lower median age at diagnosis of 39 years old [15]. One SEER‐based epidemiologic study additionally found that the age of diagnosis was lower for Hispanic patients compared to non‐Hispanic white patients across 34 cancer types; although this difference was reduced when adjusted for the age structure of each source population, there remained a significantly younger average age at diagnosis in Hispanic patients with CML [16]. These findings are inconsistent with current data from several national cancer registries that have demonstrated that patients with lower income tend to present at more advanced stages of disease [17, 18]. There is currently a dearth of studies that investigate why minority populations are often diagnosed at younger ages with CML. A likely contributor to this finding lies in the insurance status of our patient population. Whereas patients with Medicare have access to a broader range of care settings, safety‐net systems tend to serve a greater proportion of Medicaid and uninsured populations [19], thereby shifting the observed age at diagnosis toward younger patients. Still, more investigation into the biological, genetic, environmental, and socioeconomic mechanisms underpinning these findings is warranted.

Treatment Response

Compared to similar descriptive studies, our cohorts had lower rates of MMR with first‐line therapy and received more lines of therapy overall. This is true when compared to both national and international study populations. For instance, the prospective observational SIMPLICITY study comprising 997 CML patients in the US found that 77.4% of patients achieved MMR by 2 years of treatment [20]. Similarly, a Colombian study of 944 CML patients spanning 2 decades demonstrated that 72.2% of patients achieved MMR with first‐line treatment [21]. In contrast, only 44.4% of our cohort experienced MMR with first‐line TKIs, with an average time to MMR3 of 26.5 months. This discrepancy exists despite first LOT treatment selection in our cohort largely mirroring typical real‐world treatment patterns [2], with 64.8% of patients receiving imatinib and 31.5% receiving dasatinib as first LOT.

Within our cohort, 57.4% of patients experienced frontline therapy discontinuation, chiefly due to inadequate treatment response only (45.2%), treatment intolerance only (27.4%), or both (16.1%). The total proportion of patients with an inadequate response after first LOT is 35.2% (n = 38), which is consistent with, if not higher than observed in other studies. A large multicenter study in 2024 estimated that approximately 20%–30% of patients experience first‐line treatment failure, despite initial imatinib or second‐generation TKI therapy [22].

Of note, our cohort seemed to experience a significant degree of side effects. TKI tolerance varies by agent, with imatinib traditionally having the most favorable side effect profile with the lowest cumulative incidence of adverse events among all TKIs [23]. This trend is congruent with our patient population, with 64.8% of the entire cohort receiving imatinib as a first‐line therapy, and 43.5% of the first‐line therapy group discontinuing treatment due to medication intolerance compared to 50% of the second‐line therapy group. Our cohort seemed to experience a higher rate of first‐line imatinib intolerance compared to those studied in the landmark IRIS trial, in which only 6.9% of patients discontinued imatinib due to adverse events [24]. Real‐world data often corroborate IRIS findings, including a 2021 CML registry noting only 10% of patients transitioning off first‐line imatinib due to intolerance [25].

Inadequate treatment response and tolerance are multifactorial and reflect a combination of disease biology, medication adherence, toxicity, and access to care. Financial toxicity is a well‐recognized barrier to medication adherence, particularly for our safety‐net population [26]. Although TKIs are generally well‐covered by both Medicare and MediCal, the predominant insurance providers in our cohort, non‐drug costs can still be prohibitive. The burden of frequent clinic visits can lead to missed work and lost wages that steer patients toward treatment interruption. Other frequently documented social barriers in our chart review included unreliable transportation access, social isolation, and language barriers. Collectively, these factors may increase patients' allostatic load, reflecting the cumulative physiologic effects of chronic social and economic stress [27]. While allostatic load was not directly measured in this study, it provides a useful framework through which the disproportionate social burden experienced by our cohort may contribute to reduced adherence, higher toxicities, and ultimately higher frequency of medication changes and discontinuation observed in our cohort.

Comorbidities

The association between unfavorable toxicity tolerance and even overall survival with advanced age and higher comorbidity burden is well‐documented [28, 29, 30]. Patients' comorbidity burden and age are explicitly listed as key considerations by the NCCN treatment algorithm when choosing a first‐line CML therapy. Patients with underlying diabetes or cardiovascular disease are generally started on imatinib or dasatinib rather than an alternative second‐generation TKI. As identified, a significant proportion of our patients possessed comorbidity profiles that prompted initiation with imatinib or dasatinib. 77.8% of our cohort has one or more cardiovascular risk factors (BMI > 25, hypertension, and/or diabetes). Conversely, a prospective real‐world study of treatment‐naïve CML patients from northern California observed that only 52.5% of patients with newly diagnosed CML had baseline cardiovascular risk factors (including hypertension, diabetes, arrhythmia, hyperlipidemia) [31].

Though the median age at diagnosis of our cohort is lower than typically described in the literature, the prevalence of comorbidities is significant. At diagnosis, 67.6% of patients were obese (BMI > 25), 20.4% had diabetes, 8.3% had CKD/ESRD, in addition to a significant proportion with cardiovascular risk factors (28.7% with hypertension, 5.6% with history of coronary artery disease (CAD), and 2.8% with history of cerebrovascular accident (CVA)). In contrast, the comorbidity‐focused CML Study IV identified only 7% with diabetes, 3.1% with moderate to severe renal insufficiency, but found a comparable rate of hypertension (27.7%) [30]. Our findings suggest that our population's high rate of first‐line therapy discontinuation may be linked to increased toxicity exacerbated by comorbidity burden, despite having the typically protective factor of a lower age at diagnosis.

The higher levels of treatment intolerance in our cohort extend to later‐line treatment outcomes as well. Second‐generation TKI therapies (namely dasatinib, nilotinib, and bosutinib) are cited as having improved molecular responses compared to imatinib, at the expense of more distinct side effect profiles with higher intolerance rates [32]. Together, dasatinib and nilotinib accounted for 68.8% of the second‐line therapies in our cohort. Within the second‐line therapy group, 21 (34.4%) patients experienced side effects. Fourteen (23%) patients progressed to third LOT due to drug intolerance alone, and 3 (4.9%) patients progressed to third LOT due to a combination of both drug intolerance and inadequate response. This is on the higher end of the previously documented 10%–22% intolerability rates from the respective FDA labels of each TKI [33].

Study Limitations

This retrospective analysis is limited by a small sample size and imbalanced grouping, which reduces statistical power and may result in imprecise effect estimates. Moreover, since these data were collected from three medical centers within the greater Los Angeles area, the generalizability of our findings is limited. While socioeconomic status was approximated using US Census median income data, zip‐code‐level measures do not necessarily reflect the individual socioeconomic circumstances of each patient. Future analyses may benefit from collection of individual‐level measures, including educational and employment status, to more precisely characterize this aspect of our cohort.

Given the retrospective nature of this review, there is a lack of standardization among providers documenting events like treatment failures per stringent NCCN guidelines. Standardized hematologic and cytogenetic response assessments were not included in our dataset, and TFR attempts were inconsistently reported in the EHR. Future studies incorporating longitudinal monitoring of these parameters would allow for more robust evaluation of treatment efficacy. The non‐adherence rates observed in our cohort are likely underestimated as well, as physicians frequently overestimate the extent of their patients' adherence to TKIs [34, 35]. TKI selection for our cohort may reflect local practice patterns, which would limit external validity. Additionally, changes in the regulatory landscape and drug availability over the study period may have influenced treatment selection, especially in later lines of therapy, which is acknowledged as a potential source of heterogeneity in our dataset. There was a high rate of patients lost to follow‐up in our cohort, which skews our data. This is unsurprising as safety‐net hospitals such as ours see many patients who are uninsured and often switch to other plans when they obtain insurance during their treatment course. Given the high number of immigrant patients in our population, it is also not uncommon to see them return to their country of origin.

Conclusion

There is a dearth of studies focusing on CML treatment patterns and outcomes in patient populations resembling ours, an underrepresentation that extends to clinical trials in this space [36]. Our multicenter retrospective study revealed less favorable treatment outcomes across multiple lines of therapy compared to those reported in other published national and international CML cohorts, even with comparable treatment selection patterns. Despite a younger median age at diagnosis, a feature typically associated with more favorable outcomes, our cohort experienced lower rates of MMR with first‐line therapy, higher rates of clinically significant treatment toxicity, and higher rates of treatment discontinuation with more lines of therapy overall. These findings suggest that safety‐net populations may face compounding disadvantages in which the burden of increased comorbidities and socioeconomic barriers to adherence contribute to poorer outcomes compared to higher‐resourced settings.

These results have important implications for clinical practice. Providers treating similar populations may benefit from anticipating a higher burden of toxicity during treatment selection and proactively screening for adherence barriers. Future research should continue to prioritize diverse but larger cohorts with more standardized adverse event grading and quantification of socioeconomic stressors by utilizing measures like allostatic load. As the treatment landscape for CML continues to evolve, evaluating outcomes with newer agents like asciminib in this population will be an important step toward achieving parity in treatment response across demographic groups. Our findings offer a data‐informed basis for heightened and holistic vigilance when caring for underserved CML patients, and it is crucial that this population continues to be represented in future cohort studies.
