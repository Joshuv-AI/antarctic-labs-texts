Genetic approaches for targeted oxidative stress
Authors: Aninda Dey, Ryan P Barnes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer cells display dysregulated metabolic programs, which result in excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) leading to oxidative stress. ROS reaction with macromolecules, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, can result in damaging modifications with alter or nullify function. While tumors upregulate antioxidant defences for viability, they remain sensitive to additional oxidant perturbations. Because of this, therapies that overwhelm cancers with ROS are gaining clinical attention due to their potential targeting of diseased tissue over normal tissue. In this review, we summarize the available genetic tools for targeted ROS production in both cellular and organismal models, specifically focusing on tools with spatial and temporal control. Largely, these approaches use light to activate a chromophore in the cell, which produces ROS for protein inactivation, DNA damage, or cell ablation. These photosensitizers are genetically fused to target proteins of interest, and all have advantages and disadvantages for both basic and translational research, which we discuss below.

Introduction

Oxidative stress is a ubiquitous situation humans experience [1]. It is caused by an imbalance of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and antioxidant defenses, resulting in excessive ROS. One of the major regulators of antioxidants in cells is the NRF2/KEAP1 pathway, which is activated by excess ROS to transcribe genes that have an antioxidant response element (ARE) in their promoters [2]. While linked to a variety of pathologies, including lung and cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and premature aging, oxidative stress is a key facet of cancer. Cancer cells are known to upregulate antioxidant programs, including the NRF2 pathway, because their dysregulated metabolism produces excess ROS [3,4]. However, despite upregulation of antioxidants, cancer cells are still highly sensitive to further ROS increases because their defenses are already so encumbered. In this review, we will briefly summarize conventional approaches for elevating ROS in models of cancer and give a detailed summary of the genetic tools available to researchers for more sophisticated ROS production for both basic research and translational applications.

Common Approaches for ROS Generation

Arguably, the most commonly used reagent for ROS elevation in cell culture is hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). This likely stems from its ease of use and undeniable efficacy in causing severe oxidation to cells [5]. While a weak oxidant itself, this allows H2O2 to travel much further within a cell than other ROS, until it reacts with molecules like iron or proteins like myeloperoxidase to become hydroxyl radicals or hypochlorous acid [6]. Hydroxyl radicals are incredibly reactive and generate countless DNA lesions, including strand breaks. While this means application of H2O2 is profoundly genotoxic, it is difficult to interpret how and why the oxidative stress caused cell death or compromised function.

Potassium bromate (KBrO3) is often used by DNA repair labs because it is somewhat specific for inducing 8-oxo-guanine (8oxoG) when interacting with DNA. This is believed to occur by the thiols in cells (or in vitro) reducing BrO3− to BrO2, which acts as a one-electron oxidant [7]. It is also amenable to animal studies, which are often conducted for safety because it is a common food additive [8]. Like H2O2, however, bromate is not location or target-specific for oxidation. Other reagents used to model oxidative stress in animals include mitochondrial poisons (i.e. paraquat), immune insults (i.e. lipopolysaccharide), and particulate inhalation (i.e. cigarette smoke).

Like spatial and target specificity, another factor that is often overlooked in oxidative stress studies in cell culture is oxygen tension or concentration. Within the body, oxygen tension ranges from 2–13% (20–130 µM), with an average around 3–5% (30–50 µM) [9]. While culture media will lower the exact oxygen concentration cells experience, it is still significantly higher than in vivo conditions due to the environmental oxygen tension (∼20%). This elevates ROS levels in cells and alters the expression of antioxidant defenses when cells are challenged with additional oxidative stress [10]. Additionally, as the ROS-generating tools we discuss are dependent on the availability of oxygen, researchers should be aware of oxygen gradients that exist within organs, tumors, and even within cells that can impact their tool’s efficacy [11, 12].

Like H2O2, the other reagents mentioned lack simultaneous spatial and temporal control. Almost 20 years ago, Redox Biology was revolutionized with the publication of the first genetic photosensitizer (protein that absorbs light, producing a reaction), KillerRed. By fusing proteins that require light (optogenetic), a substrate or ligand (chemogenetic), or both (chemoptogenetic) to targeting peptides or proteins, researchers gained both spatial and temporal control of ROS production. Below, we will summarize the current genetic toolkit for ROS production, detailing the advantages and disadvantages of each system. Finally, we will give an overview of how these tools can be applied both for fundamental research and translational interventions.

Controlled ROS Production

Optogenetic

The green fluorescent protein (GFP) from Aequorea Victoria (a bioluminescent hydrozoan jellyfish) is commonly used in biological research as a fluorescent tag [13]. By fusing GFP to target proteins, protein localization and molecular interactions can be monitored in real time within cells and organisms [14, 15]. GFP contains a chromophore, which is a light-absorbing component of a molecule that captures specific wavelengths, enters an excited state, and typically emits light of a longer wavelength. Unlike the proteins detailed below, the shell of GFP encases the chromophore in a way that effectively prevents it from being a photosensitizer [16–18]. Directed evolution and screens, however, have identified GFP derivatives and other molecules that can act as both fluorescent tags and photosensitizers.

While differences exist in these tools, photochemically they rely on one of two types of reactions (Fig. 1) [19]. In both cases, a ground-state photosensitizer absorbs light at a specific wavelength, which, after intersystem crossing, results in an excited triplet state. When the final result of this excitation is an electron transfer to oxygen, this is termed a “Type 1” reaction, as the result is a radical. If the excited photosensitizer instead reacts with ground-state molecular oxygen, which is triplet, and transfers energy, singlet oxygen (¹O₂) is produced, which is very reactive. This is a “Type 2” reaction [20]. Below, we will detail different optogenetic tools for ROS production and give a summary of their photochemical properties.

Diagram of photosensitizer reactions. When a ground state, singlet, photosensitizer absorbs a photon of a relevant wavelength, it enters a higher energy, excited singlet state. If intersystem crossing occurs, one electron will change spin, resulting in a triplet, excited photosensitizer. This can then react with ground-state, triplet oxygen in one of two ways. If an electron is transferred and a radical formed (often but not always superoxide), this is termed a Type 1 Reaction. If instead only energy is transferred, the outer π orbital electrons of oxygen will rearrange, leaving one orbital empty, making it highly reactive (but not a radical). This is a Type 2 Reaction.

KillerRed

KillerRed is derived from a GFP-related protein from the hydrozoan species Anthoathecata [21]. A total of twenty peripheral amino acid substitutions, with Thr145Asn and Cys161Ala being particularly significant, differentiate it from its non-phototoxic progenitor, anm2CP [21, 22]. KillerRed shares its core structure with GFP, featuring an 11-stranded β-barrel capped by loops on both ends. At the center, a chromophore (Gln65-Tyr66-Gly67) resides within an internal α-helix aligned along the barrel’s axis. The structure includes a pore on the β-barrel’s cylindrical surface, formed by the backbone of six amino acids. The pore accommodates a chain of three hydrogen-bonded water molecules, establishing a continuous pathway between the protein exterior and the chromophore’s hydroxyphenyl moiety. Each water molecule in the channel engages in hydrogen bonding with neighboring water molecules as well as surrounding amino acid residues, thereby directing the flow along the channel. Mutagenesis experiments suggest that the pore plays a crucial role in chromophore maturation (biochemical process by which a fluorescent protein’s chromophore forms and becomes fully functional) by facilitating molecular oxygen access [22, 23]. Unlike other red fluorescent and non-fluorescent Anthozoa-derived GFP-like proteins, KillerRed does not form a tetramer. Instead, it exists as a dimer, with two monomers arranged side by side at an angle of approximately 90° (Fig. 2) [24]. KillerRed’s chromophore fluoresces red, with excitation and emission peaks at 585 and 610 nm, respectively. Formed from the Gln65-Tyr66-Gly67 sequence, KillerRed features a two-ring fluorophore (typical of photosensitizers) which generates superoxide anion radicals (O₂•⁻) via type 1 reactions.

Controversy has existed over the type of ROS produced by KillerRed, and if it worked through Type 1 or Type 2 reactions [21, 22, 25]. Andrew Wojtovich and colleagues conducted a recent study to quantify the quantum yields of superoxide and singlet oxygen generated by KillerRed [26]. Rose Bengal, a known chemical photosensitizer, was employed as a positive control, while mCherry, a red fluorescent protein presumed to lack photosensitizing properties, served as a negative control. Superoxide production was evaluated via high-performance liquid chromatography by measuring levels of 2-hydroxyethidium, the product of superoxide’s reaction with dihydroethidium. Singlet oxygen yield was assessed using Singlet Oxygen Sensor Green (SOSG), with fluorescence changes recorded using a spectrofluorometer. This work and additional studies support the conclusion that KillerRed acts mainly through Type 1 reactions generating superoxide but may produce some singlet oxygen via Type 2 reactions [23, 25, 27–29]. SOSG has controversial efficacy for singlet oxygen specificity however [30–32], and according to one report using ¹O₂ phosphorescence, a direct measure of singlet oxygen, KillerRed produced no signal above background [29]. SuperNova

KillerRed was the first genetically encoded photosensitizing protein tool, with phototoxicity at least 1000 times greater than other fluorescent proteins. However, KillerRed’s dimeric nature often causes abnormal localization when fused to target proteins, which unintentionally disrupts their function [21]. To address this, Takeharu Nagai and his team developed a monomeric variant, SuperNova, through random mutagenesis [33]. SuperNova contains six mutations (G3V, N145S, L160T, F162T, L172K, and M204T) relative to the original KillerRed sequence. Because it is monomeric, SuperNova does not disrupt target localization and maintains its capacity to generate ROS [33]. To broaden the color range of photosensitizing proteins, T. Nagai and team engineered SuperNova Green by introducing two mutations (Tyr66Trp and Val44Ala mutation) to its predecessor [34]. Upon blue light activation, SuperNova Green produces insignificant amounts of singlet oxygen, primarily generating superoxide and its related derivatives [34].

HyperNova

SuperNova often exhibits weak fluorescence and poor maturation when fused to other proteins, likely due to its low folding efficiency, limiting its effectiveness. Using error-prone PCR and DNA shuffling, a study by K. Takemoto and his team introduced random mutations in SuperNova and evaluated maturation in E. coli, which generated HyperNova. There are eight amino acid substitutions (S10T/S79I/D114G/H148R/F150L/H152Y/M173V/D237Y) and three silent mutations relative to SuperNova. By fusing the ferritin gene to HyperNova and expressing it in E. coli at 37 °C, they identified variants with significantly improved maturation efficiency compared to SuperNova [35]. HyperNova retains similar in vitro superoxide production to SuperNova, but matures faster and is more efficient in cell killing in mammalian cells.

KillerOrange

KillerOrange was developed from KillerRed by introducing a Tyr66Trp mutation in the chromophore, followed by random mutagenesis to enhance fluorescence and phototoxicity [36]. The mutations Gly3Cys, Asp113Ser, Asn145Ser, Phe177Leu, Tyr221His, and Glu236Gln resulted in an orange-fluorescent variant (512 nm excitation and 555 nm emission). It exhibits phototoxicity, producing superoxide, and can be used alongside KillerRed without cross-activation. Like KillerRed, it is dimeric, but a monomeric version was created by introducing the Tyr66Trp mutation into the SuperNova chromophore, forming the QWG (Gln65-Trp66-Gly67) chromophore [33]. Structural analysis revealed a water-accessible chromophore and an unusual trans-cis conformation of Trp66, likely responsible for its orange fluorescence [36].

mKate2

mKate is a red fluorescent protein, derived from Entacmaea quadricolor. mKate2 is a monomeric far-red fluorescent protein with excitation and emission peaks at 588 and 633 nm, respectively [37]. It was developed from mKate by introducing three mutations—S165A to boost brightness, and V48A and K238R to enhance maturation, pH stability, and photostability. Both mKate and mKate2 are recognized for their phototoxic properties as they both produce singlet oxygen [37–41].

mCherry

Although commonly used as a fluorescent tag, mCherry can produce ROS comparable to KillerRed [26]. mCherry is the monomeric derivative of DsRed, which is a tetrameric protein derived from the coral Discosoma striata. Care should be taken in the use of this tag for protein visualization, as excessive light exposure can inactivate the protein mCherry that is fused to or cause more extensive cellular damage [33, 39].

Mini singlet oxygen generator (MiniSOG)

MiniSOG was derived from the LOV (light, oxygen, and voltage) domain of Arabidopsis thaliana phototropin 2 through mutagenesis [25]. LOV domains are blue-light photoreceptors that respond to light by forming a reversible covalent bond between a flavin molecule and cysteine, using the energy from the excited state to drive this process [42]. Substituting the cysteine residue with alanine or glycine prevents the formation of the covalent bond and results in a phototoxic protein [43, 44]. miniSOG contains six mutations compared to its precursor, two of them involving residues surrounding the chromophore [45, 46]. This protein tightly binds flavin mononucleotide (FMN), an endogenous cofactor derived from riboflavin (KD ∼ 170 pM). miniSOG is non-toxic in the absence of light but fluoresces green (500 nm emission) under blue light (448 nm) and produces cytotoxic ROS. It was first reported to generate singlet oxygen via a Type 2 photoreaction, transferring energy to molecular oxygen from excited FMN. However, the singlet oxygen quantum yield for miniSOG has been shown by multiple groups to be less than FMN itself, calling into question its ROS specificity [11, 46–48]. Indeed, follow-up studies have concluded that the miniSOG protein shell shifts the excited FMN into a Type 1 reaction, instead of Type 2 [48].

SOPP

Enhanced derivatives of miniSOG—singlet oxygen photosensitizing protein (SOPP), SOPP2, SOPP3, and miniSOG2—have been developed [11, 49, 50]. These contain mutations in amino acids that reduce the excited photosensitizer’s electron affinity, remove electron-donating amino acids, and facilitate oxygen diffusion. SOPP3 contains all of these mutations and has dramatically improved singlet oxygen quantum yield of 0.6, matching free FMN, as well as a fluorescence quantum yield of 0.4 in vitro [11]. However, in bacteria and mammalian cells, there is evidence that SOPP3 activation generates superoxide, which likely reflects the complexity of a biological system compared to a defined buffer [51, 52].

Other LOV-based flavoproteins generate ¹O₂ and/or H2O2, though with varying efficiency and selectivity [53]. For example, Pp2FbFP and DsFbFP M49I emerged as promising tools for light-controlled bacterial killing and ROS-related signaling studies. Pp2FbFPL30M, a mutant variant of Pp2FbFP generated via site-directed mutation at position 30, has a relatively low fluorescence quantum yield but produces singlet oxygen at nearly three times the level of miniSOG [54].

Chemogenetic

While exogenous addition of H2O2 to cells is common, researchers have developed a more sophisticated genetic tool for spatial and temporal production of H2O2. The novel system D-amino acid oxidase (DAAO) produces H2O2 in response to D-amino acids and is derived from the yeast species Rhodotorula gracilis [55]. Recent studies have also discovered the presence of DAAOs in various organisms, including bacteria and humans [56, 57]. When combined with genetically encoded H2O2 sensors like HyPer, these systems create powerful platforms for real-time, site-specific investigation of H2O2’s effects in living cells [58].

Chemogenetic DAAOs are enzymes that specifically oxidize D-amino acids into their corresponding α-keto acids, producing H2O2, ammonium (NH4+), and pyruvate [59]. Since mammalian cells primarily contain L-amino acids, DAAO remains inactive unless D-amino acids are added to cell culture media or delivered in vivo. Among various D-amino acids, D-alanine is most commonly used due to its high turnover rate, enabling rapid intracellular H2O2 production [60]. From 4–8 mM D-alanine, DAAO is estimated to generate 50–100 nM H2O2 (well above typical physiological levels), sufficient to alter the cell’s redox balance [61]. Meanwhile, the additional byproducts, pyruvate and ammonium, are minimal given their abundance in cells. DAAO has been targeted to different cellular compartments such as the cytoplasm, plasma membrane, mitochondria, and nucleus.

Early H2O2 tracking techniques relied on H2O2-sensitive dyes, but these had many limitations—including irreversible oxidation, poor specificity, and lack of subcellular targeting. Numerous genetically encoded fluorescent indicators for H2O2 have been developed, classified mainly into HyPer sensors, roGFP-based sensors, and FRET-based sensors [58, 62–65]. Most HyPer-based sensors are built by inserting a circularly permuted fluorescent protein (a protein where the amino acid sequence has been rearranged, such that the original N- and C-termini are linked) to the OxyR transcription factor (Oxidative stress regulator of E. coli. that responds to H2O2) that lacks its DNA-binding domain [58, 65–67]. H2O2 oxidizes the OxyR domain, forming an internal disulfide bond that triggers a conformational change [68]. These rearrangements are then transmitted into the chromophore center of the fluorescent protein, producing a measurable fluorescence shift. The oxidized state of OxyR can be reversed by endogenous glutaredoxins, allowing the sensor to reset and respond repeatedly to H2O2 fluctuations [69].

Chemoptogenetic

FAP

The Fluorogen Activating Peptide (FAP) system was introduced by Alan Waggoner and his team in 2008 for imaging cell surface proteins [70]. It was developed from an earlier system where fluorescein arsenical hairpin (FlAsH) and resorufin arsenical hairpin (ReAsH) flourogens bind to genetically engineered tetracysteine sequences, enhancing their green and red fluorescence, respectively [71]. The FAP system consists of two primary elements: the FAP itself and the fluorogen. When the FAP binds to the fluorogen, it restricts the chromophore’s free rotation, which allows the fluorogen to be excited by light and maintain a stable, fluorescent state [72, 73].

FAPs were derived from human single-chain variable fragments (scFvs), which are engineered proteins consisting of Immunoglobulin G variable heavy (VH) and variable light (VL) domains linked by a short flexible peptide. They were primarily selected for their small size (<30 kDa) and ability to recognize a diverse array of antigens. Fluorescence-activated cell sorting was used to screen a library of roughly 109 scFvs for their ability to bind Thiazole orange (TO) and malachite green (MG). These dyes produce strong fluorescence upon binding to nucleic acids, due to restricted rotation of the chromophore, which inhibits nonradiative deactivation of the excited state [70]. Derivative compounds were generated to enhance both cell-impermeable and cell-permeable applications. The addition of chemical groups like sulfonates, polyethylene glycol (PEG), and amino groups, which generally carry a negative charge, prevents molecules from crossing the cell membrane. PEG modified MG (MG-2p) and sulfonated and PEG modified TO (TO1) were developed to increase cell impermeability [74], reduce background binding to DNA, and perform in biological systems. In contrast, cell-permeable fluorogens were designed through the addition of chemical groups like esters (MG-ester) [70].

Using MG-2p and TO1, eight distinct FAPs were identified from the library, with the smallest—L5 FAP—comprising 110 amino acids. Among these eight, two demonstrated higher binding affinity to TO1, while the other six showed affinity for MG-2p. L5 and MG-2p exhibited the highest fluorescence enhancement—approximately a 4 000-fold increase compared to the free fluorogen, resulting in a quantum yield of 0.048. When bound to their target FAPs, these fluorogens exhibit excitation peak shifts—from 607 to 655 nm for modified MG, and from 504 nm to 530 nm for modified TO. The FAP technique effectively enabled imaging of proteins on the cell surface, and the MG-ester–FAP complex allowed visualization of structures like the nuclear periphery and the Golgi apparatus [70].

Through directed evolution, the binding of FAPs to MG has been increased [75]. The L5* FAP was engineered by introducing an L89S mutation in the VL domain of the precursor L5, resulting in enhanced binding to MG and a roughly five-fold increase in fluorescence quantum yield in comparison to the original L5 FAP. Another variant, dL5** (25 kDa), was engineered by dimerizing the VL domain (L5) and introducing two mutations (L89S and E50D), resulting in a more stable and highly fluorescent complex with MG. This variant exhibits an exceptionally low picomolar dissociation constant and has a fluorescence quantum yield of 0.20 [75].

In 2016, Marcel P. Bruchez and his team developed a targeted and activated photosensitizer (TAP)-FAP system, in which a FAP binds to a heavy atom substituted fluorogen, creating an active photosensitizer capable of generating ROS upon exposure to near-infrared light (NIR) [76]. They hypothesized that suppressing nonradiative relaxation in MG fluorogens could be used to control intersystem crossing when paired with engineered fluorogens [77]. An iodinated MG-ester derivative was synthesized in which iodine fills the ortho positions of the aromatic ester group, producing di-iodinated MG-ester (MG2I) [78, 79].

The di-iodination shifted the excitation maximum of the complex into the NIR range at 666 nm. Under NIR light, MG2I and FAPdl5** together displayed significant singlet oxygen generation as evaluated by bleaching of anthracene-9,10-dipropionic acid (ADPA), a commonly used 1O2 scavenger. MG2I and FAPdl5** singlet oxygen quantum yield was reported as 0.13, while 1O2 generation from MG-ester–dL5** or free MG2I was undetectable [80]. Additionally, ADPA bleaching showed minimal FAP-TAP activation under 500 nm and 531 nm light, indicating these YFP and RFP proteins could be used in conjunction with MG2I and FAPdl5**. In deuterated PBS, the half-life of singlet oxygen is increased [81]. ADPA bleaching in deuterated PBS demonstrated a 450-fold increase in 1O2 generation upon MG2I binding to FAPdl5**, compared to MG2I alone, confirming that the free dye is non-photosensitizing, which is a key advantage over other methods [76]. Using the gold-standard approach of 1275 nm phosphorescence measurement of singlet oxygen, a separate group found a comparable singlet oxygen quantum yield for MG2I and FAPdl5** of 0.18 [12]. Even in biological systems, the cytotoxic effects of MG2I and FAPdl5** could be reduced by the singlet oxygen quencher sodium azide, but not catalase or superoxide dismutase, confirming the role of singlet oxygen and not superoxide [76]. Different research groups are working on enhancing the FAP-TAP system’s efficiency; one example is the development of the MG-HBr and MG-HI fluorogens [82, 83].

Halo-DBF

Robert Spitale and his team developed another chemoptogenetic strategy to study RNA localization in live cells, utilizing the dibromo fluorescein-halo ligand (Halo-DBF) [84]. Their approach used Halo-tagged constructs targeted to specific cellular compartments to track RNA, tagging histone H2B in the nucleus and NF-κB p65 in the cytoplasm. Upon blue light exposure, followed by an enrichment reaction, RNA localization and identification could be achieved in distinct cellular regions.

Advantages and drawbacks

Depending on the particular experiment, each ROS genetic tool has advantages and disadvantages. From an ease-of-use standpoint, optogenetic and chemogenetic tools have a lower barrier to entry since they only require light or a ligand. Because of this, not only is there more literature on their use, but some even have tailored reagents like antibodies that are commercially available for KillerRed. Since the optogenetic proteins are both photosensitizers and fluorescent, it is easy to use microscopy to visualize them in cells. Additionally, while not specific to DAAO, the availability of real-time H2O2 sensors significantly improves the rigor of studies using them.

There are important drawbacks to both systems, however. Chromophores activated by light are obviously subject to background excitation. This is especially true for those activated below 650 nm, as standard fluorescent light bulbs are a mixture of light from ∼400 nm to 630 nm. An example of this was shown with SuperNova, where the “dark control” cells displayed cell death when expressing SuperNova without experimental irradiation [85]. The authors suggested that, despite efforts to block light, SuperNova was being activated by ambient room light. Additionally, as mentioned, KillerRed exists as a dimer and can multimerize in cells, forming aggregates, potentially disrupting cell function even in the absence of irradiation [33]. And while their fluorescent properties can make imaging optogenetic tools easy, they can lead to unintentional damage as well. DAAO is a tunable system; however, H2O2 has a much greater radius of diffusion compared to singlet oxygen and hydroxyl radicals, limiting its spatial specificity [86]. DAAOs are additionally not fluorescent proteins, so they cannot be directly visualized in cells.

Our group favors the use of chemoptogenetic tools due to three key advantages. First, FAPdl5** and MG2I are activated by 666 nm light, which dramatically limits background excitation, is not absorbed by most endogenous chromophores in the cell, and has significant tissue penetration. Second, the use of a photosensitizer dye in addition to the genetic component allows for even greater temporal control and allows for the use of different dyes with the same binding protein. For example, to date, FAPdl5** has been used with MG-ester for imaging, as well as MG21 and other heavy-atom substituted MG-ester derivatives for photosensitization. New dyes may be developed to produce new effects [76, 82]. Third, the FAPdl5** and MG2I system, to our knowledge, is the only system specific for singlet oxygen production in mammalian cells. While the evolved versions of MiniSOG (SOPP) may have a greater singlet oxygen quantum yield, they still produce superoxide in biological systems as well (a consequence of secondary reactions) [52]. The drawbacks to FAPdl5** and MG2I, however, include the need for MG2I synthesis, as it is not commercially available, and a specialized 660 LED lightbox for irradiation. Additionally, since FAPdl5** itself is not fluorescent, researchers need to fuse another fluorescent protein to it (like mCerulean) or separately image with the original MG-ester dye for protein visualization without photosensitization. Applications

This section summarizes the use cases for optogenetic, chemogenetic, and chemoptogenetic ROS tools. While these tools have broad applications—including protein localization, in vivo imaging, biosensor design, and drug discovery—we will specifically focus on their ROS-dependent applications.

Chromophore-assisted light inactivation (CALI)

CALI is a powerful technique that enables protein inactivation by attaching a photosensitizer to a target protein. Upon light exposure, the excited photosensitizer produces ROS that oxidizes nearby amino acids like tryptophan, histidine, tyrosine, cysteine, and methionine, which disrupts protein structure and function. Many studies have demonstrated that optogenetic photosensitizers such as miniSOG, KillerRed, and SuperNova are efficient CALI agents [21, 24, 33, 87]. For instance, MiniSOG has been successfully employed for CALI in C. elegans to inactivate the mitochondrial electron transport chain Complex II [88]. Furthermore, Lin and colleagues developed a method known as InSynC (Inhibition of Synapses with CALI), which utilizes miniSOG to precisely and reversibly silence specific synapses in a light-dependent manner [87]. Type 2 photosensitizers producing singlet oxygen are preferred for CALI, as their short lifetime allows for more precise and localized damage than the broader effects of superoxide [89]. In mammalian systems, the chemoptogenetic FAP-TAP system has been employed for CALI in human HEK293T cells. When targeting the PLCδ1 plekstrin homology domain, MG2I and FAPdl5** demonstrated significantly greater CALI efficacy than KillerRed, achieving inactivation within just 10 seconds, compared to the 5 minutes required for KillerRed under equal light power [76].

CALI has seen broad application in cancer research and therapy [90]. It enables the selective inactivation of oncogenic signaling proteins by linking chromophores to antibodies, peptides, or genetic tags that specifically target proteins such as Src family kinases [91–93]. This approach provides precise spatial and temporal control, reducing off-target effects commonly associated with systemic inhibitors. Unlike conventional inhibitors, CALI allows protein inactivation at defined subcellular locations, which is particularly important in cancer signaling pathways where protein function is influenced by its localization [94].

Photoablation

Similar to CALI, photoablation uses light to selectively destroy cells. This approach relies on the oxidation of cellular macromolecules by ROS generated from the photosensitizer, leading to oxidative stress-induced cell death. Several genetically encoded photosensitizers, including KillerRed, SuperNova, HyperNova, and miniSOG, have proven effective for this purpose [35, 85, 95–97]. For example, miniSOG activation in the mitochondria or cytoplasm of C. elegans has been shown to induce cell death [96]. Using enhanced MiniSOG derivatives, Xu and colleagues further enhanced ablation in C. elegans by increasing singlet oxygen production and targeting the plasma membrane using SOPP [98]. MiniSOG2 ablation of neurons has also been demonstrated in Drosophila [49]. In another study, Sergey A. Lukyanov and colleagues developed HeLa Kyoto cell lines stably expressing miniSOG in mitochondria, chromatin, and the plasma membrane, which demonstrated cell ablation in a dose-dependent manner upon blue light irradiation [99].

FAP-TAPs have demonstrated robust efficacy in the photoablation of various cell types, including mouse CT26 cells, human HEK293T cells, T lymphocytes, and neuronal cells [76, 100–102]. A study by Marcel Bruchez demonstrated that HEK293T cells expressing FAPdl5** targeted to the cell membrane and treated with MG2I dye under NIR light lost cell morphology by blebbing and swelling within a short period of time. This study also compared mitochondrial-targeted photoablation using KillerRed and FAPdl5**. Exposure of HEK293T cells expressing mitochondrial KillerRed to 560 nm laser light for 90 minutes induced apoptosis and resulted in approximately 50% cell death. In contrast, FAP-TAP targeted to mitochondria achieved 100% cell killing after 90 seconds of illumination at the same laser power, using 60-fold less energy, without harming surrounding cells. Additionally, the FAP-TAP system is amenable to cell ablation in model organisms as demonstrated by Ed Burton's lab and others [76, 102–104].

As a note, although CALI and photoablation both utilize light-activated technologies, the primary distinction between the two lies in the light parameters employed: photoablation typically requires higher energy input compared to CALI. For example, chemoptogenetic strategies for photoablation have been shown to require approximately ten times more energy than those used for CALI [76]. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that switching from continuous to pulsed light exposure can significantly enhance the efficiency of optogenetic cell ablation [96, 105].

Similar to CALI, the photoablation technique has been utilized in cancer therapy. It enables precise destruction of localized solid tumors, while minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissue [106, 107]. When combined with imaging techniques like MRI, ultrasound, or CT, it allows for tumor targeting with millimeter-level precision [106]. Additionally, photoablation is frequently integrated with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or radiation to improve overall treatment outcomes. The most commonly employed forms of photoablation include Photodynamic Therapy (PDT, discussed in the next section), Laser Interstitial Thermal Therapy, and Near-Infrared Photoimmunotherapy [108, 109].

Photo-dynamic therapy

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is a light-based treatment that uses photosensitizing agents and oxygen to destroy target cells [110]. PDT involves multiple steps; a low-toxicity photosensitizer is first applied, either topically or systemically. Once it accumulates in the diseased tissue, the area is exposed to light, triggering the production of ROS that selectively destroys target cells without harming nearby healthy tissue [111]. PDT has some advantages compared to other cancer treatments as it is more tissue-specific, less toxic to healthy tissues, and significantly less invasive [112]. Conventional PDT has been used to treat different cancers such as skin cancer, head and neck cancer, lung cancer, and vaginal cancers [113]. Currently, PDT is limited to the treatment of cancer that light can reach, i.e. topical tumors. However, higher wavelength light (>650 nm) can penetrate from 5–10 mm of tissue, reaching the subcutaneous layer [114].

The use of optogenetic tools in PDT is a growing field of study that could potentially revolutionize PDT. Early work using HeLa Kyoto spheroids expressing H2B-KillerRed found pulsed irradiation was significantly more effective at tumor cell killing compared to continuous, and at irradiances which did not harm cells not expressing KillerRed [115]. In the same year, the first mouse study of KillerRed PDT was published using a novel, pH-sensitive, liposomal gene delivery system [116]. H1299 subcutaneous tumor cells received KillerRed through tail vein injection, and laser irradiation was able to shrink tumors and increase survival. A caveat to this finding is that the authors used a construct overexpressing p53 in addition to KillerRed. Years later, another study validated the effects of this system in a different mouse tumor model; however, they also found over-expression of p53 and KillerRed without irradiation significantly reduced tumor size, suggesting the efficacy of KillerRed in PDT may be limited [117].

Despite these advances, an inherent limitation of KillerRed and other lower wavelength photosensitizers for PDT is that light below 600 nm has incredibly poor tissue penetration, making them suitable only for surface-level lesions. This has not stopped researchers from engineering ingenious methods to circumvent this, including the use of upconversion nanoparticles which absorb NIR and emit visible light to activate KillerRed even below 10 mm of tissue [118, 119]. Another interesting approach for improving KillerRed efficacy in PDT is co-expression of catalase to generate oxygen in hypoxic tissue, using an oncolytic viral system [120]. Again, using a subcutaneous mouse tumor model, the authors saw tumor shrinkage in irradiated KillerRed expressing cells; however, this was minimal in comparison to infected, non-irradiated cells.

Like KillerRed, MiniSOG has been applied in PDT; however, only spheroid studies have been conducted to our knowledge [121]. Regardless, as previously mentioned, PDT requires photosensitizers that absorb longer wavelengths to penetrate tissue, rendering MiniSOG an ill fit. The FAP-TAP system has yet to our knowledge, been used in cancer PDT applications, but given its longer wavelength for activation, and efficacy in organisms like zebrafish and c. elegans, it holds promise for clinical PDT.

Oxidative DNA damage and repair

Genetically encoded photosensitizers are increasingly used to study oxidative DNA damage. KillerRed fused to histones was shown to induce widespread DNA damage and recruit DNA repair proteins to chromatin [122]. Similarly, activation of H2B-miniSOG in HeLa Kyoto cells led to extensive genomic DNA damage and triggered DNA damage response pathways [99]. The first site-specific studies of KillerRed-mediated DNA damage and repair were performed by fusing KillerRed to tetracycline repressor (TetR) or a transcriptional activator. This method was employed to study the recruitment of DNA repair proteins to euchromatic versus heterochromatic regions. The findings revealed that oxidative DNA damage is processed differentially depending on the chromatin context [123]. Additionally, Brendan Price’s team recently developed a dCas9-KillerRed fusion protein to induce targeted oxidative DNA damage. This new tool (dCas9-KR) can induce localized ROS, causing clustered oxidative DNA damage, including DNA breaks and damaged bases [124].

In a parallel line of research, Patricia Opresko and her team were the first to employ the genetically encoded FAP–TAP system to study oxidative DNA damage in human cell lines. By fusing FAP to telomeric repeat-binding factor 1 (TRF1), they generated singlet oxygen specifically at telomeres, producing the common oxidative lesion, 8oxoG [125]. This has been used for numerous projects, including the study of DNA repair protein recruitment kinetics and requirements [126, 127]. FAP-TRF1 has proved invaluable for studying telomere-specific oxidative DNA damage in both cancerous and non-cancerous cell lines [101, 125, 128–131]. The tool has allowed researchers to ask questions about telomere biology that were previously intractable with the available systems, which were either not telomere specific or not ROS specific.

Aside from cell lines, genetically encoded photosensitizers have been used to study oxidative DNA damage in model organisms. A histone miniSOG fusion (HIS-72) was employed in c. elegans to study ROS mutagenesis. The ROS-induced damage was shown to result in heritable mutations in C. elegans, demonstrating the technique’s potential as a powerful tool for genetic manipulation. The induced mutations encompassed a diverse spectrum, including single-nucleotide variants, deletions, and the insertion of extrachromosomal transgenes [132].

More recently, Aditi Gurkar and her team adapted this approach in C. elegans using FAP-TAP. They achieved this by fusing FAP to HIS-72, a histone variant expressed in all cell types, allowing for systemic induction of oxidative DNA damage. Their findings demonstrated that exposure to MG2I dye in combination with NIR light elicited DNA damage, resulting in embryonic lethality, delayed development, and significantly reduced lifespan in C. elegans [133].

Correlative light electron microscopy (CLEM)

The first reported application for MiniSOG was correlative light and electron microscopy (CLEM) [25]. This technique uses the singlet oxygen produced by MiniSOG to oxidize diaminobenzidine (DAB). Oxidized DAB reacts with osmium tetroxide, which allows for EM imaging at incredible resolution. This approach has been used for EM imaging of mitochondria as well as neuronal α-synuclein [134, 135]. While not reported, the FAP-TAP system should be amenable to this application.

Transcriptomics and proteomics

In 2019, Peng Zou and colleagues pioneered a novel approach named CAP-Seq employing miniSOG for transcriptomic profiling [136]. They developed a system enabling selective labeling of RNA in living cells by leveraging miniSOG’s ability to produce ROS in a light- and proximity-dependent manner. These oxidatively modified nucleotides act as molecular tags, facilitating enrichment via affinity-based purification and enabling their identification through high-throughput RNA sequencing. FAP-seq was developed recently with the same strategy in mind, enabling high-resolution mapping of RNA in living cells [83]. This approach utilizes a genetically encoded FAP in combination with a mono-iodinated malachite green derivative (MG-HI). Upon activation by NIR light, the FAP–MG-HI complex produces singlet oxygen, leading to the selective oxidation of nearby RNA nucleobases. These oxidized RNA species can subsequently be labeled in situ and identified through downstream analytical methods, enabling precise spatial transcriptomic profiling.

In the field of proteomics, various spatially selective chemical labeling methods have been developed to map the subcellular proteome. These typically use genetically targeted enzymes (e.g. APEX, BioID, TurboID) that generate short-lived reactive intermediates (e.g. biotin-linked radicals or adenylates) in live cells, labeling nearby proteins with high spatial precision [137–139]. However, these techniques have several drawbacks. For example, APEX relies on H2O2, which can be harmful to cells and affect their physiology. To tackle this, genetically encoded photosensitizers have been employed for the identification and profiling of the intracellular proteome. One such example is the ROS-induced protein labeling and identification (RinID) method, which was developed in 2022. In this approach, miniSOG was utilized for proteomic profiling, where photo-induced oxidation of nearby proteins facilitates their subsequent labeling with a nucleophilic probe. The labeled proteins are then enriched via affinity-based methods and identified through mass spectrometry [140]. Like miniSOG, SOPP3 was also employed for intracellular proteome tagging, where light-induced singlet oxygen oxidized nearby proteins, enabling their concurrent enrichment and subsequent proteomic profiling with high spatiotemporal resolution in living cells [51, 141].

Application of DAAO in cancer

The DAAO system has also seen success as a potential cancer therapeutic [142, 143]. When DAAO is successfully expressed in gliosarcoma cells, the addition of its substrate D-alanine leads to the localized production of H2O2, resulting in significant cytotoxic effects [144]. Furthermore, several murine studies have demonstrated that PEGylated DAAO (PEG-DAAO) is highly effective in targeting solid tumors. Notable tumor growth inhibition has been observed upon administration of D-proline in conjunction with DAAO [145, 146]. Recent innovations in chemotherapy include the conjugation of DAAO to magnetic nanoparticles, enabling site-specific delivery to tumors through external magnetic field guidance, thereby enhancing therapeutic precision and efficacy [147].

Summary

In summary, the manipulation of ROS in cells and organisms has come a long way in the past 2 decades. It is clear that researchers have come to appreciate the power of spatial and temporal control for experimentation, and optogenetic, chemogenetic, and chemoptogenetic toolkits are at the cutting-edge of oxidative stress biology. Moreover, there is immense room for these tools to be studied and applied for the treatment of human disease, including cancer. This can potentially transform cancer care by improving the clinician’s ability to target the diseased tissue and leave normal tissue unharmed in a minimally invasive manner.
