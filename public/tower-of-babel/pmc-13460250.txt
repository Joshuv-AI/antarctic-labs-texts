6,13-Pentacenequinone (PQ)–CuO nanocomposites for sunlight-driven dye degradation: first evidence of PQ as an antimicrobial active component
Authors: Ganesh P. Dawange, Vikram U. Pandit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
CuO–PQ nanocomposites with 0.5 (CP 0.5), 1.0 (CP 1.0) and 1.5 wt% (CP 1.5) of PQ were prepared using a wet impregnation route and assessed for sunlight driven dye degradation and antibacterial activity. Powder X-ray diffraction confirmed pristine monoclinic CuO together with characteristic PQ reflections in the composites. UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy showed progressive band gap narrowing from 2.32 eV for CuO to 1.75, 1.68 and 1.63 eV for CP 0.5, CP 1.0 and CP 1.5, respectively, thereby increasing visible light absorption. CP 0.5 exhibited the highest photocatalytic performance, increasing the apparent rate constants for rhodamine 6G, methylene blue and safranine from 1.80 × 10−3, 2.40 × 10−3 and 3.92 × 10−3 min−1 for bare CuO to 1.03 × 10−2, 5.40 × 10−3 and 1.44 × 10−2 min−1, respectively. In antibacterial tests against Escherichia coli (EC), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA) and Staphylococcus aureus (SA), the CuO–PQ composites displayed significantly higher inhibition zones than CuO alone, reaching up to 27 mm for CP 1.0 and CP 1.5 against SA. While PQ has previously been incorporated into photocatalytic systems, its intrinsic antimicrobial activity has, to the best of our knowledge, not been reported earlier. In the current work we show that PQ acts as an antimicrobial active component within CuO–PQ nanocomposites. The improved dual functionality is attributed to interfacial coupling between CuO and PQ, which broadens the visible light response, promotes charge separation and enables more efficient generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) under sunlight.

Introduction

Semiconductor photocatalysis is a low-cost technique for the degradation of dyes and microbiological contaminants in water. It mineralises pollutants and inhibits bacterial development via the generation of reactive species by light.1 Among the many metal oxides, CuO is an attractive p-type semiconductor due to its comparatively narrow band gap (∼1.5–2.2 eV), abundance, low cost and chemical stability and has been widely used for dye degradation, organic transformations and antimicrobial coatings.2–4 However, the photocatalytic and antibacterial activities of bare CuO are still moderate under practical settings due to the sub-optimal visible-light absorption and quick electron–hole recombination as well as limited interfacial redox kinetics.5–8 To overcome these limitations, CuO has been linked with carbon materials, other semiconductors and molecular co-catalysts and other oxides such as TiO2 and ZnO, to generate heterojunctions.9,10 This allows for charge separation and red-shifts the spectrum response without a significant increase in synthesis complexity.11–14

Here, PQ, an air-stable quinone derivative/precursor of pentacene, has emerged as a robust organic semiconductor with a band gap of around 3.0 eV, strong π-conjugation and high electron affinity.9 Previous investigations demonstrated that PQ is an effective visible-light photocatalyst for the evolution of hydrogen from H2S and the breakdown of methylene blue (MB). PQ has been used additionally in PQ–TiO2, PQ–ZnO and PQ–BiOI nanocomposites as an electron-accepting co-catalyst for better charge separation and visible-light harvesting.10–16 These results indicate that PQ can be a useful organic component in hybrid photocatalysts, especially when combined with inorganic semiconductors.17–21 However, these investigations have mostly focused on photocatalysis, and to our knowledge, the antibacterial action of PQ itself has not been documented before.

Till date, there are no comprehensive studies on PQ-modified CuO systems for combined photocatalytic and antimicrobial applications that examine both dye degradation and antibacterial performance in a single, systematically optimised series. Such hybrid nanomaterials have emphasised the importance of band-gap engineering, defect control and surface chemistry in achieving strong antimicrobial effects, as demonstrated for various Cu-, Zn- and Ti-based systems in water disinfection and biomedical contexts.22–25 These works highlight that tailoring composition and architecture can markedly improve bactericidal efficiency, yet none of them have discovered PQ as an intrinsic antimicrobial contributor. To the best of our knowledge, there is no previous report on CuO–PQ nanocomposites with controlled PQ loading that simultaneously optimises dye degradation under sunlight and antibacterial activity. This integration is expected to result in the formation of an organo–inorganic heterojunction, where CuO provides abundant surface-active sites and narrow-gap absorption, while PQ provides additional light harvesting, redox-active quinone sites and efficient electron transport through the delocalised π-system.9,26–29 Furthermore, ROS formed at this heterojunctions can synergistically contribute to the inactivation of microbial pathogens, rendering these systems interesting for integrated water purification.30,31

Therefore, the present work was aimed not only at the synthesis of CuO–PQ nanocomposites with enhanced sunlight-induced photocatalytic activity but also at the investigation, for the first time, of the potential of PQ as an antimicrobial active ingredient for such hybrid systems. In this paper, we report on the synthesis of CuO–PQ (CP x) nanocomposites with PQ loadings of 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 wt% via a straightforward wet-impregnation approach based on precipitation-derived CuO and solution-processed PQ. The materials are fully characterised by X-ray diffraction (XRD), UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS), field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and selected-area electron diffraction (SAED). The photocatalytic efficiency is evaluated for degradation of rhodamine 6G (R6G), methylene blue (MB) and saffranine (SAF) under natural sunlight. Antibacterial activity is assessed against representative Gram-negative (EC, PA) and Gram-positive (SA) strains. Structure–property interactions are analysed as a function of PQ loading and compared to earlier PQ-based composites, emphasising the impact of band-gap modulation and interfacial architecture on the dual photocatalytic and antimicrobial performance.

Experimental

Materials and methods

Copper(ii) chloride (CuCl2·2H2O), sodium hydroxide pellets (NaOH), chloroform, ethanol and analytical grade dyes R6G, MB and SAF were employed as obtained without any further processing. PQ was prepared from o-phthalaldehyde and 1,4-cyclohexanedione using our earlier published solvent free mechanochemical process to provide crystalline monoclinic PQ having a band gap of ∼3 eV. All experiments were carried out using deionised water.9

Synthesis of CuO nanoparticles

CuO nanoparticles were synthesised by precipitation-calcination method. Briefly, 5.36 g of CuCl2 was dissolved in a minimum amount of deionised water under vigorous magnetic stirring (1200 rpm). In another beaker, 1.56 g NaOH was mixed in 100 mL of deionised water. This alkaline solution was then dropwise added to the above solution until the pH > 12, producing a dark green coloured precipitate. The precipitate was washed several times with deionised water and ethanol to eliminate chloride ions and to obtain neutral pH and then dried at 80 °C for 2 h. The dried precursor was calcined in air at a heating rate of 5 °C min−1 up to 500 °C for 4 h to obtain monoclinic CuO nanoparticles.13

Synthesis of CuO–PQ (CP x) nanocomposites

For the formation of composite, a PQ stock solution (1) was obtained by dissolving 5 mg PQ in 100 mL chloroform as shown in Fig. 1.

Schematic illustration of the preparation of CuO–PQ (CP x) nanocomposites via wet impregnation of PQ onto precipitated CuO.

The appropriate aliquots of this solution were added to above CuO dispersed in chloroform to give PQ loadings of 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 wt% with respect to CuO, denoted CP 0.5, CP 1.0 and CP 1.5 accordingly. The gravimetric uncertainty on PQ loading is estimated to be within ±0.05 wt%, and this variation is reflected in the error bars of the optical band-gap. The suspensions were agitated (3) at 1200 rpm for 6 h to enable homogenous adsorption and deposition of PQ onto the CuO surface and to allow the excess chloroform to evaporate gradually. The obtained powders were further dried (4) at 60 °C for 6 h and softly processed in an agate mortar to yield fine CuO–PQ nanocomposite powders (5).

Characterization

Powder X-ray diffraction patterns of CuO and CP x samples were measured with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å) in the 2θ range of 10–80°, using a step size of 0.02° and a scan rate of 1° min−1, with instrumental peak width of approximately 0.1–0.2° at half-maximum. The solid samples were characterised for their optical absorption properties using UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy in the range of 200–800 nm with a spectral resolution of 1 nm, and their apparent band gaps were determined from Tauc plots based on a direct band-gap transition. The surface morphologies were characterised by field-emission scanning electron microscopy operated at an accelerating voltage of 5–10 kV, and the internal structure and lattice fringes of CP 1.5 were further explored by transmission electron microscopy and selected-area electron diffraction using an operating voltage of 200 kV. XPS measurements were carried out with a monochromatic Al Kα source (hν = 1486.6 eV) and an analyser pass energy of 20–40 eV for high-resolution spectra.

Photocatalytic dye degradation under sunlight

The photocatalytic activity of CuO and CP x catalysts were studied for the degradation of R6G, MB and SAF under natural sunshine. Typically, 50 mg of catalyst was disseminated in 100 mL of aqueous dye solution (10 ppm) in a 150 mL borosilicate conical flask. The suspension was agitated magnetically in the dark for 30 min to reach the adsorption–desorption equilibrium between the dye molecules and the surface of the catalyst. The flask was then exposed to the solar light during noon time in Pune, India (11:00–14:00 h IST) with constant shaking. Aliquots were taken at set time intervals, centrifuged to remove the catalyst and the remaining dye concentration was measured by the typical absorbance maxima of R6G, MB and SAF with a UV–vis spectrophotometer (1 cm quartz cuvettes, spectral bandwidth 1 nm). The deterioration efficiency and pseudo first order rate constants (kapp) were computed from the plots of C/C0 and ln (C0/Ct) vs. time, respectively.

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of CuO and CP x samples was studied against Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus, using the agar well diffusion method. Fresh bacterial suspensions were plated on nutrient agar (NA) plates to generate homogenous lawns. Known amounts of catalyst distributed in sterile water were placed into the wells, and unloaded wells served as controls. To assess antibacterial efficiency, the widths of inhibition zones around each well were measured in mm after incubation at 37 °C for 24 h.

Results and discussion

Crystal structure and phase analysis

Fig. 2 shows the XRD patterns of pure CuO and CuO–PQ nanocomposites with varying PQ contents. The crystal structure of CuO and CuO–PQ (CP x) nanocomposites were studied by XRD. The diffraction pattern of pure CuO shows characteristic peaks at 2θ ≈ 33.1°, 35.2–36.2°, 39.4°, 49.5°, 58.9°, 62.2°, 66.9° and 68.7° corresponding to the (110), (002), (111), (202), (020), (113), (311) and (220) planes of monoclinic CuO (JCPDS 00-002-1041) which confirms the formation of phase pure CuO nanoparticles. All the CuO reflections are preserved in the CP x composites. New peaks are observed near 15° and 27° which can be indexed to the (012) and (104) planes of monoclinic PQ (JCPDS 47-2123). The intensity of these PQ-related reflections increases with PQ loading, showing that the crystalline PQ has been successfully incorporated in the composites.

XRD patterns of CuO and CuO–PQ (CP 0.5, CP 1.0 and CP 1.5) nanocomposites. Diffraction peaks are indexed to monoclinic CuO (JCPDS 00-002-1041), and the peaks marked with stars correspond to monoclinic PQ (JCPDS 47-2123).

No extra impurity peaks are seen, showing that the wet-impregnation process maintains the CuO lattice and that PQ is mostly present as a surface phase, in line with the behaviour reported for PQ–ZnO and PQ–BiOI composites.

To gain further insight into the microstructural evolution induced by PQ, the unit cell parameters (a, b, c), average crystallite size (D), dislocation density (δ) and microstrain (ε) of CuO and CP x samples were estimated from the XRD data using the Debye–Scherrer relation and standard expressions for ε and δ.31 The resulting values are compiled in SI Table T1. All compositions retain lattice parameters very close to monoclinic CuO (JCPDS 00-002-1041), with PQ loading causing minor shifts (≤0.02 Å), consistent with PQ acting as a surface phase rather than a bulk dopant. At CP 0.5, the crystallite size decreases slightly (from 21.85 to 20.50 nm) and both dislocation density and microstrain increase, indicating a modest rise in lattice defects and strain at the CuO–PQ interface. For CP 1.0 and CP 1.5, the crystallite size and defect metrics move back towards the CuO values, suggesting that higher PQ loadings do not strongly distort the bulk CuO lattice but still preserve sufficient interfacial defects to assist charge separation. These microstructural trends are in good agreement with the observed enhancement of photocatalytic activities.

Optical absorption and band gap

The UV–vis DRS spectra (Fig. 3) show that bare CuO exhibits strong absorption in the visible region with an onset near 550–600 nm, consistent with its direct band gap nature.

UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectra of CuO and CuO–PQ (CP 0.5, CP 1.0 and CP 1.5) nanocomposites. PQ incorporation induces a red-shift of the absorption edge and enhances visible-light absorption.

The optical absorption properties of CuO and CP x samples were studied by DRS. The as-prepared CuO shows a substantial absorption in the visible range, with a beginning at around 550–600 nm, in agreement with its narrow band gap and direct-transition nature (Fig. 4). The PQ inclusion results in a red-shift of the CuO absorption edge and raises the overall visible-light absorption intensity, especially for CP 1.0 and CP 1.5, which show extended absorption up to about 750–800 nm. These alterations suggest that PQ serves as a light-harvesting component and alters the electrical structure of CuO to improve its ability to utilise visible and near visible light.

Tauc plots of (αhν)2versus hν for (a) CuO, (b) CP 0.5, (c) CP 1.0 and (d) CP 1.5, used to estimate the apparent direct band-gap energies.

The band gaps were determined via Tauc plots of (αhν)2versus hν using the relation (αhν)2 = A (hν – Eg), where α is the absorption coefficient, hν is the photon energy, A is a constant and Eg is the direct band-gap energy. In Fig. 4 ((a) CuO, (b) CP 0.5, (c) CP 1.0 and (d) CP 1.5), the Y-axis is labelled as (αhν)2 (arbitrary units) and the X-axis as hν (eV). The estimated band-gap energies are 2.32 ± 0.03 eV for CuO and 1.75 ± 0.02, 1.68 ± 0.02 and 1.63 ± 0.02 eV for CP 0.5, CP 1.0 and CP 1.5, respectively, based on triplicate measurements and linear fitting of the Tauc plots.

Morphology and microstructure

FESEM images of CP 1.5 (Fig. 5) reveal that the composite consists of dense aggregates of CuO nanostructures uniformly distributed over PQ micro-sheets, with the CuO phase exhibiting sharp, rod-like morphologies.

FESEM images of CP 1.5 (a) showing CuO nanostructures distributed over PQ (b) micro-sheets, forming a nano-rod-on-sheet architecture.

The PQ scaffold provides a two-dimensional platform that promotes the diagonal growth and homogeneous dispersion of CuO nanorods, closely resembling the sheet-supported architectures seen in PQ–ZnO and PQ–BiOI composites.11 For clear understanding the FESEM images of CuO also provided in the SI Fig. S1.

TEM and high-resolution images (Fig. 6) further confirm the intimate contact between CuO and PQ, with well-resolved lattice fringes corresponding to CuO planes and a polycrystalline SAED pattern consistent with monoclinic CuO. Such nano-rod-on-sheet architectures are beneficial for photocatalysis because they shorten the carrier transport pathway to the surface and expose abundant interfacial active sites.

The (a) and (b) TEM and (c) HRTEM images of CP 1.5, together with the corresponding (d) SAED pattern, confirming intimate contact between CuO and PQ and the monoclinic CuO lattice.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy was utilised to study the surface composition and oxidation states of the representative CuO–PQ composite CP 0.5. The survey spectrum verifies the presence of Cu, O and C as the only detected elements (SI Fig. S2), indicating the absence of additional metallic or non-metallic impurities within the detection limits of the technique. The high-resolution Cu 2p region exhibits Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 peaks with prominent shake-up satellite features typical of Cu2+ in monoclinic CuO and excludes contributions from Cu2O or metallic Cu. The O 1s spectrum can be deconvoluted into a broad low-binding-energy component assigned to lattice O2− in CuO and a shoulder at higher binding energy associated with surface hydroxyl groups and/or adsorbed species known to be involved in photocatalytic radical production. No additional XPS components indicative of alternative copper oxides or extraneous phases are detected, and the minor shoulders observed in the spectra can be attributed to surface hydroxyls and adsorbates rather than the formation of new crystalline phases, consistent with the absence of extra peaks in the XRD patterns. The C 1s signal from adventitious carbon and the organic PQ component further confirms the CuO–PQ composite synthesis. Although XPS was not measured for CP 1.5 due to limited instrument access, the identical CuO reflections in XRD for all CP x samples and the systematic variation of PQ-related features suggest that PQ loading up to 1.5 wt% does not modify the Cu2+ oxidation state or generate additional crystalline phases. Possible surface charging during XPS acquisition was minimised by employing a low-energy electron flood gun, and all binding energies were referenced to the adventitious C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. For DRS and UV–vis measurements, powdered samples were pressed into compact pellets on conductive holders, and no distortions or baseline shifts indicative of charging were observed in the recorded spectra.

Photocatalytic dye degradation

The photocatalytic activities of CuO and CPx (x = 0.5, 1.0, 1.5) were studied for the degradation of rhodamine 6G (R6G), methylene blue (MB) and saffranine (SAF) under natural sunlight. A typical experiment was conducted by dispersing 50 mg of catalyst in 100 mL of 10 ppm aqueous dye solution. The suspension was agitated in dark for 30 min to achieve adsorption–desorption equilibrium between dye molecules and catalyst surface.

The concentration in all kinetic plots indicates that this equilibrium state is achieved at t = 0. Control studies without catalyst and with catalyst in dark showed low dye loss over the same duration. This indicates that both adsorption and direct photolysis contribute minimally under the conditions utilised. In the presence of CuO–PQ composites, the dye concentrations fell faster in the presence of the midday sunlight (11:00–14:00 h IST in Pune, India) than with bare CuO under continuous stirring. The time-dependent C/C0 plots (Fig. 7) demonstrate that CP 0.5 always exhibits the maximum photocatalytic activity for the three dyes.

For R6G, CP 0.5 eliminates around 46% of the dye in 90 min (54.03% dye remaining) but bare CuO removes only about 9.7% (90.33% dye remaining) which corresponds to an approximately fivefold increase in removal effectiveness. Similar results are obtained for MB and SAF, where after 90 min, CP 0.5 leaves 68.18% MB and 32.56% SAF, but CuO maintains 84.98% MB and 81.16% SAF showing a substantial boost that is dye and composition dependent.

Degradation kinetics were studied using a pseudo-first-order model, ln (C0/Ct) = kappt, and the apparent rate constants (kapp) are presented in Table 1. For R6G, the kapp changes from 1.80 × 10−3 min−1 for CuO to 1.03 × 10−2 min−1 for CP 0.5. For MB, the kapp changes from 2.40 × 10−3 min−1 to 5.40 × 10−3 min−1. For SAF, the kapp changes from 3.92 × 10−3 min−1 to 1.44 × 10−2 min−1. The observed slight decrease in activity at higher PQ loadings (CP 1.0 and CP 1.5) can be attributed to partial coverage of CuO active sites and light-shielding effect of PQ micro-sheets, resulting in non-linear dependence of photocatalytic performance on PQ content.

In summary, CP 0.5 performed better than bare CuO and greater PQ loadings for all three dyes, suggesting that 0.5 wt% PQ is an optimal compromise between improved light absorption, interfacial charge separation and available CuO surface area. Comparing these results, the apparent rate constants obtained for CP 0.5 are comparable or higher than those reported for many CuO-based photocatalysts in the degradation of cationic dyes under visible-light or solar irradiation, depending on the specific architecture and testing conditions. The reported kapp values of 1.03 × 10−2, 5.40 × 10−3 and 1.44 × 10−2 min−1 for R6G, MB and SAF, respectively, are thus indicative that low PQ loadings can substantially boost the performance of CuO, placing it in the upper range of activity for such systems. Furthermore, the employment of natural sunlight instead of monochromatic irradiation emphasises the practical applicability of the CuO–PQ nanocomposites for solar-driven dye degradation. In this work, single-use photocatalytic runs are investigated to establish structure–property relationships as a function of PQ loading. Systematic evaluation of recyclability, long-term stability and Cu2+ leaching behaviour under repeated cycles and in more complex water matrices is an important direction for future work.

It is noteworthy that CP 0.5 exhibits the highest rate constants for all three dyes, whereas CP 1.0 and CP 1.5 show lower kapp values and higher residual concentrations, despite some fluctuations in the R2 values (e.g., R2 = 0.948 for MB and 0.939 for SAF with CP 0.5). The pseudo-first-order model, ln (C0/Ct) = kappt, provides an adequate description of the kinetics over the investigated time window, with R2 values ≥ 0.939 for all fits, but we recognise that deviations from ideal behaviour can occur due to changes in surface coverage, light intensity and mass-transfer conditions. A more rigorous kinetic analysis based on Langmuir–Hinshelwood or multi-step reaction schemes will be carried out in future work to refine the mechanistic understanding of the CuO–PQ systems.

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of CuO and CP x samples was evaluated against EC, PA and SA using the agar well diffusion method. The diameters of the inhibition zones for naked CuO, pure PQ and CuO–PQ composites are summarised in Table 2, and representative images are shown in SI Fig. S3 (photographs). The control wells (without catalyst) show no inhibition zones, confirming that the nutrient agar and solvents do not exhibit detectable antibacterial effects under the test conditions. Bare CuO displays only modest activity, with inhibition zones of 1 mm for EC and PA and 9 mm for SA, indicating limited bactericidal action. Control experiments also carried out with PQ, tested as a suspension deposited in agar wells, reveal measurable inhibition zones of 1 mm for EC, 4 mm for PA and 8 mm for SA under the same conditions (Table 2). Although these values are lower than those obtained for CuO–PQ composites, owing to the poor dispersibility and limited wettability of pristine PQ, they nonetheless demonstrate that PQ itself exhibits intrinsic antibacterial activity.

In contrast, all CuO–PQ composites exhibit markedly enhanced antibacterial activity. For EC, CP 0.5, CP 1.0 and CP 1.5 produce inhibition zones of 23, 20 and 20 mm, respectively, while for PA the corresponding zones are 20, 16 and 16 mm. Against SA, CP 1.0 and CP 1.5 show the largest zones (27 mm), with CP 0.5 giving a slightly lower value of 17 mm. These results indicate that PQ modification significantly strengthens the bactericidal performance of CuO toward both Gram-negative and Gram-positive strains, with some variation depending on the microorganism and PQ loading.

The improved antibacterial activity is possibly associated with the production of reactive oxygen species at the CuO–PQ interface under ambient light, in addition to probable interactions of Cu2+ species with bacterial membranes, as proposed for other PQ-based heterojunction photocatalysts. Superoxide and hydroxyl radicals, as ROS, can damage cellular components, whereas Cu2+ can interfere with membrane integrity and metabolic pathways. Although direct detection of ROS or detailed analysis of membrane damage was not performed in this work, the pronounced increase in inhibition zones upon PQ incorporation, compared with bare CuO, supports a synergistic contribution of interfacial charge transfer and Cu-related effects to the observed antibacterial behaviour. Although the enhanced antibacterial activity of CuO–PQ composites is likely associated with increased ROS generation at the heterojunction interface, as well as direct interactions of PQ with bacterial membranes, we emphasise that this mechanistic picture is tentative. No radical trapping or ROS detection experiments (e.g., spin-trapping EPR, fluorescence probes) were performed in the present study, and the proposed antibacterial mechanism should therefore be regarded as speculative pending further experimental confirmation.

Recycle study

The most active photocatalyst CP 0.5 was further evaluated for reusability in a five-cycle experiment of SAF degradation under natural sunlight (SI Fig. S4). The degrading efficiency is quite high for all the cycles with a minor decrease from 100% in the first run to 95% after the 5th run. This small loss, within experimental uncertainty, demonstrates the photo stability of the CuO–PQ photocatalyst and its ability to recycle and maintain photocatalytic activity as described for other robust CuO based nanocomposite systems.

Preliminary recyclability tests were carried out for the most active composite (CP 0.5) over three consecutive photocatalytic runs under identical conditions (50 mg catalyst, 100 mL of 10 ppm dye solution, 90 min sunlight exposure), as shown in SI Fig. S4. The degradation efficiency remained within ±5% of the initial value over these cycles, indicating that CP 0.5 retains most of its activity under repeated use. XRD patterns recorded after cycling did not reveal new impurity phases or noticeable shifts in the CuO or PQ reflections, suggesting that the crystalline framework is largely preserved. Nevertheless, the present study is focused on the influence of PQ loading on initial photocatalytic and antibacterial performance; systematic evaluation of long-term stability, Cu2+ leaching and performance in complex water matrices will be addressed in future investigations.

Proposed photocatalytic mechanism

The photocatalytic activity of CuO–PQ nanocomposites can be explained by their optical characteristics and reported band locations of CuO and PQ. UV–vis DRS and Tauc studies demonstrate that the effective optical band gap of CuO is reduced from 2.32 eV to 1.75–1.63 eV for CP 0.5–CP 1.5 by the introduction of PQ, resulting in intensified absorption in the visible light region. These results imply that PQ works as a light-harvesting and electron-accepting moiety, providing extra electronic states that assist the promotion of charge-transfer events at the CuO–PQ interface.

Based on the band-gap narrowing observed in DRS and the established electron-accepting character of PQ in hybrid semiconductors,9,10,26–29 we propose that CuO–PQ nanocomposites operate via a heterojunction mechanism in which photogenerated electrons in the conduction band of CuO can be transferred to low-lying PQ states, while holes remain predominantly in CuO. Under sunlight, CuO absorbs visible photons to create electron–hole pairs, and PQ acts as a light-harvesting and electron-accepting component that facilitates charge separation and suppresses recombination. The accumulated electrons and holes then participate in redox reactions at the surface, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as –O2− and –OH that drive dye degradation and bacterial inactivation. However, in the absence of direct experimental determination of the absolute band-edge positions of CuO and PQ (e.g., from Mott–Schottky plots or XPS valence band spectra), we refrain from assigning a definitive Type-II or Z-scheme configuration and instead treat the present mechanism as a qualitative description consistent with the observed trends. Detailed band alignment and time-resolved studies will be necessary in future work to distinguish between these possibilities. Clear differentiation of type-II vs. Z-scheme behaviour would necessitate comprehensive band-edge measurements and time-resolved spectroscopic or electrochemical experiments as seen in Fig. 8.

The enhanced activity of CP 0.5 relative to CP 1.0 and CP 1.5 suggests that an ideal PQ coverage is achieved to maximise heterojunction interfaces and light utilisation without overshielding the CuO phase or obstructing its surface-active sites. At larger PQ loadings, the expanded PQ micro-sheets might partially block CuO from the incident light and prevent the entry of dye molecules and dissolved oxygen to CuO, which could be the reason for the observed nonlinear dependence of photocatalytic and antibacterial activity on PQ content. Overall, the available structural and optical data are compatible with a CuO-PQ heterojunction where the interfacial charge transfer and the formation of reactive oxygen species are important for the dual photocatalytic and antimicrobial behaviour. We stress that this process is postulated to rationalise the observed trends and is not yet clearly established. Extensive band-structure, radical-scavenger and time-resolved studies will be needed to identify the actual charge-transfer pathway.

Conclusions

In nutshell, CuO–PQ (CP x) nanocomposites containing 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 wt% PQ were synthesised via wet-impregnation method and assessed for dye degradation and antibacterial activity. Structural and optical characterisation confirmed phase-pure monoclinic CuO together with crystalline PQ. PQ incorporation narrows the apparent band gap from 2.32 eV for CuO to 1.75, 1.68 and 1.63 eV for CP 0.5, CP 1.0 and CP 1.5, respectively, accompanied by a red-shift. Under natural sunlight, the CuO–PQ composites significantly outperformed bare CuO in the degradation of rhodamine 6G, methylene blue and safranine, with CP 0.5 exhibiting the highest activity. The apparent rate constants increased from 1.80 × 10−3, 2.40 × 10−3 and 3.92 × 10−3 min−1 for CuO to 1.03 × 10−2, 5.40 × 10−3 and 1.44 × 10−2 min−1 for CP 0.5, and the residual dye fractions after 90 min decreased to 54.03%, 68.18% and 32.56%, respectively. Antibacterial tests against E. coli, P. aeruginosa and S. aureus revealed that the CuO–PQ composites generate markedly larger inhibition zones than CuO alone, reaching up to 27 mm for S. aureus with CP 1.0 and CP 1.5. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that low PQ loadings can substantially boost both photocatalytic and antibacterial performance of CuO. Present results provide the first evidence that PQ can act as an antimicrobial active component in a CuO-based hybrid nanocomposite. The enhanced dual functionality is attributed to interfacial coupling between CuO and PQ, which promotes band-gap modulation, charge separation and ROS generation under sunlight. Future work will focus on detailed band alignment studies, radical trapping experiments and long-term recyclability tests to further substantiate the proposed mechanisms and assess the durability of these materials in realistic water treatment scenarios.
