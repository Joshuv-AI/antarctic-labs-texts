CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knock-in of the murine Y chromosomal genes Zfy1 and Zfy2
Authors: Hayden Holmlund, Yasuhiro Yamauchi, Muhammetnur Tekayev, Sydney Jakobs, Antoine Robin, Wataru Fujii, Monika A Ward
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Y-linked mouse zinc finger genes Zfy1 and Zfy2 are critical fertility factors in mice but the mechanisms by which they regulate spermatogenesis remain unclear. We recently produced Zfy1/2 double knock-out mice and observed a complete loss in fertility. However, the biochemical mechanism by which Zfy regulates spermatogenesis is unknown, and ZFY expression has not yet been confirmed at the protein level. As both Zfy homologues share ~ 95% sequence similarity, it is difficult to produce an anti-ZFY antibody specific to either homologue.ResultsTo overcome this technical challenge, we used CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing to develop tagged Zfy1 knock-in (XYZfy1−HA), Zfy2 knock-in (XYZfy2−FLAG, XYZfy2–3xFLAG, and XYZfy2−HA), and Zfy1/2 double knock-in (XYZfy1−HA,Zfy2−MYC) mice. Successful targeting was confirmed by genotyping and sequencing. The knock-in lines were fertile with normal sperm parameters. Using Western blot on testes, knock-in specific bands were detected matching the predicted ZFY expression patterns. Using immunofluorescence on testis sections from knock-in males, ZFY1 and ZFY2 expression was detected in zygotene spermatocytes, and ZFY2 expression was also detected in spermatids step 7–8 and 9.ConclusionsThese novel knock-in mice can be used in future investigations to determine how ZFY controls spermatogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12462-4.Keywords: Spermatogenesis, Sperm, Testis, Y chromosome, Knock-in, CRISPR/Cas9

Background

The Y-linked zinc finger genes Zfy1 and Zfy2 have emerged as key players in murine reproduction (reviewed in [1]) and Y chromosome evolution [2–4]. Both genes encode for putative transcription factors and are expressed exclusively in the male germline [5]. The biochemical functions of ZFY1 and ZFY2 remain elusive due to a lack of reliable antibodies and high sequence similarity between homologues. However, over the past decade we, and others, have established that the Zfy genes are critical for male reproduction.

The past findings, indicative of the spermatogenic function of Zfy, were acquired with the use of mouse models with natural mutations, sex chromosome variants, and Y chromosome derived transgenes (reviewed in [1]). The key sex chromosome variants and models used in these studies included male mice with an X chromosome carrying sex reversal factors, either Sxra (XSxra) [6] or Sxrb (XSxrb) [7], and mice with a Y chromosome contribution limited to two other genes, Sry and Eif2s3y (XEif2s3y,Sry) [8]. The presence of either Sxra, or Sxrb, or the Sry transgene, warrants development of testis. The presence of Eif2s3y, either encoded within Sxra, or added as a transgene to XOSry or XSxrbO males, allows for the initiation and progression of spermatogenesis. These three models (XSxra, XSxrb, and XEif2s3y,Sry) differ regarding the presence of Zfy: mice with just Sry and Eif2s3y transgenes have no Zfy, mice carrying Sxra have both Zfy1 and Zfy2, and mice carrying Sxrb have the Zfy2/1 fusion gene (a coding sequence of Zfy1 driven by the Zfy2-specific promoter [5]). These models were studied in either a single X chromosome (XO) context or when the X chromosome was paired with a meiotic pairing partner chromosome variant Y*X (XY*X) [9], and with or without additions of the Zfy1 and/or the Zfy2 transgenes [10]. A plethora of work was done with these mice and reported by us, and others, demonstrating critical roles that Zfy1 and Zfy2 play in spermatogenesis [3, 10–17]. For example, the introduction of either Zfy1 or Zfy2 transgene to mice with limited Y chromosome gene content increased the production of haploid round spermatids [15, 16]. The Zfy2 transgene was more efficient in meiosis II restoration than the Zfy1 transgene, and in vitro assays confirmed that Zfy2 encodes for a more potent transactivating domain than Zfy1 [15]. We also identified Zfy2 as the gene required for development of sperm functional in intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) [16]. Mice with Y chromosome contribution limited to two genes, Sry and Eif2s3y, produce haploid round spermatids incapable of undergoing morphogenesis to become sperm [17]. When a third Y chromosome gene, Zfy2, was added to these mice as a transgene, spermatid elongation and tail formation was promoted, thereby facilitating the development of spermatozoa [16].

With the emergence of genome editing strategies, the critical role of Zfy was reinforced by development of knock-out (KO) models [18–20]. To directly assess the role of endogenous Zfy in spermatogenesis, we created Zfy1 knock-out (KO), Zfy2 KO, and Zfy1/2 double knock-out (DKO) mice by deleting the entire Zfy genomic sequence [20]. Zfy1 KO mice were shown to be fertile and had no obvious spermatogenic phenotype while Zfy2 KO mice had moderate fertility impairments in vivo, with decreased sperm number, increased incidence of sperm headshape defects [20], and decreased sperm fertilization ability using conventional in vitro fertilization (IVF) [21]. Zfy DKO mice were completely infertile with severe spermatogenesis defects, evidenced as very low sperm number with complete post-meiotic arrest at the round spermatid stage observed in the most extreme cases, grossly abnormal sperm morphology and motility, and complete loss of fertilizatipn ability sperm inability in IVF. However the infertility of Zfy DKO males could be overcome by injection of round spermatids or sperm (ROSI and ICSI, respectively) directly into the oocytes [20, 21].

To determine which spermiogenesis-related pathways are regulated by ZFY, we recently performed bulk transcriptome analysis on primary spermatocytes, secondary spermatocytes, and round spermatids isolated from Zfy1 KO, Zfy2 KO, and Zfy DKO males [21]. Although only minimal transcriptional changes were observed with Zfy1 KO males, hundreds of differentially expressed genes were observed for all cell types with Zfy2 KO and Zfy DKO males. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed that spermatocytes exhibited upregulation of apoptotic pathways, whereas round spermatids exhibited downregulation of microtubule-based pathways and upregulation of chromatin pathways. Additionally, we found that alternative splicing was deregulated in Zfy KO males affecting sperm function and chromatin regulation pathways. The transcriptome data confirmed that ZFY is a potent regulator controlling different pathways at each stage of spermatogenesis.

Phenotypic characterization of Zfy mutant, transgene rescue, and KO males, as well as transcriptome studies of germ cells from Zfy KO males, revealed functional differences between Zfy1 and Zfy2. Transgenic addition of Zfy2 had a stronger effect than addition of Zfy1 [15], and a loss of endogenous Zfy2 led to stronger spermiogenic impairment and transcriptional changes than loss of Zfy1 [20, 21]. Such differences in function may be attributed to the genomic structure of Zfy. Both Zfy1 and Zfy2 are alternatively spliced, with the long “active” variant predominating for Zfy2 and the short “inactive” variant predominating for Zfy1 [5]. Furthermore, Zfy2 has a strong spermatid-specific promoter which drives high expression in post-meiotic cells [5]. To elucidate the functional differences between ZFY1 and ZFY2 using biochemical assays a homologue-specific protein detection and binding are required. This presents a technical challenge due to the high similarity of predicted protein sequences of ZFY1 and ZFY2 [5] (Fig. 1).

Here, we report on creation of tagged Zfy1 knock-in (XYZfy1−HA), Zfy2 knock-in (XYZfy2−FLAG, XYZfy2–3xFLAG, and XYZfy2−HA), and Zfy1/2 double knock-in (XYZfy1−HA,Zfy2−MYC) mice. The knock-in mice were phenotypically normal with no noticeable changes in fertility or spermatogenesis. Using Western blot and immunofluorescence and XYZfy1−HA and XYZfy2−HA testes, we detected and characterized, for the first time, ZFY1 and ZFY2 expression in germ cells. The Zfy knock-in mice can now be used to study the mechanism by which ZFY controls spermatogenesis.

Methods

Animals

The mice in this study were used for the following purposes: breeding, as sperm and oocyte donors for assisted reproduction, as vasectomized males and surrogate/foster females for embryo transfer, and for analyses. Two bred in-house strains were used, C57BL/6 and CD-1. The mice of interest in this study are tagged Zfy knock-in (KI) males: XYZfy2−FLAG, XYZfy2–3xFLAG, XYZfy1−HA, XYZfy2−HA, and XYZfy1−HA,Zfy2−MYC, which were produced by CRISPR/Cas9 mediated targeting on C57BL/6 genetic background. All KI colonies were propagated by standard breeding, assessed for fertility and general sperm function, and subsequently used for collection of tissue samples for genotyping and sequencing, Western blot (WB), immunoprecipitation (IP), and immunofluorescence (IF).

Chemicals

All chemicals were obtained from Sigma Chemical Co. (St Louis, MO, USA) unless otherwise stated.

Assisted reproduction

Assisted reproduction was performed as described by us before [22]. Briefly, for in vitro fertilization (IVF) the oocytes were collected from females induced to superovulate with injections of 5 IU eCG and 5 IU hCG given 48 h apart. Epididymal sperm were released from cauda epididymis directly into HTF medium [23] and capacitated for 1.5 h. The gametes were co-incubated for 4 h, washed with HEPES-CZB [24], and cultured in CZB medium [25] at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. The zygotes were subjected to genome editing either fresh or after cryopreservation. Genome-edited embryos were transferred to pseudopregnant CD-1 recipients at the 2-cell stage.

Production of Zfy-tagged knock-in mice

The Zfy knock-in (KI) mice were produced by genome editing using CRISPR/Cas9 system and C57BL/6 zygotes. Each CRISPR RNA (crRNA, Table S1) was annealed with trans-activating CRISPR RNA (tracrRNA). The combinations of crRNA and tracRNA, subsequently called guide RNA (gRNA), were mixed with CAS9 protein and single-stranded oligodeoxynucleotide (ssODN) template sequence to adjust the concentration (1.2 μm of gRNA, 660 nM CAS9 protein, and 4 μm ssODN) in OptiMEM-I. The ssODN template encoded Zfy homologous sequence and the FLAG (DYKDDDDK), 3xFLAG (DYKDHDGDYKDHDIDYKDDDDK), HA (YPYDVPDYA), or MYC (EQKLISEEDL) tags (Table S2). The electroporation was performed as described by us before [22]. After electroporation the zygotes were washed with HEPES-CZB, incubated overnight in CZB medium at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2, and resulting 2-cell embryos were transferred into pseudopregnant surrogate mothers. Male offspring obtained after embryo transfer were screened for successful targeting using PCR and sequencing, and those positive (F0 founders) were bred to generate F1 KI males. The presence of the desired epitope sequence was confirmed in F1 progeny by genotyping and direct sequencing analysis. The F1 and F2 males were used for WB, IP and IF assays.

Genotyping

The presence of the desired epitope sequence was confirmed at the 3’ end of the Zfy1 and Zfy2 open reading frames by PCR using primers shown in Table S3 and Fig. S1. Because of the high Zfy1/2 sequence similarity near the target region, we designed “global” primers that simultaneously amplified Zfy1 and Zfy2. The genotyping strategy allowed for simultaneous visualization of both the wild-type and mutated versions of Zfy1 and Zfy2. DNA was extracted from mouse ear clip by overnight digestion in GNTK buffer (0.5% Tween20, 0.45% Triton-X-100, 50 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.5) with 20 ug/mL Proteinase K at 55 °C. PCR was performed with KOD DNA polymerase, and PCR products were run on 2% agarose gel. Positive, negative, and blank controls were included in every assay.

Off-target analysis

The non-targeted Zfy homologue was screened to ensure there was no undesired off-target mutation, and three additional autosomal off-target loci per Zfy gRNA were predicted by TagScan (https://ccg.epfl.ch/tagger/tagscan.html) (Table S4). The off-target sites were PCR-amplified (primer sequences are shown in Table S3) and directly sequenced.

Sequencing

PCR products were excised from 2% agarose gel and purified with Zymoclean Gel DNA Recovery Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, US). Cycle sequencing was performed using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 kit (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, US). The extension products were purified using an ethanol/EDTA/sodium acetate precipitation and then separated by capillary electrophoresis in a 3730xl DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, US).

Basic sperm analyses

Basic sperm analyses including sperm number, motility and morphology were performed as described by us before [22].

Western blot

Expression of tagged ZFY proteins was measured in whole tissue protein extract from KI male testis, kidney, liver, and lung. Non-targeted XY males on the same genetic background (C57BL/6) were used as a negative control. Tissue samples were homogenized in RIPA buffer (25 mM Tris pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Igepal CA-630, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (1 µL inhibitor per 10 µL RIPA buffer), using 100 µL of RIPA buffer per 10 mg of tissue. Homogenization was performed with a TissueLyser LT (Qiagen, #85600). The homogenates were incubated on ice for 30 min, then centrifuged at 14,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was measured using the Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Fisher Scientific, #Q33239) with the Protein Broad Range assay. Samples were mixed with 4x Laemmli sample buffer (65.8 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.8, 2.1% SDS, 26.3% (w/v) glycerol, 0.01% bromophenol blue) containing β-mercaptoethanol (100 µL per 900 µL of sample buffer) and denatured at 95 °C for 5 min. Proteins were loaded onto a 10% polyacrylamide gel and separated by SDS-PAGE (75 V/30 mA for 2–3 h) in running buffer (25 mM Tris, 200 mM glycine, 1% (w/v) SDS). Protein was loaded per line either as ~ 50 µg (estimated) or 25 µg (measured). Proteins were transferred to a 45 μm nitrocellulose membrane (ThermoFischer, Waltham, MS, US) for 1 h at 100 V in transfer buffer (25 mM Tris, 200 mM glycine, and 20% (v/v) ethanol/methanol). Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature with 5% skim milk prepared either in PBST (1×PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20) or in TBST (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20, pH 7.4). Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4 °C with the HA antibody (monoclonal anti-HA antibody C29F4 produced in rabbit, 1:300 dilution or Sigma-Aldrich, #H6908; 1:1000 dilution) diluted in 5% skim milk prepared in PBST or TBST. After three washes 5 min each in PBST or 5% skim milk in TBST, membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the Peroxidase AffiniPure® Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 111-035-144; 1:1000 or 1:2000 dilution). Membranes were then washed three times for 5 min with either PBST or 5% skim milk in TBST, followed by a final wash with distilled water or TBST. Signals were developed using SuperSignal West Pico Plus® ECL (Thermo Fisher, #34580) and Immobilon ECL Ultra Western HRP Substrate (Millipore, #WBULS0100) and visualized with an ImageQuant™ LAS 4000 imager.

For loading control (GAPDH) detection, membranes were incubated in stripping buffer (100 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 2% SDS, 62.5 mM Tris HCl pH 6.7) for 30 min at room temperature or at 55 °C, with frequent agitation. After two washes in TBST, membranes were re-blocked in 5% skim milk in PBST or TBST for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature or overnight at 4 °C with the GAPDH antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #2118S, monoclonal anti- GAPDH 14C10 produced in rabbit antibody 1:2000 dilution) diluted in 5% skim milk prepared in PBST or TBST.

For quantitative analysis, WB images were saved as 8-bit TIFF files and analyzed using ImageJ [26]. A rectangular selection was used to define each lane, and lane intensity profiles were generated using Analyze > Gels > Plot Lanes to obtain densitometric curves. For each band, the area under the peak was measured using the Wand Tool in ImageJ. The relative percentage of each band was obtained via Analyze > Gels > Label Peaks. Band intensities were first normalized to a reference lane (control group) and subsequently to the loading control (GAPDH) to correct for variations in total protein loading. Statistical analysis was performed on the normalized band intensity values using appropriate tests selected based on the distribution of the data.

Immunoprecipitation

Immunoprecipitation (IP) was performed on XY, XYZfy1−HA, and XYZfy2−HA testis using Pierce™ Anti-HA Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US). Testis samples were homogenized in 8 volumes IP lysis buffer (pH 7.4, 0.025 M Tris, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.001 M EDTA, 1% NP40, 5% glycerol, 1 mM PMSF, 1 mM sodium butyrate, 1x protease inhibitor cocktail, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate, and 25 U/mL benzonase nuclease), incubated on ice for 30 min, and then centrifuged at 14,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain protein lysate. For each sample, 500 µL of the protein lysate was added to the beads (pre-washed twice in IP lysis buffer) and incubated at room temperature for 30 min with constant agitation. The beads were then washed three times with IP lysis buffer and twice with distilled water. Elution was performed in RIPA buffer for 10 min at 95 °C.

Immunofluorescence

Immunohistochemistry on testis sections was performed with minor modifications to the previous report [21]. Testes were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm. HA-tag staining was performed with Alexa Fluor 488 Tyramide SuperBoost Kit and goat anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #B40943) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, including hydrogen peroxide quenching, blocking, and antibody incubations. Blocking and washes were done either with the solutions provided in the kit or blocking was done with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100, 3% BSA and 10% horse serum, and washes were done with PBS with 0.1% Triton X-100, 3% BSA and 1% horse serum. Following deparaffinization and rehydration, and antigen retrieval carried out using Histofine (Nichirei Bioscience, #415211) or 10 mM citrate buffer (121 °C, 20 min autoclave), sections were washed and incubated with hydrogen peroxide solution (from the kit) for 1 h at room temperature. Blocking was then performed for 1 h at room temperature. The primary antibody (HA-Tag, Rabbit, Cell Signaling, 3724) was diluted (1:200 or 1:500) in blocking buffer and applied to the sections. Negative controls were prepared by omitting the primary antibody. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C. After incubation, sections were washed three times and poly-HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (from the kit), diluted in blocking solution, was applied to the sections and incubated for 1 h at room temperature in a humidified chamber. Sections were then washed three times and Tyramide working solution, conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488, was applied for 10 min at room temperature. After 10 min, the reaction stop reagent was applied and sections were washed three times with washing buffer. For multiplex staining, after completing HA-tag staining, sections were re-blocked and additional antibodies and/or lectin diluted in Can Get Signal Immunostain Solution B (TOYOBO, #NKB-601) or 5% BSA in PBS were added. Nuclei were counterstained using Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen, H3570) or DAPI (Vector Laboratories, H-1200-10). Slides were mounted with Vectashield antifade mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, H-1000-10 and H-1200) and imaged by fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX51 (Tokyo, Japan). All antibodies, lectins, and stains are listed in Table S5.

Results

Designing Zfy knock-in strategy

The ZFY predicted protein sequence contains an N-terminal acidic domain with the ability to bind to other proteins and a C-terminal zinc-finger domain with the ability to interact with DNA (Fig. 1A [5]),. Due to the high degree (~ 95%) of similarity between ZFY1 and ZFY2 protein sequences (Fig. 1B-C), the addition of an endogenous tag is a practical approach which can be utilized to allow specific detection of the distinct variants. We elected to target the C-terminal end of ZFY because it was not possible to design a homologue-specific knock-in at the N-terminal region due to sequence similarity.

Generation and characterization of FLAG-tagged Zfy knock-in mice

The ssODN and gRNA sequences were designed to add the FLAG or 3xFLAG sequence to the 3’ end of the Zfy2 coding sequence (Fig. S2A-B, Tables S1 and S2). Successful KI was verified for XYZfy2−FLAG and XYZfy2–3xFLAG males via PCR and direct sequencing analysis (Fig. S2C-H). No mutations were observed at Zfy1 or three autosomal off-target candidate sites in either KI line (Table S4, Fig. S3). XYZfy2−FLAG and XYZfy2–3xFLAG males were fertile, with no deficiencies in sperm number, testis weight, and testis weight/body weight ratio (Table 1). Sperm motility was slightly reduced in XYZfy2–3xFLAG males compared to XY control but still within the normal range of C57BL/6 strain.

We were unable to detect tagged protein expression in XYZfy2−FLAG in whole testis using Western blot and in testis sections using immunofluorescence (data not shown). The expected ZFY2 size is 88 kDa [5]. Weak KI-specific bands at ~ 140 kDa were detected by Western blot in XYZfy2–3xFLAG testis both in adult males and in juvenile males starting from Day 14 of the first wave of spermatogenesis (Fig. S4A-B, File S1), matching the predicted expression pattern of ZFY2 [5]. Knock-in specific signal was detected after immunoprecipitation with XYZfy2–3xFLAG testis, but not with XY and XYZfy2−FLAG testis (Fig. S4C, File S1). The larger-than-expected size of ZFY2-specific band (~ 140 kDa vs. 88 kDa) may be a result of a heavy post-transcriptional modification or could represent ZFY dimers. Also, the weak knock-in specific signal in relation to the non-specific bands raised concern of whether the 3xFLAG epitope sequence was having an adverse effect on ZFY2 protein structure. We were unable to detect ZFY2-3xFLAG expression in testis sections using immunofluorescence (not shown).

Creation of HA-tagged Zfy knock-in mice

Because adding a FLAG tag to ZFY2 resulted in unreliable detection, we attempted to tag ZFY1 and ZFY2 with an HA tag. The ssODN and gRNA sequences were designed to add the HA sequence to the 3’ end of the Zfy1 or Zfy2 exon 9 (Fig. 2A-B, Tables S1 and S2). To reduce potential effect of the epitope on ZFY structure, a glycine-serine (Gly-Ser) sequence was included at the 5’ end of the knock-in sequence (Fig. 2A-B). Successful targeting was confirmed for XYZfy1−HA and XYZfy2−HA males (Fig. 2C-H), and KI males had no off-target mutations (Table S4, Fig. S3). XYZfy1−HA and XYZfy2−HA males were fertile and had normal sperm parameters. Although body weight and testis weight were decreased in XYZfy2−HA males when compared to XY controls they were within the normal range of C57BL/6 strain and testis weight/body weight ratio was not statistically different from XY (Table 1).

Characterization of ZFY expression by Western blot and Immunoprecipitation

Both ZFY1 and ZFY2 are predicted to be expressed during the first wave of spermatogenesis coinciding with the presence of primary spermatocytes (reviewed by [1]). We observed KI-specific bands matching this expression pattern in XYZfy1−HA (~88 kDa + ~140 kDa) and XYZfy2−HA (~ 140 kDa) testis but not in XY testis (Figs. 3A-B and 4A-C, Fig. S5, File S1-2). In adult mice, ZFY1 and ZFY2 expression is expected to be testis-specific (reviewed by [1]); this was affirmed by the presence of knock-in specific bands in XYZfy1−HA and XYZfy2−HA males appearing in testis but not in other tested tissues (Fig. 4D-E, File S1). Because ZFY1 and ZFY2 are both predicted to be ~88 kDa, we suspect that the smaller size band observed in XYZfy1−HA to be ZFY1, and the large band observed in both XYZfy1−HA and in XYZfy2−HA males to be either ZFY dimer or to reflect heavy post-transcriptional modifications. When examining the first spermatogenesis wave KI-specific bands were absent in D7 testes where only spermatogonia are present and were seen from D14 testes onward, coinciding with primary spermatocyte appearance (Fig. 4A-C, File S1). To look with more scrutiny at the timing of expression initiation, we examined testes from D10 and D12 males, expected to contain leptotene and zygotene spermatocytes, respectively (Fig. S6, File S1-2). ZFY1-HA band appeared faintly in D10 and strongly in D12 testis, indicating that expression starts in leptotene-to-zygotene transition and is strong in early zygotene spermatocytes (Fig. S6A-B, File S1-2). ZFY2-HA band was absent D10 and D12 testes and could be seen, faintly, in D14 testes (Fig. S6C-D, File S1-2). Because ZFY is not expected to be expressed in pachytene due to MSCI, the band seen in D14 testes likely represented expression by late zygotene spermatocytes. Immunoprecipitation (IP) was performed on XYZfy1−HA and XYZfy2−HA testis, with XY males of the same genetic background serving as a negative control. Enrichment was observed for the KI-specific bands detected via WB for XYZfy1−HA (~88 kDa and ~140 kDa) and XYZfy2−HA (~140 kDa) testis (Fig. 5, File S1). Additional specific bands (~65 and ~100 kDa) were present after IP with XYZfy2−HA but not with XYZfy1−HA or XY males. Some non-specific bands (~30 and ~40 kDa) were seen in the immunoprecipitates from all males, with ~40 kDa band appearing the weakest in XY, stronger in XYZfy1−HA, and the strongest in XYZfy2−HA. We suspect the ~ 140 kDa band to be a ZFY-dimer or ZFY with posttranslational modifications, the ~40 kDa band to be degraded ZFY motifs, and the ~100 kDa band to reflect partially degraded ZFY dimer or ZFY2 with posttranslational modifications. The XYZfy2−HA-specific ~65 kDa band may also be ZFY2 without the alternatively spliced region of exon 4 (Fig. 1A) as the expected size for the short and large ZFY2 splice variants are~65 kDa and ~88 kDa, respectively (reviewed by [1]).

Characterization of ZFY expression by Immunofluorescence

ZFY1 and ZFY2 protein expression was not directly characterized before. Zfy transcripts were found to be expressed in spermatocytes during the leptotene and the zygotene stages of meiotic prophase, then silenced during the pachytene phase with the onset of Meiotic Sex Chromosome Inactivation (MSCI), and expressed again post-meiotically in spermatids [5] (reviewed in [1]). Zfy1 and Zfy2 transcripts were also found in secondary spermatocytes [15].

Here, we observed ZFY1 and ZFY2 expression in zygotene spermatocytes, and ZFY2 expression in spermatids step 7–8 and 9 (Figs. 6, 7 and 8 and Fig. S7-S10). The analysis of ZFY expression in testes from the first wave of spermatogenesis (Fig. S8) confirmed lack of expression in D7 testes, supporting that ZFY is not expressed in spermatogonia. ZFY1 and ZFY2 expression was detected in D14 testes, and continued to be observed in D21, D28 and D35 testes with a pattern of expression matching what is seen in adult testes, i.e. with both ZFY1 and ZFY2 expressed in spermatocytes and ZFY2 additionally expressed post-meiotically in late round (step 7–8) and early elongating (step 9) spermatids. The ZFY2 expression in spermatids was weaker than in spermatocytes. The germ cells expressing ZFY1 and ZFY2 located near basal lamina were identified as leptotene and/or zygotene spermatocytes based on tubule staging (Fig. 6, Fig. S7). To confirm the identity of these cells, co-staining was performed with an antibody binding a synaptonemal complex protein 3 (SYCP3), which in mouse testis detects all spermatocytes. ZFY1 and ZFY2 signals co-localized with SYCP3 in zygotene spermatocytes but not in pachytene or diplotene spermatocytes (Fig. 7A and S9A). In premeiotic cells ZFY1 and ZFY2 signals were recorded in tubules stage XII-I, and residual signals were also seen as stage I transitioned to stage II (Fig. 7A and S9A). ZFY1, but not ZFY2 signals, were also seen in tubule stages X-XI which contain both leptotene and zygotene spermatocytes.

Co-staining was also done with an antibody binding DMC1, a protein involved in homologous recombination during meiosis which in mouse testis detects exclusively leptotene and zygotene spermatocytes. A direct comparison of the same tubule stage (stage I) and four different males per genotype confirmed that ZFY2 expression is stronger than that of ZFY1 (Fig. 8A). The analysis of images acquired with a high-magnification revealed that ZFY1 and ZFY2 signals in spermatocytes, and ZFY2 in spermatids, were nuclear and overlapped with DAPI staining in these cells (Fig. 8B and S10).

Collectively, immunofluorescence analyses support that ZFY1 and ZFY2 expression in meiotic cells is confined to zygotene spermatocytes but ZFY1 expression commences earlier, in early zygotene and possibly already during leptotene-to-zygotene transition. ZFY2 expression in spermatocytes is stronger than ZFY1 and is also seen in post-meiotic round and early elongating spermatids. Both ZFY proteins are expressed in the nuclei.

Creation and characterization of Zfy double knock-in mice

To be able to detect ZFY1 and ZFY2 expression simultaneously in the same testis, Zfy1/2 double knock-in (DKI) mice were generated using the same CRISPR/Cas9 targeting strategy as used for single KOs. A ssODN sequence was designed to add the MYC sequence to the 3’ end of Zfy2 exon 9 (Fig. S11A, Table S2). Targeting was performed on zygotes obtained from XYZfy1−HA males and oocytes of wild-type females, using the same crRNA as for XYZfy2−HA targeting (Table S1) and the correct DKI sequence was confirmed in F0 and F1 offspring for both Zfy1 and Zfy2 via genotyping and direct sequencing analysis (Fig. S11B-D). The DKI males were fertile with normal sperm parameters (Table 1) and no mutations were detected at six autosomal off-target candidate sequences corresponding to either gRNA-Zfy1 or gRNA-Zfy2 (Table S4, Fig. S3). Western blot performed to detect ZFY1-HA in XYZfy1−HA,Zfy2−MYC yielded results similar to those obtained with XYZfy1−HA males, with detection of two specific bands, ~ 88 kDa and ~ 140 kDa in XYZfy1−HA,Zfy2−MYC and not XY testis (Fig. S12A). Western blot detection of ZFY2-MYC yielded no specific bands (Fig. S12B). Immunofluorescence detection of ZFY1-HA in XYZfy1−HA,Zfy2−MYC yielded results matching those obtained from XYZfy1−HA males, with ZFY1 expression seen in leptotene and zygotene spermatocytes (Fig. S12C). Immunofluorescence detection of ZFY2-MYC in XYZfy1−HA,Zfy2−MYC was not successful.

Discussion

To investigate the biochemical function of ZFY, we created five tagged Zfy knock-in models: XYZfy2-FLAG, XYZfy2–3xFLAG, XYZfy1-HA, XYZfy2-HA, and XYZfy1-HA,Zfy2-MYC. Of these models, XYZfy1-HA and XYZfy2-HA males allowed us to confidently visualize ZFY1 and ZFY2 expression using immunoblotting and immunofluorescence assays. Tagged ZFY protein expression matched the expression patterns of previously reported Zfy transcript data [21, 27–33] but additionally revealed subtle stage-specific differences between ZFY1 and ZFY2 expression.

The production of tagged Zfy knock-in mice is the first step to identifying the role of ZFY in male reproduction. Our previous KO study revealed the Zfy genes are indispensable for normal spermatogenesis, with loss of both homologues resulting in abnormal spermatogenesis, severe sperm defects, and infertility [20]. Our follow-up large-scale transcriptomics analyses revealed that chromatin, apoptosis, and spermatogenesis gene sets are deregulated in Zfy KO germ cells, suggesting that the putative ZFY transcription factor regulates these pathways and direct tests on Zfy KO sperm and testis cross sections confirmed RNA-seq findings. [21]. However, loss of function studies alone are insufficient to determine the biochemical mechanism of a transcriptional factor. The knock-in mice described in this study can be used to directly study the molecular role of ZFY in spermatogenesis by confirming that ZFY is a transcription factor and by identifying ZFY transcriptional targets and co-regulators. The results of this study also add to existing knowledge regarding targeting Y chromosome and variable utility of protein tags.

Western blot analysis on testis reveals that tagged ZFY protein levels mirror Zfy transcript expression

The spermatogenic germ cells can be divided into three general cell types: pre-meiotic spermatogonia, meiotic spermatocytes, and post-meiotic spermatids. These cell types appear in consecutive order during the first spermatogenesis wave, in neonatal and immature males, as follows: Day 7 (spermatogonia), Day 10 (leptotene spermatocytes, Day 12 (zygotene spermatocytes), Day 14 (pachytene spermatocytes), Day 21 (round spermatids), Day 28 (early elongating spermatids) and Day 35 (late elongating spermatids). The Western blot analysis revealed that both ZFY1 and ZFY2 proteins are expressed during the first wave of spermatogenesis. ZFY-specific bands were not observed in Day 7 testes and appeared prominently from Day 12 onward, supporting that ZFY is not expressed in spermatogonia and the onset of expression coincides with appearance of zygotene spermatocytes. The ZFY1 band was faintly present already in Day 10 and strongly in Day 12 testis while ZFY2 could be first seen, faintly, in Day 14 testis; thus the onset of ZFY1 expression precedes that of ZFY2.

This is consistent with transcript expression analyses reporting peak Zfy expression in spermatocytes but not spermatogonia [21, 27–33] (Fig. 9A-G). In adult males we observed testis-specific expression of tagged ZFY protein, which is consistent with published RNA-seq data that consistently identified Zfy transcript in germ cells and not in somatic testicular cells or other tissues [21, 27–33]. Collectively, Western blot data provide evidence that ZFY protein is detectable in the same tissue and spermatogenic cell types as Zfy transcripts.

Immunofluorescence analysis on testicular cross sections shows spatial and quantitative differences between ZFY1 and ZFY2 expression

The use of Western blotting to detect ZFY expression in subtypes of primary spermatogenic cells, such as spermatids at different developmental steps is not feasible due to the difficulty to isolate specific cell types. Immunofluorescence analysis of testis sections, combined with seminiferous epithelium staging and the use of spermatogenic cell markers, is a favorable alternative due to the higher resolution it provides and the possibility of detecting expression in individual specific germ cells. Previously published RT-PCR [5], RNA-FISH [15], and transcriptome [21, 27–33] data indicate that Zfy1 and Zfy2 transcripts are expressed in early meiotic cells, undergo silencing in pachytene spermatocytes due to MSCI, are reactivated in secondary spermatocytes, and are expressed in round spermatids. Figure Fig. 9A-G provides an overview of Zfy transcript expression across several published RNA-seq datasets. These datasets largely show similar expression patterns, with some differences resulting from the possible variability of cells types included in clusters or in precision of the cut-off between clusters. For example, low ZFY expression in pooled primary spermatocyte cluster (Fig. 9D&E) can be explained by the predominance of pachytene spermatocytes that do not express Zfy over leptotene and zygotene spermatocytes that express Zfy. Cumulatively, transcriptome analyses show that meiotic Zfy transcript expression peaks in pre-leptotene (Fig. 9A&F), leptotene (Fig. 9C&G) or leptotene/zygotene (Fig. 9B) primary spermatocytes while post-meiotic Zfy transcript expression peaks in mid-to-late round spermatids (Fig. 9A-B, E, F, G) or elongated spermatids (Fig. 9C-E). One of the goals for developing mice with tagged Zfy was to define spatial, temporal, and quantitative differences in protein expression between ZFY1 and ZFY2. Our immunofluorescence analysis revealed that ZFY1 and ZFY2 are expressed in zygotene spermatocytes but not in spermatogonia or pachytene spermatocytes. We also detected ZFY2 expression in round (step 7–8) and early elongating (step 9) spermatids while ZFY1 expression was notably absent in these post-meiotic cells. Our immunofluorescence analysis of ZFY expression led to three specific observations. Firstly, that ZFY2 is expressed more highly than ZFY1 throughout spermatogenesis. This finding appears consistent with the transcriptome data, as the majority of RNA-seq datasets show that Zfy2 transcript expression is higher than Zfy1 (Fig. 9A-G). Secondly, overall ZFY expression is higher in meiotic cells than in post-meiotic cells. The ZFY2-HA signal observed in spermatocytes was significantly brighter than that seen in round spermatids, and ZFY1-HA signal was not seen in post-meiotic cells at all, likely due to expression level falling below the threshold of detection. Higher meiotic expression, when compared to post-meiotic expression, is consistent with the transcriptome data (Fig. 9A, B, F, G). Our third observation is that the onset of ZFY1 expression precedes that of ZFY2 expression. In immunofluorescence analyses, ZFY1 was seen tubules stage X-XI where both leptotene and zygotene spermatocytes appear, respectively, supporting commencement of expression in early zygotene or leptotene-to-zygotene transition. ZFY2, however, was only seen tubules XII-I, where mid- and late-zygotene spermatocytes are present. In Western blot analyses ZFY1 was only faintly seen in leptotene spermatocytes in Day 10 testis and presented strongly in zygotene spermatocytes in D12 testes, while ZFY2 was not observed in Day 10 or 12 testes at all. A similar trend was observed with transcripts in one of the published studies [19], with a higher abundance of the Zfy1 transcript in pre-leptotene spermatocytes (Fig. 9B). Ongoing investigations for mapping of selected genes expression across individual spermatogenic cells and stages using previously reported scRNA-seq dataset [28] also indicate that Zfy1 transcript expression precedes that of Zfy2 (Hammoud, personal communication).

Transcript levels are not a perfect proxy for protein levels [34] and spatial and quantitative differences between transcript and protein expression are expected. Future analyses employing high-resolution confocal microscopy and mass spectrometry on sorted male germ cells are needed to clarify the differences in expression level differences between meiotic and post-meiotic cells and ZFY1 and ZFY2.

ZFY protein may form dimers or undergo heavy post-transcriptional modifications or be present as splicing variants

For ZFY1-HA, Western blot analysis showed a knock-in specific band around ~88 kDa, corresponding to the predicted ZFY1 protein sequence ([5], reviewed by [1]), with an additional band observed at ~140 kDa. Interestingly, this larger band was the only band present for ZFY2-HA; there are several possible explanations for this phenomenon. Many zinc finger transcription factors can only bind to DNA as a dimer [35]. Even though the Western blot analysis was run under denatured conditions, if ZFY primarily exists in the mouse testis as a dimer, the larger-than-expected band size could be a result of incompletely denatured ZFY-ZFY dimers. However, our previous in-silico modeling failed to produce ZFY dimer models with high degree of confidence [21]. Alternatively, it is possible that the large band is the result of a heavy post-transcriptional modification or a protein complex. Further tests including immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry are required to confirm the identity of these specific bands.

Validation of endogenously tagged Y chromosome mouse genes was previously reported to be problematic due to unanticipated splicing variants. When the FLAG epitope was added to another Y chromosome gene, Sry, using a targeting strategy like the one described by us, it failed to detect FLAG expression in knock-in mouse testis [36]. It has subsequently been shown that Sry encodes a previously unknown second exon and transcript variant, and a 3xFLAG tag was successfully added to this more stable Sry variant and tagged protein expression was confirmed [37]. Thus, the first attempt to add FLAG likely failed due to tagging of an unstable SRY isoform [36]. Similar to Sry, unknown Zfy splicing variants could account for the discrepancy in observed protein size or difficulty with validating expression. If we were targeting an unstable ZFY isoform, the addition of epitope sequences could more easily destabilize protein structure, which could help explain why FLAG and MYC tags are detrimental to ZFY tertiary/quaternary structure.

Efficiency of HA vs. FLAG and MYC epitopes

We failed to detect ZFY2-FLAG protein in homogenized testis samples, and only weak knock-in specific bands were observed for ZFY2-3xFLAG and ZFY2-MYC at ~ 140 kDa. In contrast, Western blot analysis revealed strong knock-in specific bands for ZFY1-HA at ~88kA and ~140 kDA and for ZFY2-HA at ~140 kDA. There are a couple of explanations as to why we could more easily see ZFY expression with the HA epitope. While the FLAG tag was added directly to the Zfy2 coding sequence, a glycine-serine linker sequence was added adjacent to the HA tag, with the goal of minimizing the epitope effect on protein structure. It is possible that without this linker sequence, ZFY protein structure was compromised by the addition of a FLAG tag. However, the MYC tag knock-in sequence, like the HA knock-in, contained the glycine-serine linker sequence, but only faint tagged protein expression was observed for the MYC tag. Thus, the FLAG/MYC tags may be detrimental to ZFY structure regardless of the presence of a linker sequence. Alternatively, ZFY expression could be less visible using the FLAG tag because this epitope has more non-specific interactions in the mouse testis than the HA tag, as evidenced by the strong non-specific bands observed for FLAG immunoblotting. If higher-affinity antibodies against FLAG become available, detection of ZFY carrying this tag may become feasible.

Applications for future in vivo testicular studies

Adding endogenous epitope sequences to murine testicular proteins is a practical approach to determine the biochemical pathways responsible for spermatogenesis for numerous reasons. Firstly, it is often difficult to produce antibodies suitable for assays such as mass spectrometry or chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP). Furthermore, mammalian testicular organoid models have many limitations, and their physiology does not truly resemble that of the in vivo testis [38]. Here, we characterized the expression of two critical male fertility factors, ZFY1 and ZFY2, and developed tools which can further elucidate ZFY function. The strategy described in this study can easily be applied to other Y chromosome genes such as Uty, which is also needed for normal spermatogenesis but has an unknown biochemical function [19], or to ubiquitously expressed genes belonging to X-Y gene pairs, like Eif2s3y and Eif2s3x, to pinpoint the functional differences between the homologues.

Conclusions

Using CRISPR/Cas9 we developed five new mouse models, mice with tagged Y chromosome Zfy1 and Zfy2 genes and provide the first direct characterization of ZFY proteins expression in mouse testis. We demonstrated that ZFY proteins are specifically expressed in zygote spermatocytes and in the late round and early elongating spermatids. There is an abundance of evidence that ZFY is a critical male fertility factors and a regulator of various aspects of spermatogenesis, yet the understanding on how it fulfills these roles remains limited. These novel mouse models can now be used for the investigations of molecular function of mouse ZFY.
