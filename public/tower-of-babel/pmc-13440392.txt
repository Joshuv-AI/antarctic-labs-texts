Failed metabolic adaptation to stress contributes to epidermal cell adhesion defects in Darier disease
Authors: Robert M. Harmon, Erin F. McCarthy, Annette Krol, Aashutosh Shetti, Jonas Braun, Rosemary J. DiDominicis, Lisa Godsel, Ziyou Ren, Bethany E. Perez White, Eran Cohen-Barak, Roni Dodiuk-Gad, Johann E. Gudjonsson, Amy S. Paller, Cory L. Simpson, Samuel Weinberg, Russell Dahl, Yogesh Goyal, Murali Prakriya, Kathleen J. Green
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenital pathogenic gene variants may not elicit symptoms until later in life, highlighting the importance of identifying extra-genetic factors influencing the onset and severity of heritable diseases. We explored this in Darier disease (DD or ATP2A2-nEDD), an autosomal dominant skin disorder arising from heterozygous ATP2A2 variants leading to haploinsufficiency of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) calcium pump, SERCA2. Metabolic analysis of epidermal keratinocytes from confirmed patients with DD revealed abnormalities in the pentose phosphate pathway responsible for regenerating antioxidants like glutathione, accompanied by diminished free glutathione and increased glutathione-based, oxidative modifications of SERCA2. Induction of oxidative stress weakened intercellular adhesion, a defining characteristic of DD, which antioxidant treatment improved. Treatment with antioxidants or SERCA activators also diminished glutathionylation, consistent with SERCA2 haploinsufficiency giving rise to oxidative stress and placing residual SERCA2 at risk of oxidation. We posit that partial loss of protein activity primes cells for stress-induced loss of the remaining activity, a mechanism that may drive disease flares in other haploinsufficiencies.

INTRODUCTION

Haploinsufficiencies, in which reducing the amount or activity of a given protein results in phenotypic defects that may appear years after birth, typically arise from heterozygous gene variants. Vulnerability of the remaining functional protein to extra-genetic inactivation may be key to understanding why only a subset of genes manifest phenotypic variability due to haploinsufficiency (1). To explore this possibility, we take advantage of a genetic skin disorder, Darier disease (DD), recently renamed ATP2A2-nEDD (2), linked to heterozygous variants in the gene encoding the sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+–adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase; SERCA2), ATP2A2 (3). Although expressed in many tissues, genetic perturbation of human SERCA2 causes a primarily dermatologic disorder. Existing studies also indicate a role for attenuated SERCA2 function in cardiac, metabolic, and neuronal conditions with more complex etiologies (4). Nonetheless, the intermediates connecting pump deficiency to an array of disease end points remain unclear (5–8).

DD belongs to a group of skin disorders in which keratinocytes of the epidermis lose adhesion to one another, called acantholysis, leading to skin blistering and susceptibility to infection (9, 10). DD and the related disorder, Hailey-Hailey disease, linked to pathogenic variants in another calcium ATPase, ATP2C1, both lack proven targeted therapeutics (11). The heterozygous ATP2A2 variants linked to DD result in either reduced SERCA2 expression or enzymatic activity, suggestive of haploinsufficiency as the primary pathogenic mechanism (12). Historically, two schools of thought have emerged to explain the disruption of intercellular adhesion in DD, particularly that mediated by desmosomes, which consist of transmembrane cadherins linking adjacent cells to the keratin cytoskeleton through the cytolinker, desmoplakin (DP). One perspective focuses on the perturbation of cytosolic calcium interfering with DP-keratin interactions (13), and the other on diminished lumenal ER calcium stores, resulting in impaired protein folding and ER stress (14, 15). While efforts to target the disorder therapeutically by normalizing ER calcium or indirectly through intermediary pathways linking SERCA2 deficiency to skin deterioration have been reported (16–18), they have yet to reach widespread use in the clinic. Likewise, efforts to curb disease severity with immunomodulatory biologic therapies offer promise but elicit inconsistent responses between patients and often diminish in efficacy over time (19–22).

Patients born with heterozygous ATP2A2 mutations do not exhibit skin defects at birth. Most develop acantholytic lesions during the second or third decade of life and within specific anatomical sites, most of which have higher concentrations of sebaceous glands (scalp, face, neck, and upper trunk). Flares can occur in response to stressors including heat, ultraviolet irradiation, psychological stress, and cutaneous super-infection (10, 23, 24). These observations suggest that haploinsufficiency sensitizes intercellular junctions to the damaging effects of exogenous stressors.

Here, we show that DD keratinocyte junctions exhibit heightened sensitivity to oxidative stress, with disruption of cell adhesion correlating with an inability to dampen reactive oxygen species (ROS) in response to a chemical oxidative stressor. Metabolite analysis of DD cells revealed perturbation of the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), a metabolic pathway parallel to glycolysis that produces the reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) necessary to regenerate the antioxidant activity of glutathione (GSH) (25, 26). Accordingly, DD cells had less free GSH and increased transfer of GSH to protective GSH-based modifications of SERCA2. Treatment with antioxidants or SERCA activators diminished SERCA2 glutathionylation, consistent with oxidative stress arising from SERCA2 haploinsufficiency driving residual SERCA2, itself, towards oxidation. We propose a model whereby SERCA2 deficiency or dysfunction causes PPP perturbation and oxidative stress, initially buffered by the process of glutathionylation, in which GSH binds to and protects proteins from irreversible oxidation. However, under exogenous oxidative stress, the already-depleted GSH store is shunted toward an abundant perinuclear pool of DP and keratin, leaving SERCA2 vulnerable to oxidative damage. We suggest that the delay in onset of the phenotype reflects the aging-dependent accumulation of oxidative stress or waning of antioxidant capacity (27, 28), with flares reflecting episodes of increased oxidative stress and damage responses.

Our study provides a key conceptual advance in understanding delayed onset and flare pathogenesis in a genetic disorder that could apply to other haploinsufficiencies: Latent phenotypes emerge through conditions that more closely approximate homozygous loss-of-function. In the case of DD, we hypothesize that the partial reduction in SERCA2 activity linked to ATP2A2 heterozygosity renders the residual wildtype protein prone to oxidation and inactivation. The onset of DD cutaneous flares, generally in early adulthood, would equate to intermittent stress–induced unmasking of null-like behavior in haploinsufficient cells.

RESULTS

Modeling DD with cultured patient keratinocytes

Existing animal models of SERCA2 heterozygosity fail to mimic the pathognomonic epidermal acantholysis seen in DD (29). Thus, to investigate the effect of SERCA2 haploinsufficiency on human keratinocytes, we used an in vitro model of cultured keratinocytes isolated from trunk skin of genetically characterized patients with DD (DD1-4) or from trunk skin biopsies (CTL1-2) or abdominoplasty tissue from a healthy adult (CTL3) (see Materials and Methods for donor information). Although the structural implications of the specific mutations represented in these isolates are unknown, both reside in the actuator domain responsible for transducing ATP hydrolysis into conformational changes required for calcium transport (Fig. 1A) (30). Western blotting demonstrated that patient cells expressed on average ∼70% of the SERCA2 produced by control cells via densitometry, indicating that lost protein expression only partially accounted for subsequently observed defects (Fig. 1, B and C). This modest effect on expression is consistent with observations in heterozygous null animals, which continue to produce ∼75% of the SERCA2 protein levels observed in wild-type controls (29). Calcium imaging using Fura-2 in the DD2 isolate indicated a roughly two-fold increase in baseline cytoplasmic calcium levels, aligning well with a 50% reduction in SERCA2-dependent ER calcium sequestration observed in prior studies (fig. S1, A and B) (16). Switching cells to medium containing 2 mM CaCl2 following selective inhibition of SERCA2 ATPase function by thapsigargin (TG) typically elicits a large spike in Fura-2 signals arising from store-operated calcium entry (SOCE) (31). Of note, SOCE was elevated in the DD2 cells compared to controls, perhaps implying that DD cells constitutively fail to support ER calcium sequestration and, thus, constantly activate SOCE channels (fig. S1C). The reduced SERCA2 expression and impaired calcium homeostasis supported further use of patient cells in vitro as a model for SERCA2-dependent pathology.

To study cell-cell junction maturation, intercellular adhesion was induced by raising the medium [Ca2+] from 0.07 to 1.2 mM (32). Compared to controls, DD keratinocytes showed diminished accumulation of DP, a key component of desmosomes that mechanically link adjacent cells, at nascent adhesions 1 hour after initiating cell junction assembly (Fig. 1D). This defect was readily detected by our custom pipeline incorporating Cellpose-based image analyses designed to assess the extent to which DP localizes to adhesive interfaces between cells (Fig. 1E, fig. S2, and Materials and Methods) (33, 34). DP organization normalized in DD cells with time, suggesting impairment but not full blockade of desmosome assembly. After 4 hours of junction assembly, control and DD keratinocytes exhibited comparable DP localization (Fig. 1, D and E). This suggested that flares of acantholytic skin lesions are likely precipitated by external factors that exceed the compensatory capacity of heterozygous keratinocytes.

As DD arises in heterozygous individuals, residual SERCA2 and downstream adhesion maturation should respond positively to addition of SERCA activators. The SERCA ATPase activator, CDN1163, developed by Kang et al. (6) completely reversed DP accumulation defects (Fig. 2, A and B, and fig. S2). Functional assessment of intercellular junction strength in monolayers using a mechanical disruption assay (35) corroborated both the reduced adhesive strength of DD keratinocyte cell sheets and the beneficial impact of SERCA activators like the next generation compound, NDC-9009 (36, 37) (Fig. 2, C and D). These data indicated that desmosome assembly was directly and reversibly affected by loss of SERCA2 activity in our model, rather than other cellular damage inflicted by long-term calcium dysregulation.

Still, these adhesion defects do not manifest overtly in vivo often until late in the second decade of life. To model this discrepancy in vitro, two factors were considered. First, patients with DD can experience cutaneous flares in response to environmental factors like sunlight, heat, and stress, most of which are also known to elicit oxidative stress (38–41). Second, SERCA2 influences cellular redox state and is itself a target of oxidative modifications (42). Many proteins, including SERCA2, undergo a process called glutathionylation in which the free form of the antioxidant GSH is attached to reactive cysteines in the presence of oxidative stress (43, 44). This form of reversible oxidation is thought to protect SERCA2 from irreversible oxidation and inactivation, with evidence suggesting it may modestly activate the enzyme (44). We hypothesized that SERCA2 haploinsufficiency elicited modest oxidative stress but that the process of glutathionylation prevented SERCA2 activity from diminishing below a threshold capable of inducing adhesion defects. To test this idea, proximity ligation assays (PLAs) were conducted to identify sites where glutathionylation and SERCA2 antigens are tightly juxtaposed. Under baseline conditions, all four DD keratinocyte isolates demonstrated elevated levels of SERCA2-proximal glutathionylation compared to controls (Fig. 2, E and F). Treatment with the SERCA activator, NDC-9009, reversed this phenotype, suggesting that SERCA2 haploinsufficiency caused enough oxidative stress to initiate SERCA2 glutathionylation as an adaptive response. To the extent that glutathionylation marks cysteine oxidation, these data indicate that SERCA2 in DD cells exists closer to a threshold of oxidation beyond which the activity of individual molecules would be fully ablated.

DD keratinocyte adhesion is hypersensitive to oxidative stress due to antioxidant dysfunction

We hypothesized that application of a chemical stressor would exacerbate the observed defect in cell-cell junction assembly and better recapitulate a fully acantholytic phenotype. To elicit oxidative stress, we treated cells with menadione, which drives overproduction of mitochondrial ROS (45). In our 2D model system, control cells exposed to menadione for 4 hours established robust desmosomal plaques. DD cells, however, exhibited the majority of DP localized around the nucleus, far from its proper localization at cell-cell junctions. (Fig. 3, A and B). By immunoblotting, DP protein levels varied between experiments for each DD isolate at the 4-hour time point (fig. S3). On average DD1 and DD3 expressed the same amount of DP as controls, whereas DD2 and DD4 expressed higher baseline levels (roughly 1.6 and twofold, respectively). Treatment with menadione tended to diminish DP in DD1, DD2, and DD4, although no changes were found to be statistically significant. Levels of Keratin-14 (K14), the cytoskeletal component of basal keratinocytes to which DP links desmosomal adhesions, were similar in all conditions, including with menadione treatments. α-Catenin, which links adherens junctions (AJs) to the actin cytoskeleton, and thus serves a similar role to DP in desmosomes, on average was expressed within ±25% of the control in all DD isolates and did not significantly change in response to menadione. Stability of these structural components was also unchanged when assessed after a cycloheximide pulse-chase (fig. S3).

To determine which aspect of redox control is altered in DD, we measured ROS levels using a CellRox assay and noted no difference in ROS levels between the disease and control cells at baseline. Upon addition of menadione, DD cells failed to dampen the resultant increase in ROS as efficiently as controls (Fig. 3C). Of note, the phenotype was reversible upon addition of the SERCA activator, CDN1163. This result serves as confirmation that SERCA2 deficiency disrupts the capacity of cells to reduce ROS in keratinocytes. Further, these data indicate a possible perturbation of the core metabolic pathways and/or antioxidant capacity of DD keratinocytes.

DD cells display PPP and metabolic stress response abnormalities

To investigate how SERCA2 mutations impact the metabolic response of keratinocytes to stress, we conducted untargeted metabolite profiling of control and DD cells with and without menadione treatment. Comparison of controls to DD cells in the absence of menadione revealed 29 metabolites that differed significantly (P < 0.05) and an additional 16 with notable trends that did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4A). Pathway enrichment analysis indicated that intermediates of the PPP and GSH metabolism were among those most significantly altered in DD keratinocytes (Fig. 4B), driven by diminished levels of 6-phosphogluconic acid (6-PG), allocystathionine, GSH, glutamate and a ribose species referred to hereafter as R5P (ribose-1-phosphate, ribose 5-phosphate, and ribulose 5-phosphate are indistinguishable by the instrument). The PPP is a pathway, parallel to glycolysis, which facilitates cellular responses to stress by producing nucleotide precursors required for DNA repair and the NADPH needed to replenish the antioxidant activity of GSH (26). Of relevance to the observed sensitivity of DD cells to oxidative stress, the active/reduced form of GSH was observed to be diminished in DD keratinocyte isolates. This baseline deficiency likely contributed to the sensitivity of DD cells to ROS induced by menadione.

In pairwise comparisons, each individual DD isolate clustered distinctly from the control (CTL1) keratinocytes in partial least squares discriminant analyses, demonstrating a consistent deviation of DD metabolite profiles from that of a normal keratinocyte culture (fig. S4A). R5P was the only specific metabolite to be significantly downregulated (P < 0.05) in each of the four DD isolates individually, pointing to the likely importance of PPP insufficiency in DD pathogenesis (fig. S4B). This was further demonstrated by correlation analysis of control and DD responses to oxidative stress. When CTL1 keratinocytes were exposed to menadione, a set of 44 metabolites showed strong positive Pearson correlations (rho = 0.74 ± 0.15; mean ± SD), matched by a negatively correlated group of 27 metabolites (rho = −0.48 ± 0.23 SD). A coordinated loss of glycolytic–tricarboxylic acid cycle products and a surge of PPP metabolites drove these metrics, consistent with prior studies of stress responses in A549 epithelial cells (Fig. 4, C to E, and fig. S4C) (46). These homeostatic metabolic adaptations failed to occur in DD cells (rho = 0.27 ± 0.42 and −0.08 ± 0.31, respectively). Baseline GSH deficiencies of ∼40% were observed and corroborated by a plate reader assay (Fig. 4F and fig. S4D). Upon treatment with menadione, the GSH levels of CTL and DD cells both dropped substantially, although CTL cells retained a statistically significant advantage over DD cultures. Analysis of a previously published culture model of DD (16), using CRISPR-edited, immortalized human keratinocytes carrying a heterozygous-null ablation of SERCA2 (compared to a control line having a pseudogene ablated), a similar baseline deficiency of GSH was observed, as well as diminished levels of the PPP metabolite 6-phosphogluconate (6-PG), validating this metabolic phenotype as a direct effect of SERCA2 targeting (fig. S4, E and F). DD cells, then, likely fail to shunt intermediates from glycolysis to the parallel PPP resulting in diminished capacity for regenerating GSH and an inadequate antioxidant defense (Fig. 4G). We hypothesized that, at baseline, these metabolic abnormalities elicit measurable GSH deficits but do so at levels that only modestly affect cell adhesion, consistent with what would be predicted for the buffering of mild oxidative stress by oxidation of GSH and consumption of PPP intermediates. However, these baseline deficits become problematic when stressors place heightened antioxidant demand upon already weakened homeostatic systems, likely precipitating the full acantholytic phenotype in active DD lesions.

Stressed DD keratinocytes glutathionylate a perinuclear protein pool including DP

The canonical model of GSH cycling indicates that, when oxidized, GSH dimerizes via disulfide bonding to an inactive form, GSSG. This transformation can be reversed via GSH reductase and NADPH supplied by the PPP (47). However, GSH can also be spent in the process of protein glutathionylation. Under oxidative conditions, GSH forms disulfide bonds with reactive cysteines within proteins. We hypothesized that our ROS and GSH measurements in DD cells described above may be consistent with low levels of oxidative stress that are buffered at baseline by transferring free GSH to a glutathionylated pool of protein.

To address this hypothesis, we carried out immunostaining for the GSH adduct, which enables visualization of global glutathionylation, and expanded our analysis to include the cytoskeletal element anchored at desmosomes in basal keratinocytes, K14. In control cells, with and without stress, K14 forms a basket-like structure around the nucleus with filaments extending out to cell junctions, visible both via confocal and electron microscopy (Fig. 5, A and B). As seen previously, DP collapsed around the nucleus in DD cells under stress. Notably, K14 filaments extending radially towards cell junctions appear diminished, with the network collapsing and partially colocalizing with the perinuclear ring of DP (Fig. 5C). In contrast, staining of K14 in the absence of oxidative stress was not appreciably different between DD and control cells (Fig. 5, A and C, fig. S5A).

Electron microscopy, indicated that the aberrant perinuclear accumulation of keratin in stressed DD cells consisted of finer, shortened filament bundles compared to controls (Fig. 5D). Costaining for glutathionylated protein revealed a remarkable accumulation of glutathionylated protein in a shield near the nucleus which co-localized with the collapsed K14 network and perinuclear DP (Fig. 5E). Appearance of an abnormal glutathionylated pool of protein also corresponded with unique biochemical responses within the desmosome-keratin network. Treatment with menadione decreased the solubility of DP in RIPA buffer, particularly in DD cells, commensurate with an increase in a β-mercaptoethanol (BME)-dependent urea-SDS-soluble pool, indicating that the insolubility depended on disulfide bonding (fig. S6, A and B). Likewise, nonreducing gels demonstrated extensive, BME-sensitive laddering of K14 in menadione-treated DD cells indicative of disulfide-driven oligomerization (fig. S6C). The desmosome-keratin complex, then, is redox-sensitive, consistent with prior reports of redox-responsive cysteines in K14 being important for controlling filament elongation (48). The solubility of α-catenin, which tethers AJs to the actin network, mirroring the cytolinker function of DP, did not respond to menadione, arguing that desmosomes are more sensitive to this stressor. Together, the data suggested that these critical structural elements may act as a sink for free GSH in stressed, disease cells, and DP glutathionylation may be a unique and crucial response of DD keratinocytes to oxidative stress.

To more accurately assess the glutathionylation status of DP, we used a proximity ligation assay (PLA). When applied to the four DD keratinocyte isolates, this approach revealed no significant difference in DP glutathionylation between control and DD cells at baseline. However, under oxidative stress induced by menadione, DD keratinocytes exhibited significantly higher levels of DP-proximal glutathionylation (Fig. 5, F and G). Prior work showed that glutathionylation of AJ components, which, like desmosomes, mediate intercellular adhesion, can lead to loss of cell-cell adhesion (43). Of note, the AJ components α- and β-catenin of DD cells did not demonstrate significantly elevated levels of glutathionylation in response to menadione, suggesting that keratinocyte desmosomes may be more responsive to oxidative stress than AJs (Fig. 5G and fig. S7, A and C). Costaining of DP with β-catenin indicated that DD cells treated with menadione are capable of harboring AJ-containing sites of cell-cell adhesion devoid of desmosomes (fig. S8, A and B). This mirrors the effect of the SERCA2 inhibitor, TG, on cultured keratinocytes reported by Hobbs et al. (13). Immunostaining for DP and the AJ component, α-catenin, of tissue from three patients with DD (distinct from those from which cells were isolated in this study), indicated that AJs can occupy desmosome-negative junctions proximal to sites of frank acantholysis, albeit with a somewhat disorganized appearance (figs. S8, C and D, and S9, A to D). These observations point to a particular sensitivity of desmosomes to stress in DD, which may explain its phenotypic focus in the desmosome-abundant skin tissue.

Stress-induced SERCA2 inactivation and loss of cell-cell adhesion occurs via impaired GSH buffering

DD and control tissue were additionally stained for protein glutathionylation. Control skin demonstrated strong staining in the upper layers of the epidermis. Staining in the basal and spinous layers was diffuse and nondistinct. However, in DD tissue an accumulation of glutathionylation signal was present in a perinuclear pattern, particularly in the cells most proximal to acantholytic lacunae (Fig. 6A and fig. S10). The pattern was similar to that observed in menadione-treated DD keratinocytes in culture and is consistent with the hypothesis that acantholysis entails an inability to sufficiently dampen oxidative stress.

To directly link glutathionylation to SERCA2 inactivation, control and DD cells were exposed to the potent SERCA2 inhibitor, TG. Consistent with prior reports, treatment with TG ablated the formation of desmosomes in control keratinocytes (13), with DD cells demonstrating higher sensitivity to the drug (Fig. 6B). Complete inhibition of SERCA2 activity in control cells with TG also elicited a significant increase in global protein glutathionylation, notably similar to that seen in menadione-treated DD cells (Fig. 6C). These data suggest that fully acantholytic DD lesions likely represent regions of more extreme SERCA2 inactivation, with exogenous stressors mimicking the effects of full blockade by TG. If so, treatment with an ectopic antioxidant, such as N-acetylcysteine (NAC), could provide some protection of residual SERCA2. Quantitation of the DP intensity at cell-cell borders and glutathionylation responses, in the presence and absence of NAC, confirmed this prediction (Fig. 6, D and E, and fig. S11).

Next, we tested whether NAC was sufficient to protect DD cell junctions from disruption by menadione treatment. Five millimolar NAC prevented the stress-induced loss of DP from DD cell junctions (Fig. 7, A and B). Likewise, NAC reduced the fragmentation of DD keratinocyte cell sheets subjected to mechanical stress (Fig. 7, C and D). As NAC proved protective both in the presence and absence of menadione, we concluded that oxidative stress is a contributing factor to baseline, modest adhesion defects and that antioxidant treatment may prove beneficial for prophylactically treating lesion-prone areas of DD skin to prevent stress-induced acantholysis. As NAC treatment also blunted the elevation of DP glutathionylation in menadione-treated DD cells, we suggest that protection of this junctional plaque protein is of particular importance to any potential therapeutic intervention (Fig. 7, E and F). Although NAC alone or as an adjuvant to standard treatment has been reported to improve dermatological conditions like ichthyosis and psoriasis, there remain concerns about bioavailability when used orally, suggesting topical application may be more effective (49–51). In addition to testing NAC, which functions as a precursor to GSH synthesis, we tested GSH monoethyl esther (GME), a member of an expanding class of modified GSH compounds designed to improve direct delivery of the antioxidant (52, 53). We found that GME protected DD desmosomes from menadione-induced disruption and prevented the perinuclear accumulation of K14 in stressed DD cells (fig. S12).

Considering our glutathionylation, metabolic, and functional adhesion data, we propose a model of stress hypersensitivity in DD in which SERCA2 haploinsufficiency compromises cellular metabolism to diminish PPP-dependent replenishment of the antioxidant defense system (Fig. 8). The modest oxidative stress that results is readily buffered by the large pool of GSH which remains. However, upon exposure to stress, the homeostatic system in DD cells fails to mount an adequate antioxidant response and the remaining GSH store is redistributed to glutathionylate a large perinuclear protein pool, including DP and the keratin cytoskeleton, rendering less abundant proteins such as SERCA2 unprotected from irreversible oxidation and inactivation. We propose that such a feed-forward mechanism leads to full disintegration of cell junctions, reminiscent of TG treatment, and drives the acantholytic flares characteristic of DD.

DISCUSSION

Like DD, other genetic disorders can remain subclinical until later in life, including haploinsufficiencies in which the mutant allele keeps cells from producing a sufficient amount or activity of its encoded protein products. On the basis of metabolic and cell biological analysis of cells isolated from patients with DD with pathogenic SERCA2 variants, we propose a model of sensitivity to exogenous stressors that progressively ablates the remaining pool of active protein to precipitate frank pathology. This scenario may help explain the delayed onset of symptoms and subsequent flaring of lesions in specific anatomical locations or under certain environmental conditions. Aging generally decreases antioxidant defenses and increases accumulation of oxidative damage, as does exposure to ultraviolet light, a known trigger of DD flares (27, 28, 54, 55). Lesions in DD tend to appear in seborrheic body regions and are associated with an aberrant microbiome, unusually rich in Staphylococcus aureus and depleted of Cutibacterium species (56–58). The androgen sensitivity of the cutaneous microbiome, in particular of S. aureus (59), may also explain the postpubertal onset of DD. In addition to the inflammation and resulting oxidative stress associated with colonization by pathogens, dysbiosis may also disrupt production of antioxidants by microbes like Cutibacteria (60, 61).

SERCA2 deficiency is commonly invoked as contributing to ER stress due to depletion of lumenal calcium, a perturbation that can trigger misfolding of proteins in the ER, with calcium-dependent cadherins being particularly vulnerable. Transcriptomic analysis of SERCA2-depleted keratinocyte lines reveal a cell autonomous elevation of unfolded protein response pathways in line with bulk RNA sequencing data from DD skin biopsies (16, 62). While treating the ER stress component of DD remains a rational approach, especially since trafficking of desmosomal cadherins from the ER is perturbed in DD (63), no such treatment has advanced in clinical trials. ER stress, however, is a complex event that intersects with a vast number of processes, including redox phenomena (64, 65). For example, others have noted the capacity of menadione to elicit release of intracellular calcium stores (66, 67). Understanding these intersections could illuminate the need for combinatorial therapeutic approaches in DD. Those investigating depletion of the Hailey-Hailey disease–linked Golgi pump, ATP2C1, have successfully uses this strategy in linking Golgi stress, ROS, and DNA damage repair defects (68, 69).

The misfolding that occurs when SERCA2 deficiency depletes lumenal ER calcium likely contributes to heightened oxidative stress. Within the ER, improper formation of disulfide bonds elicits a repair response that entails breaking the incorrect bonds and initiating another round of disulfide bonding. The enzymatic reactions that catalyze this unfolding and refolding cycle both produce ROS and consume GSH in the process (70). Prior studies indicate that depletion of ER calcium stores also increases mitochondrial calcium (71). Enzymes involved in driving the citric acid cycle within mitochondria respond robustly to increased calcium levels, ultimately, overloading the electron transport chain and causing increased ROS that can trigger apoptosis, which may explain the aberrant keratinocyte death (dyskeratosis) also seen in DD biopsies (72). Our understanding of ER-mitochondrial contacts, at sites referred to as mitochondria-associated membranes continues to advance, shedding light on a potential route by which ER abnormalities lead to mitochondrial oxidative stress in DD cells (73). This is a particularly intriguing perspective given recent work highlighting contact between the ER and desmosomes (74), raising the possibility that all three organelles may influence each other through physical interactions. To what extent these processes contribute to oxidative stress sensitivity in our system awaits further investigation.

Beyond an earlier study highlighting sphingolipid abnormalities in SERCA2-deficient keratinocytes, few have addressed potential metabolite imbalances in DD (75). On the basis of data reported here, connections between the PPP and glycolysis appear aberrant in DD cells, highlighting the need for future studies to address how calcium defects impinge on these key metabolic pathways. A potential contributor in this regard is the NRF2 pathway, in which disengagement of NRF2 and Keap1 in response to oxidative stress activates a suite of protective gene products, including a large number of PPP and GSH metabolism enzymes (76). Our prior RNA sequencing analysis, for example, indicates that a number of NRF2 targets are mis-regulated in DD patient tissue (62). In pachyonychia congenita (PC or KRT16-pEDD), a painful genetic skin condition linked to keratin mutations, several studies indicate that oxidative stress and GSH depletion downstream of an attenuated NRF2 response are critical components of its pathogenesis (77–79). The interplay between oxidative stress and keratin function in PC, to an extent, parallels our observations in DD and suggests that methods for normalizing NRF2 signaling may prove beneficial for multiple epidermal differentiation disorders (EDDs).

The rarity, phenotypic variability, and absence of a suitable animal model have hampered efforts to develop novel treatments or repurpose existing interventions for DD. This study broadens the field of potential therapeutic targets by defining a critical role for SERCA2 in orchestrating a coordinated metabolic response to oxidative stress, deficiency of which drives the emergence and subsequent flaring of DD pathology.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Donor information, cell lines, and culture conditions

We acquired DD keratinocyte cultures from patient biopsies collected at Northwestern University and Technion-Israel Institute of Technology. The study contains keratinocytes isolated from discarded abdominoplasty (CTL1) and lower back biopsies (CTL2-3) from three healthy adults. Cell isolates DD1 and DD2 were collected from the same patient carrying the pathogenic variant c.115A>G, p.Asn39Asp in the actuator domain (80). CTL1-3 and DD1-2 were collected and isolated by the Northwestern Skin Biology & Diseases Resource-Based Center, under a protocol approved by the Northwestern University Institutional Review Board (IRB no. STU00009443).

Cell lines DD3 and DD4 have been isolated from two distinct patients who both carry the pathogenic variant c.530A>C, p.Gln177Pro in the actuator domain (80). Participants provided written informed consent prior to their inclusion in the study, according to the protocols approved by the Emek and Rabin Medical Centers (EMC-0086-15, RMC-0312-20) institutional review board. Patient (DD5-7) and control samples (CTL4-6) used for immunohistochemistry were obtained using University of Michigan Institutional Review Board (IRB no. HUM00174864)

Cell lines were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 on J2 feeder layers in FAD medium (81). DD3 and DD4 required the inclusion of 5 μM Y27632 to establish and maintain cultures (82). For consistency, all DD isolates and CTL1 and CTL2 were maintained in Y27632 unless specifically stated otherwise. For experiments, keratinocytes were removed from feeders and plated at 80 to 90% confluency in M154 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) growth medium supplemented with 0.07 mM CaCl2, human keratinocyte growth supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific), gentamicin and amphotericin B 24 to 36 hours prior to the experiment. Y27632 was not present during experiments.

CRISPR-edited immortalized keratinocyte lines (THEKS) are described in (16) and are derived from original line N/TERT-2G (83) (a gift from J. Rheinwald, Brigham and Women’s Hospital, Boston, Massachusetts, USA). These cells were grown in keratinocyte serum-free medium ordered as a kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog 37010022) with supplements of human epidermal growth factor (0.2 ng/ml) and bovine pituitary extract (30 μg/ml). Other additives included 0.31 mM CaCl2, penicillin (100 U/ml), and streptomycin (100 μg/ml).

Antibodies and reagents

Anti-DP [11-5F was a gift from D. Garrod, NW6 described in (84)], anti-GSH conjugates (ViroGen, no. 101-A), anti-K14 (gift from J. Segre), anti–β-catenin (C2206, Sigma-Aldrich), anti–α-catenin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-17443), anti-vinculin (Sigma-Aldrich, V9131), anti–glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; Santa Cruz, sc-47724), Y27632 (Cayman Chemical, no. 10005583), CDN1163 (Neurodon), NDC-9009 (gift from Neurodon) described in (36, 37), NAC (Sigma-Aldrich, A7250), menadione (Sigma-Aldrich, M5625), anti-SERCA2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA3-919, clone 2A7), mitomycin C (Sigma-Aldrich, M4287), and Ponceau S (Sigma-Aldrich, P7170).

Western blotting

Western blots were conducted by loading 20 μg of lysate extracted with 1× Laemmli buffer + 5% β-mercaptoethanol or urea (8 M)/SDS (1%) + 5% BME buffer. Samples were resolved on 7.5% SDS/polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) gels purchased from BioRad and subjected to 80 to 100 V in tris/glycine buffer containing 0.1% SDS. Protein was then transferred to nitrocellulose (0.45-μm pore size, Amersham) overnight at 22 V in tris/glycine buffer containing 20% methanol. After checking transfer quality with Ponceau S, blots were rinsed in tris-buffered saline (TBS; pH 7.75, 200 mM NaCl), blocked in 5% milk/TBS for 1 hour, blotted overnight at 4°C with anti-SERCA2 (2A7, 1:1000) in blocking buffer. Subsequently, blots were rinsed in TBS 3× 10 min and incubated with goat–anti-mouse immunoglobulin G–horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugates (Santa Cruz) for 30 min. Following another 3× 10 min round of washing with TBS, blots were incubated with chemiluminescent solution for 2 min (Millipore, Crescendo Substrate) and developed using a LiCor Odyssey instrument. For loading controls, blots were either blotted with antibodies to detect either GAPDH (Santa Cruz, 1:1000) or anti-vinculin (Sigma-Aldrich, 1:1000).

Solubility assay

Confluent cultures were switched to 1.2 mM calcium for 1 or 4 hours and lysed on ice with cold RIPA buffer containing 1% Tx-100 and 0.1% SDS. Lysate was centrifuged and RIPA-soluble fraction was removed to a fresh tube. The pellet was resuspended in an equivalent volume of urea (8 M)/SDS (1%) buffer and allowed to incubate for 1 hour at room temperature. Following centrifugation, the urea/SDS soluble fraction was removed to a fresh tube. The remaining pellet was resuspended in an equivalent volume of urea/SDS buffer with 5% BME. Gel loading was normalized to protein levels in the RIPA lysate and equivalent volumes were loaded of the corresponding urea/SDS and urea/SDS/BME soluble fractions.

Keratin disulfide bonding analysis

Cells were lysed in 8 M urea and 1% SDS (nonreducing) and homogenized by passing through a 22-gauge needle. The homogenate was split into separate tubes and, to one, 5% BME was added to produce the reduced sample. Protein was quantified via an Amido Black assay to ensure equal loading between samples, subjected to SDS-PAGE and assessed by western blotting for K14.

Immunofluorescence and microscopy

Cells plated in glass bottom 96-well plates were fixed using either ice-cold anhydrous methanol for 2 min or 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 1% normal goat serum, and those fixed with PFA were permeabilized using 0.5% Triton X-100. All samples were mounted using a glycerol based mounting media as described (85). PLA analysis was performed as described in Hegazy et al. (85) after fixation as described above. Fluorescent apotome images were acquired using a Zeiss Axio Observer, fitted with an X-Cite 120 LED Boost System, an Apotome.2 slide module, Axiocam 503 Mono digital camera, and a Plan-Apochromat 40×/1.4, Plan-Apochromat 63×/1.4 objective, Plan-Apochromat 100×/1.4 objective (Carl Zeiss). All images have undergone Orthogonal Projection in the Z plane using ZEN software. Confocal Microscopy was performed using a Nikon/Yokogawa W1 SoRa Super Resolution Spinning Disk Confocal Microscope with a Hamamatsu ORCA-Fusion Digital CMOS C14440-20UP camera. Richardson-Lucy Method deconvolution was then performed through the NIS Elements Nikon software. A CFI Plan Apochromat Lambda D 60× Oil immersion objective was used with 0.2-μm z steps. 405-, 488-, 561-, and 640-nm solid-state lasers were used at powers between 20 and 40%.

Electron microscopy

Cells were plated on thermanox plastic coverslips and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer. Fixed sections were submitted to Northwestern University’s Center for Advanced Microscopy for further processing with standard protocols. Seventy-five–nanometer–thin sections were imaged using a JEOL 1400 120-kV transmission electron microscope (TEM) with a Lab6 source and equipped with a Gatan Ultrascan 4000 charge-coupled device camera. Images were taken using SerialEM, Leginon and Digital Micrograph software.

Immunohistochemistry

Skin samples from control and patients with DD were formaldehyde-fixed, paraffin-embedded, and cross-sectioned into 5-μm slices before mounting on slides (University of Michigan). After baking overnight at 60°C, slides were deparaffinized by sequential incubations in 100% xylene, 100% ethanol, and 70% ethanol. Samples were then subjected to antigen retrieval in pH 6.0 citrate buffer for 15 min at 95°C. When cooled, sections were blocked in 2% BSA +1% normal goat serum for 30 min. Then, samples were incubated with anti-DP (115F clone, 1:400), anti-glutathionylation (1:100) or anti–α-catenin antibodies in blocking buffer overnight at 4°C. After washing three times with TBS, samples were incubated with goat–anti-mouse or goat–anti-rabbit secondary antibodies tagged with Alexa Fluor fluorophores (1:300 in blocking buffer). Samples were then washed three times with TBS [the first wash contained 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) at 1 μg/ml], before mounting coverslips with ProLong Gold.

Dispase assay

Cells were plated near confluency in 12-well dishes, then grown for 1 to 3 days in M154 until achieving a confluent monolayer. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), each well was treated with dispase (2.4 U/ml; Sigma Millipore) diluted in high calcium M154 for 30 min at 37°C. Each lifted cell sheet was then placed in a 15-ml conical tube containing 3 ml of M154 and agitated in a rotator for 1 min. Resulting fragments were returned to 12-well dishes, Zymo Research DNase was added to a final concentration of 1 U in each well, and wells were imaged using a dissecting microscope (MZ6; Leica). Cell sheet fragments were counted manually in ImageJ from images.

PLA and other FIJI analysis

Quantitation of Western blot bands was conducted using ImageJ by measuring mean pixel intensity in regions of interest encompassing the band of a set size. After subtracting values obtained from blank portions of the blot and normalization to the loading control, relative protein abundance was calculated with respect to the control sample. PLA spots were counted in ImageJ using the protocol described (86).

Cellpose automated Python analysis

We adapted a deep learning–based image segmentation framework Cellpose (33) and combined it with a series of quality control preprocessing steps to reliably obtain segmented metadata on hundreds of individual cells, similar to recent studies (34, 87). Next, to quantify signal along the intercellular junctions, we formulated a new unbiased metric, which we termed the adhesion stain index, which goes along each cell outline, measures the number of pixels above a specified threshold (the top 20% of pixels with respect to intensity per cell) and divides this number by the total number of pixels along the cell outline (fig. S2). An alternate metric termed border intensity index (fig. S2) was used for DP staining in Fig. 6 as it was less sensitive to high cytoplasmic signal associated with the NW6 antibody used in those panels.

Measurement of glutathionylation localization in tissue

Quantitation of tissue staining in fig. S7 was conducted as follows. Using the DAPI channel, nuclei were outlined as regions of interest, then each ROI enlarged by 1-μm unit. Junctional regions were identified by α-catenin staining. For each image, a ROI was defined in a primarily collagen area of the dermis. Intensity here was used to normalize all other measurements. The ratio between glutathionylation intensity along the enlarged nuclei ROI and along the cell junction was then used as a readout for perinuclear vs junctional staining intensity.

CellRox assay

Examination of cellular ROS levels was conducted using CellRox dye (Invitrogen, C10444). In brief, keratinocytes were plated in a tissue-culture treated 96-well plate with black walls in M154 with 0.07 mM CaCl2 and incubated until reaching confluency. Cells were then switched to 1.2 mM CaCl2 overnight, preincubated with dimethyl sulfoxide or 10 μM CDN1163 for 1 hour, loaded with CellRox dye in drug containing media for 30 min, rinsed, and replaced with fresh drug-containing medium. An initial CellRox fluorescence reading was taken, then menadione was added to 20 μM and readings were taken every 60 min.

Metabolite sample preparation and analysis

Cells were grown in 10-cm dishes in M154 (0.07 mM CaCl2) until 75% confluent. At that point, cells were switched to 1.2 mM CaCl2 with 10 μM menadione or vehicle (EtOH) for 4 hours. Cells were rinsed twice with ice-cold PBS, then lysed on dry ice with 80% methanol prechilled to −80°C. After scraping into microfuge tubes, lysates were subjected to three rounds of freezing and thawing in liquid nitrogen, followed by incubation overnight at −20°C. Lysates were spun at maximum speed at 4°C for 15 min to pellet precipitated protein. Supernatant was removed, dried via SpeedVac, and delivered to the Northwestern University Metabolomics Core for liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC/MS) analysis with injections normalized to the concentration of resolubilized protein pellets. Raw peak intensities were then analyzed via Metaboanalyst 6.0.

For unpaired analysis (Fig. 3, A and B), metabolites were collected for three independent experiments comparing one control (CTL1) and the four DD (DD1-4) isolates. In addition, three experiments were conducted with two control patient isolates (CTL1-2) and two DD isolates (DD1-2) maintained without the Y27631 used to prolong culture lifespan. In total, 27 samples were subjected to comparison. Of 298 potentially identifiable metabolites, 145 passed the quality control filters available through Metaboanalyst 6.0 including removal of the least abundant 20 and 10% demonstrating near-constant values via interquartile range. Samples were normalized to the median peak value before statistical analysis.

For pairwise comparisons, the response of each metabolite within each isolate to menadione was measured per experimental repeat following normalization to median peak intensity within the three biological repeats for CTL1 and DD1-4. Correlation maps represent the average response of CTL1 and the DD isolates to menadione (Fig. 3C). For comparison of individual metabolites across all conditions (Fig. 3, D to F), each metabolite for CTL1 and DD1-4 was normalized to sample peak intensity medians in Metaboanalyst 6.0.

GSH plate reader assay

Keratinocytes were plated in a six-well dish (1 million cells per well) in M154 (0.07 mM CaCl2) and incubated till 80 to 100% confluency. Cells were switched to 1.2 mM CaCl2 ± 10 μM menadione for 4 hours. Cells were trypsinized, quenched, rinsed with PBS, pelleted, and spun down. Cells were resuspended in 200 μl of PBS then lysed by freezing and thawing in liquid nitrogen three times, spinning at max speed in microfuge for 15 min at 4°C. Supernatant (150 μl) was collected and frozen. The remaining pellet was dissolved, subjected to protein quantitation, and used to normalize variations in starting material. The supernatant was subjected to a fluorometric GSH assay (AnaSpec, AS-72158).

Statistical analysis and data plotting

Plots were generated using GraphPad Prism 10 and Adobe Illustrator. All statistics were performed on raw data to preserve variability of CTL samples, although some plots display fold change for visual clarity. Significances were determined via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-tailed t tests, except where indicated. Nonsignificance is indicated by “ns;” P values less than 0.05 are indicated with one asterisk, less than 0.01 with two asterisks, less than 0.001 with three asterisks, and less than 0.0001 are with four asterisks.
