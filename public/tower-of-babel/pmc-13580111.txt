Is AI Capable of Real-World Drug Discovery?
Authors: Georgia McGaughey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Small-molecule drug discovery frequently operates in regimes where slight structural changes have large consequences. These are situations in which current artificial intelligence (AI) methods, trained on mass data, may perform poorly. For a substantial fraction of drug-discovery projects, limited biological understanding and sparse data further constrain progress and define regimes where AI approaches remain difficult to apply. Despite these limitations, the global investment in AI continues to grow rapidly. Using two case studies characterized by structural and biochemical data, this article examines how the close integration of computational and medicinal chemistry coupled with a deep understanding of chemical shape and protein interactions enabled targeted computational molecular design to produce disproportionate gains in efficacy and selectivity. These examples highlight a challenge for current AI methods: sensitivity to subtle, low-data perturbations. For AI to achieve a transformative impact in drug discovery, it must move beyond pattern recognition to understand, or explicitly simulate, the mechanistic “why” linking subtle structural changes to biological outcomes. Pending such advances, clear opportunities for AI to productively complement human efforts, rather than be used as a stand-alone solution, are delineated.

Introduction

At the dawn of artificial intelligence (AI), humanity stands at an almost mythical threshold. Whether it be Adam and Eve, Prometheus, or Gilgamesh, as humans, we are drawn to stories of knowledge that promises transcendence (although it is worth pointing out that things do not always work out well for the transcended!). Now, with the ability to create machines that learn, reason, and predict, the possibility of recognizing patterns beyond human perception lies tantalizingly close, and what is drug design other than recognizing what our data is telling us? So, are we crossing over that threshold, or are we too caught up in Joseph Campbell’s Power of Myth?

There are now published examples of how AI has claimed to have advanced medicines into the clinic. − Collectively, these published studies and others illustrate how AI has begun to reshape drug discovery, compressing timelines, expanding chemical search space, and uncovering structure–property relationships that would have been difficult to assess through intuition alone. − Here, we define AI in the context of small-molecule discovery as autonomous drug discovery: a framework in which the traditional role of the computational chemist is increasingly replaced by an agentic AI system capable of integrating chemical, biological, structural, and experimental data to autonomously generate hypotheses, prioritize compounds, and guide iterative decision-making. Yet the true measure of these approaches lies not in benchmark performance, retrospective analyses, or those carried out on thousands of existing data points now applied to a new (oftentimes highly related) protein target or for drug repurposing, but in their impact on discovery programs with limited data, where uncertainty is high, and decisions carry tangible consequences.

The application of computational methods to drug discovery is, of course, not new. When computer-aided drug design (CADD) began to influence discovery programs in the 1980s and 1990s, it coincided with both genuine scientific advances and periodic claims that computation would revolutionize the field. Importantly, CADD was being taken seriously within project teams. Landmark medicines such as Crixivan can trace part of their lineage to CADD, and the establishment of the QSAR, Cheminformatics, and Modeling Society in 1989 underscored the maturation of the discipline. In practice, CADD rarely functions as a stand-alone solution; its impact is greatest when computational scientists are embedded within discovery teams and work in close, iterative collaboration with medicinal chemists. Typically, a computational chemist has a hypothesis that is based on discussions with the project team or one that she has developed based on data. This hypothesis is best borne out in a testable, predictive manner in collaboration with the medicinal chemistry team who then synthesizes the molecule(s) (unless the molecule already exists) and has the molecule tested in a relevant assay. This is what is typically referred to as the design-make-test-analyze or Design-Make-Test-Analyze (DMTA) cycle. This cycle continues until the molecule is deemed efficacious and safe enough to be tested in animals and ultimately humans.

The contention of this article suggests caution as well as hope in interpreting the impact of AI, specifically because optimization of small-molecule properties (efficacy, safety, solubility, etc.) often revolves around slight changes producing large differences and key design decisions based on limited data. And yet, transformative AI is at its best when trained on overwhelming, comprehensive data, and where predictions are interpolations and not extrapolations. Such utility is compelling, often amazing, but perhaps not key to moving a project from hit to lead to an active pharmaceutical ingredient (API). Even when thousands of data points are available for AlphaFold 3, the AI tool struggles with unusual RNA and DNA structures: examples where single mutations have a dramatic impact.

There is also a more philosophical question of whether we are at peace with predictions without explanation. The FDA does not require the latter when approving new drugs, and so, as a commercial consideration, this may not matter, and, in fact, predictions that are accurate often engender understanding, yet the evolution of human thought typically traverses understanding to prediction, not the other way around. Put simply, do we want to take medicines that we do not understand? The author writes this well aware that many medicines have been taken for decades (or longer: aspirin!) without any clear concept of mode of action (nor toxicity)! ,

Against this backdrop, this article provides two proof-of-concept studies in which computational approaches were tightly integrated with medicinal chemistry during active discovery campaigns. The first describes the acceleration of the design cycle that led to Belsomra. The second demonstrates how data-driven primary sequence alignment integrated with three-dimensional (3D) protein–ligand structural analysis enabled the elucidation and achievement of selectivity within the challenging phosphodiesterase (PDE) target class. Neither example employed what would now be considered AI and their transformative success was in a low-data realm, precisely where AI may struggle and where subtle small-molecular changes produced outsized effects. These case studies highlight the continuing challenge for AI in reproducing the human-driven insights (asking the “why” rather than producing the “what”) that proved critical to success under conditions of sparse data and steep structure–activity cliffs. This article therefore aims to define how computational methods can evolve for autonomous and agentic AI systems to succeed in real-world drug discovery.

Case 1: Molecular Shape

Consider the example of an optimized screening compound (Figure ) that gave rise to Belsomra, a prescription medicine for insomnia. In two-dimensional (2D), where most cheminformatics AI analysis is concentrated, the shape of the chemotype in 3D is not often described. As seen in Figure , the 3D shape of the small molecule is described as a U-shaped conformation, enabling an intramolecular π-stacking shape. This conformation was elucidated years prior to the bound complex with the orexin receptors, and in hindsight, one could argue that the conformation of the small molecule resembles a helical conformation of the seven transmembrane α helices of the G protein-coupled receptors (GPCR). In the process of this elucidation, small-molecular changes had dramatic effects on affinity because of outsized effects on the conformational distribution. Small (2D) changes that eliminated the U-shaped conformation, or shifted it away from the lowest-free-energy state, abolished activity. In contrast, molecules that maintained this motif consistently demonstrated superior efficacy.

2D depiction of ligand which gave rise to Belsomra and proposed 3D conformation.

While knowledge of the bound ligand structure is generally advantageous in drug discovery, the prior identification of the small-molecule 3D shape, years before the determination of the bound structure and the approval of Belsomra, was a huge benefit in this successful drug-discovery effort, and it became a deliberate design principle pursued by the team. This characteristic 3D shape is, in fact, conserved across subsequent orexin antagonists, even from different design groups, and later validated by published protein–ligand structures (Figure ). − Collectively, these orexin antagonists all demonstrate that the U-shaped conformation, as described first by Cox and McGaughey et al., is the bioactive conformation. In each case, this hypothesis emerged from careful, bespoke analyses that integrated computational modeling with NMR data and small-molecule X-ray structures. Physical insight and precision experimentation, not data coverage, led the successful project evolution driven by a testable hypothesis: that a U-shaped conformation would confer superior efficacy.

Ligands extracted from cited PDB structures − depicting conserved U-shaped conformation.

It is known that careful conformational analysis has demonstrated the importance of 3D conformations of small molecules in other therapeutic areas. Jorgensen first quantitated this when examining the “magic methyl effect” on potency, and it was later exemplified in lead optimization examples. , In a significant number of these matched pair examples, one methyl group, strategically placed on a molecule, can result in an activity boost of up to a factor of 10. The importance of a methyl group was also seen in a subsequent orexin antagonist to Belsomra, which is referred to as Filorexant (MK-6096), Figure . The design of the piperidine class of orexin receptor antagonists was inspired by the earlier work in understanding the U-shaped conformation. Minimization of the 1,3-allylic strain interactions was used as another design principle to synthesize the 2,5-disubstituted piperidine carboxamide with axially oriented substituents, which resulted in Filorexant. Ultimately, it was not pursued beyond phase 2 clinical trials as Belsomra was approved. However, a matched pair analog of Filorexant demonstrated a significant boost in potency when a methyl group was placed at the 6-position on the piperidine (compound 18 versus compound 22 in 24).

There is no doubt that in orexin antagonist lead optimization, human insight accelerated the molecular design, leveraging shape comparison and close collaboration with medicinal chemists. There is also no doubt, at least in the author’s mind, that AI could have carried out the simple 2D SMILES to 3D shape analyses and formulated a hypothesis that perhaps 3D shape does in fact matter, at least in the orexin drug discovery example (note that in a later retrospective study of dopamine antagonist data, the same conclusion was drawn). There seems to be no conceptual reason this could not be done, however, because “2D” SMILES strings are a “language” they have been easily adopted as the primary grist to the AI data mill. This is not to argue that 3D knowledge is always superior to 2D information, as sometimes, the 2D representation is more predictive than the 3D representation or not even relevant.

For example, a 2D-QSAR model was more predictive than corresponding 3D models for planar flavonoid inhibitors of CYP1A2, whose compact active site is structurally adapted to accommodate planar aromatic ligands. In this example, it appears that for structurally constrained, largely planar ligand series, topological representations may capture the activity-determining structural relationships as effectively as, or better than, conformation-dependent 3D descriptors. Either outcome is possible (2D being more predictive than 3D and vice versa); the key feature in enablement is understanding the why, not merely the what. In other words, AI can represent and exploit 3D molecular shape, but it does not automatically understand when shape is the decisive factor in a drug-discovery problem.

Case 2: Protein Shape

The second case example concerns the design of MK-8189 for the treatment of schizophrenia. Although the relative contributions of genetic and environmental factors to schizophrenia remain difficult to disentangle, key aspects of the underlying neurobiology are now well-established. Notably, phosphodiesterase 10 (PDE10) is selectively expressed in striatal neurons, which positions it as a mechanistically informative target. A highly selective PDE10 inhibitor therefore provides a means to directly test the hypothesis in the clinic that increasing intracellular cyclic nucleotide levels can recapitulate key features of dopamine D2 receptor antagonism and D1 receptor agonism. In this context, selectivity is not only a pharmacological refinement but also a prerequisite for interpreting target engagement and downstream effects on striatal signaling, with the goal of potentially treating the symptoms of schizophrenia.

Crucially, this hypothesis-driven design approach was tested and advanced under conditions of limited data, where mechanistic reasoning and chemical intuition, rather than data-hungry algorithmic optimization, were essential to defining the experimental strategy. As such, this example illustrates a regime of drug discovery in which human intelligence provides the primary source of insight, and where computational methods as a tool, including contemporary AI, remain inherently complementary rather than determinative.

Specifically, primary sequence alignment of the 11 different families of PDE sequences highlighted amino acid differences, which were then recapitulated in 3D. A bound fragment screening lead highlighted the central binding “hotspot,” and it was hypothesized that a pendant 5-methyl-pyridine could reach into the “selectivity pocket” and engage in a hydrogen-bond interaction with tyrosine-683, a unique amino acid to PDE10. Note that there was no literature defining a so-called “selectivity pocket,” and this was a hypothesis based on the primary sequence alignment viewed in 3D utilizing homology modeling. There were additional key interactions to be made such as (1) hydrogen bonding (directly with the enzyme or via waters), (2) entropic constraints with a trans-cyclopropyl group, and (3) π-stacking interactions with the enzyme However, the key insight enabling potency and selectivity was driven by a single amino acid difference, tyrosine-683 (Figure ). The designed molecule, MK-8189, has completed its efficacy and safety phase 2b clinical trial in participants with an acute episode of schizophrenia.

Active site of PDE10a enzyme complexed with MK-8189 depicting selectivity residue, TYR 683.

The DMTA design cycle for MK-8189 progressed rapidly, enabled by tight integration between computational and medicinal chemistry and anchored by strong, testable hypotheses regarding the structural determinants of potency and selectivity. While the individual computational steps in uncovering this insight can be coded and followed, the ultimate insight remained, at that time, fundamentally human. In this case, sequence alignment followed by analysis of the bound structures rapidly converged on a strategy to achieve selectivity in the PDE family via the introduction of a 5-methylpyridyl group. The introduction of the 5-methylpyridyl group concomitantly increased potency, underscoring the role of human judgment in guiding rational drug design.

A goal for AI is to replicate and ultimately surpass this multidimensional integration of information across diverse domains and accelerate the drug-discovery timeline. While expert computational chemists currently excel at synthesizing insights and translating them into coherent design strategies, autonomous and agentic AI methods are rapidly advancing toward the same capability. In the PDE10 example, the critical insight was recognizing how subtle structural changes in the protein produced profound effects on small-molecule potency and selectivity, precisely the type of complex, multidomain relationship that AI systems are increasingly capable of identifying and exploiting.

Forward Looking and Next Steps

It is the author’s assertion that if AI is to ever achieve its true potential to pass over that threshold of utility we eagerly anticipate, understanding the why is a key component. This quest for understanding is not a new concept, and the author cites multiple references that support a strong argument that current AI excels at recognizing statistical regularities, and achieving transformative advances in drug discovery will likely require AI systems that can infer, test, and reason about the underlying biological and chemical mechanisms: the why behind molecular activity. − For instance, why methods that incorporate 3D protein target information enable drug discovery, and when not, could engender new insights.

Current advances in 3D structure tools such as AlphaFold, Boltz2, and openFold are methods that have seen immense benefit to the preclinical molecular design space − and may further enhance the identification of the bioactive shape conformation of the small molecule. − That said, a recent publication in BioRxiv highlights challenges with the data sets used to predict outcomes of such 3D structure tools for binding small molecules. Understanding nuanced concepts such as “target mirroring” in embedded computational tools would be enabling to distinguish the what from the why. Ultimately, molecular representations determine which aspects of chemical structure are accessible to an AI model; because no single representation captures the full complexity of molecular behavior. Successful drug-discovery applications often require task-specific combinations of 2D, 3D, sequence, graph, and descriptor-based information.

Concomitantly, advances in 3D molecular generation have transformed the ability of AI to create chemically plausible molecular structures directly in three dimensions in the absence of knowledge of the protein target. Early diffusion models, including MiDi and EQGAT-Diff, jointly generated molecular graphs and coordinates, while newer flow-matching methods, such as FlowMol , and SemlaFlow, have improved both sampling speed and molecular quality. More recently, FLOWR and PILOT have extended these concepts to protein pocket-conditioned ligand generation, enabling the direct design of molecules within target binding sites. Although these approaches represent remarkable advances in generative chemistry, they primarily optimize surrogate objectives, chemical validity, geometric realism, or predicted protein interactions, rather than experimentally validated biological activity. Consequently, despite impressive benchmark performance, including recent comparisons identifying SemlaFlow as the strongest unconditional generator, these methods remain components of the discovery process rather than substitutes for experimental validation.

AI methods that incorporate the shape of both the molecule and protein (and cofolding methods) may further accelerate the discovery of preclinical small molecules. In the example of PDE10, the human insight coupled the unique tyrosine in the binding pocket with the ongoing chemical synthesis approaches contemporaneously to suggest incorporating a pyridine group to hydrogen bond with the phenol of the amino acid, tyrosine-683. This insight could easily be a part of a closed-loop optimization process embedded in a lab-of-the-future approach such as one described by Ekins et al. and would accelerate the DMTA cycle markedly. A further advancement could be that AI would understand the importance of tyrosine (the why and not just the what) and inspire other lead modifications.

There are multiple examples where AI has reduced attrition, accelerated timelines, and generated novel insights, , especially outside of the realm of CADD. A specific example to reduce clinical failures caused by unvalidated targets is to employ an internal “lab-of-the-future” platform that centralizes data for gene target validation, thereby reducing failures caused by unvalidated targets; Abbvie’s R&D Convergence Hub (ARCH) is one such tool. Although ARCH is proprietary to Abbvie, analogous approaches could be enabled using public data sets such as UK Biobank. , A more durable path forward lies in recognizing that AI expands our reach but does not confer omniscience and that progress will continue to depend on human judgment to frame the right questions, interpret uncertainty, and navigate the irreducible complexity of human biology. That said, in the specific world of CADD, the increasing power of agentic AI challenges the traditional role of the computational chemist: advances in computation, coupled with the nuanced structure–activity insights described herein, may ultimately displace the need for a human computational chemist in the traditional sense.

In the context of drug discovery, uncritical adoption of AI risks mistaking novelty for insight. The more consequential path is to embed AI within discovery teams as an agentic AI bot as a supporting capability, guided by human judgment and testable biological hypotheses. Such restraint, rather than exuberance, is most likely to yield durable advances and, ultimately, safe and effective medicines for patients who are in need. There is often perceived to be a dichotomy between human-driven innovation and autonomous AI approaches; however, these domains are increasingly convergent, complementary rather than mutually exclusive, and likely to evolve toward deep interdependence.

Amid the current enthusiasm surrounding the impact of AI in drug discovery, it is worth asking whether the field has overestimated its transformative scope, treating AI as a panacea rather than as a tool. In treating AI as a universal solution, the field risks mistaking access to powerful tools for the attainment of understanding, biting into a modern form of the forbidden apple with the expectation of effortless insight.

Drug discovery ultimately operates within the reality that biological systems are complex, heterogeneous, and shaped by genetic differences that, while individually modest, can have meaningful functional consequences. These features resist one-size-fits-all solutions and place fundamental limits on purely data-driven approaches. A more productive path forward lies in acknowledging these constraints and positioning AI as a complement to rather than a replacement for the forms of human intelligence required to navigate biological complexity. Examples of this might be the interpretation of patents, scientific publications, and noisy data sets, not the replication of human conceptualization.

Perhaps initiatives such as AlphaFold, Boltz2, and openFold will provide solutions that enable rational design, and coupled with companies that leverage physics-based approaches, we will see advances. Such approaches may provide solutions to understanding atomic interactions between small molecules and their target receptor, ultimately reducing the drug-discovery timeline. There are numerous companies in existence and others emerging which combine the latest advances in AI/machine learning, protein sciences, and biophysics to increase the speed and accuracy of small-molecule drug discovery. , These are all productive approaches to improving the discovery of small molecules while simultaneously reducing the DMTA life cycle. A very recent perspective on AI in drug discovery offers a complementary view on the field’s progress, emphasizing that ultimate success is defined by clinical benefit to patients and offers opportunities for future impact.

Summary

These two case studies illustrate the effectiveness of close collaboration between medicinal and computational chemists in advancing orexin antagonists and PDE10-selective inhibitors. In both programs, this integrated, hypothesis-driven approach led to the identification of efficacious compounds with acceptable safety profiles that progressed into clinical evaluation. Notably, one of these efforts culminated in the development of a marketed therapy (Belsomra), underscoring that sustained human-led reasoning, informed by experiments and computation but not dominated by data-intensive AI methods, can translate fundamental insight into clinically meaningful medicines. In this case study, human insight into the small molecule’s 3D shape was key to success. Could an AI approach have this insight? This author thinks the answer is a definite yes, but one should also recognize the caveat that 3D molecular understanding is not always more predictive than 2D understanding.

In the example of PDE10, the human insight coupled the unique tyrosine in the binding pocket with the ongoing chemical synthesis approaches to suggest a synthetic path that ultimately achieved unprecedented selectivity. Again, a closed-loop AI approach could have been scripted to recognize that the 3D visualization of the enzyme would have revealed a selectivity pocket.

The two examples discussed herein suggest that AI should ultimately be capable of substantially more than it currently achieves in prospective molecular discovery, regardless of the amount of data. The principal limitation is unlikely to be computational power but rather the difficulty of learning causal molecular recognition and exploiting sparse, information-rich experimental observations that experienced drug designers routinely use to guide design.
