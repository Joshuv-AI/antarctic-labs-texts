Introduction

Hair-thread tourniquet syndrome (HTTS) is a rare condition in which a strand of hair or fine thread tightly encircles tissue, causing vascular compression, ischemia, and occasionally necrosis [1], [2], [3]. When it occurs around the neck, the constricting hair may compress cervical blood vessels and the airways, resulting in a life-threatening strangulation mechanism [4], [5], [6].

Strangulation refers to external compression or constriction of the neck by hands or ligature, obstructing blood and/or air flow and leading to cerebral hypoxia, which can be fatal [7]. The type of ligature, the force applied, and the duration of compression determine the pattern and severity of injury. While strangulation is a common mechanism in domestic violence and also an established risk factor for intimate partner homicide, it is usually accidental and non-fatal in young children [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]. However, cervical HTTS and circumferential neck injuries should prompt consideration of child abuse or neglect [1], [14].

This article presents a case of non-fatal accidental cervical strangulation caused by maternal hair in a 17-month-old child during co-sleeping. Only a few such cases have been reported in the medical and forensic literature [6], [15]. Bed-sharing with individuals who have long hair may increase the risk of a child's neck becoming entrapped in the hair, potentially resulting in vascular compromise and cerebral hypoxia. The present case underscores the importance of parental education regarding safe sleeping practices and clinician awareness of this rare but preventable mechanism of pediatric neck injury.

This case was previously present as a poster at the European Academy Forensic Science (EAFS) Conference, Stockholm, Sweden, in May 2022.

Case report

A previously healthy 17-month-old child was brought to the emergency department by ambulance following an episode of apnea and transient loss of consciousness. The mother, who had been co-sleeping with the child, reported awakening after perceiving that the child had pulled her hair. The parents observed that a strand of the mother's hair was tightly wrapped around the child's neck, causing cyanosis and unconsciousness. They attempted to release the hair manually, and the father ultimately used scissors to cut it, resulting in excoriation of the child's neck (Fig. 1). The child was unresponsive for 10–20 s and resumed spontaneous breathing after the hair constriction was relieved. Initial respirations were slow and shallow. Full recovery of consciousness occurred within approximately five minutes, according to parental report. Emergency medical services were contacted, and upon arrival approximately 10 min later, the child was alert and hemodynamically stable. Pulse oximetry showed 100% oxygen saturation on room air, with normal respiratory effort.

Aggregated petechial cutaneous hemorrhages on the cheek and the circumferential ligature mark visible on the right side of the neck, with multiple petechiae superior to the mark.

Forensic examination was conducted approximately 17 h after the incident. A thin, linear, circumferential erythematous mark, approximately 0.1–0.2 cm wide, was visible around the neck without skin breach. On the right side of the neck, the ligature mark was oblique, running downward and anteriorly (Fig. 1); on the left side, it was slightly oblique, running upward and posteriorly (Fig. 2); and on the anterior aspect of the neck, it was horizontal. Posteriorly, it consisted of two parallel erythematous lines extending toward the most superior point on the right lateral aspect of the neck, where a centimeter-sized cluster of small cutaneous hemorrhages was observed (Fig. 3). On the posterior aspect of the right cheek, inferior to the earlobe and aligned with the external auditory canal, an elongated area measuring approximately 2.5 × 0.3 cm was observed, containing multiple closely aggregated petechial cutaneous hemorrhages (Fig. 1). Multiple petechiae were evenly distributed superior to the ligature mark (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). No petechiae were identified in the conjunctivae or oral mucosa (not shown). Several older contusions, pale yellow-green in appearance, were noted on the right forehead (Fig. 4), arms, back, and bilaterally on the knees (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). These were located on exposed surfaces consistent with bruising in a normally active child of this age. A small centrally located crusted lesion was noted within the forehead contusion (Fig. 4). Superficial abrasions were present on the anterior chest wall and the lower left extremity (not shown). No additional injuries were identified during the forensic examination. The child's neck circumference measured 25.5 cm.

Whole-body radiography demonstrated no acute or prior fractures, and computed tomography (CT) of the head and neck revealed no structural abnormalities. There were no signs of airway obstruction or neurological deficits. Ophthalmologic examination revealed a small punctate subconjunctival hemorrhage in the left eye.

Further investigation confirmed that the mother's hair was approximately 40 cm in length, and the segment recovered from the bed measured 35 cm (Fig. 7). Based on the clinical findings, forensic examination, scene investigation, police inquiry, and social services assessment, the incident was classified as accidental.

This case illustrates a rare instance of tourniquet-related strangulation in a co-sleeping child and highlights the importance of parental education on safe sleeping practices, as well as awareness of potential hazards associated with co-sleeping with long-haired individuals.

Discussion and literature review

Hair-thread tourniquet syndrome (HTTS) is an uncommon pediatric condition in which hair acts as a circumferential ligature, causing progressive vascular compromise, most frequently affecting acral structures or external genitalia in infants [1], [2], [3]. Cervical involvement represents an exceptionally rare manifestation within this spectrum and accounted for approximately 1% of the 226 HTTS cases included in a systematic review [6], [16]. Nevertheless, neck involvement appears pathophysiologically consistent with other tourniquet injuries and may be understood as an extreme variant within a continuum ranging from digital or genital constriction to potentially fatal asphyxial events [1], [6], [14], [16], [17]. Both cervical and uvular involvement have been described, and cervical HTTS should therefore be considered in unexplained episodes of apnea, cyanosis, irritability, or transient neurological impairment in young children [6], [16], [18], [19]. The current findings further support the conceptualization of cervical HTTS as a distinct subtype of hair-tourniquet syndrome, associated with specific clinical and forensic challenges.

A review of the literature identified ten previously reported pediatric cases of cervical HTTS worldwide [6], [15], [17], [18], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], summarized in Table 1. The affected children were between 11 and 27 months of age (median, 19 months), and all but one case [25] occurred during co-sleeping with a caregiver or sibling with long hair. In one case, the event was objectively documented by a bedroom surveillance camera; however, such direct observational evidence remains exceptional, and the extent to which these observations can be generalized is limited [23].

The reported clinical presentation of cervical HTTS appears relatively consistent and is characterized by narrow circumferential erythematous neck marks, facial petechiae, cyanosis, and transient impairment of consciousness, findings typically interpreted as manifestations of acute vascular compression or hypoxic injury [6], [13], [15], [18], [22], [25]. However, these external findings are nonspecific and may occur in both accidental and inflicted strangulation [1], [4], [5], [14], [15], [18], [25]. Morphological findings are therefore insufficient for reliable etiological classification. Furthermore, the broader strangulation literature demonstrates that severe internal injuries may be present despite minimal or entirely absent external findings, further limiting the ability to assess etiology and injury severity based solely on physical examination and injury morphology [11], [12], [13].

This substantial phenotypic overlap with abusive ligature or intentional manual strangulation places cervical HTTS within a diagnostic gray zone between accident and non-accidental trauma. Population-based studies of pediatric strangulation describe a broad spectrum of mechanisms, including hanging, ligature strangulation, self-inflicted “games,” and accidental compression, all associated with significant morbidity and mortality [11], [12], [13], [26]. Although most cases are non-accidental, accidental strangulation predominantly affects younger children and is often related to household hazards causing isolated neck compression, with children under 2 years of age almost exclusively represented in this group [8], [11], [12]. Intentional strangulation, including self-inflicted events, is more common in older children and adolescents and is associated with ligature or manual force [8], [11], [12]. Although external findings may be absent in both non-fatal and fatal strangulation, injuries to cervical bone and cartilage structures (25%) as well as injuries to body regions other than the neck (54%) have only been reported in non-suicidal fatal cases [11], [12], [26]. Abnormal head CT findings, low GCS (≤3), apnea, and agonal respiration on arrival have been associated with mortality, whereas spontaneous breathing in the emergency department and a favorable response to initial airway resuscitation have generally been associated with better outcomes [8], [12]. However, only abnormal head CT was significantly associated with mortality [12]. The incidence and injury patterns of non-fatal strangulation remain incompletely characterized, as only a minority of affected individuals seek medical attention [13]. Consequently, external clinical findings are insufficient to assess either intent or injury severity [11], [12], [13]. Forensic differentiation therefore requires an integrated evaluation of age, scene circumstances, mechanism of injury, clinical manifestations, associated trauma, and documentation of any prior injuries.

Reported investigations in cervical HTTS cases varied and included scene investigation, skeletal surveys, imaging studies, forensic consultation, and multidisciplinary child protection assessment, limiting comparability between cases. In several reports, the initial suspicion of inflicted strangulation was revised only after extensive negative investigations, multidisciplinary assessment, and detailed reconstruction of the event [6], [24], [25]. In one reported case, suspicion of abuse resulted in parental arrest and removal of all children from the home before expert review ultimately demonstrated an accidental mechanism and all charges were dismissed [17].

Although several of these cases initially raised strong suspicion of non-accidental strangulation due to their marked phenotypic similarity to ligature strangulation, no verified cases of intentionally inflicted cervical HTTS have been reported in children to date. Likewise, no cases have been described in which suspected pediatric HTTS was subsequently reclassified as strangulation caused by another object. This apparent consistency in classification should be interpreted with caution. The available evidence is limited to a small number of heterogeneous case reports with variable diagnostic evaluation and is likely affected by both reporting and publication bias. Although all reported pediatric cervical HTTS cases have ultimately been classified as accidental, the literature highlights a substantial risk of both overdiagnosis and underdiagnosis of child abuse in this context [14], [17], [27]. This is further complicated by the potential for circular reasoning, a well-recognized limitation in forensic medicine when the scientific basis for attributing findings to a specific etiology with high certainty is insufficient. Consequently, the true incidence of both genuinely accidental cases and potentially misclassified cases remains unknown. At the same time, research supporting child maltreatment in the context of these strangulation events is lacking. There is therefore a need to develop established criteria for the identification of suspected child abuse in strangulation cases, which requires rigorous investigation and systematic research.

The recurring pattern of initial forensic suspicion followed by later reclassification nevertheless illustrates the considerable diagnostic uncertainty and underscores the need for cautious interpretation of isolated clinical findings within their broader clinical, contextual, and forensic setting.

This interpretative challenge is further illustrated by rare adult cases of homicidal hair strangulation, in which hair has functioned as an intentional ligature and produced findings such as ligature marks on the neck and petechiae, corresponding to those described in pediatric non-fatal accidental cases [4], [5]. Although these cases are exceptional, they demonstrate that identical external findings may occur in association with fundamentally different outcomes. This highlights the methodological limitation that clinical external appearance alone cannot establish the mechanism of injury and underscores the need for a broad differential diagnostic approach.

Against this background, suspected cervical HTTS should initially be approached within the differential diagnosis of pediatric strangulation and managed as possible abuse, even in the absence of corroborating findings, until alternative explanations have been systematically excluded. A structured diagnostic approach is therefore necessary and should include assessment of the plausibility and consistency of the history, scene investigation, careful evaluation for associated injuries, selective imaging of the airway, cervical vasculature, and cervical spine when clinically indicated, as well as multidisciplinary child protection assessment [12], [13], [17].

The present case represents one of the first scientifically documented European cases of cervical HTTS since the report by Kindley in 1978 [20]. Forensic assessment, including review of police records, social service documentation, and scene investigation, was consistent with the history provided by the parents, indicating an accidental mechanism. The case shares key features with previously reported accidental cases, including co-sleeping, exposure to long hair, rapid clinical improvement following removal of the constricting hair, and absence of additional findings indicative of inflicted trauma. Although definitive proof of an accident mechanism is lacking and the potential for circular reasoning must be acknowledged, this case underscores the importance of early recognition, prompt intervention, and timely forensic examination, and multidisciplinary evaluation to optimize the identification of findings relevant to subsequent investigation.

From a pathophysiological perspective, potentially fatal outcomes in cervical HTTS are primarily driven by progressive vascular obstruction secondary to neck constriction rather than primary airway compromise. Initial venous occlusion may be followed by arterial compromise and, in severe cases, airway obstruction. Historical studies by Brouardel demonstrated that jugular venous occlusion can occur with as little as 2 kg of force applied to a ligature, carotid artery occlusion at approximately 5 kg, tracheal compression at 15 kg, and vertebral artery occlusion at 30 kg [28]. Children require considerably less force; experimental work has shown that airway occlusion may occur with forces of <7 N (≈0.7 kgf) applied to the suprahyoid region [17], [29], which may be achievable with human hair under certain conditions [17].

Venous obstruction occurs at the lowest external pressures. Impaired venous return leads to increased intravascular pressure, “stagnant hypoxia” and rupture of superficial vessels, resulting in petechiae above the level of constriction, which may be visible in the conjunctiva, oral mucosa, and skin. Even very brief or partial neck compression can generate sufficient venous and capillary overpressure to cause petechiae, which may become visible immediately; hypoxia is not considered a decisive factor in their formation. With increasing compressive force, carotid and occasionally vertebral arterial flow may become compromised, resulting in reduced cerebral perfusion, rapid cerebral hypoxia and loss of consciousness within 5–10 s, accompanied by anoxic seizures and, in some cases, incontinence [30], [31]. Airway compromise generally requires greater compressive force or structural injury and is more closely associated with delayed complications, such as edema and secondary respiratory failure, than with the initial neurological collapse. In contrast, isolated airway obstruction typically causes hypoxia and loss of consciousness after several minutes of sustained oxygen deprivation. Findings in the present and previous cases therefore suggest that vascular compromise, rather than primary airway obstruction, is the predominant pathophysiological mechanism in cervical HTTS.

From a preventive perspective, these observations may contribute to a broader understanding of risks associated with co-sleeping. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) has long advised against bed-sharing because of an increased risk of sudden infant death syndrome, although current evidence suggests that this risk may be context-dependent [32], [33], [34]. Available data suggest that older and more mobile children may also be vulnerable to unusual co-sleeping-related events such as cervical HTTS. However, the current evidence base remains insufficient to define specific risk levels or preventive recommendations beyond general safety advice.

Conclusion

Cervical HTTS represents an exceptionally rare but potentially life-threatening subtype of hair-thread tourniquet syndrome with substantial clinical and forensic overlap with inflicted strangulation. The present case, among the first scientifically documented European pediatric cases in recent decades, further supports co-sleeping involving long-haired caregivers as a recurring contextual factor and highlights the predominance of vascular compromise over primary airway obstruction in the underlying pathophysiology. The marked phenotypic overlap with abusive strangulation underscores the considerable diagnostic and forensic challenges associated with this condition. External findings alone are insufficient for etiological classification, and evaluation therefore requires careful integration of clinical findings, scene investigation, multidisciplinary child protection assessment, forensic analysis. Increased awareness of cervical HTTS is important for preventive measures in the home environment, timely recognition, and appropriate medical and forensic management to reduce the risk of both under- and overdiagnosis of child abuse.
