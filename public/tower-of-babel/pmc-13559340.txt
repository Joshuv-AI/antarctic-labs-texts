Direct oral anticoagulants after surgical bioprosthetic valve replacement or mitral repair: reconciling valve and atrial fibrillation guidelines
Authors: Parag Anilkumar Chevli, Prashant D Bhave
Published: 2026

Anyone who has recently cared for a patient with atrial fibrillation (AF) immediately after bioprosthetic valve surgery knows the moment: the patient is stable, surgical hemostasis is secure, and the question of which anticoagulant to prescribe hangs in the air between the cardiac surgeon and the cardiologist. Paparella and colleagues address this question in a carefully conducted substudy of the Left Atrial Appendage Occlusion Study (LAAOS) III, providing the largest comparative dataset to date on this clinical dilemma (1). Their analysis included 2,645 patients with AF who underwent aortic bioprosthetic valve replacement, mitral bioprosthetic valve replacement, combined aortic and mitral bioprosthetic valve replacement, or mitral valve repair and who were discharged on oral anticoagulation (OAC). Of these, 461 patients were discharged on a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) and 2,184 on a vitamin K antagonist (VKA). Mechanical valves, isolated coronary surgery, absence of OAC at discharge, and in-hospital endpoint events were excluded. The primary outcome was stroke or systemic thromboembolism; major bleeding was the prespecified secondary (safety) endpoint (1).

The guideline context is central to interpreting this study. Paparella et al. frame the problem by noting that societal guidelines recommend VKAs over DOACs in patients with AF who have undergone surgical implantation of a biological prosthesis within the preceding 3 months (1). That framing is accurate when viewed through valve-specific perioperative guidance. The 2020 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) valvular heart disease guideline states that anticoagulation with a VKA is reasonable in patients with AF in the first 3 months after bioprosthetic surgical aortic or mitral valve replacement (2). The 2021 European Society of Cardiology/European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery valvular guideline similarly preserves a VKA-centered approach early after surgical bioprosthetic implantation and mitral or tricuspid repair, while allowing that a non-VKA oral anticoagulant may be considered within 3 months after surgical implantation of a mitral bioprosthesis in patients with AF (3).

However, this valve-guideline framing is not the entire story and, we would argue, represents the most important omission in the Paparella manuscript. The 2023 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/American College of Chest Physicians/Heart Rhythm Society (ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS) AF guideline recommends DOACs over VKAs in patients with AF and valve disease except in those with moderate or greater mitral stenosis or a mechanical heart valve. This recommendation is grounded in meta-analytic evidence demonstrating the consistent safety and efficacy of DOACs across AF patients with non-rheumatic, non-mechanical valvular pathology (4,5). Critically, the 2023 AF guideline does not carve out an exception for the early postoperative period. It extends its DOAC-over-VKA recommendation to bioprosthetic recipients without specifying a waiting interval after surgery, placing it in direct, unresolved tension with the valve-specific guidelines. The apparent contradiction is not a minor citation issue; it is the core clinical uncertainty that the LAAOS III substudy is uniquely positioned to address. AF guidelines are DOAC-forward because pivotal randomized trials and subsequent analyses support DOACs across most non-mechanical, non-rheumatic valve disease. Valve guidelines remain more cautious in the first postoperative months because newly implanted surgical prostheses and repair material introduce a distinct, time-dependent thrombogenic and bleeding milieu that was sparsely represented in those trials. The LAAOS III substudy should therefore be read not as contradicting either set of guidelines, but as helping to bridge the gap between them.

The headline result is reassuring. Anticoagulant class was not significantly associated with thromboembolism or major bleeding during follow-up. Using the time-dependent Cox model reported in Table 2, the estimates for VKA versus DOAC were not statistically significant for stroke or systemic thromboembolism (hazard ratio, 0.831; 95% confidence interval: 0.441–1.564; P=0.143) or major bleeding (hazard ratio, 0.842; 95% confidence interval: 0.448–1.158; P=0.197) (1). Although the point estimates were below 1.0, their imprecision and the nonrandomized exposure assignment are more important than their nominal direction. The appropriate conclusion is not that DOACs are superior, and not even that noninferiority has been proven. Rather, in this large, prospectively followed surgical cohort, DOAC use did not reveal an obvious thromboembolic or major bleeding penalty compared with VKA use.

Several design features strengthen the contribution. LAAOS III was an international randomized trial of surgical left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) in patients with AF undergoing cardiac surgery, and the parent trial demonstrated that surgical LAAO reduced ischemic stroke or systemic embolism on a background of usual antithrombotic care (6). A subsequent LAAOS III analysis highlighted that OAC use remained common after surgery and that surgical LAAO should be viewed as additive rather than as a replacement for anticoagulation in most patients with AF (7). Against that background, the current substudy benefits from prospective follow-up, adjudicated outcomes, repeated capture of anticoagulant exposure, and adjustment for LAAO as an intention-to-treat variable. Treating OAC as a time-dependent exposure is particularly important because postoperative anticoagulation is not static: patients cross over, discontinue therapy, restart therapy, accumulate antiplatelet indications, and experience changes in renal function, rhythm status, and bleeding risk over time.

The principal limitation is equally important: clinicians selected the anticoagulant. Patients discharged on DOACs were older, more often male, less likely to have rheumatic heart disease, more likely to undergo aortic valve replacement, less likely to undergo mitral valve replacement, and more likely to receive aspirin at discharge (1). These imbalances are clinically intuitive. Surgeons and cardiologists often reserve VKAs for phenotypes perceived as more thrombogenic or valve related, particularly rheumatic and mitral prosthetic disease. Multivariable adjustment and time-dependent modeling reduce measured confounding, but they cannot fully account for unmeasured clinical judgment. This is therefore best interpreted as a strong comparative effectiveness analysis nested within a trial, not as a dedicated randomized comparison of DOAC versus VKA after valve surgery.

The early postoperative window deserves the greatest caution in interpretation. The first 3 months after surgery combine prosthetic sewing rings, suture material, altered atrial geometry, atrial stunning, inflammation, transient postoperative arrhythmias, variable renal function, and frequent antiplatelet exposure. That is exactly the period about which clinicians most want reassurance and in which event counts become most limiting. In the LAAOS III substudy, among the 461 patients discharged on DOACs and 2,184 discharged on VKAs, 16 and 28 primary endpoint events occurred, respectively; major bleeding events numbered 28 and 49, respectively (1). Stated this way, the smaller DOAC denominator underscores why absolute event counts should not be interpreted as crude comparative rates. These counts are valuable but still modest, especially for procedure-specific and early landmark analyses. Absence of an evident hazard is encouraging, but it is not the same as definitive proof that all DOAC strategies are interchangeable with well-managed VKA therapy immediately after every surgical valve procedure.

The quality of the VKA comparator also matters and is easy to underestimate. Warfarin is familiar, inexpensive, measurable, and reversible, but it is genuinely difficult to manage after cardiac surgery. Amiodarone, antibiotics, fluctuating nutrition, hepatic congestion, renal dysfunction, temporary procedural interruptions, and discharge to rehabilitation can all destabilize anticoagulation in the weeks after a valve operation. Time in therapeutic range is therefore not a technical footnote; it is part of the treatment effect. A DOAC strategy is most compelling when VKA quality is expected to be poor or routine monitoring is impractical. The longitudinal treatment-pattern analysis reinforces this point: patients who crossed over or stopped OAC had numerically higher risks of stroke or systemic embolism and major bleeding than patients who remained on VKA, although these associations did not reach statistical significance (1). This finding should not be overinterpreted as causal; switching or stopping OAC may be a marker of bleeding, frailty, procedural interruptions, renal change, adherence barriers, or clinician concern. Still, it highlights that anticoagulation stability may be an important part of real-world effectiveness. Conversely, the absence of granular data on time in therapeutic range, DOAC dosing, dose-reduction appropriateness, and perioperative interruptions limits mechanistic interpretation of the observed similarity between treatment groups.

It is also essential not to extrapolate from this study to populations in which DOAC harm has already been demonstrated. Mechanical prosthetic valves remain firmly a VKA domain after the Evaluate the Safety and Pharmacokinetics of Oral Dabigatran Etexilate in Patients after Heart Valve Replacement (RE-ALIGN) study showed excess thromboembolic and bleeding complications with dabigatran in that setting (8). Likewise, rheumatic heart disease-associated AF, particularly with significant mitral stenosis, remains a distinct and high-risk entity after the Investigation of Rheumatic AF Treatment Using Vitamin K Antagonists, Rivaroxaban or Aspirin Studies (INVICTUS) demonstrated superior outcomes with VKA therapy compared with rivaroxaban (9). These groups are meaningfully different from patients with surgical bioprostheses or mitral repair and should not be merged under the older, overly broad label of ‘valvular AF’. The useful contribution of the current substudy is precisely that it focuses on a population of patients where genuine clinical equipoise exists: those that undergo valve surgery without a mechanical prosthesis.

For bioprosthetic valves and repair, the evidence is steadily accumulating in favor of selective DOAC use rather than categorical avoidance. The Rivaroxaban for Valvular Heart Disease and Atrial Fibrillation (RIVER) demonstrated that rivaroxaban was noninferior to warfarin in patients with AF and a bioprosthetic mitral valve, although relatively few participants were enrolled within 3 months of surgery (10). Edoxaban in Patients after Heart Valve Repair or Bioprosthetic Valve Replacement (ENAVLE), a randomized trial, suggested that edoxaban may be an effective alternative to warfarin during the early period after surgical bioprosthetic valve implantation or valve repair, though the sample size was small (11). The Edoxaban in Anticoagulant Therapy After Surgical Bioprosthetic Valve Replacement (ENBALV) trial added randomized evidence for edoxaban within 3 months after surgical bioprosthetic valve replacement, showing very low thromboembolic event rates but a numerically higher major bleeding rate with edoxaban (12). A recent valve-specific systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 studies including 15,877 patients similarly reported no significant differences in composite thromboembolic events, major bleeding, or all-cause mortality, while emphasizing that outcomes should be interpreted by valve position and procedure type (13). Subgroup analyses from the Effective Anticoagulation with Factor Xa Next Generation in Atrial Fibrillation Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction 48 (ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48) and the Apixaban for Reduction in Stroke and Other Thromboembolic Events in Atrial Fibrillation (ARISTOTLE) trial involving patients with prior bioprosthetic valves or valve repair have also been reassuring, though underpowered (14,15). The LAAOS III substudy extends this evidence base by adding a larger, surgically relevant cohort with longitudinal anticoagulation data and a broader procedural scope.

How should this influence practice today? For patients with AF after surgical bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement, bioprosthetic mitral valve replacement, or mitral repair, a DOAC may be reasonable at discharge in selected circumstances. The prerequisites are secure surgical hemostasis, renal function that supports appropriate dosing, acceptable drug interactions, and no mechanical valve or moderate-to-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis. This approach is especially attractive when international normalized ratio (INR) monitoring is logistically difficult, early postoperative INR control is likely to be unstable, prior DOAC therapy was effective and well tolerated, or patient preference strongly favors avoiding warfarin monitoring. VKA therapy remains appropriate when the clinician has specific concerns about very early prosthesis thrombosis, rheumatic substrate, severe renal dysfunction, major interacting medications, uncertainty about DOAC dose appropriateness, or when laboratory verification of anticoagulant intensity is desired.

Bleeding risk should be individualized rather than reduced to a drug class alone. Postoperative bleeding includes not only gastrointestinal or intracranial hemorrhage, but also pericardial bleeding, tamponade, wound complications, and bleeding that delays mobilization or rehabilitation. Antiplatelet therapy is a major modifier. In the substudy, aspirin use at discharge was common and more frequent among patients discharged on DOACs (1). Contemporary AF care increasingly emphasizes avoiding unnecessary long-term combinations of OAC and antiplatelet therapy. After valve surgery, that principle remains relevant: if aspirin or another antiplatelet agent is continued at discharge, the indication and intended duration should be explicit rather than carried forward by inertia.

The study also reinforces a separate point about LAAO. Surgical LAAO reduces stroke risk in selected patients with AF undergoing cardiac surgery, but it is not a license to stop anticoagulation in most patients who otherwise merit it (6,7). Paparella et al. appropriately adjusted for LAAO, but their substudy does not answer whether anticoagulation can safely be discontinued after surgical LAAO. The nonsignificant trend toward worse outcomes among patients who crossed over or stopped OAC should be treated with caution, as it likely reflects confounding by bleeding events, frailty, or clinical instability. Even so, it serves as a reminder that continuity and appropriateness of anticoagulation may matter as much as the choice of agent.

The next studies should move beyond class-level comparisons toward the precision this clinical problem demands. A definitive trial would randomize patients at discharge or early after surgery, stratify by valve position and procedure type, include mitral repair as a prespecified subgroup, capture renal function and DOAC dose appropriateness, measure VKA time in therapeutic range, and adjudicate clinical thromboembolism, major bleeding, pericardial bleeding, and imaging-defined valve thrombosis. It should also track antiplatelet exposure, reoperation, renal trajectory, frailty, patient-reported treatment burden, and monitoring feasibility. Because event rates are low, registry-randomized or pragmatic collaborative network designs may be required to reach adequate sample sizes.

Paparella and colleagues bring the field closer to equipoise. Their analysis narrows an evidence gap at the intersection of AF and valve-surgery guidelines and, in doing so, implicitly calls attention to the guideline contradiction that remains unresolved. The practical message is not that DOACs should replace VKAs for every patient during the first 3 months after surgical bioprosthetic valve replacement or mitral repair. The message is that, in carefully selected patients with AF, nonmechanical valve surgery, and no significant rheumatic mitral stenosis, DOAC therapy may be a reasonable therapeutic option. The path forward is not categorical avoidance or indiscriminate substitution, but structured patient-specific decision-making that accounts for valve type, valve position, rheumatic substrate, renal function, antiplatelet therapy, bleeding phenotype, anticoagulation continuity, and the patient's own capacity for monitoring.
