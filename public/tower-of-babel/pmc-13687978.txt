Clinical significance of ultra-low-amplitude electrograms during lattice-tip atrial mapping
Authors: Akinobu Mizutani, Masato Okada, Mebae Mizutani, Kohei Iwasa, Nobuaki Tanaka
Published: 2026

Raw electrogram review and selective manual re-annotation may reveal residual slow conduction pathways.•Reproducibility and spatial and temporal consistency with suspected conduction may indicate that ultra-low-amplitude electrograms are clinically relevant. Introduction

Contemporary high-density electroanatomical mapping systems have improved the efficiency and accuracy of atrial arrhythmia ablation by enabling rapid acquisition of voltage and activation maps.1 The Affera mapping system further streamlines this workflow by integrating high-density mapping and ablation into a single lattice-tip catheter, Sphere-9 (both Medtronic).2, 3, 4 This single-catheter platform facilitates a rapid transition from substrate characterization to targeted energy delivery using real-time voltage maps and automated activation maps based on local activation time (LAT) annotation.

However, the efficiency of automated mapping also requires careful attention to how ultra-low-amplitude electrograms are displayed, annotated, and interpreted. In our clinical experience, ultra-low-amplitude electrograms in the 0.02-mV range may remain discernible on raw electrogram tracings but may be incompletely represented on automated activation maps, particularly within regions displayed as very low voltage or apparent scar. This case series describes 3 Affera-guided procedures in which careful interpretation of such electrograms altered the interpretation of automated maps and subsequent procedural decision-making.

Case 1

A 78-year-old man with recurrent atrial fibrillation (AF) after prior pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) underwent repeat ablation using the Affera mapping system and the Sphere-9 lattice catheter. A 20-pole BeeAT catheter (Lifeline Co, Ltd) was positioned in the coronary sinus for reference and pacing. Baseline left atrial (LA) voltage mapping during high right atrial pacing demonstrated durable PVI and a low-voltage area along the LA roof (Figure 1A). Because the roof region appeared electrically blocked, posterior wall isolation (PWI) was empirically attempted by creating a bottom line using pulsed field ablation. However, repeat activation mapping demonstrated posterior wall activation with superior-to-inferior propagation, indicating residual roof conduction (Figure 1B). To clarify the underlying conduction pathway, the baseline mapping dataset shown in Figure 1A was re-examined. Review of raw electrograms using an increased display gain of 40 μV revealed fragmented ultra-low-amplitude electrograms with a peak-to-peak amplitude of 0.024 mV. Selective manual re-annotation of their activation times bridged the apparent activation gap and revealed a continuous pathway of markedly slow conduction across the roof region (Figure 1C). Thus, the apparent conduction block on the original automated map reflected collision of opposing wavefronts at the roof due to markedly delayed roof conduction, rather than true conduction block.

Targeted pulsed field ablation at this site eliminated the residual conduction and completed PWI. Perimitral atrial flutter was subsequently induced, and linear radiofrequency ablation was performed along the anterior line. Final voltage and activation mapping confirmed PWI and bidirectional block across the anterior line, with marked voltage attenuation in the ablated regions (Figure 1D).

Case 2

A 63-year-old woman with recurrent paroxysmal AF underwent a second ablation procedure using the Affera mapping system and the Sphere-9 lattice catheter. A 20-pole BeeAT catheter was positioned in the coronary sinus for reference and pacing. Baseline voltage and activation mapping demonstrated durable PVI but reconnection of the prior PWI, with a superior-to-inferior activation pattern across the posterior wall (Figure 2A). After roof-line ablation, repeat automated activation and voltage maps suggested completion of PWI (Figure 2B). However, during systematic mapping, a fragmented ultra-low-amplitude electrogram was reproducibly recorded at a localized site near the posterior carina of the right pulmonary veins. Although this signal was not displayed on either automated map, high-gain review demonstrated a peak-to-peak amplitude of 0.018 mV (Figure 2C).

Because the electrogram was reproducibly recorded at the same localized site, pacing was performed from the Sphere-9 catheter to assess exit conduction from the posterior wall. Pacing from this site reproducibly captured the atrium, whereas slight catheter movement to a nearby site resulted in loss of atrial capture despite continued pacing, demonstrating site-specific residual exit conduction (Supplemental Figure 1). Targeted pulsed field ablation at this site eliminated the residual electrogram, and subsequent pacing from the posterior wall no longer captured the atrium, supporting completion of exit block. Final voltage and activation mapping confirmed PWI (Figure 2D).

Case 3

An 83-year-old woman with recurrent AF and atrial tachycardia (AT) underwent a third ablation procedure using the Affera mapping system and the Sphere-9 lattice catheter. Prior procedures included PVI, cavotricuspid isthmus ablation, PWI, and an anterior line. Baseline mapping during high right atrial pacing confirmed durable PVI, cavotricuspid isthmus block, and PWI. The automated activation map showed a prominent region without LAT annotation near the prior anterior line, suggesting apparent conduction block. However, review of raw electrograms revealed fragmented ultra-low-amplitude electrograms in this region, with a local peak-to-peak amplitude of 0.026 mV (Figure 3A). Subsequently, AT with a cycle length of 320 milliseconds was induced by burst pacing from the coronary sinus. During AT, the automated activation map again showed an apparent conduction block in the same region (Figure 3C). Review of raw electrograms demonstrated similar fragmented ultra-low-amplitude electrograms with a peak-to-peak amplitude of 0.026 mV. Manual re-annotation during tachycardia revealed the underlying activation sequence and identified this slow conduction pathway as part of the macroreentrant circuit (Figure 3D). Targeted pulsed field ablation at this site terminated the tachycardia, and final mapping confirmed elimination of local potentials and bidirectional block across the anterior line.

Discussion

Advanced 3-dimensional electroanatomical mapping systems and high-density multielectrode catheters have improved the efficiency and consistency of activation and voltage mapping.1 However, automated maps remain dependent on signal amplitude, annotation algorithms, and display settings. Therefore, areas displayed as very low voltage, apparent scar, or regions without LAT annotation should be interpreted in conjunction with the underlying electrograms. The present case series highlights that reproducible ultra-low-amplitude electrograms in the 0.02-mV range may have functional significance when they are spatially and temporally consistent with suspected residual conduction.

A relevant technical feature of the Affera system is the Sphere-9 close-unipolar configuration, in which electrograms are derived between the 0.7-mm mini-surface electrodes and a central internal reference.2 This configuration is designed to reduce far-field components. In a previous first-in-human study of the lattice-tip system, the electrogram noise level during baseline LA mapping was reported to be 0.009 ± 0.003 mV.2 Although corresponding noise-level data are not available for the current Affera system, this value provides useful context for the present observations. In the present cases, the residual electrograms measured 0.018–0.026 mV. These amplitudes exceeded the previously reported mean noise level for the lattice-tip system but remained extremely low and potentially difficult to distinguish from background noise. Therefore, amplitude alone cannot establish the functional significance of such electrograms. Voltage-per-second filtering on the Affera system has recently been shown to identify fractionated near-field electrograms and residual conduction channels within low-voltage areas.5 In that report, electrogram amplitudes at conduction gaps were 0.14 (0.06–0.26) mV, whereas the electrograms identified in the present cases were substantially smaller. Despite their markedly lower amplitudes, these electrograms were reproducible, temporally and spatially consistent with suspected residual conduction, and eliminated by targeted ablation. In 1 case, site-specific posterior wall pacing further demonstrated residual exit conduction. These findings supported the functional significance of the ultra-low-amplitude electrograms.

The present cases highlight that automated activation and voltage maps should be interpreted together with the underlying electrograms. Discrepancies among these sources of information should not be dismissed as simple mapping artifacts, because they may provide clues to ultra-low-amplitude local electrograms or residual conduction. Importantly, even concordant absence of LAT annotations and displayed voltage points does not necessarily exclude residual conduction. In case 1, limited automated activation continuity near the roof region prompted raw electrogram review and manual re-annotation, which revealed residual slow conduction. In case 2, neither an LAT annotation nor a corresponding voltage map point was displayed at the clinically relevant site, whereas a reproducible, ultra-low-amplitude electrogram on the live display and site-specific posterior wall pacing demonstrated residual exit conduction. In case 3, manual re-annotation of ultra-low-amplitude electrograms bridged an apparent activation gap and revealed a slow conduction pathway participating in the macroreentrant circuit. Such discrepancies should be regarded as prompts for further evaluation rather than definitive evidence of residual conduction, because ultra-low-amplitude signals may also represent noise, far-field activity, annotation error, or nonrepresentative electrograms. Therefore, raw electrogram review, selective manual re-annotation, and site-specific pacing may provide complementary confirmation when residual conduction is suspected.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a small single-center case series, and the frequency and clinical impact of ultra-low-amplitude electrograms remain to be determined in a broader patient population. Second, the amplitude and morphology of ultra-low-amplitude electrograms may be influenced by recording conditions, bandpass filter settings, and catheter–tissue contact; therefore, amplitude alone should not be used to determine whether residual conduction is present. Third, although high-gain electrogram review and manual re-annotation were useful in these cases, the optimal display settings and reproducibility of this approach across different mappers and institutions require further validation. Finally, the Affera system and its annotation algorithms continue to evolve, and future software modifications may influence the detection and display of such electrograms.

Conclusion

In this case series, ultra-low-amplitude electrograms in the 0.02-mV range were identified during Affera-guided lattice-tip atrial mapping. These electrograms were observed within regions displayed as very low voltage or incompletely represented on automated activation maps. Careful review of raw electrograms, selective manual re-annotation, and site-specific pacing helped identify residual conduction despite apparent scar or conduction block and guided targeted ablation. These findings emphasize the importance of verifying automated activation and voltage map interpretation with raw electrogram assessment when residual conduction or incomplete linear block is suspected.
