Impact of sex on outcomes in critically ill patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage: a secondary analysis of a French prospective cohort study
Authors: Sylvain Gourier, Marwan Bouras, Karim Lakhal, Yoann Launey, Karelle Leon, Marc Laffon, Claire Dahyot-Fizelier, Antoine Roquilly, Sigismond Lasocki, Olivier Langeron, Anaïs Caillard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) remains a severe condition with high morbidity and mortality. Evidence on sex-related differences in outcomes and complication profiles is inconsistent. We assessed the association between sex and 90-day functional outcome and ICU complications in aSAH patients requiring intensive care.MethodsWe performed a retrospective secondary analysis of the ATLANREA cohort (NCT02426255), a prospective multicenter ICU registry in western France. Adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted with aSAH between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2022, requiring orotracheal intubation within 24 h and for ≥ 24 h, were included. The primary outcome was favourable neurological status at day 90 (Glasgow Outcome Scale–Extended [GOS-E] > 4). A prespecified baseline multivariable logistic model included sex, age, WFNS grade, collapsed Fisher grade and mydriasis. Age subgroup analyses (≤ 42, 43–50, > 50 years) were prespecified as exploratory and complemented by a formal sex × age interaction test.ResultsAmong 865 patients, 540 (62.4%) were women. Women were older than men (58 [IQR 49–66] vs. 54 [45–63] years; p < 0.001), while WFNS and Fisher grades were similar by sex. Ninety-day GOS-E was available for 682/869 patients (78.8%). Favourable outcome rates were similar in women and men (42.2% vs. 41.4%; p = 0.90). In the baseline complete-case model, sex was not significantly associated with favourable outcome (male OR 0.745, 95% CI 0.517–1.067; p = 0.11), whereas age, higher WFNS grades and mydriasis were independently associated with worse outcome. Sensitivity analyses (best-/worst-case imputation and multiple imputation) suggested a modest sex effect under some assumptions. Ordinal modelling of 8-level GOS-E yielded consistent findings (male OR 0.767, 95% CI 0.568–1.035; p = 0.083). There was no evidence of sex × age interaction (p = 0.585). Vasospasm was more frequent in women (48.9% vs. 41.6%; p = 0.048), whereas men had more secondary pulmonary infections (34.4% vs. 18.9%; p = 0.008).ConclusionsAlthough sex was not an independent predictor of 90-day neurological outcome in this multicenter cohort of intubated ICU patients with severe aSAH, women were older and had slightly higher vasospasm rates but fewer systemic complications, particularly infections. These findings support incorporating sex and age into future prognostic work and underscore the importance of systemic complications in recovery after severe aSAH.Trial registrationATLANREA cohort, www.clinicaltrials.gov, NCT02426255.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s44158-026-00419-2.Keywords: Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, Sex difference, Outcomes, Vasospasm, Hospital-acquired pneumonia, Intensive care

Background

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is a life-threatening neurological emergency that affects approximately 10 individuals per 100,000 annually and remains associated with high rates of morbidity and mortality [1]. Survivors frequently experience long-term disability, and neurological outcomes are shaped by multiple factors, including the initial severity of hemorrhage, timeliness of aneurysm securing, and the occurrence of secondary complications such as hydrocephalus, cerebral vasospasm, and delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) [2, 3].

Among these prognostic determinants, biological sex has emerged as a potentially important, yet underexplored, modifier of disease trajectory in aSAH [4]. Women are more frequently affected by aSAH [4, 5]. In a recent meta-analysis of 33 studies (> 47,000 aSAH patients), women had higher risk of DCI and poorer long-term outcomes than men, whereas mortality did not differ; however, between-study heterogeneity for DCI was substantial [4]. Likewise, women over 50 years, likely postmenopausal, showed higher rates of DCI, vasospasm and worse 6-month outcomes, suggesting a loss of estrogen’s protective effect [5]. However, as the cohort included only Asian patients, the results may have limited generalizability to non-Asian populations due to potential ethnic and genetic differences [5]. Furthermore, some studies suggest that respiratory and infectious complications during the intensive care unit (ICU) stay also vary by sex [6, 7]. Overall, studies report conflicting results, and the role of sex in ICU course and long-term outcomes remains unclear [4–6, 8, 9].

We aimed to assess whether biological sex is independently associated with 90-day neurological outcomes in patients admitted to the ICU for aSAH. Secondary objectives included sex-based comparisons of ICU management and major complications—neurological, infectious, respiratory and cardiovascular—as well as the influence of patient age.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective secondary analysis of prospectively collected data from the ATLANREA cohort, a multicenter, non-interventional, observational study conducted in ICUs across various metropolises in western of France. The cohort was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifier NCT02426255. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the French Society of Anesthesia and Intensive Care (SFAR—N° IRB 00010254–2025—046) according to the French law [10]. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for observational research (Supplementary) [11].

Patients

We included all adult patients (≥ 18 years) with aSAH admitted between January 1, 2013 and December 31, 2022, to ICUs within the ATLANREA network in western France (Brest, Rennes, Nantes, Angers, and Poitiers). Eligibility required orotracheal intubation within the first 24 h of ICU admission, maintained for at least 24 h; aSAH was confirmed by brain CT. Age subgroup analyses were prespecified as exploratory. Subgroup analyses considered three age groups: ≤ 42 years, 43–50 years and > 50 years. Age thresholds were defined a priori: the upper cutoff approximated the typical age at natural menopause, and the lower cutoff corresponded to the onset of the perimenopausal transition, to account for potential hormone-related effects in women [12–14].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients with a favourable neurological outcome at day 90 (D90), defined as a Glasgow Outcome Scale–Extended (GOS-E) [15, 16] score greater than 4 [17, 18]. D90 GOSE-E is systematically assessed by research technician at each centre by phone call interviews. Secondary outcomes included the GOS-E score at ICU discharge, ICU and 90-day all-cause mortality and the occurrence of major complications (neurological, respiratory, infectious and cardiovascular).

Data collection

Data were extracted retrospectively from the prospectively maintained ATLANREA database to assess clinical presentation, management, complications and outcomes according to biological sex. Collected baseline variables included sex, age, body mass index and comorbidities. Initial severity was assessed using radiological scores (Fisher score [19]) and clinical measures such as the Glasgow Coma Scale and WFNS (World Federation of Neurologic Surgeons) score [20].

Management-related data included neurosurgical (hematoma evacuation, extraventricular drain, aneurysmal clipping) and interventional neuroradiology procedures, as well as neurocritical care interventions (sedation strategies, osmotherapy and intracranial pressure monitoring). Early complications were predefined and abstracted as follows: hemodynamic failure—sustained hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg or mean arterial pressure < 65 mmHg) and/or cardiac arrest, or the need for vasopressors (duration recorded) or red-blood-cell transfusion; acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)—per Berlin definition [21]; acute kidney injury requiring renal replacement therapy—initiation of any dialysis modality; stress cardiomyopathy—left-ventricular ejection fraction < 50% in the absence of known structural heart disease; neurological complications—rebleeding (new intracranial hemorrhage on CT), vasospasm (defined by vasospasm on CT angiography) and delayed cerebral ischemia (new cerebral infarction on follow-up CT); thromboembolic events—ultrasound-confirmed deep-vein thrombosis or CT-angiography–confirmed pulmonary embolism; hemorrhagic complications—clinically significant extracranial bleeding requiring intervention and/or transfusion; infections—ventilator-associated pneumonia, surgical-site or meningeal infection and catheter-related infections. All variables are listed in Table S1 (Supplemental). Outcome variables included the duration of mechanical ventilation, duration of vasopressor use, ICU and hospital length of stay, mortality rates and decisions to limit or withdraw life-sustaining therapies.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were described as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range], depending on their distribution assessed by Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Univariate analyses were conducted to identify variables associated with neurological outcomes and with sex. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical variables, and Student’s t test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, as appropriate. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The same analytical approach was applied to secondary outcomes.

To identify baseline variables independently associated with a favourable neurological outcome at day 90, a multivariable logistic regression model was constructed. Variables selected a priori based on literature and clinical relevance included age, WFNS score at admission, Fisher grade and pupillary reactivity [1, 5, 22–24]. Sex was used into the model, given the study’s primary objective. Fisher grade was collapsed a priori into I–II versus III–IV for multivariable analyses. In addition, we fitted a secondary exploratory model including in-ICU complications (intracranial hypertension, rebleeding, cerebral ischemia, infection, seizures, venous thrombosis) to describe associations during the clinical course.

Effect modification by age was assessed by including a sex–age interaction term (age as a continuous variable) and comparing models with and without interaction using a likelihood ratio test (LRT).

Missing outcome data at day 90 were handled using a prespecified strategy. We performed a complete-case analysis as the primary approach and conducted sensitivity analyses, including two extreme-case scenarios (all missing outcomes imputed as unfavourable and, alternatively, as favourable) and multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE; 20 imputations, 20 iterations). For multiple imputation, the binary outcome was imputed using logistic regression; continuous variables using predictive mean matching; and categorical variables using polytomous regression. Regression coefficients were pooled using Rubin’s rules. We additionally performed a prespecified sensitivity analysis modelling the full 8-level 90-day GOS-E using a proportional-odds ordinal logistic regression (complete cases) [25]. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with decisions to limit life-sustaining therapy (LST). As a sensitivity analysis to account for centre-level clustering and practice variability, we fitted a mixed-effects logistic regression model including a random intercept for centre.

The primary model’s discriminative ability was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), reported with its 95% confidence interval. Analyses were performed on complete cases without imputation.

All analyses were conducted using RStudio software (RStudio 2021.09.0 + 351).

Results

Population

Between January 2013 and December 2022, we identified a total of 865 patients with aSAH (Fig. 1), including 540 (62.4%) women and 325 (37.6%) men (Table 1). Women were slightly older than men, with a median age of 58 [IQR: 49–66] years compared to 54 [IQR: 45–63] years for men (p < 0.001). Similarly, the body mass index was significantly lower in women: 24.22 [IQR: 21.45–27.75] kg/m2 versus 25.61 [IQR: 22.91–28.73] kg/m2 in men (p < 0.001). Comorbidities such as heart failure, chronic kidney disease or chronic respiratory disease did not differ significantly between sexes. In contrast, cardiovascular risk factors were more prevalent among men, with higher rates of type II diabetes (6.2% vs. 2.0%, p = 0.006), alcohol use disorders (26.4% vs. 9.5%) and active smoking (50.0% vs. 32.7%) compared to women (p < 0.001 for the latter two).

Regarding initial severity, the Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS) II score was significantly higher in women (median: 48 [IQR: 37–56] vs. 46 [IQR: 34–55]; p = 0.007). However, there were no statistically significant differences between sexes in SOFA (Sepsis-related Organ Failure Assessment) or Glasgow scores (Table 1). The distribution of WFNS grades and Fisher classifications—used to assess the clinical and radiological severity of hemorrhage—was similar between men and women. A notable difference was observed in aneurysm location (p = 0.008): men more frequently presented with anterior communicating artery aneurysms (34.7% vs. 26.1%), whereas posterior cerebral artery aneurysms were more common in women (11.9% vs. 7.4%). In contrast, the incidence of intracerebral hematoma, mydriasis and other aneurysm locations did not significantly differ between sexes.

Primary outcome: favourable neurological outcome at day 90 (GOS-E > 90)

Data on the 90-day GOS-E were complete for 682 (78.8%) patients (Fig. 1). Among the assessed patients, 177 (42.2%) of women and 109 (41.4%) of men had a favourable outcome (p = 0.9). Figure 2 shows the distribution of GOSE scores according to sex. Table 2 presents the univariate analyses of variables associated with a favourable neurological outcome at day 90 (D90). To identify independent predictors of favourable neurological outcome at 90 days (GOS-E > 4), we performed a multivariate logistic using previously described methods. Sex was not significantly associated with neurological outcomes in complete-case analysis (male sex, OR 0.745, 95% CI 0.517–1.067; p = 0.11) (Table 3). Increasing age (OR 0.976, 95% CI 0.962–0.990; p = 0.00098), higher WFNS grades (e.g. WFNS V: OR 0.112, 95% CI 0.049–0.232; p = 2.0 × 10−8), and mydriasis (OR 0.358, 95% CI 0.214–0.580; p = 4.96 × 10−5) were independently associated with lower odds of favourable outcome. Collapsed Fisher grade (III–IV vs. I–II) was not associated with outcome (OR 0.768, 95% CI 0.293–1.941; p = 0.58). Multicollinearity was formally assessed using generalized variance inflation factors (VIF) and no meaningful collinearity was detected (Table S2—supplemental). The multivariable logistic regression model used to evaluate factors associated with favourable neurological outcome at day 90 showed good discriminative performance, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.727 (95% CI: 0.687–0.767) (Fig. S1—supplemental) (Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the primary findings. First, modelling the full 8-level GOSE at day 90 using a proportional-odds ordinal logistic regression (complete cases) yielded conclusions consistent with the primary dichotomized analysis (Table S3—supplemental). A total of 187/869 (21%) patients had missing 90-day GOS-E. Characteristics and factors associated with missing data are shown in Tables 4 and 5 (supplemental). Missing 90-day outcomes were explored and handled using a prespecified strategy (complete-case analysis, extreme-case scenarios and multiple imputation); across approaches, associations for key baseline severity markers remained consistent, while the estimated association between sex and outcome varied modestly depending on missing-data assumptions (Table S6—supplemental). To quantify the risk of type II error, the minimum detectable effect size (80% power, α = 0.05) for sex was approximately OR ≤ 0.63 (or ≥ 1.56) in the overall complete-case sample (Table S7—supplemental). Excluding patients with limitation of life-sustaining therapy and accounting for centre-level clustering did not materially alter effect estimates or the overall interpretation (Tables S8–S9—supplemental). An exploratory model including in-ICU complications produced similar conclusions regarding sex, and identified several in-ICU events (intracranial hypertension, rebleeding, cerebral ischemia, venous thrombosis) as being associated with outcome (Table S10—supplemental).

Sex-related differences in complications and treatment

The results revealed significant sex-based differences in neurological complications (Table 4). Although the incidence of hydrocephalus was comparable between the two groups, women had a significantly shorter median duration of external ventricular drainage compared to men (15 days [10.00–21.75] vs. 19 days [13.50–25.00], p = 0.005). Additionally, women more frequently underwent hematoma evacuation (7.8% vs. 3.3%, p = 0.023) and exhibited a higher rate of vasospasm that required specific treatment (48.9% vs. 41.6%, p = 0.048). In contrast, no significant difference was observed between sexes regarding the incidence of DCI.

Infectious complications are detailed in Table 4. Women had a significantly higher number of antibiotic-free days compared to men (14 days [6.00–22.00] vs. 11 days [4.00–20.00], p = 0.001). In contrast, men showed a higher incidence of secondary pulmonary infections (34.4% vs. 18.9%, p = 0.008), a longer duration of mechanical ventilation (15 days [5.00–26.00] vs. 12 days [4.00–23.00], p = 0.032) and a more frequent use of tracheostomy (7.8% vs. 4.1%, p = 0.032), indicating a more severe respiratory course in male patients.

The mortality rate was 38.1% at day 90, again with no significant sex-based difference D90 mortality was 40.3% in females and 34.6% in males (OR = 1.17, females vs. males; 95% CI: 0.87–1.59; p = 0.306). At ICU discharge, GOS were favourable in 43.1% of patients, without significant sex-related difference (p = 0.560). In contrast, the ICU length of stay differed significantly between sexes: men had a longer median ICU stay (22 days [IQR: 9–31]) than women (18 days [IQR: 9–29]), with this difference reaching statistical significance (p = 0.026).

Decisions to limit or withdraw life-sustaining therapies (LST) were performed in 222 patients (26.0%), with no significant difference between women (26.4%) and men (25.5%) (p = 0.826). The median time from ICU admission to therapeutic limitation was 13 days [IQR: 5–22], similar across sexes (p = 0.824). Among patients with LST decisions, withdrawal of LST was more frequent (56.6%) than withholding (43.4%), with no significant sex-based differences in distribution (p = 0.493).

Subgroup analysis according to age

In the ≤ 42-year subgroup (n = 127), baseline demographic characteristics, comorbidities and initial clinical/radiological severity were broadly similar between women and men. In this subgroup, the proportion with a favourable 90-day neurological outcome (GOS-E > 4) did not differ significantly by sex (Table 5). During the ICU stay, women developed ARDS less frequently (22.1% vs. 43.1%, p = 0.019), and underwent tracheostomy less often (1.5% vs. 12.5%, p = 0.034). Women experienced an earlier onset of vasospasm (median 5 days vs. 8 days, p = 0.043). Their functional outcomes at ICU discharge were significantly better, with 73.4% achieving a favourable GOS-E score compared to 43.6% of men (p = 0.002), and their 90-day survival rate was also higher (83.3% vs. 64.0%, p = 0.043). Among patients over 50 years old (n = 582), women (65.6%) presented with less severe forms of aSAH, as indicated by significantly lower Fisher scores (p = 0.018) and required shorter mechanical ventilation (median 13 vs. 16 days, p = 0.004) and shorter ICU stays (19 vs. 23 days, p = 0.022). There was no evidence of effect modification by age (sex × age interaction LRT p = 0.585; interaction term OR = 1.008, 95% CI 0.979–1.038), indicating that the association between sex and 90-day outcome did not differ significantly across age. Age-stratified findings were therefore interpreted as exploratory (Table S11—supplemental).

Discussion

In this large prospective multicenter cohort of severe aSAH, no sex-based differences were observed in initial clinical or radiological severity or in 90-day outcomes. In sub-group analysis, among patients under 42 years old (pre-menopausal period for women), mortality was significantly lower in women in spite of similar initial severity.

In this multicenter cohort restricted to intubated ICU patients with severe aSAH, the complete-case baseline model did not demonstrate a statistically significant association between sex and 90-day favourable outcome, although point estimates were directionally consistent with lower odds in men. Missing-data sensitivity analyses (best-case and multiple imputation) suggested at most a modest association, supporting the interpretation that any sex effect—if present—may be small and dependent on missingness assumptions. Between-centre heterogeneity was moderate, and accounting for centre did not alter the overall interpretation.

Our findings show that despite older age and slightly higher overall severity among women (SAPS II), mortality and functional outcomes did not significantly differ between sexes. These results are in line with recent multicenter studies [5, 26], which suggest that sex is not an independent prognostic factor after adjustment. Conversely, our data contrast with those of Cai et al., who reported worse outcomes among female patients, likely related to a higher incidence of cerebral ischemia [27]. Zhang et al. also demonstrated that women had a higher risk of developing DCI [28]. In our cohort, women had a slightly higher observed rate of vasospasm, consistent with existing literature identifying female sex as a risk factor [29, 30]; however, this did not translate into an increased incidence of DCI. This decoupling reinforces the increasingly recognized notion that vasospasm, while a key risk marker [31], is not systematically associated with clinically significant ischemia. Neuroinflammation is increasingly viewed as a contributor to vasospasm biology [32]; because immune responses differ by sex [9, 33, 34], this could contribute to a higher vasospasm signal in women. Because vasospasm was defined radiologically in the database and diagnostic practices may vary across centres, a degree of detection bias cannot be excluded when interpreting sex differences in vasospasm.

Interestingly, age appeared to significantly modulate sex-related clinical trajectories and outcome. We prespecified three age strata (≤ 42, 43–50 and > 50 years) to approximate stages of reproductive aging and to avoid data-driven thresholds [12–14]. The 43–50-year group was intended to capture the perimenopausal transition, during which ovarian hormone levels fluctuate markedly; the > 50-year cutoff approximates the typical age at natural menopause, and ≤ 42 years reflects predominantly premenopausal status [12–14]. Nonetheless, these age bands remain proxies for hormonal status and may misclassify some women. A key limitation is that the database did not capture direct indicators of hormonal exposure—last menstrual period, surgical menopause, menopausal hormone therapy or use of hormonal contraception—precluding assessment of how endogenous or exogenous hormones might have contributed to the observed patterns. Among patients under 42 years old in our cohort, women had notably better outcomes. They experienced an earlier onset of vasospasm (median 5 vs. 8 days, p = 0.043), which may reflect a more reactive vascular profile. Yet this earlier vasospasm was not associated with worse outcomes. On the contrary, younger women had markedly superior short- and mid-term prognoses, with 73.4% achieving favourable functional status at ICU discharge (vs. 43.6% of men, p = 0.002), and significantly higher 90-day survival (83.3% vs. 64.0%, p = 0.043). However, we found no evidence of a sex × age interaction on the primary outcome (LRT p = 0.585), and younger age strata were substantially underpowered to detect anything other than very large sex effects; therefore, subgroup results should be considered hypothesis-generating and should not be over-interpreted as reflecting distinct age-specific physiological mechanisms. Our findings are also in line with experimental evidence suggesting that estrogen and other sex hormones in premenopausal women may modulate cerebral autoregulation, inflammatory response and endothelial function—factors known to impact DCI susceptibility and recovery [35, 36]. Experimental and clinical studies suggest that estrogen confers vascular protection through multiple mechanisms, including modulation of endothelial function, reduction of oxidative stress, inhibition of vascular smooth muscle proliferation and attenuation of inflammatory responses within the arterial wall [37, 38]. These effects may contribute to the stability of intracranial vessel architecture and reduce the likelihood of aneurysm formation and/or rupture. Epidemiological data reinforce this biological hypothesis. In a large prospective study, an earlier age at menopause and a shorter overall reproductive lifespan were independently associated with an increased risk of aSAH in women, suggesting that reduced cumulative estrogen exposure over the life course is a significant risk factor for aneurysmal rupture [39]. A recent study showed that the risk of aSAH was increased by early menopause, early puberty, shorter reproductive lifespan, younger age at first birth and hysterectomy or ovariectomy. Other factors, such as contraceptive use or number of births, were not associated with SAH risk [40]. Given the observational nature of our subgroup findings and limited power, these physiological interpretations are presented cautiously and as hypotheses for future prospective, hormone-informed studies (Fig. 2).

This study provides novel evidence for sex-specific susceptibility to respiratory complications after aSAH, with men showing higher rates of prolonged mechanical ventilation, pulmonary infections and tracheostomy. Unlike prior cohorts, which have not specifically examined respiratory infections in aSAH, our findings highlight an underrecognized trajectory that may inform sex-tailored management. Male patients experienced a more severe respiratory course, yet these differences are not systematically addressed in recent large cohorts [5, 8, 26]. Male sex may represent an independent risk factor, potentially mediated by longer ventilation, higher rates of substance use and sex-related immune and inflammatory differences [34, 41]. The central nervous system—lung crosstalk is increasingly recognized [42, 43], as aSAH may trigger neurogenic pulmonary dysfunction dependence [44–46]. The higher number of antibiotic-free days in women may be compatible with a lower infectious burden, although this metric can also be influenced by prescribing practices and competing risks and is inherently correlated with ICU length of stay and exposure time. It could nonetheless align with previously described sex-related differences in immune responses, including potential immunomodulatory effects of estrogens [9, 33, 34]. Conversely, in critically ill male patients, pneumonia and ARDS have been associated in some studies with worse outcomes, which may partly relate to sex-specific immune and endothelial phenotypes and to differences in sex hormone milieu, but causal inferences cannot be drawn from our observational data [47, 48].

A preliminary finding of our study highlights a sex-related difference in aneurysm location, with anterior communicating artery (AComA) aneurysms more frequent in men. Two studies a decade apart reported anatomical and radiological sex-related differences. In healthy subjects assessed by 16-slice CT angiography, women more often had AComA aplasia, while men more frequently presented with a typical arterial configuration (59.7% vs. 46%, p < 0.01), potentially predisposing them to aneurysm formation at this site [49]. A 10-year aSAH study using 128-slice CT angiography further supported these findings: women more often had multiple small aneurysms, whereas men more frequently presented with larger, often fusiform aneurysms [50]. Beyond anatomical variation, baseline vascular risk profiles differed by sex in our cohort, with higher rates of current smoking, alcohol use disorder and type II diabetes in men. These differences could plausibly contribute to sex-specific patterns of aneurysm development and rupture site through long-term vascular remodelling and hemodynamic stress [51].

Compared to other published cohorts, our study stands out due to its large sample size and a predominance of female patients (62.1%), consistent with international epidemiological data [5, 26, 27]. Although the only other multicenter study included a significantly larger cohort (n = 5407), it was restricted to an Asian population, which may limit direct comparability with our cohort [5]. Moreover, the patients in that study generally presented with a similar initial severity—predominantly low WFNS scores. Treatment modalities also differ considerably: in our cohort of ICU patients who required orotracheal intubation initiated within the first 24 h and maintained for at least 24 h, most were managed with endovascular procedures, whereas the proportion of conventional surgical clipping remains higher in some Asian series, reflecting potentially different interventional practices that may influence postoperative morbidity and mortality [5, 26, 52].

This study is limited by its retrospective design, which may introduce selection and information biases, particularly due to missing outcome data. Missing 90-day GOS-E assessments may have biased estimates; missingness was associated with mydriasis, supporting the use of multiple imputation and sensitivity analyses, although residual bias cannot be fully excluded. Decisions to limit life-sustaining therapy are major determinants of mortality and functional outcome and may act as downstream variables influenced by severity and clinical evolution; they may therefore mediate or modify observed associations, and conditioning on LST can also introduce selection/collider bias [53]. We addressed this through sensitivity analyses excluding patients with LST decisions; however, these analyses rely on a restricted population and should be interpreted cautiously. The youngest age strata were underpowered to detect small-to-moderate sex effects, increasing the risk of type II error; thus, null findings in age-stratified analyses should not be interpreted as evidence of absence of effect. The reliance on clinical documentation for complications may have led to underreporting, and the absence of long-term neurocognitive follow-up limits functional assessment. In addition, the database did not capture granular information on the timing and intensity of several management decisions (e.g. sedation regimens, ventilator settings, imaging frequency and exact timing/dose of specific therapies) or detailed centre-level practice patterns; residual confounding by unmeasured management differences cannot be excluded.

The predominance of endovascular treatment in our cohort may affect generalizability to settings with different aSAH management practices, particularly in other countries. Lastly, generalizability is limited because our inclusion criteria selected a particularly severe ICU population (patients requiring early and prolonged invasive mechanical ventilation); therefore, these findings may not apply to the broader spectrum of aSAH patients, including those managed without intubation.

Conclusion

Although sex was not an independent predictor of 90-day neurological outcome in this large multicenter cohort of severe ICU-admitted patients with aSAH requiring at least 24 h of invasive mechanical ventilation, sex- and age-specific patterns were observed. Women were older and appeared to have slightly higher vasospasm rates, yet had comparable neurological outcomes and fewer systemic complications, particularly infections. These findings support the integration of sex and age into future prognostic models and highlight the need to consider systemic complications when assessing recovery after severe aSAH.
