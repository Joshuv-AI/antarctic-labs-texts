Engraftment of sheep splenic lymphocytes into NBSGW mice and application in Brucella infection
Authors: Xiaowei Wang, Shicheng Wan, Yanmin Guo, Hongyu Han, Fei Luo, Dongmei Jiang, Xiaorong Yang, Quanhai Pang, Haisheng Yu, Jinlian Hua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe development of immune reconstituted xenograft mouse models has addressed the limitations imposed by interspecies immunological differences in disease research. However, mouse models reconstituted with a sheep immune system remain largely unexplored.MethodsIn this study, we successfully established a sheep splenocyte‐reconstituted mouse model (Sh‐Spl mice) by intravenously transplanting ovine splenocytes into severely immunodeficient NBSGW mice, and evaluated the efficiency of immune reconstitution.ResultsResults showed that ovine‐derived CD45+, CD4+, and CD8+ cells were detectable in the peripheral blood of Sh‐Spl mice for an extended period (up to 13 weeks), and these cells were also distributed in the spleen, liver, and bone marrow. Furthermore, upon intraperitoneal infection with Brucella melitensis, Sh‐Spl mice exhibited characteristic pathological alterations and bacterial colonization, confirming the successful establishment of the infection model.ConclusionsThe sheep immune system‐reconstituted mouse model developed in this study, combined with subsequent pathogen challenge, provides a novel experimental platform for investigating the pathogenesis of sheep‐hosted pathogens and developing prevention and control strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Xenogeneic immune reconstitution models serve as crucial tools for investigating species‐specific host‐pathogen interactions. Their core objective is to reconstitute the immune components of a specific host within experimental animals, thereby overcoming the research limitations of traditional models imposed by interspecies differences. Furthermore, since certain pathogens are refractory to both wild‐type mice and common immunodeficient mouse strains, constructing models with the immune microenvironment of the natural host facilitates the establishment of an effective infection system in experimental animals. 1 In the field of xenogeneic immune cell transplantation, early studies have confirmed that heterologous immune cells can achieve functional engraftment in suitable immunodeficient mouse strains. For instance, a comparative study demonstrated that porcine bone marrow cells exhibit superior engraftment capacity in NOD‐SCID mice compared to other immunodeficient strains. 2 In addition, studies have reported that bovine peripheral blood lymphocytes transplanted into SCID mice successfully established a bovine immune cell chimeric mouse model sustained for up to 13 weeks, providing a reliable experimental platform for investigating bovine‐specific infectious diseases. 3 With the advancement of technologies, humanized immune mouse models have been extensively applied in the research of human infectious diseases and immunology, demonstrating remarkable advantages. 4 , 5 , 6 However, for sheep, an important livestock animal and natural host for various pathogens, targeted immune reconstitution animal models remain an unaddressed research gap, which has hindered in‐depth investigations into sheep host‐specific diseases such as brucellosis. Recent single‐cell transcriptomic studies in goats have begun to elucidate the complexity of immune responses in the natural host during Brucella infection, 7 further underscoring the need for a tractable small‐animal model that recapitulates such sheep‐specific immunity. Currently, such research relies heavily on natural host models or in vitro primary ovine cells. While direct sheep infection is the gold standard, it is severely limited by high costs, ethical constraints, and the strict need for large‐animal biosafety level 3 (ABSL‐3) facilities. 8 Conversely, in vitro cell models offer valuable cellular insights but fundamentally fail to recapitulate complex, multi‐organ systemic immune responses. 9 , 10 , 11 To fill this technical void, the present study utilized severely immunodeficient NBSGW mice as recipients and successfully established a stable sheep splenocyte‐reconstituted mouse model (Sh‐Spl mice) via intravenous transplantation of ovine splenic lymphocytes. We systematically evaluated the engraftment efficiency and tissue distribution of immune cells in the model, and further established a Brucella melitensis infection model based on this platform. The successful construction of this model provides a novel experimental tool for investigating sheep host immune responses (e.g., the pathogenic mechanisms of Brucella), as well as for vaccine and drug evaluation, using a mouse model system.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Bacterial revival and culture

Brucella melitensis was obtained from the Strain and Virus Seed Bank of the Guangzhou Eighth People's Hospital. The bacteria were cultured in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) with shaking until the logarithmic growth phase was reached. Bacteria were harvested and resuspended in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), followed by serial 10‐fold dilutions. The diluted suspensions were plated on Trypticase Soy Agar (TSA) plates for bacterial enumeration.

Mice

NOD.Cg‐Kit W‐41J Tyr + Prkdc scid Il2rg tm1Wjl/ThomJ (NBSGW) and NSG‐MHC I/II DKO mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. 12 All mice were acclimatized for 2–3 days and then randomly grouped. They were housed under specific pathogen‐free conditions in a biosafety level 2 (BSL‐2) animal facility, maintained at a temperature of 22 ± 2°C, and provided with autoclaved water, feed, and bedding. All animal care and experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Infectious Diseases, Guangzhou Medical University Eighth Affiliated Hospital (Animal Ethics Approval No.: IACUC‐YHS‐2025‐07).

Isolation of sheep splenic lymphocytes

Healthy, 24‐month‐old female Hu sheep were used as spleen donors for this study. Prior to tissue collection, the donor animals were clinically evaluated and confirmed to be free of specific infectious diseases, including brucellosis. Sheep spleen tissue was aseptically dissected into ~5 g pieces and digested in RPMI‐1640 medium containing 2 mg/mL collagenase, 1% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I for 20 min at room temperature with intermittent mixing. Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) was then added to a final concentration of ~5 mmol/L, and the mixture was allowed to stand for 5 min. The digested tissue was gently ground through a 100‐mesh steel sieve, and the resulting cell suspension was further filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. After centrifugation, the cell pellet was resuspended and subjected to Ficoll density gradient centrifugation (2000 rpm, 20 min) to isolate the lymphocyte layer. Following washing, red blood cells (RBCs) were lysed. The final splenocyte suspension was cryopreserved at a concentration of 1 × 108 cells/mL.

CD47 sequence alignment

Amino acid sequences of CD47 from human (Homo sapiens, accession no. CEJ95640.1), mouse (Mus musculus, accession no. BAA25401.1), sheep (Ovis aries, accession no. XP_042086841.1), and goat (Capra hircus, accession no. XP_017918728.1) were retrieved from the NCBI database. Multiple sequence alignments were performed in the R environment (version 4.4.2) using the Clustal Omega algorithm provided by the msa package (version 1.38.0). Alignment results were visualized as sequence alignment diagrams for comparative analysis.

Construction of the sheep immune system mouse model

All operations are sterile and carried out in a laminar flow hood. Twenty‐four hours before cell transplantation, NBSGW mice received an intraperitoneal injection of busulfan at a dose of 100 μg/200 μL per mouse. Sheep splenocytes were retrieved from liquid nitrogen, thawed, and resuspended in RPMI‐1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. The cells were cultured for 12 h at 37°C under 5% CO2. After centrifugation, the cell pellet was resuspended in PBS at a density of 4 × 105 cells/μL. Each mouse was anesthetized with isoflurane and administered 50 μL of the splenocyte suspension via orbital vein injection.

After transplantation, peripheral blood was collected weekly from the tail vein. Engraftment of sheep‐derived CD45+ cells was monitored by flow cytometry. At the experimental endpoint, mice were anesthetized with tribromoethanol, euthanized, and tissue samples were collected to assess the reconstitution levels of sheep‐derived cells.

Flow cytometric analysis

Peripheral blood samples were treated with red blood cell lysis buffer for 3 min to remove erythrocytes. Tissue samples were digested and mechanically dissociated to generate single‐cell suspensions. The resulting single‐cell preparations were then stained with appropriate antibodies for surface marker detection. The following antibody panel was used: BV605 anti‐mouse CD45 (BioLegend: 103140), PE anti‐sheep CD45 (BIO‐RAD: MCA2220PE), APC anti‐CD4 (BIO‐RAD: MCA2213A647), FITC anti‐CD8 (BIO‐RAD: MCA2216F). Data were acquired on a Cytek flow cytometer (USA) and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Brucella infection

Reconstituted Sh‐Spl mice with engrafted sheep immune cells were transferred to an animal biosafety level 3 laboratory (ABSL‐3). The Sh‐Spl mice were grouped according to the sheep CD45 cell levels in peripheral blood determined by flow cytometry 1 day before infection. The infected group received an intraperitoneal injection of Brucella melitensis at a dose of 1 × 106 CFU in 200 μL per mouse, 13 , 14 while the control group was administered an equal volume of PBS. Body weight and general health status were monitored daily throughout the infection period. At 14 days post‐infection (d.p.i.), all mice were euthanized, and tissue samples were collected for assessment of bacterial load and infection status.

Spleen and liver CFU assay

On the day of sampling, the spleen and liver were placed in 1 mL PBS and homogenized. The homogenate was serially diluted in 10‐fold steps ranging from 10−2 to 10−4, with three replicates per dilution. Each dilution was plated onto TSA plates and incubated at 37°C for 4 days before bacterial colonies were enumerated.

Hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, Cat. no. BL539A) for 48 h, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning. Sections underwent routine deparaffinization and rehydration. For H&E staining, sections were stained with hematoxylin (Solarbio, Cat. no. G1080), differentiated, blued, and then counterstained with eosin (Solarbio, Cat. no. G1100). After washing in water, slides were air‐dried. For IHC staining, Tris EDTA buffer (10 mmol/L Tris Base, 1 mmol/L EDTA, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 9.0) was used for antigen retrieval. Then, the sections were blocked with goat serum (ZSGB‐BIO: ZLI9056), incubated with Brucella primary antibody (BIOSS: bs‐2229R, 1:1000), and incubated with enzyme labeled polymer secondary antibody (ZSGB‐BIO: PV‐6000). Detection was performed using DAB, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, sections were dehydrated through a graded alcohol series, cleared in xylene, mounted with neutral resin, and examined under a microscope.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5). Pairwise comparisons between groups were conducted using Student's t‐test. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Sheep‐derived cells were detected in both NBSGW and NSG‐MHC I/II DKO mice following intravenous transplantation of sheep splenocytes

CD47 is a widely expressed “self‐identification” molecule on cell surfaces. It interacts with signal regulatory protein α (SIRPα) on immune cells (e.g., macrophages and dendritic cells) to transmit a “do not eat me” signal, which inhibits phagocytosis of self‐cells and plays a critical role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and preventing autoimmune damage. 15 , 16 To evaluate the feasibility of constructing an sheep‐immune‐reconstituted mouse model, we compared the CD47 amino acid sequences of mice, sheep, goats, and humans. The results showed that the sequence similarity between mouse CD47 and that of sheep, goats, and humans was 62.57%, 62.54%, and 63.58%, respectively (Figure 1A,B). Given that the similarity between sheep and mouse CD47 exceeds 60% and considering the stability of existing humanized immune system mouse models, we hypothesized that sheep CD47 may be recognized by mouse SIRPα, supporting the feasibility of establishing a sheep‐immune‐reconstituted mouse model.

Based on this hypothesis, we intravenously transplanted 2 × 107 sheep splenocytes into NSG‐MHC I/II DKO and NBSGW mice (Figure 1C) (sheep splenocyte purity is shown in Figure S1). Flow cytometry analysis revealed sheep‐derived CD45+ cells in the peripheral blood at 1, 3, and 4 weeks post‐transplantation, as well as in the spleen and bone marrow at the experimental endpoint, in both mouse strains (Figure 1D,E). Although the difference was not statistically significant, NBSGW mice exhibited higher engraftment efficiency than NSG‐MHC I/II DKO mice (Table S1).

These results indicate that sheep splenocytes can successfully engraft in the tested immunodeficient mouse models, demonstrating the feasibility of constructing a functional sheep immune system mouse model.

Busulfan pretreatment significantly enhances engraftment and survival of sheep immune cells in NBSGW mice

Although initial results confirmed the presence of sheep‐derived CD45+ cells in both NBSGW and NSG‐MHC I/II DKO mice, the proportion of these cells in the peripheral blood and tissues remained relatively low. To improve engraftment efficiency, we optimized the experimental protocol: we selected NBSGW mice (which showed higher engraftment potential) and pretreated them with an intraperitoneal injection of 100 μg busulfan per mouse 1 day before transplantation to suppress host hematopoiesis. 17 Twenty‐four hours after pre‐treatment with busulfan, 2 × 107 sheep splenic lymphocytes were transplanted into each mouse via intravenous injection (Figure 2A). Results showed that the proportion of sheep‐derived CD45+ cells in peripheral blood reached 1%–2% at week 1 post‐transplantation, representing a 10‐fold increase compared to the non‐busulfan‐treated group. Notably, these cells remained detectable for 13 weeks after transplantation (Figure 2B) (the flow gating strategy for sheepCD45+, CD4+T, and CD8+T cells in mouse peripheral blood is shown in Figure S2). Dynamic analysis of sheep CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets within the peripheral blood revealed that the percentage of CD4+ T cells among sheep‐derived CD45+ cells during weeks 1–13 was 41.98%, 47.06%, 60.68%, 54.76%, 53.20%, 53.68%, 63.16%, 60.84%, and 53.80%, respectively. Correspondingly, the proportion of CD8+ T cells was 15.50%, 15.88%, 16.92%, 21.46%, 24.64%, 22.74%, 23.94%, 19.71%, and 24.51% over the same period (Figure 2C,D). To further assess immune homeostasis, the ratio of sheep CD4+/CD8+ T cell was determined over the 13 weeks. The ratio fluctuated between 2.51 and 6.20 (Figure 2E), demonstrating that both T cell subsets were co‐maintained without exhaustion. This indicates a successful long‐term immunological equilibrium in the Sh‐Spl mice.

These findings demonstrate that busulfan preconditioning significantly enhances both the initial engraftment and long‐term persistence of sheep immune cells in NBSGW mice, providing a critical optimization strategy for constructing a robust xenotransplantation model suitable for studying sheep‐derived immune cells.

Sheep immune cells colonize multiple mouse tissues with the spleen as the primary site

At 13 weeks post‐transplantation, mice were euthanized, and flow cytometric analysis revealed widespread engraftment of sheep‐derived CD45+ cells across multiple tissues. The proportions of sheep CD45+ cells were as follows: spleen (24.23%), peripheral blood (0.16%), bone marrow (1.38%), liver (8.62%), and lung (0.76%), with the spleen exhibiting the highest level of engraftment (Figure 3A). Subset analysis of T cells within these tissues showed that CD4+ T cells accounted for 61.8%, 55.62%, 10%, 64.86%, and 60.32% of sheep‐derived CD45+ cells in the spleen, peripheral blood, bone marrow, liver, and lung, respectively. The corresponding proportions of CD8+ T cells were 14.77%, 20.57%, 15.36%, 15.90%, and 16.91% (Figure 3A).

Immunohistochemical staining further confirmed the presence of sheep CD45+ cells in the spleen, liver, and bone marrow (Figure 3B).

These results demonstrate that the transplanted sheep immune cells successfully homed to and persisted in multiple tissue compartments, with the spleen serving as the predominant site of engraftment.

Sh‐Spl mice serve as a suitable model for Brucella infection

To validate the applicability of the model, successfully reconstituted mice were intraperitoneally challenged with 1 × 106 CFU of Brucella melitensis and monitored for clinical signs over 14 days (Figure 4A). No mortality was observed in the infected group (Figure 4B), and body weight remained stable without significant decline (Figure 4C). At the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized for tissue collection. Consistent with previous studies, splenomegaly was evident in infected mice (Figure 4D), Macroscopic white calcified foci (indicated by white arrows) were observed in the liver and spleen (Figure S3A,B). 14 Organ weight analysis confirmed a significant increase in spleen weight and a non‐significant increase in liver weight in the infected group compared to controls (Figure 4E). Bacterial culture of tissue homogenates revealed high bacterial loads in both the spleen and liver of infected mice (Figure 4F), and the bacterial burden per milligram of tissue was significantly higher in the spleen than in the liver.

H&E staining showed focal necrosis (brown arrow), granulomatous hyperplasia (black arrow), and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration (orange arrow) in the spleen of infected mice, while mock‐infected controls exhibited only minimal inflammatory infiltration (red arrow). In the liver, widespread hydropic degeneration and inflammatory infiltration (red arrow) were observed in infected mice, whereas controls showed only mild hepatocellular swelling and steatosis (yellow arrow) (Figure 4G). IHC analysis confirmed the presence of Brucella antigens in the spleen, liver, heart, lung, kidney, and ovary of infected Sh‐Spl mice (Figure 4H and Figure S4).

These results demonstrate that Sh‐Spl mice exhibit characteristic histopathological responses and bacterial colonization patterns upon Brucella infection, supporting their use as an animal model for studying brucellosis.

DISCUSSION

Constructing animal models capable of simulating the immune responses of natural hosts represents a crucial approach for advancing research on species‐specific pathogens. Based on severely immunodeficient NBSGW mice, the present study successfully established a long‐term stable sheep immune system‐reconstituted mouse model via intravenous transplantation of ovine splenic lymphocytes. Furthermore, we confirmed that this model can be used to establish Brucella melitensis infections with typical pathological features. This model provides a novel experimental tool for investigating the interactions between the sheep immune system and host specificity of pathogens at the in vivo level.

Molecular rationale for sheep cell engraftment: CD47‐SIRPα interaction

Through comparative analysis of CD47 amino acid sequences, the results indicated that the similarity between sheep and mouse CD47 is 62.57%, which is comparable to the 63.58% similarity between human and mouse CD47. The interaction between CD47 and SIRPα is a core mechanism for “self‐recognition” that prevents phagocytosis of host cells by immune cells. The success of existing models such as humanized mice and humanized SCID pig has demonstrated that cross‐species CD47‐SIRPα recognition can support the survival of xenogeneic immune cells. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 This study further suggests that cross‐recognition between sheep CD47 and mouse SIRPα may underpin the engraftment of sheep immune cells in mice, providing a molecular rationale for the construction of sheep‐derived models.

Selection of immunodeficient mouse strain: NBSGW versus NSG‐MHC I/II DKO

To identify a suitable immunodeficient mouse strain for xenogeneic cell transplantation, we initially compared the engraftment efficiency of sheep splenocytes in NBSGW and NSG‐MHC I/II DKO mice. The NBSGW strain carries triple mutations in Prkdc, Il2rg, and Kit. Notably, the Kit mutation impairs stem cell factor signaling, which is in synergy with Prkdc and Il2rg deficiencies, almost completely abolishes innate immune rejection of xenogeneic grafts. This results in chimeric levels comparable to those achieved in irradiated NSG mice. 12 , 22 , 23 , 24 In contrast, NSG‐MHC I/II DKO mice lack MHC class II expression. In this background, T cell development in the host thymus primarily relies on donor MHC molecules for positive selection, leading to a more functionally competent and centrally tolerant T cell repertoire. Additionally, these mice exhibit either no or significantly delayed graft‐versus‐host disease (GvHD) upon allogeneic or xenogeneic PBMC transplantation. 25 , 26 Our results showed superior engraftment efficiency in NBSGW mice, highlighting their advantages for supporting the survival and expansion of sheep‐derived immune cells.

Optimization of engraftment efficiency: Busulfan pretreatment

To further enhance the engraftment efficiency of sheep‐derived cells in mice, NBSGW mice were pretreated with busulfan 24 h prior to cell transplantation. As an alkylating agent, busulfan suppresses hematopoietic stem cell proliferation and clears the host bone marrow niche, thereby creating “space” for the engraftment of xenogeneic immune cells. 27 , 28 Results demonstrated that busulfan preconditioning increased the proportion of sheep‐derived CD45+ cells in peripheral blood by approximately 10‐fold. These cells persisted stably for up to 13 weeks and successfully colonized multiple tissues including the spleen, liver, and bone marrow. The highest level of engraftment was observed in the spleen (24.23%), consistent with previous findings showing predominant colonization of bovine lymphocytes in the spleens of SCID mice. 3 This suggests that the spleen may serve as a critical site for the proliferation and survival of xenogeneic lymphocytes.

Furthermore, dynamic monitoring of CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets indicated that the model not only supported successful engraftment but also maintained fundamental proportional characteristics of T cell populations. This establishes a foundation for simulating sheep‐specific cellular immune responses in this chimeric system.

Validity of the Sh‐Spl model for Brucella infection

Consistent with previous animal models of Brucella infection, Sh‐Spl mice challenged with Brucella melitensis exhibited characteristic manifestations such as splenomegaly, elevated bacterial loads in visceral organs, and related histopathological alterations. 14 , 29 , 30 Our results demonstrate that the Sh‐Spl mouse model can effectively simulate the key features of Brucella infection. The Sh‐Spl model thus represents a valuable tool for studying the pathogenesis of Brucella infection. Unlike traditional mouse models, the Sh‐Spl model uniquely supports the in vivo survival of ovine immune cells, enabling direct investigation of Brucella‐host interactions within a species‐matched immune environment.

Limitations of the study

Despite its promising results, this study has several limitations. First, the overall proportion of sheep‐derived cells remained relatively low, particularly in peripheral blood (only 0.16%), which may constrain the strength and comprehensiveness of immune responses. Furthermore, due to the limited specificity of currently available flow cytometry antibodies against sheep antigens, the staining panel did not include markers for other immune cell subsets such as B cells, making it difficult to model humoral immune reactions. Second, although the CD47 sequence similarity supports partial cross‐species recognition, other immune molecules, such as cytokines and chemokines, exhibit notable species‐specific differences between sheep and mice. These discrepancies may lead to deviations in immune activation and functionality. For instance, whether sheep T cells activated in the mouse environment fully replicate their functional states in sheep remains to be validated. Third, while the bacterial colonization levels and pathological features in the model resemble those in natural sheep hosts, a deeper analysis of immune responses, such as cytokine production and antibody responses is still lacking. It remains unclear whether the model can accurately reflect sheep‐specific protective immune mechanisms, such as Th1‐type responses. Further optimization incorporating immunological readouts is necessary to enhance the physiological relevance of the model.

Future directions

Based on the findings and limitations of this study, future research directions are as follows. First, the focus will be on optimizing the model construction protocol by investigating the immunological reconstitution effects of transplantation with different cell types, such as sheep peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and CD34+ hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), 31 and attempting to use sheep specific cytokines in combination to further enhance the engraftment level and long‐term stability of ovine‐derived immune cells. 32 Advances in stem cell technologies, including the use of induced pluripotent stem cells for veterinary applications, 33 may also inform future strategies for generating more complete sheep immune system mouse models. Second, we will actively develop specific antibodies targeting key subsets of ovine immune cells, such as B cells and monocytes/macrophages, to establish a more comprehensive immune analysis system. Meanwhile, technologies such as single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) will be utilized to decipher the functional status of engrafted cells. Finally, based on the optimized model, systematic investigations will be conducted to determine whether the model can recapitulate the Th1 cell response, which is the characteristic protective immune response observed in sheep during Brucella infection. 34 Ultimately, this platform will be applied to evaluate the immunoprotective efficacy of candidate vaccines, thereby providing a robust tool for advancing the development of brucellosis prevention and control strategies.

CONCLUSION

In summary, the Sh‐Spl model developed in this study provides an innovative tool for research on sheep specific diseases such as brucellosis. Although limitations remain (e.g., incomplete immune lineage reconstruction), with further technical refinements, this model holds promise as a critical platform bridging basic research and clinical translation.
