Optimal Benchmark Design under Costly Manipulation Thanks: I thank seminar participants at Universidad Complutense de Madrid.
Ángel Hernando-Veciana

Abstract
Price benchmarks are used to incorporate market price trends into contracts, but their use can create opportunities for manipulation by parties involved in the contract. This paper examines this issue using a realistic and tractable model inspired by smart contracts on blockchains like Ethereum. In our model, manipulation costs depend on two factors: the magnitude of adjustments to individual prices (variable costs) and the number of prices adjusted (fixed costs). We find that a weighted mean is the optimal benchmark when fixed costs are negligible, while the median is optimal when variable costs are negligible. In cases where both fixed and variable costs are significant, the optimal benchmark can be implemented as a trimmed mean, with the degree of trimming increasing as fixed costs become more important relative to variable costs. Furthermore, we show that the optimal weights for a mean-based benchmark are proportional to the marginal manipulation costs, whereas the median remains optimal without weighting, even when fixed costs differ across prices. Key Words: Financial benchmarks, oracles, decentralised finance. JEL-Classification: G21, D45.

Indeed, our first main result (Proposition
2
) is that the optimal benchmark is the median or the mean, respectively, when either the variable costs or the fixed costs are negligible. This is intuitive. In the former case, the main trouble may come from a few prices large price increases, which are better handled with the median than with the mean. Quite the contrary, if it is fixed costs that are negligible, the mean is better to handle manipulations than the median as the median is very sensitive to the manipulation of a large proportion of prices.

Proving this result in general is technically challenging, as the problem formally corresponds to an optimal contract with multitask moral hazard. In this context, the manipulator acts as the agent, deciding how much to manipulate each of the market prices that feed into the benchmark. The analysis becomes even more complex in the presence of fixed costs, as the manipulator’s objective function is non-convex, even under linear contracts. Consequently, the manipulator’s optimal behavior cannot be fully characterized using only first-order conditions.

Our approach takes a different perspective. Consider the case with no fixed costs and a mean benchmark. Convex variable costs of manipulation result in the optimal manipulation strategy of uniformly increasing all prices in the market by the same amount. This uniform manipulation would also increase by the same amount any benchmark that satisfies basic reasonable properties. For instance,
symmetric benchmarks
, those equal to the point of symmetry if the distribution is symmetric, would increase the same amount if the original distribution was symmetric. However, other symmetric benchmarks may allow for alternative, less costly manipulation strategies. In this sense, the mean is optimal, as it maximizes the cost of manipulation among all symmetric benchmarks.

The case without variable costs is similar, though slightly more complex. Here, the optimal manipulation strategy of the median involves raising a sufficient mass of prices such that only half the total mass remains below the target. With careful adjustment, this minimal mass can be shifted in a way that preserves symmetry, ensuring that if the original price distribution was symmetric, the manipulated distribution remains symmetric as well. As in the previous case, this implies that the median maximizes the cost of manipulation among all symmetric benchmarks, making it optimal in this sense.

Extending the analysis beyond these extreme cases is challenging. However, in the specific scenario where the initial distribution of prices is concentrated at a single point, we can generally characterize benchmarks that induce optimal manipulations that preserve symmetry, thereby ensuring optimality among symmetric benchmarks. This turn out to be trimmed means

, where the extent of trimming increases as the fixed costs grow larger relative to the variable costs.

Finally, we study how our results extend to the case in which the prices fed into the benchmark may come from subpopulations with different observable characteristics. For instance, the sampled prices may correspond to different moments in time in which liquidity may have varied, and differences in liquidity may imply differences in the variable cost of manipulation. Similarly, different moments in time may be associated to different gas prices and thus differences in the fixed cost of manipulation.

In this case, our results for the extreme cases of no fixed costs or no variable costs are very clear cut. In the former case, the optimal benchmark is a weighted mean with weights proportional to the marginal costs of manipulation, whereas in the latter case the standard median remains optimal.

Our work is also related to the literature on robust statistics, see

In particular, these deviations are not compatible with the existence of a fixed cost of manipulation, one of the main elements of our analysis.

The Model

In our investigation, we assume a population of
unmanipulated prices
described by a probability distribution
F
. For some results we assume that the distribution is a degenerate distribution at a price that we normalise to be zero. We refer to this case as the case of no dispersion. For the rest of the paper, we assume that
F
has a single-peaked density
f
symmetric around zero. A strategic agent,
the manipulator
, can manipulate these prices at a cost. In particular, the manipulator’s strategy can be described with a conditional distribution function
G^{c}(\cdot|p)
, for any
p\in\real
, that describes how the prices of all the markets with unmanipulated price
p
are shifted into a new distribution of prices. In the case of no manipulation,
G^{c}(\cdot|p)
is simply a degenerate distribution at
p
. The manipulator’s strategy gives rise to the
final distribution of prices
:

G(p)\equiv\int G^{c}(p|\tilde{p})dF(\tilde{p}).

A
benchmark
is a unidimensional statistic computed from the final distribution of prices. In our analysis, either we require very little structure on the set of rules that may be used to compute the benchmark or restrict attention to particular families of rules that have been used in practice. In particular, we call
symmetric benchmarks
those that are equal to the point of symmetry in the case of a symmetric distribution of prices. Note that this definition does not impose any structure on distributions that are not symmetric. We define the
\tau
-trimmed mean, for
\tau\in[0,0.5]
, as the average calculated over the modified distribution obtained by truncating the original distribution at the
\tau
and
1-\tau
quantiles. Clearly,
\tau=0
and
\tau=1/2
, renders the
\tau-
trimmed mean equal to the standard mean and the median, respectively. Clearly, the
\tau
-trimmed means are symmetric benchmarks.

As a realistic example, we can think of the distribution of prices as the marginal prices offered by a set of automated market makers (AMM) at different moments in time. This marginal prices can be manipulated by submitting trade orders to the AMM whose only purpose is to upset the computed benchmark. Thus, the final prices correspond to the marginal prices displayed by the AMM’s and used in the computation of the benchmark and the unmanipulated prices refer to the counterfactual of what could have been the marginal prices if there was no manipulation. Alternatively, we could have assumed that the benchmark is computed out of a sample of transaction prices and manipulation is done by submitting additional trades.

Manipulating a price is costly. In particular, the cost of a manipulating strategy
G^{c}(\cdot|p)
is equal to:

\int\int_{{\tilde{p}\not=p}}\left(c(\tilde{p}-p)+k\right)dG(\tilde{p}|p)dF(p),

where
c
is a continuous strictly convex function, equal to zero at zero and symmetric around zero, and
k\geq 0
. Thus,
c
is the
variable cost
of manipulation and
k
the
fixed cost
of manipulation. The cost of manipulation is explained in that manipulating a price requires trading the asset. The variable cost arises because greater manipulation requieres a larger order which may imply larger costs

14

14

Both
Duffie & Dworczak 2021
, and
Baldauf et al. 2022
provide examples of why the variable cost is increasing and convex.
and the fixed cost because including a trader order has a cost independently of the size of the trade order. For instance, in the context of blockchain, manipulating a price just before it is fed to the benchmark requires paying the miner the corresponding price of gas, which may be substantial.

The symmetry of the distribution of unmanipulated prices means that any symmetric benchmark is equal to zero, and thus the
bias
of the benchmark due to manipulation is the absolute value of the benchmark computed for the final distribution of prices.

3
Optimal Benchmarks

The criterium we use to evaluate a benchmark is the minimum cost of manipulation necessary to implement a certain price
P
, the greater this minimum cost is, the better the benchmark. This is consistent with the example of collateralised loans that we provide in the introduction. A manipulator minimizes the cost to reach the price at which the liquidation clause is activated, whereas the benchmark designer maximizes this cost to make this manipulation less attractive. This is also a first step to study alternative objective functions for the manipulator. For instance, for the case in which the manipulator’s objective is to maximize the price, the optimal benchmark maximizes the marginal cost of raising it, which under some regularity conditions coincides with the benchmark that maximizes the total cost of raising the benchmark.

Definition:

•

A benchmark is optimal at price
P
if it is the symmetric benchmark that maximizes the minimum cost of manipulating the benchmark up to
P
. A benchmark is optimal if it is optimal at any price
P
.

•

A benchmark dominates another benchmark at price
P
if the former has a greater minimum cost of its manipulation to
P
than the latter. A benchmark dominates another benchmark if it dominates the latter for any
P
.

Proposition 1

A benchmark is optimal at

P

Proof.

Since the manipulation strategy of the proposition induces a symmetric final distribution of prices, it not only implements

P

∎

4
Benchmarks Based on Data from Heterogenous Markets

In this section, we consider the case in which the distribution of prices comes from different subpopulations that may differ in their manipulation costs. This difference may arise, for instance, because the benchmark is computed using prices at different moments in time and manipulation costs may vary along time. A natural example is when liquidity varies along time. In this case, lower liquidity means that the same amount of manipulation requires of a smaller order and thus smaller variable costs. Another example in the context of the blockchain is when gas prices fluctuate with time. In this case, lower gas prices correspond to lower fixed costs.

We also allow for the possibility of heterogeneity across subpopulations in the distribution of the unmanipulated prices. Formally, we assume a countable set of subpopulations
I

i\in I

\mu_{i}

The manipulator strategy is described by a family, one per subpopulation, of conditional distribution functions

G_{i}(\tilde{p})\equiv\int G^{c}_{i}(\tilde{p}|p)\,\mu_{i}\,F_{i}\left(dp\right).

The cost of a manipulating strategy

\sum_{i\in I}\int\int_{{\tilde{p}\not=p}}\left(\alpha_{i}c(\tilde{p}-p)+k_{i}\right)\,G_{i}(d\tilde{p}|p)\,\mu_{i}\,F\left(dp\right),

5
Conclusion

This paper investigates how the design of price benchmarks can mitigate the risks of manipulation, with a particular focus on the unique challenges posed by decentralized finance (DeFi). We demonstrate that the choice of benchmark formula—mean, median, or trimmed mean—plays a critical role in balancing the costs of manipulation. Our results highlight that while the mean is optimal when variable costs dominate, the median is preferable when fixed costs are significant. In intermediate cases, trimmed means emerge as optimal, with the degree of trimming reflecting the relative importance of fixed costs.

By formalizing the trade-offs between fixed and variable manipulation costs, our work offers a framework to enhance benchmark robustness in both traditional finance and DeFi. Our findings are particularly relevant for the development of DeFi, since reliable price benchmarks are essential to the trustworthiness of smart contracts and the stability of DeFi systems.

A key direction for future research is the joint optimization of sampling strategies and benchmark formulas. While this paper focuses on the formula used to aggregate sampled prices, the sampling process itself is equally critical, particularly in decentralized exchanges where liquidity and transaction timing can vary widely. Exploring how sampling protocols interact with benchmark formulas could yield further insights into designing benchmarks that are both manipulation-resistant and statistically efficient.
