Comment on outcomes of pediatric cataract surgery: Intraoperative triamcinolone injection versus dexamethasone injection
Authors: Shreyan Dani, Rajalakshmi A Ramachandran, Swathi Nagarajan, K Srikanth
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the article titled “Outcomes of pediatric cataract surgery: Intraoperative triamcinolone injection versus dexamethasone injection”[1] published in IJO in December 2025 by Agarwal et al. The study provides valuable insights about the role of intracameral steroids in pediatric cataract surgery, along with a comparison between the use of intracameral triamcinolone and intracameral dexamethasone. The study’s comprehensive evaluation of outcomes, including postoperative anterior chamber reactions, intraocular pressure, central corneal thickness, and need for resurgeries offers a holistic understanding of challenges in managing pediatric cataract surgeries. In addition, the use of the contralateral eye as study/control to prevent bias is appropriate.[1]

However, we wish to raise a few concerns regarding the study.

As mentioned, dexamethasone has rapid aqueous turnover and a short half-life (approximately 3 h). In contrast, intracameral triamcinolone has a longer half-life (approximately18 h) and intraocular bioavailability. Our concern is whether the observations at 1 and 3 months follow-up can be attributable to intracameral dexamethasone administered at the time of surgery. Also, the considerable difference in the half-life of the two drugs would influence even observations within 24 h of surgery. In this situation, similar anterior chamber reaction was observed in both groups at all times of follow-up calls into question the role of intracameral steroids.Triamcinolone, being particulate in nature and with a longer half-life, can be visualized under a slit-lamp biomicroscope, especially on postoperative day 1. How did the presence of particulate matter affect the grading of cells and flare on POD#1? In this situation, with particulate triamcinolone (t1/2 of 18 h) and nonparticulate dexamethasone (t1/2 of 3 h), how was the blinding of the assessor ensured? This fact has not been explained or taken into consideration.Intraocular lens redialling is not primarily done for postoperative inflammation. Also, were intracameral steroids given at the end of the second surgery? The manuscript is not clear on these points.The standard surgical techniques used in all patients are well described. Mention of single or multiple surgeons and their level of expertise would be valuable.
