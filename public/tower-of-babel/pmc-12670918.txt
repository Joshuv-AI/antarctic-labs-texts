Comparative evaluation of ADC and ADC ratio in differentiating Gleason Score 7 in prostate cancer imaging
Authors: D. Samaras, G. Agrotis, D. Tsivaka, M. Vakalopoulou, K. Vassiou, V. Tzortzis, M. Vlychou, I. Tsougos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultiparametric Magnetic Resonance Imaging (mpMRI), including diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), plays a key role in detecting and stratifying prostate cancer (PCa). The Apparent Diffusion Coefficient (ADC) aids in tissue characterization and may assist in distinguishing between Gleason score 7 subgroups (GS 3 +4 and GS 4 +3). This study aimed to evaluate mean ADC and ADCratio (ratio of tumor-to-normal ADC) in differentiating these subgroups and to assess the influence of magnetic field strength (1.5 T vs. 3.0 T) on diagnostic performance.MethodsThis retrospective study included 48 patients with histologically confirmed GS 7 PCa who underwent mpMRI at 1.5 T or 3.0 T. Two experienced radiologists independently and blindly delineated tumor and reference ROIs. Inter-observer agreement was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Correlation, t-tests, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were used to assess relationships, group differences, and optimal cut-off values.ResultsExcellent inter-observer agreement was found for both mean ADC and ADCratio (ICC>0.9). At 3.0 T, mean ADC (10.9 ×10⁻⁴ mm²/s vs 8.55 ×10⁻⁴ mm²/s, p = 0.011) and ADCratio (0.68 vs 0.54, p = 0.003) significantly distinguished GS 3 + 4 from GS 4 + 3, while no differences were observed at 1.5 T. Spearman’s ρ confirmed stronger correlations at 3.0 T (ADCratio ρ=−0.510; p = 0.004). For the combined (3.0 T + 1.5 T) dataset, ADCratio achieved an AUC of 0.748 and mean ADC an AUC of 0.718 (p > 0.05).ConclusionADCratio demonstrated high reproducibility and stable diagnostic performance across scanners, supporting its potential as a reliable imaging biomarker for prostate cancer grading.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the most prevalent form of cancer among men worldwide, with varying survival rates depending on cancer stage. While localized PCa boasts a 5-year survival rate of 100 %, for metastatic PCa, it drops to 29 % [1]. The Gleason Score (GS), established in 1974, is a reliable predictor of outcomes for localized PCa [2], [3], [4], though variations exist even within GS 7 cases, with 5-year biochemical risk-free survival rate of 88 % for GS 3 + 4 PCa, and 63 % for 4 + 3 PCa [5]. Additionally, studies have reported higher mortality rates for GS 4 + 3 PCa compared to GS 3 + 4 PCa [6], [7].

Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) has emerged as a valuable tool for detecting and localizing PCa and stratifying the risk associated with PCa [8], [9]. The acquisition of Apparent Diffusion Coefficient (ADC) maps, derived from Diffusion-weighted Imaging (DWI), is recommended by the PI-RADS steering committee as part of a standardized mpMRI protocol. These tools enhance the accuracy of PCa localization and correlate significantly with the GS [10], [11]. Furthermore, ADC has demonstrated a significant correlation with the GS. However, ADC values can vary widely due to patient factors and technical parameters, such as DWI b-values and magnetic field strength [12], [13], [14]. To mitigate this variability, ADCratio has been proposed. The ADCratio compares tumor’s ADC to normal prostatic tissue, potentially offering more stable measurements across different conditions [12], [15]. Regarding GS 7 adenocarcinomas, previous literature has shown that ADC metrics have proven useful in distinguishing between GS 3 + 4 and GS 4 + 3 PCa [16], [17].

In recent years, several studies have explored the potential advantages of using the ADCratio instead of absolute ADC values [12], [15], [18], [19], [20], [21]. Some researchers have advocated for the use of the ADCratio as the preferred method, demonstrating its significant ability to differentiate between GS 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 PCa [12], [18], [19], [20], [21]. However, other authors have been more doubtful regarding its effectiveness [22].

The objective of this study is to assess the utility of mean ADC and ADCratio in distinguishing between GS 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 PCa, using targeted biopsy results as a reference. Additionally, the study aims to investigate whether the ADCratio can normalize the theoretical field strength incoherence.

Materials and methods

Study population and design

This was a retrospective, multi-center study conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. The study was approved by the local Health Research Ethics Committee (approval number: 4861–25/02/2021). Clinical and imaging data were retrospectively collected from patients who underwent multiparametric MRI. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to biopsy and MRI acquisition. Our histology-proven adenocarcinoma population consisted of 104 patients, and only a subset of 48 patients with GS 7 was used to evaluate the predictive role of mean ADC and ADCratio in differentiating GS 3 + 4 from GS 4 + 3 PCa, combining data from different facilities and magnetic fields (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flow diagram of patient selection.Fig. 1

Flow diagram of patient selection.

Histopathologic findings

All biopsies were performed prospectively by a team of three senior physicians ranging from 8 to 11 years of experience in TRUS prostate biopsy and 3 years in fusion-guided biopsy. Antibiotic prophylaxis was used prior to the biopsy and anticoagulant therapies were modified. The mpMRI images were fused with TRUS using GE LOGIQ™ S8 software, and three sample were taken from each suspicious lesion as indicated by the MR study. Only one index lesion was assigned for the targeting biopsy method and considered the dominant suspicious lesion. Subsequently, the systematic biopsy approach was performed with a 12-core standard template. All samples were further analyzed by the Department of Pathology of our hospital.

MRI technique

MpMRI of the prostate was conducted across multiple facilities at 3.0 T or 1.5 T. Specifically, all images were acquired using four different MRI scanners, namely the Signa HDxt (General Electric, Milwaukee, WI, USA), Magnetom Vida, Magnetom Avanto, and Magnetom Aera (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Diverse imaging acquisition protocols encompassed transverse, coronal, and sagittal T2-weighted turbo spin-echo images (T2w TSE), transverse T1-weighted images (T1w), and DWI with a b value of 0 or 50 and 800 s/mm2. Patients with DWI acquisitions using b-values inconsistent with our protocol (500 or 1000 s/mm²) were excluded, as well as one patient with poor DWI quality. We chose the b value of 800 s/mm2 since it was consistent in all imaging protocols across different facilities. ADC maps were generated utilizing in-house MATLAB codes from the DWI images with two b-values (0 or 50 and 800 s/mm2) using the mono-exponential model fitting. DWI analysis

Two experienced radiologists with 20 and 14 years of experience in abdominal imaging independently reviewed and segmented the prostate MR images. Both readers were blinded to biopsy and histopathological results and were informed only of the lesion’s PI-RADS score and location to ensure consistent evaluation of the same index lesion.

Tumor regions of interest (ROIs) were manually delineated on ADC maps over areas of visually reduced diffusion corresponding to the reported lesion location, while reference ROIs were placed contralaterally in normal-appearing prostate tissue of similar size and location (Fig. 2).Fig. 2A peripheral zone lesion with Gleason Score 3 + 4. Axial ADC with mean ADC 38.6 × 10–4 mm2/s and ADCratio 0.384. Corresponding axial T2 image of the same lesion.Fig. 2

A peripheral zone lesion with Gleason Score 3 + 4. Axial ADC with mean ADC 38.6 × 10–4 mm2/s and ADCratio 0.384. Corresponding axial T2 image of the same lesion.

Mean ADC values were extracted for both ROIs using LIFEx (Version 7.4.7), and the ADCratio was defined as the ratio of tumor-to-normal ADC. Inter-observer agreement between the two radiologists was assessed using a two-way random-effects Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC [1], [2]), with values interpreted as poor (<0.5), moderate (0.5–0.75), good (0.75–0.9), or excellent (>0.9).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics and box plots were utilized to illustrate the characteristics of the study population. Normality test was conducted to evaluate the normal distribution of the mean ADC and ADCratio. Additionally, Levene’s test was employed to examine the homogeneity of variances among the various groups. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was employed to evaluate the strength of the association between ADC values and GS 7. An independent sample t-test was conducted to compare the mean ADC and ADCratio between the groups (GS 3 +4 vs 4 +3) and to examine whether there is variability among the various GS across different magnetic field strengths.

Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to assess the ability of mean ADC and ADCratio to discriminate between GS 3 + 4 and GS 4 + 3 prostate cancer. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated for each metric, and 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using bootstrapping with 1000 resamples to account for the limited sample size and improve the robustness of the estimates. De Long’s test was applied to compare AUCs between mean ADC and ADCratio. Optimal cutoff values were determined based on the maximum value of Youden’s index, along with their associated sensitivity and specificity.

A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 28.0.0.0, IBM Corp.).

Results

Patient and tumor characteristics

A total of 104 patients with biopsy confirmed PCa were identified from the initial query. Patients with GS 7 who did not have DWI with b= 800 s/mm2 (n = 4), patients with GS 6 or ≥ 8 (n = 51) and patients with poor DWI quality (n = 1) were excluded from the study. Consequently, our final cohort comprised 48 patients with GS 7 (Fig. 1).

Inter-observer agreement

Reliable segmentation and measurement reproducibility were confirmed before proceeding with quantitative analyses. The inter-observer consistency between the two radiologists was excellent for both metrics. The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) for mean ADC was 0.987 (95 % CI: 0.965–0.998), and for ADCratio was 0.958 (95 % CI: 0.918–0.987), demonstrating high reproducibility of ROI placement and ADC measurements across readers and magnetic field strengths

Discussion

The results of our study revealed that both mean ADC and ADCratio could effectively differentiate between GS 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 PCa cases even when data was acquired from different facilities and magnetic field strengths (1.5 T and 3.0 T), with a significant negative correlation between ADC metrics and GS 7. Moreover, we assessed the diagnostic accuracy of mean ADC and ADCratio in discriminating GS 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 using ROC analysis and determining an optimal cutoff point.

The excellent inter-observer agreement (ICC > 0.9 for both metrics) further reinforces the robustness and reproducibility of our ROI delineation and ADC measurements, confirming that the observed differences primarily reflect biological variation rather than measurement inconsistency.

Several studies have investigated the role of ADC metrics in distinguishing between GS 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 PCa [16], [17], [20], [23], however in these studies, patient data was from the same magnetic field. Alessandrino et al. [20] assessed the ability of ADC values to differentiate between GS 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 in PCa. Their findings indicated a significant difference in ADCratio between the two groups and identified mean ADC and ADCratio as the most effective predictors. The ADCratio demonstrates strong clinical potential due to its consistent performance across different magnetic field strengths and imaging protocols. Our research shows that while mean ADC values significantly differed between 3.0 T and 1.5 T for GS 3 + 4 (p = 0.048), the ADCratio remained stable (p = 0.377). For GS 4 + 3, neither the mean ADC nor the ADCratio showed significant differences between the two field strengths. These results highlight the ADCratio's reliability over the mean ADC, which is more affected by magnetic field variations, making it a promising metric for standardized diagnostic assessments in multi-center studies [24].

In our study, both mean ADC and ADCratio exhibited a significant negative correlation with GS at 3.0 T (ρ=-0.510, p = 0.004 for mean ADC and ρ=-0.405, p < 0.026 for ADCratio), which is consistent with previous studies [25], [26]. At 1.5 T, both metrics showed a negative correlation, although it was not statistically significant (ρ=-0.026, p = 0.919 for mean ADC and ρ=-0.322, p = 0.192 for ADCratio), partially aligning with previous findings for 3.0 T but contradicting them for 1.5 T [27].

Levene’s test confirmed consistent variances, for both metrics, indicating data homogeneity. Normality tests validated the use of parametric tests with independent t-tests revealing a significant difference, for mean ADC, between 3.0 T and 1.5 T for GS 3 + 4 (p = 0.046), but not for GS 4 + 3 (p = 0.429), possibly due to the small sample size (8 at 3.0 T, 4 at 1.5 T). Notably, the ADCratio remained consistent between field strengths for both GS groups (p = 0.236 for GS 3 +4 and p = 0.318 for GS 4 +3), highlighting its reliability as a diagnostic measure, even with varying sample sizes.

Mean ADC showed a significant difference between GS 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 at 3.0 T, however there was no significant difference at 1.5 T. These findings are contradictory with some studies [17], [20]. Alessandrino et al. [20] found no significant difference in the mean ADC between the two GS groups in cases conducted at 3.0 T. While Itou et al. [17] reported significant variation in mean ADC between GS 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 at 1.5 T, our study did not find significant differences. This discrepancy could be due to the different b-values used in these studies, as b-value selection can significantly impact ADC measurements. Similar observations have been made in multicenter diffusion studies, where variability in the lowest and highest b-values across scanners can influence absolute ADC values. Bengtsson et al. [27] reported that the b-values used for ADC estimation varied among sites and MRI systems, noting that low b-values (<100 s/mm²) may introduce positive bias due to incoherent blood perfusion [28], whereas very high b-values (>1000 s/mm²) can lead to negative bias in normal tissue because of the rectified noise floor [29]. In our cohort, both low-b combinations (0/800 s/mm² and 50/800 s/mm²) lie within the clinically accepted range for prostate DWI, and the higher b-value was standardized (800 s/mm²) across scanners. Thus, while small differences in ADC magnitude may occur, the relative trends between field strengths and Gleason groups are expected to remain robust [17], [30].

As for the diagnostic performance, the findings indicate that ADCratio exhibited a superior AUC of 0.748 compared to the mean ADC, which had an AUC of 0.718, in differentiating between GS 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 but the difference between the two AUCs is not statistically significant based on De Long’s test. The corresponding cutoff values, for differentiation of the two groups, were 0.55 for ADCratio and 9.48 × 10−4 mm2/s for mean ADC respectively.

Most studies have focused on the role of these ADC metrics in distinguishing PCa with different GS, but most of these studies are single center [25], [26]. Jyoti et al. [31] demonstrated a significant variance in ADCratio when distinguishing between GS 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 in 21 PCa patients with GS= 7. However, this discrepancy may be attributed to the MRI images obtained from their specific MRI scanner and acquisition protocol. Bengtsson et al. [27] examined the relationship between various ADCratios and GS in a cohort of 98 patients from 5 different MRI systems and magnetic field strengths. They did not observe any discriminatory effect on any specific ratio or ADC value. It is important to note that their study only focused on differentiating between GS 6 and GS ≥ 7, rather than distinguishing between GS 3 + 4 and 4 + 3.

Several studies have compared the mean ADC and ADCratio, and it has been found that the latter is more effective in identifying aggressive PCa [19], [26], [32], [33] but, only a few used data from different facilities with different acquisition protocols and magnetic fields [27], [34]. In our study, we found that both mean ADC and ADCratio could distinguish GS 3 + 4 from 4 + 3 when combing data from 3.0 T and 1.5 T. But, as mentioned before, mean ADC demonstrates significant variability due to a variety of factors, such as the b-values utilized and the magnetic field strength of the scanner [13]. Therefore, in our opinion, a comparison of mean ADC between GS 3 + 4 and 4 + 3, for the pooled dataset is inconsistent, and needs further investigation.

Our study shows that ADCratio is consistent across different magnetic field strengths, but the small sample size, may reduce the generalizability of our findings. Future studies should involve larger, multicenter groups to confirm these results and assess ADCratio consistency across various scanners. Also, it is important to note that biopsy results, rather than radical prostatectomy specimens, were utilized as the reference standard in this study which may underestimate the GS [35], [36].

ADCratio shows promise, but further research is needed to assess field strength and b-value impacts on mean ADC. Future studies should evaluate ADCratio in diverse clinical scenarios, including treatment response and long-term outcome predictions in prostate cancer patients. Lastly, combining ADCratio with other imaging biomarkers may enhance diagnostic accuracy and support personalized treatment strategies.

In conclusion, ADCratio demonstrates superior efficacy compared to mean ADC in differentiating between GS 3 + 4 and GS 4 + 3 PCa, particularly when analyzing data obtained from varying magnetic field strengths (1.5 T and 3.0 T). The study identified specific cutoff values for ADCratio and mean ADC that can be used as potential thresholds for distinguishing these two GS subgroups. The ADCratio showed significant potential as a reliable diagnostic tool, with better performance in terms of diagnostic performance compared to mean ADC, especially in the intercomparison between different field strengths and may be used as a potential diagnostic tool for multicenter and multi-scanner studies.
