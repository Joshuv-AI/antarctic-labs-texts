Structural Characterization of Squid Peptides and Evaluation of Their Antiphotoaging Activity in Nude Mice
Authors: Yuan Peng, Minghui Tian, Xiaoyu Liu, Fangcheng Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Solar exposure is a major cause of skin photoaging, and this damage is known to be alleviated by natural antioxidants. In this study, four novel squid cyclic peptides (SCPs) with potential antiphotoaging activity were isolated via activity-guided fractionation based on their antiphotoaging effects. We found that SCP-1 [cyclic-(His-Val-Trp-Leu)] and a squid peptide extract (SCP-A-6) protected human skin fibroblasts against ultraviolet (UV)B-induced photoaging. This protection was mediated by the inhibition of inflammatory cytokine overexpression, such as interleukin (IL)-6, IL-1β, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and suppression of NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation. Moreover, SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 alleviated UV-induced increases in inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and TNF-α) in nude mouse skin, thereby confirming their anti-inflammatory and anti-photoaging properties. In addition, histological analysis revealed that SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 improved the structural integrity of photoaged skin. Specifically, they prevented epidermal hyperplasia, reduced the activity of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), such as MMP-2 and MMP-9, and suppressed NLRP3 inflammasome activation in photoaged dermal tissue. These results suggest that SCPs may serve as beneficial ingredients for the prevention and management of skin photoaging.

INTRODUCTION

Skin aging is a complex biological process that is characterized by the progressive loss of structural integrity and increased susceptibility to photoinduced damage (Yang et al., 2023). Chronic exposure to solar ultraviolet (UV) irradiation induces oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in the skin (Gupta et al., 2023). Therefore, potential strategies to slow skin aging include mitigating oxidative stress and suppressing the overproduction of inflammatory cytokines. Prolonged exposure to UV radiation severely damages the epidermis, which is primarily composed of keratinocytes (Gu et al., 2023). This exposure also promotes collagen degradation, thereby compromising the skin’s structural integrity. Furthermore, solar irradiation activates key signaling pathways in the epidermis, including those mediated by transcription factors such as nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome (Ramos et al., 2024). These pathways inhibit the synthesis of collagen and upregulate the activity of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) (Wang et al., 2024).

MMPs comprise a large family of zinc-dependent endopeptidases that are capable of degrading all classes of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins (Liu et al., 2024). Among the ECM components, type I and III collagens are the primary structural proteins within the dermis. These collagens self-assemble into fibrillar networks that provide mechanical support, maintain skin integrity, and resist aging (San Antonio et al., 2020). However, exposure to UV radiation accelerates ECM degradation, particularly the collagen networks, by enhancing MMP-mediated proteolysis. This photodamage manifests clinically as epidermal roughness and the formation of wrinkles, with histopathological features including fragmented collagen fibers and abnormal elastotic material accumulation (Diepgen et al., 2015).

Research has suggested that natural products derived from herbs, microorganisms, and animals can protect against skin photoaging. For example, polyphenols, such as (-)-epigallocatechin-3-gallate, exert antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects through numerous mechanisms, including the inhibition of oxidative stress and the suppression of MMP expression in UVB-induced photoaging (Kim et al., 2013a).

In recent decades, natural peptides have emerged as pivotal candidates for antiphotoaging agents because of their considerable antioxidant activity (Feng et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2023). Various bioactive proteins with antiphotoaging potential have been isolated from marine organisms, such as red algae (Pallela et al., 2010). The squid (Todarodes pacificus) is a popular seafood in China, Korea, and Japan, consumed raw and semi-dried. Squid is a rich source of collagen and bioactive peptides, and it is reported to possess antioxidant (Isla Naveira et al., 2024), anti-aging (Zhen et al., 2012), angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitory (Liu et al., 2012), and anticancer properties (Alemán et al., 2011). Nevertheless, the effects of squid-derived peptides on skin photoaging have not been investigated. This study, therefore, aimed to isolate antiphotoaging peptides from squid and explore their potential mechanisms of action via in vitro and in vivo experiments.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials and reagents

Fresh squid (T. pacificus) was provided by Shenzhen Haichuang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Gibco. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits for interleukin (IL)-6, IL-1β, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α (for in vitro and in vivo analyses) were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology. All antibodies were sourced from Cell Signaling Technology. All other reagents were of analytical grade and acquired from Sigma-Aldrich. All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shenzhen University (approval no.: A202300327) and conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Isolation and identification of squid cyclic peptides (SCPs)

Crude squid protein preparation: All separation procedures were performed at 20°C－25°C (Fig. 1A). A squid homogenate (2 kg) was suspended in phosphate buffer (2.0 L), subjected to ultrasonic extraction for 30 min, and centrifuged at 3,000 g for 15 min. The supernatant was then collected, freeze-dried, and stored as the crude squid extract.

Fat removal: The squid extract (200 g) was suspended in a mixture of ethyl acetate (500 mL) and water (300 mL) with constant stirring for 30 min. After separation using a separatory funnel, the aqueous phase was collected, freeze-dried, and stored as the total squid protein.

Ultrafiltration chromatography: Total squid protein (50 g) was fractionated via ultrafiltration using membranes with molecular weight (MW) cutoffs of 1.0, 3.0, and 5.0 kDa. Four fractions were obtained: SCP-A (MW<1.0 kDa), SCP-B (1.0－3.0 kDa), SCP-C (3.0－5.0 kDa), and SCP-D (MW>5.0 kDa). The antiphotoaging activities of these fractions were evaluated in a UVB-induced human skin fibroblast (HSF) model; SCP-A exhibited the most pronounced activity.

Gel filtration chromatography: The SCP-A solution (2 mL, 2.27 g/mL) was loaded onto a Sephadex G-15 column (4.2×105 cm) and eluted with 10% (v/v) aqueous ethanol at a flow rate of 2.0 mL/min. Twenty fractions of 80 mL each were collected, freeze-dried, and assessed for antiphotoaging activity in UVB-exposed HSFs. The SCP-A-6 fraction exhibited the strongest activity.

Reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) and molecular mass determination: The SCP-A-6 (559 mg) fraction was separated via RP-HPLC on an Agilent 1200 system equipped with a Zorbax SB C-18 column (4.6×250 mm, 5 µm). The mobile phase consisted of water as solvent A and methanol as solvent B, with a flow rate of 2 mL/min. Detection was performed at 300 nm, and the column temperature was maintained at 20°C (Zhu et al., 2024).

HPLC-electrospray ionization-mass spectrometry (HPLC-ESI-MS) analysis was conducted using a SCIEX X500R Q-TOF mass spectrometer equipped with an ESI source: The mass detection range was set to m/z 100－1,000. Data were acquired in positive ion mode under the following conditions: CAD gas flow rate, 8 L/min; drying gas temperature, 500°C; ion spray voltage, 5,500 V; and declustering potential, 80 V. Data were processed using SCIEX OS v1.0 software (Rodriguez et al., 2024), and amino acid sequences of the SCPs were determined using the Marfey’s method (Takiguchi et al., 2022).

Cell culture, cell vitality, inflammatory cytokines tests, and detection of MMP-2 and MMP-9 activity

Cell culture: HSFs were purchased from Cellverse Bioscience Technology Co., Ltd. and maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. The cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM glutamine, and antibiotics.

Cell viability assay: HSFs were seeded in 6-well plates at a cell density of 2×105 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight. After starvation in serum-free DMEM for 12 h, the cells were rinsed twice with cold phosphate-buffered saline and exposed to UVB irradiation (312 nm, 25 mJ/cm2) using an SX-312 research radiometer (Uvitec) (Choi et al., 2016). Following this irradiation, the cells were treated with SCPs (100 µg/mL) or varying concentrations of SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 (0, 100, 250, 500, and 1,000 µg/mL) for 24 h. The cell viability was then assessed using a CCK-8 assay kit (Li et al., 2023).

Measurement of inflammatory cytokines: After UVB exposure, HSFs were incubated for 24 h in DMEM containing either SCP-A-6 or SCP-1 (100 µg/mL). The culture supernatants were subsequently collected, and the IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 levels were quantified using commercial ELISA kits.

Detection of MMP-2 and MMP-9 activity: HSFs were treated identically as described in the inflammatory cytokine assay. Following the 24 h incubation with SCP-A-6 or SCP-1 (100 µg/mL), the total protein was extracted, and the activities of MMP-2 and MMP-9 were measured using the corresponding commercial ELISA kits.

Animals and UV irradiation

Five-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (18±2 g, specific pathogen-free grade) were purchased from Guangzhou Yaokang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The mice were housed under standard conditions (21°C－23°C, 40%－65% humidity) with a 12 h light/dark cycle and provided ad libitum access to food and water.

After a one-week acclimatization period, the mice were randomly divided into four weight-matched groups (n=6), namely (1) blank control, (2) skin photoaging model, (3) SCP-1 treatment, and (4) SCP-A-6 treatment. Mice in the photoaging and treatment groups were exposed to daily UV irradiation for 21 days using an irradiation box equipped with three UVA lamps (40 W; wavelength range: 320－410 nm; peak: 360 nm) and one UVB lamp (40 W; wavelength range: 290－330 nm; peak: 295 nm). The lamp-to-back distance was maintained at 35 cm, and the duration of irradiation was 1 h per day. Following each UVB exposure, the dorsal skin of mice in the SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 groups was topically treated with 1 mg/mL of the respective peptide in a glycerol solution (Xu et al., 2024). Dorsal skin samples were collected 6 h after the final treatment and stored at −80°C for subsequent analysis.

Tests of transepidermal water loss (TEWL), inflammatory cytokines, and histological examination

TEWL was measured at the center of the dorsal skin using a Tewameter (Courage+Khazaka Electronic), according to a previously described method (Kim et al., 2014).

For histological examination, dorsal skin specimens (approximately 1 cm2) were fixed in 4% buffered neutral formalin for at least 24 h. The tissues then underwent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining according to standard protocols (Kim et al., 2014). Histological morphology was observed under a light microscope at 200× magnification.

For cytokine analysis, dorsal skin tissues were homogenized in cold normal saline (10%, w/v) and centrifuged at 4,500 g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected, and the IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 levels were determined using the corresponding commercial ELISA kits (Kim et al., 2014).

Western blot analysis

In vivo, dorsal skin samples (approximately 1 cm2) were collected from the treated mice and stored at −80°C until subsequent analysis. For protein extraction, the skin tissue was trimmed on ice to remove excess fat and then flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. The frozen tissue was pulverized using a mortar and homogenized in a protein extraction buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich). The homogenate was centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected. In vitro, HSFs cells were scraped off using a cell scraper, suspended in a protein extraction buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich), and incubated on ice for 30 minutes. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected. The protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay. Western blot analysis was performed according to established protocols (Kim et al., 2013b).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0, GraphPad) and are expressed as the mean±standard deviation. Differences between two groups were evaluated using the Student’s t-test. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Amino acid sequence analysis of the SCPs

Using an activity-guided purification approach targeting antiphotoaging effects, four novel SCPs were successfully isolated. Their amino acid sequences were determined (Fig. 1B), and RP-HPLC analysis at 214 nm confirmed that their purities exceeded 97.5% (Liu et al., 2025). Structural characterization was performed using tandem MS/MS, with key fragment ions for SCP-1 to SCP-4 summarized in Table 1. The absolute configurations of the constituent amino acids were verified using Marfey’s method (Takiguchi et al., 2022). Database searches in PubChem, WIPO PATENTSCOPE, and SciFinder revealed no existing records for SCP-1 to SCP-4, thus supporting their identification as novel compounds derived from squid.

SCP-1 enhances the cell viability of HSFs under UVB exposure

The antiphotoaging potential of SCP-A, SCP-A-6, and SCP-1 to SCP-4 was evaluated in a UVB-irradiated HSF model. UVB exposure significantly reduced HSF viability to 73.9% compared to that of the control group (P<0.01), thereby confirming the successful induction of photodamage (Fig. 2A). Among them, SCP-A-6 and SCP-1 exhibited the most pronounced effects, increasing viability by 29.8% (P<0.01) and 26.4% (P<0.01), respectively, relative to that of the UVB-exposed group. Although SCP-A-6 exhibited a stronger tendency to promote cell viability than SCP-1 at the same concentration, the difference was not significant (P>0.05).

We further determined the optimal concentrations of SCP-A-6 and SCP-1 using a range of 0－1,000 µg/mL. Both peptides revealed maximal protective effects at 100 µg/mL, with no significant enhancement at higher concentrations (Fig. 2B). Therefore, this concentration was selected for subsequent in vitro experiments.

SCP-1 inhibits the UVB-induced overexpression of inflammatory cytokines in HSFs

To determine whether the protective effects of SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 included the suppression of inflammation, we measured the levels of key inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6) in UVB-irradiated HSFs.

UVB exposure markedly increased the secretion of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α by 235.8% (P<0.01), 196.4% (P<0.01), and 93.5% (P<0.01), respectively, compared to those of the control group (Fig. 2C－2E). Treatment with SCP-1 or SCP-A-6 significantly attenuated this increase. Specifically, SCP-1 reduced IL-1β and IL-6 levels by 17.9% (P<0.05) and 24.7% (P<0.01), respectively, while SCP-A-6 resulted in greater reductions of 39.7% (P<0.01) and 36.1% (P<0.01), respectively. For TNF-α, SCP-A-6 induced a significant decrease of 10.8% (P<0.05), whereas the 8.80% reduction by SCP-1 was not significant (P>0.05). Notably, the inhibitory effects of SCP-A-6 on IL-1β (P<0.01) and IL-6 (P<0.05) were significantly stronger than those of SCP-1.

These results demonstrate that SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 effectively suppress the UVB-induced overexpression of inflammatory cytokines, which likely contributes to their antiphotoaging activity in HSFs.

SCP-1 inhibits the UVB-induced activation of the NLRP3 signaling pathway in HSFs

To determine whether the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway mediates the anti-inflammatory effects of SCP-1 in UVB-irradiated HSFs, we assessed the protein expression levels of NLRP3, apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC), and caspase-1.

UVB exposure significantly upregulated the expression of NLRP3, ASC, and caspase-1 compared to those of the control group, which suggests that the UVB-induced overexpression of inflammatory cytokines may be mediated via the NLRP3 pathway activation (Fig. 2F). Importantly, SCP-1 or SCP-A-6 treatment markedly suppressed the UVB-induced elevation of these proteins. These results indicate that the antiphotoaging effects of SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 are likely attributable, at least in part, to the inhibition of the UVB-triggered NLRP3 signaling pathway.

Effect of SCP-1 on the water-holding capacity and epidermal thickness in UV-induced nude mice skin

To further examine the antiphotoaging efficacy of SCP-A-6 and SCP-1 in vivo, we assessed key biomarkers, including skin thickness, hydration, and MMP activity, in a UV-induced photoaging model using nude mice. No significant differences in body weight were observed among the groups during the experimental period (data not shown).

Pronounced wrinkle formation was observed on the dorsal skin of UV-irradiated mice, which was significantly alleviated by SCP-1 or SCP-A-6 treatment (Fig. 3A). As impaired skin hydration is closely associated with wrinkle development (Han et al., 2022), we next evaluated the water-holding capacity of the skin. SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 effectively prevented UV-induced skin water loss. In particular, SCP-A-6 restored hydration to a level that was comparable to that of the blank control group (Fig. 3B).

Skin hydration is essential for maintaining barrier function by reducing TEWL. Improvements in skin barrier integrity have been demonstrated to ameliorate wrinkling (Yang et al., 2023). Therefore, our results demonstrate that SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 enhance skin barrier function by increasing hydration and reducing TEWL, thereby attenuating wrinkle formation.

Epidermal hyperplasia is another key factor in wrinkle formation. H&E staining revealed that UV irradiation significantly induced epidermal thickening, which was markedly suppressed by SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 (Fig. 3C and 3D). In addition, UV-induced photoaging was characterized by disorganized collagen fibers, sparse dermis, and sebaceous gland enlargement (Han et al., 2022). Notably, treatment with SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 improved the overall skin tissue integrity, reduced epidermal thickness, promoted denser and more structured collagen organization, and alleviated sebaceous hyperplasia. Of the two treatments, SCP-A-6 exhibited a stronger inhibitory effect on UV-induced epidermal thickening than SCP-1.

SCP-1 inhibits UV-induced activity and expression upregulation of MMPs in the skin of nude mice

We next examined whether treatment with SCP-A-6 and SCP-1 affected the expression and activity of MMP-2 and MMP-9, which are key enzymes responsible for collagen degradation and wrinkle formation. UV irradiation significantly upregulated the protein expression of MMP-2 and MMP-9 in mouse dorsal skin, an effect that was markedly suppressed with SCP-A-6 or SCP-1 treatment (Fig. 4A). We further evaluated the enzymatic activities of MMP-2 and MMP-9 and found that SCP-A-6 and SCP-1 effectively inhibited their UV-induced activation (Fig. 4B). Notably, SCP-A-6 exhibited a stronger inhibitory effect on MMP-2 activity than that of SCP-1 (P<0.05). These results indicate that SCP-A-6 and SCP-1 attenuate wrinkle formation and epidermal thickening in mouse skin by suppressing the expression and activity of UV-induced MMP-2 and MMP-9.

SCP-1 suppresses the UV-induced overexpression of inflammatory cytokines and NLRP3 signaling activation in the skin cells of nude mice

We further examined the anti-inflammatory effects of SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 in the skin of UV-irradiated nude mice. UV exposure significantly increased the levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α by 186.3% (P<0.01), 113.2% (P<0.01), and 88.4% (P<0.01), respectively, compared to those of the control group, thereby confirming UV-induced inflammatory injury (Fig. 4C－4E). Treatment with SCP-1 or SCP-A-6 significantly attenuated this response. Specifically, the IL-1β levels decreased by 21.7% (P<0.01) with SCP-1 and 29.6% (P<0.01) with SCP-A-6, while TNF-α levels were reduced by 18.6% (P<0.01) and 26.3% (P<0.01), respectively. In contrast, reductions in IL-6 levels were modest and not significant (1.87% for SCP-1 and 5.63% for SCP-A-6, both P>0.05). These results demonstrate that both peptides effectively inhibit the UV-induced overexpression of key inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-1β and TNF-α, which likely contribute to their antiphotoaging activity.

In addition, we examined whether SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 modulated the NLRP3 signaling pathway in mouse skin. As illustrated in Fig. 4F, UV irradiation markedly upregulated the protein expression of NLRP3, ASC, and caspase-1, thus indicating NLRP3 inflammasome activation. SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 treatments effectively suppressed the UV-induced elevation of these proteins. These findings suggest that the antiphotoaging effects of SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 are mediated, at least in part, through the inhibition of the NLRP3 signaling pathway in vivo.

DISCUSSION

The skin is the primary physical barrier against environmental insults, including chemicals, pathogens, and UV radiation. Skin aging occurs via two major pathways, namely intrinsic aging, which is governed by genetic and chronological factors, and extrinsic aging, which is primarily driven by environmental exposures such as chronic exposure to UVB irradiation. Extrinsic photoaging is clinically characterized by wrinkles, alterations in pigmentation, skin roughness, and loss of elasticity, which largely result from functional and structural changes in keratinocytes and fibroblasts (Zheng et al., 2013; Gupta et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023).

Cyclic peptides are closed-ring molecules that are formed by amino acids linked via peptide bonds and are widely distributed in animals, plants, and microorganisms. Compared to their linear counterparts, cyclic peptides typically exhibit more stable and rigid conformations, enhanced resistance to chemical and enzymatic degradation, and distinct bioactivity profiles (Yang et al., 2025). Furthermore, modern pharmacological studies have revealed that many cyclic peptides possess extended half lives, high potency, favorable metabolic stability, good bioavailability, and selective receptor binding (Ji et al., 2024). These properties underpin their broad biological activities, including their anticancer (He et al., 2011), antioxidant, and immunomodulatory effects (Nguyen et al., 2016). In particular, numerous cyclic peptides derived from natural products have demonstrated strong antioxidant capacity. For example, Liu et al. (2025) identified 22 antioxidant cyclic peptides from zein hydrolysates, and Xiao et al. (2023) reported that polymyxin B, isolated from Paenibacillus polymyxa, alleviates oxidative damage in mouse kidneys via the Nrf2/NQO1 pathway. Given the reported efficacy of natural antioxidant peptides in mitigating UV-induced skin injury (Kim et al., 2013b), the exploration of novel antiphotoaging agents from marine organisms remains a promising research direction.

A central mechanism in photoaging is the inflammatory cascade that is initiated by UV radiation. Chronic UV exposure induces the overproduction of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in the epidermis and dermis (Nuryana et al., 2020). These cytokines are primarily secreted by keratinocytes and fibroblasts, which in turn upregulate the generation of MMPs. These degrade collagen and elastin, which are key structural components of the skin, thereby accelerating tissue damage and wrinkle formation (Song et al., 2023; Yokoi et al., 2023). Our results are consistent with this framework, where the squid peptide extract SCP-A-6 and the purified cyclic peptide SCP-1 both significantly protected HSFs from UVB-induced cytotoxicity, thus highlighting their direct cytoprotective role.

As inflammatory damage is a pivotal driver of photoaging, anti-inflammatory interventions are a rational therapeutic strategy (Awad et al., 2018). We focused on the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway as a key mediator of UV-induced cellular stress (Li et al., 2023). Its activation is a two-step process, where priming signals (e.g., from UV radiation) trigger the transcriptional upregulation of NLRP3, pro-IL-1β, and pro-IL-18 via the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, followed by inflammasome assembly, caspase-1 activation, and the subsequent cleavage of pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their active forms (He et al., 2016). Our in vitro data reveal that SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 suppressed the UVB-induced overexpression of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, and markedly inhibited the upregulation of NLRP3, ASC, and caspase-1. These findings indicate that suppression of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway is a central mechanism for the anti-inflammatory and photoprotective effects of these peptides.

To validate these results in vivo, we employed a nude mouse model of UV-induced photoaging, which replicates the major features of human photoaged skin, including wrinkle formation, epidermal hyperplasia, impaired hydration, collagen disorganization, and sebaceous gland hyperplasia (Han et al., 2022). The topical application of SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 ameliorated these phenotypes by reducing wrinkle severity, improving skin hydration (with SCP-A-6 restoring it to nearly that of the control), attenuating epidermal thickening, enhancing collagen density and organization, and reducing sebaceous hyperplasia. Notably, SCP-A-6 consistently outperformed SCP-1 in reducing epidermal thickness in vivo, which reiterated its stronger promotion of HSF viability in vitro, thereby suggesting potential synergistic interactions among the constituents of the SCP-A-6 fraction.

Collagen degradation, largely mediated by UV-induced MMPs, is a key event in the pathogenesis of wrinkle formation (Onoue et al., 2003). Our in vivo data confirmed that UV exposure significantly elevated the expression and enzymatic activity of MMP-2 and MMP-9, which are gelatinases that target type IV and VII collagens in the basement membrane (Kim et al., 2013b; Tawaran et al., 2016). Critically, SCP-A-6 and SCP-1 both suppressed this UV-induced upregulation and activation of MMP-2 and MMP-9, thus providing a mechanistic basis for the observed reduction in wrinkles and epidermal hyperplasia.

Finally, we confirmed that the in vivo antiphotoaging effects also involved the modulation of cutaneous inflammation and NLRP3 signaling. Consistent with our cellular findings, SCP-1 and SCP-A-6 reduced the UV-induced levels of IL-1β and TNF-α and suppressed the protein expression of NLRP3, ASC, and caspase-1 in mouse skin.

In summary, this study demonstrates that SCP-1 and the peptide fraction SCP-A-6 protect against UV-induced photoaging via a multitargeted mechanism that involves enhanced cell viability, improved skin hydration, inhibition of inflammatory cytokine production, suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and downregulation of MMP expression and activity. The consistently superior efficacy of SCP-A-6 suggests that this complex peptide mixture may exert synergistic antiphotoaging effects, thereby highlighting its potential as a source of bioactive ingredients for skincare applications.
